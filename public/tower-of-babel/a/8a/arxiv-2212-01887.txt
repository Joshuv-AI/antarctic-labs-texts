The Optimality of Blocking Designs in Equally and Unequally Allocated Randomized Experiments with General Response
David Azriel, Abba M. Krieger, Adam Kapelner

Abstract
We consider the performance of the difference-in-means estimator in a two-arm randomized experiment under common experimental endpoints such as continuous (regression), incidence, proportion and survival. We examine performance under both equal and unequal allocation to treatment groups and we consider both the Neyman randomization model and the population model. We show that in the Neyman model, where the only source of randomness is the treatment manipulation, there is no free lunch: complete randomization is minimax for the estimator’s mean squared error. In the population model, where each subject experiences response noise with zero mean, the optimal design is the deterministic perfect-balance allocation. However, this allocation is generally NP-hard to compute and moreover, depends on unknown response parameters. When considering the tail criterion of Kapelner et al. (2021), we show the optimal design is less random than complete randomization and more random than the deterministic perfect-balance allocation. We prove that Fisher’s blocking design provides the asymptotically optimal degree of experimental randomness. Theoretical results are supported by simulations in all considered experimental settings. Keywords: experimental design, optimal design, blocking, unequal randomization, restricted randomization



1
Background

We consider a classic problem: an experiment with
2n

subjects
(
individuals
,
participants
or
units
). The experiment has two
arms
(
treatments
,
manipulations
or
groups
), which we will call treatment and control denoted
T
and
C
, and the number of treatments denoted
n_{T}
and number of controls denoted
n_{C}
. We consider one clearly defined
outcome
(
response
or
endpoint
) of interest for the
2n
subjects denoted
\boldsymbol{y}=\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{2n}\right]^{\top}
, which is of general type: either continuous, incidence, count or uncensored survival. Subjects have
p
observed subject-specific
covariates
(
measurements
,
features
or
characteristics
) denoted
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for the
i
th subject. The setting we investigate is where all
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
’s are known beforehand and considered fixed. This non-sequential setting was studied by
Fisher, 1925
when assigning treatments to agricultural plots and is still of great importance today. In fact, this setting is found in “many phase I studies [that] use ‘banks’ of healthy volunteers … [and] … in most cluster randomised trials, the clusters are identified before treatment is started”
(
Senn, 2013
, page 1440)
. The more common
sequential
experimental setting where subjects arrive one-by-one and must be assigned on the spot (hence all
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
’s are not seen beforehand), we will leave for future work.
Sverdlov and Ryeznik, 2019
provides an extensive discussion and simulation of common sequential unequal allocation designs.

Formally defined, the
randomization
(
allocation
or an
assignment
) is a vector
\boldsymbol{w}=\left[w_{1},\ldots,w_{2n}\right]^{\top}
whose entries indicate whether the subject received
T
(coded numerically as +1) or
C
(coded numerically as -1) and thus this vector is an element in
\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{2n}
. After the sample is provided, the only degree of control the experimenter has is to choose the entries of
\boldsymbol{w}
. The process that results in the choices of such allocations of the
2n
subjects to the two arms is termed an experimental
design
(
strategy
,
algorithm
,
method
or
procedure
). Experimental design is thus a generalized multivariate Bernoulli random variable that we denote
\boldsymbol{W}
and its number of allocations as
N_{\boldsymbol{W}}
where the design can technically be degenerate (i.e.,
N_{\boldsymbol{W}}=1
).

We show herein that the optimal asymptotic design for general response is one that blends pure randomization with deterministic optimization. Further, this optimal design is satisfied by Fisher’s historic blocking design
as commonly implemented today
. Before we provide more details about our contribution, we define the equal allocation setting and unequal allocation setting (as our result applies to both settings) in the next two subsections. To contextualize our result, we also provide some advantages and disadvantages of both classic and modern experimental designs.

1.1
Equal Allocation Experimentation

If
n_{T}=n_{C}
, the design is termed
equal allocation
. The default design here is the balanced complete randomization design (BCRD,
Wu, 1981
). This design has one constraint:
\sum_{i}w_{i}=0
which is satisfied by
\binom{2n}{n}
vectors. These vectors are then sampled uniformly. BCRD has been given the high distinction of “the gold standard”.
Cornfield, 1959
gives two reasons: (1) known and unknown covariate differences among the groups will be small and bounded and (2) it is the “reasoned basis” for causal inference, a paraphrasing of
Fisher, 1935
. Any other design we will call “restricted” as it is restricted to use fewer assignment vectors.

Why employ a restricted design? There is a large problem with BCRD that was identified immediately by
Fisher, 1925
: under some unlucky
\boldsymbol{w}
’s there are large differences in the distribution of observed covariates between the two groups. The amount of covariate value heterogeneity between groups we term
observed imbalance
. This observed imbalance leads to higher variance during estimation (as defined at the end of Section
3
). Through an abuse of terminology, many times the literature denotes this as simply
imbalance
, but this is an ambiguous term since it usually ignores the state of imbalance in the unobserved covariates which also leads to higher variance during estimation (again, see the end of Section
3
).

Mitigating the chance of large observed imbalances is the predominant reason for employing a restricted randomization procedure. These procedures take the observed covariate values as input and compute assignments with small observed imbalance as output.
Fisher, 1925
wrote “it is still possible to eliminate much of the …heterogeneity, and so increase the accuracy of our [estimator], by laying restrictions on the order in which the strips are arranged”. Here, he introduced the
block design
, a restricted design still popular today, especially in clinical trials. Another early mitigation approach can be found in
Student, 1938
who wrote that after an unlucky, highly imbalanced randomization, “it would be pedantic to [run the experiment] with [an assignment] known beforehand to be likely to lead to a misleading conclusion”. His solution is for “common sense [to prevail] and chance [be] invoked a second time”. In foregoing the first assignment and
rerandomizing
to find a better assignment, all allocations worse than a predetermined threshold of observed imbalance are eliminated. This classic strategy has been rigorously investigated only recently
(
Morgan and Rubin, 2012
;
Li et al., 2018
)
in the case of equal allocation. Another idea is to allocate treatment and control among similar subjects by using a pairwise matching (PM) design
(
Greevy et al., 2004
)
. This design creates
n
pairs (i.e., blocks with size 2) and randomizes the two subjects to T/C or C/T. All of the above-mentioned restricted designs (blocking, rerandomization, PM) are still “random” in the sense that there are still exponentially many possible allocations.

One may wonder about the “optimal” design strategy. Regardless of how optimality is defined and regardless of whichever criterion is used to measure performance, we believe this is an impossible question to answer. The multivariate Bernoulli random variable has
2^{2n}-1
parameters, an exponentially large number
(
Teugels, 1990
, Section 2.3)
. Finding the optimal design is thus tantamount to solving for exponentially many parameters using only the
2n
observations, a hopelessly unidentifiable task. Thus, to find an “optimal” design, the space of designs must be limited.

One way of limiting the space of designs is to do away with any notion of randomization whatsoever and instead locate a
deterministic optimal design
with one optimal allocation vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
, a design which we will call
perfect balance
(PB). An early advocate of this approach was
Smith, 1918
and a good review of classic works advocating this approach such as
Kiefer and Wolfowitz, 1959
is given in
Steinberg and Hunter, 1984
. A particularly edgy advocate is
Harville, 1975
who penned an article titled “Experimental Randomization: Who Needs It?”. Although unpalatable to modern ears that have been conditioned to the concept that “randomization is the gold standard”, this work and our previous work showed that under certain assumptions, there is no need for randomness in the design when the inferential focus is on effect estimation (hypothesis testing is a different discussion).

How does one find this unique allocation
\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
? One needs to first define a numerical metric of imbalance as a function of the
\boldsymbol{x}
’s and
\boldsymbol{w}
. Then, the optimization problem becomes a binary integer programming problem where the unequal allocation requirement would merely alter one of the inputted constraints. These types of problems are usually NP-hard but can be approximated via numerical optimization
(
Bertsimas et al., 2015
;
Kallus, 2018
)
. Sometimes there are polynomial-time algorithms that provide “good-enough” solutions for practical purposes, e.g. branch and bound
(
Land and Doig, 1960
)
. Selection of the imbalance metric is arbitrary but recent work by
Kallus, 2018
posits an appropriate metric to use based on assumptions about the structure of the response function.

There are even other designs that attempt to retain the best of both worlds i.e. randomizing while simultaneously optimizing. For instance,
Krieger et al., 2019
starts with BCRD and greedily switches pairs of treatment and control subjects until reaching a locally optimal minimum of observed covariate imbalance. This procedure provably retains most of the randomness of BCRD while achieving provably minuscule observed imbalance.
Zhu and Liu, 2021
also use pair-switching but do so non-greedily and thus can explore the space of forced-balanced allocations more completely.
Harshaw et al., 2019
use the Gram-Schmidt Walk design to provide allocations that have high-degree of randomness and low observed imbalance. Another approach is the Finite Selection Model procedure of
Chattopadhyay et al., 2022
. They employ a sequentially controlled Markovian random sampling to assign
w_{i}
’s based on a
D
-optimality criterion.

1.2
Unequal Allocation Experimentation

There are many practical situations where subjects are allocated to the two arms unequally. For example, to reduce the cost of the trial (e.g the treatment is an expensive surgery and the control is an inexpensive placebo), avoid loss of power from drop-out or cross-over, various ethical concerns, for gaining additional information on one manipulation preferentially
(
Dumville et al., 2006
)
, as well as if the response variance is known to be heterogeneous across manipulations
(
Wong and Zhu, 2008
)
.
Torgerson and Campbell, 1997
makes the general recommendation that unequal allocation should be employed more often in the case of economic concerns. The literature on unequal allocation is more limited than on equal allocation.

We will now discuss the historic designs above in the context of unequal allocation beginning with BCRD. It is clear BCRD must be altered as it is by definition an exclusively equal allocation design. The analogue design of BCRD will be the unique design where all
\binom{2n}{n_{T}}=\binom{2n}{n_{C}}
vectors satisfy the imbalance constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=n_{T}-n_{C}<0
. We will term this modified design as the
imbalanced complete randomization design
(iBCRD) although it has other names
(
Sverdlov and Ryeznik, 2019
, Section 3.1.1)
. Since BCRD is a special case of iBCRD, going forward we will refer to both as i/BCRD. Blocking in the case of unequal randomization requires each blocks’ allocations to largely respect the allocation ratio; and this design is usually termed the
permuted block design

(
Ryeznik and Sverdlov, 2018
, Section 2.2.2)
. As for PM, this design must be modified as it is by definition an equal allocation design. We talk about this modification in Section
5
.

Most of the extant unequal allocation literature focuses on (a) computing the allocation proportions and (b) how to then allocate sequentially. The first attempt at (a) is based on heterogeneous treatment costs:
Nam, 1973
assumes a continuous endpoint distributed normally.
Tymofyeyev et al., 2007
propose an optimization framework for (a) in the binary response case and shows it is usually convex.
Feng and Hu, 2018
employ this framework for (b).
Sverdlov and Ryeznik, 2019
propose a procedure for (a) for two or more treatment groups with a normally distributed regression endpoint. Their objective is to minimize the overall cost of the experiment subject to a minimum requirement for statistical power. Further, they discuss performance under both the
population model
and
randomization model
, two different worldviews that are fundamental to the differing results in this work. We view this work as complementary; the extensive work on (a) can be applied directly herein as a pre-step to choose the allocation ratio before implementing the design we recommend.

We are not aware of work that discusses unequal-allocation design vis-a-vis the consideration of minimizing observed imbalance in the non-sequential setting where all
\boldsymbol{x}
’s are known a priori.

1.3
The Randomness-Imbalance Tradeoff

The above gives a short introduction to the wide range of design ideas. At the “extremes” of this range, there is i/BCRD on one side and the minimized observed imbalance deterministic PB design on the other side. Everything in between these extremes attempts to compromise between observed imbalance and degree of randomness.

Is randomness worth sacrificing for less observed imbalance? This question was investigated in
Kapelner et al., 2021
for the case of a continuous outcome under equal treatment-control allocation (henceforth referred to as “our previous work”). There was a threefold answer: (I) if the experimenter is concerned that nature may provide the worst possible unobserved covariates then one should never sacrifice any randomness, (II) if the experimenter treats the unobserved covariates as random noise that can be averaged over, then one should sacrifice all randomness and (III) if the experimenter is concerned of tail events of unobserved covariates then one should sacrifice some randomness. A related work is
Nordin and Schultzberg, 2022
, which studies a measure of how correlated different assignment vectors in a design are with each other. They show that the variance of the MSE increases linearly in this measure. Therefore, in order to avoid high variance of the MSE, one needs to consider designs where this correlation metric is small.

The purpose of this work is to reconsider the above question for the most common endpoints in experiments and clinical trials: continuous, incidence, survival, count and proportion and for treatment allocation that are both equal and unequal across treatments. We prove that our previous work’s results generalize. We further uncover the asymptotic optimality of blocking designs in (III). We define our experimental setting formally in Section
2
, discuss criterions of performance and theoretical results in Section
3
, provide simulation evidence of our results in Section
4
and conclude in Section
5
.

2
Problem Setup

Consider the potential outcome framework of
Rubin, 2005
and let
\boldsymbol{y}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{C}
denote the vectors of all subject responses under the treatment and control respectively. For each
i=1,\ldots,2n
the observed response
y_{i}
is
y_{T,i}
(respectively,
y_{C,i}
) if
w_{i}=+1
(respectively,
w_{i}=-1
). Let
\boldsymbol{W}
denote the possible unequal-allocation design which produces vectors where
n_{T}\leq n_{C}
with
n_{T}
and
n_{C}
fixed. In a vector form, the
2n
random responses
\boldsymbol{Y}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\boldsymbol{y}_{T}+\boldsymbol{y}_{C}+\text{Diag}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right](\boldsymbol{y}_{T}-\boldsymbol{y}_{C})\Big).

(1)

under the Stable Unit Treatment Values Assumption. Note that the randomness in the responses in Equation
1
is only due to the randomness in the design
\boldsymbol{W}
. This limitation of randomness to the design exclusively is known as the
randomization model
or
Neyman Model

(
Freedman, 2008
;
Lin, 2013
;
Rosenberger and Lachin, 2016
, Chapter 6.3)
in contrast to a
population model
, which we will describe in Section
3.2
.

We consider the sample average treatment effect as our estimand,

\displaystyle\tau:=\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{y}_{T}-\boldsymbol{y}_{C}).

(2)

This estimand is a natural target of inference for all outcome types. In the incidence setting, this estimand is the mean probability difference (also called the
risk difference
). Sometimes, practitioners prefer the risk ratio, odds ratio or log odds ratio. Future work can examine additional estimands beyond the mean probability difference in the setting of the binary outcome.

An intuitive estimate for our estimand is
\hat{\tau}=\bar{y}_{T}-\bar{y}_{C}
where
\bar{y}_{T}
and
\bar{y}_{C}
are the average response in the treatment and control groups respectively when computed for a specific
\boldsymbol{w}
from any design
\boldsymbol{W}
. Using Equation
1
and the elementary computation found in Section
A.1
of the Supplementary Material, we can conveniently express this estimate’s estimator as

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}=\frac{1}{2n}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}^{\top}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{y}_{T}}{r}-\frac{\boldsymbol{y}_{C}}{\tilde{r}}\right)+\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{y}_{T}}{r}+\frac{\boldsymbol{y}_{C}}{\tilde{r}}\right)\right).

(3)

where
r:=n_{T}/n\in(0,1]
and
\tilde{r}:=n_{C}/n=2-r\in[1,2)
. This notation has the benefit that substitution of
r=\tilde{r}=1
(or equivalently,
r-\tilde{r}=0
) implies the equal allocation setting (the more common experimental setting) providing for easy visual simplification of our expressions going forward.

In order to prove results about the performance of this estimator under different designs, we now make two assumption about the design space:

Assumption 2.1
.

All assignments respect a specific equal or unequal allocation, i.e.
\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}=(r-\tilde{r})n
for all
\boldsymbol{w}
in the support of
\boldsymbol{W}
. Note this quantity is zero under equal allocation.

Assumption 2.2
.

All subjects have equal probability of being assigned the treatment condition, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right]=\frac{1}{2}(r-\tilde{r})\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}
. Note this quantity is the zero vector under equal allocation.

Computation found in Section
A.2
of the Supplementary Material shows our estimator is unbiased under Assumption
2.2
. The mean squared error (MSE) over all assignments is thus its variance which can be expressed as the quadratic form

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{v}

(4)

where the defining matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
denotes
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right]
, the variance-covariance matrix of the design, and
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{y}_{T}/r+\boldsymbol{y}_{C}/\tilde{r}
, the “potential outcome vector”
(
Harshaw et al., 2019
, page 6)
.

We will be analyzing performance for many types of responses. It will be useful to express potential outcome as a sum of two components: (a) what we will call the “response mean”
\mu_{i}
which is a function of the observed covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and the assignment
w_{i}
and (b)
z_{i}
which is a function of unobserved covariates
z_{i}
and the assignment
w_{i}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{T}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}+\boldsymbol{z}_{T}\in\mathcal{Y},~~\boldsymbol{y}_{C}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}+\boldsymbol{z}_{C}\in\mathcal{Y}

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}
denote the stacked vectors of the observed covariates’ contribution to the responses when
\boldsymbol{w}=+\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=-\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}
respectively. Analogously,
\boldsymbol{z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
denote the stacked vectors of the unobserved covariates’ contribution to the responses when
\boldsymbol{w}=+\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=-\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}
respectively. The potential response values belong to different sets
\mathcal{Y}
depending on response type.

(column 1) and this table also lists common generalized linear models (GLMs) for each of the responses’ means (column 4). These means likewise belong to different sets
\Theta

\Theta

z_{i}

noise
or
errors

z_{i}=y_{i}-\mu_{i}
, the space of permissible values of the

’s is analogously limited for each response type to be within
\mathcal{Y}\,-\,\Theta
and the space

3
Analyzing Designs

It is impossible to employ the MSE as the criterion in which we compare different designs since
\boldsymbol{z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
are not observed. Thus, as in our previous work, we do not employ MSE as the performance criterion
directly
but instead remove the dependence on the
z_{i}
’s in three different ways to arrive at three different practical criterions (a) the worst-case MSE, (b) the mean MSE and (c) the tail MSE. These are explored in the next three sections respectively.

Before we do so, it is worthwhile to have intuition about what drives the MSE of our estimator. Using the partitioning of the response in Equation
5
and letting let
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}/r+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}/\tilde{r}
and
\boldsymbol{z}:=\boldsymbol{z}_{T}/r+\boldsymbol{z}_{C}/\tilde{r}
, we can rewrite Equation
4
dropping the constant multiple as

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\propto\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+2\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{z}.

We will see below that the first term represents imbalance in the observed covariates and the last term represents imbalance in the unobserved covariates.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
is defined as
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right]^{\top}
where the latter term is constant for all designs by Assumption
2.2
. Its first term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{W}}}p_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}

(6)

where
p_{k}:=\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\right)
, i.e., the probability that the
k
th allocation is used in the experiment. Thus, the MSE can be alternatively expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\propto c_{r,n}+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{W}}}p_{k}\left((\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{k})^{2}+2\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}+(\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{k})^{2}\right)

(7)

where
c_{r,n}
is independent of the design. The first term within the parentheses represents the mean (over all allocations) of imbalance among the observed covariates’ mean contribution and the last term within the parentheses represents the mean (over all allocations) of imbalance among the unobserved covariates. In the case of continuous response, linear mean model and
p=1
, the first term becomes
(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{k})^{2}
which is the mean difference of the observed covariate values between treatment and control arms. The outer sum with
p_{k}
takes the mean of this mean over all allocations properly weighted.

3.1
The Worst Case MSE

We attempt to find the best design under an adversarial nature that provides us the worst possible
\boldsymbol{z}_{T},\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
values,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{*}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{W}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}_{T},\boldsymbol{z}_{C}}\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]

where
\mathcal{W}
denotes the space of all legal designs whose allocations satisfy Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. In the supremum operation, the space of legal
\boldsymbol{z}_{T}
’s and
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
’s will then depend on the response type. Since the
\boldsymbol{z}
vectors sum with
\boldsymbol{\mu}
to yield the response (Equation
5
) and the response averages are subtracted to compute
\boldsymbol{v}
, this inner problem is equivalent to
\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{v}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the space of all possible
\boldsymbol{v}
’s. When
\mathcal{V}
is unbounded the problem is ill-defined as the inner supremum is infinite. Thus, to make the problem realistic (response metrics in real-world experiments are bounded), we limit the space of vectors to
\mathcal{V}_{M}\subset\mathcal{V}
. The definition of the subspace
\mathcal{V}_{M}
depends on the response. For the continuous case it is natural to bound the space by the Euclidean norm of
\boldsymbol{v}
as in
Efron, 1971
who employs this norm to define “accidental bias” bounded at one (without loss of generality). For the count and survival response it is appropriate to bound the sup norm of
\boldsymbol{v}
. Thus, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{M}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:\|\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq 1\}&\text{continuous response}\\
\left\{0,1/r,1/\tilde{r},1/r+1/\tilde{r}\right\}^{2n}&\text{incidence}\\
\left(0,1/r+1/\tilde{r}\right)^{2n}&\text{proportion responses}\\
\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\left\{i/r+j/\tilde{r}\,:\,i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}^{n}:\max(\boldsymbol{v})\leq M\right\}&\text{count}\\
\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in(0,\infty)^{n}:\max(\boldsymbol{v})\leq M\right\}&\text{survival responses}\end{array}\right.

where
M
is a large positive constant such that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}>M\right)
is negligible for all subjects. The design location problem then becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{*}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{W}}\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{M}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{v},

(14)

which is exactly tractable for the case of the response being continuous. In this case, the inner maximum is the largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
. The
\boldsymbol{W}
that minimizes this largest eigenvalue is such where each subject is assigned on the basis of a fair coin flip and is either called the
complete randomization
design
(
Rosenberger and Lachin, 2016
, Chapter 3.2)
or the
the Bernoulli Trial

(
Imbens and Rubin, 2015
, Chapter 4.2)
. In this design,
\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{I}_{2n}
which mirrors the minimax result found in
Kallus, 2018
. However, this design violates our space of considered designs which are only those that satisfy a prespecified allocation to the two treatments (Assumption
2.1
). Within the space of allowable designs, we have the following no free lunch result which follows from
Kapelner et al., 2021
and proven in Section
A.3
in the Supplemental Material.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
and continuous response, i/BCRD is the minimax optimal design in the case of unequal / equal allocation for the worst case MSE criterion (Equation
14
).

We can think of this result as minimizing the effect of the third term in the MSE decomposition of Equation
7
while not addressing the first term.

If the response is non-continuous, the max problem embedded within Equation
14
is generally intractable. In order to make this problem tractable, we further limit the set of designs to block designs.

3.1.1
The Optimal Design Among Block Designs

We denote block designs with
B
blocks as
\boldsymbol{W}\in BL(B)
. Each block is of size
n_{B}
, where
n_{B}:=2n/B
, and in each block there will be exactly
\frac{n_{T}}{2n}n_{B}
subjects assigned to the treatment manipulation and
\frac{n_{C}}{2n}n_{B}
subjects assigned to the control manipulation; thereby the number of subjects in each block respects the overall unequal (and equal) allocation. It is assumed that
n_{B}
,
\frac{n_{T}}{2n}n_{B}
and
\frac{n_{C}}{2n}n_{B}
are integers. The block design with the smallest number of blocks (
B=1
) is equivalent to i/BCRD.

It is clear that
BL(B)
satisfies Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
. The computation in Appendix
A.3
applied to each block implies that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
is block diagonal with blocksize
n_{B}
where the blocks are all equal to
r\tilde{r}(n_{B}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{B}}-\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}})/(n_{B}-1)
where
\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}
denotes the
n_{B}\times n_{B}
matrix of all ones.

We show in Theorem
3.2
that i/BCRD is minimax among blocking designs, for all response types considered. Note that i/BCRD is a block design with one block (
B=1
,
n_{B}=2n
). The proof is found in Section
A.4
in the Supplemental Material.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider the responses listed in Table
1
and the definition of
\mathcal{V}_{M}
given in Equation
3.1
, then we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{i/BCRD}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in BL(B)}\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{M}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{v}.

In summary, if one wishes to gauge design performance by the
worst case MSE
, there is no free lunch; there is no way to mitigate observed covariate imbalances as they will expose your estimator to tail events in the unobserved covariates.

3.2
The Mean MSE

The minimax criterion forces a design that is extremely conservative to protect against the most adversarial
\boldsymbol{v}
which is one arbitrary direction in
\mathcal{V}_{M}
, a large
2n
-dimensional space. Why not instead design with the “average
\boldsymbol{v}
” in mind? As the
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
’s are fixed (and thus the
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}
are fixed), we then must consider the
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{T}
as random noise realizations. This is equivalent to assuming that each subject is sampled from a large population also known formally as the
population model
assumption
(
Rosenberger and Lachin, 2016
, Chapter 6.2)
whose validity is highly contested especially in clinical trials
(
Lachin, 1988
)
. Regardless of the long-standing debate between the population model and the randomization model, many practitioners assume the population model implicitly and we will likewise in the next two subsections. We reiterate that the mean MSE of this section is one of the most common criterions in the experimental literature.

We formally let
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
be the vector random variables that generate the
\boldsymbol{z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
vectors respectively (quantities that are canonically denoted with the Greek letter epsilon). We assume
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}\right]=\boldsymbol{0}_{n}
and that the components in
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
are independent, the components of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
are independent and the components of the former and latter are mutually independent. Although the distributional form of both
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
are dependent upon the response type specified by
\mathcal{Y}
, we do not make any formal distributional assumptions besides existence of moments.

We now consider the criterion defined as the expectation of the MSE of Equation
4
over
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
. Section
A.5
in the Supplementary Material shows this to be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+c_{Z}

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}/r+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}/\tilde{r}
and
c_{Z}
is a constant with respect to the design
\boldsymbol{W}
defined in Section
A.5
. Intuitively, this expectation operation renders the third term in the MSE decomposition of Equation
7
as a constant and eliminates the second term. Thus, the optimization problem can then be expressed as
\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Since the second term in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
is a constant with respect to the design and the first term can be written as in Equation
6
, this optimization problem can alternatively be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{*}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{W}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\right]\boldsymbol{\mu}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{W}}}p_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}~:~\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}=(r-\tilde{r})n}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}.

(16)

where the sum in the middle step above is taken over all allocations in the design and the
p_{k}
’s denote the probability of the allocation being selected in the experiment.

In the case of equal allocation, the design that satisfies Assumption
2.2
and solves the optimization problem is the design with
N_{\boldsymbol{W}}=2
where the two vectors are
\boldsymbol{w}_{*},-\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
where
\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
is defined to be the unique vector minimizing
\left|\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\right|
, the imbalance between the two arms of the observed covariates’ contribution to the responses. This is an NP-hard problem but there are heuristics that could solve it approximately given
\boldsymbol{\mu}
as input. In the case of unequal allocation, Equation
16
is a difficult problem to solve as the observed covariate imbalances must be minimized while each subject is being placed into the T group in the exact proportion
\frac{n_{T}}{2n}
over all
N_{\boldsymbol{W}}
vectors. We do not know of a heuristic that can perform this task and we leave its investigation for future work.

However, even in the case of equal allocation, the quantity
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is unknown a priori (e.g. because the values of the parameters
\beta_{j}
are unknown in the example mean response models given in Table
1
, column 4). Thus, in real-world scenarios the vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
is absolutely impossible to locate even for the simplest GLMs using heuristic methods. There is one exception — the limited case where
p=1
, a linear response model, continuous response and additive treatment effect. Here, the mean response model is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}=\left((\beta_{0}+\beta_{T})/r+(\beta_{0}-\beta_{T})/\tilde{r}\right)\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}+\beta_{1}(1/r+1/\tilde{r})\boldsymbol{x}.

Thus, the optimization problem of Equation
16
is solved by the unique vector that minimizes
|\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}|
. This problem alone can be approximated by numerical methods
(
Kapelner et al., 2021
, Section 2.2.5)
.

In summary, finding the optimal design under the mean MSE criterion is generally intractable. In order to make the problem tractable, we again consider the space of block designs only as in Section
3.1.1
.

3.2.1
The Optimal Design Among Block Designs

Unlike before, we must additionally assume that the order of the elements in the vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Formally, we assume

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{T,1}}{r}+\frac{\mu_{C,1}}{\tilde{r}}\leq\frac{\mu_{T,2}}{r}+\frac{\mu_{C,2}}{\tilde{r}}\leq\cdots\leq\frac{\mu_{T,n}}{r}+\frac{\mu_{C,n}}{\tilde{r}}.

(17)

When
p=1
, this assumption is true for all the GLMs given in Table
1
(column 4) since
\mu_{T,i}/r+\mu_{C,i}/\tilde{r}
are monotone in
x_{i}
(see also
Kapelner et al., 2022
who discusses this assumption for binary response). When
p>1
, blocks are created that approximate this order.

Consider the block designs with respect to this order as defined at the end of Section
3.1
.
Let
k
be the smallest possible block size (i.e.,
k
is the smallest integer such that both
\frac{n_{T}}{2n}k
and
\frac{n_{C}}{2n}k
are integers). Theorem
3.3
below implies that
BL(k)
has the smallest mean MSE among block designs. Its proof can be found in Section
A.6
of the Supplemental Material.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider the mean criterion given in Equation
15
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{BL(k)}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{BL(m)}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right],

(18)

for all the vectors
\boldsymbol{\mu}
that satisfy the order condition of Equation
17
and for any possible block size
m
where both
\frac{n_{T}}{2n}m
and
\frac{n_{C}}{2n}m
are integers.

Note that the inequality of Equation
18
is tight if
2n-1
of the entries in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are zero and the remaining entry is one, we get equality when
m=1
corresponding to the the iBCRD design. In the case of equal allocation, the minimum block size is
n_{B}=k=2
corresponding to the PM design.

3.3
The MSE’s Tail

The design optimizing for the
worst case MSE
hedges for tail events that have negligible probability and thus is too conservative. The
mean MSE
design (besides being intractable) does not hedge for these tail
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
’s and
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
’s that could introduce large mean squared error with non-negligible probability. Alternatively expressed, the
worst case MSE
minimizes the effect of the third term in the MSE decomposition of Equation
7
while failing to diminish the first term. The
mean MSE
minimizes the first term only because it has the luxury of underhandedly ignoring the third term. We believe these two extant criterions are too crude to measure what we believe to be important: the effect of a large fraction of deleterious tail
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
’s and
\boldsymbol{z}_{C}
’s, thereby balancing the contribution of all three terms in the MSE decomposition (Equation
7
).

To properly control this tail MSE, our previous work proposed a novel
tail criterion
which incorporates both the mean and tail events. This new criterion is less conservative than the minimax criterion for more responsive to the tail events. Our previous work showed that the tail criterion leads to a design that is more random than the PB design with two possible allocations
\boldsymbol{w}_{*},-\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
(see end of Section
3.2
), and on the other hand, is more balanced and thus more restrictive than i/BCRD. As shown in our previous work and also below, there is a fundamental trade-off: shrinking the mean MSE increases the tail risk of MSE. In this work, we go further and show that block design is asymptotically optimal for the tail criterion under some conditions for many popular response types and unequal treatment allocation. We now define the tail criterion below.

Since MSE is by definition nonnegative, the right tail at a high quantile
q
(e.g. 95% or 99%) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\text{Quantile}_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right],q\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]+c_{\boldsymbol{W},q}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]}.

(19)

There exists a constant
c_{\boldsymbol{W},q}
that depends upon the design and the quantile desired. As this constant is not tractable to analyze, we consider the criterion we call the
approximate tail criterion
where we view this constant as fixed with respect to the design,

\displaystyle Q_{q}:=\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]+c_{q}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]}.

(20)

This criterion allows us to incorporate the effect of both the mean and variance with respect to the unobserved covariates and allows us to compare designs asymptotically. We discuss other criterions that can capture both considerations in Section
5
. We demonstrate later via simulation that employing the standard normal quantile
c_{q}:=-z_{1-q}
provides near-exact quantiles as we conjecture there is a central limit theorem on
\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]
over realizations of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
if
N_{\boldsymbol{W}}
is large.

Using the expectation from Equation
15
and the variance term computed in Section
A.7
in the Supplemental Material, we can express the approximate tail criterion as

\displaystyle Q_{q}=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\left(c_{Z}+\mathcal{B}_{1}+c_{q}\sqrt{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\kappa_{Z}+4\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}+\mathcal{S}\right)+2\mathcal{R}}\right)

(21)

where the
\kappa_{z}
term is a scalar constant with respect to the design
\boldsymbol{W}
and a metric similar to the sum of excess kertoses in the components of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
(see Section
A.7
of the Supplementary Material for its formal definition) and the four other terms are functions of the design described below.

\mathcal{B}_{1}:=

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}

This is the active term in the MSE expression of Equation
15
which was explained as a squared difference or “imbalance” term capturing how different the observed covariates’ contribution to the response are between treatment and control groups. This term is unaffected by the distribution of the unobserved
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
.

\mathcal{B}_{2}:=

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}:=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}\right]/r+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}\right]/\tilde{r}
. This term is a squared difference or imbalance term weighted by the subject-specific variance terms. If all variances of the unobserved effects are bounded, this behaves similarly to term
\mathcal{B}_{1}
as it is bounded above by a constant times
\mathcal{B}_{1}
(see the proof of Theorem
3.6
below).

\mathcal{S}:=

r\tilde{r}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}:=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{T}^{3}\right]/r+\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{C}^{3}\right]/\tilde{r}
, a constant with respect to the design. The term
\mathcal{S}
measures an average difference or imbalance between the responses in the two groups (a quantity not squared and hence has a sign) and weighted by the subject-specific skewnesses. If the distribution of the
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
are approximately symmetric, this term vanishes. Theorem
3.6
below shows that
\mathcal{S}
is asymptotically negligible for block designs.

\mathcal{R}:=

\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]

If all variances of the unobserved effects are the same, this term reduces to a constant times
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\right|\right|^{2}_{F}
which equals
n
when
\boldsymbol{W}
= CRD (indicating maximal randomness) and equals
n^{2}
when
\boldsymbol{W}
is PB, the nearly deterministic mirrored pair of vectors (this result is found in Section
3.3.3
). Thus, this term is a measure of the degree of randomness of the design. The
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
factor emphasizes or de-emphasizes randomness in certain subjects weighted by the subject-specific variance terms of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}
. For example, in the case of a binary response, subjects with
\mu_{i}\approx 50\%
would have higher values of the variance of their unobserved terms than other subjects resulting in a higher contribution for their degree of randomness in this overall term.

It is helpful during our theoretical exploration to define a shifted and scaled quantity of the tail criterion
\tilde{Q}_{q}:=4n^{2}Q-c_{Z}
which can be written in the following intuitive expression:

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{q}=\mathcal{B}_{1}+c_{q}\times\sqrt{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\kappa_{Z}+4\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}+\mathcal{S}\right)+2\mathcal{R}}.

(22)

The minimization of
Q_{q}
is equivalent to minimization of
\tilde{Q}_{q}
. However, this minimization

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{*}\,|\,q:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{W}}\{\tilde{Q}_{q}\}

i.e., the optimal design for the tail criterion as a function of
q
, is
impossible
to compute as the quantities
c_{Z},c_{q},\kappa_{Z},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}
are unknown. And even if they were to be known, the problem would still be computationally intractable.

We instead focus on an asymptotic analysis of the terms of
\tilde{Q}_{q}
. For all designs,
c_{Z}=\kappa_{Z}=O(n)
as they are sums of subject-specific constants. The terms
\mathcal{B}_{1}
,
\mathcal{B}_{2}
,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{R}
are design-specific and thus under the experimenter’s control.

Our results are organized as follows. We first locate the asymptotic lower bound of
\tilde{Q}_{q}
providing a definition of asymptotic optimality in Section
3.3.1
. We then asymptotically analyze select designs beginning with i/BCRD (representing “pure” randomness) in Section
3.3.2
and PB (representing “pure” deterministic optimization) in Section
3.3.3
. These two designs were found to be optimal for the worst case MSE criterion (Section
3.1
) and the mean MSE criterion (Section
3.2
) respectively but we show neither are optimal when gauged by the tail criterion
\tilde{Q}_{q}
. We then prove that the optimal tail design must lie between these two extremes of pure randomness and pure optimization in Section
3.3.4
. We then prove in Section
3.3.5
that blocking design with number of blocks on the order between
n^{1/4}
and
n
achieves the lower bound and hence is an asymptotically optimal design.

3.3.1
An Asymptotic Lower Bound for the Tail Criterion for Any Design

To prove the lower bound, we first assume the following standard three boundedness conditions of the moments of the
Z_{i}
’s. Recall that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}^{3}\right]/r+\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}^{3}\right]/\tilde{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}:=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}\right]/r+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}\right]/\tilde{r}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}
denote the vector of the diagonal entries in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
.

Assumption 3.1
.

The entries of
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
are bounded above by
\bar{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and below by
\underline{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
for some positive constants
\underline{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\bar{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
\frac{1}{2n}\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\|^{2}\to\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
for some positive constant
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
.

Assumption 3.2
.

\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}\|^{2}\leq C_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
for some positive constant
C_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

Assumption 3.3
.

\frac{1}{2n}\kappa_{Z}\to\bar{\kappa}_{Z}
for some constant
\bar{\kappa}_{Z}
.

In Section
A.8
of the Supplementary Material, we prove the following result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}_{q}\geq c_{q}r\tilde{r}\sqrt{2(\bar{\kappa}_{Z}+2\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}})}.

(23)

To get some intuition about the lower bound of Equation
23
, notice that for i/BCRD,
\frac{\mathcal{R}}{n}\to 2r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\bar{\rho}
. Thus, a design attains the lower bound of Equation
23
, if it is asymptotically as random as i/BCRD, and the balance terms
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
(as well as
\mathcal{S})
are asymptotically negligible. In Theorem
3.6
below we show that block designs with a few number of blocks attains the lower bound under some additional conditions.

3.3.2
The Tail Criterion under i/BCRD

By the arguments in Section
A.10
of the Supplementary Material,
\mathcal{B}_{1}=O(n)
,
\mathcal{R}=O(n)
(Equation
40
),
\mathcal{B}_{2}=O(n)
(Equation
38
) and
|\mathcal{S}|=O(n)
(Equation
39
) where the first two results are also found in Section 2.2.7 of our previous work for the homoscedastic case. It follows that under i/BCRD the dominant term in
\tilde{Q}
(Equation
22
) is
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, which is
O(n)
.

3.3.3
The Tail Criterion under PB

In the case of equal allocation, the
PB
design is composed of a single
\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
and its mirror
-\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
that both uniquely minimize
|(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}|
. Here,
\mathcal{B}_{1}=O(n^{3}2^{-2n})
as shown in
Kallus, 2018
and
\mathcal{R}=O(n^{2})
as shown in Section
A.9
of the Supplementary Material. Furthermore,
\mathcal{B}_{2}
is of the same order of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
(Equation
38
) and
|\mathcal{S}|\leq\sqrt{O(n)\mathcal{B}_{1}}
(Equation
39
) as also shown in Section
A.9
of the Supplementary Material. Thus, under PB the dominant term in
\tilde{Q}
(Equation
22
) is
\sqrt{\mathcal{R}}
, which is
O(n)
. Thus, PB has the same asymptotic performance as i/BCRD.

3.3.4
The Asymptotically Optimal Design Harmonizes These Two Extreme Designs

In Section
A.9
of the Supplementary Material, we prove the following result.

Theorem 3.5
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
and under equal allocation, we have that under both i/BCRD and PB,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}_{q}\to\infty

Theorem
3.5
implies that the asymptotically optimal tail criterion design of (Equation
21
), if it exists, must be less imbalanced (and thereby less random) than i/BCRD (i.e. the orders of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
must increase slower than the rate
n
) and more imbalanced (and thereby more random) than PB (i.e. the order of
\mathcal{R}
must increase slower than the rate
n^{2}
).

3.3.5
The Asymptotically Optimal Block Design

We now study the quantile MSE criterion under the set of block designs with number of blocks
B
similar to the previous two sections. We aim to locate the optimal
B
as a function of
n
that minimizes (asymptotically) the quantile criterion.

To prove our results we make two assumptions about blocking designs and provide intuition that motivates these assumptions.

Assumption 3.4
.

\mathcal{B}_{1}\approx n/B^{2}
and if
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to 1
then
\mathcal{B}_{1}\to C_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
for some constant
C_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
.

The notation
a_{n}\approx b_{n}
denotes that the limit of
a_{n}/b_{n}
is finite and positive. Assumption
3.4
connects the number of blocks in the design and the balance term
\mathcal{B}_{1}
. As shown below, it holds if the vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is approximately equally spaced, meaning that
\mu_{j}\approx M/B
(recall that we assumed in Equation
17
that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is ordered). We discuss below a relaxation of this assumption.
Assumption
3.4
is motivated by noticing that

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}(\mu_{b,i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{b})^{2}\approx\frac{n}{B^{3}}

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}
is the sub-vector of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
that corresponds to the
b
-th block is reasonable. This is because for
b=1
(and similarly for the other block sizes)
\mu_{1,1},\ldots,\mu_{1,n_{B}}
are in
[0,\mu_{n_{B}}]
and
\mu_{n_{B}}\approx M/B
if the
\mu
’s are ordered approximately uniformly in
[0,M]
. Therefore, the sample variance satisfies

\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}(\mu_{1,i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1})^{2}\approx\frac{1}{B^{2}},

and since
n_{B}=2n/B
, Equation
24
follows. Equation
24
implies Assumption
3.4
because under block designs we have that

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}=r\tilde{r}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b},

where
\boldsymbol{B}=\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{B}}-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}
. We have that
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
, therefore,

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}-\boldsymbol{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{b})^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}-\boldsymbol{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{b})=\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}(\mu_{b,i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{b})^{2}\approx\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\frac{n}{B^{3}},

where the last approximation comes from Equation
24
. Hence,

\mathcal{B}_{1}=r\tilde{r}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}\approx B\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\frac{n}{B^{3}}\approx\frac{n}{B^{2}},

where the last equality is true because
1\leq\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\leq 2
.

Assumption 3.5
.

\frac{B\boldsymbol{\rho}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\rho}}{n^{2}}\to 0
.

When
B
is of smaller order than
n
, then Assumption
3.5
holds true because

\frac{B\boldsymbol{\rho}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\rho}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{B\lambda_{max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W})\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}=\frac{Br\tilde{r}\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{2r\tilde{r}B\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\to 0.

Thus, Assumption
3.5
is only needed to analyze the case where
B/n\to\alpha\in(0,1]
. In this case, the
\mu
’s are balanced by the design in the sense that
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}\approx 1/n
. If the second moments
\boldsymbol{\rho}
are correlated with the first moments
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, then it is expected that
\rho^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\rho
will be small. In fact it is enough to require that
\rho^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\rho=o(n)
for Assumption
3.5
to hold. In the homoskedastic case, Assumption
3.5
is always true because then
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{1}
and thus
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\rho}=0
. The main result is now given and is proven in Section
A.10
of the Supplementary Material.

Theorem 3.6
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.5
,

(i)

If
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to 0
, then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}_{q}\to\infty
.

(ii)

If
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to 1
, then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}_{q}\to C_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}+cr\tilde{r}\sqrt{2(\bar{\kappa}_{Z}+2\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}})}
.

(iii)

If
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to\infty
and
\frac{B}{n}\to 0
, then
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}_{q}\to cr\tilde{r}\sqrt{2(\bar{\kappa}_{Z}+2\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}})}
.

(iv)

If
\frac{B}{n}\to\alpha
, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}_{q}\to cr\tilde{r}\sqrt{2\left(\bar{\kappa}_{Z}+2\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\frac{1}{1-\alpha/2}\right)}
.

Theorem
3.6
(iii) implies that block designs with
B/n^{1/4}\to\infty
and
B/n\to 0
are asymptotically optimal for the tail criterion i.e. it obtains the lower bound of Equation
23
.

Assumption
3.4
is reasonable when the
\mu_{i}
’s are ordered as in Equation
17
and are approximately uniform. If this is not the case, it is expected that
\mathcal{B}_{1}
decreases slower with
B
, and then the optimal block size might be of larger order than
n^{1/4}
. For example, if
\mathcal{B}_{1}\approx n/B
(instead of
n/B^{2}
), then similar arguments used to prove Theorem
3.6
will instead imply that the optimal block size
B
satisfies
B/n^{1/2}\to\infty
and
B/n\to 0
.

We discuss now violations of our assumed knowledge of the order of the
\mu_{i}
’s (Equation
17
). The results above imply if the
\mu_{i}
’s are neither ordered nor approximately uniform, in order for block designs to perform optimally, it is enough to require that there exists a block design with
B/n\to 0
such that its balance term
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is
o(\sqrt{n})
as summarized in Remark
3.1
below.

Remark 3.1
.

Assumptions
3.4
and
3.5
, can be relaxed in the sense, that under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
, if there exists a blocking design with
B/n\to 0
such that its balance term
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is
o(\sqrt{n})
, then it asymptotically achieves the lower bound of Equation
23
and hence it is asymptotically optimal.

To interpret the condition that
\mathcal{B}_{1}=o(\sqrt{n})
, notice that

\frac{1}{2n}\mathcal{B}_{1}=r\tilde{r}\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}(\mu_{b,i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{b})^{2};

the latter expression is the average variance within blocks multiplied by the constant
r\tilde{r}
. Thus, the condition
\mathcal{B}_{1}=o(\sqrt{n})
means that the average variance within blocks is
o(1/\sqrt{n})
. For example, when
B=O(n^{3/4})
then under Assumption
3.4
, we have that the average variance within blocks is
O(1/n^{3/2})
and the requirement in Remark
3.1
is weaker, namely that it is
o(1/\sqrt{n})
.

In summary, optimizing for the tail criterion can be accomplished by using a block design with only a few blocks relative to the sample size.

5
Discussion

Randomized experimentation comes with a choice to the experimenter: how to randomize exactly? Should this choice differ depending on the experimental response metric? One extreme position is full randomization independent of subjects’ covariate imbalance across arms. Here, there can be high observed covariate imbalance among the arms translating to a highly visible risk of bias. However, we prove herein that if the experimenter is concerned with the worst possible configuration of unobserved covariates, complete randomization is the optimal design for all common experimental response types. Another extreme is to find one perfect assignment that minimizes covariate imbalance across arms. However, we prove herein that
only
if one assumes we can average over the unmeasured covariates, this deterministic allocation would be the optimal design for all response types under equal allocation. But this is still unrealistic in practice; that optimal allocation would be impossible to locate as for the vast majority of covariate-response models, knowing the covariate parameters and functional form is required. Even if the parameters and functional form were to be known, the problem would be NP-hard and thus can only be approximated.

Other commonly employed designs are in the “middle” of these two extremes; they sacrifice randomness for imbalance reduction. How to harmonize these two considerations to rigorously choose a design was the subject of our previous work which exclusively considered the continuous response and equal treatment allocation setting. Therein, we proposed the “quantile tail criterion”, a metric on designs that reveals how the degree of randomness and degree of imbalance reduction trade off with one another within a design.

Herein, we derive the quantile tail criterion for general response types. We then prove that the asymptotically optimal design is achieved with a block design with very few blocks — on the order of between
n^{1/4}
and
n
as seen in case (iii) of Theorem
3.6
. Blocking with few blocks is an intuitive imbalance-reducing idea proposed by Fisher nearly 100 years ago and is one of the most popular designs employed today in clinical trials. This work vindicates this common practice for all common response types by analyzing its performance from a new angle. We demonstrate that blocking is the optimal design harmonizing the degree of randomness and imbalance reduction. Although this work focuses on estimation, these blocking designs should have high power as well as many allocations can be generated which are highly independent
(
Krieger et al., 2020
)
.

There are many other avenues to explore. First, although we proved that blocking with few blocks are asymptotically optimal, we did not prove it is uniquely optimal. Other designs may also satisfy the lower bound of Equation
23
and offer better finite sample properties. Other optimal designs are additionally of profound importance because blocking suffers from two main practical problems (1) the curse of dimensionality as it can only handle
p\leq 3
in practice and (2) the design requires arbitrary cut-points if the covariate is continuous. Further, our definition of the blocking design has strong assumptions (Assumptions
3.4
and
3.5
) that are needed to prove its asymptotic optimality. These assumptions appear to be ostensibly realistic as demonstrated by the simulations. However, we can explore proving the results under weaker assumptions. Another area of exploration could be understanding the tail criterion for other designs such as rerandomization and greedy pair switching. Additionally, survival is an important experimental endpoint in clinical trials but this work only considered uncensored survival measurement while the vast majority of survivals collected in real-world experiments are censored. Also, we did not investigate the distribution of the
\mu_{i}
’s relative to the optimal block size and we believe this can be a source of fruitful future work. In our previous work (Section 2.3), we explored the ordinary least squares estimator. This avenue would be difficult to explore for general response types as the analogous estimators do not have closed form (e.g. if the response is binary, the multivariate logistic regression estimates are the result of computational iteration). It is our intuition the theorems found herein would extend to these commonly used covariate-controlled estimators, but more work is necessary. Further, our tail criterion is only one metric that combines mean and standard deviation of MSE. Other criterions can be explored e.g. a tail simultaneous in allocation and unmeasured covariates, the ratio of mean to standard deviation (the coefficient of variation), etc. Also, work can be done to find the optimal imbalance design under unequal allocation, the analogue of PB in equal allocation (see discussion below Equation
16
). Finally, in the case of unequal allocation, we can develop a PM analogue by performing
(n_{C}/n_{T}):1
matching. If
n_{C}/n_{T}
is not a natural number, some matches will have
\lfloor n_{C}/n_{T}\rfloor
control subjects for one treatment subject and some matches can have
\lceil n_{C}/n_{T}\rceil
control subjects for one treatment subject (in approximately the correct proportion). An algorithm to create these tuplets based on intra-subject observed covariate distance is only known to be able to be solved in polynomial time when each matched set has one treated and one control. Clearly, this more general
(n_{C}/n_{T}):1
matching problem is more complex and might very well be NP-hard.

The maximum of the quadratic form of Equation

\lambda_{\text{max}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W})\left|\left|\boldsymbol{v}\right|\right|^{2}

For two subjects

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W_{i}=+1,W_{j}=+1\right)

\displaystyle\frac{n_{T}n_{C}}{2n(2n-1)}

\displaystyle\frac{n_{C}(n_{C}-1)}{2n(2n-1)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\frac{n_{T}}{2n}+\frac{n_{C}}{2n}=\frac{r+\tilde{r}}{2}=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[W_{i}\right]

\displaystyle 1-b=\frac{n_{T}n_{C}}{n^{2}}=r\tilde{r}

).
The key idea is to show that for block designs

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{M}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{v}=r\tilde{r}M^{2}\frac{Bn_{B}^{2}}{4(n_{B}-1)}=r\tilde{r}M^{2}\frac{n^{2}}{2n-B},

(26)

where the last equality follows from the relation
n_{B}=2n/B
. Since
\frac{n^{2}}{2n-B}
is monotone in
B
, it is minimized when
B
is minimal, i.e., when
B=1
, which corresponds to i/BCRD.

In order to show Equation
26
, let
{\boldsymbol{B}}
be the matrix
\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{B}}-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}
where
\boldsymbol{J}
denotes the matrix of all ones. Recall that under
BL(B)
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
is block diagonal
B
blocks of the form
(1-b){\boldsymbol{B}}
; see Appendix
A.3
. Therefore, for block designs

\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{v}=r\tilde{r}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}_{j},

where the
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
’s are sub-vectors of
\boldsymbol{v}
. In order to maximize the quadratic form, one can maximize each term
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
separately. By Lemma
A.0.1
, the maximizer of each term contains

n_{b}/2
entries with
M
, and
n_{b}/2
entries with 0 (assuming
n_{B}
is even). Equation
27
within the lemma implies Equation
26
, completing the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Lemma A.0.1
.

The vector that maximizes the quadratic form
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n_{B}}
such that
\min(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n_{B}})\geq 0
and
\max(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n_{B}})\leq M
when
n_{B}
is even, has
n_{B}/2
entries with
M
, and
n_{B}/2
entries with 0.

Proof.

First, since
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is a convex function in
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, every entry of the maximizer must contain either 0 or
M
.
Also, by the definition of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is invariant under permutations of the entries of the vector
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. Thus, it is enough to consider vectors of the form
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}
, which is defined to be a vector whose first
k
entries are
M
and the rest are zero. We have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}^{\top}\left(\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{B}}-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}\right){\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}=\frac{M^{2}}{n_{B}-1}(n_{B}k-k^{2}).

(27)

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}_{k+1}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}=\frac{M^{2}}{n_{B}-1}[n_{B}-(2k-1)].

It follows that if
k<n_{B}/2
, then
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}_{k+1}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}
is non-negative, and if
k>n_{B}/2
, the difference is non-positive. Therefore, the maximizer is when
k=n_{B}/2
.
∎

A.5
Expectation of the MSE

For this section and following sections, let
\boldsymbol{Z}:=\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}/r+\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}/\tilde{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}/r+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}/\tilde{r}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{T},\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}\left[\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}+\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}}{n_{T}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}+\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}{n_{C}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}+\boldsymbol{Z}_{T}}{n_{T}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}+\boldsymbol{Z}_{C}}{n_{C}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{Z}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{Z}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+2\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}+\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]\right)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
is the diagonal variance-covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{Z}
. To compute the trace, we need to consider only the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
. The
i
th diagonal entry of this product is
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}}_{i\,\cdot}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}}_{\cdot\,i}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}}_{i\,i}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}}_{i\,i}
due to
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
being diagonal. From Equation
A.3
, we know that all the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
are
r\tilde{r}
and thus

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]=r\tilde{r}\,\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right].

(28)

Upon substitution, the result is

\displaystyle\footnotesize\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}}{n_{T}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}}{n_{C}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}}{n_{T}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}}{n_{C}}\right)+\underbrace{\frac{1}{4n^{2}}r\tilde{r}\,\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}}{r}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}}{\tilde{r}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{T}}{r}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}}{\tilde{r}}\right)+c_{Z}.

where the underbraced term is design-independent and denoted as
c_{Z}
.

A.6
Minimal block-size design is optimal for the mean criterion

Since
k
is the minimal possible block-size, it follows that
m
is divisible by
k
. Also, since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
is block-diagonal under block-designs, it it enough to consider sub-vectors of size
m
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})^{\top}
where
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{m}
and define
m_{k}=m/k
. Let
\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}
denote the sub-vector of the
j
-th block, and let
SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j})=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}-\boldsymbol{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\|^{2}
.
We need to show that

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{BL(m_{k})}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}=(1-b)\sum_{j=1}^{m_{k}}\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}^{\top}\left(\frac{k}{k-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{k}-\frac{1}{k-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{k}\right)\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}=(1-b)\frac{k}{k-1}SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}),

is smaller than

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{BL(1)}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}=(1-b)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}^{\top}\left(\frac{m}{m-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{m}-\frac{1}{m-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{m}\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}=(1-b)\frac{m}{m-1}SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}).

That is, we need to show that

\frac{m}{m-1}SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})-\frac{k}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{k}}SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j})\geq 0.

(29)

We prove Inequality
29
by induction on
m_{k}
using Lemma
A.0.2
below. If
m_{k}=2
, Inequality
29
follows
immediately from Lemma
A.0.2
.

Assume that Inequality
29
is true for
m_{k}=B
. Consider
m_{k}=B+1
. Assume
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}
is an ordered vector of length
m=k(B+1)
. By the induction assumption,

\displaystyle\frac{k}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{B}SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j})\leq\frac{kB}{kB-1}SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{B}).

We now apply Lemma
A.0.2
to the
m=k(B+1)
elements by considering
\ell=Bk
. This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{kB}{kB-1}SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{B})+\frac{k}{k-1}SSE(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{B+1})\leq\frac{m}{m-1}SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}).

Inequality
29
follows immediately by combining these two inequalities.

Lemma A.0.2
.

For a vector
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
of length
l
define
f({\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\frac{l}{l-1}SSE({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
. Consider a division of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}

into two sub-vectors
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1},\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}
of size
\ell
and
m-\ell
, respectively, i.e.,
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\ell})
and
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}=(\mu_{\ell+1},\ldots,\mu_{m})
. Then,

f(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})-f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1})-f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})\geq 0.

(30)

Proof.

Since we can subtract from the entries
\mu_{i}
a constant without changing the values of
f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1})
,
f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})
and
f(\boldsymbol{\mu})
, we can assume without loss of generality that the entries in
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}
are non-positive and the entries in
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}
, non-negative.

Define a vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
of length
m
as follows

\tilde{\mu}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&i=\ell+1\\
\mu_{m}+\mu_{\ell+1}&i=m\\
\mu_{i}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

and let
\underline{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{1}
and
\underline{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{2}
be the corresponding sub-vectors. Notice that
\underline{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{1}=\underline{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{1}
and
\bar{\underline{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}_{2}=\bar{\underline{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}_{2}
and
\bar{{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}=\bar{{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}
. A trivial calculation shows that

SSE(\underline{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{2})-SSE(\underline{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{2})=SSE(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}})-SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})=2\mu_{m}\mu_{\ell+1}>0.

Thus, when replacing
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}
with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
,
SSE({{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{2})
goes up by the same amount as
SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})
.
But, since
\frac{m-\ell}{m-\ell-1}>\frac{m}{m-1}
,

f(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})-f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})>f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}})-f(\underline{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{2}).

It follows that replacing
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}
with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
reduces the right hand-side of Inequality
30
and therefore we can assume that
\mu_{\ell+1}=0
.
The same argument shows that we can also assume that
\mu_{\ell+2}=0
. Continuing in the same manner implies that we can consider
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}=(0,\ldots,0,c)
for
c\geq 0
as the worst case. A symmetric argument shows that
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}=(a,0,\ldots,0)
were
a\leq 0
is the worst case for
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}
.

It follows that it is enough to prove Inequality
30
for
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}=(a,0,\ldots,0,c)
where
a\leq 0
and
c\geq 0
. First consider
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}
. Since the average of the
m-\ell
elements in
\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}
is
\frac{c}{m-\ell}
, that implies

\displaystyle SSE(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m-\ell-1)\frac{c^{2}}{(m-\ell)^{2}}+\left(c-\frac{c}{m-\ell}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{(m-\ell)^{2}}(m-\ell-1+(m-\ell-1)^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{2}\frac{m-\ell-1}{m-\ell}.

Therefore,
f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})=c^{2}
. A parallel argument shows that
f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1})=a^{2}
.
We now need
SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})
.
We have that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{m}=\frac{a+c}{m}
and hence

\displaystyle SSE(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(a-\frac{a+c}{m}\right)^{2}+(m-2)\left(\frac{a+c}{m}\right)^{2}+\left(c-\frac{a+c}{m}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}+c^{2}-m\left(\frac{a+c}{m}\right)^{2}=a^{2}+c^{2}-\frac{a^{2}+c^{2}}{m}-\frac{2ac}{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m-1}{m}(a^{2}+c^{2})-\frac{2ac}{m}\geq\frac{m-1}{m}(a^{2}+c^{2}).

The last inequality follows because
c\geq 0
and
a\leq 0
. Therefore,
f(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m})\geq f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1})+f(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})
and Inequality
30
follows.
∎

A.7
Variance of the MSE

We compute
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]
below.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\frac{1}{4n^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{Z})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{Z})\right]

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16n^{4}}\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+2\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}+\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16n^{4}}\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[2\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}+\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16n^{4}}\left(4\,\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right]+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]+4\,\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z},\,\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16n^{4}}\left(4(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]+4\,\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z},\,\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16n^{4}}\left(4\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z},\,\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]\right)+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]\right)

The covariance term is the expectation of the product of the two inputs minus the product of the expectations of the two inputs. Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=\boldsymbol{0}_{2n}
, the product of the expectations becomes zero. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z},\,\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]

The
k
th element of the expectation of the cubic form above looks like

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[Z_{k}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[Z_{k}\sum_{i,j}Z_{i}Z_{j}\sigma_{i\,,k}\right]

where
\sigma_{i\,,j}
denotes the
i,j
th entry of the
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
matrix. Due to the independence of the
Z_{i}
’s the terms in the above sum are zero except when
i=j=k
which results in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[Z_{k}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\sigma_{k\,,k}Z_{k}^{3}\right].

Since the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
are identically
r\tilde{r}
(see Equation
A.3
), then the vector whose
k
th entry we were looking at is merely

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=r\tilde{r}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}
is the vector whose elements are the third moments of
\boldsymbol{Z}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z},\,\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=r\tilde{r}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}.

(32)

We now focus on the remaining variance term in the overall variance which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z})^{2}\right]-(\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right])^{2}

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z})^{2}\right]-r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]^{2}

where the last equality follows from Equation
28
. We now compute
\mathbb{E}_{\,\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z})^{2}\right]
, the quartic form in the above expression. We define
\rho_{i}:=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{2}\right]
which is also the
i,i
th entry of the
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
matrix. The quartic form can be expanded in scalar form as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z})^{2}\right]=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\left\{1,\ldots,2n\right\}}\sigma_{i\,,j}\sigma_{k\,,\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{\ell}\right]

(34)

where the expectation operator is taken over the relevant
Z_{i}
’s. Since
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}\right]=0
for all subjects, the expectation above simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{\ell}\right]=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]\quad\text{if}\quad i=j=k=\ell\\
\rho_{i}\rho_{k}~~~\quad\text{if}\quad i=j\neq k=\ell\\
\rho_{i}\rho_{j}~~~\quad\text{if}\quad i=k\neq j=\ell~~\text{or}~~i=\ell\neq j=k\\
0~~~~~~~\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Using the above result, we write the quartic expansion of Equation
34
as

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i\,,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]+\sum_{i=j\neq k=\ell}\sigma_{i\,,i}\sigma_{k\,,k}\rho_{i}\rho_{k}+\sum_{i=k\neq j=\ell}\sigma_{i\,,j}\sigma_{i\,,j}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}+\sum_{i=\ell\neq j=k}\sigma_{i\,,j}\sigma_{j\,,i}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}.

Given that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}
is symmetric with a diagonal of entries that are identically
r\tilde{r}
(see Equation
A.3
), the above simplifies to

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]+r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\sum_{i=j\neq k=\ell}\rho_{i}\rho_{k}+\sum_{i=k\neq j=\ell}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}+\sum_{i=\ell\neq j=k}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]+\sum_{i\neq j}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\right)+2\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}.

Consider the following matrix and its entries:

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})_{i,j}=\sigma_{i\,,j}\rho_{j}

We now square this matrix and examine only the result’s diagonal entries

\displaystyle[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}]_{i,i}=\left[\sigma_{i\,,1}\rho_{1}\ldots\sigma_{i\,,2n}\rho_{2n}\right]\left[\sigma_{1\,,j}\rho_{j}\ldots\sigma_{2n\,,j}\rho_{j}\right]^{\top}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\sigma_{i\,,j}\rho_{j})(\sigma_{j\,,i}\rho_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{j}\rho_{i}

Thus,

\displaystyle\footnotesize 2\,\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{j}\rho_{i}=2\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{j}\rho_{i}+2\sum_{i=j}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{j}\rho_{i}=2\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i\,,j}^{2}\rho_{j}\rho_{i}+2r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}^{2}

(36)

Since the above shares the first term with the simplified quartic form of Equation
35
, we now write the quartic form as a function of this trace,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z})^{2}\right]=r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]+\sum_{i\neq j}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}^{2}\right)+2\,\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right].

We now substitute this into the variance expression of Equation
33
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]=r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]+\sum_{i\neq j}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}^{2}-\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]^{2}\right)+2\,\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right].

Substituting the expansion of
\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]^{2}=\sum_{i\neq j}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}^{2}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]-3\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}^{2}\right)+2\,\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\kappa_{Z}+2\,\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right].

The terms
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}^{4}\right]-3\rho_{i}^{2}
are a constant with respect to the design and we denote the sum of these terms by
\kappa_{Z}
. We chose this notation as the quantity expressed by the sum of these terms is similar to the sum of the excess kurtosis of the
Z_{i}
’s.

Substituting the two intermediate results of Equations
32
and
37
into the overall variance expression of Equation
31
, we arrive at the final expression

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[\mathbb{M}\text{SE}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\left[\hat{\tau}\right]\right]=\frac{1}{16n^{4}}\left(4\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}+r\tilde{r}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}\right)+r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\kappa_{Z}+2\,\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]\right).

A.8
An asymptotic lower bound for the tail criterion (Proof of Theorem
3.4
)

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}
does not converge, then the following argument holds for any convergent subsequence of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

Recall the definition of
\tilde{Q}
in Equation
22
. If
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is of order
n
, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}=\infty
and the lower bound (Equation
23
) clearly holds. So, we now assume that
\mathcal{B}_{1}=o(n)
. In this case, by Equation
39
below,
|\mathcal{S}|=o(n)
and since
\mathcal{R}
is at-least of order
n
(see below), then
\mathcal{S}
is negligible. The terms
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are positive. Therefore,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}+c\times\sqrt{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\kappa_{Z}+4\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}+\mathcal{S}\right)+2\mathcal{R}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}c\times\sqrt{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\kappa_{Z}/n+2\mathcal{R}/n}.

Now, by Assumption
3.3
,
\kappa_{Z}/n\to 2\bar{\kappa}_{Z}
, and by Equation
36

\frac{\mathcal{R}}{n}=\frac{\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]}{n}\geq\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\rho_{i}^{2}}{n}=r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}2\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}\to 2r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}},

where the last equality is due to Assumption
3.1
. This completed the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

A.9
Optimal Design is Harmony (Proof of Theorem
3.5
)

The arguments of Sections
3.3.3
and
3.3.2
show that under both PB and i/BCRD,
\tilde{Q}
(Equation
22
) is of order
n
and therefore,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{Q}\to\infty
.

The only thing that is left to show is that under PB and under equal allocation,
\mathcal{R}
is of order
n^{2}
. Since PB is composed from a single vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
and its mirror
-\boldsymbol{w}_{*}
, we have that under PB,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}=\boldsymbol{w}_{*}\boldsymbol{w}_{*}^{\top}
.
Recall that
\boldsymbol{\rho}
denotes the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}
and let
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{*}}
denote the element-wise product of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and
{\boldsymbol{w}_{*}}
.
Therefore, under PB,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]=\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{w}_{*}\boldsymbol{w}_{*}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\boldsymbol{w}_{*}\boldsymbol{w}_{*}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\right]=\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{w}_{*}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{*}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{*}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{*}}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{*}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{*})^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{w}_{*,i}^{2}\rho_{i}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\right)^{2},

which is of order
n^{2}
by Assumption
3.1
.

A.10
Asymptotics of the tail criterion (Proof of Theorem
3.6
)

We proceed by bounding
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}
and then computing term
\mathcal{R}
. By Assumption
3.1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq\bar{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}=\bar{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}r\tilde{r}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b}\|^{2}=\bar{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}r\tilde{r}\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\mathcal{B}_{1}\leq 2\bar{C}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}r\tilde{r}\mathcal{B}_{1}.

(38)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}|\leq\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\mu}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}}

(39)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathcal{B}_{1}\lambda_{max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W})}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{Z}\|\leq 2r\tilde{r}\sqrt{C_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}n\mathcal{B}_{1}},

where the last inequality is true because for block designs
\lambda_{max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W})=r\tilde{r}\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\leq 2r\tilde{r}
and due to Assumption
3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2}
be the vector whose entries is the entries of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}
squared. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z})^{2}\right]=r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\text{tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{B}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\text{tr}\left[(n_{B}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b})-\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left\{n_{B}^{2}\text{tr}\left[{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2})\right]-2n_{B}\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2})\right]+\text{tr}\left[[\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b})]^{2}\right]\right\}.

We now compute the above traces:

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2};

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}^{T}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2})\right]=\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{2})^{T}\right]=\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2};

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[[\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b})]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}^{T}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b})\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}^{T}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\right]=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\right]=(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}})^{2}.

Putting all terms together we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left\{n^{2}_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2}-2n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2}+(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left\{n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2}(n_{B}-1)+(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}})^{2}-n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}}{n_{B}-1}+\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left\{(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}})^{2}-n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2}\right\}.

Now notice that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\left(\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{B}}-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{n_{B}}\right)\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}=\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\left(n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}\|^{2}-(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{B}})^{2}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\frac{n_{B}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}-\sum_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}}{n_{B}-1}

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\frac{4n^{2}\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}-B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{b}}{2n-B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}4n^{2}\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}-r\tilde{r}B\boldsymbol{\rho}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}\boldsymbol{\rho}}{2n-B}.

We now consider four regimes for
B
which become the four results of Theorem
3.6
.

(i)
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to 0

By Assumption
3.4
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\tilde{Q}}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathcal{B}_{1}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}n/B^{2}\to\infty.

(ii)
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to 1

By Assumption
3.4
that in this case
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathcal{B}_{1}\to C_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
. By Assumption
3.3
,
\frac{\kappa_{Z}}{n}\to 2\bar{\kappa}_{Z}
. The bounds for terms
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}
(Equations
38
and
39
) imply that
\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2}}{n}}\to 0
and
\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{S}}{n}}\to 0
and hence those terms are negligible. For term
\mathcal{R}
, by Equation
40
and Assumptions
3.1
and
3.5
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{R}}{n}=r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\frac{4n\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}}{2n-B}+o(1)\to 2r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}.

In conclusion, we have that under this regime

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}{\tilde{Q}}\to C_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}+cr\tilde{r}\sqrt{2(\bar{\kappa}_{Z}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}})}.

(iii)
\frac{B}{n^{1/4}}\to\infty
and
B/n\to\infty

We have by Assumption
3.4
that in this case
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathcal{B}_{1}\to 0
and similarly to before terms
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}
are negligible. Also, as in the previous case
\frac{\mathcal{R}}{n}\to 2r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
.

(iv)
B/n\to\alpha

As in the previous case, terms
\mathcal{B}_{1}
,
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}
are negligible and for term
\mathcal{R}
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{R}}{n}=r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\frac{2\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}}{1-B/(2n)}+o(1)\to 2r^{2}\tilde{r}^{2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\frac{1}{1-\alpha/2}.
