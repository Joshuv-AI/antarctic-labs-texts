Abstract

1.
Introduction

The essential dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
of the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{n}
is the smallest integer
d
such that
the general polynomial
x^{n}+a_{1}x^{n-1}+\ldots+a_{n}
can be reduced to
a
d
-parameter form by a Tschirnhaus transformation. The geometric definition of essential dimension and some background material
can be found in Section
2
; for a comprehensive overview, see
[
7
,
9
]
.

Finding
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
is a long-standing open problem, which goes back to F. Klein
[
6
]
;
cf. also N. Chebotarev
[
11
]
.
Essential dimension of a finite group was formally defined by J. Buhler and the second authors
in
[
4
]
, where the inequalities

(1.1)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\geqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor\quad\text{($n\geqslant 5$)}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant n-3

were proved.
The field
k
was assumed to be of characteristic
0
in
[
4
]
, but the proof of the first inequality
in (
1.1
) given there goes through for any field
k
of characteristic different from
2
.
The second inequality is valid over an arbitrary field
k
.
A. Duncan
[
5
]
subsequently showed that in characteristic
0
,

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\geqslant\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor
for any
n\geqslant 7
.
The exact value of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
is open for every
n\geqslant 8
and every field
k
, though
it is widely believed that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
should be
n-3
for every
n\geqslant 5
,
at least in characteristic
0
.

The purpose of this paper is to show that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
can be
\leqslant n-4
in prime characteristic.
Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k
be a field of odd characteristic
p>0
and
n
is a positive integer whose binary presentation is

n=2^{m_{1}}+2^{m_{2}}+\ldots+2^{m_{r}}
, where
m_{1}>m_{2}>\ldots>m_{r}\geqslant 0
. Assume that
p
divides
n
and

r\geqslant 4
. If
r=4
, assume further that
k
contains
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, a field of
p^{2}
elements.
Then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant n-4
.

Remark 1.2
.

(a) Note that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{5})=2
and
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{6})=3
for any field
k
of characteristic
\neq 2
.
Thus, while it may be possible to weaken the assumption that
r\geqslant 4
in the statement of Theorem
1.1
,
this assumption cannot be dropped entirely.

(b) If
k\subset k^{\prime}
is a field extension, then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k^{\prime}}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
.
In particular, the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
can be rephrased as follows:

\operatorname{ed}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant n-4
if
r\geqslant 5

and
\operatorname{ed}_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant n-4
if
r=4
.

(c) Suppose

is a field of characteristic
0
and
k
is a field of characteristic
p>0
containing an algebraic closure
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Then
\operatorname{ed}_{l}(\operatorname{S}_{n})\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
for any
n\geqslant 5
; see
[
3
, Corollary 3.4(b)]
.

(d) The smallest

with

is
n=8+4+2+1=15
.

The remainder of this paper will be devoted to proving Theorem
1.1
. In Section
2
we will collect the
background material on essential dimension that will be used in the proof. In Section
3
we will introduce the
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-invariant subvariety

. In Section

, the
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-action on

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X_{1,2};\operatorname{S}_{n})=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})
.
Finally, in Section

we will complete the proof
of Theorem
1.1
by showing that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X_{1,2},\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant n-4
.

2.
Preliminaries on essential dimension

Throughout this paper
k
will denote an arbitrary base field,
\overline{k}
will denote an algebraic closure of
k
, and
G
will denote an abstract finite group. Unless otherwise specified, algebraic varieties, morphisms, rational maps, group actions, etc., will be assumed to be defined over
k
. We will refer to a variety
X
with an action of
G
as a
G
-variety.
We will say that the
G
-action on
X
(or equivalently, the
G
-variety
X
) is

•

faithful, if the induced morphism
G\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is injective,

•

primitive, if
G
transitively permutes the irreducible components of
X(\overline{k})
,

•

generically free, if there exists a dense open subset
U\subset X
such that for every
\overline{k}
-point
u\in U
, the stabilizer

\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(u)
of
u
in
G
is trivial.

A generically free action is clearly faithful. The converse holds if
X
is irreducible, but not in general (not even if the
G
-action on
X
is primitive).

Let
X
be a generically free primitive
G
-variety. We will refer to a
G
-equivariant dominant rational map
X\dasharrow Y
as a
G
-
compression
, if the
G
-action on
Y
is also generically free. The minimal dimension
of
Y
, taken over all
G
-compressions
X\dasharrow Y
is called the
essential dimension
of
X
and is denoted by
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X;G)
.
The largest value of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X;G)
, as
X
ranges over all generically free primitive
G
-varieties defined over
k
, is called the essential dimension of
G
over
k
and is denoted by
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(G)
.

We now recall two results about essential dimension that will be needed in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Note that Proposition
2.1
shows, in particular, that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(G)<\infty
for any
G
and
k
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G\hookrightarrow\operatorname{GL}(V)
be a faithful finite-dimensional representation of

G
. Denote the underlying affine space by
\mathbb{A}(V)
. Then

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(G)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathbb{A}(V);G)
.

Proof.

See
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
or
[
1
, Proposition 4.11]
or
[
7
, Propositions 3.1 and 3.11]
.
∎

Following
[
3
]
, we will say that a finite group
G
is weakly tame over a field
k
of characteristic
p\geqslant 0
if

G
has no normal
p
-subgroups, other than
\{1\}
. If
p=0
(or if
p
does not divide
|G|
),
then
G
is always weakly tame over
k
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that a finite group
G
is weakly tame group over a field
k
.
Let
X
and
Y
be generically free primitive
G
-varieties over
k
. Assume that there exists
a (not necessarily dominant)
G
-equivariant rational map
f\colon Y\dasharrow X^{\rm sm}
, where

X^{\rm sm}
denotes the smooth locus of

. Then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(Y)
.

Proof.

See
[
10
, Theorem 1.6]
.
∎

3.
Preliminaries on the affine quadric
X_{1,2}

Let
X_{1,2}
be the closed
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-invariant subvariety of
\mathbb{A}^{n}
given by

s_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=s_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0,

where
s_{i}
is the
i
th elementary symmetric polynomial and
\operatorname{S}_{n}
acts on
\mathbb{A}^{n}

by permuting the variables in the natural way. If
\operatorname{char}(k)\neq 2
, then equivalently,

(3.1)

X_{1,2}:=\{\,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}\,|\,x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}=x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}=0\,\}.

Let
\Delta
be the discriminant locus in
\mathbb{A}^{n}
, i.e., the union of the hyperplanes
x_{i}=x_{j}
for various pairs
(i,j)
, where
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
. Note that the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{n}
acts freely (i.e., with trivial stabilizers)
on
\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus\Delta
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
n\geqslant 5
. Then

(a)
X_{1,2}
is absolutely irreducible.

(b)
X_{1,2}\setminus\Delta
is a dense open subset of
X_{1,2}
. In particular, the
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-action on
X_{1,2}
is generically free.

(c) The singular locus of
X_{1,2}
is
X_{1,2}\cap D
, where
D
is the small diagonal
D
given by
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{n}
.

Proof.

By definition
X_{1,2}
is the affine cone over the projective variety

\mathbb{P}(X_{1,2})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
given by

x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}=x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}=0\,.

Parts (a), (b) and (c) of the Lemma now follow from
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
, which shows
that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1,2})
is absolutely irreducible (Lemma 2.1(c)),

\mathbb{P}(X_{1,2})
is not contained in
\mathbb{P}(\Delta)
(Lemma 2.1(f)), and the singular locus of
\mathbb{P}(X_{1,2})
is
\mathbb{P}(X_{1,2})\cap\{(1:1:\ldots:1)\}
(Lemma 2.1(b)). Note in
[
2
]
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{1,2})
is denoted by
Y_{n,2}
and
\Delta
is denoted by
\Delta_{n}
.
∎

4.
Reduction to
X_{1,2}

The purpose of this section is to prove the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume
k
and
n
are as in the statement of Theorem
1.1
.
Then

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X_{1,2};\operatorname{S}_{n})=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n}).

Note that
\operatorname{ed}(X_{1,2};\operatorname{S}_{n})
is well defined because
X_{1,2}
is an absolutely irreducible generically free

\operatorname{S}_{n}
-variety by Lemma
3.1
. Lemma
3.1
applies here because our assumptions
force

to be
\geqslant 15
; see Remark
1.2
(d).

Our proof of Proposition
4.1
will be based on the following.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume
k
and
n
are as in the statement of Theorem
1.1
.
Let
F
be a field containing
k
and
E/F
be an
n
-dimensional étale algebra. Then there exists an
element
\alpha\in E\setminus F
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^{2})=0
. Here
\operatorname{Tr}
denotes the trace function
\operatorname{Tr}_{E/F}\colon E\to F
.

Proof.

Let
E^{\rm{trace0}}
be the kernel of the trace map
\operatorname{Tr}\colon E\to F
. It is an
(n-1)
-dimensional

F
-vector subspace of
E
. Since
n
is divisible by
p=\operatorname{char}(k)=\operatorname{char}(F)
,
F
is contained in
E^{\rm{trace0}}
. Consider the symmetric
bilinear form
\psi\colon E^{\rm{trace0}}\to F
given by
\psi(a,b)=\operatorname{Tr}(ab)
and the associated quadratic form
q\colon E^{\rm{trace0}}\to F
given by

q(a)=\psi(a,a)=\operatorname{Tr}(a^{2})
. By the definition of
E^{\rm{trace0}}
,
\psi(1,a)=\operatorname{Tr}(a)=0
for every
a\in E^{\rm{trace0}}
. Thus
F=F\cdot 1
lies in the radical of
q\colon E^{\rm{trace0}}\to F
. Consequently, the bilinear form
\psi
and the quadratic form
q

descend to a bilinear form
\overline{\psi}
and a quadratic form
\overline{q}
on the
(n-2)
-dimensional quotient space

\overline{E^{\rm{trace0}}}=E^{\rm{trace0}}/(F\cdot 1)
.

Elements
\alpha\in E
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^{2})=0
are precisely the isotropic vectors of
q
in
E^{\rm{trace0}}
.
We are looking for an element
\alpha\in E^{\rm{trace0}}\setminus F
whose image in
\overline{E^{\rm{trace0}}}
is an isotropic vector for
\overline{q}
.
In other words, the lemma is equivalent to the assertion that the quadratic form
\overline{q}\colon\overline{E^{\rm{trace0}}}\to F
is isotropic.

To show that
\overline{q}
is isotropic, we will appeal to Springer’s theorem:
If
F^{\prime}/F
is a field extension of odd degree, then
\overline{q}
is isotropic in
\overline{E^{\rm{trace0}}}
if and only
if it becomes isotropic over
F^{\prime}
.
By
[
2
, Proposition 5.1]
we can choose
F^{\prime}/F
so that
E^{\prime}=E\otimes_{F}F^{\prime}
is an étale algebra
of degree
n
over
F^{\prime}
the form
E^{\prime}=E_{1}\times E_{2}\times\ldots\times E_{r}
, where
E_{i}
is an étale algebra
over
F^{\prime}
of degree
2^{m_{i}}
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
. After replacing
F
by
F^{\prime}
and
E
by
E^{\prime}
, we may assume
without loss of generality that, in fact,

E=E_{1}\times E_{2}\times\ldots\times E_{r},

where
E_{i}
is an
2^{m_{i}}
-dimensional étale algebra over
F
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
. Note that

\operatorname{Tr}_{E/F}(a)=\operatorname{Tr}_{E_{1}/F}(a_{1})+\ldots+\operatorname{Tr}_{E_{r}/F}(a_{r})=0

for any
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in E_{1}\times\ldots\times E_{r}=E
.
In particular, we now have an
r
-dimensional
F
-subalgebra of
E
,

F\times\ldots\times F\;\text{($r$ times)}\;\subset E_{1}\times\ldots\times E_{r}=E,

where the trace form is quite transparent:
\operatorname{Tr}_{E/F}\colon(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\mapsto 2^{m_{1}}c_{1}+\ldots+2^{m_{r}}c_{r}
for any

c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in F
. Specifically, we would like to show that
\overline{q}
has an isotropic vector in
V/F
, where

V=\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\in F\times\ldots\times F\,|\,\operatorname{Tr}_{E/F}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})=0\}.

Equivalently, we would like to show that
q
has an isotropic vector in
V\cap H
, where
H
is a
F
-hyperplane in
F\times\ldots\times F
(
r
times)
which does not contain the unit element
(1,\ldots,1)
. For example, we can take

to be the hyperplane

.
Explicitly, we are looking for a non-trivial solution to the system

(4.1)

\begin{cases}2^{m_{1}}c_{1}+\ldots+2^{m_{r-1}}c_{r-1}=0\\
2^{m_{1}}c_{1}^{2}+\ldots+2^{m_{r-1}}c_{r-1}^{2}=0\end{cases}

Note that all the coefficients in this system are integers.
By a theorem of Chevalley
[
8
, Theorem 5.2.1]
,
every finite field is a

-field, and consequently, the system (
4.1
) of one linear and one quadratic equations
in

variables has a non-trivial solution over

, as long as

. This completes the proof of

for

.

If

, then we may or may not be able to find a non-trivial solution of the system (

) over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, but there will certainly be one over some quadratic extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Since
\mathbb{F}_{p}
has a unique quadratic extension,
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, and we are assuming that

contains a copy of

, we conclude that in this case the system (

)
has an isotropic vector over

and hence, over

. This completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
for
r=4
.
∎

2.1
. On the other hand, by Proposition
2.2
,

(4.4)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathbb{A}^{n};\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X_{1,2};\,\operatorname{S}_{n}).

Proposition
2.2
applies here because
X_{1,2}
is an irreducible generically free
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-variety,

X_{1,2}\setminus D
is smooth (see Lemma
3.1
),
and the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{n}
is weakly tame at any prime. (Once again, here
n\geqslant 15
; see Remark
1.2
(d).)

Combining (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we obtain
the desired equality,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{S}_{n})=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X_{1,2};\operatorname{S}_{n})
.
∎

5.
Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we will complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
by establishing the following.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
k
be a base field of characteristic
p>2
. Assume that
n\geqslant 5
and
p
divides
n
.
Then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(X_{1,2};\operatorname{S}_{n})\leqslant n-4
.

Note that the assumption of Theorem
1.1
that
n
should not be a sum of three or fewer powers of
2
, is not needed here.

Before proceeding with the proof of Proposition
5.1
, we briefly outline our overall strategy.
Our goal is to show the existence of a
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-compression
\pi\colon X_{1,2}\dasharrow Y
defined over
k
,
where
\dim(Y)\leqslant n-4
. Key to our construction is the observation that

X_{1,2}
admits an action of a
2
-dimensional linear algebraic group
B
,
which commutes with the
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-action. This
B
-action is a characteristic
p
phenomenon;
it only exists when
\operatorname{char}(k)
divides
n
. The idea is then to define
\pi
as the quotient map for this action.
The remainder of this section will be devoted to working out the details of this construction.

We begin by introducing the
2
-dimensional algebraic group
B
. It is the group of upper-triangular matrices in
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}
,
i.e., the group of matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.
(Here
B
stands for “Borel subgroup”.) Consider the natural action of
B
on
\mathbb{A}^{n}
by

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\to(\alpha x_{1}+\beta,\ldots,\alpha x_{n}+\beta).

Lemma 5.2
.

(a)
X_{1,2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
is invariant under the action of
B
defined above.

(b) The stabilizer in
B
of a point
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in X_{1,2}\setminus\Delta
is trivial.

Proof.

(a) We need to check that

\operatorname{Tr}(a)=\operatorname{Tr}(a^{2})=0
implies
\operatorname{Tr}(g\cdot a)=\operatorname{Tr}((g\cdot a)^{2})=0
,
for any
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(\overline{k})

and any
g=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in B
. Here

\operatorname{Tr}
denotes the trace in
the split
\overline{k}
-étale algebra
\overline{k}\times\ldots\times\overline{k}
(
n
times), i.e.,

\operatorname{Tr}(a)=a_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}
and

\operatorname{Tr}(a^{2})=a_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{n}^{2}
.

Indeed, assume that
\operatorname{Tr}(a)=\operatorname{Tr}(a^{2})=0
. Since
\operatorname{char}(k)
divides
n
, we have

\operatorname{Tr}(g\cdot a)=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha a_{1}+\beta,\ldots,\alpha a_{n}+\beta)=\alpha\operatorname{Tr}(a)+n\beta=0+0=0

and

\operatorname{Tr}((g\cdot a)^{2})=\operatorname{Tr}((\alpha a_{1}+\beta)^{2},\ldots,(\alpha a_{n}+\beta)^{2})=\alpha^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(a^{2})+2\alpha\beta\operatorname{Tr}(a)+n\beta^{2}=0+0+0=0
,
as desired.

(b) The stabilizer of any point
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(\overline{k})
is the group subscheme of
B
cut out by the equations

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\alpha a_{1}+\beta=a_{1},\\
\ldots\\
\alpha a_{n}+\beta=a_{n}.\end{cases}

Here
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\overline{k}
are fixed, and
\alpha
and
\beta
are coordinate functions on
B
.
Rewriting this system in matrix form, we obtain

(\alpha-1,\,\beta)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\ldots&a_{n}\\
1&1&\ldots&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix}.

If
a\not\in D\subset\Delta
, i.e., at least two of the elements

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of
k
are distinct, then the
2\times n
matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\ldots&a_{n}\\
1&1&\ldots&1\end{pmatrix}

has rank
2
. Hence, the kernel of this matrix is trivial. We conclude that (scheme-theoretic) solution set to the system (
5.1
) consists of a single point,
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,0)
. In other words,
the stabilizer of
a
in
B
is trivial.
∎

We now define the
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-equivariant morphism
\pi\colon(X_{1,2}\setminus\Delta)\to\mathbb{A}^{n(n-1)(n-2)}
by

\pi\colon a=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto\Big(\,\dfrac{x_{r}-x_{s}}{x_{r}-x_{t}}\,\Big)_{(r,s,t)}

where the subscript
(r,s,t)
ranges over the
n(n-1)(n-2)
ordered triples of distinct integers in
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
,
and
\operatorname{S}_{n}
acts on these triples in the natural way. Clearly, each
\dfrac{x_{r}-x_{s}}{x_{r}-x_{t}}

is a regular function on
X_{1,2}\setminus\Delta
. Letting
Y
be the Zariski closure of the image of
\pi
in
\mathbb{A}^{n(n-1)(n-2)}
, we may view
\pi
as an
\operatorname{S}_{n}
-equivariant dominant
rational
X_{1,2}\dasharrow Y
.
The following lemma completes the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
n\geqslant 5
. Then
(a)
\operatorname{S}_{n}
acts faithfully on
Y
, and
(b)
\dim(Y)\leqslant n-4
.

Proof.

(a) Assume the contrary. The kernel
N
of this action is a non-trivial normal subgroup of
\operatorname{S}_{n}
.
Since
n\geqslant 5
,
N
is either the alternating group
\operatorname{A}_{n}
or the full symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{n}
. In both cases

\operatorname{A}_{n}
acts trivially on
\pi(a)
for every
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in X_{1,2}\setminus\Delta
. In particular,
the 3-cycle
\sigma=(2,\,4,\,5)\in\operatorname{A}_{n}
preserves
\pi(a)
.
That is,

(5.2)

\dfrac{a_{1}-a_{2}}{a_{1}-a_{3}}=\sigma\cdot\dfrac{a_{1}-a_{2}}{a_{1}-a_{3}}=\dfrac{a_{1}-a_{4}}{a_{1}-a_{3}}.

This implies
a_{2}=a_{4}
, which contradicts our assumption that
a\not\in\Delta
.

(b) Note that
\pi
sends every
B
-orbit to a point. By Lemma
5.2
(b), a general orbit of
B
in
X_{1,2}
is 2-dimensional. Hence, a general fiber of
\pi
is of dimension
\geqslant 2
. By the Fiber Dimension Theorem,
\dim(Y)\leqslant\dim(X_{1,2})-2=n-4
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

As we suggested at the beginning of this section,
\pi
is, in fact, a rational quotient for the
B
-action on
X_{1,2}
. We do not need to know this though; the explicit formula in (
5.2
) suffices for the purpose of proving Proposition
5.1
.
