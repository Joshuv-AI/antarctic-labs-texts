On evolving natural curvature for an inextensible, unshearable, viscoelastic rod
K. R. Rajagopal and C. Rodriguez

Abstract
. We formulate and consider the problem of an inextensible, unshearable, viscoelastic rod, with evolving natural configuration, moving on a plane. We prove that the dynamic equations describing quasistatic motion of an Eulerian strut, an infinite dimensional dynamical system, are globally well-posed. For every value of the terminal thrust, these equations contain a smooth embedded curve of static solutions (equilibrium points). We characterize the spectrum of the linearized equations about an arbitrary equilibrium point, and using this information and a convergence result for dynamical systems due to Brunovský and Polácik, we prove that every solution to the quasistatic equations of motion converges to an equilibrium point as time goes to infinity.



1.
Introduction

In an influential paper concerning the equilibrium of rods, Ericksen
[
4
]
considered the equilibrium states of a straight, extensible Green elastic rod, whose constitutive relation for the stored energy is a non-convex function of the deformation gradient.
1
1

1

The more geometric problem of bending of an inextensible, unshearable rod whose constitutive relation for the stored energy is a non-convex function of the rod’s curvature was later treated by Fosdick and James
[
5
]
.
This authoritative paper has motivated numerous studies which purport to study phase transition in solids but are really concerned with equilibrium states composed of co-existent parts with distinct deformation gradients. The problem studied by Ericksen concerns the equilibrium of a rod with juxtaposed parts with distinct constant deformation gradients, a purely static consideration and a purely kinematic consideration.
2
2

2

A lengthy discussion as to why these studies do not concern the actual process of phase transition but are merely concerned with a static deformed body comprised of elastic bodies with two distinct microstructures juxtaposed to each other in equilibrium, that is, they are concerned with the arrangement of a mixture of elastic materials with no phase change taking place, can be found in Rajagopal and Srinivasa
[
12
]
.

The one-sentence abstract to his paper states “For elastic bars, we discuss some material instabilities”, the study that is carried out is purely within the purview of elastostatics. Ericksen recognizes that in order to discuss “instabilities” one needs to study the problem within a dynamical context, as he states “For our purposes, it is essential to employ a stability criterion, and we employ the traditional energy criterion. As is well known, it is not completely reliable, but it is hard to improve upon it, without specifying constitutive equations which are to apply out of equilibrium.”

A little later in the introduction he makes the comment “In one sense, ours is an elementary study of phase transformations that are shear induced, i.e., induced by stresses which are not hydrostatic pressures”, and follows this up subsequently with the remark “In the analog of van der Waals’ fluid, we would be in a range where a fixed volume contains a mixture of liquid and vapor phases, when the equation of state exhibits a similar oscillation” . This remark of Ericksen’s concerning the equilibrium of distinct phases has been picked up by numerous researchers who have generalized this study to what they refer to as the problem of phase transition in elastic solids, purely within an elastostatic context wherein the elastic body is described by a non-convex stored energy function. This is an unfortunate circumstance for a variety of reasons. First, just from the viewpoint of English, it is incorrect. The term “transition” refers to a dynamic situation. As one gathers from the Oxford English Dictionary
[
15
]
, the word “transition” means “A passing or passage from one condition, action, or (rarely) place, to another: change”. Thus, if one is to study phase transition, it has to be within a dynamical context. Second, “phase transitions” are entropy producing. Without knowing the constitutive relation for the rate of entropy production, and merely knowing how the material stores energy, we cannot describe the phenomenon of “phase transition”. Third, even when there is no “phase transition”, such energy-based studies do not necessarily work and Ericksen was clearly aware of it and mentions it in the comment quoted above. Fourth, just because the governing equations are similar in totally distinct physical problems, one cannot conclude the physical quantities being described share identical attributes. Such flawed thinking can lead to disastrous consequences in physics. There are many more reasons why one ought not to interpret Ericksen’s study as being relevant to “phase transformations”, but we shall not get into a discussion of them here. Ericksen’s study reveals that there are equilibrium states of a purely elastic rod, wherein the deformation gradient could be discontinuous, no more and no less. The lack of relevance to phase transition notwithstanding, Ericksen’s study was thought provoking and opened up a fertile area for further study in nonlinear elasticity.

There are two important attributes to an elastic body, it is incapable of dissipating energy, that is, converting work into energy in its thermal form (heat), and it has a unique “natural configuration”. The notion of a natural configuration seems to have been discussed first by Eckart
[
3
]
. One could view the natural configuration of a body to be the configuration in which it would exist on the removal of all external stimuli.
3
3

3

The notion of a natural configuration is a local notion. When the external stimuli on a body that has been inhomogeneously deformed, are removed, the body might not attain a configuration which coheres properly. This is precisely the situation in an inelastic solid that has yielded. We can either consider the unloaded configuration in a non-Euclidean space, as considered by Eckart, or merely consider small locally homogeneously deformed neighborhoods that are unloaded in an Euclidean space as the local “natural configuration”.
From a purely mechanical perspective, it would be the configuration that the body would take if all the external loads were removed (see Rajagopal
[
8
]
for a discussion of the notion of natural configuration).

Unlike elastic bodies that are characterized by a single natural configuration, bodies such as viscoelastic and inelastic bodies are characterized by multiple natural configurations, and the body’s natural configuration can evolve when the body is undergoing a thermodynamic process. The manner in which the natural configuration evolves is determined by the way in which entropy is produced by the body. In bodies capable of producing entropy, entropy production could be due to various causes, due to dissipation (work being converted to thermal energy), due to conduction, due to mixing, due to phase transition, due to growth, etc. If we restrict our attention to purely mechanical processes, then the only source of entropy production is that due to dissipation. In the case of an elastic body, within a purely mechanical context, no entropy is produced, and hence its natural configuration does not evolve. However, in the case of a viscoelastic body, even within a purely mechanical context, the natural configuration can evolve, as the body is capable of producing entropy. The study of phase transition would have to take this evolution of the natural configuration of the body into consideration.

Based on these considerations of Ericksen’s work, we study the problem of an inextensible, unshearable, viscoelastic rod with evolving natural configuration, moving on a plane, with one end fixed and the other end free. Because of the symmetry of the problem, the rod’s configuration, a planar curve

Our viscoelastic rod is characterized by two scalar functions, a stored energy and a rate of total entropy production. The stored energy

\displaystyle\frac{(EI)}{2}(\mu(s,t)-\mu^{\circ}(t))^{2}+\kappa(\mu^{\circ}(t))

(1.1)

The second contribution to the total entropy production is due to a difference in the averaged curvature of the rod’s current configuration and the curvature of the rod’s current natural configuration. The form of this term we posit determines the evolution equation for the natural curvature (see (

\displaystyle=\bar{\alpha}\bar{M}_{\bar{s}}+\bar{H},

where (upon choosing appropriate units of time)

\displaystyle\bar{M}=(\bar{\mu}-\bar{\mu}^{\circ})+\bar{\mu}_{\bar{t}},

(3.8)

and
\bar{\epsilon}_{0},\bar{\epsilon}_{1},\bar{\alpha}
are positive dimensionless constants. The evolution for the natural curvature is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{\bar{t}}^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\bar{f}(\bar{D}-\bar{\Theta}(\bar{\mu}^{\circ}))-\bar{f}(-\bar{\Theta}(\bar{\mu}^{\circ})-\bar{D}).

(3.9)

Moreover, an assumption in rod theory is that a typical transversal length of the rod’s cross section is much smaller than the length of the rod which implies that
\bar{\epsilon}_{0}\ll\bar{\epsilon}_{1}
. In the remainder of this work, we will study the above system of equations in the quasi-static limit

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}_{0}=\bar{\epsilon}_{1}=0,

(3.10)

and we will drop the over bars on the remaining variables.

3.2.
Eulerian strut

We consider the boundary conditions corresponding to an Eulerian strut:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}(0,t)=\boldsymbol{o},\quad\theta(0,t)=0,\quad\boldsymbol{n}(1,t)=-\beta\boldsymbol{i},\quad M(1,t)=0,

(3.11)

where
\beta\geq 0
.
Then
\boldsymbol{n}(s,t)=-\beta\boldsymbol{i}
which implies that

\displaystyle H(s,t)=\boldsymbol{n}(s,t)\cdot\boldsymbol{b}(s,t)=\beta\sin\theta(s,t)=\beta\sin\Bigl(\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\zeta,t)d\zeta\Bigr).

(3.12)

We have
\alpha M_{s}(s,t)=-H(s,t)
which along with (
3.11
) and integration implies that
\alpha M(s,t)=\int_{s}^{1}H(\sigma,t)d\sigma
. The equations of motion and boundary conditions reduce to the following coupled differential equations with initial conditions: for all
(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,T)

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(s,t)=-\mu(s,t)+\mu^{\circ}(t)+\gamma\int_{s}^{1}\sin\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\sigma}\mu(\zeta,t)d\zeta\Bigr)d\sigma,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mu^{\circ}_{t}(t)=f\bigl(\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-f\bigl(-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))-\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t)\bigr),

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mu(s,0)=\mu_{0}(s),\quad\mu^{\circ}(0)=\mu^{\circ}_{0},

(3.16)

where
\gamma=\beta/\alpha
and
\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))=\int_{0}^{1}\mu(s,t)ds-\mu^{\circ}(t)-\kappa^{\prime}(\mu^{\circ}(t))
. For
(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
, let

\displaystyle F(\mu,\mu^{\circ})(s)=\Bigl(

\displaystyle\mu^{\circ}-\mu(s)+\gamma\int_{s}^{1}\sin\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\sigma}\mu(\zeta)d\zeta\Bigr)d\sigma,

(3.17)

\displaystyle f\bigl(\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr)

(3.18)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-f\bigl(-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))-\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t)\bigr)\Bigr).

(3.19)

Then (
3.13
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) can be written as the ordinary differential equation in
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bigl(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t)\bigr)=F(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t)),

(3.20)

\displaystyle(\mu(\cdot,0),\mu^{\circ}(0))=(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ}).

(3.21)

It is straightforward to verify that
F:L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable with Fréchet derivative
[DF(\mu,\mu^{\circ})](\xi,\xi^{\circ})=

\displaystyle\Bigl(

\displaystyle\xi^{\circ}-\xi(s)+\gamma\int_{s}^{1}\cos\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\sigma}\mu(\zeta)d\zeta\Bigr)\int_{0}^{\sigma}\xi(\zeta)d\zeta d\sigma,

(3.22)

\displaystyle f\bigl(\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr)\Bigl[\int_{0}^{1}\xi(s)ds-\kappa^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}-\Theta^{\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}\Bigr]

(3.23)

\displaystyle+f\bigl(-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))-\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr)\Bigl[\int_{0}^{1}\xi(s)ds-\kappa^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}+\Theta^{\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}\Bigr]\Bigr).

(3.24)

Indeed, writing
\theta(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}\mu(\zeta)d\zeta
and
\phi(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}\xi(\zeta)d\zeta
we have that the first component of
F
satisfies

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mu+\xi,\mu^{\circ}+\xi^{\circ})

\displaystyle=F_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\circ})+\xi^{\circ}-\xi(s)

(3.25)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\int_{s}^{1}[\sin(\theta(\sigma)+\phi(\sigma))-\sin\theta(\sigma)]d\sigma.

(3.26)

By trigonometric identities and Cauchy-Schwarz we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{s}^{1}

\displaystyle[\sin(\theta(\sigma)+\phi(\sigma))-\sin\theta(\sigma)]d\sigma-\int_{s}^{1}\cos\theta(\sigma)\phi(\sigma)d\sigma\Bigr|

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{s}^{1}\sin\theta(\sigma)[\cos\phi(\sigma)-1]d\sigma+\int_{s}^{1}\cos\theta(\sigma)[\sin\phi(\sigma)-\phi(\sigma)]d\sigma\Bigr|

(3.28)

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}^{2}+\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}^{3})

(3.29)

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{3})

(3.30)

where
C
is an absolute constant. Thus,

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mu+\xi,\mu^{\circ}+\xi^{\circ})

\displaystyle-F_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\circ})+\Bigl(\xi^{\circ}-\xi(s)\gamma+\int_{0}^{1}\phi(\sigma)\cos\theta(\sigma)d\sigma\Bigr)

(3.31)

\displaystyle=o(\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,1)})

(3.32)

as
\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\rightarrow 0
. Similarly, by using the smoothness assumptions for
\kappa
,
f
and
\Theta
, we conclude that the second component of
F
satisfies

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle(\mu+\xi,\mu^{\circ}+\xi^{\circ})=F_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\circ})

(3.33)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}\bigl(\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr)\Bigl[\int_{0}^{1}\xi(s)ds-\kappa^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}-\Theta^{\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}\Bigr]

(3.34)

\displaystyle\quad+f^{\prime}\bigl(-\Theta(\mu^{\circ}(t))-\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr)\Bigl[\int_{0}^{1}\xi(s)ds-\kappa^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}+\Theta^{\prime}(\mu^{\circ})\xi^{\circ}\Bigr]

(3.35)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+|\xi^{\circ}|)

(3.36)

as
\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+|\xi^{\circ}|\rightarrow 0
, proving (
3.23
).

3.3.
Global well-posedness for the quasistatic equations of motion

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists unique

(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})

satisfying (
3.13
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
).

Moreover, there exists a constant
C(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ},\gamma)>0
such that for all
t\in[0,\infty)

\displaystyle\|\mu(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+|\mu^{\circ}(t)|\leq C.

(3.37)

Proof.

Since (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) can be written as the differential equation (
3.20
) with
F
continuously differentiable on
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
,
the standard well-posedness theory for differential equations in Banach spaces implies that there exist
T_{+}>0
and unique

(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,T_{+});L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})
solving (
3.13
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) on a maximal time of interval of existence
[0,T_{+})
(see e.g. Chapter 3 of
[
7
]
). Moreover, we have the breakdown criterion

\displaystyle T_{+}<\infty\implies\limsup_{t\rightarrow T_{+}}[\|\mu(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+|\mu^{\circ}(t)|]=\infty,

(3.38)

and we have continuous dependence on initial conditions: if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bigl\|(\mu_{0,n},\mu^{\circ}_{0,n})-(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}=0,

(3.39)

then for all
n
sufficiently large, the unique solution

(\mu_{n},\mu_{n}^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,T_{+,n});L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})

to (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) with
(\mu_{n}(\cdot,0),\mu^{\circ}_{n}(0))=(\mu_{0,n},\mu^{\circ}_{0,n})
exists on
[0,T_{+})
, and for all
0\leq T<T_{+}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigl\|(\mu_{n}(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}_{n}(t))-(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}=0.

(3.40)

For
t\in[0,T_{+})
define

\displaystyle V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\mu(s,t)-\mu^{\circ}(t))^{2}ds+\kappa(\mu^{\circ}(t))

(3.41)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\cos\theta(s,t)ds

(3.42)

where
\theta(s,t)=\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\zeta,t)d\zeta
. We note that
V
is the difference of the total energy stored by the rod and the total work done by the terminal thrust. Then (
3.13
), (
3.15
), the relation
\theta_{ts}=\theta_{st}=\mu_{t}
and integration by parts imply that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\mu(s,t)-\mu^{\circ}(t))\mu_{t}(s,t)ds

(3.43)

\displaystyle\quad-\Bigl[\int_{0}^{1}\mu(s,t)ds-\mu^{\circ}(t)-\kappa^{\prime}(\mu^{\circ}(t))\Bigr]\mu^{\circ}_{t}(t)

(3.44)

\displaystyle\quad-\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\sin\theta(s,t)\theta_{t}(s,t)ds

(3.45)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\mu_{t}^{2}(s,t)ds+\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl[\int_{s}^{1}\sin\theta(\sigma,t)d\sigma\Bigr]\theta_{st}(s,t)ds

(3.46)

\displaystyle\quad-\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))\mu_{t}^{\circ}(t)-\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\sin\theta(s,t)\theta_{t}(s,t)ds

(3.47)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\mu_{t}^{2}(s,t)ds-|\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))\mu_{t}^{\circ}(t)|

(3.48)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))
and
\mu_{t}^{\circ}(t)
have the same sign via (
3.15
).
Thus, for all
t\in[0,T_{+})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\mu(s,t)-\mu^{\circ}(t))^{2}ds

\displaystyle+\kappa(\mu^{\circ}(t))

(3.49)

\displaystyle\leq V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))-\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\cos\theta(s,t)ds

(3.50)

\displaystyle\leq V(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})+\gamma.

(3.51)

By our assumptions on
\kappa
we conclude that
\mu^{\circ}:[0,T_{+})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded whence
\mu(\cdot,t):[0,T_{+})\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)
is bounded as well. By (
3.38
) we conclude that
T_{+}=\infty
as well as (
3.37
).
∎

3.4.
Equilibrium points

The equilibrium points
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
of the evolution equation (
3.20
) satisfy
F(\nu,\nu^{\circ})=(0,0)
i.e.

\displaystyle\nu^{\circ}-\nu(s)+\gamma\int_{s}^{1}\sin\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\sigma}\nu(\zeta)d\zeta\Bigr)d\sigma=0,\quad s\in[0,1],

(3.52)

\displaystyle|\hat{D}(\nu,\nu^{\circ})|\leq\Theta(\nu^{\circ}).

(3.53)

We observe that (
3.52
) implies that
\nu\in C^{1}([0,1])
and
\nu(1)=\nu^{\circ}
. Defining
\phi(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\nu(\zeta)d\zeta\in C^{2}([0,1])
, we see that

\displaystyle(\nu,\nu^{\circ})=(\phi^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}(1)),

(3.54)

and
(
3.52
), (
3.53
) are equivalent to

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)+\gamma\sin\phi(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1],

(3.55)

\phi(0)=0,\phi^{\prime}(1)=\nu^{\circ},
and

\displaystyle\Bigl|\phi(1)-\phi^{\prime}(1)-\kappa^{\prime}(\phi^{\prime}(1))\Bigr|\leq\Theta(\phi^{\prime}(1)).

(3.56)

The trivial equilibrium point

\displaystyle\nu=0,\quad\nu^{\circ}=0

(3.57)

gives the configuration of a straight rod.
We note that since
\hat{D}(0,0)=0
and
\Theta
is bounded from below there always exist nontrivial equilibrium points. Indeed, by continuous dependence on initial conditions for (
3.55
), there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
\alpha\in(-\delta,\delta)
the unique solution
\phi_{\alpha}\in C^{2}([0,1])
to (
3.55
) satisfying the initial conditions
\phi_{\alpha}(0)=0,\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)=\alpha
also satisfies

\displaystyle|\hat{D}(\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(1))|<\min_{\nu^{\circ}\in\mathbb{R}}\Theta(\nu^{\circ})\leq\Theta(\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(1)).

(3.58)

Then
(\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(1))
is a nontrivial equilibrium point of (
3.20
) for
\alpha\in(-\delta,\delta)\backslash\{0\}
.

We now turn to the stability properties of equilibrium points, and in what follows,
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,1])\times\mathbb{R}
is a fixed equilibrium point and
\phi(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\nu(\zeta)d\zeta
. The linearization of (
3.20
) about
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\mu(s,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle=[DF(\nu,\nu^{\circ})](\mu(s,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

(3.59)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\mu^{\circ}(t)-\mu(s,t)+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\mu(\zeta,t)\cos\phi(\sigma)d\zeta d\sigma,0\Bigr)

(3.60)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\mu^{\circ}(t)-\mu(s,t)+\int_{0}^{1}K(s,\zeta)\mu(\zeta,t)d\zeta,0\Bigr)

(3.61)

where
K(s,\zeta)=\int^{1}_{\max(s,\zeta)}\cos\phi(\sigma)d\sigma
. We denote
\mathcal{L}=DF(\nu,\nu^{\circ}):L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is bounded, the solution to the linearized equations about
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
can be written succinctly as

\displaystyle(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\mu(\cdot,0),\mu^{\circ}(0)).

(3.62)

The next proposition shows that there are only finitely many unstable directions when perturbing
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
(at the linearized level) and 0 is
always
a simple eigenvalue for
\mathcal{L}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The spectrum

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{L})=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid\mathcal{L}-\lambda I\mbox{ does not have a bounded inverse }\}

(3.63)

satisfies

(1)

\sigma(\mathcal{L})\subset\mathbb{R}
is countable,

(2)

-1\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})
and
-1
is the only possible limit point of
\sigma(\mathcal{L})
,

(3)

every
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash\{-1,0\}
is an eigenvalue with associated one dimensional eigenspace,

(4)

0\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{ker}\mathcal{L}=\mbox{span}(\theta_{0}^{\prime},\theta_{0}^{\prime}(1))

(3.64)

where
\theta_{0}\in C^{2}([0,1])
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime\prime}(s)+\gamma[\cos\phi(s)]\theta(s)=0,

(3.65)

\displaystyle\theta(0)=0,\quad\theta^{\prime}(0)=1.

(3.66)

Proof.

We first consider the invertibility properties of
\mathcal{L}-\lambda I:L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. For
(\xi,\xi^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
, we have
(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)(\mu,\mu^{\circ})=(\xi,\xi^{\circ})
if and only if
\mu^{\circ}=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\xi^{\circ}
and

\displaystyle-(1+\lambda)\mu(s)+\int_{0}^{1}K(s,\zeta)\mu(\zeta)d\zeta=\xi(s)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\xi^{\circ}.

(3.67)

Denote the integral operator
\mathcal{K}\mu(s)=\int_{0}^{1}K(s,\zeta)\mu(s)ds
. Then (
3.67
) is solvable for arbitrary
(\xi,\xi^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
if and only if
-(1+\lambda)I+\mathcal{K}:L^{2}(0,1)\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)
has a bounded inverse. Thus,
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash 0
if and only if
1+\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{K})\backslash\{1\}
. Since
K(s,\zeta)=K(\zeta,s)=\overline{K(\zeta,s)}
and
K\in C([0,1]\times[0,1])
,
\mathcal{K}
is a self-adjoint compact operator. By the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators, we can immediately conclude
(1)
,
(2)
and
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash\{-1,0\}
if and only if there exists
\mu\in L^{2}(0,1)\backslash\{0\}
satisfying

\displaystyle-(1+\lambda)\mu(s)+\int_{0}^{1}K(s,\zeta)\mu(\zeta)=0.

(3.68)

But for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
,
(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)(\mu,\mu^{\circ})=(0,0)
if and only if
\mu^{\circ}=0
and
\mu
satisfies (
3.68
). Thus, every
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash\{-1,0\}
is an eigenvalue.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash\{-1,0\}
. We claim that
\dim\operatorname{ker}(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)=1
. Let
\theta_{\lambda},\psi_{\lambda}\in C^{2}([0,1])
be the fundamental system for the linear differential equation

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)\theta^{\prime\prime}(s)+\gamma[\cos\phi(s)]\theta(s)=0,

(3.69)

satisfying

\displaystyle\theta_{\lambda}(0)=0,\quad\theta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(0)=1,

(3.70)

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}(0)=1,\quad\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(0)=0.

(3.71)

By linearity, every solution to (
3.69
) is a linear combination of
\theta_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
.
If
(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
satisfies
(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)(\mu,\mu^{\circ})=(0,0)
, then
\mu^{\circ}=0
and
\mu
satisfies (
3.68
). Thus,
\mu\in C^{1}([0,1])
and
\theta(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\sigma)d\sigma
verifies (
3.69
) (by differentiating (
3.68
)) and
\theta(0)=0
. Thus,
\theta=\theta^{\prime}(0)\theta_{\lambda}
so
\mu=\theta^{\prime}(0)\theta^{\prime}_{\lambda}
and
\operatorname{ker}(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)\subset\mbox{span}\,(\theta_{\lambda}^{\prime},\theta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(1))
. This proves the claim (and thus,
(3)
).

What remains is to prove
(4)
. Let
\theta_{0}\in C^{2}([0,1])
be the solution to (
3.66
). Then
(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})=(\theta_{0}^{\prime},\theta_{0}^{\prime}(1))
verifies

\displaystyle-\mu_{0}(s)+\mu_{0}^{\circ}+\int_{0}^{1}K(s,\zeta)\mu_{0}(\zeta)d\zeta=0,

(3.72)

which proves
\operatorname{ker}\mathcal{L}\not=\{0\}
. The proof that
\operatorname{ker}\mathcal{L}=\mbox{span}(\theta_{0}^{\prime},\theta_{0}^{\prime}(1))
is nearly identical to that showing
\operatorname{ker}(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)=\mbox{span}(\theta_{\lambda}^{\prime},\theta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(1))
for
\lambda\notin\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash\{-1,0\}
and is omitted.
∎

The following proposition elucidates the underlying source of the kernel of
\mathcal{L}
and provides a simple orbital asymptotic stability statement.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that

|\hat{D}(\nu,\nu^{\circ})|<\Theta(\nu^{\circ})
, and let
\theta_{0}
be as in Proposition
3.2
.

(1)

There exist
\epsilon>0
and a smooth embedded curve

\displaystyle(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\ni\alpha\mapsto(\nu(\alpha),\nu^{\circ}(\alpha))\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}

(3.73)

such that
(\nu(0),\nu^{\circ}(0))=(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
, for all
\alpha\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
,
(\nu(\alpha),\nu^{\circ}(\alpha))
is an equilibrium point of (
3.20
), and
\frac{d}{d\alpha}(\nu(\alpha),\nu^{\circ}(\alpha))|_{\alpha=0}=(\theta_{0}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}_{0}(1))
.

(2)

If
\sigma(\mathcal{L})\cap\{\lambda\mid\mathrm{Re}\lambda>0\}=\varnothing
, then there exist
\delta>0
,
C>0
and
\beta>0
such that if

\displaystyle\|(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})-(\nu(0),\nu^{\circ}(0))\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}<\delta,

(3.74)

then there exists
\alpha_{\infty}\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
such that the solution
(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in

C^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})
to (
3.20
) with initial data
(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})
satisfies

\displaystyle\|(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))-(\nu(\alpha_{\infty}),\nu^{\circ}(\alpha_{\infty}))\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}\leq C\delta e^{-\beta t}.

(3.75)

Proof.

(1)
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\phi_{\alpha}\in C^{2}([0,1])
be the unique solution to

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(s)+\gamma\sin\phi_{\alpha}(s)=0,

(3.76)

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(0)=0,\quad\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)=\phi^{\prime}(0)+\alpha.

(3.77)

We note that
\phi_{0}(s)=\phi(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\nu(\sigma)d\sigma
. Since
|\hat{D}(\nu,\nu^{\circ})|<\Theta(\nu^{\circ})
, continuous dependence on initial conditions implies that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all
\alpha\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
,

\displaystyle|\hat{D}(\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime},\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(1))|<\Theta(\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(1)).

(3.78)

Thus,
(\nu(\alpha),\nu^{\circ}(\alpha))=(\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha},\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(1)):(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
is a smooth embedded curve consisting entirely of equilibrium points of (
3.20
). Finally, by (
3.77
) we see that
\dot{\phi}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\alpha}|_{\alpha=0}
verifies

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(s)+\gamma[\cos\phi(s)]\dot{\phi}(s)=0,

(3.79)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(0)=0,\quad\dot{\phi}^{\prime}(0)=1,

(3.80)

and thus,
\dot{\phi}=\theta_{0}
so
\frac{d}{d\alpha}(\nu(\alpha),\nu(\alpha)^{\circ})|_{\alpha=0}=(\theta^{\prime}_{0},\theta^{\prime}_{0}(1)).

(2) The statement follows from (1) and a general asymptotic stability result discussed on p. 108-109 of
[
7
]
. For completeness, we will briefly sketch the details. By redefining
F
we may assume without loss of generality that
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})=(0,0)
, and rewrite (
3.20
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))=\mathcal{L}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ})+G(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t)),

(3.81)

where
\mathcal{L}=DF(0,0)
and
G(\mu,\mu^{\circ})=F(\mu,\mu^{\circ})-\mathcal{L}(\mu,\mu^{\circ})
is continuously Fréchet differentiable and satisfies
G(0,0)=(0,0)
and
DG(0,0)=0
(as operators). We decompose

L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}=\operatorname{span}(\theta_{0}^{\prime},\theta_{0}^{\prime}(1))\oplus\mathcal{H}

where
\mathcal{H}
is the image of the spectral projection
\Pi:L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
associated to
\sigma(\mathcal{L})\backslash\{0\}
. We then write

(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))=(\nu(\alpha(t)),\nu^{\circ}(\alpha(t)))+(\xi(\cdot,t),\xi^{\circ}(t)),

where
(\xi,\xi^{\circ})\in\mathcal{H}
, and insert this ansatz into (
3.81
), obtaining evolution equations for
\alpha
and
(\xi,\xi^{\circ})
. Since
G(0,0)=(0,0)
,
DG(0,0)=0
(as operators) and
\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{H}}
has spectrum contained in a compact subset of
(-\infty,0)
, Duhamel’s principle applied to the equation satisfied by
(\xi,\xi^{\circ})
) implies that if
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\alpha(t)|<2\delta
with
\delta
small, then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\|(\xi(\cdot,t),\xi^{\circ}(t))\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}\leq C_{1}e^{-\beta t}\|(\xi(\cdot,0),\xi^{\circ}(0))\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}},

(3.82)

where
C_{1}
is an absolute constant. This then implies via the equation satisfied by
\alpha
that
|\partial_{t}\alpha(t)|\leq C_{2}e^{-\beta t}\|(\xi(\cdot,0),\xi^{\circ}(0))\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}
where
C_{2}
is an absolute constant. A bootstrap argument then concludes the proof.
∎

We remark that Proposition
3.3
(1) generalizes our observation at the start of this subsection that there always exist nontrivial equilibrium points, and Proposition
3.3
(2) applies to
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})=(0,0)
as long as
\gamma\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}
, the critical value for buckling of the straight rod. An interesting question is the existence and local structure (in
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
) of the set of limiting equilibrium points satisfying

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{0}^{1}\nu(s)ds-\nu^{\circ}-\kappa^{\prime}(\nu^{\circ})\Bigr|=\Theta(\nu^{\circ}).

(3.83)

The answer should be highly dependent on the specific choices of
\kappa
and
\Theta
and will not be addressed here.

4.
Quasi-static Eulerian Strut: Asymptotics

In this final section, we prove that every solution
(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))
to (
3.20
) converges to an equilibrium point of (
3.20
) in
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

4.1.
Liapunov function

In the proof of Proposition
3.1
we used that

\displaystyle V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\mu(s,t)-\mu^{\circ}(t))^{2}ds+\kappa(\mu^{\circ}(t))

(4.1)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\cos\theta(s,t)ds,

(4.2)

where
\theta(s,t)=\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\sigma,t)d\sigma
, is a Liapunov function:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\mu_{t}^{2}(s,t)ds-|\hat{D}(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))||\mu_{t}^{\circ}(t)|

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq 0.

(4.4)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})
satisfy (
3.20
). If there exists
T>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\nu_{t}^{2}(s,t)ds+|\hat{D}(\nu(\cdot,t),\nu^{\circ}(t))||\nu^{\circ}_{t}(t)|=0,

(4.5)

then
(\nu(\cdot,t),\nu^{\circ}(t))
is constant in time, and
(\nu(\cdot,0),\nu^{\circ}(0))
is an equilibrium point of (
3.20
).

Proof.

It is clear from (
4.5
) that our assumptions immediately imply that
\nu_{t}(\cdot,t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Suppose that there exists a time
t_{0}\in[0,T]
such that

\displaystyle|\hat{D}(\nu(\cdot,t_{0}),\nu^{\circ}(t_{0}))||\nu^{\circ}_{t}(t_{0})|=0

(4.6)

but
\nu^{\circ}_{t}(t_{0})\neq 0
. Then
|\hat{D}(\nu(\cdot,t_{0}),\nu^{\circ}(t_{0}))|=0
which by (
3.15
) implies that

\nu^{\circ}_{t}(t_{0})=0
, a contradiction. Thus,
\nu_{t}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. By (
3.20
) we conclude that
(\nu(\cdot,0),\nu^{\circ}(0))
is an equilibrium point of (
3.20
), so by uniqueness of solutions to (
3.20
),
(\nu(\cdot,t),\nu^{\circ}(t))=(\nu(\cdot,0),\nu^{\circ}(0))
for all
t
.
∎

4.2.
Convergence to an equilibrium point

Our first step towards proving the convergence of a solution to (
3.20
) to an equilibrium point of (
3.20
) is proving the precompactness of the trajectory in
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})
solve (
3.20
). Then

\displaystyle K=\{(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))\mid t\in[0,\infty)\}

(4.7)

is precompact in
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By (
3.37
) it follows that
\{\mu^{\circ}(t)\mid t\in[0,\infty)\}
is precompact in
\mathbb{R}
. Let

\displaystyle\xi(s,t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-\tau)}\mu^{\circ}(\tau)d\tau+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{1}e^{-(t-\tau)}\sin\theta(\sigma,\tau)d\sigma d\tau

(4.8)

where, as before,
\theta(s,t)=\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\zeta,t)d\zeta
. Then (
3.13
) implies that for all
(s,t)

\displaystyle\mu(s,t)=e^{-t}\mu_{0}(s)+\xi(s,t),

(4.9)

so it suffices to show that
\{\xi(\cdot,t)\mid t\in[0,\infty)\}
is precompact in
L^{2}(0,1)
.

By (
3.37
) and repeated use of the triangle inequality we deduce that for all
(s,t)

\displaystyle|\xi(s,t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-\tau)}C+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{1}e^{-(t-\tau)}d\sigma d\tau\leq C+\gamma.

(4.10)

For all
0\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq 1
and
t
we also obtain

\displaystyle|\xi(s_{2},t)-\xi(s_{1},t)|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}e^{-(t-\tau)}\sin\theta(\sigma,\tau)d\sigma d\tau\Bigr|

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}e^{-(t-\tau)}d\sigma d\tau=\gamma|s_{2}-s_{1}|.

(4.12)

By the Arzela-Ascoli theorem we conclude that
\{\xi(\cdot,t)\mid t\in[0,\infty)\}
is precompact in
C([0,1])\subset L^{2}(0,1)
as desired.
∎

Let
(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
, and let
(\mu,\mu^{\circ})\in C^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R})
be the unique solution to (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) with
(\mu(\cdot,0),\mu^{\circ}(0))=(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})
. We denote the evolution operator by

S(t)(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})
so that

\displaystyle S(t)(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})=(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t)).

(4.13)

We recall the notion of the
\omega
-limit set relative to the semi-flow
S(t)
:
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\in\omega(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})
if and only if there exists a sequence
t_{n}\geq 0
such that
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
and
S(t_{n})(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\rightarrow(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
in
L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
. We now show that the
\omega
-limit set consists of equilibrium points.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
,
\omega(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})
is a nonempty subset of equilibrium points of (
3.20
).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
it follows that
\omega(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\not=\varnothing
.
Let
(\nu_{0},\nu^{\circ}_{0})\in\omega(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})
. Then there exists
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
such that

\displaystyle(\mu(\cdot,t_{n}),\mu^{\circ}(t_{n}))=S(t_{n})(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})\rightarrow(\nu_{0},\nu^{\circ}_{0}).

(4.14)

By continuous dependence on initial conditions (
3.40
), for all
T\in[0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|(\mu(\cdot,t_{n}+t),\mu^{\circ}(t_{n}+t))-S(t)(\nu_{0},\nu^{\circ}_{0})\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}=0.

(4.15)

Let
(\nu(\cdot,t),\nu^{\circ}(t))=S(t)(\nu_{0},\nu_{0}^{\circ})
.
We now show that
(\nu_{0},\nu^{\circ}_{0})
is an equilibrium point of (
3.20
) i.e.
(\nu(\cdot,t),\nu^{\circ}(t))=(\nu_{0},\nu^{\circ}_{0})
for all
t
. By Lemma
4.1

V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))
is non increasing and bounded below. Thus,

\ell=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}V(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))

exists. Let
t\in[0,\infty)
. By (
4.15
), for all
s\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\sigma,t_{n}+t)d\sigma=\int_{0}^{s}\nu(\sigma,t)d\sigma.

(4.16)

By the dominated convergence theorem we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\cos\theta(s,t_{n}+t)ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\cos\Bigl(\int_{0}^{s}\mu(\sigma,t_{n}+t)d\sigma\Bigr)ds

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\cos\Bigl(\int_{0}^{s}\nu(\sigma,t)d\sigma\Bigr).

(4.18)

By (
4.15
) we also have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\mu(s,t_{n}+t

\displaystyle)-\mu^{\circ}(t_{n}+t))^{2}ds+\kappa(\mu^{\circ}(t_{n}+t))

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\nu(s,t)-\nu^{\circ}(t))^{2}ds+\kappa(\nu^{\circ}(t)).

(4.20)

Thus,

\displaystyle\ell=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}V(\mu(\cdot,t_{n}+t),\mu^{\circ}(t_{n}+t))=V(\nu(\cdot,t),\nu^{\circ}(t)),

(4.21)

for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. By (
4.3
) and Lemma
4.1
we conclude that
\nu_{t}(\cdot,t)=0
and
\nu^{\circ}_{t}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
as desired.
∎

We will use compactness of the trajectory, the spectral information from Section 3 (Proposition
3.2
) and the following convergence theorem for dynamical systems to finish the proof of our convergence result for solutions to (
3.20
). The general set-up is as follows.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
F\in C(X;X)
. Let
x\in X
, and let
\omega_{F}(x)
denote the
\omega
-limit set of
x
relative to
F
:
z\in\omega_{F}(x)
if and only if there exists
n_{k}\rightarrow\infty
such that
F^{n_{k}}(x)\rightarrow z
. The set of fixed points of
F
is denoted
\mbox{Fix}(F)=\{y\in X\mid F(y)=y\}
. We assume the following hypotheses:

•

\omega_{F}(x)\subseteq\mbox{Fix}(F)
,

•

y\in\omega_{F}(x)
and there exists a neighborhood
U
of
y
such that
F|_{U}:U\rightarrow X
is continuously Fréchet differentiable,

•

\sigma(DF(y))=\sigma^{u}\cup\sigma^{c}\cup\sigma^{s}
where,
\sigma^{u}
,
\sigma^{c}
, and
\sigma^{s}
are closed subsets of
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid|\lambda|>1\}
,
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid|\lambda|=1\}
, and
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid|\lambda|<1\}
respectively.

Let
X^{i}
be the image of the spectral projection of
DF(y)
associated with the spectral set
\sigma^{i}
,
i=u,c,s
. Brunovský and Polácik proved the following in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 4.4

(Theorem B
[
2
]
)
.

Assume that either
X^{u}
is finite-dimensional or the trajectory of
x
,
\{F^{n}(x)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, is precompact in
X
and one of the following properties hold:

(1)

\dim X^{c}=1
and the trajectory is precompact,

(2)

\dim X^{c}=m<\infty
and there is a submanifold
M\subseteq X
with
\dim M=m
such that
y\in M\subseteq\mbox{Fix}(F)

Then
\omega_{F}(x)=\{y\}
.

We remark that the proof of Theorem
4.4
essentially reduces to the case of assuming
(b)
. Assuming
(b)
and
\omega_{F}(x)\neq\{y\}
, one can prove, using invariant manifold theory, that the local center manifold
W^{c}_{loc}=M
, and
\omega_{F}(x)
contains a point distinct from
y
in the local (strong) unstable manifold
W^{u}_{loc}
(see Theorem A of
[
2
]
). This contradicts the fact that
\omega_{F}(x)\subseteq\mbox{Fix}(F)
, and thus,
\omega_{F}(x)=\{y\}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists an equilibrium point
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
of (
3.20
) such that
\omega(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})=\{(\nu,\nu_{0})\}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}S(t)(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})=(\nu,\nu^{\circ}).

(4.22)

Proof.

We will apply Theorem
4.4
in conjunction with Proposition
3.2
and Lemma
4.2
. Consider the time-1 map:

\displaystyle\Phi(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})=S(1)(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ}),\quad(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}.

(4.23)

Let
\omega_{\Phi}(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})
denote the
\omega
-limit set of the trajectory
\{\Phi^{n}(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. By (
4.23
) and the semigroup property of
S(t)
, we clearly have
\omega_{\Phi}(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\subseteq\omega(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})
. We claim that
\omega_{\Phi}(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\supseteq\omega(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})
and thus,

\displaystyle\omega_{\Phi}(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})=\omega(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ}).

(4.24)

Suppose that there exist
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and
t_{k}\in[n_{k}-1,n_{k}]
such that
t_{k}\rightarrow\infty
and

\displaystyle S(t_{k})(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\rightarrow(\nu,\nu^{\circ}).

(4.25)

By Lemma
4.3
,
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
is an equilibrium point of (
3.20
). By continuous dependence on initial conditions (
3.40
), we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\bigl\|S(t)S(t_{k})(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})-S(t)(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\bigl\|S(t_{k}+t)(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})-(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}.

(4.27)

In particular, we conclude that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bigl\|S(n_{k})(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})-(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}}

(4.28)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bigl\|\Phi^{n_{k}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})-(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}},

(4.29)

and thus,
(\nu,\nu^{\circ})\in\omega_{\Phi}(\mu_{0},\mu^{\circ}_{0})
, proving (
4.24
).

By Theorem 3.4.4 of
[
7
]
) we have that
\Phi:L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable with

\displaystyle[D\Phi(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})](\xi_{0},\xi_{0}^{\circ})=(\xi_{L}(\cdot,1),\xi_{L}^{\circ}(1)),

(4.30)

where

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\xi_{L}(\cdot,t),\xi_{L}^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle=[DF(\mu(\cdot,t),\mu^{\circ}(t))](\xi_{L}(\cdot,t),\xi_{L}^{\circ}(t))

\displaystyle[D\Phi(\nu,\nu^{\circ})](\xi_{0},\xi_{0}^{\circ})=e^{\mathcal{L}}(\xi_{0},\xi_{0}^{\circ})

(4.32)

where
\mathcal{L}=DF(\nu,\nu^{\circ})
is the Fréchet derivative of

discussed in Section 3 (see (

Let
(\mu_{0},\mu_{0}^{\circ})\in L^{2}(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
4.2
, (
4.24
) and Lemma
