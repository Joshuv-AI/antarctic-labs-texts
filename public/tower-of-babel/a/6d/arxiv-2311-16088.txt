Long-range first-passage percolation on the complete graph
Remco van der Hofstad, Bas Lodewijks

Abstract
. We study a geometric version of first-passage percolation on the complete graph, known as long-range first-passage percolation. Here, the vertices of the complete graph 𝒦 n \mathcal{K}_{n} are embedded in the d d -dimensional torus 𝕋 n d \mathbb{T}_{n}^{d} , and each edge e e is assigned an independent transmission time T e = ‖ e ‖ 𝕋 n d α ​ E e T_{e}=\|e\|_{\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}^{\alpha}E_{e} , where E e E_{e} is a rate-one exponential random variable associated with the edge e e , ∥ ⋅ ∥ 𝕋 n d \|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}} denotes the torus-norm, and α ≥ 0 \alpha\geq 0 is a parameter. We are interested in the case α ∈ [ 0 , d ) \alpha\in[0,d) , which corresponds to the instantaneous percolation regime for long-range first-passage percolation on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} studied by Chatterjee and Dey [ 12 ] , and which extends first-passage percolation on the complete graph (the α = 0 \alpha=0 case) studied by Janson [ 22 ] . We consider the typical distance, flooding time, and diameter of the model. Our results show a 1 , 2 , 3 1,2,3 -type result, akin to first-passage percolation on the complete graph as shown by Janson. The results also provide a quantitative perspective to the qualitative results observed by Chatterjee and Dey on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} .

1.
Introduction and main results

The study of first-passage percolation was initiated by Broadbent and Hammersley
[
11
]
and has since attracted an enormous wealth of attention and interest, first on infinite graphs such as
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and later also on finite (random) graphs. In this paper, we study
long-range first-passage percolation on the complete graph
; a geometric analogue of first-passage percolation on the complete graph.

First-passage percolation on the complete graph was first studied by Janson
[
22
]
, where the edge-weights are independent and indentically distributed exponential random variables. Janson identified the leading asymptotics for the edge-weighted distance between fixed vertices, the flooding time (the maximum weighted distance over all pairs of vertices
(u,v)
with
u
fixed), and the diameter (the maximum weighted distance over all pairs of vertices), as well as more precise second-order behaviour. Later, this work was extended in, among others,
[
1
,
6
,
20
,
21
]
. For the study of non-exponential edges, we refer to
[
5
,
7
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, where first-passage percolation on the complete graph with edge-weights of the form
E^{s_{n}}
is analysed. Here,
E
is an exponential random variable and
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence that (possibly) depends on the number of vertices
n
. We note that these articles generalise some of the work for exponential edge-weights, where
s_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

There is also a large body of work on first-passage percolation on
random graphs
. Results for the Erdős-Rényi random graph consider, among others, the typical distance, hopcount (the number of edges on the shortest edge-weighted path between two vertices), and the flooding time
[
9
,
19
,
17
]
.

In many
scale-free
graphs, there is a clear distinction between graphs with degree distributions with finite- and infinite-variance degrees in terms of the behaviour of first-passage percolation. When the degrees have infinite variance, many such graph models are
explosive
in that the typical weighted distance between vertices converges in distribution to a finite random variable. The term
explosion
is taken from the closely related theory of age-dependent branching processes. When this condition is not met, or the degrees have finite variance, first-passage percolation is
conservative
, in the sense that the weighted distances diverge with the number of vertices in the graph.

In the configuration model, the explosive and conservative settings have both been studied in detail in, among others,
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
8
,
10
]
, as well as in preferential attachment models
[
23
,
24
]
. Finally, in spatial models such as the scale-free percolation model and geometric inhomogeneous random graph model, both the conservative as well as the explosive setting have been studied
[
18
,
28
]
, too.

In this paper, we study first-passage percolation on the complete graph, where the vertices are embedded on a
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
of volume
n
, and where each edge
e
has an edge-weight
T_{e}:=\|e\|_{\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}^{\alpha}E_{e}
, where
\alpha\geq 0
is a model parameter and the
E_{e}
is a rate-one exponential random variable. One could consider this as first-passage percolation with a penalisation of long edges. The model is known as
long-range first-passage percolation
. An infinite-size version of this model, essentially a ‘complete graph on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
’, has been studied by Chatterjee and Dey in
[
12
]
, who identified multiple phase transitions in the long-range parameter
\alpha
for the behaviour of first-passage percolation. Related to this model, a degree-penalised version of spatial scale-free random graphs has recently been studied by Komjáthy and co-authors in
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, where multiple phases are discussed based on the penalisation strength.

Main contributions.

We study the typical distance, flooding time, and diameter of long-range first-passage percolation when the spatial dependence is weak, i.e. when
\alpha<d
, which Chatterjee and Dey call the
instantaneous percolation regime
. They prove the qualitative result that, from any fixed vertex
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, any other vertex in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
can be reached within distance
t
almost surely, for any
t>0

[
12
]
. Here, we study the finite-size setting on the complete graph, where we provide a quantitative counterpart to their results for the three aforementioned quantities. In particular, our results extend those of Janson
[
22
]
on the complete graph, which is the particular case
\alpha=0
.

1.1.
Model

In this section, we define the model and fix notation.

Notation.
For
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
be the discrete
d
-dimensional torus of volume
n
. That is,
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}:=[-n^{1/d}/2,n^{1/d}/2]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. It will be convenient to consider
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
as an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
x
is equivalent to
y
when
x-y\in n^{1/d}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then, we define the torus
\infty
-norm

(1.1)

\|u\|_{\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}:=\min\|x\|_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}},

where
x
runs over all elements in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that are equivalent to
u
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
denotes the Euclidean infinity norm. In a similar way, we can then define the torus
p
-norm on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
by

(1.2)

\|u\|_{\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d},p}:=\min\|x\|_{\mathbb{Z}^{d},p},

where again
x
runs over all elements in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that are equivalent to
u
, and

\|x\|_{\mathbb{Z}^{d},p}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{p}\Big)^{1/p}

is the
p
-norm on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. From now on, we omit the subscript
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
for the norm when the context is clear, since we always work with the torus
p
-norm. Furthermore, we do specify which
p
norm we use, since the results and proofs holds for all such norms, possibly by varying constants that do not alter the results.

Let
G_{n}
denote the complete graph with vertex set
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, i.e.
G_{n}:=(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d},\mathcal{E}_{n})
where
\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\{\{u,v\}\colon u\neq v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\}
, and we study long-range first-passage percolation on it: Fix
\alpha\geq 0
and assign to each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
an edge-weight
T_{e}:=\|e\|^{\alpha}E_{e}
, where
(E_{e})_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}
is a collection of i.i.d. rate-one exponential random variables.

1.2.
Main result

We start by introducing the main objects of study. For two vertices
u,v
, let
\Pi_{u,v}
denote the set of all self-avoiding paths from
u
to
v
. We define the transmission time between
u
and
v
as

(1.3)

X_{u,v}:=\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{u,v}}\sum_{e\in\pi}E_{e}.

We also define

(1.4)

R_{n}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
u\neq\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\|u\|^{-\alpha}.

Our main result is as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(1,2,3 times
\log{n}/R_{n}
for long-range first-passage percolation on the torus)
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N},\alpha\in[0,d)
. Let
U
and
V
be two distinct vertices in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, sampled uniformly at random, and let
u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
be some fixed vertex. Then,

(1.5)

\displaystyle X_{U,V}\frac{R_{n}}{\log n}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1;

(1.6)

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{u,v}\frac{R_{n}}{\log n}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}2;

(1.7)

\displaystyle\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{u,v}\frac{R_{n}}{\log n}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}3;

where (
1.6
) holds for any
u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
.

Remark 1.2

(Interpretation main result)
.

As our proof will indicate, Theorem
1.1
can be interpreted as follows. The typical distances in (
1.5
) originate from a
continuous-time branching process
approximation of the neighborhood growth. This approximation kicks in on scale
1/R_{n}
, i.e. after rescaling the edge-weights by a factor
1/R_{n}
. Further, the vertices realizing the maxima in (
1.6
) are
traps
, meaning that their closest neighbor is at distance
(\log{n})/R_{n}
away. Thus, the
v
that realizes the maximum in (
1.6
) is one of these traps, while the
u
and
v
realizing the maximum in (
1.7
) are
both
traps. Even though our proof does not follow these lines, this intuition is very useful. It would be of interest to try to turn the above intuition into a proof.

\blacktriangleleft

Remark 1.3

(First-passage percolation on the complete graph)
.

When
\alpha=0
, all edges have an exponential weight with rate one, so that the spatial embedding of the vertices does not play a role. In this setting, we recover first-passage percolation on the complete graph and our results are in line with those of Janson
[
22
, Theorem
1.1
]
. Our proof for the lower bound on the flooding time and diameter in (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) follow that of Janson
[
22
]
. All other bounds rely on a large-deviation analysis of the exploration processes from two sources.

\blacktriangleleft

Remark 1.4

(More general distance functions)
.

The results of Theorem
1.1
also hold when using a distance function
r\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
r
is (eventually) decreasing and

\int_{1}^{n^{1/d}}r(x)x^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}x/\log n\to\infty

as
n
tends to infinity, where we assign an edge
e
the weight
E_{e}/r(\|e\|)
. In particular,
r(x)=\ell(x)x^{-\alpha}
with
\ell
a slowly varying function (i.e.
\ell
such that
\lim_{x\to\infty}\ell(cx)/\ell(x)=1
for any
c>0
) and
\alpha\in[0,d)
.

\blacktriangleleft

1.3.
Discussion and open problems

In this section, we discuss the structure of the proof of the main result, discuss the quantity
R_{n}
, and state some open problems.

The proof.

Our proof is organised as follows:
In Section
2
, we perform a large-deviation analysis for typical distances that allows us to prove the upper bounds in (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
): In Section
2.1
, we discuss the continuous-time branching process approximation of the neighborhood growth, Section
2.2
discusses the growth rate of this exploration process, Section
2.3
performs a large-deviation analysis for upper bound on the diameter in (
1.7
), while Section
2.4
extends this large-deviation analysis to prove upper bounds on the flooding time in (
1.6
) and on the typical distances in (
1.5
). Having established all upper bounds, we move to the lower bounds in Section
3
. We prove the lower bound on typical distances in (
1.5
) in Section
3.1
, the lower bound on the flooding time in (
1.6
) in Section
3.2
, and the lower bound on the diameter in (
1.7
) in Section
3.3
.

The limiting constant.

The dependence of
R_{n}
, which appears in Theorem
1.1
and is defined in (
1.4
), on
n
can be made explicit. With relative ease, one can show that there exists a limiting constant
R=R(d,p,\alpha):=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}n^{-(1-\alpha/d)}
. This implies
R_{n}
is of the order
n^{1-\alpha/d}
and provides a quantitative limit for the results in Theorem
1.1
. An interesting question is what the value
R
is for different choices of
d,p,
and
\alpha
.

In Appendix
A
we provide an integral representation for
R
for any dimension and any norm. Moreover, we explicitly identify
R
(or provide a recursive approach how one can do so) for
p=1
and
p=\infty
and all dimensions, as well as for dimension two and any
p\in[1,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
.

Open problems.

For the complete graph, i.e. for
\alpha=0
, Janson
[
22
, Theorem
1.1
]
discusses the
fluctuations
around the main asymptotics in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), while the fluctuations around (
1.7
) were identified by Bhamidi and the first author in
[
6
]
. It would be of interest to extend that analysis to our spatial setting. Janson also considers the
hopcount

H_{u,v}
, i.e. the number of edges on the shortest edge-weighted path between
u
and
v
, as well as
\max_{v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}H_{u,v}
, in the complete graph. Addario-Berry, Broutin, and Lugosi consider
\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}H_{u,v}
in
[
1
]
. It would be interesting to see if these results can be extended to the spatial setting.

It would also be of interest to see whether the result on the typical distance, as in (
1.5
), can be proved to hold for
fixed
vertices
u
and
v
, rather than vertices selected uniformly at random. Though we expect this to be the case, the proof of the
lower bound
crucially depends on the uniformity of these two vertices (in fact, it suffices if one is uniformly chosen, by translation invariance). The
upper bound
does apply in full generality. In the complete graph setting, i.e. for
\alpha=0
, independently of which vertices have already been explored, the next vertex that is to be explored is uniform among all unexplored vertices. This is only true in the spatial setting when the vertices
U
and
V
are uniform themselves. It seems to us that understanding the distribution of the set of explored vertices, and how close it is to the uniform distribution (among all sets in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
of size equal to the number of explored vertices), is important to enable one to prove (
1.5
) for fixed vertices. We expect this understanding to also be crucial in analysing the other statistics mentioned above.

Further, it would be of interest to extend the results in this paper to other edge-weight distributions or transmission times. For example, Bhamidi and the first author in
[
5
]
consider the natural example of a
power
of an exponential distribution, and identify the continuous-time branching-process approximation of the first-passage percolation exploration, which might yield a starting point for the present spatial problem.

2.
Large deviations approach for distances: upper bounds

In this section we analyse our distances in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
under the random metric. We start by proving the upper bound in (
1.7
) in Theorem
1.1
. For this, we use a large deviations approach, similar to Janson
[
22
]
. At the end of this section, we show how this argument can easily be extended to the upper bounds in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) in Theorem
1.1
.

2.1.
Long-range first-passage percolation as an exploration process

We can view the exploration process of long-range first-passage percolation process on
G_{n}
, started from any vertex
u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, as a birth process
T_{n}(u,t)
. Any vertex in this birth process has a unique spatial location in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. Here,
T_{n}(u,0)=1
and for any
j\in[n-1]
and
t\geq 0
, when
T_{n}(u,t)=j
, a birth takes place at rate

(2.1)

\Lambda_{j}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}\bigg(R_{n}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{j-1}\|v_{i}-v_{\ell}\|^{-\alpha}\bigg),

where
v_{i}
denotes the
i^{{\rm th}}
vertex born in the birth process
T_{n}(u,t)
and
v_{0}=u
. Here, the argument of the outer sum equals the rate at which the vertex
v_{\ell}
gives birth to the next vertex. It follows that
\Lambda_{j}
is random. When the
j^{{\rm th}}
birth takes place, conditionally on
\Lambda_{j}
and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1}
,

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(v_{j}=z\,|\,\Lambda_{j},v_{1},\ldots,v_{j-1}\right)=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\|z-v_{i}\|^{-\alpha}}{\Lambda_{j}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{j-1}\}.

Then, observe that, for each
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,j-1\}
, the vertices
v_{0},\ldots v_{\ell-1},v_{\ell+1},\ldots,v_{j-1}
are at least as far away from
v_{\ell}
as the
j-1
closest vertices to
v_{\ell}
. The sum of the weighted distances of these
j-1
closest vertices equals
R_{j}
. Using this thus yields the bounds

(2.3)

j(R_{n}-R_{j})\leq\Lambda_{j}\leq jR_{n}.

We thus find that for all
j=o(n)
almost surely,

(2.4)

jR_{n}(1-\mathcal{O}((j/n)^{1-\alpha/d}))\leq\Lambda_{j}\leq jR_{n}.

Moreover, if we let
E_{j}\sim\text{Exp}(\Lambda_{j})
, and, for
j\in[n-1]
,

(2.5)

E_{j,U}\sim\text{Exp}(j(R_{n}-\mathcal{O}(j^{1-\alpha/d}))),\qquad E_{j,L}\sim\text{Exp}(jR_{n}),

where the
(E_{j,L})_{j\in[n-1]}
(resp.
(E_{j,U})_{j\in[n-1]}
) are independent, then
E_{j,L}\preceq E_{j}\preceq E_{j,U}
for any
j=o(n)
, where
X\preceq Y
denotes that the random variable
X
stochastically dominates the random variable
Y
. As a result, the time until
j=o(n)
vertices have been discovered stochastically dominates, and is stochastically dominated by, a sequence of independent exponential random variables with deterministic rates that are also controllably close to one another.

2.2.
Growth rate of exploration process

We describe our first-passage percolation process started from a vertex
v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
via the random metric induced by the random edge-weights. We define

(2.6)

B_{v}(t):=\{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\colon X_{v,u}\leq t\},

as the (random) ball of radius
t
around
v
with respect to the transmission-time metric. That is,
B_{v}(t)
denotes all vertices in
T_{n}^{d}
that can be reached within time
t
from
v
. Let us also define
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
\tau_{k}:=\inf\{t>\tau_{k-1}\colon|B_{v}(t)|=k+1\},k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\tau_{0}=0
. It is clear that the intervals
\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
are exponentially distributed with rate
\Lambda_{k}
, conditionally on
\Lambda_{k}
. Since
\Lambda_{k}\approx kR_{n}
follows from (
2.4
) for
k
not too large, we have the following lemma, which controls the fluctuations of the times at which the first-passage percolation process discovers vertices in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
:

Lemma 2.1

(Distributional limit of
\tau_{k}
)
.

Let
k=k(n)
be such that
k\to\infty
and
k=o(n)
. Then,

(2.7)

R_{n}\tau_{k}-\log k\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}\Lambda,

where
\Lambda
is a Gumbel random variable. In particular, when
k=n^{\beta}(1+o(1))
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
,

(2.8)

R_{n}\tau_{k}-\beta\log n\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}\Lambda,\qquad{\rm and~thus}\qquad\tau_{k}\frac{R_{n}}{\log n}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathbb{P}}}\beta.

Lemma
2.1
proves more than we will need below, since it also identifies the
fluctuations
of
\tau_{k}
. One can interpret the Gumbel limiting variable
\Lambda
as
\log(1/E)
, where
E
is exponential with rate 1. This variable
E
is the limit of
{\mathrm{e}}^{-t}Z_{t}
, where
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the Yule process that arises as the distributional limit of the (rescaled)
(|B_{v}(t/R_{n})|)_{t\geq 0}
discovery process.

Proof.

Let
(E_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sum of i.i.d. exponential random variables with rate one. It holds that
\tau_{k+1}
is equal in distribution to

(2.9)

\tau_{k+1}\overset{d}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{E_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}},

where we recall
\Lambda_{j}
from (
2.1
), the (random) rate at which the first-passage percolation process spreads from
j
vertices to
j+1
vertices. As a result, we can write

(2.10)

R_{n}\tau_{k+1}-\log j=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{E_{j}}{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j}+\gamma+\frac{1}{2k}-\varepsilon_{k}+R_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{j}}-\frac{1}{jR_{n}}\Big)E_{j},

where we use that
\log k=H_{k}+\gamma+1/(2k)-\varepsilon_{k}
, where
H_{k}
is the harmonic sum,
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, and
\varepsilon_{k}
is an error term such that
\varepsilon_{k}\in[0,1/(8k^{2})]
. We now combine the first three terms, which contribute to the limiting random variable, and write all deterministic terms that tend to zero with
k
as
o(1)
, to obtain

(2.11)

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{E_{j}-1}{j}+\gamma+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{jR_{n}-\Lambda_{j}}{j\Lambda_{j}}E_{j}+o(1).

For the first sum, we have that as
k\to\infty
,

(2.12)

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{E_{j}-1}{j}+\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{E_{j}-1}{j}+\gamma\overset{d}{=}\Lambda,

where
\Lambda
is a Gumbel random variable. For the second sum, we use (
2.4
) to obtain

(2.13)

jR_{n}-\Lambda_{j}\leq jR_{j-1}=\mathcal{O}(j^{2-\alpha/d}).

Moreover, using that
\Lambda_{j}=jR_{n}(1-o(1))
almost surely for any
j=o(n)
, as follows from (
2.3
), we obtain for some constant
C>0
,

(2.14)

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{jR_{n}-\Lambda_{j}}{j\Lambda_{j}}E_{j}=\frac{C}{R_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{-\alpha/d}E_{j}.

It thus remain to show that sum is smaller than
\varepsilon R_{n}
with high probability for any
\varepsilon>0
to conclude that the sum on the left-hand side converges to zero. So, using a Markov bound,

(2.15)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{-\alpha/d}E_{j}\geq\varepsilon R_{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon R_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{-\alpha/d}=\mathcal{O}(k^{1-\alpha/d}/R_{n})=o(1),

since
k=o(n)
. So, for any
k
that diverges with
n
such that
k=o(n)
,

(2.16)

R_{n}\tau_{k+1}-\log k\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}\Lambda.

The desired result then follows since
\log k-\log(k+1)=o(1)
.
∎

2.3.
Large deviation analysis for upper bound diameter

We have, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{u,v}\geq(3+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{u,v}\geq(3+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{u,v}\geq(3+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right).

We now bound the transmission time between
u
and
v
from above by

(2.18)

\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}+\min_{x\in B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}),y\in B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})}T_{xy}.

Here,
\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}
denotes
\tau_{a_{n}}
for a FPP process started from
v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. Note that both
\tau_{a_{n}}^{(v)}
and
\tau_{a_{n}}^{(u)}
satisfy (
2.8
) in Lemma
2.1
(so also jointly), but they are independent if
B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)})\cap B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})=\varnothing
holds.
Indeed, if
B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)})\cap B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})=\varnothing
holds, then the time for the exploration processes of
u
and
v
to both reach size
a_{n}
plus the shortest edge between their respective explored vertices, is a possible way to connect
u
and
v
, and thus an upper bound for the transmission time. If, on the other hand,
B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)})\cap B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})\neq\varnothing
, then the third term in (
2.18
) equals zero almost surely, and the transmission time between
u
and
v
is no more than the time it took for both exploration processes to reach size
a_{n}
. As such, we obtain the upper bound

(2.19)

\displaystyle n^{2}{}

\displaystyle\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}+\min_{x\in B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}),y\in B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})}T_{xy}\geq(3+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\!\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\bigg[\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}\geq\frac{(3+\varepsilon)\log n}{R_{n}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\min_{x\in B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}),y\in B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})}\!\!\!\!T_{xy}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\log n}{R_{n}}\right)\bigg].

Let us bound the second probability on the right-hand side first. The minimum contains
(a_{n}+1)^{2}
many terms. As such, by bounding the spatial distance between any
x\in B_{u}(\tau^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}_{a_{n}})
and
y\in B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})
from above by
Cn^{1/d}
for some sufficiently large constant
C>0
, the minimum is stochastically dominated by an exponential random variable with rate
(a_{n}+1)^{2}(Cn^{1/d})^{-\alpha}=(c_{1}^{2}C^{-\alpha}+o(1))n^{1-\alpha/d}
. Hence,

(2.20)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\min_{x\in B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}),y\in B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})}T_{xy}\geq\varepsilon R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)\leq\exp(-(c_{1}^{2}C^{-\alpha}R^{-1}\varepsilon+o(1))\log n)=o(n^{-2}),

where the final equality holds when
c_{1}>\sqrt{2RC^{\alpha}/(\varepsilon(1-\alpha/d))}
. Observe that this upper bound is independent of the choice of
u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. It thus remains to upper bound

(2.21)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}\geq(3+\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right).

Again, we aim to obtain an upper bound that is
o(n^{-2})
, independently of
u
and
v
, so that combining this with (
2.20
) in (
2.19
) yields that
\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{u,v}
is at most
(3+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
with high probability, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. To this end, we replace
\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}
by
\widehat{\tau}_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}
, which is the time it takes to reach
a_{n}
vertices from
v
when all edges incident to the vertices in
B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)})
are removed. As this leaves fewer possible edges to reach vertices (and fewer vertices to be reached),
\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}\preceq\widehat{\tau}_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}
. Let us write
(z_{j})_{j=0}^{a_{n}}
for the vertices that are explored by
u
, in chronological order. That is,
z_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
vertex explored by
u
(with
z_{0}=u
):
X_{u,z_{j}}>X_{u,z_{i}}
for
i\in[j-1]
and
X_{u,z_{j}}<X_{u,z_{k}}
for any
k>j
. Then, conditionally on
B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)})
, we know that
\widehat{\tau}_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}
is a sum of
a_{n}
exponential random variables
(\widetilde{E}_{j})_{j\in[a_{n}]}
, where

(2.22)

\widetilde{E}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}\bigg(R_{n}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{j-1}\|z_{i}-z_{\ell}\|^{-\alpha}-\sum_{w\in B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)})}\|z_{\ell}-w\|^{-\alpha}\bigg)\bigg).

Similar to (
2.3
), for each
j\in[a_{n}]
we bound this rate from below by considering the
2a_{n}+1
closest vertices to
z_{\ell}
for each
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,j-1\}
. This yields a sequence of independent exponential random variables
(\widehat{E}_{j})_{j\in[a_{n}]}
such that
\widetilde{E}_{j}\preceq\widehat{E}_{j}
for each
j\in[a_{n}]
, and

(2.23)

\widehat{E}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}\big(j(R_{n}-R_{2a_{n}+1})\big),\qquad j\in[a_{n}-1].

By (
2.4
) and since
2a_{n}+1=o(n)
, it follows that the rate equals
jR_{n}(1-o(1))
, where the
o(1)
is independent of
j
. We can thus couple
\widehat{\tau}_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}
with the
(\widehat{E}_{j})_{j\in[a_{n}]}
such that

(2.24)

\widehat{\tau}_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}\preceq\sum_{j=1}^{a_{n}}\widehat{E}_{j}.

Similarly, as discussed in Section
2.1
, we can stochastically bound
\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}
from above by the sum of the random variables
(E_{j,U})_{j\in[a_{n}]}
, which are independent of the
(\widehat{E}_{j})_{j\in[a_{n}]}
. Combined with a Chernoff bound with
s=cR_{n}
and
c\in(2/(2+\varepsilon),1)
, this then yields

(2.25)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}\geq(3+\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-(3+\varepsilon)c}\prod_{j=1}^{a_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{sE_{j,U}}\right]\prod_{j=1}^{a_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{s\widehat{E}_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=n^{-(3+\varepsilon)c}\prod_{j=1}^{a_{n}}\Big(\frac{jR_{n}(1-o(1))}{jR_{n}(1-o(1))-s}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-c(3+\varepsilon)}\exp\Big(2\sum_{j=1}^{a_{n}}\frac{c}{j(1-o(1))-c}\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp(-c(3+\varepsilon)\log n+2c\log(a_{n})+o(\log n))

\displaystyle=n^{-c(2+\varepsilon)+o(1)}.

By the choice of
c
, this upper bound is
o(n^{-2})
. Combining this with (
2.20
) in (
2.19
) finally yields the desired result. This proves the upper bound on (
1.7
) in Theorem
1.1
.
∎

2.4.
Large deviation analysis for upper bounds flooding time and typical distances

We next extend this analysis to upper bounds on the flooding time in (
1.6
), and on the typical distances in (
1.5
).

Upper bound on flooding time.

A similar approach, now taking
c\in(1/(1+\varepsilon),1)
and
u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
fixed, gives

(2.26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq u\end{subarray}}X_{u,v}\geq(2+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq u\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{u,v}\geq(2+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{1-(1+\varepsilon)c+o(1)})+o(n^{-1})=o(1).

This proves the upper bound on (
1.6
) in Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Upper bound on typical distance.

Again using a similar approach, for distinct
u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
and with
c\in(0,1)
,

(2.27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{u,v}\geq(1+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}+\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)}+\min_{x\in B_{u}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(u)}),y\in B_{v}(\tau_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(v)})}T_{uv}\geq(1+2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n^{-c\varepsilon}).

As this holds for
any
distinct
u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
and the upper bound is independent of the choice of
u
and
v
, this also extends to the typical distance between
U
and
V
, two distinct vertices selected uniformly at random. This proves the upper bound on (
1.5
) in Theorem
1.1
. In particular, while we state (
1.5
) in Theorem
1.1
only for two uniform vertices, the upper bound actually holds for
all
fixed pairs as well.
∎

3.
Lower bounds on distances

Having established all upper bounds in Theorem
1.1
, we continue with the lower bounds. We analyse the lower bound on the typical distance in (
1.5
) in Section
3.1
, on the flooding in (
1.6
) in Section
3.2
and on the diameter in (
1.7
) in Section
3.3
.

3.1.
Lower bounds on typical distances

Let us start by explaining how we proceed. To analyse the distance between two vertices, selected uniformly at random from
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, as in (
1.5
), we show that their exploration processes are disjoint up to time
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2}R_{n}^{-1}\log n
with high probability. This implies that their typical distance is at least
(1-\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
with high probability.

Let
U
be vertex selected uniformly at random from
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
and let
v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
fixed. First, we write

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{U,v}\leq 2t_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(U=v\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left(B_{U}(t_{n})\cap B_{v}(t_{n})\neq\varnothing\,|\,U\neq v\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}:w\in B_{U}(t_{n}),w\in B_{v}(t_{n})\,|\,U\neq v\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{U,w}\leq t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\,|\,U\neq v\right),

where
\widetilde{X}_{v,w}
is the time for an exploration process started from
v
to reach
w
outside of
B_{U}(t_{n})
, i.e. on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash B_{U}(t_{n})
. Since
\widetilde{X}_{v,w}\succeq X_{v,w}^{\prime}
, where
X_{v,w}
is a copy of
X_{v,w}
, independent of
X_{U,w}
, we obtain the upper bound

(3.2)

\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{U,w}\leq t_{n}\,|\,U\neq v\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{w}(t_{n})|\right]\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\right).

By translation invariance,
\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{w}(t_{n})|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]
, where
\mathbf{0}
denotes the origin. We thus arrive at

(3.3)

\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]^{2}.

Finally, we use that by the upper bound in (
2.3
),

(3.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]^{2}\leq\big(\mathrm{e}^{R_{n}t_{n}}\big)^{2}=n^{1-\varepsilon},

to arrive at the desired result that

(3.5)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{U,v}\leq(1-\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{U,v}\leq 2t_{n}\right)=o(1).

An almost analogous argument can be used to yield the same result for two uniform vertices
U
and
V
as well. This proves the lower bound for (
1.5
) in Theorem
1.1
.∎

3.2.
Lower bound on flooding time

In this section we prove a lower bound on the flooding time, and is an adaptation of the idea for a lower bound on the
diameter
for the case
\alpha=0
by Janson in
[
22
]
.

Fix
v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
small, and take some set
A\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash\{v\}
such that
n_{A}:=|A|=\lfloor n^{1-\varepsilon}\rfloor
and let
B:=\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\backslash A
. We enumerate the vertices in
A
in an arbitrary order, so that we can write
A=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n_{A}}\}
. Abusing notation, we write
i\in[n_{A}]
when we mean
u_{i}\in A
.

We define, for
i\in[n_{A}]
, the random variable
U_{i}:=\min_{v\in B}T_{u_{i}v}
. That is,
U_{i}
is the transmission time from
u_{i}
to
B
. We also set
b=(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
and
\mu_{i}:=\sum_{v\in B}\|u_{i}-v\|^{-\alpha},i\in[n_{A}]
. For all
i\in[n_{A}]
, it readily holds that
\mu_{i}\leq R_{n}
. Furthermore, independently of
i\in[n_{A}]
,

(3.6)

\mu_{i}=R_{n}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in A\\
v\neq u_{i}\end{subarray}}\|u_{i}-v\|^{-\alpha}\geq R_{n}-R_{n_{A}}=R_{n}(1-\mathcal{O}(n^{\varepsilon(1-\alpha/d)})),

so that
\mu_{i}=R_{n}(1-\mathcal{O}(n^{\varepsilon(1-\alpha/d)}))
, where the
\mathcal{O}
term is independent of
i\in[n_{A}]
.
Now, for
i\in[n_{A}]
, define

(3.7)

f_{i}(x):=-\frac{1}{\mu}_{i}\log\big(\mathrm{e}^{-b\mu_{i}}+(1-\mathrm{e}^{-b\mu_{i}}\big)\mathrm{e}^{-\mu_{i}x}\big).

By
[
22
, Lemma
2.1
]
, it follows that
f_{i}(U_{i})\overset{d}{=}(U_{i}|U_{i}\leq b)
, i.e.
f_{i}(U_{i})
has the same distribution as
U_{i}
, conditionally on the event
U_{i}\leq b
.

Now, fix some
k\in[n_{A}]
and let

(3.8)

U_{i}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}f_{i}(U_{i})&\mbox{if }i<k,\\
U_{i}+b&\mbox{if }i=k,\\
U_{i}&\mbox{if }i>k.\end{cases}

If we let
\mathcal{E}_{k}:=\{U_{k}>b,U_{i}\leq b
for all
i<k\}
, then, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{k}
,
U_{i}^{\prime}
has the same distribution as
U_{i}
, by the memoryless property of the exponential distribution. As the
U_{i}
are independent, the distributional equality also holds jointly. Similarly, we set

(3.9)

T_{u_{i}v}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}T_{u_{i}v}-U_{i}+U_{i}^{\prime}&\mbox{if }i\in[n_{A}],v\in B,\\
T_{u_{i}v}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Equivalently,
T_{vu_{i}}^{\prime}=T_{u_{i}v}^{\prime}
. Again, by the memoryless property and since
T_{u_{i}v}-U_{i}
is independent of
U_{i}
, it follows that, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{k}
,
T_{u_{i}v}^{\prime}
has the same distribution as
T_{u_{i}v}
. In an equivalent manner we define
X^{\prime}_{v,u}
for
v,u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, which, for each pair
v,u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, we can think of as equal in distribution to
X_{v,u}
, conditionally on
\mathcal{E}_{k}
.

Suppose the following holds:

(3.10)

\displaystyle U_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq(1-2\varepsilon)U_{i}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }i\in[n_{A}],

\displaystyle T_{u_{k}i}

\displaystyle\geq 3R_{n}^{-1}\log n\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }i\in[n_{A}]\backslash\{k\},

\displaystyle X_{u_{k},v}

\displaystyle\geq(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n.

First, we observe that

(3.11)

T_{uv}^{\prime}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)T_{uv}\qquad\text{for all }v\neq u.

To see this, we need only consider
u\in A,v\in B
. Suppose
u=u_{i}
for some
i\in[n_{A}]
. Then, by the first line of (
3.10
),

(3.12)

T_{u_{i}v}^{\prime}=T_{u_{i}v}-U_{i}+U_{i}^{\prime}\geq T_{u_{i}v}-2\varepsilon U_{i}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)T_{u_{i}v},

as
U_{i}
is the minimal edge-weight between
u_{i}
and
B
. As
X_{u_{k},v}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
, it implies that for any path
(u_{k}=u_{i_{0}},u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{\ell}}=v)
it holds that

(3.13)

W=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}T_{u_{j-1}u_{j}}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n.

We then set
W^{\prime}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}T_{u_{j-1}u_{j}}^{\prime}
to be the conditional weight of this path. If
u_{i_{1}}\in A
, then
W^{\prime}\geq T_{u_{k}u_{i_{1}}}\geq 2R_{n}^{-1}\log n
by the second line of (
3.10
). If instead
u_{i_{1}}\not\in A
, then
T_{u_{k}u_{i_{1}}}^{\prime}=T_{u_{k}u_{i_{1}}}+b
and

(3.14)

W^{\prime}\geq b+(1-2\varepsilon)W\geq(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n+(1-2\varepsilon)^{2}R_{n}^{-1}\log n\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n.

It thus follows that, for
k\in[n_{A}]
,

(3.15)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,u_{k}}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,u_{k}}^{\prime}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\eqref{eq:Tboundrad}\text{ holds for }k\right).

We can then write

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{v,u}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcup_{u\in A}\{X_{v,u}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcup_{k\in[n_{A}]}\{X_{v,u_{k}}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{k}\right).

Since the events
\mathcal{E}_{k}
are disjoint, we can write the union as a sum and introduce
N
to be a uniform element of
[n_{A}]
, so that
u_{N}
is a uniform element of
A
. Then, we obtain

(3.17)

\displaystyle n_{A}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\{X_{v,u_{N}}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=n_{A}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,u_{N}}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{N}\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,u_{N}}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{N}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\eqref{eq:Tboundrad}\text{ holds for }N\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}\right).

Let us first argue that the sum is
1-o(1)
. As the events
\mathcal{E}_{k}
are disjoint and the random variables
(U_{i})_{i\in A}
independent,

(3.18)

\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)=1-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathcal{E}_{k}^{c}\right)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\!\left(U_{i}\leq b\text{ for all }i\in[n_{A}]\right)=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(U_{i}\leq b\right).

Now, as
b=(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
,
U_{i}\sim\text{Exp}(\mu_{i})
, and
\mu_{i}=R_{n}(1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-\varepsilon(1-\alpha/d)}))
by (
3.6
), where the
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\varepsilon(1-\alpha/d)})
term is independent of
i
, we obtain

(3.19)

1-\prod_{i=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(U_{i}\leq b\right)=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n_{A}}\big(1-\exp(-\mu_{i}b)\big)\geq 1-\exp(-n^{(2\varepsilon-1)+o(1)}n_{A})=1-\exp(-n^{\varepsilon+o(1)}).

We use this in (
3.17
) to arrive at

(3.20)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{u\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{v,u}\geq(2-6\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)\geq(1-o(1))\mathbb{P}\!\left(\eqref{eq:Tboundrad}\text{ holds for }N\right).

To show that the probability on the right-hand side converges to one, it suffices to show that the events in (
3.10
) hold with high probability, uniformly in
k\in A
, which we do now.

Fix
k\in[n_{A}]
. For the
first line
in (
3.10
), we have

(3.21)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcup_{i\in A}\{U_{i}^{\prime}<(1-2\varepsilon)U_{i}\}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in A}\mathbb{P}\!\left(U_{i}^{\prime}<(1-2\varepsilon)U_{i}\right)=\sum_{i<k}\mathbb{P}\!\left(f_{i}(U_{i})\leq(1-2\varepsilon)U_{i}\right),

where the last equality follows since
U_{i}^{\prime}\geq U_{i}
for all
i\geq k
by (
3.10
).

It now follows from
[
22
, Lemma
2.1
]
and (
3.6
) that the right-hand side can be upper bounded by

(3.22)

\sum_{i<k}\mathbb{P}\!\left(U_{i}>b/(1-2\varepsilon)-1/\mu_{i}\right)=\sum_{i<k}\mathrm{e}^{1-b\mu_{i}/(1-2\varepsilon)}\leq n_{A}\mathrm{e}^{1-bR_{n}(1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-\varepsilon}))/(1-2\varepsilon)}=o(1),

since
bR_{n}/(1-2\varepsilon)=\log n
and
n_{A}=o(n)
, and where the right-hand side is independent of the choice of
k\in A
. As a result, the first line in (
3.10
) also holds for a uniform element
N\in[n_{A}]
.

For the
second line
in (
3.10
), with
k\in A
fixed,

(3.23)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcup_{i\in A\backslash\{k\}}\!\!\{T_{u_{k}u_{i}}<3R_{n}^{-1}\log n\}\right)=1-\exp\Big(-3\frac{\log n}{R_{n}}\sum_{i\in A\backslash\{k\}}\|u_{k}-u_{i}\|^{-\alpha}\Big)\leq 3\frac{\log n}{R_{n}}R_{n_{A}-1}=o(1),

where the penultimate step uses the same idea as in (
2.4
) and where the upper bound holds uniformly in
k
and thus also for
k=N
.

Finally, the
third line
of (
3.10
) requires that the typical distance between a uniform element
u_{N}
from
A
and
v
is at least
(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
with high probability. The lower bound on the flooding time in Section
3.2
tells us this holds if we had a uniform vertex from
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
rather than from
A
. However, this argument is readily adapted to work for
u_{N}
as well. Namely, using
u_{N}
instead of
U
in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) (where we don’t have to condition on
u_{N}\neq v
since
v\not\in A
) yields for
t_{n}:=(1-2\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2}R_{n}^{-1}\log n
,

(3.24)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{u_{N},v}\leq 2t_{n}\right)\leq\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{u_{N},w}\leq t_{n}\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\right)=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\right)\frac{1}{n_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{w}(t_{n})\cap A|\right].

We can simply bound
\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{w}(t_{n})\cap A|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{w}(t_{n})|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]
, where
\mathbf{0}
is the origin. As a result, we obtain the upper bound

(3.25)

\frac{1}{n_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]\sum_{w\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X_{v,w}\leq t_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{n_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{\mathbf{0}}(t_{n})|\right]^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n_{A}}n^{1-2\varepsilon}=o(1),

where we use (
3.4
) in the penultimate step. This proves the third line of (
3.10
) and concludes the proof of the lower bound for the flooding time.
∎

3.3.
Lower bound on the diameter

This section proves a lower bound on the diameter, and uses a similar approach and the same definitions as in Section
3.2
.

Fix
k\in[n_{A}]
and suppose that the first two lines of (
3.10
) hold, while the third line is replaced with

(3.26)

\displaystyle Y_{k}:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq u_{k}\end{subarray}}X_{u_{k},v}

\displaystyle\geq(2-\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n.

Indeed, the statement in (
3.26
) holds with high probability by what is discussed in Section
3.2
, while (
3.10
) was already proved to hold with high probability.

We then claim that it follows from the first two lines of (
3.10
) and (
3.26
) that

Y_{k}^{\prime}:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\\
v\neq u_{k}\end{subarray}}X_{u_{k},v}^{\prime}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n.

Since
Y_{k}\geq(2-\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
by (
3.26
), there exists a path
(u_{k}=u_{i_{0}},u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{\ell}})
, such that

(3.27)

W:=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}T_{u_{i_{j-1}}u_{i_{j}}}\geq(2-\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n.

Consider such a path, and its conditional weight
W^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}T^{\prime}_{u_{i_{j-1}}u_{i_{j}}}
. If
i_{1}\in[n_{A}]
, then
W^{\prime}\geq T_{u_{k}u_{i_{1}}}\geq 3R_{n}^{-1}\log n
by the second line in (
3.10
). If
i_{1}\not\in[n_{A}]
, then
T^{\prime}_{u_{k}u_{i_{1}}}=T_{u_{k}u_{i_{1}}}+b
, and

(3.28)

W^{\prime}\geq b+(1-2\varepsilon)W\geq(1-2\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n+(1-2\varepsilon)(2-\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n,

where the second and third step follow from (
3.11
) and (
3.27
), respectively. It thus follows that this lower bound on the conditional transmission time holds for
any
path from
u_{k}
to
u_{i_{\ell}}
, so that
Y^{\prime}_{k}\geq X^{\prime}_{u_{k}u_{i_{\ell}}}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n
. If we let
\mathcal{C}_{k,n}
denote the event that the first two lines of (
3.10
) and (
3.26
) hold, we have thus shown that

(3.29)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{k}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{k}^{\prime}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{C}_{k,n}\right).

We next use the fact that the events
(\mathcal{E}_{k})_{k\in[n_{A}]}
are disjoint to obtain the lower bound

(3.30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{u,v\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}}X_{u,v}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{k}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{k\in[n_{A}]}\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{k}\geq(3-7\varepsilon)R_{n}^{-1}\log n\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-o(1))\min_{k\in[n_{A}]}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{C}_{k,n}\right),

where the final step follows from (
3.19
). Since we have already verified that the event
\mathcal{C}_{k,n}
holds with high probability independently of the choice of
k\in A
, we conclude that the right-hand side converges to 1 as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This proves the lower bound for (
1.7
) in Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Appendix A
The limiting constants

In this appendix we investigate the limiting constant

(A.1)

R=R(d,p,\alpha):=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}/n^{1-\alpha/d},

where we recall
R_{n}
from (
1.4
). By switching from summation to integration and using a variable transform
x=n^{1/d}w
, we obtain

(A.2)

R_{n}=(1+o(1))\int_{[-\frac{1}{2}n^{1/d},\frac{1}{2}n^{1/d}]^{d}}\|x\|_{p}^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x=(1+o(1))n^{1-\alpha/d}\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}}\|w\|_{p}^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}w.

Using symmetry and again using a variable transform
y=2w
, we arrive at

(A.3)

R_{n}=(1+o(1))2^{d}n^{1-\alpha/d}\int_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]^{d}}\|w\|_{p}^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}w=(1+o(1))2^{\alpha}n^{1-\alpha/d}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\|y\|_{p}^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y.

It follows that

(A.4)

R(d,p,\alpha)=2^{\alpha}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\|y\|_{p}^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Then, it is immediate that
R(d,p,0)=1
for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
. It thus remains to consider
\alpha\in(0,d)
. For
p=\infty
, we can use that

(A.5)

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-at}\,\mathrm{d}t=a^{-\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha),

to write

(A.6)

\displaystyle R(d,\infty,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t\max\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}\}}\,\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\max\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}\}}\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}d!}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{y_{d}}^{1}\cdots\int_{y_{2}}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-ty_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}\mathrm{d}t.

Let us define the inner integrals as

(A.7)

I(d,t):=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{y_{d}}^{1}\cdots\int_{y_{2}}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-ty_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}.

By evaluating the innermost integral and observing that
I(1,t)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-t}
, we obtain

(A.8)

I(d,t)=\frac{1}{t}I(d-1,t)-\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\frac{1}{(d-1)!}=\ldots=t^{-d}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}t^{-i}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\frac{1}{(d-i)!}=t^{-d}\bigg(1-\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\bigg).

The part between brackets equals the probability that a rate
t
Poisson random variable is at least
d
. Via a rate-one Poisson process we can see that this probability equals the probability that a Gamma
(d,1)
random variable is at most
t
. Using this in (
A.6
), we arrive at

(A.9)

\displaystyle R(d,\infty,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}d!}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-d-1}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(d-1)!}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}d}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-d-1}\,\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{d}{d-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{d}{d-\alpha}2^{\alpha}.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
a similar approach yields

(A.10)

\displaystyle R(d,p,\alpha)

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}^{p}\Big)^{-\alpha/p}\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha/p)}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha/p-1}\exp\Big(-t\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}^{p}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}

(A.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha/p)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha/p-1}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\Big(-t\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}^{p}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}y_{d}\mathrm{d}t.

We use the variable transformation
z_{i}=ty_{i}^{p}
to then obtain

(A.13)

\displaystyle R(d,p,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha/p)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha/p-1}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{p}t^{-1/p}z^{1/p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}\,\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{d}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\alpha}p^{-d}}{\Gamma(\alpha/p)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{(\alpha-d)/p-1}\gamma(1/p,t)^{d}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\gamma(1/p,t):=\int_{0}^{t}z^{1/p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}\,\mathrm{d}z
is the lower incomplete gamma function.

We are unable to explicitly evaluate this integral in full generality. For
d=2
and
\alpha\in(0,2)
, however, an explicit expression of
R
in terms of the hypergeometric function can be obtained for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. Namely, the integral in (
A.13
) can be written, using integration by parts, as

(A.14)

\displaystyle\Big[{}

\displaystyle\frac{p}{\alpha-2}t^{(\alpha-2)/p}\gamma(1/p,t)^{2}\Big]_{0}^{\infty}+\frac{2p}{2-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{(\alpha-2)/p}\gamma(1/p,t)t^{1/p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2p}{2-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{(\alpha-1)/p-1}\gamma(1/p,t)\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the last step uses that
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}t^{-1/p}\gamma(1/p,t)=p
and
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Now, Gradshteyn and Ryzhik
[
16
, 6.455.2]
show this equals

(A.15)

\frac{2p^{2}}{2-\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha/p)2^{-\alpha/p}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(1,\frac{\alpha}{p},1+\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{2}\Big),

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c,z)
denotes the hypergeometric function, defined for
|z|<1
as

(A.16)

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c,z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+n)\Gamma(c+n)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(c)\Gamma(b+n)}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

Substituting this into (
A.13
) finally yields

(A.17)

R(2,p,\alpha)=\frac{2^{1+\alpha(1-1/p)}}{2-\alpha}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(1,\frac{\alpha}{p},1+\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{2}\Big).

We also observe that the limit of
p\to\infty
on the right-hand side yields the same expression as for
R(d,\infty,\alpha)
in (
A.9
). Since the supremum-norm corresponds to the limit of the
p
-norm as
p\to\infty
, it follows that (
A.9
) also follows from (the derivation of) (
A.17
) and the dominated convergence theorem.

In the case of general dimension
d
, one interesting observation is the following: let
M_{d,p}
be the maximum of
d
i.i.d. Gamma
(1/p,1)
random variables and let
f_{M_{d}}
denote its probability density function. Then,

(A.18)

\gamma(1/p,t)^{d}=\Gamma(1/p)^{d}\mathbb{P}\!\left(M_{d,p}\leq t\right).

As a result, using integration by parts on the right-hand side of (
A.13
) (as in (
A.14
)) we obtain

(A.19)

R(d,p,\alpha)=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma(1/p)^{d}p^{-(d-1)}}{\Gamma(\alpha/p)(d-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{(\alpha-d)/p}f_{M_{d,p}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma(1/p)^{d}p^{-(d-1)}}{\Gamma(\alpha/p)(d-\alpha)}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{d,p}^{(\alpha-d)/p}\right].

Using that the density
f_{M_{d,p}}
is given by

f_{M_{d,p}}(t)=dF_{\Gamma}(t)^{d-1}f_{\Gamma}(t),

where
f_{\Gamma}
is the density of a Gamma
(1/p,1)
random variable, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[M_{d,p}^{(\alpha-d)/p}\right]

\displaystyle=d\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{(\alpha-d)/p}F_{\Gamma}(t)^{d-1}f_{\Gamma}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{d\Gamma((\alpha-(d-1))/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)}\mathbb{P}(\Gamma^{\prime}>\max_{i=1}^{d-1}\Gamma_{i}),

where
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a Gamma
((\alpha-(d-1))/p,1)
random variable, and
(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{d-1}
are i.i.d. Gamma
(1/p,1)
random variables. While these gives analytic and probabilistic expressions for
R(d,p,\alpha)
, they do not allow us to simplify the formulas further.
