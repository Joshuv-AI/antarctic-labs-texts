An oscilloscope based method for pulsed Optically Detected Magnetic Resonance in an ensemble of NV centers in diamond
Anuvab Nandi, Samiran Chakraborti, Himadri Himani, Sumit Mukherjee, Sayan Chakraborty, Chiranjib Mitra

Abstract
In this work, we have demonstrated the acquisition of pulsed ODMR data with the help of a high speed oscilloscope. We show how the on-system data averaging and the memory of the oscilloscope can be utilized to obtain high SNR data at high speeds. The problem of experimental drift and the strategy for eliminating the same has been discussed. Two distinct methods of data acquisition and processing are discussed, and their applicability to different pulsed protocols is investigated. Rabi Oscillations, Ramsey Interferometry, T 1 T_{1} measurement, and spin echo are demonstrated.

Experimental Setup

The experimental setup for the pulsed optically detected magnetic resonance in an ensemble of NV centers is shown in figure
1
. The setup can be conceptually divided into three main parts - the optical part, the microwave part, the control and detection part.

In the optical part, the continous laser light from a
532nm
source (gem 532) is passed through an Acousto-Optic Modulator (Brimrose TEM-200-100-532), and focussed on to the sample with the help of a microscope objective lens (Newport M-40x, NA-0.65). The AOM is placed at the focus of a telescopic arrangement of two plano-convex lens, and is driven by a RF driver. The first order of the diffraction pattern is chosen with the help of an iris, and is modulated to create laser pulses. Another telescopic arrangement expands the beam to overfill the aperture of the objective. When the green laser reflected by the dichroic mirror falls on the sample, a red photoluminiscence is emitted by the NV centers. However, the dichroic mirror is not able to cut-down the large amount of reflected green light from the sample which is present in addition to the red PL. The rest of the undesired light is removed with a 532 nm notch filter, as well as a
600nm
long pass filter (LPF) and a
800nm
short pass filter (SPF). The choice of the filters is guided by the emission spectrum of the NV centers. Following the appropriate spectral filtering, the red PL is passed through a
200\mu m
pinhole, and subsequently focused into the the active region of an analog avalanche photodiode (Thorlabs APD 410A2/M). The signal from the APD is carried by a
50\Omega
BNC cable to the
50\Omega
-terminated channel of the high-speed oscilloscope (Rohde and Schwarz RTM 3004). This impedance matching is crucial for reducing overshoots in the data.

A continous microwave is produced by a signal generator (Anapico APSIN4010). It is passed through the microwave switch (Mini-Circuits ZASWA-2-50-DR+) to form microwave pulses. It is then amplified by the Microwave amplifier (Mini-Circuits ZHL-16W-43-S+), and fed to a microstrip transmission line, which delivers the microwave magnetic field to the diamond sample kept on top of it. The microwave leaving the transmission line is appropriately attenuated and fed to the Spectrum Analyzer (Rohde and Schwarz FPC1500) for monitoring, and power measurements.

The TTL generator (Pulseblaster ESR pro) supplies the TTL control pulses to the microwave switch, and the RF driver of the AOM for the generation of microwave and laser pulses respectively. It also provides trigger signals for the oscilloscope, which is crucial for the stable acquisition of data.

Experimental Methods

We obtain the CW-ODMR spectrum, shown in figure

The general form of a pulse sequence is shown in figure
3
. The first green laser pulse preferentially polarizes the NV spins to to the
m_{s}=0
state. The microwave pulse sequence is applied after a buffer time. This is necessary in order to allow all the NV centers to relax back to
m_{s}=0
from the metastable singlet state. We take this duration to be
1\mu s
, following previous literature(
[
13
]
). The applied microwave pulses create coherent superpositions of the spin states, and the delays in between them allow for free precession. At the beginning of the second laser pulse, we observe a change in the level of the red PL with respect to the one in the maximally polarized state. This change reflects the change in the population of
m_{s}=0
state(
[
17
]
). The rest of the laser pulse serves to initialize the spins back to
m_{s}=0
in preparation for the next sequence. This cycle is repeated with each laser pulse, performing the readout of the previous sequence, and the initialization of the next sequence.

The repolarization rate is found to be
138.07\mu s
from figure
4
. Therefore, in order to completely initialize the ensemble, the duration of the laser pulse is fixed at one order of magnitude higher at
1.5ms
. The duration of the readout is taken to be of the same order as the repolarization rate, at
200\mu s
, because the maximum change in PL is obtained during this period.

Since the APD data collected over one cycle is extremely noisy, we need to collect and average the data over several cycles to reduce the noise. Depending on the strength of the signal and the desired signal to noise ratio, the required number of averages can vary anywhere from a few tens to several thousands. Pulsed ODMR data can be acquired in several ways using a modern oscilloscope. One could directly store the data over a given number of cycles to a flash drive connected to the scope. However, both the size of the data files and the acquisition time would become excessively large, especially when using higher sampling rates. This is primarily owing to the large initialization time in case of ensembles, which increases the length of each cycle. This would limit our data acquisition to lower sampling rates and a few averages, which might be insufficient for achieving the desired SNR. Hence, we try to use the on-board data processing of the oscilloscope. Here, the general strategy will be to use proper triggering and obtain the averaged part of the APD signal on the oscilloscope display. The data is then directly exported to the computer, via LAN or USB, either to to be directly processed in a computer code or to be stored for postprocessing. In doing so, the size of the data acquired from the scope reduces by several orders of magnitude, and so does the acquisition time. There is an additional time overhead for taking the average, but it is relatively small because of the fast hardware processing. A few ways of implementing the aforementioned strategy will be discussed in this work.

The goal of any pulsed ODMR protocol is to study the variation of the photoluminiscence contrast with a certain experimental parameter such as the microwave frequency or microwave pulse sequence. The contrast is the percentage change in PL with respect to a reference PL. The reference PL can be collected in a couple of ways.

The red PL obtained towards the end of the initialization pulse reflects the fully spin-polarized condition, and thus, serves as a good reference. Shown in figure
5
, we call it the maximally polarized reference. In terms of implementation, the oscilloscope is trigerred by the falling edge of the laser sequence. The horizontal scaling of the scope is adjusted so that the
200\mu s
readout time before and after the microwave pulse sequence is contained in the display. We wait for the waveform to be averaged over the given number of cycles, and then acquire the data. An example of acquired waveform is shown in figure
6
. A change in the PL after the application of the microwave pulse is clear. The reference and signal windows are defined with respect to the trigger signal, and the data points enclosed in them are averaged to get
I_{ref}
and
I_{sig}
respectively. Then, the percentage contrast
C[\%]
is expressed as

C[\%]=\left(\frac{I_{sig}}{I_{ref}}-1\right)\cdot 100

(1)

An alternative approach is to use the PL in the absence of the microwave sequence as the reference, as illustrated in figure
7
. However, even in the absence of microwaves, the spins get partly depolarized because of longitudinal relaxation. Hence, we refer to the corrsponding PL as the partially depolarized reference. The depolarization is negligible if the length of the microwave sequence is much smaller than the longitudinal relaxation time
T_{1}
, in which case, it becomes equivalent to the maximally polarized reference. In the experiment, if we use the laser sequence as the trigger like before, we would get incorrect data. On averaging over several cycles, the signal would get smeared over both the signal and reference. Thus, it is imperative to set the correct periodicity in the scope by providing an additional trigger signal from the TTL generator as shown in figure
7
. An example waveform acquired using this method is shown in figure
8
. Following the microwave sequence, there is a large change in PL, while in the absence of the microwave sequence, there is a slight reduction in the PL due to depolarization. As compared to the case of the maximally polarized reference, this technique requires the acquisition of a much larger part of the APD signal, only a fraction of which will finally contribute to the readout. Besides, the maximum achievable sampling rate is reduced, making it a relatively inefficient method. However, it might be more advantageous in certain scenarios as we will see later.

At first glance, there seems to be a simple solution for increasing the efficiency of data acquisition using the partially depolarized reference. That would be to collect the data serially, once in the presence of microwave sequence and then in its absence, while averaging the waveform in each case. However, such a method suffers from the problem of experimental drift, and wrecks havoc to the quality of the signal as would be demonstrated in a different context.

Experimental Results

We use the setup described in section 2 and the methods described in section 3 to implement the common pulsed protocols like
T_{1}
measurement, Rabi Oscillations, Ramsey Interferometry, and Spin Echo. Due to its higher efficiency and ease of implementation, the maximally polarized reference is used in most cases, apart from certain parts of spin echo, where the partially depolarized reference turns out be more useful. In all the experiments, we average over 200 cycles per data point, which takes around 20 seconds. The rest of the parameters like sampling rate vary among the different experiments, and are specified in the respective subsections.

4.1
Rabi Oscillations

Rabi oscillations involve a single microwave pulse whose duration is varied as shown in figure
9
. The laser pulses surrounding it have not been shown for the sake of brevity. The signal and reference data, shown in figure
10
, are quite noisy. Although the reference signal should be independent of the microwave pulse duration, there is a significant variation in its value. This is due to the drift in the oscilloscope waveform over time. This might happen because of several factors including external light sources impinging on the photodetector, or instrumental drifts of the photodetector or the oscilloscope itself. The effect of the drift is more prominent when the gap between two successive acquisitions is high(due to increased sampling rate or number of averages). As the average over several cycles is calculated, the drift affects the signal and the reference in the exact same way. This is evident from the strong correlation in the variation of the reference and the signal. So, when we take the ratio of the two and calculate the contrast, the effect of the drift is eliminated, and we get a signal with very less noise as can be seen in figure
11
(a). This has only been possible because the signal and the reference have been acquired together in the same waveform.

The Rabi oscillation is the most fundamental pulsed ODMR protocol, and determines the parameters for other pulsed protocols. When a microwave magnetic field, resonant with one of the ODMR transitions, is applied along the y-axis, the Bloch vector, initially polarized along
m_{s}=0
starts precessing in the x-z plane of the rotating frame as shown in figure
9
. The frequency of this rotation, or the Rabi frequency, is controlled by the strength of the microwave magnetic field
B_{1}
as
f_{R}=\frac{\gamma B_{1}}{2\pi}
. Thus, the duration of the microwave pulse will control the angle of rotation on the Bloch sphere as
\theta=\gamma B_{1}\tau_{mw}
where
\tau_{mw}
is the duration of the microwave pulse . Because of the interaction with the environment, the oscillation is damped at a rate of
T_{2}^{Rabi}
. The power spectral density (figure
11
(b)) of the temporal data reveals the presence of two distinct frequencies. Thus, the oscillation is fitted by equation
2
, a superposition of two exponentially decaying sinusoids. The secondary peak in the PSD at the higher frequency and lower amplitude arises because of the neighbouring hyperfine transitions which are detuned from the central peak
[
16
]
. From the fit, we get a primary Rabi frequency of
2.5MHz
, and a
T^{2}_{Rabi}
of
1.175\mu s
for a power of
14.74W
. The primary Rabi frequency is directly proportional to the square root of the microwave power, as it varies proportionally with
B_{1}
, and the microwave power is directly proportional to
B_{1}^{2}
. This is corroborated by the experimental data of figure
12
which shows a linear relation between the two. This is a standard way to check if the obtained oscillations are due to Rabi or not. The Rabi frequency is a direct measure of the strength of the microwave field experienced by the sample, and is often used synonymously with the same. From figure
13

4.2
Ramsey Interferometry

In Ramsey interferometry or free induction decay, a microwave
\pi/2
pulse rotates the Bloch vector to the x-y plane, and is allowed to freely precess about the z-axis for a given amount of time. The spins decohere and dephase during this period, with the macroscopic Bloch vector becoming smaller and gradually going inside the Bloch sphere, finally reaching the thermal state, given enough time. The second
\pi/2
pulse rotates the Bloch vector back to the z-axis for measurement. On varying the free precession period in the pulse sequence of figure
16
, we get the Ramsey signal shown in figure
15
. The oscillations decay at a rate of
T_{2}^{*}
, which is the transverse relaxation rate
T_{2}
limited by magnetic field inhomogeneities. On fitting the data to equation
3
, it is found to be
232.03ns
which is very small. This is owing to the magnetic field inhomogeneity originating both from the highly non-uniform Zeeman field of the permanent magnet, and also from the presence of randomly distributed carbon-13 nuclei present in the sample.

T1 measurement

The longitudinal relaxation rate
T_{1}
is measured with an all-optical technique. Two laser pulses are applied, and the time difference between them is varied. As illustrated in figure
17
, the first laser pulse polarizes the NV spins to the
m_{s}=0
state. During the time gap, the population of
m_{s}=0
state gradually decreases and the system moves towards the thermal equilibrium population as illustrated in figure
17

m_{s}=0
state. The longitudinal relaxation curve obtained from this experiment is shown in figure

obtained is
6.274ms

This is owing to the fact that all the NV spins in the optically addressed ensemble, along all four orientations are relaxing, and hence, all of them contribute to the contrast. While in the previous experiments, only the on-resonant and near-resonant spins, which constitutes a very small subensemble, contributed to the signal contrast.

4.4
Spin Echo

The pulse sequence for spin echo is shown in figure
18
. The first
\pi/2
pulse rotates the spin from
m_{s}=0
about the y-axis to the x-y plane. The spins are then allowed to precess freely for a time
T_{dephasing}
, during which they not only dephase but also decohere. The decoherence happens when the individual spins lose their coherence because of their interaction with the environment. On the other hand, dephasing happens when different NV centers experience different
B_{0}
fields, and therefore, precess at sligtly different Larmor frequencies. A
\pi
pulse then rotates the dephased spins about the
y-axis
, following which they are allowed to freely precess again, and they start to rephase. They are allowed to do so for a duration of
T_{rephasing}
. It is noteworthy that this process doesn’t revive the coherence at all. The final
\pi/2
pulse rotates the spins from the x-y plane back to z-axis for measurement. In the first experiment, we vary
T_{rephasing}
while keeping
T_{dephasing}
constant.
When the two become equal, we get an echo as can be seen from the experimental data of figure
19
. Hence, the spin echo sequence gets rid of any effects due to the inhomogeneity of the static magnetic field
B_{0}
. In the next experiment, we set
T_{rephasing}=T_{dephasing}=T_{free}
and vary
T_{free}
. We get the decay curve of figure
20
. The decay rate of this curve gives us
T_{2}^{\alpha}
, the transverse relaxation rate in the absence of inhomogeneities. On fitting this curve with equation
5
, we get
T_{2}^{\alpha}=3.438\mu s
.

Therefore, the envelope of the decay of the revival peaks would give us the coherence time of the NV electron spins in the absence of 13-C nuclei, which we name

The small sub-ensemble of resonant spins undergo spin echo, while the rest of the non-resonant spins undergo longitudinal relaxation. The latter is responsible for the rapidly-decreasing baseline, since we are using the maximally polarized reference. In order to avoid this baseline artefact, we need to normalize with such a reference which corresponds to the longitudinal relaxation/depolarization of the non-resonant spins only. The closest we have to that is the partially depolarized reference, which corresponds to the longitudinal relaxation of the whole ensemble - both resonant and non-resonant spins. On using the latter, the baseline becomes relatively more straight as can be seen in figure

5
Conclusion and outlook

The acquisition of pulsed ODMR data with the help of a high speed oscilloscope has been demonstrated. We describe the trigerring, and data acquisition from the oscilloscope in a lot of details. Two methods of taking the reference data have been described. The partially depolarized reference is found to be better for
T_{1}
-limited measurements while the maximally polarized reference is better for the rest of the cases. Further optimization of the system for faster acquisition is presently being carried out.
