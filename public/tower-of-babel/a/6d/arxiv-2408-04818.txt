Currents in non-equilibrium steady states of open inhomogeneous X ​ X XX -spin chains
Pierre-Antoine Bernard, Ismaël Bussière, Roberto Floreanini, Luc Vinet

Abstract
We investigate spin and heat currents in the non-equilibrium steady state of inhomogeneous X ​ X XX -spin chains, which act as interfaces between two bosonic heat baths. Using special functions that diagonalize the single-particle Hamiltonian, we derive closed-form expressions for these currents. For small temperature differences between the baths, we show that inhomogeneities breaking the mirror symmetry of the chain significantly reduce both heat and spin conductivities. Connections with perfect state transfer are discussed.



I
Introduction

Spin chains offer a convenient testbed to study how microscopic physical features of quantum systems can impact their macroscopic properties. Due to their inherent simplicity and the diverse array of phenomena they showcase, these models have become a well established tool for studying thermalization
Steinigeweg et al. 2013
;
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
, entanglement in quantum many-body systems
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
;
Eisert et al. 2010
;
Calabrese and Cardy 2005
and non-equilibrium steady states in open settings
Prosen and Žnidarič 2009
;
Prosen 2008
;
Prosen 2011
;
De Nardis et al. 2018
;
Žnidarič et al. 2016
.

Non-equilibrium steady states emerge in systems acting as interfaces between reservoirs or heat baths at different temperatures. These states exhibit non-zero expectations of heat and spin currents, making them very useful for modeling the contribution of magnetic excitations to the thermal conductivity of specific materials (e.g. see
Sologubenko et al. 2007
;
Pan et al. 2022
;
Hlubek et al. 2010
for experimental realizations). As a result, there is much interest in examining how currents in these states scale with the chain length and the temperatures of the driving baths.

Various factors significantly influence the scaling, including anisotropy
Karrasch et al. 2015
, inhomogeneous (random) couplings
Zhang et al. 2008
;
Laflorencie et al. 2004
, magnetic fields
Heidrich-Meisner et al. 2005
, the presence of dephasing
Mendoza-Arenas et al. 2013
, and combinations thereof
Lange et al. 2019
;
Lenarčič and Prosen 2015
;
Schulz et al. 2018
;
Žnidarič and Horvat 2013
. Additionally, some spin chains exhibit non-zero rectification, demonstrating asymmetric conductivity
Landi et al. 2014
.

Several approaches have been employed to investigate the effects of these microscopic features. Notable methods include linear response theory
Klümper and Sakai 2002
;
Heidrich-Meisner et al. 2005
, the study of steady states within the light cone induced by an inhomogeneous quench
Langer et al. 2009
;
Biella et al. 2019
, and the construction of solutions to Lindblad equations (e.g.
Prosen 2011
;
Prosen and Žnidarič 2009
). Using the latter, recent works have studied non-equilibrium steady states in homogeneous
XX
-spin chains of size
N
sitting in between bosonic heat baths
Benatti et al. 2021
;
Benatti et al. 2022
. The authors derived the Lindblad equation governing the reduced dynamics and obtained an explicit formula for the density matrix associated with the non-equilibrium steady state. This led to the identification of source and sink terms that characterize the spin and energy currents within the chain.

In this paper, we adopt a similar approach to investigate the conductivity of
XX
-spin chains with inhomogeneous couplings and magnetic fields. While some results exist for heat and spin currents in systems with random, localized, and alternating inhomogeneities, a comprehensive understanding of the effects of general inhomogeneities remains elusive.
A notable unanswered question is whether inhomogeneous chains designed for perfect quantum state transfer
Christandl et al. 2004
;
Vinet and Zhedanov 2012a
exhibit a particular tendency to support spin currents when their ends are coupled to the environment. Originally introduced in quantum information as potential
quantum wires
moving qubits with minimal intervention through their intrinsic dynamics, it is unclear how this property translates to spin conductivity in an open setting.

To address this issue, we focus on the specific case of gapped
XX
-spin chains coupled to bosonic baths, as detailed in
Benatti et al. 2021
;
Benatti et al. 2022
. This model allows for analytical results based on the single-particle dynamics of the system. In particular, we derive formulas that relate spin and heat currents to the special functions used to diagonalize the one-particle Hamiltonian. Our analysis yields several significant observations in different regimes, including:

(i)

Small temperature gap, low temperature
: Heat and spin flow vanish exponentially as the temperature approaches
0
due to the gap. The scaling of the currents with the system size
N
is influenced by the wavefunction of the low energy single excitation states.

(ii)

Small temperature gap, high temperature
: Any spin chain that is symmetric with respect to its center exhibits ballistic transport in this regime. However, this property is fragile; even a small perturbation of the magnetic field can cause a transition to subdiffusive transport.

(iii)

High temperature gap
: The inhomogeneities in the spin chains have minimal impact on the currents in this regime. The heat current is proportional to the energy of a spin-up located at the site adjacent to the bath at the lower temperature, which serves as the exiting bath.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
II.1
, we recall the definition and diagonalization of the Hamiltonian for inhomogeneous spin chains of type
XX
. Two examples are provided in Section
II.2
. Thermal baths interacting with the extremities of the chain are introduced in Section
II.3
, along with the derivation of the associated Lindblad equation. An explicit expression for the density matrix of the non-equilibrium steady state is derived in
III
. The associated spin and thermal currents are studied in Section
IV
.

II
Lindblad equation

II.1
Closed model

We consider inhomogeneous spin chains of type
XX
defined by the following Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}J_{\ell}\left(\sigma^{x}_{\ell}\sigma^{x}_{\ell+1}+\sigma^{y}_{\ell}\sigma^{y}_{\ell+1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}(B_{\ell}+\Delta)\left(1+\sigma^{z}_{\ell}\right),

(1)

where
J_{n}
and
B_{n}
are nearest-neighbor couplings and local magnetic field strengths, respectively and
\sigma_{\ell}^{x},\sigma_{\ell}^{y},\sigma_{\ell}^{z}
are Pauli matrices acting on site
\ell
. The parameter
\Delta
is a constant representing the strength of a constant background magnetic field.
By means of a Jordan-Wigner transformation,

a^{\dagger}_{n}=e^{-i\pi\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\sigma^{+}_{\ell}\sigma^{-}_{\ell}}\sigma^{+}_{n},

(2)

where
\sigma_{\ell}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma^{x}_{\ell}\pm i\sigma_{\ell}^{y}\right)
, the Hamiltonian (
1
) can be recast as a model of free fermions hopping on a chain,

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}J_{n}\left(a^{\dagger}_{n}a_{n+1}+a^{\dagger}_{n+1}a_{n}\right)+\sum_{n=0}^{N}(B_{n}+\Delta)a^{\dagger}_{n}a_{n},

(3)

where
a^{\dagger}_{n}
and
a_{n}
are fermionic creation and annihilation operators satisfying
\left\{a^{\dagger}_{n},a_{m}\right\}=\delta_{nm}
and
\left\{a_{n},a_{m}\right\}=0
. The diagonalization of this Hamiltonian requires considering its restriction to the subspace of single excitations. Let us denote
\left|\bm{0}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle
the state where all spins are down, which is annihilated by all operators
a_{n}
,

a_{n}\left|\bm{0}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle=0,\ \forall n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}

(4)

The action of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
on states
\ket{n}=a_{n}^{\dagger}\left|\bm{0}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle
with a single spin up at position
n
is

\mathcal{H}\ket{n}=J_{n}\ket{n+1}+B_{n}\ket{n}+J_{n-1}\ket{n-1}

(5)

which corresponds to a tridiagonal matrix
\mathrm{H}+\Delta
, with

\mathrm{H}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&J_{0}&0&\dots&0\\
J_{0}&B_{1}&J_{1}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&J_{1}&B_{2}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&J_{N-1}\\
0&\dots&0&J_{N-1}&B_{N}\end{pmatrix}

(6)

The spectral problem for
\mathrm{H}
is solved by introducing wavefunctions
\phi_{n}(x_{k})
that satisfy the three term recurrence relation

x_{k}\phi_{n}(x_{k})=J_{n}\phi_{n+1}(x_{k})+B_{n}\phi_{n}(x_{k})+J_{n-1}\phi_{n-1}(x_{k})\ ,

(7)

and the orthonormality conditions,

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi_{n}(x_{k})\phi_{n}(x_{k^{\prime}})=\delta_{kk^{\prime}},\quad\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{n}(x_{k})\phi_{m}(x_{k})=\delta_{nm}\ .

(8)

These relations can be understood in terms of an orthogonal transition matrix
U
with
U_{nk}=\phi_{n}(x_{k})
as

\text{diag}(x_{k}+\Delta)=U^{T}HU

(9)

and

UU^{T}=1,\qquad U^{T}U=1

(10)

It clearly follows that the
\phi(x_{k})
are the wavefunctions of single-excitation stationary states with energy
x_{k}+\Delta
,

(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\sum_{n}\phi_{n}(x_{k})\ket{n}=(x_{k}+\Delta)\sum_{n}\phi_{n}(x_{k})\ket{n}\ .

(11)

These wavefunctions can always be expressed in terms of discrete orthogonal polynomial families as will be discussed in section
II.2
.
Furthermore, they allow the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}
to be rewritten in a diagonal form as

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}(x_{k}+\Delta)b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}

(12)

where
b_{k}=\sum_{n}\phi_{n}(x_{k})a_{n}
and
b_{k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{n}\phi_{n}(x_{k})a_{n}^{\dagger}
are new pairs of fermionic creation and annihilation operators. A complete eigenbasis is obtained by the application of creation operators
b^{\dagger}_{k}
on the state
\left|\bm{0}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle
, with eigenvectors labeled by a binary vector
\bm{n}
representing the empty and occupied modes,

\left|\bm{n}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle=\prod_{k=0}^{N}(b_{k}^{\dagger})^{\bm{n}_{k}}\left|\bm{0}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle

(13)

These eigenstates have energies
E_{\bm{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\bm{n}_{k}\left(x_{k}+\Delta\right)
. In the following, we restrict ourselves to the case where the background magnetic field is large enough so that all modes have positive energy
x_{k}+\Delta>0
, making
\left|\bm{0}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle
the ground state.

The choice of different inhomogeneous coefficients
J_{\ell}
and
B_{\ell}
affects only the energies
x_{k}+\Delta
and wavefunctions
\phi_{n}(x_{k})
of the single-excitation stationary states. This is illustrated in the following subsections with two examples.

II.2
Two examples

Since the wavefunctions
\phi_{n}(x_{k})
are solutions to a three-term recurrence relation, Favard’s theorem implies that they can be expressed in terms of orthogonal polynomials. Consequently, the recurrence coefficients of known families of orthogonal polynomials provide examples of inhomogeneous spin chains that can be exactly diagonalized. As examples, we consider the homogeneous and Krawtchouk chains. The former is characterized by

J_{n}=1,\quad B_{n}=0\ .

(14)

The associated single excitation energy spectrum
x_{k}
and wavefunctions
\phi_{n}(x_{k})
are well-known
Crampé et al. 2021
to be given by

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{N+2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+2}}\sin\left(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{N+2}\right)U_{n}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+2}}\sin\left(\frac{(n+1)(k+1)\pi}{N+2}\right),

(15)

where
U_{n}(x)
are the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind.

The Krawtchouk spin chain
Christandl et al. 2004
;
Vinet and Zhedanov 2012a
is characterized by nearest-neighbor couplings that peak at the center of the chain and a linearly increasing magnetic field,

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}J_{n}=\sqrt{p(1-p)}\sqrt{(n+1)(N-n)},\\
B_{n}=p(N-n)+(1-p)n\ ,\end{gathered}

with
p
as a free parameter in the interval
]0,1[
. The associated single excitation energy spectrum
x_{k}
and wavefunctions
\phi_{n}(x_{k})
are

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}x_{k}=k\end{gathered}

and

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\phi_{n}(x_{k})=\sqrt{\begin{pmatrix}N\\
k\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}N\\
n\end{pmatrix}p^{k+n}(1-p)^{N-k-n}}K_{n}(k;p,N)\end{gathered}

where
K_{n}(x;p,N)
are the Krawtchouk polynomials
Koekoek et al. 2010
.

Note that both the homogeneous chain and the Krawtchouk chain with
p=1/2
exhibit mirror symmetry, meaning they have coefficients such that
J_{n}=J_{N-n-1}
and
B_{n}=B_{N-n}
. This mirror symmetry is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the chain to achieve perfect state transfer
Kay 2010
;
Vinet and Zhedanov 2012a
. Perfect state transfer occurs when a single spin-up localized at one end of the chain is transferred to the opposite end at a specified time
T
with probability
1
, i.e.

|\bra{0}e^{-iTH}\ket{N}|=1.

(20)

This phenomenon does not occur in the homogeneous chain but does occur at time

T=\pi
in the Krawtchouk chain with

p=1/2

Albanese et al. 2004
.

II.3
Open model

We recall the derivation from
Benatti et al. 2021
for the Lindblad equation associated with an
XX
spin chain which interacts with two auxiliary bosonic thermal bath described by the following Hamiltonians:

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ \nu\mathfrak{b}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\nu)\mathfrak{b}_{\alpha}(\nu),\qquad\alpha\in\{0,N\}

(21)

where
\mathfrak{b}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
satisfy the canonical bosonic commutation relations

\left[\mathfrak{b}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\nu),\mathfrak{b}_{\beta}(\nu^{\prime})\right]=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(\nu-\nu^{\prime}).

(22)

The coupling between the baths and the chain is mediated by the following interaction Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}_{I}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,N\}}\left(\sigma^{+}_{\alpha}\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha}+\sigma^{-}_{\alpha}\mathfrak{B}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\right)

(23)

where

\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\ h_{\alpha}(\nu)\mathfrak{b}_{\alpha}(\nu)

(24)

with
h_{\alpha}(\nu)
, real suitable smearing functions. This interaction Hamiltonian is appropriate only when all energies of the closed spin chain are positive. A more general interaction Hamiltonian as been considered for the homogeneous chain in
Benatti et al. 2022
.

For the case we are studying, the complete Hamiltonian is

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathcal{H}+\lambda\mathcal{H}_{I}+\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{N}

(25)

where
\lambda\ll 0
is a dimensionless coupling parameter.

We consider the two baths to be at thermal equilibirum at temperature
T_{\alpha}=1/\beta_{\alpha}
so that we can write their density matrices as

\rho_{\alpha}=\frac{e^{-\beta_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta_{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}\right)}

(26)

Assuming that there is weak coupling between the chain and the baths, the baths remain in their thermal state which means the density matrix of the total system can be written as

\rho_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=\frac{e^{-\beta_{0}\mathcal{H}_{0}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta_{0}\mathcal{H}_{0}}\right)}\otimes\rho(t)\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta_{N}\mathcal{H}_{N}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta_{N}\mathcal{H}_{N}}\right)}

(27)

where
\rho(t)
is the reduced density matrix that carries the dynamics of the chain. From the Von Neumann equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=-i\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot}},\rho_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\right],

(28)

assuming weak coupling and perfoming the secular approximation, one can derive the Lindblad master equation (as is done in the Appendix) which governs the evolution of the reduced density
\rho(t)
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho(t)=-i\left[\mathcal{H}+\lambda^{2}H_{LS},\rho(t)\right]+\mathds{D}[\rho(t)]=\mathds{L}[\rho]

(29)

where
\mathcal{H}_{LS}
, the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian corrects the energy levels and
\mathds{D}[\rho(t)]
is the dissipative contribution to the otherwise unitary evolution of
\rho(t)
. For the open spin chain, the Lamb-shift Hamiltonian is

\mathcal{H}_{LS}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\left(s_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}+\tilde{s}_{k}b_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right)

(30)

and the dissipative contribution is

\displaystyle\mathds{D}[\rho]=\lambda^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}

\displaystyle\left(d_{k}\left(b_{k}^{\dagger}\rho(t)b_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho(t),b_{x}b_{x}^{\dagger}\right\}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\tilde{d}_{k}\left(b_{k}\rho(t)b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho(t),b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\right\}\right)\right)

(31)

where

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}d_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,N\}}2\pi\phi_{\alpha}(x_{k})^{2}h_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)^{2}(n_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)+1)\\
\tilde{d}_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,N\}}2\pi\phi_{\alpha}(x_{k})^{2}h_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)^{2}n_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)\end{gathered}

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}s_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,N\}}\phi_{\alpha}(x_{k})^{2}\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ \frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}(\nu)(n_{\alpha}(\nu)+1)}{x_{k}+\Delta-\nu}\\
\tilde{s}_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,N\}}\phi_{\alpha}(x_{k})^{2}\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ \frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}(\nu)n_{\alpha}(\nu)}{\nu-x_{k}-\Delta}\ .\end{gathered}

with
n_{\alpha}(\omega)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{\alpha}\omega}-1}
.

III
Non-equilibrium steady state

Due to the dissipative term in the Lindblad equation, any initial state of the chain will converge after sufficient time to a non-equilibrium steady state, whose density matrix
\rho_{\infty}
satisfies

-i\left[\mathcal{H}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{H}_{LS},\rho_{\infty}\right]+\mathds{D}[\rho_{\infty}]=0.

(36)

The density matrix
\rho_{\infty}
was explicitly derived for a homogeneous chain in
Benatti et al. 2021
. We recall this approach here, which also applies to inhomogeneous spin chains. Consider the ansatz given by the matrix
Y
, which is a sum of eigenstate projectors:

Y\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{n}}y_{\bm{n}}\left|\bm{n}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}\right|

(37)

The commutator
\left[Y,\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{H}_{LS}\right]
from the Lindbladian acting on
Y
is zero because
\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{H}_{LS}
acts diagonally on these projectors. The action of the dissipator on the eigenstate projectors is

\displaystyle\mathds{D}[\left|\bm{n}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}\right|]=\lambda^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}

\displaystyle\left(n_{k}d_{k}-(1-n_{k})\tilde{d}_{k}\right)\times

\displaystyle\left(\left|\bm{n}_{0_{k}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}_{0_{k}}\right|-\left|\bm{n}_{1_{k}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}_{1_{k}}\right|\right)

(38)

where
\bm{n}_{i_{k}}
means the binary vector
\bm{n}
with its
k
-th component replaced by
i
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathds{D}[Y]=\lambda^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{\hat{\bm{n}}_{k}}

\displaystyle\left(d_{k}y_{\bm{n}_{1_{k}}}-\tilde{d}_{k}y_{\bm{n}_{0_{k}}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\left(\left|\bm{n}_{0_{k}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}_{0_{k}}\right|-\left|\bm{n}_{1_{k}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}_{1_{k}}\right|\right)

(39)

where the sum over
\hat{\bm{n}}_{k}
means summing over all binary vectors
\bm{n}
with the
k
-th component fixed. Looking at the coefficient, one can see that by choosing

y_{\bm{n}}=\prod_{k=0}^{N}\left(n_{k}\tilde{d}_{k}+(1-n_{k})d_{k}\right)

(40)

the coefficient in (
39
) will vanish. The following density matrix then describes the non-equilibrium steady state of the spin chain

\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{\bm{n}}\lambda_{\bm{n}}\left|\bm{n}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{n}\right|

where

\lambda_{\bm{n}}=\frac{y_{\bm{n}}}{\prod_{k=0}^{N}(d_{k}+\tilde{d}_{k})}

(41)

so that
\sum_{\bm{n}}\lambda_{\bm{n}}=1
.

It can be shown that for equal bath temperatures and identical smearing functions for the two baths, this is the same as the thermal equilibrium state
Benatti et al. 2021
,

\rho_{\infty}=\frac{e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}}\right)}\ .

IV
Heat and spin currents

We now characterize the spin and heat currents in the non-equilibrium steady state.

Given a solution
\rho(t)
of the Lindblad master equation (
29
), the associated spin flow in the chain is defined in terms of the time derivative of the total spin operator, i.e.

\mathfrak{Q}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{n}\left\langle\sigma^{z}_{n}\right\rangle=\sum_{n}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma^{z}_{n}\rho(t)\right)=\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma^{z}_{n}\mathds{L}[\rho]\right).

(42)

Using the cyclity of the trace, this expression can be rewritten in terms of an
adjoint Lindbladian

\tilde{\mathds{L}}
as

\mathfrak{Q}=\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\mathds{L}}[\sigma^{z}_{n}]\rho\right)\\

(43)

where

\tilde{\mathds{L}}[\mathcal{O}]=i[\mathcal{H}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{H}_{LS},\mathcal{O}]+\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{0}[\mathcal{O}]+\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{N}[\mathcal{O}].

(44)

The operators
\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{0}
and
\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{N}
are the
adjoint dissipators
. They respectively capture the dissipative effects due to the baths interacting at site
0
and
N
, and have the following action on an operator
\mathcal{O}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{\alpha}[\mathcal{O}]=\sum_{k}\phi_{\alpha}(x_{k})

\displaystyle\!\left(C_{\alpha,k}\left(b_{k}^{\dagger}\mathcal{O}b_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k},\mathcal{O}\right\}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\ \left.+\tilde{C}_{\alpha,k}\left(b_{k}\mathcal{O}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{b_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger},\mathcal{O}\right\}\right)\right),

(45)

with coefficients
C_{\alpha,k}
and
\tilde{C}_{\alpha,k}
given by

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}C_{\alpha,k}=2\pi h_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)^{2}(n_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)+1),\\
\tilde{C}_{\alpha,k}=2\pi h_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)^{2}n_{\alpha}(x_{k}+\Delta)\end{gathered}\ .

For currents in the non-equilibrium steady state, the contribution from the commutator vanishes, allowing us to discard it in (
44
). The adjoint dissipators separate the contributions from each bath to the spin flow, so that
\mathfrak{Q}
decomposes into components coming from the left and right bath,

\mathfrak{Q}=\mathfrak{Q}_{L}+\mathfrak{Q}_{R}

(48)

with

\mathfrak{Q}_{L}=\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{0}[\sigma^{z}_{n}]\rho\right),\qquad\mathfrak{Q}_{R}=\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\mathds{D}}_{N}[\sigma^{z}_{n}]\rho\right).

(49)

Since
\rho_{\infty}
is independent of time, it follows from equation (
42
) that the spin flow
\mathfrak{Q}
in the non-equilibrium steady state vanishes.
Consequently, the contributions from each bath are inversely related,
\mathfrak{Q}_{L}=-\mathfrak{Q}_{R}
, indicating a spin current flowing from one side of the chain to the other. Applying formula (
45
) and specializing to the case
\rho=\rho_{\infty}
, one can show that the spin flow
\mathfrak{Q}_{L}
coming from the left bath is given by

{\mathfrak{Q}}_{L}={2\lambda^{2}}\sum_{k}\frac{\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}\left(C_{0,k}\tilde{C}_{N,k}-\tilde{C}_{0,k}C_{N,k}\right)}{\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}C_{0,k}+\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}\tilde{C}_{N,k}+\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}\tilde{C}_{0,k}+\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}C_{N,k}}.

(50)

A similar approach can be applied to study the heat flow
\mathfrak{h}(t)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\langle\mathcal{H}\rho\right\rangle
in the non-equilibrium steady state

\rho_{\infty}
, along with the associated thermal currents

\mathfrak{h}_{L}
and
\mathfrak{h}_{R}
due to each bath. An analogous derivation shows that

\mathfrak{h}_{L}=-\mathfrak{h}_{R}

, and

\mathfrak{h}_{L}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{k}\frac{(x_{k}+\Delta)\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}\left(C_{0,k}\tilde{C}_{N,k}-\tilde{C}_{0,k}C_{N,k}\right)}{\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}C_{0,k}+\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}\tilde{C}_{N,k}+\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}\tilde{C}_{0,k}+\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}C_{N,k}}.

(51)

The smearing functions
h_{\alpha}
appear in the interaction Hamiltonian of equation (
23
), modulating the coupling strengths between each mode in the bath and the ends of the chain and allowing the introduction of a possible cutoff. In cases where the cutoff for the Bosonic fields has little impact on the physics at the energy scale of the spin chain, the smearing functions should vary slowly at this energy scale, allowing us to set
h_{0}(\omega)=h_{N}(\omega)=h
. Then, the average stationary spin and thermal flow simplify to

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{Q}}_{L}=4\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}\times

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\phi_0(x_k)^2\phi_N(x_k)^2(n_0(x_k + \Delta)-n_N(x_k + \Delta))}{\phi_0(x_k)^2(2n_0(x_k + \Delta)+1)+\phi_N(x_k)^2(2n_N(x_k + \Delta)+1)}$}}

(52)

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{L}=2\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}\times

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{(x_k + \Delta)\phi_0(x_k)^2\phi_N(x_k)^2(n_0(x_k + \Delta)-n_N(x_k + \Delta))}{\phi_0(x_k)^2(2n_0(x_k + \Delta)+1)+\phi_N(x_k)^2(2n_N(x_k + \Delta)+1)}$}}

(53)

These expressions simplify further for spin chains symmetric about their center, i.e. when

J_{i}=J_{N-1-i}
and
B_{i}=B_{N-i}
. In this case, the special functions diagonalizing the single-particle Hamiltonian
\mathrm{H}
defined in (
6
) verify
\phi_{0}(x_{k})^{2}=\phi_{N}(x_{k})^{2}
. The spin and heat flows can thus be expressed in terms of the first entry of the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix
\mathrm{H}+\Delta
,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{Q}}_{L}

\displaystyle={2\pi\lambda^{2}}\bra{0}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{0}+\beta_{N}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}\ket{0},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{L}

\displaystyle=\pi\lambda^{2}\bra{0}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{0}+\beta_{N}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}\ket{0}.

(54)

For a general non-symmetric chain, this simplication is not possible, but one can still use the triangle inequality to derive simple upper bound on the spin and heat flow,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{Q}}_{L}\leq{2\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}}\sum_{k}

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{0}(x_{k})\phi_{N}(x_{k})\right|\times

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(x_{k}+\Delta)\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\beta_{N}(x_{k}+\Delta)\right)\sinh\left(\beta_{0}(x_{k}+\Delta)\right)}},

(55)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{L}\leq\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}\sum_{k}

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{0}(x_{k})\phi_{N}(x_{k})\right|\times

\displaystyle\frac{(x_{k}+\Delta)\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(x_{k}+\Delta)\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\beta_{N}(x_{k}+\Delta)\right)\sinh\left(\beta_{0}(x_{k}+\Delta)\right)}}.

(56)

Note that when the temperature gap between the two baths is small, the left and right terms in these relations are equal at leading order for a mirror-symmetric chain, demonstrating that the bound is strict and that chains symmetric with respect to their center exhibit optimal conductivity in the small temperature gap limit.

By applying the triangle inequality a second time, one can further derive a less strict bound, which has the advantage of also being expressed in terms of the entries of a matrix,

\begin{split}{\mathfrak{Q}}_{L}&\leq{\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}}\bra{0}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\beta_{N}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)\sinh\left(\beta_{0}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}}\ket{0}+{\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}}\bra{N}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\beta_{N}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)\sinh\left(\beta_{0}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}}\ket{N},\end{split}

(57)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{h}_{L}&\leq\frac{\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\bra{0}\frac{(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\beta_{N}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)\sinh\left(\beta_{0}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}}\ket{0}+\frac{\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\bra{N}\frac{(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\sinh\left(\frac{\beta_{N}-\beta_{0}}{2}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\beta_{N}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)\sinh\left(\beta_{0}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}}\ket{N}.\end{split}

(58)

In the following subsection, we apply these formulas and bounds to study how the spin and heat flow are affected by the temperature

T
and the size of the system
N
, when the temperature difference between the baths is small,

IV.1
Small difference in temperature
|T_{0}-T_{N}|

According to Fourier’s law, the heat flow
q:=\mathfrak{h}_{L}
is related to the temperature gradient
\nabla T
through a linear relation

q=-\kappa\nabla T,

(59)

where
\kappa
is a constant defined as the thermal conductivity. Heat diffusion is said to be
anomalous
when
\kappa
has a dependency on the size of the system
Liu et al. 2014
. Here, we compute the thermal conductivity
\kappa
for mirror symmetric
XX
spin chain in the limit where the gap in temperature is small. We further investigate how the temperature and inhomogeneities affect the type of anomalous transport it exhibits.

In terms of the mean temperature
(T_{0}+T_{N})/2=T
(which approximate the temperature in the chain) and the temperature gap between the bath
(T_{0}-T_{N})=\delta T
, one finds that the heat flow of equation (
54
) is rewritten as

\mathfrak{h}_{L}=\pi\lambda^{2}h^{2}\bra{0}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{2\delta T}{4T^{2}-\delta T^{2}}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{4T}{4T^{2}-\delta T^{2}}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}\ket{0}.

(60)

Thus, for a small temperature gap
|\delta T|\ll T
, the matrix arising in formula (
60
) reduces to

\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{2\delta T}{4T^{2}-\delta T^{2}}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{4T}{4T^{2}-\delta T^{2}}(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)\right)}=\frac{\delta T(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)}{2T^{2}\sinh\left(\frac{(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)}{T}\right)}+o(\delta T).

(61)

Further making the identification
\delta T=-N\nabla T
, the right-hand side of (
54
) reduces to Fourier’s law with a thermal conductivity given by

\kappa=\frac{\pi\lambda^{2}h^{2}N}{2T^{2}}\bra{0}\frac{(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)^{2}}{\sinh\left(\frac{\mathrm{H}+\Delta}{T}\right)}\ket{0}

(62)

In particular, it is possible to determine the relation between the conductivity and the temperature in regime of low or high temperature

\kappa\sim\frac{\pi\lambda^{2}h^{2}N}{T^{2}}\bra{0}{(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)^{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathrm{H}+\Delta}{T}}\ket{0},\quad\quad T\ll 1,

(63)

\kappa\sim\frac{\pi\lambda^{2}h^{2}N}{2T}(B_{0}+\Delta),\quad\quad T\gg 1.

(64)

In the high-temperature regime, conductivity is inversely proportional to temperature as most bosons have energies exceeding the energy spectrum of the spin chain. It is interesting to note that at this range of temperature the magnetic field
B_{0}=B_{N}
at the ends of the chain is the only parameter influencing conductivity and that
\kappa\propto N
, indicating anomalous heat transport and ballistic transport for any (mirror symmetric) inhomogeneous
XX
spin chain. The case of non-mirror symmetric chain in this regime is discussed in the following subsection.

In the low-temperature regime, conductivity decreases exponentially as the temperature is reduced. This is because the spin chains considered are gapped, where at low temperatures, the thermal bath lacks the necessary energy to create magnetic excitations in the spin chain. Equation (
62
) shows that heat conductivity at low temperature can be influenced both by the single particle excitation energies (i.e. spectrum of
\mathrm{H}
) and the overlaps
\phi_{0}(x_{k})=\phi_{N}(x_{k})
of the associated modes with local excitation at the ends of the chain. Therefore, different type of transport can be observed depending on the inhomogeneous couplings
J_{i}
and local magnetic fields
B_{i}
.

As example, we compare different chains whose single excitation energy spectrum
x_{k}+\Delta
are scaled to be from
E_{min}
to
E_{max}
. For the homogeneous
XX
spin chain in the
N\rightarrow\infty
and
T\ll E_{min}
limit, one finds ballistic transport (
\kappa\propto N
),

\kappa\approx\frac{4\sqrt{\pi}\lambda^{2}h^{2}E_{min}^{2}e^{-E_{min}/T}}{\sqrt{T}(E_{max}-E_{min})^{2}}N

(65)

In contrast, for the Krawtchouk chain with

p=1/2
, we can compute (
62
) exactly and observe a conductivity decreases with the size of the chain, indicating sub-diffusive transport at leading order,

\kappa\approx\frac{\pi\lambda^{2}h^{2}E_{min}^{2}N}{T^{2}2^{N}}e^{-E_{min}/T}.

(66)

This indicates that a spin chain’s ability to achieve perfect state transfer does not guarantee efficient scaling of heat flow at low temperatures. Indeed, the homogeneous chain, which does not exhibit perfect state transfer, shows ballistic transport, whereas the Krawtchouk chain, which allows for perfect state transfer, exhibits sub-diffusive transport in this regime.

An explanation for the difference between the two chains lie in the probability
|\phi_{0}(x_{k})|^{2}=|\phi_{N}(x_{k})|^{2}
of an excitation of energy
x_{k}+\Delta
in the chain to visit either ends. This quantity is much smaller in the Krawtchouk case for most energies. Indeed, for the Krawtchouk case we have for
k=0,1,\dots,N
,

|\phi_{0}(x_{k})|^{2}=\binom{N}{k}\frac{1}{2^{N}},\quad x_{k}+\Delta=(E_{max}-E_{min})\frac{k}{N}+E_{min}

(67)

This is exponentially vanishing as
N
increases, except for
k\approx N/2
and
x_{k}+\Delta\approx\frac{E_{max}+E_{min}}{2}
. In the homogeneous chain, the probability of each mode to reach the ends decreases much more slowly as

1/N
,

|\phi_{0}(x_{k})|^{2}=\frac{8(E_{max}-x_{k}-\Delta)(x_{k}+\Delta-E_{min})}{(N+1)(E_{max}-E_{min})^{2}}.

(68)

At low temperatures, when only the lowest energy modes are excited, these modes can still facilitate heat transport.

IV.2
Ballistic to subdiffusive transition

Formulas (
64
) shows that any inhomogeneous chain with mirror symmetric couplings
J_{i}
and local magnetic field
B_{i}
exhibit ballistic transport at high temperature. We now show that this property can be highly sensible to the couplings in the chain and vanish once the symmetry is broken. In the high temperature and small temperature gap limit, the inequality (
56
) reduces to

\mathfrak{h}_{L}\leq\frac{\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}\delta T}{T}M(\mathrm{H}+\Delta),

(69)

where

M(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)=\sum_{k}|\phi_{0}(x_{k})\phi_{N}(x_{k})|(x_{k}+\Delta).

(70)

This implies the following upper bound on the conductivity
\kappa
,

\kappa\leq\frac{2\pi h^{2}\lambda^{2}}{T}M(\mathrm{H}+\Delta),

(71)

Note that the coefficient
M(\mathrm{H}+\Delta)
is the only part that depends on the size of the chain
N
and the choice of parameters
J_{i}
and
B_{i}
. In the case where this coefficient decreases with
N
, we can thus deduce that the transport is subdiffusive. First, we consider this quantity for homogeneous and Krawtchouk chain (
p=1/2
) perturbed by a magnetic field whose strength is linear in the position
i
in the chain, i.e.

B_{i}\rightarrow B_{i}+\xi\frac{i}{N},

(72)

where
\xi
is the strength of the perturbation.

IV.3
High temperature gap regime

When the two baths have very different temperatures (say

n_{0}(x_{k}+\Delta)
will be dominant and taking the limit
\beta_{0}\to 0

This and the monotonicity of the currents with respect to bath temperatures means that the currents never go past these strengths as

V
Discussion

We studied spin chains with arbitrary nearest-neighbor couplings with their extremities in heat baths. An exact expression for the density matrix of the non-equilibrium steady state was found in terms of the spectrum and stationary state wavefunctions of the single-particle Hamiltonian. Similar results were derived for the associated spin and heat currents. The scaling of these currents with the size of the chains was investigated in different regimes dictated by the mean temperature and the temperature difference of the baths. The low-temperature regime was identified as one where the inhomogeneities have a significant impact, with ballistic or diffusive transport depending on the wavefunction of the stationary states. At high temperatures, it was observed that chains with symmetric couplings and magnetic fields with respect to the middle enabled ballistic transport. This property was shown to be easily lost in non mirror-symmetric chains.

While one might have expected to observe higher currents in chains with perfect state transfer, this was not found to be the case. The homogeneous chain, which does not allow perfect state transfer, had a low-temperature thermal conductivity scaling with

N
, whereas the Krawtchouk chain with

p=1/2
, which exhibits perfect state transfer, had a thermal conductivity that vanishes exponentially with the size of the system in the same regime. Still, it is interesting to note that both perfect state transfer and ballistic transport at high temperatures require the chain to be mirror symmetric.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Lindblad master equation

For sake of completeness we collect in this Appendix the derivation
of the Lindblad master equation governing the reduced dynamics of an inhomogeneous spin chain weakly interacting with thermal baths (for general references on this subject, see
Benatti and Floreanini 2005
;
Rivas and Huelga 2012
;
Alicki and Lendi 2007
.)

We consider a composite system whose Hamiltonian is given by

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{H}_{\text{env}}+\lambda\mathcal{H}_{I}

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hamiltonian of the system we are interested in,
\mathcal{H}_{E}
is the Hamiltonian of the environment in which evolves the system and
\mathcal{H}_{I}
is an Hamiltonian describing the interactions between the system and the environment. The interaction Hamiltonian can be decomposed as

\mathcal{H}_{I}=\sum_{i}E_{i}\otimes S_{i}

(78)

with

\left\langle E_{i}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{env}}(E_{i})=0

(79)

This choice is justified because these mean values were non-zero, one could always absorb those in the system’s Hamiltonian by defining

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}+\sum_{i}\left\langle E_{i}\right\rangle S_{i}\ .

(80)

For the open spin chain system this means that

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}E_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ h_{0}(\nu)\mathfrak{a}_{0}(\nu)\\
E_{1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ h_{N}(\nu)\mathfrak{a}_{N}(\nu)\\
E_{2}=E_{0}^{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ h_{0}(\nu)\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{\dagger}(\nu)\\
E_{3}=E_{1}^{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ h_{N}(\nu)\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{\dagger}(\nu)\end{gathered}

and

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}S_{0}=\sigma_{0}^{+}\\
S_{1}=\sigma_{N}^{+}\\
S_{2}=S_{0}^{\dagger}=\sigma_{0}^{-}\\
S_{3}=S_{1}^{\dagger}=\sigma_{N}^{-}\ .\end{gathered}

Starting with the von Neumann equation

\dot{\rho}_{\text{tot}}(t)=-i\left[\mathcal{H}_{\text{tot}},\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)\right],

(89)

one can go to the interaction picture where interaction operators
\hat{O}
take the form

\hat{O}(t)=e^{i(H+H_{E})t}Oe^{-i(H+H_{E})t}

(90)

In this picture, the von Neumann equation reads

\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=-i\lambda\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\right]\ .

(91)

This can be solved as a perturbative series in
\lambda

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\lambda\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t),\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(s),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\right]\right]+O(\lambda^{3})\ .

(92)

Assuming weak coupling, higher order terms can be discarded. Tracing over the environment, this becomes an equation for the evolution of the reduced density matrix of the system

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\lambda\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t),\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(s),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\right]\right]

(93)

The condition
\left\langle E_{i}\right\rangle=0,\forall i
makes the first term vanish. The only term left is the quadratic term which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)=-\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{Tr}_{E}

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t-s)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\right.

\displaystyle\quad-\left.\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t-s)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t)\right)+h.c.

(94)

To progress further, we decompose the system operators
S_{i}
in term of transition energies
\omega=\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon
for the system

S_{i}(\omega)=\sum_{\varepsilon}\Pi(\varepsilon)S_{i}\Pi(\varepsilon+\omega)\ .

(95)

where
\Pi(\varepsilon)
is the projector on the eigenspace of eigenvalue
\varepsilon
of the system Hamiltonian
H
. These
S_{i}(\omega)
are what is called the Lindblad operators. It is readily seen that

S_{i}=\sum_{\omega}S_{i}(\omega)\ .

(96)

Using the definition (
95
), we have

S_{0}(\omega)=\sum_{\epsilon}\Pi(\epsilon)\sigma_{0}^{+}\Pi(\epsilon+\omega)\ .

(97)

Performing an inverse Jordan-Wigner transformation, using the eigenbasis
\left|\bm{q}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle
and going to the diagonal fermionic operators, this becomes

S_{0}(\omega)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{0}(x_{k})\sum_{\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime}|E_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}-E_{\bm{q}}=\omega}\left|\bm{q}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{q}\right|b_{k}^{\dagger}\left|\bm{q}^{\prime}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{q}^{\prime}\right|

(98)

Clearly, it must be that
\left|\bm{q}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle=b_{k}^{\dagger}\left|\bm{q}^{\prime}\middle\rangle\!\right\rangle
, therefore the only allowed transition energies are the single mode energies
E_{k}
. The only non zero cases are then

\displaystyle S_{0}(-E_{k})

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(x_{k})b_{k}^{\dagger}\sum_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}\left|\bm{q}^{\prime}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{q}^{\prime}\right|b_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\left|\bm{q}^{\prime}\middle\rangle\!\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\!\middle\langle\bm{q}^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(x_{k})b_{k}^{\dagger}

(99)

Similarly one can find the other Lindblad operators which are

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}S_{1}(-E_{k})=\phi_{N}(x_{k})b_{k}^{\dagger}\\
S_{2}(E_{k})=(-1)^{\sum_{y=0}^{N}b_{y}^{\dagger}b_{y}}\phi_{0}(x_{k})b_{k}\\
S_{3}(E_{k})=(-1)^{\sum_{y=0}^{N}b_{y}^{\dagger}b_{y}}\phi_{N}(x_{k})b_{k}\end{gathered}

In the interaction picture,

\hat{S}_{i}(\omega,t)=e^{-i\omega t}S_{i}(\omega)\ .

(103)

The interaction Hamiltonian can therefore be rewritten as

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}(t)=\sum_{i,\omega}e^{-i\omega t}S_{i}(\omega)\otimes\hat{E}_{i}(t)=\sum_{i,\omega}e^{i\omega t}S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\otimes\hat{E}_{i}^{\dagger}(t)

(104)

where the last equality follow from the hermicity of
\hat{H}_{I}(t)
. The evolution for the reduced density can be then rewritten with the help of (
104
) as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)=-\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime},i,j}e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\Gamma_{ij}(\omega^{\prime},t)\times

\displaystyle\left(S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)S_{j}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\rho}(t)-S_{j}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\rho}(t)S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\right)+h.c.

(105)

where

\Gamma_{ij}(\omega,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\ e^{i\omega s}\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left(\hat{\rho}_{E}(0)\hat{E}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\hat{E}_{j}(-s)\right)\ .

(106)

Given that the partial trace in this expression represents a correlation between bath operators at time
-s
and
0
and we expect no correlations between these operators at distant times, we can push the integration bound to infinity and define

\Gamma_{ij}(\omega):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma_{ij}(\omega,t)=\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}s\ e^{i\omega s}\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left(\rho_{E}E_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{E}_{j}(-s)\right)

(107)

such as to rewrite the evolution equation as

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)=-\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime},i,j}e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}\Gamma_{ij}(\omega^{\prime})\times

\displaystyle\left(S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)S_{j}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\rho}(t)-S_{j}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\rho}(t)S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\right)+h.c.

(108)

For the open spin chain, these quantities are computed to be

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\Gamma_{00}(\omega)=\pi h_{0}(-\omega)^{2}n_{0}(-\omega)\Theta(-\omega)\\
\qquad\qquad+i\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ \frac{h_{0}^{2}(\nu)n_{0}(\nu)}{\omega+\nu}\\
\Gamma_{11}(\omega)=\pi h_{N}(-\omega)^{2}n_{N}(-\omega)\Theta(-\omega)\\
\qquad\qquad+i\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\ \frac{h_{N}^{2}(\nu)n_{N}(\nu)}{\omega+\nu}\\
\Gamma_{22}(\omega)=\pi h_{0}(\omega)^{2}\left(n_{0}(\omega)+1\right)\Theta(\omega)\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad+i\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{h_{0}(\nu)^{2}\left(n_{0}(\omega)+1\right)}{\omega-\nu}\\
\Gamma_{33}(\omega)=\pi h_{N}(\omega)^{2}\left(n_{N}(\omega)+1\right)\Theta(\omega)\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad+i\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\frac{h_{N}(\nu)^{2}\left(n_{N}(\omega)+1\right)}{\omega-\nu}\end{gathered}

and zero otherwise, where
n_{\alpha}(\nu)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{\alpha}\nu}-1}
are bosonic mean thermal occupation numbers,
\Theta(\omega)
is the Heavyside step-function and
\mathrm{P.V.}
denotes the Cauchy principal value of the integral.

Back to the dynamical equation, what is called the secular approximation, where only terms with
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
are kept, can be performed which gives

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)=-\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega,i,j}\Gamma_{ij}(\omega)\times

\displaystyle\left(S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)S_{j}(\omega)\hat{\rho}(t)-S_{j}(\omega)\hat{\rho}(t)S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\right)+h.c.

(117)

Renaming some summation indices and defining the quantities

\begin{split}\pi_{ij}(\omega)&=\frac{-i}{2}\left(\Gamma_{ij}(\omega)-\Gamma_{ji}^{*}(\omega)\right)\\
\gamma_{ij}&=\Gamma_{ij}(\omega)+\Gamma_{ji}^{*}(\omega)\end{split}

(118)

the evolution equation takes the form

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)=

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}i\sum_{\omega,i,j}\pi_{ij}(\omega)\left[S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)S_{j}(\omega),\hat{\rho}(t)\right]

If we bring back the evolution equation to the Schrödinger picture, it becomes what is called the Lindblad Master equation

\mathcal{H}_{LS}=\sum_{\omega,i,j}\pi_{ij}(\omega)S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)S_{j}(\omega)

(129)

\mathds{D}[\rho]=\!\lambda^{2}\!\sum_{\omega,i,j}\gamma_{ij}(\omega)\!\left(\!S_{j}(\omega)\rho S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\!\left\{\rho,S_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)S_{j}(\omega)\right\}\!\right)

(130)
