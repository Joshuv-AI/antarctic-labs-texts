Round Robin Active Sequential Change Detection for Dependent Multi-Channel Data
Anamitra Chaudhuri, Georgios Fellouris, Ali Tajer

Abstract
This paper considers the problem of sequentially detecting a change in the joint distribution of multiple data sources under a sampling constraint. Specifically, the channels or sources generate observations that are independent over time, but not necessarily independent at any given time instant. The sources follow an initial joint distribution, and at an unknown time instant, the joint distribution of an unknown subset of sources changes. Importantly, there is a hard constraint that only a fixed number of sources are allowed to be sampled at each time instant. The goal is to sequentially observe the sources according to the constraint, and stop sampling as quickly as possible after the change while controlling the false alarm rate below a user-specified level. The sources can be selected dynamically based on the already collected data, and thus, a policy for this problem consists of a joint sampling and change-detection rule. A non-randomized policy is studied, and an upper bound is established on its worst-case conditional expected detection delay with respect to both the change point and the observations from the affected sources before the change. It is shown that, in certain cases, this rule achieves first-order asymptotic optimality as the false alarm rate tends to zero, simultaneously under every possible post-change distribution and among all schemes that satisfy the same sampling and false alarm constraints. These general results are subsequently applied to the problems of (i) detecting a change in the marginal distributions of (not necessarily independent) information sources, and (ii) detecting a change in the covariance structure of Gaussian information sources.



I
Introduction

In statistical decision-making, growth in the data’s complexity, dimension, and scale increases the data acquisition and computational costs, if not rendering them prohibitive altogether. Enforcing proper data-acquisition (sampling) constraints is a natural measure to contain such costs. For example, when the data of interest become available at multiple locations or sources, it may be practical and economically efficient to take observations from only a small fraction of these locations. There are many areas in science and engineering where these types of scenarios arise, e.g., multi-sensor networks, surveillance systems, cyber security, and power grids. Such sampling constraints have been considered in the sequential anomaly detection/identification problem
[

[

and
[

]
, where only one source is sampled at each time instant, and observations from different sources are assumed to exhibit temporal dependence.
Furthermore, such sampling constraints can be embedded into the general framework of “sequential design of experiments” (see, e.g.,
[

and
[
10

).

A related problem to sequential testing is that of sequential change detection, where the goal is to detect as quickly as possible a change in the distribution of the underlying process
[
11

]
. The problem of sequential change detection with controlled sensing, that is, in the presence of a set of actions that can influence the distribution of the observations, has been considered in
[
13
]
and

[

. The concrete setup where there are multiple independent sources of observations, the change affects the distributions of an unknown subset of them, and it is possible to sample only a fixed number of sources at each time instant has been considered in
[

,

. In the first four references, the sampled processes are statistically independent, whereas in the latter, the change can affect the dependence structure of a subset of these processes.
Some other relevant works in the sequential literature involving inference regarding the observed processes’ dependence structure are
[
21

,

,
24

.

In this paper, we assume that there are
K
sources generating data that are independent over time but not necessarily across sources. That is,
the samples from two different sources, generated at the same time, are not necessarily independent.
We assume that the samples initially follow a completely specified joint distribution until an unknown time instant at which the joint distribution of an unknown subset of sources changes. The post-change regime is not necessarily completely specified, but a finite set of post-change alternatives is postulated. The objective is to sequentially detect this change as quickly as possible while controlling the false alarm rate,
under the constraint that only
m
sources can be sampled at each time instant, where
m
is a user-specified integer smaller than
K
.

This problem can be embedded into the general framework of sequential change detection problems with controlled sensing, where the action at each time instant is the selection of the sampled sources. In this context, an asymptotically optimal procedure can be obtained by employing a sufficiently frequent random exploration of the action space and using a sufficiently large memory of past observations
[
14
]
. Our focus in the present paper is on a computationally simple procedure that does not employ any randomization and does not require memory of any past observations. According to it, a family of size-
m
subsets of sources is specified, and its members, to which we refer as units, are sampled in a round-robin manner. For each unit, a cumulative sum (CUSUM)-like statistic, which enjoys a recursive structure, is computed, and
if its value exceeds a pre-specified threshold, then
an alarm indicating that the change has occurred is raised; if its value becomes smaller or equal to 0, the next unit is sampled; otherwise, the same unit continues being sampled.

The motivating question for this paper is the following: under what conditions, if any, is this computationally simple procedure also statistically efficient? To answer this and quantify this test’s detection performance, we adopt a version of Lorden’s criterion
[
25
]
, in which the worst-case conditional expected detection delay is considered with respect to the change-point and the data until the change only from the
affected
sources. Then, under certain conditions on the pre-and post-change distributions, we establish a non-asymptotic upper bound for the above policy with respect to this delay metric. Subsequently, we show that, in certain cases, this policy is first-order asymptotically
optimal, as the false alarm rate goes to
0
, within the class of all procedures that satisfy the user-specified sampling and false alarm constraints. In particular, this occurs if (i) the post-change distribution is completely specified for every unit, (ii) either a single unit is affected by the change or the signal-to-noise ratio is the same for all affected units, and (iii) the family of units includes the size-
m
subset of sources that is the affected the most by the change.

We specialize this general asymptotic theory to two concrete setups. The first one is the problem of detecting a change in the marginal distributions of multiple, not necessarily independent
data sources.
In fact, the policy that we study in this work is inspired by, and also generalizes, the ones considered in
[
5
]
, and more recently in

[
15
,
17
]
. Specifically, it reduces to the policy presented in
[
15
]

when, in particular,
(i) it is possible to sample
only one of them
at each time instant
(m=1)
, and (ii) there is a completely specified post-change distribution for each source. In
[
15
]
, this scheme was shown to be second-order asymptotically optimal, i.e., to achieve up to a constant term the optimal worst-case delay that would be achievable were all sources sampled at all times,
when only one source is affected by the change
. On the contrary, we do not compare the policy’s performance with that of the optimal policy in the full-sampling case, which is not attainable in general. Instead, we compare it
with the optimal one in the family of policies that satisfy the same
sampling constraints. Thus, we show that the rule in

[
15
]
is
first-order

asymptotically optimal not only when a single source is affected by the change but also
when any possible number of data sources experience the change
as long as the signal-to-noise ratio is the same in all of them.

The formulation in the paper is not limited to the case that the change affects the marginal distributions of the sources. In fact, one of the motivating problems for this work is the detection of a change in the dependence structure. Thus, the second concrete setup to which we apply the general theory is that of detecting a change in the correlation structure of multiple Gaussian information sources. The theoretical results in this setup are also illustrated in certain simulation studies, which provide further insights.

II
Problem Formulation

II-A

Multi-channel change-point model

We consider
K
data sources or channels that generate sequences of random elements sequentially and independently over time. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the random element whose value is generated by source
k\in[K]
at time
n
by
X^{k}_{n}
, and we set

X_{n}:=(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{K}_{n})
.
We refer to the
distribution of
X_{n}
as the
global
distribution at time
n
, and we assume that it changes at an unknown, deterministic time
\nu\in\{0,1,\ldots,\}
. We denote the pre-change global distribution by
{\sf F}
and the post-change global distribution by
{\sf G}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle X_{n}\sim\begin{cases}{\sf F},\ &\text{for}\ n\leq\nu\\
{\sf G},\ &\text{for}\ n>\nu\end{cases}\ .

(1)

We assume that while the pre-change distribution,
{\sf F}
, is completely specified, this is not the case for the post-change distribution. To emphasize this, we define
\mathcal{G}
as the family that encompasses all plausible post-change global distributions. We emphasize that the change does not necessarily affect all sources, and the subset of sources whose joint distribution changes is
unknown
. To formalize this, for any
E\subseteq[K]
we denote the set of samples from the sources in
E
at time
n\in\mathbb{N}
by
X_{n}^{E}
, i.e.,

X^{E}_{n}:=\{X^{k}_{n}:k\in E\}
. Furthermore,
we denote the distributions of
X_{n}^{E}
under
{\sf F}
and
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
by
{\sf F}^{E}
and
{\sf G}^{E}
, respectively. We refer to the former as
the
local
distribution of
E
before the change and to the latter as the
local
distribution of
E
after the change under
{\sf G}
. Therefore, when the global post-change distribution is
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
,
a subset of sources
E\subseteq[K]
experiences the change if and only if
its local distributions under
{\sf F}
and
{\sf G}
are different
, that is,

if

\displaystyle\quad{\sf G}^{E}={\sf F}^{E},\quad\text{then}\quad X_{n}^{E}\sim{\sf F}^{E},\ \ \text{for every}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\ ,

(2)

if

\displaystyle\quad{\sf G}^{E}\neq{\sf F}^{E},\quad\text{then}\quad X_{n}^{E}\sim\begin{cases}{\sf F}^{E}\ &\text{for}\ n\leq\nu\\
{\sf G}^{E}\ &\text{for}\ n>\nu\end{cases}\ .

(3)

Finally, for any
E\subseteq[K]
we denote the family of all plausible post-change local distributions of
E
by
\mathcal{G}^{E}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{E}:=\{{\sf G}^{E}:{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G},\;{\sf G}^{E}\neq{\sf F}^{E}\}\ .

(4)

II-B

Sequential change detection with dynamic sampling control

The problem we consider in this work is the detection of the change as quickly as possible under the hard constraint that
only
m
of the
K
sources can be sampled at any time instant
. That is, at each time
n\in\mathbb{N}
until stopping, only
m
coordinates of
X_{n}
are observed. These coordinates, nevertheless, are selected dynamically, based on the already collected observations. Thus, at each time instant we have two intertwined tasks: (i) to decide whether to terminate the sampling process declaring that the change has occurred (
detection task
) or to continue sampling, and (ii) when the decision is to continue sampling, to also select the sources to be sampled next (
sampling task
).

To formalize these two tasks, we denote the family of subsets of
[K]
with cardinality
m
by
\mathcal{K}_{m}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\{A\subseteq[K]:\;|A|=m\}\ .

(5)

Subsequently, we define a
sampling rule
as a sequence
S:=\{S_{n}:\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, where each
S_{n}
is a
\mathcal{K}_{m}
-valued random variable that represents the set of the
m
sources that we select to sample at time
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and is a function of the collected data up to time
n-1
, i.e.,
\{X_{t}^{S_{t}}:\;t\in[n-1]\}
. We emphasize that a sampling rule
S
induces the filtration of the collected observations, which can be defined recursively as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}^{S}:=\sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{n-1}^{S},X_{n}^{S_{n}}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\ ,

(6)

where
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{S}
is a
\sigma
-algebra
independent of

\{X_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. The problem, subsequently, is to specify a sampling rule,
S
, and an
\{\mathcal{F}^{S}_{n}\}
-measurable stopping time,
T
, at which it is declared that a change has occurred. That is,
T
must be a positive, integer-valued random variable such that
\{T=n\}
\in\mathcal{F}^{S}_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We refer to such a pair
(S,T)
as a
joint sequential sampling rule and change-detection policy
, and we denote by
\Delta
the family of all such policies.

II-C

Change detection criteria

We next introduce the metrics that we adopt in order to quantify the false alarm rate and the speed of detection of a policy. To this end, we denote the underlying probability measure when there is no change by
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}={\sf F}\times{\sf F}\times\dots\ ,

(7)

and denote the underlying probability measure when the change happens at time
\nu
and the global post-change distribution is
{\sf G}
by
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}

\displaystyle=\underset{\nu}{\underbrace{{\sf F}\times\ldots\times{\sf F}}}\times{\sf G}\times{\sf G}\times\ldots\ .

(8)

Accordingly, we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
the expectations under
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
, respectively. To control the false alarm rate, we focus on policies whose expected time until raising an alarm
when there is no change is at least
\gamma
, where
\gamma>1
is a user-specified level. Thus, such policies belong to

\displaystyle\Delta(\gamma):=\{(S,T)\in\Delta:\;\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[T]\geq\gamma\}\ .

(9)

To describe the delay in detecting the change, we adopt a modified version of Lorden’s criterion
[
25
]
, as in
[
15
]
. Specifically, for each

{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
we denote by
\mathcal{A}({\sf G})

the family of subsets of
[K]
of size
m
whose local distributions are affected when the post-change global distribution is
{\sf G}
, as described in (
1
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}({\sf G}):=\{E\in\mathcal{K}_{m}:\;{\sf G}^{E}\neq{\sf F}^{E}\}\ .

(10)

Without loss of generality, we assume that this set is non-empty, i.e.,
|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})|\geq 1
for every
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
.
Accordingly, for a policy
(S,T)
we denote by
D^{{\sf G}}(S,T)
the worst-case (with respect to the change-point) conditional expected detection delay given the worst possible history of observations up to the change-point
from the sources whose distributions are affected by the change
when the post-change global distribution is
{\sf G}
. Specifically,

D^{{\sf G}}(S,T):=\sup_{\nu\geq 0}\ \text{esssup}\ \mathbb{E}^{{\sf G}}_{\nu}\left[T-\nu\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{S}_{\nu}({\sf G}),T>\nu\right]\,,

(11)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}^{S}({\sf G})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the sources in
\mathcal{A}({\sf G})
, defined in (
10
), up to time
\nu
when the sources are sampled according to the sampling rule
S
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\nu}^{S}({\sf G}):=\sigma\left(S_{t},X_{t}^{S_{t}}:t\in[\nu],\;\;S_{t}\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\right)\ .

(12)

II-D

Distributional assumptions

Throughout the paper, we assume that for every
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
and
E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})

the local distributions
{\sf G}^{E}
and
{\sf F}^{E}
are mutually absolutely continuous, and we denote
the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
{\sf G}^{E}
and
{\sf F}^{E}
by
\mathcal{I}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{E}_{0}({\sf G}):=\int\log\left(\frac{d{\sf G}^{E}}{d{\sf F}^{E}}\right)d{\sf G}^{E}\ .

(13)

We refer to
\mathcal{I}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})
as the
information number
corresponding to
E
under
{\sf G}
, as it measures how much the local distribution of
E
gets affected when the global distribution changes from
{\sf F}
to
{\sf G}
.

III
Universal Asymptotic Lower Bound

In this section, we fix an arbitrary
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
and state an asymptotic, as
\gamma\to\infty
, lower bound on

\inf_{(S,T)\in\Delta(\gamma)}D^{{\sf G}}(S,T)\ ,

(14)

which is the optimal, among all policies in
\Delta(\gamma)
, worst-case conditional expected detection delay when the global post-change distribution is
{\sf G}
. This lower bound is inversely proportional to the information number that corresponds to the subset in
\mathcal{A}({\sf G})
, defined in (
10
), whose local distribution is altered the
most
under the change. The proof combines ideas and techniques from

[

]

\displaystyle\int\left(\log\frac{d{\sf G}^{E}}{d{\sf F}^{E}}\right)^{2}d{\sf G}^{E}<\infty\quad\forall\;E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\ .

(15)

we have

IV
Round Robin Active Sequential Detection Policy

In this section, we introduce the policy
(\tilde{S},\tilde{T})
, and refer it as
Round Robin CUSUM
due to the nature of its sampling rule.
Under certain assumptions it
achieves the asymptotic lower bound of the previous section up to a multiplicative constant and, in certain cases, with equality. For this purpose, we first fix an arbitrary, non-empty subset of
\mathcal{K}_{m}
, which we denote by
{\cal U}
. We refer to the members of
{\cal U}
as
units
. For each unit
E\in{\cal U}
, we assume that
\mathcal{G}^{E}
defined in (
4
), i.e., the family of all plausible post-change local distributions of
E
,
is non-empty and finite. We denote the mixture over all its members by
{\sf H}^{E}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\sf H}^{E}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}^{E}|}\sum_{{\sf P}\in\mathcal{G}^{E}}{\sf P}\ ,

(17)

and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote the log-likelihood ratio of
{\sf H}^{E}
versus
{\sf F}^{E}
based on
X_{n}^{E}
by
\xi_{n}^{E}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\xi_{n}^{E}:=

\displaystyle\log\frac{d{\sf H}^{E}}{d{\sf F}^{E}}(X_{n}^{E})\ .

(18)

IV-A

Description of the policy

First, we fix an arbitrary permutation of the units,
E_{1},\ldots,E_{|{\cal U}|}
, and a constant
A>0
. The first unit
in the above permutation is sampled first, i.e.,
\tilde{S}_{1}=E_{1}
. Then, for each time
n\in\mathbb{N}
up to stopping, the following CUSUM-like statistic is computed,

\displaystyle Y_{n}=\max\left\{Y_{n-1},0\right\}+\xi_{n}^{\tilde{S}_{n}}\ ,\quad\text{where}\qquad Y_{0}=0\ .

(19)

Based on the value of
Y_{n}
we distinguish the following cases:

1.

If
Y_{n}\in(0,A)
, then the same unit continues being sampled at the next time instant, i.e.,

\tilde{S}_{n+1}=\tilde{S}_{n}
.

2.

If
Y_{n}\leq 0
, then the next unit in the chosen permutation is sampled at the next time instant, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{n+1}=\begin{cases}E_{d+1}&\text{if}\;\;\tilde{S}_{n}=E_{d}\;\;\text{and}\;\;d<|{\cal U}|\\
E_{1}&\text{if}\;\;\tilde{S}_{n}=E_{|{\cal U}|}\end{cases}\ .

(20)

3.

If
Y_{n}\geq A
, then an alarm is raised and sampling is terminated.

Thus, the stopping time of this procedure is

\displaystyle\tilde{T}:=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;Y_{n}\geq A\right\}\ .

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})

\displaystyle=\int\log\left(\frac{d{\sf H}^{E}}{d{\sf F}^{E}}\right)\,d\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}^{E}|}\sum_{{\sf P}\in\mathcal{G}^{E}}{\sf P}\right)

The second equality holds because
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
, and by condition (ii), it follows that all terms of the sum are equal. The third one holds by the additivity of the integral, and the last one holds due to the definition of
{\sf H}^{E}

\mathcal{J}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})
, holds by the positivity of Kullback-Leibler divergence when the two distributions are different and condition (i), which states that
{\sf H}^{E}\neq{\sf F}^{E}
. Finally, the finiteness of
\mathcal{J}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})
follows from the finiteness of the expectations in condition (ii).

Corollary IV.1

Assumptions (

)-(

(i)

for every

,

(ii)

Proof:

When condition (i) is satisfied, by the definitions of

{\sf G}

\displaystyle q^{E}_{-}({\sf G}):={\sf G}(\zeta^{E}_{-}=\infty)\ .

(43)

We note that under assumptions (
23
)-(

Proposition 2

Suppose (
22
)
-(
24

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})

\displaystyle:=\int\left(\xi_{1}^{E}-\mathcal{J}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})\right)^{2}d{\sf G}<\infty\qquad\text{for every}\quad E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}\ ,

then
(

Proof:

See Appendix
E-H

Change in the Marginal Distributions of the Sources

In this section, we illustrate the model, algorithm, and theoretical results of the previous sections in the problem of detecting a change in the marginal distributions of the sources. Specifically, we assume that at an unknown time
\nu
the global distribution changes from
{\sf F}
to some

Since it is possible to detect this change even when sampling one source at a time, we focus on the case that

\displaystyle{\sf H}^{k}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}^{k}|}\sum_{{\sf P}\in\mathcal{G}^{k}}{\sf P}\ \ \qquad\text{and}\qquad\ \xi_{n}^{k}:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{k}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\int\log\left(\frac{d{\sf F}^{k}}{d{\sf H}^{k}}\right)d{\sf F}^{k}\in(0,\infty)\ ,\qquad\text{for every}\quad k\in[K]\ ,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{k}_{0}({\sf G})

\displaystyle=\int\log\left(\frac{d{\sf H}^{k}}{d{\sf F}^{k}}\right)d{\sf G}^{k}\in(0,\infty)\ ,\qquad\text{for every}\quad\{k\}\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\ .

Change in Correlation

In this section, we illustrate the models, algorithms, and theoretical results of the previous sections
in the problem of detecting a change in the correlation structure of sources that follow
a multivariate Gaussian distribution. For simplicity, we assume that
the sources are initially independent, each with distribution
N(0,1)
. After
\nu
,
the marginal distributions still remain the same, i.e.,
N(0,1)
, while an unknown subset of sources become dependent. For each pair of sources that is affected by the change, we assume that the correlation coefficient takes a value in a finite set
\mathcal{R}\subset(0,1)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle X_{n}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}N_{K}(\boldsymbol{0},\,I)\ ,&\text{for}\ n\leq\nu\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}\;&R_{ii}=1\,\quad\forall\;i\in[K]\ ,\\
\;&R_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}\cup\{0\}\quad\forall\;i,j\in[K],\;i<j\ ,\;\\

{\sf F}\equiv N_{K}(\boldsymbol{0},I)

\mathcal{G}
, we will consider different setups depending on the specification of
\mathcal{R}
and the

2
sources at each time, i.e.,

2
. First, we will consider the case that we can sample only two sources per time instant, i.e.,

15
) is satisfied.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}({\sf G})=\{\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}:R_{ij}\neq 0\}\ .

(64)

\rho\in(0,1)

(\tilde{S},\tilde{T})
achieves

Proposition 6

, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\left\{N_{K}(\boldsymbol{0},R)\ :\;R\;\;\text{satisfies}~\eqref{eq:R}\right\},

VIII
Concluding Remarks and Future Directions

In this paper, we have considered an environment of multiple information sources or channels generating data streams in discrete time. The generated data are independent over time, but the observations from different sources can be statistically dependent. At an unknown time, the underlying joint distribution undergoes a change. The objective has been to detect the change with minimal delay, subject to a sampling constraint, when at each time, it is allowed to sample only a pre-fixed number of sources, of our choice. In the class of all sequential procedures that satisfy the sampling constraint and control the false alarm rate below a certain pre-specified level, we have established a universal asymptotic lower bound for
a modified version of Lorden’s criterion
[
25
]
, as the false alarm rate vanishes.
Subsequently, we have considered a simple, computationally efficient, and easily implementable sequential procedure that belongs to the above-mentioned class and achieves asymptotic optimality under certain assumptions regarding the underlying change-point model. Furthermore, under more general conditions, we have shown that the asymptotic relative efficiency of the proposed rule remains bounded. In contrast with most previous studies in the literature, the results in the present paper have not assumed independence among the data sources and have not relied on any prior information regarding how the sources are affected by the change. Thus, we have applied the procedures and the results to the problems of detecting a change in (i) the marginal distributions of multiple, not necessarily independent, data sources and (ii) the correlation structure of multiple Gaussian data sources. In the context of the first problem, we have generalized the results in
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
by relaxing the assumption that exactly one source is affected by the change, and establishing an asymptotic optimality property that holds for any possible number of sources affected by the change, as long as the signal to noise ratio is the same in all affected sources.

There are several potential open questions for further research, such as a more precise description of the optimal worst-case detection delay,
and the consideration of more general setups that may involve temporal dependence and/or in which the pre-change and post-change distributions belong to parametric families.
Other directions of interest include considering alternative error and delay metrics, studying an asymptotic regime where the number of sources also goes to infinity as the false alarm rate vanishes, modeling the data streams using spatial models, e.g., a Markov random field, where the special underlying dependence could be used to obtain more efficient rules in practice. Finally, it would be of interest to design procedures that provide guarantees for the correct isolation of the sources that are affected by the change under such sampling constraints.

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{S_{t}\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\}\max_{E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})}\mathcal{V}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})\;\Bigg|\;\;T>\nu\right]

(124)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})}\mathcal{V}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})\;\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left(S_{t}\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\;\Bigg|\;\;T>\nu\right)\ ,

(125)

where (
122
) follows by using the tower property of conditional expectation and the fact that,
\{T>\nu\}\in\mathcal{F}^{S}_{\nu-1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{S}_{t-1}
, and (
123
) follows from Lemma
A.1
(ii).

Appendix B
Proof of Universal Asymptotic Lower Bound

Fix any arbitrary
{\sf G}\in\mathcal{G}
,
\nu\geq 0
,
(S,T)\in\Delta(\gamma)
and consider the sequences
\{Z_{n},A_{n}:n\geq 0\}
as defined in (
102
).
Then by using Lemma
A.2
, under
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{\sf G}
and with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}^{S}:n>\nu\}
, the process
\{(Z_{n}-A_{n})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu}):n>\nu\}
is a zero-mean martingale,
which further implies that the process

\displaystyle\{\{(Z_{n}-A_{n})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\}^{2}:n>\nu\}\ ,

(126)

is a submartingale.
Note that, for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
m<n
we have

\displaystyle A_{n}-A_{m}\leq(n-m)\;\mathcal{I}_{*}\ ,\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{I}_{*}:=\max_{E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})}\mathcal{I}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})\ .

(127)

Now, for an arbitrary
0<\epsilon<1
and
\gamma>1
we define

\displaystyle N_{\gamma,\epsilon}:=\frac{\log\gamma}{\mathcal{I}_{*}}(1-\epsilon)\ .

(128)

and

\log\eta_{\gamma}:=(1-\epsilon^{2})\log\gamma=(1+\epsilon)N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{*}\ .

(129)

Then by the definition of
D^{{\sf G}}(S,T)
in (
11
) and Markov’s inequality it follows that, for any
\nu\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{{\sf G}}(S,T)&\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}[T-\nu\,|\,T>\nu]\\
&\geq N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\,\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left(T-\nu>N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\,|\,T>\nu\right)\\
&=N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\,(1-\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left(T-\nu\leq N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\,|\,T>\nu\right)\\
&=N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\,\left(1-p(\nu,T)-q(\nu,T)\right)\ ,\end{split}

(130)

where we have defined

\displaystyle p(\nu,T)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left(T-\nu\leq N_{\gamma,\epsilon},\;e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu}}>\eta_{\gamma}|T>\nu\right)\ ,

(131)

\displaystyle q(\nu,T)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left(T-\nu\leq N_{\gamma,\epsilon},\;e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu}}\leq\eta_{\gamma}|T>\nu\right)\ .

(132)

First, we find an upper bound on
p(\nu,T)
for any
\nu\geq 0
. To this end, note that

\displaystyle\left\{T\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon},e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu}}>\eta_{\gamma}\right\}

(133)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}{Z_{k}-Z_{\nu}}>\log\eta_{\gamma}\right\}

(134)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\big\{(Z_{k}-A_{k})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\big\}+\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}(A_{k}-A_{\nu})>\log\eta_{\gamma}\right\}

(135)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eta_gamma}}{=}\left\{\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\big\{(Z_{k}-A_{k})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\big\}+\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}(A_{k}-A_{\nu})>N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{*}+\epsilon N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{*}\right\}

(136)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\big\{(Z_{k}-A_{k})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\big\}>\epsilon N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{*}\right\}

(137)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\big\{(Z_{k}-A_{k})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\big\}^{2}>\epsilon^{2}N_{\gamma,\epsilon}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{*}^{2}\right\}\ .

(138)

The relationship in (
137
) holds because for every
\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}
, by using (
127
) we have:

\displaystyle A_{k}-A_{\nu}\leq(k-\nu)\mathcal{I}_{*}\leq N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{*}\ .

(139)

Now, conditioning on the event
\{T>\nu\}
and following (
138
), we have

\displaystyle p(\nu,T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left(T\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon},e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu}}>\eta_{\gamma}|T>\nu\right)

(140)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\bigg(\max_{\nu+1\leq k\leq\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\big\{(Z_{k}-A_{k})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\big\}^{2}>\epsilon^{2}N_{\gamma,\epsilon}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{*}^{2}\;\Bigg|\;T>\nu\bigg)

(141)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\sf G}\left[\left\{\left(Z_{\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}-A_{\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\right)-\left(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu}\right)\right\}^{2}\;\Bigg|\;T>\nu\right]}{\epsilon^{2}N_{\gamma,\epsilon}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{*}^{2}}

(142)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{{\sf G}}\left[\left\{\sum_{t=\nu+1}^{\nu+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}\left(\lambda_{t}^{S_{t}}({\sf G})-\mathcal{I}^{S_{t}}_{0}({\sf G})\right)\right\}^{2}\;\Bigg|\;T>\nu\right]}{\epsilon^{2}N_{\gamma,\epsilon}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{*}^{2}}

(143)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})}\mathcal{V}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})\;\frac{N_{\gamma,\epsilon}}{\epsilon^{2}N_{\gamma,\epsilon}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{*}^{2}}

(144)

\displaystyle=\max_{E\in\mathcal{A}({\sf G})}\mathcal{V}^{E}_{0}({\sf G})\;\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{*}^{2}}\equiv\xi_{\epsilon}(\gamma)\ .

(145)

The inequality in (
142
) holds by using Lemma
A.4
for the submartingale process,

\{\{(Z_{n}-A_{n})-(Z_{\nu}-A_{\nu})\}^{2}:n>\nu\}
, and the inequality in (
144
) follows from Lemma
A.5
.
Now, since for any given
\epsilon>0
,
N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\to\infty
as
\gamma\to\infty
, we have
\xi_{\epsilon}(\gamma)\to 0
as
\gamma\to\infty
.

Next, we characterize an upper bound on
q(\nu,T)
. For this purpose, we follow the approach developed in the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
26
]
.
Let
n_{\gamma}
be the largest integer less than or equal to
\left(\log\gamma\right)^{2}
. Then, as shown in the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
26
]
, there exists
\nu_{\gamma}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(T\geq\nu_{\gamma}\right)>0\ ,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(T<\nu_{\gamma}+n_{\gamma}|T\geq\nu_{\gamma}\right)\leq\frac{n_{\gamma}}{\gamma}\ .

(146)

Thus, for
\gamma
large enough,

\displaystyle q(\nu_{\gamma},T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\gamma}}^{{\sf G}}\left(T\leq\nu_{\gamma}+N_{\gamma,\epsilon},e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu_{\gamma}}}\leq\eta_{\gamma}|T>\nu_{\gamma}\right)

(147)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\gamma}}^{{\sf G}}\left(\nu_{\gamma}<T\leq\nu_{\gamma}+N_{\gamma,\epsilon},e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu_{\gamma}}}\leq\eta_{\gamma}\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(T>\nu_{\gamma})}

(148)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu_{\gamma}}}\mathbbm{1}\{\nu_{\gamma}<T\leq\nu_{\gamma}+N_{\gamma,\epsilon},e^{Z_{T}-Z_{\nu_{\gamma}}}\leq\eta_{\gamma}\}]}{\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(T>\nu_{\gamma})}

(149)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta_{\gamma}\,\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\nu_{\gamma}<T\leq\nu_{\gamma}+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(T>\nu_{\gamma})}

(150)

\displaystyle=\eta_{\gamma}\,\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(T\leq\nu_{\gamma}+N_{\gamma,\epsilon}|T>\nu_{\gamma}\right)

(151)

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{\gamma}\,\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(T\leq\nu_{\gamma}+n_{\gamma}|T>\nu_{\gamma}\right)

(152)

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{\gamma}\,\frac{n_{\gamma}}{\gamma}

(153)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{-\epsilon^{2}}\left(\log\gamma\right)^{2}

(154)

\displaystyle\equiv\xi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\gamma)\ .

(155)

The equality in (
148
) follows from the fact that,
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\gamma}}^{{\sf G}}(T>\nu_{\gamma})=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(T>\nu_{\gamma})
since
\{T>\nu_{\gamma}\}\in\mathcal{F}^{S}_{\nu_{\gamma}}
. The inequality in (
152
) follows because for sufficiently large
\gamma
,
N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\leq n_{\gamma}
for every
\epsilon>0
, and the inequality in (
153
) follows from (
146
).
It is important to observe that
\xi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\gamma)\to 0
as
\gamma\to\infty
for any given
\epsilon>0
.
Finally from (
130
), we have, for all large
\gamma
,

\displaystyle D^{{\sf G}}(S,T)

\displaystyle\geq N_{\gamma,\epsilon}\,\left(1-p(\nu_{\gamma},T)-q(\nu_{\gamma},T)\right)

(156)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log\gamma}{\mathcal{I}_{*}}(1-\epsilon)\;(1-\xi_{\epsilon}(\gamma)-\xi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\gamma))\ .

(157)

Now, we let first
\gamma\to\infty
, and then
\epsilon\to 0
, and the proof is complete.

Appendix C
Some Useful Lemmas for Performance Analysis

In this appendix,
we present some lemmas that are useful in proving
Theorem
IV.1
and Theorem
IV.2
.
All random variables are defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\sf P})
, and we denote by
{\sf E}[\cdot]
the corresponding expectation.

For the first two lemmas, namely Lemma
C.1
and Lemma
C.3
, we assume that
\{Z_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables,
and we set

\displaystyle S_{n}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_{t},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\ .

(158)

Furthermore, we fix some arbitrary
A>0
, and for any
x\in[0,A)
we set
\pi(x):=\min\{\pi_{-}(x),\pi_{+}(x)\}
, where

\displaystyle\pi_{-}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{n\geq 1:S_{n}<-x\right\}\ ,

(159)

\displaystyle\pi_{+}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{n\geq 1:S_{n}>A-x\right\}\ .

(160)

Furthermore, we assume that the first two moments of
Z_{1}
exist, and we denote them as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:={\sf E}[Z_{1}]\ \qquad\text{and}\qquad\ \mathcal{V}:={\sf E}\left[\left(Z_{1}-\mathcal{I}\right)^{2}\right]\ .

(161)

Lemma C.1

If
0<\mathcal{I}<\infty
, then for any
x\in[0,A)
we have, as
A\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\sf E}\left[\pi(x)\right]}{{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\right)}\leq\frac{A-x}{\mathcal{I}}+o(A)\ .

(162)

If, also,
\mathcal{V}<\infty
, then the inequality (
162
) holds for every
A>0
with the
o(A)
term being replaced with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\left(1+\frac{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{I}^{2}}\right),\qquad\text{where}\;\;\;p:=\;{\sf P}\left(\pi_{-}(0)=\infty\right)>0\ .

(163)

In particular, when
x=0
, the upper bound holds even without the factor
1/p
.

Proof:

For any
x\in[0,A)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\,{\sf E}\left[\pi(x)\right]

\displaystyle={\sf E}\left[S_{\pi(x)}\right]

(164)

\displaystyle={\sf E}\left[S_{\pi(x)}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\}\right]+{\sf E}\left[S_{\pi(x)}\;\mathbbm{1}\{\pi_{-}(x)<\pi_{+}(x)\}\right]

(165)

\displaystyle\leq{\sf E}\left[S_{\pi(x)}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\}\right]

(166)

\displaystyle=(A-x)\;{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\right)+{\sf E}\left[(S_{\pi(x)}-(A-x))\;\mathbbm{1}\{\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\}\right]

(167)

\displaystyle\leq(A-x)\;{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\right)+o(A),

(168)

where the equality in (
164
) follows from Wald’s identity, the inequality in (
166
) follows from the fact that

\displaystyle S_{\pi(x)}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi_{-}(x)<\pi_{+}(x)\}=S_{\pi_{-}(x)}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi_{-}(x)<\pi_{+}(x)\}\leq 0\ ,

(169)

and the inequality in (
168
) holds from (
164
) along with the fact, due to the elementary renewal theorem, that, as
(A-x)\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[S_{\pi(x)}\right]=\mathcal{I}\,{\sf E}\left[\pi(x)\right]\leq(A-x)+o(A-x)\ ,

(170)

and also,
o(A-x)/A\leq o(A-x)/(A-x)\to 0
. The inequality in (
168
) further leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{I}\;{\sf E}\left[\pi(x)\right]}{{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq(A-x)+\frac{o(A)}{{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\right)}

(171)

\displaystyle\leq(A-x)+\frac{o(A)}{{\sf P}\left(\pi_{-}(x)=\infty\right)}

(172)

\displaystyle\leq(A-x)+\frac{o(A)}{p}\ .

(173)

The inequalities in (
172
) and (
173
) follow by using the fact that

\displaystyle{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(x)<\pi_{-}(x)\right)\geq{\sf P}\left(\pi_{-}(x)=\infty\right)\geq{\sf P}\left(\pi_{-}(0)=\infty\right)=p\ ,

(174)

where the second inequality in (
174
) follows from the fact that, for every
x\geq 0
,
\pi_{-}(x)\geq\pi_{-}(0)
as
\pi_{-}(x)
is an increasing function with respect to
x
. Furthermore, the positivity of
p
follows from the fact that, for large
A
,

\displaystyle p={\sf P}\left(\pi_{-}(0)=\infty\right)=\frac{1}{{\sf E}\left[\pi_{+}(A)\right]}\geq\frac{1}{{\sf E}\left[\pi_{+}(0)\right]}\geq\left(\frac{A+o(A)}{\mathcal{I}}\right)^{-1}>0\ ,

(175)

where, in (
175
), the second equality follows from
[
27
, Corollary 8.39]
, the first inequality holds because
\pi_{+}(0)\geq\pi_{+}(A)
, as
\pi_{+}(x)
is a decreasing function with respect to
x
, and the second inequality follows from what is shown next.
Note that the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{{\sf E}\left[\pi(0)\right]}{{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(0)<\pi_{-}(0)\right)}\ ,

(176)

represents the expected detection delay of a CUSUM process adapted to the sequence
\{S_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}

[
12
]
.
Since for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the CUSUM process at time
n
can always be bounded below by
S_{n}
, the detection delay can be consequently bounded above by
\pi_{+}(0)
. Therefore, as
A\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\,\frac{{\sf E}\left[\pi(0)\right]}{{\sf P}\left(\pi_{+}(0)<\pi_{-}(0)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}\,{\sf E}\left[\pi_{+}(0)\right]

(177)

\displaystyle={\sf E}\left[S_{\pi_{+}(0)}\right]

(178)

\displaystyle\leq A+o(A)\ ,

(179)

where the equality in (
178
) follows from Wald’s identity and the inequality in (
179
) follows similarly to (
168
)
If, also,
\mathcal{V}<\infty
, then by Theorem 1 in
[
28
]
it follows that, for every
A>0
, each of the above relations holds with the
o(A)
term being replaced with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}}{\sf E}\left[\left({S_{1}^{+}}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\mathcal{V}+\mathcal{I}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}}\ ,

(180)

where the inequality follows from the fact that
S_{1}^{+}\leq|S_{1}|
.
This completes the proof.

Lemma C.2

For any non-negative random variable
X

H_{1}
and
H_{2}

(183)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\sf E}\left[X\mathbbm{1}\{H_{1}\}\right]}{{\sf P}(H_{1})}+\frac{{\sf E}\left[X\mathbbm{1}\{H_{2}\}\right]}{{\sf P}(H_{2})}={\sf E}\left[X\;\Big|\;H_{1}\right]+{\sf E}\left[X\;\Big|\;H_{2}\right]\ .

(184)

Lemma C.3

Let
W
be a random variable independent of
\{Z_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

\displaystyle{\sf P}(W>x)\leq e^{-x},\quad x\geq 0\ .

(185)

If
-\infty<\mathcal{I}<0
, then for every
A>0
large enough, we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W\in(0,A)\right]\leq C\ ,

(186)

where
C<\infty

A
.

Proof:

Since
\{Z_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is independent of
W
, for any
x\in(0,A)
we have, as
x\to\infty
, and hence,
A\to\infty

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W=x\right]\leq{\sf E}\left[\pi_{-}(x)\right]\leq-\frac{x}{\mathcal{I}}+o(x)\ ,

(187)

where the second inequality follows from
the elementary renewal theorem (also see the derivation in the proof of Lemma
C.1
). Fix any arbitrary
\epsilon>0
. Then, there exists
M>0
such that for every
x\geq M
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W=x\right]\leq\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}:=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}}+\epsilon\ .

(188)

\kappa>1
such that
{\sf P}(W>M)>e^{-\kappa M}
. Otherwise, we have
{\sf P}(W\in(0,M])=1
, and thus, for every
A>M
,

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W\in(0,A)\right]={\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W\in(0,M]\right]\leq{\sf E}\left[\pi_{-}(W)\mathbbm{1}\{W\in(0,M]\}\right]\leq{\sf E}\left[\pi_{-}(M)\right]\ ,

(189)

and the proof is complete.
Then, for every
A\geq\kappa M
, we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W\in(0,A)\right]

(190)

\displaystyle\leq{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W\in(0,M]\right]+{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W\in(M,A)\right]

(191)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\mathbbm{1}\{W\in(0,M]\}\right]}{{\sf P}(W\in(0,M])}+{\sf E}\left[{\sf E}\left[\pi(W)\;\Big|\;W,W\in(M,A)\right]\;\Big|\;W\in(M,A)\right]

(192)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\sf E}\left[\pi_{-}(M)\right]}{1-e^{-M}}+{\sf E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}W\mathbbm{1}\{W\in(M,A)\;\Big|\;W\in(M,A)\right]\ ,

(193)

where the inequality in (
191
) follows from Lemma
C.2
and the inequality in (
193
) follows from (
185
).
Next, we bound the second term in (

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}W\mathbbm{1}\{W\in(M,A)\;\Big|\;W\in(M,A)\right]

(194)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\sf P}\left(W\mathbbm{1}\{W\in(M,A)>x\;\Big|\;W\in(M,A)\right)dx

(195)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{A}\frac{{\sf P}\left(\{x\vee M\}<W<A\right)}{{\sf P}\left(M<W<A\right)}dx

(196)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\left\{\int_{0}^{M}\frac{{\sf P}\left(M<W<A\right)}{{\sf P}\left(M<W<A\right)}dx\;+\;\int_{M}^{A}\frac{{\sf P}\left(x<W<A\right)}{{\sf P}\left(M<W<A\right)}dx\right\}

(197)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\left\{\int_{0}^{M}dx\;+\;\int_{M}^{A}\frac{{\sf P}\left(x<W\right)}{{\sf P}\left(M<W\right)-{\sf P}\left(A<W\right)}dx\right\}

(198)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\left\{M\;+\;\int_{M}^{A}\frac{e^{-x}}{{\sf P}\left(M<W\right)-e^{-A}}dx\right\}

(199)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\left\{M\;+\;\frac{e^{-M}-e^{-A}}{{\sf P}\left(M<W\right)-e^{-A}}dx\right\}

(200)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon}\left\{M\;+\;\frac{e^{-M}}{{\sf P}\left(M<W\right)-e^{-\kappa M}}\right\}\ ,

(201)

where the inequality in (
199
) follows from (
185
) and the inequality in (
201
) follows from the fact that
A\geq\kappa M
. The proof is complete.

The following lemma is a generalization of Wald’s identity that we use repeatedly later.

Lemma C.4

Let
\{Y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence of Bernoulli random variables, and set

\displaystyle N:=\inf\{n\geq 1:Y_{n}=1\}\ .

(202)

Furthermore, let

W
be a random variable so that
Z_{1},Y_{1},W
are independent of
\{(Z_{n},Y_{n}):n\geq 2\}
, and set

\displaystyle\mu_{x}\equiv{\sf E}\left[Z_{1}\,|\,W=x\right]\in\mathbb{R}\ \qquad\text{and}\ \qquad p_{x}\equiv{\sf P}(Y_{1}=1\,|\,W=x)\ .

(203)

(i)

If
\{(Z_{n},Y_{n}):n\geq 2\}
are i.i.d. with

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[Z_{n}\right]=\mu\in\mathbb{R}\ \qquad\text{and}\qquad\ {\sf P}(Y_{n}=1)=p>0\ ,

(204)

then

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z_{t}\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right]=\mu_{x}+(1-p_{x})\left(\frac{\mu}{p}\right)\ .

(205)

(ii)

If
there exists an
L\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
i\in\{2,\dots,L+1\}
,
\{(Z_{jL+i},Y_{jL+i}):j\geq 0\}
are i.i.d. with

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[Z_{i}\right]=\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\ \qquad\text{and}\qquad{\sf P}(Y_{i}=1)=p_{i}>0\ ,

(206)

then

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z_{t}\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right]=p_{x}\times\frac{\mu_{x}}{p_{x}}+{(1-p_{x})}\times\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}\;w_{i}\,\frac{\mu_{i}}{p_{i}}\ ,

(207)

where

\displaystyle w_{i}\equiv\frac{p_{i}\,\prod_{j=2}^{i-1}\,(1-p_{j})}{1-\prod_{j=2}^{L+1}\,(1-p_{j})}\ ,1<i\leq L+1\ ,

(208)

and
\prod_{j}^{i}=1
whenever
j>i
.

Proof:

We first observe that if
{\sf E}[N|W=x]<\infty
, then
by Wald’s identity we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z_{t}\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right]

\displaystyle={\sf E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}{\sf E}[Z_{t}\,|\,W=x]\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right].

(209)

(i) By (
209
) we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z_{t}\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mu_{x}+\mu\;\left({\sf E}[N|W=x]-1\right)\ .

(210)

To this point, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[N\;|\;W=x\right]=1+\frac{1-p_{x}}{p}\ .

(211)

Indeed, the conditional distribution of
N
can be easily found as follows.

\displaystyle{\sf P}\left(N=k\,|\,W=x\right)=\begin{cases}p_{x}\quad&\text{if}\quad k=1\\
(1-p_{x})(1-p)^{k-2}p\quad&\text{if}\quad k\geq 2\end{cases}\ ,

(212)

which further yields that

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[N\,|\,W=x\right]

\displaystyle=p_{x}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}k(1-p_{x})(1-p)^{k-2}p

(213)

\displaystyle=p_{x}+(1-p_{x})\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+k)(1-p)^{k-1}p

(214)

\displaystyle=p_{x}+(1-p_{x})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-p)^{k-1}p+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k(1-p)^{k-1}p\right)

(215)

\displaystyle=p_{x}+(1-p_{x})\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)

(216)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1-p_{x}}{p}\ .

(217)

(ii) By (
209
) we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z_{t}\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right]

\displaystyle={\sf E}\left[Z_{1}|W=x\right]+\sum_{t=2}^{\infty}{\sf E}\left[Z_{t}\mathbbm{1}\;\{N\geq t\}\;\Bigg|\;W=x\right]

(218)

\displaystyle=\mu_{x}+\sum_{t=2}^{\infty}{\sf E}\left[Z_{t}\right]\;{\sf P}\left(N\geq t\;\Big|\;W=x\right)

(219)

\displaystyle=\mu_{x}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{t=jL+2}^{(j+1)L+1}{\sf E}\left[Z_{t}\right]{\sf P}\left(N\geq t\;\Big|\;W=x\right)\ ,

(220)

where the equality in (
219
) follows from the fact that, since
N
is a stopping time with respect to the filtrations generated by
\{W,Y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, for every
t\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\{N\geq t\}\in\sigma\left(W,Y_{k}:k\in[t-1]\right)\ .

(221)

Thus, the event
\{N\geq t\}
is independent of
(Z_{t},Y_{t})
, which is also independent of
W
.
To this end, also following (
220
), it is necessary to derive the expressions for the conditional tail probabilities

\displaystyle{\sf P}\left(N\geq t\;\Big|\;W=x\right)\ ,\qquad t\geq 2\ ,

(222)

and we do it as follows. For every
t\geq 2
in the form

t=j_{t}L+i_{t}
,
for some
j_{t}\geq 0
and
i_{t}\in\{2,\dots,L+1\}
, we have

\displaystyle{\sf P}\left(N\geq t\;\Big|\;W=x\right)

\displaystyle={\sf P}\left(Y_{k}=0\;\;\text{for every}\;\;k=1,\dots,t-1\;|W=x\right)

(223)

\displaystyle={\sf P}(Y_{1}=0\,|\,W=x)\prod_{k=2}^{t-1}{\sf P}(Y_{k}=0)

(224)

\displaystyle={\sf P}(Y_{1}=0\,|\,W=x)\prod_{j=0}^{j_{t}-1}\prod_{i=2}^{L+1}{\sf P}(Y_{jL+i}=0)\times\prod_{i=2}^{i_{t}-1}{\sf P}(Y_{j_{t}L+i}=0)

(225)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{x})\prod_{j=0}^{j_{t}-1}\prod_{i=2}^{L+1}(1-p_{i})\times\prod_{i=2}^{i_{t}-1}(1-p_{i})

(226)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{x})\left(\prod_{i=2}^{L+1}(1-p_{i})\right)^{j_{t}}\prod_{i=2}^{i_{t}-1}(1-p_{i})\ ,

(227)

where the equality in (
223
) follows from the definition of
N
, and the equality in (
224
) holds since
\{Y_{n}:n\geq 2\}
is independent of
W
. Thus, following (
220
),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{t=jL+2}^{(j+1)L+1}{\sf E}\left[Z_{t}\right]{\sf P}\left(N\geq t\;\Big|\;W=x\right)

(228)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}{\sf E}\left[Z_{jL+i}\right]\;{\sf P}\left(N\geq jL+i\;\Big|\;W=x\right)

(229)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}\mu_{i}(1-p_{x})\left(\prod_{k=2}^{L+1}(1-p_{k})\right)^{j}\prod_{k=2}^{i-1}(1-p_{k})

(230)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{x})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{L+1}(1-p_{k})\right)^{j}\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}\mu_{i}\prod_{k=2}^{i-1}(1-p_{k})

(231)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{x})\left\{\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}\mu_{i}\prod_{k=2}^{i-1}(1-p_{k})\right\}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{L+1}(1-p_{k})\right)^{j}

(232)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{x})\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}\mu_{i}\prod_{k=2}^{i-1}(1-p_{k})\frac{1}{1-\prod_{k=2}^{L+1}(1-p_{k})}

(233)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{x})\,\sum_{i=2}^{L+1}\;w_{i}\,\frac{\mu_{i}}{p_{i}}\ ,

(234)

where
w_{i},2\leq i\leq L+1
is defined in (
208
).

Proof:

For every

\displaystyle\tau_{n}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t>\tau_{n-1}:\tilde{S}_{t+1}\neq\tilde{S}_{\tau_{n-1}+1}\}\ ,\quad\text{where}\quad\tau_{0}\equiv 0\ ,

\displaystyle z_{n}

\displaystyle\tilde{T}=\sum_{n=1}^{M}\zeta_{n}\ ,

denotes the stage at which the process is terminated, i.e.,

\displaystyle M:=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}:z_{n}=1\}\ ,

(241)

and, for every

we consider a pre-specified permutation

that remains unchanged over time, then, clearly, for every

\displaystyle\{(\zeta_{j|{\cal U}|+i},z_{j|{\cal U}|+i}):j\geq 0\}

is i.i.d. under

with common expectation
(\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\zeta^{E_{i}}],\ \mathbb{P}_{\infty}(z^{E_{i}}=1))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{M}\zeta_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|{\cal U}|}\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\zeta^{E_{i}}]\;\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(z^{E_{j}}=1))}{1-\prod_{j=1}^{|{\cal U}|}(1-\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(z^{E_{j}}=1))}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{|{\cal U}|}\frac{(1-e^{-A})^{i-1}}{1-(1-e^{-A})^{|{\cal U}|}}=e^{A}\,\frac{1-(1-e^{-A})^{|{\cal U}|}}{1-(1-e^{-A})^{|{\cal U}|}}

(245)

\displaystyle=e^{A}\ ,

(246)

which proves that setting

guarantees the desired error control. The

(ii), whereas the inequality in (

for every

and also because

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(z^{E}=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(\zeta^{E}_{A}<\zeta^{E}_{-})

(247)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(\xi_{\zeta^{E}}^{E}>A)\leq e^{-A}\ .

(248)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{*}({\sf G})}:=\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}w^{j}_{l}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\kappa(j,l)}_{0}({\sf G})}\ ,

(284)

where the weight corresponding to any element is the conditional probability that during the
j
-cycle we stop while sampling from that particular element given that we stop in the
j
-cycle, i.e.,

\displaystyle w^{j}_{l}=\frac{{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l)}({\sf G})\;\prod_{s=1}^{l-1}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}}{1-\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}\ ,\ \qquad\text{for every}\quad l\in[|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|]\ .

(285)

Since the inverse of the information number of any unit in
\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}
represents the delay or difficulty of detection when sampling only from that element after the change,
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{*}({\sf G})
represents the overall difficulty of detection averaged over all elements in
\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}
when

is the first element in
\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}
that we sample from immediately after the change. Hence,
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{*}({\sf G})
can be interpreted as the information number corresponding to the
j
-cycle.

(342)

Leveraging the following relationship that,

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[0,A)}g(x)=g(0)\bigvee\max_{x\in(0,A)}g(x)\ ,

(343)

it suffices to provide upper bounds for both quantities on the right-hand side. First, for any
x\in(0,A)
, we upper bound
g(x)
as follows.

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\mu_{x}^{j}+(1-p^{j}_{x})\times\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}w_{l}^{\kappa(j,2)}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(\kappa(j,2),l)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(\kappa(j,2),l)}({\sf G})}

(344)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{x}^{j}+(1-p^{j}_{x})\times\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})|}w_{l}^{\kappa(j,2)}\;\left(\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{\kappa(\kappa(j,2),l)}({\sf G})}+\;\text{constant}\right)

(345)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{x}^{j}+(1-p^{j}_{x})\;\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}+\;\text{constant}

(346)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x\in(0,A)}\;\left(\mu^{j}_{x}+(1-p^{j}_{x})\;\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}\right)+\;\text{constant}\ ,

(347)

where the inequality in (
345
) follows from Lemma
C.1
.
Now, we use Wald’s bounds to solve the maximization problem in (
347
), where the objective function is denoted by

\displaystyle h(x):=\left(\mu^{j}_{x}+(1-p^{j}_{x})\;\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}\right)\ ,\qquad\text{for}\;x\in(0,A)\ .

(348)

To be specific, for any
x\in(0,A)
, we use the following results (see
[
12
, pp. 127-128]
and
[
28
, Theorem 1]
), which are crucial to bound
h(x)
from above. If (
339
) holds, then

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\omega_{0}x}-1}{e^{-\omega_{0}x}-\omega_{1}e^{\omega_{0}(A-x)}}\leq p_{x}^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\omega_{2}e^{-\omega_{0}x}-1}{\omega_{2}e^{-\omega_{0}x}-e^{\omega_{0}(A-x)}}\ ,\qquad\text{for some}\;\;\omega_{0}<0<\omega_{1}<1<\omega_{2}\ .

(349)

\displaystyle\mu_{x}^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(A-x)p_{x}^{j}-x(1-p_{x}^{j})}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\;+\;\text{constant}\ .

(350)

Then, for any
x\in(0,A)
, we have

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(A-x)p_{x}^{j}-x(1-p_{x}^{j})}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+(1-p_{x}^{j})\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}+\;\text{constant}

(351)

\displaystyle=\frac{A-x}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+A(1-p_{x}^{j})\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)+\;\text{constant}\ ,

(352)

where the first inequality follows by using (
350
). Now, if
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})\geq\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})
, then following (
352
) and using the lower bound in (
349
) we can further upper bound

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A-x}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+A\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)\frac{1-\omega_{1}e^{\omega_{0}(A-x)}}{e^{-\omega_{0}x}-\omega_{1}e^{\omega_{0}(A-x)}}+\;\text{constant}

(353)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A-x}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+A\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)e^{\omega_{0}x}+\;\text{constant}\ ,

(354)

where the last inequality follows by using the inequality that,

\displaystyle\frac{1-b}{B-b}\leq\frac{1}{B}\ ,\quad\text{for any}\;\;0\leq b<1\leq B\ .

(355)

Therefore, following (
354
) and by using Lemma
F.1
we have

\displaystyle\max_{x\in(0,A)}h(x)\leq\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}\ +\;\text{constant}\ .

(356)

On the other hand, if
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})>\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})
, then following (
352
) and using the upper bound in (
349
) we can further bound

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A-x}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+A\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)\frac{1-e^{\omega_{0}(A-x)}}{\omega_{2}e^{-\omega_{0}x}-e^{\omega_{0}(A-x)}}+\;\text{constant}

(357)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A-x}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+A\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)\frac{e^{\omega_{0}x}-e^{\omega_{0}A}}{\omega_{2}-e^{\omega_{0}A}}+\;\text{constant}

(358)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A-x}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}+A\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)\frac{e^{\omega_{0}x}-e^{\omega_{0}A}}{\omega_{2}}+\;\text{constant}\ .

(359)

Therefore, following (
359
) and again by using Lemma
F.2
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{x\in(0,A)}h(x)&\leq\frac{A+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\left(\log(-A\omega_{0})+\log\left(1-{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}/{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}\right)-\log\omega_{2}+1\right)}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\\
&\qquad-\frac{Ae^{\omega_{0}A}}{\omega_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)+\;\text{constant}\ .\end{split}

(360)

Next, following (
343
) we also need to upper bound
g(0)
, which is done as follows.

\displaystyle g(0)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}^{j}+(1-p^{j}_{0})\times\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}w_{l}^{\kappa(j,2)}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(\kappa(j,2),l)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(\kappa(j,2),l)}({\sf G})}

(361)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})+\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{j}({\sf G}))w_{l}^{\kappa(j,2)}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}({\sf G})}

(362)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})+\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|-1}w_{l+1}^{j}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}({\sf G})}\;+\;\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(j,|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|+1)}({\sf G})\frac{\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}{1-\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}

(363)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})+\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|-1}w_{l+1}^{j}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}({\sf G})}\;+\;\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})\frac{\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))-1+1}{1-\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}

(364)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})-\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})+\sum_{l=2}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}w_{l}^{j}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l)}({\sf G})}\;+\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})}w^{j}_{1}

(365)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}w_{l}^{j}\;\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l)}({\sf G})}{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l)}({\sf G})}

(366)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}w_{l}^{j}\;\left(\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\kappa(j,l)}_{0}({\sf G})}+\;\text{constant}\right)

(367)

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{j}({\sf G})}+\;\text{constant}\ ,

(368)

where the equality in (
362
) follows from the observations that
\mu_{0}^{j}=\tilde{\mu}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})
and
p^{j}_{0}=\tilde{q}_{A}^{j}({\sf G})
, the equality in (
363
) follows from the fact that, for every
l\in[|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|]
,

\displaystyle(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{j}({\sf G}))w_{l}^{\kappa(j,2)}=\frac{(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{j}({\sf G}))\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}\prod_{s=1}^{l-1}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s+1)}({\sf G}))}{1-\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s+1)}({\sf G}))}=\frac{\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,l+1)}\prod_{s=1}^{l}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}{1-\prod_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|}(1-\tilde{q}_{A}^{\kappa(j,s)}({\sf G}))}\ ,

(369)

which coincides with
w_{l+1}^{j}
when
l\in[|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|-1]
.
The inequality in (
367
) follows from Lemma
C.1
.

Therefore, from the above results we derive the final upper bound as follows.
If
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})\geq\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})
, then following (
343
), (
347
), (
356
), and (
368
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\sf G}\left[T_{1}\;\Big|\;\tilde{S}_{\nu}=E_{i_{j}({\sf G})},Y_{\nu},\tilde{T}>\nu\right]\leq\left(\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{j}({\sf G})}\bigvee\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}\right)+\;\text{constant}\ .

(370)

On the other hand, if
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})>\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}^{\sf G}\left[T_{1}\;\Big|\;\tilde{S}_{\nu}=E_{i_{j}({\sf G})},Y_{\nu},\tilde{T}>\nu\right]

(371)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(\frac{A}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{j}({\sf G})}\bigvee\Bigg(\frac{A+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\left(\log(-A\omega_{0})+\log\left(1-{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}/{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}\right)-\log\omega_{2}+1\right)}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}

(372)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{Ae^{\omega_{0}A}}{\omega_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{*}^{\kappa(j,2)}({\sf G})}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{0}^{j}({\sf G})}\right)\Bigg)\Bigg)+\;\text{constant}\ .

(373)

Thus, the exact forms of the functions
\{\Phi_{j}:j\in[|\mathcal{A}({\sf G})\cap{\cal U}|]\}
can be derived from the above, where the constants are obtained from Lemma
C.1
. This completes the proof.
