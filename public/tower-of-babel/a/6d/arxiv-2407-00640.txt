Physics-augmented neural networks for constitutive modeling of hyperelastic geometrically exact beams
Jasper O. Schommartz, Dominik K. Klein, Juan C. Alzate Cobo, Oliver Weeger

Abstract
We present neural network-based constitutive models for hyperelastic geometrically exact beams. The proposed models are physics-augmented, i.e., formulated to fulfill important mechanical conditions by construction , which improves accuracy and generalization . Strains and curvatures of the beam are used as input for feed-forward neural networks that represent the effective hyperelastic beam potential. Forces and moments are received as the gradients of the beam potential, ensuring thermodynamic consistency. Normalization conditions are considered via additional projection terms. Symmetry conditions are implemented by an invariant-based approach for transverse isotropy and a more flexible point symmetry constraint, which is included in transverse isotropy but poses fewer restrictions on the constitutive response. Furthermore, a data augmentation approach is proposed to improve the scaling behavior of the models for varying cross-section radii. Additionally, we introduce a parameterization with a scalar parameter to represent ring-shaped cross-sections with different ratios between the inner and outer radii. Formulating the beam potential as a neural network provides a highly flexible model. This enables efficient constitutive surrogate modeling for geometrically exact beams with nonlinear material behavior and cross-sectional deformation, which otherwise would require computationally much more expensive methods. The models are calibrated and tested with data generated for beams with circular and ring-shaped hyperelastic deformable cross-sections at varying inner and outer radii , showing excellent accuracy and generalization. The applicability of the proposed point symmetric model is further demonstrated by applying it in beam simulations. In all studied cases, the proposed model shows excellent performance.



Keywords:
Cosserat rod, geometrically exact beam, warping, physics-augmented neural networks, hyperelasticity

Introduction

Recent advances in additive manufacturing have sparked the development of elastomeric beam-lattice structures. These metamaterials enable the design of architected mechanical properties unprecedented in classical materials, making them promising for many engineering applications
[
23
,
31
,
53
,
51
]
.
At the same time, numerical simulation of these metamaterials in both an
accurate
and
efficient
manner remains a significant challenge due to their multiscale nature and high nonlinearities on the material
[
57
]
as well as the structural scale
[
54
,
12
]
.
Accurate simulation of beam-lattice metamaterials starts at their smallest building block, i.e., the simulation of single beams. There, the highly nonlinear behavior of many 3D printing materials
[
57
]
and complex deformations of the beams
[
15
]
pose significant challenges. Furthermore, the often parameterized geometry of beams within a structure, e.g., varying radii, must be considered
[
11
]
.
Further applications and challenges for mechanical modeling of beam-like structures include flexible cables, hoses, and wires
[
9
]
, soft robotic actuators
[
7
]
, or textiles
[
41
,
8
]
.

The most accurate modeling approach for beams is their representation as volume continuum bodies. Thereby, no further assumptions on the behavior of the continuum body are made, rendering it a very non-intrusive approach. In theory, this allows the use of arbitrary nonlinear (and also inelastic) material models for the beam, as well as the representation of arbitrary complex deformations
[
23
,
53
]
.
However, the numerical methods used to solve the associated boundary value problems quickly reach their limits, as slender beams and locking phenomena can lead to vast numbers of degrees of freedom required for accurate numerical solutions
[
10
]
. This can cause computation times to be far outside an acceptable range for parametric studies, optimization, and other development workflows in engineering design.
Methods such as solid beam finite element (FE) formulations can alleviate these limitations to some degree
[
14
,
46
]
. However, the computational cost remains high if complex shapes and deformations of beam cross-sections are considered
[
14
]
.

The most efficient method for simulating slender structures is to model them as 1D bodies instead of 3D continua. These 1D bodies are referred to as
beams
. The geometrically exact Simo-Reissner beam theory
[
49
]
has been recognized as one of the most versatile, as it enables modeling anisotropic rigid cross-sections and initial curvatures and incorporates deformation from shear, longitudinal, bending, and torsion strain at arbitrarily large deformations
[
39
]
. In the literature, this beam model is also referred to as special Cosserat rod
[
3
]
, nonlinear Timoshenko beam
[
43
]
, or simply as
geometrically exact beam
.
The reduction from a 3D continuum to a 1D beam relies on several assumptions on the kinematics and constitutive behavior of the body. One assumption, which is particularly limiting, is the rigidity of the beam’s cross-section. Consequently, the geometrically exact beam theory is generally limited to small strains and applied using linear elastic constitutive models. However, these assumptions fail if beams are thick, strains are large, or the base material is considered outside of its linear elastic range, quickly reducing the accuracy of simple beam modeling approaches.
Therefore, some works have focused on including cross-sectional deformation in the geometrically exact beam theory by introducing additional degrees of freedom
[
48
,
5
]
. However, these approaches are still restricted to relatively simple cross-sectional deformations.

.

To overcome this limitation, some works focus on treating the geometrically exact beam as a multiscale problem, where the cross-sectional deformation is computed separately from the beam deformation in an FE
2
approach
[
4
,
33
,
35
,
18
,
19
]
, cf.
Fig.

1
. This enables modeling of in- and out-of-plane deformation for arbitrary cross-sectional shapes.
For given strains and curvatures of the beam, the resulting deformed cross-section is evaluated by solving a two-dimensional warping problem. From this, the resulting forces and moments can be calculated. Overall, this enables the simulation of very complex cross-sectional shapes and deformations and the use of nonlinear constitutive models for the beam.
In
[
33
,
4
]
, a warping problem is formulated that models the cross-sectional deformation for any combination of shear, longitudinal, bending, and twisting strain and is directly applicable in the framework of the geometrically exact Simo-Reissner beam theory.

While the accuracy of beam simulations is increased in a concurrent multiscale FE
2
setting, these approaches require computing the solution of a warping problem on the cross-section scale for every quadrature point of the simulation on the beam scale, which has to be repeated for every iteration of the numerical solver
[
20
]
. Solving boundary value problems across scales leads to a drastic increase in computational cost, undermining the main benefit of using beam models, i.e., efficiency. In
[
35
]
, this issue was addressed with a sequential multiscale approach, where the warping problem is solved for some sampled values in the strain space before conducting simulations on the beam scale. Together with a multidimensional interpolation scheme, the resulting database is then used in the macroscale beam simulation. While pre-solving can speed up the macroscale simulation, a sufficiently dense sampling quickly leads to large datasets and expensive data generation. This holds particularly if all six strain measures of a shear-deformable beam – and possibly additional geometric parameters describing the beam’s cross-section – are considered, in contrast to only four strain measures considered for the non-shearable beam in
[
35
]
.
To conclude, while these multiscale approaches for considering nonlinear material effects and warping offer excellent accuracy, their efficiency remains a drawback, which calls for efficient surrogate techniques for multiscale material modeling of beams.

Shifting the focus to constitutive modeling, various constitutive models for 3D continuum bodies based on
physics-augmented neural networks
(PANNs) have been proposed in recent years. These models combine the extraordinary flexibility of neural networks (NNs) with a sound mechanical basis, making them applicable to a wide range of materials
[
52
,
25
,
26
]
.
Furthermore, embedding mechanical knowledge in the models enables calibration with sparse datasets
[
36
]
.

In the finite elasticity theory of 3D continuum bodies, it is well understood how to formulate PANN constitutive models that fulfill all relevant mechanical conditions by construction. PANN models usually represent the corresponding energy potentials with an NN, which ensures thermodynamic consistency
[
36
]
. To fulfill objectivity and material symmetry, the potentials are typically formulated in terms of strain invariants
[
36
]
. However, some models only approximately fulfill these conditions by learning them through suitable calibration data
[
16
,
27
]
,
which is referred to as data augmentation
. Then, the NN potential is complemented by additional growth and normalization terms
[
27
,
36
]
. For polyconvex potentials, input-convex neural network architectures can be applied
[
27
,
2
]
. Finally, different approaches for representing parameterized material behavior were proposed
[
11
,
30
,
37
]
.

The extraordinary flexibility of these approaches enables their application to sequential multiscale methods of 3D solid bodies
[
26
,
38
,
29
]
. Thereby, PANN constitutive models act as surrogates for the homogenized behavior of microstructured materials. Since this is often a highly nonlinear functional relationship, conventional constitutive models usually are not flexible enough for this task
[
27
]
. Even for highly nonlinear material behavior, very shallow – often single-layered – NN architectures are sufficiently flexible and, thus, computationally efficient to evaluate.

While PANN approaches are well established for 3D continuum material modeling, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, they have not yet been applied to beam constitutive modeling. Consequently, the present work proposes a hyperelastic PANN constitutive model for geometrically exact, shear deformable beams. The model represents the relationship between strains/curvatures in the beam and the resulting forces/moments. Since the model is based on NNs, it can represent highly nonlinear behaviors, which are to be expected when considering hyperelastic base materials and warping in the beam’s cross-section.
The model is also physics-augmented, i.e., it is formulated to fulfill important mechanical conditions relevant to beam theory. First, thermodynamic consistency is considered by introducing a beam potential represented by an NN. Furthermore, objectivity is guaranteed by the choice of input and output, while projection terms ensure stress and energy normalization.
For
circular or ring-shaped
cross-sections
without

handedness
,
invariant-based transverse isotropy according to
[
3
,
17
]
is introduced in the model and compared to a more flexible point symmetry constraint. The latter can be derived from transverse isotropy but is found to be more suitable for deformable cross-sections
. Furthermore, a parameterization of the model
for ring-shaped cross-sections with different ratios between the inner and outer radii is proposed
.

This model aims to bridge the gap between accuracy and efficiency in FE
2
beam simulations. This is achieved by applying the PANN beam constitutive model as a surrogate for the warping problem proposed in
[
4
]
, ultimately providing a very
accurate
model, which represents both material and geometrical nonlinearities occurring on the cross-section scale of the beam while also being computationally very
efficient
.

The outline of the work is as follows. In
Section

2
, the fundamentals of the geometrically exact beam theory are introduced for both rigid and deformable cross-sections. In
Section

2
, the formulation of the PANN constitutive model is proposed. In
Section

Section

2
Fundamentals of geometrically exact beam theory

In this section, the geometrically exact, shear-deformable Cosserat rod, also called the Simo-Reissner beam theory, is introduced. After kinematics in
Section

2.1
and balance equations in
Section

2.2
, constitutive relations for rigid and deformable cross-sections are described in
2.3
. Finally, in
Section

2.4
, the cross-sectional warping problem is outlined.

2.1
Kinematics of the Cosserat rod

Figure 2
:
The geometrically exact beam in its initial (left) and current configuration (right).

In the Cosserat rod theory, a slender object is described via a centerline curve
\boldsymbol{r}:s\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and a rotation
\boldsymbol{R}:s\mapsto\text{SO}(3)
, where the arc length parameter
s\in[0,L]
denotes the position on the centerline
[
49
]
. The orientation of the cross-section is represented by the 3D rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{R}(s)=(\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{d}_{3})
, which consists of a set of orthonormal directors
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}
. Here, the out-of-plane component
\boldsymbol{d}_{3}(s)=\boldsymbol{d}_{1}(s)\times\boldsymbol{d}_{2}(s)
is normal to the cross-sectional plane spanned by the in-plane directors
\boldsymbol{d}_{\alpha}(s),\,\alpha=1,2
. Without loss of generality, we consider an initially straight beam with a constant cross-section starting at the origin of the global coordinate system with
\boldsymbol{D}_{i}=\boldsymbol{E}_{i}
, i.e., the beam’s directors in the undeformed configuration are aligned with the global coordinate system
and the cross-section’s principal axes of inertia
.

2.1.1
Rigid cross-section

Assuming a rigid cross-section, a material point in the undeformed configuration is given by

\boldsymbol{X}(s,\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})=\boldsymbol{s}(s)+\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}\,,

(1)

cf.
Fig.

2
. Here, the vector
\boldsymbol{s}(s)=s\boldsymbol{E}_{3}
points to the centerline, and
\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}=X_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{E}_{\alpha}
denotes the position vector in the cross-sectional plane. The directors in the initial and current configurations are related via

\boldsymbol{d}_{i}(s)=\boldsymbol{R}(s)\boldsymbol{D}_{i}.

(2)

Hence, a material point in the deformed configuration reads

\boldsymbol{x}(s,\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})=\boldsymbol{r}(s)+\boldsymbol{R}(s)\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}.

(3)

With
()^{\prime}=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}
denoting the derivative w.r.t. the arc length parameter
s
, the deformation gradient follows as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}(s,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{d}_{3}\right)+\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}\right)\otimes\boldsymbol{E}_{3}+\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{E}_{i}

(4)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{R}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\kappa}\times\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}\right)\otimes\boldsymbol{E}_{3}+\boldsymbol{I}\right]\,.

Here,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
denote the strain measures in the reference configuration

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\text{ax}\,\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\,,

(5)

where
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
capture shear strain, while
\epsilon_{3}
denotes axial stretch. Curvature and twist are represented by the components
\kappa_{1,2}
and
\kappa_{3}
, respectively. Since the strain measures
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
only depend on the arc-length parameter
s
, they prescribe the strain state for any position
\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}
on the cross-section. Hence, other authors have referred to them as
strain prescriptors

[
18
]
.
Furthermore,
Eq.

4
contains the decomposition of the deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{R}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}
into the rotation
\boldsymbol{R}(s)
and the
non-symmetric strain
tensor
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})
, where the latter depends exclusively on the position on the cross-section
\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}
and the vectors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(s)
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(s)
.
Note that the decomposition
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{R}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}
is different from the classical polar decomposition as
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}
is not a pure stretch tensor
[
42
]
.

2.1.2
Deformable cross-section

In general, the cross-section of a beam does not remain rigid but deforms depending on the beam’s strain and curvature state. Furthermore, the cross-sectional deformation at position
s
does not only depend on the local strain state
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(s),\boldsymbol{\kappa}(s))
but it is influenced by strains in the neighborhood of
s
as well
[
33
]
. Assuming slowly varying strains along the length of the beam, the latter effect can be neglected. This assumption was initially proposed by
[
33
]
and is known as the
helical Chauchy-Born rule
.
Based on this assumption, the current configuration of a beam with a deformable cross-section is defined as

\boldsymbol{x}(s,\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})=\boldsymbol{r}(s)+\boldsymbol{R}(s)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})\,.

(6)

Here,
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}
denotes the deformed configuration of the cross-section. It may be expressed as

\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})=\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}+\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}),

(7)

where
u_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})
denote the in-plane displacements and
u_{3}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})
denotes the out-of-plane warping
[
4
]
. Since
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})
does not depend on
s
, which results from assuming the helical Cauchy-Born rule, the deformation gradient can be derived analogously to
Eq.

4
. It reads

\boldsymbol{F}\big(s,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})\big)=\boldsymbol{R}(s)\left[\big(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\kappa}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}+\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\big)\otimes\boldsymbol{E}_{3}+\frac{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}}{\partial X_{\alpha}}\otimes\boldsymbol{E}_{\alpha}\right].

(8)

Here, we find the same strain measures as in
Eq.

5
. Note that this derivation does not include the implicit relation between
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}
and the strain measures
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
. Finding it will be the topic of
Section

2.4
.

2.2
Stress resultants and balance equations

The quasi-static balance equations of a beam in spatial description are given as

\begin{split}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}+\bar{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}&=\boldsymbol{0},\\
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}+\bar{\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}&=\boldsymbol{0}.\end{split}

(9)

Here,
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(s)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}(s)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
denote forces and moments acting on the cross-section of the beam, with

\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=n_{i}\boldsymbol{d}_{i},\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}=m_{i}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}.

(10)

The components
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
denote shear forces,
n_{3}
the normal force,
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
bending moments, and
m_{3}
the twisting moment.
Furthermore,
\bar{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}
and
\bar{\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
represent external distributed forces and moments, respectively. The boundary value problem for determining the deformed configuration
(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{R})
of the beam is complemented by Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions for
s=0
and
s=L
:

\begin{split}&\boldsymbol{r}(s)=\boldsymbol{r}_{s},\qquad\boldsymbol{R}(s)=\boldsymbol{R}_{s},\\
&\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(s)=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{s},\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}(s)=\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{s}.\end{split}

(11)

Here,
\boldsymbol{r}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
denote prescribed positions and rotations, respectively, while
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{s}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}
are externally applied forces and moments.

The stress resultants in the material description read

\boldsymbol{n}=n_{i}\boldsymbol{E}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{m}=m_{i}\boldsymbol{E}_{i}.

(12)

They are related to the spatial description as

\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{m}.

(13)

2.3
Constitutive relations

A constitutive model is required to relate the beam’s strains and curvatures to the resulting forces and moments. For this, assuming hyperelastic material behavior, the beam potential

\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\,,\qquad(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa})\mapsto\psi(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa})

(14)

is introduced, from which stress resultants are derived as

\boldsymbol{n}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{m}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}.

(15)

Furthermore, the stiffnesses can be derived from the beam potential as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\,,\qquad{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\,,\qquad{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\,.}

(16)

The formulation of the beam potential
\psi
determines if rigid or deformable cross-sections are considered and defines the material behavior in the beam’s cross-section. Note that
\psi
is associated with the beam scale and is not to be confused with the description of the beam’s base material in terms of a hyperelastic continuum constitutive model, cf.
Section

2.3.2
.

2.3.1
Linear elastic model

In its simplest form, the beam potential is given by the quadratic expression

\psi(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{T}{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\,.

(17)

Assuming
circular or ring-shaped
homogeneous cross-sections, the constitutive matrices take the diagonal shapes

{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\text{diag}\left[k_{1}GA,k_{2}GA,EA\right]\,,\qquad{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\text{diag}\left[EI_{1},EI_{2},GJ\right]\,,\qquad{\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(18)

Here,
E
and
G
denote Young’s and shear moduli, respectively, with the remaining parameters given as

A=\pi{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\big(R^{2}-R_{i}^{2}\big)}\,,\qquad I_{1}=I_{2}=\frac{\pi}{4}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\big(R^{4}-R_{i}^{4}\big)}\,,\qquad J=I_{1}+I_{2}\,,

(19)

where
R
is the outer and
R_{i}
the inner radius of a circular, ring-shaped cross-section (with
R>R_{i}\geq 0
),
and the shear correction factors are set to
k_{1}=k_{2}=\frac{5}{6}
.
This choice of potential implies a linear relation between strains and stresses, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{n}={\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{m}={\mathbb{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\,.

(20)

In the remainder of this work, this linear elastic constitutive model is referred to as “LEM”.

2.3.2
Hyperelastic model with rigid cross-section

Let us consider a hyperelastic base material of the beam represented by the potential

\bar{\psi}:\operatorname{GL}^{+}(3)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{F}\mapsto\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{F})\,.

(21)

The potential
\bar{\psi}
describes the nonlinear material behavior within the beam and its cross-section.
Furthermore assuming a rigid cross-section, as introduced in
Section

2.1.1
, the beam potential is calculated by integrating the potential
\bar{\psi}
over the cross-section, i.e.,

\psi(s)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{F}(s,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}))\,dX_{1}dX_{2},

(22)

where
\boldsymbol{F}
is the deformation gradient of the rigid cross-section as introduced in
Eq.

4
.
The stress resultants follow as
[
47
]

\boldsymbol{n}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\,dX_{1}dX_{2}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{m}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\boldsymbol{X}_{CS}\times\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\,dX_{1}dX_{2}\,,

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{F})=\partial\bar{\psi}/\partial\boldsymbol{F}
is the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.
In the remainder of this work, this hyperelastic constitutive model with rigid cross-section is referred to as “RHM”.

Remark 2.1
.

While the RHM model seems reasonable for considering the nonlinearity of a beam’s base material, it comes with a significant disadvantage over the LEM. This relates to the treatment of cross-sectional deformation. Strictly speaking, the LEM does not include any statement about the cross-section but instead depends exclusively on the values assigned to its parameters. However, in practice, the Young’s modulus
E
is determined for uniform cross-sectional deformation, and the shear correction factors
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are chosen to prevent overly stiff behavior under shear. Hence, cross-sectional deformation is included implicitly by the LEM. The RHM, in contrast, strictly enforces cross-sectional rigidity through
Eq.

4
, leading to undesired in-plane reaction forces
[
39
]
. The resulting overestimation of stiffness has been reported by several authors
[
4
,
33
]
and is demonstrated again in
Section

4.1.2
.

2.3.3
Hyperelastic model with deformable cross-section

In this case, hyperelastic behavior of the beam’s base material, as introduced in
Eq.

21
, is considered in conjunction with a deformable cross-section as introduced in
Section

2.1.2
. Based on these assumptions, the beam potential is derived as

\psi(s)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{F}(s,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})))\,dX_{1}dX_{2}\,,

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{F}
is the deformation gradient of the deformable cross-section as introduced in
Eq.

8
. The stress resultants follow as

\boldsymbol{n}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\,dX_{1}dX_{2}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{m}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}\times\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\,dX_{1}dX_{2}\,.

(25)

In the remainder of this work, this hyperelastic constitutive model with deformable cross-section is referred to as “DHM”.

2.4
Cross-sectional deformation with the warping problem

Before evaluating the DHM, the deformed cross-section
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}
for given strain measures
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
must be calculated. In this work, the method proposed by
[
4
]
is applied and briefly summarized now.
Note that in
[
4
]
, strains and stresses are assumed to vary slowly along the beam’s arc length, cf.
Section

2.3.3
.
The deformed configuration of the beam’s cross-section,
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}
, is defined by the state of minimal strain energy

\underset{\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}}{\mathrm{min}}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa},\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}(\boldsymbol{X}_{CS})))\,dX_{1}dX_{2}.

(26)

Here, the rotational part of the deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{F}
is set to
\boldsymbol{R}(s)=\boldsymbol{I}
, cf.
Eq.

8
, as it does not influence the strain energy. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{F}
no longer depends explicitly on the arc-length parameter
s
.

Two sets of constraints are introduced to prevent rigid body motions in the solution of
Eq.

26
. Firstly, the center of mass should remain in the origin of the local cross-sectional coordinate system. Therefore, three translational degrees of freedom are constrained by

\int_{\Omega_{0}}\rho\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}\,dX_{1}dX_{2}=\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(27)

Secondly, the directors of the local coordinate system should be aligned with the principal axes of the cross-section. Thus, the three rotational degrees of freedom are constrained by

\int_{\Omega_{0}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}\,dX_{1}dX_{2}=\boldsymbol{0},

(28)

where

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\hat{X}_{2}\hat{X}_{3}\\
\rho\hat{X}_{1}\hat{X}_{3}\\
\rho\hat{X}_{1}\hat{X}_{2}\\
\mathrm{arctan}\,\left(\cfrac{\hat{X}_{2}}{\hat{X}_{1}}\right)-\mathrm{arctan}\,\left(\cfrac{X_{2}}{X_{1}}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(29)

Note that
\mathfrak{m}
has four components despite being used to constrain three degrees of freedom.
According to
[
4
]
, including all four rotational constraints is relevant for circular cross-sections, while the first three constraints may be used for non-circular cross-sections. However, other authors have successfully forgone applying only constraints one, two, and four for circular and non-circular cross-sections
[
18
,
19
]
.

With the objective function in
Eq.

26
, subject to the constraints in
Eq.

27
and
Eq.

28
, the cross-sectional warping problem can be formulated as a constrained Lagrangian optimization problem. The Lagrange functional reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left[\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{F})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\rho\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}\right]dX_{1}dX_{2},

(30)

where the Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
are defined globally on the domain. Since this work focuses on homogeneous cross-sections, the dummy density
\rho=1\text{ kg}/\text{m}^{3}
is set from now on. The first variation of
Eq.

30
yields the weak form
[
4
]

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left[\left[\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{E}_{3}\times\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{M}}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right]\cdot\delta\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}+\right.\left.\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{E}_{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\partial\delta\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}}{\partial X_{\alpha}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{CS}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{\mu}\right]dX_{1}dX_{2}=0\,,

(31)

with the abbreviation

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{M}}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\hat{X}_{3}&\hat{X}_{2}\\
\hat{X}_{3}&0&\hat{X}_{1}\\
\hat{X}_{2}&\hat{X}_{1}&0\\
-\cfrac{\hat{X}_{2}}{\hat{X}_{1}^{2}+\hat{X}_{2}^{2}}&\cfrac{\hat{X}_{1}}{\hat{X}_{1}^{2}+\hat{X}_{2}^{2}}&0.\end{bmatrix}.

(32)

For a detailed explanation of the cross-sectional warping problem and the derivation of the strong form, the stress resultants, and the constitutive matrix, the reader is referred to
[
4
]
. The process of computing the stress resultants
\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m}
, and the beam potential
\psi
for given strain measures
\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa}
with the DHM and the warping problem is visualized in
Fig.

3
.

3
Physics-augmented neural network constitutive model

In this section, a physics-augmented neural network (PANN) constitutive model for hyperelastic beams is proposed. This model will be applied as a surrogate for the computationally expensive warping problem, and the DHM model discussed in
Section

2
.
Ultimately, this PANN provides a very accurate and computationally efficient surrogate model that represents both material and geometrical nonlinearities occurring on the cross-section scale of the beam.
After discussing the mechanical conditions relevant to beam constitutive modeling in
Section

3.1
, the PANN model is introduced in
Section

3.2
. This is followed by extensions of the PANN model to include
transverse isotropy and a less restrictive point symmetry in
Section

3.3
and
ends with a
geometric parameterization
for ring-shaped cross-sections
in
Section

3.4
.

3.1
Mechanical conditions

With the beam potential
\psi
introduced in
Section

2.3
and the beam’s forces and moments defined as the potential’s gradients,
thermodynamic consistency
is fulfilled by construction
[
50
]
. Moreover,
objectivity
is guaranteed by the definition of the strain measures according to
Eq.

5

[
3
]
.

Furthermore, in the strain-free state, the beam potential and the stress resultants should vanish, which is formalized as the
normalization
conditions

\psi\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\big\rvert_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}=0\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{q}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\big\rvert_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}=\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(33)

Here, the abbreviations
\boldsymbol{p}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa})
and
\boldsymbol{q}=(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m})
for the strain measures and stress resultants are introduced. This condition is fulfilled trivially in the LEM model described in
Section

2.3.1
. For the RHM and DHM models introduced in
Sections

2.3.2
and
2.3.3
, the normalization conditions are fulfilled since the potential
\bar{\psi}
applied as a base material model for the beam fulfills equivalent normalization conditions, cf.
[
36
]
.

Symmetry:

Symmetries in the beam’s cross-section imply symmetries in the beam’s constitutive response
[
3
,
17
]
.

In this work, only beams with circular or ring-shaped cross-sections without chirality or handedness are considered. Under these circumstances, a transversely isotropic symmetry of the constitutive behavior is typically considered
[
17
,
3
]
. The corresponding invariant-based potential is then expressed as

\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}})=\Gamma\big(\epsilon_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\alpha},\epsilon_{3},(\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha})^{2},\kappa_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha},\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\beta},(\kappa_{3})^{2},\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha}\kappa_{3}\big)\,,

(34)

where the arguments of
\Gamma
are invariants for transverse isotropy
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
. Here,
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}
denotes the Levi-Civita symbol, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\beta}=\epsilon_{1}\kappa_{2}-\epsilon_{2}\kappa_{1}

[
17
]
. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the condition in
Eq.

34
has only been shown for the RHM and not for the DHM undergoing large cross-sectional deformation.

It is is known for anisotropic hyperelasticity that symmetry properties can change between linear and finite deformations
[
24
]
. Similarly, it seems reasonable that
Eq.

34
, which was derived for beams with rigid cross sections, might not always hold for beams with deformable cross sections. This motivates the symmetry condition

\psi(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})=\psi(-\epsilon_{1},-\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3},-\kappa_{1},-\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})\,,

(35)

which may be interpreted as point symmetry w.r.t. the origin of the
\mathbb{R}^{4}
space of strain measures
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
. Clearly,
Eq.

35
fulfills the symmetry condition
Eq.

34
while being less restrictive. While the authors are unaware of any proof of
Eq.

35
for circular or ring-shaped deformable cross-sections,
Section

4.3.1
will investigate the utility of
Eq.

35
in formulating neural network-based material models compared to
Eq.

34
.

Scaling:

Another important property of the DHM model is the scaling behavior of the beam potential when multiplying the cross-sectional dimensions by a factor
\lambda
. According to
[
33
]
, the beam potential for a scaled deformable cross-section
\psi_{\lambda}
at given strain
\boldsymbol{p}_{\lambda}
can be computed by evaluating the beam potential of a reference cross-section
\psi
at the reference strain

\boldsymbol{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\lambda}\\
\lambda\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\lambda}\end{bmatrix}.

(36)

That is

\psi_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\lambda})=\lambda^{2}\psi(\boldsymbol{p}).

(37)

Remark 3.1
.

In continuum hyperelasticity, more mechanical conditions than the ones introduced above are applied
[
21
]
. However, their application to beams with deformable cross-sections is not straightforward.
For example, a
growth condition
is applied, ensuring that a material body can neither be compressed to zero volume nor dilated to an infinite volume. Similar growth conditions could be formulated for beam potentials. However,
Section

2.1
shows that for geometrically exact beams, this assumption translates not just to the material model but to a geometric problem. In
[
3
]
, growth conditions for beams with rigid cross-sections that consider the cross-sectional shape are derived. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no equivalent condition for beams with deformable cross-sections.
Other essential conditions of continuum hyperelasticity are grounded in the concept of convexity, i.e., ellipticity and polyconvexity
[
40
]
. Again, while similar convexity conditions can be formulated for beams with rigid cross-sections
[
42
]
, to the best of the author’s knowledge, there are no equivalent conditions for beams with deformable cross-sections.

3.2
NN-based beam potential

Based on the mechanical conditions for beam constitutive modeling introduced in
Section

3.1
, an NN-based beam potential similar to NN-based potentials of continuum hyperelasticity
[
36
,
27
]
can be formulated. The overall PANN model consists of an NN part, which is complemented by projection terms that ensure the normalization conditions, i.e.,

\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\psi^{\text{NN}}(\boldsymbol{p})+\psi^{\text{energy}}+\psi^{\text{stress}}(\boldsymbol{p})\,.

(38)

Here,
\psi^{\text{NN}}
denotes the NN part of the potential, which is given as a scalar-valued feed-forward neural network (FFNN) that receives the beam strain measures
\boldsymbol{p}
as input, i.e.,

\psi^{\text{NN}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\text{FFNN}(\boldsymbol{p})\,.

(39)

The normalization terms for energy
\psi^{\text{energy}}
and stress
\psi^{\text{stress}}
follow as the projection terms
[
22
,
12
]

\psi^{\text{energy}}=-\left.\psi^{\text{NN}}\right\rvert_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}\,,\qquad\psi^{\text{stress}}(\boldsymbol{p})=-\left.\frac{\partial\psi^{\text{NN}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{p}}\right\rvert_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\,.

(40)

The model’s flow and structure are visualized in
Fig.

4
. In this work, this architecture is signified by
{\mathcal{W}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

The NN part of the model
\psi^{\text{NN}}
provides excellent flexibility to capture the nonlinear constitutive behavior of hyperelastic beams. In this work, fully connected FFNNs
[
32
]
are applied to represent
\psi^{\text{NN}}
. With input
\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{6}
, output
\boldsymbol{x}_{H+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
H
hidden layers, the FFNNs are given as the mapping

\boldsymbol{x}_{h}=\sigma^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{W}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{x}_{h-1}+\boldsymbol{b}^{(h)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}}\,,\qquad h=1,...,H+1.

(41)

Here, the weights
\boldsymbol{W}^{(h)}
and biases
\boldsymbol{b}^{(h)}
are the parameters of the NN potential, which are optimized to fit a given dataset. The activation functions of the FFNN are denoted by
\sigma^{(h)}
. In this work, softplus activation functions are used in the hidden layers, while linear activation is used in the output layer.

As the PANN-based beam potential represented by
Eqs.

38
,
39
and
40
is a twice continuously differentiable function, the stress resultants and stiffness matrix components can be easily calculated as
\boldsymbol{q}=\partial\psi^{\text{PANN}}/\partial\boldsymbol{p}
and
\mathbb{C}=\partial^{2}\psi^{\text{PANN}}/\partial\boldsymbol{p}^{2}
. This involves computing the first and second derivatives of the FFNN concerning its inputs, which can be done via automatic differentiation or analytically using the chain rule according to the derivations in
[
13
,
44
]
.
Finally, the PANN-based beam potential can be combined with the scaling law described by
Eqs.

37
and
36

[
33
]
. In this work scaled models will be signified by
{\mathcal{W}}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\lambda})=\lambda^{2}\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\boldsymbol{p})
, where
\lambda
may be replaced with a cross-section specific scaling variable.

Figure 4
:
Physics-augmented neural network-based beam constitutive model. The inputs are the strains and curvatures
\boldsymbol{p}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\kappa})
, which are converted to the FFNN inputs
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}
– in the simplest case
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=\boldsymbol{p}
. The forces and moments
\boldsymbol{q}=(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m})
are derived from the beam strain energy
\psi^{\text{PANN}}
, the model output.

3.3
Enforcing symmetry

As discussed in
Section

3.1
, beams with circular deformable cross-sections may be expected to exhibit symmetric constitutive behavior, e.g., according to
Eq.

34
or
Eq.

35
. Here, we present how the PANN-based constitutive model presented in
Section

3.2
can be extended to fulfill these conditions by construction.

Transverse isotropy:

To enforce transverse isotropy, the strain measure-based NN potential from
Eq.

39
can be reformulated using the transversely isotropic invariants
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}}
. This approach is in close analogy to the hyperelastic invariant-based PANN models that have been developed for 3D continuum materials
[
28
,
36
]
. The transversely isotropic beam potential maintains the same basic structure as in
Eq.

38
. However, the NN term of the PANN potential now receives the transversely isotropic invariants as inputs:

\psi^{\text{NN}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}})=\text{FFNN}\big(\epsilon_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\alpha},\epsilon_{3},(\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha})^{2},\kappa_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha},\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\beta},\kappa_{3}^{2},\epsilon_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha}\kappa_{3}\big).

(42)

Consequently, the normalization term simplifies to

\psi^{\text{stress}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}})=-\left.\frac{\partial\psi^{\text{NN}}}{\partial\epsilon_{3}}\right\rvert_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}\epsilon_{3},

(43)

i.e., normalization must only be enforced for the stress resultant
n_{3}
, as the remaining stress resultants are automatically normalized through the quadratic and cubic nature of the invariants.
Finally, the corresponding energy normalization term is chosen as

\psi^{\text{energy}}=-\left.\psi^{\text{NN}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}})\right\rvert_{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}.

(44)

In the scope of this work, transversely isotropic models are signified by
{\mathcal{W}}^{\text{ti}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{ti}})
.

Point symmetry:

If no invariant-based formulation is available, symmetry can be enforced by modifying the strain measures before passing them to the PANN-based beam potential in
Eq.

38
. For the symmetry described by
Eq.

35
this can be done as follows:

Consider the strain state
\boldsymbol{p}=(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}},\epsilon_{3},\kappa_{3})
with the symmetric part of the strain vector
\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}}=(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
.
From
Eq.

35
, we can deduce that there is a second strain state
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}=(-\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}},\epsilon_{3},\kappa_{3})
whose material response is equivalent to
\boldsymbol{p}
.
To distinguish the two associated strain states, a hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}=\{\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}4}:f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})=0\}
is introduced using the linear function
f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})
. Here,
{\mathcal{H}}
could be any hyperplane that intersects the origin of the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
. For this work, the function

f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}

(45)

is used. With
Eq.

45
, the surrogate strain state
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}
is defined as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}{\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}}\\
\epsilon_{3}\\
\kappa_{3}\end{bmatrix}}&\text{if}\quad f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})<0\,,\\
{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}}\\
\epsilon_{3}\\
\kappa_{3}\end{bmatrix}}&\text{if}\quad f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})\geq 0\,.\end{array}\right.}

(46)

After evaluating
\psi^{\text{PANN}}
for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}
and taking the derivatives, the surrogate stress measures
\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\text{sym}},n_{3},m_{3})
are obtained and have to be reflected analogously to get the final stress measures:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}{\begin{bmatrix}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\text{sym}}\\
\epsilon_{3}\\
\kappa_{3}\end{bmatrix}}&\text{if}\quad f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})<0\,,\\
{\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\text{sym}}\\
\epsilon_{3}\\
\kappa_{3}\end{bmatrix}}&\text{if}\quad f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})\geq 0\,.\end{array}\right.}

(47)

Ultimately, the reflections conducted in
Eqs.

46
and
47
for
f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}})<0
must also be applied to all components of the stiffness matrix
\mathbb{C}
that contain mixed derivatives
\partial^{2}\psi^{\text{PANN}}/(\partial\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}}\partial\epsilon_{3})
or
\partial^{2}\psi^{\text{PANN}}/(\partial\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{sym}}\partial\kappa_{3})
between symmetric and non-symmetric strain measures. In this work, point symmetric PANN models are signified by
{\mathcal{W}}^{\text{sym}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}})
.

Ring parameterization

For practical application, a parameterization of the PANN-based beam potential with geometric parameters, e.g., the inner radius
R_{i}
of a circular cross-section, is highly desirable. For the LEM, such a parameterization is trivial as the cross-sectional area and moments of inertia can be directly calculated for any
R_{i}
according to
Eq.

19
. For the PANN model, building on
Eq.

38
or
Eq.

42
, the parameterized potential takes the form
1
1

1

For the sake of a simple notation, the same symbols are applied for parameterized and non-parameterized PANN models in this work.

\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\,,\qquad(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P)\mapsto\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P),

(48)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the FFNN input vector containing strain measures or invariants, and the parameter
P=R_{i}/R
is the ratio between the ring’s inner and outer radius with
0\leq P<1
. Note that the beam potential’s functional dependency on
P
is not restricted by the mechanical conditions introduced in
Section

3.1
, cf.
[
30
]
for discussion.

The parameterization can be incorporated into the PANN-based beam potential from
Eq.

38
, by passing
P
as an additional input to the FFNN, i.e,

\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P)=\psi^{\text{NN}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P)+\psi^{\text{energy}}(P)+\psi^{\text{stress}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P).

(49)

Consequently, the normalization terms now depend on the parameter
P
, cf.
[
30
]
for a discussion. In this work, ring-parameterized architectures according to
Eq.

49
are signified by
{\mathcal{W}}_{P}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P)=\psi^{\text{PANN}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},P)
. The architecture is visualized in
Fig.

5
.
Note that the
{\mathcal{W}}_{P}
model can be extended with transverse isotropy or point symmetry as presented in
Section

3.3
. Furthermore, scaling according to
Eq.

37
can be applied due to the dimensionless definition of
P

4.1
Data generation

Calibration of the PANN models requires datasets of the form

{\mathcal{D}}=\{(\prescript{1}{}{\boldsymbol{p}},\prescript{1}{}{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\prescript{2}{}{\boldsymbol{p}},\prescript{2}{}{\boldsymbol{q}}),...\}

(50)

for the non-parameterized models
{\mathcal{W}}
,
{\mathcal{W}}^{\text{ti}}
, and
{\mathcal{W}}^{\text{sym}}
as well as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{D}}_{P}}=\{(\prescript{1}{}{\boldsymbol{p}},\prescript{1}{}{\boldsymbol{q}},\prescript{1}{}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}),(\prescript{2}{}{\boldsymbol{p}},\prescript{2}{}{\boldsymbol{q}},\prescript{2}{}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}),...\}

(51)

or

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}}=\{(\prescript{1}{}{\boldsymbol{p}},\prescript{1}{}{\boldsymbol{q}},\prescript{1}{}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lambda}}),(\prescript{2}{}{\boldsymbol{p}},\prescript{2}{}{\boldsymbol{q}},\prescript{2}{}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lambda}}),...\}

(52)

for the parameterized
and scaled

architecture
{\mathcal{W}}_{P}

and
{\mathcal{W}}_{\lambda}
, respectively
. Acquisition of these datasets involves a two-step process, which starts with sampling values for the input quantities
\boldsymbol{p}
and
P
/
\lambda
and ends with computing the corresponding outputs
\boldsymbol{q}
.

4.1.1
Concentric sampling of strain measures

Sampling of the inputs
\boldsymbol{p}
and
P
is conducted in a seven-dimensional space. We require the sampled strain states to be physically sensible in the sense that the volumetric compression ratios fulfill the impenetrability condition
J=\det\boldsymbol{F}>0

[
21
]
anywhere on the cross-section. Furthermore, the sampled strain measures shall represent the space of admissible strain measures well while keeping the total number of samples as low as possible.

Concentric sampling is applied in this work to create a large variety of samples efficiently
[
34
]
. The idea is to decompose a vector into a unit direction
\boldsymbol{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{6}
and an amplitude
t
, which are sampled separately and afterward joined to form a vector. Here, the decomposition of the strain measures reads

\boldsymbol{p}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}\right\rVert\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol{p}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}\right\rVert}=t\boldsymbol{N}.

(53)

First, the directions are chosen to be uniformly distributed in the six-dimensional strain space
{\mathbb{R}}^{6}
. A method for generating uniformly distributed points on
n
-dimensional spheres using energy minimization is applied here
[
34
]
. Next, the number of strain amplitudes between the minimum and maximum values
t_{\text{min}}
and
t_{\text{max}}
is defined.

After joining directions and amplitudes, random perturbations are applied to every data point. The perturbed strain measures read

\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{perturbed}}=\boldsymbol{p}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}\right\rVert\cdot\epsilon\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{\text{rand}},

(54)

where
\boldsymbol{N}_{\text{rand}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{6}
is a random unit direction, which is regenerated for every data point, and
\epsilon
is a scalar that denotes the magnitude of the perturbation relative to the norm
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}\right\rVert
of the initial strain state. In practice,
\epsilon
should be chosen moderately to ensure that the jumps between two consecutive strain states are not too large during the numerical simulation. Lastly, the perturbed strain states are filtered by applying limits to every strain measure.
For this, the limits
\varepsilon_{1},\,\varepsilon_{2}\in[-0.2,\,0.2]
,
\varepsilon_{3}\in[-0.2,\,0.5]
, and
\kappa_{1},\,\kappa_{2},\,\kappa_{3}\in[-2\pi/L,\,2\pi/L]
are applied. These values are motivated by the conception of a beam with length
L=10
subjected to bending or twist of 360°, 20% compression, or 50% elongation.
Lastly, the physical sensibility of the sampled strain measures is evaluated. For that, a reference rectangle is placed around the cross-section. Theoretical deformation gradients at the corner points
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
to
\boldsymbol{X}_{4}
of the rectangle are computed with
Eq.

4
. If all four points comply with the impenetrability of matter
[
21
]
, i.e.,

\begin{split}&\det\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})>0\quad i\in\{1,2,3,4\},\end{split}

(55)

the associated strain measures are added to the input dataset. An example of a concentrically sampled load path with perturbation is shown in
Fig.

10(a)
.

In the FEniCS implementation of the warping problem, the rotational degrees of freedom were constrained following the practice presented in
[
4
]
, i.e., all four constraints from
Eq.

29
were used, which yielded reliable convergence.
On the cross-section scale, a Neo-Hookean hyperelastic material model with the following strain energy function was employed:

\bar{\psi}=\frac{\bar{\mu}}{2}\left(I_{1}-\log\left(J^{2}\right)-3\right)+\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{8}\log\left(J^{2}\right)^{2}

(56)

with

\bar{\mu}=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},\qquad\bar{\lambda}=\frac{2\bar{\mu}\nu}{1-2\nu},

(57)

where
\bar{\lambda}
refers to a Lamé parameter and is not to be confused with the scaling parameter presented in Eq. (
37
). Additionally,

I_{1}=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{F})
and
J=\det(\boldsymbol{F})
denote the first invariant
of the right Cauchy-Green tensor
\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}
and the Jacobi determinant of the deformation gradient, respectively. The material parameters
E=$70\text{\,}\mathrm{MPa}$
and
\nu=0.4
correspond to a thermoplastic polyurethane used for 3D printing with fused deposition modeling. The units will not be considered in the remainder of this work.

To understand the effect of cross-sectional deformation on the constitutive behavior, the stress resultants for the DHM, RHM, and LEM are plotted for pure shear and pure bending in
Fig.

7
. These two simple deformations were chosen because they already show the most relevant effects present in more complicated deformation states.

The comparison demonstrates that the LEM approximates the DHM well in the small strain regime with amplitude
t<0.2
. In this range, the LEM accurately captures the dominating transversal force
n_{1}
and the twisting moments
m_{1}
for shear and bending, respectively. It shows that the LEM implicitly accounts for some cross-sectional deformation through its constitutive parameters. In contrast, the RHM drastically overestimates the dominating stress resultants
n_{1}
and
m_{1}
in both cases.

Looking at larger strains with
t>0.2
, the differences in the stress resultants between the RHM and the DHM keep increasing. Furthermore, significant differences between the DHM and the LEM become observable. While the dominating stress resultants
n_{1}
and
m_{1}
are still captured decently by the LEM, it does not capture any of the distinct couplings with the normal force
n_{3}
. Simultaneously, the RHM only models the coupling for the pure bending case but not for pure shear.

The implications of allowing for cross-sectional deformation also become apparent in the right column of
Fig.

7
, where the deformed configurations computed by the warping problem are shown for the last load steps of the two load paths. Under pure shear strain, the cross-section exhibits out-of-plane warping and almost no in-plane deformation. In contrast, bending leads to large in-plane but no out-of-plane deformation.

Although the deformations in
Fig.

7
are specific to homogeneous circular cross-sections, it is evident that accounting for cross-sectional deformation is necessary when applying hyperelastic material models and large strains in the framework of geometrically exact beams. It leads to a more accurate representation of the beam stiffness and enables the modeling of coupling effects that the LEM neglects entirely. Nonetheless, coupling and nonlinearities are only relevant for large strains in the studied examples.

4.2
PANN model calibration

Next, the PANN models are fitted to the datasets
{\mathcal{D}}
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{P}
, or
{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
obtained with the DHM and the data generation procedure presented in
Section

4.1
. In the context of NNs, this process is known as training or calibration, where a loss function
{\mathcal{L}}
is minimized w.r.t. the model parameters.

In the supervised learning scenario relevant to this work, the loss represents the error between the predicted and the actual stress resultants for a given set of strain measures. However, the absolute error can be very unevenly distributed between the different stress resultants depending on
the parameter
P
and the scaling factor
\lambda
, which in this work correspond to the outer beam radius, i.e.,
\lambda=R
. For example, we
know that
n_{i}\sim R^{2}
and
m_{i}\sim R^{4}
hold for the LEM. Consequently,
n_{i}
and
m_{i}
can have different orders of magnitude depending on the beam radius. If the radius is large, as in
Fig.

7
, forces and moment have the same magnitude. Meanwhile, if the radius is reduced to
R/10
, the forces and moments are reduced by
10^{-2}
and
10^{-4}
, respectively, for a given strain.

While the radius-dependency may differ slightly for the DHM, different orders of magnitude pose a significant challenge to the training process. To enable equally accurate predictions for all stress resultants, the loss function is formulated as

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{6n}\sum_{i=1}^{6}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\prescript{ij}{}{w}\sum_{k=1}^{\prescript{j}{}{m}}\left\lVert\prescript{jk}{}{q_{i}}-q_{i}(\prescript{jk}{}{\boldsymbol{p})}\right\rVert^{2}.

(58)

Here,
n
denotes the number of unique
tuples
\prescript{j}{}{(R,P)}
in the dataset, and
\prescript{j}{}{m}
is the number of data points
belonging to a tuple
\prescript{j}{}{(R,P)}
. The weights
\prescript{ij}{}{w}
are defined as

\prescript{ij}{}{w}=\frac{1}{\prescript{j}{}{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{\prescript{j}{}{m}}\left\lVert\prescript{jk}{}{q_{i}}\right\rVert^{2},

(59)

resulting in one weight per combination of
\prescript{j}{}{(R,P)}
and stress resultant
q_{i}
. As the loss term is formulated in terms of
the first derivative
of the FFNN outputs w.r.t. its inputs, the training strategy used in this work represents a form of Sobolev training
[
6
]
. Note that
neither the
the value of the strain energy
\psi
, which is the immediate model output
, nor the stiffnesses/elasticity tensors as second derivatives were
considered during training in this work, as this was not deemed necessary or beneficial in previous studies

[
27
,
36
,
13
]
.

The PANN models presented in this work were implemented in Python 3.11 with Tensorflow 2.15 and calibrated using the ADAM optimizer, with a mini-batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 0.002. All calibrations were performed on an Intel i7-13700K CPU or comparable desktop PC hardware.

4.4
PANN model application

In this section, we illustrate the direct applicability of the PANN beam material model in beam simulations.
The aim is to showcase that considering nonlinear behavior and cross-section deformation in beams can lead to significantly different results than the standard assumption of linear elastic material behavior.

The DHM was not considered in this study, as implementing it would require complex run-time coupling of the numerical solution of the cross-section warping problem and the beam, which is avoided by using the PANN material model.

For this study
, the
PANN model is
implemented into a mixed isogeometric collocation method
[
55
]
.
For this purpose, the analytical expressions of the derivatives of the PANN, which are required to evaluate the stress measures
\boldsymbol{q}=\partial\psi^{\text{PANN}}/\partial\boldsymbol{p}
and the elasticity tensor
\mathbb{C}=\partial^{2}\psi^{\text{PANN}}/\partial\boldsymbol{p}^{2}
of the beam model, are implemented.
Since the PANN model requires just one hidden layer, the analytical first and second derivatives are particularly easy to compute and implement
[
13
]
.
For the results presented, the weights of the point symmetric model
{\mathcal{W}}^{\text{sym}}
for beam radius
R=1.0
corresponding to the training run with the lowest evaluation loss presented in
Section

4.3.2
are used.

For the isogeometric discretization of the beams, 22 B-spline basis functions with degree 5 and 16 knot spans (elements) are used. As a collocation approach is employed, the assembly of the force vector and tangent stiffness matrix requires 22 evaluations of the constitutive model.

Discussion.

In summary, the PANN beam model was demonstrated to be suitable for application in beam simulations. The coupling effects arising from the cross-section warping problem translate to visible changes in the deformation of thick beams. Furthermore, the PANN beam model is stable enough to simulate the challenging scenarios of buckling under compression
as well as compression of a lattice structure with buckling and contacts
.

The PANN can also be considered much more numerically efficient than the DHM for simulating lattice structures or optimizing beams or lattices with many iterations.
For instance, in the lattice simulation above with 216 beams and 22 evaluation points per beam,
216\cdot 22=4752
constitutive model evaluations are required per assembly. For the deformation-controlled simulation up to 25% compression, 50 load steps were used, which required roughly 6 Newton iterations per load step due to the instabilities and contacts, i.e., a total of
50\cdot 6=300
assemblies. Overall, this amounts to 1.4 million constitutive model evaluations (of stress resultants and stiffnesses) to carry out one nonlinear lattice simulation.
Using the PANN instead of the LEM only marginally increases the overall computational effort since constitutive model evaluations contribute only a small part of the numerical operations. On the other hand, in the DHM, 1.4 million cross-sectional warping FEM problems would have to be solved concurrently within the beam simulations.
Even if such simulations could be implemented extremely efficiently by tracing and storing the load paths of all 4752 evaluation points (which would require extensive memory), the numerical effort would still be much higher compared to the only 256 load paths generated for training the PANN
{\mathcal{W}}^{\text{sym}}
, compare
Section

4.3.2
.

5
Conclusion

The present work proposes a neural network-based constitutive model for hyperelastic, shear-deformable geometrically exact beams. The model is physics-augmented, i.e., formulated to fulfill important mechanical conditions relevant to beam constitutive modeling. As such, it aims to bridge the gap between accuracy and efficiency in multiscale beam simulations. This is achieved by applying the PANN beam constitutive model as a surrogate for the warping problem proposed in
[
4
]
, enabling efficient modeling of beams with both material and geometrical nonlinearities occurring on the cross-section.

First, in
Section

3
, physics-augmented neural network (PANN) constitutive models for hyperelastic beams are proposed. For this, the hyperelastic beam potential is represented with an NN, which receives the strains and curvatures of the beam as inputs. Forces and moments are obtained as the gradients of the beam potential, ensuring thermodynamic consistency. To fulfill normalization conditions, the NN potential is complemented by additional projection terms. For beams with
circular or ring-shaped
cross-sections
and no chirality
,
extensions to transverse isotropy and a derived point symmetry constraint are proposed
.

Additionally, a parameterized model fulfilling all the aforementioned mechanical conditions is formulated for ring-shaped cross-sections with different ratios between the inner and outer radius.

In
Section

4
, the proposed models are calibrated to data generated for beams with hyperelastic base materials and deformable cross-sections. Strains and curvatures are sampled using a concentric sampling strategy. Data is generated for beams with circular
and ring-shaped
cross-sections and different
inner and outer
radii.
The proposed PANN models yield excellent predictions of the beam’s forces and moments in all studied cases.
In particular, the ring-parameterized PANN model accurately predicts the stress resultants across a large range of ring shapes. Furthermore, the scaling behavior of the proposed models was improved significantly through a data augmentation approach.

Finally, the straightforward applicability of our approach for beam simulations is demonstrated with several numerical examples for beams and beam structures subject to complex deformation scenarios.

The proposed model can now readily be applied for hyperelastic base materials and subsequent simulation or design optimization of soft, deformable beams, e.g., cables and additively manufactured lattice structures.
Taking inspiration from recent advances in PANN constitutive modeling for continuum bodies
[
45
]
, future work will be concerned with formulating inelastic PANN constitutive models for beams.
