Fractional Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problems, II
M. Dehghan and A. B. Mingarelli

Abstract
We continue the study of a non self-adjoint fractional three-term Sturm-Liouville boundary value problem (with a potential term) formed by the composition of a left Caputo and left-Riemann-Liouville fractional integral under Dirichlet type boundary conditions. We study the existence and asymptotic behavior of the real eigenvalues and show that for certain values of the fractional differentiation parameter α \alpha , 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 , there is a finite set of real eigenvalues and that, for α \alpha near 1 / 2 1/2 , there may be none at all. As α → 1 − \alpha\to 1^{-} we show that their number becomes infinite and that the problem then approaches a standard Dirichlet Sturm-Liouville problem with the composition of the operators becoming the operator of second order differentiation.

1
Introduction

This is a continuation of
[
1
]
where the results therein are extended to three-term Fractional Sturm-Liouville operators (with a potential term) formed by the composition of a left Caputo and left-Riemann-Liouville fractional integral. Specifically, the boundary value problem is of the form,

-^{c}\mathscr{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}\circ\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)+q(t)y(t)=\lambda y(t),\qquad 1/2<\alpha<1,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,

(1.1)

with boundary conditions

\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t)|_{t=0}=c_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t)|_{t=1}=c_{2},

(1.2)

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are real constants and the real valued unspecified potential function,
q\in L^{\infty}[0,1]
. We note that these are not self-adjoint problems and so there may be non-real spectrum, in general. A well-known property of the Riemann-Liouville integral gives that if the solutions are continuous on
[0,1]
then the boundary conditions (
1.2
) reduce to the usual fixed end boundary conditions,
y(0)=y(1)=0
, as
\alpha\to 1
.

For the analogue of the Dirichlet problem described above we study the existence and asymptotic behavior of the real eigenvalues and show that for each
\alpha
,
0<\alpha<1
, there is a finite set of real eigenvalues and that, for
\alpha
near
1/2
, there may be none at all. As
\alpha\to 1^{-}
we show that their number becomes infinite and that the problem then approaches a standard Dirichlet Sturm-Liouville problem with the composition of the operators becoming the operator of second order differentiation acting on a suitable function space.

Preliminaries

We recall some definitions from Fractional Calculus and refer the reader to our previous paper
[
1
]
for further details.

Definition 2.1

The left and the right Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals
\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}
and
\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}
of order
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
are defined by

\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{a}^{t}\frac{f(s)}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}ds,\quad t\in(a,b],

(2.1)

and

\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}f(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{t}^{b}\frac{f(s)}{(s-t)^{1-\alpha}}ds,\quad t\in[a,b),

(2.2)

respectively. Here
\Gamma(\alpha)
denotes Euler’s Gamma function. The following property is easily verified.

Property 2.1

For a constant
C
, we have
\mathscr{I}_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}C=\frac{(t-a)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\cdot C
.

The proof is by direct calculation.

Definition 2.2

The left and the right Caputo fractional derivatives
{}^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}
and
{}^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}
are defined by

^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(t):=\mathscr{I}^{n-\alpha}_{a^{+}}\circ\mathscr{D}^{n}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}\int_{a}^{t}\frac{f^{(n)}(s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha-n+1}}ds,\quad t>a,

(2.3)

and

^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}f(t):=(-1)^{n}\mathscr{I}^{n-\alpha}_{b^{-}}\circ\mathscr{D}^{n}f(t)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}\int_{t}^{b}\frac{f^{(n)}(s)}{(s-t)^{\alpha-n+1}}ds,\quad t<b,

(2.4)

respectively, where
f
is sufficiently differentiable and
n-1\leq\alpha<n
.

Definition 2.3

Similarly, the left and the right Riemann-Liouville fractional derivatives
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}
and
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}
are defined by

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(t):=\mathscr{D}^{n}\circ\mathscr{I}^{n-\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\int_{a}^{t}\frac{f(s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha-n+1}}ds,\quad t>a,

(2.5)

and

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}f(t):=(-1)^{n}\mathscr{D}^{n}\circ\mathscr{I}^{n-\alpha}_{b^{-}}f(t)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\int_{t}^{b}\frac{f(s)}{(s-t)^{\alpha-n+1}}ds,\quad t<b,

(2.6)

respectively, where
f
is sufficiently differentiable and
n-1\leq\alpha<n
.

Property 2.2

For
\Re(\nu)>-1
,
0<\alpha<1
, and
t>0
, we have

D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}(t^{\nu})=\frac{\Gamma(1+\nu)}{\Gamma(1+\nu-\alpha)}t^{\nu-\alpha}

Property 2.3

For
\Re(\nu)>0
,
0<\alpha<1
, and
t>0
, we have

{}^{c}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}(t^{\nu})=\frac{\Gamma(1+\nu)}{\Gamma(1+\nu-\alpha)}t^{\nu-\alpha}

Property 2.4

If
y(t)\in L^{1}(a,b)
and
\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{a^{+}}y,\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{b^{-}}y\in AC[a,b]
, then

\begin{split}\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}y(t)&=y(t)-\frac{(t-a)^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{a^{+}}y(a),\\
\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}y(t)&=y(t)-\frac{(b-t)^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{b^{-}}y(b).\end{split}

Property 2.5

If
y(t)\in AC[a,b]
and
0<\alpha\leq 1
, then

\begin{split}\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}{{}^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}}y(t)&=y(t)-y(a),\\
\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}{{}^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}}y(t)&=y(t)-y(b).\end{split}

Property 2.6

For
0<\alpha<1
we have

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(t)=\frac{f(a)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}(t-a)^{-\alpha}+^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(t)

The Mittag-Leffler function

The function
E_{\delta}(z)

E_{\delta}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\delta}}{\Gamma(\delta k+1)},\quad(z\in\mathbb{C},\Re(\delta)>0),

(2.7)

(2.8)

The generalized Mittag-Leffler function

(2.9)

and

\theta=1

coincides with the Mittag-Leffler function (
2.7

E_{\delta,1}(z)=E_{\delta}(z).

(2.10)

Two other particular cases of (
2.9
) are as follows:

E_{1,2}(z)=\frac{e^{z}-1}{z},\quad E_{2,2}(z)=\frac{\sinh(\sqrt{z})}{\sqrt{z}}.

(2.11)

Property 2.7

For any
\delta
with
\Re(\delta)>0
and for any

we have

E_{\delta,\delta}(z)=\frac{1}{z}E_{\delta,0}(z)

Further properties of this special function may be found in
[
2
]
.

Property 2.8

(See
[
3
]
, p.43.)
If

and
\mu\in(\frac{\delta\pi}{2},\min(\pi,\delta\pi))

has the following exponential expansion as

\displaystyle E_{\delta,\theta}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\delta}z^{\frac{1-\theta}{\delta}}\exp(z^{\frac{1}{\delta}})-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta-\delta k)}\frac{1}{z^{k}}+O(\frac{1}{z^{N+1}}),\qquad|\arg(z)|\leq\mu,\\
\\
-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta-\delta k)}\frac{1}{z^{k}}+O(\frac{1}{z^{N+1}}),\qquad\mu\leq|\arg(z)|\leq\pi.\end{array}\right.

3
Existence and uniqueness of the solution of SLPs

First, we proceed formally. Separating terms in (
1.1
), we get

{}^{c}\mathscr{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}\circ\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)=(q(t)-\lambda)y(t),\qquad 1/2<\alpha<1,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Taking the left Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals
\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}
on both sides of the above equation and using Property
2.5
, we have

\mathscr{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}y(t)-\mathscr{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}y(t)|_{t=0}=\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}((q(t)-\lambda)y(t)).

Taking the left Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals
\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}
from both sides of the above equation once again and using Property
2.4
, we get

y(t)-\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)|_{t=0}-\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}(\mathscr{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}y(t)|_{t=0})=\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}(\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}((q(t)-\lambda)y(t)))

Using Property
2.1
, we can write

y(t)=c_{1}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+c_{2}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\mathscr{I}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}(\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}((q(t)-\lambda)y(t)))

in which

c_{1}=\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)|_{t=0},\qquad c_{2}=\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)|_{t=0}.

We get through the double fractional integral in the above equation as follows

y(t)=c_{1}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+c_{2}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\frac{1}{\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{(q(r)-\lambda)y(r)}{(s-r)^{1-\alpha}}dr\right)ds.

By changing the order of integrals in the above equation we get

y(t)=c_{1}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+c_{2}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\frac{1}{\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(q(r)-\lambda)y(r)\left(\int_{r}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}ds\right)dr

Solving the inner integral gives us

y(t,\lambda)=c_{1}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+c_{2}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(q(s)-\lambda)y(s,\lambda)(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}ds.

(3.1)

We will now show that (
3.1
) has a solution that exists in a neighbourhood of
t=0
and is unique there. Working backwards will then provide us with a unique solution to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). Although this result already appears in
[
4
]
, we give a shorter proof part of which will be required later.

To this end, let
t>0
. Define

y_{n}(t,\lambda)=y_{0}(t,\lambda)+\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}(q(s)-\lambda)y_{n-1}(s,\lambda)ds,

(3.2)

where

y_{0}(t,\lambda)=c_{1}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+c_{2}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.

(3.3)

Let
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
,
|\lambda|<\Lambda
, where
\Lambda>0
is arbitrary but fixed. Then,

\begin{split}|y_{1}(t,\lambda)-y_{0}(t,\lambda)|&\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}|q(s)-\lambda||y_{0}(s,\lambda)|ds\\
&\leq\frac{||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}|y_{0}(s,\lambda)|ds,\end{split}

(3.4)

in which
||q||_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|q(t)|
.
Substituting (
3.3
) in (
3.4
) and using the fact that,

\int_{a}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}(s-a)^{\beta-1}ds=\frac{(t-a)^{\alpha+\beta-1}\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)},

we have

|y_{1}(t,\lambda)-y_{0}(t,\lambda)|\leq(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)\left(\frac{c_{1}}{\Gamma(3\alpha)}t^{3\alpha-1}+c_{2}\frac{t^{3\alpha}}{\Gamma(3\alpha+1)}\right),

Now, for
n=2
in (
3.2
) we get

\begin{split}|y_{2}(t,\lambda)-y_{1}(t,\lambda)|&\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}|q(s)-\lambda||y_{1}(s,\lambda)-y_{0}(s,\lambda)|ds\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}|q(s)-\lambda|\left((||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)(\frac{c_{1}}{\Gamma(3\alpha)}s^{3\alpha-1}+c_{2}\frac{s^{3\alpha}}{\Gamma(3\alpha+1)})\right)ds\\
&\leq(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{2}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{\Gamma(5\alpha)}t^{5\alpha-1}+\frac{c_{2}}{\Gamma(5\alpha+1)}t^{5\alpha}\right).\end{split}

(3.5)

Continuing in this way we get that the series

y_{0}(t,\lambda)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(y_{n}(t,\lambda)-y_{n-1}(t,\lambda))

(3.6)

where

\begin{split}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|y_{n}(t,\lambda)-y_{n-1}(t,\lambda)|&\leq c_{1}t^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{n}}{\Gamma(2n\alpha+\alpha)}t^{2n\alpha+\alpha}+c_{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{n}}{\Gamma(2n\alpha+\alpha+1)}t^{2n\alpha+\alpha}.\end{split}

(3.7)

converges uniformly on compact subsets of
(0,1]
. Denote the sum of the infinite series in (
3.6
) by

. So, by virtue of (
3.3
) and (
3.7
), (
3.6
) gives us,

\begin{split}|y(t,\lambda)|&\leq|y_{0}(t,\lambda)|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|y_{n}(t,\lambda)-y_{n-1}(t,\lambda)|\\
&\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}t^{\alpha-1}+\frac{c_{2}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}t^{\alpha}+c_{1}t^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{n}}{\Gamma(2n\alpha+\alpha)}t^{2n\alpha+\alpha}+c_{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{n}}{\Gamma(2n\alpha+\alpha+1)}t^{2n\alpha+\alpha}\\
&=c_{1}t^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{n}}{\Gamma(2n\alpha+\alpha)}t^{2n\alpha+\alpha}+c_{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)^{n}}{\Gamma(2n\alpha+\alpha+1)}t^{2n\alpha+\alpha}\\

Note that for a solution
y(t,\lambda)
of (

) to be
C([0,1])
, it is necessary and sufficient that
c_{1}=0
, i.e.,
\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)|_{t=0}=0
.
This then proves the global existence of a solution of (

) on
[\delta,1]
,
\delta>0
, since
q\in L^{\infty}[0,1]
for given

and

, as defined in (

).

From the proof comes the following
a-priori
estimate when
c_{1}=0
, that is,

\displaystyle|y(t,\lambda)|\leq c_{2}{t^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\bigg|E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}((||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)t^{2\alpha})\bigg|\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\bigg|E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}((||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)t^{2\alpha})\bigg|\right)

valid for each
t\in[0,1]
and all
|\lambda|<\Lambda
.

The previous bound can be made into an absolute constant by taking the sup over all t and
|\lambda|<\Lambda
. Of course, the bound goes to infinity as
|\lambda|\to\infty

\displaystyle|y(t,\lambda)|\leq c_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\sup_{|\lambda|<\Lambda,t\in[0,1]}\bigg|E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}((||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)t^{2\alpha})\bigg|\right)

\displaystyle=c_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+|E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}((||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda))|\right):=c_{3}.

(3.8)

for all
|\lambda|<\Lambda,t\in[0,1]
. Uniqueness follows easily by means of Gronwall’s inequality, as usual. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that (
3.1
) has two solutions
y(t,\lambda),z(t,\lambda)
. Since

and
|\lambda|<\Lambda
we can derive that,

|y(t,\lambda)-z(t,\lambda)|\leq\varepsilon e^{\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha)}(||q||_{\infty}+\Lambda)\frac{t^{2\alpha}}{2\alpha}}.

4
Another integral equation

In the previous section we showed that (
3.1
) has a solution that, for each
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
, exists on
[0,1]
, is unique, and is continuous there if and only if
c_{1}=0
. On the other hand, if
c_{1}\neq 0
then the solution is merely continuous on all compact subsets of
(0,1]
. In this section we find another expression for the integral equation which is equivalent to both (
3.1
) and the problem (
1.1
) with boundary conditions (
1.2
).

Lemma 4.1

For
0<\alpha<1
and
0<t<1
, we have

-^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)=\lambda t^{\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})

Proof 1

Using properties of the Mittag-Leffler function we can write

\begin{split}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)&=\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k+\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+\alpha)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{2\alpha k+\alpha-1}\right)}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+\alpha)}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k-1}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k)}\\
&=t^{-1}E_{2\alpha,0}(\lambda t^{2\alpha})\\
&=-\lambda t^{2\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha}),\end{split}

(4.1)

in which the third and the last equalities come from Property
2.2
and Property
2.7
, respectively. Now, taking the left Caputo fractional derivative of both sides of (
4.1
) we get

\begin{split}-^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)&=^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(\lambda t^{2\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)\\
&=\lambda^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k+2\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+2\alpha)}\right)\\
&=\lambda\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}{{}^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}}\left(t^{2\alpha k+2\alpha-1}\right)}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+2\alpha)}\right)\\
&=\lambda t^{\alpha-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+\alpha)}\\
&=\lambda t^{\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\end{split}

as required.

Lemma 4.2

For
0<\alpha<1
and
0<t<1
, we have

-^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)=\lambda t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})

Proof 2

Once again, using the properties of the Mittag-Leffler function we can write

\begin{split}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)&=\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k+\alpha}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+\alpha+1)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{2\alpha k+\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+\alpha+1)}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+1)}\\
&=E_{2\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha}).\end{split}

(4.2)

in which the third equality comes from Property
2.3
. Now, taking the left Caputo fractional derivative of both sides of (
4.2
) we get

\begin{split}{{}^{c}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)&={{}^{c}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(E_{2\alpha,1}(\lambda t^{2\alpha})\right)\\
&={{}^{c}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+1)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}{{}^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}}\left(t^{2\alpha k}\right)}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+1)}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{\alpha(2k-1)}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha(2k-1))}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k+1}t^{\alpha(2k+1)}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha(2k+1))}\\
&=-\lambda t^{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}t^{2\alpha k}}{\Gamma(2k\alpha+\alpha+1)}\\
&=-\lambda t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\end{split}

as desired.

Lemma 4.3

For
0<\alpha<1
and
0<t<1
, we have

-^{c}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})q(s)y(s)ds\right)=-q(t)y(t)+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})q(s)y(s)ds

Proof 3

Let
c_{4}=1/\Gamma(1-\alpha)
. Observe that,

\begin{split}\mathscr{I}^{1-\alpha}_{0^{+}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{2\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})q(s)y(s)ds&=c_{4}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\int_{0}^{r}(r-s)^{2\alpha-1}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda(r-s)^{2\alpha})q(s)y(s)ds}{(t-r)^{\alpha}}dr\\
&=c_{4}\int_{0}^{t}q(s)y(s)\left(\int_{s}^{t}\frac{(r-s)^{2\alpha-1}}{(t-r)^{\alpha}}E_{2\alpha,2\alpha}(-\lambda(r-s)^{2\alpha})dr\right)ds\\
&=c_{4}\int_{0}^{t}q(s)y(s)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{\Gamma(2\alpha k+2\alpha)}\int_{s}^{t}\frac{(r-s)^{2\alpha-1+2\alpha k}}{(t-r)^{\alpha}}dr\right)ds\\

This is clear from the definition, the possible values of
\alpha
, and since
y(t,\lambda)
is itself entire and of order at most
1/2\alpha
, from Lemma

.

Lemma 7.6

The boundary value problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) has infinitely many complex eigenvalues (real eigenvalues are not to be excluded here).

Proof 13

By Lemma
7.5
, we know that
\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t,\,\lambda)
is entire for each
t\in[0,1]
, and
1/2<\alpha<1
as well. So, the eigenvalues of our problem are given by the zeros of
\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(1,\,\lambda)
, which must be countably infinite in number since the latter function is of fractional order
1/2\alpha
(on account of the restriction on
\alpha
). This gives us the existence of infinitely many eigenvalues, generally in
\mathbf{C}
.

Next, we give the asymptotic distribution of these eigenvalues when
\alpha
is either very close to
1/2
from the right or very close to
1
from the left. Recall (
6.2
) with
c_{2}=1
, so that

y(t,\lambda)=t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{E_{2\alpha,0}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})}{\lambda(t-s)}q(s)y(s,\lambda)\,ds.

(7.4)

Keeping in mind the boundary condition (
1.2
) at
t=1
, we calculate
\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t,\,\lambda)
and then evaluate this at
t=1
in order to find the dispersion relation for the eigenvalues.

A straightforward though lengthy calculation using (
7.4
) and the definition of the Mittag-Leffler functions show that

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t,\,\lambda)=\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}\{t^{\alpha}E_{2\alpha,\alpha+1}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})\}+\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{E_{2\alpha,0}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})}{\lambda(t-s)}q(s)y(s,\lambda)\,ds.\right),

(7.5)

\displaystyle=tE_{2\alpha,2}(-\lambda t^{2\alpha})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{E_{2\alpha,1-\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}q(s)y(s,\lambda)\,ds

(7.6)

so that the eigenvalues of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) are given by those
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
such that

\displaystyle E_{2\alpha,2}(-\lambda)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{E_{2\alpha,1-\alpha}(-\lambda(1-s)^{2\alpha})}{(1-s)^{\alpha}}q(s)y(s,\lambda)\,ds=0.

(7.7)

Let us consider first the case where
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
. Lemma
7.3
implies that the right side of (
7.1
) tends to
0
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Indeed this, combined with (
7.2
), implies that

\left|\frac{E_{2\alpha,1-\alpha}(-\lambda(t-s)^{2\alpha})}{\lambda(t-s)^{\alpha}}\,y(s,\lambda)\right|=\Large{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}\right)

for all sufficiently large
\lambda
.

Thus, the real eigenvalues of the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) become the zeros of a transcendental equation of the form,

E_{2\alpha,2}(-\lambda)+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)=0.

We are concerned with the asymptotic behaviour of these real zeros. Recall the distribution of the real zeros of
E_{2\alpha,2}(-\lambda)
in
[
1
]
. There we showed that, for each
n=0,1,2,\ldots,N^{*}-1
, where
N^{*}
depends on
\alpha
, the interval

I_{n}(\alpha):=\left(\left(\frac{(2n+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha})\pi}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})}\right)^{2\alpha},\quad\left(\frac{(2n+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha})\pi}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})}\right)^{2\alpha}\right),

(7.8)

always contains at least two real zeros of
E_{2\alpha,2}(-\lambda)
. For
\alpha\to 1
, these intervals approach the intervals

\left((2n+1)^{2}\,\pi^{2},(2n+2)^{2}\,\pi^{2}\right),

whose end-points are each eigenvalues of the Dirichlet problem for the classical equation
-y^{\prime\prime}=\lambda\,y
on
[0,1]
. Since each interval
I_{n}
contains two zeros we can denote the first of these two zeros by
\lambda_{2n}(\alpha)
. Equation (
7.8
) now gives the
a-priori
estimate

\left(\frac{(2n+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha})\pi}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})}\right)^{2\alpha}\leq\lambda_{2n}(\alpha)\leq\left(\frac{(2n+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha})\pi}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})}\right)^{2\alpha}.

(7.9)

For each
\alpha<1
, and close to
1
, and for large
\lambda
, the real zeros of the preceding equation approach those of
E_{2\alpha,2}(-\lambda)
and spread out towards the end-points of intervals of the form (
7.8
). For
\alpha
close to
1/2
there are no zeros, the first two zeros appearing only when
\alpha\approx 0.7325
. For
\alpha
larger than this critical value, the zeros appear in pairs and in intervals of the form (
7.8
).

Next, recall that for
\alpha<1
there are only
finitely many
such real zeros, (see
[
1
]
) their number growing without bound as
\alpha\to 1
. It also follows from Lemma
7.6
that, for each
\alpha
, the remaining infinitely many eigenvalues must be non-real. As
\alpha\to 1^{-}
these non-real eigenvalues tend to the real axis thereby forming more and more real eigenvalues until the spectrum is totally real when
\alpha=1
and the problem then reduces to a (classical) regular Sturm-Liouville problem.

Finally, for
\alpha
close to
1
, (
7.9
) leads to the approximation,

\lambda_{2n}(\alpha)\approx\left(\frac{(2n+2)\pi}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})}\right)^{2\alpha},

from which this, in conjunction with (
7.8
) and
\alpha\to 1
, we can derive the classical eigenvalue asymptotics,
\lambda_{n}\sim n^{2}\pi^{2}
as
n\to\infty
.

8
Closing remarks

We have shown that the fractional eigenvalue problem

-^{c}\mathscr{D}_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}\circ\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}y(t)+q(t)y(t)=\lambda y(t),\qquad 1/2<\alpha<1,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,

with mixed Caputo and Riemann-Liouville derivatives subject to the boundary conditions involving the Riemann-Liouville integrals,

\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t)|_{t=0}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{I}_{0^{+}}^{1-\alpha}y(t)|_{t=1}=0,

admits, for each
\alpha
under consideration, and for eigenfunctions that are in
C[0,1]
, a finite number of real eigenvalues and an infinite number of non-real eigenvalues. The real eigenvalues, though finite in number for each
\alpha
, are approximated by (
7.8
) and (
7.9
), which as
\alpha\to 1
gives the classical asymptotic relation

\lambda_{n}\sim n^{2}\pi^{2}
as
n\to\infty
.

As
\alpha\to 1^{-}
we observe that the spectrum obtained approaches the Sturm-Liouville spectrum of the classical problem

-y^{\prime\prime}+q(t)y=\lambda y,\quad\quad y(0)=y(1)=0.

The same results hold if the eigenfunctions are merely
C(0,1]
(i.e.,
c_{1}\neq 0
) except that now the latter have an infinite discontinuity at
t=0
for each
\alpha
. The proofs are identical and are therefore omitted.
