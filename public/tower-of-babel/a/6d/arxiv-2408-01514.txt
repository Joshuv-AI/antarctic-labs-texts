Abstract Left-Definite Theory: A Model Operator Approach, Examples, Fractional Sobolev Spaces, and Interpolation Theory Thanks: P. H. is partially supported by grant #521719 from the Simons Foundation.
Christoph Fischbacher, Fritz Gesztesy, Paul Hagelstein, Lance L. Littlejohn

Abstract
. We use a model operator approach and the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators in a Hilbert space to derive the basic results of abstract left-definite theory in a straightforward manner. The theory is amply illustrated with a variety of concrete examples employing scales of Hilbert spaces, fractional Sobolev spaces, and domains of (strictly) positive fractional powers of operators, employing interpolation theory. In particular, we explicitly describe the domains of positive powers of the harmonic oscillator operator in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}) ( and hence that of the Hermite operator in OPEN L 2 ​ ( ℝ , e − x 2 ​ d ​ x ) ) L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx)\big) ) in terms of fractional Sobolev spaces, certain commutation techniques, and positive powers of (the absolute value of) the operator of multiplication by the independent variable in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}) .

Introduction and a Brief Historical Overview of General Left-Definite Theory

Left-definite theory has its roots in the theory of second-order
Sturm–Liouville differential equations. Indeed, consider the Sturm–Liouville
(SL) generalized eigenvalue equation

(\tau f)(x):=r(x)^{-1}\big[(-p(x)f^{\prime}(x))^{\prime}+q(x)f(x)\big]=\lambda f(x)\,\text{ for a.e.~$x\in(a,b)$,}

(1.1)

with
(a,b)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. Here, we make the
usual assumptions that
1/p,

q,

r
are real-valued (Lebesgue) a.e. on
(a,b)
, locally integrable
on
(a,b)
, with
p>0
a.e. on
(a,b)
and
f\in AC_{loc}((a,b))
with
f^{[1]}:=pf^{\prime}\in AC_{loc}((a,b))
. The two obvious sesquilinear forms in the study of (
1.1
) are

\int_{a}^{b}r(x)dx\,\overline{f(x)}g(x)\,\text{ and }\,\int_{a}^{b}dx\,\big[p(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)}g^{\prime}(x)+q(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x)\big].

(1.2)

If
r>0
a.e. on
(a,b)
, this SL problem is called
right-definite
since in this case the sesquilinear form

\int_{a}^{b}r(x)dx\,\overline{f(x)}g(x),\quad f,g\in L^{2}((a,b);rdx)

(1.3)

is positive in the sense that it defines the scalar product in the Hilbert space
L^{2}((a,b);rdx)
. The problem is called
left-definite
if the sequilinear form

\int_{a}^{b}dx\,\big[p(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)}g^{\prime}(x)+q(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x)\big]

(1.4)

is positive, that is, it defines a positive scalar product. When a SL problem is both right-definite and left-definite, the sesquilinear forms are connected through
Dirichlet’s formula

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\tau f,g\right)_{L^{2}((a,b);rdx)}&=\int_{a}r(x)dx\,\overline{(\tau f)(x)}g(x)\\
&=\int_{a}^{b}dx\,\big[-(p(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)})^{\prime}+q(x)\overline{f(x)}\,\big]g(x)\\
&=\int_{a}^{b}dx\,\big[p(x)^{-1}\overline{f^{[1]}(x)}g^{[1]}(x)+q(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x)\big]\\
&=\int_{a}^{b}dx\,\big[p(x)\overline{f^{\prime}(x)}g^{\prime}(x)+q(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x)\big]:=(f,g)_{1}.\end{split}

(1.5)

Here
f,g\in AC_{loc}((a,b))
,
pf^{\prime},pg^{\prime}\in AC_{loc}((a,b))
with
f,g
of compact support in
(a,b)
. In the literature, the inner product
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{1}
, extended to elements in appropriate function spaces (e.g., Sobolev-type spaces), is called the
left-definite inner
product. Littlejohn and
Wellman
[
45
]
,
[
46
]
refer to
(\,\cdot\,.\,\cdot\,)_{1}
as the
first
left-definite inner
product. Curiously, before the work of Littlejohn and Wellman, there is no
mention of other left-definite inner products in the literature associated
with the Sturm–Liouville expression
\tau
. R. Vonhoff
[
64
]
was the first to consider the (first) left-definite analysis of a fourth-order (Legendre type) differential expression. We recommend the texts
of Bennewitz, Brown, and Weikard
[
7
, Ch. 5]
and Zettl
[
66
, Chs. 5, 12]
for in-depth
discussions and an extensive literature of left-definite SL theory.

4.
The Hermite Operator, The Harmonic Oscillator, Fractional Sobolev Spaces, and Interpolation Theory

In this section we take an in-depth look at Hermite polynomials, the underlying Hermite operator
A_{H}
in
L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)
, and the unitarily equivalent harmonic oscillator operator
T_{HO}
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Our main technique in establishing the underlying left-definite theory relies on interpolation theory of fractional Sobolev spaces and domains of (strictly) positive fractional powers of
T_{HO}
, certain commutation techniques, and a precise description of
\dom\big(T_{HO}^{s}\big)

(
and hence that of
\dom\big(A_{H}^{s}\big)
)
,
s\in(0,\infty)
.

The Hermite differential expression is of the type

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tau_{H}&=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+2x\frac{d}{dx}+c\\
&=\frac{1}{e^{-x^{2}}}\bigg[-\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^{2}}\frac{d}{dx}+ce^{-x^{2}}\bigg],\quad c\in(0,\infty),\;x\in{\mathbb{R}},\end{split}

(4.1)

which generates the self-adjoint and maximally defined Hermite operator
A_{H}
in
L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)
given by

\displaystyle(A_{H}f)(x)=(\tau_{H}f)(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

(4.2)

\displaystyle f\in\dom(A_{H})=\big\{g\in L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)\,\big|\,g,g^{\prime}\in AC_{loc}({\mathbb{R}});\,(\tau_{H}g)\in L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)\big\},

since
\tau_{H}
is in the limit point case at
\pm\infty
in
L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)
. One verifies that
A_{H}
has a compact resolvent,

(A_{H}-zI)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}\big(L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)\big),\quad z\in\rho(A_{H}),

(4.3)

with

\sigma(A_{H})=\sigma_{d}(A_{H})=\{2m+c\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}},\quad\text{$\sigma(A_{H})$ is simple}.

(4.4)

In fact, because of (
4.3
), the resolvent of
A_{H}
actually lies in the trace ideal

(A_{H}-zI)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{p}\big(L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)\big),\quad z\in\rho(A_{H}),

(4.5)

for each
p\in(1,\infty)
. The normalized eigenfunctions associated with the eigenvalues
2m+c
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, are the Hermite polynomials,

H_{m}(x),\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\;x\in{\mathbb{R}},

(4.6)

given explicitly by

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{m}(x)&=m!\,2^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2^{2j}j!(m-2j)!}x^{m-2j}\\
&=(-1)^{m}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{m}e^{-x^{2}}}{dx^{m}};\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}.\end{split}

(4.7)

(We recall that
\lfloor x\rfloor
equals the greatest integer less than or equal to
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, see also (
1.27
).) Explicitly,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H_{0}(x)=1,\quad H_{1}(x)=2x,\quad H_{2}(x)=4x^{2}-2,\quad H_{3}(x)=8x^{3}-12x,\\
&H_{4}(x)=16x^{4}-48x^{2}+12,\quad H_{5}(x)=32x^{5}-160x^{3}+120x,\;\text{ etc.}\end{split}

(4.8)

In particular,

(H_{m},H_{m^{\prime}})_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx)}=\pi^{1/2}2^{m}m!\,\delta_{m,m^{\prime}},\quad m,m^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(4.9)

One can show the identity (see

\displaystyle(\tau_{H}^{n}f)(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}c_{j}(n,c)e^{x^{2}}\big(e^{-x^{2}}f^{(j)}(x)\big)^{(j)},

(4.10)

m\end{pmatrix}S^{(j)}_{n-m}2^{-m}c^{m},\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}},\end{split}

(4.11)

(4.12)

are the Stirling numbers of the second kind. One has

,

(4.13)

since

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big(A_{H}^{n/2}f,A_{H}^{n/2}g\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}(n,c)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-x^{2}}dx\,\overline{f^{(j)}(x)}g^{(j)}(x),&\\

T_{HO}
in

is of the form

\tau_{HO}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x^{2},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

(4.29)

\pm\infty
with respect to

and hence gives rise to the self-adjoint and maximally defined associated operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(T_{HO}f)(x)=(\tau_{HO}f)(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
&f\in\dom(T_{HO})=\big\{g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\,\big|\,g,g^{\prime}\in AC_{loc}({\mathbb{R}});\,(\tau_{HO}g)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\big\}\end{split}

(4.30)

in

. The unitary maps

\displaystyle U\colon\begin{cases}L^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}};e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}),\\

(4.31)

yield the following unitary equivalence of the shifted harmonic oscillator

and the Hermite operator

in the form

(4.32)

Thus,

\sigma(T_{HO})=\sigma_{d}(T_{HO})=\{2m+1\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}},\quad\text{$\sigma(T_{HO})$ is simple},

(4.33)

and for each

0<{\delta^{\prime\prime}}\leqslant\delta^{\prime}
sufficiently small such that

C_{V,\theta,k^{\prime\prime}}\|f_{k^{\prime\prime},{\delta^{\prime\prime}}}-f_{k^{\prime\prime}}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}<\varepsilon/3
, one then gets

\displaystyle\|f_{k^{\prime\prime},{\delta^{\prime\prime}}}-f\|_{H_{V}^{\theta}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f-f_{k^{\prime\prime},{\delta^{\prime\prime}}}\|_{H^{\theta}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\big\|V^{\theta/2}(f_{k^{\prime\prime}}-f)\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\big\|V^{\theta/2}(f_{k^{\prime\prime},{\delta^{\prime\prime}}}-f_{k^{\prime\prime}})\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon/3+\varepsilon/3+\varepsilon/3=\varepsilon,

(4.48)

finishing the proof.

(4.51)

Corollary 4.4
.

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exist constants
C_{k},C_{k}^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\big\|x^{2k}f^{\prime\prime}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{k}^{\prime}\big\|f^{(2k+2)}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{1/(1+k)}\big\|x^{2k+2}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{k/(1+k)}

(4.52)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{k}\big[\big\|f^{(2k+2)}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\big\|x^{2k+2}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\big],\quad f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}).

(4.53)

Similarly, one gets

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\frac{d^{2k}}{dx^{2k}}(x^{2}f)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\leqslant&C_{k}^{\prime}\big\|f^{(2k+2)}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{1/(1+k)}\big\|x^{2k+2}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{k/(1+k)}\\
\leqslant&C_{k}\big[\big\|f^{(2k+2)}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\big\|x^{2k+2}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\big],\quad f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}).\end{split}

(4.54)

Proof.

Let

,

,

, and

satisfies all the assumptions in Theorem

, from which the inequality in (

4.53

4.54
). By taking Fourier transforms we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\frac{d^{2k}}{dx^{2k}}(x^{2}f)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}=&\big\|\xi^{2k}\widehat{f}^{\prime\prime}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\leqslant C_{k}\big[\big\|\widehat{f}^{(2k+2)}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\big\|\xi^{2k+2}\widehat{f}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\big]\\
=&C_{k}\big[\big\|x^{2k+2}{f}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}+\big\|{f}^{(2k+2)}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\big],\end{split}

(4.55)

where we used inequality (
4.53
) to estimate
\big\|\xi^{2k}\widehat{f}^{\prime\prime}\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
P=i\ d/dx
and
X
denote the momentum operator and operator of multiplication by
x
defined on
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
, respectively. Then, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exist
a_{k},b_{k},s_{k},t_{k}\in(0,\infty)
such that

P^{4k}+X^{4k}\leqslant a_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2k}+b_{k}I\leqslant s_{k}\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)+t_{k}I

(4.56)

in the quadratic form sense.

Proof.

We begin by showing the first inequality in (
4.56
), which we will prove by induction. For the base case
k=1
, consider

\displaystyle\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=P^{4}+X^{2}P^{2}+P^{2}X^{2}+X^{4}=X^{4}+P^{4}+2PX^{2}P+\big[P,\big[P,X^{2}\big]\big]

\displaystyle\geqslant X^{4}+P^{4}-2I,

(4.57)

where we used that
PX^{2}P\geqslant 0
in the sense of quadratic forms and
\big[P,\big[P,X^{m}\big]\big]=-m(m-1)X^{m-2}
, which is a consequence of the commutation relation
[P,X]=iI
. Later, we will also use
\big[X,\big[X,P^{m}]]=-m(m-1)P^{m-2}
. Hence, if
k=1
, then (
4.56
) is satisfied with
a_{1}=1
and
b_{1}=2
.

For the inductive step, assume that (
4.56
) holds up to
k
. Multiplying both sides of (
4.56
) by
P^{2}+X^{2}
from the left and right yields

\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)\leqslant a_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2(k+1)}+b_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}.

(4.58)

First, we focus on the left-hand side of this inequality:

\displaystyle(P^{2}+X^{2})(P^{4k}+X^{4k})(P^{2}+X^{2})

\displaystyle\quad=P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}+X^{2}P^{4k}X^{2}+P^{2}X^{4k}P^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+X^{2}P^{4k+2}+P^{4k+2}X^{2}+P^{2}X^{4k+2}+X^{4k+2}P^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}+X^{2}P^{4k+2}+P^{4k+2}X^{2}+P^{2}X^{4k+2}+X^{4k+2}P^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}+2PX^{4k+2}P+\big[P,\big[P,X^{4k+2}\big]\big]+2XP^{4k+2}X

\displaystyle\qquad+\big[X,\big[X,P^{4k+2}\big]\big]

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}+\big[P,\big[P,X^{4k+2}\big]\big]+\big[X,\big[X,P^{4k+2}\big]\big]

\displaystyle\quad=P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}-(4k+2)(4k+1)\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)

(4.59)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\frac{1}{2}P^{4(k+1)}-(4k+2)(4k+1)P^{4k}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}X^{4(k+1)}-(4k+2)(4k+1)X^{4k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)-c_{k}I,

(4.60)

where
c_{k}>0
is a constant such that

\frac{1}{2}t^{4(k+1)}-(4k+2)(4k+1)t^{4k}\geqslant-\frac{c_{k}}{2},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

(4.61)

which ensures that the second and third term in (
4.59
) are each bounded from below by
-(c_{k}/2)I
, thus resulting in the final estimate (
4.60
). Applying this estimate to (
4.58
) then yields

\frac{1}{2}\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)-c_{k}I\leqslant a_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2k}+b_{k}I.

(4.62)

Next, we focus on the right-hand side of (
4.62
). Here, we will use the fact that for any
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Big(f,\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}f\Big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}&=\|T_{HO}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\leqslant\big\|T_{HO}^{k+1}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\\
&=\Big(f,(P^{2}+X^{2})^{2(k+1)}f\Big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\end{split}

(4.63)

where the inequality
\|T_{HO}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\leqslant\big\|T_{HO}^{k+1}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}
follows from the fact that the spectrum of
T_{HO}
is given by
\{(2n+1)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. Consequently, we may estimate
\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}\leqslant\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2(k+1)}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, (
4.62
) can be estimated by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)-c_{k}I&\leqslant a_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2(k+1)}+b_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}\\
&\leqslant(a_{k}+b_{k})\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2(k+1)},\end{split}

(4.64)

which is equivalent to

\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)\leqslant 2(a_{k}+b_{k})\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2(k+1)}+2c_{k}I,

(4.65)

and thus (
4.56
) holds for
k+1
with
a_{k+1}=2(a_{k}+b_{k})
and
b_{k+1}=2c_{k}
.

Next, we will show the second inequality in (
4.56
):

a_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2k}+b_{k}I\leqslant s_{k}\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)+t_{k}I,

(4.66)

once again by induction.

For what follows, it will be useful to note that for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and any
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})

\displaystyle\big(f,\big(P^{2}X^{4k+2}+X^{4k+2}P^{2}\big)f\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle\quad=2\text{\rm Re}\big(f,P^{2}X^{4k+2}f\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 2\big|\big(f,P^{2}X^{4k+2}f\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\big|

\displaystyle\quad=2\big|\big(X^{2k}P^{2}f,X^{2k+2}f\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\big|

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\big\|X^{2k}P^{2}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}+\big\|X^{2k+2}f\big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\big(f,\big(P^{2}X^{4k}P^{2}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)f\big)_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},

(4.67)

and consequently

P^{2}X^{4k+2}+X^{4k+2}P^{2}\leqslant P^{2}X^{4k}P^{2}+X^{4(k+1)}

(4.68)

in the quadratic form sense. Similarly, one obtains

X^{2}P^{4k+2}+P^{4k+2}X^{2}\leqslant X^{2}P^{4k}X^{2}+P^{4(k+1)}.

(4.69)

For the base case
k=1
, one has
a_{1}=1
and
b_{1}=2
. One then gets

\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}+2I=P^{4}+P^{2}X^{2}+X^{2}P^{2}+X^{4}+2\leqslant 2\big(P^{4}+X^{4}\big)+2I,

(4.70)

where we used (
4.68
) for
k=0
. This shows the base case with
s_{1}=2
and
t_{1}=2
. For the inductive step, suppose (
4.66
) holds up to
k
. Multiplying both sides of (
4.66
) by
\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)
then yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&a_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2(k+1)}+b_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}\\
&\quad\leqslant s_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)+t_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}.\end{split}

(4.71)

First, we focus on the term
\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)
:

\displaystyle\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)\big(P^{4k}+X^{4k}\big)\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\quad=P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}+X^{2}P^{4k}X^{2}+P^{2}X^{4k}P^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+X^{2}P^{4k+2}+P^{4k+2}X^{2}+P^{2}X^{4k+2}+X^{4k+2}P^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 2P^{4(k+1)}+2X^{4(k+1)}+2X^{2}P^{4k}X^{2}+2P^{2}X^{4k}P^{2},

(4.72)

where the last inequality follows from (

4.69

4.4
, that

\displaystyle X^{2}P^{4k}X^{2}\leqslant 2C_{k}\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)\,\text{ and }\,P^{2}X^{4k}P^{2}\leqslant 2C_{k}\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big),

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant s_{k}(2+4C_{k})\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)+t_{k}\big(P^{2}+X^{2}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant s_{k}(2+4C_{k})\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)+2t_{k}\big(P^{4}+X^{4}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant[s_{k}(2+4C_{k}+2t_{k}]\big(P^{4(k+1)}+X^{4(k+1)}\big)+2t_{k}I,

(4.77)

Theorem 4.11

The domains of the fractional powers

,

Appendix B
More on Fractional Sobolev Spaces on
(0,2\pi)

Although not needed in the bulk of this paper, we include the following results as they seem interesting in their own right.

The motivation for writing this appendix, in part, stems from the classical result that the form domain,
\dom\big(T_{F}^{1/2}\big)
, of the Friedrichs extension,
T_{F}
, of a lower semibounded symmetric operator
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
in the separable, complex Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, is a proper subset of the form domain of any other nonnegative self-adjoint extension of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
(cf. e.g.,
[
3
]
). In the following we assume that
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
has a strictly positive lower bound,
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}\geqslant\varepsilon I_{{\mathcal{H}}}
for some
\varepsilon>0
and non-zero, finite deficiency indices. Viewing the inclusion relation “
\subseteq
” as a partial order on the form domains of all nonnegative self-adjoint extensions of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
, this means that
\dom\big(T_{F}^{1/2}\big)
is its unique minimal element.

Since for any two nonnegative self-adjoint extensions
T_{1},T_{2}
of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
, one has (this is a corollary of
[
3
, Proposition 2.4, Theorem 3.1]
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\dom\big(T_{1}^{1/2}\big)\subseteq\dom\big(T_{2}^{1/2}\big)\,\text{ if and only if }\\
&\quad\dom(T_{2})\cap\dom(T_{F})\subseteq\dom(T_{1})\cap\dom(T_{F}),\end{split}

(B.1)

the maximal elements with respect to this partial order correspond to the form domains of those extensions
T_{2}
of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
that are disjoint (equivalently, relatively prime) to
T_{F}
, that is
6
6

6

Since the deficiency indices of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
are assumed to be finite, disjointness also implies transversality, see, e.g.,
[
6
, Lemma 1.7.7]
, they satisfy
\dom(T_{2})\cap\dom(T_{F})=\dom(\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T})
.
The Krein–von Neumann extension of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
is one such example, but there are of course infinitely many more. Hence, assuming that
T_{2}
satisfies
\dom(T_{2})\cap\dom(T_{F})=\dom(\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T})
, one concludes that for any nonnegative self-adjoint extension
T_{1}
of
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{T}
, with
T_{1}\neq T_{F}
, one has

\dom\big(T_{F}^{1/2}\big)\subsetneq\dom\big(T_{1}^{1/2}\big)\subseteq\dom\big(T_{2}^{1/2}\big).

(B.2)

Next, we choose
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{A}
to be the usual minimal Laplacian on the interval
(0,2\pi)
:

(A_{min}f)(x)=-f^{\prime\prime}(x),\quad x\in(0,2\pi),\quad f\in\dom(A_{min})=H^{2}_{0}((0,2\pi)),

(B.3)

whose Friedrichs extension
A_{F}
is the Dirichlet Laplacian

\displaystyle(A_{F}f)(x)=-f^{\prime\prime}(x),\quad x\in(0,2\pi),

\displaystyle f\in\dom(A_{F})=\big\{g\in H^{2}((0,2\pi))\,\big|\,g(0)=g(2\pi)=0\big\}

(B.4)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=H^{2}((0,2\pi))\cap H^{1}_{0}((0,2\pi)),

whose form domain is given by

\dom\big(A_{F}^{1/2}\big)=H^{1}_{0}((0,2\pi)).

(B.5)

For the disjoint extension, we choose the Neumann Laplacian
A_{N}
, given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(A_{N}f)(x)=-f^{\prime\prime}(x),\quad x\in(0,2\pi),\\
&f\in\dom(A_{N})=\big\{g\in H^{2}((0,2\pi))\,\big|\,g^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime}(2\pi)=0\big\},\end{split}

(B.6)

with form domain

\dom\big(A_{N}^{1/2}\big)=H^{1}((0,2\pi)).

(B.7)

For the form domains, one gets the inclusions

\dom\big(A_{F}^{1/2}\big)\subsetneqq\dom\big(A_{\phi}^{1/2}\big)\subsetneqq\dom\big(A_{N}^{1/2}\big).

(B.8)

We now show that for this particular example, the inclusions

\dom\big(A_{F}^{s/2}\big)\subsetneqq\dom\big(A_{\phi}^{s/2}\big)\subsetneqq\dom\big(A_{N}^{s/2}\big),\quad s\in[1/2,1),

(B.9)

hold. However, if
s\in(0,1/2)
, these inclusions collapse and one obtains

\dom\big(A_{F}^{s/2}\big)=\dom\big(A_{\phi}^{s/2}\big)=\dom\big(A_{N}^{s/2}\big)=H^{s}((0,2\pi)),\quad s\in(0,1/2),

(B.10)

instead. To this end, we need the following result.
