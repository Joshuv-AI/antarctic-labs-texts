Likelihood for a Network of Gravitational-Wave Detectors with Correlated Noise
Francesco Cireddu, Milan Wils, Isaac C. F. Wong, Peter T. H. Pang, Tjonnie G. F. Li, Walter Del Pozzo

Abstract
The Einstein Telescope faces a critical data analysis challenge with correlated noise, often overlooked in current parameter estimation analyses. We address this issue by presenting the statistical formulation of the likelihood that includes correlated noise for the Einstein Telescope or any detector network. By considering varying degrees of correlation, we probe the impact of noise correlations on the parameter estimation analysis of a GW150914-like event. We show that neglecting these correlations may significantly reduce the accuracy of the chirp mass reconstruction. This emphasizes how critical a proper treatment of correlated noise is, as presented in this work, to unlocking the wealth of results promised by the Einstein Telescope.

II.1
Likelihood for correlated noise

Within a network of
GW
detectors, we indicate with
\bm{n}_{\ell}
the noise time series for the
\ell\text{-th}
interferometer, assuming a sampling frequency of
1/\Delta t
.
In the usual assumption of Gaussian, zero-mean and wide-sense stationary noise, the spatial and temporal correlations between the noise in the
\ell\text{-th}
and the
m\text{-th}
interferometers are expressed by the cross-covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{\ell m}\coloneq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{\ell}\bm{n}_{m}^{T}\right]
.
In particular,
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{\ell m}
assumes a Toeplitz form for
\ell\neq m
and a symmetric Toeplitz form for
\ell=m
.

Conventionally, the noise time series of multiple detectors is expressed as a matrix
\bm{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times N}
, where each row
\ell=1,2,3
corresponds to the time series of the
\ell\text{-th}
interferometer, and
N
denotes the number of data points in each time series.
To facilitate the characterization of the spatial and temporal correlation, we introduce the vectorized noise time series defined as follows:

\bm{n}\coloneq\text{vec}(\bm{N}^{T})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{n}_{1}\\
\bm{n}_{2}\\
\bm{n}_{3}\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
.
The spatial and temporal correlations of the noise process
\bm{n}
are characterized by the
3N\times 3N
network covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}
, given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}\bm{n}^{T}\right]

(2a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[1.1]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{1}\bm{n}_{1}^{T}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{1}\bm{n}_{2}^{T}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{1}\bm{n}_{3}^{T}\right]\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{2}\bm{n}_{1}^{T}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{2}\bm{n}_{2}^{T}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{2}\bm{n}_{3}^{T}\right]\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{3}\bm{n}_{1}^{T}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{3}\bm{n}_{2}^{T}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}_{3}\bm{n}_{3}^{T}\right]\end{bmatrix}

(2b)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[1.3]\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{11}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{12}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{13}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{21}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{22}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{23}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{31}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{32}&\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{33}\end{bmatrix}.

(2c)

In particular, the diagonal blocks characterize the temporal correlation of the noise process in each detector, and the off-diagonal blocks characterize the spatial correlation of the noise processes between different detectors.
For the off-diagonal blocks
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{\ell m}=(\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{m\ell})^{T}
.

Consider the hypothesis that the data
\bm{d}
recorded by the
ET
contain a
GW
signal that depends on a set of source parameters
\bm{\theta}
.
We model the output as

\bm{d}=\bm{s}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{n}

(3)

where
\bm{s}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{n}
are the
GW
signal and the noise component, respectively.
For such a hypothesis, the likelihood of observing the data
\bm{d}
follows the distribution of the noise, that is

p(\bm{d}|\bm{\theta})=\dfrac{1}{|2\pi\bm{\Sigma}_{n}|^{1/2}}\exp{\bigg[\hskip-3.0pt-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{d}}-\bm{s})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}(\bm{d}-\bm{s})\bigg],

(4)

which corresponds to the maximum entropy distribution of the zero-mean noise processes constrained by the network covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}

.

Although the time-domain formulation is essential for understanding the data as it is recorded, given the reduced complexity it is useful to present the frequency-domain version of Eq. (
4
).
Let
\bm{S}_{n}
be the spectral matrix defined as

\bm{S}_{n}\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}[1.3]\bm{S}_{n}^{11}&\bm{S}_{n}^{12}&\bm{S}_{n}^{13}\\
\bm{S}_{n}^{21}&\bm{S}_{n}^{22}&\bm{S}_{n}^{23}\\
\bm{S}_{n}^{31}&\bm{S}_{n}^{32}&\bm{S}_{n}^{33}\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\ell m}(f_{k})=2\Delta f\hskip 3.0pt\delta_{jk}\hskip 3.0pt\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{n}_{\ell}(f_{j})\tilde{n}_{m}^{*}(f_{k})\right]

(6a)

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{n}^{\ell m}=\text{diag}\left[S_{n}^{\ell m}(f_{k_{\text{low}}}),\dots,S_{n}^{\ell m}(f_{k_{\text{high}}})\right],

(6b)

with the tilde indicating the Fourier transform and
\Delta f=1/N\Delta t

In particular,

is the

component of the one-sided

(
PSD
) for the
\ell\text{-th}
interferometer, and

It can be shown that each (cross-)covariance Toeplitz matrix

In other words, each block of the covariance matrix

Since the entries of

One can rewrite Eq. (

represent the time series of the equivalent detectors in the two-dimensional signal space, and
\bm{n}^{p}
represents the time series of the null stream in the one-dimensional null space, which contains no
GW
signals
Goncharov_2022
.
This principal coordinate system is equivalent to the
A,E
and
T
channels commonly used in the
LISA
community
Adams_2010
;
Littenberg_2020
.

Assume now that for a certain frequency
f_{k}
, the spectral matrix has the form

\bm{S}_{n}(f_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}1&\alpha_{k}&\alpha_{k}\\
\alpha_{k}&1&\alpha_{k}\\
\alpha_{k}&\alpha_{k}&1\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

Transforming this matrix into the principal coordinate system yields:

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{p}_{n}(f_{k})

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{T}\bm{S}_{n}(f_{k})\bm{U}

(23a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1-\alpha_{k}&0&0\\
0&1-\alpha_{k}&0\\
0&0&1+2\alpha_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(23b)

As we can see, in the assumption of Gaussian noise, the spread of the likelihood
p(\bm{d}^{p}(f_{k})|\bm{\theta})
in the two-dimensional signal space depends on the sign of the correlation coefficient
\alpha_{k}
, and it is proportional to
\sqrt{|\text{diag}(1-\alpha_{k},1-\alpha_{k})|}
.
Specifically, compared to the uncorrelated case (
\alpha_{k}=0
), the spread of the likelihood increases when
\alpha_{k}<0
and decreases when
\alpha_{k}>0
.

This example provides an explanation for the behaviour of
\rho^{\text{corr}}
in our analysis, which decreases for negative correlations and increases for positive correlations, along with its subsequent impact on the spread of the chirp mass posterior distribution.

It is however important to underline that from Eq. (
23
) one cannot immediately conclude that the
PE
improves for positive correlations and worsens for negative, as the whole analysis strongly relies on an accurate estimation of the
PSD
s and
CSD
s, which were assumed to be known in our study.
In this sense, the impact of the sign of
\alpha
on the null space should also be considered, as it could indicate an enhanced (or diminished) capacity to estimate the noise through the null stream
Goncharov_2022
;
janssens2023formalism
.
Moreover, from Fischer’s inequality
hornMatrixAnalysis1987
, the overall spread of the likelihood, which is proportional to
\sqrt{|\bm{S}_{n}|}
, is reduced with respect to the zero-correlation case as long as there are any nonzero components of correlation, regardless of the specific structure of the spectral matrix or the sign of
\alpha
.
We emphasize that a comprehensive investigation is necessary to fully assess the impact of correlated noise on
PE
analyses for the
ET
.

IV
Conclusions

In the era of the
ET
, handling correlated noise is key to unlocking its full scientific potential.
We have presented the time and frequency-domain likelihoods for a network of
GW
interferometers when correlated noise is present.
Analysing a GW150914-like event, we have shown that the accuracy of the
PE
is significantly reduced when ignoring such correlations.
Specifically, this results in a broader (narrower) posterior reconstruction for the chirp mass in the case of positive (negative) correlations.

This work shows the crucial role of accounting for correlations in
PE
analysis, enabling precise studies such as tests of General Relativity, neutron star equations of state reconstruction, and cosmological parameter inference.
Neglecting correlations may also lead to imprecise identification of resolvable signals, critical for searches of stochastic
GW
s background, core-collapse supernovae, and rotating neutron stars.
In that sense, the issue might also be relevant to
LISA
, where
PE
analysis is conducted on an unknown number of signals from different sources simultaneously, in what is known as the “global fit”
Littenberg_2020
;
Littenberg_2023
.

Our analysis envisages the necessity for a thorough investigation of the impact of correlated noise on
PE
, incorporating a refined model for noise correlations and exploring different
SNR
values.
Special attention must also be given to different
GW
sources, particularly those more sensitive to the low frequency region where correlations are expected
Janssens_magnetic_noise
;
Ball_lightning_strokes
;
Janssens_newtonian_seismic
;
Janssens_NN_2024
, such as mergers of intermediate-mass black holes (whose chirp frequency is
\lesssim 10
Hz) and binary neutron star coalescences (which will remain in the
ET
low frequency band for hours)
ET_science_case
;
ET_design_report
.

Future work will focus on assessing the full impact of the correlated versus the uncorrelated likelihood on the
ET
science cases. We advocate abandoning the assumption of uncorrelated detectors which does not fully exploit the information content in the data (such as their correlations) by using the correlated likelihood in Eq. (
7
).

Appendix A
Standard and compact representations for the multivariate Whittle likelihood

We discuss here the equivalence between the standard Whittle likelihood representation
whittle1953_multivariate
and the compact representation considered in this work.

In a single detector analysis, the frequency-domain approximation of the likelihood (i.e., the univariate Whittle likelihood
whittle1953_estimation
) is generally used to reduce the complexity, specifically to avoid the inversion of the covariance matrix.
Since the noise is assumed to be stationary, the
PSD
matrix is diagonal, making the inversion straightforward.
The extension from the univariate to the multivariate case takes essentially the same form
Calder1997
.

Following the compact representation considered in this work, the spectral matrix
\bm{S}_{n}
takes the subsequent form for a three detectors analysis:

\bm{S}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}[1.3]\bm{S}_{n}^{11}&\bm{S}_{n}^{12}&\bm{S}_{n}^{13}\\
\bm{S}_{n}^{21}&\bm{S}_{n}^{22}&\bm{S}_{n}^{23}\\
\bm{S}_{n}^{31}&\bm{S}_{n}^{32}&\bm{S}_{n}^{33}\end{bmatrix},

(24)

where each block
\bm{S}^{\ell m}_{n}
is a diagonal matrix.
Compared to the standard Whittle likelihood, this block representation has the advantage of clearly illustrating the nature of the noise in each detector and detector pair, making the expression for the correlated likelihood compact and straightforward.
While the inversion of a
3N\times 3N
matrix nominally involves
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
computations
farebrother2018linear
, the inverse of
\bm{S}_{n}
can be computed analytically with the following form:

\bm{S}_{n}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5](\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}&-(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}\bm{\rho}_{1}&-(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}\bm{\rho}_{2}\\
-\bm{\rho}_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}&(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}+\bm{\rho}_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}\bm{\rho}_{1}&-(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\rho}_{1}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}\bm{\rho}_{2}\\
-\bm{\rho}_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}&-\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}+\bm{\rho}_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}\bm{\rho}_{1}&(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}+\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\rho}_{2}^{\dagger}(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}\bm{\rho}_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(25)

where

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}_{n}^{22}-\bm{S}_{n}^{23}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{32},

(26)

\displaystyle(\bm{S}_{n}^{-1})^{11}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{S}_{n}^{11}-\bm{S}_{n}^{12}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{21}+2\Re\left[\bm{S}_{n}^{12}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{23}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{31}\right]-\bm{S}_{n}^{13}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{31}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-\bm{S}_{n}^{13}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{32}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{23}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{31}\right)^{-1},

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}_{n}^{12}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}-\bm{S}_{n}^{13}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{32}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1},

(28)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\bm{S}_{n}^{12}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{23}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}+\bm{S}_{n}^{13}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}+\bm{S}_{n}^{13}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{32}(\bm{S}_{n}^{22/33})^{-1}\bm{S}_{n}^{23}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1},

(29)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}\

\displaystyle=\bm{S}_{n}^{23}(\bm{S}_{n}^{33})^{-1}.

(30)

This reduces the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(N)
.

Although this compact representation provides a clearer view of the noise interactions between detectors, the standard multivariate Whittle likelihood may be more suitable for practical implementation. In such representation, the time-domain likelihood is asymptotically decomposed into
N
independent likelihoods, each dependent on a
3\times 3
spectral matrix evaluated at the
N
frequency components.
As a result, the problem of inverting the covariance matrix reduces to the inversion of the
N

3\times 3
spectral matrices, without the necessity to use the analytical expression in Eq. (
25
).

Despite their differences, the two representations are connected through a simple permutation.
In particular, let
\bm{P}
be the permutation matrix defined as:

\bm{P}[i,j]=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if } $i=k+dN$ \text{ and } $j=d+3k$\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(31)

with
k=k_{\text{low}},\dots,k_{\text{high}}
index over the frequency components and
d=1,2,3
index over the number of detectors.
We have that
\bm{P}
separates the frequency components of
\bm{S}_{n}
as

\bm{P}^{T}\bm{S}_{n}\bm{P}=\bm{S}^{\prime}_{n}=\text{diag}\left[\bm{S}_{n}(f_{k_{\text{low}}}),\dots,\bm{S}_{n}(f_{k_{\text{high}}})\right]

(32)

where each
\bm{S}_{n}(f_{k})
is the block matrix for the
k\text{-th}
frequency component used in the standard Whittle approximation:

\bm{S}_{n}(f_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}[1.3]S_{n}^{11}(f_{k})&S_{n}^{12}(f_{k})&S_{n}^{13}(f_{k})\\
S_{n}^{21}(f_{k})&S_{n}^{22}(f_{k})&S_{n}^{23}(f_{k})\\
S_{n}^{31}(f_{k})&S_{n}^{32}(f_{k})&S_{n}^{33}(f_{k})\end{bmatrix}

(33)

As shown, since
\bm{S}^{\prime}_{n}
is block diagonal, its inverse is computed by inverting its
3\times 3
blocks, which has complexity
\mathcal{O}(N)

farebrother2018linear
.
As a final note, applying the inverse permutation on
{(\bm{S}^{\prime}_{n})}^{-1}
yields from Eq. (
32
):

\bm{P}{(\bm{S}^{\prime}_{n})}^{-1}\bm{P}^{T}={(\bm{S}_{n})}^{-1}

(34)

This demonstrates that the inverse of the spectral matrix
\bm{S}_{n}
retains the same
3\times 3
block matrix structure, where each block is a
N\times N
diagonal matrix.

Appendix B
Lower limit for the correlation coefficient

We provide here a proof for the lower limit of
-0.5
for the correlation coefficient
\alpha
used in our simulations.

In the assumption that the
PSD
and the
CSD
are the same for all the three
ET
detectors and detector pairs, we have for a certain frequency
f_{k}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{n}_{\ell}(f_{k})\tilde{n}_{\ell}^{*}(f_{k})\right]=\dfrac{1}{2\Delta f}S_{n}^{\ell\ell}(f_{k})=\dfrac{1}{2\Delta f}S_{n}(f_{k})

(35a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{n}_{\ell}(f_{k})\tilde{n}_{m}^{*}(f_{k})\right]=\dfrac{1}{2\Delta f}S_{n}^{\ell m}(f_{k})=\dfrac{\alpha_{k}}{2\Delta f}S_{n}(f_{k})

(35b)

with
\ell\neq m
.
By cross-correlating the noise of one detector with the sum of the other two, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{n}_{\ell}(f_{k})\Big(\tilde{n}_{m}^{*}(f_{k})+\tilde{n}_{r}^{*}(f_{k})\Big)\right]\hskip 3.0pt=\dfrac{\alpha_{k}}{\Delta f}S_{n}(f_{k}),

(36)

and from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
mukhopadhyay2020probability
, we can write

\displaystyle\Big|\hskip 3.0pt\mathbb{E}\Big[\tilde{n}_{\ell}(f_{k})\Big(\tilde{n}_{m}^{*}(f_{k})+\tilde{n}_{r}^{*}(f_{k})\Big)\Big]\hskip 3.0pt\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{n}_{\ell}(f_{k})\tilde{n}_{\ell}^{*}(f_{k})\right]\hskip 3.0pt\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\tilde{n}_{m}(f_{k})+\tilde{n}_{r}(f_{k})\Big)\Big(\tilde{n}_{m}^{*}(f_{k})+\tilde{n}_{r}^{*}(f_{k})\Big)\Big]}

(37a)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta f}S_{n}(f_{k})\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\alpha_{k}}{2}}

(37b)

We have therefore, comparing Eqs. (
36
) - (
37b
),

\left|\alpha_{k}\right|\leq\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\alpha_{k}}{2}}

(38)

from which
-0.5\leq\alpha_{k}\leq 1
.
