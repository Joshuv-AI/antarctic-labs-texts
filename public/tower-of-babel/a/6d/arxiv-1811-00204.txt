Étale and crystalline companions, I
Kiran S. Kedlaya

Abstract




Abstract.
Let
X
be a smooth scheme over a finite field of characteristic
p
.
Consider the coefficient objects of locally constant rank on
X
in
\ell
-adic Weil cohomology: these are lisse Weil sheaves in étale cohomology when
\ell\neq p
, and overconvergent
F
-isocrystals in rigid cohomology when
\ell=p
. Using the Langlands correspondence for global function fields in both the étale and crystalline settings
(work of Lafforgue and Abe, respectively), one sees that on a curve, any coefficient object in one category has “companions” in the other categories with matching characteristic polynomials of Frobenius at closed points.
A similar statement is expected for general
X
; building on work of Deligne, Drinfeld showed that any étale coefficient object has étale companions. We adapt Drinfeld’s method to show that any crystalline coefficient object has étale companions; this has been shown independently by Abe–Esnault.
We also prove some auxiliary results relevant for the construction of crystalline companions of étale coefficient objects; this subject will be pursued in a subsequent paper.

Keywords.
crystalline cohomology, overconvergent
F
-isocrystals, étale cohomology, lisse sheaves, companions, compatible systems

1. Introduction

1.1. Coefficients, companions, and conjectures

Throughout this introduction (but not beyond; see §
2.1
), let
k
be a finite field of characteristic
p

and let
X
be a smooth scheme over
k
. When studying the cohomology of motives over
X
, one typically fixes a prime
\ell\neq p

and considers étale cohomology with
\ell
-adic coefficients; in this setting,
the natural coefficient objects of locally constant rank are the
lisse Weil
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaves
. However, étale cohomology with
p
-adic coefficients behaves poorly in characteristic
p
; for
\ell=p
, the correct choice for a Weil cohomology with
\ell
-adic coefficients is Berthelot’s
rigid cohomology
, wherein
the natural coefficient objects of locally constant rank are the
overconvergent
F
-isocrystals
.
For the purposes of this introduction, the exact nature of these objects is not material; we suggest
our recent survey
[
Ked22a
]
as a starting point for this topic.

For
\mathcal{E}
a coefficient object on
X
and

a closed point of
X
, let
\mathcal{E}_{x}
denote the fiber of
\mathcal{E}
at
x

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-vector space carrying an invertible linear action by (geometric) Frobenius, whose reverse characteristic polynomial we denote by
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)

For the remainder of this introduction, let
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
be two primes (which may or may not equal each other and/or

), and fix an isomorphism of the algebraic closures of
\mathbb{Q}
within
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
and
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell^{\prime}}
(which may be nontrivial in case
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
).
We say that an
\ell
-adic coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
and an
\ell^{\prime}
-adic coefficient object
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
are

if for all
x
,
the characteristic polynomials of Frobenius on
\mathcal{E}_{x}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{x}
coincide;
given this relation, each object determines the other up to semisimplification (see Theorem
4.17
).

In his work on the Weil conjectures, Deligne introduced a far-reaching set of conjectures

[

, Conjecture 1.2.10]
motivated by the idea that coefficient objects should only exist for “geometric reasons” (more on which below); we state here a lightly modified version of this conjecture.
(Compare
[

(i)

is

(

is

, the roots of

have trivial

-adic valuation at all finite places of

To summarize, when

1.2. Curves and the Langlands correspondence

A key step in the proof of Conjecture
1.1

is the construction of the
\ell
-adic Langlands correspondence for the group
\GL_{n}
over a global function field of positive characteristic. This was already anticipated by Deligne in
[
Del81
]
; at the time, the Langlands correspondence was available for
n=2
,
\ell\neq p
by the work of Drinfeld
[
Dri78
]
.
Deligne observed that Drinfeld’s construction not only established the requisite bijection between
\ell
-adic coefficient objects on a curve and automorphic representations on the associated function field,
but also realized the latter as the realizations of certain motives appearing in moduli spaces of shtukas; this then provides a geometric origin for these coefficient objects.

Following Drinfeld’s approach,
L. Lafforgue
[
Laf02
]
established the Langlands correspondence for
\GL_{n}

for arbitrary
n
for
\ell\neq p
. The case
\ell=p
had to be excluded at the time due to the limited foundational development of
p
-adic cohomology at the time; this issue has subsequently been remedied (see
[
Ked22a
]
for a summary), and this enabled Abe
[
Abe18a
]
to extend Lafforgue’s work to the case
\ell=p
.
This immediately implies all of Conjecture
1.1
in the case where
\dim(X)=1

[
Laf02
, Thèoréme VII.6]
,
[
Abe18a
, §4.4]
.

To sum up, we have in hand the following statement, which we will use as a black box in what follows;
see §
3.3
for the proof.

Theorem 1.2
.

Conjecture
1.1
holds in all cases where
\dim(X)=1
.

1.3. Avoiding residual representations

In order to make further progress without losing the case
\ell=p
, one must replace several key arguments in étale cohomology that make direct use of the representation-theoretic interpretation of étale coefficient objects.
These cannot be used for
\ell=p
because only a rather small subcategory of coefficient objects can be described in terms of representations of the étale fundamental group (namely the
unit-root
objects; see §
2.2
). Such arguments can sometimes be circumvented using cohomological considerations, as in the following key examples.

\bullet

For
\ell\neq p
, the uniqueness of
\ell
-adic companions up to semisimplification is usually obtained by applying
the Chebotarëv density theorem to mod-
\ell^{n}
representations
(as in the proof of
[
Laf02
, Proposition VI.11]
).
For
\ell=p
, we use instead an argument of Tsuzuki based on Deligne’s theory of weights (see Theorem
4.17
). This result is already used crucially
in the work of Abe
[
Abe18a
, Proposition A.4.1]
,
and is akin to the argument of Deligne described below to control coefficient fields
(see especially
[
Del12
, Proposition 2.5]
).

\bullet

The extension of Conjecture
1.1
from curves to higher-dimensional varieties makes frequent use of a fact which we call the
Lefschetz slicing principle
: for any closed point
x\in X
and any irreducible coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
, after possibly making a finite base extension of
k
(depending on
x
and
\mathcal{E}
), one can find a curve
C
in
X
containing
x
such that the restriction of
\mathcal{E}
to
C
is again irreducible. For
\ell\neq p
, this is shown by an analysis of the images of representations, as in
[
Del12
, §1.7]
(correcting the proof of
[
Laf02
, Proposition VII.7]
; see also
[
EK12
, Appendix B]
). Using cohomological arguments, we are able to make a fairly similar argument (Lemma
4.14
); a related but distinct approach has been taken by Abe–Esnault (see below).

We mention briefly one further example for which cohomological considerations do not suffice.
For
\ell\neq p
, one can eliminate all wild monodromy at the boundary using a single finite étale cover
(by trivializing a residual representation); this plays an important role in the methods of Deligne and Drinfeld to attack aspects of Conjecture
1.1
(see below). The analogous statement for
\ell=p
is the

semistable reduction theorem for overconvergent
F
-isocrystals
(see Proposition
2.37
): for every
p
-adic coefficient object, one can pull back by a suitable alteration (in the sense of de Jong

[
deJ
]
) and then make a logarithmic extension to some good compactification.
The proof of this cannot be considered a “cohomological argument” because in fact the foundations of rigid cohomology are deeply rooted in this fact; see
[
Ked22a
, §11]
or the introduction to
[
Abe18a
]
.

.

Let

Definition 2.8

For

2.2. Slopes of isocrystals

Definition 2.9

For
L

Definition 2.11
.

For
\mathcal{E}\in\FIsoc(X)
, we define the
Newton polygon

N_{x}(\mathcal{E})
for

(not necessarily a closed point) using the Dieudonné–Manin classification theorem, as in

\mathcal{E}\in\FIsoc(X)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}

(0,0)
and right endpoint at
(\rank_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}(\mathcal{E}),*)

\mathbb{Z}\times[L:\mathbb{Q}_{p}]^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
where

\mathbb{Q}_{p}

\mathcal{E}\in\FIsoc(X)\otimes L

We say
\mathcal{E}
is

Lemma 2.12
.

For
\mathcal{E}\in\FIsoc(X)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
and

x\in X^{\circ}
, write
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}T^{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[T]
with
a_{0}=1
.
Then
N_{x}(\mathcal{E})
equals the boundary of the
lower convex hull of the set of points

\left\{\left(i,\frac{1}{[\kappa(x)\colon\!k]}v_{p}(a_{i})\right)\mid i=0,\dots,d\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In particular, if
\mathcal{E}\in\FIsoc(X)^{\dagger}\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, then
N_{x}(\mathcal{E})=N_{x}(\mathcal{E},\lambda)
where
\lambda
is the place defined by the embedding
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
.

Proof.

We may assume at once that
X=\{x\}
.
Using the Dieudonné–Manin decomposition, we may further reduce to the case where
\mathcal{E}
is unit-root;
we may then also assume that
L=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
In this case, an object of
\FIsoc(X)

is a finite-dimensional
K
-vector space

equipped with an isomorphism

,
and the unit-root condition corresponds to the existence of a

-lattice

such that

induces an isomorphism

∎

2.3. Monodromy groups

We next introduce the formalism of monodromy groups,
following
[
Ked22a
, §9]
and
[
D’Ad20
, §3.2]
in the crystalline case. (The original reference in the crystalline case is
[
Cre92
, §5]
, but it has somewhat restrictive hypotheses which are not suitable for our present discussion; see

[
Ked22a
, Remark 9.9]
.)

Hypothesis 2.19
.

Throughout §
2.3
,
assume that
X
is connected, and fix a closed point
x\in X^{\circ}
and a geometric point
\overline{x}=\Spec(\overline{k})
of
X
lying over
x
.
In the crystalline case, also choose an embedding
W(\overline{k})\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
.

Definition 2.20
.

On any category of coefficient objects, let
\omega_{x}
be the neutral (over the full coefficient field) fiber functor defined by pullback from
X
to
x
.
Note that in this construction, we implicitly discard the Frobenius structure: coefficient objects on
x
correspond to vector spaces equipped with a fixed automorphism (see Definition
2.6
),
and
\omega_{x}
does not retain the data of the automorphism. (In the crystalline case,
this definition depends on the choice of the embedding
W(\overline{k})\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
;
see
[
Ked22a
, Definition 9.5]
.)

For
\mathcal{E}
a coefficient object on
X
, let
G(\mathcal{E})
denote the automorphism group of
\omega_{x}
on the Tannakian category generated by
\mathcal{E}
.
The group
G(\mathcal{E})
is the
arithmetic monodromy group
associated to
\mathcal{E}
.
This group turns out not to be especially useful for our purposes; compare with Definition
2.22
.

In the étale case, the construction of Definition
2.20
has a rather concrete interpretation which we recall here.

Definition 2.21
.

Suppose that
X
is geometrically irreducible (by enlarging
k
if necessary), and consider the exact sequence

1\to\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})\to\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X,\overline{x})\to G_{k}\to 1.

The group
G_{k}
is isomorphic to
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
; let us normalize this isomorphism so that geometric Frobenius corresponds to 1. We may then pull back from
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
to
\mathbb{Z}
to obtain an exact sequence

1\to\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})\to W_{X}\to\mathbb{Z}\to 1;

the group
W_{X}
is the
Weil group
associated to
X
, and for any algebraic extension
L
of
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
,
the category
\Weil(X)\otimes L
may be identified
with (or even defined as) the category of continuous representations of
W_{X}
on finite-dimensional
L
-vector spaces.
(To use this as the definition when
L
is not finite over
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
, one must check that the definition commutes with colimits; for this, see
[
KS99
, Remark 9.0.7]
.)
For any object
\mathcal{E}
in this category,
G(\mathcal{E})
may then be naturally identified with the Zariski closure of the image of
W_{X}
in the associated representation.

Definition 2.22
.

Retain notation as in Definition
2.20
. In the étale case, we define the
geometric monodromy group

\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
to be the Zariski closure of the image of
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})
in the associated representation. Equivalently, define the category of
geometric coefficient objects
by forming the category of continuous representations of
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})
on finite dimensional
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-vector spaces, then extracting the Tannakian subcategory generated by coefficient objects.
We may then equivalently define
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
as the automorphism group of
\omega_{x}
on the Tannakian subcategory of geometric coefficient objects generated by
\mathcal{E}
.

In the crystalline case, we define the category of
geometric coefficient objects
by forming the category of
overconvergent isocrystals (without Frobenius structure) with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
(by a suitable 2-colimit over finite extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, as described in
[
Ked22a
, Definition 9.2]
in the presence of Frobenius structures) and then extracting the Tannakian subcategory generated by coefficient objects. We then define
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
again as the automorphism group of
\omega_{x}
on the Tannakian subcategory of geometric coefficient objects generated by
\mathcal{E}
. We may then turn around and define the

Weil group
of
\mathcal{E}
as the semidirect product of
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
by
\mathbb{Z}
via the action of Frobenius.

Remark 2.23
.

The definition of monodromy groups given here is essentially
[
D’Ad20
, Definition 3.2.4]
;
in the crystalline case, see also
[
Ked22a
, Definition 9.8]
and subsequent remarks.
In particular, as pointed out in
[
D’Ad20
, Remark 3.2.5]
, the definition of
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
agrees with Crew’s definition of the differential Galois group
[
Cre92
, §2]
when
X
is a curve and
x\in X(k)
.

Remark 2.24
.

For any open dense subset
U
of
X
containing
x
, the inclusion
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E}|_{U})\to\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
is an isomorphism. This is straightforward in the étale case; in the crystalline case, see
[
Ked22a
, Remark 9.10]
. (See also Lemma
4.38
below.)

Remark 2.25
.

By definition, any coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
belongs to
\Weil(X)\otimes L
or
\FIsoc^{\dagger}(X)\otimes L
for some finite extension
L
of the base coefficient field. Choose such an
L
, then suppose that
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
is finite.
In the étale case, it is obvious that
\mathcal{E}
gives rise to a discrete
L
-representation of
\pi_{1}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})
. It is a key observation of Crew that this also holds in the crystalline case;
namely, the underlying isocrystal of
\mathcal{E}
admits a canonical unit-root Frobenius structure (see
[
Ked22a
, Corollary 9.17]
) and then Proposition
2.13
applies
(see
[
Ked22a
, Lemma 9.18]
).

Remark 2.26
.

As a special case of Remark
2.25
,
note that a coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
is constant if and only if
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
is trivial. In particular, this condition passes to subobjects.

Remark 2.27
.

As the terminology suggests, the definition of the geometric monodromy group is invariant under base extension on
k
.
However, it is not the case that the geometric monodromy group coincides with the arithmetic monodromy group after a sufficiently large base extension. For example, if
\mathcal{E}
is constant of rank
1
but not of finite order, then
G(\mathcal{E}_{k^{\prime}})=\mathbb{G}_{m}
for all finite extensions
k^{\prime}
of
k
, whereas
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
is trivial.

One consequence of this is that it is not immediately obvious whether a coefficient object which is
absolutely irreducible
(by which we mean it remains irreducible after any finite extension of the base field) is

geometrically irreducible
(by which we mean it is irreducible as a geometric coefficient object,
or equivalently that
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
acts irreducibly on its tautological representation).
This does turn out to be true;
moreover, for any irreducible coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
,
there exists a positive integer
n
such that on
X_{n}
,

\mathcal{E}
splits as a direct sum of geometrically irreducible subobjects

\mathcal{E}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{E}_{n}
over
X_{n}
which are cyclically permuted by the action of the
k
-Frobenius.
See
[
Ked22a
, Remark 9.13]
and references therein.

In light of Remark
2.27
, we may refine Lemma
2.4
using the following statement.

Lemma 2.28
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible and
\det(\mathcal{E})
is of finite order. Then for any positive integer
n
, every irreducible constituent of the pullback of
\mathcal{E}
to
X_{n}
has determinant of finite order.

Proof.

By Remark
2.27
,

\mathcal{E}
splits as a direct sum of irreducible subobjects

\mathcal{E}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{E}_{m}
over
X_{n}
which are cyclically permuted by the action of the
k
-Frobenius.
By Lemma
2.4
, we can choose a coefficient object
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
on
k_{n}
of rank
1

such that
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
has determinant of finite order. By extracting a suitable
n
-th root,
we may realize
\mathcal{L}
(not uniquely) as the pullback of a coefficient object
\mathcal{L}
on
k
of rank 1.
The products
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{L},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{L}
are then cyclically permuted by the action of the
k
-Frobenius; consequently, since the first one has finite order, so do the others. In particular,
\det(\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{L})
is of finite order, as then is
\mathcal{L}
, as then are
\det(\mathcal{E}_{1}),\dots,\det(\mathcal{E}_{n})
.
∎

Proposition 2.29
.

For any coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
,
there is a natural surjective continuous homomorphism
\psi_{\mathcal{E}}:\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X,\overline{x})\to\pi_{0}(G(\mathcal{E}))
,
which induces a surjective continuous homomorphism

\overline{\psi}_{\mathcal{E}}:\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})\to\pi_{0}(\overline{G}(\mathcal{E}))

In the étale case, this is apparent from Definition
2.21
.
For the crystalline case, see
[
D’Ad20

.

2.4. Tame and docile coefficients

In much of the study of companions, a crucial role is played by coefficient objects for
which the local monodromy at the boundary is tamely ramified and unipotent.

Definition 2.32
.

Let
X\hookrightarrow\overline{X}
be an open immersion with dense image.
Let
D
be an irreducible divisor of
\overline{X}
with generic point
\eta
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be a coefficient object on
X
.
In the étale case, we say that
\mathcal{E}
is
tame
(resp.
docile
) along
D
if the action of the inertia group of
\eta
on
\mathcal{E}
(arising from Definition
2.21
) is tamely ramified (resp. tamely ramified and unipotent).

In the crystalline case, we say that
\mathcal{E}
is
tame
(resp.
docile
) along
D
if
\mathcal{E}
has

\mathbb{Q}
-unipotent monodromy in the sense of
[
Shi10
, Definition 1.3]

(resp. unipotent monodromy in the sense of
[
Ked07
, Definition 4.4.2]
) along
D
.

We say that
\mathcal{E}
is
tame
(resp.
docile
) if it is so with respect to every choice of
\overline{X}
and
D
. By definition, this property is stable under arbitrary pullbacks.

Remark 2.33
.

The category of tame (resp. docile) coefficient objects on
X
is stable under subquotients and extensions. This is clear in the étale case; in the crystalline case, see
[
Ked07
, Proposition 3.2.20, Proposition 6.5.1]
.

To make this definition more useful in practice, we need the concept of a
good compactification
.

Definition 2.34
.

A
smooth pair
over
k
is a pair
(Y,Z)
in which
Y
is a smooth
k
-scheme and
Z
is a strict normal crossings
divisor on
Y
; we refer to
Z
as the
boundary
of the pair. (Note that
Z=\emptyset
is allowed.)
A
good compactification
of
X
is a smooth pair
(\overline{X},Z)
over
k
with
\overline{X}
projective (not just proper) over
k
, together with an isomorphism
X\cong\overline{X}\setminus Z
; we will generally treat the latter as an identification.

If
\overline{X}
is a good compactification of
X
with boundary
Z
, then a coefficient object on
X
is tame (resp. docile) if and only if it is so with respect to each component of
Z
. Namely, this follows from Zariski–Nagata purity in the étale case, and from
[
Ked07
, Theorem 6.4.5]
in the crystalline case.

For general
X
, the existence of a good compactification is (to the best of our understanding) not known; it would follow from resolution of singularities in characteristic
p
. As a workaround, we use de Jong’s theorem on alterations.

Definition 2.35
.

An
alteration
of
X
is a morphism
f:X^{\prime}\to X
which is proper, surjective, and generically finite étale. This corresponds to a
separable alteration
in the sense of de Jong
[
deJ
]
.

Proposition 2.36

(de Jong)
.

There exists an alteration
f:X^{\prime}\to X
such that
X^{\prime}
admits a good compactification. (Beware that
X^{\prime}
is not guaranteed to be geometrically irreducible over
k
.)

Proof.

Keeping in mind that
k
is perfect, see

[
deJ
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

We will frequently make use of the fact that the tame and docile conditions can always be achieved after pullback along a suitable alteration.

Proposition 2.37
.

For any coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
, there exists an alteration
f:X^{\prime}\to X

such that
X^{\prime}
admits a good compactification and
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
is docile.

(
As in Proposition
2.36
, we cannot guarantee that
X^{\prime}
is geometrically irreducible over
k.)

Proof.

We first treat the étale case.
We may assume that
X
is connected and choose a geometric basepoint
\overline{x}
.
By Definition
2.21
,
\mathcal{E}
corresponds to a representation of
W_{X}
on some finite-dimensional
L
-vector space
V
. The action of
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{et}}(X_{\overline{k}},\overline{x})
on
V
has compact image in

\GL(V)
, so we may choose a lattice
T
stable under this action. By taking
f
to trivialize the mod-
\ell
action on
T
(using Proposition
2.36
), we enforce that the actions of inertia are all tame. They are also quasi-unipotent by the usual argument of Grothendieck: the eigenvalues of Frobenius form a multiset of length at most
r:=\rank(\mathcal{E})
which is stable under taking
p
-th powers, so this multiset must consist entirely of roots of unity. To upgrade from quasi-unipotence to unipotence, it suffices to further trivialize the action on
T
modulo some power of
\ell
, which may be bounded either in terms of
p
and
r
(by the previous consideration)
or
k
(because
k
only contains finitely many
\ell
-power roots of unity).

In the crystalline case, we instead apply the semistable reduction theorem for overconvergent
F
-isocrystals
[
Ked22a
, Theorem 7.6]
.
∎

Remark 2.38
.

In the étale case, the proof of Proposition
2.37
also shows that there exists a finite étale cover
f_{0}:X_{0}^{\prime}\to X
such that
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
is docile (but we cannot then guarantee the existence of a good compactification without a further alteration).
After establishing the existence of étale companions, this will also hold in the crystalline case; see
Remark
4.30
.

Remark 2.39
.

In the crystalline case, our notion of a tame coefficient object matches that of Abe–Esnault

[
AE19
, §1.2]
. Note that in either the étale or crystalline case,
a coefficient object on
X
is tame if and only if it becomes docile after pullback along some finite étale cover of
X
which is tamely ramified at the boundary.

Lemma 2.40
.

A coefficient object on
X
is tame (resp. docile) if and only if its restriction to every curve in
X
is tame (resp. docile).

Proof.

We begin this discussion by recalling the statement of the Langlands correspondence for curves.

3.4. Compatibility of ramification

We now spell out in more detail what the compatibility assertion of Theorem
3.4
says about the local behavior of companions. For this, we need to introduce local monodromy representations, particularly in the crystalline case.

Definition 3.13
.

For
x\in\overline{X}
, let
I_{x}
denote the inertia group of
\overline{X}
at
x
.
For
\mathcal{E}
a coefficient object, denote by
\rho_{x}(\mathcal{E})
the
semisimplified local monodromy representation
of
I_{x}

associated to
\mathcal{E}
. In the étale case, this is simply the semisimplification of the restriction of the associated Weil group representation; in the crystalline case, see
[
Ked22a
, Remark 4.12]
.
We need the following properties of the construction.

\bullet

\mathcal{E}
is tame at
x
if and only if
\rho_{x}(\mathcal{E})
is at most
tamely ramified. In particular, if we write
\Swan_{x}(\mathcal{E})
for the Swan conductor of
\rho_{x}(\mathcal{E})
, then
\mathcal{E}
is tame at
x
if and only if
\Swan_{x}(\mathcal{E})=0
.

\bullet

\mathcal{E}
is docile at
x
if and only if
\rho_{x}(\mathcal{E})
is unramified. In particular, this is true if
x\in X
.

\bullet

The construction of
\rho_{x}
is functorial in the following sense: if
f:\overline{X}^{\prime}\to\overline{X}
is a finite flat morphism and
f(y)=x
, then
\rho_{y}(f^{*}\mathcal{E})
is the restriction of
\rho_{x}(\mathcal{E})
along

.

For curves, the Euler characteristic is related to local monodromy via the Grothendieck–Ogg–Shafarevich formula.

Proposition 3.14
.

For any coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
, we have

\chi(\mathcal{E})=\chi(X)\rank(\mathcal{E})-\sumop\displaylimits_{x\in Z}[\kappa(x)\colon\!k]\Swan_{x}(\mathcal{E}).

Proof.

See
[
Gro77
]
in the étale case and

[
Ked06b
, Theorem 4.3.1]
in the crystalline case.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
be coefficient objects on
X

(
possibly in different categories

which are companions.

\mathcal{E}
is tame
(

\mathcal{F}

(
By Lemma

From Definition
2.8

from

In the docile case, we may assume at once that both
\mathcal{E}

For each

; we remind the reader that we do not insist that curves be proper,
so the existence of such a curve is elementary (that is, it does not depend on anything as difficult as Poonen’s Bertini theorem
[
Poo04
]
).

4.1. Weights

We first address part (i) of Conjecture
1.1
by consolidating Deligne’s theory of weights from
[
Del81
]

using the Langlands correspondence in the form of Theorem
1.2
.
In the process, we will make a first use of the geometric setup that will recur in the discussion of Lefschetz slicing
(§
4.2
).

Hypothesis 4.2
.

Throughout §
4.1
,
fix a coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
,
and let
\iota
be an embedding of the full coefficient field of
\mathcal{E}
into
\mathbb{C}
.
(This choice is for expediency only; see Remark
4.13
.)
Also, let
q
denote the cardinality of
k
.

Definition 4.3
.

For
x\in X^{\circ}
, the
multiset of
\iota
-weights of
\mathcal{E}
at
x

is the multiset consisting of
-2\log_{\#\kappa(x)}\left|\iota(\lambda)\right|
as
\lambda
varies over the roots of
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)
(counted with multiplicity). Recall that these roots are reciprocals of eigenvalues of Frobenius.

We say that
\mathcal{E}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
w
if for all
x\in X^{\circ}
, the multiset of
\iota
-weights of
\mathcal{E}
at
x
consists of the single element
w
. If the determinant of
\mathcal{E}
is of finite order, we must then have
w=0
.

We will make crucial use of the following form of “Weil II”.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
w
. Then the eigenvalues of the Frobenius
\varphi
on
H^{1}(X,\mathcal{E})
all have
\iota
-absolute value at least
q^{(w+1)/2}
.

Proof.

See
[
Del81
, Théorème 3.3.1]
in the étale case and

[
Ked22a
, Theorem 10.3]
in the crystalline case.
∎

As in
[
Del81
, Théorème 3.4.1(3)]
, we have the following corollary.
(See also
[
D’Ad20
, Corollary 3.5.2]
.)

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}
is
\iota
-pure.

(a)

In the category of geometric coefficient objects,
\mathcal{E}
is semisimple.

(b)

In the category of coefficient objects,
\mathcal{E}
admits an
isotypical decomposition
: a direct sum decomposition in which each summand is a successive extension of a single irreducible object.

Proof.

We prove both assertions at once by induction on
\rank(\mathcal{E})
.
Suppose first that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the sum of all irreducible subobjects of
\mathcal{E}
in the category of geometric coefficient objects; then
\mathcal{F}
is stable under Frobenius, and hence a subobject of
\mathcal{E}
in the category of coefficient objects. It follows that
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}
, proving (a); meanwhile, (b) is vacuous.

Suppose next that
\mathcal{E}
is reducible. Then there exists
an exact sequence

(4.1)

0\to\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}_{2}\to 0

of coefficient objects in which
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is irreducible and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is nonzero.
If this sequence splits, then we may apply the induction hypothesis to
\mathcal{E}_{2}
to deduce both (a) and (b). Otherwise, from the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence (compare

[
Del81
, Lemme 3.4.2]
in the étale case), we obtain an exact sequence

(4.2)

H^{0}(X,\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1})_{\varphi}\to\Ext(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1})\to H^{1}(X,\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1})^{\varphi}

where the
\Ext
group is defined in the category of coefficient objects.
Since
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
0
, the term on the right vanishes by Lemma
4.4
.
It follows that the extension (
4.1
) splits in the category of geometric coefficient objects,
so we may deduce (a) from the induction hypothesis.
Meanwhile, the nonvanishing of the middle term of (
4.2
) implies that the finite-dimensional vector space
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1})
has nonzero
\varphi
-coinvariants and hence nonzero
\varphi
-invariants; that is, there is a nonzero homomorphism
\mathcal{E}_{2}\to\mathcal{E}_{1}

in the category of coefficient objects (not just geometric coefficient objects).
By the induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{E}_{2}
admits an isotypical decomposition, which must admit a nonzero summand corresponding to
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. Let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{1}
be the preimage of this summand in
\mathcal{E}
; then the same argument applied to the exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{2}\to 0

shows that this sequence must split. Since
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{1}
is isotypical, we may apply the induction hypothesis to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{2}
to deduce (b).
∎

The following lemma will occur in passing in the construction of the weight filtration, but will be used
much more heavily in the study of uniqueness of companions (§
4.3
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that
X
is irreducible of pure dimension
d
and that
\mathcal{E}
is

\iota
-pure of weight
0
. Then
the pole order of
L(\mathcal{E},T)
at
T=q^{-d}
equals the multiplicity of
1
as an eigenvalue of Frobenius on
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{E}^{\vee})

(
viewed as a vector space over the full coefficient field
)
.

Proof.

In the Lefschetz trace formula (
2.1
), the factor
i=0
contributes the predicted value to the pole order. Meanwhile, by Lemma
4.4
, the eigenvalues of
F
on
H^{i}(X,\mathcal{E}^{\vee})
for
i>0
all have absolute value at least
q^{1/2}
,
so the corresponding factor of (
2.1
) only has zeroes or poles in the region

|T|\geq q^{-d+1/2}
. This proves the claim.
∎

By probing
X
with curves, we gain some initial data about weights.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that
X
is irreducible.

(a)

The multiset of
\iota
-weights of
\mathcal{E}
at
x\in X^{\circ}
is independent of
x
.
We may thus refer to it as the
multiset of
\iota
-weights of
\mathcal{E}
.

(b)

There exist a constant twist
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{E}
and a positive integer
N
such that
the multiset of
\iota
-weights of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
has least element
0

and for each
x\in X^{\circ}
, the product of the roots of
P(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{x},T)
of
\iota
-weight
0

equals a root of unity of order dividing
N
.

Proof.

To check (a), it suffices to compare two points
x,y\in X^{\circ}
; this may be done by restricting to a curve in
X
through

x,y
. That is, we may assume that
X
is a curve; in this case, we may further assume that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible and
(by making a constant twist dictated by
Lemma
2.4
) its determinant is of finite order. We may then apply Theorem
1.2

to deduce that
\mathcal{E}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
0
.

To check (b), we may first twist to ensure that for some
x\in X^{\circ}
, the product in question equals
1
.
Given any other point
y\in X^{\circ}
, choose a curve
C
in
X
containing
x
and
y
; by Theorem
1.2
again,
the eigenvalues in question arise from some irreducible constituents of
\mathcal{E}|_{C}
. By Lemma
2.4
,
we see that the product of the eigenvalues at
y
is a root of unity; since these eigenvalues belong to an extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
or
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of degree bounded independently of

, the order of the root of unity is also bounded.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Suppose that for some
w\in\mathbb{R}
,
there exists an open dense subset
U
of
X
such that
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
w
.
Then
\mathcal{E}
is also
\iota
-pure of weight
w
.

Proof.

This is immediate from Lemma
4.7
(a).
∎

We now introduce the geometric setup for Lefschetz slicing.
We recall here a commonly used construction found in
[
Art73
, Proposition 3.3]
.
(One can make a similar construction without enlarging
k
using Poonen’s Bertini theorem; see §
4.7
.)

Lemma 4.9
.

Pick
x\in X(k)
and let

\tilde{X}
be the blowup of
X
at
(
the reduced closed subscheme supported at
)

x
.
Then for some finite extension
k^{\prime}
of
k
,
there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x
in
X_{k^{\prime}}
whose inverse image
\tilde{U}
in
\tilde{X}_{k^{\prime}}
can be expressed
as a fibration
f:\tilde{U}\to S
in curves over some smooth proper
k^{\prime}
-scheme
S
.

Proof.

Since we are free to replace
X
with an open subscheme containing
x
, we may ensure that
X
is affine of dimension
n
.
Let
\overline{X}
be the Zariski closure of
X
in some projective embedding
X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}_{k}
.
For some choice of
k^{\prime}
, we can choose sections
s_{0},\dots,s_{n-1}
of
\mathcal{O}(1)
on
\mathbb{P}^{N}_{k^{\prime}}
whose zero loci
H_{0},\dots,H_{n-1}
on
\mathbb{P}^{N}_{k^{\prime}}
have the following properties
(because each property holds generically).

\bullet

The intersection
L=H_{0}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-1}
has dimension
N-n
and contains
x
.

\bullet

The intersection
L\cap X_{k^{\prime}}
is transversal at
x
.

\bullet

The intersection
L\cap(\overline{X}_{k^{\prime}}\setminus X_{k^{\prime}})
is empty.

Put
Z:=(L\cap X_{k^{\prime}})\setminus\{x\}
;
then the sections
s_{0},\dots,s_{n-1}
define a map

f:\tilde{X}_{k^{\prime}}\setminus Z\to S:=\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{k^{\prime}}
.
The exceptional divisor of
\tilde{X}_{k^{\prime}}\to X_{k^{\prime}}

is the image of a section
g:S\to\tilde{X}_{k^{\prime}}
of
f
.
Each fiber of
f
is isomorphic to an intersection of
X_{k^{\prime}}\setminus Z
with
n-1
hyperplanes which is transversal at
x
; consequently, there is a unique irreducible component of this fiber containing
x
, and this component is smooth of dimension 1 at
x
.
We now take
\tilde{U}
to be the set obtained from
\tilde{X}_{k^{\prime}}\setminus Z
by removing:

\bullet

the union of all irreducible components of fibers of
f
which do not meet
g(S)
; and

\bullet

the set of all singular points of irreducible components of fibers of
f
which do meet
g(S)
.

This yields an open subscheme
\tilde{U}
of
\tilde{X}_{k^{\prime}}\setminus Z
not meeting
g(S)
;
we take
U
to be the image of
\tilde{U}
in
X_{k^{\prime}}
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

We iterate a key point from the proof of Lemma
4.9
: the exceptional divisor of the blowup
\tilde{U}\to U
is the image of a section
g:S\to\tilde{U}
of
f

on which the pullback of
\mathcal{E}
is constant.
We will combine this geometric construction with the following observation: for any coefficient object
\mathcal{F}
in the same category as
\mathcal{E}
, there exists an open dense subscheme
V
of
S
(depending on
\mathcal{F}
)
such that
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
exists as a coefficient object on
V
and its formation commutes with arbitrary base change on
V
.
(See
[
Ked06a
, Theorem 7.3.3]
for the crystalline case.)

We finally obtain the main theorem on weights.

Theorem 4.11
.

The following statements hold.

(a)

If
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible, then it is
\iota
-pure of some weight.

(b)

There exists a unique filtration

0=\mathcal{E}_{0}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{E}_{l}=\mathcal{E}

for which there exists an increasing sequence
w_{1}<\cdots<w_{l}
of real numbers
for which
\mathcal{E}_{i}/\mathcal{E}_{i-1}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
w_{i}
. We call this filtration the
weight filtration
of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

For any given
X
, part (a) of Theorem
4.11
implies part (b) using Lemma
4.4
;
see
[
Ked22a
, Corollary 10.5]
or
[
D’Ad20
, Corollary 3.5.2]
.
For
\dim(X)=1
, we may apply Theorem
1.2
to obtain (a).

It thus remains to prove (a) for general
X

using the truth of (b) for curves. For this, we may assume that
X
is irreducible; moreover,
by Corollary
4.8
, we may check the claim after replacing
X
with an open dense subscheme.
We may also make a constant twist on
\mathcal{E}
; hence by Lemma
4.7
,
we may assume that the multiset of
\iota
-weights of
\mathcal{E}
has least element
0
with some multiplicity
m
, and that for each
x\in X^{\circ}
the product of
the corresponding roots of
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)
is a root of unity of order dividing some fixed positive integer
N
.

After shrinking
X
and enlarging
k
, we may apply Lemma
4.9
and Remark
4.10
to
\mathcal{F}:=\wedge^{mN}(\mathcal{E}^{\oplus N})
.
On each fiber of
f
, we may apply the induction hypothesis to produce a weight filtration of
\mathcal{F}
; the first step
of this filtration is an object of rank
1
with all Frobenius eigenvalues equal to 1, so by Lemma
4.6

it is trivial.
On some open subscheme
V
of
S
, we may form the pushforward
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
in a manner compatible with arbitrary base change (Remark
4.10
);
this pushforward must therefore be trivial of rank 1, and we may pull back to obtain
a trivial rank-
1
subobject of
\mathcal{F}
(initially on
f^{-1}(V)
, but by
Lemma
2.3
this subobject extends over
X
).
By multilinear algebra, we recover a subobject of
\mathcal{E}
of rank
m
;
this is a contradiction unless
m=\rank(\mathcal{E})
, meaning that
\mathcal{E}
is
\iota
-pure of weight
0
.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

An alternate approach to proving Theorem
4.11
would be to use the fact that
part (a) can be checked after an alteration; in light of Proposition
2.37
, this means that it suffices to check (a) under the additional assumption that
\mathcal{E}
is tame. In this context, one can obtain very strong versions of Lefschetz
slicing; see §
4.7
.

Remark 4.13
.

We again remind the reader of
[
Del81
, Remarque 1.2.11]
:
the use of an algebraic embedding
\iota
in Theorem
4.11
is merely for expository convenience.

A posteriori
, we will only need to embed various number fields into
\mathbb{C}
.

On a similar note, the weight of an irreducible coefficient object may depend on the choice of
\iota
,
but in light of Lemma
2.4
this reduces to a rank-1 issue.
In any case, note that changing
\iota
may change the order of the successive quotients in the weight filtration,
in which case some splitting must occur.

4.2. Lefschetz slicing and valuations of eigenvalues

As discussed in the introduction, a key tool in reducing the study of general coefficient objects to the case of curves is the preservation of irreducibility of a coefficient object under restriction to a suitable curve,
but the usual proof of this in the étale case does not apply in the crystalline case.
We give a replacement argument here using weights; we make no effort to strengthen the final result because this can easily be done

a posteriori
(see §
4.7
).
We then deduce parts (iii) and (iv) of Conjecture
1.1
, as well as a weak version of (ii).

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a geometrically irreducible coefficient object on
X

and choose
x\in X^{\circ}
.
Then for some positive integer
n
, there exists a curve
C
in
X_{n}
containing
x
such that
\mathcal{E}|_{C}
is irreducible.

Proof.

Instead of keeping track of the extension degree
n
, we adopt the convention that we may enlarge
k
freely during the argument, replacing
x
with some closed point lying over it.
To begin with, we may assume that
x\in X(k)
.

Apply Lemma
4.9
and Remark
4.10
with
\mathcal{F}
equal to the trace-zero component of
\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}
.
By Remark
2.27
,
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
must act irreducibly on its standard representation, so

H^{0}(X,\mathcal{E}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E})
is the span of the identity map and
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{F})=0
.

Since
g^{*}\mathcal{E}
is a trivial coefficient object, so is
g^{*}\mathcal{F}
.
By adjunction,
f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F}
corresponds to a subobject of
\mathcal{F}
, so
g^{*}f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F}
corresponds to a nonzero subobject of
g^{*}\mathcal{F}
;
hence
g^{*}f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F}
is trivial.
Since
g
is a section of
f
, there is a natural identification
g^{*}f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F}\cong f_{*}\mathcal{F}
; hence
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
is trivial, as then is
f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F}
.
In particular, the dimension of
H^{0}(X,f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F})
equals the rank of
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
; since
H^{0}(X,f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{F})
is a subspace of
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{F})=0
, we conclude that
f_{*}\mathcal{F}=0
.

Choose a point
y\in S(k)
and take
C:=f^{-1}(y)
. The vanishing of
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
implies that
H^{0}(C,\mathcal{F}|_{C})=0
.
Pick an embedding
\iota
of the coefficient field of
\mathcal{E}
into
\mathbb{C}
; by Theorem
4.11
,
\mathcal{E}
is

\iota
-pure of some weight, and likewise after restricting to
C
. By Corollary
4.5
,
\mathcal{E}|_{C}

admits an isotypical decomposition; combined with the vanishing of
H^{0}(C,\mathcal{F}|_{C})
, this implies first that
\mathcal{E}|_{C}
is isotypical, and second that
\mathcal{E}|_{C}
is irreducible.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

Another way to establish Lemma
4.14
, which does not require a prior study of weights,
is to
show that the inclusion
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E}|_{C})\to\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})
is an isomorphism
by applying Lemma
4.9
and Remark
4.10
with
\mathcal{F}
being
a sufficiently large object in the Tannakian category generated by
\mathcal{E}
.
This is in the spirit of Corollary
4.39
below, but requires slightly more care because the open set
U

varies with
\mathcal{F}
; one must thus make some argument to give an
a priori
choice of
\mathcal{F}
that suffices to rule out all unwanted possibilities for
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E}|_{C})
.
(As in Lemma
4.7
, the key point is that an extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
or
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of bounded degree contains a bounded number of roots of unity.)

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a coefficient object on
X
which is irreducible with determinant of finite order.
Let
\lambda
be a reciprocal root of
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)
for some
x\in X^{\circ}
.

(a)

\lambda
is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}
.

(b)

At any
(
finite or infinite
)
place of
\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)
not lying above
p
,
\lambda
has absolute value
1
.

(
For infinite places, this is included in Theorem
4.11
.
)

(c)

At any place of
\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)
lying above
p
,
\lambda
has absolute value at least that of
\kappa(x)
to the power
\frac{1}{2}\rank(\mathcal{E})
.

In particular, parts (i), (iii), and (iv) of Conjecture
1.1
hold.

Proof.

We start with some initial reductions.
For
n
a positive integer, let
f_{n}:X_{n}\to X
be the canonical morphism. For
x_{n}\in X_{n}^{\circ}
lying over
x\in X^{\circ}
,
the roots of
P((f_{n}^{*}\mathcal{E})_{x_{n}},T)
are the
[\kappa(x_{n}):\kappa(x)]
-th powers of the roots of
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)
;
consequently, for any particular
x
, checking the claims at
x
is equivalent to checking them at
x_{n}
.

This means that by Remark
2.27
and Lemma
2.28
, we may reduce to the case where
\mathcal{E}
is geometrically irreducible. Then for each
x\in X^{\circ}
, we may apply Lemma
4.14
to find a curve
C
in
X_{n}
through
x
, for some
n

depending on
x
, for which
\mathcal{E}|_{C}
is again irreducible.
We may then invoke Theorem
1.2
to deduce that the desired assertions hold at
x
.
∎

4.3. Uniqueness of companions via weights

For étale coefficient objects, the uniqueness of a companion in a given category is an easy consequence of the Chebotarëv density theorem applied to residual representations. In order to obtain similar assertions that also cover crystalline coefficient objects, one must replace the use of Chebotarëv density with a weight-theoretic argument. The argument we have in mind is due to Tsuzuki (as presented in
[
Abe18a
, Proposition A.4.1]
,
[
Ked22a
, Theorem 10.8]
), but it must be noted that similar arguments occur in étale cohomology in the context of independence-of-
\ell
statements, as in Larsen–Pink
[
LP95
, Proposition 2.1]
(see also
[
Pál15
, Proposition 8.20]
).

Theorem 4.17

(Tsuzuki)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
be two algebraic coefficient objects on
X
which are companions.

(a)

If
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is irreducible, then so is
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
are in the same category, then
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
have the same semisimplification.

Proof.

In both cases, we may assume that
X
is irreducible of pure dimension
d
, and
(by Theorem
4.11
) that
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
are both pure of weight 0 and semisimple.

In case (a), note that by Schur’s lemma,
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is irreducible if and only if
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{i})^{\varphi}
is one-dimensional. We may thus apply Lemma
4.6
to
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}
to conclude.

In case (b), it suffices to check that any irreducible subobject
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
(which must also be pure of weight 0) also occurs as a summand of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
To this end, note that
\mathcal{F}
occurs as a summand of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
if and only if
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{F}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{i})^{\varphi}\neq 0
;
we may thus apply Lemma
4.6
to
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{F}
to conclude.
∎

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
be algebraic coefficient objects on
X
(not necessarily in the same category).
If there exists an open dense subscheme
U
of
X
such that
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
and
\mathcal{F}|_{U}
are companions,
then
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
are companions.

Proof.

It suffices to check the equality
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)=P(\mathcal{F}_{x},T)
at an arbitrary
x\in X^{\circ}
.
By restricting to a suitable curve in
X
containing
x
, we may further assume that
\dim(X)=1
.
At this point, we may assume that
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
are semisimple.
By Corollary
3.11
,
\mathcal{E}
admits a semisimple companion
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
in the same category as
\mathcal{F}
. On

, the restrictions
\mathcal{F}|_{U}
and
\mathcal{F}|^{\prime}_{U}
are semisimple (by Lemma

)
companions of each other; by Theorem

\mathcal{F}|_{U}\cong\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|_{U}
.
By Lemma
2.3
again, this isomorphism extends to
X
; this yields the desired result.
∎

Corollary 4.19
.

Let
U
be an open dense subscheme of
X
. Let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
be algebraic coefficient objects on
X
and
U
, respectively
(
not necessarily in the same category
)
, with
\mathcal{F}
semisimple. If
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
and
\mathcal{F}
are companions, then
\mathcal{F}
extends to a coefficient object on
X
which is a companion of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.18
, we only need to check that
\mathcal{F}
extends to
X
.
Using the “cut-by-curves” criterion (see
[
Ked22a
, Theorem 5.16]
in the crystalline case),
it suffices to check that for any morphism
f:C\to X
over
k
where
C
is a curve over
k
, the pullback
f^{*}\mathcal{F}
extends
from
C\times_{X}U
to
C
.
To this end, apply Corollary
3.11
to construct a semisimple crystalline companion
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
on
C
. By Lemma
2.3
, the restriction of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
to
C\times_{X}U
is again semisimple.
Now
f^{*}\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|_{C\times_{X}U}
are semisimple companions of each other in the same category,
so by Theorem
4.17
(b) they are isomorphic; this proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 4.20
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
be algebraic coefficient objects on
X

(
not necessarily in the same category
)
.
If
\det(\mathcal{E})
is of finite order, then so is
\det(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

Since
\det(\mathcal{E})
and
\det(\mathcal{F})
are again companions, we may assume at once that
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
are of rank 1. Choose a positive integer
n
for which
\mathcal{E}^{\otimes n}
is trivial; then
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes n}
is a companion of the trivial object, and so by Theorem
4.17
(b)
is itself trivial.
∎

Corollary 4.21
.

The conclusion of Corollary
3.12
, which was originally stated for

,
holds for general
X
.

Proof.

We may replace the application of Theorem
3.8
in the proof of Corollary
4.21
with Theorem
4.17
, and then the rest of the argument remains unchanged.
∎

Remark 4.22
.

A related assertion to Theorem

(b)
is that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and

are forced to have the same semisimplification
as soon as the equality

holds for

determined by

and

and the wild monodromy of

.

Remark 4.23
.

While Theorem
4.17
is formulated in terms of an algebraically closed full coefficient field,
it also implies a corresponding assertion for a restricted coefficient field. Namely, suppose that
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
are two objects in
\Weil(X)\otimes L
or
\FIsoc^{\dagger}(X)\otimes L
, where
L
is a finite extension
of the base coefficient field, which are companions of each other. Suppose further that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible
as a coefficient object (
i.e.
, it remains irreducible after replacing

with any finite extension).
Then Theorem

implies that
\mathcal{E}
and

are isomorphic as objects

4.4. Uniform algebraicity

We next apply the method of Deligne
[
Del12
]

to address part (ii) of Conjecture
1.1
.
This discussion constitutes our first use of
uniformity
across curves within a fixed variety, as a technique for studying coefficient objects on higher-dimensional varieties; this technique will prove to be extremely fruitful as we continue.

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
U
be an open dense subscheme of
X
, let
E
be a number field, and let
\mathcal{E}
be a coefficient object on
X
.
If
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
is
E
-algebraic, then so is
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Since
E
-algebraicity is a pointwise condition for any given
E
, to check it at some
x\in X^{\circ}
it suffices to do so after restriction to some curve in
X
containing
x
. We may thus assume hereafter that
X
is a curve over
k
.
We may further assume that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible.

We first check that
\mathcal{E}
is uniformly algebraic (but not necessarily
E
-algebraic). By Lemma
2.4
, there exists a
constant coefficient object
\mathcal{F}
of rank 1 such that
\det(\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{F})
has finite order.
By restricting to
U
, we see that
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes\rank(\mathcal{E})}
is (uniformly) algebraic, as then is
\mathcal{F}
.
Consequently, to check that
\mathcal{E}
is uniformly algebraic, we may reduce to the case where
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible with finite determinant, in which case Theorem
3.8
applies.

By the previous paragraph, we can find a Galois number field
E^{\prime}
containing
E
such that
\mathcal{E}
is
E^{\prime}
-algebraic.
By Corollary
3.9
, for each automorphism
\tau\in\Gal(E^{\prime}/E)
, we can find another coefficient object
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
in the same category as
\mathcal{E}
whose Frobenius characteristic polynomials are related to those of
\mathcal{E}
by application of
\tau
. By hypothesis,
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}|_{U}
are companions, as then are
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
by Corollary
4.18
. This proves the claim.
∎

Theorem 4.25
.

Any algebraic coefficient object on
X
is uniformly algebraic.

Proof.

We use the axiomatized form of Deligne’s argument described in
[
Del12
, Remarque 3.10]

(see also
[
EK12
, Theorem 5.1]
for an alternate exposition).
By Lemma
4.24
, there is no harm in replacing
X
with an open dense subscheme during the argument.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an algebraic coefficient object on
X
.
For each positive integer
n
, consider the function
t_{n}
assigning to each
x\in X(k_{n})
the trace of
F^{n}
on
\mathcal{E}_{x}
. These functions have the following properties.

(a)

For each morphism
f:Z\to X
with
Z
smooth of dimension 1 (but not necessarily geometrically irreducible over
k
), the functions
t_{n}\circ f
arise from an étale coefficient object
\mathcal{F}_{Z}
on
Z
. This follows from Corollary
3.11
.

(b)

There exists a connected finite étale cover
X^{\prime}
of
X
such that in (a), if
f
factors through
X^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{Z}
is tame.
In light of Proposition
2.37
and Corollary
3.15
,
this becomes true after replacing
X
with a suitable open dense subscheme (but see Remark
4.30
below).

Given these properties,
[
Del12
, Remarque 3.10]
implies the claim.
∎

Corollary 4.26
.

For
\mathcal{E}
a coefficient object on
X
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{E}
is algebraic.

(ii)

\mathcal{E}
is uniformly algebraic.

(iii)

Each irreducible constituent of
\mathcal{E}
is the twist of an object with determinant of finite order by an algebraic object of rank
1
pulled back from
k
.

(iv)

There exists a positive integer
n
such that the pullback of
\mathcal{E}
to
X_{n}
satisfies (iii)
(
with
k
replaced by
k_{n})
.

In other words, the conclusion of Corollary
3.10

(
which applies when
X
is a curve over
k)
holds for arbitrary
X
. In particular, part (ii) of Conjecture
1.1
holds.

Proof.

It is again obvious that (ii) implies (i) and that (iii) implies (iv).
By Lemma
2.4
, (i) implies (iii).
By Lemma
4.16
(a), (iv) implies (i).
By Theorem
4.25
, (i) implies (ii).
∎

4.5. Étale companions

We address part (v) of Conjecture
1.1
by
adapting the method of Drinfeld
[
Dri12
]
to include the crystalline case.

Lemma 4.27
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an object of
\Weil(X)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
.
Then
\mathcal{E}
is a lisse étale
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf if and only if for each
x\in X^{\circ}
, the roots of
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)

all have
\ell
-adic valuation
0
; in particular, this holds if
\mathcal{E}
is
p
-plain.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible.
If
\mathcal{E}
is a lisse étale
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf, then the image of
W_{X}
in the corresponding representation is compact; this implies that the roots of
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)
all have
\ell
-adic valuation
0
.

To check the converse implication, as per Definition
3.2

we may further assume that
\mathcal{E}
is of rank 1.
By Lemma
2.4
, we may write
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{L}
where

\mathcal{F}
is of finite order and
\mathcal{L}
is constant.
We may then check the claim after replacing
\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{L}
; that is, we may assume that
X=\Spec(k)
,
for which the desired statement is obvious.
(Compare
[
D’Ad20
, Proposition 3.1.16]
.)
∎

Theorem 4.28
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a coefficient object on
X
.

(a)

If
\mathcal{E}
is algebraic, then it has an étale companion.

(b)

If
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible with determinant of finite order, then it has an étale companion which is an irreducible lisse étale
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf with determinant of finite order.

In particular, part (v) of Conjecture
1.1
holds.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, it suffices to prove (b).
It was shown by Drinfeld
[
Dri12
, Theorem 2.5]
that a lisse étale
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf on
X
can be constructed out of any family of Frobenius characteristic polynomials at closed points with the following properties
(analogous to the ones used in the proof of Theorem
4.25
).

(a)

On any curve in
X
, there is a (not necessarily irreducible) lisse étale
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf giving rise to the specified characteristic polynomials.

(b)

The sheaves occurring in (a) obey a uniformity property: their pullbacks along some fixed alteration of
X
are all tame.

The polynomials
P(\mathcal{E}_{x},T)
satisfy these conditions: condition (a) follows from Corollary
3.11
, noting that Lemma
4.16
(b) and Lemma
4.27
together guarantee that we get a lisse étale
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf, not just a lisse Weil
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf;
condition (b) follows from Proposition
2.37
and Corollary
3.15
.

This proves that
\mathcal{E}
has an étale companion
\mathcal{F}
, which by Theorem
4.17
(a) is again irreducible.
By Corollary
4.20
,
\det(\mathcal{F})
is of finite order.
∎

Corollary 4.29
.

Conjecture
1.4
holds when
\ell^{\prime}\neq p
,
and Theorem
1.3
holds.

Proof.

Theorem
4.28
(a) implies that Conjecture
1.4
holds when
\ell^{\prime}\neq p
.
As for Theorem
1.3
,
see Theorem
4.11
(a) for part (i);
see Corollary
4.26
for part (ii);
see Lemma
4.16
for parts (iii), (iv);
and see Theorem
4.28
(b) for part (v).
∎

.

As noted above, the semistable reduction theorem for overconvergent
F
-isocrystals
[
Ked22a

makes it possible
(in conjunction with Remark

) to choose a finite étale cover of

.

4.6. Reductions for crystalline companions

One unexpected side benefit of the construction of étale companions is that it helps with some key reductions in the construction of crystalline companions. These reductions will be used in
[
Ked22b
]
.

The following is essentially
[
D’Ad20
, Proposition 4.3.6]
.

Lemma 4.31
.

Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
be two algebraic coefficient objects which are companions.
Then there is a bijection between the irreducible constituents of
\mathcal{E}
and those of
\mathcal{F}
, in which
any two objects in correspondence are again companions.

Proof.

Suppose first that at least one of
\mathcal{E}
or
\mathcal{F}
is an étale coefficient object, say
\mathcal{F}
.
Apply Theorem
4.28
to
each irreducible constituent of
\mathcal{E}
to obtain objects in the same category as
\mathcal{F}
; by Theorem
4.17
(a), these objects are all irreducible.
Taking their sum yields obtain another companion
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{E}
in the same category as
\mathcal{F}
;
by Theorem
4.17
(b),
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
have the same semisimplification.

Suppose next that
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
are both crystalline objects.
By Theorem
4.28
, we may insert an étale companion
\mathcal{G}
between
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
, and then apply the previous paragraph to pass from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Lemma 4.32
.

Let
f:X^{\prime}\to X
be a finite étale morphism.

(a)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an irreducible algebraic coefficient object on
X
,
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a semisimple companion of
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
. Then
there exist a companion
\mathcal{F}_{0}
of
\mathcal{E}
and an isomorphism
f^{*}\mathcal{F}_{0}\cong\mathcal{F}
.

(b)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a semisimple algebraic coefficient object on
X^{\prime}
,
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a semisimple companion of
f_{*}\mathcal{E}
. Then there exists a companion
\mathcal{F}_{0}
of
\mathcal{E}

which is a direct summand of
f^{*}\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

We first treat (a).
Since
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
is a companion of
f_{*}f^{*}\mathcal{E}

and
\mathcal{E}
arises as a direct summand of
f_{*}f^{*}\mathcal{E}
, we may apply Lemma
4.31

to deduce that
\mathcal{E}
has an irreducible companion
\mathcal{F}_{0}
which is a constituent of
f_{*}\mathcal{F}
.
By Proposition
2.30
,
f^{*}\mathcal{F}_{0}
is semisimple
(note that
\overline{G}(\mathcal{F}_{0})
and
\overline{G}(f^{*}\mathcal{F}_{0})
have the same connected part thanks to
Proposition
2.29
).
Since
f^{*}\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}
are both semisimple companions of
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
, by Theorem
4.17
they are isomorphic to each other.

We next treat (b).
By Proposition
2.30
again,
f^{*}\mathcal{F}
is semisimple.
Since
f^{*}\mathcal{F}
is a semisimple companion of
f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{E}

and
\mathcal{E}
occurs as a direct summand of
f^{*}f_{*}\mathcal{E}
, we may again apply Lemma
4.31

to deduce that every constituent of
\mathcal{E}
has an irreducible companion which is a direct summand of
f^{*}\mathcal{F}
.
This proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 4.33
.

Let
f:X^{\prime}\to X
be an alteration.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be an algebraic coefficient object on
X
.
If
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
admits a crystalline companion, then so does
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

We may assume from the outset that
\mathcal{E}
is irreducible.
Suppose first that
f
is flat (and hence finite and separable).
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a crystalline companion of
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
.
Let
U
be the open dense subscheme of
X
over which
f
is étale (and hence finite étale because
f
is proper).
By Lemma
4.32
(a), there exist a crystalline companion

\mathcal{F}_{0}
for
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
and an isomorphism
f^{*}\mathcal{F}_{0}\cong\mathcal{F}
.
This isomorphism gives rise to a descent datum for
\mathcal{F}
on the hypercovering of
U
generated by
f|_{U}
;
by the full faithfulness of restriction (Lemma
2.3
), this descent datum extends over
X
.
By faithfully flat descent for modules, the descent datum arises from an object of
\FIsoc^{\dagger}(X)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
whose restriction to
U
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}_{0}
; by
Lemma
2.3
again,
\mathcal{F}_{0}
must itself extend to
X
.
By Corollary
4.18
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is a crystalline companion of
\mathcal{E}
, as desired.

We now treat the general case.
Let
U
be the open dense subscheme of
X
over which
f
is flat; since
X^{\prime}
is smooth,
the complement
Z:=X\setminus U
has codimension at least 2 in
X
.
By the previous paragraph,
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
admits a crystalline companion
\mathcal{F}
;
by Lemma
2.3
,
\mathcal{F}
extends uniquely to
X
.
By Corollary
4.18
,
\mathcal{F}
is a crystalline companion of
\mathcal{E}
, as desired.
∎

Corollary 4.34
.

To prove Conjecture
1.1
, it suffices to prove the following: for every
X
admitting a smooth compactification
\overline{X}
and every docile étale coefficient object
\mathcal{E}
on
X
which is irreducible with finite determinant,
there exists an open dense subscheme
U
of
X
such that
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
has a crystalline companion.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
(for preservation of irreducibility upon restriction to an open dense subscheme),
Remark
2.33
(preservation of docileness upon passage to subquotients),
and Corollary
4.19
, the hypothesis would imply that every docile
\ell
-adic coefficient object has a crystalline companion.
By Proposition
2.37
and Corollary
4.33
, we would then obtain
Conjecture
1.4
for
\ell^{\prime}=p
. By Corollary
4.20
and Lemma

,
the crystalline companion of an étale coefficient object which is irreducible of finite determinant is again irreducible of finite determinant. In light of Theorem

, this proves the claim.
∎

4.7. Comparison with Abe–Esnault and Lefschetz slicing

As mentioned above, there is a strong overlap between the results described in this section and those of Abe–Esnault

[
AE19
]
; the main differences are in the handling of Lefschetz slicing. We first indicate the main differences, then indicate how to reconcile the results.

Remark 4.35
.

For tame crystalline coefficient objects, Abe–Esnault prove a form of the slicing principle
without recourse to weights
[
AE19
, Theorem 2.5]
.
This then yields the existence of étale companions for tame crystalline coefficient objects

[
AE19
, Proposition 2.7]
using the arguments of Deligne and Drinfeld described above.

Abe–Esnault then use the existence of étale companions in the tame case to establish a form of the slicing principle without the tame restriction
[
AE19
, Theorem 3.10]
. This in turn yields the existence of étale companions
as above.

Remark 4.36
.

Note that the forms of the slicing principle obtained in
[
AE19
]
are
a priori
stronger than Lemma
4.14
, in that there is no base extension necessary. On the other hand, with Theorem
1.3
in hand, one can transfer
any
version of the Lefschetz slicing principle from the étale case to the crystalline case; in particular, one can recover these stronger statements
a posteriori
by working on the étale side, as demonstrated by Theorem
4.37
and Theorem
4.41
below.
However, even in the étale case some care is required; for example, an incorrect version of the slicing principle is asserted in
[
Laf02
, VII.7]
. See
[
Esn17
, Theorem 8.1]
for further discussion of this point,
and the rest of that article for a broader discussion of Lefschetz slicing.

The following result reproduces
[
AE19
, Theorem 2.5]
,
modulo a theorem of Poonen
[
Poo04
, Theorem 1.1]
which can be used to produce curves as described passing through any finite set of closed points. Compare
[
Dri12
, Proposition C.1]
.
(See
[
Esn17
, Lemma 5.4]
for an example showing that the tame hypothesis cannot be omitted.)

Theorem 4.37
.

Let
U
be an open dense affine subscheme of
X
.
Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{n-1}
be very ample divisors on
U
which intersect transversely.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be a tame irreducible coefficient object on
X
.
Then the restriction of
\mathcal{E}
to
C:=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-1}
is irreducible.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
,
\mathcal{E}|_{U}
is irreducible; we may thus assume hereafter that
U=X
.
Using Lemma
2.4
, we may reduce to the case where
\mathcal{E}
has determinant of finite order;
by Corollary
4.26
,
\mathcal{E}
is algebraic.
By Theorem
4.28
and Lemma
4.31
,
\mathcal{E}
admits an irreducible
\ell
-adic companion for any
\ell\neq p
; we may thus further reduce to the étale case. In this setting, the claim follows from the fact that for any connected finite étale cover
W
of
X
which is tamely ramified at the boundary,
W\times_{X}C
is also connected;
this follows from the Bertini connectedness theorem
[
FL81
, Theorem 2.1(A)]

as in
[
Dri12
, Lemma C.2]
.
∎

We now turn around and invoke a lemma from
[
AE19
]
that allows one to formally upgrade
Theorem
4.37
.

Lemma 4.38

(Abe–Esnault)
.

Let
f:Y\to X
be a morphism of smooth connected
k
-schemes.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be a coefficient object on
X
. Suppose that for each positive integer
n
,
for
\mathcal{F}:=(\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{\otimes n}
, the map

H^{0}(X,\mathcal{F})\to H^{0}(Y,f^{*}\mathcal{F})
is an isomorphism.
Then the morphism
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})\to\overline{G}(f^{*}\mathcal{E})

(
defined with respect to a consistent choice of base points
\,)
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
AE19
, Proposition 1.9]
modulo a slight change of hypotheses; we sketch the argument to show that nothing is affected. Let
[\mathcal{E}]
be the Tannakian category of geometric coefficient objects generated by
\mathcal{E}
. By hypothesis,
f^{*}
restricts to a fully faithful functor on
[\mathcal{E}]
. This is not enough to prove the claim; however, a quick group-theoretic argument
[
AE19
, Lemma 1.7]
shows that this does hold provided that every
\mathcal{G}\in[\mathcal{E}]
of rank 1 is of finite order. By Remark
2.27
,
\mathcal{G}
promotes to a true coefficient object on
X_{n}

for some
n
; we may thus apply Lemma
2.4
to deduce the claim.
∎

Corollary 4.39
.

With notation as in Theorem
4.37
, the induced morphism
\overline{G}(\mathcal{E})\to\overline{G}(\mathcal{E}|_{C})

(
defined with respect to a consistent choice of base points
)
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.37
, for every constituent of

(\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{E}^{\vee})^{\otimes n}
as a coefficient object, the pullback to
C
is again an
irreducible coefficient object.
By Lemma
2.28
, the same is true for geometric coefficient objects.
We may thus apply Lemma
4.38
to conclude.
∎

On a related note, we mention the following result.

Corollary 4.40
.

For any positive integer
r
, any category of coefficient objects only contains finitely many isomorphism classes of irreducible tame coefficient objects of rank
r
on
X
up to twisting by constant objects of rank
1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.28
, it suffices to check the case of a category of étale coefficient objects.
By Lemma
2.3
, we may assume that
X
is affine.
By Corollary
4.39
and the aforementioned theorem of Poonen, we can find a curve
C
in
X

such that restriction of coefficient objects from
X
to
C
is fully faithful;
by Corollary
3.18
, we deduce the claim.
∎

Without the tameness hypothesis, one has the following result which reproduces
[
AE19
, Theorem 3.10]
.

Theorem 4.41
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an irreducible coefficient object on
X
.
There is a dense open subscheme
U
of
X
such that for any finite subset
S\subseteq U^{\circ}
,
there exist a curve
C
and a morphism
f:C\to X
over
k
such that
f
admits a section on

and
f^{*}\mathcal{E}
is irreducible.

Proof.

We may reduce to the étale case as in the proof of Theorem
4.37
;
then
[
Esn17
, Theorem 8.1]
gives the desired result.
(This amounts to applying Hilbert irreducibility in the form of
[

, Theorem 2.15]
.)
∎

4.8. On a conjecture of de Jong

The following conjecture is due to de Jong, as reported in

[
ES18
,
Shi14
]
where some special cases are established.

Conjecture 4.42

(de Jong)
.

Let
X
be a smooth, proper, geometrically connected variety over a
(
not necessarily finite
)
perfect field
k
of characteristic
p
with trivial geometric étale fundamental group.
Then every convergent isocrystal on
X
is constant.

Using étale companions, one obtains the following result in the direction of this conjecture.
(Compare
[
AE19
, Remark 4.5]
.)

Proposition 4.43
.

Suppose that
(k
is finite and
\,)

X
is proper over
k
and has trivial geometric étale fundamental group.
Then the functor

2\mbox{-}\!\!\varinjlim_{n\to\infty}\FIsoc^{\dagger}(\Spec k_{n})\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\to 2\mbox{-}\!\!\varinjlim_{n\to\infty}\FIsoc^{\dagger}(X_{n})\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}

is an equivalence of categories.

Proof.

We are free to enlarge
k
, so we may assume that
X
contains a
k
-rational point; we may also assume that
X
is irreducible.
Since
X
contains a
k
-rational point, the functor
\FIsoc^{\dagger}(\Spec k)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\to\FIsoc^{\dagger}(X)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
is fully faithful; moreover, it preserves extension groups.
Consequently, it remains to check that the essential image contains an arbitrary irreducible
\mathcal{E}\in\FIsoc^{\dagger}(X)\otimes\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, possibly after enlarging
k
.
At this point, there is no harm in twisting by a constant rank-1 object; by Lemma
2.4
,
we may thus reduce to the case where
\det(\mathcal{E})
is of finite order.
By Theorem
4.28
,
\mathcal{E}
arises as a crystalline companion
of some lisse Weil
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-sheaf. By hypothesis, the latter
must arise by pullback from
k
, as then must
\mathcal{E}
by Theorem
4.17
(b).
∎
