The Saddle-Point Accountant for Differential Privacy
Wael Alghamdi, Shahab Asoodeh, Flavio P. Calmon, Juan Felipe Gomez, Oliver Kosut, Lalitha Sankar, Fei Wei

Abstract
We introduce a new differential privacy (DP) accountant called the saddle-point accountant (SPA). SPA approximates privacy guarantees for the composition of DP mechanisms in an accurate and fast manner. Our approach is inspired by the saddle-point method—a ubiquitous numerical technique in statistics. We prove rigorous performance guarantees by deriving upper and lower bounds for the approximation error offered by SPA. The crux of SPA is a combination of large-deviation methods with central limit theorems, which we derive via exponentially tilting the privacy loss random variables corresponding to the DP mechanisms. One key advantage of SPA is that it runs in constant time for the n n -fold composition of a privacy mechanism. Numerical experiments demonstrate that SPA achieves comparable accuracy to state-of-the-art accounting methods with a faster runtime.

Introduction

Differential privacy (DP) is a widely adopted standard for privacy-preserving machine learning (ML). Differentially private mechanisms used in ML tasks typically operate in the

Privacy accounting is particularly important in the composition setting. In this setting, we can form a PLRV for the composed mechanism that splits additively. In other words,

To justify the use of the saddle-point method in approximating the privacy curve
\delta_{L^{(n)}}
, we show that
\delta_{L^{(n)}}
can be expressed in terms of an integral on the complex plane by means of the Fourier transform. We then provide two different approximations for this integral that involve the cumulant generating function (CGF)

K_{L}(t)\triangleq\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tL}\right]

(5)

of the PLRV corresponding to the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
. Both approximations require a judicious choice of the integration path in the complex plane: integration is done over the line parallel to the imaginary axis with real part corresponding to the
saddle-point
of the integrand. The saddle-point is given by the unique solution of

\boxed{nK^{\prime}_{L}(t)=\varepsilon+\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{t+1}.}

(6)

The first approximation is based on expanding the integrand around the saddle-point via Taylor expansion (see Section
3.3
). In Mathematical Physics, this corresponds to the well-known
method of steepest descent
(MSD)
[
22
]
. This approach leads to the following approximation:

\boxed{\delta_{L^{(n)}}(\varepsilon)\approx\delta_{L^{(n)},\,\textup{SP-MSD}}^{(0)}(\varepsilon)\triangleq\frac{e^{nK_{L}(t)-\varepsilon t}}{\sqrt{2\pi[nt^{2}(1+t)^{2}K^{\prime\prime}_{L}(t)+t^{2}+(1+t)^{2}]}},}

(7)

where
t
is the unique solution of (
6
). We also derive a more accurate approximation based on the same approach that includes additional terms, denoted
\delta^{(1)}_{L^{(n)},\text{SP-MSD}}(\varepsilon)
(see Sec.
3.3.1
for details).

The second approximation involves recentering the density of
L
by introducing its exponentially tilted version
\tilde{L}
and then applying CLT to
\tilde{L}
(see Section
3.3.2
). As discussed in Section
3
, tilting the distribution is equivalent to selecting the integration path in the complex plane. By choosing the tilting parameter
t
that satisfies (
6
), this approach leads to the following approximation:

\boxed{\delta_{L^{(n)}}(\varepsilon)\approx\delta_{L^{(n)},\,\textup{SP-CLT}}(\varepsilon)\triangleq e^{nK_{L}(t)-\varepsilon t}~\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-t(Z-\varepsilon)}\big(1-e^{-(Z-\varepsilon)}\big)^{+}\Big],}

(8)

where
Z
is a Gaussian random variable with mean
nK^{\prime}_{L}(t)
and variance
nK^{\prime\prime}_{L}(t)
. Notice that the expectation in (
8
) can be analytically computed.
While the first approximation can exhibit a better accuracy in the numerical experiments in Section
5
, the second one is more amenable toward the error analysis given in Section
4
.
Both approximations hold even if we consider composition of
n
different mechanisms
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
, except that in this case we replace
nK_{L}(t)
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{L_{i}}(t)
, where
L_{i}
is a PLRV corresponding to
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

For a random variable

The

Subsampling.

Subsampling is a fundamental tool in the analysis of differentially private mechanisms.
Informally speaking, subsampling entails applying a differentially private mechanism to a
small set of randomly sampled datapoints from a given dataset. There are several ways of formally defining
the subsampling operator, see, e.g.,
[
3
]

which indicates the probability of selecting a datapoint. More formally, the subsampled datapoints from a dataset
D

\{x\in D~:~B_{x}=1\}

B_{x}

\lambda

x\in D

\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
as the composition of
\mathcal{M}
and the Poisson subsampling operator.
Characterizing the privacy guarantees of subsampled mechanisms is the subject of “privacy amplification by subsampling” principle
[
26

. This principle is well-studied particularly for characterizing the privacy guarantees of subsampled Gaussian mechanisms in the context of a variant of differential privacy, namely, Rényi differential privacy
[
43

1

35

\varepsilon
and
\delta
for the subsampled Gaussian mechanisms. Recall that a Gaussian mechanism satisfies
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{N}(f(D),\sigma^{2}I_{d})

f
is a query function with
\ell_{2}

1

(20)

where
P=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
and
Q=(1-\lambda)P+\lambda P^{\prime}
, and
P^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\sigma^{2}I_{d})

is the first standard basis vector.
In the following lemma, we show that the above maximum is always attained by

3
Saddle-point Accountant Methodology

This section describes the foundations of our saddle-point accountants. We first introduce our key tool, namely, exponential tilting. As a warm-up, we use exponential tilting to rederive—in a new way—two elementary variations of the moments accountant
[
1
]
that render tighter privacy guarantees. We then discuss the connection between exponential tilting and the method of steepest descent, and describe in details how these two methods lead to our saddle-point accountants.

We assume that, for the purposes of bounding or approximating the optimal privacy curve
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}(\varepsilon)
for a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
, we have access to a PLRV
L
such that
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}(\varepsilon)\leq\delta_{L}(\varepsilon)
. In most cases, the relevant variable is
L^{(n)}=L_{1}+\cdots+L_{n}
, such that, as discussed above,
\delta_{L^{(n)}}
is the composition curve. However, in this section we derive approximations for any variable
L
. We assume that the distribution of
L
is known to an extent that expectations of functions of
L
—i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[f(L)]
—can be computed. We proceed to derive approximations and bounds on
\delta_{L}(\varepsilon)
based on such expectations.

3.1
Exponential Tilting

The moment generating function (MGF) for a PLRV

M_{L}(z)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[e^{zL}]

K_{L}(z)\triangleq\log M_{L}(z).

(23)

Let
\mathcal{C}

t

is an interval,

5

5

This can be seen by conditioning on the sign of

We may also evaluate

We may rewrite the induced privacy curve for

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-is\varepsilon}g(is)M_{L}(is)\ ds,

The fact that the integrals in (

Definition 7
.

The

with parameter

\tilde{L}
whose probability measure is given by

P_{\tilde{L}}(B):=\frac{1}{M_{L}(t)}\int_{B}e^{tu}\ dP_{L}(u)

If

A key feature of exponential tilting is that, for independent

\tilde{L}=\tilde{L}_{1}+\cdots+\tilde{L}_{n}

\tilde{L}_{i}

The expectation of any function of

\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{L})]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{tL}f(L)]}{M_{L}(t)}.

Thus, the MGF of the tilted variable

\displaystyle M_{\tilde{L}}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[e^{z\tilde{L}}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{tL}e^{zL}]}{M_{L}(t)}=\frac{M_{L}(t+z)}{M_{L}(t)}.

In addition,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-e^{-(L-\varepsilon)}\right)^{+}\right]

\displaystyle=M_{L}(t)\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(t+is)\varepsilon}M_{\tilde{L}}(is)g(t+is)ds

3.2
Elementary Improvements of the Original Moments Accountant Method

The goal of this section is to motivate the method of steepest descent as a more accurate refinement to the moments accountant. We do this by showing that the moments accountant method proposed in

Define

3.3
The Method of Steepest Descent and the Saddle-point Approximation

To derive the saddle-point approximation, we rewrite (
28
) once more as

\delta_{L}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{t-i\infty}^{t+i\infty}e^{F_{\varepsilon}(z)}dz

(48)

where we have defined the exponent as

F_{\varepsilon}(z)\triangleq K_{L}(z)-z\varepsilon-\log z-\log(1+z).

(49)

The key in the saddle-point method is the choice of
t
. The
saddle-point
is defined as the real value
t_{0}
for which
F^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})=0
. We assume that
\varepsilon<\esup L
. Let
K_{L}|_{\mathbb{R}}
be the restriction of the CGF to the real axis. Then,
K_{L}|_{\mathbb{R}}
is convex, and thus,
F_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathbb{R}}
is strictly convex. Thus, the minimum of
F_{\varepsilon}
over the reals is unique and satisfies
F^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})=0
, i.e.,

K_{L}^{\prime}(t_{0})=\varepsilon+\frac{1}{t_{0}}+\frac{1}{t_{0}+1}.

(50)

Observe that the original moments accountant aims to solve
K_{L}^{\prime}(t)=\varepsilon
without the last two terms. The use of the term
saddle-point
can be justified by writing the second-order Taylor expansion of
F_{\varepsilon}(z)
around
t_{0}
:

F_{\varepsilon}(z)\approx F_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})+\frac{(z-t_{0})^{2}}{2}F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0}).

(51)

Since
F_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathbb{R}}
is strictly convex,
F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})>0
, so along the real line,
F_{\varepsilon}(z)
has a minimum at
z=t_{0}
, whereas parallel to the imaginary axis,
F_{\varepsilon}(z)
has a maximum at
z=t_{0}
(because in this case the coefficient
(z-t_{0})^{2}
becomes negative).

From this point, two closely related paths can be taken to approximate (
48
). The first approach is a direct application of the method of steepest descent, where
F_{\varepsilon}
is expanded around the saddle-point
t_{0}
. The second approach expands
only

K_{L}
around
t_{0}
, which is equivalent to deriving the Edgeworth series expansion of the distribution of
\tilde{L}
with tilting
t_{0}
. We outline both approaches here, but note that the second path (i.e., the expansion of
K_{L}
) is more amenable towards an approximation error analysis (see Section
4
), while the first path leads to better approximations in our numerical experiments.

3.3.1
Method of Steepest Descent

For a fixed
\varepsilon

\displaystyle\delta_{L}(\varepsilon)=\frac{e^{F_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-s^{2}F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})/2}\left(1+\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{(is)^{k}}{k!}B_{k}(0,0,F_{\varepsilon}^{(3)}(t_{0}),\ldots,F_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}(t_{0}))\right)ds

(57)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{F_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})}}{2\pi}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})}}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}\sqrt{2\pi}}{2^{m}m!F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})^{m+1/2}}B_{2m}(0,0,F_{\varepsilon}^{(3)}(t_{0}),\ldots,F_{\varepsilon}^{(2m)}(t_{0}))\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{F_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})}}{\sqrt{2\pi F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})}}\left(1+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{m}m!F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})^{m}}B_{2m}(0,0,F_{\varepsilon}^{(3)}(t_{0}),\ldots,F_{\varepsilon}^{(2m)}(t_{0}))\right).

(59)

\delta_{L}(\varepsilon)\approx\delta_{L,\,\text{SP-MSD}}^{(0)}(\varepsilon)\triangleq\frac{e^{F_{\varepsilon}(t_{0})}}{\sqrt{2\pi F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})}}=\frac{e^{K_{L}(t_{0})-\varepsilon t_{0}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{t_{0}(t_{0}+1)K_{L}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})+t_{0}^{2}+(1+t_{0})^{2}}}.

Conclusion

We have introduced the saddle-point accountant (SPA) for DP. Via exponentially tilting the PLRV—and choosing the tilt in accordance with the saddle-point method from statistics—SPA combines the desirable behaviors of both large-deviation methods and central limit theorem based approaches. Two consequences follow: SPA outperforms both of the aforementioned methods, and it maintains the independence of the runtime in the setting of
n
-fold composition. We have demonstrated through numerical experiments that SPA shows comparable performance to state-of-the-art DP accountants.

t=d/\sigma_{L}
, so
d
is a variable over
(0,\infty)
. The saddle-point equation can be rewritten as

\left(\rho_{2}(t)-\frac{b}{\sigma_{L}}\right)d^{2}-\left(b+\frac{2}{\sigma_{L}}\right)d-\left(1-\frac{\rho_{2}(t)d^{3}}{\sigma_{L}}\right)=0.

(141)

We rewrite the saddle-point equation in this “quadratic” form since it closely approximates the quadratic
d^{2}-bd-1=0
at the saddle-point. Indeed, let
d_{0}>0
be such that
t_{0}=d_{0}/\sigma_{L}
. We obtain from (
141
) the inequality
\frac{1}{2}d_{0}^{2}-(b+1)d_{0}-1\leq 0
for all large
n
. This latter inequality yields that

d_{0}\leq b+1+\sqrt{(b+1)^{2}+2}=o(n^{1/6}).

(142)

Hence,
\rho_{2}(t_{0})d_{0}^{3}/\sigma_{L}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, i.e., the “constant” term in (
141
) approaches
1
. Thus, for all large
n
, completing the square in (
141
) yields

d_{0}=\frac{b+\frac{2}{\sigma_{L}}+\sqrt{\left(b+\frac{2}{\sigma_{L}}\right)^{2}+4\left(1-\frac{\rho_{2}(t_{0})d_{0}^{3}}{\sigma_{L}}\right)\left(\rho_{2}(t_{0})-\frac{b}{\sigma_{L}}\right)}}{2\left(\rho_{2}(t_{0})-\frac{b}{\sigma_{L}}\right)}.

(143)

Taking
n\to\infty
, we obtain

d_{0}\sim\frac{b+\sqrt{b^{2}+4}}{2},

(144)

which gives the desired asymptotic formula for the saddle-point
t_{0}=d_{0}/\sigma_{L}
.
