Gohberg Lemma and Spectral Results for Pseudodifferential Operators on Locally Compact Abelian Groups
Néstor Jara, Marius Măntoiu †

Abstract
We provide a new type of proof for known or new Gohberg lemmas for pseudodifferential operators on Abelian locally compact groups 𝖷 {\sf X} . We use C ∗ C^{*} -algebraic techniques, which also give spectral results to which the Gohberg lemma is just a corollary. These results extend most of those appearing in the literature in various directions. In particular, compactness or a a Lie structure are not needed. The ideal of all the compact operators in L 2 ​ ( 𝖷 ) {\rm L}^{2}({\sf X}) is replaced by all the ideals having a crossed product structure, which is a consistent generalization. We also indicate several new examples, mostly connected to specific behaviors of functions on the dual Ξ \Xi of 𝖷 {\sf X} .

Roughly, a Gohberg-type lemma (called

-lemma

{\sf Op}(f)
, defined by a symbol

and acting in a Hilbert space

\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{H})
of compact operators. The theory started long ago
[

,

,

,

,

]
, but recently it resurfaced as a consequence of certain progress in Harmonic Analysis
[

,

,

,

,

]
. The new background is that of global pseudodifferential operators for groups that might be different from

, as treated in
[
13
,
14
]
(see also the references therein). At the beginning this concerned singular integral operators, but soon it was extended to pseudodifferential operators. The results mostly involved groups
{\sf X}
, with
\mathscr{H}={\rm L}^{2}({\sf X})
,
[
15
]
being a notable exception. Part of the interest of
G
-lemmas is the fact that the mentioned lower bound, although referring to a highly non-commutative situation, is expressed directly on values taken by the symbol, more precisely on its asymptotic behavior at infinity in a certain sense.

This article contributes to a different and hopefully interesting point of view upon
G
-lemmas. The approach is through
C^{*}
-algebras and the basic tool is the simple observation that spectra of operators or of elements in
C^{*}
-algebras are invariant by applying monomorphisms. Analytical arguments are not needed. The
G
-lemma will then be a consequence and in fact one will get an equality instead of a lower bound, which is an improvement.

Reaching a situation in which such a principle can be applied needs studying the connection between pseudodifferential operators on Abelian locally compact groups (in our case) and
C^{*}
-algebras. Those appearing here are
crossed products
involving
the action

\Theta
of the dual group
\Xi:=\widehat{\sf X}
on Abelian algebras of functions defined on
\Xi
. These are basically composed of complex functions on
\Xi\!\times\!\Xi
, but a partial Fourier transformation in the first variable converts them in functions
f:{\sf X}\!\times\!\Xi\to\mathbb{C}
; we show that the pseudodifferential calculus
{\sf Op}
can be applied on such “symbols”. A priori, this does not make use of Hörmander-type conditions, which are actually not defined in many cases. When they are available, as a rule, the conditions emerging from the algebraic framework are more general and do not make use of an infinity of conditions on derivatives. See however Remark
5.3
, making the comparison with Grushin’s treatment of the case
{\sf X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The formalism allows to deal with a large class of algebras, coding the behavior of the symbol at infinity, as well as ideals which may be different from
\mathbf{K}\big[{\rm L}^{2}({\sf X})\big]
(this one will be labeled as

). One obstacle in finding the right arguments is the fact that the mentioned isomorphisms only takes place in suitable quotients of
C^{*}
-algebras, which are not naturally represented in
{\rm L}^{2}({\sf X})
or in some related Hilbert space.

2
Framework and conventions

We start with some notations and conventions that will be used all over the paper. If
\mathscr{H}
is a complex Hilbert space,
\mathbf{B}(\mathscr{H})
will denote the
C^{*}
-algebra of all bounded linear operators acting in
\mathscr{H}
. The ideal of compact operators will be denoted by
\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{H})
. Our locally compact spaces will always be Hausdorff. For such a space
Z
we set
{\rm BC}(Z)
for the space of all bounded and continuous complex functions defined on
Z
. Those which are uniformly continuous (whenever this makes sense) are elements of
{\rm BC_{u}}(Z)
and those which tend to zero at infinity (outside arbitrarily large compact sets) are elements of
{\rm C}_{0}(Z)
. One also encounters
{\rm C}_{0}(Z,\mathfrak{Y})
, where
\big(\mathfrak{Y},\star,^{\star},\parallel\!\cdot\!\parallel_{\mathfrak{Y}}\!\big)
is a
C^{*}
-algebra, with norm

\parallel\!f\!\parallel_{{\rm C}_{0}(Z,\mathfrak{Y})}\,:=\sup_{z\in Z}\parallel\!f(z)\!\parallel_{\mathfrak{Y}}\,,

involution
f^{*}(\cdot):=f(\cdot)^{\star}
and product
(fg)(\cdot):=f(\cdot)\star g(\cdot)
.

By
ideal
in a
C^{*}
-algebra we mean a two-sided closed ideal which is stable under the involution.
Morphisms
between
C^{*}
-algebras (in particular Hilbert space representations) are assumed by definition to intertwine the involutions. For two
C^{*}
-algebras
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
we write
\mathfrak{A}_{1}\!\ll\mathfrak{A}_{2}
if
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{1}\!\lll\mathfrak{A}_{2}
if
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
(with the convention above). With previous notations one has

\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{H})\lll\mathbf{B}(\mathscr{H})\,,\quad{\rm BC_{u}}(Z)\ll{\rm BC}(Z)\,,\quad{\rm C}_{0}(Z)\lll{\rm BC}(Z)\,.

Let
{\sf X}
be a locally compact Abelian group, with (Abelian) Pontryagin dual
\widehat{\sf X}\equiv\Xi
. Both group laws will be denoted multiplicatively. To make things interesting, we will assume that
\Xi
is
not compact
, i. e.
{\sf X}
is not discrete. However, note that
{\sf X}
is allowed to be compact.

On
{\sf X}
and
\Xi
we consider correlated Haar measures
d{\sf m}(x)\equiv dx
and
d\widehat{\sf m}(\xi)\equiv d\xi
. The Fourier transformation
\mathcal{F}:{\rm L}^{1}({\sf X})\to{\rm C}_{0}(\Xi)
can also be defined as a unitary operator
\mathcal{F}:{\rm L}^{2}({\sf X})\to{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)
and it is essentially given by

(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(\xi):=\int_{\sf X}\overline{\xi(x)}\varphi(x)dx\,,

with inverse

\big(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\psi\big)(x):=\int_{\Xi}\xi(x)\psi(\xi)d\xi\,.

The Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma and the fact that
{\sf X}
is isomorphic to the dual of
\Xi
(by the Pontryagin-van Kampen Theorem) also insures the interpretation
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!:{\rm L}^{1}(\Xi)\to{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})
(linear contraction). Actually it extends to an isomorphism between
{\sf C}^{*}(\Xi)
, the group
C^{*}
-algebra of
\Xi
, and
{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})
.

We denote by
\theta:\Xi\to{\rm Homeo}(\beta\Xi)
the canonical extension to
\beta\Xi
, the Stone-Cec̆h compactification of
\Xi
, of the action of
\Xi
on itself by translations:

\theta_{\eta}(\xi):=\xi\eta\,,\quad\forall\,\xi,\eta\in\Xi\,,

which lifts to an action on
{\rm BC}(\Xi)
by

\big[\Theta_{\eta}(\psi)\big](\xi):=\psi\big[\theta_{\eta}(\xi)\big]=\psi(\xi\eta)\,,\quad\forall\,\xi,\eta\in\Xi\,.

This one is pointwise continuous in
\eta
only on uniformly continuous functions.

3
Abelian ideals and
C^{*}
-algebras

In this section we define suitable pairs of
C^{*}
-algebras of bounded uniformly continuous functions on
\Xi
, one of them being an ideal of the other. They are invariant under translations and their quotient has interesting dynamical properties.

Definition 3.1
.

The
C^{*}
-subalgebra
\mathrm{J}(\Xi)
of
\,\mathrm{BC_{u}}(\Xi)
is called
admissible
if (a) it contains
\mathrm{C}_{0}(\Xi)
and (b) it is invariant under translations: if
\psi\in\mathrm{J}(\Xi)
and
\eta\in\Xi
then
\Theta_{\eta}(\psi)\in\mathrm{J}(\Xi)
.

Definition 3.2
.

An admissible
C^{*}
-subalgebra
\mathrm{J}(\Xi)
being given, we define

\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}(\Xi):=\big\{\psi\in\mathrm{BC_{u}}(\Xi)\,\big|\,\Theta_{\zeta}(\psi)-\psi\in\mathrm{J}(\Xi)\,,\ \forall\,\zeta\in\Xi\big\}\,.

When
\Xi
is understood or irrelevant, we just write
\mathrm{J}
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
respectively. It is easy to show that
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra of uniformly continuous functions. It is also invariant under translations: for every
\psi\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
and every
\eta\in\Xi
one has
\Theta_{\eta}(\psi)\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
. We have

\mathrm{J}_{\rm st}:=\mathrm{C}_{0}\lll\mathrm{J}\lll\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\gg\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{\rm st}\ggg\mathrm{C}_{0}\,,

so both
\mathrm{C}_{0}
and
\mathrm{J}
are ideals in
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
. The label
{\rm st}
refers to what will be called subsequently
the standard case
, since it is the
C^{*}
-subalgebra (and ideal) usually considered for Gohberg lemmas
[
4
]
. Even in non-standard cases, as in
[
9
]
, the spaces of symbols are usually ideals of
\mathrm{BC_{u}}
, but for our construction a
C^{*}
-subalgebra is enough. Note, however, that if
\mathrm{J}
is unital, then
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}=\mathrm{J}
and the construction becomes trivial. Because of this, we restrict to the case where
\mathrm{J}
is non-unital, thus a proper ideal of
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
. A particular case appears when
\mathrm{J}
is a (proper) ideal of
\mathrm{BC_{u}}
.

The Gelfand spectrum
\widetilde{\Sigma}
of
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
is a regular compactification of
\Xi
(i. e.​ we can see
\Xi
as an open dense subset of
\widetilde{\Sigma}\,
) . A neighborhood base of
\sigma\in\widetilde{\Sigma}
is given by

\left\{W(\sigma|\epsilon;\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m})\,\,\big|\,\,\epsilon>0\,,\,\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\,,\,m\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

(3.1)

where

W(\sigma|\epsilon;\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}):=\left\{\nu\in\widetilde{\Sigma}\,\big|\,\,|\nu(\varphi_{j})-\sigma(\varphi_{j})|<\epsilon\,,\,\forall\,j=1,\dots,m\right\}.

(3.2)

Since
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
is invariant, the action of
\Xi
on itself by left translations extends to a continuous action on
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, also denoted by
\theta
. Note that
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is a quotient of
\beta\Xi
and this action on
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is compatible with the previous
\theta
defined on
\beta\Xi
through the canonical surjection. The Gelfand isomorphism
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\cong\mathrm{C}\big(\widetilde{\Sigma}\big)
consists here in associating to any function belonging to
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
its unique continuous extension to
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, denoted by
\widetilde{\psi}
.

The locally compact space
\Sigma
, the Gelfand spectrum of
\mathrm{J}
, will not be needed explicitly. Note, however, that by the relation
{\rm C}_{0}\lll\mathrm{J}
, one can identify
\Xi
with one of its open subsets; we may thus write

\Xi\subset\Sigma\subset\widetilde{\Sigma}\,.

Let us denote by
\Sigma^{\bullet}\!:=\widetilde{\Sigma}\!\setminus\!\Sigma
the corresponding
corona set
, which is compact since
\Sigma
is open
in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. It is (homeomorphic to) the Gelfand spectrum of the quotient
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathrm{J}^{\bullet}\!:=\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}/\mathrm{J}
. We recall that
\mathrm{J}^{\bullet}
can be seen as the set of characters of
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
which are trivial on the ideal
\mathrm{J}
. Extending terminology used for
\mathrm{J}=\mathrm{C}_{0}

[
11
]
, we could call
\widetilde{\Sigma}

the
\mathrm{J}
-Higson compactification
and
\Sigma^{\bullet}

the
\mathrm{J}
-Higson corona
. When
\mathrm{J}\neq\mathrm{C}_{0}
, there is an extra compact space
\widetilde{\Sigma}\!\setminus\!\Xi
containing strictly
{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
.

We are interested now in the dynamical properties of this quotient
{\mathrm{J}}^{\bullet}\!=\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}/\mathrm{J}\cong\mathrm{C}\big({\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)
.
If
\zeta\in\Xi
and
\sigma\in{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
, for every
\psi\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
one has

\big(\theta_{\zeta}(\sigma)-\sigma\big)(\psi)=\sigma\big(\Theta_{\zeta}(\psi)-\psi\big)=0\,,

since
\Theta_{\zeta}(\psi)-\psi\in\mathrm{J}
. This shows that

\theta_{\zeta}(\sigma)=\sigma\,,\quad\forall\,\sigma\in\Sigma^{\bullet},\,\zeta\in\Xi\,.

This will be crucial for our arguments, so we make a formal statement:

Lemma 3.3
.

The elements of
\,{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
are all fixed points under the action
\theta
.

4
Algebras of pseudifferential operators on Abelian compact groups

Relying on the couple
\big(\,\widetilde{\mathrm{J}},\mathrm{J}\big)
, we are going to introduce
C^{*}
-algebras of symbols and connect them to the
{\sf Op}
-calculus via Hilbert space representations. We will make use below, sometimes implicitly, of the known monomorphisms (each time, the first two are isomorphisms)

{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\!\otimes\!\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\cong{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\!\otimes\!{\rm C}\big(\,\widetilde{\Sigma}\,\big)\cong{\rm C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\,\big)\,\hookrightarrow{\rm C}({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma})\,,

{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\!\otimes\!\mathrm{J}^{\bullet}\cong{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\!\otimes\!{\rm C}\big(\Sigma^{\bullet}\big)\cong{\rm C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\Sigma^{\bullet}\big)\,\hookrightarrow{\rm C}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma^{\bullet}}\big)\,,

(4.1)

where
{\sf X}_{\infty}
denotes the Alexandrov compactification of
{\sf X}
if it is not compact and
{\sf X}
if it is.

With the action
\Theta
one can form the crossed product
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}:=\Xi\!\ltimes\!\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
. It is the enveloping
C^{*}
-algebra of the Banach
∗
-algebra
{\rm L}^{1}\big(\Xi;\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\,\big)
(isomorphic as a Banach space to the projective tensor product
{\rm L}^{1}\big(\Xi)\overline{\otimes}\,\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}

)
, with the obvious norm, the composition law

(\Phi\diamond\Psi)(\xi):=\int_{\Xi}\Theta_{\eta}\big[\Phi(\xi\eta^{-1})\big]\Psi(\eta)d\eta

and the involution

\Psi^{\diamond}(\xi):=\Theta_{\xi}\big[\Psi(\xi^{-1})^{*}\big]\,.

Using notations as
\big[\Psi(\xi)\big](\zeta)\equiv\Psi(\xi,\zeta)
, the algebraic structure of
{\rm L}^{1}\big(\Xi;\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\,\big)
can be reformulated as

(\Phi\diamond\Psi)(\xi,\zeta):=\int_{\Xi}\Phi\big(\xi\eta^{-1}\!,\zeta\eta\big)\Psi(\eta,\zeta)d\eta\,,

\Psi^{\diamond}(\xi,\zeta):=\overline{\Psi\big(\xi^{-1}\!,\zeta\xi\big)}\,.

With the invariant ideal
\mathrm{J}
one can make the same crossed product construction, leading to the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathfrak{J}\!:=\Xi\!\ltimes\!\mathrm{J}
. In the following we will use the abbreviations
\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}:={\rm L}^{1}\big(\Xi;\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\,\big)
and
\mathfrak{L}:={\rm L}^{1}\big(\Xi;\mathrm{J}\big)
. It is known that the obvious inclusion
\mathfrak{L}\subset\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}
extends to a monomorphism between the corresponding crossed products, the first one being an ideal of the second, thus finally
\mathfrak{J}\lll\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
.

There is a natural (Schrödinger) representation of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
in the Hilbert space
{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)
given for
\Psi\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}
by

\big[{\sf Sch}(\Psi)w\big](\xi):=\int_{\Xi}\Psi\big(\xi\eta^{-1}\!,\eta\big)w(\eta)d\eta\,,\hskip 11.38092pt\forall\,w\in{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)\,,\,\xi\in\Xi\,.

It is clear that
{\sf Sch}
also transforms isomorphically the Hilbert space
{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi\!\times\!\Xi)
into the Hilbert space
\mathbf{B}^{2}\big[{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)\big]
of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators in
{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)
.

Most elements of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
are functions (of a certain type) defined on
\Xi\!\times\!\Xi
. But the symbols of pseudodifferential operators are naturally defined on
{\sf X}\!\times\!\Xi
. We will turn to this situation using a partial Fourier transformation in the first variable: for suitable
g:{\sf X}\!\times\!\Xi\to\mathbb{C}
, we consider
\mathbb{F}_{(1)}g:\Xi\!\times\!\Xi\to\mathbb{C}
by

\big(\mathbb{F}_{(1)}g\big)(\eta,\xi):=\int_{\sf X}\overline{\eta(x)}g(x,\xi)dx\,,

which in some sense is the tensor product between
\mathcal{F}
and
{\sf id}
. Our construction is described basically by

\mathfrak{B}:=\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\mathfrak{J}\,,\quad\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}:=\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}\,.

What actually happens is that
\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}
maps linearly, contractively and densely (but not multiplicatively)
\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}
into
{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\!\otimes\!\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\cong{\rm C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\,\big)
(an easy extension of the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma for Abelian locally compact groups). It also extends to the full crossed product
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
: On the
{\rm L}^{1}
-level, one pushes forward the crossed product algebraic structure to the dense image of
\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}
, getting a (non-commutative) Banach
∗
-algebra. This structure is not needed in an explicit form (the involution is simply complex conjugation while the product is already a certain type of symbol composition). Then
\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}
is the enveloping
C^{*}
-algebra and the inverse partial Fourier transformation extends to an isomorphism of
C^{*}
-algebras. The same arguments leads to
\mathfrak{B}
and one gets
\mathfrak{B}\lll\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}
. For subsequent use, we mention below part of the conclusion:

\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}\subset\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}\cap{\rm C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\,\big)\,.

(4.2)

The basic fact is that

(and other similar Fourier transformed crossed products) in the Hilbert space

5
A spectral result and a G-lemma

To formulate our main results, one must introduce operator versions of the previously defined
C^{*}
-algebras. We set

\mathbf{B}\!:={\sf Op}\big(\mathfrak{B})\lll\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}:={\sf Op}\big(\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}\big)\ll\mathbf{B}(\mathscr{H})\,.

(5.1)

The pair formed of the
C^{*}
-algebra
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
and its ideal
\mathbf{B}
will play a central role, as well as the quotient
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}\!:=\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}/\mathbf{B}
. We set
{\sf Op}^{\bullet}(f)\!:={\sf Op}(f)+\mathbf{B}
for the image of
{\sf Op}(f)
in the quotient. One denotes by
{\rm sp}_{\mathfrak{A}}(T)
the
spectrum
of an element
T
in a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
and by
\rho_{\mathfrak{A}}(T)
its
spectral radius
.

For
f\in\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}
, which is a function with domain
{\sf X}\!\times\!\Xi
, there is a unique continuous extension to
{\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}
(see also (
4.2
)). We call this extension
\tilde{f}
and write
f^{\bullet}
for its restriction to
{\sf X}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
. Moreover, for such
f
, there is a unique continuous extension to
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\Xi
(see (
4.1
)), where
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!:={\sf X}\cup\{x_{\infty}\}
denotes the Alexandrov compactification of
{\sf X}
if it is not compact and
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!:={\sf X}
otherwise. We call this extension
f_{\infty}
; it satisfies
f_{\infty}(x_{\infty},\xi)=0
for every
\xi\in\Xi
. Then one analogously defines
\widetilde{f}_{\infty}
on
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}
and
f^{\bullet}_{\infty}
on
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\Sigma^{\bullet}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}\subset\mathrm{C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\big)
. Then

{\rm sp}_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\bullet}}\big({\sf Op}^{\bullet}(f)\big)=f^{\bullet}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)\,.

(5.2)

Proof.

Step 1.
It follows from (
5.1
) that the asignation

f+\mathfrak{B}\mapsto{\sf Op}^{\bullet}(f)={\sf Op}(f)+\mathbf{B}

provides the isomorphism
{\mathfrak{B}}^{\bullet}\!=\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}/\mathfrak{B}\cong\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}/\mathbf{B}={\mathbf{B}}^{\bullet}
. Thus

{\rm sp}_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\bullet}}\!\big({{\sf Op}^{\bullet}}(f)\big)={\rm sp}_{{\mathfrak{B}}^{\bullet}}\!\big(f+\mathfrak{B}\big)\,,

(5.3)

so one has to show that the two r. h. s. of (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) agree.

Step 2.
As a consequence of the above preparations and of some general facts that will be explained, one has the sequence of
C^{*}
-morphisms

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}\!\cong\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}/\mathfrak{B}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\cong}\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}/\mathfrak{J}\overset{(b)}{\cong}\Xi\!\ltimes\!\big(\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}/\mathrm{J}\big)\overset{(c)}{\cong}\Xi\!\ltimes\!\mathrm{C}\big({\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{\cong}{\sf C}^{*}(\Xi)\!\otimes\!{\rm C}\big({\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)\overset{(e)}{\cong}{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\!\otimes\!{\rm C}\big({\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(f)}{\cong}{\rm C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\Sigma^{\bullet}\big)\overset{(g)}{\hookrightarrow}{\rm C}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\Sigma^{\bullet}\big)\,,

which is the core of the proof. At any step
(y)
we set
\Gamma_{y}
for the corresponding morphism; all except
\Gamma_{g}
are in fact isomorphisms. Remarkably, the quotient
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}
turns out to be Abelian.

First,
\Gamma_{a}
is obtained by passing to the quotient the isomorphism
\mathbb{F}_{(1)}:\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}\to\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
, which also maps
\mathfrak{B}
onto
\mathfrak{J}
. So

\Gamma_{a}\big(f+\mathfrak{B}\big):=\mathbb{F}_{(1)}f+\mathfrak{J}\,.

The isomorphism
\Gamma_{b}:\Xi\!\ltimes\!\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}/\Xi\!\ltimes\!\mathrm{J}\!\to\Xi\!\ltimes\!\big(\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}/\mathrm{J}\big)
is a basic known fact in the theory of crossed products; these ones provide exact functors (see
[
19
, Proposition 3.19]
): The action
\Theta
on
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
restricts to an action on the invariant ideal
\mathrm{J}
and this defines an obvious action on the quotient. There are corresponding crossed products (in which we do not indicate the action at the level of notations). If
\Phi
belongs to the dense subspace
{\rm C}_{\rm comp}\big(\Xi,\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\,\big)
, one has

\big[\Gamma_{b}\big(\Phi+\mathfrak{J}\big)\big](\xi)=\Phi(\xi)+\mathrm{J}\,,\quad\forall\,\xi\in\Xi\,.

The isomorphism
\Gamma_{c}
arises from the isomorphism
\mathscr{G}:\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}/\mathrm{J}\to{\rm C}(\Sigma^{{\bullet}})\,,\ \mathscr{G}(\psi+\mathrm{J})=\psi^{\bullet}
, which is a matter of Gelfand theory. Then, this isomorphism lifts to an isomorphism on the crossed products (see
[
19
, Lemma 2.65]
). Explicitly

\big[\Gamma_{c}(\Psi)\big](\xi):=\mathscr{G}\big[\Psi(\xi)\big]\,.

Thus, until now, for every
\xi\in\Xi
and
\sigma\in{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
we get

\big[\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{b}\Gamma_{a}(f+\mathfrak{B})\big](\xi,\sigma)=\big[(\mathbb{F}_{(1)}f)(\xi)\big]^{\bullet}(\sigma)\equiv\big(\mathbb{F}_{(1)}f\big)^{\bullet}(\xi,\sigma)\,.

(5.5)

The critical point is the fourth isomorphism: Based on the fact that the action of
\Xi
on
\Sigma^{\bullet}
(and thus on
{\rm C}\big({\Sigma}^{\bullet}\,\big)
) is trivial (see Lemma
3.3
), it follows by
[
19
, Lemma 2.73]
that the corresponding crossed product is isomorphic to the tensor product of
{\rm C}\big({\Sigma}^{\bullet}\,\big)
with the group
C^{*}
-algebra
{\sf C}^{*}(\Xi)
.

The fifth isomorphism follows immediately from the fact that
{\sf C}^{*}(\Xi)
is isomorphic to
{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})
via an extension of the inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!:{\rm L}^{1}(\Xi)\to{\rm C}_{0}({\sf X})\equiv{\rm C}_{0}\big(\widehat{\Xi}\big)
, as indicated in
[
19
, Proposition 3.1]
. This extends then to the tensor products.

The final morphisms
\Gamma_{f}
and
\Gamma_{g}
are a standard facts in the theory of Abelian
C^{*}
-algebras; see (
4.1
). One gets

\big[\Gamma_{g}\Gamma_{f}\Gamma_{e}\Gamma_{d}(\Phi)\big](x,\sigma)=\big(\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\Phi\big)^{\bullet}_{\infty}\,(x,\sigma)\,,\quad\forall\,x\in{\sf X}_{\infty}\,,\,\sigma\in\Sigma^{\bullet}.

(5.6)

Let us set
\Gamma\!:=\Gamma_{g}\Gamma_{f}\Gamma_{e}\Gamma_{d}\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{b}\Gamma_{a}
. Combining (
5.5
) with (
5.6
), in which the extension-restriction indicated by
\,{}^{\bullet}\,
only refers to the second variable, we obtain finally

\left[\Gamma\!\left(f+\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{G}}\right)\right](x,\sigma)=f^{\bullet}_{\infty}(x,\sigma)\,,\hskip 11.38092pt\forall\,(x,\sigma)\in{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}.

Step 3.
Note that the spectrum of
f^{\bullet}_{\infty}
in
{\rm C}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\Sigma^{\bullet}\big)
is simply its image, since it has a compact domain. Now, by applying Steps 1 and 2 and the obvious fact that spectra are invariant under injective
C^{*}
-morphisms, we finish the proof of the theorem.
∎

We are going now to rephrase (
5.2
) in terms of values taken by
f
only on the phase space
{\sf X}\!\times\!\Xi
. For any base of neighborhoods
\mathcal{B}
of
\Sigma^{\bullet}
in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
we set
\mathcal{B}|_{\Xi}\!:=\{B\cap\Xi\!\mid\!B\in\mathcal{B}\}
(by the density of
\Xi
in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, each such “trace”

is non-void). Examples are
\mathcal{B}\equiv\mathcal{V}
, the family of all the neighborhoods,
\mathcal{B}\equiv\mathcal{N}
, the family of all the closed (thus compact) neighborhoods, or
\mathcal{B}\equiv\mathcal{W}
, the family of the neighborhoods of
\Sigma^{\bullet}
deduced from the collection (

is a neighborhood of a set if and only if it is a union of neighborhoods of all of its points).

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}\subset\mathrm{C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\big)
and let
\mathcal{B}
be a base of neighborhoods of
\,\Sigma^{\bullet}
in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. Then

\displaystyle{\rm sp}_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\bullet}}\big({\sf Op}^{\bullet}(f)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{B}|_{\Xi}}\overline{f\big({\sf X}\!\times\!A\big)}\,

(5.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,\big|\,\forall\,\epsilon>0\,,A\in\mathcal{B}|_{\Xi}\,,\,\exists\,(x,\xi)\in{\sf X}\!\times\!A:|f(x,\xi)-\lambda|<\epsilon\big\}\,.

Proof.

The second equality follows by a direct use of basic definitions. By Theorem
5.1
, we are left with showing that

f^{\bullet}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{B}_{\Xi}}\overline{f\big({\sf X}\!\times\!A\big)}\,.

It is also easy to see that if this is proven for one base
\mathcal{B}
, it will also hold for any other one. So we will work with
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{N}
.

Let
N\in\mathcal{N}
; then
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!N
is the closure of
{\sf X}\!\times\!(N\cap\Xi)
in the compact space
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}
. The continuous function
\widetilde{f}_{\infty}:{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{C}
is also closed, so it commutes with closures. So we only need to show that

f^{\bullet}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)=\bigcap_{N\in\mathcal{N}}\widetilde{f}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!N\big)\,.

For each
(x,\sigma)\in{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
and
\lambda\neq f(x,\sigma)
, there is a compact neighborhood
V
of
f(x,\sigma)
with
\lambda\not\in V
. Then
M\!:=f^{-1}(V)
is a compact neighborhood of the point
(x,\sigma)
and this one is not mapped into
\lambda
. Since
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
is compact, finitely many of such
M
can cover it, forming a compact neighborhood of
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}
which is not mapped into
\lambda
, thus the second equivalence below follows:

\displaystyle\lambda\in f^{\bullet}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)=\widetilde{f}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!{\Sigma}^{\bullet}\big)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\lambda=\widetilde{f}_{\infty}(x,\sigma)\,,\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for some }(x,\sigma)\in{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\Sigma^{\bullet}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\lambda\in\widetilde{f}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!N\big)\,,\hskip 5.69046pt\forall\,N\in\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\lambda\in\bigcap_{N\in\mathcal{N}}\widetilde{f}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!N\big)\,,

hence we finish the proof of the Corollary.
∎

The next Corollary is a version of Gohberg’s Lemma for our case; note however that it consists of equalities, while traditionally one only gets lower bounds for the norm.
Given a filter base

\displaystyle\limsup_{\mathcal{C}}\varphi:

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\big|\,\,\forall\,C\in\mathcal{C}\,,\ \exists\,z\in C\ {\rm such\ that}\ \varphi(z)>\mu\right\}

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\max\bigcap_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\overline{\varphi(C)}\,.

Note that for
\mathcal{S}\!:=\{K^{c}\,|\,K\text{ relatively compact}\}
on a locally compact space (referred to as the

), one obtains
the usual definition of the upper limit.

Apart from the neighborhood base
\mathcal{B}
, we need the following family of subsets of

Hence, from Theorem

6
The standard case

We give now more details concerning the ideal
\mathrm{C}_{0}(\Xi)
, which is the one usually considered.
For this smallest admissible
C^{*}
-subalgebra
{\rm C}_{0}(\Xi)=:\mathrm{J}_{\rm st}
(which is also an ideal of
\mathrm{BC_{u}}
), one refers to
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{\rm st}\equiv{\rm VO}(\Xi)
as to the
C^{*}
-algebra of
vanishing oscillation
(or
slowly oscillating
) functions on
\Xi
. Note that in this standard case one has simply
\Sigma_{\rm st}^{\bullet}\!=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\rm st}\!\setminus\!\Xi
. Set
\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}_{\rm st}={\rm L^{1}}(\Xi;\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{\rm st})
; at the level of crossed products it follows that
{\sf Sch}:\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}_{\rm st}\to\mathbf{B}[{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)]
maps the ideal
\mathfrak{J}_{\rm st}=\Xi\!\ltimes\!{\rm C}_{0}
onto the ideal
\mathbf{K}[{\rm L}^{2}(\Xi)]
of compact operators. From this fact and diagram (
4.3
), it is immediate that
{\sf Op}(\mathfrak{B}_{\rm st})=:\mathbf{B}_{\rm st}=\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{H})
. As
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\rm st}\ll\mathbf{B}(\mathscr{H})
, we conclude that
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{\rm st}
is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathbf{B}(\mathscr{H})/\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{H})
, the
Calkin algebra
. In this case the spectrum
{\rm sp}_{\mathbf{B}_{\rm st}^{\bullet}}\equiv{\rm sp}_{\rm ess}
is
the essential spectrum
, having well known equivalent descriptions.

So one can apply the results of the preceding section in this more explicit setting. But it is possible in this case to make the
G
-lemma more concrete, in terms of a usual asymptotic behavior (but replacing the equality with a lower bound).
We start with a lemma, recalling that
\mathcal{S}
already denoted the family of all the complements of relatively compact subsets of
\Xi
. It is a filter on
\Xi
; in the proof we will use some elementary facts about filters and ultrafilters.

Lemma 6.1
.

For every
\psi\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{\rm st}
one has

\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\overline{\psi(S)}\subset\widetilde{\psi}\big(\Sigma_{\rm st}^{\bullet}\big)\,.

Proof.

For
\lambda\in\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\overline{\psi(S)}
(the cluster set of
\psi
under the filter
\mathcal{S}
), there is an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
finer than
\mathcal{S}
such that
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\psi=\lambda
. Set
j:\Xi\to\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\rm st}
for the canonical injection. Then,
j(\mathcal{U})
is a filter base on
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\rm st}
and
\lim_{j(\mathcal{U})}\widetilde{\psi}=\lambda
. Thus, for every ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
on
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\rm st}
finer than the filter defined by
j(\mathcal{U})
, one has
\lim_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}=\lambda
. Moreover, as
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\rm st}
is compact, every ultrafilter on it is principal, i.e. it corresponds to the filter of neighborhoods of some
\sigma\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\rm st}
. As
\mathcal{S}
is a free filter,
\mathcal{U}
does not have a limit on
\Xi
, hence the accumulation points of
j(\mathcal{U})
do not intersect
\Xi
. This implies that
\sigma
, the limit of
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, does not belong to
\Xi
, and as in the standard case one has
\Sigma_{\rm st}=\Xi
, then
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\bullet}_{\rm st}
. Hence
\lambda=\widetilde{\psi}(\sigma)\in\widetilde{\psi}\big(\Sigma_{\rm st}^{\bullet}\big)
.
∎

For simplicity of notations, we stated and proved this lemma for functions
\psi
only defined on
\Xi
. The same arguments, involving the family
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\!:=\{{\sf X}_{\infty}\}\!\times\!\mathcal{S}
(a filter in the compact space
{\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}
) , lead to the formula

\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\overline{f(X\!\times\!S)}\subset f^{\bullet}_{\infty}\big({\sf X}_{\infty}\!\times\!\Sigma_{\rm st}^{\bullet}\big)\,,\quad\forall\,f\in\mathrm{C}_{0}\big({\sf X}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\big)\supset\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}\,,

making an obvious connection with Theorem
5.1
. Instead of (
5.7
) one gets

{\rm sp}_{\rm ess}\big({\sf Op}(f)\big)\supset\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\overline{f\big({\sf X}\!\times\!S\big)}

and a

{\rm dist}\big({\sf Op}(f),\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{H})\big)\geq\limsup_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}|f|=\min_{x\in{\sf X}}\limsup_{\xi\to\infty}|f(x,\xi)|\,.

(6.1)

The last two formulas, although just inequalities, present the advantage that they are expressed in terms of the behavior of the symbol on the familiar complements of compact sets. Actually the
\limsup
is also a standard concept here, as seen in (
6.1
).

It is a known fact that if
\mathfrak{A}_{1}\ll\mathfrak{A}_{2}
are
C^{*}
-algebras and
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{1}
has an inverse
b\in\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, then
b\in\mathfrak{A}_{1}
. On the other hand, Atkinson’s Theorem
[
12
, Proposition 3.3.11]
states that an element in
\mathbf{B}(\mathscr{H})
is
a Fredholm operator
if and only if its projection into the Calkin algebra is invertible. In other words, an element
{\sf Op}(f)\in\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\rm st}
is a Fredholm operator if and only if
{\sf Op}^{\bullet}(f)
is invertible in
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{\rm st}
. As Theorem
5.1
gives an explicit form of the spectrum of
{\sf Op}^{\bullet}(f)
, we obtain a criterion to determine when
{\sf Op}(f)
is a Fredholm operator. As mentioned in Remark
5.3
, if
{\sf X}
is not compact, then for every symbol
f
we have
\tilde{f}_{\infty}\!\left(\{x_{\infty}\}\!\times\!\widetilde{\Sigma}\right)=\{0\}
, thus
0\in{\rm sp}_{\rm ess}\big({\sf Op}(f)\big)
. We only state a weak form of a conclusion, using the familiar asymptotic quantity:

Corollary 6.2
.

If
\,{\sf X}
is not compact, for every symbol
f\in\mathbb{F}_{(1)}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}_{\rm st}
, the operator
{\sf Op}(f)
is not Fredholm. On the other hand, if
\,{\sf X}
is compact, then
{\sf Op}(f)
is a Fredholm operator if

\min_{x\in{\sf X}}\liminf_{\xi\to\infty}|f(x,\xi)|>0\,.

A sharper but less explicit form (involving equivalence) follows from Corollary
5.4
applied to this case.

Remark 6.3
.

In his work
[
4
]
V. Grushin also proved a
G
-lemma, only involving a lower bound, for pseudodifferential operators with bounded symbols for
{\sf X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The symbols used by him are some kind of Hörmander class functions:
f\in{\rm C}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{n}\big)
such that for every
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, with
\beta\neq 0
, there is a constant
c_{\alpha\gamma}>0
and a function
h_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\in{\rm C}_{0}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\big)
such that

\left|\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial\xi^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{\beta+\gamma}}{\partial x^{\beta+\gamma}}f(x,\xi)\right|\leq h_{\alpha\beta\gamma}(x)(1+|\xi|)^{-|\alpha|}\,,\hskip 11.38092pt\left|\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial\xi^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial x^{\gamma}}f(x,\xi)\right|\leq c_{\alpha\gamma}(1+|\xi|)^{-|\alpha|}\,.

This presents various differences with respect to our symbols. They require much more stringent regularity. However, due to the condition
\beta\neq 0
, the first estimation implies that a Grushin symbol needs no
\textrm{C}_{0}
-bahavior in the
x
-variable, as in our case.
On the other hand, the second estimation implies that a Grushin symbol
f
verifies
f(x,\cdot)\in{\rm VO}(\Xi)
for every fixed
x
, but they are much more exigent regarding the decaying ratios, involving an infinity of conditions. We do not need smoothness, and when it is present, only one first-order condition in the variable
\xi
is enough; see also Remark 7.1.

7
Further examples and remarks

Remark 7.1
.

Consider the case
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, corresponding to
{\sf X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. There is a simple device, to be used below, allowing to construct elements of
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
from the behavior required in
\mathrm{J}
: If a bounded function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is differentiable and each first order derivative
\partial_{j}\psi
belongs to
\mathrm{J}
for some admissible
C^{*}
-subalgebra on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\psi\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
. This follows from the gradient Theorem for line integrals:

\psi(\xi+\zeta)-\psi(\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}\!\nabla\psi(\xi+t\zeta)\cdot\zeta\,dt\,.

We finish this study by visiting some non-standard examples illustrating the constructions. We focus on the couple
\big(\mathrm{J},\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}\,\big)
, which then generates the entire formalism.
Let
\big\{\mathrm{J}_{i}\,\big|\,i\in I\big\}
be a family of admissible (non-unital)
C^{*}
-subalgebras. It is trivial to check that
\bigcap_{i}\!\mathrm{J}_{i}
is once again admissible and
\widetilde{\bigcap_{i}\!\mathrm{J}_{i}}=\bigcap_{i}\!\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{i}
. Such intersections can be used to model complex behavior of symbols. All the following examples are not just
C^{*}
-subalgebras of
\mathrm{BC_{u}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, but also ideals, most of which can be regarded as the ideal of bounded uniformly continuous functions which vanish with respect to some filter.

Example 7.2
.

This is just a warm up, since it can be covered in various ways from subsequent examples. For
\Xi={\sf X}=\mathbb{R}
, let us consider the ideal

\mathrm{J}_{+}\!=\Big\{\phi\in{\rm BC}_{\rm u}(\mathbb{R})\,\Big|\,\lim_{\xi\to+\infty}\phi(\xi)=0\Big\}\,,

which is clearly admissible and much larger than
{\rm C}_{0}=\mathrm{J}_{\rm st}
.
Thus, if the derivative
\psi^{\prime}
decays to zero at
+\infty
and is bounded and uniformly continuous (a very generous condition, to be satisfied “to the left”), the function
\psi
belongs to
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}
. For instance, its restriction to an interval
(a,+\infty)
, with
a
large, could be

\psi(\xi)=\sin\big[\beta(\xi)\big]\,,\quad{\rm with}\quad|\beta^{\prime}(\xi)|\underset{+\infty}{\to}0\,.

Examples as
\beta(\xi):=\xi^{\alpha}
where
\alpha<1
are available (as many others), and this is very far from requiring convergence to some limit. This and the very weak conditions towards
-\infty
prove that the vanishing oscillation functions with respect to certain filters may form quite large classes of functions.

Example 7.3
.

If
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}=\Xi
, it is easy to see that

\mathrm{J}_{\omega_{0}}=\Big\{\psi\in{\rm BC}_{\rm u}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\big)\,\Big|\,\lim_{\xi\to\omega^{\infty}_{0}}\psi(\xi)=0\Big\}

is an admissible ideal. The meaning of
\lim_{\xi\to\omega^{\infty}_{0}}\psi(\xi)=0
is that for every
\epsilon>0
there exist
V\in\mathcal{V}(\omega_{0})
and
R>0
such that
|\psi(\xi)|<\epsilon
if
\xi/|\xi|\in V
and
|\xi|>R
. This can also be interpreted differently if one places
\omega_{0}
at infinity (on the boundary of a radial compactification, for instance). Note that the previous example corresponds to
n=1
and
\omega_{0}=1
.

Consider for instance
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\omega_{0}=(1,0,0)
and a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
such that on the space
\xi_{1}\geq 1
it is given by
\psi\big(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\big)=\cos\big(\xi_{1}^{-2}\cos(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3})\big)
. Then

\nabla\psi\big(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\big)=\xi_{1}^{-2}\sin\big(\xi_{1}^{-2}\cos(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3})\big)\sin(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3})\big(-2\xi_{1}^{-3},1,1\big)\,,

which clearly verifies
\partial_{\xi_{1}}\psi,\partial_{\xi_{2}}\psi,\partial_{\xi_{3}}\psi\in\mathrm{J}_{\omega_{0}}
, thus
\psi\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{\omega_{0}}
by Remark
7.1
.

Example 7.4
.

To make it more general, one could start with a regular dynamical compactification
\Omega
of
\Xi
(that is
\Xi
is open and dense in
\Omega
and the action by translations extend to
\Omega
). Choose
\Omega_{0}
, a closed invariant subset of the boundary
\Omega\!\setminus\!\Xi
(an orbit closure, for instance). We define
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(\Omega_{0})
to be the family of neighborhoods of
\Omega_{0}
in
\Omega
, set

\mathrm{J}_{\Omega_{0}}=\big\{\psi\in{\rm BC}_{\rm u}\big(\Xi\big)\,\big|\,\forall\,\epsilon>0\,,\,\exists\,U\in\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(\Omega_{0})\ {\rm s.\,t.}\ |\psi(\xi)|<\epsilon\ {\rm if}\ \xi\in U\cap\Xi\big\}\,,

and check that is an admissible ideal. Note that Example
7.3
corresponds to consider
\Omega
to be the
radial compactification
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Omega_{0}=\{\omega_{0}^{\infty}\}
.

Example 7.5
.

Let
E
be a subset of
\Xi
such that
EK\neq\Xi
for every compact
K
. This means that
E
is
not syndetic
. The set
E=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[a_{n},b_{n}]
, composed of disjoint closed intervals of
\Xi=\mathbb{R}
, is syndetic if and only if the lengths of the gaps
\alpha_{n}:=a_{n+1}-b_{n}
is bounded. The numbers with prime absolute value in
\Xi=\mathbb{Z}
are not syndetic. We define an admissible ideal
\mathrm{J}_{E}
of bounded uniformly continuous functions on
\Xi
by the extra condition

\forall\,\epsilon>0\,,\ \exists\,K\subset\Xi\ {\rm compact\ \,s.t.}\ |\psi(\xi)|<\epsilon\,\ {\rm if}\ \xi\notin EK.

In such a situation we write
\lim_{[E]}\psi=0
. Setting
\Xi=\mathbb{R}
and
E=(-\infty,a]
for some real
a
leads to Example
7.2
, while any non-void interval
[a,b]
serves to recover the standard case.

Let again
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Chose
E
to be any non compact submanifold of dimension
m<n
. Such
E
fulfills the previous condition. In this case, we can give generic examples of functions belonging to the ideal
\mathrm{J}_{E}
. For
\xi\in E
, let
N_{\xi}
be the normal space to
E
at the point
\xi
. Pick then some orthonormal base
\{\eta_{\xi,1},\eta_{\xi,2},\dots,\eta_{\xi,n-m}\}
. Then
\varphi\in\mathrm{J}_{E}
follows from the condition

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\left\{\sup_{\xi\in E}\max_{1\leq i\leq n-m}\varphi\big(\xi+s\eta_{\xi,i}\big)\right\}=0\,.

(7.1)

Moreover, this implies that if
\psi
is differentiable and

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\{\sup_{\xi\in E}\max_{1\leq i\leq n-m}\partial_{j}\psi(\xi+s\eta_{\xi,i})\right\}=0\,,

then
\psi\in\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{E}
. Set for instance
E:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\xi=(t,t^{2}),\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
the graph of the function
\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto t^{2}
. At any point
(t,t^{2})
the normal space is generated by
(-2t,1)
. Then, one may rewrite condition (
7.1
) as

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\left(t-\frac{2ts}{\sqrt{1+4t^{2}}},t^{2}+\frac{s}{\sqrt{{1+4t^{2}}}}\right)\right)=0\,,

which means that for every
\epsilon>0
there is
\hat{s}>0
such that outside the set

\left\{\left(t-\frac{2ts}{\sqrt{1+4t^{2}}},t^{2}+\frac{s}{\sqrt{{1+4t^{2}}}}\right)\,\Big|\,t\in\mathbb{R},|s|<\hat{s}\right\}

the function
\varphi
is
\epsilon
-small.

Example 7.6
.

Let
\mathbb{D}:=\big\{D_{n}\,\big|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}
a family of compact subsets of
\Xi
satisfying by definition for every
n
: (a)
0\neq\widehat{\sf m}(D_{n})
, (b)
D_{n}\subset D_{n+1}
and (c) every compact set
L\subset\Xi
is contained in some
D_{n}
. It follows that
\cup_{n}D_{n}=\Xi
(the group
\Xi
is
\sigma
-compact) and
\lim_{n}\widehat{\sf m}(D_{n})=\infty
. We say that
A\subset\Xi
is
\mathbb{D}
-full
, and we write
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
if it is closed and
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\!\frac{\widehat{\sf m}(A\cap D_{n})}{\widehat{\sf m}(D_{n})}=1
.
It is useful to note that
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
is stable under finite intersections, translations by elements
\zeta\in\Xi
and that it contains all the complements of relatively compact subsets. In addition, if
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
and
K
is relatively compact, then
A\!\setminus\!K\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
.

One defines

\mathrm{J}_{\mathbb{D}}:=\big\{\psi\in{\rm BC_{u}}\,\big|\,\forall\,\epsilon>0\,,\,\exists\,A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}\,\ {\rm s.t.}\ \,|\psi(\xi)|\leq\epsilon\ \,{\rm if}\ \,\xi\in A\big\}\,.

(7.2)

By using the stated properties of the family
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
one easily shows that
\mathrm{J}_{\mathbb{D}}
is an admissible ideal of
{\rm BC_{u}}
. Slightly formally, the extra condition reads
\psi|_{B}\in{\rm C}_{0}(B)
for some
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
depending on
\psi
. Anyhow, it is easy to give examples: just choose a
\mathbb{D}
-full subset
A
, impose the
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-condition on
A
and let
\psi
be arbitrary outside
A
, up to boundedness and uniform continuity. Then, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one may use Remark
7.1
to indicate non-trivial examples of elements of the
C^{*}
-algebra
\widetilde{\mathrm{J}}_{\mathbb{D}}
. In
\Xi=\mathbb{R}
one can use balls
D_{n}\!:=\{\xi\!\mid\!|\xi|\leq n\}
, and then
\mathrm{J}_{\mathbb{D}}
is connected with the concept of
Cesáro limit
; see
[
2
, Lemma 4.1]
: their elements are characterized by the condition

\lim_{n}\frac{\int_{D_{n}}\!|\psi|}{\widehat{\sf m}(D_{n})}=0\,.

The reader may try various examples of infinite unions of closed finite intervals as potential elements of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}
.
Belonging to
\mathrm{J}_{\mathbb{D}}
is decided in terms of the lengths and the gaps involved.
