Abstract
Neural Networks (NN) has been used in many areas with great success. When a NN’s structure (Model) is given, during the training steps, the parameters of the model are determined using an appropriate criterion and an optimization algorithm (Training). Then, the trained model can be used for the prediction or inference step (Testing). As there are also many hyperparameters, related to the optimization criteria and optimization algorithms, a validation step is necessary before its final use. One of the great difficulties is the choice of the NN’s structure. Even if there are many ”on the shelf” networks, selecting or proposing a new appropriate network for a given data, signal or image processing, is still an open problem. In this work, we consider this problem using model based signal and image processing and inverse problems methods. We classify the methods in five classes, based on: i) Explicit analytical solutions, ii) Transform domain decomposition, iii) Operator Decomposition, iv) Optimization algorithms unfolding, and v) Physics Informed NN methods (PINN). Few examples in each category are explained. key words: Deep Neural Network, Inverse problems; Bayesian inference; Model based DNN structure



1
Introduction

Neural Networks (NN), and in particular Deep Neural Networks (DNN) have been used in many area with great succes, mentioning here only a few main area: Computer vision, Speech recognition, Artificial Intelligence (AI) with Large Language Models (LLM).

Nowadays, there are a great number of DNN models which are freely accessible for different use. However, when a NN’s structure (Model) is selected, either, they are pre-trained and ready for use in that special domain of interest, or, we have selected one that we want to use for our proper application. In this case, in general, we have to follow the following steps:

1.

Training step: For this, we need to access the training data in a data base. Then, we have to choose an appropriate criterion, based on our category of the problem to be solved: Classification, Clustering, Regression, etc. The next step is to choose an appropriate optimization algorithm, some parameters such as the learning rate, and finally, train the model. This means that the parameters of the model are obtained at the end of the convergence of the optimization algorithm.

2.

Model validation and hyperparameters tuning:
As there are many hyperparameters (related to the optimization criteria and optimization algorithms), a validation step is necessary before the final use of the model. In this step, in general, we may use a subset of the training data, or some other good quality training data, those which are qualified to be more confident, to tune the hyperparameters of the model and validate it.

3.

One last step before using the model is Testing it and evaluating its performances on a Testing data set. There are also a great number of testing and evaluation metrics, which have to be selected appropriately depending of the objectives of the considered problem.

4.

Final step is uploading the implemented model and use it. The amount of the memory needed for this step has to be considered.

All these steps are very well studied and many solutions have been proposed. One of the great difficulties is, in fact, in the first step: the choice of the NN’s structure. Even if there are many ”on the shelf” networks, selecting or proposing a new appropriate network for a given data, signal or image processing, is still an open problem. Which structure to choose? How many layers are sufficient ? Which kind of layer is more effective: a dense one, a convolutional one?

In this paper, we consider this problem using model based signal and image processing and inverse problems methods. We classify the methods in five classes:

1.

Methods based on the Explicite analytical solutions;

2.

Methods based on the Transform domain processing;

3.

Methods based on Operator decomposition structure;

4.

Methods based on the Unfolding of the iterative optimization algorithms; and

5.

Methods known as the Physics Informed NN (PINN).

Few examples in each category are explained.

The structure of the rest of this paper is the following:
As we focus on Forward modeling and Inverse problems methods, in Section 2, we present first two inverse problems that we will use later as the applications of the developped methods: Infrared imaging (IR), and Acoustical imaging.
In Section 3, a short presentation of the Bayesian inference framework, and its link with the deterministic regularization methods, are presented.
From this point, the four categories of the previously mentionned methods for selecting the structure of the NN are presented with details in the Sections 4, 5, 6 and 7. Finally, a very short applications of these methods in industry is presented and conclusions of this work are given.

7
Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINN)

The main idea behind the PINN are:

•

NNs are universal function approximators. Therefore a NN, provided that it is deep enough, can approximate any function, and also the solution for the differential equations.

•

Computing the derivatives of a NN output with respect to any of its input (and the model parameters during backpropagation), using Automatic Differentiation (AD), is easy and cheap. This is actually what made NNs so efficient and successful.

•

Usually NNs are trained to fit the data, but do not care from where come those data. This is where physics informed comes into play.

•

Physics based or Physics Informed: If, besides fitting the data, fit also the equations that govern that system and produce those data, their predictions will be much more precise and will generalize much better.

In the following, we consider two cases: i) the case where the forward model is explicitely given and an operator
H
, ii) the cases where the forward model are given either by an ODE or PDE.

When the forward operator
H
is known, we can use it to propose the following structure, to define a criterion to use for optimizing the parameters of the NN. Main references on PINN are:

[
21
,
14
,
12
,
5
]
.

7.1
PINN when an explicie forward model is available

When an explicie forward model
H
is available, we can use it at the output of the NN, shown in Figure

.

\mbox{Data~}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}\longrightarrow\framebox{~~NN({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}})~~}\longrightarrow\widehat{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}}\longrightarrow\framebox{{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}}\longrightarrow\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}})\longrightarrow J({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}},\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}}))=\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}-\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}})\|^{2}

Figure 15:
PINN when an explicie forward model
H
is available.

Here, the NN is used as a proxi or surrogate inversion method which parameters
w
are obtained by the optimization of the loss function
J({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}},\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}})
. If for an experiment, we have a set of data, we can use them, to define a loss function with two parts, shown in Figure
16
.

\mbox{Data~}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}_{i}\longrightarrow\framebox{~~NN({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}})~~}\longrightarrow\widehat{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}}_{i}\longrightarrow\framebox{{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}}\longrightarrow\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}\longrightarrow J({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}},\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}}))=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}\sum_{i}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}-\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}({{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{f}^{2}}\sum_{i}\|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}_{i}-\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}}\|^{2}

Figure 16:
PINN when an explicie forward model
H
is available

which can also be interpreted in a Bayesian way:

\displaystyle p({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}},\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}|{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\epsilon}),\quad\mbox{with~}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\epsilon}=\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}

(8)

\displaystyle p({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}|\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}|\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}},\sigma_{f}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}),\quad\mbox{with~}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{f}=\sigma_{f}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}

(9)

One last extension is to define the loss function as:

J({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}},\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}})=\mbox{Tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\epsilon}\right]+\mbox{Tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{f}\right]\mbox{~~with~~}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\epsilon}=\sum_{i}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}-\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}][{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}-\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{i}]^{t},\mbox{~~and~~}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{f}=\sum_{i}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}_{i}-\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}}][{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}_{i}-\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}}}]^{t}.

(10)

In this way, at the end of training, we also have a good estimate of the covariances

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\epsilon}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{f}
, which can, eventually, used as uncertainty priors for a new test input
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mbox{\boldmath$g$}}}_{j}
.

7.2

PINN for inverse problems modelled by ODE or PDE was conceptually proposed by Raissi et al., back in 2017

The main idea consists in creating a hybrid model where both the observational data and the known physical knowledge are present in model training.

Inverse problems in ODE or PDE can be categorized in three, more and more difficult, cases. To illustrate this, let us to consider a simple dynamical system:

•

Unknown parameters model. As a simple dynamical system, consider:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}a}u+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}b},

where the problem becomes to estimate the the parameters
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}a},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}b})
.

•

Unknown right side function:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}f(t,u)}.

•

Unknown parameters as well as the unknown function
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}f(t,u)}
:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}a}u+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}b}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}f(t,u)}

9
Conclusions

The main conclusions of this work are:

•

Deep Neural Networks (DNN) have been extendedly used in Computer Vision, and many other area. There are, nowadays, a great number of NN structures ”on the shelf”, and many users select one, and try to use. If it works, it is keeped, if not, just slects another, and so on!

•

In particular, in Inverse problems of Computer Vision, there is a need for guding the selection of an explainable structure, and so, explainable ML and AI.

•

In this work, a few directions have been proposed. These methods are classified as Model-based, Physics-based, or still, Physics Informed NN (PINN).
Model based or Physics based models have become a necessity for all the Computer Vision and imaging systems to develop robust methods.

•

PINN has originally been developed for inverse problems described by Ordinary or Partial Differentitial Equations (ODE/PDE).
The main idea in all these problems is to choose appropriately NNs and use them as ”Explainable” AI in industrial applications.
