Communication: Non-adiabatic derivative coupling elements for the coupled cluster singles and doubles model Preprint: APS/123-QED
Eirik F. Kjønstad, Henrik Koch

Abstract
We present an efficient implementation of analytical non-adiabatic derivative coupling elements for the coupled cluster singles and doubles model. The derivative coupling elements are evaluated in a biorthonormal formulation in which the nuclear derivative acts on the right electronic state, where this state is biorthonormal with respect to the set of left states. This stands in contrast to earlier implementations based on normalized states and a gradient formula for the derivative coupling. As an illustration of the implementation, we determine a minimum energy conical intersection between the n ​ π ∗ n\pi^{\ast} and π ​ π ∗ \pi\pi^{\ast} states in the nucleobase thymine.



I
Introduction

The nuclear dynamics that follows photoexcitation typically involves non-adiabatic population transfer between several electronic states. For example, in the nucleobase thymine, photoexcitation to the bright
\pi\pi^{\ast}
state is followed by rapid (
60
fs) non-adiabatic population transfer to the dark
n\pi^{\ast}
state.
Wolf et al. 2017
As is well known, the approximate description of the electronic structure can have a dramatic qualitative impact on the simulated nuclear dynamics, often complicating the task of correctly identifying the actual physics behind the processes observed in pump-probe experiments.
Domcke, Yarkony, and Köppel 2011
;
Curchod and Martinez 2018
A recent example is the ongoing debate about the dynamics that follows excitation to the bright
B_{3u}
state in pyrazine.
Kanno et al. 2015
;
Horio et al. 2016
;
Mignolet et al. 2018
;
Sun et al. 2020
;
Scutelnic et al. 2021
The ambiguities involved in interpreting time-resolved spectra illustrate the need for highly accurate description of the electronic structure.

A number of electronic structure methods has a long history of being applied to treat non-adiabatic effects, including complete active space
Roos, Taylor, and Sigbahn 1980
(CAS) methods, density functional theory
Kohn and Sham 1965
(DFT), and algebraic diagrammatic
construction
Schirmer 1982
(ADC). These methods are often complementary, where some are able to describe static correlation in the ground state and ground state intersections (CAS) while others better capture dynamical correlation but are unable to treat static correlation in the ground state as well as actual crossings with the ground state (DFT, ADC). In the latter category, there is still a need for a method that has systematically improvable accuracy that extends beyond a perturbative description of double excitations.

Coupled cluster theory is now well-established as the method of choice whenever this level of accuracy is required and the ground state is accurately described by a single determinant. However, initial progress towards its use in nonadiabatic dynamics simulations was slowed down with the realization
Hättig 2005
;
Köhn and Tajti 2007
that the method produces non-physical results at electronic degeneracies when the states that cross span the same symmetry. Later work by the present authors and collaborators showed that these artifacts were caused by the loss of electronic state orthogonality (matrix defects)
Kjønstad et al. 2017
and that they could be fully removed by enforcing orthogonality relations between the electronic states.
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
Our current understanding is that coupled cluster methods are able to describe conical intersections when the states span different symmetries but corrections
Köhn and Tajti 2007
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
are required when the states span the same symmetry. However, these conclusions are based on studies of the potential energy surfaces and not from considerations of the predicted physics. It still remains an open question to what extent the artifacts at same-symmetry intersections negatively affect the predicted dynamics in trajectory-based simulation methods like surface hopping
Tully 1990
and
ab initio
multiple spawning.
Ben-Nun, Quenneville, and Martinez 2000

Already in 1999 Christiansen
Christiansen 1999
derived expressions for the derivative coupling elements in coupled cluster theory, but the first implementation was given later by Tajti and Szalay
Tajti and Szalay 2009
at the singles and doubles level (CCSD). These authors did not, however, implement the expressions in Ref.
19
.
Instead, the coupling was evaluated from the gradient of the two states as well as the gradient of a fictitious summed state; this summed-state approach was also used in a more recent implementation of the CCSD coupling elements.
Faraji, Matsika, and Krylov 2018

In addition, they proposed modifications to account for the fact that the coupled cluster states are not normalized, building on earlier work by Gauss and coworkers
Gauss et al. 2006
who had found that normalization is important when evaluating the diagonal Born-Oppenheimer correction to the energy. The need for normalization in dynamics, which is not trivial to achieve, was later questioned by Shamasundar.
Shamasundar 2018
In a recent publication, we confirmed this by showing that a biorthonormal formalism exists in which there is no dependence on the norm of the electronic states.
Kjønstad and Koch 2021

In the present work, we provide a derivation (which is equivalent to Ref.
19
) and implementation, at the CCSD level of theory, of the derivative coupling between ground and excited states as well as between excited states. The derivation follows the Lagrangian approach for the derivative coupling proposed by Hohenstein in the context of CAS configuration interaction (CASCI),
Hohenstein 2016
while the present implementation builds on an efficient implementation of analytical gradients, exploiting Cholesky decomposed electronic repulsion integrals, recently published by the authors and collaborators.
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022

II
Theory

II.1
Lagrangian

The derivative coupling between states
i
and
j
is
Christiansen 1999
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2021

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i}^{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nabla\psi_{j}^{R}\rangle,\quad i,j=0,1,2,\ldots,

(1)

where
L
and
R
signify that these are the left and right electronic states, and the gradient
\nabla
is taken with respect to the coordinates of the atomic nuclei.

Analytical expressions for
\bm{F}_{ij}
may be derived by using the Lagrangian technique.
Here, we use the Lagrangian proposed by Hohenstein.
Hohenstein 2016
For the coupled cluster case, this Lagrangian can be expressed as
Kjønstad and Koch 2021

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{ij}=\mathscr{O}_{ij}+\text{conditions}

(2)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i}^{L}(\bm{x}_{0})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{j}^{R}(\bm{x})\rangle.

(3)

Here we have made the dependence on the nuclear geometry explicit:
\bm{x}_{0}
is the geometry where the derivative is to be evaluated, while
\bm{x}
is allowed to vary. Upon differentiating
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
, the derivative operation
\nabla
only acts on the ket vector. As a result, the derivative of
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
at
\bm{x}_{0}
is identical to
\bm{F}_{ij}
at
\bm{x}_{0}
.
Hohenstein 2016
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2021

The conditions in
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
are those that are required to specify the right state
\psi_{j}^{R}
for all values of
\bm{x}
. These are: the Hartree-Fock equations, for specifying the orbitals; the amplitude equations, for specifying the ground state cluster amplitudes; and the excited state eigenvalue equations, for specifying the excited state amplitudes. Written out in detail, the Lagrangian reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{L}_{ij}&=\mathscr{O}_{ij}+\sum_{\mu}\bar{\zeta}_{\mu}\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle\\
&+\sum_{\mu}\bar{\gamma}_{\mu}\bigl(\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},R_{j}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle-\omega_{j}R_{\mu}^{j}\bigr)\\
&+\bar{\xi}(1-\langle L_{j}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{j}\rangle)+\sum_{ai}\bar{\kappa}_{ai}\mathscr{F}_{ai},\end{split}

(4)

where we have suppressed the dependence on
\bm{x}_{0}
for notational convenience.

This expression for
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
introduces various quantities. The coupled cluster conditions are expressed in terms of the similarity-transformed Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\bar{H}=\exp(-T)\exp(\kappa)H\exp(-\kappa)\exp(T),

(5)

where we have introduced the orbital rotation operator

\displaystyle\kappa=\sum_{ai}\kappa_{ai}E_{ai}^{-},\quad E_{ai}^{-}=E_{ai}-E_{ia},

(6)

as well as the cluster operator

\displaystyle T=\sum_{\mu}t_{\mu}\tau_{\mu}

(7)

The scalars
t_{\mu}
are known as cluster amplitudes, and the
\tau_{\mu}
denote excitation operators. The
E_{ai}
are singlet one-electron excitation operators and
E_{ia}
are corresponding deexcitation operators. Here,
\kappa(\bm{x}_{0})=0
by assumption.

The electronic states are conveniently expressed as

\displaystyle|\psi_{k}^{R}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{k}\exp(T)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{k}^{L}|

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{HF}|\mathcal{L}_{k}\exp(-T)

(9)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{0}^{k}+R_{k}=R_{0}^{k}+\sum_{\mu}R_{\mu}^{k}\tau_{\mu}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}

\displaystyle=L_{0}^{k}+L_{k}=L_{0}^{k}+\sum_{\mu}L_{\mu}^{k}\tau_{\mu}^{\dagger}.

(11)

We will also find it useful to write

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=R^{k}_{0}|\mathrm{HF}\rangle+|R_{k}\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{k}|

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{HF}|L^{k}_{0}+\langle L_{k}|.

(13)

Furthermore, we have let

\displaystyle\omega_{k}=\langle L_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},R_{k}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(14)

and defined the Fock matrix as

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{pq}=h_{pq}+\sum_{k}(2g_{pkkq}-g_{pqkk}).

(15)

Here,
h_{pq}
and
g_{pqrs}
are the one- and two-electron integrals of the Hamiltonian. Following the conventional notation, we let
p,q,r,
and
s
denote generic orbitals;
i,j,k,
and
l
denote occupied orbitals;
a,b,c,
and
d
denote virtual orbitals. Lagrangian multipliers are denoted with a bar (
\bar{\zeta}_{\mu},\bar{\kappa}_{ai}
,
\bar{\xi}
).

The left-state quantities in
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
, that is,
\psi_{i}^{L}
and
L_{j}
, are
constants
that define
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
. They are evaluated at
\bm{x}_{0}
. Thus, the Lagrangian’s dependencies are understood as

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{ij}=\mathscr{L}_{ij}(\bm{x},\bm{t},\bm{R}_{j},\bm{\kappa},\bar{\bm{\zeta}},\bar{\xi},\bar{\bm{\kappa}};\bm{x}_{0}),

(16)

where the semicolon denotes that
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
depends only parametrically on
\bm{x}_{0}
.

II.2
Lagrangian stationarity conditions

The derivative coupling becomes the partial derivative of
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
when the Lagrangian is stationary with respect to all variables and multipliers that depend implicitly on
\bm{x}
. We begin by considering stationarity for
\bm{R}_{j}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{ij}}{\partial R_{\sigma}^{j}}&=L^{i}_{\sigma}+\sum_{\mu}\bar{\gamma}_{\mu}A_{\mu\sigma}\\
&-\omega_{j}\bar{\gamma}_{\sigma}-\sum_{\nu}L^{j}_{\nu}A_{\nu\sigma}\sum_{\mu}\bar{\gamma}_{\mu}R_{\mu}^{j}-\bar{\xi}L^{j}_{\sigma}=0,\end{split}

(17)

where

\displaystyle A_{\mu\nu}=\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},\tau_{\nu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle.

(18)

Using vector notation, this condition reads

\displaystyle\bm{0}=\bm{L}_{i}^{T}+\bar{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}(\bm{A}-\omega_{j})-(\omega_{j}\bar{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{R}_{j}+\bar{\xi})\bm{L}_{j}^{T}

(19)

Clearly, with
\bar{\xi}=-\omega_{j}\bar{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}\bm{R}_{j}
, the last term in the equation vanishes, and we
obtain stationarity provided

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}=\frac{1}{\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}}\bm{L}_{i}^{T}.

(20)

We thus see that the excited state multipliers (
\bar{\xi},\bar{\bm{\gamma}}
) can be expressed in terms of the excited states (
\bm{L}_{i}
,
\bm{R}_{j}
) and the associated excitation energies (
\omega_{i},\omega_{j}
).

Stationarity with respect to
\bm{t}
yields

\displaystyle\bm{0}={{}^{t}}\bm{\eta}^{T}+\bar{\bm{\zeta}}^{T}\bm{A}

(21)

where

\displaystyle{{}^{t}}\eta_{\sigma}=\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\tau_{\sigma}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{j}\rangle+(\bm{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{\bm{\gamma}})\bm{R}_{j})_{\sigma},

(22)

with the well-known
Koch and Jørgensen 1990

F
-matrix defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bar{\bm{\gamma}})_{\mu\nu}=\langle\bar{\gamma}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[[\bar{H},\tau_{\mu}],\tau_{\nu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle,\quad\langle\bar{\gamma}|=\langle\sigma|\bar{\gamma}_{\sigma}.

(23)

Similarly, stationarity with respect to
\bm{\kappa}
yields

\displaystyle\bm{0}={{}^{\kappa}}\bm{\eta}^{T}+\bar{\bm{\kappa}}^{T}\bm{A}^{\mathrm{HF}},

(24)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{{}^{\kappa}}\eta_{ai}&=\langle\mathcal{L}_{i}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptE_{ai}^{-}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{j}\rangle+\langle\bar{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[E_{ai}^{-},\bar{H}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle\\
&+\langle\bar{\gamma}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[[E_{ai}^{-},\bar{H}],R_{j}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle,\end{split}

(25)

and where
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{HF}}
is the Hartree-Fock Hessian. The amplitude and orbital conditions, given by Eqs. (
21
) and (
24
), are solved numerically for
\bar{\bm{\zeta}}
and
\bar{\bm{\kappa}}
.

II.3
Derivative coupling elements

Once
\bar{\bm{\zeta}}
and
\bar{\bm{\kappa}}
are known, we can evaluate the coupling by taking the partial derivative of
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
with respect to the nuclear components
\{q\}
. This yields
Christiansen 1999
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2021

\displaystyle F_{ij}^{q}=\frac{\langle L_{i}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H}^{q},R_{j}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle}{\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}}+\langle\bar{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{q}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\bar{\kappa}_{ai}\mathscr{F}_{ai}^{q},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\bar{H}^{q}=\exp(-T)H^{q}\exp(T).

(27)

Here
H^{q}
denotes the partial derivative of
H
with respect to the
q
th nuclear coordinate,
x_{q}
. By expanding the commutator in Eq. (
26
), we obtain the equivalent expression

\displaystyle F_{ij}^{q}=\frac{\langle L_{i}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{q}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{j}\rangle}{\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}}+\langle\tilde{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{q}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\bar{\kappa}_{ai}\mathscr{F}_{ai}^{q},

(28)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}=\bar{\bm{\zeta}}-\bm{J},\quad J_{\mu}=\frac{\langle L_{i}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{j}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mu\rangle}{\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}}=\frac{j_{\mu}}{\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}}.

(29)

Clearly,
\bm{F}_{ij}
is the sum of an excited state gradient and a ground state gradient, plus an orbital relaxation term. The expression in Eq. (
28
) is convenient when invoking an existing molecular gradient code.

So far we have assumed that the right state (
\psi_{j}^{R}
) is an excited state. This raises the question of how to evaluate the coupling when
\psi_{j}^{R}
is the ground state (
j=0
). When this is the case, the excited state condition in
\mathscr{L}_{ij}
can be removed. As a result, the
\bm{t}
stationarity simplifies to

\displaystyle\bm{0}=\bm{L}_{i}^{T}+\bar{\bm{\zeta}}^{T}\bm{A},

(30)

so that

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\zeta}}^{T}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{i}}\bm{L}_{i}^{T}=\frac{1}{E_{0}-E_{i}}\bm{L}_{i}^{T},

(31)

where
E_{k}
denotes the electronic energy of the
k
th state. The orbital multiplier equation is also simplified by the removal of
\langle\bar{\gamma}|
, but this equation must still be solved numerically. Once
\bar{\bm{\kappa}}
is known, we can evaluate
\bm{F}_{i0}
as

\displaystyle F_{i0}^{q}=\frac{\langle L_{i}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{q}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle}{E_{0}-E_{i}}+\bar{\kappa}_{ai}\mathscr{F}_{ai}^{q}.

(32)

II.4
Significance of orbital connections

Hamiltonian derivatives are treated in the same way as for molecular energy gradients. That is, we take
H
to be expressed, for all
\bm{x}
, in a non-unique orthonormal MO (OMO) basis which is defined by an orbital connection.
Olsen et al. 1995
Any orbital connection can be used, but the choice may actually affect the expression for the derivative coupling. In fact, as we will explain below, the formula in Eq. (
26
) is only correct when we use the natural connection.
Olsen et al. 1995
;
Christiansen 1999
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2021

For other connections, such as the widely-used symmetric connection, the partial derivative of
\mathscr{O}_{ij}
is non-zero and must be added to the expression for
\bm{F}_{ij}
.
Hohenstein 2016

To show this, we express the derivative of
\mathscr{O}_{ij}
in terms of the orbital connection.
Given a connection matrix
\bm{T}
, we define the OMOs as

\displaystyle\psi_{p}=\sum_{q}T_{pq}\varphi_{q},

(33)

where the unmodified MOs (UMOs) are given as

\displaystyle\varphi_{q}=\sum_{\alpha}C_{\alpha q}(\bm{x}_{0})\chi_{\alpha}(\bm{x}).

(34)

Here,
\{C_{\alpha q}\}
denotes MO coefficients, and
\{\chi_{\alpha}\}
denotes atomic orbitals. The UMOs are generally only orthonormal at
\bm{x}_{0}
, that is,

\displaystyle S_{rs}=\langle\varphi_{r}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\varphi_{s}\rangle\neq\delta_{rs},\quad\bm{x}\neq\bm{x}_{0}.

(35)

This is, of course, why an orbital connection is required in the first place; consistently evaluating the derivative is most easily done in a Fock space defined by an orbital basis that is orthonormal for all values of
\bm{x}
.

LiH/cc-pVQZ derivative coupling calculated for CCSD and FCI. For CCSD, we present couplings both for the direct evaluation of the coupling (this work) and for the summed-gradient values in Ref.

.

Now, the derivative of
\mathscr{O}_{ij}
can be written
Olsen et al. 1995

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{O}_{ij}^{q}=\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{ij}}{\partial x_{q}}\Big|_{0}=\sum_{rs}D_{rs}^{ij}Y_{rs}^{q},\end{split}

(36)

where
\bm{D}^{ij}
is the transition state density at
\bm{x}_{0}
, and

\displaystyle Y_{rs}^{q}=\Big\langle\psi_{r}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\psi_{s}}{\partial x_{q}}\Big\rangle\Big|_{0}.

(37)

For the natural connection, we have, by construction,
Olsen et al. 1995

\displaystyle Y_{rs}^{q}=0,

(38)

and so we can conclude that
Olsen et al. 1995
;
Christiansen 1999

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{ij}^{q}=0.

(39)

Next, let us consider the symmetric connection. In this case,
\bm{T}=\bm{S}^{-1/2}
, which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{rs}}{\partial x_{q}}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial S_{rs}}{\partial x_{q}}\Big|_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}(W_{rs}^{q}+W_{sr}^{q}),

(40)

where

\displaystyle W_{rs}^{q}=\Big\langle\varphi_{r}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\varphi_{s}}{\partial x_{q}}\Big\rangle\Big|_{0}.

(41)

Consequently,

\displaystyle Y_{rs}^{q}=W_{rs}^{q}-\frac{1}{2}(W_{rs}^{q}+W_{sr}^{q})=\frac{1}{2}(W_{rs}^{q}-W_{sr}^{q}),

(42)

and so

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{O}_{ij}^{q}&=\sum_{rs}D_{rs}^{ij}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}(W_{rs}^{q}-W_{sr}^{q})\Bigr)\\
&=\sum_{rs}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}(D_{rs}^{ij}-D_{sr}^{ij})\Bigr)W_{rs}^{q}.\end{split}

(43)

For the symmetric connection, therefore, the derivative of
\mathscr{O}_{ij}
is equal to the anti-symmetrized density matrix contracted with a ket-derivative of an overlap matrix.
Hohenstein 2016
This overlap derivative is evaluated as

\displaystyle W_{rs}^{q}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}C_{\alpha r}C_{\beta s}\Big\langle\chi_{\alpha}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\chi_{\beta}}{\partial x_{q}}\Big|_{0}\Big\rangle.

(44)

For the natural connection,
\bm{W}^{q}
is of course not needed for
\mathscr{O}_{ij}^{q}
(which is zero). However,
\bm{W}^{q}

is
required for the reorthonormalization terms associated with the Hamiltonian. For the natural connection, the ket-derivative
\bm{W}^{q}
plays the same role that the braket-derivative
\bm{S}^{q}
does for the symmetric connection.
Olsen et al. 1995
These reorthonormalization terms are the same for derivative couplings and molecular energy gradients, so we refer the reader to the literature for more details.
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022

II.5
Relation to previous implementations

In the literature, the derivative coupling has been implemented through a summed-state formula
Tajti and Szalay 2009
;
Faraji, Matsika, and Krylov 2018
which is closely related to the one presented in this work. However, we have not been able to show that the two formulations are equivalent, except in the FCI limit. As we will see, our values for the coupling deviates to some extent from the values presented by Tajti and Szalay for the LiH molecule.

\bm{h}

\displaystyle\bm{h}=(E_{2}-E_{1})\bm{F}_{12}.

(50)

The gradient

is used in combination with a Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) solver already implemented in

V
Summary and outlook

In this work we have presented an efficient implementation of derivative coupling elements that will enable us to perform large-scale simulations of nonadiabatic dynamics at the CCSD level of theory. Chemical systems of interest are now within the reach of CCSD dynamics using
e.g.
the multiple spawning framework;
Ben-Nun, Quenneville, and Martinez 2000
for example, a single-point calculation on thymine with a cc-pVDZ basis, including gradients of the
n\pi^{\ast}
and
\pi\pi^{\ast}
states, as well as the coupling between them, can be performed in a matter of minutes on a modern CPU node (see Schnack-Petersen
et al.
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
for representative timings).

We emphasize that for systems where the intersecting states span the same symmetry, the wavepacket may end up in regions that encompasses a defective intersection. We then expect that corrections must be applied to the standard CC methods in order to extract meaningful results, though this will depend on the size of the defective intersection seam, which, in turn, depends on the truncation level. Work on extending the present implementation to the similarity constrained coupled cluster method (SCCSD), where such defects are completely eliminated,
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
is in progress. Note that the Lagrangian approach makes such an extension straight-forward; we simply need to add the orthogonality condition to the Lagrangian and solve the resulting response equations.

The case that
can
be treated with standard coupled cluster theory is that of intersections where the states span different symmetries (
e.g.
the
n\pi^{\ast}
and
\pi\pi^{\ast}
states in thymine). We may expect that such systems can be accurately described in dynamics simulations where coupled cluster theory provides the underlying electronic structure.
This is the subject of a forthcoming article.
