Abstract




Geodesic solutions

Our geodesic equation is given by (see (72) of
[
1
]
)

0=U^{a}\nabla_{a}U^{b}

To find solutions, we start by expressing this equation in terms of Minkowski coordinates
(T,X,Y,Z)
, where the spatial coordinate origin of this system is located at the position of our inertial center (i.e.
r=0\rightarrow(X,Y,Z)=(0,0,0)
). Since our metric in terms of this Minkowski coordinate parametrization takes the form

-d\chi^{2}=-c^{2}dT^{2}+\sum_{i}dX_{i}^{2}

where
i=1,2,3
and
X^{1}=X,X^{2}=Y,X^{3}=Z
, our metric components in Minkowski coordinates
g_{\mu\nu}
are all constants. Thus, our affine connection tensor for Minkowski coordinates is zero:
\Gamma^{c}_{ab}|_{\rm Minkowski}=0
. Our equations of motion in Minkowski coordinates are then found to be

\frac{d^{2}T}{d\sigma^{2}}=0\indent\indent{\rm and}\indent\indent\frac{d^{2}X^{i}}{d\sigma^{2}}=0

(1)

where
\sigma\rightarrow\chi=\sqrt{\Lambda}\cdot\int r(\tau)d\tau
for massive geodesics in the theory of inertial centers (see the introduction or equation (57) of
[
1
]
). Examining only our spatial components, we see that

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d^{2}X^{i}}{d\sigma^{2}}=\frac{d}{d\sigma}\bigg(\frac{dT}{d\sigma}\frac{dX^{i}}{dT}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}T}{d\sigma^{2}}\frac{dX^{i}}{dT}+\bigg(\frac{dT}{d\sigma}\bigg)^{2}\frac{d^{2}X^{i}}{dT^{2}}

which leads us to

\frac{d^{2}X^{i}}{dT^{2}}=0

(2)

where we have used the equation of motion for our Minkowski time coordinate
T
.

The solutions to (
2
) are of the form

X^{i}(T)=v^{i}T+X^{i}_{0}

(3)

where
X^{i}_{0}
is our spatial Minkowski coordinate location when
T=0
. Nevertheless, we know from the form of our affine parameter
\chi
that we wish to express this coordinate parametrization of our geodesics in terms of the more physically applicable radial Rindler coordinates, where the coordinate transformations from the Minkowski coordinate parameterization employed above are

\displaystyle T=\frac{r}{c}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)

\displaystyle X=r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}

\displaystyle Y=r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi}

\displaystyle Z=r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\cos{\theta}

Using these transformations in (
3
), we find

\displaystyle r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}=\bigg(\frac{v^{X}}{c}\bigg)r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+X_{0}

\displaystyle r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi}=\bigg(\frac{v^{Y}}{c}\bigg)r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+Y_{0}

\displaystyle r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\cos{\theta}=\bigg(\frac{v^{Z}}{c}\bigg)r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+Z_{0}

Solving for
r
by squaring each one of these equations and adding the results together

r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)=\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+X^{i}_{0}\bigg)^{2}

where we choose our time coordinates
T
and
t
to coincide for our initial conditions (i.e.
t=0\iff T=0
and
[dt=dT]|_{t=T=0}
). This requirement forces our Minkowski constant
c
to be equal to
\sqrt{\Lambda}r_{0}
where
r_{0}
here is the initial radial position of our object along the geodesic and
\sqrt{\Lambda}
is fixed by the value of the Hubble constant. Remember that our inertial time coordinate
t
progresses at the same rate as the clock of a stationary observer located the same distance away from our inertial center as our object. This will hold true when the object is located at any particular position along its path, since at each radial location, our line element for a stationary observer reduces to
-\Lambda r^{2}d\tau_{S}^{2}=-\Lambda r^{2}dt^{2}
(see (57) and (1) of
[
1
]
). Thus, coordinate time
t
is physically equivalent to the elapsed time measured by an observer carried with the object in question but in the limit where the speed of this ‘carried’ observer relative to the inertial center in question is zero at all points along the traversed path (i.e. the ‘carried’ observer remains stationary at each point that the object reaches).

Expanding the expression above,

r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)=\sum_{i}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)^{2}r^{2}\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+2\frac{v^{i}}{c}X_{0}^{i}r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+(X^{i}_{0})^{2}\bigg]

But

\sum_{i}(X^{i}_{0})^{2}=\sum_{i}(X^{i}(T))^{2}|_{T=0}=\sum_{i}(X^{i}(t))^{2}|_{t=0}=r_{0}^{2}

Using this relation,

\displaystyle r_{0}^{2}+2r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)X_{0}^{i}=r^{2}\bigg[\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)-\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=r^{2}\bigg[\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)-\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg(1-1+\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=r^{2}\bigg[\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)-\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg(1-\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=r^{2}\bigg[1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg(1-\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\bigg]

However, for
R^{2}=\sum_{i}(X^{i})^{2}
, we see that
v^{R}(T)=dR/dT
is given by

Rv^{R}=\sum_{i}X^{i}\frac{dX^{i}}{dT}=\sum_{i}X^{i}v^{i}

Yet, since
dX^{i}/dT
is a constant for all
T
, we have

\sum_{i}X_{0}^{i}v^{i}=Rv^{R}|_{T=0}=Rv^{R}|_{t=0}=r_{0}v_{0}^{r}

(4)

where
v_{0}^{r}=dr/dt|_{t=0}
is the initial radial velocity of the object in the inertial system. Plugging in above, we find

0=r^{2}\bigg[1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg(1-\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{v^{i}}{c}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\bigg]-2r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\cdot r_{0}\frac{v^{r}_{0}}{c}-r_{0}^{2}

Solving for
r(t)
,

r(t)=r_{0}\bigg[\frac{\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\pm\sqrt{(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})^{2}\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+[1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)(1-\sum_{i}(\frac{v^{i}}{c})^{2})]}}{1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)(1-\sum_{i}(\frac{v^{i}}{c})^{2})}\bigg]

When
t=0
, we want our expression to reduce to
r_{0}
and not
-r_{0}
. Therefore, we take only the positive root for our solution.

Additionally, we see that
\sum_{i}(v^{i})^{2}
can be expressed in terms of the induced Riemannian metric components
h_{ij}
for a space-like hypersurface in our original Minkowski coordinates in the following manner:

\sum_{i}(v^{i})^{2}=\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}v^{i}v^{j}=v_{0}^{2}

However, this is simply the squared magnitude of the
v^{i}
’s in Minkowski coordinates along this hypersurface. Furthermore, since our time coordinates coincide at
t=T=0
,
v_{0}
can be interpreted physically as the initial speed of our inertially traveling object measured in terms of the clock of a stationary observer located at
r=r_{0}
. In other words, since
v^{i}=dX^{i}/dT=dX^{i}/dT|_{T=0}
,

\displaystyle v_{0}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}\frac{dX^{i}}{dT}\frac{dX^{j}}{dT}=\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}\frac{dX^{i}}{dT}\frac{dX^{j}}{dT}\bigg|_{T=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{k,l}h_{ij}\frac{\partial X^{i}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{k}}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{k}}{dT}\frac{\partial X^{j}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{l}}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{l}}{dT}\bigg|_{T=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l}\bigg(\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}\frac{\partial X^{i}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{k}}\frac{\partial X^{j}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{l}}\bigg)\frac{d\tilde{x}^{k}}{dt}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{l}}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l}\tilde{h}_{kl}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{k}}{dt}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{l}}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}

(5)

where
\tilde{h}_{kl}
are induced Riemannian spatial metric components along the
t=T=0
hypersurface and
\tilde{x}^{k}
are spatial coordinates. Both tilded expressions are in radial Rindler coordinates. Plugging back into our expression for
r(t)
where we remember to take only the positive root, we find for the radial position of our object

r(t)=r_{0}\bigg[\frac{\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\sqrt{1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)(1+(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})^{2}-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2})}}{1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)(1-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2})}\bigg]

(6)

where
c=\sqrt{\Lambda}r_{0}
and

\displaystyle(v_{0}^{r})^{2}-v_{0}^{2}=(v_{0}^{r})^{2}-\sum_{k,l}\tilde{h}_{kl}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{k}}{dt}\frac{d\tilde{x}^{l}}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=(v_{0}^{r})^{2}-\bigg[(v_{0}^{r})^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\bigg((v_{0}^{\theta})^{2}+\sin^{2}{\theta_{0}}(v_{0}^{\phi})^{2}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=-r_{0}^{2}\bigg((v_{0}^{\theta})^{2}+\sin^{2}{\theta_{0}}(v_{0}^{\phi})^{2}\bigg)

(7)

Furthermore, our normalization condition for the ‘four-velocity’ along these geodesics is

-k=g_{ab}U^{a}U^{b}

where
U^{\mu}=dx^{\mu}/d\sigma
and

k=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}0&\quad\textrm{null geodesics}\\
1&\quad\textrm{time-like geodesics}\\
\end{array}\right.

We can express this normalization condition in our original Minkowski coordinates in the following manner:

-k=-c^{2}\bigg(\frac{dT}{d\sigma}\bigg)^{2}+\sum_{i}\bigg(\frac{dX^{i}}{d\sigma}\bigg)^{2}=-\bigg(\frac{dT}{d\sigma}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[c^{2}-\sum_{i}(v^{i})^{2}\bigg]=-\bigg(\frac{dT}{d\sigma}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[c^{2}-v_{0}^{2}\bigg]

which provides us with the constraints that one would expect

\displaystyle v_{0}=c\indent\textrm{massless objects}

(8)

\displaystyle v_{0}<c\indent\textrm{massive objects}

(9)

Returning to our coordinate transformations, the angular positions of our object as a function of
t
are found to be

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\tan^{-1}\bigg[\frac{\frac{v^{y}_{0}}{c}r(t)\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+Y_{0}}{\frac{v^{x}_{0}}{c}r(t)\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+X_{0}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}+\tan^{-1}\bigg[\frac{r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\frac{v_{0}^{\phi}}{c}r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}}{r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}+r\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)[\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\sin{\theta_{0}}+\frac{v_{0}^{\theta}}{c}r_{0}\cos{\theta_{0}}]}\bigg]

(10)

and

\displaystyle\theta(t)=\cos^{-1}\bigg[\frac{v^{z}_{0}}{c}\tanh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\frac{Z_{0}}{r(t)\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\cos^{-1}\bigg[\tanh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\cos{\theta_{0}}-\frac{v_{0}^{\theta}}{c}r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}\bigg)+\frac{r_{0}\cos{\theta_{0}}}{r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\bigg]

(11)

where

\displaystyle X(t)|_{t=0}=X_{0}=r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}\cos{\phi_{0}}

(12)

\displaystyle Y(t)|_{t=0}=Y_{0}=r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}\sin{\phi_{0}}

(13)

\displaystyle Z(t)|_{t=0}=Z_{0}=r_{0}\cos{\theta_{0}}

(14)

and

\displaystyle v^{X}=v_{0}^{x}=v_{0}^{r}\sin{\theta_{0}}\cos{\phi_{0}}+v_{0}^{\theta}r_{0}\cos{\theta_{0}}\cos{\phi_{0}}-v^{\phi}_{0}r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}\sin{\phi_{0}}

(15)

\displaystyle v^{Y}=v_{0}^{y}=v_{0}^{r}\sin{\theta_{0}}\sin{\phi_{0}}+v_{0}^{\theta}r_{0}\cos{\theta_{0}}\sin{\phi_{0}}+v^{\phi}_{0}r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}\cos{\phi_{0}}

(16)

\displaystyle v^{Z}=v_{0}^{z}=v_{0}^{r}\cos{\theta_{0}}-v_{0}^{\theta}r_{0}\sin{\theta_{0}}

(17)

One can obtain initial velocity expressions (
15
)-(
17
) by plugging our spatial coordinate transformations into each of our
dX^{i}/dT|_{T=0}
terms.

We have yet to address the relationship between our time coordinate
t
and the proper time of our object
\tau
in this particular inertial reference frame. For clarification, we remember that
\tau
represents the physical elapsed time as measured by a clock
moving
with our object along the traversed geodesic. From our normalization condition for massive objects (
\sigma=\chi
),

\frac{dT}{d\chi}=\frac{1}{c\sqrt{1-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2}}}

Choosing to examine the proper time of our clock in the particular inertial frame of reference in which the object orbits about our inertial center, we have
d\chi/d\tau=\sqrt{\Lambda}r
and so

\frac{dT}{d\tau}=\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda}r}{c}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2}}}

Expressing
T
in radial Rindler coordinates,

\displaystyle\frac{dT}{d\tau}=\frac{d}{d\tau}\bigg(\frac{r}{c}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\frac{dt}{d\tau}\bigg(\frac{dr}{dt}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\sqrt{\Lambda}r\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg)

and plugging in above

\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2}}}\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda}r(t)}{\frac{dr}{dt}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\sqrt{\Lambda}r(t)\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}

(18)

Notice, when
t=0
we have

\frac{dt}{d\tau}\bigg|_{t=0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2}}}

(19)

which is simply the familiar special relativistic relation.

Using (
6
) in (
18
), we find

\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\bigg[\frac{2}{1+\frac{\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{}|_{+v}\cdot[(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\sqrt{}|_{+v}]}\bigg]^{-1}

(20)

where

\gamma_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(\frac{v_{0}}{c})^{2}}}\indent\indent\textrm{and}\indent\indent\sqrt{}|_{+v}=\sqrt{1+\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\bigg[\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}\bigg]}

In the limit as
t\rightarrow\pm\infty
, this expression reduces to

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\gamma_{0}\frac{\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}{\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\bigg[2\gamma_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}}}\frac{1}{(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})+\sqrt{(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}}}\bigg]^{-1}\bigg|_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\rightarrow\infty

However, examining the radial velocity of our object as
t\rightarrow\pm\infty
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{dr}{dt}=-\sqrt{\Lambda}r_{0}\frac{\cosh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}{\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\gamma_{0}^{2}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\bigg)+\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}}\bigg]\bigg|_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}

where

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}r(t)=r_{0}\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\bigg)+\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}}\bigg]\bigg|_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}

And so we find that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{dr}{dt}=\mp\sqrt{\Lambda}r|_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}

Therefore, since the radial velocity of our object approaches that of light in the limit as
t\rightarrow\pm\infty
(see (30) and (37) of
[
1
]
as well), we should expect the above result for our difference in clock rates (
20
) which says that the clock of a stationary observer located at the same radial position as our object in this limit will see the clock of our now nearly light-speed object not progressing in time.

Furthermore, after integrating (
20
), we find that the elapsed proper time in terms of the elapsed time measured by our ‘carried’ stationary observer is

\tau(t)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}\bigg\{\tan^{-1}\bigg[\frac{\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}{\gamma_{0}}\bigg]+\tan^{-1}\bigg[\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\bigg(\frac{1-\beta_{0}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)-\sqrt{}|_{+v}}{\beta_{0}-\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}+(\beta_{0}+\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c})[\beta_{0}\sinh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)+\sqrt{}|_{+v}]}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg\}

(21)

where

\beta_{0}=\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{v_{0}^{r}}{c}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{2}}}

and we take
\tau(t=0)=0
so that these clocks coincide for our initial conditions. One could also express our geodesics in terms of the clock of an observer located at a different spatial position than that of our object by comparing these two clocks using the form of our invariant interval, similar to how we obtained (66) at the end of “Quantization of a real scalar field” in
[
1
]
.

Massless solutions to Klein-Gordon extension

We begin from the proposed extension of the Klein-Gordon equation for a real scalar field in the theory of inertial centers (see (60) of
[
1
]
)

(\Box-\tilde{\mu}^{2}r^{2})\phi=0

where
\Box=\nabla_{a}\nabla^{a}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator and
\nabla_{a}
is the derivative operator compatible with the metric
g_{ab}
(i.e.
\nabla_{a}g_{bc}=0
). Additionally,
\tilde{\mu}=m\sqrt{\Lambda}/\hbar
where
m
is the mass of our scalar field,
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant, and
\sqrt{\Lambda}
is taken to be the Hubble constant. As we are examining solutions (
\phi_{i}
) to this equation for a massless real scalar field, we set
m=0
and find

\Box\phi_{i}=0

(22)

Expanding (
22
) in radial Rindler coordinates:

0=-\frac{1}{\Lambda r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\partial_{t}(\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\partial_{t}\phi_{i})+\frac{1}{r^{3}}\partial_{r}(r^{3}\partial_{r}\phi_{i})+\frac{1}{r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sin{\theta}}\partial_{\theta}(\sin{\theta}\partial_{\theta}\phi_{i})+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}{\theta}}\partial^{2}_{\phi}\phi_{i}\bigg]

Then for
\phi_{i}=Z_{i}\cdot g(t)\cdot h(r)\cdot Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\phi)
where
Z_{i}
is a normalization constant, we find

0=-\frac{1}{g\cdot\Lambda r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\partial_{t}(\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\partial_{t}g)+\frac{1}{h\cdot r^{3}}\partial_{r}(r^{3}\partial_{r}h)-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}

Grouping functions of
t
and
r
:

\frac{1}{h\cdot r}\partial_{r}(r^{3}\partial_{r}h)=\frac{1}{g\cdot\Lambda\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\partial_{t}(\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\partial_{t}g)+\frac{l(l+1)}{\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}=-(4\alpha^{2}+1)

where
\alpha
is a constant. This gives us two differential equations

\displaystyle 0=r^{2}\partial^{2}_{r}h+3r\partial_{r}h+(4\alpha^{2}+1)h

(23)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{\Lambda\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}\partial_{t}(\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\partial_{t}g)+\bigg[\frac{l(l+1)}{\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)}+(4\alpha^{2}+1)\bigg]

(24)

The “positive frequency” solution to (
24
) was found in Appendix F of
[
1
]
to be

g(\eta)=\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}\cdot P_{l}^{-2i\alpha}(\eta)

where
\eta=\tanh(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)
and
P_{l}^{-2i\alpha}
is the Legendre function of degrees
l
and imaginary order
-2\alpha
. Thus, the only difference between our solutions for the massive and massless cases occurs in the radial function
h
as one can see from (
23
). Yet (
23
) is the Cauchy-Euler equation of second order given in general form by (see Chapter 61 of
[
2
]
)

x^{2}y^{\prime\prime}+axy^{\prime}+by=0

with solution
y(x)=c_{+}x^{\lambda_{+}}+c_{-}x^{\lambda_{-}}
for distinct roots
\lambda_{+}
,
\lambda_{-}
or
y(x)=c_{1}x^{\lambda}+c_{2}\ln(x)x^{\lambda}
for double roots to the characteristic polynomial equation

0=\lambda^{2}+(a-1)\lambda+b

For our case, we have
a=3
and
b=4\alpha^{2}+1
, which gives

\lambda_{\pm}=-1\pm 2i\alpha

And so we have for possible solutions
h_{\alpha}
in the massless case

h^{\pm}_{\alpha}(r)\propto\frac{1}{r}\cdot(r^{2})^{\pm i\alpha}

(25)

The inner product of two positive frequency solutions is given by

(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dr\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\bigg[r^{2}\cosh^{2}(\sqrt{\Lambda}t)\sin{\theta}\bigg(\frac{\phi_{2}}{\sqrt{\Lambda}|r|}\partial_{t}\bar{\phi}_{1}-\frac{\bar{\phi}_{1}}{\sqrt{\Lambda}|r|}\partial_{t}\phi_{2}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg|_{t=0}

where
\bar{\phi_{i}}
is the complex conjugate of
\phi_{i}
. Plugging in for our massless solutions,

(\phi^{\pm}_{1},\phi^{\pm}_{2})=-iZ_{1}Z_{2}\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}}\delta_{m_{1}m_{2}}[g_{2}\partial_{\eta}\bar{g}_{1}-\bar{g}_{1}\partial_{\eta}g_{2}]\|_{\eta=0}\cdot\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\cdot(r^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dr}{r}(r^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}\bigg]

But letting
\tilde{r}=-r
in the second integral, we see that

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dr}{r}(r^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}=-\int_{\infty}^{0}\frac{d\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}}(\tilde{r}^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{d\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}}(\tilde{r}^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}

Our inner product then reduces to

(\phi^{\pm}_{1},\phi^{\pm}_{2})=-2iZ_{1}Z_{2}\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}}\delta_{m_{1}m_{2}}[g_{2}\partial_{\eta}\bar{g}_{1}-\bar{g}_{1}\partial_{\eta}g_{2}]\|_{\eta=0}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\cdot(r^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}

However, if we take
x=\ln{r}
such that
dx=dr/r
, our integral becomes

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r}\cdot(r^{2})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx(e^{2x})^{\pm i(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}

Notice that we do not have to worry about negative values of
r
with this transformation to
x
as our limits of integration for the integral on the left-hand side of the above equation are only over positive values of
r
. Furthermore, letting
y=2x
, our inner product becomes

(\phi^{\pm}_{1},\phi^{\pm}_{2})=-iZ_{1}Z_{2}\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}}\delta_{m_{1}m_{2}}[g_{2}\partial_{\eta}\bar{g}_{1}-\bar{g}_{1}\partial_{\eta}g_{2}]\|_{\eta=0}\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\pm iy(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}dy

But

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\pm iy(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}dy=2\pi\delta(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})

where the
\pm
sign has no impact on the form of the delta function on the right-hand side of this equation. Then,

(\phi^{\pm}_{1},\phi^{\pm}_{2})=-2\pi iZ_{1}^{2}\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}}\delta_{m_{1}m_{2}}\delta(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})[g_{1}\partial_{\eta}\bar{g}_{1}-\bar{g}_{1}\partial_{\eta}g_{1}]\|_{\eta=0}

In Appendix F of
[
1
]
, we found

\bar{g}\partial_{\eta}g-g\partial_{\eta}\bar{g}=-\frac{4i}{\pi}\sinh(\pi\alpha)\cosh(\pi\alpha)

So

(\phi^{\pm}_{1},\phi^{\pm}_{2})=8\sinh(\pi\alpha_{1})\cosh(\pi\alpha_{1})Z_{1}^{2}\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}}\delta_{m_{1}m_{2}}\delta(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})

where
\alpha\neq 0
as in the massive case. Thus, our normalization constant is found to be

Z_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\sinh(\pi\alpha)\cosh(\pi\alpha)}}
