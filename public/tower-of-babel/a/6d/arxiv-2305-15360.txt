On Program Completion, with an Application to the Sum and Product Puzzle
Vladimir Lifschitz University of Texas

Abstract
This paper describes a generalization of Clark’s completion that is applicable to logic programs containing arithmetic operations and produces syntactically simple, natural looking formulas. If a set of first-order axioms is equivalent to the completion of a program then we may be able to find standard models of these axioms by running an answer set solver. As an example, we apply this “reverse completion” procedure to the Sum and Product Puzzle.



1
Introduction

Program completion
[
2
,
11
]
is a transformation that converts
logic programs into sets of first-order formulas. The study of
completion improved our understanding of the relationship between these two
knowledge representation formalisms, and it has been used in the
design of answer set solvers
[
7
,
10
]
.

The definition of completion has been extended to
programs with operations on integers
[
4
]
. That
generalized completion process produces formulas in a two-sorted first-order
language
[
9
, Section 5]
.
In addition to “general” variables, which range over
both symbolic constants and (symbols for) integers, a formula in that
language may include also variables ranging over integers only.
The need to
use a language with two sorts is explained by the fact that function symbols
in a first-order language are supposed to represent total functions, and
arithmetic operations are not defined on symbolic constants.
In answer set programming languages, applying arithmetic operations to
symbolic constants is usually handled in a different way; when a rule is
instantiated, a substituition is not used unless it is
“well-formed”
[
1
, Section 3]
.

In this paper, the idea of a natural translation
[
8
]
is used
to define a version of generalized completion
that is limited to relatively simple (“regular”) rules but
produces simpler, and more natural-looking,
formulas. The modified completion operator is denoted by NCOMP, for
“natural completion.” For example, the natural
completion of the one-rule program

\verb|even(2*X) :- X = -10..10.|

(1)

in the input language of the answer set solver
clingo

[
6
]

is the sentence

\forall V(\emph{even\/}(V)\leftrightarrow\exists I(\overline{-10}\leq I\leq\overline{10}\,\land\,V=\overline{2}*I)).

(2)

Here
V
is a general variable,
I
is an integer variable, and

\overline{-10}
,
\overline{10}
,
\overline{2}
are
“numerals”—object constants representing integers.

Two theorems, stated in Section
3.4
and proved in
Section
5
, relate stable models
of a regular program to standard models of its completion
(standard in the sense that they interpret symbols related to
integers as usual in arithmetic). These theorems
extend well-known results due to François Fages
[
3
]
.

If a set of first-order axioms happens to be equivalent to the completion of a
regular program then we may be able to find standard models of these
axioms by running an answer set solver.
As an example, we apply this “reverse completion” procedure to a
formalization of the Sum and Product Puzzle
(
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_and_Product_Puzzle
).
From the perspective of knowledge representation
and automated reasoning, that puzzle presents a challenge: express it in a
formal declarative language so that the answer can be found, or at least
verified, by an automated reasoning tool. This has been accomplished
using first-order axioms for Kripke-style possible worlds and the
first-order theorem prover
fol

[
12
]
, and also using a
modal logic of public announcements and the epistemic model checker

demo

[
14
]
.
More recently, Jayadev Misra
proposed a simple first-order formalization
that does not refer to possible worlds
[
13
, Section 2.8.3]
.
In Section
4
we show that the
answer to the puzzle can be found by applying the reverse completion
process to a variant of his axiom set and then running
clingo
.

We assume that

Regular comparisons

are expressions of the forms

•

•

4
) is a

)

In this paper, integer variables are denoted by capital letters from the

3
Completion

3.1
Replacing variables

In the process of constructing the natural completion of a regular
program
\Pi
, the bodies of rules of
\Pi
will be transformed into
formulas over
\sigma_{\Pi}
. Since general variables are not allowed in
a formula in the scope of an arithmetic operation, this process has

A

of a regular rule

is a general variable

such that at least one occurrence of

in

scope of an arithmetic operation or is part of a comparison of the form

t_{1}=t_{2}\,..\,t_{3}
. For every regular rule

, choose a
function
f_{R}
that maps its critical variables to pairwise distinct
integer variables. This function

is extended
to other subexpressions of

as follows. For a tuple

is the tuple of terms over
\sigma_{\Pi}
obtained from

occurrences of every critical variable

Applying

to a regular atom and to a comparison that does not contain

to

\emph{not\/}\ A
is defined as the

, and the result of applying
f_{R}

to a comparison

is
f_{R}(t_{2})\leq f_{R}(t_{1})\leq f_{R}(t_{3})
.
Finally, applying
f_{R}

to the body

gives the formula

f_{R}(B_{1})\land f_{R}(B_{2})\land\cdots
.

For instance, if

is rule (

is critical,

maps

to some integer variable

It transforms the term

, and the body

\overline{-10}\leq I\leq\overline{10}

The puzzle

Two mathematicians, S and P, talk about two integers,
M
and
N
.
S knows the sum
M+N
, and P knows the product
M\times N
. Both S and P
know also that the integers are greater than 1; that their sum is not
greater than 100; and that
N
is greater than
M
.
The following conversation occurs:

1.

S says: P does not know
M
and
N
.

2.

P says: Now I know
M
and
N
.

3.

S says: Now I also know
M
and
N
.

What are
M
and
N
?

4.1
First-order axioms

Jayadev Misra’s approach to translating this puzzle into a
first-order language
[
13

involves the use of binary
predicates
b_{0},\dots,b_{3}
. The formula
b_{0}(M,N)
expresses that

M
,
N
was
considered a possible solution. This can be expressed by the formula

b_{0}(M,N)\leftrightarrow 1<M<N\land M+N\leq 100.

(11)

The formula
b_{1}(M,N)
expresses that
M
,
N
was considered a
possible solution at step 1, that is, after hearing the words
“P does not know
M
and
N
”; and so forth.

There are several ways to write axioms for
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
and
b_{3}
. One
possibility is described below.

We say that an integer
I
is
puzzling at time 0
if
is there is more than one way
to represent it as the product of two numbers
J
,
K
satisfying

b_{0}(J,K)
:

\begin{array}[]{r}\!\!\emph{puzzling\/}_{0}(I)\leftrightarrow\exists J_{1}K_{1}J_{2}K_{2}(b_{0}(J_{1},K_{1})\land b_{0}(J_{2},K_{2})\\
\land\,I=J_{1}\times K_{1}=J_{2}\times K_{2}\land J_{1}\neq J_{2}).\end{array}

(12)

We say that an integer
I
is
possibly easy
if it can be represented
as the sum of two numbers
J
,
K
satisfying
b_{0}(J,K)
such that

is not puzzling at time 0:

\emph{possibly\_easy\/}(I)\leftrightarrow\exists JK(b_{0}(J,K)\land I=J+K\land\neg\emph{puzzling\/}_{0}(J\times K)).

(13)

Then the assumption

“at Step 1, S knows that P does not know

and

”

can be expressed by the axiom

b_{1}(M,N)\leftrightarrow b_{0}(M,N)\land\neg\emph{possibly\_easy\/}(M+N).

(14)

We say that an integer

is
puzzling at time 1

is there is more than one way
to represent it as the product of two numbers
J
,
K
satisfying

:

\begin{array}[]{r}\!\!\emph{puzzling\/}_{1}(I)\leftrightarrow\exists J_{1}K_{1}J_{2}K_{2}(b_{1}(J_{1},K_{1})\land b_{1}(J_{2},K_{2})\\
\land\,I=J_{1}\times K_{1}=J_{2}\times K_{2}\land J_{1}\neq J_{2}).\end{array}

(15)

The assumption

“at Step 2, P knows
M
and
N
”

can be expressed by the axiom

b_{2}(M,N)\leftrightarrow b_{1}(M,N)\land\neg\emph{puzzling\/}_{1}(M\times N).

(16)

We say that an integer
I
is
puzzling at time 2

is there is more than one way
to represent it as the sum of two numbers
J
,

satisfying

\begin{array}[]{r}\!\!\emph{puzzling\/}_{2}(I)\leftrightarrow\exists J_{1}K_{1}J_{2}K_{2}(b_{2}(J_{1},K_{1})\land b_{2}(J_{2},K_{2})\\
\land\,I=J_{1}+K_{1}=J_{2}+K_{2}\land J_{1}\neq J_{2}).\end{array}

Finally, the assumption

“at Step 3, S knows

and

can be expressed by the axiom

Since axioms (

the predicates represented by the symbols

4.2
Reverse completion

Consider the regular program

5.1
Review: Completion according to Fandinno et al.

For any regular term
t
, the formula
\emph{val\,}_{t}({Z})
, where
Z
is a general
variable that does not occur in
t
, is defined recursively:

•

if
t
is a numeral or a variable then
\emph{val\,}_{t}({Z})
is
Z=t
;

•

if
t
is
t_{1}+t_{2}
then
\emph{val\,}_{t}({Z})
is

\exists IJ(Z=I+J\land\emph{val\,}_{t_{1}}({I})\land\emph{val\,}_{t_{2}}({J})),

and similarly for
t_{1}-t_{2}
and
t_{1}\times t_{2}
.

If
t
is a symbolic constant then
\emph{val\,}_{t}({Z})
stands for
Z=t
.
If
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
is a tuple of symbolic constants and regular terms, and

Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k}
are pairwise distinct general variables that do not occur in

t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
, then
\emph{val\,}_{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}}({Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k}})
stands for

\emph{val\,}_{t_{1}}({Z_{1}})\land\cdots\land\emph{val\,}_{t_{k}}({Z_{k}})
.
If
t
is
t_{1}\,..\,t_{2}
then
\emph{val\,}_{t}({Z})
stands for

\exists IJK(\emph{val\,}_{t_{1}}({I})\land\emph{val\,}_{t_{2}}({J})\land I\leq K\leq J\land Z=K).

The translation
\tau^{B}
transforms expressions in the body of a regular rule
into formulas as follows:

•

\tau^{B}(p({\bf t}))
is

\exists{\bf Z}(\emph{val\,}_{\bf t}({\bf Z})\land p({\bf Z}))
,
where
\bf Z
is a tuple of distinct program variables that do not occur
in
\bf t
;

•

\tau^{B}(\emph{not\/}\ p({\bf t}))
is

\exists{\bf Z}(\emph{val\,}_{\bf{}_{t}}({\bf Z})\land\neg p({\bf Z}))
;

•

\tau^{B}(t_{1}\prec t_{2})
is

\exists Z_{1}Z_{2}(\emph{val\,}_{t_{1},t_{2}}({Z_{1},Z_{2}})\land Z_{1}\prec Z_{2})
;

•

\tau^{B}(t_{1}=t_{2}\,..\,t_{3})
is

\exists Z_{1}Z_{2}(\emph{val\,}_{t_{1}}({Z_{1}})\land\emph{val\,}_{t_{2}\,..\,t_{3}}({Z_{2}})\land Z_{1}=Z_{2})
;

•

\tau^{B}(B_{1}\land B_{2}\land\cdots)
is

\tau^{B}(B_{1})\land\tau^{B}(B_{2})\land\cdots
.

The
completed definition of
p/n
in
\Pi

in the sense of Fandinno et al.
is the sentence over
\sigma_{\Pi}

constructed as follows.
Choose a tuple
\bf V
of
n
general variables that do not occur in
\Pi
.
For every rule
R
in the definition
D
of
p/n
in
\Pi
, by
F^{\prime}_{R}
we
denote the formula

\tau^{B}(\emph{Body\/})\land\emph{val\,}_{\bf t}({\bf V})

if
R
is (
5
), and

\tau^{B}(\emph{Body\/})\land\emph{val\,}_{\bf t}({\bf V})\land p({\bf V})

if
R
is (
6
).
The completed definition of
p/n
in
\Pi
according to Fandinno
et
al.
is the sentence

\forall{\bf V}\left(p({\bf V})\leftrightarrow\bigvee_{R\in D}\exists{\bf U}^{\prime}_{R}F^{\prime}_{R}\right),

where
{\bf U}^{\prime}_{R}
is the list of all variables occurring in
R
.

For example, the completed definition of one-rule program (

\forall V\left(\emph{even\/}(V)\leftrightarrow\exists X\left(\exists Z_{1}Z_{2}\left(Z_{1}=X\land\emph{val\,}_{\overline{-10}\,..\,\overline{10}}({Z_{2}})\land Z_{1}=Z_{2}\right)\land\emph{val\,}_{\overline{2}\times X}({V})\right)\right),

6
Discussion

In the presence of arithmetic operations, the completed definition
in the sense of Fandinno et al. is often longer and syntactically more complex
than the “natural” completed
definition introduced in Section
3.2
; compare, for instance,
formula (
21
) with (
2
). On the other hand, the approach of
Fandinno et al. is applicable to some types of rules that are accepted
by
clingo
but are not regular,
such as

p(1..8,1..8).
p(2*(1..8)).

These two rules can be easily rewritten as regular rules:

p(X,Y) :- X = 1..8, Y = 1..8.
p(2*X) :- X = 1..8.

Regularizing the rule

q(X/5) :- p(X).

in a similar way gives the rule

q(Y) :- p(X), X = 5*Y+Z, Z = 0..4.

that the current version of
clingo
considers unsafe.

The translation COMP is used in the design of the proof assistant

anthem

[
4
]
, and our Main Lemma shows that NCOMP
can be employed in the same way. In the process of interacting with
anthem
,
the user often has to read and modify completion formulas. A
version of
anthem
that implements
natural completion would make this work easier.

It would be interesting to extend the definition of NCOMP to programs
containing conditional literals
[
6
, Section 3.1.11]
. Such an
extension would make the reverse completion process applicable to some
formulas that are more complex syntactically than the current version. For
instance, it may be able to handle the formula

\begin{array}[]{l}b_{1}(M,N)\,\leftrightarrow\\
\qquad b_{0}(M,N)\land\neg\exists JK(b_{0}(J,K)\land M+N=J+K\land\neg\emph{puzzling\/}_{0}(J\times K)),\end{array}

which can replace axioms (
13
), (
14
) in the first-order
formalization of the Sum and Product Puzzle.
