Regularization by noise for rough differential equations driven by Gaussian rough paths
Rémi Catellier, Romain Duboscq

Abstract
. We consider the rough differential equation with drift driven by a Gaussian geometric rough path. Under natural conditions on the rough path, namely non-determinism, and uniform ellipticity conditions on the diffusion coefficient, we prove path-by-path well-posedness of the equation for poorly regular drifts. In the case of the fractional Brownian motion B H B^{H} for H > 1 4 H>\frac{1}{4} , we prove that the drift may be taken to be κ > 0 \kappa>0 Hölder continuous and bounded for κ > 3 2 − 1 2 ​ H \kappa>\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2H} . A flow transform of the equation and Malliavin calculus for Gaussian rough paths are used to achieve such a result.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we want to study a regularization by noise property for the following rough differential equation :

(1)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t}=b(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},

where
\mathbf{w}
is a (weakly) geometric rough path. We intend to show that under suitable conditions on
\sigma
and
\mathbf{w}
this equation is wellposed when
b
has (very) poor regularity properties.

This phenomenon of regularization by noise is now well-studied in several situations. A lot of work has been devoted to the additive case
\sigma\equiv 1
and for several kind of processes. One can think of the seminal work of
[
70
,
51
]
for strong solutions of this equation when
w
is a Brownian motion. In this additive case and when
w
is a Brownian motion, Davie
[
28
]
exhibits a new and stronger notion of uniqueness and proved that the previous equation has a strong solution and enjoys "path-by-path" uniqueness whenever
b\in L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
, whereas standard theory (with supporting counterexamples) requires
b
to be Lipschitz continuous. One can consult
[
66
]
for a deep discussion about notions of solutions in the additive case.

Since then, Davie’s work has led to a certain number of results in several directions which are usually done in the additive cases. One can take
w
to be a more general stochastic process and still has (even better) regularization by noise phenomenon. For example one can consider
w
to be a fractional Brownian motion
[
20
]
, a Lévy process, see
[
62
,
3
]
and the references therein, or a more general stochastic process
[
43
,
46
,
30
]
.
In a more general context some similar results when
w
is non random and as general as possible can be exhibited
[
39
,
20
]
and
[
38
,
64
]
.
This phenomenon of regularization by noise in a path-by-path manner was also study in other kind of problems, such as SPDEs
[
21
,
24
,
23
,
22
,
2
,
19
]
, mean field differential equations
[
41
,
9
]
, and in a mixed additive/multiplicative setting
[
10
,
40
]
.

A general strategy in this additive context is to look at the averaged field

t,x\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}b(x+w_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

and to study its space-time regularity properties. To do so, one could use for example an Itô-Tanaka trick (see
[
25
]
in the fractional Brownian motion setting) or the corresponding Kolmogorov equation
[
62
]
.
More recently, techniques involving stochastic sewing lemma
[
52
]
and/or properties of the occupation measure/local time of the process
[
46
]
where used. Once a space-time regularity of the averaged field is exhibited, one can then use some pathwise non-linear calculus
[
37
]
to conclude. We will see in the following how this strategy of proof is implemented in our setting.

In the full multiplicative case, there are very few results (up to our knowledge only two). First of all, one must make sense of the equation in a pathwise sense. To do so, one usually relies on rough path theory
[
55
,
27
,
56
,
5
,
45
,
35
,
36
]
.

Up to our knowledge in this setting, the first related result is due (again) to Davie
[
29
]
, where he studies Equation (
1
) where
\mathbf{w}
is the Stratonovitch Brownian rough path. In this setting, when
\sigma
is an invertible
C^{3}_{b}
matrix, he shows that uniqueness holds whenever
b\in L^{\infty}
. The strategy of the previous work is to consider a strong solution
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of the Stratonovitch SDE

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{r})\circ\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{r},

to use a Girsanov transform on

W_{t}=B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{r})^{-1}b(X_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

and to get back to the driftless Stratonovitch SDE on an equivalent probability measure
\tilde{\mathbbm{P}}
.

Y_{t}=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{t})\circ\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{r},.

Then the use of the Kolmogorov PDE related to the previous equation enables to prove that
Y
enjoys some regularizing properties. Finally, one can write
X=Y+Z
and
Z
solve the following equation

Z_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(Z_{r}+Y_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Hence, under
\tilde{\mathbbm{P}}
, one can use the regularizing properties of
Z
and prove the result.

It is not directly possible to implement such a strategy in our context. Firstly, one lacks of a suitable Girsanov transform in a general (Gaussian) rough path setting. Secondly, there is no Kolmogorov equation. Nevertheless, one can see two ingredients of the proof : going back to the driftless equation, and considering an ODE where the drift and the solution of the driftless equation appears.

The second result about uniqueness of SDE in a poor regularity setting is due to Athreya, Bhar and Shekhar
[
1
]
when
\mathbf{w}
is the fractional Brownian motion rough path (for
H>\frac{1}{3}
). There strategy is to make Lamperti transform to get back to an additive problem, and then to use the result of
[
20
]
. Whereas in dimension
d=1
this strategy works pretty well, in dimension
d\geq 2
one has to ask that
\sigma^{-1}
is conservative. This is a huge restriction, since one could expect that
\sigma
strictly elliptic should be sufficient.

While we were finishing the writing of this paper, a paper by Dareiotis and Gerencśer with similar results
[
26
]
came up. Note that the techniques involved are quite different, and we are able to handle more general cases (general Gaussian rough paths instead of only fractional Brownian motion). The price to pay in our case is a slightly worse condition on
\sigma
and on
b
, but a far better result on the flow of the equation.

Finally let us mention that in the rough path setting, some results have appeared considering non-Lipschitz drift
[
11
,
63
]
. These works focus on growth (non-linear damping) of the coefficients and wellposedness in that context. Furthermore, the
local
-Lipschitz continuity is always needed.

Our strategy to tackle the problem of uniqueness of Equation (
1
) with singular coefficients may be summarize as follow, and is inspired by the work of Davie
[
29
]
. In order to compensate the lack of a Girsanov transform in this setting, we rely on a flow transformation presented in the rough path setting in
[
63
]
. Indeed, it allows us to consider the regularization properties of the flow of the driftless equation. Furthermore, in order to replace the Kolmogorov equations arising in a Brownian context, we will use some Malliavin calculus in a Gaussian rough path setting, which have been developed by several authors
[
8
,
14
,
15
,
54
,

,
44
,

1.1.
Difficulties and extended plan of the paper

Let us emphasize the difficulties and achievements of the paper. We have divided the study of the uniqueness of solutions of Equation (
1
) in three parts.

The first one, in Section
2
, recalls the basic definitions and results about rough path theory. The main idea of this part is a development of an idea of
[
63
]
, and consists of proving an equivalence for the uniqueness of solution (in the sense of Davie) of the rough differential equation (
1
) with the uniqueness of the solution to the standard ODE

y^{\prime}(t)=D\varphi_{t}\big(y(t)\big)^{-1}b\big(\varphi_{t}(y(t)\big),

where
\varphi
is the flow arising from the rough differential equation when
b\equiv 0
. This is done in Theorem
2.8
. Somehow, this is a way of avoiding the Girsanov transform from Davie’s work
[
29
]
while still working with the solution of the driftless equation. However, this comes at a price: we need to work with the flow instead of a trajectory of the driftless system. Furthermore, we can see the appearance of the inverse of the Jacobian
(D\varphi)^{-1}
that we need to handle. Another objective of this part is to exploit an idea from
[
20
]
and to exhibit a criterion for this averaged field

t,x\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{r}(x)\big)^{-1}b\big(\varphi_{r}(x)\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

such that the ODE (and hence the RDE) has a unique solution.

The second part (Section
3
) focuses on the regularity of the averaged field whenever the flow
\varphi
has some stochastic properties, this is done in Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.7
. The idea here is to use Burkholder-Davis-Gundy (BDG) inequality for martingales in an infinite dimensional setting (namely in
L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
). This is quite close to the use of the stochastic sewing lemma in infinite dimensional spaces
[
53
]
. Nevertheless, since those work were developed in parallel we keep here with our presentation. Furthermore, since a BDG inequality in Banach spaces is not such a common topic, and in order to be as self contained as possible, we have included in the Appendix
C
for some key elements on this topic. Note that in this section we have proved a general Kolmogorov criterion for regularity of the averaged field in Besov spaces which could be interesting by itself.

The third part (Section
4
and
5
) focuses on a tool to avoid the use of Kolmogorov equations: the Malliavin calculus. Indeed, by using Section
3
, the main idea to deduce the regularizing properties of the averaged field constructed thanks to the flow of the driftless RDE is to have a integration by part formula, such that, formally

\mathbbm{E}[D\varphi_{t}(x)^{-1}\nabla b\big(\varphi_{t}(x)\big)|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\mathbbm{E}[b\big(\varphi_{t}(x)\big)H_{s,t}(x)|\mathcal{F}_{s}],

with

\|H_{s,t}(x)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\lesssim|t-s|^{-H},

for some
H>0
. This is precisely one of the key points of Malliavin calculus. Note that, in that setting, one has to focus on conditional Malliavin calculus. Hence, Section
4
recalls the standard facts and results about Malliavin calculus in a Gaussian context. In this section, some conditional integrations by part results are also proved. Section
5
focuses itself on Malliavin calculus for solutions of driftless rough differential equation driven by Gaussian rough paths.

Finally, in Section
6
we are able to prove the desired wellposedness result which can be stated as follow (see Theorem
6.3
and
6.1
for precise statements and assumptions):

Theorem
.

Let
2<p<4
. Let
\mathbf{W}
be a
p
-geometric Gaussian rough path such that its first component
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}=(\mathbf{W}^{1}_{0,t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is
\alpha
-locally non-determinism, namely

\inf_{0\leq s\leq t\leq T}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\var\left[W_{t}-W_{s}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[0,s]}\vee\mathcal{F}_{[t,1]}\right]=c_{W}>0.

Assume that
\sigma\in C^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
is uniformly elliptic. Let
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}
with
\kappa+\frac{1}{\alpha}>\frac{3}{2}
and
\kappa>0
. Then almost surely there is a unique solution of Equation (
1
). Furthermore the solutions of this equation are locally-Lipschitz continuous with respect to the initial condition.

Moreover, when
\mathbf{B}^{H}
is the geometric rough path above the fractional Brownian motion of Hurst parameter
\frac{1}{4}<H<\frac{1}{2}
, one can take
\alpha=2H
.

Finally, in order to be self-contained as possible, we have included some multidimensional Garsia-Rodemich-Rumsey inequalities in the Appendix (Appendix
D
) as well as some background on Besov spaces (Appendix
A
).

1.2.
Notations and preliminary

We gather here some useful notations and definitions for the rest of the article.

Throughout this paper, we consider
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbbm{P})
to be a filtered probability space. We recall that
\mathcal{S}
is the Schwartz space (see
[
69
]
) defined as

\mathcal{S}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}):\;\forall k\in\mathbbm{N},\|f\|_{\mathcal{S},k}<+\infty\right\},

where

\|f\|_{\mathcal{S},k}=\max_{|\beta|+|\gamma|\leq k}\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}|x^{\beta}D^{\gamma}f(x)|.

It is a topological vector space that becomes a Fréchet space when it is equipped with the distance

d_{\mathcal{S}}(\phi,\varphi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\frac{\|\phi-\varphi\|_{\mathcal{S},k}}{1+\|\phi-\varphi\|_{\mathcal{S},k}}.

Its continuous dual is
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
the space of tempered distributions and, moreover, we have the following Gelfand triple

\mathcal{S}\subset L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{S}^{\prime},

where
\mathcal{S}
is dense in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with respect to the weak topology of
L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. We denote
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the duality product which is an extension of the scalar product in
L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. The Besov spaces
B^{s}_{p,r}
(associated to the Littlewood-Paley blocks) are Banach spaces constructed by a completion in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

\displaystyle B_{p,r}^{s}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}):\|u\|_{B_{p,r}^{s}}:=\left(\sum_{j=-1}^{+\infty}2^{rjs}\|\Delta_{j}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{r}\right)^{1/r}<\infty\right\},

where
s\in\mathbbm{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
(see Appendix
A
for details and some results).

We also denote, for any
k\geq 0
,
C^{k}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{\ell})
the set of functions from
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
to
\mathbbm{R}^{\ell}
that are continuous,
k
times differentiable and whose derivatives are continuous. We denote
C^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{\ell})=\bigcap_{k=1}^{+\infty}C^{k}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{\ell})
. We denote by
C^{k}_{b}
(respectively
C^{\infty}_{b}
) the functions in
C^{k}
(respectively in
C^{\infty})
bounded together with all their derivatives.

Definition 1.1
.

A function from
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
to
\mathbbm{R}_{+}\backslash\{0\}
is called a weight. Let
f
be a function from
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
to

. Let us define the weighted sup norm of

\|f\|_{\infty,w}=\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x)|}{w(|x|)}.

Furthermore, we define

When there exists

such that

, we say that

has
\gamma
-power growth.
Let us now (with a slight abuse of notations) consider
w=(w_{k})_{k\geq 0}
to be a sequence of positive functions from
\mathbbm{R}_{+}
to
\mathbbm{R}_{+}\backslash\{0\}
. We say that

has
\gamma
-power growth if each

has a
\gamma
-power growth.

.

Let

\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{w}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{\ell}):=\{f:\mathbbm{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}^{\ell}\,:\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{w}}<+\infty\}.

To simplify notations, we denote
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{w}
when

, and we have (see Appendix
A
)
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}=B^{\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}
for all

. We also denote

C^{k}_{lin}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})=C^{k}_{(1+|\cdot|,1,\cdots,1)}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})

.

Note that when

, then
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{w}
denotes the set of Lipschitz continuous function with a growth rate

.

Furthermore, we also extend the definition of the Hölder space for functions of two variables. That is, for some Banach space

\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}=\mathcal{C}^{\nu}(\Delta^{2}_{T};\mathbbm{R}^{d})=\left\{\psi:\Delta^{2}_{T}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}:\;\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}}:=\sup_{(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}}\frac{\|\psi_{s,t}\|_{E}}{|t-s|^{\nu}}<\infty\right\},

2.
Rough differential equation with drift

In this section, we recall some basic facts about rough paths and rough differential equations. For more details, one can consult the seminal papers
[
55
,
27
,
45
]
, but also the monographs
[
56
,
35
,
36
]
. Note that an interesting perspective for flow driven by rough differential equations is given by Bailleul in
[
5
]
. We will focus here on the Davie’s definition of solutions of rough differential equations driven by Hölder (weakly) geometric
p
-rough path, with
p\geq 2
.

2.1.
Rough paths and differential equations in a nutshell

For any
N\in\mathbbm{N}
, a truncated tensor algebra
\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
is defined by

\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes k},

with the convention
(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 0}=\mathbbm{R}
. Let
(e_{1},\cdots,e_{d})
be the canonical basis of
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
, then for any
k\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
,

(e_{I})_{I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}}:=\big(e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{k}}\big)_{I=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{k})\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}}

is the canonical basis of
(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
, and for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
,

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{k\in\{0,\cdots,N\}}\mathbf{x}^{k}=x^{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,N\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{x}^{k,I}e_{I},

where
\mathbf{x}^{k}
is the projection of
\mathbf{x}
on the
k
-th tensor.

This space is equipped with a vector space structure as well as an operation
\otimes
defined by

(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y})^{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}(\mathbf{x}^{k-\ell})\otimes\mathbf{y}^{\ell},\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}),

In the end,
(\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}),+,\otimes)
is an associative algebra with unit element
\mathbf{1}\in(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 0}
.

For any
s<t
and
k\geq 1
, we define the simplex

\Delta^{k}_{s,t}=\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\in[s,t]^{k};u_{1}<\ldots<u_{k}\}.

We also denote
\Delta^{k}_{T}:=\Delta^{k}_{0,T}
. A multiplicative functional is a continuous map
\mathbf{w}:\Delta^{2}_{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
that verifies, for any
s<u<t
,

\mathbf{w}_{s,t}=\mathbf{w}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{u,t}

A fundamental example of such a map are the iterated integrals of a smooth paths
w:[0,T]\to\mathbbm{R}^{d}

\mathbf{w}^{k}_{s,t}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leq d}\left(\int_{\Delta^{k}_{s,t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w^{i_{1}}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w^{i_{k}}\right)e_{i_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{i_{k}},

where
(e_{1},\cdots,e_{d})
is the canonical basis of
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
and
k\geq 1
.
Then, we call the signature of
w
the mapping
S_{N}(w):\Delta^{2}_{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
given by

(s,t)\to\mathbf{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{s,t}^{k}.

It turns out that every signature is a multiplicative functional that belongs to
G^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
which is a subset of
\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
of group-like elements given by

G^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}):=\exp^{\oplus}(L^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})),

where
L^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
is the linear span of elements that can be written as a commutator
a\otimes b-b\otimes a
with
a,b\in\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. Furthermore, there is a Carnot-Caratheodory norm on
G^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
, denoted
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{CC}}
, which is homogeneous with respect to the natural scaling operation on
\mathcal{T}^{N}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. We can now introduce the notion of rough paths.

Definition 2.1
.

The space of weakly geometric
p
-rough paths is the set of multiplicative functionals
\mathbf{x}:\Delta^{2}\to G^{\lfloor p\rfloor}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
such that

\|\mathbf{w}\|_{p-var;[0,T]}:=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi([0,T])}\left(\sum_{[u,v]\in\pi}\|\mathbf{w}_{u,v}\|_{\mathrm{CC}}^{p}\right)^{1/p}<\infty,

where
\Pi([0,T])
is the set of all subdivisions of
[0,T]
. The space of geometric
p
-rough paths is the closure under
\|\cdot\|_{p-var;[0,T]}
of smooth signatures
\left\{S_{\lfloor p\rfloor}(w);\;w\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,T];\mathbbm{R}^{d})\right\}
.

In the following, we will also use the Hölder norm for rough paths that is given by, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\alpha\textrm{-H\"{o}l};[0,T]}:=\sup_{[s,t]\subset[0,T]}\frac{\|\mathbf{w}_{s,t}\|_{\mathrm{CC}}}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}.

2.2.
Flow generated by driftless differential equations driven by rough paths

In this subsection, we give known and basic properties about solutions of rough differential equations of the form

(2)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y_{t}=\sigma(y_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},\quad y_{0}=x,

where
\sigma:\mathbbm{R}^{d}\mapsto(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2}
is such that

\sigma(x)w=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(x)w^{i},

for any
w=(w^{i})_{i\in\{1,\cdots,d\}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
.
Following
[
6
]
we identify
\sigma_{i}
with a first order differential operator, namely for any smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sigma_{i}f(x)=Df(x)\sigma_{i}(x),\quad x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}.

For
k\geq 1
and
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}
we define the
k
-th order differential operator

\sigma_{I}=\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{k}}

via
\sigma_{I}f=\sigma_{i_{1}}(\cdots(\sigma_{i_{k}}f))
, with the slight abuse of notations
\sigma_{I}(x)=(\sigma_{I}id)(x)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
p\geq 2
,
T>0
,

\mathbf{w}
be a
\frac{1}{p}
-Hölder weakly geometric
p
-rough path and
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}
for some
\beta\geq\lfloor p\rfloor
.
A function
y\in\mathcal{C}^{\frac{1}{p}}\big([0,T];\mathbbm{R}^{d}\big)
is a solution (in the sense of Davie) to the driftless Rough Differential Equation (RDE) (
2
) if there exists
a>1
such that for all
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
,

\Bigg|(y_{t}-y_{s})-\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{w}^{k,I}_{s,t}\sigma_{I}(y_{s})\Bigg)\Bigg|\lesssim_{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\frac{1}{p}-\text{H\"{o}l};[0,T]},\sigma}|t-s|^{a}.

The existence, uniqueness and flow properties of solution to driftless RDE is given in
[
45
,
36
,
35
,
27
,
7
,
5
,
6

The proof here is simply an adaptation of the one from

Remark that

\mathbf{v}_{s,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor p\rfloor}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{w}_{s,t})^{\otimes k},

Corollary 2.5
.

Let

,

Proof.

Let us remark that under the previous hypothesis, both
\varphi
and

.

2.3.
Solution of RDE with drift : flow transform

We turn now to the study of rough differential equations with a drift:

(4)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t}=b(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},\quad x_{0}=x,\quad t\in[0,T].

In the spirit of Definition
2.2
, let us define the solutions of the rough differential equation with drift as follow

Definition 2.6
.

Let
p\geq 2
,
T>0
and
n\geq 0
. Let
b\in C^{0}_{b}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{d})
,
\sigma\in C^{\lfloor p\rfloor}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
and
\mathbf{w}
be a
\frac{1}{p}
-Hölder weakly geometric
p
-rough path. A path
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\in\mathcal{C}^{\frac{1}{p}}([0,T];\mathbbm{R}^{d})
is a solution to Equation (
4
) if
x_{0}=x
and there exists a constant
a>1
independent of
\mathbf{w}
such that for all
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
,

\Bigg|x_{t}-\Bigg(x_{s}+b(x_{s})(t-s)+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor p\rfloor}\sum_{I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}}\mathbf{w}^{k,I}_{s,t}\sigma_{I}(x_{s})\Bigg)\Bigg|\lesssim_{\mathbf{w},\sigma,b}|t-s|^{a}.

Remark 2.7
.

As proved in
[
6
]
and
[
18
]
(we also refer to
[
7
]
for the precise value of the following constants), the following equivalent notion of solutions can be used :
Let

be as in the previous Definition. Then
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a solution to Equation (

such that for all

\bigg|f(x_{t})-\bigg((bf)(x_{s})(t-s)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\

This point will be crucial in the following.

Several results concerning the (optimal) regularity of the drift are available in order to have existence and uniqueness for rough differential equations. Namely, in
[
36
]

[

[
11
]
(Lyapounov conditions on

[
63
]
) we give an equivalent formulation for the solutions of Equation (
4
).

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{v}^{k,I}_{s,t}\left((b\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})(t-s)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{w}^{k^{\prime},I^{\prime}}_{s,t}(\sigma_{I^{\prime}}\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})+R^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{v}^{k,I}_{s,t}(\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{w}^{k,I}_{s,t}(\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})

\displaystyle+(b\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})(t-s)+R_{s,t}+R^{\prime}_{s,t}.

Here, we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\big(|R_{s,t}|+|R^{\prime}_{s,t}|+|R^{\prime\prime}_{s,t}|\big)\lesssim_{\mathbf{w},\sigma,b,T}|t-s|^{a}

for a certain
a>1
. Furthermore, note that since
b
is bounded,
\sigma\in C^{2(\lfloor p\rfloor+1)}_{b}
and
\psi_{0,s}\in C^{\lfloor p\rfloor+1}_{lin}
, for all
k^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor+1\}
and for all
I,I^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}|(b\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})(t-s)|\lesssim|t-s|\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{w}^{k^{\prime},I^{\prime}}_{s,t}(\sigma_{I^{\prime}}\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})|\lesssim|t-s|^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{p}},

where the previous bounds depend on
\mathbf{w},\sigma,T,b
.
Hence, there exists
a>1
and
\tilde{R}_{s,t}
with
\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}|\tilde{R}_{s,t}|\lesssim|t-s|^{a}

such that

\displaystyle z(t)-z(s)=

\displaystyle(b\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})+\tilde{R}_{s,t}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
j\in\{1,\cdots,k-1\}\\
J\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{j}\\
J^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k-j}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{v}^{j,J}_{s,t}\mathbf{w}^{k-j,J^{\prime}}_{s,t}(\sigma_{J}\sigma_{J^{\prime}}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{v}^{k,I}_{s,t}(\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{w}^{k,I}_{s,t}(\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})+\tilde{R}_{s,t}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{1,\cdots,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\\
I\in\{1,\cdots,d\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\big(\mathbf{v}_{s,t}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{s,t}\big)^{k,I}\big(\sigma_{I}\psi_{0,s}\big)(x_{s}).

Note that since
\mathbf{v}_{s,t}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{s,t}=\mathbf{1}
, we have for all
k\geq 1
,
(\mathbf{v}_{s,t}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{s,t})^{k}=0
.

Furthermore
D\psi_{0,s}(x)=\big(D\varphi_{0,s}(\psi_{0,s}(x))\big)^{-1}
, hence

(b\psi_{0,s})(x_{s})=\big(D\varphi_{0,s}(\psi_{0,s}(x_{s}))\big)^{-1}b\left(\varphi_{0,s}(\psi_{0,s}(x_{s}))\right)=\big(D\varphi_{0,s}(z(s))\big)^{-1}b\left(\varphi_{0,s}(z(s))\right).

Finally, for
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
small enough

z(t)-z(s)=\big(D\varphi_{0,s}(z(s))\big)^{-1}b\left(\varphi_{0,s}(z(s))\right)+\tilde{R}_{s,t}.

We also know that
s\mapsto\big(D\varphi_{0,s}(z(s))\big)^{-1}b\left(\varphi_{0,s}(z(s))\right)
is a continuous functions. Hence, thanks to a standard Riemann sum argument,

z(t)-z(s)=\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)^{-1}b\left(\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

and
(z(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a solution of Equation (
5
), and
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}=(\varphi_{0,t}(z(t))\big)_{t\in[0,T]}
, which ends the proof.
∎

2.4.
Rough differential equation with drift : the averaged field

Whenever
\sigma
is regular (and bounded), and
b
is a bit more than continuous, Theorem
2.8
allows us to focus, in order to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to rough differential equations with drift

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t}=b(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},\quad x_{0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d},\quad t\in[0,T],

to existence and uniqueness of solutions, the ordinary differential equation

z^{\prime}(t)=\big(D\varphi_{0,t}(z(t))\big)^{-1}b\big(\varphi_{0,t}(z(t))\big),\quad z_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d},\quad t\in[0,T],

where
\varphi
is the flow generated by the driftless RDE (
2
).

The previous equation may be rewritten in its integral form as

(9)

z(t)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)^{-1}b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,\quad t\in[0,T].

In order to study wellposedness of the previous equation, we rely on ideas from
[
20
]
and we will try to exhibit a general criterion of regularity of the space-time averaged fields defined in the following definition :

Definition 2.9
.

Let
p\geq 2
,
T>0
,
\sigma\in C^{\lfloor p\rfloor+2}_{b}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
and
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}_{b}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. We define the averaged field
\mathcal{T}b
of
b
along the flow generated by the driftless rough differential equation driven by
\mathbf{w}
has the following space-time vector field :

(10)

(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathcal{T}b_{t}(x):=\int_{0}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(x)\big)^{-1}b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(x)\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

For all
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
we denote by
\mathcal{T}b_{s,t}=\mathcal{T}b_{t}-\mathcal{T}b_{s}
.

When
\sigma=1
and
\mathbf{w}
is a Brownian motion (or fractional Brownian motion), one can see (in
[
28
]
and
[
20
]
for example) that the previous averaged field enjoys better regularity properties (in space) than
b
. On can also consult
[
39
]
and
[
38
]
for a systematic study of the previous averaged field in a more general context.

In our setting, one generally use non-linear Young integration theory (see
[
20
]
and
[
37
]
) to link the space-time regularity of the averaged field (
10
) and the wellposedness of the integral equation (
9
). Nevertheless the examples that our techniques will allow us to treat (Gaussian rough path, see Section
4
) will not allow us to go beyond
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}
for
\kappa>0
. In that case, Equation (
9
) always makes sense, it has a solution (thanks to Peano’s existence theorem) and one only needs to focus on uniqueness. The following theorem is our main result in a general context :

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
p\geq 2
,
T>0
,
\kappa>0
,
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}^{d})
,
\sigma\in C^{2(\lfloor p\rfloor+1)}_{b}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2})
and
\mathbf{w}
be a
\frac{1}{p}
-Hölder weakly-geometric
p
-rough path. Assume that there exists
1\geq\nu>\frac{1}{2}

\alpha>0
such that
\kappa+\frac{1}{\alpha}>2-\nu
, a weight
w_{0}
(see Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
) such that

\|\mathcal{T}b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa+\frac{1}{\alpha}}_{w_{0}}}<+\infty.

Then there is a unique solution to Equation (
9
).

Furthermore, let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
and
b_{1},b_{2}
satisfying the previous hypothesis, and let
z_{1}
(respectively
z_{2}

9

and
x=x_{1}
(respectively

and

Then there is a non decreasing positive function

,

,

,
\|b_{1}\|_{\infty}
,

,

and

such that

\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty,[0,T]}\leq K\big(|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|\big)\big(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\|\mathcal{T}b_{1}-\mathcal{T}b_{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{1}_{w_{0}}}).

In this setting, there is a unique solution to the RDE with drift (
4
) in the sense of the Definition
2.6

The proof is an adaptation of

20

Lemme 2.11
.

Let
V
be a Banach space and
A:\Delta^{2}_{T}\mapsto V

and

such that for all

\mathcal{A}_{0}=0
and for all

\mathcal{A}_{t}-\mathcal{A}_{s}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\Pi([s,t])\\

The end of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.10

Lemme 2.12
.

Let us suppose that the hypothesis of Theorem
2.10
are satisfied.
Let
(z(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a continuous functions. Then for all
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
,

\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)^{-1}b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\Pi([s,t])\\
|\pi|\to 0\end{subarray}}\sum_{(u,v)\in\pi}\mathcal{T}b_{u,v}(z(u)).

Proof.

First, let us remark that thanks to Theorem
2.3
, for all
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
,
x\mapsto\varphi_{s,t}(x)
and
x\mapsto\big(D\varphi_{s,t}(x)\big)^{-1}
are Lipschitz continuous uniformly in time, and
x\mapsto\big(D\varphi_{s,t}(x)\big)^{-1}
is bounded. Furthermore, since
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}_{b}
for some
\alpha>0
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r-\mathcal{T}b_{s,t}(z(s))\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)^{-1}\Big(b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)-b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z(s))\big)\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\int_{s}^{t}\Big(\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z(r))\big)^{-1}-D\varphi_{0,r}(z(s))\big)^{-1}\Big)b\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z(s))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mathbf{w}}\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}_{b}}\int_{s}^{t}|z(r)-z(s)|^{\kappa}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Since
z
is uniformly continuous on
[0,T]
the result follows by standard arguments.
∎

Until the end of this Section, we write
\kappa^{\prime}=\kappa+\frac{1}{\alpha}
.

Lemme 2.13
.

Again, let us work in the setting of Theorem
2.10
.
Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{b}([0,T];\mathbbm{R}^{d})

K_{0}:\mathbbm{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}_{+}\backslash\{0\}

\|\mathcal{T}b_{1}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{w_{0}}}

\|\mathcal{T}b_{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{w_{0}}}
,

\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z_{1}(r))\big)^{-1}b_{1}\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z_{1}(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r-\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z_{2}(r))\big)^{-1}b_{2}\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z_{2}(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\\
-\big((\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{s}(z_{1}(s))-(\mathcal{T}b_{2})_{s}(z_{2}(s))\big)\bigg|\\

where
\tilde{w_{0}}(x)=(1+x)w_{0}(x)
,

\llbracket z\rrbracket_{\nu,[s,t]}=\sup_{(r,r^{\prime})\in\Delta^{2}_{s,t}}\frac{|z(r^{\prime})-z(r)|}{|r^{\prime}-r|^{\nu}}.

Proof.

Let us define for
(s,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
,

A_{s,t}=(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{s,t}(z_{1}(s))-(\mathcal{T}b_{2})_{s,t}(z_{2}(s)).

Thanks to the previous lemma, we already know that each integral is the limit of the Riemann sum involving

\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z_{1}(r))\big)^{-1}b_{1}\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z_{1}(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r-\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z_{2}(r))\big)^{-1}b_{2}\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z_{2}(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\\
=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\Pi([s,t])\\
|\pi|\to 0\end{subarray}}\sum_{(u,v)\in\pi}A_{u,v}.

Furthermore, we have for
(s,u),(u,t)\in\Delta^{2}_{T}
,

\displaystyle A_{s,u}+A_{u,t}-A_{s,t}=

\displaystyle\Big((\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}(z_{1}(u))-(\mathcal{T}b_{2})_{u,t}(z_{2}(u))\Big)-\Big((\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}(z_{1}(s))-(\mathcal{T}b_{2})_{u,t}(z_{2}(s))\Big)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}(z_{1}(u))-(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}(z_{2}(u))

\displaystyle-\Big((\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\big(z_{1}(u)-z_{2}(u)+z_{2}(s)\big)-(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\big(z_{2}(s)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle+(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\big(z_{1}(u)-z_{2}(u)+z_{2}(s)\big)-(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\big(z_{1}(s)\big)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})_{u,t}\big(z_{2}(u)\big)-\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})_{u,t}\big(z_{2}(s)\big).

\displaystyle A_{s,u}+A_{u,t}-A_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(D(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\Big(\lambda\big(z_{1}(u)-z_{2}(u)\big)+z_{2}(u)\Big)-D(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\Big(\lambda\big(z_{1}(u)-z_{2}(u)\big)+z_{2}(s)\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\big(z_{1}(u)-z_{2}(u)\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(D(\mathcal{T}b_{1})_{u,t}\Big(\lambda\big((z_{1}-z_{2})(u)-(z_{1}-z_{2})(s)\big)+z_{2}(s)\Big)\bigg)\big((z_{1}-z_{2})(u)-(z_{1}-z_{2})(s)\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle|A_{s,u}+A_{u,t}-A_{s,t}|\lesssim

\displaystyle\|D(\mathcal{T}b_{1})\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}-1}_{w_{0}}}w_{0}(\|z_{1}\|_{\infty,[0,T]}+\|z_{2}\|_{\infty,[0,T]})\llbracket z_{2}\rrbracket^{\kappa^{\prime}-1}_{\mathcal{C}^{1}_{T}}

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\times\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty,[s,t]}|t-s|^{\nu+\kappa^{\prime}-1}

and

Remark 2.14
.

Thanks to the previous proof, one can choose
K_{0}
as follow :

K_{0}(x)=c\big(1+\|\mathcal{T}b_{1}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{w_{0}}}+\|\mathcal{T}b_{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{w_{0}}}\big)(1+x){w}_{0}\big(x\big),

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

Let us consider directly that we have
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
and
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
as in the theorem.
Let us remark that thanks to Peano’s existence theorem, there exists

and

solutions of the corresponding integral equations. Furthermore, we have

z_{1}(t)=x_{1}+\int_{s}^{t}\big(D\varphi_{0,r}(z_{1}(r))\big)^{-1}b_{1}\big(\varphi_{0,r}(z_{1}(r))\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Since
b_{1}
and

are bounded, there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\mathbf{w},b_{1},\sigma,T)

and the same holds for

(with the corresponding constant
C_{2}

K=c\Big(\big(1+\|\mathcal{T}b_{1}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{w_{0}}}+\|\mathcal{T}b_{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{w_{0}}}\big)\tilde{w}_{0}\big(2(C_{1}+C_{2})(1+|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|)\Big),

Hence, for all

\big|\big(z_{1}(t)-z_{2}(t)\big)-\big(z_{1}(s)-z_{2}(s)\big)\big|\leq K(|z_{1}(s)-z_{2}(s)|+\|\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}L^{\infty}_{w_{0}}})|t-s|^{\nu}\\
+K\big(\|\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{1}_{w_{0}}}+\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty,[s,t]}+\llbracket z_{1}-z_{2}\rrbracket_{\nu,[s,t]}\big)|t-s|^{a}.

Here the second line comes directly from the previous Lemma and Remark
2.14

\llbracket z_{1}-z_{2}\rrbracket_{\nu,[s,t]}\leq\frac{1}{3(t-s)^{\nu}}(\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty,[s,t]}+\|\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}L^{\infty}_{w_{0}}})\\

as well as

\llbracket z_{1}-z_{2}\rrbracket_{\nu,[s,t]}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{(t-s)^{\nu}}\right)(\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty,[s,t]}+\|\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{1}_{w_{0}}}),

This gives

\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty,[s,t]}\leq 3\left(|z_{1}(s)-z_{2}(s)|+\|\mathcal{T}(b_{1}-b_{2})\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{1}_{w_{0}}}\right).

One can iterate the previous bound on small intervals to deduce the desired estimate.

Note that the uniqueness of the solution follows directly by setting

and

3.
Kolmogorov type theorem for averaged fields

In the previous section, and especially in Theorem
2.10
, we exhibit a general criterion in terms of the averaged field
\mathcal{T}b
such that Equation (
4
) has a unique solution. Nevertheless, we observe that without using any additional property of the flow
\varphi
, even in the case when

Hence, in this section, we will focus ourselves on the action a the random flow

In order to be as general as possible, we want to take

as generic as possible. In order to do so, we rely on estimates of the type

\|\mathcal{T}^{(D\varphi)^{-1},\varphi}f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}([0,T];B^{\alpha}_{p,r}))}\leq C\|f\|_{B^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{\ell,m}}

and any

\phi:[0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}^{d}\to(\mathbbm{R}^{d})^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+G_{p,t^{j}_{k}}\left(1+\mathbbm{E}\left[\|\det(J_{\varphi^{-1}})\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}^{d})}|\mathcal{F}_{t^{j}_{k}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+G_{p,t^{j}_{k+1}}\left(1+\mathbbm{E}\left[\|\det(J_{\varphi^{-1}})\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}^{d})}|\mathcal{F}_{t^{j}_{k+1}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\right).

Note that thanks to the hypothesis and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\sup_{j\geq 0}\sup_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n_{j}\}}\mathbbm{E}[(\kappa^{j}_{k})^{q}]<+\infty.

Hence, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}[\|M^{j}_{n_{j}}-M^{j}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}\|M^{j}_{k+1}-M^{j}_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-s)^{\varepsilon q}\|\Delta_{j}f\|^{q}_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\left(\frac{(t-s)^{1-\varepsilon}}{n_{j}}+2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{H}j}\right)^{q}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}(\kappa_{k}^{j})^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-s)^{\varepsilon q}\|\Delta_{j}f\|^{q}_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\left(\frac{(t-s)^{1-\varepsilon}}{n_{j}}+2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{H}j}\right)^{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n_{j}-1}\mathbbm{E}[(\kappa^{j}_{k})^{q}]^{\frac{2}{q}}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-s)^{\varepsilon q}\|\Delta_{j}f\|^{q}_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\left(\frac{(t-s)^{1-\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}+\sqrt{n_{j}}2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{H}j}\right)^{q}.

We now optimize the estimate on
n_{j}\geq 1
. We choose

n_{j}=\frac{(t-s)^{1-\varepsilon}}{2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{H}j}}+\iota,

for a certain
\iota\in[0,1]
. This yields, since
2^{-\frac{j}{H}}\leq(t-s)
,

\sqrt{n_{j}}2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{H}j}=(t-s)^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}}2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}j}\sqrt{1+\iota\frac{2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{H}j}}{(t-s)^{1-\varepsilon}}}\leq\sqrt{2}(t-s)^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}}2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}j},

and, also,

\frac{(t-s)^{1-\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}\leq(t-s)^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}}2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}j}.

Thus, we deduce that

\mathbbm{E}\left[\|M^{j}_{n_{j}}-M^{j}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}\lesssim(t-s)^{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}2^{-\frac{(1-\varepsilon)}{2H}j}.

Using the fact that
(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}=M^{j}_{n_{j}}
as well as the estimate (
20
), we obtain for all
j\geq\mathfrak{j}
,

(21)

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}\lesssim(t-s)^{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}2^{-j\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}.

It follows from (
18
) that the previous estimate holds for all
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and any
j\geq-1
. Thus, we deduce that for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}f)_{s,t}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq-1}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(t-s)^{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}}\sum_{j=-1}^{+\infty}2^{-j\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}=(t-s)^{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}}\|f\|_{B^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}_{p,1}}.

It follows from Theorem
D.1
that, for all
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
and
f\in B^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}_{p,1}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega;C^{\nu}([0,T];L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbbm{E}\left[\int_{[0,T]^{2}}\frac{\|(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}f)_{s,t}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}}{|t-s|^{q\frac{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]^{1/q}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{B^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}_{p,1}}\int_{[0,T]^{2}}|t-s|^{q\frac{\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\|f\|_{B^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}_{p,1}},

with
\nu=\frac{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}-\frac{1}{q}>0
, which is exactly the theorem in the case
p<+\infty
when we remind that whenever

, one has for

\|f\|_{B^{\alpha-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}_{p,1}}=\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{j(\alpha-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})j}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\leq\left(\sum_{j\geq-1}2^{-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\frac{r}{r-1}j}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\left(\sum_{j\geq-1}2^{\alpha rj}\|\Delta_{j}f\|^{r}_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\\
\lesssim_{\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}\|f\|_{B^{\alpha}_{p,r}}.

The

The proof is pretty much the same as the proof in the previous case. We provide the main arguments for completeness. Let
0\leq s<t\leq T
be fixed. We denote

and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]

\left|(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(x)\right|\leq(t-s)^{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}}2^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}j}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}^{d})}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}.

Moreover, it follows from Lemma
3.4

\displaystyle\left|\mathbbm{E}[(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(x)|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\right|\lesssim(t-s)^{\eta}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}2^{-\frac{1-\eta}{H}j}G_{\infty,s}.

,

Lemme 3.6

Let

\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi})_{t}f(x)=\sum_{\gamma\leq\beta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\mu|\leq|\gamma|\\
\upsilon\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma,\mu}\end{subarray}}C_{\beta,\gamma,\mu,\upsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}^{\Lambda_{\gamma,\upsilon}(\varphi)\partial^{\beta-\gamma}\phi,\varphi}\big(\partial^{\mu}f\big)\right)_{t}(x),

where
\Lambda_{\gamma,\upsilon}(\varphi):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\delta|\leq|\gamma|\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}(\partial^{\delta}\varphi_{j}(r,x))^{\upsilon_{\delta_{j}}}
and

\mathcal{N}_{\gamma,\mu}:=\left\{\upsilon\in\mathbbm{N}^{d}:\;\sum_{1\leq|\delta|\leq|\gamma|}\upsilon_{\delta_{j}}=\mu_{j},\;\textrm{for any}\;1\leq j\leq d,\;\mathrm{and}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\delta|\leq|\gamma|\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}\delta\upsilon_{\delta_{j}}=\gamma\right\},

Proof.

The proof of the lemma is a straightforward consequence of Leibniz’s rule of differentiation, which yields

\partial^{\beta}\phi(r,x)b(\varphi(r,x))=\sum_{\gamma\leq\beta}C^{1}_{\beta,\gamma}\partial^{\beta-\gamma}\phi(r,x)\partial^{\gamma}(b(\varphi(r,x))),

for some constants

and Faà di Bruno’s formula, which gives

\partial^{\gamma}(b(\varphi(r,x)))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\mu|\leq|\gamma|\\
\upsilon\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma,\mu}\end{subarray}}C^{2}_{\mu,\upsilon}\partial^{\mu}b(\varphi(r,x))\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\delta|\leq|\gamma|\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}(\partial^{\delta}\varphi_{j}(r,x))^{\upsilon_{\delta_{j}}},

for some constants
\{C^{2}_{\upsilon,\mu}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\mu|\leq|\gamma|\\
\upsilon\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma,\mu}\end{subarray}}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{+}

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
r\in[1,+\infty]
,
n\geq 1
and
p\in[2,+\infty]
,
q\geq 2
and
(\phi,\varphi)
such that, for any

,
\gamma\leq\beta

Then, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
,
\kappa\in(0,n]
,
f\in B^{\kappa-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2H}}_{p,r}
,
\eta>d/q
and such that,

, the following estimate holds

\|\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\nu}([0,T];E_{p,\kappa}))}\lesssim\|f\|_{B^{\kappa-\frac{1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}{2H}}_{p,r}},

with
E_{p,\kappa}=B^{\kappa}_{p,p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
if

and
E_{\infty,\kappa}=\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}_{w}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
if

, where

is a weight with

and

Proof.

As usual, one needs to distinguish between
p=+\infty
and
2\leq p<+\infty
.

The
p<+\infty
case.

Thanks to Lemma
3.6
and (
21
), we obtain that, for any
\beta\in\mathbbm{N}^{d}
,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
,
q\geq 2
and
j\geq-1

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\gamma\leq\beta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|\mu|\leq|\gamma|\\

In particular, we deduce that, for any

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}\right\|_{B_{p,p}^{2k}}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

, any

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(x)-\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(x)-\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(y)\right|^{q}\right]^{1/q}\leq|x-y|^{\theta}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\nabla\partial^{\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\phi,\varphi}\Delta_{j}f)_{s,t}(z)\right|^{2\theta q}\right]^{1/(2q)}

4.
Malliavin Calculus

In order to be self-contained, and in the spirit of Section
2
we recall in this section some facts about Malliavin calculus. Most of them are well-known facts, but Subsection
4.4
yields some non-standard estimates that we will use in the following. In particular, we will derived a (not so surprising) conditional integration by part formula, which will allow us to check whenever a flow generated by a RDE driven by a Gaussian rough path satisfy the conditions of Theorem
3.3
or Corollary
3.7
.
One may consult

[
57
,
59
]
for more details.

4.1.
Isonormal Gaussian processes

Definition 4.1
.

(Isonormal Gaussian process) An Isonormal Gaussian process is the set of:

(1)

a real and separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
whose scalar product is denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
and its norm as
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
,

(2)

a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbbm{P})
,

(3)

a real-valued Gaussian process
W:h\in\mathcal{H}\to W(h)
,
i.e.

(W(h))_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
is a family of centered Gaussian random variables such that
\mathbbm{E}[W(h)W(g)]=\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, for any
h,g\in\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 4.2
.

(1)

By Kolmogorov’s theorem, given only
\mathcal{H}
, we can construct
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbbm{P})
and
W
satisfying the above conditions.

(2)

The mapping
h\to W(h)
is linear.

The two following examples will be of interest below. The first one is a classical construction linked to the standard Brownian motion.

Example 4.3
.

(Isonormal Gaussian process associated to the Brownian motion) Let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion defined on its canonical probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbbm{P})
. Let
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{+};\mathbbm{R}^{d})
and, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let us define
W(h)
as the Wiener integral

W(h)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{+\infty}h_{k}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B^{k}_{s}.

The second example deals with a generic construction of an isonormal Gaussian process associated to a generic Gaussian process. This example will be of primer importance in the following, as we will always work in this specific setting.

Example 4.4
.

Let us consider any vector-valued continuous centered Gaussian process
W=(W^{1},\cdots,W^{d})
with iid components and let us construct a corresponding (vector-valued) isonormal process. Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
d\geq 1
. We denote the covariance function of
W
as

R_{W}(t,s):=\mathbbm{E}\left[W_{t}^{j}W_{s}^{j}\right].

Fix
T>0
. We now consider
\mathcal{E}
the set of step functions on
[0,T]

\mathcal{E}:=\left\{f=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}:\;m\in\mathbbm{N}^{*},\,(a_{k})_{1\leq k\leq m}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m-1},\,(t_{k})_{1\leq k\leq m+1}\in\Pi([0,T])\right\}.

We define
\mathcal{H}
as the closure of
\mathcal{E}
with respect to the scalar product

\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]},\mathbbm{1}_{[0,s]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=R_{W}(t,s),

which leads to the norm defined on
\mathcal{E}
by

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{m}a_{k}a_{\ell}\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t_{k}]},\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t_{\ell}]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{m}a_{k}a_{\ell}R_{W}(t_{k},t_{\ell}).

We can extend the linear mapping
\mathcal{K}:\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}\in\mathcal{E}\to W^{j}(t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to an isometry
\mathcal{K}:h\in\mathcal{H}\to W^{j}(h)
since

\mathbbm{E}\left[\mathcal{K}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]})\mathcal{K}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,s]})\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[W^{j}_{t}W^{j}_{s}\right]=R_{W}(t,s)=\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]},\mathbbm{1}_{[0,s]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Then,
\mathcal{K}:h\in\mathcal{H}\to W^{j}(h)
is an isonormal Gaussian process. Let us construct the vector-valued isonormal Gaussian process
\mathcal{K}:h\in\mathcal{H}^{\oplus d}\to W(h)=(W^{1}(h_{1}),\cdots,W^{d}(h_{d}))\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. In the following, we will treat the case
n=1
without loss of generality.

Remark 4.5
.

(1)

An interesting instance is, for example, the case where
W=B^{H}
the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
and covariance

R_{B^{H}}(t,s)=\mathbbm{E}[B^{H}_{t}B^{H}_{s}]=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right).

(2)

For
W_{0}=0
(and
R_{W}(0,0)=0
), we have, by
[

(26)

\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(t)h_{2}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R_{W}(t,s),

whenever the
2
d Young integral on the right-hand-side is well-defined.

Fix
[a,b]\subset[0,T]
for the rest of the section.
Since
\mathcal{H}
is the closure of indicator functions, for any
[a,b]\subset[0,T]
, we can also define
\mathcal{H}([a,b])
the restriction of
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}([0,T])
to
[a,b]
. Thus, for almost every
s\in[a,b]^{c}
,
h(s)=0
for
h\in\mathcal{H}([a,b])
. Furthermore, we directly deduce that, for any
h,g\in\mathcal{H}([a,b])
,

\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}=\langle h\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b]},g\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

In particular, if
h\in\mathcal{H}([a,b])
then
h=h\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b]}\in\mathcal{H}
. For any set
B
which is a finite union of intervals of
[0,T]
, we denote
\mathcal{F}_{B}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u,v]}),\,[u,v]\subset B\}
.

4.2.
The Malliavin derivative

We consider the set of smooth cylindrical fields
S
given by

S=\{f(W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{1},v_{1}]}),\ldots,W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{n},v_{n}]})):\;f\in C^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{n}),\;[u_{k},v_{k}]\subset[0,1],\;\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\;n\geq 0\},

where
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
denotes the set of functions from
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
to
\mathbbm{R}
that are infinitely differentiable and with all their partial derivatives having polynomial growth. Furthermore, we denote
C^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
the subset of
C^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
where all the partial derivatives are bounded. The smooth random variables associated to this space
C^{\infty}_{b}
is denoted by
S_{b}
.
For any set
B
which is a finite union of intervals of
[0,T]
, we also denote
S_{b}(B)
(resp.
S(B)
) the natural restriction of
S_{b}=S_{b}(B)
(resp.
S=S(B)
) to
B
.
We note that
S_{b}(B)
(and
S(B)
) is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{B},\mathbb{P})
.

Definition 4.6
.

For any
F=f\big(W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{1},v_{1}]})\cdots W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{n},v_{n}]})\big)\in S
with
f\in C^{\infty}_{p}
, we define the Malliavin derivative of
F
restricted on
[a,b]\subset[0,1]
as

D_{[a,b]}F=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}f(W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{1},v_{1}]}),\ldots,W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{n},v_{n}]}))\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{k},v_{k}]}\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b]},

which takes values in
\mathcal{H}([a,b])
.

We can directly check that, for any
F,G\in S
, we have the "chain rule" relation

D_{[a,b]}(FG)=GD_{[a,b]}F+FD_{[a,b]}G.

Let us also introduce
the so-called
Cameron-Martin space
denoted
\mathcal{H}_{1}
which is defined as the completion of

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\left\{t\to\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}R_{W}(t_{k},t):\;n\in\mathbbm{N},\,(a_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n},\,(t_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}\subset[0,T]\right\},

with respect to the scalar product

\langle R_{W}(t,\cdot),R_{W}(s,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=R_{W}(t,s).

Then, the mapping defined by

(27)

\mathcal{R}:\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}\in\mathcal{E}\to R_{W}(t,\cdot)\in\tilde{\mathcal{E}}

can be extended to an isometry
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}
since

\langle\mathcal{R}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}),\mathcal{R}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,s]})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=R_{W}(t,s)=\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]},\mathbbm{1}_{[0,s]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

In the same fashion as for
\mathcal{H}
, we define
\mathcal{H}_{1}([a,b])
the restriction of
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}([0,T])
to
[a,b]
.

We have the following integration by parts formula.

Lemme 4.7
.

For any
F,G\in S
and
h\in\mathcal{H}([a,b])
, we have

\mathbbm{E}[G\langle D_{[a,b]}F,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}]=-\mathbbm{E}[F\langle D_{[a,b]}G,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}]+\mathbbm{E}[FGW(h)].

Furthermore, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.8
.

For any
p\geq 1
, the linear operator
D_{[a,b]}
is closable from
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{[0,1]},\mathbb{P})
to
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{[0,1]},\mathbb{P};\mathcal{H}([a,b]))
.

We can iteratively define, for any
m\geq 1
,

D_{[a,b]}^{m}F=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{m}=1}^{n}\partial_{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{m}}f(W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{1},v_{1}]}),\ldots,W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{n},v_{n}]}))\left(\otimes_{\ell=1}^{m}(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{k_{\ell}},v_{k_{\ell}}]}\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b]})\right)

which takes values in
\mathcal{H}([a,b])^{\otimes m}
. Moreover, following Proposition
4.8
, we can close
D^{m}_{[a,b]}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

For any
m\geq 1
and
p\geq 1
, the linear operator
D^{m}_{[a,b]}
is closable from
S
to
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}([a,b])^{\otimes m})
.

By using the same notation for its extension, the domain of the operator
D^{m}_{[a,b]}
is the space
\mathbbm{D}^{m,p}_{[a,b]}
which is the completion of
S
with respect to the norm

\|F\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{m,p}_{[a,b]}}:=\left(\mathbbm{E}[|F|^{p}]+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbbm{E}[\|D^{k}_{[a,b]}F\|_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])^{\otimes k}}^{p}]\right)^{1/p}.

In the previous norm, in the case of multidimensional processes, we remark that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])^{\otimes k}}
is not an operator norm but the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.
For
E
a Banach space,
we also denote by
\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{m,p}(E)
the
E
-valued random variables that belong in
\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{m,p}
. Furthermore, for any
k,p\geq 1
, we have Hölder’s inequality

\|FG\|_{\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{k,p}}\leq\|F\|_{\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{k,r}}\|G\|_{\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{k,q}},

for any
r,q\geq 1
such that
1/r+1/q=1/p
.
We now state a chain rule with respect to the Malliavin derivative.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
g\in C^{1}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R})
with bounded derivatives,
p\geq 1
and
F=(F^{1},\ldots,F^{d})
be a random vector such that
F^{k}\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{1,p}
for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Then,
g(F)\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{1,p}
and

D_{[a,b]}(g(F))=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}g(F)D_{[a,b]}F^{k}.

4.3.
The divergence operator

The divergence operator
\delta_{[a,b]}
is the adjoint of the derivative
D_{[a,b]}
. In fact,
\delta_{[a,b]}
is an unbounded operator from
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}([a,b]))=\mathbbm{D}^{0,2}_{[a,b]}(\mathcal{H}([a,b]))
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
such that:

(1)

its domain
\textrm{Dom}(\delta_{[a,b]})
is the set of random variables
u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H}([a,b]))
such that, for all
F\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{1,2}
,

\left|\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\right]\right|\leq c_{u}\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(2)

for any
u\in\textrm{Dom}(\delta_{[a,b]})
, we have
\delta_{[a,b]}(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and the following duality relation holds, for all
F\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{1,2}
,

\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[F\delta_{[a,b]}(u)\right].

We have the following result concerning the continuity of
\delta
.

Theorem 4.11
.

For any
p>1
and
m\geq 1
, the operator
\delta_{[a,b]}
is continuous from
\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{m-1,p}(\mathcal{H}([a,b]))
to
\mathbbm{D}^{m,p}_{[a,b]}
. That is, the following inequality holds for any
u\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{m-1,p}(\mathcal{H}([a,b]))
,

\|\delta(u)\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{m-1,p}_{[a,b]}}\leq c_{m,p}\|u\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{m,p}_{[a,b]}(\mathcal{H}([a,b]))}.

This yields in particular that
\mathbbm{D}^{m,p}_{[a,b]}(\mathcal{H}([a,b]))\subset\textrm{Dom}(\delta_{[a,b]})
for any
p>1
and
m\geq 1
.

For any random variable
F\in\mathbbm{D}^{1,2}_{[a,b]}
, we know that
D_{[a,b]}F
is a stochastic process in
\mathcal{H}([a,b])
that we can denote
(D_{[a,b],t}F)_{t\in[a,b]}
which is defined almost surely with respect to the measure
\lambda\times\mathbbm{P}
(where
\lambda
is the usual Lebesgue measure). With this, we can deduce a
local property
of the derivative operator.

Lemme 4.12
.

Let
[u,v]\subset[0,T]
and
F\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{1,2}\cap L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{[u,v]},\mathbbm{P})
. Then we have

D_{[a,b],s}F(\omega)=0,

for
(\lambda\times\mathbbm{P})
-almost every
(s,\omega)\in([a,b]\backslash[u,v])\times\Omega
.

Proof.

We have, for any
F\in S_{b}([u,v])
,

D_{[a,b],s}F=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}f(W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{1},v_{1}]}),\ldots,W(\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{n},v_{n}]}))\mathbbm{1}_{[u_{k},v_{k}]}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b]}(s),

which yields the desired result since, for
1\leq k\leq n
,
[u_{k},v_{k}]\subset[u,v]
.

∎

4.4.
Conditional Integration by parts formula

In the previous section, we have seen that
\delta_{[a,b]}
is the adjoint operator of
D_{[a,b]}
and, as such, it satisfies a duality relation. It turns out that this duality relation also holds under a conditional expectation. That is,
for any
F\in S
and
G_{[a,b]^{c}}\in S([a,b]^{c})
, with
[a,b]^{c}=[0,T]\backslash[a,b]
, we can see that, thanks to Lemma
4.12
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]G_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}G_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbbm{E}\left[F\langle DG_{[a,b]^{c}},u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\right]+\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}(FG_{[a,b]^{c}}),u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[FG_{[a,b]^{c}}\delta_{[a,b]}(u)\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[\mathbbm{E}\left[F\delta_{[a,b]}(u)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]G_{[a,b]^{c}}\right].

This yields in fact the following duality relation under a conditional expectation since
S
(resp.
S([a,b]^{c})
) is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
(resp.
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}},\mathbb{P})
)

(28)

\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle DF,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[F\delta_{[a,b]}(u)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right].

We can now proceed to prove a conditional integration by parts formula.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
F\in\mathbbm{D}^{1,2}_{[a,b]}
,
G
be a random variable and
u
be an
\mathcal{H}([a,b])
-valued random variable such that

\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\neq 0\quad\textrm{and}\quad G\frac{u}{\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}}\in\textrm{Dom}(\delta_{[a,b]}).

Then, for any function
f\in C^{1}
with bounded derivatives, we have that

\mathbbm{E}\left[f^{\prime}(F)G\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[f(F)H_{[a,b]}(F,G)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right],

where

H_{[a,b]}(F,G):=\delta_{[a,b]}\left(G\frac{u}{\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}}\right).

Proof.

We apply Proposition
4.10
to deduce that

f^{\prime}(F)=\left\langle D_{[a,b]}(f(F)),\frac{u}{\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}.

Then, it follows from (
28
) that

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[f^{\prime}(F)G\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}(f(F)),u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}(\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])})^{-1}G\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[\langle D_{[a,b]}(f(F)),Gu(\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])})^{-1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[f(F)\delta_{[a,b]}\left(Gu(\langle D_{[a,b]}F,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])})^{-1}\right)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right],

which is the desired result.
∎

We may now introduce the Malliavin matrix associated to a random vector.

Definition 4.14
.

Let
p>1
and
F=(F^{1},\ldots,F^{d})
be a random vector such that
F^{k}\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{1,p}
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. The covariance matrix
\gamma_{F}
associated to
F
is defined as

\gamma_{F,[a,b]}:=(\langle D_{[a,b]}F^{i},D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])})_{(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{2}}.

We then have the following Lemma
[
58
, Lemma 7.2.3]
:

Lemme 4.15
.

Let
\gamma
be a
d\times d
random matrix such that
\textrm{det}(\gamma)>0
almost surely and
\textrm{det}(\gamma)^{-1}\in\cap_{p\geq 1}L^{p}(\Omega)
. We assume that
(\gamma)_{i,j}\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{\infty}:=\cap_{p\geq 1}\cap_{k\geq 1}\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{k,p}
for any
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{2}
. Then,
(\gamma^{-1})_{i,j}\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{\infty}
, for any
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{2}
, and

D_{[a,b]}(\gamma^{-1})_{i,j}=-\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{m}(\gamma^{-1})_{i,k}(\gamma^{-1})_{\ell,j}D_{[a,b]}\gamma_{k,\ell}.

Thanks to the Malliavin matrix, we define a
nondegeneracy condition
for random vectors.

Definition 4.16
.

We say that a random vector
F=(F^{1},\ldots,F^{d})
, whose components are in
\mathbbm{D}^{\infty}_{[a,b]}
, is nondegenerate if the Malliavin matrix
\gamma_{F,[a,b]}
is invertible almost surely and

\textrm{det}(\gamma_{F,[a,b]})^{-1}\in\cap_{p\geq 1}L^{p}(\Omega).

We now state a more general conditional integration by parts result for nondegenerate random vectors.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
F=(F^{1},\ldots,F^{d})
be a nondegenerate random vector,
G\in\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{\infty}
and
g\in C^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. Then, for any multiindex
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{k}
,
k\geq 1
, there exists an element
H_{\alpha,[a,b]}(F,G)\in\mathbbm{D}^{\infty}_{[a,b]}
such that

\mathbbm{E}\left[\partial^{\alpha}g(F)G\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]=\mathbbm{E}\left[g(F)H_{\alpha,[a,b]}(F,G)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right].

The variable
H_{\alpha,[a,b]}(F,G)
is given recursively by

H_{\alpha,[a,b]}(F,G)=H_{\alpha_{k},[a,b]}(F,H_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1}),[a,b]}(F,G))

where, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

H_{i,[a,b]}(F,G)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\delta_{[a,b]}\left(G(\gamma_{F,[a,b]}^{-1})_{i,j}DF^{j}\right).

Proof.

It follows from the chain rule that, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,

\langle D_{[a,b]}(g(F)),D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}g(F)\langle D_{[a,b]}F^{i},D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}g(F)(\gamma_{F,[a,b]})_{i,j}.

Thus, we obtain that

\partial_{i}g(F)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\gamma_{F,[a,b]}^{-1})_{i,j}\langle D_{[a,b]}(g(F)),DF^{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}.

By exploiting the duality relation (
28
), this gives

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\partial_{i}g(F)G\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\langle D_{[a,b]}(g(F)),\sum_{j=1}^{d}G(\gamma_{F,[a,b]}^{-1})_{i,j}D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}([a,b])}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\left[g(F)H_{i,[a,b]}(F,G)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{[a,b]^{c}}\right].

Thanks to Lemma
4.15
, we can deduce that
\gamma_{F,[a,b]}\in\mathbbm{D}^{\infty}_{[a,b]}
. Furthermore, by Theorem
4.11
, this yields that
H_{i,[a,b]}(F,G)\in\mathbbm{D}^{\infty}_{[a,b]}
if
G\in\mathbbm{D}^{\infty}_{[a,b]}
. From here, we can proceed by induction and deduce the result.
∎

We finally give an estimate for the random variables
H_{\alpha,[a,b]}
.

Lemme 4.18
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
p>1
. For any
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{k}
, we have

\|H_{\alpha,[a,b]}(F,G)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{\alpha,d,p}\|G\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{k,q}_{[a,b]}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\gamma_{F,[a,b]}^{-1})_{\alpha_{\ell},j}D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\right\|_{\mathbbm{D}_{[a,b]}^{k-\ell+1,r_{\ell}}}

where
1/p=1/q+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}1/r_{\ell}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.11
and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|H_{\alpha,[a,b]}(F,G)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{p,d}\left\|H_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1}),[a,b]}(F,G)\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\gamma_{F,[a,b]}^{-1})_{\alpha_{k},j}D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\right\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{1,p}_{[a,b]}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{p,d}\left\|H_{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1}),[a,b]}(F,G)\right\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{1,q}_{[a,b]}}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\gamma_{F,[a,b]}^{-1})_{\alpha_{k},j}D_{[a,b]}F^{j}\right\|_{\mathbbm{D}^{1,r}_{[a,b]}},

where
1/p=1/q+1/r
, which yields the desired result by induction.
∎

5.
Malliavin calculus and rough paths

The idea of this Section is to use Malliavin calculus (and especially the conditional bounds proved in the previous subsection) to derive some nice bounds on flow generated by rough differential equations. In order to do so, we will need some properties of the Cameron-Martin spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

5.1.
Embedding of the spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}

In this section, we give some embedding of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
into the space of continuous functions with finite
p
-variation, with
p>0
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
f:[0,T]\to\mathbbm{R}^{d}
be a continuous function. For any
p>0
, we define its
p
-variation on
[a,b]\subset[0,T]
as

\|f\|_{p-var;[a,b]}=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi([a,b])}\left(\sum_{[u,v]\in\pi}|f(v)-f(u)|^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where we remind that
\Pi([a,b])
is the set of all subdivisions of
[a,b]
. The set of continuous functions with finite
p
-variation on
[a,b]
is denoted
\mathcal{C}^{p-var}([a,b];\mathbbm{R}^{d})=\mathcal{C}^{p-var}([a,b])
.

A similar, mixed
(p,q)
-variation notion also exists for continuous functions on
[0,T]^{2}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
R:[0,T]^{2}\to\mathbbm{R}^{d}
be a continuous function. For any
p,q>0
, we define its
(p,q)
-variation on the square
[a,b]\times[c,d]
as

\|R\|_{(p,q)-var;[a,b]\times[c,d]}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi_{1}\in\Pi([a,b])\\
\pi_{2}\in\Pi([c,d])\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{[u_{2},v_{2}]\in\pi_{1}}\left(\sum_{[u_{1},v_{1}]\in\pi_{2}}|\square_{[u_{1},v_{1}]\times[u_{2},v_{2}]}R|^{p}\right)^{q/p}\right)^{1/q},

where
\square_{[u_{1},v_{1}]\times[u_{2},v_{2}]}R=R(v_{1},v_{2})-R(v_{1},u_{2})-R(u_{1},v_{2})+R(u_{1},u_{2})
. The set of continuous functions with finite
(p,q)
-variation on
[a,b]\times[c,d]
is denoted
\mathcal{C}^{(p,q)-var}([a,b]\times[c,d];\mathbbm{R}^{d})=\mathcal{C}^{(p,q)-var}([a,b]\times[c,d])
.

We also denote
\|R\|_{p-var;[a,b]^{2}}=\|R\|_{(p,p)-var;[a,b]^{2}}
as well as
\mathcal{C}^{p-var}([a,b]^{2}):=\mathcal{C}^{(p,p)-var}([a,b]^{2})
.

Remark 5.3
.

We can see that

\|R\|_{p\vee q-var;[a,b]^{2}}\leq\|R\|_{(p,q)-var;[a,b]^{2}}\leq\|R\|_{p\wedge q-var;[a,b]^{2}}.

Furthermore, we have the following definition.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
\rho\in[1,2)
and
[a,b]\subset[0,T]
. We say that
R
has finite Hölder-controlled
\rho
-variation on
[a,b]
if
\|R\|_{\rho-var;[a,b]^{2}}<+\infty
and if the following estimate holds, for any
[s,t]\subset[a,b]
,

\|R\|_{\rho-var;[s,t]^{2}}\lesssim(t-s)^{1/\rho}.

We are now in position to state a first assumption on the process
W
that we will consider on the rest of the section.

Assumption 5.5
.

The process
W
is a
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
-valued continuous centered Gaussian process starting at
0
with iid components and covariance
R_{W}
which belongs in

Burkolder Davis Gundy inequality in Lebesgue spaces

In this section, we recall some standard results about martingales in Banach spaces, and the corresponding inequalities. We refer to Pisier
[

]

[

]
and Veraar and coauthors

]
for proofs.

The aim of this section is to give an almost self-contain proof of Burkolder-Davis-Gundy inequality in infinite dimensional Banach spaces, especially in
L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
spaces. The idea of the proof is to use the Kahane-Khinchine inequality, and UMD properties via smoothness of spaces.

C.1.
Discrete Fourier transform and Kahane-Khinchine inequality

We recall some of standard feature about the Fourier transform on the hypercube.

Let
\Omega_{n}=\{-1,1\}^{n}
. We endowed
\Omega_{n}
with the uniform measure
\mu_{n}
, such that for a function
f:\Omega_{n}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}
we have

\int_{\Omega_{n}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=\mathbbm{E}[f(X)]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\Omega_{n}}f(x)

where
X=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
is a vector of i.i.d. random variables such that
\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=1)=\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Remark that for any
Y\in L^{2}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n}),\mu_{n})
a unique function
f:\Omega_{n}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}
exists such that
Y=f(X)
. Hence
L^{2}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n}),\mu_{n})
is a finite dimensional vector space of dimension
2^{n}
. For
Y_{1}=f_{1}(X),Y_{2}=f_{2}(X)\in L^{2}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n}),\mu_{n})
let us write the standard inner product as

\langle Y_{1},Y_{2}\rangle=\mathbbm{E}[f_{1}(X)f_{2}(X)].

Furthermore let us define for
S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
, we define

X_{S}=\prod_{l\in S}X_{l},\quad X_{\emptyset}=1.

Remark that
\langle X_{S},X_{S}\rangle=1
and for
S^{\prime}\neq S
we have
\langle X_{S},X_{S}^{\prime}\rangle=0
. Hence
X_{S}
is a orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n}),\mu_{n})
. Furthermore for
Y\in L^{2}(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n}),\mu_{n})
and
S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
, we define

\hat{Y}(S)=\langle Y,X_{S}\rangle=\mathbbm{E}[YX_{S}],

such that

Y=\sum_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}\hat{Y}(S)X_{S},

and

\mathbbm{E}[YZ]=\sum_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}\hat{Y}(S)\hat{Z}(S).

Note that we have the two following identities :

\mathbbm{E}[Y^{2}]=\sum_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}\hat{Y}(S)^{2}

and

\mathbbm{E}[Y]=\hat{Y}(\emptyset).

Theorem C.1

(Khinchine-Kahane Inequality, via Szarek’s proof,
[
12
, Theorem 5.20]
)
.

Let
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
be a normed vector space. Then for any
p\in[1,+\infty]
, there exists a constant
C_{p}
such that for any
n\geq 2
and for any
b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}\in B
, we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}X_{i}\right|^{p}_{B}\right]\leq C_{p}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}X_{i}\right|_{B}\right],

where
X=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
is an iid random vector with
\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=1)=\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
k\geq 1
such that
k\leq p<k+1
.

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}X_{i}\right|^{p}_{B}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}X_{i}\right|^{k+1}_{B}\right]^{\frac{1}{k+1}}\\
\leq C_{k+1}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}X_{i}\right|_{B}\right].

It is enough to prove the Theorem for
k+1
.
Let us define

Z=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}X_{j}\right|_{B},

Let us define for
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,

X^{(i)}=(X^{(i)}_{1},\cdots X^{(i)}_{n})=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{i-1},-X_{i},X_{i+1},\cdots,X_{n}),

such that
X^{(i)}\overset{(d)}{=}X
, and

Z^{(i)}=\left|\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}b_{j}X^{(i)}_{j}\right|_{B}

and finally

\bar{Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z^{(i)}.

The core of the proof is then the following : we will give some lower and upper bounds for
\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}\bar{Z}]
with respect to
\mathbbm{E}[Z]
,
\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k+1}]
. To do so, we will give a direct lower bound to
\bar{Z}
. For the upper bound, we will dramatically use the Fourier transform.

Let us focus first on the lower bound. We have

\bar{Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X^{(i)}_{j}b_{j}\right|_{B}\\
\geq\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X^{(i)}_{j}b_{j}\right|_{B}\\
=(n-2)Z.

Since
Z^{k}
is non negative, we have

(n-2)\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k+1}]\leq\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}\bar{Z}].

Let us now focus on the upper bound. First note that if we define for
S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
and
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,

X_{S}^{(i)}=\prod_{l\in S}X^{(i)}_{l},\quad X_{\emptyset}^{(i)}=1,

then
(X_{S}^{(i)})_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}
is a orthonormal basis of
L^{2}
and furthermore note that
(Z^{(i)},X^{(i)}_{S})
has the same distribution as
(Z,X_{S})
for all
i
and all
S
.
Note also that

X_{S}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}X_{S}&\text{if }i\notin S\\
-X_{S}&\text{if }i\in S\end{cases}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{Z}}(S)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{E}[Z^{(i)}X_{S}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\notin S}\mathbbm{E}[Z^{(i)}X^{(i)}_{S}]-\sum_{i\in S}\mathbbm{E}[Z^{(i)}X^{(i)}_{S}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n-2|S|)\mathbbm{E}[ZX_{S}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n-2|S|)\hat{Z}(S).

Not also that if there exists
m
such that
|S|=2m+1
, since
X\overset{(d)}{=}-X
we have

\hat{Z}(S)=\mathbbm{E}[ZX_{S}]=\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}-X_{j}b_{j}\right|_{B}\prod_{l\in S}(-X_{l})\right]=-\hat{Z}(S),

and

\hat{Z}(S)=0.

Hence we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}\bar{Z}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}\widehat{Z^{k}}(S)\hat{\bar{Z}}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}(n-2|S|)\widehat{Z^{k}}(S)\hat{Z}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\widehat{Z^{k}}(\emptyset)\hat{Z}(\emptyset)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}\\
|S|=2m\\
S\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(n-2|S|)\widehat{Z^{k}}(S)\hat{Z}(S)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}]\mathbbm{E}[Z]+(n-4)\sum_{S\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}\widehat{Z^{k}}(S)\hat{Z}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}]\mathbbm{E}[Z]+(n-4)\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k+1}].

Putting upper and lower bounds together, we get

\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k+1}]\leq 2\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}]\mathbbm{E}[Z].

By a direct induction, we have

\mathbbm{E}[Z^{k}]^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq 2^{1-\frac{1}{k}}\mathbbm{E}[Z],

which is the wanted bound with
C_{k}=2^{1-\frac{1}{k}}

∎

C.2.
UMD spaces

Definition C.2

(
[
61
, Definition 8.7]
,
[
48
, Definition 4.2.1]
)
.

Let
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
]
be a Banach space and
p\in(1,+\infty)
. We say that
B
as the
Unconditionnal Martingale Difference
-
p
(
\text{UMD}_{p}
) property if there exists a constant

K_{p}(B)>0
such that for any filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0},\mathbbm{P})
and any
B
value martingale
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with
p
moments, any
N\geq 1
and any
\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{N}\in\{-1,1\}
,

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\varepsilon_{k}(M_{k+1}-M_{k})\right|^{p}_{B}\right]\leq K_{p}(B)^{p}\mathbbm{E}[|M_{N}-M_{0}|_{B}^{p}]

We say that
B
as the UMD property if
B
is
\text{UMD}_{p}
for all
p\in(0,+\infty)
.

The following theorem is a fundamental tool in the theory of UMD spaces. We refer to
[
61
]
for a proof of it (and much more).

Theorem C.3

(
[
61
, Theorem 8.12]
)
.

Let
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
be a Banach space with the
\text{UMD}_{p}
property. Then
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
has the UMD property.

A direct consequence of the remark after
[
61
, Definition 8.7]
and the previous theorem is the following fact :

Proposition C.4
.

The finite dimensional vector space
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
endowed with its standard euclidean norm has the UMD property.

Proof.

The proof relies on the classical discrete Burkolder Davis Gundy (BDG) inequality. Indeed let
(M_{n})_{n}
be an
L^{p}
martingale in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. Let
\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{N}\in\{-1,1\}
,
M^{\varepsilon}_{0}=0
and for
N\in\{1,\cdots,N\}

M^{\varepsilon}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{k}(M_{k+1}-M_{k}).

Then
(M^{\varepsilon}_{n})_{n\in\{0,\cdots,N\}}
is a martingale, and we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|M^{\varepsilon}_{N}\right|^{p}\right]\lesssim\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left|M^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}-M^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\\
=\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left|M_{k+1}-M_{k+1}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\lesssim\mathbbm{E}[|M_{N}-M_{0}|^{p}],

were we have applied twice the BDG inequality.

∎

Theorem C.5

(
[
61
, Corollary 8.19]
)
.

Let
p\in(1,+\infty)
, let
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
be an Banach space with the UMD and let
(A,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measured space. The space
L^{p}(A;B)
is a UMD space.

Proof.

The proof follows easily from the previous results. Indeed, it is enough to prove that
L^{p}(A;B)
has the
\text{UMD}_{p}
property. Let
(M_{n})_{n}
be a
L^{p}
martingale with value in
L^{p}(A;B)
. Remark that for
\mu
-almost all
x\in A
,
\big(M_{n}(x)\big)_{n}
is a
B
value martingale. Furthermore, thanks to the Fubini property, we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[\|M_{n}\|_{L^{p}(A;B)}^{p}\right]=\int_{A}\mathbbm{E}[|M_{n}(x)|_{B}^{p}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Hence for
\mu
-almost all
x
,
\mathbbm{E}[|M_{n}(x)|_{B}^{p}]<+\infty
and
\big(M_{n}(x)\big)_{n}
is a
L^{p}
martingale with value in
B
. Hence for all
\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
, we have for almost all
x\in A
and all
N\geq 1

\mathbbm{E}[|M_{N}^{\varepsilon}(x)|^{p}_{B}]\leq K_{p}(B)^{p}\mathbbm{E}[|M_{N}(x)-M_{0}(x)|^{p}_{B}].

Hence, we have

\mathbbm{E}[\|M^{\varepsilon}_{N}\|_{L^{p}(A;B)}^{p}]=\int_{A}\mathbbm{E}[|M^{\varepsilon}_{N}(x)|^{p}_{B}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\\
\leq K_{p}(B)^{p}\int_{A}\mathbbm{E}[|M_{N}(x)-M_{0}(x)|^{p}_{B}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=K_{p}(B)^{p}\mathbbm{E}[\|M_{N}-M_{0}\|_{L^{p}(A;B)}^{p}].

Hence,
L^{p}(A;B)
is
\text{UMD}_{p}
and then UMD.
∎

Corollary C.6
.

Let
p,q\in(1,+\infty)
and let
s\in\mathbbm{R}
. Then

is UMD.

Proof.

Is previously it is enough to prove that

is
\text{UMD}_{q}
. Furthermore, since

is UMD we have for al

and all

,

\mathbbm{E}[\|M^{\varepsilon}_{N}\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}}^{q}=\sum_{j\geq-1}2^{sjq}\mathbbm{E}[\|\Delta_{j}M^{\varepsilon}_{N}\|^{q}_{L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R})}]=K_{q}(L^{p}(\mathbbm{R}^{d};\mathbbm{R}))^{q}\mathbbm{E}[\|M_{N}-M_{0}\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}}^{q}],

C.3.
Banach space, smoothness and type

Definition C.7

(
[
61
, Definitions p-108]
)
.

Let
p\in(1,2]
and
B,|\cdot|_{B})
be a Banach space. We say that
B
is of type
p
if there exists a constant
M_{p}
such that for every
N\geq 0
, every iid sequence
X_{1}\cdots,X_{n}
with
\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=1)=\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
and every
b_{1},\cdots,b_{N}\in B
, we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}b_{k}\right|_{B}\right]\leq M_{p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}|b_{k}|_{B}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Let us first give an example :

Proposition C.8

(
[
61
, Proposition 4.33]
)
.

Let
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
with its Euclidean norm. Then
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
is of type
2
. More generally any Hilbert space is of type
2
.

Proof.

The proof is quite straightforwerd. Let us denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot,\rangle
the inner product. We have for
N\geq 1
,
b_{1},\cdots,b_{N}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
and
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
a sequence of iid random variables with
\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=1)=\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}b_{k}\right|^{2}\right]=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}\left[X_{k}X_{l}\right]\langle b_{k},b_{l}\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}|b_{k}|^{2}.

Applying the Khinchine-Kahane inequality, we have the result.
∎

Theorem C.9

(
[
61
, Proposition 4.34]
)
.

Let
(A,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measured space and let
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
be a type
p
Banach space with
p\in(1,2]
. Let
q\in[1,+\infty)
. Then
L^{q}(A;B)
is of type
p\wedge q
.

Proof.

Take
N\in\mathbbm{N}
,
b_{1},\cdots,b_{N}\in L^{q}(A;B)
and
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
a sequence of iid random variables with
\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=1)=\mathbbm{P}(X_{1}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Applying twice the Khinchine-Kahane inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}b_{k}\right\|_{L^{q}(A;B)}\right]\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}b_{k}\right|^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{A}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}b_{k}(x)\right|_{B}^{q}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{A}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}b_{k}(x)\right|_{B}\right]^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{A}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|b_{k}(x)|_{B}^{p}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

where we have used the fact that
(B,|\cdot|_{B})
is of type
p
in the last inequality.

Now, let us suppose that
q\leq p
. We have for every
N\geq 1
and
y_{1},\cdots,y_{N}\in\mathbbm{N}
,

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}|y_{i}|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}|y_{i}|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Finally we have

\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}b_{k}\right\|_{L^{q}(A;B)}\right]\lesssim\left(\int_{A}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|b_{k}(x)|_{B}^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\lesssim\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|b_{k}\|_{L^{q}(A;B)}^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Hence, in that case we have proved
L^{q}(A;B)
which is of
q
type.

Let us suppose that
p\leq q
. Note that we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}b_{k}\right\|_{L^{q}(A;B)}\right]\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(\left\{\int_{A}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|b_{k}(x)|_{B}^{p}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right\}^{\frac{p}{q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\int_{A}|b_{k}(x)|_{B}^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|b_{k}\|_{L^{q}(A;B)}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

In that case
L^{q}(A;B)
if of
q
type, which ends the proof.
∎

Corollary C.10

Appendix D
The multi-parameter and multi-dimensionnal Garsia-Rodemich-Rumsey inequality

Let us recall the standard Garsia-Rodemich-Rumsey inequality
[
42
]
.

Theorem D.1
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
\alpha>p^{-1}
and
f\in C([0,1])
. Then, the following inequality holds

(55)

|f(t)-f(s)|\lesssim_{\alpha,p}\kappa_{f}|s-t|^{\alpha-1/p}

for all
t,s\in[0,1]^{2}
, where

\kappa_{f}=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\frac{|f(u)-f(v)|^{p}}{|u-v|^{\alpha p+1}}dudv\right)^{1/p}.

We can extend the previous result to the case
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times B(0,R))
where
B(0,R)\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
is a closed ball of radius
R>0
. By denoting, for any
x,y\in[0,1]^{d}
and
s,t\in[0,1]
,

\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)=f(s,x)-f(s,y)-f(t,x)+f(t,y),

we have the following result.

Corollary D.2
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
\alpha_{1}>1/p
,
\alpha_{2}>d/p
and
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times B(0,R))
. Then, we have the following estimate

(56)

|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|^{p}\lesssim_{d,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},p}\kappa_{f}|s-t|^{\alpha_{1}p-1}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}p-d}

for all
t,s\in[0,1]
and
x,y\in B(0,R)
, where

\kappa_{f}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}\times B(0,R)^{2}}\frac{|\square_{(u,z)}f(v,w)|^{p}}{|u-v|^{\alpha_{1}p+1}|z-w|^{\alpha_{2}p+d}}dudvdzdw.

Proof.

We essentially use the arguments from
[
68
]
and
[
47
]
. Let
\{(E_{j},d_{j})\}_{1\leq j\leq m}
be a family of separable metric space each endowed with a finite doubling measure
\upsilon_{j}
defined on the Borel sets of
E_{j}
(we essentially need that Lebesgue’s differentiation theorem holds) and
\Psi
a positive increasing convex function such that
\Psi(0)=0
(and, thus,
\Psi^{-1}
is a positive increasing concave function). We define
\sigma_{j}(r)=\min_{x\in E_{j}}\upsilon_{j}(B(x,r))
the volume (under
\upsilon_{j}
) of the smallest ball of radius
r>0
in
E_{j}
. For any metric space
(E,d)
endowed with a measure
\upsilon
defined on the Borel sets of
E
and for any scalar-valued function
f\in\mathcal{C}(E)
, we define

\bar{f}(A):=\upsilon(A)^{-1}\int_{A}f(x)\upsilon(dx),

for any Borel set
A\in E
. For any
(E,d,\upsilon)\in\{(E_{j},d_{j},\upsilon_{j})\}_{1\leq j\leq m}
, we have, by Lebesgue’s differentiation theorem

\bar{f}(B(x,r))\underset{r\to 0}{\to}f(x).

We denote in the following
\mathcal{E}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}E_{j}
the product space that is can be made metric by considering
d(x,y)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}d(x_{j},y_{j})^{2}\right)^{1/2}
. It can also be made measurable by considering the product measure
\upsilon(A)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\upsilon(A_{j})
for any Borel set
A=\prod_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}
. For any
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}),y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{m})\in\mathcal{E}
, we denote
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1})
(for
m\geq 2
) and define, for any
1\leq k\leq m
,

V_{k,y}x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},y_{k},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{m}).

Furthermore, for any function
f
defined on
\mathcal{E}
, we denote
V_{k,y}f(x)=f(V_{k,y}x)
. We proceed to define the joint increment of
f
as

\square_{y}f(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\textrm{id}-V_{k,y})f(x).

From the previous definitions, we can see that, for any
x,y\in\mathcal{E}
and
f
defined on
\mathcal{E}

\square_{y}f(x)=\square_{y^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime},x_{m})-\square_{y^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime},y_{m}).

We can now state an intermediate result.

Lemme D.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})
be such that

\kappa_{f}:=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\int_{\mathcal{E}}\Psi\left(\frac{|\square_{y}f(x)|}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}(x_{i},y_{j})}\right)\upsilon(dx)\upsilon(dy)<+\infty.

Then the following inequality holds for any
x,y\in\mathcal{E}

|\square_{y}f(x)|\leq 18^{m}\int_{0}^{d_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})/2}\ldots\int_{0}^{d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})/2}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{1}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{m}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction; the case
m=1
being the classical Garsia-Rodemich-Rumsey. We assume that the result holds for a certain
m-1\geq 1
. For any
x,y\in\mathcal{E}
, we denote
g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(z)=\square_{y^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime},z)
. For any Borel sets
A_{1},A_{2}\subset E_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(A_{1})-\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(A_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\left|g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m})-g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(y_{m})\right|\frac{\upsilon_{m}(dx_{m})\upsilon_{m}(dy_{m})}{\upsilon_{m}(A_{1})\upsilon_{m}(A_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2})\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\left|\frac{g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m})-g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(y_{m})}{d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})}\right|\frac{\upsilon_{m}(dx_{m})\upsilon_{m}(dy_{m})}{\upsilon_{m}(A_{1})\upsilon_{m}(A_{2})},

where
d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2})=\sup_{x_{m}\in A_{1},y_{m}\in A_{2}}d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{|g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m})-g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(y_{m})|}{d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})}=\left|\square_{y^{\prime}}\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f(x^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 18^{m-1}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

where we denote
D=[0,d_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})/2]\times\ldots\times[0,d_{m-1}(x_{m-1},y_{m-1})/2]
,
r=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m-1})
and
\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f(x^{\prime})=(f(x^{\prime},x_{m})-f(x^{\prime},y_{m}))/d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})
. Thus, by Fubini’s theorem and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(A_{1})-\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(A_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 18^{m-1}d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2})\int_{D}\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\right)\frac{\upsilon_{m}(dx_{m})\upsilon_{m}(dy_{m})}{\upsilon_{m}(A)\upsilon_{m}(B)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq 18^{m-1}d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2})\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{\kappa_{\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{\upsilon_{m}(dx_{m})\upsilon_{m}(dy_{m})}{\upsilon_{m}(A_{1})\upsilon_{m}(A_{2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

We remark that

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\kappa_{\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f}\upsilon_{m}(dx_{m})\upsilon_{m}(dy_{m})\leq\int_{E_{m}}\int_{E_{m}}\kappa_{\delta_{x_{m},y_{m}}f}\upsilon_{m}(dx_{m})\upsilon_{m}(dy_{m})=\kappa_{f},

and, thus, we obtain

(57)

\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(A_{1})-\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(A_{2})\right|\leq 18^{m-1}d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2})\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\upsilon_{m}(A_{1})\upsilon_{m}(A_{2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

We now denote
\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},r)=\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(B(x_{m},r))
for any
x_{m}\in E_{m}
and

B(x_{m},r)=\{x\in E_{m};d_{m}(x,x_{m})\leq r\}

for a certain
r>0
. We now fix
x_{m},y_{m}\in E_{m}
and let
\lambda_{k}=d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})2^{-k}
for any
k\geq 0
. Since
d_{m}(B(x_{m},\lambda_{0}),B(y_{m},\lambda_{0}))=3\lambda_{0}
,
\sigma_{m}(\lambda_{k})\geq\sigma_{m}(\tau)
for any
\tau\leq\lambda_{k}
and
\Psi^{-1}
is increasing, it follows from (
57
) that

\displaystyle\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},\lambda_{0})-\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(y_{m},\lambda_{0})\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 3\times 18^{m-1}\lambda_{0}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\lambda_{0})^{2}}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 6\times 18^{m-1}\int_{0}^{d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})/2}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\tau)^{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Furthermore, by using (
57

d_{m}(B(x_{m},\lambda_{\ell}),B(x_{m},\lambda_{\ell+1}))=\lambda_{\ell}+\lambda_{\ell+1}=6(\lambda_{\ell+1}-\lambda_{\ell+2})
for any

\sigma_{m}(\lambda_{k})\geq\sigma_{m}(\tau)
for any

and

is increasing, we obtain that, for any

,

\displaystyle\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},\lambda_{0})-\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},\lambda_{k})\right|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},\lambda_{\ell+1})-\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},\lambda_{\ell})\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 6\times 18^{m-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\lambda_{\ell+1}-\lambda_{\ell+2})\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\lambda_{\ell+1})\sigma_{m}(\lambda_{\ell+2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 6\times 18^{m-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\int_{\lambda_{\ell+2}}^{\lambda_{\ell+1}}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\tau)^{2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 6\times 18^{m-1}\int_{\lambda_{k+1}}^{\lambda_{1}}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\tau)^{2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Letting

, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m},\lambda_{0})-g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x_{m})\right|\leq

(59)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 6\times 18^{m-1}\int_{0}^{d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})/2}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\tau)^{2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,

and, similarly,

\displaystyle\left|\bar{g}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(y_{m},\lambda_{0})-g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(y_{m})\right|\leq

(60)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq 6\times 18^{m-1}\int_{0}^{d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})/2}\int_{D}\Psi^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\sigma_{j}(r_{j})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}(\tau)^{2})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Thanks to (
58
), (

) and (

), we obtain the desired result.

We can finally state a Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem.

Theorem D.4
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
\alpha_{1}>1/p
,
\alpha_{2}>d/p
and
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times\mathbbm{R}^{d})
a random process. Assume that there exists
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
such that

(61)

\vartheta_{f}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t\in[0,1]\\
x,y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|^{p}}{|t-s|^{\alpha_{1}p+\varepsilon_{1}}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}p+\varepsilon_{2}}}\right]^{1/p}<+\infty.

Then, for any
\varepsilon_{3}>\varepsilon_{2}
, there exists a positive random variable
\Upsilon\in L^{p}(\Omega)
which depends on
f,d,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},p,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{3}
such that,
\mathbbm{P}
-a.s.,

(62)

|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|\leq\Upsilon|s-t|^{\alpha_{1}-1/p}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}-d/p}(1+|x|+|y|)^{(d+\varepsilon_{3})/p},

for all
t,s\in[0,1]
and
x,y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d};\;1\leq|x|\leq 2\}
be an annulus in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
such that
\mathbbm{R}^{d}=B(0,1)\cup\bigcup_{j=0}^{+\infty}2^{j}\mathcal{A}
.
It follows from Corollary
D.2
and (
61
) that, for any
j\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,1]\\
x,y\in 2^{j}\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|^{p}}{|s-t|^{\alpha_{1}p-1}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}p-d}(1+|x|+|y|)^{d+\varepsilon_{3}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{C_{d,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},p}\vartheta_{f}}{(1+2^{j+1})^{d+\varepsilon_{3}}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}\times B(0,1+2^{j+2})^{2}}\frac{du_{1}du_{2}dz_{1}dz_{2}}{|u_{1}-u_{2}|^{1-\varepsilon_{1}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{d-\varepsilon_{2}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C_{d,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},p}\vartheta_{f}\frac{2^{(d+\varepsilon_{2})(j+2)}}{(1+2^{j+1})^{d+\varepsilon_{3}}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}\times B(0,2)^{2}}\frac{du_{1}du_{2}dzdw}{|u_{1}-u_{2}|^{1-\varepsilon_{1}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{d-\varepsilon_{2}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\lesssim 2^{j(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3})}.

Thus, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,1]\\
x,y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|^{p}}{|s-t|^{\alpha_{1}p-1}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}p-d}(1+|x|+|y|)^{d+\varepsilon_{3}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\mathbbm{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,1]\\
x,y\in B(0,1)\end{subarray}}\frac{|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|^{p}}{|s-t|^{\alpha_{1}p-1}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}p-d}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,1]\\
x,y\in 2^{j}\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\square_{(s,x)}f(t,y)|^{p}}{|s-t|^{\alpha_{1}p-1}|x-y|^{\alpha_{2}p-d}(1+|x|+|y|)^{d+\varepsilon_{3}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\lesssim 1+\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}2^{j(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3})}<+\infty,

which ends the proof.
∎

We also recall another standard Kolmogorov theorem.

Theorem D.5
.

Let
f\in C([0,1];L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}))
be a random process and
p\geq 1
such that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,1]\\
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{|f(t,x)-f(s,x)|^{p}}{|t-s|^{\alpha p+\varepsilon}}\right]<+\infty,

for a certain
\alpha>1/p
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a positive random variable
\Upsilon\in L^{p}(\Omega)
which depends on
f,d,\alpha
and
p
such that

|f(t,x)-f(s,x)|\leq\Upsilon|s-t|^{\alpha-1/p}(1+|x|)^{(1+\iota)/p},

where
\iota>0
.
