In this paper we prove new results in two areas of theoretical computer science that have received a lot of attention recently:

Streaming algorithms
is a model of computation introduced by Alon, Matias, and Szegedy

In the last decade, there have been several results in the direction of proving upper and lower bounds for streaming algorithms for combinatorial optimization problems

The goal here is to obtain a

One favourite problem considered by many works is the well-known

, or its generalization over large alphabets

;

r
to
(r-\varepsilon)
-approximation, where

1.1.
Our results

Summarizing our main contributions, we first prove a version of hypercontractivity for matrix-valued functions

1.2.
Matrix hypercontractive inequality (over large alphabets)

Fourier analysis on the Boolean cube.

We first discuss the basics of Fourier analysis before stating our result. Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function, then the Fourier decomposition of
f
is

f(x)=\sum_{S\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\widehat{f}(S)(-1)^{S\cdot x},

where
S\cdot x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and the
Fourier coefficients

\widehat{f}(S)
of
f
are
\widehat{f}(S)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(x)(-1)^{S\cdot x}]
, the expectation taken over uniformly random
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. One of the technical tools in the area of theoretical computer science is the hypercontractivity theorem proven by Bonami and Beckner
(
Bonami 1970
;
Beckner 1975
)
. In order to understand the hypercontractivity theorem, we first need to define the noise operator: for a noise parameter
\rho\in[-1,1]
, let
\operatorname{T}_{\rho}
be the operator on the space of functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

({\operatorname{T}}_{\rho}f)(x)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)}[f(y)],

where
y\sim\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(x)
denotes that the random string
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is drawn as
y_{i}=x_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho
and as
y_{i}=x_{i}\oplus 1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho
for each
i\in[n]
independently. One can show that the Fourier expansion of
{\operatorname{T}}_{\rho}f
can be written as (see, e.g.,
(
O’Donnell 2014
, Proposition 2.47)
)

({\operatorname{T}}_{\rho}f)(x)=\sum_{S\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\rho^{|S|}\widehat{f}(S)(-1)^{S\cdot x}.

One way to intuitively view this expression is that “large-weight” Fourier coefficients are reduced by an exponential factor while “small-weight” Fourier coefficients remain approximately the same. Another property of the noise operator is that it is a contraction, i.e.,
\|{\operatorname{T}}_{\rho}f\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}
for every
p\geq 1
, where
\|f\|_{p}\triangleq\big(\mathbb{E}_{x}[|f(x)|^{p}]\big)^{1/p}
is the standard normalized
p
-norm of the function
f
. The main hypercontractivity theorem states that this inequality holds true even if we increase the left-hand size by a larger norm (meaning that norms under the noise operator are not just contractive, but
hypercontractive
), i.e., for every
p\in[1,2]
and
\rho\leq\sqrt{p-1}
, we have that
\|{\operatorname{T}}_{\rho}f\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{p}
,
2

The hypercontractivity theorem can be stated for arbitrary

(1)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{S\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\rho^{2|S|}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|f(x)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

1.3.1.
Communication complexity: Hidden Matching and its variants

The Boolean Hidden Matching (
\mathsf{BHM}
) problem was introduced by Bar-Yossef et al.
(
Bar-Yossef
et al. 2004
)
(which was in turn inspired by Kerenidis and de Wolf
(
Kerenidis and de Wolf 2003
)
for proving
\mathsf{LDC}
lower bounds) in order to prove exponential separations between quantum and classical one-way communication complexities. We describe below the generalized Hidden Matching problem over larger alphabets and hypermatching. Here a hypermatching is a set of hyperedges without common vertices.
The
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching (
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
) problem is a two-party communication problem between Alice and Bob: Alice is given
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and Bob is given a string
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
and
\alpha n/t
-many disjoint
t
-tuples from
[n]
(where
\alpha\in(0,1]
). The disjoint
t
-tuples from
[n]
can be viewed as hyperedges of size
t
, which, in turn, can also be viewed as an incidence matrix
M\in\{0,1\}^{\alpha n/t\times n}
(each row corresponding to a hyperedge). In the
\mathsf{YES}
instance it is promised that
w=Mx
(over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
), while in the
\mathsf{NO}
instance it is promised that
w
is uniformly random, and the goal is to decide which is the case using a message sent from Alice to Bob.

There have been a few lines of work on understanding the problem of Hidden Hypermatching:
(i)
the seminal work of Bar-Yossef et al.
(
Bar-Yossef
et al. 2004
)
and Gavinsky et al.
(
Gavinsky et al. 2007
)
showed that, for
r=t=2
,
\mathsf{BHM}
can be solved using
O(\log n)
qubits but requires
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
classical bits of communication;
(ii)
Verbin and Yu
(
Verbin and Yu 2011
)
considered the problem where
r=2
and
t\geq 2
(which in fact inspired many follow-up works on using hypermatching for classical streaming lower bounds) and showed a classical lowed bound of
\Omega(n^{1-1/t})
, which was subsequently generalized to a
\Omega(n^{1-2/t})
quantum lower bound by Shi, Wu, and Yu
(
Shi et al. 2012
)
;
(iii)
Guruswami and Tao
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
studied the problem for when
t=2
and
r\geq 2
, proving a classical
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
lower bound;
(iv)
apart from these, there have been more exotic generalizations of the Hidden Hypermatching problem, e.g., Kapralov, Khanna, and Sudan
(
Kapralov
et al. 2015
)
proposed the Boolean Hidden Partition, where Bob does not receive a matching anymore (a graph made up of pair-wise disjoint edges), but the edges of any graph
G
, and Doriguello and Montanaro
(
Doriguello and
Montanaro 2020
)
expanded the
2\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
problem to computing a fixed Boolean function on the hyperedges of Bob’s hypermatching instead of the Parity function. Here we shall consider the standard Hidden Hypermatching problem over a larger alphabet and give both upper and lower bounds for its quantum and classical communication complexities for every
t,r\geq 2
.

Upper bounds on Hidden Hypermatching.

For a given
t\geq 2
, the same classical communication protocol for
r=2
can be used for general
r>2
. The idea is that Alice picks
O((n/\alpha)^{1-1/t})
entries of
x
uniformly at random to send to Bob. By the Birthday Paradox, with high probability Bob will obtain all the values from one of his hyperedges
i
, and thus can compare
(Mx)_{i}
with the corresponding
w_{i}
. If they are equal, he outputs
\mathsf{YES}
, otherwise he outputs
\mathsf{NO}
, which leads to an one-side error of
O(1/r)
. The total amount of communication is
O(\log{(rn)}(n/\alpha)^{1-1/t})
bits.
4
4

4

One can further improve this complexity to
O(\log{(n\log{r})}+(\log r)\cdot(n/\alpha)^{1-1/t})
by Newman’s theorem
(
Newman 1991
)
.
The situation is more interesting in the quantum setting. For
t=2
, we prove that Hidden Hypermatching can be solved using only a logarithmic amount of qubits for every
r=\poly(n)
.

Result 3 (
Theorem

2
).

There is a one-way protocol for
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,2,n)
with one-sided error
1/3
using
O(\log{(nr)}/\alpha)
qubits.

The above bound uses a non-trivial procedure that allows one to learn the sum of two numbers modulo
r
by using just one “query” and crucially uses the knowledge of the string
w
: given a suitable superposition of two numbers, one can obtain their sum with one-sided error by using one measurement. As far as we are aware, such a statement was not known prior to our work. However, the knowledge of
w
is vital, which means that the protocol does not work for more general settings where there is no promise on the inputs (e.g., a relational version of the
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching problem where Bob must output one hyperedge
i
and its corresponding value
(Mx)_{i}
), and it also cannot be used as a building block for the general case
t,r>2
. The current upper bound on the quantum communication complexity of the
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
problem with
t,r>2
thus matches the classical one. In view of the lower bounds stated below and the known upper bound
O(n^{1-1/\lceil t/2\rceil}\log{n})
for
r=2

(
Kerenidis and de Wolf 2003
)
(note that
\lceil t/2\rceil
is the number of quantum queries required to learn the parity of
t
bits
(
Farhi
et al. 1998
;
Beals et al. 2001
)
), we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.

If
t,r>2
, there is a protocol for
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
using
O(\log{(rn)}(n/\alpha)^{1-1/\lceil t/2\rceil})
qubits.

Lower bounds on Hidden Hypermatching.

The standard approach for proving a lower bound on the amount of communication required to solve the Hidden Hypermatching problem is via Fourier analysis. In the classical proofs of Gavinsky et al.
(
Gavinsky et al. 2007
)
, Verbin and Yu
(
Verbin and Yu 2011
)
, and Guruswami and Tao
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
, the total variation distance between the probability distributions arising from the
\mathsf{YES}
and
\mathsf{NO}
instances is bounded using the inequality of Kahn, Kalai, and Linial
(
Kahn
et al. 1988
)
(which can be seen as a corollary of the hypercontractivity inequality). On the other hand, Shi, Wu, and Yu
(
Shi et al. 2012
)
obtained a quantum lower bound by bounding the Schatten 1-norm between the possible density matrices received by Bob in both
\mathsf{YES}
and
\mathsf{NO}
instances via the matrix-valued hypercontractivity from Ben-Aroya, Regev, and de Wolf
(
Ben-Aroya et al. 2008
)
. We follow a similar approach by using our
generalized matrix-valued hypercontractive
inequality from
Result

1
in order to obtain the following lower bound (for
r=2
our lower bound is exponentially better in
\alpha
than
(
Shi et al. 2012
)
).

Result 4 (
Theorem

4
,
Theorem

3
).

Every constant-bias protocol for
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
with
t,r\geq 2
requires
\Omega((n/t)^{1-2/t}/\alpha^{2/t})
qubits of communication or
\Omega((n/t)^{1-1/t}/\alpha^{1/t})
bits of communication.

The phase transition from
t=2
to
t>2
in the quantum communication complexity of
r
-
\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
is reminiscent of the well-known fact that parity of
n
bits can be computed exactly using
\lceil n/2\rceil
quantum queries
(
Farhi
et al. 1998
;
Beals et al. 2001
)
: a function requiring more than one query within the same hyperedge will lead to a large communication complexity since, by design of the Hidden Matching problem, it is hard to extract information from the same hyperedge more than once.

1.3.2.
Streaming lower bounds

One-way communication complexity lower bounds has been used by several recent works to prove streaming lower bounds
(
Verbin and Yu 2011
;
Kapralov
et al. 2015
;
Guruswami
et al. 2017
;
Guruswami and Tao 2019
;
Chou
et al. 2020
)
. To see this, suppose a problem
P
has inputs
(X,Y)
and the goal is to find space-efficient streaming algorithms to compute
P(X,Y)
, when
X,Y
are presented in a stream (i.e., presented bit-by-bit). Then, one way to lower bound the
space complexity
is to prove lower bounds on the following problem: consider the one-way communication problem where Alice gets the input
X
, Bob gets the input
Y
, their goal is to compute
P(X,Y)
and only Alice is allowed to communicate to Bob. Then one can show that any lower bound for randomized one-way communication implies an equivalent lower bound for streaming algorithms. This technique has been used by a sequence of papers to prove lower bounds on space complexity of Max-Cut
(
Kapralov
et al. 2015
;
Kapralov and
Krachun 2019
;
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
, matching
(
Goel
et al. 2012
)
, Max-CSP
(
Guruswami
et al. 2017
;
Chou
et al. 2020
;
Chou
et al. 2022a
)
and counting cycles
(
Verbin and Yu 2011
;
Assadi and Dudeja 2021
)
. One problem that is used often in this direction is the aforementioned Boolean Hidden Matching and related variants. Using our classical and quantum communication lower bounds (
Result

4
) we present two lower bounds for streaming problems on generalizations to Max-Cut.

There are a few natural generalizations to Max-Cut. One is Max-
t
-Cut, i.e., finding the maximum cut value on a hypergraph with
t
-sized hyperedges, to which the
\Omega(n)
classical lower bound from
(
Kapralov and
Krachun 2019
)
already applies. Another is the Unique Games problem, a constraint satisfaction problem defined on a graph, where a linear constraint (a permutation) over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
is specified on each edge and the goal is to find a vertex assignment over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
that maximizes the number of satisfied constraints. When
r=2
, Unique Games reduces to Max-Cut. Guruswami and Tao
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
studied the streaming complexity of the Unique Games problem and proved a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
in the adversarial model by using a reduction from Hidden Matching over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
. This bound was improved to
\Omega(n)
by Chou et al.
(
Chou et al. 2022b
)
for a larger set of problems including Unique Games.

Here we join both directions, i.e., Max-
t
-Cut and the standard Unique Games problem, into a generalized version of Unique Games defined on a hypergraph and obtain a streaming
quantum
lower bound in the adversarial model for any value
t,r\geq 2
.
5
5

5

We note that Kapralov et al.
(
Kapralov
et al. 2015
)
proved a classical lower bound
\Omega(n^{1-\varepsilon})
for
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximations of Max-Cut in the adversarial model and their proof, together with
Result

4
, readily implies a similar quantum lower bound.

Result 5 (
Theorem

12
).

Every streaming algorithm giving a
(r-\varepsilon)
-approximation for Unique Games on
t
-hyperedge
n
-vertex hypergraphs over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
uses
\Omega(n^{1-2/t})
quantum space.

The above result follows from generalizing the proof of Guruswami and Tao
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
. As far as we are aware, these are the first quantum lower bounds for Unique Games in the streaming model.

1.4.
Locally decodable codes

A locally decodable code (
\mathsf{LDC}
) is an error correcting code that allows to retrieve a single bit of the original message (with high probability) by only examining a few bits in a corrupted codeword. More formally, a
(q,\varepsilon,\delta)
-
\mathsf{LDC}
was defined by Katz and Trevisan
(
Katz and Trevisan 2000
)
as a function
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
that satisfies the following: for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
,
i\in[n]
and
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
that satisfies
d(C(x),y)\leq\delta
(i.e., a
\delta
-fraction of the elements of
C(x)
are corrupted), there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that makes
q
queries to
y
non-adaptively and outputs
\mathcal{A}^{y}(i)\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
such that
\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{y}(i)=x_{i}]\geq 1/r+\varepsilon
(where the probability is over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
). Over
\{0,1\}
,
\mathsf{LDC}
s have found several applications in private information retrieval
(
Chor
et al. 1998
)
, multiparty computation
(
Ishai and
Kushilevitz 2004
)
, data structures
(
Chen et al. 2010
)
, and average-case complexity theory
(
Trevisan 2004
)
.

The natural question in constructing
\mathsf{LDC}
s is the trade-off between
N
and
n
. A well-known
2
-query
\mathsf{LDC}
is the Hadamard encoding that maps
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
into the string
C(x)=(\langle x,y\rangle)_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
: on input
i\in[n]
, a decoding algorithm queries
C(x)
at a uniformly random
y
and
y\oplus e_{i}
and retrieves
C(x)=\langle x,y\oplus e_{i}\rangle-\langle x,y\rangle
, where
e_{i}=0^{i-1}10^{n-i}
. Here the encoding length is
N=2^{n}
, and an important question is, are there
2
-query
\mathsf{LDC}
s with
N\ll 2^{n}
? For the case
r=2
, Goldreich et al.
(
Goldreich et al. 2002
)
showed a lower bound
N=2^{\Omega(n)}
for
linear codes
, which was later improved by Obata
(
Obata 2002
)
. Later, Kerenidis and de Wolf
(
Kerenidis and de Wolf 2003
)
proved an exponential lower bound for
non-linear codes
using a quantum argument!
6
6

6

For simplicity in exposition, we omit the dependence on
\delta,\varepsilon
in these lower bounds.

This left open the setting where
r>2
. Following these works, for
2
-query
non-linear

\mathsf{LDC}
s
C:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
(note the inputs are over
\{0,1\}
and not
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
), Wehner and de Wolf
(
Wehner
and de Wolf 2005
)
proved the lower bound
N=2^{\Omega(n/r^{2})}
. On the other hand, Dvir and Shpilka
(
Dvir and Shpilka 2005
)
showed a lower bound of
N=2^{\Omega(n)}
for every
2
-query
linear

\mathsf{LDC}

C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
, even independent of the field size. To prove their result, they crucially observed that, given a linear
\mathsf{LDC}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, one can construct a linear
\mathsf{LDC}
over
\{0,1\}
(with almost the same parameters) and then invoked the result of Goldreich et al.
(
Goldreich et al. 2002
)
. This reduction, however, fails for non-linear codes and motivates the question of whether there are

non-linear

\mathsf{LDC}
s
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
with
N\ll 2^{n}
.

The main contribution here is a lower bound for
non-linear

\mathsf{LDC}
s over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
that scale as
2^{\Omega(n/r^{2})}
, and which gives a super-polynomial lower bound for
r=o(n^{1/2})
. Our lower bound comes from using the non-commutative Khintchine’s inequality
(
Tropp 2015
)
.
7
7

7

We thank Jop Briët for helping us prove this theorem.

Result 6 (
Theorem

5
).

If
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
is a
(2,\delta,\varepsilon)
-
\mathsf{LDC}
, then
N=2^{\Omega(\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}n/r^{2})}
.

We briefly mention that, if one requires the success probability to be larger than, for example,
1/2+\varepsilon
instead of
1/r+\varepsilon
, so that plurality vote can be used and the success probability amplified, then
\varepsilon
becomes a constant bounded away from
1/r
(if
r>2
) and our lower bound is no longer dependent on
\varepsilon
. Moreover, we stress that the success probability is bounded by
1/r+\varepsilon
, meaning that the lower bound of Wehner and de Wolf
(
Wehner
and de Wolf 2005
)
is not directly applicable by restricting the messages to binary strings. Finally, we remark that in an earlier version of the paper, we proved a similar lower bound using our matrix-valued hypercontractivity via the proof technique in
(
Ben-Aroya et al. 2008
, Theorem 11)
. This leads, however, to a weaker bound,
N=2^{\Omega(\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{4}n/r^{4})}
. After our preprint appeared online, we were made aware of the non-commutative Khintchine’s inequality which leads us to the improved lower bound of
2^{\Omega(\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}n/r^{2})}
.

Preliminaries

Let
[n]\triangleq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For
r\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r\geq 2
, we let
\mathbb{Z}_{r}\triangleq\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
be the ring with addition and multiplication modulo
r
, and let
\omega_{r}\triangleq e^{2\pi i/r}
. Given
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, we write
|S|\triangleq|\{i\in[n]:S_{i}\neq 0\}|
for its Hamming weight. Let
\operatorname{D}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
be the set of all quantum states over
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, i.e., the set of positive semi-definite matrices with trace
1
. For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, its (unnormalized) Schatten
p
-norm is defined as
\|M\|_{p}\triangleq(\operatorname{Tr}|M|^{p})^{1/p}=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(M)^{p}\big)^{1/p}
, where
\{\sigma_{i}(M)\}_{i}
are the singular values of
M
, i.e., the eigenvalues of the positive semi-definite operator
|M|\triangleq\sqrt{M^{\dagger}M}
. Moreover, let
\|M\|\triangleq\max_{i\in[m]}\sigma_{i}(M)
and
\|M\|_{F}\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}|M_{ij}|^{2}}
be its spectral and Frobenious norms, respectively (notice that
\|M\|=\|M\|_{\infty}
). Given a vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, its
p
-norm is
\|v\|_{p}\triangleq\big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|v_{i}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}
. Given two probability distributions
P
and
Q
on the same finite set, their total variation distance is
\|P-Q\|_{\text{tvd}}\triangleq\sum_{i}|P(i)-Q(i)|
(we might abuse notation and use random variables instead of their probability distributions in
\|\cdot\|_{\text{tvd}}
). For a probability
p=1/r+\varepsilon
with fixed
r\in\mathbb{Z}
, we refer to
\varepsilon
as its
advantage
, and to
\varepsilon r/(r-1)
as its
bias
.

The Fourier transform of a matrix-valued function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is a function
\widehat{f}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(S)=\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}f(x)\omega_{r}^{-S\cdot x},

where
S\cdot x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}x_{i}
is a sum over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
. Here the Fourier coefficients
\widehat{f}(S)
are
m\times m
complex matrices and we can write
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
as

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\widehat{f}(S)\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}.

We shall need the Holevo-Helstrom theorem
(
Helstrom 1969
)
which characterizes the optimal success probability of distinguishing between two quantum states.

Fact 1 (
(
Watrous 2018
, Theorem 3.4)
).

Let
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}
be two quantum states that appear with probability
p
and
1-p
, respectively. The optimal success probability of predicting which state it is by a POVM is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|p\rho_{0}-(1-p)\rho_{1}\|_{1}.

We will need the following result derived from the non-commutative Khintchine’s inequality.

Lemma 2.

Given
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{r}^{k_{i}}A_{i}\right\|\leq 2\sqrt{2\log(2N)}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A_{i}\|^{2}}.

Proof.

\begin{aligned} \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{r}^{k_{i}}A_{i}\right\|&=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\omega_{r}^{k_{i}}A_{i}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\big[\omega_{r}^{k^{\prime}_{i}}A_{i}\big]\right)\right\|\\
&\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k,k^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{r}^{k_{i}}-\omega_{r}^{k^{\prime}_{i}})A_{i}\right\|\\
&=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k,k^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\omega_{r}^{k_{i}}-\omega_{r}^{k^{\prime}_{i}})A_{i}\right\|\\
&\leq 2\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{k\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\omega_{r}^{k_{i}}A_{i}\right\|\\
&\leq 2\sqrt{2\log(2N)}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A_{i}\|^{2}},\end{aligned}

using the non-commutative Khintchine’s inequality
(
Tropp 2015
, Theorem 4.1.1)
in the last step.
∎

Hypercontractive Inequality

In this section we prove our main result, a hypercontractive inequality for matrix-valued functions over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, generalizing a result from
(
Ben-Aroya et al. 2008
)
. The proof is by induction on
n
and the base case

3.1

(

)

3.2

3.1.
Generalizing Ball, Carlen, and Lieb

We first state the powerful inequality of Ball, Carlen, and Lieb
(
Ball
et al. 1994
, Theorem 1)
.

Theorem 1 (Optimal 2-uniform convexity).

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and
p\in[1,2]
. Then

\left(\frac{\|A+B\|_{p}^{p}+\|A-B\|_{p}^{p}}{2}\right)^{2/p}\geq\|A\|_{p}^{2}+(p-1)\|B\|_{p}^{2}.

As previously mentioned in the introduction, this inequality was first proven by Tomczak-Jaegermann
(
Tomczak-Jaegermann 1974
)
for
p\leq 4/3
, before being generalized by Ball, Carlen, and Lieb
(
Ball
et al. 1994
)
for all
p\in[1,2]
in 1994. Since then it has found several applications
(
Carlen and Lieb 1993
;
Duchi et al. 2010
;
Lee and Naor 2004
;
Naor 2017
)
. The above result can be recast in a slightly different way.

Theorem 2.

Let
p\in[1,2]
and
Z,W\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}[|Z|^{p-2}ZW^{\dagger}]=\operatorname{Tr}[|Z|^{p-2}WZ^{\dagger}]=0
(where
|Z|^{p-2}=(ZZ^{\dagger})^{p/2-1}
). Then

\displaystyle\|Z+W\|_{p}^{2}\geq\|Z\|_{p}^{2}+(p-1)\|W\|_{p}^{2}.

Theorem

2
is implicit in the proof of
(
Ball
et al. 1994
, Theorem 1)
, and it is where most of the difficulty lies, while the reduction from
Theorem

1
to
Theorem

2
is done by defining

\displaystyle Z=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&A\end{bmatrix},\qquad W=\begin{bmatrix}B&0\\
0&-B\end{bmatrix}.

Nonetheless,
Theorem

2
holds more generally for any
Z,W\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
that satisfy
\operatorname{Tr}[|Z|^{p-2}ZW^{\dagger}]=\operatorname{Tr}[|Z|^{p-2}WZ^{\dagger}]=0
. By using this result, we can prove the following generalization of
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 3 (A generalization of
(
Ball
et al. 1994
)
).

Let
r\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r\geq 2
. Let
\omega_{r}\triangleq e^{2i\pi/r}
,
A_{0},\ldots,A_{r-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and
p\in[1,2]
, then

(5a)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\|A_{j}\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}A_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2}+\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\left\|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{-jk}A_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2},

(5b)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|A_{0}\right\|_{p}^{2}+\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{p}^{2}.

Notice that for
r=2
we recover
Theorem

1
, since
\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}=p-1
.

Proof.

In order to prove this theorem, first note that both inequalities are equivalent: just define
A^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{-jk}A_{j}\iff A_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A^{\prime}_{j}
. Therefore we shall focus on
Equation

5b
. In order to prove it, let us first define the
rn\times rn
matrices

(6)

\displaystyle Z_{j}\triangleq\operatorname{diag}(\{\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\}_{k=0}^{r-1})=\begin{bmatrix}A_{j}&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&\omega_{r}^{j}A_{j}&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&\omega_{r}^{2j}A_{j}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&\omega_{r}^{(r-1)j}A_{j}\end{bmatrix}

for
j\in\{0,\dots,r-1\}
. Now, since the trace is additive for block matrices, we have

(7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}Z_{j}\right|^{p}=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\right|^{p}.

Moreover, observe that

\displaystyle\|Z_{j}\|_{p}^{2}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\operatorname{Tr}|\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}|^{p}\right)^{2/p}=(r\operatorname{Tr}|A_{j}|^{p})^{2/p}=r^{2/p}\|A_{j}\|_{p}^{2}.

Therefore we can rewrite
Equation

5b
as

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}Z_{j}\right\|^{2}_{p}\geq\|Z_{0}\|_{p}^{2}+\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\|Z_{j}\|_{p}^{2}.

The above can be proven by repeated applications of
Theorem

2
as follows: consider a permutation of
[r-1]
given by
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r-1})
. Since

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[|Z_{j}|^{p-2}Z_{j}Z_{k}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\begin{bmatrix}|A_{j}|^{p-2}A_{j}A_{k}^{\dagger}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\omega_{r}^{j-k}|A_{j}|^{p-2}A_{j}A_{k}^{\dagger}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&\omega_{r}^{(r-1)(j-k)}|A_{j}|^{p-2}A_{j}A_{k}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[|A_{j}|^{p-2}A_{j}A_{k}^{\dagger}]\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{\ell(j-k)}=0

for any
j\neq k
(and similarly
\operatorname{Tr}[|Z_{j}|^{p-2}Z_{k}Z_{j}^{\dagger}]=0
), then (define
k_{0}\triangleq 0
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[|Z_{k_{j}}|^{p-2}Z_{k_{j}}\left(\sum_{l=j+1}^{r-1}Z_{k_{l}}\right)^{\dagger}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[|Z_{k_{j}}|^{p-2}\left(\sum_{l=j+1}^{r-1}Z_{k_{l}}\right)Z_{k_{j}}^{\dagger}\right]=0

for every
j\in\{0,1,\dots,r-2\}
, meaning that
Theorem

2
can be applied, which implies

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}Z_{j}\right\|^{2}_{p}

\displaystyle\geq\|Z_{0}\|_{p}^{2}+(p-1)\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}Z_{j}\right\|^{2}_{p}

\displaystyle\geq\|Z_{0}\|_{p}^{2}+(p-1)\|Z_{k_{1}}\|_{p}^{2}+(p-1)^{2}\left\|\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}Z_{k_{j}}\right\|^{2}_{p}\geq\|Z_{0}\|_{p}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(p-1)^{j}\|Z_{k_{j}}\|_{p}^{2}.

Averaging the above inequality over all the
(r-1)!
permutations of the set
[r-1]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}Z_{j}\right\|^{2}_{p}

\displaystyle\geq\|Z_{0}\|_{p}^{2}+\frac{1}{(r-1)!}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\|Z_{j}\|_{p}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}(r-2)!(p-1)^{k}

\displaystyle=\|Z_{0}\|_{p}^{2}+\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\|Z_{j}\|_{p}^{2},

proving our theorem statement.
∎

Remark 4.

It is not hard to see that
\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}\geq\frac{p-1}{r-1}
and
\lim_{p\to 2}\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}=1
.

Observe that
t\mapsto t^{p/2}
is concave for
p\in[1,2]
, hence
Theorem

3
implies the seemingly weaker

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|A_{0}\right\|_{p}^{2}+\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{p}^{2}

for
p\in[1,2]
. Nonetheless, the above inequality also implies
Theorem

3
(this fact was already pointed out for
r=2
by
(
Ball
et al. 1994
)
). Indeed, consider again the
rn\times rn
matrices
Z_{j}
from
Equation

6
. Then, similar to
Equation

7
(which only considered the
\ell=0
case below), for any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{j\ell}Z_{j}\right|^{p}=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\right|^{p}\implies\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{j\ell}Z_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\right\|^{p}_{p}\right)^{2/p}.

Since
\|Z_{j}\|^{2}_{p}=r^{2/p}\|A_{j}\|^{2}_{p}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
,
Equation

8
implies (define
\zeta\triangleq\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}
for simplicity)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{0}\right\|_{p}^{2}+\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{p}^{2}=\frac{\left\|Z_{0}\right\|_{p}^{2}}{r^{2/p}}+\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\frac{\left\|Z_{k}\right\|_{p}^{2}}{r^{2/p}}\leq\frac{r^{-2/p}}{r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{j\ell}Z_{j}\right\|_{p}^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}A_{j}\right\|^{p}_{p}\right)^{2/p},

which is exactly
Theorem

3
.

3.2.
Proving
(2,p)
-hypercontractive inequality over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}

Having proven the base case of our main theorem statement, we are now ready to prove our hypercontractivity theorem for matrix-valued functions over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

Theorem 5.

Let
p\in[1,2]
. For every
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and

\displaystyle\rho\leq\sqrt{\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}},

we have

\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\rho^{2|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\|f(x)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where
|S|\triangleq|\{i\in[n]:S_{i}\neq 0\}|
.

Proof.

For ease of notation, define
\zeta\triangleq\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}
. It suffices to prove the inequality for
\rho=\sqrt{\zeta}
. Our proof closely follows the one in
(
Ben-Aroya et al. 2008
)
and is by induction on
n
. For
n=1
, the desired statement is

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\zeta^{|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\|f(x)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}

\displaystyle\Updownarrow

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}(0)\|_{p}^{2}+\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\|\widehat{f}(k)\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}\widehat{f}(j)\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p},

where we used that
f(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{jk}\widehat{f}(j)
. But this is precisely
Theorem

3
with
A_{k}=\widehat{f}(k)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

We now assume the inequality holds for
n
and prove it for
n+1
. Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
g_{i}=f|_{x_{n+1}=i}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
be the function obtained by fixing the last coordinate of
f(\cdot)
to
i
. By the induction hypothesis we have that, for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
and
p\in[1,2]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\zeta^{|S|}\|\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\|g_{i}(x)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}.

We now take the
\ell_{p}
average of each of these
r
inequalities to obtain

(10)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\zeta^{|S|}\|\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\right)^{p/2}\right)^{2/p}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\|g_{i}(x)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n+1}}\|f(x)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}.

The right-hand side of the inequality above is exactly the right-hand side of the conjectured hypercontractive inequality. Below, we show how to lower bound the left-hand side of the above inequality by the desired left-hand side of the conjectured statement. To do so, we will need the following Minkowski’s inequality.

Lemma 6 (Minkowski’s inequality,
(
Hardy
et al. 1952
, Theorem 26)
).

For any
r_{1}\times r_{2}
matrix whose rows are given by
u_{1},\dots,u_{r_{1}}
and whose columns are given by
v_{1},\dots,v_{r_{2}}
, and any
1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\infty
,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\|v_{1}\|_{q_{2}},\dots,\|v_{r_{2}}\|_{q_{2}}\right)\right\|_{q_{1}}\geq\left\|\left(\|u_{1}\|_{q_{1}},\dots,\|u_{r_{1}}\|_{q_{1}}\right)\right\|_{q_{2}}.

Now, consider the
r^{n}\times r
matrix whose entries are given by

c_{S,i}=r^{n/2}\big\|\zeta^{|S|/2}\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\big\|_{p}

for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
and
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
. Then the left-hand side of
Equation

10
can be written as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\zeta^{|S|}\|\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\right)^{p/2}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}c_{S,i}^{2}\right)^{p/2}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}c_{S,i}^{p}\right)^{2/p}\right)^{1/2}

(11)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\zeta^{|S|}\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\|\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\big\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}\right)^{1/2}.

Now, for a fixed
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, we use the base case
n=1
,
Equation

9
, on the functions
h(i)=\widehat{g_{i}}(S)
in order to get

\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\|\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\zeta^{|i|}\left\|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}h(j)\omega_{r}^{-ij}\right\|_{p}^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\zeta^{|i|}\left\|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\widehat{g_{j}}(S)\omega_{r}^{-ij}\right\|_{p}^{2}.

Plugging this back into
Equation

11
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\zeta^{|S|}\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\|\widehat{g_{i}}(S)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\zeta^{|S|+|i|}\left\|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\widehat{g_{j}}(S)\omega_{r}^{-ij}\right\|_{p}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n+1}}\zeta^{|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where we used the fact that
g_{j}=f|_{x_{n+1}=j}
, so, for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
and
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, we have that
\widehat{f}(S,i)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\widehat{g_{j}}(S)\omega_{r}^{-ij}
. The lower bound we obtained above is exactly the left-hand side of the conjectured hypercontractive inequality, which proves the theorem statement.
∎

Remark 7 (Comparison with hypercontractivity for real numbers).

For real functions
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, it is known that
(
Latała and
Oleszkiewicz 2000
;
Wolff 2007
)
(see also
(
O’Donnell 2014
, Theorem 10.18)
)

\left(\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\rho^{2|S|}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}|f(x)|^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where
\rho\leq\sqrt{\frac{(r-1)^{1-1/p}-(r-1)^{-(1-1/p)}}{(r-1)^{1/p}-(r-1)^{-1/p}}}
. Moreover, this bound on
\rho
is perfectly sharp, meaning that our bound
\rho\leq\sqrt{\frac{(p-1)(1-(p-1)^{r-1})}{(r-1)(2-p)}}
in
Theorem

5
can possibly be improved. We note that, for
r=2
, both bounds become
\rho\leq\sqrt{p-1}
.

4.
Hidden Hypermatching Problem

In the following, an
\alpha
-partial
t
-hypermatching
M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
on
n
vertices is defined as a sequence of
\alpha n/t
disjoint hyperedges
(M_{1,1},\dots,M_{1,t}),\dots,(M_{\alpha n/t,1},\dots,M_{\alpha n/t,t})\in[n]^{t}
with
t
vertices each, where
\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
is the set of all such hypermatchings. If
\alpha=1
, we shall write
\mathcal{M}_{t,n}
.

Definition 1.

Let
n,t\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
t|n
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. In the
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching
(r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n))
problem, Alice gets
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, Bob gets an
\alpha
-partial
t
-hypermatching
M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
and a string
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
. The hyperedges of
M
are
(M_{1,1},\dots,M_{1,t}),\dots,(M_{\alpha n/t,1},\dots,M_{\alpha n/t,t})
. Let
M\in\{0,1\}^{\alpha n/t\times n}
also be the incidence matrix of Bob’s hypermatching. Consider the distributions:

(1)

\mathsf{YES}
distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}
, let
w=Mx
(where the matrix product
Mx
is over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
);

(2)

\mathsf{NO}
distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
,
w
is uniformly random in
\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
.

In the
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching problem, Alice sends a message to Bob who needs to decide with high probability if
w
is drawn from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}
or
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
.

4.1.
Quantum protocol for
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching

For
t=2
, we obtain a efficient quantum communication protocol to solve the
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching problem.

Theorem 2.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, there is a protocol for the
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,2,n)
problem with one-sided error
\varepsilon
and
O(\frac{1}{\alpha}\log{(nr)}\log(1/\varepsilon))
qubits of communication from Alice to Bob.

Proof.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{2,n}
be Bob’s matching with disjoint edges
(M_{1,1},M_{1,2}),\dots,(M_{\alpha n/2,1},M_{\alpha n/2,2})\in[n]^{2}
. Alice first sends the following state in
\mathbb{C}^{r}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
to Bob,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i},i\rangle.

Bob then measures the state with the POVM
\{E_{1},\dots,E_{\alpha n/2},\mathbb{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha n/2}E_{i}\}
, where

\displaystyle E_{i}\triangleq|M_{i,1}\rangle\langle M_{i,1}|+|M_{i,2}\rangle\langle M_{i,2}|,\qquad\forall i\in[\alpha n/2]

(note that
|M_{i,j}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
for
i\in[\alpha n/2],j\in[2]
). With probability
1-\alpha
the POVM outputs the final outcome, and with probability
\alpha
he will obtain a measurement outcome
E_{i}
with
i\in[\alpha n/2]
and get the state

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|x_{M_{i,1}},M_{i,1}\rangle+|x_{M_{i,2}},M_{i,2}\rangle).

By repeating the procedure
O(1/\alpha)
times, Bob obtains an outcome
i\in[\alpha n/2]
with high probability.

For the ease of notation, we can write
M_{i,1}=0
and
M_{i,2}=1
(note that Bob knows the values of both
M_{i,1},M_{i,2}
explicitly). Bob now attaches a
\lceil\log_{2}{r}\rceil
-qubit register in the state
|0\rangle
to
|\psi\rangle
and applies a quantum Fourier transform
Q_{r}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
to it to obtain

\displaystyle|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}|k\rangle|\psi\rangle.

From now on we shall consider a parameter
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
to be determined later. Let
X
be the usual Pauli operator and let
S_{\ell}
and
P
be the shift and phase operators over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
defined as
S_{\ell}|k\rangle=|\ell-k\rangle
and
P|k\rangle=\omega_{r}^{k}|k\rangle
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
. Let
C_{\ell}\triangleq PS_{\ell}P\otimes X
. Bob applies the controlled unitary
U_{\ell}
defined as
U_{\ell}|k\rangle|\psi\rangle=|k\rangle C_{\ell}^{k}|\psi\rangle
on his state, followed by an inverse quantum Fourier transform
Q^{\dagger}_{r}
on his first register to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}U_{\ell}|k\rangle|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}|k\rangle C_{\ell}^{k}|\psi\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Q^{\dagger}_{r}\otimes\mathbb{I}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{-jk}|j\rangle C_{\ell}^{k}|\psi\rangle.

Let us calculate
C_{\ell}|\psi\rangle
and
C_{\ell}^{2}|\psi\rangle
. We have

\displaystyle C_{\ell}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(PS_{\ell}P\otimes X)(|x_{0},0\rangle+|x_{1},1\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(PS_{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{I})(\omega_{r}^{x_{0}}|x_{0},1\rangle+\omega_{r}^{x_{1}}|x_{1},0\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(P\otimes\mathbb{I})(\omega_{r}^{x_{0}}|\ell-x_{0},1\rangle+\omega_{r}^{x_{1}}|\ell-x_{1},0\rangle)

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{r}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}}(|\ell-x_{1},0\rangle+|\ell-x_{0},1\rangle)

and

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{2}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{r}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}}(PS_{\ell}P\otimes X)(|\ell-x_{1},0\rangle+|\ell-x_{0},1\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{r}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}}(PS_{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{I})(\omega_{r}^{\ell-x_{1}}|\ell-x_{1},1\rangle+\omega_{r}^{\ell-x_{0}}|\ell-x_{0},0\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{r}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{2}}(P\otimes\mathbb{I})(\omega_{r}^{\ell-x_{1}}|x_{1},1\rangle+\omega_{r}^{\ell-x_{0}}|x_{0},0\rangle)

\displaystyle=\omega_{r}^{2\ell}|\psi\rangle.

We can see from the above that
C_{\ell}^{2k}|\psi\rangle=\omega_{r}^{2k\ell}|\psi\rangle
. By defining
\Delta_{\ell}\triangleq\ell-(x_{0}+x_{1})
and
\delta_{k}=1
if
k
is odd and
0
otherwise, Bob’s final state is

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{k(\ell-j)}|j\rangle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|x_{0}+\Delta_{\ell}\delta_{k},0\rangle+|x_{1}+\Delta_{\ell}\delta_{k},1\rangle).

Now observe that, if
\ell=x_{0}+x_{1}
, then
C_{\ell}|\psi\rangle=\omega_{r}^{\ell}|\psi\rangle
in
Equation

12
. This means that Bob’s state in
Equation

13
becomes
|x_{0}+x_{1}\rangle|\psi\rangle
, and if he measures his first register, he obtains
x_{0}+x_{1}~(\text{mod}~r)
with certainty.

On the other hand, if
\ell\neq x_{0}+x_{1}
, then the probability of measuring the first register and obtaining the outcome
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}[m]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{(\ell-m)(k_{1}-k_{2})}(\langle x_{0}+\Delta_{\ell}\delta_{k_{2}}|x_{0}+\Delta_{\ell}\delta_{k_{1}}\rangle+\langle x_{1}+\Delta_{\ell}\delta_{k_{2}}|x_{1}+\Delta_{\ell}\delta_{k_{1}}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}~\text{even}}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{(\ell-m)(k_{1}-k_{2})}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}~\text{odd}}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{(\ell-m)(k_{1}-k_{2})}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k~\text{even}}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{k(\ell-m)}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k~\text{odd}}^{r-1}\omega_{r}^{k(\ell-m)}\right|^{2}.

It is not hard to see that the above probability is maximal when
m=\ell
, in which case

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}[m=\ell]=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left\lfloor\frac{r+1}{2}\right\rfloor^{2}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor^{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\qquad&r~\text{even},\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\qquad&r~\text{odd}.\end{cases}

Given the considerations above, Bob uses the following strategy: he picks
\ell
as the corresponding entry
w_{i}
from
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/2}
given the measured hyperedge
(M_{i,1},M_{i,2})
. If the outcome
m
from measuring his final state in
Equation

13
equals
w_{i}
, then he outputs
\mathsf{YES}
, otherwise he outputs
\mathsf{NO}
. Indeed, in the
\mathsf{YES}
instance,
w_{i}=x_{M_{i,1}}+x_{M_{i,2}}
and so
m
equals
w_{i}
with probability
1
, while in the
\mathsf{NO}
instance,
m
equals
w_{i}
with probability at most
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}
. Thus the communication protocol has one-sided error at most
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Pr}[\text{error}|\mathsf{YES}]=0
and
\operatorname{Pr}[\text{error}|\mathsf{NO}]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}
. By repeating the whole protocol
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
more times, the one-sided error probability can be decreased to
\varepsilon
: if in any of the repetitions the final measurement outcome is different from
w_{i}
, then Bob knows that
\mathsf{NO}
is the correct answer.
∎

4.2.
Quantum lower bound on
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching

In this section we shall turn our attention to proving quantum and classical lower bounds on the amount of communication required by the
r
-
\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
problem, but first we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\operatorname{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}})
be any mapping from an
n
-bit alphabet to
m
-qubit density matrices. Then for any
\delta\in[0,1/(r-1)]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\delta^{|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|^{2}_{1}\leq 2^{2(r-1)\delta m}.

Proof.

Let
p\triangleq 1+(r-1)\delta
. First note that, given the eigenvalues
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2^{m}}
from
f(x)
, which are non-negative reals that sum to
1
, we have

\displaystyle\|f(x)\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{m}}\sigma_{i}^{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2^{m}}\sigma_{i}=1.

Using
Theorem

5
and
Remark

4
, we now get

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left(\frac{p-1}{r-1}\right)^{|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\|f(x)\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{2/p}\leq\left(\frac{1}{r^{n}}\cdot r^{n}\right)^{2/p}=1.

On the other hand, note
p\leq q\implies\big(\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{m}}\sigma_{i}^{p}\big)^{1/p}\leq\big(\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{m}}\sigma_{i}^{q}\big)^{1/q}
by Hölder’s inequality, hence

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left(\frac{p-1}{r-1}\right)^{|S|}2^{-2m/p}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{p}^{2}\geq\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left(\frac{p-1}{r-1}\right)^{|S|}2^{-2m}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{1}^{2}.

Rearranging the inequalities leads to

\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left(\frac{p-1}{r-1}\right)^{|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{1}^{2}\leq 2^{2m(1-1/p)}\leq 2^{2m(p-1)}.\qed

We are now ready to state and prove our main quantum communication complexity lower bound for the
r
-ary Hidden Hypermatching problem.

Theorem 4.

Any quantum protocol that achieves advantage
\varepsilon>0
for the
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
problem with
t\geq 3
and
\alpha\leq\min(1/2,(r-1)^{-1/2})
requires
\Omega(r^{-(1+1/t)}(\varepsilon^{2}/\alpha)^{2/t}(n/t)^{1-2/t})
qubits of communication from Alice to Bob.

Notice that for
r=2
our lower bound reads
\Omega(\alpha^{-2/t}(n/t)^{1-2/t})
, which has a better dependence on
\alpha
compared to the lower bound
\Omega(\log(1/\alpha)(n/t)^{1-2/t})
from
(
Shi et al. 2012
)
. Also, see
Remark

9
at the end of the section for an improvement on the requirement
\alpha\leq\min(1/2,(r-1)^{-1/2})
.

Proof.

Consider an
m
-qubit communication protocol. An arbitrary
m
-qubit protocol can be viewed as Alice sending an encoding of her input
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
into a quantum state so that Bob can distinguish if his
w
was drawn from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}
or
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
.
Let
\rho:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\operatorname{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}})
be Alice’s encoding function. For our ‘hard’ distribution, Alice and Bob receive
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and
M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
, respectively, uniformly at random, while Bob’s input
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
is drawn from the distribution
\mathcal{D}\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
, i.e., with probability
1/2
is comes from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}
, and with probability
1/2
it comes from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
. Let
p_{x}\triangleq r^{-n}
,
p_{M}\triangleq|\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{t,n}|^{-1}
and
p_{w}\triangleq r^{-\alpha n/t}
, then our hard distribution
\mathcal{P}
is

(14)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}[x,\mathsf{YES},M,w]=\frac{1}{2}p_{x}\cdot p_{M}\cdot[Mx=w],\qquad\operatorname{Pr}[x,\mathsf{NO},M,w]=\frac{1}{2}p_{x}\cdot p_{M}\cdot p_{w}.

Conditioning on Bob’s input
(M,w)
, from his perspective, Alice sends the message
\rho(x)
with probability
\operatorname{Pr}[x|M,w]
. Therefore, conditioned on an instance of the problem (
\mathsf{YES}
or
\mathsf{NO}
), Bob receives one of the following two quantum states
\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}
and
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}
, each appearing with probability
\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{YES}|M,w]
and
\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{NO}|M,w]
, respectively,

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}&=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\operatorname{Pr}[x|\mathsf{YES},M,w]\cdot\rho(x)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{YES},M,w]}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\operatorname{Pr}[x,\mathsf{YES},M,w]\cdot\rho(x),\\
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}&=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\operatorname{Pr}[x|\mathsf{NO},M,w]\cdot\rho(x)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{NO},M,w]}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\operatorname{Pr}[x,\mathsf{NO},M,w]\cdot\rho(x).\end{aligned}

Bob’s best strategy to determine the distribution of
w
conditioning on his input
(M,w)
is no more than the chance to distinguish between these two quantum states
\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}
and
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}
.

Now let
\varepsilon_{\rm bias}
be the bias of the protocol that distinguishes between
\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}
and
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}
. According to
1
, the bias
\varepsilon_{\rm bias}
of any quantum protocol for a fixed
M
and
w
can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm bias}\leq\big\|{\operatorname{Pr}}[\mathsf{YES}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}-\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{NO}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}\big\|_{1}.

We prove in
Theorem

5
below that, if
m\leq\frac{\gamma}{r^{1+1/t}}(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\alpha})^{2/t}(n/t)^{1-2/t}
for a universal constant
\gamma
, then the average bias over
M
and
w
is at most
\varepsilon^{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{(M,w)\sim\mathcal{P}_{M,w}}[\varepsilon_{\rm bias}]\leq\varepsilon^{2},

where
\mathcal{P}_{M,w}
is the marginal distribution of
\mathcal{P}
. Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, for at least a
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of
M
and
w
, the bias in distinguishing between
\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}
and
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}
is
\varepsilon
-small. Therefore, Bob’s advantage over randomly guessing the right distribution will be at most
\varepsilon
(for the event that
M
and
w
are such that the distance between
\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}
and
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}
is more than
\varepsilon
) plus
\varepsilon/2
(for the advantage over random guessing when
\varepsilon_{\rm bias}\leq\varepsilon
), and so
m=\Omega(r^{-(1+1/t)}(\varepsilon^{2}/\alpha)^{2/t}(n/t)^{1-2/t})
.
∎

Theorem 5.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
,
M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
,
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
and
b\in\{\mathsf{YES},\mathsf{NO}\}
, consider the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
defined in
Equation

14
. Given an encoding function
\rho:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\operatorname{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}})
, consider the quantum states
\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}
and
\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}
from
Equation

15
. If
\alpha\leq\min(1/2,(r-1)^{-1/2})
, then there is a universal constant
\gamma>0
(independent of
n
,
t
,
r
and
\alpha
), such that, if
m\leq\frac{\gamma}{r^{1+1/t}}(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\alpha})^{2/t}(n/t)^{1-2/t}
for all
\varepsilon>0
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{(M,w)\sim\mathcal{P}_{M,w}}\left[\big\|\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{YES}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}-\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{NO}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}\big\|_{1}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

Proof.

For the ease of notation, we shall write

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm bias}\triangleq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{(M,w)\sim\mathcal{P}_{M,w}}\left[\big\|\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{YES}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}-\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{NO}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}\big\|_{1}\right].

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm bias}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}\operatorname{Pr}[M,w]\cdot\big\|\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{YES}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{YES}}^{M,w}-\operatorname{Pr}[\mathsf{NO}|M,w]\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{NO}}^{M,w}\big\|_{1}

(
Equation

15
and conditional probability)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}\Big\|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\Big(\operatorname{Pr}[x,\mathsf{YES},M,w]\cdot\rho(x)-\operatorname{Pr}[x,\mathsf{NO},M,w]\cdot\rho(x)\Big)\Big\|_{1}

(
Equation

14
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}\Big\|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}p_{x}\cdot p_{M}\left(\big[Mx=w\big]-p_{w}\right)\rho(x)\Big\|_{1}

(Fourier decomposition of
\rho
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}\Big\|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}p_{x}\cdot p_{M}\left(\big[Mx=w\big]-p_{w}\right)\cdot\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\widehat{\rho}(S)\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}\Big\|\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}u(M,w,S)\widehat{\rho}(S)\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}|u(M,w,S)|\cdot\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1},

where we defined

(16)

\displaystyle u(M,w,S)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}p_{x}\cdot p_{M}\cdot\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\left(\big[Mx=w\big]-p_{w}\right).

Next, we upper bound the quantity
u(M,w,S)
using the lemma below. In the following lemma, let
I(M)=\bigcup_{i\in[\alpha n/t],j\in[t]}\{M_{i,j}\}
be the set of indices of the
\alpha
-partial matching
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n,t}^{\alpha}
. Moreover, we shall write
S|_{M_{i}}=S_{M_{i,1}}S_{M_{i,2}}\dots S_{M_{i,t}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{t}
to denote the string
S
restricted to the hyperedge
M_{i}=(M_{i,1},\dots,M_{i,t})
, where
S_{M_{i,j}}
is the
M_{i,j}
-th entry of
S
. The same applies to
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
.

Lemma 6.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{t,n}
and
I(M)=\bigcup_{i\in[\alpha n/t],j\in[t]}\{M_{i,j}\}
. Define the set

\displaystyle\Delta(M)=\{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\setminus\{0^{n}\}~|~S_{M_{i,1}}=S_{M_{i,2}}=\cdots=S_{M_{i,t}}~\forall i\in[\alpha n/t]~\text{and}~S_{j}=0~\forall j\notin I(M)\}.

Given
u(M,w,S)
as defined in
Equation

16
for
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
and
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, we have

u(M,w,S)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot r^{-\alpha n/t}\cdot p_{M}

if
S\in\Delta(M)
and 0 if
S\notin\Delta(M)
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
u
:

u(M,w,S)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}p_{x}\cdot p_{M}\cdot\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\left(\big[Mx=w\big]-p_{w}\right).

In order to understand this expression, we start with the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\big[Mx=w\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\left[(Mx)_{i}=w_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{t}x_{M_{i,j}}=w_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left(\prod_{j\notin I(M)}\omega_{r}^{S_{j}x_{j}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\omega_{r}^{S|_{M_{i}}\cdot x|_{M_{i}}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{t}x_{M_{i,j}}=w_{i}\right]\right)

(Relabelling
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j\notin I(M)}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\omega_{r}^{S_{j}x}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{t}}\omega_{r}^{S|_{M_{i}}\cdot x}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{t}x_{j}=w_{i}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=r^{n(1-\alpha)}[S_{j}=0~\forall j\notin I(M)]\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{t}}\omega_{r}^{S|_{M_{i}}\cdot x}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{t}x_{j}=w_{i}\right].

Now we use that
\sum_{j=1}^{t}x_{j}=w_{i}\implies x_{t}=w_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}x_{j}
modulo
r
, so that

\displaystyle S|_{M_{i}}\cdot x=\sum_{j=1}^{t}S_{M_{i,j}}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}S_{M_{i,j}}x_{j}+S_{M_{i,t}}\left(w_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}x_{j}\right)=S_{M_{i,t}}w_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}(S_{M_{i,j}}-S_{M_{i,t}})x_{j}

modulo
r
. This leads to (assuming that
S_{j}=0
for all
j\notin I(M)
)

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\big[Mx=w\big]

\displaystyle=r^{n(1-\alpha)}\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\omega_{r}^{S_{M_{i,t}}w_{i}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{t-1}}\omega_{r}^{\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}(S_{M_{i,j}}-S_{M_{i,t}})x_{j}}=\frac{r^{n}}{r^{\alpha n/t}}\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\omega_{r}^{S_{M_{i,t}}w_{i}}

if, for all
i\in[\alpha n/t]
,
S_{M_{i,j}}
is constant for all
j\in[t]
, i.e., if
S_{M_{i,1}}=S_{M_{i,2}}=\dots=S_{M_{i,t}}
for all
i\in[\alpha n/t]
. Otherwise the above expression is
0
.
Thus, if
S_{M_{i,1}}=S_{M_{i,2}}=\dots=S_{M_{i,t}}
for all
i\in[\alpha n/t]
and
S_{j}=0
for
j\notin I(M)
, then we can use
Equation

17
to get (remember that
p_{x}\triangleq r^{-n}
and
p_{w}\triangleq r^{-\alpha n/t}
)

\displaystyle|u(M,w,S)|=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}p_{x}p_{M}\omega_{r}^{S\cdot x}\left(\big[Mx=w\big]-p_{w}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{M}}{2r^{\alpha n/t}}\left|\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha n/t}\omega_{r}^{S_{M_{i,t}}w_{i}}-[S=0^{n}]\right|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&~\text{if}~S=0^{n},\\
\frac{1}{2}r^{-\alpha n/t}p_{M}&~\text{if}~S\neq 0^{n}.\end{cases}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle|u(M,w,S)|=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}~S=0^{n},\\
\frac{1}{2}r^{-\alpha n/t}p_{M}&\text{if}~S_{M_{i,1}}=S_{M_{1,2}}=\dots=S_{M_{i,t}}~\forall i\in[\alpha n/t]~\text{and}~S_{j}=0~\forall j\notin I(M),\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

proving the lemma statement
∎

We now proceed to upper bound
\varepsilon_{\rm bias}
using the expression for
|u(M,w,S)|
from
Lemma

6
. For
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, let
|S|\triangleq|\{i\in[n]:S_{i}\neq 0\}|
. Notice that, if
S\in\Delta(M)
, then
|S|=kt
for some
k\in[\alpha n/t]
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm bias}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{t,n}\\
S\in\Delta(M)\end{subarray}}p_{M}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}}\frac{1}{r^{\alpha n/t}}\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha n/t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\
|S|=kt\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{t,n}\\
S\in\Delta(M)\end{subarray}}p_{M}\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha n/t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\
|S|=kt\end{subarray}}\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[S\in\Delta(M)]\cdot\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1},

using that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{t,n}:S\in\Delta(M)\end{subarray}}p_{M}=\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}_{t,n}}[S\in\Delta(M)].

We now upper bound this probability using the following lemma.

Lemma 7.

Let
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
with
k_{j}\triangleq\frac{1}{t}\cdot|\{i\in[n]:S_{i}=j\}|\in\mathbb{Z}

for
j\in\{1,\dots,r-1\}
. Let
k\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}k_{j}
. For any
M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
, let
\Delta(M)
be the set from
Lemma

6
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[S\in\Delta(M)]=\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\frac{k!}{(kt)!}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(k_{j}t)!}{k_{j}!}.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
S=1^{k_{1}t}2^{k_{2}t}\dots(r-1)^{k_{r-1}t}0^{n-kt}
. First note that the total number
|\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}|
of
\alpha
-partial hypermatchings is
n!/\big((t!)^{\alpha n/t}(\alpha n/t)!(n-\alpha n)!\big)
. This can be seen as follows: pick a permutation of
n
, view the first
\alpha n/t
tuples of length
t
as
\alpha n/t
hyperedges, and ignore the ordering within each hyperedge, the ordering of the
\alpha n/t
hyperedges and the ordering of the last
n-\alpha n
vertices. Now, given our particular
S
, notice that
S\in\Delta(M)
if, for
j\in[r-1]
,
M
has exactly
k_{j}
hyperedges in

\left\{1+t\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}k_{i},~2+t\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}k_{i},~3+t\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}k_{i},\ldots,(k_{j}-1)+t\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}k_{i},~t\sum_{i=1}^{j}k_{i}\right\},

i.e.,
k_{1}
hyperedges in
\{1,\dots,k_{1}t\}
,
k_{2}
hyperedges in
\{k_{1}t+1,\dots,(k_{2}+k_{1})t\}
, etc., and also
\alpha n/t-k

. The number of ways to pick

(k_{j}t)!/((t!)^{k_{j}}k_{j}!)

(n-kt)!/((t!)^{\alpha n/t-k}(\alpha n/t-k)!(n-\alpha n)!)

\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[S\in\Delta(M)]

\frac{\frac{(n-kt)!}{(t!)^{\alpha n/t-k}(\alpha n/t-k)!(n-\alpha n)!}}{\frac{n!}{(t!)^{\alpha n/t}(\alpha n/t)!(n-\alpha n)!}}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(k_{j}t)!}{(t!)^{k_{j}}k_{j}!}=\frac{(n-kt)!(\alpha n/t)!}{n!(\alpha n/t-k)!}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(k_{j}t)!}{k_{j}!}=\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\frac{k!}{(kt)!}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(k_{j}t)!}{k_{j}!}.\qed

Using

as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm bias}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha n/t}\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1}\geq 0\\

|S|_{i}=k_{i}t,~i\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\frac{k!^{2}}{(kt)!^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(k_{j}t)!^{2}}{k_{j}!^{2}}}\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha n/t}\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{kt}}}\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1}\geq 0\\

. Since

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1}\geq 0\\

where the last equality follows from the the multinomial theorem, then

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon_{\rm bias}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha n/t}\alpha^{k}\frac{\binom{n/t}{k}}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{kt}}}(r-1)^{k/2}\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}:|S|=kt\end{subarray}}\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1}^{2}},

for

Sum I

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}:|S|=kt\end{subarray}}\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{kt}}\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\delta^{|S|}\|\widehat{f}(S)\|_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{kt}}2^{2r\delta m}=\left(\frac{2^{1/(2t)}4rm}{k}\right)^{kt}.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=4rm}^{\alpha n/t}\alpha^{k}\frac{\binom{n/t}{k}}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{kt}}}(r-1)^{k/2}\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}:|S|=kt\end{subarray}}\|\widehat{\rho}(S)\|_{1}^{2}}

Remark 9.

The dependence of

4.3.
Quantum streaming lower bound for Unique Games on hypergraphs

The Unique Games problem is a generalization of the classical Max-Cut and can in fact be viewed as constraint satisfaction problems on a graph but over a larger alphabet. Consider a graph on
n
vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and edges in
E
. The constraint on an arbitrary edge
(i,j)\in E
is specified by a permutation
\pi_{i,j}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
and the goal is to find an assignment of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
that maximizes

\sum_{(i,j)\in E}[\pi_{i,j}(x_{i})=x_{j}].

In this section, we consider a generalization of Unique Games to hypergraphs.

Definition 10 (Unique Games instance on hypergraphs).

A hypergraph
H=(V,E)
is defined on a vertex set
V
of size
n
with
t
-sized hyperedges
E
(i.e.,
t
-sized subsets of
V
). Given a linear constraint on a hyperedge
e\in E
, i.e., a linear function
\pi_{e}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, the goal is to compute

\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\sum_{e\in E}[\pi_{e}(x_{e})=0],

where
x_{e}
corresponds to the set of vertex-assignment in the hyperedge
e\in E
.

Definition 11.

Let
H=(V,E)
be a hypergraph and let
\operatorname{OPT}
be the optimal value of the Unique Games on
H
. For
\gamma\geq 1
, a randomized algorithm gives a
\gamma
-approximation to a Unique Games instance with failure probability
\delta\in[0,1/2)
if, on any input hypergraph
H
, it outputs a value in the interval
[\operatorname{OPT}/\gamma,\operatorname{OPT}]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

A uniformly random assignment of
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
to the vertex set
V
will satisfy a
1/r
-fraction of the hyperedges, since each linear constraint
\pi_{e}(x_{e})
is satisfied with probability
1/r
. This gives a trivial
r
-approximation algorithm for the problem above. Below we show that any better than trivial approximation requires space that scales as
n^{\beta}
for constant
\beta>0
.

Theorem 12.

Let
r,t\geq 2
be integers. Every quantum streaming algorithm giving an
(r-\varepsilon)
-approximation for Unique Games on hypergraphs (as in
Definition

10
) with at most
t
-sized hyperedges with alphabet size
r
and success probability at least
2/3
over its internal randomness, needs
\Omega((n/t)^{1-2/t})
space (which hides dependence on
r,\varepsilon
).

The proof of this theorem combines techniques used by Guruswami and Tao
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
and Kapralov, Khanna, and Sudan
(
Kapralov
et al. 2015
)
. Akin to these works, based on the Hidden Matching problem, we will construct instances of the hypergraph for which a Unique Games instance is hard to solve space-efficiently in the streaming model.

Input distributions.

To this end, we construct two distributions
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{N}
such that
\mathcal{Y}
is supported on satisfiable Unique Games instances and
\mathcal{N}
is supported on instances for which at most an
O(1/r)
-fraction of the constraints is satisfied. We now define these instances in a multi-stage way (using
k
stages). First, sample
k
independent
\alpha
-partial
t
-hypermatchings on
n
vertices and then construct a hypergraph
G
by putting together all the hyperedges from these
k
stages. Note that
G
still has
n
vertices, while the number of hyperedges is
k\cdot\alpha n/t
(since each stage has
\alpha n/t
many hyperedges and we allow multiple hyperedges should they be sampled). Now we specify the constraints
\pi_{e}
in
Definition

10
for the
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{N}
distributions:

•

\mathcal{Y}
distribution: sample
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and for each
e\in E
, let
\pi_{e}(x_{e})=\sum_{i\in e}(x_{i}-z_{i})
(where by
i\in e
we mean all the vertices in the hyperedge
e
).

•

\mathcal{N}
distribution: for each
e\in E
, pick a uniform
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
and let
\pi_{e}(x_{e})=q-\sum_{i\in e}x_{i}
.

It is clear that, in the
\mathcal{Y}
distribution, the optimal solution is when all the
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are just set to
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
. Below we show that for the
\mathcal{N}
distribution, the value of the optimal solution is at most
(1+\varepsilon)/r
with high probability.

Lemma 13.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. If
k=O(rt\log(r)/(\alpha\varepsilon^{2}))
, then for the Unique Games instance sampled from the distribution
\mathcal{N}
above, the optimal fraction of satisfiable constraints (i.e., number of hyperedges
e\in E
for which
\pi_{e}(\cdot)
evaluates to
0
) over all possible vertex labelling is at most
(1+\varepsilon)/r
with high probability.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma is similar to the proof in
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
, Lemma 4.1)
. Fix an assignment
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
. Let
X^{\ell}_{e}
be the random variable that indicates that the hyperedge
e\in E
appears in the
\ell
-th stage and is satisfied by
x
. Let
S=\sum_{\ell,e}X^{\ell}_{e}
. The expectation of
S
is
k\alpha n/t\cdot 1/r
, since the total number of hyperedges is
\alpha n/t
for each of the
k
stages and the probability that a uniform
x
satisfies a
t
-hyperedge (i.e., probability that
\sum_{e\in E}x_{e}=q
for some fixed
q
) is
1/r
. Using the same analysis as in
(
Guruswami and Tao 2019
)
, we can show that the variables
X^{\ell}_{e}
are negatively correlated. Indeed, first note that hyperedges from different stages are independent. Now suppose we know that the random variables
X^{\ell}_{e_{1}},\dots,X^{\ell}_{e_{s}}
have value
1
, and we also know a hyperedge
e\in E
. If
e\cap e_{u}\neq\emptyset
for some
u\in[s]
, then
X^{\ell}_{e}=0
, since the hyperedges of a given stage form a matching. Otherwise, the conditional expectation of
X^{\ell}_{e}
(conditioned on
e\cap e_{u}=\emptyset
for all
u\in[s]
) is
\frac{\alpha n/t-s}{r}\binom{n-ts}{t}^{-1}
, which is less than its unconditional expectation of
\frac{\alpha n/t}{r}\binom{n}{t}^{-1}
. Therefore, in all cases one has
\mathbb{E}[X^{\ell}_{e}|X^{\ell}_{e_{1}}=\cdots=X^{\ell}_{e_{s}}=1]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{\ell}_{e}]
, which means negative correlation.

Hence, a Chernoff bound for negative-correlated variables (see, e.g.,
(
Panconesi and
Srinivasan 1997
, Theorem 3.2)
) yields

\Pr[S\geq(1+\varepsilon)(k\alpha n/t)/r]\leq\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}k\alpha n/(3rt))=\exp(-O(n\log r)),

where the inequality used the choice of
k
. Applying a union bound over the set of
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The reduction to

Lemma 14.

\mathcal{N}

In order to prove this lemma we need a few definitions and facts. First, towards proving the lemma above, let us assume there is a

\mathcal{A}
for
Lemma

14
. During the execution of the streaming protocol on instances from the

\mathcal{N}

8

Suppose there exists a streaming protocol for distinguishing the

\Omega(1/k)

Suppose

(3)

and constraints of stage
j^{*}+1
(which is classical) to the quantum state after
j^{*}+1
. Thus we have
|\phi^{\mathsf{YES}}\rangle=|\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle=U|\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}},C^{\mathcal{Y}}\rangle
and
|\phi^{\mathsf{NO}}\rangle=U|\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}},C^{\mathcal{N}}\rangle
, where
C^{\mathcal{Y}}
and
C^{\mathcal{N}}
are the constraints corresponding to the
\mathsf{YES}
and
\mathsf{NO}
distributions, respectively, and, similarly, we have
|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle=U|{\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}}},C^{\mathcal{N}}\rangle
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\big\||\phi^{\mathsf{YES}}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathsf{NO}}\rangle\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\big\||\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle\big\|_{1}-\big\||\phi^{\mathsf{NO}}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\big\||\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle\big\|_{1}-\big\||\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}}\rangle\big\|_{1}=\Omega(1/k),

where the second inequality used that
\big\||\phi^{\mathsf{NO}}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}+1}\rangle\big\|_{1}=\big\|U|\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}},C^{\mathcal{N}}\rangle-U|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}},C^{\mathcal{N}}\rangle\big\|_{1}\leq\||\phi^{\mathcal{Y}}_{j^{*}}\rangle-|\phi^{\mathcal{N}}_{j^{*}}\rangle\|_{1}
(since unitaries preserve norms) and the third inequality is because
j^{*}
is an informative index. Hence in Step (3) of the procedure above, the bias of Bob in obtaining the right outcome is
\Omega(1/k)
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

12
.

Finally, by picking
k=O(rt\log(r)/(\alpha\varepsilon^{2}))
in order to invoke
Lemma

13
and using our lower bound in
Theorem

4
with
\alpha=O(1)
, we get our desired lower bound of

\Omega(r^{-(1+1/t)}(k^{2}\alpha)^{-2/t}(n/t)^{1-2/t})=\Omega((n/t)^{1-2/t}).\qed

Remark 16.

It is possible to prove a classical version of
Theorem

12
by using the classical lower bound on
r
-
\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
, which leads to
\Omega((n/t)^{1-1/t})
classical space (hiding dependence on
r,\varepsilon
). Such bound, though, is already subsumed by
\Omega(n)
from Chou et al.
(
Chou et al. 2022b
)
.

5.
Locally Decodable Codes

In this section we prove our lower bound on locally decodable codes over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
. Before that, let us first formally define an
\mathsf{LDC}
.

Definition 1 (Locally decodable code).

A
(q,\delta,\varepsilon)
-locally decodable code over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
is a function
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
that satisfies the following: for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
, there exists a (randomized) algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that, on any input
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
that satisfies
d(y,C(x))\leq\delta N
, makes
q
queries to
y
non-adaptively and outputs a number
\mathcal{A}^{y}(i)\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
that satisfies
\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{y}(i)=x_{i}]\geq 1/r+\varepsilon
(where the probability is only taken over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
).

As is often the case when proving
\mathsf{LDC}
lower bounds, we use the useful fact proven by Katz and Trevisan
(
Katz and Trevisan 2000
)
that, without loss of generality, one can assume that an
\mathsf{LDC}
is smooth, i.e., the queries made by
\mathcal{A}
have “reasonable" probability over all indices, and that
\mathcal{A}
makes queries to a codeword (and not a corrupted codeword). We first formally define a smooth code below.

Definition 2 (Smooth code).

We say
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
is a
(q,c,\varepsilon)
-smooth code if there exists a decoding algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that satisfies the following: for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
,
\mathcal{A}
makes at most
q
non-adaptive queries to
C(x)
and outputs
\mathcal{A}^{C(x)}(i)\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
such that
\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{C(x)}(i)=x_{i}]\geq 1/r+\varepsilon
(where the probability is only taken over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
). Moreover, for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
,
i\in[n]
and
j\in[N]
, on input
i
, the probability that
\mathcal{A}
queries the index
j
in
C(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
is at most
c/N
.

Crucially note that smooth codes only require a decoder to recover
x_{i}
when given access to an actual codeword, unlike the standard definition of
\mathsf{LDC}
where a decoder is given a noisy codeword. With this definition in hand, we state a theorem of Katz and Trevisan.

Theorem 3 (
(
Katz and Trevisan 2000
)
).

A
(q,\delta,\varepsilon)
-
\mathsf{LDC}

C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
is a
(q,q/\delta,\varepsilon)
-smooth code.

We remark that a converse to this theorem holds: a
(q,c,\varepsilon)
-smooth code is a
(q,\delta,\varepsilon-c\delta)
-
\mathsf{LDC}
, since the probability that the decoder queries one of
\delta N
corrupted positions is at most
(c/N)(\delta N)=c\delta
.

We now present our lower bound for
\mathsf{LDC}
s over
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
using the non-commutative Khintchine’s inequality. We thank Jop Briët for the proof.

Remark 4.

It is possible to prove a weaker lower bound
N=2^{\Omega(\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{4}n/r^{4})}
using our matrix-valued hypercontractivity via the proof technique in
(
Ben-Aroya et al. 2008
, Theorem 11)
.

Theorem 5.

If
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{N}
is a
(2,\delta,\varepsilon)
-
\mathsf{LDC}
, then
N=2^{\Omega(\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}n/r^{2})}
.

Proof.

We know that
C
is also a
(2,2/\delta,\varepsilon)
-smooth code. Fix some
i\in[n]
. In order to decode
x_{i}
, we can assume, without loss of generality, that the decoder
\mathcal{A}
picks some set
\{u,v\}
, where
u,v\in[N]
, with probability
p(u,v)
, queries those bits, and then outputs
f^{u,v}_{i}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
that depends on the query-outputs. Given the smooth code property of outputting
x_{i}
with probability at least
\frac{1}{r}+\varepsilon
for every
x
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{Pr}_{u,v\sim[N]}[f^{u,v}_{i}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})=x_{i}]=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\sum_{u,v\in[N]}p(u,v)\omega_{r}^{k(f^{u,v}_{i}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})-x_{i})}\iff

\displaystyle r\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\sum_{u,v\in[N]}p(u,v)\omega_{r}^{k(f^{u,v}_{i}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})-x_{i})},

where the if and only if comes from separating the term
k=0
, which equals
\sum_{u,v\in[N]}p(u,v)=1
.

For
k\in[r-1]
, define the function
h_{i;k}^{u,v}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
by
h_{i;k}^{u,v}(x)=\omega_{r}^{kf_{i}^{u,v}(x)}
. Consider its Fourier transform,
\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
, defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(S)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{2}}h_{i;k}^{u,v}(x)\omega_{r}^{-S\cdot x}.

Hence we can write

\displaystyle\omega_{r}^{kf_{i}^{u,v}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(a,b)\omega_{r}^{aC(x)_{u}+bC(x)_{v}}.

Let
F^{i}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{rN\times rN}
be the matrix defined as
(F^{i}_{k})_{(u,a),(v,b)}=p(u,v)\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(a,b)
and let
C(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{rN}
be the vector defined as
C(x)_{(u,a)}=\omega_{r}^{aC(x)_{u}}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in[N]}p(u,v)\omega_{r}^{kf_{i}^{u,v}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})}=C(x)^{\top}F^{i}_{k}C(x),

which means that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left[\omega_{r}^{-kx_{i}}C(x)^{\top}F^{i}_{k}C(x)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\sum_{u,v\in[N]}p(u,v)\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left[\omega_{r}^{k(f_{i}^{u,v}(C(x)_{u},C(x)_{v})-x_{i})}\right]\geq r\varepsilon.

By summing over all
i\in[n]
, we finally get to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left[C(x)^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{r}^{-kx_{i}}F^{i}_{k}\right)C(x)\right]\geq r\varepsilon n.

The left-hand side can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left[C(x)^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{r}^{-kx_{i}}F^{i}_{k}\right)C(x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{r}^{-kx_{i}}F^{i}_{k}\right\|\|C(x)\|^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=rN\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{r}^{-kx_{i}}F^{i}_{k}\right\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2rN\sqrt{2\log(2rN)}\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|F_{k}^{i}\|^{2}},

where we used
Lemma

2
in the last step. Consider the submatrix
[p(u,v)\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(a,b)]_{a,b}
of
F_{k}^{i}
for fixed
u,v\in[N]
. Since

\displaystyle\|[p(u,v)\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(a,b)]_{a,b}\|^{2}\leq\|[p(u,v)\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(a,b)]_{a,b}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}p(u,v)^{2}|\widehat{h_{i;k}^{u,v}}(a,b)|^{2}=p(u,v)^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\delta^{2}N^{2}},

where we used Parseval’s identity and
p(u,v)\leq 2/\delta N
for all
u,v\in[N]
by the definition of smooth code, then
\|F_{k}^{i}\|^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\delta^{2}N^{2}}
. This finally leads to

2rN\sqrt{2\log(2rN)}(r-1)\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{\delta N}\geq r\varepsilon n\implies\log(2rN)\geq\frac{\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{8r^{2}}n\implies N\geq\frac{1}{2r}2^{\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}n/8r^{2}}.\qed

6.
2-server private information retrieval

As mentioned in the introduction, the connection between
\mathsf{LDC}
s and
PIR
is well known since the results of
(
Katz and Trevisan 2000
;
Goldreich et al. 2002
)
. In general, upper bounds on
\mathsf{LDC}
s are derived via
PIR
schemes, which in turn means that our
\mathsf{LDC}
lower bounds translate to
PIR
lower bounds, which we illustrate below. We first define the notion of private information retrieval.

Definition 1.

A one-round,
(1-\delta)
-secure,
k
-server private information retrieval
\mathsf{(PIR)}
scheme with alphabet
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, recovery probability
1/r+\varepsilon
, query size
t
and answer size
a
, consists of a randomized user and
k
deterministic algorithms
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
(the servers) that satisfy the following:

(1)

On input
i\in[n]
, the user produces
k
queries
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{t}
and sends them to the
k
servers respectively. The servers reply back with a string
a_{j}=S_{j}(x,q_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}
, and based on
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
and
i
, the user outputs
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

(2)

For every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
, the output
b
of the user satisfies
\Pr[b=x_{i}]\geq 1/r+\varepsilon
.

(3)

For every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and
j\in[k]
, the distributions over
q_{j}
(over the user’s randomness) are
\delta
-close for different
i\in[n]
.

We crucially remark that for the lower bounds that we present below, the function
S_{j}
could be an arbitrary (not necessarily linear) function over
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

Our
PIR
lower bound follows from a result of Goldreich et al.
(
Goldreich et al. 2002
)
and from a generalization of
(
Kerenidis and de Wolf 2003
, Lemma 2)
. We remark that Goldreich et al. state the lemma below only for
r=2
, but the exact same analysis carries over to
r>2
. In the following we shall assume
\delta=0
.

Lemma 2 (
(
Goldreich et al. 2002
, Lemma 5.1)
).

If there is a classical
2
-server
\mathsf{PIR}
scheme with alphabet
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, query size
t
, answer size
a
, and recovery probability
1/r+\varepsilon
, then there is a
(2,3,\varepsilon)
-smooth code
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow(\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a})^{m}
with
m\leq 6r^{t}
.

Lemma 3.

Let
r\geq 2
be prime. Let
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to(\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a})^{m}
be a
(2,c,\varepsilon)
-smooth code. Then there is a
(2,cr^{a},2\varepsilon/r^{a+2})
-smooth code
C^{\prime}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{mr^{a}}
that is good on average, i.e., there is a decoder
\mathcal{A}
such that, for all
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}\left[\operatorname{Pr}[\mathcal{A}^{C^{\prime}(x)}(i)=x_{i}]\right]\geq\frac{1}{r}+\frac{2\varepsilon}{r^{a+2}}.

Proof.

We form a new code
C^{\prime}
by transforming each old string
C(x)_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}
using the Hadamard code into
C^{\prime}(x)_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{r^{a}}
,
C^{\prime}(x)=(\langle C(x),y\rangle)_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}
. The total length of
C^{\prime}
is
mr^{a}
. The new decoder uses the same randomness as the old one. Let
f:(\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a})^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}
be the output function of the old decoder. Fix the queries
j,k\in[m]
. We now describe the new decoding procedure.

First, if for
j,k
the function
f
is such that
\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(C(x)_{j},C(x)_{k})=x_{i}]\leq\frac{1}{r}
, then the new decoder outputs a random value in
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, in which case it is as good as the old one for an average
x
. Consider now the case
\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(C(x)_{j},C(x)_{k})=x_{i}]=\frac{1}{r}+\eta
for some
\eta>0
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(C(x)_{j},C(x)_{k})=x_{i}]=\frac{1}{r}+\eta\iff\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\big[\omega_{r}^{\ell(f(C(x)_{j},C(x)_{k})-x_{i})}\big]=r\eta.

For
\ell\in[r-1]
, define the function
h_{\ell}:(\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a})^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
by
h_{\ell}(a,b)=\omega_{r}^{\ell f(a,b)}
. Consider its Fourier transform
\widehat{h}_{\ell}:(\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a})^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
. Hence we can write

\displaystyle h_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{S,T\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}\widehat{h}_{\ell}(S,T)\omega_{r}^{S\cdot a+T\cdot b}

and then

\displaystyle r\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}\sum_{S,T\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}\widehat{h}_{\ell}(S,T)\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\big[\omega_{r}^{S\cdot C(x)_{j}+T\cdot C(x)_{k}-\ell x_{i}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}\sum_{S,T\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}|\widehat{h}_{\ell}(S,T)|^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}\sum_{S,T\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\big[\omega_{r}^{S\cdot C(x)_{j}+T\cdot C(x)_{k}-\ell x_{i}}\big]\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{r-1}\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}\sum_{S,T\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\big[\omega_{r}^{S\cdot C(x)_{j}+T\cdot C(x)_{k}-\ell x_{i}}\big]\right|^{2}},

where we used Parseval’s identity
\sum_{S,T\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}}|\widehat{h}_{\ell}(S,T)|^{2}=1
for all
\ell\in[r-1]
. It then follows that there are
S_{0},T_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a}
and
\ell_{0}\in[r-1]
such that

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\big[\omega_{r}^{S_{0}\cdot C(x)_{j}+T_{0}\cdot C(x)_{k}-\ell_{0}x_{i}}\big]\right|\geq\frac{\eta}{r^{a}}.

We now use the following useful lemma whose proof is left to the end of the section.

Lemma 4.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Suppose that
\Big|{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}\big[\omega_{r}^{f(x)}\big]\Big|\geq\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=y]\geq\frac{1}{r}+\frac{2\alpha}{r^{2}}.

According to the above lemma, there is
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[S_{0}\cdot C(x)_{j}+T_{0}\cdot C(x)_{k}-y=\ell_{0}x_{i}]\geq\frac{1}{r}+\frac{2\eta}{r^{a+2}}.

Since both numbers
S_{0}\cdot C(x)_{j}
and
T_{0}\cdot C(x)_{k}
are in the code
C^{\prime}
, it is possible to recover
x_{i}
(the inverse of
\ell_{0}
is well defined since
r
is prime) with just two queries and average probability at least
1/r+2\eta/r^{a+2}
. Averaging over the classical randomness, i.e.,
j,k
and
f
, gives the lemma. Finally, the
c
-smoothness of
C
translates into
cr^{a}
-smoothness of
C^{\prime}
.
∎

We now get the following main theorem.

Theorem 5.

Let
r\geq 2
be prime. A classical
2
-server
\mathsf{PIR}
scheme with alphabet
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, query size
t
, answer size
a
, and recovery probability
1/r+\varepsilon
satisfies
t\geq\Omega\big((\varepsilon^{2}n/r^{4a+6}-a)/\log{r}\big)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2
, there is a
(2,3,\varepsilon)
-smooth code
C:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\rightarrow(\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{a})^{m}
with
m\leq 6r^{t}
.
Lemma

3
allows us to transform
C
into a
(2,3r^{a},2\varepsilon/r^{a+2})
-smooth code
C^{\prime}:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{mr^{a}}
. Using
Theorem

5
on
C^{\prime}
(note the theorem can be applied directly to a smooth code that is good on average) yields

mr^{a}\geq 2^{\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}n/r^{4a+6})},

and since
m=O(r^{t})
, we get the desired lower bound in the theorem statement.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4
.

First notice that

\displaystyle\Big|{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}\big[\omega_{r}^{f(x)}\big]\Big|=\left|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}{\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}[f(x)=k]\omega_{r}^{k}\right|,

hence, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\left|{\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}[f(x)=k]-\beta\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\left|{\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}[f(x)=k]\omega_{r}^{k}-\beta\omega_{r}^{k}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\left(\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=k]\omega_{r}^{k}-\beta\omega_{r}^{k}\right)\right|=\Big|{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}\big[\omega_{r}^{f(x)}\big]\Big|\geq\alpha.

Therefore, there exists
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
such that

\displaystyle\left|{\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}}[f(x)=y]-\beta\right|\geq\frac{\alpha}{r}.

Take
\beta=\frac{1}{r}-\alpha\frac{r-2}{r^{2}}
. Then either
\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=y]\geq\beta+\frac{\alpha}{r}=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{2\alpha}{r^{2}}
, and so the lemma follows, or
\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=y]\leq\beta-\frac{\alpha}{r}=\frac{1}{r}-2\alpha\frac{r-1}{r^{2}}
. In the second case,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=k]=1\implies\sum_{k\neq y}\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=k]\geq 1-\frac{1}{r}+2\alpha\frac{r-1}{r^{2}},

and so there exists
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
such that

\operatorname*{Pr}_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}[f(x)=z]\geq\frac{1}{r-1}-\frac{1}{r(r-1)}+\frac{2\alpha}{r^{2}}=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{2\alpha}{r^{2}}.\qed

Appendix A
Classical Hidden Hypermatching lower bound

The general idea behind the proof of
Theorem

8
is already well established and is a simple generalization of results from
(
Gavinsky et al. 2007
;
Verbin and Yu 2011
;
Guruswami and Tao 2019
;
Doriguello and
Montanaro 2020
)
. We shall need the following well-known fact and a generalization of the KKL inequality
(
Kahn
et al. 1988
)
.

Fact 1.

Given just one sample, the best success probability in distinguishing between two probability distributions
p
and
q
is
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|p-q\|_{\operatorname{tvd}}
.

Lemma 2 (Generalized KKL inequality).

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be its indicator function (
f(x)=1
iff
x\in A
). Then, for every
\delta\in[0,1/r]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\delta^{|S|}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{|A|}{r^{n}}\right)^{2/(1+r\delta)}.

Proof.

Apply the hypercontractive inequality to real-valued functions with
p=1+r\delta
.
∎

Theorem 3.

Any classical protocol that achieves advantage
\varepsilon>0
for the
r\text{-}\mathsf{HH}(\alpha,t,n)
problem with
t\geq 2
and
\alpha\leq 1/2
needs
\Omega(r^{-1}(\varepsilon^{4}/\alpha)^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t})
bits of communication from Alice to Bob.

Proof.

By the minimax principle, it suffices to analyse
deterministic
protocols under some ‘hard’ input distribution. For our input distribution, Alice and Bob receive
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
and
M\in\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}
, respectively, uniformly at random, while Bob’s input
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
is drawn from the distribution
\mathcal{D}\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
, i.e., with probability
1/2
is comes from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}
, and with probability
1/2
it comes from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
.

Fix a small constant
\varepsilon>0
and let
c=\gamma r^{-1}(\varepsilon^{4}/\alpha)^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t}
for some universal constant
\gamma
. Consider any classical deterministic protocol that communicates at most
C\triangleq c-\log(1/\varepsilon)
bits. Such protocol partitions the set of all
r^{n}

x
’s into
2^{C}
sets. These sets have size
r^{n}/2^{C}
on average, and by a counting argument, with probability
1-\varepsilon
, the set
A
corresponding to Alice’s message has size at least
\varepsilon r^{n}/2^{C}=r^{n}/2^{c}
. Given Alice’s message, Bob knows that the random variable
X
corresponding to her input was drawn uniformly at random from
A
, and he also knows his input
M
. Therefore his knowledge of the random variable
MX
is described by the distribution

\displaystyle p_{M}(z)\triangleq\operatorname{Pr}[MX=z|M,A]=\frac{|\{x\in A|Mx=z\}|}{|A|}.

Given one sample of
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
, Bob must decide whether it came from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{YES}}
(the distribution
MX
) or from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{NO}}
(the uniform distribution
U
). According to
1
, the advantage of any classical protocol in distinguishing between
p_{M}
and
U
is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{4}\|p_{M}-U\|_{\text{tvd}}
. We prove in
Theorem

4
below that, if
\alpha\leq 1/2
and
c\leq\frac{\gamma}{r}(\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\alpha})^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t}
, then the average advantage over all hypermatchings
M
is at most
\varepsilon^{2}/4
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|_{\text{tvd}}]\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, for at least a
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of
M
, the advantage in distinguishing between
p_{M}
and
U
is
\varepsilon/4
-small. Hence, Bob’s total advantage over randomly guessing the right distribution will be at most
\varepsilon
(for the event that
A
is too small) plus
\varepsilon
(for the event that
M
is such that the distance between
MX
and
U
is more than
\varepsilon
) plus
\varepsilon/4
(for the advantage over random guessing when
\|p_{M}-U\|_{\text{tvd}}\leq\varepsilon
), and so
c=\Omega(r^{-1}(\varepsilon^{4}/\alpha)^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t})
.
∎

Theorem 4.

Let
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
be uniformly distributed over a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
of size
|A|\geq r^{n}/2^{c}
for some
c\geq 1
. If
\alpha\leq 1/2
, there is a universal constant
\gamma>0
(independent of
n
,
t
,
r
and
\alpha
), such that, for all
\varepsilon>0
, if
c\leq\frac{\gamma}{r}(\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\alpha})^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t}
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|_{\operatorname{tvd}}]\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

Proof.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be the characteristic function of
A
, i.e.,
f(x)=1
iff
x\in A
. We shall bound the Fourier coefficients of
p_{M}
, which are related to the Fourier coefficients of
f
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{M}(V)=\frac{1}{r^{\alpha n/t}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}p_{M}(z)\omega_{r}^{-V\cdot z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|r^{\alpha n/t}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}|\{x\in A:Mx=z\}|\cdot\omega_{r}^{-V\cdot z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|r^{\alpha n/t}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}|\{x\in A:(Mx)\cdot V=k\}|\cdot\omega_{r}^{-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|r^{\alpha n/t}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}|\{x\in A:x\cdot(M^{\top}V)=k\}|\cdot\omega_{r}^{-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|r^{\alpha n/t}}\sum_{x\in A}\omega_{r}^{-x\cdot(M^{\top}V)}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{n}}{|A|r^{\alpha n/t}}\widehat{f}(M^{\top}V).

We now start bounding the expected
squared
total variation distance,

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|^{2}_{\operatorname{tvd}}]

\displaystyle\leq r^{2\alpha n/t}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|^{2}_{2}]

(Parseval’s identity)

\displaystyle=r^{2\alpha n/t}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\left[\sum_{V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}\setminus\{0^{\alpha n/t}\}}|\widehat{p}_{M}(V)|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\left[\sum_{V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}\setminus\{0^{\alpha n/t}\}}|\widehat{f}(M^{\top}V)|^{2}\right].

Note that there is at most one
V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}
such that
S=M^{\top}V
for a given
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
(and that the only
V
that makes
M^{\top}V=0^{n}
is
V=0^{\alpha n/t}
). This allows us to transform the expectation over hypermatchings into a probability,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|^{2}_{\operatorname{tvd}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}\left[\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\setminus\{0^{n}\}}|\{V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}:M^{\top}V=S\}|\cdot|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\setminus\{0^{n}\}}\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\exists V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}:M^{\top}V=S]\cdot|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}.

Now observe that
\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\exists V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}:M^{\top}V=S]
is exactly the probability from
Lemma

7
, i.e., given
S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
with
k_{j}\triangleq\frac{1}{t}\cdot|\{i\in[n]:S_{i}=j\}|\in\mathbb{Z}
for
j\in[r-1]
(the number of entries from
S
equal to
j\neq 0
must be a multiple of
t
), and defining
k\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}k_{j}
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Pr}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\exists V\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{\alpha n/t}:M^{\top}V=S]=\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\frac{k!}{(kt)!}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(k_{j}t)!}{k_{j}!}\leq\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}},

and so

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|^{2}_{\operatorname{tvd}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\alpha n/t}\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\
|S|=kt\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}.

Similarly to the quantum proof, we shall split the above sum into two parts: one in the range
1\leq k<2rc
, and the other in the range
2rc\leq k\leq\alpha n/t
.

Sum I
(
1\leq k<2rc
): in order to upper bound each term, pick
\delta=k/(2rc)
in
Lemma

2
, thus

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\
|S|=kt\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}\leq\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\frac{1}{\delta^{kt}}\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}\delta^{|S|}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{kt}}\left(\frac{r^{n}}{|A|}\right)^{2r\delta/(1+r\delta)}\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{kt}}\left(\frac{r^{n}}{|A|}\right)^{2r\delta}\leq\left(\frac{2^{1/t}2rc}{k}\right)^{kt}.

By using that
c\leq\frac{\gamma}{r}(\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\alpha})^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t}
and
\binom{q}{s}\binom{\ell q}{\ell s}^{-1}\leq(\frac{s}{q})^{(\ell-1)s}
(see
(
Shi et al. 2012
, Appendix A.5)
) for
q=n/t,s=k,\ell=t
, we therefore have

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{2rc-1}\alpha^{k}\frac{\binom{n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\
|S|=kt\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{2rc-1}\alpha^{k}\left(\frac{kt}{n}\right)^{(1-1/t)kt}\left(\frac{2^{1/t}2rc}{k}\right)^{kt}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{2rc-1}\ \left(\frac{2^{1/t}2\gamma\varepsilon^{4/t}}{k^{1/t}}\right)^{kt}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{2},

where we used that
\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}\leq\alpha^{k}\binom{n/t}{k}
for
\alpha\in[0,1]
at the beginning and picked
\gamma
sufficiently small.

Sum II
(
2rc\leq k\leq\alpha n/t
): first note that the function
g(k)\triangleq\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}/\binom{n}{kt}
is decreasing in the interval
1\leq k\leq\alpha n/t
(since
\alpha\leq 1/2
). Hence, by using Parseval’s identity
\sum_{S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}=|A|/r^{n}
and the inequality
\binom{q}{s}\binom{\ell q}{\ell s}^{-1}\leq(\frac{s}{q})^{(\ell-1)s}
(for
q=n/t,s=2m,\ell=t
) in order to bound
g(2rc)
,

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2n}}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{k=2rc}^{\alpha n/t}\frac{\binom{\alpha n/t}{k}}{\binom{n}{kt}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\\
|S|=kt\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{c}g(2rc)\leq 2^{c}\alpha^{2rc}\left(\frac{2rc}{n/t}\right)^{2(t-1)rc}=2^{c}\alpha^{2rc/t}\left(\frac{2\gamma\varepsilon^{4/t}}{(n/t)^{1/t}}\right)^{2(t-1)rc}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{2},

where the last step used that
2(1-1/t)c\geq 1\implies\varepsilon^{2(1-1/t)c}\leq\varepsilon
and picked
\gamma
sufficiently small.

Summing both results, if
c\leq\frac{\gamma}{r}(\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\alpha})^{1/t}(n/t)^{1-1/t}
, then
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|^{2}_{\operatorname{tvd}}]\leq\varepsilon^{4}
. By Jensen’s inequality, we finally get
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|_{\operatorname{tvd}}]\leq\sqrt{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{M\sim\mathcal{M}_{t,n}^{\alpha}}[\|p_{M}-U\|^{2}_{\operatorname{tvd}}]}\leq\varepsilon^{2}
.
∎
