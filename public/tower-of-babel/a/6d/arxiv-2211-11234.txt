The measure transfer for subshifts induced by a morphism of free monoids Thanks: The first author was partially supported by ANR Project IZES ANR-22-CE40-0011
Nicolas Bédaride, Arnaud Hilion, Martin Lustig

Abstract
. Every non-erasing monoid morphism σ : 𝒜 ∗ → ℬ ∗ \sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*} induces a measure transfer map σ X ​ M : ℳ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) → ℳ ⁡ ( σ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) ) \sigma_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X)) between the measure cones ℳ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \cal M(X) and ℳ ⁡ ( σ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) ) \cal M(\sigma(X)) , associated to any subshift X ⊆ 𝒜 𝒵 X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}} and its image subshift σ ⁡ ( X ) ⊆ ℬ 𝒵 \sigma(X)\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}} respectively. We define and study this map in detail and show that it is continuous, linear and functorial. It also turns out to be surjective [ 3 ] . Furthermore, an efficient technique to compute the value of the transferred measure σ X ​ M ​ ( μ ) \sigma_{X}M(\mu) on any cylinder [ w ] [w] (for w ∈ ℬ ∗ w\in\cal B^{*} ) is presented. Theorem: If a non-erasing morphism σ : 𝒜 ∗ → ℬ ∗ \sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*} is injective on the shift-orbits of some subshift X ⊆ 𝒜 𝒵 X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}} , then σ ​ M X \sigma M_{X} is injective. The assumption on σ \sigma that it is “injective on the shift-orbits of X X ” is strictly weaker than “recognizable in X X ”, and strictly stronger than “recognizable for aperiodic points in X X ”. The last assumption does in general not suffice to obtain the injectivity of the measure transfer map σ X ​ M \sigma_{X}M .

1.
Introduction

The prime objects of this paper are morphisms
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
of free monoids
\cal A^{*}
and
\cal B^{*}
over finite sets
\cal A
and
\cal B
respectively. These sets are called
alphabets
, and their elements are
letters
, denoted here by
a_{k}\in\cal A
or
b_{j}\in\cal B
. To
\cal A
and
\cal B
there are canonically associated the spaces
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
, equipped both with the product topology and a
shift operator
, here always denoted by
T
. All morphisms
\sigma
in this paper are
non-erasing
(i.e.
none of the
a_{k}\in\cal A
are mapped to the empty word),
and such
\sigma
induces canonically a map
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}:\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
. But while
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is continuous, in general it fails to be a “morphism of dynamical systems” from
(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}},T)
to
(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}},T)
in the classical topological dynamics meaning, in that most of the time we have

(1.1)

T\circ\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\,\,\neq\,\,\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\circ T\,.

This creates a number of well known technical problems; for instance any non-empty, closed, shift-invariant subset (called a
subshift
)
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
has
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
-image that is in general not shift-invariant, and thus not a subshift by itself. Still, there is a well defined image subshift (see Definition-Remark
2.2
below), denoted here by
\sigma(X)
, which has been studied previously in many occasions.

The focus of this paper is on the set
\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})
of measures
\mu
on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
that are
invariant
, which means that

\mu
is a
finite
Borel measure, and that the shift operator preserves
\mu\,
. The map
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
gives us directly the classical “push-forward” measure
\mu_{*}
on
\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
, but because of (
1.1
) this measure will almost never be invariant, as it will not be preserved by the shift operator on
\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Nevertheless, any invariant measure
\mu
on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
can canonically be “transferred” by
\sigma
to define an invariant measure on
\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Setting up properly and studying
carefully this

measure transfer map

\sigma M:\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})\to\cal M(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}})

induced by any non-erasing free monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is the main purpose of this paper.
We show (see Subsection
3.4
and Lemma
5.5
):

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
be any non-erasing morphism, and let
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
be any subshift over
\cal A
, with image subshift
Y:=\sigma(X)\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then the induced measure transfer map
\sigma M
restricts/co-restricts to a well defined map

\sigma_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(Y)\,,\,\,\mu\mapsto\mu^{\sigma}

which has the following properties:

(1)

\sigma_{X}M
is an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-linear map of cones.

(2)

\sigma_{X}M
is continuous.

(3)

\sigma_{X}M
is functorial:
(\sigma^{\prime}\circ\sigma)_{X}M={\sigma^{\prime}}_{\sigma(X)}M\circ\sigma_{X}M

(4)

If the subshift
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
is the support of
a measure
\mu\in\cal M(X)
, then
\sigma(X^{\prime})
is the support of
\sigma_{X}M(\mu)
.

(5)

If a measure
\mu\in\cal M(X)
is ergodic, then so is
\mu^{\sigma}\in\cal M(Y)
.

(In addition, the map
\sigma_{X}M
is surjective, see Proposition 4.4 of
[
3
]
.)

As indicated already before, the above introduced measure
\mu^{\sigma}
, obtained from a given measure
\mu\in\cal M(X)
via the measure transfer induced by a morphism
\sigma
, is almost always quite different from the push-forward measure
\mu_{*}
as defined by
\sigma
. It turns out that the popular sources for basics in symbolic dynamics do only consider this very particular case
\mu^{\sigma}=\mu_{*}\,
, see for instance
[
15
]
, Proposition 5.22 . The general set-up for the measure transfer, as studied here, doesn’t seem to be available in the existing literature. The basic properties of the transferred measure proved below are in particular used in our cousin paper
[
3
]
for the purpose of an explicit treatment of the transferred measure by means of S-adic expansions of a given subshift.

In order to underline that, despite its divergence from the usual push-forward concept, the measure transfer is a very natural tool under the given circumstances, we’d like to mention that there are several “ad hoc” occasions where the measure transfer has already appeared beforehand, see Example
1.6
below. A special but rather relevant such case takes place if the given morphism is an endomorphisms
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A^{*}
, thus called a
substitution
. We then have the symbolic dynamics version of the well known and important situation where a system of zero entropy coexists with a system of positive entropy, giving rise to a wealth of subtle and profound interactions. For geometers, a geometric counterpart is obtained, for example, by considering the attractive lamination of a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism
f
on a hyperbolic surface: this is a geodesic lamination of zero entropy (for the geodesic flow), but the action of the pseudo-Anosov is of positive entropy (given by the dilation coefficient
\lambda
of this homeomorphism). In such contexts, it is natural to consider the invariant measures of the zero-entropy system that are
projectively
invariant under the positive entropy transformation operator (the substitution
\sigma
or the pseudo-Anosov
f
in the above examples). Relating back to our main topic, the measure transfer, we observe:

Remark 1.2
.

Any non-erasing substitution
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A^{*}
with
\sigma^{n}(\cal A)\cap\cal A=\emptyset
for some sufficiently large
n\geq 1

defines a
substitutive subshift

X_{\sigma}
which has the property that its image subshift
\sigma(X_{\sigma})
is equal to
X_{\sigma}\,
.
To every non-negative eigenvector
\vec{v}
of the transition matrix
M(\sigma)
, with eigenvalue
\lambda>1
, there is canonically associated an invariant measure
\mu_{\vec{v}}
on the subshift
X_{\sigma}
(see
[
2
]
). It can be shown (using Proposition 4.3 and Remark 4.2 of
[
3
]
) that the measure transfer map induced by
\sigma
acts on
\mu_{\vec{v}}
as homothety with stretching factor
\lambda\,
, giving

\mu_{\vec{v}}^{\sigma}=\lambda\cdot\mu_{\vec{v}}\,.

The last equality indicates already that
the transferred measure of a probability measure will in general not be probability. Indeed, we have
(see Remark
4.4
and Proposition
4.5
below):

Proposition 1.3
.

For any invariant measure
\mu
on the full shift
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and any non-erasing morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
the transferred measure

\mu^{\sigma}=\sigma M(\mu)
satisfies

\mu^{\sigma}(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}})=\sum_{a_{k}\in\cal A}\sum_{b_{j}\in\cal B}|\sigma(a_{k})|_{b_{j}}\cdot\mu([a_{k}])=\sum_{a_{k}\in\cal A}|\sigma(a_{k})|\cdot\mu([a_{k}])\,.

More specifically, for the “vectors” of letter frequencies we have the
matrix equality

([\mu^{\sigma}(b_{j})])_{b_{j}\in\cal B}\,\,=\,\,M(\sigma)\cdot([\mu(a_{k})])_{a_{k}\in\cal A}\,.

Here
|w|_{u}
denotes the number of (possibly overlapping) occurrences of the word
u
as factor in the word
w
, and
[w]
is the “cylinder” that consists of all biinfinite words
\ldots x_{n}x_{n+1}\ldots
for which the
positive half-word

x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
starts with
w
as prefix. By
M(\sigma)
we denote the
incidence matrix
of

\sigma
,
which has
coefficient
|\sigma(a_{k})|_{b_{j}}
at the position
(j,k)
.

In order to compute the
transferred
measure
\mu^{\sigma}([w^{\prime}])
of
an arbitrary
cylinder
[w^{\prime}]\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
, as done in Proposition
1.3
for the special case
w^{\prime}=b_{j}\in\cal B
, it turns out
that it is useful to introduce the number
\lfloor\sigma(w)\rfloor_{u}
of
essential occurrences
of
u
as a factor of
\sigma(w)
, by which we mean that the first letter of
u
occurs in the
\sigma
-image of first letter of
w
, and the last letter of
u
occurs in the
\sigma
-image of last letter of
w
. The following is proved in Proposition
4.2
below, and several examples of concrete computations of cylinder values
\mu^{\sigma}([w^{\prime}])
are given in Sections
3
and
4
.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
be any non-erasing monoid morphism, and let
\mu
be any invariant measure on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then for any
w^{\prime}\in\cal B^{*}

with
|w^{\prime}|\geq 2

the transferred measure
\mu^{\sigma}:=\sigma M(\mu)
, evaluated on the cylinder
[w^{\prime}]
,
is given by the finite sum

\mu^{\sigma}([w^{\prime}])=\sum_{w\,\in\,W(w^{\prime})}{\lfloor\sigma(w)\rfloor}_{w^{\prime}}\cdot\mu([w])\,,

for
W(w^{\prime})={\big\{}w\in\cal A^{*}\,:\,|w|\leq\frac{|w^{\prime}|-2}{\min\{|\sigma(a_{k})|\,\,:\,\,a_{k}\in\cal A\}}+2{\big\}}
.

As stated above in Proposition
1.1
, any invariant measure
\mu^{\prime}
on
the image subshift
Y=\sigma(X)
is equal to the transfer
\sigma_{X}M(\mu)
of some measure
\mu
on
the given preimage subshift
X
. However, for a general non-erasing morphism
\sigma
, this measure
\mu
will be far from uniquely determined by
\mu^{\prime}
.

On the other hand, the injectivity of the measure transfer map, if given, is a strong and useful tool in many circumstances, see Example
6.9
.
In particular, it can be used as key ingredient for the construction of subshifts with entropy 0 but infinitely many distinct ergodic probability measures. In §7 of our cousin paper
[
3
]
this has been detailed out for the special case of minimal subshifts, so that the additional assumption that
\sigma
is recognizable in
X
could be used.
This additional assumption implies indeed the injectivity of the measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M
(see
Corollary 3.9 of
[
3
]
). We show here the following stronger result (see Theorem
5.6
below):

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
be a non-erasing morphism of free monoids on finite alphabets, and let
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
be any subshift.

If the map induced by
\sigma
on the shift-orbits of
X
is injective,
then the measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X))
is injective.

To be specific, we’d like to note that the hypothesis “injectivity on the set of shift-orbits of
X
” is strictly weaker than “recognizable in
X
”, and strictly stronger than “recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
”. Also, the converse to the conclusion of Theorem
1.5
does not hold in full generality. Furthermore, the two properties “recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
” and “injectivity of the induced measure transfer map” are logically independent. The implications among all of these properties and their refusals are conveniently summarized in Fig. 1, and an exhaustive discussion with proofs and counter-examples is given in Section
5
.

To terminate this introduction, we’d like to list
some
incidents where our readers may already have encountered the measure transfer map, perhaps “disguised” in a different setting or language:

Example 1.6
.

(1)
Let
S
be a compact surface with non-empty boundary, and
\tau\subseteq S
a train track that fills
S
and satisfies the usual conditions on its complementary components (see
for instance
[
10
]
for details). Then a proper choice of arcs
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
transverse to
\tau
gives rise to intervals with the following property: Any oriented geodesic lamination
\Lambda\subseteq S
, for which we assume that it can be isotoped into an interval-fibered neighborhood
\cal N(\tau)
of
\tau
in such a way that
\Lambda
becomes transverse to all interval fibers, defines an interval exchange transformation system (IET) on the intervals
\alpha_{k}
, and thus a subshift
X_{\Lambda}\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
for
\cal A=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\}
. Any transverse measure
\mu_{\Lambda}
on
\Lambda
defines an invariant measure
\mu
on
X_{\Lambda}
.

Assume now that (as shown by Thurston for any “pseudo-Anosov” homeomorphism) that some homeomorphism
h:S\to S
, after being properly isotoped, maps
\cal N(\tau)
in an interval-fiber preserving fashion into
\cal N(\tau)
. Then any transverse measure
\mu_{\Lambda}
gives rise to an “
h
-image transverse measure”
\mu^{\prime}_{\Lambda}
, which, when translated back to the corresponding invariant measure
\mu^{\prime}
on

X_{h(\Lambda)}
,
turns out to be precisely the transferred measure
\sigma M(\mu)
, where
\sigma
is the morphism on
\cal A^{*}

induced by
h
.

In the most frequently considered pseudo-Anosov case the

h
-invariant
lamination
\Lambda
as well as the
corresponding
subshift
X_{\Lambda}
turn out to be both, minimal and uniquely ergodic, so that we have
\sigma M(\mu)=\lambda\,\mu\,
, where
\lambda>1
is the celebrated “stretching factor” (= the Perron-Frobenious eigenvalue of
M(\sigma)
) for
h
.

(2)
Any word
w\in\cal A^{*}
(or rather, its conjugacy class in the free group
F(\cal A)
)
defines a finite subshift
X_{w}
that consists of
the sequence
w^{\pm\infty}=\ldots www\ldots\,\,
and its finitely many shift translates.
To
w

there
is canonically associated a
characteristic measure

\mu_{w}\in\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})
with support equal to
X_{w}
and total measure
\mu_{w}(X_{w})=\mu_{w}(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})=|w|
(see (
2.5
) for more details).

For any non-erasing morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
the transferred measure of any characteristic measure is again a characteristic measure, given by

\sigma M(\mu_{w})=\mu_{\sigma(w)}\,.

(3)
For the special case that
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
extends to a free group automorphism
\varphi_{\sigma}:F(\cal A)\to F(\cal B)
, one can use the well established 1-1 relationship between invariant measures on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
on one hand and currents on
F(\cal A)
on the other, as well as the similarly well established action of
\mbox{Aut}(F(\cal A))
on the current space
\mbox{Curr}(F(\cal A))\cong\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})
(see
[
11
]
for details). Denoting by
\mu
also the current on
F(\cal A)
defined by the invariant measure
\mu
, one has

\varphi_{\sigma}(\mu)=\sigma M(\mu)\,.

2.
Notation and conventions

2.1.
Standard terminology and well known facts

Throughout this paper we denote by
\cal A,\cal B,\cal C,\ldots
finite
non-empty
sets, called
alphabets
. For
any such alphabet, say
\cal A
, we denote by
\cal A^{*}
the free monoid over the set
\cal A
, given by all finite words
w=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}
with
x_{i}\in\cal A
, and equipped with the multiplication defined by concatenation. We denote by
|w|:=n
the
length
of any such word.
The empty word
\varepsilon\in\cal A^{*}
is defined by
|\varepsilon|=0
; it is the unit element with respect to the multiplication in
\cal A^{*}
.

The elements
a_{i}\in\cal A
are called the
letters
; they constitute a set of generators of
\cal A^{*}

and moreover a basis of the associated free group
F(\cal A)
.
Any
subset
\cal L\subseteq\cal A^{*}

(often assumed to be infinite)
is called a
language
over
\cal A
.

A
monoid morphism

\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is well defined by knowing the image
word
\sigma(a_{i})\in\cal B^{*}
for each of the letters
a_{i}\in\cal A
. Conversely, each choice of such image words defines a monoid morphism
\sigma
as above. The monoid morphism
\sigma
is
non-erasing
if
|\sigma(a_{i})|\geq 1
for each of the letters
a_{i}\in\cal A
. In this paper we will only consider non-erasing morphisms. Note that any non-erasing morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is “finite-to-one”, i.e. for any element
w\in\cal B^{*}
the preimage set
\sigma^{-1}(w)
is finite.

Every monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
defines an
incidence matrix

(2.1)

M(\sigma)=(|\sigma(a_{j})|_{b_{i}})_{b_{i}\in\cal B,a_{j}\in\cal A}

where
|\sigma(a_{j})|_{b_{i}}
denotes the number of occurrences of the letter
b_{i}\in\cal B
in the
\sigma
-image of any
a_{j}\in\cal A
.
One easily verifies the formula
M(\sigma)=M(\sigma_{2})\cdot M(\sigma_{1})
for any composition of monoid morphisms
\sigma=\sigma_{2}\circ\sigma_{1}
.

To any alphabet
\cal A
there is canonically associated the shift space

\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Its elements are written as biinfinite words
{\bf x}=\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots
with
x_{i}\in\cal A
. The set
\cal L({\bf x})\subseteq\cal A^{*}
of all finite subwords
(called
factors
)

{\bf x}_{[k,\ell]}:=x_{k}x_{k+1}\ldots x_{\ell}
is the
language associated to
{\bf x}
. The one-sided infinite
positive half-word

x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
of
\bf x
is denoted by
{\bf x}_{[1,\infty)}
.
Similarly, we denote the complementary infinite half-word
\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}
of
\bf x
by
{\bf x}_{(-\infty,0]}
.

The shift space
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}

is canonically equipped with
the
shift operator

T:\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
which maps the word
{\bf x}=\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots
to the word
{\bf y}=\ldots y_{j-1}y_{j}y_{j+1}\ldots
given by
y_{k}=x_{k+1}
for all indices
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Similarly,

\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is naturally equipped with the product topology
(with respect to the discrete topology on
\cal A
), which makes

\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}

into a compact space, indeed a Cantor set
(unless
\mbox{card}(\cal A)=1
).
For any
w\in\cal A^{*}
the
cylinder

[w]\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is open and closed (and thus compact); it consists of all biinfinite words
{\bf x}=\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots
with
{\bf x}_{[1,|w|]}=w
. The set of all cylinders and their shift-translates constitutes a basis for the topology on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The shift operator
T
acts as homeomorphism on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

A subset
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is called a
subshift
if
X
is non-empty, closed, and
if it satisfies
T(X)=X
.
To any
infinite
language
\cal L\subseteq\cal A^{*}
there is canonically associated the subshift
X(\cal L)

generated by
\cal L
: It is defined through

{\bf x}\in X(\cal L)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\cal L({\bf x})\subseteq\cal L^{f}\,,

where
\cal L^{f}
denotes the
factorial closure
of
\cal L
, which is the language obtained from
\cal L
by adding in all
factors
(= subwords) of any word
w\in\cal L
.
Conversely, every subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
determines an
associated subshift language

\cal L(X):=\bigcup\{\cal L({\bf x})\mid{\bf x}\in X\}
, which is infinite and equal to its factorial closure, so that one has
X=X(\cal L(X))
.

A subshift
X
is
minimal
if for any element
{\bf x}\in X
the set
X
is equal to
the closure of the
shift-orbit

\cal O({\bf x}):=\{T^{n}({\bf x})\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\,.

Important particular examples are minimal subshifts that are finite: They consist of a single orbit
\cal O({\bf w})
that is given by the finitely many shift-translates of a periodic word
{\bf w}=\ldots www\ldots
.
To be specific, we fix the indexing of such a periodic word by the requirement that
w
is a prefix of the positive half-word
{\bf w}_{[1,\infty)}
; in this case
{\bf w}
is denoted by
w^{\pm\infty}
,
and we write
{\bf w}_{[1,\infty)}=:w^{+\infty}
and
{\bf w}_{(-\infty,0]}=:w^{-\infty}
.

The space
\Sigma(\cal A)
of all subshifts
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is naturally equipped with the partial order given by the inclusion; the minimal elements with respect to this partial order are precisely the minimal subshifts. The shift space
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
itself is the only maximal element with respect to this partial order; it is often also called the
full shift over
\cal A
. Similarly, a subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is sometimes called
a subshift over
\cal A
.

The space
\Sigma(\cal A)
is also equipped with a natural topology, inherited from the
canonical embedding
\Sigma(\cal A)\subseteq\cal P(\cal A^{*})
which is defined by the above bijection between subshifts and their associated languages.
Since the topology of the shift space doesn’t play a role in this paper, we
will not give details here and refer the reader instead to

[
14
]
. Just for “general interest” we note that the
subset of
\Sigma(\cal A)
, which consists of all subshifts that are a union of finitely many shift-orbits, is dense in
\Sigma(\cal A)
. More information about the topology on
\Sigma(\cal A)
can be found in
[
14
]
, where in particular it is shown that
\Sigma(\cal A)
is a
Pełczyński
space.

An
invariant measure

\mu
on a subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a finite Borel measure on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
with support in
X
that is invariant under
T
, i.e.

\mu(T^{-1}(B))=\mu(B)
for every measurable set
B\subseteq X
.
Any invariant measure
\mu
defines a
weight function

\omega_{\mu}:\cal A^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\,\,w\mapsto\mu([w])\,,

by which we mean any function
\omega:\cal A^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies the
Kirchhoff equalities

(2.2)

\omega(w)=\sum_{a_{i}\in\cal A}\omega(a_{i}w)=\sum_{a_{i}\in\cal A}\omega(wa_{i})

for all
w\in\cal A^{*}
. Conversely, it is well known (see

[
1
]
,
[
16
]
)
that every weight function
\omega:\cal A^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defines an invariant measure
\mu_{\omega}
via
\mu_{\omega}([w]):=\omega(w)
for all
w\in\cal A^{*}
. This gives

\mu_{\omega_{\mu}}=\mu

\omega_{\mu_{\omega}}=\omega

An invariant measure

\mu_{2}

[

]
,

given by
\mu\mapsto(\mu(w))_{w\in\cal A^{*}}
.

It follows that the set

is a convex linear cone which is naturally embedded into the non-negative cone of the infinite dimensional vector space

are in 1-1 relation with the ergodic measures on

It is well known (see

An important class of ergodic invariant measures on the full shift

2.2.
“Not so standard” basic facts and terminology

Let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
a non-erasing monoid morphism. Then there is a canonically induced map

(2.8)

\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}:\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}

that maps a biinfinite word
{\bf x}=\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots\in\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
to the biinfinite word
{\bf y}=\ldots y_{j-1}y_{j}y_{j+1}\ldots\in\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
obtained from concatenating the
\sigma(x_{i})
in the obvious way, starting with the convention

\sigma(x_{1})=y_{1}\ldots y_{|\sigma(x_{1})|}\,.

This map
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
will in general not inherit any of the properties of
\sigma
(for instance,

\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}

is almost never surjective and almost never finite-to-one). However, it satisfies
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}(T({\bf x}))=T^{k}({\bf y})
for suitable
k\geq 0
depending on

{\bf x}
,
so that the shift-orbit
\cal O({\bf x})
has a well defined image shift-orbit
\cal O({\bf y})
. Hence
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
induces a map

\sigma^{T}:\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}/\langle T\rangle\to\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}/\langle T\rangle,\,\,\cal O({\bf x})\mapsto\cal O(\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}({\bf x}))

on the associated “leaf spaces” (i.e. the set of shift-orbits), which turns out to be a lot more meaningful than the map
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
itself.

Remark 2.1
.

It follows straight from the definitions that both induced maps
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\sigma^{T}
behave functorially: in particular, for any two non-erasing monoid morphisms
\sigma_{1}:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
and
\sigma_{2}:\cal B^{*}\to\cal C^{*}
we have

(\sigma_{2}\circ\sigma_{1})^{\mathbb{Z}}=\sigma_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}\circ\sigma_{1}^{\mathbb{Z}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\sigma_{2}\circ\sigma_{1})^{T}=\sigma_{2}^{T}\circ\sigma_{1}^{T}\,.

We now come to
a third
map induced by any non-erasing monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
, namely the map

(2.9)

\sigma^{\Sigma}:\Sigma(\cal A)\to\Sigma(\cal B)\,,\,\,X\mapsto\sigma^{\Sigma}(X)

from the space
\Sigma(\cal A)
of subshifts
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
to the space
\Sigma(\cal B)
of subshifts
Y\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We know of
three natural ways to define the image
\sigma^{\Sigma}(X)\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
under this map, for any given subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, listed below as follows.
Notice
that here the assumption on
\sigma

Definition-Remark 2.2

If

is non-erasing,

(2)

Remark 2.6
.

Although not central to the topics evoked in this paper, for completeness we would like to state here how a non-erasing morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
acts on the complexity and the topological entropy of a subshift. Recall that for any subshift

the

(also called

) is given by the function

of

[

]

]

2.3.
About injectivity

Injectivity of monoid morphisms
\sigma
and of their induced maps
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}},\sigma^{T}
, and
\sigma^{\Sigma}
is an important and often tricky issue.
We start out in sub-Subsection
2.3.1
to list several problems and give examples where these often undesired phenomena do occur. In sub-Subsection
2.3.2
, which can be read independently, we will then define the subtle notion of “shift-period preservation”,
which will be used in Sections
5
and
6
below as well as in
[
3
]
.

2.3.1.
Typical injectivity problems

For starters, we have the following well known phenomenon:

Remark 2.7
.

There exist non-erasing morphisms
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
which are injective, while the associated free group homomorphism
F(\cal A)\to F(\cal B)
is not injective. An example
is given by

\sigma:\{a,b,c\}^{*}\to\{d,e\}^{*}\,,\,\,a\mapsto dde,\,\,b\mapsto dee,\,\,c\mapsto dede\,

which maps
c(ac^{-1}b)^{-2}
to the neutral element
1\in F(\{d,e\})
.

A second, also well known disturbance comes from the fact that injective free group morphisms need not induce injective maps on the set of conjugacy classes. This happens for example
for the morphism induced by the quotient map,
if two boundary curves of a surface (with at least one more boundary component to ensure free fundamental groups) are glued together. In a classical monoid morphism situation we observe that for the “Thue-Morse
morphism”

(2.11)

\sigma_{\text{TM}}:\{a,b\}^{*}\to\{c,d\}^{*}\,,\,\,\,a\mapsto cd,\,\,b\mapsto dc

the images
\sigma_{\text{TM}}(a)
and
\sigma_{\text{TM}}(b)
are conjugate in
F(\{c,d\})
,
while
\sigma_{\text{TM}}
is injective on all of
F(\{a,b\})
.
This yields:

Remark 2.8
.

There exist non-erasing monoid morphisms
\sigma
which are injective, but for which the induced map
\sigma^{T}
is not injective. For example, for the Thue-Morse morphism one has
\cal O(\sigma_{\text{TM}}(a^{\pm\infty}))=\cal O(\sigma_{\text{TM}}(b^{\pm\infty}))
.

Two further, more subtle “injectivity disturbances” can be observed by the following two examples,
despite the fact that in both cases the induced map on the shift-orbits is injective. Indeed, in both cases we consider a subshift which consists of a single periodic shift-orbit: in the first case the map
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
restricted to this orbit is not injective, while in the second case it is, but a minimal period in the preimage orbit is not mapped to a minimal period in the image.

Remark 2.9
.

(1)
The morphism
\sigma_{1}:\{a,b\}^{*}\to\{c\}^{*}\,,\,\,\sigma_{1}(a)=\sigma_{1}(b)=c
maps the orbit
\cal O((ab)^{\pm\infty})
to
\cal O(c^{\pm\infty})
, but the restriction of
\sigma_{1}^{\mathbb{Z}}
to
\cal O((ab)^{\pm\infty})
is not injective.

(2)
The morphism
\sigma_{2}:\{a\}^{*}\to\{b\}^{*}\,,\,\,\sigma_{2}(a)=b^{2}
maps the orbit
\cal O(a^{\pm\infty})

injectively
to
\cal O(b^{\pm\infty})
, but the “shift-period” is not preserved:
For both,
a^{\pm\infty}
and
b^{\pm\infty}
the minimal shift-period has length 1, but
\sigma_{2}
maps the shift-period
a
of
a^{\pm\infty}
to a word of length 2, which is hence not the minimal shift-period of
b^{\pm\infty}
.

2.3.2.
Shift-period preservation

For any periodic word
{\bf x}\in\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
we define the
shift-period exponent
of
{\bf x}

to be the smallest integer
k\geq 1
such that
T^{k}({\bf x})={\bf x}
. If
{\bf x}=w^{\pm\infty}
for some
w\in\cal A^{*}
, then
k
divides
|w|
. If
w
can not be written as proper power (see (
2.4
)), then the shift-period exponent of
\bf x
is given by
k=|w|
. In this case any cyclic permutation of
w
will be called a
shift-period
of the periodic word
{\bf x}
.

Definition 2.10
.

A morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is said to
preserve the shift-period

of some biinfinite periodic word
{\bf x}\in\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
if any shift-period
w\in\cal A^{*}
of
{\bf x}
is mapped by
\sigma
to a shift-period of the image word
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}({\bf x})\in\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

In other words, if
{\bf x}=w^{\pm\infty}
and
\sigma(w)
is a proper power, then so is
w

(or else
\sigma
doesn’t preserve the shift-period of
{\bf x}
).

Remark 2.11
.

In the special case
where
|\sigma(a_{i})|=1
for all
a_{i}\in\cal A
we observe that
\sigma
preserves the shift-period of some periodic biinfinite word
{\bf x}
if and only if the shift-period exponents of
\bf x
and of its image
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}({\bf x})
agree.

3.
The measure transfer

In this section we will carefully define for any non-erasing monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
and any invariant measure
\mu

\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}

\mu^{\sigma}
on
\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The simplest and most natural way to understand this measure transfer is achieved by decomposing the given morphism

3.1.
Subdivision morphisms

Let
\cal A

\ell:\cal A\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
be any map, called

. We now define a new
subdivision alphabet

\cal A_{\ell}
which consists of letters
a_{i}(k)
for any
a_{i}\in\cal A
and any
k\in\{1,\ldots,\ell(a_{i})\}
. We then define the associated
subdivision morphism

given by

(3.1)

\pi_{\ell}:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A_{\ell}^{*}\,,\,\,a_{i}\mapsto a_{i}(1)\ldots a_{i}({\ell(a_{i}))}\,.

Remark 3.1
.

(1)
The name and the intuition here comes from picturing
\cal A
as edge labels of an oriented “rose”

, i.e. a 1-vertex graph with
\mbox{card}(A)

oriented edges. Then any edge with label

is subdivided by introducing
\ell(a_{i})-1

a_{i}(1),a_{i}(2),\ldots,a_{i}(\ell(a_{i}))
. Then any edge path
\gamma(w)
in
RA
, which reads off a word

, will after the subdivision read off the word
\pi_{\ell}(w)
.

(2)
The natural “geometrization” of the
subdivision monoid

\cal A_{\ell}^{*}
as rose

is however not quite the above subdivision of the rose
RA
, but is obtained from the latter by identifying all subdivision vertices into a single vertex. This is reflected by the fact that the subdivision morphism
\pi_{\ell}
defined above is
not surjective (except in the trivial case where all
\ell(a_{i})=1
so that
\pi_{\ell}
is a bijection).
Its image generates a subshift of finite type in
\cal A_{\ell}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

From the above definitions we deduce directly the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\pi_{\ell}:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A_{\ell}^{*}
be a subdivision morphism as in (
3.1
). Then the following holds:

(1)

The monoid morphism
\pi_{\ell}
is injective.

(2)

The induced map
\pi_{\ell}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\cal A_{\ell}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is injective.

(3)

The map
\pi_{\ell}^{T}
induced on shift-orbits is injective.

(4)

The morphism
\pi_{\ell}
preserves the shift-period of any biinfinite periodic word

.

(5)

The map
\pi_{\ell}^{\Sigma}
induced on subshifts over

Proof.

All the listed properties follow directly from the definition of the map
\pi_{\ell}
, since for any element
w^{\prime}\in\pi_{\ell}(\cal A^{*})
the (uniquely determined) preimage

is directly visible through replacing every factor
a_{i}(1)\ldots a_{i}(\ell(a_{i}))
by the letter
a_{i}\,
.

Definition 3.3

Let

Lemma 3.4
.

The function

inherits from

Remark 3.5

The subdivision measure defined by a probability measure will in general not be probability: Unless

3.2.
Letter-to-letter morphisms

Recall that a monoid morphism
\alpha:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is called

if for any letter
a_{i}\in\cal A
the length of its image is equal to
|\alpha(a_{i})|=1
. In other words:
\alpha
is induced by a map
\alpha A:\cal A\to\cal B
on the alphabets
1
1

1

Some authors (see for instance

[
7
]
) require in addition that a letter-to-letter morphism must be surjective. All letter-to-letter morphisms occurring in this paper are indeed surjective, but formally we do not need this condition anywhere.

It follows directly that both, the letter-to-letter morphism

and the induced map

, are injective and/or surjective if and only if
\alpha A
is injective and/or surjective. Furthermore,

commutes with the shift maps (both denoted by

As a consequence, the image

\alpha^{\Sigma}(X)
over

. Furthermore, for any invariant measure

is an invariant measure on

, with support equal to

. In particular, if

is a probability measure, then so is

According to the defining equation

\mu_{*}(S):=\mu(f^{-1}(S))
for any measurable set

we obtain for the weight function associated to

and for any

the finite sum decomposition

\alpha_{*}(\mu)(w)=\sum_{u\,\in\,\alpha^{-1}(w)}\mu(u)\,.

Note

3.3.
The induced measure morphisms

We now consider an arbitrary non-erasing monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
(as usual for finite alphabets
\cal A
and
\cal B
). Then
\sigma
defines a subdivision length function
\ell_{\sigma}:\cal A\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\,
, given by

\ell_{\sigma}(a_{i}):=|\sigma(a_{i})|

for
any
a_{i}\in\cal A
.
This gives
a subdivision alphabet
\cal A_{\sigma}:=\cal A_{\ell_{\sigma}}
as well as a subdivision morphism

\pi_{\sigma}:=\pi_{\ell_{\sigma}}:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A_{\sigma}^{*}\,.

Furthermore,
\sigma
defines a letter-to-letter morphism given by

\alpha_{\sigma}:\cal A_{\sigma}^{*}\to\cal B^{*}\,,\,\,a_{i}(k)\mapsto\sigma(a_{i})_{k}\,,

for any
a_{i}\in\cal A
and
1\leq k\leq|\sigma(a_{i})|
, where

\sigma(a_{i})_{k}\in\cal B

denotes the
k
-th letter of the word
\sigma(a_{i})\in\cal B^{*}
.

Definition-Remark 3.6
.

(1) From the above definitions we observe that any non-erasing monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
admits a
canonical decomposition

(3.4)

\sigma=\alpha_{\sigma}\circ\pi_{\sigma}

as product of a subdivision morphism
\pi_{\sigma}
with a subsequent letter-to-letter morphism
\alpha_{\sigma}
.

(2)
As a consequence, the morphism
\sigma
induces a canonical
measure transfer

\sigma M:\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})\to\cal M(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}})
which maps any invariant measure
\mu
on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
to the measure
(\alpha_{\sigma})_{*}(\mu_{\ell_{\sigma}})
,
which for simplicity will sometimes be denoted by
\mu^{\sigma}
.
For the associated weight function
\sigma M(\mu)
on
\cal B^{*}
we obtain, for any
w\in\cal B^{*}
, the formula

(3.5)

\sigma M(\mu)(w)\,\,\,[=:\mu^{\sigma}(w)]=\sum_{u\,\in\,{\alpha_{\sigma}^{-1}(w)}}\mu_{\ell_{\sigma}}(u)\,=\sum_{u\,\in\,{\alpha_{\sigma}^{-1}(w)}}\mu(\widehat{u})\,,

where
\widehat{u}\in\cal A^{*}
is defined above in Definition
3.3

that if
\widehat{u}
doesn’t exist for some

The above canonical decomposition of an arbitrary morphism
has been used previously; it can be found for instance in Lemma 3.4 of
[

]

3.4.
Basic properties of the measure transfer map

In this subsection we want to show some first properties of the measure transfer map
\sigma M
defined in the previous subsection. We start out with some basic facts: their proof is an elementary (and not very illuminating) exercise, based on the canonical decomposition (
3.4
) of any morphism
\sigma

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
be a non-erasing monoid morphism. Then the induced measure transfer map
\sigma M
has the following properties:

(a)

The map
\sigma M:\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})\to\cal M(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}})
is

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-linear.

(b)

The map
\sigma M
is functorial: For any second non-erasing monoid morphism
\sigma^{\prime}:\cal B^{*}\to\cal C^{*}
one has
(\sigma^{\prime}\sigma)M={\sigma^{\prime}}M{\sigma}M
.

(c)

The image
\sigma M(\mu)
of a probability measure
\mu
on
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is in general not a probability measure on
\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(d)

For any word
w\in\cal A^{*}
the characteristic measure
\mu_{w}
is mapped by
\sigma M
to the characteristic measure
\mu_{\sigma(w)}
.

(e)

For any word
w\in\cal A^{*}
the measures of corresponding cylinders
[w]\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
[\sigma(w)]\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy the
inequality

(3.6)

\mu([w])\leq\sigma M(\mu)([\sigma(w)])\,.

If
\sigma
is a subdivision morphism, then (
3.6
) becomes an equality, but for a letter-to-letter morphism the inequality (
3.6
) will in general be strict.

\sqcap

For the next observation we recall from Section
2.1

-topology on the space

Lemma 3.8

For any non-erasing monoid morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}

\sigma M:\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})\to\cal M(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}})

Proof.

Following (

) we decompose
\sigma
as product
\sigma=\alpha_{\sigma}\circ\pi_{\sigma}
. In order to show that the first factor of this decomposition, the morphism
\pi_{\sigma}:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A_{\sigma}^{*}
, induces a continuous map on
\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})
, we recall that by definition this map is defined via
\mu\mapsto\mu_{\ell_{\sigma}}
, with
\mu_{\ell_{\sigma}}(w)=\mu(\widehat{w})

The second factor

\cal M(\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}})\to\cal M(\cal A_{\sigma}^{\mathbb{Z}})\to\cal M(\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}})\,,\,\,\mu\mapsto\mu_{\ell_{\sigma}}\mapsto(\alpha_{\sigma})_{*}(\mu_{\ell_{\sigma}})

The following discussion, suggested to us by a comment of a referee, concerns a potentially alternative approach to the measure transfer map.
We use here the terminology from §3 of

Remark 3.11
.

Given
a subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, one may consider the group

H(X,\mathbb{Z})=C(X,\mathbb{Z})/\partial_{T}C(X,\mathbb{Z})
, where
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
denotes the abelian group of continuous integer valued functions on
X
, and

\partial_{T}(X)
is the subgroup generated by functions of the form
f\circ T-f
.
To every invariant measure
\mu
on
X
one associates the linear form

\alpha_{\mu}:H(X,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{R}\,,\,\,f\mapsto\int fd\mu\,,

i.e. an element in the dual group
H(X,\mathbb{R})^{*}:={\rm Hom}(H(X,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{R})
.

The group
H(X,\mathbb{Z})
inherits from
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
the canonical order as well as the “order unit” given by the characteristic function
{\bf 1}_{X}
(see §2.1.1 of
[
7
]
).
Under the mild additional assumption that
X
is recurrent (i.e. there exists some
{\bf x}\in X
with positive half-word
{\bf x}_{[1,+\infty)}
that is dense in
X
), the group
H(X,\mathbb{Z})
is a “unital ordered group”, sometimes denoted by
K^{0}(X,T)
(see
[
7
]
, §3.3). An order- and unit-preserving morphism from
K^{0}(X,T)
to
\mathbb{R}
is called a
state
. The above map
\mu\mapsto\alpha_{\mu}
gives a well known bijection (see Theorem 3.9.3 of
[
7
]
) from the set of invariant probability measures on
X
to the set of states on
K^{0}(X,T)
. This bijection extends to an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-linear isomorphism
i_{X}M
from
\cal M(X)
to the non-negative cone
H_{+}^{*}(X,\mathbb{R})
in the dual group
H^{*}(X,\mathbb{R})
.

Given now a morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
, then for the image subshift
Y=\sigma(X)
the measure transfer map defines canonically a map
i_{Y}M\circ\sigma_{X}M\circ(i_{X}M)^{-1}:H_{+}^{*}(X,\mathbb{R})\to H_{+}^{*}(Y,\mathbb{R})
, and hence, after normalization, a map from the set of states for
X
to the set of states for
Y
. The natural question arises, whether this map can be defined alternatively by a more direct approach.

Indeed, if
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is letter-to-letter, then for two subshifts
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
Y=\sigma(X)=\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}(X)
any function
g\in\partial_{T}C(Y,\mathbb{Z})
defines a function
g\circ\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
which also satisfies

(3.8)

g\circ\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\in\partial_{T}C(X,\mathbb{Z})\,.

Thus
\sigma
defines a contravariant morphism
H(\sigma):H(Y,\mathbb{Z})\to H(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and hence an induced dual map
H(\sigma)^{*}:H(X,\mathbb{R})^{*}\to H(Y,\mathbb{R})^{*}
.
Since
X
is assumed to be recurrent,
we obtain via the above isomorphisms
i_{X}M
and
i_{Y}M
a map
(i_{Y}M)^{-1}\circ H(\sigma)^{*}\circ i_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(Y)
, which agrees
with the map
\sigma M
, since in this letter-to-letter case both coincide
with the push-forward map
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}_{*}\,
.

However, for any attempt to
push the above approach further to get an alternative “coinvariant” description of the measure transfer map,
there is a problem at the very base, in that the property (
3.8
) fails to hold in most cases where
\sigma
is not letter-to-letter. We describe below a simple example which highlights the basic problem:

Set
\cal A=\{a,b,c\}
and
B=\{d,e\}
, and define
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
by setting
\sigma(a)=d\,,\,\,\sigma(b)=e
and
\sigma(c)=de
. Set
X=\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
Y=\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
and notice
Y=\sigma(X)
.

Consider now the function
g:Y\to\mathbb{Z}
defined by
g({\bf y})=0
if
{\bf y}\in[d]
and
g({\bf y})=1
if
{\bf y}\in[e]
, which gives
g({\bf y})=0
if
{\bf y}\in[dd]\cup[de]
as well as
g({\bf y})=1
if
{\bf y}\in[ed]\cup[ee]
. Since
T([dd]\cup[ed])=[d]
and
T([de]\cup[ee])=[e]
, we obtain
g\circ T({\bf y})=0
if
{\bf y}\in[dd]\cup[ed]
and
g\circ T({\bf y})=1
if
{\bf y}\in[de]\cup[ee]
. Hence the function
g\circ T-g\in\partial_{T}C(Y,\mathbb{Z})
satisfies
(g\circ T-g)({\bf y})=0
if
{\bf y}\in[dd]\cup[ee]
,
(g\circ T-g)({\bf y})=1
if
{\bf y}\in[de]
and
(g\circ T-g)({\bf y})=-1
if
{\bf y}\in[ed]
.

Let us now consider the function
(g\circ T-g)\circ\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and its value on the periodic word
{\bf x}\in X
given by
{\bf x}=\ldots ccc\ldots
. We have
{\bf x}_{[1,+\infty)}=ccc\ldots
and thus
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}({\bf x})_{[1,+\infty)}=\sigma({\bf x}_{[1,+\infty)})=dedede\ldots
, so that
\sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}({\bf x})\in[de]
. As a consequence we obtain

\begin{array}[]{clc}(\alpha_{\sigma})_{*}(\mu_{\sigma})(cc)=&[\mu_{\sigma}(a_{1}a_{1})+\mu_{\sigma}(a_{1}a_{3})+\mu_{\sigma}(a_{1}b_{2})+\mu_{\sigma}(a_{1}b_{3})]\\

4.2.
An alternative evaluation method

As illustrated by the example considered in the previous subsection, already for fairly simple morphisms

.
In this subsection we explain how a more efficient evaluation technique is obtained (compare formulas (
4.3
) and (

Given a morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
, consider any word

and its image
\sigma(w)=y_{1}\ldots y_{m}\in\cal B^{*}

x_{i}\in\cal A
and

An
occurrence of

in

is a factor
y_{k^{\prime}}\ldots y_{\ell^{\prime}}
of

which satisfies

An occurrence of
w^{\prime}
in
\sigma(w)
is called

if its first letter occurs in
\sigma(x_{1})
and its last letter in
\sigma(x_{n})
.
In particular, for
|w|=1
any occurrence of

in
\sigma(w)
is essential. If

has length

, then an occurrence of
w^{\prime}
in

is essential if the factor

\sigma(x_{1})
into
\sigma(x_{2})

For

a factor

of
\sigma(w)

\sigma(x_{2}\ldots x_{n-1})

The number of all essential occurrences of

in

must satisfy

(4.1)

\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{||\sigma||}\leq|w|\leq\frac{|w^{\prime}|-2}{\langle\sigma\rangle}+2\,,

where

(4.3)

\mu^{\sigma}(w^{\prime})=\sum_{{\big\{}u\,\in\,\cal A^{*}\,{\big|}\,|u|\leq\frac{|w^{\prime}|-2}{\langle\sigma\rangle}+2{\big\}}}{\lfloor\sigma(u)\rfloor}_{w^{\prime}}\cdot\mu(u)\,.

Recall

5.

In this section we will establish a natural criterion which guaranties that the measure transfer map
\sigma M
is

, when restricted to measures which are supported by suitable subshifts. We first need to recall and specify the notation from Section

:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
be a non-erasing monoid morphism, and let

be any subshift.

(1)

We say that
\sigma
is

if the map

restricted to the shift-orbits of

is injective.

(2)

We say that
\sigma
is
shift-period preserving in

if
\sigma
preserves the shift-period for every periodic orbit in
X
(see Definition
2.10
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\sigma^{\prime}:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}:\cal B^{*}\to\cal C^{*}
be two non-erasing morphisms
(so that the composition

\sigma:=\sigma^{\prime\prime}\circ\sigma^{\prime}
is
also non-erasing.)
For any subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
consider
the image subshift
Y=\sigma(X)
.
Then we have:

(1)

The map

\sigma
is shift-orbit injective in
X
if and only if
\sigma^{\prime}
is shift-orbit injective in
X
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
is shift-orbit injective in
Y
.

(2)

The map

\sigma
is shift-period preserving in
X
if and only if
\sigma^{\prime}
is shift-period preserving in
X
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
is shift-period preserving in
Y
.

Proof.

Using the surjectivity from Lemma
2.4
(2), we obtain the first of these statements as direct application of the fact that for any two composable surjective maps
f
and
g
the composition
g\circ f
is injective if and only if both maps
f
and
g
are injective.
Statement (2) follows directly from the
trivial
observation that
\sigma(w)
is a proper power (see (

)) if and only if one of the three,
w,\sigma^{\prime}(w)
or
\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}(w))
is a proper power.

-adic community is not “recognizable” but “recognizable for aperiodic points”, or “eventually recognizable for aperiodic points”.
The relation of this notion to the concepts introduced in this section
(see Fig. 1) is given by the following; a formal proof
is given in Remark
3.10 (3) of
[
3
]
.

Proposition 5.9
.

If a non-erasing morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is shift-orbit injective in a subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
\sigma
is recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
.

\sqcup
\sqcap

The converse implication of Proposition
5.9

turns however
out to be wrong:
A very simple counterexample is given by the letter-to-letter morphism

\sigma_{1}:\{a,b\}^{*}\to\{c\}^{*}
from Remark
2.9
and the subshift
X=\{a^{\pm\infty},b^{\pm\infty}\}
.
Since
\{c\}^{*}
consists only of periodic words, it follows (see Warning
5.8
) that
\sigma_{1}
is automatically recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
. However, since
\sigma_{1}^{T}(\cal O(a^{\pm\infty}))=\cal O(c^{\pm\infty})
and
\sigma_{1}^{T}(\cal O(b^{\pm\infty}))=\cal O(c^{\pm\infty})
, the map
\sigma_{1}
is not shift-orbit injective in
X
.

This example shows also that “
\sigma
recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
” is in general too weak to be able to deduce that the measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X))
is injective: For
\sigma_{1}
as above one observes directly from Lemma
3.7
(d) that
\sigma_{1}M(\mu_{a})=\sigma_{1}M(\mu_{b})=\mu_{c}
.

We also observe that the injectivity of the measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M
does in general not imply the injectivity of the map
\sigma_{X}^{T}
induced by
\sigma
on the orbits of
X
, so that the converse of Theorem
5.6
does not hold in full generality. Indeed, we have:

Remark 5.10
.

Let
X\subseteq\{a,b\}^{*}
be the countable subshift which consists of the two orbits defined by

\ldots aaa\ldots\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ldots aaabaaa\ldots\,.

Set
Y=\{c\}^{*}
, and consider again the morphism
\sigma_{1}
from Remark
2.9
, given by
a\mapsto c
and
b\mapsto c
. This map is not injective on the shift-orbits of
X
, but the map
(\sigma_{1})_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(Y)
is injective, since
the only non-trivial invariant probability measure on
X
is the atomic measure
\mu_{a}
concentrated on the element
\ldots aaa\ldots
. This can be seen from a direct application of the Kirchhoff rules (
2.2
). Alternatively one can use the fact that
X
is the substitutive subshift associated to the substitution given by
a\mapsto a^{2}\,,\,\,b\mapsto aba
and apply Corollary 3.5 (1) of
[
2
]
.

More generally, if a subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
contains a non-empty subset
X_{0}\subseteq X
which consists entirely of biinfinite words that lie outside the support of any invariant measure on
X
, then the violation of the injectivity of
\sigma^{T}
on
X_{0}
will not have any impact on the injectivity of the measure transfer map
\sigma M
, for any morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
.
This is the basic principle which is now used in the proof of the following proposition, which shows that the injectivity of a map
\sigma_{X}M
as above is in general too weak to deduce that
\sigma
is recognizable for aperiodic points. It completes the set of implications and their refusals between the properties discussed here, as summarized in Fig. 1.

Proposition 5.11
.

There exist a subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and a morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
which is not recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
, while the induced measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X))
is injective.

Proof.

We will give a concrete example of
X
and
\sigma
as claimed: Set
\cal A=\{a,b,c\}
and
\cal B=\{d,e\}
, and let
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
be the letter-to-letter morphism given by
\sigma(a)=d
and
\sigma(b)=\sigma(c)=e
. Let now
X\subseteq\cal A^{*}
be the countable subshift which consists of the three orbits defined by

\ldots aaa\ldots\,\,\,\,\,,\,\,\,\,\,\ldots aaabaaa\ldots\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ldots aaacaaa\ldots\,.

As in Remark
5.10
the only invariant probability measure on
X
is the characteristic measure
\mu_{a}\,
, so that the induced measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X))
is automatically injective. On the other hand, the two biinfninite indexed words
\ldots aaa\cdot baaa\ldots
and
\ldots aaa\cdot caaa\ldots
lie in distinct shift-orbits and have the same image
\ldots ddd\cdot eddd\ldots\,
, which is aperiodic. It follows that
\sigma
is not recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
.

\sqcap

Remark 5.12
.

There exist also stronger but slightly more intricate examples as given in the last proof, where the image subshift
\sigma(X)
is completely aperiodic. They are based on the same construction principle, but the periodic orbit of
\ldots aaa\ldots
is replaced by the minimal and uniquely ergodic subshift
X_{\tau}
defined by any primitive substitution
\tau:\cal A^{*}\to\cal A^{*}
, which in addition we assume to possess a left-infinite word
V
and a right-infinite word
W
that are both fixed by
\tau
. Such a substitution is given for example by the square of the Fibonacci substitution
\tau(x)=xyx\,,\,\,\tau(y)=xy
with
V=\ldots\tau^{2}(xy)\,\tau(xy)\,xy\,x
and
W=x\,yx\,\tau(yx)\,\tau^{2}(yx)\ldots
.

One now considers three additional letters
a,b,c
not contained in
\cal A
and defines the letter-to-letter morphism
\sigma:(\{b,c\}\cup\cal A)^{*}\to(\{a\}\cup\cal A)^{*}
by setting
\sigma(a_{i})=a_{i}
for any
a_{i}\in\cal A
and
\sigma(b)=\sigma(c)=a
. We define
X=X_{\tau}\cup\cal O(VbW)\cup\cal O(VcW)
and observe that the biinfinite indexed words
V\cdot bW
and
V\cdot cW
are sent by
\sigma
both to the same image word
V\cdot aW
which is not periodic, so that
\sigma
is not recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
. On the other hand,
X
is easily seen to be the subshift
X=X_{\tau^{\prime}}
associated to the substitution
\tau^{\prime}:(\{b,c\}\cup\cal A)^{*}\to(\{b,c\}\cup\cal A)^{*}
which coincides with
\tau
on
\cal A
and sends
b
and
c
to
vbw
and
vcw
respectively, for an arbitrary non-empty finite suffix
v
of
V
and prefix
w
of
W
. It then follows from Corollary 3.5 (1) of
[
2
]
that
X
is uniquely ergodic, so that
\sigma_{X}M
is automatically injective.

Remark 5.13
.

(1)
From the examples in the proof of Proposition 5.10 and from Remark 5.11 one may get the impression that the only possibility, for a subshift
X
and a morphism
\sigma
which is not recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
while its associated measure transfer map
\sigma M_{X}
is injective, can occur if there is no invariant measure
\mu\in\cal M(X)
with
\mbox{Supp}(\mu)=X
. This impression, however, is treacherous: In Example 4.3 of
[
5
]
a uniquely ergodic infinite minimal subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
(indeed the substitution subshift of a primitive substitution) is exhibited as well as a morphism
\sigma
which is
2:1
on shift-orbits throughout all of
X
.

(2)
If one attempts to find additional conditions on
X
and
\sigma
which ensure that the two properties “recognizable for aperiodic points” and “injectivity of the measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M\,
” become equivalent, one should be aware of the following:

If
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective on
X
, then we have shown above that both properties are satisfied. If, on the other hand, two distinct shift-orbits
\cal O({\bf x})\neq\cal O({\bf x^{\prime}})
of
X
have the same
\sigma
-image, then we observe:

(a)

The first property is violated if and only if the image orbit
\cal O({\sigma(\bf x}))=\cal O(\sigma({\bf x^{\prime}}))
is not periodic.

(b)

For the second property we consider the subshifts
\overline{\cal O({\bf x})}
and
\overline{\cal O({\bf x^{\prime}})}
in
X
which are generated by
\bf x
and
\bf x^{\prime}
respectively. In order to produce a violation to this second property, we need that
\overline{\cal O({\bf x})}\neq\overline{\cal O({\bf x^{\prime}})}
. If one has furthermore that their measure cones
\cal M(\overline{\cal O({\bf x})})
and
\cal M(\overline{\cal O({\bf x^{\prime}})})
are distinct subcones of
\cal M(X)
, then indeed the second property is violated.

Remark 5.14
.

To finish this section, let us again consider the totality of the implications and their refusals as presented in Fig.1, for subshifts on which we impose additional assumptions. As before, we always consider a morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
as well as subshifts
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
Y=\sigma(X)\subseteq\cal B^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(1)

If
X
is minimal, then the four left-most properties in Fig.1 for
\sigma
on
X
are equivalent to each other, while the right-most property is a consequence, but the converse implication fails (as shown above in Remark
5.13
(1)).

(2)

The same is true if the image subshift
\sigma(X)
(and hence also
X
itself) is aperiodic, since in that case the equivalence between
“
\sigma
recognizable for aperiodic points in
X
” and “
\sigma
recognizable in

” comes for free.

(3)

If
\sigma(X)
is not aperiodic, one may add the assumption that

has a dense orbit, or a dense positive half-orbit.
However, neither of these assumptions suffices to turn any of the 6 refused implications in Fig.1 into an implication.
To be specific, we point out examples to the following three implication-refusals; the other three follow by using the implications from Fig.1.

(a)

“shift-orbit injective”
\,\,\Longrightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!/\quad
“shift-orbit injective and shift-period preserving”:

The simplest example with dense positive half-orbit in

and
\sigma:\{a\}^{*}\to\{b\}^{*}
with
\sigma(a)=b^{2}
.

More interesting examples are given by taking

to be any primitive substitution on two or more letters, and set
X^{\prime}
to be the substitution subshift
X^{\prime}=X_{\tau}
. One then “perturbes”
\tau
into
\tau^{\prime}
by adding a new generator
a^{\prime}
to
\cal A

\cal A^{\prime}

\tau^{\prime}(a_{k})=\tau(a_{k})a^{\prime}
for any
a_{k}\in\cal A

\tau^{\prime}(a^{\prime})=a^{\prime 2}

, and any

which is different from

, while the shift-period of

.

(b)

“recognizable for aperiodic points”
\,\,\Longrightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!/\quad
“measure transfer injective”:

We start with
\tau
and

as above, but add now two letters
a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}
to
\cal A
to get the alphabet
\cal A^{\prime\prime}
. We then define the substitution
\tau^{\prime\prime}:\cal A^{\prime\prime}\to\cal A^{\prime\prime}
by setting

\tau^{\prime\prime}(a_{k})=a^{\prime\prime}\tau(a_{k})a^{\prime}
for any

, as well as
\tau^{\prime\prime}(a^{\prime})=a^{\prime 2}
and
\tau^{\prime\prime}(a^{\prime\prime})=a^{\prime\prime 2}
.
Again, the periodic orbits
a^{\prime\pm\infty}
and
a^{\prime\prime\pm\infty}
belong to the substitution subshift

, and any

which is different from

and

has positive half-orbit which is dense in

. For
\cal A^{\prime}=\cal A\cup\{a^{\prime}\}
we now define
\sigma:\cal A^{\prime\prime*}\to\cal A^{\prime*}
to be the identity on
\cal A^{*}\subseteq\cal A^{\prime\prime*}
and by setting
\sigma(a^{\prime})=\sigma(a^{\prime\prime})=a^{\prime}
. Then

is recognizable for aperiodic points in

, and the two characteristic measures
\mu_{a^{\prime}}
and

are distinct, but have the same image
\mu_{a^{\prime}}
under the measure transfer map
\sigma_{X}M
.

(c)

“measure transfer injective”
\,\,\Longrightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!/\quad
“recognizable for aperiodic points”:

Here we can point to the infinite minimal substitution subshift
X
and the

\sigma
from Example 4.3 of

]
as cited above in Remark

(1), which has all the required properties.

6.
The injectivity of the measure transfer for letter-to-letter morphisms

Throughout this section we assume that
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
is a letter-to-letter morphism of free monoids, and that
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a given subshift. From Definition-Remark
3.6
we observe directly that in this special case the induced measure transfer map
\sigma M
is given by the classical push-forward map
\mu\mapsto\sigma_{*}(\mu)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective in
X
, and if for some
{\bf x}\in X
the image word
\sigma({\bf x})
is periodic, then
\bf x
is periodic too.

Proof.

From the definition of the image subhift (see Definition-Remark
2.2

and Lemma
2.4
)
it follows directly
that for any biinfinite word
{\bf x}\in X
the closure
\overline{\cal O({\bf x})}
of its orbit
\cal O({\bf x})
is a subshift which satisfies

\sigma(\overline{\cal O({\bf x})})=\overline{\cal O(\sigma({\bf x}))}\,.

The claim is hence a direct consequence of the well known and easy to prove fact that the shift-orbit of any biinfinite word is closed if and only if the word is periodic.

\sqcup
\sqcap

For any word
w\in\cal L(X)
and any integer
n\geq 0
let us consider the set
W_{n}(w)
of both-sided prolongations of
w
in
\cal L(X)
by
n
letters on each side, i.e.

(6.1)

W_{n}(w)\,\,:=\,\,\{uwv\in\cal L(X)\mid|u|=|v|=n\}\,.

For any
w^{\prime}=uwv\in W_{n}(w)
we
now consider the image
\sigma(w^{\prime})
and its preimage set

\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(w^{\prime}))\cap\cal L(X)
. We split the set
W_{n}(w)
into a disjoint union,

(6.2)

W_{n}(w)=U_{n}(w)\sqcup A_{n}(w)\,,

by setting

(6.3)

w^{\prime}\in U_{n}(w)\,\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\,\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(w^{\prime}))\cap\cal L(X)\subseteq W_{n}(w)

and

(6.4)

w^{\prime}\in A_{n}(w)\,\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\,\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(w^{\prime}))\cap\cal L(X)\nsubseteq W_{n}(w)\,.

In other words, for any
w^{\prime}=uwv\in A_{n}(w)
there exists a word
u^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}\in\cal L(X)
with
|u^{\prime}|=|v^{\prime}|=n
and
|w^{\prime\prime}|=|w|
such that
w^{\prime\prime}\neq w
and
\sigma(u^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime})=\sigma(uwv)
,
while for any

w^{\prime}=uwv\in U_{n}(w)
any word
u^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}\in\cal L(X)
with
|u^{\prime}|=|v^{\prime}|=n
and
\sigma(u^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime})=\sigma(uwv)
one has
w^{\prime\prime}=w
(and thus
u^{\prime}w^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}\in W_{n}(w)
).

We now fix some
w\in\cal L(X)
and observe directly from the definition of the sets
A_{n}(w)

that their elements define cylinders for which their union
[A_{n}(w)]:=\bigcup\{[uwv]\mid uwv\in A_{n}(w)\}\subseteq X
satisfies

T^{-n-1}([A_{n+1}(w)])\,\,\subseteq\,\,T^{-n}([A_{n}(w)])\,.

Remark 6.2
.

(1)
We denote by
A_{\infty}(w)
the intersection of the nested family of the
T^{-n}([A_{n}(w)])
.
Since by definition we have
A_{n}(w)\subseteq\cal L(X)
for all
n\geq 0
we know that
A_{\infty}(w)\subseteq X
.

(2)
For any invariant measure
\mu
on
X
and any
n\geq 0
one has
thus

\mu([A_{n+1}(w)])\leq\mu([A_{n}(w)])
and

\lim\mu([A_{n}(w)])=\mu(A_{\infty}(w))\,.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective in
X
, then for any
w\in\cal L(X)
the intersection
A_{\infty}(w)
consists only of periodic words.

Proof.

For any biinfinite word
{\bf x}\in A_{\infty}(w)
there exists, by definition of
A_{\infty}(w)
, a sequence of words
u_{n},v_{n},u^{\prime}_{n},v^{\prime}_{n},w^{\prime}_{n}\in\cal L(X)
which have the following properties:

(1)

|u_{n}|=|v_{n}|=|u^{\prime}_{n}|=|v^{\prime}_{n}|=n\quad\text{and }\quad|w^{\prime}_{n}|=|w|

(2)

u^{\prime}_{n}w^{\prime}_{n}v^{\prime}_{n}\in\cal L(X)
and
u_{n}wv_{n}\in\cal L(X)

(3)

\sigma(u_{n}wv_{n})=\sigma(u^{\prime}_{n}w^{\prime}_{n}v^{\prime}_{n})

(4)

w^{\prime}_{n}\neq w

(5)

\lim wv_{n}={\bf x}_{[1,+\infty)}
and
\lim u_{n}={\bf x}_{(-\infty,0]}

The statement (5) needs a bit of interpretation: We think of the words
wv_{n}
as being indexed from
1
to
|wv_{n}|
and of
u_{n}
as being indexed from
-|u_{n}|+1
to
0
, and we pass to the limit while keeping the indices fixed. In other words, statement (5) is equivalent to

(5’)

\lim{\bf y}(n)={\bf x}
in
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where for any
n\geq 0
the biinfinite word
{\bf y}(n)
is defined through
{\bf y}_{[1,\infty)}(n)=wv_{n}^{+\infty}
and
{\bf y}_{(\infty,0]}(n)=u_{n}^{-\infty}
.

We now proceed analogously with the sequence of words
w^{\prime}_{n}v^{\prime}_{n}
(indexed from
1
to
|w^{\prime}_{n}v^{\prime}_{n}|
) and the words
u^{\prime}_{n}
(indexed from
-|u^{\prime}_{n}|+1
to
0
),
and pass to a subsequence of the integers
n
such that there exists a biinfinite “limit word”
{\bf z}\in\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
which satisfies

(6)

\lim w^{\prime}_{n}v^{\prime}_{n}={\bf z}_{[1,+\infty)}
and
\lim u^{\prime}_{n}={\bf z}_{(-\infty,0]}\,.

Again, these limits are meant to be equivalent to the statement

(6’)

\lim{\bf z}(n)={\bf z}
in
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where the
{\bf z}(n)\in\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
are defined via
{\bf z}_{[1,\infty)}(n)=w^{\prime}_{n}{v^{\prime}_{n}}^{+\infty}
and
{\bf z}_{(\infty,0]}(n)={u^{\prime}_{n}}^{-\infty}
.

Since by property (2) we have
u^{\prime}_{n}w^{\prime}_{n}v^{\prime}_{n}\in\cal L(X)
, for all indices
n
in the limits (6) or (6’), we obtain
{\bf z}\in X
. Furthermore, from the above limit set-up we know that the factor
{\bf z}_{[1,|w|]}
of
\bf z
is equal to some
w^{\prime}_{n}
, and thus (by property (4)) distinct from
w
. We thus deduce

{\bf z}\neq{\bf x}\,.

On the other hand, we obtain from property (3) directly

\sigma({\bf z})=\lim\sigma({\bf z}_{n})=\lim\sigma({\bf y}_{n})=\sigma({\bf x})\,.

We now use the assumption that
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective in
X
to deduce that
\bf x
and
\bf z
are shift-translates of each other: There exists some integer
k\neq 0
such that
T^{k}({\bf z})={\bf x}
. We thus have
T^{k}(\sigma({\bf x}))=T^{k}(\sigma({\bf z}))=\sigma(T^{k}({\bf z}))=\sigma({\bf x})
. But any biinfinite word which is equal to a non-trivial shift-translate of itself must be periodic. Hence
\sigma({\bf x})
is periodic, and now we can employ Lemma
6.1
in order to deduce that the biinfinite word
{\bf x}
is periodic.

\sqcup
\sqcap

We denote by
\text{\rm Per}(X)
the subset of all biinfinite periodic words in
X
. We observe that both, the countable set
\text{\rm Per}(X)
and its complement
X\smallsetminus\text{\rm Per}(X)
are measurable subsets of
\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Any measure
\mu\in\cal M(X)
satisfies
\mu(\text{\rm Per}(X))=0
if and only if
\mu
is non-atomic. This gives rise to a
decomposition

(6.5)

\mu\,\,=\,\,\mu^{per}+\mu^{na}

of
\mu
as sum of a non-atomic measure
\mu^{na}
and a

periodic
measure
\mu^{per}
,
by which we mean that
\mu^{per}
is
a countable or finite sum of scalar multiples of the characteristic measures
\mu_{w}
from (
2.5
).
Note that this decomposition is canonical, in that
for any measurable set
B\subseteq X
one has

(6.6)

\mu^{per}(B)\,\,=\,\,\mu(B\cap\text{\rm Per}(X))\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{na}(B)\,\,=\,\,\mu(B\cap(X\smallsetminus\text{\rm Per}(X)))\,.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
be two invariant measures on
X

which are both non-atomic.
Assume furthermore that
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective.
Then one has

\sigma_{*}(\mu)=\sigma_{*}(\mu^{\prime})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu=\mu^{\prime}\,.

Proof.

We fix an arbitrary word
w\in\cal L(X)
and consider for any
n\geq 0
the subsets
W_{n}(w),U_{n}(w)
and
A_{n}(w)
as defined in the equalities (
6.1
), (
6.3
) and (
6.4
),
abbreviated here to
W_{n},U_{n}
and
A_{n}
respectively.

Let us also fix some integer
n\geq 0
. From the definition of a weight function (see Section
2.1
) we see directly that for any measure
\mu
on
X
the corresponding weight function
satisfies

(6.7)

\mu(w)=\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W_{n}(w)}\mu(w^{\prime})\,.

From (
6.2
) we know that
the set
W_{n}
decomposes as disjoint union of
U_{n}
and
A_{n}
,
so that (
6.7
) gives us

(6.8)

\mu(w)=\mu(U_{n})+\mu(A_{n})\,,

where we set
\mu(U_{n}):=\underset{w^{\prime}\in U_{n}}{\sum}\mu(w^{\prime})
and
\mu(A_{n}):=\underset{w^{\prime}\in A_{n}}{\sum}\mu(w^{\prime})
.

We now define
V_{n}:=\sigma(U_{n})
and observe from the definition of
U_{n}
that
one has

\sigma^{-1}(V_{n})\cap\cal L(X)=U_{n}
.
Hence we
deduce from the definition of the push-forward measure
\sigma_{*}(\mu)
,
together with the fact that by definition of
\mu\in\cal M(X)
one has

\mu(w_{0})=0
for any
w_{0}\notin\cal L(X)
,
that

(6.9)

\mu(U_{n})=\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\sigma_{*}(\mu)(v)=:\sigma_{*}(\mu)(V_{n})\,.

We thus deduce from the equalities (
6.8
) and (
6.9
) that for any integer
n\geq 0
one has

\mu(w)=\mu(U_{n})+\mu(A_{n})=\sigma_{*}(\mu)(V_{n})+\mu(A_{n})\,.

We now pass to the limit for
n\to\infty
and
recall from Remark
6.2
(2)
that
\lim\mu(A_{n})=\mu(A_{\infty}(w))
. From Lemma
6.3
we know
A_{\infty}(w)\subseteq\text{\rm Per}(X)

\lim\mu(A_{n})=0

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\mu
be any shift-invariant measure on the subshift
X
. Then we have:

(1)

If
\mu
is periodic (i.e.
\mu(X\smallsetminus\text{\rm Per}(X)=0

\sigma_{*}(\mu)

is also periodic (i.e.
\sigma_{*}(\mu)(\sigma(X)\smallsetminus\text{\rm Per}(\sigma(X))=0

(2)

Under the additional hypothesis that
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective, we also have: If
\mu
is non-atomic (i.e.
\mu(\text{\rm Per}(X))=0
), then
\sigma_{*}(\mu)
is also non-atomic (i.e.
\sigma_{*}(\mu)(\text{\rm Per}(\sigma(X)))=0
).

Proof.

(1) If
\mu(X\smallsetminus\text{\rm Per}(X))=0
, then
\mu
is carried entirely by the (countable) set of periodic words in
X
: There exist a coefficient
\lambda_{w}\geq 0
for any
w\in\cal L(X)
such that, using the characteristic measures
\mu_{w}
from (
2.5
), the measure
\mu
can be expressed as countable sum

\mu=\sum_{w\in\cal L(X)}\lambda_{w}\,\mu_{w}\,.

But then Lemma
3.7
(d) gives directly

\sigma_{*}(\mu)=\underset{w\in\cal L(X)}{\sum}\lambda_{w}\,\mu_{\sigma(w)}\,

which is clearly the zero-measure outside of

(2)
For any

that
\sigma^{-1}({\bf x})

\mu(\text{\rm Per}(X))=0

\sigma_{*}(\mu)({\bf x})=\mu(\sigma^{-1}({\bf x}))=0

Proposition 6.6
.

For the subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
let
\cal M_{per}(X)\subseteq\cal M(X)
and
\cal M_{na}(X)\subseteq\cal M(X)
be the subsets of periodic and of non-atomic invariant measures on
X
respectively.

For any shift-orbit injective morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
the injectivity of the push-forward map

\sigma_{*}^{X}:\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X)),\,\mu\mapsto\sigma_{*}(\mu)
follows if one proves the injectivity for the restrictions of
\sigma_{*}^{X}
to both,
\cal M_{per}(X)
and
\cal M_{na}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\cal M(X)
be invariant measures which satisfy
\sigma_{*}(\mu_{1})=\sigma_{*}(\mu_{1})=:\mu_{0}
. Consider the canonical decompositions into a periodic and an non-atomic measure from (
6.5
) given by
\mu_{1}=\mu_{1}^{per}+\mu_{1}^{na}
,
\mu_{2}=\mu_{2}^{per}+\mu_{2}^{na}
and
\mu_{0}=\mu_{0}^{per}+\mu_{0}^{na}
. From Lemma
6.5

and the uniqueness of the canonical decomposition for
\mu_{0}
(see the equalities (
6.6
)) we derive
that
\sigma_{*}(\mu_{1}^{per})=\sigma_{*}(\mu_{2}^{per})=\mu_{0}^{per}
and
\sigma_{*}(\mu_{1}^{na})=\sigma_{*}(\mu_{2}^{na})=\mu_{0}^{na}
. Injectivity of
\sigma_{*}^{X}
on
\cal M_{per}(X)
and
\cal M_{na}(X)
implies
\mu_{1}^{per}=\mu_{2}^{per}
and
\mu_{1}^{na}=\mu_{2}^{na}
, which shows
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

Theorem 6.7
.

For any letter-to-letter morphism
\sigma:\cal A^{*}\to\cal B^{*}
and any subshift
X\subseteq\cal A^{\mathbb{Z}}
one has: If
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective in
X
, then the push-forward map
\cal M(X)\to\cal M(\sigma(X)),\,\mu\mapsto\sigma_{*}(\mu)
is injective.

Proof.

From Proposition
6.6
we know that it suffices to prove the injectivity of the measure map
\mu\mapsto\sigma_{*}(\mu)
for the two cases, where

\mu
is periodic or where
\mu
is non-atomic.

The latter case is already dealt with in Proposition
6.4
, so that we can from now on assume that
\mu
is carried by
\text{\rm Per}(X)
. But in this case the injectivity of the map
\mu\mapsto\sigma_{*}(\mu)
is trivial, since by the injectivity assumption on the shift-orbits together with Lemma
6.1
we know that for every periodic word
{\bf x}\in\sigma(X)
the set
\sigma^{-1}({\bf x})\cap X

consists of words
{\bf y}_{i}
which are periodic as well and belong all to a single shift-orbit
\cal O({\bf y}_{i})
. Hence we have
\mu(\cal O({\bf y}_{i}))=\sigma_{*}(\mu)(\cal O({\bf x}))
.
Since any invariant measure on
X
which is carried by
\text{\rm Per}(X)
is determined by knowing its evaluation on every periodic orbit, it follows that

\mu
is entirely determined by
\sigma_{*}(\mu)
.

The following remark serves as continuation of the discussion started at the end of last section:

Remark 6.8
.

(1)
We would like to point out a subtlety in the last proof: Although the preimage set
\sigma^{-1}(\cal O({\bf x}))
of any periodic orbit
\cal O({\bf x})
is a single periodic orbit, it is in general not true that the preimage set
\sigma^{-1}({\bf x})
consists of a single biinfinite word
{\bf y}_{i}\in\sigma^{-1}(\cal O({\bf x}))
. It is here that the missing assumption “shift-period preserving” materializes, compared to the case where we assume “
\sigma
recognizable in
X
” instead of our weaker assumption “
\sigma
is shift-orbit injective”.

(2)
On the other hand, as pointed out at the end of last section, “shift-orbit injective” is a slightly stronger property than “recognizable for aperiodic points”, and as we have observed in the last section, the latter doesn’t suffice to deduce the injectivity of the push-forward map on
\cal M(X)
.
In fact, this injectivity
fails in general already for non-atomic measures on
the given subshift
X
, as can be seen easily from the morphism which sends all letters of an alphabet of size
\geq 2
to a single letter of the image alphabet; any such morphism is a forteriori recognizable for aperiodic points in the full shift
(see Warning
5.8
).
