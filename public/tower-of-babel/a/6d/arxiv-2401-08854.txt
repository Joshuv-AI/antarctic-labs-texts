Remote sensing of a levitated superconductor with a flux-tunable microwave cavity
Philip Schmidt ∗, Remi Claessen ∗, Gerard Higgins, Joachim Hofer, Jannek J. Hansen, Peter Asenbaum, Martin Zemlicka, Kevin Uhl, Reinhold Kleiner, Rudolf Gross, Hans Huebl, Michael Trupke, Markus Aspelmeyer

Abstract
We present a cavity-electromechanical system comprising a superconducting quantum interference device which is embedded in a microwave resonator and coupled via a pick-up loop to a 6 µ ​ g 6\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro g} magnetically-levitated superconducting sphere. The motion of the sphere in the magnetic trap induces a frequency shift in the SQUID-cavity system. We use microwave spectroscopy to characterize the system, and we demonstrate that the electromechanical interaction is tunable. The measured displacement sensitivity of ​ 10 − 7 m − 1 {10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1} defines a path towards ground-state cooling of levitated particles with Planck-scale masses at millikelvin environment temperatures.

A key parameter of the electromechanical detector is its imprecision level. It can be described using the formalism developed for cavity optomechanical systems where the optical (mechanical) subsystem’s loss rate is denoted by
\kappa
(
\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}
)
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
. There, the imprecision level is given by
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{co}}=\kappa/(16\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}G^{2})
where we assume the absence of photon loss, added noise from our detection chain, and that the system is far from the sideband-resolved regime (
\Omega_{\textrm{m}}\ll\kappa
). The effective coupling rate (
\sqrt{\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}}g_{0}
) and the system loss rates
\kappa
and
\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}
give the quantum cooperativity
C_{\mathrm{q}}=4\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}g_{0}^{2}/(\kappa\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}n_{\mathrm{th}})
, where
n_{\mathrm{th}}\approx k_{\mathrm{B}}T/(\hbar\Omega_{\textrm{m}})
is the thermal phonon occupation and
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant. The imprecision level can then be written in terms of the cooperativity,
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{co}}=S_{\mathrm{gs}}/C_{\mathrm{q}}
, where
S_{\mathrm{gs}}=4\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}^{2}/\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}n_{\mathrm{th}}
corresponds to resolving displacements on the scale of the mechanical ground state extent on a timescale comparable to the thermal decoherence time. The quantum cooperativity also allows to define a measurement efficiency,
\eta_{\mathrm{e}}=(1+1/C_{\mathrm{q}})^{-1}
. The observed imprecision level
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{det}}
will be equal to or greater than
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{co}}
due to limitations of the apparatus. This increase is quantified by the detection efficiency
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}=S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{co}}/S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{det}}
. Together, these two efficiencies yield the total measurement efficiency,
\eta=\eta_{\mathrm{d}}\eta_{\mathrm{e}}

Clerk et al. 2003
;
Magrini et al. 2021
.

The best value for
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{det}}
obtained in this first-generation device is
100\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}
, with a readout photon number around
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}\approx 20
. To investigate the cavity’s noise floor, we apply a weaker probe power to reach
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=0.05\pm 0.03
, thus deep in its linear coupling regime
Pirkkalainen et al. 2015
and we set the flux sensitivity at
\textit{f}_{\Phi}=$1.7\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$/\Phi_{0}
via
I_{\mathrm{ext}}
. We then tune the interaction strength by varying the trap gradient
b_{\mathrm{z}}
, and determine the coupling strength
G
and imprecision level for each value of
b_{\mathrm{z}}
. The results are plotted for all three mechanical modes in Fig.
4
. The coupling strength differs for the individual modes as they couple differently to the PUL, cf. Appendix
D
. Nonetheless, we find that the coupling and imprecision of all modes follow
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{det}}\cdot(G/2\pi)^{2}=$0.61\pm 2\text{\,}\mathrm{THz}$
(black solid line; see Appendix
E
for a magnified view of the data range). The quantum-limited noise density for perfect detection is
S_{\mathrm{imp}}^{\mathrm{co}}\cdot(G/2\pi)^{2}=$26\pm 13\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
. Thus we find
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}=(4.3\pm 2.1)\cdot 10^{-5}
. We attribute the decreased efficiency to signal lost from the cavity, added noise at the high-electron mobility transistor (HEMT) amplifier, and imperfect room temperature amplification (see Appendix
E
for details).

III
Outlook

Path towards the quantum regime

Following the description in the previous section, the detection efficiency determines the imprecision level that can be reached. In turn, this resolution gives the phonon number that the mechanical system can be directly cooled to, with sufficiently strong feedback, and is given by
n_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{min}}=(1/\sqrt{\eta}-1)/2

Magrini et al. 2021
. Therefore
\eta>1/9
is required for ground state cooling. For our system, this value corresponds to a noise spectral density below
S_{\mathrm{gs}}=($0.8\text{\,}\mathrm{fm}$)^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}
highlighted in Fig.
4
as a blue dashed line for the
z
-mode
Magrini et al. 2021
. Thus, it will be necessary to increase the coupling strength and reduce the detection noise. To quantify the necessary improvements, we focus only on the
z
-mode along the strong trap axis, as it enables higher frequencies and coupling rates. In addition, we assume to reach similar mechanical properties as discussed in an earlier work employing an identical trap design. Namely
T=$15\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
and
Q=\Omega_{\textrm{m}}/\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}=2.6\cdot 10^{7}

Hofer et al. 2023
.

We also conservatively consider all loss channels contributing to the thermal decoherence, thus upon ground state cooling the effective linewidth broadens to
\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}\rightarrow\Gamma_{\textrm{eff}}=\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}n_{\mathrm{th}}
. Describing the displacement noise spectral density of the detector requires the back-action force noise spectral density
S^{\mathrm{ba}}_{\mathrm{FF}}=4\hbar^{2}G^{2}\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}/\kappa
(in the limit
\Omega_{\textrm{m}}\ll\kappa
) to be multiplied by the mechanical susceptibility
\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\Gamma)=(m(\Omega_{\textrm{m}}^{2}-\Omega^{2}-i\cdot\Gamma\Omega))^{-1}
. This requires to distinguish between the back-action force noise of an element in equilibrium with the thermal environment, having a linewidth of
\Gamma_{\textrm{m}}
and an element cooled to its quantum mechanical ground state with broadened linewidth
\Gamma_{\textrm{eff}}
. In particular, we define the on-resonance (
\Omega\rightarrow\Omega_{\textrm{m}}
) back-action displacement noise spectral densities
S_{\mathrm{ba}}^{\mathrm{th}}=S^{\mathrm{ba}}_{\mathrm{FF}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\Gamma_{\textrm{m}})|^{2}
and
S_{\mathrm{ba}}^{\mathrm{gs}}=S^{\mathrm{ba}}_{\mathrm{FF}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\Gamma_{\textrm{eff}})|^{2}
.

The microwave sensing implementation enables the use of squeezed light to enhance the detection efficiency, in particular by using Josephson parametric amplifiers. For quantum-limited amplifier systems,
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}
up to
0.7
has been demonstrated
Renger et al. 2021
. Implementing such a device with a critically coupled microwave cavity would allow us to reach efficiencies of up to
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}=0.4
for
30\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
gain, achievable using a non-degenerate parametric amplifier (black dotted line in Fig.
4
)
Winkel et al. 2020
, cf. Appendix
E
. With such a detection efficiency, the electromechanical quantum cooperativity
C_{\mathrm{q}}
required to reach
\eta>1/9
is
0.38
.

In the measurement presented in Fig.
4
we achieve a quantum cooperativity of
C_{\mathrm{q}}=5\cdot 10^{-17}
at a readout level of
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=0.05
. While this presents a significant gap towards the parameters required for quantum control, we are confident that this can be achieved. The experimental factors which contribute to the small cooperativity in this first-generation device offer multiple opportunities for improvement. In particular, the flux coupling can be significantly increased: Firstly,
in situ
tuning of the position of the sphere relative to the PUL, affecting
\beta
, provides a predicted enhancement in cooperativity by a factor
4\cdot 10^{10}
. This fine-tuning can be achieved through the utilization of nano-positioners, which can adjust the position of the PUL to bring it closer to the particle. Alternatively, the particle can be shifted closer to the PUL by manipulating the trap center with auxiliary coils. This improvement, together with the use of a higher readout photon number
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=10
, are highlighted by the downward diagonal arrow in Fig.
4
. Secondly,
\alpha
can be improved by reducing the parasitic inductance
L_{\mathrm{t}}
from the flux transformer and by better alignment between the input coil and the SQUID loop. We find that the enhanced flux transformation will translate to an increase of
C_{\mathrm{q}}
by a factor of
2.5\cdot 10^{3}
. Together, these improvements will be sufficient to enable ground-state cooling. We list further possibilities for improvement of about four orders of magnitude, relating to the cavity parameters, in Appendix
E
. In addition, different levitator geometries, e.g. microrings, can be beneficial to enhance the coupled flux
Navau et al. 2021
. The presented coupling can also be used to couple the levitated object to superconducting qubit circuits
Il’ichev and Greenberg 2007
;
Bal et al. 2012
;
Toida et al. 2023
, enabling the implementation of quantum sensing methods for nonclassical state preparation
Romero-Isart et al. 2012
;
Streltsov et al. 2021
.

Conclusions

In summary, we have implemented and characterized a cavity-based scheme to read out the motion of a magnetically levitated, superconducting microsphere using a superconducting, flux-tunable microwave resonator. Our experiment successfully demonstrates
in situ
control over the electromechanical coupling strength via the trap strength and the cavity bias field. The imprecision noise in our setup is presently limited by signal loss to the cryogenic microwave amplifier, which has a noise temperature of
2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, and by imperfections in the room temperature electronics used at present.
These findings allow to describe a clear path towards the quantum regime of mechanical motion for microgram-scale masses.

The data of this study are available at the Zenodo repository
Schmidt et al. 2024
.

Experimental details

Coupling details

The experiment is depicted in Fig.
5
and
1
of the main text. A
m\ \approx\ $6\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro g}$
lead-tin sphere of radius
R=$50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
is levitated in a magnetic field gradient
b_{i}
with
i\in x,y,z
the coordinates of the trap. For simplicity, we focus on the vertical axis, parallel to the gravity axis, hence
i=z\rightarrow\
b_{\mathrm{z}}
,
\Omega_{\textrm{m}}^{z}
, etc. The other modes can be described analogously. The sphere acts as a linear oscillator with frequency
\Omega_{\textrm{m}}^{z}
and displacement
\hat{z}=\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}^{z}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b})
, described semi-classically by the phonon creation operator
\hat{b}^{\dagger}
, phonon annihilation operator
\hat{b}
, and the zero-point motion
\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}^{z}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\Omega_{\textrm{m}}^{z})}
. The motion of the sphere
\delta z
along the
z
-axis changes the flux within the superconducting PUL
\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{pul}}^{z}=\beta b_{\mathrm{z}}R^{2}\delta z
. Here,
\beta
describes how effectively the motion is coupled into the loop. As the flux within the transformer remains, screening currents are generated to counter
\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{pul}}
. The currents’ strengths depend on the inductances of the loop, consisting of the detecting PUL inductance
L_{\mathrm{pul}}
, the accumulated inductances to guide the current to the detector
L_{\mathrm{t}}
, and an input coil at the detector
L_{\mathrm{i}}
, mounted on top of it in a flip-chip configuration. A fraction of these induce a flux
\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}^{z}
in the SQUID loop of the microwave cavity. The loop efficiency
\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}/\Phi_{\mathrm{pul}}
is denoted by
\alpha=M/(L_{\mathrm{pul}}+L_{\mathrm{i}}+L_{\mathrm{t}})
, independent of the motion axis. Here,
M
describes the mutual inductance between flip chip coil and SQUID loop
M=\nu\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{i}}L_{\mathrm{s}}}
with the input coupling coefficient
\nu
. Thus, a mechanical motion of the sphere with amplitude
\delta z
induces a flux in the SQUID loop of the electric resonator as
\alpha\beta b_{\mathrm{z}}R^{2}\delta z
. Experimentally, the oscillating sphere will give rise to an rms flux noise peak which can be related to its phonon number
n_{\mathrm{m}}=\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}\rangle
:

\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}^{z}\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}=\alpha\beta b_{\mathrm{z}}R^{2}\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}^{z}\sqrt{(2n_{\mathrm{m}}+1)}.

(4)

The flux induced in the SQUID will shift the resonance frequency of the flux-tunable cavity by

\delta\omega=\frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\Phi}\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}^{z}.

(5)

In leading order this gives rise to an electromechanical coupling as
\omega_{\mathrm{r}}(\hat{z})=\left(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}-G\bar{z}\right)
, where
G
=
\partial\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial z
. Thus the interaction Hamiltonian becomes:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=-\hbar g_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}\right).

(6)

The vacuum coupling strength
g_{0}=G\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}
is derived to be:

g_{0}=\frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\Phi}\frac{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}}{\partial z}\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\Phi}\alpha\beta b_{\mathrm{z}}R^{2}\zeta_{\mathrm{zpf}}.

(7)

Microchip fabrication

Flux-tunable microwave cavity

The cavity fabrication begins by using a commercial high resistivity silicon wafer (
>$10\text{\,}\mathrm{k\SIUnitSymbolOhm}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
), which is diced into 20
\times$20\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
batches. After wet cleaning, a double layer resist is spin coated on top. The whole electronic structure, consisting of coupling capacitance, coplanar waveguide and Josephson junctions, is then patterned using electron beam lithography. Then the batch is developed and two aluminum layers of thickness
30\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and
60\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
are grown using shadow-angle electron beam evaporation. Before the evaporation, a mild ion etching is applied and in between the evaporations, the first layer is oxidized for
5\text{\,}\mathrm{min}
at an oxygen pressure of
5\text{\,}\mathrm{mbar}
, generating the Josephson tunneling barrier. Then a lift-off process is applied and the batch is finally diced into 5
\times$2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
-sized individual cavity chips.

Gradiometric pick-up loop

We use a high resistivity silicon two inch waver and sputter
300\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
of niobium on top. Using optical lithography with a mask-less aligner and SF
6
etching, the gradiometric pick-up loops are patterned into the niobium. Finally, the structures are diced into 45
\times$5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
individual chips.

Flip chip coil

For the flip chip coil, the first step is the same as for the pick-up loop except the thickness of the niobium is
75\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
. Using optical lithography with a mask-less aligner and SF
6
etching, the multiturn coil and the supply lines with one disconnection to the middle of the coil are patterned into the niobium film. We use Plasma Enhanced Chemical Vapor Deposition (PECVD) to coat
150\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
of Si
3
N
4
as dielectric and a second niobium layer of
180\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
to connect the middle of the coil to the supply line. For both steps lift off is used for structuring.

Room temperature electronics

The room temperature setup forms a homodyne interferometer in the microwave domain using an IQ-mixer for down-conversion. It is detailed in Fig.
5
d. In particular, a microwave signal generator MW acts as photon source. The majority of the signal (
99\%
) enters the local oscillator (LO) arm that is matched in length with the signal path as the LO input of the mixer, which requires a strong and fixed power bias. An additional phase shifter (
\varphi
) enables fine tuning between the incoming signals at the IQ-mixer to rotate the I and Q quadratures if needed. The signal arm has a switchable attenuator (R), which we use to set the probing photon number. A splitter couples incoming tones from a VNA in. The signal is then sent to the input line of the cryostat
S_{\mathrm{in}}
. The outcoming signal from the cryostat
S_{\mathrm{out}}
is amplified and split 99:1 with the minority being sent to the VNA for a direct measurement of the microwave cavity. The majority is further amplified and downconverted in the IQ-mixer. The two quadratures (I and Q) are amplified and split equally between an analog digital converter (ADC) and field programmable gate array (FPGA) used for cavity locking to ensure a constant flux sensitivity of it. The lock consisted of two proportional integral derivative (PID) controllers, where one locked slow, strong drifts (
\approx$1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
) and a faster one that we regulated up to (
\approx$40\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
), such that the mechanical signal remained in the measured signal (detected at the ADC). The lock signals were sent to the bias coil of the FTR (slow) and the feedback signal of the trap (fast), see Fig.
5
a. In addition, a calibration tone was sent to the calibration coil to determine the flux (
S_{{\Phi\Phi}}
) hence the frequency fluctuations (
S_{\omega\omega}
) of the microwave cavity.

Appendix B
Details on calibration / from time domain to spectral densities

Motion

As detailed in Ref.
Hofer et al. 2023
, we record the motion of the microsphere as a shadow image with a frame rate of
300\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
(or above). The frames are analyzed in terms of the position, sphere radius, and relative position to the PUL in pixels. Next the position data is converted into meters using the sphere’s radius of
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, returning
\textbf{x}(t)
. We convert the time trace into a power spectrum (PS) using the Welch-Algorithm with a Hamming window and an effective noise bandwidth (ENBW) of around
0.09\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
. The PS allows us to extract the mechanical frequencies (cf. Fig.
1
e) of each mode and its corresponding variance of the mechanical displacement (
\langle x_{i}\rangle^{2}=
PS(
\Omega_{i}
)).

Flux calibration

For the flux calibration of the calibration coil we first determine the voltage to flux ratio of the external bias coil (
V_{\mathrm{ext}}
), as picked up by the readout circuit. We find a flux periodicity of
467\pm\pm 1\text{\,}\mathrm{mV}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}
. We then flux lock the microwave cavity, sweep a static voltage at the calibration coil while recording the DC-shift of the flux lock through the external bias coil. We find a relation of
21.7\pm\pm 0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{mV}
of countering voltage on
V_{\mathrm{ext}}
per applied Volt on the calibration coil. In combination with the calibration of the external coil we determine an induced flux of
46.5\pm\pm 0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
per applied Volt on
V_{\mathrm{cb}}
.

Frequency fluctuations

The microwave cavity is probed on resonance, homodyne downconverted using an IQ-mixer, fed into a digitizer, and recorded with a bandwidth of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
. For this mixing process we have matched the LO and rf paths of the microwave interferometer, however a finite rotation of I and Q depending on the probe frequency remained. To ensure to record the phase quadrature (thus the mechanical motion), we apply a quasi heterodyne mixing in post processing to the I and Q channels (at
400\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
). The unwrapped phase of this mixing then contains the full data of the mechanical signal and the calibration tone of the calibration coil. Similar to the video data, we transform the recorded voltage trace via Welch’s method with a ’Hamming’ window of
1/10
of the full data length into a power spectral density (PSD in
\mathrm{V}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}
) and PS in
\mathrm{V}^{2}

for a recording of
120\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
, where
\mathrm{PSD}=\mathrm{PS}/\mathrm{ENBW}
and for an oscillation of

\langle x_{i}(t)\rangle^{2}=\mathrm{PS}(\Omega_{i})

Heinzel et al. 2002
.
We extract the peak heights of the PS trace for the individual peaks at

(

x
,
y
,

motion and calibration coil), corresponding to the detected mean value

Another important aspect of the electric resonator under scrutiny is its inherent nonlinearity. In contrast to linear cavity opto- or electromechanical systems, the radiation pressure cannot be increased arbitrarily by the number of read-out photons. Appendix D
Electromechanical conversion

Pick-up loop efficiency

The induced flux of the particle is transferred to the SQUID with an efficiency
\alpha=\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}/\Phi_{\mathrm{pul}}=M/(L_{\mathrm{pul}}+L_{\mathrm{i}}+L_{\mathrm{t}})
. The individual coil inductances are
L_{\mathrm{pul}}=$0.9\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}$
,
L_{\mathrm{i}}\approx$20\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}$
, and
L_{\mathrm{t}}\approx$100\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}$
. The dominant inductance thus is the transfer inductance between the coils, which also has the highest uncertainty as it is based on precharacterizations but may change due to the mounting geometry
Hofer et al. 2023
. The mutual inductance of the SQUID and the input coil depends on the alignment of the flip chip, quantified by the input coupling coefficient
\nu
as
M=\nu\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{i}}L_{\mathrm{s}}}
with the SQUID loop inductance of
L_{\mathrm{s}}=$0.12\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}$
. We estimated the alignment at room temperature and believe drifts when cooling down to be negligible, in comparison to the dimension of the coil with a diameter of
200\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
. For the alignment we reached, a numerical simulation yields a field factor of
\nu\approx 0.1
, hence we find
M\approx$160\text{\,}\mathrm{pH}$
. Overall the flux transfer efficiency is estimated to be
\alpha=1.3\cdot 10^{-3}
.

Dependence of sensitivities on pickup position

We have determined a flux sensitivity of each mode to be
\partial\Phi/(\partial i)=(70,800,80)\,\Phi_{0}/\mathrm{m}
at a trap gradient vector of
b_{\mathrm{i}}=(39,40,80)\,\mathrm{T}/\mathrm{m}
. As the induced flux of the particle’s motion scales with the applied gradient, the z-mode is expected to couple two times more strongly than the horizontal modes. However, we find that the sensitivity to particle motion along the y-direction is around an order of magnitude higher than the sensitivities to the particle motion in the other modes. This behavior arises from different sensitivities of the PUL to the different modes described by
\beta_{i}
. This sensitivity depends on the PUL to particle displacement and the weak sensitivity to the z-mode indicates that the relative displacement of the PUL and the trap centre is such that
\beta_{z}
is close to a minimum.

The employed model of the flux coupling was used to calculate the improvements of the coupling at optimal positioning, which we find to be

Characterization of the detection efficiency

We model the total detection efficiency to arise from the following three contributions, which are depicted in Fig.
12
: The photon efficiency of the microwave cavity
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{cav}}
(blue), the effective detection of the first employed amplifier
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{cold}}

\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{warm}}
(orange), thus
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}=\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{cav}}\cdot\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{cold}}\cdot\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{warm}}

12
we indicate losses as photons exiting the system and added noise by incoming photons.

One of the photon losses is described by the cavity efficiency, which relates how many photons leave to the environment as internal loss and not to the signal line. It is quantified by

Schliesser et al. 2008

The noise from the remaining detection path relates to the efficiency of the amplifier cascade, which can be determined by Friis’ formula as

T_{\mathrm{tot}}=T_{1}+\frac{T_{2}}{G_{1}}+\frac{T_{3}}{G_{1}G_{2}}+\cdots,

(11)

where each amplifier at position
i
in the cascade contributes via its noise temperature
T_{i}
and gain
G_{i}
. Losses beyond the first amplifier are considered negligible in comparison to the gain. Perfect detection
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}=1
would require the amplifier chain temperature to be
T_{\mathrm{tot}}=0
. Hence, the first amplifier having a low noise and high amplification is crucial for the overall performance. Most recent generations of quantum limited amplifiers and cryogenic HEMTs enable detection efficiencies of up to
\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{cold}}=0.81
, given gain values of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
and a HEMT performance of
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
.
Winkel et al. 2020
;
Renger et al. 2021

5

T_{1}=$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
and
G_{1}=$42\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}$
. In addition to this noise temperature relating to
n_{\mathrm{HEMT}}
in Fig.
12
, any loss on the way to the amplifier has to be taken into account (
n_{\mathrm{cold}}
). Besides careful design using superconducting microwave cables, the distance and used components between cavity and amplifier can cause significant losses, which are challenging to quantify in the cryostat. Therefore, we first want to address the accessible room temperature part of the amplifier chain.

It was designed to add less than
1\%

\eta_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{warm}}\approx 0.99
. However, by analyzing the dark noise of the amplifier chain we found an increase of the current noise by improper amplification of
\times 75

Determination of the detector dark noise

Figure 13:
Measuring the voltage spectral noise density along the microwave amplification chain at the detector is essential for characterizing system performance. Upon conducting data analysis, we identified a significant discrepancy between the expected noise level of our amplification chain and the measured noise. To investigate the origin of this discrepancy, we performed a dark-noise measurement. Initially, all amplifiers and input signals were turned off. We conducted an initial measurement by unplugging the cables from our detector and performed a typical time trace measurement. We then determined the PSD
S_{{\mathrm{VV}}}
, which yielded an average value of approximately
S_{{\mathrm{VV}}}^{(1)}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{-11}\text{\,}{\mathrm{V}}^{2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Hz}}^{-1}$
.

Subsequently, we systematically powered on the devices from the detector up to the device under test (DUT). Fig.
13
a illustrates the sequence of measurements. The results are presented in Fig.
13
b. The initial noise floor remained low until a notable increase in measurement 7. This observation suggests the source of the higher noise floor to stem from the third or second amplifier in the chain. Indeed, further investigation revealed that the third amplifier is not suitable for measurements within our frequency range, as it is specified to operate above 5 GHz rather than the applied 4.4 GHz. Comparing the final (full chain, no signal tone, measurement 8) and the initial noise level of the warm part (that is measurement 1), we find

Optimizing the system

To highlight the capabilities of the device, we will briefly discuss potential future improvements of the presented platform. Key quantity for an optimized design to achieve ground state cooling is the quantum cooperativity, which can be written in terms of platform parameters as:

C_{\mathrm{q}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\mu_{\mathrm{0}}}{2\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}}\frac{b_{\mathrm{trap}}I_{\mathrm{trap}}\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}Q_{\textrm{m}}R}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{th}}\kappa}\left(2\pi\hbar\textit{f}_{\Phi}\alpha\beta\right)^{2},

(13)

where we introduced the temperature of the environmental bath surrounding the particle. In the optimization discussion, we will focus on electric design while keeping the trap and mechanical element unchanged. In particular, we keep the experimental

given in table
1
, and assume the mode to thermalize at
T_{\mathrm{th}}=$15\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
, cf. Ref.
Hofer et al. 2023
for details. We will focus on the mode along the trap axis (
z
-direction). Potential improvements are discussed in the main text and the appendices and are summarized in the following:

In our measurements we found the cavity to decrease the imprecision level linearly with the readout power up to
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=10

200

4

C

D. Zoepfl et al. 2023

Guiding the flux to the SQUID loop is another important aspect that affects the system performance. This process is characterized by the scaling factor
