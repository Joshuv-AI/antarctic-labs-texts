Signal-Plus-Noise Decomposition of Nonlinear Spiked Random Matrix Models
Behrad Moniri and Hamed Hassani

Abstract
In this paper, we study a nonlinear spiked random matrix model where a nonlinear function is applied element-wise to a noise matrix perturbed by a rank-one signal. We establish a signal-plus-noise decomposition for this model and identify precise phase transitions in the structure of the signal components at critical thresholds of signal strength. To demonstrate the applicability of this decomposition, we then utilize it to study new phenomena in the problems of signed signal recovery in nonlinear models and community detection in transformed stochastic block models. Finally, we validate our results through a series of numerical simulations.



1
Introduction

Spiked Random Matrix models, where a low-rank signal is added to a large-dimensional noise matrix, have been studied extensively in recent years. Among many applications, these models have successfully been used for a theoretical study of spectral methods for signal recovery and detection (see, e.g.,
[
Joh01
,
BAP05
,
EAK18
,
PWBM18
,
BDM+18
,
EAKJ20
,
GKKZ22
,
PKK24
]
, etc.), and community detection in stochastic block models (see, e.g.,
[
Abb18
,
HLY23
,
LL23
,
McS01
]
, etc.). In these models, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is assumed that we observe

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{W}+\lambda{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top},

(1)

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a noise matrix,
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the unit norm signal, and
\lambda>0
is the signal strength.

Given the noisy observation
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
, the top eigenvector of
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
is used as an estimate of
{\bm{x}}
. This algorithm is based on the fact that the spectrum of
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
undergoes a sharp phase transition when varying
\lambda
: below a critical threshold of the signal strength, there is no alignment between the signal
{\bm{x}}
and the top eigenvector, and the top eigenvector is not useful for signal recovery. However, beyond this threshold, the top eigenvector exhibits a non-trivial alignment with the signal, enabling signal recovery. See Section
2
for more details.

In this paper we study an element-wise transformation of this model, which we call a
nonlinear spiked random matrix model
. We define

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f\left(\mathbf{W}+\lambda\sqrt{n}{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\right),

(2)

where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a nonlinear function applied element-wise. This is a natural extension of spiked random matrix models. The function
f
can model a nonlinear sensing mechanism or a function applied to truncate, censor, or normalize the data.

We study such random matrix models in the regime where the size
n\to\infty
, and
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\rm op}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{n})
and
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\overset{P}{\to}1
. We consider a signal strength that can potentially grow with
n
, given by

\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
where
c_{\lambda}>0
and
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
and characterize the spectral properties of this model and the problem of estimating
{\bm{x}}
given the nonlinear noisy observation
\mathbf{Y}
.

1.1
Examplar Applications

Transformed Stochastic Block Models.

Assume we have a stochastic block model with
n\in\mathbb{N}
nodes, where each node belongs to one of two communities, labeled
-1
or
1
. Let the vector
{\bm{u}}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
represent the community membership of each node. We consider a weighted, fully connected graph where the weights of edges between nodes within the same community are drawn from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
with mean
\gamma
, and the weights of edges between nodes in different communities are drawn from the distribution
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
with mean
\bar{\gamma}
. Due to censoring, preprocessing, or the use of a nonlinear measuring device, instead of observing the weight matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we observe an element-wise transform of it,
f(\mathbf{A})
, for a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Assuming that
\gamma+\bar{\gamma}=0
, the expected value of the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}=\frac{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}{2}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}
, and the observed transformed adjacency matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f\left((\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A})+\frac{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}{2}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}\right),

which is in the form of nonlinear spiked random matrix models of equation
2
with
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}
,
{\bm{x}}={\bm{u}}/\sqrt{n}
, and
\lambda=\sqrt{n}(\gamma-\bar{\gamma})/2
. In this problem, the goal is to recover the community membership information in
{\bm{u}}
from the observation
f(\mathbf{A})/\sqrt{n}
. We will study this problem in detail in Section
4.2
.

Signed Signal Recovery.

Recovering the signal
{\bm{x}}
given the nonlinear observation of equation
2
can be significantly more challenging than recovering
{\bm{x}}
given the linear observation in equation
1
. For instance, consider a setting where
f
is an even function. Due to the symmetries in the problem, it becomes impossible to recover the sign of the vector
{\bm{x}}
, regardless of how large the signal strength is. For a general function
f
, we show that there can be a gap in the signal strength required for signed and unsigned signal recovery and signed signal recovery can require an asymptotically larger signal strength. We will study this problem in detail in Section
4.1
.

Other Examples.

Beyond the problems discussed above, the model in equation
2
appears naturally in other contexts as well. For example,
[
BES+22
,
MLHD23
,
CPD+24
]
study a two-layer neural network and demonstrate that the first layer weights, after the initial training step, can be written as a spiked random matrix and the feature matrix of the two-layer neural network conforms to the form in equation
2
, with the step-size acting as the signal strength. Also, the theoretical model for unsigned low-rank matrix completion analyzed in
[
YPT21
]
is a special case of such nonlinear random matrix models with
f(x)=|x|
.

1.2
Contributions

•

In Section
3
, we derive a novel signal-plus-noise decomposition for
\mathbf{Y}
and show that
\mathbf{Y}
can be approximated in operator norm by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{W})
(i.e., noise) plus several terms depending on
{\bm{x}}
(i.e., signal). The number and structure of the signal components in this expansion depend on the power
\alpha
and undergo a series of sharp phase transitions as
\alpha
varies. Specifically, we show that as
\alpha
increases from
0
to
1/2
, new higher degree signal components emerge at critical values
\alpha_{\ell}=\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

In Section
4.1
, we use this decomposition to study the signed signal recovery problem and characterize the precise signal strength required to recover the signal
{\bm{x}}
given
\mathbf{Y}
. We show that recovering the sign can require an asymptotically larger signal strength compared to the signal strength required for recovering
{\bm{x}}
up to ambiguity in its sign. We thoroughly analyze this gap and study under what conditions such a gap exists.

•

In Section
4.2
, we study the transformed stochastic block model problem. We fully characterize the phase transitions for weak recovery of community membership of nodes as a function of
\gamma-\bar{\gamma}
. Specifically, we examine how the signal strength required for recovering the community structure depends on the function
f
and the distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
.

•

In Section
5
, we confirm our theoretical findings through various numerical simulations.

Prior Works
on Nonlinear Spiked Random Matrix Models

Spectrum of random matrices in the form of equation
2
have been studied extensively when
\lambda=0
(see e.g.,
[
EK10b
,
EK10a
,
CBG16
,
PW17
,
Péc19
,
WES+22
]
, etc.) and has found many applications in the analysis of the performance of two-layer neural network with first layer fixed at initialization, and the second layer trained using ridge regression, i.e., random features models (see e.g.,
[
LLC18
,
GLR+22
,
MM22
,
LD21
,
ALP22
,
AP20
,
TAP21
,
LMH+23
,
LGC+21
]
, etc.).

For the linear model of equation
1
, when
\mathbf{W}
has i.i.d. non-Gaussian entries (up to symmetric constraints),
[
LKZ15
,
PWBM18
]
show that there exists a nonlinear element-wise transformation of
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
that exploits this non-Gaussianity and can improve the performance of signal recovery and detection using the top eigenvector. However, they assume i.i.d. entries, only study the problem in the setting where
\lambda=\Theta(1)
(i.e.,
\alpha=0
), and only for a specific nonlinear function
f
.

The case where
\mathbf{W}
is a symmetric matrix with i.i.d. Gaussian entries has been studied for a general function
f
in
[
GKK+23
,
Fel23
]
in a signal strength regime where the spectrum of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Y}
consists of a bulk of eigenvalues that stick together and only one outlier (spike). In the deep learning theory literature, and in the context of feature learning,
[
MLHD23
]
studies nonlinear spiked random matrix models in the case where
\mathbf{W}
that is the product of a symmetric random matrix with i.i.d. Gaussian entries and an independent matrix with rows drawn uniformly from the unit sphere.

In applications such as transformed stochastic block models, the entries of the noise matrix are not identically distributed and the signal-plus-noise decompositions from prior work do not hold. Also, for signed signal recovery, the natural scaling of the signal strength should lead to several outlying eigenvalues in the spectrum and again it cannot be handled by prior work.

In this paper, we provide a general purpose signal-plus-noise decomposition that does not assume Gaussianity, or identical distribution of the noise matrix entries. The signal-plus-noise decomposition of Theorem
3.3
has a novel structure that shows how the distribution of the entries of
\mathbf{W}
and the nonlinear function
f
interact and affect the spectrum of
\mathbf{Y}
. Also, we provide our results for a signal strength
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
and
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. This enables us to study problems in regimes where there are multiple outlying eigenvalues which is essential for the analysis of signed signal recovery.

2
Review of Spiked Wigner Models

In this section, we will review the classical results for the (linear)
spiked Wigner model. First, recall the definition of Wigner random matrices.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric random matrix. We call
\mathbf{W}
a Wigner matrix and write
\mathbf{W}\sim\mathrm{Wig}_{n}(\mathcal{D})
if its elements are independent up to symmetric constraints and are identically distributed according to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
which is assumed to have bounded fifth moment.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution on
\mathbb{R}
with mean zero and variance
\sigma_{w}^{2}
. In the spiked random matrix of equation
1
, let
\mathbf{W}\sim\mathrm{Wig}_{n}(\mathcal{D})
, and assume that
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}1
. It is known that with probability one, the empirical eigenvalue distribution of
\mathbf{W}/\sqrt{n}
will converge to the semi-circle law which is supported on
[-2\sigma_{w},2\sigma_{w}]
. See
[
BS10
, Theorem 2.5]
. The spectrum of
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
has been studied extensively in the literature (see e.g.,
[
Péc06
,
BGGM11
,
BGN11
,
CDMF12
,
BV13
,
PRS13
]
, etc.). It is known that when
\lambda<\sigma_{w}
, the top eigenvalue is again
2\sigma_{w}
and its corresponding eigenvector will not be correlated with
{\bm{x}}
. If
\lambda>\sigma_{w}
, the top eigenvalue will be isolated and larger than
2\sigma_{w}
and the corresponding eigenvector to the top eigenvalue will have a non-trivial alignment with
{\bm{x}}
. The following classic theorem, often called the BBP phase transition, formalizes this phase transition of the leading eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
. See e.g.,
[
PRS13
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 2.2

(BBP Phase Transition)
.

Let
\lambda_{1},{\bm{u}}_{1}
be the leading eigenvalue and its corresponding eigenvector of the matrix
\mathbf{Y}_{\rm Lin}
. Assuming that
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}1
, if
\lambda<\sigma_{w}
, we have:

•

If
\lambda_{1}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}2\sigma_{w}
, and
\langle{\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{x}}\rangle^{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}0
.

•

If
\lambda>\sigma_{w}
, we have
\lambda_{1}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\lambda+\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\lambda}
, and
\langle{\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{x}}\rangle^{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}1-\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}
.

3
Signal-Plus-Noise Decomposition

In this section, we will provide a signal plus noise decomposition for the nonlinear spiked random matrix model in different regimes of the signal strength
\lambda
. This decomposition will be the key tool that we will use to characterize the spectrum of
\mathbf{Y}
and to analyze the performance of spectral algorithms applied to it.

Our theoretical analysis applies under the following conditions.

Condition 3.1
.

We make the following assumptions on the signal
{\bm{x}}
and the noise matrix
\mathbf{W}
.

•

The matrix
\mathbf{W}
satisfies
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\rm op}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{n})
and its elements have bounded moments.

•

The
\ell_{2}
-norm of
{\bm{x}}
satisfies
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\rm 2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}1
and the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of
{\bm{x}}
satisfies
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\infty}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\kappa(n)/\sqrt{n}\right)
, where
\kappa(n)
is
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{polylog}(n))
.

•

We assume that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\left\|{\bm{x}}^{\circ k}\right\|_{2}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}({n^{(1-k)/2}})
.

This condition is fairly general and properly normalized subgaussian vectors
{\bm{x}}
and properly normalized matrices
\mathbf{W}
with independent subgaussian entries satisfy these conditions (see e.g.,
[
Ver18
, Chapter 3 and Chapter 4]
). The conditions on
{\bm{x}}
hold also when the entries of
{\bm{x}}
are i.i.d. sub-Weibull random variables (see e.g.,
[
VGNA20
,
Sam23
]
, etc.). We also make the following assumption of the function
f
.

Condition 3.2

(Polynomial Bound)
.

We assume that the function
f
is infinitely differentiable almost everywhere, and there exists
C_{f}\in\mathbb{R}
and
m_{f}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle|f^{(k)}(x)|\leq C_{f}+x^{m_{f}},\quad\text{for all }\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

whenever
f^{(k)}(x)
exists.

Note that, for example, any polynomial with a finite degree satisfies this condition. Under these conditions, we can state the main theorem of the paper.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. If Conditions
3.1
–
3.2
hold, we have
\|\mathbf{Y}-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{W})+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\mathbf{H}_{k},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{H}_{k}=\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}}{k!}\left(\mathbb{E}f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right),

where the function
f
and its derivatives are applied element-wise.

The proof of this theorem can be found in Section
A.1
. This theorem shows that if the signal strength grows as
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
, the matrix
\mathbf{Y}
can be approximated in operator norm by the matrix
f(\mathbf{W})/\sqrt{n}
plus the sum of
\ell
-many terms that contain information about the signal
{\bm{x}}
. The rank of each of these terms
\mathbf{H}_{k}
can be upper bounded as
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{H}_{k})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{E}f^{(i)}(\mathbf{W}))
.

Working directly with the nonlinear model in equation
2
is difficult because of the existence of interactions between the signal
{\bm{x}}
and the noise
\mathbf{W}
through the nonlinearity
f
. Theorem
3.3
shows that in fact the model is equivalent to a spiked matrix where the noise and signal terms are disentangled. To demonstrate the applicability of this decomposition, in the next section we will apply it to two natural nonlinear signal recovery problems.

4
Applications

4.1
Signed Signal Recovery

In this section, we consider a general function
f
and use the decomposition of Theorem
3.3
to study the problem of
signed
signal recovery described in Section
1.1
.

We first derive a signal-plus-noise decomposition of the nonlinear spiked matrix model in equation
2
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\sim\mathrm{Wig}(\mathcal{D})
and denote
\mu_{f^{(k)}}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}f^{(k)}(Z)
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Let
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. If Conditions
3.1
–
3.2
hold, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{\Lambda}+\frac{\mu_{f^{(0)}}}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}\mu_{f^{(k)}}}{k!}\,{\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}+\mathbf{\Delta},

where
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(f(\mathbf{W})-\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{W})\right)
is a mean-zero Wigner matrix, and
\|\mathbf{\Delta}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

The proof for this theorem can be found in Section
A.2
. This proposition states that when
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
, the spectrum of the matrix
\mathbf{Y}
will consist of the bulk spectrum resulting from
\mathbf{\Lambda}
, and up to
\ell+1
rank-one spikes. These spikes are aligned to the element-wise powers of the vector
{\bm{x}}
and the number of these spikes is non-decreasing in
\alpha
.
This signal-plus-noise decomposition shows that a spectral algorithm can be used for signal recovery in this problem. Using the assumption on the
\ell_{2}
norm of
{\bm{x}}^{\circ k}
in Condition
3.1
, for the signal strength of each signal component, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}\mu_{f^{(k)}}}{k!}\,{\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right\|_{\rm op}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{k(\alpha-1/2)+1/2}\right).

(3)

Recall that we are assuming
\alpha<1/2
, which mean that for any fixed
\alpha
, terms with higher degree
k
have smaller signal strength.

If at least one odd power of the vector
{\bm{x}}
appears in the expansion of
\mathbf{Y}
, the top-
\ell
eigenspace of the
\mathbf{Y}
will contain information about
{\bm{x}}
and a spectral algorithm can be used to recover it. However, if the rank-one terms in the expansion of
\mathbf{Y}
only consist of even powers of
{\bm{x}}
, the top-
\ell
eigenspace of
\mathbf{Y}
will lose all information on the sign of
{\bm{x}}
.

To focus solely on the problem of sign recovery in nonlinear matrix models, we make the following assumption on the vector
{\bm{x}}
.

Condition 4.2
.

We assume that the signal vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
{\bm{x}}=\boldsymbol{\zeta}/\sqrt{n}\quad\text{ with }\quad\boldsymbol{\zeta}=[\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\text{ where }\quad\zeta_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Rademacher}(1/2).

This vector satisfies the conditions in Proposition
4.1
. Note that, the vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\circ k}
equals
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
if
k
is odd and equals to
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if
k
is even. Thus, using Proposition
4.1
, assuming that
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{\Lambda}+\left(\sum_{k=1,k\text{ odd}}^{\ell}\frac{c_{\lambda}^{k}n^{(\alpha-1/2)k}\mu_{f^{(k)}}}{k!\sqrt{n}}\right)\boldsymbol{\zeta}\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\top}+\left(\sum_{k=0,k\text{ even}}^{\ell}\frac{c_{\lambda}^{k}n^{(\alpha-1/2)k}\mu_{f^{(k)}}}{k!\sqrt{n}}\right)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{\Delta},

(4)

with
\|\mathbf{\Delta}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. To analyze the performance of spectral algorithms for signal recovery, we define the even-index and the odd-index of a function
f
and distribution
\mathcal{D}
as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

Given a distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
\mathbb{R}
and a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the even-index and the odd-index, denoted by
I_{e}
and
I_{o}
respectively, as

\displaystyle I_{e}(f;\mathcal{D}):=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\text{ and }k\text{ even}\;|\;\mu_{f^{(k)}}\neq 0\},\,\text{ and }

\displaystyle I_{o}(f;\mathcal{D}):=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }k\text{ odd}\;|\;\mu_{f^{(k)}}\neq 0\},

where
\mu_{f^{(k)}}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}f^{(k)}(Z)
. We will drop the argument
(f;\mathcal{D})
when it is clear from context.

In the case where
I_{o}>I_{e}
, the top eigenvalue will corresponds to the eigenvector
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If the signal strength is large enough, the second top eigenvector of
\mathbf{Y}
will be aligned to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
and can be used to recover it. This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\sim\mathrm{Wig}(\mathcal{D})
and
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha,c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that Conditions
3.1
,
3.2
and
4.2
hold and
I_{o}>I_{e}
. Define

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{c_{\lambda}^{I_{o}}}{I_{o}!}\left(\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}f^{(I_{o})}(Z)\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\sigma_{f}=\mathrm{SD}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}(f(Z)).

Then, the second top eigenvalue and eigenvector pair of
\mathbf{Y}
,
(\gamma_{2},{\bm{u}}_{2})
, satisfies the following.

(a)

If
\alpha<\frac{I_{o}-1}{2I_{o}}
, we have
\gamma_{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}2\sigma_{f}
and
\frac{|\langle{\bm{u}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle|}{\|{\bm{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}0
.

(b)

If
\alpha=\frac{I_{o}-1}{2I_{o}}
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\begin{cases}2\sigma_{f}&\kappa<\sigma_{f}\\
\kappa+\frac{\sigma_{f}^{2}}{\kappa}&\kappa>\sigma_{f}\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{|\langle{\bm{u}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle|}{\|{\bm{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\begin{cases}0&\kappa<\sigma_{f}\\
\sqrt{1-\frac{\sigma_{f}^{2}}{\kappa_{2}^{2}}}&\kappa>\sigma_{f}\end{cases}

(c)

If
\alpha>\frac{I_{o}-1}{2I_{o}}
, we have
\gamma_{2}=\omega_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and
\frac{|\langle{\bm{u}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle|}{\|{\bm{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}1
.

When
I_{e}<I_{o}
, if the signal strength is large enough, the top eigenvector will be aligned to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let conditions of Theorem
4.4
hold, but with
I_{o}<I_{e}
. Let

{\bm{u}}_{2})
.

4.2
Transformed Stochastic Block Model

In this section, we will use the signal-plus-noise decomposition
of Theorem
3.3
for community detection in transformed stochastic block models described in Section
1.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}_{n}=([n],\mathcal{E}_{n})
with
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{(i,j)\;|\;i,j\in[n],i\neq j\}
. Assume that the vertices in this graph exhibit a community structure and they can be grouped into two non-overlapping communities
\mathcal{V}_{+}=\{1,\cdots,\beta n\}
and
\mathcal{V}_{-}=\{\beta n+1,\cdots,n\}
for some
0<\beta<1
. Assume that the edges of this graph are weighted and define
A_{ij}
as the weight of the edge between distinct vertices
i,j\in[n]
that we further assume are drawn independently according to

\displaystyle A_{ij}=A_{ji}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}&i,j\in\mathcal{V}_{+}\quad\text{or}\quad i,j\in\mathcal{V}_{-}\\
\bar{\mathcal{D}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathcal{D}
are two distributions over
\mathbb{R}
. We define
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to be the adjacency matrix with entries
[\mathbf{A}]_{i,j}=A_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[n]
. Variants of the problem of weighted stochastic block have been studied in
[
JL15
,
LL18
,
XJL20
]
. Recently,
[
LL23
]
studied the problem of weak recovery in a two community weighted stochastic block model. See also
[
JCL23
,
HLY23
]
. The problem of exact recovery in Gaussian weighted stochastic block models was also studied in
[
PK24
]
.

Here we consider a transformed version of this problem where we only have access to
f(\mathbf{A})
where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known function applied element-wise to
\mathbf{A}
. The goal is to recover the community membership
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}
using the observation
f(\mathbf{A})
. Our results on transformed stochastic block models depend on the following quantities:

\displaystyle\gamma=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}Z,\quad\bar{\gamma}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\bar{\mathcal{D}}}Z,\quad\gamma_{f^{(k)}}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}f^{(k)}(Z-\gamma),\quad\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(k)}}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\bar{\mathcal{D}}}f^{(k)}(Z-\bar{\gamma}),

\displaystyle\sigma=\mathrm{SD}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}}(f(Z-\gamma)),\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\sigma}=\mathrm{SD}_{Z\sim\bar{\mathcal{D}}}(f(Z-\bar{\gamma})).

(5)

For simplicity, we assume that
\gamma+\bar{\gamma}=0
. The expected value of
\mathbf{A}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}]=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma\,\mathbf{1}_{\beta n\times\beta n}&\bar{\gamma}\,\mathbf{1}_{\beta n\times(1-\beta)n}\\
\bar{\gamma}\,\mathbf{1}_{(1-\beta)n\times\beta n}&\gamma\,\mathbf{1}_{(1-\beta)n\times(1-\beta)n}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}{2}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top},\,\text{in which}\;{\bm{u}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{\beta n}\\[2.84544pt]
-\mathbf{1}_{(1-\beta)n}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
f(\mathbf{A})=f(\bar{\mathbf{A}}+\frac{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}{2}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top})
where
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}
which means that
f(\mathbf{A})
is a nonlinear spiked random matrix model defined in equation
2
with
\mathbf{W}=\bar{\mathbf{A}}
,
{\bm{x}}={\bm{u}}/\sqrt{n}
, and
\lambda=\frac{{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}}{2}\sqrt{n}
. As a result, based on Theorem
3.3
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
{{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}}=2c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha-1/2}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\mathbf{W}:=\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}
satisfies Condition
3.1
and the function
f
satisfies Condition
3.2
, we have
\|\bar{\mathbf{Y}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{A})\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
with

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{Y}}=\mathbf{\Gamma}+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\frac{c_{\lambda}^{k}n^{(\alpha-1/2)k}}{k!\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\gamma_{f^{(k)}}+\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(k)}}}{2}{\bm{u}}^{\circ k}{\bm{u}}^{\circ k\top}+\frac{\gamma_{f^{(k)}}-\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(k)}}}{2}{\bm{u}}^{\circ(k+1)}{\bm{u}}^{\circ(k+1)\top}\right),

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(f(\bar{\mathbf{A}})-\mathbb{E}f(\bar{\mathbf{A}}))
.

The proof can be found in Section
A.5
. To study the phase transitions in the eigenspace of
\mathbf{Y}
, we first need to study the unperturbed component
\mathbf{\Gamma}
. In the following proposition, we apply results on Wigner-type random matrices to characterizes the spectrum of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
in the limit where
n\to\infty
. See Section
B
for an overview of the results on the spectrum of Wigner-type random matrices.

Proposition 4.7
.

Assume that
f(Z-\gamma)
and
f(Z^{\prime}-\bar{\gamma})
with
Z\sim\mathcal{D}
and
Z^{\prime}\sim\bar{\mathcal{D}}
have bounded moments. The Stieltjes transform
m(z)
of the empirical eigenvalue distribution of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
satisfies

\displaystyle m(z)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{\Gamma}\right)^{-1}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\beta\bar{m}_{1}(z)+(1-\beta)\bar{m}_{2}(z),

in which
\bar{m}_{1}(z)
and
\bar{m}_{2}(z)
are the solutions to the following quadratic vector equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}z\bar{m}_{1}(z)+\beta\sigma^{2}\bar{m}_{1}^{2}(z)+(1-\beta)\bar{\sigma}^{2}\bar{m}_{1}(z)\bar{m}_{2}(z)+1=0,\\
z\bar{m}_{2}(z)+\beta\bar{\sigma}^{2}\bar{m}_{1}(z)\bar{m}_{2}(z)+(1-\beta)\sigma^{2}\bar{m}_{2}^{2}(z)+1=0.\end{cases}

The proof can be found in Section
A.6
. This proposition shows that the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
does not carry information about the community structure. For instance, if
\beta=0.5
, we will have
\bar{m}_{1}(z)=\bar{m}_{2}(z)
equal to the Stiltjes transform of a semi-circle distribution.

In order for us to be able to recover the signal
{\bm{u}}
using spectral methods, an odd power of
{\bm{u}}
must appear in the expansion in Proposition
4.6
. To characterize this, we define the signal and the constant index of a function
f
and two distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
as follows.

Definition 4.8
.

Given two distribution
\mathcal{D}
and
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
supported on
\mathbb{R}
and a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the signal index and constant index, denoted by
J_{s}
and
J_{c}
respectively, as

\displaystyle J_{s}(f;\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}):=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\;|\;\gamma_{f^{(k)}}+(-1)^{k+1}\,\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(k)}}\neq 0\},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle J_{c}(f;\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}):=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\;|\;\gamma_{f^{(k)}}+(-1)^{k}\,\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(k)}}\neq 0\}.

We will drop the argument
(f;\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
when it is clear from context.

The signal index
J_{s}
is the first
k
such that an odd power of
{\bm{u}}
has non-zero coefficient in the decomposition of Proposition
4.6
and the constant index
J_{c}
is the the first
k
such that an even power of
{\bm{u}}
has non-zero coefficient.

Community detection is straightforward when
J_{s}=0
. In this case, even if the two communities initially have weights with the same mean
\gamma=\bar{\gamma}
, the difference in the means of the transformed matrix,
\gamma_{f^{(0)}}
and
\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(0)}}
, allows a spectral algorithm to recover the community structure. This illustrates how transformation can aid signal recovery. Therefore, we now assume that
J_{s}\neq 0
. Similar to Section
4.1
, we study signal recovery in two cases:
J_{s}<J_{c}
and
J_{s}>J_{c}
.

In the case where
J_{s}>J_{c}
, the top eigenvalue corresponds to the eigenvector
\mathbf{1}
and the second top eigenvector of
\mathbf{Y}
is aligned to
\boldsymbol{{\bm{u}}}
, if
|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}|
is large enough. This is shown in the following theorem. Here, for simplicity we assume that we have a balanced stochastic block model with each community having the same number of nodes (i.e.,
\beta=1/2
), which enables us to derive closed-form expressions for the critical values of the phase transition. The same technique can be used for the general
\beta
which gives a qualitatively similar result (with phase transitions happening at different critical values).

Theorem 4.9
.

In the stochastic block model defined above, let
{{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}}=2c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha-1/2}
with
\alpha\in[0,1/2),c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that the conditions of Proposition
4.6
hold. Let
(\gamma_{2},{\bm{u}}_{2})
be the second top eigenvalue and eigenvector pair of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{A})
, and define

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{c_{\lambda}^{J_{s}}(\gamma_{f^{(J_{s})}+(-1)^{J_{s}+1}}\bar{\gamma}_{f^{(J_{s})}})}{2J_{s}!}.

(a)

If
\alpha<\frac{J_{s}-1}{2J_{s}}
, we have
\gamma_{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\sqrt{2(\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2})}
and
\frac{|\langle{\bm{u}}_{2},{\bm{u}}\rangle|}{\|{\bm{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}0
.

(b)

If
\alpha=\frac{J_{s}-1}{2J_{s}}
, we have

\displaystyle\hskip-36.98866pt\gamma_{2}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\begin{cases}\sqrt{2(\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2})}&\kappa<\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}}{2}}\\
\kappa+{\frac{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{2\kappa}}&\kappa>\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}}{2}}\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{|\langle{\bm{u}}_{2},{\bm{u}}\rangle|}{\|{\bm{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}\begin{cases}0&\kappa<\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}}{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2}}}&\kappa>\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\sigma}}{2}}\end{cases}

(c)

If
\alpha>\frac{J_{s}-1}{2J_{s}}
, we have
\gamma_{2}=\omega_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and
\frac{|\langle{\bm{u}}_{2},{\bm{u}}\rangle|}{\|{\bm{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{P}}{\to}1
.

When
J_{s}<J_{c}
, if the signal strength is large enough, the top eigenvector will be aligned to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let conditions of Theorem
4.4
hold, but with
J_{s}<J_{c}
.
Let
(\gamma_{1},{\bm{u}}_{1})
be the top eigenvalue and eigenvector pair of
\mathbf{Y}
. Then, Part (a), (b), and (c) of Theorem
4.4
will hold for
\gamma_{1},{\bm{u}}_{1}
.

The proofs of these two theorems can be found in Section
A.7
and Section
A.8
. These theorems show that in order for it to be possible to recover the community membership using spectral methods, the signal strength
\sqrt{n}(\gamma-\bar{\gamma})
should grow
\Theta(n^{\frac{J_{s}-1}{2J_{s}}})
that is
\omega(1)
if
J_{s}>1
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel signal-plus-noise decomposition for a nonlinear spiked random matrix model in which a nonlinear function is applied element-wise to a rank-one perturbation of a random matrix. Through the study of the problems of signed signal recovery and community detection in transformed stochastic block models, we showed that this novel decomposition enables the study of phase transitions in problems that could not be handled by prior works, and reveals interesting phenomena in these models. Nonlinear spiked random matrix model appears naturally in the study of feature learning in shallow neural networks (see e.g.,
[
BES+22
,
MLHD23
,
DKL+23
,
CPD+24
,
WWF24
,
DTA+24
]
, etc.). We leave the potential applications to problems in deep learning theory as future work.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Consider the matrix model defined in equation
2
and let
\lambda=c_{\lambda}n^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[\frac{\ell-1}{2\ell},\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2})
. To analyze this model, we first consider a Taylor approximation of
\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{W}+\lambda\sqrt{n}{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top})
around
\mathbf{W}
; i.e., we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{W})+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}}{k!}\left(f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right)+\mathcal{E},

in which
f^{(k)}
is the
k
-th derivative of
f
and is applied element-wise.

We show that the matrix
\mathcal{E}
will have a negligible operator norm.
Using the Taylor remainder theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{\lambda^{\ell+1}n^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell+1)!}\left(f^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ(\ell+1)}{\bm{x}}^{\circ(\ell+1)\top}\right),

where
|\mathbf{\Delta}|\leq\sqrt{n}\,|\lambda{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}|
element-wise. The reminder matrix
\mathcal{E}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{\lambda^{\ell+1}n^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell+1)!}\Bigg[\text{diag}\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ(\ell+1)}\right)\left(f^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})\right)\text{diag}\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ(\ell+1)}\right)\Bigg].

Hence, the operator norm of the matrix
\mathcal{E}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\lambda|^{\ell+1}n^{\frac{\ell}{2}}}{(\ell+1)!}M_{\bm{x}}^{2\ell+2}\|f^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})\|_{\rm op},

where
M_{\bm{x}}=\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\infty}
.
Also, we can upper bound the operator norm of
f^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})
using the Frobenius norm, to get

\displaystyle\|f^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})\|_{\rm op}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{(\ell+1)}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})\|_{\rm Fr}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f^{(\ell+1)}(W_{ij}+\Delta_{ij})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(C_{f}+(W_{ij}+\Delta_{ij})^{m_{f}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(2C_{f}^{2}+4W_{ij}^{2m_{f}}+4\Delta_{ij}^{2m_{f}}\right),

where in the second inequality, we have used Condition
3.2
. Also,

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij}^{2m_{f}}\leq\lambda^{2m_{f}}n^{m_{f}}M_{\bm{x}}^{4m_{f}}\leq c_{\lambda}^{2m_{f}}n^{(2\alpha-1)m_{f}}\kappa^{4m_{f}}(n)=o(1),

where the last equality holds as long as
\kappa(n)=o(n^{\frac{1-2\alpha}{4}})
.
With this, the expected value of
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}^{2}
can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}^{2}\leq\frac{|\lambda|^{2\ell+2}n^{\ell}}{((\ell+1)!)^{2}}M_{\bm{x}}^{4\ell+4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(2C_{f}^{2}+4\mathbb{E}W_{ij}^{2m_{f}}+o(1)\right).

Using the fact that
\mathbb{E}W_{ij}^{2m_{f}}
is bounded using Condition
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}n^{\frac{\ell+2}{2}}M_{\bm{x}}^{2\ell+2}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ell+\alpha}\kappa^{2\ell+2}(n)\right).

Hence,
\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}=o(1)
because
\alpha<\frac{\ell}{2\ell+2}
.

Then, using Markov’s inequality, we have
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}=o(1)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{W})+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}}{k!}\left(f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right)+\mathcal{E},

with
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Next, we show that the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}}{k!}\left(f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})-\mathbb{E}f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right)

has
\|\mathbf{\Gamma}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. To show this, we write

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\Gamma}\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{|\lambda|^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}}{k!}\left\|\left(f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})-\mathbb{E}f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right)\right\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{|\lambda|^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}M_{\bm{x}}^{2k}}{k!}\left\|f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})-\mathbb{E}f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right\|_{\rm op}.

Also, the matrix
f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})-\mathbb{E}f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})
is a mean zero Wigner-type random matrix and its operator norm is known to be
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{n})
(see e.g.,
[
EM19
, Theorem 2.1]
). A similar order-wise argument as the one used above gives
\|\mathbf{\Gamma}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Hence, putting everything together, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(\mathbf{W})+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{k}n^{(k-1)/2}}{k!}\left(\mathbb{E}f^{(k)}(\mathbf{W})\right)\circ\left({\bm{x}}^{\circ k}{\bm{x}}^{\circ k\top}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{E}},

with
\|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1

When
\mathbf{W}
is a Wigner matrix, for any

Proof of Theorem

(6)

We will prove each case where

Appendix B
Wigner-Type Random Matrices

First, we recall the definition of Wigner-type matrices from the random matrix theory literature (see e.g.,
[
AEK17
,
Erd19
]
, etc.).

Definition B.1

(Wigner-type matrix)
.

Let
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a real symmetric random matrix. The matrix
\mathbf{H}
is said to be Wigner-type if its entries
H_{ij}
are mean zero and independent (up to symmetric constraints).

Next, for a Wigner-type ensemble, the matrix of variances
\mathbf{S}=[S_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is defined as

\displaystyle S_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left|H_{ij}\right|^{2}.

For every such matrix
\mathbf{S}
, the
quadratic vector equation
(QVE),

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m_{i}(z)}=z+\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{ij}m_{j}(z),\quad\text{for all}\quad i\in[n],\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},

(15)

for
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},...,m_{n}):\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}_{+}^{n}
on the complex upper half plane has a unique solution
[
AEK17
]
. The case where the matrix
\mathbf{S}
is doubly stochastic is referred to as the generalized Wigner matrix, and
m_{i}(z)=m_{\rm sc}(z);\forall i\in[n]
where
m_{\rm sc}(z)
is the Stieltjes transform of the semi-circle law, is the solution to equation
15
(see, e.g.,
[
Erd19
]
, etc.).

Next, we state the local law for Wigner-type matrices from
[
AEK17
]
, i.e. that for large
n
, under some conditions on
\mathbf{S}
, the resolvent
\mathbf{G}(z)=(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})^{-1}
, with spectral parameter
z=\tau+i\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, is close to
\mathrm{diag}({\bm{m}}(z))
, as long as
\eta\gg\frac{1}{n}
. To do this, first, we recall the notion of stochastic domination.

Definition B.2

(Stochastic domination)
.

Suppose
n_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
. For two sequences of random variables
\varphi^{(n)}
and
\psi^{(n)}
, of non-negative random variables we say that
\varphi^{(n)}
is stochastically dominated by
\psi^{(n)}
if for all
\varepsilon>0
and
D>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\varphi^{(n)}>n^{\varepsilon}\psi^{(n)}\right)\leq n^{-D},\quad n\geq n_{0}(\varepsilon,D).

In this case we write
\varphi^{(n)}\prec\psi^{(n)}
.

We are now ready to state the local law for the Wigner-type random matrices. Let
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},...,m_{n})
be the unique solution to the QVE and define the density

\displaystyle\rho(\tau):=\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}\frac{1}{\pi n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Imag}\;m_{i}(\tau+i\eta).

(16)

The properties of
\rho
are studied in
[
AEK17
, Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 1.3]
. The following result is proved in
[
AEK17
, Theorems 1.7 and 1.13]
.

Theorem B.3

(Local Law for Wigner-Type Ensembles)
.

Let
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a Wigner-type ensemble with entries that have bounded moments, and assume that for fixed
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
L\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that for any
n
,

\displaystyle S_{ij}\leq\frac{a}{n},\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathbf{S}^{L})_{ij}\geq\frac{b}{n},\quad i,j\in[n].

Fix some
\gamma>0
. Then, for any
z=\tau+i\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
with
\eta\geq n^{\gamma-1}
, the resolvent matrix
\mathbf{G}(z)=(\mathbf{H}-z\mathbf{I})^{-1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{i,j\in[n]}\left|G_{ij}(z)-m_{i}(z)\delta_{ij}\right|\prec\frac{1+\sqrt{\rho(z)}}{\sqrt{\eta n}}+\frac{1}{\eta n}.

Also, for any two deterministic unit
\ell_{2}
-norm vectors
{\bm{w}},{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\left|\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{w}_{i}G_{ij}(z)v_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}(z){w}_{i}v_{i}\right|\prec\frac{1+\sqrt{\rho(z)}}{\sqrt{\eta n}}+\frac{1}{\eta n}.
