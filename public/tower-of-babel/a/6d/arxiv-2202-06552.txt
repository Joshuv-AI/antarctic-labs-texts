An excursion to multiplications and convolutions on modulation spaces
Nenad Teofanov, Joachim Toft

Abstract
. We give a self-contained introduction to (quasi-)Banach modulation spaces of ultradistributions, and review results on boundedness for multiplications and convolutions for elements in such spaces. Furthermore, we use these results to study the Gabor product. As an example, we show how it appears in a phase-space formulation of the nonlinear cubic Schrödinger equation.

Proposition 0.1
.

Let

,

\theta_{1}=\max\left(1,\frac{1}{p_{0}},\frac{1}{q_{1}},\frac{1}{q_{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{2}=\max\left(1,\frac{1}{p_{1}},\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right).

Then

\displaystyle M^{p_{1},q_{1}}\cdot M^{p_{2},q_{2}}

\displaystyle\subseteq M^{p_{0},q_{0}},

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{0}},

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}+\frac{1}{q_{0}},

\displaystyle M^{p_{1},q_{1}}*M^{p_{2},q_{2}}

\displaystyle\subseteq M^{p_{0},q_{0}},

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}

\displaystyle=\theta_{2}+\frac{1}{p_{0}},

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}}.

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad M^{p_{1},q_{1}}

\displaystyle*M^{p_{2},q_{2}}\subseteq M^{p_{0},q_{0}}

\displaystyle\ell^{p_{1}}

1.
Preliminaries

In this section we give an exposition of
background material related to the definition and basic properties of modulation spaces.
Thus we recall some facts on the short-time Fourier transform and related projections,
the (Fourier invariant) Gelfand-Shilov spaces,
weight functions, and mixed-norm spaces of Lebesgue type. We also recall
convolution and multiplication in weighted Lebesgue sequence spaces.

1.1.
The short-time Fourier transform

In what follows we let
\mathscr{F}
be the
Fourier transform which takes the form

(\mathscr{F}f)(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi)\equiv(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,dx

when
f\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Here
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the usual
scalar product
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. The same notation is used for the usual dual form
between test functions and corresponding
(ultra-)distributions. We recall that map
\mathscr{F}
extends
uniquely to a homeomorphism on the space of tempered distributions

\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
to a unitary operator on
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and restricts
to a homeomorphism on the Schwartz space of smooth rapidly
decreasing functions
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, cf.
(
1.29
).
We also observe with our choice of the Fourier
transform, the usual
convolution identity for the Fourier transform
takes the forms

\mathscr{F}(f\cdot g)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\widehat{f}*\widehat{g}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{F}(f*g)=(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g}

(1.1)

when
f,g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

In several situations it is convenient to use a localized version
of the Fourier transform, called the short-time Fourier transform, STFT for short.
The short-time Fourier transform of

f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with respect to the fixed
window function

\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is defined by

\displaystyle(V_{\phi}f)(x,\xi)

\displaystyle\equiv(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}(f,\phi(\,\cdot\,-x)e^{i\langle\,\cdot\,,\xi\rangle})_{L^{2}}.

(1.2)

Here
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{L^{2}}
denotes the unique continuous
extension of the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
restricted to

\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
into a continuous map from
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
to
\mathbf{C}
.

We observe that using certain properties for tensor products
of distributions,

\displaystyle(V_{\phi}f)(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{F}(f\cdot\overline{\phi(\,\cdot\,-x)})(\xi).

(
1.2
)
′

(cf.
[
34
,
53
]
). If in addition

f\in L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

for some
p\in[1,\infty]
, then

\displaystyle(V_{\phi}f)(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(y)\overline{\phi(y-x)}e^{-i\langle y,\xi\rangle}\,dy.

(
1.2
)
′′

We observe that the domain of
V_{\phi}

is
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
The images are contained
in
C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
, the set of smooth functions
defined on the phase space
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\simeq\mathbf{R}^{2d}
.

The short-time Fourier transform appears in different contexts
and under different names. In quantum mechanics it is rather common
to it the
coherent state transform

(see e. g.
[
38
]
). It is also closely related to the
so-called Wigner distribution or radar ambiguity function (see e. g.

[
37
]
). In time-frequency analysis, it is also sometimes called a

Voice transform
.

The main idea with the design of short-time Fourier transform is
to get the Fourier content, or the frequency resolution of localized
functions and distributions. Roughly speaking, short-time
Fourier transform give a simultaneous information both on
functions or distributions themselves as well as their
Fourier transforms in the sense that the map

x\mapsto V_{\phi}f(x,\xi)

resembles on
f(x)
, while the map

\xi\mapsto V_{\phi}f(x,\xi)

resembles on
\widehat{f}(\xi)
.

As for the ordinary Fourier transform, there are several mapping
properties which hold true for the short-time Fourier transform.
An elegant way to approach such mapping in the framework of
distributions, we may follow ideas given in
[
25
]
by Folland.

In fact, let
T
is the semi-conjugated tensor map

\displaystyle T(f,\phi)

\displaystyle=f\otimes\overline{\phi},

(1.3)

U
be the linear pullback

\displaystyle(UF)(x,y)

\displaystyle=U(y,y-x)

(1.4)

and
\mathscr{F}_{2}
be the partial Fourier transform given by

\displaystyle(\mathscr{F}_{2}F)(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}F(x,y)e^{-i\langle y,\xi\rangle}\,dy.

(1.5)

Then

V_{\phi}f=(\mathscr{F}_{2}\circ U\circ T)(f,\phi),

(1.6)

when
f,\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

We observe that the mappings

\displaystyle T:\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}),

\displaystyle\quad U,\mathscr{F}_{2}:\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

(1.7)

are continuous and uniquely extendable to continuous
mappings

\displaystyle T:\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}),

\displaystyle\quad U,\mathscr{F}_{2}:\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}),

(1.8)

which in turn restricts to isometric mappings

\displaystyle T:L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}),

\displaystyle\quad U,\mathscr{F}_{2}:L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}).

(1.9)

Here that
T
is isometric means that

\|T(f,\phi)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}.

It is now natural to define
V_{\phi}f
as the right-hand side of
(
1.6
) when
f,\phi\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, in which

V_{\phi}f
is well-defined as an element in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
.

Proposition 1.1
.

The map

\displaystyle(f,\phi)

\displaystyle\mapsto V_{\phi}f

\displaystyle:\,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

(1.10)

is continuous, which extends uniquely to a continuous map

\displaystyle(f,\phi)

\displaystyle\mapsto V_{\phi}f

\displaystyle:\,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}),

(1.11)

which in turn restricts to an isometric map

\displaystyle(f,\phi)

\displaystyle\mapsto V_{\phi}f

\displaystyle:\,

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}).

(1.12)

If
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, then
(
1.11
) shows that
V_{\phi}f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
.
On the other hand, it is easy to see that the right-hand side
of (
1.2
) defines a smooth function. Consequently
beside (
1.11
) and (
1.10
),
we also have the continuous map

(f,\phi)\mapsto V_{\phi}f:\,\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}).

(1.13)

For short-time Fourier transform, the Parseval identity
is replaced by the so-called Moyal identity, also known as the
orthogonality relation

given by

(V_{\phi}f,V_{\psi}g)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})}=(\psi,\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}(f,g)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})},

(1.14)

when
f,g,\phi,\psi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. The identity (
1.14
)
is obtained by rewriting the short-time Fourier transforms by
(
1.2
)
′
and then applying the Parseval identity
in suitable ways. We observe that the right-hand side
makes sense also when
f
,
g
,
\phi
and
\psi
belong to
other spaces than
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. For example we may let

\displaystyle(f,g,\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

(1.15)

\displaystyle(f,g,\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle(f,g,\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\text{or}\qquad(f,g,\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle\in L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

when
p,p^{\prime},q,q^{\prime}\in[1,\infty]
satisfy

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1.

By Moyal’s identity (
1.14
) it follows that if

\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus 0
, then the identity operator
on
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is given by

\operatorname{Id}=\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\right)\cdot V_{\phi}^{*}\circ V_{\phi},

(1.16)

provided suitable mapping properties
of the (
L^{2}
-)adjoint
V_{\phi}^{*}
of
V_{\phi}
can be established.
Obviously,
V_{\phi}^{*}
fullfils

(V_{\phi}^{*}F,g)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=(F,V_{\phi}g)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})}

(1.17)

when
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and
g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

By expressing the scalar product and the short-time Fourier
transform in terms of integrals in (
1.17
),
it follows by straight-forward manipulations that
the adjoint in (
1.17
) is given by

(V_{\phi}^{*}F)(x)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}F(y,\eta)\phi(x-y)e^{i\langle x,\eta\rangle}\,dyd\eta,

(1.18)

when
F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
. We may now use mapping properties
like (
1.11
)–(
1.12
) to extend
the definition of
V_{\phi}^{*}F
when
F
and
\phi
belong to
various classes of function and distribution spaces. For example,
by (
1.11
),
(
1.10
) and (
1.12
),
it follows that the map

(F,g)\mapsto(F,V_{\phi}g)_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})}

defines a sesqui-linear form on

\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,

\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and on

. This implies that
if
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, then

V_{\phi}^{*}
in (

\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
to
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
which is

to
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

to
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle V_{\phi}^{*}:\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\to\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle V_{\phi}^{*}

1.2.
STFT projections and a suitable twisted
convolution

If
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
satisfies
\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}=1
, then
(
1.16
) shows that
V_{\phi}^{*}\circ V_{\phi}

is the identity operator on
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. If we swap the
order of this composition we get certain types of projections.
In fact, for any
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus 0
, let
P_{\phi}

be the operator given by

P_{\phi}\equiv\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}\circ V_{\phi}^{*}.

(1.20)

We observe that
P_{\phi}
is continuous on
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
,

L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
due to the mapping properties
for
V_{\phi}
and
V_{\phi}^{*}
above.

It is clear that
P_{\phi}^{*}=P_{\phi}
, i. e.
P_{\phi}
is self-adjoint. Furthermore,

P_{\phi}
is an involution:

P_{\phi}^{2}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}\circ\Big(\underset{\text{The identity operator}}{\underbrace{\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}^{*}\circ V_{\phi}}}\Big)\circ V_{\phi}^{*}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}\circ V_{\phi}^{*}=P_{\phi}.

Hence,

P_{\phi}^{*}=P_{\phi}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\phi}^{2}=P_{\phi},

(1.21)

which shows that
P_{\phi}
is an orthonormal projection.

The ranks of
P_{\phi}
are given by

\displaystyle P_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))=V_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})),\qquad P_{\phi}(L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))

\displaystyle=V_{\phi}(L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})),

(1.22)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad P_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))

\displaystyle=V_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})).

In fact, if
F\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
, then

P_{\phi}F=V_{\phi}f,

where
f=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}V_{\phi}^{*}F\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. This shows that

P_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))\subseteq V_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
.
On the other hand, if
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
F=V_{\phi}f
, then

P_{\phi}F=\Big(V_{\phi}\circ\Big(\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{-2}\cdot V_{\phi}^{*}\circ V_{\phi}\Big)\Big)f=V_{\phi}f,

which shows that any element in
V_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))

equals to an element in
P_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))
, i.e.
P_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d}))=V_{\phi}(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
. This gives
the last identity in (
1.22
). In the same way, the
first two identities are obtained.

Remark 1.2
.

Let
F\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

the last identity in (

F=V_{\phi}f
for some

F=P_{\phi}F.

(1.23)

Furthermore, if (

F=V_{\phi}f
with

(1.24)

There is a twisted convolution which is linked to
the projection in (

F\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

and
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus 0

and

(1.25)

where the

twisted convolution

*_{V}
is defined by

\displaystyle(F*_{V}G)(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}\!\!F(x-y,\xi-\eta)G(y,\eta)e^{-i\langle y,\xi-\eta\rangle}\,dyd\eta.

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}\!\!F(y,\eta)G(x-y,\xi-\eta)e^{-i\langle x-y,\eta\rangle}\,dyd\eta,

(1.26)

when

. We observe that the definition of

is uniquely extendable in different ways. For example, Young’s
inequality for ordinary convolution also holds for the twisted

(F,G)\mapsto F*_{V}G

extends uniquely to continuous mappings from
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\times\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
or
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\times\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

to
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

it follows that

(F*_{V}G)*_{V}H=F*_{V}(G*_{V}H),

(1.27)

when
F,H\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and
G\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
,
or
F,H\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and
G\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

Let
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\phi_{j}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,

j=1,2,3
.
By straight-forward applications of Parseval’s formula it follows
that

\big((V_{\phi_{2}}\phi_{3})*_{V}(V_{\phi_{1}}f)\big)(x,\xi)=(\phi_{3},\phi_{1})_{L^{2}}\cdot(V_{\phi_{2}}f)(x,\xi),

(1.28)

which is some sort of reproducing kernel
of short-time Fourier transforms in the background of

*_{V}
.

1.3.
Gelfand-Shilov spaces

Before defining the Gelfand-Shilov spaces, we recall that
the Schwartz space
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
consists of all
(complex-valued) smooth functions
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big(|x^{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|\big)\leq C_{\alpha,\beta},

(1.29)

for some constants
C_{\alpha,\beta}>0
, which only depend on
the multi-indices
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbf{N}^{d}
. The Schwartz space
possess several convenient properties. and is heavily used in
mathematics, science and technology. For example, the Schwartz
space is invariant under Fourier transformation. By duality the
same holds true for its (
L^{2}
-)dual
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, the set of
tempered distributions on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

On the other hand, we observe that there are no conditions on the growths
of the constants
C_{\alpha,\beta}
with respect to

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbf{N}^{d}
. This implies that in the context of the spaces

\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, it is almost impossible to investigate
important properties like analyticity or related regularity properties
which are stronger than pure smoothness. In order for investigating
such stronger regularity properties, we need to modify
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

and the estimate (
1.29
) by imposing suitable growth conditions on the
constants
C_{\alpha,\beta}
. This leads to the definition of
Gelfand-Shilov spaces,
[
27
,
41
]
.

We only discuss Fourier invariant
Gelfand-Shilov spaces and their properties.
Let
0<s\in\mathbf{R}
be fixed. We have two different
types of Gelfand-Shilov spaces. The Gelfand-Shilov
space
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

of Roumieu type with parameter
s

consists of all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\big(|x^{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|\big)\leq Ch^{|\alpha+\beta|}(\alpha!\beta!)^{s},

(1.30)

for some constants
C,h>0
. In the same way, the Gelfand-Shilov
space
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
of Beurling type with parameter
s

consists of all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that for every
h>0
, there
is a constant
C=C_{h}>0
such that (
1.30
). Hence,
in comparison with the definition of Schwartz functions, we have limited
ourself to constants
C_{\alpha,\beta}
in
(
1.29
) which are not allowed to grow faster than those
of the form

Ch^{|\alpha+\beta|}(\alpha!\beta!)^{s}

when dealing with Gelfand-Shilov spaces.

It can be proved that
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\Sigma_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

are dense in
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
when
s\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
t>\frac{1}{2}
. We call such
s
and
t
admissible.
On the other hand, for the other choices of
s
and
t

we have

\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\Sigma_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\{0\},\quad\text{when}\quad s<\frac{1}{2},\ t\leq\frac{1}{2}.

One has that
\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
consists of real analytic functions,
and that
\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
consists of smooth functions on

\mathbf{R}^{d}
which are extendable to entire functions on
\mathbf{C}^{d}
.
The topologies of
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

are defined by the semi-norms

\|f\|_{\mathcal{S}_{s,h}}\equiv\sup\frac{|x^{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|}{h^{|\alpha+\beta|}(\alpha!\beta!)^{s}}.

(1.31)

Here the supremum should be taken
over all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbf{N}^{d}
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
. We equip

\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
by the canonical inductive limit
topology and projective limit topology, respectively, with respect to
h>0
, which are
induced by the semi-norms in (
1.31
).

Let
\mathcal{S}_{s,h}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be the Banach space which
consists of all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
\|f\|_{\mathcal{S}_{s,h}}

in (
1.31
) is finite, and let
\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be
the (
L^{2}
-)dual of
\mathcal{S}_{s,h}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. If
s\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then
the
Gelfand-Shilov distribution space

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

of
Roumieu type
is the projective limit of

\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with respect to
h>0
. If instead

s>\frac{1}{2}
, then
the
Gelfand-Shilov distribution space

\Sigma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

of
Beurling type
is the inductive limit of

\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with respect to
h>0
. Consequently,
for admissible
s
we have

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\bigcap_{h>0}\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\bigcup_{h>0}\mathcal{S}_{s,h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}).

It can be proved that
\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\Sigma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

are the (strong) duals to
\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
respectively.

We have the following embeddings and density properties for
Gelfand-Shilov and Schwartz spaces we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

(1.32)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad t>s\geq\frac{1}{2},

with dense embeddings.
Here
A\hookrightarrow B
means that
the topological spaces
A
and
B
satisfy
A\subseteq B
with
continuous embeddings.

The Fourier transform possess convenient mapping properties
on Gelfand-Shilov spaces and their distribution spaces.
In fact, the Fourier transform extends
uniquely to homeomorphisms on
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and on
\Sigma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for admissible
s
.
Furthermore,
\mathscr{F}
restricts to homeomorphisms on

\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and on
\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

One of the most important characterizations of
Gelfand-Shilov spaces is performed in terms of
estimates of the functions and their Fourier
transforms. More precisely, in
[
8
,
15
]
it is proved that
if
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
s>0
, then
f\in\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

(
f\in\Sigma_{s}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
), if and only if

|f(x)|\lesssim e^{-r|x|^{\frac{1}{s}}}\quad\text{and}\quad|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\lesssim e^{-r|\xi|^{\frac{1}{s}}},

(1.33)

for some
r>0
(for every
r>0
).
Here
g_{1}\lesssim g_{2}
means that
g_{1}(\theta)\leq c\cdot g_{2}(\theta)

holds uniformly for all
\theta

in the intersection of the domains of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}

for some constant
c>0
, and we
write
g_{1}\asymp g_{2}

when
g_{1}\lesssim g_{2}\lesssim g_{1}
.

The analysis in
[
8
,
15
]
can also be applied on
the Schwartz space, from which it follows that an element

f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
belongs to
\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, if and only if

|f(x)|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-N}\quad\text{and}\quad|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-N},

(1.34)

for every
N\geq 0
. Here and in what follows we let

\langle x\rangle=(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

1.4.
Weight functions

A
weight
or
weight function
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
is a
positive function
\omega\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
1/\omega\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
The weight
\omega
is called
moderate
,
if there is a positive weight
v
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and a constant
C\geq 1
such that

\omega(x+y)\leq C\omega(x)v(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(1.41)

If
\omega
and
v
are weights on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that
(
1.41
) holds, then
\omega
is also called

v
-moderate
.
We note that (
1.41
)
implies that
\omega
fulfills
the estimates

C^{-1}v(-x)^{-1}\leq\omega(x)\leq Cv(x),\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(1.42)

We let
\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be the set of all moderate weights on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

We say that
v
is
submultiplicative
if

v(x+y)\leq v(x)v(y)\quad\text{and}\quad v(-x)=v(x),\qquad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(1.43)

We observe that if

v\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is even and satisfies

v(x+y)\leq Cv(x)v(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d},

(1.44)

for some constant
C>0
, then for

v_{0}=C^{1/2}v
, one has that

v_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is submultiplicative and

v\asymp v_{0}
(see e. g.
[
18
,
20
,
29
]
).

We also recall from
[
30
]

that if
v
is positive and locally bounded and
satisfies (
1.44
),
then
v(x)\leq C_{0}e^{r_{0}|x|}
for some positive constants
C_{0}
and
r_{0}
.
In fact, if
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

r=\sup_{|x|\leq 1}\log v(x),\quad c=\log C

and
n
is an integer such that
n-1\leq|x|\leq n
, then
(
1.44
) gives

v(x)=v(n\cdot(x/n))\leq C^{n}v(x/n)^{n}\leq C^{n}e^{rn}=e^{(r+c)n}\leq e^{(r+c)(|x|+1)},

which gives the statement.

Therefore, if
v
is a submultiplicative weight, then

v(x)\lesssim e^{r|x|},\qquad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d},

(1.45)

for some
r\geq 0
. Hence, if
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, then
(
1.41
) and (
1.45
) imply

\omega(x+y)\lesssim\omega(x)e^{r|y|},\quad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

(1.46)

for some
r>0
. In particular, (
1.42
) shows that
for any
\omega_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, there is a constant
r>0
such that

e^{-r|x|}\lesssim\omega_{0}(x)\lesssim e^{r|x|},\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

If (
1.41
) holds, then there is a smallest positive even function

v_{0}
such that (
1.41
)
holds with
C=1
. We remark that this
v_{0}
is given by

v_{0}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\omega(x+y)}{\omega(y)},\frac{\omega(-x+y)}{\omega(y)}\right),

and is submultiplicative
(see e. g.
[
20
,
28
,
50
]
). Consequently, if
\omega

is a moderate weight, then it is also moderated by submultiplicative
weights. In the sequel,
v
and
v_{j}
for
j\geq 0
, always stand for
submultiplicative weights if
nothing else is stated.

We also remark that in the literature it is common to
define submultiplicative weights as (
1.43
) should hold,
without the condition
v(-x)=v(x)
, i. e. that
v
does not have to
be even (cf. e. g.
[
18
,
20
,
26
,
29
]
). However, in the sequel
it is convenient for us to include this property in the definition.

There are several subclasses of
\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
which are interesting
for different reasons. Though our results later on are formulated
in background of
\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, we here mention some subclasses
which especially appear in time-frequency analysis.
First we observe the class
\mathscr{P}^{0}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, which consists of all

\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

such that (
1.46
) holds for every
r>0
.

The class
\mathscr{P}_{E}^{0}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is important
when dealing with spectral invariance for
matrix or convolution operators on

\ell^{2}(\mathbf{Z}^{d})
(see e. g.
[
31
]
).
If
v\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is submultiplicative,
then
v\in\mathscr{P}_{E}^{0}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}v(nx)^{\frac{1}{n}}=1

(1.47)

(see e. g.
[
24
]
). The
condition (
1.47
) is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(v(nx))}{n}=0,

(
1.47
)
′

and is usually called the
GRS condition
, or

Gelfand-Raikov-Shilov condition
.

A more restrictive condition on
v
compared to
(
1.47
)
′
is given
by the Beurling-Domar condition

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log(v(nx))}{n^{2}}<\infty.

(1.48)

This condition is strongly linked to non quasi-analytic
classes which contain non-trivial compactly supported
elements (see e. g.
[
30
]
). Any subexponential
submultiplicative weight satisfies the Beurling-Domar condition. That is,
suppose that
\theta\in(0,1)
and that
v(x)=e^{r|x|^{\theta}}
,

x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, then (
1.48
) is fulfilled. We
let
\mathscr{P}_{\operatorname{BD}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be the set of all weights which
are moderated by submultiplicative weights which satisfy
the Beurling-Domar condition.

Finally we let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be the set of all weights on
\mathbf{R}^{d}

which are moderated by polynomially bounded functions. That is,

\omega\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, if and only if there are positive
constants
r
and
C
such that

\omega(x+y)\leq C\omega(x)(1+|y|)^{r},\qquad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Here we observe that
v(x)=(1+|x|)^{r}
is submultiplicative.

Among these weight classes we have

\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\subsetneq\mathscr{P}_{\operatorname{BD}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\subsetneq\mathscr{P}_{E}^{0}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\subsetneq\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d}).

(1.49)

In fact, it is clear that the ordering in (
1.49
)
holds. On the other hand, if
r>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, then
due to the submultiplicative weights

\displaystyle e^{r|x|^{\theta}}

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{P}_{\operatorname{BD}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

(1.50)

\displaystyle e^{r|x|/\log(e+|x|)}

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{P}_{E}^{0}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus\mathscr{P}_{\operatorname{BD}}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad e^{r|x|}

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus\mathscr{P}_{E}^{0}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

it also follows that the inclusions in (
1.49
)
are strict.

We refer to
[
16
,
29
,
30
,
50
]

for more facts about weights in time-frequency
analysis.

1.5.
Mixed norm spaces of Lebesgue type

For every
p,q\in(0,\infty]
and weight
\omega
on
\mathbf{R}^{2d}
, we set

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L_{(\omega)}^{p,q}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\equiv\|G_{F,\omega,p}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})},

where

\displaystyle\quad G_{F,\omega,p}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\|F(\,\cdot\,,\xi)\omega(\,\cdot\,,\xi)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

and

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{p,q}_{*,(\omega)}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\equiv\|H_{F,\omega,q}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})},

where

\displaystyle\quad H_{F,\omega,q}(x)

\displaystyle=\|F(x,\,\cdot\,)\omega(x,\,\cdot\,)\|_{L^{q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})},

when
F
is (complex-valued) measurable function on
\mathbf{R}^{2d}
. Then

L^{p,q}_{(\omega)}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
(
L^{p,q}_{*,(\omega)}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
)
consists of all measurable functions

F
such that
\|F\|_{L_{(\omega)}^{p,q}}<\infty

(
\|F\|_{L^{p,q}_{*,(\omega)}}<\infty
).

In similar ways, let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
be discrete sets,

\omega
be a positive function on
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
and

\ell_{0}^{\prime}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})
be the set of all formal (complex-valued)
sequences
c=\{c(j,k)\}_{j\in\Omega_{1},k\in\Omega_{2}}
. Then
the discrete Lebesgue spaces, i.e. the Lebesgue sequence spaces

\ell^{p,q}_{(\omega)}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\ell^{p,q}_{*,(\omega)}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})

of mixed (quasi-)norm types consist of all

c\in\ell_{0}^{\prime}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})

such that

\|c\|_{\ell_{(\omega)}^{p,q}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}<\infty

respectively

\|c\|_{\ell_{*,(\omega)}^{p,q}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}<\infty
.
Here

\displaystyle\|c\|_{\ell_{(\omega)}^{p,q}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}

\displaystyle\equiv\|G_{F,\omega,p}\|_{\ell^{q}(\Omega_{2})},

where

\displaystyle\quad G_{c,p}(k)

\displaystyle=\|F(\,\cdot\,,k)\omega(\,\cdot\,,k)\|_{\ell^{p}(\Omega_{1})}

and

\displaystyle\|c\|_{\ell^{p,q}_{*,(\omega)}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}

\displaystyle\equiv\|H_{c,\omega,q}\|_{\ell^{p}(\Omega_{1})},

where

\displaystyle\quad H_{c,q}(j)

\displaystyle=\|c(j,\,\cdot\,)\omega(j,\,\cdot\,)\|_{\ell^{q}(\Omega_{2})},

when
c\in\ell_{0}^{\prime}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})
.

1.6.
Convolutions and multiplications for
discrete Lebesgue spaces

Next we discuss extended Hölder and Young relations
for multiplications and convolutions on discrete Lebesgue spaces.
The Hölder and Young conditions on Lebesgue exponent are then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}},

2.
Modulation spaces, multiplications and convolutions

In this section we introduce modulation spaces,
and recall their basic properties, in particular in the context of Gelfand-Shilov spaces.
Notice that we permit the Lebesgue exponents to belong to the full interval
(0,\infty]

instead of the most common choice
[1,\infty]
, and general moderate weights which may have a (sub)exponential growth.
Here we also recall some facts on Gabor expansions for modulation spaces.

Then we deduce multiplication and convolution
estimates on modulation spaces.
There are several approaches to multiplication and convolution

(see
[

,

,

,

,

,

]
). Here we consider the case
when these exponents belong

(see also
[

,
2
,
26
,

,

,

]
).
In addition, and in order to keep the survey style of our exposition,
we focus on the bilinear case, and refer to
[

]

for extension of these results to multi-linear products as well as allowing the

2.1.
Modulation spaces

The (classical) modulation spaces, essentially introduced in

(See e. g.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

,
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and

\phi\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus 0
.

(1)

The
modulation space

consists of all

such that

\|f\|_{M^{p,q}_{(\omega)}}\equiv\|V_{\phi}f\|_{L^{p,q}_{(\omega)}}

the (quasi-)norm

(2)

The

consists of all

such that

\|f\|_{W^{p,q}_{(\omega)}}\equiv\|V_{\phi}f\|_{L^{p,q}_{*,(\omega)}}

W^{p,q}_{(\omega)}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is defined by
the (quasi-)norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{W^{p,q}_{(\omega)}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Modulation spaces possess several convenient properties.
In fact, let
p,q\in(0,\infty]
,
\omega\in\mathscr{P}_{E}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
and

(see

,

,

and

•

which increase with

if

2.2.
Multiplications and
convolutions in modulation spaces

As a first step for approaching multiplications and
convolutions for elements in modulation spaces, we
reformulate such products in terms of short-time Fourier transforms.
Let
\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be fixed
such that

\phi_{0}=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}

(2.23)

and let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Then the multiplication

f_{1}f_{2}
can be expressed by

\displaystyle F_{0}(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\big(F_{1}(x,\,\cdot\,)*F_{2}(x,\,\cdot\,)\big)(\xi).

(2.24)

where

\displaystyle F_{j}

\displaystyle=V_{\phi_{j}}f_{j},\qquad j=0,1,2,

(2.25)

In fact, by Fourier’s inversion formula we get

\big((V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})(x,\,\cdot\,)*(V_{\phi_{2}}f_{2})(x,\,\cdot\,)\big)(\xi)\\[4.30554pt]
=(2\pi)^{-d}\iiint f_{1}(y_{1})\overline{\phi_{1}(y_{1}-x)}f_{2}(y_{2})\overline{\phi_{2}(y_{2}-x)}e^{-{i\langle y_{1},\xi-\eta\rangle}}e^{-i\langle y_{2},\eta\rangle}\,dy_{1}dy_{2}d\eta\\[4.30554pt]
=\int f_{1}(y)\overline{\phi_{1}(y-x)}f_{2}(y)\overline{\phi_{2}(y-x)}e^{-i\langle y,\xi\rangle}\,dy=(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}(V_{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}}(f_{1}f_{2}))(x,\xi).

We also observe that we may extract
f_{0}=f_{1}f_{2}
by the formula

f_{0}=(\|\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}})^{-1}V_{\phi_{0}}^{*}F_{0},

(2.26)

provided
\phi_{0}
is not trivially equal to
0
.

In the same way, let
\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be
fixed such that

\phi_{0}=(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\phi_{1}*\phi_{2},

(2.27)

and let
f_{1},f_{2},g\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Then the

3.
Gabor products and modulation spaces

In this section we give an illustration how the multiplication
properties for modulation spaces can be used when treating
certain nonlinear problems. We consider the Gabor product
which is connected to such multiplication properties.
It is introduced in
[
14
]
in order
to derive a phase space analogue to the usual
convolution identity for the Fourier transform
(
1.1
).
We will prove a formula related to
(
2.24
), and then use results
from previous section to extend the
Gabor product initially defined on

M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\times M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

to some other spaces. Finally, we show how the Gabor product
gives rise to a phase-space formulation of the qubic
Schrödinger equation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\phi\in M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\setminus 0
,
and let
F_{1},F_{2}\in M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
.
Then the Gabor product
\natural_{\phi}

is given by

\left(F_{1}\natural_{\phi}\,F_{2}\right)(x,\xi)\\[4.30554pt]
=(2\pi)^{-d}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\iiint_{\mathbf{R}^{3d}}\overline{\widehat{\phi}\left(\zeta-\xi\right)}e^{i\langle x,\zeta\rangle}F_{1}(y,\eta)F_{2}(y,\zeta-\eta)\,dyd\eta d\zeta.

(3.1)

In the proof of
[
14
, Lemma 13]
it is justified that
the Gabor product in (
3.1
) is well-defined,
and that

\natural_{\phi}:M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\times M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})\to M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})

is a continuous map.

The Gabor product is particularly well-suited in the
context of the STFT.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\backslash\left\{0\right\}
.
Then

(\phi_{2},\phi_{1})_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}V_{\phi}(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})=(V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})\natural_{\phi}(V_{\overline{\phi}_{2}}f_{2}),\quad f_{1},f_{2}\in M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}).

(3.2)

Moreover,
V_{g}(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})\in M^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{2d})
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle((V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})

\displaystyle\natural_{\phi}(V_{\overline{\phi}_{2}}f_{2}))(x,\xi)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(\zeta-\xi)}e^{i\langle x,\zeta\rangle}G(y,\zeta)\,dyd\zeta,

(3.4)

where

\displaystyle G(y,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}(V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})(y,\eta)(V_{\overline{\phi}_{2}}f_{2})(y,\zeta-\eta)\,d\eta.

By Parseval’s formula we get

\displaystyle G(y,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}(V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})(y,\eta)(V_{\overline{\phi}_{2}}f_{2})(y,\zeta-\eta)\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathscr{F}(f_{1}\overline{\phi_{1}(\,\cdot\,-y)})(\eta)\mathscr{F}(f_{2}{\phi_{2}(\,\cdot\,-y)})(\zeta-\eta)\,d\eta

\displaystyle=(\mathscr{F}(f_{1}\overline{\phi_{1}(\,\cdot\,-y)})\,,\,\mathscr{F}(\overline{f_{2}\,\phi_{2}(\,\cdot\,-y)}e^{i\langle\,\cdot\,,\zeta\rangle}))_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=(f_{1}\overline{\phi_{1}(\,\cdot\,-y)}\,,\,\overline{f_{2}\,\phi_{2}(\,\cdot\,-y)}e^{i\langle\,\cdot\,,\zeta\rangle})_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f_{1}(z)\overline{\phi_{1}(z-y)}f_{2}(z)\phi_{2}(z-y)e^{-i\langle z,\zeta\rangle}\,dz.

By inserting this into (
3.4
) and using
Fubini’s theorem we get

\displaystyle((V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})

\displaystyle\natural_{\phi}(V_{\overline{\phi}_{2}}f_{2}))(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{2d}}\overline{\widehat{\phi}(\zeta-\xi)}e^{-i\langle z-x,\zeta\rangle}f_{1}(z)f_{2}(z)H(z)\,dzd\zeta,

where

\displaystyle H(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\phi_{2}(z-y)\overline{\phi_{1}(z-y)}\,dy=(\phi_{2},\phi_{1})_{L^{2}}.

Hence, by evaluating the integral with respect to
\zeta

\displaystyle((V_{\phi_{1}}f_{1})

\displaystyle\natural_{\phi}((V_{\overline{\phi}_{2}}f_{2})))(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}(\phi_{2},\phi_{1})_{L^{2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\overline{\phi(z-x)}e^{i\langle x-z,\xi\rangle}f_{1}(z)f_{2}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=(\phi_{2},\phi_{1})_{L^{2}}V_{\phi}(f_{1}f_{2})(x,\xi),

which gives (
3.2

We end the paper by formally demonstrating how the Gabor

Schrödinger equation.
Consider the elliptic nonlinear Schrödinger
equation (NLSE) given by

i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\Delta\psi+\lambda|\psi|^{2}\psi=0,

(3.11)

\psi(x,0)=\varphi(x).

Here
\lambda=\pm 1
stands for an attracting

(\lambda=+1)
or repulsive
(\lambda=-1)

\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}.

Thus we consider
\psi=\phi(x,t)
with

and

Using the following intertwining relations

V_{\phi}(x_{j}\psi)=-D_{\xi_{j}}V_{g}(\psi),\qquad V_{\phi}(D_{x_{j}}\psi)=\left(\xi_{j}+D_{x_{j}}\right)V_{\phi}\psi,

(3.12)

Here,
D_{x_{j}}=-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
,

F(x,\xi,t)=V_{\phi}(\psi(\,\cdot\,,t))(x,\xi)\\[4.30554pt]
=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\psi(y,t)\overline{\phi(y-x)}e^{-i\langle y,\xi\rangle}\,dy,\quad x,\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\ t\in\mathbf{R},

and

\widetilde{F}(x,\xi)=\overline{F(x,-\xi)}.

(3.13)

By considering (

Let
