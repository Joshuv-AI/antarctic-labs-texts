From Optimal Transport to Efficient Mechanisms for the m m -Capacitated Facilities Location Problem with Bayesian Agents Thanks: A preliminary version of this paper appeared in the proceedings of the International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (?).
Gennaro Auricchio gennaro.auricchio@unipd.it, Jie Zhang jz2558@bath.ac.uk, Mengxiao Zhang mengxiao.zhang@durham.ac.uk

Abstract
In this paper, we study of the m m -Capacitated Facility Location Problem ( m m -CFLP) on the line from a Bayesian Mechanism Design perspective and propose a novel class of mechanisms: the Extended Ranking Mechanisms (ERMs). We first show that an ERM is truthful if and only if it satisfies a system of inequalities that depends on the capacities of the facilities we need to place. We then establish a connection between the m m -CFLP and a norm minimization problem in the Wasserstein space, which enables us to show that if the number of agent goes to infinity the limit of the ratio between the expected Social Cost of an ERM and the expected optimal Social Cost is finite and characterize its value. Noticeably, our method generalizes to encompass other truthful mechanisms and other metrics, such as the l p l_{p} and Maximum costs. We conclude our theoretical analysis by characterizing the optimal ERM tailored to a m m -CFLP and a distribution μ \mu , that is the ERM whose limit Bayesian approximation ratio is the lowest compared to all other feasible ERMs. We consider mainly two frameworks: (i) in the first framework, the total facility capacity matches the number of agents, (ii) in the second framework, m = 2 m=2 . When we consider the Maximum Cost, we retrieve the optimal ERM for every μ \mu , while for the Social Cost, we characterize the solution when the measure μ \mu is symmetric. Lastly, we numerically compare the performance of the ERMs against other truthful mechanisms and evaluate how quickly the Bayesian approximation ratio converges to its limit.

Introduction

Mechanism Design seeks to establish protocols for aggregating the private information of a group of strategic agents to optimize a global social objective.
Nonetheless, optimizing a communal goal solely based on reported preferences often leads to undesirable manipulation driven by the agents’ self-interested behaviour.
Hence, a key property that a mechanism should possess is
truthfulness
, which ensures that no agent can gain an advantage by reporting false information.
Unfortunately, this stringent condition is often incompatible with the optimization the communal objective, hence implementing a truthful mechanism necessarily leads to suboptimal outcomes.
To quantify the efficiency loss entailed by a truthful mechanism, Nisan and Ronen introduced the concept of
worst-case approximation ratio
, which represents the maximum ratio between the objective achieved by the truthful mechanism and the optimal objective attainable across all possible agents’ reports (?).
Therefore, the lower the worst-case approximation ratio of a mechanism is, the better.
Ever since this pioneering work, Mechanism Design has been used to approach many key problems in Social Choice Theory, such as one-sided matching (?, ?, ?), two-sided matching (?, ?, ?), auction design (?, ?, ?), and scheduling (?, ?).

A prominent problem in Mechanism Design is the
m
-Facility Location Problem (
m
-FLP) (?, ?).
In its fundamental guise, the
m
-FLP involves locating
m
facilities amidst
n
self-interested agents.
Each agent requires access to a facility, making it preferable for them to have a facility located as close as possible to their position.
Furthermore, each facility can accommodate any number of agents, thus agents can select which facility to use devoid of any concern about possible overloads.
The study of
m
-FLP from an algorithmic mechanism design viewpoint was initiated by Procaccia and Tennenholtz in (?).
When
m=1,2
there are several truthful mechanisms, such as the median mechanism (?) and its generalizations (?, ?), that achieve small constant worst-case approximation ratios.
When
m>2
, however, these efficiency guarantees are much more negative.
Indeed, it is well-known that no truthful mechanism has a bounded approximation ratio while being deterministic and anonymous (?).
It is worthy of notice however, that this impossibility result does not apply to randomized mechanisms (?) and to instances where the number of agents is equal to the number of facilities plus one (?).

A natural extension of the
m
-FLP is the
m
-Capacitated Facility Location Problem (
m
-CFLP), in which every facility has a capacity limit.
The capacity limit constraints the amount of agents that the facility can serve.
In this case, the solution does not only elicit the positions of the facilities, but it also specifies to which facility every agent is assigned to, ensuring that no facility is overloaded.
To the best of our knowledge, there are very few works that analyzed the Mechanism Design aspects of the
m
-CFLP.
Moreover, all the existing results are conducted in the classic worst-case analysis, where the designer has no information on the agents and therefore aims to define mechanisms that work well on every possible input, regardless of the likelihood of the input.
This type of analysis is too pessimistic and gives little insight into how to select a mechanism tailored to a specific task.
For example, there are currently no mechanisms capable of locating more than two capacitated facilities while achieving a finite approximation ratio.
Indeed, it seems that most of the negative results known for the
m
-FLP hold for the
m
-CFLP as well.
Furthermore, even if we restrict our attention to the case where
m=2
, the approximation ratio of all the known truthful mechanisms depends linearly on the number of agents (?), making these efficiency guarantees less meaningful as the scale of the problem increases.
In this paper, we show that these issues do not arise when we study the
m
-CFLP from a Bayesian Mechanism Design perspective.
In Bayesian Mechanism Design, every agent’s location is a random variable whose law is known to the designer (?) thus the scope of the mechanism is not to minimize the cost in the worst possible case but rather to define truthful routines that work well in expectation.
A previous version of this paper appeared in the proceedings of the International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (?).
In this improved version, we extend our results to the Maximum Cost and the
l_{p}
costs and generalize the techniques to retrieve the optimal ERM to a wider class of problems.
In particular, we provide a sufficient condition that characterizes the optimal ERM with respect to the Social Cost for any measure and retrieve an algorithm to compute the optimal ERM for any probability measure with respect to the Maximum Cost.
Moreover, we run a comprehensive set of experiments that evaluates the performances of the mechanisms with respect to the Maximum and
l_{p}
costs and their convergence rates.

1.1
Our Contribution and Structure of the Paper

Our contribution is as follows.
In Section
3
, we define and study the Extended Ranking Mechanisms (ERMs), a generalization of the class of Ranking Mechanisms introduced in (?).
Our extension allows to tackle a broader framework in which the combined capacity of the facilities surpasses the total amount of agents.
Moreover, a Ranking Mechanism is truthful if and only if it places all the facilities at one or two different locations, while a ERM does not necessarily require such constraint to ensure truthfulness.

In Section
4
, we study the ERMs in a Bayesian framework, thus every agent’s position is represented by a set of i.i.d. random variables.
Our investigation reveals that
m
-CFLP is equivalent to a norm minimization problem over a subset of the Wasserstein space.
By leveraging the properties of the Wasserstein distance, we then establish the convergence of the Bayesian approximation ratio, i.e., the ratio between the expected cost incurred by the mechanism and the expected optimal cost, as the number of agents tends toward infinity.
We show that the limit Bayesian approximation ratio depends on the probability distribution
\mu
describing the agents, the vector
\vec{q}
determining the capacities of the facilities, and the characteristics of the mechanism.
We then characterize the set of condition under which our results extended to the Maximum and
l_{p}
costs.
Finally, we show that our techniques allow to study the Bayesian approximation ratio of previously introduced mechanisms.

In Section
5
, we identify the optimal ERM tailored to the
m
-CFLP whose agents are distributed according to
\mu
given the capacities determined by
\vec{q}
.
First, we show that an optimal ERM exists and characterize it as a solution to a minimization problem.
We then narrow our focus on two scenarios:
no-spare capacity
framework in which the total capacity of the facilities matches the total number of agents, and the
2
-CFLP.
In particular, we fully characterize the optimal ERM for a uniformly distributed population.

Finally, in Section
6
, we validate our findings through extensive numerical experiments.
In particular, we compare the performances of the ERMs with the performances of other truthful mechanisms, such as the InnerGap Mechanism (?) and the Extended Endpoint Mechanism (?).
From our experiments, we observe that a well-tuned ERM outperforms all the other mechanisms whenever
n\geq 20
.
Furthermore, the limit Bayesian approximation ratio is a reliable estimation of the Bayesian approximation ratio of the ERM when the number of agents is greater than
20
.

Related Work

The
m
-Facility Location Problem (
m
-FLP) and its variations are significant issues in many practical domains, such as disaster relief (?), supply chain management (?), healthcare (?), clustering (?), and public facilities accessibility (?).
Procaccia and Tennenholtz initially studied the Mechanism Design study of the
m
-FLP, laying the groundwork for this field in their pioneering work (?).
Following that, mechanisms with constant approximation ratios to place one or two facilities on trees (?), circles (?), general graphs (?), and metric spaces (?, ?) were introduced.
Notice however that all these positive results are confined to the case in which we have at most two facilities to place and/or the number of agents is limited.
Moreover, different works generalize the initial framework proposed in (?), by considering different agents’ preferences (?, ?), different costs (?), and additional constraints (?, ?, ?).

In this paper, we analyze the
m
-Capacitated Facility Location Problem (
m
-CFLP), a variant of the
m
-FLP in which each facility can accommodate a finite number of agents.
The Mechanism Design aspects of the
m
-CFLP have only recently begun to attract attention.
Indeed, the game theoretical framework for the
m
-CFLP that we consider was introduced in (?).
This work defined and studied various truthful mechanisms, like the InnerPoint Mechanism, the Extended Endpoint Mechanism, and the Ranking Mechanisms.
A more theoretical study of the problem was then presented in (?), demonstrating that no mechanism can position more than two capacitated facilities while adhering to truthfulness, anonymity, and Pareto optimality.
Additionally, another paper dealing with Mechanism Design aspects of the
m
-CFLP is (?).
However, it explores a different framework where only one facility needs to be placed and is unable to serve all agents.
To the best of our knowledge, all the results on the
m
-CFLP concerned the classic Mechanism Design framework that evaluates the performances of the mechanism based on worst-case analysis.
In this paper, we consider an alternative approach: the Bayesian Mechanism Design perspective.
Unlike traditional Mechanism Design, where the designer lacks information about agent types, in the Bayesian Mechanism Design framework each agent’s type follows a known probability distribution (?, ?).
In this framework, we are able to determine a probability distribution over the set of all the possible inputs of the mechanism, enabling us to consider the expected cost of a mechanism.
Bayesian Mechanism Design has been applied to investigate routing games (?), facility location problems (?), combinatorial mechanisms using
\epsilon
-greedy mechanisms (?), and auction mechanism design (?, ?).

Over the past few decades, Optimal Transport (OT) has found application in Theoretical Computer Science related fields, such as Computer Vision (?, ?), Computational Statistics (?), Clustering (?), and Machine Learning (?, ?, ?, ?).
However, the only field related to mechanism design that has been explored using OT theory is auction design (?).
In their work, the authors demonstrated that the optimal auction mechanism for independently distributed items can be characterized by the Dual Formulation of an OT problem.
Moreover, they utilized this relationship to derive the optimal mechanism for various item classes, thereby establishing a fruitful application of OT theory in the context of mechanism design.

m

The
m
-Capacitated Facility Location Problem.

Given a set of self-interested agents

\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the vector containing the agents’ positions.
Likewise, given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote with
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
the vector containing the capacities of the facilities, namely
\vec{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
.
In this setting, a facility location is defined by three objects:

(i)

a
m
-dimensional vector
\vec{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
whose entries are
m
positions on the line,

(ii)

a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
that decides the capacity of the facility built at
y_{j}
, so that
c_{\pi(j)}
is the capacity of the facility built at
y_{j}
, and

(iii)

a matching

that determines how the agents are assigned to facilities, i.e.
(i,j)\in\Gamma
if and only if the agent at
x_{i}
is assigned to
y_{j}
. Due to the capacity constraints, the degree of vertex
j\in[m]
according to
\Gamma
is at most
c_{\pi(j)}
. Similarly, every agent is assigned to only one facility, thus the degree of every
i\in[n]
according to
\Gamma
is
1
.

Given the positions of the facilities
\vec{y}
and a matching
\Gamma
, we define the cost of an agent positioned in

as
d_{i,\Gamma}(x_{i},\vec{y})=|x_{i}-y_{j}|
, where
(i,j)
is the unique edge in
\Gamma
adjacent to
i
.
Given a matching
\Gamma
and a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
, a cost function is a map
C_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to[0,+\infty)
that associates to
(\vec{x},\vec{y})
the overall cost of placing the facilities at the positions in
\vec{y}
following the permutation
\pi
and assigning the agents positioned at
\vec{x}
according to
\Gamma
.

Given a vector

The most studied cost function is the

Since multiplying the cost function by a constant does not affect the approximation ratio results, throughout the paper we set

Albeit throughout most of the paper the results will be stated for the Social Cost, we will also show that the techniques we propose can be extended to the

, which are defined as

C_{l_{p}}(\vec{x},\vec{y}):=\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i,\Gamma}(x_{i},\vec{y})^{p}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad\quad p\geq 1.

.

Mechanism Design and the Worst-Case Analysis.

An
m
-facility location mechanism
is a function
f
that takes the agents’ reports
\vec{x}
in input and returns a set of
m
positions
\vec{y}
on the line, a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
, and an agent-to-facility matching
\Gamma
.
An agent may misreport its position if it results in a facility location such that the agent’s incurred cost is smaller than reporting truthfully.
A mechanism
f
is said to be
truthful
(or
strategy-proof
) if, the cost of every agent is minimized when it reports its true position. That is,

d_{i}(x_{i},f(\vec{x}))\leq d_{i}(x_{i},f(\vec{x}_{-i},x_{i}^{\prime}))

for any misreport
x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\vec{x}_{-i}
is the vector
\vec{x}
without its
i
-th component.
Although deploying a truthful mechanism prevents agents from getting a benefit by misreporting their positions, this leads to a loss of efficiency.
To evaluate this efficiency loss, Nisan and Ronen introduced the notion of approximation ratio (?).
Given a truthful mechanism
f
, its worst-case approximation ratio with respect to the Social Cost is defined as
ar(f):=\sup_{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{SC_{f}(\vec{x})}{SC_{opt}(\vec{x})}
,
where
SC_{f}(\vec{x})
is the Social Cost of placing the facilities and assigning the agents to them following the output of
f
, while
SC_{opt}(\vec{x})
is the optimal Social Cost achievable when the agents’ report is
\vec{x}
.
The approximation ratio with respect to the
l_{p}
cost or the Maximum Costs are defined similarly.

The
Ranking Mechanisms
are a class of mechanisms for the
m
-CFLP that work under the assumption that the total capacity of the facilities matches the number of agents (?).
Each Ranking Mechanism is defined by two parameters: a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
and a vector
\vec{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in[n]^{m}
.
Given
\pi
and
\vec{t}
the routine of the Ranking Mechanism is as follows:

(i)

given
\vec{x}
the vector containing the agents’ reports ordered non-decreasingly, then the mechanism places the facility with capacity
c_{\pi(j)}
at
x_{t_{j}}
.

(ii)

The agents are assigned to the facility from left to right while respecting the capacity constraints.

It was shown in (?) that a Ranking Mechanism is truthful if and only if every
t_{j}
admits at most two different values.
Moreover, the approximation ratio of these mechanisms is bounded only when the number of agents is even, the number of facilities to places is
2
, and the two facilities have the same capacity.
In this case, the mechanism is also called InnerPoint Mechanism (IM) and its approximation ratio is
\frac{n}{2}-1
.

Bayesian Mechanism Design.

In Bayesian Mechanism Design, we assume that the agents’ types follow a probability distribution and study the performance of mechanisms from a probabilistic viewpoint (?, ?).
Every agent’s type is then described by a random variable
X_{i}
.
In what follows, we assume that every
X_{i}
is identically distributed according to a law
\mu
and independent from the other random variables.
A mechanism
f
is said to be truthful if, for every agent
i
, it holds

\mathbb{E}_{\vec{X}_{-i}}[d_{i}(x_{i},f(x_{i},\vec{X}_{-i}))]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\vec{X}_{-i}}[d_{i}(x_{i},f(x_{i}^{\prime},\vec{X}_{-i}))]\quad\quad\quad\forall x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

where
x_{i}
agent
i
’s true type,
\vec{X}_{-i}
is the
(n-1)
-dimensional random vector that describes the other agents’ type, and
\mathbb{E}_{\vec{X}_{-i}}
is the expectation with respect to the joint distribution of
\vec{X}_{-i}
.
Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, a mechanism
f
is a
\beta
-approximation if
\mathbb{E}[SC_{f}(\vec{X}_{n})]\leq\beta\,\mathbb{E}[SC_{opt}(\vec{X}_{n})]
holds, so that the lower
\beta
is, the better the mechanism is.
To unify the notation, we define the Bayesian approximation ratio for the Social Cost as the ratio between the expected Social Cost of a mechanism and the expected Social Cost of the optimal solution.
More formally, given a mechanism
f
, its Bayesian approximation ratio is defined as follows

B_{ar,1}^{(n)}(f):=\frac{\mathbb{E}[SC_{f}(\vec{X}_{n})]}{\mathbb{E}[SC_{opt}(\vec{X}_{n})]},

(2)

where the expected value is taken over the joint distribution of the vector
\vec{X}_{n}:=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.
Notice that, if
B_{ar,1}^{(n)}(f)<+\infty
, then
f
is a
B_{ar,1}^{(n)}(f)
-approximation.
Similarly, we define the Bayesian approximation ratio of
f
with respect to the Maximum Cost and the
l_{p}
costs: we denote the Bayesian approximation ratio of
f
with respect to the
l_{p}
cost with
B_{ar,p}^{(n)}(f)
and the Bayesian approximation ratio with respect to the Maximum Cost with
B_{ar,\infty}^{(n)}(f)
.

Basic Notions on Optimal Transport.

In the following, we denote with
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
the set of probability measures over
\mathbb{R}

Given

We denote with

That is,

\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}

\nu_{j}\geq 0

Given two measures

W_{p}^{p}(\alpha,\beta)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|x-y|^{p}d\pi,

(3)

where
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
is the set of probability measures over
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
whose first marginal is equal to
\alpha
and the second marginal is equal to
\beta
(?).
When

W_{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}\max_{(x,y)\in spt(\pi)}|x-y|.

It is well-known that, for every

is a metric over

.

Basic Assumptions.

Finally, we layout the basic assumptions of our framework.
In what follows, we tacitly assume that the underlying distribution

(i)

\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})

(ii)

(iii)

The Extended Ranking Mechanisms

Since our study aims to examine the behaviour of the mechanism as the number of agents goes to infinity, expressing the problem in terms of absolute capacities is unsuitable.
For this reason, we rephrase the specifics of the
m
-CFLP in terms of percentages.
In particular, we shift our focus to the percentage capacity vector (p.c.v.)
\vec{q}\in(0,1)^{m}
.
Each value
q_{j}
corresponds to the percentage of agents that the
j
-th facility can accommodate.
Given
\vec{q}
and a number of agents
n
, we recover the absolute capacity of the
j
-th facility by setting
c_{j}=\left\lfloor q_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1
.
Conversely, when we are given the absolute capacities
c_{j}
for a given number of agents
n
, the corresponding p.c.v. is
q_{j}=\frac{c_{j}}{n}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the entries of
\vec{q}
are ordered non-increasingly.

Mechanism 1

(Extended Ranking Mechanisms (ERMs))
.

Let
\vec{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})
be a p.c.v..
Given a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
and a non-decreasingly ordered vector
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}
, that is
v_{j}\leq v_{j+1}
, the routine of the ERM associated with
\pi
and
\vec{v}
, namely
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
, is as follows:

(i)

First, we collect the reports of the agents and order them non-decreasingly. We denote with
\vec{x}
the ordered vector containing the agents’ reports, thus
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}
.

(ii)

Second, we elicit
m
positions on the line by setting
y_{j}=x_{\left\lfloor v_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}
for every
j\in[m]
and place the facility with capacity
q_{\pi(j)}
at
y_{j}
.

(iii)

Finally, we assign every agent to the facility closer to the position they reported (break ties arbitrarily without overloading the facilities).

Notice that, in the routine of the ERM, the vector
\vec{v}
plays the role that
\vec{t}
plays in the routine of a Ranking Mechanism.
Indeed, the main difference between the Ranking Mechanisms and our generalization lies in how the mechanism matches the agents to the facilities.
While the Ranking Mechanisms assign the agents monotonically from left to right, the ERM assigns every agent to the facility that is closer to their report.
Depending on the pair
(\pi,\vec{v})
and
\vec{q}
, however, the matching returned by the ERM might overload some of the facilities.
We say that a couple
(\pi,\vec{v})
induces a
feasible
ERM if, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the output of
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is a facility location for the
m
-CFLP induced by
\vec{q}
.
Given a p.c.v.
\vec{q}
, the set of parameters
(\pi,\vec{v})
that induce a feasible ERM is characterizable through a system of inequalities.
For the sake of simplicity, we first consider the case in which
\vec{v}
does not have two equal entries, that is
v_{j}\neq v_{l}
for every
j\neq l
.

Theorem 1
.

Given
\vec{q}
and
\vec{v}
such that
v_{j}\neq v_{i}
for every

, then
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is feasible if and only if the following system of inequalities are satisfied

\begin{cases}q_{\pi(1)}\geq v_{2}\\
q_{\pi(2)}\geq v_{3}-v_{1}\\
\quad\quad\vdots\\
q_{\pi(m-1)}\geq v_{m}-v_{m-2}\\
q_{\pi(m)}\geq 1-v_{m-1}\end{cases}.

(4)

Proof.

Let
\vec{x}
be the vector that contains the agents’ reports ordered in a non-decreasing order.
First, we show that every time the system (

By definition of

Furthermore, every facility shares its position with an agent, depending on the vector
\vec{v}

Let us now focus the

y_{j}=x_{\left\lfloor v_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}

Without loss of generality, let us assume that

y_{j+1}
, such that
y_{j-1}\leq y_{j}\leq y_{j+1}
.
The maximum number of agents whose report is closer to
y_{j}
is
\left\lfloor v_{j+1}(n-1)\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor v_{j-1}(n-1)\right\rfloor-1
.
Since the capacity of the
j
-th facility is
\left\lfloor q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1
, we have that the facility at
y_{j}
cannot be overloaded if and only if
\left\lfloor q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1\geq\left\lfloor v_{j+1}(n-1)\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor v_{j-1}(n-1)\right\rfloor-1
.
We now show that the latter condition is implied by
q_{\pi(j)}\geq v_{j+1}-v_{j-1}
or, equivalently,
q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\geq(v_{j+1}-v_{j-1})(n-1)
.
Indeed, it is easy to see that
\left\lfloor q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1\geq q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\geq\left\lfloor q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\right\rfloor
, thus we have
\left\lfloor q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1\geq q_{\pi(j)}(n-1)\geq(v_{j+1}-v_{j-1})(n-1)\geq\left\lfloor v_{j+1}(n-1)\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor v_{j-1}(n-1)\right\rfloor-1
.
Through a similar argument, we deal with
y_{1}
and
y_{m}
.

We now show the inverse implication, i.e. if
(\pi,\vec{v})
satisfies system (
4
), then
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is feasible.
Toward a contradiction, assume that one of the inequalities does not holds.
Without loss of generality, assume that
v_{2}>q_{\pi(1)}
, there then exists a value
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
v_{2}-\frac{2}{N-1}>q_{\pi(1)}
.
Let us then consider
n=N
, then it holds
\left\lfloor v_{2}(N-1)\right\rfloor-1>\left\lfloor q_{\pi(1)}(N-1)\right\rfloor
, hence
\left\lfloor v_{2}(N-1)\right\rfloor>\left\lfloor q_{\pi(1)}(N-1)\right\rfloor+1
.
Let us then consider the instance
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined as
x_{i}=0
if
i=1,\dots,\left\lfloor v_{2}(N-1)\right\rfloor
and
x_{i}=1
otherwise.
On this instance
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
place a facility at
y_{1}=0
and all the others at
1
, the facility at
0
has capacity
\left\lfloor q_{\pi(1)}(N-1)\right\rfloor+1
.
By definition, the number of agents whose closest facility is
y_{1}
is
\left\lfloor v_{2}(N-1)\right\rfloor>\left\lfloor q_{\pi(1)}(N-1)\right\rfloor+1
, which contradicts the feasibility of the mechanism.
∎

The feasibility of the ERM ensures also its truthfulness.

Theorem 2
.

Given a p.c.v.
\vec{q}
, any feasible
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is truthful.
Thus it is also truthful in the Bayesian framework.

Proof.

Let
\vec{q}
be a p.c.v. and let
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
be a feasible ERM.
Toward a contradiction, let
\vec{x}
be an instance in which an agent is able to manipulate.
Without loss of generality, let us assume that the manipulative agent’s real position is
x_{i}
.
Since every agent is assigned to the facility that is closer to the position they report, the manipulation performed by the agent at
x_{i}
must alter the position of the facilities, as otherwise the cost of the manipulative agent cannot decrease.
We notice that the positions of the facilities are determined through the same routine of the Percentile mechanisms, (?).
Owing to the truthfulness of the Percentile mechanisms, we infer that no agent can misreport in such a way that a facility gets closer to its position, which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Given a p.c.v.
\vec{q}\in(0,1)^{m}
, let us fix
\vec{v}\in[0,1]^{m}
.
Then, if
(\max_{j\in[m]}v_{j}-\min_{j\in[m]}v_{j})>\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}-1
, the mechanism
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is not feasible for every
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
1
, indeed if
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is feasible, then (
4
) holds.
If we sum all the inequalities of system (
4
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{\pi(j)}=\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}

\displaystyle\geq v_{2}+(v_{3}-v_{1})+\dots+(v_{m}-v_{m-2})+(1-v_{m-1})=1+v_{m}-v_{1},

since
\vec{v}
is ordered increasingly.
Thus, if
v_{m}-v_{1}=\big(\max_{j\in[m]}v_{j}-\min_{j\in[m]}v_{j}\big)>\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}-1

holds, system (
4
) does not, which concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we notice that if
\vec{v}
has at least two equal entries, not all the feasible ERMs necessarily satisfy system (
4
).
This is the case for the
all-median
mechanism, which places all the facilities at the median agent.
Indeed, depending on
\vec{q}
, the vector
\vec{v}=(0.5,\dots,0.5)
may not satisfy system (
4
) for any
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
.
When
v_{j}=v_{l}=v
for some indices
j,l\in[m]
, we need to consider all the facilities placed at
x_{\left\lfloor v(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}
as if they were a unique facility whose capacity is the total capacity of the facilities placed at
x_{\left\lfloor v(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}
.
By doing so, we extend Theorem
1
and
2
to the cases in which
\vec{v}
has at least two equal entries.
In particular, given
\vec{v}\in[0,1]^{m}
, let
\vec{v}^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{m^{\prime}}
be the vector containing all the different entries of
\vec{v}
, then, the mechanism
\texttt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is feasible if and only if the following system is satisfied

\begin{cases}\sum_{l\in[m];\;s.t.\;v_{\pi(l)}=v_{1}^{\prime}}q_{\pi(l)}\geq v_{2}^{\prime}\\
\sum_{l\in[m];\;s.t.\;v_{\pi(l)}=v_{2}^{\prime}}q_{\pi(l)}\geq v_{3}^{\prime}-v_{1}^{\prime}\\
\quad\quad\vdots\\
\sum_{l\in[m];\;s.t.\;v_{\pi(l)}=v_{m-1}^{\prime}}q_{\pi(l)}\geq v_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}-v_{m^{\prime}-2}^{\prime}\\
\sum_{l\in[m];\;s.t.\;v_{\pi(l)}=v_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}}q_{\pi(l)}\geq 1-v_{m^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}\end{cases}.

Notice that Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
, and Corollary

hold regardless of the objective we are seeking to minimise, that is the Social Cost, the
l_{p}
costs, and the Maximum Cost.

4
The Bayesian Analysis of the ERMs

In this section, we present our main result, which characterizes the limit of the Bayesian approximation ratio of any feasible ERM as a function of
\vec{v}
,
\pi
,
\mu
, and
\vec{q}
for all the relevant costs.
We start considering the Social Cost and extend all our results to the
l_{p}
costs and the Maximum Cost in a dedicated section.
From now on, we tacitly assume that
q_{i}\,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.1
The Social Cost case

First, we relate the
m
-CFLP to a norm minimization problem in the Wasserstein Space.

Lemma 1
.

Given a p.c.v.
\vec{q}
, let
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector containing the agents’ reports.
Let us fix
\mu_{\vec{x}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\delta_{x_{i}}
.
Then, it holds

SC_{opt}(\vec{x})=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,\vec{q}}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\zeta),

(5)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,\vec{q}}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of probability measures such that
\zeta=\sum_{j\in[m]}\zeta_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
, where
y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\dots\leq y_{m}
and
\zeta_{j}\leq q_{\sigma(j)}
for every
j\in[m]
.
Similarly, given a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
and a vector
\vec{v}
that induce a feasible ERM, it holds

SC_{\pi,\vec{v}}(\vec{x})=\min_{\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\pi(j)},\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\lambda),

(6)

where
SC_{\pi,\vec{v}}(\vec{x})
is the Social Cost attained by
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
on instance
\vec{x}
,
\lambda=\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{x_{r_{j}}}
, and
r_{j}=\left\lfloor v_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1
.

Proof.

First, we show that (
5
) holds.
Given
\vec{x}
and
\vec{q}
, let us consider an optimal solution to the
m
-CFLP.
In particular, we denote with
\vec{y}
be the vector containing
m
positions on the line,
\sigma
the permutation that determines how to distribute the facilities among the positions in
\vec{y}
, and
\Gamma
a matching that assigns every agent to a facility without overloading the facilities.
Let us now consider the following transportation plan
\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\Gamma}\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{j})}
.
By definition of
\Gamma
, we have that
\sum_{j\in[m]}\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=\frac{1}{n}
and
\sum_{i\in[n]}\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=\frac{k_{j}}{n}\leq\frac{c_{\sigma(j)}}{n}=q_{\sigma(j)}
,
where
k_{j}
is the degree of
j\in[m]
according to
\Gamma
.
Thus, if we set
\nu_{n,m}=\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{k_{j}}{n}\delta_{y_{j}}
, we have that
\nu_{n,m}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})
.
It is easy to see that
SC(\vec{x})=\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}|x_{i}-y_{j}|\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}\geq W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n,m})
, hence
SC_{opt}(\vec{x})\geq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\zeta)
.
To conclude, we show that the inverse inequality holds.

Let
(\sigma,\nu_{m,n})
be a minimizer of the right hand side of (
5
), we show that there exists a set of
m
locations
\vec{y}
, a permutation
\sigma^{\prime}
, and a matching
\Gamma
whose Social Cost is
W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n,m})
.
First, let us set
\vec{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
, where
\{y_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
is the support of
\nu_{m,n}
.
Let
q_{j}=\frac{c_{j}}{n}
, then there exists an optimal transportation plan
\pi
between
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{m,n}
such that for every
i\in[n]
, there exists a unique
j\in[m]
such that
\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}\neq 0
(?).
Notice that, by definition of transportation plan, if
\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}\neq 0
for only a couple of values
i\in[n]
,
j\in[m]
, then
\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=\frac{1}{n}
.
Thus, the set
\Gamma=\{(i,j)\in[n]\times[m];\;\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=\frac{1}{n}\}
is well defined.
Since for every
i\in[n]
we have that there exists a unique
j\in[m]
for which it holds
\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=\frac{1}{n}
, we have that the degree of every
i\in[n]
is one.
Since
\sum_{i\in[n]}\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=(\nu_{m,n})_{j}\leq\frac{c_{\sigma(j)}}{n}
, we have that the degree of every
j\in[m]
is less than
c_{\sigma(j)}
.
Thus the triplet
(\vec{y},\sigma,\Gamma)
is a feasible location for the
m
-CFLP.
Since the Social Cost of
(\vec{y},\sigma,\mu)
is equal to
\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}|x_{i}-y_{j}|\pi_{x_{i},y_{j}}=W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{m,n})
, we have that
\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\zeta)=W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{m,n})\geq SC_{opt}(\vec{x})
,
which concludes the proof.

Notice that, since
\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\zeta)=SC_{opt}(\vec{x})
holds, we can refine the previous argument and show that, given an optimal facility location for the
m
-CFLP it is possible to build a solution to the minimization problem in the right hand side of (
5
).
Vice-versa, given a solution to the minimization problem in the right hand side of (
5
), it is possible to build an optimal facility location for the
m
-CFLP.
Using the same argument and the same constructions, it is possible to show that (
6
) holds.
∎

The connection between the
m
-CFLP and Optimal Transport theory highlighted in Lemma
1
enables us to exploit the properties of the Wasserstein distances and to characterize the limit Bayesian approximation ratio of every feasible ERM.

Theorem 3
.

Given the p.c.v.
\vec{q}
, let
\vec{v}\in(0,1)^{m}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
be such that
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is feasible.
Then, it holds

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[SC_{\pi,\vec{v}}(\vec{x})]}{\mathbb{E}[SC_{opt}(\vec{x})]}=\frac{W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})}{W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})},

(7)

where
\nu_{m}
is a solution to the following minimization problem

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,\vec{q}}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu,\zeta),

(8)

and
\nu_{\vec{v}}
is a solution to

\min_{\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\pi(j)},\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1}W_{1}\Big(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})}\Big).

(9)

Proof.

The proof consists of three steps:

(i)

First, we show that the expected optimal Social Cost for the
m
-CFLP converges to the objective value of problem
(
8
).

(ii)

Second, we show that the expected Social Cost of
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
converges to the objective value of the minimization problem in (
9
).

(iii)

We combine the two convergence results to retrieve (
7
).

The limit of the expected optimal Social Cost.

First, we show that a solution to problem (
8
) always exists.
For every
m
the number of permutation is finite, thus it suffices to show that, for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
there exists a solution to the problem
\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu,\zeta)
.
Since
\mu
has finite first moment, i.e.
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|d\mu<+\infty
, the minimization problem is well-posed and its minimum is finite.
Moreover, the set
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})
is a closed set in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, thus a solution to the minimization problem does exist (although it may not be unique).
Let
\nu_{m}
be such that
W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,\vec{q}}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu,\zeta)
.
From Lemma
1
, we know that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a
\nu_{\vec{x},m}
such that
SC_{opt}(\vec{x})=W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})
.
By definition of
\nu_{m}
, we have that
W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{x},m})
.
Since
W_{1}
is a distance, it holds
W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{x},m})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})+W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})
.
By rearranging the terms and by taking the expected value with respect to the distribution of
\vec{X}
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})]-\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})]\leq\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})]
.
By a similar argument, we have that
W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})\leq W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{m})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})+W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})
, hence
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})]-\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})]\leq\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})]
.
We then infer that
|\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})]-\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})]|\leq\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})]
.
Since the right handside of this inequality converges to
0
as
n
goes to
+\infty
(see (?)), we infer that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},m})]=W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})
,
which concludes the first part of the proof.

The limit of the expected Social Cost of the Mechanism.

First, we retrieve a minimizer to problem (
9
).
Given
\vec{v}
, let us set
y_{j}=F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})
.
For every
\lambda=\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
such that
\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1
, we have that

W_{1}(\mu,\lambda)=\sum_{j\in[m]}\int_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}\big(\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}\lambda_{l}\big)}^{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}\big(\sum_{l=1}^{j}\lambda_{l}\big)}|x-y_{j}|d\mu\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\min_{j\in[m]}|x-y_{j}|d\mu.

Let us now consider the measure
\nu_{\vec{v}}=\sum_{j\in[m]}\Big(F_{\mu}(z_{j})-F_{\mu}(z_{j-1})\Big)\delta_{y_{j}}
,
where
z_{j}=\frac{y_{j+1}+y_{j}}{2}
if
j=2,\dots,m-1
,
z_{0}=-\infty
and
z_{m}=+\infty
.
We then have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[m]}\int_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}\big(\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}F_{\mu}(z_{l})-F_{\mu}(z_{l-1})\big)}^{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}\big(\sum_{l=1}^{j}F_{\mu}(z_{l})-F_{\mu}(z_{l-1})\big)}|x-y_{j}|d\mu=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\min_{j\in[m]}|x-y_{j}|d\mu.

Thus
\nu_{\vec{v}}
is a solution to problem (
9
).

We now study the limit of the expected Social Cost of the mechanism.
The argument used for this part is similar to the one used for the limit expected optimal Social Cost but more delicate.
Indeed, in this case, the set on which we minimize the Wasserstein distance does depend on the agents’ report
\vec{x}
.
Moreover, the sets on which are formulated problem (
9
) and (
6
) are, in general, different.
To overcome these issues, we need to define two auxiliary probability measures, namely
\phi
and
\psi
.
Given
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\nu_{\vec{v}}
and
\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}}
be the solutions to problem (
9
) and (
6
), respectively.
We define the measures
\phi=\sum_{j\in[m]}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}})_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
, where
y_{j}
is the support of the measure
\nu_{\vec{v}}
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have that
W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\phi)\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})+W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}})+W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}},\phi).

We therefore infer

W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})-W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})+W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}},\phi).

(10)

Similarly, given
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\psi=\sum_{j\in[m]}(\nu_{\vec{v}})_{j}\delta_{y_{\vec{x},j}}
, where
\{y_{\vec{x},j}\}_{j\in[m]}
is the support of
\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}}
.
We then have

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}})-W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})+W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{v}},\psi).

(11)

Since the Wasserstein distance is always non negative, we can combine the estimations in (
10
) and (
11
), to obtain

|W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}})-W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})|\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{n})+W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{v}},\psi)+W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}},\phi).

If we take the expectation of both sides of the inequality the inequality still holds.
Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\mu)]=0
(see (?)), we just need to prove that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{v}},\psi)]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}},\phi)]=0
.
Let us consider
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{v}},\psi)]
, the convergence of
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}},\phi)]
follows by a similar argument.
We notice that
\psi
and
\nu_{\vec{v}}
have different supports, but
\psi_{j}=(\nu_{\vec{v}})_{j}
, thus it holds
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{v}},\psi)]\leq\sum_{j\in[m]}\psi_{j}\mathbb{E}[|y_{j}-y_{\vec{x},j}|]
, where
y_{\vec{x},j}
is the
j
-th point in the support of
\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}}
.
By definition of ERM, it holds
y_{\vec{x},j}=x_{\left\lfloor v_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}
.
Since the
\big(\left\lfloor v_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1\big)
-th order statistics converges to the
v_{j}
-th quantile of
\mu
(?), we have that
\mathbb{E}[|y_{j}-y_{\vec{x},j}|]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Indeed, since
(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}})_{j}\leq 1
for every
j\in[m]
, we have
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\nu_{\vec{x},\vec{v}},\phi)]\leq\sum_{j\in[m]}\mathbb{E}[|y_{\vec{x},j}-y_{j}|]
, where
y_{\vec{x},j}=x_{\left\lfloor v_{j}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}
and
y_{j}=F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})
.
Using Bahadur’s representation formula (?), we have that

X_{\left\lfloor(n-1)v_{j}\right\rfloor+1}-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})=\frac{S_{n}(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j}))-v_{j}}{\rho_{\mu}(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j}))}+R_{n},

(12)

where
R_{n}
is the rest of the Bahadur’s formula, for which holds
R_{n}\leq O(n^{-\frac{3}{4}})
with probability
1
,
\rho_{\mu}
is the density of
\mu
, and
S_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(X_{i})_{\{X_{i}\leq t\}}
where
\mathbb{I}(X_{i})_{\{X_{i}\leq t\}}=1
if
X_{i}\leq t
and
\mathbb{I}(X_{i})_{\{X_{i}\leq t\}}=0
otherwise.
Since
\rho_{\mu}
is always strictly positive on the interior of the support of
\mu
and
v_{j}\in(0,1)
for every
j=1,\dots,m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle|y_{\vec{x},j}-y_{j}|]\leq K\mathbb{E}\big[|S_{n}(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j}))-v_{j}|\big]+\mathbb{E}[|R_{n}|]

\displaystyle\leq K\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{i}\leq F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})\}}(X_{i})-v_{j})|\bigg]+Cn^{-\frac{3}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq K\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{i}\leq F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})\}}(X_{i}))-v_{j})}{n}\Big|\Big]+Cn^{-\frac{3}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{n}}Var(\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{1}\leq F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})\}}(X_{1}))+Cn^{-\frac{3}{4}},

since the

are i.i.d. and

is a Bernoulli variable that with probability

is equal to

We then conclude that

converges to

Characterizing the Bayesian approximation ratio.

To conclude, notice that the distance between an absolutely continuous measure and a discrete measure is always greater than zero, thus
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[SC_{opt}(\vec{X})]>0
.
For this reason, we have that the limit of the ratio is equal to the ratio of the limits, which proves (

∎

Notice that our result applies only to feasible

Finally, we notice that the Bayesian approximation ratio is invariant to positive affine transformation of

Corollary 2
.

Let
\vec{q}
be a p.c.v. and let
X
be the random variable associated with
\mu
.
Given

and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, let

be the probability distribution associated with

Then, the asymptotical Bayesian approximation ratio of any feasible ERM is the same regardless of whether the agent type is distributed according to
\mu
or
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}

Proof.

Let
\nu^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}
be the solution to
\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu_{\alpha,\beta},\zeta)
and let
\nu_{m}
be the solution to (
8

Let us fix

.
Since

is a bijective and monotone-increasing function.
We denote the inverse function of

with
H
.
It is well-known that if

is a random variable whose law is
\mu
, then the law of

is
L_{\#}\mu
.
We recall that
L_{\#}\mu
is the pushforward of the measure
\mu
, defined as
L_{\#}\mu(A)=\mu(H(A))
, see (?).
By definition, we have that

L_{\#}(\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{x_{j}})=\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{\alpha x_{j}+\beta}
.
We now show that
\nu^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}=L_{\#}\nu_{m}
.
First, notice that, since
\alpha>0
the function
L
is monotone increasing, thus if
\nu_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})
, then
L_{\#}\nu_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})
.
Toward a contradiction, let us now assume that

is such that

Let us now define
\eta=H_{\#}\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R})
.
Since

is also monotone increasing, we have
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})
and
\gamma=L_{\#}\eta
.
Furthermore, by the properties of the Wasserstein distance, we have

and
W_{1}(L_{\#}\nu_{m},L_{\#}\mu)=\alpha W_{1}(\nu_{m},\mu)
.
In particular, we get

which contradicts the optimality of
\nu_{m}

We therefore conclude that

W_{1}(\nu^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}\mu_{\alpha,\beta})=\alpha W_{1}(\nu_{m},\mu)
.
To conclude, we observe that

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\sigma(j)},\\
\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle W_{1}\Big(\mu_{\alpha,\beta},\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{\alpha y_{j}+\beta}\Big)=\alpha\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\sigma(j)},\\
\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1\end{subarray}}W_{1}\Big(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}\Big),

for any set of

.
We then observe that

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\sigma(j)},\\

By taking the ratio of the two expectations, the factor

X_{\left\lfloor\lambda(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}\leq\frac{X_{1}+X_{n}}{2}
, since
q_{2}<0.5
.
Let us now set
\epsilon>0
and
r=\left\lfloor\lambda(n-1)\right\rfloor+1
, denote with
A^{c}
the complement of the set
A
, we have

\displaystyle P(

\displaystyle\vec{X}\in C_{1})\leq P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{X_{1}+X_{n}}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{X_{1}+X_{n}}{2}\Big|X_{1}\leq\epsilon,X_{n}\geq 1-\epsilon\Big)P(X_{1}\leq\epsilon,X_{n}\geq 1-\epsilon)

\displaystyle\quad+P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{X_{1}+X_{n}}{2}\Big|\Big\{X_{1}\geq\epsilon,X_{n}\leq 1-\epsilon\Big\}^{c}\Big)P\left(\Big\{X_{1}\geq\epsilon,X_{n}\leq 1-\epsilon\Big\}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\Big)\big(P(X_{1}\leq\epsilon)+P(X_{n}\geq 1-\epsilon)\big)+P(X_{1}\geq\epsilon)+P(X_{n}\leq 1-\epsilon),

P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{X_{1}+X_{n}}{2}\Big|\Big\{X_{1}\geq\epsilon,X_{n}\leq 1-\epsilon\Big\}^{c}\Big)\leq 1
.
Notice that the cumulative distribution function of the
n
-th order statistic of a uniform distributed random variable is
t\to\Big(F_{\mu}(t)\Big)^{n}=t^{n}
hence
P(X_{n}\leq 1-\epsilon)=(1-\epsilon)^{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}P(X_{n}\leq 1-\epsilon)=0
.
Likewise, we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P(X_{1}\geq\epsilon)=0
.
Lastly, we need to show that
P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\Big)
goes to
0
as
n
goes to infinity.
It is well-known that, for a suitable constant
K
, it holds
K\sqrt{n}(X_{\left\lfloor\lambda(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}-\lambda)
converges in distribution to the standard Gaussian
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Given
\epsilon<2\lambda-1
, we have that
P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\Big)=P\Big(K\sqrt{n}(X_{r}-\lambda)\leq K\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}-\lambda\Big)\Big)
.
Thus

\displaystyle P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big|P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\Big)-P\Big(Z\leq K\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}-\lambda\Big)\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+P\Big(Z\leq K\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}-\lambda\Big)\Big).

Since
\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}-\lambda\Big)<0
, we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P\Big(Z\leq K\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}-\lambda\Big)\Big)=0
.
Finally, by the convergence in distribution of
X_{r}
, see (?), we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big|P\Big(X_{r}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\Big)-P\Big(Z\leq K\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}-\lambda\Big)\Big)\Big|=0
,
which allows us to conclude that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\vec{X}\in C_{1})=0
.

Similarly, we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\vec{X}\in C_{2})=0
, hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\vec{X}\in C_{3})=1
.
Lastly, we need to show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[SC_{\pi,\vec{v}}(\vec{X})|\vec{X}\in C_{3}]=W_{1}(\mu,(1-q_{2})\delta_{0}+q_{2}\delta_{2(1-q_{2})})
.
Notice that if
\vec{x}\in C_{3}
, the EEM places a facility at
x_{1}
and the other one at
2x_{n-(\left\lfloor q_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+2)}-x_{1}
, the first
n-(\left\lfloor q_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1)
agents are assigned to the facility placed at
x_{1}
, while the others to the facility at
2x_{n-\left\lfloor q_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+2}-x_{1}
.
Since every agent is assigned to its closest facility, we can adapt the argument used to prove Lemma
1
, and infer that

SC_{EEM}(\vec{x})=W_{1}(\mu_{n},q_{1}\delta_{x_{1}}+(1-q_{1})\delta_{2x_{n-\left\lfloor q_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+2}-x_{1}}).

By the same argument used in the proof of Theorem
3
, it is possible to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}W_{1}(\mu_{n},q_{1}\delta_{x_{1}}

\displaystyle+(1-q_{1})\delta_{2x_{\left\lfloor q_{1}(n-1)\right\rfloor+2}-x_{1}})=W_{1}(\mu,(1-q_{2})\delta_{0}+q_{2}\delta_{2(1-q_{2})}),

which concludes the proof.

5
The Optimal Extended Ranking Mechanism

Owing to Theorem
3
, the limit Bayesian approximation ratio of any ERM depends on
\mu
,
\vec{q}
,
\pi
, and
\vec{v}
.
While
\mu
and
\vec{q}
are specifics of the problem,
\pi
and
\vec{v}
are parameters that the mechanism designer can tune.
In this section, we study how to determine an optimal ERM tailored to
\mu
and
\vec{q}
, i.e. a mechanism
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{ar,1}\big(\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}\big)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{ar,1}\big(\texttt{ERM}^{(\pi^{\prime},\vec{v}^{\prime})}\big)

for any other feasible
\texttt{ERM}^{(\pi^{\prime},\vec{v}^{\prime})}
.
Similarly, we define the optimal ERM with respect to the other objective functions.

Theorem 5
.

Given
\mu
, a p.c.v.
\vec{q}
and an objective function, there always exist a tuple
(\pi,\vec{v})
whose associated ERM, that is
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
.
Moreover, the couple
(\pi,\vec{v})
is a solution to the following minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\min_{\vec{v}\in(0,1)^{m}}W_{p}\left(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\eta_{j}\delta_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})}\right)

(20)

s.t.

\displaystyle v_{j+1}-v_{j-1}\leq q_{\pi(j)}\;\;\text{for}\;\;j=2,\dots,m-1,\;\;v_{1}\leq q_{\pi(1)},\;\text{and}\;\;v_{m}\leq 1-q_{\pi(m)},

\displaystyle\eta_{j}=F_{\mu}\left(\frac{F^{[-1]}_{\mu}(v_{j+1})+F^{[-1]}_{\mu}(v_{j})}{2}\right)-F_{\mu}\left(\frac{F^{[-1]}_{\mu}(v_{j})+F^{[-1]}_{\mu}(v_{j-1})}{2}\right),

(21)

for
j=2,\dots,m-1
,
\eta_{1}=F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{F^{[-1]}_{\mu}(v_{2})+F^{[-1]}_{\mu}(v_{1})}{2}\Big)
,
\eta_{m}=1-\eta_{m-1}
, and
where
p
depends on the objective to minimize.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we prove the theorem for
p=1
, which correspond to the Social Cost.
First, we show that a solution to problem (
20
) always exists.
Since for every
m
there are a finite number of
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
, it suffices to show that for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
there exists a solution to problem

\displaystyle\min_{\vec{v}\in(0,1)^{m}}

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\eta_{j}(\vec{v})\delta_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})}\right)

(22)

s.t.

\displaystyle v_{j+1}-v_{j-1}\leq q_{\pi(j)}\;\;\text{for}\;\;j=2,\dots,m-1,\;v_{1}\leq v_{\pi(q)},\;\text{and}\;\;v_{m}\leq 1-v_{\pi(m)},

where
\eta_{j}(\vec{v})
are the coefficient associated with the permutation
\pi
defined as in (
21
).
Indeed, following the argument used to prove Theorem
3
, we have that, given
\vec{v}
, the probability measure
\eta(\vec{v})=\sum_{j\in[m]}\eta_{j}(\vec{v})\delta_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})}
is a solution to

W_{1}(\mu,\eta(\vec{v}))=\min_{\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\sigma(j)},\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1}W_{1}\left(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})}\right).

Therefore, problem (
20
) can be reduced to problem (
22
).
First, we consider the case in which
\mu
has a compact support, hence the minimization problem is well defined over the closed set
[0,1]^{m}
, since the values
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(0)
and
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(1)
are well-defined.
Notice that
[0,1]^{m}
is compact, thus if we show that the objective function of the problem is continuous with respect to
\vec{v}
we conclude the proof for this case.
Let
\vec{v}^{(n)}
be a succession of feasible vectors that converges to
\vec{v}
.
From our basic assumptions, we have that both
F_{\mu}
and
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}
are continuous, hence
\vec{v}\to\eta_{j}(\vec{v})
is continuous for every
j\in[m]
.
Likewise, every function
\vec{v}\to y_{j}(\vec{v}):=F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})
is continuous, thus the sequence
\eta_{n}=\eta(\vec{v}^{(n)})
weakly converges to
\eta(\vec{v})
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
.
Since
W_{1}
induces the weak topology over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
(?), we infer
\lim_{n\to\infty}W_{1}(\mu,\eta(\vec{v}^{(n)}))=W_{1}(\mu,\eta(\vec{v}))
, thus a minimizer always exists.
To conclude the proof, we need to show that the minimizer, namely
\vec{v}
, is such that
v_{j}\neq 0,1
for every
j\in[m]
.
Toward a contradiction, let us assume that
v_{1}=0
.
Without loss of generality, let us assume that
v_{2}>0
, thus
y_{1}<y_{2}
.
By definition, we have that
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{1})=a
and
F_{\mu}(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{1}))=v_{1}=0
.
Let us denote with
[a,b]
the support of
\mu
, then there exists a
\epsilon>0
such that
a+\epsilon<F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{2})=:y_{2}
, thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\min_{j\in[m]}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})|d\mu=\int^{\frac{a+y_{2}}{2}}_{a}(x-a)d\mu+\sum_{j>1}^{m}\int_{\frac{a+y_{2}}{2}}^{b}\min_{j>1}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})|d\mu

\displaystyle>\int_{a}^{\frac{a+y_{2}}{2}}|x-(a+\epsilon)|d\mu+\sum_{j>1}^{m}\int_{\frac{a+y_{2}}{2}}^{b}\min_{j>1}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})|d\mu

\displaystyle>\int_{a}^{b}\min_{j\in[m]}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v^{(\epsilon)}_{j})|d\mu=\mathcal{W}(\vec{v}^{(\epsilon)}),

where
\vec{v}^{(\epsilon)}=(F_{\mu}(a+\epsilon),v_{2},\dots,v_{m})
, which contradicts the optimality of
\vec{v}
.

Let us now consider the case in which
\mu
has unbounded support.
Let
\vec{v}^{(n)}
be a minimizing sequence.
Up to a sub-sequence, we can assume that the
\vec{v}^{(n)}
converges to
\vec{v}
in
[0,1]^{m}
.
We now show that
\vec{v}\in(0,1)^{m}
.
First, let us assume that there exists a
j\in[m]
for which
v_{j}\in(0,1)
.
Without loss of generality, let us assume that
v_{1}=0
and that
0<v_{2}<1
(if there are multiple entries of
\vec{v}
that are equal to
0
the study is the same).
Let us now consider
\vec{w}^{(n)}
be such that
w^{(n)}_{1}=v_{1}^{(n)}
and
w_{j}^{(n)}=v_{j}
for
j>1
.
Since
\vec{w}^{(n)}
and
\vec{v}^{(n)}
have the same limit and
\mathcal{W}
is continuous, we have that
\vec{w}^{(n)}
is a minimizing sequence.
Since
w^{(n)}_{1}\to 0
, we have that
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(w_{1}^{(n)})\to-\infty
, thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\vec{w}^{(n)})=\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{y_{1}^{(n)}+y_{2}}{2}}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(w_{1}^{(n)})|d\mu+\int_{\frac{y_{1}^{(n)}+y_{2}}{2}}^{+\infty}\min_{j>1}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})|d\mu.

Since
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(w_{1}^{(n)})\to-\infty
, we have that
\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\min_{j>1}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})|d\mu
.
Let us consider the vector
\vec{v}^{(\epsilon)}=(\epsilon,v_{2},\dots,v_{m})
, where
v_{2}>\epsilon>0
, then it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\min_{j>1}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{j})|d\mu>\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\min_{j\in[m]}|x-F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v^{(\epsilon)}_{j})|d\mu=\mathcal{W}(\vec{v}^{(\epsilon)}),

which contradicts the optimality of
\vec{v}
.
Similarly, we deal with the case
\vec{v}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and the case in which the support of
\mu
is unbounded only from one side.
∎

Notice that the limit Bayesian approximation ratio of the optimal ERM does not necessarily converge to
1
, as the next example shows.

Example 1
.

Let
\mu
be the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
and let
\vec{q}=(0.8,0.4)
be a p.c.v..
Since
\mu
is symmetric, one of the solutions to problem (
8
) is
\nu_{2}=(0.4)\delta_{0.2}+(0.6)\delta_{0.7}
(the other one is
\nu_{2}^{\prime}=(0.6)\delta_{0.3}+(0.4)\delta_{0.8}
).
However, by Corollary
1
, there does not exist a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
such that
\mathtt{ERM}^{((0.2,0.7),\pi)}
is feasible.
Thus, by Theorem
3
, no feasible ERM is such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{ar,1}(\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})})=1
.

We now characterize the optimal ERM in two specific cases.
In the first one, the total capacity of the facilities is the same as the number of agents.
In the second one, we need to place two capacitated facilities and
\mu
is symmetric.

5.1
The No-Spare Capacity Case

In the no-spare capacity case the total capacity of the facilities matches the number of agents, thus
\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}=1
.
Due to Corollary
1
, feasible ERMs are induced by vectors
\vec{v}
such that
v_{j}=p\in[0,1]
for every
j\in[m]
.
This property allows to characterize the optimal ERM with respect to the Social, Maximum, and
l_{2}
costs.

Theorem 6
.

In the no-spare capacity case, the optimal ERM is unique.
Moreover, we have that

(i)

the
all-median
mechanism, which places all the facilities at the median agent, is optimal with respect to the Social Cost,

(ii)

the
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is optimal with respect to the
l_{2}
cost if
\vec{v}=(F_{\mu}(m),\dots,F_{\mu}(m))
where
m
is the mean of
\mu
, and

(iii)

if
\mu
has compact support, then
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
is optimal with respect to the Maximum Cost if
\vec{v}=(F_{\mu}(\frac{a+b}{2}),\dots,F_{\mu}(\frac{a+b}{2}))
where
a=\inf\{x\in spt(\mu)\}
and
b=\sup\{x\in spt(\mu)\}
.

Proof.

Owing to Corollary
1
, we have that any truthful ERM, namely
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
, must be such that
\vec{v}=(p,p,\dots,p)
where
p\in(0,1)
.
Thus, to complete the proof it is sufficient to prove that the function
M:y\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-y|d\mu
is decreasing on
(-\infty,med(\mu))
and increasing on
(med(\mu),+\infty)
.
Indeed, let us assume that
y\in(-\infty,med(\mu))
and let us consider
\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-(y+h)|d\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-y|d\mu}{h}
.
By the linearity of the integral, we have that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-y|d\mu=\int_{-\infty}^{y}(y-x)d\mu+\int_{y}^{+\infty}(x-y)d\mu
, similarly
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-(y+h)|d\mu=\int_{-\infty}^{y+h}(y+h-x)d\mu+\int_{y+h}^{+\infty}(x-y-h)d\mu
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-(y+h)|d\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-y|d\mu

\displaystyle=2\int_{y}^{y+h}(y-x)d\mu+\int_{-\infty}^{y}hd\mu-\int_{y}^{+\infty}hd\mu

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{y}hd\mu-\int_{y}^{+\infty}hd\mu,

thus
\partial_{y}M(y)=F_{\mu}(y)-(1-F_{\mu}(y))
.
Since
y<med(\mu)
, we infer that
\partial_{y}M(y)<0
.
Similarly, it holds
\partial_{y}M(y)>0
if
y>med(\mu)
, which concludes the proof for the Social Cost.
The results for the
l_{2}
costs and the Maximum Cost follow by a similar argument.
∎

When the agents are distributed following a uniform distribution, that is
\mu=\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, we can express the limit Bayesian approximation ratio of the
all-median
mechanism as a function of
\vec{q}
.
Indeed, since
\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}=1
, any solution to problem (
8
) separates the interval
[0,1]
into
m
intervals whose length is
q_{j}
.
Notice that the order in which
[0,1]
is divided is irrelevant.
Furthermore, the facility is placed at the median of such interval, thus the objective value of (
8
) is
\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}^{2}
.
By Theorem
3
, the limit Bayesian approximation ratio of the
all-median
mechanism is
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{ar,1}(\texttt{all-median})=(\sum_{j\in[m]}q_{j}^{2})^{-1}
, since the asymptotic cost of placing all the facilities at the median point is
\int_{0}^{1}|x-0.5|dx=\frac{1}{4}
.
The limit Bayesian approximation ratio gets closer to
1
as the values of
\vec{q}
become concentrated at one index.
Conversely, if all the facilities have the same capacity
\frac{1}{m}
, the Bayesian approximation ratio of the
all-median
becomes the largest possible, that is
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{ar,1}(\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})})=m
.

5.2
The optimal ERM for two facilities and symmetric distributions – The Social Cost case

In this section, we retrieve the optimal ERMs with respect to the Social Cost when
m=2
and
\mu
is symmetric.
Since
\mu
is symmetric, the asymptotic expected Social Cost of any feasible
\mathtt{ERM}^{(\pi,\vec{v})}
does not depend on
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
For this reason, we fix
\pi=Id
and search for the vector
\vec{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})
that induces the optimal ERM.
First, we rewrite the objective value of problem (
20
) in terms of
y_{i}=F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{i})
for
i=1,2
.
Since
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}
is monotone, we have that
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
.
We then set

\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=W_{1}\bigg(\mu,F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)\delta_{y_{1}}+\Big(1-F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)\Big)\delta_{y_{2}}\bigg).

(23)

Since both
F_{\mu}
and
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}
are bijective, any
\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
identifies a unique
\vec{v}
and vice-versa.
Using the properties of the Wasserstein distances on the line (?), we have
\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}|x-y_{1}|\rho_{\mu}(x)dx+\int_{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}^{+\infty}|x-y_{2}|\rho_{\mu}(x)dx
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mu
be a symmetric probability measure.
Then,
\mathcal{W}
is differentiable and its gradient is defined by the formula

\nabla\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=\Big(2F_{\mu}(y_{1})-F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big),2F_{\mu}(y_{2})-1-F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)\Big).

Furthermore, we have
\nabla\mathcal{W}(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(0.25),F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(0.75))=(0,0)
.

Proof.

Let
\vec{q}
be a p.c.v., due to the symmetry of
\mu
, we assume that
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
are the positions of the facility and that
y_{1}
has capacity
q_{1}
and
y_{2}
has capacity
q_{2}
.
We then define

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{y_{1}}(y_{1}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx+\int_{y_{1}}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}(x-y_{1})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}^{y_{2}}(y_{2}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx+\int_{y_{2}}^{+\infty}(x-y_{2})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx,

thus we have
\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=A(y_{1},y_{2})+B(y_{1},y_{2})+C(y_{1},y_{2})+D(y_{1},y_{2})
, where

\displaystyle A(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{y_{1}}(y_{1}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx,\quad

\displaystyle B(y_{1},y_{2})=\int_{y_{1}}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}(x-y_{1})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx,

\displaystyle C(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}^{y_{2}}(y_{2}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx,\quad

\displaystyle D(y_{1},y_{2})=\int_{y_{2}}^{+\infty}(x-y_{2})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx.

We now compute the derivative of
\mathcal{W}
with respect to
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
.
Let us consider the derivative
\partial_{y_{1}}\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})
.
Since
\partial_{y_{1}}D(y_{1},y_{2})=0
, we have
\partial_{y_{1}}\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=\partial_{y_{1}}A(y_{1},y_{2})+\partial_{y_{1}}B(y_{1},y_{2})+\partial_{y_{1}}C(y_{1},y_{2})
.
First, let us consider
\partial_{y_{1}}A(y_{1},y_{2})
.
We have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{y_{1}+h}(y_{1}+h-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{y_{1}}(y_{1}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\Big(h\int_{-\infty}^{y_{1}+h}\rho_{\mu}(x)dx-\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{1}+h}(y_{1}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{y_{1}+h}\rho_{\mu}(x)dx+\frac{1}{h}\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{1}+h}(y_{1}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx.

By the Lebesgue’s theorem,
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{1}+h}(y_{1}-x)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx=0
, thus
\partial_{y_{1}}A(y_{1},y_{2})=F_{\mu}(y_{1})
.

Second, let us compute
\partial_{y_{1}}B(y_{1},y_{2})
.
We have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Big(

\displaystyle\int_{y_{1}+h}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}+\frac{h}{2}}(x-y_{1}-h)\rho_{\mu}(x)dx-\int_{y_{1}}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}(x-y_{1})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\Big(-h\int_{y_{1}+h}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}+\frac{h}{2}}\rho_{\mu}(x)dx+\int_{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}}^{\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}+\frac{h}{2}}(x-y_{1})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx-\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{1}+h}(x-y_{1})\rho_{\mu}(x)dx\Big).

Owing again to Lebesgue’s theorem, we have
\partial_{y_{1}}B(y_{1},y_{2})=-\Big(F_{\mu}\big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\big)-F_{\mu}(y_{1})\Big)+\frac{(y_{2}-y_{1})}{4}\rho_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)
.
By a similar computation, we have that
\partial_{y_{1}}C(y_{1},y_{2})=-\frac{y_{2}-y_{1}}{4}\rho_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)
.
Putting all together, we infer
\partial_{y_{1}}\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=2F_{\mu}(y_{1})-F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)
.
Similarly, we have that
\partial_{y_{2}}\mathcal{W}(y_{1},y_{2})=2F_{\mu}(y_{2})-1-F_{\mu}\Big(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\Big)
,
which concludes the first part of the proof.
Finally, due to the properties of cumulative distribution functions of symmetric measures, we infer that
\nabla\mathcal{W}\big(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(0.25),F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(0.75)\big)=(0,0)
.
∎

Now that we have an explicit formula for the gradient of
\mathcal{W}
, we need to express the set of feasible
\vec{y}
.
From Theorem
1
, we have that
v_{1}\leq q_{1}
and
1-v_{2}\leq q_{2}
, i.e.
1-q_{2}\leq v_{2}
.
Since
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}
is monotone, we have that
y_{2}=F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(v_{2})\leq F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(q_{1})
and that
F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(1-q_{2})\leq y_{1}
.
Therefore we have that the set of feasible
\vec{y}
lays in a triangle, namely
T(\vec{q})
, whose vertexes are
(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(1-q_{2}),F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(q_{1}))
,
(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(q_{1}),F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(q_{1}))
, and
(F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(1-q_{2}),F_{\mu}^{[-1]}(1-q_{2}))
.
To exemplify this procedure, we carry out the computation for the Uniform Distribution.

The uniform distribution.

Theorem
7
allows us to fully characterize the optimal ERM with respect to the Social Cost when
\mu
is a uniform distribution.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\vec{q}
be a p.c.v. such that
q_{2}\leq q_{1}
, then the optimal ERM for a uniform distribution is unique and induced by
(Id,\vec{v})
where

(i)

\vec{v}=(0.25,0.75)
if
q_{2}\geq 0.75
,

(ii)

\vec{v}=(1-q_{2},q_{1})
if
3q_{2}-2-q_{1}\leq 0
, and

(iii)

\vec{v}=(1-q_{2},1-\frac{q_{2}}{3})
otherwise.

Proof.

Notice that
\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})=\int_{0}^{1}\min\{|x-v_{1}|,|x-v_{2}|\}dx
, that is
\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})=\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{(1-v_{2})^{2}}{2}+\frac{(v_{2}-v_{1})^{2}}{4}
, hence
\nabla\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})=\frac{1}{2}\big(3v_{1}-v_{2},3v_{2}-2-v_{1}\big)
.
Moreover, a simple computation allows us to compute the Hessian of
\mathcal{W}
, that is

H\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\
-1&3\end{pmatrix}.

Since
H\mathcal{W}
is definite positive, we have that if
q_{2}\geq 0.75
, then
(0.25,0.75)
is the minimum.

We now consider the other cases, i.e.
q_{2}\leq 0.75
.
Notice that the set of points on which the first derivative of
\mathcal{W}
nullifies is the line
v_{2}=3v_{1}
, while the set of points on which the second derivative of
\mathcal{W}
nullifies is
v_{2}=\frac{1}{3}(v_{1}+2)
, thus
\nabla\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})=(0,0)
if and only if
\vec{v}=(0.25,0.75)
.
Therefore, the solution must lay on the border of the feasible
\vec{v}
, which is the triangle, namely
T(\vec{q})
, whose vertexes are
(q_{1},q_{1})
,
(1-q_{2},1-q_{2})
, and
(1-q_{2},q_{1})
.
If
3q_{2}-2-q_{1}\leq 0
, we have that
\partial_{y_{1}}\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})>0
and
\partial_{y_{2}}\mathcal{W}(\vec{v})<0
.
Thus the gradient cannot be perpendicular to either
\{(1-q_{2},t)\;\text{s.t.}\;t\in[1-q_{2},q_{1}]\}
and
\{(t,q_{1})\;\text{s.t.}\;t\in[1-q_{2},q_{1}]\}
, hence the minimum is at the vertex
(1-q_{2},q_{1})
, which concludes the proof for this case.
In the remaining cases, we have that the boundary of
T(\vec{q})
intersect the line on which
\partial_{y_{2}}\mathcal{W}=0
.
In particular,
\{\partial_{y_{2}}\mathcal{W}=0\}
intersects with the edge
\{(1-q_{2},t)\;\text{s.t.}\;t\in[1-q_{2},q_{1}]\}
at the point
(1-q_{2},1-\frac{q_{2}}{3})
.
On such point, we have that the gradient is perpendicular to the boundary of
T(\vec{q})
and points inward, thus is a relative minimum.
To conclude, we show that this is the only possible relative minimum.
Indeed, it is easy to see that if the boundary of
T(\vec{q})
intersect the line
v_{2}=3v_{1}
, the gradient points outward due to the sign of
\nabla\mathcal{W}
.
∎

First, we consider the case in which

In particular, to find a solution to (

\min_{\pi\in\{Id,\sigma\}}\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi,\vec{q}}(\mathbb{R})}W_{\infty}(\mu,\zeta),

5.4
Optimal Extended Ranking Mechanisms in the Generic Framework

Lastly, we propose a sufficient and necessary condition that guarantees the existence of an optimal ERM whose limit Bayesian approximation ratio converges to
1
.
Noticeably this criteria is applicable to every cost function, every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, and every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 11
.

Given
\mu
and
\vec{q}
, let
(\pi,\nu_{m})
be a solution to Problem (
8
).
We denote with
\{y_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
the points in the support of
\nu_{m}
ordered non-decreasingly.
Moreover, we set
y_{0}=-\infty
and
y_{m+1}=+\infty
.
Let
\zeta_{j}=F_{\mu}(y_{j+1})-F_{\mu}(y_{j-1})
and let
\hat{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
be a permutation that orders the values
\zeta_{j}
in a non-increasing order.
Then, the optimal ERM has limit Bayesian approximation ratio equal to
1
if and only if
\zeta_{\hat{\pi}(j)}\leq q_{j}
for every
j\in[m]
.

Proof.

Let
(\sigma,\nu_{m})
be a solution to (
8
).
Let us define
v_{j}=F_{\mu}(y_{j})
and let
\gamma=\hat{\pi}^{-1}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
.
By definition of
v_{j}
, we have that
v_{j+1}-v_{j-1}=F_{\mu}(y_{j+1})-F_{\mu}(y_{j-1})\leq q_{\gamma(j)}
for every
j\in[m]
, hence
\texttt{ERM}^{(\gamma,\vec{v})}
is feasible (Theorem
1
).
Owing to Theorem
3
, the asymptotic expected Social Cost of
\texttt{ERM}^{(\gamma,\vec{v})}
converges to
W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})
, where
\nu_{\vec{v}}
is a solution to

\min_{\lambda_{j}\leq q_{\gamma(j)},\sum_{j\in[m]\lambda_{j}=1}}W_{1}\Big(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}\Big)

(25)

since
v_{j}=F_{\mu}(y_{j})
.
To conclude, we need to show that
(\gamma,\nu_{\vec{v}})
is a solution to (
8
).
Since
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
is optimal, it suffices to show that
\min_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma,m}(\mathbb{R})}W_{1}(\mu,\zeta)\geq W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})
.
First, let us consider the following minimization problem

\min_{\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}=1}W_{1}\Big(\mu,\sum_{j\in[m]}\lambda_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}\Big),

(26)

and let us set
\nu=\sum_{j\in[m]}(F_{\mu}(z_{j})-F_{\mu}(z_{j-1}))\delta_{y_{j}}
, where
z_{j}=\frac{y_{j}+y_{j+1}}{2}
for every
j\in[m-1]
,
z_{0}=-\infty
, and
z_{m}=+\infty
.
Since the optimal transportation plan between two measures on a line is monotone (?), we infer
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\min_{j\in[m]}|x-y_{j}|\,d\mu
,
thus
\nu
is a minimizer of problem (
26
).
Finally, since
F_{\mu}
is monotone increasing, it holds
F_{\mu}(z_{j})-F_{\mu}(z_{j-1})\leq F_{\mu}(y_{j+1})-F_{\mu}(y_{j-1})\leq q_{\gamma(j)}
.
In particular,
\nu
is feasible for problem (
25
), thus
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})
.
Likewise,
\nu_{m}
is feasible for problem (
26
), thus
W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{\vec{v}})=W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{1}(\mu,\nu_{m})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

That is, we calculate the relative error as
err_{rel}=\frac{\text{empirical }B_{ar}-\text{limit of }B_{ar}}{\text{limit of }B_{ar}}.

Figure
3
(and Table
3
in Appendix
A
) shows the relative error for the six cases.
Each average is computed over
500
instances.
We observe that the relative error decreases as the number of agents increases, regardless of the distribution or the p.c.v.
\vec{q}
.
Moreover, in all cases but
\vec{q}=(0.8,0.4),(0.85,0.35)
, the relative errors of the ERM are less than
0.05
as long as
n\geq 20
, which validates Theorem
3
as a tool to predict the Bayesian approximation ratio for small values of
n
.
In other cases, we observe a slower convergence, as the percentage error is slightly larger and its highest value is
0.33
.
Lastly, we point out that the results for the
l_{2}
cost show no substantial differences from what we have observed for the Social cost.
For the sake of completeness, we report in Figure
9
and Table
6
in Appendix
B
the relative error under
l_{2}

In this section, we report the results of our experiments for the Maximum Cost.

Comparison with the EEM and the IG.

We first consider the case of balanced capacities, i.e.
\vec{q}=(q,q)
, in which we are able to compare the ERM to the IG and to the EEM.
We consider the best ERM obtained through Theorem
10
.
Figure
4
(and Table
7
in Appendix
C
) shows the average and the CI of the Bayesian approximation ratio of all the three mechanisms.
For the balanced case, we observe that the results for the Maximum Cost are in line with what observed for the Social and
l_{2}
costs: the best ERM has the lowest (or tied for lowest) Bayesian approximation ratio most of the cases.
It is worth to notice that the IG and the best ERM have again the same limit.

We then consider the unbalanced case, where we can compare the best ERM only to the EEM.
Figure
5
(and Table
8
in Appendix
C
) shows the results on unbalanced cases.
In this case, we observe that the best ERM is outperformed by the EEM only when the distribution is symmetric and
q_{2}
is much lower than
q_{1}
.
Indeed, for such instances, the Bayesian approximation ratio of the EEM is lower than the one achieved by the best ERM.
Moreover the CI of the EEM is tighter than the CI of the best ERM.
In all other cases, the best ERM outperforms the EEM, in line with what we observed in all other cases.

7
Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we introduced the Extended Ranking Mechanisms, a generalization of Ranking Mechanisms introduced in (?).
After establishing the conditions under which ERMs remain truthful, we characterized the limit Bayesian approximation ratio of truthful ERM in terms of the probability distribution
\mu
, the p.c.v.
\vec{q}
, and the mechanism’s parameters, namely
\pi
and
\vec{v}
.
We have shown that, given
\mu
and
\vec{q}
, there exists an optimal ERM and characterized it via a minimization problem, which we solved in two relevant frameworks.
Lastly, we conducted extensive numerical experiments to validate our findings, from which we inferred that a well-tuned ERM consistently outperforms all other known mechanisms.
An interesting extension would be to extend the ERMs to handle problems in higher dimensions using the decomposition proposed in (?).
Lastly, we plan to study the connections between the Optimal Transportation problem and other Mechanism Design problems.
