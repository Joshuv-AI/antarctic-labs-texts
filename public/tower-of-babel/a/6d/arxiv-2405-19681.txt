Bayesian Online Natural Gradient ( bong )
Matt Jones, Peter Chang, Kevin Murphy

Abstract
We propose a novel approach to sequential Bayesian inference based on variational Bayes (VB). The key insight is that, in the online setting, we do not need to add the KL term to regularize to the prior (which comes from the posterior at the previous timestep); instead we can optimize just the expected log-likelihood, performing a single step of natural gradient descent starting at the prior predictive. We prove this method recovers exact Bayesian inference if the model is conjugate. We also show how to compute an efficient deterministic approximation to the VB objective, as well as our simplified objective, when the variational distribution is Gaussian or a sub-family, including the case of a diagonal plus low-rank precision matrix. We show empirically that our method outperforms other online VB methods in the non-conjugate setting, such as online learning for neural networks, especially when controlling for computational costs.



1
Introduction

Bayesian methods for neural network (NN) training aim to minimize the
Kullback-Leibler divergence between true and estimated posterior distributions.
This is equivalent to minimizing the variational loss (or negative
ELBO)

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi})=-\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\log p(\mathcal{D}|{\bm{\theta}})}\right]+D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}}}|{p_{0}}\right)

(1)

Here
{\bm{\theta}}
are the network parameters,

\bm{\psi}
are the variational parameters of the approximate posterior

q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})
,

\mathcal{D}
is the training dataset,
and
p_{0}({\bm{\theta}})
is the prior.
The two terms in the variational loss correspond to data fit and regularization to the prior,
the latter being analogous to a regularizer

r({\bm{\theta}})=-\log p_{0}({\bm{\theta}})

in traditional point estimation methods like SGD.

An important set of approaches learns the variational parameters by gradient descent on
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi})

(
Blundell et al., 2015
)
.
More recently Khan and colleagues
(
Khan et al., 2018b
;
Khan and Rue, 2023
;
Shen et al., 2024
)
have proposed using the natural gradient

\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}}^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi})

where
\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}}
is the Fisher information matrix of the variational family evaluated at
q_{\bm{\psi}}
.
Natural gradient descent (NGD) is often more efficient than vanilla GD because it accounts for the intrinsic geometry of the variational family
(
Amari, 1998
)
.

Khan and Rue (2023)
call this approach the "Bayesian Learning Rule" or BLR. Using various choices for the variational distribution,
generalized losses replacing negative log-likelihood,
and other approximations, they reproduce many standard optimization methods such as Adam,
and derive new ones.

We study Bayesian NN optimization in online learning, where the data are observed in sequence,

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{({\bm{x}}_{k},{\bm{y}}_{k})_{k=1}^{t}\}
,
and the algorithm maintains an approximate posterior

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\approx p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\mathcal{D}_{t})
,
which it updates at each step.
Fast updates (in terms of both computational speed and statistical efficiency) are critical for many online learning applications

(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
.
To allow for nonstationarity in the datastream,
we include a time index on
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
,
to represent that the parameters may change over time,
as is standard for approaches based on state-space models and the extended Kalman filter
(see e.g.,
(
Sarkka and Svensson, 2023
)
).
The belief state is updated recursively using the prior

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}

derived from the previous time step so that the variational loss becomes

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi}_{t})=-\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}}}\!\left[{\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})}\right]+D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

(2)

One option for this online learning problem is to apply
NGD on
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi}_{t})

at each time step, iterating until
\bm{\psi}_{t}
converges before consuming the next observation.
Our first contribution is a proposal for skipping this inner loop by
(a) performing a single natural gradient step with unit learning rate and
(b) omitting the
D_{\mathbb{KL}}
term in
Eq.

2
so that learning is based only on expected loglikelihood:

\bm{\psi}_{t}=\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)}\right]

(3)

These two modifications work together: instead of regularizing toward the prior explicitly using

D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)
,
we do so implicitly by using
\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}
as the starting point of our single natural gradient step.
This may appear as a heuristic but we prove in
Proposition

4.1
that it yields exact Bayesian inference when

q_{\bm{\psi}}
and
p\left({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}\right)
are conjugate
and
q_{\bm{\psi}}
is an exponential family with natural parameter
\bm{\psi}
.
Thus our proposed update can be viewed as a relaxation of the Bayesian update to the non-conjugate variational case.
As is common in work on variational inference, we view the result for the conjugate case as a motivating foundation that ensures our method is exact in certain simple settings.
The experiments reported in
Section

5
and
Appendix

B
complement the theory by showing our method also works well in more general settings.
We call
Eq.

3
the Bayesian online natural gradient (
bong
).

Our second contribution concerns ways of computing
the expectation in
Eqs.

1
,
2
and
3
.
This is intractable for NNs,
even for variational distributions that are easy to compute,
since
the likelihood takes the form

p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t}))

with
f({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
, representing the function computed by the network,
is a complex, nonlinear function of
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
.
Many previous approaches have approximated the expected loglikelihood by sampling methods which add variance and computation time depending on the number of samples
(
Blundell et al., 2015
;
Shen et al., 2024
)
.
We propose a deterministic, closed-form update that applies when the variational distribution is Gaussian (or a sub-family) and the likelihood is an exponential family with natural parameter

f({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

and mean parameter

h({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

(e.g., for classification,
f
returns the vector of class logits,
h
returns class probabilities, and
h={\rm softmax}(f)
).
This update can be derived in two equivalent ways.
First, we use a local linear approximation of the network

h({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})\approx\bar{h}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

(
Immer et al., 2021a
)
and a Gaussian approximation of the likelihood

\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}}_{t}|\bar{h}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}),\mathbf{R}_{t})

(
Ollivier, 2018
;
Tronarp et al., 2018
)
.
Under these assumptions the expectation in
Eq.

3
can be calculated analytically.
Alternatively, we use a different linear approximation

f({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})\approx\bar{f}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

and a delta approximation

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\approx\delta_{\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

where

\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}=\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\right]

is the prior mean, so that the expectation in
Eq.

3
is replaced by a plugin prediction.
The linear(
h
)-Gaussian approximation is previously known but the linear(
f
)-delta approximation is new, and
we prove in
Proposition

4.2
that they yield the same update,
which we call linearized
bong
, or
bong-lin
.

Finally, we discuss different ways of approximating the Hessian of the objective, which is needed for NGD.

Our
bong
framework unifies several existing methods for Bayesian online learning,
and it offers new algorithms based on alternative variational families or parameterizations.
We define a large space of methods by

combining 4 different update rules
with 4 different ways of computing the relevant
expected gradients and Hessians
and 3 different variational families
(Gaussians with full, diagonal, and
diagonal-plus-low rank precision matrices).

We conduct experiments systematically testing how these factors affect performance.
We find support for all three principles of our approach—
NGD, implicit regularization to the prior, and linearization—
in terms of both statistical and computational efficiency.
Code for our experiments is available at https://github.com/petergchang/bong/.

2
Related work

Variational inference approximates the Bayesian posterior from within some suitable family in a way that bypasses the normalization term
(
Zellner, 1988
;
Jordan et al., 1999
)
.
A common choice for the variational family is a Gaussian.
For online learning, the exact update equations for Gaussian variational filtering are given by the
rvga
method of
(
Lambert et al., 2021
)
.
This update is implicit but can be approximated by an explicit
rvga
update which we show arises as a special case of
bong
.
Most applications of Gaussian VI use a mean-field approximation defined by diagonal covariance, which scales linearly with model size. More expressive but still linear in the model size are methods that express the covariance
(
Tomczak et al., 2020
)
or precision
(
Mishkin et al., 2018
;
Lambert et al., 2023
;
Chang et al., 2023
)
as a sum of diagonal and low rank matrices (DLR).
In this paper, we consider variational families defined by full covariance,
diagonal covariance, and DLR covariance.

For NNs and other complicated models, even the variational approximation can be intractable, so methods have been developed to approximately minimize the VI loss.
Bayes by backprop (
bbb
)
(
Blundell et al., 2015
)
learns a variational distribution on NN weights by iterated GD on the VI loss of
Eq.

1
. They focus on mean-field Gaussian approximations but the approach also applies to other variational families. Here we adapt
bbb
to online learning to compare to our methods.

The Bayesian learning rule (
blr
) replaces
bbb
’s GD with NGD
(
Khan and Rue, 2023
)
.
Several variants of
blr
have been developed including
von
and
vogn
for a mean-field Gaussian prior
(
Khan et al., 2018b
)
and
slang
for a
dlr
Gaussian
(
Mishkin et al., 2018
)
.

Blr
has also been used to derive versions of many classic optimizers
including SGD, RMSprop and Adam
(
Khan et al., 2018a
;
Khan and Rue, 2023
;
Lin et al., 2024
;
Shen et al., 2024
)
.
Although
blr
has been applied to online learning,
we are particularly interested in Bayesian filtering
including in nonstationary environments,
where observations must be processed one at time
and updates are based on the posterior from the previous step,
often in conjunction with parameter dynamics.
We therefore develop filtering versions of
blr
to compare to
bong
,
some of which reduce to
von
,
vogn
and
slang
in the batch setting,
while others are novel.
We also note
blr
is a mature theory including several clever tricks we have not yet incorporated into our framework.

Khan and Rue (2023)
observe that conjugate updating is equivalent to one step of
blr
with learning rate 1. This is similar to our
Proposition

4.1
except that
blr
retains the KL term in the variational loss.
Blr
and
bong
agree in this case because the gradient of the KL is zero on
blr
’s first iteration:
\nabla_{\bm{\psi}=\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)=0
. Therefore
bong
can be seen as a special case of
blr
with one update step per observation and learning rate 1. Our contribution is to recognize that doing a single update step allows the KL term to be dropped entirely, yielding a substantially simpler algorithm which our experiments show also performs better.

While
blr
allows alternative losses in place of the NLL in
Eq.

2
,
we can also replacing the KL term with other divergences
(
Knoblauch et al., 2022
)
. Our approach fits within that "generalized VB"
framework in that it drops the divergence altogether.
Our approach of implicitly regularizing to the prior using a single NGD step is also similar to the implicit MAP filter of
(
Bencomo et al., 2023
)
which performs truncated GD from the prior mode. The principal difference is they perform GD on model parameters (
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
) while we do NGD on the variational parameters (
\bm{\psi}_{t}
). Thus
bong
maintains a full prior and posterior while IMAP is more concerned with how the choice of optimizer can substitute for explicit tracking of covariance.

We show two other ways to derive the
bong
update in
Appendix

D
, one of which is to replace the expected NLL
in
Eq.

2
with a linear approximation and solve the resulting equation exactly.
Several past works have taken this approach, arriving at updates similar to ours.

Chérief-Abdellatif et al. (2019)
study streaming variational Bayes and propose solving
Eq.

2
with a linearized expected NLL. When the variational family is an exponential family their update becomes NGD
(
Khan and Lin, 2017
)
and matches the
bong
update.

Hoeven et al. (2018)
show how mirror descent can be derived as a special case of Exponential Weights
(
Littlestone and Warmuth, 1994
)
, which is closely related to Bayesian updating. The resulting algorithm is similar to
bong
and follows from linearizing the NLL instead of expected NLL, with an additional delta assumption at the prior mean.

Lyu and Tsang (2021)
study relaxed block-box optimization where the objective is
\arg\min_{\bm{\psi}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{x}}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{f({\bm{x}})}\right]
for some target function
f
. They use a mirror descent formulation with linearized expected loss and KL regularizer and show the resulting update is NGD on expected loss, formally equivalent to our BONG update.
From the perspective of this prior work, our contribution is to express the
bong
update simply as NGD on the expected NLL, motivated by replacing the KL with implicit regularization, and to show how this yields a variety of known and novel algorithms for Bayesian filtering.

EKF applications to NNs apply Bayesian filtering using a local linear approximation of the network, leading to simple closed form updates
(
Singhal and Wu, 1989
;
Puskorius and Feldkamp, 1991
)
.
The classic EKF assumes a Gaussian observation distribution but it has been extended to other exponential families (e.g. for classification) by matching the mean and covariance in what we call the conditional moments EKF (
cm-ekf
)
(
Ollivier, 2018
;
Tronarp et al., 2018
)
.
Applying a KL projection to diagonal covariance yields the variational diagonal EKF (
vd-ekf
)
(
Chang et al., 2022
)
. Alternatively, projecting to diagonal plus low rank
precision
using SVD gives
lo-fi

(
Chang et al., 2023
)
. We derive all these methods as special cases of
bong-lin
.
Further developments in this direction include the method of
(
Titsias et al., 2024
)
which does Bayesian filtering on only the final weight layer, and WoLF
(
Duran-Martin et al., 2024
)
which achieves robustness to outliers through data-dependent weighting of the loglikelihood.

Background

We study online supervised learning where the agent receives input
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\real^{D}
and observation
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{C}
on each time step,
which it aims to model with a function
f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=f({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
such as a NN with weights
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in\real^{P}
.
The predictions for
{\bm{y}}_{t}
are given by some observation distribution

p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))
.
For example,
f
may compute the mean for regression or the class logits for classification.

We work in a Bayesian framework where the agent maintains an approximate posterior distribution over
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
after observing data

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\left\{({\bm{x}}_{k},{\bm{y}}_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{t}\}
.
The filtering posterior

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\approx p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\mathcal{D}_{t})

is approximated within some parametric family indexed by the variational parameter
\bm{\psi}_{t}
.
We allow for nonstationarity by assuming
{\bm{\theta}}
changes over time according to some dynamic model
p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})
.
By pushing the posterior from step
t-1
through the dynamics we obtain a prior for step
t
given by

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\approx p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\mathcal{D}_{t-1})
.
For example suppose the variational posterior
from the previous step is Gaussian,

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}|\bm{\mu}_{t-1},\bm{\Sigma}_{t-1})
,
and the dynamics model is an
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process,
as proposed in prior work

(
Kurle et al., 2020
;
Titsias et al., 2024
)

to handle non-stationarity,
i.e., the dynamics model has the form

{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\gamma_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}+(1-\gamma_{t})\bm{\mu}_{0},\mathbf{Q}_{t})
,
where
\mathbf{Q}_{t}=(1-\gamma_{t}^{2})\bm{\Sigma}_{0}
is the covariance of the noise process,

0\leq\gamma_{t}\leq 1
is the degree of drift,
and
p({\bm{\theta}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
is the prior.
In this case,
the parameters of the prior
predictive distribution are

\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}=\gamma_{t}\bm{\mu}_{t-1}+(1-\gamma_{t})\bm{\mu}_{0}

and

\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}=\gamma_{t}^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{t-1}+\mathbf{Q}_{t}
.
In general the predict step may require approximation to stay in the variational family
(e.g., if the dynamics are nonlinear).
In this paper,
our focus is the update step from
\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}
to
\bm{\psi}_{t}
upon observing
({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{t})
,
so for simplicity we assume
constant (static) parameters,
i.e.,
p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{-1})=\delta({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})
(equivalently
\gamma_{t}=1
), so
\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}=\bm{\psi}_{t-1}
;
however, our method can trivially handle non-stationary parameters.

Variational inference seeks an approximate posterior that minimizes the KL divergence from the exact Bayesian update from the prior.
In the online setting this becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{*}_{t}=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\arg\min}\,D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}|{Z_{t}^{-1}q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\,p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))}\right)=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\arg\min}\,\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{\psi})

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is the online VI loss defined in
Eq.

2
,
and the normalization term
Z_{t}
(which depends on
{\bm{x}}_{t}
) drops out as an additive constant.
Our goal is an efficient approximate solution to this variational optimization problem.

We will sometimes assume the variational
posterior
q_{\bm{\psi}}
is an exponential family
distribution with natural parameter
\bm{\psi}
so that

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\exp\left(\bm{\psi}_{t}^{\intercal}T({\bm{\theta}}_{t})-\Phi(\bm{\psi}_{t}\right))
,
with log-partition function
\Phi
and sufficient statistics
T({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
.
Assuming
\Phi
is strictly convex (which holds in the cases we study) there is a bijection between
\bm{\psi}_{t}
and the dual (or expectation) parameter

\bm{\rho}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}}}\!\left[{T({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}\right]
.
Classical thermodynamic identities imply
that the Fisher information matrix has the form

\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}=\partial\bm{\rho}_{t}/\partial\bm{\psi}_{t}
.
This has important implications for NGD on exponential families
(
Khan and Rue, 2023
)

because it implies that for any function
\ell
defined on the variational parameter space the natural gradient wrt natural parameters

\bm{\psi}_{t}

is the regular gradient wrt the dual parameters

\bm{\rho}_{t}
,
i.e.,

\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}\ell=\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t}}\ell.

4
Methods

We propose to approximate the variational optimization problem in
Eq.

4
using the
bong
update in
Eq.

3
.
When
q_{\bm{\psi}}
is an exponential family,
the fact that the
natural gradient wrt the natural parameters

\bm{\psi}_{t}

is the regular gradient wrt the dual parameters

\bm{\rho}_{t}

implies an equivalent mirror descent form
(see
Appendix

D
for further analysis of
bong
from the MD perspective):

\bm{\psi}_{t}=\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}+\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t|t-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})}\right]

(5)

This is NGD with unit learning rate on the variational loss in
Eq.

2
but ignoring the

D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

term.
In this section we first prove this method is optimal when the model is conjugate
and then describe extensions to more complex cases of practical interest.

4.1
Conjugate case

Our approach is motivated by the following result which states that
bong
matches exact Bayesian inference when the variational distribution and the likelihood are conjugate exponential families:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let the observation distribution (likelihood) be an exponential family with natural parameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

(where

T_{l}({\bm{y}}_{t})={\bm{y}}_{t}

is the sufficient statistics for the likelihood
and
A({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
is the log-partition function)

\displaystyle p_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{y}}_{t}-A({\bm{\theta}}_{t})-b({\bm{y}}_{t})\right)

(6)

and let the prior be the conjugate
exponential family

\displaystyle q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}T({\bm{\theta}}_{t})-\Phi(\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1})\right)

(7)

with
T({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\left[{\bm{\theta}}_{t};-A({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\right]
.
Then the exact Bayesian update agrees with
Eq.

5
.

The proof is in
Appendix

C
.
Writing the natural parameters of the prior as

\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}=[\bm{\chi}_{t|t-1};\nu_{t|t-1}]
,
we show the Bayesian update and
bong
both yield

\bm{\chi}_{t}=\bm{\chi}_{t|t-1}+{\bm{y}}_{t}
and

\nu_{t}=\nu_{t|t-1}+1
.
Intuitively, we are just accumulating a sum of the observed sufficient statistics, and a counter of
the sample size (number of observations seen so far).

4.2
Variational case

In practical settings the conjugacy assumption of
Proposition

4.1
will not be met, so
Eqs.

3
and
5
will only approximate the Bayesian update.
In this paper we restrict to Gaussian variational families.
We refer to the unrestricted case as FC (full covariance),
defined by the variational distribution

\displaystyle q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\right)

(8)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}
can be any positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix. The natural and dual parameters are

\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mu},-\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm vec}(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}))
and

\bm{\rho}=(\bm{\mu},{\rm vec}(\bm{\mu}\bm{\mu}^{\intercal}+\bm{\Sigma}))
.

Section

E.1.1
shows that
Eq.

5

translated back to
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})

gives the following
bong
update for the FC case:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))}\right]}_{{\bm{g}}_{t}}

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{2}\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))}\right]}_{\mathbf{G}_{t}}

(10)

which matches the explicit update in the RVGA method of
(
Lambert et al., 2021
)
.

4.3
Monte Carlo approximation

The integrals over the prior
q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}
in

Eqs.

9
and
10

are generally intractable and must be approximated.
One option is to use Monte Carlo, in what we call
bong-mc
.
Given
M
independent samples

\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{(m)}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}
,
we estimate the expected gradient

{\bm{g}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))}\right]

and expected Hessian

\mathbf{G}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{2}\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

as the empirical means

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{\textsc{mc}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{{\bm{g}}}_{t}^{(m)},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{g}}}_{t}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{(m)}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{mc-hess}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{t}^{(m)},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{t}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}_{\underset{}{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{(m)}}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))

(12)

We use
\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{mc-hess}}
only for small models.
Otherwise we use empirical Fisher (
Section

4.5
).

4.4
Linearized
bong

As an alternative to
bong-mc
, we propose a linear approximation
we call
bong-lin
that yields a deterministic and closed-form update.
Assume the likelihood is an exponential family as in
Proposition

4.1

but with natural parameter predicted by some function

f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=f({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
:

\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\exp\left(f_{t}\!\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}{\bm{y}}_{t}-A(f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))-b({\bm{y}}_{t})\right)

(13)

We also define the dual (moment) parameter of the likelihood as

h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}\right]
.
In a NN,
f_{t}
and
h_{t}
are related by the final response layer.
For example in classification
f_{t}
and
h_{t}
give the class logits and probabilities,
with
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})={\rm softmax}(f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))
,
with
{\bm{y}}_{t}
being the one-hot encoding.

We now define two methods for approximating the expected gradient
{\bm{g}}_{t}
and expected Hessian
\mathbf{G}_{t}
,
based on linearizing the predictive model at the prior mean
\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}

in terms of either
f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
or
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
,
and then prove their equivalence.

The
linear(
h
)-Gaussian
approximation
(
Ollivier, 2018
;
Tronarp et al., 2018
)

linearizes
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle\bar{h}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}+\mathbf{H}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}

\displaystyle=h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial h_{t}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}

(16)

and approximates the likelihood by a Gaussian with variance based at
\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{t}^{\rm LG}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}}_{t}|\bar{h}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}),\mathbf{R}_{t}),\quad\mathbf{R}_{t}=\mathbb{V}\left[{{\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}\right]

(17)

The
linear(
f
)-delta approximation

linearizes
f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
and maintains the original exponential family likelihood distribution in
Eq.

13

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})+\mathbf{F}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}

(19)

\displaystyle\bar{p}^{\rm LD}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\bar{f}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})^{\intercal}{\bm{y}}_{t}-A(\bar{f}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))-b({\bm{y}}_{t})\right)

(20)

It also uses a plug-in approximation that replaces

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

with a point mass

\delta_{\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})

so that the expected gradient and Hessian are approximated by their values at the prior mean, i.e.,

\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}\log\bar{p}_{t}^{\rm LD}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

and

\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}\log\bar{p}_{t}^{\rm LD}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
,
rather than being sampled.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under a Gaussian variational distribution,
the linear(
h
)-Gaussian and linear(
f
)-delta approximations
yield the same values for the expected gradient and Hessian

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{lin-hess}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}

(22)

See
Appendix

C
for the proof.
The main idea for the
{\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}
part is that
the linear-Gaussian assumptions make the gradient linear in
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

so the expected gradient equals the gradient at the mean.
The main idea for the
\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{lin-hess}}_{t}
part is that
eliminating the Hessian of the NN requires different linearizing assumptions for the Gaussian and delta approximations,
and the remaining nonlinear terms (from the log-likelihood in
Eq.

13
) agree because of the property of exponential families
that the Hessian of the log-partition
A
equals the conditional variance
\mathbf{R}_{t}
.

Applying
Proposition

4.2
to
Eqs.

9
and
10
gives the
bong-lin
update for a FC Gaussian prior

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\left(\mathbf{R}_{t}+\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}

(25)

where
\mathbf{K}_{t}
is the Kalman gain matrix
(see
Section

E.1.2
). This matches the
cm-ekf

(
Tronarp et al., 2018
;
Ollivier, 2018
)
.

The methods in

These are too expensive for large models or high-dimensional observations.
Instead we can use an empirical Fisher approximation that
replaces the Hessian with the outer product of the gradient
(see e.g,
(

).

For the
mc-ef

we make the following approximation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{mc-ef}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{M}\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{(1:M)}_{t}\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{(1:M)^{\intercal}}_{t}

where

\hat{\mathbf{G}}^{(1:M)}_{t}=[\hat{{\bm{g}}}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\hat{{\bm{g}}}_{t}^{(M)}]

We can also consider a similar approach for the

variant that is Jacobian-free and sampling-free.
Note that if
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}
were the true value of
\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right]

(i.e., if the model were correct)
then we would have

\mathbb{E}\left[{({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{\intercal}}\right]=\mathbf{R}_{t}

implying

\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}\left({\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}}\right]=-\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{lin-hess}}_{t}
.
This suggests using

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin-ef}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{y}}_{t}-h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\right)_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{y}}_{t}-h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}))={\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\textsc{lin-ef}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-{\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}\left({\bm{g}}^{\textsc{lin}}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}

(29)

where

Eq.

29
is the EF approximation to
Eq.

22
.

A more accurate EF approximation is possible by sampling virtual observations

from

or

(respectively)
(

However, in our experiments we use the actual observations

which is faster and follows previous work (e.g.,
(
Khan et al., 2018b
)
).

4.7
Variational families and their parameterizations

We investigate five variational families for the posterior distribution:
(1) FC Gaussian using natural parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mu},-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})
,
(2) FC Gaussian using central moment parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
,
(3) diagonal Gaussian using natural parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\sigma}^{-2}\bm{\mu},-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}^{-2})
(using elementwise exponents and products),
(4) diagonal Gaussian using central moment parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2})
, and
(5) DLR Gaussian with parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Upsilon},\mathbf{W})

and precision
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}=\bm{\Upsilon}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\intercal}

where

\bm{\Upsilon}\in\real^{P\times P}
is diagonal and

\mathbf{W}\in\real^{P\times R}
with
R\ll P
.
The moment parameterizations are included to test the importance of using natural parameters per
Proposition

4.1
.
The diagonal family allows learning of large models because it scales linearly in the model size
P
.
DLR also scales linearly but is more expressive than diagonal, maintaining some of the correlation information between parameters that is lost in the mean field (diagonal) approximation
(
Lambert et al., 2023
;
Mishkin et al., 2018
;
Chang et al., 2023
)
.

Optimizing the
bong
objective
wrt
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Upsilon},\mathbf{W})

using NGD methods is challenging
because the Fisher information matrix in this parameterization cannot be efficiently inverted.
Instead we first derive the update
wrt the FC natural parameters
(leveraging the fact that the prior

\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}
is DLR
to make this efficient),
and then use SVD to project the posterior
precision back to low-rank form,
following our prior
lo-fi
work

(
Chang et al., 2023
)
.
However, if we omit the Fisher preconditioning matrix and use GD as in
bog
and
bbb
,
we can directly optimize the objective
wrt
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Upsilon},\mathbf{W})

(see
Section

E.5
).

Experiments

This section presents our primary experimental results.
These are based on

mnist
(
D=784
,
N_{\text{train}}=60
k,
N_{\text{test}}=10
k,

C=10
classes)
(
LeCun et al., 2010
)
.
See
Appendix

B

on these experiments,
and more results on MNIST and other datasets.
We focus on training on a prefix of the first
T=2000
examples from each dataset, since our main interest is in online learning from potentially nonstationary distributions, where rapid adaptation of a model in response to a small number of new data points
is critical.

Our primary evaluation objective is the negative
log predictive density (NLPD)
of the test set as a function of the number of training points observed so far.
1
1

1

We assume the training and test sets are drawn
from the same static distribution.
Alternatively, if there is only one stream of data
coming from a potential notstationary source,
we can use the prequential or one-step-ahead

Gama et al. (2013)
.
We leave studying the non-stationary case
to future work.

It is defined as

\text{NLPD}_{t}=-\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{\text{test}}}\log\left[\int p({\bm{y}}_{i}|f({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{t}))q_{\bm{\psi}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\rm d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right]
.
We approximate this integral in two main ways:
(1) using Monte Carlo sampling
2
2

2

That is, we compute

S=100
posterior samples

and then use

p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{s})
.
For efficiently sampling from a DLR Gaussian we follow
Mishkin et al. (2018)
.
Following

Immer et al. (2021b)
, we find it better to linearize
the model (i.e., replacing

\bar{h}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

defined in

)
before pushing posterior samples through.
We call this the Lin-MC approximation.

,or (2) using a plugin approximation,
where we replace the posterior

with a delta function centered at the mean,

\delta({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t})
.

For methods that require a learning rate
(i.e., all methods except

All methods require specifying the prior

We optimize over

As Hessian approximations,

as well as the deterministic

6
Conclusions, limitations and future work

Our experiment results show benefits of
bong
’s three main principles:
NGD,
implicit regularization to the prior,
and linearization.
The clear winner across datasets and variational families is
bong
-
lin-hess
, which embodies all three principles.

Blr
-
lin-hess
nearly matches its performance but is much slower.
Several of the best-performing algorithms are previously known (notably
cm-ekf
and
lo-fi
) but we explain these results within a systematic theory that also offers new methods (including
blr
-
lin-hess
).

Bong
is motivated by
Proposition

4.1
which applies only in the idealized setting of a conjugate prior. Nevertheless we find it performs well in non-conjugate settings.

Proposition

4.2
.

The intuition behind this proof is as follows.
For the mean update in
Eq.

9

\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\ell_{t}
is linear in
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
so the expectation equals the value at the mean.
For the covariance update in
Eq.

10

\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\ell_{t}
is independent of
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
so we can drop the expectation operator.
The tricky part is why we need different linearizations for the Hessians to agree. It has to do with making the Hessian of the NN disappear (as in GGN). In the Gaussian approximation this happens when the predicted mean (
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
) is linear in
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
. In the plugin approximation it happens when the outcome-dependent part of the loglikelihood (
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})^{\intercal}{\bm{y}}_{t}
) is linear in
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
. In the latter case the only nonlinear term remaining in the log-likelihood is the log-partition
A
, and the two methods end up agreeing because of the property that the Hessian of the log-partition equals the conditional variance
\mathbf{R}_{t}
.

Formally, under the linear(
h
)-Gaussian approximation in
Eqs.

14
and
17

the expected gradient and Hessian can be calculated directly:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\log\bar{p}^{\rm LG}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}))}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})}\right]

(48)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

(49)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}^{2}\log\bar{p}^{\rm LG}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{-\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}}\right]

(50)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}

(51)

For the linear(
f
)-delta approximation we use the properties of exponential families that (1) the gradient of the log-partition
A
with respect to the natural parameter
f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
equals the expectation parameter
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
, (2) the Hessian of the log-partition with respect to the natural parameter equals the conditional variance, and consequently (3) the Jacobian of the expectation parameter with respect to the natural parameter equals the conditional variance
\mathbf{R}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\eta}=f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})}A(\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})=\hat{{\bm{y}}}

(52)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\eta}=f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})}A(\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}\left[{{\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}\right]=\mathbf{R}_{t}

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}{\partial f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}_{|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{t}

(54)

The last of these implies
\mathbf{F}_{t}=\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}
.
Therefore the expected gradient and Hessian can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim\delta_{\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}\log\bar{p}^{\rm LD}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}}\log\bar{p}^{\rm LD}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

(55)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\intercal}{\bm{y}}_{t}-\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\intercal}\nabla_{\bm{\eta}=f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})}A(\bm{\eta})

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\intercal}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

(57)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

Appendix D
Mirror descent formulation

In this section we give a more detailed derivation of
bong
as mirror descent
and use this to give two alternative interpretations of how
bong
approximates exact VB:
(1) by approximating the expected NLL as linear in the expectation parameter
\bm{\rho}
, or
(2) by replacing an implicit update with an explicit one.

Assume the variational family is an exponential one as introduced at the end of
Section

3
, with natural and dual parameters
\bm{\psi}
and
\bm{\rho}
, sufficient statistics
T({\bm{\theta}})
, and log-partition
\Phi(\bm{\psi})
:

\displaystyle q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\bm{\psi}^{\intercal}T({\bm{\theta}})-\Phi(\bm{\psi}\right))

(63)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{T({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(64)

We first review how NGD on an exponential family is a special case of mirror descent
[
Khan and Rue, 2023
,
Martens, 2020
]
.
The mirror map is the gradient of the log-partition, which satisfies the thermodynamic identity

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}=\nabla\Phi(\bm{\psi})

(65)

This is a bijection when
\Phi
is convex (which includes the cases we study), so we can implicitly treat
\bm{\psi}
and
\bm{\rho}
as functions of each other.
Given a loss function
L(\bm{\psi})
, MD iteratively solves the local optimization problem

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\arg\min}\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{i}}L(\bm{\psi}_{i}),\bm{\rho}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{D}_{\Phi}(\bm{\psi}_{i},\bm{\psi})

(66)

The first term is a linear (in
\bm{\rho}
) approximation of
L
about the previous iteration
\bm{\psi}_{i}

and the second term is the Bregman divergence

\mathbb{D}_{\Phi}(\bm{\psi}_{i},\bm{\psi}_{i+1})=\Phi(\bm{\psi}_{i})-\Phi(\bm{\psi}_{i+1})-\left(\bm{\psi}_{i}-\bm{\psi}_{i+1}\right)^{\intercal}\bm{\rho}_{i+1}

(67)

The Bregman divergence acts as a regularizer toward
\bm{\psi}_{i}
and captures the intrinsic geometry of the parameter space because of its equivalence with the (reverse) KL divergence

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{i+1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{i+1}}}}\!\left[{\left(\bm{\psi}_{i+1}-\bm{\psi}_{i}\right)^{\intercal}T({\bm{\theta}})+\Phi(\bm{\psi}_{i})-\Phi(\bm{\psi}_{i+1})}\right]

(68)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}_{\Phi}(\bm{\psi}_{i},\bm{\psi}_{i+1})

(69)

Importantly, this recursive regularizer is not part of the loss and serves only to define an iterated algorithm that converges to a local minimum of
L
.
Solving
Eq.

66
by differentiating by
\bm{\rho}
yields the MD update

\bm{\psi}_{i+1}=\bm{\psi}_{i}-\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{i}}L(\bm{\psi}_{i})

(70)

Because the Fisher matrix for an exponential family is
\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}}=\partial\bm{\rho}/\partial\bm{\psi}
, this is equivalent to NGD with respect to
\bm{\psi}
.

Khan and Rue [2023]
offer this as a derivation of the BLR, when
L(\bm{\psi})
is taken to be the variational loss from
Eq.

1
.

By applying this analysis to the online setting,
our approach can be seen to follow from two insights.
First, the online variational loss in
Eq.

2
already includes KL divergence from the previous step,
so we do not need the artificial regularizer in
Eq.

66
.
That is, if we start from the online variational problem in
Eq.

4

and define
L_{t}(\bm{\psi})
as the expected NLL,

L_{t}(\bm{\psi})=-\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\log p({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))}\right]

(71)

then replacing
L_{t}(\bm{\psi})
with a linear approximation based at
\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}
and applying
Eq.

69
leads to

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\arg\min}\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t|t-1}}L_{t}(\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}),\bm{\rho}\right\rangle+\mathbb{D}_{\Phi}(\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1},\bm{\psi})

(72)

By comparing to
Eq.

66
we see this defines an MD algorithm with unit learning rate that works in a single step rather than by iterating.
Paralleling the derivation of
Eq.

70
from
Eq.

66
we get

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}-\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t|t-1}}L_{t}(\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1})

(73)

which matches the
bong
update in
Eq.

5
.
Thus
bong
can be seen as an approximate solution of the online variational problem in
Eq.

4

based on linearizing the expected NLL wrt
\bm{\rho}
.
(Note this is different from the assumption underlying
bong-lin
that
f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
or
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
is linear in
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
.)

Second, in the conjugate case, this linearity assumption is true:
L_{t}
is linear in
\bm{\rho}
(see proof of
Proposition

4.1
).
Therefore
72
is equivalent to solving
Eq.

4
exactly:

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\arg\min}\,L_{t}(\bm{\psi})+\mathbb{D}_{\Phi}(\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1},\bm{\psi})

(74)

This recapitulates
Proposition

4.1
that
bong
is Bayes optimal in the conjugate case.
In general the exact solution to
Eq.

74
is

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}-\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t}}L_{t}(\bm{\psi}_{t})

(75)

This is an implicit update because the gradient is evaluated at the (unknown) posterior,
whereas
Eq.

73
is an explicit update because it evaluates the gradient at the prior.
(In the Gaussian case these can be shown to match the implicit and explicit RVGA updates of
[
Lambert et al., 2021
]
.)
Therefore
bong
can be also interpreted as an approximation of exact VB that replaces the implicit update,
Eq.

75
, with an explicit update,
Eq.

73
.

E.2
Full covariance Gaussian, Moment parameters

The Bonnet and Price theorems give

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle={\bm{g}}_{t,i}

(158)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{G}_{t,i}

(159)

From
Section

E.1
we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)

(160)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}^{-1}\right)

(161)

We write the Fisher with respect to the moment parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\mu},{\rm vec}(\bm{\Sigma}))

as a block matrix:

\mathbf{F}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}}&\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma}}\\
\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\mu}}&\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\Sigma}}\end{array}\right]

(162)

The blocks can be calculated by the second-order Fisher formula

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(163)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}

(164)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(165)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\left(\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})}\right]

(166)

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

(167)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\Sigma}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(168)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)}\right]

(169)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\begin{array}[]{c}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\\
+\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)-\left(\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)\end{array}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}

(172)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\otimes\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}

(173)

In the final line we used

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Sigma_{k\ell}}\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)_{ik}\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)_{j\ell}

(174)

\displaystyle=-\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\otimes\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)_{ij,k\ell}

(175)

with
ij
and
k\ell
treated as composite indices in
[P^{2}]
.
Therefore the preconditioner in the NGD methods is

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&2\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}\otimes\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}+\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}

(195)

Inverting these relationships gives

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}\right)^{-1}\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}

(196)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}-\left(\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}\right)^{2}

(197)

The KL divergence in the VI loss is

D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)^{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}-\log\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}\right)+\mathrm{const}

(198)

with gradients

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)

(199)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}-\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{-2}\right)

(200)

For any scalar function
\bm{\ell}
the chain rule gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}{\partial\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell+\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}{\partial\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(201)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell-2\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(202)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}{\partial\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell+\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}{\partial\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(203)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(204)

Therefore

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle={\bm{g}}_{t,i}-\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{G}_{t,i}\right)\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}

(205)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{G}_{t,i}\right)

(206)

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{-2}\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}

(207)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\rho}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}-\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{-2}\right)

(208)

Following the same approach for
\bm{\psi}
gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}{\partial\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell+\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}{\partial\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}}\ell

(209)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}\right)^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell

(210)

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell

(211)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}{\partial\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell+\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}{\partial\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(212)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}\right)^{-2}\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}\right)^{-2}\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(213)

\displaystyle=2\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\ell+2\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{4}\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\ell

(214)

Therefore

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}{\bm{g}}_{t,i}

(215)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=2\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}{\bm{g}}_{t,i}+\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{4}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{G}_{t,i}\right)

(216)

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(1\right)}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)

(217)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}^{\left(2\right)}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{4}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}-\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{-2}\right)

(218)

Our implementations often make use of the following trick:
Suppose
\mathbf{A}\in\real^{n\times m}

and
\mathbf{B}\in\real^{m\times n}
.
Then we can efficiently compute

\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})
in
O(mn)
time
using

(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}A_{ij}B_{ji}
.

For
mc-hess
methods,
we approximate the diagonal of the Hessian for each MC sample
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{(m)}

using Hutchinson’s trace estimation method
[
Hutchinson, 1989
]

which has been used in other DNN optimization papers such as adahessian
Yao et al. [2021]
.
This involves an extra inner loop with size denoted
N
.

E.4
Diagonal Gaussian, Moment parameters

Throughout this subsection, vector multiplication and exponents are elementwise.

The Bonnet and Price theorems give

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle={\bm{g}}_{t,i}

(243)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|f_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{G}_{t,i}\right)

(244)

From
Section

E.3
we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)

(245)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{2}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-2}-\bm{\sigma}_{t,i-1}^{-2}\right)

(246)

We write the Fisher with respect to the moment parameters
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2})

as a block matrix:

\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}}&\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\\
\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\sigma}^{2},\bm{\mu}}&\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\sigma}^{2},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\end{array}\right]

(247)

The blocks can be calculated by the second-order Fisher formula

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(248)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Diag}\left(\bm{\sigma}^{-2}\right)

(249)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(250)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\mathrm{Diag}\left(({\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\mu})\bm{\sigma}^{-4}\right)}\right]

(251)

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

(252)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\sigma}^{2},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\nabla_{\bm{\sigma}^{2},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\log q_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{\theta}})}\right]

(253)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{{q_{\bm{\psi}}}}\!\left[{\mathrm{Diag}\left(-\left(\bm{\mu}-{\bm{\theta}}\right)^{2}\bm{\sigma}^{-6}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}^{-4}\right)}\right]

(254)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Diag}\left(\bm{\sigma}^{-4}\right)

(255)

Therefore the preconditioner for the NGD methods is

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{Diag}\left(\sigma_{t,i-1}^{2}\right)&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&2\mathrm{Diag}\left(\sigma_{t,i-1}^{4}\right)\end{array}\right]

E.5
Diagonal plus low rank

Assume the prior is given by

q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\right)

(274)

with
\mathbf{W}\in\real^{P\times R}
and diagonal
\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}\in\real^{P\times P}
.
We sometimes abuse notation by writing
\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}
for the
vector
\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}\right)
when the meaning is
clear from context.

Substituting the DLR form in the gradients for the KL divergence derived
in
Section

E.2
gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right)

(275)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}-\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}-\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)

(276)

For any function
\ell
the chain rule gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}}\ell

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{diag}\left(\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}}\ell\right)\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\right)

(277)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}}\ell

\displaystyle=-2\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}}\ell\right)\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}

(278)

Therefore the gradients we need are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mu}_{t,i-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|h_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle={\bm{g}}_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i}}

(279)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|h_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{diag}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{t,i}\\
\times\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}}\!\left[{\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|h_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=-\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{t,i}

\displaystyle\quad\enskip\times\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}

(282)

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{diag}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\\
\times\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}-\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}-\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)\\
\times\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}}D_{\mathbb{KL}}\!\left({q_{\bm{\psi}_{t,i-1}}}|{q_{\bm{\psi}_{t|t-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t|t-1}-\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{W}_{t|t-1}^{\intercal}-\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i-1}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{t,i-1}

(286)

The Fisher matrix can be decomposed as a block-diagonal with blocks for
\bm{\mu}_{t,i}
and for
(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i},\mathbf{W}_{t,i})
, but (in contrast to the FC Gaussian case in
Section

E.2
)
we have not found an efficient way to analytically invert the latter block, which has size
P+RP
.
To avoid the
O\left(R^{3}P^{3}\right)
cost of brute force inversion,
we use a different strategy for
bong
and
blr
of performing the
update on the natural parameters of the FC Gaussian as in
Section

E.1

and projecting the result back to rank
R
using SVD. Specifically,
assume the updated precision from applying
Eq.

125

for
bong
or
Eq.

140
for
blr
can be written
as

\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t,i}^{-1}=\tilde{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{t,i}+\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{t,i}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{t,i}^{\intercal}

(287)

and let the SVD of
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{t,i}
be

\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{t,i}=\mathbf{U}_{t,i}\bm{\Lambda}_{t,i}\mathbf{V}_{t,i}^{\intercal}

(288)

with
\mathbf{U}_{t,i},\mathbf{V}_{t,i}
orthogonal and
\bm{\Lambda}_{t,i}
rectangular-diagonal.
Following
[
Mishkin et al., 2018
,
Chang et al., 2023
]
we define the update

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{t,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}_{t,i}\left[:,{:R}\right]\bm{\Lambda}_{t,i}\left[{:R},{:R}\right]

(289)

\displaystyle\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{\Upsilon}}_{t,i}+\mathrm{diag}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{t,i}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{t,i}^{\intercal}-\mathbf{W}_{t,i}\mathbf{W}_{t,i}^{\intercal}\right)

(290)

so that
\mathbf{W}_{t,i}
contains the top
R
singular vectors and values
of the FC posterior and the diagonal is preserved:
\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{\Upsilon}_{t,i}+\mathbf{W}_{t,i}\mathbf{W}_{t,i}^{\intercal}\right)=\mathrm{diag}\left(\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t,i}^{-1}\right)
.
This approach works for
mc-ef
and
lin-ef
methods but not
mc-hess
, which we omit.

Finally, in the
mc-ef
methods we sample from the DLR prior using the routine in
[
Mishkin et al., 2018
,
Lambert et al., 2023
]
which takes
O(R(R+M)P)
.

-
mc-ef
-
dlr

Eqs.

297
,
296
,
298
,
294
and
299

.
