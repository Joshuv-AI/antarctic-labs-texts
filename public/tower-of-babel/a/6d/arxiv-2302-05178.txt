Well-Posedness and large deviations for the stochastic Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch equation
Soham Gokhale, Utpal Manna

Abstract




1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
60H15

Abstract:
The stochastic Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch equation in dimensions
1,2,3
perturbed by pure jump noise (in the Marcus canonical form) is considered. The proof for the existence of a martingale solution uses the classical Faedo-Galerkin approximation, followed by compactness and tightness arguments, including those of Aldous, and Jakubowski’s version of the Skorohod representation theorem, among others. Pathwise uniqueness and the theory of Yamada and Watanabe give the existence of a strong solution (for dimensions
1,2
). Later, a weak convergence method is used to establish a Wentzell-Freidlin type large deviations principle for the small noise asymptotic of solutions (for dimensions
1,2
).

1.
Introduction

Initiated by Weiss (see
[
9
]
and references therein) and further developed by Landau and Lifshitz (
[
46
,
47
]
) and Gilbert
[
38
]
, the study of the theory of ferromagnetism is one of the areas of rapidly increasing importance and applications.
The proposed equation of motion for a ferromagnetic medium is now a basis for many studies of magnetic structures, aiding substantially in the field of information storage and processing.
For temperatures (
\mathbb{T}
) below the Curie temperature (
\mathbb{T}_{c}
), the magnetization can be modeled by the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation. The LLG equation assumes that the magnetization length remains a constant, which is a serious restriction. This approach is not suitable for higher temperatures. For instance, in heat assisted magnetic recording, the electronic temperature can rise. Also, the magnetization is an average over some distribution function, and its length can change
[
36
]
.

1.1.
The Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch Equation

Garanin
[
35
]
(see also
[
36
,
37
]
) developed a thermodynamically consistent approach, the Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch (LLB) equation for ferromagnetism. The LLB equation is valid for temperatures both below and above the Curie temperature and essentially interpolates between the LLG equation at low temperature and the Ginzburg-Landau theory for phase transition. LLB micromagnetics has become a real alternative to LLG micromagnetics for temperatures that are close to the Curie temperature
\left(\mathbb{T}\geq\frac{3\mathbb{T}_{c}}{4}\right)
. For instance, in light-induced demagnetization with powerful fs lasers, the electronic temperature is normally raised higher than
\mathbb{T}_{c}
. While LLG micromagnetics cannot work under these, micromagnetics based on the LLB equation has been proven to describe the observed fs magnetization dynamics
[
36
]
.

The average spin polarization
m
in a domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},d=1,2,3
for
t>0
satisfies the following LLB equation.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}=\gamma m\times H_{\text{eff}}+L_{1}\frac{1}{|m|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}}(m\cdot H_{\text{eff}})m-L_{2}\frac{1}{|m|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}}m\times(m\times H_{\text{eff}}),\\
&\frac{\partial m}{\partial\eta}(t,x)=0,\ t>0,\ x\in\partial\mathcal{O},\\
&m(0)=m_{0}.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here
\gamma>0
is the gyromagnetic ratio and
L_{1},L_{2}
are the longitudinal and transverse damping parameters respectively and
H_{\text{eff}}
is the effective field.
\eta
denotes the outward pointing normal to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
.

Using the identity

\displaystyle a\times(b\times c)=b(a\cdot c)-c(a\cdot b)\ \text{for}\ a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

for the last term gives

\displaystyle m\times(m\times H_{\text{eff}})=(m\cdot H_{\text{eff}})m-H_{\text{eff}}\left|m\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}.

For temperature above the Curie temperature, we have
L_{1}=L_{2}=\kappa_{1}
(say).
Therefore the resulting equation is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}=\gamma m\times H_{\text{eff}}+\kappa_{1}H_{\text{eff}}.

(1.2)

The effective field is given by

H_{\text{eff}}=\Delta m-\frac{1}{\mathcal{X}_{||}}\left(1+\frac{3}{5}\frac{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{c}}\left|m\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)m.

(1.3)

\mathcal{X}_{||}
is the longitudinal susceptibility. On the right hand side of (
1.3
), the first term denotes the exchange field, the second denotes the entropy correction field.

Using (
1.3
), we can write equation (
1.2
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\gamma m\times\Delta m-\frac{\gamma}{\mathcal{X}_{||}}\left(1+\frac{3}{5}\frac{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{c}}\left|m\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)\left(m\times m\right)+\kappa_{1}\Delta m

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\mathcal{X}_{||}}\left(1+\frac{3}{5}\frac{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{c}}\left|m\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)m.

(1.4)

Denoting
\mu=\frac{3}{5}\frac{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{c}}
and
\kappa=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\chi_{||}}
results in the following equality.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}\Delta m+\gamma m\times\Delta m-\kappa\left(1+\mu\left|m\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)m.

(1.5)

Le
[
48
]
showed the existence of a weak solution in a bounded domain. For some recent developments on the LLB equation, we refer the reader to
[
6
,
41
,
49
,
59
,
67
]
among others.
Note that the said (deterministic) LLB equation turns out to be insufficient, for instance, to capture the dispersion of individual trajectories at high temperatures.
Hence, according to Brown
[
9
,
10
]
the LLB equation is modified (see
[
33
,
37
]
for discussions on stochastic form of LLB equation) in order to incorporate random fluctuations and to describe noise induced transitions between equilibrium states of the ferromagnet.

The study of thermal fluctuations in magnetic materials is one of the fundamental issues of modern micromagnetism, and its applications
[
5
]
. For instance, magnetic nanostructures are very susceptible to thermal excitations. Also, many new writing techniques have thermal excitation playing an important role, either as a byproduct or triggering magnetization switching (for example, thermally induced magnetization switching).
Brown
[
9
,
10
]
incorporated thermal fluctuation into the Landau-Lifshitz model as formal random fields.
Garanin and Chubykalo-Fesenko
[
37
]
suggested the treatment of LLB equation following Brown’s treatment of the LLG equation. However, the requirement of the Boltzmann distribution in equilibrium is not fulfilled in the vicinity of the Curie temperature.
Evans
et. al.
in
[
33
]
introduced a different form of the stochastic LLB equation, which is consistent with the Boltzmann distribution at arbitrary temperatures. For more details about the LLB equation and its applications, we refer the reader, for instance, to
[
5
,
66
]
among others and references therein.

Following the pioneering work of Le
[
48
]
, Brzeźniak, Goldys, and Le
[
14
]
considered the stochastic counterpart of the LLB equation (perturbed by Gaussian noise) and showed the existence of a weak martingale solution in dimensions
1,2,3
along with pathwise uniqueness of the obtained solution for dimensions
1,2
, also proving the existence of invariant measures.
The authors in
[
43
]
show the existence of a weak martingale solution to the stochastic LLB equation (perturbed by Gaussian noise).
Similarly, the authors in
[
61
]
consider the stochastic LLB equation in 1 dimension and establish large deviations, along with the central limit theorem.

Our study also has another physical motivation other than understanding the phenomenon of phase transition between equilibrium states induced by thermal fluctuations. Consider a magnet that is traversed by a domain wall. There can be random imperfections (for example, defects, impurities, etc.) within the material. Hence the domain wall is not free to move and is pinned to the imperfections, which act as local potential barriers. When an external magnetic field is applied to move the domain wall away from the imperfection, the free wall moves for a while and is then trapped by another imperfection. The hysteresis loop is therefore distinguished by a series of jumps, known as Barkhausen jumps. Mayergoyz,
et. al.
, in
[
52
,
53
]
introduced jump noise process into magnetization dynamics equations in order to account for the random thermal effects. The exact mechanism of the pinning (imperfection) is imperfectly known
[
44
,
60
]
. Our work might help in a better understanding of the said effects.

The aim of this work is twofold. The first is to show the existence and uniqueness of the solution for the stochastic LLB equation (with pure jump noise).
Taking motivation from
[
14
]
and
[
18
,
20
]
(see also
[
17
,
21
,
22
,
37
]
), we perturb the effective field
H_{\text{eff}}
.
A key technical issue while considering a jump noise is that the noise must preserve the invariance property under coordinate transformation. This is important, for example, in preserving the constraint condition for the LLG equation.
Marcus
[
51
]
, Kunita
[

]
(see also
[

,
4

) provide a framework to resolve this issue.

Another important point is the following. The proof for the existence of a martingale solution uses the classical Faedo-Galerkin approximation. To show the tightness of the family of laws of the approximate solutions, the work of Flandoli and Gatarek
[
34
]
is usually followed. But this method is not applicable when one considers jump noise.
The approach by Métivier
[
54
]
, Brzeźniak,
et. al.

[

,

]

[

depends on a deterministic compactness result, which further depends on some energy bounds and the Aldous condition, which is a stochastic version of the equicontinuity result of Arzéla and Ascoli. A similar idea has been used in
[

17
]
for the stochastic LLG equation.

We first state the problem that we have considered. Let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},d=1,2,3

\mathbb{R}^{N}

.

\displaystyle m(t)=

\displaystyle m_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\Delta m(s)+m(s)\times\Delta m(s)-\left(1+\left|m(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)m(s)\right]\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left(m(s)\times h+h\right)\diamond dL(s),\ t\in[0,T].

(1.6)

with the Neumann boundary condition. Here
L(t)=\left(L_{1}(t),\dots,L_{N}(t)\right),N\in\mathbb{N}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued Lévy process with pure jump. For the sake of simplicity, we assume

. We postpone more details about the process

and equation (
1.1
) to

-

.

We take motivation from the work of Brezniak, Goldys, and Jegaraj

Brzeźniak and Manna in

The same authors, along with Panda in

The layout of the work is as follows. Section
3
describes the considered problem (
1.1
) in more detail.
The main aim of the section is to show the existence of a weak martingale solution. First, the equation (
1.1
) is written using the Marcus mapping defined in Section

. The section also outlines some basic properties of the defined Marcus map.
To prove the existence of a weak martingale solution, we use the Faedo-Galerkin approximation, followed by some compactness arguments and the use of a generalized Skorokhod Theorem. Section
4
shows that (for

) the obtained solution is pathwise unique, and hence when combined with the theory of Yamada and Watanabe, the existence of a strong solution is shown (Theorem

).
Section

[
31
]
. Budhiraja, Chen, and Dupuis in
[
23
]
establish two sufficient conditions for a sequence of laws to satisfy the large deviations principle.
In Section

Section

is dedicated to the verification (for

) of the said conditions. The verification is done in a number of lemmas.

2.2.
The Marcus Mapping

This subsection describes the Marcus mapping and the resulting form of the equation (
1.1
).
Let

\bar{g}:H^{1}\ni v\mapsto g(v)+h\in H^{1},

with

g:H^{1}\ni v\mapsto v\times h\in H^{1},

with fixed (given)
h\in W^{2,\infty}
.
We observe that the map
g
, and hence the map
\bar{g}
is a bounded map.
We now define a map

\Phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times H^{1}\to L^{1},

such that for each
x\in H^{1}
, the mapping

t\mapsto\Phi(t,l,x),

is a
C^{1}
solution to the ordinary differential equation

\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\left(t,l,x\right)=l\bar{g}\left(\Phi(t,l,x)\right),

(2.7)

with

\Phi(0,l,x)=x
.

The equation (
2.7
) can be written as

\Phi(t,l,x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}l\bar{g}\left(\Phi(s,l,x)\right)\,ds.

(2.8)

Arguing similarly, we can show that the map

\bar{g}:H^{1}\ni v\mapsto g(v)+h\in H^{1},

is bounded. Therefore, the mappings

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times L^{2}\ni\left(t,l,x\right)\mapsto\Phi\left(t,l,x\right)\in L^{2},

and

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times H^{1}\ni\left(t,l,x\right)\mapsto\Phi\left(t,l,x\right)\in H^{1},

are well defined.
In what follows, we fix
t=1
.
\Phi(l,\cdot):=\Phi(1,l,\cdot)
,
l\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.3.
Some Properties of the Marcus Mapping and some corresponding operators

This subsection enlists some properties of the mapping
\Phi
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X\in\left\{L^{2},H^{1}\right\}
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
l\in B
, the following hold.

\left|\Phi(l,x)\right|_{X}\leq C\left(1+\left|x\right|_{X}\right),\ x\in X.

(2.9)

\left|\Phi(l,x)\right|_{X}^{2}\leq C\left(1+\left|x\right|_{X}^{2}\right),\ x\in X.

(2.10)

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

We give a brief proof for the second inequality with
X=L^{2}
. The other cases can be done similarly. For the first inequality,

\displaystyle\Phi(l,x)=x+\int_{0}^{1}l\bar{g}\bigl(\Phi\left(s,l,x\right)\bigr)\,ds.

(2.11)

Taking the
L^{2}
norm of both sides gives

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(l,x)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}+\left|\int_{0}^{1}l\bar{g}\bigl(\phi\left(s,l,x\right)\bigr)\,ds\right|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{1}\left|l\right|\left|\bar{g}\bigl(\phi\left(s,l,x\right)\bigr)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}+C\int_{0}^{1}\left|l\right|\left(1+\left|\phi\left(s,l,x\right)\right|_{L^{2}}\right)\,ds.

(2.12)

The use of the Gronwall inequality gives the desired result.

For the second inequality, we again have

\displaystyle\Phi(l,x)=x+\int_{0}^{1}l\bar{g}\bigl(\phi\left(s,l,x\right)\bigr)\,ds.

(2.13)

Therefore by Young’s inequality, we can show the following.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|\Phi(l,x)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}l\left\langle\bar{g}\left(\Phi(s,l,x)\right),\Phi(s,l,x)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}l\left[\left\langle g\left(\Phi(s,l,x)\right),\Phi(s,l,x)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle h,\Phi(s,l,x)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}l\left[\left\langle\Phi(s,l,x)\times h,\Phi(s,l,x)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle h,\Phi(s,l,x)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}l\left\langle h,\Phi(s,l,x)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\left|l\right|\left[\frac{1}{2}\left|h\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left|\Phi(s,l,x)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left|h\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\left|l\right|\left|\Phi(s,l,x)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds.

Use of the Gronwall inequality, there exists constants
\tilde{C},C>0
such that

\left|\Phi(l,x)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\tilde{C}\left(1+\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)e^{\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{C}\,ds}\leq C\left(1+\left|x\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Note that all of the previous calculations go through for any
l\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, choosing
l\in B
can make the constants independent of
l
. ∎

Consider the following mappings on
L^{2}

\displaystyle G(l,v)=\Phi(l,v)-v,\ v\in L^{2}:

(2.14)

\displaystyle H(l,v)=\Phi(v)-v-l\bar{g}(v),\ v\in L^{2}.

(2.15)

With the above two mappings in mind, we state the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every

and

\displaystyle\left|G(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(1+\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}\right),

(2.16)

\displaystyle\left|G(l,u)-G(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\left|H(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(1+\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}\right),

(2.18)

\displaystyle\left|H(l,u)-H(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}.

(2.19)

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

We give proofs for Lipschitz continuity ((
2.17
),(
2.19

5.5

\displaystyle\left|G(l,u)-G(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(l,u)-u-\Phi(l,v)+v\right|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|l\right|\left|\bar{g}(\Phi(s,l,u))-\bar{g}(\Phi(s,l,v))\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|\left|\Phi(s,l,u)-\Phi(s,l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds.

(2.20)

We take a small detour here. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(l,u)-\Phi(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}-\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(l,u)-u-\Phi(l,v)+v\right|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|\left|\Phi(s,l,u)-\Phi(s,l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds.

(2.21)

In particular,

\left|\Phi(l,u)-\Phi(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{1}\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|\left|\Phi(s,l,u)-\Phi(s,l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds.

(2.22)

By the Gronwall inequality, we have,

\left|\Phi(l,u)-\Phi(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}e^{1\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|}.

(2.23)

Therefore

\displaystyle\left|G(l,u)-G(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|\left|\Phi(s,l,u)-\Phi(s,l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|e^{s\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|}\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|\left(e^{\left|h\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|l\right|}-1\right)\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}.

(2.24)

Therefore there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left|G(u)-G(v)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}.

(2.25)

Now,

\displaystyle\left|H(l,u)-H(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(l,u)-u-l\bar{g}(u)-\Phi(l,u)+u+l\bar{g}(u)\right|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}+\left|G(l,u)-G(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}+\left|l\right|\left|\bar{g}(u)-\bar{g}(v)\right|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left|u-v\right|_{L^{2}}.

(2.26)

Hence both
G,H
are Lipschitz continuous. Hence they also have linear growth. This concludes the proof of the Lemma
3.5
.
∎

3.
Existence of a Weak Martingale Solution

The section is dedicated to the existence of a weak martingale solution for the problem (

We now give a definition for a weak martingale solution to the problem (

Definition 3.1

(Weak Martingale solution)
.

A weak martingale solution to the problem (
2.4
) is a tuple

\displaystyle\left(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{\prime},M^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}\right),

such that

(1)

\left(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\right)
is a probability space (with filtration
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\prime}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}
) satisfying the usual hypotheses.

(2)

\eta^{\prime}
is a time homogeneous Poisson random measure on the measurable space
\left(B,\mathcal{B}\right)
over
\left(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\right)
with intensity measure
Leb\otimes\nu
.

(3)

m^{\prime}:[0,T]\times\Omega^{\prime}\to H^{1}
is an
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
-progressively measurable weakly cádlág process which satisfies the following estimates. There exists a constant
C>0
that depends upon
T,m_{0},h
such that

(a)

\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|m^{\prime}(t)\right|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq C,

(3.1)

(b)

\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{T}\left|m^{\prime}(t)\right|_{H^{2}}^{2}\,dt\leq C.

(3.2)

(4)

For each
V\in L^{4}(\Omega^{\prime}:H^{1})
, (
2.4
) is satisfied
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
-a.s. in the weak (PDE) sense as follows.

\displaystyle m^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\,m_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\nabla m^{\prime}(s),\nabla V\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle m^{\prime}(s)\times\nabla m^{\prime}(s),\nabla V\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(1+\left|m^{\prime}(s,x)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)\left\langle m^{\prime}(s,x),V(x)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle b\bigl(l,m^{\prime}(s)\bigr),V\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left\langle G\bigl(l,m^{\prime}(s)\bigr),V\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,\tilde{\eta}(dl,ds),\ t\in[0,T].

(3.3)

Remark 3.2
.

Since we are allowing the solution
m^{\prime}
to be
H^{1}
- valued, a natural question is how the terms
\Delta m^{\prime}
,

are understood, in spite of not assuming the existence of a second derivative. We understand the terms as elements of
(H^{1})^{\prime}
.
First, let

and
\phi\in H^{1}
.
Using Stokes theorem (see for example
[
65
]
), we have the following.

(1)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta v,\psi\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=-\left\langle\nabla v,\nabla\psi\right\rangle_{L^{2}},

(3.4)

(2)

\displaystyle\left\langle v\times\Delta v,\psi\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\left\langle v\times\nabla v,\nabla\psi\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Therefore for

, the terms
\Delta v
,

are understood as elements of
(H^{1})^{\prime}
as follows.

(1)

\ {}_{(H^{1})^{\prime}}\left\langle\Delta v,\phi\right\rangle_{H^{1}}=-\left\langle\nabla v,\nabla\phi\right\rangle_{L^{2}},\ \phi\in H^{1},

(3.5)

(2)

\ {}_{(H^{1})^{\prime}}\left\langle v\times\Delta v,\phi\right\rangle_{H^{1}}=-\left\langle v\times\nabla v,\nabla\phi\right\rangle_{L^{2}},\ \phi\in H^{1}.

(3.6)

(Existence of a Weak Martingale Solution)

Let

Proof of Lemma

We give a proof only for the second inequality.
Recall that

\displaystyle\left|\Phi(l,v)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\int_{0}^{1}l\left\langle\bar{g}_{n}\bigl(\Phi(s,l,v)\bigr),\Phi(s,l,v)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

Consider the mapping

\displaystyle\left\{\psi:H_{n}\ni v\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

\displaystyle\psi\bigl(m_{n}(t)\bigr)-\psi(m_{n}(0))=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\psi^{\prime}(m_{n}(s))\biggl(F_{n}\bigl(m_{n}(s)\bigr)\biggr)\,ds

\displaystyle\psi(m_{n}(t))+

(3.38)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left[\psi\biggl(\Phi\bigl(l,m_{n}(s-)\bigr)\biggr)-\psi\bigl(m_{n}(s-)\bigr)-l\left\langle\psi^{\prime}\bigl(m_{n}(s)\bigr),\bar{g}\bigl(m_{n}(s)\bigr)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\,\nu(dl)\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}C\left[\biggl(1+\psi\bigl(m_{n}(s-)\bigr)\biggr)+\psi\bigl(m_{n}(s-)\bigr)+\left|l\right|\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}\bigl(1+\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}\bigr)\right]\,\nu(dl)\,ds

(3.39)

Combining this with the inequality (

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{H^{1}}^{2}\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{4}}^{4}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C\left|m_{n}(0)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left[\psi\bigl(\Phi(l,m_{n}(s-))\bigr)-\psi\bigl(m_{n}(s-)\bigr)\right]\tilde{\eta}(ds,dl)\right|

The second term and the third term on the left hand side are non-negative and hence can be neglected, still preserving the inequality.

Definition 3.13

(Aldous Condition)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of cádlág, adapted stochastic processes in a Banach space
E
. We say that the sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the Aldous condition on
E
if for every
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for every sequence
\left\{\tau_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times with
\tau_{n}+\theta\leq T
one has

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq\delta}\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\|X_{n}\left(\tau_{n}+\theta\right)-X_{n}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\|_{E}\geq\eta\bigr\}\leq\varepsilon.

(3.62)

We now state a result (see Métivier
[
54
]
, Motyl
[
55
]
) stating a condition which guarantees that the sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the Aldous condition (as in Definition
3.13
) in a separable Banach space.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
E
be a separable Banach space. Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
E
-valued random variables. Assume that for every sequence
\left\{\tau_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times with
\tau_{n}\leq T
, and
\theta\geq 0
, with
\tau_{n}+\theta\leq T
, we have

\mathbb{E}\biggl[\bigl\|X_{n}(\tau_{n}+\theta)-X_{n}(\tau_{n})\bigr\|_{E}^{\gamma_{1}}\biggr]\leq C\theta^{\gamma_{2}},

(3.63)

for some
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
and some constant

. Then the sequence
\left\{X_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the Aldous condition in
E
.

Definition 3.15

(Modulus of Continuity,
[
54
]
)
.

Let
\left(\mathbb{S},\rho\right)
be a complete separable metric space. Let

be given. A modulus of continuity of

w_{[0,T],\mathbb{S}}\left(u,\delta\right):=\inf_{\prod_{\delta}}\max_{t_{i}\in\bar{w}}\sup_{t_{i}\leq s<t<t_{i+1}\leq T}\rho\bigl(u(t)-u(s)\bigr),

(3.64)

where
\prod_{\delta}
is the set of all increasing sequences
\bar{w}=\left(0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}=T\right)

t_{i+1}-t_{i}\geq\delta,\ i=0,1,\dots n-1.

We state the following criterion (see Chapter II in
[
54
]

\mathbb{S}
is a complete separable metric space with metric

Theorem 3.16
.

A set
A\subset\mathbb{D}([0,T]:\mathbb{S})
has compact closure if and only if it satisfies the following conditions

(a)

such that for each

\left\{u(t),u\in A\right\}
has compact closure in

(b)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{u\in A}w_{[0,T]}\left(u,\delta\right)=0.

(Motyl

]

Using the continuous embedding

along with the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have the following.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left(1+\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)m_{n}(s)\,ds\right|_{X^{-\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}m_{n}(s)\,ds\right|_{X^{-\beta}}+\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}m_{n}(s)\,ds\right|_{X^{-\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}\,ds+C\mathbb{E}\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{4}}^{3}\,ds+C\mathbb{E}\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\theta^{\frac{1}{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{4}}^{4}\,ds\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}+C\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\theta^{\frac{1}{4}}+\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

(3.80)

Calculations for
I_{4}
:

Using the continuous embedding
X^{-\beta}\hookrightarrow L^{2}
along with the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have the following.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}b_{n}(m_{n}(s))\,ds\right|_{X^{-\beta}}\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|b_{n}(m_{n}(s))\right|_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left|b_{n}(l,m_{n}(s))\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}C\left(\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\left(1+\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\,ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}C.

(3.81)

Calculations for
I_{5}
:
By the Itô-Lévy Isometry, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\int_{B}G_{n}(l,m_{n}(s))\,\tilde{\eta}(dl,ds)\right|_{X^{-\beta}}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\int_{B}G_{n}(l,m_{n}(s))\,\tilde{\eta}(dl,ds)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\int_{B}\left|G_{n}(l,m_{n}(s))\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\nu(dl)\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{n}}^{\tau_{n}+\theta}\left(1+\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\theta C\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(1+\left|m_{n}(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\theta C.

(3.82)

Combining the above estimates completes the proof for the lemma.

The Large Deviations Principle

We remind the reader that we will be restricting ourselves to dimensions

2

5.1.
Background, Definitions, Formulation of the Problem

Definition 5.1

(Good rate function)
.

Let
Z
be a Polish space. A function
I:Z\to[0,\infty]
is called a
rate function
if
I
is lower semicontinuous. A rate function
I
is a good rate function if for arbitrary
M\in[0,\infty)
, the level set
K_{M}=\{x:I(x)\leq M\}
is a compact subset of
Z
.

Definition 5.2

(Large deviations principle)
.

A family of probability measures
\{\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon>0\}
is said to satisfy the Large Deviations Principle (LDP) on
Z
with a good rate funciton
I:Z\to[0,\infty]
if

(1)

for each closed set
F_{1}\subset Z
,

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{1}\right)\leq-\inf_{x\in F_{1}}I(x),

(5.1)

(2)

for each open set
F_{2}\subset Z
,

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{2}\right)\geq-\inf_{x\in F_{2}}I(x).

(5.2)

For
\varepsilon>0
, our aim is to establish Freidlin-Wentzell type large deviations of the solutions of the following equation on a given probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
:

\displaystyle dm^{\varepsilon}(t)=\left(\Delta m^{\varepsilon}(t)+m^{\varepsilon}(t)\times\Delta m^{\varepsilon}(t)-\left(1+\left|m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right|^{2}\right)m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\,dt+\varepsilon\bar{g}\left(m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\diamond dL^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(t),\ t\geq 0,

(5.3)

with
m^{\varepsilon}(0)=m_{0}
.

We establish some notations here in order to introduce the stochastic control equation in Section
5.5
. We refer the reader to
[
69
,
50
]
for more details.

Let
\eta
denote the time homogeneous Poisson Random Measure (PRM) associated with
L
. Therefore

\eta\bigl([0,t]\times A\bigr):=\#\left\{s\in[0,t]:L(s)-L(s-)\in A\right\}.

If
\nu
is the compensator, then

t\nu(A):=\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\bigl([0,t]\times A\bigr)\right].

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
for now. The Lévy process
L
(with the continuous part 0) is scaled as follows.

L^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(t):=L(\varepsilon^{-1}t),\ t\geq 0.

(5.4)

Let
\eta^{\varepsilon^{-1}}
denote the PRM corresponding to the process
L^{\varepsilon^{-1}}
. One can show then that

\eta^{\varepsilon^{-1}}\left([0,t]\times A\right)=\eta\bigl([0,\varepsilon^{-1}t]\times A\bigr).

(5.5)

Hence,

\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}=\varepsilon^{-1}\nu.

(5.6)

The compensated PRM corresponding to
L^{\varepsilon^{-1}}
, denoted by
\tilde{\eta}
, is given by

\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}}=\eta^{\varepsilon^{-1}}-t\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}=\eta^{\varepsilon^{-1}}-\varepsilon^{-1}t\nu.

(5.7)

The subsection defines the Marcus mapping corresponding to the jump process (for the equation (

)).
Let

denote the solution to the ordinary differential equation

.

\displaystyle dm^{\varepsilon}(t)=

\displaystyle+\int_{B}\bigl[\Phi^{\varepsilon}\bigl(1,l,m^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)-m^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr]\,\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl,dt)

\displaystyle+\int_{B}\bigl[\Phi^{\varepsilon}\bigl(1,l,m^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)-m^{\varepsilon}(t)-\varepsilon l\bar{g}\bigl(m^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)\bigr]\,\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl)\,dt,

(5.10)

\displaystyle dm^{\varepsilon}(t)=

\displaystyle\left(\Delta m^{\varepsilon}(t)+m^{\varepsilon}(t)\times\Delta m^{\varepsilon}(t)-\left(1+\left|m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right|^{2}\right)m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{B}\bigl[\Phi\bigl(\varepsilon,l,m^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)-m^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr]\,\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl,dt)

\displaystyle+\int_{B}\left[\Phi\left(\varepsilon,l,m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right)-m^{\varepsilon}(t)-\varepsilon l\bar{g}\left(m^{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\right]\,\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl)\,dt.

(5.11)

5.3.
Notations, some spaces:

We recall that
B=B(0,1)\backslash\{0\}
. Let
B_{T}=[0,T]\times B
. Let
\mathbb{M}=\mathcal{M}(B_{T})
denote the space of all measures
\mathcal{V}
on
\left(B_{T},\mathcal{B}(B_{T})\right)
such that
\mathcal{V}(K)<\infty
, for any compact subset
K\subset B_{T}
.
We endow
\mathbb{M}
with the topology
\mathcal{T}\left(\mathbb{M}\right)
, which is the weakest topology such that for each
v\in C_{C}(B_{T})
, the map

\mathcal{V}\mapsto\left(v,\mathcal{V}\right):=\int_{B_{T}}v(l,s)\,\mathcal{V}(dl,ds),

(5.15)

is continuous. This topology can be metrized such that
\mathbb{M}
is a Polish space.
Let
X=B\times[0,\infty)
and
X_{T}=B_{T}\times[0,\infty)
. The spaces
\bar{\mathbb{M}}=\mathcal{M}(X_{T})
and
\mathcal{T(\bar{\mathbb{M}})}
are defined analogously.
There exists a unique probability measure
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
on
\left(\bar{\mathbb{M}},\mathcal{T}\left(\bar{\mathbb{M}}\right)\right)
such that the canonical map
\bar{\eta}(\bar{m})=\bar{m}
is a Poisson random measure with intensity measure
\nu(dl)\,dt\,dr
. The corresponding compensated Poisson random measure is denoted by
\tilde{\bar{\eta}}
and given by

\tilde{\bar{\eta}}\left(dt\,dl\,dr\right):=\bar{\eta}\left(dt\,dl\,dr\right)=\nu(dl)\,dt\,dr.

(5.16)

Let
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
denote the completion of
\sigma\left\{\bar{\eta}\left((0,s]\times D\right):s\in[0,t],\ D\in\mathbb{X}\right\}
under
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
. Let
\bar{\mathbb{F}}:=\left\{\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. Let
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
denote the
\bar{\mathbb{F}}
predictable sigma field on
[0,T]\times\bar{\mathbb{M}}
, with the filtration
\left\{\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}
on
\left(\bar{\mathbb{M}},\mathcal{B}\left(\bar{\mathbb{M}}\right)\right)
. Let
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
denote the class of all
\left(\bar{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{B}(B)\right)\backslash\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))
-measurable maps

\phi:B_{T}\times\bar{\mathbb{M}}\to[0,\infty).

For
\phi\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
, define a counting process
\eta_{C}^{\phi}
(denoted by
\eta^{\phi}
for brevity) on
B
by

\eta^{\phi}\left((0,t]\times D\right)=\int_{\left((0,t]\times D\right)}\int_{(0,\infty)}\chi_{[0,\phi(s,l)]}(r)\,\bar{\eta}\left(ds\,dl\,dr\right),\ t\in[0,T],\ D\in\mathcal{B}(B).

(5.17)

Similarly, we define

\tilde{\eta}^{\phi}\left((0,t]\times D\right)=\int_{\left((0,t]\times D\right)}\int_{(0,\infty)}\chi_{[0,\phi(s,l)]}(r)\,\tilde{\bar{\eta}}\left(ds\,dl\,dr\right),\ t\in[0,T],\ D\in\mathcal{B}(B).

(5.18)

\eta^{\phi}
is a controlled Poisson random measure. Clearly,

\tilde{\eta}^{\phi}\left((0,t]\times D\right)=\eta^{\phi}\left((0,t]\times D\right)-\int_{(0,t]\times D}\phi(s,l)\,\nu(dl)\,ds.

(5.19)

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

\mathcal{S}^{K}:=\left\{\theta:B_{T}\to[0,\infty):\mathcal{L}_{T}\leq K\right\},

(5.20)

where

\mathcal{L}_{T}:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B}\biggl[\biggl(\theta(t,l)\log\bigl(\theta(t,l)\bigr)\biggr)-\theta(t,l)+1\biggr]\nu(dl)\,dt.

(5.21)

A function
\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{K}
can be identified with a measure
\nu^{\theta}\in\mathbb{M}
, defined by

\nu^{\theta}(A_{T})=\int_{A_{T}}\theta(t,l)\,\nu(dl)\,dt,\ A_{T}\in\mathcal{B}(B_{T}).

(5.22)

That is,

\frac{\nu^{\theta}(dl,dt)}{\nu(dl)dt}=\theta.

(5.23)

This identification induces a topology on
\mathcal{S}^{K}
, under which
\mathcal{S}^{K}
is a compact space. Let us denote

\mathbb{S}=\cup_{K\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathcal{S}^{K}.

(5.24)

For
\phi\in\mathbb{S}
, we have

\tilde{\eta}^{\phi}\left(A_{T}\right)=\eta^{\phi}\left(A_{T}\right)-\nu^{\phi}\left(A_{T}\right),\ A_{T}\in\mathcal{B}(B_{T}).

(5.25)

The bounds (especially linear growth) established in Lemma
2.4
can also be shown for the operators
G(\varepsilon,l,\cdot),\ H(\varepsilon,l,\cdot)
. In particular, we make precise the dependence of the constants on
\varepsilon
in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
l\in B
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that the following inequalities hold.

(1)

\left|G\left(\varepsilon,l,v\right)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left|l\right|\left(e^{C\varepsilon}-1\right)\left(1+\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}\right),

(5.26)

(2)

\left|H\left(\varepsilon,l,v\right)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left|l\right|\left(e^{C\varepsilon}-1\right)\left(1+\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}\right).

(5.27)

Hence, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left|b\left(\varepsilon,v\right)\right|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(e^{C\varepsilon}-1\right)\left(1+\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}\right).

(5.28)

Remark 5.6
.

We observe that in Lemma
2.4
, the operators

defined above with

2.4
, we can conclude Lipschitz continuity for the above mentioned operators.

(5.29)

with

Theorem 5.7
.

\theta\in\mathbb{S}
,

(5.31)

We postpone the proof of this theorem to Appendix
A
.

(5.32)

\mathbb{D}([0,T]:L^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T:H^{1})
.

The Map
J^{0}
:

5.5.
The Stochastic Control Equation

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\phi\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
. We consider the following stochastic partial differential equation.

\displaystyle dm^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)=

\displaystyle\left(\Delta m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)+m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)\times\Delta m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)-\left(1+\left|m^{\varepsilon,\phi}\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\right)m^{\varepsilon,\phi}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{B}\left[\Phi\left(\varepsilon,l,m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)\right)-m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)\right]\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}(dl,dt)

\displaystyle+\int_{B}\left[\Phi\left(\varepsilon,l,m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)\right)-m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)-\varepsilon l\bar{g}\left(m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)\right)\right]\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1},\phi}(dl)\,dt

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\int_{B}l\bar{g}\left(m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)\right)\left(\phi\left(t,l\right)-1\right)\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl)\,dt,\ t\in[0,T].

(5.33)

The above equation (
5.5
) can be written in the integral form and with the operators
G,H,b
as follows. To simplify notation, we denote
m^{\varepsilon,\phi}(t)
by
M(t)
.

\displaystyle M(t)=

\displaystyle m_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Delta M(s)+M(s)\times\Delta M(s)-\left(1+\left|M(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)M(s)\right)\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}G\left(\varepsilon,l,M(s)\right)\,\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}(dl,ds)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}G\left(\varepsilon,l,M(s)\right)\left(\phi(s,l)-1\right)\,\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl)\,ds

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}b\left(\varepsilon,M(s)\right)\,ds.

(5.34)

Let
\{K_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of compact sets such that
\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}=B
. Let

\bar{\Omega}=\bar{\mathbb{M}},\ \bar{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{T}\left(\bar{\mathbb{M}}\right).

Let us denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{b}:=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\phi\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}:\phi\left(t,x,\omega\right)\in\left[\frac{1}{n},n\right]\text{ if }\left(t,x,\omega\right)\in[0,T]\times K_{n}\times\bar{\Omega}

and

\displaystyle\phi\left(t,x,\omega\right)=1\text{ if }\left(t,x,\omega\right)\in[0,T]\times K_{n}^{c}\times\bar{\Omega}\bigg\}.

(5.35)

We now give a brief proof of the existence of a weak martingale solution to the above equation (
5.5
).

Theorem 5.9
.

Fix
0<T<\infty
. Let
\phi\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{b}
be such that
\text{esssup}_{\omega\in\bar{\Omega}}\mathcal{L}_{T}(\phi)<\infty
. Then the problem (
5.5
) admits a weak martingale solution.

Proof of Theorem
5.9
.

Let
\phi\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{b}
. Let
\psi=\phi^{-1}
. Clearly,
\psi\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
. Therefore there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a compact set
K_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\psi\left(t,l,\omega\right)

\displaystyle\in\left[\frac{1}{n},n\right]\ \text{for}\ \left(t,l,\omega\right)\in[0,T]\times K_{n}\times\bar{\Omega},

and

\displaystyle\psi\left(t,l,\omega\right)

\displaystyle=1\ \text{for}\ \left(t,l,\omega\right)\in[0,T]\times K_{n}^{c}\times\bar{\Omega},

(5.36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}(\psi):=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg\{\int_{(0,t]\times B\times[0,\varepsilon^{-1}\phi(s,l)]}\log\left(\psi(s,l)\right)\bar{\eta}(dl,ds,dr)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times B\times[0,\varepsilon^{-1}\phi(s,l)]}\left(-\psi(s,l)+1\right)\nu(dl)ds\,dr\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg\{\int_{(0,t]\times K_{n}\times[0,\varepsilon^{-1}\phi(s,l)]}\log\left(\psi(s,l)\right)\bar{\eta}(dl,ds,dr)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times K_{n}\times[0,\varepsilon^{-1}\phi(s,l)]}\left(-\psi(s,l)+1\right)\nu(dl)ds\,dr\bigg\}.

(5.37)

(1)

By Lemma 2.3 in
[
27
]
,
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\psi)
defined above is a
\bar{\mathbb{F}}
-martingale on
\left(\bar{\Omega},\bar{\mathcal{F}},\bar{\mathbb{F}},\bar{\mathbb{P}}\right)
.

(2)

Therefore by Theorem
5.4
(a similar formulation can be done for the problem (
5.5
) with
\phi
satisfying the required regularity criterion), the problem (
5.5
) admits a unique martingale solution. The tuple

\left(\bar{\Omega},\bar{\mathcal{F}},\bar{\mathbb{F}},\mathbb{P}^{\varepsilon}_{T},M^{\varepsilon}:=J^{\varepsilon}\left(\eta^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}\right),\eta^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}\right),

(5.38)

is a weak martingale solution to the above mentioned Stochastic Control Equation (

).

(3)

The formula

\mathbb{P}_{T}^{\varepsilon}(A)=\int_{A}\mathcal{E}_{T}(\psi)\,d\bar{\mathbb{P}},\ A\in\bar{\mathcal{F}},

(5.39)

defines a probability measure on

\mathbb{P}_{T}^{\varepsilon}
and

are equivalent on

\bar{\mathbb{P}}(A)\leq C_{1}\mathbb{P}_{T}^{\varepsilon}(A)\leq C_{2}\bar{\mathbb{P}}(A),\quad\ \forall A\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}.

(5.40)

(4)

On

Therefore by (2), Theorem
5.4

5.5.1.
Pathwise Uniqueness:

We reiterate here that we restrict ourselves to
d=1,2
. We now show that the obtained martingale solution in Theorem
5.9
is Pathwise unique and thereby also complete the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
d=1,2
. Let
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual assumptions. Let
M_{1},M_{2}
denote two solutions of (
5.5
) corresponding to
\phi
as in Theorem
5.9
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

M_{1}(t)=M_{2}(t)\ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

(5.41)

Proof.

Let
M_{1},M_{2}
be as given in the statement of the theorem.
Then the process
M=M_{1}-M_{2}
satisfies the following equality.

\displaystyle M(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Delta M(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left(M_{1}(s)\times\Delta M_{1}(s)-M_{2}(s)\times\Delta M_{2}(s)\right)\,ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\left[\left(1+\left|M_{1}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)M_{1}(s)+\left(1+\left|M_{2}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)M_{2}(s)\right]\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left[G\bigl(\varepsilon,l,M_{1}(s)\bigr)-G\bigl(\varepsilon,l,M_{2}(s)\bigr)\right]\,\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}(dl,ds)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left[G\bigl(\varepsilon,l,M_{1}(s)\bigr)-G\bigl(\varepsilon,l,M_{2}(s)\bigr)\right]\left(\phi(s,l)-1\right)\,\nu^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(dl)\,ds

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[b\bigl(\varepsilon,M_{1}(s)\bigr)-b\bigl(\varepsilon,M_{2}(s)\bigr)\bigr]\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{6}C_{i}I_{i}(t).

(5.42)

We apply the Itô formula (see Gyöngi and Krylov
[
40
]
, see also Appendix in
[
18
]
) to the function

\left\{L^{2}\ni v\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left|v\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

The resulting equation is the following.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|M(t)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Delta M(s),M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\left(M_{1}(s)\times\Delta M_{1}(s)-M_{2}(s)\times\Delta M_{2}(s)\right),M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\left[\left(1+\left|M_{1}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)M_{1}(s)+\left(1+\left|M_{2}(s)\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}\right)M_{2}(s)\right],M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\left[b\big(\varepsilon,M_{1}(s)\big)-b\big(\varepsilon,M_{2}(s)\big)\right],M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left\langle\left[G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{1}(s)\big)-G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{2}(s)\big)\right],M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}(dl,ds)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\left|G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{1}(s)\big)-G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{2}(s)\big)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{\varepsilon^{-1}\phi}(dl,ds)

\displaystyle+\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\phi(s,l)\left|G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{1}(s)\big)-G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{2}(s)\big)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\nu(dl)\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\bigl(\phi(s,l)-1\bigr)\left\langle\bigl[G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{1}(s)\big)-G\big(\varepsilon,l,M_{2}(s)\big)\bigr],M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,\nu(dl)\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}C_{i}I_{i}(t)+\mathcal{M}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{M}_{2}(t)+\sum_{i=5}^{6}I_{i}(t).

(5.43)

For the first term, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Delta M(s),M(s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\,ds=-\int_{0}^{t}\left|\nabla M(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds\leq 0.

(5.44)

Let
C>0
denote a constant. Let us define an auxiliary function
\psi_{C}
as follows. for
d=2

\displaystyle\psi_{C}(t)=

\displaystyle C\bigg(1+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\left|M_{1}(t)\right|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{L^{\infty}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{H^{1}}^{2}\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{H^{1}}\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{H^{2}}\bigg).

(5.45)

The case of
d=1
can be tackled similarly, with
\psi
defined as follows.

\psi_{C}(v)=C\left(1+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\left|M_{1}(t)\right|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{L^{\infty}}\right)+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\left|M_{2}(t)\right|_{H^{1}}^{4}\right).

(5.46)

Note that in both the cases (
d=1,2
),
\psi_{C}
is integrable over
[0,T],\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us define a stopping time
\tau_{N}
as follows.

\tau_{N}=\inf\left\{t:\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|M_{1}(s)\right|_{H^{1}}\wedge\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|M_{2}(s)\right|_{H^{1}}+\int_{0}^{t}\left|M_{1}(s)\right|_{H^{2}}^{2}\,ds\wedge\int_{0}^{t}\left|M_{2}(s)\right|_{H^{2}}^{2}\,ds>N\right\}\wedge T.

(5.47)

Note that due to the energy estimates on the solutions
M_{1},M_{2}
, we have the following for
i=1,2
.

\mathbb{E}\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\left|M_{i}(s)\right|_{H^{1}}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\left|M_{i}(s)\right|_{H^{2}}^{2}\,ds<\infty.

(5.48)

We recall that the mappings
b,\ G
are Lipschitz continuous. Therefore there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|I_{4}(t)\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left|M(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.49)

Similarly, the following holds for
i=5,6
, for some constant
C>0
.

\displaystyle\left|I_{i}(t)\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left|M(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(5.50)

Combining the above calculations, we get (by the choice of
\psi_{C}
)

\displaystyle\left|M(t)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left|M(s)\right|_{H^{1}}^{2}\,ds\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{C}(s)\left|M(s)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,ds+\mathcal{M}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{M}_{2}(t).

We apply the Itô formula to the function

\left|M(t)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\mapsto e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{C}(s)\,ds}\left|M(t)\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For a similar technique, see
[
62
,
50
]
among others. As a result of the formula along with the stoping time
\tau_{N}
and some simplification, we have the following.

\displaystyle e^{-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}}\phi_{C}(s)\,ds}\left|M(t\wedge\tau_{N})\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\phi_{C}(r)\,dr}\bigl(\mathcal{M}_{1}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{2}(s)\bigr)\,ds.

(5.51)

Since
\phi_{C}
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. integrable and non-negative, we have

e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{C}(r)\,dr}\leq 1,\ t\in[0,T].

(5.52)

In particular, the process

\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\phi_{C}(r)\,dr}\bigl(\mathcal{M}_{1}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{2}(s)\bigr)\,ds,\ t\in[0,T],

(5.53)

is a martingale.
Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}}\phi_{C}(s)\,ds}\left|M(t\wedge\tau_{N})\right|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\phi_{C}(r)\,dr}\bigl(\mathcal{M}_{1}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{2}(s)\bigr)\,ds\right]=0.

(5.54)

Due to the bounds assumed on the solutions
M_{1},M_{2}
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\tau_{N}=T.

(5.55)

Therefore (see for example
[
50
]
), for each
t\in[0,T]
,

M_{1}(t)=M_{2}(t),\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(5.56)

This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Existence of a Solution for Skeleton Equation

Proof of Theorem

Brief idea of the proof:
