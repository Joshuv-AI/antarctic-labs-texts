Enumerating combinatorial resultant decompositions of 2-connected rigidity circuits
Goran Malić, Ileana Streinu

Abstract
A rigidity circuit (in 2D) is a minimal dependent set in the rigidity matroid (in 2D), i.e. a minimal graph supporting a non-trivial stress in any generic placement of its vertices in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Any rigidity circuit on n ≥ 5 n\geq 5 vertices can be obtained from rigidity circuits on a fewer number 4 ≤ n ′ < n 4\leq n^{\prime}<n of vertices by applying the combinatorial resultant (CR) operation [ 9 , 11 ] . The inverse operation is called a combinatorial resultant decomposition (CR-decomposition). Therefore, any rigidity circuit on n ≥ 5 n\geq 5 vertices can be successively decomposed into smaller circuits, until the complete graphs K 4 K_{4} are reached. This sequence of CR-decompositions has the structure of a rooted binary tree called the combinatorial resultant tree (CR-tree). A CR-tree encodes an elimination strategy for computing circuit polynomials via Sylvester resultants. Different CR-trees lead to elimination strategies that can vary greatly in time and memory consumption. It is an open problem to establish criteria for optimal CR-trees, or at least to characterize those CR-trees that lead to good elimination strategies. In [ 12 ] we presented an algorithm for enumerating CR-trees where we describe in detail the algorithms for decomposing 3-connected rigidity circuits in polynomial time. In this paper we focus on the rigidity circuits that are not 3-connected, which we simply call 2-connected. In order to enumerate CR-decompositions of a 2-connected rigidity circuit G G , a brute force exponential time search has to be performed among the subgraphs induced by the subsets of V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) . This exponential time bottleneck is not present in the 3-connected case. In this paper we will argue that we do not have to account for all possible CR-decompositions of 2-connected rigidity circuits to find a good elimination strategy; we only have to account for those CR-decompositions that are a 2-split, all of which can be enumerated in polynomial time. We present algorithms and computational evidence in support of this heuristic.

By a better elimination strategy we mean a strategy that will outperform others with regard to elapsed time and the difficulty of computation. We present limited, but revealing evidence that 2-splits outperform other possible elimination strategies.

Preliminaries: 2D rigidity circuits, the Sylvester resultant and circuit polynomials

We start by reviewing, in an informal way, those concepts and results from combinatorial rigidity theory in 2D that are relevant for our paper.

Notation.
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the vertex set
V(G)
of a graph
G
is labeled with a subset of
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
. The
neighbours

N(v)

are the vertices adjacent to
v
in
G
. The support of

is its set of edges

. The

of a set of edges

is the set of all vertices in
[n]
incident to edges in

. A subgraph

. For

such that

(V(G),E(G)\cup e)

Rigid graphs.
We say that a graph
G=(V,E)
is
rigid
if, up to rigid motions, the number possible placements with respect to any set of generic edge-lengths is finite, and
flexible
otherwise. A
minimally rigid
or
Laman
graph is a rigid graph in 2 dimensions such that the deletion of any edge results in a flexible graph. A graph with
n\geq 2
vertices is a Laman graph if and only if it (a) has a total of
2n-3
edges, and (b) it is (2,3)-sparse, i.e. no subset of
n^{\prime}\leq n
vertices spans more than
2n^{\prime}-3
edges. A
Laman-plus-one graph
is obtained by adding one edge to a Laman graph: it has
2n-2
edges and thus doesn’t satisfy the
(2,3)
-sparsity condition (b).

Rigidity Matroids.
A matroid is a pair
(E,\mathcal{I})
of a finite ground set
E
and a collection of subsets
\mathcal{I}
of
E
called
independent sets
, which satisfy certain axioms
[
13
]
which we skip (they are not relevant for our presentation). A
base
is a maximal independent set. A set which is not independent is called
dependent
and a
minimal dependent set
is called a
circuit
. For our purposes the following facts are relevant: (a) (hereditary property) a subset of an independent set is also independent; (b) all bases have the same cardinality, called the
rank
of the matroid; (c) (the unique circuit property) if
I
is independent and
I\cup\{e\}
dependent for some
e\in E
, then there exists a unique circuit in
I\cup\{e\}
that contains
e
.

The
rigidity matroid
is defined on a ground set given by all the edges
E_{n}:=\{ij:1\leq i<j\leq n\}
of the complete graph
K_{n}
. Its bases are the Laman graphs on
n
vertices. A
Laman-plus-one
graph is a dependent graph obtained by adding an edge to a Laman graph, thus it has exactly
2n-2
edges. A
rigidity circuit
is a circuit in the rigidity matroid. It is a dependent graph with minimum edge support: deleting any edges leads to a Laman graph on its vertex set. In particular, a circuit in the rigidity matroid is a graph on
k\leq n
vertices which (a) spans exactly
2k-2
edges and (b) any subset of
k^{\prime}<k
vertices spans at most
2k^{\prime}-3
edges. A circuit whose vertex set is
V=[n]
is said to be a
spanning rigidity circuit
(Fig.
1
). A spanning rigidity circuit
C=(V,E)
is a special case of a Laman-plus-one graph. Furthermore, a Laman-plus-one graph contains a unique circuit, which in general is not spanning.

In the rest of a paper rigidity circuits will simply be called circuits.

Circuit polynomials in the algebraic rigidity matroid.
Consider
n
points in the Euclidean plane and let
x_{i,j}
denote the squared distance between points
i
and
j
. The
Cayley-Menger matrix
on
n
points is the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix obtained by augmenting the
n\times n
Euclidean distance matrix on
n
points with a top row
(0~1~\cdots~1)
and a leftmost column
(0~1~\cdots~1)^{\textnormal{T}}
of dimension
n+1
. The
Cayley-Menger ideal

\operatorname{CM}_{n}
on
n
points is the prime ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n]
generated by all
5\times 5
minors of the Cayley-Menger matrix on
n
points.

The algebraic rigidity matroid on
n
points is a matroid on ground set
X_{n}=\{x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
. Its collection of independent sets
\mathcal{I}
given by the subsets

such that
\operatorname{CM}_{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}[I]=\{0\}
. The algebraic rigdity matroid and the rigidity matroid are isomorphic. This result is folklore; we provided a proof in
[
11
]
.

A circuit in the algebraic rigidity matroid is a minimal
C\subset X_{n}
such that
\operatorname{CM}_{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}[C]\neq\{0\}
. It follows from a result of Dress and Lovasz
[
5
]
that for a circuit

the ideal
\operatorname{CM}_{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}[C]
is principal and generated by a polynomial
p_{C}\in\mathbb{Q}[C]
unique up to multiplication with a rational number and irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. The polynomial

is called the

for

.

Sylvester resultant.
Let
f=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots a_{m}x^{m}
and
g=b_{0}+b_{1}x+\cdots b_{n}x^{n}
be polynomials of degrees
m
and
n
, with coefficients in a polynomial ring
R
. The Sylvester matrix
\operatorname{Syl}(f,g,x)
of
f
and

is the
(m+n)\times(m+n)
whose entries are the coefficients of

and
g
:

\operatorname{Syl}(f,g,x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{m}&a_{m-1}&a_{m-2}&\cdots&a_{0}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&a_{m}&a_{m-1}&\cdots&a_{1}&a_{0}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&a_{m}&\cdots&a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a_{m}&a_{m-1}&a_{m-2}&\cdots&a_{0}\\
b_{n}&b_{n-1}&b_{n-2}&\cdots&b_{0}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&b_{s}&b_{n-1}&\cdots&b_{1}&b_{0}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&b_{s}&\cdots&b_{2}&b_{1}&b_{0}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&b_{s}&b_{n-1}&b_{n-2}&\cdots&b_{0}\end{pmatrix}

The submatrix consisting of the coefficients of

is of dimension

and the submatrix consisting of the coefficients of

is of dimension

. Note that the columns align as show above only when

The

. We say that the Sylvester resultant

the variable

from

and

[
2
]
.
We will be using the following lemma when discussing computational experiments.

Lemma 2
.

[
11
]

Let

, where

itself is a ring of polynomials. Denote by
\deg_{x}f
and
\deg_{x}g
the degrees in

and

, and let

and

denote their homogeneous degrees. The homogeneous degree of the Sylvester resultant

is

h_{f}\cdot\deg_{x}g+h_{g}\cdot\deg_{x}f-\deg_{x}f\cdot\deg_{x}g.

Combinatorial resultants and combinatorial resultant trees

This section reviews basic definitions on combinatorial resultants and combinatorial resultant trees from
[
9
,
11
]
. Recall that
V_{\cup}=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
and

.

Definition 1
.

If

exists, we say that the graph
(V_{\cup},E_{\cup}-\{e\})
is the

of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
on edge

, and denote by
\operatorname{CRes}(G_{1},G_{2},e)
.

We will use the abbreviation CR in place of “combinatorial resultant” for the rest of the paper.

CRs of circuits.

Given two circuits
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
with non-empty edge set intersection and
e\in E_{1}\cap E_{2}
, their CR with respect to edge

is not always a circuit. When the common part
(V_{\cap},E_{\cap})
is a Laman graph on its vertex set

, then the CR is necessarily a Laman-plus-one graph but not always a circuit. Identifying the necessary and sufficient conditions for a CR of circuts to be a circuit is an open problem
[
9
,
11
]
. However, we will work exclusively with the case when the CR of two circuits is a circuit.

Definition 2
.

If a circuit
G
is obtained as a
G=\operatorname{CRes}(G_{1},G_{2},e)
of two other circuits
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, then we say that
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
is a

.

Theorem 1

(
[
9
,
11
]
)
.

Every

-connected circuit

with

vertices has a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
such that

is 3-connected and has

G_{2}
has at most

vertices (but not necessarily 3-connected).

A circuit that is not 3-connected has a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
in which both

and
G_{2}
have at most
n-1
vertices (and both might not be 3-connected).

That

is the circuit which is necessarily 3-connected when

is 3-connected is merely a convention that we keep throughout this paper. In general we can interchange

and

arbitrarily.

The proof of

for 3-connected circuits relies on the existence of non-adjacent pairs of
admissible vertices
in 3-connected circuits

of degree 3 in

such that for some non-edge

of

with endpoints in

the graph

is again a 3-connected circuit

]
. Such vertices may be absent from circuits that are not 3-connected (cf.

Theorem

provides an approach for computing circuit polynomials that avoids the costly Gröbner basis computation with respect to an elimination order. In

,

]
we prove the following

Theorem 2

(
[

,
11
]
)
.

Let

be a circuit with a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
and assume that the circuit polynomials

and

for

for

To determine which irreducible factor of

Overview of the 3-connected CR-decomposition algorithm.
The proof of
Theorem

1
is constructive, and a polynomial time algorithm for computing a CR-decomposition of a 3-connected circuit is presented in detail in
[
12
]
. For completeness we recall it here briefly: first identify a pair of non-adjacent admissible vertices
\{v,w\}
of a circuit
G
. Set
G_{1}=G-v+e
where
e
is a non-edge of
G
with vertices in
N(v)
such that
G_{1}
is a 3-connected circuit (at least one such

is guaranteed to exist
[
1
]
). For
G_{2}
consider first

. The graph

is a Laman graph, and therefore a basis in the rigidity matroid. Therefore

is dependent and there exists a unique circuit in

containing

. We take

to be that unique circuit.

A CR-decomposition is not unique, and moreover not all CR-decompositions are captured by this theorem; certain circuits

can have a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
such that one or both of
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
have the same number of vertices as

. However, we are interested in inductive constructions from which smaller circuits are used to build larger ones, hence for that purpose we are interested only in the CR-decompositions which produce circuits with fewer vertices than
G
.

CR-trees.

Given a rigidity circuit
G
with
n\geq 5
we can construct a tree by repeatedly applying
Theorem

1
to 3-connected circuits or 2-splits to 2-connected circuits. This construction stops once all leaves are

circuits. We call this tree a

.

Definition 3
.

Let
G
be a rigidity circuit with
n\geq 5
vertices. A
combinatorial resultant tree (CR-tree)
for
G
is a rooted binary tree with its root labeled with
G
, such that:

(i)

each node is labeled with a rigidity circuit;

(ii)

leaves are labeled with graphs isomorphic to
K_{4}
;

(iii)

children of non-leaf nodes
G^{\prime}
have labels
L
and
R
which satisfy
G^{\prime}=\operatorname{CRes}(L,R,e)
for some
e\in L\cap R
and both have fewer vertices than
G^{\prime}
.

Recall that
Theorem

1
states that for 3-connected circuits
G
with a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
, the circuit
G_{1}
is the one that has
n-1
vertices and is 3-connected by convention. In this definition there is no requirement to keep that convention, or to even make a consistent choice that specifies which one of
L
or
R
is 3-connected with
n-1
vertices. Furthermore, note that in
[
11
]
, this is referred to as a
combinatorial circuit resultant tree
.

A CR-tree is not unique, and already for circuits with a small number of vertices the number of different possibilities grows rapidly
[
12
]
.

Algorithmic aspects and data structures.
We have presented an algorithm for enumerating the CR-trees of a 3-connected circuit in
[
12
]
. It relies on a memoization technique which computes the CR-decompositions only of those circuits that appear for the first time up to isomorphism. Once an isomorphic instance
H
of a previously decomposed circuit
G
is encountered, the CR-tree is truncated at
H
, i.e.
H
becomes a leaf in this tree. Hence in
[
12
]
we are enumerating
truncated CR-trees
.

Definition 4
.

Let
G
be a rigidity circuit with
n\geq 5
vertices. The
first truncated CR-tree
for
G
is obtained as follows. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the collection of pairwise non-isomorphic circuits, currently containing only
G
.

(i)

Compute a CR-decomposition
(L,R,e)
for the root
G
. If
L
and
R
are isomorphic, mark one as truncated (e.g.
R
) and add the other (e.g.
L
) to
\mathcal{C}
. Otherwise add both to
C
. Proceed in a depth-first manner to the first child that hasn’t been truncated.

(ii)

For any circuit
G^{\prime}
computed so far that has not been marked as truncated, compute a CR-decomposition
(L^{\prime},R^{\prime},e^{\prime})
. If
L^{\prime}
(resp.
R^{\prime}
) is not isomorphic to any circuit in
\mathcal{C}
, add it to
\mathcal{C}
. Otherwise, mark it as truncated. Proceed to the first node that hasn’t been truncated in a depth-first manner.

(iii)

Repeat
(ii)
in a depth-first manner until all leaves are a
K_{4}
or marked as truncated.

All remaining truncated CR-trees for
G
are computed in the same way, with the exception that now in step
(i)
both children could be isomorphic to circuits in
\mathcal{C}
.

Truncated CR-trees are motivated as a method of reducing duplicate data. Ideally we want to enumerate isomorphism classes of CR-trees, where
f\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
is defined as a CR-tree isomorphism if it is an isomorphism of rooted binary trees and such that siblings in
T_{1}
are isomorphic to siblings in
T_{2}
. More precisely, if
(G_{1},G_{2})
are siblings in
T_{1}
on nodes
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, then for siblings
(H_{1},H_{2})
on nodes
f(N_{1})
and
f(N_{2})
either (i)
G_{1}\cong H_{1}
and
G_{2}\cong H_{2}
, or (ii)
G_{1}\cong H_{2}
and
G_{2}\cong H_{1}
, where
\cong
denotes graph isomorhpism. However, computing all such isomorphisms is costly and truncated CR-trees mitigate that cost while still reducing the duplicate data to an extent.

Note that if we keep the convention in which
L
is always 3-connected with one vertex fewer than its parent, then the left-most branch of the first truncated CR-tree is never truncated and therefore of maximal depth.

A truncated CR-tree is represented by a data structure that keeps a list of (pointers to)
circuit nodes
(C-nodes), and a list of (pointers to)
branching nodes
(B-nodes). A B-node encodes a single CR-decomposition of a circuit
G
as a triple of pointers to
G
, its left child
L
and its right child
R
. A C-node points to a unique circuit
G
and to a list of B-nodes (one B-node for each possible CR-decomposition of
G
). Moreover it also stores an isomorphism of
G
so that proper relabeling can be applied when necessary. Therefore the space of truncated CR-trees for a circuit
G
is completely encoded by a list of unique circuits appearing in all possible CR-trees of
G
, a list of C-nodes (each C-node pointing to a unique circuit) and a list of B-nodes (one for each CR-decomposition of each unique circuit). This data is sufficient to reconstruct any CR-tree of
G
. Complete details are given in
[
12
]
.

4
The CR-decomposition of a 2-connected circuit

For the rest of the paper we consider only those rigidity circuits
G
that are 2- but not 3-connected. However, at times we will have to compare 2- but not 3-connected circuits to 3-connected circuits; hence, for the purpose of brevity, when we say that a circuit is 2-connected, we will also mean that it is
not
3-connected.

Recall from the previous section that an admissible pair for
G
is a vertex
v
of degree 3 and a non-edge
e
of
G
with vertices in
N(v)
such that
G-v+e
is 3-connected. The main obstacle to applying the CR-decomposition algorithm to 2-connected circuits is that admissible pairs might not exist. This property is exhibited already on the smallest 2-connected circuit, the “
double banana
” shown in the top row of
Fig.

3
. However, it is still possible to decompose the double banana into smaller circuits in two ways: as a 2-sum of two
K_{4}
circuits, or as a combinatorial resultant of two 4-wheels, as shown on the bottom of
Fig.

3
.

Figure 3
:
Left: a CR-decomposition of the double banana into two
K_{4}
graphs which is a 2-split. Right: a CR-decomposition of the double banana into two wheels on 4 vertices with the double triangle 2354 in common. The elimination edge for both cases is shown in red.

Recall that by
Lemma

1
any 2-split of
G
results in two rigidity circuits and by a theorem of Tutte
[
14
]

G
can eventually be decomposed into unique 3-connected components via 2-splits. A 2-split is a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
where
e
is a non-edge between a separating pair of
G
, hence a CR-tree for
G
can be obtained by first splitting
G
into the 3-connected components given by Tutte’s theorem, and then by decomposing each 3-connected component with the CR-decomposition algorithm until we reach
K_{4}
graphs. However,
G
can have CR-decompositions into 3-connected circuits that are not 2-splits, as shown in
Fig.

3
, which can lead to different CR-trees for
G
. For an efficient algorithm that can find all CR-decompositions of 2-connected circuits, we require necessary and sufficient criteria to establish when a CR
\operatorname{CRes}(G_{1},G_{2},e)
is a circuit. The graphs
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
in a CR-decomposition of
G
necessarily have to intersect on a Laman graph by following a sparsity count, however we are not aware of any sufficient criteria.

Open Problem 1
.

Find necessary and sufficient conditions for a 2-connected rigidity circuit on
n\geq 6
vertices to have a CR-decomposition other than a 2-split.

We therefore distinguish between two approaches, a naive exponential-time approach which brute-force enumerates all possible CR-decompositions of a 2-connected circuit in
Section

4.1
, and a partial, linear-time approach which enumerates only those CR-decompositions that are 2-splits in
Section

4.2
.

To motivate the approach which enumerates only the 2-splits, recall that the problem that motivates the enumeration of CR-trees is to find a good algebraic elimination strategy when computing circuit polynomials. We do not need to necessarily know all possible CR-decompositions in order to compute a circuit polynomial, we only need one strategy, or a small set of strategies that will in general outperform all others. Experimental results (c.f.
Section

5
), although currently limited, suggest that decomposing a 2-connected circuit via a 2-split is the better strategy. At this point we can only conjecture that a CR-decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2},e)
that is a 2-split will result in a “simpler” algebraic computation because the variables of the corresponding circuit polynomials are already separated. That is, the only common variable in the supports of the circuit polynomials
p_{G_{1}}
and
p_{G_{2}}
is
x_{e}
, whereas in a CR-decomposition that is not a 2-split, there might be a large number of variables in common.

We conjecture that a decomposition of
G

best possible
in the sense that the corresponding circuit polynomials are the least complicated with respect to the number of indeterminates, number of monomial terms, degrees in individual indeterminates, or the homogeneous degree. We have limited data supporting this conjecture given in Examples 1–5.

Recall that to find a circuit polynomial via
Theorem

2

In all examples provided the Sylvester resultants were all shown to be irreducible, therefore they are the sought circuit polynomials. All computations were performed on a machine with the AMD Ryzen 9 5950x 16-core CPU (with a base 3.4GHz clock overclocked to 5GHz) and 64GB of DDR4 RAM.

6
Concluding remarks

Evidence presented in Examples 1–5 shows that 2-splits outperform other CR-decompositions in those cases and supports our heuristic for choosing a good elimination strategy for computing the circuit polynomials of 2-connected circuits. Whether this extends to larger examples remains an open problem to be tackled by a comprehensive project in which new data structures and algorithms optimized for very large polynomials would have to be considered.
