Minimizing Control for Credit Assignment with Strong Feedback
Alexander Meulemans, Matilde Tristany Farinha, Maria Cervera, João Sacramento, Benjamin F. Grewe

Abstract
The success of deep learning ignited interest in whether the brain learns hierarchical representations using gradient-based learning. However, current biologically plausible methods for gradient-based credit assignment in deep neural networks need infinitesimally small feedback signals, which is problematic in biologically realistic noisy environments and at odds with experimental evidence in neuroscience showing that top-down feedback can significantly influence neural activity. Building upon deep feedback control (DFC), a recently proposed credit assignment method, we combine strong feedback influences on neural activity with gradient-based learning and show that this naturally leads to a novel view on neural network optimization. Instead of gradually changing the network weights towards configurations with low output loss, weight updates gradually minimize the amount of feedback required from a controller that drives the network to the supervised output label. Moreover, we show that the use of strong feedback in DFC allows learning forward and feedback connections simultaneously, using learning rules fully local in space and time. We complement our theoretical results with experiments on standard computer-vision benchmarks, showing competitive performance to backpropagation as well as robustness to noise. Overall, our work presents a fundamentally novel view of learning as control minimization, while sidestepping biologically unrealistic assumptions.

Introduction

The error backpropagation (BP) method
(
Rumelhart et al. 1986
;
Werbos 1982
)
has emerged as the method of choice for training deep artificial neural networks, due to its efficient computation of gradients, which is crucial for learning in high-dimensional parameter spaces
(
LeCun et al. 2015
)
. Although biological networks in the neocortex form similar hierarchies
(
Yamins & DiCarlo 2016
)
, it is not clear yet if they follow similar principles to implement credit assignment (CA), i.e., determining how to change synaptic connectivity strengths to get closer to some desired output. Several key properties of the BP method are highly incompatible with cortical networks
(
Crick 1989
;
Lillicrap et al. 2020
)
. For example, synaptic plasticity in biological neurons is local in space and time and tightly coupled to neural activity. By contrast, artificial neural networks trained with BP process activity and weight update signals separately and in distinct phases while requiring exactly the same synaptic weights in the forward and feedback pathways.

To couple synaptic plasticity directly to neural activity, recent work designed cortical network models that propagate CA signals by leveraging the network dynamics
(
Scellier & Bengio 2017
;
Whittington & Bogacz 2017
;
Sacramento et al. 2018
;
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
or using multiplexed neural signals
(
Payeur et al. 2021
)
. However, these methods require tightly coordinated plasticity mechanisms, possibly through the use of distinct training phases, and it remains unclear whether cortical networks can exhibit these levels of coordination. Moreover, to approximate gradient-based optimization these methods consider the
weak-feedback
limit where, to avoid interference, feedback cannot significantly alter the feedforward processing of the network. This weak-feedback assumption is problematic in biologically realistic noisy environments, and is in stark contrast with biological observations showing that top-down feedback can significantly affect forward processing
(
Jordan & Keller 2020
;
Keller et al. 2020
)
.

To date, several studies used strong feedback control signals in combination with error-based learning rules to drive the network activity to track a reference output
(
Gilra & Gerstner 2017
;
Alemi et al. 2018
;
Denève et al. 2017
;
Bourdoukan & Deneve 2015
)
. Although these models use strong feedback influences on neural activity, they have only been successfully applied to train single-layer recurrent neural networks with fixed feedback weights and it remains unclear whether they can be extended for training deep neural networks. Furthermore, even though these methods are capable of reducing the output loss during training, it remains an open question whether they extract gradient information to enable efficient learning. Hence, gradient-based CA theories for training deep neural networks with strong feedback influences and without the need for tightly coordinated plasticity mechanisms are still lacking.

Building upon Deep Feedback Control (DFC)
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
, we introduce Strong-DFC, a gradient-based approach to CA in deep neural networks that makes use of strong feedback influences on neural activity. Like DFC, we use a feedback controller to drive the network to a desired output target and update the network weights with a learning rule fully local in space and time which leverages the dynamic change in neural activity. Unlike DFC, we allow for strong feedback influences by taking the supervised label as the desired output target instead of weakly nudging the current network output towards the supervised label. This new setting results in significant feedback influence on the neural activities, as the initial network output is far from the supervised label at the beginning of training.

Interestingly, because the output targets remain invariant throughout training, Strong-DFC naturally leads to a fundamentally different perspective on neural network optimization. As the controller drives the network to the supervised output labels at each point in training, the goal of optimizing the network parameters shifts from minimizing the supervised loss towards minimizing the amount of feedback control needed to reach the supervised output labels. To formalize this
minimizing control
perspective we introduce a surrogate loss that quantifies the amount of control feedback, and show that, under flexible conditions on the feedback connectivity, the Strong-DFC updates follow its negative gradient. Intriguingly, Strong-DFC enables us to learn the forward and feedback weights simultaneously, overcoming the two-phase requirement of DFC, while inheriting the close connection to recently proposed dendritic compartment models of cortical pyramidal neurons equipped with voltage-dependent plasticity rules
(
Sacramento et al. 2018
)
. Finally, we complement our theoretical findings with experimental results on standard computer vision benchmarks, showing that Strong-DFC provides principled CA in deep neural networks using strong feedback influences without the need for tightly coordinated plasticity mechanisms.

Background

We revisit DFC
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
to investigate CA with strong feedback influence on neural activities. The control framework used by DFC was designed for feedforward networks with continuous dynamics. More specifically, DFC considers a multilayer network with the following dynamics (for
1\leq i\leq L)
:

\displaystyle\tau_{v}\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)+W_{i}\phi\big(\mathbf{v}_{i-1}(t)\big)+Q_{i}\mathbf{u}(t),

(1)

where
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is a vector of pre-nonlinearity activations in layer
i
,
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
its time derivative,
W_{i}
the forward weights,
\phi
a smooth nonlinear activation function,
Q_{i}
the feedback weights, and
\tau_{v}
the neural time constant. We define
\mathbf{r}_{i}=\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i})
as the post-nonlinearity activity of layer
i
. Note that without feedback influences (
\mathbf{u}=0
), the steady state of the above dynamics corresponds to a feedforward neural network, as the input
\mathbf{r}_{0}
remains constant over time.

The feedback signal
\mathbf{u}
is computed by a proportional integral feedback controller, using the output error
\mathbf{e}(t)=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}-\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)
, and serves to push the network to a desired output target
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{u}^{\text{int}}(t)+k\mathbf{e}(t),

\displaystyle\tau_{u}\dot{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{int}}(t)=\mathbf{e}(t)-\alpha\mathbf{u}^{\text{int}}(t),

(2)

where
k
is the proportional control constant and
\alpha
the leakage constant limiting the magnitude of
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}
. DFC weakly nudges the network output in the direction of lower loss, since the output target
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{-}-\lambda\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}\right|_{\mathbf{r}_{L}=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{-}}^{T},

(3)

with
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{-}
the steady state of the output without the presence of a controller,
\mathcal{L}
the loss function, and
\lambda
the nudging strength. The theoretical results linking DFC to a variant of Gauss-Newton optimization consider the
weak-feedback
regime of
\lambda\rightarrow 0

(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
. Note that we cannot enforce
strong feedback
in this theoretical framework by keeping the small nudging factor
\lambda\rightarrow 0
and making
Q_{i}
arbitrarily large, as the limit considered by
Meulemans et al. 2021
relies on vanishing feedback influence on neural activity, similar to other seminal work in dynamic CA methods that approximate gradient-based optimization
(
Sacramento et al. 2018
;
Scellier & Bengio 2017
;
Whittington & Bogacz 2017
)
. DFC then updates the feedforward weights with the following plasticity rule that is fully local in space and time:

\displaystyle\tau_{W}\dot{W}_{i}(t)=\big(\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i}(t))-\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t))\big)\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t)^{T}.

(4)

Meulemans et al. 2021
.

Using a multi-compartment model of a neuron (Fig.
1
B), one can interpret this learning rule as a delta rule that uses the difference between the outgoing firing rate of the neuron
\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i})
and the estimated firing rate based only on the feedforward input of the neuron
\phi(\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i})=\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i-1})

(
Urbanczik & Senn 2014
)
. Standard DFC learns its feedback weights
Q_{i}
in a separate ‘sleep’ phase with a simple anti-Hebbian learning rule, making use of the feedback compartment of the multi-compartment neuron,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}=Q_{i}\mathbf{u}
. Note that DFC assumes a separation of timescales between the plasticity dynamics (
4
) and the network dynamics (
1
-
2
), such that the weights
W_{i}
and
Q_{i}
can be considered constant on the timescale of the network-controller interactions.

3
Training neural networks with strong feedback by minimizing control

Here, we introduce our
minimizing control
framework that allows for gradient-based CA with strong feedback influences in deep neural networks. We use the feedback controller (
2
) to strongly drive the network towards the supervised output label at each point during training. Consequently, the focus of optimizing the network parameters shifts from minimizing the training loss
\mathcal{L}
towards minimizing the amount of feedback control needed to reach the supervised output label. We quantify the magnitude of the control input with a surrogate loss
\mathcal{H}
, and show that under flexible constraints on the feedback connectivity, the Strong-DFC updates perform gradient descent on this new loss function. Finally, we show that by minimizing
\mathcal{H}
, we implicitly minimize
\mathcal{L}
as well. Throughout this section, we assume the network is stable, on which we elaborate at the end of the section. Although the theoretical results shown here are tailored towards the proportional integral controller (
2
), these can easily be extended to other controller types such as a pure proportional controller (App.
A.5
).

Strong-DFC.

We adapt DFC to allow for strong feedback influence on neural activity by setting the output target
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}
to the supervised output label
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true}}
, instead of weakly nudging it towards values of lower loss (
3
) (see Fig.
1
). This new setting results in strong feedback influence, as the network output without feedback is far from the supervised labels at the beginning of training.
Furthermore, as the existing theory on DFC relies on the assumption that the feedback only infinitesimally changes the network activity, it is no longer valid in this new setting of DFC with strong feedback (Strong-DFC).

Learning as control minimization.

Here, we propose a novel view on neural network optimization that justifies strong feedback influences on neural activity. In Strong-DFC, at every stage of training the feedback controller drives the network to the supervised label
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true}}
, resulting in zero training loss at steady state. Hence, instead of gradually changing the network weights towards configurations with low output loss, the goal of optimizing the network parameters is now to gradually reduce the amount of feedback required from the controller to reach the supervised output label (see Fig.
1
C-D). At the individual neuron level, one can interpret this new perspective on optimization as each neuron minimizing its feedback input by altering its feedforward weights.

We formalize this notion of
minimizing control
by introducing a surrogate loss function representing the magnitude of the feedback control input:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}\|Q\mathbf{u}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{ss}}\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

with
Q\triangleq[Q^{T}_{1},...,Q^{T}_{L}]^{T}
the concatenated feedback weights and
\mathbf{u}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{ss}}
the steady-state control feedback of a data point
b
. The control feedback
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}
depends on the feedforward weights
W_{i}
through the network and controller dynamics (Eq.
1
-
2
). Using the implicit function theorem, in Theorem
1
we compute the total derivative of
\mathcal{H}
w.r.t.
W_{i}
and show that, under flexible constraints on the feedback weights, it results in a local update in space and time that aligns with a simplified version of the Strong-DFC weight updates (full proof in App.
A.1
).

Theorem 1
.

Assuming
J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q+\tilde{\alpha}I
is invertible, the total derivative of
\mathcal{H}
w.r.t.
W_{i}
is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}}{\mathrm{d}W_{i}^{T}}=-\sum_{b}J_{i,\mathrm{ss}}^{(b)T}\left(J_{\mathrm{ss}}^{(b)}Q+\tilde{\alpha}I\right)^{-T}Q^{T}Q\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}^{(b)}\mathbf{r}_{i-1,\mathrm{ss}}^{(b)T},

(6)

with
J_{i,\mathrm{ss}}\triangleq\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{i}}
and
J_{\mathrm{ss}}\triangleq[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}},...,\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}}]
, both evaluated at steady state, and
\tilde{\alpha}\triangleq\frac{\alpha}{1+k\alpha}
.

Furthermore, assuming Condition
1
holds, the network is stable,
\alpha\rightarrow 0
, and
J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q+\tilde{\alpha}I
is invertible, then the following steady-state (ss) updates for the forward weights, averaged over the batch samples,

\displaystyle\Delta W_{i,\mathrm{ss}}=\eta(\mathbf{v}_{i,\mathrm{ss}}-\mathbf{v}_{i,\mathrm{ss}}^{\mathrm{ff}})\mathbf{r}_{i-1,\mathrm{ss}}^{T}\,\,,

(7)

with
\eta
the stepsize, align with the negative gradient
-\frac{\text{d}\mathcal{H}}{\text{d}W_{i}}
.

Condition 1
.

The column space of the concatenated feedback weights
Q
is equal to the row space of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}\triangleq[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}},...,\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}}]\big|_{\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i,\mathrm{ss}}}
, the network Jacobian at steady state.

Theorem
1
links Strong-DFC to gradient descent on a surrogate loss function
\mathcal{H}
. Although the steady-state updates (
7
) of Theorem
1
are not exactly equal to the DFC updates (
4
), previous work showed that it is a good approximation (see App.
A.3
-
A.4
and
Meulemans et al. 2021
).

Flexible feedback connectivity constraint.

Condition
1
is a flexible connectivity constraint on the feedback weights that represents the requirement that the feedback controller influences the network activity in the most efficient manner (see App.
A.2
). More specifically, if Condition
1
is not satisfied, the feedback input
Q\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}
is partially lost in the nullspace of the network and hence the same influence on the network output could be reached with a smaller feedback input. Interestingly, Condition
1
generalizes the feedback requirements of commonly used learning rules for approximating gradient-based optimization methods. For example, backpropagation and its approximations require
Q=J^{T}

(
Rumelhart et al. 1986
;
Lansdell et al. 2020
;
Akrout et al. 2019
)
and common variants of target propagation require the pseudoinverse
Q=J^{\dagger}

(
Meulemans et al. 2020
)
, both satisfying Condition
1
. More generally, Condition
1
implies that the feedback weights of Strong-DFC do not necessarily need to align with the forward pathway or its inverse to provide gradient-based CA by following the negative gradient of
\mathcal{H}
.
Remarkably, the fundamentally distinct theoretical framework of standard DFC gives rise to a similar condition on
Q
, however with
J
evaluated at the feedforward activities without feedback present instead of the steady-state activities
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
.

Minimizing
\mathcal{H}
leads to minimizing
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem
1
links Strong-DFC to gradient descent on the surrogate loss function
\mathcal{H}
. Hence, to show that Strong-DFC minimizes the original loss function
\mathcal{L}
, we need to connect
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{L}
. Proposition
2
shows that if
\mathcal{H}
is minimized to zero,
\mathcal{L}
is minimized to zero as well, which is trivial to prove as
\mathcal{H}=0
translates to all training samples reaching exactly their supervised output label without help from the controller.

Proposition 2
.

For all
\mathcal{L}
that obey
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})=0
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\sum_{b}\|Q\mathbf{u}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{ss}}\|_{2}^{2}=0\iff\sum_{b}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{r}_{L}^{-(b)},\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true,}(b)})=0,

with
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{-}
the steady state of the output without the presence of a controller.

Recent work in the field of deep learning has shown that, for overparameterized networks, it is possible to minimize the training loss
\mathcal{L}
to zero while reaching good generalization
(
Belkin et al. 2019
)
. We show empirically in Section
5
that Strong-DFC can minimize
\mathcal{H}
, and hence
\mathcal{L}
to zero, thereby validating the minimizing control framework for optimizing neural networks.

Stability of Strong-DFC.

So far, we assumed that the network and controller dynamics reach their steady state. However, this is not a given, as the feedback interaction could lead to unstable loops causing the network to diverge. Adapting the results of
Meulemans et al. 2021
, we investigate the local stability of a simplified version of Strong-DFC that uses integral control and assumes a separation of timescales
\tau_{v}\ll\tau_{u}
. At steady state, the Jacobian matrix of this dynamical system is equal to
-(J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q+\alpha I)
, resulting in the following stability condition on the feedback weights (see App.
B
).

Condition 2
.

Given the network Jacobian evaluated at steady state
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
, the real parts of the eigenvalues of
-J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q
are all below
\alpha
.

In App.
B
, we compute the Jacobian matrix of the full dynamical system representing Strong-DFC with proportional integral control and with no assumptions on a separation of timescales. However, a local stability analysis on this system does not reveal clearly interpretable stability conditions for the feedback weights. Therefore, we monitor instead the stability of the full system empirically.

4
A single-phase learning scheme for forward and feedback weights

Conditions
1
and
2
highlight the importance of the feedback weights for learning and stability, respectively. As training causes the forward weights and thus the network Jacobian
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
to change, the set of feedback connectivity patterns satisfying Conditions
1
and
2
shift during learning as well. To keep the feedback weights compatible with the changing network, we learn the feedback weights together with the forward weights.

Here, we introduce plasticity dynamics for the feedback weights that make it possible to update both forward and feedback weights simultaneously in a single phase, in contrast to standard DFC and other methods that need two separate phases. Our new feedback plasticity dynamics leverage two essential properties of Strong-DFC. (i) The strong feedback influences make it possible to have noise in the dynamics while learning the forward weights, while keeping a good signal-to-noise ratio of the learning signals. (ii) The conditions on the feedback weights for learning and stability required for our minimizing control framework consider the network activity at steady state. Hence, the feedback weights should be learned at the same steady state used for training the forward weights. Leveraging the noise intrinsically available to neurons, we use a simple anti-Hebbian learning rule for the feedback weights to drive them towards satisfying Conditions
1
and
2
.

Noisy neural dynamics.

Our feedback weight plasticity dynamics make use of noise injected in the neural dynamics, which we model as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{v}\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)+W_{i}\phi\big(\mathbf{v}_{i-1}(t)\big)+Q_{i}\mathbf{u}(t)+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(t),

(8)

where the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
represents exponentially filtered white noise with time constant
\tau_{\epsilon}
, i.e., an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, and
\sigma
the standard deviation of the noise. Note that such Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes can, for example, be obtained in the limit of many independent Poisson inputs with infinitesimal synaptic weights
(
Gerstner et al. 2014
)
.

An anti-Hebbian learning rule for
Q
.

The noise fluctuations that propagate through the network carry information about the network Jacobian
J
and we can extract this information by correlating the noise fluctuations at the output with the noise fluctuations at each layer. Inspired by the feedback weight learning of standard DFC
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
, we instantiate this correlation technique with the following simple anti-Hebbian plasticity rule:

\displaystyle\tau_{Q}\dot{Q}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{L-i}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}(t)\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}(t)^{T}-\beta Q_{i}.

(9)

The feedback compartment
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}
contains a part of the injected noise,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}=Q_{i}\mathbf{u}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\mathrm{fb}}
, and the pre-synaptic plasticity signal
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}
is a high-pass filtered version of the incoming control signal
\mathbf{u}
, which extracts the output noise fluctuations.

We high-pass filter
\mathbf{u}(t)
by subtracting the exponentially averaged signal from it:
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}(t)\triangleq\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{lp}}(t)
, with
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{lp}}\triangleq\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(t-t^{\prime})\big)\mathbf{u}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime}
and
\tau_{f}
the filtering time constant.

Our feedback weight plasticity dynamics (
9
) introduce two important novelties with respect to DFC. (i) The high-pass filtering of
\mathbf{u}
makes it possible to learn the feedback weights using the noise fluctuations of
\mathbf{u}
, while simultaneously letting the controller push the network towards the output target for learning the forward weights. (ii) The scaling factor
(1+\tau_{v}/\tau_{\epsilon})
enables the network to learn an optimal feedback connectivity pattern, while having a finite neural time constant
\tau_{v}
, which introduces delays in the network. As the correlation signal decreases for increasing time delays between the pre- and postsynaptic noise fluctuations, this needs to be compensated by an appropriate scaling factor.

Debiasing the forward weight updates.

While we need noise correlations for training the feedback weights, these can bias the forward weight updates (see App.
C.4
). We solve this challenge by low-pass filtering the pre-synaptic plasticity signal for the forward weight updates, such that the pre-synaptic noise fluctuations are removed:

\displaystyle\tau_{W}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}W_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\Big(\phi\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)\big)-\phi\big(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t)\big)\Big)\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lp},i-1}^{T},

with

an exponential moving average of
\mathbf{r}_{i}
with filtering time constant
\tau_{f}
.

5
Experiments

Here, we validate our theoretical findings with empirical results. By studying in detail a synthetic toy example and modest computer-vision benchmarks, we show that the minimizing control framework succeeds in minimizing the training loss
\mathcal{L}
, and that Strong-DFC equipped with single-phase learning succeeds in approximating the gradient of
\mathcal{H}
, thereby validating itself as a principled CA method. Finally, we show that Strong-DFC is significantly more noise-robust compared to standard DFC, due to its strong feedback influences on neural activity.

Strong-DFC performs CA by minimizing feedback control.

We start with validating our main theoretical claim that minimizing control is a suitable new theoretical framework for learning. To this end, we first focus on a low-dimensional teacher-student nonlinear function approximation problem, which can be efficiently simulated.

In this problem, the goal of learning is to reduce the squared error
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}[\|f_{\text{trgt}}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}_{L,\mathrm{ss}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}]
between the target
f_{\text{trgt}}(\mathbf{x})
provided by the teacher network we wish to approximate and the steady-state last-layer activity of our student network, which is a function of the input
\mathbf{x}
.
The student network is trained with Strong-DFC, and its input layer activity
\mathbf{r}_{0}
is clamped to the same random pattern
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
used to probe the teacher function. To confirm that Theorem
1
and Proposition
2
hold in practice, we study an idealized setting. We disable neural noise (
\sigma=0
), simulate the neural dynamics (
8
) to equilibrium, and then apply a single weight change to
W
according to the steady-state Strong-DFC update (
7
). Moreover, to ensure that Condition
1
is satisfied in this idealized setting at all points during training, we manually set the feedback weights equal to the transposed network Jacobian:
Q=\left.J^{T}\right|_{\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}(t)}
(see Condition
1
). We use Euler’s forward method to simulate the dynamics and we initialize the network state to the feedforward prediction for each datapoint.

Our experiments confirm that the surrogate loss
\mathcal{H}
is reduced as training progresses, eventually reaching a low value (of the order of
10^{-7}
), in accordance with Theorem
1
(see Fig.
2
A). Crucially, we also observe that as
\mathcal{H}
decreases, so does the actual loss of interest
\mathcal{L}
. At the end of training,
\mathcal{L}

6
Discussion

Modern deep neural networks are invariably learned by backpropagation-of-error. While backpropagation has proven to be a highly effective gradient-based credit assignment method for training deep artificial neural networks, it is unsatisfactory as a model of learning in cortical networks in at least three important aspects. First, it needs precisely-symmetric forward and feedback connections. Second, backpropagated errors do not influence neural activity. Third, it requires two precisely-clocked phases. Instead of attempting to remedy these issues one by one, we propose a fundamentally new approach for deep learning, that overcomes all of these by design, and provide first results indicating that it is an effective method for credit assignment.

Our study shows that these three critical issues can be overcome at once within the framework of gradient-based optimization, by casting learning as a control minimization problem. For this, we first augment standard deep neural networks with a feedback controller, which dynamically changes the hidden neural activity to reach the desired output. Then, we derive a synaptic plasticity rule with the objective of minimizing the influence of the feedback controller on the neural activity. This principled derivation led us to Strong-DFC, a variation of the recently proposed DFC method which inherits all of its appealing properties as a biologically plausible deep learning framework. Like DFC, our weight update rule is local in space and time for a broad range of (not necessarily symmetric) feedback connectivity patterns and neural feedback controller architectures, and it can be interpreted as a biologically inspired dendritic voltage-dependent synaptic plasticity rule
(
Urbanczik & Senn 2014
)
. Importantly, however, top-down feedback signals are no longer required to be vanishingly small for Strong-DFC.

This strong feedback influence sets our minimizing control framework apart from previous theories of how the brain might estimate objective function gradients by measuring small changes in neural activity, generated by ‘nudging’ the network towards a slightly better state
(
Hinton & McClelland 1988
;
O’Reilly 1996
;
Xie & Seung 2003
;
Scellier & Bengio 2017
;
Sacramento et al. 2018
;
Lillicrap et al. 2020
;
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
. In biology, such theories might face a fundamental obstacle during learning, since cortical activity is notoriously noisy
(
Rusakov et al. 2020
)
, making it hard to estimate small activity changes as plasticity signals. Moreover, they are at odds with a growing number of experimental neuroscience observations reporting large neural activity changes in the presence of unexpected or novel events, which are thought to drive synaptic plasticity
(
Keller & Mrsic-Flogel 2018
)
, and the existence of top-down feedback signals that are sufficient to drive secondary receptive field responses in pyramidal neurons
(
Keller et al. 2020
)
.
Our minimizing control framework shows how to perform principled mathematical optimization of an objective function, while being consistent with these findings.

A long-standing question in neuroscience is whether noise plays a computational role in the brain – whether it is ‘a bug or a feature’ of biological neural circuits
(
Rusakov et al. 2020
)
. Our study suggests that noise could play a key role in ensuring that gradient-based credit assignment information is transmitted backwards through the network. Building on the noise-tolerance of Strong-DFC, we leverage noise to concurrently and seamlessly learn both feedforward and feedback weights so that Condition
1
is constantly fulfilled. In contrast to standard two-phase learning of forward and backward connections
(
Akrout et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2015
;
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
, our plasticity model does not require any pauses, distinct phases, plasticity switches, or alternations between noise- and input-driven modes. Our always-on plasticity rules are arguably more biologically plausible than alternative two-phase learning algorithms, and simpler to implement in neuromorphic hardware.

In our pyramidal cell model, top-down apical synapses transmit feedback information which instructs the plasticity of bottom-up basal synapses, an idea that can be traced back to the seminal work of
Körding & König 2001
. Interpreted as such, our results suggest that the plasticity rules for top-down and bottom-up connections might display major differences, which is experimentally testable. Both rules include a voltage-dependent postsynaptic factor, in agreement with phenomenological plasticity models
(
Clopath & Gerstner 2010
)
. However, the presynaptic components of our apical and basal plasticity rules are high- and low-pass filtered, respectively. Such presynaptic activity filters are a recurring element in reward-driven synaptic plasticity rules
(
Seung 2003
)
. Moreover, our apical plasticity rule is anti-Hebbian and its postsynaptic term depends only on the local dendritic voltage, not on the somatic spiking activity. This may be consistent with the fact that, for pyramidal cells, backpropagating action potentials often fail to reach the apical dendrite, questioning their role as the main drivers of apical synaptic plasticity
(
Spruston 2008
;
Gambino et al. 2014
)
.

We highlight that, despite the close methodological connections between DFC and Strong-DFC, our minimizing control framework is fundamentally different from the underlying theory of DFC and many other recent biologically plausible learning methods that link their weight updates to a new variant of Gauss-Newton optimization with a minibatch size of one
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
;
Meulemans et al. 2020
;
Podlaski & Machens 2020
;
Bengio 2020
)
. While the specific optimization characteristics of these methods are not yet fully understood, our minimizing control framework can be cast as gradient descent on a surrogate loss function. Hence, the rich field studying the application of stochastic gradient descent to neural networks
(
Bottou et al. 2018
)
can be applied on our new loss to uncover the optimization characteristics of Strong-DFC. Furthermore, our minimizing control approach draws from control theory, but it is distinct from standard optimal control. In particular, it is well known that the adjoint state method from optimal control is equivalent to backpropagation on the supervised loss function, when applied to deep feedforward neural networks
(
LeCun et al. 1988
)
, and hence different from our minimizing control framework that performs gradient descent on a surrogate loss function.

In practice, the Strong-DFC updates do not exactly follow the negative gradient of
\mathcal{H}
, due to (i) the limited number of iterations for training the feedback weights, (ii) the limited capacity of the linear pathway
Q
to satisfy Condition
1
for every input sample, and (iii) the difference between the steady-state weight update (
7
) and the Strong-DFC weight update (
4
). Despite these challenges, Fig
3
shows that Strong-DFC approximates the theory well. Future work can further improve Strong-DFC by investigating alternative feedback mappings and network architectures. As Strong-DFC uses continuous dynamics, it is costly to simulate on conventional deep learning hardware, preventing it from being tested on large-scale machine learning benchmarks. A promising solution is to implement Strong-DFC on analog and neuromorphic hardware, where the dynamics can coincide with the physical dynamics of the analog components. Consequently, this can make Strong-DFC an attractive principled approach for CA on analog deep learning implementations, commonly used in Edge-AI and other low-energy applications of deep learning
(
Xiao et al. 2020
;
Misra & Saha 2010
)
.

By providing a novel optimization approach for deep neural networks that differs from the usual direct minimization of the output loss, our minimizing control framework allowed us to derive a novel gradient-based credit assignment method that relies on strong feedback influence. Instantiating our framework with Strong-DFC naturally leads to biologically desired characteristics, such as single-phase learning, flexible feedback connectivity requirements, and local learning rules, further underlining its promise for understanding credit assignment in the brain.

A.2

Here, we investigate in more depth why Condition

\|Q\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}\|_{2}^{2}
is its steepest ascent direction, thus all the components of this gradient should influence the network output and consequently, the amount of needed control feedback, because otherwise there would exist a ’steeper’ direction. Therefore, if the weight update given by Eq. (
7

Now, let us specify the

of the network. As the dimension of the concatenated forward weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is higher than the dimension of the network output, there exist certain weight updates
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
that do not result in any change to the output
\mathbf{r}_{L}
of the network, as they lie inside the nullspace of the network. For small weight updates, the nullspace of the network at steady state is connected to the network Jacobian, as we have that
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{L}\approx J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
, with
R_{\mathrm{ss}}
as defined in Lemma
S2
. From this formulation, one can see that the nullspace of the network is equal to the nullspace of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
.

When vectorizing the steady-state weight updates (Eq.
7
), we see that they lie inside the rowspace of
Q^{T}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
. If we have that the column space of
Q
(hence, the rowspace of
Q^{T}
) is equal to the row space of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
, the steady-state weight updates will always lie inside the rowspace of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
, and hence, can never lie inside the nullspace of the network.

The negative gradient of
\|Q\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}\|_{2}^{2}
is its steepest descent direction, thus all the components of the gradient should influence the network output and consequently the amount of needed control input. Therefore, if the weight update Eq. (
7
) contains nullspace components, it cannot be fully aligned with the gradient. Consequently, Condition
1
is a necessary condition for the steady-state update (Eq.
7
) to align with the negative gradient. The sufficiency of Condition
1
follows from the proof of Theorem
1
.

To illustrate the above arguments further, let us investigate in more detail what happens when we update the forward network parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Assuming a small weight update
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we can do a first-order Taylor expansion of the steady-state network output after the weight update:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{L,\mathrm{ss}}^{(m+1)}\approx\mathbf{r}_{L,\mathrm{ss}}^{(m)}+J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}+J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},

(37)

with
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}=J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
and
m
the training iteration. The weight update changes the amount of control needed to reach the output target, which we capture by introducing
\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}
. As the feedback controller drives the network to the supervised label during each training iteration (assuming
\alpha\rightarrow 0
), we have that
\mathbf{r}_{L,\mathrm{ss}}^{(m+1)}=\mathbf{r}_{L,\mathrm{ss}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true}}
and hence

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\approx-J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(38)

The above equation relates the update
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
to the change in steady-state control input
Q\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}
. In order to maximally reduce
\mathcal{H}
and hence
\lVert Q\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}\rVert^{2}_{2}
, the magnitude of the LHS should be as big as possible. As a result, we want
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
to lie fully inside the rowspace of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
, because otherwise,
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
will partially be lost in its nullspace, reducing the magnitude of the LHS. As
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}=R_{\mathrm{ss}}Q\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}
, we have that the columnspace of
R_{\mathrm{ss}}Q
needs to lie inside the rowspace of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}R_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
. This condition is equivalent to the columnspace of
Q
lying inside the rowspace of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
. Note that the above arguments should be interpreted on an intuitive level, a detailed mathematical consideration of the above arguments is more nuanced, but leads to the same high-level intuition.

A.3
Effect of the nonlinearity
\phi
on the weight updates

The main effect of including the nonlinearity
\phi
into the Strong-DFC weight updates (Eq.
4
), in contrast to the steady-state updates (Eq.
7
) of Theorem
1
, is ensuring that saturated neurons refrain from updating their synaptic weights for the current data sample, as
\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i})-\phi(\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i})\approx 0
in this case.

Meulemans et al. 2021
showed that including
\phi
in the learning rule is a useful heuristic for improving the alignment of the DFC weight updates with the theoretically ideal update (which in our case would be the negative gradient of
\mathcal{H}
), when the column space condition on
Q
is not perfectly satisfied. Consequently, including
\phi
also improves the performance in this case. When the column space condition on
Q
is perfectly satisfied, including
\phi
in the learning rule makes no difference.

A.4
Steady-state weight updates vs. continuous weight updates

Our theoretical results assume that the weight update is performed at steady state, giving rise to the update defined in Eq. (
7
). However, in practice, plasticity is always on, giving rise to the continuous plasticity dynamics defined in Eq. (
4
). Following
Meulemans et al. 2021
, we show that when the steady state is quickly reached, the steady-state update (
7
) approximates the resulting continuous update (
4
) well.

Assuming a separation of time scales between the network dynamics and the synaptic plasticity (
\tau_{v},\tau_{u}\ll\tau_{W}
), the accumulated weight update resulting from the plasticity dynamics (
4
) is given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\text{d}W_{i}=\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{ss}}}\text{d}W_{i}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{\mathrm{ss}}}{\tau_{W}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i,\mathrm{ss}})-\phi(\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i,\mathrm{ss}})\right)\mathbf{r}_{i-1,\mathrm{ss}}^{T}.

(39)

Now, if the dynamics quickly settle towards their steady state, relative to the time interval the input is presented, i.e.,
t_{\mathrm{ss}}\ll t_{1}
, the weight update is dominated by the second term in the RHS of the above equation, which corresponds to the steady-state weight update with
\phi
included. Furthermore, the controller quickly pushes the activity close to its steady state, with possibly some oscillations around it, so the first term
\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{ss}}}\text{d}W_{i}
will approximate the steady state well.

In conclusion, the Strong-DFC weight update resulting from the plasticity dynamics described in Eq. (
4
) will approximate the steady-state weight update
\left(\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i,\mathrm{ss}})-\phi(\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i,\mathrm{ss}})\right)\mathbf{r}_{i-1,\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
. Combined with the arguments of Appendix
A.3
, we see that the Strong-DFC weight update approximates the steady-state update of Theorem
1
well.

A.5
The minimizing control framework is compatible with various controller types

In Section
3
, we introduced our theory on the minimizing control framework tailored towards a proportional intergral (PI) controller used by DFC. However, our theory can easily be extended to other controller types that reach the correct steady state.

In the proof of Theorem
1
, the only assumption on the controller is its steady state equation (
21
), given by Eq. (
21
):

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{\theta})\triangleq\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\tilde{\alpha}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}=0.

Therefore, Theorem
1
applies to all stable controllers that give rise to this same steady-state equation,
F(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

For example, pure proportional control,
\mathbf{u}(t)=k\mathbf{e}(t)
gives rise to the same steady-state equation
F(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{\theta})
, with
\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{1}{k}
. For pure integral control, we have the same steady-state equation
F(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{\theta})
but with
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha
. If we add derivative control to any of the above controller types, we reach the same steady-state equation as the derivative of the error vanishes at steady state.

In conclusion, Theorem
1
is compatible with all possible combinations of the proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller framework, except for the pure derivative control. Furthermore, all other controller types that satisfy the steady-state equation
F(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{\theta})
are also compatible with Theorem
1
.

A.6
Adapting the minimizing control framework for classification with softmax and cross-entropy loss

For classification with neural networks, one uses conventionally a one-hot label
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true}}
in combination with a cross-entropy loss and a softmax layer as output of the network. In our minimizing control framework, the controller drives the network to perfectly match the output target
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true}}
. However, a softmax layer only results in a one-hot vector if it has an infinite input. To overcome this challenge, we use soft targets which have
a
on its label entry and
\frac{1-a}{n_{L}-1}
on the other entries, with
a
close to 1 (e.g., 0.99).

A nice property of the combination of the cross-entropy loss with the softmax output is that the curvature of the exponentials in the softmax cancel out the curvature of the logarithms in the cross-entropy loss, leading to clean gradients that do not saturate easily. However, if our controller uses as control error
\mathbf{e}(t)=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}-\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)
, with
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}
the soft target and
\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)
the softmax output, it cannot make use of the cross-entropy loss to provide clean gradients. To overcome this challenge, we use a linear output layer,
\mathbf{r}_{L}
, and absorb the softmax into the cross-entropy loss, leading to the combined loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{combined}}=-\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{r}_{L}^{\mathrm{true},(b)T}\log\big(\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{r}_{L}^{(b)})\big).

(40)

Next, we slightly adapt the minimizing control framework to include this combined loss in a principled way. Currently, the controller dynamics is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t)

\displaystyle=k\mathbf{e}(t)+\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}(t),\quad\tau_{u}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\mathbf{e}(t)-\alpha\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}(t)

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}-\mathbf{r}_{L}(t).

(42)

Note that
\mathbf{e}(t)
can be interpreted as
-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}\rvert_{\mathbf{r}_{L}=\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)}
, with
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}-\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Now, if we use another loss
\mathcal{L}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{combined}}
), we can generalize the control error
\mathbf{e}(t)
to

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}(t)=-\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}\right\rvert_{\mathbf{r}_{L}=\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)}^{T}.

(43)

For the cross entropy loss with the softmax, this boils down to

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}(t)=-\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}\right\rvert_{\mathbf{r}_{L}=\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)}^{T}=\mathbf{p}^{*}-\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)),

(44)

with
\mathbf{p}^{*}
the soft target.

Appendix B
Stability analysis

In this section, we build further upon the stability analysis results of
Meulemans et al. 2021
, which are applicable to Strong-DFC with minimal adaptations.

As a first step, we investigate a pure integral controller (
k=0
) and assume a separation of timescales
\tau_{v}\ll\tau_{u}
, i.e., we can replace the network dynamics by a deterministic function and only retain the controller dynamics. This provides us with the following controller dynamics:

\displaystyle\tau_{u}\dot{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\mathbf{e}\left(\mathbf{u}(t)\right)-\alpha\mathbf{u}(t),

(45)

where the error,
\mathbf{e}
, can be seen as a deterministic function of the control input,
\mathbf{u}(t)
(for simplicity, we omit the dependence on the network parameters and input, as they are considered fixed during the controller dynamics). To investigate the local stability at steady state, we consider the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix of these dynamics evaluated at steady state. Using Eq. (
23
), we have that:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\left.\left(\mathbf{e}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)-\alpha\mathbf{u}\right)\right|_{\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}}=-\left(J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q+\alpha I\right).

(46)

Hence, the condition for local stability around the steady state is that all eigenvalues of
-\left(J_{\mathrm{ss}}Q+\alpha I\right)
should be negative, resulting in Condition
2
.

When considering the proportional integral controller without a separation of timescales, the local stability condition becomes harder to interpret. Following a similar derivation as proposed by
Meulemans et al. 2021
, we reach the following Jacobian of the network-controller dynamics:

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{PI}}=-\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\tau_{v}}(I-W\phi^{\prime})&-\frac{1}{\tau_{v}}Q\\
\frac{1}{\tilde{\tau}_{u}}S\phi^{\prime}-\frac{k}{\tau_{v}}S\phi^{\prime}(I-W\phi^{\prime})&\frac{k}{\tau_{v}}S\phi^{\prime}Q\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{\tilde{\tau}_{u}}I,\end{bmatrix}

(47)

with
\tilde{\tau}_{u}\triangleq\frac{\tau_{u}}{1+\alpha k}
,
\tilde{\alpha}\triangleq\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha k}
,
\phi^{\prime}\triangleq\left.\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{v})}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\right|_{\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{ss}}}
, and

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&\ldots&0\\
W_{2}&0&0&\ldots&0\\
0&W_{3}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&\ddots&\ldots&0\\
0&0&\ldots&W_{L}&0\end{bmatrix}

(48)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}0&\ldots&0&I\end{bmatrix}

(49)

The condition for local stability is now that all eigenvalues of
A_{\mathrm{PI}}
are negative. However, this condition provides no straightforward interpretations for defining conditions on the feedback weights,
Q
, such that local stability is achieved. One can apply Gershgoring’s circle theorem for finding sufficient conditions on
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
and Q to ensure local stability, however, the resulting conditions are too conservative and hence not usable for designing feedback weight learning rules.

C.1
Stochastic dynamics for the single-phase learning setting

In this section, we provide a detailed description of the stochastic dynamics used for training the forward and feedback weights in a single phase. The network and controller obey the following dynamics:

\displaystyle\tau_{v}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)+W_{i}\phi\big(\mathbf{v}_{i-1}(t)\big)+Q_{i}\mathbf{u}(t)+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(t)

(50)

\displaystyle\tau_{u}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}-\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)-\alpha\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}(t)

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t)

\displaystyle=k\big(\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}-\mathbf{r}_{L}(t)\big)+\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}(t)

(52)

with
\sigma^{2}
the noise variance and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)
exponentially filtered white noise, with time constant
\tau_{\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}(t-t^{\prime})\big)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime},

(54)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is white noise. The time constant
\tau_{\epsilon}
determines the auto-correlation of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. For simplicity, we take that the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
enters solely in the feedback compartment:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}(t)=Q_{i}\mathbf{u}(t)+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(t).

(55)

However, all the arguments and theorems of the following sections also apply for noise entering all compartments.

Now, we define the following error-based learning rule for the forward weights and anti-Hebbian learning rule for the feedback weights:

\displaystyle\tau_{W}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}W_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\Big(\phi\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)\big)-\phi\big(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t)\big)\Big)\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i-1}^{T}

(56)

\displaystyle\tau_{Q}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}Q_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{L-i}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}-\beta Q_{i}

(57)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}(t)-\bar{\mathbf{u}}(t)

(58)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{u}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\big(\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(t-t^{\prime})\big)\mathbf{u}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime}

(59)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\big(\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(t-t^{\prime})\big)\mathbf{r}_{i}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime}

(60)

with
\tau_{f}
the filtering time constant used to high-pass filter
\mathbf{u}
and low-pass filter
\mathbf{r}_{i}
.

C.2
Intuition behind the feedback weight learning rule

Here we expand on the intuition behind our feedback weight learning rule. The central technique, inspired by the weight mirroring method
(
Akrout et al. 2019
)
and DFC
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
, is to inject white noise in the network that carries information about the network Jacobian,
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
, towards the output. If we now measure the correlation of the output noise w.r.t. the injected noise in the layers, we can recover the information on
J_{\mathrm{ss}}
. Below, we summarize the intuitive explanation on a normal feedforward neural network, provided by
Meulemans et al. 2021
. We end with explaining how to go from this intuitive explanation to our single-phase feedback weight plasticity, and how it differs from the original feedback weight plasticity of DFC.

Now, let us consider a normal feedforward neural network
\mathbf{r}_{i}=\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i})=\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1})
. We perturb each layer with white noise
\sigma\boldsymbol{\xi}
, which is propagated forward through the network:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}

\displaystyle=W_{i}\phi(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i-1})+\sigma\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}

(61)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{0}

(62)

Assuming
\sigma
is small, we can approximate the network output as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{L}\approx\mathbf{r}_{L}+\sigma J\boldsymbol{\xi},

(63)

with
J=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}},&\ldots&,\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}}\end{bmatrix}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
the concatenation of all
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
. Now, we can use an output error
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{r}_{L}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{L}
to extract the noise, and use this in a feedback learning rule
\Delta Q=-\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{e}^{T}-\beta Q
. In expectation, this update rule results in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta Q]=\sigma^{2}J^{T}-\beta Q.

(64)

Hence, we see that this simple update rule drives the feedback weights to align with
J^{T}
.

Although the above explanation grasps the intuition behind the feedback learning rule (
9
), there are some important differences when we consider the continuous single-phase plasticity dynamics.

1.

The pre-synaptic plasticity signal of the feedback weight update (
9
) is the high-pass filtered control signal
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}
instead of the output noise we used before. When training the feedback and forward weights simultaneously,
\mathbf{u}
consists of two parts: (i) the control signal used to drive the network in expectation towards the output target
\mathbf{r}_{L}^{*}
, and (ii), the output noise fluctuations integrated over time by the controller. As we are interested in the noise fluctuations, we need to extract them, which we do by using the high-pass filtered version of
\mathbf{u}
.

2.

The postsynaptic plasticity signal is the activity in the feedback compartment
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}=Q_{i}\mathbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
, instead of purely the noise. As
Q_{i}\mathbf{u}
will correlate with
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}
, and
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}
contains the integrated noise fluctuations over time, instead of the direct noise fluctuations, the feedback weights
Q
will not align exactly with
J^{T}
, but with
J^{T}M
instead, where
M
is a positive definite matrix.

3.

To incorporate noise in the continuous dynamics of Strong-DFC, we need to use stochastic differential equations (SDEs), instead of discrete noise perturbations. Unlike discrete feedforward neural networks, the network dynamics do not propagate the layer activity instantaneously towards the output, but have a delay proportional to
\tau_{v}
. As white noise has no correlation in time, naively correlating the output noise fluctuations with the current layer noise would result in a signal of zero due to the time delays. In order to have correlations over time, we use exponentially filtered white noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
with time constant
\tau_{\epsilon}
, instead of pure white noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. As the time delay between the output noise fluctuations and the layer noise increases for upstream layers, we need to compensate for the decreasing correlation signal by scaling the plasticity updates with
\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{L-i}
, as is done in Eq. (
9
).

Although our single-phase plasticity dynamics (
9
) are closely related to the two-phase plasticity dynamics of DFC introduced by
Meulemans et al. 2021
, and these authors discussed briefly the possibility of having single-phase plasticity dynamics, our plasticity dynamics introduce some important advances, both in theory and practice.

1.

Our minimizing control framework explicitly motivates learning the forward and feedback weights simultaneously, as the feedback weights are trained to satisfy Condition
1
, which is evaluated at the steady state of the network for training the forward weights. By contrast, the theory for DFC in the weak-nudging regime requires the feedback weights to be trained at the network steady state without influence from the controller, and therefore different from the network steady state used for training the forward weights
(
Meulemans et al. 2021
)
.

2.

In their theoretical results on the feedback learning rule,
Meulemans et al. 2021
require that the controller leakage
\alpha
is big. However, this cannot be combined with learning the feedback and feedforward weights simultaneously, as for training the feedforward weights, we need
\alpha\rightarrow 0
. In our new theoretical results, we overcome this important challenge.

3.

In their theoretical results on the feedback learning rule,
Meulemans et al. 2021
assume instant network dynamics (
\tau_{v}\rightarrow 0
). However, in practice, a finite network time constant is used, giving rise to delays in the propagation of noise fluctuations to the output, leading to weaker correlation signals for upstream layers. Our new theory embraces finite time constants and investigates in detail the required relation between the various time constants
\tau_{v}
,
\tau_{\epsilon}
,
\tau_{u}
, and
\tau_{f}
. As a result, we introduce the scaling term
\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{L-i}
which improves the feedback learning significantly.

\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{ss}}
. Using
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=(I-W\phi^{\prime})\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)\triangleq[\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(t)^{T},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]^{T}
to simplify notation we get

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=-\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\tau_{v}}(I-W\phi^{\prime})&-\frac{1}{\tau_{v}}(I-W\phi^{\prime})Q\\
\frac{1}{\tau_{u}}J_{\mathrm{ss}}&\frac{\alpha}{\tau_{u}}I\end{bmatrix}}_{\triangleq A}\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sigma}{\tau_{v}}(I-W\phi^{\prime})\\
0\end{bmatrix}}_{\triangleq B}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)

(74)

We solve this set of linear time-invariant differential equations using the variation of constants method
(
Särkkä & Solin 2019
)
, taking
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(t_{0})=0
and
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t_{0})=0
(i.e., we consider that the network and controller dynamics are already converged at
t_{0}
).

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\exp\big(-A(t-t^{\prime})\big)B\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime}.

(75)

Now that we solved the differential equations for
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
, we turn our attention to the feedback weight plasticity rule (
9
). We start by investigating
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]
. As the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
enters through the apical compartment, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}(t)

\displaystyle=Q\mathbf{u}(t)+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)=Q\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ss}}+Q\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)

(76)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]

\displaystyle=Q\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ss}}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]+Q\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]+\sigma\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]

(77)

The first term of Eq. (
77
) is equal to zero, as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and consequently
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
are zero-mean. We now turn our attention to the third term. Using
S_{u}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}0&I\end{bmatrix}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=S_{u}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]

\displaystyle=S_{u}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]=S_{u}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\exp\big({-}A(t-t^{\prime})\big)B\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]\text{d}t^{\prime}

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau_{\epsilon}}S_{u}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\exp\big({-}(A+\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}I)(t-t^{\prime})\big)B\text{d}t^{\prime},

(79)

for which we used
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]=\frac{1}{2\tau_{\epsilon}}\exp\big(\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}|t-t^{\prime}|\big)
. Using
\int_{0}^{T}\exp(At)\text{d}t=\big(\exp(AT)-I\big)A^{-1}
and assuming stability and
t-t_{0}\gg\tau_{u}
, which is justified by the condition
\tau_{Q}\gg\tau_{u}
, we can solve the previous integral:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]=\frac{1}{2\tau_{\epsilon}}S_{u}\big(A+\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}I\big)^{-1}B.

(80)

Using the solution for the inverse of
2\times 2
block matrices of
Lu & Shiou 2002
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]=

(81)

\displaystyle{-}\frac{1}{2\tau_{\epsilon}}\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}+\frac{\alpha}{\tau_{u}}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{\tau_{u}}J_{\mathrm{ss}}\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}I+\frac{1}{\tau_{v}}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}+\frac{\alpha}{\tau_{u}}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{\tau_{v}}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)Q\frac{1}{\tau_{u}}J_{\mathrm{ss}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\sigma}{\tau_{v}}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)

(82)

Now, using the condition
\tau_{v},\tau_{\epsilon}\ll\tau_{u}
, we can approximate the above with:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]

\displaystyle\approx{-}\frac{\sigma}{2\tau_{u}}J_{\mathrm{ss}}\left(\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)

(83)

Using Lemma
S5
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(t)^{T}]

\displaystyle\approx{-}\frac{\sigma}{2\tau_{u}}\left.\begin{bmatrix}\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{-L}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}}&\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{-(L-1)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{2}}&\ldots&\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}}\end{bmatrix}\right|_{\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{ss}}}

(84)

Now we turn our attention towards the second term of Eq. (
77
). As
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)^{T}]
is a covariance matrix, it is positive semi-definite.

Combining Eq. (
9
) with Eq. (
81
) and the above insights, we get

\displaystyle\tau_{Q}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{E}[Q(t)]\triangleq\tau_{Q}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bar{Q}(t)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\tau_{u}}J_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}-\bar{Q}(\tilde{M}+\beta I),

(85)

with
\tilde{M}
a positive semi-definite matrix. We used the fact that the layer-wise scaling with
\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{L-i}
in Eq. (
9
) cancels out the scaling factors
\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{-(L-i)}
in Eq. (
81
). Using
M\triangleq\tilde{M}+\beta I
, the stable point of the above dynamics is

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau_{u}}J_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}M^{-1}.

(86)

As
M
is positive definite, the dynamics are stable and the above steady state is reached. Furthermore, as
J_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}M^{-}1
is a permutation of the columns of
J_{\mathrm{ss}}^{T}
, it satisfies Condition
1
; and as
M^{-1}
is positive definite, it satisfies Condition
2
.

Now the only thing left to show is that
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}\triangleq\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{lp}}\overset{?}{=}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
, with
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{lp}}
the low-pass filtered version of
\mathbf{u}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{lp}}(t)=\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\exp\left({-}\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(t-t^{\prime})\right)\mathbf{u}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime}.

(87)

As we have that
\tau_{f}\gg\tau_{u},\tau_{\epsilon},\tau_{v}
, the low-pass filtering will remove all fluctuations and we have that
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{lp}}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ss}}
. Consequently, we have that
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{hp}}=\mathbf{u}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ss}}=\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
, thereby concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma S5
.

For some scalar
a\neq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle J\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)=\left.\begin{bmatrix}a^{-L}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}}&a^{-(L-1)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{2}}&\ldots&a^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}}\end{bmatrix}\right|_{\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}^{\prime}}

(88)

with
J
and
\phi^{\prime}
evaluated at some activity pattern
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First, we note that
\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)
is of full rank for
a\neq 0
, as it is a triangular matrix with
a
on its diagonal. The inverse of
\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)
is given by:

\displaystyle\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{-1}I&0&0&\ldots&0\\
a^{-2}J_{2,1}&a^{-1}I&0&\ldots&0\\
a^{-3}J_{2,1}&a^{-2}J_{3,2}&a^{-1}I&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a^{-L}J_{L,1}&\ldots&\ldots&a^{-2}J_{L,L-1}&a^{-1}I\end{bmatrix}

(89)

with
J_{i,j}\triangleq\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{j}}
. One can easily verify this by computing
\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)^{-1}=I
upon noting that
J_{i,i-1}=W_{i}\phi^{\prime}_{i-1}
. Next, we have that:

\displaystyle\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}a^{-1}I&0&0&\ldots&0\\
(a^{-2}-a^{-1})J_{2,1}&a^{-1}I&0&\ldots&0\\
(a^{-3}-a^{-2})J_{2,1}&(a^{-2}-a^{-1})J_{3,2}&a^{-1}I&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
(a^{-L}-a^{-(L-1)})J_{L,1}&\ldots&\ldots&(a^{-2}-a^{-1})J_{L,L-1}&a^{-1}I\end{bmatrix}

(90)

Using
J=[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}},\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{2}},\ldots,\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}}]
, we have

\displaystyle J\left(aI-W\phi^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(I-W\phi^{\prime}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}a^{-L}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{1}}&a^{-(L-1)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{2}}&\ldots&a^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{L}},\end{bmatrix}

(91)

thereby concluding the proof.
∎

C.4
Noise can bias the feedforward weight updates

As observed by
Meulemans et al. 2021
, when we inject noise into the dynamics while training the forward weights, the resulting noise correlations can bias the forward weight updates. This is a general issue for methods using error feedback for learning while having realistic noise dynamics, as discussed in more detail in App. C.4 in
Meulemans et al. 2021
. Here, we investigate how these issues manifest themselves in Strong-DFC and how we can solve them.

The forward weight update is given by Eq. (
4
). If the noise fluctuations in the postsynaptic term
\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i}(t))-\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t))
are correlated to the noise fluctuations in the presynaptic term
\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t)
, the expected forward weight update contains this extra correlation term and is hence biased.

The difference
\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i}(t))-\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t))
is mainly determined by the feedback input
Q\mathbf{u}(t)
. Using the notation of Section
C.3
, we divide the control signal of the converged dynamics in two parts:
\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}+\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)
, with
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ss}}
the part of the control signal useful for learning the forward weights, and
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
the noise fluctuations that are used for learning the feedback weights. These noise fluctuations are a function of the noise fluctuations of all network layers (e.g., see Eq.
75
). Hence, the noise fluctuations of
\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i}(t))-\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t))
will contain a part of the noise fluctuations of
\mathbf{r}_{i-1}(t)
, therefore causing the pre- and post-synaptic term in the plasticity rule to be correlated.

A simple approach to remove the noise correlation in the plasticity rule for the forward weights, is either to remove the noise fluctuations in the pre- or post-synaptic term, or both. We choose to remove the noise fluctuations in the pre-synaptic term, by using a low-pass filtered version of
\mathbf{r}_{i-1}
:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\big(\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(t-t^{\prime})\big)\mathbf{r}_{i}(t^{\prime})\text{d}t^{\prime},

(92)

with
\tau_{f}
the filtering time constant. This low-pass filtering can be interpreted as a leaky biophysical mechanism that accumulates the pre-synaptic input used for the plasticity of the synapse. As the pre-synaptic term of the forward plasticity dynamics no longer contains the noise fluctuations, the updates will no longer be biased.

Simulation and algorithms

D.1
Simulating the dynamics

We use the Euler-Maruyama method to simulate the stochastic differential equations (SDE), which simulates a (non)linear SDE

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)

(93)

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}[m+1]=\mathbf{x}[m]+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}[m],m\Delta t)\Delta t+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}[m],m\Delta t)\sqrt{\Delta t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m},

(94)

with
\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}
a Gaussian random variable with distribution
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},I)
. Note that the noise term has stepsize
\sqrt{\Delta t}
instead of
\Delta t
, as Brownian motion has a variance of
t
and not
t^{2}
.

Applying the Euler-Maruyama method to the SDEs of Section
C.1
defining the neural and controller dynamics gives us the following discrete update equations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}[m+1]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}[m]+\frac{1}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\big(-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}[m]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,m}\big)

(95)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}[m]

\displaystyle=Q_{i}\mathbf{u}[m]+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}[m]

(96)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}[m+1]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}[m]+(\mathbf{e}[m]-\alpha\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}[m])\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{u}}

(97)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}[m]

\displaystyle=k\mathbf{e}[m]+\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{int}}[m]

(98)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i}[m+1]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{i}[m]+(-\mathbf{v}_{i}[m]+W_{i}\phi(\mathbf{v}_{i-1}[m])+\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}[m])\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{v}}

(99)

And for the weight updates:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{u}}[m+1]

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{u}}[m]+\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(-\bar{\mathbf{u}}[m]+\mathbf{u}[m])\Delta t

(100)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i}[m+1]

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i}[m]+\frac{1}{\tau_{f}}(-\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i}[m]+\mathbf{r}_{i}[m])\Delta t

(101)

\displaystyle\Delta W_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{W}}\sum_{m}\big(\mathbf{r}_{i}[m]-\phi(W_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i-1}[m])\big)\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i-1}[m]^{T}

(102)

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{Q}}\left(1+\frac{\tau_{v}}{\tau_{\epsilon}}\right)^{L-i}\sum_{m}-\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}[m](\mathbf{u}[m]-\bar{\mathbf{u}}[m])^{T}-M_{\max}\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{Q}}\beta Q_{i}

(103)

with
M_{\max}
the total number of simulation steps.

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{u}^{\text{int}}(t)+k\mathbf{e}(t),\quad\tau_{u}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{u}^{\text{int}}(t)=\mathbf{e}(t)-\tilde{\alpha}\mathbf{u}(t).

\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{fb}}_{i}[m+1]=Q_{i}\mathbf{u}[m+1]

\mathbf{e}[m]

\Delta t
this has almost no effect, but for bigger stepsizes it has the benefit of better reflecting the control interaction.

Following the exponential filtering approach for the low-pass filtering of
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{u}

[m+1]
of
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{u}

Below we further illustrate the robustness of Strong-DFC weight updates under noisy conditions. Recall that this is a direct consequence of the main novelty of Strong-DFC compared to standard DFC: the use of the true output target

F.1
Training Strong-DFC without re-visiting samples

To investigate whether Strong-DFC requires consistency or re-visiting training samples multiple times to perform well, we performed the following experiment using the student-teacher setting. Using a teacher of size 30-10-10-10-5 and a student of size 30-50-50-50-5, we trained networks for 300 epochs, each containing 500 training samples. In the standard setting, the 500 training samples were identical across epochs. In the modified setting, each training sample was independently drawn from the training distribution, so that no sample was seen twice. This was achieved by feeding the teacher with a new set of randomly-generated input samples at each epoch. Therefore, training consisted of a total of 150000 samples, but in the modified setting all these samples were unique. The results obtained are presented in Table
S1
.
