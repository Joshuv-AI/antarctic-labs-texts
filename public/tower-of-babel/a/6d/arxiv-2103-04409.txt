Risk Prediction with Imperfect Survival Outcome Information from Electronic Health Records
Stephanie F. Chan 1†, Jue Hou 1†, Xuan Wang 1, and Tianxi Cai 1,2,∗ 1 Department of Biostatistics, Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, Boston, MA 02115 2 Department of Biomedical Informatics, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA 02115 † Equal Contributors

Abstract
Readily available proxies for time of disease onset such as time of the first diagnostic code can lead to substantial risk prediction error if performing analyses based on poor proxies. Due to the lack of detailed documentation and labor intensiveness of manual annotation, it is often only feasible to ascertain for a small subset the current status of the disease by a follow up time rather than the exact time. In this paper, we aim to develop risk prediction models for the onset time efficiently leveraging both a small number of labels on current status and a large number of unlabeled observations on imperfect proxies. Under a semiparametric transformation model for onset and a highly flexible measurement error models for proxy onset time, we propose the semisupervised risk prediction method by combining information from proxies and limited labels efficiently. From an initial estimator solely based on the labelled subset, we perform a one-step correction with the full data augmenting against a mean zero rank correlation score derived from the proxies. We establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the proposed semi-supervised estimator and provide a resampling procedure for interval estimation. Simulation studies demonstrate that the proposed estimator performs well in finite sample. We illustrate the proposed estimator by developing a genetic risk prediction model for obesity using data from Partners Biobank Electronic Health Records (EHR).



keywords
Current status data, semi-supervised learning, measurement error, risk prediction.

1
Introduction

Electronic health records (EHRs), containing detailed medical history of individuals in the health care system, hold immense potential for translational research
(
Jensen
et al. 2012
)
. In recent years, EHR data has been increasingly explored for
developing risk prediction models to assist in clinical decision making
(
Eapen et al. 2013
;
Calvert et al. 2016
;
Jin
et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.
The longitudinal EHR data contain information on the occurrence time of
clinical events which can be used as outcomes for risk prediction modeling.
Rich clinical features, including lab measurements, medication prescriptions and co-morbidities,
can be extracted as risk factors. Such clinical information can be effectively extracted from either
codified data such as billing codes and procedure codes and from free text clinical notes via natural
language processing (NLP). At research institutions, EHR data have also been linked with biobanks
where genetic information can be included in addition to clinical features to further improve risk prediction.

Although longitudinal EHR data is of great value for risk prediction modeling, precise information
on clinical event time of interest,
T
, are not readily available.
Timing and number of diagnostic codes and mentions of the disease in the clinical notes
can serve as poxies of the true event time and status, but they are often not highly accurate.
For example, at Partner’s healthcare, having at least one diagnostic code of obesity only attained a
sensitivity of 73% and positive predictive value (PPV) of 81%; while having at least one NLP mention of
obesity attained a higher sensitivity of 92% but a lower PPV of 68%. It is even more challenging to
approximate event time. For lung cancer recurrence, time of chemotherapy and radiation
therapy initiations can only predict the true recurrence times up to 5.9 and 6.7 months on average

(
Uno et al. 2018
)
. Directly using proxy event times
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
to replace the true
T
for risk modeling can
lead to substantial bias due to the measurement error in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
. On the other hand, extracting
event time via manual annotation is too resource consuming for large scale research. Furthermore, the
exact timing of the event may not be precisely documented in the record and hence it is often only feasible
to annotate current status of the event
\delta=I(T\leq C)
, i.e. whether the event has occurred by the end of
the follow-up
C
. In this paper, we aim to develop an efficient EHR-based risk prediction procedure under a
semi-supervised (SS) setting with data from a small set of gold standard labels on
\delta
and a large
set of unlabeled data with mis-measured event times
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
.

With current status survival data, regression methods have proposed for commonly used survival models including the hazards (PH) and proportional odds (PO) models and the semi-parametric transformation model (STM)
(
Huang
et al. 1996
;
Rossini and
Tsiatis 1996
;
Huang and
Rossini 1997
;
Van Der Laan and
Robins 1998
;
Carroll
et al. 1997
;
Sun and
Sun 2005
)
. Estimation procedures have also been proposed
for other models including the additive hazard model and accelerated failure time model
(
Lin
et al. 1998
;
Chen and
Sun 2010
;
Betensky
et al. 2001
;
Tian and
Cai 2006
)
. Developing risk prediction models with mis-measured survival outcomes is a challenging problem and few methods currently exist, in part due to the additional complexity induced by censoring. Recently,
Oh
et al. 2018
introduced a bias correction procedure for hazard ratio estimates in the PH model.
Using a validation data where both the true and error prone survival times are both available,
Braun et al. 2018
proposed a non-parametric bias correction procedure for models using
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
as predictors.

No regression procedure currently exists for the (SS) setting with observations on both
\delta
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
. In addition, even without censoring, existing methods incorporating mis-measured outcomes largely require restrictive parametric measurement error model assumptions. To fill this gap, we propose an efficient and robust SS estimation procedure for the STM under flexible measurement error models without distributional assumptions. Our SS estimation starts with an initial supervised estimator based on the current status data via solving a system of kernel smoothed estimating equations and then constructs an augmented estimator by optimally combining the initial estimator with information from
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}
derived from a rank estimation procedure. We analyze the limiting distribution of the proposed estimator and develop inference method accordingly. For the scenario of the unlabelled data is much larger than the labelled data, we discover an interesting “space collapse” phenomenon of the SS estimator,
which requires special arrangements for estimation and inference.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We detail our SS procedures in Section 2.
In Section 3, we present results from simulation studies to examine the finite sample behavior of the SS estimator and compare its efficiency to the initial supervised estimator. In Section 4, we apply our methods to develop an age-specific risk prediction model for obesity based on demographic and genetic information using EHR data from the Partners Biobank. Concluding remarks are giving in Section 5.
Technical details are in the appendices.

2
Methods

Suppose there are a total of
N
subjects in the EHR cohort and a subset of
n
subjects are randomly sampled into the labeled set to have their event status annotated via manual chart review. Let
T_{i}
,
C_{i}

respectively denote the true event time, follow up time, and
p
dimensional baseline covariates for the
i
th subject. The true event time
T_{i}
is not observable but
\delta_{i}=I(T_{i}\leq C_{i})

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}=(\mathscr{T}_{1},...,\mathscr{T}_{K})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
, that can be viewed as proxies of
T

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}

and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=(\Delta_{1},...,\Delta_{K})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
, where
\mathscr{X}_{k}=\mathscr{T}_{k}\wedge C
and
\Delta_{k}=I(\mathscr{T}_{k}\leq C)
. The full underlying data of the EHR cohort consist of
\{(T_{i},C_{i},{\bf Z}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{T}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},i=1,\ldots,N\}
while the observed data consist of the labeled data
\mathscr{L}=\{(\delta_{i},C_{i},{\bf Z}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},i=1,\ldots,n\}
and the unlabeled data
\mathscr{U}=\{(C_{i},{\bf Z}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},i=n+1,\ldots,N\}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
C
has a continuous distribution with twice continuously differentiable density with finite support
[\mathscr{C}_{l},\mathscr{C}_{r}]
.

We predict
T_{i}
with
{\bf Z}_{i}
via the STM which includes PH and PO models as special cases:

P(T_{i}\leq t\mid{\bf Z}_{i})=g\{h_{0}(t)+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}

(1)

where
g(\cdot)
is a known smooth probability distribution function,
h_{0}(t)
is an unspecified smooth increasing function, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
is the unknown regression coefficient.
Under the STM (
1

E(\delta_{i}\mid C_{i},{\bf Z}_{i})=P(T_{i}\leq C_{i}|C_{i},{\bf Z}_{i})=g(h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i})

(2)

from which we later derive our estimating equations for the initial estimator.

For each of the mis-measured survival outcome
\mathscr{T}_{k}
, we assume that

{\cal H}(\mathscr{T}_{ki})={\cal H}(T_{i})+\epsilon_{ki},\quad\mbox{for }k=1,...,K,

(3)

where and
{\cal H}(\cdot)
is an unknown smooth transformation function and
\epsilon_{ki}
is independent of
(T_{i},{\bf Z}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},C_{i})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
with a completely unspecified distribution.
We also leave the within-subject correlation structure among
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{K})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}
unspecified. Leaving both
{\cal H}(\cdot)
and the distribution of
\epsilon_{k}
unspecified allow a wide range of measurement error models, including both additive and multiplicative measurement errors.
With the finite observation window ending at
\mathscr{C}_{r}
,
the truncated mis-measured survival outcome
\mathscr{T}_{ki}^{*}=\mathscr{T}_{ki}\wedge\mathscr{C}_{r}
is of greater practical interest,
as no event beyond
\mathscr{C}_{r}
is observable.
We show in
Appendix A
that
\mathscr{T}_{ki}^{*}
follows a generalized regression model as defined in

Han 1987
. It follows from
Han 1987
and
Sherman 1993
that the direction of the true parameter

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\cal B}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}/\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}
, is identified by the maximizer of the population rank correlation:

\boldsymbol{\cal B}_{0}=\mathop{\mbox{argmax}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\Omega_{p}}{\cal Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\;\quad\mbox{where}\quad{\cal Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=P\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}\mid\mathscr{T}_{ki}^{*}<\mathscr{T}_{kj}^{*}\right)

\Omega_{p}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}=1\}
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the
L_{2}
norm.
From the first order KKT condition,

\boldsymbol{\cal S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}_{0})={\bf 0},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\cal S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=E({\bf Z}_{i}-{\bf Z}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j},\mathscr{T}_{ki}^{*}<\mathscr{T}_{kj}^{*}).

(4)

Based on (
4
), we derive our score for the full cohort,
through which we update the initial estimator with the information
from the mis-measured survival outcomes.

2.1
Estimation

To estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, we first obtain an initial estimator using the labeled data

according to the moment equation (
2
).
Specifically, we adopt the kernel smoothed estimation procedure and estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}

\displaystyle[\delta_{i}-g\{h(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]=0

.
We define the operation through the following
(p-1)\times K(p-1)
matrix

\mathbb{P}_{k,j}=\left(\mathbb{O}_{(p-1)\times(pj-p)},\mathbb{I}_{p,-j},\mathbb{O}_{(p-1)\times(p^{2}-pj)}\right),

(16)

where
\mathbb{O}_{s\times r}
and
\mathbb{I}_{p,-j}
are defined with (
11
).
Likewise, the optimal combination of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}
and
\mathbb{P}_{j}{\widehat{\bf S}}({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})

is given by the projection

{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\mbox{cov}\{{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta},\mathbb{P}_{k,j}{\widehat{\bf S}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})\}\mbox{var}\{\mathbb{P}_{k,j}{\widehat{\bf S}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})\}^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{k,j}{\widehat{\bf S}}({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})

while the projection
\mbox{cov}\{{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta},\mathbb{P}_{k,j}{\widehat{\bf S}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})\}\mbox{var}\{\mathbb{P}_{k,j}{\widehat{\bf S}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})\}^{-1}
approaches asymptotically

\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}(\mathbb{P}_{k,j})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}=\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}(\mathbb{B}^{-1})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{A}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{P}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\left\{2\mathbb{P}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}^{-1}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}(\mathbb{B}^{-1})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{A}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{P}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\right\}^{-1}\mathbb{P}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}

(17)

We combine the
Kp
projections with
\mathbb{P}_{k,j}
,
k=1,\dots,K
,
j=1,\dots,p
,

{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\overline{\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\bf S}}({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}),\;\overline{\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}=\frac{1}{K|\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})|}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j\in\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})}\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}(\mathbb{P}_{k,j})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{P}_{k,j}.

Suppose
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}(\mathbb{P}_{k,j})
is an estimator for
\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}(\mathbb{P}_{k,j})
,
we obtain the final estimator by

{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}={\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf comb}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\bf S}}({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}),\;\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf comb}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}=\frac{1}{K|\widehat{\mathrm{supp}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})|}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j\in\widehat{\mathrm{supp}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})}\widehat{\mathbb{W}}(\mathbb{P}_{k,j})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{P}_{k,j}.

(18)

We derive the asymptotic approximation of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
as

{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{-1}\mathbb{B}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{-1}\mathbb{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}{\bf U}_{i}+o_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}\right).

(19)

Thus, the asymptotic distribution of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
is

\sqrt{n}({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\leadsto N\left(\mathbf{0},\frac{4\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{-1}\mathbb{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\right).

(20)

2.2
Inference

In both scenarios, the estimation of the optimal combination boils down to the estimation
of
\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}(\mathbb{P})
with operation
\mathbb{P}
.
We propose a resampling based procedure
(
Jin
et al. 2001
)
. Specifically, for
b=1,...,B
with
B\gg n
, we generate a vector of
N
independent and identically distributed random variables
\{{\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)},i=1,...,N\}
with
E({\cal V}_{i})=\mbox{var}({\cal V}_{i})=1
and obtain
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}
as the solution to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-t)[\delta_{i}-g\{h_{0}(t)+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]{\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}[\delta_{i}-g\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]{\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}

\displaystyle=0.

Then we obtain the perturbed counterpart of
{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
as

{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=\frac{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}{\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}{\cal V}_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}({\bf Z}_{i}-{\bf Z}_{j})K_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}}{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}{\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}{\cal V}_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}},\quad\mbox{for $k=1,...,K$},

where
{\widehat{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(t)=\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(C_{i}\geq t){\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}\}/(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\cal V}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})
. Subsequently, we obtain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}={\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}/\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}\|_{2}
and
{\widehat{\bf S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})=[{\widehat{\bf S}}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},...,{\widehat{\bf S}}_{K}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}]
.
We consider the least square problem

\widehat{{\bf w}}_{j}(\mathbb{P})=\mathop{\mbox{argmin}}_{{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}}\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left\{{\widehat{\beta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}-{\bf w}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{P}{\widehat{\bf S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})\right\}^{2},

(21)

where
r
is the row dimension of
\mathbb{P}
.
We assemble the estimated coefficients
\widehat{{\bf w}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}),\dots,\widehat{{\bf w}}_{p}(\mathbb{P})

to construct estimated projection matrix

\widehat{\mathbb{W}}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{P}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}(\widehat{{\bf w}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\bf w}}_{p})=\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}(\mathbb{P})+O_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}\right).

(22)

By (
22
) and
the consistency of support recovery
\widehat{\mathrm{supp}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
, we have
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf comb}=\overline{\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}}+O_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
.

To quantify the estimation error,
it is natural to consider the perturbed SSL estimator

{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}={\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf comb}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\bf S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}),\;b=1,...,B.

By
Jin
et al. 2001
,
{\widehat{\bf S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})
has the same asymptotic distribution of

{\widehat{\bf S}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta})
.
From (
10
), we have
{\widehat{\bf S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.
Since
{\widehat{\bf S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})
converges to zero,
the variability from estimating
\overline{\mathbb{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf opt}}
by
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf comb}

has negligible effect on the asymptotical distribution of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
.
Hence,
\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf comb}
can be used directly in the perturbed SSL estimator
without an extra layer of perturbation.
Let
Q_{\alpha}({\bf v})
be the
\alpha
-quantile of
the perturbations
\{{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}:b=1,\dots,B\}
.
Observing the skewness in the finite sample distribution of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
,
we recommend the following
(1-\alpha)\times 100\%
confidence interval
for
{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
with re-centered empirical quantiles

\left[Q_{\alpha/2}({\bf v})+{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL},Q_{1-\alpha/2}({\bf v})+{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}\right].

(23)

In the
N\gg n
scenario, we discover an interesting “space collapse” phenomenon from the representation
(
19
).
The SSL estimation error
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
concentrates in a one-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
spanned by

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
with large probability.
For any vector
{\bf v}
such that
{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=0
, the SSL estimator
{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}

is super efficient,

{\bf v}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}=o_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}\right).

A typical example is the zero element in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
indexed by
j\notin\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
,
which can be represented with
j
-th natural basis
{\bf e}_{j}
,

{\bf e}_{j}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\beta_{0,j}=0,\;{\bf e}_{j}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}=\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle SSL,j}=o_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}\right).

Since
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle SSL,j}-0)
is no longer asymptotically regular,
the perturbation samples
\sqrt{n}\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}
might not capture its asymptotic distribution.
To provide a valid inference for
\beta_{0,j}
, we perform the norm preserving soft-thresholding,

\displaystyle{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}=(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,1},\dots,\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,p})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\;\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,j}=\mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j})\max\{|\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}|-\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}/|\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}|,0\},

\displaystyle{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}=(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)},\dots,\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,p}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}},\;\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}=\mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})\max\{|\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}|-\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}/|\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}|,0\},

\displaystyle{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std}={\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}\frac{\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}\|_{2}}{\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}\|_{2}},\;{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}={\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}\frac{\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}\|_{2}}{\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}\|_{2}}

for
n^{-1/2}\gg\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}\gg n^{-1}
.
With the soft-thresholding, we also require that the re-centering of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}
preserves the sign,

\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf center,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}=\mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})\max\left\{|\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}|+\mathrm{sign}(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)})\left(\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std,j}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf std,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}\right),0\right\}

Let
Q_{std,j,\alpha}
be the
\alpha
-quantile of
the perturbations
\{\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf center,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}:b=1,\dots,B\}
.
We construct the
(1-\alpha)\times 100\%
confidence interval
for
\beta_{0,j}
with
\left[Q_{std,j,\alpha/2},Q_{std,j,1-\alpha/2}\right]
.
Since we have
\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}
is asymptotically regular and

\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf center,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}

\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf center,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}=\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}+O(\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft})=\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}+o(n^{-1/2}),

the confidence interval for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,j}\neq 0

with the re-centered norm preserving soft-thresholded perturbation
must achieve asymptotically the nominal coverage.
The confidence interval for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,j}=0
,
however,
would have
100\%
coverage rate asymptotically as the
\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,j}
and
\hat{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf(b)}
,
b=1,\dots,B
are all shrunk to zero with large probability.

We recommend select
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf soft}
through a cross-validation scheme targeting the aggregated rank correlation of
mis-measured survival times

\hat{{\cal Q}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{{\cal Q}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{I(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}>\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}}.

Exact evaluation of higher order terms in (
19
) is possible
but would multiply the computational burden.
Since testing
\beta_{0,j}=0
can be achieved solely in an unsupervised fashion with the mis-measured survival times based
on the rank correlation
(
Cai and
Cheng 2007
)
, we choose not to deviate from our SSL framework and expand in such direction.

3
Simulations

We conducted simulation studies to assess the performance of our estimator in finite sample settings. Throughout, we set the labelled sample size as
n=500
and consider two sizes for total sample

N=1000
and
N=10000
.
The two total sample sizes characterize the
n\asymp N
and
n\ll N
scenarios,
respectively.
We first generated a
p=10
dimensional
{\bf Z}
from a zero-mean multivariate normal with unit variance and correlation 0.2. Then we generated
T_{i}
from

3\log(T_{i}/4)=-0.7(Z_{i1}+Z_{i2}+Z_{i3})+0.5(Z_{i4}+Z_{i5}+Z_{i6})-0.3(Z_{i7}+Z_{i8}+Z_{i9})+\varepsilon_{i}

where
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,1)
. The censoring
C_{i}
was generated from from Uniform
(0,a)
with
a
chosen such that

P(\delta_{i}=1)=0.5
. We consider
K=2
surrogates generated from
\log\mathscr{T}_{ki}=\log T_{i}+\epsilon_{ki}
, where

\epsilon_{k}
is generated from a normal mixture
\sim D_{ki}N(\mu_{k-},\sigma_{k-}^{2})+(1-D_{ki})N(\mu_{k+},\sigma_{k+}^{2})
and
D_{ki}\sim\mbox{Bernoulli}(0.5)
. The normal mixture measurement error distribution was chosen to allow for more heterogeneity in how their records appear in the EHR system.
We consider two scenarios for the error distribution: (A) low measurement error with

(\mu_{1-},\sigma_{1-},\mu_{1+},\sigma_{1+})=(0.2,0.3,-0.1,0.1)
and

(\mu_{2-},\sigma_{2-},\mu_{2+},\sigma_{2+})=(0,0.2,0.3,0.1)
; (B) high measurement error with

(\mu_{1-},\sigma_{1-},\mu_{1+},\sigma_{1+})=(1,1.5,-0.5,0.5)
and

(\mu_{2-},\sigma_{2-},\mu_{2+},\sigma_{2+})=(0,1,1.5,0.5)
.

The bandwidth
h
for estimating
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}
was chosen to be
\widehat{\tau}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i})^{-0.25}
, where
\widehat{\tau}
is the empirical standard deviation of
C
. The bandwidth
h_{k}^{\prime}
for
{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}
is chosen to be
\widehat{\sigma}(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{ki})^{-0.3}
where
\widehat{\sigma}
is the empirical standard error of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\cal B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\mathbf{Z}
. For each scenario, we summarize results using 500 datasets. For the standard error estimates via resampling, we use
B=200
replications.

In Table
1
, we show results for the bias, mean square error (MSE) of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
and the relative efficiency (RE) of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
compared to the supervised estimator
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}
. Both estimators have negligible biases across all settings.
When
N\asymp n
, the SS estimator
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
is more efficient than the supervised estimator
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}
under the low measurement error model with efficiency gain ranging from 27% to 62% but the efficiency gain is minimal for the high measurement error setting. When
N\gg n
,
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
is substantially more efficient than
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}
with efficiency gain ranging from 185% to 9964% for the low measurement error setting and from 133% to 1559% for the high measurement error setting. The efficiency gain is the highest for the zero coefficient as expected from our theoretical findings on the super efficiency of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\sf SSL}
on zero coefficients. These results also suggest that
the semi-supervised learning improves efficiency even with large measurement error in surrogates
when the unlabelled data is large.

As one of the most serious public health problems in the 21st century, obesity affects about 12% of adults globally
(
GBD 2015
Obesity Collaborators 2017
)
. Although there are lifestyle changes that can be made to prevent it, there is a strong genetic component to obesity, which is a risk factor for many other conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes. We applied our proposed method to develop a genetic risk prediction model for obesity using data from Partner’s Healthcare Biobank (PHB) where both genetic and EHR data are available for 30,685 participating patients.

Among the PHB subjets, 268 patients have their obesity status
\delta
annotated by domain experts via manual chart review. The genetic risk score (GRS) for obesity was constructed based on published log odds ratio information on 55 SNPs previously identified as significantly associated with obesity
(
Speliotes
et al. 2010
;
Hung
et al. 2015
)
. For this analysis, we use patient age as time scale. We use 2 surrogates for event time: age at the first diagnostic code for obesity and age at the first NLP mention of obesity. In addition to the GRS, we included sex, and the first 5 principal components of 128 SNPs associated with the ancestry informative genetic markers
(
Kosoy
et al. 2009
)
to adjust for population stratification. We let
g(\cdot)
be the logistic link and hence
\boldsymbol{\beta}
corresponds to log odds ratio of the risk factors.

The point estimators of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, as well as their 95% confidence intervals are shown in Table
3
.
The results show that the supervised and semi-supervised point estimators are reasonably consistent with each other.
Our analysis confirms that higher GRS is significantly associated with an elevated risks of
developing obesity, with estimated effect 0.610 and p-value 0.022 from the semi-supervised
method.
The negative association between male gender and risk of obesity in our analysis,
with estimated effect -0.365 from the semi-supervised
method,
is also consistent with the findings among the literatures
(
Wang and
Beydoun 2007
)
.
Moreover, the 95% CIs from the semi-supervised estimator are always smaller than the supervised estimator.
For example, the estimated standard error of GRS coefficient is 0.327 in the supervised
estimator and 0.274 in the semi-supervised estimator, resulting in an efficiency gain of 1.415.
This again demonstrates the benefit of leveraging the information from the mis-measured
event times in our proposed procedure.

Discussion

We proposed a robust SS estimators for risk prediction modeling in the EHR setting. Our proposed SSL estimator is able to effectively integrate two sets of imperfect information on the survival time in the EHR database: (i) widely available but noisy surrogate event times; and (ii) current status information manually annotated for a limited set of patients. The SSL method can efficiently estimate the risk model without requiring precise information on the event time for any patients in the EHR, which greatly improves the feasibility of performing risk prediction modeling using noisy EHR data. Our numerical results demonstrate that the SSL approach can significantly improve the efficiency of the estimation with large unlabelled data compared to the supervised estimator that only uses the current status data.

In the obesity risk modeling example with PHB data, we used age as the time scale and hence baseline is defined at birth. In such a case, only time invariant covariates such as sex and genetic information can be used as risk factors. However, our method is not restricted to such settings provided that a valid baseline can be defined and subjects are free of the event of interest at baseline.
For example, the baseline can be set as a year after the first encounter with the EHR. Patients without any ICD code and/or NLP mention of the disease of interest can be considered as free of event at baseline since ICD and NLP mentions are often highly sensitive but not specific
(
Liao et al. 2019
)
. For such cases, standard risk factors beyond genetics such as lifestyle information can be included as covariates.

Appendix A
Verification of Single Index Model

Here we show that each of the
\mathscr{T}_{k}
follows a single index model.
Suppose
f_{\epsilon_{k}}(u)
is the probability density function of
\varepsilon_{ki}
.
By direct calculation, we find that conditional distribution function of
\mathscr{T}_{ki}
is

\displaystyle P(\mathscr{T}_{ki}\leq t|{\bf Z}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({\cal H}(\mathscr{T}_{ki})\leq{\cal H}(t)|{\bf Z}_{i})=P({\cal H}(T_{i})+\epsilon_{ki}\leq{\cal H}(t)|{\bf Z}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({\cal H}(T)\leq{\cal H}(t)-\epsilon_{ki}|{\bf Z}_{i})=P(T\leq{\cal H}^{-1}({\cal H}(t)-\epsilon_{ki})|{\bf Z}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int g(h_{0}({\cal H}^{-1}({\cal H}(t)-u))+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i})f_{\epsilon_{k}}(u)du,

which is still a increasing function of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}
and
\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}
.
The proof here shows that the truncated
\mathscr{T}_{ki}\wedge\mathscr{C}_{r}
also follows the single index model,
as

P(\mathscr{T}_{ki}\wedge\mathscr{C}_{r}\leq t|{\bf Z}_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}P(\mathscr{T}_{ki}\leq t|{\bf Z}_{i})&t<\mathscr{C}_{r},\\
1&t\geq\mathscr{C}_{r}\end{array}\right.

is also an increasing function of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}
.

Asymptotic property of the initial estimator

Under the semiparametric single index model (
2
) for current status
\delta_{i}
, our initial estimation is a special case of the quasi-likelihood approach considered in
Carroll
et al. 1997
.
To be specific, our initial estimator is the fully iterated estimator with misspecified variance in their paper.
By their Remark 2 and Theorem 3, we have the consistency and asymptotic normality of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}

In the following, we derive the asymptotic distribution of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}

The estimators

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}[\delta_{i}-g\{\widehat{h}(C_{i})+{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]=0

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-t)[\delta_{i}-g\{\widehat{h}(t)+{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]=0,\;t=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{n}.

(A.1)

We denote the remainder rate as

R_{n}=\sqrt{n}\left\{\left\|\widehat{h}-h_{0}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\right\|_{2}\right\}\left\{\left\|\widehat{h}-h_{0}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\right\|_{2}+(nh)^{-1/2}+h^{2}\right\}+\sqrt{n}h^{2}.

To derive the asymptotic distribution of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}

we first consider the local approximation of the first sets of equations in (
A.1
),

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}[\delta_{i}-g\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]=

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}[g\{\widehat{h}(C_{i})+{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}-g\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\{\widehat{h}(C_{i})-h_{0}(C_{i})\}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n}\mbox{E}\left[{\bf Z}_{i}{\bf Z}_{i}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\right]\{{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\}

\displaystyle+O_{p}\left(R_{n}\right).

The key is the analysis of the first order term for the nonparametric component

n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\{\widehat{h}(C_{i})-h_{0}(C_{i})\}

(A.2)

From the second set of equations in (
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})[\delta_{i}-g\{h_{0}(C_{j})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})[g\{\widehat{h}(C_{j})+{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}-g\{h_{0}(C_{j})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{j})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\{\widehat{h}(C_{j})-h_{0}(C_{j})\}

\displaystyle+n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{j})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\{{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\}

\displaystyle+O_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}R_{n}\right).

With

being the density of censoring time
C_{i}
and

a_{0}(t)=\mbox{E}[f_{c}(t)g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(t)+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}],\;{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{1}(t)=\mbox{E}[f_{c}(t)g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(t)+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}{\bf Z}_{i}].

defined in (
7
),
we may approximate the estimation error of the nonparametric component
\widehat{h}(C_{i})-h_{0}(C_{i})
as

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(C_{j})-h_{0}(C_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{a_{0}(C_{j})\right\}^{-1}\left((nh)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})[\delta_{i}-g\{h_{0}(C_{j})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}]-{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{1}(C_{j})^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\{{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\}\right)

\displaystyle+O_{p}\left(n^{-1/2}R_{n}\right).

(A.3)

Here we also obtain

Plugging (

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\{\widehat{h}(C_{i})-h_{0}(C_{i})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\left\{a_{0}(C_{i})\right\}^{-1}n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})[\delta_{j}-g\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}\}]

\displaystyle-\sqrt{n}\mbox{E}\left[{\bf Z}_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{1}(C_{i})g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}/a_{0}(C_{i})\right]^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}\{{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\delta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\}

as defined in (
7

By change the order of the sum over

and

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\left\{a_{0}(C_{i})\right\}^{-1}n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})[\delta_{j}-g\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}\}]

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\delta_{j}-g\{h_{0}(C_{j})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}\}]n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\left\{a_{0}(C_{i})\right\}^{-1}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})

\displaystyle-n^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Z}_{i}g^{\prime}\{h_{0}(C_{i})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}\}\left\{a_{0}(C_{i})\right\}^{-1}\mathbb{K}_{h}(C_{i}-C_{j})

Gathering the results, we have reached

Appendix C
Asymptotic property of rank correlation

We focus our analysis on the numerator of
{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
,

{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\bf Z}_{i}-{\bf Z}_{j})\mathbb{K}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}.

The denominator of
{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
does not contain the parameter
\boldsymbol{\cal B}
,
and it is easy to show its convergence to its mean,

N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}\to\mbox{E}\left\{\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}\right\}=1/2.

We may eventually substitute the denominator by its limit
1/2
through a Slutsky’s Theorem argument,

2{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})-{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=o_{p}\left({\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}_{0})+\|\boldsymbol{\cal B}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}_{0}\|_{2}\right).

(A.4)

Now, we establish the connection between
{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})

and the rank correlation

\widehat{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}I(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}>0)\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}.

(
9
)

We define the anti-derivative of the symmetric smooth kernel
\mathbb{K}(\cdot)
as

\mathbb{F}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbb{K}(u)du,\;\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbb{K}_{h^{\prime}}(u)du=\mathbb{F}(x/h^{\prime}).

(A.5)

Assuming
\mathbb{K}(\cdot)
is a symmetric probability density function with second moment,
then functions
\mathbb{F}(x)-I(x>0)
and
x\{\mathbb{F}(x)-I(x>0)\}
are absolutely integrable
over
\mathbb{R}
,
and

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{F}(x)-I(x>0)dx=0.

Thus for any continuously differentiable function
f(x)
, we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}(x)-I(x>0)\}f(x)dx=O(h^{\prime}f^{\prime}(0)).

(A.6)

Our score
{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
is the gradient of the smoothed rank correlation

\widehat{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{\widehat{G}(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}},\;{\widehat{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=\nabla\widehat{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}).

(A.7)

Cai and
Cheng 2007
has thoroughly studied the rank correlation without smoothing
\widehat{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
,
and
we shall develop the properties for
\widehat{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
by connecting it to those of
\widehat{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
.

By the uniform consistency of empirical distribution
\widehat{G}(\cdot)

(
Dvoretzky
et al. 1956
)
,
we may replace the estimated
\widehat{G}(\cdot)
by the true
G(\cdot)
in
\widehat{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})

and
\widehat{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
with an
O_{p}\left(N^{-1/2}\right)
error,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}=\widehat{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})+O_{p}\left(N^{-1/2}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}I(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}>0)\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}=\widehat{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})+O_{p}\left(N^{-1/2}\right).

(A.8)

Define the limiting processes for
\widetilde{Q}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})

and
\widetilde{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
as

q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=\mbox{E}\{q_{kij}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})\}
,

\displaystyle q_{ijk}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\left\{I(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}>0)\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+I(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}>0)\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{kj}<\mathscr{X}_{ki})\Delta_{kj}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{kj})^{2}}\right\},

\displaystyle q_{ijk}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{kj}<\mathscr{X}_{ki})\Delta_{kj}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{kj})^{2}}\right\}.

(A.9)

We assume that

is twice continuously differentiable.
By a U process argument
(
Nolan and
Pollard 1987
)
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\cal B}:\|\boldsymbol{\cal B}\|_{2}\leq 2}|\widetilde{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})-q(\boldsymbol{\cal B})|+|\widetilde{Q}^{*}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})-q^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})|\to 0

almost surely on the compact support
\|\boldsymbol{\cal B}\|_{2}\leq 2
.
By the property of
\mathbb{F}_{h^{\prime}}
as in (

),
we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\cal B}:\|\boldsymbol{\cal B}\|_{2}\leq 2}|q^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})-q(\boldsymbol{\cal B})|=O(h^{\prime})=o(1).

Thus, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\cal B}:\|\boldsymbol{\cal B}\|_{2}\leq 2}|\widetilde{Q}^{*}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})-q(\boldsymbol{\cal B})|\to 0,\;\text{almost surely.}

(A.10)

Next, we show that
\widetilde{{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}
has a continuous limit
so that
we may establish its limit as
\dot{{\bf q}}_{k}
.
We first consider

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=

\displaystyle N^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\dot{{\bf q}}_{ijk}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}),

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf q}}_{ijk}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\{({\bf Z}_{i}-{\bf Z}_{j})\mathbb{K}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{ki}<\mathscr{X}_{kj})\Delta_{ki}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{ki})^{2}}\right.

(A.11)

\displaystyle+\left.({\bf Z}_{j}-{\bf Z}_{i})\mathbb{K}_{h^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\cal B}^{{\sf\scriptscriptstyle{T}}}{\bf Z}_{i})\frac{I(\mathscr{X}_{kj}<\mathscr{X}_{ki})\Delta_{kj}}{G(\mathscr{X}_{kj})^{2}}\right\}

(A.12)

Using the same U process argument
(
Nolan and
Pollard 1987
)
, we can show that for
h^{\prime}\gg 1/N

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\cal B}:\|\boldsymbol{\cal B}\|_{2}\leq 2}\|\widetilde{{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})-\dot{{\bf q}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})\|_{2}\to 0,\;a.s.,\quad\dot{{\bf q}}_{k}^{*}=\mbox{E}\{\dot{{\bf q}}_{ijk}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})\}=\nabla q_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B}).

(A.13)

where we use the dot notation for gradient.
As the smoothed version of
\dot{{\bf q}}_{k}
,
the continuity of
\dot{{\bf q}}^{*}_{k}
is implied by the continuity of
\dot{{\bf q}}_{k}
.
Thus, we have shown that
\widehat{{\bf S}}_{k}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\cal B})
converges to a continuous limit.
By a calculus result, the differentiation and limit are commutable for a uniformly convergent function sequence
whose derivative converges uniformly to a continuous function.
Combining (
A.10
) and (
A.13
),
we apply the calculus result point-wisely in the probability space to obtain

Asymptotic collinearity of

We first show a property of the quantities derived from the rank correlation in (

).

\boldsymbol{\cal B}

We identify the optimal projection as
