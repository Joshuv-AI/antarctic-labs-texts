Stochastic super-resolution for Gaussian microtextures Thanks: The authors acknowledge the support of the project MISTIC (ANR-19-CE40-005).
Émile Pierret, Bruno Galerne

Abstract
Super-Resolution (SR) is the problem that consists in reconstructing images that have been degraded by a zoom-out operator. This is an ill-posed problem that does not have a unique solution, and numerical approaches rely on a prior on high-resolution images. While optimization-based methods are generally deterministic, with the rise of image generative models more and more interest has been given to stochastic SR, that is, sampling among all possible SR images associated with a given low-resolution input. In this paper, we construct an efficient, stable and provably exact sampler for the stochastic SR of Gaussian microtextures. Even though our approach is limited regarding the scope of images it encompasses, our algorithm is competitive with deep learning state-of-the-art methods both in terms of perceptual metric and execution time when applied to microtextures. The framework of Gaussian microtextures also allows us to rigorously discuss the limitations of various reconstruction metrics to evaluate the efficiency of SR routines.



Keywords:
stochastic super-resolution, Gaussian textures, conditional simulation, kriging, super-resolution with a reference image

1
Introduction

Super-Resolution (SR) algorithms aim at producing a High Resolution (HR) image corresponding to a Low Resolution (LR) one, the main challenge being to restore sharp edges as well as high frequency texture content that are lost when applying the zoom-out operator.
Since the set of HR images compatible with a given LR input is an affine subspace of high dimension, a strong prior on realistic images is necessary to recover HR images of high visual quality within this subspace.
In recent works, this prior is generally conveyed either by exploiting a large datasets of HR images via deep learning models or using a reference HR image to specify an adapted model for the unknown HR image.
The former solution is rather generic while the later is particularly adapted for the SR of texture images that benefits from a particular prior on local statistics of the HR unknown image.
Besides, rather than being deterministic, several contributions propose to tackle the one to many dilemma via stochastic SR which consists in sampling among all acceptable HR images associated with an LR input.
Motivated by such recent contributions, in this paper we solve the stochastic SR problem when the HR texture image is assumed to follow a stationary Gaussian distribution.

1.2
Contributions

Our approach builds on the kriging framework used for Gaussian inpainting
[
22
,
21
]
.
We further exploit the stationarity of the zoom-out operator to obtain an exact and fast convolution-based algorithm for stochastic SR of grayscale Gaussian textures.
Due to correlation between color chanels, this algorithm does not translate straightforwardly to RGB textures, but we propose an approximate algorithm for RGB textures that proves to be experimentally close to the exact but time-consuming algorithm.
Finally, on a practical viewpoint, we extend our approach to the context of SR given a reference HR image, and show that, when applied to Gaussian textures, our fast Gaussian SR algorithm is both perceptually better and order of magnitudes faster than recent deep learning or optimal transport-based methods.
While our approach has several inherent limitations that we discuss and illustrate, our strong experimental results show that it is of practical interest when dealing with simple microtextures.

2
The conditional super-resolution of grayscale Gaussian microtextures

In this section we solve the problem of Stochastic super-resolution of grayscale Gaussian microtextures using a known zoom-out operator given by a convolution.
Exploiting both the stationarity of the LR measurements and the HR texture model, our main result states that one can exactly sample new HR microtextures by applying a specific convolution operator.
We first recall the specifics of the zoom-out operator and the ADSN microtexture model. Then we recall how solving for kriging coefficients allows for conditional Gaussian simulation and establish our main result.

2.1
The zoom-out operator

The most common zoom-out operator used in the SR literature is the Matlab bicubic zoom-out operator provided by the function

[

]

Let us denote

It can be described as a convolution followed by a subsampling

2.3
The kriging reasoning

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M,N}}}
be a random vector following a Gaussian multivariate law
\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
with
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M,N}}\times{\Omega_{M,N}}}
and let
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}\times{\Omega_{M,N}}}

The simulation of
{\boldsymbol{X}}

{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}

21

Theorem 1

(Contidional Gaussian simulation)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}}\sim\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}
be a linear operator. The two Gaussian vectors

{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}})
and
{\boldsymbol{X}}-{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}})
are independent. Consequently, if
\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}
is independent of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
with the same distribution then
{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}})+[\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}-{\mathbb{E}}(\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}})]
has the same distribution as
{\boldsymbol{X}}
knowing
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}
.

Furthermore, in the Gaussian context, if the distribution is zero-mean, the relation between the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}})
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}
is linear, as detailed in the following.

Theorem 2

(Gaussian kriging)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}}\sim\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
be a
Gaussian
vector with
zero mean
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}\times{\Omega_{M,N}}}
be a linear operator.
There exists
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{{{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}}\times{{\Omega_{M,N}}}}
such that
{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
verifies the matrix equation

{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}={\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}.

(2)

Theorem
2
is a standard
[
38
]

Yet since solving Equation

The matrix

is called the kriging matrix and Equation

In our context of super-resolution,

Using Theorem

Note that

2.4
Solving the kriging equation in the super-resolution context

We show in this section that in our SR context, the kriging equation can be solved efficiently with a non-iterative algorithm.

Equation
2
may have several solutions and we only consider the specific solution
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T})^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
where
({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T})^{\dagger}
is the pseudo-inverse of
{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}

The main practical issue is now to compute efficiently

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is an exact solution of Equation

and for all

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\star({\boldsymbol{S}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{v}})

where

The application of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}
implies the division by
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}
in the Fourier domain. However, in practice, this division can be unstable since a non-zero low value of
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}
amplifies its corresponding frequency. Actually, in our context,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}
is applied to LR versions of realizations of an ADSN model that are
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}}\star{\boldsymbol{w}})
with
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M,N}}}
an unknown white Gaussian noise. Proposition
2
ensures that the application of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}
is stable when applied to images that comply with this assumption.

Proposition 2

(Stability of the kriging operator on the subspace of the LR ADSN samples)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M,N}}\times{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}}
. Then,

\forall{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{M,N}},\left\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}(t\star{\boldsymbol{w}})\right\|_{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\|_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{t}}\|_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2}.

(5)

Proof.

As a reminder
[
48
]
, let
{\boldsymbol{M}}
being a real-valued matrix,

{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\dagger}=\left({\boldsymbol{M}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{M}}\right)^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{M}}^{T}

With
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{T}
,

{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{T\dagger}=\left({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}})^{T}\right)^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}=\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}

Consequently, for
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M,N}}}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}}\star{\boldsymbol{w}})=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}{\boldsymbol{w}}={\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}})^{T}\left(({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}})^{T}\right)^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{w}}

and

\left\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{T}{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}}\star{\boldsymbol{w}})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\right\|_{2}\underbrace{\left\|({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}})^{T}\left(({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}})^{T}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}}_{\leq 1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\right\|_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{t}}\right\|_{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{2},

using that for any matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
one has
\|{\boldsymbol{M}}{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq 1
.

3
The conditional super-resolution of RGB Gaussian microtextures

3.1
Description of the framework and notation

We denote by
3{\Omega_{M,N}}
the space of the RGB images and by
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{3}
the three chanels of a given RGB image
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M,N}}}
.
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
designates the Discrete Fourier transform of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
defined as the 2D DFT of each chanel.
The associated texton of an RGB image
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is
{\boldsymbol{t}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{NM}}({\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M,N}}}
where
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M,N}}}
is the mean RGB color. The ADSN model
\ADSN({\boldsymbol{u}})
is defined as the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{m}}+\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}\star{\boldsymbol{w}}\\
{\boldsymbol{t}}_{2}\star{\boldsymbol{w}}\\
{\boldsymbol{t}}_{3}\star{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{pmatrix}
where
{\boldsymbol{w}}\sim\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\Omega_{M,N}}})
.
Note that the white Gaussian noise is the same in each chanel
[
19
]
.

\ADSN({\boldsymbol{u}})
is a multivariate Gaussian law associated with the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}}\\
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{2}}\\
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{3}} \\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}}\\
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{2}}\\
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{3}} \\
\end{pmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M,N}}\times 3{\Omega_{M,N}}}
which is the matrix of a multi-channel convolution, that is, each output chanel is a linear combination of 2D convolutions of the input RGB chanels.
More precisely, for all
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M,N}}}
,

\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)_{i}={\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\star\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}}\star{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\star\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{2}}\star{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}+{\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\star\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{3}}\star{\boldsymbol{u}}_{3},\quad 1\leq i\leq 3.

(6)

Unfortunately, this matrix is not diagonal in the Fourier basis which makes a major difference with the grayscale framework. We still denote by
{\boldsymbol{A}}={\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}
the zoom-out operator that acts on each chanel for an RGB image.

3.2
Approximating the kriging operator in the RGB case

To simulate conditional sample of the RGB ADSN model, the kriging equation
2
and Lemma
1
are still valid, except that now
{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}
and
\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}
are multi-chanel convolutions.
However, while the pseudo-inverse of a 2D convolution is diagonal in the Fourier basis, this is not the case for a multi-chanel convolution.

Computing the pseudo-inverse
\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}
turns out to be a critical task.
Even for small images, a direct computation of the pseudo-inverse of
{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}
with standard routines leads to instabilities.
A more principled approach is to remark that for each
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}}
and each
\omega\in{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}
, there exists a
3\times 3
matrix
\widehat{K}(\omega)
such that
\mathscr{F}_{r}({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{v}})(\omega)=\widehat{K}(\omega)\widehat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\omega)
, reducing the problem to computing one 3
\times
3 pseudo-inverse for each frequency.

Still, each matrix is close to be singular which leads to a high instability in practice.

In order to keep an algorithm which is as fast and as stable as in the grayscale case (Algorithm
1
), we propose to make the following approximation: let
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M,N}}}
, following the Gaussian law
\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}\mid{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{3}\right]\approx\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}\mid{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}\right],\quad 1\leq i\leq 3.

(7)

The rational behind this approximation is that to reconstruct the chanel
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}
from the three chanels
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}=({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{3})
, the more relevant information is in
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}
. This is a reasonable assumption with respect to the behavior of natural images and the form of the bicubic convolution kernel.
This approximation amounts to use

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{approx}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{2}&{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}\times 3{\Omega_{M,N}}}

(8)

as an approximate solution of Equation
2
in the RGB setting,
where for each
1\leq i\leq 3
,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}\times{\Omega_{M,N}}}
is a solution of

{\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}={\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq 3,

(9)

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}
being the covariance matrix of the
i
th chanel.
While
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{approx}}
is not a solution of the kriging equation, it is the kriging matrix associated with an ADSN law for which the RGB chanels are uncorrelated (which is not an interesting model to generate textures).
However, we apply this kriging operator
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{approx}}^{T}
to textures having properly correlated chanels according to the ADSN model
\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
.
More precisely, a RGB Gaussian SR sample
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\text{SR}}
is defined as

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\text{SR},i}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}^{T}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{\text{LR},i}-{\boldsymbol{A}}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{i})+\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq 3,

(10)

where, for
1\leq i\leq 3
,
\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}_{i}={\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\star{\boldsymbol{w}}
with
{\boldsymbol{w}}\sim\mathscr{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{I}}_{{\Omega_{M,N}}})
the common noise used for each channel and the multiplication by
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}^{T}
is done following Algorithm
1
.

In the next section, we assess that our proposed approximation is harmless by evaluating it with an exact reference iterative alternative.

3.3
Comparison with the reference CGD algorithm

In
[
21
]
, the authors propose to solve the kriging equation for Gaussian microtextures to resolve the inpainting by conditional simulation.
To solve Equation
2
, they apply a CGD to compute
\left({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}\right)^{\dagger}\varphi
for a given
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{{\Omega_{M/r,N/r}}}
. For completeness, the algorithm is recalled in
C
. This is an iterative algorithm with a stopping criteria
\varepsilon
and a given number of iterations. The output
\psi
of CGD tends to minimize

\left\|({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T})^{2}\varphi-({\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T})\psi\right\|_{2}

(11)

which is the residual for the normal equation associated with Equation
2
.
We consider CGD with a high number of
10^{6}
steps as a reference.
The recommended number of steps for the inpainting problem is
10^{2}
for fast results and
10^{3}
for high-quality results
[
20
]
and our experiments show that these recommendations remain valid for super-resolution.
Results from our fast convolution-based algorithm and this reference CGD-based algorithm are presented in Figure
3
for both the grayscale and RGB cases.
The same realization of noise is used for the simulations so that both algorithm should produce the same image.
Samples are visually similar for the two examples and when visualized as a sequence the texture details added by the CGD algorithm do not evolve after
10^{3}
steps.

Ìn

The high PSNR indicates that our direct Gaussian SR algorithm is more precise than CGD

In the color case, as expected, Gaussian SR is not exact and provides higher residual and PSNR values.
Still the Gaussian SR samples are closer to CGD outputs with a number of steps between

r

This validates our approach since the minor approximation we introduced is in practice negligible.
Let us recall that the main objective of our approximation is to reduce the simulation time.
As reported in

4
Gaussian SR in practice: Reference image and comparison with state-of-the-art

4.1
Gaussian SR with a reference image

To apply Gaussian SR algorithm, the
\ADSN
distribution followed by
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{HR}}
has to be known (via the computation of the associated texton
{\boldsymbol{t}}
).
In the experiments of the previous section, the texton is computed from the ground truth HR image
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{HR}}
, making the algorithm impractical.

From now on, following recent contributions on texture super-resolution
[
25
,
2
]
,
we consider that the input image
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{LR}}
is given with a companion reference HR image
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{ref}}
such that both HR images
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{ref}}
and the unknown
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{HR}}
are supposed to be realizations of the same texture model.
More precisely, in our context based on the ADSN model, Algorithm
1
will be called with input an LR image
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{LR}}
to be upscaled and the texton

{{\boldsymbol{t}}}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}({{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{ref}}-m\mathbf{1}_{{\Omega_{M,N}}})

associated with the reference image
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{ref}}
, meaning that
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{HR}}
is supposed to be a realization of the
\ADSN({{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\mathrm{ref}})
law.
This makes our routine a stochastic super-resolution algorithm with reference image.

Figure
4
presents illustrative results that show the practicality of this reference-based approach when the reference images are HR images of another part of the same material.
Figure
4
also displays the kriging and innovation components and one can observe that the ADSN model from the reference image is good enough to compute an adapted kriging component.

4.2
Comparison with other methods

Contrary to neural networks trained on a very large dataset of generic images, our method only works on the restricted area of Gaussian microtextures. This limited scope is a main drawback of Gaussian SR that cannot work on more generic images. Moreover, our method necessitates a HR reference image.
Still it is interesting to compare our method with existing super-resolution algorithms, although one should keep in mind that these methods are designed to be used on a broader range of images.

We compare our method with other super-resolution methods on two microtextures in Figure

,
6
and Table

.

5
Limitations and extensions

As shown in the previous section, when applied to Gaussian textures our Gaussian SR algorithm outperforms state-of-the-art methods regarding results quality and execution time.
However, Gaussian SR has several inherent limitations that we now highlight.

5.1
Limited scope

Gaussian microtextures represent a limited scope of images. The images are supposed to be stationary with no geometric structures, excluding a lot of textures such as the brick wall in Figure

.
When applying our Gaussian SR algorithm to such a structured texture,
one can observe that the independent stationary stochastic innovation component is not aligned with the brick lines recovered in the kriging component. Also, textures with too big individual objects are unadapted to the Gaussian microtexture assumption, such as the bark texture in the second row of Figure

. The kriging component is attached to the LR data but the innovation component provides a stationary grainy texture that is not in accordance with the piecewise flat nature of the texture. This is a well-known limitation of the ADSN model

6
Conclusion

This work provides an efficient sampling algorithm for solutions of the stochastic SR problem for Gaussian microtextures.
Our solution is exact for grayscale images and a we propose a fast and reliable approximation for RGB images using a well-known mathematical kriging reasoning.
Gaussian SR is a fast and qualitatively better than involved generic methods when applied to Gaussian microtextures accompanied with a reference HR image.
Besides, of a more general interest, our comparative study shows that LPIPS is a pertinent metric to evaluate stochastic SR results that do not suffer from excessive blur.
While our Gaussian SR has inherent limitations discussed in the paper,
it could be of interest for fast and reliable restoration of HR stationary microtextures, even in the presence of motion blur.
