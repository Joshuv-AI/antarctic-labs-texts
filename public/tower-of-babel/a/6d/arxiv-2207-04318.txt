Determinant Maximization via Matroid Intersection Algorithms
Adam Brown, Aditi Laddha, Madhusudhan Pittu, Mohit Singh, Prasad Tetali

Abstract
Determinant maximization problem gives a general framework that models problems arising in as diverse fields as statistics [ Puk06 ] , convex geometry [ Kha96 ] , fair allocations [ AGSS16 ] , combinatorics [ AGV18 ] , spectral graph theory [ NST19a ] , network design, and random processes [ KT12 ] . In an instance of a determinant maximization problem, we are given a collection of vectors U = { v 1 , … , v n } ⊂ ℝ d U=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , and a goal is to pick a subset S ⊆ U S\subseteq U of given vectors to maximize the determinant of the matrix ∑ i ∈ S v i ​ v i ⊤ \sum_{i\in S}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top} . Often, the set S S of picked vectors must satisfy additional combinatorial constraints such as cardinality constraint ( | S | ≤ k ) \left(|S|\leq k\right) or matroid constraint ( S S is a basis of a matroid defined on the vectors). In this paper, we give a polynomial-time deterministic algorithm that returns a r O ⁡ ( r ) r^{O(r)} -approximation for any matroid of rank r ≤ d r\leq d . This improves previous results that give e O ⁡ ( r 2 ) e^{O(r^{2})} -approximation algorithms relying on e O ⁡ ( r ) e^{O(r)} -approximate estimation algorithms [ NS16 , AG17 , AGV18 , MNST20 ] for any r ≤ d r\leq d . All previous results use convex relaxations and their relationship to stable polynomials and strongly log-concave polynomials. In contrast, our algorithm builds on combinatorial algorithms for matroid intersection, which iteratively improve any solution by finding an alternating negative cycle in the exchange graph defined by the matroids. While the det ( . ) \det(.) function is not linear, we show that taking appropriate linear approximations at each iteration suffice to give the improved approximation algorithm.

1.1
Our Results and Contributions

In this work, we introduce new combinatorial methods for determinant maximization under a matroid constraint and give an
O(d^{O(d)})
-deterministic approximation algorithm. While previous works have used a convex programming approach and the theory of stable polynomials, our approach builds on the classical matroid intersection algorithm. Our first result focuses on the case when the rank of the matroid is exactly
d
, i.e., the output solution will contain precisely
d
vectors.

Theorem 1

There is a polynomial time algorithm which, given a collection of vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a matroid
\mathcal{M}=([n],\mathcal{I})
of rank
d

S\in\mathcal{I}
such that

\det\left(\sum_{i\in S}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{d^{O(d)}}\right)\max_{S^{*}\in\mathcal{I}}\det\left(\sum_{i\in S^{*}}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right).

Our results improve the
e^{O(d^{2})}
-approximation algorithm which relies on the
e^{O(d)}
-estimation algorithm
[
AGV18
,
AG17
,
MNST20
]
. Our algorithm builds on the matroid intersection algorithm and is an iterative algorithm that starts at any feasible solution and improves the objective in each step. To maintain feasibility in the matroid constraint, each step of the algorithm is an exchange of multiple elements as found by an alternating cycle of an appropriately defined exchange graph.

Technical Overview.
For intuition, let
\vol(S)
denote the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the vectors in
S
. Then
\vol(S)^{2}=\det\left(\sum_{i\in S}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)
, for any
S\subseteq U
with
|S|=d
, so we can think of
\vol(S)
as an equivalent objective function. First, observe that the feasibility problem of checking whether there is a set
S\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\vol(S)>0
can be reduced to matroid intersection. Indeed, the feasibility problem is equivalent to checking if there is a common basis of the matroid
\mathcal{M}
and the linear matroid defined by the vectors
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. Since we aim to maximize
\vol(S)
over all independent sets
S
, a natural approach is to use the weighted matroid intersection algorithm. Unfortunately, our weights are not linear, i.e.,
\vol(S)
does not equal
\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}
or log-linear
\prod_{i\in S}w_{i}
for some weights
w
on the vectors. Nonetheless, the matroid intersection algorithm forms the backbone of our approach.

Overview of Matroid Intersection.

Before we describe our algorithm, let us review a classical algorithm to find a maximum weight common basis of two matroids. Given
U=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, a weight function
w:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and two matroids
\mathcal{M}_{1}=(U,\mathcal{I}_{1})
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}=(U,\mathcal{I}_{2})
, the goal is to find a common basis
S
of maximum weight
w(S):=\sum_{e\in S}w_{e}
. We assume that there exists a common basis of the two matroids. Consider the following simple algorithm that also introduces some of the basic ingredients necessary for our algorithm. The algorithm will take as an input a common basis
S
and either certify that
S
is a maximum weight common basis or return a new common basis
\widehat{S}
of higher weight. To explain the algorithm, we recall the important concept of the
exchange graph
. Given the set
S
, we construct a directed bipartite graph
G(S)
with bipartitions given by
U\setminus S
and
S
. For any
u\in U\setminus S
and
v\in S
,
G(S)
contains an arc from
u
to
v
if
S-v+u
is a basis in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
and an arc from
v
to
u
if
S-v+u
is a basis in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
. For convenience, we use
S-v+u
to refer to the set
(S\cup\{u\})\setminus\{v\}
. Moreover, give each vertex
u\in U\setminus S
a weight
-w_{u}
and each vertex
v\in S
a weight of
w_{v}
. A nice fact from matroid theory is that
S
is a maximum weight basis if and only if there is no negative weight cycle in this directed graph (Chapter 41, Theorem 41.5
[
Sch03
]
). Moreover, if
C
is a directed negative weight cycle with minimum hops
2
2

2

Hops here refers to the number of arcs of the cycle.
, then
S\Delta C
forms a common basis of the two matroids whose weight is strictly larger than the weight of
S
. Thus, the algorithm finds a maximum weight basis by iteratively finding a negative weight cycle in such an exchange graph.

With the above algorithm as a guiding tool, we describe our algorithm. The two matroids are precisely the constraint matroid
\mathcal{M}
and the linear matroid defined over the vectors. A first challenge is that the objective function
\vol(S)^{2}=\det\left(\sum_{i\in S}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}\right)
is not linear. Thus it is not possible to define the vertex weights as was done above. But a natural function to work with instead is the function
\log\vol(S)
, which is known to be submodular. While we do not use submodularity explicitly, our algorithm takes linear approximations of this function at each iteration while searching for improvements as in the matroid intersection algorithm. We use the geometric relationship between
\vol
and
\det
closely. The first new ingredient in our algorithm is to introduce
arc
weights rather than vertex weights in the exchange graph
G(S)
. Indeed for the forward arcs
(u,v)
for
u\not\in S
and
v\in S
that correspond to the linear matroid, we introduce a weight of
-\log\frac{\vol(S-v+u)}{\vol(S)}
. We also introduce a weight of
0
for the backward arcs, which correspond to the arcs for the constraint matroid
\mathcal{M}
. The crucial observation is the following interpretation of the weight
\log\frac{\vol(S-v+u)}{\vol(S)}
: write the vector
u\not\in S
in the basis
S
, i.e.
u=\sum_{v\in S}a_{uv}v
, for some
a_{uv}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
v\in S
. Then
\frac{\vol(S-v+u)}{\vol(S)}=|a_{uv}|
(See Lemma
3
). Such relationships between the ratio of volumes and coefficients in expressing the vectors in basis given by
S
play an important role.

Our first lemma shows that if the volume of the current solution is
much
smaller than the optimal solution, then there must be a cycle such that the sum of weights of the arcs on the cycle is significantly negative.

Lemma 1
(Determinant to Cycle)

Let
S
be a basis of
\mathcal{M}
and
OPT
be the basis of
\mathcal{M}
maximizing
\vol(OPT)
. If
\vol(OPT)\geq e^{5d\log d}\cdot\vol(S)
, then there exists a directed cycle
C
of
2\ell
hops for some
\ell>0
in the exchange graph
G(S)
such that

\prod_{(u,v)\in C,u\notin S,v\in S}|a_{uv}|\geq 2(\ell!)^{3}=:f(\ell).

We call such a cycle an
f
-violating cycle. Observe that such a cycle can be found as a negative weight
2\ell
-hop cycle when weights are updated to
w_{\ell}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\ell}\log f(\ell)-\log|a_{uv}|
for a forward arc
(u,v)
where
u\not\in S
and
v\in S
. The lemma relies on the following observation. Abusing notation slightly, let
T
and
S
be matrices whose columns are the vectors in
OPT
and
S
, respectively. Writing each vector in
OPT
in the basis given by
S
we obtain
T=SA^{\top}
for some matrix
A
. The condition in the lemma implies that
\det(A)\geq e^{5d\log d}
. Also observe that the weight of any
(u,v)
where
u\in OPT
and
v\in S
is exactly
-\log|a_{uv}|
where
a_{uv}
is the
(u,v)^{\text{th}}
-entry in
A
. Combining these facts, we can show there exists a cycle satisfying the conditions of the lemma.

The next step in the algorithm is to find an
f
-violating cycle
C
and then update the solution to
T=S\Delta C
. Again, we relate the change in objective
\vol(T)
to the coefficients. While the entries
|a_{uv}|
of the cycle are large, the objective of the new solution
T
depends not only on the weight of the edges of the cycle but the weight of all arcs between all vertices in
C\setminus S
and
C\cap S
. Indeed, consider a square matrix
B
with rows and columns indexed by
C\setminus S
and
S\cap C
respectively with entry
(u,v)
as
a_{uv}
. Recall
a_{uv}
is the coefficient of vector
v
when
u
is expressed in basis
S
. Then
\vol(T)=|\det(B)|\cdot\vol(S)
(Lemma
6
). Thus it remains to lower bound the determinant of
B
. The entries on the diagonal of the matrix
B
exactly correspond to entries that define the weights of the forward arcs on the cycle
C
. Thus Lemma
1
implies that the product of the diagonal entries of
B
is large. In the next lemma, we show that if the cycle
C
is the
minimum hop

f
-violating cycle, then we can in fact lower bound the determinant of the matrix.

Lemma 2
(Cycle to Determinant)

If
C
is a minimum hop
f
-violating cycle in the exchange graph
G(S)
, then
\vol(S\Delta C)\geq 2\cdot\vol(S)
. Moreover,
S\Delta C
is also a basis of
\mathcal{M}
.

This lemma crucially uses the fact that
C
is a minimum hop
f
-violating cycle as in the case for matroid intersection algorithms. Indeed, off-diagonal entries of the matrix
B
correspond to arcs that form chords of the cycle
C
. The minimality of
C
allows us to show upper bounds on all the off-diagonal entries of the matrix
B
. A technical calculation then allows us to lower bound the determinant.

Determinant Maximization under Matroid Constraints.
As mentioned earlier, determinant maximization under a matroid constraint is considerably challenging and the bounds also depend on the rank

-estimation algorithms when

,

]

A special case of the determinant maximization problem is the Nash Social Welfare problem

While we focus on the determinant objective, the problem is also interesting when considering other spectral objectives including minimizing the trace or the maximum eigenvalue of the

We first show the algorithm and the analysis for a partition matroid with rank

Consider a partition matroid

The following theorem is a specialization of Theorem

Theorem 3

Given a partition matroid
\mathcal{M}
with

be the optimal solution to the determinant maximization problem on

We begin by formally defining the exchange graph, the different weight functions, and then the algorithm which helps establish Theorem

(Exchange Graph)

Formally, for a subset of vectors

The weight of a cycle

\displaystyle|b_{i,j}(i)-b_{i,j}(1)|

\displaystyle<\frac{1.5}{0.816}\sum_{s=1}^{i-1}\frac{f(\ell-j+s)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\binom{i}{s}\cdot f(i-s).

(14)

Note that
\frac{f(\ell-j+s)\cdot f(i-s)}{f(\ell-j+i)}=\frac{2((\ell-j+s)!)^{3}\cdot((i-s)!)^{3}}{((\ell-j+i)!)^{3}}=2\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\binom{\ell-j+i}{i-s}}\right)^{3}
. For any

\frac{1}{\binom{\ell-j+i}{i-s}}\leq\frac{1}{\ell-j+i}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{f(\ell-j+i-s)\cdot f(s)}{f(\ell-j+i)}\leq\frac{1}{\binom{\ell-j+i}{i-s}}\cdot\frac{2}{(\ell-j+i)^{2}}\,.

Plugging this in (

\displaystyle|b_{i,j}(i)-b_{i,j}(1)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f(\ell-j+i)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\frac{3.68}{(\ell-j+i)^{2}}\cdot\left(\sum_{s=1}^{i-1}\binom{i}{s}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{\ell-j+i}{i-s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\ell-j+i)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\frac{3.68\cdot i!(\ell-j)!}{(\ell-j+i)^{2}\cdot(\ell-j+i)!}\cdot\sum_{s=1}^{i-1}\binom{\ell-j+s}{\ell-j}\,.

Using (
10
) again, we get
\sum_{s=1}^{i-1}\binom{\ell-j+s}{\ell-j}\leq\binom{\ell-j+i}{\ell-j+1}
and this gives

\displaystyle|b_{i,j}(i)-b_{i,j}(i)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f(\ell-j+i)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\frac{3.68\cdot i!(\ell-j)!}{(\ell-j+i)^{2}\cdot(\ell-j+i)!}\cdot\binom{\ell-j+i}{\ell-j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\ell-j+i)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\frac{3.68\cdot i}{(\ell-j+i)^{2}\cdot(\ell-j+1)}\,.

(15)

The function
\frac{i}{(\ell-j+i)^{2}}
is maximized at

. So for any

, we have

\displaystyle|b_{i,j}(i)-b_{i,j}(1)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f(\ell-j+i)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\frac{3.68}{4(\ell-j)\cdot(\ell-j+1)}\leq 0.5\cdot\frac{f(\ell-j+i)}{f(\ell)}.

Using the fact that
|b_{i,j}(1)|\leq f(\ell-j+i)/f(\ell)
, we have
|b_{i,j}(i)|\leq 1.5\cdot f(\ell-j+i)/f(\ell)
for
i<j<\ell
.

For
j=\ell
and
i\geq 2
, equation (

) gives

\displaystyle|b_{i,\ell}(i)-b_{i,\ell}(1)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f(i)}{f(\ell)}\cdot\frac{3.68}{i}\leq 1.84\cdot\frac{f(i)}{f(\ell)},

and therefore
|b_{i,\ell}(i)|\leq 2.84\cdot\frac{f(i)}{f(\ell)}
.
This concludes the proof of parts
2
and
3
.

For
i=j
and
j<\ell
, using (
11
), we get

\displaystyle|b_{j,j}(j)-1|\leq\frac{1.84\cdot j}{\ell^{2}(\ell-j+1)}\leq\frac{1.84\cdot(\ell-1)}{2\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{0.92}{\ell}.

For

\displaystyle|b_{\ell,\ell}(\ell)-b_{\ell,\ell}(1)|

\displaystyle<\frac{2.84}{1-0.92/\ell}\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}\binom{\ell}{s}\cdot f(\ell-s)\cdot\frac{f(s)}{f(\ell)}\,.

Following the proof outline of equation (

Since

Update Step for General Matroids

Consider the case when
\mathcal{M}=([n],\mathcal{I})
is a general matroid of rank
d
. When we exchange on a cycle
C
and update
S\leftarrow S\Delta C
, the resulting set is guaranteed to be independent in the linear matroid because of the determinant bounds in Lemma
7
, but it is not clear that it would be independent in the general constraint matroid,
\mathcal{M}
, when
\mathcal{M}
is not a partition matroid. However, by exchanging on a minimal
f
-violating cycle in our algorithm, we can make the same guarantee.

In this section, we prove the existence of an
f
-violating cycle for any matroid
\mathcal{M}
with rank
d
when the current basis
S
is sufficiently smaller in volume than the optimal solution
OPT
. We also prove that exchanging on a minimal
f
-violating cycle preserves independence in
\mathcal{M}
.

Theorem 4

For any basis
S
with
|S|=d
and
\vol(S)>0
, if
\vol(S)<\vol(OPT)\cdot e^{-5d\log(d)}
, then there exists an
f
-violating cycle in
G(S)
.

Proof
Since
S
and
OPT
are independent and
|S|=|OPT|
, there exists a
perfect matching between
OPT\backslash S
and
S\backslash OPT
using the backward arcs in
G(S)
(Chapter 39, Corollary 39.12a,
[
Sch03
]
). Let
X=OPT\backslash S
,
Y=S\backslash OPT
, and
|X|=|Y|=k
. Without loss of generality, let
Y=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
and
X=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
such that
(v_{i}\rightarrow u_{i})
is an arc in
G(S)
for all
i\in[k]
.

Let
T
and
S
be matrices whose columns are the vectors in
OPT
and
S
, respectively. Let
A
be the coefficient matrix of
T
w.r.t.
S
, i.e.,

T=SA^{\top}
. Then
A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{k}&A^{\prime}\\
0&I_{d-k}\end{bmatrix}
, where
A_{k}
is the sub-matrix of
A
corresponding to rows in
X
and columns in
Y
. Then by the same proof as in
5
, there exists a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}|a_{i\sigma(i)}|>|\det(A)|\cdot e^{-k\log(k)}\geq e^{4k\log(k)}\,.

(16)

Let the cycle decomposition of
\sigma
be
\sigma=\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{\ell}\}
where
C_{i}=(i_{1}\rightarrow i_{2}\rightarrow\ldots i_{j}\rightarrow i_{1})
. Since there is an edge from
v_{\sigma(j)}
to
u_{\sigma(j)}
for all

, every cyclic permutation

corresponds to a cycle
(u_{i_{1}}\rightarrow v_{i_{2}}\rightarrow u_{i_{2}}\rightarrow v_{i_{3}}\ldots\rightarrow u_{i_{j}}\rightarrow v_{i_{1}}\rightarrow u_{i_{1}})
in

. We claim that at least one of these cycles is an

\prod_{i\in C_{j}}|a_{i\sigma(i)}|\leq 2(|C_{j}|!)^{3}
for all

. Multiplying over all the cycles in

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}|a_{i\sigma(i)}|=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\prod_{i\in C_{j}}|a_{i\sigma(i)}|\leq\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}2(|C_{j}|!)^{3}<e^{4k\log(k)},

where the last inequality follows from

. This contradicts (
16
), so

must contain an
f
-violating cycle.

\Box

Lemma 9

If
C
is a minimal
f
-violating cycle in
G(S)
, then
S\Delta C
is independent in
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof
For clarity, let
V(C)
denote the vertex set of
C
. Let
T:=S\Delta V(C)
and let
|C|=2\ell
. Lets consider the graph
G(S)
with weights
w_{\ell}
, and define
w_{\ell}(D):=\sum_{e\in D}w_{\ell}(e)
for any cycle
D
. Since
C
is an
f
-violating cycle,
w_{\ell}(C)=w_{0}(C)+\log(f(\ell))<0
.

Let the set of backward arcs in
C
be
N_{1}
, and the set of forward arcs be
N_{2}
.
For the sake of contradiction, assume that
T\notin\mathcal{I}
. Then, there exists a matching
N_{1}^{\prime}
on
V(C)
consisting of only backward arcs such that
N_{1}\neq N_{1}^{\prime}
(Chapter 39, Theorem 39.13,
[
Sch03
]
). Let
A
be a multiset of arcs consisting of all arcs in
N_{2}
twice and all arcs
N_{1}
and
N_{1}^{\prime}
(with arcs in
N_{1}\cap N_{1}^{\prime}
appearing twice). Consider the directed
graph
D=(V(C),A)
. Since
N_{1}\neq N_{1}^{\prime}
,
D
contains
a directed circuit
C_{1}
with
V(C_{1})\subsetneq V(C)
. Every vertex in
V(C)
has exactly two in-edges and two out-edges in
A
. Therefore,
D
is Eulerian, and we can decompose
A
into directed circuits
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
. Since only arcs in
N_{2}
have non-zero weights, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{\ell}(C_{i})=2w_{\ell}(C)
.

Because
V(C_{1})\subsetneq V(C)
, at most one cycle
C_{j}
can have
V(C_{j})=V(C)
. If for some
j
,
V(C)=V(C_{j})
, then
w_{\ell}(C_{j})=w_{\ell}(C)
as
C_{j}
must contain every edge in
N_{2}
. So,
\sum_{i\neq j}w_{\ell}(C_{i})=w_{\ell}(C)<0
and there exists a cycle
C_{i}
such that
V(C_{i})\subsetneq V(C)
and
w_{\ell}(C_{i})<0
. Otherwise
V(C_{j})\subsetneq V(C^{\prime})
for all
j
and
\sum_{i}w_{\ell}(C_{i})=2w_{\ell}(C)<0
. Again, there exists a cycle
C_{i}
such that
V(C_{i})\subsetneq V(C)
and
w_{\ell}(C_{i})<0
.

Let
C^{\prime}
be the directed cycle such that
V(C^{\prime})\subsetneq V(C)
and
w_{\ell}(C^{\prime})\leq w_{\ell}(C)\leq 0
. Define
y:=|C^{\prime}|/2
. Thus
w_{\ell}(C^{\prime})=y\cdot\log(f(\ell))/\ell+w_{0}(C^{\prime})<0
. Since
y<\ell
,
\log(f(y))/y\leq\log(f(\ell))/\ell
. Therefore
w_{0}(C^{\prime})\leq-y\cdot\log(f(\ell))/\ell\leq-\log(f(y))
.
So
C^{\prime}
is an
f
-violating cycle with
V(C^{\prime})\subset V(C)
, which contradicts the fact that
C
is a minimal
f
-violating cycle.

\Box

2
. Consider a matroid
\mathcal{M}=([n],\mathcal{I})
with rank

. Starting with a basis

with non-zero volume, we will use a slight modification of Algorithm
2
to iteratively find a basis with strictly larger volume. However since the set

is not full dimensional, our edge weight functions will be different.

Let
S=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
be a basis of
\mathcal{M}
with
\vol(S)>0
. We can write any vector

in
\mathcal{M}

The change in volume on replacing some

Before we prove the existence of an

For convenience, to specify the volume of a set of vectors

\vol(\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}\})

\vol(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r})

Observation 3

For any set of vectors
\{v_{1}^{a},v_{1}^{b},v_{2},\dots,v_{r}\}
,

\displaystyle\vol(v_{1}^{a}+v_{1}^{b},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})

\displaystyle\leq\vol(v_{1}^{a},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})+\vol(v_{1}^{b},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})\,,

\displaystyle\vol(v_{1}^{a}+v_{1}^{b},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})

\displaystyle\geq\vol(v_{1}^{a},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})-\vol(v_{1}^{b},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})\,.

Proof
Let
P^{\perp}
be the projection matrix orthogonal to
\Span(v_{2},\dots,v_{r})
. By triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert P^{\perp}(v_{1}^{a}+v_{1}^{b})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert P^{\perp}v_{1}^{a}\right\rVert+\left\lVert P^{\perp}v_{1}^{b}\right\rVert,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left\lVert P^{\perp}(v_{1}^{a}+v_{1}^{b})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert P^{\perp}v_{1}^{a}\right\rVert-\left\lVert P^{\perp}v_{1}^{b}\right\rVert.

Since
\vol(v_{1}^{a},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})=\left\lVert P^{\perp}v_{1}^{a}\right\rVert\cdot\vol(v_{2},\dots,v_{r})
, multiplying both sides by
\vol(v_{2},\dots,v_{r})
gives us the required inequalities.

\Box

Observation 4

If
v_{i}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal projection of
v_{i}
onto
\Span(S-v_{i})

\displaystyle\vol(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\cdot\prod_{i=k+1}^{r}\left\lVert v_{i}^{\perp}\right\rVert\leq\vol(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})

for any
k\in[r]
.

Proof
Let
S_{i}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
for
i\in[r]
and
S_{0}=\emptyset
. Since
\vol(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\vol(S_{i})}{\vol(S_{i-1})}
where

, it suffices to prove that
\frac{\vol(S_{i})}{\vol(S_{i-1})}\geq\left\lVert v_{i}^{\perp}\right\rVert=\frac{\vol(S)}{\vol(S\backslash\{v_{i}\})}
which follows from the submodularity of
\log\vol(\cdot)
as
S_{i-1}\subseteq S\backslash\{v_{i}\}
.

\Box

Lemma 12

(18)

is the derangement number defined in Remark

.

Since all permutations in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\backslash\{id_{\ell}\}
have at most
\ell-2
fixed points and at least
1
exceedance, we can further expand (
40
) as

\perm(B_{\ell})=1+\sum_{n=2}^{\ell}\;\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\,.

(41)

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)
,

\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}=\prod_{i>\sigma(i)}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\cdot\prod_{i<\sigma(i)}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq\prod_{i>\sigma(i)}2\tilde{f}(i-\sigma(i))\cdot\prod_{i<\sigma(i)}\frac{2\tilde{f}(\ell-\sigma(i)+i)}{\tilde{f}(\ell)}\,.

where the last inequality follows from the hypothesis of the Lemma.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}i-\sigma(i)=0
for any permutation
\sigma
,
\sum_{i>\sigma(i)}i-\sigma(i)+\sum_{i<\sigma(i)}\ell-\sigma(i)+i=\ell\cdot|\{i:\sigma(i)>i\}|=\ell\cdot k
for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)
. Therefore, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)
, then there exist integers
1\leq x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\leq n-1
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=\ell\cdot k
, such that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq 2^{n}\cdot\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{f}(x_{i})}{\tilde{f}(\ell)^{k}}\,.

Since
\tilde{f}
satisfies
\tilde{f}(a+b)\geq\tilde{f}(a)\cdot\tilde{f}(b)
, under the constraints on
x_{i}
’s, for any
k\geq n/2
,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq 2^{n}\cdot\frac{\tilde{f}(\ell-1)^{k}\cdot\tilde{f}(2k-n+1)\cdot\tilde{f}(1)^{n-k-1}}{\tilde{f}(\ell)^{k}}\,,

and for
k<n/2
,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq 2^{n}\cdot\frac{\tilde{f}(\ell-1)^{k-1}\cdot\tilde{f}(\ell-n+2k-1)\cdot\tilde{f}(1)^{n-k}}{\tilde{f}(\ell)^{k}}\,.

Using the definition of
\tilde{f}
, for any
k\geq n/2
,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq 2^{2n-k-1}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{11}}{\ell^{11k}}\,,

(42)

and for
k<n/2
,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq 2^{2n-k}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{11(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{11}}\,.

(43)

For some
k
and
n
with
k<n/2
, summing over all permutations in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq\binom{\ell}{n}\cdot T(n,k)\cdot 2^{2n-k}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{11(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{11}}\,.

(44)

We will bound the three terms of equation (
44
), namely
\binom{\ell}{n}
,
T(n,k)
, and
2^{2n-k}
separately.

Expanding the first term, we get

\displaystyle\binom{\ell}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))}=\frac{\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+1)}{n!\cdot\ell^{(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))}<\frac{1}{n!\cdot\ell^{k-1}}\,.

(45)

For the third term, note that
k<n/2
implies that
2^{2n-k}<2^{3n-3k}
, and since
k\geq 1
,
\ell-n+2k>2
. Using these two facts, we get

2^{2n-k}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{3(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{3}}<\frac{2^{3(n-k)}}{(\ell-n+2k)^{3(n-k)}}<1\,.

(46)

Plugging (
45
) and (
46
) in (
44
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq\frac{1}{n!\cdot\ell^{k-1}}\cdot T(n,k)\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{7(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{7}}\,.

(47)

For
k=1
,
T(n,k)=1
and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{1}{(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2))^{7}}\,.

(48)

For any
2\leq k<n/2
, using Lemma
19
, we have
T(n,k)\leq(2k+3)^{n}
. Since
k\geq 2
,
2k+3\leq 2\cdot 2k
, and therefore
T(n,k)\leq(2\cdot 2k)^{n+2}
. Plugging this is (
47
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!\cdot\ell^{k-1}}\cdot(2k)^{n}\cdot 2^{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{7(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{7}}\,.

(49)

Moreover
k<n/2
implies that
n-k>n/2
, and as a result
n\leq 2(n-k)
and
2^{n}\cdot(2k)^{n}\leq 2^{2(n-k)}\cdot(2k)^{2(n-k)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle 2^{n}\cdot(2k)^{n}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{4(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{4}}

\displaystyle<2^{2(n-k)}\cdot(2k)^{2(n-k)}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{4(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{4}}

\displaystyle<\frac{(2)^{2(n-k)}}{(\ell-n+2k)^{2(n-k)}}\cdot\frac{(2k)^{2(n-k)}}{(\ell-n+2k)^{2(n-k)}}<1,

(50)

where the last inequality follows from
2k\leq\ell-n+2k
and
2\leq\ell-n+2k
.

Combining (
49
) and (
50
), we have for any
2\leq k<n/2
,

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq\frac{1}{n!\cdot\ell^{k-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{3(k-1)}\cdot(\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+2k))^{3}}\,.

(51)

For a fixed
k
and
n
with
k\geq n/2
, summing over all permutations in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\binom{\ell}{n}\cdot T(n,k)\cdot 2^{2n-k-1}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{11}}{\ell^{11k}}\,.

(52)

We will again bound the three terms, namely
\binom{\ell}{n}
,
T(n,k)
, and
2^{2n-k-1}
separately.

For the first term, since
n/2\leq k
,
2n-k-1\leq 3k-1
, and therefore

\displaystyle 2^{2n-k-1}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{3}}{\ell^{3k}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{3k-1}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{3}}{\ell^{3k}}\,.

Since
2k-n+1\leq k
, and
k+1\leq\ell
,

\displaystyle 2^{3k-1}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{3}}{\ell^{3k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(2^{k}\,k!)^{3}}{(k+1)^{3k}}\,.

For
k=1,2,3,4,5
,
\frac{(2^{k}k!)^{3}}{(k+1)^{3k}}\leq 1
. For
k\geq 6
,
2^{k}k!\leq k^{k}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle 2^{2n-k-1}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{3}}{\ell^{3k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

(53)

Expanding the third term,

\displaystyle\binom{\ell}{n}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{2}}{\ell^{2k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell\cdot(\ell-1)\ldots(\ell-n+1)}{n!}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{2}}{\ell^{2k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{2}}{\ell^{2k-n}}=\frac{1}{\ell(n-1)!}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{2}}{n\ell^{2k-n-1}}\leq\frac{(2k-n+1)!}{\ell(n-1)!}\,.

Since
k+1\leq n
, we have
2k-n+1\leq n-1
, and therefore

\displaystyle\binom{\ell}{n}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{2}}{\ell^{2k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2k-n+1)!}{l(n-1)!}\leq\frac{1}{\ell}\,.

(54)

Plugging in (
53
) and (
54
) in (

),

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\ell}\cdot T(n,k)\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}\,.

(55)

Since
T(n,n-1)=1
, for
k=n-1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\ell}\cdot\frac{((n-1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6(n-1)}}\,.

(56)

By Lemma
19
, for
k<n-1
,
T(n,k)\leq(2n-2k+5)^{n}
for

, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\ell}\cdot(2n-2k+5)^{n}\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}\,.

Let

, then

\displaystyle(2n-2k+5)^{n}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}=(2k-2z+5)^{2k-z}\cdot\frac{((z+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}

\displaystyle\leq(2k-z+5)^{2k-z}\cdot\frac{((z+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}\,.

Taking derivative of
\frac{(2k-z+7)^{2k-z+2}}{\ell^{6k}}
with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{(2k-z+5)^{2k-z}}{\ell^{6k}}\left(2\cdot\log(2k-z+5)+2\cdot\frac{2k-z}{2k-z+5}-6\log(\ell)\right)\leq 0\,.

Therefore
\frac{(2k-z+5)^{2k-z+2}}{\ell^{6k}}
is a non-increasing function of

. Since
n=2k-z\geq 1

2k\geq z+1

\frac{(2k-z+5)^{2k-z}}{\ell^{6k}}

\displaystyle(2k-z+5)^{2k-z}\cdot\frac{((z+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}

\displaystyle\leq 6\cdot\frac{((z+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}=6\cdot\frac{((2k-n+1)!)^{6}}{\ell^{6k}}\leq 6\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{6(n-k-1)}}\,,

where the last inequality follows from

.
Plugging this bound in (

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\ell}\cdot\frac{6}{\ell^{6(n-k-1)}}\,.

(57)

Plugging in (

), (
51
), (
56
), and (
57
) into (

),

\displaystyle\perm(B_{\ell})=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n=2}^{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n=2}^{\ell}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,1)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}+\sum_{n=5}^{\ell}\sum_{2\leq k<n/2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=2}^{\ell}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,n-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}+\sum_{n=2}^{\ell}\sum_{k=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n-2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\sigma(i)}

Remark 1
(Exceedances and the Eulerian Number)

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
,

•

The exceedance of
\sigma
is defined as
|\{i\in[n-1]:\sigma(i)>i\}|
.

•

The Eulerian number
E(n,k)
is defined to be the number of permutations in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
with
k-1
exceedances.

•

The derangement number
T(n,k)
is defined to be the number of derangements in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
with
k
exceedances.

The explicit formula for
E(n,k)
is
E(n,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{j}{\binom{n+1}{j}}\cdot(k+1-j)^{n}
(page 273,
[
Com74
]
). The exponential generating function of
E(n,k)
is given by (page 273,
[
Com74
]
)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}E(n,k)\;t^{k}\;\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{t-1}{t-e^{(t-1)x}}\,.

(58)

The exponential generating function of
T(n,k)
is given by (Proposition 5,
[
Bre90
]
)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}T(n,k)\;t^{k}\;\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=e^{-t}\cdot\frac{t-1}{t-e^{(t-1)x}}\,.

(59)

Comparing (
58
) and (
59
), we infer

T(n,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{(n-j)}\cdot\binom{n}{j}\cdot E(j,k)\,.

(60)

Lemma 19

For any positive integer
n
,

•

T(n,1)=T(n,n-1)=1
,

•

T(n,k)=T(n,n+1-k)<(2k+3)^{n}
for any
k\in\{2,\ldots,n-2\}
.

Proof
Since
E(n,m)=\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{k}{\binom{n+1}{k}}\cdot(m+1-k)^{n}
, taking absolute values, we get

\displaystyle E(n,m)

\displaystyle<\binom{n+1}{0}\cdot(m+1)^{n}+\binom{n+1}{2}\cdot(m+1-2)^{n}+\ldots

\displaystyle<(m+1)^{n}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{2i}\right)\leq(m+1)^{n}\cdot 2^{n}.

Using equation
60
and taking absolute values, we get

\displaystyle T(n,k)

\displaystyle<\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\cdot E(j,k)<\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\cdot 2^{j}\cdot(k+1)^{j}=(1+2(k+1))^{n}.

\Box

The following lemma is an extension to
lemma

6
.

Lemma 20

Let
S
be a subset of
r<d
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
C
be a cycle in
G(S)
, and let
X=C\backslash S
and
Y=S\backslash C
such that
|X|=|Y|=\ell
. Let
X=YA_{C}+X_{\perp}
, where
X_{\perp}
denotes the projection of vectors in
X
orthogonal to
\Span(Y)
. Then the change in objective value is given by

\frac{\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}}{\vol(S)^{2}}\geq\det(A_{C}^{\top}A_{C}).

In particular, if
C^{\prime}
is the projection of the vectors in
C
onto
\Span(S)
, then
\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}\geq\vol(S\Delta C^{\prime})^{2}
.

Proof
Let
W=S\backslash Y
. Let us abuse notation to denote by
X
the matrix whose columns are the vectors in set
X
and similarly for other sets defined above. Then let
X=YA_{C}+WA^{\prime}+Z
, where
Z
is the component of
X
which is orthogonal to
\Span(S)
. Let
Y=Y_{\perp}+Y_{W}
, where
Y_{\perp}
is the component of
Y
orthogonal to
\Span(W)
.

Let
M
denote the matrix whose columns are the elements of
S\Delta C
. Concretely,
M=[X\,\,W]
following the notation above.
Note that

\displaystyle\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}

\displaystyle=\det(M^{\top}M)=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}X^{\top}\\
W^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X&W\end{bmatrix}\right)=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}X^{\top}X&X^{\top}W\\
W^{\top}X&W^{\top}W\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(W^{\top}W\right)^{1/2}\det\left(X^{\top}X-X^{\top}W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}X\right),

(61)

where the last equation follows from the fact that
M^{\top}M
is positive definite.

Now, since
Z
is orthogonal to both
Y
and
W
, we get

X^{\top}X=(YA_{C})^{\top}(YA_{C})+(WA^{\prime})^{\top}(WA^{\prime})+Z^{\top}Z+(YA_{C})^{\top}(WA^{\prime})+(WA^{\prime})^{\top}(YA_{C}).

(62)

Additionally,
W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}
is the projection matrix on the column space of
W
, so

W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}X=Y_{W}A_{C}+WA^{\prime}\,.

(63)

Multiplying equation (
63
) with
X
gives

X^{\top}W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}X=(YA_{C})^{\top}(Y_{W}A_{C})+(YA_{C})^{\top}(WA^{\prime})+(WA^{\prime})^{\top}(Y_{W}A_{C})+(WA^{\prime})^{\top}(WA^{\prime}).

(64)

Subtracting equation (
64
) from equation (
62
), we see that

\displaystyle X^{\top}X-X^{\top}W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}X

\displaystyle=(YA_{C})^{\top}(Y-Y_{W})A_{C}+(WA^{\prime})^{\top}(Y-Y_{W})A_{C}+Z^{\top}Z

\displaystyle=(Y_{\perp}A_{C})^{\top}(Y_{\perp}A_{C})+Z^{\top}Z.

Substituting the value of
X^{\top}X-X^{\top}W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}X
from the above equation in equation (
61
), we conclude that

\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}=\det\left(W^{\top}W\right)\det\left((Y_{\perp}A_{C})^{\top}(Y_{\perp}A_{C})+Z^{\top}Z\right).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\vol(S)^{2}

\displaystyle=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}Y^{\top}\\
W^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Y&W\end{bmatrix}\right)=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}Y^{\top}Y&Y^{\top}W\\
W^{\top}Y&W^{\top}W\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(W^{\top}W\right)^{1/2}\det\left(Y^{\top}Y-Y^{\top}W(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}Y\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(W^{\top}W\right)^{1/2}\det\left(Y^{\top}Y-Y^{\top}Y_{W}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=\det\left(W^{\top}W\right)^{1/2}\det\left(Y_{\perp}^{\top}Y_{\perp}\right).

(66)

Finally, dividing equation (
64
) by equation (
13
) gives

\frac{\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}}{\vol(S)^{2}}=\frac{\det\left((Y_{\perp}A_{C})^{\top}(Y_{\perp}A_{C})+Z^{\top}Z\right)}{\det\left(Y_{\perp}^{\top}Y_{\perp}\right)}.

Since
Z^{\top}Z
is positive semidefinite, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}}{\vol(S)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\det\left((Y_{\perp}A_{C})^{\top}(Y_{\perp}A_{C})+Z^{\top}Z\right)}{\det\left(Y_{\perp}^{\top}Y_{\perp}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\det\left((Y_{\perp}A_{C})^{\top}(Y_{\perp}A_{C})\right)}{\det\left(Y_{\perp}^{\top}Y_{\perp}\right)}=\det(A_{C}^{\top}A_{C}).

If
C^{\prime}
is the projection of
C
onto
\Span(S)
, then Lemma
6
implies that
\vol(S\Delta C^{\prime})^{2}=\vol(S)^{2}\cdot\det(A_{C}^{\top}A_{C})\leq\vol(S\Delta C)^{2}
.

\Box
