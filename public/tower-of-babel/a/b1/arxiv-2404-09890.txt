Abstract
. The theta characteristics on a Riemann surface are permuted by the induced action of the automorphism group, with the orbit structure being important for the geometry of the curve and associated manifolds. We describe two new methods for advancing the understanding of these orbits, generalising existing results of Kallel & Sjerve, allowing us to establish the existence of infinitely many curves possessing a unique invariant characteristic as well as determine the number of invariant characteristics for all Hurwitz curves with simple automorphism group. In addition, we compute orbit decompositions for a substantial number of curves with genus ≤ 9 \leq 9 , allowing the identification of where current theoretical understanding falls short and the potential applications of machine learning techniques.



1.
Introduction

Given a smooth, compact, connected Riemann surface
\mathcal{C}
, a
theta characteristic
on
\mathcal{C}
is a line bundle
L\to\mathcal{C}
such that
L^{2}:=L\otimes L
is isomorphic to the canonical bundle
K_{\mathcal{C}}
. Given that
\deg K_{\mathcal{C}}=2g-2
is even, where
g=g(\mathcal{C})
is the genus of the curve, it is necessarily true that theta characteristics exist and moreover that there are exactly
2^{2g}
of them. The latter follows for, given a theta characteristic
L
and a 2-torsion degree-0 line bundle
L^{\prime}\in\Pic^{0}(\mathcal{C})[2]
, necessarily
L\otimes L^{\prime}
is also a theta characteristic. As such the set of characteristics
S(\mathcal{C})
is an affine space over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
modelled on
H^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Theta characteristics are further distinguished by their
parity
: a theta characteristic
L
is called odd/even if
\dim H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L)
is odd/even. There are exactly
2^{g-1}(2^{g}-1)
odd theta characteristics and
2^{g-1}(2^{g}+1)
even theta characteristics
[
Ati71
,
Fay73
]
. For a general review of theta characteristics and their applications, see
[
Far12
]
.

Theta characteristics are permuted by the action of the automorphism group
\Aut\mathcal{C}
, and in his landmark paper
[
Ati71
]
Atiyah showed that every
f\in\Aut(\mathcal{C})
leaves at least one characteristic invariant. Kallel and Sjerve
[
KS10
]
then greatly expanded upon this result, showing how to compute the orbits of characteristics given knowledge of the rational representation of their automorphism group, and using that to give further results about the number of characteristics invariant under a given automorphism. In §
2
-
3
we will briefly review their results, as we will then use this to give a group cohomology description of invariant characteristics. This allows us to generalise the results of
[
KS10
]
, giving the two main new results of this paper

Proposition 1.1
.

If there exists
f\in G\leq\Aut(\mathcal{C})
such that
f
has odd order,
\left\langle f\right\rangle
is subnormal in
G
, and
g(\mathcal{C}/\left\langle f\right\rangle)=0
, then
\mathcal{C}
has a unique theta characteristic invariant under the action of
G
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There are infinitely many curves, both non-hyperelliptic and hyperelliptic, with a unique invariant characteristic.

2.
Action of the Rational Representation

Choosing a basis
\left\{a_{i},b_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{g}
of the homology group
H_{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z})
with canonical intersection matrix
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{g}\\
-I_{g}&0\end{pmatrix}
, i.e.

a_{i}\circ b_{j}=\delta_{ij},\quad a_{i}\circ a_{j}=0=b_{i}\circ b_{j},

yields the
rational representation

\rho_{r}:\Aut(\mathcal{C})\to\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. The starting point for this paper is the work of
[
KS10
]
, where the authors use the identification between theta characteristic and spin structures on a Riemann surface to provide a particular choice of isomorphism
S(\mathcal{C})\cong H_{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z}_{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
such that
{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2g}
transforms under
f\in\Aut(\mathcal{C})
as

(1)

{x}\mapsto R^{T}{x}+{v}{\mod 2},

where
R=\rho_{r}(f)
and the vector
{v}
is computed as
v_{i}=\sum_{j<j^{\prime}}R_{ji}R_{j^{\prime}i}J_{jj^{\prime}}
. One can think of this action on
{x}
as matrix multiplication

\begin{pmatrix}{x}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}R^{T}&{v}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{x}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Example 2.1
.

Suppose
R=I
is the identity matrix, then

v_{i}=\sum_{j<j^{\prime}}\delta_{ji}\delta_{j^{\prime}i}J_{jj^{\prime}}=0.

As such, as a sense check we can see that any theta characteristic is invariant under the identity transformation.

This affine action is a classical result (see, for example,
[
Igu72
, §V.1]
), but one of the strengths of Kallel and Sjerve’s approach using spin structures is that the following result is not difficult to prove.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
Joh80
]
, §5)
.

Writing
{x}=({u},{v})
for
{u},{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{g}
, the parity of the associated theta characteristic is
q({x}):={u}\cdot{v}
.

As a result of Lemma
2.2
we see the parity of a spin structure is given by a quadratic form on
H_{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z}_{2})
such that the associated bilinear form
H({x},{y}):=q({x}+{y})-q({x})-q({y})
is the reduction mod 2 of the intersection pairing on
H_{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z})
.

Given explicitly the rational representation, Equation (
1
) and Lemma
2.2
provide a fast and exact method to compute the orbit decomposition of theta characteristics (as opposed to slow and numerical methods using the analytical representation and the Abel-Jacobi map, see
[
DH23

BDH22
]
), split by parity. An implementation of this method may be seen in the code from
[
BDH22
]
available at
https://github.com/DisneyHogg/Brings_Curve
, and this is how we shall compute tables of orbit decomposition in §
4
.

We shall at this stage introduce another concept which shall be used later, that of the

of the rational representation.

Definition 2.3

(

]

Take

the data of the tuple

\gamma_{1}^{c_{1}}=\dots=\gamma_{r}^{c_{r}}=\prod_{i=1}^{g_{0}}(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\beta_{i}^{-1})\prod_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{j}=1.

The Riemann-Hurwitz theorem immediately says that

(2)

2g-2=\left\lvert G\right\rvert\left[(2g_{0}-2)+\sum_{i}\left(1-\frac{1}{c_{i}}\right)\right].

The (character of the) rational representation completely determines the corresponding signature

3.
Invariant Characteristics and Group Cohomology

Suppose we had parametrised our set of theta characteristics differently in terms of
{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2g}
where
{x}={x}^{\prime}+{y}
for some fixed
{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2g}
. This is reparametrisation by translation. One can derive the corresponding action on
{x}^{\prime}
by noting

\displaystyle{x}={x}^{\prime}+{y}

\displaystyle\mapsto R^{T}({x}^{\prime}+{y})+{v},

\displaystyle=R^{T}{x}^{\prime}+\left[{v}+(R^{T}-I){y}\right]+{y},

and so

{x}^{\prime}\mapsto R^{T}{x}^{\prime}+{v}_{y},

where
{v}_{y}={v}+(R^{T}-I){y}

{y}
were fixed by the action (
1

{v}_{y}=0
and moreover the converse is true. As such, we have seen the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

There is an invariant characteristic on a curve
\mathcal{C}
if and only if the corresponding affine representation on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2g}

\begin{pmatrix}{x}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}R^{T}&{v}\\

1\end{pmatrix}

is equivalent by a translation

to the linear action

\begin{pmatrix}{x}^{\prime}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}R^{T}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{x}^{\prime}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

for some
{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2g}
.

Lemma 3.5

\displaystyle=q(x+y)-q(x)-q(y),

\displaystyle=\left[q(A(x+y))+H(A(x+y),b)\right]-\left[q(Ax)+H(Ax,b)\right]

Example 3.6

(Hyperelliptic Involution)

Suppose we just have

has a unique theta characteristic invariant under the action of

This condition captures the existence of unique invariant characteristics in the case of even-order cyclic group actions seen earlier in Example

for some odd integer

Proposition 3.10
.

Given a SOC curve when the subnormal cyclic group
\left\langle f\right\rangle
has odd order
n
and signature
(0;c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
, the parity is

n\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{c_{i}-c_{i}^{-1}}{8}\mod 2.

Proof.

Note that the
c_{i}
must be odd because they divide
n
. Hence, using
[
Ser90
, Equation 16]
we have that the parity is

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\left\lvert\operatorname{Stab}(P)\right\rvert^{2}-1}{8}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{C}/G}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{P\in\mathcal{C}}\\
{\pi(P)=Q}\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\lvert\operatorname{Stab}(P)\right\rvert^{2}-1}{8}\mod 2,

\displaystyle=n\sum_{\pi(P)\in\mathcal{C}/G}\frac{\left\lvert\operatorname{Stab}(P)\right\rvert-\left\lvert\operatorname{Stab}(P)\right\rvert^{-1}}{8}\mod 2,

\displaystyle=n\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{c_{i}-c_{i}^{-1}}{8}\mod 2.

∎

5.2.
The Method of Dolgachev

Approximately four months after constructing these machine estimates we were alerted by Vanya Cheltsov to the paper
[
Dol97b
]
, which allows one to get analytic solutions to the questions raised in Table
2
, which we shall describe now.

Denote with
\Pic(\mathcal{C})^{G}
the group of
G
-invariant isomorphism classes of line bundles on
\mathcal{C}
, that is line bundles
L\to\mathcal{C}
that admit a collection of isomorphisms
\phi_{g}:g^{\ast}L\to L
for each
g\in G
. Moreover, let
\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})
denote the group of
G
-linearised line bundles on
\mathcal{C}
and corresponding linearisations, that is
G
-invariant line bundles that admit a collection of isomorphisms such that

\forall g,h\in G,\quad\phi_{g\circ h}=\phi_{h}\circ h^{\ast}\phi_{g}.

Thinking in terms of divisors
\Div(\mathcal{C})
, principal divisors
\PDiv(\mathcal{C})
, and nonzero meromorphic functions
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})^{\times}
instead of line bundles,
\Pic(\mathcal{C})^{G}
corresponds to
(\Div(\mathcal{C})/\PDiv(\mathcal{C}))^{G}
, while
\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})
corresponds to
\Div(\mathcal{C})^{G}/[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})^{\times}]^{G}

[
Dol97b
, Corollary 2.3]
. The key results of Dolgachev are the following.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
Dol97b
]
, Proposition 2.2)
.

We have a short exact sequence of abelian groups

(6)

0\to\Hom(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})\to\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})\to\Pic(\mathcal{C})^{G}\to H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})\to 0.

Here
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
is the group cohomology viewing
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
as a trivial
G
-module, which is known as the Schur multiplier of the group
G
.

Proof.

Dolgachev proves this using the spectral sequence of
[
Gro57
, §5.2]
, but we shall give a more elementary approach here following
[
FV89
]
.

Observe that the Short Exact Sequence (SES)

0\to\PDiv(\mathcal{C})\to\Div(\mathcal{C})\to\Pic(\mathcal{C})\to 0,

gives rise to the Long Exact Sequence (LES)

0\to\PDiv(\mathcal{C})^{G}\to\Div(\mathcal{C})^{G}\to\Pic(\mathcal{C})^{G}\to H^{1}(G,\PDiv(\mathcal{C}))\to H^{1}(G,\Div(\mathcal{C})).

We can use
[
FV89
, Lemma 4.1]
to say

•

H^{1}(G,\Div(\mathcal{C}))=0
, and

•

H^{1}(G,\PDiv(\mathcal{C}))\cong H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
.

Moreover, from the SES

0\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})^{\times}\to\PDiv(\mathcal{C})\to 0,

where
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})
are the meromorphic functions on the curve, we get the portion of the LES

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})^{\times}]^{G}\to\PDiv(\mathcal{C})^{G}\to\Hom(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})\to 0,

where we have used
[
Wei95
, Exercise 6.1.6]
to equate
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})=\Hom(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
. As such, quotienting by
[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})^{\times}]^{G}
we are done.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The map
\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})\to\Pic(\mathcal{C})^{G}
sends any
G
-linearised line bundle
L
and its choice of linearisation to the isomorphism class of
L
. In this way, we think of
\Hom(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
as parametrising the possible
G
-linearisations of the trivial bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposition 5.3

(
[
Dol97b
]
)
.

Suppose that
G
acts on
\mathcal{C}
with signature
(0;c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
. Then we have

(7)

\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\left[\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r-1}\mathbb{Z}/(d_{i}/d_{i-1})\mathbb{Z}\right],

where

d_{i}=\gcd\left\{\prod_{j\in S}c_{j}\,|\,S\subset\left\{1,\dots,r\right\},\left\lvert S\right\rvert=i\right\},\quad\text{taking}\quad d_{0}=1.

The generator of the
\mathbb{Z}
factor
\gamma
has underlying
G
-invariant line bundle (isomorphism class)
\Gamma
determined by

K_{\mathcal{C}}=N\left(r-2-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{c_{i}}\right)\Gamma,

where
N=\lcm(c_{i})
.

Proof.

We shall not recreate the entire proof but note that, as it shall be helpful later, letting
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
be the branch points of the projection
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/G
, then
\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})
is generated by the
G
-invariant divisors

D_{i}=\pi^{-1}(Q_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{P\in\mathcal{C}}\\
{\pi(P)=Q_{i}}\end{subarray}}P.

These are subject to the equivalence relation
c_{i}D_{i}-c_{j}D_{j}=\pi^{\ast}(Q_{i}-Q_{j})\sim 0
. Using Smith normal form to compute the abelian invariants yields the
d_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

In the situation of Proposition
5.3
, letting
e=\left\lvert\left\{\text{even $c_{i}$}\right\}\right\rvert
,

\displaystyle\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})/2\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{Z}_{2},&e=0,\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{e},&e\neq 0.\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0,&e=0,\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{e-1},&e\neq 0.\end{array}\right..

Proof.

The first equality from writing

\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})/2\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})=\left\langle D_{i},\,i=1,\dots,r\,|\,\forall i,j,\,c_{i}D_{i}-c_{j}D_{j}=0=2D_{i}\right\rangle.

If all
c_{i}
are odd, then we may use the second relation to reduce the first relation to
D_{i}=D_{j}
, and then the cokernel has presentation
\left\langle D_{1}\,|\,2D_{1}=0\right\rangle
. If there are
c_{i}
which are even, say
c_{1},\dots,c_{e}
then they will drop out of the first relation to just give
D_{i}=0
for
i>e
and then the cokernel has presentation
\left\langle D_{i},\,i=1,\dots,e\,|\,\forall i,\,2D_{i}=0\right\rangle
.

The second follows from observing that

(8)

\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\oplus\left[\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r-1}\mathbb{Z}/(d_{i}/d_{i-1})\mathbb{Z}\right]=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}/(d_{i}/d_{i-1})\mathbb{Z}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}/c_{i}\mathbb{Z}.

The 2-torsion part of the left hand side is
\mathbb{Z}_{\gcd(2,N)}\oplus\Pic(G;\mathcal{C})_{2}
, while 2-torsion of the right hand side is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{e}
. The result follows using

\mathbb{Z}_{\gcd(2,N)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0,&e=0,\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2}&e\neq 0.\end{array}\right.

∎

The lifting order of any involution in

Proof.

6.
Outlook

In this work we’ve laid the groundwork for a more developed theory of invariant characteristics, moving beyond the work of
[
Ati71
,
KS10
,
Bea13
]
which considered only those invariant under a single automorphism. We have approached this from two separate directions: by rephrasing the question of invariant characteristics in terms of group cohomology of an affine action; and by understanding the whole group of invariant line bundles. At present, it is not clear how the two may be linked, and clarifying this will undoubtedly serve to help the development of theory.

At present, sufficient conditions for unique invariant characteristics have been found, but not any strong necessary conditions. In our current use of the group cohomology method, we have not utilised much of the theory of the rational representation, and it seems likely that better understanding this will be helpful. This is especially pressing as we provide examples through our computation of curves for which the SOC property does not explain the existence of a UIC, though the property is enough to cover the majority of curves seen.

With regards to Hurwitz curves with a simple automorphism group, though we have provided a criterion for checking whether certain generating vectors of
A_{n}
Hurwitz curves yield a UIC, it is not clear whether a general statement can be made. Further research should go into investigating what generating vectors of
A_{n}
as a Hurwitz group can occur.

For future work, one could also generalise to consider
r
-spin structures
, which are
r
th-roots of the canonical bundle. These exist when
r|2g-2
, and in principle our methods can be extended to study the orbits of these.

. Wiman distinguishes his curves by whether they lie on the nonsingular quadric or the cone, and we have followed this putting those that lie on the cone in the first portion of the table. For the curves which lie on the nonsingular quadric Wiman described the curve by providing the quadric and cubic, hence one must use the resultant to get a single plane equation, and this may require a projective linear transformation to find a nondegenerate coordinate system. We find a typo in Wiman’s curve (8) with octahedral symmetry.

•

Above genus 4 we are unaware of any complete lists classifying curves by their automorphism groups and giving plane models of the curves. Curves of genus
(d-1)(d-2)/2
for
d\geq 3
stand out because of the Plücker formula. In order to implement the numerical method for computing the rational representation it is also necessary to have a model of the curve with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
, and so this further limits the possible curves we may investigate.

•

As the LMFDB does not contain the data of signatures with quotient genus
>0
at genera
>4
, we shall sometimes omit the signature of the action where it is unknown.

•

The first non-hyperelliptic curve of genus 5 used in Table
8
is the family of Humbert curves given in
[
KR89
, (5.9)]
, the second from
[
Swi16
]
, and the third comes from taking resultants of the polynomials provided in
[
Wim95b
]
, hence one may choose to call it the Wiman octic for want of a better name.

•

[

]

[
Swi16

[
MSSV02
]

[

,
Edg81
]

The curve with automorphism group
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{8})
is the Fricke-Macbeath curve
[
Fri99
,
Mac65
]
, the unique Hurwitz curve of genus 7, the rational plane model of which is a attributed to Brock in
[
Hid17
]

[

[

[
Swi16

[

\mathbb{P}^{3}
