Sample Identifying Complexity of Encrypted Control Systems Under Least Squares Identification footnoteinfo This work was supported by JSPS Grant-in-Aid for JSPS Fellows Grant Number JP21J22442 and JSPS KAKENHI Grant Number JP22H01509.
Kaoru Teranishi, Kiminao Kogiso

Abstract
A sample identifying complexity has been introduced in the previous study to capture an adversary’s estimation error of system identification. The complexity plays a crucial role in defining the security of encrypted control systems and designing a controller and security parameter for the systems. This study proposes a novel sample identifying complexity of encrypted control systems under an adversary who identifies system parameters using a least squares method. The proposed complexity is characterized by a controllability Gramian and ratio of identification input variance to the noise variance. We examine the tightness of the proposed complexity and its changes associated with the Gramian and variance ratio through numerical simulations. The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed complexity captures a behavior of estimation error with a sufficient level. Moreover, it confirmed that the effect of controllability Gramian in the proposed complexity becomes larger as the variance ratio increases.

Introduction

Outsourcing computation of control systems to a cloud server, such as control as a service (CaaS), is one form of realization of cyber-physical systems that improve the efficiency and flexibility of traditional control systems.
However, such computing services often face threats that adversaries eavesdrop and learn about private information of control systems.
Homomorphic encryption is the major countermeasure against such threats because it provides direct computation on encrypted data without accessing the original messages
(
Acar et al. 2019
)
.
The encryption was applied to realize an encrypted control that is a framework for secure outsourcing computation of control algorithms
(
Kogiso and Fujita 2015
;
Farokhi et al. 2017
;
Kim et al. 2016
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Darup et al. 2021
)
.
Owning to the benefits of encrypted control, various controls, such as model predictive control
(
Alexandru et al. 2018
;
Darup et al. 2018
)
, motion control
(
Qiu and Ueda 2019
;
Shono et al. 2022
)
, and reinforcement learning
(
Suh and Tanaka 2021
)
were implemented in encrypted forms.

Although many encrypted control methods were proposed, it is not sufficiently clarified how secure an encrypted control system is against what type of adversary.
In order to solve this problem, some recent studies have tried to define and analyze the security of encrypted control systems through two approaches.
One of them is a cryptographic approach that defines the provable security of encrypted controls and reveals a relation between the security and existing security notions in cryptography
(
Teranishi and Kogiso 2022
)
.
In this security definition, an adversary and information used for attacks are respectively formulated as a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm and its inputs instead of assuming specific attacks.
Using the security notion, we can analyze qualitative security for a broad class of encrypted control systems.
In contrast, another study has employed a control theoretic approach that considers the security of encrypted control systems under an adversary who wants to learn the system parameters by system identification
(
Teranishi et al. 2022
)
.
The security in this approach is defined by the system identification error and computation time for the process.
Unlike the cryptographic approach, the security notion in this approach enables quantifying a security level of encrypted control systems.
The study has also solved an optimization problem for designing a controller and security parameter to minimize the computation costs of encryption algorithms while satisfying the desired security level.
Meanwhile, this approach depends on a specific attack scenario: which parameters are identified and what algorithm is used for the identification.

This study proposes a novel sample identifying complexity of encrypted control systems under an adversary who attempts system identification of a plant in order to extend the application of the control-theoretic security analysis.
Although the previous work has focused on an adversary identifying a system matrix of a closed-loop system
(
Teranishi et al. 2022
)
, this study considers an adversary estimating the system and input matrices of a plant controlled by an encrypted controller.
Such an adversary represents an eavesdropper executing man-in-the-middle attacks and a malicious server infected by malware or spoofing an authorized server computing encrypted control algorithms.
Furthermore, the adversary employs a basic least squares identification method, which is more prevalent in practical use than the Bayesian estimation method discussed in the previous study
(
Teranishi et al. 2022
)
.
The proposed sample identifying complexity is characterized by a controllability Gramian of the identified plant and variance ratio of adversarial input for the system identification and plant noise.

The proposed sample identifying complexity is beneficial for evaluating the adversary’s capability and essential for defining the security of encrypted control systems.
Using the proposed quantity, we can estimate how precisely the adversary is expected to identify a given plant for a certain number of data.
Moreover, the proposed sample identifying complexity is extended to a closed-loop case.
The extended result suggests a defense policy that a controller should be designed to maximize the stability degree of a closed-loop system to prevent system identification.
Our analysis also reveals that such a defense policy is effective when the variance of plant noise is sufficiently smaller than the variance of adversarial identification input.

2.2
Homomorphic encryption and encrypted control

This section defines the syntax of homomorphic encryption and encrypted control.

Definition 1

Homomorphic encryption is a tuple
\Pi=(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec},\mathsf{Eval})
, where the algorithms are defined as follows.

•

(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})
: A key generation algorithm takes a security parameter
1^{\lambda}
and outputs a public key
\mathsf{pk}
and secret key
\mathsf{sk}
, where
1^{\lambda}
is the unary representation of
\lambda>0
.

•

\mathsf{ct}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},m)
: An encryption algorithm takes
\mathsf{pk}
and a plaintext
m
and outputs a ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
.

•

m\leftarrow\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk},\mathsf{ct})
: A decryption algorithm takes
\mathsf{sk}
and a ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}
and outputs a plaintext
m
.

•

\mathsf{ct}\leftarrow\mathsf{Eval}(f,\mathsf{ct}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{ct}_{N})
: An evaluation algorithm takes a function
f:(m_{1},\dots,m_{N})\mapsto f(m_{1},\dots,m_{N})
and ciphertexts
\mathsf{ct}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{ct}_{N}
and outputs a ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},f(m_{1},\dots,m_{N}))
, where
\mathsf{ct}_{i}=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},m_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,N
.

For a vector (matrix) plaintext and ciphertext, the algorithms are assumed to perform each element of the vectors (matrices).

An encrypted controller is defined based on the evaluation algorithm of homomorphic encryption as follows.

Definition 2

An encrypted controller
\mathsf{EC}
of controller
f:(\Phi,\xi)\mapsto\psi
is an algorithm defined as follows, where
\Phi
is a controller parameter,
\xi
is a controller input, and
\psi
is a controller output.

•

\mathsf{ct}_{\psi}\leftarrow\mathsf{EC}(f,\mathsf{ct}_{\Phi},\mathsf{ct}_{\xi})
: An encrypted controller algorithm takes a controller
f
and ciphertexts
\mathsf{ct}_{\Phi}=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},\Phi)
and
\mathsf{ct}_{\xi}=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},\xi)
and outputs a ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}_{\psi}=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},f(\Phi,\xi))
using the evaluation algorithm
\mathsf{Eval}
in Definition
1
.

The controller parameter and input need to be encoded to plaintexts before encryption because control systems typically operate over real numbers.
Although the encoding causes quantization errors, we ignore the errors for simplicity.
Note that this is the worst case scenario for a defender.

2.3
Security of encrypted control systems

This section introduces the security definition of encrypted control systems proposed in the previous study
(
Teranishi et al. 2022
)
.
The security is defined for encrypted control systems under an adversary who performs system identification of the system by using two notions, sample identifying complexity and sample deciphering time.

A sample identifying complexity is defined for capturing how the expectation of estimation error of the system identification decreases according to the increase of a sample size as follows.

Definition 3

Let
N
be a sample size for system identification by an adversary.
A sample identifying complexity
\gamma
is defined as a function satisfying
\gamma(N)\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits[\epsilon]
, where
\epsilon
is an estimation error of the system identification.

A sample deciphering time is defined based on a computation time
\tau
for breaking an encryption scheme offering
\lambda
-bit security can be estimated by
\tau=2^{\lambda}/\Upsilon
if an attacker employs a computer of which performance is
\Upsilon
floating point number operations per second (FLOPS).

Definition 4

A sample deciphering time
\tau
is a computation time required for breaking
N
ciphertexts used for system identification by an adversary defined as
\tau(N,1^{\lambda})=2^{\lambda}N/\Upsilon
.

The security of encrypted control systems is defined using the sample identifying complexity and sample deciphering time as follows.

Definition 5

Let
\gamma_{c}
be an acceptable estimation error, and
\tau_{c}
be a life span of plant.
An encrypted control system is secure if there does not exist
N
such that
\gamma(N)<\gamma_{c}
and
\tau(N,1^{\lambda})\leq\tau_{c}
, where
\gamma
and
\tau
are defined in Definition
3
and Definition
4
, respectively.
Otherwise, the encrypted control system is unsecure.

Remark 6

The sample deciphering time in Definition
4
is defined for encrypted control systems using dynamic-key encryption, of which keys are updated every time step
(
Teranishi et al. 2022
)
.
In the case of using a traditional homomorphic encryption scheme, the sample deciphering time is computed as
\tau(1,1^{\lambda})
because an adversary can obtain the original message of any ciphertext once the encryption scheme is broken.

Attack Model

This section formulates an attack model considered in this study.

Suppose the plant in Fig.

x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+w_{t+1},

(1)

x_{0}=w_{0}

Definition 7

The adversary attempts to identify
(A,B)

In the first step of Definition

\displaystyle X_{p}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&\cdots&x_{j-1}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad X_{f}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\cdots&x_{j}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle U_{p}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}u_{0}&\cdots&u_{j-1}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad W_{f}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}&\cdots&w_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

X_{f}=AX_{p}+BU_{p}+W_{f}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X_{p}\\
U_{p}\end{bmatrix}+W_{f}.

(2)

The least squares estimators

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{A}&\hat{B}\end{bmatrix}\!=\!\mathop{\mathrm{arg~min}}\limits_{[A\ B]}\left\|X_{f}-\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X_{p}\\
U_{p}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{F}^{2}\!=\!X_{f}\begin{bmatrix}X_{p}\\

4
Sample Identifying Complexity

A sample identifying complexity and sample deciphering time are crucial for defining the security of encrypted control systems in Definition
5
.
The sample deciphering time in Definition
4
can be computed by determining a security parameter and computer performance of the adversary.
In contrast, a computation method for a sample identifying complexity is not obvious because it depends on system dynamics and a system identification method.
This section proposes a sample identifying complexity of (
1
) under the adversary in Definition
7
.
To this end, we define an estimation error of the least squares identification method as follows.

Definition 9

An estimation error
\epsilon
of (
3
) is defined as

\epsilon=\cfrac{1}{c}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\hat{A}&\hat{B}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{F}^{2},

where
c=n(n+m)
is the number of entries of
A
and
B
.

By Definition
9
,
\epsilon
is a mean square error of the estimates
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
.
It should be noted here that one of the best strategies for the adversary in Definition
7
to design the malicious inputs
a_{0},\dots,a_{j}
minimizing the error
\epsilon
is that the inputs are independently and identically sampled from the Gaussian distribution with mean zero.
Under this setting, the following theorem reveals a lower bound for the expectation of estimation error.

Theorem 10

Let
N=j+1
.
Suppose malicious inputs
a_{0},\dots,a_{j}
are i.i.d. noises following the Gaussian distribution with mean
\bm{0}
and variance
\sigma_{u}^{2}I
.
The expectation of estimation error in Definition
9
is bounded from below by

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\epsilon\right]\!\geq\!\gamma(N)\!\coloneqq\!\cfrac{(m+n)\sigma_{w}^{2}}{j\left(m+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{1})\right)\sigma_{u}^{2}+\left(jn+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{2})\right)\sigma_{w}^{2}},

(4)

where
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
are controllability Gramians respectively obtained by solving the following discrete Lyapunov equations,

\displaystyle A\Psi_{1}A^{\top}-\Psi_{1}+BB^{\top}=O,

\displaystyle A\Psi_{2}A^{\top}-\Psi_{2}+I=O.

{pf}

Let
D=[X_{p}^{\top}\ U_{p}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
It follows from (
2
) and (
3
) that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits[\epsilon]

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\left\|\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}-X_{f}D^{+}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\left\|\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}-\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}D+W_{f}\right)D^{+}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\left\|W_{f}D^{+}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\left\|\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f}D^{+})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f}D^{+})\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f}D^{+})^{\top}\right)\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\left(D^{+}\!\otimes\!I\right)^{\top}\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f})\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f})^{\top}\left(D^{+}\!\otimes\!I\right)\right)\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{c}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[D^{+}(D^{+})^{\top}\otimes I\right]\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f})\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f})^{\top}\right]\right),

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product.
Additionally,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f})\mathop{\mathrm{vec}}\limits(W_{f})^{\top}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}w_{1}^{\top}&\cdots&w_{1}w_{j}^{\top}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w_{j}w_{1}^{\top}&\cdots&w_{j}w_{j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right]=\sigma_{w}^{2}I.

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits[\epsilon]

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{c}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[D^{+}(D^{+})^{\top}\otimes I\right]\right),

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{c}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[D^{+}(D^{+})^{\top}\right]\right)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(I),

\displaystyle=\cfrac{n\sigma_{w}^{2}}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(D^{\top}(DD^{\top})^{-1}(DD^{\top})^{-1}D\right)\right],

\displaystyle=\cfrac{n\sigma_{w}^{2}}{c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left((DD^{\top})^{-1}\right)\right].

Using Jensen’s inequality, the expectation of trace of inverse matrix is bounded from below by

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left((DD^{\top})^{-1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq(m+n)^{2}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(DD^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right],

\displaystyle\geq(m+n)^{2}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(DD^{\top}\right)\right]^{-1},

\displaystyle=(m+n)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[DD^{\top}\right]\right)^{-1}.

The denominator is computed as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[DD^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\begin{bmatrix}X_{p}X_{p}^{\top}&X_{p}U_{p}^{\top}\\
U_{p}X_{p}^{\top}&U_{p}U_{p}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[X_{p}X_{p}^{\top}\right]\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[U_{p}U_{p}^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&\cdots&x_{j-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
x_{j-1}^{\top}\\
\end{bmatrix}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\begin{bmatrix}u_{0}&\cdots&u_{j-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_{0}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
u_{j-1}^{\top}\\
\end{bmatrix}\right]\right),

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right]\right)\!+\!\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}\right]\right).

It follows from (
1
) that

x_{t}=A^{t}x_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}A^{t-1-k}Bu_{k}+w_{t}.

Thus, the traces of expectations are given as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}A^{t}x_{0}x_{0}^{\top}(A^{t})^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}A^{t-1-k}Bu_{k}u_{k}^{\top}B^{\top}(A^{t-1-k})^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}w_{t}w_{t}^{\top}\right]\right),

\displaystyle=jn\sigma_{w}^{2}\!+\!\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\!\left(\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}\!A^{t}(A^{t})^{\!\top}\!\!\right)\!\!+\!\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\!\left(\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}\!\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\!A^{k}BB^{\top}\!(A^{k})^{\!\top}\!\!\right)\!,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}\right]\right)=\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}\sigma_{u}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(I)=jm\sigma_{u}^{2}.

Furthermore, the matrices are bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}A^{k}BB^{\top}(A^{k})^{\top}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A^{k}BB^{\top}(A^{k})^{\top}=\Psi_{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}A^{t}(A^{t})^{\top}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}A^{t}(A^{t})^{\top}=\Psi_{2}.

Therefore, we obtain the bound for the expectation of estimation error as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits[\epsilon]

\displaystyle=\cfrac{n\sigma_{w}^{2}}{n(n+m)}\cdot(m+n)^{2}\cdot\cfrac{1}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits\left[DD^{\top}\right]\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\cfrac{(m+n)\sigma_{w}^{2}}{j\left(m+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{1})\right)\sigma_{u}^{2}+\left(jn+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{2})\right)\sigma_{w}^{2}}.

This completes the proof. ∎

From Theorem
10
, the lower bound
\gamma
in (
4
) is a sample identifying complexity of (
1
) under the adversary in Definition

.
Now, if
j
is sufficiently large,
\gamma
can be approximated by a simple equation.

Corollary 11

Let
N=j+1
and
R_{\sigma}=\sigma_{u}^{2}/\sigma_{w}^{2}
.
Suppose
j
is sufficiently large.
Then, the sample identifying complexity is given as

\gamma(N)=\cfrac{m+n}{j\left[(m+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{1}))R_{\sigma}+n\right]}.

(5)

{pf}

If
j
is sufficiently large, the denominator of (
4
) is approximated as
j\left(m+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{1})\right)\sigma_{u}^{2}+jn\sigma_{w}^{2}
.
Then, (
5
) holds by dividing both the numerator and denominator of (
4
) by
\sigma_{w}^{2}
. ∎

The equation (
5
) shows that the sample identifying complexity is characterized by the eigenvalues of controllability Gramian
\Psi_{1}
and variance ratio
R_{\sigma}
.
If
R_{\sigma}
is small, the term
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\limits(\Psi_{1})R_{\sigma}
becomes small.
Then, the sample identifying complexity
\gamma
is almost independent of the eigenvalues of controllability Gramian.
Meanwhile, the smaller eigenvalues of the controllability Gramian are, the larger sample identifying complexity is.
In other words, the expectation of estimation error in Definition
9
is larger if the stability degree of plant (
1
) becomes larger.
This implies that, when
R_{\sigma}
is large, the information leakage on the dynamics of (
1
) can be reduced by making (
1
) more stable because a plant state is not sufficiently driven by an input for system identification.
Note that a similar observation has been discussed in the previous study in which the adversary aims to identify a system matrix of a closed-loop system using the Bayesian estimation
(
Teranishi et al. 2022
)
.

Now we consider extending the sample identifying complexity to a closed-loop case.
With the plant (
1

\mathsf{EC}(f_{\mathrm{sf}},\mathsf{ct}_{F},\mathsf{ct}_{r},\mathsf{ct}_{x})

, the closed-loop system is given as
x_{t+1}=A_{F}x_{t}+Br_{t}+w_{t+1}
, where
A_{F}=A+BF
,
F
is a feedback gain, and

is a reference input to be falsified by an adversary.
We assume that an adversary focuses on identifying the parameters
A_{F}
and
B
by a least squares method in Section

with the data
\{(r_{k},x_{k})\}_{k=0}^{j}
, which is obtained by deciphering
\{(\mathsf{ct}_{r_{k}},\mathsf{ct}_{x_{k}})\}_{k=0}^{j}
.
Then, a controllability Gramian
\Psi_{1}
is given by solving the discrete Lyapunov equation
A_{F}\Psi_{1}A_{F}^{\top}-\Psi_{1}+BB^{\top}=O
, and explicitly formulated as
\Psi_{1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{F}^{k}BB^{\top}(A_{F}^{k})^{\top}
.
Here the controllability Gramian is a function of the feedback gain
F
.
Thus, we would be able to improve
\gamma
of the closed-loop system by designing the feedback gain so that eigenvalues of the controllability Gramian are minimized.
Note that this defense policy would be effective only when the variance ratio is sufficiently large since the effect of controllability Gramian for the sample identifying complexity can be insignificant if the ratio is small.

6
Conclusion

This study proposed a sample identifying complexity under an adversary who tries to identify parameters of a plant in an encrypted control system using a least squares method.
The proposed sample identifying complexity is computed by system dimensions, controllability Gramians, and noise and input variances.
The simulation results demonstrated that the proposed complexity captures the expectation of estimation error with a sufficient level.
Our future work includes deriving a sample identifying complexity of multi-agent and nonlinear systems.
