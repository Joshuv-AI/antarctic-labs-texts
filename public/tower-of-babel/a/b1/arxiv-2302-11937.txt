Stochastic equations with singular drift driven by fractional Brownian motion
Oleg Butkovsky Khoa Lê Leonid Mytnik

Abstract
We consider stochastic differential equation d ​ X t = b ⁡ ( X t ) ​ d ​ t + d ​ W t H , dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}^{H}, where the drift b b is either a measure or an integrable function, and W H W^{H} is a d d -dimensional fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) H\in(0,1) , d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} . For the case where b ∈ L p ​ ( ℝ d ) b\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) , p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] we show weak existence of solutions to this equation under the condition d p < 1 H − 1 , \frac{d}{p}<\frac{1}{H}-1, which is an extension of the Krylov–Röckner condition (2005) to the fractional case. We construct a counter-example showing optimality of this condition. If b b is a Radon measure, particularly the delta measure, we prove weak existence of solutions to this equation under the optimal condition H < 1 d + 1 H<\frac{1}{d+1} . We also show strong well-posedness of solutions to this equation under certain conditions. To establish these results, we utilize the stochastic sewing technique and develop a new version of the stochastic sewing lemma.

Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) driven by fractional Brownian motion (fBM) are commonly used in mathematical biophysics, where fBM is considered a canonical model for anomalous diffusion, see, e.g.,
[
BW21
,
Goy09
,
JM10
,
WV18
]
. In this article we study multidimensional SDE

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}^{H},\quad t\in[0,T]

(1.1)

\displaystyle X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
T>0
,
b
is a finite signed Radon measure or
b\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
W^{H}
is an fBM with the Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
, and the initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We show weak existence of solutions to this equation in the optimal regime (
Theorems

2.5
and
2.13
) and construct a counter-example to prove optimality of our assumptions (
Theorem

2.6
). For
d=1
we obtain strong well-posedness under certain conditions (
Theorem

2.16
). Additionally, we provide a connection between equation (
1.1
) and a stochastic equation involving local time (
Theorem

2.14
). As a byproduct of our results, we deduce a short direct proof of classical results of Geman–Horowitz
[
GH80
]
and Xiao
[
Xia97
]
that if
Hd<1
, then
W^{H}
has a local time of certain space-time regularity. We also show that a sum of a fractional Brownian motion and an adapted process of bounded variation has a jointly continuous local time under the optimal condition
H(d+1)<1
(
Theorem

2.18
). To prove these results, we further develop the stochastic sewing technique initiated by
[
Lê20
]
and obtain a Rosenthal-type stochastic sewing lemma
Theorem

3.6
which relaxes the assumptions on high moments of increments of the process.

It has been known for a long time that an ill-posed deterministic system can become well-posed when randomly perturbed. Indeed, the differential equation
dX_{t}=\sign(X_{t})|X_{t}|^{\alpha}dt
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
X_{0}=0
has infinitely many solutions. On the other hand, the perturbed equation
dX_{t}=\sign(X_{t})|X_{t}|^{\alpha}dt+dW_{t}
, where
W
is a Brownian motion, has a unique solution. This phenomenon is called regularization by noise; we refer the reader to monograph
[
Fla11
]
for many interesting examples and further discussion.

Regularization by noise is particularly well-studied in the case of Brownian noise
(H=\frac{1}{2})
, and many sharp results have been obtained. The pioneering works of Zvonkin and Veretennikov
[
Zvo74
,
Ver80
]
showed that if the drift
b
is a measurable bounded function, then the SDE

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

(1.2)

where
W
is a Brownian motion, has a unique strong solution. This was later extended by Krylov and Röckner
[
KR05
]
to unbounded integrable drifts
b\in L_{q}([0,1],L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
p\geqslant 2
, under the condition
\frac{2}{q}+\frac{d}{p}<1
. The critical case has been treated in
[
BC05
]
(
d=1
) and
[
Kry21b
]
(
d\geqslant 3)
. It is also known that the Krylov–Röckner condition is optimal: if
\frac{d}{p}>1
, then one can construct a drift
b\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that (
1.2
) has no solutions
[
BFGM19
, Example 7.4]
,
[
Kry21a
, Example 1.1]
. Further generalizations can be found in
[
Zha11
]
.

The case where the drift
b
is a Radon measure has also been thoroughly studied. In this case, the term
b(X_{t})
in (
1.2
) is not defined, and one needs to explain what is meant by a solution to this equation. The main idea is that, instead of defining
b(X_{t})
at each fixed
t
, one directly defines the integral
\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{r})dr
either via the local time of
X
or via approximations of
b
, see
[
LG84
,
BC01
]
and the discussion in
Section

2
. It is known from
[
LG84
]
and
[
BC05
, Theorem 4.5]
that if
d=1
and
b
is a finite signed Radon measure such that
|b(\{x\})|\leqslant 1
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then (
1.2
) has a unique strong solution. This condition is also optimal: if the measure
b
has atoms of size larger than
1
, then (
1.2
) has no solutions, see
[
BC05
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
HS81
, p. 312]
.

Regularization by noise phenomena outside from martingale contexts such as SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motion and stochastic partial differential equations are much less understood. This is not because regularization by noise only occurs in the Brownian case, but rather because there are very few tools available to study this problem in other setups. Indeed, all the proofs in the aforementioned articles heavily rely on Itô calculus and PDE techniques; these instruments are largely inapplicable beyond the semimartingale setting.
Recall that the rougher the driving noise
W
is in (
1.2
), the more irregular drift
b
one can take so that (
1.2
) remains well-posed, see
[
CG16
]
for a collection of results highlighting this phenomenon. The idea behind this is that if the fluctuations of the noise are bigger than the fluctuations of the drift
\int b(X_{r})dr
, then the noise averages out singularities of the drift
b

so that the map
\eta\mapsto\int b(W+\eta)dr
is Lipschitz in suitable metrics.
If the noise is not irregular enough, then we give an explicit example showing that (
1.2
) may have no solutions, see
Theorem

2.6
.
On the other spectrum,
[
HP21
, Theorem 1.2]
constructs a random continuous forcing
W
, which is so irregular that equation (
1.2
) has a unique solution for
any
Schwartz distribution
b
. We refer the reader to
[
GG22
, Section 1]
and
[
CdR18
, end of Section 1]
for further discussion of this principle.

Therefore, an important objective is to generalize sharp results on regularization by Brownian noise to non-semimartingale noises while maintaining optimality.

To understand what the optimal condition for well-posedness of (
1.1
) should be, we apply the following heuristics. Clearly,
W^{H}\in\mathcal{C}^{H-\varepsilon}
. This implies that
X\in\mathcal{C}^{H-\varepsilon}
. Further,
if
b\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, then
b\in\mathcal{C}^{-\frac{d}{p}}
and very informally we would get
b(X_{\cdot})\in\mathcal{C}^{-\frac{(H-\varepsilon)d}{p}}
. We gain
1
in regularity by integration, and hence we see that the drift of (
1.1
)
\int b(X_{r})\,dr\in\mathcal{C}^{1-\frac{(H-\varepsilon)d}{p}}
. By the above principle, we expect to have well-posedness if

1-\frac{Hd}{p}>H.

(1.3)

As a sanity check, for the Brownian case
H=\frac{1}{2}
, (
1.3
) becomes
\frac{d}{p}<1
and coincides with the Krylov–Röckner condition. Another version of the same heuristic which uses the scaling argument and leads to (
1.3
) can be found in
[
GG22
]
.

Regularization by fractional Brownian noise was initiated by Nualart and Ouknine in
[
NO02
,
NO03
]
.
This problem sparks new interests recently, with new different approaches
[
CG16
,
ART23
,
GG22
]
. Unfortunately, these works do not allow to deduce well-posedness for
b\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
or for
b
being a Radon measure under condition (
1.3
).

Our main result is the weak existence of solutions to (
1.1
) under the above condition. In the case where
b
is a measure, we also obtain weak existence under the corresponding analogue of (
1.3
). We construct a counterexample that shows the optimality of (
1.3
).
Further, we show that, under certain assumptions, a solution to (
1.1
) has a jointly continuous local time. This extends to the case of irregular drifts the corresponding results obtained in
[
LO17
,
SSV24
]
where existence of local times of solutions to SDEs driven by fBM with smooth drifts was established.

In the particular case where
b=\delta_{0}
, with
\delta_{0}
being the delta measure at
0
, equation (
1.1
) becomes

dX_{t}=\delta_{0}(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}^{H},\quad t\in[0,T]

(1.4)

and its solution is called
skew fractional Brownian motion
(sfBM). Strong well-posedness of sFBM is known for
H=\frac{1}{2}
(
[
HS81
]
) and
H\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
(
[
CG16
,
ART23
]
). This leaves the interval
H\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
where well-posedness of (
1.4
) is expected but has not yet been proved. In this article we reduce this gap. We show weak existence of solutions to (
1.4
) in the whole range
H<\frac{1}{2}
and strong unqiueness for
H<(\sqrt{1}3-3)/2
. The remaining part — strong uniqueness for
H\in[(\sqrt{1}3-3)/2,1/2)
— poses a significant challenge that would likely require the development of vastly different techniques.

Now, let us say a few words about our proof methods. As we explained above, well-posedness for (
1.1
) with
H=\frac{1}{2}
has been analyzed mostly using PDE methods and Itô’s formula. A breakthrough in studying (
1.1
) for
H\neq\frac{1}{2}
has been achieved in
[
CG16
]
where deterministic sewing was applied.
[
Lê20
]
introduced a new technique called
stochastic sewing
and applied it to analyze (
1.1
). Further developments of stochastic sewing and its application to studying equation (
1.1
) were made in
[
ART23
,
GG22
,
MP24
,
Ger22
]
. We also apply stochastic sewing, however compared with the above works, our proof strategy has the following new ingredients which allowed us to get optimal results on the existence of weak solutions.

First, as discussed above, the drift
\int b(X_{r})\,dr
has Hölder regularity
1-\frac{Hd}{p}-\varepsilon<1
. On the other hand, if we measure its regularity in the variation scale, then it is easy to see that this drift is always of finite
1
-variation.
By combining this insight with the idea of random control, which was introduced in
[
ABLM24
]
, we improve the corresponding powers in stochastic sewing from
1-\frac{Hd}{p}-\varepsilon
to
1
, see the proof of
Lemma

4.6
.

Next, we replace the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality in stochastic sewing lemma with the Rosenthal–Burkholder inequality. This yields a new version of stochastic sewing lemma,
Theorem

3.6
. Morally, it says that instead of bounding
m
th moment (where
m
is large) of the difference
\delta A_{s,u,t}
, it is enough to have a good bound only on the second conditional moment
\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt[\delta A_{s,u,t}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{u}]
.
Remark

4.8
explains why this improvement is crucial for getting weak existence in the optimal regime.

Finally, we also utilize the quantitative John–Nirenberg inequality
[
Lê22
]
and taming singularities technique
[
BFG21
,
LL21
]
.
Remark

4.3
elaborates why this leads to better results than just the application of stochastic sewing alone.

We have made significant effort to avoid using Girsanov’s transform. This is because we do not expect Girsanov’s theorem to be applicable over the entire range of
p\in[1,\infty]
,
H\in(0,1)
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, where (
1.3
) holds. Therefore, to extend the corresponding bounds from fBM to perturbed fBM, we rely solely on stochastic sewing.
A parallel project
[
BLM25
]
focuses on the range of
p
,
H
,
d
for which Girsanov’s theorem is applicable. It establishes the strong existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1.1
) for these values of the parameters (a subset of the range of parameters for which (
1.3
) holds). Weak uniqueness for SDE (
1.1
) for
b
being a Schwartz distribution was considered in recent work
[
BM24
]
.

The fact that our approach does not rely on Girsanov’s formula or Itô’s lemma makes it quite robust. We believe that it can be transferred to other types of noise, see
Remark

2.10
. In particular, it can be used to study regularization by noise and local times for stochastic partial differential equations, potentially complementing the results from
[
ABLM24
,
Zam03
]
.

We will consider the following class of solutions.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that a regularized solution

to (

in

Following

Theorem 2.6

Remark 2.9

Remark 2.10
.

Recall that the fractional Brownian motion has a scaling property

with the driving noise

in place of

and

.

Now we turn to study the case where
b
is a measure. Here one can again define a solution to
(
1.1
) in the regularised sense. However there is also another way to make sense of the drift
\int b(X_{t})dt
which was pioneered by Stroock and Yor
[

, Theorem 1.9]
and Le Gall
[

]
, see also
[

]

[

[

Definition 2.11
.

Let
b\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
T>0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that a continuous process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
solves

X_{t}=x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L^{X}_{t}(y)\,b(dy)+W_{t}^{H},\quad t\in[0,T],

(2.9)

where

L^{X}_{t}(y):=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{v_{d}\varepsilon^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}\I(|X_{s}-y|<\varepsilon)\,ds,\quad t\in[0,T],\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.10)

if the occupation measure of
X
,
\mu^{X}_{t}(A):=\int_{0}^{t}\I(X_{s}\in A)\,ds
,
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and (
2.9
) holds
\mathsf{P}
-almost surely. The integration with respect to the measure is understood in the Lebesgue sense.

In what follows by solution to equation (
2.9
) we always mean a solution in the sense of
Definition

2.11
.

It is clear, that if
b
has a density
\rho_{b}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L^{X}_{t}(y)\,b(dy)=\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{b}(X_{s})\,ds,

(2.11)

and (
2.9
) becomes
Eq(

)
.

Remark 2.12
.

Clearly,
L^{X}
in (
2.9
)–(
2.10
) is just a local time of
X
, that is
L_{t}^{X}=\frac{d\mu_{t}^{X}}{d\Leb}
.
However, since measure
b
can be singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure, it is crucial to carefully select an appropriate version of the Radon–Nikodym derivative (as it was done in (
2.10
)) in order to avoid absurd situations. Indeed, let
d=1
and consider an equation
dX_{t}=\delta_{0}(X_{t})\,dt+dW_{t}^{H}
.
Let
L^{W}
be the local time of
W^{H}
. Put

\widetilde{L}^{W}_{t}(x):=L^{W}_{t}(x)\I(x\neq 0).

Then
\widetilde{L}^{W}
is also a version of the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{d\mu_{t}^{W}}{d\Leb}
. Further, by definition,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{L}^{W}_{t}(y)\,\delta_{0}(dy)=0
, and thus

W_{t}^{H}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{L}^{W}_{t}(y)\,\delta_{0}(dy)+W_{t}^{H}.

Hence, if we had allowed
L^{X}
in (
2.9
) to be
any
version of the local time of
X
, we would have concluded that
W^{H}
solves the equation
dX_{t}=\delta_{0}(X_{t})dt+dW_{t}^{H}
, which makes no sense. Le Gall
[
LG84
]
avoided this issue by requiring
L^{X}
to be the semimartingale local time of
X
, defined via the Tanaka formula. Since Itô’s calculus for a generic
X
is unavailable when
H\neq 1/2
, we had to manually select the correct version of
L^{X}
in (
2.10
).

Theorem 2.18

Let

0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T\end{subarray}}\frac{|L^{W}([s,t],x)-L^{W}([s,t],y)|}{|t-s|^{1-Hd-H\gamma-\varepsilon}|x-y|^{\gamma}}<\infty.

(2.15)

2.

Assume that

(t,x)

Remark 2.19

The condition

[

Finally, let us briefly describe our proof strategy. We sketch here informally the main steps. First, using stochastic sewing and a quantitative version of John–Nirenberg inequality, we are able to bound all moments of

) allows to squeeze an additional finite variation random drift

4.6
; see also

4.8

By taking in these bounds

and

The John–Nirenberg inequality (

Our next result takes the best of both worlds. It says that to bound all the moments of
\mathcal{A}
, it suffices to control only the first two conditional moments of the increments of its approximation

and to have a very mild bound on a high moment of the increments of

.

\displaystyle\leqslant\Gamma_{2}|t-s|^{\alpha_{2}}2^{-k(\alpha_{2}+(\beta_{2}-1)\wedge 0)}\|\lambda_{2}(s,t)^{\beta_{2}}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}.

(3.20)

It is also not difficult to bound
I_{3}
. Using (
3.10
) and the fact than
n\geqslant m
, we see that

\displaystyle\|I_{3}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|I_{3}\|_{L_{n}(\Omega)}=\|\max_{i=0,\ldots,2^{k}-1}|A_{u_{i}^{k},t_{i+1}^{k}}|\|_{L_{n}(\Omega)}\leqslant\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{2^{k}-1}\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt|A_{u_{i}^{k},t_{i+1}^{k}}|^{n}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Gamma_{3}|t-s|^{\alpha_{3}}2^{-k(\alpha_{3}-\frac{1}{n})}.

(3.21)

Substituting (
3.19
), (
3.1
), and (
3.1
) into (
3.1
), we finally get

\displaystyle\|A^{k}_{s,t}-A^{k+1}_{s,t}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Gamma_{1}|t-s|^{\alpha_{1}}\|\lambda_{1}(s,t)^{\beta_{1}}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}2^{-k(\alpha_{1}+(\beta_{1}-\frac{1}{2})\wedge 0)}

\displaystyle\quad+\Gamma_{2}|t-s|^{\alpha_{2}}2^{-k(\alpha_{2}+(\beta_{2}-1)\wedge 0)}\|\lambda_{2}(s,t)^{\beta_{2}}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}+C\Gamma_{3}|t-s|^{\alpha_{3}}2^{-k(\alpha_{3}-\frac{1}{n})}.

Summing this inequality over
k
, and using the fact that, thanks to (
3.8
),
\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}>1/2
,
\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}>1
,
\alpha_{3}>\frac{1}{n}
we get for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
(recall that
A^{0}_{s,t}=A_{s,t}\I_{s\leqslant\tau}
)

\displaystyle\|A^{k}_{s,t}-A_{s,t}\I_{s\leqslant\tau}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|A^{i+1}_{s,t}-A^{i}_{s,t}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Gamma_{1}|t-s|^{\alpha_{1}}\|\lambda_{1}(s,t)^{\beta_{1}}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}+C\Gamma_{2}|t-s|^{\alpha_{2}}\|\lambda_{2}(s,t)^{\beta_{2}}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad+C\Gamma_{3}|t-s|^{\alpha_{3}},

for
C=C(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},m,n)>0
.
An application of Fatou’s lemma, assumption (
3.12
), and (
3.11
) yields the desired result (
3.13
).
∎

3.2
Uniqueness of solutions to the Young differential equation

An important tool in establishing uniqueness of SDE (
1.1
) is the following result related to (deterministic) Young differential equations.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
d=1
. Suppose that
X\in\mathcal{C}^{p-\textrm{var}}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
,
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{q-\textrm{var}}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
,
p,q\geqslant 1
,
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}>1
and
X
,
Y
are continuous. Then the following ODE

Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\,dX_{s},\quad t\in[0,1],\quad Y_{0}=0,

(3.22)

has only trivial zero solution
Y\equiv 0
.

Before we begin the proof let us emphasize that we do not assume here
p<2
; otherwise the result is standard, see, e.g.,
[
Bau17
, Chapter 3, Theorem 3.1]
. Actually, we are interested in the opposite situation:
Y
is a “nice” relatively smooth process with
q\in[1,2)
and
X
is a very irregular process. Without the additional assumption on regularity of
Y
, (
3.22
) would become a rough differential equation and one would have to lift
X
to rough path in order to make sense of the integral. We consider (
3.22
) as a Young differential equation and are interested in uniqueness among all smooth enough solutions.

Proof.

The main idea of the proof was communicated to us by Fedja Nazarov.

It is sufficient to consider the case
p>2>q
. Indeed, otherwise, if
p\leqslant q
, then
p<2
and uniqueness of solutions to (
3.22
) follows from
[
Bau17
, Chapter 3, Theorem 3.1]
.
Assume that (
3.22
) has a non-zero solution. Without loss of generality, we assume that
Y_{r}>0
for some
r\in(0,1]
(the case when
Y
is nonpositive is done in exactly the same way). Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
,
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1
such that

Y_{s}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2K},\quad Y_{t}=\varepsilon,\quad\inf_{r\in[s,t]}Y_{r}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2K},

where we defined
K:=\exp(\|X\|_{L_{\infty}([0,1])})
. We claim now that

X\in\mathcal{C}^{q-\textrm{var}}([s,t],\mathbb{R})
.

Indeed, by Young-Lóeve estimate
[
FV10
, Theorem 6.8]
we have for any
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\Delta_{[s,t]}

|Y_{t^{\prime}}-Y_{s^{\prime}}-Y_{s^{\prime}}(X_{t^{\prime}}-X_{s^{\prime}})|=\Bigl|\int_{s^{\prime}}^{t^{\prime}}Y_{r}dX_{r}-Y_{s^{\prime}}(X_{t^{\prime}}-X_{s^{\prime}})\Bigr|\leqslant C\|Y\|_{q-\textrm{var};[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]},

for
C=C(p,q)>0
.
Therefore, taking into account that
Y_{s^{\prime}}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2K}
for
s^{\prime}\in[s,t]
we get

|X_{t^{\prime}}-X_{s^{\prime}}|\leqslant\frac{2K}{\varepsilon}(|Y_{t^{\prime}}-Y_{s^{\prime}}|+C\|Y\|_{q-\textrm{var};[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]})\leqslant\frac{2K}{\varepsilon}(C+1)\|Y\|_{q-\textrm{var};[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]}.

This implies
\|X\|_{q-\textrm{var};[s,t]}\leqslant\frac{2K}{\varepsilon}(C+1)\|Y\|_{q-\textrm{var};[s,t]}
, and thus
X\in\mathcal{C}^{q-\textrm{var}}([s,t],\mathbb{R})
.

However, since
q<2
and
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}^{q-\textrm{var}}([s,t],\mathbb{R})
, equation (
3.22
) has a unique solution on
[s,t]
by
[
Bau17
, Chapter 3, Theorem 3.1]
. T his solution is given by
Y_{r}=Y_{s}e^{X_{r}}
,
r\in[s,t]
. Thus,

\varepsilon=Y_{t}=Y_{s}e^{X_{t}}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2K}K=\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

which is a contradiction. Therefore (
3.22
) has only zero solution.
∎

4
Key integral bounds

In this section we establish key integral bounds which allow to establish existence and uniqueness of solutions to equation (
1.1
). Without loss of generality, we will be working on the time interval
[0,1]
. Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
H\in(0,1)
, and the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
W^{H}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
–fractional Brownian motion. Recall representation (
2.2
). For
(s,t)\in\Delta_{[0,1]}
consider a process

V_{s,t}:=W^{H}_{t}-\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}W^{H}_{t}=\int_{s}^{t}K_{H}(t,r)dB_{r}.

(4.1)

We begin with the following basic bound. Its proof uses just the original stochastic sewing lemma from
[
Lê20
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable function. Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}
where
q\in[2,\infty]
,
\alpha<0
. Assume that

\alpha>-\frac{1}{2H}.

(4.2)

Let
m\in[2,\infty)
such that
m\leqslant q
.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(H,d,\alpha,m,q)>0
such that for any
0\leqslant S_{0}<S\leqslant T\leqslant 1
, any
deterministic
measurable function
x\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
one has

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\int_{S}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})dr\Bigr\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+\alpha H}\bigl((S-S_{0})^{-\frac{Hd}{q}}+\I(H<1/2)(S-S_{0})^{-\frac{d}{2q}}(T-S)^{\frac{d}{2q}-\frac{Hd}{q}}\bigr).

(4.3)

Proof.

We apply stochastic sewing lemma from
[
Lê20
]
, that is,
Proposition

3.4
, to the following processes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{t}:=\int_{S}^{t}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr,\quad t\in[S,T];

\displaystyle A_{s,t}:=\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}\int_{s}^{t}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})dr,\quad s,t\in\Delta_{[S,T]}.

We stress once again that the process
x
is deterministic. Let us check that all the conditions of
Proposition

3.4
are satisfied.

Using heat kernel bound (
B.4
), we derive for
s,t\in\Delta_{[S,T]}

\displaystyle\|A_{s,t}\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{s}^{t}\|\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}\,dr

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(t-s)^{1+\alpha H}\bigl((S-S_{0})^{-\frac{Hd}{q}}+\I(H<1/2)(S-S_{0})^{-\frac{d}{2q}}(t-s)^{\frac{d}{2q}-\frac{Hd}{q}}\bigr),

(4.4)

for
C=C(H,d,\alpha,q)>0
.
We see that thanks to (
4.2
),
1+\alpha H>1/2
; further if
H<1/2
, then

1+H(\alpha-\frac{d}{q})+\frac{d}{2q}>1/2
. Thus, condition (
3.3
) holds thanks to an obvious bound

\|\delta A_{s,u,t}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|A_{s,t}\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}+\|A_{s,u}\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}+\|A_{u,t}\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)},

where we also used that
m\leqslant q
.

Next, note that for any
S\leqslant s\leqslant u\leqslant t\leqslant T
we have

\delta A_{s,u,t}=\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}\int_{u}^{t}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr-\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{u}\int_{u}^{t}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr,

which implies that
\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}\delta A_{s,u,t}=0
. Thus condition (
3.4
) holds.

Thus it remains to verify (
3.5
). Let
\Pi:=\{S=t_{0},t_{1},...,t_{k}=t\}
be an arbitrary partition of
[S,t]
. Denote by
|\Pi|
its mesh size. Note that thanks to our choice of the processes
\mathcal{A}
and
A
, for any
i\in[0,k-1]
we have
\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{t_{i}}(\mathcal{A}_{t_{i+1}}-\mathcal{A}_{t_{i}}-A_{t_{i},t_{i+1}})=0
and
\mathcal{A}_{t_{i+1}}-\mathcal{A}_{t_{i}}-A_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{i+1}}
–measurable. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}_{t}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}A_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
is a sum of martingale differences. Then, by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality,

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\mathcal{A}_{t_{i+1}}-\mathcal{A}_{t_{i}}-A_{t_{i},t_{i+1}})\Bigr\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|\mathcal{A}_{t_{i+1}}-\mathcal{A}_{t_{i}}-A_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}(t-S)|\Pi|

for
C>0
.
Therefore,
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}A_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}
converges to
\mathcal{A}_{t}
in probability as
|\Pi|\to 0
and hence condition (
3.5
) holds.

Thus, all the conditions of
Proposition

3.4
are satisfied and (
3.6
) implies

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\int_{S}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr\Bigr\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+\alpha H}\bigl((S-S_{0})^{-\frac{Hd}{q}}+\I(H<1/2)(S-S_{0})^{-\frac{d}{2q}}(T-S)^{\frac{d}{2q}-\frac{Hd}{q}}\bigr),

for
C=C(H,d,\alpha,m,q)>0
,
where again we used (
4
). This implies (
4.1
).
∎

The next step is to remove the singularity at
S_{0}
in (
4.1
) as well as the unnatural restriction
m\leqslant q
. This is done using the taming singularities lemma and then applying the John–Nirenberg inequality,
Propositions

3.1
and
3.2
. The former proposition allows us to take
S=S_{0}
in (
4.1
), while the latter allows us to take
m
arbitrarily large.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable function. Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}
where
q\in[2,\infty]
,
\alpha<0
such that

\alpha>-\frac{1}{2H}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\alpha-\frac{d}{q}>-\frac{1}{H}.

(4.5)

Let
m\in[1,\infty)
.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(H,d,\alpha,m,q)>0
such that for any
0\leqslant S_{0}\leqslant S\leqslant T\leqslant 1
, any
deterministic
measurable function
x\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
one has

\Bigl\|\int_{S}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})dr\Bigr\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+\alpha H-\frac{Hd}{q}}.

(4.6)

Proof.

Step 1
. We prove (
4.6
) for
m=1
. We tame the singularities with
Proposition

3.1
. Fix
(S,T)\in\Delta_{[0,1]}
and put

Y_{t}:=\int_{S+t}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr,\quad t\in(0,T-S].

We see that (
4.5
) implies
\alpha H>-\frac{1}{2}
, and thus by
Lemma

4.1
, for
(s,t)\in\Delta_{[0,T-S]}
we have

\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(t-s)^{1+\alpha H}\bigl(s^{-\frac{Hd}{q}}+\I(H<1/2)s^{-\frac{d}{2q}}(t-s)^{\frac{d}{2q}-\frac{Hd}{q}}\bigr),

for
C=C(H,d,\alpha,q)>0
.
We also see from (
4.5
) that
1+\alpha H>\frac{Hd}{q}
and
1+\alpha H+\frac{d}{2q}-\frac{Hd}{q}>\frac{d}{2q}
. Hence all the conditions of
Proposition

3.1
are satisfied and we get that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,T-S]
,

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\int_{S+\varepsilon}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr\Bigr\|_{L_{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigl\|\int_{S+\varepsilon}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr\Bigr\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}=\|Y_{T-S}-Y_{\varepsilon}\|_{L_{q}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S-\varepsilon)^{1+\alpha H-\frac{Hd}{q}},

where
C=C(H,d,\alpha,q)>0
.
By passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get (
4.6
) for
m=1
; here we used Fatou’s lemma and boundedness of the function
f
.

Step 2
. Now let us prove (
4.6
) for all
m\in[1,\infty)
; actually, the proof of Step 1 works for
m\leqslant q
, but not for large
m
. We apply
Proposition

3.2
. Put

\mathcal{A}_{t}:=\int_{S}^{t}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})\,dr,\quad t\in[S,T].

Then for any
(s,t)\in\Delta_{[S,T]}
we have

\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}|\mathcal{A}_{t}-\mathcal{A}_{s}|=\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}\Bigl|\int_{s}^{t}f(\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}V_{S_{0},r}+V_{s,r}+x_{r})\,dr\Bigr|=\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt\Bigl|\int_{s}^{t}f(V_{s,r}+y_{r})dr\Bigr|\bigg\rvert_{y_{r}=\hskip 0.45206pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.3014pt^{s}V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r}},

where we used independence of
(V_{s,r})_{r\geqslant s}
and
\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Applying (
4.6
) with
m=1
obtained from Step 1, we get

\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}|\mathcal{A}_{t}-\mathcal{A}_{s}|\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(t-s)^{1+\alpha H-\frac{Hd}{q}}\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+\alpha H-\frac{Hd}{q}},\quad(s,t)\in\Delta_{[S,T]},

where
C=C(H,d,\alpha,q)>0
.
Thus, (
3.2
) is satisfied with
\Gamma:=C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+\alpha H-\frac{Hd}{q}}
. Clearly, the process
\mathcal{A}
is continuous since
f
is bounded. Thus, all the conditions of
Proposition

3.2
are satisfied and hence we get for
m\in[1,\infty)

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\int_{S}^{T}f(V_{S_{0},r}+x_{r})dr\Bigr\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{A}_{T}-\mathcal{A}_{S}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+\alpha H-\frac{Hd}{q}},

where
C=C(H,d,\alpha,m,q)>0
,
which is (
4.6
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that if
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}
, then
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha-\frac{d}{q}}_{\infty}
. Therefore, if we apply
Lemma

4.1
alone, we would obtain exactly the same bound (
4.6
), but under the much more restrictive condition
\alpha-\frac{d}{q}>-\frac{1}{2H}
. The above proof shows that an application of the taming singularities lemma (
Proposition

3.1
) leads to a better condition (
4.5
). Thus, a combination of the taming singularities technique and stochastic sewing gives much better results than stochastic sewing alone.
Proposition

3.2
allows us to further refine the obtained results. Namely, when combined with stochastic sewing, it allows us to bound all the moments of the integral (rather than just the first few moments).

Next, we need an analogue of (
4.6
), but for the difference of functions.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable function. Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}
where
q\in[2,\infty]
,
\alpha<0
. Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
m\in[1,\infty)
. Assume that

\displaystyle\alpha-\lambda>-\frac{1}{2H}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\alpha-\frac{d}{q}-\lambda>-\frac{1}{H}

(4.7)

Then there exists a constant
C=C(H,d,\alpha,\lambda,m,q)>0
such that the following bound holds for any
deterministic
measurable function
x\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(S,T)\in\Delta_{[0,1]}
:

\Bigl\|\int_{S}^{T}(f(V_{S,r}+x_{r}+y)-f(V_{S,r}+x_{r}))\,dr\Bigr\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}|y|^{\lambda}(T-S)^{1+H(\alpha-\lambda-\frac{d}{q})}.

(4.8)

Proof.

Introduce the shift operator
\tau_{y}f(z):=f(y+z)
,
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us apply (
4.6
) to the function
\tau_{y}f-f
with
\alpha-\lambda
in place of
\alpha
. We see that condition (
4.7
) ensures that (
4.5
) holds. Then we get

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\int_{S}^{T}(f(V_{S,r}+x_{r}+y)-f(V_{S,r}+x_{r}))\,dr\Bigr\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|\tau_{y}f-f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha-\lambda}_{q}}(T-S)^{1+H(\alpha-\lambda-\frac{d}{q})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}|y|^{\lambda}(T-S)^{1+H(\alpha-\lambda-\frac{d}{q})},

where
C=C(H,d,\alpha,\lambda,m,q)>0
and the last inequality follows from (
A.2
). This yields (
4.8
).
∎

Remark 4.5
.

By taking in (
4.8
)
S=0
,
x\equiv 0
,
f=\delta_{u}
,

,

\alpha=-\frac{d}{2}
,

,

we get that if

, then

\|L^{W}(u-y,t)-L^{W}(u,t)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant C|y|^{\lambda}t^{1-H(d+\lambda)},\quad t\in[0,1],

where
L^{W}
is the local time of fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
and we also used (
A.2
). This provides a short and direct proof of Xiao’s result about moments of local time
[
Xia97
, Lemma 2.5]
.
It just remains to justify that
Lemma

4.2
is applicable also for distributions and not only for bounded functions, which is done in the proof of
Theorem

2.18
(i) by approximating a distribution by smooth functions (note that the right-hand side of (
4.6
) depends only on
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}}
and does not depend on higher Besov norms of

).

Now we are ready to move on to the main lemma of this section, which allows us to replace a deterministic drift in (
4.6
) with a stochastic drift. It is clear that such a replacement should come with certain restrictions: one must impose that the stochastic drift
\psi
is more regular (in a certain sense) than the driving noise
W^{H}
. Indeed, otherwise, one could simply take
\psi=-W
, thereby completely canceling the noise. An important observation is that it is much better to measure regularity on the
p
-variation scale rather than in the Hölder scale. Indeed, if
b\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then the drift
\int b(X_{r}),dr
in
Eq(
x;b
)
is only
(1-\frac{Hd}{p})
-Hölder (and this exponent can be arbitrarily small), but it always has finite
1
-variation.

The proof of the lemma strongly relies on the Rosenthal-type stochastic sewing,
Theorem

3.6
, and the integral bounds already obtained in this section.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
m\in[2,\infty)
.
Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function,
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q}
, where
q\in[2,\infty]
,
\alpha<0
. Suppose further that
f
is bounded or
f\geqslant 0
. Let
z\colon[0,1]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a (possibly
non-deterministic
) continuous process adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}

such that

\|\,\|z\|_{1-\textrm{var};[0,1]}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}<\infty.

(4.9)

Let
\tau
be a measurable random variable taking values in
[0,1]
. Assume
that the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{d}{q}\Bigr)H>-1+H,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\alpha>\bigl(\frac{Hd}{q}\wedge\frac{1}{2}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2H}.

(4.11)

Then there exists a constant
C=C(H,d,\alpha,m,q)>0
such that for any
(s,t)\in\Delta_{[0,1]}

Proposition C.2
.

Let
\mu
be a finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose there exists a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of Lebesgue measure
0
and a continuous function
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A}\Bigl|\frac{\mu(\mathbf{B}(x,2^{-k}))}{v_{d}2^{-kd}}-\ell(x)\Bigr|=0.

(C.13)

Then the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and its Radon–Nikodym derivative is given by
d\mu/d\Leb=\ell
.

Before we begin the proof, let us note that the supremum in (
C.13
) is crucial. Indeed, if
\mu=\delta_{0}
, then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{0}
, we obviously have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\delta_{0}(\mathbf{B}(x,2^{-k}))}{v_{d}2^{-kd}}=0,

so an analogue of (
C.13
) without the supremum holds for
\ell(x)\equiv 0
, but
\delta_{0}
is not absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

Step 1
.
We show that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and take
K=K(\varepsilon)>0
large enough so that

\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbf{B}(0,K))<\varepsilon
. Since
\ell
is continuous, it is bounded on
\mathbf{B}(0,K)
. Therefore, recalling (
C.13
), we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbf{B}(0,K)\setminus A\\
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(\mathbf{B}(x,2^{-k}))}{v_{d}2^{-kd}}\leqslant M,

(C.14)

for some constant
M=M(K)
. Since
\Leb(A)=0
, we see that for any
x\in\mathbf{B}(0,K)
,
r\in(0,1/2)
there exists
\widetilde{x}\in\mathbf{B}(0,K)\setminus A
, such that

\mathbf{B}(x,r)\subset\mathbf{B}(\widetilde{x},2r)\subset\mathbf{B}(\widetilde{x},2^{-k})
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is such that
2^{-k-1}<2r\leqslant 2^{-k}
. Therefore, applying (
C.14
), we get

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{B}(0,K),\,r\in(0,1]}\frac{\mu(\mathbf{B}(x,r))}{v_{d}r^{d}}\leqslant 4^{d}M.

(C.15)

Let now
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an arbitrary set of zero Lebesgue measure. Then a set
B\cap\mathbf{B}(0,K)
can be covered by a countable union of balls
\mathbf{B}(x_{i},r_{i})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu_{d}r_{i}^{d}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4^{d}M}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbf{B}(0,K)
. Applying (
C.15
), we deduce

\mu(B)\leqslant\mu(B\cap\mathbf{B}(0,K))+\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbf{B}(0,K))\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(\mathbf{B}(x_{i},r_{i}))+\varepsilon\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we see that
\mu(B)=0
and thus
\mu\ll\Leb
.

Step 2
. Since
\mu\ll\Leb
, by the Radon–Nikodym theorem, for any set
S\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
\mu(S)=\int_{S}\widetilde{\ell}(x)\,dx
for some measurable function
\widetilde{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem implies that for
\Leb
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mu(\mathbf{B}(x,2^{-k}))}{v_{d}2^{-kd}}=\widetilde{\ell}(x).

Comparing this with (
C.13
), we see that
\widetilde{\ell}=\ell
Lebesgue a.e., and thus
d\mu/d\Leb=\ell
.
∎

Proposition C.3
.

Let
\mu
be a finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that
\mu\ll\Leb
and that Radon-Nikodym derivative
\ell:=d\mu/d\Leb
is continuous. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\ell(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mu(\mathbf{B}(x,\varepsilon))}{v_{d}\varepsilon^{d}}.

Proof.

Follows immediately from continuity of
\ell
and the fact that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\inf_{y\in\mathbf{B}(x,\varepsilon)}\ell(y)\leqslant\frac{\mu(\mathbf{B}(x,\varepsilon))}{v_{d}\varepsilon^{d}}\leqslant\sup_{y\in\mathbf{B}(x,\varepsilon)}\ell(y).\qed

Let
