Distances in sparse sets of large Hausdorff dimension
Malabika Pramanik and K S Senthil Raani

Abstract
. The distance set Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) of a set E E consists of all non-negative numbers that represent distances between pairs of points in E E . If E E is quantifiably large, how large is Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) ? How does the structure of E E influence that of Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) ? Questions of this nature play a fundamental role in geometric measure theory. This paper studies sparse (less than full-dimensional) Borel sets in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 with a focus on identifying properties of their distance sets. Our results are of four distinct types. According to a classical result of Steinhaus [ 64 ] , if a Borel set E ⊆ [ 0 , 1 ] d E\subseteq[0,1]^{d} has positive Lebesgue measure, then Δ ⁡ ( E ) ⊇ [ 0 , a ) \Delta(E)\supseteq[0,a) for some a > 0 a>0 . The interval length a a is bounded below by a d d -dependent constant that is uniform in E E , provided the Lebesgue measure of E E is large enough. Our first result (Theorem 1.1 ) generalizes this statement to the setting where E E has high s s -dimensional Hausdorff content (larger than 1 − ρ 1-\rho for some small ρ \rho ), for s s close to but less than d d . For such sets, we show that Δ ⁡ ( E ) ⊇ [ a , b ] \Delta(E)\supseteq[a,b] , where 0 < a < b 0<a<b depend only on d d and ρ \rho . The above result sheds light on the structure of Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) when E E is an arbitrary Borel set of Hausdorff dimension close to d d . In Theorem 1.2 , we show that Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) contains a union of intervals whose lengths are dictated by the sizes of cubes where E E holds high density. This can be viewed as a quantitative version of a result of Mattila and Sj o ¨ \ddot{\text{o}} lin [ 56 ] , which says that int ​ ( Δ ​ ( E ) ) ≠ ∅ \text{int}(\Delta(E))\neq\emptyset if the Hausdorff dimension of E E is larger than ( d + 1 ) / 2 (d+1)/2 . In [ 9 ] , Bourgain proved that for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , any set in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with positive upper density contains all sufficiently large distances. Rice [ 60 ] has shown that the positive density requirement cannot be generalized in an obvious way. As a consequence of Theorem 1.2 , we obtain an alternative size condition (Theorem 1.5 ) for unbounded subsets of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} that guarantees all sufficiently large distances, while admitting a rich class of examples in the sparse setting. Theorem 1.2 can also be used to construct totally disconnected sets of strictly positive codimension that contain all, not just all sufficiently large, distances (Corollary 1.4 ) with infinite multiplicity. The last type of results explores special properties of Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) if E E is assumed to have certain structural regularity in addition to large Hausdorff dimension. The regularity information is harnessed via L 2 L^{2} -Fourier asymptotics of measures supported on E E . In Theorem 1.7 , we show that for sets E E supporting a probability measure whose L 2 L^{2} -Fourier integral has controlled growth, the distribution of points in Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) is dictated by the growth rate. Applications of this phenomenon give new information (Theorem 1.8 ) on the structure of Δ ⁡ ( E ) \Delta(E) when E E is locally uniformly s s -dimensional and quasi-regular, in the terminology of Strichartz [ 65 ] . The main technical novelties of the article lie in the use of a spectral gap condition and L 2 L^{2} -Fourier asymptotics in identifying distances. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first applications of these conditions in distance set problems, and indicates potential for wider usage. Originally introduced by Kuca, Orponen and Sahlsten [ 44 ] in a different context, the spectral gap condition is satisfied by certain measures on all large sets. Fourier-asymptotic growth of measures, on the other hand, carries finer information about the supporting sets. A number of new examples, counterexamples and open problems are discussed.

Introduction

Let
E,F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two Borel sets,
d\geq 1
. Their distance set
\Delta(E,F)
is by definition the collection of all distances generated by connecting points in
E
to points in
F
:

\Delta(E,F):=\Bigl\{|x-y|:x\in E,\;y\in F\Bigr\}=\Bigl\{|z|:z\in E-F\Bigr\}\subseteq[0,\infty).

Here
E-F
is the algebraic difference set, and
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm:

|x|:=(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{ for }\quad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

When
E=F
, the set
\Delta(E,E)
is called the distance set of
E
, denoted
\Delta(E)
for short.

A rich branch of geometric measure theory is devoted to the study of
\Delta(E,F)
given certain largeness assumptions on
E
and
F
. A fundamental and representative example of this is the classical theorem of Steinhaus
[
64
]
on
\mathbb{R}
, and its generalization to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
due to Piccard
[
59
]
. The generalized version states: if
E
and
F
have positive Lebesgue measure, then the difference set
E-F
contains a ball centred at the origin. As a result, the distance set
\Delta(E,F)
also has non-empty interior, and in particular contains an interval of the form
[0,a)
for some
a>0
. Steinhaus’s theorem has led to a vast array of subsequent work concerning
\Delta(E,F)
and other configuration sets of a similar nature involving generalized distance functions. We refer the reader to
[
19
,
56
,
15
,
35
,
25
,
5
,
27
,
58
,
24
,
34
,
41
,

,
7
,
13
,
28
,
52
,
26
,
63
,
20
,
14
]
and their extensive bibliography for significant milestones in the evolution of the subject.

Four distinct but intertwined lines of study concerning distances are central to this article:

•

Quantitative Steinhaus-like theorems for sparse sets,

•

Intervals of distances generated by sets of large Hausdorff dimension,

•

Sparse sets that admit all, or all sufficiently large, distances,

•

Finer structure of distance sets
\Delta(E)
if
E
has additional regularity.

The word “sparse” is used here in a qualitative sense. Depending on the context, it could refer to a set that is Lebesgue-null, or of strictly positive co-dimension, or of zero asymptotic density. Let us describe these four types of problems, the accompanying notions of sparsity and our results in more detail.

1.1.
Quantitative Steinhaus-like theorems for sparse sets

The results of Steinhaus
[
64
]
and Piccard
[
59
]
lead to a natural query: if
E,F
have positive Lebesgue measure, how large can an open set contained in
E-F
or
\Delta(E,F)
be? Following
[
64
,
59
]
and in the special case
d=1
, Boardman
[
8
]
obtains quantitative bounds on the length of an interval contained in
\Delta(E,F)
, depending on the Lebesgue measures of
E,F
. More precisely, given
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1]
the article
[
8
]
provides estimates for

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathtt{a}_{\alpha,\beta}=\inf\{\mathtt{a}(E,F):E,F\subseteq[0,1],\;\lambda_{1}(E)=\alpha,\;\lambda_{1}(F)=\beta\},\;\text{ where }

\displaystyle\mathtt{a}(E,F):=\sup\bigl\{a>0:\Delta(E,F)\supseteq[0,a)\bigr\},

and
\lambda_{1}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. A straightforward generalization of this argument gives the following statement in all dimensions; a proof is given in the appendix Section
10.1
.

Theorem A
.

[
8
, Lemma II]

Let
d\geq 1
. Given any
\rho<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists
c_{\rho}>0
, depending only on
\rho
and
d
, such that for all Borel sets
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with
\min\{\lambda_{d}(E),\lambda_{d}(F)\}\geq 1-\rho
, the following inclusion holds:

E-F\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}]^{d};\quad\text{ as a result }\quad\Delta(E,F)\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}\sqrt{d}].

The constant
c_{\rho}\searrow 0
as
\rho\nearrow\frac{1}{2}
. Here
\lambda_{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

The assumption on the smallness of
\rho
in the statement of Theorem
A
cannot be removed. Indeed, for
d=1
, it has been shown in
[
8
]
that

\mathtt{a}_{\alpha,\beta}=0\text{ if }\alpha+\beta\leq 1,\quad\text{where $\mathtt{a}_{\alpha,\beta}$ is as in \eqref{a-alpha-beta-def}}.

In particular for
\rho\geq\frac{1}{2}
, examples in
[
8
]
show that one
cannot
find a positive
c_{\rho}
with
\Delta(E)\supseteq[0,c_{\rho})
for
all
sets
E\subseteq[0,1]
with
\lambda_{1}(E)=1-\rho
. A similar statement holds in all dimensions if
\lambda_{d}(E)+\lambda_{d}(F)
is small; an example in support of this is provided in Section
10.3
. Unlike
d=1
, the threshold value of
\rho
appears to be unknown for
d\geq 2
.

An important feature of Theorem
A
is the universality of the constant
c_{\rho}
, which works for all sets
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
whose Lebesgue measures exceed
1-\rho>\frac{1}{2}
. There does not appear to be a statement in the literature analogous to Theorem
A
for Lebesgue-null sets. A focus of this article is to provide such a statement for sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, that have Hausdorff dimension less than but sufficiently close to
d
. Our main result in this direction is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

(a)

For
d\geq 2
, there exist dimensional constants
\bar{a}_{d},\bar{b}_{d},\rho_{d},\bar{\varepsilon}_{d}\in(0,1)
as follows. For Borel sets
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with
\min\bigl\{\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(E),\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(F)\bigr\}\geq 1-\rho_{d}
, one has

(1.2)

\Delta(E,F):=\Bigl\{|x-y|:x\in E,y\in F\Bigr\}\supseteq[\bar{a}_{d},\bar{b}_{d}],\quad\text{ provided }s\geq d-\bar{\varepsilon}_{d}.

(b)

For
d\geq 2
, there exists a constant
\rho_{d}\in(0,1)
with the following property. For every
\rho\in(0,\rho_{d}]
, one can find
a_{\rho},b_{\rho},\varepsilon_{\rho}
depending on
\rho
such that for all Borel sets
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
,

(1.3)

\Delta(E,F)\supseteq[a_{\rho},b_{\rho}],\;\text{ provided }\;\min\bigl\{\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(E),\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(F)\bigr\}\geq 1-\rho\text{ and }s\geq d-\varepsilon.

The parameters
a_{\rho},b_{\rho}
and

.

More precisely, there exists a dimensional constant

such that for any

can be chosen to obey

a_{\rho}=\rho^{c_{0}},\quad b_{\rho}=a_{\rho}^{\kappa}=\rho^{c_{0}\kappa},\quad\varepsilon_{\rho}=\frac{c_{0}\rho}{\log(1/\rho)},

provided
\rho
is sufficiently small depending on
\kappa

Here

denotes the

-dimensional dyadic Hausdorff content; its definition has been recalled in Section

1.2.
A quantitative Mattila-Sj
\ddot{\text{o}}
lin theorem

Much of the interest and activity surrounding distance sets stem from Falconer’s distance set conjecture. Originating in
[
19
]
, the conjecture postulates that if
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(E)>d/2
for some Borel set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, then
\Delta(E)
must have positive Lebesgue measure. The definitions of
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathbb{H}^{s}
and Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}
have been briefly recalled in Section
2.1
. An early result of Mattila and Sj
\ddot{\text{o}}
lin
[
56
]
following the work of Falconer
[
19
]
shows that for sets of large enough Hausdorff dimension, the conjecture is true in a stronger sense.

Theorem B
.

[
56
]

For
d\geq 2
, any Borel set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of Hausdorff dimension strictly larger than
(d+1)/2
has a distance set with non-empty interior.

In light of Steinhaus’s result and Theorem
B
, it is tempting to wonder whether
\Delta(E)
might contain intervals specifically of the form
[0,a)
if the Hausdorff dimension
s=\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(E)>(d+1)/2
. This, however, is not true in general even for
s
arbitrarily close to
d
, as has been pointed out in
[
56
]
. This observation is based on an example given in
[
19
, Theorem 2.4]
; see Section
2
for a discussion of this example. Thus an interval in
\Delta(E)
provided by Theorem
B
could in general be of the form
(a,b)
for some
0<a<b
. Further, if
E
has large Hausdorff dimension but not necessarily large Hausdorff content (so that Theorem
1.1
no longer applies), the length
(b-a)
could be arbitrarily small. It is therefore of interest to study the structure of
\Delta(E)
near the origin for general Lebesgue-null sets
E
with large Hausdorff dimension.

While Theorem
B
ensures the existence of an interval in
\Delta(E)
, its proof is qualitative and non-constructive. The proof strategy in
[
56
]
is as follows: if
\mathbb{H}^{s}(E)>0
for some
s>(d+1)/2
, then a certain non-trivial measure supported on
\Delta(E)
admits a density function that is either smooth or H
\ddot{\text{o}}
lder continuous (depending on
s
). As a result, the support
\Delta(E)
must contain an interval. Theorem
B
does not give an indication, based on properties of
E
, which interval(s) might lie in
\Delta(E)
. The number of connected components of
\Delta(E)
, their sizes and their distribution are also not deducible from the proof. Finding a quantitative version of the Mattila-Sj
\ddot{\text{o}}
lin theorem is a second objective of this article. Theorem
1.2
is a structure theorem that specifies the behaviour of distances, near zero, for sets of large Hausdorff dimension; the noteworthy feature is that the intervals of
\Delta(E)
accumulating near the origin depend solely on the sizes of cubes where
E
has large Hausdorff content.

Theorem 1.2
.

Given
d\geq 2
and
\rho\in(0,1)
sufficiently small, there exist constants
0<a_{\rho}<b_{\rho}<1
and
\varepsilon_{\rho}>0
depending only on
d
and
\rho
with the following property.

Let
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
be any Borel set whose
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathbb{H}^{s}(E)
is positive for some
s>d-\varepsilon_{\rho}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(E;s)
denote the collection of all dyadic cubes

contained in
[0,1]^{d}
such that

(1.4)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(E\cap Q)>(1-\rho)\ell(Q)^{s},

where
\ell(Q)
denotes the side-length of the cube
Q
. Then,

(1.5)

\Delta(E)\supseteq\bigcup\Bigl\{\ell(Q)[a_{\rho},b_{\rho}]:\;Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(E;s)\Bigr\}.

The collection

1.3.
Sparse sets containing all sufficiently large distances

Apart from identifying size dependencies in the structure of the distance sets and providing concrete information for specific examples, quantitative statements like Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
have other applications. One such application brings us to the third theme listed on page
1
, namely, investigation of large sets that contain all sufficiently large distances. Local structure theorems for configuration sets can often be utilized to deduce abundance of these configurations in certain large unbounded sets. A simplified example of this phenomenon can be observed in the following easy consequence of Theorem
A
.

Theorem C
.

[
8
]

For any
d\geq 1
, let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set with the property

(1.6)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\lambda_{d}(A\cap Q(x;R))}{R^{d}}>\frac{1}{2},\quad Q(x;R):=x+[0,R]^{d}.

Then
\Delta(A)
contains all distances, i.e.
\Delta(A)=[0,\infty)
.

To clarify, Theorem
A
provides a bound on the size of the distance set associated to a bounded set of positive Lebesgue measure; this leads to a criterion, outlined in Theorem
C
, for an unbounded set to contain all distances. The proof of Theorem
C
follows from Theorem
A
by scaling; see Section
10.1
. A similar argument, included in Section
2.7
, yields a sparse version of this statement as a by-product of Corollary
1.3
.

Corollary 1.4
.

For
d\geq 2
and
s
sufficiently close to
d
, there exists a totally disconnected Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the following properties:

\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(A)=s<d\quad\text{ and }\quad\Delta(A\setminus B(0;R))=[0,\infty)\text{ for every $R>0$.}

In view of Theorem
C
, it is natural to ask about properties of
\Delta(A)
when the
\limsup
in (
1.6
) is less than or equal to
\frac{1}{2}
. An important landmark in this direction is the following result, obtained independently and roughly concurrently by three sets of authors, with three distinct proofs: Bourgain
[
9
]
using harmonic analysis; Falconer and Marstrand
[
21
]
using geometric measure theory; Furstenberg, Katznelson and Weiss
[
23
]
using ergodic theory.

Theorem D

(
[
9
,
21
,
23
]
)
.

Suppose
d\geq 2
, and that
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
has positive upper density, i.e.,

(1.7)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lambda_{d}(A\cap B(0;R))}{\lambda_{d}(B(0;R))}>0,

where
B(x;R)
denotes a ball of radius
R
centred at
x
. Then
A
contains all sufficiently large distances; namely, there exists
R_{0}=R_{0}(A)>0
with the property that for every
R\geq R_{0}
, there exist
x,y\in A
with
|x-y|=R
.

The proof in
[
9
]
, with minor modifications, shows that the same conclusion as Theorem
D
holds if (
1.7
) is replaced by the weaker assumption

(1.8)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x}\frac{\lambda_{d}(A\cap B(x;R))}{\lambda_{d}(B(x;R))}>0,

thus providing a generalization of Theorem
C
. A sketch of this modified proof is given in Section
10.2
.

Conditions (
1.6
), (
1.7
) and (

1.7
) or (
1.8
) fail, but just barely. A possible example of marginal failure could be extremely slow decay in
R
of the expressions occurring in (
1.7
) or (
1.8
), as
R\rightarrow\infty
. A strong negative answer to this intuitive query has been obtained by Rice
[
60
]
. For
d\geq 2
and any

with
\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}f(R)=0
, the article
[
60
]
gives a construction of a set

and a sequence

such that

\frac{\lambda_{d}(A\cap B(0;R_{n}))}{\lambda_{d}(B(0;R_{n}))}\geq f(R_{n})\text{ for all $n$, }\quad\text{ and yet }\quad\Delta(A)\cap\{R_{n}:n\geq 1\}=\emptyset.

The same set
A
continues to serve as a counterexample if the slow decay of the quantity in (
1.7
) is replaced by that in (
1.8
).

The example in
[
60
]
also precludes the possibility of a result like Theorem

if the measure
\lambda_{d}
in (
1.7
) is replaced by
s
-dimensional dyadic Hausdorff content for

; indeed

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(A\cap B(0;R))}{\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(B(0;R))}>0\quad\text{ for the set $A$ constructed in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{2020Rice}{}{}]}. }

\displaystyle A_{R}(x)

\displaystyle Q(x;R)

\displaystyle:=x+[0,R]^{d}\text{ and }\mathbb{T}_{x,R}(y):=\frac{y-x}{R},

. For
\rho\in(0,1)

1.4.
The role of Fourier asymptotics in distance sets

The information provided by Theorems
1.2
and
1.1
is universal, in the sense that the intervals in
\Delta(E)
ensured by these results apply to any set
E
with high enough Hausdorff dimension (Theorem
1.2
) or Hausdorff content (Theorem
1.1
). The identifiers of these intervals, namely the sizes of high-density cubes of
E
, are in general arbitrary. However, if additional properties of
E
are available, one may extract further information about the structure of
\Delta(E)
, addressing finer questions. For example, what is the distribution or separation of points in
\Delta(E)
? Does the existence of a point in
\Delta(E)
ensure that there are other points in
\Delta(E)
nearby? It turns out that such problems can be explored via
L^{2}
-Fourier asymptotics of measures supported on
E
. The following theorem is a first result in this direction.

Given a probability measure
\mu
, let us denote by

(1.14)

\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(T):=\int_{|\xi|\leq T}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,d\xi

the partial
L^{2}
-Fourier integral of
\mu
. If
E
is Lebesgue-null, then
\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(T)\nearrow\infty
as
T\rightarrow\infty
for any probability measure supported on
E
. Conversely, if
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
has positive Lebesgue measure, then
\{\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(T):T\geq 1\}
is bounded for
\mu=\mathbf{1}_{E}/\lambda_{d}(E)
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
d\geq 2
. For every choice of constants
c\in(0,\frac{d-1}{4})
,
T_{0}\geq 1
and
M\geq 4
, there exists a positive constant
\delta_{M}
(depending only on these quantities) as follows.

Suppose that
\mu
is a probability measure supported on a set
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
, whose
L^{2}
-Fourier integral obeys the growth condition

(1.15)

\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(T_{1}T_{2})\leq T_{1}^{c}\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(T_{2})\quad\text{ for all }T_{1},T_{2}\geq T_{0}.

If there exists
\delta\in(0,\delta_{M})
and a finite sequence
\{R_{j}:1\leq j\leq J\}\subseteq(T_{0},\infty)
satisfying

(1.16)

R_{j+1}\geq\delta^{-2}R_{j}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(R_{j})>\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{M}\Bigr)\mathbb{F}_{\mu}\bigl(\delta^{-2}R_{J}\bigr),

then at least one of the points
\{\delta R_{j}^{-1}:1\leq j\leq J\}
must lie in
\Delta(E)
.

1.6.
Proof overview

The conceptual novelty of the article lies in:

•

Identifying Hausdorff content and Fourier asymptotics as quantitative indicators of distances, the former for identifying intervals in
\Delta(E)
(Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
), the latter for distribution of points in
\Delta(E)
(Theorems
1.7
,
1.8
);

•

Using local structure theorems for distance sets to make statements about abundance of distances in unbounded sets (Theorems
1.5
,
1.6
, Corollary
1.4
).

The methodological novelty involves:

•

Use of a spectral gap condition in identifying distances; specific details appear in Propositions
6.1
-
6.4
, and their proofs in Sections
7
and
8
). Such a condition first appeared in the work of Kuca, Orponen and Sahlsten
[
44
]
in connection with a continuous Sárk
\ddot{\text{o}}
zy type problem, unrelated to the distance set problem considered in this paper; see also an extension of
[
44
]
by Bruce and the first author
[
10
]
. The essential role played by the spectral gap condition in these widely disparate settings indicates potential for further usage.

•

Hands-on analysis of specific sets; combined with the main theorems, they provide new examples and counter-examples of distance phenomena in the sparse setting: All these examples appear in Section
2
, and may be of independent interest.

Theorems
1.2
,
1.5
and
1.6
are direct consequences of Theorem
1.1
; Theorem
1.8
follows from Theorem
1.7
. The inter-dependencies of various sections are presented in Figure
1
.

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.7
exploit a common strategy, widely used in the literature. They all involve estimation of a configuration integral, defined as
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
in Section
5
. This integral, if nonzero, signals the existence of points
x\in\text{supp}(\mu),y\in\text{supp}(\nu)
with
|x-y|=t
. Versions of this integral have appeared in previous work
[
9
,
56
]
. It admits a Fourier representation, given in (
5.6
), that is useful for estimation. The non-vanishing of the integral is determined by analysing its size on three mutually exclusive and exhaustive domains of integration - low, medium and high frequencies. The dominant contribution to the integral in (
5.6
) comes from low frequencies, where all the factors in the integrand take large values close to 1. The contribution from high frequencies is small, by virtue of convergence of the integral, and from well-known Fourier decay estimates of the spherical measure
\sigma
. The main challenge is to control the contribution from intermediate frequencies. Indeed, we do not know how to control this term for a general measure. The distinctive feature of our proof is the construction of a special measure that is supported on the set of interest and enjoys a spectral gap property for which this term can be suitably controlled.

2.1.
Preliminaries

2.1.1.
Hausdorff dimension and content

For any Borel set
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
and
s\in(0,d]
, the standard
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure

\mathbb{H}^{s}(E)
[
55
, Chapter 2]
is defined as:

(2.1)

\mathbb{H}^{s}(E):=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0+}\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\bigl[\text{diam}(E_{i})\bigr]^{s}:E\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i},\;\text{diam}(E_{i})\leq r\Bigr\},

where the infimum is taken over all countable covers of
E
consisting of sets
E_{i}
with diameter at most
r
. It is well-known that the
Hausdorff dimension
of
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by

(2.2)

\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(E)=\inf\bigl\{s:\mathbb{H}^{s}(E)=0\bigr\}=\sup\bigl\{s:\mathbb{H}^{s}(E)=\infty\bigr\}.

The notions of
s
-dimensional
dyadic Hausdorff measure and dyadic Hausdorff content of
E
are closely related to the standard Hausdorff measures:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0+}\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\ell(Q_{i})^{s}:E\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}Q_{i},\;Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}_{d},\ell(Q_{i})\leq r\Bigr\},\noindent

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(E)

\displaystyle:=\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\ell(Q_{i})^{s}:E\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}Q_{i},\;Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}_{d}\Bigr\}.

Here
\mathcal{Q}_{d}
denotes the collection of all closed dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{d}
, we write
Q=c(Q)+\ell(Q)[0,1]^{d}
, so that
c(Q)
is the vertex of
Q
which is smallest in every coordinate, and
\ell(Q)
denotes its side-length. It follows from the definitions that for any set
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
,

•

the quantities
\mathbb{H}^{s}(E),\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)\in[0,\infty]
, while
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(E)\in[0,1]
;

•

these three quantities are either all zero or all positive.

In particular, the definition (
2.2
) of Hausdorff dimension remains the same if
\mathbb{H}^{s}
is replaced by
\mathcal{H}^{s}
. These facts will be used throughout the paper without further reference.

2.1.2.
Definitions associated with Fourier asymptotics

Let us also recall some relevant terminology from
[
65
]
that appeared in Section
1.4
and
1.5
. A probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]^{d}
is
locally uniformly
s
-dimensional
if there is a constant
C>0
such that

(2.5)

\mu\bigl(Q\bigr)\leq C\ell(Q)^{s}\quad\text{ for all cubes }Q\subseteq[0,1]^{d}.

The definition implies that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. We will say that an
s
-dimensional set
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
is
quasi-regular
if there exists
\kappa>0
such that

(2.6)

\liminf_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{(2r)^{s}}\mathbb{H}^{s}\bigl(E\cap B(x;r)\bigr)\geq\kappa\text{ for $\mathbb{H}^{s}$-almost every $x\in E$}.

2.2.
Notation

In the sequel, we will occasionally use
\ll,\gg,\sim
to denote size relationships between two quantities. These are to be interpreted as follows:
A\ll B
, which is equivalent to
B\gg A
, implies the existence of a positive constant
C_{d}
, depending only on dimension, such that
A\leq C_{d}B
. We write
A\sim B
if there exists
C_{d}>1
such that
C_{d}^{-1}\leq B/A\leq C_{d}
.

2.3.
A building block example

Let us fix an exponent
s\in(0,d)

For ease of exposition, we will assume that

To start off, let us record that

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}-\mathtt{F}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{\frac{p-p^{\prime}}{q}+q^{-\sigma}[-1,1]^{d}\;\Bigl|\;p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}\right\}

Since the cube

\mathtt{Q}^{\ast}[p;q^{-\sigma}]

|p|/q

\displaystyle\mathtt{J}_{r}\subseteq|\mathtt{Q}^{\ast}[p;q^{-\sigma}]|\subseteq\mathtt{I}_{r},\text{ where }r=|p|,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{r}:=\frac{r}{q}+\gamma_{d}^{-1}q^{-\sigma}\bigl[-1,1\bigr],\quad\mathtt{J}_{r}:=\frac{r}{q}+\gamma_{d}q^{-\sigma}\bigl[-1,1\bigr].

can be written in terms of these constituent intervals (not necessarily disjoint), as follows:

coalesce to generate a much larger interval, whose length is bounded below by a fixed dimensional constant. The interval

2.4.
A counterpoint to Rice’s example

In Section

, we noted an example due to Rice
[
60

, which shows that given any

of infinite Lebesgue measure that avoids infinitely many large distances, for which the positivity criteria (

There exists a set of infinitesimally small Lebesgue measure whose connected components can be made to shrink arbitrarily fast at infinity, yet which manages to capture all distances outside every compact set. The requirement of small connected components is to rule out trivial examples, such as lines or curves; the set
A
we construct is totally disconnected at infinity. In particular, the criteria (

) and (
1.8
) fail in a very strong sense for such a set. Not only is its upper density zero, its density on any ball of radius

Lemma 2.4

The proof is a small variation on the example in Section

\displaystyle\sup\{x_{1}:x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in A_{n}\}

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{j}=|x-y|

\displaystyle\geq 10d|\mathtt{d}_{n}-\mathtt{d}_{m}|-|p_{x}-p_{y}|-|u_{x}-u_{y}|

\displaystyle\geq 10d\mathtt{d}_{n}-10d\mathtt{d}_{m}-2\sqrt{d}\mathtt{d}_{n}-2\sqrt{d}\kappa_{m}>\mathtt{d}_{n},

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{j}=|x-y|

\displaystyle\leq 10d|\mathtt{d}_{n}-\mathtt{d}_{m}|+\bigl|p_{x}-p_{y}\bigr|+\bigl|u_{x}-u_{y}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq 20d\mathtt{d}_{n}+2\sqrt{d}\mathtt{d}_{n}+2\kappa_{m}\sqrt{d}<100d^{2}\mathtt{d}_{n}.

In view of the previous deduction

Sets

•

However, Lemma

2.6.2.
Locally uniform and quasi-regular sets

Fix
\sigma=d/s
a rational number close to but larger than 1, and integers
q,q^{\sigma}\in\{2^{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Setting
\mathbb{Z}_{q}:=\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}
as before, we use the sets
\mathtt{F}[q]
in (
2.7
) as building blocks to define a sparser set
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
as follows:

(2.25)

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}^{\ast}

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathtt{E}_{n},\quad\text{ where }\mathtt{E}_{1}:=\mathtt{F}[q],\;\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{n}=\mathtt{E}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup\Bigl\{\frac{p}{q}+q^{-\sigma}\mathtt{E}_{n-1}:\;p\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}\Bigr\}

(2.26)

\displaystyle\;=\Bigl\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{p_{k}}{q^{(k-1)\sigma+1}}+q^{-n\sigma}[0,1]^{d}:p_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}\Bigr\}\;\text{ for }n\geq 2.

In other words,
\mathtt{E}_{n}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
is a
q^{nd}
-fold disjoint union of basic (dyadic) cubes of sidelength
q^{-n\sigma}
. Given
\mathtt{E}_{n}

\mathtt{E}_{n+1}
is obtained by inserting an affine copy of
\mathtt{E}_{1}
in each basic cube of
\mathtt{E}_{n}

\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
is a bounded self-similar set of Hausdorff dimension
s
for any choice of

[
65
]
, and therefore locally uniformly
s

[
65

. The construction ensures it is totally disconnected. Let us first record the Hausdorff dimension and content of
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any rational
\sigma>1
and integers
q,q^{\sigma}
that are powers of 2, the following conclusions hold for the set
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}[q]
as in (
2.25

(2.27)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ for }\tau>s,\\
1&\text{ for }\tau\leq s.\end{cases}

In particular,
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=s
. Moreover, for
\tau\leq s
, and any

,

(2.28)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\cap Q)=\ell(Q)^{\tau}\text{ for every basic cube $Q\subseteq\mathtt{E}_{n}$, $\ell(Q)=q^{-n\sigma}$.}

Proof.

Constructions of the type (
2.25
) are ubiquitous in fractal geometry. Methods for computing their Hausdorff dimension and measure may be found in standard textbooks,
[
17
, Chapters 2, 3, 4]
or
[
18
, Section 1.5]
. We only sketch the proof of the lemma, referring to the existing literature for further details. For instance,
[
18
, Theorem 1.15]
shows that

\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=s\;\text{ and }\;\mathbb{H}^{s}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=1,\;\text{ and hence }\mathbb{H}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=0\text{ for $\tau>s$.}

As mentioned in Section
2.1
, the quantities
\mathbb{H}^{\tau}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\cdot)
vanish simultaneously, resulting in
\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=0
for

. On the other hand
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
is self-similar, hence the results of
[
22
]
imply that the Hausdorff measure and Hausdorff content of
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
match at the critical dimension; namely,
\mathbb{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=1
. Consequently,
1=\mathbb{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})\leq\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})\leq\mathbb{H}^{s}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=1
. Combining with the relation
1=\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})\leq\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})\leq 1
leads to
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=1
for
\tau\leq s
. This proves (

).

We turn now to (

). The self-similar structure of
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
implies that for any basic cube

with
\ell(Q)=q^{-n\sigma}
and
\mathbb{T}_{Q}(x):=(x-c(Q))/\ell(Q)
we have

\mathbb{T}_{Q}\bigl[\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\cap Q\bigr]=\mathtt{E}^{\ast},\;\text{ therefore }\;\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\cap Q)=\ell(Q)^{\tau}\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=\ell(Q)^{\tau}.

The last step follows from (
2.27
) for
\tau\leq s
, and the translation and scaling properties of

, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

The relation (
2.28
) says that

has full density in every basic cube of
\mathtt{E}_{n}
. Each such cube (which has sidelength
q^{-n\sigma}
) therefore lies in
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})
for every

. Theorem

states:

(2.29)

\Delta(\mathtt{E}^{\ast}[q;\sigma])\supseteq\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{-n\sigma}[a_{\rho},b_{\rho}]\quad\text{ provided }s\in(d-\varepsilon_{\rho},d).

2.6.3.
Locally uniform and quasi-regular sets with and without the Steinhaus property

We will say that a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the
Steinhaus property
if there exists
a>0
such that
[0,a)\subseteq\Delta(E)
. Corollary
1.3
claims the existence of a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
of Hausdorff dimension
s<d
that has the Steinhaus property. We prove this corollary in this section. The set
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
defined in (
2.25
) does not have the Steinhaus property but it is possible to create one that does using finitely many disjoint affine copies of it.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix
\sigma>1,\sigma\in\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose that
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}=\mathtt{E}^{\ast}[q,\sigma]
is as in (
2.25
), where
q
is an integer power of 2 that is large enough to satisfy
c_{0}q^{\sigma-1}\gg 1
, with
c_{0}
being the absolute constant specified in Lemma
2.1
.
Then

\Delta(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})\not\supseteq[0,a)\quad\text{ for any $a>0$}.

Proof.

The main observation is that
\mathtt{E}_{n}
is a disjoint union of cubes of the form
p/q_{n}+q_{n}^{-\sigma_{n}}[0,1]^{d}
,
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{q_{n}}^{d}
, and hence

(2.30)

\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathtt{E}_{n}\subseteq\mathtt{F}[q_{n};\sigma_{n}],\;\text{ with }q_{n}=q^{(n-1)\sigma+1},\;\sigma_{n}=\frac{n\sigma}{(n-1)\sigma+1}\;\text{ so that }q_{n}^{\sigma_{n}}=q^{n\sigma}.

Here
\mathtt{F}[q;\sigma]
denotes the building block set (
2.7
). Let us note that
\sigma_{n}>1
for all
n\geq 1
, so Lemma
2.1
applies. Further,
q_{n}^{\sigma_{n}-1}=q^{\sigma-1}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}-2=\frac{n\sigma}{(n-1)\sigma+1}-2=-\frac{(n-2)\sigma+2}{(n-1)\sigma+1},\quad\text{ so that }

\displaystyle q_{n}^{\sigma_{n}-2}=q^{-(n-2)\sigma-2}\longrightarrow 0\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Given any
a>0
, we can therefore choose
n
such that
c_{0}q_{n}^{\sigma_{n}-2}=c_{0}q^{-(n-2)\sigma-2}<a
. It follows from (
2.30
) that
\Delta(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})\subseteq\Delta(\mathtt{F}[q_{n};\sigma_{n}])
for every
n\geq 1
. The assumption
c_{0}q_{n}^{\sigma_{n}-1}=c_{0}q^{\sigma-1}\gg 1
ensures that Lemma
2.1
(
a
) is applicable. It implies that
\Delta(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})
is contained in a disjoint union of intervals of length
q_{n}^{-\sigma_{n}}=q^{-n\sigma}
lying in
[0,c_{0}q_{n}^{\sigma_{n}-2}]
. The proof now follows exactly in the same way as Lemma
2.5
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Given
d\geq 2
and
\rho>0
, let
\varepsilon_{\rho},a_{\rho},b_{\rho}
be as in Theorem
1.2
. For
s\in(d-\varepsilon_{\rho},d)\cap\mathbb{Q}
, let us choose an integer
q
with
q,q^{\sigma}\in\{2^{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
q^{\sigma}>b_{\rho}/a_{\rho}
, where
\sigma=d/s
.

Let
m
be the largest integer such that
q^{\sigma}>(b_{\rho}/a_{\rho})^{m}
. Then there exist
\{x_{k}:0\leq k\leq m\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{u_{k}:0\leq k\leq m\}\subseteq(0,\infty)
such that the compact and totally disconnected set

(2.31)

\mathtt{K}^{\ast}:=\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{m}\bigl(x_{k}+u_{k}\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\bigr),\text{ which has }\quad\text{dim}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathtt{K}^{\ast})=s

also has the Steinhaus property, i.e.,
\Delta(\mathtt{K}^{\ast})\supseteq[0,a)
for some
a>0
. In particular, Corollary
1.3
holds.

Proof.

Set
x_{0}=0
,
u_{0}=1
. The dilation factors
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
will be determined shortly, but given these, the translation parameters
x_{k}
are chosen so that the cubes
Q(x_{k};u_{k})=x_{k}+[0,u_{k}]^{d}
are disjoint for
0\leq k\leq m
. Since
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
,
the choice of
u
and
x
shows that
\mathtt{K}^{\ast}
is the disjoint union of
(m+1)
affine copies of the compact set
\mathtt{E}^{\ast}
. As a result,

\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathtt{K}^{\ast})=\max_{0\leq k\leq m}\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(u_{k}\mathtt{E}^{\ast}+x_{k})=\dim_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathtt{E}^{\ast})=s.

This establishes the statement about the size of
\mathtt{K}^{\ast}
.

We turn now to the statement about the distance set of
\mathtt{K}^{\ast}
. Let us set
\mathtt{I}_{n}:=q^{-n\sigma}[a_{\rho},b_{\rho}]
. Applying (
2.29
), we note that

(2.32)

\Delta(\mathtt{K}^{\ast})\supseteq\bigcup_{k=0}^{m}\Delta(u_{k}\mathtt{E}^{\ast}+x_{k})\supseteq\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{k=0}^{m}u_{k}\mathtt{I}_{n}\supseteq\bigcup\bigl\{\mathtt{I}_{n}\cup u_{k}\mathtt{I}_{n}:n\geq 0,1\leq k\leq m\bigr\}.

Given the integer
m
as in the statement of the lemma, we choose the scale parameters
\{u_{k}:1\leq k\leq m\}
in the following way

(2.33)

u_{1}=\frac{b_{\rho}}{a_{\rho}}q^{-\sigma},\quad u_{2}=u_{1}\frac{b_{\rho}}{a_{\rho}}=\Bigl(\frac{b_{\rho}}{a_{\rho}}\Bigr)^{2}q^{-\sigma},\quad\ldots,\quad u_{m}=u_{m-1}\frac{b_{\rho}}{a_{\rho}}=\Bigl(\frac{b_{\rho}}{a_{\rho}}\Bigr)^{m}q^{-\sigma}.

The main claim concerning the intervals
\mathtt{I}_{n}
is the following:

(2.34)

\mathtt{I}_{n+1}\cup u_{1}\mathtt{I}_{n}\cup\cdots\cup u_{m}\mathtt{I}_{n}\cup\mathtt{I}_{n}\supseteq\text{convex hull$(\mathtt{I}_{n}\cup\mathtt{I}_{n+1})$}=\bigl[q^{-(n+1)\sigma}a_{\rho},q^{-n\sigma}b_{\rho}\bigr].

In other words, the intervals
\{u_{k}\mathtt{I}_{n}:1\leq k\leq m\}
fill the gap between
\mathtt{I}_{n}
and
\mathtt{I}_{n+1}
, with the right endpoint of one interval lining up with the left endpoint of the next. Since the intervals
\bigl[q^{-(n+1)\sigma}a_{\rho},q^{-n\sigma}b_{\rho}\bigr]
are overlapping, combining (
2.34
) and (
2.32
) leads to

\Delta(\mathtt{K}^{\ast})\supseteq\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigl[q^{-(n+1)\sigma}a_{\rho},q^{-n\sigma}b_{\rho}\bigr]=(0,b_{\rho}],

which is the Steinhaus property asserted in the statement of the lemma. It remains to justify the claim (
2.34
). Let
\mathscr{L}(\mathtt{I})
and
\mathscr{R}(\mathtt{I})
denote the left and right endpoints respectively of an interval
\mathtt{I}
. The choice (
2.33
) of
a_{\rho},b_{\rho}
,
q
and
u_{1}
ensure that

\mathscr{R}(\mathtt{I}_{n+1})=b_{\rho}q^{-(n+1)\sigma}=u_{1}a_{\rho}q^{-n\sigma}=\mathscr{L}(u_{1}\mathtt{I}_{n})<a_{\rho}q^{-n\sigma}=\mathscr{L}(\mathtt{I}_{n}).

In general for
1\leq k\leq m-1
, the assumption
q^{\sigma}>(b_{\rho}/a_{\rho})^{k+1}
implies that

\mathscr{R}(u_{k}\mathtt{I}_{n})=u_{k}b_{\rho}q^{-n\sigma}=u_{k+1}a_{\rho}q^{-n\sigma}=\mathscr{L}(u_{k+1}\mathtt{I}_{n})<a_{\rho}q^{-n\sigma}=\mathscr{L}(\mathtt{I}_{n}).

However, the choice of
m
ensures that
q^{\sigma}\leq(b_{\rho}/a_{\rho})^{m+1}
, as a result of which
\mathscr{R}(u_{m}\mathtt{I}_{n})=u_{m}b_{\rho}q^{-n\sigma}\geq q^{-n\sigma}a_{\rho}=\mathscr{L}(\mathtt{I}_{n})
.
This completes the proof of the claim (
2.34
).
∎

Sets of the form (

Proof of Corollary

As in Proposition
2.3

(2.35)

5.
An integral that identifies distances

Given two compact sets
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
, we will determine the distances generated by
E
and
F
using a certain linear functional, as in
[
9
]
. Such functionals are often called configuration integrals. In this section, we will set up the configuration integral that is relevant for identifying a specific distance
t
, and discuss its properties.

Given probability measures
\mu,\nu
supported on the compact sets
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
respectively, and any
t>0
, we define a linear functional
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
as follows: for any continuous function
f\in C\bigl([0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}\bigr)
,

(5.1)

\langle\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t),f\rangle:=\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0}\int\mu_{\kappa}(x)\nu_{\kappa}(x+t\omega)f(x,x+t\omega)dxd\sigma(\omega),

with the notational convention
\Lambda_{\mu}:=\Lambda_{\mu,\mu}
. Here
\sigma
denotes the normalized surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
\mu_{\kappa}:=\mu\ast\varphi_{\kappa}
for an approximate identity sequence
\{\varphi_{\kappa}:\kappa>0\}
,
\varphi_{\kappa}(x)=\kappa^{-d}\varphi(x/\kappa)
, where
\varphi
is a non-negative Schwartz function with
\widehat{\varphi}(0)=1
. The following proposition verifies that under certain conditions,
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
is well-defined as a bounded linear functional and an effective instrument for identifying distances realized by joining points in
E
with points in
F
.

Let us recall from
[
55
, Chapters 2 and 3, Theorems 2.8 and 3.10]
the definition of
s
-energy of a measure
\mu
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
: for
0<s\leq d
,

(5.2)

\mathbb{I}_{\mu}(s):=\iint|x-y|^{-s}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=\gamma(d,s)\int\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}|\xi|^{s-d}d\xi

where
\gamma(d,s)=\pi^{s-d/2}\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})/\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})
is a positive finite constant.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
are two probability measures, supported on the compact sets
E
and
F
respectively, each wih finite
(d+1)/2
-energy:

(5.3)

\mathbb{I}_{\mu}((d+1)/2)<\infty,\quad\mathbb{I}_{\nu}((d+1)/2)<\infty.

Then the following conclusions hold.

(a)

For every continuous function
f
on
[0,1]^{2d}
, the functional
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
is well-defined; in other words, the limit in (
5.1
) exists and is independent of the choice of the approximate identity
\{\varphi_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon>0\}
. Moreover, there exists an absolute constant
C_{d}
depending only on
d
such that

(5.4)

\bigl|\langle\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t),f\rangle\bigr|\leq C_{d}t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\bigl[\mathbb{I}_{\mu}((d+1)/2)\times\mathbb{I}_{\nu}((d+1)/2)\bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}||f||_{\infty}.

Thus
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
can be identified as a non-negative finite measure.

(b)

If the measure
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
is non-trivial (i.e., has positive mass), it is supported on

(5.5)

X_{\mu}(t):=\bigl\{(x,y)\in E\times F:|x-y|=t\bigr\}.

In this case, the total mass of
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
is given by

(5.6)

||\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)||=\int\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}\widehat{\sigma}(t\xi)\,d\xi>0.

Conversely, if the integral in (
5.6
) s strictly positive, then
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}(t)
is a non-trivial measure, and hence its support
X_{\mu}(t)
is non-empty; in other words,
t\in\Delta(E,F)
.

5.1.
Existence of
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists an absolute positive constant
C_{d}
as follows. Given
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in(0,1)
,
\beta\geq 0
, and a probability measure
\mu
, the integral
\mathscr{I}(a,t;\mu)
defined by

(5.7)

\mathscr{I}(a,t;\mu):=\int\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,\bigl(1+t|a+\xi|\bigr)^{-\beta}\,d\xi

obeys the bound

(5.8)

\bigl|\mathscr{I}(a,t;\mu)\bigr|\leq C_{d}\bigl[|a|^{d}+t^{-\beta}\mathbb{I}_{\mu}(d-{\beta})\bigr].

Proof.

Let us write
\mathscr{I}=\mathscr{I}_{1}+\mathscr{I}_{2}
, where
\mathscr{I}_{1}
represents the integral over
\{\xi:|a+\xi|\leq|\xi|/2\}
. Since
|\xi|\leq|\xi+a|+|a|\leq|\xi|/2+|a|
, the domain of integration of

is contained in

. The integrand is trivially bounded above by 1. These observations lead to

(5.9)

\mathscr{I}_{1}(a,t;\mu):=\int_{|a+\xi|\leq|\xi|/2}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,\bigl(1+t|a+\xi|\bigr)^{-\beta}\,d\xi\leq\int_{|\xi|\leq 2|a|}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}d\xi\leq(2|a|)^{d}.

The remaining integral
\mathscr{I}_{2}
, whose domain of integration is

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{2}(a,t;\mu)

\displaystyle:=\int_{|a+\xi|\geq|\xi|/2}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,\bigl(1+t|a+\xi|\bigr)^{-\beta}\,d\xi

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq\int\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\bigl(t|\xi|/2\bigr)^{-\beta}\,d\xi\leq C_{d}t^{-\beta}\mathbb{I}_{\mu}(d-\beta).

Combining (

) and (

).
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Under the finite energy assumptions (

) and for any smooth function

supported on

in the approximate identity

Proof.

Given a smooth function

of compact support, let us define,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}^{\ast}(t;f,\kappa)

\displaystyle:=\int\mu_{\kappa}(x)\nu_{\kappa}(x+t\omega)f(x,x+t\omega)dxd\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\iiint\widehat{f}\bigl(\xi,\eta\bigr)\,\widehat{\mu}_{\kappa}(-\xi-\eta-\zeta)\,\widehat{\nu}_{\kappa}(\zeta)\,\widehat{\sigma}(t(\eta+\zeta))\,d\xi\,d\eta\,d\zeta,

where the last step follows from Fourier inversion. We will derive the existence of the limit in (

from the dominated convergence theorem. In view of

\widehat{\mu}_{\kappa}(\xi)=\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\widehat{\varphi}(\kappa\xi)\longrightarrow\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\text{ as }\kappa\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\bigl|\widehat{\mu}_{\kappa}(\xi)\bigr|\leq||\widehat{\varphi}||_{\infty}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|,

we are led to verify the absolute convergence of the following integral:

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}^{\ast}:=

\displaystyle\iint\bigl|\widehat{f}(\xi,\eta)\bigr|\Bigl[\int\bigl|\widehat{\nu}(\zeta)\bigr|\,\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(-\xi-\eta-\zeta)\bigr|\bigl(1+t|\eta+\zeta|)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\,d\zeta\Bigr]\,d\xi\,d\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint\bigl|\widehat{f}(\xi,\eta)\bigr|\mathscr{J}(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta.

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(\xi,\eta):=

\displaystyle\int\bigl|\widehat{\nu}(\zeta)\bigr|\,\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(-\xi-\eta-\zeta)\bigr|\bigl(1+t|\eta+\zeta|)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\,d\zeta

\displaystyle\bigl[\mathscr{I}(\eta,t;\nu)\bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\bigl[\mathscr{I}(\xi,t;\mu)\bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}

(5.12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{d}\bigl[|\xi|^{d}+t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathbb{I}_{\mu}((d+1)/2)\bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\bigl[|\eta|^{d}+t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathbb{I}_{\nu}((d+1)/2)\bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

\mathfrak{D}_{1}:=\bigl\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:t|\xi|\leq\delta\},\;\mathfrak{D}_{2}:=\bigl\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\delta<t|\xi|\leq t^{-N+1}\},\;\mathfrak{D}_{3}:=\bigl\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:t|\xi|>t^{-N+1}\}.

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathfrak{I}_{1}(t)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{1}}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\widehat{\sigma}(t\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{1}}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,d\xi-\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{1}}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\bigl|1-\widehat{\sigma}(t\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{(}1-\delta)\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(\delta t^{-1})\geq\mathbb{(}1-\delta)\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(\delta b^{-1}).

The last two steps in the above sequence use the following estimate for

\displaystyle\bigl|1-\widehat{\sigma}(t\xi)\bigr|=\Bigl|\int\bigl(1-e^{-itx\cdot\xi}\bigr)d\sigma(x)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\delta\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|x|d\sigma(x)\leq\delta\quad\text{ if }t|\xi|\leq\delta,

and the inclusion

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{2}}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\bigl|\widehat{\sigma}(t\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi

in the third step of the display above. Finally we consider

and the finite energy condition (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}_{3}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{3}}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}(t|\xi|)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{\mathfrak{D}_{3}}\bigl|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}|\xi|^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}|\xi|^{-\alpha}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq t^{N\alpha-\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathbb{I}_{\mu}\bigl(\frac{d+1}{2}+\alpha\bigr)\leq C_{0}b^{N\alpha-\frac{d-1}{2}}.

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(\delta b^{-1})-a-C_{0}b^{N\alpha-\frac{d-1}{2}}

provided (

) holds. Here

\displaystyle\bigl|1-\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int\bigl(1-e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\bigr)d\mu(x)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl|\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|x||\xi|d\mu(x)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{ for }|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\leq\delta b^{-1},\text{ so that }

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(\delta b^{-1})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{F}_{\mu}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\Bigr)\geq\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\frac{\omega_{d}}{2^{d}d^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Bigr)=4c_{d}.

Lemma 8.3

We continue to part (

\displaystyle\bigl|\psi(x)-\psi(0)\bigr|=\Bigl|\int_{B(0;2)}\widehat{\psi}(\xi)(e^{ix\cdot\xi}-1)\,d\xi\Bigr|

\displaystyle\hskip 65.04256pt\leq|x|\int_{B(0;2)}\widehat{\psi}(\xi)|\xi|d\xi\leq 2|x|\psi(0)\leq\frac{\psi(0)}{2}\text{ if }|x|\leq\frac{1}{4},

Proposition 9.1

\{\mathfrak{G}_{2}(t_{j}):1\leq j\leq J\}
, as a result of which

\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{J}\mathfrak{G}_{2}(t_{j})\subseteq\bigl\{\xi:|\xi|\leq\bigl(\delta t_{J}\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\}.

This disjointness is a consequence of the requirement
\delta^{-1}t_{j}^{-1}=\delta^{-2}R_{j}\leq R_{j+1}=\delta t_{j+1}^{-1}
, as per (
1.16
). The relation (
9.13
) contradicts the second criterion in (
1.16
), proving the proposition. The positivity of the integral
\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(\delta t_{j}^{-1})=\mathbb{F}_{\mu}(R_{j})
claimed in (
9.2
) follows from the fact that
R_{j}\geq T_{0}\geq 1
, and
\xi\mapsto\widehat{\mu}(\xi)
is continuous, with
\widehat{\mu}(0)=1
.
∎

10.
Appendix: A few proofs and counterexamples

Certain facts were used without proof in earlier sections, most of them likely well-known to experts. This section provides more detailed explanation for the statements for which we could not find an easily citable reference.

10.1.
Sets of large Lebesgue measure have large distance sets

Proofs of Theorems
A
and
C
.

Let us start with the proof of Theorem
A
, which is based on the argument in
[
8
, Lemma II]
. Suppose
E,F\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
,
\min\{\lambda_{d}(E),\lambda_{d}(F)\}\geq 1-\rho>\frac{1}{2}
. Since
2(1-\rho)>1
, we can choose a constant
c_{\rho}>0
small enough so that
(1+c_{\rho})^{d}<2(1-\rho)
. Then for any
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,c_{\rho}]^{d}
,

\displaystyle(x+F)\cup E\subseteq\prod_{j=1}^{d}\bigl[0,1+x_{j}\bigr]\subseteq\prod_{j=1}^{d}[0,1+c_{\rho}]\;\text{ and hence }

\displaystyle\lambda_{d}\bigl[(x+F)\cup E\bigr]\leq(1+c_{\rho})^{d}<2(1-\rho)\leq\lambda_{d}(x+F)+\lambda_{d}(E).

But this implies
\lambda_{d}\bigl[(x+F)\cap E\bigr]>0
, and hence
(x+F)\cap E
is non-empty, i.e.,
x\in E-F
. Since this is true for all
x\in[0,c_{\rho}]^{d}
, we conclude that
E-F\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}]^{d}
. This in turn implies
\Delta(E,F)\supseteq\Delta([0,c_{\rho}]^{d})=[0,c_{\rho}\sqrt{d}]
.

Theorem
C
follows from Theorem
A
. Given a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
obeying (
1.6
), one can find a sequence
R_{n}\nearrow\infty
and
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that

\frac{\lambda_{d}((A\cap Q(x_{n};R_{n}))}{\lambda_{d}\bigl(Q(x_{n};R_{n})\bigr)}\geq 1-\rho>\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{ for all $n$.}

Let us denote by
\mathbb{T}_{n}
the linear transformation that maps
Q(x_{n};R_{n})
onto
[0,1]^{d}
:

\mathbb{T}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\mathbb{T}_{n}(y):=\frac{y-x_{n}}{R_{n}}\quad\text{ and }\quad E_{n}:=\mathbb{T}_{n}(A\cap Q(x_{n};R_{n})).

Then
\lambda_{d}(E_{n})\geq 1-\rho
. Invoking Theorem
A
, we obtain
\Delta(E_{n})\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}\sqrt{d}]
, and hence

\Delta(A)\supseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Delta\bigl[\mathbb{T}_{n}^{-1}(E_{n})\bigr]\supseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{n}\Delta(E_{n})\supseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl[0,c_{\rho}\sqrt{d}R_{n}\bigr]=[0,\infty),

as claimed.
∎

10.2.
Sets with all sufficiently large distances

Lemma 10.1
.

For
d\geq 2
, let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set obeying (
1.8
). Then
A
contains all sufficiently large distances.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward consequence of the following proposition that appears in
[
9
]
, which is also key to the proof of Theorem
D
. We use this proposition in the following way. The assumption (
1.8
) ensures the existence of
\kappa>0
and a sequence
R_{n}\nearrow\infty
and
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{d}(A\cap B(x_{n};R_{n}))}{\lambda_{d}(B(x_{n};R_{n})}>\kappa;\;\text{ therefore }\lambda_{d}(E_{n})>\kappa\text{ where }

\displaystyle E_{n}:=\mathbb{T}_{n}\bigl[A\cap B(x_{n};R_{n})\bigr]\subseteq B(0;1).

Here, as in Section
10.1
,
\mathbb{T}_{n}(y)=(y-x)/R_{n}
is the linear transformation mapping
B(x_{n};R_{n})
onto
B(0;1)
. Let
J=J(\kappa,d)
be the integer provided by Proposition
10.2
corresponding to this value of
\kappa
.

Towards a contradiction, suppose if possible that
A
avoids infinitely many large distances; i.e., there exists an infinite sequence
\mathtt{d}_{j}\nearrow\infty
with
\Delta(A)\cap\bigl\{\mathtt{d}_{j}:j\geq 1\bigr\}=\emptyset
. Without loss of generality, passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\mathtt{d}_{j+1}>2\mathtt{d}_{j}
. Let us fix any sufficiently large choice of
n
such that
R_{n}>\mathtt{d}_{J}
, and set
t_{j}=\mathtt{d}_{J+1-j}/R_{n}
. Then the set
E_{n}
and the sequence
\{t_{j}:1\leq j\leq J\}
obey the hypotheses, and therefore the conclusion, of Proposition
10.2
. In other words,
t_{j}\in\Delta(E_{n})
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots J\}
. But this implies
R_{n}t_{j}=\mathtt{d}_{J+1-j}\in\Delta(A)
, contradicting the assumption that none of the numbers
d_{j}
lie in the distance set
\Delta(A)
.
∎

Proposition 10.2
.

[
9
, Proposition 3 and Section 2]

Let
d\geq 2
. Given any
\kappa>0
, there exists a positive integer
J=J(\kappa,d)
with the following property.

For every set
E\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with
\lambda_{d}(E)>\kappa
, and any sequence
\{t_{j}:j\geq 1\}\subseteq(0,1)
with
t_{j+1}<t_{j}/2
, one can find
1\leq j\leq J
such that

\Lambda_{\mu}(t_{j})>\frac{1}{2}.

Here
\mu=\mathtt{1}_{E}/\lambda_{d}(E)
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on
E
and
\Lambda_{\mu}(t)
is the distance-identifying integral defined in (
5.1
). In particular,
t_{j}\in\Delta(E)
.

10.3.
Distance sets containing arbitrarily small intervals

A remark on page
1.1
states that Theorems
A
and
1.1
can only be true for small values of
\rho
. We prove this assertion here.

Let
\varrho\in(0,1)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Setting
\mathbb{Z}_{N}:=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
, we define a set

(10.1)

E=E(\varrho,N):=\Bigl\{\frac{p}{N}+\frac{\varrho}{N}[0,1]^{d}:p\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{d}\Bigr\}\text{ so that }\lambda_{d}(E)=\varrho^{d}>0.

For fixed
\varrho
, let us consider the family of sets

\mathscr{E}_{\varrho}=\{E(\varrho,N):N\in\mathbb{N},N\geq 2\},

with
E=E(\varrho,N)
defined as in (
10.1
). Every
E\in\mathscr{E}_{\varrho}
has the property that
\lambda_{d}(E)=\varrho^{d}>0
, independent of
N
. On the other hand,

(10.2)

E-E=\Bigl\{\frac{p-p^{\prime}}{N}+\frac{\varrho}{N}[-1,1]^{d}:p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{d}\Bigr\}.

If
\varrho<\frac{1}{2}
, (
10.2
) shows that
E-E
is a disjoint union of cubes of sidelength
2\varrho/N
centred at
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{d}/N
.
Therefore, the largest cube centred at the origin and contained in
E-E
has sidelength
\varrho/N
, which can be made arbitrarily small by letting
N\rightarrow\infty
. This can be expressed as follows: for
\varrho<\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathtt{c}_{\varrho}:=\inf\bigl\{\mathtt{c}(E):E\subseteq[0,1]^{d},\lambda_{d}(E)=\varrho^{d}\bigr\}

(10.3)

\displaystyle\quad\;=\inf\bigl\{\mathtt{c}(E):E\in\mathscr{E}_{\varrho}\bigr\}=\inf\bigl\{\frac{\varrho}{N}:N\geq 1\bigr\}=0,\text{ where }

\displaystyle\mathtt{c}(E)=\sup\bigl\{c>0:E-E\supseteq[0,c]^{d}\bigr\}.

The relation (
10.2
) also shows that

\Delta(E)\subseteq\bigcup\Bigl\{\mathbb{I}_{r}:r\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{d}-\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{d}\Big\},\quad\text{ where }\mathbb{I}_{r}:=\frac{|r|}{N}+\Bigl[-\frac{\varrho\sqrt{d}}{N},\frac{\varrho\sqrt{d}}{N}\Bigr].

Choosing
\varrho\sqrt{d}<\frac{1}{2}
, we observe that
\mathbb{I}_{r}
contains the origin if and only if
r=0
. Thus, the largest interval in
\Delta(E)
containing the origin has length
\leq\varrho\sqrt{d}/N
, which also
\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. This leads to

\displaystyle\mathtt{a}_{\varrho}:=\inf\bigl\{\mathtt{a}(E):E\subseteq[0,1]^{d},\lambda_{d}(E)=\varrho^{d}\bigr\}\leq\inf\bigl\{\mathtt{a}(E):E=E(\varrho,N)\in\mathscr{E}_{\varrho}\bigr\}

(10.4)

\displaystyle\quad\;\leq\inf\bigl\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{I}_{0})=2\frac{\varrho\sqrt{d}}{N}:N\geq 1\bigr\}=0,\text{ where }

\displaystyle\mathtt{a}(E)=\sup\bigl\{a>0:\Delta(E)\supseteq[0,a]\bigr\}.

Combining (
10.3
), (
10.4
), we see that Theorem
A
cannot hold if
\lambda_{d}(E),\lambda_{d}(F)
are both small, specifically
<(2\sqrt{d})^{-d}
.

10.4.
Dyadic Hausdorff content of a uniformly distributed family of cubes

In Section
2.3
, we studied a set
\mathtt{F}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
given by (
2.7
), consisting of small cubes arranged on a lattice grid. The dyadic Hausdorff content of
\mathtt{F}
was claimed to be 1. We prove this statement in Lemma
10.5
of this section.

Lemma 10.3
.

For any
d\geq 1
,
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}([0,1]^{d})=1
for all
\tau\in(0,d]
.

Proof.

Let us recall the definition of dyadic Hausdorff content from (
2.4
). Choosing the trivial cover
Q=[0,1]^{d}
with
\ell(Q)=1
shows that

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}([0,1]^{d})\leq 1.

For the converse inequality, let us fix any cover
\{Q_{i}:i\geq 1\}
of
[0,1]^{d}
, where each
Q_{i}
is a dyadic cube. Then,

\sum_{i}\ell(Q_{i})^{d}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{d}(Q_{i})\geq\lambda_{d}([0,1]^{d})=1.

For any
0<p\leq 1
, the well-known inequality
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}|\leq\bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}|^{p}\bigr]^{\frac{1}{p}}
implies that

1\leq\sum_{i}\ell(Q_{i})^{d}\leq\Bigl[\sum_{i}\ell(Q_{i})^{dp}\Bigr]^{\frac{1}{p}}=\Bigl[\sum_{i}\ell(Q_{i})^{\tau}\Bigr]^{\frac{d}{\tau}}\text{ for }p=\frac{\tau}{d},

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 10.4
.

For
d\geq 1
and any dyadic cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(Q)=\ell(Q)^{\tau}\qquad\text{ for any }\tau\in(0,d].

Proof.

The proof is by scaling. Let
Q=c+r[0,1]^{d}
be a dyadic cube with
\ell(Q)=r
, where
r=2^{N}
and
c=m2^{N}
, for some
m,N\in\mathbb{Z}
. On one hand, the definition (
2.4
) implies that
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(Q)\leq\ell(Q)^{\tau}=r^{\tau}
. On the other, given any dyadic cover
\{Q_{i}\}
of
Q
, the cubes
\tilde{Q}_{i}=(Q_{i}-c)/r
are also dyadic, and form a cover for
[0,1]^{d}
. It follows from Lemma
10.3
that

\sum_{i}\ell(\tilde{Q}_{i})^{\tau}=r^{-\tau}\sum_{i}\ell(Q_{i})\geq 1;\text{ in other words, }\sum_{i}\ell(Q_{i})^{s}\geq r^{\tau}=\ell(Q)^{\tau}.

This is the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma 10.5
.

Let
\mathtt{F}
be the set given by (
2.7
), with

\sigma=\frac{d}{s}\in(1,\infty)\cap\mathbb{Q},\quad q=2^{N_{1}}\text{ and }\quad q^{\sigma}=2^{N_{2}}

for some choice of integers
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,
N_{1}<N_{2}
. Then for every dyadic cube
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with
\ell(Q)=2^{-N_{1}+k}
,
0\leq k\leq N_{1}
, an optimal cover of
\mathtt{F}\cap Q
that realizes the infimum (
2.4
) defining
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q)
is given by the collection of basic cubes of
\mathtt{F}
(of sidelength
q^{-\sigma}
) contained in
Q
; consequently,

(10.5)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q)=2^{kd}q^{-d}=\ell(Q)^{d}.

In particular,

(10.6)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{F})=1\text{ for all }\tau\leq s.

Proof.

Let us recall from (
2.7
) that
\mathtt{F}
is a
q^{d}
-fold disjoint union of basic cubes
\mathscr{Q}_{p}
:

F=\bigsqcup_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d}}\mathscr{Q}_{p}\quad\text{ where }\quad\mathscr{Q}_{p}:=\frac{p}{q}+q^{-\sigma}[0,1]^{d}.

We will prove the first statement of the lemma, leading up to (
10.5
), by induction on
k
. To establish the base case
k=0
, let us note that for any dyadic cube
Q
with
\ell(Q)=2^{-N_{1}}=1/q
, the set
\mathtt{F}\cap Q
consists of a single basic cube
\mathscr{Q}_{p}
. It therefore follows from Lemma
10.3
that

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q)=\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathscr{Q}_{p})=\ell(\mathscr{Q}_{p})^{-\sigma}=q^{-\sigma s}=q^{-d}.

An optimum cover of
\mathtt{F}\cap Q
is the single cube
\mathscr{Q}_{p}
.

Let us continue to the induction step. Suppose that (
10.5
) holds for
k\leq k_{0}-1
, and that for every dyadic
Q
with
\ell(Q)=2^{-N_{1}+k}<2^{-N_{1}+k_{0}}
, an optimum cover of
F\cap Q
consists of the collection of dyadic cubes
\mathscr{Q}_{p}
of side-length
q^{-\sigma}
contained in
\mathtt{F}\cap Q
. Let
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{d}
be a cube
\ell(Q_{0})=2^{-N_{1}+k_{0}}
. Then
\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0}
can be written as

\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0}=\bigsqcup\Bigl\{\mathtt{F}\cap Q:Q\subsetneq Q_{0},\;Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{d},\;\ell(Q)=2^{-N_{1}+k_{0}-1}\Bigr\}.

Our goal is to determine a dyadic cover of
\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0}
that realizes the infimum in
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0})
. If
Q
is a dyadic descendant of
Q_{0}
, the induction hypothesis dictates that an optimal cover of
F\cap Q
consists of the basic cubes of
\mathtt{F}
contained in
Q
. Thus, for the purpose of realizing the infimum (
2.4
) defining
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0})
, it suffices to consider the following two covering candidates:

\mathcal{Q}_{1}=\{Q_{0}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\Bigl\{\mathscr{Q}_{p}:\mathscr{Q}_{p}\subseteq\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0}\Bigr\}.

We compare the contribution towards (
2.4
) from each cover:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathtt{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}}\ell(\mathtt{q})^{s}

\displaystyle=\sum\bigl\{\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q):Q\text{ children of }Q_{0}\bigr\}=2^{d}2^{(k_{0}-1)d}q^{-d}=2^{(-N_{1}+k_{0})d}

\displaystyle\text{ whereas }\sum_{\mathtt{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}}\ell(\mathtt{q})^{s}

\displaystyle=\ell(Q_{0})^{s}=2^{(-N_{1}+k_{0})s}\geq 2^{(-N_{1}+k_{0})d}=\sum_{\mathtt{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}}\ell(\mathtt{q})^{s}.

The last inequality follows from the assumptions that
k\leq N_{1}
and
s\leq d
. This means that
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0})=\ell(Q_{0})^{d}
, with the optimal cover given by
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, which consists of the basic cubes
\mathscr{Q}_{p}
in
\mathtt{F}\cap Q_{0}
. This completes the induction step.

For
\tau=s
, the conclusion (
10.6
) follows from (
10.5
) with
k=N_{1}
; in this case,
Q=[0,1]^{d}
and
\mathtt{F}\cap Q=\mathtt{F}
. For
\tau\leq s
, we observe that

\ell(Q_{i})^{s}\leq\ell(Q_{i})^{\tau}\text{ since $\ell(Q_{i})\leq 1$;}\quad\text{ hence }1\leq\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(\mathtt{F})\leq\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\tau}(\mathtt{F})\leq 1,

completing the proof of the claim (
10.6
).
∎
