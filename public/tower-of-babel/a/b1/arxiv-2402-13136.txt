Relaxing Trust Assumptions on Quantum Key Distribution Networks
Nilesh Vyas, Paulo Mendes

Abstract
Quantum security over long distances with untrusted relays is largely unfounded and is still an open question for active research. Nevertheless, quantum networks based on trusted relays are being built across the globe. However, standard QKD network architecture implores a complete trust requirement on QKD relays, which is too demanding and limits the use cases for QKD networks. In this work, we explore the possibility to securely relay a secret in a QKD network by relaxing the trust assumptions (if not completely) on the relay. We characterize QKD relays with different trust levels, namely, Full Access Trust (FAT), Partial Access Trust (PAT), and No Access Trust (NAT). As the name suggests, each level defines the degree with which a relay is required to be trusted with the secret provided by the key management system for end-to-end communication. We then review and propose multiple constructions of the QKD key management system based on the different trust levels. Main contribution of the paper is realized by evaluating key management systems with no access trust level. In principle, we review key management with centralized topology and propose a new decentralized key management system. These different topologies provide various advantages based on the QKD network requirements, allowing an operational flexibility in the architecture. We believe this work presents a new perspective to the open problem of providing a confiding and a practical solution for future long range secure communications.



I
Introduction

Quantum Key Distribution
(QKD) facilitates the information-theoretic-secure transmission of critical data, based on QKD protocols that allow the distribution, over a quantum link, of symmetric random bit strings as a secret key, securing communication against an eavesdropper with unbounded computational resources
[
1
]
. Today, point-to-point QKD systems are already a reality, with commercially available systems performing mostly around one hundred kilometers over optical fibers, and recent academic demonstrations over hundreds of kilometers.

Besides commercial systems, QKD is being studied by standardization organizations such as
European Telecommunications Standards Institute
(ETSI) and the
International Telecommunication Union Telecommunication Standardization Sector
(ITU-T). ETSI activities aim to target QKD systems for large-scale trusted networks, as is the case of the ETSI Industry Specification Group on Quantum Key Distribution for Users (ISG-QKD)
[
2
]
. ITU-T has released several QKD recommendations, in particular ITU-T Y.3803
[
3
]
, for a
Key Management System
(KMS) in a QKD network. The development of KMS aims to support realistic deployment scenarios, in which applications are installed on devices connected to the same local area network as a QKD node and a KMS, as illustrated in figure
1
. It is assumed that controlling access inside such a local network relies upon conventional classical cryptographic techniques that strictly provide local authentication, and that KMS interacts with a local system (
e.g.,
kerberos) able to locally disseminate QKD generated keys.

Basic QKD network model

The basic QKD network model illustrated in figure
1
comprises multiple geographically separated QKD nodes, being assumed that each QKD node is trusted and physically located in a local area network consisting of several heterogeneous computing devices. However, a large-scale development of such a generic model is still fundamentally limited by the transmission loss
[
4
]
between QKD nodes. This is a bottleneck for deploying large scale QKD networks for end-to-end secure communication.

Extending the QKD range for point-to-point secure communication and overcoming the current fundamental limitations requires sophisticated quantum repeater technology, which is far from practicality. Therefore, quantum networks based on point-to-point QKD links have been developed using QKD trusted relays
[
3
]
instead of quantum repeaters. A
QKD relay
- or just a relay in this paper - is a QKD node able to forward secret material encrypted based on a pair of QKD keys generated over quantum links towards adjacent QKD nodes. Besides relays, a QKD network encompasses
QKD end-hosts
, which are QKD nodes able to provide security keys to local applications.

In this context, Free-space QKD links to
Low Earth Orbit
(LEO) satellites have been demonstrated by the Chinese Micius satellite as a trusted courier
[
5
]
. A trusted relay acts as a node that measures the quantum optical signals to yield a classical output, and new quantum optical signals are generated based on the classical outcome and are sent out to the next relay. However, this classical output is a weak link in the communication chain, as it is vulnerable to hacking and eavesdropping, meaning that security is only achieved if the relays can be trusted.

Providing quantum security over long distance with untrusted relays is still an open question to realise in practice
[
6
]
. Hence this paper aims to answer the following question:
Is it possible to securely relay secret information in a QKD network by relaxing the trust assumptions (if not completely) on the relays?

We address this question and explore novel constructions of a KMS by relaxing the trust requirements on relays. We believe that the answer to this question presents a new perspective to the open problem of providing a confiding and a practical solution for future long range secure communications.

II
Related Work

In order to investigate suitable solutions for QKD networks, several experiments have been conducted. For instance, BBN Technologies
[
7
]
proposed the first - DARPA - QKD network with ten quantum nodes encompassing active optical switches and trusted nodes. The DARPA network adopted the BB84 protocol
[
1
]
to generate security keys, achieving a key generating rate of 400 bps over 29 km.

In 2004, the European Commission project
Secure Communication based on Quantum Cryptography
(SECOQC) launched a QKD Network
[
8
]
that adopted a trusted node network framework with six quantum nodes and five different QKD protocols to distribute local keys. The best performance in terms of the key generation rate was 3.1 kbps over 33 km.

Although the DARPA and the SECOQC networks lay the groundwork for implementing QKD networks, both rely on the existence of trusted nodes. To achieve key distribution in the presence of untrusted nodes, previous research mainly focused on two aspects. The first aspect is using
Measurement Device Independent QKD
(MDI-QKD)
[
9
]
or
Twin-Field QKD
(TF-QKD)
[
10
]
to construct a QKD network
[
11
]
. These studies propose a hybrid network architecture based on trusted and untrusted nodes, which does not need to trust measurement devices. The aim was to reduce the number of trusted nodes. However, the proposed solution still requires trusted relays to extend the QKD distance, because it does not allow direct connection between two untrusted nodes
[
12
]
.

Another important factor to improve the ability of QKD networks to resist untrusted nodes is to rely on space diversity, by making use of multiple paths to protect key security. However, prior art shows the need to have at least one more disjoint path than the number of relays in order to avoid eavesdropping
[
13
]
.

Other approaches aim to develop QKD networks based on quantum satellites
[
6
]
. In such a scenario a satellite generates QKD keys via a ground-to-satellite quantum channel with two QKD nodes deployed in the ground. After generating the two QKD keys, the satellite uses a XOR operation to create a joint QKD key that can be transferred over a classical satellite channel to the two QKD ground stations. The caveat here is that the satellite knows the two intermediate keys, and therefore the final secret key, meaning that it must be trusted by the ground devices. Moreover, the delay to deploy the QKD keys in the ground nodes may be considerable if using LEO satellites, since it is directly proportional to the orbit time of the satellite.

III
Relaxing trust assumptions in QKD Networks

This paper explores new techniques to securely relay a secret material in a QKD network by relaxing the level of trust required on relays. In an ideal scenario, we should be able to deploy large-scale QKD networks by interconnecting a set of untrusted relays. The development of such a system would represent a game-changer for QKD. However, practical demonstrations of such systems are largely unfounded. Therefore, in this paper we provide an analysis of different steps that may sustain the idea of progressively relaxing the trust assumption of current QKD networking systems.

III-A

Trust levels

We consider a QKD network setting where two distant nodes want to share a secret key, for application use, via a series of relays, defined as honest-but-curious: a legitimate participant in a communication protocol who will not deviate from the defined protocol, but will attempt to learn all possible information from legitimately received messages. This paper characterizes the trust levels of such relays with respect to their capability to access information about shared secret material. The levels of trust are defined as follows:

•

Full Access Trust
(FAT): relays have complete knowledge of secret keys and need to be fully trusted.

•

Partial Access Trust
(PAT): relays do not have complete knowledge, but have partial access to secret keys and so need to be partially trusted.

•

No Access Trust
(NAT): relays do not learn any information about secret keys.

When considering the terminology found in prior art about trusted and untrusted relays, the former is a relay with FAT trust level, while untrusted relays can be of PAT or NAT level, with respect to the information provided at the key management level of the QKD network.

III-B

Key management system constructions

We consider the setting where a sender ’Alice’ wants to exchange a secret
K_{S}
with a distant receiver ’Bob’. Alice and Bob do not share a direct quantum link, but are connected via a chain of
N
trusted relays. Let the keys shared by adjacent relays be denoted as
K_{ij}
, for
i\neq j
,
i,j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
. Let
K_{A1}
be the key shared between Alice and its adjacent relay, similarly
K_{NB}
is the key between the last relay and Bob.

From a key management point of view, different systems can be used to develop QKD networks with different trust levels. One example is a decentralized system where a routing and forwarding scheme is used to allow QKD nodes serving applications to exchange secret keys over a chain of trusted relays, which decrypt and encrypt the forwarded keys.

An evolution of such a FAT system encompasses a QKD network that is able to split a secret key into different parts, which are transmitted based on spatial or temporal diversity methods. The former relies on multiple paths, and the latter method requires different parts of the key to be sent over different time windows over the same path. This constitutes a PAT system in which relays are able to access part of the key used to encrypt end-to-end communications.

Besides FAT and PAT systems, the most desirable configuration of a QKD network is based on NAT relays, which have no access, totally or partially, to secret keys used to encrypt end-to-end communications.

IV
QKD Networks with Full Access Trust

In a simple networking setup, distant QKD nodes can exchange QKD keys by means of a QKD network comprising two or more trusted relays,
e.g.,
relays with a full access Trust.

QKD networks with full access trust are built by having each relay, along a lengthy chain, decrypting and re-encrypting received keys before forwarding them to the next hop. Since forwarded keys are encrypted using QKD keys generated over the quantum links connecting adjacent nodes, they are protected from eavesdropping over classical channels. In this process, end-hosts share the QKD key generated by one of them over its local quantum link, or by using a local quantum device. However all relays are acquainted with the transmitted key, which is used to secure the communication between end-hosts.

As illustrated in figure

, key forwarding between two nodes, Alice and Bob, that are not directly connected via a quantum link, requires, in a basic approach, the following set of operations:

Alice generates a key,

2.

Alice uses
K_{A1}

K_{S}

K_{S}\oplus K_{A1}

3.

5.

6.

K_{S}\oplus K_{NB}

In this QKD network setting, relays are able to learn the complete information about the secret
K_{S}

This solution however allows all relays to be acquainted with the transmitted secret, meaning that all relays need to be trusted. From a network management perspective, such assumption is rather undesirable, since it is difficult to quantify the security risk and hence meaningfully relate it to business assets (confidential information) that are exchanged over the quantum network. Nevertheless, this transmission paradigm is gaining attention and finds itself implemented in various quantum demonstration networks.

V
QKD Networks with Partial Access Trust

Transmission over multiple paths is a straightforward remedy to relieve the stringent requirement of QKD networks with FAT trust level. In the simplest setting, two QKD nodes, Alice and Bob, share a common secret over multiple paths. This is achieved by having Alice splitting the secret into multiple parts, sending each one of them though different paths in a similar fashion as described in section
IV
. Bob combines all the different parts received over different paths, thus obtaining the complete secret information. Alice and Bob need to agree on the order in which the secret pieces are combined, which can be achieved leveraging the classical channel.

A simple skeleton of multipath transmission can be derived from the well-known polynomial
(k,n)
-secret-sharing due to Shamir (1979) . Let the adversary’s threshold be
t
, i.e. no more than
t
nodes in the network can be compromised. Furthermore, Alice and Bob are connected by
r
node-disjoint paths between them. For
r>t
, the secret can be distributed between the two as follows:

1.

Choose at least
k
out of
r>t
node-disjoint paths for transmission.

2.

Alice split the secret
K_{S}
into
k
shares, transmitted over each one of the
k
paths between Alice and Bob.

3.

Bob reconstructs the secret from the received shares.

As long as an adversary cannot control all paths, the security of the secret can be ensured. The adversary can only learn partial information about the secret by controlling up to
t<r
paths. Thus, multipath key forwarding satisfies the PAT level, as relays can access only partial information of the secret flowing though its path.

VI-A

Centralized Key Management System

A Centralized KMS consists of several chained relays connected to a central key manager. The key manager is a logically centralized node and can be implemented in different ways. Each relay is connected to two adjacent neighbors, with whom it shares a pair of QKD keys.

In this approach, QKD keys generated by relays are used to create local secure encrypted masks. In particular, each relay performs a XOR of the two QKD keys shared with its neighbors, and Alice XORes the secret
K_{S}
with the QKD key shared with its adjacent node. The encrypted masks generated at the different relays, with the exception of Bob, are collected by the central key manager. It is assumed that the masks can not be eavesdropped during the creation and submission to the central KMS. The central KMS XORes all the received keys and share the output with Bob, allowing it to decrypt and receive the secret
K_{S}
, as shown in figure
3
.

As no secret information for end-to-end encryption is exchanged through the relays, the trust requirements on the relay node can be relaxed to NAT level. This centralized architecture reduces the trust requirements of the individual relays however it has a single point of failure as central KMS. If the central KMS fails, the whole QKD network is not operational. Therefore, the central key manager must remain secure.

VI-B

Decentralized Key Management System

Decentralized KMS aim to support QKD networks based on a set of NAT QKD nodes, while not relying on a central KMS system, which may be a single point of failure.

In order to avoid relays to gather information about keys shared between QKD end-hosts supporting end-to-end communication, several levels of security and indirection can be used, as follows:

1.

Having QKD end-hosts splitting the key generated over their QKD link. If splitting is done based on a local method, QKD end-hosts will not need to trust adjacent relays.

2.

Use multiple paths to send keys, or parts of keys, in order to make it difficult for an eavesdropper to gather relevant QKD key material.

3.

Sending the keys, or partial key material, encrypted via links protected by QKD keys, or links protected by regular secure transport protocols.

4.

Encrypting application data with keys that are locally generated in each QKD end-host, and never transmitted in a network.

A low cost high secure QKD network can be achieved with two paths only, one over a set of relays and another over an regular
Geostationary Orbit
(GEO) satellite. GEO satellites provide long range communication with lower cost and higher security than regular Internet paths, which require data (keys) to cross a higher number of active networking nodes. Over the satellite link, keys may be transported by means of a regular secure transport protocol, such as QUIC
[
14
]
or TCP/TLS
[
15
]
.

The transmission of keys over a chain of relays is performed based on the method illustrated in figure

In order to ensure that none of the visiting relays have access to the QKD keys shared between the end-hosts, these should leverage the available multiple paths to shared partial QKD key information over each one of them; Partial key material results from splitting the QKD key generated over the local QKD links.

Since executing the mentioned levels of security and indirection may induce delay in furnishing applications with security keys, a suitable KMS should ensure that key pools maintained by QKD end-hosts have always enough keys to fulfill the key rate requirements of applications.

By using the described levels of security and indirection methods, a decentralized QKD system should be able to support secure communications, by having QKD end-hosts furnishing applications with security keys stored in local Key pools. Such security keys are created locally in the end-host by XORing the three QKD keys stored in the same entry of the key pool associated with such end-to-end communication. Such three QKD keys correspond to the key received over the chain relays, the key received over the satellite link and the locally generated divided key. VII
Discussion

This section aims to briefly discuss methods that may support the development of QKD networks that do not need to rely on trusted nodes, namely the methods described in section
VI
related to centralized or decentralized key management systems.

The
Centralized KMS
relies on a shared communication medium (
e.g.,
satellite link) to send secret material from several relays to a central device, a satellite, via a wireless medium that may need to be protected. If a relay is able to eavesdrop the communication over the shared wireless medium, it can in principle obtain the secret key and the KMS setting will no longer offer NAT trust level. Moreover, a computationally unbounded adversary with capabilities to spoof the network traffic can easily impersonate the central KMS and obtain all messages.

The practical limitation of a centralized approach may be mitigated, as described in section
VI-A
, by securing and protecting the communication channel between the central key manager and each QKD node (relays and end-hosts). Such extra protection can be ensured by replacing a regular satellite with a QKD-enabled satellite, in order to allow the transmission between the satellite and the QKD nodes to be protected by a QKD key. Another possibility is to use
Post-Quantum Cryptography
(PQC) to protect or encrypt the channel between the QKD nodes and the central KMS. Both these solutions are able to protect the secret material against a quantum adversary. The former solution with a QKD-enabled satellite is an information theoretic solution (ITS) upholding the ITS quality of the QKD network. However, this is an expensive and not scalable solution as it puts a lot of hardware burden on the central key manager. On the contrary, the PQC solution is an inexpensive and scalable solution with simple implementation, but only offers computational security, challenging the ITS security of the QKD.

On the other hand,
Decentralized KMS
aims to support QKD networks based on a set of untrusted QKD nodes, while not relying on a central KMS system, which may be a single point of failure and a security breach. A decentralized KMS may bring several advantages, starting by the potential lower cost, since it does not rely on Quantum enabled satellites and does not require QKD relays to use expensive satellite communications. Moreover, by making use of complementary security and indirection methods none of the relays have access to the secure key used to protect the end-to-end communication. Finally, as described, a decentralized framework does not require the deployment of a third-party quantum device (
e.g.,
quantum computer) to generate the secure key to be used by applications.

However, in order to implement all the mentioned security and indirection methods, a decentralized QKD network may induce a high delay in provisioning the security keys requested by applications. So, there is the need to evaluate how the system can provide QKD keys fulfilling the key rate requested by applications, while ensuring a good usage of resources needed for the local key pools.

In reality, a practical QKD network will be complex system with arbitrary topology, which may benefit from a hybrid solution for relaying the secret keys securely between two distant end hosts. In principle, a hybrid key management system can be designed by considering a partially centralized and partially decentralized topology, like a rooted tree structure. For instance, a central KMS supporting key management in metropolitan areas under the control of specific entities (
e.g.,
local governments), being such areas connected via a decentralized KMS allowing the secure routing of the secret keys over long distances.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we have investigated the possibility to securely relay secret information in a QKD network by relaxing the trust assumptions (if not completely) on QKD relays. To address this issue, we characterized the trust assumptions on relays into three different levels, namely
Full Access Trust
(FAT),
Partial Access Trust
(PAT), and
No Access Trust
(NAT). As the name suggests, each level is defined by the ability of an QKD relay to access information provided at the key management layer of the QKD network stack, i.e., the secret key used for end-to-end communication.

We explore various constructions of a key management system considering different trust levels. Striking feature of the work is the QKD network construction with no access trust requirement on QKD relays. We describe two different QKD key management frameworks, based on centralized and the decentralized topology, respectively. These different topologies provide various advantages based on the QKD network requirements, allowing an operational flexibility in the architecture.

We believe that this work presents a new perspective to the open problem of providing a confiding and a practical solution for future long range secure communications based on QKD technology.
