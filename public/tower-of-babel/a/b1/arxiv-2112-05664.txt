Leveraging Joint-Diagonalization in Transform-Learning NMF
Sixin Zhang, Emmanuel Soubies, Cédric Févotte

Abstract
Non-negative matrix factorization with transform learning (TL-NMF) is a recent idea that aims at learning data representations suited to NMF. In this work, we relate TL-NMF to the classical matrix joint-diagonalization (JD) problem. We show that, when the number of data realizations is sufficiently large, TL-NMF can be replaced by a two-step approach—termed as JD+NMF—that estimates the transform through JD, prior to NMF computation. In contrast, we found that when the number of data realizations is limited, not only is JD+NMF no longer equivalent to TL-NMF, but the inherent low-rank constraint of TL-NMF turns out to be an essential ingredient to learn meaningful transforms for NMF.



I
Introduction

Let
{\mathbf{Y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times N}
be a random matrix that satisfies the following moment conditions:
\forall(m^{\prime},m,n)
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{E}}([{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn})=0,&\text{(mean)}\\
{\mathbb{E}}([{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})=[\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn},&\text{(variance)}\\
{\mathbb{E}}([{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}[{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{m^{\prime}n})=0,&\text{(covariance)}\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}
is an orthogonal matrix and

\bar{\mathbf{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M\times\bar{K}}
,
\bar{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{\bar{K}\times N}
are two nonnegative matrices such that
[\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn}>0
. Here
\bar{K}
denotes the non-negative rank of
\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}

[
1
]
. These fairly general conditions encompass, for instance, the popular Gaussian composite model (GCM)
[
2
,
3
]
, which reads as

[{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}\underset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,[\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn}),\;\forall(m,n),

(2)

where
\mathcal{N}
denotes the real-valued Gaussian distribution.

In this paper, we are concerned with the problem of estimating the triplet
({\bar{\bm{\Phi}}},\bar{\mathbf{W}},\bar{\mathbf{H}})
from
S
i.i.d. realizations of
{\mathbf{Y}}
, which we denote by
\{{\mathbf{Y}}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
(this includes the most frequent scenario
S=1
). In the literature, this problem is referred to as transform-learning nonnegative matrix factorization (TL-NMF)
[
4
,
5
]
and is also a special case of independent low-rank tensor analysis (ILRTA)
[
6
]
. When the columns of
{\mathbf{Y}}
are composed of overlapping segments of a temporal signal and
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
is a fixed frequency-transform matrix such as the DCT (we only consider real-valued transforms in this paper), the product
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}
defines a short-time frequency transform. Its power values
[{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2}
define a
spectrogram
. Equation (
1
) dictates that the spectrogram is given in expectation by the nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF)
\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}
. The zero-mean assumption is valid for centered temporal signals such as audio signals. The zero-covariance assumption is a working assumption that reflects uncorrelation of the transform coefficients. The dictionary matrix
\bar{\mathbf{W}}
captures meaningful spectral patterns while the activation matrix
\bar{\mathbf{H}}
describes how the spectral samples are decomposed onto the dictionary.

NMF has found a large range of applications in audio signal processing, such as source separation or music transcription
[
3
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
In TL-NMF, the fixed transform assumption is relaxed and
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
is estimated together with
\bar{\mathbf{W}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{H}}

[
4
]
. This was shown to lead in some cases to more meaningful representations. For example, it was shown in
[
6
]
that better source separation performance can be achieved with adaptive learned transform. In
[
5
]
, it was shown that learning
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
allows to capture harmonic atoms with fundamental frequencies that precisely match the music notes in the data, when a DCT transform can only abide by a fixed frequency grid. TL-NMF was inspired by works about learning sparsifying transforms
[
10
]
and to a lesser extent independent component analysis (ICA)
[
11
,
12
]
.

TL-NMF can be tackled through the resolution of the following optimization problem

({\bm{\Phi}}^{\ast},{\mathbf{W}}^{\ast},{\mathbf{H}}^{\ast})\in\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)\\
({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}\end{subarray}}C_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{O}(M)
denotes the real orthogonal matrix group on
{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
and
F_{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times K}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{K\times N}_{+}
is a constraint set for the nonnegative matrices
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
which will be made explicit later. Here
K
represents the factorization rank whose choice depends on the considered dataset and application
[
9
,
13
]
.
The objective function
C_{S}
in (
3
) is defined by

C_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=\sum_{m,n=1}^{M,N}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})+{\epsilon_{0}}}{[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn}+{\epsilon_{0}}}\\
+\log\left([{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn}+{\epsilon_{0}}\right)

(4)

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})
is a consistent estimator of
{\mathbb{E}}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})
computed from the
S
i.i.d. realizations
\{{\mathbf{Y}}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
, and
{\epsilon_{0}}\geq 0
.

The rationale behind the choice of Problem (
3
) is that, when

i)

S=\infty
(i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})={\mathbb{E}}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})
), and
{\epsilon_{0}}=0
,

ii)

K\geq\bar{K}
(i.e., the NMF rank
K
in (
3
) is larger than
\bar{K}
, the true rank in model (
1
)),

each global minimizer
({\bm{\Phi}}^{\ast},{\mathbf{W}}^{\ast},{\mathbf{H}}^{\ast})
of
C_{S}
is such that the rows of
{\bm{\Phi}}^{\ast}
and
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
span the same subspaces (we say that
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
is identifiable by solving (
3
)) and
{\mathbf{W}}^{\ast}{\mathbf{H}}^{\ast}=\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}

[
5
]
. Moreover, under the GCM,
C_{S}
corresponds to the expected negative log-likelihood of
{\mathbf{Y}}
conditioned to
({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})
.

Contributions and Outline

This identifiability result relies on a key property of
C_{S}
that is, in the regime
S=\infty
and
K\geq\bar{K}
,

\forall{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M),\;\min_{({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}}C_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=L_{S}({\bm{\Phi}}),

(5)

where
L_{S}
is related to the joint-diagonalization (JD) criterion derived in
[
14
]
(and formally defined in Section
II
).

This suggests the following two-step
alternative to Problem (
3
)

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet}\in&\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\argmin_{{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)}\,L_{S}({\bm{\Phi}}),\\
({\mathbf{W}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{H}}^{\bullet})\in&\displaystyle\mkern-15.0mu\argmin_{({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}}C_{S}({\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}}),\end{array}

(6)

which we refer to as JD+NMF.
Although it is straightforward from (
5
) that problems (
3
) and (
6
) are equivalent in the ideal case
S=\infty
and
{\epsilon_{0}}=0
, their relation in the more practical situation
S<\infty
and
{\epsilon_{0}}>0
(usually required for numerical stability)
needs to be analyzed. In particular:

•

How does (
6
) deviate from (
3
) with respect to (w.r.t.)
S
?

•

Is one of these two formulations preferable from an optimization perspective? (quality of the reached local minimizer, convergence speed)

The main contribution of this work is to provide theoretical and numerical insights to these questions. In Section
II
, we set out the basic assumptions and definitions required for our analysis and prove in Theorem
1
that both TL-NMF (Problem (
3
)) and JD+NMF (Problem (
6
)) admit at least one solution. Then, Section
III
is dedicated to the characterization of the closeness between the solution sets of these two problems. We first identify in Theorem
2
quantities that allow to characterize three regimes where the solution sets of TL-NMF and JD+NMF are i) disjoint, ii) partially intersecting, and iii) equal, respectively.
Then, by analyzing the asymptotic behavior (w.r.t.
S
) of these key quantities, we show that with high probability,
the gaps between the two solution sets converge to zero (in terms of the Itakua-Saito divergence
[
15
,
2
]
) as
S
grows
(Proposition
2
and Theorem
3
).
Under the GCM, we further prove in Theorem
4
that the rate of convergence is at least of the order of
O(1/S)
. In Section
IV
, we adapt existing TL-NMF and JD minimization algorithms to Problems (
3
) and (
6
) as they come with slightly different objective functions and constraint sets. Finally, in Section
V
, we deploy these algorithms to illustrate and complement numerically our theoretical findings (tightness of the expected asymptotic behavior, behavior when
S
is small).

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Definitions and Useful Results

First, from now on, and throughout the paper, we set
\epsilon_{0}>0
. Then, let us provide
a reformulation of the moment conditions in (
1
) that will be relevant in our analysis.

Proposition 1
.

The moment conditions (
1
) can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbf{y}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(7)

\displaystyle{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Diag}(\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{n}),

(8)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}={\mathbb{E}}(\mathbf{y}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\intercal})
denotes the covariance matrix of the
n
-th column of
{\mathbf{Y}}
.

Proof.

The mean condition in (
1
) implies that for any
n
,

{\mathbb{E}}({\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}\mathbf{y}_{n})=\mathbf{0}
, thus we have
{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbf{y}_{n})=\mathbf{0}

due to the invertibility of
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
. The variance
condition implies that the diagonal terms of

{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\intercal}

equal to the vector
\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{n}
.
This is because, by definition,

[{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\intercal}]_{mm}=[\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn}=(\bar{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{n})[m]
. Similarly, the covariance condition
implies that the off-diagonal terms of
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\intercal}

are zero.
∎

Finally, to complete the formulation of Problems (
3
) and (
6
)
we define below
the constraint set
F_{K}
as well as
the empirical expectation operator
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(\cdot)
used in the definition of
C_{S}
in (
3
).

Constraint Set

we define the constraint set
F_{K}
as

F_{K}=\{({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times K}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{K\times N}_{+}\;|\;\forall k,\|\mathbf{w}_{k}\|_{1}=1\},

(9)

on which
C_{S}
is well-defined (i.e., no singularity) when
{\epsilon_{0}}>0
. The normalization constraint on each column of
{\mathbf{W}}
allows to break the scaling ambiguity between
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
.
It is worth mentioning that there are other types ambiguities in NMF
[
16
,
9
]
. These include permutations of the columns of
{\mathbf{W}}
and rows of
{\mathbf{H}}
, or local nonnegativity-preserving rotations. Yet, considering the scaling ambiguity turns out to be an important ingredient to prove the existence of a solution to TL-NMF and JD+NMF (see the proof of Theorem
1
in Appendix
B
). This motivates the definition of
F_{K}
in (
9
).

Empirical Expectation

We consider the empirical expectation of
[{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2}
, that is

{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}[{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{(s)}]_{mn}^{2}.

(10)

Moreover, we use the notation
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
to refer to the point-wise empirical expectation, i.e.,
[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})]_{mn}={\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})
.
Finally, we introduce the following empirical covariance matrix:

\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}^{(s)}({\mathbf{y}}_{n}^{(s)})^{\intercal}.

(11)

II-B

From TL-NMF to JD+NMF

For any integer
S>0
, one can easily verify
the following decomposition of the TL-NMF objective in (
4
),

C_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=L_{S}({\bm{\Phi}})+I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}}),

(12)

with
L_{S}
and
I_{S}
defined by

\displaystyle L_{S}({\bm{\Phi}})=MN+\sum_{m,n=1}^{M,N}\log({\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})+{\epsilon_{0}}),

(13)

\displaystyle I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=D_{\epsilon_{0}}({\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})|{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}),

(14)

where
D_{\epsilon_{0}}(\cdot|\cdot)
denotes a regularized form of the Itakura-Saito (IS) divergence.
For two non-negative matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, it is defined by

D_{\epsilon_{0}}(\mathbf{A}|\mathbf{B})=\sum_{m,n}\left(\frac{[\mathbf{A}]_{mn}+{\epsilon_{0}}}{[\mathbf{B}]_{mn}+{\epsilon_{0}}}-\log\left(\frac{[\mathbf{A}]_{mn}+{\epsilon_{0}}}{[\mathbf{B}]_{mn}+{\epsilon_{0}}}\right)-1\right).

(15)

The term
I_{S}
in (
14
) is related to Itakura-Saito NMF (IS-NMF)
[
2
]
. Indeed, when
{\bm{\Phi}}
is fixed, the minimization of
I_{S}
with respect to
({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}
produces a NMF of
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
where the fit is measured by the IS divergence.

The term
L_{S}
is related to joint-diagonalization (JD), as specified by Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1
.

For all
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
,

L_{S}({\bm{\Phi}})=MN+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\det\D({\bm{\Phi}}(\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}+{\epsilon_{0}}\mathbf{I}){\bm{\Phi}}^{\intercal}),

(16)

which corresponds, up to a constant term, to the JD criterion derived in
[
14
]
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

The minimization of
L_{S}
over
\mathcal{O}(M)
leads to an orthogonal matrix
{\bm{\Phi}}
such that
{\bm{\Phi}}\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\intercal}
is as diagonal as possible.
The additional diagonal matrix
{\epsilon_{0}}\mathbf{I}
in (
16
) can be interpreted (up to a constant normalization) as a linear shrinkage estimator for covariance matrix estimation
[
17
]
.

Hence, from the decomposition (
12
), TL-NMF can be interpreted as a trade-off between the JD of the covariance matrices
\{\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}\}_{n}
and the NMF of the variance matrix
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
. In contrast, the alternative scheme (
6
) attempts to achieve the same goal in a two-step fashion by first solving a JD problem to obtain
{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet}
and then solving an IS-NMF problem given
{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet}
to get
({\mathbf{W}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{H}}^{\bullet})
.

Remark 1
.

From Lemma
1
, one can see that the “regularization” parameter
{\epsilon_{0}}>0

ensures that
L_{S}
is continuous over
\mathcal{O}(M)
.
Indeed, when
S<M
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}
have vanishing eigenvalues meaning that some diagonal entries of
{\bm{\Phi}}\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\intercal}
may be zero. Hence, taking
{\epsilon_{0}}>0
ensures that all the log-terms of (
16
) are non-degenerate.

Remark 2
.

The JD criterion derived in
[
14
]
and essentially given by (
16
) does not assume
{\bm{\Phi}}
to be explicitly orthogonal but merely non-singular. Many orthogonal JD algorithms were designed
in the early age of ICA when whitening was still
a standard data pre-processing step.
A seminal example is the Jacobi algorithm
by Cardoso and Souloumiac
[
18
]
for JD with a least-squares criterion.
However, to ensure optimal one-step performance
[
19
]
,
the whitening step was eventually dropped in the ICA community. Non-orthogonal JD became the mainstream, and many algorithms ensued for various criteria (such as based on least-squares, maximum likelihood or information measures), see, e.g.,
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. More recently,
[
25
]
leverages Riemannian optimization to unify many existing methods and introduce new ones under various constraints for
{\bm{\Phi}}
.

In this work, we restrict
{\bm{\Phi}}
to be orthogonal because TL-NMF
aims to generalize short-time frequency transforms for which orthogonality is somehow natural or desired.
Moreover when
S=\infty
, we showed that the row subspaces of

\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}
are identifiable
[
5
]
.
Such an analysis is more difficult in the general case (see
[
26
,
27
]
for related discussions). Still, removing the orthogonality assumption of
{\bm{\Phi}}
could be beneficial in practice
and forms a relevant research direction
(see further discussion in Section
VI
).

III
Relationship between JD+NMF and TL-NMF

III-A

Existence of a Solution

Before analyzing the relation between TL-NMF and JD+NMF, it is worth checking that they both admit at least one solution.
To the best of our knowledge, this question has never been addressed in the TL-NMF literature, nor in NMF literature (i.e., existence of global minimizer(s) for
I_{S}
, see Lemma
3
).

Theorem 1
.

The solution sets
\Omega^{*}
of TL-NMF (Problem (
3
)) and
\Omega^{\bullet}
of JD+NMF (Problem (
6
)) are nonempty and compact.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

Remark 3
.

The assumption
{\epsilon_{0}}>0
is necessary for the functions
L_{S}
and
I_{S}
to be well-defined and to show the existence of solutions to TL-NMF and JD+NMF. We chose to include
{\epsilon_{0}}
in the definitions of
L_{S}
and
I_{S}
but we could alternatively have set
{\epsilon_{0}}=0
in
L_{S}
and
I_{S}
, and add an inequality of the form
{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}\geq{\epsilon_{0}}
in the constraint set
F_{K}
. This is related to the approach followed by
[
28
]
to study the convergence of NMF with a large range of divergences (including the generalized Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
29
]
) under constraints of the form
{\mathbf{W}}\geq{\epsilon_{0}}
,
{\mathbf{H}}\geq{\epsilon_{0}}
.

III-B

When does JD+NMF meet TL-NMF?

In Theorem
2
, we characterize the closeness between the solutions of JD+NMF and the solutions of TL-NMF.
A graphical illustration of this result is depicted in Figure

.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Omega^{*}
and
\Omega^{\bullet}
be defined as in Theorem
1
. Define

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}^{*}=\min_{({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in\Omega^{*}}I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}}),

(17)

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{*}=\max_{({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in\Omega^{*}}I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}}),

(18)

and similarly
\underline{\lambda}^{\bullet}

\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}
by replacing
\Omega^{*}
by
\Omega^{\bullet}
. Then,

0\leq\underline{\lambda}^{*}\leq\bar{\lambda}^{*}\leq\underline{\lambda}^{\bullet}\leq\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}<+\infty.

(19)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\Omega^{\bullet}\cap\Omega^{*}\neq\emptyset\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\bar{\lambda}^{*}=\underline{\lambda}^{\bullet},

(20)

\displaystyle\Omega^{\bullet}=\Omega^{*}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\underline{\lambda}^{*}=\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}.

(21)

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Fig. 1:

Graphical illustration of Theorem
2
. The relationship between the solutions sets
\Omega^{*}
and
\Omega^{\bullet}
of TL-NMF and JD+NMF is described in terms of specific level lines of

. Note that we represented here

There are at least two situations where JD+NMF is equivalent to TL-NMF (i.e., (

) is satisfied):

•

S=\infty
and

,

•

S<\infty
and

.

Indeed, in these two cases we have that,
\forall{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
,

\exists({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K},\text{ such that }\;{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}},

(22)

i.e., there exists an exact factorization of
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
. When
K\geq\min\{M,N\}=N
it suffices to populate
{\mathbf{W}}
with
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
,
{\mathbf{H}}
with
{\mathbf{I}}_{N}
, and to complete with zeros. A similar exact factorization can be obtained when
K\geq\min\{M,N\}=M
by switching the roles of
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
.
For the case
S=\infty
, we refer the reader to the proof of Proposition
2
in Appendix
D
. Consequently, in such cases, we get from (
22
) that

\forall{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M),\;\min_{({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}}I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=0.

(23)

It then follows from (
3
) (resp., (
6
)) and (
12
) that

\forall({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in\Omega^{*}\,(\mathrm{resp.,}\,\Omega^{\bullet}),\;I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=0

(24)

and thus
\underline{\lambda}^{*}=\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}=0
.

The situation is more involved when
S<\infty
and
K<\min\{M,N\}
.
In this case, we get from Theorem
2
that the closeness between the solutions of JD+NMF and TL-NMF is controlled by the inner
\underline{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\bar{\lambda}^{*}
and
the outer
\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\underline{\lambda}^{*}
gaps. Indeed, by definition, we get that for any
({{\bm{\Phi}}}^{*},{{\mathbf{W}}}^{*},{{\mathbf{H}}}^{*})\in\Omega^{*}
and
({\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{W}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{H}}^{\bullet})\in\Omega^{\bullet}
,

0\leq\underline{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\bar{\lambda}^{*}\leq I_{S}^{\bullet}-I_{S}^{*}\leq\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\underline{\lambda}^{*},

(25)

where
\displaystyle I_{S}^{\bullet}=I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{W}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{H}}^{\bullet})
and
\displaystyle I_{S}^{*}={I_{S}}({{\bm{\Phi}}}^{*},{{\mathbf{W}}}^{*},{{\mathbf{H}}}^{*})
. Moreover, one can see that the outer gap also controls the proximity between minimizers in terms of

values because

0\leq C_{S}^{\bullet}-C_{S}^{*}\leq I_{S}^{\bullet}-I_{S}^{*}\leq\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\underline{\lambda}^{*},

where the first inequality is obtained by combining (

\displaystyle L_{S}^{\bullet}\leq L_{S}^{*}
. Note that here,

\displaystyle C_{S}^{\bullet}
and
\displaystyle C_{S}^{*}
(resp.,
\displaystyle L_{S}^{\bullet}

Finally, let us emphasize that the inequalities (

Remark 4
.

The above discussion about the quality of the learned
{\bm{\Phi}}
in terms of
I_{S}
values may raise the following question: why not directly minimizing
I_{S}
with respect to
({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})
? The latter approach was in particular the one considered in the original TL-NMF paper
[
4
]
(with an additional sparsity-enforcing term for
{\mathbf{H}}
), an arbitrary choice inherited from IS-NMF
[
30
]
. As a matter of fact, in light of our results, several arguments play in favor of minimizing

rather than

. First, under the GCM,

comes with a probabilistic interpretation (log-likelihood function). Second, when

such that

for all

(see (

I_{S}
does not constitutes a good measure to discriminate the

. In contrast, the minimization of

allows to identify the true transform
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
(the term

acts somehow as a penalization term)
[

]
. Lastly, this ability to learn meaningful transform (e.g., close to
{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}}
) by minimizing

appears to be also true when

is small (see Section

).

IV-A

General Principle of QN Methods over
\mathcal{O}(M)

For both problems, the main difficulty in deriving a QN method for updating
{\bm{\Phi}}
comes from the handling of the orthogonality constraint (i.e.,
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
). In this work, we follow the standard approach that consists in defining a local parameterization
\rho_{{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}}:\Upsilon\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(M)
of the neighborhood of an iterate
{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}
(see for instance
[
37
]
). The idea is then to compute a QN direction based on a quadratic approximation of the local objective function
g\circ\rho_{{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}}
(here
g
stands for either
C_{S}(\cdot,{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})
or
L_{S}
). From the second-order Taylor expansion around
\mathbf{0}
, we get

g(\rho_{{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}}({\mathbf{E}}))=g({\bm{\Phi}}_{j})+{\langle}{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{E}}{\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\langle}{\mathbf{E}}|{\mathcal{H}}|{\mathbf{E}}{\rangle}+O\left(\|{\mathbf{E}}\|^{3}\right),

(36)

using the fact that
\rho_{{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}}(\mathbf{0})={\bm{\Phi}}_{j}
, and denoting respectively by
{\mathbf{G}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}
and
{\mathcal{H}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M\times M\times M}
the gradient matrix and the Hessian tensor of
g\circ\rho_{{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}}
at
\mathbf{0}
. In (
36
), the inner products should be read as
{\langle}{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{E}}{\rangle}=\sum_{a,b}[{\mathbf{G}}]_{ab}[{\mathbf{E}}]_{ab}
and
{\langle}{\mathbf{E}}|{\mathcal{H}}|{\mathbf{E}}{\rangle}=\sum_{a,b,c,d}[{\mathcal{H}}]_{abcd}[{\mathbf{E}}]_{ab}[{\mathbf{E}}]_{cd}
.

Because the Hessian is usually costly to compute, we will consider an approximation of it, denoted
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
, and define a QN direction through the resolution of

{\mathbf{E}}_{j}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{E}}\in\Upsilon}{\langle}{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{E}}{\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\langle}{\mathbf{E}}|\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}|{\mathbf{E}}{\rangle}.

(37)

Given this QN direction, the current estimate
{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}
is updated according to

{\bm{\Phi}}_{j+1}=\rho_{{\bm{\Phi}}_{j}}(\eta{\mathbf{E}}_{j}),

(38)

where
\eta>0
is obtained by line search (e.g., backtracking
[
34
]
or Wolfe
[
38
]
).
This generic QN approach is summarized in Algorithm
4
. In the next two sections, we provide details on its instantiation to our problems. More precisely, the main task is to compute the gradient
{\mathbf{G}}
, the Hessian approximation
\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}
, as well as the QN direction in (
37
).

For the minimization of

IV-D

Computational Complexity

We now briefly discuss the computational complexity of the two solvers in Algorithm
1
and
2
. First of all, one can see from Algorithm
3
that an iteration of MU has a complexity of the order of
O(KMN)
(the main cost being the products of matrices with sizes
M\times K
and
K\times N
).

Concerning the QN step, the main cost comes from the computation of the gradient and the Hessian approximation which lead to a complexity per iteration of the order of
O(SNM^{2})
for TL-NMF (see (
40
) and (
41
)) and
O(NM^{3})
for JD (see (
45
) and (
46
)).

The overall complexities of the two solvers are thus of the order of

•

O\left(J(J_{\mathrm{NMF}}KMN+J_{\mathrm{TL}}SNM^{2})\right)
for TL-NMF,

•

O\left(J(J_{\mathrm{NMF}}KMN+J_{\mathrm{TL}}NM^{3})\right)
for JD+NMF.

In complement of Remark
7
, we see that the QN strategy deployed for TL-NMF is preferable when
S\leq M
, whereas the one used for JD when
S\geq M
. Yet, for the latter, the storage of the
N
covariance matrices
\{\bm{\Sigma}_{n,S}\}_{n\leq N}
is required, which results in a memory overload of
O(NM^{2})
.

V
Numerical Experiments

From the theoretical analysis conducted in Section
III
, we know that the solutions of TL-NMF and JD+NMF are getting closer as
S
grows. More precisely, the closeness between the solution sets of these two problems is controlled by the inner and outer
\lambda
’s gaps (namely
\underline{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\bar{\lambda}^{*}
and
\bar{\lambda}^{\bullet}-\underline{\lambda}^{*}
) which converge to zero with high probability as
S
grows (Proposition
2
and Theorem
3
). Moreover, under the GCM, they converge at least at a rate of
O(1/S)
(Theorem
4
).

Yet, these theoretical results only provide a partial answer to the questions we identified in the introduction. For instance, the situation where
S
is small (including the extreme but very relevant case
S=1
) remains unclear. If TL-NMF and JD+NMF are not equivalent, does one of them provide “better” solutions
(from an application-oriented perspective) than the other? On the other hand, when they are equivalent (i.e., when the gap is approaching zero),
is one of them preferable from the point of view of computational complexity? And lastly, how tight are the derived theoretical bounds? The goal of this section is to provide numerical insights on these questions so as to illustrate and complement our theoretical findings.
The results presented in this section can be reproduced using the
Python code available at
https://github.com/sixin-zh/tlnmf-tsp
.

VI
Discussion and Concluding Remarks

From our theoretical and numerical analysis, we can describe the relationship between TL-NMF and JD+NMF by distinguishing two situations.
On the one hand, when
S
is large and
K\geq\bar{K}
, the two problems are equivalent (Theorems
2
and
3
) and—at least on the reported numerical experiments—JD+NMF seems to enjoy a faster convergence (Figure
4
). On the other hand, when
S
is small (in particular
S=1
), they are not equivalent anymore and the solutions obtained by TL-NMF appear to be more meaningful from an application point of view (Figure
3
). One explanation of this phenomenon comes from the inherent low-rank constraint in the
{\bm{\Phi}}
update of TL-NMF which favors the learning of orthogonal transforms that are better suited to a low-rank NMF. This is clearly visible when comparing the values of
I_{S}
at the obtained solutions (see Figure
2
and Theorem
2
).

In this work, we fixed
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
to the empirical expectation given by (
10
), which naturally led us to analyze the relationship between the two problems as a function of the number of realizations
S
. However, we can see from Proposition
2
that the main ingredient to make TL-NMF and JD+NMF equivalent is to build a good estimator of the variance
{\mathbb{E}}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
, in the sense that the Itakura-Saito divergence between the estimator and the true expectation is small (i.e.,
Q_{S}({\bm{\Phi}})
uniformly small). The empirical expectation is a natural choice when several signal realizations are available, but other choices are possible. Indeed, even for
S=1
, the variance
{\mathbb{E}}(|{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}|^{\circ 2})
can be estimated by a local moving average, under suitable local stationary assumptions. An example is when
{\mathbf{H}}
is given a block structure (with identical columns inside the blocks), similarly to the
block Gaussian model
of
[
14
]
. Actually, such a block structure can be seen alternatively as having access to several realizations of
\mathbf{Y}
(with
S>1
the size of the block).

In this paper we only considered real-valued transform for mathematical convenience. Complex-valued transform such as the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) are more customary in audio signal processing (in particular for shift-invariance). Extending our work to such complex-valued transform is an appealing research direction. Note also that the complex-valued Fourier transform is an orthogonal transform endowed with a specific form of Hermitian symmetry. In particular, when applied to real-valued signals, only half of the power spectrum needs to be considered. Imposing such a structure to
\bm{\Phi}
, to mimic some of the properties of the Fourier transform, would be a very interesting topic. This is however a challenging problem, both in terms of mathematical analysis and design of optimisation methods. Another exciting research direction would be to lift the orthogonal constraint imposed on
\bm{\Phi}
and only assume invertibility.
However, for TL-NMF to be useful to signal processing tasks,
it is important that
{\bm{\Phi}}
can be inverted for temporal reconstruction and thus that its inverse be well-conditioned. Existing JD algorithms such that
[
24
,
25
]
might be useful for this purpose.

B-A

Existence of a Solution for JD+NMF (Problem (
6
))

Given the two-step nature of Problem (
6
), the existence of a solution
({\bm{\Phi}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{W}}^{\bullet},{\mathbf{H}}^{\bullet})\in\Omega^{\bullet}
is a direct consequence of the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2
.

The set of global minimizers of
L_{S}
over
\mathcal{O}(M)
is nonempty and compact.

Proof.

As
\epsilon_{0}>0
,
\log({\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})+{\epsilon_{0}})
is well defined and

L_{S}
is continuous on the compact manifold
\mathcal{O}(M)
.
Therefore
\inf_{{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)}\;L_{S}({\bm{\Phi}})
is attained and finite
(Weierstrass Theorem). Furthermore, the solution set is closed (it is a level set of the continuous function
L_{S}
) and bounded because it is included in the bounded set
\mathcal{O}(M)
. This proves the compactness and completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For all
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
, the set of global minimizers of
I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},\cdot,\cdot)
over
F_{K}
is nonempty and compact.

Proof.

By definition, for all
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
,
I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous over
F_{K}
. As

F_{K}
is closed but unbounded, a sufficient argument to complete the proof is to show that, for all
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
,
I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive over
F_{K}
, i.e.,

\forall{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M),\;\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\|_{1,1}\rightarrow\infty\\
({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}\end{subarray}}I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})=+\infty.

(56)

Since all norms being equivalent in finite dimension, we use the norm
\|({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\|_{1,1}=\|{\mathbf{W}}\|_{1,1}+\|{\mathbf{H}}\|_{1,1}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{1,1}
denotes the entrywise
\ell_{1}
-norm for matrices.
Because of the normalization constraint on
{\mathbf{W}}
in
F_{K}
, we have

\forall({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K},\;\|({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\|_{1,1}=K+\|{\mathbf{H}}\|_{1,1}.

(57)

Now, let
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
,
\nu>0
and set

\rho=KMN\mathrm{e}^{\nu+\log(D)+1}+K>K,

(58)

where
D=\max_{m,n}{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{m,n}^{2})+{\epsilon_{0}}>0
. Then we have the following implications:

({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}\,\text{ and }\,\|({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\|_{1,1}\geq\rho\\
\quad\ \Longrightarrow\,\|{\mathbf{H}}\|_{1,1}\geq\rho-K>0\\
\Longrightarrow\,\exists(k_{0},n_{0})\text{ s.t. }h_{k_{0}n_{0}}\geq\frac{\rho-K}{KN},

(59)

where the last implication comes from the fact that
{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{K\times N}
. Similarly, using the fact that
\mathbf{w}_{k_{0}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
and
\|\mathbf{w}_{k_{0}}\|_{1}=1
, we get that
\exists m_{0}
s.t.
w_{m_{0}k_{0}}\geq 1/M
. Combining the latter with (
59
), we obtain

[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{m_{0}n_{0}}\geq\frac{\rho-K}{MKN}\underset{\eqref{eq:Proof_ExistenceMinIs-3}}{\geq}\mathrm{e}^{\nu+\log(D)+1}.

(60)

Finally, denoting
f(x)=x-\log(x)-1
we have

\displaystyle I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{M,N}f\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{mn}^{2})+\epsilon_{0}}{[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{mn}+\epsilon_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq f\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{m_{0}n_{0}}^{2})+{\epsilon_{0}}}{[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{m_{0}n_{0}}+\epsilon_{0}}\right)\quad\text{(as $f\geq 0$ on ${\mathbb{R}}_{+}$)}

\displaystyle\geq-\log\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{S}([{\bm{\Phi}}{\mathbf{Y}}]_{m_{0}n_{0}}^{2})+{\epsilon_{0}}}{[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{m_{0}n_{0}}+\epsilon_{0}}\right)-1

\displaystyle\geq\log([{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{H}}]_{m_{0}n_{0}}+\epsilon_{0})-\log(D)-1\underset{\eqref{eq:Proof_ExistenceMinIs-5}}{\geq}\nu.

Hence, we have shown that for all
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathcal{O}(M)
and all
\nu>0
, there exists
\rho>0
(i.e., (
58
)) such that

({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\in F_{K}\text{ and }\|({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\|_{1,1}\geq\rho\\
\Longrightarrow I_{S}({\bm{\Phi}},{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{H}})\geq\nu,

(61)

which completes the proof.
∎

is well defined on
\mathcal{O}(M)
in the sense that the

Proof.

Using the parametrization of the anti-symmetric matrix

where we recall that
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle

\left\langle\cdot|\cdot|\cdot\right\rangle

Given that

\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}

Because

Appendix G
Multi-Initialization Strategy

We describe a multi-initialization strategy that allows to obtain numerical solutions that are close to the solution sets
\Omega^{*}
and
\Omega^{\bullet}
. To that end, we first consider
P
random initializations

\{({\bm{\Phi}}_{0}^{(p)},{\mathbf{W}}_{0}^{(p)},{\mathbf{H}}_{0}^{(p)})\}_{p\leq P}

(131)

from which we run the TL-NMF and JD+NMF solvers in Algorithms
1
and
2
. Given an initialization, each method is run for
J
iterations. In order to robustify the process, the solutions obtained with JD+NMF (resp. TL+NMF)

\{({\bm{\Phi}}_{J}^{(p)},{\mathbf{W}}_{J}^{(p)},{\mathbf{H}}_{J}^{(p)})\}_{p\leq P}

are used as extra
P
initializations

\{({\bm{\Phi}}_{0}^{(p)},{\mathbf{W}}_{0}^{(p)},{\mathbf{H}}_{0}^{(p)})\}_{P+1\leq p\leq 2P}

(132)

for TL-NMF (resp. JD+NMF).
Finally, for TL-NMF, we preserve only the solution
(\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\ast},{\hat{\mathbf{W}}}^{\ast},{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}^{\ast})
that achieves the smallest
C_{S}
. For JD+NMF, we preserve only the solution
\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\bullet}
that achieves the smallest
L_{S}
. We then minimize
I_{S}(\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\bullet},\cdot,\cdot)
, started from
\{({\mathbf{W}}_{0}^{(p)},{\mathbf{H}}_{0}^{(p)})\}_{p\leq 2P}
, and preserve the best solution
({\hat{\mathbf{W}}}^{\bullet},{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}^{\bullet})
that achieves the smallest loss
I_{S}
.
