A Matrix Exponential Generalization of the Laplace Transform of Poisson Shot Noise
Nicholas R. Olson, Jeffrey G. Andrews

Abstract
We consider a generalization of the Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise defined as an integral transform with respect to a matrix exponential. We denote this as the matrix Laplace transform and establish that it is in general a matrix function extension of the scalar Laplace transform. We show that the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise admits an expression analogous to that implied by Campbell’s theorem. We demonstrate the utility of this generalization of Campbell’s theorem in two important applications: the characterization of a Poisson shot noise process and the derivation of the complementary CDF (CCDF) and meta-distribution of signal-to-interference-and-noise (SINR) models in Poisson networks. In the former application, we demonstrate how the higher order moments of Poisson shot noise may be obtained directly from the elements of its matrix Laplace transform. We further show how the CCDF of this object may be bounded using a summation of the first row of its matrix Laplace transform. For the latter application, we show how the CCDF of SINR models with phase-type distributed desired signal power may be obtained via an expectation of the matrix Laplace transform of the interference and noise, analogous to the canonical case of SINR models with Rayleigh fading. Additionally, when the power of the desired signal is exponentially distributed, we establish that the meta-distribution may be obtained in terms of the limit of a sequence expressed in terms of the matrix Laplace transform of a related Poisson shot noise process.

One of the key tools in analyzing functionals of Poisson shot noise is Campbell’s Theorem, a version of which we restate here for completeness

Theorem 1

(Campbell’s Theorem)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}(f)=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-e^{-f(x)}\right)\Lambda(dx)\right).

Campbell’s theorem has also proved useful for the characterization of the CCDF of interference modeled as Poisson shot noise. Contributions and Summary

Motivated by these challenges and gaps in the literature, in this paper we provide a characterization of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise and demonstrate how this characterization can provide improved tractability in the settings detailed in the previous subsection.

The development of the matrix exponential generalization of the Laplace transform, termed the matrix Laplace transform, and its characterization for non-negative Poisson shot noise are presented in Sec.
II
. Our principle contributions in this section are as follows.

•

We define the matrix Laplace transform for general random variables and characterize the conditions under which it exist. In the case of non-negative random variables, we establish that the matrix Laplace transform is in general the natural matrix function
[
28
]
extension of the typical scalar Laplace transform. These results are summarized in Proposition
1
in Sec.
II
.

•

Building upon the results in Proposition
1
, we derive an expression for the matrix Laplace transform of general non-negative Poisson shot noise. In particular, we establish various conditions under which its matrix Laplace transform may be expressed in terms of a matrix exponential of a certain matrix valued integral in a manner analogous to Corollary
1
. These results form the basis for our generalization of Campbell’s theorem and are summarized in Theorem
2
in Sec.
II
. Although similar to Corollary
1
, the characterization of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise in Theorem

is not a trivial extension of Corollary

.

As we show in the applications considered in Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
, the generalization of Campbell’s theorem in Theorem
2
enables improved tractability in the important challenges mentioned in the previous sub-section, in addition to other more general settings.
In Sec.
III
we demonstrate how Theorem
2
may be used to tractably characterize a linear combination of samples of a non-negative Poisson shot noise process. The contributions of our paper pertinent to this section are as follows.

•

We establish that the higher order moments of the aforementioned object may be obtained directly from the elements of its matrix Laplace transform. This is summarized in Corollary
4
in Sec.
III
. The resulting expression obtained in Corollary
4
is significantly more concise than would be afforded by approaches based on differentiating the scalar Laplace transform. Moreover, the general approach to obtaining the higher order moments via the matrix Laplace transform is applicable to any random variable for which the higher order moments in question are finite.

•

We additionally demonstrate how Theorem
2
may be used to characterize the CCDF of the aforementioned object in terms of a limit of a sequence of approximations of the CCDF which are expressed in terms of its matrix Laplace transform. Notably, this characterization is such that upper and lower bounds on the CCDF may be obtained in terms of any term in the sequence. This result is summarized in Corollary

in Sec.

is applicable to any non-negative random variable, and is established in Proposition
2
in Sec.

This general approach to characterizing the distribution of Poisson shot noise provides a potentially more tractable alternative to the common approach of characterizing the distribution via characteristic function inversion and additionally allows for a straightforward, novel means of obtaining upper and lower bounds on its distribution.

In Sec.
IV
, we consider the application of Theorem
2
to the coverage probability and meta-distribution analysis of the SINR of a potentially non-stationary downlink Poisson cellular network. The contributions of our paper pertinent to this section are as follows.

•

We show how Theorem

This result is summarized in Corollary

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
is convergent on the spectra of
\mathbf{S}
it again follows by linearity that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}~{\rm blkdiag}\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{J}_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{S}).

(16)

Thus,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{S})
exists.

Part ii) is a direct consequence of
[
30
, Cor. 2]
, in light of i).
[
30
, Cor. 2]
states that, for all
\lambda
in the interior of the region of convergence of
\mathcal{L}_{X}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(j)}_{X}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}\left[(-X)^{j-1}e^{-\lambda X}\right]\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathbb{N}

(17)

This follows by definition for
j=0
when
\lambda
is in the region of convergence of
\mathcal{L}_{X}
. Hence, so long as
\mathbf{S}
satisfies the conditions stated in part ii), we have that
\mathcal{L}_{X}
is defined on the spectra of
\mathbf{S}
. Moreover, (
17
) implies that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
is convergent on the spectra of
\mathbf{S}
. Therefore it follows that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{S})
is the matrix function corresponding to
\mathcal{L}_{X}
.
∎

Part i) of Proposition
1
indicates that the matrix Laplace transform of a random variable is simply the matrix function generalization of its scalar Laplace transform. The matrix Laplace transform always entails the evaluation of the scalar Laplace transform on the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
, and when evaluated at non-diagonalizable matrices, the matrix Laplace transform entails the evaluation of the higher order terms of the form stated in (
13
). Under appropriate conditions on
\lambda
, as detailed for non-negative
X
in part ii), these higher order terms are the higher order derivatives of the scalar Laplace transform. Hence, the matrix Laplace transform may be seen as an alternative method of obtaining the higher order derives of scalar Laplace transform. This implies its utility in the characterization of the higher order moments and distributional characteristics of
X
, as we demonstrate for the particular case of Poisson shot noise in Sec.
III
.
Overall, Proposition
1
is important for the generalization of Campbell’s theorem in Theorem
2
, as it ensures that the matrix Laplace transform of a non-negative random variable always exists when
\mathbf{S}
has eigenvalues with positive real part. Additionally, Proposition
1
allows us to leverage established properties of matrix functions, as developed in
[
28
]
.

II-B

Matrix Laplace Transform of Poisson Shot Noise

Having established a well defined notion of matrix Laplace transforms, we now turn to our main result generalizing Campbell’s theorem for the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise. In some sense, part ii) of Proposition
1
implies that the corollary of Campbell’s theorem stated in Corollary
1
is all that is required to obtain a characterization the matrix Laplace transform. However, this characterization would only hold for matrices with eigenvalues strictly within the region of convergence of the scalar Laplace transform and would further require the derivatives of the scalar Laplace transform to be obtained. Such a characterization would provide no further tractability in practical applications, in comparison to prior work, if used directly.

Thus, alternatively, we establish an equivalent characterization of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise in terms of an integral closed form matrix function that is analogous to Corollary
1
. As we will discuss further in Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
, this formulation provides improved tractability in a variety of practical applications. Moreover, since our proof is direct, it allows for us to consider the characterization of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise for matrices that would not necessarily allow for the application of part ii) of Proposition
1
to Corollary
1
. We first establish the generalization of Campbell’s theorem for different types of Jordan block matrices in the following theorem. Note that, without loss of generality, we drop the dependence of the shot noise
I(y)
presented in (
1
) on
y
.

Theorem 2
.

(Matrix Laplace Transform of Poisson Shot Noise)

Let
\Phi
be a PPP on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity measure
\Lambda
and
H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a random field independent of
\Phi
such that
H
is independent
\Lambda
-a.s. Let the matrix Laplace transform of
H(x)
be denoted as
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\cdot;x)
.

Consider the Poisson shot noise
I=\sum_{X_{k}\in\Phi}H(X_{k})
.
The following hold.

i)

Let
\mathbf{J}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a Jordan block matrix with eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(\lambda)>0
.
Then the matrix Laplace transform of
I
evaluated at
\mathbf{J}
,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{J})
, exists and may be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{J})=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)&\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}\left(\lambda;x\right)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right\rvert<\infty\\
\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}&{\rm otherwise}.\end{cases}

(18)

ii)

Let
\mathbf{J}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
n>1
, be a Jordan block matrix with eigenvalue equal to zero. Then, if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E[H(x)^{n-1}]\Lambda(dx)
converges,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{J}_{0})=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J}_{0};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right).

(19)

Otherwise,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{J}_{0})
does not exist.

iii)

Let
\mathbf{J}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a Jordan block matrix with eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(\lambda)=0
. Assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}E[H(x)^{n-1}]\Lambda(dx)
is convergent. Then,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{J})=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right).

(20)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
.
∎

Theorem
2
, in tandem with part i) of Proposition
1
, leads to a complete characterization of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise which analogous to Corollary
1
.
This is formalized in the following corollary of Theorem
2
for matrices with eigenvalues in the interior of the right half complex plane.

Corollary 2
.

(Generalization of Campbell’s Theorem.)

Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a matrix such that the real parts of its eigenvalues are strictly positive. Assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then,
I
is finite a.s. and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{S})=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right).

(21)

Otherwise,
I
is infinite a.s. and
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}
.

Proof.

Note that the fact that
I
is finite a.s. when
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all

s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and infinite a.s. otherwise follows from Lemma
1
in Appendix
A
.

First assume that
I
is finite a.s. It
suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}_{I}(s)\neq 0
, for
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
when
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Assume that this is not the case, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Re(\lambda)>0
, be such a that
\mathcal{L}_{I}(\lambda)=0
. In light of Corollary
1
, this occurs only if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}\left(\lambda;x\right)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is divergent. Hence, by Theorem
2
and Proposition
1
, we must have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{I}^{(j)}(\lambda)=0

\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}.

(22)

Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{I}(s)
is a flat function at
s=\lambda
. Moreover, since
\lambda
is in the interior of the region of convergence of
\mathcal{L}_{I}
, by
[
30
, Cor. 2]
we have that
\mathcal{L}_{I}
is holomorphic. Hence, it follows that
\mathcal{L}_{I}
is analytic in the neighborhood of
\lambda

\mathcal{L}_{I}
is 0 in the neighborhood of
\lambda
. This is a contradiction as this, in turn, implies that
\mathcal{L}_{I}(s)=0
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. This cannot be the case as the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
implies that
\mathcal{L}_{I}(s)>0
for
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
in light of Corollary
1
.

The fact that
\mathcal{L}_{I}(s)\neq 0
, for
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
, implies that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is always well defined when
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Thus, (
21
) follows from Theorem
2
and Proposition
1
. The fact that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}
when
I
is infinite a.s. follows from Lemma
4
in Appendix
B
and Proposition
1
.
∎

Hence, for all shot noise models of practical interest, (
21
) always holds. This formulation of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise is more tractable than the direct application of Proposition
1
as it alleviates the need to explicitly determine the derivatives of the corresponding scalar Laplace transform,
\mathcal{L}_{I}(s)
. One need only determine the matrix Laplace transforms of the random field at distinct indices,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)

Note further that, while the integral in (
21

elements of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)
, one need only consider an integral over
n

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)=\mathbf{P}~{\rm blkdiag}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J}_{i};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)\right)_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{P}^{-1}.

(23)

By definition, each of the component matrix Laplace transforms,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J}_{i};x)

T(\mathbf{r})
to produce the upper triangular Toeplitz matrix with first row given by

, we may express (
21
) as

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)=\mathbf{P}{~{\rm blkdiag}}\left(\exp\left(-T\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\rm T}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{J}_{i};x)_{1,:}\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)\right)\right)_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{P}^{-1}

(24)

This only requires at most
n
integrals to be performed, which can be done in parallel when evaluated numerically.

Finally, note that (

For

Consequently, in light of Definition

III
Characterization of the Distribution and Moments of Poisson Shot Noise

We now consider several applications of the generalization of Campbell’s theorem in Theorem
2
, which demonstrate its utility to practical scenarios. In this section, we first show how the result may by used to tractably characterize linear combinations of samples of a general non-negative Poisson shot noise process. To that end, a characterization of the higher order moments is presented in Corollary
4
in Sec.
III-C
, and approximations and bounds for the CCDF of this object are presented in Corollary
5
in Sec.
III-D
. Approximation guarantees for our proposed approximation method, which is applicable to any non-negative random variable, are further established in Proposition
2
in Sec.
III-D
.

III-A

Phase-Type Distributions

The applications of Theorem
2
considered in this section and Sec.
IV
make use of
phase-type distributions
in some manner, and we first summarize their key properties before proceeding. A phase-type random variable,
Z
, is characterized by two parameters: a matrix,
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, called the sub-generator matrix, and a vector,
\mathbf{p}\in[0,1]^{n}
, called the sub-PMF. We concisely denote the distribution of
Z
as
Z\sim{\rm PH}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{p})
. Phase-type distributions are a general class of non-negative random variables which correspond to the hitting time to the absorbing state of an
n+1
state continuous time Markov chain (CTMC) with
n
transient states and one absorbing state. Thus,
\mathbf{G}
is the sub-matrix of the transition rate matrix corresponding to the transient states (termed phases), and
\mathbf{p}
is the subset of the initial distribution of the CTMC corresponding to the transient phases.

Of particular use to us in the following subsections and Sec.
IV
, is that the CCDF of a phase-type distribution may be expressed in terms of a matrix exponential
[
29
, Thm. 3.1.7]
. Namely for
Z\sim{\rm PH}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{p})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z\geq\tau)=\mathbf{p}^{\rm T}\exp\left(\mathbf{G}\tau\right)\mathbf{1}_{N\times 1}.

(27)

Note that the negation of feasible sub-generator matrices, i.e.
-\mathbf{G}
, have eigenvalues with strictly positive real parts
[
29
, Cor. 3.1.15]
, and, thus, allow for the application of Corollaries
2
and
3
. In light of this, in contrast to the typical convention, we shall represent phase-type distributions in terms of the negation of their sub-generator matrices,
\mathbf{S}=-\mathbf{G}
.

The class of phase-type distributions is quite broad and includes the exponential and Erlang distributions as special cases. In particular, an Erlang
(n,\lambda)
distribution corresponds to a PH
(-\lambda\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{e}_{1})
phase type model, where
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an upper triangular Toeplitz matrix with first row
\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)_{1,:}=[1~-1~\mathbf{0}_{1\times n-2}]
. Thus, in wireless applications with Rayleigh and Nakagami fading with an integer shape parameter, the corresponding fading powers belong to the class of phase type distributions – the exponential and Erlang distributions, respectively. Moreover, phase-type distributions are dense with respect to random variables with support on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

[
29
, 3.2.10]
. Consequently, they may be used to represent any non-negative random variable to arbitrary precision.

III-B

Formulation of Shot Noise Model

In both of the following sub-sections, we characterize the higher order moments and CCDF of the following shot noise model. Namely, consider the general Poisson shot noise process
I(y)
as defined in (
1
) and define

\displaystyle\tilde{I}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}I(y_{i})

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\geq 0,y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y_{i}\neq y_{j}.

(28)

The model we consider for
\tilde{I}
may be taken to correspond to a space-time interference model as discussed in
[
25
]
, and allows one to capture spatial correlations of interference arising from a Poisson shot noise process.
It will be useful to observe that
\tilde{I}
may be expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{I}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}H(x;y_{i})\Phi(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{H}(x)\Phi(dx),

(29)

where we have defined
\tilde{H}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}H(x;y_{i})
. In light of this formulation, for
m>1
we additionally stipulate that
\Lambda
is diffuse. This ensures that
\tilde{H}
is independent
\Lambda
-a.s. Hence
\tilde{I}
is itself an instance Poisson shot noise. Note that for
m=1
we do not require
\Lambda
to be diffuse, and
\tilde{I}
is simply an arbitrary instance of general non-negative Poisson shot noise.

III-C

Characterization of Higher Order Moments

We may directly obtain the higher order moments of
\tilde{I}
, in addition to a necessary and sufficient condition for the higher order moments of
\tilde{I}
to be finite, using the following corollary of Theorem
2
.

Corollary 4
.

(Moments of Poisson Shot Noise)

Let
\tilde{I}
be Poisson shot noise of the form stated in (
28
), and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathbf{J}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a Jordan block matrix with eigenvalue equal to zero. Then,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{I}^{n-1}]
is finite iff

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{H}(x)^{n-1}]\Lambda(dx)
is convergent, in which case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{I}^{n-1}]=(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\left(\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\left(\alpha_{i}\mathbf{J}_{0};x-y_{i}\right)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)\right)_{1,n}.

(30)

Proof.

This corollary exploits the fact that, for an arbitrary non-negative random variable,
X
, in light of Proposition
1

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{J}_{0})_{1,j}=\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{j-1}\right]

\displaystyle j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

(31)

The corollary then follows from (
31
) and Theorem
2
.
∎

The fact that
\mathbf{J}_{0}
is a Jordan block matrix simplifies the form of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{H}}(\mathbf{J}_{0};x)
. In particular, the integral in (
30
) is simply

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\left(\alpha_{i}\mathbf{J}_{0};x-y_{i}\right)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)_{1,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j-1)!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{H}(x)^{j-1}\right]\Lambda(dx).&j>1\\
0&j=1\end{cases}

(32)

Thus, the
n^{th}
moment of
\tilde{I}
may be obtained as a function of integrals of the first
n
moments of
\tilde{H}(x)
with respect to the
\Lambda
. The resulting expression for
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{I}^{n-1}]
is notably concise and relatively tractable compared to methods based on directly evaluating the higher order derivative of the scalar Laplace transform.

III-D

Characterization of the CCDF

We may further exploit Theorem
2
to characterize approximations of and bounds on the CCDF of
\tilde{I}
,
\bar{F}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
. Except for a few special cases, the CCDF of
\tilde{I}
is often not available in closed form. Consequently, we consider characterizing the CCDF as

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{I}\geq\tau Z_{N}\right),

(33)

for an appropriately chosen sequence of non-negative random variables,
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, which converge to
1
a.s. and are independent of
\tilde{I}
. This type of approximation has the desirable property that each term in the limit in (
33
) corresponds to a feasible CCDF

\displaystyle P^{(N)}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{I}\geq\tau Z_{N}\right).

(34)

The rationale for this approach is that, if we can construct
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
P^{(N)}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
is tractable, for large enough
N
,
P^{(N)}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
will be a good approximation of
\bar{F}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
which is easier to obtain.

Given that their CDFs may be expressed in terms of a matrix exponential and their generality, we consider candidates for
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, from the family of phase-type distributions. In particular, we construct
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

1.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
Z_{N}
is an
N
-phase PH
(-\mathbf{S}_{N},\mathbf{p}_{N})
random variable.

2.

\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}]
= 1.

3.

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}=1
a.s.

Under this general construction, we have that
P^{(N)}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
may be characterized in terms of the matrix Laplace transform of
\tilde{I}
:

\displaystyle{P}_{\tilde{I}}^{(N)}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{I}\geq\tau Z_{N}\lvert\tilde{I}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbf{p}_{N}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\tau^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{N}\tilde{I}\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{N\times 1}

\displaystyle=1-\mathbf{p}_{N}^{\rm T}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\tilde{I}}\left(\tau^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{N}\right)\mathbf{1}_{N\times 1}.

(35)

To make full use of this approach to characterize
\bar{F}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
, it remains to determine the parameters for the phase-type distributions for each term of
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
. To that end, we establish the following proposition, Proposition
2
, which indicates that taking
Z_{N}\sim{\rm Erlang}(N,N)
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
is optimal in terms of minimizing an upper bound on the approximation error of
{P}_{\tilde{I}}^{(N)}(\tau)
with respect to
\bar{F}_{\tilde{I}}(\tau)
. Indeed, more broadly, Proposition
2
establishes that this choice of
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is optimal for minimizing an upper bound on the approximation error associated with the application of the method detailed above to the characterization of the CCDF of any arbitrary non-negative random variable. The proposition additionally establishes a method of obtaining upper and lower bounds on the true CCDF in terms of the approximate CCDFs.

Proposition 2
.

(Approximation Guarantees for
{P}_{I}^{(N)}(\tau)
)

Let
X
be an arbitrary non-negative random variable with CCDF
\bar{F}_{X}(\tau)
, and
(\tilde{Z}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence of phase-type random variables independent of
X
such that
\tilde{Z}_{N}
has
N
phases,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{Z}_{N}]=1
, and
(\tilde{Z}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to 1 a.s. Moreover, let
\tilde{P}_{X}^{(N)}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}\left({X}\geq\tau\tilde{Z}_{N}\right)
. Then, the following hold

i)

\tilde{P}_{X}^{(N)}(\tau)
converges to
\bar{F}_{X}(\tau)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

ii)

Define
\tilde{e}^{(N)}(\tau)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{e}^{(N)}(\tau)=\max\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\inf_{\theta\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{Z}_{N}}(-\theta)e^{-\theta X\tau^{-1}}\right)\mathds{1}\{X\geq\tau\}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\inf_{\theta\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{Z}_{N}}(\theta)e^{\theta X\tau^{-1}}\right)\mathds{1}\{X<\tau\}\right]\right).

(36)

Then

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{F}_{X}(\tau)-\tilde{P}_{X}^{(N)}(\tau)\right\rvert\leq\tilde{e}^{(N)}(\tau).

(37)

iii)

Let
(Z_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

IV
SINR Analysis in Downlink Poisson Cellular Networks

In addition to providing tractable characterizations of Poisson shot noise, the generalization of Campbell’s theorem in Theorem
2
allows for improved tractability in the characterization of the CCDF and meta-distribution of SINR models associated with from Poisson networks. To that end, we consider the analysis of an exemplary SINR model pertinent to a downlink Poisson cellular network. In particular, when the desired signal power follows a phase-type distribution, the distribution of the SINR may be obtained in a manner similar to the classical form encountered in the case where the desired signal power is exponentially distributed, see e.g.
[
13
, Thm. 1]
, using the matrix Laplace transform of the interference. This result is stated in Corollary
6
in Sec.
IV-B
.
Moreover, when the desired signal power follows an exponential distribution, the meta-distribution of the SINR may be expressed in terms of the distribution of a certain shot noise process which itself may be characterized using Corollary
5
and Proposition
2
. This is summarized in Proposition
3
in Sec.
IV-C
.

IV-A

Summary of SINR Model

In both subsections, we consider the following SINR model which corresponds to a general downlink Poisson cellular network. Note, however, that the results obtained in this section may be replicated for other types of network models. Let
\Phi
be a PPP on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity measure
\Lambda
denoting the locations of base stations (BSs), and consider a user receiver located at
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The user connects with the nearest BS,
X_{y}\in\Phi
, defined as

\displaystyle X_{y}=\inf\{\lvert\lvert X-y\rvert\rvert:X\in\Phi\}.

(46)

All other BSs in the network are interferers from the perspective of the user at
y
. Denote these points of
\Phi
as
\Phi^{!X_{y}}=\Phi-\delta_{X_{y}}
. Each transmitted signal undergoes distance dependent path loss,
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The power of the signal transmitted by the serving BS (without accounting for path loss) is denoted as
P_{y}
, and is in general a random variable independent of
\Phi
. The signal powers of the interfering BSs (again prior to the application of path loss) arise from the independent random field
H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
that is also independent of
P_{y}
and
\Phi
.
\ell(r)
is assumed only to be monotonically decreasing in
r
and, similar to the path loss models considered in
[
31
]
, that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[H(x;y)\right]\ell\left(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert\right)\Lambda(dx)<\infty.

(47)

Consequently, the interference observed by the user at
y
is

\displaystyle I(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\epsilon)}H(x;y)\ell\left(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert\right)\Phi^{!X_{y}}(dx)

\displaystyle\epsilon>0.

(48)

Note that (
47
) ensures that the
I(y)
has a finite mean conditioned on
X_{y}
.
Putting these terms together and letting
L_{y}=\ell\left(\lvert\lvert X_{y}-y\rvert\rvert\right)
, the SINR observed by the user at
y
admits the expression

\displaystyle{\sf SINR}(y)=\frac{P_{y}L_{y}}{I(y)+W},

(49)

where
W
is a constant denoting the noise power.

The performance of the cellular network as captured by this SINR model may be quantified with respect to two commonly studied metrics. The first is the coverage probability with respect to the typical user located at
y
, defined as

\displaystyle P_{\rm cov}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}^{y}\left({\sf SINR}(y)\geq\tau\right),

(50)

where
\mathbb{P}^{y}
denotes the Palm distribution of the point process of users. As is typical, the user point process is assumed to be independent of the other random features of the network. Hence, the Palm distribution reduces to the nominal probability measure. The second metric of interest is the meta-distribution of the SINR with respect to the typical user at
y
. As first considered in
[
24
]
, the meta-distribution is defined with respect to the conditional success probability

\displaystyle P_{\rm S}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}\left({\sf SINR}(y)\geq\tau\lvert\Phi\right).

(51)

Then the meta-distribution is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{P_{\rm S}}(\zeta)=\mathbb{P}^{y}\left(P_{\rm S}(\tau)\geq\zeta\right)

\displaystyle\zeta\in[0,1].

(52)

Under certain conditions pertaining to the distribution of
P_{y}
, the generalization of Campbell’s theorem in Theorem
2
provides improved tractability in the analysis of the coverage probability and meta-distribution.

IV-B

Coverage Probability Analysis

We first consider the characterization of coverage probability when
P_{y}\sim{\rm PH}(-\mathbf{S},\mathbf{p})
, for general
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Using the properties of phase-type distributions and Corollary
2
, we may express the coverage probability with respect to
{\sf SINR}
as an expectation of the conditional matrix Laplace transform of
I
. We summarize this in the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

(Coverage Probability with Phase-Type Distributed Desired Signal Power)

Let
{\sf SINR}
be of the form specified in (
49
) and let the matrix Laplace transform of
H(x;y)
be denoted as
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\cdot;x,y)
. Then, when
P_{y}
follows a PH
(-\mathbf{S},\mathbf{p})
distribution the coverage probability corresponding to this SINR model may be expressed as

\displaystyle P_{\rm cov}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}_{L_{y}}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\left(\frac{\tau\ell(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert)}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S};x,y\right)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\tau W}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S}\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{n\times 1}.

(53)

Proof.

This result follows from the CDF of a phase-type distribution stated in (
27
) in light of the fact that
\mathbf{S}
is feasible with respect to Corollary
2
:

\displaystyle P_{\rm cov}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}({\sf SINR}(y)\geq\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(P_{y}L_{y}\geq\tau\left(I(y)+W\right)\big|\Phi,I(y)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{p}^{\rm T}\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S}(I(y)+W)\right)\mathbf{1}_{n\times 1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S}I(y)\right)\bigg|L_{y}\right]\exp\left(-\frac{\tau W}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S}\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{n\times 1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}_{L_{y}}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\left(\frac{\tau\ell(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert)}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S};x,y\right)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\tau W}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S}\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{n\times 1}.

(54)

The last line follows from Corollary
2
and the fact that, conditioned with
L_{y}
,
\Phi^{!X_{y}}
is a PPP with intensity measure

\displaystyle\Lambda^{!X_{y}}(B)=\Lambda(B\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))

\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(55)

∎

Interestingly, in light of the differential representation of matrix functions in Definition
3
, the expression for coverage probability in Corollary
6
is closely related to commonly employed expressions in prior work based on Faà di Bruno’s formula, stated in (
4
). However, Corollary
6
is arguably more tractable as it alleviates the need to explicitly consider (
4
) as it is implicitly entailed in evaluation of the matrix Laplace transform.
This expression is additionally more general than prior work considering coverage probability with phase-type fading, e.g
[
32
]
.

Moreover, this formulation allows for one to determine coverage probability through the characterization of the matrix integral,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\left(\frac{\tau\ell(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert)}{L_{y}}\mathbf{S};x,y\right)\right)\Lambda(dx).

(56)

This is analogous to the case where
P_{y}\sim{\rm Exp}(\lambda)
, which corresponds to the well known Rayleigh fading scenario, in which case one need only consider the scalar integral

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))}\left(1-{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\left(\frac{\tau\ell(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert)}{L_{y}}\lambda;x,y\right)\right)\Lambda(dx).

(57)

Indeed, in light of Corollary
3

\mathbf{S}

IV-C

Meta-Distribution Analysis

We conclude this section by considering the meta-distribution of the SINR in the case where
{P_{y}\sim{\rm Exp}(\lambda)}
. Using Corollary
5
, the meta-distribution may be obtained as follows.

Proposition 3
.

(Meta-Distribution of the SINR with Exponentially Distributed Desired Signal Power)

Let
{\sf SINR}
be of the form specified in (
49
) and let the Laplace transform of
H(x;y)
be denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{H}(\cdot;x,y)

follows an Exp
(\lambda)
distribution, the meta-distribution corresponding to this SINR model may be expressed as follows.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}

denote the negation of the sub-generator matrix corresponding to an Erlang

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{P_{\rm S}}(\zeta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}_{L_{y}}\left[\exp\left(-\mathbf{S}^{(N)}(L_{y},\zeta)\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{N\times 1}.

Consider first the conditional success probability. Under the assumption the

\displaystyle P_{\rm S}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}({\sf SINR}(y)\geq\tau|\Phi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(P_{y}\geq\frac{\tau}{L_{y}}(I(y)+W)\bigg|\Phi\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda\tau}{L_{y}}I(y)\right)\bigg|\Phi\right]\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda\tau W}{L_{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))}\log\left(\mathcal{L}_{H}\left(\frac{\lambda\tau\ell(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert)}{L_{y}};x,y\right)^{-1}\right)\Phi^{!X_{y}}(dx)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda\tau W}{L_{y}}\right),

(60)

where the last line follows from Corollary
1
.
Thus, defining the non-negative shot noise

\displaystyle\hat{I}(y;L_{y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(y,\ell^{-1}(L_{y}))}\log\left(\mathcal{L}_{H}\left(\frac{\lambda\tau\ell(\lvert\lvert x-y\rvert\rvert)}{L_{y}};x,y\right)^{-1}\right)\Phi^{!X_{y}}(dx),

(61)

we have

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{P_{\rm S}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\rm S}(\tau)\geq\zeta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{I}(y;L_{y})+\frac{\lambda\tau W}{L_{y}}\leq\log(\zeta^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{L_{y}}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{I}(y;L_{y})\leq\left(\log(\zeta^{-1})-\frac{\lambda\tau W}{L_{y}}\right)_{+}\bigg|L_{y}\right)\right],

(62)

where
(z)_{+}
denotes
\max(z,0)
.
(
59
) then follows from Corollary
5
noting that, conditioned on
L_{y}
,
\Phi^{!X_{y}}
is a PPP with an intensity measure of the form specified in (
55
).
∎

Note that Proposition
3
additionally implies that upper and lower bounds on the meta-distribution of the form specified in Proposition

only requires that the transmit power of the serving BS,

This provides an alternative approach to the characterization of the meta-distribution employed in prior work, which typically employs the Gil-Paelez theorem to characterize the meta distribution in terms of the imaginary moments of
P_{\rm S}(\tau)

[
24

V
Conclusion

We have developed a generalization of the Laplace transform of a random variable defined as an integral transform with respect to a matrix exponential. Defining this integral transform as the
matrix Laplace transform
, we have established its existence as the matrix function generalization of the typical scalar Laplace Transform. We further developed a generalization of Campbell’s theorem for the Laplace functional of a PPP to address the characterization of the matrix Laplace transform of Poisson shot noise.
The generalization of Campbell’s theorem allows for improved tractability in several applications of interest. In particular, we showed how the generalization of Campbell’s theorem may be used to obtain higher order moments of Poisson shot noise in terms of elements of its matrix Laplace transform. We additionally established a method to obtain arbitrarily accurate approximations of and upper and lower bounds on the CCDF of Poisson shot noise in terms of its matrix Laplace transform. The general method we used to obtain these characterizations of the CCDF is further applicable to any non-negative random variable. Additionally, we demonstrated how the generalization of Campbell’s theorem enables one to characterize the coverage probability of downlink cellular networks when the SINR model has phase-type distributed desired signal power. The resulting expression for coverage probability is analogous to the canonical case when the fading terms in the SINR model correspond to Rayleigh fading. Finally, we showed that, when the SINR model has exponentially distributed desired signal power, the meta-distribution of the SINR may be obtained in terms of the CCDF of a certain instance of Poisson shot noise. The CCDF of this shot noise may, in turn, be tractably characterized using the methods mentioned earlier.

In this appendix, we provide a series of lemmas which will prove useful for the main results detailed in Sec.

\displaystyle I=\sum_{X_{k}\in\Phi}H(X_{k}),

(63)

\Phi

\mathbb{R}^{d}

First, we establish a necessary condition for

Lemma 1
.

(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Finiteness of Poisson Shot Noise)

Let
\mathcal{L}_{H}(\cdot;x)
denote the Laplace Transform of
H(x)
and consider the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
If the integral is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
I
is finite a.s. Otherwise,
I
is infinite a.s.

Proof.

First, assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The integral may be expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathbb{E}[e^{-sH(x)}|\Phi]\right)\Lambda(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-e^{-sH(x)}\right)\right]\Lambda(dx)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-e^{-sH(x)}\right)\Lambda(dx)\right],

(64)

where the second line follows from the fact that
H
and
\Phi
are independent, and the last line follows from the Fubini-Tonelli theorem. Thus, finiteness of
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
implies that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-e^{-sH(x)}\right)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent a.s. Appealing to the version of Campbell’s theorem presented in
[
2
, Thm. 4.6]
, it follows that
I
is finite a.s.

Now, assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(s;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)
diverges for some

, say at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then, for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I\leq\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(e^{-\theta I}\geq e^{-\theta\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\theta\tau}\mathcal{L}_{I}(\theta)

\displaystyle=e^{\theta\tau}\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{H}(\theta;x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(65)

where the second line follows from Markov’s Inequality, and the third line follows from the variation of Campbell’s theorem presented in Corollary
1
. Thus it follows that

is infinite a.s.
∎

Next, we establish an intermediate form of a generalization of Campbell’s theorem which enables the matrix Laplace transform

to be obtained when

is finite a.s.

Lemma 2
.

(Generalization of Campbell’s theorem for Shot Noise Induced by Finite PPPs).

Let
\Phi
be such that

, and consider the shot noise,

, induced by
\Phi
as in (

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\cdot;x)
denote the matrix Laplace transform for
H(x)

\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a matrix such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\Lambda(dx)

is convergent. Then, the matrix Laplace transform of

exists at
\mathbf{S}
and may be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{S})=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right).

(66)

Proof.

The condition that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\Lambda(dx)
is convergent implies that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)
exists
\Lambda
-a.s. Using this fact we have

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{I}(\mathbf{S})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{X_{k}\in\Phi}\mathbf{S}H(X_{k})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{X_{k}\in\Phi}\exp\left(-\mathbf{S}H(X_{k})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{X_{k}\in\Phi}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\mathbf{S}H(X_{k})\right)\bigg|\Phi\right]\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{X_{k}\in\Phi}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};X_{k})\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{X_{k}\in\Phi}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};X_{k})\big|\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right]\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\Lambda(dx)\right)^{\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{=}}e^{-\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}{k!}\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\Lambda(dx)\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(g)}}{{=}}\exp\left(-\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mathbf{I}\right)\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\Lambda(dx)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(h)}}{{=}}\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{H}(\mathbf{S};x)\right)\Lambda(dx)\right).

(67)

Each step is justified as follows: (a) follows from the fact that

are commutative, and thus that the matrix exponential of the sum of the sequence is the product of matrix exponentials applied to the sequence

are almost surely independent conditioned on
\Phi
; (c) follows from the fact that

exists and the definition of the matrix Laplace transform;
(d) follows from the fact that the atoms of
\Phi

\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\frac{\partial\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}\in\cdot|\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)}{\partial\Lambda}=\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{-1}

commutative and that

We further establish bounds on modulus of the elements of the matrix Laplace transform of non-negative random variables. These are necessary for various steps of the proof of Theorem
2
in particular, including the application of Lemma
2
.

Lemma 3
.

(Bounds on the Matrix Laplace Transforms of Non-negative Random Variables)

Let
X
be a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
\mathbf{J}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a Jordan block matrix with eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(\lambda)>0
.
Additionally, for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
let
Z_{j}\sim
Erlang
(j,\Re(\lambda))
independent of
X
, and let
Z_{0}=0
.
Then, the matrix Laplace transform of
X
at
\mathbf{J}
,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{J})
admits the following bounds

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{J})_{1,j}\right\rvert\leq{\Re(\lambda)^{1-j}}\max\left(\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}>X\right),\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j-1}>X\right)\right)\leq{\Re(\lambda)^{1-j}}

(68)

Proof.

In light of Proposition
1
and Definition
3
we have

\displaystyle\left|\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{J})_{1,j}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(-X)^{j-1}\exp\left(-\lambda X\right)\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|(-X)^{j-1}\exp\left(-\lambda X\right)\right\rvert\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\mathbb{E}\left[(X)^{j-1}\exp\left(-\Re\left(\lambda\right)X\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\left(\Re\left(\mathbf{J}\right)\right)_{1,j}\right\rvert.

(69)

The second line follows from Jensen’s Inequality. Thus, it follows that
\left\lvert\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\mathbf{J})_{1,j}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\mathbf{J}\right))_{1,j}\right\rvert
.

Now, define the matrix
\mathbf{V}={\rm diag}((-\Re(\lambda))^{t})_{t=0}^{n-1}
and let
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an upper triangular Toeplitz matrix with first row

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)_{1,:}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&\mathbf{0}_{1\times n-2}\end{bmatrix}.

(70)

Note that
\Re\left(\mathbf{J}\right)
may be factored as
\Re\left(\mathbf{J}\right)=\Re(\lambda)\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
. Hence, by Proposition
1
and
[
28
, Thm. 1.13]
we have

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\left(\Re\left(\mathbf{J}\right)\right)=\mathbf{V}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\left(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q}\right)\mathbf{V}^{-1}.

(71)

Since
\mathbf{V}
is diagonal it follows that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}\left(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q}\right)
is an upper triangular Toeplitz matrix. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\mathbf{J}\right))_{1,j}=(-1)^{j-1}{\Re(\lambda)^{1-j}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})_{1,j}

(72)

Hence we need only show that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})_{1,j}\right\rvert\leq\max\left(\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}>X|\Phi\right),\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j-1}>X\right)\right).

(73)

To show this, we exploit the fact that
\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q}
corresponds to the sub-generator matrix of the phase-type representation of an Elang
(n,\Re(\lambda))
random variable (see Sec.
III-A
for a discussion of phase type random variables). In particular, for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
define
\mathbf{w}_{j}
as

\mathbf{w}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{1\times j}&\mathbf{0}_{1\times n-j}.\end{bmatrix}^{\rm T}
. Then,
we may express
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})_{1,j}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})_{1,j}=\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{w}_{j}-\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{w}_{j-1},

(74)

For all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{X}(\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{w}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\rm T}\exp\left(-\Re\left(\lambda\right)\mathbf{Q}X\right)\mathbf{w}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{Z}_{j}>X\right).

(75)

Hence (
68
) follows noting that
\left\lvert\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}\geq X\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j-1}\geq X\right)\right\rvert\leq\max\left(\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}>X\right),\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j-1}>X\right)\right)
.
∎

(Limits of

\displaystyle\exp

Proof of Proposition
