Topology optimization of contact-aided thermo-mechanical regulators
Anna Dalklint, Joe Alexandersen, Andreas Henrik Frederiksen, Konstantinos Poulios, Ole Sigmund

Abstract
Topology optimization is used to systematically design contact-aided thermo-mechanical regulators, i.e. components whose effective thermal conductivity is tunable by mechanical deformation and contact. The thermo-mechanical interactions are modeled using a fully coupled non-linear thermo-mechanical finite element framework. To obtain the intricate heat transfer response, the components leverage self-contact, which is modeled using a third medium contact method. The effective heat transfer properties of the regulators are tuned by solving a topology optimization problem using a traditional gradient based algorithm. Several designs of thermo-mechanical regulators in the form of switches, diodes and triodes are presented.



1
Introduction

Managing heat transfer poses a significant challenge in modern technology.
While this challenge always has existed for conventional heating and cooling devices like heat exchangers and refrigerators, it has become increasingly important in modern applications to e.g. enhance the lifespan of electronics, improve advanced manufacturing processes, minimize energy consumption in high-performance computing or data storage clusters, and enable certain functionalities in space applications. Traditionally, the thermal properties of these systems are regulated using linear and passive thermal components, whose thermal properties are mainly governed by their static thermal conductivity and size. However, there is a growing interest in exploring components that harness switchable and non-linear heat transfer mechanisms to obtain tunable heat transfer control. This development has lead to the realization of advanced components such as thermal switches, diode and triodes, cf.
Wehmeyer et al. 2017
and
Castelli et al. 2023
. In this work, we refer to components that exhibit non-linear heat transfer functions as
thermal regulators
.

Thermal regulators are useful in various applications. For example, they can be used to actively regulate the indoor climate of buildings (
Miao et al. 2022
), or actively manage heat dissipation rates in e.g. electronics (
Yang et al. 2019
) and spacecrafts (
Milanez and Mantelli 2003
). See review articles by e.g.
Wehmeyer et al. 2017
as well as
Wong et al. 2021
and
Ram et al. 2023
for an exhaustive exploration of application possibilities. Actuation mechanisms and thereby design of the thermal regulators can vary. One approach to the design of thermal regulators relies on active actuation, i.e. when the effective conductivity of the regulator is actively controlled via e.g. applied pressure (
Cho et al. 2007
), electric fields (
Almanza et al. 2018
) or magnetic fields (
Bosisio et al. 2015
). Another approach relies on passive actuation, such that the component self-regulates in response to the exterior temperature changes via thermal expansion/shrinking (
Heo et al. 2019
) or material phase changes (
Gulfam et al. 2019
). Existing designs of regulators often leverage conduction via moving structural parts, i.e. mechanics, (
Cho et al. 2007
), or gas/fluid convection (
Yang et al. 2019
) to change their effective thermal conductivity during actuation. In this work, we focus on the former, i.e.
thermo-mechanical
regulators.

A thermal regulator should ultimately exhibit a non-linear or switchable heat transfer function, but the desired shape of this transfer function depends on the application. For example, a
thermal switch
exhibits two distinct functional states: an “on” state facilitating heat transfer and an “off” state where heat transfer is impeded. A
thermal diode
, on the other hand, should rectify the heat flow, such that the heat transfer along a specific axis is facilitated or impeded depending on the sign of the temperature gradient. An even more complex component is the
thermal triode
, which, analogously to an electrical triode/transistor, should be able to amplify an incoming signal. A natural and efficient way of controlling the effective thermal conductivity in a thermo-mechanical regulator is to develop designs that utilize structural contact. In this way, the effective heat conductivity is high when contact occurs, and otherwise low, cf. the schematic of a contact-aided thermal switch in
Fig.
1
.

Fig. 1.
:
A schematic of a thermal switch leveraging contact.

In the existing literature, thermal regulators are designed based on human intuition and time-consuming design-and-experimental iterations. This process can prove cumbersome when designing regulators with highly complex heat transfer functions. Fortunately, this design process is an obvious candidate for more efficient systematic design approaches such as topology optimization. One hurdle to the topology optimization of contact-aided thermal regulators is however their modeling complexity, caused by 1) the coupled thermo-mechanics and 2) the intrinsically non-linear contact physics.

The topology optimization of thermo-mechanical problems appears in numerous works. For example,
Sigmund 2001a
,
Sigmund 2001b
designed thermal actuators undergoing finite deformations using topology optimization, which later were realized by
Jonsmann et al. 1999
. Recently,
Granlund et al. 2024
considered the topology optimization of coupled transient non-linear thermo-mechanical problems. A topology optimization approach to inverse motion based form finding of coupled static thermo-mechanical problems was investigated by
Sui et al. 2023
.
Chung et al. 2020
used a level-set approach to the topology optimization of structures undergoing large deformations due to thermal and mechanical loads.

Recently,
Bluhm et al. 2021
illustrated the power of the so-called third medium contact method in topology optimization problems of contact-aided mechanisms. This contact model, pioneered by
Wriggers et al. 2013
, approximates contact pressures via the reactive tractions that occur when a third medium, that must be present in between the contacting surfaces, is compressed. Indeed, via appropriate constitutive modeling, the third medium naturally exists in the topology optimization formulation in the form of the ersatz material.
Bluhm et al. 2021
employed a monolithic approach to the topology optimization problem, i.e. solved the minimization of the objective and physical residuals simultaneously using Newton’s method. In a recent work,
Frederiksen et al. 2023
instead implemented the approach in the traditional staggered fashion, and use MMA (Method of Moving Asymptotes, cf.
Svanberg 1987
) to update the design. Other examples of the the third medium contact method in topology optimization include
Bluhm et al. 2023
, whereby non-linear springs are designed, and
Dalklint et al. 2023
, who design metamaterials that utilized self-contact to obtain a prescribed macroscopic stress-strain behavior. Very recently,
Faltus et al. 2024
extended a third medium contact model to pneumatically actuated systems.

To summarize; in this work, we use topology optimization to design thermo-mechanical regulators that harness self-contact to exhibit highly non-linear heat transfer functions. To this end, we extend the prospect of the third medium contact method to thermo-mechanical problems, such that we now seek a third medium that, in addition to contact tractions, transfers heat when contact occurs, but otherwise approximates an isolating material. The finite element method is used to model the fully coupled non-linear thermo-mechanical problem. The optimization problems are solved using the gradient-based optimizer MMA, and the gradients of the cost and constraint functions are obtained from an adjoint sensitivity analysis. The numerical results display various designs of thermo-mechanical switches, diodes and triodes.

Preliminaries

In our steady-state thermo-mechanical formulation, the reference, undeformed configuration
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the current, deformed configuration
\Omega_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are distinguished. A material point
\text{\boldmath$X$}\in\Omega
is displaced to its deformed, spatial position
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\Omega_{c}
via the displacement field
\text{\boldmath$u$}(\text{\boldmath$X$}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that
\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$X$}+\text{\boldmath$u$}(\text{\boldmath$X$})
. The local deformation is described by the deformation gradient
\text{\boldmath$F$}=\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$1$}+\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\text{\boldmath$u$}
, where
\nabla
is the gradient operator with respect to
X
, and
J=\text{det}(\text{\boldmath$F$})
. The temperature field is denoted
\theta(\text{\boldmath$X$}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The deformation gradient is assumed to satisfy the multiplicative split
\text{\boldmath$F$}=\text{\boldmath$F$}^{e}\text{\boldmath$F$}^{\theta}
, where
\text{\boldmath$F$}^{e}
and
\text{\boldmath$F$}^{\theta}
are the elastic and thermal parts of the deformation gradient, respectively. An isotropic thermal deformation is assumed, i.e.
\text{\boldmath$F$}^{\theta}=\left(J^{\theta}\right)^{1/3}\text{\boldmath$1$}
with
J^{\theta}=\text{det}(\text{\boldmath$F$}^{\theta})=e^{3\alpha(\theta-\theta_{o})}
, where
\alpha
is the thermal expansion coefficient and
\theta_{o}
is the reference temperature. For future reference, we denote
J^{e}=\text{det}(\text{\boldmath$F$}^{e})
.

The undeformed boundary
\partial\Omega
with normal
n
, is decomposed into the complementary surfaces
\partial\Omega_{u}
and
\partial\Omega_{t}
for the mechanical and
\partial\Omega_{\theta}
,
\partial\Omega_{q}
and
\partial\Omega_{q_{c}}
for the thermal boundary value problems. For the mechanical problem, we prescribe the displacement
\text{\boldmath$u$}=\bar{\text{\boldmath$u$}}
on
\partial\Omega_{u}
and the traction
\text{\boldmath$t$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
on
\partial\Omega_{t}
, whereas for the thermal problem we prescribe the temperature
\theta=\bar{\theta}
on
\partial\Omega_{\theta}
, the heat flux
\text{\boldmath$q$}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{q}
and Newton convection
\text{\boldmath$q$}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$}=h\left(\theta-\theta_{\infty}\right)
on
\partial\Omega_{q_{c}}
, where
h
is the heat transfer coefficient and
\theta_{\infty}
is the temperature of the surrounding medium. For simplicity, it is always assumed that
\text{\boldmath$u$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
on
\partial\Omega_{q_{c}}
. The heat flux in the undeformed configuration follows from Fourier’s law, i.e.

\text{\boldmath$q$}=-J\text{\boldmath$F$}^{-1}\text{\boldmath$\kappa$}\text{\boldmath$F$}^{-T}\cdot\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\theta,

(1)

where
\text{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\kappa\text{\boldmath$1$}
is the spatial isotropic heat conductivity tensor and
1
is the identity.

Following
Miehe 1995
and
Holzapfel 2000
, we assume reversible, elastic, mechanical response, such that the weak forms corresponding to the thermo-mechanical problem are

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{u}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta,\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u};\delta\text{\boldmath$u$})&\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Psi_{,u}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta;\delta\text{\boldmath$u$})\,dV+\int_{\partial\Omega_{u}}\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u}\cdot\delta\text{\boldmath$u$}\,dS=0,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta,\lambda_{\theta};\delta\theta)&\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\delta\theta\cdot\text{\boldmath$q$}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta)\,dV+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\theta}}\lambda_{\theta}\cdot\delta\theta\,dS+\int_{\partial\Omega_{q_{c}}}h\left(\theta-\theta_{\infty}\right)\delta\theta\,dS=0,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\lambda_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u};\delta\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u})&\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega_{u}}\left(\text{\boldmath$u$}-\bar{\text{\boldmath$u$}}\right)\cdot\delta\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u}\,dS=0,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\lambda_{\theta}}(\theta,\lambda_{\theta};\delta\lambda_{\theta})&\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega_{\theta}}\left(\theta-(\theta_{o}+\bar{\theta})\right)\cdot\delta\lambda_{\theta}\,dS=0,\end{array}

(2)

for all
(\delta\text{\boldmath$u$},\delta\theta,\delta\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u},\delta\lambda_{\theta})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R})
, and where the boundary conditions are applied in a weak sense via the Lagrange multiplier fields
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u}\in\partial\Omega_{u}
and
\lambda_{\theta}\in\partial\Omega_{\theta}
. It is noted that
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u}
and
\lambda_{\theta}
represents unknown reaction tractions and normal heat fluxes, i.e.
\text{\boldmath$n$}\cdot\text{\boldmath$q$}
, respectively. In the above, the Kelvin-Joule effect is neglected and vanishing heat sources are assumed within
\Omega
. We further let
\Psi
denote the strain energy density, which is choosen according to the isotropic neo-Hookean type hyperelastic model

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\Psi(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta)&\displaystyle=\frac{K}{2}\text{ln}(J^{e})^{2}+\frac{G}{2}\left((J^{e})^{-2/3}\left\lVert\text{\boldmath$F$}^{e}\right\rVert^{2}-3\right)\\[10.0pt]
&\displaystyle=\frac{K}{2}\left(\text{ln}(J)^{2}-6\alpha(\theta-\theta_{o})\text{ln}(J)+9\alpha^{2}(\theta-\theta_{o})^{2}\right)+\frac{G}{2}\left(J^{-2/3}\left\lVert\text{\boldmath$F$}\right\rVert^{2}-3\right),\end{array}

(3)

where
K=E/3(1-2\nu)
,
G=E/2(1+\nu)
,
E
and
\nu
denote the initial bulk modulus, shear modulus, Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio in the limit of infinitesimal strains. From the strain energy density in
Eq. (
3
)
, the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is computed as

\text{\boldmath$P$}(\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\text{\boldmath$u$})=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\text{\boldmath$F$}}=K\left(\text{ln}(J)\text{\boldmath$F$}^{-T}-3\alpha(\theta-\theta_{o})\text{\boldmath$F$}^{-T}\right)+GJ^{-2/3}\text{dev}(\text{\boldmath$F$}\text{\boldmath$F$}^{T})\text{\boldmath$F$}^{-T},

(4)

where
\text{dev}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=\text{\boldmath$A$}-\frac{1}{3}\text{tr}(\text{\boldmath$A$})\text{\boldmath$1$}
for an arbitrary second order tensor
A
. In this way, we find
\Psi_{,u}=\text{\boldmath$P$}:\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\delta\text{\boldmath$u$}
, cf.
Eq. (
2
)
. For later convenience, we define

\mathcal{R}(\text{\boldmath$a$};\delta\text{\boldmath$a$})=\mathcal{R}_{u}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta,\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u};\delta\text{\boldmath$u$})+\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta,\lambda_{\theta};\delta\theta)+\mathcal{R}_{\lambda_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$u$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u};\delta\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u})+\mathcal{R}_{\lambda_{\theta}}(\theta,\lambda_{\theta};\delta\lambda_{\theta})=0,

(5)

where
\text{\boldmath$a$}=(\text{\boldmath$u$},\theta,\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{u},\lambda_{\theta})
.

3
Model components

3.1
Topology optimization

The goal of the topology optimization is to distribute the material in the design domain
\Omega
, such that an objective function is minimized. To this end, let
z\in\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
denote the design variable volume fraction field. To regularize the optimization problem, the design space is restricted via the PDE filter (
Lazarov and Sigmund 2011
), such that fine scale oscillations of
z
are penalized. This provides a smoothed field
\zeta\in\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
, as a solution to the following boundary value problem

\mathcal{R}_{\zeta}(\zeta,z;\delta\zeta)=\int_{\Omega}l^{2}\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\zeta\cdot\text{\boldmath$\nabla$}\delta\zeta\,dV+\int_{\Omega}\left(\zeta-z\right)\delta\zeta\,dV=0,

(6)

for all
\delta\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
. In the above,
l
is the filter radius.

This filtering invariably produces regions wherein
\zeta(\text{\boldmath$X$})\in(0,1)
. In order to push the solution towards a “black and white” design, thresholding (
Guest et al. 2004
) is employed via the approximate Heaviside projection (
Wang et al. 2011
)

\bar{\zeta}=H_{\beta,\eta}(\zeta)=\frac{\text{tanh}(\beta\eta)+\text{tanh}(\beta(\zeta-\eta))}{\text{tanh}(\beta\eta)+\text{tanh}(\beta(1-\eta))},

(7)

where
\beta
and
\eta=0.5
are numerical parameters that control the slope and position of the Heaviside function. Additionally, intermediate
\zeta(\text{\boldmath$X$})\in(0,1)
regions are implicitly penalized via the SIMP scheme (
Bendsøe 1989
), such that the material parameters ultimately are interpolated as

\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle K=\delta_{o}^{E}+(1-\delta_{o}^{E})\bar{\zeta}^{p}K_{o},\ \ G=\delta_{o}^{E}+(1-\delta_{o}^{E})\bar{\zeta}^{p}G_{o},\\[10.0pt]
\kappa=\delta_{o}^{\kappa}+(1-\delta_{o}^{\kappa})\bar{\zeta}^{p}\kappa_{o}\ \ \text{and}\ \ K\alpha=\bar{\zeta}^{p}K\alpha_{o}.\end{array}

(8)

In the above,
K_{o},G_{o},\kappa_{o}
and
\alpha_{o}
are the reference bulk-, shear modulus, thermal conductivity and thermal expansion coefficient, respectively. Also,
p\geq 0
is the penalization parameter and
\delta_{o}^{E},\delta_{o}^{\kappa}\geq 0
and
\delta_{o}^{\alpha}=0
define the ersatz material properties.

3.2
Internal contact

In topology optimization, it has recently been suggested to employ the so-called third medium contact method (
Wriggers et al. 2013
) to model internal contact, cf.
Bluhm et al. 2021
,
Frederiksen et al. 2023
, and
Dalklint et al. 2023
. In this work, the prospect of the third medium contact method is further extended to the problem of heat transfer between bodies in contact, such that the third medium now transfers heat when contact occurs, but otherwise acts as a thermally isolating material. Analogously to the purely mechanical problem, it can be shown that this behavior can be obtained exclusively via appropriate constitutive modeling of the third medium. This effect is readily seen by examining the definition of the heat flux in
Eq. (
1
)
. When the third medium is compressed,
J\rightarrow 0
and since
\text{\boldmath$F$}^{-1}
is inversely proportional to
J
, the heat flux in the normal direction of the surface in contact
\text{\boldmath$n$}\cdot\text{\boldmath$q$}\rightarrow\infty
.

In all real world thermal contact problems, a so called
thermal contact resistance
exists due to irregularities in the contacting surfaces which impede the heat transfer. This contact resistance will give rise to a temperature jump across the contact interface at the macroscopic scale. Experiments show that the thermal contact resistance decreases with increased pressure, explained by the fact that the increased pressure causes more surface area to come into contact, increasing the heat transfer. Unfortunately, there exist no canonical form of the relationship between the thermal contact resistance, or its inverse; the thermal contact conductance, and the applied pressure. Indeed, this relationship is usually explored in an experimental setting, cf. e.g.
Rosochowska et al. 2003
and
Semiatin et al. 1987
. However, there exist numerical models which approximates the thermal contact resistance. For example,
Aalilija et al. 2021
illustrated the use of a diffuse contact interface model to capture the thermal contact resistance. As we will show in the subsequent numerical examples, the third medium contact method also naturally captures the effect of this thermal contact resistance via the hyperelastic modeling of the third medium, i.e. the choice of the strain energy density function and material parameters.

3.3
Numerical solution procedure

The fully coupled thermo-mechanical system
Eq. (
5
)
is solved using Newton’s method, i.e. in each step the full system
Eq. (
5
)
is linearized to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{{R}}(\text{\boldmath$a$}+d\text{\boldmath$a$};\delta\text{\boldmath$a$})\approx\displaystyle\mathcal{{R}}(\text{\boldmath$a$};\delta\text{\boldmath$a$})+\mathcal{{R}}_{,a}(\text{\boldmath$a$};\delta\text{\boldmath$a$},d\text{\boldmath$a$})={0},

(10)

which is iteratively solved for
d\text{\boldmath$a$}
, such that
\text{\boldmath$a$}\leftarrow\text{\boldmath$a$}+d\text{\boldmath$a$}
. Without loss of generality, we limit the numerical examples to plane strain two dimensional problems. The presented thermo-mechanical topology optimization framework is implemented in Python where all tangent matrices and residual vectors are computed via the finite element library GetFEM (
Renard and Poulios 2020
), which performs all underlying computations in C++. Design updates are based on a Python implementation (
Deetman 2021
) of the Method of Moving Asymptotes (MMA) (
Svanberg 1987
,
Svanberg 2007
).

3.4
Thermal contact

To illustrate how the third medium contact method facilitates thermal contact, the simple rod problem depicted in

\delta_{o}^{\kappa}
in the following analysis. For simplicity we assign
\alpha=0

)
. We set
h=1
kW/m
2

\theta_{\infty}=20

\degree

\partial\Omega_{u}

To obtain self-contact in the rod, the Dirichlet condition
\text{\boldmath$u$}=(\bar{u},0)

\bar{u}=-0.5L
, cf.

-\frac{d}{dX}\left(\kappa\frac{d\theta(X)}{dX}\right)=0,\quad X\in\Omega,

(11)

over the deformed rod after contact has been initiated, i.e.
\Omega=[0,L_{c}]

\delta_{o}^{\kappa}

\delta_{o}^{\kappa}\in\{10^{-4},10^{-3}\}

7
)
) is used, wherefore we expect the final design to be approximately binary, i.e. the above 2) conclusion still holds.

3.5
Optimization problem

The optimization problems solved can be written in the following format

\mathbb{P}\ \begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{z}{\text{min}}\ C({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$}),\\[8.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\begin{cases}{\mathcal{R}}(\text{\boldmath$a$};\delta\text{\boldmath$a$})={0},\\[5.0pt]
\mathcal{R}_{\zeta}(\zeta,z;\delta\zeta)={0},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle g=\int_{\Omega}H_{\beta,\eta_{d}}(\zeta)\,dV-V^{*}\leq 0,\\[8.0pt]
0\leq z\leq 1,\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(14)

where we define the objective function
C
and an upper bound volume constraint
g
, ensuring lightweight designs. In this work, thin features are penalized in the designs via the inclusion of the volume constraint on the dilated design field
H_{\beta,\eta_{d}}(\zeta)
, where
\eta_{d}=0.4
(cf.
Frederiksen et al. 2023
).

3.5.1
Sensitivity analsysis

The topology optimization problem is solved in a staggered fashion using the adjoint method, wherefore in each design iteration,
Eq. (
2
)
is solved exactly. In the adjoint method, the augmented objective function

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle C^{*}({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$})=&\displaystyle C({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$})+\mathcal{{R}}_{a}({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a})+\mathcal{R}_{\zeta}(\zeta,z;\mu_{\zeta}),\end{array}

(15)

is defined, where
\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a}=(\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{u},\mu_{\theta},\mu_{\lambda_{u}},\mu_{\lambda_{\theta}})
is the adjoint vector.
Taking the first variation with respect to the design variable field
z
renders

\begin{array}[]{ll}\delta C^{*}=&\mathcal{R}_{\zeta,z}(\zeta,z;\mu_{\zeta},\delta z)\\[10.0pt]
&+C^{*}_{,\zeta}+\mathcal{{R}}_{,\zeta}({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a},\delta\zeta)+\mathcal{R}_{\zeta,\zeta}(\zeta,z;\mu_{\zeta},\delta\zeta)\\[10.0pt]
&+C^{*}_{,a}+\mathcal{{R}}_{,a}({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a},\delta\text{\boldmath$a$}),\end{array}

(16)

and to annihilate the implicit variation
\delta\text{\boldmath$a$}
,
\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a}
is assigned such that

C^{*}_{,a}+\mathcal{{R}}_{,a}({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a},\delta\text{\boldmath$a$})=\text{\boldmath$0$}.

(17)

Next, the implicit variation
\delta\zeta
is annihilated by assign
\mu_{\zeta}
as the solution to

C^{*}_{,\zeta}+\mathcal{{R}}_{,\zeta}({\zeta},\text{\boldmath$a$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{a},\delta\zeta)+\mathcal{R}_{\zeta,\zeta}(\zeta,z;\mu_{\zeta},\delta\zeta)=0.

(18)

Finally, the sensitivity expression reduces to

\delta C^{*}=\mathcal{R}_{\zeta,z}(\zeta,z;\mu_{\zeta},\delta z).

(19)

The sensitivity of the volume constraint
g
is trivial and therefore omitted in this presentation.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have via several numerical examples exemplified how the third medium contact method can be utilized to model complex thermo-mechanical contact problems. Indeed, it has been shown that the method inherently captures the thermal contact resistance that is present in real-world contact problems via the third medium, i.e. ersatz material properties. It has specifically been shown that the magnitude of the thermal contact resistance can be tuned via the ersatz material thermal conductivity contrast,
\delta_{o}^{\kappa}
. The implemented thermo-mechanical contact framework was shown to correspond well to an analytical solution.

The fully coupled thermo-mechanical numerical framework was applied to solve several topology optimization problems involving the design of thermal regulators that leverage contact as a non-linear mechanism. More specifically, we designed thermal switches, triodes and a diode. To validate the designs, a post-processing analysis using body-fitted meshes was conducted.
