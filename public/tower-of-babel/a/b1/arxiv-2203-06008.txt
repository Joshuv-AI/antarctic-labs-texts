Delaunay-like Triangulation of Smooth Orientable Submanifolds by ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -Norm Minimization
Dominique Attali, André Lieutier

Abstract
In this paper, we study the shape reconstruction problem, when the shape we wish to reconstruct is an orientable smooth d d -dimensional submanifold of the Euclidean space. Assuming we have as input a simplicial complex K K that approximates the submanifold (such as the Čech complex or the Rips complex), we recast the problem of reconstucting the submanifold from K K as an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm minimization problem in which the optimization variable is a d d -chain of K K over the field ℝ \mathbb{R} . Providing that K K satisfies certain reasonable conditions, we prove that the considered minimization problem has a unique solution which triangulates the submanifold and coincides with the flat Delaunay complex introduced and studied in a companion paper [ 6 ] . Since the objective is a weighted ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm and the constraints are linear, the triangulation process can thus be implemented by linear programming.

Our reconstruction method consists in computing the minimal cycle

In contrast, thanks to Pythagorean Theorem, weighting edges by the

\operatorname{E_{\operatorname{del}}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{e}\operatorname{length}(e)^{3}\,|\gamma(e)|

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\gamma}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle\gamma}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\operatorname{E_{\operatorname{del}}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\partial\gamma=0,

\displaystyle\operatorname{flux}_{[a,b]}(\gamma)=1

We aim at generalizing the above problem beyond dimension one
manifolds and at identifying sufficient conditions for the support of
its solution to be a triangulation. But for that we need first to make
a detour to Delaunay complexes.

Variational formulation for Delaunay complexes.

The starting point of the work presented in this paper is the
observation that when we consider a point cloud
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
its Delaunay complex can be expressed as the solution of a particular

\ell_{p}
-norm minimization problem. This fact is best explained by
lifting the point set
P
vertically onto the paraboloid
\mathscr{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
whose equation is
x_{d+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}
. Denoting by
\hat{P}
the lifted points, it is well-known that
the Delaunay complex of
P
is isomorphic to the boundary complex of
the lower convex hull of
\hat{P}
.

Starting from this equivalence, Chen has observed in

[
23
]
that the Delaunay complex of
P
minimizes over
all triangulations
T
of
P
the
\ell_{p}
-norm of the difference
between the following two functions: the first function maps each
point
x

to its vertical projection onto the lifted triangulation
\hat{T}
and
the second function maps each point
x

to the lifted point
\hat{x}\in\mathscr{P}
. This variational formulation has been successfully
exploited in

[
2
,
21
,
24
]
for
the generation of
Optimal Delaunay Triangulations
. When
p=1
,
the
\ell_{p}
-norm associated to
T
is what we call in this paper the
Delaunay energy of
T
and, can be interpreted as the
(d+1)
-volume
enclosed between the lifted triangulation
\hat{T}
and the
paraboloid
\mathscr{P}
. Given a
d
-simplex
\sigma
, we call the

(d+1)
-volume enclosed between the convex hull of
\hat{\sigma}
and

\mathscr{P}
the
Delaunay weight
of
\sigma
and denote it as

\omega(\sigma)
. The Delaunay energy of
T
can then be simply
expressed as the sum of the Delaunay weights of its
d
-simplices.

Contributions.

We present a variational formulation to submanifold reconstruction in
Euclidean space, that both extends our variational approach for curve
reconstruction in the plane and the variational approach for
generating Delaunay complexes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider a finite set of
points
P
that sample an unknown
d
-dimensional submanifold
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and suppose that we have at hand a simplicial
complex
K
whose vertex set is
P
(such as for instance the Čech
complex of
P
or the Vietoris-Rips complex of
P
). Given as input

K
, our goal is to find a triangulation of
\mathcal{M}
contained in
K
.

A crucial ingredient in our variational formulation is to embed the
triangulations contained in
K
inside the vector space formed by
simplicial
d
-cycles
1
1

1

Or relative
d
-cycles when the
considered domain has a boundary.
of
K
over the field
\mathbb{R}
. In
spirit, this is similar to what is done in the theory of minimal
surfaces, when oriented surfaces are identified to particular elements
of a much larger set, namely the space of currents

[
41
]
, which enjoys the nice property of being a
vector space. Our method for reconstructing submanifolds consists in
solving the following optimization problem over
d
-chains of
K
:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\gamma}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle\gamma}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\operatorname{E_{\operatorname{del}}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\gamma}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\partial\gamma=0,

\displaystyle\operatorname{load}_{A}(\gamma)=1

The objective function is the Delaunay energy, whose definition
we adapt to the
d
-chains
\gamma
of
K
by setting

\operatorname{E_{\operatorname{del}}}(\gamma)=\sum_{\sigma}\omega(\sigma)\,|\gamma(\sigma)|,

where the sum is over all
d
-simplices
\sigma
of
K
and

\omega(\sigma)
is the Delaunay weight of
\sigma
. The first
constraint expresses the fact that we are searching for

d
-cycles. The second constraint is a linear equation which may be
interpreted as a kind of normalization of
\gamma
that extends the
condition
\operatorname{flux}_{[a,b]}(\gamma)=1
beyond dimension one. The letter

A
designates a set of parameters that specifies where the load is
computed.
Thus, our optimization problem is an
\ell_{1}
-norm
minimization problem under linear constraints. As such, it can be
turned into a linear optimization problem in the standard form through
slack variables as explained in
Appendix
A
, and can be addressed by
standard linear programming techniques such as the simplex algorithm.

One important point is that the objective function (
i.e.
the
Delaunay energy) is a weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm. Put it simply, we are
searching for weighted
\ell_{1}
-minima. The celebrated sparsity of

\ell_{1}
-minima manifests itself in our context by the fact that the
support of such minima is sparse, in other words it is non-zero only
on a small subset of simplices of
K
. Our main result is a set of
conditions under which our optimization problem has a unique solution
whose support is a triangulation of
\mathcal{M}
(either with theoretical or
practical normalization).

Proof technique.

The proof requires us to introduce an elaborate construction, the
Delloc complex
of
P
, as a tool to describe the solution. The

d
-simplices of that complex possess exactly the property that we
need for our analysis. In a companion paper

[
6
]
we show that the Delloc complex
indeed provides a triangulation of the manifold, assuming the set of
sample points
P
to be sufficiently dense, safe, and not too
noisy. Incidently, the Delloc complex coincides with the
flat
Delaunay complex
introduced in our companion paper

[
6
]
and is akin to the
tangential Delaunay complex
introduced and studied in

[
9
,
10
]
. When the
manifold is sufficiently densely sampled by the data points, all three
constructions are locally isomorphic to a (weighted) Delaunay
triangulation computed in a local tangent space to the
manifold. Intuitively, this indicates that the Delaunay energy should
locally reach a minimum for all three constructions and, therefore
ought to be also a global minimum. Actually, turning this intuitive
reasoning into a correct proof turns out to be more tricky than it
appears and is the main purpose of the present paper. In particular,
we need to globally compare the Delaunay energy of the cycle carrying
the Delloc complex with that of alternate
d
-cycles, and this
requires us to carefully distribute the Delaunay energy along
barycentric coordinates.

For the purpose of the proof, it is convenient to first consider a
rather artificial problem (Problem (
5.1
))
where, besides the sample
P
, the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is known. At the end
of the paper, we show how to turn this problem into a more realistic
one (Problem (
17
)) that takes as input only the
sample of the unknown manifold, and is correct assuming that
reasonable sampling conditions are met.

An overview of the proof is provided in Section

6.2
.

2.2
Simplicial complexes

In this section, we review some background notation on algebraic
topology and refer the reader to
[
43
]
for a
detailed introduction to the topic.

All simplices and simplicial complexes that we consider in the paper
are abstract. We recall that an
abstract simplicial complex
is
a collection
K
of finite non-empty sets with the property that if

\sigma
belongs to
K
, so does every non-empty subset of

\sigma
. Each element
\sigma
of
K
is called an
abstract
simplex
and its
dimension
is one less than its cardinality,

\dim\sigma=\operatorname{card}\sigma-1
. A simplex of dimension

i
is called an
i
-simplex. If
\tau
and
\sigma
are two
simplices such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma
, then
\tau
is called a

face
of
\sigma
, and
\sigma
is called a
coface
of

\tau
. The elements of
\sigma
are also referred to as the

vertices
of
\sigma
and the
vertex set
of
K
is the
set of vertices of all simplices in
K
,
\operatorname{Vert}{K}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in K}\sigma
. When an abstract simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
has its vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it is
naturally associated to the geometric simplex defined as

\operatorname{conv}\sigma
.
The dimension of
\operatorname{conv}\sigma
, which is the dimension of the affine space

\operatorname{aff}\sigma
, cannot be larger than the dimension of the
abstract simplex
\sigma
. When the dimension of the geometric simplex

\operatorname{conv}\sigma
coincides with that of the abstract simplex
\sigma
,
we say that
\sigma
is
non-degenerate
.
For a simplicial complex
K
with vertices in

\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we say that

is

) if (1)

.

Given a set of abstract simplices

with vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(not

designate the set of

\Sigma
. We define the

-chain

The

shall say that a chain

is
carried by
a subcomplex

of

if
\gamma
has value 0 on every simplex that is not in

D
. The
support
of
\gamma
is the set of simplices on which

\gamma
has a non-zero value. It is denoted by

\operatorname{Supp}\gamma
. The
boundary operator
is a homomorphism

\partial:C_{d}(K,\mathbb{R})\to C_{d-1}(K,\mathbb{R})
that associates to

-simplex

the

-chain:

\partial\sigma=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(-1)^{i}\,[v_{0},\ldots,\hat{v}_{i},\ldots,v_{d}],

deleted from the sequence of vertices forming

.

3
Background on Delaunay complexes

In this section, we recall basic facts about Delaunay complexes
(Section
3.1
). We then
give a variational characterization of Delaunay complexes
(Section
3.2
).
Throughout the section,

designates a finite point set of

\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

3.1
Definitions and basic property

Definition 1

(Delaunay simplex)
.

A

of

is an abstract simplex
\sigma\subseteq P
for which there exists a ball
B
whose boundary
circumscribes
\sigma
and whose interior does not contain any point
of

.

Definition 2

(Delaunay complex)
.

The set of Delaunay simplices form an abstract simplicial complex
called the

of
P
and is denoted as
\operatorname{Del}(P)
.

We now state a classical result on Delaunay complexes, for which we
need two extra definitions.

Definition 3

(General position)
.

Letting

, we say that

is in

if no
d+2
points of

lie on a
common
(d-1)
-dimensional sphere and no

points of

lie on the same
k
-dimensional flat for

.

Definition 4

(Triangulation)
.

A

of

is an abstract simplicial complex whose

, whose shadow is
\operatorname{conv}P
, and which is

Theorem 1
.

When

is in general position,
\operatorname{Del}(P)
is a triangulation of

.

3.2
A variational characterization

The Delaunay complex of

optimizes many functionals over the set of

In preparation for this, we recall a famous result which says that building a

is topologically equivalent to

\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
. For simplicity, we
shall identify each point

with the point

in

\mathbb{R}^{N+1}

of

is

. Recall that
the lower convex hull of

is the portion of

visible to a viewer standing at

if and only if

is contained in the lower convex hull of

[

]
.

We are now ready to define the Delaunay energy of any triangulation

Let us recall a well-known result

4
Delaunay weight

In this section, we define the Delaunay weight of a simplex with
vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

(Section
4.1
). We show that
the Delaunay energy defined in the previous section can be expressed

(Section

). We then provide

(Section

motivates extending the Delaunay energy to collection of
d
-simplices
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and more generally to
d
-chains of simplicial
complexes with vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, as will be done in the next
section.

4.1
Definition

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an abstract simplex. If

\sigma
is non-degenerate, we define

as the smallest

(N-1)
-sphere that circumscribes
\sigma
. We also let
Z(\sigma)

and
R(\sigma)
denote the center and radius of
S(\sigma)
,
respectively. Finally, we introduce the map

x\mapsto\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)=\|x-Z(\sigma)\|^{2}-R(\sigma)^{2},

which associates each point

with the power
distance of
x
from
S(\sigma)
.

Definition 5

(Delaunay weight)
.

The Delaunay weight of an abstract
d
-simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is:

\omega(\sigma)=\begin{cases}-\int_{x\in\operatorname{conv}\sigma}\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)\,dx\quad\quad&\text{if $\sigma$ is non-degenerate,}\\
0\quad\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Figure
3
, right depicts graphically the Delaunay
weight of a simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It is
worth noting that the Delaunay weight is defined for any abstract

d
-simplex
\sigma
, irrespective of whether

\sigma
is the Delaunay simplex of some point set
P
or not. The
reason for calling it a Delaunay weight will become clear in the
next section.

4.2
Delaunay energy as a sum of Delaunay weights

Before showing that the Delaunay energy can be expressed as a sum of
Delaunay weights in Lemma
4
,
we first provide a useful expression of
\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)
when
x

is an affine combination of the vertices of
\sigma
(Lemma

3
) and deduce an
alternative expression of the Delaunay weight in Lemma
4
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a non-degenerate simplex and
let
x
be an affine combination of the vertices of
\sigma
. For every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)=\|x-z\|^{2}-\sum_{a\in\sigma}\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{a}^{\sigma}(x)\,\|a-z\|^{2}.

Proof.

Recall that
\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)=\|x-Z(\sigma)\|^{2}-R(\sigma)^{2}
. On one hand, we have

\displaystyle\|x-Z(\sigma)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|x-z\|^{2}+2(x-z)\cdot(z-Z(\sigma))+\|z-Z(\sigma)\|^{2}.

On the other hand, writing
\lambda_{a}=\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{a}^{\sigma}(x)
for short, we have

\displaystyle R(\sigma)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\sigma}\lambda_{a}\|Z(\sigma)-a\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\sigma}\lambda_{a}\left[\|Z(\sigma)-z\|^{2}+2(Z(\sigma)-z)\cdot(z-a)+\|z-a\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|Z(\sigma)-z\|^{2}+2(Z(\sigma)-z)\cdot(z-x)+\sum_{a\in\sigma}\lambda_{a}\|z-a\|^{2}.

Substracting the above expressions of
\|x-Z(\sigma)\|^{2}
and
R(\sigma)^{2}
yields the result.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For any non-degenerate abstract
d
-simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, its
Delaunay weight represents the
(d+1)
-volume between the lifted
geometric simplex
\operatorname{conv}\hat{\sigma}
and the paraboloid

\mathscr{P}
, i.e.

\omega(\sigma)=\int_{x\in\operatorname{conv}\sigma}\|\hat{x}-x_{\sigma}^{\star}\|\,dx,

where
\hat{x}=(x,\|x\|^{2})
and
x_{\sigma}^{\star}=\sum_{a\in\sigma}\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{a}^{\sigma}(x)\hat{a}
. If
P

designates a finite point set of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
in general position
and
d=\dim(\operatorname{aff}P)
, then the Delaunay energy of any
triangulation
T
of
P
can be expressed as

\operatorname{E_{\operatorname{del}}}(T)=\sum_{\sigma\in T^{[d]}}\omega(\sigma).

Proof.

Letting
x\in\operatorname{conv}\sigma
, we show that
-\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)=\|\hat{x}-x_{\sigma}^{\star}\|
. Applying Lemma

3
with
z=0
and
writing
\lambda_{a}=\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{a}^{\sigma}(x)
for short, we get that

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\sigma}\lambda_{a}\,\|a\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{a\in\sigma}\lambda_{a}(a,\|a\|^{2})-(x,\|x\|^{2})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{a\in\sigma}\lambda_{a}\hat{a}-\hat{x}\right\|

\displaystyle=\|x^{\star}_{\sigma}-\hat{x}\|.

The
expressions of both
\omega(\sigma)
and
\operatorname{E_{\operatorname{del}}}(T)
follow
immediately.
∎

The above lemma suggests the following interpretation of the Delaunay

whose restriction to any

-simplex

. The graph of

is
a

which
has been depicted for two different triangulations in Figure

be interpreted as the
(d+1)
-volume of the region lying below
\mathcal{W}_{T}

and above
\operatorname{conv}P
.

4.3
Intrinsec closed expression

Below, we give a closed expression for the Delaunay weight due to Chen
and Holst in
[
21
]
. For completeness, we provide a
proof. Writing
\operatorname{vol}(\sigma)
for the
d
-dimensional volume of

\operatorname{conv}\sigma
, we have:

Lemma 5

(
[
21
]
)
.

The weight of the abstract
d
-simplex
\sigma=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{d}\}
is

\omega(\sigma)=\frac{1}{(d+1)(d+2)}\operatorname{vol}(\sigma)\left[\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq d}\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|^{2}\right].

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. If

\sigma
is degenerate, then
\operatorname{vol}(\sigma)=0
and the result is
clear. Suppose that
\sigma
is non-degenerate and recall that
the standard simplex is

\Delta_{d}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\leq 1;\lambda_{i}\geq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,d\}.

We introduce the map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined by
\psi(\lambda)=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(a_{i}-a_{0})
, which establishes a
one-to-one correspondence between the points
\lambda
of the standard
simplex
\Delta_{d}
and the points
x=\psi(\lambda)
of
\operatorname{conv}\sigma
. Making the change of
variable
x=\psi(\lambda)\rightarrow\lambda
, we get that:

w(\sigma)=\int_{\lambda\in\Delta_{d}}-\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(\psi(\lambda))\cdot|\det(\mathrm{D}\psi)(\lambda)|\,d\lambda.

Noting that
\mathrm{D}\psi(\lambda)
is the
d\times d
matrix whose

i
th column is the vector
a_{i}-a_{0}
, we deduce that

|\det(\mathrm{D}\psi)(\lambda)|=d!\operatorname{vol}(\sigma)
. Observing that

\psi(\lambda)
has (normalized) barycentric coordinates
(1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{d})

and applying Lemma
3
with
z=a_{0}
, we
can write:

\operatorname{Power}_{\sigma}(\psi(\lambda))=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\|a_{i}-a_{0}\|^{2}\right)+\|\psi(\lambda)-a_{0}\|^{2},

and thus obtain (after plugging in the expression of
\psi(\lambda)
)

w(\sigma)=d!\operatorname{vol}(\sigma)\int_{\lambda\in\Delta_{d}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\|a_{i}-a_{0}\|^{2}-\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(a_{i}-a_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]\,d\lambda.

We then use a formula for integrating a homogeneous polynomial on
the standard simplex that may be found in
[
48
]
:

\int_{\lambda\in\Delta_{d}}\lambda_{1}^{\eta_{1}}\ldots\lambda_{d}^{\eta_{d}}\,d\lambda=\frac{\eta_{1}!\ldots\eta_{d}!}{(d+\sum_{i}\eta_{i})!}.

We obtain that

w(\sigma)=\frac{1}{(d+1)(d+2)}\operatorname{vol}(\sigma)\left[d\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|a_{i}-a_{0}\|^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(a_{i}-a_{0})\cdot(a_{j}-a_{0})\right].

Observing that
\|a_{i}-a_{0}\|^{2}+\|a_{j}-a_{0}\|^{2}-2(a_{i}-a_{0})\cdot(a_{j}-a_{0})=\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|^{2}
, we can further rearrange
the above formula to get the result.
∎

It follows from Definition
5
but also
from the expression of the Delaunay weight given in

5
that two isometric simplices have the same
Delaunay weight. Hence, a Delaunay energy can be straightforwardly
associated to any collection
\Sigma
of
d
-simplices
living in

. It is then tempting to ask what would

and whose union is homeomorphic to that submanifold. As

5.3
Geometric conditions on the sample

Recall that our goal is to give conditions under which a solution to
Problem (
5.1
) provides a faithful
reconstruction of
\mathcal{M}
. To express the conditions that we need, let us
introduce some definitions and notations.

Definition 7

(Dense sample)
.

We say that
P
is an
\varepsilon
-dense sample of
\mathcal{M}
if for
every point
m\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a point
p\in P
with
\|p-m\|\leq\varepsilon
or, equivalently, if
\mathcal{M}\subseteq P^{\oplus\varepsilon}
.

Definition 8

(Accurate sample)
.

We say that
P
is a
\delta
-accurate sample of
\mathcal{M}
if for
every point
p\in P
, there is a point
m\in\mathcal{M}
with
\|p-m\|\leq\delta
or, equivalently, if
P\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\delta}
.

The
separation
of a point set
P
is

\operatorname{separation}(P)=\min_{p\neq q\in P}\|p-q\|.

We recall that the
height
of a simplex
\sigma
is

0pt{\sigma}=\min_{v\in\sigma}d(v,\operatorname{aff}(\sigma\setminus\{v\})).

The height of
\sigma
vanishes if
and only if
\sigma
is degenerate.
The
protection
of a simplex
\sigma
relatively to a point set
Q
is

\operatorname{protection}(\sigma,Q)=\min_{q\in\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\sigma}(Q\setminus\sigma)}d(q,S(\sigma)).

We stress that our definition of a simplex protection differs slightly
from the one in
[
11
,
10
]
. We now
associate to a finite point set
P
and a scale
\rho
three
quantities that describe the quality of the pair
(P,\rho)
at dimension
d
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{height}(P,\rho)

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma}0pt{\sigma},

\displaystyle\operatorname{angularDeviation}_{\mathcal{M}}(P,\rho)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma}\operatorname{angularDeviation}_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma),

\displaystyle\operatorname{protection}(P,\rho)

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma}\operatorname{protection}(\sigma,P\cap B(c_{\sigma},\rho)),

where the two minima and the maximum are over all
\rho
-small

d
-simplices
\sigma\subseteq P
. Observe that assuming

\operatorname{height}(P,\rho)>0
is equivalent to assuming that all
\rho
-small

d
-simplices of
P
are non-degenerate.

Definition 9

(Safety condition)
.

Let
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and
\rho
be non-negative real
numbers. The safety condition on
(P,\varepsilon,\delta)
at
scale
\rho
is the existence of a real number
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{6}\right]
such that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{angularDeviation}_{\mathcal{M}}(P,\rho)\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\frac{\theta}{2}-\arcsin\left(\frac{\rho+\delta}{\mathcal{R}}\right),

\displaystyle\operatorname{separation}(P)\;

\displaystyle>\;8(\delta\theta+\rho\theta^{2})+6\delta+\frac{2\rho^{2}}{\mathcal{R}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{protection}(P,3\rho)\;

\displaystyle>\;8(\delta\theta+\rho\theta^{2})\left(1+\frac{4d\varepsilon}{\operatorname{height}(P,\rho)}\right).

Roughly speaking, assuming the safety condition on

(P,\varepsilon,\delta)
at scale

enforces
\rho
-small

d
-simplices of

to make a sufficiently small angle relatively to

\mathcal{M}
. It also enforces

to be both sufficiently separated and
protected at scale

. As explained in the companion paper

[
6
]
, the safety condition on

(P,\varepsilon,\delta)
can be met by considering a

\left(\frac{20\varepsilon}{21}\right)
-dense

\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)
-accurate point set

and perturbing
it as described in
[
6
]
.

6.1
Technical lemma

The proof of Theorem
7

technical lemma which we now state and prove.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an orientable
d
-dimensional submanifold (with or without boundary) of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let
K
be a
simplicial complex with vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Assume that there is a continuous function
\varphi:\boldsymbol{\lvert}K\boldsymbol{\rvert}\to\mathcal{D}
. Suppose that for each

d
-simplex
\sigma\in K
, we have two positive weights

W(\sigma)\geq W_{\min}(\sigma)
and that there exists an integrable function
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
W_{\min}(\sigma)=\int_{\varphi(\operatorname{conv}\sigma)}f
. Consider the
d
-chain
\gamma_{\min}
on
K
defined by

\gamma_{\min}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sign}_{\mathcal{D}}(\sigma)\quad\quad&\text{if $W_{\min}(\sigma)=W(\sigma)$,}\\
0\quad\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Suppose that

\sum_{\sigma\in K^{[d]}}\gamma_{\min}(\sigma)\operatorname{sign}_{\mathcal{D}}(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(\operatorname{conv}\sigma)}(x)=1
, for almost all
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Then,

\gamma_{\min}
is the unique solution to the following optimization problem over the set of chains in
C_{d}(K,\mathbb{R})
:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\gamma}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle\gamma}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\|\gamma\|_{1,W}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\gamma}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in K^{[d]}}\gamma(\sigma)\operatorname{sign}_{\mathcal{D}}(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(\operatorname{conv}\sigma)}(x)=1,\hskip 5.69054pt

\displaystyle\text{for almost all }x\in\mathcal{D}

Proof.

We note that the problem is invariant under change of orientation of

d
-simplices in
K
and thus we may assume that every
d
-simplex

\sigma
in
K
has an orientation that is consistant with that of

\mathcal{D}
, that is,
\operatorname{sign}_{\mathcal{D}}(\sigma)=1
for all
\sigma\in K^{[d]}
.
With this assumption, the lemma simply asserts the following.
Consider the
d
-chain
\gamma_{\min}
on
K
defined by

\gamma_{\min}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}1\quad\quad\text{if $W_{\min}(\sigma)=W(\sigma)$,}\\
0\quad\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Suppose that

\sum_{\sigma\in K^{[d]}}\gamma_{\min}(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(\operatorname{conv}\sigma)}(x)=1
, for almost all
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Then the
\ell_{1}
-like
norm
\|\gamma\|_{1,W}
attains its minimum over all
d
-chains
\gamma
such
that

\sum_{\sigma\in K^{[d]}}\gamma(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(\operatorname{conv}\sigma)}(x)=1,\quad\text{for almost all }x\in\mathcal{D}

(6)

if and only if
\gamma=\gamma_{\min}
.

We write
\tilde{\sigma}=\varphi(\operatorname{conv}\sigma)
throughout the proof for a shorter notation.
We prove the lemma by showing that for all
d
-chains
\gamma
on

K
that satisfy constraint (
6
), we have:

\displaystyle\|\gamma\|_{1,W}\;\geq\;\|\gamma\|_{1,W_{\min}}\;\geq\;\int_{\mathcal{D}}f\;=\;\|\gamma_{\min}\|_{1,W_{\min}}\;=\;\|\gamma_{\min}\|_{1,W},

(7)

with the first inequality being an equality if and only if
\gamma=\gamma_{\min}
. Clearly,
\|\gamma\|_{1,W}\geq\|\gamma\|_{1,W_{\min}}

because
W(\sigma)\geq W_{\min}(\sigma)
. To obtain the second
inequality, recall that we have assumed
\sum_{\sigma}\gamma(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\sigma}}(x)=1
almost everywhere in
\mathcal{D}
. We use this to write that:

\displaystyle\|\gamma\|_{1,W_{\min}}\;\geq\;\sum_{\sigma}\gamma(\sigma)\int_{\tilde{\sigma}}f\;=\;\sum_{\sigma}\gamma(\sigma)\int_{\mathcal{D}}f\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{D}}f\sum_{\sigma}\gamma(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{D}}f,

(8)

where sums are over all
d
-simplices
\sigma
in
K
. Setting

\gamma=\gamma_{\min}
in (
8
), we observe that
the inequality in (
8
) becomes an equality because
none of the coefficients of
\gamma_{\min}
are negative by
construction. It follows that
\int_{\mathcal{D}}f=\|\gamma_{\min}\|_{1,W_{\min}}
. Finally,
\|\gamma_{\min}\|_{1,W_{\min}}\;=\;\|\gamma_{\min}\|_{1,W}
because
\gamma_{\min}
has been defined
so that for all simplices
\sigma
in its support,
W_{\min}(\sigma)=W(\sigma)
. We have thus established (
7
). Suppose
now that
\gamma\neq\gamma_{\min}
and let us prove that
\|\gamma\|_{1,W}>\|\gamma\|_{1,W_{\min}}
, or equivalently that

\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Supp}\gamma}|\gamma(\sigma)|\;(W(\sigma)-W_{\min}(\sigma))>0.

Since none of the terms in the above sum are negative, it
suffices to show that there exists at least one simplex
\sigma\in\operatorname{Supp}\gamma
for which
W(\sigma)>W_{\min}(\sigma)
. By
contradiction, assume that for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Supp}\gamma
,

W(\sigma)=W_{\min}(\sigma)
. By construction, we thus have the
implication:
\gamma(\sigma)\neq 0\implies\gamma_{\min}(\sigma)=1
, and therefore

\operatorname{Supp}\gamma\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}\gamma_{\min}
. But, since

\sum_{\sigma}\gamma_{\min}(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\sigma}}(x)=1

and coefficients of
\gamma_{\min}
are either

or

, point

is
covered by a unique

-simplex in the support of
\gamma_{\min}
.
Hence, the simplices in
\operatorname{Supp}\gamma_{\min}
have pairwise disjoint
interiors while their union covers
\mathcal{D}
. Since
\sum_{\sigma}\gamma(\sigma)\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\sigma}}(x)=1
for almost all
x\in\mathcal{D}
,
the simplices in
\operatorname{Supp}\gamma
must also cover
\mathcal{D}
while using
only a subset of simplices in
\operatorname{Supp}\gamma_{\min}
. The only
possibility is that
\gamma=\gamma_{\min}
, yielding a
contradiction.

6.3
Delloc complexes

In this section, we define the Delloc complex. We then recall a key

6.4
Comparing power distances

The goal of this section is to relate the two maps
\operatorname{Power}_{\alpha}(x)

and
\operatorname{Power}_{\beta}(y)
for two
d
-simplices
\alpha\in\operatorname{Delloc}_{d}(P,\rho)
and
\beta\subseteq P
, and for two points
x\in\operatorname{conv}\alpha

and
y\in\operatorname{conv}\beta
, such that
\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(y)
. The main
result of the section is stated in the following lemma and proved at
the end of the section. We recall that given a non-degenerate simplex

\alpha
and a point
x\in\operatorname{aff}\sigma
, the (normalized) barycentric
coordinates of
x
relatively to the simplex

are real numbers

\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a\in\alpha}
such that

and
\sum_{a\in\alpha}\lambda_{a}=1
. We write

\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{a}^{\alpha}(x)=\lambda_{a}

Lemma 11
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta,\rho\geq 0
such that
0\leq 2\varepsilon\leq\rho
, and
16\delta\leq\rho\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}}{3}
. Suppose that
P\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\delta}
. Let

\mathsf{p}=\operatorname{protection}(P,3\rho)
,
\mathsf{s}=\operatorname{separation}(P)
, and
\Theta=\operatorname{angularDeviation}_{\mathcal{M}}(P,\rho)
. Assume that
\Theta\leq\frac{\pi}{6}
and

10\rho\,\Theta\left(\varepsilon+\rho\,\Theta\right)<\mathsf{p}^{2}+\mathsf{p}\mathsf{s}.

Then, for every non-degenerate
\varepsilon
-small
d
-simplex
\alpha\in\operatorname{Delloc}_{d}(P,\rho)
, every
non-degenerate
\rho
-small
d
-simplex
\beta\subseteq P
, every
x\in\operatorname{conv}\alpha
, and every
y\in\operatorname{conv}\beta
such that
\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(y)
:

-\operatorname{Power}_{\beta}(y)+\operatorname{Power}_{\alpha}(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathsf{p}^{2}+\mathsf{p}\mathsf{s}\right)\,\sum_{b\in\beta\setminus\alpha}\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{b}^{\beta}(y).

Lemma 12
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two non-degenerate abstract

d
-simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\alpha\in\operatorname{Del}(\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(\alpha\cup\beta))
. Let
\mathsf{p}=\operatorname{protection}(\alpha,\beta)
.
Then for every
y\in\operatorname{conv}\beta
, we have

\operatorname{Power}_{\beta}(y)\leq\operatorname{Power}_{\alpha}(\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(y))-(\mathsf{p}^{2}+2\mathsf{p}R(\alpha))\sum_{b\in\beta\setminus\alpha}\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{b}^{\beta}(y).

Proof.

See Figure
12
. Let
Z(\alpha)
be the
radius of the
(d-1)
-dimensional circumsphere of
\alpha
. Clearly,

\|a-Z(\alpha)\|=R(\alpha)
for all
a\in\alpha
. Since
\alpha\in\operatorname{Del}(\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(\alpha\cup\beta))

and
\mathsf{p}=\operatorname{protection}(\alpha,\beta)
, we get:

\displaystyle(R(\alpha)+\mathsf{p})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(b)-Z(\alpha)\|^{2},\qquad\text{for all $b\in\beta\setminus\alpha$},

\displaystyle R(\alpha)^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(b)-Z(\alpha)\|^{2},\qquad\text{for all $b\in\beta\cap\alpha$}.

Let
\mu_{b}=\operatorname{BarycentricCoord}_{b}^{\beta}(y)
and note that
\mu_{b}\geq 0
. Multiplying both sides of each equation above by
\mu_{b}
and summing over all
b\in\beta
, we obtain:

R(\alpha)^{2}+(\mathsf{p}^{2}+2\mathsf{p}R(\alpha))\sum_{b\in\beta\setminus\alpha}\mu_{b}\leq\sum_{b\in\beta}\mu_{b}\|\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(b)-Z(\alpha)\|^{2}.

(11)

For short, write
y^{\prime}=\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(y)
and
\beta^{\prime}=\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(\beta)
. Noting that
y^{\prime}=\sum_{b\in\beta}\mu_{b}b^{\prime}
and applying Lemma

3
with

, we get that

\operatorname{Power}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})=\|y^{\prime}-Z(\alpha)\|^{2}-\sum_{b\in\beta}\mu_{b}\|\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(b)-Z(\alpha)\|^{2}.

Substracting
\|y^{\prime}-Z(\alpha)\|^{2}
from both sides of (
11
) and using the
above expression, we obtain

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Power}_{\alpha}(y^{\prime})+(\mathsf{p}^{2}+2\mathsf{p}R(\alpha))\sum_{b\in\beta\setminus\alpha}\mu_{b}

\displaystyle\leq-\operatorname{Power}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y^{\prime}).

Applying Lemma
3
again, with
Z=y^{\prime}
and
Z=y
respectively, we get that:

-\operatorname{Power}_{\beta^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})=\sum_{b\in\beta}\mu_{b}\|\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(b)-\pi_{\operatorname{aff}\alpha}(y)\|^{2}\leq\sum_{b\in\beta}\mu_{b}\|b-y\|^{2}=-\operatorname{Power}_{\beta}(y),

which concludes the proof.

8
Conclusion

We have shown that the submanifold reconstruction problem can be
recast as a weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm minimization problem under linear
constraints and as such is solvable by linear programming.

In the future, it would be interesting to study variants of this
minimization problem. For instance, one could imagine constraining the
solution to be a homology representative
d
-cycle (instead of a
normalized
d
-cycle). Indeed, when
\mathcal{M}
is orientable and connected,
its
d
-homology group with real coefficients is one-dimensional, and
the homology class of a normalized generator of it is called the

manifold fundamental class
. Furthermore, it is known that if

K
is either the Čech complex of
P
or the Vietoris-Rips complex
of
P
, then
K
and
\mathcal{M}
are homotopy equivalent, assuming that
P

samples sufficiently densely and accurately the manifold
\mathcal{M}
and for
a careful choice of the scale parameter of these complexes

[
19
,
18
,
7
,
45
,
39
]
.
Hence, it follows that the
d
-homology group of
K
is also
one-dimensional. One representative of a generator of the fundamental
class of
\mathcal{M}
can then be obtained, up to a multiplicative constant,
by taking any non-boundary
d
-cycle
\gamma_{0}
of
K
(performing for
this standard linear algebra operations on the boundary operators

\partial_{d}
and
\partial_{d+1}
of
K
). Thus, a variant to our
problem can be expressed as follows: among all the
d
-chains of
K

homologous to
\gamma_{0}
, search for the one with smallest Delaunay
energy. We believe that it would be possible to adapt the proof
presented in the paper and establish conditions under which the
solution to this variant is also a
d
-chain which carries a
triangulation of
\mathcal{M}
(namely, the Delloc complex of
P
).

Appendix A
A linear programming formulation

For the sake of completeness, we recall in this appendix how
minimizing a weighted
\ell^{1}
-norm under linear constraints can be expressed
as a linear programming problem through slack variables. Consider the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\sum_{i}\omega_{i}|x_{i}|

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle Ax=b.

Here
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the variable, and
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
are parameters.
This problem is equivalent to the linear programming problem:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\sum_{i}\omega_{i}s_{i}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle Ax=b,

\displaystyle x_{i}\leq s_{i},\quad

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle x_{i}\geq-s_{i},\quad

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n,

where the variables are
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Each

s_{i}
is called a
slack variable
.

C^{2}
-submanifold of Euclidean space

We recall the definition of a smooth submanifold; see for instance
https://maths-people.anu.edu.au/˜andrews/DG/DG_chap3.pdf
.

Definition 12
.

A subset
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a
d
-dimensional
C^{2}
submanifold if for
every point
x
in
\mathcal{M}
there exists a neighborhood
V
of
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a
C^{2}
map
\xi:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\xi
is a homeomorphism onto
\mathcal{M}\cap V
, and the differential
\mathrm{D}_{y}\xi
is injective for every
y\in U
.

A
d
-dimensional
C^{2}
submanifold is a
C^{2}
manifold topologically
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
but the converse is not true in general. For
instance, the square is not a submanifold of the plane. Yet, it
is a
C^{\infty}
manifold topologically embedded in the
plane. Precisely, the square can be defined as the image of the circle

\mathbb{S}^{1}
(which is a
C^{\infty}
manifold) by a
C^{\infty}
map that
is non-regular (
i.e.
whose derivative is non-injective)
precisely at the four corners of the square.

A compact
C^{2}
submanifold has positive reach
[
33
]
.
Moreover one has (see for example Lemma 4 and following paragraph in
[
13
]
or
Proposition 2.3 in
[
1
]
) that:

Lemma 22

(The inverse of the reach bounds the curvature)
.

If
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a
C^{2}

d
-dimensional submanifold with reach
\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})>0
then
1/\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})

bounds the (absolute values of the ) principal curvatures at
m\in\mathcal{M}
in the direction
v
for any vector
v
in the space normal to
\mathcal{M}
at
m
.
In particular
1/\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})
bounds the principal curvatures when
\mathcal{M}
has codimension
1
.

The following lemma, due to Federer, bounds the distance of a point
q\in\mathcal{M}
to the tangent space at a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
.
It holds for any set with positive reach and in particular for
C^{2}
submanifolds.

Lemma 23

(Distance to tangent space, Theorem 4.8(7) of
[
33
]
)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\|p-q\|<\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})
. We have

\displaystyle\sin\angle\left([pq],T_{p}\mathcal{M}\right)\leq\frac{\|p-q\|}{2\,\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})},

(32)

and

\displaystyle d(q,T_{p}\mathcal{M})\leq\frac{\|p-q\|^{2}}{2\,\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})}.

(33)

Next lemma bounds the angle variation for
C^{2}
manifolds (a slightly weaker condition si given for
C^{1,1}
manifolds in the same paper):

Lemma 24

(Corollary 3 in
[
13
]
)
.

For any
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
, we have

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\angle(T_{p}\mathcal{M},T_{q}\mathcal{M})}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\|p-q\|}{2\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}.

Using Lemmas
23
and
24
we can show that the projection on a tangent space
defines a local chart for
\mathcal{M}
.
Indeed, if
m\in\mathcal{M}
then for any
p,q\in\mathcal{M}\cap B\big(m,\sin(\pi/4)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}\big)
, (
32
) gives:

\sin\angle([pq],T_{p}\mathcal{M})\leq\frac{\|p-q\|}{2\,\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\frac{2\sin(\pi/4)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}{2\,\operatorname{reach}(\mathcal{M})}=\sin(\pi/4)

So that:

\displaystyle\angle([pq],T_{p}\mathcal{M})\leq\pi/4,

(34)

and Lemma
24
gives:

\sin\left(\frac{\angle(T_{p}\mathcal{M},T_{m}\mathcal{M})}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\|p-m\|}{2\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}\leq\frac{\sin(\pi/4)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}{2\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}=\frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{2}

so that:

\angle(T_{p}\mathcal{M},T_{m}\mathcal{M})\leq 2\arcsin\left(\frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{2}\right)<\pi/4

Summing with (
34
) gives:

\angle([pq],T_{m}\mathcal{M})<\pi/2

and this in turn implies:

p\neq q\Rightarrow\pi_{T_{m}\mathcal{M}}(p)\neq\pi_{T_{m}\mathcal{M}}(q)

We have shown that the restriction of
\pi_{T_{m}\mathcal{M}}
to
\mathcal{M}\cap B(m,\sin(\pi/4)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}
is injective,
and, by Invariance of Domain Theorem (
[
16
]
),
it is an homeomorphism on its image, which gives us:

Lemma 25

(Projection on tangent space is a local chart)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact
C^{2}
submanifold of Euclidean space, and
m\in\mathcal{M}
then, identifying
T_{m}\mathcal{M}
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
through a given frame,
the restriction of
\pi_{T_{m}\mathcal{M}}
to
\mathcal{M}\cap B\left(m,\sin(\pi/4)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}\right)^{\circ}
is a local chart of
\mathcal{M}
.
Moreover, for any
p\in\mathcal{M}\cap B\left(m,\sin(\pi/4)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}\right)^{\circ}
, one has:

\angle(T_{p}\mathcal{M},T_{m}\mathcal{M})<\pi/4

Following
[
12
, Notation 1.1]
, we call Thickness of a
k
-simplex:

t(\sigma)=\frac{h}{kL}

where
h
is the smallest altitude of
\sigma
and
L
the length of the longest edge.
Then we have (adaptation of
[
47
, Section IV.15]
, proven in
[
10
, Lemma 8.11]
):

Lemma 26

(Angle between simplices tangent space)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact
C^{2}
submanifold of Euclidean space,
\sigma
a
d
-simplex with vertices in
\mathcal{M}
, and
p
a vertex of
\sigma
:
then:

\angle\operatorname{aff}\sigma,T_{p}\mathcal{M}\leq\frac{L}{t(\sigma)\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}=\frac{dL^{2}}{h\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}

We shall also need the Whitney angle bound established in
[
11
]
.

Lemma 27

(Whitney angle bound
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Consider a
d
-dimensional affine space
H
and a simplex
\sigma

such that
\dim\sigma\leq d
and
\sigma\subseteq H^{\oplus t}
for
some
t\geq 0
. Then

\sin\angle(\operatorname{aff}\sigma,H)\leq\frac{2t\dim(\sigma)}{0pt{\sigma}}

Building on these results, we derive yet another bound between the affine
space spanned by a simplex and a nearby tangent space.

Lemma 28
.

Consider a non-degenerate
\rho
-small simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\delta}
with
16\delta\leq\rho\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}}{3}
. Let
x\in\operatorname{conv}\sigma
. Then,

\angle(\operatorname{aff}\sigma,\mathbf{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)}\mathcal{M})\leq\arcsin\left(\frac{2\dim(\sigma)}{0pt{\sigma}}\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}}{\mathcal{R}}+\delta\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
v\in\sigma
. Write
v^{\ast}=\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(v)
and
x^{\ast}=\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
. We know from
[
33
, page 435]
that for
0\leq h<\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}
, the projection map
\pi_{\mathcal{M}}
onto
\mathcal{M}
is

\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}-h}\right)
-Lipschitz for points at
distance less than
h
from
\mathcal{M}
. By Lemma

14
, both
x
and
v
belong to

\mathcal{M}^{\oplus h}
for
h=\frac{\rho}{4}
, we thus have

\|v^{\ast}-x^{\ast}\|\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}-\frac{\rho}{4}}\times\|v-x\|\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}-\frac{\mathcal{R}}{3\times 4}}\times 2\rho=\frac{24}{11}\rho\leq\sqrt{6}\rho

Applying Lemma
23
, we get that

d(v,\mathbf{T}_{x^{\ast}}\mathcal{M})\leq d(v^{\ast},\mathbf{T}_{x^{\ast}}\mathcal{M})+\|v-v^{\ast}\|\leq\frac{\|v^{\ast}-x^{\ast}\|^{2}}{2\mathcal{R}}+\delta\leq\frac{3\rho^{2}}{\mathcal{R}}+\delta.

Hence,
\sigma\subseteq(\mathbf{T}_{x^{\ast}}\mathcal{M})^{\oplus t}
for
t=\frac{3\rho^{2}}{\mathcal{R}}+\delta
and applying Whitney angle bound
(Lemma
27
), we conclude that
\sin\angle(\operatorname{aff}\sigma,\mathbf{T}_{x^{\ast}}\mathcal{M})\leq\frac{2\dim(\sigma)}{0pt{\sigma}}\left(\frac{3\rho^{2}}{\mathcal{R}}+\delta\right)
.

Appendix D
Smooth local parametrization of the normal bundle

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a
C^{2}

d
-dimensional submanifold with reach greater than
\rho>0
.
If
x\in\mathcal{M}
, the vector spaces
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
N_{x}\mathcal{M}
are the respective tangent and normal spaces to
\mathcal{M}
at
x
.
To avoid confusion, we denote by
\boldsymbol{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
\boldsymbol{N}_{x}\mathcal{M}
the corresponding
affine subspaces of ambient
\mathbb{R}^{N}
:
\boldsymbol{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}=x+T_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
\boldsymbol{N}_{x}\mathcal{M}=x+N_{x}\mathcal{M}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
the
\rho
-tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}
.
The normal bundle of
\mathcal{M}
is denoted
N\mathcal{M}
and defined as:

N\mathcal{M}\underset{\text{def.}}{=}\>\coprod_{x\in\mathcal{M}}N_{x}\mathcal{M}

where
\coprod
is the disjoint union. The normal bundle restricted to radius
\rho
is denoted
N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}
:

N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}\underset{\text{def.}}{=}\>\coprod_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{(x,v)\in N_{x}\mathcal{M},\,\|v\|\leq\rho\right\}

We know from
[
33
, Item (13) of Theorem 4.8]
that, since
\rho<\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}
,
the map
\Psi:\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}\rightarrow N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined by:

\Psi(y)=\left(\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(y),y-\pi_{\mathcal{M}}(y)\right)

is a Lipschitz homeomorphism between
\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
and
N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}

whose inverse
\sigma:N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
defined as

\sigma(x,v)=x+v

(35)

is Lipschitz as well.
We have
[
34
]
that inside
\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
the distance function
d_{\mathcal{M}}
is
C^{2}
and the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{M}}
is
C^{1}
.
It follows that
\Psi
is
C^{1}
and since its inverse is Lipschitz, its Jacobian cannot be singular.
It follows that
\Psi
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphims between
\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
and
N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}
.

For
x\in\mathcal{M}
we consider an open neighborhood
U_{0}
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a
C^{2}

injective map
\xi:U_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, regular in the sense that
differential
\left(\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}(u)\right)_{j=1,d}
has rank
d
for any
u\in U_{0}
,
and such that
\xi(0)=x
.
Let us choose
\xi
such that, moreover,
\left(\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\right)_{j=1,d}
forms an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
.

Also, we consider, as in the proof of Lemma 6.3 of
[
40
]
, a set of
(N-d)

C^{1}
-smooth
vector valued maps
(w_{k})_{k=1\ldots N-d}
,
where
w_{k}:U_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
and such that, for any
u\in U_{0}
,
(w_{k}(u))_{k=1,N-d}

is an orthonormal basis of
N_{\xi(u)}\mathcal{M}
.

As done by J.Milnor in
[
40
, paragraph 6, proof of Lemma 6.3]
,
\xi
and
(w_{k})_{k=1\ldots N-d}

defines a local trivialization of the normal bundle
N\mathcal{M}
, that is a chart
\phi
of

N\mathcal{M}
in the neighborhood of
(x,0)

where the parameter
\left(u^{1},\ldots,u^{d},t^{1},\ldots,t^{N-d}\right)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{B}^{N-d}(0,\rho)
corresponds to the point:

\displaystyle\phi(u,t)

\displaystyle=\phi\left(u1,\ldots,u^{d},t^{1},\ldots,t^{N-d}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\xi\left(u^{1},\ldots,u^{d}\right),\>\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}w_{k}(u^{1},\ldots,u^{d})\right)\in N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}

and

\sigma\circ\phi\left(u,t\right)=\xi\left(u1,\ldots,u^{d}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}w_{k}(u^{1},\ldots,u^{d})\in\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}

(36)

Derivating (
36
) at
u=0
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\sigma\circ\phi\left(u,t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}

(37)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt^{k}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\sigma\circ\phi\left(u,t\right)

\displaystyle=w_{k}(0)

(38)

In order to express the derivative of
\sigma:N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}

at point
(x,v)\in N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}
we need, besides the chart
\phi
of a neighborhood of
(x,v)
,
a chart
\hat{\phi}
of a neighborhood of
\sigma(x,v)=x+v
in euclidean space.
A natural choice for
\hat{\phi}
is

\hat{\phi}\big(y\big)=\hat{\phi}\big(y^{1},\ldots y^{N}\big)=x+v+\sum_{k=1}^{d}y^{k}\frac{d\xi}{du^{k}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}y^{d+k}w_{k}

Observe that, since
\big(\frac{d\xi}{du^{1}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\ldots,\frac{d\xi}{du^{d}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{N-d}\big)
is an orthonormal basis,
seeing
N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
as Riemannian manifolds, the metric tensor associated to the
charts
\phi
and
\hat{\phi}
respectively at
(x,v)
and
\sigma(x,v)=x+v
are the identity matrix.

One has:

\mathrm{D}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{y=0}\hat{\phi}=\Big(\frac{d\xi}{du^{1}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\ldots,\frac{d\xi}{du^{d}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{N-d}\Big)

(39)

and since te columns of (
39
) are unitary and pairwise orthogonal,
taking the inner product of (
37
) and (
38
)
with the columns of
\mathrm{D}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\hat{\phi}
, namely

\left(\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\right)_{j=1,d}
and
\left(w_{k}\right)_{k=1,N-d}
, gives an expression of the Jacobian of

\sigma:N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}

at the point
\phi(0,t)
in the charts
\phi
and
\hat{\phi}
as:

\mathrm{D}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\,\left({\hat{\phi}}^{-1}\circ\sigma\circ\phi\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle\frac{d\xi}{du^{i}},\,\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\right\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}},\,\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\right\rangle&\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}},\,w_{l}\right\rangle\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}

(40)

Where in both
N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
the tangent space is decomposed as direct sum of the tangential and orthogonal fibers.

Denoting by
\mathrm{I}
the first fundamental form of
\mathcal{M}
:

\mathrm{I}=\left\langle\frac{d\xi}{du^{i}},\,\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\right\rangle.

Since we have chosen
\xi
in such a way that

\left(\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{u=0}\right)_{j=1,d}
is an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
, we have at
x=\xi(0)
that
\mathrm{I}=\mathds{1}
.

For a unit vector

v\in N_{x}\mathcal{M}
with
v=\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}_{v}w_{k}
we can call, following J. Milnor again, the second fundamental form in the direction
v
:

\mathrm{II}_{v}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}_{v}\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}},\,\frac{d\xi}{du^{j}}\right\rangle.

We also denote the “torsion” term by
T_{v}
:

T_{v}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-d}t^{k}_{v}\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}},\,w_{l}\right\rangle

In fact we claim that, without loss of generality, one can choose the maps
(w_{k})_{k=1,N-d}

in such a way that
T_{v}=0
. Indeed, since, for any
u\in U_{0}
,
\left\langle w_{k}(u),\,w_{l}(u)\right\rangle=\delta_{k,l}
where

\delta_{k,l}
, the Kronecker delta, is constant, one has:

0=\frac{d}{du^{i}}\left\langle w_{k},\,w_{l}\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}},\,w_{l}\right\rangle+\left\langle w_{k},\,\frac{dw_{l}}{du^{i}}\right\rangle

in other words,
\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}},\,w_{l}\right\rangle
is antisymmetric and can then be seen as an infinitesimal rotation, i.e., formally,
an element of the Lie algebra of
SO(N-d)
.
It results that if we replace
w
by
w^{\prime}
defined by :

w^{\prime}(u^{1},\ldots,u^{d})=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}\left\langle\frac{dw_{k}}{du^{i}}_{|_{u=0}},\,w_{l}\right\rangle\right)w(u^{1},\ldots,u^{d}),

we get:

\left\langle\frac{dw^{\prime}_{k}}{du^{i}}_{|_{u=0}},\,w^{\prime}_{l}\right\rangle=0.

Since the
\exp
term is a rotation depending smoothly (
C^{\infty}
) on the
u_{i}
and is applied to the basis
w
,
w
can be replaced by
w^{\prime}
which proves the claim.

We have that for
\tau\in[0,\rho]
, at the point
(x,\tau v)\in N^{\rho}\mathcal{M}
with
\sigma(x,\tau v)=x+\tau v\in\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}

the Jacobian (
40
) can be expressed as, with the assumption
T_{v}=0
:

\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}&T_{v}\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}&0\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}

(41)

Thanks to Lemma
22
,

\|\mathrm{II}_{v}\|_{2}
, the operator
L^{2}
norm of
\mathrm{II}_{v}
,
which is the maximal extrinsic curvature of
\mathcal{M}
, is upper
bounded by the inverse of the reach of
\mathcal{M}
:

\forall v\in N_{x}\mathcal{M},\|v\|=1,\>\left\|\mathrm{II}_{v}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}

(42)

Since in (
41
) one has
\tau\leq\rho<\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}
,
we get that
\left\|\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\tau}{\operatorname{reach}\mathcal{M}}<1
and
\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}
,
the upper left bloc in (
41
),
is inversible. It follows that matrix (
41
) is inversible:

\mathrm{D}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{y=0}\,\left(\phi^{-1}\circ\Psi\circ\hat{\phi}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}&0\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}\right)^{-1}&0\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}

(43)

By local inversion Theorem, (
43
) gives us the Jacobian of
\Psi=\sigma^{-1}
at
x+\tau v\in\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
.
In particular, the first row of (
43
):

\begin{pmatrix}\left(\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}\right)^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}

(44)

is the Jacobian of
\xi^{-1}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{M}}\circ\hat{\phi}
at
x+\tau v\in\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}
.

Appendix E
Restriction of
\pi_{\mathcal{M}}
to a
d
-dimensional affine space in a neighborhood of
x+\tau v\in\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}

Let
\Pi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote a
d
-dimensional affine space that contains the point
y=x+\tau v\in\mathcal{M}^{\oplus\rho}

for
x\in\mathcal{M}
,
v\in N_{x}\mathcal{M}
a unit vector and
\tau<\rho
.
If
\left(y,e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\right)
is an orthonormal frame of
\Pi
centered at
y
.
This frame defines a parametrization
\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\Pi
defined by

\left(z^{1},\ldots,z^{d}\right)\mapsto y+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{i}e_{i}.

(45)

Each vector
e_{i}
can be decomposed uniquely as a sum
e_{i}=e_{i}^{T}+e_{i}^{N}
where
e_{i}^{T}\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
e_{i}^{N}\in N_{x}\mathcal{M}
.
From the definition (
25
) one has:

\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|z\|=1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{i}e_{i}^{T}\right\|=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|z\|=1}\left\|\pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{i}e_{i}\right)\right\|=\cos\angle\Pi,T_{x}\mathcal{M}

(46)

Using (
44
) we get:

\frac{d}{dz^{j}}\raise-2.15277pt\hbox{$|$}_{z=0}\>\xi^{-1}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{M}}\left(y+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{i}e_{i}\right)=\left(\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}\right)^{-1}e_{j}^{T}

(47)

Since
\tau\leq\rho
and the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{II}_{v}
are the principal curvatures, by Lemma

they are upper bounded by
\mathcal{R}^{-1}
, the inverse of the reach of
\mathcal{M}
.
The eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
\left(\mathds{1}+\tau\mathrm{II}_{v}\right)^{-1}
are therefore included in:

\left[\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}+\rho},\,\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}-\rho}\right]

and therefore its determinant included by:

\left[\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}+\rho}\right)^{d},\,\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}-\rho}\right)^{d}\right]

From (

) we have that the determinant of

\left(z^{1},\ldots,z^{d}\right)\mapsto\pi_{T_{x}\mathcal{M}}\left(y+\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{i}e_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{i}e_{i}^{T}

Appendix F
Transfering orientation

In this section, we start by recalling what it means for a manifold to
be orientable. Given a manifold with a prescribed orientation, we then
explain how to orient a simplex consistently with the manifold
(Definition
15
). Finally, we
provide conditions under which the property for a simplex to be
consistently oriented with a manifold is preserved under projection
onto a nearby tangent space (Lemma

32
).

Definition 13

(Manifold orientation)
.

An orientation of a
C^{1}
-manifold
\mathcal{M}
consists of an atlas

\left\{\left(U_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M},\psi_{i}:U_{i}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\right\}_{i\in I}

such that:

U_{i}\cap U_{j}\neq\emptyset\>\implies\>\forall m\in U_{i}\cap U_{j},\>\det\mathrm{D}\left(\psi_{j}\circ\psi_{i}^{-1}\right)(\psi_{i}(m))>0.

\mathcal{M}
is said to be orientable if such an atlas exists.

Definition 14

(Chart consistent with the manifold orientation)
.

If
U
is an open subset of the oriented manifold
\mathcal{M}
, a local chart
\psi:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called consistent
with the orientation of
\mathcal{M}
defined by the atlas
\left\{\left(U_{i},\psi_{i}\right)\right\}_{i\in I}

if the following implication holds:

U_{i}\cap U\neq\emptyset\>\implies\>\forall m\in U_{i}\cap U,\>\det\mathrm{D}\left(\psi\circ\psi_{i}^{-1}\right)(\psi_{i}(m))>0.

Let us associate to each oriented non-degenerate abstract
d
-simplex

the linear map

defined by

where

are the coordinates of

Definition 15

(Consistent orientation between a simplex and a manifold)
