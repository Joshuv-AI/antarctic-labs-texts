Improving Estimation Efficiency In Structural Equation Models By An Easy Empirical Likelihood Approach
ShanWang ??, Hanxiang Peng

Abstract
In this article, we construct empirical likelihood (EL)-weighted estimators of linear functionals of a probability measure in the presence of side information. Motivated by nuisance parameters in semiparametric models with possibly infinite dimension, we consider the use of estimated constraint functions and allow the number of constraints to grow with the sample size. We study the asymptotic properties and efficiency gains. The results are used to construct improved estimators of parameters in structural equations models. The EL-weighted estimators of parameters are shown to have reduced variances in a SEM in the presence of side information of stochastic independence of the random error and random covariate. Some simulation results on efficiency gain are reported.

Introduction

Structure equation models (SEM) is a popular multivariate technique for analyzing data in behavioral, medical and social sciences. It is an analysis of moment structures in which the variance-covariance matrix
\Sigma=\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits({\mathbf{Z}})
of a random vector

{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{p}
is specified by a parametric matrix function,
\Sigma=\Sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}),\,\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}\in\Theta
for some subset
\Theta
of
{\mathcal{R}}^{q}
. Given independent and
identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations
{\mathbf{Z}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{Z}}_{n}
of
{\mathbf{Z}}
, one focuses on estimating
the parameter vector
\vartheta
. A moment-type estimator of
\vartheta
is based on the criterion of

minimum discrepancy function (MDF)
, see e.g. Shapiro (2007)
[
17
]
.
For a
p\times p
matrix
\mathbf{M}
, denote by
\mathrm{vecs}(\mathbf{M})
the
p(p+1)/2
-dimensional vector formed by stacking its columns
of the upper triangular matrix.
Let
\Xi
be a subset of
{\mathcal{R}}^{p(p+1)/2}
consisting of
\mathrm{vecs}(\mathbf{M})
over all
p\times p
semi-positive definite
matrices
\mathbf{M}
. A function
F
on
\Xi\times\Xi
is called a
discrepancy function
if it satisfies:
(i)
F({\mathbf{t}},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath})\geq 0
for all
{\mathbf{t}},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}\in\Xi
,
(ii)
F({\mathbf{t}},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath})=0
if and only if
{\mathbf{t}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}
,
(iii)
F({\mathbf{t}},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath})
is twice continuously differentiable, and
(iv) For any fixed
{\mathbf{s}}\in\Xi
,
F({\mathbf{t}},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath})\to\infty
as
{\mathbf{t}}\to{\mathbf{s}}
and

\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}\to\infty
.
We shall abuse notation to write
F({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})=F(\mathrm{vecs}({{\mathbf{A}}}),\mathrm{vecs}({\mathbf{B}}))
for matrices
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
.

Often
\Sigma
is estimated by the unstructured sample variance-covariance matrix,

\mathbb{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})^{\top},

(1.1)

where
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{Z}}_{i}
is the sample version of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$\unboldmath}=E({\mathbf{Z}})
. Let
{\mathbf{s}}_{n}=\mathrm{vecs}(\mathbb{S}_{n})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$\unboldmath}=\mathrm{vecs}(\Sigma)
. The MDF estimator of
\vartheta
is any value
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}_{n}
in
\Theta
that satisfies

F({\mathbf{s}}_{n},\sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}_{n}))=\inf_{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}\in\Theta}F({\mathbf{s}}_{n},\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$\unboldmath}(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath})).

(1.2)

One commonly used discrepancy function is the maximum-likelihood (ML) discrepancy function of two matrix-valued variables given by

F_{ML}({\mathbf{S}},\Sigma)=\log|\Sigma|-\log|{\mathbf{S}}|+\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{S}}\Sigma^{-1})-p,

(1.3)

where
\mathbf{S},\Sigma
are positive definite matrices.
Another is the generalized least squares (GLS) discrepancy
function given by

F_{GLS}(\mathbf{S},\Sigma)=\mathrm{trace}((\mathbf{S}-\Sigma){{\mathbf{W}}}^{-1}({\mathbf{S}}-\Sigma){{\mathbf{W}}}^{-1}),

(1.4)

where
{{\mathbf{W}}}
is a symmetric matrix, for instance,
{{\mathbf{W}}}=\mathbb{S}_{n}
and
{{\mathbf{W}}}={\mathbf{I}}_{p}
.
Suppose that there is available some information about SEM that can be expressed by a vector equation of expectation,

E({\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}))=0,

(1.5)

where
{\mathbf{g}}
is some measurable function taking values in
{\mathcal{R}}^{m}
.
While the moment estimator
\mathbb{S}_{n}
completely ignores the information, the empirical likelihood (EL)-weighted estimator
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{S}$\unboldmath}}_{n}
utilizes the information to provide an improved estimator,

\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{S}$\unboldmath}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})^{\top}}{1+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\zeta}$\unboldmath}},

(1.6)

where
\tilde{\zeta}
is a solution to the equation

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})}{1+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\zeta}$\unboldmath}}=0.

(1.7)

Accordingly, an improved MDF estimator
\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\vartheta}$\unboldmath}_{n}
of
\vartheta
is any value in
\Theta
that satisfies

F(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath${\mathbf{s}}$\unboldmath}}_{n},\sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\vartheta}$\unboldmath}_{n}))=\inf_{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}\in\Theta}F(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath${\mathbf{s}}$\unboldmath}}_{n},\sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath})),

(1.8)

where
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath${\mathbf{s}}$\unboldmath}}_{n}=\mathrm{vecs}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{S}$\unboldmath}}_{n})
.

In many semiparamatric models,
{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{z}})
involves in nuisance parameters which must be estimated,
leading to a plug-in estimator
\hat{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{z}})
. Using it, we work with

\hat{\mathbb{S}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})({\mathbf{Z}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})^{\top}}{1+\hat{\mathbf{g}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{\zeta}$\unboldmath}},

(1.9)

where
\hat{\zeta}
is the solution to Eqt (
1.7
) by replacing
{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})=\hat{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})
.
As a result, an improved MDF estimator
\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{\vartheta}$\unboldmath}_{n}
of
\vartheta
is any value in
\Theta
that satisfies

F(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{n},\sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{\vartheta}$\unboldmath}_{n}))=\inf_{\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}\in\Theta}F(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{n},\sigma(\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath})),

(1.10)

where
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{n}=\mathrm{vecs}(\hat{\mathbb{S}}_{n})
.

The improved MDF estimator
\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\vartheta}$\unboldmath}_{n}
is more efficient than the usual MDF estimator
\mbox{\boldmath$\vartheta$\unboldmath}_{n}
.
The efficiency criteria used are that of a least dispersed regular estimator or that of a locally asymptotic minimax estimator, and are based on the convolution theorems and
on the lower bounds of the local asymptotic risk in LAN and LAMN families,
see the monograph by Bickel,
et al.
(1993)
[
1
]
.

The side information contained in (
1.5
) is carried by the EL-weights
(n(1+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{Z}}_{i})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\zeta}$\unboldmath}))^{-1}
based on the principle of the maximum empirical likelihood.
There is an extensive amount of literature on the empirical likelihood.
It was introduced by Owen (1990, 1991)
[
10
,
11
]
to construct confidence intervals in a nonparametric setting.
Soon it was used to construct point estimators. Qin and Lawless (1994)
[
14
]
studied
maximum empirical likelihood estimators (MELE).
Bravo (2010)
[
3
]
studied a class of M-estimators based on generalized empirical likelihood
with side information and showed that the resulting class of
estimators is efficient in the sense that it achieves the same asymptotic lower bound as that of
the efficient GMM estimator with the same side information.
Parente and Smith (2011)
[
12
]
investigated generalized empirical likelihood estimators
for irregular constraints.

Estimators of the preceding EL-weighted form were investigated in Zhang (1995, 1997)
[
21
,
22
]

in M-estimation and quantile processes in the presence of auxiliary information.
Hellerstein and Imbens (1999)
[
8
]
exploited such estimators for the least squares estimators
in a linear regression model.
Yuan
et al.
(2012)
[
20
]
explored such estimators in U-statistics.
Tang and Leng (2012)
[
19
]
utilized the form to construct improved estimators of
parameters in quantile regression. Asymptotic properties of the EL-weighted estimators
were obtained for a finite number of known constraints. Motivated by nuisance parameters in semiparametric models and the infinite dimension of such models,
Peng and Schick (2013)
[
13
]
considered the use of estimated constraint functions and studied
a growing number of constraints in MELE. MELE enjoy high efficiency and is particularly convenient to incorporate side information.
Just like any other optimization problems, however, it is not trivial to numerically find MELE especially
for a large number of constraints.
Peng and Schick
[
13
]
employed one-step estimators to construct MELE.
The EL-weighted approach reduces the number of constraints
and are thus computationally easier than general MELE.

This article used the EL-weighted approach to construct
efficient estimators of linear functionals of a probability measure in the presence of side information
for two cases, viz, known marginal distributions and equal but unknown marginals,
each of which is equivalent to infinitely many constraints. The rest of the article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we shall construct the EL-weighted estimator of the linear functional of a probability
measure in the presence of side information which is expressed by an finite or infinite number of known
or estimated constraints, and present the asymptotic properties.
In Section
3
, we give examples of side information and study
the asymptotic properties of the improved estimators in SEM.
The form of SEM can be extended to great extent in a variety of ways.
We shall focus on the extensions that have been described in Bollen (1989)
[
2
]
as well as in the LISREL software manual (Jöreskog and Sörbom (1996)
[
9
]
).
The components present in a general SEM are a path analysis, the conceptual synthesis of latent variable and measurement models, and general estimation procedures. In SEM, only information up to the second moments
is used, while other forms of information such higher order moments, independence or symmetry
of the random errors are ignored, which can be used by the EL-weighting method to improve efficiency.
In Section
4
, we report simulation results. Technical details are collected in Section
5
.

The main results

Suppose that
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}

.
We are interested in efficient estimation of the linear functional

\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}=\int\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}\,dQ

(C)

To utilize the information contained (C), consider the the empirical likelihood,

\mathscr{R}_{n}=\sup\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{n}n\pi_{j}:\mbox{\boldmath$\pi$\unboldmath}\in\mathscr{P}_{n},\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}{\mathbf{u}}(Z_{j})=0\Big\},

Following Owen (1990)
[
10
]

{\tilde{\pi}}_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{1+{\mathbf{u}}(Z_{j})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\zeta}$\unboldmath}},\quad j=1,\dots,n,

(2.1)

where
\tilde{\zeta}
is the solution to the equation

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{{\mathbf{u}}(Z_{j})}{1+{\mathbf{u}}(Z_{j})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\zeta}$\unboldmath}}=0.

(2.2)

These
{\tilde{\pi}}_{j}

\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}}$\unboldmath}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\tilde{\pi}}_{j}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})}{1+{\mathbf{u}}(Z_{j})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{\zeta}$\unboldmath}}.

(2.3)

For
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}_{\mathbf{t}}({\mathbf{z}})=\mathbf{1}[{\mathbf{z}}\leq{\mathbf{t}}]

for

Remark 2.1

{\mathbf{h}}^{*}
is a degenerate kernel. Let

.
For

, one has
E({\mathbf{k}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}_{j}){\mathbf{k}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}_{k},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}_{l})^{\top})=E({\mathbf{k}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$\unboldmath}_{2})^{\otimes 2})

In this section, we first give two useful general theorems. As applications, we prove the
theorems presented in Section
2
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
be
m
-dimensional vectors. Set

\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\quad x_{*}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\|,\quad\mathbb{S}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\top},

x^{(\nu)}=\sup_{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|=1}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{\nu}\Big\|,\quad\nu=3,4,

and let
\lambda
and
\Lambda
denote the smallest and largest eigen value
of the matrix
\mathbb{S}
,

\lambda=\inf_{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|=1}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbb{S}{\mathbf{u}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Lambda=\sup_{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|=1}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbb{S}{\mathbf{u}}.

With these we associate the empirical likelihood

\mathscr{R}=\sup\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{n}n\pi_{j}:\mbox{\boldmath$\pi$\unboldmath}\in\mathscr{P}_{n},\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=0\Big\}.

Peng and Schick
[
PS12
]
carefully examined the above maximization as a numeric problem and detailed some very useful properties. We quote their Lemma 5.2 below for our application.

Lemma 5.1
.

The inequality
\lambda>5\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|x_{*}
implies that there is a unique

\zeta
in
{\mathcal{R}}^{m}
satisfying the below (
5.1
) to (
5.7
).

1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}>0,\quad j=1,\dots,n,

(5.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}{1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}=0,

(5.2)

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|\leq\frac{\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|}{\lambda-\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|x_{*}},

(5.3)

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|x_{*}\leq\frac{\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|x_{*}}{\lambda-\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|x_{*}}<\frac{1}{4},

(5.4)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}\mathbb{S}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\leq\Lambda\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{\Lambda\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}}{(\lambda-\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|x_{*})^{2}},

(5.5)

\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{{\mathbf{r}}_{i}}{1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j}+{\mathbf{r}}_{j}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\Big\|\leq\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}\Big\|+\dfrac{4}{3}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\|\;|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}|^{3},

(5.6)

for vectors
{\mathbf{r}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{r}}_{n}
of the same dimension, and

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}-\mathbb{S}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\leq 2\Big(\dfrac{1}{\lambda}+\dfrac{\Lambda}{9\lambda^{2}}\Big)\|\zeta\|^{4}x^{(4)}.

(5.7)

Now use the fact that
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|=\sup_{\|{\mathbf{v}}\|=1}{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}
,
the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,
(
5.3
), (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) to bound the square of the first term
of the right-hand side of (
5.6
) by

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{4}\sup_{\|{\mathbf{v}}\|=1}{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}^{\top}\Big){\mathbf{v}}\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|^{4}x^{(4)}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}^{\top}\Big|_{o}

and the square of the second term by

\frac{16}{9}x_{*}^{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\|^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{4}\leq\frac{16d}{9}(x_{*}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|)^{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|^{4}{\mathbf{x}}^{(4)}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}^{\top}\Big|_{o},

where
d
is the dimension of
r_{j}
.
Combining the above we obtain

\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\!\dfrac{{\mathbf{r}}_{i}}{1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j}+{\mathbf{r}}_{j}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\Big\|^{2}\!\!\!\leq\!\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|^{4}x^{(4)}\!\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\!{\mathbf{r}}_{j}{\mathbf{r}}_{j}^{\top}\!\Big|_{o}\big[1+\frac{16d}{9}(x_{*}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}\|)^{2}\big].

(5.8)

We now apply the above results to random vectors.
Let
{\mathbf{T}}_{n1},\dots,{\mathbf{T}}_{nn}
be
m_{n}
-dimensional random vectors.
With these random vectors we associate the empirical likelihood

\mathscr{R}_{n}=\sup\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{n}n\pi_{j}:\mbox{\boldmath$\pi$\unboldmath}\in\mathscr{P}_{n},\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}=0\Big\}.

To study the asymptotic behavior of
\mathscr{R}_{n}
we introduce

T_{n}^{*}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}\|,\quad\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj},\quad T_{n}^{(\nu)}=\sup_{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|=1}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj})^{\nu}\Big\|,

and the matrix

\mathbb{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top},

and let
\lambda_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
denote the smallest
and largest eigen values of
\mathbb{S}_{n}
,

\lambda_{n}=\inf_{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|=1}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbb{S}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Lambda_{n}=\sup_{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|=1}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbb{S}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}.

We impose the following conditions on
{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}
.

(A1)

T_{n}^{*}=o_{p}(m_{n}^{-3/2}n^{1/2})
.

(A2)

\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|=O_{p}(m_{n}^{1/2}n^{-1/2})
.

(A3)

There is a sequence of regular
m_{n}\times m_{n}
dispersion matrices
{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
such that

|\mathbb{S}_{n}-{\mathbf{W}}_{n}|_{o}=o_{p}(m_{n}^{-1}).

We impose the following conditions on
\psi
and
{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}
.

(B1)

n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}-E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)\big)=o_{p}(m_{n}^{-1/2})
.

(B2)

There exists some measurable function
\chi
from
{\mathcal{Z}}
into
{\mathcal{R}}^{d}
such that

\int\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}\,dQ=0
,
\int\|\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}\|^{2}\,dQ<\infty
and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{T}}_{ni}-\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}(Z_{i})\right)=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

where
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=:n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)
.

Let us first consider the case that
m_{n}
tends to infinity with the sample size.
We have the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose (A1)-(A3) and (B1)-(B2) hold.
Then there exists unique
\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}
which satisfies

1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}>0,\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}}{1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}}=0,

(5.9)

such that as
m_{n}
tends to infinity,

\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{n}=:\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})}{1+\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}}-\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(5.10)

where
\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}}=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})
with
\chi
given in (B2).

Proof.

It follows from (A1) and (A2) that
T_{n}^{*}\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|=o_{p}(1)
,
and from (A3) that there are positive numbers
a<b
such that

P(a\leq\lambda_{n}\leq\Lambda_{n}\leq b)\to 1
.
Thus all three conditions imply that the probability of the event

\{\lambda_{n}>5T_{n}^{*}\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|\}
tends to one.
Consequently, by Lemma
5.1
, there exists an
m_{n}
-dimensional random vector
\zeta

which is uniquely determined on this event by the properties (
5.1
)–(
5.8
) including (
5.9
).
To prove (
5.10
), we apply (
5.8
) with
{\mathbf{r}}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})
.
Note first that

T_{n}^{(4)}\leq\Lambda_{n}(T_{n}^{*})^{2}.

(5.11)

This, (
5.3
) and (A1)-(A2) imply that the right side of (
5.8
) is bounded by

\Big(1+\frac{d}{9}\Big)\frac{\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|^{4}}{(\lambda_{n}-\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|T_{n}^{*})^{4}}\Lambda_{n}(T_{n}^{*})^{2}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})^{\top}\big|_{o}=o_{p}(m_{n}^{-1}n^{-1}),

where the equality holds since the spectral norm of the average is bounded due to the square-integrability of
\psi
. Thus from (
5.8
) it follows

\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(5.12)

In view of (B2) the desired (
5.10
) now follows from (
5.13
)-(
5.15
) below.

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}-E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)\right)\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(5.13)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}-\mathbb{S}_{n}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\big)=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(5.14)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)\big(\mathbb{S}_{n}^{-1}-{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}\big)\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(5.15)

Note first that (B1), (A2) and (
5.3
) imply (
5.13
). Next we show

{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)=O(m_{n}^{1/2}).

Indeed, by Cauchy inequality,

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|E\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}^{\top}\big)\|^{2}\leq E\big(\|\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{1})\|^{2}\big)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E(\|{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 156.49014pt=E\big(\|\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{1})\|^{2}\big)\mathrm{trace}\left(E(\mathbb{S}_{n})\right).

But by (A3), the above trace is bounded by

\|\mathrm{trace}\left(E(\mathbb{S}_{n}-{\mathbf{W}}_{n})\right)\|+\mathrm{trace}\left(E({\mathbf{W}}_{n})\right)\leq m_{n}E\left(|\mathbb{S}_{n}-{\mathbf{W}}_{n}|_{o}\right)+\Lambda_{n}m_{n},

Thus
\|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}\|^{2}=O(m_{n})
. This, the regularity of
{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
in (A3), (
5.7
), (
5.11
) and (A1) imply that
the square of the right side of (
5.14
) is bounded by

\displaystyle O(m_{n})O_{p}(\|\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$\unboldmath}_{n}\|^{4}T_{n}^{(4)})

\displaystyle=O(m_{n})O_{p}(\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|^{4}(T_{n}^{*})^{2})

\displaystyle=O(m_{n})o_{p}(m_{n}^{2}n^{-2}m_{n}^{-3}n)=o_{p}(m_{n}^{-1}n^{-1}),

hence (
5.14
) is proved. Again the rate of
A_{n}
and (A2)-(A3) imply (
5.15
).
This completes the proof.

Theorem 5.2
.

Proof
of Theorem
2.3
.
We apply Theorem
5.1
with
{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}={\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z_{j})
to prove the result, i.e.
verify its conditions (A1)-(A3) and (B1)-(B2). Obviously
(
2.14
), (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) correspond to (A1), (A3) and (B1) respectively.
It follows from the regularity of
{\mathbf{W}}_{n}
that
\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{W}}_{n})\leq Bm_{n}
for some constant
B
. Thus from
nE[\|\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{n}\|^{2}]=\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{W}}_{n})=O(m_{n})
it yields (A2). We are now left to prove (B2).
Noticing
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{u}}_{n}^{\top}(Z))
and
{\mathbf{W}}_{n}=E({\mathbf{u}}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)^{\top})
, and

{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}_{n}
is the projection of
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)
onto the closed linear span
[{\mathbf{u}}_{n}]
, so
that
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)
is the conditional expectation of
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)
given
{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)
, i.e.,

{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)=E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)).

Since
E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)),n\geq 1
forms a martingale with respect to the sigma algebras,

\sigma({\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)),n\geq 1
, generated by
{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)
, it follows from Lévy’s martingale convergence theorem (see e.g. page 510, Shiryaev
[
18
]
) that

E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|\sigma({\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)))\to E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|\sigma({\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}(Z))),\quad a.s.\quad n\to\infty.

By the property of conditional expectation (see e.g. Proposition 1, page 430, Bickel,
et al.

[
1
]
), the last conditional expectation is the projection
of
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)
onto the closed linear span
[{\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}(Z)]
, i.e.,

E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|\sigma({\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}(Z)))=\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|[{\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}(Z)])
, hence

\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}(Z)=\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|[{\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}(Z)])=E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)|{\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}(Z)).

Thus that (B2) is satisfied with
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}
follows from

nE\Big(\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z_{i})-\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}(Z_{i})\right)\|^{2}\Big)=E\Big(\|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z)-\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}(Z)\|^{2}\Big),

which converges to zero as
n
tends to infinity by the property of the convergence of
Fourier series. This completes the proof.

Proof
of Theorem
2.4
. We prove the result by verifying conditions (A1)-(A3) and (B1)-(B2) of Theorem
5.1
with
{\mathbf{T}}_{nj}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z_{j})
. Clearly
(A1)
,
(A3)
and (B1) correspond to (
2.18
), (
2.19
) and (
2.20
) respectively, while (A2) follows from (
2.21
) and (
5.16
) below.
We are left to verify (B2). First, by Cauchy inequality and (
2.21
),

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z_{j})\otimes(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z_{j})-{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z_{j})\right)\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq E(\|\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\|^{2})\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E\left(\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z_{j})-{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z_{j})\|^{2}\right)=o(m_{n}^{-1}),

so that the
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
in (B2) satisfies

{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\right)+o(m_{n}^{-1/2}).

Note that
\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{U}}_{n})\leq m_{n}|{\mathbf{U}}_{n}|_{o}=O(m_{n})
and

\displaystyle nE(\|\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{n}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=E(\|{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\|^{2})\leq|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{1/2}|_{o}^{2}E(\|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\|^{2})

\displaystyle=|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{1/2}|^{2}_{o}\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{U}}_{n}).

This shows

\|\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{n}\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}m_{n}^{1/2}).

(5.16)

By Cauchy inequality,

\|E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\right)\|^{2}\leq E\big(\|\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\|^{2}\big)E\big(\|{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\|^{2}\big).

But

\displaystyle E\big(\|{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{1/2}|_{o}^{2}E\big(\|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=|{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{1/2}|_{o}^{2}\mathrm{trace}({\mathbf{U}}_{n})=O(m_{n}).

Hence

E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\right)=O(m_{n}^{1/2}).

Therefore combining the above and in view of (
2.22
) we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{n}(Z_{j})

\displaystyle=\big(E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\right)+o(m_{n}^{-1/2})\big){\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\big(\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{n}+o_{p}(m_{n}^{-1/2}n^{-1/2})\big)

\displaystyle=E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\right){\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{n}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

Analogous to the proof of (B2) in Theorem
2.3
(or applying
{\mathbf{u}}_{n}={\mathbf{v}}_{n}
), we have

E\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}_{n}(Z)\right){\mathbf{W}}_{n}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{n}=\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}=\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}|[{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}])
is the projection of
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}(Z)
onto the closed linear
span
[{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}]
. Clearly
\int\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}\,dQ=0
and
\int|\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}|^{2}\,dQ<\infty
.
Thus (B2) is proved with
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$\unboldmath}=\Pi(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}|[{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}])
.
This finishes the proof.
