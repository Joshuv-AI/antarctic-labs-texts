A gauge-invariant unique continuation criterion for waves in asymptotically anti-de Sitter spacetimes
Athanasios Chatzikaleas, Arick Shao

Abstract
. We reconsider the unique continuation property for a general class of tensorial Klein-Gordon equations of the form □ g ​ ϕ + σ ​ ϕ = 𝒢 ⁡ ( ϕ , ∇ ϕ ) ​ , σ ∈ ℝ \displaystyle\Box_{g}\phi+\sigma\phi=\mathcal{G}(\phi,\nabla\phi)\text{,}\qquad\sigma\in\mathbb{R} on a large class of asymptotically anti-de-Sitter spacetimes. In particular, we aim to generalize the previous results of Holzegel, McGill, and the second author [ 16 , 17 , 28 ] (which established the above-mentioned unique continuation property through novel Carleman estimates near the conformal boundary) in the following ways: (1) We replace the so-called null convexity criterion —the key geometric assumption on the conformal boundary needed in [ 28 ] to establish the unique continuation properties—by a more general criterion that is also gauge invariant . (2) Our new unique continuation property can be applied from a larger, more general class of domains on the conformal boundary. (3) Similar to [ 28 ] , we connect the failure of our generalized criterion to the existence of certain null geodesics near the conformal boundary. These geodesics are closely related to the classical Alinhac-Baouendi counterexamples to unique continuation [ 8 ] . Finally, our gauge-invariant criterion and Carleman estimate will constitute a key ingredient in proving unique continuation results for the full nonlinear Einstein-vacuum equations, which will be addressed in a forthcoming paper of Holzegel and the second author [ 18 ] .



1.
Introduction

The Anti-de Sitter (or AdS) spacetime
\left(\mathcal{M}_{\text{AdS}},g_{\text{AdS}}\right)
is the maximally symmetric solution to the Einstein-vacuum equations

(1.1)

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\alpha\beta}R+\Lambda g_{\alpha\beta}=0

with negative cosmological constant
\Lambda<0
.
With respect to polar coordinates,

\displaystyle(r,t,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{AdS}}:=\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\text{,}

this solution reads

\displaystyle g_{\text{AdS}}(r,t,\omega)=\left(1+r^{2}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}-\left(1+r^{2}\right)dt^{2}+r^{2}d\omega^{2}\text{,}

where
d\omega^{2}
is the standard round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

For our purposes, it is more convenient to introduce the coordinate
\rho
given by

\displaystyle 4r:=\rho^{-1}(2+\rho)(2-\rho)\text{.}

With respect to the parameters
(t,\rho,\omega)
, AdS spacetime is described as

(1.2)

\displaystyle(t,\rho,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{AdS}}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{R}\times\left(0,2\right]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\text{,}

\displaystyle g_{\text{AdS}}(\rho,t,\omega)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-2}\left[d\rho^{2}+(-dt^{2}+d\omega^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}(dt^{2}+d\omega^{2})+\frac{1}{16}\rho^{4}(-dt^{2}+d\omega^{2})\right]\text{,}

which we refer to as its
Fefferman-Graham form
.
Moreover, ignoring the factor
\rho^{-2}
in (
1.2
), we can then formally attach a boundary
(\mathcal{I}_{\text{AdS}},\mathfrak{g}_{\text{AdS}})
to AdS spacetime:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\text{AdS}}:=\{\rho=0\}\approx\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\text{,}\qquad\mathfrak{g}_{\text{AdS}}:=-dt^{2}+d\omega^{2}\text{.}

We refer to
(\mathcal{I}_{\text{AdS}},\mathfrak{g}_{\text{AdS}})
as the
conformal boundary
of AdS spacetime.

We use the term
asymptotically AdS
(aAdS) to refer to a class of spacetimes
(\mathcal{M},g)
that “have a similar structure with a conformal boundary”.
More specifically, such spacetimes can, at least near its conformal boundary, be written in the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}:=(0,\rho_{0})\times\mathcal{I}\text{,}\qquad g(\rho,x):=\rho^{-2}\left[d\rho^{2}+\mathfrak{g}(x)+\rho^{2}\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(x)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{3})\right]\text{,}

for some Lorentzian manifold
(\mathcal{I},\mathfrak{g})
and symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor
\bar{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Again, we call the specific form (
1.3
) of the metric the
Fefferman-Graham
gauge, and we refer to
(\mathcal{I},\mathfrak{g})
as the associated
conformal boundary
.
In particular, observe that the conformal boundary is allowed to have arbitrary boundary topology and geometry.

AdS spacetime (
1.2
) is one example of an aAdS spacetime.
The AdS-Schwarzschild and AdS-Kerr families are also aAdS spacetimes, all with conformal boundary
(\mathcal{I}_{\text{AdS}},\mathfrak{g}_{\text{AdS}})
.

1.1.
Unique continuation and previous results

As the conformal boundary
\mathcal{I}
is timelike, it fails to be a Cauchy hypersurface for the associated aAdS spacetime.
Consequently, the problem—inspired from AdS/CFT—of solving the Klein-Gordon equations

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Box_{g}\phi+\sigma\phi=\mathcal{G}(\phi,\nabla\phi)\text{,}\qquad\sigma\in\mathbb{R}

on a fixed aAdS background given Cauchy data (i.e., appropriately defined Dirichlet
and
Neumann traces) on
\mathcal{I}
may not be well-posed.

As a result, we consider instead a different but related mathematical problem:

Question
.

Are solutions (whenever they exist) of the Klein-Gordon equation (
1.4
) uniquely determined by the (Dirichlet and Neumann) boundary data on
\mathcal{I}
?

In terms of physics, the above can be interpreted as follows:

Question
.

Is there a “one-to-one correspondence” between solutions of (
1.4
) in the interior and an appropriate space of boundary data on the conformal boundary
\mathcal{I}
?

The standard unique continuation results for wave equations follow from the now-classical results of Hörmander for general linear operators—see
[
19
, Chapter 28]
and
[
24
]
.
There, the crucial criterion required is that the hypersurface from which one uniquely continues must be
pseudoconvex
.
Unfortunately, these classical results fail to apply to aAdS settings, as the conformal boundary
\mathcal{I}
(barely) fails to be pseudoconvex.
Thus, a more refined understanding of the near-boundary geometry is needed to deduce unique continuation properties.

Remark 1.1
.

For instance, one consequence of these new difficulties is that while the classical results can be localized to a sufficiently small neighborhood of a point, one must to prescribe data on a sufficiently large part of
\mathcal{I}
in order to uniquely continue the solution.
See the introduction of
[
16
]
for more extensive references and discussions of these issues.

The first positive answer to the above questions was provided by Holzegel and the second author in
[
16
]
, but only in the case of a static conformal boundary.
This was later extended by the same authors, in
[
17
]
, to non-static boundaries.
In both
[
16
,
17
]
, the key results were proved by establishing degenerate Carleman estimates near the conformal boundary.

The most current results in this direction, which further improved upon
[
16
,
17
]
, are given by McGill and the second author in
[
28
]
.
Its main result can be roughly stated as follows:

Theorem 1.2

(

28

Let

1.2.
Gauge transformations and invariance

Another important idea in the description of aAdS spacetimes and their conformal boundaries is that of
gauge invariance
.
Roughly speaking, the term
gauge transformation
refers to a change of parameters on
\mathcal{M}
,

(1.7)

\displaystyle(\rho,x)\longrightarrow(\sigma,y)\text{,}\qquad\rho,\sigma>0\text{,}\quad x,y\in\mathcal{I}\text{,}

that preserves the Fefferman-Graham gauge (
1.3
), that is,
g
is also expressed as

(1.8)

\displaystyle g(\sigma,y):=\sigma^{-2}\left[d\sigma^{2}+\mathfrak{p}(y)+\sigma^{2}\bar{\mathfrak{p}}(y)+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})\right]\text{.}

On one hand, such a gauge transformation clearly leaves the spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
unchanged, so that (
1.3
) and (
1.8
) describe the same physical object.
However, the conformal boundary is
not
left invariant by gauge transformations.
In particular, the boundary metrics
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{p}
fail to coincide; in fact, it is well-known that they differ via a conformal factor.
Similarly, the subsequent coefficients
\bar{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\bar{\mathfrak{p}}
are also related through a more complicated formula (namely, the conformal transformation law for the Schouten tensor).

4
4

4

Gauge tranformation laws can also be derived for higher-order coefficients in the Fefferman-Graham expansion not listed in (
1.3
); see, for instance,
[
11
,
22
]
for details.

Precise descriptions of gauge transformations and their effects on the conformal boundary are given in Section
3
.

Now,
one key criticism of Theorem
1.2
is that its crucial geometric assumption, the null convexity criterion, fails to be gauge-invariant
.
More specifically, it is possible that (
1.5
)–(
1.6
) hold in one gauge, but not after applying a gauge transformation (
1.7
).
(A similar shortcoming holds for the corresponding geometric assumptions for
[
16
,
17
]
.)
Thus, according to Theorem
1.2
, whether the unique continuation property holds depends not only on the properties of the physical system, but also on the gauge with which we choose to view it.
This is clearly an undesirable feature of the existing unique continuation results.

Therefore, a key objective of the present article is to further generalize Theorem
1.2
by
replacing the null convexity criterion by a gauge-invariant condition
.
This will be important for upcoming applications to AdS/CFT, where one again wishes to state any assumptions on the conformal boundary in a gauge-invariant—and hence physically relevant—manner.

1.3.
Statement of main results

We now state the main results of this paper.
The first is an analogue of Theorem
1.2
, except that the null convexity criterion (
1.5
)–(
1.6
) is replaced by a gauge-invariant condition, which we call the
generalized null convexity criterion
.
This fulfills the goal that was discussed in the preceding subsection.

Theorem 1.8

(Main Result 1: Unique continuation)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be an aAdS spacetime expressed in the Fefferman-Graham gauge (
1.3
).
Furthermore, assume the following:

(1)

There exists a constant
p>0
such that
\mathcal{G}
, from (
1.4
), satisfies

(1.9)

\left|\mathcal{G}(\psi,\nabla\psi)\right|^{2}\lesssim\rho^{3p}\left|\psi\right|^{2}+\rho^{4+p}\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}\text{.}

(2)

\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
is a domain that satisfies the following
generalized null convexity criterion
: There exists a constant
c>0
and a smooth function
\eta:\bar{\mathcal{D}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for all tangent vectors
\mathfrak{X}\in T\bar{\mathcal{D}}
with
\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})=0
,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left[\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta-\eta\cdot\bar{\mathfrak{g}}\right](\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})>c\cdot\mathfrak{h}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})\text{ in }\mathcal{D}\text{,}\\
\eta>0\text{ in }\mathcal{D}\text{,}\\
\eta=0\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}\text{,}\end{cases}

where
\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta
denotes the Hessian of
\eta
with respect to
\mathfrak{g}
, and where
\mathfrak{h}
denotes an arbitrary but fixed Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{I}
.

Then, if
\phi
is any scalar or tensorial solution to the Klein–Gordon equation (
1.4
) such that

•

\phi
is compactly supported on each level set of
(\rho,t)
, and

•

\rho^{-\kappa}\phi\rightarrow 0
in
C^{1}
as
\rho\rightarrow 0
over
\mathcal{D}
; where
\kappa
depends on
g
, the rank of
\phi
, and
\sigma
,

then
\phi=0
in a neighborhood in
\mathcal{M}
of the boundary domain
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.

Theorem
1.8
states that solutions of (
1.4
) can be uniquely continued from the boundary region
\mathcal{D}
as long as
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the generalized null convexity criterion.
Note this generalized null convexity criterion plays the same role as the null convexity criterion in Theorem
1.2
.
Later, in Proposition
3.6
, we will show that the generalized null convexity criterion is indeed gauge-invariant.
The precise version of Theorem
1.8
is given later as Corollary
5.12
.

Remark 1.9
.

We note that Theorem
1.8
is a generalization of Theorem
1.2
.
In particular, if the hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
hold, then so does the hypotheses of Theorem
1.8
, with
\mathcal{D}:=\{t_{0}<t<t_{1}\}
, and with
\eta
being a specially chosen function of
t
.
For more details, in particular on the choice of
\eta
here, see Proposition
3.13
and its proof.

Furthermore, note that Theorem
1.2
is only applicable to boundary domains
\mathcal{D}
of the form
\{t_{0}<t<t_{1}\}
, while Theorem
1.8
extends this to more general
\mathcal{D}
.

Remark 1.10
.

In this article, while we restrict our attention to smooth quantities for convenience, however we require far less regularity for Theorem
1.8
to hold.
For instance, we only require our metric
g
to be
C^{3}
in the proof of our Carleman estimates.
Furthermore, we only require that any lower-order coefficients decay toward
\mathcal{I}
at the rates encoded in (
1.9
).

Remark 1.11
.

In general, the vanishing exponent
\kappa
in Theorem
1.8
must be sufficiently large to absorb any critical lower-order terms.
However, when
\psi
is scalar, or when the conformal boundary is static, one can take
\kappa
to be the optimal exponent from applying a separation of variables ansatz to
\psi

[
9
]
.
See Remark
5.13
for further details and precise values.

5
5

5

In practice, a large
\kappa
does not cause any issues, since given sufficient regularity, one can derive that
\psi
vanishes to arbitrarily higher order as long as
\psi
satisfies the optimal vanishing rate in Remark
5.13
.

Next, similar to
[
28
]
, we also establish a theorem connecting the generalized null convexity condition in Theorem
1.8
to the behavior of null geodesics near the conformal boundary.

Theorem 1.12

(Main result 2: Characterization of null geodesics)
.

Let the aAdS spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
be as in Theorem
1.8
, and let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
satisfy the generalized null convexity criterion (
1.10
).
Let
\Lambda:(s_{-},s_{+})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be a complete
g
-null geodesic, written as

\displaystyle\Lambda(s)=\left(\rho(s),\lambda(s)\right)\in(0,\rho_{0})\times\mathcal{I}\text{.}

Then, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
sufficiently small (depending on
g
and
\mathcal{D}
) such that if

(1.11)

\displaystyle 0<\rho(s_{0})<\epsilon_{0}\text{,}\qquad|\dot{\rho}(s_{0})|\lesssim\rho(s_{0})\text{,}\qquad\lambda(s_{0})\in\mathcal{D}\text{,}

for some
s_{0}\in(s_{-},s_{+})
, then at least one of the following statements holds:

•

\Lambda
initiates from the conformal boundary within
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\searrow s_{-}}\rho(s)=0\text{,}\qquad\lim_{s\searrow s_{-}}\lambda(s)\in\mathcal{D}\text{.}

•

\Lambda
terminates at the conformal boundary within
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\nearrow s_{+}}\rho(s)=0\text{,}\qquad\lim_{s\nearrow s_{+}}\lambda(s)\in\mathcal{D}\text{.}

Theorem
1.12
can be interpreted as follows.
The assumption (
1.11
) tells us that the null geodesic is, at parameter
s_{0}
, both sufficiently close to the conformal boundary and over the region
\mathcal{D}
.
The conclusions of Theorem
1.12
then imply that
\Lambda
must either start from or terminate at the conformal boundary within
\mathcal{D}
.
In other words, there are no null geodesics that are sufficiently close to the conformal boundary and that “fly over
\mathcal{D}
without touching
\mathcal{D}
”.
As a result, Theorem
1.12
implies that as long as
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the generalized null convexity condition, then one cannot construct the geometric optics counterexamples of Alinhac-Baouendi
[
8
]
to unique continuation from the region
\mathcal{D}
.

A more precise statement of Theorem
1.12
is given later in Theorem
4.1
.

1.4.
Main Ideas of the Proof

We now describe some of the ideas of the proofs of Theorems
1.8
and
1.12
.
As most parts of the proofs have direct analogues in
[
16
,
17
,
28
]
, here we will focus primarily on how the present proofs differ from those of previous works.

First, the main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.8
is a new Carleman estimate for the Klein–Gordon operator near the conformal boundary.
(As is common in unique continuation literature, Theorem
1.8
follows from this Carleman estimate via a standard argument.)
For the Carleman estimate itself, the most noteworthy feature here is the function
f
that defines the weight within the estimate, and for which the level sets are strongly pseudoconvex.

6
6

6

Since the conformal boundary barely fails to be pseudoconvex (see the discussions in
[
16
,
17
,
28
]
), then the pseudoconvexity of the level sets of
f
must degenerate as one approaches the conformal boundary.

In
[
16
,
17
,
28
]
, this
f
was defined to be of the form

\displaystyle f:=\frac{\rho}{\eta_{\ast}(t)}\text{,}

where
t
is a given time function on the spacetime and conformal boundary.
Note in particular that
\{f=0\}
lies on the conformal boundary, while
\{f=c\}
for any
c>0
lies in the interior as long as
\eta_{\ast}(t)>0
.
Moreover, all the level sets of
f
focus at the conformal boundary at any point where
\eta_{\ast}(t)=0
.
In
[
28
]
, it was shown that if the null convexity criterion holds, and if
\eta_{\ast}
is a specific function defined in terms of the constants
A
and
B
in (
1.5
)–(
1.6
), then the level sets of
f
are pseudoconvex for small enough values of
f
.

For our new Carleman estimate, we take instead

\displaystyle f:=\frac{\rho}{\eta}\text{,}

the key difference being that
\eta_{\ast}(t)
is replaced by a more general function
\eta
, defined on some subset of
\mathcal{I}
, that is allowed to depend on all coordinates of
\mathcal{I}
.
In particular, we consider a larger class of foliations
f
that can depend also on the spatial components of
\mathcal{I}
.
A crucial computation in this article (see Lemma
2.20
) shows that if the generalized null convexity criterion (
1.10
) holds, then the level sets of this new
f
are once again pseudoconvex near the conformal boundary.
Moreover, note that in the setting of (
1.10
), the level set
\{f=0\}
corresponds to the boundary domain
\mathcal{D}
, while the level sets
\{f=c\}
, with
c>0
, are hypersurfaces “flying over
\mathcal{D}
” that refocus at the conformal boundary on
\partial\mathcal{D}
.

From this point, the proof of the Carleman estimate resembles that of
[

Remark 1.14

Note this connection between

Now, by (

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}(d\sigma^{2}+\mathsf{h}_{ab}dy^{a}dy^{b})\text{,}

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\rho^{-2}(d\rho^{2}+\mathsf{g}_{ab}dx^{a}dx^{b})

Comparing the two expansions for

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}\mathsf{h}_{ab}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=[\mathfrak{g}_{bc}+\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{d}\partial_{d}\mathfrak{g}_{bc}\,\sigma+(\mathfrak{r}_{1}^{2}\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{bc}+\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d}\partial_{d}\mathfrak{g}_{bc}+\mathfrak{q}^{d}_{1}\mathfrak{q}^{e}_{1}\partial_{de}\mathfrak{g}_{bc})\,\sigma^{2}]

From the above, we deduce the following values for our coefficients:

(3.11)

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{A}_{a,0}=\mathfrak{g}_{ab}\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{b}\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{b}=0\text{,}

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{A}_{1}=2\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{r}_{2}\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{r}_{2}=0\text{,}

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{A}_{a,1}=2\mathfrak{g}_{ab}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{b}+\mathfrak{r}_{1}\partial_{a}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{a}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{g}^{ab}\partial_{b}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\text{,}

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{A}_{a,2}=3\mathfrak{g}_{ab}\mathfrak{q}_{3}^{b}\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{q}_{3}^{a}=0\text{,}

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{A}_{2}=4\mathfrak{g}_{ab}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{a}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{b}+4\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{r}_{3}\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathfrak{r}_{3}=-\frac{1}{4}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{g}^{cd}\partial_{c}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\partial_{d}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\text{.}

Finally, from the last equation of (
3.10
), along with (

), (
3.9
), and (

), we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}\rho^{2}\mathsf{h}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{g}_{cd}\partial_{a}x^{c}\partial_{b}x^{d}+\partial_{a}\rho\partial_{b}\rho

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{g}_{ab}+(\mathfrak{r}_{1}^{2}\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{ab}+\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{c}\partial_{c}\mathfrak{g}_{ab}+\mathfrak{g}_{ad}\partial_{b}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d}+\mathfrak{g}_{bd}\partial_{a}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d})\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})

\displaystyle\qquad+\partial_{a}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\partial_{b}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\,\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})

The non-tensorial terms in (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{c}\partial_{c}\mathfrak{g}_{ab}+\mathfrak{g}_{ad}\partial_{b}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d}+\mathfrak{g}_{bd}\partial_{a}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{e}\mathfrak{g}_{ab}-\mathfrak{g}_{ad}\mathfrak{T}_{be}^{d}-\mathfrak{g}_{bd}\mathfrak{T}_{ae}^{d})\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{e}+\mathfrak{D}_{b}(\mathfrak{g}_{ad}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d})+\mathfrak{D}_{a}(\mathfrak{g}_{bd}\mathfrak{q}_{2}^{d})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{D}_{b}(\mathfrak{r}_{1}\partial_{a}\mathfrak{r}_{1})-\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{D}_{a}(\mathfrak{r}_{1}\partial_{b}\mathfrak{r}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{D}_{a}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{D}_{b}\mathfrak{r}_{1}-\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{D}_{ab}^{2}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\text{.}

Combining (

), (

), and the above yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{h}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{g}_{ab}+(\mathfrak{r}_{1}\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{ab}-\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{D}_{ab}^{2}\mathfrak{r}_{1})\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})}{\mathfrak{r}_{1}^{2}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{g}^{cd}\mathfrak{D}_{c}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{D}_{d}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\,\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{r}_{1}^{-2}\mathfrak{g}_{ab}+\left(\bar{\mathfrak{g}}_{ab}-\mathfrak{r}_{1}^{-1}\mathfrak{D}_{ab}^{2}\mathfrak{r}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{r}_{1}^{-2}\mathfrak{g}^{cd}\mathfrak{D}_{c}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{D}_{d}\mathfrak{r}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{ab}\right)\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})\text{.}

The desired (

) follows from the above after setting
\mathfrak{r}_{1}=\mathfrak{a}^{-1}
.
∎

Using the transformations (
3.5
), we can show that the GNCC is indeed gauge-invariant:

Proposition 3.6

(Gauge invariance)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be as above, and assume the two FG-gauges (
3.3
) and the comparison (
3.4
).
In addition, let
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathfrak{h}
be as in Definition
3.1
.
Then,
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the generalized null convexity condition with respect to
(\rho,\mathsf{g})
-gauge if and only if it satisfies the generalized null convexity condition with respect to
(\sigma,\mathsf{h})
-gauge.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the GNCC in the
(\rho,\mathsf{g})
-gauge, so there exists
\eta\in C^{4}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})
with

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta-\eta\bar{\mathfrak{g}})(\mathfrak{Y},\mathfrak{Y})>c\eta\,\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{Y},\mathfrak{Y})&\text{in }\mathcal{D}\text{,}\\
\eta>0&\text{in }\mathcal{D}\text{,}\\
\eta=0&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{D}\text{,}\end{cases}

with
c>0
, and with
\mathfrak{g}
-null
\mathfrak{Y}\in T\mathcal{D}
.
Now, let
\mathfrak{a}:=\mathfrak{r}^{-1}>0
, and let
\xi:=\mathfrak{a}\eta
.
Furthermore, we denote by
\mathfrak{O}
the Levi-Civita connection with respect to
\mathfrak{h}
.

Clearly,
\xi>0
on
\mathcal{D}
and
\xi=0
on
\partial\mathcal{D}
by definition.
Let
\mathfrak{Z}\in T\mathcal{D}
be
\mathfrak{h}
-null; since
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{a}^{2}\mathfrak{g}
by (
3.5
), then
\mathfrak{Z}
is
\mathfrak{g}
-null as well.
Moreover, using standard formulas for conformal transformation to relate
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{O}
, along with (
3.5
), we compute that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{O}^{2}\xi(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{D}^{2}\xi(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})-\mathfrak{a}^{-1}[2\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\xi-\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{D}^{\sharp}\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{D}^{\sharp}\xi)]

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{D}^{2}(\mathfrak{a}\eta)(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})-2\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{Z}}(\mathfrak{a}\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})+\eta\mathfrak{D}^{2}\mathfrak{a}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})-2\eta\mathfrak{a}^{-1}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\mathfrak{a})^{2}\text{,}

\displaystyle\xi\,\bar{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}\eta\,\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})+\eta\mathfrak{D}^{2}\mathfrak{a}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})-2\eta\mathfrak{a}^{-1}(\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\mathfrak{a})^{2}\text{.}

In particular, the above implies that

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{O}^{2}\xi-\xi\,\bar{\mathfrak{h}})(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}(\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta-\eta\,\bar{\mathfrak{g}})(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})>c\xi\,\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})\text{,}

since
\mathfrak{a}>0
.
This shows that GNCC indeed holds with respect to the
(\sigma,\mathsf{h})
-gauge.
Finally, the converse statement can be proved using a symmetric argument.
∎

3.2.
Examples and Counterexamples

In this subsection, we study some conditions under which the GNCC either is satisfied or fails to hold.
We begin with counterexamples; for this, our first tool is to restrict (
3.1
) to individual null geodesics.

Lemma 3.7

(Necessary size of
\bar{\mathfrak{g}}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
be open and with compact closure, and assume the GNCC (
3.1
) holds on
\mathcal{D}
, for some
\eta
,
c
,
\mathfrak{p}
.
In addition, let
\lambda:[0,\ell]\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}
be any
\mathfrak{g}
-null geodesic on
\mathcal{I}
that also satisfies the following conditions:

(3.13)

\lambda(0),\lambda(\ell)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\text{,}\qquad\lambda(s)\in\mathcal{D}\text{,}\quad 0<s<\ell\text{.}

Then,
\bar{\mathfrak{g}}
satisfies the following inequality along
\lambda
:

(3.14)

\sup_{s\in[0,\ell]}[-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\dot{\lambda}(s),\dot{\lambda}(s))]>\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\text{.}

Proof.

For convenience, we define the following along
\lambda
:

(3.15)

\theta(s):=\eta(\lambda(s))\text{,}\qquad\mathfrak{c}(s):=\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\dot{\lambda}(s),\dot{\lambda}(s))\text{,}\qquad 0\leq s\leq\ell\text{.}

Observe that by compactness, there is some constant
0<p<\frac{\pi}{\ell}
such that

(3.16)

c\,\mathfrak{p}(\dot{\lambda}(s),\dot{\lambda}(s))\geq p^{2}\text{,}\qquad 0\leq s\leq\ell\text{.}

In addition, we consider the quantity

(3.17)

\varphi(s):=\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}-p^{2}}\cdot s\right)\text{,}\qquad 0\leq s\leq\ell\text{.}

Then, (
3.1
) and (
3.15
)–(
3.17
) imply that the following hold:

(3.18)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ddot{\theta}-(\mathfrak{c}+p^{2})\theta>0&\quad\text{on $[0,\ell]$,}\\
\theta>0&\quad\text{on $(0,\ell)$,}\\
\theta(0)=\theta(\ell)=0\text{,}\end{cases}\qquad\begin{cases}\ddot{\varphi}+\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}-p^{2}\right)\varphi=0&\quad\text{on $[0,\ell]$,}\\
\varphi>0&\quad\text{on $(0,\ell]$,}\\
\varphi(0)=0\text{,}\quad\varphi(\ell)>0\text{.}\end{cases}

Assume, for a contradiction, that the desired (
3.14
) does not hold, that is,

(3.19)

-\mathfrak{c}(s)\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\text{,}\qquad 0\leq s\leq\ell\text{.}

We now apply a Sturm comparison argument—from (
3.18
), the assumption (
3.19
), and two integrations by parts, we obtain for each
0<s<\ell
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\int_{0}^{s}[\ddot{\theta}-\mathfrak{c}\,\theta-p^{2}\,\theta]\varphi-\int_{0}^{s}\theta\left(\ddot{\varphi}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\,\varphi-p^{2}\,\varphi\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dot{\theta}(s)\varphi(s)-\theta(s)\dot{\varphi}(s)-\int_{0}^{s}\left(\mathfrak{c}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right)\theta\varphi

\displaystyle\leq\varphi^{2}(s)\left(\frac{\theta}{\varphi}\right)^{\prime}(s)\text{.}

Dividing the above by
\varphi^{2}(s)>0
and integrating from a fixed
0<s_{0}<\ell
yields

\frac{\theta(s)}{\varphi(s)}>\frac{\theta(s_{0})}{\varphi(s_{0})}\text{,}\quad s_{0}<s<\ell\text{,}\qquad\theta(\ell)\geq\frac{\theta(s_{0})}{\varphi(s_{0})}\cdot\varphi(\ell)>0\text{,}

where the second part follows from taking
s\nearrow\ell
.
On the other hand, since
\lambda(\ell)\in\partial\mathcal{D}
, then (
3.15
) implies
\theta(\ell)=0
, contradicting the above.
As a result, (
3.14
) must hold.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

An equivalent formulation of Lemma
3.7
is that if, in the same setting,

-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\dot{\lambda}(s),\dot{\lambda}(s))\leq B^{2}\text{,}\qquad 0\leq s\leq\ell

for some constant
B>0
, then
\ell>\frac{\pi}{B}
.

Lemma
3.7
is useful for identifying situations in which the GNCC cannot hold:

Corollary 3.9

(Failure of GNCC)
.

Suppose
-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})\leq 0
for all vectors
\mathfrak{X}\in T\mathcal{I}
satisfying
\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})=0
.
Then, the GNCC is not satisfied by any domain
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
3.7
to any
\mathfrak{g}
-null geodesic
\lambda
passing through
\mathcal{D}
yields a contradiction, since (
3.14
) implies that
-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\dot{\lambda},\dot{\lambda})
must be positive somewhere.
∎

Recall (see
[
32
]
) that when
(\mathcal{M},g)
is Einstein-vacuum,

\operatorname{Ric}(g)=\Lambda g\text{,}\qquad\Lambda:=-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}<0\text{,}

and has boundary dimension
n\geq 3
, then one also has

-(n-2)\bar{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{Ric}(\mathfrak{g})\text{,}

with
\mathfrak{Ric}
being the Ricci curvature for
\mathfrak{g}
.
As a result, Corollary
3.9
implies that
in vacuum spacetimes, no conformal boundary that is non-positively curved in null directions
,

\mathfrak{Ric}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})\leq 0\text{,}\qquad\mathfrak{X}\in T\mathcal{I}\text{,}\quad\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{X})=0\text{,}

can have a subdomain
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
satisfying the GNCC
.
In particular, the above applies to planar and toric Schwarzschild-AdS spacetimes (which satisfy
\mathfrak{Ric}=0
):

Corollary 3.10

(Counterexamples to GNCC)
.

Given any planar or toric Schwarzschild-AdS spacetime, no subdomain of its conformal boundary can satisfy the GNCC.

Our next objective is to connect the GNCC to the null convexity criterion (abbreviated
NCC
) of
[
28
]
.
This will allow us to generate some common examples for which the GNCC is satisfied.
First, let us recall a precise formulation of the NCC:

Definition 3.11

(Null Convexity Criterion,
[
28
]
)
.

Let
t
be a time function on
\mathcal{I}
, satisfying

K^{-1}\leq-g(\mathfrak{D}^{\sharp}t,\mathfrak{D}^{\sharp}t)\leq K\text{,}\qquad K\geq 1\text{,}

and assume that the level sets of
t
are compact Cauchy hypersurfaces.
Then, we say that the
null convexity criterion
holds on an open subset
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, with associated constants
0\leq B<C
, iff the following inequalities hold for any vector
\mathfrak{Z}\in\mathcal{T}\mathcal{D}
with
\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})=0

(3.20)

-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})\geq C^{2}\cdot(\mathfrak{Z}t)^{2}\text{,}\qquad|\mathfrak{D}^{2}t(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})|\leq 2B\cdot(\mathfrak{Z}t)^{2}\text{.}

Remark 3.12
.

The compactness assumptions in Definition

, and elsewhere in this paper, are imposed to simplify discussions.
Otherwise, one encounters additional technical issues in proving unique continuation results for (
1.4
), due to integrability issues and the need for uniform bounds on the geometry.
For further discussions, see
[
17
,
28
]
, which sidestep these issues by assuming compact support properties on their solutions of (

).

Our main observation relating the GNCC and the NCC can be roughly characterized as the NCC implying the GNCC on a sufficiently large timespan:

Proposition 3.13

(NCC implies GNCC)
.

Let
t
be a time function on
\mathcal{I}
satisfying the same assumptions as in Definition
3.11
, and consider a boundary domain of the form

(3.21)

\mathcal{D}_{t_{-},t_{+}}:=\{t_{-}<t<t_{+}\}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\text{,}\qquad\inf_{\mathcal{I}}t<t_{-}<t_{+}<\sup_{\mathcal{I}}t\text{.}

Then, if the NCC holds on
\mathcal{D}_{t_{-},t_{+}}
, with constants
0\leq B<C
, and if

19
19

19

Here, the value of
\tan^{-1}
in (
3.22
) is chosen to lie in
[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
.

(3.22)

t_{+}-t_{-}>\mathcal{T}_{+}(B,C):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{C^{2}-B^{2}}}\,\tan^{-1}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{C^{2}-B^{2}}}{B}\right)\text{,}

\mathcal{D}_{t_{-},t_{+}}

Proof.

Observe that there exist constants
B<b<c<C
such that

t_{+}-t_{-}=\mathcal{T}_{+}(b,c)>\mathcal{T}_{+}(B,C)\text{.}

By time translation, we can also assume without loss of generality
t_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{T}_{+}(b,c)

Let

(3.23)

\varphi(s):=e^{-b|s|}\,\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt{c^{2}-b^{2}}}{2}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(b,c)-2|s|)\right)\text{,}\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}\text{.}

From direct computations (see also

), we see that

\begin{cases}\ddot{\varphi}(s)-2b\,\dot{\varphi}(s)+c^{2}\varphi(s)=0\text{,}&\qquad s\leq 0\text{,}\\

Next, we define on
\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{t_{-},t_{+}}

(3.25)

\eta:=\varphi(t)\text{.}

By (
3.23
) and (

\eta>0
on
\mathcal{D}_{t_{-},t_{+}}

\eta=0

\partial\mathcal{D}_{t_{-},t_{+}}

Furthermore, given any
\mathfrak{g}
-null vector

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Z}^{a}\mathfrak{Z}^{b}[\ddot{\varphi}(t)\,\partial_{a}t\partial_{b}t+\dot{\varphi}(t)(\partial_{ab}t-\mathfrak{T}^{d}_{ab}\partial_{d}t)]

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{D}^{2}\eta-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}\,\eta)(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}[\mathfrak{D}^{2}t(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})+2b(\mathfrak{Z}t)^{2}]\dot{\varphi}(t)+[-\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{Z},\mathfrak{Z})-c^{2}(\mathfrak{Z}t)^{2}]\varphi(t)\text{,}&\quad t\leq 0\text{,}\\

3.3.
Causal Diamonds

We conclude this section with some examples of boundary domains that satisfy or violate the GNCC.
Here, we focus our attention on causal diamond domains, which are often considered in the physics literature.

For simplicity, we restrict our attention to the case of Minkowski boundary geometry,

3.3.1.
The Case

First, we study the case

Finally, by (

Thus, by taking

Next, suppose (3) holds, and let

be the smallest parameter with

Then, the above implies

In particular, the above yields (

5.
Carleman estimate

In this section, we precisely state and then prove the first main theorem of this paper, Theorem

The main focus of this section, however, will be on the corresponding Carleman estimate, which is the key tool for proving unique continuation.

5.1.
Preliminaries

The key applications of our Carleman estimates will require that they also apply to general vertical tensor fields.
As a result, we will need to define some additional concepts and notations concerning vertical and mixed tensorial quantities.

Definition 5.1
