Weyl’s Law for compact rank one symmetric spaces
Sai Sriharsha Indukuri, Ritwik Mukherjee

Abstract
. Weyl’s law is a fundamental result governing the asymptotic behaviour of the eigenvalues of the Laplacian. It states that for a compact d d dimensional manifold M M (without boundary), the eigenvalue counting function N M ​ ( λ ) N_{M}(\lambda) has the following asymptotic growth N M ​ ( λ ) = Vol ​ ( M ) ​ ( ω d ( 2 ​ π ) d ) ​ λ d + O ⁡ ( λ d − 1 ) . N_{M}(\lambda)=\textnormal{Vol}(M)\left(\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right)\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1}). A natural question is: when is the error term sharp and when can it be improved? It has been known for a long time that the error term is sharp for the round sphere (since 1968 1968 ). In contrast, it has only recently been shown (in 2019 2019 ) by Iosevich and Wyman that for the product of spheres, the error term can be polynomially improved. They conjecture that a polynomial improvement should be true for products in general. In this paper we extend both these results to Compact Rank One Symmetric Spaces (CROSSes). We show that for CROSSes, the error term is sharp. Furthermore, we show that for a product of CROSSes, the error term can be polynomially improved. This gives further evidence to the conjecture made by Iosevich and Wyman.

1.
Introduction

Let
(M,g)
be a compact
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary
and let
\Delta_{M}
be the
Laplace-Beltrami operator (acting on functions). Associated to this operator,
we define the eigenvalue counting function

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\#\{\textnormal{eigenvalues of $\Delta_{M}$}\leq\lambda^{2}\}.

In other words,
N_{M}(\lambda)
is the number of eigenvalues of
\Delta_{M}
less than or equal to
\lambda^{2}

(counted with multiplicities). It is a fundamental result that the asymptotic growth of
N_{M}(\lambda)

is governed by Weyl’s law:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\longrightarrow\infty}\frac{N_{M}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{d}}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Vol}(M)\left(\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right),

(1)

where

\omega_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A proof of (
1
)
can be found in
[
2
, page 95, corollary 2.43]
; it uses the the asymptotic expansion of the heat kernel.

As with any question on asymptotics, it is natural to ask what can be said about the error term.
In
1920
, Richard Courant showed that the error term is of the order of
O(\lambda^{d-1}\log(\lambda))

(
[
5
]
). Since then, an extensive amount of work has been done by mathematicians to
study the error term.

It was shown by Lars Hörmander in
1968
(
[
7
]
) that the error term is

O(\lambda^{d-1})
.
He further showed in that paper that the
O(\lambda^{d-1})

error term is sharp for the round sphere
S^{d}

(the special case of the three dimensional sphere
S^{3}
was worked out earlier by Avacumovič in
[
1
]
).

Although the example of the sphere shows that the error, in general, can not
be made better than
O(\lambda^{d-1})
, it is still
natural to ask under what special cases the error can be improved. In
[
6
]
and
[
9
]
, Duistermaat and Guillemin
and Ivrii show that if the measure of all periodic geodesic billiards is zero, then the error term can be improved
(see
[
8
, page 595, theorem 1.3]
for the exact statement).

In
[
8
]
, Iosevich and Wyman show

[
8
, page 596, theorem 1.4]

that for a product of
at least two compact
Riemannian manifolds without boundary, the
hypothesis of
Duistermaat and Guillemin
and Ivrii’s Theorem holds; hence the error term can be improved. But that is not all, they show that
for the product of spheres, the
error term
can be
polynomially
improved. More precisely, they show
[
8
, page 596, theorem 1.4]

that if
M
is the product of
n
spheres, then

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Vol}(M)\left(\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right)\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}).

(2)

Based on this theorem, Iosevich and Wyman make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

[
8
, page 597]

Let
M:=M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n}
be the product of
at least two compact Riemannian manifolds without boundary. Then there exists a
\delta>0

such that

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Vol}(M)\left(\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right)\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\delta}).

(3)

In this paper, we give further evidence to support the above conjecture.

Before stating the main result of this paper, let us
recall from
[
3
, page 76]

that a Compact Rank One Symmetric Spaces (CROSS)
is either the sphere
S^{d}
, the
real projective space
\mathbb{RP}^{d}
,
the complex projective space
\mathbb{CP}^{d}
, the quaternionic projective space
\mathbb{HP}^{d}

or the two dimensional octonionic projective space
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
.
Note that aside from
S^{1}
and
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
(which is again
S^{1}
with a different radius), all the other CROSSes
have real dimension at least two.
The main result of this paper is as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M:=M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n}
be the product of
at least two compact Riemannian manifolds without boundary.
Suppose each
M_{i}
is one of the CROSSes of real dimension at least two.
Then

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Vol}(M)\left(\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right)\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}).

(4)

Note that theorem
1.2
supports Conjecture
1.1
, with
\delta:=\frac{n-1}{n+1}
.

The second result of our paper is as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M
be one the

CROSSes
.
Then the error term in Weyl’s law can not be improved.

2.
Structure of the paper

In order to prove theorem
1.2
, we use a fundamental
lattice counting result proved by Iosevich and Wyman

[
8
, page 597, theorem 1.8]
.
The main observation of this paper is that their lattice counting result
applies to a much more general setting than spheres; in particular it applies to all CROSSes.

In section
3
, we describe the general setup in which we can prove
theorem
1.2
. The approach will work if the eigenvalues and multiplicities are explicitly given
and a certain condition on the multiplicity function can be verified.

In section
5
we show that
theorem
1.2
is true for product of all manifolds whose multiplicity function satisfies the symmetry condition.
In the proof, we make extensive use of Iosevich and Wyman’s lattice counting result.
In section
6
,
we verify that the symmetry condition is indeed satisfied for all the CROSSes.

Finally in section
7
, we show that the error is sharp for a single CROSS.

3.
Setup and notation

Our set up is as follows.
Let

We consider the eigenvalues of the Laplacian acting on functions. Assume that the (square root of) the

\displaystyle:=\{\sqrt{\alpha_{M}(k)}:k=0,1,2,\ldots\},\qquad\textnormal{where}\qquad\alpha_{M}(k):=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M}.

\displaystyle=Ct,\qquad\textnormal{if}\qquad d=2,

and

is some constant.

Definition 3.1

.
If conditions (

), (

) and (

) are satisfied, then

-manifold.

.

.
The main results of this paper can be rephrased by the following three theorems.

-manifolds of dimensions

Let

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle:=M_{1}\times M_{2}\times\ldots\times M_{n},\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad d:=d_{1}+d_{2}\ldots+d_{n}.

If

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Vol}(M)\left(\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right)\lambda^{d}+O\left(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}\right).

.

Let
(M,g)
be a

\mathsf{W}
-manifold. Then the error term in Weyl’s law can not be improved.

Theorem 3.4
.

Compact Rank One Symmetric Spaces

We note that theorems

Theorem 4.1
.

.

Let
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 2}
and
n\geq 2
.
let
\mathsf{Q}_{d_{i}-1}(t)
be a degree
d_{i}-1
polynomial in
t
,
that does not have a
t^{d_{i}-2}
term, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{d_{i}-1}(t)

\displaystyle=C_{i}t^{d_{i}-1}+\varphi_{i}(t),\qquad\textnormal{if}\qquad d_{i}>2\qquad\textnormal{and}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{d_{i}-1}(t)

\displaystyle=C_{i}t,\qquad\textnormal{if}\qquad d_{i}=2,

(35)

where
C_{i}
is a constant and
\varphi_{i}(t)
is a polynomial of degree at most
d_{i}-3
.
Let
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
be
n
fixed real numbers and

P_{1},P_{2},\ldots P_{n}
be
n
fixed positive real numbers.
Given a real number
\lambda
, let
N(\lambda)
be defined as

\displaystyle N(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}-y_{1},\ldots,m_{n}-y_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\\
P_{1}m_{1}^{2}+\ldots+P_{n}m_{n}^{2}\leq a\lambda^{2}+b\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Q}_{d_{1}-1}(m_{1})\ldots\mathsf{Q}_{d_{n}-1}(m_{n}),

(36)

where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\displaystyle N(\lambda)

\displaystyle=C\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}),

(37)

where
d:=d_{1}+d_{2}\ldots+d_{n}
and
C
is a constant.

Proof:
Let us unwind the summand of
N(\lambda)
. Let us denote the summation of the leading
power as
T_{1}
. The sum of the remaining powers will be denoted by
T_{2}
. In other words,

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}-y_{1},\ldots,m_{n}-y_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\\
P_{1}m_{1}^{2}+\ldots+P_{n}m_{n}^{2}\leq a\lambda^{2}+b\end{subarray}}Cm_{1}^{d_{1}-1}\ldots m_{n}^{d_{n}-1}.

(38)

Note that the sum total of the powers of all the
m_{i}
is
d-n
.

Next, we note that
T_{2}
is a finite sum of the type

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}-y_{1},\ldots,m_{n}-y_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\\
P_{1}m_{1}^{2}+\ldots+P_{n}m_{n}^{2}\leq a\lambda^{2}+b\end{subarray}}Cm_{1}^{e_{1}}\ldots m_{n}^{e_{n}},

(39)

where the sum of the total power
e_{1}+\ldots e_{n}
is at most
d-n-2

(due to the condition
35
). In particular, the sum total of the powers of
m_{i}
in the
summand of each of the terms of
T_{2}
is at least
2
less than the
sum total of the powers of the
m_{i}
in the summand of
T_{1}
.

Using theorem
4.2
, we conclude that

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=C\Big(a\lambda^{2}+b\Big)^{\frac{d}{2}}+O\left((a\lambda^{2}+b)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)}\right).

(40)

Using binomial theorem, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Big(a\lambda^{2}+b\Big)^{\frac{d}{2}}

\displaystyle=a^{\frac{d}{2}}\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-2}).

(41)

Combining (
40
) and (
41
), we conclude that

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=C\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}).

(42)

Similarly, we again apply theorem
4.2
and the
binomial theorem to each of the terms of
T_{2}
. Doing that, we
conclude that

T_{2}
will be given by the right hand side of (
42
) with
d
replaced by
d-2
, i.e.

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=C\lambda^{d-2}+O(\lambda^{d-3-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}).

(43)

However, (
43
) implies that

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=O(\lambda^{d-2}).

(44)

Since
N(\lambda)=T_{1}+T_{2}
, we conclude using (
42
) and (
44
), that

\displaystyle N(\lambda)

\displaystyle=C\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}),

(45)

thereby proving theorem
5.1
. ∎

We are now ready to prove theorem
3.2
.

Proof of theorem
3.2
:
Let
N_{M}(\lambda)
denote the eigenvalue counting function on
M
.
Recall that
N_{M}(\lambda)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\\
\alpha_{M_{1}}(k_{1})+\ldots+\alpha_{M_{n}}(k_{n})\leq\lambda^{2}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{R}_{M_{1}}(k_{1})\ldots\mathsf{R}_{M_{n}}(k_{n}).

(46)

Let us now make a substitution

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle:=k_{1}+\frac{B_{M_{1}}}{2A_{M_{1}}},\ldots,m_{n}:=k_{n}+\frac{B_{M_{n}}}{2A_{M_{n}}}.

(47)

Let
\mathsf{Q}_{M_{i}}(t)
be as defined by (
7
), i.e.

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{R}_{M_{i}}\Big(t-\frac{B_{M_{i}}}{2A_{M_{i}}}\Big).

(48)

With this, the counting function becomes

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}-\frac{B_{M_{1}}}{2A_{M_{1}}},\ldots,m_{n}-\frac{B_{M_{n}}}{2A_{M_{n}}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\\
A_{M_{1}}m_{1}^{2}+\ldots+A_{M_{n}}m_{n}^{2}\leq\lambda^{2}+\kappa\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Q}_{M_{1}}(m_{1})\ldots\mathsf{Q}_{M_{n}}(m_{n}),

(49)

where
\kappa
is given by

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{B_{M_{i}}^{2}}{4A_{M_{i}}}-C_{M_{i}}\Big).

Since each of the
M_{i}
is a

\mathsf{W}
-manifold, we can use theorem
5.1

and conclude that

\displaystyle N_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=C\lambda^{d}+O(\lambda^{d-1-\frac{n-1}{n+1}}).

(50)

The constant
C
can now be obtained from Weyl’s law for compact manifolds without boundary.

6.
CROSSes are
\mathsf{W}
-manifolds: proof

We will now prove theorem
3.4
, i.e. we will
verify that all the CROSSes are
\mathsf{W}
-manifolds.

First of all, we recall that the eigenvalues and multiplicities of the
Laplacian can be packaged together in the zeta function.
If we are in the setting of section
3
, we get that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)\exp(-\alpha_{M}(k)s).

(51)

We will now use the results of the paper
[
4
]
, where the authors explicitly compute

\zeta_{M}(s)
for all CROSSes. Knowing
\zeta_{M}(s)
immediately tells us what is

\alpha_{M}(k)
and
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
. Let us now explicitly write down the zeta function for
each of these spaces. Although the case of the sphere has been worked out in
[
8
]
, we will
include it here for completeness.

6.1.
Odd dimensional spheres

Let us start with the odd dimensional sphere
M=S^{2d-1}
, with
d\geq 3
. Following

[
4
, page 7, theorem 2.4]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{k+d-1}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{k+j}{j}\Big)\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{k^{2}+2k(d-1)}{4(d-1)}\Big)s\Big).

(52)

Comparing (
52
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{k^{2}+2k(d-1)}{4(d-1)}\Big)\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=\Big(\frac{k+d-1}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{k+j}{j}\Big).

(53)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
2d-2
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{4(d-1)}k^{2}+\frac{1}{2}k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{4(d-1)},B_{M}=\frac{1}{2},C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=d-1.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}(t-(d-1))=\frac{t}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{t-(d-1)+j}{j}=\frac{t}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j},

(54)

where
a_{j}:=j-(d-1),\ j=1,\dots,2d-3
.

We claim that the coefficient of
t^{2d-3}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero. To see this, we note that it
is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of
t^{2d-4}
in
\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}
, that is,
\sum_{j=1}^{2d-3}a_{j}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2d-3}a_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2d-3}\left(j-d+1\right)=\frac{(2d-3)(2d-2)}{2}-(d-1)(2d-3)=0,

and hence, we are done.

To finish the case of odd spheres, we also need to separately consider
M=S^{3}
.
Following

[
4
, page 6, equation 2.3 (a)]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{2}\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{k^{2}+2k}{4}\Big)s\Big).

(55)

Comparing (
55
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{k^{2}+2k}{4}\Big)\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=(k+1)^{2}.

(56)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
2
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{4}k^{2}+\frac{1}{2}k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{4},B_{M}=\frac{1}{2},C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=1.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that the coefficient of
t
in the polynomial

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}(t-1)

(57)

is zero.
This completes all the odd spheres.

6.2.
Even dimensional spheres

Next, we consider even dimensional spheres
M=S^{2d}
, with
d\geq 1
.
Following

[
4
, page 8, theorem 3.2]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2k+2d-1}{2d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{k+j}{j}\Big)\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{k^{2}+k(2d-1)}{4d-2}\Big)s\Big).

(58)

Comparing (
58
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{k^{2}+k(2d-1)}{4d-2}\Big)\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=\Big(\frac{2k+2d-1}{2d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{k+j}{j}\Big).

(59)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
2d-1
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{4d-2}k^{2}+\frac{2d-1}{4d-2}k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{4d-2},B_{M}=\frac{1}{2},C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{2d-1}{2}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\Big(t-\frac{2d-1}{2}\Big)=\frac{2}{(2d-1)}t\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j},

(60)

where
a_{j}:=-\frac{2d-1}{2}+j,\ j=1,\dots,2d-2
.

We claim that the coefficient of
t^{2d-2}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero. To see this, we note that it is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of
t^{2d-3}
in
\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}
, that is,
\sum_{j=1}^{2d-2}a_{j}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2d-2}a_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2d-2}\left(j-\frac{2d-1}{2}\right)=\frac{(2d-2)(2d-1)}{2}-\frac{(2d-1)(2d-2)}{2}=0,

and hence, we are done. This completes all the spheres.

6.3.
Odd dimensional real projective spaces

Let us now move on to the real projective spaces.
We will start with the odd dimensional real projective spaces

M=\mathbb{RP}^{2d-1}
, with
d\geq 3
.
Following
[
4
, page 8, corollary 2.6]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2k+d-1}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{2k+j}{j}\Big)\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{k^{2}+k(d-1)}{d-1}\Big)s\Big).

(61)

Comparing (
61
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k^{2}+k(d-1)}{d-1}\right)\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=\left(\frac{2k+d-1}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{2k+j}{j}\right).

(62)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
2d-2
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{d-1}k^{2}+k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{d-1},B_{M}=1,C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{d-1}{2}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\Big(t-\frac{d-1}{2}\Big)=\frac{2t}{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{2t+a_{j}}{j},

(63)

where
a_{j}:=j-(d-1),\ j=1,\dots,2d-3
.

We claim that the coefficient of
t^{2d-3}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero. To see this,
we note that it is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of

t^{2d-4}
in
\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{2t+a_{j}}{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{t+\frac{a_{j}}{2}}{\frac{j}{2}}
, that is,
\sum_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{a_{j}}{2}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2d-3}\frac{a_{j}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2d-3}\left(j-d+1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(2d-3)(2d-2)}{2}-(d-1)(2d-3)\right)=0,

and hence, we are done.

To finish the case of the odd real projective spaces,
we also need to separately consider
M=\mathbb{RP}^{3}
.
Following

[
4
, page 6, equation 2.3 (b)]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+1)^{2}\exp\Big(-(k^{2}+k)s\Big).

(64)

Comparing (
64
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=k^{2}+k\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=(2k+1)^{2}.

(65)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
2
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=1,B_{M}=1,C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{1}{2}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and observe that the coefficient of
t
in the polynomial

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\Big(t-\frac{1}{2}\Big)=4t^{2}

(66)

is zero.
This completes all the odd real projective spaces.

6.4.
Even dimensional real projective spaces

Next, we consider even dimensional real projective space
M=\mathbb{RP}^{2d}
, with
d\geq 1
.
Following

[
4
, pages 8-9, corollary 3.4]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{4k+2d-1}{2d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{2k+j}{j}\Big)\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{2k^{2}+k(2d-1)}{2d-1}\Big)s\Big).

(67)

Comparing (
67
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2k^{2}+k(2d-1)}{2d-1}\Big)\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=\Big(\frac{4k+2d-1}{2d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{2k+j}{j}\Big).

(68)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)

is a polynomial of degree
2d-1
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{2}{2d-1}k^{2}+k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{2}{2d-1},B_{M}=1,C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{2d-1}{4}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\Big(t-\frac{2d-1}{4}\Big)=\frac{4t}{2d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{2t+a_{j}}{j},

(69)

where
a_{j}:=j-\frac{2d-1}{2},\ j=1,\dots,2d-2
. We claim that the coefficient of
t^{2d-2}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero.
To see this, we note that it is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of
t^{2d-3}
in
\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{2t+a_{j}}{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{t+\frac{a_{j}}{2}}{\frac{j}{2}}
, that is,
\sum_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{a_{j}}{2}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2d-2}\frac{a_{j}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2d-2}\left(j-\frac{2d-1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(2d-2)(2d-1)}{2}-\frac{(2d-1)(2d-2)}{2}\right)=0,

and hence, we are done.

6.5.
Complex projective spaces

Let us now move on to the complex projective spaces
M=\mathbb{CP}^{d}
,
with
d\geq 2
. Notice that the real dimension of this manifold is
2d
.
Following
[
4
, page 9, theorem 4.2]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2k+d}{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\Big(\frac{k+j}{j}\Big)^{2}\Big)\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{k^{2}+kd}{d+1}\Big)s\Big).

(70)

Comparing (
70
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k^{2}+kd}{d+1}\right)\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)=\left(\frac{2k+d}{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\Big(\frac{k+j}{j}\Big)^{2}\right).

(71)

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
2d-1
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{d+1}k^{2}+\frac{d}{d+1}k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{d+1},B_{M}=\frac{d}{d+1},C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{d}{2}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\left(t-\frac{d}{2}\right)=\frac{2t}{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}\right)^{2},

(72)

where
a_{j}:=j-\frac{d}{2},\ j=1,\dots,d-1
. We claim that the coefficient of
t^{2d-2}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero. To see this, we note that it is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of
t^{2d-3}
in
\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}\right)^{2}
, that is,
\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}2a_{j}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}2a_{j}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(j-\frac{d}{2}\right)=2\left(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\right)=0,

and hence, we are done.

6.6.
Quaternionic projective spaces

Let us now move on to the quaternionic projective spaces
M=\mathbb{HP}^{d-1}
,
with
d\geq 2
.
We are referring to the (quaternionic) dimension as
d-1
as opposed to
d
in order to make it easy to follow the
notation of
[
4
]
.
Notice that the real dimension of

\mathbb{HP}^{d-1}

is
4d-4
.
Following
[
4
, page 10, theorem 5.2]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2k+2d-1}{2d-1}\prod_{j=2}^{2d-2}\frac{k+j}{j}\prod_{l=1}^{2d-3}\frac{k+l}{l}\Big)\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{k^{2}+2kd-k}{2(d+1)}\Big)s\Big).

(73)

Comparing (
73
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{k^{2}+2kd-k}{2(d+1)}\Big)\qquad\textnormal{and}

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2k+2d-1}{2d-1}\prod_{j=2}^{2d-2}\frac{k+j}{j}\prod_{l=1}^{2d-3}\frac{k+l}{l}\Big).

Notice that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
4d-5
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{2(d+1)}k^{2}+\frac{2d-1}{2(d+1)}k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{2(d+1)},B_{M}=\frac{2d-1}{2(d+1)},C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{2d-1}{2}=d-\frac{1}{2}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\left(t-\left(d-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)=\frac{2t}{2d-1}\prod_{j=2}^{2d-2}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}\prod_{l=1}^{2d-3}\frac{t+b_{l}}{l},

(74)

where

\displaystyle a_{j}

\displaystyle=j-\left(d-\frac{1}{2}\right),\ j=2,\dots,2d-2,

\displaystyle b_{l}

\displaystyle=l-\left(d-\frac{1}{2}\right),\ l=1,\dots,2d-3.

We claim that the coefficient of
t^{4d-6}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero. To see this, we note that it is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of
t^{4d-7}
in
\prod_{j=2}^{2d-2}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}\prod_{l=1}^{2d-3}\frac{t+b_{l}}{l}
, that is,
\sum_{j=2}^{2d-2}a_{j}+\sum_{l=1}^{2d-3}b_{l}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{2d-2}a_{j}+\sum_{l=1}^{2d-3}b_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{2d-2}j+\sum_{l=1}^{2d-3}l-(4d-6)\left(d-\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(2d-2)(2d-1)}{2}-1\right)+\frac{(2d-3)(2d-2)}{2}-\frac{(4d-6)(2d-1)}{2}=0,

and hence, the claim is true.

6.7.
Octonionic projective plane

Finally, we consider
M=\mathbb{OP}^{2}
, the octonionic projective plane.
Note that the real dimension of this space is
16
.
Following

[
4
, page 11, theorem 6.2]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2k+11}{11}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{k+j}{j}\right)\left(\prod_{l=4}^{7}\left(\frac{k+l}{l}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\prod_{m=8}^{10}\frac{k+m}{m}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(k^{2}+11k)}{18}s\right).

(75)

Comparing (
75
) with (
51
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k^{2}+11k)}{18}\qquad\textnormal{and}

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{2k+11}{11}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{k+j}{j}\right)\left(\prod_{l=4}^{7}\left(\frac{k+l}{l}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\prod_{m=8}^{10}\frac{k+m}{m}\right).

Note that
\mathsf{R}_{M}(k)
is a polynomial of degree
15
. Now,

\alpha_{M}(k)=\frac{1}{18}k^{2}+\frac{11}{18}k=A_{M}k^{2}+B_{M}k+C_{M},

where
A_{M}=\frac{1}{18},B_{M}=\frac{11}{18},C_{M}=0
and therefore,

\frac{B_{M}}{2A_{M}}=\frac{11}{2}.

Next, we apply definition (
7
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{M}\Big(t-\frac{11}{2}\Big)=\frac{2t}{11}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}\prod_{l=4}^{7}\left(\frac{t+a_{l}}{l}\right)^{2}\prod_{m=8}^{10}\frac{t+a_{m}}{m},

(76)

where

\displaystyle a_{j}

\displaystyle=j-\frac{11}{2},\ j=1,\dots,10.

We claim that the coefficient of
t^{14}
in
\mathsf{Q}_{M}(t)
is zero. To see this, we note that it is sufficient to prove that the numerator of the coefficient of
t^{13}
in
\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{t+a_{j}}{j}\prod_{l=4}^{7}\left(\frac{t+a_{l}}{l}\right)^{2}\prod_{m=8}^{10}\frac{t+a_{m}}{m}
, that is,
\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}+\sum_{l=4}^{7}2a_{l}+\sum_{m=8}^{10}a_{m}
is zero. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}+\sum_{l=4}^{7}2a_{l}+\sum_{m=8}^{10}a_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{3}j+\sum_{m=8}^{10}m+2\sum_{l=4}^{7}l+3\left(-\frac{11}{2}\right)+3\left(-\frac{11}{2}\right)+8\left(-\frac{11}{2}\right)=0,

and hence, the claim is proved.

This completes the proof of theorem
3.4
. ∎

7.
Error is sharp for a single
\mathsf{W}
-manifold: proof

In this section, we will prove theorem
3.3
.
It was shown by Hörmander in
[
7
]
that the error term in Weyl’s law can not
be improved for the round sphere.
We will generalize his proof to
\mathsf{W}
-manifolds.
Let us first prove a few auxiliary results.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let us define the following function

\psi_{k}:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, given by

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(n)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{k-1}

\displaystyle=1^{k-1}+2^{k-1}+\ldots+n^{k-1},

(77)

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{k}}{k}+\frac{1}{2}n^{k-1}+\mathcal{R}_{k-2}(n),

(78)

where
\mathcal{R}_{k-2}(n)
is a polynomial in
n
of degree at most
k-2
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 2}
.
Note that

\displaystyle(n+1)^{k}-n^{k}=kn^{k-1}+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}n^{k-2}+\cdots+1

(79)

\displaystyle n^{k}-(n-1)^{k}=k(n-1)^{k-1}+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}(n-1)^{k-2}+\cdots+1

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle 2^{k}-1^{k}=k\cdot 1^{k-1}+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\cdot 1^{k-2}+\cdots+1.

Adding up all the terms on
the left hand side of
equation
(
79
), we get a telescopic sum which equals

(n+1)^{k}-1^{k}
.
Equating this with the summation of the right hand
side of equation (
79
),
we conclude that

(n+1)^{k}-1^{k}=k\psi_{k}(n)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\psi_{k-1}(n)+\cdots+\psi_{1}(n).

(80)

In equation (
80
), we note that
\psi_{1}(n)=n
.
Using the fact that
\psi_{2}(n)=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}

and equation (
80
), equation (
78
)
follows by using induction on
k
.
This proves the desired result.
∎

Next, we prove the following result.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. For
\lambda

sufficiently large and positive, define

\displaystyle M(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{[\sqrt{a\lambda^{2}+b}-y]^{d}}{d}+(y+\frac{1}{2})[\sqrt{a\lambda^{2}+b}-y]^{d-1}.

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\longrightarrow+\infty}\frac{M(\lambda)}{\lambda^{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}.

(81)

Furthermore, the error term
\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
can not be
O(\lambda^{d-1-\varepsilon})

for any positive
\varepsilon
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=M(\lambda)-\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}\lambda^{d}.

Proof:
Equation (
81
) is immediate using the fact that

\displaystyle A+B-1

\displaystyle\leq[A+B]\leq A+B+1.

(82)

An application of (
82
), binomial theorem
and the squeeze rule immediately gives us (
81
).
We now prove the second assertion. We will prove by contradiction. Suppose

\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
was in
O(\lambda^{d-1-\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Let us define the following discrete subset of
\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:a\lambda^{2}+b>0,\sqrt{a\lambda^{2}+b}-y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\Big\}.

Define
\gamma:\Lambda\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, given by

\displaystyle\gamma(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{a\lambda^{2}+b}-y.

(83)

To lighten the notation,
we will abbreviate
\gamma(\lambda)
by
\gamma
; however, it should always
be understood that
\gamma
depends on
\lambda
via equation (
83
).
Hence, if the error term was in
O(\lambda^{d-1-\varepsilon})
, then it would imply that there is some constant

C>0
such that for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\Big(y+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\gamma^{d-1}+\Big(\frac{\gamma^{d}}{d}-\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}\lambda^{d}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{d-1-\varepsilon}.

(84)

Using the fact that
|A|-|B|\leq|A+B|

(note that
|A+B-B|\leq|A+B|+|B|
which implies what we have written),
we conclude that

\displaystyle\Big|\Big(y+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\gamma^{d-1}\Big|-\Big|\frac{\gamma^{d}}{d}-\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}\lambda^{d}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{d-1-\varepsilon}.

(85)

Here we set

A:=\Big(y+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\gamma^{d-1}\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad B:=\Big(\frac{\gamma^{d}}{d}-\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}\lambda^{d}\Big).

Now we divide both sides of equation (
85
)
and take the limit as
\lambda
tends to infinity (and
\lambda\in\Lambda
).
The right hand side will obviously be zero. We first note that

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\longrightarrow\infty\\
\lambda\in\Lambda\end{subarray}}\frac{\gamma^{d}}{d}-\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}\lambda^{d}

\displaystyle=0.

This follows by taking a binomial expansion of
\gamma
and noticing that after subtracting the
leading term (namely
\frac{a^{\frac{d}{2}}}{d}\lambda^{d}
), we are left with a terms of order
\lambda^{d-2}
or less.
Hence, what remains is the first term on the left hand side of equation
(
85
).
We note that

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\longrightarrow\infty\\
\lambda\in\Lambda\end{subarray}}\Big(y+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\frac{\gamma^{d-1}}{\lambda^{d-1}}

\displaystyle=\Big(y+\frac{1}{2}\Big).

Since
y
is non negative, the above limit is positive. This gives us a contradiction. ∎

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 2}

and
\mathsf{Q}_{d-1}(t)
be a degree
d-1
polynomial in
t
that does not have the
t^{d-2}
term. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{d-1}(t)

\displaystyle=Ct^{d-1}+\varphi(t),\qquad\textnormal{if}\qquad d>2\qquad\textnormal{and}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{d-1}(t)

\displaystyle=Ct,\qquad\textnormal{if}\qquad d=2,

where
\varphi(t)
is a polynomial of degree at most
d-3
and

C
is some non-zero constant.
Let
y
be a fixed non negative real number and

A
a fixed positive real numbers.
Define

N:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

as

\displaystyle N(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m-y\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\\
Am^{2}\leq a\lambda^{2}+b\end{subarray}}\mathsf{Q}_{d-1}(m).

(86)

where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then the error term in the asymptotic expression of
N(\lambda)

(after subtracting off the leading term) can not be improved by
O(\lambda^{d-1-\varepsilon})

for any positive
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us set
C
to be equal to one.
We sum up the leading term of
\mathsf{Q}_{d-1}(t)

(namely the degree
t^{d-1}
terms) using Proposition
7.1
.
The first two terms precisely correspond to
M(\lambda)
as given by
Lemma
7.2
(replace
a
by
\frac{a}{A}
and replace
b
by
\frac{b}{A}
).
The remaining terms are all of order
\lambda^{d-2}
or less
(this is where we use the fact that
\mathsf{Q}_{d-1}(t)
does not have a
t^{d-2}
term).
Hence the remaining terms can not compensate
for the error term from
M(\lambda)
. We have already seen that the error term for
M(\lambda)

can not be improved. This shows that the error term for
N(\lambda)
can not be improved.
∎

Since the Eigenvalue counting function
N(\lambda)

for all
\mathsf{W}
-manifolds is of the form
considered in Theorem
7.3
, we have proved Theorem
1.3

8.
Counting Lattice Points in a Ball

To keep the paper self contained,
we give a proof of
equation (
27
) (even though it is a standard fact).
We will prove the following general result.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
p
be any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d\geq 1
. Given
\lambda>0
, let
B_{p}(\lambda)
denote the closed
p
-ball of radius
\lambda
around the origin, i.e.
B_{p}(\lambda):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:p(x)\leq\lambda\}
.
Let
N^{p}(\lambda):=\#(\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap B_{p}(\lambda))
denote
the number of integer lattice points in
B_{p}(\lambda)
.
Then,
N^{p}(\lambda)\in O(\lambda^{d})
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

The supremum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is particularly suited for counting integer lattice points, so this is the case we first deal with.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
p_{\infty}
denote the supremum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
p_{\infty}(x):=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|
. Then
\forall\lambda>0
,
N^{p_{\infty}}(\lambda)\leq(2\lambda+3)^{d}
.

We will simplify notation slightly:
B_{\infty}(\lambda)
will denote
B_{p_{\infty}}(\lambda)
and
N^{\infty}(\lambda)
will denote
N^{p_{\infty}}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Observe that
B_{\infty}(\lambda)=[-\lambda,\lambda]^{d}
is a
d
-dimensional cube centered at the origin.

We will first assume
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
. It is then clear that

x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap B_{\infty}(\lambda)\iff x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-\lambda,\lambda]\text{ for each }1\leq i\leq d

.

As
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, this means each
x_{i}
can take one of precisely
2\lambda+1
values, i.e

x_{i}\in\{-\lambda,-\lambda+1,\ldots,0,\ldots,\lambda-1,\lambda\}

and we conclude

N^{\infty}(\lambda)=(2\lambda+1)^{d}\text{ whenever }\lambda\in\mathbb{N}

(87)

Now, let
\lambda>1
be any real number. Letting
\lfloor\lambda\rfloor
denote the greatest integer less than or equal to
\lambda
, it is clear that

N^{\infty}(\lambda)=N^{\infty}(\lfloor\lambda\rfloor)\leq N^{\infty}(\lfloor\lambda\rfloor+1)=(2\lfloor\lambda\rfloor+3)^{d}

where the last equality follows from (
87
) with
\lfloor\lambda\rfloor+1
instead of
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finally, we have

N^{\infty}(\lambda)\leq(2\lfloor\lambda\rfloor+3)^{d}\leq(2\lambda+3)^{d}

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
\lfloor\lambda\rfloor\leq\lambda
and
x\to(2x+3)^{d}
is an increasing function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
∎

With Lemma
8.2
in hand, the proof of Proposition
8.1
follows easily.

Proof of Proposition
8.1
:

As all norms are equivalent on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have positive constants
m,M
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

mp(x)\leq p_{\infty}(x)\leq Mp(x)

(88)

We make a simple observation: for any
\lambda>0

B_{p}(\lambda)\subset B_{\infty}(M\lambda)

(89)

This follows immediately from the second inequality in (
88
).

From (
89
) and the definitions of
N^{p}
and
N^{\infty}
we get that for any
\lambda>0
,

N^{p}(\lambda)\leq N^{\infty}(M\lambda)

(90)

Applying Lemma
8.2
to
N^{\infty}(M\lambda)
, we get that for any
\lambda>0

N^{\infty}(M\lambda)\leq(2M\lambda+3)^{d}

(91)

Finally, (
90
) and (
91
) combine to give that for any
\lambda>0
,

N^{p}(\lambda)\leq N^{\infty}(M\lambda)\leq(2M\lambda+3)^{d}

And that is enough to show that
N^{p}(\lambda)\in O(\lambda^{d})
as
\lambda\to\infty
∎
