Poisoning Prevention in Federated Learning and Differential Privacy via Stateful Proofs of Execution
Norrathep Rattanavipanon 1 and Ivan De Oliveira Nunes 2

Abstract
The rise in IoT-driven distributed data analytics, coupled with increasing privacy concerns, has led to a demand for effective privacy-preserving and federated data collection/model training mechanisms. In response, approaches such as Federated Learning (FL) and Local Differential Privacy (LDP) have been proposed and attracted much attention over the past few years. However, they still share the common limitation of being vulnerable to poisoning attacks wherein adversaries compromising edge devices feed forged (a.k.a. “poisoned”) data to aggregation back-ends, undermining the integrity of FL/LDP results. In this work, we propose a system-level approach to remedy this issue based on a novel security notion of Proofs of Stateful Execution ( 𝖯𝗈𝖲𝖷 \sf{PoSX} ) for IoT/embedded devices’ software. To realize the 𝖯𝗈𝖲𝖷 \sf{PoSX} concept, we design 𝖲𝖫𝖠𝖯𝖯 \sf{SLAPP} : a S ystem- L evel A pproach for P oisoning P revention. 𝖲𝖫𝖠𝖯𝖯 \sf{SLAPP} leverages commodity security features of embedded devices – in particular ARM TrustZone-M security extensions – to verifiably bind raw sensed data to their correct usage as part of FL/LDP edge device routines. As a consequence, it offers robust security guarantees against poisoning. Our evaluation, based on real-world prototypes featuring multiple cryptographic primitives and data collection schemes, showcases 𝖲𝖫𝖠𝖯𝖯 \sf{SLAPP} ’s security and low overhead.

These attacks, known as

In particular, embedded/IoT devices are highly susceptible to software exploits that potentially lead to these attacks due to their inherent lack of security mechanisms

This intensifies the risk of poisoning attacks as
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}

Existing mitigation techniques are either data-driven (e.g., in the case of data poisoning detection

In this work, we propose a systematic and practical treatment to address data poisoning at its source by leveraging architectural security features of contemporary embedded systems. This ensures that raw sensed data is correctly linked with its respective local processing, ultimately generating local aggregated data whose integrity and authenticity can be verified by back-ends in
FL/LDP-based
applications.

Specifically, we build upon the recently introduced concept of Proofs of Execution (
\sf{PoX}
)
[
13
]
for simple embedded systems.
\sf{PoX}
allows a low-end embedded device – Prover (
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
) – to convince a remote Verifier (
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
) that a specific function
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
has been executed successfully (i.e., from its first to its last instruction) on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
. Furthermore, PoX binds obtained results (or outputs) to a timely instance of this execution. A similar notion
[
40
]
has also been explored in high-end systems (e.g., general-purpose computers and servers, as opposed to embedded devices) based on trusted platform modules (TPMs)
[
68
]
.

Intuitively,
\sf{PoX}
can convey if the result received by an FL/LDP back-end (local aggregate data) truly originates from an edge device that has obtained this data through the correct execution of the expected software, thus thwarting poisoning attacks.
On the other hand, as detailed next, the adoption of
\sf{PoX}
in
FL/LDP-based mechanisms
introduces unique non-trivial challenges that require re-thinking and re-designing existing
\sf{PoX}
methods for this purpose.

I-A

On the Insufficiency of Classic
\sf{PoX}
to Avert Poisoning

\sf{PoX}
is a challenge-response protocol composed of the following steps:

1.

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
sends an authenticated request containing a cryptographic challenge (
\sf{\mathcal{C}hal}
) and asking
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
to execute
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
.

2.

\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
authenticates the request, executes
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}
obtaining output
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
, and generates a cryptographic proof
\sigma
of this execution by measuring (signing or a MAC-ing)
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
’s implementation in program memory along with received
\sf{\mathcal{C}hal}
, produced
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
, and execution metadata that conveys to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
if
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
execution was performed correctly.

3.

\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
returns the output and proof (
\sigma
,
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
) to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.

4.

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
verifies whether
\sigma
corresponds to the expected
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
code, received output
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
, and expected execution metadata. If so, it concludes that
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
has executed successfully on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
with result
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
.

Step 2 above must be securely implemented by a root of trust (RoT) within
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
to ensure (1) temporal consistency between
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
’s measurement and its execution, (2) correctness of
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
execution and generated
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
at run-time, and (3) confidentiality of the cryptographic secret used to compute
\sigma
. This RoT implementation must be unmodifiable, even when
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
’s application software is fully compromised. The latter is typically obtained through hardware support, e.g., from ARM TrustZone
[
55
]
(see Section
II-B
) or similar mechanisms.

We observe that the aforementioned
\sf{PoX}
notion has important practical limitations. It assumes
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
to be: (1) inputless, i.e.,
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
cannot depend on inputs external to
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
, and (2) stateless, i.e.,
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
must not depend on states produced by prior
\sf{PoX}
instances in
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
.
As a result, PoX is only suitable for simple self-contained programs that may process locally collected data (via
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
local I/O interfaces) but do not depend on external inputs or prior execution states. This assumption becomes problematic when attempting to apply
\sf{PoX}
to
FL/LDP-based mechanisms
.

For FL integrity,
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
should prove that a training function
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
was executed on local training dataset
D
using
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
-supplied global weights
W
and training parameters.
Moreover, no portion of
D
should be revealed to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
, requiring multiple
\sf{PoX}
instances (e.g., multiple sensing routines executed over time) to correctly produce all data points in
D
.
As detailed in Section
II-A
, similar requirements exist in
LDP algorithms
. Therefore, standard
\sf{PoX}
can not be applied in these settings.

I-B

Our Contributions

To address the aforementioned limitations, this work introduces the security notion of Proof of Stateful Execution (
\sf{PoSX}
) to enable

and

, in addition to classic
\sf{PoX}

\sf{PoX}

\sf{PoSX}

\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

\sf{PoSX}

In essence, PoSX offers assurance that execution of

\sf{\mathcal{I}}
and state

, occurred faithfully, without disclosing
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.

To realize
\sf{PoSX}
on real-world IoT settings, we design and implement

\sf{SLAPP}

This facilitates the immediate real-world implementation of

We show that

Compared to prior data-centric mitigations in FL

II-B

ARM TrustZone-M Security Extensions

ARM TrustZone-M is a hardware security extension that enables a trusted execution environment (TEE) in ARM Cortex-M MCUs commonly used in low-cost and energy-efficient IoT applications. TrustZone divides software states into two isolated worlds:
Secure
and
Non-Secure
. The Non-Secure world contains and executes (untrusted) application software while security-critical (trusted) software is stored and runs in the Secure world.

In particular, we leverage two security properties of ARM TrustZone-M in this work:

•

Hardware-enforced World Isolation.
TrustZone-M ensures complete isolation of these worlds by implementing several hardware controls (i.e., SAU/IDAU) to enforce access control to hardware resources (e.g., program and data memory, peripherals) for these two worlds.
With isolation in place, TrustZone ensures the Non-Secure World is unable to access any code and data located in the Secure World. This assures that the Secure world remains secure even if an adversary can fully modify or compromise the Non-Secure world software state.

•

Controlled Invocation.

In TrustZone-M, the only legal way for the Non-Secure world to access a function inside the Secure World is by making a call to predefined entry points. These entry points are located in a designated area within the Secure World, known as the Non-Secure Callable (NSC) region.
As a part of the Secure World, the NSC region cannot be tampered with by the Non-Secure World’s software.
As a result, this mechanism combined with TrustZone-M secure context switching enables controlled invocation of the Secure World functions, preventing attacks that aim to compromise secure functions by executing them partially, i.e., by jumping into or exiting from the middle of the function.

III
System Model and Assumptions

III-A

Network and Usage Model

We consider an IoT setting as shown earlier in Fig.
1
, consisting of two entity types: one
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
and multiple
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s.
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
is a resource-constrained sensor deployed in a physical space of interest, e.g., a smart home, office, or factory.
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
is a remote service provider that orchestrates these
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s.
As an example,
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s could be smart light bulbs that are used in many smart homes and can be controlled by the end-user through Philips
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
’s application services; or Samsung could act as
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
that provides a service for the end-user to command all SmartThings-compatible devices.

Besides offering this service,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
wishes to collect sensor data generated by
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s to further improve the service performance or enhance the user experience.
In this work,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
has the option to employ
LDP-DC
or
FL-DC
as its preferred data collection scheme, depending on the desired outcome and privacy considerations.
As poisoning attacks could sabotage the collection outcome,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
also aims to detect and prevent such attacks to safeguard the authenticity of the outcomes.

III-B

Adversary Model

We consider an
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
who can modify/compromise
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
’s application software at will.
Once compromised,
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
can access, modify, or erase any code or data in
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
unless explicitly protected by hardware-enforced access control rules.
Consequently,
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
may use this ability to perform poisoning attacks by corrupting a sensor function
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
or its execution to spoof arbitrary results sent to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.

In the context of FL, in addition to data poisoning,
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
may use this capability to launch
model
poisoning attacks that aim to compromise the global machine learning model by introducing malicious local models (i.e., gradient updates) to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.

Invasive hardware-based/physical attacks (e.g., fault-injection attacks or physical hardware manipulation) are out of scope in this work, as they require orthogonal tamper-proofing techniques
[
58
]
.

We also assume
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
has full control over the communication channel between
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
and
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.
They may perform any network-based attacks, e.g., reading, modifying, replaying, or dropping any message sent from/to
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
.

Further, we consider
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
to be adaptive
[
67
]
, i.e., it is aware of the algorithm and all the specifics of the protocol executed between
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
and
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
. As a result,
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
is allowed to modify its attack strategy by modifying
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
’s software state (except for hardware-enforced protections) and the communication between
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
and
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
to attempt to circumvent the proposed defense.

Finally, in line with the standard LDP and FL
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
models, we also consider the possibility of malicious
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.
In the latter,
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
’s goal is to learn sensitive data on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
while executing the protocol.

III-C

Device Model

\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s are small embedded/IoT devices equipped with TrustZone-M, e.g., ARM embedded devices running on Cortex-M23/33 MCUs, which are optimized for low-cost and energy efficiency.
Following the PoX assumption
[
13
]
, we assume that the function whose execution is being proven (
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
, i.e., the code implementing the data collection task according to the underlying scheme) is correct and contains no implementation bug that can lead to run-time exploits within itself. In practice,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
can employ various pre-deployment vulnerability detection techniques to fulfill this requirement
[
10
]
.

We also adhere to standard TEE-based security assumptions, i.e., we assume the small TCB implementing the
\sf{PoSX}
RoT located inside TrustZone’s Secure World is trusted and TrustZone hardware modules are implemented correctly such that
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
cannot modify this RoT implementation or violate any security guarantees implemented by the Secure World-resident code.
The latter implies the existence of secure persistent storage, exclusively accessible by the Secure World and unmodifiable when the device is offline.
This storage is used to store our TCB along with a counter-based challenge
c
and two cryptographic keys:
\sf{sk_{{\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}}}}
and
\sf{pk_{{\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}}}}
, where
\sf{sk_{{\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}}}}
corresponds to
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
private key, whose public counterpart
\sf{pk_{{\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}}}}
is known to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
. Similarly,
\sf{pk_{{\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}}}}
denotes
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
public key with its private counterpart
\sf{sk_{{\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}}}}
securely managed by
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
.
In TrustZone-M implementations, secure persistent memory is supported by a standard secure boot architecture
[
3
]
with the physical memory storing cryptographic keys being physically inaccessible through I/O interfaces (e.g., USB/J-TAG, etc.).

Finally, we assume these keys are correctly distributed to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
and
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
out-of-band, e.g., by physically fusing these keys on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
during manufacture time or using any key-provisioning mechanism
[
44
]
after
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
deployment.

IV
\sf{PoSX}
and Associated Definitions

To define
\sf{PoSX}
security goal, we start by revisiting classic
\sf{PoX}
guarantees and their limitations. Then we formulate auxiliary notions that address each limitation and in conjunction imply
\sf{PoSX}
end-to-end goal.

{definition}

[PoX Security
[
13
]
]
Let
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
represent an arbitrary software function (code) execution of which is requested by
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
, producing output
{\sf{\mathcal{O}}}
.
A protocol is considered PoX-secure
if and only if the protocol outputting
\top
implies:

(i)

{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}
code (as defined by
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
) executes atomically and completely between
{\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}}
sending a request and receiving the response, and

(ii)

\sf{\mathcal{O}}
is a direct outcome of this execution of
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}()
.

Definition
IV
states the classic
\sf{PoX}
security notion, as described in
[
13
]
.
A violation of conditions (i) or (ii) in Definition
IV
must be detectable by
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
, resulting in a protocol abort (i.e., by outputting
\bot
).
As discussed in
[
13
]
, this notion can be used to construct “sensors that cannot lie” irrespective of compromised software states.

However, it only supports self-contained IoT applications that are independent of
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
-defined inputs or pre-computed states due to two limitations:

L1 – Lack of input validation.
Definition
IV
considers
inputless

\sf{\mathcal{F}}
functions. This is because classic
\sf{PoX}
does not support verification of the origin and integrity of inputs received by
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
. This limitation is significant for applications where
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
must provide input
\sf{\mathcal{I}}
as part of
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
execution on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
(e.g., FL/LDP cases discussed in Section
II-A
).

L2 – No state preservation across
\sf{PoX}
instances.

Definition
IV
only supports
\sf{PoX}
of stateless
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
functions.
In other words,
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
must rely solely on data generated/acquired within its current execution instance and must not depend on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
states (denoted
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
) produced by prior executions.
Similar to
L1
case, a
\sf{PoX}
protocol satisfying Definition
IV
provides no guarantee or validation to the correct use of some pre-existent/expected state
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
in
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
. Thus, attacks that tamper with
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
in between subsequent
\sf{PoX}
instances may result in illegal alteration of the end result
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
.

Remark:
when
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
contains only public information,
L2
can be obviated by
L1
by making
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
a part of the authenticated output of a
\sf{PoX}
instance and used as a
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
-defined input to
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
in a subsequent
\sf{PoX}
instance. However, when
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
must remain hidden from
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
(the case of our target applications – recall Section
II-A
), consistency of
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
must be ensured locally at
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
, making
L1
and
L2
independent challenges.

To address these limitations systematically,
we introduce two new
\sf{PoX}
-related security notions.
As these notions may be of independent interest, we first present them separately and finally compose them into an end-to-end
\sf{PoSX}
goal.

{definition}

[IV-
\sf{PoX}
Security]
Let
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
represent an arbitrary software function (code) and
{\sf{\mathcal{I}}}
represent an input, both defined by
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
. Let
{\sf{\mathcal{O}}}
represent the output and
\sigma
denote a proof of
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}({\sf{\mathcal{I}}})
execution produced by
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
.
A protocol is considered IV-
\sf{PoX}
-secure
if and only if the protocol outputting
\top
implies:

(i)

{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}
executes
with input arguments
{\sf{\mathcal{I}}}
, atomically and completely between
{\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}}
sending the request and receiving
\sigma
, and

(ii)

\sf{\mathcal{O}}
is a direct outcome of this
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}({\sf{\mathcal{I}}})
execution

To overcome
L1
, we present the notion of input-validating
\sf{PoX}
(or IV-
\sf{PoX}
), as shown in Definition
IV
.
In IV-
\sf{PoX}

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
aims to execute
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
with its own provided input
\sf{\mathcal{I}}
.
Thus, the proof generated by

Thus, an IV-

{definition}

[SP-
\sf{PoX}
Security]
Let
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
represent an arbitrary software function (code) requested by
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
to run on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
with state
{\sf{\mathcal{S}}}
, where
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
was produced by some prior
\sf{PoX}
on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
but is oblivious to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
. Let
{\sf{\mathcal{O}}}
represent the output and
\sigma
denote a proof of
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}
execution produced by
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
.
A protocol is considered SP-
\sf{PoX}
-secure if and only if the protocol outputting
\top
implies:

(i)

{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}
executes atomically and completely between
{\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}}
sending the request and receiving
\sigma
with
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
state corresponding to
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
when
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
execution starts (denote this execution by
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}_{\sf{\mathcal{S}}}()
), and

(ii)

\sf{\mathcal{O}}
is a direct outcome of this
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}_{\sf{\mathcal{S}}}()
execution, and

(iii)

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
cannot infer the value of
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
beyond what is revealed by
\sf{\mathcal{O}}
, and

(iv)

\sf{\mathcal{S}}
was not modified between the current

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
-authorized
\sf{PoX}

To address

(SP-

.
Similar to IV-
\sf{PoX}

also conveys two critical aspects: (1) correct use of

\sf{\mathcal{F}}

\sf{\mathcal{F}}
and
\sf{\mathcal{S}}

In addition, it requires
\sf{\mathcal{S}}

\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
and prohibits
\sf{\mathcal{S}}

\sf{PoX}
instances.

Finally, Definition

combines IV-
\sf{PoX}
and SP-

to state the goal of
\sf{PoSX}
-Security.

{definition}

[
\sf{PoSX}
Security]

A scheme is
\sf{PoSX}
-secure
if and only if it satisfies both IV-
\sf{PoX}
(Def.

) and SP-
\sf{PoX}
(Def.
IV
) Security.

VI
\sf{PoSX}
Security Analysis

Our security argument is based on the following properties:

P1 - Request Verification.

In
\sf{SLAPP}
,
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
’s TCB always verifies freshness and authenticity of a
\sf{PoSX}
request before executing
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
and generating the proof
\sigma
. This prevents
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
from exploiting forged or replayed requests to manipulate the protocol outcome. See step 4 of Protocol
V-B
.

P2 - Input Validation.

A valid
\sigma
serves as authentication for the correct usage of
\sf{\mathcal{I}}
during
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
execution.
This prevents
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
from feeding malicious input to
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
while still succeeding in the
\sf{SLAPP}
protocol.
Since
\sf{\mathcal{I}}
is included in a
\sf{PoSX}
request, this is implied by
P1
and the fact that
\sf{\mathcal{I}}
is directly used by
\sf{SLAPP}
to invoke
{\sf{\mathcal{F}}}
in step 7 of Protocol
V-B
.

P3 - State Privacy.

\sf{SLAPP}
protects privacy of

VII
\sf{SLAPP}
Extensions and Variations

Cryptographic Choices.

Although Protocol
V-B
uses public-key cryptography,
it can seamlessly transition to symmetric cryptography by simply substituting public-key operations (i.e.,
Sign
and
Verify
) with MAC operations.
If quantum threats are in scope,
\sf{SLAPP}
can be similarly adjusted to support a post-quantum signature scheme.
We demonstrate this versatility by implementing our prototype (Section
IX
) using three distinct cryptographic choices (public-key, symmetric, and post-quantum cryptography).

Space-Time Trade-Off.

Section
V-B
discussed choices for managing
{\sf{\mathcal{S}}}_{sec}
in the Secure World: (1) storing the entire
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
value; or (2) maintaining a hash of
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
value.
The first prioritizes runtime efficiency since it requires no run-time hash computation while the second conserves storage in the Secure World by condensing the potentially large
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
into a fixed-size digest.
A third option, that eliminates storage in the Secure World, is to have
SetState
compute a MAC of
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
(instead of a hash) and pass it back to the Non-Secure World for storage. Subsequently, the Secure World can authenticate
\sf{\mathcal{S}}
received from the Normal World based on the MAC, yielding equivalent security guarantees as the previous two approaches.
By default,
\sf{SLAPP}
prototype adopts the second design choice, striking a balance between space and time overhead.

Multiple Stateful Functions.

Our description of
\sf{SLAPP}
assumes that
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
intends to obtain one
\sf{PoSX}
per
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
at a time.
Nonetheless, it can be extended to accommodate simultaneous
\sf{PoSX}
-s by maintaining a map between multiple
{\sf{\mathcal{S}}}_{sec}
-s and each ongoing
\sf{PoSX}
, in the Secure World.
To that end,
CheckState
and
SetState
should be extended to assign
{\sf{\mathcal{S}}}_{sec}=map[id]
for a given function identifier
id
.
Also, in the last step of the protocol,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
must additionally inspect
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
binary to ensure that it correctly calls
CheckState
and
SetState
with the correct function identifier.

Relaxing Atomicity Requirement.

\sf{SLAPP}
security mandates atomicity (uninterruptability) during
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
execution.
This requirement may clash with real-time needs on
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
, potentially preventing time-sensitive tasks from completing while
\sf{SLAPP}
is running.
Recent studies
[
8
,
49
]
propose techniques to relax this atomicity requirement in classic
\sf{PoX}
and related schemes. These can also be adopted in
\sf{SLAPP}
as follows:
rather than completely disabling interrupts, the Secure World “locks” (i.e., making them read-only)
PMEM
, data currently in use by the
\sf{PoSX}
task, and the Interrupt Vector Table (IVT).
It also includes IVT in the snapshot
h
before invoking
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
.
As a consequence, the
\sf{PoSX}
context is protected across interrupts.
Once
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
completes, respective memory can be unlocked.

Baseline.

\sf{\mathcal{F}}
are included as part of TCB in the Secure World.

\sf{PoSX}

\sf{SLAPP}

\sf{\mathcal{F}}

\sf{SLAPP}

This reduces available RAM for normal applications in the Non-Secure World.
Moreover, it makes the Secure World code (which should be immutable post-deployment – recall Section

Integrating Complimentary Techniques with
\sf{SLAPP}
for Additional Benefits

As mentioned in Section
I
,
\sf{SLAPP}
offers two notable advantages: (1) it allows
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
to convince
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
that execution of a function
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
happened without any input/state assumption about
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
and (2) most
\sf{SLAPP}
operations are performed on the client-side (i.e.,
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
), making it possible to combine
\sf{SLAPP}
with any server-side techniques.
In this section, we present two concrete examples that leverage these advantages to enhance
\sf{SLAPP}
benefits beyond poisoning protection in the scope of our system model.
We note that while we focus on
FL-DC
+
, the discussion in this section is also relevant to
LDP-DC
+
.

First,
FL-DC
+
builds upon FedAVG algorithm, which is shown to perform poorly with non-identically distributed (IID) data across
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s
[
76
]
.
To cope with this, several studies have extended FedAVG to better handle non-IID data.
For instance, FedProx
[
37
]
introduces a proximal term to the loss function during the local training process to help constrain local updates to be closer to the global model.
As
\sf{SLAPP}
supports
\sf{PoSX}
for arbitrary
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
,

FL-DC
+
can be adapted to incorporate FedProx by implementing the FedProx algorithm as
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
in the
Train
phase.
With this minor modification,
FL-DC
+
extends poisoning prevention to the non-IID setting.

Second,
\sf{SLAPP}
threat model focuses on software-only attacks while considering attacks that manipulate
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
hardware or the physical environment being measured by
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
(e.g. the ones considered in
[
26
,
71
]
) out of scope.
As
FL-DC
+
builds atop
\sf{SLAPP}
, it inherits the same assumption for poisoning prevention.
One common approach to address poisoning attacks in FL against hardware/physical attacks is through Byzantine-robust aggregation techniques
[
4
,
27
]
.
These techniques modify the aggregation step on
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
to make it more robust against malicious updates under the Byzantine assumptions (i.e., only a certain number of
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s can be compromised at a time), alleviating the impact from (but not completely preventing) hardware/physical
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
.
For example, the work in
[
11
]
replaces the arithmetic mean of local gradients (as used in FedAVG) with the geometric median of means during global model updates.
If hardware/physical attacks are of concern (in addition to software-based attacks),
FL-DC
+
can be adapted to support Byzantine robustness mechanisms.
In particular, after receiving all verified local models from
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s,
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
in
FL-DC
+
can update the global model via Byzantine-robust aggregation rules.
This helps mitigates poisoning attacks from Byzantine hardware/physical
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
in addition to software-only
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
. We believe the combination of both approaches to significantly strengthen overall security of these schemes.

Poisoning Prevention in LDP and FL.

We divide existing approaches to preventing poisoning attacks into 3 categories: data-driven, algorithmic and system-level.
Data-driven approaches detect poisoning attacks based solely on the collected data without modifying the underlying collection scheme, e.g., by applying normalization
[
7
]
and analyzing distances between or error rates from data
[
6
,
38
,
24
]
.
Meanwhile, algorithmic approaches modify the data collection algorithm to make it resilient against poisoning attacks, e.g., by incorporating with Byzantine fault-tolerant techniques
[
4
,
27
,
72
]
or using modified loss functions
[
25
,
32
,
62
]
.
Finally, system-level approaches
[
59
]
leverage
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
’s security architecture to mitigate poisoning attacks.
\sf{SLAPP}
falls into the last category.

Based on these categories, we qualitatively compare
\sf{SLAPP}
with current defenses in Table
III
.
It shows that
\sf{SLAPP}
is the only approach that offers strong robustness against adaptive adversaries without making assumptions about Byzantine
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
(i.e.,
\sf{SLAPP}
allows
\sf{\mathcal{A}dv}
to corrupt any number of
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s) or data distributions (i.e.,
\sf{SLAPP}
allows each
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
to have independent data distributions).
As
\sf{SLAPP}
can deterministically discern poisoned data from the benign, it results in no utility loss on aggregate data.
It also supports both LDP and FL-based data collection schemes.
As a system-level approach,
\sf{SLAPP}
requires
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

Moreover,

process for validating its correctness

Besides, to the best of our knowledge, none of existing work provides
\sf{PoSX}
-equivalent guarantee of provable integrity all the way from data acquisition until its
de facto
usage as an aggregated statistical result or global ML model. They also do not address the issues of input validation or state preservation for arbitrary functions
\sf{\mathcal{F}}
to be executed on simple
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

To secure low-end embedded devices, various
low-cost security architectures have been proposed for remote verification of their software state via integrity proofs

correct binary presence (remote attestation)

, while more expressive ones support verification of arbitrary code execution. Aside from
\sf{PoX}
architectures
[
13
,
8
,
54
,
40
]
, control flow attestation/auditing (CFA) techniques
[
49
,
1
,

,

,

]
prove to
\sf{\mathcal{V}rf}
the exact order in which instructions have executed within a particular code in
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}

[

Private Data Collection on Edge Devices.
Complementary to privacy mechanisms focusing on hiding private data from back-ends (e.g., LDP/FL), recent work has delved into assuring that private data is secure against compromised sensing devices “from its birth”, i.e., from the moment when it is digitized. VERSA
[
52
]
was proposed as a HW/SW architecture to guarantee that only the correct execution of expected and explicitly authorized software can access and manipulate sensing interfaces. As a consequence, it blocks malware/modified software from accessing sensitive sensed quantities by default. Following this notion, Sensing And Actuation As A Privilege (SA4P)
[
16
,
17

realizes this concept using ARM TrustZone.

System-level Approaches in FL.

Besides poisoning prevention, several system-level approaches have been proposed to provide different security and privacy guarantees in FL for higher-end
\sf{\mathcal{P}rv}
-s.
In terms of privacy, PPFL
[
43
]
introduces a layer-wise training method within a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) to provide confidentiality of training data while adhering to the memory constraints of the TEE. GradSec
[
42
]
improves upon this approach by significantly reducing the runtime overhead associated with the training process.
Also, other approaches such as EBFA
[
72
]
, CrowdGuard
[
59
]
and Hashemi et al.
[
28
]
propose the use of TEE to protect privacy of the training data/model while running poisoning prevention techniques outside the TEE.
For security, Pelta
[
56
]
leverages TEE to mitigate adversarial (a.k.a. evasion) attacks in FL. It securely hides critical model parameters and updates these parameters (i.e., backpropagration) inside the client-side TEE. As a result, it limits a malicious client’s access to only a partial model, making it harder to craft adversarial examples.
While sharing the similarity of leveraging TEE, these approaches do not use TEE to address data/model poisoning attacks in FL, which is one of the focal points in this work.

XII
Conclusion

We defined and developed stateful proofs of execution, a system security primitive to thwart poisoning in applications such as differential privacy and federated learning. We analyze the security of our design (
\sf{SLAPP}
) and evaluate its performance with an open-source prototype. Results indicate strong poisoning prevention guarantees at modest overhead applicable even to MCU-based resource-constrained IoT devices.
