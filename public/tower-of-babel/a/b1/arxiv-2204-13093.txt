Abstract
. We construct small amplitude gravity-capillary water waves with small nonzero vorticity, in three spatial dimensions, bifurcating from uniform flows. The waves are symmetric, and periodic in both horizontal coordinates. The proof is inspired by Lortz’ construction of magnetohydrostatic equilibria in reflection-symmetric toroidal domains [ 23 ] . It relies on a global representation of the vorticity as the cross product of two gradients, and on prescribing a functional relationship between the Bernoulli function and the orbital periods of the water particles. The presence of the free surface introduces significant new challenges. In particular, the resulting free boundary problem is not elliptic, and the involved maps incur a loss of regularity under Fréchet differentiation. Nevertheless, we show that a version of the Crandall–Rabinowitz local bifurcation method still applies in this setting, by carefully tracking the loss of regularity.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The steady water wave problem

The gravity-capillary water wave problem concerns the flow of an inviscid, incompressible fluid under the influence of gravity and surface tension. The fluid domain is bounded below by a rigid horizontal bottom
\{x_{3}=-d\}
and above by a free surface
\{x_{3}=\eta(t,{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\}
. We consider steady waves travelling with constant speed
c
along the horizontal
x_{1}
-axis. That is, waves for which the dependence on
x_{1}
and
t
is through
x_{1}-ct
alone.

Using a frame of reference moving with the wave, we can assume that the velocity field
{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})
and the surface
\eta({\bm{x}}^{\prime})
are independent of time. The fluid domain is then given by

\displaystyle\Omega^{\eta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:-d<x_{3}<\eta({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\},

with boundary
\partial\Omega^{\eta}=S^{\eta}\cup B
, where

\displaystyle S^{\eta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:x_{3}=\eta({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\}

is the top surface and

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:x_{3}=-d\}

the flat bottom. Here we use the notation
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}^{\prime},x_{3})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, while
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
.

Assuming that the fluid has constant, unit density, the motion is governed by the incompressible Euler equations

(1.1a)

\displaystyle({\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla){\bm{u}}+\nabla p+g{\bm{e}}_{3}=0,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}=0

in
\Omega^{\eta}
, where
p
is the pressure and
g>0
the constant gravitational acceleration. The corresponding kinematic boundary conditions read

(1.2a)

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $S^{\eta}$},

(1.2b)

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $B$},

where

(1.3)

{\bm{n}}[\eta]\coloneqq\frac{(-\nabla\eta,1)}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla\eta\rvert^{2}}}

is the upward-pointing unit normal on the surface. Their interpretation is simply that there is no flow through the boundary.

Finally, the dynamic boundary condition is the requirement that

p=\sigma\nabla\cdot{\bm{n}}\qquad\text{on $S^{\eta}$,}

where
\sigma\geq 0
is the coefficient of surface tension. One speaks of
pure gravity waves
when
\sigma=0
, and of
gravity-capillary waves
when
\sigma>0
. We will always assume the latter in this work, as the surface tension plays a crucial part.

We will concentrate on three-dimensional solutions, in the sense that the velocity field depends in a nontrivial way on all three spatial variables, while the surface pattern is truly two-dimensional. More specifically, we look for solutions that are
doubly periodic
; that is, they are periodic in two distinct horizontal directions. Given wavelengths
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
, introduce the dual pair of lattices
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{*}
through

\Lambda\coloneqq\lambda_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\lambda_{2}{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad\Lambda^{*}=\kappa_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\kappa_{2}{\mathbb{Z}},

where
\lambda_{i}\kappa_{i}=2\pi
. For convenience, we also let

\Omega_{0}^{\eta}\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\Omega^{\eta}:{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in(0,\lambda_{1})\times(0,\lambda_{2})\}

denote the fundamental domain. We will look for (horizontally)
\Lambda
-periodic solutions to the Euler equations. In fact, we will show a posteriori that the the solutions are actually periodic with respect to the finer diamond lattice

(1.4)

\accentset{\diamond}{\Lambda}\coloneqq\bigg\{m\frac{{\bm{\lambda}}}{2}+n\frac{{\bm{\lambda}}^{*}}{2}:m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\bigg\},

where
{\bm{\lambda}}\coloneqq(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and
{\bm{\lambda}}^{*}\coloneqq(\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2})
.

We also impose the symmetry conditions

(1.5)

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}(R_{j})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}R_{j}{\bm{u}},

\displaystyle\eta(R_{j})

\displaystyle=\eta

for
j=1,2
, where
R_{j}
denotes reflection of the
j
th coordinate. More generally, we say that a vector field
{\bm{f}}
is
(\pm)
-symmetric if it satisfies

{\bm{f}}(R_{j})=\pm(-1)^{j}R_{j}{\bm{f}},

and that a scalar field
\varphi
is
(\pm)
-symmetric if

\varphi(R_{j})=(\pm 1)^{j}\varphi.

In particular, (
1.5
) entails that we look for
(+)
-symmetric
{\bm{u}}
and
\eta
. Note that the curl operator flips the symmetry of a vector field, so that the
vorticity

{\bm{\omega}}\coloneqq\nabla\times{\bm{u}}

is
(\mp)
-symmetric if
{\bm{u}}
is
(\pm)
-symmetric. Moreover, if
\varphi
is
(-)
-symmetric, then
\nabla\varphi
is
(+)
-symmetric.

In contrast to most other studies of three-dimensional steady water waves (see e.g.
[
10
,
14
,
12
,
20
,
19
,
26
,
30
]
), we do
not
assume that the velocity field is irrotational. In fact, we explicitly seek to construct solutions with nonzero vorticity. This means, in particular, that
{\bm{u}}
is not the gradient of a velocity potential. While the two-dimensional water wave problem with vorticity has received considerable attention in recent years (see e.g.
[
7
,
9
,
29
,
32
,
16
]
and references therein), the literature on the corresponding three-dimensional problem is very scarce. Recently,
[
22
]
proved the existence of small-amplitude doubly periodic solutions under the assumption that
{\bm{u}}
is
Beltrami
; or, more specifically, that
{\bm{\omega}}=\alpha{\bm{u}}
for some constant
\alpha
. An alternative existence proof with a discussion of pure gravity waves can be found in
[
15
]
, and an extension to internal waves in
[
27
]
. Apart from this, there is a non-existence result for steady three-dimensional waves in the case of constant
{\bm{\omega}}

[
33
]
(see also
[
8
,
24
,
4
]
and references therein for related results), and some existence results for explicit Gerstner-type solutions in the setting of edge waves or geophysical fluid dynamics; see e.g.
[
6
,
5
,
17
]
and references therein. In this work, we adopt a new approach.

1.2.
Lortz’ ansatz

Our existence result is inspired by an ansatz that
[
23
]
used to construct symmetric magnetohydrostatic equilibria in toroidal domains, with fixed boundaries. The fact that this idea can be used in the context of water waves follows from the well-known correspondence between the steady Euler equations and the magnetohydrostatic equations; where one identifies the velocity field with the magnetic field, and the vorticity with the current density. For simplicity, we explain Lortz’ ansatz in the context of fluid mechanics.

By the identity

({\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla){\bm{u}}+{\bm{u}}\times{\bm{\omega}}=\nabla\bigg\lparen\frac{1}{2}\lvert{\bm{u}}\rvert^{2}\bigg\rparen

we have that (
1.1a
) is equivalent to

(1.6)

\nabla H={\bm{u}}\times{\bm{\omega}},

where

H\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert{\bm{u}}\right\rvert^{2}+p+gx_{3}

is the so-called
Bernoulli function
. Since

({\bm{u}}\times{\bm{\omega}})\cdot{\bm{u}}=({\bm{u}}\times{\bm{\omega}})\cdot{\bm{\omega}}=0,

this function is necessarily constant on both streamlines and vortex lines.

Recall also that a divergence free vector field can locally be written as the cross product of two gradients (see e.g.
[
3
]
and references therein), which in particular applies to the vorticity
{\bm{\omega}}
. Part of Lortz’ ansatz is to assume that this can be done globally, with one of the functions being the Bernoulli function.
In other words, we suppose that

(1.7)

{\bm{\omega}}=\nabla H\times\nabla\tau

for two potentials
H
and
\tau
. At this stage, we momentarily forget about (
1.6
), and suppose that
H
and
\tau
are arbitrary. However, since

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}\times{\bm{\omega}}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}\times(\nabla H\times\nabla\tau)

\displaystyle=({\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla\tau)\nabla H-({\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla H)\nabla\tau

under (
1.7
), we recover (
1.6
) if we further assume that

(1.8)

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla H

\displaystyle=0

and

(1.9)

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla\tau

\displaystyle=1,

respectively, and in turn (
1.1a
) if we let

p\coloneqq H-\frac{1}{2}\lvert{\bm{u}}\rvert^{2}-gx_{3}+Q

for some constant
Q
. It is important to note here that (
1.7
) is an implicit equation for
{\bm{u}}
, because
H
and
\tau
depend on
{\bm{u}}
through (
1.8
) and (
1.9
).

Let us now
define

\tau
through the transport equation (
1.9
), with the boundary condition

\left.\tau\right\rvert_{x_{1}=0}=0,

which uniquely determines
\tau
as long as
u_{1}
has a definite sign.
The value
\tau({\bm{x}})
has an interpretation as the time it takes to travel to
{\bm{x}}
from a point on the symmetry plane
\{x_{1}=0\}
, along the streamline passing through
{\bm{x}}
. See
Figure

1
. In turn, we use
\tau
to introduce

q\coloneqq\tau({\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\cdot$}}+\lambda_{1}{\bm{e}}_{1})-\tau,

which satisfies

{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla q=0

by periodicity of
{\bm{u}}
, and is therefore constant on the streamlines.

Figure 1
.
The figure shows a streamline of
{\bm{u}}
between the planes
x_{1}=0
and
x_{1}=\lambda_{1}
. A particle travelling along the streamline starting on the plane
x_{1}=0
at time
t=0
reaches the point
{\bm{x}}
on the streamline at time
t=\tau({\bm{x}})
. The function
q
is constant along the streamline and its value is the time it takes the particle to reach the plane
x_{1}=\lambda_{1}
.

Under the periodicity and symmetry assumptions on
{\bm{u}}
, all the streamlines are periodic, and we can interpret
q({\bm{x}})
as the orbital period of the particle at
{\bm{x}}
. That is, the time required to travel from the plane
\{x_{1}=0\}
to the plane
\{x_{1}=\lambda_{1}\}
along the streamline passing through
{\bm{x}}
. Moreover, we will see in
Proposition

3.5
that
q
is both
\Lambda
-periodic and
(+)
-symmetric. Making the further ansatz that
H\coloneqq h(q)
for some differentiable function
h
, we do indeed have

{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla H=h^{\prime}(q)({\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla q)=0,

whence (
1.8
) is automatically fulfilled.

Based on the above computations, we therefore look for solutions of

(1.10a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}(q)\nabla q\times\nabla\tau,

(1.10b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=0

in
\Omega^{\eta}
, together with the kinematic boundary conditions (
1.2a
) and (
1.2b
), the dynamic boundary condition

(1.10c)

\frac{1}{2}\lvert{\bm{u}}\rvert^{2}+g\eta+\sigma\nabla\cdot{\bm{n}}-h(q)=Q

on
S^{\eta}
, and the integral condition

(1.10d)

\fint_{\Omega_{0}^{\eta}}u_{1}\,d{\bm{x}}=c,

where
\fint
denotes the mean. The number
c
can be thought of as a wave speed, and will be used as a bifurcation parameter. In particular, we consider it part of the solutions rather than a fixed constant.

Lortz used the above ansatz, combined with a fixed point argument, to solve the magnetohydrostatic problem in a
fixed
domain. As we shall see, the fact that we have a free boundary, with the additional boundary condition (
1.10c
), introduces significant challenges to overcome.

1.3.
Trivial solutions and dispersion relation

If we
choose
the constant on the right-hand side of (
1.10c
) to be

Q=Q(c)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}c^{2}-h\bigg\lparen\frac{\lambda_{1}}{c}\bigg\rparen,

for each
c\neq 0
, then

(1.11)

(\accentset{0}{{\bm{u}}}[c],\accentset{0}{\eta})=(c{\bm{e}}_{1},0)

become trivial solutions of (
1.10
), with corresponding time functions given by

(1.12)

\accentset{0}{\tau}[c]=\frac{x_{1}}{c},\qquad\accentset{0}{q}[c]=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{c}.

If we linearise the problem around such a trivial solution, and look for solutions of the form
\eta({\bm{x}}^{\prime})=e^{i{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\cdot{\bm{k}}}
for
{\bm{k}}\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
, we will obtain the linear dispersion relation

(1.13)

\ell_{{\bm{k}}}(c)\coloneqq g+\sigma\lvert{\bm{k}}\rvert^{2}-c^{2}k_{1}^{2}{\mathring{{\mathfrak{f}}}}_{\lvert{\bm{k}}\rvert}=0,\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathring{{\mathfrak{f}}}}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{\coth(kd)}{k},

which is the same as the one for the irrotational water-wave problem (see
Section

4.1
). Note that solutions to this equation always come in quadruples when
k_{2}\neq 0
, due to symmetry: If
{\bm{k}}
is a solution, then so are
-{\bm{k}}
and
\pm{\bm{k}}^{*}
.

The multiplicity of solutions to (
1.13
) has been studied by many authors, including
[
26
]
, who gave a detailed analysis. For completeness, and since we are using different parameters, we include a result similar to theirs. Its proof is deferred to
Section

4.1
.

Proposition 1.1
.

There exists a cocountable set
\Sigma({\bm{\kappa}},g,d)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
such that
\pm{\bm{\kappa}}
and
\pm{\bm{\kappa}}^{*}
are the only solutions to (
1.13
) when

c=c^{*}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\bigg\lparen\frac{g+\sigma\lvert{\bm{\kappa}}\rvert^{2}}{{\mathring{{\mathfrak{f}}}}_{\lvert{\bm{\kappa}}\rvert}}\bigg\rparen^{1/2}

and
\sigma\in\Sigma
. Here
{\bm{\kappa}}=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
.

1.4.
Main result

In order to state our main result, we first introduce some notation: When dealing with a mapping
T
from some vector space
X
(of any dimension) into a function space
Y
, we will typically use square brackets to denote the argument
x\in X
, so that
T[x]
denotes a function in
Y
. We will refer to the space of all bounded
k
-linear maps from
X^{k}
to
Y
as
{\mathcal{L}}^{k}(X,Y)
, with
{\mathcal{L}}^{0}(X,Y)
interpreted as simply
Y
.

For an open, non-empty set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\coloneqq{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, we let
C^{k}({\overline{\Omega}})
denote the class of functions
\map{f}{{\overline{\Omega}}}{{\mathbb{R}}}
that are
k
times continuously differentiable in
\Omega
, and whose derivatives are bounded and extend continuously to
{\overline{\Omega}}
. This is a Banach space when equipped with the usual norm

\lVert f\rVert_{k}\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq k\\
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega\end{subarray}}{\lVert D^{j}f({\bm{x}})\rVert_{{\mathcal{L}}^{j}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}}.

In addition, when
r\in(0,1)
, we let
C^{k+r}({\overline{\Omega}})
be the class of
f\in C^{k}({\overline{\Omega}})
whose derivative of order
k
is
r
-Hölder. This is again a Banach space under the norm

\lVert f\rVert_{k+r}\coloneqq\lVert f\rVert_{k}+\sup_{{\bm{x}}\neq{\bm{y}}\in\Omega}{\frac{\lVert D^{k}f({\bm{x}})-D^{k}f({\bm{y}})\rVert}{\lvert{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rvert^{r}}}.

We also use the notation
C^{s}_{\textnormal{loc}}({\overline{\Omega}})
for functions that belong to
C^{s}({\overline{U}})
for every bounded open
U\subset\Omega
, along with
C^{s}({\overline{\Omega}};{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
(or its local version) for vector fields.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
c=c^{*}(\sigma)
, so that
\pm{\bm{\kappa}}
and
\pm{\bm{\kappa}}^{*}
are the only solutions of the dispersion equation (
1.13
), and fix a non-integer
s>4
. Then, for every sufficiently small
h\in C^{s}({\mathbb{R}})
, there exists a family of nontrivial,
\accentset{\diamond}{\Lambda}
-periodic,
(+)
-symmetric solutions

({\bm{u}}[t],\eta[t],c[t])\quad\in\quad C^{s}\big\lparen{\overline{\Omega^{\eta[t]}}};{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\big\rparen\times C^{s+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\times{\mathbb{R}}

for
0<\lvert t\rvert\ll 1
, to the problem (
1.2
), (
1.10
), bifurcating from the trivial solution
(\accentset{0}{{\bm{u}}}[c^{*}],0,c^{*})
at
t=0
. Moreover,

\eta[t]=\accentset{1}{\eta}t+o(t),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\accentset{1}{\eta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\coloneqq\cos(\kappa_{1}x_{1})\cos(\kappa_{2}x_{2}),

in
C^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, and the solutions are rotational if
h^{\prime}(\accentset{0}{q}[c^{*}])\neq 0
.

Remark 1.3
.

From now on,
s
will always refer to a non-integer in this paper.

A more precise statement of the existence part of
Theorem

1.2
, with regularity properties of the solution branch with respect to the parameter
t
, is provided in
Theorem

4.5
. There are two complications regarding this dependence: The first is that the domain
\Omega^{\eta[t]}
, in which
{\bm{u}}[t]
is defined, depends on the parameter
t
. This is of course standard in free boundary problems, and is solved by considering a ‘flattened’ version of the problem (see
Section

2
). The second is that, contrary to most studies of steady water waves, regularity with respect to
t
comes at the price of losing regularity in
{\bm{x}}
. This phenomenon can be seen already in the expansion of the surface in
Theorem

1.2
, which is valid in
C^{s}
rather than the expected
C^{s+1}
. The loss is related to the ‘elliptic-hyperbolic’ nature of our problem, whereas most other versions of the steady water wave problem involve elliptic free boundary problems. In particular, there is a loss of derivatives in the dependence of the function
\tau
on the vector field
{\bm{u}}
(see
Section

3.2
).

A somewhat similar phenomenon occurs in the irrotational three-dimensional pure gravity-wave problem
[
20
,
19
]
. However, there the problem comes from the dynamic boundary condition, rather than the equations in the interior. Fortunately, our situation is more favourable; in the sense that we can solve the problem by an adaptation of the Crandall–Rabinowitz local bifurcation theorem, instead of needing to resort to a Nash–Moser scheme. This is accomplished with the implicit function and Banach fixed-point theorems, combined with carefully keeping track of the differentiability properties of fixed points. We need to do this when the involved mappings are only differentiable with a loss of regularity; see
Appendices

A
and
B
. The results in these appendices, and their proofs, draw inspiration from similar earlier results on scales of Banach spaces
[
31
,
18
]
.

The fact that the resulting solutions actually have nonzero vorticity is not completely obvious from the construction. We prove this after the fact in
Proposition

4.6
, by explicitly expanding the solution curve in terms of the bifurcation parameter
t
.

1.5.
Previous results

We conclude this introduction with some historical remarks about doubly periodic steady water waves. The first rigorous construction of such waves in the irrotational setting is due to
[
26
]
, who considered periodic lattices of the same type as in (
1.4
). Later,
[
30
]
proved a similar result under the influence of pressure forcing. Waves with an arbitrary fundamental domain were constructed by
[
10
]
, using a variational Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction and topological methods. Similar results can also be obtained using the spatial-dynamics method, in which the equations of motion are seen as an infinite-dimensional dynamical system where one of the horizontal spatial directions plays the role of time. Symmetric diamond waves were constructed by
[
14
]
, while
[
13
]
considered arbitrary fundamental domains (see also
[
25
]
and
[
1
]
for related results in other physical settings). As previously mentioned, the case of pure gravity waves is much more challenging, due to the appearance of small divisors. Nevertheless, existence theories for both symmetric and asymmetric doubly periodic waves have been established by
[
20
,
19
]
.

Turning to water waves with vorticity, the result that is most closely related to
Theorem

1.2
is the construction of doubly periodic gravity-capillary waves on Beltrami flows by
[
22
]
(see also the recent alternative proof and discussion of pure gravity waves by
[
15
]
and the extension to internal waves by
[
27
]
). Note that the interior equations are elliptic in the Beltrami setting, and therefore the problem can be solved by more standard bifurcation methods, like in the irrotational gravity-capillary problem. However, since the class of Beltrami fields is not invariant under the reflections
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, it does not make sense to look for
(+)
-symmetric solutions there. This was handled in
[
22
]
by using a multi-parameter bifurcation approach. The symmetry property is, on the other hand, a key part of our approach here. It is not even clear how to generalise Lortz’ ansatz to a form that admits asymmetric solutions. The same symmetries
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are also used in
[
26
]
in the reduction to a one-dimensional bifurcation problem.

2.
Reformulation of the problem using a flattening transformation

If we introduce the flattening transform
\map{\Pi}{\Omega^{0}}{\Omega^{\eta}}
through

\Pi({\overline{\bm{x}}})={\overline{\bm{x}}}+\pi({\overline{\bm{x}}}){\bm{e}}_{3},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\pi({\overline{\bm{x}}})=\eta({\overline{\bm{x}}}^{\prime})\Big\lparen 1+\frac{{\overline{x}}_{3}}{d}\Big\rparen,

then

(2.1)

J\coloneqq D\Pi=\id_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}+{\bm{e}}_{3}\otimes\nabla\pi,

with

(2.2)

\nabla\pi({\overline{\bm{x}}})=\bigg\lparen\nabla\eta({\overline{\bm{x}}}^{\prime})\Big\lparen 1+\frac{{\overline{x}}_{3}}{d}\Big\rparen,\frac{\eta({\overline{\bm{x}}}^{\prime})}{d}\bigg\rparen.

Consequently,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\coloneqq\det(J)=1+{\bm{e}}_{3}\cdot\nabla\pi

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\eta}{d},

and

J^{-1}=\id_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}-\frac{1}{\rho}{\bm{e}}_{3}\otimes\nabla\pi.

Given any scalar field
\varphi
on
\Omega^{\eta}
, we define the corresponding
flattened
scalar field
{\overline{\varphi}}
on
\Omega^{0}
through

{\overline{\varphi}}=\varphi(\Pi)(=\varphi\circ\Pi),

while for any vector field
{\bm{f}}
on
\Omega^{\eta}
, we define the flattened vector field
{\overline{\bm{f}}}
by

(2.4)

J{\overline{\bm{f}}}=\rho{\bm{f}}(\Pi)

instead. The next lemma describes the effect that this flattening has on the differential operators appearing in the Euler equations. Its proof is standard, and is thus omitted.

Lemma 2.1
.

The gradient of a flattened scalar field is given by
1
1

1

We adopt the convention that
\nabla\varphi=(D\varphi)^{\top}
is a column vector.

(2.5)

\displaystyle\nabla{\overline{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}J^{\top}J{\overline{\nabla\varphi}},

while

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\overline{\bm{f}}}

\displaystyle=\rho{\overline{\nabla\cdot{\bm{f}}}}

and

(2.6)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\Big\lparen\frac{1}{\rho}J^{\top}J{\overline{\bm{f}}}\Big\rparen

\displaystyle={\overline{\nabla\times{\bm{f}}}}

for vector fields. In particular, the property of being divergence free is conserved under flattening.

Since

\displaystyle J{\overline{\nabla q\times\nabla\tau}}

\displaystyle=\rho(\nabla q\times\nabla\tau)(\Pi)

3.1.
Div-curl problem

For all

Proposition 3.1
.

The map

\map{C}{{\mathcal{V}}_{\epsilon}^{s+1}}{{\mathcal{L}}\big\lparen{\mathring{{\mathbb{X}}}}_{+}^{s};{\mathbb{X}}^{s-1}_{-}\times{\mathbb{R}}\big\rparen}

is analytic, and

is an isomorphism for every

when

Proof.

By continuity with respect to
\eta
, it is sufficient to show that
C[0]
is an isomorphism. To that end, suppose first that
C[0]{\bm{f}}=0
for some
{\bm{f}}\in{\mathring{{\mathbb{X}}}}_{+}^{s}
. Then
{\bm{f}}
satisfies

\begin{aligned} \nabla\times{\bm{f}}&=0\\
\nabla\cdot{\bm{f}}&=0\end{aligned}\quad\text{in $\Omega^{0}$,}\qquad f_{3}=0\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega^{0}$,}\quad\text{and}\quad\fint_{\Omega_{0}^{0}}f_{1}\,d{\bm{x}}=0,

where taking the curl implies
\Delta{\bm{f}}=0
. In particular, we must have

by uniqueness of the solution of the homogeneous Dirichlet problem. In turn,

\nabla\times{\bm{f}}=\big\lparen-\partial_{3}f_{2},\partial_{3}f_{1},\partial_{1}f_{2}-\partial_{2}f_{1}\big\rparen=0

shows that
f_{1},f_{2}
are independent of

. They are therefore bounded harmonic functions on

, and thus constant by Liouville’s theorem. This constant vanishes by symmetry and the integral condition, whence
{\bm{f}}=0
, and

is injective.

For surjectivity, suppose that
{\bm{g}}\in{\mathbb{X}}^{s-1}_{-}
and
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Standard theory for second order linear elliptic equations yields the existence of
\Lambda
-periodic and
(-)
-symmetric

such that

\Delta{\bm{h}}={\bm{g}}\quad\text{in $\Omega^{0}$}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{1}=h_{2}=\partial_{3}h_{3}=0\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega^{0}$,}

and it is straightforward to check that

{\bm{f}}\coloneqq c{\bm{e}}_{1}-\nabla\times{\bm{h}}\in{\mathring{{\mathbb{X}}}}_{+}^{s}

satisfies
C[0]{\bm{f}}=({\bm{g}},c)
.
∎

Due to
Proposition

3.1
, it makes sense to define

and

by

\displaystyle\coloneqq C[\eta]^{-1}(0,1)

when

The derivative of

4.5.
Bernoulli surfaces

The level surfaces of the Bernoulli function
H
, known as Bernoulli surfaces, are interesting in view of Arnold’s structure theorem
[
2
, Ch. II, §1]
. This theorem roughly says that the Bernoulli surfaces are either invariant tori or diffeomorphic to the annulus
{\mathbb{R}}\times S^{1}
. The latter happens when a surface touches the boundary. In our case, everything has to be understood by taking the quotient
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda
. In general, the streamlines on the annuli are closed (or rather periodic in our setting), while those on the tori are either all closed or dense in the surface. However, the latter cannot happen in our case due to the symmetry assumptions, which guarantee periodicity of the streamlines.

The main conclusion of this section is that for the fluid flows constructed in this paper, both tori and annuli appear as Bernoulli surfaces. This is different from two-dimensional water waves with vorticity, where the surface and bottom are always Bernoulli surfaces, and all other Bernoulli surfaces are tori strictly contained in the interior of the fluid domain.

In this section, we always work under the assumption
h^{\prime}(\accentset{0}{q}[c^{*}])\neq 0
. Due to the relationship between

and the Bernoulli function
H
, the Bernoulli surfaces are then also level surfaces of

. By combining (
4.11
) and (
4.14

, where we recall that
{\mathfrak{s}}>4
. In view of (
