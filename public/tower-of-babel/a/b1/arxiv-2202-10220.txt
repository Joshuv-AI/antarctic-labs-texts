Maximum principle for time-fractional parabolic equations with a reaction coefficient of arbitrary sign
Natalia Kopteva

Abstract
We consider time-fractional parabolic equations with a Caputo time derivative of order α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) . For such equations, we give an elementary proof of the weak maximum principle under no assumptions on the sign of the reaction coefficient. This proof is also extended for weak solutions, as well as for various types of boundary conditions and variable-coefficient variable-order multiterm time-fractional parabolic equations.



1
Introduction

Proofs of various versions of the maximum principle for classical elliptic and
parabolic equations can be found in many textbooks on PDEs and their applications
[
3
,
4
,
10
,
12
]
.
In
the parabolic case, the presence of the reaction term
cu
with
c
of arbitrary sign is usually addressed
by a simple solution substitution
u=e^{\mu t}\hat{u}
with a sufficiently large constant
\mu
.

For time-fractional parabolic equations,
the weak maximum principle
was proved by Luchko
[
7
]

in 2009
assuming a nonnegative coefficient
c
in an equation of the form (
1.1
) below.
However, it was not until 2017 that
this restriction on the sign of
c
was removed
by Luchko and Yamamoto
[
8
]
.
It is also worth noting that the authors,
who are unquestionably leading experts in the area,
concluded that the substitution
u=e^{\mu t}\hat{u}
does not work in the time-fractional case,
and thus
they devised an alternative new proof method.
The latter method relies on eigenfunction expansions and a fixed point theorem,
so naturally applies to weak solutions, but, apart from being quite intricate,
it is limited to self-adjoint spatial operators with time-independent coefficients.

The purpose of this paper is to give an elementary proof of the weak maximum principle
for strong solutions
of time-fractional parabolic equations with
c
of arbitrary sign.
This new proof, surprisingly, combines the fractional-derivative representation used by Luchko in
[
7
]

(see (
2.2
)) with the substitution
u=e^{\mu t}\hat{u}
.
We shall also extend this proof for
weak solutions in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}H^{1}(\Omega)
, as well as for
various types of boundary conditions and variable-coefficient variable-order
multiterm time-fractional parabolic equations.

We shall consider fractional-order parabolic equations, of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
, of type

D_{t}^{\alpha}u+{\mathcal{L}}u+c(x,t)\,u=f(x,t),\quad\mbox{where}\quad c(x,t)\geq-\lambda,

(1.1)

for
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T]
,
subject to the initial condition
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}
in
\Omega
, and the boundary
condition
\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}u=g
on
\partial\Omega
for
t>0
.
This problem is posed in a bounded Lipschitz domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(where
d\in\{1,2,3\}
), and involves
a spatial linear second-order elliptic operator
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}(t)
of the form

{\mathcal{L}}u:=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\!\bigl(a_{ij}(x,t)\,\partial_{x_{j}}\!u\bigr)+\sum_{i}^{d}b_{i}(x,t)\,\partial_{x_{i}}\!u,

(1.2)

with a symmetric positive definite coefficient matrix
\{a_{ij}(x,t)\}_{i,j=1}^{d}

\forall(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T]
.
The Caputo fractional derivative in time, denoted by
D_{t}^{\alpha}
, is defined
[
2
]
,
for
t>0
, by

D_{t}^{\alpha}u:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\,\partial_{s}v(\cdot,s)\,ds,

(1.3)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function, and
\partial_{t}
denotes the partial/ordinary derivative in
t
.

Note that maximum principles for time-fractional parabolic equations
of type (
1.1
), with a reaction coefficient of arbitrary sign,
can be immediately applied to
extend
the new a posteriori error estimation methodology
[
5
,
6
]
to the semilinear case.
It is also worth mentioning that analogous results have been used to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions of classical semilinear parabolic equations
[
9
,
12
]
, a direction still to be fully explored in the context of fractional parabolic equations.

Notation.
We use the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the norm
\|\cdot\|

in the space
L_{2}(\Omega)
, as well as the standard spaces
L_{\infty}(\Omega)
,
H^{1}(\Omega)=W^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

W^{1,\infty}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime};\,L_{\infty}(\Omega))
,  and
C([0,T];L_{\infty}(\Omega))

(see
[
3
, Sec. 5.9.2]
for the notation used for functions of
x
and
t
).
The notation
v^{+}:=\max\{0,\,v\}
is used for the positive part of a generic function
v
.

2
Maximum principle for strong solutions

The key role in our analysis will be played by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any fixed
\lambda
and
\mu
such that

0\leq\lambda\leq\mu^{\alpha}
,
let
v\in C[0,T]\cap W^{1,\infty}(\epsilon,t)
for any
0<\epsilon<t\leq T
,

v(0)\leq 0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t_{0})-\lambda v(t_{0})\leq 0\quad\mbox{for~some}\;\;t_{0}\in(0,T].

(2.1)

Then the function
\hat{v}(t):=e^{-\mu t}v(t)
cannot attain a positive

\max_{[0,t_{0}]}\!\hat{v}\,

at
t=t_{0}
.

Remark 2.2
.

One important feature of
the above lemma
is that the differential inequality in (
2.1
) is assumed at a single point
t_{0}>0
.
If it were true
\forall\,t_{0}>0
, then the result
of Lemma
2.1
(and, in fact, that
v(t)\leq 0

\forall\,t>0
)
would follow from an explicit solution representation for
D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)-\lambda v=w

(see, e.g.,
[
2
,
11
]
and a discussion in
[
5
, Remark 2.1]
).

Proof.

In view of (
1.3
), replacing
\partial_{s}v(s)
in
D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)
by
\partial_{s}\{v(s)-v(t)\}
and then integrating by parts, one gets
(see also
[
7
,
1
]
)

\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)=t^{-\alpha}\{v(t)-v(0)\}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}\,\{v(t)-v(s)\}\,ds.

(2.2)

Next, dividing both parts by
e^{\mu t}
and using
v(t)=e^{\mu t}\,\hat{v}(t)
yields

\displaystyle e^{-\mu t}\Bigl(\Gamma(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle\,D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)+t^{-\alpha}v(0)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=t^{-\alpha}\hat{v}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\!\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}\,\{\hat{v}(t)-e^{-\mu(t-s)}\hat{v}(s)\}\,ds

\displaystyle=\bigl[t^{-\alpha}+\Lambda(t)\bigr]\,\hat{v}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\!\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}\,e^{-\mu(t-s)}\{\hat{v}(t)-\hat{v}(s)\}\,ds,

(2.3)

where

\Lambda(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\!\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}\,\{1-e^{-\mu(t-s)}\}\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\!\alpha s^{-\alpha-1}\,\{1-e^{-\mu s}\}\,ds.

It will be helpful to rewrite (
2.3
) using the notation
\Lambda_{\infty}:=\Lambda(\infty)
and

\omega(t):=\int_{t}^{\infty}\!\!\alpha s^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\mu s}ds.

(2.4)

With this new notation, a simple calculation shows that
\Lambda_{\infty}-\Lambda(t)=t^{-\alpha}-\omega(t)
,
so

t^{-\alpha}+\Lambda(t)=\omega(t)+\Lambda_{\infty}
, so (
2.3
) becomes

e^{-\mu t}\Bigl(\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)+t^{-\alpha}v(0)\Bigr)=\bigl[\omega(t)+\Lambda_{\infty}\bigr]\,\hat{v}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\!\partial_{s}\omega(t-s)\,\{\hat{v}(t)-\hat{v}(s)\}\,ds.

(2.5)

Note that here
\omega(t)>0
, while
\partial_{s}\omega(t-s)>0
(in view of
\omega^{\prime}(t)<0
).
Also, for
\Lambda_{\infty}
, using
\hat{s}:=\mu s
and then integrating by parts, one gets

\Lambda_{\infty}=\mu^{\alpha}\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!{\hat{s}}^{-\alpha-1}\,\{1-e^{-\hat{s}}\}\,d\hat{s}=\mu^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!{s}^{-\alpha}\,e^{-s}ds=\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\mu^{\alpha}.

(2.6)

To complete the proof, set
t:=t_{0}
in (
2.5
),
then subtract
e^{-\mu t_{0}}\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\lambda v(t_{0})=\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\lambda\hat{v}(t_{0})
,
and, finally, recall (
2.1
), which yields

0\geq\underbrace{\bigl[\omega(t_{0})+\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,(\mu^{\alpha}-\lambda)\bigr]}_{\geq\omega(t_{0})>0}\,\hat{v}(t_{0})+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\!\underbrace{\partial_{s}\omega(t_{0}-s)}_{>0}\,\{\hat{v}(t_{0})-\hat{v}(s)\}\,ds.

(2.7)

Now, assuming that
\max_{[0,t_{0}]}\hat{v}=\hat{v}(t_{0})>0

immediately leads to a contradiction.
∎

Now we are prepared to prove the weak maximum principle for strong solutions of (
1.1
)–(
1.3
)
with the coefficients

\{a_{ij}\}
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
, and
\{b_{i}\}
and
c
in
C(\Omega)
.

Theorem 2.3

(maximum/comparison principle)
.

Let
c(x,t)\geq-\lambda

\forall\,(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T]
with some constant
\lambda\geq 0
.

Suppose that

u(x,0)\leq 0

\forall\,x\in\Omega
and
u(x,t)\leq 0

\forall\,(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
,
and also
u\in C(\bar{\Omega}\times[0,T])
,
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{2}(\Omega)
for any
t>0
,
and
u(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,\infty}(\epsilon,t)
for any
x\in\Omega
and
0<\epsilon<t\leq T
.
Then
(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c(x,t))u\leq 0
in
\Omega\times(0,T]
implies
u\leq 0
in
\bar{\Omega}\times[0,T]
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
u
cannot take positive values at any point in
\Omega\times(0,T]
.
Let
\hat{u}:=e^{-\mu t}u
, where the constant

\mu^{\alpha}\geq\lambda\geq 0
.
Suppose that
\max_{\bar{\Omega}\times[0,T]}\hat{u}=\hat{u}(x^{*},t^{*})>0
for some
x^{*}\in\Omega
and
t^{*}\in(0,T]
.
Then a standard argument using the positive definiteness of the coefficient matrix
\{a_{ij}\}

shows that
{\mathcal{L}}\hat{u}(x^{*},t^{*})\geq 0

(see, e.g.,
[
4
, proof of Theorem 3.1]
,
[
10
, Chap. 2, Sec. 2]
). Hence,
{\mathcal{L}}u(x^{*},t^{*})\geq 0
, so

(D_{t}^{\alpha}+c(x^{*},t^{*}))(x^{*},t^{*})\leq 0
, and so
(D_{t}^{\alpha}-\lambda)u(x^{*},t^{*})\leq 0
.
Combining the latter with
u(x^{*},0)\leq 0
,
by
Lemma
2.1
, one concludes that
\hat{u}(x^{*},t)
cannot attain a positive maximum on
[0,t^{*}]
at
t=t^{*}
,
which yields a contradiction.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The representation (
2.5
) for
D_{t}^{\alpha}v
, may be considered a version of (
2.2
) written in terms of
\hat{v}
and using the kernel
\omega
from (
2.4
)
and
\Lambda_{\infty}=\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\mu^{\alpha}
from (
2.6
).
Note that setting
\mu:=0
yields
\omega(t)=t^{-\alpha}
and
\Lambda_{\infty}=0
, so in this case (
2.5
) is identical
with (
2.2
).
On the other hand, an integration by parts in (
2.5
) yields

e^{-\mu t}\,\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)=\bigl[\Lambda_{\infty}\,\hat{v}(t)-\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\infty}(t)\,\hat{v}(0)\bigr]+\int_{0}^{t}\!\omega(t-s)\,\partial_{s}\hat{v}(s)\,ds.

(2.8)

Here we also used
v(0)=\hat{v}(0)
and

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\infty}(t):=t^{-\alpha}e^{-\mu t}-\omega(t)=e^{-\mu t}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\alpha(s+t)^{-\alpha-1}\,\{1-e^{-\mu s}\}\,ds\leq\Lambda_{\infty},\quad\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\infty}(0)=\Lambda_{\infty}.

Likewise, (
2.8
) may be considered a version of (
1.3
) written in terms of
\hat{v}
.

3
Maximum principle for weak solutions

In this section, we shall extend the above maximum principle to the case of weak solutions in
W^{2,1}(\Omega)=H^{1}(\Omega)
.
For this purpose, we borrow some definitions and general ideas from
[
4
, Chap. 8]

(where they are used to establish the weak maximum principle for functions
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
in the context of elliptic equations).

Throughout this section, it will be assumed for the elliptic operator
{\mathcal{L}}
from (
1.2
) that its coefficients are in
L_{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
,
and that

C_{{\mathcal{L}}}:=\sup_{\Omega\times(0,T)}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}^{2}}{4\lambda_{a}}<\infty

(3.1)

where
\lambda_{a}=\lambda_{a}(x,t)>0
is the minimal eigenvalue of the
symmetric positive definite
coefficient matrix
\{a_{ij}(x,t)\}
.

Now,
u(\cdot,t)\in\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}H^{1}(\Omega)
is said to satisfy (
1.1
) in a weak sense if

\langle(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c)u,\,\chi\rangle=\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}\langle f,\,\chi\rangle

\forall\chi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\forall t\in(0,T]
.
Similarly,
u
is said to satisfy

(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c)u\leq 0

(\geq 0)
in a weak sense if
\langle(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c)u,\,\chi\rangle\leq 0

(\geq 0)

for all nonnegative
\chi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Additionally,
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is said to satisfy
w\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega

if
w^{+}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 3.1

(maximum principle for weak solutions)
.

Let
\inf_{\Omega\times(0,T)}c\geq-\lambda
for some constant
\lambda\geq 0
.
Suppose that

u(\cdot,0)\leq 0
in
\Omega
and
u(\cdot,t)\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
for any

t\in[0,T]
,

and also

u
is in

C([0,T];L_{\infty}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,\infty}(\epsilon,t;\,L_{\infty}(\Omega))
for any
0<\epsilon<t\leq T
,
while

u(\cdot,t)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
for any
t>0
.
Then
(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}-c)u\leq 0
, understood in a weak sense, implies

\sup_{\Omega}u(\cdot,t)\leq 0

\forall\,t\in[0,T]
.

To prove the above theorem we shall employ the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
w\in L_{\infty}(\Omega)\cap\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}H^{1}(\Omega)

and

w\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, and, additionally,

0<M^{\prime}<\sup_{\Omega}w\leq M
for some constants
M^{\prime}
and
M
.
Then for the function
\chi_{0}:=(w-M^{\prime})^{+}
,
one has

\langle{\mathcal{L}}w,\chi_{0}\rangle\geq-C_{{\mathcal{L}}}(M-M^{\prime})\,\langle 1,\chi_{0}\rangle,

(3.2)

where

{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}(t)
for any fixed
t\in(0,T]
and

C_{{\mathcal{L}}}
is from (
3.1
).

Proof.

First, note that

\chi_{0}=\max\{0,\,w-M^{\prime}\}
is in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

(in view of
w\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
)

and has a nonempty support
\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega
.
Also,
\chi_{0}
takes values in
[0,M-M^{\prime}]
, while

\partial_{x_{i}}\chi_{0}=\partial_{x_{i}}\!w

\forall i
in
\Omega_{0}

(see also a more detailed discussion in
[
4
, Sec. 8.1]
).

Now, applying an integration by parts
to the first sum in
\langle{\mathcal{L}}w,\chi_{0}\rangle
,
and then using
the positive definiteness of the coefficient matrix
\{a_{ij}\}
and (
3.1
), yields

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{L}}w,\chi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\!\!a_{ij}\,(\partial_{x_{j}}\!w)\,(\partial_{x_{i}}\chi_{0})+\sum_{i}^{d}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\!\!(b_{i}\,\partial_{x_{i}}\!w)\,\chi_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega_{0}}\!\!\lambda_{a}|\nabla w|^{2}-\int_{\Omega_{0}}\!\!2(\lambda_{a}C_{{\mathcal{L}}})^{1/2}|\nabla w|\,|\chi_{0}|

\displaystyle\geq-C_{{\mathcal{L}}}\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}.

(3.3)

It remains to note that
\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}=\langle\chi_{0},\chi_{0}\rangle\leq(M-M^{\prime})\,\langle 1,\chi_{0}\rangle
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\hat{u}:=e^{-\mu t}u
, where the constant

\mu^{\alpha}\geq\lambda\geq 0
.
As

u\in C([0,T];L_{\infty}(\Omega))
, so

S(t):=\sup_{\Omega}\hat{u}(\cdot,t)
is in
C[0,T]
, with
S(0)\leq 0
,
and it suffices to prove that
S(t)\leq 0

\forall t
.

Suppose that
\max_{t\in[0,T]}S(t)=S(t_{0})=\sup_{\Omega}\hat{u}(\cdot,t_{0})=M>0
for some
t_{0}\in(0,T]
.
Next, for any arbitrarily small
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}M)
, set
M^{\prime}:=M-\varepsilon>0

and
consider

\chi_{0}:=(\hat{u}(\cdot,t_{0})-M^{\prime})^{+}
, which has a non-empty support
\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
one gets (
3.2
) with
w:=\hat{u}(\cdot,t_{0})
and
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}(t_{0})
.
So for
\chi:=\chi_{0}/\langle 1,\chi_{0}\rangle
, one gets

\langle{\mathcal{L}}w,\chi\rangle\geq-C_{{\mathcal{L}}}(M-M^{\prime})
, or, equivalently,

\langle{\mathcal{L}}(t_{0})\hat{u}(\cdot,t_{0}),\chi\rangle\geq-\varepsilon C_{{\mathcal{L}}}
.
Also,

(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c)u\leq 0
implies

\langle(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}(t_{0})+c(\cdot,t_{0}))u(\cdot,t_{0}),\,\chi\rangle\leq 0

for the nonnegative
\chi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
As
\hat{u}(\cdot,t_{0})
is nonnegative in
\Omega_{0}
, so is
u(\cdot,t_{0})
,
so

\langle c(\cdot,t_{0})\,u(\cdot,t_{0}),\,\chi\rangle\geq-\lambda\langle u(\cdot,t_{0}),\,\chi\rangle
.
Combining these observations,
one concludes that
\langle D_{t}^{\alpha}u(\cdot,t_{0}),\chi\rangle-e^{\mu t_{0}}\,\varepsilon C_{{\mathcal{L}}}-\lambda\langle u(\cdot,t_{0}),\chi\rangle\leq 0.

Hence, setting
v(t)=\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}u}(\cdot,t),\chi\rangle
and
\hat{v}(t):=\langle\hat{u}(\cdot,t),\chi\rangle=e^{-\mu t}\,v(t)

yields

D_{t}^{\alpha}v(t_{0})-\lambda v(t_{0})\leq e^{\mu t_{0}}\,\varepsilon C_{{\mathcal{L}}},\qquad\quad v(0)\leq 0.

(3.4)

Note also that both
v
and
\hat{v}
are in
C[0,T]\cap W^{1,\infty}(\frac{1}{2}t_{0},t_{0})
.
Additionally, in view of
\hat{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\geq M^{\prime}=M-\varepsilon
in
\Omega_{0}
and
\langle 1,\chi\rangle=1
,
one gets

\hat{v}(t_{0})\geq M-\varepsilon,\quad\{\hat{v}(t_{0})-\hat{v}(s)\}\geq-\varepsilon\;\;\forall\,s,\quad\{t_{0}-s\}^{-1}|\hat{v}(t_{0})-\hat{v}(s)|\leq C_{0}\;\;\forall\,s\in({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}t_{0},t_{0}),

for some constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(t_{0})
.

(Importantly,
C_{0}
depends only on the norm of
\hat{u}
in
{W^{1,\infty}({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}t_{0},t_{0};\,L_{\infty}(\Omega))}
, so is independent of
\chi
, or, equivalently, of
\varepsilon
.)

In view of the similarity of (
3.4
) with (
2.1
),
an inspection of the proof of
Lemma
2.1
shows that now we have a version of
(
2.7
)
with
0
in the left-hand side replaced by

\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\varepsilon C_{{\mathcal{L}}}
, i.e.

\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\varepsilon C_{{\mathcal{L}}}\geq\omega(t_{0})\,\hat{v}(t_{0})+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\!\partial_{s}\omega(t_{0}-s)\,\{\hat{v}(t_{0})-\hat{v}(s)\}\,ds.

Here, recalling the definition (
2.4
) of
\omega(t)
, for the integral kernel one has

\int_{0}^{t_{0}-\varepsilon}\!\!\!\!\!\partial_{s}\omega(t_{0}-s)\,ds\leq\omega(\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\;\int_{t_{0}-\varepsilon}^{t_{0}}\!\!\{t_{0}-s\}\,\partial_{s}\omega(t_{0}-s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\!\!\alpha s^{-\alpha}e^{-\mu s}ds\leq\frac{\alpha\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}.

So combining the above observations yields

\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,\varepsilon C_{{\mathcal{L}}}\geq\omega(t_{0})\,(M-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}-C_{0}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}.

As
\varepsilon
here is arbitrarily small, one concludes that
0\geq\omega(t_{0})\,M
, or
M\leq 0
.

\square

4
Further extensions

4.1
Periodic and mixed boundary conditions

Theorem
3.1
applies to the case of Neumann/Robin boundary condition of the form
{\mathcal{B}}u+g\,u:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\nu_{i}\,a_{ij}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\!u+g\,u\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega

(instead of
u\leq 0
)
with any weight
g\geq 0
,
where
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
are the Cartesian coordinates of the outward normal vector to
\partial\Omega
.
Note that
{\mathcal{B}}u+g\,u\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
combined with

(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c)u\leq 0
in
\Omega

for a weak solution
u

is understood in the sense that

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}\langle(D_{t}^{\alpha}+{\mathcal{L}}+c)u,\,\chi\rangle+\int_{\partial\Omega}({\mathcal{B}}u+g\,u)\chi\leq 0

for all nonnegative
\chi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

For this case, we require a version of Lemma
3.2
with
the assumption
w\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
dropped, and
(
3.2
) modified to

\langle{\mathcal{L}}w,\chi_{0}\rangle+\int_{\partial\Omega}({\mathcal{B}}w+g\,w)\chi_{0}\geq-C_{{\mathcal{L}}}(M-M^{\prime})\,\langle 1,\chi_{0}\rangle
.
Note that
\chi_{0}
is no longer in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, which leads to a minor change in the proof in that the integration by parts in the bound (
3.3
) for

\langle{\mathcal{L}}w,\chi_{0}\rangle

yields an additional term

\int_{\partial\Omega}(-{\mathcal{B}}w)\chi_{0}
. So once
\int_{\partial\Omega}({\mathcal{B}}w+g\,w)\chi_{0}
is added, the bound includes one additional
term
\int_{\partial\Omega}(gw)\chi_{0}\geq 0
, which is nonnegative, so can be dropped.

Similarly, Theorem
3.1
is easily extended for mixed and periodic boundary conditions.

4.2
Multiterm time-fractional parabolic problem

Our results seamlessly extend to variable-coefficient
multiterm time-fractional parabolic equations (see
[
6
]
and references therein) of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}(t)\,D_{t}^{\alpha_{i}}u(x,t)+{\mathcal{L}}u+c(x,t)\,u=f(x,t),\quad\mbox{where}\quad c(x,t)\geq-\lambda,

(4.1)

with

0<\alpha_{\ell}<...<\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{1}\leq 1
for some positive integer
\ell
,
under the conditions
q_{i}(t)\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}(t)\geq C_{q}>0

\forall\,t\in[0,T]
.
Setting
\mu>\max_{i=1\ldots,\ell}\{C_{q}^{-1}\lambda\}^{1/\alpha_{i}}

guarantees that
\lambda<\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}\mu^{\alpha_{i}}

(with the strict inequalities used here to accommodate a possible
\alpha_{1}=1
)
.
Then Lemma
2.1
holds true with
D_{t}^{\alpha}
replaced by

D_{t}^{\bar{\alpha}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}(t_{0})\,D_{t}^{\alpha_{i}}
(and, if
\alpha_{1}=1
, under an additional assumption that
v\in C^{1}(0,T]
).
Hence, if
\alpha_{1}<0
, one immediately gets versions of Theorems
2.3
and
3.1
,
while if
\alpha_{1}=1
, one only gets Theorem
2.3
under the additional assumption that

u(x,\cdot)\in C^{1}(0,T]
for any
x\in\Omega
.

4.3
Variable-order time-fractional parabolic equations

Variable-order time-fractional equations
have received a
lot of attention in recent years (see
[
13
]
and its references).
One example from
[
13
]
involves
D_{t}^{\bar{\alpha}(t)}=\partial_{t}u+q(t)D_{t}^{\alpha(t)}
,
using (
1.3
) with a variable

\alpha=\alpha(t)\in(0,1)
and
q(t)\geq 0
.
Note that Lemma
2.1
immediately applies to this case under the condition that

\color[rgb]{0,0,0.6}0\leq\lambda<\mu+q(t_{0})\mu^{\alpha(t_{0})}

(and with the obvious change

D_{t}^{\bar{\alpha}(t_{0})}v(t_{0})-\lambda v(t_{0})\leq 0
in (
2.1
)).
Furthermore, all conclusions of §
4.2
apply to a variable-order version of (
4.1
)
with
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i}(t)
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.
