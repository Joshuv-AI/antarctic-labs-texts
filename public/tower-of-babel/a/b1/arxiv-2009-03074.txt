One-Clock Priced Timed Games with Negative Weights
Thomas Brihaye ORCID:0000-0001-5763-3130, Gilles Geeraerts, Axel Haddad, Engel Lefaucheux ORCID:0000-0003-0875-300X, Benjamin Monmege ORCID:0000-0002-4717-9955

Abstract
. Priced timed games are two-player zero-sum games played on priced timed automata (whose locations and transitions are labeled by weights modelling the cost of spending time in a state and executing an action, respectively). The goals of the players are to minimise and maximise the cost to reach a target location, respectively. We consider priced timed games with one clock and arbitrary integer weights and show that, for an important subclass of them (the so-called simple priced timed games), one can compute, in pseudo-polynomial time, the optimal values that the players can achieve, with their associated optimal strategies. As side results, we also show that one-clock priced timed games are determined and that we can use our result on simple priced timed games to solve the more general class of so-called negative-reset-acyclic priced timed games (with arbitrary integer weights and one clock). The decidability status of the full class of priced timed games with one-clock and arbitrary integer weights still remains open.

Introduction

Game theory is nowadays a
well-established framework in theoretical computer science, enabling
computer-aided design of computer systems that are
correct-by-construction. It allows one to describe and analyse the
possible interactions of antagonistic agents (or players) as in the

controller synthesis
problem, for instance. This problem asks,
given a model of the environment of a system, and of the possible
actions of a controller, to compute a controller that constraints the
environment to respect a given specification. Clearly, one cannot
assume in general that the two players (the environment and the
controller) will collaborate, hence the need to find a
strategy
for the controller
that enforces the specification
whatever
the environment does
. This question thus reduces to computing a
so-called winning strategy for the corresponding player in the game
model.

In order to describe precisely the features of complex computer
systems, several game models have been considered in the
literature. In this work, we focus on the model of Priced Timed Games
(PTGs for short), which can be regarded as an extension (in several
directions) of classical finite automata. First, like timed
automata
[
AD94
]
, PTGs have
clocks
, which are
real-valued variables whose values evolve with time elapsing, and
which can be tested and reset along the transitions. Second, the
locations are associated with weights representing rates and transitions are labeled
by discrete weights, as in priced timed
automata
[
BFH+01
,
ALTP04
,
BBBR07
]
. These weights allow one to
associate a price with each play (or run), which depends on the
sequence of transitions traversed by the play, and on the time spent in
each visited location. Finally, a PTG is played by two players,
called
\mathsf{Min}
and
\mathsf{Max}
, and each location of the game is owned
by either of them (we consider a turn-based version of the game). The
player who controls the current location decides how long to wait, and
which transition to take.

In this setting, the goal of
\mathsf{Min}
is to reach a given set of
target locations, while minimising the price of the play to reach such
a location. Player
\mathsf{Max}
has an antagonistic objective: it tries to
avoid the target locations, and, if not possible, to maximise the
accumulated cost up to the first visit of a target location. To
reflect these objectives, we define the upper value
\overline{{\sf Val}}
of the
game as a mapping of the configurations of the PTG to the least price
that
\mathsf{Min}
can guarantee while reaching the target, whatever the
choices of
\mathsf{Max}
. Similarly, the lower value
\underline{{\sf Val}}
returns the
greatest price that
\mathsf{Max}
can ensure (letting the price be

+\infty
in case the target locations are not reached).

Related work.

PTGs are a special case of hybrid games
[
dAHM01
,
MPS95
,
WT97
]
, independently investigated in
[
BCFL04
]
and
[
ABM04
]
. For (non-necessarily turn-based)
PTGs with
non-negative
weights, semi-algorithms are given to
decide the
value problem
, that is to say, whether the upper
value of a location (the best price that
\mathsf{Min}
can guarantee
starting with a clock value
0
), is below a given threshold. It
was also shown that, under the
strongly non-Zeno assumption
on
weights (asking the existence of
\kappa>0
such that every cycle in
the underlying region graph has a weight at least
\kappa
), the
proposed semi-algorithms always terminate. This assumption was
justified in
[
BBR05
,
BBM06
]
by showing that, without it, the

existence problem
, that is to decide whether
\mathsf{Min}
has a
strategy guaranteeing to reach a target location with a price below a
given threshold, is indeed undecidable for PTGs with non-negative
weights and three or more clocks. This result was recently extended in
[
BJM14
]
to show that the value problem is also
undecidable for PTGs with non-negative weights and four or more
clocks. In
[
BCJ09
]
, the undecidability of the existence problem
has also been shown for PTGs with arbitrary weights on locations
(without weights on transitions), and two or more clocks.
Finally, PSPACE-hardness of the value problem has been established for
one-clock PTGs in
[
FIJS20
]
. On a
positive side, the value problem was shown decidable by
[
BLMR06
]
for PTGs with one clock when the weights are
non-negative: a 3-exponential time algorithm was first proposed,
further refined in
[
Rut11
,
HIJM13
]
into an exponential time
algorithm. The key point of those algorithms is to reduce the problem
to the computation of optimal values in a restricted family of PTGs
called
Simple Priced Timed Games
(SPTGs for short), where
the underlying automata contain no guard, no reset, and the play is
forced to stop after one time unit. More precisely, the PTG is
decomposed into a sequence of SPTGs whose value functions are
computed and re-assembled to yield the value function of the original
PTG. Alternatively, and with radically different techniques, a
pseudo-polynomial time algorithm to solve one-clock PTGs with
arbitrary weights on transitions, and rates restricted to two values
amongst
\{-d,0,+d\}
(with
d\in\mathbb{N}
) was given in
[
BGK+14
]
. More recently, a large subclass of PTGs with
arbitrary weights and no restrictions on the number of clocks was
introduced in
[
BGMR17
]
, whose value can be computed in
double-exponential time: they are defined via a partition of strongly
connected components with respect to the sign of all the cycles they
contain. A survey summarising results on PTGs can be found in
[
Bou15
]
.

Contributions.

Following the decidability results
sketched above, we consider PTGs with one clock. We extend those
results by considering arbitrary (positive and negative)
weights. Indeed, all previous works on PTGs with only one clock
(except
[
BGK+14
]
) have considered non-negative weights only,
and the status of the more general case with arbitrary weights has so
far remained elusive. Yet, arbitrary weights are an important
modelling feature. Consider, for instance, a system which can consume
but also produce energy at different rates. In this case, energy
consumption could be modelled as a positive rate, and production by a
negative rate. As another example, imagine the billing system for
electrical power, in a smart house that itself produces energy: the
money spent/earned while using/producing energy contains both timed
components (the more energy the house produces, the more money the
owner gets), and discrete ones (the electricity provider charges
discrete costs per month). Such a model has been studied in
[
BDG+16
]
. In the untimed setting, such extension to negative
weights has been considered in
[
BGHM15
,
BGHM16
]
: our result
heavily builds upon techniques investigated in these works, as we will
see later. Our main contribution is a
pseudo-polynomial time
algorithm to compute the value of one-clock SPTGs with arbitrary
weights
. While this result might sound limited due to the
restricted class of simple PTGs we can handle, we recall that the
previous works mentioned above
[
BLMR06
,
Rut11
,
HIJM13
]
have
demonstrated that solving SPTGs is a key result towards solving
more general PTGs. Moreover, this algorithm is, as far as we know,
the first to handle the full class of SPTGs with arbitrary weights,
and we note that the solutions (either the algorithms or the proofs)
known so far do not generalise to this case. Notice also
that previous algorithms provided exponential-time algorithms
(
2^{O(n^{2})}
for
[
Rut11
]
and
O(12^{n})
for
[
HIJM13
]
, with

n
the number of locations of the game), whereas we obtain a
pseudo-polynomial time complexity (see Theorem
27

for the exact bound).

2.
Quantitative reachability games

The semantics of the priced timed games we study in this work can be
expressed in the setting of
quantitative reachability games
as
defined below. Intuitively, in such a game, two players (
\mathsf{Min}
and

\mathsf{Max}
) play by changing alternatively the current configuration of
the game. The game ends when it reaches a final configuration, and

\mathsf{Min}
has to pay to
\mathsf{Max}
a price associated with the sequence of
configurations and of transitions taken (hence,
\mathsf{Min}
is trying to
minimise this price while
\mathsf{Max}
wants to maximise it).

Note that the framework of quantitative reachability games that
we develop here (and for which we prove a determinacy result, see
Theorem
1
) can be applied to other settings than
priced timed games. For example, special cases of quantitative
reachability games are
finite
quantitative reachability
games—where the set of configurations is finite—that have been
thoroughly studied in
[
BGHM16
]
under the name of
min-cost
reachability games
. In this article, we will rely on quantitative
reachability games with
uncountably
many states as the
semantics of priced timed games. Similarly, our quantitative
reachability games could be used to formalise the semantics of hybrid
games
[
BBC06
,
BBJ+08
]
or any (non-probabilistic) game with a
reachability objective.
We start our discussion by defining formally those games:

{defi}
[Quantitative reachability games]
A
quantitative reachability game
is a tuple

G=(C_{\mathsf{Min}},C_{\mathsf{Max}},F,\Sigma,E,{\sf Cost})
, where
C=C_{\mathsf{Min}}\uplus C_{\mathsf{Max}}\uplus F
is the
set of
configurations
(that does not need to be finite, nor
countable), partitioned into the set
C_{\mathsf{Min}}
of configurations of
player
\mathsf{Min}
, the set
C_{\mathsf{Max}}
of configurations of
player
\mathsf{Max}
, and the set
F
of
final configurations
;

\Sigma
is a (potentially infinite) alphabet whose
elements are called letters;
E\subseteq(C\setminus F)\times\Sigma\times C
is
the transition relation; and
{\sf Cost}\colon(C\Sigma)^{*}C\to\mathbb{R}
maps each
finite sequence
c_{1}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}c_{n}
to a real number called
the
cost
of
c_{1}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}c_{n}
.

A
play
is a finite or infinite sequence
\rho=c_{1}a_{1}c_{2}\cdots

alternating between configurations and letters, and such that for all

i\geqslant 0
:
(c_{i},a_{i},c_{i+1})\in E
. The length of a play
\rho
, denoted
|\rho|

is the number of configuration occurring in it. As such, if
\rho
is infinite,

|\rho|=+\infty
. For the sake of clarity, we denote a play

c_{1}a_{1}c_{2}\cdots
as
c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots
.
A
completed play
is either (1) an
infinite play, or
(2) a finite play ending in a
deadlock, i.e. a configuration
c
such that the set

\{(c,a,c^{\prime})\in E\mid a\in\Sigma\wedge c^{\prime}\in C\}
is empty. Note that
every play reaching a final state ends in a deadlock, hence infinite
plays never visit
F
.

We take the viewpoint of player
\mathsf{Min}
who wants to reach a final
configuration. Thus, the
price
of a completed play

\rho=c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots
, denoted
{\sf Price}(\rho)
is
either
+\infty
if either
|\rho|=+\infty
or
\rho
is a finite
play that does not end in a final state (this is the worst situation
for
\mathsf{Min}
, which explains why the price is maximal in this case);
or
{\sf Cost}(c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{n})
if
|\rho|=n
and
c_{n}\in F
.

A
strategy
for player
\mathsf{Min}
is a function
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}

mapping all finite plays ending in a configuration
c\in C_{\mathsf{Min}}

(excluding deadlocks) to a transition
(c,a,c^{\prime})\in E
. Strategies

\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
of player
\mathsf{Max}
are defined accordingly. We let

{\sf Strat}_{\mathsf{Min}}(G)
and
{\sf Strat}_{\mathsf{Max}}(G)
be the sets of
strategies of
\mathsf{Min}
and
\mathsf{Max}
, respectively. A pair

(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})\in{\sf Strat}_{\mathsf{Min}}(\mathcal{G})\times{\sf Strat}_{\mathsf{Max}}(\mathcal{G})
is called a
profile of
strategies
. Together with an initial configuration
c_{1}
, it
defines a unique completed play

\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})=c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots
such
that for all
i\geqslant 0
:

(c_{i},a_{i},c_{i+1})=\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}(c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{i})

if
c_{i}\in C_{\mathsf{Min}}
; and

(c_{i},a_{i},c_{i+1})=\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}(c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{i})

if
c_{i}\in C_{\mathsf{Max}}
. We let
\mathsf{Play}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})

(resp.
\mathsf{CPlay}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
) be the set of plays (resp. completed
plays) that conform with
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
. That is,

c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots\in\mathsf{Play}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
iff for all

i\geqslant 0
:
c_{i}\in C_{\mathsf{Min}}
implies

(c_{i},a_{i},c_{i+1})=\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}(c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{i})
.
We let
\mathsf{Play}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
(resp.
\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
) be the
subset of plays from
\mathsf{Play}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
(resp.
\mathsf{CPlay}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
)
that start in
c_{1}
. We define
\mathsf{Play}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})
and

\mathsf{Play}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})
as well as the completed variants
accordingly. Given an initial configuration
c_{1}
, the price of a
strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
of
\mathsf{Min}
is:

{\sf Price}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})=\sup_{\rho\in\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})}{\sf Price}(\rho)\,.

It matches the intuition to be the largest price that
\mathsf{Min}
may pay
while following strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
. This definition is equal to

\sup_{\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}}{\sf Price}(\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}))
, which is
intuitively the highest price that
\mathsf{Max}
can force
\mathsf{Min}
to pay
if
\mathsf{Min}
follows
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
. Similarly, given a strategy

\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
of
\mathsf{Max}
, we define the price of
\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
as

{\sf Price}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})=\inf_{\rho\in\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})}{\sf Price}(\rho)=\inf_{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}{\sf Price}(\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}))\,.

It corresponds to the least price that
\mathsf{Min}
can achieve once

\mathsf{Max}
has fixed its strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
.

From there, two different definitions of the value of a configuration

c_{1}
arise, depending on which player chooses its strategy first. The

upper value
of
c_{1}
, defined as

\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})=\inf_{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}\sup_{\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}}{\sf Price}(\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}))

corresponds to the least price that
\mathsf{Min}
can ensure when choosing
its strategy
before

\mathsf{Max}
, while the
lower value
,
defined as

\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})=\sup_{\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}}\inf_{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}{\sf Price}(\mathsf{CPlay}(c_{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}))

corresponds to the least price that
\mathsf{Min}
can ensure when choosing
its strategy
after

\mathsf{Max}
. It is easy to see that

\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})\leqslant\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
, which explains the chosen
names. Indeed, if
\mathsf{Min}
picks its strategy after
\mathsf{Max}
, it has
more information, and then can, in general, choose a better response.

In general, the order in which players choose their strategies can
modify the outcome of the game. However, for quantitative reachability
games, this makes no difference, and the value is the same whichever
player picks its strategy first. This result, known as the

determinacy property
, is formalised here:

Theorem 1

(Determinacy of quantitative reachability
games)
.

For all quantitative reachability games
G
and configurations

c_{1}
,
\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})=\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
.

Proof 2.1
.

To establish this result, we rely on a general determinacy result of
Donald Martin
[
Mar75
]
. This result concerns
qualitative

games (i.e. games where players either win or lose the game, and do
not pay a price), called Gale-Stewart games. So, we first explain how
to reduce a quantitative reachability game

G=(C_{\mathsf{Min}},C_{\mathsf{Max}},F,\Sigma,E,{\sf Cost})
to a family of such
Gale-Stewart games
\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
parametrised by a
threshold
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

The Gale-Stewart game
\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
is played on an
infinite tree whose vertices are owned by either of the players. A
play is then a maximal branch in this tree, built as follows: the
player who owns the root of the tree first picks a successor of the
root that becomes the current vertex. Then, the player who owns this
vertex gets to choose a successor that becomes the current one,
etc. The game ends when a leaf is reached, where the winner is
declared thanks to a given set
\mathit{Win}
of winning leaves.

In our case, the vertices of
\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
are the finite plays

c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
of
G
starting from configuration

c_{1}
. Such a vertex
v=c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
is owned by

\mathsf{Min}
iff
c_{n}\in C_{\mathsf{Min}}
; otherwise
v
belongs to
\mathsf{Max}
. A
vertex
v=c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
has successors only if

c_{n}\not\in F
. In
this case, the successors of
v
are all the vertices
v\xrightarrow{a}c

such that
(c_{n},a,c)\in E
. Finally, a leaf
c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}

(thus, with
c_{n}\in F
) is winning for
\mathsf{Min}
iff

{\sf Cost}(c_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}c_{2}\cdots c_{n})\leqslant r
.

As a consequence, the set of winning plays in

\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
is:

\mathit{Win}=\bigcup_{v\in L\text{ s.t. }{\sf Cost}(v)\leqslant r}\{\textit{branch}(v)\}

where
L
is the set of leaves of
\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
, and

\textit{branch}(v)
is the (unique) branch from
c_{1}
to
v
. Then,
we rewrite the definition of
\mathit{Win}
as:

\mathit{Win}=\bigcup_{v\in L\text{ s.t. }{\sf Cost}(v)\leqslant r}\textit{Cone}(v)

where
\textit{Cone}(v)
is the set of plays in

\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
that visit
v
. Indeed, when
v
is a
leaf, the set
\textit{Cone}(v)
reduces to the singleton containing
only
\textit{branch}(v)
. Thus, the set of winning plays (for

\mathsf{Min}
) is an open set, defined in the topology generated from the

\textit{Cone}(v)
sets, and we can apply
[
Mar75
]
to conclude
that
\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
is a determined game for all
quantitative reachability games
G
and all thresholds
r\in\mathbb{R}

i.e. either
\mathsf{Min}
or
\mathsf{Max}
has a winning strategy from the
root of the tree. Moreover, notice that
\mathsf{Min}
wins the game

\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
iff it guarantees in

\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})\leqslant r
. Similarly,

wins the game

\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
iff it guarantees in

.

We rely on this result to prove that

\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})\geqslant\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
in
G
(the other
inequality holds by definition of
\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
and

\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
). We consider two cases:

(1)

If
\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})=-\infty
, then, since

\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})\leqslant\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
, we have

\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})=-\infty
too.

(2)

If
\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})>-\infty
, consider any real number
r

such that

. Therefore,
\mathsf{Min}
loses in the game

\textit{Threshold}(G,r)
. By determinacy,
\mathsf{Max}
wins in this
game, i.e.
\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})\geqslant r
. Therefore,

implies
r\leqslant\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
. Thus, we have shown that, for
all

implies
r\leqslant\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
. This is equivalent to saying that for all

either

, or

. This
can happen only when

\overline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})\leqslant\underline{{\sf Val}}(c_{1})
.

Now that we have showed that quantitative reachability games are
determined, we can denote by

the value of the game, defined
as
{\sf Val}=\overline{{\sf Val}}=\underline{{\sf Val}}
.

3.3.
Simple priced timed games

As sketched in the introduction, our main contribution is to solve the

3.3.1.
Proof strategy

Let us now highlight the main steps
that will allow us to establish this theorem. The central argument

\displaystyle{\sf Cost}{(\rho)}

we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\sf Cost}{(\rho_{i})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|I_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lfloor(|\rho_{i}|-1)/|L|\rfloor

(6)

Now, we can further bound these three new sums.
The
intervals
I_{i}
are consecutive, hence
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|I_{i}|\leqslant 1
. Next,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}=k|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}
. But
since
k\leqslant|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}|
, we obtain that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}\leqslant|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}||L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}
. For the last sum, we observe
that, by definition of the split of
\rho
into
\rho_{1}
,

\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{k}
(with
k-1
extra transitions in-between),

|\rho|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\rho_{i}|
, hence

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lfloor(|\rho_{i}|-1)/|L|\rfloor\geqslant\frac{|\rho|}{|L|}-2|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}|
,
since
|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}|\geqslant k
. Plugging these three bounds in (
6
),
we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\sf Cost}{(\rho_{i})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}||L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}-\frac{|\rho|}{|L|}+2|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}|\,.

(7)

Finally, using the bounds (
5
) and (
7
)
in (
4
), we obtain:

\displaystyle{\sf Cost}{(\rho)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+(|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}|-1)\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}+|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}||L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}-\frac{|\rho|}{|L|}+2|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}|\,,

which concludes the proof, using the fact that
|L|\geqslant 1
.

3.3.5.
Fake-optimal strategies

Next, to characterise the fact
that
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
must allow
\mathsf{Min}
to reach a cost which is

small enough, without necessarily reaching a target location
, we
define the
fake value
of an NC-strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
from a
configuration
s
as:

\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}(s)=\sup\{{\sf Price}(\rho)\mid\rho\in\mathsf{Play}(s,\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}),\rho\textrm{ reaches a target}\}

i.e. the
value obtained when
ignoring
the
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
-induced plays that

do not
reach the target: we let
\sup\emptyset=-\infty
. Thus,
clearly,
\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}(s)\leqslant{\sf Val}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}(s)
. We say that an NC-strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
is

fake-optimal
if

\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}(s)={\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(s)
for all
configurations
s
.

Let us now explain why this notion of fake-optimal strategy is
important. As we are about to show, we can combine any fake-optimal
NC-strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
with an attractor strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2}

into a switching strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
, which forces to eventually
reach the target with a price that we can make as small as desired
(since
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
is an NC-strategy) when (negative) cycles can be
enforced in the game by
\mathsf{Min}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an SPTG such that
{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(s)\neq+\infty
, for
all
s
. Let
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
be an NC-strategy of
\mathsf{Min}
in

\mathcal{G}
, and
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2}
be an attractor strategy. Then, for
all

n\in\mathbb{N}
, the switching strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
described by the pair

(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1},\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2})
and the switching threshold

K=|L|\times\big(2\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+(2|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}|)\times|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}+3|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}|-\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))\big)

is such
that

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}(s)\leqslant\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))
for all configurations

s
.

Remark 7
.

In particular, in the case where
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
is a fake-optimal
NC-strategy, and
{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(s)\neq-\infty
, and when we choose the
parameter
n
in the definition of the threshold s.t.

n>-{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(s)
, then, we obtain a strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
that is
optimal for
\mathsf{Min}
from configuration
s
.

Proof 3.3

(Proof of Lemma
6
)
.

In order to establish that

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}}(s)\leqslant\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))
(under the assumptions of the
lemma), we will consider any play
\rho
in
\mathsf{Play}(s,\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})

and show that

{\sf Price}(\rho)\leqslant\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))
.

There are two possibilities regarding
\rho\in\mathsf{Play}(s,\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}})
,
depending on whether the switch has happened or
not:

(1)

If
\rho
reaches the target without switching from

\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
to
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2}
, then

\rho\in\mathsf{Play}(s,\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1})
and thus

{\sf Price}(\rho)\leqslant\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s)\leqslant\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))
.

(2)

If
\rho
reaches the target after the switch happened from

\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
to
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2}
, we can decompose
\rho
into the
concatenation of a prefix
\rho_{1}
of length
K+1
conforming to

\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
, and a play prefix
\rho_{2}
conforming to

\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2}
. As
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}
is an NC-strategy, every

|L|
steps, either the time valuation of the play changed of
interval of
\mathsf{int}(\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1})
or a cycle occured within
the same interval in which the cost of the discrete transitions is
at most
-1
. In other words, if
(m+|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}|)|L|
steps
occured, at least
m
cycle occured reducing the
discrete cost of the play by at least
m
. As a
consequence, thanks to Lemma
5

\rho_{1}
has
K
steps with

K=|L|\times\big(\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+(2|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}|-1)\times|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}+3|\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}|-[\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))-\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}-|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}]\big)

we know that

{\sf Cost}{(\rho_{1})}\leqslant\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))-\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}-|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}
. Moreover,

{\sf Cost}{(\rho_{2})}\leqslant\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+|L|\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}
since

\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{2}
follows an attractor computation and must thus reach
the target in at most
|L|
transitions (and at most

time). Hence,

{\sf Price}(\rho)={\sf Cost}{\rho_{1}}+{\sf Cost}{\rho_{2}}\leqslant\max(-n,\mathsf{fake}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{1}}(s))\,.

This result allows us to identify the conditions we need to check to
make sure than an SPTG admits optimal strategies that can be
described in a
finite way
. Formally, we say that an SPTG is

finitely optimal
if:

(1)

\mathsf{Min}
has a fake-optimal
NC-strategy;

(2)

\mathsf{Max}
has an optimal
FP-strategy; and

(3)

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell)
is a cost
function, for all locations
\ell
.

The central point in establishing Theorem
4
will thus be to
prove that
all SPTGs are finitely optimal

(Theorem
23
), as this guarantees the existence of
well-behaved optimal strategies and value functions. We will also show that
these have a pseudo-polynomial number of cutpoints
(Theorem
27
), which easily induces that they can be computed
in pseudo-polynomial time.
The proof is by induction on the number of non-urgent locations of the
SPTG. In Section
4
, we address the base case of
SPTGs with urgent locations only (where no time can elapse). Since
these SPTGs are very close to the
untimed
min-cost
reachability games of
[
BGHM16
]
, we adapt the algorithm in this
work and obtain the
solveInstant
function
(Algorithm
1
). This function can also
compute
{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,1)
for all
\ell
and all games
\mathcal{G}
(even
with non-urgent locations) since time cannot elapse anymore when the
clock has value
1
. Next, using the continuity result of
Theorem
2
, we can detect locations
\ell

where
{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)\in\{+\infty,-\infty\}
, for all

\nu\in[0,1]
, and remove them from the game. Finally, in
Section
5
we handle SPTGs with non-urgent
locations by refining the technique of
[
BLMR06
,
Rut11
]
(that
work only on SPTGs with non-negative weights).

4.
SPTGs with only urgent locations

Throughout this section, we consider an
r
-SPTG

where all non-final locations are

. We also fix an initial
clock value
\nu

\mathcal{G}

no time will elapse, all configurations will have the same clock value

{\sf Price}(\rho)=+\infty
if

and

{\sf Price}(\rho)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\pi(\ell_{i},\ell_{i+1})+\varphi_{\ell_{k}}(\nu)

4.1.
Computing the game value for a particular clock value

We first explain how we can compute the value function of the game for
a
fixed
clock value
\nu\in[0,r]
: more

({\sf Val}(\ell,\nu))_{\ell\in L}
of values for all

{\sf Val}_{\nu}(\ell)
the value

{\sf Val}(\ell,\nu)
, so that
{\sf Val}_{\nu}
is the vector we
want to compute. Since no time can elapse, it consists in an
adaptation of the techniques developed in
[
BGHM16
]
to solve
(untimed)
min-cost reachability games
. The main difference
concerns the weights being rational (and not integers) and the presence
of final cost functions.

Following the arguments of
[
BGHM16
]
, we first observe that
locations
\ell
with values
{\sf Val}_{\nu}(\ell)=+\infty
and

{\sf Val}_{\nu}(\ell)=-\infty
can be pre-computed (using
respectively attractor and mean-payoff techniques) and removed from
the game without changing the other values. Then, because of the
particular structure of the game
\mathcal{G}
(where a real cost is paid
only on the target location, all other weights being integers), for all
plays
\rho
,
{\sf Price}(\rho)
is a value from the set

\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}=\mathbb{Z}+\{\varphi_{\ell}(\nu)\mid\ell\in L_{f}\}

further define
\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{+\infty}=\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Clearly,
\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}

contains at most

values between two consecutive integers,
i.e.

\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}\quad|[i,i+1)\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}|\leqslant|L_{f}|

(8)

Then, we define an operator

\mathcal{F}\colon(\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{+\infty})^{L}\to(\mathbb{Z}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{+\infty})^{L}

\displaystyle{\min_{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})\in\Delta}}\big(\pi(\ell,\ell^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell^{\prime}}\big)&\textrm{if }\ell\in L_{\mathsf{Min}}\,.\end{cases}

We will obtain

must reach the target within

Remark 28

The pseudo-polynomial lower-bound on the number of cutpoints shown

In order to bound the number of resets of a
\kappa
-NRAPTG, we
first prove a bound on the value of such
games, that will be useful in the following. We let
k=|\mathsf{Reg}_{\mathcal{G}}|
be
the number of regions. Recall
that
M
is a bound on the valuations taken by the clock in

\mathcal{G}
, as discussed on page
3.1
.

Lemma 30
.

For all
\kappa
-NRAPTGs
\mathcal{G}
and

(\ell,\nu)\in{\sf Conf}_{\mathcal{G}}
: either

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)\in\{-\infty,+\infty\}
, or

-|L|M\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}-|L|^{2}(|L|+2)\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}-\Pi^{\mathrm{fin}}\leqslant{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)\leqslant|L|M\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+|L|k\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}+\Pi^{\mathrm{fin}}\,.

Proof 6.2
.

Consider the case where

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)\notin\{-\infty,+\infty\}
. Let

\kappa>2\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exist
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
and

\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}

\varepsilon
-optimal strategies for
\mathsf{Min}
and

\mathsf{Max}
, respectively.

Let
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{\neg c}
be any memoryless strategy of
\mathsf{Min}
in
the reachability timed game induced by
\mathcal{G}
such that no play
consistent with
\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{\neg c}
goes twice in the same couple
(location, region). If such a strategy does not exist, as the clock
constraints are the same during the first and second occurrences of
this couple,
\mathsf{Max}
can enforce the cycle infinitely often, thus
the reachability game is winning for
\mathsf{Max}
and the value of
\mathcal{G}
is

+\infty
. Let us note

\rho=\mathsf{CPlay}((\ell,\nu),\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}^{\neg c},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})
. By

\varepsilon
-optimality of
\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
,

{\sf Price}(\rho)\geqslant{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)-\varepsilon
. Let

{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho)
be the price of
\rho
due to the weights of the
transitions, and
{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\rho)
be the weight due to the time
elapsed in the locations of the game:

{\sf Cost}{(\rho)}={\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho)+{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\rho)
. As there are no
cycles in the game according to couples (location, region), there
are at most
|L|k
transitions, thus

{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho)\leqslant|L|k\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}
. Moreover, the
absence of cycles also implies that we do not take two transitions
with a reset ending in the same location or one transition with a
reset ending in the initial location, thus we take at most

|L|-1
such transitions. Therefore at most
|L|M
units of
time elapsed and
{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\rho)\leqslant|L|M\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Adding the final cost, this
implies that

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)-\varepsilon\leqslant{\sf Price}(\rho)\leqslant|L|M\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}+|L|k\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}+\Pi^{\mathrm{fin}}\,.

By taking the limit of
\varepsilon
towards
0
, we obtain the
announced upper bound.

We now prove the lower bound on the value. To that extent, consider
now the completed play
\rho=\mathsf{CPlay}((\ell,\nu),\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}},\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}})
. We have that

{\sf Price}(\rho)\leqslant{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)+\varepsilon
.

We want to lower bound the price of
\rho
, therefore
non-negative cycles can be safely ignored. Let us show that there
are no negative cycles around a transition with a reset. If it was
the case, since the game is a
\kappa
-NRAPTG, this cycle has
cost at most
-\kappa
. Since the strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
is

\varepsilon
-optimal, and
\kappa>\varepsilon
, it is not possible that

\sigma_{\mathsf{Max}}
decides alone to take this bad cycle. Therefore,

\sigma_{\mathsf{Min}}
has the capability to enforce this cycle, and to exit it
(otherwise,
\mathsf{Max}
would keep it inside to get value
+\infty
):
but then,
\mathsf{Min}
could decide to cycle as long as it wants, then
guaranteeing a value as low as possible, which contradicts the fact
that
{\sf Val}(\ell,\nu)\notin\{-\infty,+\infty\}
. Therefore,
the only cycles in
\rho
around transitions with resets, are
non-negative cycles. This implies that its price is bounded below by
the price of a sub-play obtained by removing the cycles in
\rho
.

We now consider a play where each reset transition is taken at most
once in
\rho
, and lower-bound its price.

If
\rho
contains a cycle around a location
\ell^{\prime}\in L_{\mathsf{Max}}

without reset transitions, this cycle has the form

(\ell^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\xrightarrow{c^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime\prime},\nu^{\prime}+t)\cdots\xrightarrow{c^{\prime\prime}}(\ell^{\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime})
with

\nu^{\prime\prime}\geqslant\nu^{\prime}
, followed in
\rho
by a transition
towards configuration
(\ell^{\prime\prime\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime}+t^{\prime})
. Thus, another
strategy for
\mathsf{Max}
could have consisted in skipping the cycle by
choosing as delay in the first location
\ell^{\prime}
,

\nu^{\prime\prime}-\nu^{\prime}+t^{\prime}
instead of
t
. This would get a new
strategy that cannot make the price increase above

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)+\varepsilon
, since it is still playing
against an
\varepsilon
-optimal strategy of
\mathsf{Min}
. Therefore, we can
consider the sub-play
\rho_{f}
of
\rho
where all such cycles are
removed: we still have

{\sf Price}(\rho_{f})\leqslant{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)+\varepsilon
.

Suppose now that
\rho_{f}
contains a cycle around a location

\ell^{\prime}\in L_{\mathsf{Min}}
without reset transitions, of the form

(\ell^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\xrightarrow{c^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime\prime},\nu^{\prime}+t)\cdots\xrightarrow{c^{\prime\prime}}(\ell^{\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime})
with
\nu^{\prime}
and

\nu^{\prime\prime}
in the same region, composed of
\mathsf{Min}
’s locations
only, and followed in
\rho
by a transition towards configuration

(\ell^{\prime\prime\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime}+t^{\prime})
. Then, the transition weight of this
cycle is non-negative, otherwise
\mathsf{Min}
could enforce this cycle
it entirely controls, while letting only a bounded time pass
(smaller and smaller as the number of cycles grow). This is not
possible.

Therefore, we have that two occurrences of a same
\mathsf{Max}
’s
location in
\rho_{f}
are separated by a reset transition and two
occurrences of a same
\mathsf{Min}
’s couple (location, region) are either
separated by a reset or by a
\mathsf{Max}
’s location. As there are at
most
|L|-1
resets,
|L|
locations of
\mathsf{Max}
and

|L|k
couples (location, region) for
\mathsf{Min}
,
\rho_{t}
contains
at most
|L|^{2}
locations of
\mathsf{Max}
and

|L|k(|L|^{2}+|L|-1+1)
locations of
\mathsf{Min}
, which
makes for at most
|L|^{2}(|L|k+k+1)
locations. Thus

{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\rho_{t})\geqslant-|L|^{2}(|L|k+k+1)\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Moreover, as at most
|L|-1
resets are taken in
\rho_{f}
and
that the game is bounded by
M
,

{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\rho_{f})\geqslant-|L|M\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}
. Adding the final cost, this implies that

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,\nu)+\varepsilon\geqslant{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\rho_{f})+{\sf Cost}^{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho_{t})\geqslant-|L|M\Pi^{\mathrm{loc}}-|L|^{2}(|L|k+k+1)\Pi^{\mathrm{tr}}-\Pi^{\mathrm{fin}}\,.

Taking the limit when
\varepsilon
tends to
0
, we obtain the
desired lower bound.

Using this bound on the value of a
\kappa
-NRAPTG, one can give a
bound on the number of cycles needed to be allowed. The idea is that
if a reset is taken twice and the generated cycle has positive cost,
either
\mathsf{Min}
can modify its strategy so that it does not take this
cycle or the value of the game is
+\infty
as
\mathsf{Max}
can prevent

\mathsf{Min}
from reaching a final location. On the contrary if the
cycle has negative cost, then by definition of a
\kappa
-NRAPTG,
this cost is less than
-\kappa
. Thus by allowing enough such
cycles, as we have bounds on the values of the game, we know when we
will have enough cycles to get under the lower bound of the value of
the game. By solving the copies of the game, if we reach a value that
is smaller than the lower bound of the value, then it means that the
value is
-\infty
.

Lemma 31
.

For all
\kappa>0
, the value of a
\kappa
-NRAPTG can be computed
by solving
\lceil 2n({\sf Val}^{sup}-{\sf Val}_{inf})/\kappa\rceil
PTGs
without resets and using the same set of guards, where
{\sf Val}^{sup}

and
{\sf Val}_{inf}
are the upper and lower bounds of the value of the
game given by Lemma
30
. Moreover, from

\varepsilon
-optimal strategies on those
k
games, we can build

\varepsilon\lceil 2n({\sf Val}^{sup}-{\sf Val}_{inf})/\kappa\rceil
-optimal
strategies in the original game.

Solving the PTGs without resets can be
done by using the same algorithms as the one described before for
SPTGs: indeed, since we play in a region-PTG, we can focus on the
resolution of a subgame staying in the same region until the final
transition, and such a game can even be decomposed into simpler games
where every region has length
1
, which can then be interpreted as a
SPTG. Another possibility would be to rescale the time and the weights
in order to transform a region
(a,b)
into
(a,a+1)
, avoiding to
split the region into
b-a
different subregions.

The values
{\sf Val}^{sup}
and
{\sf Val}_{inf}
, and therefore also

\lceil 2n({\sf Val}^{sup}-{\sf Val}_{inf})/\kappa\rceil
, are
pseudo-polynomial in the size of the original game, which allows us to
conclude:

Theorem 32
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
\mathcal{G}
be a
\kappa
-NRAPTG. Then for every
location
q\in Q
, the function

\nu\mapsto{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(q,\nu)
is computable in
pseudo-polynomial time and is piecewise-affine with at most a
pseudo-polynomial number of cutpoints. Moreover, for every

\varepsilon>0
, there exist (and we can effectively compute)

\varepsilon
-optimal strategies for both players.

The robust games defined in
[
BCR14
]
restricted to one-clock are a
subset of the NRAPTG, therefore their value is computable with the
same complexity. While we cannot extend the computation of the value
to all (one-clock) PTGs, we can still obtain information on the
nature of the value function:

Theorem 33
.

The value functions of all one-clock PTGs are cost functions with
at most a pseudo-polynomial number of cutpoints.

Proof 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a one-clock PTG. Let us replace all transitions

(\ell,g,\top,\ell^{\prime})
resetting the clock by
(\ell,g,\bot,\ell^{\prime\prime})
,
where
\ell^{\prime\prime}
is a new final location with

\varphi_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}={\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell^{\prime},0)
—observe that

{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}(\ell,0)
exists even if we cannot compute it, so this
transformation is well-defined. This yields a reset-acyclic PTG
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
such that
{\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}={\sf Val}_{\mathcal{G}}
. The pseudo-polynomial
number of cutpoints of reset-acyclic PTG, as for SPTGs, does not
depend on the size of final prices (but only on the price of
transitions, and the number of locations), which allows us to
conclude.

As a consequence, in the particular case of non-negative prices only
where transitions with a reset can be unfolded to remove cycles in
which they are contained, this ensures that the exponential-time
algorithms of
[
BLMR06
,
Rut11
,
HIJM13
]
indeed have a
pseudo-polynomial time complexity.

Corollary 34
.

The value functions of all one-clock PTGs with only non-negative
prices can be computed in pseudo-polynomial time.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we study, for the first time, priced timed games with
arbitrary weights and one clock, showing how to compute optimal values
and strategies in pseudo-polynomial time for the special case of
simple games. This complexity result
is better than previously obtained results in the case of non-negative
weights only
[
HIJM13
,
Rut11
]
(where an exponential complexity
was obtained), and we follow different paths to prove termination and
(partial) correctness (due to the presence of negative weights). In
order to push our algorithm as far as we can, we introduce the class
of negative-reset-acyclic games for which we obtain the same result:
as a particular case, we can solve all priced timed games with one
clock for which the clock is reset in every cycle of the underlying
region automaton. As future works, it is appealing to solve the full
class of priced timed games with arbitrary weights and one clock. We
have shown why our technique seems to break in this more general
setting, thus it could be interesting to study the difficult negative
cycles without reset as their own, with different techniques.
