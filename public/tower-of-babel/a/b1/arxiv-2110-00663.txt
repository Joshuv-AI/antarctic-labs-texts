On grid homology for lens space links: combinatorial invariance and integral coefficients
Samuel Tripp

Abstract
. Following the approach to grid homology of links in S 3 S^{3} , we prove combinatorially that the grid homology of links in lens spaces defined by Baker, Grigsby, and Hedden is a link invariant. Further, using the sign assignment defined by Celoria, we prove that the generalization of grid homology to integral coefficients is a link invariant.



1.
Introduction

Heegaard Floer homology is a package of invariants for closed, oriented 3-manifolds first introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó
[
10
]
. Knot Floer homology is an extension of this invariant to null-homologous knots in closed, oriented 3-manifolds, introduced independently by Ozsváth and Szabó
[
8
]
and Rasmussen
[
14
]
. In
[
11
]
, Ozsváth and Szabó extended knot Floer homology to nullhomologous links in closed, oriented 3-manifolds. The theory was later generalized to non-nullhomologous links in rational homology spheres by Ni
[
7
]
and Ozsváth and Szabó
[
12
]
.

The above invariants are computed from a Heegaard diagram for a link in the 3-manifold, along with further analytic choices. To this data, one associates a chain complex whose differential counts holomorphic disks in the
g
-fold symmetric product of the Heegaard surface, where
g
is the genus of the surface. The conditions these disks must satisfy depend on which version of the above invariant one is computing. The simplest form yields a bigraded chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. The quasi-isomorphism type of the chain complex does not depend on the choice of diagram for the link or on the choice of analytic data, and the bigraded Euler characteristic of this chain complex is the Alexander polynomial of the link.

In
[
15
]
, Sarkar and Wang showed that the simplest flavors of Heegaard Floer and knot Floer homology admit a combinatorial description. This description relies on a Heegaard diagram for the link in the manifold which is
nice
. They showed that any nullhomologous link in a closed, oriented 3-manifold has a nice Heegaard diagram. On such diagrams, the differential counts embedded rectangles and bigons, and does not require any analytic choices. Soon after, Manolescu, Ozsváth, Szabó, and Thurston used
grid diagrams
for links in
S^{3}
to give a combinatorial description of all flavors of knot Floer homology
[
6
]
. In
[
6
]
, the authors also provide a standalone combinatorial proof of invariance without appealing to the equivalence with the holomorphic theory. Additionally, they give a way to assign signs to the rectangles counted by the differential, allowing the combinatorial theory to be generalized to integral coefficients.

In
[
2
]
, Baker, Grigsby, and Hedden undertook a similar combinatorial program for links in lens spaces. They introduced
twisted toroidal grid diagrams
, the combinatorics of which capture the topology of links in lens spaces. To a twisted toroidal grid diagram
\mathbb{G}
for a link
L
, they associate a chain complex
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[V_{0},\ldots,V_{n-1}]
, where
n
is the size of the grid. Each variable
V_{i}
is associated to a marking on the grid, and the actions of
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
are homotopic if the markings they are associated to correspond to the same component of the link. As a result,
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
can be viewed as a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
, where
\ell
is the number of components of
L
. Let each
U_{i}
act by multiplication by
V_{i_{j}}
, where
V_{i_{j}}
corresponds to any marking on the
i^{\text{th}}
component of
L
.

In
[
2
]
, it is shown that this chain complex is quasi-isomorphic to one that computes the knot Floer homology of the link. By
[
11
]
, they conclude the quasi-isomorphism type of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
is an invariant of the link. A combinatorial proof that the quasi-isomorphism type of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
is a link invariant is the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
are two twisted toroidal grid diagrams associated to the same
\ell
-component link
L
in
L(p,q)
. Then
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
and
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}^{\prime}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-^{\prime}})
are quasi-isomorphic as chain complexes over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
via a combinatorially defined quasi-isomorphism.

Following the program for links in
S^{3}
, Celoria gives a way to assign signs to the embedded parallelograms in twisted toroidal grid diagrams
[
4
]
. Celoria also proves that for a fixed twisted toroidal grid diagram
\mathbb{G}
, the
sign assignment
is unique up to quasi-isomorphism. Utilizing this sign assignment, one can generalize the above complex to integral coefficients. We prove that this generalization is a link invariant; below, let
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-};\mathbb{Z})
be the chain complex incorporating the sign assignment of
[
4
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
are two twisted toroidal grid diagrams associated to the same
\ell
-component link
L
in
L(p,q)
. Then
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-};\mathbb{Z})
and
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}^{\prime}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-^{\prime}};\mathbb{Z})
are quasi-isomorphic as chain complexes over
\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
. Moreover, there is a combinatorially defined quasi-isomorphism between them.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a twisted toroidal grid diagram associated to an
\ell
-component link
L
in
L(p,q)
. Let
\CFK^{-}(L,L(p,q);\mathbb{Z})
be the quasi-isomorphism type of
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-};\mathbb{Z})
viewed as a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
. Define the
grid homology
\HFK^{-}(L,L(p,q);\mathbb{Z})
of
L
to be the homology of
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-};\mathbb{Z})
.

Corollary 1.3
.

The quasi-isomorphism type
\CFK^{-}(L,L(p,q);\mathbb{Z})
and the
\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
module
\HFK^{-}(L,L(p,q);\mathbb{Z})
are invariants of the link
L
.

We note that Theorem
1.2
did not follow from an identification with the holomorphic theory. While there is an extension of the holomorphic theory for links to integral coefficients, it is not known if the two definitions agree. For example, it is not understood how the local orientation systems necessary to define the holomorphic theory with integral coefficients change under combinatorial moves.

We also mention that from these invariants, one could extract the simpler
\widetilde{\HFK}
and
\widehat{\HFK}
invariants, by collapsing some of the variables.

There remains an interesting open question of whether there can be torsion found in these integral coefficient grid homology groups. No examples have been produced yet of links in
S^{3}
or lens spaces which have torsion in these groups, but that is not expected to hold in general
[
9
]
.
For knots in
S^{3}
, another complex homotopy equivalent to grid homology, but more computationally feasible, was described in
[
3
]
and implemented in
[
5
]
. An adaptation of this approach to links in lens spaces would allow for greater computation, and is one possible direction of future inquiry, as more computational evidence would help investigate the potential presence of torsion.

Organization

In Section
2
we recall the definition and some properties of twisted toroidal grid diagrams. In Section
3
, we recall the definition of grid homology introduced in
[
2
]
for links in lens spaces. We then take a detour in Section
4
to introduce the gradings on grid homology, and provide some combinatorial proofs of their properties.

In Section
5
we prove directly that the grid homology of a lens space link is well-defined, using some results about the combinatorics of twisted toroidal grid diagrams. Section
6
introduces the definition of sign assignments of
[
4
]
, which is used in Section
7
to define the grid homology of links in lens spaces with integral coefficients, and prove that the quasi-isomorphism type of this is well-defined.

2.
Twisted toroidal grid diagrams

In this section we recall the definition of twisted toroidal grid diagrams introduced in
[
2
]
. The combinatorics of these diagrams capture the topology of links in lens spaces. We will assume throughout that
p
and
q
are relatively prime integers with
p
positive and
-p<q<p
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
2
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

A
(twisted toroidal) grid diagram

\mathbb{G}
with grid number
n
for
L(p,q)
consists of a five-tuple
\mathbb{G}=(T^{2},\overrightarrow{\alpha},\overrightarrow{\beta},\mathbb{X},\mathbb{O})
, where:

•

T^{2}
is the standard oriented torus
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, identified with the quotient of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(with its standard orientation) by the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattice generated by the vectors
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
.

•

\overrightarrow{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\}
are the
n
images
\alpha_{i}
in
T^{2}
of the lines
y=\frac{i}{n}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. Their complement
T^{2}-\alpha_{0}-\ldots-\alpha_{n-1}
has
n
connected annular components, which we call the
rows
of the diagram.

•

\overrightarrow{\beta}=\{\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{n-1}\}
are the
n
images
\beta_{i}
in
T^{2}
of the lines
y=-\frac{p}{q}(x-\frac{i}{pn})
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. Their complement
T^{2}-\beta_{0}-\ldots-\beta_{n-1}
has
n
connected annular components, which we call the
columns
of the diagram.

•

\mathbb{O}=\{O_{0},\ldots,O_{n-1}\}
are
n
points in
T^{2}-\overrightarrow{\alpha}-\overrightarrow{\beta}
with the property that no two
O
’s lie in the same row or column.

•

\mathbb{X}=\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1}\}
are
n
points in
T^{2}-\overrightarrow{\alpha}-\overrightarrow{\beta}
with the property that no two
X
’s lie in the same row or column.

Associated to such a twisted toroidal grid diagram, there is a unique isotopy class of link
L
in
L(p,q)
. First, note that
(T^{2},\overrightarrow{\alpha},\overrightarrow{\beta},\mathbb{X})
is a multi-pointed Heegaard diagram for
L(p,q)
. The lens space
L(p,q)
is comprised of two solid tori
V_{\alpha}
and
V_{\beta}
, glued along their common boundary
T^{2}
. We view
V_{\alpha}
as laying below
T^{2}
, and
V_{\beta}
as laying above it. The curves
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
are meridians of
V_{\alpha}
, and
\overrightarrow{\beta}
are meridians of
V_{\beta}
. A link
L
in
L(p,q)
can then be gotten from a grid diagram
G
by connecting each marking
X_{i}
to the unique marking
O_{j}
in the same row as it via an oriented embedded arc in
T^{2}
disjoint from
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
. Next, each marking
O_{j}
is connected to the unique marking
X_{k}
in the same column as it via an oriented embedded arc in
T^{2}
disjoint from
\overrightarrow{\beta}
. Pushing the interiors of the arcs disjoint from
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
down into
V_{\alpha}
and the interiors of the arcs disjoint from
\overrightarrow{\beta}
up into
V_{\beta}
, we get an oriented link
L
. As a result, we occasionally denote a grid diagram
\mathbb{G}
by
\mathbb{G}_{L}
, to remind ourselves of the link associated to the grid diagram.

It is also true that for any link
L
in a lens space, there is a grid diagram which represents it (see
[
2
, Proposition 4.3]
).

Furthermore,
[
1
]
makes explicit the sense in which the combinatorics of twisted toroidal grid diagrams capture the topology of links in lens spaces:

Theorem 2.2

(
[
1
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
be grid diagrams representing smooth links
L
and
L^{\prime}
in
L(p,q)
. Then
L
and
L^{\prime}
are smoothly isotopic if and only if
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
are related by a sequence of grid moves: stabilizations, destabilizations, and commutations.

We will describe these moves in Section
5
.

We can choose any number of fundamental domains for
T^{2}
.
In general, we will look at one specific class of fundamental domains. Throughout the rest of this paper, whenever we fix a fundamental domain, it is assumed to be one of this form.
Each such is a choice of a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
bounded vertically between
y=\frac{k}{n}
and
y=\frac{k}{n}+1
, with those two edges identified, for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and horizontally between
y=-\frac{p}{q}(x-\frac{l}{pn})
and
y=-\frac{p}{q}(x-\frac{l}{pn}-1)
, with those two edges identified, for some
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Choosing a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
allows us to order the
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
bottom-to-top and the
\overrightarrow{\beta}
left-to-right. In a fundamental domain, each
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
intersect
p
times. Other than in the definition of a twisted toroidal grid diagram, when we refer to an indexed curve
\alpha_{i}\in\overrightarrow{\alpha}
or
\beta_{j}\in\overrightarrow{\beta}
, this index is with respect to the ordering induced by choice of fundamental domain.

In depicting these fundamental domains, we will often use a different model, which we will call the
straight model
. This model is bounded by
y=0
,
x=0
,
y=1
, and
x=1
. The left and right boundary are identified directly, and the top and the bottom are identified with a twist:
(x,0)\sim(x+p/q\pmod{1},1)
. Morally, this model is just straightening out the previously described fundamental domain, while remembering the underlying twisting.

3.
Grid homology

In
[
2
]
, to a grid diagram
\mathbb{G}
the authors associate a module
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
and an endomorphism
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}
on
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
. They show that
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
is quasi-isomorphic to the knot Floer complex of
[
11
]
. This equivalence is powerful: it proves that the endomorphism is a differential, and that the homology with respect to the differential is an invariant of the link. In
[
4
]
, the author provides a combinatorial proof that the endomorphism is a differential.

After recalling the definition of the chain complex associated to a grid diagram, we will recreate the combinatorial proof from
[
4
]
that it is indeed a chain complex, as the case analysis will prove fruitful in later proofs. A combinatorial proof of the fact that the homology is a link invariant is the content of Section
5
.

3.1.
Preliminaries

Let
L
be a link with
\ell
components in
L(p,q)
, and let
\mathbb{G}
be a grid diagram of size
n
for
L
. Define
\mathcal{G}
to be the set of unordered
n
-tuples of intersection points in
\overrightarrow{\alpha}\cap\overrightarrow{\beta}
with exactly one point on each curve in
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
and exactly one point on each curve in
\overrightarrow{\beta}
. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
. Given a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
, and thus an ordering of
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
and
\overrightarrow{\beta}
,
\mathbf{x}
corresponds to a bijection between
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
and
\overrightarrow{\beta}
, which can be thought of as an element
\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}\in S_{n}
; namely, each component of
\mathbf{x}
is in
\alpha_{i}\cap\beta_{\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}(i)}
for some
0\leq i<n
. Denote by
x_{i}
the component of
\mathbf{x}
in the intersection of
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}(i)}
.

The choice of a fundamental domain also gives an ordering of the
p
points of
\alpha_{i}\cap\beta_{\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}(i)}
from left-to-right for each
0\leq i<n
. To each component
x_{i}
of
\mathbf{x}
we can assign an element of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
recording on which intersection point of
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}(i)}
the component lies.

Thus, a choice of a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
gives us a bijection between
\mathcal{G}
and
S_{n}\times(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}
. For a generator
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
and a choice of a fundamental domain, we can then write
\mathbf{x}
as a pair
(\sigma_{\mathbf{x}},(a^{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\ldots,a^{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}))
. We call the first component the
permutation associated to
\mathbf{x}
, and the second component the
p
-coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
. As an example, the generator given by darkened circles in Figure
2.1
has permutation
\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&2\\
1&2&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, and
p
-coordinates
(4,0,3)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}
. A
parallelogram
r
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
is a disk embedded in
T^{2}
along with the data of the generators
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
such that

•

the boundary of
r
(which is oriented) lies on
\overrightarrow{\alpha}\cup\overrightarrow{\beta}
,

•

\mid\mathbf{x}\setminus\mathbf{y}\mid=2=\mid\mathbf{y}\setminus\mathbf{x}\mid
, and

•

\partial(\partial_{\alpha}(r))=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
and
\partial(\partial_{\beta}(r))=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
, where
\partial_{\alpha}(r)=\partial(r)\cap\overrightarrow{\alpha}
and
\partial_{\beta}(r)=\partial(r)\cap\overrightarrow{\beta}
.

There is also the more general idea of a domain, a generalization of the above concept.

Definition 3.2

(
[
9
, Definition 4.6.4]
)
.

Fix
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}
. A
domain
\varphi
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
is a formal linear combination of the closure of regions in
\mathbb{G}\setminus(\overrightarrow{\alpha}\cup\overrightarrow{\beta})
such that
\partial(\partial_{\alpha}\varphi)=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
and
\partial(\partial_{\beta}\varphi)=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
, along with the data of the generators
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
. We will call
\mathbf{x}
the
initial generator
for the domain, and
\mathbf{y}
the
terminal generator
. The
support
of a domain
\varphi
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
is the underlying linear combination. Thus, two domains can have the same support without being equal.

Given a domain
\varphi_{1}
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
and a domain
\varphi_{2}
from
\mathbf{y}
to
\mathbf{z}
, we can form the
juxtaposition

\varphi_{1}*\varphi_{2}
, which is a domain as follows. The underlying formal linear combination of
\varphi_{1}*\varphi_{2}
is the sum of those underlying
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, and it has as initial generator
\mathbf{x}
and terminal generator
\mathbf{z}
. In general, the domains we consider will be juxtapositions of two specified types of domains, such as parallelograms or other polygons we will define later.

Let
\PG(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
be the space of parallelograms from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
. Note that
\PG(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is the empty set unless
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
agree at
n-2
points. Call a parallelogram
empty
if
\Int(r)\cap\mathbf{x}=\emptyset=\Int(r)\cap\mathbf{y}
. Let
\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
be the space of empty parallelograms from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
. Further, let
\Int(\varphi)
denote the interior of the support of
\varphi
. Finally, let
\PG(\mathbb{G}):=\bigcup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}}\PG(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, and
\PG^{\circ}(\mathbb{G}):=\bigcup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}}\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that if an embedded parallelogram
r
connects
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
, then
\sigma_{\mathbf{y}}=\tau_{i,j}\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}
, where
\tau_{i,j}
is the transposition
(i\;j)\in S_{n}
. Furthermore, we have that
a^{\mathbf{x}}_{s}=a^{\mathbf{y}}_{s}
for all
s\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\setminus\{i,j\}
, and
a^{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-a^{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=a^{\mathbf{y}}_{j}-a^{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=k
for some

.

For an embedded parallelogram

, denote the above transposition by

.

An example of a parallelogram

can be seen in Figure

, connecting the generator given by darkened circles to the generator given by empty circles. In this case, we see

.

3.2.
Aside on twisting

Before we provide the definition of grid homology, we take a minute to discuss the impact of the twisting of the grid diagrams on the domains we will be discussing. In the case of grid diagrams for
S^{3}
, it is not possible for an empty rectangle to intersect a curve
\alpha\in\overrightarrow{\alpha}
multiple times; the rectangle either intersects
\alpha
in a single segment or not at all. As a result, we can always choose a fundamental domain such that any rectangle embedded in
T^{2}
can be embedded in the fundamental domain considered as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

This is no longer true for twisted toroidal grid diagrams for links in lens spaces: there are parallelograms which intersect a given curve
\alpha\in\overrightarrow{\alpha}
in multiple segments. There is no choice of fundamental domain in which they will be an embedded disk, considered as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We refer to such parallelograms as
wrapping
around the domain, in reference to the fact that their support on any fundamental domain, considered as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is disconnected.

Figure
3.1
provides some examples of parallelograms on the twisted toroidal grid diagrams. We see that there are many options for the parallelogram extending vertically: it can wrap any number of times around the diagram (less than
p
). Later on, when analyzing the combinatorics of decompositions of domains, we will often not display this potential complexity. We will draw domains as embedded, wrapped, or with dashed lines to acknowledge potential wrapping. Regardless of how the domain is drawn, it will stand in for all domains with the same combinatorics governing its decompositions, which will be independent of the number of times the domain wraps around the diagram.

Figure 3.1
.
Examples of parallelograms on fundamental domains. The first and second examples wrap around the diagram one and two times respectively. The third example does not wrap around the diagram, as there are many examples of fundamental domains on which the parallelogram would be embedded (as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
).

3.3.
Definition of grid homology

Let
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
be the free
\mathbb{F}[V_{0},\ldots,V_{n-1}]
module generated by
\mathcal{G}
. Define an endomorphism of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
on generators
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
by:

\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
r\cap\mathbb{X}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}V_{i}^{n_{O_{i}}(r)})\mathbf{y},

where
n_{O_{i}}(r)=\#(O_{i}\cap\Int(r))
. Extend this map to
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
linearly; note then that
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}(V_{i}\mathbf{x})=V_{i}\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}(\mathbf{x})
.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
2
, Proposition 2.2]
)
.

(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
is isomorphic to a chain complex which computes the knot Floer homology
(CF^{-}(L(p,q),K),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
.

Corollary 3.5
.

The endomorphism
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}
is a differential on
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
.

The above corollary was proved combinatorially in
[
4
]
. We recreate that proof here in detail to lay out cases and notation which will be useful in later sections.

Proposition 3.6

(
[
4
, Proposition 2.13]
)
.

The map
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}
is a differential on
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
, i.e.
(\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})^{2}=0
.

Remark 3.7
.

Note that if a domain
\varphi
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{z}
has a decomposition as
\varphi=r_{1}*r_{2}
, for
r_{1}\in\PG(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and

While
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
is a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[V_{0},\ldots,V_{n-1}]
, the link invariant is the quasi-isomorphism type of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
as a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
, where each variable corresponds to the action of a distinct component of the link. To see this is well-defined, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.9

(cf.
[
6
, Lemma 2.11]
)
.

Suppose
O_{i}
and
O_{j}
are markings corresponding to the same component of
L
. Then multiplication by
V_{i}
is chain homotopic to multiplication by
V_{j}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose
O_{i}
is in the same row as
X_{a}
, which is in the same column as
O_{j}
. Define
\Phi_{X_{a}}:C^{-}(\mathbb{G})\to C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
on a generator
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
by

\Phi_{X_{a}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
\Int(r)\cap\mathbb{X}=X_{a}\end{subarray}}V_{o}^{n_{O_{0}}(r)}\cdots V_{n-1}^{n_{O_{n-1}}(r)}\mathbf{y}.

Extend this linearly to
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
.

We will show that this is the homotopy between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
. Our goal is to show that
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}\circ\Phi_{X_{a}}+\Phi_{X_{a}}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}=V_{i}-V_{j}
. The left hand side counts domains which are the juxtapositions of two embedded parallelograms, one of which contains
X_{a}
. Any such domain has an alternate decomposition given in the proof of Proposition
3.6
, regardless of the placement of
X_{a}
. In that proof we do not need to consider the domains which connect
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
to
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
, because they consist of a single row or a single column, either of which contains some marking
X\in\mathbb{X}
. Here, however, domains are allowed to cover
X_{a}\in\mathbb{X}
, so the left hand side above counts the annulus of width one covering
X_{a}
and the annulus of height one covering
X_{a}
. Each of these connects
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{x}
; the row contributes a coefficient of
V_{i}
and the column contributes a coefficient of
V_{j}
, proving the above equality.

If
O_{i}
and
O_{j}
are not connected by a single marking
X_{a}
, then a sequence of the above homotopies, which can be found since
O_{i}
and
O_{j}
correspond to the same component, proves the actions of
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
are homotopic.
∎

4.
Gradings

Grid homology as defined comes with three gradings
[
2
]
. After recalling the definitions of these below, we will prove directly that each of these is well-defined, i.e. independent of the choice of a fundamental domain on which to compute them. Like the fact that
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}
is a differential, this is proved in
[
2
]
by appealing to the correspondence with the holomorphic theory.

Throughout, let
\mathbb{G}
be a grid diagram for an
\ell
-component link
L
.

4.1.
\spinc
grading

The
\spinc
grading takes values in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Fix a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbb{O}}
be the generator with components occupying the lower-left corner of each region of
T^{2}-\overrightarrow{\alpha}-\overrightarrow{\beta}
containing an
O
marking. For any generator
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
, let
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a^{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-a^{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbb{O}}}_{i}\pmod{p}
, and let
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{x})+q-1\pmod{p}
. Extend this to a grading on all of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
by setting
\mathbf{S}(V_{i}\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{x})
.

As an example, consider the generator given in Figure
2.1
by darkened circles, and denote it by
\mathbf{z}
. We noted earlier it has
p
-coordinates
(4,0,3)
. We can see on the diagram that
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbb{O}}
has
p
-coordinates
(3,4,2)
. We compute
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{z})=(4-3)+(0-4)+(3-2)\pmod{3}=3
, so
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{z})=4
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
\mathbf{S}
is well-defined.

Proof.

Consider the fundamental domain given by moving up by
1/n
in the
y
-direction, which corresponds to vertical rotation of the rows. This will not change the
p
-coordinates of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbb{O}}
or
\mathbf{x}
, so we get that
\mathbf{S}
is well-defined under vertical rotation.

Consider the fundamental domain given by moving right by
1/pn
in the
x
-direction, which corresponds to horizontal rotation of the columns. This shift will decrease both
a^{\mathbf{x}}_{\sigma^{-1}(0)}
and
a_{\sigma^{-1}(0)}^{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbb{O}}}
by one, and preserve all the other
p
-coordinates, so will preserve
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{x})
.
∎

4.2.
Maslov grading

We will follow
[
2
]
. To define the
Maslov grading
(and the
Alexander grading
, later), we need several auxiliary functions. It is unsurprising that we need to give coordinates to our generators to compute combinatorial gradings, and the first two functions do that. The third function compares sets of coordinate pairs.

Fix a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
, with lower-left corner at
k/pn,l/n
. We use the function

\displaystyle W:\Big\{\text{finite sets}

\displaystyle\text{ of points in $\mathbb{G}$}\Big\}

\displaystyle\to\Big\{\text{finite sets of pairs $(a,b)\in[0,pn)\times[0,n)$}\Big\}

which takes a set of points in
\mathbb{G}
and computes its coordinates with respect to the basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by
\{\overrightarrow{v_{1}}=(\frac{1}{np},0),\overrightarrow{v_{2}}=(\frac{-q}{np},\frac{1}{n})\}
, then subtracts
k
from each
x
coordinate and
l
from each
y
coordinate. In effect, this computes coordinates as if the fundamental domain had lower left corner at the origin.

We compute this above function on the example generator
\mathbf{z}
from Figure
2.1
given by darkened circles. We have
W(\mathbf{z})=\{(13,0),(2,1),(9,2)\}
.

Next, we define a function

\displaystyle C_{p,q}:\Big\{\text{finite sets}

\displaystyle\text{ of pairs $(a,b)\in[0,pn)\times[0,n)$}\Big\}

\displaystyle\to\Big\{\text{finite sets of pairs $(a,b)\in[0,pn)\times[0,pn)$}\Big\}.

It is defined on an
n
-tuple of coordinates
\{(a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
by

C_{p,q}(\{(a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=0}^{n-1}):=\{((a_{i}+nqk)\mod np,b_{i}+nk)_{i=0,k=0}^{i=n-1,k=p-1}\}.

This corresponds to taking the coordinates of a generator
\mathbf{x}
and lifting them to the coordinates of a generator
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
of the chain complex associated to
\tilde{L}
in
S^{3}
, the lift of
L
in the universal cover of
L(p,q)
. The effect of this function can be seen in Figure
4.1
.

We will often conflate a generator
\mathbf{x}
and its coordinates
W(\mathbf{x})
, and we will do the same for
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
and
C_{p,q}(W(\mathbf{x}))
.

Note that the output of
C_{p,q}
is
p
horizontal strips of points, each with
y
-coordinates between
nk
and
n(k+1)
for
0\leq k<p
. We will occasionally want to refer to each such strip, so will denote each by
C_{p,q}^{k}(\mathbf{x}):=\{((a_{i}+nqk)\pmod{np},b_{i}+nk)\}_{i=0}^{i=n-1}
.

For the generator
\mathbf{z}
of Figure
2.1
given by darkened circles, we can compute algebraically or via inspection of Figure
4.1
that

\displaystyle C_{p,q}(W(\mathbf{z}))=\{

\displaystyle(13,0),(2,1),(9,2),(4,3),(8,4),(0,5),(10,6),(14,7),

\displaystyle(6,8),(1,9),(5,10),(12,11),(7,12),(11,13),(3,14)\}.

The third and final of these auxiliary functions is
\mathcal{I}
, defined in
[
6
]
, which takes in two sets of coordinate pairs,
A
and
B
. It is defined by

\mathcal{I}(A,B):=\#\{\big((a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\big)\in A\times B:a_{1}<b_{1}\text{ and }a_{2}<b_{2}\}.

With these auxiliary functions in hand, we can define a function
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}
by

\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})-\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})+\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbb{O}}),

and the
Maslov grading
on a generator by
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x})}{p}+d(p,q,q-1)+1
, where
d(p,q,q-1)
is a recursively defined correction term whose definition can be found in
[
2
]
.

As a grading, we can extend
\mathbf{M}
to all of

by declaring
\mathbf{M}(V_{i}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})-2
for
0\leq i<n
.

We can now compute the Maslov grading of the generator
\mathbf{z}
of Figure
2.1
. We get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=52

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})

\displaystyle=55

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=40

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})

\displaystyle=42.

Asserting that
d(5,2,1)=-\frac{2}{5}
, we get that
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{5}(52-55-40+42)-\frac{2}{5}+1=\frac{2}{5}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The function
\mathbf{M}
is well-defined.

Proof.

Fix a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
, and let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
. Denote by
a
the component of
\mathbf{x}
with
W(a)=(r,0)
. We will denote by
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}
the same generator and component, but considered with respect to the fundamental domain given by moving up by
1/n
in the
y
-direction.
Note that for all components of
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the
x
-coordinate remains the same after this shift, and for all components except
a
, the
y
-coordinate decreases by 1.
We see that
a^{\prime}
has coordinates
(r,n-1)
.

For any
b\in\mathbf{x}
with first coordinate less than
r
, we have that
(b,a^{\prime})
contributes to
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
but
(b,a)
does not contribute to
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
. Correspondingly, if
b
has first coordinate greater than
r
, then
(a,b)
contributes to
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
but
(a^{\prime},b)
does not contribute to
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. Thus we have that
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-(n-r-1)+r=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+2r+1-n
. In shifting up the fundamental domain by
1/n
, we have also changed the coordinates of one

. If it originally had coordinates
(l+1/2,1/2)
, then a similar analysis yields
\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O}^{\prime},\mathbb{O}^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbb{O})+2l+1-n
. For the last two terms, we can break up the analysis into one more step to yield

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbb{O})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbb{O})-(n-r)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbb{O}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbb{O})+(l+1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbb{O}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbb{O})+r+l+1-n,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x})+r

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-(n-l-1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x})+r+l+1-n.

In the end, we get equality in the sum:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbb{O}^{\prime})+\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O}^{\prime},\mathbb{O}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})+2r+1-n)-(\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x})+r+l+1-n)

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbb{O})+r+l+1-n)+(\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbb{O})+2l+1-n)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbb{O})+\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\mathbb{O}).

Further, this sum is well defined for any strip
C_{p,q}^{k}(\mathbf{x})
and
C_{p,q}^{k}(\mathbb{O})
. Finally, because the vertical shift only changes the
y
-coordinates of any point and not the

-coordinates, we get that two points in
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\cup\tilde{\mathbb{O}}
from different strips contribute to
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x})
if and only if the corresponding points in
\tilde{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\cup\tilde{\mathbb{O}^{\prime}}
contribute to
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, as their
x
-coordinates are the same, and their
y
-coordinates satisfy the same inequality. Thus,
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Let’s show the same is true for a horizontal shift of the fundamental domain to the right by
1/pn
. Instead of proving this strip by strip, let’s look at the lift of the generator and markings to prove this case. There is one component of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
with
x
-coordinate 0; suppose it has
y
-coordinate
s
. Similarly, there is one component of
\tilde{\mathbb{O}}
with
x
-coordinate 1/2; suppose it has
y

1/2+t
. We will assume that
s\geq t
. As in the first case, let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
be the same generator, but with coordinates given with respect to the fundamental domain shifted to the right by
1/pn
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}^{\prime}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-(n-t-1)+s.

4.3.
Alexander grading

The third and final grading comes, in its most general form, as a filtration on the chain complex. Throughout this paper, however, we have restricted ourselves to a differential which counts rectangles which do not contain any markings in
\mathbb{X}
in their interior, which is the differential on the associated graded complex coming from the filtration. As such, on the complex
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
, we get a grading, called the Alexander grading.

In an analogous way to what we will here call
\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{O}}
defined above, we can define
\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{X}}
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
, we then define
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{O}}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbf{x})
. Recall that
L
is a link with
\ell
components. The
Alexander grading
of a generator
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
is defined by

(4.3)

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{x})-(n-\ell)}{2}.

We extend this to all of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
by declaring
\mathbf{A}(V_{i}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})-1
. It is clear that
\mathbf{A}
is well-defined, as
\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{X}}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{O}}
are well-defined by Lemma
4.2
.

Let us compute the Alexander grading of the generator
\mathbf{z}
of Figure
2.1
. We compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=52

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}},\tilde{\mathbb{X}})

\displaystyle=67

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{X}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=52

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{X}},\tilde{\mathbb{X}})

\displaystyle=62.

We recall that
d(5,2,1)=-\frac{2}{5}
, so
\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{5}(52-67-52+62)-\frac{2}{5}+1=-\frac{2}{5}
. Thus we have
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{2}{5}-(-\frac{2}{5})=\frac{4}{5}
, and
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\frac{4}{5}-(3-1)}{2}=-\frac{3}{5}
.

4.4.
Embedded parallelograms

The above gradings behave well with respect to embedded parallelograms. This was proved in
[
4
]
, but we recreate the proof here slightly more generally, as we will adapt it to study embedded pentagons later.

Proposition 4.4

(cf.
[
4
, Proposition 2.13]
)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}
are connected by an embedded parallelogram

. Then:

(1)

\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{y})

(2)

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})+1-2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n_{O_{i}}(r)

(3)

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n_{X_{i}}(r)-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n_{O_{i}}(r)
.

Proof.

To prove (
1
), recall that Remark
3.3
tells us if
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are connected by an empty parallelogram
r
, then
a^{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=a^{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
for all but at most two
p
-coordinates
0\leq k<n
. Further, if
\tau_{r}=(i\;j)
, then
a^{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-a^{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=a^{\mathbf{y}}_{j}-a^{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
. Plugging these values in to the definition of
\mathbf{S}
tells us that
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{y})
.

To simplify notation in the proof of (
2
), let

. One could hope that the Maslov grading behaves on each of the
p
strips as it does on a grid diagram for
S^{3}
, so we would get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})+p

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\mathbb{O})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}},\mathbb{O})+ps

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{O},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})+ps.

Sadly it does not, as Figure
4.1
demonstrates. Instead of trying to relate the Maslov gradings of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
one strip at a time, we will do it one empty parallelogram at a time, in the following sense.

Fix a fundamental domain for
T^{2}
, and consider
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
, which are connected by
p
parallelograms, denoted
\tilde{r}=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{p}\}
as in Figure
4.1
. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i}
denote the result of applying
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{i}\}
to
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
, so
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{0}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{p}=\mathbf{y}
. Note that the
\mathbb{O}
and
\mathbb{X}
markings are fixed. Finally, we will abuse notation to write
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})-\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})-\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})+\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})
.

We will tackle three cases, depending on how
r_{i}
sits on the fundamental domain. Suppose
r_{i}
does not wrap around the diagram, i.e. it is an embedded disk in the fundamental domain as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})+1
. Furthermore, we have
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})+s
, and similarly
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})+s
. As a result,
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}^{i-1})=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}^{i})+1-2s
.

Suppose on the fundamental domain
r_{i}
wraps horizontally around the diagram, i.e. is the disjoint union of two embedded disks which border the curves in
\overrightarrow{\beta}
which have coordinates
0
and
pn
. Each of these embedded disks covers the same number of rows (consider on the fundamental domain as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), which we will denote
l
.

Each row must have a component of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1}
on it, and since the rectangle is empty, there must be
l-1
components in the complement of
r_{i}
in the horizontal annulus containing those
l
rows. Each such component counts twice towards
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})
but not at all towards
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})
. Furthermore, the corners of
r_{i}
contribute once to
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})
but not to
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})
. Therefore, we get
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})=\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})-1-2(l-1)
.

Similarly, there is one marking per row, so there are
l
markings contained in the horizontal annulus containing those
l
rows. If a marking is contained in
r_{i}
, it contributes equally to
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})
and
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})
. There are
l-s
markings contained in the annulus but not in
r_{i}
; each contributes to
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})
and
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})
but not to
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})
or
\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})
, so we get

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})-1-2(l-1))-(\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})-(l-s))-(\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})-(l-s))+\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\mathbb{O}},\tilde{\mathbb{O}})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})+1-2s.

Suppose on the fundamental domain
r_{i}
wraps vertically around the diagram, i.e. is the disjoint union of two embedded disks which border the curves in
\overrightarrow{\alpha}
which have coordinates
0
and
n
. Then the analogous argument gives us that again
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i-1})=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{i})+1-2s
.

If we choose a fundamental domain such that some rectangle has lower left corner at
(0,0)
, we ensure that no rectangle wraps both horizontally and vertically. Thus we have that
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{0})=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{p})+p-2ps=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{y})+p-2ps
, so

, as desired.

We can conclude (
3

\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbf{y})+1-2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n_{X_{i}}(r)

by a similar argument, and combining this with Definition

gives us that

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n_{X_{i}}(r)-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n_{O_{i}}(r).\qed

5.

By Theorem

if and only if they are related by a finite sequence of moves called commutations, stabilizations, and destabilizations. After defining these moves, we will provide explicit homotopy equivalences between the chain complexes associated to two grids connected by such a move. Throughout, let
\mathbb{G}
be a grid diagram with grid number

5.1.
Commutations

We will discuss in detail the case of a commutation of columns, and at the end explain how this can be modified to work for a commutation of rows. Consider a pair of consecutive columns in
\mathbb{G}
with the first column bounded by
\beta_{i-1}
and
\beta_{i}
, and the second column by
\beta_{i}
and
\beta_{i+1}

do not interleave
if there is some point on
\beta_{i}
such that when traveling along
\beta_{i}
starting from that point we pass the regions of

containing both of the markings from one column, then the regions containing both of the markings from the other column.

If the markings in these two columns do not interleave, we can perform a
commutation move
, which consists of exchanging the markings in the first column with those in the second. Let
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
be the result of applying a commutation move to
\mathbb{G}

be the link specified by
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}

,

3.4
we have that
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})\simeq(CF^{-}(L(p,q),L),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})
and
(C^{-}(\mathbb{G}^{\prime}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})\simeq(CF^{-}(L(p,q),L^{\prime}),\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-^{\prime}})

In this section we will recall the definition of a sign assignment on a grid diagram, and present a few results of

Definition 6.1

(
[
4
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

Given a grid diagram
\mathbb{G}
, let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}
. Let
r_{1}\in\PG(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})
,
r_{2}\in\PG(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{z})
,
r_{3}\in\PG(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
, and
r_{4}\in\PG(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
. Then a
sign assignment
on
\mathbb{G}
is a function

\mathcal{S}:\PG(\mathbb{G})\to\{\pm 1\}

satisfying the following three properties:

(S1)

If
r_{1}*r_{2}=r_{3}*r_{4}
and
\mathbf{t}\neq\mathbf{w}
, then
\mathcal{S}(r_{1})\mathcal{S}(r_{2})=-\mathcal{S}(r_{3})\mathcal{S}(r_{4})
.

(S2)

If
r_{1}*r_{2}
is a row, then
\mathcal{S}(r_{1})\mathcal{S}(r_{2})=1
.

(S3)

If
r_{1}*r_{2}
is a column, then
\mathcal{S}(r_{1})\mathcal{S}(r_{2})=-1
.

In
[
4
]
, it is proved that on grid diagrams for links in lens spaces, sign assignments exist, and are unique (up to homology).

Theorem 6.2

(
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Sign assignments exist on all grids representing a link
L\subset L(p,q)
. Moreover, the sign-refined grid homology does not depend on the choice of a sign assignment.

The above theorem is constructive, but we will only use the above three properties of any sign assignment, so we refer the reader to
[
4
]
for an example of how to explicitly define a sign assignment.

, the definition of grid homology for twisted toroidal grid diagrams was extended to integer coefficients, using a sign assignment. After recalling the definition, we prove that this homology is a link invariant.

For
L\subset L(p,q)
, define
C^{-}(\mathbb{G};\mathbb{Z})
as follows. Let
\mathbb{G}
be a grid diagram for a link
L
with
\ell
components, and fix a sign assignment
\mathcal{S}
on
\mathbb{G}
. Let
C^{-}(\mathbb{G};\mathbb{Z})
be the free
\mathbb{Z}[V_{0},\ldots,V_{n-1}]
module generated by
\mathcal{G}
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
, define

\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
r\cap\mathbb{X}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}(r)(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}V_{i}^{n_{O_{i}}(r)})\mathbf{y}.

That this is a differential and thus defines a chain complex comes from the first axiom of a sign assignment, and the alternate decompositions in the proof of Proposition
3.6
.

We note that we really should denote the differential by
\partial_{\mathbb{X},\mathcal{S}}^{-}
, but Theorem
6.2
, proven in
[
4
]
, proves that the differentials assigned to any two sign assignments on the same grid define quasi-isomorphic chain complexes.

Furthermore, we will prove the link invariant is the quasi-isomorphism type of
C^{-}(\mathbb{G};\mathbb{Z})
as a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]
, where each variable corresponds to the action of a distinct component of the link. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose
O_{i}
and
O_{j}
are markings corresponding to the same component of
K
. Then multiplication by
V_{i}
is chain homotopic to multiplication by
V_{j}
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as that of Lemma
3.9
, adapted to integral coefficients. Define
\Phi_{X_{a}}:C^{-}(\mathbb{G})\to C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
on a generator
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
by

\Phi_{X_{a}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
\Int(r)\cap\mathbb{X}=X_{a}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}(r)V_{o}^{n_{O_{0}}(r)}\cdots UV{n-1}^{n_{O_{n-1}}(r)}\mathbf{y}.

Extend this linearly to
C^{-}(\mathbb{G};\mathbb{Z})
. The only difference is that now the annulus of width one which connects a generator
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
to itself is counted with coefficient
-1
, and the annulus of height one is counted with coefficient
1
. Thus, using
-\Phi_{X_{a}}
as our homotopy, we get that
-\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}\circ\Phi_{X_{a}}-\Phi_{X_{a}}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}=V_{i}-V_{j}
.
∎

To show that this is a link invariant, not just dependent on the grid representing the link, requires showing that the commutation and stabilization maps defined before are compatible with the sign assignments.

7.1.
Commutations

Before we define our desired chain map, we need a way to assign signs to pentagons. We are equipped with a way to assign signs to parallelograms, so we define a map which takes a pentagon on the combined grid diagram and returns a parallelogram. Suppose, as in Section
5.1
, that
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
are two grid diagrams connected by a commutation of columns. Note that the only thing which has changed is the position of the markings, and a sign assignment doesn’t depend on the position of the markings, so there is a bijection between sign assignments on
\mathbb{G}
and on
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
. Fix a common sign assignment and denote it
\mathcal{S}
.

Let
a
denote the intersection point of
\beta
and
\gamma
which is included in every pentagon counted by
\Phi_{\beta\gamma}
. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. The component
y^{\prime}\in\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
which lays on
\gamma
can be slid along the
\alpha\in\overrightarrow{\alpha}
curve it occupies, without crossing any other
\overrightarrow{\beta}
curves, to a unique intersection point of that
\alpha
and
\beta
, which we will call
y
. Define the generator
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}
by replacing
y^{\prime}
with
y
in
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
. Bounded by this
\alpha
arc, the arc of
\beta
connecting
y
to
a
, and the arc of
\gamma
connecting
a
to
y^{\prime}
is an embedded triangle, which we will denote
t_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}
. We could do the same process in reverse to
\mathbf{x}
to get
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and
t_{\mathbf{x}}
. If we have
s\in\Pent^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
, then either
s*t_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
or
t_{\mathbf{x}}*s\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
. There is a natural one-to-one correspondence between
\PG^{\circ}(\mathbb{G})
and
\PG^{\circ}(\mathbb{G}^{\prime})
, which if both of the above parallelograms exists, maps them to each other. Thus we can define
R(s)
to be this parallelogram, which we will take to live in
\PG^{\circ}(\mathbb{G})
.

We call a pentagon a left (respectively right) pentagon if it has multiplicity one to the left of

(respectively right), or, equivalently, if the above defined parallelogram is a left (respectively right) parallelogram.

For
s\in\Pent^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
, define a sign assignment on pentagons denoted
\mathcal{S}_{\text{pent}}
by

\mathcal{S}_{\text{pent}}(s):=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})+1}\mathcal{S}(R(s))&\text{if $s$ is a left pentagon,}\\
(-1)^{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})}\mathcal{S}(R(s))&\text{if $s$ is a right pentagon.}\end{cases}

We will abuse notation and denote the above just
\mathcal{S}
.

Define
\Phi_{\beta\gamma,\mathcal{S}}:C^{-}(\mathbb{G})\to C^{-}(\mathbb{G}^{\prime})
on generators
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}
by

\Phi_{\beta\gamma,\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\Pent^{\circ}_{\beta\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
p\cap\mathbb{X}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}(p)V_{0}^{n_{O_{0}}(p)}\cdots V_{n-1}^{n_{O_{n-1}}(p)}\mathbf{y}.

We will abuse notation and leave the sign assignment implicit, denoting the above map by
\Phi_{\beta\gamma}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

The map
\Phi_{\beta\gamma}
is a chain map.

Proof.

This is similar to the proof for the case of knots in
S^{3}
(
[
9
, Lemma 15.3.3]
].

As in Lemma
5.6
, we want to prove
\Phi_{\beta\gamma}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}=\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}\circ\Phi_{\beta\gamma}
, so we again consider domains which are the juxtaposition of a pentagon and a parallelogram or are the juxtaposition of a parallelogram and a pentagon. Each such domain is given with an alternate decomposition in the proof of Lemma
5.6
. For the domains of Figure
5.2
, Figure
5.3
, and Figure
5.6
, we have that the domain
\varphi
connecting
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{z}
has alternate decompositions as
\varphi=r*s
and
\varphi=s^{\prime}*r^{\prime}
for some
r\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})
,
s\in\Pent^{\circ}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{z})
,
s^{\prime}\in\Pent^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}^{\prime})
, and
r^{\prime}\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime},\mathbf{z})
for some generators
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. We have that
r*R(s)=R(s^{\prime})*r^{\prime}
, so
\mathcal{S}(r)\mathcal{S}(R(s))=-\mathcal{S}(R(s^{\prime}))\mathcal{S}(r^{\prime})
. Both
s
and
s^{\prime}
are right pentagons in the displayed domains, but the analysis is the same when they are both left pentagons. The Maslov degrees of the initial generators of
s
and

differ by one, since
\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}
is degree
-1
with respect to Maslov grading, so we get that
\mathcal{S}(r)\mathcal{S}(s)=\mathcal{S}(s^{\prime})\mathcal{S}(r^{\prime})
, proving our desired equality in this case.

For the domains of Figure
5.5
and Figure
5.8
, we have the same decompositions as above. If, however, we have
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{G}
as defined by sliding
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
along a curve
\alpha\in\overrightarrow{\alpha}
as described above, then
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{t}
, so we no longer appeal to the first axiom of a sign assignment. Instead, we have that
\mathcal{S}(r)\mathcal{S}(R(s))=\mathcal{S}(R(s^{\prime}))\mathcal{S}(r^{\prime})
. The Maslov degrees of the initial generators of
s
and
s^{\prime}
differ by one, and
s
and
s^{\prime}
are pentagons of different sides. The effect of these differences cancels out, and we get
\mathcal{S}(r)\mathcal{S}(s)=\mathcal{S}(s^{\prime})\mathcal{S}(r^{\prime})
, again proving our desired equality in this case.

For the domains in Figure
5.4
and Figure
5.7
, we have that the domain
\varphi
has alternate decompositions as
\varphi=r*s
and
\varphi=r^{\prime}*s^{\prime}
for some
r\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})
,
s\in\Pent^{\circ}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{z})
,
r^{\prime}\in\PG^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
and
s^{\prime}\in\Pent^{\circ}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
for some generators
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{G}
(or has two decompositions as the juxtaposition of a pentagon and parallelogram, but the analysis is the same). In this case, we have
\mathcal{S}(r)\mathcal{S}(R(s))=-\mathcal{S}(r^{\prime})\mathcal{S}(R(s^{\prime}))
. The Maslov degrees of the initial generators of
s
and
s^{\prime}
are the same, and they are same-sided pentagons, so
\mathcal{S}(r)\mathcal{S}(s)=-\mathcal{S}(r^{\prime})\mathcal{S}(s^{\prime})
. Therefore, these terms drop out from one side of our desired equality.

For the domains in Figure
5.9
, regardless of the decomposition, after mapping each pentagon to its appropriate parallelogram on the diagram, we get that each decomposition forms a column, so is counted with coefficient
-1
. The pentagons, however, are on different sides. If the decompositions are of the same type, then, they are counted with opposite sign and cancel in the sum. If they are of opposite type, then the Maslov degrees of the initial generators differ by one, so we get that the decompositions are counted with the same sign, proving our desired equality.
∎

We will show that
\Phi_{\beta\gamma}
is a chain homotopy equivalence, with
-\Phi_{\gamma\beta}
as a homotopy inverse, with a homotopy provided by
-\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}
, after defining these maps with signs below.

First, we need a way to assign signs to hexagons. Each hexagon in
\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}
contains an arc of

which borders a bigon between

and

, and define
\mathcal{S}(h)=\mathcal{S}(R(h))
, by an abuse of notation. Now we can define
\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta,\mathcal{S}}:C^{-}(\mathbb{G})\to C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
by

\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta,\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in\Hex^{\circ}_{\beta\gamma\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
\Int(h)\cap\mathbb{X}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}(h)V_{0}^{n_{O_{0}}(h)}\cdots V_{n-1}^{n_{O_{n-1}}(h)}\mathbf{y}.

We will abuse notation and denote the above by
\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

The map
\Phi_{\beta\gamma}
is a chain homotopy equivalence.

Proof.

Again, this is similar to the proof for the case of knots in
S^{3}
(
[
9

].

We are trying to prove
\Id+\Phi_{\gamma\beta}\circ\Phi_{\beta\gamma}+\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}\circ\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}+\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}=0
as maps on
C^{-}(\mathbb{G})
. We can follow Lemma
5.9
and characterize a portion of the above equation as

(7.4)

(\Phi_{\gamma\beta}\circ\Phi_{\beta\gamma}+\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-}\circ\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}+\mathscr{H}_{\beta\gamma\beta}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{X}}^{-})(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\pi^{\circ}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\\
\Int(\varphi)\cap\mathbb{X}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}N(\varphi)V_{0}^{n_{O_{0}}(\varphi)}\cdots V_{n-1}^{n_{O_{n-1}}(\varphi)}\mathbf{z},

where this time,

is the signed number of decompositions of
\varphi

N(\varphi)=0
for
m\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, and

for

The cases for

are very similar to the proof of Lemma
7.2

For the case

5.17

or

is counted with coefficient

. For decompositions of columns contributing to

(-1)^{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})}(-1)^{\mathbf{M}(\Phi_{\beta\gamma}(\mathbf{x}))}
(respectively
(-1)^{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})+1}(-1)^{\mathbf{M}(\Phi_{\beta\gamma}(\mathbf{x}))+1}

preserves Maslov grading, we have that the sign differs by a factor of 1, so the column is still counted with coefficient
-1
.

Thus, as desired we have (

, which connects it to
\mathbf{x}
with coefficient
-1
, so this map is the negative identity.
∎

Proposition 7.5
.

Suppose
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}

and

\mathbb{Z}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}]

Suppose the diagrams differ by a commutation of columns. Then the chain map

∎

Analyzing possible placements of
