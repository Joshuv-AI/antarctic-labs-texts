Abstract
. In this paper we consider the variational setting for SPDE on a Gelfand triple ( V , H , V ∗ ) (V,H,V^{*}) . Under the standard conditions on a linear coercive pair ( A , B ) (A,B) , and a symmetry condition on A A we manage to extrapolate the classical L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} -estimates in time to L p \mathrm{L}^{p} -estimates for some p > 2 p>2 without any further conditions on ( A , B ) (A,B) . As a consequence we obtain several other a priori regularity results of the paths of the solution. Under the assumption that V V embeds compactly into H H , we derive a universal compactness result quantifying over all ( A , B ) (A,B) . As an application of the compactness result we prove global existence of weak solutions to a system of second order quasi-linear equations.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we consider stochastic evolution equations in the variational setting. This is the stochastic version of Lions classical setting
[
22
]
and goes back to the work of
[
5
,
20
,
27
]
. For details we refer the reader to the monographs
[
23
,
30
]
.

This paper consists of two main parts. In the first part we prove an extrapolation result, which
improves the usual a priori regularity estimates for linear equations with operators that satisfy the usual coercivity conditions. As a consequence we derive a new type of compactness result for linear equations, which is universal in the sense that for given constants in the estimates it varies over all admissible operators and data.
In the second part we apply the compactness result to obtain global existence for a class of stochastic parabolic systems which is not well-understood yet.

The triple
(V,H,V^{*})
are Hilbert spaces such that
V\hookrightarrow H\hookrightarrow V^{*}
densely, where we identify the Hilbert space
H

with its dual, and where
V^{*}
is the dual with respect to the inner product of
H
. The stochastic equations we consider can be written as:

(LP)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}u(t)+A(t)u(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t&=f(t)\,\,\mathrm{d}t+(B(t)u(t)+g(t))\,\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\\
u(0)&=u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here
A:(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})
and
B:(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)
, and
W
is a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion, where
U
is a real separable Hilbert space. The time
T>0
is finite. For the inhomogeneities
(f,g,u_{0})
we assume
f:(0,T)\times\Omega\to V^{*}
,
g:(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)
and
u_{0}:\Omega\to H
, and the standard measurability conditions are supposed to be satisfied.

Consider the following condition on the pair
(A,B)
.

Assumption 1.1
.

There exist constants
\Lambda,\lambda>0
and
M\geq 0
such that pointwise in
[0,T]\times\Omega
for all
v\in V
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\,\langle Av,v\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|Bv\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\|v\|_{V}^{2}-M\|v\|_{H}^{2}

(coercivity),

\displaystyle\|Av\|_{V^{*}}\leq\Lambda\|v\|_{V}\quad

\displaystyle\&\quad\|Bv\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)}^{2}\leq\Lambda\|v\|_{V}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(boundedness)}.

Under Assumption
1.1
it is standard that (
LP
) has a unique strong solution
u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;V)\cap\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)
a.s. and the following a priori estimate holds for a constant

only depending on

,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}+\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)}\leq C\big[\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;H)}&+\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);V^{*})}\\
&+\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))}\big].\end{aligned}

In the deterministic setting it follows from
[
13
]
that one can find some
p_{0}>2
depending on

,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and

such that for every
p\in[2,p_{0}]
there exists a constant

such that

(2)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}\leq C_{p}\big[\|u_{0}\|_{(H,V)_{1-\frac{2}{p}}}

\displaystyle+\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V^{*})}\big].

Actually this even holds true outside the setting of Hilbert space and variational problems. On the other hand, (
2
) does not hold for all
p\in(2,\infty)
.
In
[
13
]
the estimate (
2
) is used to obtain a compactness result, which in turn is used to prove global existence of solutions to a quasi-linear parabolic PDE.
On the other hand, if

does not depend on time, then (
2
) holds for all

(see
[

, Theorem 17.2.26 (4)]
). A stochastic version of an extrapolation result holds if

(or small), and was proved in
[
24
]
. In the general case

the result is false since the
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
-integrability can fail to hold (see
[
10
]
) and a more restrictive coercivity condition is needed (see
[

]
). However, it would be interesting to obtain some result with mixed integrability
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V))
in case of general

satisfying Assumption

and any

.

To prove (
2
) one can use Sneiberg’s lemma for complex interpolation. In the latter argument one needs an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X_{i},Y_{i})
, where
(X_{0},X_{1})
and
(Y_{0},Y_{1})
are Banach couples. By complex interpolation also
T\in\mathcal{L}(X_{\theta},Y_{\theta})
for all
\theta\in(0,1)
. Sneiberg’s result states that the interval of
\theta\in(0,1)
for which

is invertible is open. In the deterministic setting one can take
X_{i}={}_{0}\mathrm{W}^{1,p_{i}}(0,T;V^{*})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p_{i}}(0,T;V)
and

with
1<p_{0}<2<p_{1}<\infty
and

. The boundedness of

is trivial. Now if one takes
\theta\in(0,1)
such that
\frac{1-\theta}{p_{0}}=\frac{\theta}{p_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}
, then

is invertible by (

) for

and with

. Thus, Sneiberg’s lemma provides us with an open interval around this value of

, which yields the required result. As far as we could see it seems impossible to define an operator

in the stochastic framework
so that the above steps can be completed.

New estimates for the linear problem

In this paper we circumvent the above problem at the prize of a symmetry condition on
A
, and prove the following result, where we emphasise that we do not need any regularity conditions on
(A,B)
.

Theorem 1.2

(Extrapolation of integrability and regularity)
.

Suppose that Assumption
1.1
holds and that on
[0,T]\times\Omega
and for
u,v\in V
the
symmetry condition
\langle Au,v\rangle=\langle Av,u\rangle
holds.
Then there exists
p_{0}>2
only depending on
\Lambda,\lambda,T\vee 1
and
M
such that for all
p\in[2,p_{0}]
,
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);V^{*})
,
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))
and
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}(\Omega;(H,V)_{1-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}},p})
, problem (
LP
)
admits a unique solution
u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)
. Moreover, for
\delta\in(1/p,1/2)
and
\theta\in[0,1/2)
we have

u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathrm{C}^{\delta-\frac{1}{p}}([0,T];[H,V]_{1-2\delta})),\ \ \text{and}\ \ u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathrm{C}([0,T];(H,V)_{1-\frac{2}{p},p})).

In the above we used the notation
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\theta}
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta,p}
for complex and real interpolation, respectively. The main novelties are
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-integrability in
\Omega\times(0,T)
and the improved smoothness of the paths, which is much better than the classical path space
\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)
used in (
1
).

Theorem
1.2
is a special case of Theorem
2.2
, where additional linear estimates are stated as well. As mentioned, we cannot use Sneiberg’s lemma
to prove Theorem
1.2
. Instead we will use abstract Stein interpolation. The proof was inspired by the recent work
[
7
]
on
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-bounds for heat semigroups.

The symmetry condition can be written equivalently as
A^{*}=A
. We do not know if the symmetry condition is needed in Theorem
1.2
. It is certainly not necessary. For many operators
A
satisfying Assumption
1.1
, one can always apply Theorem
1.2
to its symmetric part
\frac{A+A^{*}}{2}
and use a perturbation argument
A=\frac{A+A^{*}}{2}+R
with
R=\frac{A-A^{*}}{2}
(see
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
). The only thing which needs to be checked is the following relative boundedness condition: for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

\|Rv\|_{V^{*}}\leq\varepsilon\|v\|_{V}+C_{\varepsilon}\|v\|_{H}.

For instance for
2m
-th elliptic differential operators with smooth coefficients in space the highest order differentiation cancels out and
R
turns out to be of order
2m-1
, and thus it satisfies the above relative boundedness condition.

Theorem
1.2
implies that the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-theory in
[
1
]
can be extended to
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-theory for some range
p\in(2,p_{0}]
. Although this leads to minimal changes in the proof, it has many consequences for applications since for several problems it turns out that any

is sufficient. For instance this occurs for SPDEs in dimension

. Due to the fact that taking for instance
H=\mathrm{H}^{1}
,

and

, the space

does not embed into
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
as this instance of the Sobolev embedding fails. Using
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-theory instead one only needs that

embeds into
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}

. We will investigate these applications in a subsequent paper.

Universal compactness

Thanks to Theorem
1.2
we obtain a compactness/tightnesss result for the solution mapping
(f,g,u_{0})\mapsto u
for (
LP
) for the path space
\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)
. We expect that this has many consequences. In earlier works tightness of laws is obtained with
H
replaced by
V^{*}
or even larger spaces for different but related settings (cf.
[
11
]
and
[
29
]
). Tightness plays a key role in the stochastic compactness method (see
[
8
]
) for obtaining weak solutions. Using a smaller path space can give more information on the weak solution and its approximation.

Theorem 1.3

(Universal compactness for variational problems)
.

Suppose that the embedding
V\hookrightarrow H
is compact. Let
\lambda,\Lambda,T>0
,
M\geq 0
and
K>0
be fixed. Then there exists a
p_{0}>2
only depending on
\lambda,\Lambda,T\vee 1,M
such that for all
p\in(2,p_{0}]
, the laws
\{\mathscr{L}(u):u\}
on
C([0,T];H)
are tight, where
u
runs over all strong solutions to (
LP
) and all
(A,B)
satisfying Assumption
1.1
with
A=A^{*}
, and all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);V^{*})
,
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))
and
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}(\Omega;(H,V)_{1-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}},p})
satisfying
\|f\|\leq K
,
\|g\|\leq K
and
\|u_{0}\|\leq K
in the respective norms.

As before, the condition
A=A^{*}
can be weakened by a perturbation argument.

To illustrate the power of Theorem
1.3
we will show that it can be effectively used in the stochastic compactness method to prove global existence of a system of quasi-linear SPDEs (see Theorem
6.6
below). A technical issue in applying the stochastic compactness method is that the a.s. limit, obtained by tightness and the Skorohod embedding theorem, needs to be identified as a solution. The following advantages turn up as a consequence of the uniform estimate we obtain in the proof of Theorem
1.2
:

(i)

Tightness in
\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)
(if
V\hookrightarrow H
is compact);

(ii)

uniform estimates in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathrm{C}([0,T];H))
;

(iii)

uniform estimates in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)
.

Here we let
u
run over all solution with data
(A,B,f,g,u_{0})
as in Theorem
1.3
. As a consequence of (1) we obtain subsequences which converge in
\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)
. As a consequence of (2) and (3) we obtain weak compactness in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)
and uniform integrability of
\{\|u\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];H)}^{2}:u\}
and
\{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}^{2}:u\}
, where
u
runs over all strong solutions for the given data
(A,B,f,g,u_{0})

The uniform integrability can be effectively combined with Vitali’s convergence theorem.

u^{\alpha}&=0,\ \ &\text{ on }\partial D,\\
u^{\alpha}(0)&=u^{\alpha}_{0},\ \ &\text{ on }D.\end{aligned}\right.

We state a special case of Theorem

.

(Weak existence for quasi-linear systems)
.

Let

. Then there is

,

Besides that we are able to treat highly coupled systems, even in the case of scalar quasilinear equations the result of Theorem

contains new features:

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

Often when applying the stochastic compactness method only the torus is considered for simplicity. Actually, equations on domains with for instance Dirichlet boundary condition can lead to complications:

•

Bootstrapping regularity can be problematic due the presence of boundary values;

•

It is often unclear in what extrapolation space to formulate compactness/tightness.

Open problems

Given the well-posedness theory and the deterministic extrapolation results it is natural to state the following open problem.

Problem 1.5
.

Do Theorems

and
1.3
hold without the symmetry condition
A^{*}=A
?

The well-posedness theory of Lions is usually stated in the setting of nonlinear operators
(A,B)
satisfying a continuity, monotonicity and coercivity condition. We covered only
the linear setting in Theorems

and
1.3
. This leads to the following natural question.

Problem 1.6
.

Do Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
hold in the setting of nonlinear monotone operators
(A,B)
?

This problem seems to be open even in the deterministic setting.

The central finding of this section is that solutions to a complex family of variational problems have analytic dependence provided this was the case for the complex family, see Proposition

To ease notation, we introduce data and solution spaces for the variational setting.

Definition 3.1

Define the data spaces
\mathrm{E}_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);V^{*})
and
{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))
, and the solution space
\mathrm{E}_{1}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;\mathrm{C}([0,T];H))
, where the subscript
\mathcal{F}
indicates the subspace of progressively measurable functions.

3.1.
A (complex) variational setting

The following variational well-posedness result is well-known in its real formulation
[
23
]
. The complex version follows by
“forgetting”
the complex structure. For instance, we can interpret a complex vector space
H
as a real vector space if we equip it with the inner product
(\cdot|\cdot)_{H_{\mathbb{R}}}=\operatorname{Re}(\cdot|\cdot)_{H}
and so on.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{E}_{0}
,
g\in{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}
. Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds. Then (
P
) with
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}(H)
has a unique solution
u\in\mathrm{E}_{1}
, and there exists a constant
C_{L}
depending on
\Lambda
,
\lambda
,
M
, and
T\vee 1
, such that

(9)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}_{1}}\leq C_{L}\bigl(\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(H)}+\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}\bigr).

3.2.
Analytic dependence of solutions

In the following result we show analytic dependence of solutions.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be open and for
z\in O
let
A_{z}
and
B_{z}
be operator functions that satisfy Assumption
2.1
uniformly in
z
and that depend analytically on
z
in the uniform operator topology. Furthermore, let
f\in\mathrm{E}_{0}
,
g\in{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}
and
u_{0}\in H
. Then, for fixed
z
, the unique solution
u_{z}
of the problem

(10)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}u(t)+A_{z}(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+(B_{z}(t)u(t)+g(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad u(0)=u_{0},

gives rise to an analytic function
O\ni z\mapsto u_{z}\in\mathrm{E}_{1}
.

Proof.

Since we consider differences below, we have zero initial data in the equations in this proof. For fixed
z
, Proposition
3.2
yields a unique solution
u_{z}
of (
10
).

Step 1
:
O\ni z\mapsto u_{z}\in\mathrm{E}_{1}
is continuous.

Let
z\in O
and
h
small enough so that
z+h\in O
. The function
v\coloneqq u_{z+h}-u_{z}
is the unique solution of

(11)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}v(t)+A_{z+h}v(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=-(A_{z+h}-A_{z})u_{z}\,\mathrm{d}t+(B_{z+h}v(t)+(B_{z+h}-B_{z})u_{z})\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

Therefore, by Proposition
3.2
,

(12)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathrm{E}_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{L}\bigl(\|(A_{z+h}-A_{z})u_{z}\|_{\mathrm{E}_{0}}+\|(B_{z+h}-B_{z})u_{z}\|_{{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}\bigr).

Hence, continuity of
A_{z}
and
B_{z}
yield the claim.

Step 2
:
O\ni z\mapsto u_{z}\in\mathrm{E}_{1}
is analytic.

Write
D^{h}
for difference quotients of
u
,
A
, and
B
with respect to
z
, for example
D^{h}u=\frac{u(z+h)-u(z)}{h}
.
One has that
D^{h}u
is the unique solution of

(13)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}v(t)+A_{z}v\,\mathrm{d}t=-(D^{h}A_{z})u_{z+h}\,\mathrm{d}t+(B_{z}v+(D^{h}B_{z})u_{z+h})\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

We show that
D^{h}u
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{E}_{1}
. Then, by the very definition of complex differentiability,
z\mapsto u_{z}
is analytic. To this end, consider
h_{1}\neq h_{2}
. The difference
w\coloneqq D^{h_{1}}u-D^{h_{2}}u
solves

(14)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}w(t)+A_{z}w\,\mathrm{d}t=-\Bigl(\bigl(D^{h_{1}}A-D^{h_{2}}A\bigr)u_{z+h_{1}}+(D^{h_{2}}A)(u_{z+h_{1}}-u_{z+h_{2}})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

(15)

\displaystyle+\Bigl(B_{z}w+\bigl(D^{h_{1}}B-D^{h_{2}}B\bigr)u_{z+h_{1}}+(D^{h_{2}}B)(u_{z+h_{1}}-u_{z+h_{2}})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

A calculation using Proposition
3.2
shows

(16)

\displaystyle\|D^{h_{1}}u-D^{h_{2}}u\|_{\mathrm{E}_{1}}

(17)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{L}^{2}\Bigl(\|D^{h_{1}}A-D^{h_{2}}A\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}+\|D^{h_{1}}B-D^{h_{2}}B\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))}\Bigr)(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}})

(18)

\displaystyle\quad+C_{L}\Bigl(\|D^{h_{2}}A\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}+\|D^{h_{2}}B\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))}\Bigr)\|u_{z+h_{1}}-u_{z+h_{2}}\|_{\mathrm{E}_{1}}.

First, the difference quotients of
A
and
B
are Cauchy sequences for
A
and
B
are holomorphic. Second, the difference quotients of
A
and
B
are, again by analyticity, uniformly bounded. Third,
\|u_{z+h_{1}}-u_{z+h_{2}}\|_{\mathrm{E}_{1}}
tends to zero by Step 1. We conclude that
D^{h}u
is a Cauchy sequence as claimed.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be open and for
z\in O
let
A_{z}
and
B_{z}
be operator functions that satisfy Assumption
2.1
uniformly in
z
and that depend analytically on
z
in the uniform operator topology. Furthermore, let
f:O\to\mathrm{E}_{0}
,
g:O\to{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}
and
u_{0}:O\to H
be analytic. Then, for fixed
z
, the unique solution
u_{z}
of the problem

(19)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}u(t)+A_{z}(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=f_{z}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+(B_{z}(t)u(t)+g_{z}(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad u(0)=u_{0},

gives rise to an analytic function
O\ni z\mapsto u_{z}\in\mathrm{E}_{1}
.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.3
it follows that
S_{z}:O\to\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{E}_{0}\times{\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\times H,\mathrm{E}_{1})
given by
S_{z}=u_{z}
, where
u_{z}
is the solution to (
10
) is analytic in the strong operator topology. Therefore,
S
is analytic in the uniform operator topology (see
[
4
, Proposition A.3]
). Now the analyticity follows as the solution to (
19
) is given by
S_{z}(f_{z},g_{z},u_{0,z})
and is the composition of analytic functions.
∎

4.
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-estimates for small perturbations of the autonomous case

In this section, we establish
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-theory by means of a perturbation result with the autonomous case. The quantities in the assumption of Theorem
4.6
look cumbersome on the first glance, but we will see in Section
5
that they are, in fact, very closely tied to the concept of coercivity.

Throughout this section, fix some
p>2
. We extend Definition
3.1
.

Definition 4.1
.

Put
\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);V^{*})
and
{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))
for the data spaces and
\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;\mathrm{C}([0,T];(H,V)_{1-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}},p}))
for the solution space.

Let us introduce a reference operator for the perturbation argument.

Definition 4.2
.

Consider the operator
A_{0}\colon V\to V^{*}
given by
\langle A_{0}(u),v\rangle\coloneqq(u|v)_{V}
.

Remark 4.3
.

The operator
A_{0}
is invertible, positive and self-adjoint.

The following deterministic maximal
L^{p}
-regularity result holds.

Lemma 4.4
.

There is a constant
C_{p}
such that, for all
\mu>0
,
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V^{*})
, and
u
a strong solution to
u^{\prime}+(\mu A_{0})u=f
with
u(0)=0
, one has the a-priori estimate

(20)

\displaystyle\mu\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V^{*})}.

Proof.

The fact that
A_{0}
has maximal
L^{p}
-regularity on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
follows from de Simon’s result (see
[
18
, Corollary 17.3.8]
). From the proof of
[
18
, Theorem 17.2.26]
it follows that
\mu A_{0}
has maximal
L^{p}
-regularity on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with the same constant
C_{p}
. Therefore, by
[
18
, Lemma 17.2.16]
),
\mu A_{0}
has maximal
L^{p}
-regularity on
(0,T)
with the same constant
C_{p}
. This gives the desired bound with constant independent of
\mu

∎

Next we will present a similar result in the stochastic setting, but without the optimal scaling in
\mu
as this will not be needed later.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\mu>0
, the operator
\mu A_{0}
admits stochastic maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity, that is to say, there is
c(p,\mu)>0
such that for all
f\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}
and
g\in{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}
there is a unique strong solution
u\in\mathrm{E}_{1}
to

(21)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}u(t)+\mu A_{0}u\,\mathrm{d}t=f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}W(t),\qquad u(0)=0,

satisfying the estimate

(22)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}\leq c(p,\mu)\bigl(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}\bigr).

Proof.

Let
\mu>0
. By linearity and Lemma
4.4
it suffices to consider
f=0
. As
\mu A_{0}
is positive and self-adjoint on
V^{*}
, it is also positive and self-adjoint on
H
(see
[
26
, Proposition 1.24]
). Therefore, it has a bounded
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-calculus with constant
1

(see
[
17
, Proposition 10.2.23]
). Moreover,
H
is, as a separable Hilbert space, isomorphic to
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, from the definition of
A_{0}
and from
[
25
]
we obtain

\mu^{1/2}\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}=\|(\mu A_{0})^{1/2}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H)}\leq c(p)\|g\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H))}=c(p)\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}.

∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}
and
g\in{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}
. Employ Assumptions
2.1
with
M=0
and recall the constant
\Lambda(B)
. Suppose that there are
\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that

(i)

for some
\mu>0
one has
C_{p}\mu^{-1}\|A-\mu A_{0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}\leq 1-\varepsilon
, where
C_{p}
is the constant from Lemma
4.4
, and

(ii)

c\Lambda(B)\leq 1-\delta
, where
c=c(\Lambda,\mu,T,\varepsilon,p)
.

Then there exists a unique strong solution
u\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
to (
P
) with
u_{0}=0
satisfying the estimate

(23)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}\leq\frac{c}{\delta}\bigl(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}\bigr).

Proof.

We tacitly impose the initial condition
u(0)=0
whenever we speak about equations in this proof. First, we investigate the problem with
B=0
, that is to say, we want to show that there exists a unique strong solution
u\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
to the stochastic problem

(24)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}u(t)+A(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)

satisfying the estimate

(25)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}\leq c(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}})

with a constant
c
depending only on
\varepsilon
,
\Lambda
,
\mu
,
T\vee 1
, and
p
. This constant is the constant postulated in hypothesis
(ii)
of the theorem.

Step 1
: Reduction to an a-priori estimate.

For
\theta\in[0,1]
consider
A_{\theta}\coloneqq(1-\theta)\mu A_{0}+\theta A
. Since
A_{\theta}-\mu A_{0}=\theta(A-\mu A_{0})
, one has

C_{p}\mu^{-1}\|A_{\theta}-\mu A_{0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}=C_{p}\mu^{-1}\theta\|A-\mu A_{0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}\leq 1-\varepsilon.

Therefore, in the light of a stochastic version of the method of continuity (see
[
28
, Prop. 3.10]
), it suffices to show the a priori estimate (
25
) for any strong solution
u
of (
24
).

Step 2
: Show the a-priori estimate.

Let
u
a strong solution of (
24
). By Lemma
4.5
there is a strong solution
v\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
of

\,\mathrm{d}v(t)+(\mu A_{0})v(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)

satisfying the estimate

(26)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}\leq c(p,\mu)(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}).

Define
w\coloneqq u-v\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
. Fix
\omega\in\Omega
. Use the shorthand notation
w_{\omega}(t)=w(\omega,t)
, and so on. By construction,
w_{\omega}
is almost surely a strong solution to the deterministic problem

\partial_{t}w_{\omega}+(\mu A_{0})w_{\omega}=-(A_{\omega}-\mu A_{0})u_{\omega}.

Lemma
4.4
lets us compute

(27)

\displaystyle\mu\|w_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\|(A_{\omega}-\mu A_{0})u_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V^{*})}

(28)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\|A_{\omega}-\mu A_{0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}\Bigl(\|v_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}+\|w_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}\Bigr).

Divide this by
\mu
and use the assumption to give

(29)

\displaystyle\|w_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\mu^{-1}\|(A_{\omega}-\mu A_{0})\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}\Bigl(\|v_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}+\|w_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}\Bigr)

(30)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon)\|v_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}+(1-\varepsilon)\|w_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}.

Absorb the second term on the right-hand side into the left-hand side to deduce

(31)

\displaystyle\|w_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}\leq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\|v_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;V)}.

According to
[
28
, Step 1 in Thm. 3.9]
,
u
is progressively measurable. Hence, averaging the last bound over
\Omega
yields

(32)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}\leq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}.

In conjunction with
u=v+w
and (
26
) we obtain in summary

(33)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}+\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega\times(0,T);V)}\leq\frac{c(p,\mu)}{\varepsilon}\bigl(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}\bigr).

Step 3
: Including
B
.

As is standard (see
[
28
, Prop. 3.10]
, for instance), we solve the problem (
P
) with
u_{0}=0
,

(34)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}u(t)+A(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+(B(t)u(t)+g(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

using a fixed-point argument. For
\Phi\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
, replace
Bu
by
B\Phi
in (
34
). This problem possesses a unique solution in
\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
in virtue of the first part of the proof. Write
R(\Phi)
for it. To show unique solvability of (
34
), it suffices to show that
R
is a strict contraction.

Let
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}
. By linearity,
R(\Phi_{1})-R(\Phi_{2})
is the unique strong solution to the problem

\,\mathrm{d}u(t)+A(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=B(t)(\Phi_{1}(t)-\Phi_{2}(t))\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

Hence, (
25
) with
f=0
and
g=B(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2})
gives

(35)

\displaystyle\|R(\Phi_{1})-R(\Phi_{2})\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|B(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2})\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}\leq C\Lambda(B)\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}.

Thus,
R
is a strict contraction by the assumption
C\Lambda(B)\leq 1-\delta
.

For the estimate, apply the a-priori estimate (
25
) once more to find

(36)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}}+\|Bu\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}})\leq C(\|f\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{0}}+\|g\|_{{\mathbb{E}^{p}_{\frac{1}{2}}}})+(1-\delta)\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}^{p}_{1}}.

We can absorb the second term of the right-hand side into the left-hand side to conclude.
∎

5.
Extrapolation of integrability in time

Our main result follows from an interpolation argument using abstract Stein interpolation
[
32
]
. For details on interpolation the reader is referred to
[
6
,
16
,
31
]
. The last two sections constitute the endpoint cases of this interpolation. Before we can conclude in Section
5.2
, we have to construct an analytic family of auxiliary problems first.

Assumption 5.1
.

For fixed
t
, the form
V\times V\ni(u,v)\mapsto\langle A(\omega,t)u,v\rangle
is hermitian almost surely. To ease notation, write
a(u,v)\coloneqq a_{\omega,t}(u,v)\coloneqq\langle A(\omega,t)u,v\rangle
.

This symmetry assumption is used in a crucial way in Lemmas
5.4
and
5.7
.

Convention 5.2
.

We ignore the dependence on
(\omega,t)
in our notation. For instance, we let
A
denote the operator
V\to V^{*}
given by
\langle Au,v\rangle\coloneqq\langle A(\omega,t)u,v\rangle
. Also, we put
a(u,v)\coloneqq\langle Au,v\rangle
and
a[u]\coloneqq a(u,u)
.

The following lemma translates upper bounds on the diagonal of
V\times V
to the whole form. That the optimal constant is
1
is a consequence of Assumption
5.1
, but is actually not needed later. A simpler polarization argument without Assumption
5.1
would lead to an extra factor
2
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.1
holds. If for some
c>0
one has
|a[u]|\leq c\|u\|_{V}^{2}
, then also
|a(u,v)|\leq c\|u\|_{V}\|v\|_{V}
for all
u,v\in V
.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in V
. We can of course assume
u\neq 0\neq v
. There is a complex number
z
with
|z|=1
such that
za(u,v)
is real and positive. By the polarization identity one has

(37)

\displaystyle za(u,v)=a(zu,v)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(a[zu+v]-a[zu-v]+\mathrm{i}a[zu+\mathrm{i}v]-\mathrm{i}a[zu-\mathrm{i}v]\bigr).

Since
a
is hermitian,
a[u]
is real for any
u\in V
. Hence, taking the real part of (
37
) gives

|a(u,v)|=za(u,v)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(a[zu+v]-a[zu-v]\bigr).

Expanding the quadratic forms in conjunction with the bound on the diagonal leads to

(38)

\displaystyle|a(u,v)|=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(2a[zu]+2a[v]\bigr)\leq\frac{c}{2}\bigl(\|u\|_{V}+\|v\|_{V}\bigr).

It remains to use
|a(u,v)|=|a(\lambda u,\lambda^{-1}v)|
, apply (
38
), and minimize over
\lambda>0
.
∎

5.1.
Complex family of auxiliary problems

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds with
M=0
and that Assumption
5.1
holds.
Put
\mu=\Lambda(A)
and
\rho=\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda(A)}
. Then one has

\Bigl|\mu^{-1}\Bigl(a[v]-\frac{1}{2}\|Bv\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)}^{2}\Bigr)-\|v\|_{V}^{2}\Bigr|\leq(1-\rho)\|v\|_{V}^{2}.

Proof.

To ease notation, put
c[v]\coloneqq a[v]-\frac{1}{2}\|Bv\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)}^{2}
. By Assumptions
2.1
(with
M=0
) and
5.1
one has
\lambda\|v\|_{V}^{2}\leq c[v]\leq\Lambda(A)\|v\|_{V}^{2}
. Let
s\geq 0
. Assumption
5.1
implies that
sc[v]-\|v\|_{V}^{2}
is real, hence

(39)

\displaystyle|sc[v]-\|v\|_{V}^{2}|=\max(sc[v]-\|v\|_{V}^{2},\|v\|_{V}^{2}-sc[v]).

Consider the first term. Since

sc[v]-\|v\|_{V}^{2}\leq(s\Lambda(A)-1)\|v\|_{V}^{2},

the maximum in (
39
) coincides with the second term for any choice
s\leq\frac{1}{\Lambda(A)}
. On the other hand,

\|v\|_{V}^{2}-sc[v]\leq(1-s\lambda)\|v\|_{V}^{2},

so the right-hand side of the last estimate is minimal for the maximal admissible choice
s=(\Lambda(A))^{-1}
. Put
\mu=s^{-1}=\Lambda(A)
and
\rho=s\lambda=\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda(A)}
. In summary, we obtain

(40)

\displaystyle|\mu^{-1}c[v]-\|v\|_{V}^{2}|\leq(1-\rho)\|v\|_{V}^{2}.

The same calculation but applied to
a[v]
instead of
a[v]-\frac{1}{2}\|Bv\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(U,H)}^{2}
in conjunction with Lemma
5.3
gives the following.

Corollary 5.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds with
M=0
and that Assumption
5.1
holds. For the same
\mu
and
\rho
as in Lemma
5.4
one has
\|\mu^{-1}A-A_{0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(V,V^{*})}\leq 1-\rho
.

We use
\mu
and
\rho
from the lemma to define complex perturbations of
A
and
B
.

Definition 5.6
.

With
\mu
and
\rho
from Lemma
5.4
, fix numbers
0<r<1<R
satisfying

(i)

R(1-\rho)<1
, and

(ii)

r\min(C_{p}(1-\rho),c\Lambda(B))<1
, where
C_{p}
is the constant from Lemma
4.4
and
c
is the constant from Theorem
4.6

Moreover, with the holomorphic function

6.
Quasilinear problem and well-posedness result

On an open and bounded set

(QL)

\displaystyle\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}u^{\alpha}&=\big[\partial_{i}(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(u)\partial_{j}u^{\beta})+\partial_{i}\Phi^{\alpha}_{i}(u)+\phi^{\alpha}(u)\big]\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sum_{n\geq 1}\big[b^{\alpha\beta}_{n,j}(u^{\beta})\partial_{j}u^{\beta}+g^{\alpha}_{n}(u)\big]\,\mathrm{d}w_{n},\ \ &\text{ on }D,\\

\Omega

, and

in the notation for function spaces if this cannot cause any confusion. Let

Let

Assumption 6.1

(

)
.

(i)

(ii)

(iv)

\displaystyle|\Phi(t,x,y)|+|\phi(t,x,y)|

Remark 6.2
.

Some discussion of the hypotheses is in order.

(i)

When we work with non-linearities of linear growth, see Section
7
, then the diagonal structure assumed in
(i)
is not needed. This is why (
QL
) is formulated for the non-diagonal case.

(ii)

The non-linearities for
a

\Phi
and
\phi
lead to a coupling between the components even when working with a diagonal structure.

(iii)

To identify the limit in the stochastic compactness method, it seems crucial that

only depends on the
\beta
th component. In fact, it is a surprising strength of our approach that this structural assumption is not needed for
a

Remark 6.3

(diagonal structure)
.

Instead of imposing a diagonal structure, we could also strengthen the coercivity condition as follows: for
q\in[0,2(h-1)+\varepsilon]
assume that there exists
\lambda_{q}>0
such that, for all

,
x\in D
, and

For instance, such a condition can be ensure for perturbations of diagonal systems in which

whenever

is sufficiently small. For clarity of exposition, we only work with the diagonal case. This case is also the most important one in reaction–diffusion equations. However, we do not know whether the structural condition we have made is essential. It is needed at a technical point in the proof in the identification of the limit in the stochastic compactness method.

(i)
and
(ii)
with the same constants.

Proof.

Fix

For better readability, suppress dependence on

\sum_{\beta}b^{\alpha\beta}_{n,j}(y^{\beta})\xi_{j}^{\beta}=\vec{b}_{n,j}^{\alpha}(y)\cdot\xi_{j}

Observe first that for

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}{}_{m}b^{\gamma\alpha}_{n,i}(y^{\alpha}){}_{m}b^{\gamma\beta}_{n,j}(y^{\beta})\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\xi_{j}^{\beta}=\sum_{n}\sum_{\gamma}\Bigl|\sum_{j}{}_{m}\vec{b}^{\gamma}_{j}(y)\cdot\xi_{j}\Bigr|_{2}^{2},

Write

Using that the convolution kernel is positive with normalized integral, Jensen’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sum_{\gamma}\Bigl|\sum_{j}{}_{m}\vec{b}^{\gamma}_{j}(y)\cdot\xi_{j}\Bigr|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n}\sum_{\gamma}\Bigl|\sum_{j}\vec{b}^{\gamma}_{j}(\bm{x})\cdot\xi_{j}\Bigr|_{2}^{2}\ast\rho_{m}(y)

(65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\Bigl(b^{\gamma\alpha}_{n,i}(\bm{x}^{\alpha})b^{\gamma\beta}_{n,j}(\bm{x}^{\beta})\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\xi_{j}^{\beta}\Bigr)\ast\rho_{m}(y).

Hence, it follows from Assumptions

(ii)
and positivity of the convolution kernel that

(66)

\displaystyle\quad\Big({}_{m}a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(y)-\frac{1}{2}{}_{m}b^{\gamma\alpha}_{n,i}(y^{\alpha}){}_{m}b^{\gamma\beta}_{n,j}(y^{\beta})\Big)\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\xi_{j}^{\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Big(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(z)-\frac{1}{2}b^{\gamma\alpha}_{n,i}(z^{\alpha})b^{\gamma\beta}_{n,j}(z^{\beta})\Big)\xi_{i}^{\alpha}\xi_{j}^{\beta}\rho_{m}(y-z)\,\mathrm{d}z\geq\lambda|\xi|^{2}.\qed

7.2.

It will turn out useful in the sequel to consider a semi-linear version of (

\displaystyle\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \,\mathrm{d}u^{\alpha}&=\big[\partial_{i}(\bm{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\partial_{j}u^{\beta})+\partial_{i}\Phi^{\alpha}_{i}(u)+\phi^{\alpha}(u)\big]\,\mathrm{d}t\\

(iii)

7.4.
Stochastic compactness argument

In Theorem
1.3
we have already seen that a general tightness result can be deduced from a priori estimates such as the ones of Proposition
7.7
. In the application to the quasi-linear system we need a slight variation of the tightness result in which a certain weak compactness is also taken into account.

In this section we suppose Assumption
7.1
holds, and we fix
p_{0}
,
p\in(2,p_{0}]
, and
(u_{m})_{m\geq 1}
as in Proposition
7.6
.
Put

(93)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{u}\coloneqq\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\mathrm{w}}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(D)),\quad\mathcal{X}_{W}\coloneqq\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathcal{U}_{0}),\quad\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{W}.

With the subscript
“
\mathrm{w}
”
we indicate that the space
\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(D))
is equipped with the weak topology. As a consequence of separability, this has no consequence for questions of measurability.
The space
\mathcal{U}_{0}\supseteq\mathcal{U}
is chosen in such a way that the cylindrical Brownian motion converges almost surely.
The random vectors
(u_{m},W)
take values in
\mathcal{X}
. Write
\mathcal{L}_{m}
for their (joint) laws.

We claim that the family
(\mathcal{L}_{m})_{m}
is tight. It is sufficient to show tightness of the laws
\mathcal{L}(u_{m})
on
\mathcal{X}_{u}
. Let us emphasise that no “diagonal structure” of the coefficients is needed.

Lemma 7.8

(Tightness)
.

The family of laws
(\mathcal{L}(u_{m}))_{m}
on
\mathcal{X}_{u}
is tight.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
. For brevity, put

\mathcal{X}_{u}^{\theta,p}\coloneqq\mathrm{C}^{\theta-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}([0,T];\mathrm{H}^{1-2\theta}(D))\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(D))

for the open ball of radius

in
\mathcal{X}_{u}^{\theta,p}

First, by Chebyshev’s inequality and Proposition
7.7

(94)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\|u_{m}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{u}^{\theta,p}}\geq R)&\leq R^{-p}\mathbb{E}\|u_{m}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{u}^{\theta,p}}^{p}\\

Second, we claim that the closure of
\mathrm{B}_{R}
is compact in
\mathcal{X}_{u}
. Indeed, pre-compactness in
\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))
follows from the vector-valued Arzéla-Ascoli theorem
[
21
, Theorem III.3.1]
, taking into account the compact embedding

, and compactness in the space

is clear, for
\mathrm{B}_{R}
is bounded in the norm topology.
∎

Therefore, Prokhorov’s theorem allows to pass to a weakly convergent subsequence (for convenience, we use the same symbol for this subsequence). Second, the Jakubowski–Skorohod theorem
[
8
, Thm. 2.7.1]
gives the following almost sure convergence.

Lemma 7.9

.

There exists a probability space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
, a sequence of
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables

-valued random variable

-almost surely that

in

The inclusion
