Deformations of I-surfaces with elliptic singularities
Robert Friedman, Phillip Griffiths

Abstract
. An I-surface S S is an algebraic surface of general type with K S 2 = 1 K_{S}^{2}=1 and p g ​ ( S ) = 2 p_{g}(S)=2 . Recent research has centered on trying to give an explicit description of the KSBA compactification of the moduli space of these surfaces. The possible normal Gorenstein examples have been enumerated by work of Franciosi-Pardini-Rollenske. The goal of this paper is to give a more precise description of such surfaces in case their singularities are simple elliptic and/or cusp singularities, and to work out their deformation theory. In particular, under some mild general position assumptions, we show that deformations of the surfaces in question are versal for deformations of the singular points, with two exceptions where the discrepancy is analyzed in detail.



Introduction

A fundamental problem in the study of algebraic surfaces is to find a good description of their compactified moduli spaces. For
K3
surfaces, this problem has a long history, dating back to early work of Shah
[
Sha80
]
,
[
Sha81
]
, and continuing through the very recent work of Alexeev-Engel
[
AE23
]
. Aside from its intrinsic interest, this study has applications to the asymptotic behavior of the period map and to various related questions such as Torelli problems. A common theme throughout has been the interplay between certain elliptic singularities, notably simple elliptic and cusp singularities, and normal crossing models with trivial dualizing sheaf. In the case of simple elliptic singularities, there is a good understanding of how these two models are related (cf. for example Mérindol
[
Mér82
]
and Grojnowski and Shepherd-Barron
[
GSB21
]
), but the relationship in the cusp case is still a mystery. Controlling the deformation theory of the singular models is also important, both in the study of the period map and to understand how the various boundary strata of the compactified moduli space are related. In general, however, the deformation theory of the elliptic singularity model should contain more information than that of the normal crossing model.

For surfaces of general type there is a very general compactification of the moduli space, the KSBA compactification. However, at present it is not practical to describe the myriad possibilities for boundary components in general. Thus, attention has focused in recent years on trying to enumerate all of the possible boundary strata for very simple examples of surfaces
S
with “small” canonical class
K_{S}
; here small could refer either to the size of
K_{S}^{2}
or to that of
\dim H^{0}(S;K_{S})
or both. An appealing class for surfaces for which both invariants are small are the
I-surfaces
:

Definition 0.1
.

An
I-surface

S
is a minimal surface of general type such that
K_{S}^{2}=1
and
\dim H^{0}(S;K_{S})=2
. In this case, necessarily
H^{1}(S;\mathcal{O}_{S})=0
.

I-surfaces were considered classically by Castelnuovo-Enriques. They provided the first examples of surfaces of general type where the
4

\varphi_{4K}
fails to be birational, thus showing that Bombieri’s result that
\varphi_{5K}
is birational is sharp. The modern study of these surfaces goes back at least to Kodaira (unpublished) and Horikawa
[
Hor76
]
, who showed in particular that

has a single base point, necessarily simple, and that

is the minimal resolution of a weighted hypersurface of degree
10
with at worst rational double points contained in the smooth locus of the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(1,1,2,5)
. The analysis was extended to the Gorenstein case by Franciosi-Pardini-Rollenske, who showed in particular
[
FPR17
]

Theorem 0.2

Let

\omega_{Y}
is ample,
\omega_{Y}^{2}=1

is isomorphic to a weighted hypersurface of degree
10

\mathbb{P}(1,1,2,5)

The goal of this paper is to study the corresponding deformation theory of I-surfaces with the most basic elliptic singularities, namely simple elliptic or cusp singularities. The ideal result would be one along the following lines (a more precise discussion is given in §1):

Ideal Theorem:

For
Y
as above and
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
either simple elliptic or cusp singularities, the deformations of
Y
are versal for the local deformations of the singular points except in Case
(vi).

Here, versal means roughly that, given any collection of local deformations of the germs of the singular points
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
, there is a global deformation of the projective surface
Y
which realizes these deformations. In particular, the singular points can be smoothed or deformed independently. This also explains why Case (vi) is exceptional: in this case, only simple elliptic singularities can arise and the exceptional divisors must all be isogenous. Thus, any deformation of
Y
which is equisingular at the points
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
has to preserve the condition that the exceptional divisors are isogenous. In fact, this is the only constraint on the deformation theory as we shall see.

The deformations of a Gorenstein I-surface
Y
are unobstructed. More precisely, it follows easily from the description of
Y
as a degree
10
surface in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,2,5)
that the obstruction space
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}
to the deformation space of
Y
is
0
(Theorem
1.22
). Given this, the “Ideal Theorem” would reduce the study of the discriminant locus in the moduli space of an I-surface with simple elliptic or cusp singularities to the corresponding discriminant locus for the local deformation space of the singular points. For example, in case
Y
has just one singular point which is simple elliptic, there is a neighborhood of
Y
in the moduli space where the locus where the surface continues to have a simple elliptic singularity is a smooth submanifold whose codimension can be read off from the multiplicity of the simple elliptic singularity. Blowing up this submanifold, and taking a cyclic branched cover along the exceptional divisor, leads to a normal crossing model for the degenerations of the I-surface. Given a theory of semistable models for cusp singularities, a similar picture should hold in the cusp case.

As stated, the “Ideal Theorem” is not quite the most ideal version that one could hope for: it would be natural to show that the deformations of
Y
are also versal for the rational double points, or at least that these can all be smoothed in a family which is equisingular for the elliptic singularities. However, in order to keep this paper at a reasonable length, we shall ignore this question and in fact make the running assumption that

has elliptic singularities, but not rational double points (Assumption

The contents of this paper are as follows. In Section

We will generally identify line bundles with the corresponding divisor classes, in particular for the canonical bundle of a surface. Thus we often use additive notation for the tensor product of two line bundles where we mean the sum of the corresponding divisor classes. For two divisors

1.
Deformations of simple elliptic and cusp singularities

1.1.
Preliminary results on simple elliptic and cusp singularities

Throughout this section,
U
is the germ of an isolated Gorenstein surface singularity
p
and
\pi\colon\widetilde{U}\to U
is its minimal resolution. We will also use
U
to denote a contractible Stein representative of the germ. Let
D=\bigcup_{i}E_{i}
be the exceptional set
\pi^{-1}(p)
. The germ
U
has a
minimally elliptic singularity
if
R^{1}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{U}}
has length one. Some special minimally elliptic singularities are given as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

The germ
(U,p)
is a
simple elliptic singularity
if
D
is a smooth elliptic curve. The germ
(U,p)
is a
cusp singularity
if
D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
is either an irreducible nodal curve or a cycle of smooth rational curves, i.e.
E_{i}\cdot E_{i+1}=1
and
E_{r}\cdot E_{1}=1
, with necessarily
E_{i}^{2}\leq-2
for all
i
and
E_{i}^{2}\leq-3
for at least one
i
. In this case the integer
r
is the
length
of the singularity (or of
D
), where
r=1
if
D
is irreducible.
The germ
(U,p)
is a
Dolgachev or triangle singularity
if
D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
with
r\leq 3
, where
D
is a cuspidal rational curve if
r=1
, two smooth rational curves with a tacnode singularity if
r=2
, and three smooth rational curves meeting at a point if
r=3
. We will also inaccurately refer to
D
as a simple elliptic, cusp or triangle singularity.
In all cases the integer
m=-D^{2}
is the
multiplicity
of the singularity (or of
D
). Here, in the cusp case, with
D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
and
E_{i}^{2}=-e_{i}
,
m=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e_{i}-2)
if
r\geq 2
. A similar result holds in the triangle case. (Note however that
m
is the multiplicity in the usual sense only when
m\geq 2
.)

A simple elliptic singularity is determined up to analytic isomorphism by the multiplicity
m
and the
j
-invariant (i.e. the isomorphism class) of the elliptic curve
D
. A cusp singularity is determined up to analytic isomorphism by the sequence
(-e_{1},\dots,-e_{r})
up to cyclic permutation and reflection. This is usually phrased as saying that cusp singularities are
taut
. A similar result is almost true for triangle singularities: there are two analytic types, depending on whether the singularity does or does not have a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action.

Remark 1.2
.

The above singularities are exactly the minimally elliptic singularities with reduced fundamental cycle, which is equivalent in this case to:
K_{\widetilde{U}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{U}}(-D)
. It then follows that
R^{i}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{U}}(-D)=0
for
i>0
. For a simple elliptic or cusp singularity, this says that
(U,p)
is a Du Bois singularity
[
Ste83
, (3.8)]
. However, the analogous statement does not hold for a triangle singularity, because the minimal resolution is not a log resolution.

While we will only consider the case of simple elliptic and cusp singularities in this paper, many of the classification results in §
2
carry over to the case where some of the singularities are allowed to be triangle singularities.

We shall be concerned here with singularities of multiplicity
1
or
2
. An immediate calculation gives:

Lemma 1.3
.

In the above notation, if

(i)

(ii)

1.2.
Adjacencies of some elliptic singularities

Definition 1.4
.

An

to

1.3.
Deformations of elliptic singularities: the local case

As usual, we denote by
\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)
the sheaf of differential
1
-forms with logarithmic poles along
D
. Let
T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D)=(\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D))^{\vee}
be the dual sheaf. Then
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}})
(roughly) classifies first order deformations of the noncompact manifold
\widetilde{U}
and
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))
(roughly) classifies first order deformations of
\widetilde{U}
keeping the effective divisors
E_{i}
. A key technical result is the following:

Lemma 1.7
.

(i)

\dim H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))=1
if
D
is a simple elliptic singularity.

(ii)

H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))=0
if
D
is a cusp singularity.

Proof.

There is a perfect pairing

\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)\to\Omega^{2}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)=K_{\widetilde{U}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{U}}(D)\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{U}}.

Thus the dual
T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D)
of
\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)
is identified with

\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)
.
From the Poincaré residue sequence

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)\to\bigoplus_{i}\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}}\to 0

and the fact that
H^{2}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}})=0
for dimension reasons,
there is a long exact sequence

\bigoplus_{i}H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})\to H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}})\to H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D))\to\bigoplus_{i}H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})\to 0.

Note that
\bigoplus_{i}H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})=0
if
D
is a cusp singularity whereas
\bigoplus_{i}H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})=H^{1}(D;\mathcal{O}_{D})
has dimension one if
D
is simple elliptic.
There is an exact sequence (cf.
[
FL22
, Lemma 2.1(iii)]
)

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)(-D)\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}\to\Omega^{1}_{D}/\tau^{1}_{D}\to 0,

where
\Omega^{1}_{D}/\tau^{1}_{D}
is the Kähler differentials mod the torsion subcomplex. Since cusp singularities are log canonical, and hence Du Bois, it follows from a result of Steenbrink
[
Ste97
, p. 1369]
that

H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)(-D))=0

(this is a very special case of the “extra vanishing lemma”
[
FL22
, Lemma 2.5]
). Moreover,
H^{2}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)(-D))=0
for dimension reasons. Thus

H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}})\cong H^{1}(D;\Omega^{1}_{D}/\tau^{1}_{D})\cong\bigoplus_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};\Omega^{1}_{E_{i}})\cong H^{2}(D;\mathbb{C})\cong H^{2}(\widetilde{U};\mathbb{C}).

The map
\bigoplus_{i}H^{0}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})\to H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}})
from the Poincaré residue sequence is the same as the fundamental class map
\bigoplus_{i}H^{0}(E_{i})\to\bigoplus_{i}H^{2}(D_{i})
and hence is an isomorphism (by negative definiteness). Hence

H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))\cong H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D))\cong\bigoplus_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})

and thus has dimension
1
if
D
is simple elliptic and is
0
if
D
is a cusp.
∎

Lemma 1.8
.

In the simple elliptic case, the map
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D;T_{D})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

By Lemma
1.7
, both
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))
and
H^{1}(D;T_{D})
have dimension
1
. The map
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D;T_{D})
is surjective since
H^{2}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-D))=0
. Thus it is an isomorphism.
∎

Using the middle row of the above commutative diagram then gives:

Corollary 1.9
.

(i)

In the simple elliptic case, there is an exact sequence

0\to H^{1}(D;T_{D})\to H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}})=H^{0}(U;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{U}})\to H^{1}(D;N_{D/\widetilde{U}})\to 0.

(ii)

In the cusp case,

H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}})=H^{0}(U;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{U}})\cong\bigoplus_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widetilde{U}}).\qed

Remark 1.10
.

For a simple elliptic singularity, we can consider the
equisingular
deformations, i.e. those for which the singularity remains simple elliptic but the
j
-invariant of the resolution, i.e. the isomorphism type, is allowed to vary. (For a cusp singularity, an equisingular deformation is the same thing as the trivial deformation.) We saw in the course of the proof of Lemma
1.7
that
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)(-D))=0
. By local duality and the first line of the proof of Lemma
1.7
,

0=(H^{1}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D)(-D)))^{\vee}=H^{1}_{D}(\widetilde{U};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{U}}(\log D))=H^{1}_{D}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D)).

By Wahl’s theory
[
Wah76
, Proposition 2.5, Proposition 2.7]
, since
H^{1}_{D}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))=0
, there is an inclusion
H^{1}(\widetilde{U};T_{\widetilde{U}}(-\log D))\to H^{0}(U;T^{1}_{U})
which identifies the tangent space to the equisingular deformations inside the tangent space to all deformations.

1.4.
Deformations of elliptic singularities: the global case

We now consider the following global issue: Let
Y
be a compact analytic surface or a projective surface with a collection of simple elliptic and cusp singularities of multiplicity at most
2
, or more generally which are local complete intersections. Then
T^{2}_{Y}=0
and
H^{1}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})=0
since the singularities are isolated. The local to global spectral sequence for
\mathbb{T}^{i}_{Y}=\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Omega^{1}_{Y},\mathcal{O}_{Y})
then reduces to the exact sequence

0\to H^{1}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})\to H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})\to\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}\to 0.

Thus we have:

Lemma 1.11
.

(i)

If the map
H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})\to H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})
is surjective, then
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
.

(ii)

If
H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})=0
, then
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
and the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective. ∎

This is related to the Leray spectral sequence as follows. Since
\pi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
is the minimal resolution, it is equivariant and hence
T^{0}_{Y}\cong R^{0}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}}
. Then the Leray spectral sequence
E^{p,q}_{2}=H^{p}(Y;R^{q}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})\implies H^{p+q}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})
gives an exact sequence

0\to H^{1}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})\to H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})\to H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to 0.

Lemma 1.12
.

(i)

If
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
.

(ii)

If
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
and the map

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D;T^{0}_{D})

is surjective.

Proof.

We have the two exact sequences

\displaystyle 0\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D)\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D)\to T^{0}_{D}\to 0;

\displaystyle 0\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D)\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}\to\bigoplus_{i}N_{E_{i}/\widetilde{Y}}\to 0.

Then the fact that
H^{2}(D;T^{0}_{D})=\bigoplus_{i}H^{2}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widetilde{Y}})=0
immediately implies the lemma.
∎

The following is the key result linking the deformation theory of
Y
to certain vanishing statements:

Proposition 1.13
.

(i)

In the simple elliptic case, suppose that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
. Then the map

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})

is surjective and
H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})=\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
. Thus
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective.

(ii)

In the cusp case, suppose that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
. Then the map

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})

is surjective and
H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})=\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
. Thus
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective.

Proof.

In the simple elliptic case, there is a commutative diagram

\begin{CD}H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))@>{}>{}>H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>H^{1}(D;N_{D/\widetilde{Y}})\\
@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V@V{}V{=}V\\
0@>{}>{}>H^{1}(D;T_{D})@>{}>{}>H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>H^{1}(D;N_{D/\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>0.\end{CD}

By Lemma
1.12
, under the assumptions of (i),
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
and hence
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{1}(D;N_{D/\widetilde{Y}})
is surjective. Since
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
, the map
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D;T_{D})
is surjective. Thus
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})
is surjective. Again by Lemma
1.12
,
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
. The Leray spectral sequence then gives an exact sequenece

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})\to 0.

Since
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})
is surjective,
H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})=0
. By Lemma
1.11
(ii),
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
and the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective. The proof in the cusp case is similar (and simpler).
∎

Example 1.14
.

Suppose that
D\in|-K_{Y}|
. Then

H^{2}(Y;T_{Y}(-D))=H^{2}(Y;T_{Y}\otimes K_{Y})=H^{2}(Y;\Omega^{1}_{Y}).

If
Y
is rational, then
H^{2}(Y;\Omega^{1}_{Y})=0
. If
Y
is the blowup of a minimal elliptic ruled surface, then
\dim H^{2}(Y;\Omega^{1}_{Y})=1
. In this case, it is a standard result (due in various forms to Kulikov-Persson-Roan) that
D=D_{1}+D_{2}
has two connected components
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, both sections of the ruling. The exact sequence
0\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D)\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D)\to T_{D}\to 0
is identified with the Poincaré residue sequence

0\to\Omega^{1}_{Y}\to\Omega^{1}_{Y}(\log(D_{1}+D_{2}))\to\mathcal{O}_{D_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{D_{2}}\to 0.

The Gysin map
H^{1}(D_{i};\mathcal{O}_{D_{i}})\to H^{2}(Y;\Omega^{1}_{Y})
is an isomorphism for
i=1,2
. Hence the image of
H^{1}(Y;T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))
in
H^{1}(D_{1};T_{D_{1}})\oplus H^{1}(D_{2};T_{D_{2}})
has dimension one and
H^{2}(Y;T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
. Note that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))\neq 0
; roughly speaking, this represents the obstruction to deforming the two elliptic curves
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
independently. However,
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
since this is a birational invariant of
\widetilde{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
is a ruled surface, and one can check that
H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})\to H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})
is surjective. Thus
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
in this case as well. We will discuss this case in more detail below.

1.5.
Some variations of the above

In some of our applications, Proposition
1.13
will not hold, and so we will need some variations on it. For the first variation, assume that
Y
has simple elliptic singularities at the points
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
and is otherwise smooth. We begin by fixing some notation:

Definition 1.15
.

As always, let
\pi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
be the minimal resolution and let
D_{i}=\pi^{-1}(p_{i})
. Set
D_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j\neq i}D_{j}
. We can form partial resolutions in two different ways: first, let
\pi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to Y
be a resolution at the singular point
p_{i}
and an isomorphism elsewhere. The exceptional set of
\pi_{i}
is
D_{i}
. Then there is an induced morphism
\psi_{i}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{i}
, with exceptional set
D_{i}^{\prime}
. There is also the morphism
\pi_{i}^{\prime}\colon Y_{i}^{\prime}\to Y
, which is a resolution at the points
p_{j},j\neq i
and is an isomorphism over
p_{i}
. Thus the exceptional set of
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
is
D_{i}^{\prime}
. Of course, if
k=2
, then
\pi_{1}\colon Y_{1}\to Y
is identified with
\pi_{2}^{\prime}\colon Y_{2}^{\prime}\to Y
. Let
\psi_{i}^{\prime}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{i}^{\prime}
be the corresponding induced morphism, with exceptional set
D_{i}
.

We shall look at
\pi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to Y
more systematically in §
2
. At present, we are concerned with the morphism
\pi_{i}^{\prime}\colon Y_{i}^{\prime}\to Y
.

Definition 1.16
.

Let
\Omega^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(\log D_{i}^{\prime})
be the sheaf equal to
\Omega^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}
on the singular surface
Y_{i}^{\prime}-D_{i}^{\prime}
and to
\Omega^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(\log D_{i}^{\prime})
on the smooth surface
Y_{i}^{\prime}-\{p_{i}\}
, glued together in the obvious way. Set
T^{i}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})=\mathit{Ext}^{i}(\Omega^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(\log D_{i}^{\prime}),\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}})
and
\mathbb{T}^{i}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})=\operatorname{Ext}^{i}(\Omega^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(\log D_{i}^{\prime}),\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}})
.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
\mathbf{Def}_{Y_{i}^{\prime},D_{i}^{\prime}}
be the functor of deformations of
Y_{i}^{\prime}
keeping the effective Cartier divisor
D_{i}^{\prime}
. Then the tangent space to
\mathbf{Def}_{Y_{i}^{\prime},D_{i}^{\prime}}
is
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})
and
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})
is an obstruction space for
\mathbf{Def}_{Y_{i}^{\prime},D_{i}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

This is a minor variation on the proof for smooth
Y
(cf. e.g. Sernesi
[
Ser06
, 3.4.17]
).
∎

Corollary 1.18
.

In the above notation, suppose that
H^{2}(Y_{i}^{\prime};T^{0}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))=0
and that
p_{i}
is a local complete intersection. Then there exists a deformation of
Y
which smooths the singular point
p_{i}
and for which the remaining points
p_{j}
,
j\neq i
, remain simple elliptic singularities.

Proof.

In any case, identifying
H^{0}(Y_{i}^{\prime};T^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}})
with
H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y,p_{i}})
and using the fact that
H^{0}(Y;T^{2}_{Y,p_{i}})=0
by our assumption, the local to global spectral sequence for
\mathbb{T}^{i}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})
gives an exact sequence

\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y,p_{i}})\to H^{2}(Y_{i}^{\prime};T^{0}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))\to\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})\to 0.

Under the hypothesis that
H^{2}(Y_{i}^{\prime};T^{0}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))=0
,
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})=0
, and hence
\mathbf{Def}_{Y_{i}^{\prime},D_{i}^{\prime}}
is unobstructed. Moreover, the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y,p_{i}})
is surjective. We can then find a deformation
\mathcal{Y}_{i}^{\prime}\to\Delta
of
Y_{i}^{\prime}
which smooths the singular point
p_{i}
and keeps the effective Cartier divisor
D_{i}^{\prime}
, in the sense that there exists a relative Cartier divisor
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{i}^{\prime}
which restricts over
0
to
D_{i}^{\prime}
. By Wahl’s theory
[
Wah76
, Proposition 2.7]
, we can blow down
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}
to get a flat family over
\Delta
which is a deformation of
Y
smoothing
p_{i}
and which is equisingular (in the sense of Remark
1.10
) at the remaining points
p_{j}
as desired.
∎

By analogy with Proposition
1.13
(i), we then have:

Proposition 1.19
.

(i)
Suppose that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})(-D_{i}))=0
. Then
H^{2}(Y_{i}^{\prime};T^{0}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))=0
and thus the conclusions of Corollary
1.18
hold.

(ii)
If the natural map

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D_{i};T_{D_{i}})
is surjective and
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})(-D_{i}))=0
and the conclusions of Corollary
1.18
hold.

Proof.

(i) A minor variation on the proof of Proposition
1.13
(i) shows that the Leray map

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))\to H^{0}(Y_{i}^{\prime};R^{1}(\psi_{i}^{\prime})_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))

is surjective and that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))=0
. Thus

H^{2}(Y_{i}^{\prime};T^{0}_{Y_{i}^{\prime}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))=H^{2}(Y_{i}^{\prime};R^{0}(\psi_{i}^{\prime})_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime}))=0.

(ii) From the exact sequence

0\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})(-D_{i})\to T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D)\to T_{D_{i}}\to 0,

we get a long exact sequence

H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D_{i};T_{D_{i}})\to H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})(-D_{i}))\to H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D)).

Thus, if the first map is surjective and the last group is
0
, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})(-D_{i}))=0
.
∎

Example 1.20
.

Returning to Example
1.14
, with
\widetilde{Y}
the blowup of a minimal elliptic ruled surface,
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
two disjoint sections with negative self-intersection, and
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D_{1}-D_{2})
,

(T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{1})(-D_{2}))^{\vee}\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D_{1})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D_{2}-D_{1}-D_{2})=\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D_{1})(-D_{1}).

Thus by Serre duality
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{1})(-D_{2}))
is dual to
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D_{1})(-D_{1}))
. There is an exact sequence

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D_{1})(-D_{1})\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}\to\Omega^{1}_{D_{1}}\to 0.

Thus
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D_{1})(-D_{1}))=0
since
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(D_{1};\Omega^{1}_{D_{1}})
is an isomorphism. It follows that, if the singularity at
p_{2}
is a local complete intersection, then we can smooth the point corresponding to
p_{2}
while keeping a simple elliptic singularity in the deformation corresponding to
p_{1}
. Such a deformation will have a minimal resolution which is a rational surface.

The second variation is the following:

Proposition 1.21
.

Assume that
Y
has a simple elliptic singularity at
p_{1}
, a local complete intersection cusp singularity at
p_{2}
, and is otherwise smooth. As before, let
\pi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
be the minimal resolution and let
D_{i}=\pi^{-1}(p_{i})
. Suppose that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log(D_{1}+D_{2})))=0
. Then
the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y_{1}}(-\log D_{1})\to H^{0}(Y_{1};T^{1}_{Y_{1},p_{2}})
is surjective. In particular,
there exists a deformation of
Y
which is equisingular at
p_{1}
and deforms
p_{2}
to a simple elliptic singularity with the same multiplicity as
p_{2}
.

Proof.

As in Definition
1.15
, let
\pi_{1}\colon Y_{1}\to Y
be the minimal resolution at
p_{1}
and let
\psi_{1}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{1}
be the induced morphism. A Leray spectral sequence argument applied to the morphism
\psi_{1}
and the sheaf

R^{0}\psi_{1}{}_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{1})=T^{0}_{Y_{1}}(-\log D_{1})

as in the proof of Proposition
1.13
(ii) shows that
H^{2}(Y_{1};T^{0}_{Y_{1}}(-\log D_{1}))=0
. Thus
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y_{1}}(-\log D_{1})=0
and the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y_{1}}(-\log D_{1})\to H^{0}(Y_{1};T^{1}_{Y_{1},p_{2}})
is surjective, proving the first statement. An adjacency of
p_{2}
to a simple elliptic singularity with the same multiplicity gives a global
1
-parameter deformation of the pair
(Y_{1},D_{1})
keeping the Cartier divisor
D_{1}
and deforms
p_{2}
as desired. Blowing down the family of Cartier divisors corresponding to
D_{1}
as in the proof of Corollary
1.18
then gives a deformation of
Y
which is equisingular at
p_{1}
and is as claimed at
p_{2}
.
∎

1.6.
Deformations of I-surfaces are unobstructed

We prove a very general result about weighted hypersurfaces of degree
10
in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,2,5)
. However, it is not of much use without further information of the kind discussed above.

Theorem 1.22
.

Let
Y
be a weighted hypersurface of degree
10
in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,2,5)
, not passing through the singular locus. Then
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
, so that the deformations of
Y
are unobstructed.

Proof.

For simplicity of notation denote
\mathbb{P}(1,1,2,5)
by
\mathbb{P}
. The conormal sequence for
Y
is

0\to I_{Y}/I_{Y}^{2}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}}|Y\to\Omega^{1}_{Y}\to 0.

In particular the long exact Ext sequence yields an exact sequence

H^{1}(Y;N_{Y/\mathbb{P}})\to\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(\Omega^{1}_{Y},\mathcal{O}_{Y})\to H^{2}(Y;T_{\mathbb{P}}|Y).

Here
N_{Y/\mathbb{P}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Y}(10)
. A standard argument shows that
H^{1}(\mathbb{P};\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(a))=H^{2}(\mathbb{P};\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(a))=0
for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and hence that
H^{1}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y}(a))=0
by using the long exact sequence associated to

0\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(-Y)(a)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(a-10)\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(a)\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}(a)\to 0.

Thus
H^{1}(Y;N_{Y/\mathbb{P}})=0
.
The remaining term
H^{2}(Y;T_{\mathbb{P}}|Y)
is Serre dual to
H^{0}(\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}}|Y\otimes\omega_{Y})
. The Euler exact sequence for weighted projective space is

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(-a_{i})\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\to 0,

with the weights
a_{i}=1,1,2,5
. Restricting to
Y
and tensoring with
\omega_{Y}
gives an exact sequence

0\to H^{0}(Y;\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}}|Y\otimes\omega_{Y})\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}H^{0}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1-a_{i}))\to H^{0}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1)).

In our case,
1-a_{i}
is nonnegative only for the two weights
a_{i}=1
,
H^{0}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1))
has dimension
2
, and the map
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}H^{0}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1-a_{i}))\to H^{0}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1))
is easily checked to be surjective and hence an isomorphism. Thus
H^{0}(Y;\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}}|Y\otimes\omega_{Y})=0
, and so finally
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
.
∎

2.
Structure of I-surfaces with elliptic singularities

2.1.
Some preliminary results

Note the following:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\rho\colon S\to\overline{S}
be the iterated blowup of a smooth surface
\overline{S}
and let
\bigcup_{i}C_{i}
be the fiber of
\rho
over a point
p
. Let
K_{S}=\rho^{*}K_{\overline{S}}+\sum_{i}a_{i}C_{i}
where the
a_{i}
are nonnegative integers. Then
a_{j}>0
for all
j
. If
C_{j}^{2}=-1
for some
j
(i.e.
C_{j}
is an exceptional curve) and
\rho^{-1}(p)
is reducible, then
a_{j}\geq 2
.

Proof.

The first statement is clear since every local section of
\rho^{*}K_{\overline{S}}
vanishes along
C_{j}
as a section of
K_{S}
. If
C_{j}^{2}=-1
, then by adjunction
C_{j}\cdot K_{S}=-1
. But
K_{S}=\rho^{*}K_{\overline{S}}+\sum_{i}a_{i}C_{i}
. For
j\neq k
,
C_{j}\cdot C_{k}\geq 0
, and by hypothesis
C_{j}\cdot C_{k}>0
for some
k
as the fiber is connected. Thus, for this choice of
k
,

-1=a_{j}C_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}(C_{j}\cdot C_{i})\geq-a_{j}+a_{k}.

Hence
a_{j}\geq 2
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
S
be a smooth surface.

(i)

If
S
is the blowup of a geometrically ruled surface over a base curve of genus at least
2
, then
S
does not contain a smooth elliptic curve or a cycle of rational curves.

(ii)

If
S
is the blowup of a geometrically ruled surface over a base curve of genus at least
1
, then
S
does not contain a cycle of rational curves.

(iii)

If
S
is the blowup of a surface with
\kappa(S)=0
or
\kappa(S)=1
and
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{S})=0
, then
S
does not contain a cycle of rational curves.

Proof.

(i), (ii): Let
S
be the blowup of the geometrically ruled surface
\overline{S}
, let
\bar{\rho}\colon\overline{S}\to C
be the ruling and let
\rho\colon S\to C
be the induced morphism. Then all fibers of
\rho
are iterated blowups of fibers of
\bar{\rho}
, hence their reductions are trees of smooth rational curves. The image of a smooth elliptic curve would be a point, and hence the curve would have to be contained in the blowup of a fiber, but this is impossible. Likewise, a cycle of rational curves would have to be contained in the blowup of a fiber, but this is again impossible. A similar argument handles (ii).

(iii): First assume that
\kappa(S)=1
and let
\overline{S}
be the minimal model of
S
and let
\rho\colon S\to C
be the canonical elliptic fibration. Then, by the structure theory of elliptic surfaces, all fibers of
\rho
have smooth reduction. If there exists a cycle of rational curves on
S
, then there exists a rational curve on
\overline{S}
, necessarily not contained in a fiber, and hence
g(C)=0
. Moreover,
p_{g}(\overline{S})=0
and
q(\overline{S})=1
. Then there exists a finite étale cover of
\overline{S}
which is biholomorphic to a product of two smooth nonrational curves (e.g.
[
Bea78
, Thm. VI.13]
or
[
FM94
, Chapter 2]
). This contradicts the existence of a rational curve on
\overline{S}
. The case where
\kappa(S)=0
is similar (and simpler).
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Y
be a normal projective surface with
k
isolated minimally elliptic singularities and let
\pi_{1}\colon Y_{1}\to Y
be a partial resolution of
Y
at
k^{\prime}
of the singularities. Then
\chi(Y_{1};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{1}})=\chi(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})-k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We have the Leray spectral sequence
E_{2}^{p,q}=H^{p}(Y;R^{q}\pi_{1}{}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y_{1}})\implies H^{p+q}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})
. Thus, since
R^{0}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y_{1}}=\mathcal{O}_{Y}
and
R^{1}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{Y_{1}}
is a skyscraper sheaf with one-dimensional stalks at each of the
k^{\prime}
singular points resolved by
\pi_{1}
,

\chi(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=\chi(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})-k^{\prime}
.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
Y
be a normal projective surface with
k>0
isolated singularities which are either simple elliptic or cusp singularities. Assume that
h^{1}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})=0
and set
p_{g}=h^{2}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})
. Then
k\leq p_{g}+1
. If
k=p_{g}+1
, then all singularities of
Y
are simple elliptic and a smooth minimal model for
Y
is either an elliptic ruled surface, an elliptic surface such that all fibers have smooth reduction, or is an abelian surface isogeneous to a product of two elliptic curves.

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
be the minimal resolution.
If
k>p_{g}+1
, then

\chi(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=\chi(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})-k=1+p_{g}-k<0,

by Lemma
2.3
. Hence, by the Castelnuovo-de Franchis theorem,
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of a geometrically ruled surface over a base curve of genus at least
2
. This contradicts (i) of Lemma
2.2
.

Now assume that
k=p_{g}+1
and let
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
be a smooth minimal model of
\widetilde{Y}
. Then

\chi(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}})=\chi(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=\chi(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})-k=1+p_{g}-k=0.

Thus
c_{1}(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}})^{2}+c_{2}(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}})=12\chi(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}})=0
. In particular, since
\widetilde{Y}
is not ruled over a curve of genus at least
2
, by classification
c_{1}(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}})^{2}\geq 0
and
c_{2}(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}})\geq 0
, hence
c_{1}(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}})^{2}=c_{2}(Y_{\text{\rm{min}}})=0
. Thus, by the classification of such surfaces,
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is either an elliptic ruled surface, an elliptic surface over a curve
B
such that all fibers have smooth reduction, or is an abelian surface, necessarily isogeneous to a product of two elliptic curves. In all cases, by Lemma
2.2
, the blowup
\widetilde{Y}
of
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
will not contain a cycle of rational curves, so all singularities of
Y
are simple elliptic.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

With more effort, one can extend the proof of Theorem
2.4
to cover the case where the singularities of
Y
are just assumed to be minimally elliptic.

2.2.
Numerology of I-surfaces

We begin by defining more carefully the surfaces considered in this paper.

Definition 2.6
.

An
I-surface with simple elliptic or cusp singularities
is a normal Gorenstein surface
Y
with
k
singularities which are only simple elliptic, cusp, or RDP singularities, such that
\omega_{Y}
is ample,
\omega_{Y}^{2}=1
,
h^{1}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})=0
,
h^{2}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})=h^{0}(Y;\omega_{Y})=2
and thus
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Y})=3
. (In fact, the condition
h^{1}(Y;\mathcal{O}_{Y})=0
follows from the other two.) Let
\pi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
be a minimal resolution of singularities, so in particular no exceptional curve on
\widetilde{Y}
is contained in a fiber of
\pi
. Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be the simple elliptic or cusp singularities, let
D_{i}=\pi^{-1}(p_{i})
, and set
D=\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}
. Thus each
D_{i}
is either a smooth elliptic curve or a cycle of rational curves, and
D
is the exceptional set of
\pi

\iff
there are no RDP singularities. Set
m_{i}=-D_{i}^{2}
and
m=-D^{2}=\sum_{i}m_{i}
, with
m_{i}>0
for every
i
. In particular,
k\leq m
. By Theorem
2.4
,
k\leq 3
.
Let
L
be the nef and big line bundle
\pi^{*}\omega_{Y}
.

Assumption 2.7
.

As noted in the introduction, to keep the number of cases manageable we will assume for the rest of this paper that
Y
has no RDP singularities. Thus, in the notation of Definition
2.6
,
L\cdot C\geq 0
for every irreducible curve
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
, and
L\cdot C=0

\iff

C
is a component of
D_{i}
for some
i
. In particular,
L\cdot C>0
for every exceptional curve
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
.

Remark 2.8
.

If there are some RDP singularities on
Y
, then it is still true that
L\cdot C>0
for every exceptional curve
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
. Under this assumption, the arguments in this section for the classification of the possible
Y
will carry over with minor modifications.

We have
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\pi^{*}\omega_{Y}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D)
, i.e.
L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D
. Since
L\cdot D_{i}=0
for every
i
,

K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot D_{i}+D_{i}^{2}=0.

More precisely, by adjunction,
(K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{i})|D_{i}=0
as a divisor on
D_{i}
.
Hence
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot D_{i}=m_{i}>0
. Also,

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle=L\cdot(K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D)=L\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}=1;

\displaystyle K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(L-D)\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}=L\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}-m=1-m.

We can also consider partial resolutions:

Definition 2.9
.

As in Definition
1.15
, let
\pi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to Y
be the minimal resolution of the singular point
p_{i}
and an isomorphism from
Y_{i}-D_{i}
to
Y-p_{i}
. Note that, in case
k=1
,
Y_{i}=\widetilde{Y}
. Denote by
\psi_{i}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{i}
the induced morphism. Finally set
M_{i}=\psi_{i}^{*}\omega_{Y_{i}}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

In the above notation,
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=2
. Moreover,

(i)

M_{i}=L-D_{i}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{i}^{\prime}
, where
D_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j\neq i}D_{j}
. Here, if
k=1
, then
D_{i}^{\prime}=D_{1}^{\prime}=0
and
M_{1}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}
.

(ii)

M_{i}
is effective and
L\cdot M_{i}=1
. In particular,
M_{i}
is not numerically equivalent to
0
.

(iii)

For
i\neq j
,
M_{i}\cdot M_{j}=1
. In case
i=j
,
\omega_{Y_{i}}^{2}=M_{i}^{2}=1-m_{i}
. (Here
\omega_{Y_{i}}^{2}
means the self-intersection of the Cartier divisor on
Y_{i}
.)

(iv)

M_{i}\cdot D_{i}^{\prime}=0
,
M_{i}\cdot D_{i}=m_{i}
, and
M_{i}\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}=1-m_{i}
.

(v)

If
k>1
and either
h^{1}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
or
\widetilde{Y}
is a blown up elliptic ruled surface, then

\dim H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(M_{i}))=1.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Lemma
2.3
. Then (i) is clear since
\widetilde{Y}
is a resolution of
Y_{i}
at the points
p_{j},j\neq i
. Since
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=2
,
h^{2}(Y_{i};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})\neq 0
and hence
h^{0}(Y_{i};\omega_{Y_{i}})\neq 0
.
Thus
M_{i}
is effective. Since
\omega_{Y_{i}}
is trivial in a neighborhood of
D_{i}^{\prime}
,
M_{i}\cdot D_{i}^{\prime}=0
. Moreover,
L\cdot M_{i}=L\cdot(L-D_{i})=L^{2}=1
. We have

M_{i}\cdot M_{j}=(L-D_{i})\cdot(L-D_{j})=L^{2}+(D_{i}\cdot D_{j})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $i\neq j$;}\\
1-m_{i},&\text{if $i=j$.}\end{cases}

Then
M_{i}\cdot D_{i}=(L-D_{i})\cdot D_{i}=-D_{i}^{2}=m_{i}
. Likewise
M_{i}\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}=M_{i}\cdot(M_{i}-D_{i}^{\prime})=M_{i}^{2}=1-m_{i}
.

To see (v), note that
\dim H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(M_{i}))=h^{2}(Y_{i};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})
. Since
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=2
, it suffices to prove that
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=0
. The Leray spectral sequence gives an exact sequence

0\to H^{1}(Y_{i};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})\to H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(R^{1}\psi_{i}{}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}).

Thus, if
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
, then
H^{1}(Y_{i};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=0
. If
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of an elliptic ruled surface with base an elliptic curve
B
, then all of the singularities on
Y_{i}
are simple elliptic, and
H^{0}(R^{1}\psi_{i}{}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})\cong\bigoplus_{j\neq i}H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{D_{j}})
. Choosing a
j\neq i
, the map
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{D_{j}})
is an isomorphism since
D_{j}\to B
is an isogeny. Thus
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(R^{1}\psi_{i}{}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})
is injective so that
H^{1}(Y_{i};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=0
in this case as well.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

The passage from
Y
or
Y_{i}
to
\widetilde{Y}
is reversible, in the following sense. Suppose that
\widetilde{Y}
is a smooth surface and that
D=\sum_{i}D_{i}
is an effective divisor on
\widetilde{Y}
, where the
D_{i}
are the connected components of
D
and each
D_{i}
is a smooth elliptic curve, an irreducible nodal rational curve or a cycle of smooth rational curves (one could also allow configurations corresponding to the triangle singularity case). By adjunction,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D)|D_{i}\cong\omega_{D_{i}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D_{i}}
. Suppose in addition that the intersection matrix of
D
is negative definite, or equivalently that there exists a contraction
\pi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
, where
Y
is a normal analytic surface and
\pi(D_{i})=p_{i}\in Y
. In particular, the singular points are simple elliptic or cusp singularities. Thus
\widetilde{Y}
is the minimal resolution of a Gorenstein surface
Y
with elliptic singularities. There exists a contractible Stein neighborhood
U_{i}
of
p_{i}
in
Y
. Let
\widetilde{U}_{i}=\pi^{-1}(U_{i})
. A standard argument using the Grauert-Riemenschneider theorem shows that restriction of line bundles induces an isomorphism
\operatorname{Pic}\widetilde{U}_{i}\to\operatorname{Pic}D_{i}
. Let
L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D)
. By the above,
L|\widetilde{U}_{i}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D)|\widetilde{U}_{i}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{U}_{i}}
. Hence
L
is the pullback of a line bundle on
Y
, necessarily
\omega_{Y}
. A similar statement holds for the line bundles
M_{i}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D_{i}^{\prime})
and the morphism
\psi_{i}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{i}
. Note that
\omega_{Y}
is nef and big
\iff

L
is nef and big, and in this case
\omega_{Y}
is ample
\iff
the irreducible curves
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
such that
L\cdot C=0
are exactly the components of
D_{i}
for some
i
.

2.3.
The main geometric result

We begin with the following, due to Franciosi-Pardini-Rollenske
[
FPR15
, Theorem 4.1]
,
[
FPR17
, Proposition 4.3]
, which deals with the case where
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})\geq 0
, i.e.
\widetilde{Y}
is not rational or ruled (and does not require Assumption
2.7
):

Theorem 2.12
.

Suppose that
L^{2}=1
and that
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})\geq 0
. Let
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
be the minimal model of
\widetilde{Y}
.

(i)

If
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=2
, i.e.
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is of general type, then
\widetilde{Y}=Y=Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
and
k=0
, i.e. there are no simple elliptic or cusp singularities.

(ii)

If
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=1
, i.e.
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is properly elliptic, then
\widetilde{Y}=Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
,
k=m=1
,
\widetilde{Y}
is a multiplicity two logarithmic transform of an elliptic
K3
surface at a single fiber, one component of
D=D_{1}
is a bisection of the elliptic fibration, and either
D
is smooth with
D^{2}=-1
or
D
consists of a bisection of square
-3
plus smooth components of square
-2
lying in a fixed fiber. In this case,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(F)
, where
F
is the multiple fiber,
D\cdot F=1
, and
L=F+D
.

(iii)

If
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=0
, then either
k=m=2
and
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is an Enriques surface or
k=1
,
m=2
and
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is a
K3
surface. In this case,
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
at one point. If
C
is the exceptional curve, then
C\cdot D=2
if
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is a
K3
surface and
C\cdot D_{1}=C\cdot D_{2}=1
if
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is an Enriques surface. Finally,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\equiv C
and
L\equiv D+C
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
be the canonical morphism. Then
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\rho^{*}K_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}}+\sum_{i}a_{i}C_{i}
, where the
C_{i}
are the positive dimensional fibers of
\rho
and
C_{j}
is an exceptional curve for at least one
j
. Since
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})\geq 0
,
K_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}}
is nef and
K_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}}^{2}\geq 0
. Since
L
is nef and big,
L\cdot\rho^{*}K_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}}\geq 0
, with equality
\iff

K_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}}\equiv 0
by the Hodge index theorem. Hence, if
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})\geq 1
, from

1=L^{2}=L\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}=L\cdot\left(\rho^{*}K_{Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}}+\sum_{i}a_{i}C_{i}\right),

and the fact that
L\cdot C_{j}>0
for at least one
j
, the sum
\sum_{i}a_{i}C_{i}
must be empty, i.e.
\widetilde{Y}=Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
. If
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=0
and
\widetilde{Y}\neq Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
, then
\sum_{i}a_{i}(L\cdot C_{i})=1
. By Lemma
2.1
and the fact that
L\cdot C>0
for every exceptional curve
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
, there is a unique connected component of the fiber of
\rho
and it is an irreducible exceptional curve, i.e.
\rho\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
is a single blowup.

The case
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=2
:

Then
K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}>0
. By the Hodge index theorem,

1\leq(L^{2})(K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2})=K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}\leq(L\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}})^{2}=L^{2}=1.

Since equality holds,
L
and
K_{\widetilde{Y}}
are numerically equivalent, hence
D\equiv 0
and
k=0
.

The case
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=1
:
Since
\widetilde{Y}
is minimal,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}=0
and
1=L^{2}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}+m
, so
m=k=1
and
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot D=1
. In particular
D=D_{1}
is connected.
By Lemma
2.3
,
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=3-k=2
. Since
D^{2}<0
and
p_{a}(D)=1
,
D
cannot be a union of fiber components (it would have to be a complete fiber). Hence
D
contains a multisection of the elliptic fibration. By the canonical bundle formula,

K_{\widetilde{Y}}\equiv(2+2g-2)f+\sum_{\alpha}(m_{\alpha}-1)F_{\alpha}=2gf+\sum_{\alpha}(m_{\alpha}-1)F_{\alpha},

where the
F_{\alpha}
are the multiple fibers, of multiplicity
m_{\alpha}\geq 2
. Since
D\cdot F_{\alpha}\geq 1
for all
\alpha
and
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot D=1
, the only possibility is that
g=0
, there is a unique multiple fiber
F_{\alpha}
with
m_{\alpha}=2
and
D\cdot F_{\alpha}=1
. Hence
D
contains a bisection of the elliptic fibration, with the other components equal to components of a single reducible fiber. In particular, the Jacobian elliptic surface corresponding to
\widetilde{Y}
is an elliptic surface over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with a section and holomorphic Euler characteristic equal to
2
, hence is an elliptic
K3
surface. Then
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\equiv F
. The remaining statements are clear or follow from adjunction: either
D
is an irreducible bisection, or
D
is reducible and one component
E
is a bisection which is smooth rational and the other components are smooth components of a fiber. In this case
-2=K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot E+E^{2}=1+E^{2}
, so that
E
is the unique component of
D
of self-intersection
-3
and all other components have self-intersection
-2
.

The case
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=0
:
In this case,
\widetilde{Y}\neq Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
, because otherwise

0=2p_{a}(D_{i})-2=D_{i}^{2}+K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot D_{i}=D_{i}^{2},

contradicting
D_{i}^{2}<0
. By the remarks at the beginning of the proof,
\widetilde{Y}
is then the blowup of
Y_{\text{\rm{min}}}
at one point. Thus,

1=L^{2}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}+m=-1+m,

so that
m=2
. Either
k=m=2
and
D=D_{1}+D_{2}
with
D_{i}^{2}=-1
or
k=1
and
D=D_{1}
is connected with
D^{2}=-2
. In the first case,
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=3-2=1
, so
\widetilde{Y}
is an Enriques surface. In the second case,
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=3-k=2
, so
\widetilde{Y}
is a
K3
surface.
∎

We are thus reduced to analyzing the case
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
. First note the following special case of
[
FPR15
, Lemma 4.5]
:

Lemma 2.13
.

If
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
, then there are two possibilities:

(i)

\widetilde{Y}
is a rational surface and
k=2
.

(ii)

\widetilde{Y}
is a ruled surface over an elliptic curve
B
and
k=3
. In this case all singularities are simple elliptic.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, if
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
, then either
\widetilde{Y}
is rational and hence
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=1
, or
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of an elliptic ruled surface and hence
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
and all singularities are simple elliptic. By Lemma
2.3
,
k=2
in the first case and
k=3
in the second case.
∎

The strategy now is to see what prevents the divisors
M_{i}
introduced in Definition
2.9
from being nef.

Lemma 2.14
.

If
C
is an irreducible curve on
\widetilde{Y}
such that
M_{i}\cdot C<0
, then
C
is an exceptional curve and
D_{i}^{\prime}\cdot C=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.10
,
M_{i}
is effective. Thus a curve
C
such that
M_{i}\cdot C<0
must satisfy
C^{2}<0
. If
C
is a component of
D_{i}^{\prime}
, then
M_{i}\cdot C=0
since
M_{i}
is trivial in a neighborhood of
D_{i}^{\prime}
. It follows that
C
is not a component of
D_{i}^{\prime}
so that
D_{i}^{\prime}\cdot C\geq 0
. Then

0>M_{i}\cdot C=(K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{i}^{\prime})\cdot C\geq K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot C.

Thus
C
is exceptional,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot C=-1
, and therefore

D_{i}^{\prime}\cdot C=0
.
∎

The main result is then the following, which generalizes Theorem
2.12
to the case where
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
:

Theorem 2.15
.

With notation as above, suppose that
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
and in particular that
k>1
. Then exactly one of the following holds:

(i)

M_{i}
is not nef. Then there exists an exceptional curve
C
such that
C\cdot D_{i}^{\prime}=0
. In this case, if
\gamma\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
is the surface obtained by blowing down
C
, then
M_{i}=C
,
C\cdot D_{i}=2
and
m_{i}=2
. Finally, identifying
D_{i}^{\prime}
with its image in
Y_{0}
,
K_{Y_{0}}=-D_{i}^{\prime}
,
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of
Y_{0}
at a point not on
D_{i}^{\prime}
, and
D_{i}
is the proper transform of a divisor
\Gamma_{i}
on
Y_{0}
disjoint from
D_{i}^{\prime}
and containing
p
as a point of multiplicity
2
with
p_{a}(\Gamma_{i})=2
.

(ii)

M_{i}
is nef. Then
M_{i}^{2}=0
and
m_{i}=1
. Moreover,
|2M_{i}|
is a base point free linear system defining an elliptic fibration
\widetilde{Y}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
(not necessarily relatively minimal) with at most
3
multiple fibers, all of multiplicity
2
. More precisely, there is a single multiple fiber if
\widetilde{Y}
is rational and there are
2
or
3
multiple fibers if
\widetilde{Y}
is an elliptic ruled surface. Finally, either
D_{i}
is an irreducible bisection or one irreducible component of
D_{i}
is a smooth bisection of the fibration, and the remaining components are curves of self-intersection
-2
contained in a single reducible, non-multiple fiber.

Proof.

Case (i):
M_{i}
is not nef. Let
C
be as in the statement of Lemma
2.14
. Contracting
C
gives a morphism
\gamma_{1}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{1}
, where
Y_{1}
is a smooth surface containing a union of curves isomorphic to
D_{i}^{\prime}
, which we will continue to denote by
D_{i}^{\prime}
. Let
N_{1}=\gamma_{1}{}_{*}M_{1}
, so that
N_{1}=\gamma_{1}{}_{*}(K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{i}^{\prime})=K_{Y_{1}}+D_{i}^{\prime}
and
M_{i}=\gamma_{1}^{*}N_{1}+C=\gamma_{1}^{*}K_{Y_{1}}+C+D_{i}^{\prime}
. The argument of Lemma
2.14
shows that, if
N_{1}
is not nef, then there exists an exceptional curve
C^{\prime}
on
\overline{Y}
such that
N_{1}\cdot C^{\prime}<0
and
C^{\prime}\cdot D_{i}^{\prime}=0
. Then we can keep repeating this argument, which cannot continue indefinitely. Thus we eventually find a birational morphism
\gamma\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
, where
Y_{0}
is smooth, and a nef divisor
N_{0}
on
Y_{0}
such that
M_{i}=\gamma^{*}N_{0}+\sum_{j}a_{j}C_{j}=\gamma^{*}K_{Y_{0}}+\sum_{j}a_{j}C_{j}+D_{i}^{\prime}
, where the
C_{j}
are the components of the one-dimensional fibers of the morphism
\gamma\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
and
a_{j}>0
. Then
L\cdot\gamma^{*}N_{0}\geq 0
since
L
and
\gamma^{*}N_{0}
are nef, and
L\cdot a_{j}C_{j}\geq a_{j}
if
C_{j}
is exceptional. Since at least one curve in every connected positive dimensional fiber of
\gamma
is exceptional and

1=L\cdot M_{i}=(L\cdot\gamma^{*}N_{0})+\sum_{j}a_{j}(L\cdot C_{j}),

the only possibility via Lemma
2.1
is that there is just one exceptional curve
C=C_{j}
,
a_{j}=1
, and
L\cdot\gamma^{*}N_{0}=0
. Since
N_{0}
is nef,
N_{0}^{2}=(\gamma^{*}N_{0})^{2}\geq 0
. By the Hodge index theorem,
\gamma^{*}N_{0}\equiv 0
and hence
N_{0}\equiv 0
. Thus
M_{i}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{i}^{\prime}\equiv C
. Since
M_{i}
is effective and
C
is irreducible with
C^{2}<0
,
M_{i}=C
. Hence
L=M_{i}+D_{i}=D_{i}+C
and
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=C-D_{i}^{\prime}
. Then
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\gamma^{*}K_{Y_{0}}+C=C-D_{i}^{\prime}
, so that
\gamma^{*}K_{Y_{0}}=-D_{i}^{\prime}=-\gamma^{*}D_{i}^{\prime}
where we view the divisor
D_{i}^{\prime}
as living on
Y_{0}
. Thus
K_{Y_{0}}=-D_{i}^{\prime}
since
\gamma^{*}
is injective. Next we claim that
C\cdot D_{i}=2
, as follows from the equalities

1=L^{2}=L\cdot(D_{i}+C)=L\cdot C=(D_{i}+C)\cdot C=(D_{i}\cdot C)+C^{2}=(D_{i}\cdot C)-1.

The statement that
m_{i}=2
follows from the equalities

0=L\cdot D_{i}=(M_{i}+D_{i})\cdot D_{i}=(D_{i}+C)\cdot D_{i}=-m_{i}+2.

If
\Gamma_{i}=\gamma(D_{i})
, then
D_{i}=\gamma^{*}\Gamma_{i}-2C
because
D_{i}\cdot C=2
. This proves the remaining statements in Case (i).

Case (ii):
M_{i}
is nef. Then
M_{i}^{2}=1-m_{i}\geq 0
. Hence
m_{i}=1
and
M_{i}^{2}=0
. Next we claim that
|2M_{i}|
is a base point free linear system with
\dim|2M_{i}|\geq 1
. This is a minor variation on a standard argument in classification theory (cf. e.g.
[
Fri98
, Lemma 24 p. 295]
): First,
h^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(nM_{i}))=h^{0}(Y_{i};\omega_{Y_{i}}^{\otimes n})
. By an easy extension of the Riemann-Roch theorem to a projective Gorenstein surface with isolated singularities,

\chi(Y_{i};\omega_{Y_{i}}^{\otimes n})=\frac{n^{2}M_{i}^{2}-nM_{i}^{2}}{2}+\chi(Y_{i};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{i}})=2.

For
n\geq 2
,
h^{2}(Y_{i};\omega_{Y_{i}}^{\otimes n})=h^{0}(Y_{i};\omega_{Y_{i}}^{\otimes 1-n})=h^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}((1-n)M_{i}))=0
since
M_{i}
is effective and nonzero. Thus

h^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(nM_{i}))=h^{0}(Y_{i};\omega_{Y_{i}}^{\otimes n})\geq 2

for all
n\geq 2
, in particular for
n=2
. Write
2M_{i}=G_{f}+G_{m}
, where
G_{f}
is the fixed curve and
G_{m}
the free part of the linear system
|2M_{i}|
. Since
M_{i}
is nef,
M_{i}\cdot G_{f}\geq 0
and
M_{i}\cdot G_{m}\geq 0
, and hence
M_{i}\cdot G_{f}=M_{i}\cdot G_{m}=0
. By the Hodge index theorem, since
M_{i}
is not numerically equivalent to
0
,
G_{f}
and
G_{m}
are rational multiples of
M_{i}
and hence
G_{m}^{2}=G_{f}^{2}=0
. Thus
|2M_{i}|=|G_{m}|
defines a fibration
\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to B
over some base curve
B
. Let
G
be a general fiber of
\varphi
, so that
G
is smooth and
G^{2}=0
. Since
G\cdot M_{i}=G_{f}\cdot M_{i}=G_{m}\cdot M_{i}=0
, every effective curve
\Gamma\in|M_{i}|
is contained in fibers of the morphism
\varphi
as is
G_{f}
. Hence
\Gamma
and
G_{f}
are numerically equivalent to positive rational multiples of
G
since
M_{i}^{2}=G_{f}^{2}=0
. Also,
M_{i}
is a primitive class since
L\cdot M_{i}=1
. Since
2M_{i}=G_{f}+G_{m}
, either
G_{f}=0
and
G_{m}=2M_{i}
or
G_{f}\equiv G_{m}\equiv M_{i}
. Then
G_{m}\equiv aG
for some positive integer

and hence
L\cdot G\leq 2
. Moreover,
G\cdot K_{\widetilde{Y}}=0
by Lemma
2.10
(iv), hence

is an elliptic curve.

Let
\varphi_{0}\colon Y_{0}\to B
be the relatively minimal model of
\varphi
. Then, identifying
G
with a general fiber of
\varphi_{0}
,

K_{Y_{0}}=(2g-2+\chi)G+\sum_{\alpha}(m_{\alpha}-1)F_{\alpha},

where

is the genus of

,
\chi=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}})=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}})\geq 0
, and the

are the multiple fibers of multiplicities

. Since

,

as well, and hence
m_{\alpha}=2
for every
\alpha
. Because
\kappa(Y_{0})=\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
,

,

,
\chi=0
(the elliptic ruled case) or
1
(the rational case), and

\chi-2+\sum_{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{\alpha}}\right)=\chi-2+\frac{\#(\alpha)}{2}<0.

In particular, in the rational case there is at most one multiple fiber. In the elliptic ruled case,
Y_{0}
is a logarithmic transform of a product elliptic surface

and there are either
0
,
2
or
3
multiple fibers, because a logarithmic transform of a product surface at a single fiber is never algebraic (cf.
[
FM94
, I.6.12]
). Then we have the following formula for the canonical bundle of

:

K_{\widetilde{Y}}=(\chi-2)G+\sum_{\alpha}F_{\alpha}+\sum_{t}a_{t}C_{t},

where the
C_{t}
are the exceptional curves in the blowup
\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
. Since
M_{i}\cdot D_{i}^{\prime}=0
, the connected components of
D_{i}^{\prime}
are contained in fibers of
\varphi
and have arithmetic genus one. Hence

D_{i}^{\prime}
is a union of proper transforms of fibers of
\varphi_{0}
, and so

D_{i}^{\prime}=nG+\sum_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}F_{\alpha}-\sum_{t}b_{t}C_{t},

where
n,n_{\alpha},b_{t}
are nonnegative integers. Since
M_{i}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{i}^{\prime}
and
M_{i}^{2}=0
,
a_{t}=b_{t}
for all

. Thus

M_{i}=(n+\chi-2)G+\sum_{\alpha}(n_{\alpha}+1)F_{\alpha}.

First suppose that
\widetilde{Y}
is rational. Then
G\equiv M_{i}
is not possible: in this case, we would have
G=M_{i}
, hence
h^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}(M_{i}))=2
, contradicting Lemma
2.10
(v). Thus
n\leq 1
. Since
M_{i}
is effective, there has to be at least one multiple fiber. But
\widetilde{Y}
is rational, so there is exactly one multiple fiber
F
, necessarily of multiplicity
2
. In this case,
M_{i}=F
since
M_{i}
is primitive and
G=2M_{i}=2F
.

Now suppose that
\widetilde{Y}
is elliptic ruled. As noted above, there are either no multiple fibers or there are
2
or
3
multiple fibers
F_{\alpha}
, all of multiplicity
2
. If there are no multiple fibers, then
Y_{0}\cong G\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a product elliptic surface. In this case
G\equiv M_{i}
is not possible, because otherwise
M_{i}
is numerically equivalent to a fiber
G\times\{t\}
. Then
M_{i}=\pi_{1}^{*}\lambda\otimes\pi_{2}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(1)
for a line bundle
\lambda
of degree
0
on
B
, and then
h^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(M_{i}))
is either
0
or
2
, contradicting Lemma
2.10
(v). But
G\equiv 2M_{i}
is also not possible, for then
M_{i}=(n+\chi-2)G\equiv 2mM_{i}
for some integer
m
, but
M_{i}
is not numerically trivial.
Hence there are
2
or
3
multiple fibers. In this case,
G\equiv M_{i}
is not possible, because then
M_{i}=2F_{\alpha}
would not be primitive.

In all cases,
G=2M_{i}
, hence
G_{f}=0
and
G_{m}=G
. Note that
M_{i}
is effective, so
M_{i}=F
for a multiple fiber
F
. Since
M_{i}\cdot D_{i}=1
,

is a bisection of the fibration. More precisely, since
M_{i}
is nef, exactly one component of
D_{i}
is a bisection and the remaining components are fiber components. In particular, if

is reducible, then
\widetilde{Y}
is a rational surface. Since
D_{i}
is then both a bisection and a cycle of rational curves, it is clear that the remaining components must lie in a single reduced fiber.
∎

3.
The case
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=1

3.1.
The cotangent bundle

We begin with a fairly general result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is an elliptic surface with
p_{g}(X)=p_{g}
and that the only non-reduced fibers of
\pi
are multiple fibers
F_{i}
with multiplicities
m_{i}
. Then there is an exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{X}(-2f+\sum_{i}(m_{i}-1)F_{i})\to\Omega^{1}_{X}\to I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}((p_{g}+1)f)\to 0,

where
Z
is a zero-dimensional subscheme of length
\ell(Z)=12(1+p_{g})
supported at the singular points of the reductions of the fibers, and at a point
x
of a fiber whose reduction has a normal crossing singularity,
I_{Z,x}=\mathfrak{m}_{x}
is the maximal ideal at
x
.

Proof.

The natural homomorphism
\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\to\Omega^{1}_{X}
vanishes along the multiple fibers, to order exactly
m_{i}-1
along
F_{i}
. Thus there is an induced homomorphism

\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\sum_{i}(m_{i}-1)F_{i})=\mathcal{O}_{X}(-2f+\sum_{i}(m_{i}-1)F_{i})\to\Omega^{1}_{X}

whose cokernel is torsion free and hence of the form
I_{Z}\otimes\lambda
for a line bundle
\lambda
. By taking determinants and using the the canonical bundle formula,

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=K_{X}\otimes(\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\sum_{i}(m_{i}-1)F_{i}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{X}((p_{g}-1)f+\sum_{i}(m_{i}-1)F_{i})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(2f-\sum_{i}(m_{i}-1)F_{i})=\mathcal{O}_{X}((p_{g}+1)f).

It follows that

c_{2}(X)=c_{2}(T_{X})=c_{2}(\Omega^{1}_{X})=\ell(Z),

and hence that
\ell(Z)=12(1+p_{g})
. A local calculation then establishes that
I_{Z,x}=\mathfrak{m}_{x}
in case the reduction of the fiber containing
x
is nodal.
∎

We will need a somewhat specialized variant of Theorem
3.1
for logarithmic
1
-forms:

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is an elliptic surface such that all non-multiple fibers are nodal and there is exactly one multiple fiber
F
of
\pi
, of multiplicity
2
. Let
D=E_{1}+\cdots+E_{r}
be a reduced normal crossing cycle of rational curves, where
E_{1}
is a smooth bisection and the
E_{i}
for

are components of a non-multiple I
a
fiber

, with
E_{1}\cdot E_{2}=E_{1}\cdot E_{r}\neq 0
and
E_{i}\cdot E_{i+1}=1
,

. Then there is an exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{X}(-2f+F+\sum_{i>1}E_{i})\to\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D)\to I_{W}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}((p_{g}+1)f+E_{1})\to 0,

where

is a zero-dimensional subscheme of length
\ell(W)=12(1+p_{g})-r+1
. Moreover,
x\in\operatorname{Supp}W

\iff
either
x
is a singular point of a fiber not equal to
f_{t}
,
x
is a singular point of
f_{t}-D
, or
x
is a ramification point for the induced double cover morphism
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, necessarily not on
f_{t}
. Finally,

(i)

If
x
is a point of a fiber different from
f_{t}
whose reduction has a normal crossing singularity at
x
and
x\notin E_{1}
, then
I_{W,x}=\mathfrak{m}_{x}
is the maximal ideal at
x
.

(ii)

If
x\in E_{1}-F
is a ramification point for the induced double cover morphism
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
x
is not a singular point of the corresponding fiber, then
I_{W,x}=\mathfrak{m}_{x}
.

(iii)

If
x\in E_{1}-F
is a ramification point for the induced double cover morphism
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
x
is a singular point of the corresponding fiber, then
\ell(I_{W,x})=2
.

(iv)

If
x\in f_{t}-D
is a singular point of
f_{t}
, then
I_{W,x}=\mathfrak{m}_{x}
.

Proof.

Note that
\det\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D)=K_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)=\mathcal{O}_{X}((p_{g}-1)f+F+D)
. As in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, the natural map
\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\to\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D)
vanishes along
F
and has a torsion free cokernel away from
F+D
. If
x\notin D
, then the analysis of the proof of Theorem
3.1
shows that the cokernel of
\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(F)\to\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D)
is locally free at

is not a singular point of a fiber and is locally isomorphic to
\mathfrak{m}_{x}
if
x
is a singular point of a fiber.

Next suppose that
x\in E_{1}-f_{t}
. If the fiber containing
x
meets
E_{1}
transversally (including the case where
x\in E_{1}\cap F
), then the cokernel of
\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(F)\to\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log D)
is locally free at
x
. Otherwise,

is a branch point of the morphism
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
and a local calculation shows that
\ell(I_{W,x})=1
if

is a smooth point of the fiber and
\ell(I_{W,x})=2
otherwise.

Thus we may assume that
x\in f_{t}\cap D=\sum_{i>1}E_{i}
. If

is a smooth point of

, then, locally around

, there exist local coordinates

such that

is defined by

and the morphism

is given by

. A local basis for

is
\displaystyle\frac{dz_{1}}{z_{1}},dz_{2}
and the image of

. Thus the cokernel of the induced morphism

is not in the support of the scheme

. Next suppose that

such that the fiber is defined at

by

This vanishes to order one along

and the cokernel of the induced morphism

.

. A similar argument shows that, if

Thus,

∎

3.2.
Multiple fibers of multiplicity
2
and double covers

Let
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be a relatively minimal elliptic surface with a single multiple fiber, of multiplicity
2
, with reduction
F
, together with an irreducible, not necessarily smooth bisection
\Sigma
. There is an involution on the generic fiber
X_{\eta}
, defined by: if
p
is a smooth point of a fiber
f
, then
\iota(p)=p^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{O}_{X}(\Sigma)|f\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}(p+p^{\prime})
.
Hence (by relative minimality) there an induced involution
\iota\colon X\to X
, whose fixed locus is a
4
-section together with some isolated fixed points, in particular two isolated fixed points on
F
. Note that
\iota
only depends on the restriction of
\Sigma
to the generic fiber. We make the following additional assumption on
X
, which will be satisfied in the case of interest (Lemma
3.5
, Theorem
3.6
):

Assumption 3.3
.

The multiple fiber
F
is irreducible, the non-multiple fibers of
\pi
have at most two components, and, in case there are two components,
\Sigma
has intersection number one with each component.

Assumption
3.3
has the following consequences: Every non-multiple fiber is reduced, and is either a nodal or a cuspidal rational curve, if irreducible, or a I
2
fiber or a tacnodal fiber, if there are two components. The involution
\iota
has exactly two isolated fixed points and they both lie on
F
. Finally, if the fiber has two components, they are exchanged by
\iota
. Thus, all fibers of the induced morphism
X/\iota\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
are irreducible, but
X/\iota
has two ordinary double points at the fixed points of
\iota
and is otherwise smooth.

Then as in
[
Hor78
]
,
[
Fri95
]
, the blowup of
X
at the two fixed points is obtained as follows: let
\mathbb{F}_{N}
be a Hirzebruch surface,
N\geq 0
, and let
\sigma_{0}
be the negative section if
N>0
and any section of square
0
in case
N=0
. Blow up a point
p
in a fiber
f
, such that
p\notin\sigma_{0}
, and denote the exceptional curve by
d_{1}^{\prime}
and the proper transform
f-d_{1}^{\prime}
by
d_{2}^{\prime}
. Note that we can always assume that we are in the case
p\notin\sigma_{0}
, because if
p\in\sigma_{0}
then the proper transform of
\sigma_{0}
has self-intersection
-N-1
and the proper transform of the fiber is a disjoint exceptional curve. Thus we could have started instead with the elementary transform
\mathbb{F}_{N+1}
of
\mathbb{F}_{N}
and a point not in the negative section. In particular, in case
N=0
, there is always a section
\sigma_{0}
of square
0
passing through a point
p
. Hence we can in fact always assume that
N\geq 1
in what follows. Finally blow up the intersection point of
d_{1}^{\prime}
and
d_{2}^{\prime}
and let
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{N}
be the resulting surface. Denote the new exceptional curve by
e
and the proper transforms of
d_{1}^{\prime},d_{2}^{\prime}
by
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. Then
d_{1}^{2}=d_{2}^{2}=-2
,

and

, where
f
is the class of a general fiber of
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{N}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Identifying
\sigma_{0}
with its preimage in
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{N}
,
d_{2}\cdot\sigma_{0}=1
,
d_{1}\cdot\sigma_{0}=e\cdot\sigma_{0}=0
, and
e\cdot d_{1}=e\cdot d_{2}=1

Note: the labeling of the curves

is the opposite of that in

.

Choose a smooth

\displaystyle=B_{0}+d_{1}+d_{2}=4\sigma_{0}+2kf-4e-2d_{1}+f+d_{1}+d_{2}

\displaystyle=4\sigma_{0}+2kf-2e+2d_{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle=B\cdot e=4;

\displaystyle B\cdot\sigma_{0}

Then

3.3.
A vanishing theorem: the case where
D
is irreducible

For the rest of this section, we assume that
\widetilde{Y}
is the minimal resolution of an I-surface with
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=1
. In particular,
\widetilde{Y}
is minimal and either there exists an irreducible bisection
D
with
D^{2}=-1
and
p_{a}(D)=1
or there exists a smooth rational bisection
E_{1}
with
E_{1}^{2}=-3
. Thus we can apply the previous results to
X=\widetilde{Y}
. First we note the following, recalling that Assumption
2.7
, that there are no rational double points on
Y
, is always in force:

Lemma 3.5
.

If
D
is irreducible, then
F
is irreducible and every fiber of the morphism
\widetilde{Y}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
has at most two components. In particular, every non-multiple fiber is reduced, and is either a nodal or a cuspidal rational curve, if irreducible, or an I
2
fiber or a tacnodal fiber, if there are two components.

If
D
is reducible, then exactly one component of
D
is an irreducible bisection and the remaining components are contained in a single fiber
f_{t}
. In this case,
F
is irreducible and all nonmultiple fibers except for
f_{t}
have at most two components and are nodal, cuspidal, or a tacnode. Moreover, all but at most two components of
f_{t}
are contained in
D
.

Proof.

By assumption, if
\Gamma
is an irreducible curve on
\widetilde{Y}
and
\Gamma
is not a component of
D
, then
(F+D)\cdot\Gamma>0
. However, if
\Gamma
is a component of a reducible fiber, then
F\cdot\Gamma=0
so that
D\cdot\Gamma>0
. But
D\cdot F=1
and
D\cdot f=2
, where
f
is any non-multiple fiber. It follows that
F
has just one component and every non-multiple fiber not containing a component of
D
has at most two. By the classification of singular fibers, every non-multiple fiber with at most two components is nodal, cuspidal, or a tacnode, and in particular is reduced. If
D
is reducible, then exactly one component is a bisection, necessarily smooth rational of square
-3
, and the remaining components therefore form a chain of
-2
-curves. In particular, since the chain is connected and has intersection number
0
with a general fiber, it must be contained in a single fiber
f_{t}
. Finally, since
D\cdot f=2
and
D\cdot C>0
for every component of
f_{t}
which is not a component of
D
, it is clear that there can be at most two such components.
∎

In this subsection, we consider the irreducible case and show:

Theorem 3.6
.

If
D
is irreducible and there are no cusp or tacnodal fibers, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
.

Proof.

Since
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D)=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(F+D)
, by duality it suffices to show that

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D))=H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(F+D))=0.

By Theorem
3.1
, there is an exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-2f+F)\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}\to I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f)\to 0,

where
Z
is the scheme defined by the singular points of the singular, non-multiple fibers. Hence
\ell(Z)=24
and by our assumptions
Z
consists of
24
distinct points. Thus it suffices to show that

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-2f+2F+D))=H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+D))=0.

First, since
-2f+2F+D=D-f
,
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-2f+2F+D))=0
as
D-f
is not effective (because
D^{2}<0
). So it is enough to show that
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+D))=0
. Referring to §
3.2
, there is a blowup
\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}
of
\widetilde{Y}
at two smooth points of
F
and a degree
2
morphism
\nu\colon\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}\to\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
, branched along a divisor in
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
of the form
B_{0}+d_{1}+d_{2}
, where
B_{0}\in|4\sigma_{0}+5f+2d_{2}|
. Here, if
e_{1},e_{2}
are the exceptional curves on
\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}
and
F^{\prime}
is the proper transform of the fiber, then
\nu^{*}\sigma_{0}=D
,
\nu^{*}d_{i}=2e_{i}
,
\nu^{*}e=F^{\prime}
, and (using
f
to denote a general fiber of
\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
as well as
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
),
\nu^{*}f=f
. Also, we can identify
Z
with the corresponding subscheme of
\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}
. The total transform of
F
on
\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}
is
F^{\prime}+e_{1}+e_{2}
, and hence

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+D))=H^{0}(\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}};I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}}(2f+F^{\prime}+e_{1}+e_{2}+D)).

By the above remarks, since
\nu^{*}d_{i}=2e_{i}
, there is an inclusion

\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}}(2f+F^{\prime}+e_{1}+e_{2}+D)\subseteq\nu^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(2f+e+d_{1}+d_{2}+\sigma_{0}).

In what follows, we will repeatedly use the following for divisor classes on
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
:

2f+e+d_{1}+d_{2}+\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{0}+3f-e.

For every line bundle
M
on
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
, we have
\nu_{*}\nu^{*}M=M\oplus(M\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(-\frac{1}{2}B))
,
where
\frac{1}{2}B
is the divisor
2\sigma_{0}+3f-e+d_{2}
. Applying this to
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)
, first note that

\sigma_{0}+3f-e-(2\sigma_{0}+3f-e+d_{2})=-\sigma_{0}-d_{2}.

Since this last divisor is not effective, every section of
\nu^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)
is the pullback of a section
s
of
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)
, and we need to find sections vanishing on
\nu(Z)
. If
x\in Z
, then
x
is singular point of the fiber of
\widetilde{Y}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
which contains it. Viewing
x
as a point on
\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}
,
x
is the preimage of a point on a fiber of
\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
which is tangent to
B_{0}
and the point
\nu(x)
is a point of tangency. In particular,
\nu(Z)
consists of
24
points and the section
s
has to vanish at those points of
B_{0}
. (This includes the case where
F
is singular; since
B_{0}\cdot e=2
and
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
meet
e
transversally,
F
is singular
\iff

B_{0}
meets
e
doubly at a single point.) Note that

(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)-B_{0}=-3\sigma_{0}-3f-d_{2}+e+d_{1}

is not effective, and so a section
s
of
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)
vanishing along
B_{0}
has to vanish identically. Conversely, if
s\neq 0
, then the curve
(s)
defined by
s
satisfies:
\#((s)\cap B_{0})
is finite and
\geq 24
. Hence
B_{0}\cdot(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)\geq 24
. But

\displaystyle B_{0}\cdot(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)=(4\sigma_{0}+5f+2d_{2})\cdot(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)

\displaystyle=-4+12+5+2-2=13<24.

Thus, a section
\sigma
of
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)
vanishing at the
24
points of
\nu(Z)
must be identically
0
. Hence
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};I_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+D))=0
, so that
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The proof still works if there are a small number of cuspidal or tacnodal fibers. Concretely, let
c
be the number of cuspidal fibers and
t
the number of tacnodal fibers. Then the above proof works as long as

. It is possible that this, or a more detailed analysis, is enough to handle the general case.

Corollary 3.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.6
, the deformations of

are versal for the deformations of the simple elliptic singularity or cusp singularity, i.e. the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective. ∎

3.4.
A vanishing theorem: the case where
D
is reducible

We prove an analogue of Theorem
3.6
in the case of a cusp singularity with
r>1
, again under some mild general position assumptions.

Theorem 3.9
.

Suppose that all fibers of
\widetilde{Y}
are nodal. Let
D=E_{1}+\cdots+E_{r}
correspond to a cusp singularity, where
E_{1}
is a smooth bisection with
E_{1}^{2}=-3
and the remaining components are fiber components of the fiber
f_{t}
, with
r\leq 12
. Then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
3.6
. We must show that
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D)\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
. Let
\nu\colon\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}\to\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
be the double cover morphism of §
3.2
, where
\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}
is the blowup of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
described at the end of §
3.2
. The exact sequence of Theorem
3.2
gives an exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-2f+2F+\sum_{i>1}E_{i})\to\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D)\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}}\to I_{W}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+E_{1})\to 0,

with
\ell(W)=25-r
. Moreover, if
x\in\operatorname{Supp}W
, then either
x
is a singular point of a fiber or
x
is a ramification point for the morphism
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
not on
F
. Since
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a double cover with
E_{1}
a smooth rational curve, the morphism
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
ramifies at the intersection
E_{1}\cap F
and at one other point. In all cases, if
x\in\operatorname{Supp}W
, then either
x
is a singular point of its fiber and hence
x\in B_{0}
or
x
is the unique ramification point of
E_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
not on
F
and again
x\in B_{0}
, and
W
has length at most
2
at
x
. Thus, if
\overline{W}=\nu(W)
, then
\overline{W}\subseteq B_{0}
and

\#(\overline{W})\geq\ell(W)-1=24-r.

As
-2f+2F+\sum_{i>1}E_{i}=-f+\sum_{i>1}E_{i}
is the negative of an effective divisor,

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-2f+2F+\sum_{i>1}E_{i}))=0.

We must show that
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};I_{W}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+E_{1}))=0
as well. It suffices to show that

H^{0}(\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}};I_{W}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}}}(2f+F^{\prime}+e_{1}+e_{2}+E_{1}))=0,

which will follow if we show that

H^{0}(\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}};I_{W}\otimes\nu^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}}(2f+e+d_{1}+d_{2}+\sigma_{0}))=0.

We again use the equality of divisor classes

2f+e+d_{1}+d_{2}+\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{0}+3f-e.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.6
,

H^{0}(\widetilde{\widetilde{Y}};\nu^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e))=H^{0}(\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2};\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)).

It suffices to show that
H^{0}(\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2};I_{\overline{W}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e))=0
. Suppose by contradiction that there is a nonzero section
s
of
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}}(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)
vanishing along
\overline{W}
. As in the proof of Theorem
3.6
, such a section cannot vanish identically along
B_{0}
. Now we compute as before, ignoring the distinction between
B_{0}
and
\overline{B}_{0}
because it does not affect the computation:

B_{0}\cdot(\sigma_{0}+3f-e)=(B_{0}\cdot\sigma_{0})+3(B_{0}\cdot f)-2(B_{0}\cdot e)=1+12-2=11.

In particular, we must have
\#(\overline{W})\leq 11
. But we have seen that
\#(\overline{W})\geq 24-r
, hence
r\geq 13
. Conversely, if
r\leq 12
, then
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};I_{W}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(2f+F+E_{1}))=0
. Thus
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.9
, the deformations of

are versal for the deformations of the cusp singularity, i.e. the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective. ∎

4.1.

We begin by defining various lattices which will arise in what follows.

(i) Let
\Lambda_{0}(n)
be the lattice with basis vectors

, satisfying:

,

for

for

(including the case

(ii) Let

,
e_{n}\cdot g_{2}=f_{m}\cdot g_{2}=1
,

, and all other intersections are

.

(iii) Let
\Lambda_{2}(n,m)
be the lattice spanned by vectors

with

,

for

, and

(including the case

), similarly

for

, and

(including the case

(i,j)\neq(0,0)
.

A straightforward argument shows:

Lemma 4.2
.

The lattices
\Lambda_{0}(n)
,
\Lambda_{1}(n,m)
, and
\Lambda_{2}(n,m)
are nondegenerate of signatures
(1,n)
,
(1,n+m+1)
, and
(1,n+m+1)
respectively. ∎

4.2.
A lemma on double covers

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
\nu\colon W\to Z
is a double cover branched along
\Sigma
, where
Z
,
W
, and
\Sigma
are all smooth. Let
\lambda
be the line bundle defining the double cover, i.e.
\lambda^{\otimes 2}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Z}(\Sigma)
. Then there is an exact sequence

0\to\Omega^{1}_{Z}\to\nu_{*}\Omega^{1}_{W}\to\Omega^{1}_{Z}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\lambda^{-1}\to 0.

Proof.

Choose local holomorphic coordinates
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
near a point of
\Sigma
so that
\Sigma
is defined by
z_{n}=0
and the map
\nu
is given by
z_{i}=w_{1}
,
i<n
, and
z_{n}=w_{n}^{2}
, i.e.
w_{n}=\sqrt{z_{n}}
. Thus
z_{n}
is a local section of
\mathcal{O}_{Z}(-\Sigma)
and
\sqrt{z_{n}}
is a local section of
L^{-1}
. Then

dz_{n}=2w_{n}\,dw_{n}
, i.e.
\displaystyle dw_{n}=\frac{dz_{n}}{2\sqrt{z_{n}}}
. Thus a basis for
\nu_{*}\Omega^{1}_{W}
as an
\mathcal{O}_{Z}
-module is given locally by

dz_{1},\sqrt{z_{n}}dz_{1},\dots,dz_{n-1},\sqrt{z_{n}}dz_{n-1},dz_{n},\frac{dz_{n}}{\sqrt{z_{n}}}.

Hence the quotient of
\nu_{*}\Omega^{1}_{W}
by the subspace
\Omega^{1}_{Z}
has as a basis

\sqrt{z_{n}}dz_{1},\dots,\frac{dz_{n}}{\sqrt{z_{n}}}=\sqrt{z_{n}}\cdot\frac{dz_{n}}{z_{n}}.

This says invariantly that the quotient is
\Omega^{1}_{Z}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\lambda^{-1}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

In fact, the natural involution on
\nu_{*}\Omega^{1}_{W}
coming from the double cover involution induces a splitting

\nu_{*}\Omega^{1}_{W}\cong\Omega^{1}_{Z}\oplus\Big(\Omega^{1}_{Z}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\lambda^{-1}\Big).

4.3.
The case where
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of a
K3
surface

Throughout this subsection, we assume that the minimal model
Y_{0}
of
\widetilde{Y}
is a (smooth)
K3
surface. By Theorem
2.12
(iii), there exists a morphism
\rho\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
, where
\rho
is the blowup of
Y_{0}
at a single point
p\in Y_{0}
, and the exceptional curve
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
satisfies:
C\cdot D=2
. In particular,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(C)
.

First, we consider the case where
D
is irreducible, and is thus either an elliptic curve or a nodal rational curve.

Theorem 4.5
.

If
D
is irreducible, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
. Hence the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective.

Proof.

With notation as above, let
\overline{D}=\rho(D)
be the image of
D
in
Y_{0}
. Then
\overline{D}
is an irreducible curve with either a node or a cusp, and
p_{a}(\overline{D})=2
. Thus
\overline{D}^{2}=2
and the complete linear system
|\overline{D}|
defines a morphism
\varphi\colon Y_{0}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
, for which
\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D})=\varphi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)
. By hypothesis,
L=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D+C)
is finite on the complement of
D
. Thus, if
\Gamma
is an irreducible curve in
\widetilde{Y}
not equal to
D
, then either
D\cdot\Gamma>0
or
C\cdot\Gamma>0
, so for every irreducible curve
\Gamma_{0}
on
Y_{0}
,

\Gamma_{0}\cdot\overline{D}=\Gamma_{0}\cdot\rho_{*}D=\rho^{*}\Gamma_{0}\cdot D>0.

Hence
\varphi\colon Y_{0}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a finite degree
2
morphism and its branch locus is a smooth sextic curve
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Thus the square root
\lambda
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Sigma)
is
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(3)
.

By Serre duality,
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))
is dual to

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(C))
. A section of
\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(C)
defines a section of
\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D})
over
Y_{0}-\{p\}
which then extends to a holomorphic section of
\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D})
over
Y_{0}
by Hartogs’ theorem. Thus it will suffice to prove that
H^{0}(Y_{0};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D}))=0
. Equivalently, since
\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D})=\varphi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)
, it suffices to show that
H^{0}(\varphi_{*}\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1))=0
. By Lemma
4.3
, since
\lambda^{-1}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-3)
, it is enough to show that

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1))=H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2))=0.

The fact that
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1))=0
is well-known and follows from looking at the global sections coming from the Euler exact sequence

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)\to\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\right)^{3}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)\to 0.

As for the remaining equality
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2))=0
, the Poincaré residue sequence gives

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2)\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\log\Sigma)(-2)\to\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(-2)\to 0.

On global sections,
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2))=0
via the Euler exact sequence, and
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(-2))=0
because
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2)|\Sigma
is a line bundle of negative degree. Thus
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D)=0
as claimed.
∎

In case
D
is reducible, we have the following which uses a mild general position assumption.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose that
D
is reducible, and assume that, if there is a component
E
of
D
of self-intersection
-4
, then the exceptional curve
C
meets
E
in
2
distinct points.
Then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
, and hence the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective.

Proof.

Since all components of
D
are smooth rational,
C
is disjoint from every component of square
-2
. If there is a component
E
of self-intersection
-4
, then
C\cdot E=2
, and if there are two components of square
-3
, then
C
meets each of them transversally at one point. Thus, the hypothesis of the theorem is exactly that the curve
C+D
is nodal on
\widetilde{Y}
, or equivalently that
\overline{D}=\rho(D)
is nodal.

The group
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))
is dual to
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(C))
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.5
, it suffices to prove that

H^{0}(Y_{0};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\log\overline{D}))=0
. Thus it suffices to prove that the fundamental classes of the components of
\overline{D}
are independent in cohomology. This follows from Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Suppose that there is a component
E
of
D
of self-intersection
-4
with

or two components of self-intersection
-3
with
r\geq 3
. Then there is a deformation of
Y
to an I-surface

with a cusp or simple elliptic singularity of multiplicity

. Hence the minimal resolution
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
is a surface with
\kappa(\widetilde{Y}_{t})=1
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorem
4.6
and Theorem

(iii).
∎

Before we turn to an example showing that there are indeed examples satisfying the hypothesis of Corollary
4.7
, we note the following standard result:

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X
be a
K3
surface and let

be smooth rational curves on

meeting transversally whose fundamental classes are linearly independent in cohomology. Let

and hence the map

is surjective.

Proof.

By Serre duality, we have to show that
H^{0}(X;\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log C))=0
, which is an easy consequence of the Poincaré residue sequence.
∎

4.4.
A remark on semistable smoothings

The method of proof above can be applied to show the existence of semistable smoothings. This picture is essentially the semistable version of one of Wahl’s “exotic” adjacencies (Theorem
1.5
(iii)(b)). First we recall the terminology of
[
Fri83b
]
:

Definition 4.12
.

Let
Z=Z_{1}\cup_{D}Z_{2}
be the simple normal crossing surface obtained by gluing the two smooth surfaces
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
along a smooth divisor
D\subseteq Z_{i}
, where
D\subseteq Z_{1}
is identified with
D\subseteq Z_{2}
by the choice of an isomorphism
\gamma
. Then
Z
is
d
-semistable
if
N_{D/Z_{1}}\otimes\gamma^{*}N_{D/Z_{2}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D}
.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
X
be a
K3
surface with an irreducible nodal rational curve
\overline{D}
with
p_{a}(\overline{D})=1
, so that
\overline{D}^{2}=0
. Let
\widetilde{X}
be the blowup of
X
at the singular point of
\overline{D}
and let
D
be the proper transform of
\overline{D}
, so that
D^{2}=-4
. Then:

(i)

H^{2}(\widetilde{X};T_{\widetilde{X}}(-\log D))=0
.

(ii)

The
d
-semistable surface
Z
obtained by gluing
\widetilde{X}
to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
by identifying
D
with a smooth conic in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is smoothable, and its smoothings are elliptic surfaces with
p_{g}=1
and a single multiple fiber, of multiplicity
2
.

Proof.

(i) The arguments used in the proof of Theorem
4.6
show that the dual of
H^{2}(\widetilde{X};T_{\widetilde{X}}(-\log D))
injects into
H^{0}(X;\Omega^{1}_{X}(\log\overline{D}))
, and this is
0
as the fundamental class of
\overline{D}
is nonzero in
H^{1}(X;\Omega^{1}_{X})
.

(ii) Let
\nu\colon\widetilde{X}\amalg\mathbb{P}^{2}\to Z
be the normalization. By a local calculation (cf.
[
FL23
, Lemma 4.6(iv)]
, there is an exact sequence

0\to T^{0}_{Z}\to\nu_{*}(T_{\widetilde{X}}(-\log D)\oplus T_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-\log D))\to i_{*}T_{D}\to 0,

where
i\colon D\to Z
is the inclusion. Then
H^{1}(D;T_{D})=0
since
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
H^{2}(\widetilde{X};T_{\widetilde{X}}(-\log D))=0
by (i), and
H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{2};T_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-\log D))=0
by an easy direct argument. Thus
H^{2}(Z;T^{0}_{Z})=0
. By construction,
T^{1}_{Z}=\mathcal{O}_{D}
. Since
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
H^{1}(Z;T^{1}_{Z})=H^{1}(D;\mathcal{O}_{D})=0
. Thus
\mathbb{T}_{Z}^{2}=0
and
\mathbb{T}_{Z}^{1}\to H^{0}(Z;T^{1}_{Z})=H^{0}(Z;\mathcal{O}_{D})
is surjective. In particular, there is a deformation of
Z
over the disk whose image in
H^{0}(Z;T^{1}_{Z})
is an everywhere generating section. By
[
Fri83b
, Proposition 2.5]
,
Z
is smoothable. We omit the straightforward argument that the smoothings are as claimed.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

(i) The point in Wahl’s construction is that the deformation space of the rational singularity whose minimal resolution is a smooth rational curve of self-intersection
-4
has two different components, one corresponding to rational ruled surfaces of degree

in

and the other to the Veronese surface (the “non-Artin component”).

(ii) A slightly weaker version of Theorem
4.13
is already present in
[

, Theorem 3.1]
. Here, the result is stated under the somewhat loosely worded hypothesis that “the number of moduli of
\widetilde{X}
keeping the curve

is
-\chi(T_{\widetilde{X}})-3
,” i.e. it is exactly
3
conditions in moduli to keep the smooth rational curve

with self-intersection
-4
. For the blown up

, there are

moduli:

for the

(which will necessarily have nodal singular fibers near

and then two conditions because the point to blow up has to be the singular point for a nearby fiber.
Of course, a more convincing way to express the above condition about the number of moduli is to note that, since

, the map

(iii) Following the method of

]
, one can explicitly construct degenerations of an elliptic surface with

to the normal crossing surface

by degenerating the branch locus.

(iv) The degeneration of a

.

4.5.
Blown up Enriques surfaces

In this subsection, we assume that the minimal model
Y_{0}
of
\widetilde{Y}
is a (smooth) Enriques surface. By Theorem
2.12
(iii), there exists a morphism
\rho\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
, where
\rho
is the blowup of
Y_{0}
at a single point
p\in Y_{0}
, and the exceptional curve
C
on
\widetilde{Y}
satisfies:
C\cdot D=2
. In particular,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(C)
. If
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are the two connected components of
D
, then
C\cdot D_{1}=C\cdot D_{2}=1
. Thus, if
\overline{D}_{1}
and
\overline{D}_{2}
are images of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
in
Y_{0}
, then
\overline{D_{1}}
and
\overline{D_{2}}
are isomorphic to
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
respectively,
\overline{D}_{1}
and
\overline{D}_{2}
meet transversally at
1
point, and each
D_{i}
is either a smooth elliptic curve, an irreducible nodal curve, or a cycle of smooth rational curves. It follows that
\overline{D}_{1}
and
\overline{D}_{2}
are the reductions of multiple fibers in two different elliptic fibrations.

As in the
K3
case, we begin with the case where
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible, in fact smooth in this case:

Theorem 4.15
.

If
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are smooth, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
. Hence the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective. In particular, the two elliptic singularities on
Y
can be deformed independently.

Proof.

With
\rho\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}
and
\overline{D}_{i}
as above, let
\overline{D}=\overline{D}_{1}+\overline{D}_{2}
, where the
\overline{D}_{i}
are smooth elliptic curves meeting transversally. The proof of Theorem
4.5
shows that it suffices to prove that

H^{0}(Y_{0};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}\otimes K_{Y_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D}))=0,

where
K_{Y_{0}}
is a
2
-torsion line bundle of order
2
.

Let
\tau\colon Z\to Y_{0}
be the
K3
étale double cover of
Y_{0}
corresponding to
K_{Y_{0}}
and let
E_{i}
be the preimage of
\overline{D}_{i}
in
Z
. Thus each
E_{i}
is also a smooth elliptic curve and
E_{1}\cdot E_{2}=2
. Since
Z\to Y_{0}
is étale and
\tau^{*}K_{Y_{0}}=K_{Z}=\mathcal{O}_{Z}
, the pullback of
\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}\otimes K_{Y_{0}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}}(\overline{D})
is
\Omega^{1}_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Z}(E)
, where
E=E_{1}+E_{2}
. It will suffice to prove that
H^{0}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Z}(E))=0
.

By standard results, the linear system
E
defines a degree
2
morphism
\nu\colon Z\to Q
, where
Q
is a smooth quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and the
E_{i}=\nu^{-1}(\ell_{i})
, where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are the two rulings on
Q
. In other words,
\mathcal{O}_{Z}(E)=\nu^{*}\mathcal{O}_{Q}(1,1)
. An argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.5
shows that
\nu
is finite. The branch divisor
\Sigma
of the double cover
\nu
is a smooth
(4,4)
-divisor on
Q
, and thus
L=\mathcal{O}_{Q}(2,2)
. Applying Lemma
4.3
, it suffices to prove that

H^{0}(Q;\Omega^{1}_{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Q}(1,1))=H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{Q}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Q}(-1,-1))=0.

Since
Q\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
\Omega^{1}_{Q}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Q}(-2,0)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{Q}(0,-2)
and thus

\Omega^{1}_{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Q}(1,1)\cong\mathcal{O}_{Q}(-1,1)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{Q}(1,-1).

Hence
H^{0}(Q;\Omega^{1}_{Q}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Q}(1,1))=0
. By a Poincaré residue argument, since
\mathcal{O}_{Q}(-1,-1)|\Sigma
is a line bundle on
\Sigma
of negative degree,

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2};\Omega^{1}_{Q}(\log\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Q}(-1,-1))=0.

Putting this all together,
H^{0}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{Z}(E))=0
and thus
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D))=0
.
∎

Next we deal with the case where both
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
correspond to cusp singularities:

Theorem 4.16
.

If
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible nodal curves or cycles of smooth rational curves, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
. Hence the homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective and the two elliptic singularities on

can be deformed independently.

Proof.

Note that the divisor
\overline{D}
is a normal crossing divisor on
Y_{0}
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.15
, it suffices to prove that
H^{0}(Y_{0};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\log\overline{D})\otimes K_{Y_{0}})=0
, and then that
H^{0}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z}(\log E))=0
, where
E
is the inverse image of
\overline{D}
on the
K3
cover
Z
. More precisely, since

\tau_{*}\Omega^{1}_{Z}(\log E)=\tau_{*}\tau^{*}\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\log\overline{D})=\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\log\overline{D})\oplus(\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\log\overline{D})\otimes K_{Y_{0}}),

the group
H^{0}(Y_{0};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}(\log\overline{D})\otimes K_{Y_{0}})
is the space of anti-invariant sections of
H^{0}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z}(\log E))
. Since
H^{0}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z})=0
, it is enough to show that the images of the anti-invariant fundamental classes are linearly independent in
H^{1}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z})\subseteq H^{2}(Z;\mathbb{C})
. Here “anti-invariant fundamental class” means the classes of the form
\Gamma^{+}-\Gamma^{-}
, where
\Gamma
is a component of
E
and
\iota(\Gamma^{+})=\Gamma^{-}
, where
\iota\colon Z\to Z
is the involution corresponding to the double cover.
In particular, if
D_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\Gamma_{i}^{(1)}
and
D_{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\Gamma_{i}^{(2)}
, then the preimage in
Z
of each
\Gamma_{i}^{(j)}
is a union
\Gamma_{i}^{(j)}{}^{+}+\Gamma_{i}^{(j)}{}^{-}
of two disjoint smooth rational curves exchanged under
\iota
. Then a calculation shows that, assuming for simplicity that
n,m\geq 2
, the anti-invariant classes in the lattice spanned by the components of
E
are a lattice with basis
e_{0},\dots,e_{n},f_{0},\dots,f_{m}
and satisfying
e_{i}^{2}=f_{i}^{2}=-4
,
e_{i}\cdot e_{j}=0
for
i\neq j\pm 1\pmod{n}
, and
e_{i}\cdot e_{i+1}=e_{n}\cdot e_{0}=2
, and similarly for the
f_{i}
,
e_{0}\cdot f_{0}=2
, and
e_{i}\cdot f_{j}=0
if
(i,j)\neq(0,0)
. Thus, in the notation of Definition
4.1
, they span the lattice
\Lambda_{2}(n,m)(2)
. Hence, by Lemma
4.2
, the images of the anti-invariant fundamental classes are linearly independent in
H^{1}(Z;\Omega^{1}_{Z})
as claimed.

The second statement is then a consequence of Proposition
1.13
(ii).
∎

A similar but simpler argument, using Proposition
1.21
, shows the following:

Theorem 4.17
.

If
D_{1}
is a smooth elliptic curve and
D_{2}
is either an irreducible nodal curve or a cycle of smooth rational curves, then
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log(D_{1}+D_{2})))=0
. Hence there is a deformation of
Y
to an I-surface with two simple elliptic singularities. ∎

5.
The case
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
: the rational case

Throughout this section, we assume that
\kappa(\widetilde{Y})=-\infty
and
k=2
, or equivalently that
\widetilde{Y}
is a rational surface.

5.1.
The case
(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2)

We begin what is essentially a converse statement to Theorem
2.15
(i):

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
Y_{0}
be a rational surface with a negative definite anticanonical divisor
D_{2}
which is either smooth or nodal. Let
\Gamma\subseteq Y_{0}
be a reduced curve with
p_{a}(\Gamma)=2
such that
\Gamma\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
, with either a node or a cusp at
p_{0}\in\Gamma
, and which is either irreducible or a cycle of curves with all singularities nodal with the possible exception of
p_{0}
. Let
\widetilde{Y}
be the blow up of
Y_{0}
at
p_{0}
, let
D_{1}
be the proper transform of
\Gamma
, let the new exceptional curve be denoted by
C
, so that
D_{1}=\Gamma-2C
, and identify
D_{2}
with its preimage in
\widetilde{Y}
.

(i)

The divisor
L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}+D_{2}
is a nef and big divisor and satisfies
L^{2}=1
and
L\cdot D_{i}=0
.

(ii)

If
L
has positive intersection with every exceptional curve which is not a component of
D_{1}
or
D_{2}
, then
m_{2}=-D_{2}^{2}=2
if
M_{2}=L+D_{1}
is not nef, and
m_{2}=1
if
M_{2}
is nef.

(iii)

If
L
has positive intersection with every exceptional curve which is not a component of
D_{1}
or
D_{2}
, then the contraction of
\widetilde{Y}
at
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
is an I-surface with
\omega_{Y}
ample.

(iv)

Conversely, if
\widetilde{Y}
is the minimal resolution of an I-surface
Y
with two simple elliptic or cusp singularities, one of which has multiplicity
2
, and no other singularities, then
\widetilde{Y}
arises as above.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is irreducible, then
\Gamma^{2}=\Gamma^{2}+\Gamma\cdot K_{Y_{0}}=2
, so that
\Gamma^{2}=2
and
D_{1}^{2}=-2
. A similar argument shows that, if
\Gamma
is not irreducible but
p_{0}
lies on just one component, then the component of
\Gamma
containing
p_{0}
has arithmetic genus one and hence self-intersection
0
, and all other components are smooth rational with self-intersection
-2
. In this case, the unique component of
D_{1}
meeting
C
has self-intersection
-4
and the remaining components have self-intersection
-2
. Similarly, if
p_{0}
lies on two different components, then each of them is smooth rational and their proper transforms on
\widetilde{Y}
have self-intersection
-3
. In particular,
D_{1}^{2}=-2
in all cases and
D_{1}
is a negative definite curve corresponding to a simple elliptic or cusp singularity of multiplicity
2
.

Since
K_{Y_{0}}=-D_{2}
,
L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}+D_{2}=C+D_{1}
. Thus
L^{2}=1
,
L|D_{i}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D_{i}}
and
L\cdot C=1
. Thus
L
is nef and big. If
L
has positive intersection with every exceptional curve, the proof of Theorem
2.15
shows that
m_{2}=2
, if
M_{2}
is not nef, and
m_{2}=1
if
M_{2}
is nef. This proves (i) and (ii). As for (iii), since
L=\pi^{*}\omega_{Y}
, it follows that
\omega_{Y}
is ample,
\omega_{Y}^{2}=1
, and
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Y})=3
by Lemma
2.3
. Thus
Y
is an I-surface. Finally, (iv) is a consequence of Theorem
2.15
(i).
∎

For the rest of this subsection, we assume that
\widetilde{Y}
is a rational surface and that
(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2)
. By Proposition
5.1
, there exists a blowdown of
\widetilde{Y}
to a rational surface with an effective anticanonical divisor of square
-2
. The following is an explicit example:

Example 5.2
.

Let
Q
be a plane quartic in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
meeting a smooth cubic
\overline{D}_{2}
in
11
points, one of them double, let
Y_{0}
be the blow up
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at the
11
points, so the proper transform
\Gamma
of
Q
is a smooth curve of genus
2
. The proper transform
D_{2}
of
\overline{D}_{2}
then satisfies
D_{2}^{2}=-2
. Further degenerate
Q
so that it has a second double point not lying on
\overline{D}_{2}
. Then let
\widetilde{Y}
be the blow up of
Y_{0}
at the second double point, let
D_{1}
be the proper transform of
\Gamma
and let the new exceptional curve be
C
, so that
D_{1}=\Gamma-2C
. Then
\Gamma^{2}=2
,
D_{1}^{2}=\Gamma^{2}-4=-2
, and
C\cdot D_{1}=2
. Note the symmetry between the curves
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
in this example: let
\ell
be the line in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
joining the two double points of
Q
. The proper transform of
\ell
on
\widetilde{Y}
is an exceptional curve meeting
D_{1}
in two points and disjoint from
D_{1}
, and so can also be blown down to get the symmetric picture reversing the roles of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
.

There is an alternate description of the surface
\widetilde{Y}
in case
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible:

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{Y}
is the minimal resolution of an I-surface containing two irreducible divisors
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
with
D_{i}^{2}=-2
. Then there exists an exceptional curve
C_{1}
on
\widetilde{Y}
such that
C_{1}\cdot D_{1}=2
. If
\rho_{1}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}^{(1)}
is the contraction of
C_{1}
, then there exists a degree two morphism
\nu\colon Y_{0}^{(1)}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}
branched along a smooth divisor in
|2\sigma_{0}+6f|
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the negative section, such that
\rho_{1}(D_{1})=\overline{D}_{1}=\nu^{*}(\sigma_{0}+f)
and
D_{2}=\nu^{*}\sigma_{0}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.15
, without assuming that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible, there exist exceptional curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
on
\widetilde{Y}
with
C_{1}\cdot D_{1}=2
,
C_{1}\cdot D_{2}=0
,
C_{2}\cdot D_{1}=0
,
C_{2}\cdot D_{2}=2
, and

K_{\widetilde{Y}}=C_{1}-D_{2}=C_{2}-D_{1}.

Thus
C_{1}\cdot C_{2}=1
because

-1=K_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot C_{1}=(C_{2}-D_{1})\cdot C_{1}=(C_{1}\cdot C_{2})-2.

Moreover,
L\cdot C_{i}=1
and

L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}+D_{2}=C_{1}+D_{1}=C_{2}+D_{2}.

Let
\rho_{1}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}^{(1)}
be the contraction of
C_{1}
. Then
\overline{D}_{1}=\rho_{1}(D_{1})
is a nef divisor with
(\overline{D}_{1})^{2}=2
,
p_{a}(\overline{D}_{1})=2
, and
K_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}=\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(-D_{2})
. By symmetry, we can make the same construction by taking the contraction
\rho_{2}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y_{0}^{(2)}
of
C_{2}
, with
\overline{D}_{2}=\rho_{2}(D_{2})
. The divisor
\overline{D}_{i}
is nodal
\iff

D_{i}
meets
C_{i}
in two distinct points.

The effective divisor
\overline{D}_{1}
is a nef divisor with positive self-intersection disjoint from
D_{2}
. It follows from standard results (e.g.
[
Fri15
, Theorem 4.12]
) that the linear system
|\overline{D}_{1}|
has no fixed components or base locus and defines a degree
2
morphism
\varphi\colon Y_{0}^{(1)}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
which contracts
D_{2}
. Note that
\overline{D}_{1}
is induced by the divisor
D_{1}+2C_{1}
on
\widetilde{Y}
, i.e.

\rho_{1}^{*}\overline{D}_{1}=D_{1}+2C_{1}=L+C_{1}.

Claim 5.4
.

The only irreducible curves that
\varphi
contracts to a point are components of
D_{2}
or components of
\overline{D}_{1}
.

Proof.

If
A
is an irreducible curve on
Y_{0}^{(1)}
, not a component of
\overline{D}_{1}
, such that
\overline{D}_{1}\cdot A=0
, and
A^{\prime}
is its proper transform on
\widetilde{Y}
, then
A^{\prime}\cdot D_{1}=A^{\prime}\cdot C_{1}=0
. Hence
L\cdot A^{\prime}=0
, so
A^{\prime}
is a component of
D_{2}
.
∎

Returning to the proof of Proposition
5.3
, suppose that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible. Let
p\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
be the image
\varphi(D_{2})
and let
\mathbb{F}_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
be the blowup of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at
p
. Then we claim that
\varphi\colon Y_{0}^{(1)}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
extends to a finite degree two morphism
\nu\colon Y_{0}^{(1)}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}
branched along a smooth divisor in
|2\sigma_{0}+6f|
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the negative section.
To see this, note that

\rho_{1}^{*}(2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2})=2L+2C_{1}-D_{2}=(C_{2}+D_{2})+L+2C_{1}-D_{2}=L+2C_{1}+C_{2}.

Thus the linear system
|2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}|
corresponds to the linear system
|L+2C_{1}+C_{2}|
on
\widetilde{Y}
. In any case,
L+2C_{1}+C_{2}
is nef and big. In fact, we have:

\displaystyle(L+2C_{1}+C_{2})\cdot C_{1}=0;

\displaystyle\qquad(L+2C_{1}+C_{2})\cdot C_{2}=1;

\displaystyle(L+2C_{1}+C_{2})\cdot D_{1}=4;

\displaystyle\qquad(L+2C_{1}+C_{2})\cdot D_{2}=2;

\displaystyle(L+2C_{1}+C_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=6.

In particular, if
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible, then

C_{1}
is the only irreducible curve on
\widetilde{Y}
which has intersection number
0
with
L+2C_{1}+C_{2}
. Since
2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}=2\overline{D}_{1}+K_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}
and
2\overline{D}_{1}
is nef and big, Kodaira-Mumford vanishing implies that

H^{1}(Y_{0}^{(1)};\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}))=0
.
Riemann-Roch then implies that
\dim|2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}|=4
. Hence, if
p=\varphi(D_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
is the image of
D_{2}
, the natural inclusion
|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(2)-p|\subseteq|2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}|
is an equality.
Then
|2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}|
is a base point free linear system and the induced morphism
\nu\colon Y_{0}^{(1)}\to\mathbb{P}^{4}
has degree
2
onto its image in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, which is
\mathbb{F}_{1}
. By construction, the image of
D_{2}
is the negative section
\sigma_{0}
and hence
D_{2}=\nu^{*}\sigma_{0}
. Moreover,
K_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}=\mathcal{O}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(-D_{2})
, so that, if
\Sigma
is the branch locus, then

-\sigma_{0}=K_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\Sigma=-2\sigma_{0}-3f+\frac{1}{2}\Sigma.

Thus
\Sigma\in|2\sigma_{0}+6f|
. By construction,
2\overline{D}_{1}-D_{2}=\nu^{*}(\sigma_{0}+2f)
and thus
\overline{D}_{1}=\nu^{*}(\sigma_{0}+f)
. This completes the proof of Proposition
5.3
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Suppose that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are irreducible. Then

H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D_{1}-D_{2}))=0.

Hence the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective.

Proof.

By Serre duality, we need to show that

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D_{1}+D_{2}))=0.

By the above,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{Y}}(D_{1}+D_{2})=L=C_{1}+D_{1}
. By the usual Hartogs argument,

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(C_{1}+D_{1}))\subseteq H^{0}(Y_{0}^{(1)};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(\overline{D}_{1})).

Proposition
5.3
gives a finite degree two morphism
\nu\colon Y_{0}^{(1)}\to\mathbb{F}_{1}
, with
\overline{D}_{1}=\nu^{*}(\sigma_{0}+f)
. Thus, by Lemma
4.3
, there is an exact sequence

0\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+f)\to\nu_{*}\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(\overline{D}_{1})\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\log\Sigma)(\sigma_{0}+f)\otimes\lambda^{-1}\to 0,

with
\lambda=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+3f)
. So we must show that

H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+f))=H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\log\Sigma)(-2f))=0.

The second vanishing follows from the inclusion

H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\log\Sigma)(-2f))\subseteq H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\log\Sigma)),

the vanishing of
H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}})=0
, and the Poincaré residue sequence. As for the first group, the relative cotangent sequence for the morphism
g\colon\mathbb{F}_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is

0\to g^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}/\mathbb{P}^{1}}\to 0.

Then
g^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(-2f)
and
\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}/\mathbb{P}^{1}}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(-2\sigma_{0}-f)
. Thus

H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};g^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+f))=H^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{1};\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}/\mathbb{P}^{1}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}(\sigma_{0}+f))=0.

So finally
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-D_{1}-D_{2}))=0
as claimed.
∎

For the case of at least one cusp singularity, we have the following, with a mild general position hypothesis:

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose that either
\overline{D}_{1}
or
\overline{D}_{2}
is nodal. Then

H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log(D_{1}+D_{2})))=0.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case where
\overline{D}_{1}
is nodal. In this case,

\displaystyle H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log(D_{1}+D_{2}))\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}})

\displaystyle\subseteq H^{0}(Y_{0}^{(1)};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(\log(\overline{D}_{1}+D_{2}))\otimes K_{Y_{0}^{(1)}})

\displaystyle\subseteq H^{0}(Y_{0}^{(1)};\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}^{(1)}}(\log(\overline{D}_{1}+D_{2}))).

Then this last group is
0
by the Poincaré residue sequence and the fact that, by Lemma
4.2
, the components of
\overline{D}_{1}
and
D_{2}
are linearly independent in cohomology.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Suppose that either
\overline{D}_{1}
or
\overline{D}_{2}
is nodal.

(i)

If both singularities are cusp singularities, then the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective.

(ii)

If one singularity is a cusp singularity and the other is simple elliptic, then there is a deformation of
Y
to an I-surface with two simple elliptic singularities of multiplicity
2
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Proposition
1.13
(ii) and the second statement from Proposition

.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

Smoothing one of the elliptic singularities leads to an I-surface whose minimal resolution is a blown up
K3
surface. In case one of the singularities is a cusp singularity, an exotic deformation to an elliptic singularity of multiplicity one leads to the case
(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,1)
, where the minimal resolution is necessarily rational. If both singularities are cusp singularities, taking exotic deformations of both elliptic singularities of multiplicity one leads to the case
(m_{1},m_{2})=(1,1)
.
A priori
, the minimal resolution could be a blown up Enriques surface or a rational surface. However, we shall see below that minimal resolution must be rational.

5.2.
The case
(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,1)

As in §
5.1
, we begin with an explicit example:

Example 5.9
.

Let
X
be the blowup of
9
points on a smooth plane cubic and let
F
be the proper transform of the cubic. We assume that the points are chosen so that the normal bundle
N_{F/X}
is a nontrivial
2
-torsion point on
F
, but are general otherwise. Then it is a standard result that
|F|
is just one point, i.e.
h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(F))=1
, but
|2F|
is a pencil and defines an elliptic surface
X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
where
F
is the unique multiple fiber, of multiplicity
2
. If
E
is an exceptional curve on
X
, then
E\cdot 1=1
, hence
E
is a bisection of the fibration. Here
K_{X}=\mathcal{O}_{X}(-F)
.

Consider the linear system
|E+F|
. Note that
E\cdot(E+F)=0
and
F\cdot(E+F)=1
. Hence
(E+F)^{2}=1
, so that
E+F
is nef and big, and
(E+F)\cdot K_{X}=-1
. By Riemann-Roch,

\chi(\mathcal{O}_{X}(E+F))=h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(E+F))=\frac{1}{2}(1+1)+1=2.

Thus
|E+F|
is a pencil. Since
\mathcal{O}_{F}(F)
is a nontrivial line bundle of degree
0
on
F
,
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{F}(F))=0
and then
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(F))=0
. It follows that the natural map
H^{0}(X;\mathcal{O}_{X}(E+F))\to H^{0}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})
is surjective, as is the map
H^{0}(X;\mathcal{O}_{X}(E+F))\to H^{0}(F;\mathcal{O}_{F}(q))
, where
q
is the point of
F
such that
\mathcal{O}_{F}(E+F)=\mathcal{O}_{F}(q)
. In particular
|E+F|
has a simple base point at
q
, so the general element
\Gamma
is a smooth bisection of the elliptic fibration. From

0=2p_{a}(\Gamma)-2=\Gamma^{2}+K_{X}\cdot\Gamma,

\Gamma
is a smooth elliptic curve. Now let
\widetilde{Y}
be the blow up of the two points of intersection of
\Gamma
with a general fiber
G
of the elliptic fibration defined by
|2F|
and let
C_{1},C_{2}
be the new exceptional curves. Identifying a divisor on
X
with its total transform on
\widetilde{Y}
, let
D_{1}
be the proper transform of
G
, so that
D_{1}=G-C_{1}-C_{2}
, and let
D_{2}
be the proper transform of
\Gamma
, so that
D_{2}=E+F-C_{1}-C_{2}
. Thus
D_{1}^{2}=-2
,
D_{2}^{2}=-1
and
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
. We have

\displaystyle M_{2}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}

\displaystyle=-F+C_{1}+C_{2}+G-C_{1}-C_{2}=F;

\displaystyle M_{1}=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{2}

\displaystyle=-F+C_{1}+C_{2}+E+F-C_{1}-C_{2}=E;

\displaystyle L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}+D_{2}

\displaystyle=-F+C_{1}+C_{2}+f-C_{1}-C_{2}+E+F-C_{1}-C_{2}=E+f-C_{1}-C_{2}.

Thus
K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}
is nef and
(K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1})^{2}=0
,
K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{2}
is the exceptional curve
E
with
E\cdot D_{1}=E\cdot f=2
and
E\cdot D_{2}=0
, and
L=K_{\widetilde{Y}}+D_{1}+D_{2}
satisfies

L^{2}=(E+f)^{2}-2=-1+4-2=1.

Furthermore
L=E+D_{1}=F+D_{2}
and
L\cdot E=1
,
L\cdot F=1
. Thus
L
is nef and big,
L\cdot D_{1}=(E+D_{1})\cdot D_{1}=0
,
L\cdot D_{2}=(F+D_{1})\cdot D_{1}=0
, and
L\cdot A>0
for all other curves
A
as long as all fibers of
X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
are irreducible.

If one of the blowup points were on
E
, then the proper transform
E^{\prime}
of
E
on
\widetilde{Y}
would be a curve such that
L\cdot E^{\prime}=0
. By our assumptions, this does not happen, so that we can identify
E
with its image in
\widetilde{Y}
. Then blowing down
E
produces a new rational surface
X^{\prime}
with
K_{X^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}_{X^{\prime}}(-D_{2})
. In particular, this gives an explicit realization of
\widetilde{Y}
corresponding to the two possibilities in Theorem
2.15
.

Next we show that all possibilities essentially arise as in Example
5.9
:

Proposition 5.10
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{Y}
is the minimal resolution of an I-surface with two elliptic singular points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
of multiplicities
m_{1}=2
,
m_{2}=1
. Then there exists a rational elliptic surface
X
with a multiple fiber
F

is a smooth point of
\Gamma
and hence is not equal to

.

(ii) Arguing as in (i), if

is a fiber component of
\mu_{2}
,

. In particular,

meets every fiber component. But

can have at most one component and the remaining fibers can have at most two. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

To complete the proof of Proposition

, the surface
\widetilde{Y}
is a blowup of the elliptic surface

at the singular point

of

,

is the proper transform of an irreducible fiber containing

as required.
∎

Proposition 5.19
.

In the notation of Proposition
5.17
, suppose that

and

are irreducible and that all fibers of

are nodal. Then

Hence the map

is surjective.

Proof.

In the notation of Proposition
5.17
,

, where

is the exceptional divisor of the blowup

at the point

, and

Then

is Serre dual to

H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}\otimes L)=H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(3F+E-2C))=H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(f+F+E-2C)).

A local calculation shows that
\rho_{2}{}_{*}\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(-2C_{2})\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{p}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{Y_{0}}

By Theorem
3.1
, there is an exact sequence

where

is a reduced

since

Since

As for the remaining term, we have

As in the proof of Proposition
5.11

of

6.
Geometry of certain elliptic ruled surfaces

It is well-known that an algebraic elliptic surface
X
i.e. a relatively minimal fibration over a base curve whose general fiber is an elliptic curve, is also an elliptic ruled surface if and only if the base curve is
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, all fibers have smooth reduction, and
X
is either a product surface
E\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
or the log transform of
E\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
at two fibers with the same multiplicities
m
, or the log transform of
E\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
at three multiple fibers, all of multiplicity
2
(cf.
[
FM94
, I.3.23 and I.6.12]
). We describe these surfaces and their geometry in some detail.

6.1.
The case of two multiple fibers

Let
E
be an elliptic curve and
e\in E
a point of order
2
. Define
X_{0}=(E\times\mathbb{P}^{1})/\iota
, where
\iota(x,t)=(x+e,-t)
. Thus there is an induced map
\psi\colon X_{0}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}/\{t\mapsto-t\}
. The general fiber of
\psi
is
E
, but over
0
and
\infty
there are two multiple fibers
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
isomorphic to
E/(e)=B
. There is also the map
\rho\colon X_{0}\to E/(e)=B
. Since
E\to B
is étale,
\rho
is a fiber bundle with all fibers isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Note in fact that there is a
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
-action on
E\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
generated by
\iota_{1}
and
\iota_{2}
where
\iota_{1}(x,t)=(x+e,t)
and
(\iota_{2}(x,t)=(x,-t)
; thus
\iota=\iota_{1}\circ\iota_{2}
. Hence
X_{0}
is also a double cover of
B\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
branched along two fibers
(B\times\{0\})+(B\times\{\infty\})=\pi_{2}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(2)
, but with the square root of this divisor of the form
\pi_{1}^{*}\eta\otimes\pi_{2}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(1)
for a nontrivial
2
-torsion line bundle on
B
. The map
X_{0}\to B\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
is given by the maps
(\rho,\psi)
. Of course, a similar construction works if
e
is a point of order
m\geq 1
and we consider the
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-action on
E\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
generated by
(x,t)\mapsto(x+e,\zeta t)
, where
\zeta
is a primitive
m^{\text{\rm{th}}}
root of unity.

As a ruled surface,
X_{0}
has the two disjoint sections
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
. The normal bundle of
\sigma_{i}
in
X_{0}
is
\eta
, where
\eta
is a
2
-torsion line bundle, since
\sigma_{i}
is also a multiple fiber of multiplicity
2
. Thus
X_{0}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{B}\oplus\eta)
, where the two sections of
X_{0}
correspond to the sections of
\mathcal{O}_{B}\oplus\eta
and
(\mathcal{O}_{B}\oplus\eta)\otimes\eta=\eta\oplus\mathcal{O}_{B}
. (From this point of view, it is slightly more complicated to show that
|2\sigma_{i}|
is a pencil.)

6.2.
The case of three multiple fibers

Let
B
be an elliptic curve, fix a point
p_{0}\in B
, and let
W
be the unique nonsplit extension of
\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})
by
\mathcal{O}_{B}
:

0\to\mathcal{O}_{B}\to W\to\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})\to 0.

Let
\rho\colon X_{1}=\mathbb{P}(W)\to B
be the corresponding ruled surface over
B
. The section of
W
defines a section
\sigma_{p_{0}}
with
\rho_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(\sigma_{p_{0}})=W
. Applying
\rho_{*}
to the exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}\to\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(\sigma_{p_{0}})\to\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{p_{0}}}(\sigma_{p_{0}})\to 0,

and identifying
\sigma_{p_{0}}
with
B
, there is an exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{B}\to W\to\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{p_{0}}}(\sigma_{p_{0}})\to 0.

Hence
\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{p_{0}}}(\sigma_{p_{0}})\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})
. In particular
\sigma_{p_{0}}^{2}=1
. Using the shorthand notation
p\cdot f
for the divisor
\rho^{*}p
on
X_{1}
, it follows that

K_{X_{1}}=-2\sigma_{p_{0}}+p_{0}\cdot f,

as one checks by adjunction.

Lemma 6.1
.

(i)
If
\sigma
is a section of
X_{1}
with
\sigma^{2}=1
, then
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{p_{0}}
.

(ii)
For every
p\in B
, there are exactly
4
sections
\sigma
with
\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(\sigma)|\sigma\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}(p)
via the natural isomorphism
\sigma\cong B
. We will denote any such section by
\sigma_{p}
.

Proof.

This is an easy consequence of the description of sections of
X_{1}
. For (ii), compare also Lemma
6.4
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

With notation as above,

(i)

The divisor
\Gamma_{q}=2\sigma_{p_{0}}-q\cdot f
is effective
\iff

q
is a
2
-torsion point on
B
for the group law with origin
p_{0}
.

(ii)

With
q
as in
(i)
,
2\Gamma_{q}
is linearly equivalent to
-2K_{X_{1}}
.

(iii)

With
q
as in
(i)
,
|2\Gamma_{q}|
is a pencil on
X_{1}
defining a morphism
\psi\colon X_{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The general fiber is a smooth elliptic curve
\phi
, linearly equivalent to
-2K_{X_{1}}
and every fiber which is not smooth is one of the three multiple fibers
2\Gamma_{q_{i}}
where the
q_{i}
are the
2
-torsion points on
B
.

(iv)

With
q
as in
(i)
, the normal bundle
N_{\Gamma_{q}/X_{1}}
is a nontrivial
2
-torsion line bundle on
\Gamma_{q}
.

Proof.

(i) First note that

H^{0}(X_{1};\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(\Gamma_{q}))=H^{0}(B;\rho_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(2\sigma_{p_{0}}-q\cdot f))=H^{0}(B;\rho_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(2\sigma_{p_{0}})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(-q)).

Moreover,
\rho_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(2\sigma_{p_{0}})\cong\operatorname{Sym}^{2}W
. Also,
W\otimes W=\det W\oplus\operatorname{Sym}^{2}W
. Since
W
has rank
2
,

W\cong W^{\vee}\otimes\det W
, and hence

W\otimes W\cong W\otimes W^{\vee}\otimes\det W\cong\mathit{Hom}(W,W)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(p).

By a well-known argument,
\mathit{Hom}(W,W)\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}\oplus\eta_{1}\oplus\eta_{2}\oplus\eta_{3}
,
where the
\eta_{i}
are the three nontrivial
2
-torsion line bundles on
B
. Thus

W\otimes W\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})\oplus(\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})\otimes\eta_{1})\oplus(\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})\otimes\eta_{2})\oplus(\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0})\otimes\eta_{3}).

It follows that

\operatorname{Sym}^{2}W\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(-q)\cong(\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0}-q)\otimes\eta_{1})\oplus(\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0}-q)\otimes\eta_{2})\oplus(\mathcal{O}_{B}(p_{0}-q)\otimes\eta_{3}).

Thus
H^{0}(X_{1};\mathcal{O}_{X_{1}}(\Gamma_{q}))\neq 0

\iff

\mathcal{O}_{B}(q-p_{0})\cong\eta_{i}
for some
i

\iff

q-p_{0}
is a
2
-torsion point in the corresponding group law on
B
.

(ii) If
q
is a
2
-torsion point,
2q=2p_{0}
, and thus
2\Gamma_{q}=4\sigma_{p_{0}}-2q\cdot f=4\sigma_{p_{0}}-2p_{0}\cdot f=-2K_{X_{1}}
.

(iii) The proof that
|2\Gamma_{q}|
is a pencil on
X_{1}
is deferred until the next subsection. Since
(\Gamma_{q})^{2}=0
and
|2\Gamma_{q}|
contains three disjoint effective divisors,
|2\Gamma_{q}|
has no base locus or fixed curves and so defines a morphism
\psi
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. If
\phi
is a general element, its genus
g(\phi)=1
since
\phi^{2}+\phi\cdot K_{X_{1}}=0
. The other statements follow easily from (ii).

(iv) This is clear since
\Gamma_{q}
is the reduction of a multiple fiber with multiplicity
2
.
∎

In terms of numerical equivalence
\equiv
,
\operatorname{Num}X_{1}=\mathbb{Z}\cdot\sigma_{p_{0}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\cdot f
. Thus
\sigma_{p}\equiv\sigma_{p_{0}}
for all
p\in B
and
\Gamma_{q}\equiv\Gamma_{q^{\prime}}
for all
q,q^{\prime}\in B
. Also,
\sigma_{p}^{2}=\sigma_{p}\cdot\sigma_{p^{\prime}}=1
for all
p,p^{\prime}\in B
,
\Gamma_{q}^{2}=\Gamma_{q}\cdot\Gamma_{q^{\prime}}=0
for all points
q,q^{\prime}
on
B
and
\Gamma_{q}\cdot\sigma_{p}=1
. If
\phi
is the fiber, then
\phi\cdot\sigma_{p}=2(\Gamma_{q}\cdot\sigma_{p})=2
and hence the
\sigma_{p}
are bisections of the fibration
\psi
.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
X=\mathbb{P}(V)
is a geometrically ruled surface over an elliptic curve
B
and there exist two irreducible curves
\sigma
,
\Gamma
on
X
with
\sigma^{2}=1
,
\Gamma^{2}=0
and
\Gamma
is not a fiber of the ruling, then
X\cong X_{1}=\mathbb{P}(W)
as defined at the beginning of §
6.2
. In this case,
\sigma=\sigma_{p}
for some
p\in B
and
\Gamma\equiv c\Gamma_{q}
for some positive integer
c
and some point
q\in B
.

Proof.

First, the intersection form on
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is odd. By the classification of rank
2
bundles over an elliptic curve, there are two possibilities: Either
V
is either unstable of odd degree, hence after twisting by a line bundle is of the form
\mathcal{O}_{B}\oplus\lambda
, where
\deg\lambda=e
is odd and positive, or
V\cong W
. In the first case, by a standard calculation
[
Har77
, V 2.20]
, every irreducible curve of nonnegative self-intersection which is not the class of a fiber has self-intersection
\geq e\geq 1
, so this case does not arise. Thus, after a twist,
V\cong W
, so
X\cong X_{1}
and has invariant
-1
.
[
Har77
, V 2.21]
, if
C
is the class of an irreducible curve, then either
C\equiv\sigma_{p_{0}}+bf
and
C^{2}=2b+1
, or
C\equiv a\sigma_{p_{0}}+bf
with
a>1
and
b\geq-\frac{1}{2}a
. In this case,
C^{2}=a^{2}+2ab=a(a+2b)\geq 0
, with equality holding
\iff

b=-\frac{1}{2}a
. Thus, with the notation of the statement of the lemma,
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{p_{0}}
and hence
\sigma=\sigma_{p}
for some
p\in B
. Moreover,
\Gamma\equiv a(\sigma_{p_{0}}-\frac{1}{2}f)
. Since
\Gamma
is an integral class,
a
is even and
\Gamma\equiv\frac{a}{2}(2\sigma_{p_{0}}-f)\equiv c\Gamma_{q}
for a positive integer
c
. Thus
\Gamma\equiv c\Gamma_{q}
.
∎

6.3.
X_{1}
as a symmetric product

Fixing the point
p_{0}
on
B
defines a group law. The addition map
B\times B\to B
then defines a morphism
\rho\colon\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B\to B
. The fiber
f_{x}=\rho^{-1}(x)
is the set of all unordered pairs
(e_{1},e_{2})
with
e_{1}+e_{2}\equiv x+p_{0}
, and hence is
\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Note that
\nu\colon B\times B\to\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B
is a double cover of
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B
branched along the diagonal
\Delta

\Delta
with its image
\overline{\Delta}=\nu(\Delta)
in
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B
.

For each
a\in B
, we have the image
\sigma^{(a)}
of
\{a\}\times B
or equivalently of
B\times\{a\}
. Then
\sigma^{(a)}\cap f_{x}=\{a,b\}
, where

\sigma^{(a)}
is a section of
\rho
. For

\sigma^{(a)}\cap\sigma^{(b)}=\{a,b\}
, and the intersection is clearly transverse since
\{a\}\times B
and

\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B}(\sigma^{(b)})|\sigma^{(a)}\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}(a+b)

\sigma^{(a)}\cdot\sigma^{(b)}=1
for all

and hence for all

\nu^{*}(\sigma^{(a)})=(\{a\}\times B)+(B\times\{a\})

\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B}(\sigma^{(a)})|\sigma^{(a)}\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}(2a)
. Note that, for every point

of
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B
of the form
\{a,b\}
with

, there are two different sections
\sigma^{(a)}
,
\sigma^{(b)}
passing through

By construction, the image

is the branch locus of
\nu

where

\nu(\Delta+(0,q))=\nu(\Delta+(q,0))

\nu^{-1}(\nu(\Delta+(0,q)))

and

and

to its image
\Gamma_{q}\cong B/(q)
. Clearly
(\nu^{*}\Gamma_{q})^{2}=0
, hence
\Gamma_{q}^{2}=0
. Also,

\nu^{*}\Gamma_{q}\cap\nu^{*}\sigma^{(a)}=\{(a,a+q),(a+q,a)\},

with the intersections transverse. Thus
\Gamma_{q}\cdot\sigma^{(a)}=1
. Thus, by Lemma
6.3
, we have:

Lemma 6.4
.

As a geometrically ruled surface,
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}B\cong X_{1}

\sigma^{(a)}

\sigma_{p}

7.4.
Applications to deformations

The goal now is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
\widetilde{Y}
be the minimal resolution of an I-surface with
k=3
.

(i)

H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0
.

(ii)

The image of
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}
in

has codimension
2

(iii)

For all

,
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D_{i}^{\prime})(-D_{i}))=0
, and hence there is a deformation of
Y
which smooths
p_{i}
and is equisingular at the remaining points

.

(iv)

If
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(2,1,1)
, then smoothing
Y
at the point of multiplicity
2
yields an I-surface whose minimal resolution is a blown up Enriques surface with two simple elliptic singularities of multiplicity one and smoothing
Y
at a point of multiplicity
1
yields an I-surface whose minimal resolution is a a rational surface with one simple elliptic singularity of multiplicity
2
and another of multiplicity
1
.

(v)

If
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(1,1,1)
, then smoothing
Y
at a point
p_{i}
such that
\pi^{-1}(p_{i})=\sigma
is a section yields an I-surface whose minimal resolution is a rational surface with two simple elliptic singularities of multiplicity one and smoothing
Y
at a point
p_{i}
such that
\pi^{-1}(p_{i})=\Gamma
is a bisection yields an I-surface whose minimal resolution is a blown up Enriques surface with two simple elliptic singularities of multiplicity one.

Proof.

Since the cases
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(2,1,1)
and
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(1,1,1)
are formally identical as far as (i)—(iii) are concerned, we shall just write down the details for the case
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(1,1,1)
. In this case
\widetilde{Y}
is the blowup of a surface
X_{1}=\mathbb{P}(W)
where the
D_{i}
are the proper transforms of
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
,
\sigma_{3}
respectively, where the
\Gamma_{i}
are bisections and
\sigma_{3}
is a section. We turn now to the proofs of the various statements.

(i): By Serre duality,
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))
is Serre dual to
H^{0}(\widetilde{Y};\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{Y}}(\log D)\otimes K_{\widetilde{Y}})
. By the usual Hartogs argument, it is enough to prove that

H^{0}(X_{1};\Omega^{1}_{X_{1}}(\log(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\sigma_{3}))\otimes K_{X_{1}})=0.

Applying Serre duality again, we have to show that

H^{2}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}}(-\log(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\sigma_{3})))=0.

Using the normal bundle sequence, we get an exact sequence

0\to T_{X_{1}}(-\log(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\sigma_{3}))\to T_{X_{1}}\to N_{\Gamma_{1}/X_{1}}\oplus N_{\Gamma_{2}/X_{1}}\oplus N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}}\to 0.

Since

is a nontrivial
2
-torsion line bundle on
\Gamma_{i}
and
N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}}
has degree
1
,

H^{1}(\Gamma_{i};N_{\Gamma_{i}/X_{1}})=H^{1}(\sigma_{3};N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}})=0.

Moreover,
H^{2}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})=0
since
H^{2}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})
is a birational invariant (or directly). Thus

H^{2}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}}(-\log(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\sigma_{3})))=H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0.

(ii): Using the fact that
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{Y}=0
, which holds generally for I-surfaces, it suffices to prove that
H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})
has dimension
2
. By the proof of
Lemma
1.11
since
H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})=0
,
H^{2}(Y;T^{0}_{Y})\cong\operatorname{Coker}\{H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})\}
. Using the commutative diagram of Proposition
1.13
and the fact that

H^{0}(D;N_{D/Y})=H^{2}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))=0,

there is a commutative diagram with exact rows

\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))@>{}>{}>H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>H^{1}(D;N_{D/\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>0\\
@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V@V{}V{=}V\\
0@>{}>{}>H^{1}(D;T_{D})@>{}>{}>H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>H^{1}(D;N_{D/\widetilde{Y}})@>{}>{}>0.\end{CD}

It follows that

\operatorname{Coker}\{H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})\to H^{0}(Y;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widetilde{Y}}\}\cong\operatorname{Coker}\{H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D;T_{D})\}.

In our case,

(iii): By Proposition
1.19
and (i), it suffices to prove that the map
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D_{i};T_{D_{i}})
is surjective.

First consider the deformation theory of
X_{1}
. There is the usual exact sequence

0\to T_{X_{1}/B}\to T_{X_{1}}\to\rho^{*}T_{B}\to 0.

Then
R^{1}\rho_{*}T_{X_{1}/B}=R^{1}\rho_{*}T_{X_{1}}=0
and there is an exact sequence

0\to\operatorname{ad}W\to R^{0}\rho_{*}T_{X_{1}}\to T_{B}\to 0.

The simple bundle
W
satisfies
H^{0}(B;\operatorname{ad}W)=H^{1}(B;\operatorname{ad}W)=0
. Thus

H^{1}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})\cong H^{1}(B;R^{0}\rho_{*}T_{X_{1}})\cong H^{1}(B;T_{B}).

This says that
\mathbf{Def}_{X_{1}}\to\mathbf{Def}_{B}
is smooth, in fact it is an isomorphism on the analytic germs representing the functors.

Given an element
\theta\in H^{1}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})\cong H^{1}(B;T_{B})
, let
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
be the corresponding morphism over
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]
. Because
H^{1}(\Gamma_{i};N_{\Gamma_{i}/X_{1}})=H^{1}(\sigma_{3};N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}})=0
, the curves
\Gamma_{i}
and
\sigma_{3}
extend to Cartier divisors
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{3}
on
\mathcal{X}
which are flat over
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]
and étale over
\mathcal{B}
. Blowing up the intersections
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\cap\widetilde{\sigma}_{3}
leads to a scheme
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathcal{B}
with Cartier divisors
\mathcal{D}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 3
. Thus
\mathcal{D}_{i}\cong\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}
for
i=1,2
and
\mathcal{D}_{3}\cong\widetilde{\sigma}_{3}
. Hence the element
\hat{\theta}\in H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}})
corresponding to
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
lies in the image of
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))
. Its image in
H^{1}(D_{i};T_{D_{i}})
maps onto the class corresponding to
\mathcal{B}
, which is a generator of the one-dimensional vector space
H^{1}(B;T_{B})
. Moreover,
H^{1}(D_{i};T_{D_{i}})\cong H^{1}(B;T_{B})
since the morphisms
D_{i}\to B
induced by projection are étale. Hence the map
H^{1}(\widetilde{Y};T_{\widetilde{Y}}(-\log D))\to H^{1}(D_{i};T_{D_{i}})
is surjective as required.

Remark 7.5
.

In terms of moduli, the exact sequence for
T_{X_{1}}
above shows that
H^{0}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})\cong H^{0}(B;T_{B})
. Then in the case
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(1,1,1)
, there is an exact sequence

H^{0}(B;T_{B})\to H^{0}(\sigma_{3};N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}})\to H^{1}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}}(-\log\overline{D}))\to H^{1}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})\to 0,

where
\overline{D}
is the divisor
\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}+\sigma_{3}
. It is easy to check that
H^{0}(B;T_{B})\to H^{0}(\sigma_{3};N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}})
is an isomorphism, hence
H^{1}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}}(-\log\overline{D}))\to H^{1}(X_{1};T_{X_{1}})
is an isomorphism. Since the exceptional divisors of
\widetilde{Y}\to X_{1}
are determined by
\overline{D}
, the number of moduli of the pair
(\widetilde{Y},D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3})
, or equivalently the dimension of the space of equisingular deformations of
Y
, is one. The surface
\widetilde{Y}
depends however on
4
moduli, because for a small deformation of
\widetilde{Y}
the blowup points don’t have to lie on intersections of the
\overline{D}
.

There is a similar picture for the case
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(2,1,1)
, except that in this case we use the configuration
\Gamma+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{3}
, and the map
H^{0}(B;T_{B})\to H^{0}(\sigma_{2};N_{\sigma_{2}/X_{1}})\oplus H^{0}(\sigma_{3};N_{\sigma_{3}/X_{1}})
has a one-dimensional cokernel. Thus the number of moduli of the pair
(\widetilde{Y},D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3})
in this case is two.

(iv): If
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(2,1,1)
and we smooth a point of multiplicity
1
, then the result
Y_{t}
is an I-surface with two simple elliptic singularities, one of multiplicity
2
and another of multiplicity
1
. By Theorem
2.12
and Lemma
2.13
, the minimal resolution
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
is a rational surface. To deal with the point of multiplicity
2
, recall that
\widetilde{Y}
is obtained as follows: starting with the configuration
\Gamma,\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
, where
\Gamma
is a bisection and
\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
are sections, blow up the three intersection points. Then the proper transform
D_{1}
of
\Gamma
is the component of
D
of square
-2
, and the other components
D_{2}
and
D_{3}
meet an exceptional curve disjoint from
D_{1}
. Thus, we can obtain
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
in this case by blowing up
\Gamma\cap\sigma_{2}
and
\Gamma\cap\sigma_{3}
, then blowing up the intersection point of
(\sigma_{2})_{t}
and
(\sigma_{3})_{t}
(note that the
\sigma_{i}
are stable submanifolds since
H^{1}(\sigma_{i};N_{\sigma_{i}/X_{1}})=0
). The proper transform
D_{1}
of
\Gamma
on the two point blowup is numerically equivalent to an anticanonical divisor, and
2D_{1}
is linearly equivalent to twice an anticanonical divisor. Thus
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
is the blowup of a numerically
K
-trivial surface at one point. Hence it is a blown up Enriques surface.

(v): If
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=(1,1,1)
and we smooth a component which is the proper transform of a bisection, an argument similar to that in (iv) shows that the result is a blown up Enriques surface. Suppose that we start with a configuration
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\sigma_{3}
and blow up the two intersection points
\Gamma_{i}\cap\sigma_{3}
. As usual, let
D_{i}
be the proper transform of
\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
and let
D_{3}
be the proper transform of
\sigma_{3}
. The proper transform
f^{\prime}
of the fiber of the ruled surface passing through
\Gamma_{1}\cap\sigma_{3}
satisfies:
f^{\prime}\cdot D_{1}=1
and
f^{\prime}\cdot D_{2}=2
and becomes an exceptional curve on
\widetilde{Y}
. Now smooth the surface obtained by blowing down
D_{3}
and let
Y_{t}
be the deformed surface with minimal resolution
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
. Since
f^{\prime}
is disjoint from
D_{3}
, it is a stable submanifold and hence deforms to
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
. Then there is an exceptional curve on
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
which meets
D_{2}
in
2
points. Blowing down this exceptional curve, the image
\overline{D}_{2}
is a curve of square
3
. In particular,
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
cannot be the blowup of an Enriques surface at a point, so it must be a rational surface.
∎
