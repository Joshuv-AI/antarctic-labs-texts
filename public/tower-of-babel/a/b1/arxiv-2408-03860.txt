A zero-sum differential game for two opponent masses
Fabio Bagagiolo, Rossana Capuani, Luciano Marzufero

Abstract
We investigate an infinite dimensional partial differential equation of Isaacs’ type, which arises from a zero-sum differential game between two masses. The evolution of the two masses is described by a controlled transport/continuity equation, where the control is given by the velocity vector field. Our study is set in the framework of the viscosity solutions theory in Hilbert spaces, and we prove the uniqueness of the value functions as solutions of the Isaacs equation.



Keywords.
zero-sum games; differential games; infinite-dimensional Isaacs equation; mass transportation; viscosity solutions.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification.
Primary: 49N70; Secondary: 49N75; 49L12; 49L25; 35Q49.

1
Introduction

In the recent paper
[
3
]
, the present authors study a finite-horizon differential game of pursuit-evasion type between a single player and a mass of agents. The player and the mass directly control their own evolution, which for the single agent is an ordinary differential equation and for mass is given by a first order partial differential equation of transport/continuity type. By dynamic programming techniques an infinite dimensional Isaacs equation is derived and it is proved that the value function is the unique viscosity solution on a suitable invariant subset of a Hilbert space.

In the present paper, we extend the model to a competition between two opponent masses/population of agents, say: population
X
and population
Y
. In this case, the controlled dynamics are both given by the following transport/continuity equation

m^{i}_{t}+\operatorname{div}(\beta^{i}m^{i})=0,

(1)

where, for
i\in\{X,Y\}
,
m^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
is the time-dependent mass-distribution of the population
i
(i.e., for
t\in[0,T]
,
m^{i}(t):=m^{i}(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
is the spatial distribution at time
t
) and
\beta^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the time-dependent control of the population
i
, that regulates the velocity and the spreading of the mass in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(i.e., for
t\in[0,T]
,
\beta^{i}(t):=\beta^{i}(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the space-control activated at time
t
).

The two populations are competing in a pursuer-evader fashion, and we model such a competition by a cost as

J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,\beta^{X},\beta^{Y})=\\
\int_{t}^{T}\ell(m^{X}(s),m^{Y}(s),s,\beta^{X}(s),\beta^{Y}(s))ds+g(m^{X}(T),m^{Y}(T)),

where
\ell
and
g
are two suitable functions,
s\longmapsto m^{X}(s),m^{Y}(s)
are the evolutions, as space-dependent functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given by the corresponding equations (
1
), respectively governed by the controls
s\longmapsto\beta^{X}(s),\beta^{Y}(s)
and starting, at time
t
, from
\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
. The competition is then analytically realized by the fact that the population
X
wants to minimize
J
and the population
Y
wants to maximize.

Using an adapted concept of non-anticipating strategies
\gamma^{X},\gamma^{Y}
(see
[
14
]
and
[
4
]
), we define the lower and the upper values of the game as, respectively,

\displaystyle\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\gamma^{X}}\sup_{\beta^{Y}}J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,\gamma^{X}[\beta^{Y}],\beta^{Y}),

\displaystyle\overline{\text{V}}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma^{Y}}\inf_{\beta^{X}}J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,\beta^{X},\gamma^{Y}[\beta^{X}]),

where the non-anticipating strategy
\gamma^{i}
is a suitable function taking controls for the opponent population
\hat{i}\in\{X,Y\}\setminus\{i\}
and giving a control for the population
i
in such a way that it is not dependent upon future behavior.

Since the controlled evolutions
s\mapsto m^{i}(s)
given by the equations in (
1
) are evolutions in a functions space (possibly a Hilbert space), by dynamic programming techniques we get that both lower and upper values are viscosity solutions of a final condition Cauchy problem for an infinite dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs equation which is, respectively

\begin{cases}-\underline{V}_{t}+\\
\inf_{b^{Y}}\sup_{b^{X}}\left\{\langle D_{X}\underline{V},{\rm div}(b^{X}\bar{m}^{X})\rangle+\langle D_{Y}\underline{V},{\rm div}(b^{Y}\bar{m}^{Y})\rangle-\ell(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,b^{X},b^{Y})\right\}=0,\\
\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},T)=g(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y}),\end{cases}

(2)

\begin{cases}-\overline{\text{V}}_{t}+\\
\sup_{b^{X}}\inf_{b^{Y}}\left\{\langle D_{X}\overline{\text{V}},{\rm div}(b^{X}\bar{m}^{Y})\rangle+\langle D_{Y}\overline{\text{V}},{\rm div}(b^{Y}\bar{m}^{Y})\rangle-\ell(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,b^{X},b^{Y})\right\}=0,\\
\overline{\text{V}}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},T)=g(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y}),\end{cases}

(3)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the scalar product in the Hilbert space,
D_{i}
is the Fréchet differential with respect to
m^{i}
, and
b^{i}
is in
B^{i}
, the set of the admissible constant controls
b^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the population
i
. The first problem (
2
) is called the
upper problem
and the second one (
3
) the
lower problem
.

By suitably restricting the domain
{\mathcal{M}}\ni(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)
of the equations in (
2
)-(
3
), however coherently with the controlled evolution in (
1
), we get a uniqueness result proving that the lower value
\underline{V}
(resp. the upper value
\overline{\text{V}}
) is the unique continuous viscosity solution of the upper problem (resp. the lower problem). In particular, when the two Isaacs equations coincide (for example when the running cost
\ell
does not depend on the controls
b^{X},b^{Y}
), then, by uniqueness,
\underline{V}=\overline{\text{V}}
which means that the game has a value.

Similarly to
[
3
]
, the main issues in performing what we said above, are that the differential game is of finite horizon type and, more crucially, that the Isaacs equations are infinite dimensional. This entails that, especially for uniqueness but not only, the restricted domain
{\mathcal{M}}
as above must be suitably constructed, in particular for overcoming the lacking of compactness in the infinite dimensional spaces.

In
[
3
]
one of the two opponents is a single (finite dimensional) player and the motivation for that was a possible further study of a mean field zero-sum game between two populations. In the present paper, the opponents are two (infinite dimensional) players, the two masses. The study of a game between two opponent masses seems to be rather new, in particular in connection with minimax equilibria and the corresponding Isaacs equation set in a Hilbert space. Some problems of this type, in a rather weak set as the Wasserstein probability space, are studied in
[
23
,
15
,
18
,
24
,
6
]
. However, here the goal is to work in a Hilbert setting, that is in the case where the measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, representing the distribution of the masses, have a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We believe that this approach may be more prone to possible real applications, in particular concerning the construction of an optimal feedback. Some of those possible applications may be found, for example, in
[
25
,
16
,
9
,
20
,
21
,
17
,
27
]
.

The study of viscosity solutions for infinite dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi equations goes back to the early stage of the viscosity solutions theory
[
10
,
11
]
(see also
[
4
]
for the finite dimensional case). We also quote
[
19
]
for studies of an infinite dimensional Isaacs equation which however presents many different features with respect to the present one. In particular, in the present model, the controls directly act on the differential operator in the evolution equations (
1
), which are first order equations. Other games with more than one masses are of course already studied, in particular we remind the reader to the papers
[
1
,
8
,
5
,
26
]
where, however, the game is not of the minimax form, as it is in the present paper.

As already said, in this paper we set the problem in a Hilbert space, and we suitably settle it in a viable compact subsets of the space. This approach seems also to be useful for the study of a further model where the first order continuity equations (
1
) are replaced by second order parabolic equations of the form

m^{i}_{t}-\sigma\Delta m^{i}+{\rm div}(\beta^{i}m^{i})=0.

In Section
5
we argue on such a possible future study and we also point out an interesting possible “vanishing viscosity” feature as
\sigma\to 0^{+}
.

In Section
4
we report direct calculations on some one-dimensional examples (
d=1
), and we also argue on the extreme difficulty in doing that, due to the infinite dimensional feature of the problem.

The plan of the rest of the paper is the following: in Section
2
we give some results on the solution of (
1
); in Section
3
we introduce the differential game and its notation. Moreover we prove the dynamic programming principle and study the corresponding Isaacs equation.

2
On the mass distribution: estimates and technicalities

In this section, we provide some preliminary results and useful remarks on the evolution of a mass on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which will be necessary for the next sections. We refer to
[
3
]
for more details, proofs and general references.

2.1
The continuity equation for the mass

The mass distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be represented by the evolution
t\longmapsto\mu(t)
of a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where the quantity of mass in
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
at time
t
is the measure of
A
at the same time:
\mu(t)(A)
. When the mass is moving according to a given time-dependent vector field
\beta:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then, at least formally, the evolution of the measure
\mu
satisfies the following continuity equation (in the distributional sense for measures):

\begin{cases}\mu_{s}(x,s)+\operatorname{div}(\beta(x,s)\mu(x,s))=0,&(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times]t,T[\\
\mu(\cdot,t)=\bar{\mu},\end{cases}

(4)

where
\bar{\mu}
is the value of the measure at the initial time
t
,
T>0
is the final time and, here and in the sequel,
\operatorname{div}
stands for the spatial divergence.

The well-posedness of (
4
) is closely related to the well-posedness of the following Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

\begin{cases}y^{\prime}(s)=\beta(y(s),s),&s\in]t,T[\\
y(t)=x.\end{cases}

(5)

Here we assume the following hypothesis:

(H)

the time-dependent controls
\beta
in (
5
) and (
4
) are functions from
[0,T]
to the set
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, which, for a given fixed
M>0
, is defined as

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:=\left\{b\in W^{2,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}):\right.\\
\left.\|b\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq M,\ \|b\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq M,\ \|\operatorname{div}b\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq M\right\},

(6)

and moreover they belong to

{\mathcal{B}}:=\{\beta\in L^{2}([0,T],W^{2,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})):\beta(\cdot,t)\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\ \text{ for a.e. }t\}.

(7)

Under such hypothesis, the existence and the uniqueness of solutions to equations (
5
) and (
4
) are guaranteed for any initial data
(x,t)
and Borel measure
\bar{\mu}
, respectively. Weaker conditions may also suffice, as noted in references
[
13
,
12
,
2
]
. In particular, the solution to (
4
) is given by the push-forward of
\bar{\mu}
through the flow
\Phi(\cdot,t,s):\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
associated to (
5
). Due to the (only spatial) regularity assumed in (
6
) for the admissible controls, we have the following first remark.

Remark 1
.

If all the fields
b\in\tilde{\cal B}
have their support contained in the same bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then the
L^{2}
-closure
\overline{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
of (
6
) is a compact subset of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is important because the compactness of the admissible constant in time controls (which here are functions of the space
x
, anyway) will be crucial in order to construct suitable non-anticipating strategies for deriving the infinite dimensional Isaacs equations satisfied by the values of the game.

When
\bar{\mu}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
{\mathcal{L}}^{d}
, that is
\bar{\mu}=\bar{m}\mathcal{L}^{d}
, where
\bar{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is the density, all the measures
\mu(\cdot,s)
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{d}
. Their density
m(\cdot,s)
can be explicitly computed as (see
[
2
]
)

m(\cdot,s)=\frac{\bar{m}(\cdot)}{{|\rm{det}}J\Phi(\cdot,t,s)|}\circ\Phi^{-1}(\cdot,t,s),

(8)

where
\Phi^{-1}(\cdot,t,s)
is the inverse of the flow associated to (
5
) and
J\Phi
is its Jacobian matrix. In the following, let
m(x,s;\beta,t,\bar{m})
denote the value at
(x,s)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times]t,T]
of the density of the solution of (
4
) with
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}
, where the initial datum at time
t
,
\bar{\mu}
, has density
\bar{m}
. From formula (
8
) and the regularities in (
6
)-(
7
), the following results can be proved.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
(H)
holds and
\bar{m}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then

(i)

for every
t\in[0,T]
and
s\in[t,T]

x\longmapsto m(x,s;\beta,t,\bar{m})\text{ belongs to }H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

(ii)

for every
t\in[0,T]

m(\cdot,\cdot;\beta,t,\bar{m})\in C^{0}([t,T],H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),

where the modulus of continuity is independent of
t,\beta
and only depends on the norms of
\bar{m}
;

(iii)

for every
t\in[0,T]
and
s\in[t,T]

\|m(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m})\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq e^{MT}\sup\{1+\tilde{M},L_{\Phi^{-1}}\}\|\bar{m}(\cdot)\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},

(9)

where the constant
\tilde{M}>0
encloses the bounds for the spatial derivative of
\operatorname{div}\beta
,
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
, independent of
t,\beta
, and
L_{\Phi^{-1}}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\Phi^{-1}(\cdot,t,s)
.

Moreover, it holds the following

\|m(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{1},\bar{m}^{1})-m(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{2},\bar{m}^{2})\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\tilde{L}\left(|t_{1}-t_{2}|+\|\bar{m}^{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right),

(10)

for every
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
,
s\in[\max\{t_{1},t_{2}\},T]
and for some
\tilde{L}
independent of
t_{1},t_{2}
and
\beta
.

Thanks to Proposition
1
, we have the following second remark.

Remark 2
.

By Proposition
1
and assumption
(H)
, we observe that
\operatorname{div}(\beta m),\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T])
. Moreover, since
\mu
is the solution of (
4
) in the sense of measures, we have that its density
m
is solution, in the distributional sense for measures, of
\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}+\operatorname{div}(\beta m)=0
. Since
\frac{\partial m}{\partial t},\operatorname{div}(\beta m)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T])
,
m
is the unique solution in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T])
(see
[
12
]
). In particular, we have that
\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}=-\operatorname{div}(\beta m)
as functions in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,T])
. From this, we can conclude that if
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, then the map

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle:[t,T]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\longmapsto\xi(\tau)=\varphi(m(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}))

is differentiable and
\xi^{\prime}(\tau)=-\langle D_{m}\varphi,\operatorname{div}(\beta m)\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
, where
D_{m}\varphi
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi
w.r.t.
m
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
the scalar product in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. This last fact is essential for working with dynamic programming and viscosity solutions.

2.2
A suitable invariant set for the evolution of the mass

The aim of this section is to construct a suitable set
{\mathcal{M}}
which is compact in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]
and invariant for the controlled evolution
m(\cdot,s,{\alpha},t,\bar{m})
. Up to proper adjustments, this will be the set in which we are going to consider the Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs equation of the differential game (see §
3.2
).

We fix an open bounded subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a constant
K>0
such that all the admissible initial distributions of the mass belong to

{\mathcal{G}}=\left\{m_{0}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega):\|m_{0}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq K,\ \mathrm{supp}\;m_{0}\subset\Omega\right\}.

Referring to point
(iii)
in the Proposition
1
, we define
B:=\max\{1+\tilde{M},L_{\Phi^{-1}}\}K
. For all
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{G}}
,
{\beta}\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
s\in[0,T]
, we obtain

\|m(\cdot,s;{\beta},0,m_{0})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq Be^{MT}.

(11)

Recalling that
M
is the bound for
\|{\beta}(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}
(see (
6
)), we define, for all
t\in[0,T]
and for all
s\in[t,T]
, the bounded sets

\Omega_{1}(t)=\overline{B}(\Omega,Mt),\ \ \Omega_{1}(s,t)=\overline{B}\left(\Omega_{1}(t),M(s-t)\right),

being, as example,
\overline{B}(\Omega,Mt)=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\text{dist}(x,\Omega)\leq Mt\}
. A sort of semigroup property holds:
\Omega_{1}(0)=\overline{\Omega}
,
\Omega_{1}(s,t)=\Omega_{1}(s)
for all
t\in[0,s]
and, in particular,
\Omega_{1}(T)=\Omega_{1}(T,t)
. Namely, assuming
\bar{m}\in W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\mathrm{supp}\;\bar{m}\subset\Omega_{1}(t):=\overline{B}(\Omega,Mt)
, we have, independently of
{\beta}\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

\mathrm{supp}\;m(\cdot,s^{\prime};{\beta},\bar{m}^{X})\subset\Omega_{1}(s^{\prime},t)\subset\Omega_{1}(s^{\prime\prime},t)\subset\Omega_{1}(T,t)=\Omega_{1}(T)\\

for all
t\leq s^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime\prime}\leq T
. Observe that all such functions
m
can be extended to the entire set
\Omega_{1}(T)
by setting them equal to
0
outside their support. Now, for every
t\in[0,T]
, let
K(t)\subset H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(T))
be a compact set such that
K(t^{\prime})\subset K(t)
for every
0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t\leq T
,
m\in K(t)
if
K(t_{n})\ni m_{n}\longrightarrow m
in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(T))
and
t_{n}\to t
in
[0,T]
, and for every
0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t\leq T
,
K(t)
contains
m(\cdot,t;\beta,t^{\prime},\bar{m})
for every
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
\bar{m}\in K(t^{\prime})
. Observe that, by (
10
) and suitable possible convergences on the set of controls
\beta
, a simple example of such compact sets
K(t)
may be the points of all trajectories up to the time
t
started at time
0
from points of a compact set
K
in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(T))
. Recalling (
11
), then for all
t\in[0,T]
, we define the following sets:

{\mathcal{M}}(t):=\left\{\bar{m}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}(T))\cap K(t):\mathrm{supp}\;\bar{m}\subset\Omega_{1}(t),\right.\\
\left.\|m(\cdot,s;{\beta},t,\bar{m})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}(T))}\leq Be^{MT}\ \text{for all }s\in[t,T]\text{ and }{\beta}\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\};

{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\left({\mathcal{M}}(t)\times\{t\}\right)=\left\{(\bar{m},t)\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}(T))\cap K(t)\times[0,T]:\right.\\
\left.\mathrm{supp}\;\bar{m}\subset\Omega_{1}(t),\ \|m(\cdot,s;{\beta},t,\bar{m})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}(T))}\leq Be^{MT}\ \text{for all }s\in[t,T]\text{ and }\ \beta\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}.

By construction,
{\mathcal{M}}
is compact in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(T))\times[0,T]
and hence also in
L^{2}(\Omega_{1}(T))\times[0,T]
, as indeed similarly requested in
[
3
]
.

Thanks to compactness, the
H^{1}
and
L^{2}
norms are topologically equivalent in
{\mathcal{M}}
. That is, there exists a modulus of continuity
\tilde{\omega}
such that for every
(m_{1},t_{1}),(m_{2},t_{2})\in{\mathcal{M}}
one has

\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(T))}\leq\tilde{\omega}\left(\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}(T))}\right).

(12)

Furthermore,
{\mathcal{M}}
contains all the possible evolutions
s\longmapsto(m(\cdot,s;{\beta},t,\bar{m}),s)=(m(s),s)
(with field
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}
) starting from any
(\bar{m},t)\in{\mathcal{M}}
. In fact, for all
s\in[t,T]
and for
\tau\in[s,T]
, it holds

\|m(\cdot,\tau;{\beta},s,m(s))\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}(T))}=\|m(\cdot,\tau;{\beta},t,\bar{m})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}(T))}\leq Be^{MT}.

Thus
(m(s),s)\in{\mathcal{M}}
because
\mathrm{supp}\;m(s)\subset\Omega_{1}(s)
since
\mathrm{supp}\;\bar{m}\subset\Omega_{1}(t)
. Therefore
{\mathcal{M}}
is invariant for the evolution of the continuity equation with field
{\beta}\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Specifically, it contains all the trajectories
t\longmapsto(m(t),t)
starting from
(m_{0},0)
with
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{G}}\cap K(0)
.

3
The differential game model

In this section, we introduce the model of the differential game between two opponent masses, we prove the dynamic programming principle and study the corresponding Isaacs equation.

The controlled equations for the evolution of the masses
m^{X}
and
m^{Y}
are given by

\begin{cases}m^{X}_{s}(\cdot,s)+\operatorname{div}({\alpha}(\cdot,s)m^{X}(\cdot,s))=0,&s\in]t,T]\\
m^{X}(\cdot,t)=\bar{m}^{X},\end{cases}

(13)

\begin{cases}m^{Y}_{s}(\cdot,s)+\operatorname{div}({\beta}(\cdot,s)m^{Y}(\cdot,s))=0,&s\in]t,T]\\
m^{Y}(\cdot,t)=\bar{m}^{Y},\end{cases}

(14)

where
t\in[0,T]
,
\bar{m}^{i}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
i\in\{X,Y\}
, and the controls
{\alpha}
and
{\beta}
belong to

{\mathcal{A}}(t):=\{{\alpha}\in L^{2}([t,T],W^{2,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})):{\alpha}(\cdot,s)\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\ \text{ for a.e. }s\}

(15)

and

{\mathcal{B}}(t):=\{{\beta}\in L^{2}([t,T],W^{2,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})):{\beta}(\cdot,s)\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\ \text{ for a.e. }s\}

(16)

respectively, where the sets
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
are taken as (
6
) with the constant
M
which can be generally different for each population.

Consequently, the same holds for the estimates in Proposition
1
: the constants in (
9
)-(
10
) can generally be different for populations
X
and
Y
. Note also that, in contrast to the previous section and the Introduction, in (
15
)-(
16
) we explicitly introduce the initial-time-dependence of the controls in the notation. We call
\alpha
the controls for the mass
X
(named as
\beta^{X}
in the Introduction) and
\beta
the controls for the mass
Y
(named as
\beta^{Y}
in the introduction). Coherently, in the following we will use
a
to denote a generic constant (in time) control for the mass
X
belonging to
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
, as well as
b
to denote for a generic constant (in time) control for the mass
Y
belonging to
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
.

Let us consider the following.

•

Running cost

\displaystyle\ell:H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[

\displaystyle(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},s,a,b)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\ell(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},s,a,b),

and suppose that it is bounded, strongly continuous and uniformly strongly continuous w.r.t.
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)
uniformly w.r.t.
(a,b)
, that is, there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{\ell}
such that, for any fixed
a,b
it is

|\ell(\bar{m}_{1}^{X},\bar{m}_{1}^{Y},s_{1},a,b)-\ell(\bar{m}_{2}^{X},\bar{m}_{2}^{Y},s_{2},a,b)|\\
\leq\omega_{\ell}\left(\|\bar{m}_{1}^{X}-\bar{m}_{1}^{Y}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\|\bar{m}_{2}^{X}-\bar{m}_{2}^{Y}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+|s_{1}-s_{2}|\right)

for all
(\bar{m}_{1}^{X},\bar{m}_{1}^{Y},s_{1}),(\bar{m}_{2}^{X},\bar{m}_{2}^{Y},s_{2})\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]
.

•

Final cost

\displaystyle\psi:H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[

\displaystyle(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\psi(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y}),

and suppose that it is bounded and uniformly strongly continuous w.r.t.
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})
. We denote by
\omega_{\psi}
its modulus of continuity.

The corresponding cost functional
J
is given by

J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,{\alpha},\beta)=\int_{t}^{T}\ell\left(m^{X}\left(\cdot,s;{\alpha},t,\bar{m}^{X}\right),m^{Y}\left(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}\right),s,{\alpha}(\cdot,s),\beta(\cdot,s)\right)ds\\
+\psi\left(m^{X}\left(\cdot,T;{\alpha},t,\bar{m}^{X}\right),m^{Y}\left(\cdot,T;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}\right)\right),

for all
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,{\alpha},{\beta})\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]\times{\mathcal{A}}(t)\times{\mathcal{B}}(t)
.

In our model, the mass
m^{X}
aims to minimize the cost
J
while the mass
m^{Y}
aims to maximize it. Therefore, let us first introduce the non-anticipating strategies for the mass
m^{X}

\Gamma(t)=\{\gamma:{\mathcal{B}}(t)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{A}}(t):\beta\longmapsto\gamma[\beta]\ \text{non-anticipating}\}.

We point out that “non-anticipating”, in this context, means that for all
\tau\in[t,T]
,

\beta_{1}(\cdot,s)=\beta_{2}(\cdot,s)\ \text{in $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}$ a.e. $s$}\in[t,\tau]\ \Rightarrow\gamma[\beta_{1}](\cdot,s)=\gamma[\beta_{2}](\cdot,s)\ \text{in $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$}\ \text{a.e.}\ s\in[t,\tau].

Next, we define the lower value function associated to the cost functional
J
as follows:

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(t)}\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)}J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,\gamma[\beta],\beta).

(17)

In the same way, we can define the non-anticipating strategies for the mass
m^{Y}
:

\Delta(t)=\{\delta:{\mathcal{A}}(t)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{B}}(t):\alpha\longmapsto\delta[\alpha]\ \text{non-anticipating}\},

and consider the upper value function

\overline{\text{V}}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)=\sup_{\delta\in\Delta(t)}\inf_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}(t)}J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,\alpha,\delta[\alpha]).

As mentioned in the Introduction,
\underline{V}
and
\overline{\text{V}}
will be the unique continuous viscosity solutions of the upper Isaacs problem and of the lower Isaacs problem, respectively. In the following, we will focus on
\underline{V}
and the upper problem, as the lower problem is similar and symmetric.

3.1
Dynamic Programming Principle and regularity of the value function

In this section, we deduce first the dynamic programming principle and then a regularity result for the value function.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that the assumptions on
\ell
,
\psi
and (
7
) hold. Then the lower value function
\underline{V}
, defined in (
17
), satisfies the Dynamic Programming Principle. More precisely, for all
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
and for all
\tau\in[t,T]
,

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\\
=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(t)}\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)}\Bigg(\int_{t}^{\tau}\ell\left(m^{X}\left(\cdot,s;\gamma[\beta],t,\bar{m}^{X}\right),m^{Y}\left(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}\right),s,\gamma[\beta](\cdot,s),\beta(\cdot,s)\right)ds\\
+\underline{V}(m^{X}\left(\cdot,\tau;\gamma[\beta],t,\bar{m}^{X}\right),m^{Y}(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),\tau)\Bigg).

(18)

Proof.

For all
(p,q,\tau)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
and for all
\varepsilon>0
we choose
\gamma_{(p,q,\tau)}\in\Gamma(\tau)
such that

\underline{V}(p,q,\tau)\geq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)}J(p,q,\tau,\gamma_{(p,q,\tau)}[\beta],\beta)-\varepsilon.

Let
t\in[0,\tau]
. For simplicity, we denote by
w(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)
the right-hand side of (
18
). In order to prove the (
18
), we show the following:

(i)

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\leq w(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)
;

(ii)

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\geq w(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)
.

As usual, the proof of
(ii)
is more involved than the one of
(i)
(see for example
[
4
]
for the finite dimensional case). Here, we prove
(i)
for the present case with two masses. For
(ii)
, we refer to
[
3
]
, with appropriate modifications for the two masses scenario.

Let us consider
\bar{\gamma}\in\Gamma(t)
such that

w(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\geq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)}\Bigg(\int_{t}^{\tau}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\bar{\gamma}[\beta],t,\bar{m}^{X}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),s,\bar{\gamma}[\beta](s),\beta(\cdot,s))ds\\
+\underline{V}(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\bar{\gamma}[\beta],\tau,\bar{m}),m^{Y}(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}),\tau)\Bigg)-\varepsilon.

Taken
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)
, and still denoting by
\beta
its restriction to
[\tau,T]
, we have
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(\tau)
. We define
\tilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma(t)
as

\tilde{\gamma}[\beta](s)=\begin{cases}\bar{\gamma}[\beta](s),&s\in[t,\tau]\\
\gamma_{(m^{X}(\cdot,\tau;\bar{\gamma}[\beta],t,\bar{m}^{X}),m^{Y}(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),\tau)}[\beta](s),&s\in[\tau,T]\end{cases}

where
m^{X}(\cdot,\tau;\bar{\gamma}[\beta],t,\bar{m}^{X})
is the density at time
\tau
of the solution to (
13
) with
{\alpha}=\bar{\gamma}[\beta]
. The strategy
\tilde{\gamma}
is well-defined and non-anticipating, i.e., it belongs to
\Gamma(t)
. So we have

w(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\geq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)}\Bigg(\int_{t}^{\tau}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{X}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),s,\bar{\gamma}[\beta](s),\beta(\cdot,s))ds\\
+\underline{V}(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\beta,\tau,\bar{m}),m^{Y}(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}),\tau)\Bigg)-\varepsilon\\
\geq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)}\Bigg(\int_{t}^{\tau}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{X}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),s,\bar{\gamma}[\beta](s),\beta(\cdot,s))\\
+J(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{X}),m^{Y}(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),\tau,\gamma_{(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{X}),m^{Y}(\cdot,\tau;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y}),\tau)}[\beta],\beta)\Bigg)-2\varepsilon\\
=\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)}J(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t,\tilde{\gamma}[\beta],\beta)-2\varepsilon\geq\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)-2\varepsilon,

and this concludes the proof of point
(i)
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that the assumptions on
\ell
,
\psi
and (
7
) hold. Then the lower value function
\underline{V}
is bounded and uniformly continuous.

Proof.

Since
\ell
and
\psi
are bounded function, we have that for all
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)\leq G_{1}T+G_{2},

where
G_{1}>0
and
G_{2}>0
are the bounds for
\ell
and
\psi
respectively. So
\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)
is bounded.

Consider
(m^{X}_{1},m^{Y}_{1},t_{1}),(m^{X}_{2},m^{Y}_{2},t_{2})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
, and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma(t_{2})
be such that

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2})\geq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{2})}J(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2},\gamma_{2}[\beta],\beta)-\varepsilon.

We define
\gamma_{2}^{1}\in\Gamma(t_{1})
(and hence applying to controls in
{\mathcal{B}}(t_{1})
, which are defined on
[t_{1},T]
) as

\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta](s)=\begin{cases}\gamma_{2}[\tilde{\beta}](s)&\text{for any }s\ \text{if }t_{2}\leq t_{1}\\
\bar{a}&\text{for }t_{1}\leq s\leq t_{2}\ \text{if }t_{1}\leq t_{2}\\
\gamma_{2}[\beta_{|[t_{2},T]}](s)&\text{for }s\geq t_{2}\ \text{if }t_{1}\leq t_{2}\end{cases},

where
\bar{a}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
is any a priori fixed constant (in time) control for the mass
X
and, if
t_{2}\leq t_{1}
,

\tilde{\beta}(\cdot,s)=\begin{cases}\tilde{b}&\text{if }t_{2}\leq s\leq t_{1}\\
\beta(\cdot,s)&\text{if }s\geq t_{1}\end{cases}.

where
\tilde{b}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is any a priori fixed constant (in time) control for the mass
Y
.

Consider
\beta_{1}\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{1})
such that

J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta_{1}],\beta_{1})\geq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{1})}J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta],\beta)-\varepsilon.

Moreover, let us take
\beta_{1}^{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{2})
such that

\beta_{1}^{2}(\cdot,s)=\beta_{1}(\cdot,s)\ \text{for }s\geq t_{2}+|t_{1}-t_{2}|.

So we have

\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1})-\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2})\\
\leq\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{1})}J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta],\beta)-\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{2})}J(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2},\gamma_{2}[\beta],\beta)+\varepsilon\\
\leq J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta_{1}],\beta_{1})-J(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2},\gamma_{2}[\beta_{1}^{2}],\beta_{1}^{2})+2\varepsilon.

Now, if

|J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta_{1}],\beta_{1})-J(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2},\gamma_{2}[\beta_{1}^{2}],\beta_{1}^{2})|

(19)

is infinitesimal as
|t_{1}-t_{2}|+\|\bar{m}^{X}_{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{X}_{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\bar{m}^{Y}_{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
, it goes to zero then we conclude.

We prove it in the case
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
. The case
t_{2}\leq t_{1}
goes similarly. Let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})
and
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t_{1})
, then (
19
) is equal to

|J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta_{1}],\beta_{1})-J(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2},\gamma_{2}[\beta_{1}^{2}],\beta_{1}^{2})|=\\
\Bigg|\int_{t_{1}}^{T}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t_{1},\bar{m}^{X}_{1}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1}),s,\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta_{1}],\beta_{1}(\cdot,s))ds\\
+\psi(m^{X}(\cdot,T;\alpha,t_{1},\bar{m}^{X}_{1}),m^{Y}(\cdot,T;\beta,t_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1}))\\
-\int_{t_{2}}^{T}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t_{2},\bar{m}^{X}_{2}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}),s,\gamma_{2}[\beta_{1}^{2}](s),\beta_{1}^{2}(\cdot,s))ds\\
-\psi(m^{X}(\cdot,T;\alpha,t_{2},\bar{m}^{X}_{2}),m^{Y}(\cdot,T;\beta,t_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}))\Bigg|\\
=\Bigg|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t_{1},\bar{m}^{X}_{1}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1}),s,\bar{a},\beta_{1}(\cdot,s))ds\\
+\int_{t_{2}}^{T}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t_{1},\bar{m}^{X}_{1}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{1},m^{Y}_{1}),s,\gamma_{2}[\beta_{|[t_{2},T]}](s),\beta_{1}^{2}(\cdot,s))ds\\
-\int_{t_{2}}^{T}\ell(m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t_{2},\bar{m}^{X}_{2}),m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}),s,\gamma_{2}[\beta_{|[t_{2},T]}](s),\beta_{1}^{2}(\cdot,s))ds\\
+\psi(m^{X}(\cdot,T;\alpha,t_{1},\bar{m}^{X}_{1}),m^{Y}(\cdot,T;\beta,t_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1})))-\psi(m^{X}(\cdot,T;\alpha,t_{2},\bar{m}^{X}_{2}),m^{Y}(\cdot,T;\beta,t_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}))\Bigg|.\\

Since
\ell
and
\psi
are uniformly strongly continuous, and using (
10
) in Proposition
1
, we derive the following estimation

|J(\bar{m}^{X}_{1},\bar{m}^{Y}_{1},t_{1},\gamma_{2}^{1}[\beta_{1}],\beta_{1})-J(\bar{m}^{X}_{2},\bar{m}^{Y}_{2},t_{2},\gamma_{2}[\beta_{1}^{2}],\beta_{1}^{2})|\\
\leq G_{1}|t_{1}-t_{2}|+(\omega_{\psi}+T\omega_{\ell})\left(\tilde{L}\left(\|\bar{m}^{X}_{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{X}_{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\|\bar{m}^{Y}_{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\right)\right),

where
\tilde{L}
is a suitable constant (see (

) and also the comments after (
15
)-(

)).
Therefore, as
|t_{1}-t_{2}|+\|\bar{m}^{X}_{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{X}_{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\bar{m}^{Y}_{1}(\cdot)-\bar{m}^{Y}_{2}(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
goes to zero, (
19
) is infinitesimal. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
The Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs equation for
\underline{V}

In this section, we derive the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs equation and we are going to consider it in a suitable set
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
for the variables
(m^{X},m^{Y},t)
, which is compact in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]
and invariant for the controlled evolutions
m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t,\bar{m}^{X})
and
m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y})
. Such a set is detected by an analogous construction as the one in §
2.2
, one per each population
X
and
Y
. Indeed, there will be a set
{\mathcal{M}}^{X}
for the evolution
m^{X}(\cdot,s;\alpha,t,\bar{m}^{X})
and a set
{\mathcal{M}}^{Y}
for the evolution
m^{Y}(\cdot,s;\beta,t,\bar{m}^{Y})
, and both of them are constructed with suitable constants and sets for each population. Therefore, the domain we are looking for is, for suitable compact sets
K^{X}(t)\subset H^{1}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))
and
K^{Y}(t)\subset H^{1}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))
as in §
2.2
,

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\left({\mathcal{M}}^{X}(t)\times{\mathcal{M}}^{Y}(t)\times\{t\}\right)\\
=\left\{(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{X},t)\in\left(W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))\cap K^{X}(t)\right)\times\left(W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))\cap K^{Y}(t)\right)\times[0,T]:\right.\\
\left.\mathrm{supp}\;\bar{m}^{X}\subset\Omega_{1}^{X}(t),\ \mathrm{supp}\;\bar{m}^{Y}\subset\Omega_{1}^{Y}(t),\right.\\
\left.\|m^{X}(\cdot,s;{\alpha},t,\bar{m}^{X})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}\leq B^{X}e^{M^{X}T},\ \|m^{Y}(\cdot,s;{\beta},t,\bar{m}^{Y})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}\leq B^{Y}e^{M^{Y}T}\right.\\
\left.\text{for all }s\in[t,T],\ {\alpha}\in{\mathcal{A}}(t)\ \text{ and }\ \beta\in{\mathcal{B}}(t)\right\}

(20)

and it satisfies the above required properties. We also notice that, in general, the constants
B^{X},B^{Y}
as well as
M^{X},M^{Y}
may be different.

We define the Hamiltonian function
H:\tilde{\mathcal{M}}\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

H(m^{X},m^{Y},t,p,q):=\min_{b\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\max_{a\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}}\left\{\langle p,\operatorname{div}(am^{X})\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\langle q,\operatorname{div}(bm^{Y})\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}-\ell(m^{X},m^{Y},t,a,b)\right\}.

In the following, we will consider the fields
{\alpha}(\cdot,s)
and
\beta(\cdot,s)
defined on the compact sets
\Omega_{1}^{X}(T)
and
\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T)
(see §
2.2
), i.e.,
{\alpha}:\Omega_{1}^{X}(T)\times[t,T]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\beta:\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T)\times[t,T]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, respectively. Additionally,
D_{X}
and
D_{Y}
will denote the Fréchet differentials with respect to
m^{X}
and
m^{Y}
, respectively.

Definition 1
.

A function
u\in C^{0}(\tilde{\mathcal{M}})
is a viscosity subsolution of

-u_{t}(m^{X},m^{Y},t)+H(m^{X},m^{Y},t,D_{X}u,D_{Y}u)=0\quad\text{in }\tilde{\mathcal{M}}

(21)

if, for any
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T])
,

-\varphi_{t}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})+H(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t},D_{X}\varphi(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t}),D_{Y}\varphi(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t}))\leq 0

at any local maximum point
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
of
u-\varphi
. Similarly,
u\in C^{0}(\tilde{\mathcal{M}})
is a viscosity supersolution of (
21
) if, for any
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T])
,

-\varphi_{t}(\tilde{m}^{X},\tilde{m}^{Y},\tilde{t})+H(\tilde{m}^{X},\tilde{m}^{Y},\tilde{t},D_{X}\varphi(\tilde{m}^{X},\tilde{m}^{Y},\tilde{t}),D_{Y}\varphi(\tilde{m}^{X},\tilde{m}^{Y},\tilde{t}))\geq 0

at any local minimum point
(\tilde{m}^{X},\tilde{m}^{Y},\tilde{t})\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
of
u-\varphi
. Finally,
u
is a viscosity solution of (
21
) if it is simultaneously a viscosity sub- and supersolution.

We observe that the above local maximum/minimum point is w.r.t.
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
and not necessarily w.r.t.
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]
, where the test function is defined. This is not a problem. As discussed in §
2.2
,
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
is invariant for our evolutions and moreover, in
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
the convergences of
m^{X}
and
m^{Y}
in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
are equivalent.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the previous assumptions, the lower value function
\underline{V}
is a viscosity solution of

\begin{cases}-V_{t}+H(m^{X},m^{Y},t,D_{X}V,D_{Y}V)=0&\text{in }\tilde{\mathcal{M}}\\
V(m^{X},m^{Y},T)=\psi(m^{X},m^{Y}),&(m^{X},m^{Y})\in{\mathcal{M}}^{X}(T)\times{\mathcal{M}}^{Y}(T)\times\{T\}\end{cases}.

(22)

Proof.

We will prove only that
\underline{V}
is a supersolution of (
22
). The proof that it is also a subsolution requires a suitable lemma, which is provided below as Lemma
1
(see also Remark
1
). This lemma is formulated for our infinite-dimensional case with two masses. The proof of the lemma can be easily derived from the corresponding result in
[
3
]
for the case with one mass and a single player.

Take
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T])
. Let
(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})
be a local minimum point for
\underline{V}-\varphi
, and
\underline{V}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})=\varphi(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})
. By contradiction, we suppose that

-\varphi_{t}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})+H(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t},D_{X}\underline{V},D_{Y}\underline{V})=-\theta<0.

By the definition of
H
, there exists
b^{*}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
such that

-\varphi_{t}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t})+\langle D_{X}\varphi(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t}),\operatorname{div}(a\bar{m}^{X})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}\\
+\langle D_{Y}\varphi(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t}),\operatorname{div}(b^{*}\bar{m}^{Y})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}-\ell(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},\bar{t},a,b^{*})\leq-\theta

for all
a\in A
. For
\tau
sufficiently close to

(m_{2}^{X},m_{2}^{Y},t_{2})\longmapsto-u_{2}(m_{2}^{X},m_{2}^{Y},t_{2})+\varphi^{2}(m_{2}^{X},m_{2}^{Y},t_{2}),

where

\varphi^{2}(m_{2}^{X},m_{2}^{Y},t_{2})=u_{1}(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X},\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{1})-\frac{\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-m_{2}^{X}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-m_{2}^{Y}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}}{\xi^{2}}-\frac{|\tilde{t}_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}}{{\alpha}^{2}},

but
u_{2}
is a viscosity supersolution of (
22
) and
(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2})\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
. Then

-\varphi^{2}_{t}(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2})+H(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2},D_{X}\varphi^{2}(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2}),D_{Y}\varphi^{2}(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2}))\\
=\frac{2(\tilde{t}_{2}-\tilde{t}_{1})}{{\alpha}^{2}}+H\left(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X})}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y})}{\xi^{2}}\right)\geq 0.

By subtracting the two viscosity inequalities, we obtain

H\left(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X},\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{1},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X})}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y})}{\xi^{2}}\right)\\
-H\left(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X})}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y})}{\xi^{2}}\right)\leq-\eta.

Now we use Lemma
2
to get

H\left(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X},\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{1},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X})}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y})}{\xi^{2}}\right)-H\left(\tilde{m}_{2}^{X},\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y},\tilde{t}_{2},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X})}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{2(\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y})}{\xi^{2}}\right)\\
\leq M\left(\frac{\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}}{\xi^{2}}\right)\\
+\omega_{\ell}\left(\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}+\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}+|\tilde{t}_{1}-\tilde{t}_{2}|\right)\leq-\eta.

But, on one side,
\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{x}_{2}^{X}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))},\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))},|\tilde{t}_{1}-\tilde{t}_{2}|\to 0
as
\varepsilon,\xi,{\alpha}\to 0
(see (
24
)) and hence also
\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}\to 0
and
\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}\to 0
as
\xi\to 0
since (
12
) holds. On the other side

M\left(\frac{\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{X}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{X}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{X}(T))}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\|\tilde{m}_{1}^{Y}-\tilde{m}_{2}^{Y}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1}^{Y}(T))}}{\xi^{2}}\right)\to 0\quad\text{when }\varepsilon,\xi\to 0.

The above inequality gives a contradiction.

\hfill\square

4
Two one-dimensional examples

In this section, we argue about two one-dimensional examples (
d=1
).

Following the one-dimensional examples reported in
[
3
]
, here we may consider the case where the running cost
\ell
is zero and the final cost is given by

\psi_{1}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{X}(x)\bar{m}^{Y}(x)dx

(26)

or, for some a-priori fixed
\delta>0
,

\psi_{2}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})=\left(\int_{\tilde{m}^{X}-\delta}^{\tilde{m}^{X}+\delta}\bar{m}^{Y}(x)dx-\int_{\tilde{m}^{Y}-\delta}^{\tilde{m}^{Y}+\delta}\bar{m}^{X}(x)dx\right)^{2},

(27)

where
\tilde{m}^{X}
and
\tilde{m}^{Y}
are the mean of
\bar{m}^{X}:\int_{\mathbb{R}}xm^{X}(x)dx
and of
\bar{m}^{Y}:\int_{\mathbb{R}}xm^{Y}(x)dx
, respectively.
Or

\psi_{3}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})=(\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y})^{2},

(28)

Note that, since
\ell\equiv 0
, then the lower and upper Isaacs equations coincide and hence, under our hypotheses on controls as in the previous sections, the game has a value, which in the sequel we are going to denote by
V
. Moreover, in all examples, besides the final datum, the Isaacs problem is given by

-V_{t}+\sup_{a}\langle D_{X}V,(a\bar{m}^{X})_{x}\rangle+\inf_{b}\langle D_{Y}V,(b\bar{m}^{Y})_{x}\rangle=0.

(29)

The cases (
26
), (
27
) seem to be not immediate to be treated “by hand”, as the example 1 in
[
3
]
, because they are likely to involve a change in the shape of the masses making the solution highly dependent upon the Fréchet differentials. However, we are going to briefly argue about the case (
26
) in the present
second example
.

First example
. The case (
28
) is instead easier as example 2 in
[
3
]
, giving a “rigid optimal movement”. Indeed, suppose that both masses have mass equal
1
, compact support and that the admissible controls take values in the interval
[-c,c]
. Then the value function is the constant in time:

V(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)=\psi_{3}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})=(\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y})^{2}.

Indeed,
V_{t}=0,D_{X}V=2(\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y})x,D_{Y}V=-2(\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y})x
, and hence (
29
) becomes

-0+2c|\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y}|-2c|\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y}|=0,

where, for example, we have used the fact that, integrating by parts and recalling that
m^{X}
is positive, has compact support and total mass
1
,

2(\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y})\sup_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x(a\bar{m}^{X})_{x}dx=-2(\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y})\sup_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}}a\bar{m}^{X}dx=2c|\tilde{m}^{X}-\tilde{m}^{Y}|.

The optimal movement is then “rigid”: the (Nash) equilibrium is given by simultaneously moving at the maximal velocity
c
for the agents of both masses and in the same suitable one of the two directions (left or right).

Second example.
Here we consider the problem with zero running cost and final cost (
26
)

\psi_{1}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{X}(x)\bar{m}^{Y}(x)dx.

We assume that the admissible controls at disposal of the two masses are constant with respect to
x\in\mathbb{R}
(and hence the movements are rigid) and that the velocities are the same. This means that the sets of controls (
6
) are the same for both masses and that
|a|,|b|\leq c
,
a_{x},b_{x}=0
. In this case, the value function turns out to be constant in time, that is

V(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y},t)=\psi_{1}(\bar{m}^{X},\bar{m}^{Y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{X}(x)\bar{m}^{Y}(x)dx.

Indeed, using the fact that the controls are constant w.r.t.
x
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle V_{t}=0,\ D_{X}V=\bar{m}^{Y},\ D_{Y}V=\bar{m}^{X},\\
\displaystyle\sup_{a\in[-c,c]}\langle D_{X}V,(a\bar{m}^{X})_{x}\rangle=\sup_{a\in[-c,c]}\left(a\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{X}_{x}\bar{m}^{Y}dx\right)=c\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{X}_{x}\bar{m}^{Y}dx\right|,\\
\displaystyle\inf_{b\in[-c,c]}\langle D_{Y}V,(b\bar{m}^{Y})_{x}\rangle=\inf_{b\in[-c,c]}\left(b\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{Y}_{x}\bar{m}^{X}dx\right)=-c\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{Y}_{x}\bar{m}^{X}dx\right|.\end{array}

(30)

But, since the masses have compact supports, it is

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\bar{m}^{X}\bar{m}^{Y})_{x}dx=0\ \Rightarrow\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{X}_{x}\bar{m}^{Y}dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{m}^{Y}_{x}\bar{m}^{X}dx,

and we conclude that the sum of the last two lines in (
30
) is, for some optimal
\tilde{c}\in\{-c,c\}
,

\tilde{c}\left(\int\bar{m}^{X}_{x}\bar{m}^{Y}dx+\int\bar{m}^{Y}_{x}\bar{m}^{X}dx\right)=\tilde{c}\int(\bar{m}^{X}\bar{m}^{Y})_{x}dx=0,

and then equation (
29
) is satisfied by
V
. Also in this case the (Nash) equilibrium is given by a simultaneous movement of both masses at the maximal velocity
c
in the direction given by the sign of
\tilde{c}
.

Of course, a more general and realistic model should require, as admissible, controls which are also space-dependent. In that case, the optimal behavior in general is not a rigid movement but involves a change masses’ shape. For example, suppose that a population of lions in the savannah is initially mixed with a population of antelopes. That is the mass of the antelopes,
m^{A}
, has a support that contains in its interior the support of the mass of the lions,
m^{L}
. Assuming the whole mass of lions equal to
1
, at the initial time it is

\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{A}m^{L}dx\geq a^{\prime}>0,

where
a^{\prime}>0
is such that
m^{A}\geq a^{\prime}
in the support of
m^{L}
. It is quite evident that a good behavior of the antelopes is to scatter as much as possible, with maximal velocity (which is
c>0
, the same of the lions by hypothesis) at the boundary of their support, obtaining then
m^{A}\leq a^{\prime\prime}<a^{\prime}
in the support of the lions and hence

\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{A}m^{L}dx\leq a^{\prime\prime}<a^{\prime},

showing that a change of shape of the mass
m^{A}
is in this case necessary for optimization. A possible control for the antelopes at time
t
, in order to perform as above, is, if at the time
t
the support of the antelopes is
[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

a(x,t)=\begin{cases}-c,&x\leq\xi_{1}\\
c,&x\geq\xi_{2}\\
\text{linearly interpolating between $-c$ and $c$ in $[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]$.}\end{cases}

Of course, a simple guess with your hands for the optimal behavior of lions and antelopes, as function of
m^{A},m^{L}
and their shapes, is, in general, almost unachievable.

5
Something on a possible parabolic case

The model described in the previous sections is in some sense “centralized” in the evolution of the masses. The fields
\beta
in (
1
) are not “constructed” by the single choices of the single agents of the masses. A natural question may be to weaken such a centralized feature. A first step could be to introduce a stochastic disturbance in the evolution of the single agents represented by the ordinary system (
5
). This is also a natural way to look at the movement of a huge number of agents/particles forming a mass. We then may assume that the evolution of the single agents of the masses are subject to a stochastic noise in their motion, that is, system (
5
) is replaced by

dY_{s}(s)=\beta(Y_{s},s)+\sqrt{2\sigma}B_{s},

where
B_{s}
is a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\sigma>0
. In this case, the expected equation for the motion of the mass is the so-called Fokker-Planck parabolic equation

m_{t}-\sigma\Delta m+\mbox{div}(\beta m)=0.

(31)

Whenever the field
\beta
is sufficiently regular (and the hypotheses on the admissible controls for our problem (see (
15
)-(
16
)), together with the fact that they can be assumed with compact support, are certainly in that direction), and the initial datum is also regular, the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation is expected to belong to
C^{0}([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(see
[
22
,
7
]
), and one also expects that, for a test function
\varphi\in C^{1}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, the composition
\psi:t\longmapsto\varphi(m(t))
(see Remark
2
) is differentiable and satisfies

\psi^{\prime}(t)=\langle D_{m}\varphi(m),\sigma\Delta m-\mbox{div}(\beta m)\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

or, for a test function
\varphi\in C^{1}(H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
,

\psi^{\prime}(t)=-\langle\nabla D_{m}\varphi(m),\sigma\nabla m\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}-\langle D_{m}\varphi(m),\mbox{div}(\beta m)\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},

where
\nabla D_{m}\varphi(m)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is the (spatial) gradient of
D_{m}\varphi(m)
when it can be identified with a function in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Hence, the expected Isaacs equations (here only the upper equations) are, respectively:

-\underline{V}_{t}+\inf_{b}\sup_{a}\left\{\langle D_{X}\underline{V},-\sigma\Delta m^{X}+{\rm div}(am^{X})\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\langle D_{Y}\underline{V},-\sigma\Delta m^{Y}+{\rm div}(bm^{Y})\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\right.\\
\left.-\ell(m^{X},m^{Y},t,a,b)\right\}=0

(32)

or

-\underline{V}_{t}+\inf_{b}\sup_{a}\left\{\langle\nabla D_{X}\underline{V},\sigma\nabla m^{X}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\langle D_{X}\underline{V},\mbox{div}(am^{X})\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\right.\\
\left.+\langle\nabla D_{Y}\underline{V},\sigma\nabla m^{Y}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\langle D_{Y}\underline{V},\operatorname{div}(bm^{Y})\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}-\ell(m^{X},m^{Y},t,a,b)\right\}=0.

(33)

Note that, in any case, such equations are infinite dimensional equations of first order because they involve only the (first) Fréchet differential with respect to
m^{X}
and
m^{Y}
.

A rigorous analysis, derivation and justification of such Isaacs equation will be the subject of future works. Moreover, an interesting vanishing-viscosity-type observation seems to reasonably hold in our case, and hence to be certainly worth studying. Let, for any
\sigma>0
,
u_{\sigma}
be a viscosity solution of, let us say, the second equation (
33
). Observe that equations (
32
)-(
33
) are expected to hold in a similar set as
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
(
20
), which is compact in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times[0,T]
. Since for
\sigma=0
the solution of (
31
) (i.e. the solution of (
1
)) has already the good regularity, one may expect that such a compact set
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
can be somehow taken independent on
\sigma
. Hence the following result may be expected but certainly worth studying.

Suppose that
u_{\sigma}
, for
\sigma\to 0
uniformly converges on
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
to a function
u
. Then
u
is a viscosity solution of (
22
). Indeed, let
P_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
be a point of local strict maximum for
u-\varphi
. Since
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
is compact, let
P_{\sigma}
be a point of maximum in
N\cap\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
for
u_{\sigma}-\varphi
, where
N
is the closed neighborhood of
P_{0}
in which it is a strict point of maximum. Then by uniform convergence and strict maximality,
P_{\sigma}\to P_{0}
as
\sigma\to 0
. In particular, for
\sigma
small enough,
P_{\sigma}
is also a point of local maximum for
u_{\sigma}-\varphi
. Since
u_{\sigma}
is subsolution of the corresponding equation, by the convergence and the equi-boundedness of the points
P_{\sigma},P_{0}
in the corresponding norm involved in
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
, we get that
u
is a subsolution of (
22
).

We point out again that such a “vanishing-viscosity” is not given by adding a second order term to the infinite dimensional Isaacs equation and letting it go to zero, but it is given by adding a second order term in the controlled evolution of the masses. These are still deterministic equations because they come from a stochastic perturbation of the first order ordinary equations which give the motion of the microscopic agents of the masses.
