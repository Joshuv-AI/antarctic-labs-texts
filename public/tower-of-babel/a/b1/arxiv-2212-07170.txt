Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature based on Gauss methods
Lehel Banjai, Matteo Ferrari

Abstract
An error analysis of Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature based on Gauss methods applied to hyperbolic operators is given. The order of convergence relies heavily on the parity of the number of stages, a more favourable situation arising for the odd cases than the even ones. Moreover, for particular situations the order of convergence is higher than for Radau IIA or Lobatto IIIC methods when using the same number of stages. We further investigate an application to transient acoustic scattering where, for certain scattering obstacles, the favourable situation occurs in the important case of the exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. Numerical experiments and comparisons show the performance of the method.



Keywords
Runge-Kutta Gauss methods, convolution quadrature, wave equation, Dirichlet-to-Neumann

1
Introduction

In this paper, we develop and analyze convolution quadrature based on Runge-Kutta Gauss methods used to compute integrals of the form

\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)g(\tau)\,\text{d}\tau,\quad t>0

(1)

for a given kernel
k
and a casual function
g
. The kernel
k
is not given directly, but instead via its Laplace transform
K
which is assumed to be of hyperbolic type, i.e.,
K
is assumed to be analytic and polynomially bounded in a region containing the half-space
\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma_{0}>0
. Convolutions of this type arise, for example, when solving partial differential equations of hyperbolic or parabolic type by boundary integral equations
[
8
,
5
]
. The convolution quadrature method was developed in
[
12
,
13
,
15
]
for parabolic and in
[
14
]
for hyperbolic problems. The underlying idea is to replace the convolution kernel
k
in (
1
) with the inverse Laplace transform of
K
, thus arriving at

\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)g(\tau)\,\text{d}\tau=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}K(s)y_{s}(t)\text{d}s,\quad t>0

(2)

where
\mathcal{C}
is an appropriate contour and
y_{s}
solves the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

y_{s}^{\prime}(t)=sy_{s}(t)+g(t),\qquad y_{s}(0)=0.

Convolution quadrature is obtained by replacing
y_{s}
with its approximation obtained by solving the ODE by an appropriate linear multistep or Runge-Kutta method.

For multistep-based convolution quadrature,
A
-stability of the underlying ODE-solver is necessary, therefore the achievable convergence order is limited by the Dahlquist barrier
p=2
. On the other hand,
A
-stable Runge-Kutta methods of arbitrary order are available and convolution quadratures based on them often outperform those based on linear multistep methods. In
[
3
,
4
]
error analysis of Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature applied to hyperbolic kernels is developed. However, in both works the analysis is limited to stiffly accurate
A
-stable Runge-Kutta methods, among which we mention Radau IIA and Lobatto IIIC methods. In this paper we extend the theory of convolution quadrature for hyperbolic symbols to Runge-Kutta methods based on Gauss-Legendre quadratures nodes. The analysis is mainly based on the location of zeros related of the Padé approximants of the exponential function; these rational polynomials are the stability functions of the Runge-Kutta Gauss methods.

The main motivation for extending the convolution quadrature theory to Gauss methods is their energy conservation property. Our main result concerns their convergence order and its dependence on the number of stages of the Gauss Runge-Kutta method, and on the growth exponent of the Laplace transform
K
. Moreover, we show that the order of convergence relies heavily on the parity of the stage order, a better situation arising for the odd cases than the even ones. On the other hand, a weaker regularity requirement for the datum
g
is needed for Gauss methods with even order of stages. For particular kernels, when using Gauss methods with odd number of stages, the order of convergence is higher than for Radau IIA methods with the same number of stages. This situation includes the important case of the exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann map and the inverse of the single-layer operator for the acoustic scattering problem with a convex scattering obstacle.

The paper is organized as follows: in the next section we define hyperbolic symbols, and recall some properties of the Runge-Kutta methods and associated convolution quadrature formulas.
In Section
3
we analyze the stability function of the Runge-Kutta Gauss methods, and we present some results on the zeros of associated complex polynomials.
In Section
4
we develop the error analysis, the main result of the paper is proved, and numerical tests are shown that support the results of the theory.
Finally, in Section
5
as an application of our main theorem, we also study Gauss Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature for a boundary integral equation formulation of a wave scattering problem, and we present two numerical tests which confirm the sharpness of the theoretical results.

2
Setting

2.1
Hyperbolic symbols

We denote the space of bounded linear operators between Banach spaces
X
and
Y
by
\mathcal{B}(X,Y)
and denote by
\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}s>0\}
the right complex half-plane. We assume that
K(s)
, the Laplace transform of a kernel
k(t)
, is a hyperbolic symbol, i.e., that it satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The function
K(s):\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathcal{B}(X,Y)
is analytic in the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma_{0}>0
, and bounded there as

\|K(s)\|_{\mathcal{B}(X,Y)}\leq M\absolutevalue{s}^{\mu}

for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and a constant
M>0
.

Remark 1
.

Often hyperbolic symbols satisfy a more refined bound of the form

\|K(s)\|_{\mathcal{B}(X,Y)}\leq M\frac{\absolutevalue{s}^{\mu}}{(\operatorname{Re}s)^{\nu}},\quad\text{for all~}\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma_{0}>0

for some real exponent
\nu\geq 0
. However, in contrast to the analysis in
[
4
]
, we are not able to make use of the resulting stronger bound in convex sectors of the form
|\operatorname{arg}(s)|<\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}-\theta
for
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
. This is due to Gauss methods not being
L
-stable, i.e., their stability region is exactly the right-half complex plane. An effect of this can be seen in the existence of poles of the discretized integrands in (
2
) localized in the vicinity of the imaginary axis.

If
\mu<-1
, the inverse Laplace transform
k=\mathcal{L}^{-1}K
is a causal (
k(t)=0
,
t<0
), continuous function and thus for integrable
g
, we can define the convolution

u(t)=K(\partial_{t})g(t)=\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)g(\tau)\differential\tau,\quad t>0.

For a general
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the convolution via

u(t)=K(\partial_{t})g(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{K(s)G(s)\}(t),\quad t>0,

(3)

where
G
is the Laplace transform of
g
. This is consistent with the above definition of the convolution in the case
\mu<-1
. Similarly, the case
\mu>-1
can be investigated by assuming that for
m>\mu+1
,
g\in C^{m-1}(\mathbb{R})
is a causal function satisfying
g^{(j)}(0)=0
,
j=0,\dots,m-1
, and
g^{(m)}
is locally integrable. Then,

K(\partial_{t})g(t)=\frac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\int_{0}^{t}k_{m}(t-\tau)g(\tau)\differential\tau,\quad t>0,

where
k_{m}
is the inverse Laplace transform of
K_{m}(s)=s^{-m}K(s)
.

The motivation behind the operational notation
K(\partial_{t})g
, can be seen when considering the case
K(s)=s^{m}
, where the above definition implies that
K(\partial_{t})g=\partial_{t}^{m}g
, where
\partial_{t}
denotes the causal derivative (see e.g.
[
5
]
). Furthermore, the composition rule
K_{2}K_{1}(\partial_{t})g=K_{2}(\partial_{t})K_{1}(\partial_{t})g
holds for hyperbolic kernels
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
. In the next section we describe convolution quadrature, a numerical method for approximating
K(\partial_{t})g
that conserves such properties of the operational convolution.

2.2
Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature

We consider an implicit Runge-Kutta method with
m
stages, and coefficients
\{b_{i},a_{ij},c_{i}\}
. The discretization with time step
h>0
of the initial value problem
y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,y(t)),\,y(0)=y_{0}
is given by the recurrence

\displaystyle Y_{ni}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+h\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{ij}f(t_{n}+c_{j}h,Y_{nj}),\quad i=1,\ldots,m\,,

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+h\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}f(t_{n}+c_{j}h,Y_{nj}),

where
t_{j}=jh
. The Runge-Kutta method has (classical) order
p
and stage order
q\leq p
, if for sufficiently smooth
f
,

y_{1}=y(h)+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1})\quad\text{~and~}\quad Y_{0j}=y(t_{0}+c_{j}h)+\mathcal{O}(h^{q+1}),

respectively (see
[
10
]
). We make use of the standard notation based on the Butcher tableau:

\displaystyle b^{T}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}),\qquad\qquad\quad c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})^{T},

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&\dots&a_{1m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{m1}&&a_{mm}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 2.84544pt\quad\mathbbm{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

A Runge-Kutta method is said to be
A-stable
if
I-zA
is non singular for
\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0
and the stability function

R(z)=1+zb^{T}(I-zA)^{-1}\mathbbm{1},

satisfies
\absolutevalue{R(z)}\leq 1
for
\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0
. If
A^{-1}
exists, we denote
R(\infty)=\lim_{\absolutevalue{z}\to\infty}R(z)=1-b^{T}A^{-1}\mathbbm{1}
. Important families of Runge-Kutta methods that are
A
-stable are Radau IIA, Lobatto IIIC and Gauss methods.

We describe a CQ discretization of (
3
) based on the above Runge-Kutta method, as first introduced in
[
15
]
for parabolic operators, and then analyzed for hyperbolic operators in
[
3
,
4
]
. We refer also to
[
7
]
for an application and analysis of Runge-Kutta CQ to homogeneous Volterra equations, which includes Gauss methods.

Considering a uniform time step
h
, at the time
t_{n}=hn
, the discretization is given by

K(\partial_{t}^{h})g(t_{n})=u_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\gamma_{n-j}U_{j}\qquad\text{~and~}\qquad U_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}W_{n-j}(K)g(t_{n}+ch),

where the coefficients are determined by generating functions

K\left(\frac{\Delta(\zeta)}{h}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}W_{j}(K)\zeta^{j},\quad\gamma(\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\zeta^{j},

and

\Delta(\zeta)=\left(\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\mathbbm{1}b^{T}+A\right)^{-1},\quad\gamma(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta b^{T}A^{-1}}{1-\zeta(1-\beta^{T}A^{-1}\mathbbm{1})}.

The spectrum of
\Delta(\zeta)
for
\absolutevalue{\zeta}<1
plays a key role in the analysis of Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature. We recall that in
[
4
, Lemma 2.6]
it has been proved that if
A
is invertible and the Runge-Kutta method is
A
-stable, then for
\absolutevalue{\zeta}<1

\sigma\left(\Delta(\zeta)\right)\subset\sigma(A^{-1})\cup\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R(z)\zeta=1\}.

(4)

We are able to prove a slightly improved version of this result by virtue of the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 1
.

An implicit Runge-Kutta method whose coefficient matrix
A
is invertible,
b_{j}\neq 0
for all
j
, and
c_{i_{1}}\neq c_{i_{2}}
for all
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
, satisfies

b^{T}x\neq 0\text{ for all eigenvectors~}x\text{ of~}A,

(5)

and

\mathbbm{1}^{T}y\neq 0\text{ for all eigenvectors~}y\text{ of }A^{T}.

(6)

Proof.

The proof relies on the following properties of implicit Runge-Kutta methods (see
[
6
]
): for all
k=1,\ldots,m
, where
m
is the number of stages, it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}c_{j}^{k-1}a_{ij}=\frac{b_{i}(1-c_{i}^{k})}{k},\qquad\text{for~}i=1,\ldots,m,

(7)

and

\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{k-1}a_{ij}=\frac{c_{i}^{k}}{k},\qquad\text{for~}i=1,\ldots,m.

(8)

Let us start by proving property (
5
). By contradiction suppose
b^{T}x=0
with
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
Ax=\lambda x
for some
\lambda\neq 0
.
Then, we obtain, using (
7
) with
k=1
,

0=\lambda b^{T}x=b^{T}Ax=(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*(\mathbbm{1}-c))^{T}x=b^{T}x-(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c)^{T}x=(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c)^{T}x,

where
.*
stands for the pointwise multiplication between vectors.
We iterate the procedure, using (
7
) with
k=2
,

0=\lambda(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c)^{T}x=(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c)^{T}Ax=\frac{(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*(\mathbbm{1}-c^{2}))^{T}}{2}x=\frac{b^{T}x}{2}-\frac{(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c^{2})^{T}x}{2}=\frac{(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c^{2})^{T}x}{2}.

Using the same mechanism we can show that
(b.\hskip-2.84544pt*c^{k})^{T}x=0
for
k=0,\ldots,m
. The first
m
equations are equivalent to the system

\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{m}\\
b_{1}c_{1}&b_{2}c_{2}&\cdots&b_{m}c_{m}\\
b_{1}c_{1}^{2}&b_{2}c_{2}^{2}&\cdots&b_{m}c_{m}^{2}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
b_{1}c_{1}^{m-1}&b_{2}c_{2}^{m-1}&\cdots&b_{m}c_{m}^{m-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\cdots\\
\cdots\\
x_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\cdots\\
\cdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}

of which the only solution is
x=0
, and this leads to a contradiction. Indeed, the matrix on the left hand side is essentially a Vandermonde matrix whose determinant is

\begin{vmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{m}\\
b_{1}c_{1}&b_{2}c_{2}&\cdots&b_{m}c_{m}\\
b_{1}c_{1}^{2}&b_{2}c_{2}^{2}&\cdots&b_{m}c_{m}^{2}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
b_{1}c_{1}^{m-1}&b_{2}c_{2}^{m-1}&\cdots&b_{m}c_{m}^{m-1}\end{vmatrix}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\prod_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq m}(c_{i_{1}}-c_{i_{2}})

and this is not zero since all the
b_{j}
are non zero and the
c_{i}
are different.

The proof of (
6
) is similar. We start by contradiction supposing that there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mathbbm{1}^{T}y=0
and
A^{T}y=\mu y
for
\mu\neq 0
. First, using (
8
) iteratively we can show that
(c^{k})^{T}y=0
for
k=0,\ldots,m
. The first
m
equations are again equivalent to a system whose matrix is now exactly a Vandermonde matrix associated to the vector
c
, whose determinant is not zero since all the
c_{i}
are different. This leads to a contradiction.
∎

We are able to state a new result on the characterization of
\sigma(\Delta(\zeta))
under the following hypothesis.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the Runge-Kutta coefficient matrix
A
is invertible with all its eigenvalues of multiplicity one.

We have numerically verified that all the Radau IIA, Lobatto IIIC and Gauss methods satisfy Assumption
2
up to
m=6
stages. It is worth investigating if Assumption
2
is always satisfied by these methods, but this goes beyond the scope of present paper.

Proposition 1
.

Assume an
A
-stable Runge-Kutta method satisfies Assumption
2
. Moreover, suppose that
b_{j}\neq 0
for all
j
, and
c_{i_{1}}\neq c_{i_{2}}
for all
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
.
Then, the following holds

\sigma(\Delta(\zeta))=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R(z)\zeta=1\}

(9)

for all
0<\absolutevalue{\zeta}<1
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
A^{-1}
. We want to show that
\det(\lambda I - \Delta(\zeta))\neq 0
, for all
0<\absolutevalue{\zeta}<1
. If the latter holds, then, by virtue of (
4
), we can deduce (
9
).
Using the Sherman-Morrison formula we can rewrite

\Delta(\zeta)=A^{-1}-\zeta\frac{A^{-1}\mathbbm{1}b^{T}A^{-1}}{1-\zeta R(\infty)},

and thus

\displaystyle\det(\lambda I - \Delta(\zeta))=\det( \lambda I - A^{-1} + \zeta\frac{A^{-1} \mathbbm{1} b^T A^{-1}}{1- \zeta R(\infty)})=\det( A \lambda- I + \zeta\frac{A^{-1} \mathbbm{1} b^T }{1- \zeta R(\infty)})\det\bigl(A^{-1}\bigr).

To evaluate the determinant, we recall
[
16
, Equation (9)]
: for all
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n},u\in\mathbb{C}^{n},v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
it holds that

\det\bigl(B+uv^{T}\bigr)=\det(B)+v^{T}\adj(B)u

(10)

where
\adj
stands for the adjugate matrix.
We apply (
10
) with
B=A\lambda-I
,
u=A^{-1}\mathbbm{1}
,
v=\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta R(\infty)}b
to obtain

\displaystyle\det(\lambda I - \Delta(\zeta))

\displaystyle=\det(A\lambda-I)+\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta R(\infty)}b^{T}\adj(A\lambda-I)A^{-1}\mathbbm{1}\det\bigl(A^{-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta R(\infty)}b^{T}\adj(A\lambda-I)A^{-1}\mathbbm{1}\det\bigl(A^{-1}\bigr),

where in the last step we used that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A^{-1}
. It remains to show that

b^{T}\text{adj}(A\lambda-I)A^{-1}\mathbbm{1}\neq 0\quad\text{~for all~}\lambda\text{~eigenvalue of~}A^{-1}.

(11)

By hypothesis all the eigenvalues of
A^{-1}
are simple, so for any eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A^{-1}
,
\operatorname{rank}(\adj(A\lambda-I))=1
. Therefore, for all
\lambda
there exists
\mu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\adj(A\lambda-I)=\mu xy^{T}
. Employing the well-known property
\adj(B)B=B\adj(B)=\det(B)I
for all
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
, we deduce that
x
is an eigenvector of
A
and
y
of
A^{T}
. Therefore, condition (
11
) is equivalent to

b^{T}x\neq 0\text{~and~}\mathbbm{1}^{T}y\neq 0\text{ for all~eigenvectors }x\text{ of~}A\text{~and all eigenvectors~}y\text{ of }A^{T},

but the latter is true by Lemma
1
.
∎

3
Gauss methods

The
m
-stage Runge-Kutta method based on Gauss-Legendre quadrature nodes has stage order
m
and classical order
2m
. Its stability function
R_{m}(z)
is the
(m,m)
-Padé approximant of the exponential
e^{z}
.
Namely, defining the monic polynomial

P_{m}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}z^{j}\quad\text{with}\quad p_{j}=\frac{(2m-j)!}{j!(m-j)!},

(12)

the stability function associated to the
m
-stage Gauss Runge-Kutta is

R_{m}(z)=\frac{P_{m}(z)}{P_{m}(-z)}.

By virtue of Proposition
1
, we are interested in investigating the solutions of equations of the type
R_{m}(z)\zeta=1
.

Solutions of
R_{m}(z)=e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}
for
t\in\mathbb{C}
and
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
, when
\absolutevalue{t}\to 0

Exploiting the expression of
R_{m}(z)
, we find that the
m
solutions
\{\hat{z}_{j}(t,\theta)\}_{j=1}^{m}
of

R_{m}(z)=e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}

(13)

are the zeros of the polynomial (in
z
)

P_{m}(z)-e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}P_{m}(-z)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}\left(1+(-1)^{j+1}e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)z^{j}.

Using the expansion
e^{t}=1+t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
, we readily obtain

P_{m}(z)-e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}P_{m}(-z)=M_{m}(\theta,z)-te^{\mathrm{i}\theta}P_{m}(-z)+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})P_{m}(z),

where

M_{m}(\theta,z)=P_{m}(z)-e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}P_{m}(-z).

(14)

We look for the solutions
\{\hat{z}_{j}(t,\theta)\}_{j=1}^{m}
in the vicinity of the zeros of the polynomial (in
z
)
M_{m}(\theta,z)
. A first, crucial, result is the following.

Proposition 2
.

For all
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
, all the zeros (in
z
) of
M_{m}(\theta,z)
are purely imaginary.

Proof.

We note that all the zeros of
P_{m}(z)
are in the open left half-plane (see e.g.
[
19
]
) and thus the polynomial
P_{m}(-z)
has all its zeros in the open right-half complex plane. Let us suppose by contradiction that
M_{m}(\theta,z)
has a zero
\hat{z}
with
\operatorname{Re}\hat{z}>0
. If we let
\{\hat{s}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\{-\hat{s}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be the zeros of
P_{m}(z)
and
P_{m}(-z)
, respectively, from (
14
) we deduce that

\absolutevalue{P_m(\hat z)}=\absolutevalue{P_m(-\hat z)}
, but this is impossibile since

\absolutevalue{P_m(\hat z)}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\hat{z}-\hat{s}_{j}|<\prod_{j=1}^{m}\absolutevalue{\hat z + \hat s_j}=\absolutevalue{P_m(-\hat z)}.

A similar contradiction is obtained when assuming
\operatorname{Re}\hat{z}<0
.
∎

Before characterizing the solutions of (
13
) we need the two following technical lemmas.

Lemma 2
.

Let
F(z)
be analytic in a vicinity of the left half-plane and let

|F(z)|\leq 1,\qquad\text{for }\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0.

Then,
F(\mathrm{i}y)=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
for some
\theta,y\in\mathbb{R}
implies
F^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)\neq 0
.

Proof.

We will proceed by contradiction. Suppose
F^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)=0
. As
F
is analytic in the vicinity of
z=\mathrm{i}y
we have that

F(z)=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+c(z-\mathrm{i}y)^{k}+\mathcal{O}\bigl((z-\mathrm{i}y)^{k+1}\bigr),

for some integer
k\geq 2
and some
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus

|F(z)|=|1+re^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}(z-\mathrm{i}y)^{k}|+\mathcal{O}((z-\mathrm{i}y)^{k+1}),

for some
r>0
,
\varphi\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Let us now choose
z
so that for some
\varepsilon>0

(z-\mathrm{i}y)^{k}=\varepsilon^{k}e^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi},

i.e., for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we can set

z=\mathrm{i}y+\varepsilon e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{(\varphi+2\pi j)}{k}}.

We want to choose
j
so that

\operatorname{Re}z=\varepsilon\cos\left(\frac{\varphi+2\pi j}{k}\right)\leq 0.

Since
k\geq 2
, note that we necessarily have that
\nicefrac{{|\varphi|}}{{k}}\leq\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
. Let us first assume that
\varphi\geq 0
and set
j=\lfloor\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}\rfloor
. If
k
even, then
j=\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}
and we have that

\pi\leq\frac{\varphi+2\pi j}{k}=\frac{\varphi}{k}+\pi\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi.

If
k
odd, then
j=\nicefrac{{(k-1)}}{{2}}
and
k\geq 3
and thus

\frac{2}{3}\pi\leq\frac{\left(\varphi+2\pi j\right)}{k}=\frac{\varphi}{k}+\pi\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)<\frac{3}{2}\pi.

Therefore in both cases
\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0
and

|F(z)|=1+r\varepsilon^{k}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\varepsilon^{k+1}\bigr),

implying that for small enough
\varepsilon>0
,
|F(z)|>1
, which gives us the contradiction we were looking for. For
\varphi\leq 0
, the same contradiction is obtained with the choice
j=-\lfloor\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}\rfloor
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
R_{m}(z)=\nicefrac{{P_{m}(z)}}{{P_{m}(-z)}}
be the
(m,m)
-Padé approximant to
e^{z}
and let
R_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
for some
\theta,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for
\absolutevalue{t}<r
with small enough
r>0
, there exists a unique
z(t)
in the vicinity of
\mathrm{i}y
such that
R_{m}(z(t))=e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}
. Furthermore

z(t)=\mathrm{i}y+\beta t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),

when
\absolutevalue{t}\to 0
, where

\beta=\frac{1}{1-\frac{y^{2m}}{|P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)|^{2}}}>1.

Proof.

By virtue of Lemma
2
it holds
R^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)\neq 0
, therefore, the implicit function theorem implies that

z(t)=\mathrm{i}y+\beta t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),

when
\absolutevalue{t}\to 0
, with

\beta=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}{R^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)}.

To prove the simplified expression for
\beta
we will use the following properties of the polynomials
P_{m}
:

2P_{m}^{\prime}(z)=P_{m}(z)-zP_{m-1}(z)

(see
[
9
, Lemma 7]
), and that the only term with an odd power of
z
in the product
P_{m}(-z)P_{m-1}(z)
is the term of highest power, i.e.,
(-1)^{m}z^{2m-1}
(see
[
9
, Lemma 1]
).

Using
R(\mathrm{i}y)=\nicefrac{{P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)}}{{P_{m}(-\mathrm{i}y)}}=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
we have that

\begin{split}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}R^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)&=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\left(\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)}{P_{m}(-\mathrm{i}y)}+\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(-\mathrm{i}y)P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)}{P_{m}(-\mathrm{i}y)^{2}}\right)=\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)}{P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)}+\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(-\mathrm{i}y)}{P_{m}(-\mathrm{i}y)}\\
&=2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)}{P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)}\right)=2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}y)P_{m}(-\mathrm{i}y)}{|P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)|^{2}}\right)\\
&=1-\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}yP_{m}(-\mathrm{i}y)P_{m-1}(\mathrm{i}y)}{|P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)|^{2}}\right)=1-\frac{y^{2m}}{|P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y)|^{2}}.\end{split}

Thus we have obtained the required expression for
\beta
, and proved that
\beta
is a real number. It remains to show
\beta>1
.

Let
\operatorname{Re}t>0
, implying
|R_{m}(z(t))|>1
and thus by A-stability
\operatorname{Re}z(t)>0
. Choosing
|t|
small enough implies that
\beta>0
and, due to the above expression for
\beta
, that in fact
\beta>1
.
∎

We are now able to characterize
\{\hat{z}_{j}(t,\theta)\}_{j=1}^{m}
with the following proposition, that precludes two extremal cases.

Proposition 3
.

The
m
solutions of the equation

R_{m}(z)=e^{t+\mathrm{i}\theta}

where
R_{m}(z)
is stability function of the
m
-stage Runge-Kutta Gauss method,
t\in\mathbb{C}
and
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
with the exception
\theta=0
if
m
is even, and
\theta=\pm\pi
if
m
is odd, satisfy

\hat{z}_{j}(t,\theta)=\mathrm{i}y_{j}(\theta)+\beta_{j}(\theta)t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})

when
\absolutevalue{t}\to 0
.
All the
\beta_{j}(\theta)
and
y_{j}(\theta)
are real numbers uniformly bounded in terms of
\theta
, when
\theta
is distant from
\{-\pi,0,\pi\}
. Moreover, it holds that
\beta_{j}(\theta)>1
.

Proof.

We recall that

M_{m}(\theta,z)=P_{m}(z)-e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}P_{m}(-z)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}p_{j}(1+(-1)^{j+1}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})z^{j}.

The degree of
M_{m}(\theta,z)
is exactly
m
if and only if
\theta\neq 0
for even
m
and
\theta\neq\pm\pi
for odd
m
. Since by hypothesis we are in that case, let denote by
\{\mathrm{i}y_{j}(\theta)\}_{j=1}^{m}
, the
m
zeros (in
z
) of
M_{m}(\theta,z)
. Note that
y_{j}(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}
thanks to Proposition
2
. Recalling standard estimates on the location of zeros of polynomials (see e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 27.2]
), we can bound

\max_{j=1,\ldots,m}\absolutevalue*{y_j(\theta)}\leq 1+\max_{j=1,\ldots,m}p_{j}\left|\frac{1+(-1)^{j+1}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}{1+(-1)^{m+1}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}\right|.

It is easy to see that, for each
0<d<\pi
, for
\theta\in[-\pi+d,-d]\cup[d,\pi-d]
, we obtain
\max_{j=1,\ldots,m}\absolutevalue{y_j(\theta)}\leq M(m,d)
, where
M(m,d)>0
does not depend on
\theta
. However, when
\theta
is near
0
if
m
is even, or
\theta
is near
\pm\pi
if
m
is odd, we can only bound, respectively

\max_{j=1,\ldots,m}\left|y_{j}(\theta)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\max_{j=1,\ldots,m}\left|y_{j}(\theta)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\pm\pi-\theta}\right).

Finally, thanks to Lemma
3
, the
m
solutions of
R_{m}(z)=e^{t+i\theta}
can be written as

\hat{z}_{j}(t,\theta)=\mathrm{i}y_{j}(\theta)+\beta_{j}(\theta)t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),\quad j=1,\ldots,m

where
\beta_{j}(\theta)>1
are the coefficients

\beta_{j}(\theta)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{y_{j}(\theta)^{2m}}{|P_{m}(\mathrm{i}y_{j}(\theta))|^{2}}}.

∎

Now we consider the other two cases.

Proposition 4
.

The solutions
\{\hat{z}_{j}(t,0)\}_{j=1}^{m}
of
R_{m}(z)=e^{t}
, for
t\in\mathbb{C}
when
\absolutevalue{t}\to 0
, can be characterized as

\hat{z}_{1}(t,0)=t+\mathcal{O}\bigl(t^{2m+1}\bigr),\quad\quad\begin{cases}\hat{z}_{2\ell}(t,0)=\mathrm{i}r_{\ell}+\delta_{\ell}t+\mathcal{O}\bigl(t^{2}\bigr),\\
\hat{z}_{2\ell+1}(t,0)=-\mathrm{i}r_{\ell}+\delta_{\ell}t+\mathcal{O}\bigl(t^{2}\bigr),\end{cases}

(15)

for
\ell=1,\ldots,\left\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\right\rceil-1
, where
r_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\delta_{\ell}>1
. If
m
is even, (
15
) gives the expression of
m-1
solutions, whereas the last one satisfies

\hat{z}_{m}(t,0)=\frac{D_{m}}{t}+\mathcal{O}(1)

with
D_{m}>0
, when
\absolutevalue{t}\to 0
.

Proof.

Recall that the solutions are the zeros of the polynomial

M_{m}(0,z)-tP_{m}(-z)+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})P_{m}(z),

(16)

where

M_{m}(0,z)=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil}p_{2\ell-1}z^{2\ell-1}.

Again we look for
\{\hat{z}_{j}(t,0)\}_{j=1}^{m}
in the vicinity of the roots of the polynomial
M_{m}(0,z)
of degree
2\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil-1
. If
m
is odd, then we find in this way all the solutions as in the previous case, if
m
is even we are missing exactly one of them. One solution is always of the form
\hat{z}_{1}(t,0)=t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+1})
since
R_{m}(t)
is the
(m,m)
-Padé approximant of
e^{t}
.
We write, for
\ell=1,\ldots,\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil-1
, the solutions in pairs of the form, since in
M_{m}(0,z)
appear only odd powers of
z
,

\begin{cases}\hat{z}_{2\ell}(t,0)&=\mathrm{i}r_{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(t),\\
\hat{z}_{2\ell+1}(t,0)&=-\mathrm{i}r_{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(t),\end{cases}

where
\{\pm ir_{\ell}\}
are the
2\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil-2
zeros of
M_{m}(0,z)
different from
0
. Moreover, we can characterize these
\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil-1
pairs as

\begin{cases}\hat{z}_{2\ell}(t,0)&=\mathrm{i}r_{\ell}+\delta_{\ell}t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),\\
\hat{z}_{2\ell+1}(t,0)&=-\mathrm{i}r_{\ell}+\delta_{\ell}t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}),\end{cases}

for the same positive constants
\delta_{\ell}
. Indeed, thanks to Lemma
3
, it holds that

\delta_{\ell}=\frac{P_{m}(-ir_{\ell})}{2\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil}(2j-1)p_{2j-1}(\mathrm{i}r_{\ell})^{2j-2}}>1.

Since
M_{m}(0,ir_{\ell})=M_{m}(0,-ir_{\ell})=0
, in
P_{m}(-ir_{\ell})
only even powers of
-ir_{\ell}
appears, and we conclude that
\delta_{\ell}
is the same shared constant both for
\hat{z}_{2\ell}(t,0)
and
\hat{z}_{2\ell+1}(t,0)
.
We introduce a last type of solution that only appears for even
m
. We notice that the product of the zeros of the polynomial (
16
) is fixed, and this is equal to
p_{0}
, defined in (
12
). From this, we deduce that

\hat{z}_{m}(t,0)=\frac{p_{0}}{t\prod_{\ell=1}^{\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}}r_{\ell}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(1)=\frac{D_{m}}{t}+\mathcal{O}(1)

with
D_{m}>0
.
∎

A similar result holds for
\theta=\pm\pi
, the proof of which we omit for simplicity.

Proposition 5
.

The solutions
\{\hat{z}_{j}(t,\pi)\}_{j=1}^{m}
of

,

. If

is odd, (

Lemma 4
.

For

and for

We have the general formula

R^{\prime}_{m}(z)=\frac{P_{m}^{\prime}(z)P_{m}(-z)+P_{m}^{\prime}(-z)P_{m}(z)}{P_{m}^{2}(-z)}.

(19)

We only prove the result for

∎

Lemma 5

The following holds

\begin{cases}R^{\prime}_{m}(\hat{z}_{m}(t,0))=\mathcal{O}(t^{2})&\text{~for even~}m,\\

Lemma 6
.

Let

1

\displaystyle b^{T}A^{-1}K\left(\frac{\Delta(e^{-sh})}{h}\right)e^{csh}\frac{e^{-sh}}{1-R_{m}(\infty)e^{-sh}}

\displaystyle=K(s)+s^{2m+\mu}\mathcal{O}(h^{2m})

\displaystyle+\begin{cases}s^{2-\mu}\mathcal{O}(h^{2-2\mu})&\text{~if~~}m=2,\\

Proof.

Proceeding as in
[

\displaystyle b^{T}A^{-1}

\displaystyle K\left(\frac{\Delta(e^{-sh})}{h}\right)e^{csh}\frac{e^{-sh}}{1-R_{m}(\infty)e^{-sh}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}K\left(\frac{z}{h}\right)b^{T}A^{-1}(zI-\Delta(e^{-sh}))^{-1}e^{csh}\frac{e^{-sh}}{1-R_{m}(\infty)e^{-sh}}\,\differential\Gamma_{z}

, which is composed by Proposition

for

So we have

\displaystyle b^{T}A^{-1}K\left(\frac{\Delta(e^{-sh})}{h}\right)e^{csh}\frac{e^{-sh}}{1-R_{m}(\infty)e^{-sh}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}K\left(\frac{z}{h}\right)b^{T}A^{-1}(zI-A^{-1})^{-1}e^{csh}\frac{e^{-sh}}{1-R_{m}(z)e^{-sh}}\,\differential\Gamma_{z},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}K\left(\frac{z}{h}\right)b^{T}A^{-1}(zI-A^{-1})^{-1}e^{csh}\frac{e^{-sh}}{1-R_{m}(z)e^{-sh}}\,\differential\Gamma_{z}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}K\left(\frac{\hat{z}_{j}(sh)}{h}\right)b^{T}A^{-1}(\hat{z}_{j}(sh)I-A^{-1})^{-1}e^{csh}\frac{1}{R_{q}^{\prime}(\hat{z}_{j}(sh))}.

Using the characterization of Proposition
4

\{\hat{z}_{j}(sh)\}_{j=1}^{m}

The first residual
\hat{z}_{1}(sh)

Exactly as in

The pair of residuals
\hat{z}_{2\ell}(sh)

\hat{z}_{2\ell+1}(sh)

\ell=1,\ldots,\lceil\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rceil-1

(\alpha-z)b^{T}A^{-1}(\alpha I-A^{-1})^{-1}e^{cz}=e^{z}-R(\alpha)+\alpha b^{T}(I-\alpha A)^{-1}\mathcal{O}(z^{m+1})

Remark 2
.

In some cases an even better result can be shown as we discuss in this remark, and we verify in an important example in the last section.

For the pairs of residuals

5
Application to acoustic wave propagation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

d=2
or
d=3
, a bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
.
For
\operatorname{Re}s>0
, the single- and double-layer potentials for the Helmholtz equation

-\Delta U(x)+s^{2}U(x)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma

(28)

are defined by

\displaystyle S(s)\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}K(x-y,s)\phi(y)\,\differential\Gamma_{y},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma

\displaystyle D(s)\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{n_{y}}K(x-y,s)\phi(y)\,\differential\Gamma_{y},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma

where
K
is the fundamental solution of the operator
-\Delta\cdot+s^{2}\cdot
. The latter is given by

K(x,s)=\begin{cases}\vskip 5.69046pt\frac{1}{4}K_{0}(s\|x\|)&d=2,\\
\frac{e^{-s\|x\|}}{4\pi\|x\|}&d=3\\
\end{cases}

where
K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of second kind of order 0. The single-layer potential is continuous across
\Gamma
, we denote its boundary trace by

V(s)\phi(x)=\int_{\Gamma}K(x-y,s)\phi(y)\,\differential\Gamma_{y},\quad x\in\Gamma.

We recall from
[
1
]
that
V(s)
is invertible and

\|V^{-1}(s)\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma))}\leq M\frac{\absolutevalue{s}^{2}}{\operatorname{Re}s}\quad\text{for~}\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma_{0}>0,

for a constant
M>0
.
We define the corresponding boundary operator for the double-layer potential as the average of the two traces on
\Gamma

K(s)\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma^{+}+\gamma^{-})D(s)\phi(x),\quad x\in\Gamma.

The exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
DtN^{+}(s):H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)\to H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)
is defined as
DtN^{+}(s)g=\partial_{n}^{+}U
where
U
solves (
28
) with the Dirichlet condition
\gamma^{+}U=g
on
\Gamma
. In
[
1
,
11
]
it was proved that

DtN^{+}(s)=V^{-1}(s)\left(-\frac{1}{2}+K(s)\right),

and that it holds

\|DtN^{+}(s)\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma),H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma))}\leq M\frac{\absolutevalue{s}^{2}}{\operatorname{Re}s}\quad\text{for~ }\,\operatorname{Re}\,s\geq\sigma_{0}>0.

However, as proved in
[
2
]
for the case
\Omega=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and argued for general convex domains, the following stronger estimate holds true in some situations

\|DtN^{+}(s)+s\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma),H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma))}\leq C\quad\text{for~}\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma_{0}>0,

(29)

for
C>0
.
In
[
18
]
, the same result has been proved for
s
in a sector of the form
\absolutevalue{\arg s}\leq\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}-c
for
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}})
, for all Lipschitz domains
\Omega
. In the same paper, the property

\|V^{-1}(s)-2s\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma),H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma))}\leq C

(30)

has been proved for
s
in a sector as before. It is still an open question whether (
29
) or (
30
) hold for all Lipschitz domains in a half-plane of the type
\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma>0
.

Assuming that we have a Lipschitz domain for which (
29
) or (
30
) holds in the whole right-half complex plane, then we are able to prove a stronger convergence theorem related to the
DtN^{+}
or
V^{-1}
operators, when the number of stages of the Gauss Runge-Kutta method is odd.

.

m

2
with odd

\operatorname{Re}s\geq\sigma_{0}>0

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed and analyzed a numerical approach for computing integrals of the form (
1
) by means of a convolution quadrature based on Gauss Runge-Kutta methods. The analysis performed is based on the localization of zeros of complex polynomials associated to Padé approximants of the exponential.
When the number of stages
m
is even, the convergence rates are worse than those obtained for stiffly accurate Runge-Kutta methods (such as Radau IIA and Lobatto IIIC).On the other hand, when
m
is odd, Theorem
1
guarantees the same convergence as in the case of CQ based on stiffly accurate Runge-Kutta methods (see
[
3
, Theorem 4.1]
) for hyperbolic kernels satisfying Assumption
1
. In the case of kernels satisfying the refined bound in Remark
1
, a better convergence rate holds for stiffly accurate Runge-Kutta methods (see
[
4
]
). In contrast, it does not seem to be possibile to use this more refined bound in the analysis for Gauss Runge-Kutta methods irrespective of the parity of
m
. However, in the odd case, we establish an improvement, even with respect to Radau IIA, for kernels satisfying condition (
24
). In particular, we show that in certain situations, including the exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, an extra order of convergence with respect to those based on Radau IIA can occur. This property, for the
DtN^{+}
map, heavily relies on inequality (
29
) that, at the moment, has been proved only when the scattering obstacle is a sphere or the whole right half plane.
