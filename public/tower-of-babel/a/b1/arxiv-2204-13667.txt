On weak convergence of quasi-infinitely divisible laws
A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We study a new class of so-called quasi-infinitely divisible laws, which is a wide natural extension of the well known class of infinitely divisible laws through the Lévy–Khinchine type representations. We are interested in criteria of weak convergence within this class. Under rather natural assumptions, we state assertions, which connect a weak convergence of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions with one special type of convergence of their Lévy–Khinchine spectral functions. The latter convergence is not equivalent to the weak convergence. So we complement known results by Lindner, Pan, and Sato (2018) in this field.



Keywords and phrases
: quasi-infinitely divisible laws, characteristic functions, the Lévy–Khinchine formula, weak convergence.

1
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the questions concerning weak convergence within a new class of so-called quasi-infinitely divisible probability laws.

Let
F
be a distribution function of a probability law on the real line
\mathbb{R}
with the characteristic function

\displaystyle f(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that
F
(and the corresponding law) is called
infinitely divisible
if for every positive integer
n
there exists a distribution function
F_{1/n}
such that
F=(F_{1/n})^{*n}
, where “
*
” is the convolution, i.e.
F
is
n
-fold convolution power of
F_{1/n}
. It is well known that
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if the characteristic function
f
is represented by
the Lévy–Khinchine formula
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-\tfrac{it}{\tau}\sin(\tau x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

with some
\tau>0
,
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and with a bounded non-decreasing
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is assumed to be right-continuous with condition
G(-\infty)=0
(throughout the paper,
G(\pm\infty)
denote the limits at
\pm\infty
correspondingly). We use
u\mapsto\tfrac{1}{\tau}\sin(\tau u)
as the “centering function” in the integral in (
1
) following to Zolotarev
[
32
]
and
[
33
]
. If formula (
1
) holds for some
\tau=\tau_{0}>0
, then it holds for any
\tau>0
, where
\gamma
will depend on
\tau
, but
G
will not. It is well known that
the spectral pair

(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
. The Lévy–Khinchine formula plays a fundamental role in probability theory, and it also has a lot of applications in related fields (see
[
3
]
and
[
30
]
).

It turns out that there exists a rather wide class of probability laws that are very similar to infinitely divisible laws. This class of so-called
quasi-infinitely divisible laws
was introduced by Lindner and Sato
[
23
]
. Following them, a distribution function
F
(and the corresponding law) is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
if its characteristic function
f
admits the representation (
1
) with some
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}
(not necessarily monotone), and for some (any)
\tau>0
. Here
G
is assumed to be right-continuous with condition
G(-\infty)=0
as before and so
f
(and
F
) uniquely determines
the spectral pair

(\gamma,G)
(see
[
13
]
p. 80). Observe that, due to the Jordan decomposition, we can represent
G(x)=G^{+}(x)-G^{-}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, with some bounded non-decreasing functions
G^{+}
and
G^{-}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Also we can always write
\gamma=\gamma^{+}-\gamma^{-}
with some numbers
\gamma^{+}
and
\gamma^{-}
from
\mathbb{R}
. Then it is clear that
f(t)=f^{+}(t)/f^{-}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
are characteristic functions of some two infinitely divisible distribution functions
F^{+}
and
F^{-}
with the spectral pairs
(\gamma^{+},G^{+})
and
(\gamma^{-},G^{-})
correspondingly, and so
F*F^{-}=F^{+}
. Starting from this point of view, it is rather natural to call distribution function
F
(and the corresponding law)
rationally infinitely divisible
. So every infinitely divisible law is quasi-infinitely divisible, but the converse is not true. There are a lot of interesting examples of quasi-infinitely divisible laws, which are not infinitely divisible (see
[
13
]
p. 82–83,
[
24
]
p. 165,
[
25
]
p. 123–124). Moreover, it seems that the class of quasi-infinitely divisible laws is essentially wider than the class of infinitely divisible ones. In particular, it is clearly seen within discrete probability laws (see
[
1
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
, and
[
22
]
).

Various forms of definition and the first detailed analysis of the class of quasi-infinitely divisible laws on
\mathbb{R}
was performed in
[
22
]
, the multivariate case is considered in the recent papers
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
, and
[
21
]
. There are some results for discrete probability laws in this field (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
, and
[
19
]
) and for mixed laws (see
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
). It should be noted that quasi-infinitely divisible laws now have interesting applications in theory of stochastic processes (see
[
23
]
and
[
28
]
), number theory (see
[
26
]
), physics (see
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
), and insurance mathematics (see
[
31
]
).

We now focus on a weak convergence of quasi-infinitely divisible laws. Recall that, by definition, the sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(where
\mathbb{N}
is the set of positive integers) of distribution functions
weakly converges
to a distribution function
F
(we will write
F_{n}\xrightarrow{w}F
,
n\to\infty
) if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dF_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dF(x),\quad n\to\infty,

(2)

for any bounded continuous function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. It is well known fact that this is equivalent to the following convergence:

\displaystyle F_{n}(x)\to F(x),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in S,

(3)

where
S
is an arbitrary dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
and, in particular, it can be choosen as the set of all continuity points of
F
. The latter convergence is usually called
weak
too (see
[
25
]
).

The weak convergence is also introduced for the class of real-valued functions of bounded variation on the real line (or for corresponding signed measures). Following Bogachev
[
10
]
, it is analogously defined by formula (
2
), but instead of
F_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
F
we write some functions of bounded variation
G_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
G
correspondingly. We will save the notation
G_{n}\xrightarrow{w}G
,
n\to\infty
, in this case. It should be noted that here weak convergence is not equivalent to the analog of convergence (
3
) with functions of bounded variation (see
[
10
]
Section 1.4).

There are rather general results by Lindner, Pan, and Sato in
[
22
]
concerning the weak convergence of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions. The authors state the conditions under which the weak convergence of distribution functions implies the weak convergence of the corresponding spectral functions together with the convergence of the shift parameters and vice versa. Namely, let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions and let
(\gamma_{n},G_{n})
be the spectral pair of
F_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
F
be a quasi-infinitely divisible distribution function with the spectral pair
(\gamma,G)
. Then the results from
[
22
]
are in fact the following: 1) if
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
and
G_{n}\xrightarrow{w}G
,
n\to\infty
, then
F_{n}\xrightarrow{w}F
,
n\to\infty
; 2) if we suppose
F_{n}\xrightarrow{w}F
,
n\to\infty
, then, under some assumptions on tightness and uniform boundedness for
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
and
G_{n}\xrightarrow{w}G
,
n\to\infty
. Here we omitted some details, the full formulation will be given in Section 3.

In this work we complement the results by Lindner, Pan, and Sato. We connect the weak convergence of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions with one type of convergence of their spectral functions. The latter convergence is a special modification of the convergence (
3
) (see the next section for details), and we think that it is more natural and explicit than the weak convergence for the class of functions of bounded variation. A very similar convergence appeared in
[
10
]
in Theorem 1.4.7., but we are not aware of existence of a definition for such convergence. So we introduce the necessary definition in Section 2. We also show that the introduced convergence for function of bounded variations follows from the pointwise convergence of their Fourier–Stieltjes transforms under some natural assumptions. This and other close propositions are key tools for our main results devoted the weak convergence of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions. The main results are presented in Section 3. All proofs are provided in Section 4.

2
Preliminaries and tools

Let us consider the class of all functions
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of bounded variations on
\mathbb{R}
. Since we will interesting in the functions
G
, which generate the the Lebesgue–Stieltjes signed measures
\mu_{G}
, we will focus only on right-continuous functions
G
. So for the measures we will have
\mu_{G}((a,b])=G(b)-G(a)
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\leqslant b
. Recall that all intervals
(a,b]
consist the generating semiring for
\mu_{G}
. So if there are two right-continuous functions
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
of bounded variations such that
G_{2}(x)=G_{1}(x)+C
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
C\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant, then the corresponding measures are the same. Therefore we will consider only functions
G
that satisfy
G(-\infty)=0
.

Let
\boldsymbol{V}
denote the class of all functions
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}
, which are right-continuous at every point
x\in\mathbb{R}
and satisfy
G(-\infty)=0
. For every
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
its total variation on
\mathbb{R}
will be denoted by
\|G\|
and the total variation on
(-\infty,x]
— by
|G|(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. So we have

\displaystyle|G(x)|\leqslant|G|(x)\leqslant\|G\|,\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(4)

and
|G|(+\infty)=\|G\|
.

We now introduce a special type of convergence on the class
\boldsymbol{V}
. Suppose that a whole sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a function
G
are from
\boldsymbol{V}
. We say that
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converges basically
to
G
, and write
G_{n}\Rightarrow G
,
n\to\infty
, if
every its subsequence contain a further subsequence
(G_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle G_{n_{k}}(x_{2})-G_{n_{k}}(x_{1})\to G(x_{2})-G(x_{1}),\quad k\to\infty,

for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
except at most a countable set, which in general depends on the choice of the subsequences.

Let us show that the basic convergence is equivalent to the weak convergence for distribution functions. Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of distribution functions and let
F
be a distribution function. Suppose that
F_{n}\xrightarrow{w}F
,
n\to\infty
. Then we have (
3
), where
S
is the set of all continuity points of
F
. Hence

\displaystyle F_{n}(x_{2})-F_{n}(x_{1})\to F(x_{2})-F(x_{1}),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\text{for all}\quad x_{1},x_{2}\in S.

Since
\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!S
is at most countable set, we conclude that
F_{n}\Rightarrow F
,
n\to\infty
, by definition. We now suppose that
F_{n}\Rightarrow F
,
n\to\infty
. Let
(F_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be arbitrary subsequence of
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle F_{n_{k}}(x_{2})-F_{n_{k}}(x_{1})\to F(x_{2})-F(x_{1}),\quad k\to\infty,

(5)

for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
except at most a countable set
D
. Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
r_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
\pm r_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!D
and
1-F(r_{\varepsilon})+F(-r_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
. We define
T_{k}(r)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1-F_{n_{k}}(r)+F_{n_{k}}(-r)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
. Due to (
5
), there exists
k_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
T_{k}(r_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
for all
k\geqslant k_{\varepsilon}
. Taking
r_{\varepsilon}
greater to provide
T_{k}(r_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
for all
k<k_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}T_{k}(r_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
because, due to monotonicity of every
F_{n_{k}}
, the inequality
T_{k}(r_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
still holds for all
k\geqslant k_{\varepsilon}
. Thus
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}T_{k}(r)\to 0
,
r\to\infty
, and, in particular,
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}F_{n_{k}}(-r)\to 0
,
r\to\infty
. Due to the latter, it is easy to check that (
5
) yields the convergence
F_{n_{k}}(x)\to F(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
except at most countable set
D
. Since
\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!D
is dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
, we have
F_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{w}F
,
k\to\infty
. Thus, according to definition of a basic convergence, every subsequence of
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contains a further subsequence
(F_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies (
5
) and hence weakly converges to
F
. By the well known fact (see
[
8
]
p. 337), it means that the whole sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to
F
.

The proved assertion can be generalized for bounded non-decreasing functions
F\in\boldsymbol{V}
and
F_{n}\in\boldsymbol{V}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but here the basic convergence
F_{n}\Rightarrow F
,
n\to\infty
, must be taken together with an additional condition that
F_{n}(+\infty)\to F(+\infty)
,
n\to\infty
(see
[
13
]
, p. 39). It should be noted that basic and weak convergences are not equivalent in a general case for functions from
\boldsymbol{V}
. Indeed, the weak convergence implies the basic one that will follow from Theorem
1
below, and also it is seen from Theorem 1.4.7. in
[
10
]
. However, the converse is not true. The latter assertion is concluded from the following simple examples.

Example 1.

Let us define
G_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathds{1}_{n}(x)-\mathds{1}_{n+1}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It is easily seen that
G_{n}\in\boldsymbol{V}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
G_{n}(x)\to 0
,
n\to\infty
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Setting
G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the basic convergence
G_{n}\Rightarrow G
,
n\to\infty
. Here
G_{n}(+\infty)=G(+\infty)=0
, and
\|G_{n}\|=2
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. However, for the continuous and bounded function
x\mapsto\cos(\pi x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cos(\pi x)\,dG_{n}(x)\nrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cos(\pi x)\,dG(x)=0
,
n\to\infty
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cos(\pi x)\,dG_{n}(x)=\cos(\pi n)-\cos(\pi(n+1))=(-1)^{n}-(-1)^{n+1}=2\cdot(-1)^{n}\nrightarrow 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Thus
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
doesn’t weakly converge to
G
.
\Box

Example 2.

Let
G_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}n\mathds{1}_{0}(x)-n\mathds{1}_{1/n^{2}}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So
G_{n}\in\boldsymbol{V}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
G_{n}(x)\to 0
,
n\to\infty
, for all
x\neq 0
. We set
G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and we obtain that
G_{n}\Rightarrow G
,
n\to\infty
. Observe that
\|G_{n}\|=2n\to\infty
,
n\to\infty
. Hence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can not be weak convergent sequence, because, under the weak convergence, total variations must be uniformly bounded (see Proposition 1.4.4. in
[
10
]
, p. 22). Moreover, it even fails to hold that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dG_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dG(x),\quad n\to\infty,

(6)

for any continuous function
h
with compact support. Indeed, let
h(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sqrt{x}
for
x\in[0,1]
,
h(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sqrt{2-x}
for
x\in[1,2]
, and
h(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
for
x\notin[0,2]
. Obviously, the function
h
satisfies the required properties. So we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dG_{n}(x)=h(0)\cdot n-h(1/n^{2})\cdot n=0-\sqrt{1/n^{2}}\cdot n=-1,\quad\text{for every}\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

but
\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dG(x)=0
. Thus (
6
) does not hold. However, it is interesting to note that there is a convergence of Fourier–Stieltjes transforms. Indeed, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG_{n}(x)=\bigl(1-e^{it/n^{2}}\bigr)\cdot n=-\dfrac{it}{n}\,(1+o(1))\to 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x),\quad n\to\infty.\quad\Box

The next example shows that the use of the sebsequences in the definition of basic convergence is essential.

Example 3.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we set
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
satisfying
2^{k_{n}}\leqslant n<2^{k_{n}+1}
. We define

\displaystyle G_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathds{1}_{a_{n}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{b_{n}}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\text{where}\quad a_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dfrac{n-2^{k_{n}}}{2^{k_{n}}}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dfrac{n+1-2^{k_{n}}}{2^{k_{n}}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

It is seen that the interval
[a_{n},b_{n}]
is vanishing (
b_{n}-a_{n}=2^{-k_{n}}\to 0
) and shifting over
[0,1]
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded continuous function. Due to the uniform continuity of
h
on
[0,1]
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,dG_{n}(x)=h(a_{n})-h(b_{n})\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

So
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to
G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, by the comments above,
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
basically converges to
G
that can be also checked directly by definition. However, for any
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\in[0,1)
there is no limit either for
G_{n}(x_{0})
or
G_{n}(x_{2})-G_{n}(x_{1})
as
n\to\infty
, because
G_{n}(x)
takes an infinite number of times each of the values
1
or
0
, when
x\in[a_{n},b_{n})
or not correspondingly. Note that, due to the weak convergence of
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, there is a convergence of Fourier–Stieltjes transforms:
\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x)=0
as
n\to\infty
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
\Box

The following example shows that it is important to use the differences of values of the functions at points
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
in the definition of the basic convergence in order to stay within
\boldsymbol{V}
.

Example 4.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define
G_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1+\tfrac{x}{n}
for
x\in[-n,n]
,
G_{n}(x)=0
for
x<-n
, and
G_{n}(x)=1
for
x>n
. So
G_{n}
are non-decreasing continuous functions, and
G_{n}(+\infty)=\|G_{n}\|=2
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We see that
G_{n}(x)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. However, an identical 1 doesn’t belong to
\boldsymbol{V}
(it must be
0
at
-\infty
). At the same time for any real
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
we have
G_{n}(x_{2})-G_{n}(x_{1})\to 0
,
n\to\infty
, and we conclude that
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
basically converges to the function
G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which is from
\boldsymbol{V}
. Of course,
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
doesn’t weakly converge to
G
here, because
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dG_{n}(x)=2\nrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dG(x)=0
,
n\to\infty
.

Note that for
t\neq 0

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG_{n}(x)=\dfrac{1}{n}\int_{-n}^{n}e^{itx}\,dx=\dfrac{e^{itn}-e^{-itn}}{itn}\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG_{n}(x)\biggr|_{t=0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dG_{n}(x)=G_{n}(+\infty)=2,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus the Fourier–Stieltjes transforms of
G_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, pointwisely converge to the Fourier–Stieltjes transform of
G
(i.e. to identical 0) for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
\Box

We now consider a general question about the relationship between the basic convergence of functions from
\boldsymbol{V}
and the convergence of their Fourier–Stieltjes transforms. We are not pretended to full studing of this question here, and we present only those assertions that will be used below in the main results of the article.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

We will use the following assumption:

, and

,

,

We are not aware of any results with such assertion. There are some close remarks in

[

We next formulate the analog of Theorem

with using of the decompositions

Proofs

Proof of Theorem
1
.
First, observe that the function
g
is measurable, because it is a almost everywhere limit of continuous (hence measurable) functions
g_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g_{n}(t)\rho(t)\,dt\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t)\rho(t)\,dt,\quad n\to\infty,

(16)

for any function
\rho\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, due to (
7
), there exists a constant
B_{0}>0
such that
|g_{n}(t)|\leqslant\|G_{n}\|\leqslant B_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and convergence (
16
) holds by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem.

Let us define the function

\displaystyle\varphi(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\rho(t)\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g_{n}(t)\rho(t)\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG_{n}(x)\biggr)\rho(t)\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\rho(t)\,dt\biggr)\,dG_{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{n}(x).

(17)

Let us consider the last integral. Due to (
4
) and (
7
), by Helly’s first theorem (see
[
27
]
, pp. 222 and 240), there exists a subsequence
(G_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a function of bounded variation
G_{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
G_{n_{k}}(x)\to G_{*}(x)
as
k\to\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that, in general,
G_{*}
may not be right-continuous (see Example 3). But
\varphi
is bounded and continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and hence there exists the Riemann–Stieltjes integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{*}(x)
. Also the (Lebesgue–Stieltjes) integrals
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{n}(x)
coincide with the corresponding Riemann–Stieltjes integrals. Next, due to the well known fact that
\varphi(x)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
, by Helly’s second theorem (see
[
27
]
, p. 240), we have the following convergence for the Riemann–Stieltjes integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{n_{k}}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{*}(x),\quad k\to\infty.

Let us define
G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}G_{*}(x+0)-G_{*}(-\infty)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
(note that
G_{*}(-\infty)\neq 0
in general, see Example 4). So
G
is right-continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and
G(-\infty)=0
, i.e.
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
. Since
G(x)
equals
G_{*}(x)-G_{*}(-\infty)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
except at most countable set, due to the continuity of
\varphi
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{*}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG(x),

where the integral in the right-hand side can be considered as Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral. Thus we have the following convergence with the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{n_{k}}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG(x),\quad k\to\infty.

The integral in the right-hand side admits the following representation analogously to (
17
):

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x)\biggr)\rho(t)\,dt.

Due to (
16
) and (
17
), we also have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\,dG_{n_{k}}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t)\rho(t)\,dt,\quad k\to\infty.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t)\rho(t)\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x)\biggr)\rho(t)\,dt

(18)

for any function
\rho\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. This implies that

\displaystyle g(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x)\quad\text{for almost every}\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(19)

Indeed, conversely, suppose that there exists a bounded set
E
of non-zero Lebesgue measure such that
\Delta(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x)\neq 0
,
t\in E
. Let us introduce the sets

\displaystyle E_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{t\in E:\mathrm{Re}\,\Delta(t)>0\},\quad E_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{t\in E:\mathrm{Re}\,\Delta(t)<0\},

\displaystyle E_{3}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{t\in E:\mathrm{Im}\,\Delta(t)>0\},\quad E_{4}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{t\in E:\mathrm{Im}\,\Delta(t)<0\}.

It easily seen that
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{3}\cup E_{4}
. Hence at least one
E_{j}
has non-zero Lebesgue measure. We denote any such set by
E_{*}
. Next, according to the property of strict positivity of integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{E_{*}}\Delta(t)\,dt\biggr|\geqslant\biggl|\int_{E_{*}}\mathrm{Re}\,\Delta(t)\,dt\biggr|=\int_{E_{*}}\bigl|\mathrm{Re}\,\Delta(t)\bigr|\,dt>0,\quad\text{for}\quad E_{*}=E_{1}\,\text{ or }\,E_{*}=E_{2},

and

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{E_{*}}\Delta(t)\,dt\biggr|\geqslant\biggl|\int_{E_{*}}\mathrm{Im}\,\Delta(t)\,dt\biggr|=\int_{E_{*}}\bigl|\mathrm{Im}\,\Delta(t)\bigr|\,dt>0,\quad\text{for}\quad E_{*}=E_{3}\,\text{ or }\,E_{*}=E_{4}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\Biggl|\int_{E_{*}}g(t)\,dt-\int_{E_{*}}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dG(x)\biggr)\,dt\Biggr|=\biggl|\int_{E_{*}}\Delta(t)\,dt\biggr|>0.

This contradicts (
18
) when we choose
\rho
as follows:
\rho(t)=1
,
t\in E_{*}
, and
\rho(t)=0
,
t\notin E_{*}
. It is valid since
\rho\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
due to the boundedness
E_{*}\subset E
. Thus (
19
) is true.

Let us show that (
19
) holds for every continuity point of the function
g
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of all
t\in\mathbb{R}
for which (
19
) holds. Hence the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}\setminus\!\mathcal{T}
equals zero. Let
g
be continuous at the fixed point
t_{0}
. So we can choose
t_{m}\in\mathcal{T}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t_{m}\to t_{0}
,
m\to\infty
. Then

g(t_{m})\to g(t_{0})
,
m\to\infty
, and at the same time

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\mathrm{Re}\,\varphi_{t,\tau}(x)\,\cos(sx)+\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi_{t,\tau}(x)\,\sin(sx)\bigr)\,dx

Proof of Theorem
5
.
Let

be a characteristic function of the limit distribution function
F
. By the continuity theorem, we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)\to f(t),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\text{for every}\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(29)

Moreover, it is well known (see
[
25
]
) that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|f_{n}(t)-f(t)|\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,\quad\text{for any}\quad T>0.

22
]

,

,

. We now show that

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. For any fixed

and

\displaystyle|f_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\cos(tx)-1\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{n}(x)\biggr\}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\exp\biggl\{-\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\cos(tx)-1\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{n}(x)\biggr|\biggr\}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\exp\biggl\{-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(1-\cos(tx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,d|G_{n}|(x)\biggr\}.

Let us estimate the inner function

\displaystyle{}-\tfrac{1}{2}\biggl(i2t\gamma_{n}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(e^{itx}\bigl(e^{-isx}+e^{isx}\bigr)-2-\tfrac{i2t}{\tau}\sin(\tau x)\Bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{n}(x)\biggr)

.

. So we have (

) (see comments in the proof of Theorem

Recall that
f_{n}(t)\neq 0
,

,

. Let us choose
\delta>0
such that
f(t)\neq 0
,

(it is possible because

is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and

(see
[

]
p. 79) with parameter

,

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}(f)=-\dfrac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\ln|f(s)|\,ds,\qquad\chi_{\delta}(f_{n})=-\dfrac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\ln|f_{n}(s)|\,ds,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

), we have

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}(f_{n})\to\chi_{\delta}(f),\quad n\to\infty.

(46)

Observe that

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}(f_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\cos(sx)-1\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{n}(x)\biggr)\,ds

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\bigl(1-\cos(sx)\bigr)\,ds\biggr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\sin(\delta x)}{\delta x}\Bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG_{n}(x),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

\displaystyle\bigl(\cos(sx)-1\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\Bigr|_{x=0}=-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2},\qquad\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\sin(\delta x)}{\delta x}\Bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\Bigr|_{x=0}=\tfrac{\delta^{2}}{3!},

(48)

according to known expansions

,

Proof of Theorem
7
.
Let
(F_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be arbitrary subsequence of
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which weakly converges to some distribution function
F_{*}
. Due to the assumption of relative compactness of
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such subsequence exists. By Theorem
5
,
F_{*}
is quasi-infinitely divisible with some spectral pair
(\gamma_{*},G_{*})
, where
\gamma_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
and
G_{*}\in\boldsymbol{V}
. Moreover,
\gamma_{n_{k}}\to\gamma_{*}
and
G_{n_{k}}\Rightarrow G_{*}
,
k\to\infty
. According to the assumption that
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
,
n\to\infty
, we conclude that
\gamma_{*}=\gamma
. Let us show that
G_{*}=G
. By definition, the convergence
G_{n_{k}}\Rightarrow G_{*}
,
k\to\infty
, implies an existence of a subsequence
(G_{n^{\prime}_{l}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
in
(G_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle G_{n^{\prime}_{l}}(x_{2})-G_{n^{\prime}_{l}}(x_{1})\to G_{*}(x_{2})-G_{*}(x_{1}),\quad l\to\infty,

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
except at most countable set
D^{\prime}
. Due to the assumption that
G_{n}\Rightarrow G
,
n\to\infty
, we can choose a further subsequence
(G_{n^{\prime\prime}_{l}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
in
(G_{n^{\prime}_{l}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle G_{n^{\prime\prime}_{l}}(x_{2})-G_{n^{\prime\prime}_{l}}(x_{1})\to G(x_{2})-G(x_{1}),\quad l\to\infty,

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
except at most countable set
D^{\prime\prime}
(and let
D^{\prime}\neq D^{\prime\prime}
in general). Therefore

\displaystyle G_{*}(x_{2})-G_{*}(x_{1})=G(x_{2})-G(x_{1})

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
except at most countable set
D^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime\prime}
. Letting
x_{1}\to-\infty
over
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\!(D^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime\prime})
we have
G_{*}(x_{2})=G(x_{2})
for every
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\!(D^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime\prime})
and, consequently, for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, because
G_{*},G\in\boldsymbol{V}
, i.e. they are right-continuous and
G_{*}(-\infty)=G(-\infty)=0
. Thus we proved that
\gamma_{*}=\gamma
and
G_{*}=G
.

The previous remark means that
(\gamma,G)
is the spectral pair for some quasi-infinitely divisible distribution function
F
. We also saw that every subsequence
(F_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, which weakly converges to some distribution function, converges exactly to
F
, because a spectral pair uniquely determines a distribution function. Therefore, since
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is relatively compact, we conclude that whole sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to
F
(this is known fact, see
[
8
]
p. 337).
\Box
