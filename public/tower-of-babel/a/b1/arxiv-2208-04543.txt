Taylor–Couette flow with temperature fluctuations: Time periodic solutions
Eduard Feireisl, Young–Sam Kwon

Abstract
We consider the motion of a viscous compressible and heat conducting fluid confined in the gap between two rotating cylinders (Taylor–Couette flow). The temperature of the cylinders is fixed but not necessarily constant. We show that the problem admits a time–periodic solution as soon as the ratio of the angular velocities of the two cylinders is a rational number.

Introduction

The Taylor–Couette flow is one of the iconic examples of turbulent fluid motion driven
by the adherence of a viscous fluid to the kinematic boundary. Specifically, consider two
not necessarily concentric cylinders

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}=B_{\underline{r}}({\bf a})\times[0,1]

\displaystyle,\ \mathcal{C}_{2}=B_{\overline{r}}({\bf b})\times[0,1],\

\displaystyle B_{\underline{r}}({\bf a})=\left\{x_{h}\equiv(x_{1},x_{2})\ \Big|\ |x_{h}-{\bf a}|\leq\underline{r}\right\}

\displaystyle,\ B_{\overline{r}}({\bf b})=\left\{x_{h}\equiv(x_{1},x_{2})\ \Big|\ |x_{h}-{\bf b}|\leq\overline{r}\right\},

\displaystyle 0<\underline{r}

\displaystyle,\ |{\bf a}-{\bf b}|+\underline{r}<\overline{r}.

For the sake of simplicity, we suppose the motion is periodic in the vertical direction
x_{3}
therefore the
fluid domain
\Omega\subset R^{3}
is given as

\Omega=\Big({\rm int}[\mathcal{C}_{2}]\setminus\mathcal{C}_{1}\Big)\times\mathbb{T}^{1},\ \mathbb{T}^{1}\equiv[0,1]\Big|_{\{0,1\}}.

(1.1)

The cylinders
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
rotate with constant angular velocities
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
respectively. Finally, we suppose the surface temperatures
\theta_{1}
of
\partial\mathcal{C}_{1}
,

\theta_{2}
of
\partial\mathcal{C}_{2}
are independent of time but not necessarily constant.

We consider a general viscous and heat conducting fluid. Accordingly, the fluid velocity
{\bf u}={\bf u}(t,x)

satisfies the no–slip boundary conditions

{\bf u}|_{\partial\Omega}={\bf u}_{B},\ \mbox{where}\ {\bf u}_{B}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\omega_{1}(x_{h}-{\bf a})^{\perp}\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{1},\\
\omega_{2}(x_{h}-{\bf b})^{\perp}\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{2}.\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

Similarly, we impose the Dirichlet boundary conditions for the fluid temperature
\vartheta=\vartheta(t,x)
. Although the temperature of the cylinders is independent of time, the fact that they rotate enforces the
time dependent

boundary conditions:

\vartheta|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B},\ \vartheta_{B}(t,x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\theta_{1}\left({\bf a}+\underline{r}[\cos(\omega_{1}t),\sin(\omega_{1}t)],x_{3}\right)\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{1},\\
\theta_{2}\left({\bf b}+\overline{r}[\cos(\omega_{2}t),\sin(\omega_{2}t)],x_{3}\right)\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{2}.\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

If the ratio

\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}=k-\ \mbox{a rational number}

(1.4)

the boundary temperature
\vartheta_{B}
is periodic in time,

\vartheta_{B}(t+T,x)=\vartheta_{B}(t,x)\ \mbox{for a certain}\ T\geq 0.

(1.5)

As pointed out in Yang et al
[
28
]
: “
Many natural and industrial turbulent flows are subjected to time-dependent boundary conditions.
”

1.1
Field equations

The time evolution of the fluid density
\varrho=\varrho(t,x)
, the (absolute) temperature
\vartheta=\vartheta(t,x)
and the velocity
{\bf u}={\bf u}(t,x)
is governed by the
Navier–Stokes–Fourier (NSF) system
of partial differential equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})+\nabla_{x}p(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})+\varrho\nabla_{x}G,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta))+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u})+\nabla_{x}{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}-p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u},

(1.8)

where
\mathbb{S}
is the viscous stress given by Newton’s rheological law

\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}+\nabla_{x}^{t}{\bf u}\right),\ \mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})=\mu(\vartheta)\left(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}+\nabla_{x}^{t}{\bf u}-\frac{2}{3}{\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}\mathbb{I}\right)+\eta(\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}\mathbb{I},

(1.9)

and
{\bf q}
is the heat flux given by
Fourier’s law

{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)=-\kappa(\vartheta)\nabla_{x}\vartheta.

(1.10)

The pressure
p=p(\varrho,\vartheta)
and the internal energy are interrelated by Gibbs’ equation

\vartheta Ds=De+pD\frac{1}{\varrho},

(1.11)

where
s
is a new thermodynamic function called entropy.

1.2

Hypotheses and main result

2.1
Hypotheses

Following
[
14
]
,
[
17
, Chapter 4, Section 4.3]
we impose the following hypotheses concerning
the equation of state:

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=p_{m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+p_{r}(\vartheta)+p_{HS}(\varrho),\ e(\varrho,\vartheta)=e_{M}(\varrho,\vartheta)+e_{r}(\varrho,\vartheta)+e_{HS}(\varrho),

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle p_{m}(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),\ p_{r}(\vartheta)=\frac{a}{3}\vartheta^{4},\ a>0,\ p_{HS}(\varrho)=\frac{b\varrho}{(\overline{\varrho}-\varrho)^{s}},\ b>0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle e_{m}(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\varrho}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),\ e_{r}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{a}{\varrho}\vartheta^{4},\ e_{HS}(\varrho)=\int_{\overline{\varrho}/2}^{\varrho}\frac{b}{z(\overline{\varrho}-z)^{s}}{\rm d}z,\ s>3,

(2.3)

where
P\in C^{1}[0,\infty)
satisfies

P(0)=0,\ P^{\prime}(Z)>0\ \mbox{for}\ Z\geq 0,\ 0<\frac{\frac{5}{3}P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z}\leq c\ \mbox{for}\ Z>0.

(2.4)

In particular, the function
Z\mapsto P(Z)/Z^{\frac{5}{3}}
is decreasing, and we suppose

\lim_{Z\to\infty}\frac{P(Z)}{Z^{\frac{5}{3}}}=p_{\infty}\geq 0.

(2.5)

The associated entropy

reads

s(\varrho,\vartheta)=s_{m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+s_{r}(\varrho,\vartheta),\ s_{m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),\ s_{r}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{4a}{3}\frac{\vartheta^{3}}{\varrho},

(2.6)

where

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(Z)=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\frac{5}{3}P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z^{2}}.

(2.7)

Finally, in accordance with the Third law of thermodynamics, we suppose

\lim_{\vartheta\to 0}s(\varrho,\vartheta)=0\ \mbox{for any fixed}\ \varrho>0,\ \mbox{meaning}\ \mathcal{S}(Z)\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ Z\to\infty.

(2.8)

The reader may consult
[
17
, Chapter 4]
for the physical background of the above hypotheses.

In addition,
the transport coefficients
\mu
,
\eta
, and
\kappa
are continuously differentiable functions of the temperature
\vartheta
satisfying

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\mu}\left(1+\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\vartheta)\leq\overline{\mu}\left(1+\vartheta\right),\ |\mu^{\prime}(\vartheta)|\leq c\ \mbox{for all}\ \vartheta\geq 0,\ \frac{1}{2}\leq\Lambda\leq 1,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\eta(\vartheta)\leq\overline{\eta}\left(1+\vartheta\right),

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\kappa}\left(1+\vartheta^{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(\vartheta)\leq\overline{\kappa}\left(1+\vartheta^{\beta}\right),\ \beta>6.

(2.9)

The pressure as well as the internal energy are augmented by the so–called hard–sphere component

p_{HS}
,

and force the density

Weak solution

(

We say that a trio

) - (

•

Integrability.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\varrho<\overline{\varrho}\ \mbox{a.a. in}\ S_{T}\times\Omega,\ \varrho\in C_{\rm weak}(S_{T};L^{q}(\Omega))\ \mbox{for any}\ 1\leq q<\infty;

\displaystyle 0

•

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)}{\vartheta}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(S_{T}\times\Omega)
,
\varphi\geq 0
.

•

Ballistic energy balance.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T}}\partial_{t}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}-{\bf u}_{B}|^{2}+\varrho e-\tilde{\vartheta}\varrho s\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t+\int_{S_{T}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\frac{\tilde{\vartheta}}{\vartheta}\left[\mathbb{S}:\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}-\frac{{\bf q}\cdot\nabla_{x}\vartheta}{\vartheta}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{S_{T}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u}+p(\varrho,\vartheta)\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})\right]:\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}_{B}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{S_{T}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}|{\bf u}_{B}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

for any
\psi\in C^{1}(S_{T})
,
\psi\geq 0
, and any

\tilde{\vartheta}\in C^{1}(S_{T}\times\overline{\Omega}),\ \tilde{\vartheta}>0,\ \tilde{\vartheta}|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}.

(2.10)

It remains to handle the convective term

Let

(4.23)

for any
\omega>0

To control the remaining two integrals in (

\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{1-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}.

\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{1,\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{4}{3}}(\partial\Omega)}.

\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{4}{3}}(\partial\Omega)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{4},2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{1,\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega)},\ 1\leq q\leq=\frac{12}{5}.

Thus we may infer that

\displaystyle\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,R^{3})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega;R^{3})}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})}^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.24)

where we have here used the trace theorem.

Going back to (
4.22
), we have to estimate the products

\int_{\Omega}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}||{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}|\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}.

It follows from (
4.24
)

\displaystyle\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}

\displaystyle\|{\bf w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega;R^{3})}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\omega\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega;R^{3})}+c(\omega)\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})},

where the first term on the right–hand side may be absorbed by the left–hand side of (
4.20
) if
\omega>0
is small enough. Finally, repeating the same argument,

\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})}\leq\omega\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}^{2}+c(\omega)\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega;R^{3})}^{2}

we conclude that also this term is controlled by the left–hand side of (
4.20
) if
\omega>0
is fixed
small enough and
\varepsilon\to 0
.

In view of the preceding argument, inequality (
4.20
) gives rise to the desired conclusion

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T}}\left(\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}+\|\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla_{x}\log(\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;R^{3})}^{2}\right)\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{|\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\vartheta_{B}|^{\beta+1}}{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}}\ {\rm d}\sigma_{x}\ \,{\rm d}t+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\partial\Omega}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\bf u}_{B}|^{2}\ {\rm d}\sigma_{x}\ \,{\rm d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}1

(4.25)

uniformly for
\varepsilon\to 0
.

4.4
Pressure estimates

The uniform bounds (
4.6
), (
4.25
) are strong enough to control all terms in the field equations with the only exception of the pressure and the associated integral energy that are singular for
\varrho\to\overline{\varrho}
.
The desired estimates can be derived following the arguments of
[
7
]
.

First, we introduce the so–called Bogovskii operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}:L^{q}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\left\{f\in L^{q}(\Omega)\ \Big|\ \int_{\Omega}f\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0\right\}\to W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega,R^{d}),\ 1<q<\infty,

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{x}\mathcal{B}[f]

\displaystyle=f.

The operator
\mathcal{B}
can be constructed by means of the original ansatz of Bogovskii
[
3
]

elaborated by Galdi
[
19
, Chapter 3]
, and later revisited by Geissert, Heck, and Hieber
[
20
]
.

\mathcal{B}
maps bounded sets of
L^{q}_{0}(\Omega)
into bounded sets of
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega;R^{d})
and
bounded sets in
(W^{1,q}(\Omega))^{*}\perp 1
to bounded sets of
L^{q}(\Omega;R^{d})
provided
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain.

4.4.1
Integrability of the pressure

We consider the quantity

\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\mathcal{B}\left[\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]

as a test function in the approximate momentum balance (
4.3
). After a straightforward manipulation, we get

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\left[\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathcal{B}[{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})]-\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\otimes{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}:\nabla_{x}\mathcal{B}\left[\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta_{\varepsilon},\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}):\mathbb{D}_{x}\mathcal{B}\left[\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}G\cdot\mathcal{B}\left[\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

(4.26)

As
\varrho_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded, it is easy to check that the right–hand side of (
4.26
) is bounded
by means of the uniform estimates established in (
4.25
). Since the total mass is constant, we get

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}<\overline{\varrho}.

In particular, boundedness of the integral on the right–hand side of (
4.26
) yields a uniform bound

\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}1

(4.27)

uniformly for
\varepsilon\to 0
. Moreover, as observed in
[
7
, Section 2.1, formula (2.4)]
,
uniform integrability of the hard–sphere pressure yields equi–integrability of the associated internal energy.
Specifically,

(\varrho_{\varepsilon}e(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon>0}\ \mbox{is}\ L^{1}-\mbox{equi--integrable.}

(4.28)

Finally, using (
4.28
), we may deduce from the ballistic energy inequality (
4.10
), exactly as for the
initial–value problem,

(\varrho_{\varepsilon}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2})_{\varepsilon>0},\ (\varrho_{\varepsilon}e(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon>0}\ \mbox{bounded in}\ L^{\infty}(S_{T};L^{1}(\Omega)).

(4.29)

4.4.2
Equi–integrability of the pressure

The ultimate goal of this section is to establish
L^{1}
–integrability of the pressure. To this end, we repeat the
above procedure with the test function

\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\mathcal{B}\left[b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]

in (
4.3
), where
b
is a function compatible with the renormalized equation of continuity (
4.2
).
After a straightforward manipulation, we get

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\left[b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\otimes{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}:\nabla_{x}\mathcal{B}\left[b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta_{\varepsilon},\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}):\mathbb{D}_{x}\mathcal{B}\left[b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\nabla_{x}G\cdot\mathcal{B}\left[b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathcal{B}\left[(b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-b^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}(b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-b^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathcal{B}[{\rm div}_{x}(b(\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\bf u}_{\varepsilon})]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t.

(4.30)

The main idea is to consider
b(\varrho)=p_{HS}^{\nu}(\varrho)
, where
\nu>0
is sufficiently small. In view of (
4.27
),
p_{HS}^{\nu}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})
is uniformly bounded in the Lebesgue space
L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, where
q>1
can be arbitrarily large provided
\nu>0
is small. It would follow that

p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})^{1+\nu}\ \mbox{is bounded in}\ L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)

provided we can show that all integrals on the right–hand side of (
4.30
) are bounded uniformly for
\varepsilon\to 0
.

There are certain technical difficulties to carry out the above programme. To begin,
p_{HS}
is singular therefore
not directly eligible for the renormalized equation (
4.2
). Fortunately, we may consider
b
any
C^{1}
truncation of
p_{HS}
as
\varrho_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded and behaviour of
b
for large argument is irrelevant.
In particular, we may consider

b(\varrho_{\varepsilon})=(p^{N}_{HS})^{\nu}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}),

with
P^{N}_{HS}
given by (
3
). The desired conclusion then follows as soon as we are able to show that
the right–hand side of (
4.30
) remains bounded uniformly for
N\to\infty
,
\varepsilon\to 0
. Given the
properties of the operator
\mathcal{B}
, the only problematic term is the integral

\displaystyle\int_{S_{T}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathcal{B}\Big[((p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}((p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\Big]\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t.

(4.31)

Indeed this integral contains the derivative
[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}
that is more singular than

(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}
in the neighbourhood of
\overline{\varrho}
when
N\to\infty
.

On the one hand,

[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho)\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\overline{\varrho}-\varrho)^{-(\nu s+1)}\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho\leq\overline{\varrho}-\frac{1}{N}\\
\\
N^{\nu s+1},\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho>\overline{\varrho}-\frac{1}{N},\end{array}\right.

(4.32)

On the other hand, in accordance with (
4.29
),

(\varrho_{\varepsilon}e(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon>0}\ \mbox{bounded in}\ L^{\infty}(S_{T};L^{1}(\Omega)).

Thus, by means of hypothesis (
2.3
),

\left((\overline{\varrho}-\varrho)^{1-s}\right)_{\varepsilon>0}\ \mbox{bounded in}\ L^{\infty}(S_{T};L^{1}(\Omega)).

(4.33)

Comparing (
4.32
) with (
4.33
) we conclude

[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon})\ \mbox{bounded in}\ L^{\infty}(S_{T};L^{q}(\Omega))\ \mbox{provided}\ s\geq q\nu s+q+1.

(4.34)

If
s>3
, we can find
\nu
small enough so that
q>2
in (
4.34
). Consequently,

([(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}\ \mbox{is bounded in}\ L^{2}(S_{T};L^{\beta}(\Omega))\ \mbox{for some}\ \beta>1;

whence

\mathcal{B}\Big[((p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}((p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})-[(p_{HS}^{N})^{\nu}]^{\prime}(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\varrho_{\varepsilon}){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\Big]

is bounded in
L^{2}(S_{T};L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega;R^{3}))
. In view of the uniform bounds (
4.25
),
we conclude that the integral (
4.31
) remains bounded uniformly for
N\to\infty
,
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Thus we have obtained the desired conclusion

\int_{S_{T}}\int_{\Omega}p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})^{1+\nu}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \,{\rm d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}1\ \mbox{for some}\ \nu>0.

(4.35)

4.5
Conclusion, limit
\varepsilon\to 0

In the previous section, we have obtained all available uniform bounds, specifically, the uniform density estimates (
4.6
), the “dissipative estimates” (
4.25
), and the pressure estimates (
4.35
).
Now it is standard, extracting suitable subsequences as the case may be, to identify the limits

\displaystyle\varrho_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\ \mbox{in}\ C_{\rm weak}(S_{T};L^{q}(\Omega))\ \mbox{for any finite}\ q,

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to{\bf u}\ \mbox{weakly in}\ L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega;R^{3}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to\vartheta\ \mbox{weakly in}\ L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega).

(4.36)

Moreover, it follows from (
4.25
) that the limit velocity and temperature satisfy the desired
boundary conditions

{\bf u}|_{\partial\Omega}={\bf u}_{B},\ \vartheta|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}.

(4.37)

Finally, it is a routine matter to perform the limit in the approximate equation of continuity
(
4.1
), (
4.2
), the momentum equation (
4.3
), the entropy inequality (
4.4
), and the
ballistic energy balance (
4.10
) as long as we can show strong (pointwise a.a.) convergence

\varrho_{\varepsilon}\to\varrho,\ \vartheta_{\varepsilon}\to\vartheta\ \mbox{a.a. in}\ (0,T)\times\Omega.

(4.38)

This is a non–trivial task, however nowadays well understood. In particular, the compactness arguments based on Div-curl Lemma and Lions’ identity can be modified to accommodate the time periodic
setting exactly as in
[
15
, Section 9.3]
.

We have proved Theorem
2.2
.

5
Concluding remarks

As already pointed out, the above result can be easily extended to the case of time periodic boundary velocity as well as time periodic potential
G
. A more delicate issue would be considering general inflow/outflow boundary
conditions in the spirit of
[
1
]
. The present approximation does not apply as the total mass is generally not conserved. Adopting the indirect method of
[
1
]
based applying a fixed point argument to the associated Poincaré map would result in essential technical difficulties due to the presence of the
internal energy equation.

The possibility of eliminating the hard–sphere pressure component remains largely open as long as the boundary
velocity is non–zero. The main and possibly the only unsurmountable problem is controlling the convective term
as in Section
4.3.2
. Note that the same difficulty arises at the level of stationary solutions, cf.

[
11
]
.
