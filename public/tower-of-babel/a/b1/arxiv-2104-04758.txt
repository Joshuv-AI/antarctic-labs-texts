Splitting Spanner Atoms: A Tool for Acyclic Core Spanners
Dominik D. Freydenberger, Sam M. Thompson

Abstract
This paper investigates regex 𝖢𝖰 {\mathsf{CQ}} s with string equalities ( 𝖲𝖤𝖱𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{SERCQ} s), a subclass of core spanners. As shown by Freydenberger, Kimelfeld, and Peterfreund (PODS 2018), these queries are intractable, even if restricted to acyclic queries. This previous result defines acyclicity by treating regex formulas as atoms. In contrast to this, we propose an alternative definition by converting 𝖲𝖤𝖱𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{SERCQ} s into 𝖥𝖢 − 𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ} s – conjunctive queries in 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} , a logic that is based on word equations. We introduce a way to decompose word equations of unbounded arity into a conjunction of binary word equations. If the result of the decomposition is acyclic, then evaluation and enumeration of results become tractable. The main result of this work is an algorithm that decides in polynomial time whether an 𝖥𝖢 − 𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ} can be decomposed into an acyclic 𝖥𝖢 − 𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ} . We also give an efficient conversion from synchronized 𝖲𝖤𝖱𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{SERCQ} s to 𝖥𝖢 − 𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ} s with regular constraints. As a consequence, tractability results for acyclic relational 𝖢𝖰 {\mathsf{CQ}} s directly translate to a large class of 𝖲𝖤𝖱𝖢𝖰 \mathsf{SERCQ} s.

Introduction

Document spanners were introduced by Fagin, Kimelfeld, Reiss, and Vansummeren
[
7
]
as a formalization of AQL, an information extraction query language used in IBM’s SystemT.
Informally, they can be described in two steps.
First, so-called
extractors
convert an input document, a word over a finite alphabet, into relations of so-called
spans
.
We assume the extractors to be
regex formulas
(as described in
[
7
]
), which are regular expressions with capture variables.
Consider the following example of a regex formula

\gamma(x):=\Sigma^{*}\cdot x\{(\mathtt{EBDT})\lor(\mathtt{ICDT})\}\cdot\Sigma^{*}.

Given some input word,
\gamma(x)
can be used to extract a unary relation of spans such that each span represents a factor of the input word that is either “
\mathtt{EBDT}
” or “
\mathtt{ICDT}
”.

The second step is that the extracted relations are combined using a relational algebra. Classes of spanners can be defined by the choice of relational operators.

Regular spanners
allow for union
\mathrel{\cup}
, projection
\pi
, and natural join
\bowtie
.
Depending on how they are represented, regular spanners have been shown to be efficient.
For example, if a regular spanner is given as a so-called vset-automaton, results can be enumerated with constant delay after linear time preprocessing
[
9
,
2
]
.
However, if a regular spanner is given as a join of regex formulas, evaluation is intractable –
as shown in
[
13
]
, evaluation for spanners of the form
P:=\pi_{\emptyset}(\gamma_{1}\bowtie\gamma_{2}\cdots\bowtie\gamma_{n})
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete,
even if
P
is acyclic.

Core spanners
extend regular spanners by allowing equality selection
\zeta^{=}
, which checks whether two (potentially different) spans represent the same factor of the input document.
Even when core spanners are restricted to queries of the form
\pi_{\emptyset}\zeta^{=}_{x_{1},y_{1}}\cdots\zeta^{=}_{x_{m},y_{m}}\gamma

for a single regex formula
\gamma
, the evaluation problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
11
]
. Therefore, both joins and equalities introduce computational hardness.

Regex
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s can be understood as the spanner version of relational
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s, which are a central topic in database theory.
In each case, a conjunctive query is a projection over a join of atoms.
Apart from the setting, the key difference is that while the tables for relational
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s are usually part of the input,
the tables for regex
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s are defined implicitly through the regex formulas.
Hence, while one could extract these tables and then perform a standard
CQ
over the extractions, the number of tuples in the materialized relations may be exponential.
As a consequence, tractable restrictions on relational queries (such as
acyclic
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s
) do not lead to tractable fragments of regex
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s
[
13
]
.

So-called
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s extend regex
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s by also allowing string equality, thus allowing us to examine both previously discussed sources of intractability.
Consider the following
\mathsf{SERCQ}

P:=\pi_{x,y}\;\zeta^{=}_{x,x^{\prime}}\left(\gamma_{\mathsf{sen}}(z)\bowtie\gamma_{\mathsf{prod}}(x)\bowtie\gamma_{\mathsf{pos}}(y)\bowtie\gamma_{\mathsf{factors}}(x,x^{\prime},z)\bowtie\gamma_{\mathsf{factor}}(y,z)\right),

where we assume
\gamma_{\mathsf{sen}}
extracts sentences,

\gamma_{\mathsf{prod}}
extracts product names,
\gamma_{\mathsf{pos}}
extracts positive sentiments (such as “enjoyed”), and
\gamma_{\mathsf{factors}}(x,x^{\prime},z)
and
\gamma_{\mathsf{factor}}(y,z)
ensure that
x
and
x^{\prime}
are successive (but not necessarily consecutive) factors of
z
, and
y
is a factor of
z
respectively.
Therefore,
P
extracts spans representing products that are mentioned twice within a sentence, along with a positive sentiment that appears in the same sentence.

Syntactic restrictions on conjunctive queries have been incredibly fruitful for finding tractable fragments. A well known result of Yannakakis
[
25
]
is that for
acyclic
conjunctive queries, evaluation can be solved in polynomial time. Further research on the complexity of acyclic conjunctive queries
[
16
]
and the enumeration of results for acyclic conjunctive queries
[
3
]
has shown the efficacy of this restriction. On the other hand, for document spanners, such syntactic restrictions are yet to unlock tractable fragments.

To address this gap, we consider a different approach and represent
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s as a conjunctive query fragment of the logic
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
, introduced by Freydenberger and Peterfreund
[
14
]
.
This logic is based on word equations, regular constraints, and first-order logic connectives.
Consider the following
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
conjunctive query

\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}(x,y)\leftarrow(z\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{2}\cdot x\cdot z_{3}\cdot x\cdot z_{4})\land(z\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{5}\cdot y\cdot z_{6})\land(z\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma_{\mathsf{sen}})\land(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma_{\mathsf{prod}})\land(y\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma_{\mathsf{pos}}).

If
\gamma_{\mathsf{sen}}
is a regular expression that accepts sentences,
\gamma_{\mathsf{prod}}
accepts a product name, and
\gamma_{\mathsf{pos}}
accepts a positive sentiment, then
\varphi
is “equivalent” to the previously given
\mathsf{SERCQ}
.
They are not equivalent in a strict sense – a key difference being that
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s reason over spans, whereas
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s reason over factors of the input words.
Reasoning over words does bring some advantages:
For example,
\varphi
simply uses relations of words (for example,
\gamma_{\mathsf{prod}}
) encoded as a regular expression, and if we wanted to do something analogous for regex-formulas, we would first have to extract the corresponding relation of spans.

When dealing with word equations, we run into an issue that we already encountered for regex formulas: Their relations may contain an exponential number of tuples.
This is due to the unbounded arity of word equations.
However, an
\mathsf{FC}
atom can be considered shorthand for a concatenation term.
For example, the word equation
y\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}
can be represented as
y\mathbin{\dot{=}}f(f(x_{1},x_{2}),f(x_{3},x_{4}))
where
f
denotes binary concatenation.
This then lends itself to the “decomposition” of the word equation into a
{\mathsf{CQ}}
consisting of smaller word equations.
We can express the above word equation as
(y\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{1}\cdot z_{2})\land(z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{1}\cdot x_{2})\land(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{3}\cdot x_{4})
.
For such a
decomposition
, the relations defined by each word equation can be stored in linear space and we can enumerate them with constant delay.
Thus, if the resulting query is acyclic, then the tractability properties of acyclic conjunctive queries directly translate to the
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.

Contributions of this paper

The goal of this work is to bridge the gap between acyclic relational
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s and information extraction.
To this end, we define
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s, a conjunctive query fragment of
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
, and show show that any so-called synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}
can be converted into an equivalent
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
in polynomial time (
\cref
Prop:RGXtoPatCQ).

We define the decomposition of an
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
into a
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, where
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
denotes the set of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s where the right-hand side of each word equation is of at most length two.
Our first main result is a polynomial-time algorithm that decides whether a
pattern
1

1

For the purposes of this introduction, a pattern can be considered a single
\mathsf{FC}
atom.
can be decomposed into an acyclic
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
(
\cref
polytime).

Building on this, we give a polynomial-time algorithm that decomposes an
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
into an acyclic
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, or determines that this is not possible (
\cref
theorem:LVJoinTree).
As soon as we have an acyclic
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, the upper bound results for model checking and enumeration of results follow from previous work on relational acyclic
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s
[
16
,
3
]
.

We mainly focus on
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s (i. e., no regular constraints) due to the fact that we can add regular constraints for “free”.
This is because regular constraints are unary predicates, and therefore can be easily incorporated into a join tree.
Thus, our work defines a class of
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s for which model checking can be solved in polynomial time, and results can be enumerated with polynomial-delay (both in terms of combined complexity).

Our approach offers a new research direction for tractable document spanners.
Most of the current literature approaches regular spanners by “compiling” the spanner representation (regex formulas that are combined with projection, union, and joins) into a single automaton, where the use of joins can lead to a number of states that is exponential in the size of the original representation.
Instead, we look at decomposing
\mathsf{FC}
conjunctive queries into small and tractable components.
This allows us to use the wealth of research on relational algebra, while also allowing for the use of the string equality selection operator.

Related Work

Regarding data complexity, Florenzano, Riveros, Vgarte, Vansummeren, and Vrgoc
[
9
]
gave a constant-delay algorithm for enumerating the results of deterministic vset-automata, after linear time preprocessing.
Amarilli, Bourhis, Mengal, and Niewerth
[
2
]
extended this result to non-deterministic vset-automata.
Regarding combined complexity,
Freydenberger, Kimelfeld, and Peterfreund
[
13
]
introduced regex
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s and proved that their evaluation is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete (even for acyclic queries), and that fixing the number of atoms and the number of string equalities in
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s allows for polynomial-delay enumeration of results.
Freydenberger, Peterfreund, Kimelfeld, and Kröll
[
12
]
showed that non-emptiness for a join of two sequential regex formulas is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, under schemaless semantics, even for a single character document.
Connections between the theory of concatenation and spanners have been considered in
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
, which give many of the lower bound complexity results for core spanners. Schmid and Schweikardt
[
24
]
examined a subclass of core spanners called refl-spanners, which incorporate string equality directly into a regular spanner.
Peterfreund
[
22
]
considered extraction grammars, and gave an algorithm for unambiguous extraction grammars that enumerates results with constant-delay after quintic preprocessing.

Let

denote the
empty set
, and for
n\geq 1
let
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
Given a set
S
, we use
|S|
for the
cardinality
of
S
.
If
S
is a subset of
T
then we write
S\subseteq T
and if
S\neq T
also holds, then
S\subset T
.
We write
\mathcal{P}(S)
for the powerset of
S
.
The difference of two sets
S
and
T
is denoted as
S\setminus T
.
If
\vec{x}
is a tuple, we write
x\in\vec{x}
to indicate that
x
is a component of
\vec{x}
.
Let
A
be an alphabet.
We use
|w|
to denote the length of some word
w\in A^{*}
and
\varepsilon
to denote the
empty word
.
The number of occurrences of
a\in A
within
w
is
|w|_{a}
.
We write
u\cdot v
or just
uv
for the concatenation of words
u,v\in A^{*}
.
If
u=p\cdot v\cdot s
for
p,s\in A^{*}
then
v
is a
factor
of
u
, denoted
v\sqsubseteq u
.
If
u\neq v
also holds, then
v\sqsubset u
.
Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet of
terminal symbols
and let
\Xi
be an infinite alphabet of
variables
.
We assume that
\Sigma\mathrel{\cap}\Xi=\emptyset
and
|\Sigma|\geq 2
.

If
T:=(V,E)
is a tree, then a path between
x_{1}\in V
and
x_{n}\in V
is the shortest sequence of edges from
x_{1}
to
x_{n}
.
If
(\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\{x_{2},x_{3}\},\dots,\{x_{n-1},x_{n}\})
is a path, then we say a node
y

lies
on this path if

for some

We call the number of edges on a path from

.

Document Spanners

Given
w:=w_{1}\cdot w_{2}\cdots w_{n}
where
w_{i}\in\Sigma
for all
i\in[n]
, a so-called
span
of
w
is an interval
[i,j\rangle
where
1\leq i\leq j\leq n+1
. A span
[i,j\rangle
defines a factor
w_{[i,j\rangle}:=w_{i}\cdot w_{i+1}\cdots w_{j-1}
of
w
.
Let
V\subset\Xi
, where
V
is finite, and let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.
A
(V,w)
-tuple
is a function
\mu
that maps each
x\in V
to a span
\mu(x)
of
w
.
A
spanner

P
, with variables
V
, is a function that maps every
w\in\Sigma^{*}
to a set
P(w)
of
(V,w)
-tuples.
By
{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P\right)}
, we denote the set of variables of
P
.

Like
[
7
]
, we use
regex formulas
as the primary extractors.
Regex formulas are an extension of regular expressions with so-called
capture variables
.
More formally:
\emptyset
,
\varepsilon
, and
\mathtt{a}
where
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
are all regex formulas, and if
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are regex formulas then so are
(\gamma_{1}\cdot\gamma_{2})
,
(\gamma_{1}\lor\gamma_{2})
,
(\gamma_{1})^{*}
, and
x\{\gamma_{1}\}
where
x\in\Xi
.
We use
\Sigma
as a shorthand for
\bigvee_{a\in\Sigma}a
. We can omit the parentheses when the meaning is clear.
A variable binding
x\{\gamma\}
matches the same words as
\gamma
and assigns the corresponding span of the input word to
x
.
A regex formula is
functional
if on every match, each variable is assigned exactly one span. We denote the set of functional regex formulas by
\mathsf{RGX}
.
For
\gamma\in\mathsf{RGX}
, we use
\llbracket\gamma\rrbracket
to define the corresponding spanner as follows.
Every match of
\gamma
on
w
defines
\mu
, a
({\mathsf{Vars}\left(\gamma\right)},w)
-tuple, where for each
x\in{\mathsf{Vars}\left(\gamma\right)}
, we have that
\mu(x)
is the span assigned to
x
.
We use
\llbracket\gamma\rrbracket(w)
to denote the set of all such
({\mathsf{Vars}\left(\gamma\right)},w)
-tuples.
See
[
7
]
for more details.

We now define
synchronized
\mathsf{RGX}
-formulas
(this follows the definition by Freydenberger, Kimelfeld, Kröll, and Peterfreund in
[
12
]
).
An expression
\gamma\in\mathsf{RGX}
is
synchronized
if for all sub-expressions of the form
(\gamma_{1}\lor\gamma_{2})
, no variable bindings occur in
\gamma_{1}
or
\gamma_{2}
.
We denote the class of synchronized
\mathsf{RGX}
-formulas by
\mathsf{RGX_{sync}}
.

The motivation for synchronized
\mathsf{RGX}
-formulas is that non-synchronized formulas allow for “hidden” disjunctions within the atoms. This goes (arguably) against the spirit of
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s and (as shown in
[
12
]
) leads to “un-
{\mathsf{CQ}}
-like” behavior.

Example 1
.

Consider the regex formula
\gamma:=\Sigma^{*}\cdot x\{\mathtt{a}\lor(\mathtt{b})^{*}\}\cdot y\{\Sigma^{*}\}\cdot\Sigma^{*}
.
We have that
\llbracket\gamma\rrbracket(w)
contains those
\mu
such that
\mu(x)
is a factor of
w
which is either an
\mathtt{a}
or a sequence of
\mathtt{b}
symbols, and the span
\mu(y)
occurs directly after
\mu(x)
.
Since
\gamma
is functional, and for every sub-expression of the form
(\gamma_{1}\lor\gamma_{2})
, we have that
{\mathsf{Vars}\left(\gamma_{1}\right)}={\mathsf{Vars}\left(\gamma_{2}\right)}=\emptyset
, it follows that
\gamma
is a
synchronized regex formula
.

Essentially, a synchronized regex formula is functional if no variable is redeclared, and no variable is used inside of a Kleene star.

This is extended into a
relational algebra
comprised of
\mathrel{\cup}
(union),
\pi
(projection),
\bowtie
(natural join), and
\zeta^{=}
(string equality). Let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be spanners. We say
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are
compatible
if
{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\right)}={\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{2}\right)}
. If two spanners
P_{1}
an
P_{2}
are compatible, then
(P_{1}\mathrel{\cup}P_{2})(w):=P_{1}(w)\mathrel{\cup}P_{2}(w)
. For
Y\subseteq{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\right)}
, the
projection

\pi_{Y}P_{1}(w)
is defined as the restriction of all
\mu\in P_{1}(w)
to the set of variables
Y
, and hence
{\mathsf{Vars}\left(\pi_{Y}P_{1}\right)}:=Y
.

The
natural join
,
P_{1}\bowtie P_{2}
, is obtained by defining
{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\bowtie P_{2}\right)}:={\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\right)}\mathrel{\cup}{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{2}\right)}
, and
(P_{1}\bowtie P_{2})(w)
as the set of all
({\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\right)}\mathrel{\cup}{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{2}\right)},w)
-tuples for which there exists
\mu_{1}\in P_{1}(w)
and
\mu_{2}\in P_{2}(w)
such that
\mu_{1}(x)=\mu_{2}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\right)}\mathrel{\cap}{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{2}\right)}
. The
string equality operator

\zeta^{=}_{x_{1},x_{2}}P_{1}
is defined by
\zeta^{=}_{x_{1},x_{2}}P_{1}(w):=\{\mu\in P_{1}(w)\mid w_{\mu(x_{1})}=w_{\mu(x_{2})}\}
, where
{\mathsf{Vars}\left(\zeta^{=}_{x_{1},x_{2}}P_{1}\right)}:={\mathsf{Vars}\left(P_{1}\right)}
.

Given a class of regex-formulas
C

and a spanner algebra
\mathsf{O}
,
we use
C^{\mathsf{O}}
to denote the set of spanner representations which can be constructed by repeated combinations of operators from
\mathsf{O}
with a regex-formula from
C
. We write
\llbracket C^{\mathsf{O}}\rrbracket
to denote the closure of
\llbracket C\rrbracket
under
\mathsf{O}
.

The class of
core spanners
(introduced by Fagin, Kimelfeld, Reiss, and Vansummeren
[
7
]
) is defined as
\llbracket\mathsf{RGX}^{\mathsf{core}}\rrbracket
where
\mathsf{core}:=\{\pi,\zeta^{=},\mathrel{\cup},\bowtie\}
. The class of
regex
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s with string equality
(
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s) is defined as expressions of the form:

P:=\pi_{Y}\left(\zeta^{=}_{x_{1},y_{1}}\cdots\zeta^{=}_{x_{l},y_{l}}(\gamma_{1}\bowtie\cdots\bowtie\gamma_{k})\right),

where
\gamma_{i}\in\mathsf{RGX}
for all
i\in[k]
. We call an
\mathsf{SERCQ}
a
synchronized

\mathsf{SERCQ}
if every regex formula is a synchronized
\mathsf{RGX}
-formula.

Example 2
.

Consider
P:=\zeta^{=}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\gamma_{1}\bowtie\gamma_{2}\right)
where
\gamma_{1}:=\Sigma^{*}\cdot x_{1}\{\Sigma^{+}\}\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdot\Sigma^{*}
and
\gamma_{2}:=\Sigma^{*}\cdot x_{2}\{\Sigma^{+}\}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot\Sigma^{*}
. Given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we have that
\llbracket P\rrbracket(w)
contains those
\mu
such that the factor
w_{\mu(x_{1})}
is non-empty, and is immediately followed by the symbol
\mathtt{a}
, the factor
w_{\mu(x_{2})}
is immediately followed by the symbol
\mathtt{b}
, and
w_{\mu(x_{1})}=w_{\mu(x_{2})}
. Since both
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are synchronized,
P
is a synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}
.

Computational Model and Complexity Measures

We use the
random access machine
model with uniform cost measures,
where the size of each machine word is logarithmic in the size of the input.
We represent factors of a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
as spans of
w
. This allows us to check whether
u=v
for
u,v\sqsubseteq w
in constant time after preprocessing that takes linear time and space
[
17
,
5
]
(see
\cref
lemma:datastructure for more details).
The complexity results we state are in terms of
combined complexity
.
That is, both the query and the word are considered part of the input.
When considering the enumeration of results for a query executed on a word, we say that we can enumerate results with
polynomial-delay
if there exists an algorithm which returns the first result in polynomial time, the time between two consecutive results is polynomial, and the time between the last result and terminating is polynomial.

3
Conjunctive Queries for FC

This section introduces
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s, a conjunctive query fragment of
\mathsf{FC}
with regular constraints. We give some complexity results regarding
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s and show an efficient conversion from synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s to
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.

A pattern is a word
\alpha\in(\Sigma\cup\Xi)^{*}
, and a
word equation
is a pair
\eta:=(\alpha_{L},\alpha_{R})
where
\alpha_{L},\alpha_{R}\in(\Sigma\mathrel{\cup}\Xi)^{*}
are patterns known as the
left
and
right
side respectively.
We usually write such
\eta
as
(\alpha_{L}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{R})
.
The length of a word equation, denoted
|(\alpha_{L}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{R})|
, is
|\alpha_{L}|+|\alpha_{R}|
.
A
pattern substitution
is a morphism
\sigma\colon(\Sigma\cup\Xi)^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
such that
\sigma(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{a}
holds for all
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
.
Since
\sigma
is a morphism, we have
\sigma(\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2})=\sigma(\alpha_{1})\cdot\sigma(\alpha_{2})
for all
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(\Sigma\cup\Xi)^{*}
.

A pattern substitution
\sigma
is a
solution
to a word equation
(\alpha_{L}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{R})
if and only if
\sigma(\alpha_{L})=\sigma(\alpha_{R})
.
When applying a pattern substitution
\sigma
to a pattern
\alpha
, we assume that its domain
\mathsf{dom}(\sigma)
satisfies
\mathsf{var}(\alpha)\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\sigma)
.
Freydenberger and Peterfreund
[
14
]
introduced
\mathsf{FC}
as a first-order logic that is based on word equations. In the present paper, we do not consider the full logic
\mathsf{FC}
. Instead, we introduce its conjunctive queries.

Definition 3
.

An
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is an
\mathsf{FC}
-formula of the form
\varphi(\vec{x}):=\exists\vec{y}\colon\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
, where
\eta_{i}:=(x_{i}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{i})
,
x_{i}\in\Xi
, and
\alpha_{i}\in(\Sigma\mathrel{\cup}\Xi)^{*}
for all
i\in[n]
. We use the shorthand
\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})\leftarrow\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
where
\vec{x}
is the tuple of free variables. We call
\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})
the
head
of
\varphi
, and
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
the
body
of
\varphi
.

We write
\varphi(\vec{x})
to denote that
\vec{x}
is the set of free variables of
\varphi
.
The set of all variables used in
\varphi
is denoted by
\mathsf{var}(\varphi)
.
We distinguish a variable
\mathfrak{u}\in\Xi
, called the
universe variable
,
that shall represent the input document
w
.
The universe variable is not considered a free variable, and we adopt the convention that
\mathfrak{u}\notin\mathsf{var}(\varphi)
for all
\varphi
(even if
\mathfrak{u}
occurs in
\varphi
).
Next, we define the semantics for
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.

Definition 4
.

For
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
and a pattern substitution
\sigma
with
\mathsf{var}(\varphi)\mathrel{\cup}\{\mathfrak{u}\}\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\sigma)
, we define
\sigma\models\varphi
as follows:
\sigma\models(\alpha_{l}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{R})
if
\sigma(\eta_{L})=\sigma(\eta_{R})
and
\sigma(x)\sqsubseteq\sigma(\mathfrak{u})
for all
x\in\mathsf{var}(\alpha_{L}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{R})
.
For
\sigma\models\exists x\colon\varphi
we have that
\sigma_{x\mapsto u}\models\varphi
holds for some
u\sqsubseteq\sigma(\mathfrak{u})
, where
\sigma_{x\mapsto u}
is defined as
\sigma_{x\mapsto u}(x):=u
and
\sigma_{x\mapsto u}(y):=\sigma(y)
for all
y\in(\Sigma\cup\Xi)
where
y\neq x
. We use the canonical definition for conjunction.

Hence, for all
\sigma\models\varphi(\vec{x})
, the universe for variables in
\mathsf{var}(\varphi)
is the set of factors of
\sigma(\mathfrak{u})
.
If
\varphi(\vec{x})\in\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, then
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
denotes the set of all
\sigma(\vec{x})
such that
\sigma\models\varphi
and
\sigma(\mathfrak{u})=w
.
When determining
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
for a given
w
, we know that
\mathfrak{u}
represents
w
, and hence
\mathfrak{u}
can be treated as a constant (see
[
14
]
for more information on the role of the universe variable).
If
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC}
is
Boolean
(that is, it has no free variables),
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
is either the empty set, or the set containing the empty tuple, which we interpret as
{\mathsf{False}}
and
{\mathsf{True}}
, respectively.

In
[
14
]
,
\mathsf{FC}
was extended to
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
by adding
regular constraints
.
This allows for atoms of the form
(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
, where
\gamma
is a
regular expression
;
and
\sigma\models(x\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
if and only if
\sigma(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\gamma)

and
\sigma(x)\sqsubseteq\sigma(\mathfrak{u})
.
We extend
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
to
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
in the same way.

Complexity

We now define various decision problems for
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
and
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
:
The
non-emptiness problem
is, given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
\varphi
, decide whether
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)\neq\emptyset
. The
evaluation problem
is, given
\sigma
and
\varphi
, decide whether
\sigma\models\varphi
. The
model checking problem
is the special case of non-emptiness and evaluation that only considers Boolean queries, note that for Boolean queries
\mathsf{Dom}(\sigma)=\{\mathfrak{u}\}
.
Given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
\varphi
, the
enumeration problem
is outputting all
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)
.
The
containment problem
is, given
\varphi
and
\psi
, decide whether
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(w)\subseteq\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(w)
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Previous results on patterns and
\mathsf{FC}
(see
[
4
,
6
,
14
]
) directly imply the following.

Proposition 5
.

For each of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
and
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, the evaluation problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, and the containment problem is undecidable.

As discussed in
[
14
]
,
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
can be evaluated analogously to relational first-order logic (
\mathsf{FO}
), by materializing the tables that are defined by the atoms and then proceeding “as usual”.
Hence, bounding the width of a formula (the maximum number of free variables in a subformula) bounds the size of the intermediate tables, and thereby the complexity of evaluation.
As the complexity of evaluating
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{FO}
are the same (
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete in general,
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for the existential-positive fragment), it is no surprise that this correspondence also translates to conjunctive queries.
From
\cref
sec:acycCQFC on, we further develop this connection by finding tractable subclasses of
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.

As containment for
{\mathsf{CQ}}
s is decidable (although
\mathsf{NP}
-complete), it can be used for query minimization (see Chapter 6 of
[
1
]
). But by
\cref
CQlowerBounds, this does not apply to
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.

Document Spanners and FC-CQs

Our next goal is to establish a connection between
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s and
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s. However, first we must overcome the fact that
\mathsf{FC}
reasons over strings, whereas spanners reason over intervals of positions. We deal with this by defining the notion of an
\mathsf{FC}
-formula
realizing
a spanner, as described in
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
.

Definition 6
.

A pattern substitution
\sigma

expresses
a
(V,w)
-tuple
\mu
, if for all
x\in V
, we have that
\mathsf{Dom}(\sigma)=\{x^{P},x^{C}\mid x\in V\}
, and
\sigma(x^{P})=w_{[1,i\rangle}
and
\sigma(x^{C})=w_{[i,j\rangle}
for the span
\mu(x)=[i,j\rangle
. An
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

\varphi

realizes
a spanner
P
if
\mathsf{free}(\varphi)=\{x^{P},x^{C}\mid x\in{\mathsf{Vars}\left(P\right)}\}
and
\sigma\models\varphi
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
where
\sigma(\mathfrak{u})=w
, if and only if
\sigma
expresses some
\mu\in P(w)
.

Less formally, for each
\mu\in P(w)
, we have that
\mu(x)=[i,j\rangle
is uniquely represented by the prefix,
\sigma(x^{P})=w_{[1,i\rangle}
, and the content,
\sigma(x^{C})=w_{[i,j\rangle}
.

Example 7
.

Consider the following
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.

\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}(x_{1}^{P},x_{1}^{C},x_{2}^{P},x_{2}^{C})\leftarrow(\mathfrak{u}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{1}^{P}\cdot x_{1}^{C}\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdot s_{1})\land(\mathfrak{u}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{2}^{P}\cdot x_{2}^{C}\cdot\mathtt{b}\cdot s_{2})\\
\land(x_{1}^{C}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{2}^{C})\land(x_{1}^{C}\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\Sigma^{+})\land(x_{2}^{C}\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\Sigma^{+}).

We can see that
\varphi
realizes the
\mathsf{SERCQ}
given in
\cref
example:regexCQ.

Recall that synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s consist of
\mathsf{RGX}
-formulas that do not have variables within sub-expressions of the form
(\gamma_{1}\lor\gamma_{2})
.
As we observe in the following result, a synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}
can be efficiently translated into an equivalent
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.

Lemma 8
.

Given a synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}

P
, we can construct in polynomial time an
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
that realizes
P
.

The proof of
\cref
Prop:RGXtoPatCQ follows from
[
14
,
11
,
10
]
.
The converse of
\cref
Prop:RGXtoPatCQ follows directly from
[
14
]
. However, one would need to define how
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
-formulas can be realized by regex formulas closed under spanner algebra (details on this can be found in
[
10
,
14
]
). We omit such a result as it is not the focus on this work.

In this section, we have introduced
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s, and shown an efficient conversion from synchronized
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s to
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.
Therefore, while the present paper mainly considers a tractable fragment of
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, this tractability carries over to a subclass of
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s.

4
Acyclic Pattern Decomposition

This section examines decomposing terminal-free patterns (i. e., patterns
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
) into acyclic
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s, where
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
denotes the set of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s where each word equation has a right-hand side of at most length two.
Patterns are the basis for
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
atoms, and hence, this section gives us a foundation on which to investigate the decomposition of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.
We do not consider regular constraints, or patterns with terminals.
This is because regular constraints are unary predicates, and therefore can be easily added to a join tree; and terminals can be expressed through regular constraints.
We use
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s for two reasons.
Firstly, binary concatenation is the most elementary form of concatenation, as it cannot be decomposed into further (non-trivial) concatenations.
Secondly, this ensures that each word equation has very low width, and therefore we can store the tables in linear space and enumerate them with constant delay – as shown in the following.

Proposition 9
.

Given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we can construct a data structure in linear time that, for
x,y,z\in\Xi
, allow us to enumerate
\llbracket x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z\rrbracket(w)
with constant-delay, and to decide in constant time if
\sigma\in\llbracket x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z\rrbracket(w)
holds.

Although the cardinality of
\llbracket x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z\rrbracket(w)
is cubic in
|w|
,
\cref
lemma:datastructure allows us to represent this relation in linear space.
As we can query such relations in constant time, they behave “nicer” than relations in relational algebra.
Furthermore, after materializing the relations defined by each atom of an
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
,
\cref
lemma:datastructure allows us to treat the
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
as a relational conjunctive query.
We now introduce a way to
decompose
a pattern into a conjunction of word equations where the right hand side of each atom is at most length two.
We start by looking at a canonical way to decompose terminal-free patterns.

Let
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
be a terminal-free pattern.
To decompose
\alpha
, first we factorize
\alpha
so that it can be written using only binary concatenation
We define
\mathsf{BPat}
, the set of all
well-bracketed patterns
, recursively as follows:

Definition 10
.

x\in\mathsf{BPat}
for all
x\in\Xi
, and if
\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}\in\mathsf{BPat}
, then
(\tilde{\alpha}\cdot\tilde{\beta})\in\mathsf{BPat}
.
2
2

2

For convenience, we tend use
\tilde{\alpha}
to denote a bracketing of the pattern
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
.

We extend the notion of a factor to a
sub-bracketing
.
We write
\tilde{\alpha}\sqsubseteq\tilde{\beta}
if
\tilde{\alpha}
is a factor of
\tilde{\beta}
and
\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}\in\mathsf{BPat}
.
Let
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
, by
\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)
we denote the set of all bracketings which correspond to the pattern
\alpha
(i. e., if we remove the brackets, then the resulting pattern is
\alpha
).
Every
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)
can be converted into an equivalent formula
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
using the following.

Definition 11
.

While there exists
\tilde{\beta}\sqsubseteq\tilde{\alpha}
where
\tilde{\beta}=(x\cdot y)
for some
x,y\in\Xi
, we replace every occurrence of
\tilde{\beta}
in
\tilde{\alpha}
with a new, unique variable
z\in\Xi\setminus\mathsf{var}(\alpha)
and add the word equation
(z\mathbin{\dot{=}}x\cdot y)
to
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
.
When
\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\beta}
, we have that
z=\mathfrak{u}
.

Therefore, up to renaming of variables, every
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}
has a corresponding formula
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.
We call
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
the
decomposition
of
\tilde{\alpha}
.
The decomposition can be thought of as a logic formula expressing a
straight-line program
of the pattern (see
[
20
]
for a survey on algorithms for SLPs).
We now give an example of
decomposing
a bracketing.

Example 12
.

Let
\alpha:=x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}x_{1}x_{2}
and let
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)
be defined as follows:

\tilde{\alpha}:=(((x_{1}\cdot x_{2})\cdot x_{1})\cdot(x_{1}\cdot x_{2})).

We now list
\tilde{\alpha}
after every sub-bracketing is replaced with a variable. We also give the corresponding word equation that is added to
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
.

\displaystyle((\underline{(x_{1}\cdot x_{2})}\cdot x_{1})\cdot\underline{(x_{1}\cdot x_{2})})

\displaystyle z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{1}\cdot x_{2}

\displaystyle(\underline{(z_{1}\cdot x_{1})}\cdot z_{1})

\displaystyle z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{1}\cdot x_{1}

\displaystyle\underline{(z_{3}\cdot z_{1})}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{3}\cdot z_{1}

Therefore, we get the decomposition
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, which is defined as

\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}:=\mathsf{Ans}()\leftarrow(z_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{1}\cdot x_{2})\land(z_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{1}\cdot x_{1})\land(\mathfrak{u}\mathbin{\dot{=}}z_{2}\cdot z_{1}).

Notice that every sub-bracketing of
\tilde{\alpha}
has a corresponding word equation in
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
.

The decomposition of
\tilde{\alpha}
is somewhat similar to the
Tseytin transformations
, see
[
23
]
, which transforms a propositional logic formula into a formula in
Tseytin normal form
.

Our next focus is to study which patterns can be decomposed into an
acyclic

2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

Definition 13

(Join Tree)
.

A
join tree
for
\Psi\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
with body
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{i}
is an undirected tree
T:=(V,E)
, where
V:=\{\chi_{i}\mid i\in[n]\}
, and for all

and

, then

\chi_{i}
and
\chi_{j}
in

.

Note that we use
\chi
(with indices) to denote atoms of a

to distinguish them from word equations with arbitrarily large right-hand sides – which we denote by
\eta
(with indices).
We call
\Psi\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

acyclic
if there exists a join tree for
\Psi
. Otherwise, we call
\Psi

cyclic
.

Definition 14

(Acyclic Patterns)
.

If
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is a decomposition of
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}
and
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}

\tilde{\alpha}

acyclic
. If
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}

\tilde{\alpha}

cyclic
. If there exists

is

\alpha
is
cyclic
.

When determining whether a decomposition
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is acyclic, we treat each word equation (atom) of
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
as a single relational symbol.
We also consider
\mathfrak{u}
to be a constant symbol, since
\sigma(\mathfrak{u})=w
always holds.
This raises the question as to whether every pattern has an acyclic decomposition. The answers is no, as the following result shows.

Proposition 15
.

x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}x_{3}x_{1}
is a cyclic pattern, and
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{1}
is an acyclic pattern that has a cyclic bracketing.

This leads to the following question:
Can we decide whether a pattern is acyclic in polynomial time?

Given a pattern
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
, we have that
|\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)|=C_{|\alpha|-1}
, where
C_{i}
is the
i^{th}

Catalan number
, see
[
21
]
.
As the Catalan numbers grow exponentially, a straightforward enumeration of bracketings to finding an acyclic bracketing is not enough.

If
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is a decomposition of
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)
, then we call the variable
x\in\Xi
which represents the whole pattern the
root variable
. If
x
is the root variable, then the atom
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y\cdot z)
for some
y,z\in\Xi
, is called the
root atom
. So far, the root variable has always been
\mathfrak{u}
.
In
\cref
sec:acycCQFC, different root variables will be considered.

Let
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
be the decomposition of
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
. We define the

of

as a rooted, undirected, binary tree
\mathcal{T}:=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},<,\Gamma,\tau,v_{r})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is a set of nodes and
\mathcal{E}
is a set of undirected edges.
If
v
and
v^{\prime}
have a shared parent node, then we use

to denote that
v
is the left child and
v^{\prime}
is the right child of their shared parent.
We also have
\Gamma:=\mathsf{var}(\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}})
and the function
\tau\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\Gamma
that

labels
nodes from the concatenation tree with variables from
\mathsf{var}(\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}})
.
We use
v_{r}
to denote the root of
\mathcal{T}
.
The
concatenation tree
of
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
is defined as follows.

Definition 16
.

Let
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}:=\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})\leftarrow\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(z_{i}\mathbin{\dot{=}}x_{i}\cdot x_{i}^{\prime})
be a decomposition of
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}(\alpha)
.
We carry out the construction of a concatenation tree in two steps.
First, we build a tree recursively.
If
v\in\mathcal{V}
is labeled with
z_{i}
for
i\in[n]
, then there exists a left and right child of
v
that are labeled with
x_{i}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}
respectively.

In the second step, we prune the result of the above construction to remove redundancies.
For each set of
non-leaf nodes
that share a common label, we define an ordering
\ll
.
If
\tau(v_{i})=\tau(v_{j})
and the distance from the root of
\mathcal{T}
to
v_{j}
is strictly less than the distance from the root to
v_{i}
, then
v_{j}\ll v_{i}
. If
\tau(v_{i})=\tau(v_{j})
and the distance from
v_{r}
to
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
is equal, then
v_{j}\ll v_{i}
if and only if
v_{j}
appears to the

of
v_{i}
.
For each set of non-leaf nodes that share a common label, all nodes other than the
\ll

.
All descendants of redundant nodes are removed.

Concatenation trees for
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

[
20
]
.
While the pruning may seem somewhat unnatural, the concatenation tree of a decomposition is a useful tool that we shall use in

Due to the pruning procedure, every non-leaf node represents a unique sub-bracketing.
For every node

and right child

\mathsf{atom}(v):=(\tau(v)\mathbin{\dot{=}}\tau(v_{l})\cdot\tau(v_{r}))
.
Note that for any two non-leaf nodes

where

, we have that
\mathsf{atom}(v)\neq\mathsf{atom}(v^{\prime})
. We call

an

Definition 17

Let
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}
and let
\mathcal{T}
be the concatenation tree for
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
.
For some

Theorem 20

Whether

5
Acyclic FC-CQs

In this section, we generalize from decomposing patterns to decomposing
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.
The main result of this section is a polynomial-time algorithm to determine whether an
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
can be decomposed into an acyclic
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.
We do this to find a notion of acyclicity for
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s such that the resulting fragment is tractable.

Decomposing a word equation
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha)
where
x\in\Xi
and
\alpha\in(\Xi\setminus\{x\})^{+}
is analogous to decomposing
\alpha
, but whereas
\mathfrak{u}
is the root variable when decomposing a pattern, we use
x
as the root variable when decomposing
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha)
.

If every atom of
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is acyclic, then
\varphi
does not necessarily have tractable model checking.
If this were the case, then any decomposition
\Psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
of some
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}
would have tractable model checking (because every word equation of the form
z\mathbin{\dot{=}}x\cdot y
is acyclic).
This would imply that the membership problem for patterns can be solved in polynomial time, which contradicts
[
6
]
, unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.
Furthermore, if we define
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
to be acyclic if there exists a join tree for
\varphi
where every word equation is an atom, then model checking for
\varphi
is not tractable.
To show this, consider
\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}()\leftarrow(\mathfrak{u}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha)
.
Model checking for
\varphi
is equivalent to the membership problem for
\alpha
, which is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
6
]
.
Therefore, we require a more refined notion of acyclicity for
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.

In
\cref
sec:decomp, we studied the decomposition of terminal-free patterns.
If
\varphi
is an
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
with the body
\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})\leftarrow\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
, then the right-hand side of some
\eta_{i}
may not be terminal-free.
Therefore, before defining the decomposition of
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s, we define a way to
normalize

\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s in order to better utilize the techniques of
\cref
sec:decomp.

Definition 21
.

We call an
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
with body
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{i})

normalized
if for all
i,j\in[n]
, we have
\alpha_{i}\in\Xi^{+}
,
x_{i}\notin\mathsf{var}(\alpha_{i})
,
\mathfrak{u}\notin\mathsf{var}(\alpha_{i})
, and
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{j}
if and only if
i=j
.

An
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
with body
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{i})\land\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}(y_{j}\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\gamma)
is
normalized
if the subformula
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{i})
is normalized.

Since we are interested in polynomial time algorithms, the following lemma allows us to assume that all
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s are normalized without affecting any claims about complexity.

Lemma 22
.

Given
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, we can construct an equivalent, normalized
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
in time
\mathcal{O}(|\varphi|^{2})
.

To prove
\cref
lemma:normalization we use a simple re-writing procedure.
We replace every terminal factor in our formula with a new variable, and use a regular constraint to determine which terminal word that variable represents.
If
\sigma
is a morphism that satisfies
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in\Xi
, then
|\sigma(x)|=|\sigma(\alpha)|
.
Therefore, if
x\in\alpha
, then
|\sigma(x)|=|\sigma(\alpha_{1})|+|\sigma(x)|+|\sigma(\alpha_{2})|
where
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\cdot x\cdot\alpha_{2}
.
We can then determine that
\sigma(\alpha_{1})\cdot\sigma(\alpha_{2})=\varepsilon
.
Hence,
x\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha
can be replaced with
(x\mathbin{\dot{=}}y)\land\bigwedge_{z\in\mathsf{var}(\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2})}(z\mathbin{\dot{\in}}\varepsilon)
where
y
is a new and unique variable.
An analogous method is used if
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathsf{var}(\alpha)
.

Example 23
.

We define an
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
along with an equivalent normalized
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

\displaystyle\varphi:=

\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

For every
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

Definition 25

(Acyclic
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s)
.

If
\Psi_{\varphi}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is a decomposition of
\varphi\in\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, we say that
\Psi_{\varphi}
is
acyclic
if there exists a join tree for
\Psi_{\varphi}
. Otherwise,
\Psi_{\varphi}
is
cyclic
. If there exists an acyclic decomposition of
\varphi
, then we say that
\varphi
is

. Otherwise,
\varphi
is

.

Recall that, since
\mathfrak{u}
is always mapped to

, we can consider
\mathfrak{u}
a constant symbol.
Therefore, if
T:=(V,E)
is a join tree for some decomposition of
\varphi
, then there can exist two nodes that both contain
\mathfrak{u}
, yet it is not necessary for all nodes on the path between these two nodes to also contain
\mathfrak{u}
.
Referring back to
\cref
example:LVDecomp, we can see that
\varphi
is acyclic by executing the GYO algorithm on the decomposition (see Chapter 6 of
[
1
]
for more information on acyclic joins).
Our next focus is to study which
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s are acyclic, and which are not.

Lemma 26
.

If
\Psi_{\varphi}\in 2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
is a decomposition of
\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})\leftarrow\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
, and we have a join tree

\eta_{i}

To prove
\cref

\varphi

We show that this subgraph is connected, and since the introduced variables are disjoint for separate atoms of
\varphi

Let
\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})\leftarrow\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}

\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

\varphi

\varphi

\varphi
is

Clearly weak acyclicity is not sufficient for tractability, as discussed at the start of the current section.

If

We know that

\cref
lemma:subtree.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\varphi:=\mathsf{Ans}(\vec{x})\leftarrow\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
be a normalized
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
. If any of the following conditions holds, then
\varphi
is cyclic:

1.

\varphi
is weakly cyclic,

2.

\eta_{i}
is cyclic for any
i\in[n]

3.

|\mathsf{var}(\eta_{i})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{var}(\eta_{j})|>3

i,j\in[n]
where
i\neq j
, or

4.

|\mathsf{var}(\eta_{i})\mathrel{\cap}\mathsf{var}(\eta_{j})|=3
, and
|\eta_{i}|>3
or
|\eta_{j}|>3

i,j\in[n]
where
i\neq j
.

Condition 1 can be proven by simply replacing
T^{i}
with a single node
\eta_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.
Condition 2 follows directly from
\cref
lemma:subtree.
Conditions 3 and 4 can be proven by a contradiction: Consider the shortest path from any atom of the decomposition of
\eta_{i}
to any atom of the decomposition of
\eta_{j}
.
Since the end points of these paths cannot contain all the variables that
\eta_{i}
and
\eta_{j}
share, it follows that
T:=(V,E)
is not a join tree.

While Conditions 3 and 4 might seem strict, we can pre-factor common subpatterns.
For example, the conjunction
(x_{1}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\alpha_{3})\land(x_{2}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{4}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\alpha_{5})

where

is a new variable.
We illustrate this further in the following example.

Example 29
.

Consider the following

One could alter our definition of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

The authors believe it is likely that such a definition of

\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

.

Lemma 32

Let

While

Lemma 33
.

Given a normalized
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}

\Psi

and

\mathcal{O}(|\alpha|^{7})
.

We prove
\cref

\cref

The purposes of
\cref

to be acyclic:

\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
.

\Psi\in 2\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
of
\varphi
if and only if the following conditions hold:

1.

\varphi
is weakly acyclic,

2.

for all
i\in[m]
the pattern
\alpha_{i}
is acyclic, and

3.

for every
i\in[m]
, there is a decomposition
\Psi_{i}
of

such that for all

\Psi_{j}
of
x_{j}\mathbin{\dot{=}}\alpha_{j}
where there exists an atom
\chi_{i}

A Note on k-ary Decompositions

We now generalize the notion of pattern decomposition so that the length of the right-hand side of the resulting formula is less than or equal to some
k\geq 2
. While the binary decompositions might be considered the natural case, we show that generalizing to higher arities increases the expressive power of acyclic patterns. By
k\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
we denote the set of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
formulas that have a right-hand side of at most length
k
. We write
\mathsf{BPat}_{k}
for the set of
k
-ary bracketed patterns over
\Xi
. We define
\mathsf{BPat}_{k}
formally using the following recursive definition: For all
x\in\Xi
we have that
x\in\mathsf{BPat}_{k}
, and if
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i}\in\mathsf{BPat}_{k}
where
i\leq k
, then
(\tilde{\alpha}_{1}\cdot\tilde{\alpha}_{2}\cdots\tilde{\alpha}_{i})\in\mathsf{BPat}_{k}
. We write
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}_{k}(\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in\Xi^{+}
if the underlying, unbracketed pattern of
\tilde{\alpha}
is
\alpha
. We can convert
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathsf{BPat}_{k}
into an equivalent
k\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
analogously to the binary case, see
\cref
defn:conclogConversion.

Consider the following
4
-ary bracketing:

In this section, we have briefly examined

A systematic study into

\mathsf{FC}
-formulas with bounded width
[

However, more general approaches such as bounded treewidth for binary decompositions appear to be a more promising direction for future work.

7
Conclusions

Freydenberger and Peterfreund
[
14
]
introduced
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
as a logic for querying and model checking words that behaves similar to relational
\mathsf{FO}
.
The present paper develops this connection further by providing a polynomial-time algorithm that either decomposes an
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
into an acyclic
2\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
, or determines that this is not possible.
These acyclic
2\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
formulas allow for polynomial-time model checking, and their results can be enumerated with polynomial-delay.
Consequently, the present paper establishes a notion of tractable acyclicity for
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.
Due to the close connections between
\mathsf{FC}[\mathsf{REG}]
and core spanners, this provides us with a large class of tractable
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s.

But this is only the first step in the study of tractable
\mathsf{SERCQ}
s and
\mathsf{FC[REG]\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s.
It seems likely that more efficient algorithms for model checking and enumeration can be found by utilizing string algorithms rather than materializing the relations for each atom.

Another future direction for research is the consideration of other structural parameters, like treewidth.
A systematic study of the decomposition of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s into
2\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s of bounded treewidth would likely yield a large class of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s with polynomial-time model checking.
As a consequence, one could define a suitable notion of treewidth for core spanners.
Determining the exact class of
\mathsf{FC\mathchar 45\relax CQ}
s with polynomial-time model checking is likely a hard problem.
This is because such a result would solve the open problem in formal languages of determining exactly what patterns have polynomial-time membership.
