Evaluating Large Language Models Beyond Textual Understanding with ChildPlay
Gonçalo Carvalho, Oscar Knap, Robert Pollice

Abstract




Abstract

We developed a benchmark set to assess the generalization of state-of-the-art large language models on problems beyond linguistic tasks and evaluate it on a systematic progression of GPT models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini). Using simple games like Tic-Tac-Toe, Connect Four, Battleship, and a Shape Recognition Game, all encoded in ASCII, we test strategic capabilities and spatial reasoning, core abilities any artificial intelligence would need to master for solving problems in chemistry. To probe generalization, we introduce two new games for spatial logic: LEGO Connect Language (LCL) and Guess-the-SMILES (GtS), a operationally simple chemistry benchmark. Our results show that GPT models provide meaningful responses for several tasks but, generally, perform poorly. A systematic performance progression with increased model capabilities (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o) is only observed for 4 out of the 7 benchmark tasks. All models consistently struggle with Battleship, LCL, and GtS. This suggests that while GPT models can emulate conversational proficiency and basic rule comprehension, they have limited generalization with respect to strategy and spatial reasoning. Particularly poor performance is observed for interpreting molecular graphs when encoded in ASCII. The results provided by our open-source benchmark suite (
ChildPlay
GitHub Repository
) caution against claims of emergent intelligence in GPT models, which appear more specialized than general.

Keywords

Large Language Models, Benchmarking, Non-linguistic Reasoning, Zero-shot Learning, Molecular graphs, SMILES

Introduction

Current Large Language Models (LLMs) are transformer-based. They process input and generate output coherently and contextually appropriate
1
. They use self-attention to weigh the relative importance of different words in a sentence
2
,
1

1

3

In addition, recent studies have tested LLMs in non-linguistic tasks. Liga and Pasetto used Tic-Tac-Toe in ASCII form, pitting LLMs against the minimax algorithm to explore emergent features, suggested to resemble consciousness
16
. While performing respectably, LLMs still often only achieved draws or lost
16
. Topsakal and Harper
17
found GPT-4 to win more often than GPT-3.5 but still not to play in an optimal manner. Some studies, as those by Lappin et al. and Zečević et al., caution against overestimating the capabilities of LLMs, stating they may excel at language but seem to struggle with reasoning and causal understanding
18
,
19
. Both Bender et al. and Schaeffer et al. critique the transparency of evaluation metrics, arguing that some so-called emergent abilities may be artifacts of evaluation rather than genuine behavior
20
,

Another issue in evaluating LLMs is training-test set cross-contamination
1
. Massive training sets, often untraceable, make replication difficult and risk rendering benchmarks meaningless due to contamination
1
,
22
. N-Gram Overlap is one method to detect contamination but has limitations, such as failing to account for context and synonyms
23

This critique of evaluation metrics invites exploration of general intelligence and how it can be reliably measured through rigorous and realistic tests beyond linguistics that include broader cognitive functions. For general intelligence (GI), the so-called g-factor quantifies the ability to reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, and learn quickly across many domains
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28

29
,
30

31
.

This Work
We present
ChildPlay
, a scalable suite of non-language games comprising Tic-Tac-Toe, Connect-Four, Battleship, Lego Connect Language, and Shape Recognition. It also includes the hidden chemistry task Guess-the-SMILES (GtS), which probes the translation of ASCII depictions of chemical graphs into the SMILES representation. For this last task, the source code is not provided to obviate future model overfitting. These games assess LLM strategy, symbolic reasoning, spatial logic, and pattern recognition beyond linguistics. Notably, these are all aspects that are particularly relevant for applying LLMs in the context of chemistry when structure-property relationships need to be accounted for. We chose games because they offer a simple structure with clear success criteria that is suitable for evaluating strategic thinking and decision-making in dynamic, adversarial scenarios
32
,
16
,
17
. All games are ASCII-encoded to minimize dataset contamination
1
,
16
. The game rules are provided in the prompts, which is critical to evaluate how well LLMs can generalize from instructions. We also proposed
ChildPlay
for inclusion in BIG-bench
11
, which aims to standardize challenging LLM benchmarks.

Methods

General Methodology
Tasks in BIG-bench
11

1

Repository

To study a systematic series, we tested

(GPT-3.5),
gpt-4-1106-preview
(GPT-4),
gpt-4o-2024-08-06

B.6

Board Games: Battleship, Tic-Tac-Toe, and Connect-Four (BTC)
For the BTC games, each board state was introduced with a game explanation. The LLMs, always playing as the first player, received the current board state and faced an opponent making random moves, which corresponds to a weak opponent. As these games are fully observable, the initial instruction and the current state allow for optimal play. Inputs are two scalars for the row-column pair or a single scalar for the column in Connect-Four. In Battleship, ships (’S’) were randomly initialized horizontally, varying from 2 to 5 cells in size. Hits were marked with an ’X’ and misses with an ’O’. The symbol ’
\sim
’ denoted an unexplored square.

Shape Recognition Game
We first probed what geometric shapes the LLMs considered basic. The shapes consistently mentioned were square, circle, and triangle. Accordingly, the Shape Recognition Game consists of identifying a basic geometric shape ”hidden” behind 1s in a matrix of 0s in a multiple-choice fashion with four options: circle, rectangle, triangle, and cross. Only the latter three were ever shown (cf. Figure
2
).

Lego Connect Language
We invented the LEGO Connect Language (LCL). We propose
LCL_{2}
as a language to instruct assembly in 2D. In the current implementation, only 2x4 pieces are allowed for a construction,
M
, which is valid
if
no pieces are overlapping and all pieces are connected to other pieces. A Lego piece can only be connected through interlocking pegs. Two Lego pieces overlap when they share the same y-coordinate and any part of their length has the same x-coordinate (cf. Supporting Information, Section
C.2
for a formal definition).

Figure 3
:
Left: A standard LEGO piece in
LCL_{2}
. Right: A
valid
construct in
LCL_{2}
.

LCL includes two games, validity testing, which evaluates the ability to assess constructs as valid or invalid, and construct generation, where models are tasked to generate valid LCL constructs from prompts. Constructs are described by tuples specifying the coordinates and colors of pieces. In both games, we evaluate the proportion of correct answers. We generated 800 constructs for the validity test (400 valid, 400 invalid) and, for the construct generation, queried each model to produce 100 constructs. A construct example encoded in ASCII is
[(1,1,^{\prime}red^{\prime}),(2,1,^{\prime}blue^{\prime}),(3,1,^{\prime}green^{\prime})]
, where each tuple represents a brick’s position and color.

Guess-the-SMILES
Finally, we have a hidden task with undisclosed source code about interpreting ASCII drawings of molecules (cf. Figure
4
and Supporting Information, Section
A.6
). The goal is to provide a corresponding SMILES, an established string representation of molecular graphs
34
. This task evaluates both spatial and chemical logic of the LLM. It allows to assess whether LLMs understand the relative spatial arrangement of atoms as encoded in a SMILES string, which is critical for structure-property-relationships.

Depicting a molecular graph in an ASCII representation.

When prompted, all tested models are able to explain SMILES of molecules and manipulate them. GtS is implemented as Flask web application. We provide an API allowing to request ASCII drawings, submit predicted SMILES, and collect results. We provide a user interface (UI) to test the game (Figure
5
), available via this
website
.

Discussion

The simple games of
ChildPlay
allow us to assess reasoning and problem-solving abilities of LLMs while minimizing the data contamination issues most other benchmarks suffer from. Our results show that the series of GPT models tested in this work can produce valid suggestions for simple games but often struggles to do so sensibly. In addition, they fail to apply optimal strategies such as blocking winning moves of the opponent or playing winning moves. This reveals severe limitations of these LLMs in strategic reasoning and problem solving, highlighting important areas for future improvement.

Many current LLM benchmarks used to assess LLM performance permit using stochastic patterns instead of genuine reasoning.
In contrast, the tasks in
ChildPlay
test reasoning without relying on language, forcing the models to play the game rather than describe it and thus avoid ”parroting”
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
19
,
20
. Such non-language benchmark tasks challenge models to generalize across different encodings and probe out-of-training-distribution scenarios.

The
ChildPlay
benchmark is most closely related to the ”non-language” category of BigBench, which includes ASCII recognition, chess, and Sudoku, but none like ours in terms of spatial reasoning involving games or construction tasks
11
. Additionally, LCL is related to benchmark tasks developed by Bubeck et al.
7
, where GPT-4 was tasked to generate images using JavaScript or LaTeX. However, unlike these examples, which are prone to training data contamination, the BTC games, LCL, and GtS remain abstract as they rely on new ”languages” or representations, challenging the model to operate outside its learned distributions. Hence, we believe
ChildPlay
is a valuable addition to current benchmarks.

Another related set of benchmarks is provided by the ongoing ARC-AGI competition
39
, which tests systems under a GI definition akin to the one in our introduction (i.e., equivalent to higher-order cognition enabling general problem solving beyond specific skills or knowledge domains). The ARC-AGI results highlight current deep learning limitations as, at the time of writing, open-source models only achieve limited performance, particularly in abstract reasoning. This aligns with our
ChildPlay
findings, where LLMs struggle with strategic reasoning and rule adherence, showing challenges in generalization beyond training data.

Our findings reveal a limitation in current LLM development. Progress in fluency and efficiency does not entail comparable progress in reasoning and strategic decision-making. Modern LLM benchmarks primarily adapt Turing’s test
40
to assess language processing, potentially overlooking deeper complexities. While models like GPT-4 and GPT-4o exhibit what Humboldt described as the ”infinite use of finite means”
41
as popularized by Chomsky
42
, generating contextually relevant sentences
43
, this does not imply reasoning proficiency.

As one might have expected, we observe a systematic improvement in performance across GPT versions. However, this systematic progression is only reflected in some of the benchmark tasks. For instance, both GPT-4 and GPT-4o perform well in simple shape recognition but fail completely in Battleship and show no improvement in Connect-Four. This inconsistency suggests that advancements in model architecture do not necessarily translate into improved generalization capabilities. A possible explanation for this lack of improvement is overfitting. Modern LLMs are increasingly trained on massive, structured datasets, which may enhance their performance on familiar tasks but limit their ability to generalize. This likely explains the strong performance of GPT-4 and GPT-4o in the Shape Recognition Game, which may overlap with training data. This is in strong contrast to their struggles in LCL, Battleship, and GtS.

Given that these LLMs are able to explain details of the SMILES representation and how they connect to molecular structures and the corresponding graphs, we were disappointed by the poor performance of all the tested LLMs in combining spatial reasoning with chemical logic in the GtS benchmark task. Both the low response accuracies and chemical similarity scores indicate that, on average, hardly any of the proposed SMILES is reasonably close to the ASCII depiction of its molecular graph. We believe that this indicates that the tested LLMs are unable to connect changes in molecular connectivity, as represented by the ASCII of the molecular graph, to changes in SMILES strings. However, general purpose LLMs like the GPT series of models have been used previously for predicting molecular properties
44
, conducting inverse design
44
, and guiding reaction optimization
45
. Our results hint at training data contamination and overfitting being likely responsible for these results rather than genuine chemical reasoning.

Limitations

Overall, we pursued benchmarking under zero-shot conditions, but future work should also test Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, Tree-of-Thought (ToT) prompting, and Few-Shot learning
46
,
47
,
1
. These methods reduce individual task complexity and, thus, may improve performance.

Additionally,
ChildPlay
sometimes uses single character output, potentially leading to single character token
myopia
. However, most LLMs like GPT have a multi-character tokenizer
1
. This is problematic when encoding state information as single characters. Furthermore, binary outcomes from our benchmark tasks can bias perceived capabilities. This can misrepresent gradual improvements and cause sharp evaluation changes
21
. Accordingly, the shape recognition and LCL tasks might benefit from continuous metrics, distinguishing smoother performance differences to reflect reasoning abilities more accurately. This is why we incorporated both continuous and discontinuous metrics in the GtS benchmark, where we observe that the continuous chemical similarity score captures more subtle model improvements compared to the response accuracy. This is evident when comparing the results from GPT-4 and GPT-4o. The latter shows significantly higher chemical similarity scores without any improvements in response accuracy.

Moreover, following Schaeffer et al., the
ChildPlay
games may not reflect true generalization or emergent abilities
21
. Benchmarks with nonlinear metrics can exaggerate LLM weaknesses or strengths. For instance, a sharp failure in Battleship might not indicate poor strategy universally but rather under specific conditions. We believe this not to be the case as our benchmarks are simple and fully observable games. We diminish this effect further by evaluating move heatmaps and by also counting illegal moves, missed blocks, and missed wins for more detailed performance evaluation (cf. Supporting Information).

Future
ChildPlay
enhancements could involve feedback loops to reduce task complexity.
Another important aspect is our ASCII representations, which might not convey the board states sufficiently well. More complex symbolic representations might enhance model performance. Furthermore, we plan to benchmark additional models more thoroughly, including open-source LLMs. Our preliminary analysis shows comparable or worse performance relative to GPT-3.5, GPT-4, or GPT-4o (cf. Supporting Information, Section
F
).

Conclusions

With
ChildPlay
, we developed a set of game-based benchmark tasks for LLMs that minimize training data contamination and allow us to critically assess reasoning and problem-solving abilities. Our results reveal that while GPT models are capable of handling many tasks, they fail to generalize consistently across a diverse range of non-linguistic reasoning challenges and often show disappointing problem-solving capabilities. While significant improvements can be observed over model iterations, these gains are not consistent across all tasks. Performance in strategic games, construct generation, and construct recognition remain moderate at best, suggesting difficulties with rule-based and spatial reasoning tasks. The disappointing performance of all the tested LLMs on connecting depictions of molecules to the underlying SMILES representation hints at a lack of understanding chemical structures and their encoding schemes, suggesting any apparent proficiency in that regard likely resulting from overfitting rather than chemical reasoning. The inconsistent performance progression across the various benchmark tasks suggests that improvements in LLMs may be more focused on efficiency and fluency rather than fundamental advancements in reasoning.

The key takeaway of our work is not just the performance of the individual models in specific games. Our research highlights how LLMs handle tasks requiring higher-order reasoning, which is crucial for advanced applications such as scientific research. Practically, if LLMs cannot reason accurately on novel data, they should not be trusted to do so in standard applications. That is, if LLMs already struggle with simple games, users should be more critical of their outputs.

A.1
Generating Games

Our program enables simulation, testing, and analysis of game-play of the different benchmarks found in ChildPlay.

The program begins by setting up variables for debug outputs and game configurations, initializes the game environment based on these configurations, and sets up players accordingly. It then simulates a series of games, saving results and logs in JSON format, and generates heatmaps to analyze gameplay strategies and player decisions. The script also handles results aggregation and visualization, including the generation of bar plots of the results.

Our program is structured to simulate games with various players through a base class and its derived classes, each representing different player types. This class, the PlayerBase class, serves as the foundational class for all player types. It initializes player properties such as ID, name, and a debug flag, and it also manages message storage for debugging or interaction logging. A method is available that can store or print board states and plays based on the debug flag, facilitating debugging and tracking game interactions.

The LLMPlayer class, derived from PlayerBase, represents a player that utilizes a large language model, in our case, this player is either GPT-3.5 or GPT-4. Its constructor initializes the player with an ID, name, model details, and the game being played. It inherits from PlayerBase and extends functionality for LLM interactions. We introduce a method to print messages related to API interactions, and another that formulates a prompt for the LLM, invokes it, and processes its response. Then, a different method interprets the LLM’s response to determine the validity of a move within the game’s context.

The TextPlayer class, also derived from PlayerBase, represents a player who inputs moves via text, through the console - this is a human player. Its constructor initializes player-specific details and a callback function for receiving input. There is a method that manages receiving and validating player inputs according to game rules.

The RandomPlayer class, another derived class from PlayerBase, simulates a player making random valid moves based on the current game state. We have designed a method in this class to generate random possible moves that always comply with the game’s rules given the present game state.

We have two possible game loops, one designed to execute a series of games between two players, and another to simulate one single game. The former takes parameters including the game instance, the two players, the number of games to play, and a debug flag. This function runs a loop for the specified number of games, recording results, tracking messages, and logging game states and moves. It returns a summary of the game results and collected messages for further analysis. The second game loop simulates a single game between two players by initializing the game, managing turn-based gameplay, and logging detailed move information. It returns a tuple with game messages, counts of invalid moves, a log of moves, and the game outcome.

Other functions, all helper functions, enable basic functionality, for example, in producing multiple random moves to generate a dataset, or loading data from a JSON file. We can also create a comprehensive dataset from multiple games and save it to a JSON file, load it, and print the board state for a specific record index. As stated before, we can also generate heatmaps showing the frequency of moves in the case of the board games, or of predicted versus actual shapes in the case of the Shape Recognition Game.

A.2
Tic-Tac-Toe

For the Tic-Tac-Toe task we first initialize the game with optional settings. If no specific options are provided, it defaults to a 3x3 board size. Customizable options include setting the board size and a debug flag. Upon initialization, a method is called to set up or reset the game state. Additionally, it establishes a game name and provides a prompt that explains the rules and how to input moves for an algorithmic explanation of the code used to run Tic-Tac-Toe games).

The
reset_game
method is responsible for resetting the game by creating a board of the specified size, defaulting to 3x3, filled with spaces that represent empty cells. It also initializes the current player as ”P1”, sets up a list to track moves made, and marks the game status as not over.

The
get_text_state
method constructs a text-based or ASCII representation of the current game state. It displays column and row headers for the board and formats the board with dividers to clearly delineate rows and columns.

The guess method manages a player’s move by first validating it to ensure it falls within the board boundaries and that the chosen position is not already occupied. If the move is valid, it updates the board with the player’s symbol (’X’ for player index 0 and ’O’ for player index 1). After each valid move, the method checks for a win or a tie. If neither condition is met, it switches the turn to the next player. If a player makes a wrong move they lose the game.

The
check_win
method determines if the current player has won by checking all possible winning conditions. This includes vertical, horizontal, and diagonal lines. It returns True if any of these conditions are met with consecutive symbols matching the current player’s.

The
check_tie
method checks for a tie by determining if all cells are filled without any player achieving a winning condition.

A
switch_player
method is available that alternates turns between the two players, switching control from ”P1” to ”P2” after each valid move.

A.3
Connect-Four

The Connect-Four constructor initializes the game with optional settings as well, such as row and column size and debug mode. If no specific options are provided, the game defaults to a 7x7 grid. Otherwise, it reads the values from the provided options dictionary. The constructor sets up basic game properties, including a reset board, a tuple to track the last move, the game over status, and the current player. It also includes a game instruction prompt.

The
reset_board
method resets the board to a 7x7 grid (or a custom size if specified) filled with dots (.) to represent empty cells.

The
check_tie
method determines if the game has ended in a tie. It checks if the top row of the board is completely filled without any winner being declared. This method ensures that the
check_win
method returns False before declaring a tie.

The
check_win
method assesses whether a winning condition has been met after a move. It examines the cell corresponding to the last move and checks horizontally, vertically, and diagonally (in both positive and negative directions) for four consecutive identical symbols (either ’X’ or ’O’).

The guess method handles a player’s move attempt. It returns an invalid move if the attempt is made outside of valid columns or on a full column. For a valid move, it updates the board, checks for a win or a tie, and switches to the next player if the game is still ongoing.

The
get_text_state
method generates a string representation of the current board state, displaying ’X’ and ’O’ for the two players.

The
switch_player
method alternates the active player between ”P1” (Player 1) and ”P2” (Player 2).

The
board_size
property returns the number of columns in the board, which is necessary for validation and display purposes.

A.4
Battleship

The Battleship constructor initializes, once again, the game with optional settings. If no options are provided, it defaults to a 5x5 board size. The constructor allows customization of the board size and a debug flag. It sets up four game boards for each player—two for ship placement and two for tracking guesses—using nested list comprehensions. Additionally, it initializes lists to keep track of the ship positions for both players, 2 lists each, 4 in total - these correspond to the players own boards which they can see both the ships and hits, and their adversaries, where they can see only their own hits and misses. A dictionary,
ship_types
, defines ship names and their sizes, which scale according to the board size. The
place_ships
method is then called to randomly place ships on each player’s board. Initial game properties are also set, including the current player and a game prompt that explains the rules.

The board property dynamically returns the guess board of the current player, in case we want to display it or carry out logic checks.

The
place_ships
method is responsible for randomly placing ships on a specified board. It attempts to place each ship either vertically or horizontally without overlapping or touching another ship, including diagonally adjacent spaces.

The
is_space_free
method checks if a specified space on the board is free to accommodate a ship of a given size and orientation. It ensures that there is no overlap with existing ships and that the ship is not placed directly adjacent to another ship.

The
get_text_state
method provides a string representation of both the ship and guess boards for a specified player, formatted for easy reading. This functionality is useful for displaying the game state in a text-based interface.

The guess method processes a player’s guess by checking if it hits a ship and updating the boards accordingly. It also switches the player after each guess and checks for a win condition. Hits are marked with ’X’ and misses with ’O’ on the guess and ship boards.

The
check_win
method determines if the current player has won by checking if all positions of the opponent’s ships have been hit.

The
switch_player
method alternates turns between the two players after each guess.

A.5
Shape Recognition

The Shape Recognition Game starts by defining two constants,
empty_character
and
full_character
, which are represented by ”0” and ”1” respectively. These constants denote empty and filled cells in the grid.

One of the primary functions in the script is
bar_plot_shapes
, which generates bar plots illustrating the counts of correct and incorrect answers for each shape. This function takes several parameters, including
base_path
for saving plots, models which is a list of GPT-type models used in the game, temperatures which refers to different temperature settings in the GPT-model predictions, and shapes representing the various shapes used in the game. The function iterates over the models and temperatures, loads game logs, counts the correct and incorrect answers, and plots these counts in bar charts. The resulting plots are saved to specified directories.

The script also includes several shape-drawing functions. The
create_board
function initializes an empty board of specified dimensions filled with
empty_character
. The
draw_rectangle
function allows drawing a rectangle or square on the board by filling cells with
full_character
. The
draw_circle
function draws an approximate circle using the midpoint circle algorithm. The
draw_triangle
function draws an equilateral triangle on the board by filling cells within the triangle’s bounds. The
draw_cross
function draws a cross on the board with a specified arm length centered at a given point.

A key component of the script is the
Shapes
class, which manages the game’s logic. This class initializes the game with optional settings such as board size and debug mode. It sets the game name, the shape to be drawn, and a prompt explaining the game rules. The
reset_game
method creates a new board and randomly draws the specified shape (square, triangle, or cross) on it. It shuffles possible answer options and resets game state variables. The
get_text_state
method generates a text-based representation of the game board and possible answers for display. The
guess
method processes a player’s guess, checks if it matches the correct shape, and ends the game, returning the outcome (win or loss). Methods
check_win
,
check_tie
, and
check_loss
are used to determine the game’s outcome.

The script supports both visualization and analysis of game outcomes. The
bar_plot_shapes
function helps visualize the correctness of guesses. The shape-drawing functions facilitate the random generation of different shapes for each new session. The Shapes class encapsulates the game’s logic, managing the board state, processing guesses, and determining game outcomes, thus supporting a single-player interaction where the player guesses the shape drawn on the board from a list of options.

A.6
Guess-the-SMILES (GtS)

The hidden experiment, GtS, is a combination of two distinct parts: a Flask web application that interacts with a molecule generation and evaluation system, and a benchmarking script that tests different AI models’ performance on predicting SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) strings from molecular representations.

The generated molecules can be visualized as an ASCII print or a PNG image using functions like print_mol_ascii and draw_mol_coordgen. These representations provide users or models with a visual way to infer the SMILES string. Users can submit a predicted SMILES string for a given molecule, which the application evaluates using both chemical similarity, via molecular fingerprints, and string similarity, via Levenshtein distance. The evaluate_prediction endpoint compares the predicted SMILES with the original, returning whether it is correct, along with similarity metrics.

The Flask app serves an HTML page where users can interact with the system by generating molecules, viewing their ASCII representations, and submitting predictions. The benchmarking script is designed to evaluate the performance of various AI models in predicting the correct SMILES string from molecular ASCII representations. Models and temperature settings are defined and can be used as hyper-parameters to influence the AI’s predictions.

The results of these experiments are saved to CSV files for further analysis.

The data in the final results Table (cf. Main Text) was gathered by evaluating the performance of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 language models, under different temperature settings (t=0, t=0.5, t=1, t=1.5). The goal was to assess each model’s ability to correctly predict the SMILES ASCII string representation of a molecule given an ASCII depiction of its structure.

The process was repeated 100 times for each model and temperature setting. The data includes counts of correct and incorrect predictions, specific counts of incorrect SMILES, as well as computed accuracy, average chemical similarity, and average string distance.

The script to reproduce the experiment is available through the
ChildPlay GitHub Repository
.

A.7
Lego Connect Language (LCL)

In the LCL script, we enable validating, and visualizing Lego-like constructs with different types of players.

We start by defining LCLVisualizer, which handles the visualization of Lego constructs. Its methods include
draw_piece
, which draws a single Lego piece on a plot, and
display_construct
, which displays and saves a complete Lego construct.

The LCLGame class manages the logic for generating and validating Lego constructs. It contains attributes such as pieces, a list of tuples representing Lego pieces,
valid_colors
, a list of valid colors for these pieces, and metrics, a dictionary for tracking validation metrics and construct details. Its methods include
can_place_piece
, which determines if a Lego piece can be placed at a given position,
find_all_valid_positions
, which identifies all valid positions for placing new pieces, and
build_random_valid_assembly
, which constructs a valid Lego assembly by placing pieces in valid positions. Additionally,
generate_random_piece
and
generate_random_construct
are methods for generating random Lego pieces and constructs, respectively. The
is_valid_construct
method checks the validity of a given construct, while
generate_valid_or_invalid_construct
creates either a valid or invalid construct based on a given parameter. The class also includes methods like
create_tower
,
create_bridge
, and
create_staircase
for generating specific types of constructs, and
validate_construct
, which validates a player’s response regarding the construct’s validity. Finally,
save_metrics
saves the validation metrics to a file.

The RandomPlayer class represents a player that generates random answers. Its primary method, generate_random_answer, returns a random validity answer, either ”valid” or ”invalid.”

The LLMPlayer class is designed for the GPT models to generate answers. It has attributes like model, which specifies the GPT-model type used, and temperature, which sets the temperature for the model’s responses. Its methods include
generate_llm_answer_validity

generate_llm_answer_list

The main function orchestrates the simulation of games, including the generation of constructs, validation, and saving of results. It initializes both the game and visualizer, creates directories for saving experiment results, and defines the number of experiments, models, and temperatures. The function conducts two main types of experiments:

Validity Testing: In this experiment, constructs are generated (either valid or invalid), and players (either random or LLM) are asked to validate them. The results and visualizations of these constructs are saved.

Construct Generation: Here, LLM players are prompted to generate valid constructs based on a given prompt. These constructs are then validated, and results and visualizations are recorded.

The results of these experiments are saved to CSV files for further analysis.

The product of these experiments differs slighlty per type of game. We collected results (see Table
S2

S1

In the case of LCL, we save the results directly to two dataframes. For the construct experiment we record the model temperature, the model type, the experiment number, if the output is valid or not, the LLM’s answer, and if the response is true or false. In the case of the validity experiment, we record the temperature, the model, the experiment number, if the output is valid or not, if the LLM’s response is correct or incorrect, and the actual LCL construct.

B.5.1
Incorrect Move Probability

The probability of incorrect moves
P_{\text{incorrect}}
as a function of temperature is given by:

P_{\text{incorrect}}(T)=\frac{\text{Incorrect Moves at }T}{\text{Total Moves}}

For Tic-Tac-Toe, Connect Four, and Battleship, the results are as follows:

P_{\text{incorrect, TTT}}=\left[4.7,7.6,7.6,8.1\right]

P_{\text{incorrect, CF}}=\left[1.8,1.0,0.65,0.6\right]

P_{\text{incorrect, BS}}=\left[3.583,3.708,4.042,3.875\right]

These values indicate that the probability of incorrect moves varies across different games as the temperature changes. For Tic-Tac-Toe, the probability of incorrect moves increases with temperature, suggesting that higher temperatures may lead to less optimal decisions. In Connect Four, however, the probability of incorrect moves decreases as temperature increases, indicating a possible improvement in decision-making or adaptation to the conditions. In Battleship, the probability fluctuates but remains relatively stable.

Average Number of Steps
The average number of steps
\langle S\rangle
as a function of temperature is calculated by averaging the steps observed at different temperatures:

\langle S\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Steps at }T_{i}

For Tic-Tac-Toe, Connect Four, and Battleship, the average number of steps are as follows:

\langle S_{\text{TTT}}\rangle=2.5

\langle S_{\text{CF}}\rangle=5.0

\langle S_{\text{BS}}\rangle=6.0

These values show that the average number of steps taken in each game tends to stabilize regardless of temperature changes. This suggests that while the probability of making incorrect moves may fluctuate with temperature, the number of steps taken does not vary significantly.

Tic-Tac-Toe

Tic-Tac-Toe is a two-player game played on a 3x3 grid. Each player takes turns marking a square with their symbol (X or O), aiming to get three of their symbols in a row, column, or diagonal. To play optimally, prioritize securing the center square and blocking opponent’s winning moves.

Battleship

Battleship is a two-player game where players hide ships on a grid and take turns guessing their opponent’s ship locations. The goal is to sink all of the opponent’s ships. To play optimally, start by targeting areas with higher probabilities of containing a ship and strategically target adjacent squares after a hit to maximize efficiency.

Connect Four

Connect Four is a two-player game played on a 6x7 grid. Players drop colored discs into columns, aiming to connect four of their own discs in a row, column, or diagonal. To play optimally, prioritize creating your own winning formations while blocking opponent’s potential winning moves.
