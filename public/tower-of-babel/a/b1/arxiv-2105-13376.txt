Quantum to classical crossover in many-body chaos and scrambling from relaxation in a glass
Surajit Bera 1 , Venkata Lokesh K. Y 2 and Sumilan Banerjee 1

Abstract
Chaotic quantum systems with Lyapunov exponent λ L \lambda_{\mathrm{L}} obey an upper bound λ L ≤ 2 ​ π ​ k B ​ T / ℏ \lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\leq 2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}T/\hbar at temperature T T , implying a divergence of the bound in the classical limit ℏ → 0 \hbar\to 0 . Following this trend, does a quantum system necessarily become ‘more chaotic’ when quantum fluctuations are reduced? Moreover, how do symmetry breaking and associated non-trivial dynamics influence the interplay of quantum mechanics and chaos? We explore these questions by computing λ L ​ ( ℏ , T ) \lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(\hbar,T) in the quantum spherical p p -spin glass model, where ℏ \hbar can be continuously varied. We find that quantum fluctuations, in general, make paramagnetic phase less and the replica symmetry-broken spin glass phase more chaotic. We show that the approach to the classical limit could be non-trivial, with non-monotonic dependence of λ L \lambda_{\mathrm{L}} on ℏ \hbar close to the dynamical glass transition temperature T d T_{d} . Our results in the classical limit ( ℏ → 0 \hbar\to 0 ) naturally describe chaos in super-cooled liquid in structural glasses. We find a maximum in λ L ​ ( T ) \lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(T) substantially above T d T_{d} , concomitant with the crossover from simple to slow glassy relaxation. We further show that λ L ∼ T α \lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\sim T^{\alpha} , with the exponent α \alpha varying between 2 and 1 from quantum to classical limit, at low temperatures in the spin glass phase.

Conclusions.—
In this work, we have shown how quantum mechanics influences chaos in a solvable quantum spin glass model.
We derive relation between chaos and relaxation rates in the complex glassy regime above the glass transition. So far, such direct relation between scrambling and relaxation has only been established for weakly interacting systems
Aleiner et al. 2016
;
Stanford 2016
;
Banerjee and Altman 2017
;
Patel et al. 2017
. In future, studying the connection between many-body chaos and relaxation in simulation

Dobrosavljevic and Thirumalai 1990
;
Nieuwenhuizen and Ritort 1998
;
Obuchi et al. 2007
;
Nieuwenhuizen and Ritort 1998

S1
Imaginary-time path integral and saddle point equations

The imaginary time path-integral and saddle point equations for
p
-spin glass model are discussed in Refs.
Cugliandolo et al. 2000
;
Cugliandolo et al. 2001
. Here we sketch the basic steps of the calculations. The thermodynamic properties of the model [Eq.(
1
)] is obtained from the free-energy,

\displaystyle F=-k_{B}T\overline{\ln Z},

(S1.1)

where the overline denotes disorder average over configuration
\{J^{(p)}_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}\}
of the random
p
-spin couplings and
T
is the temperature of the system. We use the replica trick,
\overline{\ln Z}=\lim_{n\rightarrow 0}(\overline{Z^{n}}-1)/n
, to obtain disorder averaged free energy. The replicated imaginary-time path integral is obtained as

\displaystyle Z^{n}

\displaystyle=\int\left[\prod_{a}\mathcal{D}s_{a}(\tau)\delta\left(\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i,a}(\tau)-N\right)\right]\exp\bigg[-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{\beta\hbar}_{0}d\tau\bigg(\sum_{i,a}\frac{M}{2}\left(\frac{\partial s_{i,a}}{\partial\tau}\right)^{2}+\sum_{p,i_{1}<...<i_{p},a}J^{(p)}_{i_{1}...i_{p}}s_{i_{1},a}(\tau)...s_{i_{p},a}(\tau)\bigg)\bigg]

(S1.2)

where
a=1,\dots,n
is the replica index and the
\delta
-function in the above imposes the spherical constraint. We write the
\delta
-function as
\int\mathcal{D}z_{a}(\tau)\exp\big[-\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}z_{a}(\tau)\big(\sum_{i,a}s^{2}_{i,a}(\tau)-N\big)\big]
.
After averaging over disorder, introducing bi-local order parameter field
Q_{ab}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=(1/N)\sum_{i}s_{i,a}(\tau)s_{i,b}(\tau^{\prime})
and integrating out the fields
\{s_{ia}(\tau)\}
, we obtain an effective action in terms of
Q_{ab}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\overline{Z^{n}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}Q_{ab}\exp\big[-{S_{\text{eff}}[Q_{ab}]}/{\hbar}\big].

(S1.3)

For the equilibrium saddle point
Q_{ab}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=Q_{ab}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
and we use Matsubara frequencies,
\omega_{k}=2\pi kT
with integers
k
, to write down the effective action as

\displaystyle-\frac{S_{\text{eff}}}{\hbar}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\sum_{k}\textup{Tr}\ln[(\beta\hbar)^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q}(\omega_{k})]+\frac{N}{2}\sum_{k,a,b}\bigg[\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{\hbar}(M{\omega^{2}_{k}}+z)\delta_{ab}Q_{ab}(\omega_{k})\bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{J^{2}N\beta}{4\hbar}\sum_{a,b}\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}d\tau\bigg[\frac{1}{\beta\hbar}\sum_{k}\exp\big(-\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}\tau\big)Q_{ab}(\omega_{k})\bigg]^{p}+\frac{Nn\beta}{2}z

(S1.4)

where
z_{a}(\tau)=z
and
Q_{ab}(\omega_{k})=\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}d\tau\exp(\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}\tau)Q_{ab}(\tau)
.
For
N\rightarrow\infty
limit the following saddle-point equation is obtained by minimizing the above effective action as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\big(M\omega^{2}_{k}+z\big)\delta_{ab}

\displaystyle=\big(Q^{-1}\big)_{ab}(\omega_{k})+\sum_{p}\frac{J^{2}_{p}p}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\beta\hbar}d\tau\exp(\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}\tau)[Q_{ab}(\tau)]^{p-1}

(S1.5)

We work with the dimensionless quantities -
\tilde{J_{p}}=J_{p}/J
, energy and temperature in units of
J
, and time
\tilde{\tau}=J\tau/\hbar
and frequency
\tilde{\omega_{k}}=\omega_{k}\hbar/J
, to obtain

\displaystyle\big(Q^{-1}\big)_{ab}(\tilde{\omega}_{k})

\displaystyle=Q^{-1}_{0}(\tilde{\omega}_{k})\delta_{ab}-\Sigma_{ab}(\tilde{\omega}_{k})

(S1.6)

where

\displaystyle Q^{-1}_{0}(\tilde{\omega}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\omega}^{2}_{k}}{\Gamma}+\tilde{z},\hskip 14.22636pt\Gamma=\hbar^{2}/MJ

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ab}(\tilde{\omega}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\frac{\tilde{J}_{p}^{2}p}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tilde{\tau}\exp(\mathfrak{i}\tilde{\omega}_{k}\tilde{\tau})[Q_{ab}(\tilde{\tau})]^{p-1}

(S1.7)

with
\tilde{z}=z/J
. The quantum fluctuation parameter
\hbar
enters through the parameter
\Gamma=\hbar^{2}/MJ
in the saddle point equation.
From here on, we work with these dimensionless variables and, to simplify the notation, we omit the hat from the symbols.

S1.1
Saddle point equation and spectral function calculation in the PM phase

In the PM phase, the order parameter is replica symmetric and diagonal, i.e.
Q_{ab}(\omega_{k})=Q(\omega_{k})\delta_{ab}
. So, the saddle point Eqs.(
S1.6
) and (
S1.7
) in the PM phase, e.g., for
p=3
spin glass model simplify to

\displaystyle Q^{-1}(\omega_{k})=\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{\Gamma}+z-\Sigma(\omega_{k})

(S1.8a)

\displaystyle\Sigma(\tau)=\frac{3\tilde{J_{3}}^{2}}{2}[Q(\tau)]^{2}

(S1.8b)

To obtain the spectral function, we solve the above equations numerically after analytical continuation from Matsubara to real frequency i.e
\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}\rightarrow\omega+\mathfrak{i}0^{+}
, i.e. in terms of the retarded functions

\left(Q^{R}(\omega)\right)^{-1}=-\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{\Gamma}+z-\Sigma^{R}(\omega)

(S1.9)

where
\Sigma_{R}(\omega)
is obtained from
\Sigma(\omega_{k})
, i.e.

\Sigma(\omega_{k})=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau e^{\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}\tau}\Sigma(\tau)=\frac{3\tilde{J_{3}}^{2}}{2\beta}\sum_{k}Q(\omega_{k})Q(\omega_{n}-\omega_{k})

(S1.10)

We perform the Matsubara summation using the spectral representation
Q(\omega_{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\rho(\omega)/(\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}-\omega)
, where
\rho(\omega)=-\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega)/\pi
is the spectral function. After analytical continuation, we obtain

\Sigma^{R}(\omega)=\frac{3\tilde{J}_{3}^{2}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\rho(\omega_{1})\rho(\omega_{2})\frac{n_{\mathrm{B}}(-\omega_{1})n_{\mathrm{B}}(-\omega_{2})-n_{\mathrm{B}}(\omega_{1})n_{\mathrm{B}}(\omega_{2})}{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega-\mathfrak{i}0^{+}}.

(S1.11)

Here
n_{\mathrm{B}}(\omega)=1/(e^{\beta\omega}-1)
is the Bose function. To numerically evaluate the self-energy
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)
efficiently, we use the identity
1/(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+\mathfrak{i}0^{+})=-\mathfrak{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{\mathfrak{i}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+\mathfrak{i}0^{+})t}
to rewrite the above equation as follows

\Sigma^{R}(\omega)=\mathfrak{i}\tilde{J_{3}}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{\mathfrak{i}\omega t}[n_{1}(t)^{2}-n_{2}(t)^{2}],

(S1.12)

where

n_{1}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega e^{-\mathfrak{i}\omega t}\rho(\omega)n_{B}(-\omega)

and

n_{2}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega e^{-\mathfrak{i}\omega t}\rho(\omega)n_{B}(\omega)
.

The Lagrange multiplier
z
in Eq.(
S1.9
) can be determined either from the imaginary-time calculation using Eqs.(
S1.8
) subjected to the spherical constraint
Q_{aa}(\tau=0)=1
, or we can determine
z
from real frequency calculation itself. To determine
z
from real frequency calculation, we can vary
z
and the correct
z
will give physical solution which satisfies the spherical constraint expressed in terms of the spectral function,

-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\rho(\omega)n_{B}(\omega)=1.

(S1.13)

We use the above sum rule condition Eq.(
S1.13
) to check the accuracy of the spectral function obtained numerically by iterating the saddle-point equations.

We solve the self-consistent saddle point equations (
S1.9
), (
S1.12
) by discretizing over frequency (
\omega
) and starting with some initial guesses for Lagrange multiplier
z
and
Q^{R}(\omega)
, e.g. the non-interacting retarded function for
J_{3}=0
.
We calculate the retarded self-energy
\Sigma^{R}(\omega)
and
n_{i}(t)
,
i=1,2
using fast-Fourier transform (FFT) and iterate for
Q^{R}(\omega)
until convergence with a required numerical accuracy. We repeat this process by varying

S1.13
) is satisfied. We find that the process is much more efficient if we split

as

S1.2
Saddle point equation in the SG phase

In the quantum spherical
p
-spin glass model, there are two types of SG phases – a thermodynamic SG phase and a marginal SG phase. A detailed discussion can be found in Refs.
Cugliandolo et al. 2000
;
Cugliandolo et al. 2001
. Here we briefly discuss these two different SG phases for completeness and then mainly focus on the saddle-point solution for the marginal SG phase.

The replica-symmetric spin-glass phase, unlike that for
p=2

Kosterlitz et al. 1976
, is unstable for
p\geq 3
and one needs to consider the replica symmetry breaking. In this model, one step replica symmetry (1-RSB) breaking is exact
Cugliandolo et al. 2001
and the order parameter in the imaginary time is given by

Q_{ab}(\tau)=(q_{d}(\tau)-q_{\text{EA}})\delta_{ab}+q_{\text{EA}}\epsilon_{ab}

(S1.14)

where
q_{\text{EA}}
is Edward-Anderson order parameter and
\epsilon_{ab}=1
for the diagonal blocks and zero otherwise, as described in the main text also. The above 1-RSB order parameter in Matsubara frequency reads as

Q_{ab}(\omega_{k})=(q_{d}(\omega_{k})-\tilde{q}_{{EA}})\delta_{ab}+\tilde{q}_{{EA}}\epsilon_{ab},

(S1.15)

where
\tilde{q}_{{EA}}=\beta q_{\text{EA}}\delta_{\omega_{k},0}
and
q_{d}(\omega_{k})
is Matsubara Fourier transformation of
q_{d}(\tau)=Q_{aa}(\tau)
. Now, it is convenient to write
q_{d}(\tau)=q_{\text{reg}}(\tau)+q_{\text{EA}}
.
The inverse matrix
\boldsymbol{Q^{-1}}(\omega_{k})
has the following structure

\big(Q^{-1}\big)_{ab}(\omega_{k})=A(\omega_{k})\delta_{ab}+B(\omega_{k})\epsilon_{ab}

(S1.16)

with

A(\omega_{k})=\frac{1}{q_{d}(\omega_{k})-\tilde{q}_{{EA}}},\hskip 22.76228ptB(\omega_{k})=\frac{-\tilde{q}_{{EA}}}{q_{d}(\omega_{k})^{2}-(m-1)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}^{2}+(m-2)q_{d}(\omega_{k})\tilde{q}_{{EA}}}.

(S1.17)

Here
m
is the break point
Cugliandolo et al. 2001
.
The diagonal element of the inverse matrix (
\boldsymbol{Q^{-1}}
) is given by

(Q^{-1})_{aa}=A(\omega_{k})+B(\omega_{k})=\frac{q_{d}(\omega_{k})+(m-2)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}}{(q_{d}(\omega_{k})^{2}+(m-2)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}q_{d}(\omega_{k})-(m-1)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}^{2})}.

(S1.18)

The off-diagonal element (
a\neq b
) is

\big(Q^{-1}\big)_{ab}(\omega_{k})=B(\omega_{k}).

(S1.19)

The saddle-point equation for diagonal component
q_{d}(\omega_{k})
can be obtained by using equations (
S1.6
) and (
S1.18
) as

\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{\Gamma}+z=\frac{q_{d}(\omega_{k})+(m-2)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}}{(q_{d}(\omega_{k})^{2}+(m-2)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}q_{d}(\omega_{k})-(m-1)\tilde{q}_{{EA}}^{2})}+\Sigma(\omega_{k}),

(S1.20)

with
\Sigma(\omega_{k})=\Sigma_{aa}(\omega_{k})
, the Fourier transform of
\Sigma_{aa}(\tau)=\frac{\tilde{J}_{p}^{2}p}{2}[q_{d}(\tau)]^{p-1}
.
Using equations (
S1.6
) and (
S1.19
), the saddle point equation for
q_{\text{EA}}
is obtained from

0=-\frac{q_{\text{EA}}}{q_{d}(0)^{2}-(m-1)\beta^{2}q_{\text{EA}}^{2}+(m-2)\beta q_{d}(0)q_{\text{EA}}}+\frac{p}{2}q_{\text{EA}}^{p-1}.

(S1.21)

It is convenient to separate out constant and
\tau-
dependent part of
q_{d}(\tau)
and self-energy
\Sigma_{aa}(\tau)
. So, we write these as below

q_{\text{reg}}(\tau)=q_{d}(\tau)-q_{\text{EA}},\hskip 19.91684pt\Sigma_{\text{reg}}(\tau)=\frac{\tilde{J}_{p}^{2}p}{2}[q_{d}(\tau)]^{p-1}-\frac{\tilde{J}_{p}^{2}p}{2}q_{\text{EA}}^{p-1}.

(S1.22)

Using saddle point Eq.(
S1.20
), we can rewrite Eq. (
S1.20
) as follows

\frac{\omega^{2}_{k}}{\Gamma}+z=\frac{1}{q_{\text{reg}}(\omega_{k})}+\Sigma_{\text{reg}}(\omega_{k})+\bigg[\frac{-\beta q_{\text{EA}}\delta_{\omega_{k},0}}{q_{d}(\omega_{k})^{2}-(m-1)q_{\text{EA}}^{2}\beta^{2}\delta_{\omega_{k},0}+(m-2)q_{d}(\omega_{k})q_{\text{EA}}\beta\delta_{\omega_{k},0}}+\frac{p}{2}q_{\text{EA}}^{p-1}\beta q_{\text{EA}}\delta_{\omega_{k},0}\bigg]\\

The term inside the third bracket above is zero due to Eq.(
S1.21
), and we obtain the simplified saddle point equation

\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{\Gamma}+z=\frac{1}{q_{\text{reg}}(\omega_{k})}+\Sigma_{\text{reg}}(\omega_{k}),

(S1.23)

where

\Sigma_{\text{reg}}(\omega_{k})=\frac{\tilde{J}^{2}_{p}p}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau e^{\mathfrak{i}\omega_{k}\tau}\times(q_{d}^{p-1}(\tau)-q_{\text{EA}}^{p-1}).

(S1.24)

Moreover, it is convenient to split the Lagrange multiplier
z
as
z=z^{\prime}+\Sigma_{\text{reg}}(\omega_{k}=0)
with

z^{\prime}\hskip 7.11317pt=\hskip 7.11317pt\frac{1}{q_{d}(0)(1-y)}=\frac{p}{2}\beta mq_{\text{EA}}^{p-1}\frac{1+x_{p}}{x_{p}},

(S1.25)

where
y={\beta q_{\text{EA}}}/{q_{d}(0)}
and
x_{p}={my}/{(1-y)}
. The saddle point equation [Eq.(
S1.20
)] for the Edward-Anderson parameter
q_{\text{EA}}
can be rewritten as

\frac{p(\beta m)^{2}}{2}q_{\text{EA}}^{p}=\frac{x_{p}^{2}}{1+x_{p}}.

(S1.26)

In the thermodynamic SG phase, the break point
m
is determined by extremizing the free-energy functional
F[m]

Cugliandolo et al. 2001
, leading to

\ln\bigg[\frac{1}{1+x_{p}}\bigg]+\frac{x_{p}}{1+x_{p}}+\frac{x_{p}^{2}}{p(1+x_{p})}=0.

(S1.27)

On the other hand, in the marginal SG phase, the marginal stability condition

\Lambda_{T}
is given by

\Lambda_{T}=\beta^{2}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{q_{d}(0)-\beta q_{\text{EA}}}\bigg)^{2}-\frac{p(p-1)}{2}q_{\text{EA}}^{p-2}\bigg]

(S1.28)

Setting

S2
Real-time Schwinger-Keldysh contour for OTOC for paramagnetic and spin glass phase

Here we briefly discuss the Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) formulation for the disorder averaged regularized OTOC
Maldacena et al. 2016
,

\displaystyle F(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{ij}\overline{\mathrm{Tr}[ys_{i}(t_{1})ys_{j}(0)ys_{i}(t_{2})ys_{j}(0)]},

(S2.34)

where
y^{4}=\exp(-\beta H)/\mathrm{Tr}[\exp(-\beta H)]
. The above correlation function can be formulated as a many-body path integral
Kamenev 2011
on a Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) contour with four real-time branches that are separated by
\beta/4
in the imaginary-time
Maldacena and Stanford 2016
;
Stanford 2016
;
Banerjee and Altman 2017
, namely by writing the path integral representation for the following generating function

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H/4}U(t_{0},t_{f})e^{-\beta H/4}U(t_{f},t_{0})e^{-\beta H/4}U(t_{0},t_{f})e^{-\beta H/4}U(t_{f},t_{0})\right].

(S2.35)

Here
Z=\mathrm{Tr}[\exp(-\beta H)]
is the equilibrium partition function and
U(t,t_{0})=\mathcal{T}_{t}\exp[-(\mathfrak{i}/\hbar)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}H(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}]
is the time evolution operator with
\mathcal{T}
denoting time ordering;
t_{0}
is the initial and
t_{f}
some final time. In the Keldysh formalism, typically the disorder averaging can be performed without introducing replicas
Kamenev 2011
. This procedure can be used to describe the real-time dynamics and compute dynamical correlations such as OTOC, e.g., in the PM phase of the quantum
p
-spin glass model. However, to obtain the dynamical correlations in the SG phase, one needs to consider the actual physical situation, e.g., the initial condition or the initial density matrix more carefully
Houghton et al. 1983
;
Cugliandolo et al. 2019
. For instance, to have a clear physical situation in mind, one can weakly couple the system at
t_{0}
with a bath at temperature
T
, while the systems is at a higher temperature
>T
, and let the system cool down to the temperature
T

Cugliandolo et al. 2001
in the presence of bath. In this case the system could be equilibrated up to
T\gtrsim T_{d}
in the PM phase. Hence, to compute the OTOC in the PM phase above
T_{d}
, we can directly disorder average the generating function
\mathcal{Z}
[Eq.(
S2.35
)] and take
t_{0}\to-\infty
and
t_{f}\to\infty
limits and vanishing coupling with the bath. In this way, the correlation and retarded functions become time-translational invariant and identical to the equilibrium ones obtained by solving the saddle-point equations in the PM phase in Sec.
S1.1
.

Below
T_{d}
, while getting cooled in the presence of the bath, the system enters the so-called aging regime where, e.g., the spin-spin correlation
C(t_{w},t_{w}+t)
depends on the waiting time
t_{w}
even for asymptotically large
t_{w}
. However, by formally taking
t_{w}\to\infty
and then coupling to the bath to zero in the saddle-point equations on the SK contour
Cugliandolo et al. 2001
, one arrives at solutions identical to the 1-RSB mSG in Sec.
S1.3
. Hence, to compute OTOC using the SK contour of Fig.
S1
, rather than taking the above more complicated route of taking
t_{w}\to\infty
and then the coupling to bath to zero, one can simply replicate
Houghton et al. 1983
;
Cugliandolo et al. 2019
the generating function
\mathcal{Z}
and take
t_{0}\to-\infty,~t_{f}\to\infty
limits, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{n}

S2.2
Numerical diagonalization of kernel

We can view Eq.(
S2.43
)) as an eigenvalue equation,
\int\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}K_{a}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})f_{a}(\omega^{\prime})=\lambda f_{a}(\omega)
, with eigenvalue
\lambda=1
.
The kernel Eq.(
S2.44
) is not symmetric with respect to frequencies (
\omega
,
\omega^{\prime}
). We symmetrize the kernel using particle-hole symmetry of the Hamiltonian. Using this symmetry it is easy to show that

Q^{R}_{aa}\left(\omega+\frac{\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)Q^{R}_{aa}\left(-\omega+\frac{\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)=\left|Q^{R}_{aa}\left(\omega+\frac{\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)\right|^{2}

(S2.45)

We can now define a symmetric kernel by defining
f_{a}(\omega)=|Q^{R}_{aa}(\omega+\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}/2)|\tilde{f}_{a}(\omega)
and obtain the symmetric kernel as

\tilde{K_{a}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=3J^{2}\left|Q^{R}_{aa}\left(\omega+\frac{\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)\right|{Q^{W}_{aa}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}\left|Q^{R}_{aa}\left(\omega^{\prime}+\frac{\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)\right|

(S2.46)

Hence the eigenvalue equation becomes

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\tilde{K_{a}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\tilde{f}_{a}(\omega^{\prime})=\lambda\tilde{f}_{a}(\omega)

(S2.47)

To solve the above eigenvalue equation we first discretize frequency, namely
\omega_{n}
,
n
being integer, ranging from
-\omega_{\text{max}}
to
\omega_{\text{max}}
with spacing
2\omega_{\text{max}}/N_{\omega}
, where
N_{\omega}
is total number of discretized frequency points and
\omega_{\mathrm{max}}
is a upper frequency cutoff. We diagonalize the matrix
\tilde{K}_{a}
and find the value of
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
such that
\tilde{K}_{a}
has at least has one eigenvalue
\lambda=1
.

In this section we discuss the evolution spectral function as a function of

For bosonic systems

S4.4
Perturbative analysis in the mSG phase

We have shown in Sections
S1.3
and
S2.1
that the saddle point equations and the kernel equation for OTOC in the mSG phase have forms similar to those in the PM phase of the model [Eq.(
1
)] with
p=2
and
p=3
terms with couplings
J_{2}=\sqrt{3q_{\text{EA}}}J
and
J_{3}=J
. Here, we obtain
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(T)
by treating the
p=3
term as perturbation around the
p=2
saddle point in the low-temperature quantum limit
T\ll\Gamma
. The analysis is similar to that done in the Fermi liquid (FL) phase
Kim and Cao 2020
;
Kim et al. 2020
of a large-
N
fermionic model related to the SYK model. The perturbative analysis is well controlled, even though the ratio
J_{3}/J_{2}=\sqrt{3q_{\text{EA}}}
is not necessarily small, since the
p=3
spin interaction term is irrelevant at low energies.

The kernel equation for the PM phase of the
p=2+p=3
model can be written as

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=K_{1}K_{2}

(S4.49)

\displaystyle K_{2}f(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left|Q^{R}\left(\omega+\frac{\mathfrak{i}\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)\right|^{2}f(\omega)

(S4.50)

\displaystyle K_{1}f(t)

\displaystyle=[J_{2}^{2}+3J_{3}^{2}Q^{W}(t)]f(t)

(S4.51)

where
Q^{R}
and
Q^{W}
are retarded and Wightmann functions, respectively. The kernel
K_{1}
is diagonal in the time domain and
K_{2}
is diagonal in the frequency domain.

To calculate
\lambda_{L}
we need to obtain
|Q^{R}|^{2}
and
Q^{W}
. The saddle point equation in real frequency is given by

(Q^{R})^{-1}(\omega)=-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Gamma}+z-J_{2}^{2}Q^{R}(\omega)-\Sigma^{R}_{3}(\omega)\\

(S4.52)

where
\Sigma^{R}_{3}(\omega)
is the self-energy in real frequency for
p=3
spin interaction term. The self-energy in imaginary time is given as
\Sigma_{3}(\tau)=(3/2)J_{3}^{2}[Q(\tau)]^{2}
. When
J_{3}=0
, the saddle-point equation above can be solved exactly. The spectral function can be made gapless, like in the mSG phase, by choosing
z=2J_{2}
. In this case the solution is given by

Q^{R}_{0}(\omega)=\frac{2}{J_{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\tilde{\omega}^{2}+\mathfrak{i}\tilde{\omega}\sqrt{1-\tilde{\omega}^{2}}\bigg)

(S4.53)

where
\tilde{\omega}=\frac{\omega}{2\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}
.
At low energies
\tilde{\omega}<<1
, using
\sqrt{1-\tilde{\omega}^{2}}\simeq 1
we get

Q^{R}_{0}(\omega)=\frac{2}{J_{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\tilde{\omega}^{2}+\mathfrak{i}\tilde{\omega}\bigg)=q_{1}^{0}+\mathfrak{i}q_{2}^{0},

(S4.54)

where
q^{0}_{1}
and
q^{0}_{2}
are real and imaginary parts of
Q^{R}_{0}(\omega)
, respectively, leading to

\rho^{0}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}Q^{R}_{0}(\omega)=-\frac{\omega}{\pi J_{2}\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}

(S4.55)

To calculate the Lyapunov exponent perturbatively, we need to compute
|Q_{R}|^{2}
and
Q_{W}
perturbatively around the
J_{3}=0

For
|Q^{R}|^{2}
, the zeroth order
J_{3}=0

\Sigma_{3}(\tau)
. The imaginary part of
\Sigma^{R}_{3}(\omega)

Coleman 2015
can be computed perturbatively using the zeroth order solution. At the leading order and small frequency we get

\text{Im}\Sigma^{R}_{3}(\omega)\simeq\frac{J_{3}^{2}}{2\pi J_{2}^{3}\Gamma}\bigg[\frac{\omega^{3}}{2}+2\pi^{2}\omega T^{2}\bigg]+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{5})

(S4.56)

Using the saddle-point equation [Eq.(
S4.52
)] and
\Sigma^{R}_{3}(\omega)
above, we obtain

J_{2}^{2}\left|Q^{R}\left(\omega+\mathfrak{i}\frac{\lambda_{L}}{2}\right)\right|^{2}=1+\frac{1}{J_{2}^{2}q^{0}_{2}}\left[-\frac{\omega\lambda_{L}}{\Gamma}-\frac{J_{3}^{2}}{2\pi J_{2}^{3}\Gamma}\left(\frac{\omega^{3}}{2}+2\pi^{2}T^{2}\omega\right)\right]

(S4.57)

For
Q^{W}
, the
J_{3}=0
solution is sufficient and the Wightmann function
Q^{W}(t)=Q(it+\beta/2)
in real frequency is given by

Q^{W}(\omega)=-\frac{\pi\rho^{0}(\omega)}{\sinh[\beta\omega/2]}=\frac{\omega}{J_{2}\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}{\sinh[\beta\omega/2]}}

(S4.58)

By doing Fourier transformation we obtain

Q^{W}(t)=\frac{\pi T^{2}}{J_{2}\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}\sech^{2}(T\pi t)

(S4.59)

Using equations (
S4.57
) and (
S4.58

S4.49

K=1-\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}-\frac{J_{3}^{2}\pi T^{2}}{J_{2}^{3}\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}+\frac{3J_{3}^{2}}{2\pi J_{2}^{3}\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}\partial_{t}^{2}+\frac{3J_{3}^{2}\pi T^{2}}{J_{2}^{3}\sqrt{J_{2}\Gamma}}\sech^{2}(t\pi T)

(S4.60)

Using rescaled variable

and equating the maximum eigenvalue of

\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}-\sech^{2}s\bigg)f(s)=-\frac{1}{3}\bigg(\lambda_{L}\frac{J_{2}^{3}}{\pi J_{3}^{2}T^{2}}+1\bigg)f(s)

(S4.61)

The bracketed term in the left-hand side of the above equation is the Schrödinger Hamiltonian with Pöschl-Teller potential

whose eigenvalues are well known. The ground-state energy eigenvalue which maximizes
\lambda_{L}
is

(S4.62)

From Fig:
S9
we see that the coefficient

has weak dependence on temperature for low temperature regime in the quantum limit
\hbar\gtrsim 1
. Therefore, due to quasi-particle decay as in a Fermi liquid
Banerjee and Altman 2017
;
Kim and Cao 2020
;
Kim et al. 2020
, we also find the Lyapunov exponent

in the quantum limit at low temperature. This analytical result agrees well with the numerically obtained power-law form

in Fig.

(d) in the main text. From numerical fitting, we find that the exponent
\alpha

to

.

On the contrary, we find
\lambda_{L}\propto T^{1.1}\sim T
[Fig

(\hbar\ll 1)
for the low temperature regime. The above analytical calculation therefore does not hold in the classical regime. There are a few possible reasons behind this. Our perturbative analysis in the mSG phase is done by drawing analogy to the

for the PM phase in contrast to the mSG phase where
-\int d\omega\rho(\omega)n_{B}(\omega)=1-q_{\text{EA}}

. The violation of this sum rule is small for
\hbar\gtrsim 1

, as seen can be seen in Fig:

\alpha
here. However, for
\hbar\lesssim 1
, the sum rule violation is large and the temperature dependence of
q_{\text{EA}}
is also stronger. Presumably due to these, the analytical result (
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\propto T^{2}
) in this section is not applicable for the classical limit where
\lambda_{L}\propto T
.

S5
Correlation function and the onset of two-step relaxation

In this section we discuss the two-step glassy relaxation in the correlation function
C(t)
in the PM phase above the dynamical transition temperature (
T_{d}
). In equilibrium, the correlation function can be obtained from retarded function using fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Using the fluctuation-dissipation theorem,

C(\omega)=\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega),

(S5.63)

in the frequency domain. We obtain the correlation function
C(t)
by doing Fourier transform, i.e.
C(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(d\omega/2\pi)e^{-\mathfrak{i}\omega t}C(\omega)
. A two-step relaxation or decay of correlation function is usually seen in classical glasses in the so-called
\beta
-relaxation regime above the glass transition temperature. The correlation functions initially has a faster decay to a plateau-like regime
Götze 2008
;
Kob 1997
;
Reichman and Charbonneau 2005
, where correlation decays with slow power laws, before eventually decaying as a stretched exponential in the
\alpha
-relaxation regime. In the quantum
p
-spin glass model,
C(t)
also exhibits two-step relaxation as shown in Fig.
4
(c) (main text) and in Fig.
S10
(a). However, here the
\beta
-plateaus are mixed with oscillations, presumably due to quantum fluctuations and the existence of a soft gap (see Sec.
S3
) in the spectrum. To describe the overall decay profile of
C(t)
[Fig.
S10
(a)], we take the following general two-step relaxation form

f(t)=A\exp\left[-(t/\tau_{1})^{\beta_{1}}\right]+B\exp\left[-(t/\tau_{\alpha})^{\beta}\right].

(S5.64)

We fit numerically the correlation function
C(t)
with the above function. The second stretched exponential describes the final
\alpha
-decay of the correlation function
C(t)
. We show the extracted fitting parameters
A,~B,~\tau_{1},~\tau_{\alpha},~\beta_{1},~\beta
in Figs.
S10
(b),(c) and (d) for a few temperatures for the classical limit
\hbar=\sqrt{0.001}
. We find that initial decay time
\tau_{1}
more or less remains constant approaching
T_{d}
, whereas the
\alpha
-relaxation time
\tau_{\alpha}
tends to diverge for
T\to T_{d}
[Fig.
S10
(b)]. The contributions
A
and
B
for the two decays change with temperature with the
\alpha
-relaxation starting to dominate close to
T_{d}
[Fig.
S10
(c)]. We find both the initial and later relaxations to be stretched exponentials as shown in Fig.
S10
(d). (Note that we denote the stretched exponent
\beta
for the
\alpha
-relaxation following the standard notation
Reichman and Charbonneau 2005
in the literature. This symbol should not be confused with inverse temperature ‘
\beta
’).

Figure S10:
(a) The correlation function
C(t)
(solid line) is shown as a function of time
(t)
for several values of temperature in the classical limit,

A
and

As the correlation function has oscillations around the
\beta
plateaus, so it is slightly tricky to obtain the onset temperature (
T_{\beta}
) of the two-step relaxation. We approximately estimate the
T_{\beta}
from the temperature where the value of
C(t)
at the first minimum of oscillation in the
\beta
plateau turns negative to positive approaching from high temperature. In Fig.
S10
(a), we see that the first minimum of
C(t)
for temperature
T\sim 0.97
turns positive and then for all temperatures
T<0.97
,
C(t)
remains positive.
We thus estimate
T_{\beta}\approx 0.97
for
\hbar=\sqrt{0.001}
. Similarly, we find
T_{\beta}
’s for other values of
\hbar
and obtain
T_{\beta}(\hbar)
curve as shown in Fig.
1
in the main text. The two-step relaxation cannot be clearly distinguished for
\hbar\gtrsim 1.4
.

S6
Chaos from relaxation in PM phase

In this section, we discuss about the nontrivial effect of complex relaxation in glasses on chaos near dynamical transition temperature (
T_{d}
). We numerically show that only the stretched exponential part
\sim\exp[-(t/\tau_{\alpha})^{\beta_{a}}]
from the
\alpha
-relaxation of the correlation function
C(t)
(shown in Fig.
4
a, main text) with the rapidly increasing
\tau_{\alpha}
shown in Fig.
4
(b) (main text) can give rise to the non-monotonic
\lambda_{L}(T)
and the broad maximum in it above
T_{d}
. For concreteness, we mainly focus on the classical limit
\hbar=\sqrt{0.001}
, where we find stretching exponent
\beta_{a}\simeq 1-2.5
as shown in Fig.
S10
(c). We further show numerically that, instead of stretched exponential, even Debye exponential relaxation (
\beta_{a}=1
) with
\tau_{\alpha}
shown in Fig.
4
(b) alone also gives rise to the non-monotonic
\lambda_{L}(T)
and the broad maximum above
T_{d}
. Furthermore, we analytically derive the non-monotonic behaviour of
\lambda_{L}(T)
above
T_{d}
using a Debye exponential decay in classical or high temperature limit.

Numerical results

We first approximate the correlation function as stretched exponential i.e

C(t)=A\exp[(-(|t|/\tau_{\alpha})^{\beta_{a}}]

(S6.65)

where
A
is a normalisation factor.
Then, we compute
C(\omega)
numerically in the frequency domain using fast Fourier transform (fft). The spectral function is obtained using FDT from
C(\omega)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\tanh{(\frac{\omega}{2T})}C(\omega)

(S6.66)

\displaystyle\rho(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega)

(S6.67)

The normalisation factor
A
is determined using the spherical constraint relation i.e
-\int^{\infty}_{\infty}d\omega\rho(\omega)n_{B}(T,\omega)=1
. We further calculate
Q_{R}(\pm\omega+\mathfrak{i}\frac{\lambda_{L}}{2})
via spectral representation of
Q_{R}(\omega)
. Finally, we numerically solve the eigenvalue equation

S2.43
with kernel
S2.44
for PM phase. The result for
\lambda_{L}(T)
, clearly exhibiting the broad maximum above
T_{d}
, is shown in Fig.
S11
(a).

To verify that result is not specific to the stretched exponential form, we approximate the correlation function by Debye exponential relaxation, which is ubiquitous in liquids and many other systems, i.e

C(t)=Ae^{-\Gamma_{\alpha}|t|},\hskip 14.22636ptC(\omega)=A\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}+\omega^{2}}

(S6.68)

where
\Gamma_{\alpha}=1/\tau_{\alpha}
. Again, using FDT relation
S6.66
, we obtain the retarded function as

\displaystyle\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=A\tanh{\bigg(\frac{\omega}{2T}\bigg)}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}+\omega^{2}}

(S6.69)

\displaystyle\rho(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega)

(S6.70)

The normalization constant (
A
) is fixed by imposing spherical constraint as earlier. We use this spectral function to obtain
\lambda_{L}
numerically from the eigenvalue equation
S2.43
. The result
\lambda_{L}(T)
is shown in Fig.
S11
(b). This qualitatively gives the same result as that for the stretched exponential relaxation, namely a non-monotonic temperature dependence for
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
.

\lambda_{L}(T)

.

Analytical result

In this section we derive an analytical result for
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
for Debye exponential relaxation
\sim\exp(-t/\tau_{\alpha})
with a relaxation time
\tau_{\alpha}\sim(T-T_{d})^{\gamma}
(
\gamma>0
). Thus we establish an explicit relation between relaxation and chaos in the complex glassy relaxation regime where the relaxation time rapidly increases approaching the glass transition..

We solve the eigenvalue equation (
S2.43
) for the kernel (
S2.44
) in the classical limit, where the retarded function
Q^{R}(\omega)
is obtained from
C(\omega)
[Eq.(
S6.68
)] using the classical FDT relation i.e. the high-temperature limit (
T\gg\omega
) of Eq.(
S6.69
),

\displaystyle\text{Im}Q^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=A\frac{\omega}{2T}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}+\omega^{2}},

(S6.71)

and

\displaystyle Q^{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=A\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{2T}\frac{1}{\Gamma_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{i}\omega},

(S6.72)

where
\Gamma_{\alpha}=1/\tau_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}(T-T_{d})^{\gamma}
, which goes to zero as we approach the dynamical transition temperature
T_{d}
. From numerical fit to
\tau_{\alpha}
shown in Fig.
4
(b) (main text) with the above power law form, we obtain
\gamma=0.95\simeq 1
and
A_{\alpha}=0.55
. Using
n_{B}(\omega)=1/(e^{\beta\omega}-1)\simeq T/\omega
for
T\gg\omega
in the spherical constraint,
A=2
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle Q^{R}(\pm\omega+\mathfrak{i}\frac{\lambda_{L}}{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{T}\frac{1}{\Gamma_{\alpha}+\frac{\lambda_{L}}{2}\mp\mathfrak{i}\omega}

(S6.73)

and

\displaystyle Q^{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi\rho(\omega)}{\sinh(\omega/2T)}\simeq 2\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}+\omega^{2}}\hskip 14.22636pt(T\gg\omega).

(S6.74)

From the eigenvalue Eq.
S2.43
, with kernel
S2.44
,
we get

\displaystyle 6J^{2}\bigg(\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{T}\bigg)^{2}\frac{1}{(\Gamma_{\alpha}+\lambda_{L}/2)^{2}+\omega^{2}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}+(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}}f(\omega^{\prime})=f(\omega)

(S6.75)

Now to analytically solve the above equation for
\lambda_{L}
and
f(\omega)
, we make the following approximation of replacing a Lorentzian by a Gaussian, which is expected to give qualitatively the same result,

\frac{1}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}+\omega^{2}}\to\frac{1}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{-\omega^{2}/2\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}

(S6.76)

The above approximation with appropriate normalization maintains the spherical constraint. Thus, we get

\displaystyle\frac{3J^{2}}{\pi}\bigg(\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{T}\bigg)^{2}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}\frac{\pi}{2}e^{-\omega^{2}/2\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega^{\prime}e^{-(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}/2\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}f(\omega^{\prime})=f(\omega)

(S6.77)

where
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}=\Gamma_{\alpha}+\lambda_{L}/2
. The above integral equation can be solved by using

f(\omega)=e^{-\omega^{2}/2\sigma^{2}},

(S6.78)

so that,

\displaystyle\frac{3J^{2}}{2T^{2}}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}e^{-\omega^{2}/2\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega^{\prime}e^{-(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)^{2}/2\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}e^{-\omega^{\prime 2}/2\sigma^{2}}=e^{-\omega^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}.

(S6.79)

We clearly can see that the Gaussian form of the
f(\omega)
satisfies the above equation since the integral over
\omega^{\prime}
above produces another Gaussian, provided

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}+\frac{2}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}-\frac{\bar{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}{\Gamma^{4}_{\alpha}}

(S6.80)

\displaystyle\bar{\Gamma}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}{a\Gamma_{\alpha}}

(S6.81)

where
1/\bar{\Gamma}_{\alpha}^{2}=1/\Gamma_{\alpha}^{2}+1/\sigma^{2}
and
a=c^{2}J^{2}/T^{2}
with
c=(3\sqrt{2\pi}/2)^{1/2}\approx 2
. From the above, we obtain

\frac{1}{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}_{\alpha}}+\frac{2}{\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}-\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}^{4}_{\alpha}}{a^{2}\Gamma^{6}_{\alpha}}=\frac{a^{2}\Gamma^{2}_{\alpha}}{\tilde{\Gamma}^{4}_{\alpha}}

(S6.82)

Solving the above equation for
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}
for real roots, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}

\displaystyle=2\Gamma_{\alpha}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{cJ}{T}\bigg)^{2}+\bigg(\frac{cJ}{T}\bigg)\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{cJ}{T}\bigg)^{2}+4}}-1\bigg]

(S6.83)

The above result for
\lambda_{L}
is plotted in Fig.
S11
for different values of
\gamma
. The non-monotonic variation of
\lambda_{L}
with
T
and the broad maximum at a temperature
T_{m}>T_{d}
is nicely captured by the analytical result [Eq.(
S6.83
)]. However, in this approximation,
\lambda_{L}\propto\Gamma_{\alpha}\to 0
as
T\to T_{d}
. This is, of course, an artifact of approximating the two-step relaxation in Fig.
4
a (main text) by a single exponential with a diverging time scale. The same is true for the numerical results of the preceding subsection where we use a single stretched exponential or exponential decay for
C(t)
[Eq.(
S6.65
)]. Nevertheless, the approximation captures the non-monotonic part of
\lambda_{L}(T)
.

We now analytically estimate
T_{m}
assuming
cJ/T\gg 1
, where

\lambda_{L}\approx 2A_{\alpha}(T-T_{d})^{\gamma}\bigg[\frac{cJ}{T}-1\bigg].

(S6.84)

From the above expression, it is clear that
\lambda_{L}
initially increases for
T\gtrsim T_{d}
due to the increase of
\Gamma_{\alpha}=1/\tau_{\alpha}
, but eventually
\lambda_{L}
is expected to decrease due to crossover from strong (
J>T
) to weak (
J<T
) coupling at high temperature. In between, the maximum in
\lambda_{L}(T)
appears due to competition between the increasing
1/\tau_{\alpha}
and factor
(cJ/T-1)
decreasing with temperature.
The position (
T_{m}
) of maximum of
\lambda_{L}
is found from
\partial\lambda_{L}/\partial T=0
,

T_{m}=\frac{cJ(\gamma-1)+\sqrt{c^{2}J^{2}(\gamma-1)^{2}+4\gamma cJT_{d}}}{2\gamma}

(S6.85)

For
\gamma\approx 1
, as estimated from the numerical fit to
\tau_{\alpha}
shown in Fig.
4
(b) (main text), we get

T_{m}=\sqrt{cJT_{d}}

(S6.86)

which satisfies
T_{d}<T_{m}<cJ
since
cJ>T_{d}
. Thus
T_{m}
signifies a crossover in chaos, arising due to an interplay of relaxation, the rapid increase of relaxation time in the glassy regime, and the crossover from strong coupling (
J\gtrsim T
) to weak coupling (
J\lesssim T
). This result is more general than the model considered here and should have implications for complex relaxations in liquids and many other interacting systems, since exponential Debye relaxation is ubiquitous. As shown in Fig.
1
(main text), we also observe a correlation between
T_{m}
and the onset temperature
T_{\beta}
for two-step relaxation. It is quite likely that the onset of non-trivial temperature dependence of
\tau_{\alpha}\sim(T-T_{d})^{-\gamma}
, which is connected with the non-monotonic
\lambda_{L}(T)
, is correlated with the onset of the two-step relaxation too. However, to properly establish this relation, we need an independent understanding of
T_{\beta}
, which is beyond the scope of the current work and will be an interesting future direction of study.
