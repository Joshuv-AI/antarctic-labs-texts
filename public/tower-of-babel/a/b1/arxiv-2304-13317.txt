A study of 2-ended graphs via harmonic functions
Agelos Georgakopoulos, Alex Wendland

Abstract
We prove that every recurrent graph G G quasi-isometric to ℝ \mathbb{R} admits an essentially unique Lipschitz harmonic function h h . If G G is vertex-transitive, then the action of A ​ u ​ t ​ ( G ) Aut(G) preserves ∂ h \partial h up to a sign, a fact that we exploit to prove various combinatorial results about G G . As a consequence, we prove the 2-ended case of the conjecture of Grimmett & Li that the connective constant of a non-degenerate vertex-transitive graph is at least the golden mean. Moreover, answering a question of Watkins from 1990, we construct a cubic, 2-ended, vertex-transitive graph which is not a Cayley graph.



Keywords:

2-ended graph, Lipschitz harmonic function, vertex-transitive, Cayley graph, connective constant, golden mean, electrical current, quasi-isometry.

Introduction

An influential paper of Kleiner
[
21
]
provides a proof of Gromov’s polynomial growth theorem using harmonic functions on Cayley graphs. Shalom & Tao
[
27
]
refined Kleiner’s method providing tighter quantitative results, thereby proving that every finitely generated Cayley graph
G\
admits a non-constant Lipschitz harmonic function, and if
G\
has polynomial growth, then the vector space of those functions has finite dimension.
These proofs triggered significant interest in the study of Lipschitz harmonic functions on graphs, see
[
3
,
4
,
20
]
and references therein. The study of harmonic functions with restrictions on their growth is classical in Riemannian geometry
[
6
,
7
,
31
]
, and the origins of the aforementioned proofs can be found there; see
[
19
]
for a nice overview.

Our first result provides the exact dimension of the (real) vector space of Lipschitz harmonic functions on a 2-ended Cayley graph
G
, and more generally on graphs that asymptotically resemble
\mathbb{R}
. Recall that a function
h:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}
is
harmonic
, if
h(v)
equals the average of
h(u)
over the neighbours
u
of
v
, and it is

|h(v)-h(u)|<C
holds for a fixed constant

and every edge
vu\in E(G)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G\
be a locally finite, recurrent graph which is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}
. Then the vector space of Lipschitz harmonic functions on
G\
has dimension equal to 2.

In other words, every such graph admits a non-constant Lipschitz harmonic function, which is unique up to an additive and a multiplicative constant.

We remark that since recurrence is preserved under quasi-isometries between bounded degree graphs
[
25
, THEOREM 3.2]
, we can replace the recurrence condition by the bounded degree condition in Theorem
1.1
. Theorem
1.1
probably applies to Riemannian manifolds of bounded geometry that are quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}
, with a similar proof.

The type of harmonic functions supported by a graph or manifold is in general unstable under quasi-isometries
[
2
,
24
]
, and so it is not easy to deduce Theorem
1.1
from the analogous statement for
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
is not difficult, but uses a mixture of ingredients involving physical intuition around electrical networks, coarse geometry, and the classical fact that a recurrent graph admits no positive, non-constant, harmonic functions. The main message of this paper is perhaps that Theorem
1.1
has direct implications for the combinatorial structure of 2-ended vertex-transitive graphs. We say that a graph is
k
-regular
if all its vertices have degree
k
, and say that it is
cubic
if it is
3
-regular. As a first application, we use Theorem

to prove

Corollary 1.2
.

Every cubic, 2-ended, vertex-transitive graph
G\
is 3-edge-colourable.

Since transitive graphs are regular, we immediately deduce from this that the edge set of
G\
decomposes into three perfect matchings. Cubic graphs are special among the 2-ended, vertex-transitive graphs in that they cannot arise as a cartesian product of a finite graph and a 2-way infinite path, and so understanding the cubic case is an important first step towards understanding all 2-ended vertex-transitive graphs.

We remark that it is a well-known open problem whether every finite, cubic, Cayley graph is
3
-edge-colourable
[
1
]
. It is known that every infinite, connected, vertex-transitive graph admits a perfect matching
[
8
,
22
]
.

Using Theorem
1.1
again, we obtain a simple proof of a theorem of Watkins
[
29
]
saying that every 2-ended edge-transitive graph has even vertex degrees (Corollary

).

Techniques using electrical networks or harmonic functions have found many applications, but usually to the study of stochastic processes or analytic aspects of graphs; see e.g.
[
10
,
12
,
18
,
23
]
. Purely combinatorial implications like the above are perhaps more surprising.

The

of an infinite graph

is the exponential growth rate of self-avoiding walks in

starting at a fixed vertex. This invariant is of great interest to the statistical mechanics community, see e.g.

Every 2-ended vertex-transitive graph

This settles the 2-ended case of a conjecture of Grimmett & Li

stating that

holds for every infinite, vertex-transitive

A graph can be thought of as a metric space by endowing it with the graph distance

(

3
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. Let
LH(G)
denote the real vector space of Lipschitz harmonic functions on a graph
G
. We start with the lower bound
\dim(LH(G))\geq 2
, which we can prove in greater generality as follows:

Proposition 3.1
.

Every connected, locally finite graph with more than one end admits a non-constant Lipschitz harmonic function.

This provides a positive answer to Problem
1.1
in the multi-ended case. We will construct the desired harmonic function as a limit of ‘electrical potentials’ along an increasing sequence of finite subgraphs, corresponding to an electrical current of fixed intensity flowing through a cut separating the ends. We thereby exploit the frequently used viewpoint of harmonic functions as electrical potentials. We start by recalling the relevant electrical network theory.

3.1
Harmonic functions and electrical network basics

Let
G=(V,E)
be a (finite or infinite) graph. Let
\vec{E}
denote the set of ordered pairs
(x,y)
with
xy\in E
. We write
\overrightarrow{xy}
to denote
(x,y)
. Note that for every
xy\in E
, both
\overrightarrow{xy}
,
\overrightarrow{yx}
(
=:\overleftarrow{xy}
) lie in
\vec{E}
.

We say that a function
f:\vec{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
antisymmetric
, if
f(\overrightarrow{xy})=-f(\overleftarrow{xy})
for every
xy\in E
. All functions we consider from now on are antisymmetric.

Given
f:\vec{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we write
f^{*}(x):=\sum_{y\sim x}f(\overrightarrow{xy})
for the net flow of
f
out of
x
. Here
y\sim x
means that
xy\in E
.

Fix a graph
G=(V,E)
for the rest of this section.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that an antisymmetric function
f:\vec{E}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
Kirchhoff’s Node Law (KNL)
at a vertex
x
, if
f^{*}(x)=0
holds.

We say that
f
is a
(sourceless) flow
if it satisfies KNL at every
x\in V
.

Intuitively, KNL says that current is preserved at
x
.
Given
p\neq q\in V
, we say that
f:\vec{E}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
p-q
flow
, or a flow from
p
to
q
, if (KNL) holds for every
x\not\in\{p,q\}
, and
f
is antisymmetric. The
intensity
of
f
is defined as
f^{*}(p)
. If
G\
is finite, then one can easily show that
f^{*}(q)=-f^{*}(p)
by applying KNL to each other vertex.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that a flow
f:\vec{E}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
Kirchhoff’s Cycle Law (KCL)
, if for every closed walk (equivalently for every cycle)
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}(=x_{0})
in
G\
we have
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\overrightarrow{x_{i}x_{i+1}})=0
.

Definition 3.4
.

An
(electrical) current
of intensity
I\in\mathbb{R}
from
p
to
q
in
G
, is a
p
-
q
flow
i:\vec{E}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying Kirchhoff’s Cycle Law, such that
i^{*}(p)=I=-i^{*}(q)
.

We say that a pair of functions
i:\vec{E}\to\mathbb{R}
,
u:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies
Ohm’s Law (OL)
, if
i(\overrightarrow{xy})=u(x)-u(y)
holds for every
x,y\in V
.

Note that if
i,u
satisfies (OL), then so does
i,(u+a)
for every constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
. We say that
i
is the
Ohm dual
of
u
and
u
is an Ohm dual of
i
, if (OL) holds.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that a function
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
is
harmonic
, if

u(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}u(y)/d(x)
holds for every
x\in V
.

Proposition 3.6
.

If the pair
i,u
satisfies Ohm’s Law, then
u
is harmonic at
x\in V
if and only if
i
satisfies Kirchhoff’s Node Law at
x
, i.e. if
i^{*}(x)=0
.

This is well-known, but we include the proof for convenience as it is quite short:

Proof.

Since
i,u
satisfy (OL), we have

\sum_{y\sim x}u(y)=\sum_{y\sim x}\big(u(x)-i(\overrightarrow{xy})\big)=d(x)u(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}i(\overrightarrow{xy}),

which is by definition equal to
d(x)u(x)-i^{*}(x)
. Thus
i^{*}(x)=0
if and only if
\sum_{y\sim x}u(y)/d(x)=u(x)
, which is the definition of being harmonic.
∎

Proposition 3.7

(The maximum/minimum principle
[
11
, Lemma 3.2]
)
.

Suppose
G\
is finite and connected, and
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
is harmonic at every vertex of
V^{\prime}\subset V
. If the maximum or minimum of

is achieved at some vertex of
V^{\prime}
, then
u
is constant.

Note that every function
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
has a (unique) Ohm dual
\partial u
defined by
\partial u(\overrightarrow{xy})\mapsto u(x)-u(y)
. For the converse we have the following well-known fact:

Proposition 3.8

(
[
11
, Proposition 3.7.]
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph, and
i:\vec{E}\to\mathbb{R}
antisymetric. Then, there is
u:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the pair
i,u
satisfies OL if and only if

satisfies KCL. This

is unique up to an additive constant.

Perhaps better-known among electrical engineers, is the fact that no branch of an electrical network

can enhance the current flowing into

from the external source:

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph, and

. Let

be the
p
–

electrical current of intensity 1 in

|f(\overrightarrow{wv})|\leq 1
holds for every
\overrightarrow{wv}\in\vec{E}

Proof.

Let
h
be an Ohm dual of

as provided by Proposition
3.8
. Given an edge

, let

A:=\{x\in V\mid h(x)\geq\frac{h(w)+h(v)}{2}\}

and
Z:=V\backslash A
. Thus the cut

separates

whenever

, which we may assume is the case. Note that

f(\overrightarrow{xz})>0
holds for every

.

(1)

Moreover, we have
\sum_{x\in A}f^{*}(x)\leq 1
since

is a current of unit intensity. By double counting, we have
\sum_{x\in A}f^{*}(x)=\sum_{x\in A,z\in Z}f(\overrightarrow{xz})\geq|f(\overrightarrow{wv})|
, where the last inequality follows from (
1

∎

Our proof of Theorem

Lemma 3.10

Let

For every sourceless flow

E(P,N)
is non-empty. We claim that
E(P,N)
consists of finitely many edges. For if
{x_{n}}
is a sequence of distinct end-vertices of edges in
E(P,N)
lying in
P
, then some subsequence will converge to
\epsilon
or
\zeta
. We would then have
|h(x_{n})|\to\infty
by (
2
) and its analogue for
\zeta
. But this is impossible: since
h
is Lipschitz, and
x_{n}
has a neighbour in
N
, the values
h(x_{n})
must be uniformly bounded.

Recall that we are assuming
\partial h(X,Y)=0
, and therefore Lemma
3.10
implies
\partial h(P,N)=0
. This is impossible, because
E(P,N)
is non-empty, and
\partial h(x,y)>0
holds for every
xy\in E(P,N)
with
x\in P,y\in N
.
This contradiction proves that
h
is constant as claimed.
∎

3.4
Completing the proof of Theorem
1.1

Our main result now follows as a direct combination of Proposition
3.1
and Lemma
3.11
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Proposition
2.1
,
G
has exactly two ends
\epsilon,\zeta
.
To see that
\dim(LH(G))\geq 2
, let
f_{1}\equiv 1
, and let
f_{2}
be a non-constant Lipschitz harmonic function of
G\
as provided by Proposition
3.1
. Then
f_{1},f_{2}
clearly span a 2-dimensional subspace of
LH(G)
.

For the converse inequality, fix a finite cut
E(X,Y)
of
G\
separating
\epsilon,\zeta
. We claim that if two functions
h_{1},h_{2}\in LH(G)
satisfy
\partial h_{1}(X,Y)=\partial h_{2}(X,Y)
and
h_{1}(o)=h_{2}(o)
for some
o\in V(G)
, then
h_{1}=h_{2}
holds. This clearly implies that
\dim(LH(G))\leq 2
.

To prove this claim, let us consider the difference
h:=h_{1}-h_{2}\in LH(G)
. Notice that
\partial h(X,Y)=0
. Thus Lemma
3.11
says that
h
is constant, and since
h_{1}(o)=h_{2}(o)
we deduce that
h\equiv 0
and so
h_{1}=h_{2}
.
∎

Let us explore the tightness of the conditions of the results of this section.

Proposition
3.1
fails if
G\
is 1-ended, as can be easily checked by letting
G\
be a 1-way infinite path.

Theorem
1.1
(and Lemma
3.11
) becomes false if we drop the recurrence condition. To see this, start with the 2-way infinite ladder
L
, i.e. the graph obtained from the disjoint union of two 2-way infinite paths
H_{1},H_{2}
by joining each vertex of
H_{1}
to the corresponding vertex of
H_{2}
with an edge. We call the edges in each
H_{i}

horizontal
, and the remaining edges
vertical
. Obtain
G\
by replacing each horizontal edge of
L
at distance
n
from the origin by
2^{n}
paths of length 2. The interested reader will be able to construct a bounded harmonic function

that converges to four different values as we move along the four horizontal rays. One way of doing so is by noticing that simple random walk on
G\
will almost surely traverse only finitely many vertical edges, and defining
h(v)
as the probability for random walk starting at
v
to eventually stick to a fixed horizontal ray.

Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
3.11
easily become false if we drop the quasi-isometry condition, even if we assume 2-endedness: let
H
be an 1-ended graph that admits non-constant bounded harmonic functions, and connect two copies of
H
by one edge.

If we drop the Lipschitz condition in Lemma
3.11
then we do get non-constant harmonic functions, but the only examples I know grow exponentially in the distance from a fixed origin. I suspect this cannot be improved:

Conjecture 3.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a locally finite, recurrent graph which is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}
. Let
h
be a harmonic function on
G
, and suppose that for some finite cut

separating the two ends of

we have

. Then

is either constant or grows exponentially, i.e. there is a constant

and a sequence of vertices
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

.

Proposition
3.1
shows that the condition

is crucial here.

4.1
Connective constant

We now prove Theorem
1.3
, which provides a lower bound on the connective constant

of a 2-ended, cubic, vertex-transitive graph. We will refrain from recalling the definition of

as we will never use it, and refer the interested reader to

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We follow the approach of Grimmett & Li
[
17
]
, who proved the statement for a cubic Cayley graph
G

[
15

. Their proof starts by constructing a non-constant harmonic function

of

is
skew-difference-invariant

, and

g(z(v))-g(z(u))=\sigma(z)(g(v)-g(u)),

(4)

where
\sigma(z)\in\{-1,1\}
. The rest of their proof (starting at the top of p. 34 of
[

]
) does not rely on the structure of

and can be repeated verbatim. Thus it suffices to construct such a function

.

For this, let
g:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-constant, Lipschitz, harmonic function, provided by Theorem
1.1
. Given any automorphism
z\in\mbox{Aut}(G)
, the function
g\circ z
is also in

. By Theorem
1.1
,

equals

up to a multiplicative constant
c_{z}\in\mathbb{R}
and an additive constant
a_{z}
. Note that if
F
is a finite cut of
G\
separating the two ends
\epsilon,\zeta
, then the net flow of
\partial g
from the component of
G-F
containing
\epsilon
to the component of

containing

is independent of the choice of

up to a sign by Lemma

. It follows that

. Thus letting

satisfies (

).
∎

4.2

Corollary 4.1

)

Proof.

Suppose

5
A cubic 2-ended vertex-transitive graph which is not Cayley

Watkins
[
28
]
asked whether every 2-ended vertex-transitive graph can be obtained via a certain product operation between a finite vertex-transitive graph and the 2-way infinite path. A positive answer to Watkins’ question would imply that every 2-ended cubic vertex-transitive graph is a Cayley graph. Whether this is true was more recently explicitly asked by Grimmett & Li
[
17
]
due to its relevance to Theorem
1.3
. In this section we construct an example of a 2-ended cubic vertex-transitive graph which is not a Cayley graph, thus providing a negative answer to these questions.

Let
\Gamma
be the graph with
V(\Gamma)=\{v_{n,k}|n\in{\mathbb{Z}},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}
and

E(\Gamma)=\{v_{n,k}v_{n,k+1}\ |\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}\cup\{v_{n,2k+1}v_{n+1,4k+2}\ |\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

By construction,
\Gamma
is a cubic graph. A useful way to think of this graph is as a 2-way infinite stack of layers
L_{n}:=\{v_{n,k}|k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. Each
L_{n}
spans a 10-cycle, and between any two layers
L_{n}
and
L_{n+1}
there is a Petersen-graph-like structure. Thus
\Gamma
is clearly 2-ended, since contracting each
L_{n}
into a vertex transforms
\Gamma
into a 2-way infinite path. Checking that
\Gamma
has all other desired properties is rather routine, but we include the details for the sake of completeness.

Claim 1
.

\Gamma
is vertex-transitive.

Proof.

We introduce the following two maps
\sigma,\tau:V(\Gamma)\to V(\Gamma)
, which will allow us to map any vertex of
\Gamma
to any other:

\sigma(v_{n,k})=v_{n+1,k}\ \mbox{ and }\ \tau(v_{n,k})=\begin{cases}v_{-n,k+1}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 0\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{-n,3-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 1\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{-n,k+9}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 2\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{-n,7-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 3\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\end{cases}.

Thus
\sigma
just shifts all the layers up by 1, whereas
\tau
rotates the 10-cycle on
L_{0}
by one position, which flips the stack of layers by mapping
L_{n}
to
L_{-n}
, and inverts the orientation of the 10-cycles at layers of odd index.
It is easy to see that each of
\tau
and
\sigma
is a bijection preserving edges, and so
\sigma,\tau\in\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
. Let

G:=\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle\leq Aut(\Gamma)

be the group of automorphisms of
\Gamma
generated by
\sigma
and
\tau
.
For any two vertices
v_{n,k},v_{n^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\in V(\Gamma)
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{n^{\prime}}\tau^{k^{\prime}-k}\sigma^{-n}(v_{n,k})=

\displaystyle\sigma^{n^{\prime}}\tau^{k^{\prime}-k}(v_{0,k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{n^{\prime}}(v_{0,k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{n^{\prime},k^{\prime}}

and so
G
acts transitively on
V(\Gamma)
. This proves Claim 1.
∎

A classical theorem of Sabidussi
[
26
]
states that a graph
G\
is isomorphic to a Cayley graph if and only if there is a subgroup of
\mbox{Aut}(G)
acting
regularly
, i.e. freely and transitively, on
V(G)
. For the purposes of this paper, they reader may use Sabidussi’s theorem as the definition of a
Cayley graph
. We will use this to prove that our
\Gamma
is not a Cayley graph.

Claim 2
.

G
is not a regular subgroup of
\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\tau^{-3}\sigma\tau\sigma(v_{0,k})

\displaystyle=\tau^{-3}\sigma\tau(v_{1,k})

\displaystyle=\tau^{-3}\sigma(v_{-1,3-k})

\displaystyle=\tau^{-3}(v_{0,3-k})

\displaystyle=v_{0,-k}.

This implies
\tau^{-3}\sigma\tau\sigma(v_{0,0})=v_{0,0}
, yet
\tau^{-3}\sigma\tau\sigma\not=1_{\Gamma}
as
\tau^{-3}\sigma\tau\sigma(v_{0,1})=v_{0,9}
. Thus the action of
G
on
\Gamma
is not free, proving Claim 2.
∎

We remind the reader the
n
th layer is denoted
L_{n}=\{v_{n,k}|\ k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}
for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and we define the partition
\mathcal{C}:=\{L_{n}\ |\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
of
V(\Gamma)
.

Claim 3
.

Let
\phi\in\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
satisfy
\phi(L_{a})=L_{b}
. If for some
\chi\in\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
we have
\phi(x)=\chi(x)
for every
x\in L_{a}
, then
\phi=\chi
. Moreover,
\phi
preserves the partition
\mathcal{C}:=\{L_{n}\ |\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\phi(v_{a+1,2k})
and
\phi(v_{a-1,2k+1})
are uniquely determined by
\phi(x),x\in L_{a}
because each vertex in
L_{b}
has exactly one neighbour outside
L_{b}
. This in turn uniquely determines
\phi(v_{a+1,2k+1})
and
\phi(v_{a-1,2k})
by a similar argument. Continuing like this, we see that
\phi(\{v_{a+\epsilon,k}|k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\})=\{v_{b\pm\epsilon,k}|k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}
for
\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}
. By an inductive argument, this uniquely determines
\phi
, and moreover
\phi
preserves
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Claim 4
.

Any
\phi\in\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
preserves the partition
\mathcal{C}=\{L_{n}|n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

Proof.

If
\phi
does not fix
\mathcal{C}
, then by Claim
3
we have
\phi(L_{0})\not=L_{a}
for every
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. It is not hard to see that no 10-cycle of
\Gamma
other than the
L_{i}
separates
\Gamma
into two infinite components. This contradicts that
\phi
is an automorphism. (An alternative way of proving the claim is by using Watkins’s theorem that every 2-ended vertex-transitive and edge-transitive graph has even degree
[
28
]
.)
∎

Claim 5
.

Any
\phi\in\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
is uniquely determined by
\phi(v_{0,1})
and
\phi(v_{0,2})
.

Proof.

Assume
\phi(v_{0,1})=v_{a,b}
. Then by Claim
4
,
\phi(v_{0,2})\in N(v_{a,b})\cap\{v_{a,k}|k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\{v_{a,b+1},v_{a,b-1}\}
. In either case, by Claim
4
this uniquely determines
\phi(v_{0,k})
for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}/10{\mathbb{Z}}
. By Claim
3
, this uniquely determines
\phi
.
∎

We remark that this implies
\mbox{Stab}_{\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)}(v_{0,0})=\langle\tau^{-3}\sigma\tau\sigma\rangle={\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. So
\langle\tau,\sigma\rangle=\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
.

Claim 6
.

\Gamma
is not isomorphic to a Cayley graph.

Proof.

Let
T\leq\mbox{Aut}(\Gamma)
be a transitive subgroup. Thus we can find automorphisms
\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\in T
such that
\sigma^{\prime}(v_{0,1})=v_{1,1}
and
\tau^{\prime}(v_{0,1})=v_{0,2}
. By Claim
4
,
T
preserves the partition
\mathcal{C}
. So either
\tau^{\prime}(v_{0,2})=v_{0,3}
and
\tau^{\prime}=\tau
or
\tau^{\prime}(v_{0,2})=v_{0,1}
and

\tau^{\prime}(v_{n,k})=\sigma\tau\sigma(v_{n,k})=\tilde{\tau}(v_{n,k}):=\begin{cases}v_{-n,3-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 0\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{-n,k-1}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 1\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{-n,7-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 2\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{-n,k+1}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 3\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\end{cases}

by Claim
5
. Similarly, either
\sigma^{\prime}(v_{0,2})=v_{1,2}
and
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma
or
\sigma^{\prime}(v_{0,2})=v_{1,0}
and

\sigma^{\prime}(v_{n,k})=\sigma\tau^{-1}\sigma\tau\sigma(v_{n,k})=\tilde{\sigma}(v_{n,k}):=\begin{cases}v_{n+1,2-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 0\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{n+1,4-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 1\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{n+1,8-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 2\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\\
v_{n+1,6-k}&\mbox{ if }n\equiv 3\mbox{ (mod }4\mbox{)}\end{cases}.

by Claim
5
. By Claim
2
, if
\{\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\}=\{\sigma,\tau\}
then
T
is not regular. If
\{\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\}=\{\tilde{\sigma},\tau\}
then

\displaystyle\tau\tilde{\sigma}\tau\tilde{\sigma}(v_{0,k})

\displaystyle=\tau\tilde{\sigma}\tau(v_{1,2-k})

\displaystyle=\tau\tilde{\sigma}(v_{-1,k+1})

\displaystyle=\tau(v_{0,5-k})

\displaystyle=v_{0,6-k}

giving
\tau\tilde{\sigma}\tau\tilde{\sigma}(v_{0,3})=v_{0,3}
yet
\tau\tilde{\sigma}\tau\tilde{\sigma}\not=1_{\Gamma}
. Similarly, if
\{\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\}=\{\sigma,\tilde{\tau}\}
then

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}\sigma\tilde{\tau}\sigma(v_{0,k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\tau}\sigma\tilde{\tau}(v_{1,k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\tau}\sigma(v_{-1,k-1})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\tau}(v_{0,k-1})

\displaystyle=v_{0,4-k}

giving
\tilde{\tau}\sigma\tilde{\tau}\sigma(v_{0,2})=v_{0,2}
yet
\tilde{\tau}\sigma\tilde{\tau}\sigma\not=1_{\Gamma}
. Lastly, if
\{\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\}=\{\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\tau}\}
then

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}(v_{0,k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}\tilde{\tau}(v_{1,2-k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}(v_{-1,1-k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\tau}(v_{0,5+k})

\displaystyle=v_{0,-2-k}

giving
\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}(v_{0,9})=v_{0,9}
yet
\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}\not=1_{\Gamma}
. Therefore

is never regular, and so

is not a Cayley graph by Sabidussi’s theorem mentioned above.
∎

6
Further problems

In this paper we have used harmonic functions to deduce various facts about 2-ended, vertex-transitive graphs. We feel that the technique can be exploited further to yield a better understanding of this class of graphs, possibly leading to a complete description. Towards this aim, we propose

Problem 6.1
.

Provide an effective enumeration of the family of 2-ended, locally finite, vertex-transitive graphs.

By an
effective enumeration
of a family
\mathcal{F}
we mean an algorithm that outputs a sequence of finite strings
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that each
S_{i}

encodes
an element of
\mathcal{F}
, and each element of
\mathcal{F}
is encoded by some
S_{i}
. We do not prescribe a specific way to encode our graphs, but one option is to use our partite presentations from
[
13
]
, which is a generalisation of group presentations to arbitrary vertex-transitive graphs. It would be already interesting to provide an effective enumeration of the cubic, 2-ended, vertex-transitive graphs

A vertex-transitive graph
G\
is said to be
n
-Cayley, if there is a subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mbox{Aut}(G)
such that
\Gamma
acts freely (i.e. without fixing any point) on
V(G)
and has
n
orbits of vertices. Thus
G\
is isomorphic to a Cayley graph if and only if it is 1-Cayley by Sabidussi’s aforementioned theorem. If
n=2
we say that
G\
is
bi-Cayley
.

Problem 6.2
.

Is every 2-ended, cubic, vertex-transitive graph Cayley or bi-Cayley?

Note that this is a relaxation of Watkins’ question that we negatively answered with Theorem
1.4
. Our case analysis in the proof of Corollary
1.2
is helpful for this problem: in cases 1 & 3 it is not hard to obtain a positive answer.

For graphs of degree higher than 3 we do not expect this to be true, as one can obtain such a graph as the cartesian product of an arbitrary finite vertex-transitive graph with the 2-way infinite path. But we expect that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is
f(k)\in\mathbb{N}
such that every
k
-regular, 2-ended, vertex-transitive graph is
f(k)
-Cayley.

Problem 6.3
.

Provided lower and upper bounds for
f(k)
.

Finally, it would be interesting to know whether Corollary
1.2
generalises to graphs of arbitrary degree:

Problem 6.4
.

Let
G\ =(V,E)
be a 2-ended,
k
-regular, vertex-transitive graph. Must
G\
be
k
-edge-colourable? Must
E
decompose into
k
perfect matchings?

Our harmonic function technique for Corollary
1.2
seems useful for this general case.
