On the Adversarial Convex Body Chasing Problem
Yue Guan, Longxu Pan, Daigo Shishika, Panagiotis Tsiotras

Abstract
In this work, we extend the convex bodies chasing problem (CBC) to an adversarial setting, where an agent (the Player) is tasked with chasing a sequence of convex bodies generated adversarially by another agent (the Opponent). The Player aims to minimize the total cost associated with its own movements, while the Opponent tries to maximize the same cost. The set of feasible convex bodies is finite and known to both agents, which allows us to provide performance guarantees with max-min optimality. Under certain assumptions, we show the continuity of the optimal value function, and propose an algorithm to numerically approximate the optimal policies for both the Player and the Opponent within a guaranteed tolerance. Finally, the theoretical results are verified through numerical examples.

Introduction

The Convex Bodies Chasing (CBC) problem was proposed in
[
1
]
to study the interaction between convexity and metrical task systems.
It was soon realized that many problems of practical interest could be viewed as variants of the CBC problem, including scheduling
[
2
]
, efficient covering
[
3
]
, safe machine-learned advice
[
4
,
5
]
, self-organizing lists
[
6
]
, the k-server problem
[
7
,
8
]
, and other online convex optimization problems
[
9
]
.
In the CBC problem, an
online
agent (the Player) receives a request sequence of
T
convex sets
\mathcal{Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{T}
contained in a normed space
\mathcal{X}
of dimension
d
.
The Player starts at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and, at time step
t
, observes the set
\mathcal{Q}_{t}
and then moves to a new point
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, which induces a cost
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\right\|
.
The objective of the Player is to maintain a constant ratio, known as the competitive ratio, against the minimum cost possible in hindsight, i.e., knowing the sequence of sets in advance.
The existence of a finite competitive ratio was first conjectured in
[
1
]
.
Partial results on restricted cases were established later, including:
chasing subspaces
[
10
]
and
chasing nested bodies
[
11
,
12
]
.
The conjecture was first resolved in
[
13
]
, which provided an
2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
upper bound.
A nearly optimal competitive ratio was later derived in
[
14
]
for nested convex bodies using the classical Steiner point
[
15
]
.
The more recent work
[
16
]
has achieved a competitive ratio
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d\log T})
without restrictions on the convex bodies, despite the fact that the proposed algorithm chooses
\mathbf{x}_{t}
without knowledge of the future convex sets
\mathcal{Q}_{t+1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{T}
.

In the classic CBC problem, with no restriction on the mechanism that generates the convex sets, the Player needs to select a point that balances the future cost for all possible subsequent convex sets.
Consequently, the competitive
ratio
is considered as the performance metric for most of the previous algorithms in the literature.
However, this performance metric can be ineffective in case of a high dimensional space
\mathcal{X}
.
Moreover, in many real-world scenarios the convex bodies are selected (potentially adversarially) from a known set of convex sets (e.g., dynamic Blotto game
[
17
]
).
With this additional information, one expects to obtain better performance guarantees than with the competitive ratio.

In this work, we consider the
adversarial
convex bodies chasing (aCBC) problem, where a (finite) set of compact convex bodies is known prior to starting the game, but the sequence of selected bodies is unknown to the Player, and is generated from the given set by an adversary (the Opponent).
The adversarial selection of the convex bodies is further constrained over a graph, which implies that the currently selected convex body has an impact on the convex bodies available at the next time step
1
1

1

One can remove the graph constraint by using a fully-connected graph.
.
The Player’s movement is also constrained within the (compact) reachable set constructed from its current state.
We formulate this competitive game as a zero-sum sequential game
[
18
]
where the Player aims to minimize its total cost, while the Opponent tries to maximize it.

The contribution of this work is threefold:
(i) we provide a novel formulation of the adversarial CBC game;
(ii) we provide theoretical guarantees for the existence of a Lipschitz continuous max-min value function under mild assumptions;
and (iii) we propose a numerical algorithm that provides bounded
\varepsilon
-suboptimal performance with respect to the max-min solution.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows:
Section
2
formally presents the formulation of the adversarial convex bodies chasing problem;
Section
3
introduces the optimal value function and provides theoretical results regarding its continuity
2
2

2

The terms “optimal value function” and “value function” are used interchangeably in this paper.
;
Section
4
proposes a numerical algorithm that discretizes the domain and approximates the optimal policies for the Player and the Opponent.
In the same section
we further prove that the total cost from the obtained policy is within
\varepsilon
-suboptimality of the optimal min-max solution;
Section
5
demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm through numerical examples.
Finally, Section
6
concludes this work.

Problem Formulation

The adversarial convex bodies chasing (aCBC)
game
is played sequentially between two agents: the Player and the Opponent.
The game evolves over a compact subset
\mathcal{X}
of a normed Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
At each time step
t
, a convex region
\mathcal{Q}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is selected by the Opponent, and then the Player chooses a point
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, inducing a corresponding cost.
Once
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is chosen, the Opponent selects the next convex set
\mathcal{Q}_{t+1}
and the process continues until finite time horizon
T
.
The timeline of the game is presented in Figure
1
.
The Player tries to minimize its total cost
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}c(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+1})
over a finite horizon
T
for some non-negative cost function
c
, while the Opponent aims to maximize this cost.

Figure 1:
Timeline of the aCBC game. At each time step
t
, the Opponent first selects a state
i_{t}
to assign a corresponding convex set
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}
for the Player.
The Player then selects a point
\mathbf{x}_{t}
within the assigned convex set.

We use
\mathbf{x}_{t}
to denote the state of the Player at time step
t
and treat
\mathcal{X}
as the state space of the Player.
Different from the classical CBC problem, we restrict the Player’s selection of its next state to a neighborhood of its current state characterized by a
reachability correspondence
(a set-valued map).
Specifically, we require that
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
for all
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, where
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
represents the
reachable set
of the Player at the next time step from the current state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
.
We use
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{X}
to denote the reachability correspondence
\mathcal{R}
as a set-valued
map
3
3

3

One can also use
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}
to denote the reachability correspondence
\mathcal{R}
as a single-valued map.
.

Assumption 1
.

For all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})
is solid, compact, and convex.

At time
t
, the Opponent’s state is defined as the node
i_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
in a directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
that constrains the Opponent movements.
Given the Opponent’s current state
i_{t}
, the Opponent can move to any one of the neighboring nodes
i_{t+1}
, such that
(i_{t},i_{t+1})\in\mathcal{E}
.
We denote the set of all neighbors of node
i_{t}
as
\mathcal{N}_{i_{t}}
.
Before the game, we assign a finite collection of convex regions
\mathcal{Q}=\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i)}\big\}_{t=0,i=1}^{T,|\mathcal{V}|}\subset\mathcal{X}
to the Opponent.
At each time step
t
,
the Opponent selects a convex region
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}
from
\mathcal{Q}
by selecting the feasible Opponent state
i_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{i_{t-1}}
from its previous state
i_{t-1}\in\mathcal{V}
.
In other words, instead of having the freedom to choose an arbitrary convex subset of
\mathcal{X}
as in the classical CBC problem, the Opponent in an aCBC game can only choose from a given finite set of convex bodies by selecting the next state (node of
\mathcal{G}
) to visit.
We make the following two assumptions on
\mathcal{Q}
and the information structure of the game.

Assumption 2
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{V}
and
t=0,\ldots,T
, the set
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i)}
is solid, compact, and convex.

Assumption 3
.

The collection
\mathcal{Q}
and the graph
\mathcal{G}
are common knowledge to both agents prior to the game.

Once a convex region
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}
is selected at time
t
by the Opponent, the Player needs to move to a feasible point
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\cap\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}
.
For ease of notation, we introduce the following intersection correspondence

\Theta_{t}^{(i_{t})}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\cap\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}.

(1)

To avoid degeneracy, we assume that any admissible sequence of convex bodies chosen by the Opponent is always feasible for the Player.
Consequently, the aCBC game is an optimization problem rather than a feasibility problem, similar to the classical CBC problem.

Assumption 4
.

For all
t=1,\ldots,T
and
i_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
, the following holds:

\mathrm{int}\Big(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})\cap\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}\Big)\neq\varnothing,\quad\forall\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t-1}^{(i_{t-1})},\text{ where }i_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{i_{t-1}}.

(2)

Assumption
4
ensures that the optimization problem faced by the Player is strictly feasible for all time steps.
With Assumptions
1
-
4
, the major difference of the aCBC from the classic CBC formulation is that
(i) the selection of a new convex body considers the feasibility from the previous convex body, (ii) the feasible convex bodies
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i)}
are compact and solid, and (iii) the set of all convex bodies in the aCBC game is
finite
and is
common knowledge
to both agents.
An illustrative example of the proposed aCBC game is shown in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
Example of an aCBC game with a 4-node graph and a two-dimensional Player state
\mathcal{X}
.
At time step
0
, the Opponent starts at node 2 and the Player selects a point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
in
\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{(2)}
.
The Opponent then moves to node
4
and the Player selects the point
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\cap\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{(4)}
, inducing a cost of
c(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})
.
The Opponent then moves to node
3
and the Player moves to
\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\cap\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{(3)}
incurring a cost of
c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})
, and so on.
The game continues until reaching the finite time horizon
T
.

The assumptions on the compactness of the convex bodies and the finiteness of its collection
\mathcal{Q}
significantly reduce the Opponent’s freedom of selecting convex regions.
This allows the construction of a min-max solution that is discussed in the next section.
To ensure the existence of a min-max solution, however,
we need to further make the following assumptions on the continuity of the cost function
c
and the reachability correspondence
\mathcal{R}
.

Assumption 5
.

The cost function
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
continuous.

Notice that the cost function here can be an arbitrary continuous function and thus is more general than the norm-cost used in the classic CBC formulation.
To distinguish the continuity of correspondences from the continuity of single-valued maps, we need to introduce the concepts of lower and upper semi-continuity.

Definition 1

(upper semi-continuity
[
19
]
)
.

A set-valued map
F:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{Y}
is upper semi-continuous (usc) at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
if for every open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that
F(\mathbf{x})\subseteq U
, there exists a neighborhood
V
of
\mathbf{x}
such that
F(V)\subseteq U
.
F
is usc on
\mathcal{X}
if it is usc at every point in
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 2

(lower semi-continuity
[
19
]
)
.

A set-valued map
F:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{Y}
is lower semi-continuous (lsc) at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
if for every open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that
F(\mathbf{x})\cap U\neq\varnothing
there exists a neighborhood
V
of
\mathbf{x}
such that
F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cap U\neq\varnothing
for all
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in V
.
F
is lsc on
\mathcal{X}
if it is lsc at every point in
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 3

(set-valued map continuity
[
19
]
)
.

A set-valued map
F:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{Y}
is continuous on
\mathcal{X}
if it is lsc and usc on
\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 6
.

The reachability correspondence
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{X}
is continuous.

We consider Markov policies for both agents.
To initialize the game, at time
t=0
, the Opponent first selects a node
i_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
according to its policy
\sigma_{0}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{G})
.
Here, we make the policy dependence on
\mathcal{Q}
and the graph
\mathcal{G}
explicit.
After observing the Opponent’s selection
i_{0}
, the Player selects a point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{(i_{0})}
according to the policy
\pi_{0}(i_{0})
.
The Player’s (deterministic) policies at time
{t\geq 1}
is given by
\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\cap\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}
, which explicitly considers the Opponent-selected convex body
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i_{t})}
at time
t
and the Player’s reachability constraint.
The Opponent’s policy at time
t\geq 1
is given by
\sigma_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t-1})\in\mathcal{N}_{i_{t-1}}
, which reflects the graph constraint on the Opponent’s state.
We collect the sequences of the policies used by the Player and the Opponent to the strategies
\pi=\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
and
\sigma=\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, respectively.

A strategy pair
(\pi,\sigma)
provides a trajectory for the Player and, consequently, induces a total movement cost
C(\pi,\sigma)
characterized as
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{c~(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+1})}
.
We are interested in subgame perfect equilibria, namely,
equilibria where, at each stage of the game, the Player always minimizes its future cumulative cost-to-go while the Opponent maximizes it.
We denote the total cost under a subgame perfect equilibrium as the optimal total cost
C^{*}
.

Problem 1
.

Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
, a collection of solid and compact convex bodies
\mathcal{Q}=\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{(i)}\big\}_{t=0,i=1}^{T,|\mathcal{V}|}
, a cost function
c
, and a reachablility correspondence
\mathcal{R}
, find the optimal total cost
C^{*}
of the aCBC game along with the corresponding optimal strategies for the Player and the Opponent under the information structure in Assumption
3
.

To solve Problem
1
, we follow a value-based approach, where at each decision point, the agents compute a policy that optimizes its “cost-to-go.”
The rest of the paper will address the technical details regarding the solution to this
optimization problem.

2.1
Connection to the Dynamic Defender-Attacker Blotto Game

At first glance,
one may find Assumption
4
restrictive.
However, for safety-critical problems, it is common to first construct a set of policies that are safe/feasible and then consider optimality in the safe domain.
One potential application of the aCBC game is the dynamic defender-attacker Blotto game (dDAB)
[
17
]
, which is a dynamic and adversarial resource allocation problem in a graph environment.
In the dDAB a team of defender robots is tasked to ensure a numerical advantage over a team of attackers at every node.
The two teams reallocate their robots in sequence and each robot (resource) can move at most one hop at each time step.
The dDAB game is formulated as a game of kind, and the game terminates with the attacker’s victory if any node has more attacker robots than defender robots.
In
[
17
]
is is shown that the defender’s feedback strategy is specified by the safe sets given as a function of the attacker’s allocation.
In effect, the attacker (the Opponent) is selecting a sequence of safe sets, which the defender’s allocation (the Player’s state) must stay in, for the sake of successful defense.
The collection of safe-sets in dDAB satisfies Assumption
4
and is one of the major motivations of this work.
Consequently, the aCBC framework can naturally extend the dDAB game to a game-of-degree formulation by introducing costs to the defender movements.

3
Optimal Value Functions

To reflect the different information available to the two agents at their decision points, we introduce two value functions:
V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})
for the optimal value function of the Player and
U_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t-1})
for the Opponent at time
t
.
These two optimal value functions will be computed through a backward induction scheme, similar to other finite horizon decision-making problems
[
20
,
21
]
.

3.1
Backward Induction

At the terminal time step
T
, the Player has knowledge on its previous state
\mathbf{x}_{T-1}
and the Opponent state
i_{T}
, and the Player is about to make its final move.
Since there are no moves after time
T
, the Player only needs to consider the optimality with respect to the immediate cost
c~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},\mathbf{x}_{T})
.
Consequently, the optimal terminal value for the Player can be formulated as

V_{T}~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},i_{T})\triangleq\inf_{\mathbf{x}_{T}~\in~\Theta_{T}^{(i_{T})}(\mathbf{x}_{T-1})}{c~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},\mathbf{x}_{T})}.

(3)

In other words, the above value depicts the best feasible outcome for the Player, given its previous state
\mathbf{x}_{T-1}
and the Opponent state
i_{T}
at the terminal time step
T
.

For time steps
t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\}
, the Player needs to optimize its selection of a new state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
in order to minimize both the immediate cost and the worst-case future cost.
Specifically, the Player has to also consider the fact that the Opponent will observe the new Player state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and then best-respond with
i_{t+1}
to maximize the future cumulative costs.
Consequently, the optimal value for the Player is formulated as

V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})\triangleq\inf_{\mathbf{x}_{t}~\in~\Theta_{t}^{(i_{t})}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{\left\{c~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{t})+\max_{i_{t+1}~\in~\mathcal{N}_{i_{t}}}V_{t+1}~(\mathbf{x}_{t},i_{t+1})\right\}}.

(4)

Finally, for the initial Player state selection at
t=0
there is no reachability constraint or immediate cost.
As a result, the optimal value function only depends on the initial Opponent state
i_{0}
, while the optimization only covers the worst-case future cost similar to the value function in (
4
), and we have

V_{0}~(i_{0})\triangleq\inf_{\mathbf{x}_{0}~\in~\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{(i_{0})}}\;\max_{i_{1}~\in~\mathcal{N}_{i_{0}}}V_{1}~(\mathbf{x}_{0},i_{1}).

(5)

The Opponent’s optimal value function is constructed similarly to that of the Player.
The only difference comes from the information structure.
Namely, at time step
t
, the Opponent makes a decision based on the previous Player state
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
and its own previous state
i_{t-1}
.
Formally, the Opponent’s optimal values are formulated as

\displaystyle U_{T}~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},i_{T-1})

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{i_{T}~\in~\mathcal{N}_{i_{T-1}}}{\left\{\inf_{\mathbf{x}_{T}~\in~\Theta_{T}^{(i_{T})}(\mathbf{x}_{T-1})}{c~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},\mathbf{x}_{T})}\right\}},

(6)

\displaystyle U_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t-1})

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{i_{t}~\in~\mathcal{N}_{i_{t-1}}}{\left\{\inf_{\mathbf{x}_{t}~\in~\Theta_{t}^{(i_{t})}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{\{c~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{t})+U_{t+1}~(\mathbf{x}_{t},i_{t})\}}\right\}},

(7)

\displaystyle U_{0}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{i_{0}~\in~\mathcal{V}}{~\inf_{\mathbf{x}_{0}~\in~\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{(i_{0})}}}{U_{1}~(\mathbf{x}_{0},i_{0})}.

(8)

Remark 1
.

The Opponent’s value
U_{0}
is equivalent to the optimal total cost
C^{*}
of the aCBC game.

Remark 2
.

From time step
1
to
T
, we implicitly assume
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t-1}^{(i_{t-1})}
and
i_{t-1}\in\mathcal{V}
for all value functions
U_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t-1})
.
Likewise, for the value functions
V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})
we assume that
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t-1}^{(i_{t-1})}
and
i_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{i_{t-1}}
.

3.2
Continuity of the Optimal Value Functions

The first question one may ask regarding the value functions is whether the infimum in (
3
)-(
8
) can be attained.
To answer this question, we first show that the intersection correspondence

\Theta_{t}^{(i)}
is continuous, then we prove the continuity of the value functions with respect to the
\mathbf{x}
-arguments.
Assumption
6
only regards the continuity of the reachability correspondence
\mathcal{R}
rather than of the intersection correspondence
\Theta_{t}^{i}
.
To bridge this gap, we present the following lemma to guarantee the continuity of
\Theta_{t}^{i}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Gamma:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{Y}
be continuous, and let
\Gamma(\mathbf{x})
be compact and convex for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Consider a closed convex set
F\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that
\mathrm{int}(\Gamma(\mathbf{x})\cap F)\neq\varnothing
for all
\mathbf{x}\in C
, where
C\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is closed. Then, the correspondence
\Xi:C\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{Y}
defined by
\Xi(\mathbf{x})=\Gamma(\mathbf{x})\cap F
is continuous on
C
.

Proof.

Please see Appendix
A
for details.
∎

The following lemma provides insight into the continuity of marginal functions of the form

\phi(\mathbf{x})=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma(\mathbf{x})}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

(9)

Lemma 2

(Proposition 2.9 in
[
19
]
)
.

Consider a continuous function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a continuous correspondence
\Gamma:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{Y}
. If
\Gamma
has compact values, then the marginal function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in (

) is continuous.

Note that the value functions in (

Consequently, we can utilize Lemma

to prove the continuity of the value functions.
This result is stated in the following theorem.

.

For all
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
and

,

, the optimal value functions
V_{t}~(\cdot,j):\mathcal{Q}_{t-1}^{(i)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

are both
continuous.

.

, and for all

,
i_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
such that

\displaystyle V_{T}~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},i_{T})

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathbf{x}_{T}~\in~\Theta_{T}^{(i_{T})}(\mathbf{x}_{T-1})}c~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},\mathbf{x}_{T}),

\displaystyle V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})

(10b)

\displaystyle V_{0}~(i_{0})

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathbf{x}_{0}~\in~\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{(i_{0})}}~\max_{i_{1}~\in~\mathcal{N}_{i_{0}}}V_{1}~(\mathbf{x}_{0},i_{1}),

(10c)

Similarly, the optimal value functions of the Opponent are given as follows.

\displaystyle U_{T}~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},i_{T-1})

Optimal Policies

With the optimal value functions computed, Remark

Specifically,

\displaystyle\pi_{T}^{*}~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},i_{T})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}_{T}\;\in\;\Theta_{T}^{(i_{T})}(\mathbf{x}_{T-1})}c~(\mathbf{x}_{T-1},\mathbf{x}_{T}),

(16a)

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{*}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\;\in\;\Theta_{t}^{(i_{t})}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}\{c~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{t})+U_{t+1}~(\mathbf{x}_{t},i_{t})\},\quad\forall\;t=1,\ldots,T-1,

(16b)

\displaystyle\pi_{0}^{*}~(i_{0})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{(i_{0})}}U_{1}~(\mathbf{x}_{0},i_{0}).

(16c)

Similarly, for the Opponent the optimal policy can be obtained as follows

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{*}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t-1})

\displaystyle\in\argmax_{i_{t}~\in~\mathcal{N}_{i_{t-1}}}V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t}),\quad\forall\;t=1,\ldots,T,

(17a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}^{*}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\in\argmax_{i_{0}~\in~\mathcal{V}}V_{0}~(i_{0}).

(17b)

Consequently, given the Player state
\mathbf{x}
and Opponent state

However, since the

One natural approach is to properly discretize the domain of the
\mathbf{x}
-argument and approximate

and

\mathbf{x}
-domain.
Section

4.1
Lipschitz Continuity of the Value Functions

We impose the following two assumptions to ensure the Lipschitz continuity of
V_{t}
and
U_{t}
.

Assumption 7
.

The cost function

is Lipschitz continuous under the Manhattan distance.
That is, for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
,

\left|c~(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-c~(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right|\leq L_{c}\,(\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|+\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|),

where
L_{c}
denotes the Lipschitz constant.

Before introducing the next assumption, we first need to define the
Hausdorff distance
between two sets.

Definition 4

(Hausdorff distance
[
19
]
)
.

Given two subsets
E
and
F
of the normed space
\mathcal{X}
, the Hausdorff distance
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(E,F\right)
between

and
F
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(E,F\right)=\max\big\{\hskip-2.0pt\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in E}\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in F}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|\hskip-1.0pt,\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in F}\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in E}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|\hskip-2.0pt\big\}.

In case the sets
E
and
F
are compact, the sup and inf can be replaced by max and min, respectively,

Assumption 8
.

The correspondence
\Theta_{t}^{(i)}
is uniformly
L_{\Theta}
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to
i\in\mathcal{V}
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
under the Hausdorff distance.
That is, there exists a constant
L_{\Theta}
such that, for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, the following holds:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\Theta_{t}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}),\Theta_{t}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\leq L_{\Theta}\,\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|,\quad\forall\;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t-1}^{(j)},\text{ where }i\in\mathcal{N}_{j}.

.

Under Assumptions
7
and

,
i_{t-1}\in\mathcal{V}
and

(18)

\left|V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},i_{t})-V_{t}~(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t-1},i_{t})\right|\leq L_{v,t}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t-1}\right\|,

Remark 4
.

At every time step
t
, the discretized value
\widehat{V}_{t}
corresponds to the optimal worst-case performance of the Player using the discretization scheme
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
.
This implies that the Opponent has perfect knowledge of the discretization scheme used by the Player.
Moreover, the Opponent exploits this knowledge when maximizing the Player cost-to-go at every time step
t
.

If both the Player and Opponent apply strategies
\widehat{\pi}^{*}
and
\widehat{\sigma}^{*}

\widehat{U}_{0}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})
in (

In this case,

On the other hand, if the Opponent unilaterally deviates and applies a strategy different from
\widehat{\sigma}^{*}

\widehat{U}_{0}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})

Similarly, the total cost will be greater than or equal to
\widehat{U}_{0}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})
if the Player unilaterally deviates from
\widehat{\pi}^{*}

Whenever one agent deviates from its optimal strategy, the optimal strategy of the undeviated agent also changes accordingly.
Moreover, it is also noteworthy that

as it is shown in the next section,
which means that the approximate equilibrium induced by discretizing the Player’s domain yields, as expected, a lower Player performance than the actual equilibrium of the game.

4.3
Error Bounds for Discretization

In this subsection, we discuss the relation between the discretization size
\delta_{\mathcal{X},t}
and the performance level
\varepsilon
that bounds the discretization error
|\widehat{U}_{0}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})-U_{0}~(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Q})|

In the end, we introduce an algorithm that computes the discretized optimal policies.

Based on the Lipschitz continuity properties of the value functions, one expects to have a better approximation of the optimal value function with a finer mesh.
However, the discretized value function at time step

is computed based on the

Consequently, the approximation error propagates over time, and it is relatively unclear how large a discretization size

We answer the above question in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Given a discretization scheme
\{\delta_{\mathcal{X},t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
satisfying (

)-(
19c

for all

and
{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{t-1}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{t-1}^{(i_{t-1})}}

\displaystyle U_{t}~(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},i_{t-1})\leq\widehat{U}_{t}~(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},i_{t-1})

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have extended the convex body chasing problem to an adversarial setting, where a Player chases a sequence of convex bodies assigned adversarially by an Opponent.
We showed that under the assumption that the set of convex bodies is finite and known to both agents, max-min optimal policies can be obtained, which have a stronger performance guarantee than those in the classical CBC literature that use the competitive ratio.
We showed how to compute the optimal value of the game and proved its continuity under certain assumptions.
A discretization scheme has also been proposed to numerically solve for the value function of the game with
performance guarantees for the corresponding discretized policies.
Numerical examples verified the theoretical developments.
Future work will address the case of the probabilistic selection of the Opponent of the convex sets, and the extension of the numerical solution beyond box convex sets to address more realistic scenarios.
We also plan to
utilize this framework to introduce a cost structure to adversarial resource allocation problems, such as the dynamic Defender-Attacker Blotto Games
[
17
]
.

Lemma 9
.

Let the cost be defined by
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
.
This cost is 1-Lipschitz continuous,
that is, for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
,

\left|c~(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-c~(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right|\leq(\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}).

Proof.

For all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, using the Triangle Inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\left|c~(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-c~(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}-\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
P
and
Q
be boxes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
P\cap Q\neq\varnothing
.
Then, for all
\mathbf{q}\in Q
,

\argmin_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P}{\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}}\subseteq P\cap Q.

(37)

Consequently,

\min_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P}\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}=\min_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P\,\cap\,Q}\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}.

(38)

Proof.

Since
P
and
Q
are boxes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can write

\displaystyle P=\prod_{k=1}^{d}{\left[a_{k},b_{k}\right]},\qquad Q=\prod_{k=1}^{d}{\left[c_{k},d_{k}\right]},

for some
a_{k}<b_{k}
and
c_{k}<d_{k}
.
Since boxes and norms are compact and continuous, respectively, it follows that
\argmin_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P}\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}\neq\varnothing
for all
{\mathbf{q}\in Q}
.
Finding
\min_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P}\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}
is equivalent to finding
\min_{p_{k}\,\in\,[a_{k},b_{k}]}\left|p_{k}-q_{k}\right|
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Moreover,
{P~\cap~Q\neq\varnothing}
implies that
[a_{k},b_{k}]~\cap~[c_{k},d_{k}]\neq\varnothing
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Now, fix
\mathbf{q}\in Q
.
Let
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P}\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}
and
let
\mathcal{K}=\{k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}:q_{k}\notin[a_{k},b_{k}]\}
.
For all
k\in\mathcal{K}
, we have

\widehat{p}_{k}\in\argmin_{p_{k}\,\in\,[a_{k},b_{k}]}\left|p_{k}-q_{k}\right|\subseteq\{a_{k},b_{k}\}.

Since
[a_{k},b_{k}]~\cap~[c_{k},d_{k}]\neq\varnothing
, then at least one of
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
is within the interval
[c_{k},d_{k}]
.
We will next show that, for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
,
\widehat{p}_{k}\in[c_{k},d_{k}]
.
Without loss of generality, assume
b_{k}\in[c_{k},d_{k}]
and
a_{k}\notin[c_{k},d_{k}]
.
Since
a_{k}<b_{k}
,
b_{k}\in[c_{k},d_{k}]
and
a_{k}\notin[c_{k},d_{k}]
, then
a_{k}<c_{k}\leq b_{k}<q_{k}\leq d_{k}
, implying that
q_{k}-a_{k}>q_{k}-b_{k}>0
.
Therefore,
b_{k}\in\argmin_{p_{k}\,\in\,[a_{k},b_{k}]}\left|p_{k}-q_{k}\right|
, we have
\widehat{p}_{k}=b_{k}
.
We conclude that, for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
, we have
\widehat{p}_{k}\in[a_{k},b_{k}]~\cap~[c_{k},d_{k}]
.

For all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
q_{k}\in[a_{k},b_{k}]
, one can easily observe that
\widehat{p}_{k}=q_{k}
.
Hence,
\widehat{p}_{k}~\in~[a_{k},b_{k}]\cap[c_{k},d_{k}]
.
Finally, for fixed
\mathbf{q}\in Q
, let
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{p}\,\in\,P}\left\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}
.
We then have

\widehat{\mathbf{p}}\in\prod_{k=1}^{d}~\left([a_{k},b_{k}]~\cap~[c_{k},d_{k}]\right)=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}~[a_{k},b_{k}]\right)~\bigcap~\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}~[c_{k},d_{k}]\right)=P\cap Q.

This proves (
37
), which further implies (
38
).
∎

Lemma 11
.

The correspondence
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\rho\},

(39)

is 1-Lipschitz continuous under the Hausdorff distance.

Proof.

From (
39
), one can write
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})
as

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\left[x_{k}-\rho,x_{k}+\rho\right],

(40)

which is a box in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}
.
Notice that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{h}
, which implies that there exists
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
such that
\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\mathbf{h}\right\|_{2}
for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})
.
Consequently,

\min_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2},

which further implies that

\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})}~\min_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}~\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

In a similar way, one can also show that

\max_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}~\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})}~\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

From Definition
4
, we have that, for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\leq\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 12
.

The reachability correspondence
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{X}

26
) is 1-Lipschitz continuous under the Hausdorff distance.
That is, for all

\mathbb{R}^{d}

For all

From Lemma

\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
