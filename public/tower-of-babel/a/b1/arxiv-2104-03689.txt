Numerics and analysis of Cahn–Hilliard critical points
Tobias Grafke, Sebastian Scholtes, Alfred Wagner, Maria G. Westdickenberg

Abstract
We explore recent progress and open questions concerning local minima and saddle points of the Cahn–Hilliard energy in d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and the critical parameter regime of large system size and mean value close to − 1 -1 . We employ the String Method of E, Ren, and Vanden-Eijnden—a numerical algorithm for computing transition pathways in complex systems—in d = 2 d=2 to gain additional insight into the properties of the minima and saddle point. Motivated by the numerical observations, we employ a method of Caffarelli and Spruck to study convexity of level sets in d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 .



Keywords.
Cahn-Hilliard, complex energy landscapes, saddle points, String Method.

1
Introduction

The Cahn–Hilliard energy is a fundamental model of phase separation, with wide-ranging applications in materials science and other natural sciences. Introduced by Cahn and Hilliard to capture the mixing of a binary alloy, both the energy and the dynamics have been the subject of extensive numerical and analytical studies; we refer for instance to
[
6
,
7
,
23
]
and the articles cited therein. Despite the large body of existing work on the model, significant open questions remain.

In this paper we analyze critical points of the renormalized energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\phi}^{\xi}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}\frac{\phi}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\phi}\Big(G(u)-G(-1+\phi)\Big)\,dx

(1.1)

on

\displaystyle X_{\phi}:=\Big\{u\in H^{1}\cap L^{4}(\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}):\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}u\,dx=-1+\phi\Big\},

(1.2)

where
\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}
is the
d
-dimensional torus with side length
\phi L
. Here
G
is a nondegenerate double-well potential with minima
\pm 1
(the standard choice being
G(u)=\frac{1}{4}(1-u^{2})^{2}
); see Remark
1.1
below. We are interested in the so-called critical regime, that is, the regime such that

\displaystyle L\sim\phi^{-(d+1)/d}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi\ll 1.

We recall the origins of the problem in Section
2
below.

In
[
12
]
the existence of a nonconstant
local energy minimizer
and
saddle point
of the energy in the critical regime was established. Moreover, the following
constrained energy minimizers
were introduced and analyzed:
functions
u_{\omega,\phi}
that minimize
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}^{\xi}
subject to

\displaystyle u_{\omega,\phi}\in X_{\phi}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu(u_{\omega,\phi})=\omega,

(1.3)

where the “volume” of a function
u\in X_{\phi}
is defined as

\displaystyle\nu(u):=\int_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}\chi(u)\,dx,

(1.4)

for a smooth function
\chi
such that
\chi(s)=0
for
s\leq 1-2\phi^{1/3}
and
\chi(s)=1
for
s\geq 1-\phi^{1/3}
. Since it can be shown that volume-constrained minimizers are bounded above by
1+\phi^{1/3}
,
\nu
roughly measures the volume
|\{u\approx 1\}|
.

While much is known about the shape of minimizers on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see for instance
[
16
,
2
,
20
]
), the shape of minimizers on bounded domains is less well understood; as seminal works on the torus, we mention
[
18
,
4
]
. The Cahn-Hilliard problem on the torus features a competition between the periodic boundary conditions and the energetic preference for spherical level sets. We are interested in
understanding and quantifying this competition
. Note that this issue is more than a matter of mathematical/geometric curiosity; the appropriateness of the often-used sphericity assumption is of interest in nucleation theory in physics and engineering applications; see for instance
[
21
]
.

Quantitative estimates
on the energy and shape of critical points were derived in
[
12
,
13
]
. For example the Fraenkel asymmetry of the superlevel sets of
u_{\omega,\phi}
is shown to be bounded by
\phi^{1/6}

[
12
]
; see Section
2
below for more detail. In addition, the volume-constrained minimizers are shown to be equal to their Steiner symmetrization
[
13
]
and hence have connected superlevel sets. It is
plausible
that the saddle point (whose existence was pointed out already by Cahn and Hilliard in
[
7
]
and is established via a mountain-pass argument in
[
12
]
) is in fact equal to a volume-constrained minimizer of appropriate volume, however this has not yet been proved. Hence although bounds are obtained for the constrained minimizers,
[
12
,
13
]
stop short of establishing such bounds for the actual saddle point.

Given the open questions in the analysis, in this article we apply the String Method
[
9
]
to look numerically at the local minimum and saddle point of the Cahn–Hilliard energy in
d=2
in Section
3
.
The String Method is a well-known algorithm for computing transition pathways in complex systems. Given two local minimizers of an energy, it locates a saddle point on the boundary of the corresponding basins of attraction. Alternatively, given two arbitrary states in distinct basins of attraction, the string evolves to find both the local minima of the basins and a saddle point between them. We review the method in Subsection
3.1
and summarize our numerical results in Subsection
3.3
. Then in Section
4
, motivated in part by our numerical observations, we study convexity of the sub/superlevel sets.
In particular, the results of the String Method suggest that for both the minimum and saddle point there is a value
t_{*}\in(-1,1)
such that

\displaystyle\text{the superlevel sets }\{u>t\}\text{ for $t\geq t_{*}$ and the sublevel sets }\{u<t\}\text{ for $t\leq t_{*}$}

are convex.
While proving such a fact is currently out of reach, we show how the method of Caffarelli and Spruck
[
5
]
can be applied to obtain a partial result in this direction; see Proposition
4.6
.

Background and open questions

The original Cahn–Hilliard energy landscape consists of the energy

\displaystyle E(u):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+G(u)\,dx

(2.1)

considered on the set

\displaystyle\Big\{u\in H^{1}\cap L^{4}(\Omega):\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=m\Big\},

where the mean value
m
is a given value strictly between the minima of
G
. Studying the problem on a large domain
|\Omega|
is equivalent, after a rescaling of space, to studying the energy

\displaystyle E_{\phi}(u):=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\frac{\phi}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\phi}G(u)\,dx

for
\phi\ll 1
on the fixed domain
\Omega_{1}
. It is well-known since the seminal work of Modica and Mortola
[
22
]
that for

fixed and
\phi\downarrow 0
, the energy
E_{\phi}

\Gamma

Droplet formation or nucleation in a low-density phase is more subtle, as is pointed out by Biskup, Chayes, and Kotecky in
[

For the Cahn-Hilliard model,

probed the

between large system size and mean value close to

\displaystyle\phi\sim L^{-d/(d+1)}

in the following sense: There is a dimension-dependent constant
\xi_{d}>0
such that for
\xi<\xi_{d}
and

\displaystyle\phi=\xi L^{-d/(d+1)},

the global minimizer

\xi>\xi_{d}

is
not

When the limit energy

or about the shape or properties of this set. This gap in the analysis motivates this paper.

2.1
Quantitative bounds

In
[
12
]
, the following bounds were developed for the local minimizer
u_{m,\phi}
and volume-constrained minimizers
u_{\omega,\phi}
.
The local minimum
u_{m,\phi}
has energy and “volume” close to
c_{m}
and
\nu_{m}
with the bounds

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{E}_{\phi}^{\xi}(u_{m,\phi})-c_{m}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\phi^{1/3},

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\nu(u_{m,\phi})-\nu_{m}|

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\xi)\phi^{1/6}.

(2.4)

Here and throughout,
\lesssim
is used to indicate an upper bound by a constant depending at most on the dimension and the potential
G
. In addition,
u_{m,\phi}
is estimated in
L^{2}
against the function
\Psi_{m}
that is
+1
in a ball of volume
\nu_{m}
and
-1
otherwise according to

\displaystyle|u_{m,\phi}-\Psi_{m}|_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}C(\xi)\phi^{1/6}&\text{for}\quad d=2,3\\
\phi^{1/(2d)}&\text{for}\quad d\geq 4,\end{cases}

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle|u-\Psi_{m}|_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}

\displaystyle:=\inf_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}||u-\Psi_{m}(\cdot-x_{0})||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\phi L})},

and
\Psi_{m}
is understood in the periodic sense. In addition via elementary arguments one obtains the following bound on the volume of the interfacial region:

\displaystyle\lvert\{-1+2\phi^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq u_{m,\phi}\leq 1-2\phi^{\frac{1}{3}}\}\rvert\lesssim\phi^{1/3}.

(2.6)

The analogous results hold for
u_{\omega,\phi}
with volume
\omega
and energy
f_{\xi}(\omega)
. Our numerical investigation in Subsection
3.3
is in part intended to explore whether these exponents are sharp.

Similarly, let
\tilde{u}_{s}
denote any volume-constrained minimum with energy
c_{s}+O(\phi^{1/3})
. Then we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{\phi}^{\xi}(\tilde{u}_{s})-c_{s}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\phi^{1/3},

(2.7)

\displaystyle|\nu(\tilde{u}_{s})-\nu_{s}|

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\xi)\phi^{1/6},

(2.8)

and

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}_{s}-\Psi_{s}|_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}C(\xi)\phi^{1/6}&\text{for}\quad d=2,3\\
\phi^{1/(2d)}&\text{for}\quad d\geq 4,\end{cases}

(2.9)

where
\Psi_{s}
is
+1
in a ball of volume
\nu_{s}
and
-1
otherwise. The
saddle point

u_{s,\phi}
obeys (
2.6
) and(
2.7
). We present numerical results in Subsection
3.3
that explore whether it also satisfies (
2.8
) and (
2.9
).

In
[
13
]
it is also shown that the constrained minima are
equal to their Steiner symmetrization
with respect to some point in the torus. This yields in particular connectedness of appropriate superlevel and sublevel sets. Open questions include whether the constrained minima are unique and whether their level sets are convex. The numerical results in Subsection
3.3
support these conjectures. A partial result on convexity of superlevel sets is given in Proposition
4.6
.

3
Numerical results

In this section we compute the minimum and saddle point
configurations numerically for a range of values of
\phi
in order to compare
against the scaling predictions outlined above.
To find
the minimum, we can simply start from a localized approximate droplet shape and
relax via the Cahn-Hilliard dynamics until they become (approximately) stationary. In order to find the saddle points without
inserting any prior knowledge of the system, we will compute
the heteroclinic orbit connecting the spatially homogeneous solution
to the droplet minimum. This heteroclinic orbit must necessarily pass
through a saddle point in-between, which can then easily be
identified. In order to compute this heteroclinic orbit, we employ the
String Method
[
9
]
.

There are other algorithms to compute stable and unstable
fixed points of dynamical systems, including gentlest ascent
dynamics
[
11
]
, Newton methods, and edge tracking and
dimer-type algorithms
[
17
,
19
]
. Here, we use the String Method despite its
comparable computational inefficiency because (1) it does not require
higher derivatives of the system, (2) it does not rely on approximate
knowledge of the saddle point configuration, and (3) is very simple to
implement given an existing code integrating the dynamics.

3.1
The String Method

The String Method starts with a collection of states in phase space (a
“string” of states). In the first step, one evolves each of the
states forward in time. In the second step, one reparameterizes the
string to keep the states well-separated in phase space. As an
example, consider the gradient flow

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla V(x).

Suppose that
x_{*}
and
x_{**}
are two isolated minima of
V
. We
want to find the orbit connecting
x_{*}
and
x_{**}
, i.e., the curve

\gamma
connecting
x_{*}
and
x_{**}
such that

\displaystyle(\nabla V)^{\perp}(\gamma)=0,

where
(\nabla V)^{\perp}
is the component of
\nabla V
normal to

\gamma
:

\displaystyle(\nabla V)^{\perp}(\gamma)=\nabla V(\gamma)-(\nabla V(\gamma),\hat{\tau})\hat{\tau}.

Here
\hat{\tau}
represents the unit tangent vector to the curve

\gamma
and
(\cdot,\cdot)
represents the Euclidean inner
product. Note that the orbit will pass through at least one additional
critical point, the saddle point that we seek.

The first step is to choose a method of parameterizing paths

\{\phi(\alpha)\colon\alpha\in[0,1]\}
. Parameterization by
arclength is a convenient choice (although other methods offer
advantages; see
[
10
]
). Given any initial path
\gamma_{0}

connecting
x_{*}
and
x_{**}
, we will generate the family of
time-dependent paths
\phi(\alpha,t)
in the following way. In the
original String Method, the evolution is prescribed as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}=-\nabla V(\phi)^{\perp}+\lambda\hat{\tau},\qquad\phi(\alpha,0)=\gamma_{0},

where
\dot{\phi}
denotes the time derivative of
\phi
. The first term
on the right-hand side moves the string in the direction normal to the
string and the second term on the right-hand side is a Lagrange
multiplier term that acts in the tangential direction and enforces the
chosen parameterization. Numerical implementation consists of
discretizing the path into the images
\{\phi_{i}(t),i=0,1,\ldots,M\}

and following a two-step method: first evolve according to

\dot{\phi}_{i}=-\nabla V(\phi_{i})^{\perp}
, and second reparameterize. Note
that the reparameterization is essential, since otherwise the points
will cluster around the minima.

The simplified String Method is based upon the observation that we can
absorb part of
(\nabla V(\phi))^{\perp}
in the tangential component
and that doing so simplifies the algorithm and improves both stability
and accuracy
[
10
]
. In this case, the evolution of the string is
best conceptualized as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}=-\nabla V(\phi)+\bar{\lambda}\hat{\tau},

where
\bar{\lambda}=(\nabla V(\gamma),\hat{\tau})+\lambda
. The first
step is then to evolve according to the simplified dynamics

\dot{\phi}_{i}=-\nabla V(\phi_{i})
and the second step is, as before, to
reparameterize. See
[
10
]
for a full discussion.

We say that the string has converged when there is a point along the
string and away from the endpoints where the energy gradient vanishes
(to within some tolerance). In our application, we find only one
critical point between the local minima. We then read off the local
minimizers at the endpoints of the string and the saddle point as the
interior zero of the energy gradient.

Remark 3.1

(Generalizations)
.

We note that one does not need to know
x_{*}
a priori. If one
endpoint of the initial string lies in the basin of attraction of

x_{*}
and is left free, the end of the string will evolve
automatically toward the stable stationary point.

We also note that the applicability of the String Method is not
limited to gradient flows (although it has been mainly used in that
context). One can consider

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=b(x),\qquad x(0)=x_{0}

for general vector fields
b
and use the String Method to find
the orbit connecting
x_{0}
and a zero
x_{*}
of
b
, i.e., a
path
\gamma
connecting
x_{0}
and
x_{*}
such that

\displaystyle(b)^{\perp}(\gamma)=0.

3.2
Implementation and convergence

We implemented the String Method with a simple pseudo-spectral
algorithm and the FFT in MATLAB. One string typically had a size of

512
images, although for
\phi\in\{0.025,0.0125\}
we had to take
reduce to
256
to cut down the size of the string. Computations were
done in double precision. The spatial mesh size was chosen to be

\displaystyle N_{x}:=2\left\lceil\frac{\xi^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\phi^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right\rceil

and the size of the time step was set to
N_{t}:=N_{x}^{2}\phi
. When
computing the saddle point for
\phi=0.05

\phi=0.0125

criterion, so we let it run for as long as we could. The tolerance
was reduced relatively quickly (approximately 439556.72 s) to
approximately the level that we achieved in the end. After that
results were slow to improve.

4
Convexity

In the following sections we consider the volume-constrained minimizers; that is, we consider the minimizers of the energy (
1.1
)
over the set

\displaystyle X_{\omega,\phi}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}\cap L^{4}(\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}):\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}u\,dx=-1+\phi,\quad\nu(u)=\omega\right\},

(4.1)

where the “volume”
\nu(\cdot)
is given by (
1.4
).
We are interested in geometric properties of the minimizers.
For simplicity of notation, we will write
u
instead of
u_{\omega,\phi}
.

We would like to understand convexity of the superlevel sets of the constrained minimizers. Based on the numerics (see for instance Figure
3.3
), it seems likely that the superlevel sets are convex not only near the maximum but for all values up to some critical value, at which point the convexity changes and the sublevel sets around the minimum become convex. Such a result is currently out of reach, but we will adapt a method of Caffarelli and Spruck to demonstrate convexity of superlevel sets under Hypothesis
(H2)
, below. Before turning to this result, we summarize some basic facts from the previous analysis.

We begin by recalling two results from
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
.
First, according to the mean condition, the definition of
\nu(\cdot)
,
and Lemma 2.9 from
[
12
]
, one has the following uniform bounds.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
\omega_{1}>0
there exists
\phi_{0}>0
such that for all
\phi\in(0,\phi_{0})
, any volume-constrained minimizer
u
for

\omega\in[\omega_{1},\xi^{3}/2]
satisfies

\displaystyle-1-\phi^{1/3}\leq\inf_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}u\leq-1+\phi\leq 1-2\phi^{1/3}\leq\sup_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}u\leq 1+\phi^{1/3}.

(4.2)

The upper bound
\phi_{0}
from Lemma
4.1
is the only restriction we will place on
\phi>0
for the rest of the article. As a consequence of the uniform bounds, any constrained minimizer is smooth and satisfies the Euler Lagrange equation

\displaystyle-\phi\Delta u+\frac{1}{\phi}G^{\prime}(u)+\lambda_{\phi}+\lambda_{\omega}\chi^{\prime}(u)=0\qquad\hbox{in}\>\mathbb{T}_{\phi L},

(4.3)

where
\lambda_{\phi}
,
\lambda_{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}
are Lagrange parameters corresponding to the mean and volume constraints, respectively.
Next we recall the symmetry of the constrained minimizers.
We assume without loss of generality that the location of the unique maximum of
u
is
x=0
.
By symmetry it is often sufficient to restrict to the first quadrant, i.e., to

\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}^{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}:x_{i}>0,\,i=1,\ldots,d\}.

Theorem 4.2

(Theorem 1.1,
[
13
]
)
.

Any volume-constrained minimizer
u
is equal (up to a translation) to its Steiner symmetrization about the origin. In particular, its superlevel sets are simply connected, and

u
is strictly decreasing along all rays starting at the (unique) maximum of
u
. In fact for any ray
e
starting in the maximum, the directional derivative is strictly negative:

\displaystyle\partial_{e}u<0\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}^{+}.

(4.4)

Remark 4.3
.

The first part of the theorem is precisely Theorem 1.1 from
[
13
]
, and (
4.4
) and the nonvanishing of the gradient follow from the Strong Maximum Principle, as shown in the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
.

4.1
Convexity method of Caffarelli and Spruck

We will now use the Euler Lagrange equation (
4.3
) and a method that Caffarelli and Spruck introduced in
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]

to establish convexity of the superlevel sets of volume-constrained minimizers
u=u_{\omega,\phi}
under an assumption on the nonlinearity, specified in Hypothesis
(H2)
, below.

Remark 4.4
.

By symmetry of
u
we have for
i\neq j
that

\displaystyle\partial_{i}u(0,\ldots,0,x_{j},0,\ldots,0)=0.

Hence the Hessian matrix
D^{2}u(0)
is diagonal, and we have

\displaystyle\left(D^{2}u(0)\right)_{ij}=\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij}

where
(\delta_{ij})_{ij}
denotes the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Because
x=0
is a maximum point of
u
, we have
\lambda_{i}\leq 0
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. More is true:

Lemma 4.5
.

The origin is a non-degenerate critical point of any volume-constrained minimizer
u
, and
\partial^{2}_{e}u(0)<0
for any ray
e
starting in the origin.

Proof.

Let
e
be any ray starting in the origin. We choose
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that

e
points into the halfspace
\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}^{i}:=\{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}:x_{i}>0\}
.
According to Theorem
4.2
, we have

\partial_{e}u<0
in
\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}^{i}
. Note that by (
4.3
) the
function
\partial_{e}u
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle-\phi\Delta(\partial_{e}u)+\left(\frac{1}{\phi}G^{\prime\prime}(u)+\lambda_{\omega}\>\chi^{\prime\prime}(u)\right)\partial_{e}u=0\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}^{i}$. }

Also for
\phi
sufficiently small, there holds

\displaystyle\sup_{\{u>1-\phi^{\frac{1}{3}}\}}\frac{1}{\phi}G^{\prime\prime}(u)>0.

(4.5)

For fixed
0<\phi\ll 1
, we choose a radius
\rho>0
small enough so that the ball
B_{\rho}(0)
satisfies

B_{\rho}(0)\Subset\{u>1-\phi^{\frac{1}{3}}\}.

Then the definition of
\chi
implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi}G^{\prime\prime}(u)+\lambda_{\omega}\>\chi^{\prime\prime}(u)=\frac{1}{\phi}G^{\prime\prime}(u)\,\overset{\eqref{cpos}}{>}\,0\qquad\text{for all }x\in B_{\rho}(0).

Because the function
\partial_{e}u
takes its maximum on
\overline{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}^{i}\cap B_{\rho}(0)}
in
x=0
,
Hopf’s maximum principle hence yields

\lambda=\partial^{2}_{e}u(0)<0
. Consequently
x=0
is a non-degenerate maximum point.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
4.5
, the superlevel sets
S_{c}:=\{u>c\}

are strictly convex for all
c
sufficiently close to
u_{max}:=\sup_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}u
, which
according to (
4.2
) is close to
1
. Thus there exists
c^{*}<u_{max}
such that
S_{c}

is convex for all
c^{*}\leq c<u_{max}
.

We would now like to investigate convexity of superlevel sets of
u
further away from
u_{max}
.
Suppose that
c_{*}>\inf_{\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}}u
is some value such that

Hypothesis (H1)
:

\displaystyle S_{c_{*}}=\{u>c_{*}\}\text{ is convex and precompact and }\partial S_{c_{*}}\text{ is smooth.}

We assume that
c^{*}>c_{*}
since otherwise there is nothing to prove.
We will show that all sublevel sets
S_{c}
for
c_{*}<c<c^{*}
are convex.

In the remainder of this section we will write the Euler-Lagrange equation of
u
in the form

\Delta u=f(u)
, where

\displaystyle f(u):=\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}G^{\prime}(u)+\frac{\lambda_{\phi}}{\phi}+\frac{\lambda_{\omega}}{\phi}\chi^{\prime}(u).

(4.6)

Note that because of the smoothness of
G
and
\chi
,
f
is also smooth. We will work under the assumption:

Hypothesis (H2)
:

\displaystyle f(c_{*})\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\prime}(c)>0\quad\hbox{for all}\quad c_{*}<c<u_{max}.

Our main theorem is:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
u
solve
\Delta u=f(u)
in
\mathbb{T}_{\phi L}
, where
f
is given by (
4.6
).
Assume
u
takes its unique maximum point
u_{max}
in
x=0
and
\partial_{e}u<0
along all rays
e
starting at the origin.
Let
c_{*}<u_{max}
be a level such that Hypotheses
(H1)
and
(H2)
hold.
Then
S_{c}
is convex
for all
c_{*}<c<u_{max}
.

In light of Theorem
4.2
and translating if necessary, the assumption of the unique maximum in zero and directional derivative on rays is without loss of generality for the desired application.

Following the strategy of
[
5
]
, we will work with the following definitions.

Definition 4.7
.

We will call three points
x,y,z\in S_{c_{*}}
a
triple
if
z=tx+(1-t)y
for some
t\in[0,1]
, and we will write
z\in[x,y]
for short and speak of “the triple”
(x,y,z)
. We will call a triple
admissible
if it is an element of

\displaystyle{\cal{A}}:=\{(x,y,z)\colon x,y\in S_{c_{*}},\;x,y,z\hbox{ are a triple},\,\text{and }\>u(x)>u(z)\}.

(4.7)

Note that
{\cal{A}}
is not empty since
u
is strictly decreasing along all rays emanating from the origin.
Now consider the function

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x,y,z):=u(y)-u(z),\qquad\hbox{for}\quad(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}.

Because of the equivalence in Lemma
4.8
below, we will be interested in

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}}\mathcal{F}(x,y,z).

Let
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})\in{\cal{A}}
be a maximizing sequence for
\mathcal{F}
.
By compactness of
\overline{S_{c_{*}}}
, there exist limit
points
x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\in\overline{S_{c_{*}}}
. By passing to suitable subsequences,
we may assume that
x_{k}\to x_{0}
,
y_{k}\to y_{0}
and
z_{k}\to z_{0}
.
Moreover

\displaystyle z_{0}=t_{0}x_{0}+(1-t_{0})y_{0}\qquad\text{for some $t_{0}\in[0,1]$}

(4.8)

and
u(x_{0})\geq u(z_{0})
. A priori it may be that

x_{0}=z_{0}
or
z_{0}=y_{0}
or even
x_{0}=z_{0}=y_{0}
, however see Remark
4.10
.

In
[
5
]
it is observed that
\mathcal{F}
can be used to capture convexity:

Lemma 4.8
.

The convexity of
S_{c}
for all
c_{*}\leq c\leq u_{max}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}}\mathcal{F}(x,y,z)\leq 0.

(4.9)

Proof.

Step 1
. We begin by assuming that the sets
S_{c}
are convex for all
c_{*}<c<u_{max}
.
Let
(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}
. If
u(y)>u(x)
, then convexity of the
superlevel sets gives
u(z)\geq u(x)
for all
z\in[x,y]
and
(x,y,z)
is not admissible.
Hence any admissible triple satisfies
u(x)\geq u(y)
. However then
the convexity of the superlevel sets gives
u(z)\geq u(y)
, so that
\mathcal{F}(x,y,z)\leq 0
, and taking the supremum over

{\cal{A}}
preserves this inequality.

Step 2
. We now assume
\sup_{(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}}\mathcal{F}(x,y,z)\leq 0
and will show that
S_{c}
is convex for all
c_{*}<c<u_{max}
by showing that for any

x,y\in S_{c}
and any
z\in[x,y]
, there holds
z\in S_{c}
.
On the one hand, if
(x,y,z)
is admissible, then our assumption on
\mathcal{F}
yields
u(z)\geq u(y)>c
(and
z\in S_{c}
). On the other hand if
(x,y,z)

is not admissible, then by definition
4.7
there holds

u(z)\geq u(x)>c
(and
z\in S_{c}
).
∎

In order to prove the convexity of
S_{c}
for all
c_{*}\leq c\leq u_{max}
, we will use Lemma
4.8
and argue by
contradiction. Thus we assume that (
4.9
) does not hold and we will discuss the extremal situation, by which we mean the following:

Definition 4.9
.

We will call
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
a
positive extremal
if there exists a maximizing sequence
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})\in{\cal{A}}
with
x_{k}\to x_{0}
,
y_{k}\to y_{0}
and
z_{k}\to z_{0}
such that

(
P_{1}
)

x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\in\overline{S_{c_{*}}}
and
z_{0}=t_{0}x_{0}+(1-t_{0})y_{0}
for some
t_{0}\in[0,1]
;

(
P_{2}
)

u(x_{0})\geq u(z_{0})
;

(
P_{3}
)

there holds
0<\sup_{(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}}\mathcal{F}(x,y,z)=\mathcal{F}(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
, so that

\displaystyle u(y_{0})>u(z_{0}).

(4.10)

Our plan in order to show convexity is as follows.

•

Using maximizing sequences
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})
,
we derive necessary conditions for any positive extremal
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.
The proofs rely heavily on Lemma
4.5
, however they do not explicitly use the equation for
u
. In particular
(H2)
is not needed.

•

We then assume for a contradiction that
\sup_{(x,y,z)\in{\cal{A}}}\mathcal{F}(x,y,z)>0
. It is only here that Hypothesis
(H2)
is needed.
The necessary conditions for the limit
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
are used to define admissible sequences
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})
that lead to a contradiction.

Remark 4.10
.

Clearly for any positive extremal there holds
y_{0}\neq z_{0}
(i.e.,
t_{0}\neq 0
), since this would imply
\mathcal{F}(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=0
, in violation of (
P_{3}
). Also

x_{0}\neq y_{0}
, since this would yield
x_{0}=y_{0}=z_{0}
(by (
P_{1}
)), again violating (
P_{3}
). On the other hand, the case
x_{0}=z_{0}
cannot be excluded.

Lemma 4.11
.

If the triple
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
is a positive extremal, then
y_{0}\neq 0
.

Proof.

For a contradiction we consider any triple
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
with
y_{0}=0
, i.e.,

y_{0}
is the unique point
where
u
takes its maximum. We will show that
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
is not the limit of a sequence of admissible
triples
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})\in{\cal{A}}
as
k\to\infty
. Thus assume that there exists a sequence
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})\in{\cal{A}}

such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.
We distinguish two cases.

1. If
y_{0}=0
and
x_{0}\neq z_{0}
,
then by (
P_{1}
), we have
z_{0}=tx_{0}
for some
t\in(0,1]
. Since by Lemma
4.5
),

u
is strictly decreasing along any ray starting in the origin, there holds
u(z_{0})>u(x_{0})
, which contradicts (
P_{2}
).

2. Now suppose
y_{0}=0
and
x_{0}=z_{0}
.
Because
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})
is admissible, we have
u(x_{k})>u(z_{k})
. From the collinearity of
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
and
y_{0}=0
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k}-z_{k}}{|x_{k}-z_{k}|}=\frac{x_{k}-y_{k}}{|x_{k}-y_{k}|}\to\frac{x_{0}}{|x_{0}|}=:e_{0}\quad\text{as $k\to\infty$.}

Hence

\displaystyle 0<\frac{u(x_{k})-u(z_{k})}{|x_{k}-z_{k}|}=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{x_{k}-z_{k}}{|x_{k}-z_{k}|}\cdot Du(sx_{k}+(1-s)z_{k})\>ds\to e_{0}\cdot Du(x_{0})=\partial_{e_{0}}u(x_{0}),

and we obtain in the limit
k\to\infty
that

\partial_{e_{0}}u(x_{0})\geq 0,

which contradicts the fact that the directional derivative is strictly negative along rays from the origin (cf. Theorem
4.2
).

∎

By definition, any positive extremal lies in
\overline{S_{c_{*}}}
; we now show that, in fact, any positive extremal lies in the interior.

Lemma 4.12
.

For any positive extremal
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
, there holds

\displaystyle\text{(i) }\,y_{0}\in S_{c_{*}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\text{(ii) }\,z_{0}\in S_{c_{*}}.

Proof.

Since necessarily
y_{0},z_{0}\in\overline{S_{c_{*}}}
, the issue is to show
y_{0},z_{0}\notin\partial S_{c_{*}}
. We begin by considering
y_{0}
. Since
z_{0}\in\overline{S_{c_{*}}}
, we have
u(z_{0})\geq c_{*}
. Combining this with (
4.10
) implies (i).

We now turn to
z_{0}
. Let us assume for a contradiction that
z_{0}\in\partial S_{c_{*}}
,
i.e.,
u(z_{0})=c_{*}
. By (i) we have
y_{0}\in S_{c_{*}}
. By (
P_{1}
) the points
x_{0}
,
y_{0}

and
z_{0}
are collinear. Since
S_{c_{*}}
is convex, the ray that starts in
y_{0}
and passes
through
z_{0}
and
x_{0}
intersects
\partial S_{c_{*}}
in exactly one point. By (
P_{1}
) and
(
P_{2}
) the point
x_{0}
must be in
\partial S_{c_{*}}
and thus
x_{0}=z_{0}
. Now let

(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})\in{\cal{A}}
be a maximizing sequence converging to
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
as
in Definition
4.9
. For sufficiently large
k
the following holds:
Since
x_{k},z_{k}\to x_{0}=z_{0}\in\partial S_{c_{*}}
, for
k
large there exists a ray that
starts in
y_{k}
, passes through

z_{k}
and
x_{k}
and intersects
\partial S_{c_{*}}
in exactly one point.
This point is denoted by
\tilde{x}_{k}
. Note that
x_{k}\in[\tilde{x}_{k},z_{k}]
and
\tilde{x}_{k}\to x_{0}
as
k\to\infty
. Since
(H1)
holds and since
\partial_{e}u<0
on rays from the origin, for
k
sufficiently large there holds
\partial_{\nu}u(\tilde{x}_{k})<0
, where
\nu
denotes the outer unit normal vector in
\tilde{x}_{k}\in\partial S_{c_{*}}
. This implies that
u
is strictly decreasing along
[z_{k},\tilde{x}_{k}]
and hence
u(z_{k})>u(x_{k})
, contradicting the admissibility of
(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})
. As a consequence
z_{0}\notin\partial S_{c_{*}}
.
∎

4.2
Optimality conditions for extremals

We assume
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
is a positive extremal, so that in particular (
4.10
) holds.
Note that

\displaystyle z_{0}=t_{0}\,x_{0}+(1-t_{0})\,y_{0}

for some
t_{0}\in(0,1]
.
Our first optimality condition is:

Lemma 4.13
.

For any positive extremal with
0<t_{0}\leq 1
we have

\displaystyle T_{4}=\epsilon(p)-T_{2}-b^{2}\,p\cdot D^{2}u(z_{0})\cdot p+b\,p\cdot D^{2}u(z_{0})\cdot p-\frac{1}{\alpha}T_{3}

for some function
\epsilon(p)
with
\epsilon(0)=0
and
\epsilon(p)>0
for

. Then for
p\neq 0
the perturbed sequence satisfies the admissibility condition (
4.51
), since

\displaystyle 0<u(x_{k})-u(z_{k})=(1-t_{k})^{3}\epsilon(p)+O((1-t_{k})^{4}).

In the next step we insert the expression for
T_{4}
into (
4.52
). Then maximality of
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
implies that the following inequality must hold:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq u(y_{k})-u(z_{k})-(u(y_{0})-u(z_{0}))

\displaystyle=(1-t_{k})^{2}\left\{-b\,\epsilon(p)+\left(\frac{1}{b}+b-1\right)\,T_{3}+b\,T_{2}+\left(b^{3}-\frac{3}{2}b^{2}\right)\,p\cdot D^{2}u(z_{0})\cdot p+\frac{1}{2}p\cdot D^{2}u(y_{0})\cdot p\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+O((1-t_{k})^{3}).

We will be done if we can show that this last inequality does not hold.

Let

\displaystyle W(p):=-b\,\epsilon(p)+\left(\frac{1}{b}+b-1\right)\,T_{3}+b\,T_{2}+\left(b^{3}-\frac{3}{2}b^{2}\right)\,p\cdot D^{2}u(z_{0})\cdot p+\frac{1}{2}p\cdot D^{2}u(y_{0})\cdot p.

Clearly
W(0)=0

\Delta_{p}W(p)
:

Since
T_{3}

p

\Delta_{p}T_{3}=0
. Moreover

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}\left(p\cdot D^{2}u(z_{0})\cdot p\right)=2\,\Delta u(z_{0})=2\,f(u(z_{0})),\quad\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{p}\left(p\cdot D^{2}u(y_{0})\cdot p\right)=\Delta u(y_{0})=f(u(y_{0})),

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}T_{2}=-b^{2}(y_{0}-z_{0})\cdot\nabla u(z_{0})=0

4.13

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}W(p)=-b\Delta_{p}\epsilon(p)+\left(2b^{3}-3b^{2}+1\right)\,f(u(z_{0}))+f(u(y_{0}))-f(u(z_{0})).

We now define
\epsilon(p)

\displaystyle\epsilon(p):=\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2n\,b}|p|^{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{p\in B_{r}(0)}W(p)\geq c(\gamma)r^{2}.

This is the desired contradiction.
