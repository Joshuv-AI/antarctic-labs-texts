Abstract
Top- k k error has become a popular metric for large-scale classification benchmarks due to the inevitable semantic ambiguity among classes. Existing literature on top- k k optimization generally focuses on the optimization method of the top- k k objective, while ignoring the limitations of the metric itself. In this paper, we point out that the top- k k objective lacks enough discrimination such that the induced predictions may give a totally irrelevant label a top rank. To fix this issue, we develop a novel metric named partial Area Under the top- k k Curve (AUTKC). Theoretical analysis shows that AUTKC has a better discrimination ability, and its Bayes optimal score function could give a correct top- K K ranking with respect to the conditional probability. This shows that AUTKC does not allow irrelevant labels to appear in the top list. Furthermore, we present an empirical surrogate risk minimization framework to optimize the proposed metric. Theoretically, we present (1) a sufficient condition for Fisher consistency of the Bayes optimal score function; (2) a generalization upper bound which is insensitive to the number of classes under a simple hyperparameter setting. Finally, the experimental results on four benchmark datasets validate the effectiveness of our proposed framework.

\min_{f}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\sum_{k}\ell_{k}\left(f;\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right],

(C1)

\ell_{k}

(C2)

Targeting

•

•

Related Work

II-A

TOP-k Optimization

The early literature on top-
k
optimization focuses on the binary scenarios where only
the top-ranked instances
are of interest, such as retrieval (relevant/irrelevant)
[
13
]
and recommend (like/dislike)
[
14
]
. These methods minimize a pairwise ranking loss that punishes more on the errors related to the top-ranked instances
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

As the scale of classes grows rapidly in modern benchmark datasets
[
5
,
1
]
, the semantic overlap between classes inevitably emerges. In this light, the top-
k
error has been extended to multiclass classification
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
1
]
. Similar to the binary case, this metric evaluates the model performance on the top-ranked items. The difference lies in it measuring
the ranking of the ground-truth label
, rather than that of instance. The early work in this direction adopts an alternative approach to minimize the top-
k
error. For example,
[
21
]
models the classes missing from the annotation by the structured learning technique with latent variables.
[
22
]
analyzes the problem from the perspective of submodular reward functions.
[
23
]
proposes a probabilistic method explicitly modeling the total number of objects in an image. Besides,
[
24
,
25
]
use metric learning to find potential labels, especially when the number of classes is dynamic.

Recently, more efforts focus on directly minimizing the top-
k
error. To this end,
[
7
]
relaxes the multiclass hinge loss to penalize the top-
k
highest prediction. Although this approach is intuitive, the induced loss functions are not top-
k
consistent
[
4
]
. In other words, minimizing these losses does not guarantee an ideal top-
k
performance. To this end,
[
4
]
analyzes the calibration property of some top-
k
loss functions truncated from the multiclass hinge loss and the cross-entropy loss. The theoretical results are further extended to multi-label classification
[
8
]
. Most recently,
[
9
]
points out that the condition for top-
k
calibration proposed by
[
4
]
is invalid when ties exist in the prediction. To fix this issue,
[
9
]
develops a necessary and sufficient property for top-
k
optimality named top-
k
preserving. Based on this property, a top-
k
consistent hinge-like loss is finally established.

Compared with existing efforts on the optimization method of the top-
k
objective, this paper focuses on the limitations of the metric itself, whose details are shown in Sec.
III-B
. To solve this problem, we propose a novel metric named AUTKC. Moreover, an efficient framework for AUTKC optimization is also established.

II-B

Label Ranking

The setting we discuss is naturally associated with the label ranking problem, where the predictions are also evaluated by the ranking of labels. Specifically, given the label space
\mathcal{Y}
, label ranking aims to assign each instance
\boldsymbol{x}
with
the correct ranking of all the labels
, that is, a complete/partial, transitive, and asymmetric relation
\succ_{\boldsymbol{x}}
defined on
\mathcal{Y}
, where
i\succ_{\boldsymbol{x}}j
means that the label
i
precedes the label
j
in the ranking associated with
\boldsymbol{x}
. According to the taxonomy established by
[
26
]
, label ranking methods can be divided into four categories: the ones decomposing the original problem to multiple simple objectives such as pointwise function
[
27
,
28
]
and pairwise ranking loss
[
29
,
30
]
; probabilistic methods including tree-based model
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
, Gaussian mixture model
[
36
]
and structured learning
[
37
]
; the ones based on similarity
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
; and the rule-based ones
[
41
,
42
]
. Please refer to the surveys for more details
[
43
,
26
]
.

Compared with label ranking, top-
k
optimization follows the setting of classification, where each instance
\boldsymbol{x}
is assigned with a single label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
i.e.
, the ground-truth label. As a result, the common measures used in label ranking become invalid, such as Spearman’s rank
[
44
]
and Kendall’s tau
[
45
]
. And top-
k
optimization only considers
the ranking associated with the ground-truth label
,
i.e.
, ensuring
y\succ_{\boldsymbol{x}}j,j\in\mathcal{Y}
. Concretely, top-
k
optimization requires that
|\{j\in\mathcal{Y}:j\succ_{\boldsymbol{x}}y\}|<k
, where
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
represents the cardinality of a set. Besides, our analysis in Sec.
V-A
shows that optimizing AUTKC encourages the prediction to preserve
the ranking of the underlying top-
K
labels
. In this sense, AUTKC optimization essentially aims to recover the ranking of partial labels under a restricted condition where only the ground-truth label is available.

II-C

AUC Optimization

Our proposed framework is also related to AUC optimization, where the optimization objective is also a pairwise ranking loss. Specifically, AUC, the Area Under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) Curve, measures the probability that the positive instances are ranked higher than the negative ones, with the assumption that the possibility to observe ties in the comparisons equals zero
[
12
]
. Being insensitive to the label distribution, AUC has become a popular metric in the class-imbalanced applications such as disease prediction
[
46
]
and rare event detection
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
.

Traditional research on AUC optimization targets the whole area under the ROC curve. At the early stage, the major studies in this direction focus on the off-line setting
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
]
. As the scale of datasets increases rapidly, it becomes infeasible to optimize the pairwise loss in a full-batch manner. For this reason, more studies explore the extension of AUC optimization methods in the online setting
[
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
]
. Besides, the theoretical guarantee of the learning framework is another appealing topic, including generalization bound
[
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
]
and consistency property
[
68
,
69
]
. Recently, more literature focuses on the partial area under the ROC curve since only the order of the top-ranked instances are of interest in many practical applications
[
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
. In this sense, partial AUC optimization is related to top-
k
optimization in binary scenarios.

Compared with partial AUC optimization, our proposed framework focuses on the ranking of multiple labels. Moreover, we assume that ties exist in the scores. These two differences will lead to totally different theoretical results, whose details are shown in Sec.
V
and Sec.
VI
.

III-A

Standard TOP-k Metric

In multi-class classification, one generally assumes that the samples are drawn
i.i.d.
from a product space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the input space and
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\cdots,C\}
is the label space. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the joint distribution defined on
\mathcal{Z}
, and
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y}=\mathbb{P}\left[y|\boldsymbol{x}\right]
denotes the conditional probability of instance
\boldsymbol{x}
according to distribution
\mathcal{D}
. We assume that there exist no ties in conditional probability
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
. In other words, we have
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[1]}>\cdots>\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[C]}
, where
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k]}
is the
k
-th greatest element of
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})
.
Then, our task is to learn a score function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
to estimate the conditional probability for each class. Generally, a loss function
L:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}^{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is used to measure the quality of the score function
f
at
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
. For example, a standard measure for classification is top-
1
error, which compares the ground-truth label with the only one guess,
i.e.,
the class with the highest score.

Given

•

Given the ground-truth label

\pi_{f(\boldsymbol{x})}(y)>\pi_{f(\boldsymbol{x})}(y^{\prime})

Remark 1
.

The two formulations apply to different situations:

•

The first formulation is a population-level assumption where the conditional probability
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})
is available. It states that, when ties exist, the ranking results of

are opposite to those of
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})
. We apply this formulation in the proof of Thm.
3

4

5

•

The second formulation is an empirical-level assumption where only the ground-truth label is available. It states that, when ties exist, the ground-truth label will be ranked lower. We apply this formulation in the proof of Thm.
2

•

In summary, these two conditions ensure that we will always choose the worst result when there is a tie in a conservative sense.

III-B

Motivation

As mentioned in the introduction,
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
has its limitations. In this subsection, we will make a further analysis to reveal its reasons.

On one hand,
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}

is not discriminating enough, leaving some fatal errors free from penalty (L1)
. As shown in Fig.
3
, we assume that
Lake
is the ground-truth label;
Sky
and
Cloud
are the labels with ambiguity;
Beach
and
Valley
are the irrelevant labels. Consider the following two score function such that:

\displaystyle f_{1}:

\displaystyle\textbf{{ Lake}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Sky}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Cloud}}\succ\texttt{Beach}\succ\texttt{Valley},

\displaystyle f_{2}:

\displaystyle\texttt{ Beach}\succ\textbf{{Lake}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Sky}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Cloud}}\succ\texttt{Valley}.

Obviously,
f_{1}
is an ideal score function since it ranks
Lake
first, while
f_{2}
is a
bad
score function since it ranks the irrelevant label
Beach
higher than the ground-truth label. Based on this observation, we expect

err(y,f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}))>err(y,f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})).

However, when
k\geq 2
, we have

err_{k}(y,f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}))=err_{k}(y,f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}))=0.

In other words, although using the top-
k
error can relax the punishment on label ambiguity, it also relaxes the punishment on the fatal errors. In this sense,
we need to seek out a more discriminating metric to selectively relax the punishment
.

One may wonder whether a clever selection of
k
could eliminate this limitation. Unfortunately,
the number of ambiguous labels generally differs among classes, while the hyperparameter
k
is universal for the classes
. This contradiction leads to a dilemma during the training period: if the value of
k
is too small for some classes, label ambiguity might make these classes hard to optimize, which is the motivation of
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimization. But, if the value of
k
is too large, irrelevant labels will be more likely to be ranked higher than the ground-truth label, which is also unexpected. This suggests that merely looking at a single
k
is not enough to address
(L1)
.

On the other hand,
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}

is a static measure, ignoring the changes in operator’s decision condition (L2)
. To be specific, empirical results suggest that models trained with different hyperparameters
k
generally perform inconsistently on the top-
k
curve
[
4
,
8
]
. As shown in Fig.
4
, model
f
outperforms model
g
when
k<4
, while the results reverse when
k>4
. In view of this, the operator might ask: which model should we select to deploy? In practice, the answer depends on which
k
is of interest during the deployment phase. However,
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimization methods fix the value of
k
during the training phase. As a result, the model performance cannot be guaranteed if the
k
of interest changes after the model is deployed.

Being aware of the limitations of
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
, we have the following question:

(
Q1
)
Whether there exists a ”better” metric for label ambiguity?

III-C

AUTKC and its Partial Variant

Our answer to
(Q1)
is to evaluate the score function from a more comprehensive perspective, rather than a single point of the top-
k
curve. Taking the inspiration from Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC)
[
12
]
, we propose to adopt
the area under the top-
k
curve (AUTKC)
to evaluate the quality of the given score function:

\mathsf{AUTKC}\text{-}\mathsf{W}^{\downarrow}(f):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{C}\int_{1}^{C}err_{k}(y,f(\boldsymbol{x}))dk\right],

(4)

The original definition of
\mathsf{AUTKC}
considers the whole area under the top-
k
curve. However, we generally focus on the performance within a given range of
k
. In this case, Eq. (
4
) provides a biased estimation of the expected performance by considering the unrelated regions. This motivates us to adopt its variant involving the given range of
k
:

\begin{split}&\mathsf{AUTKC}\text{-}\mathsf{P}^{\downarrow}(f)\\
&\phantom{-}:=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{K_{1}-K_{2}+1}\int_{K_{1}}^{K_{2}}err_{k}(y,f(\boldsymbol{x}))dk\right],\end{split}

(5)

where
K_{1},K_{2}
are the minimum and the maximum number of ambiguous labels, respectively. Since
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{1}
is the most common metric of interest, and there generally exist some instances involving no ambiguous labels, we set
K_{1}=1
and denote
K_{2}
as
K
for the sake of conciseness in the following discussion.

Furthermore, since
k
is discrete, Eq. (
5
) enjoys a much simpler formulation:

\mathsf{AUTKC}\text{-}\mathsf{P}^{\downarrow}(f):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[aerr_{K}(y,f(\boldsymbol{x}))\right],

(6)

where

aerr_{K}(y,f(\boldsymbol{x}))=\frac{1}{K}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}err_{k}\left(y,f(\boldsymbol{x})\right)}.

(7)

Note that we
denote
\mathsf{AUTKC}\text{-}\mathsf{P}^{\downarrow}
as
\mathsf{AUTKC}^{\downarrow}
in the following discussion for the sake of convenience. Similar to
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
, the partial area under top-
k
accuracy curve, denoted as
\mathsf{AUTKC}^{\uparrow}
, is equivalent to
\mathsf{AUTKC}^{\downarrow}
, and we use
\mathsf{AUTKC}
to refer to the two metrics when there exists no ambiguity.

III-D

AUTKC vs TOP-k

Intuitively,
\mathsf{AUTKC}
is a better measure than
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
. On one hand,
\mathsf{AUTKC}
is more discriminating than
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
, which helps overcome the limitation
(L1)
. For example, when we set
K=3
,
\mathsf{AUTKC}
tells us
f_{1}
is the ideal score function and indeed better than
f_{2}
:

aerr_{K}(y,f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\frac{1}{3}\left(0+0+0\right)=0,

and

aerr_{K}(y,f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\frac{1}{3}\left(1+0+0\right)=\frac{1}{3}.

On the other hand,
\mathsf{AUTKC}
only requires a universal hyperparameter
K
for all the instances, that is, the maximum number of ambiguous labels. Such a simplification is beneficial to overcoming the limitation
(L2)
.

Although the aforementioned example indicates the advantages of AUTKC, more theoretical clues are necessary to validate our argument. To this end, we first present the definition of
consistent
and
discriminating
, which is a classic technique for comparing measures
[
12
]
.

Definition 1

(Degree of consistency
[
12
]
)
.

Given two measures
f
and
g
on domain
\Psi
and two predictions
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\Psi
, let
R=\{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})|f(\boldsymbol{a})>f(\boldsymbol{b}),g(\boldsymbol{a})>g(\boldsymbol{b})\},S=\{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})|f(\boldsymbol{a})>f(\boldsymbol{b}),g(\boldsymbol{a})<g(\boldsymbol{b})\}
. The degree of consistency between
f
and
g
is defined as
\mathbf{C}=\frac{\left\lvert R\right\rvert}{\left\lvert R\right\rvert+\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\in[0,1]
, where
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
is the cardinality of a set.

Remark 2
.

The degree of consistency estimates the probability that the two measures make the same judgment on the given domain. A relatively high degree of consistency is a necessary condition for measure comparison.

Definition 2

(Degree of discriminancy
[
12
]
)
.

Given two measures
f
and
g
on domain
\Psi
and two predictions
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\Psi
, let
P=\{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})|f(\boldsymbol{a})>f(\boldsymbol{b}),g(\boldsymbol{a})=g(\boldsymbol{b})\},S=\{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})|g(\boldsymbol{a})>g(\boldsymbol{b}),f(\boldsymbol{a})=f(\boldsymbol{b})\}
. The degree of discriminancy between
f
and
g
is
\mathbf{D}=\frac{\left\lvert P\right\rvert}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}
.

Remark 3
.

Degree of discriminancy describes whether a measure could find the discrepancy that the other measure fails to discover. For example, the top-1 error is intuitively more discriminating than the top-
k
error when
k>1
.

Definition 3

(Consistent and discriminating
[
12
]
)
.

The measure
f
is consistent and more discriminating than
g
if and only if
\mathbf{C}>0.5
and
\mathbf{D}>1
. In this case, we conclude, intuitively, that
f
is a ”better” measure than
g
. Especially, if
\mathbf{C}=1.0
and
\mathbf{D}=\infty
, we say the measure
f
is strictly consistent and more discriminating than
g
.

On top of consistency and discrimination, the following theorem suggests that
\mathsf{AUTKC}
is a better measure than
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
. Please see Appendix.
A
for the proof.

Theorem 1
.

For any
k<K
,
\mathsf{AUTKC}
is strictly consistent and more discriminating than
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
.

Remark 4
.

Being strictly consistent with
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
for any
k<K
means that
\mathsf{AUTKC}
is insensitive to the
k
of interest, which overcomes the limitation
(L2)
. Meanwhile, being more discriminating implies that
\mathsf{AUTKC}
might be a better optimization objective than
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
, thus providing a clue to overcoming the limitation
(L1)
. Further analysis in Sec.
V-A
will support these conjectures.

IV
Roadmap for AUTKC Optimization

So far, we have known the advantage of
\mathsf{AUTKC}
. Then, a natural question is

\displaystyle\text{({Q2}) {How to design }}

\displaystyle\text{{a learning algorithm }}

\displaystyle\text{{that optimizes $\mathsf{AUTKC}$ effectively?}}

Following the standard machine learning paradigm
[
74
]
, we first reformulate the metric optimization problem to a risk minimization problem:

\begin{split}(OP_{0})\phantom{|}\min_{f}\ &\mathcal{R}_{K}(f):=\mathsf{AUTKC}^{\downarrow}(f)\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k\leq K}\ell_{0-1}\left(s_{y}-(s_{\setminus y})_{[k]}\right)\right],\end{split}

(8)

where
s_{y}:=f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y}
denotes the score of the class
y
,
(s_{\setminus y})_{[k]}
is the
k
-th greatest entry of
f(\boldsymbol{x})
except
s_{y}
, and
\ell_{0-1}(t):=\mathbf{1}\left[t\leq 0\right]
is the 0-1 loss. Note that we set
\ell_{0-1}(0)=1
since according to Asm.
1
, any ties will lead to an error.

For the sake of conciseness, we provide an equivalent reformulation of
(OP_{0})
, where the annoying operation
\setminus y
is removed. See Appendix.
B
for the proof.

Theorem 2
.

When Asm.
1
holds, the original optimization problem
(OP_{0})
is equivalent to the following problem:

(OP_{1})\phantom{|}\min_{f}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K+1}\ell_{0-1}\left(s_{y}-s_{[k]}\right)\right].

(9)

Remark 5
.

Thm.
2
bridges
\mathsf{AUTKC}
optimization with two classic problems: AUC optimization and label ranking. Specifically, AUC optimization is a binary classification problem with a pairwise ranking objective
[
12
]
:

(OP_{AUC})\phantom{|}\min_{f}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{x^{+}}\sim\mathcal{D}^{+}\atop\boldsymbol{x^{-}}\sim\mathcal{D}^{-}}\left[\ell_{0-1}(f(\boldsymbol{x^{+}})-f(\boldsymbol{x^{-}}))\right],

where
\mathcal{D}^{+},\mathcal{D}^{-}
are the distribution of positive and negative samples, respectively. If we view the ground-truth label as the positive instance and the other labels as the negative ones, then
\mathsf{AUTKC}
optimization is similar to
a partial variant of AUC optimization in the context of label ranking
. Although partial AUC optimization has been well studied
[
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
, there still exist two main differences:

•

The objective of AUC optimization assumes that there exist no ties in
f(\boldsymbol{x})
, which contradicts Asm.
1
.

•

The expectation operation of AUC involves two samples, while in
\mathsf{AUTKC}
it only involves one label.

These differences will lead to
different theoretical results and practical implementation
. Please refer to Sec.
V
and Sec.
VI
for the details.

According to Eq.(
9
), the main challenges for directly minimizing
\mathcal{R}_{K}(f)
are two-fold:

(C1)

The loss function
\ell_{0-1}
is not differentiable;

(C2)

The data distribution
\mathcal{D}
is unavailable.

Next, we will present the solutions to
(C1)
and
(C2)
in Sec.
V
and Sec.
VI
, respectively. Finally, we will obtain an end-to-end framework that optimizes the empirical risk of
\mathsf{AUTKC}
efficiently.

V
Surrogate Risk Minimization for AUTKC

In this section, our task is to handle
(C1)
. Specifically, the discrete
\ell_{0-1}
in
(OP_{1})
will be replaced by a differentiable surrogate loss
\ell
, leading to the following surrogate risk minimization problem:

(OP_{2})\phantom{|}\min_{f}\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K+1}\ell\left(s_{y}-s_{[k]}\right)\right].

(10)

But,

(
Q2-1
)
What kind of surrogate loss should we select?

We next answer this question in three steps. First, we study the Bayes optimal function for
\mathsf{AUTKC}
, that is, the optimal solution of
(OP_{1})
. Next is the condition of
\ell
such that optimizing
(OP_{2})
leads to the Bayes optimal function. In other words, when the surrogate risk is Fisher consistent with the
\ell_{0-1}
-based
\mathsf{AUTKC}
risk. Finally, we answer the question
(Q2-1)
by discussing the consistency property of some common surrogate losses.

V-A

AUTKC Bayes Optimality

We first define the Bayes optimal score function for
\mathsf{AUTKC}
, which is the best score function we could approximate:

Definition 4

(
\mathsf{AUTKC}
Bayes optimal)
.

The score function
f^{*}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
is
\mathsf{AUTKC}
Bayes optimal if

f^{*}\in\arg\inf_{f}\mathcal{R}_{K}(f).

(11)

Our goal is to find the solution to Eq.(
11
). To this end, we first present the top-
K
ranking-preserving property:

Definition 5

(Top-
K
ranking-preserving property)
.

Given
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
, we say that
\boldsymbol{b}
is top-
K
ranking-preserving with respect to
\boldsymbol{a}
, denoted as
\mathsf{RP}_{K}(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{a})
, if for any
k\leq K
,

\pi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(y)=k\Longrightarrow\pi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(y)=k.

The negation of this statement is
\lnot\mathsf{RP}_{K}(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{a})
. Note that
\mathsf{RP}_{K}
is transitive. In other words, if
\mathsf{RP}_{K}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{b})
and
\mathsf{RP}_{K}(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{a})
, then we have
\mathsf{RP}_{K}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{a})
.

Then, the following theorem illuminates the connection between
\mathsf{RP}_{K}
and
\mathsf{AUTKC}
optimality. Please see Appendix.
C-A
for the proof.

Theorem 3
.

The score function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
is
\mathsf{AUTKC}
Bayes optimal if and only if the score is top-
K
ranking-preserving with respect to the conditional risk. More concretely, we have

f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{1}}>f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{2}}\text{ if }\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{1}}>\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{2}}\geq\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[K]}.

It is worth noting that this theorem sheds more light on the advantages of optimizing
\mathsf{AUTKC}
:

Fig. 5:
The comparison between
\mathsf{AUTKC}
Bayes optimality and
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
Bayes optimality.
\mathsf{AUTKC}
optimality requires that
\textbf{{Lake}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Sky}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Cloud}}\succ\texttt{Beach}\succ\text{others}
, while
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimality does not.

Remark 6
.

Top-
K
ranking-preserving is stricter than the optimality property for
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimization
[
9
]
. To be specific, given any
\boldsymbol{x}
, the
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimality property requires that

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y}>f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k+1]}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y}>\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k+1]},

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y}<f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k]}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y}<\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k]}.

In other words, this property only considers the ranking involving
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k+1]}
and
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k]}
. As a comparison, Top-
K
ranking-preserving
\mathsf{RP}_{K}
also considers the ranking between
f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{1}}
and
f(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{2}}
, where
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{1}}>\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{y_{2}}>\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[K]}
. As shown in Fig.
5
,
\mathsf{AUTKC}
optimality requires that

\textbf{{Lake}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Sky}}\succ\underline{\texttt{Cloud}}\succ\texttt{Beach}\succ\text{others},

while
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimality only requires that

\textbf{{Lake}},\underline{\texttt{Sky}},\underline{\texttt{Cloud}},\texttt{Beach}\succ\text{others}.

The advantage of this strict property is three-fold:

•

It reveals how
\mathsf{AUTKC}
overcomes limitation
(L1)
: optimizing
\mathsf{AUTKC}
encourages the classes with a higher conditional probability being ranked higher, while the
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
objective does not.

•

It reveals how
\mathsf{AUTKC}
overcomes limitation
(L2)
and escapes from the dilemma that the value of hyperparameter
k
is hard to determine: as long as the hyperparameter
K
is not so small, the induced optimal prediction will rank the ground-truth label and the ambiguous ones higher than the irrelevant ones.

•

It helps us obtain a simpler criterion to select surrogate loss, whose details are presented in Sec.
V-B
.

Remark 7
.

If a score function
f
is
\mathsf{AUTKC}
Bayes optimal, no ties will exist in

\{f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[1]},\cdots,f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[K]},f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[K+1]}\}.

In other words,
optimizing
\mathsf{AUTKC}
encourages breaking the ties
, which is an appealing property. Meanwhile, ties might exist in

\{f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[K+1]},\cdots,f(\boldsymbol{x})_{[C]}\}

since it does not break the top-
K
ranking-preserving property.

V-B

Consistency analysis for surrogate risk minimization

On top of Thm.
3
, we could investigate the answer to the question
(Q2-1)
. A basic requirement for
\ell
is consistency. That is, the solution returned by minimizing
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)
should recover the Bayes optimal score function:

Definition 6

(
\mathsf{AUTKC}
consistency)
.

The surrogate loss
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is consistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
if for every function sequence
\left\{f_{t}\right\}_{t=1,2,\cdots}
, we have:

\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f_{t})\to\inf_{f}\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)\Rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{K}(f_{t})\to\inf_{f}\mathcal{R}_{K}(f).

(12)

Next, we provide a sufficient condition for
\mathsf{AUTKC}
consistency, which enjoys a simpler formulation than checking whether the optimal solutions of
(OP_{2})
are Bayes optimal. Please see Appendix.
C-B
for the proof.

Theorem 4
.

The surrogate loss
\ell(t)
is
\mathsf{AUTKC}
consistent if it is bounded, differentiable and strictly decreasing.

So far, we have discussed the consistency
w.r.t.
all the measurable functions. However, common surrogate losses are not bounded. To this end, we next restrict the functions within a special function set
\mathcal{F}
, which induces the concept of
\mathcal{F}
-consistency:

Definition 7

(
\mathsf{AUTKC}

\mathcal{F}
-consistency)
.

The surrogate loss
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is consistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
if for every function sequence
\left\{f_{t}\right\}_{t=1,2,\cdots},f_{t}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have:

\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f_{t})\to\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)\Rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{K}(f_{t})\to\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{R}_{K}(f).

(13)

According to Thm.
4
, the following proposition and corollary clearly hold:

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the set of functions whose outputs are bounded in
[0,1]
. Then the surrogate loss
\ell(t)
is
\mathcal{F}
-consistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
if it is differentiable and strictly decreasing in
[0,1]
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the set of functions whose outputs are bounded in
[0,1]
. Then the following statements hold:

•

The square loss
\ell_{sq}(t)=(1-t)^{2}
is
\mathcal{F}
-consistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
.

•

The exponential loss
\ell_{exp}(t)=\exp(-t)
is
\mathcal{F}
-consistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
.

•

The logit loss
\ell_{logit}(t)=\log(1+\exp(-t))
is
\mathcal{F}
-consistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
.

Traditional
\mathsf{TOP}\text{-}\mathsf{k}
optimization methods adopt the hinge loss
\ell_{hinge}\left(t\right)=\left[1-t\right]_{+}
as the surrogate loss, where
\left[t\right]_{+}=\max\left\{0,t\right\}
. However, we point out that its variant in
\mathsf{AUTKC}
optimization is inconsistent, whose proof is shown in Appendix.
C-C
.

Theorem 5
.

The hinge loss is inconsistent with
\mathsf{AUTKC}
when there exists
\boldsymbol{x}
such that
\sum_{k=K+2}^{C}\eta(\boldsymbol{x})_{[k]}>\frac{K}{K+1}
.

Proof Sketch
.

Given any sample
\boldsymbol{x}
satisfying the above condition and its Bayes optimal score
\boldsymbol{s}^{*}
, Thm.
5
is induced by the fact that we could construct a score
\boldsymbol{s}
such that
\lnot\mathsf{RP}(\boldsymbol{s},\eta(\boldsymbol{x}))
but
\boldsymbol{s}
has a smaller risk than
\boldsymbol{s}^{*}
does. This construction holds by the property that
\ell_{hinge}(t)=0
for any
t\geq 1
. Please refer to Appendix.
C-C
for the details.

VI
Generalization Analysis for AUTKC Optimization

In this section, we aim to relieve
(C2)
. Since the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
is unknown, we turn to optimizing its empirical estimation based on the given dataset
\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
sampled
i.i.d.
from
\mathcal{D}
. Let
s^{(i)}_{y}
denote
f(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})_{y^{(i)}}
for convenience. Then, we have the following optimization problem:

(OP_{3})\phantom{|}\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{K}^{\ell}(f,\mathcal{S}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K+1}\ell\left(s^{(i)}_{y}-s^{(i)}_{[k]}\right),

(14)

The results in Sec.
V
guarantee a small
\mathsf{AUTKC}
risk
\mathcal{R}_{K}
when we minimize the surrogate risk
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}
, as long as the surrogate loss
\ell
is
\mathsf{AUTKC}
consistent. But,

\displaystyle\text{({Q2-2}) {Does }}

\displaystyle\text{{minimizing the empirical risk }}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{K}^{\ell}(f,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\text{{ lead to a small generalization error }}\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)\text{{?}}

In other words, we should ensure
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{K}^{\ell}(f,\mathcal{S})
is bounded with high probability, and the upper bound goes to zero as the number of samples increases,
especially when the number of classes is large
. To this end, we next provide a Gaussian-complexity-based analysis for this bound in Sec.
VI-A
. Since deep neural networks have been one of the most popular function sets for large-scale classification, we further present its result in Sec.
VI-B
.

VI-A

General Result for AUTKC Generalization Bound

Gaussian Complexity
[
74
]
is a classic measure of generalization error. Here we present its formal definition.

Definition 8

(Gaussian Complexity)
.

Given the dataset
\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions defined on the sample space
\mathcal{Z}
. Then, the empirical Gaussian complexity is defined as:

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{F}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{g}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{(i)}f(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\right],

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{g}=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)},\cdots,g^{(n)})
are the independent random variables sampled from the standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
.

Then, the following lemma provides the upper bound of
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{K}^{\ell}(f,\mathcal{S})
via Gaussian complexity.

Lemma 1

(Generalization Bound with Gaussian Complexity
[
74
]
)
.

Suppose that the loss function
L
is bounded by
B>0
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with a probability of at least
1-\delta
, the following holds

\Phi(\mathcal{S},\delta)\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\mathfrak{G}_{\mathcal{S}}(L\circ\mathcal{F}),

(16)

where

\Phi(\mathcal{S},\delta)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\mathcal{R}_{K}^{\ell}(f)-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{K}^{\ell}(f,\mathcal{S})\right)-3B\sqrt{\frac{\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{2n}},

and
L\circ\mathcal{F}:=\{L\circ f|f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is the set that consists of the composition of the loss function
L:\mathbb{R}^{C}\to\mathbb{R}
and the score function
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

In multi-class classification, this classic bound scales
w.r.t.

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{C})

[
74
,
75
,
76
]
. This result is rather unfavorable when facing numerous classes. Thanks to the recent advance in learning theory, a Lipschitz assumption on the loss function
L
can help eliminate this problem
[
77
]
:

Assumption 2

(Lipschitz Continuity
w.r.t.
a Variant of the
\ell_{2}
-Norm
[
77
]
)
.

The loss function
L:\mathbb{R}^{C}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. a variant of the
\ell_{2}
-norm involving index
y\in\mathcal{Y}
if for all
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}

\displaystyle\left|L(\boldsymbol{s})-L(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\left\|(s_{1}-s^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,s_{C}-s^{\prime}_{C})\right\|_{2}+L_{2}\left|s_{y}-s^{\prime}_{y}\right|,

where
L_{1},L_{2}
are Lipschitz constants.

Then, according to Lemma 1 in
[
77
]
, we obtain the following generalization bound:

Proposition 2

(Generalization Bound with the Lipschitz continuity variant of the
\ell_{2}
-Norm)
.

If the loss function
L
satisfies Asm.
2
, then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability of at least
1-\delta
, we have

\Phi(S,\delta)\leq 2\sqrt{\pi}\left[L_{1}C\mathfrak{G}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}})+L_{2}\mathfrak{G}_{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}})\right].

(17)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}=

Generalization Bound for Convolutional Neural Networks

According to Proposition
2
and Thm.
6
, we could obtain the final generalization bound as soon as a proper upper bound of Gaussian complexity is available. To this end, we first present an important technique for the bound of Gaussian complexity named chaining in Sec.

VI-B
1
Chaining Bound for Gaussian Complexity

Similar to Gaussian complexity, covering number
[
78
]
is another technique to measure the complexity of the given (pseudo)metric space:

Definition 9

(
\epsilon
-covering)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{F},d\}
be a (pseudo)metric space and
\mathcal{B}(f,r)
denote the ball of radius
r
centered at
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Given
\Theta\subset\mathcal{F}
, we say
\mathcal{F}_{m}=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is
\epsilon
-covering of
\Theta
if
\Theta\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}\left(f_{i},\epsilon\right)
. In other words, for any
\theta\in\Theta
, there exists
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
such that
d(\theta,f_{i})<\epsilon
.

Definition 10

(Covering Number)
.

On top of Def.
9
, covering number of
\Theta
is defined as the minimum cardinality of any
\epsilon
-covering of
\Theta
:

\mathfrak{C}(\Theta,\epsilon,d):=\min\{m:\mathcal{F}_{m}\text{ is }\epsilon\text{-covering of }\Theta\}.

(18)

Our task is to bound the Gaussian Complexity with covering number. To this end, we first check the sub-Gaussian property of Gaussian Complexity. See Appendix.
D-B
for the proof.

Definition 11

(Sub-Gaussian Process
w.r.t.
the Metric
d_{X}
)
.

A collection of zero-mean random variables
\{X_{f},f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is a sub-Gaussian process w.r.t. a metric
d_{X}
on
\mathcal{F}
if

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(X_{f}-X_{f^{\prime}})}]\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}d_{X}^{2}(f,f^{\prime})}{2}},\forall f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Proposition 3

(Sub-Gaussian Property of Gaussian Complexity)
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, let

T_{f}(\boldsymbol{g})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{C}g_{i,j}s^{(i)}_{j},

Then,
Z:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nC}}(T_{f}(\boldsymbol{g})-T_{f^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{g}))
is a sub-Gaussian process with metric

d_{\infty,\mathcal{S}}(s,s^{\prime})=\max_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{S}}|s_{y}-s^{\prime}_{y}|,

where
s^{(i)}_{j}=f(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})_{j}
.

Since
d_{\infty,\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\leq 1
and

\mathfrak{G}_{\tilde{S}}(\mathcal{F})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{g}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{nC}T_{f}(\boldsymbol{g})\right],

we have the chaining bound of Gaussian Complexity according to Dudley’s entropy integral
[
78
]
:

Proposition 4

(Chaining bound of Gaussian Complexity)
.

\mathfrak{G}_{\tilde{S}}(\mathcal{F})\leq\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\left(C_{1}\alpha+\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{nC}}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\sqrt{\log{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{F},\epsilon,d_{\infty,\mathcal{S}})}}d\epsilon\right),

(19)

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are two constants.

Practical Result for Convolutional Neural Networks

On top of Eq.(
19
), we are now ready to present the generalization bound of a family of neural networks, where both convolution layers and fully-connected layers exist. In fact, it is an application of the recent advance in
[
79

.

The family of neural networks.
The network consists of
L_{c}
convolutional layers and followed by
L_{f}
fully-connected layers. Each convolutional layer consists of a convolution followed by an activation function and an optional pooling operation. All the convolutions use zero-padding
[
80
]
with the kernel
{KN}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{l}\times p_{l}\times c_{l-1}\times c_{l}}
for layer
l\in\{1,\cdots,L_{c}\}
. All the activation functions and pooling operations are
1
-Lipschitz continuous. Meanwhile, we denote the parameters of the
l
-the fully-connected layer as
V^{(l)}
. Finally, Let
\Theta=\{{KN}^{(1)},\cdots,{KN}^{(L_{c})},V^{(1)},\cdots,V^{(L_{f})}\}
denote the set of all the parameters in the given network.

Regularization of the parameters.
The model input
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times c}
satisfies
||\mathsf{vec}(\boldsymbol{x})||\leq\chi
, where
c
is the number of channels and
\mathsf{vec}(\cdot)
is the vectorization operation. Note that convolution is essentially a linear operation, and we denote the operator matrix of the kernel
{KN}^{(l)}
as
\mathsf{mt}({KN}^{(l)})

\Theta_{0}

\displaystyle||\mathsf{mt}({KN}^{(l)}_{0})||_{2}\leq 1+\nu,l=1,\cdots,L_{c},

\displaystyle||\mathsf{mt}(V^{(l)}_{0})||_{2}\leq 1+\nu,l=1,\cdots,L_{f}.

And the distance from
\Theta_{0}
to the current parameters
\Theta
is bounded:

\begin{split}||\Theta-\Theta_{0}||&:=\sum_{l=1}^{L}||\mathsf{mt}({KN}^{(l)})-\mathsf{mt}({KN}_{0}^{(l)})||_{2}\\
&\phantom{\sum_{l=1}^{L}||\mathsf{mt}({KN}^{(l)})}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}||V^{(l)}-V_{0}^{(l)}||_{2}\\
&\leq\beta.\end{split}

(20)

Finally, let
\mathcal{F}_{\beta,\nu}
denote the set of neural networks discribed above. According to Proposition
5
in Appendix.
D-C
, where the covering number in Eq.(
19
) and the Gaussian complexity in Eq.(
17
) are bounded, we have the final result for the generalization bound of
\mathcal{F}_{\beta,\nu}

Theorem 7

(Generalization bound of Convolutional Neural Networks)
.

Given the set of convolution neural networks
\mathcal{F}_{\beta,\nu}

L
satisfies Asm.
2
, then for any
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\Phi(S,\delta)\precsim\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{WL_{1}\sqrt{C}\log(nCB_{\beta,\nu,\chi})}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{WL_{2}\log(nB_{\beta,\nu,\chi})}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

Remark 8
.

According to Thm.
3
and the generalization bound, it seems that we can simply set
K
as the number of classes. However, only the ranking of the ground-truth label and the ambiguous labels is of interest, which are all top-ranked classes. In this sense, a large
K
will provide a biased estimation of the performance by including more uninformative loss terms when taking the average. Motivated by this, a moderate
K
, which is exactly equal to or slightly larger than the maximum number of relevant labels, might be a better choice. Since the maximum number of relevant labels is proportional to the number of classes, we set
K\geq p\cdot C
, where
p
is a universal constant. Note that
L_{1}\sim\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}})
according to Thm.
6
. In this sense,
\Phi(\mathcal{S},\delta)
becomes irrelevant to
C
.

Computational Complexity.
On one hand, the proposed method shares a similar convergence rate with prior arts since it can be optimized efficiently via common optimizers such as SGD
[
81
]
. On the other hand, the additional complexity of the proposed method comes from the top-
K
ranking operator. With the help of heapsort, the average complexity of each sample is in order of
\mathcal{O}(C\log K)
. Then, the total complexity is in order of
\mathcal{O}(nC\log K+nK)
, where
n
is the number of samples. As analyzed in the revised remark for Thm.9, we prefer choosing a moderate
K
. Thus, compared with top-
k
optimization, whose complexity is in order of
\mathcal{O}(nC+n)
, the complexity of the proposed method is still moderate.
