Classifying fermionic states via many-body correlation measures
Mykola Semenyakin, Yevheniia Cheipesh, Yaroslav Herasymenko

Abstract
Understanding the structure of quantum correlations in a many-body system is key to its computational treatment. For fermionic systems, correlations can be defined as deviations from Slater determinant states. The link between fermionic correlations and efficient computational physics methods is actively studied but remains ambiguous. We make progress in establishing this connection mathematically. In particular, we find a rigorous classification of states relative to k k -fermion correlations, which admits a computational physics interpretation. Correlations are captured by a measure ω k \omega_{k} , a function of k k -fermion reduced density matrix that we call twisted purity. A condition ω k = 0 \omega_{k}=0 for a given k k puts the state in a class 𝒢 k \mathcal{G}_{k} of correlated states. Sets 𝒢 k \mathcal{G}_{k} are nested in k k , and Slater determinants correspond to k = 1 k=1 . Classes 𝒢 k = O ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{G}_{k={\rm O}(1)} are shown to be physically relevant, as ω k \omega_{k} vanishes or nearly vanishes for truncated configuration-interaction states, perturbation series around Slater determinants, and some nonperturbative eigenstates of the 1D Hubbard model. For each k = O ⁡ ( 1 ) k={\rm O}(1) , we give an explicit ansatz with a polynomial number of parameters that covers all states in 𝒢 k \mathcal{G}_{k} . Potential applications of this ansatz and its connections to the coupled-cluster wavefunction are discussed.

In this work, we give a classification of
k

\omega_{k}
, a correlation measure which is a function of the
k
-body reduced density matrix, invariant under single-particle rotations. Twisted purity is also a Hermitian observable on two copies of a state. If a state yields

\mathcal{G}_{k}

\mathcal{G}_{k}

Our results complement the categorization of states via entanglement scaling

Plücker relations.
A general Slater determinant (or free-fermion) state

\displaystyle\ket{v}=U\ket{S_{0}},~U=\exp\left(i\textstyle\sum(\theta_{pq}\psi^{\dagger}_{p}\psi_{q}+\theta_{pq}^{*}\psi^{\dagger}_{q}\psi_{p})\right),

(1)

Defining conditions for state

\displaystyle\Omega\equiv\textstyle\sum^{l}_{r=1}\psi_{r}\otimes\psi^{\dagger}_{r},

\displaystyle\Omega\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}=0.

The Plücker relations are usually formulated as algebraic equations in amplitudes

\displaystyle v(1,2)v(3,4)-v(1,3)v(2,4)+v(1,4)v(2,3)=0,

which in this case is the only independent relation coming from Equation

Plücker relations can be connected to a scalar correlation measure.
Equation

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=|\Omega\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}|^{2}=\rm{Tr}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{1}^{2})\equiv\omega_{1},

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{pq}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{v}\psi^{\dagger}_{q}\psi_{p}\ket{v},

,
55

Generalized Plücker relations and twisted purity.

In the non-Slater part of the Hilbert space, states can contain differing degrees of correlation.
This work is centered around states
\ket{v}
with limited correlations, as defined by our generalization of Plücker relations:

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}=0,

(7)

where
k
is a positive integer.
We denote
\mathcal{G}_{k}\subset\mathcal{H}
as the set of states satisfying Eq.
7
— in particular,
\mathcal{G}_{k=1}
are Slater (cf. Eq.
3
). From the structure of Eq.
7
, we have
\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}}\subset\mathcal{G}_{k_{2}}
if
k_{1}<k_{2}
, inducing a nested pattern of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
(Fig.
1
). Because
\Omega^{n+1}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}
vanishes for any
n
-fermion state,
\mathcal{G}_{k=n+1}\equiv\mathcal{H}
.

We now introduce twisted purity
\omega_{k}
, defined as

\displaystyle\omega_{k}\left(\ket{v}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\left|\frac{\Omega^{k}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}}{k!}\right|^{2},

(8)

Equation
7
is manifestly equivalent to
\omega_{k}
being zero. The twisted purity is single-particle invariant and can be expressed as

\displaystyle\omega_{k}=\rm{Tr}[\rho_{k}\tilde{\rho}_{k}],

(9)

where
\rho_{k}
is an order
k
-body reduced density matrix (
k
-RDM) with matrix elements

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{Q,P}=\bra{v}\Psi^{\dagger}_{P}\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{Q}\ket{v}

(10)

for sets
P
and
Q
(
|P|=|Q|=k
) and
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
its twisted version, which we define as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k}^{Q,P}=\bra{v}\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{Q}\Psi^{\dagger}_{P}\ket{v}.

(11)

The term ‘twisted’ is a reference to ‘twist product’
[
56
]
,
implying a flipped multiplication order of
\Psi^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{Q}
and
\Psi^{\dagger}_{P}
compared to
\rho_{k}^{Q,P}
. Twisted
k
-RDM
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
can also be viewed as a particle-hole dual of
{\rho}_{k}
.
Furthermore,
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
can be expressed in ordinary
k^{\prime}
-RDMs for
k^{\prime}\leq k
by commuting
\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{Q}
through
\Psi^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}_{P}
(see Appendix
B
). Because such
k^{\prime}
-RDMs are marginals of the
k
-RDM, we have that
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
and
\omega_{k}
are functions of
\rho_{k}
.

The twisted purity
\omega_{k}
generalizes the single-body purity
\omega_{1}
(Eq.
5
) to
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
\omega_{k}
is different from the reduced purity
\rm{Tr}[\rho_{k}^{2}]
of Ref.
[
57
]
.
For example,
\rm{Tr}[\rho_{k}^{2}]
reaching its maximal value is a criterion for Slater states
[
58
]
, while vanishing of
\omega_{k}
defines
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, which is a broader class. Twisted purity is also distinct from k-RDM cumulants
[
59
,
60
]
. The cumulants enjoy the property of additive separability, which twisted purities are lacking. On the other hand, our main result – classes
\mathcal{G}_{k}
and their connection to computational physics – do not have a known analogue for k-RDM cumulants.

Twisted purities are in principle observable experimentally if two copies of the studied state
\ket{v}
can be produced at will. Indeed,
\omega_{k}
is an expectation value of a
4k
-body Hermitian operator
(\Omega^{\dagger})^{k}\Omega^{k}
on a state
\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}
(cf. Eq.
8
).
Extracting
\rho_{k}
by tomography provides another route to obtaining
\omega_{k}
from an experiment.

Meaning of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
classification.

To understand the type of correlations captured by twisted purity
\omega_{k}
, we study various examples of states in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
. A broad class of examples is given by states
\ket{v}
which obey the condition

\displaystyle\forall S_{1},S_{2},~v(S_{1,2})\neq 0:~~\frac{1}{2}|S_{1}\triangle S_{2}|<k,

(12)

where
\triangle
means symmetric difference.
In other words, occupation numbers in basis states
\ket{S}
in support of such
\ket{v}
are close to each other in Hamming distance.
Any
\ket{v}
satisfying Eq.
12
belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{k}
,

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}

\displaystyle\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}=\sum_{R\subset[l],|R|=k}\Psi_{R}\otimes\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{R}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subset[l]\\
R\subset S_{1}\backslash S_{2},\\
|R|=k\end{subarray}}v(S_{1})v(S_{2})\Psi_{R}\ket{S_{1}}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{R}\ket{S_{2}}=0.

(13)

The condition
R\subset S_{1}\backslash S_{2}
appeared in the sum due to
\Psi_{R}\ket{S_{1}}
(
\Psi^{\dagger}_{R}\ket{S_{2}}
) vanishing unless
R\subset S_{1}
(
R\cap S_{2}=\varnothing
). For such
S_{1,2}
that
v(S_{1,2})\neq 0
we have
|S_{1}\backslash S_{2}|=\frac{1}{2}|S_{1}\triangle S_{2}|<k
.
Therefore, the sum over
R\subset S_{1}\backslash S_{2}
for
|R|=k
only contains zero elements, yielding Eq.
13
.

Information-theoretically, the condition in Eq.
12
captures the locality of correlations, discriminating between Bell-like correlated states and GHZ-like. For
(l,n)=(8,4)
, state
\ket{v_{1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{1,2,3,4}+\ket{1,2,5,6})
obeys Eq.
12
for
k=3
, while
\ket{v_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{1,2,3,4}+\ket{5,6,7,8})
only for
k=5
. Moreover, one can prove that the state
\ket{v_{2}}
will not obey Eq.
12
for
k=3
in
any
single-particle mode basis. This follows from
\ket{v_{2}}\notin\mathcal{G}_{3}
, which can be checked by a direct computation:
\omega_{3}(\ket{v_{2}})\neq 0
. From the single-particle invariance of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, if
\ket{v}
obeys Eq.
12
in at least some single-particle basis, then
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
.

Counting the degrees of freedom remaining under Eq.
12
for a given
k
is a difficult task. Let us instead bound it from below. Consider a subset of such states, obeying a condition relative to a fixed basis state
\ket{S_{0}}
:

\displaystyle\forall S,~v(S)\neq 0:|S\triangle S_{0}|<k.

(14)

Eq.
14
implies Eq.
12
: from
|S_{1}\triangle S_{0}|<k
and
|S_{2}\triangle S_{0}|<k
one recovers
\frac{1}{2}|S_{1}\triangle S_{2}|<k
. States obeying Eq.
14
form a linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
of poly-sized dimension

\displaystyle\sum^{\lfloor{k/2}\rfloor}_{r=0}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
r\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}l-n\\
r\end{pmatrix}\sim n^{\lfloor{k/2}\rfloor}(l-n)^{\lfloor{k/2}\rfloor}

(15)

for

. This lower bounds the number of degrees of freedom in the set defined by Eq.
12
, and therefore also in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
. Later we upper bound the size of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
by counting the degrees of freedom in our non-Slater ansatz.

States which obey Eq.
12
(and thus belong to
\mathcal{G}_{k}
) naturally arise in perturbation theory truncated at finite order. Consider the Dyson series for a ground state
\ket{v}
of
H=H_{0}+\textstyle\sum V
. Here

is free-fermionic with a unique ground state
\ket{S_{0}}
, and

are

-fermion Coulomb interactions. The perturbation theory is converging if

is gapped (e.g., for a band insulator) and

is sufficiently weak.
The

\ket{v}^{(r)}
of

consists of terms proportional to

for

. Since

has up-to-

\ket{S}
contributing to

More broadly than truncated perturbation series, Eq.

From a quantum chemistry standpoint, our classes

Conversely, nonzero

Extended Wick’s rule and
\mathcal{G}_{k}
parameterization.

To show that states
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
admit an explicit non-Slater ansatz, the crucial step is showing a version of Wick’s rule
[
67
,
68
]
for amplitudes of
\ket{v}
. It is a generalization of Wick’s rule for amplitudes that holds for Slater states
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
. To spell out both the Slater and non-Slater versions, organize basis states
\ket{S}
as ‘excitations’ with respect to a reference
\ket{G}
, namely
\ket{S}=\ket{G\cup Q\backslash P}
for sets
P\subset G
and
Q\subset([l]\backslash G)
,
|P|=|Q|

[
69
]
.
In a
Slater
state
\ket{v}
, the amplitudes of ‘multi-particle excitations’ (i.e.,
|P|=|Q|>1
) partition into single-particle excitations (assuming
v(G)\neq 0
),

\displaystyle\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=(-1)^{|P|(|P|-1)/2}\mathrm{det}\left[\frac{v(G\cup q\backslash p)}{v(G)}\right],

(16)

where the bracketed expression is a
|P|

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{R\subset(B\backslash A),|R|=k}\,v(A\cup R)\,v(B\backslash R)

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\end{subarray}}\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}\frac{v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})}{v(G)}~

For all terms on the right hand side of Eq.

\displaystyle T_{k^{\prime}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\subset G,Q\subset[l]\backslash{G},\\

\displaystyle F(x_{1},\,

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\int\limits_{0}^{1}\dfrac{x_{1}+3x_{3}\mu^{2}+5x_{5}\mu^{4}+..}{1+2(x_{2}\mu^{2}+x_{4}\mu^{4}+..)}d\mu\right).

are its unknown free parameters and amplitude

Discussion.

An interesting open direction is to apply the ansatz of Eq.
19
in a practical computation, such as the search for a ground state energy of a given model.
One may employ the structure of Eq.
19
, which is similar to the coupled-cluster ansatz — except the latter uses
F(T_{1},..T_{k})=\exp(T_{1}+..+T_{k})
. Because of this similarity, the numerical methods for coupled-cluster which don’t rely on the exponential form of
F(T_{1},..T_{k})
, can be used with our ansatz (e.g., see Refs.
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
). Another intriguing question is whether the structure of
k
-RDMs identified in this work can be used in the context of
N
-representability problem
[
75
,
76
,
77
,
78
]
.

Using the formalism developed here, one may study the structure of states coming from a system with weak and
sparse
interactions. For such states, one expects that for any
k
, all but a few components in
\Omega^{k}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}
are extremely small. One may define a class of states where these small components are set to vanish exactly. For states in this class, the ansatz of the type given in Eq.
19
may be compact due to many vanishing parameters
\theta_{P,Q}
. Unlike
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, such a class would depend on the choice of the single-particle basis. Pinning down this structure is an interesting research direction.

An object worth further study is the generating function of twisted purities,
Z(\beta)=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\beta^{2k}
. In this work, we found
Z(\beta)
to be a useful analytical tool, owing to its multiplicativity for products of independent subsystems (see Appendices
D
,
E
). However, the structure of this generating function could also have useful interpretations. For instance, its factorization gives a necessary condition that a state is a single-particle transformed product of small subsystems. Developing such criteria further could be viewed as a ‘size-consistent’
[
79
]
extension of the present work.

Other open research directions include extending our formalism to the states which are mixed or whose particle number is not fixed. Here, it may prove useful that operator
\Lambda
of Refs.
[
16
,
80
]
equals
\Omega+\Omega^{\dagger}
up to normalization. Finally, by analogy with the entanglement theory, a worthwhile goal is to investigate if twisted purities define a monotone under the free-fermionic operations, including measurements.

Appendix A
Fermionic algebra and sequence manipulations

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space of
n
-fermion states on
l
modes. Consider any fixed single-particle basis for the fermionic modes, defined by annihilation operators
\{\psi_{j}|j\in[l]\}
. Operators
\psi_{j}
and their Hermitian conjugate creation operators
\psi^{\dagger}_{j}
obey the algebra

\displaystyle\psi_{j_{1}}\psi_{j_{2}}=-\psi_{j_{2}}\psi_{j_{1}},~\psi_{j_{1}}\psi^{\dagger}_{j_{2}}+\psi^{\dagger}_{j_{2}}\psi_{j_{1}}=\delta_{j_{1}j_{2}},

(22)

where
\delta_{j_{1}j_{2}}
is the Kronecker delta. For an ordered sequence
S=(s_{1},..s_{n})
(
0\leq s_{1}<..<s_{n}\leq l
), we define annihilation operator monomial
\Psi_{S}=\psi_{s_{1}}..\psi_{s_{n}}
. Notation
\Psi^{\dagger}_{S}
is defined as
\Psi^{\dagger}_{S}=\left(\Psi_{S}\right)^{\dagger}=\psi^{\dagger}_{s_{n}}..\psi^{\dagger}_{s_{1}}
(note the change in multiplication order).
The basis
\{\ket{S}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
is defined as
\ket{S}=\psi^{\dagger}_{s_{n}}..\psi^{\dagger}_{s_{1}}\ket{\varnothing}=\left(\Psi_{S}\right)^{{\dagger}}\ket{\varnothing}
. Here
\ket{\varnothing}
is the Fock space vacuum.

For more convenient fermionic algebra manipulations, we introduce some formalities involving sequences of integers. We will only consider finite sequences; the length of a sequence
A=(a_{1},..,a_{|A|})
is denoted as
|A|
. If all elements in
A
are smaller than all elements in
B
(
a<b,~\forall a\in A,~b\in B
), we say
A<B
. The ordered version of a sequence
A
without repeating elements is denoted
\mathrm{ord}(A)
,
i.e.,
\mathrm{ord}(A)=(a_{\pi(1)},a_{\pi(2)}..a_{\pi(|A|)})
for the ordering permutation
\pi:[|A|]\rightarrow[|A|]
,
a_{\pi(1)}<a_{\pi(2)}<..<a_{\pi(|A|)}
. A signature function
\sigma(A)=\pm 1
for
A
without repeating elements is equal to the sign of a permutation
\pi
required to order
A
. We also define
\sigma(A,B)
for a pair of sequences
A=(a_{1},a_{2},..a_{|A|})
and
B=(b_{1},..b_{|B|})

without shared or repeating elements
, as a signature for a single concatenated sequence
\sigma\left((a_{1},a_{2},..a_{|A|},b_{1},..b_{|B|})\right)
. Through sequence concatenation we also define
\sigma(A,B,C)
for three arguments, as well as
\sigma
for more arguments.

An ordered sequence
A=(a_{1},..a_{|A|})
without repeating elements naturally maps to a set, namely
\mathrm{set}(A)\equiv\{a_{1},..a_{|A|}\}
. Vice-versa, a set of integers
A_{\mathrm{set}}=\{a_{1},..a_{|A|}\}
can be mapped to an ordered sequence
\mathrm{seq}(A_{\mathrm{set}})\equiv\mathrm{ord}\left((a_{1},..a_{|A|})\right)
. This one-to-one mapping allows to use set-theoretic notions for ordered sequences without repeating elements. An intersection of two sequences is defined as
A\cap B=\mathrm{seq}(\mathrm{set}(A)\cap\mathrm{set}(B))
, same for union
\cup
, difference
\backslash
and symmetric difference
\triangle
.
A
is a subsequence of
B
, or
A\subset B
, if
\mathrm{set}(A)\subset\mathrm{set}(B)
. For an integer
l
with
[l]
we denote the sequence
(1,2,..l)
, rather than a set
\{1,2,..,l\}
as per usual. Thus adapting set-theoretic notation to sequences allows to give a simpler form to many technical parts of this work.

A few useful properties of the signature function
\sigma
can be stated with help of set-theoretic notation. For ordered sequences without repeating or shared elements
A
,
B
and
C
, we have (note
A\cup B=\mathrm{ord}\left((a_{1},..,a_{|A|},b_{1},..,b_{|B|})\right)
)

\displaystyle\sigma(A,B,C)

\displaystyle=\sigma(A,B)\sigma(A\cup B,C),

(23)

\displaystyle\sigma(A\cup B,C)

\displaystyle=\sigma(A,C)\sigma(B,C),

(24)

\displaystyle\sigma(A,B)

\displaystyle=\sigma(B,A)(-1)^{|A|\cdot|B|},

(25)

\displaystyle\sigma(A,B)

\displaystyle=1~\rm{if}~A<B.

(26)

As announced, the introduced formalities are convenient in dealing with fermionic algebra. For example

\displaystyle\Psi_{A}\Psi_{B}

\displaystyle=\sigma(A,B)\Psi_{A\cup B}\,\mathrm{for}\,A\cap B=\varnothing,

(27)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\dagger}_{B}\ket{A}

\displaystyle=\sigma(A,B)\ket{A\cup B}\,\mathrm{for}\,A\cap B=\varnothing,

(28)

\displaystyle\Psi_{B}\ket{A}

\displaystyle=\sigma(A\backslash B,B)\ket{A\backslash B}\,\mathrm{for}\,B\subset A.

(29)

Appendix D
Products of Bell-like states and their thermodynamic limits

We call the state

\ket{\varphi}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{1,2}+\ket{3,4})

(35)

a Bell-like fermionic state. It is one of the simplest states for which
\omega_{1}\neq 0
. In this Appendix we compute twisted purities of a state which is a high tensor power of
\ket{\varphi}
. It proves convenient to use the generating function

Z(\ket{v},\beta)=\sum_{k=0}^{l}\omega_{k}(\ket{v})\beta^{2k}

(36)

because of its multiplicative properties. Let
\ket{v}=\ket{v_{1}}\wedge\ket{v_{2}}
be the product state of fermionic system containing two subsystems. In this case
\Omega=\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2}
,
[\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}]=0
. Noting that

\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\beta^{2k}=|e^{\beta\Omega}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}|^{2}.

(37)

one can deduce

\displaystyle Z(\ket{v_{1}}\wedge\ket{v_{2}},\beta)=|e^{\beta\Omega}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}|^{2}=|e^{\beta(\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2})}(\ket{v_{1}}\wedge\ket{v_{2}})\otimes(\ket{v_{1}}\wedge\ket{v_{2}})|^{2}=

\displaystyle=|(e^{\beta\Omega_{1}}\ket{v_{1}}\otimes\ket{v_{1}})\otimes(e^{\beta\Omega_{2}}\ket{v_{2}}\otimes\ket{v_{2}})|^{2}=Z(\ket{v_{1}},\beta)Z(\ket{v_{2}},\beta).

(38)

Now consider a state
(\ket{\varphi})^{\wedge p}
on
l=4p
sites and with
n=2p
particles. A direct computation shows that
Z(\ket{\varphi},\beta)=1+\beta^{2}+\beta^{4}
, so

Z(\ket{\varphi}^{\wedge l/4},\beta)=(1+\beta^{2}+\beta^{4})^{l/4}.

(39)

For example, for
l=12
this equation gives
Z=1+3\beta^{2}+6\beta^{4}+7\beta^{6}+6\beta^{8}+3\beta^{10}+\beta^{12}
.

We use the multiplicativity of
Z
to identify the properties of
\omega_{k}
for locally-correlated systems in the thermodynamic limit. In the process, we will obtain a version of central limit theorem. By ‘locally-correlated’ we mean that the system can be broken into a set of subsystems, whose sizes are small compared to the whole, while the correlations between subsystems can be neglected. Let
Z_{0}(\beta^{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\beta^{2k}\omega_{0,k}
be the generating function of twisted purities for the state of one small subsystem. Generically, it will be a polynomial in
\beta^{2}
of degree
r=\rho d
, where
d\ll l
is the number of fermionic sites in the subsystem and
\rho=n/l
is the filling factor of the system. We assume
\rho<1/2
. The generating function for the whole system would be

Z(\beta^{2})=(Z_{0}(\beta^{2}))^{\tfrac{l}{d}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+p_{i}\beta^{2})^{\tfrac{l}{d}}.

(40)

for some coefficients
p_{i}
, coming from factorization of the polynomial
Z_{0}(\beta^{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+p_{i}\beta^{2})
with the property
Z_{0}(0)=1
. Using the binomial and Stirling formulas one can find that each term behaves in
l\to\infty
limit as

(1+p_{i}\beta^{2})^{\tfrac{l}{d}}\sim\sum_{k}\beta^{2k}a_{i}\exp\left(lb_{i}-lc_{i}(k/l-\varkappa_{i})^{2}\right),

(41)

a_{i}=\dfrac{1+p_{i}}{\sqrt{2\pi l}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{p_{i}}},~~~b_{i}=\frac{\log[1+p_{i}]}{d},~~~c_{i}=\dfrac{d(1+p_{i})^{2}}{2p_{i}},~~~\varkappa_{i}=\dfrac{p_{i}}{d(1+p_{i})}.

(42)

Now, multiplying the expressions with the different
p_{i}
, one finds total generating function to be

Z(\beta^{2})\sim\sum_{k}\beta^{2k}\tilde{a}\exp\left(l\tilde{b}-l\tilde{c}(k/l-\tilde{\varkappa})^{2}\right),~~~\text{where}~~~\tilde{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_{i},~~~\dfrac{1}{\tilde{c}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\dfrac{1}{c_{i}},~~~\tilde{\varkappa}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varkappa_{i}.

(43)

Taking logarithmic derivatives of
Z_{0}(\beta^{2})
, the parameters can be expressed in terms of the purities of subsystems

e^{l\tilde{b}}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\omega_{0,k}\right)^{l/d},~~~\tilde{\varkappa}=\dfrac{1}{d}\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{r}k\omega_{0,k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{r}\omega_{0,k}},~~~\dfrac{1}{\tilde{c}}=\dfrac{2}{d}\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{r}k^{2}\omega_{0,k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{r}\omega_{0,k}}-\dfrac{2}{d}\left(\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{r}k\omega_{0,k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{r}\omega_{0,k}}\right)^{2}.

(44)

Thus we find that in thermodynamic limit, only a few parameters control the dominating contributions to
\omega_{k}
.

It is interesting to note that a tensor power of Bell states does not belong to a class
\mathcal{G}_{k}
for a finite
k
, despite consisting of disentangled finite blocks. This example demonstrates that strong fermionic correlations signalled by nonzero
\omega_{k=O(n)}
, similarly to extensive fermionic magic
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, are compatible with an absence of system-wide entanglement. It is an intriguing open question, whether entanglement and
k
-body fermionic correlations can be simultaneously captured with a single measure in a useful way. In our view, the answer to this question may well be negative. It also connects to the problem of constructing size-consistent fermionic correlation measures, discussed in the main text.

Appendix E
Purities of Haar random states

In this section we will compute average twisted purities of the real random states
\ket{v}=\sum_{A\in[l]^{k}}v_{A}\ket{A}
,
v_{A}\in\mathbb{R}
, in a Hilbert space of
n
fermions on
l
sites. We consider the states to be distributed uniformly, i.e. the states are given by a random points on
S^{d}
,
d={l\choose n}
, with the
SO(d)
invariant distribution. We will refer to these states as to (real) Haar states, and denote the average over this measure by
\langle~~~\rangle_{|v|^{2}=1}
. The averaged purity is

\langle\omega_{k}\rangle=\dfrac{1}{(k!)^{2}}\left\langle\bra{v}\otimes\bra{v}(\Omega^{{\dagger}})^{k}\Omega^{k}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}\right\rangle_{|v|^{2}=1}.

(45)

Because of
SO(d)
invariance, the four-point correlation function
\langle v_{A}v_{B}v_{C}v_{D}\rangle_{|v|^{2}=1}
should be some quadratic combination of Kronecker
\delta
-symbols. Since it is symmetric under permutation of indices, up to overall factor it should be equal to
\delta_{AB}\delta_{CD}+\delta_{AC}\delta_{BD}+\delta_{AD}\delta_{BC}
. The normalization factor can be fixed contracting the correlation function with
\delta_{AB}\delta_{CD}
, and using that
\langle 1\rangle_{|v|^{2}=1}=1
. This results in

\langle v_{A}v_{B}v_{C}v_{D}\rangle_{|v|^{2}=1}=\dfrac{1}{d(d+2)}(\delta_{AB}\delta_{CD}+\delta_{AC}\delta_{BD}+\delta_{AD}\delta_{BC}).

(46)

which gives for the averaged twisted purities

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{k}\rangle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{d(d+2)}\sum_{|I|,|J|=k}\sum_{|A|,|B|=n}\left(\langle A|\Psi_{I}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{J}|A\rangle\langle B|\Psi^{\dagger}_{I}\Psi_{J}|B\rangle+\right.

(47)

\displaystyle\left.+\langle A|\Psi_{I}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{J}|B\rangle\langle B|\Psi^{\dagger}_{I}\Psi_{J}|A\rangle+\langle A|\Psi_{I}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{J}|B\rangle\langle A|\Psi^{\dagger}_{I}\Psi_{J}|B\rangle\right).

(48)

Three terms in the sum can be computed as

\sum_{|I|,|J|=k}\sum_{|A|,|B|=n}\langle A|\Psi_{I}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{J}|A\rangle\langle B|\Psi^{\dagger}_{I}\Psi_{J}|B\rangle=\sum_{|I|,|J|=n}\sum_{|A|,|B|=K}\delta_{I=J}\delta_{I\cap A=\varnothing}\delta_{J\subset B},

(49)

\sum_{|I|,|J|=k}\sum_{|A|,|B|=n}\langle A|\Psi_{I}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{J}|B\rangle\langle B|\Psi^{\dagger}_{I}\Psi_{J}|A\rangle=\sum_{|I|,|J|=k}\sum_{|A|,|B|=n}\delta_{A\cup I=B\cup J}\delta_{J\cap B=\varnothing}\delta_{I\cap A=\varnothing}\delta_{A\backslash J=B\backslash I}\delta_{J\subset A}\delta_{I\subset B},

(50)

\sum_{|I|,|J|=k}\sum_{|A|,|B|=n}\langle A|\Psi_{I}\Psi^{{\dagger}}_{J}|B\rangle\langle A|\Psi^{\dagger}_{I}\Psi_{J}|B\rangle=0,

(51)

which gives after summation

\langle\omega_{k}\rangle=\dfrac{{l\choose k}{l-k\choose n}{l-k\choose n-k}+{l\choose n-k}{l-n\choose k}{l-n+k\choose k}}{{l\choose n}\left({l\choose n}+2\right)}=\frac{1}{{l\choose n}+2}\dfrac{k!(l-k)!+n!(l-n)!}{(k!)^{2}(n-k)!(l-n-k)!}.

(52)

Since this formula is exact, we can see explicitly how
\langle\omega_{k}\rangle
gets concentrated around its thermodynamically preferred value at
l,n\gg 1
. Using the Stirling formula
p!\sim\sqrt{2\pi p}(p/e)^{p}
one can estimate the relation of contributions of the first and second terms in Eq.
52
as

\dfrac{k!(l-k)!}{n!(l-n)!}\sim e^{l(s(\varkappa)-s(\rho))},~~~s(x)=\Lambda(x)+\Lambda(1-x),~~~\Lambda(x)=x\log(x)

(53)

where
k=\varkappa l
,
n=\rho l
. For half-filled system one has
0<\varkappa<\rho<1/2
and
s(x)
is monotonically decreasing, so
s(\varkappa)>s(\rho)
. Thus the second term in (
52
) is exponentially suppressed. Now the dominating value of
k
can be found by extremizing the function

\dfrac{\log\langle\omega_{k}\rangle}{l}\sim\Lambda(\rho)+\Lambda(1-\rho)+\Lambda(1-\varkappa)-\Lambda(\rho-\varkappa)-\Lambda(\varkappa)-\Lambda(1-\rho-\varkappa)

(54)

with respect to
\varkappa
. This gives:

\langle\omega_{k}\rangle\sim a\exp\left(lb-lc(k/l-\varkappa_{*})^{2}\right)

(55)

where

2\varkappa_{*}(1-\varkappa_{*})=\rho(1-\rho)~~~\Rightarrow~~~\varkappa_{*}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\kappa),~~~\kappa=\sqrt{1-2\rho(1-\rho)},

(56)

a=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi l}}\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\kappa}{1-\kappa}},~~~c=\dfrac{2\sqrt{1-2\rho(1-\rho)}}{\rho(1-\rho)},

(57)

b=\Lambda(1-\rho)+\Lambda(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}\Lambda(1+\kappa)-\frac{1}{2}\Lambda(1-\kappa)-\frac{1}{2}\Lambda(\kappa-2\rho+1)-\frac{1}{2}\Lambda(\kappa+2\rho-1).

(58)

It is curious to note the simple behaviour of
\langle\omega_{k}\rangle
at
k\ll n<l

\langle\omega_{k}\rangle\sim\dfrac{(\rho(1-\rho))^{k}l^{k}}{k!}=\dfrac{(\langle\omega_{1}\rangle)^{k}}{k!}.

(59)

In this case the generating function from Eq.
36
behaves as

\langle Z(\beta)\rangle\sim e^{\beta^{2}\langle\omega_{1}\rangle}

(60)

which is consistent with multiplicativity under the disjoint union of the systems because
\langle\omega_{1}\rangle
is additive.

Appendix F
Upper bound on the twisted purities

The values of the twisted purities can be bounded applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the operators

\mathrm{Tr}[A^{{\dagger}}B]\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}[A^{\dagger}A]\mathrm{Tr}[B^{\dagger}B]}.

(61)

Using that both k-RDM and twisted k-RDM are Hermitian operators
(\rho_{k}^{P,Q})^{*}=\rho_{k}^{Q,P}
,
(\tilde{\rho}_{k}^{P,Q})^{*}=\tilde{\rho}_{k}^{Q,P}
one finds

\omega_{k}=\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}_{k}\rho_{k}]\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\rho_{k}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\rho}_{k}]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{k}^{\dagger}\rho_{k}]}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}_{k}^{2}]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{k}^{2}]}.

(62)

The operator
\rho_{k}
is positive, i.e. all of its eigenvalues are
\geq 0
, which gives for its trace
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{k}^{2}]\leq(\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{k}])^{2}
. To compute the traces of k-RDM

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{k}]=\sum_{P\subset[l],\,|P|=k}\rho_{k}^{P,P}=\sum_{0\leq i_{k}<...<i_{1}\leq l}\bra{v}\psi_{i_{k}}^{{\dagger}}...\psi_{i_{1}}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{i_{1}}...\psi_{i_{k}}\ket{v}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{i_{k}\neq...\neq i_{1}}\bra{v}n_{i_{k}}...n_{i_{1}}\ket{v}

(63)

note that the boundary terms in a sum can be resolved as

\sum_{i_{k}\neq...\neq i_{1}}=\sum_{i_{k}}\sum_{i_{k-1}\neq...\neq i_{1}}-\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}\sum_{i_{k-1}=i_{\alpha}\neq...\neq i_{1}}.

(64)

Together with
\sum_{0\leq i\leq l}{n_{i}}=n
and
(n_{i})^{2}=n_{i}
this gives a recursive formula

\sum_{i_{k}\neq...\neq i_{1}}\bra{v}n_{i_{k}}...n_{i_{1}}\ket{v}=(n-k+1)\sum_{i_{k-1}\neq...\neq i_{1}}\bra{v}n_{i_{k-1}}...n_{i_{1}}\ket{v}=...=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.

(65)

Similar consideration is applicable to
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
with
n_{i}
being replaced by
1-n_{i}
and
n
being replaced by
l-n
. Collecting all the formulas together one gets a bound

\omega_{k}\leq{n\choose k}{l-n\choose k}.

(66)

Appendix G
Derivation of extended Wick’s rule (recursive form)

Theorem 1
.

Let
\ket{v}=\sum_{S\subset[l],|S|=n}v(S)\ket{S}
be a state in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\Omega^{k}\ket{v}\ket{v}=0
. Consider three ordered sequences
G=(1,..n)
,
P\subset G
and
Q\subset[l]\backslash{G}
. If
|Q|=|P|>k
,

\displaystyle\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}\,\frac{|P^{\prime}|}{|P|}\,\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})\,\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})~\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}\frac{v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})}{v(G)}.

(67)

Proof.

Since
\Omega^{k}\ket{v}^{\otimes 2}=0
implies
\Omega^{k^{\prime}}\ket{v}^{\otimes 2}=0
for all
k^{\prime}>k
, it is sufficient to prove Eq.
67
for
P
and
Q
such that
|P|=|Q|=k+1
. Consider the component
\bra{A}\bra{B}\Omega^{k}\ket{v}\otimes\ket{v}=0
(Eq.
17
):

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{R\subset(B\backslash A),|R|=k}\sigma(A,R)\,\sigma(B\backslash R,R)~v(A\cup R)\,v(B\backslash R)=0,

(68)

choosing
A=(G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p}
and
B=G\cup Q\backslash\tilde{p}
for some fixed
\tilde{p}\in P
.
Observe that
R
is a subset of
(B\backslash A)=Q\cup P\backslash\tilde{p}
with length
|R|=k
, while
|Q|=|P|=k+1
. This implies that
R
can be represented as
R=\left(P^{\prime}\cup(Q\setminus Q^{\prime})\right)\backslash\tilde{p}
for certain
P^{\prime}\subset P
and
Q^{\prime}\subset Q
such that
\tilde{p}\in P^{\prime}
and
|Q^{\prime}|=|P^{\prime}|
. We also introduce simplified notations
\bar{Q}^{\prime}=Q\backslash Q^{\prime}
and
\bar{P}^{\prime}=P\backslash P^{\prime}
. The goal of these substitutions is to give the amplitude product
v(A\cup R)\,v(B\backslash R)
the form
v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})\,v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})
, bringing Eq.
68
closer to the desired structure of Eq.
67
.

With the above definitions for
A
,
B
,
R
and noting that
Q>G
and
Q>P
by assumptions of Theorem
1
, let us restructure the sign factor
\sigma(A,R)\,\sigma(B\backslash R,R)
:

\displaystyle\sigma((G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p},R)

\displaystyle~\sigma((G\cup Q\setminus\tilde{p}\setminus R),R)=\sigma((G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p},\bar{Q}^{\prime})~\sigma((G\cup Q\setminus P^{\prime}\backslash\bar{Q}^{\prime}),P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma((G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(G\backslash P^{\prime},Q^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma((G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(G\backslash P^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p},Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})~(-1)^{|Q^{\prime}|(|P^{\prime}|-1)}

\displaystyle=\sigma((G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(G\backslash P^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma(G\backslash P^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(\tilde{p},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(G\backslash P^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(\tilde{p},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(\tilde{p},\bar{P}^{\prime})~\sigma(\tilde{p},P\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|}~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime}\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},\tilde{p})~\sigma(\tilde{p},P\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|}~\sigma(\tilde{p},P\backslash\tilde{p})~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

(69)

The strategy in this derivation was to (i) separate
\sigma
’s involving
Q
on the one hand and
G
and
P
on the other hand, then to (ii) eliminate the dependency of the expression on
G
, and finally to (iii) separate
\sigma
’s involving
P^{\prime}
and
\tilde{p}
. Since the factor
\sigma(\tilde{p},P\backslash\tilde{p})
does not depend on the choice of
P^{\prime}
or
Q^{\prime}
, it can be eliminated from Eq.
68
altogether, yielding (recall substitutions
A=(G\backslash P)\cup\tilde{p}
,
B=G\cup Q\backslash\tilde{p}
, and
R=(P^{\prime}\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime})\backslash\tilde{p}
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subset P,~Q^{\prime}\subset Q\\
\rm{:}\,\tilde{p}\in P^{\prime}\&|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})~v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})~(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|}~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})=0.

(70)

Moving the terms corresponding to
P^{\prime}\neq P
,
Q^{\prime}\neq Q
to the right-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle v(G)~v(G\cup Q\setminus P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\\
\rm{:}\,\tilde{p}\in P^{\prime}\&|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})~v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})~(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime}).

(71)

Dividing both sides by
v(G)^{2}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\\
\rm{:}\,\tilde{p}\in P^{\prime}\&|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}\frac{v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})}{v(G)}~(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime}).

(72)

The theorem statement (Eq.
67
) is then obtained by summing up Eq.
72
for all
\tilde{p}\in P
:

\displaystyle|P|~\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{p}\in P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\\
\rm{:}\,\tilde{p}\in P^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}\frac{v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})}{v(G)}~(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\end{subarray}}\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}\frac{v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})}{v(G)}~|P^{\prime}|~(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}~\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime}).

(73)

∎

Appendix H
Generalized Wick’s rule

Consider a state
\ket{v}=\sum v(S)
that lies in
\mathcal{G}_{k}\subset\mathcal{H}
and three ordered sequences
G=(1,..n)
,
P\subset G
,
Q\subset[l]\backslash{G}
. We are concerned with decomposing multi-excitation amplitudes
v(G\cup Q\backslash P)/v(G)
into few-excitation
\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}
(
|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|\leq k
). As was mentioned in the main text, giving an explicit formula for such a decomposition is difficult. The key simplifying step is to use ‘connected amplitudes’
v^{(c)}_{P,Q}
:

\displaystyle v^{(c)}_{P,Q}\equiv\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\end{subarray}}\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}\frac{v(G\cup\bar{Q}^{\prime}\backslash\bar{P}^{\prime})}{v(G)}~(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}\,\frac{|P^{\prime}|}{|P|}\,\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},{P}^{\prime})~\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime}).

(74)

which capture the deviation of
\ket{v}
from recursive Wick’s rule (Eq.
67
) — if
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
and
|P|=|Q|>k
,
v^{(c)}_{P,Q}=0
. For
|P|=|Q|=1
, we define simply
v^{(c)}_{P,Q}=\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}
. Theorem
2
below will expresses amplitudes
\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}
of
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
in terms of
v^{(c)}_{P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}}
for
|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|\leq k
alone. One can think of this statement as an amplitude version of a cumulant expansion. Via Eq.
74
, this implies a decomposition of
\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}
into
\frac{v(G\cup Q^{\prime}\backslash P^{\prime})}{v(G)}
for
|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|<k
.

To formally state the theorem, further terminology needs to be introduced. We denote
\mathrm{Part}(P,Q)
the set of ‘partitions of
(P,Q)
’. Namely,
\mathrm{Part}(P,Q)
consists of all sets
\mathcal{R}
of type
\mathcal{R}=\{(P^{\prime}_{1},Q^{\prime}_{1}),..(P^{\prime}_{|\mathcal{R}|},Q^{\prime}_{|\mathcal{R}|})\}
such that
|P^{\prime}_{a}|=|Q^{\prime}_{a}|
, and for (disjoint!) unions
P(\mathcal{R})\equiv\bigcup^{|\mathcal{R}|}_{a=1}P^{\prime}_{a}=P
and
Q(\mathcal{R})\equiv\bigcup^{|\mathcal{R}|}_{a=1}Q^{\prime}_{a}=Q
. A more refined set
\mathrm{Part}_{k}(P,Q)\subset\mathrm{Part}(P,Q)
is given by applying another constraint
|P^{\prime}_{a}|,|Q^{\prime}_{a}|\leq k
to all
(P^{\prime}_{a},Q^{\prime}_{a})\in\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{Part}(P,Q)
. The signature function for
\mathcal{R}
is defined as

\sigma\left(\mathcal{R}\right)=\sigma\left(P^{\prime}_{|\mathcal{R}|},P^{\prime}_{|\mathcal{R}|-1},..,P^{\prime}_{1}\right)\,\sigma\left(Q^{\prime}_{1},Q^{\prime}_{2},..,Q^{\prime}_{|\mathcal{R}|}\right).

(75)

Next, we introduce a vector
\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R})=(m_{1},m_{2},..,m_{k})
, where each
m_{k^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
gives the number of tuples
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{R}
such that
|P|=|Q|=k^{\prime}
. In other words,
\mathbf{m}
encodes an integer partition of
|P|=|Q|
that is defined by
\mathcal{R}
(which itself is a ‘partition of
(P,Q)
’). For a general vector
\mathbf{m}
, we denote one-norm
\sum_{k^{\prime}}m_{k^{\prime}}
as
|\mathbf{m}|
, and also will make use of the expression
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}\equiv\sum_{k^{\prime}}k^{\prime}m_{k^{\prime}}
. If
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R})
for
\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{Part}_{k}(P,Q)
, we have simply
|\mathbf{m}|=|\mathcal{R}|
and
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}=|P|
. We say
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}<\mathbf{m}
if for all
k^{\prime}
holds
m^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\leq m_{k^{\prime}}
and at least for one
k^{\prime}
one has strictly
m^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}<m_{k^{\prime}}
.

Theorem 2
.

For any state
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
, the decomposition holds:

\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{Part}_{k}(P,Q)\end{subarray}}\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}))~\sigma(\mathcal{R})\prod_{(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}}v^{(c)}_{{P^{\prime}},{Q^{\prime}}}.

(76)

Here the function
\nu(\mathbf{m})
is equal to 1 if
|\mathbf{m}|=0
or
|\mathbf{m}|=1
, and otherwise is defined by the recursive relation

\displaystyle\nu(\mathbf{m})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}<\mathbf{m}}(-1)^{\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})+1}\frac{\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}}~\nu(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})~\nu(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})~\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{\prime}\\
m_{1}\end{pmatrix}\cdot..\cdot\begin{pmatrix}m_{k}^{\prime}\\
m_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(77)

Proof.

We will show Eq.
76
for
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
by first proving a general statement for all
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{H}
:

\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{Part}(P,Q)\end{subarray}}\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}))~\sigma(\mathcal{R})\prod_{(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}}v^{(c)}_{{P^{\prime}},{Q^{\prime}}}.

(78)

Note that coefficients
v^{(c)}_{{P^{\prime}},{Q^{\prime}}}
are zero for
|P^{\prime}|,|Q^{\prime}|>k
if
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
. Since the contributions of
\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{Part}(P,Q)\backslash\mathrm{Part}_{k}(P,Q)
to Eq.
78
are proportional to at least one such coefficient, these contributions vanish; therefore, Eq.
78
yields Eq.
76
.

We now prove Eq.
78
by induction in
|P|
. For the base of induction
|P|=|Q|=1
, by definition we have
\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=v^{(c)}_{{P},{Q}}
. Since
\rm{Part}(P,Q)
in this case only consists of the trivial
\mathcal{R}=\{(P,Q)\}
, for which
\sigma(\mathcal{R})=1
and
\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}))=1
, we recover Eq.
78
directly. We now assume the validity of Eq.
78
for
|P|=|Q|=k_{\mathrm{ind}}
and prove it for
|P|=|Q|=k_{\mathrm{ind}}+1
. By definition in Eq.
74
and induction step, we have (denoting
\bar{Q}^{\prime}=Q\backslash Q^{\prime}
and
\bar{P}^{\prime}=P\backslash P^{\prime}
):

\displaystyle\frac{v(G\cup Q\backslash P)}{v(G)}=v^{(c)}_{{P},{Q}}~+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}\subsetneq P,~Q^{\prime}\subsetneq Q\\
|P^{\prime}|=|Q^{\prime}|\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Part}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\\
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Part}(\bar{P}^{\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{(P^{\prime\prime},Q^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}v^{(c)}_{{P^{\prime}},{Q^{\prime}}}\prod_{(\bar{P}^{\prime\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime\prime})\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}}v^{(c)}_{{\bar{P}^{\prime}},{\bar{Q}^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle\times(-1)^{|\bar{P}^{\prime}|+1}\,\frac{|P^{\prime}|}{|P|}~{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}))}{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}))}~\sigma(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})\sigma(\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})\sigma(\bar{Q}^{\prime},Q^{\prime})

(79)

Using the property of the sign function
\sigma(A)\sigma(B)\sigma(A,B)=\sigma(A\cup B)
and the definition of
\sigma(\mathcal{R})
(Eq.
75
), one finds a simplification
\sigma(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})\sigma(\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})\sigma(\bar{P}^{\prime},P^{\prime})\sigma(Q^{\prime},\bar{Q^{\prime}})=\sigma(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cup\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
.

Let us show that expression Eq.
79
reproduces the desired sum in Eq.
78
. The term for
\mathcal{R}=\{(P,Q)\}
of this sum is given directly by
v^{(c)}_{{P},{Q}}
in Eq.
79
, since for such
\mathcal{R}
the coefficient
{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}))}~\sigma(\mathcal{R})
is equal to
1
. To reproduce the rest of the sum in Eq.
78
, consider the products
\left(\sigma(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cup\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})\prod_{(P^{\prime\prime},Q^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}v^{(c)}_{{P^{\prime\prime}},{Q^{\prime\prime}}}\prod_{(\bar{P}^{\prime\prime},\bar{Q}^{\prime\prime})\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}}v^{(c)}_{{\bar{P}^{\prime\prime}},{\bar{Q}^{\prime\prime}}}\right)
in Eq.
79
. These have the form
\sigma(\mathcal{R})\prod_{({P}^{\prime\prime},{Q}^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{R}}v^{(c)}_{{{P}^{\prime\prime}},{{Q}^{\prime\prime}}}
for
\mathcal{R}\equiv\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cup\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
. Such
\mathcal{R}
is a nontrivial (
\mathcal{R}\neq\{(P,Q)\}
) partition of
(P,Q)
. Let us now determine the coefficient in front of such a product for any
\mathcal{R}
, examining the terms in Eq.
79
coming from all pairs
\mathcal{R}^{\prime},~\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
which yield
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cup\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}
. We observe that for any nontrivial
\mathcal{R}
every possible splitting into
\mathcal{R}^{\prime},~\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
appears in the sum in Eq.
79
exactly once — in the component of the sum where
P^{\prime}=P(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
,
Q^{\prime}=Q(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
. Collecting the factor in front of
\sigma(\mathcal{R})\prod_{(P^{\prime\prime},Q^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{R}}v^{(c)}_{{{P}^{\prime\prime}},{{Q}^{\prime\prime}}}
in Eq.
79
, we find

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{R}}(-1)^{|P(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})|-|P(\mathcal{R})|+1}\dfrac{|P(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})|}{|P(\mathcal{R})|}{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}))}{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}\backslash{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}<\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R})}(-1)^{\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R})-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})+1}\dfrac{\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R})}{\nu(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})}{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R})-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{\prime}\\
m_{1}(\mathcal{R})\end{pmatrix}\cdot..\cdot\begin{pmatrix}m_{k}^{\prime}\\
m_{k}(\mathcal{R})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\nu(\mathbf{m}(\mathcal{R}))}

(80)

Thus restoring the coefficient in Eq.
78
, we show that the sum in Eq.
79
gives the part of the sum over
\mathcal{R}
in Eq.
78
over nontrivial
\mathcal{R}\neq\{(P,Q)\}
.

∎

Appendix I

In this section we prove that Theorem

\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
:

Theorem 3
.

Any state
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}

\displaystyle\ket{v}

\displaystyle=v(G)\,F(T_{1},..T_{k})\ket{G},

(81)

\displaystyle T_{k^{\prime}}

Proof.

The proof consists of two parts. First we show from Theorem

(84)

for coefficient
\nu(\mathbf{m})
defined in Theorem
2
and parameters in

chosen as
\theta_{P,Q}={v}^{(c)}_{P,Q}\sigma(G\backslash P,P)
for

defined in Appendix

. Subsequently, we show that the generating function of

\displaystyle F(x_{1},..,x_{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\frac{\nu(\mathbf{m})}{m_{1}!m_{2}!..m_{k}!}x_{1}^{m_{1}}..x_{k}^{m_{k}},

. Here we use the fact that
