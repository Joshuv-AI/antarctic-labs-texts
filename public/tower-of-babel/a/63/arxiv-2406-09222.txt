Well-posedness and regularity of solutions to neural field problems with dendritic processing.
Daniele Avitabile, Nikolai V. Chemetov, P. M. Lima

Abstract
We study solutions to a recently proposed neural field model in which dendrites are modelled as a continuum of vertical fibres stemming from a somatic layer. Since voltage propagates along the dendritic direction via a cable equation with nonlocal sources, the model features an anisotropic diffusion operator, as well as an integral term for synaptic coupling. The corresponding Cauchy problem is thus markedly different from classical neural field equations. We prove that the weak formulation of the problem admits a unique solution, with embedding estimates similar to the ones of nonlinear local reaction-diffusion equations. Our analysis relies on perturbing weak solutions to the diffusion-less problem, that is, a standard neural field, for which weak problems have not been studied to date. We find rigorous asymptotic estimates for the problem with and without diffusion, and prove that the solutions of the two models stay close, in a suitable norm, on finite time intervals. We provide numerical evidence of our perturbative results.

Introduction

The dynamics of brain activity is
intrinsically nonlocal: at microscopic level, the cellular nucleus of a neuron is in
its
soma
, whose average diameter is 20 micrometres; its
dendritic
branches
, collecting input from other neurons, have an extent variable between 100
micrometers and 1 millimetre
[
27

; its

, which
transmit activity to other neurons, have terminations as far as 1 metre away from the
soma
[

.

Coarse-grained descriptions of large-scale neuronal activity inherit this
nonlocality, and model long-distance
cortical connections via integral terms. The simplest and most popular of these
models is the
neural field equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(x,t)=-\gamma v(x,t)+\int_{\Omega}W(x,x^{\prime})S(v(x^{\prime}))dx^{\prime}+G(x,t),

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T],

(1)

\displaystyle v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),

\displaystyle x\in\Omega.

In this model,

represents the macroscopic voltage at time

and position

in the continuum cortex
\Omega

\gamma
, and is
influenced by the nonlocal integral coupling, and by the external input
G
. The
former collects contributions from the whole cortex: the
firing rate function

S
acts as a nonlinear gain, and the
synaptic kernel

W
models connections
from point
x^{\prime}
to point
x
in the cortex.

Since their introduction by Wilson and Cowan
[
48
]
, and by

[

patterns of activity (see the texbooks and monographs

[

Mathematical neuroscientists have progressively developed dynamical systems

,

,

,

Since solutions in closed form are available only in simple cases, numerical methods
have been proposed for one- and two-dimensional cortices. These algorithms must
overcome the problem of evaluating efficiently the nonlocal term, and schemes that
leverage rank-reduction

The neural field equation

Models with nonlocal terms arise also in other branches of the life sciences.
In particular, a wide range of nonlocal reaction-diffusion equations have been
studied to describe specific aspects of the evolution of biological populations
(see
[

In such models integral terms arise naturally when modelling populations competing
for global resources, speciation, and cooperation in reproduction. In this context,
a notable example is the nonlocal Fisher-KPP

Nonlocal models are also found in models of tumour growth, as a consequence of variations in

nonlocal behaviour of solutions with respect different biological parameters.
Important investigations of non-local reaction-diffusion theory can be found, for
instance, in

[

,
36
]
.

The present article concerns the theoretical treatment of one of the more recent
extensions to the neural field equations, which include a diffusive term. Neural
fields with diffusive terms have been analysed in models with delays

[
41
]
: in this context, the powerful machinery of sun-star
calculus has been exploited to tackle the diffusive term. Recently, two models
without delays have been proposed: in the first one, an anisotropic diffusion term
emerges in the voltage equation
eq.

1

[
4
]
;
in the second one, a neural field for voltage dynamics is coupled to a

[

The latter models have an interesting and unexplored mathematical structure. The
diffusion-less neural field equation
eq.

1

solutions, and the technical apparatus to analyse them relies heavily on the
compactness, boundedness, and Lipschitz properties of the integral operator. In models
with diffusion the scenario changes considerably, owing to the presence of unbounded
differential operators. The motivation behind the present article is that, in
models with diffusion and without delays, one should be able to study solutions using
classical methods for PDE analysis, and view the integral term as a nonlinear,
well-behaved perturbation of a parabolic problem.

Vanishing diffusion problems are of great importance also outside of mathematical
neuroscience, in the mathematical and physical communities. For instance, such
problems model atmospheric-oceanographic flows and landscape evolution when fluvial erosion
dominates over smoothing tendencies of the soil diffusion; further, artificial
vanishing diffusion is used for stabilization of numerical simulations. An outstanding problem in
fluid mechanics is the control of boundary layer turbulence: small forces of viscous
friction may perceptibly affect the motion of fluids, even in fluids with small
viscosity, such as water and air. Knowledge of the behavior of solutions
for small viscosities is crucial for understanding turbulence phenomena, which have
strong consequences in branches of engineering dealing with car and aircraft
productions, turbine blades, and nano-technology. We refer to

[
43
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14

for mathematical studies on
problems with vanishing viscosity.

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}v(x,0,t)=0,\qquad\partial_{\xi}v(x,L,t)=0,

\displaystyle\leq C\left(|v_{0}(x,\xi)|^{2}+\|G(x,\xi)\|_{L^{2}(0,\tau)}^{2}+\|F(u)(x,\xi)\|_{L^{2}(0,\tau)}^{2}\right).\qquad

Integrating this inequality over

, using

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\gamma\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\partial_{t}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}+K^{2}_{F})=:C_{0}^{\prime}.

Therefore setting
M_{\tau}=C_{0}^{\prime}
in (

P\colon\mathcal{B}_{\tau}\to\mathcal{B}_{\tau}
. We then show that

is a contraction
for small enough
\tau
. Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\tau}
and

be solutions

eq.

8
with right hand side

and initial data
g
defined by

respectively. Since
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
satisfy the ODE

8
, with data defined
above, the difference

satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|z|^{2}+\gamma|z|^{2}=(F(u_{1})

\displaystyle-F(u_{2}))z

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}|F(u_{1})-F(u_{2})|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}|z|^{2}\qquad\text{for a.e. $(x,\xi,t)\in\Omega_{\tau}$.}

In the last passage, we have multiplied the ODE by
z
, and used the inequality
2\alpha\beta=2(\alpha\varepsilon)(\beta/\varepsilon)\leq(\alpha\varepsilon)^{2}+(\beta/\varepsilon)^{2}
for

\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, with

\gamma=1/\varepsilon^{2}
. We now neglect the terms proportional to
\gamma z^{2}

integrate over
\Omega
with respect to
(x,\xi)

(0,\tau)
with respect to

to obtain

\|z(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{\ast}\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{1}(s)-u_{2}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds\leq C_{\ast}t\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2},\quad t\in(0,\tau),

for some constant
C_{*}
. Selecting

such that

we obtain

\|P(u_{1})-P(u_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}=\|z\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}<\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))},

hence

is a contraction and by Banach’s fixed

, that is, the solution of
eq.

10
on the
interval
[0,\tau]
. Studying the differential equation
eq.

10
on the time

[\tau,2\tau]

10

. This

theorem

.

Finally, we show uniqueness of the solution. If there exist two different solutions

and

then the difference

satisfies the problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=-\gamma v+F(v_{1})-F(v_{1})

\displaystyle\text{on $\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T]$},

\displaystyle v=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Omega\times\{t=0\}$},

Hence we have

\frac{d}{dt}|v|^{2}+\gamma|v|^{2}=(F(v_{1})-F(v_{2}))v\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}|F(v_{1})-F(v_{2})|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}|v|^{2},\qquad\text{a.e. $(x,\xi,t)\in\Omega_{T}$}.

Integrating and using

Existence of solution to the regular problem

Lemma 5
.

Fix

\displaystyle\textrm{a.e. in $(0,T)$},

(14)

\displaystyle z(0)=g,

\displaystyle\text{on $U$},

is a constant depending solely on

and on the size
L
of the
interval

The proof of existence and uniqueness of weak solutions to

6

Lemma 6
.

For any given
\nu,\gamma>0

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\partial_{t}v,\varphi\rangle+\nu(\partial_{\xi}v,\partial_{\xi}\varphi)&+\gamma(v,\varphi)\\

\displaystyle\textrm{a.e. in $(0,T)$},

(15)

\displaystyle z(0)=g

\displaystyle\text{on $\mathbb{T}^{n}\times U$},

where
\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle

In addition

\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\times H^{1}(U)))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})

\displaystyle\times H^{1}(U)^{*})

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle+\nu\|\partial_{\xi}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\gamma\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{n}\times H^{1}(U)^{\ast})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\tau})}^{2}\right),

is a constant depending solely on
T
, and on the size
L
of the interval

.

Proof.

For fixed
\varepsilon>0
, let

be the unique weak solution to

14

in the

(and

3, pages 302–303]
. Owing to the estimate in

and a function

such that

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup v

\displaystyle\text{$*$-weakly in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$,}

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\partial_{x}v_{\varepsilon},\partial_{\xi}v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup v_{\varepsilon},0,\partial_{\xi}v

\displaystyle\text{weakly in $L^{2}(\Omega_{T})$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$,}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}v

\displaystyle\text{weakly in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{*})$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$,}

respectively, and using these convergence results we obtain that the limit function

15

After these preliminaries, we can address the well-posedness of the weak problem for the

Theorem 7
(Weak solution,
\nu>0
).

Assume
Hypothesis

1
, and fix
\nu>0
,
\gamma\geq 0
,

v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
G\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
.
There exists a unique
v
satisfying the regularity condition
eq.

16
such
that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\partial_{t}v,\varphi\rangle+&\nu(\partial_{\xi}v,\partial_{\xi}\varphi)+\gamma(v,\varphi)\\
&=(F(v)+G,\varphi),\qquad\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\times H^{1}(U),\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\textrm{a.e. in $(0,T)$},

(18)

\displaystyle v(0)=v_{0},

\displaystyle\text{on $\mathbb{T}^{n}\times U$}.

In addition, there exists a constant
C_{0}
depending on
\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}
,

\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}\times U)}
, and on the constant

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\nu\|\partial_{\xi}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\gamma\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\times H^{1}(U)^{\ast})}^{2}\leq C_{0}.

Proof.

The structure of this proof resembles the one of
theorem

4
: we use a
local-in-time Banach fixed point argument, which we bootstrap to extend the
solution on

[0,T]
. Consider the set

\mathcal{B}_{\tau}=\{u\in L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))\colon\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq M_{\tau}\},

(19)

that is, the closed ball of radius
M_{\tau}
centered at the origin in
L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))
, where
\tau\in[0,T]
and
M_{\tau}
will be specified
later on. We define the operator

P\colon L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))\rightarrow L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega)),\qquad u\mapsto v,

where

is the solution to the weak problem
eq.

15
in
lemma

6

with forcing and initial conditions

f(x,\xi,t)=F(u)(x,\xi,t)+G(x,\xi,t),\qquad g(x,\xi)=v_{0}(x,\xi),\qquad(x,\xi,t)\in\Omega_{\tau}.

(20)

We claim the existence of
\tau\in[0,T]
and of
M_{\tau}>0
such that

is a contraction which in turn, by Banach’s fixed point
theorem, implies the existence on the time interval
(0,\tau)
of a solution to

eq.

18
, that is, a weak
solution to the nonlinear neural field problem with anisotropic diffusion

eq.

6
with
\nu>0
.

Firstly, we select
M_{\tau}
so that

for any

\tau\in[0,T]
.

6

is injective. The estimate

, of which we
neglect the second, third, and fourth term on the left-hand side, guarantees that

to itself.
The choice

guarantees also that

,
hence

for any
\tau\in[0,T]
.

Secondly, we show that

is a contraction, that is, we prove that, for sufficiently
small
\tau
, we can find a constant

\|P(u_{1})-P(u_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq K_{*}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}\qquad u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\tau}

To this end, fix
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\tau}

satisfies, for any test function

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}z,\varphi\rangle+\nu(\partial_{\xi}z,\partial_{\xi}\varphi)+\gamma(z,\varphi)

\displaystyle=(F(u_{1})-F(u_{2}),\varphi),

\displaystyle\textrm{ a.e. $(0,\tau)$},

\displaystyle z(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on $\Omega$}.

We choose
\varphi=z(t)
, recall that

\langle\partial_{t}z,z\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{a.e. in $(0,\tau)$},\qquad z\in C([0,\tau];L^{2}(\Omega)),

(21)

and by the result in
[
35
, page 35]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\nu\|\partial_{\xi}z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|F(u_{1})-F(u_{2})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{a.e. in $(0,\tau)$.}

Neglecting the second and third terms on the left-hand side, and using that

is Lipschitz function, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|v_{1}(t)-v_{2}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{1}(s)-u_{2}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}t\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau;L^{2}(\Omega))}\qquad\text{for all $t\in(0,\tau)$,}

for some positive constant
C_{*}
depending on

, as defined in

lemma

2
.
Therefore, picking

We have thus proven the existence of a unique solution to
eq.

18

[0,\tau]
. The function
v
is continuous on the time variable with values in

by
eq.

16
, and can be extended to
a continuous function on
[0,2\tau]
by repeating the steps above on the

eq.

18

(\tau,2\tau)

. Iterating this process we find a continuous solution on the whole

satisfies the equality

18

, and

. Choosing

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\nu\|\partial_{\xi}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\gamma\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=(F(v_{1})-F(v_{2}),v)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|F(v_{1})-F(v_{2})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{a.e. in $(0,T)$}.

Then, integrating this inequality with respect to the time variable over the

2

Finally, setting

20

5
Continuous dependence on the diffusion parameter

\nu

Now that we have studied the well-posedness of the
regular and singular problem, we can address the dependence of solutions on the
diffusion parameter
\nu
. We prove continuous dependence on
\nu
by expanding both
solutions in a suitable basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and bounding their difference.

Theorem 8
.

Assume
Hypothesis

1
, and fix
\gamma\geq 0
,

v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
G\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
. The solutions
v(t)
and

of the singular problem
eq.

10
, and of the

eq.

18
, respectively, satisfy

\|v-v_{\nu}\|_{C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\to 0\qquad\text{as $\nu\to 0$.}

(22)

Proof.

Using the integral operator
F
in
eq.

5
, throughout the proof we set

N=G+F(v),\qquad N_{\nu}=G+F(v_{\nu}),\qquad\text{a.e in $\Omega_{T}$}.

(23)

By
theorems

7
and
4
we have
v,v_{\nu}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
, uniformly on
\nu>0
. The
main hypothesis
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
lemma

2
give

\|N\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})},\|N_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}<\infty,

which, combined with Fubini’s theorem, imply

N(x,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}),N_{\nu}(x,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu})\in L^{2}(U_{T}),\qquad v_{0}(x,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu})\in L^{2}(U)\qquad\text{for a.e. $x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}$.}

(24)

To demonstrate
eq.

22
it is enough to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a function

\nu\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\|v-v_{\nu}\|^{2}_{C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all $\nu<\nu(\varepsilon)$.}

The proof proceeds in steps, and leads to the norm estimate for
v(t)-v_{\nu}(t)

through various calculations involving point-wise estimates and evaluations
for fixed
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
. We shall sometimes omit the dependence on
x
, to
simplify the notation.

Step 1: eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.
Since
v_{\nu}(t)

satisfies the system
eq.

18
, then, by the classical results
[
1
]
,
for each
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
we can expand
v_{\nu}
as a linear combination of
eigenfunctions of the following spectral problem associated to
eq.

18
,

\displaystyle\nu\psi^{\prime\prime}-\gamma\psi=\lambda\psi,\quad\text{on $U$,}\qquad\psi=0,\quad\text{on $\partial U$.}

(25)

The eigenpairs
\{(\lambda_{k},\psi_{k})\}_{k\geq 0}
of this problem are known

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\xi)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{L}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=-\gamma,

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(\xi)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\cos\biggl(\frac{k\pi\xi}{L}\biggr),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=-\gamma-\nu\biggl(\frac{k\pi\xi}{L}\biggr),

\displaystyle k\geq 1,

and the set
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthogonal basis in
L^{2}(U)
with
respect to the standard inner product and norm

(u,z)_{L^{2}(U)}=\int_{0}^{L}u(\xi)z(\xi)d\xi,\qquad\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(U)}=(u,u)_{L^{2}(U)},

respectively. Moreover,
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is also the orthogonal basis
in the space
H^{1}(U)
with respect to inner product and norm

(u,z)_{H_{1}(U)}=\int_{0}^{L}\big[\gamma u(\xi)z(\xi)+u^{\prime}(\xi)z^{\prime}(\xi)\big]d\xi,\qquad\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(U)}=(u,u)_{H^{1}(U)}.

Step 2: expansion of
v_{\nu}
.
We now seek to expand
v_{\nu}(x,\xi,t)

in the form

v_{\nu}(x,\xi,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}(x,t)\psi_{k}(\xi),

(26)

and we determine coefficients
g_{k}
so that
v_{\nu}
satisfies

eq.

18
in the sense of distributions. By the initial condition
in
eq.

18
it follows that

v_{\nu}(x,\xi,0)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}(x,0)\psi_{k}(\xi)=v_{0}(x,\xi).

(27)

On the other hand, applying Parseval’s equality and using
eq.

24
, we have

\displaystyle v_{0}(x,\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{0,k}(x)\psi_{k}(\xi),

\displaystyle v_{0,k}(x)=\bigl(v_{0}(x,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}),\psi_{k}\bigr)_{L^{2}(U)},

(28)

\displaystyle N_{\nu}(x,\xi,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{\nu,k}(x,t)\psi_{k}(\xi),

\displaystyle N_{\nu,k}(x,t)=\bigl(N_{\nu}(x,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},t),\psi_{k}\bigr)_{L^{2}(U)},

which imply

\displaystyle\|v_{0}(x)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{0,k}^{2}(x)<\infty,

\displaystyle\text{a.e. $x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}$,}

(29)

\displaystyle\|N_{\nu}(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{\nu,k}^{2}(x,t)<\infty,

\displaystyle\text{a.e. $(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\times[0,T],$}

and, by the monotone convergence theorem, we obtain

\|N_{\nu}(x)\|_{L^{2}(U_{T})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}N_{\nu,k}^{2}(x,t)dt<\infty,\qquad\text{a.e. $x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}$.}

(30)

Integrating
eqs.

29
and
30
over
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and applying again the
monotone convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{v}_{0,k}^{2}<\infty,

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{0,k}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}v_{0,k}^{2}(x)dx,

(31)

\displaystyle\|N_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\hat{N}_{\nu,k}^{2}(t)dt<\infty,

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{\nu,k}^{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}N_{\nu,k}^{2}(x,t)dx.

Substituting the expansions
eqs.

26
to
28
into system
eq.

18
, we
conclude that for each

, the
coefficients

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{k}(x,t)=\lambda_{k}g_{k}(x,t)+N_{\nu}(x,t),

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\times[0,T],

(32)

\displaystyle g_{k}(x,0)=v_{0,k}(x),

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{T}^{n},

and are therefore given by

g_{k}(x,t)=v_{0,k}(x)\exp\bigl(\lambda_{k}t\bigr)+\int_{0}^{t}N_{\nu,k}(x,s)\exp\bigl(\lambda_{k}(t-s)\bigr)ds,

such that

. This, together with

.

v_{\nu}(x,\xi,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}(\xi)\Bigl[v_{0,k}(x)\exp\left(\lambda_{k}t\right)+\int_{0}^{t}N_{\nu,k}(x,s)\exp\left(\lambda_{k}(t-s)\right)ds\Bigr].

(34)

Step 3: expansion of
v

The next step is to expand the solution
v

singular neural field system

10

(35)

By the regularity (
24
) we have

N(x,\xi,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{k}(x,t)\psi_{k}(\xi),\qquad N_{k}(x,t)=\bigl(N(x,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},t),\psi_{k}\bigr)_{L^{2}(U)},

(36)

and by Parseval’s equality and the monotone convergence theorem

\displaystyle\|N(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{k}^{2}(x,t)<\infty,

\displaystyle\text{a.e.$(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\times[0,T]$},

(37)

\displaystyle\|N(x)\|_{L^{2}(U_{T})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}N_{k}^{2}(x,t)dt,

\displaystyle\text{a.e. $x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}$},

\displaystyle\|N\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\hat{N}_{k}^{2}(t)dt<\infty,

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{k}^{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}N_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx

Substituting
eqs.

28
and
36
into the right-hand side of
eq.

35
we obtain

v(x,\xi,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}(\xi)\biggl[v_{0,k}(x)e^{-\gamma t}+\int_{0}^{t}N_{k}(x,s)e^{-\gamma(t-s)}ds\biggr].

(38)

Step 4: expanding
v-v_{\nu}
in sums over functions
A_{k}
and

B_{k}
.
Using the previous
two steps, we will now derive an priori estimate for the difference of
v
and

v_{\nu}
in the norm of the Banach space
L^{2}(U)
.

Henceforth we write

-\mu_{k}(\nu)=-\nu(k\pi/L)^{2}=\lambda_{k}(\nu,\gamma)+\gamma
, where

\lambda_{k}
are the eigenvalues
eq.

25
, and omit the dependence on

\nu
when possible.

Combining these definitions
with
eqs.

38
,
26
and
33
we obtain

\displaystyle v(x,\xi,t)-v_{\nu}(x,\xi,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}(\xi)\biggl[

\displaystyle v_{0,k}(x)(1-e^{-\mu_{k}t})e^{-\gamma t}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(N_{k}(x,s)-N_{\nu,k}(x,s)e^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}\bigr)e^{-\gamma(t-s)}ds\biggr].

Since

is the orthonormal basis in

taking the

\|v(x,t)-v_{\nu}(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(A_{k}+B_{k})^{2}\leq 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{k}^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B_{k}^{2},

(39)

where

\displaystyle A_{k}(x,t)=v_{0,k}(x)(1-e^{-\mu_{k}t})e^{-\gamma t},

(40)

\displaystyle B_{k}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[N_{k}(x,s)-N_{\nu,k}(x,s)e^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}\bigr]e^{-\gamma(t-s)}ds,

and a further integration over
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
gives

\|v(t)-v_{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}A_{k}^{2}dx+2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}B_{k}^{2}dx.

(41)

Step 5: bounding the sum in
A^{2}_{k}
in the expansion
eq.

41
.
By the first equality in
eq.

31
, we have that for any
\varepsilon>0
there
exists
k_{1}(\varepsilon)>0,
such that

and

, hence we estimate

\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}A_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx<\frac{\varepsilon}{4D},\qquad t\in[0,T].

(42)

Moreover, for almost any

, and all

we have

\sum_{k=0}^{k_{1}-1}A_{k}^{2}(x,t)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{k_{1}-1}|v_{0,k}(x)|^{2}(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{1}}t})^{2}e^{-2\gamma t}\leq\|v_{0}(x)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{1}}T})^{2},

\sum_{k=0}^{k_{1}-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}A_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx\leq\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{1}}T})^{2},\qquad t\in[0,T]

(43)

Recalling the dependence of
\mu_{k}(\nu)
, and using the continuity and monotonicity of
the exponential function, we obtain that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

\nu_{1}(\varepsilon)
such that

(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{1}}(\nu)T})^{2}=(1-e^{-\nu(k_{1}\pi/L)^{2}T})^{2}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4\|v_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}D}\qquad\text{for all $\nu<\nu_{1}(\varepsilon)$.}

Using
eqs.

42
and
43
, we deduce that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists

\nu_{1}(\varepsilon)
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}A_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx<\frac{\varepsilon}{2D},\qquad\text{for all $\nu<\nu_{1}(\varepsilon)$ and $t\in[0,T]$,}

(44)

that is, we have found a bound for the sum in the terms

in
eq.

41
.

Step 6: bounding the sum in
B^{2}_{k}
in the expansion
eq.

41
.

With this goal,
we rewrite the

in

40
in the form

\displaystyle B_{k}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}N_{k}(x,s)(1-e^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)})e^{-\gamma(t-s)}ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[N_{k}(x,s)-N_{\nu,k}(x,s)\bigr]e^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}e^{-\gamma(t-s)}ds,

Recalling that
(\alpha+\beta)^{2}\leq 2\alpha^{2}+2\beta^{2}
, it follows that

B^{2}_{k}(x,t)=2T(1-e^{-\mu_{k}T})^{2}\int_{0}^{t}N^{2}_{k}(x,s)ds+2T\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[N_{k}(x,s)-N_{\nu,k}(x,s)\bigr]ds.

(45)

From the third inequality in
eq.

37
we deduce that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there
exists an integer
k_{2}=k_{2}(\varepsilon)
such that

\sum_{k=k_{2}}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\hat{N}_{k}^{2}(s)ds<\frac{\varepsilon}{4D}(2T)^{-1},

hence, integrating

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}B_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx

\displaystyle\leq 2T\Biggl(\sum_{k=k_{2}}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\hat{N}_{k}^{2}(s)ds\Biggr)

\displaystyle+2T(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{2}}T})^{2}\Biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{k_{2}-1}\int_{0}^{T}\hat{N}_{k}^{2}(s)ds\Biggr)

\displaystyle+2T\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\bigl|N_{k}(x,s)-N_{\nu,k}(x,s)\big|^{2}dxds.

Using the definition of
N
in
eq.

23
, and the Lipschitzianity of the function
F
, we estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}B_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4D}

\displaystyle+2T(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{2}(\varepsilon)}(\nu)T})^{2}\|G+F(v)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle+2TK_{F}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)-u_{\nu}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}ds.

We choose
\nu_{2}(\varepsilon)
such that

(1-e^{-\mu_{k_{2}(\varepsilon)}(\nu)T})^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4D}\left(2T\|G+F(v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\right)^{-1},\qquad\text{for all $\nu<\nu_{2}(\varepsilon)$},

and we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}B_{k}^{2}(x,t)dx\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2D}

\displaystyle+2TK_{F}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)-u_{\nu}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}ds.

(46)

Step 7: final estimate.

We set
\nu(\varepsilon)=\min(\nu_{1}(\varepsilon),\nu_{2}(\varepsilon))
, and combine

39
,
44
and
46

\|v(t)-v_{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{D}+2TK_{F}\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)-v_{\nu}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds,\qquad\text{for all $\nu<\nu(\varepsilon)$.}

Applying Gronwall’s inequality, recalling that

, we find

\|v(t)-v_{\nu}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{e^{2T^{2}K_{F}}}{D}\varepsilon=\varepsilon,\qquad\text{for all $\nu<\nu(\varepsilon)$ and $t\in[0,T]$},

6
Convergence rate of the sequence
v_{\nu}-v
to
0

The statement below shows that if, in addition to the hypotheses of
theorem

8
, one adds regularity assumptions to the initial data and external input,
then it is possible to prove
v_{\nu}-v
is
O(\nu^{1/2})
.

Theorem 9
.

Assume
Hypothesis

1
, and fix
\gamma\geq 0
,
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
G\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
. If, in addition,
\partial_{\xi}v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\partial_{\xi}G\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
, then the
solutions
v
and
v_{\nu}
to
eqs.

10
and
18
, respectively, satisfy

\|v_{\nu}-v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq Ce^{DT}\nu^{1/2},

(47)

where positive constants
C
and
D
depend on
\gamma
, the constant
K_{F}
in

lemma

2
, and the norms
\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
\|\partial_{\xi}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}
,
and
\|\partial_{\xi}G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}
.

Proof.

We initially find an estimate for
v-v_{\nu}
in a different way to what was
done in the proof of
theorem

8
. This new derivation will reveal that
estimate
eq.

47
is possible if we gain control over
\partial_{\xi}v
,
which we then proceed to bound.

The difference
w=v_{\nu}-v
satisfies, for any test function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\partial_{t}w,\varphi\rangle&+\nu(\partial_{\xi}v_{\nu},\partial_{\xi}\varphi)\\
&+\gamma(w,\varphi)=(F(v_{\nu})-F(v),\varphi),\quad\varphi,\partial_{\xi}\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega),\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\text{a.e. in $(0,T)$}

\displaystyle w=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\Omega\times\{t=0\}$ }.

Choosing the test function
\varphi=w
gives, for almost any in
t\in(0,T)
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\nu\|\partial_{\xi}v_{\nu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\bigl(F(v_{\nu})-F(v),w\bigr)+\nu(\partial_{\xi}v_{\nu},\partial_{\xi}v)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\|F(v_{\nu})-F(v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|\partial_{\xi}v_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|\partial_{\xi}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Disregarding the terms in
\|\partial_{\xi}v_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, integrating
with respect to the time variable over the interval
(0,t)
, and using the
Lipschitzianity of
F
from
lemma

2
, we obtain

\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\gamma}{2}\|w(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds\\
\leq C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|w(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds+\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{\xi}v(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds,

(48)

for all
t\in(0,T)
, where
C_{1}=2K_{F}/\gamma
. Inspecting the previous
inequality we note that a bound on
\|\partial_{\xi}v(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is
necessary to find an estimate for
\|w(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. We put the inequality
above aside, and we devote the rest of the proof to finding the bound on
\|\partial_{\xi}v(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

We begin by extending the singular neural field solution
v
in the spatial
variable
\xi
. For fixed
\delta>0
, we set

\hat{U}=(-\delta,L+\delta)\supset U,\qquad\hat{\Omega}=\mathbb{T}^{n}\times\hat{U}\supset\Omega,\qquad\hat{\Omega}_{T}=\hat{\Omega}\times[0,T]\supset\Omega_{T}.

Following the approach of
[
21
, Theorem 1, p. 268]
,
we deduce that for any
\delta>0
there exist functions
\hat{v}_{0}
and

\hat{G}
such that

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{0}\in L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}),

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}\hat{v}_{0}\in L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}),

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{0}=v_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle\hat{G}\in L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}_{T}),

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}\hat{G}\in L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}_{T}),

\displaystyle\hat{G}=G

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{T}$}.

We can now use Theorem
4
to show there exists a unique weak solution

\hat{v}
to the following problem, on the extended domain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{v}=-\gamma\hat{v}+F(\hat{v})+\hat{G}

\displaystyle\text{a.e. in $\hat{\Omega}_{T}$},

\displaystyle\hat{v}=\hat{v}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{a.e. in $\hat{\Omega}\times\{t=0\}$},

such that

\hat{v}=v\quad\text{a.e. in $\Omega_{T}$,}\qquad\|\hat{v}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\gamma\|\hat{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C_{2},

(49)

for some positive constant
C_{2}
which depends only on

\|\hat{v}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}
,
\|\hat{G}\|_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}_{T})}
and
K_{F}
defined in
lemma

2
.

We now introduce the following notation: for a function
\hat{\psi}\colon\hat{\Omega}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, and a real number
h
with
|h|<\delta
, we introduce the
function

\hat{\psi}_{h}\colon\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x,\xi,t)\mapsto\hat{\psi}(x,\xi+h,t)-\hat{\psi}(x,\xi,t).

The function
\hat{v}_{h}
satisfies the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{v}_{h}=-\gamma\hat{v}_{h}+\bigl[F(\hat{v}({\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}+h,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}))-F(\hat{v})\bigr]+\hat{G}_{h}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega_{T}$},

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{h}=(\hat{v}_{0})_{h}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Omega\times\{t=0\}$}.

Multiplying by
\hat{v}_{h}
, and using Young’s inequality for products, we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|\hat{v}_{h}|^{2}+\gamma|\hat{v}_{h}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl([F(\hat{v}({\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}+h,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}))-F(\hat{v})]+\hat{G}_{h}\bigr)\hat{v}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\Bigl(|F(\hat{v}({\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}+h,{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu}))-F(\hat{v})|^{2}+|\hat{G}_{h}|^{2}\Bigr)+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\hat{v}_{h}|^{2}\qquad\text{a.e. in $\Omega_{T}$}

Integrating the last deduced inequality over
\Omega\times(0,t)
using

lemma

2
we obtain

\|\hat{v}_{h}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{3}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|\hat{v}_{h}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\hat{G}_{h}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)ds+\|(\hat{v}_{0})_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad t\in(0,T),

where
C_{3}
depends on
\gamma
and
K_{F}
. A further application of
Gronwall’s inequality gives

\|\hat{v}_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq e^{C_{3}T}C_{4}\big(\|(\hat{v}_{0})_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\hat{G}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\big),

where the constant
C_{4}
depends on
T
,
\gamma
, and
K_{F}
.

Applying
[
1
, Theorem 4, page 120]
and the first identity of

eq.

49
, we conclude that

\partial_{\xi}v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

such that

\|\partial_{\xi}v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq e^{C_{3}T}C_{4}\big(\|\partial_{\xi}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\xi}G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}),

(50)

with
C_{4}
dependent on
T
,
\gamma
, and
K_{F}
.

Now that we have bounded
\|\partial_{\xi}v(t)\|
uniformly in
t
, we return to

eq.

48
. Applying Gronwall’s lemma to the inequality obtained from

eq.

48
upon disregarding the second term on the left-hand side, and using

50
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|w(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\nu\frac{e^{C_{1}T}}{2}\|\partial_{\xi}v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\nu\frac{e^{C_{1}T}e^{C_{3}T}}{2}C_{4}\big(\|\partial_{\xi}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\xi}G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}),

Numerical results

In this section we present numerical results in support of the theory presented in
the past sections. We simulate system

2
on a rescaled domain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v(x,\xi,t)=(-\gamma+\nu\partial_{\xi}^{2})v(x,\xi,t)&+G(x,\xi,t)\\
&+F(v({\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},{\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu},t))(x,\xi),\end{aligned}

\displaystyle(x,\xi,t)\in\Omega_{T},

(51)

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}v(x,-L_{\xi},t)=0,\qquad\partial_{\xi}v(x,L_{\xi},t)=0,

\displaystyle(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,T],

\displaystyle v(x,\xi,0)=v_{0}(x,\xi)

\displaystyle(x,\xi)\in\Omega,

with the functional and parameter choices similar to

4
]
. In

\displaystyle\xi_{0},\kappa\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle S(u,\mu,\theta)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\mu(u-\theta))},

\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\theta\in\mathbb{R},

and consider null external input,

, and a localised initial condition given by

\alpha(-x)\delta_{\sigma}(\xi)&\text{if $x\leq 0$,}\\
\end{cases}\qquad\alpha(x)=1-S(x,\rho,x_{0}),\qquad\rho,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

(54)

We simulate the system above using the implicit-explicit time stepper derived in

[
4
]
, with time step size

, and number of spatial grid

,

x_{0}
and
\sigma
in the

and
\xi

\xi=\xi_{0}
.

We first consider the system in the absence of diffusion,
\nu=0
, as shown in

fig.

2
(a). At

the activity is localised with two peaks, the first

\xi=0
, and the other at

, while the latter is forming spontaneously, owing to nonlinear

2
(b)-(c)) the profiles are merged by diffusion, and the final

8
Conclusions

In this paper we studied the well-posedness and regularity of solutions to neural
field models with dendrites. These models are strongly anisotropic, as diffusion
acts only on one of the spatial direction (the dendritic direction). The particular
laminar structure of the model allows one to characterise solutions as perturbations
to a classical (diffusion-less) neural field models. This structure has already been
exploited numerically in
[
4
]
, but was not supported by analytical
investigations.

Tackling this problem required studying perturbed weak solutions to the problem,
and this opens up the possibility of devising schemes in full generality. The
heuristic schemes presented in
[
4
]
relied on collocation on a
simple domain. The weak formulations studied here are the starting point for the
formulation of finite-element schemes, in which the domain
\Omega
is arbitrary, an
avenue that will be explored in future work.

Furthermore, our study shows that solutions to the dendritic neural fields can be
approximated, in the limit of small diffusion, with classical neural fields, for
which finite-element and spectral methods have recently been developed. We have also
provided numerical evidence that this perturbation results hold on
O(1)
time
horizons, in spite of the exponentially growing constants predicted by

theorem

9
.

This suggests that it should be possible to study splitting schemes in which, at each time
interval, a predictor step is carried out using standard neural field models, while
corrections are made to solve the original problem with diffusion. The latter problem
is relatively cheap to carry out, in view of the anisotropic diffusion operator,
which acts solely along
\xi
. It will be of particular interest to try this
splitting strategy on problems for which diffusion is not necessarily small, and
study the corresponding accuracy of the schemes.
