Abstract
. It is broadly known that any parallelepiped tiles space by translating copies of itself along its edges. In earlier work relating to higher-dimensional sandpile groups, the second author discovered a novel construction which fragments the parallelepiped into a collection of smaller tiles. These tiles fill space with the same symmetry as the larger parallelepiped. Their volumes are equal to the components of the multi-row Laplace determinant expansion, so this construction only works when all of these signs are non-negative (or non-positive). In this work, we extend the construction to work for all parallelepipeds, without requiring the non-negative condition. This naturally gives tiles with negative volume, which we understand to mean canceling out tiles with positive volume. In fact, with this cancellation, we prove that every point in space is contained in exactly one more tile with positive volume than tile with negative volume. This is a natural definition for a signed tiling. Our main technique is to show that the net number of signed tiles doesn’t change as a point moves through space. This is a relatively indirect proof method, and the underlying structure of these tilings remains mysterious.

Definition 3.1
.

Let

For each

\vskip 4.26773pt|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}

3.2.
Signed Tiling Construction

To form a signed tiling, we parameterize tiles formed by translating the fundamental parallelepiped of fragment matrices by integer combinations of the columns of
M
.

Definition 3.4
.

Recall Definitions
2.1
and
3.1
.
For any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
and
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
, the
tile
parameterized by the pair
(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
is defined as

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma):={\Pi}(S_{\sigma}(M))+M\mathbf{z}.

Note that since
{\Pi}(S_{\sigma}(M))
depends on
\mathbf{w}
, the tile
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
will depend on
\mathbf{w}
as well. Nevertheless, the precise choice of
\mathbf{w}
is not important for our results as long as it remains fixed (and sufficiently generic, see Section
2.2
). Also, note that we usually think of a tile
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
as a polytope made up of a collection of points, not the points themselves. With this perspective in mind, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.5
.

Consider the sets of tiles

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}^{+}(M):

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\left(\biguplus_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~\det(S_{\sigma}(M))>0}\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)\right),

\displaystyle\text{and }{\mathbf{T}}^{-}(M):

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\left(\biguplus_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~\det(S_{\sigma}(M))<0}\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)\right).

The set
{\mathbf{T}}^{+}(M)
is the set of
positive tiles
, and
{\mathbf{T}}^{-}(M)
is the set of
negative tiles
.
We also write
{\mathbf{T}}(M):={\mathbf{T}}^{+}(M)\biguplus{\mathbf{T}}^{-}(M)
.
Note that we don’t include the tiles where
\det(S_{\sigma}(M))=0
, but in this case,
S_{\sigma}(M)
is not invertible, and
{\Pi}(S_{\sigma}(M))
is empty.

Remark 3.6
.

In
[

]
and
[

]
, the word

is used differently, instead referring to the union of a particular collection of parallelepipeds.

Definition

Theorem 3.7
.

The function

, defined by

is constant with value
(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M))
.

For the convenience of talking about tilings, we don’t discuss this generalization.

in Section

4.
Average Weight of the Tiling

In this short section, we show by determinant computations that the average value of
f(\mathbf{p})
is
(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M))
.
To state this idea precisely, we use integrals and ideas from introductory calculus.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
, the following equality holds:

\det(S_{\sigma}(M))=\det({C}_{\sigma}(M))\cdot\det(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M))\cdot\sgn(\sigma,\widehat{\sigma}).

The next lemma is a direct application of the Laplace determinant expansion formula.
Note that the
(-1)^{k}
term is included because the bottom
k
rows of
M
are given by
-\overline{C}_{[r+k]}(M)
instead of
\overline{C}_{[r+k]}(M)
.

Lemma 4.2

(Multiple Row Laplace Expansion)
.

Using the notation above, we have the following chain of equalities.

(-1)^{k}\det(M)=\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}}\det(S_{\sigma}(M))=\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}}\det(C_{\sigma}(M))\det(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M))\sgn(\sigma,\widehat{\sigma}).

Now, we are ready for the main result of the section.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f
be the function defined in Theorem
3.7
. Then,

\int_{{\Pi}(M)}f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=(-1)^{k}\det(M).

Proof.

First, note that for any
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
, there is precisely one
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
such that
\mathbf{p}-M\mathbf{z}\in{\Pi}(M)
.
This is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.3
.
Referring to Definition
3.4
, for a point
\mathbf{p}\in{\Pi}(S_{\sigma}(M))
, there is exactly one choice of
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
so that the intersection of
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
and
{\Pi}(M)
contains
\mathbf{p}
.
Therefore, all the intersections of the various
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
with
{\Pi}(M)
contains equivalent points to a single copy,
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{0},\sigma)
.
This gives us the following equality:

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r+k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{0},\sigma)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

Directly applying definitions gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r+k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{0},\sigma)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r+k}}\mathbbm{1}_{S_{\sigma}(M)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=|\det(S_{\sigma}(M))|.

Combining the definition of
f
, a carefully considered change to the order of summation and integration, the definitions of
\mathbf{T}^{+}(M)
and
\mathbf{T}^{-}(M)
, and the above equalities, it follows that:

\displaystyle\int_{{\Pi}(M)}f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\sum_{T\in\mathbf{T}^{+}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{T}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\sum_{T\in\mathbf{T}^{-}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{T}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathbf{T}^{+}(M)}\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{T}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\sum_{T\in\mathbf{T}^{-}(M)}\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{T}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~\det(S_{\sigma}(M))>0,~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~\det(S_{\sigma}(M))<0,~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~\det(S_{\sigma}(M))>0}|\det(S_{\sigma}(M))|-\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~\det(S_{\sigma}(M))<0}|\det(S_{\sigma}(M))|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}}\det(S_{\sigma}(M)).

The result now follows directly from Lemma
4.2
.
∎

From here, we can directly compute the average value of
f
over the domain
{\Pi}(M)
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f
be the function defined in Theorem
3.7
. Then,

\left(\int_{{\Pi}(M)}f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)/\left(\int_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{{\Pi}(M)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)=(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M)).

It is immediate from the definition of
\mathbf{T}(M)
that
f(\mathbf{p})=f(\mathbf{p}+M\mathbf{z})
for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
.
In particular, Corollary
4.4
also holds over any domain that is a union of translates of
{\Pi}(M)
by integer linear combinations of the column of
M
.
Since these translates cover
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
by Lemma
2.3
, we say colloquially that Corollary
4.4
implies that
(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M))
is the “average value” of
f
over
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
.
In particular, once we show in Section
6
that
f
is constant, this will imply that its value is
(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M))
.

5.
Crossing Boundaries of Tiles

The goal of this section is to prove the value of the function
f
from Theorem
3.7
does not change when leaving one tile and entering another.
In Section
6
, we will use this idea to show that
f
is constant over all of
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
.
Most of this section is working towards a sub-goal, which is to pair up collections of facets that have opposite signs in a sense.
This pairing is illustrated in Figure
3
.

Recall that
f
expresses the sum of indicator functions of a collection of half-open parallelepipeds.
Each of these indicator functions is constant on all of
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
, except on the boundary of the associated tile.
Thus, in order to prove that
f
is constant, it is useful to consider these boundary points, which lie in the facets of the tile.

5.1.
Initial Definitions and Lemmas

First, we give names for the
facets
of a half-open parallelepiped
\Pi(N)
(i.e., the maximal faces of the closure of
\Pi(N)
).
We only give a definition for the facets of parallelepipeds under consideration in this work (namely elements of
\mathbf{T}(M)
), but the definition is easily generalized.

Definition 5.1
.

Fix
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
and
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
. There are
2(r+k)
facets of the tile
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
, which come in pairs.
We first define the “lower” facet of each pair, then the “upper” facet based upon the “lower”.
For
j\in[r+k]
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0):

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[r+k]\setminus\{j\}}\left\{x_{i}S_{\sigma}(M)_{i}:\begin{array}[]{l}0<x_{i}\leq 1\text{ if $(S_{\sigma}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w})_{i}<0$}\\
0\leq x_{i}<1\text{ if $(S_{\sigma}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w})_{i}>0$}\end{array}\right\}+M\mathbf{z},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)+S_{\sigma}(M)_{j}.

We conflate these two definitions into a single parametrization,
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
.
Characterizing the parameters,
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
is a translational parameter,
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
determines the fragment,
j\in[r+k]
indexes which facet of
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
is considered and indicates which vector of
S_{\sigma}(M)
is excluded, and
s\in\{0,1\}
is the choice between “upper” and “lower” facet.
When
S_{\sigma}(M)
is not invertible, let
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s):=\emptyset
.

While definitionally,
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
, we conflate this notion with another, where
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
is a single discrete object, the combinatorial facet of some parallelepiped.
We switch between these perspectives, depending on the context.

In practice, we find that it was more useful to work with a slight variant of
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
, which we define below.

Definition 5.2
.

Fix
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
.
For
j\in[r+k]
and
s\in\{0,1\}
, let

\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z}-s\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma,j,s)&\text{ if $j\in\sigma$,}\\
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z}+s\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma,j,s)&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is the
j^{th}
standard basis vector. Note that
\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)
for any choice of
\mathbf{z}
,
\sigma
, and
j
.

Definition
5.2
is a bit less natural than Definition
5.1
since
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
is always a facet of
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
, while
\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
is a facet of
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
if
s=0
and a facet of
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z}\pm\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma)
if
s=1
.
However, this groups the facets into more convenient collections.
We will show in Section
5.2
the reasons we made this change, and the convenience that arises.

Recall that each fragment matrix
S_{\sigma}(M)
is essentially a block matrix, where the first
r
coordinates of each vector are either those from
M
or
0
, and the last
k
coordinates are either the negatives of
M
or
0
.
Because of this, it will be useful for us to consider the following projection maps.

Definition 5.3
.

We write
p_{r}
for the map from
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
which projects into the first
r
coordinates and
\widehat{p}_{k}
for the map from
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
which projects to the last
k
coordinates. In particular, for any
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
, we have

\mathbf{p}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{r}(\mathbf{p})\\
\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathbf{p})\end{bmatrix}.

Recall that we define
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}:=p_{r}(\mathbf{w})
and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}:=\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathbf{w})
.

The following lemma is immediate from the structure of
M
and
S_{\sigma}(M)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
and
j\in[r+k]
. Then
p_{r}(M_{j})={\mathbf{c}}_{j}
,
\widehat{p}_{k}(M_{j})=-\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{j}
,

p_{r}(S_{\sigma}(M)_{j})=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{c}}_{j}&\text{if $j\in\sigma$,}\\
\mathbf{0}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\widehat{p}_{k}(S_{\sigma}(M)_{j})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}&\text{if $j\in\sigma$,}\\
\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{j}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The following two lemmas allow us to describe any facet
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
in terms of the columns of
C_{\sigma}(M)
and
\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
and
j\in[r+k]
such that
S_{\sigma}(M)
is invertible. We have the following equalities,

p_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))=\sum_{i\in\sigma\setminus j}\left\{x_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}:\begin{array}[]{l}0<x_{i}\leq 1\text{ if $(C_{\sigma}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}<0$}\\
0\leq x_{i}<1\text{ if $(C_{\sigma}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}>0$}\end{array}\right\}+p_{r}(M\mathbf{z}).

\widehat{p}_{k}(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))=\sum_{i\in\widehat{\sigma}\setminus j}\left\{x_{i}\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{i}:\begin{array}[]{l}0<x_{i}\leq 1\text{ if $(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime})_{\widehat{\sigma}^{-1}(i)}<0$}\\
0\leq x_{i}<1\text{ if $(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime})_{\widehat{\sigma}^{-1}(i)}>0$}\end{array}\right\}+\widehat{p}_{k}(M\mathbf{z}).

Proof.

First, note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)={\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)
, so we can focus on Definition
5.1
. We claim that for any
i\in[r+k]
, the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle(S_{\sigma}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w})_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(C_{\sigma}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}&\text{ if $i\in\sigma$, and}\\
(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M)^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime})_{\widehat{\sigma}^{-1}(i)}&\text{ if $i\in\widehat{\sigma}$.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle p_{r}(x_{i}S_{\sigma}(M)_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}x_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}&\text{ if $i\in\sigma$, and}\\
\mathbf{0}&\text{ if $i\in\widehat{\sigma}$.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{k}(x_{i}S_{\sigma}(M)_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}&\text{ if $i\in\sigma$, and}\\
x_{i}\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{i}&\text{ if $i\in\widehat{\sigma}$.}\end{cases}

All three parts of this claim follow from the block structure of
S_{\sigma}(M)
, and the bottom two equalities are also simple corollaries of Lemma
5.4
.
From here, the result follows from Definition
5.1
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
and
j\in[r+k]
.

p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1))=\begin{cases}p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))&\text{if $j\in\sigma$,}\\
p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))+\mathbf{c}_{j}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1))=\begin{cases}\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))+\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{j}&\text{if $j\in\sigma$,}\\
\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

First, suppose that
j\in\sigma
. Then, by combining Definitions
5.1
and
5.2
, and applying Lemma
5.4
, we find that

\displaystyle p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1))

\displaystyle=p_{r}(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma,j,0)+S_{\sigma}(M)_{j})

\displaystyle=p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)-M\mathbf{e}_{j}+S_{\sigma}(M)_{j})

\displaystyle=p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))-p_{r}(M_{j})+p_{r}(S_{\sigma}(M)_{j})

\displaystyle=p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))-\mathbf{c}_{j}+\mathbf{c}_{j}

\displaystyle=p_{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1))

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))-\widehat{p}_{k}(M_{j})+\widehat{p}_{k}(S_{\sigma}(M)_{j})

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))-(-\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{j})+\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}_{k}(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0))+\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{j}.

The case where
j\not\in\sigma
is analogous.
∎

5.2.
Finding Structure by Grouping Facets

We were not able to prove Theorem
3.7
by considering individual facets.
Nevertheless, when collections of facets are grouped in a particular way, a useful structure becomes apparent.
In particular, one can use Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
to show that each collection defined below is made up of facets contained within the same hyperplane.

Definition 5.7
.

Fix
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
and
\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1}
. We define the collection of facets

{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}:=\biguplus_{j\in\widehat{\tau}}\left(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,1)\right).

Similarly, fix
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
and
\gamma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r+1}
. We define the collection of facets

{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}:=\biguplus_{j\in\gamma}\left(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\gamma\setminus j,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\gamma\setminus j,j,1)\right).

The following proposition and corollary show that the collections of
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
and
{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}
partition the set of facets.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)
be a facet of the tile
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)\in\mathbf{T}(M)
.

If
j\in\sigma
, then

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)=\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z}+s\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma,j,s)\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z}+s\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma\setminus j)}(M)}.

If
j\not\in\sigma
, then

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s)=\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z}-s\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma,j,s)\in{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z}-s\mathbf{e}_{j},\sigma\cup j)}(M)}.

Proof.

The first equality is immediate from Definition
5.2
, while the inclusion follows from Definition
5.7
.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

Given the definitions above, we find the following equality:

\biguplus_{\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1},~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}\hskip 10.0pt{\biguplus}\hskip 10.0pt\biguplus_{\gamma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r+1},~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}=

\biguplus_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k},~\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~j\in[r+k],~s\in\{0,1\}}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,s).

Proof.

First note that for any fixed
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
, we get the following chain of equalities.

\displaystyle\biguplus_{\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1}}{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1},~j\in\widehat{\tau}}\left(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,1)\right)

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~j\in\sigma}\left(\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1)\right)

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~j\in\sigma}\left(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z}-{\mathbf{e}}_{j},\sigma,j,1)\right).

Since
\mathbf{z}\to\mathbf{z}+{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
gives a bijection on
\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
for every
j\in[r+k]
, taking the union over every
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
gives the following equality:

\displaystyle\biguplus_{\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1},~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}=\biguplus_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~j\in\sigma,~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\left(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1)\right).

An analogous calculation shows that:

\displaystyle\biguplus_{\gamma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r+1},~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}=\biguplus_{\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r},~j\in\widehat{\sigma},~\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\left(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,0)\uplus\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z},\sigma,j,1)\right).

The result follows from combining these two equalities.
∎

Corollary
5.9
enables us to focus on sets of the form
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
and
{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}
, while still retaining the full character of the problem.

One important property of these sets is the following result.

Proposition 5.10
.

Fix any
\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
. The relative interior of
p_{r}(F)
is the same for all
F\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
.
In particular, this region is given by the open
(r-1)
-dimensional parallelepiped

\sum_{i\in\tau}\left\{x_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}:0<x_{i}<1\right\}+p_{r}(M\mathbf{z}).

Similarly, fix any
\gamma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r+1}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
. The relative interior of
\widehat{p}_{k}(F)
is the same for all
F\in{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}
. In particular, this region is given by the open
(k-1)
-dimensional parallelepiped

\sum_{i\in\widehat{\gamma}}\left\{x_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}:0<x_{i}<1\right\}+\widehat{p}_{k}(M\mathbf{z}).

Proof.

This follows from directly from Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
. Notice that the expression in Lemma
5.5
is simpler when we are only concerned with the relative interior of
p_{r}(F)
.
∎

For the remainder of this section, we will focus primarily on the
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
setting, but analogous statements about
{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}
hold as well.

5.3.
“Up” Facets and “Down” Facets

For this and the following subsection, we will look closer at the facets which make up
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
for a specific choice of
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}
and
\tau\in\binom{[r+k]}{r-1}
. We begin by defining two functions,
\wsgn
and
\tsgn
, which both map from
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}\to\pm 1
.

First, we define
\wsgn
, which keeps track of whether
F
is “below” or “above” its associated tile in the
\mathbf{w}
direction.

Definition 5.11
.

Suppose that
F=\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,s)\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
and choose
\mathbf{p}
to be an arbitrary point on the interior of
F
.
Let
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}-M(\mathbf{z}-s\mathbf{e}_{j})
, which is a point on the boundary of
{\Pi}(S_{\tau\cup j}(M))
.
Then,

\wsgn(F):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathbf{q}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}\in{\Pi}(S_{\tau\cup j}(M))$ for all sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$,}\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Next, we define
\tsgn
, which keeps track of whether
F
is a facet of a “positive tile” or a “negative tile”.

Definition 5.12
.

Suppose that
F=\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,s)\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
. Then,

\tsgn(F):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\det(S_{\tau\cup j}(M))>0$,}\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Recall that
F=\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}(\mathbf{z},\tau\cup j,j,s)
is a facet of the parallelepiped
T
, where

T=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z}+s\mathbf{e}_{j},\tau\cup j)={\Pi}(S_{\tau\cup j}(M))+M(\mathbf{z}+s\mathbf{e}_{j}).

It follows from Definition
5.11
, that a particle crossing
F
in the direction
\mathbf{w}
will enter
T
if
\wsgn(F)=1
and will exit
T
if
\wsgn(F)=-1
.

Furthermore, recall that
T\in\mathbf{T}^{+}(M)
if
\det(S_{\tau\cup j})(M)>0
and
T\in\mathbf{T}^{-}(M)
if
\det(S_{\tau\cup j}(M))<0
. It follows from Definition
5.12
that
T\in\mathbf{T}^{+}(M)
if
\tsgn(F)=1
and
T\in\mathbf{T}^{-}(M)
if
\tsgn(F)=-1
.

Next, consider the product
\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F)
. If
\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F)=1
, then a particle crossing
F
if the direction
\mathbf{w}
either
enters
a
positive
tile or
exits
a
negative
tile. Alternatively, if
\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F)=-1
, then the particle either
enters
a
negative
tile or
exits
a
positive
tile.

Note that even through we only defined
\wsgn(F)
and
\tsgn(F)
for
F\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
, analogous definitions hold for
F\in{\overline{\mathbf{F}}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\gamma)}(M)}
. See the end of this section for a bit more discussion about this generalization.

Recall the function
f
defined in Theorem
3.7
. The product
\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F)
indicates the change in contribution to
f
coming from
T
when a particle crosses
F
in the
\mathbf{w}
direction. More precisely, we have the following result.

Lemma 5.13
.

Fix
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that the line segment between
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}
intersected with the set of all facets of tiles in
\mathbf{T}(M)
forms a single point,
\mathbf{q}
, which lies on the interior of this line segment.
Then,

f(\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{F\ni\mathbf{q}}\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F),

where
F\ni\mathbf{q}
indicates the set of all facets of tiles in
\mathbf{T}(M)
which contain the point
\mathbf{q}
.

With Lemma
5.13
in mind, we partition each set
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
into two subsets.

Definition 5.14
.

Let

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}^{\uparrow}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}

\displaystyle:=\biguplus_{i\in\widehat{\tau}}\left\{F\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}:\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F)>0\right\}\text{, and}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}^{\downarrow}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}

\displaystyle:=\biguplus_{i\in\widehat{\tau}}\left\{F\in{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}:\wsgn(F)\tsgn(F)<0\right\}.

In the next two subsections, we will prove Corollary
5.26
which shows that the facets in
{\mathbf{F}^{\uparrow}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
and the facets in
{\mathbf{F}^{\downarrow}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}
each contain a collection of disjoint subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
whose union covers the same subset of
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
(except possibly a minor inconsistency at the boundary).

In Section
6
, we will combine this result with Lemma
5.13
to show that
f
is constant.

5.4.
A Few Linear Algebra Tools

The next two subsections form the most technical part of the paper. In this section, we give some general linear algebra techniques.

We first give two versions of the well-known Cramer’s rule (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Section 2.1.2]
).

Lemma 5.15

(Cramer’s Rule Version 1)
.

Let
{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1},\mathbf{a}
be a collection of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\mathbf{v}_{2}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{n}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline\\
\mathbf{a}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{n+1}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline\\
\end{bmatrix}.

For every
i\in[n]
, the
i^{th}
entry of
\mathbf{a}
, which we will denote
a_{i}
, is given by

(4)

a_{i}=\det(\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&&\vline&\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{i-1}&\mathbf{v}_{n+1}&\mathbf{v}_{i+1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{n}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&&\vline&\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix})/\det(\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\mathbf{v}_{2}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{n}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix})

Notice that by rearranging the columns of the numerator of (
4
), we obtain the following expression for
a_{i}
.

(5)

a_{i}=(-1)^{n-i}\det(\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&&\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{i-1}&\mathbf{v}_{i+1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{n+1}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&&\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix})/\det(\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\mathbf{v}_{2}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{n}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix})

We will also use an alternate version of Cramer’s rule, which can be obtained from Lemma
5.15
through straightforward algebraic means.
In particular, this second version follows from replacing the entries of
\mathbf{a}
in the matrix equation with the expressions given in (
5
), then expanding the product, multiplying the denominator, and bringing all of the terms to the same side.

Lemma 5.16

(Cramer’s Rule Version 2)
.

Let
{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1}
be a collection of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,

\sum_{i\in[n+1]}(-1)^{i}\det(\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&&\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{i-1}&\mathbf{v}_{i+1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{n+1}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&&\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix})\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline\\
\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{0}

Before returning to the context of the paper, we give one more technical result which follows from Cramer’s rule. We will work with vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and write
\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}
for our sufficiently generic vector. Let

V:=\begin{bmatrix}\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}&\mathbf{v}_{2}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\\
\vrule height=10.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt\vline&\vline&&\vline\\
\end{bmatrix}

be an
k\times(k+1)
matrix of rank
k
. For each
i\in[k+1]
, let
V_{\widehat{i}}
be the
k\times k
matrix obtained by removing the
i^{th}
column of
V
. Additionally, recall the zonotope
Z(V)
which was defined in Definition
2.1
. Note that since the columns of
V
have rank
k
, the kernel of
V
is one-dimensional.

Proposition 5.17
.

Consider the definitions in the previous paragraph and let
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n+1})^{\top}
be any non-zero vector in the kernel of
V
. Then,

(6)

\bigsqcup_{i\in[n+1]}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\Pi}(V_{\widehat{i}})&\text{if $h_{i}<0$}\\
{\Pi}(V_{\widehat{i}})+{\mathbf{v}}_{i}&\text{if $h_{i}>0$}\end{array}\right\}=Z(V).

Proof.

It is immediate that all of the parallelepipeds on the left are contained in the zonotope on the right. Thus, it suffices to show that for any

, there is a unique

or

\mathbf{p}\in{\Pi}(V_{\widehat{i}})+{\mathbf{v}}_{i}

Fix

, ensuring that this value is small enough that
\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}\in Z(V)
. By definition, there exists some

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k+1})^{\top}\in[0,1]^{k+1}

such that
\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}=V\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in[k+1]}x_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
. Furthermore, since
\mathbf{h}
generates the kernel of

, it follows that for every real number

\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}=V(\mathbf{x}+\zeta\mathbf{h}).

In fact, the line given by

\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}
by

Next, we consider the intersection of the line

with the region
[0,1]^{k+1}

\mathbf{x}
. Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})^{\top}

In order for
\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}
to be in at least one of the parallelepipeds on the left of (

(1)

\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}=V\mathbf{u}

where

(2)

\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}=V\mathbf{u}
for some
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k+1})\in[0,1]^{k+1}
where

h_{j}>0
.

For both cases, given any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0

\mathbf{u}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbf{h}\not\in[0,1]^{k+1}

We have established that
\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}^{\prime\prime}=V\mathbf{y}

Suppose that there is more than one

Let
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
be the vector
\mathbf{y}

We also know that

In particular, if any facets in
{\mathbf{F}^{~}_{(\mathbf{z},\tau)}(M)}

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider any
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+k}

Consider any

\mathbf{p}\in T

\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}
is on the interior of

\mathbf{p}\not\in T

\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}\not\in T

such that
\mathbf{p}\in T
if and only if
\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w}\in T
. By the definition of

, this means that
f(\mathbf{p}+\varepsilon\mathbf{w})=f(\mathbf{p})
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

The function
f
is constant on all of
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
be any two points on

By Lemma
6.1
, it suffices to consider the case where
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
are not on the boundaries of any facets.
Consider a curve

which connects
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
such that whenever

crosses any facet, it crosses on the interior of that facet and parallel to

By definition,

is constant when it does not cross a facet.
By Theorem
5.29
, the value of

is constant when

Therefore the value of

is constant along

, and the result follows.

By Corollary

f
is
(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M))
.
Therefore
f
is constant with value
(-1)^{k}\sgn(\det(M))
.
∎

While it is possible to construct a signed tiling from any invertible matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{r+k}

r+k>2

r

Definition 7.1
.

Let
S

\mathbb{R}^{r+k}

\slice_{r}(S)
for the intersection of
S

0

Notice that
\slice_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{r+k})
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Furthermore, since Theorem
3.7
is true for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
, the following is an immediate corollary.

Corollary 7.2
.

The function
f(\mathbf{p}):\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}

f(\mathbf{p}):=\left(\sum_{T\in\mathbf{T}^{+}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{\slice_{r}(T)}(\mathbf{p})\right)-\left(\sum_{T\in\mathbf{T}^{-}(M)}\mathbbm{1}_{\slice_{r}(T)}(\mathbf{p})\right),

is constant with value

Proposition 7.3
.

Suppose that all of the entries in
\overline{C}_{[r+k]}(M)
are integers. Further suppose that the GCD of the maximal minors of
\overline{C}_{[r+k]}(M)
is
1
. Then, there exists some
r\times r
matrix
B
such that for each
\sigma\in\binom{[r+k]}{r}
, there is a collection
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\det(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M))}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
vectors which satisfy

\biguplus_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+k}}\slice_{r}(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma))=\biguplus_{i\in[\det(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M))]}\left(\biguplus_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}{\Pi}(C_{\sigma}(M))+\mathbf{x}_{i}+B\mathbf{z}\right).

Proof.

We only give a sketch of the proof here. For more discussion, see
[
3
, Section 7]
.

The main idea is that each tile of the form
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{z},\sigma)
maps to a collection of
|\det(\overline{C}_{\widehat{\sigma}}(M))|
many translates of
{\Pi}(C_{\sigma}(M))
in
\slice_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{r+k})
. This follows after applying column operations to
M
in order to get a matrix of the form

\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
I_{k}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
A
and
B
are integer matrices,
I_{k}
is the
k\times k
identity matrix, and
0
is the
k\times r
all zeros matrix.

∎

Remark 7.4
.

Since we want to preserve the column lattice of
M
, our column operations do not allow for multiplication by anything other than
1
or
-1
. This is the reason why we require the minors of
\overline{C}_{[r+k]}(M)
to have GCD
1
; otherwise it would be impossible to get
I_{k}
on the bottom left of the block matrix. Additionally, note that if we had fixed the last
k

\mathbf{0}
in
\slice_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{r+k})
, we would get the exact same tiling under some translation.

8.
Open Problems

The main motivation for this project was an attempt to gain a deeper understanding of a curious phenomenon (in particular Corollary
3.10
). While we were successful at generalizing this statement to Theorem
3.7
, this new result is just as surprising. We expect that a deeper exploration of this problem will lead more surprises in the future, and we have several specific directions in mind the explore.

Our initial approach when attempting to prove Theorem
3.7
was to consider an arbitrary point in
\mathbb{R}^{r+k}
(or
\Pi(M)
) and compute which tiles contain this point. A direct proof of this form would give additional insight about the tiling, since it would allow us to calculate the number and type of tiles containing a given point. However, this method was more challenging than we expected, and we ended up relying on an indirect method by focusing on the facets and proving that
f
is constant.

Open 8.1
.

What is the best algorithm to determine which tiles contain a given point? Can such an algorithm be used to give a more direct proof of Theorem
3.7
?

Another promising method to prove Theorem
3.7
is to use Fourier analysis, applying similar methods to those used in
[
2
]
(see also
[
6
]
). Perhaps these ideas could lead to a more elegant proof once the background is established.

Open 8.2
.

Is there a proof for Theorem
3.7
using Fourier analysis?

In addition to an alternate proof of the main theorem, we would also be interested in generalizing this result. As written, our construction relies on a choice of coordinates. While it should be possible to translate the statement into coordinate-free language, this is not a trivial task. Nevertheless, such a generalization would likely provide additional insight into the underlying phenomenon behind our construction.

Open 8.3
.

Is there a coordinate-free analogue to Theorem
3.7
or Corollary
3.10
?
