Multiplicative Invariance for a Class of Subsets of the Complex Plane
Neil MacVicar

Abstract
. Multiplicative invariance is a well-studied property of subsets of the unit interval. The theory in the complex plane is less developed. This paper introduces an analogous definition for multiplicative invariance in the complex plane coinciding with a more general definition concerning subsets of attractors of iterated function systems satisfying the strong separation condition. We establish similar results to those of Furstenberg’s in the unit interval. Namely, that the Hausdorff and box-counting dimensions of a multiplicatively invariant set are equal and, furthermore, are equal to the normalized topological entropy of an underlying subshift. We also extend results concerning the box-counting dimension of intersections of base- b b restricted digit sets with their translates where b b is a suitably chosen Gaussian integer.

1.
Multiplicative Invariance in
\mathbb{R}

In this section we recall multiplicative invariance for subsets of the unit interval and review their fractal properties which inspired the main result.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
r
be a positive integer. Define the map

(3)

\begin{split}&T_{r}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1)\\
&x\mapsto rx\mod{1}\end{split}

A nonempty closed subset
Y\subset[0,1]
is called
\times r-
invariant
if
T_{r}(Y)\subset Y
. A subset
Y
is called
multiplicatively invariant
if it is
\times r
-invariant for some
r\geq 2
.

Example 1.2
.

Let
r
be a positive integer. Suppose
D
is a nonempty subset of
\Lambda_{r}:=\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}
. We call the set

(4)

C_{r,D}:=\bigg\{\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{k}r^{-k}\in\mathbb{R}:d_{k}\in D\bigg\}

the
base
-
r

restricted digit Cantor set with digit set

D
. These sets are
\times r
-invariant.

We bring attention to a slight abuse of terminology. This example includes both the cases when
D
is either a singleton or equal to
\Lambda_{r}
. The set
C_{r,D}
is not a Cantor set in these two extreme cases.

The fractal properties of multiplicatively invariant sets are expressed through their Hausdorff and box-counting dimensions. We recall these dimensions here.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\delta>0
and
V
be a subset of a metric space
X
. A countable collection of sets
\{U_{k}\subset X\}
is called a
\delta
-
cover
of
V
if

(i)

V\subset\bigcup_{k}U_{k}
,

(ii)

\diam{U_{k}}\leq\delta
for each
k
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
V
be a subset of a metric space and let
s>0
. For every
\delta>0
, define the quantity

(5)

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(V):=\inf{\bigg\{\sum_{k}(\diam{U_{k}})^{s}:\{U_{k}\}\>\text{is a $\delta$-cover of}\>V\bigg\}}.

The
s
-
dimensional Hausdorff measure
of
V
is the limiting value
\mathcal{H}^{s}(V):=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(V)
.

We call the quantity

(6)

\dim_{H}{V}:=\inf{\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(V)=0\}}

the
Hausdorff dimension
of
V
.

The Hausdorff dimension can be equivalently defined using less general covers. For example, it is common to add the condition that the
\delta
-covers only contain balls.

Proposition 1.5
.

[K. Falconer,
[
4
]
, section 2.4] Let
V
be a subset of a metric space and define

(7)

\mathcal{B}_{\delta}^{s}(V):=\inf{\bigg\{\sum_{k}(\diam{B_{k}})^{s}:\{B_{k}\}\>\text{is a $\delta$-cover of}\>V\>\text{by balls}\bigg\}}.

Then
\dim_{H}{V}:=\inf{\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{B}^{s}(V)=0\}}
where
\mathcal{B}^{s}(V)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{B}_{\delta}^{s}(V)
.

The Hausdorff dimension exhibits desirable properties, but it is difficult to compute directly. The box-counting dimension is a popular alternative because of the comparative ease of computing it.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
V
be a subset of a metric space
X
. Let
\delta>0
. Let
N_{\delta}(V)
denote the minimum number of subsets of
X
of diameter at most
\delta
required to cover
V
. If it exists, we call the limit

(8)

\dim_{B}V:=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\log{N_{\delta}(V)}}{-\log{\delta}}

the
box-counting dimension
of
V
.

In the event the limit does not exist, we refer to the upper and lower limits of the above function of
\delta
as the
upper
and
lower
box-counting dimensions respectively. This fractal dimension is useful because the
N_{\delta}
function has several equivalent formulations (see
[
4
]
section 3.1 for a list). In particular, we use the fact that we may replace
N_{\delta}
by the function that takes a set

to the minimum number of closed balls of radius
\delta
needed to cover it in the proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Multiplicatively invariant subsets of the unit interval are also connected to subshifts. We recall the relevant definitions.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set equipped with the discrete topology. Let
\Sigma=\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence space equipped with the product topology and define the left shift map

\sigma:\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma

(\omega_{k})_{k\geq 1}\mapsto(\omega_{k+1})_{k\geq 1}.

We call

a

if it is closed and satisfies

Definition 1.8
.

Let

be a subshift. The

of

is the limit

(9)

\mathcal{E}(A):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log|\mathcal{L}_{n}(A)|}{n}

where
\mathcal{L}_{n}(A):=\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}):a_{1}=\omega_{1},\ldots,a_{n}=\omega_{n}\;\textit{for some}\;(\omega_{k})_{k\geq 1}\in A\}
.

We remark that a more general definition of topological entropy can be found in chapter 7 section 1 of
[
19

for continuous maps defined on compact spaces. This more general formulation is shown in theorem 7.13 of

]
to reduce to the formula above in the case of subshifts. In particular, the limit exists.

The proof of Theorem

Let

\rho^{{}^{\prime}}=\rho\rho_{1}

Lemma 1.13
.

Let
A\subset\Sigma
be a subshift. Let
R
be the collection of all finite subwords of sequences in
A
. Suppose there exists a finite collection of subwords
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset R
such that whenever
\rho\in R
is of sufficient length, it is divisible by
\rho_{k}
for some
k
. If

\sum_{k=1}^{K}r^{sl(\rho_{k})}<1
where
s>0
and
r\in(0,1)
, then
\sum_{R}r^{sl(\rho)}
converges.

Proof.

Let
\langle\rho_{k}\rangle
be the semigroup generated by
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
using concatenation. We have

(10)

\begin{split}\sum_{\langle\rho_{k}\rangle}r^{sl(\rho_{k_{1}}\rho_{k_{2}}\ldots\rho_{k_{n}})}&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n})}r^{sl(\rho_{k_{1}}\rho_{k_{2}}\ldots\rho_{k_{n}})}\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}r^{sl(\rho_{k_{i}})}\bigg)\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}r^{sl(\rho_{k})}\bigg)^{n}.\\
\end{split}

The last sum is a convergent geometric series by our assumption that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}r^{sl(\rho_{k})}<1
. We can use this to prove that
\sum_{R}r^{sl(\rho)}
converges. It is always the case that if
\rho=\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\in R
, then
\rho_{1}\in R
. By the shift invariance of
A
, it must also be that
\rho_{2}\in R
. By assumption, the set
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
has the property that every element of
R
of length greater than some
N
is divisible by one of the elements of
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. Combining these two properties allows us to divide until there is no more room to do so. This yields

(11)

\rho=\rho_{k_{1}}\rho_{k_{2}}\ldots\rho_{k_{n}}\rho_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}

where
\rho_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}
is some element of
R
that is of insufficient length to be divided further. The set of these remainders is finite since there are only finitely many words whose length is less than
N
, say
J
of them. It suffices to argue that
\sum_{R}r^{sl(\rho)}
is finite when we restrict the index set to those words of length at least
N
. Observe that

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho\in R,l(\rho)\geq N}r^{sl(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in R,l(\rho)\geq N}r^{sl(\rho_{k_{1}}\rho_{k_{2}}\ldots\rho_{k_{n}}\rho_{j}^{{}^{\prime}})}

(13)

\displaystyle<\sum_{\langle\rho_{k}\rangle}\sum_{j=1}^{J}r^{sl(\rho_{k_{1}}\rho_{k_{2}}\ldots\rho_{k_{n}}\rho_{j}^{{}^{\prime}})}

(14)

\displaystyle<J\sum_{\langle\rho_{k}\rangle}r^{sl(\rho_{k_{1}}\rho_{k_{2}}\ldots\rho_{k_{n}})}.

The last quantity is finite since the quantity in (
10

\sum_{R}r^{sl(\rho)}
converges.

2.
Invariance for Iterated Function Systems

This section contains a number of definitions and lemmas concerning iterated function systems that are used to prove our main result (Theorem
4.1
).

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. A map
f:X\to X
is called a
contraction
if there exists
c\in(0,1)
such that for all
x,y\in X
we have
d(f(x),f(y))\leq cd(x,y)
.

A finite collection of contractions
\mathcal{F}
is called an iterated function system (IFS). We can use
\mathcal{F}
to define a map
F:\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(X)
by setting

(15)

F(S)=\cup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(S),

for any
S\subset X
. The map
F
is sometimes referred to as the Hutchinson operator.

Hutchinson proved in
[
11
]
that there exists, among the class of nonempty compact subsets of a complete metric space, a unique set invariant under
F
.

Theorem 2.1
.

[J.E. Hutchinson,
[
11
]
, theorem 1]Let
X
be a complete metric space and
\mathcal{F}
be an IFS defined on
X
. There exists a unique nonempty compact set
E\subset X
such that

F(E)=E
.

The set
E
is called the
attractor
of the IFS. There is a natural way of relating the attractor to a symbolic space. This is accomplished by viewing the IFS
\mathcal{F}
as a finite alphabet.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\}
be an IFS defined on a complete metric space
X
. Let
E
denote the attractor of
\mathcal{F}
and
\Sigma=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The map
\pi:\Sigma\to E
defined by

(16)

\pi((a_{k})_{k\geq 1})=\cap_{m\geq 1}(f_{a_{1}}\circ f_{a_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{m}})(E)

is called the
coding map
and
\Sigma
is referred to as the
coding space
.

We can see that the coding map is surjective by considering the orbit of the attractor under
F
.

If the attractor
E
is a subset of euclidean space then the coding map is equivalent to
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}\mapsto\lim_{m\to\infty}(f_{a_{1}}\circ f_{a_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{m}})(0)
. For a fixed word
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{m}
we call the cylinder set
E_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}}:=(f_{a_{1}}\circ f_{a_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{m}})(E)
an
m
-tile
. Using this language, the coding map takes a decreasing sequence of
m
-tiles and maps them to the unique point in their intersection.

There are a number of special classes of IFS. In this document we focus on those that satisfy the strong separation condition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an IFS defined on a complete metric space
X
. Let
E
denote the attractor of
\mathcal{F}
. The IFS
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
strong separation condition
if for every pair of distinct maps
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
f_{1}(E)\cap f_{2}(E)=\emptyset
.

The coding map
\pi
is injective under the strong separation condition. This can be used to define a map
T:E\to E
given by
T=\pi\circ\sigma\circ\pi^{-1}
. Here,
\sigma
denotes the left shift on the coding space.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space. Suppose that
E\subset X
is the attractor of an IFS satisfying the strong separation condition. A nonempty closed subset
K
of
E
is called
\times(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n})
-invariant
if
T(K)\subset K
. Here the numbers
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}
are the contraction coefficients associated with the IFS. If the IFS is homogeneous (
c_{1}=c_{2}=\cdots=c_{n}=c
), then we simply call the set
K

\times c
-invariant
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space. Suppose that
E\subset X
is the attractor corresponding to an IFS that satisfies the strong separation condition. Let
\Sigma
be the coding space associated with this system and let
\pi
be the coding map. Suppose that
A\subset\Sigma
is a subshift.

(i)

The set
\pi(A)
is
\times(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n})
-invariant, where
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}
are the contraction coefficients associated with the IFS.

(ii)

We have
\mathcal{E}(A)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\log{N_{m}(\pi(A))}}{m}
, where
N_{m}(\pi(A))
denotes the smallest number of

-tiles required to cover

and
\mathcal{E}(A)
is the topological entropy of

.

Moreover, if

is
\times(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n})
-invariant, then
\pi^{-1}(K)\subset\Sigma

Proof.

Since the coding map is continuous and

is compact, we obtain that
\pi(A)
is a compact subset of

. We have

. If

is the left shift operator on the coding space, then we see that

. The sequence

is an element of

since

is a subshift and so we see that
\pi(A)
is invariant under

. This proves claim

. This same entwining of

and
\sigma
, in addition to the continuity of
\pi
, shows that the preimage of a
\times(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n})
-invariant subset

under

is a subshift.

We now observe that the topological entropy of

can be expressed using covers of
\pi(A)
by

-tiles. The assumption that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the strong separation condition implies that
\pi
is a bijection. The coding map then induces a bijective correspondence between the cylinder sets
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}]
and the

-tiles. This is a bijection between
m
-tiles and the subwords of length

in

(17)

N_{m}(\pi(A))=|\mathcal{L}_{m}(A)|

and in particular,

(18)

\frac{\log{N_{m}(\pi(A))}}{m}=\frac{\log{|\mathcal{L}_{m}(A)|}}{m}.

Taking the limit as

yields the result.
∎

Definition 2.6
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space. Suppose that
E\subset X
is the attractor corresponding to an IFS
\mathcal{F}
.
For
s,\delta>0
and
V\subset E
, we define the quantity

(19)

\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{s}(V):=\inf{\bigg\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\diam{T_{k}})^{s}:\{T_{k}\}\;\text{is a $\delta$-cover of}\;V\;\text{where each $T_{k}$ is an $m_{k}$-tile}\bigg\}}.

We denote the limit
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{s}(V)
by
\mathcal{T}^{s}(V)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and
E\subset X
be the attractor of a homogeneous IFS with contraction coefficient
c
. Suppose that the number of
m
-tiles that a ball of diameter less than or equal to
c^{m}\diam{E}
is bounded by a constant independent of
m
. For any
V\subset E
, we have
\dim_{H}{V}=\inf{\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{T}^{s}(V)=0\}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\{B_{k}\}
is a
\delta
-cover of
V
by balls. Since our ultimate concern is with the limit as
\delta
tends to zero, we assume
\delta\in(0,1)
.

For each
k
, we can find an integer
m_{k}
such that
c^{m_{k}+1}\diam{E}<\diam{B_{k}}\leq c^{m_{k}}\diam{E}
. The collection of these
m_{k}
-tiles, over all
k
, form a
c^{-1}\delta
-cover of
V
. Let
T^{(k)}_{j}
denote the
j
th
m_{k}
-tile that intersects
B_{k}
. Let
M
be the upper bound on the number of
m_{k}
-tiles that
B_{k}
can intersect. For
s>0
we have

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{j}(\diam{T^{(k)}_{j}})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}M(\diam{T^{(k)}_{1}})^{s}

(21)

\displaystyle=M\sum_{k}(c^{m_{k}}\diam{E})^{s}

(22)

\displaystyle=Mc^{-s}\sum_{k}(c^{m_{k}+1}\diam{E})^{s}

(23)

\displaystyle\leq Mc^{-s}\sum_{k}(\diam{B_{k}})^{s}.

Since

is a collection of
m_{k}
-tiles that form a

-cover of

, we obtain

\mathcal{T}_{c^{-1}\delta}^{s}(V)\leq Mc^{-s}\sum_{k}(\diam{B_{k}})^{s}.

Since the

-cover of balls is arbitrary, this implies
\mathcal{T}_{c^{-1}\delta}^{s}(V)\leq Mc^{-s}\mathcal{B}_{\delta}^{s}(V)
(see Proposition
1.5
to recall this notation). The Hausdorff measure is defined using arbitrary countable

. Taking limits as

yields

(25)

Both

and
\mathcal{B}^{s}(V)
have the property that they are

and

. It follows that

shares this property. Therefore

(26)

\inf{\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{T}^{s}(V)=0\}}=\inf{\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(V)=0\}}=\dim_{H}V.

∎

In this section, we define

(

The following result from
[

provides conditions on a Gaussian integer

to ensure that any complex number can be written with respect to

where the coefficients of the expansion are chosen from the set

Theorem 3.1
.

[I. Katai, J. Szabo,
[
13
]
, theorem 2] Suppose

is a positive integer and set

. Let

be an element of

and some integer

such that

The expansions are called
radix expansions
. The set
\Lambda
implicitly depends on the base
b
. Our convention will be to not include
b
in the notation. This is because we only consider a single Gaussian integer base at a time in all our discussions and wish to keep our notation simple.

Definition 3.2
.

Let

where

is a positive integer. Suppose

is a nonempty subset of

base-

We again omit any indication of the base

for the same reason the base

is omitted from the notation
\Lambda

, we maintain this terminology even when

is a singleton or equal to
\Lambda
. The set

is notably not a Cantor set in either of these extreme cases.

Consider the following two facts about base-

restricted digit Cantor sets.

Lemma 3.3
.

The base-
b
restricted digit set

is the attractor of the IFS
\mathcal{F}=\{z\mapsto\frac{z+d}{b}:d\in D\}
.

Proof.

Since
|b|>1
, the maps in
\mathcal{F}
are contractions. The equation

(29)

C_{D}=\cup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(C_{D})

can be verified directly. Let us explain why

is compact. We first endow

with the discrete topology. Observe that

with the product topology is a compact space. The coding map
\pi:D^{\mathbb{N}}\to C_{D}
is equivalent to the map
(d_{k})_{k\geq 1}\mapsto\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{k}b^{-k}
in this context. To see this, observe that for
f_{d_{1}},f_{d_{2}},\ldots,f_{d_{m}}\in\mathcal{F}
we have

(30)

f_{d_{1}}\circ f_{d_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{d_{m}}(0)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{k}b^{-k}.

This map is both continuous and surjective. Since the image of a compact set under a continous map is compact, we see that
C_{D}
is compact. Theorem
2.1
identifies

as the attractor.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
b=-n+i
with
n\geq 2
and suppose

satisfies the condition that for all

.

We postpone the proof of this lemma to after our definition for
\times b
-invariance (Definition
3.6
). We can now argue that the corresponding iterated function system of a restricted digit set with sufficiently separated digits satisfies the strong separation condition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
b=-n+i
where

and suppose

corresponding to

satisfies the strong separation condition. That is, if

Proof.

Let

be elements of

. By Lemma

, there exist unique radix expansions for

(31)

\displaystyle z=\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}b^{-k}

(32)

\displaystyle w=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}b^{-k}

respectively. The digits

and

are elements of

are distinct maps in

(33)

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)=d_{1}b^{-1}+\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}b^{-(k+1)},

Lemma 4.3

Let Y be a nonempty subset of a restricted digit Cantor set
