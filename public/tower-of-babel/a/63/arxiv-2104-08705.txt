A theory of integration for Cesàro limits
Jonathan M. Keith, Greg Markowsky

Abstract
The Cesàro limit - the asymptotic average of a sequence of real numbers - is an operator of fundamental importance in probability, statistics and analysis. Surprisingly, spaces of sequences with Cesàro limits have not previously been studied. This paper introduces spaces of such sequences, denoted K p ​ ( 𝒜 ) K_{p}({\mathcal{A}}) , with the Cesàro limit acting as a kind of integral. The space ℱ {\mathcal{F}} comprised of all binary sequences with a Cesàro limit is studied first, along with the associated functional ν : ℱ → [ 0 , 1 ] \nu:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1] mapping each such sequence to its Cesàro limit. It is shown that ℱ {\mathcal{F}} can be factored to produce a monotone class on which ν \nu induces a countably additive set function. The space K p ​ ( 𝒜 ) K_{p}({\mathcal{A}}) is then defined, and a quotient denoted 𝒦 p ​ ( 𝒜 ) {\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}}) is shown to be isometrically isomorphic, under certain conditions, to the function space ℒ p ​ ( ℕ , 𝒜 , ν ) {\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu) , where 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} is a field of sets isomorphic to a subset of ℱ {\mathcal{F}} , and ν \nu is a finitely additive measure induced by the functional mentioned above. The Cesàro limit of an element of K p ​ ( 𝒜 ) K_{p}({\mathcal{A}}) is shown to be equal to its integral. The complete ℒ p ​ ( ℕ , 𝒜 , ν ) {\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu) spaces (and by implication, the 𝒦 p ​ ( 𝒜 ) {\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}}) spaces isomorphic to them) are characterised, and a sufficient condition for these spaces to be separable is identified.

1
Introduction

Consider limits of the form

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}

where
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(here and throughout this paper,
{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the natural numbers excluding 0). These are known as Cesàro limits (see
bishop2014
for example) or sometimes as Cesàro means or Cesàro averages (as in
crismale2017
), and arise naturally in multiple mathematical fields, including statistics, probability, functional and general analysis, and in the study of stochastic processes, particularly ergodic processes. They are important in many applications (see list of references in
bishop2014
). They are named for mathematician Ernesto Cesàro (1859-1906), who was not the first to consider the asymptotic properties of a sequence of averages, but used them to define a generalised limit for divergent series (see
ferraro1999first
, and the references therein, for an interesting historical account).

Cesàro limits seem to be a kind of expectation - at any rate, ergodic theorems, for example that of Birkhoff
birkhoff1931
, establish one kind of relationship between a Cesàro limit and an expectation operator. Moreover, one may think of any sequence of real numbers
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
as a function
x:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. It seems reasonable then to consider a space of such functions for which Cesàro limits exist, and to identify conditions under which the Cesàro limit may be regarded as a kind of integral, or expectation operator. Such spaces could potentially be useful in the analysis of ergodic processes, especially if one can construct complete, separable, normed (or pseudo-normed) linear spaces on which an ergodic process may be regarded as a random element. This potential application is the motivation for introducing the
K_{p}
function spaces analysed in this paper, although connections to ergodic processes will not be explored herein. At any rate, these spaces are of interest in their own right, and may have wider applications than this original motivation.

This paper first considers binary sequences with Cesàro limits. The collection of such sequences may be identified with a collection
{\mathcal{F}}
of subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
, defined in Section
2
. A set function
\nu
that maps such subsets to their corresponding Cesàro limits is also defined. The basic properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu
are enumerated in that section. It turns out that
\nu
has many of the properties of a finitely additive measure, also known as a
charge
(see
bhaskararao1983
). However,
{\mathcal{F}}
is not a field, and thus
\nu
is not a charge unless restricted to a field of sets contained in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Section
2
also introduces the collection of
null sets

{\mathcal{N}}
, comprised of subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
that induce binary sequences with zero Cesàro limits.

Although
\nu
is not countably additive on
{\mathcal{F}}
, it turns out that chains (totally ordered sets) in
{\mathcal{F}}
on which
\nu
is countably additive have a number of useful properties. Two sections of the paper are devoted to exploring the properties of such chains. Section
3
characterises such chains in terms of uniform convergence to Cesàro limits. Section
5
develops a construction that is here called a
null modification
. This construction modifies the elements of a chain of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
by adding and/or removing null sets to produce a new chain on which
\nu
is countably additive. This section makes frequent reference to Boolean algebras and their quotients: a brief review of this topic is therefore included in Section
4
, with special attention to the Boolean quotient
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

Section
6
considers the space
[{\mathcal{F}}]
- the image of
{\mathcal{F}}
under the quotient map
\xi:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. The collection of equivalence classes
[{\mathcal{F}}]
is shown to be a monotone class in the Boolean quotient
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. This is a useful insight into the structure of
{\mathcal{F}}
because it implies that every field of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
can be extended in such a way that the extension maps to a countably complete subalgebra of
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, as a consequence of the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras, which is reviewed in Section
4
. This version of the monotone class theorem is an abstraction of the well known version for fields of sets, and is a slight generalisation of similar results in the literature. Consequently, a full proof is presented in the preprint version of this paper.

The function space
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is defined in Section
7
, where
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
is a field of sets (not necessarily a
\sigma
-field) and
p\in[1,\infty)
. The space
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
contains real-valued functions on a
charge space

({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
. Charge spaces are generalisations of measure spaces, and are comprised of a sample space (here
{\mathbb{N}}
), a field of subsets of the sample space (here
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
), and a finitely additive measure, also known as a
charge
. A comprehensive introduction to the theory of charges is provided in
bhaskararao1983
. A concise summary is provided in
keith2022
, including an introduction to
L_{p}
spaces and their quotients under equivalence almost everywhere (denoted
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
spaces). That paper also extends the theory of bounded charges, presenting new characterisations of key properties that are applied in the present paper.

Section
7
explores the properties of
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces and their quotients (denoted
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces). In particular,
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is shown to be a dense subspace of
L_{p}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
, such that the integral of any function in
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
corresponds to the Cesàro limit of the corresponding sequence. In the rest of this paper, the function space
L_{p}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
and its quotient
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
will be abbreviated as
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
respectively, since the sample space
{\mathbb{N}}
and charge
\nu
are assumed throughout. Under certain conditions, the quotient spaces
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
are shown to be isomorphic.

The remaining sections consider
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces that are complete and separable. Section
8
characterises those
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces for which
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
\sigma
-field and
\nu
is countably additive; these spaces have a particularly simple form, lacking generality. Section
9
characterises more general
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces that are complete, and identifies an isometric isomorphism between a complete
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
space and a conventional (ie. Lebesgue) function space. Section
10
identifies a sufficient condition for an
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
space to be separable, specifically if
{\mathcal{A}}
is in a certain sense generated by a chain, and shows that this is equivalent to being generated (in a certain sense) by a countable sub-field of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

2
Cesàro limits of binary sequences

Let
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
denote the power set of an arbitrary set
X
and consider the following definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

For any
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, define a partial average

\nu_{N}(A):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{A}(n),

for each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
I_{A}
is the indicator function for the set
A
.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, define the upper and lower Cesàro limits respectively as

\nu^{+}(A):=\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)\mbox{ and }\nu^{-}(A):=\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A).

Naturally, the sets upon which
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
coincide are of particular interest, motivating the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the collection of subsets
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\nu(A):=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)

exists in the interval
[0,1]
. That is,
{\mathcal{F}}
is the collection of subsets
A
of
{\mathbb{N}}
for which the Cesàro limit of the binary sequence
x_{n}:=I_{A}(n)
(for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) exists, and
\nu(A)
is that limit for any
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Note that

1.

0\leq\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(A)\leq 1
,

2.

\nu^{-}(A)=\nu^{+}(A)\iff A\in{\mathcal{F}}
and

3.

A\in{\mathcal{F}}\implies\nu(A)=\nu^{+}(A)
.

While
\nu_{N}
is a measure on the power set of
{\mathbb{N}}
, being simply a scaling of counting measure on a finite set, it is evident that
\nu
is not; to see this, note that any singleton set
\{k\}
will have
\nu(\{k\})=0
, but
\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\{k\}={\mathbb{N}}
, so that
\nu
is not countably additive. It is, however, finitely additive, and is thus a charge when restricted to fields of sets contained in
{\mathcal{F}}
. It may be helpful to consider specific examples of sets for which
\nu
exists. If
D_{m}
is the set of multiples of an integer
m
, then
\nu(D_{m})=\frac{1}{m}
. The same holds if
D^{r}_{m}
is the set of all numbers equal to
r
modulo
m
, and by taking unions of such sets one can obtain a set with any rational number as its charge. To obtain an irrational number
s
as a charge is only slightly harder, and it can be achieved by the following algorithm. Start with
A_{1}:=\{1\}
, then for
N\geq 2
, if
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{A_{N}}(n)<s
let
A_{N+1}:=A_{N}\cup\{N+1\}
, else let
A_{N+1}:=A_{N}
. It is straightforward to show that if
A:=\cup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}A_{n}
, then
\nu(A)=s
.

The following method of describing sets is also useful for constructing specific examples. Let
z_{n}
be a sequence of positive integers for
n\geq 2
, and let
z_{1}
be a non-negative integer. Let
Z_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{n}
. Then let
A
be defined by

I_{A}(n):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if $Z_{2k-1}+1\leq n\leq Z_{2k}$ for some $k\geq 1$}\\
0&\mbox{if $n\leq z_{1}$ or $Z_{2k}+1\leq n\leq Z_{2k+1}$ for some $k\geq 1$}\end{cases}.

In words,
I_{A}(n)
is
z_{1}
zeroes, followed by
z_{2}
ones, followed by
z_{3}
zeroes, etc. It may be checked that
\nu^{+}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\nu_{Z_{2N}}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N}}
and
\nu^{-}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\nu_{Z_{2N-1}}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N-1}}
. To form a simple example of a set which is not in
{\mathcal{F}}
, let
z_{n}=2^{n-1}
. Then
Z_{n}=2^{n}-1
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}2^{2j-1}=\frac{2}{3}(2^{2N}-1)
. Furthermore,

\nu^{+}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{3}(2^{2N}-1)}{2^{2N}-1}=\frac{2}{3}

and

\nu^{-}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{3}(2^{2(N-1)}-1)}{2^{2N-1}-1}=\frac{1}{3}.

This set can then be used to construct two sets
B,C\in{\mathcal{F}}
, such that
B\cap C\notin{\mathcal{F}}
, thereby showing that
{\mathcal{F}}
is not a field. Let
B
be the set of all even numbers, and let
C
be defined by

I_{C}(n):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if $n$ is even and $\frac{n}{2}\in A$, or $n$ is odd and $\frac{n+1}{2}\notin A$ }\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

It is clear
B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, with
\nu(B)=\frac{1}{2}
, and the same conclusion follows for
C
upon noting that exactly one of
\{2k-1,2k\}
lies in
C
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. However,
B\cap C=2A
, the set of the doubles of elements of
A
, and therefore
B\cap C\notin{\mathcal{F}}
.

For the purpose of intuition, it is profitable to think of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
in this manner, as defined by the concatenation of alternating strings of zeroes and ones of variable length. The following result describes the lengths of these strings allowable for a set to be in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Consider any
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
. For each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
P_{A}(N)
to be the smallest integer
k>0
such that
I_{A}(N+k)=1
or define
P_{A}(N)=\infty
if no such integer exists. Similarly, define
Q_{A}(N)
to be the smallest integer
k>0
such that
I_{A}(N+k)=0
or define
Q_{A}(N)=\infty
if no such integer exists.

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
.

1.

If
A
contains
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(F)>0
, then
\nu^{-}(A)>0
and
P_{A}
is
o(N)
.

2.

If
A
is contained in
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(F)<1
, then
\nu^{+}(A)<1
and
Q_{A}
is
o(N)
.

Proof:
Suppose
A
contains
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(F)>0
. Then
\nu^{-}(A)\geq\nu^{-}(F)=\nu(F)>0
. Moreover
F
contains infinitely many integers, so
P_{A}(N)\leq P_{F}(N)<\infty
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
F
excludes only finitely many integers then
P_{A}(N)=P_{F}(N)=1
for large enough
N
, making
P_{A}(N)
trivially
o(N)
, so assume
F
excludes infinitely many integers. There are thus infinitely many positive integers
N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots
such that
I_{F}(N_{i}+1)\neq I_{F}(N_{i})
. Note

\nu(F)=\nu_{N_{1}}(F)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\nu_{N_{i+1}}(F)-\nu_{N_{i}}(F))

is a series consisting of alternating positive and negative terms corresponding respectively to runs of ones and zeros in the sequence
(I_{F}(1),I_{F}(2),\ldots)
. Since the series converges, terms corresponding to runs of zeros must decrease in magnitude to 0.

Now consider any
N
with
P_{F}(N)=k+1>0
. Then

\nu_{N+k}(F)-\nu_{N}(F)=\nu_{N}(F)\frac{N}{N+k}-\nu_{N}(F)=-\nu_{N}(F)\left(\frac{k}{N+k}\right).

In particular, if
N_{i}
corresponds to the end of a run of ones, then the subsequent run of zeros contributes a term

-\nu_{N_{i}}(F)\left(\frac{P_{F}(N_{i})-1}{N+P_{F}(N_{i})-1}\right)

to the above series, and since
\nu_{N_{i}}(F)\rightarrow\nu(F)>0
, these terms can only go to 0 if

\frac{P_{F}(N_{i})-1}{N+P_{F}(N_{i})-1}\rightarrow 0

implying
P_{A}(N)\leq P_{F}(N)
is
o(N)
. The second part of the lemma follows by applying the first part to
A^{c}
.
∎

It can be shown that the term
o(N)
in Proposition
2.4
cannot be replaced by
o(N^{1-\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
, as follows. For positive integer
q
let
z_{n}=n^{q}
, and form a set
A
by the method described earlier in this section. Then, by comparing the sum with an integral, the easy estimates
\frac{N^{q+1}}{q+1}\leq Z_{N}\leq\frac{(N+1)^{q+1}}{q+1}
are obtained, and since
\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}=2^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}
it follows also that
\frac{2^{q}N^{q+1}}{q+1}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}\leq\frac{2^{q}(N+1)^{q+1}}{q+1}
. Thus,
\nu^{+}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N}}\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{2^{q}(N+1)^{q+1}}{(2N)^{q+1}}=\frac{1}{2}
, and
\nu^{-}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N-1}}\geq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{2^{q}(N-1)^{q+1}}{(2N-1)^{q+1}}=\frac{1}{2}
, so
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
. However,
P_{A}(Z_{2N})=(2N+1)^{q}+1
, and
Z_{2N}\leq\frac{(2N+1)^{q+1}}{q+1}
, hence
\frac{P_{A}(Z_{2N})^{(q+1)/q}}{Z_{2N}}
is bounded below by a positive constant for any
q>0
, giving the result.

Sets that
\nu
maps to zero play an important role in this paper.

Definition 2.5
.

The null sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
are the elements of
{\mathcal{N}}:=\{A\in{\mathcal{F}}:\nu(A)=0\}
.

Null sets are easy to find. The set of square numbers is a null set, as is the set of cubes, etc. The set of powers of 2, or of any other base, is a null set. The set of primes is shown to be a null set by the prime number theorem.

The next proposition, which describes key properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
and the Cesáro limits of its elements, is of fundamental importance in subsequent sections.

Proposition 2.6
.

The collection
{\mathcal{F}}
and set functions
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
have the following properties.

1.

\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, with
\nu(\emptyset)=0
and
\nu({\mathbb{N}})=1
.

2.

For all
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A^{c}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A^{c})=1-\nu(A)
.

3.

For all
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A\cup B\in{\mathcal{F}}
if and only if
A\cap B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, and if either is true then
\nu(A\cup B)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)-\nu(A\cap B)
.

4.

If
A,B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, then
\nu^{+}(A\cup B)\leq\nu^{+}(A)+\nu^{+}(B)
. If
A
,
B
, and
A\cup B
are all in
{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\nu(A\cup B)\leq\nu(A)+\nu(B)
.

5.

For
A,B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
A\subseteq B
,

(a)

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)
,

(b)

\nu^{+}(B\setminus A)\geq\nu^{+}(B)-\nu^{+}(A)
,

(c)

\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)
, and

(d)

\nu^{-}(B\setminus A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)-\nu^{-}(A)
.

If in addition
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then

(a)

\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
,

(b)

B\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

(c)

\nu(B\setminus A)=\nu(B)-\nu(A)
, and

(d)

\nu(A)=\nu(B)\iff B\setminus A\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

6.

If
A\in{\mathcal{N}}
, then any
B\subseteq A
satisfies
B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Consequently, for any
C\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
,

(a)

A\cap C,A\setminus C\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and

(b)

\nu^{+}(A\cup C)=\nu^{+}(C\setminus A)=\nu^{+}(C)
.

If
C\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
A\cup C,C\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

7.

For pairwise disjoint sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A_{1}\cup\ldots\cup A_{K}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with

\nu(A_{1}\cup\ldots\cup A_{K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu(A_{k}).

8.

Consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Then

\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup\{\nu^{-}(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}.

In particular, if
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint, then

\nu^{-}(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k}).

9.

Consider a chain
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(A)
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then

(a)

\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
, and

(b)

if
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(A)
.

In particular, if
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint with
\nu_{N}(A_{k})\leq\nu(A_{k})
for all
k,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

10.

Consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
. If
\sup\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}=1
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=1
. In particular, if
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint with
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})=1
, then
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})=1
.

Proof:
Property 1 is trivial. For Property 2, consider any
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
and note

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A^{c})=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(1-I_{A}(n))=1-\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)

hence
A^{c}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(A^{c})=1-\nu(A)
.

For 3, consider
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
and note
I_{A\cup B}=I_{A}+I_{B}-I_{A\cap B}
, hence

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A\cup B)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)-\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A\cap B)

if either limit exists, and the statement follows immediately.

For 4, note
I_{A\cup B}\leq I_{A}+I_{B}
, hence
\nu_{N}(A\cup B)\leq\nu_{N}(A)+\nu_{N}(B)
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The first statement then follows by taking the
\limsup
and the second by taking limits as
N\rightarrow\infty
. (The second statement alternatively follows from 3).

For 5, note
I_{A}\leq I_{B}
, hence
\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)
and
\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)
. Also
I_{B}=I_{A}+I_{B\setminus A}
, hence

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B)\leq\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)+\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B\setminus A).

That is,
\nu^{+}(B\setminus A)\geq\nu^{+}(B)-\nu^{+}(A)
. That
\nu^{-}(B\setminus A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)-\nu^{-}(A)
is shown similarly. If
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\nu(A)=\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)=\nu(B)
and

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B)-\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)=\nu(B)-\nu(A)

thus
B\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(B\setminus A)=\nu(B)-\nu(A)
. Hence
\nu(B)-\nu(A)=0\iff\nu(B\setminus A)=0
.

For 6, note for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

0\leq\nu_{N}(B)\leq\nu_{N}(A)

and
B\in{\mathcal{N}}
follows by letting
N\rightarrow\infty
. The consequences 6(a) follow because
A\cap C\subseteq A
and
A\setminus C\subseteq A
. For 6b, note

\nu^{+}(C)=\nu^{+}(C)-\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(C\setminus A)\leq\nu^{+}(A\cup C)\leq\nu^{+}(A)+\nu^{+}(C)=\nu^{+}(C).

If
C\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A\cup C\in{\mathcal{F}}
by 3, and
C\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
by 5.

For 7, use Property 3 with
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\emptyset
, to conclude that
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(A_{1}\cup A_{2})=\nu(A_{1})+\nu(A_{2})
. The property then follows by induction.

For 8, by Property 5,
\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\nu^{-}(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence
\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup\{\nu^{-}(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
. The second part of 8 follows by defining
B_{j}:=\cup_{k=1}^{j}A_{k}
, so that by Property 7,
B_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(B_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\nu(A_{k})
for each
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then apply the first part of 8 to
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
.

For 9, note for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\nu_{N}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu_{N}(A)\leq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
, hence
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\leq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
. Moreover, for any
\epsilon>0
, one can choose
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\nu^{+}(C)>\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)-\epsilon
. Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
. If
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, then by 8,

\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(A)=\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}),

hence
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(A)
. The last part of 9 follows by setting
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
as defined in the proof of 8. Then 9b gives
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})=\sup_{k}\nu(B_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

For 10, note that
1\geq\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}=1
, using Property 8. Hence
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})
and the first part follows. The second part of 10 follows by applying the first part to
{\mathcal{C}}=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
defined above in the proof of 8.
∎

Property 9 is particularly important in what follows, so it may be helpful to discuss an example. Let
{\mathcal{O}}
denote the set of odd numbers which are at least 3, and for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
set
D_{k}=\{2^{k}m:m\in{\mathcal{O}}\}
. Then it may be checked that
\nu_{N}(D_{k})\leq\nu(D_{k})
for all
N
and
\nu(D_{k})=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}
. The set
D:=\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}D_{k}
is all of
{\mathbb{N}}
with a null set (the powers of
2
) removed, and thus
\nu(D)=1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\nu(D_{k})
, so that Property 9 holds. If
1
were included in
{\mathcal{O}}
the same conclusion would hold, even though the sufficient condition
\nu_{N}(D_{k})\leq\nu(D_{k})
would not (it is evident, however, that this or some other condition is needed to ensure countable additivity, since
\nu
is not a measure). This example will appear briefly again in Section
5
, which contains a method for modifying sets by removing a null set so that Property 9 can be applied.

Since
{\mathcal{F}}
contains
\emptyset
and is closed under complements and finite disjoint unions, it is an object known as an
additive class
.

3
Uniform convergence of chains in
{\mathcal{F}}

A real-valued function on any set
X
generates a chain of subsets consisting of inverse images of rays in
\mathbb{R}
. Thus the properties of chains consisting of subsets of
X
are relevant to the study of real-valued functions on
X
. In this section and Section
5
, two analysis tools for studying chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
are developed. The first of these is a characterisation of a certain class of chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
in terms of uniform convergence of partial averages over the sets in the chain.

The following notation is helpful to describe this characterisation. Consider a chain of sets
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
(that is, a collection of sets that is totally ordered by set inclusion). Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
denote the closure of
{\mathcal{T}}
under unions and intersections, respectively. That is,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}:=\{\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}\}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}:=\{\bigcap{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}\}
. Also set
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}:={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
. Some basic properties of
{\mathcal{T}}_{*},{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
are the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is a chain. Then

1.

{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
are chains.

2.

{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is closed under unions and intersections.

Proof:
To prove 1, first consider
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
. If
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
with
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
, then either
(i)

A\subseteq C
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, in which case
A\subseteq B
, or
(ii)

A\supseteq C
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, in which case
A\supseteq B
. Alternatively, suppose
A=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
with
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. By the previous argument, for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
either
C\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
or
C\supseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
; if
C\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, otherwise
C\supseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, in which case
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\supseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. Thus
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
is a chain. A complementary argument shows
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
is a chain. To show
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is a chain, one must identify an ordering between
A=\bigcap{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
and
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
with
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. If
C\supseteq D
for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1},D\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, then
B\subseteq A
, otherwise
C\subseteq D
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1},D\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, in which case
A\subseteq B
.

As for 2, suppose first that
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
and consider
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
. It is possible
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
, but in this case there is nothing to prove since
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
, so assume
B\notin{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
. Any
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
can be expressed as an intersection of sets in
{\mathcal{T}}
, and if each of these sets contained
B
then one would have
B\subseteq A
and hence
B=A
, contradicting
B\notin{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
. Hence there exists
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}
that contains
A
but not
B
. Then
A\subseteq A^{\prime}\subseteq B
, since
({\mathcal{T}}_{\cap})_{\cup}
is a chain by the first part of this lemma. It follows that
B=\bigcup\{A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}:A^{\prime}\subseteq B\}
, and hence
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
. This shows
({\mathcal{T}}_{\cap})_{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}={\mathcal{T}}_{*}
. Now suppose
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
. Then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}=B_{1}\cup B_{2}
where
B_{1}\in({\mathcal{T}}_{\cap})_{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
and
B_{2}\in({\mathcal{T}}_{\cup})_{\cup}={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
. But
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is a chain, hence
B_{1}\cup B_{2}
is either
B_{1}
or
B_{2}
. Either way
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
, hence
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is closed under unions. A complementary argument shows
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is closed under intersections.
∎

The following theorem identifies three alternative characterisations of a class of well behaved chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
. The first characterisation implies countable additivity of the restriction of
\nu
to the chain: it thus identifies chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
on which
\nu
behaves like a measure. The other characterisations identify other useful properties of such chains, in particular, uniform convergence of partial averages of elements of the chain.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
be a chain of sets. Then the following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and for any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
and
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
.

2.

There exists a chain
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
such that
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
and
\nu({\mathcal{U}}):=\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{U}}\}
is dense in
[0,1]
.

3.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)\rvert<\epsilon
for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and all
N>N_{\epsilon}
.

Moreover, if any of the three statements holds then

1.

{\mathcal{U}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and for any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
and
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
,

2.

\nu({\mathcal{U}}_{*})=[0,1]
,

3.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)\rvert<\epsilon
for all
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
and all
N>N_{\epsilon}
.

Proof:
(
1\implies 2
) Set
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}={\mathcal{T}}
. If there is no open interval
(b,c)\subset[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-1}
such that
(b,c)\cap\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{0})=\emptyset
, then set
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}:={\mathcal{T}}_{0}
. If there is exactly one such open interval, define sets
B:=\bigcup\{A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0}:\nu(A)<b\}
and
C:=\bigcap\{A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0}:\nu(A)>c\}
. Statement 1 implies
B,C\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
,
\nu(B)\leq b<c\leq\nu(C)
and
(\nu(B),\nu(C))\cap\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{0})=\emptyset
. Form a set
A
(called a
midpoint set
) containing
B
and every second element of the sequence generated by listing the elements of
C\setminus B
in increasing order. Then
B\subset A\subset C
and it is straightforward to show
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=(\nu(B)+\nu(C))/2
. Set
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
to be
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
plus the midpoint set thus formed. If there are two disjoint open intervals of width at least
2^{-1}
, both of which have empty intersection with
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{0})
, then find the midpoint sets for both intervals and add them to
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
to form
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
. Note there cannot be more than two such intervals. Then
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
is a chain that satisfies
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and for any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
and
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. Moreover,
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{1})\subseteq[0,1]
does not exclude any open intervals in
[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-1}
. Proceeding inductively, one can generate a non-decreasing sequence of chains
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{2}\subseteq\ldots
such that
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{k})
does not exclude any open intervals in
[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-k}
. (Note
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{k-1})
cannot exclude more than
2^{k}
disjoint open intervals in
[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-k}
, so at most
2^{k}
mid-point sets are added to
{\mathcal{T}}_{k-1}
to form
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
.) Thus the chain
{\mathcal{U}}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
contains
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\nu({\mathcal{U}})
is dense in
[0,1]
.

(
2\implies 3
) Suppose without loss of generality that
\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
(if not, simply add them). Fix
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists finite
F\subseteq\nu({\mathcal{U}})
such that for every
x\in[0,1]
there are
b,c\in F
with
b\leq x\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon/2
, since
\nu({\mathcal{U}})
us dense in
[0,1]
. For each
b\in F
, there exists
A_{b}\in{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\nu(A_{b})=b
.

Since
F
is finite, there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert\nu_{N}(A_{b})-\nu(A_{b})\rvert<\epsilon/2
for all
N>N_{\epsilon}
and all
b\in F
. Now for any
A\in{\mathcal{U}}
, there exist
b,c\in F
with
A_{b}\subseteq A\subseteq A_{c}
,
b\leq\nu(A)\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon/2
. Thus,

\lvert\nu_{N}(A_{b})-\nu(A)\rvert\leq\lvert\nu_{N}(A_{b})-b\rvert+\lvert\nu(A)-b\rvert\leq\lvert\nu_{N}(A_{b})-\nu(A_{b})\rvert+\lvert c-b\rvert<\epsilon

and similarly
\lvert\nu_{N}(A_{c})-\nu(A)\rvert<\epsilon
.

Since
A_{b}\subseteq A\subseteq A_{c}
,

\nu(A)-\epsilon<\nu_{N}(A_{b})\leq\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu_{N}(A_{c})<\nu(A)+\epsilon,

which implies
\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)\rvert<\epsilon
. Hence Condition 3 holds for all
A\in{\mathcal{U}}
, and thus for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
.

(
3\implies 1
) Consider
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
and fix
\epsilon>0
. Define
\nu_{\cup}(A):=\sup\{\nu(C):C\in{\mathcal{T}},C\subseteq A\}
. Then there exists
C_{1}\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
C_{1}\subseteq A
and
\lvert\nu(C)-\nu_{\cup}(A)\rvert<\epsilon/2
for all
C\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
C_{1}\subseteq C\subseteq A
. By assumption, there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert\nu_{N}(C)-\nu(C)\rvert<\epsilon/2
for all
C\in{\mathcal{T}}
and for all
N>N_{\epsilon}
. For any
N>N_{\epsilon}
, there exists
C_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}\subseteq A
and
I_{A}(n)=I_{C_{2}}(n)
for
n=1,\ldots,N
, so that
\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(C_{2})\rvert=0
. Thus

\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{\cup}(A)\rvert\leq\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(C_{2})\rvert+\lvert\nu_{N}(C_{2})-\nu(C_{2})\rvert+\lvert\nu(C_{2})-\nu_{\cup}(A)\rvert<\epsilon.

Hence
\nu_{N}(A)\rightarrow\nu_{\cup}(A)
, implying
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\nu_{\cup}(A)
. Similarly, for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
,
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\nu_{\cap}(A):=\inf\{\nu(C):C\in{\mathcal{T}},A\subseteq C\}
. Hence
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
.

Now consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
and define
A:=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
. Then
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(A)=\nu_{\cup}(A)
as shown in the preceding paragraph. It is straightforward to check
\nu_{\cup}(A)=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. Similarly,
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
.

If any of the three statements hold for
{\mathcal{T}}
, then Statement 2 also holds with
{\mathcal{T}}
replaced by
{\mathcal{U}}
, since trivially
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
. Thus
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, by Statement 1. But then Statement 2 holds with both
{\mathcal{T}}
and
{\mathcal{U}}
replaced by
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. Hence Statements 1 and 3 hold with
{\mathcal{T}}
replaced by
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. Finally,
\nu({\mathcal{U}}_{*})=[0,1]
, since for any
x\in[0,1]
, the set
A_{x}:=\bigcup\{A\in{\mathcal{U}}:\nu(A)\leq x\}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
with
\nu(A_{x})=x
.
∎

Theorem
3.2
is used in the proof of Theorem
7.12
(more specifically, it is used in the proof of Lemma
7.1
, on which the proof of Theorem
7.12
depends).

The following two corollaries respectively provide a simplification of Theorem
3.2
for chains that are also sequences (Corollary
3.3
), and a fourth characterisation of the class of chains described in Theorem
3.2
in terms of maximal chains (Corollary
3.4
).

Corollary 3.3
.

Consider pairwise disjoint sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Let
B_{k}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
for each
k
and let
B=\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}
. The following conditions are logically equivalent:

1.

B\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(B)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

2.

For any
\epsilon>0
there exists a positive integer
N_{\epsilon}=N_{\epsilon}(B_{1},B_{2},\ldots)
such that

\lvert\nu_{N}(B_{k})-\nu(B_{k})\rvert<\epsilon

for all
N\geq N_{\epsilon}
and for all
k
.

Moreover, if either statement holds then
\lvert\nu_{N}(B)-\nu(B)\rvert<\epsilon
for all
N\geq N_{\epsilon}
.

Proof:
Note
{\mathcal{T}}=\{B_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a chain in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note also
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}={\mathcal{T}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}={\mathcal{T}}\cup\{B\}
.

(
1\implies 2
) Statement 1 gives
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}={\mathcal{T}}\cup\{B\}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. For any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
,
\bigcap{\mathcal{C}}
is the smallest element of
{\mathcal{C}}
, hence
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. If there is a largest element of
{\mathcal{C}}
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
is that largest element, otherwise
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}=B
. In the case of a largest element,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. In the case
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}=B
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\nu(B)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
again. Hence the uniform convergence condition holds on all
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
by Theorem
3.2
.

(
2\implies 1
) Statement 2 is the uniform convergence condition of Theorem
3.2
as it applies to
{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(B)=\sup_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(B_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
be a chain of sets. Then
{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
3.2
if and only if there exists a maximal chain
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
(maximal in the sense that it is not a proper subset of any other chain in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
) such that
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
and
\nu({\mathcal{U}}_{**})=[0,1]
.

Proof:

(\implies)

First define the chain
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
as described in Theorem
3.2
, and assume without loss of generality that
\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
B_{k}:=\bigcup\{A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}:k\notin A\}
and
C_{k}:=\bigcap\{A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}:k\in A\}
. Let
D_{k}=C_{k}\setminus B_{k}
(it is straightforward to verify
B_{k}\subset C_{k}
). Note
C_{k}
is the smallest set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
containing
k
, and
B_{k}
is the largest set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
not containing
k
. These sets have the following properties, which are left to the reader to verify.

1.

If
k^{\prime}\in D_{k}
, then
D_{k}=D_{k^{\prime}}
.

2.

If
B_{k}\subseteq A\subseteq C_{k}
for some
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then either
A=B_{k}
or
A=C_{k}
.

3.

If
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, then for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
either
A\subseteq B_{k}
or
C_{k}\subseteq A
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
D_{k}=\{x_{k1},x_{k2},\ldots\}
, ordered by increasing magnitude; this set may be finite or infinite. Now let
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
be
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
together with all sets of the form
B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}
, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
(if
D_{k}
is finite, restrict
N
accordingly). Then
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
is a maximal chain, shown as follows. Let
E,F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
. If
E,F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, they are comparable since
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
is a chain. If
E\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*},F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, then choose
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that
F
is of the form
B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}
. By
3
above either
E\subseteq B_{k}
, in which case
E\subseteq F
, or
C_{k}\subseteq E
, in which case
F\subseteq E
, so in either case
E
and
F
are comparable. If
E,F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, then they must be of the form
E=B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}
and
F=B_{k^{\prime}}\cup\{x_{k^{\prime}1},\ldots,x_{k^{\prime}N^{\prime}}\}
; if
k^{\prime}\in D_{k}
then one must contain the other by 1 above, whereas if
k^{\prime}\notin D_{k}
the result follows by noting that in this case either
C_{k}\subseteq B_{k^{\prime}}
or
C_{k^{\prime}}\subseteq B_{k}
(by 2 above). Thus
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
is a chain.

Suppose there exists a chain
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
with
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
E\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Suppose
C_{k}\subseteq E
for all
k\in E
. Then
\cup_{k\in E}C_{k}\subseteq E
, implying

. Alternatively, suppose there exists
k\in E
such that
E\subseteq C_{k}
. Then
B_{k}\subseteq E\subseteq C_{k}
, since

. Either

, or there is a largest

is maximal.

Finally,
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, since
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, and every set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
differs from a set in

by at most a finite (and therefore null) set.

(\impliedby)
This is immediate from Statement 2 of Theorem

.
∎

4
Boolean algebras, quotients and the monotone class theorem

The set
{\mathcal{F}}
can in a certain sense be factored by the null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
to produce a simple structure known as a monotone class, on which the induced function
\nu
is countably additive. This useful result is Corollary
6.5
below. The proof involves a technique for manipulating chains in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
that is here called
null modification
, described in Section
5
. Both sections involve Boolean quotients, and while the theory of Boolean algebras and their quotients will be familiar to many readers, it may nevertheless be helpful to briefly review key definitions and results. That is the purpose of this section. There are no new results in this section, but it does contain a slight generalisation of the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras (Theorem
4.1
), based on the proof for fields of sets given in Paul Halmos’ classic text on Measure Theory.

A
Boolean algebra

givant2009
is an abstraction of a field of sets consisting of a non-empty set
{\mathcal{A}}
equipped with two binary operators called
join

\vee
and
meet

\wedge
, a unary
complement
operator
′
and containing special elements called the
zero

0
and
unit
(or
one
)
1
, satisfying the following axioms:

\displaystyle p\wedge 1=p,

\displaystyle p\vee 0=p,

\displaystyle p\wedge p^{\prime}=0,

\displaystyle p\vee p^{\prime}=1,

\displaystyle p\wedge q=q\wedge p,

\displaystyle p\vee q=q\vee p,

\displaystyle p\wedge(q\vee r)=(p\wedge q)\vee(p\wedge r),

\displaystyle p\vee(q\wedge r)=(p\vee q)\wedge(p\vee r).

These four pairs of axioms are known as the
identity laws
,
complement laws
,
commutative laws
and
distributive laws
respectively, and entail a number of other well known identities including associative laws and De Morgan’s laws. Other common Boolean operators and relations can be composed from the meet, join and complement, for example
p-q:=p\wedge q^{\prime}
and
p+q:=(p\wedge q^{\prime})\vee(p^{\prime}\wedge q)
. Another example is the partial order defined by
p\leq q\iff p\vee q=q
.

The simplest example of a Boolean algebra is the set
\{0,1\}
, with basic Boolean operations defined by

\displaystyle 0\wedge 0=0,

\displaystyle 1\wedge 1=1,

\displaystyle 0\wedge 1=1\wedge 0=0,

\displaystyle 0\vee 0=0,

\displaystyle 1\vee 1=1,

\displaystyle 0\vee 1=1\vee 0=1,

\displaystyle 0^{\prime}=1,

\displaystyle 1^{\prime}=0.

Any field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
on an arbitrary set
X
is a Boolean algebra with pairwise intersection
\cap
as the
meet
operator, pairwise union
\cup
as the
join
operator, set complement
c
as the Boolean complement operator, the empty set
\emptyset
as the zero and
X
as the unit. Note also
p-q
is the set difference
p\setminus q
,
p+q
is the symmetric difference
p\triangle q
and the partial order
p\leq q
is the subset relation
p\subseteq q
.

A
Boolean homomorphism
is a mapping
f:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
between Boolean algebras
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
that respects the basic set operations. Specifically, a homomorphism satisfies

\displaystyle f(A\vee B)

\displaystyle=f(A)\vee f(B),

\displaystyle f(A\wedge B)

\displaystyle=f(A)\wedge f(B),

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=0,\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle f(1)

\displaystyle=1,

for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}
. It follows that
f(A^{\prime})=f(A)^{\prime}
, and indeed all finite combinations of basic Boolean operations are respected, including the partial order, that is
p\leq q\implies f(p)\leq f(q)
. A
Boolean isomorphism
is a homomorphism with an inverse homomorphism.

A
Boolean ideal

{\mathcal{M}}

givant2009
is a non-empty subset of a Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfying the following axioms:

\displaystyle p,q\in{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle p\vee q\in{\mathcal{M}},

\displaystyle p\in{\mathcal{M}},q\in{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle p\wedge q\in{\mathcal{M}}.

For example, for any charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, the set
\mu^{-1}(0):=\{A\in{\mathcal{A}}:\mu(A)=0\}
, called the
kernel
of
\mu
, is an ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and the set
\{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\mu^{*}(A)=0\}
, where
\mu^{*}(A):=\inf\{\mu(B):B\in{\mathcal{A}},A\subseteq B\}
, forms an ideal of
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
. The set
{\mathcal{N}}
defined in Definition
2.5
is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, since by Proposition
2.6

A\cup B\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and
A\cap C\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
C\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
.

A Boolean ideal
{\mathcal{M}}
induces an equivalence relation
\sim
on the containing Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
such that

p\sim q\iff p+q\in{\mathcal{M}}.

The collection of equivalence classes
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}:=\{[p]:p\in{\mathcal{A}}\}
, where
[p]
denotes the equivalence class of
p
under the equivalence relation induced by
{\mathcal{M}}
, is called the
quotient
of
{\mathcal{A}}
by
{\mathcal{M}}
. When the Boolean algebra in question is ambiguous it is convenient to write
[p]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}}
to identify both the underlying algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and the ideal
{\mathcal{M}}
.

A key example in this paper is the Boolean quotient

{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\},

where
[A]
denotes the equivalence class of
A
under the equivalence relation
A\sim B\iff A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

A quotient is itself a Boolean algebra when equipped with the Boolean operators
[p]\wedge[q]:=[p\wedge q]
,
[p]\vee[q]:=[p\vee q]
,
[p]^{\prime}:=[p^{\prime}]
, and with
[0]
and
[1]
as the zero and unit respectively. The map
p\mapsto[p]
is a Boolean homomorphism. This map respects the partial order, and in fact
[p]\leq[q]
if and only if there exists
p^{*}\in[p]
such that
p^{*}\leq q
, or equivalently there exists
q^{*}\in[q]
such that

.

For any
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
(not necessarily a sub-algebra), define
[{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}]:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\}
. If
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space and
{\mathcal{M}}
is the kernel of

, the induced function

given by
\mu[A]:=\mu(A)
for all

is finitely additive. (Here and throughout the paper, parentheses delimiting a function argument are omitted when the argument is contained in square brackets.)

A new version of the monotone class theorem, which generalises the version in
keisler1977monotone
, is presented below. The new result makes use of the following definitions, some of which are non-standard.

A Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is said to be
countably complete
if every countable subset
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{A}}
has a least upper bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
. A subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
will here be called
countably complete
if every countable subset
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{B}}
has an upper bound in
{\mathcal{B}}
that is less than any other upper bound of this subset in
{\mathcal{A}}
. This upper bound is called the
supremum
of the subset and denoted
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
. In that case, it is straightforward to show (by taking complements) that every countable subset also has a lower bound in
{\mathcal{B}}
that is greater than any other lower bound of the subset in
{\mathcal{A}}
, called the
infimum
of the subset and denoted
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
. By definition these two elements are unique.

An important subtlety is that a proper subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
will not here be called countably complete if it is only true that every countable subset of
{\mathcal{B}}
has an upper bound in
{\mathcal{B}}
that is less than any other upper bound of that subset in
{\mathcal{B}}
: it must be less than any other upper bound of that subset in
{\mathcal{A}}
. The reason for this requirement is that, without it, the supremum of a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
of a subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
could differ from the supremum of
{\mathcal{C}}
when viewed as a subset of a distinct subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
. This can occur even if
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
are both countably complete algebras when the containing algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is ignored. Thus the requirement is needed to ensure the supremum of
{\mathcal{C}}
is uniquely defined across all countably complete subalgebras of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

A subset
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
will be called a
monotone class
if:

1.

for any non-decreasing sequence
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots
in
{\mathcal{M}}
, there is an upper bound in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is less than any other upper bound of the sequence in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and

2.

for any non-increasing sequence
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\ldots
in
{\mathcal{M}}
, there is a lower bound in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is greater than any other lower bound of the sequence in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Similarly to countably complete subalgebras, this least upper bound will be called the supremum of the sequence, denoted
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
, and this greatest lower bound will be called the infimum of the sequence, denoted
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
. The same word of caution is necessary here as for countably complete subalgebras:
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
must be less than any other upper bound of the sequence in
{\mathcal{A}}
, not just in
{\mathcal{M}}
, and similarly
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
must be greater than any other lower bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
, not just in
{\mathcal{M}}
.

In fact, the version of the monotone class theorem presented in
keisler1977monotone
also requires countably complete subalgebras and monotone classes to be understood in this sense, though this is not explicitly stated. Note the version of the monotone class theorem presented in that paper differs from the one below in requiring the containing algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
to be countably complete.

As stated above, the version of the monotone class theorem below is adapted from
keisler1977monotone
. There it is claimed that the result is proved in
halmos2013measure
; however this may be an example of mathematical folklore, as the result in that reference applies only to fields of sets, which are less general than Boolean algebras. A full proof is therefore provided.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a Boolean algebra and let
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
be a monotone class. Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
be a subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and define
\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
to be the smallest monotone class in
{\mathcal{A}}
that contains
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
. Then
\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})
is also the smallest countably complete subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
that contains
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.

Proof of the monotone class theorem depends on the following lemma, which is analogous to
(
halmos2013measure
, Thm. A, p. 27)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}
is a Boolean algebra, and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a subalgebra that is also a monotone class. Then
{\mathcal{M}}
is a countably complete subalgebra.

Proof:

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots\}
. Then, since
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Boolean algebra, the elements
p_{1},p_{1}\vee p_{2},p_{1}\vee p_{2}\vee p_{3},\ldots
are also in
{\mathcal{M}}
. These elements form an increasing sequence, hence this sequence has an upper bound in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is less than any other upper bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
. It may be checked this upper bound is also the supremum of
{\mathcal{C}}
, implying
{\mathcal{M}}
is countably complete.
∎

The following proof is derived from the proof of
(
halmos2013measure
, Thm. B, p. 27)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
:

It will be sufficient to show that
\sigma:=\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})
is a Boolean subalgebra, for then it will be countably complete by Lemma
4.1
, and in fact it will be the smallest countably complete subalgebra containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
because any smaller countably complete subalgebra containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
would also be a smaller monotone class containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.

For
q\in{\mathcal{A}}
, let

K(q)=\{p\in{\mathcal{A}}:p-q,q-p,q\vee p\in\sigma\}.

These sets possess a convenient symmetry:
p\in K(q)
if, and only if,
q\in K(p)
. Suppose
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence of elements in
K(q)
. Then
p_{k}\vee q\in\sigma
for all
k
, and since
\sigma
is a monotone class it follows that

\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}(p_{k}\vee q)=\Big(\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\Big)\vee q\in\sigma.

Similar arguments show that
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}-q,q-\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\in\sigma
as well, and it follows that
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\in K(q)
. A parallel argument shows that
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\in K(q)
, and thus
K(q)
is a monotone class. If
q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
, then
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\subseteq K(q)
, and thus, since
K(q)
is a monotone class,
\sigma\subseteq K(q)
. However, the symmetry mentioned above now implies that if
p\in\sigma
then
q\in K(p)
for any
q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
, and then, since
K(p)
is a monotone class, that
\sigma\subseteq K(p)
. This implies in particular that, for any
p,q\in\sigma
, the elements
p-q,q-p,q\vee p
are all in
\sigma
, and hence
q\wedge p\in\sigma
as well, since
q\wedge p=(q\vee p)-(p-q)-(q-p)
. It follows that
\sigma
is a Boolean subalgebra.
∎

5
Null modification

This section develops another analytic tool for studying chains in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
: a construction that is here called a
null modification
. A null modification takes a set in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
and constructs a new set of a form described in Property 9 of Proposition
2.6
, using Algorithm 1.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, Algorithm 1 decomposes
A
into disjoint sets
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
and
F\in{\mathcal{N}}
such that

1.

A=A^{\prime}\cup F
,

2.

\nu^{+}(A^{\prime})=\nu^{+}(A)
, and

3.

\nu_{N}(A^{\prime})\leq\nu^{+}(A^{\prime})
for all
N\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Moreover, if
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Algorithm 1
Given
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, construct
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
and
F\in{\mathcal{N}}

Set
A^{\prime}=F=\emptyset
.

for

N=1,2,\ldots

if

N\in A
then

Add
N
to
A^{\prime}

if

\nu_{N}(A^{\prime})>\nu^{+}(A)
then

Remove
N
from
A^{\prime}
and add it to
F
.

endif

endif

endfor

Proof:
Algorithm 1 trivially ensures
A^{\prime}
and
F
are disjoint,
A=A^{\prime}\cup F
and
\nu_{N}(A^{\prime})\leq\nu^{+}(A)
for all
N
. Moreover, if
N\in F
, then

\nu_{N}(A^{\prime})>\nu^{+}(A)-\frac{1}{N}.

Next show
F\in{\mathcal{N}}
as follows. This is trivial if
F
is a finite set, so suppose it is infinite. Fix
\epsilon>0
and choose
N_{\epsilon}
so that the following conditions are met:

1.

N_{\epsilon}\in F
,

2.

N_{\epsilon}>2/\epsilon
, and

3.

\nu_{N}(A)<\nu^{+}(A)+\epsilon/2
for all
N\geq N_{\epsilon}
.

Now for any
N\geq N_{\epsilon}
, if
N\in F
then

\displaystyle\nu_{N}(F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(A^{\prime})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(A)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}-\left(\nu^{+}(A)-\frac{1}{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{1}{N_{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\epsilon.

If
N\notin F
then

\nu_{N}(F)<\nu_{N^{\prime}}(F)<\epsilon

where
N^{\prime}
is the largest integer less than
N
for which
N^{\prime}\in F
, noting that
N^{\prime}\geq N_{\epsilon}
. Hence
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(F)=0
, implying
F\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Proposition
2.6
(6) gives
\nu^{+}(A^{\prime})=\nu^{+}(A)
. If
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
, Proposition
2.6
(6) gives
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A^{\prime})=\nu(A)
.
∎

Recalling the example given at the end of Section
2
, it can be checked that the set
{\mathcal{O}}
defined there, the set of all odd numbers at least 3, can be obtained by applying this algorithm to the set of all odd numbers. The null set removed by the algorithm is simply
\{1\}
.

Null modification can be used to transform chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
to acquire a useful topological property, defined in terms of the following pseudo-metric. Let
d_{\nu}(B,C):=\nu^{+}(B\triangle C)
for all
B,C\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Trivially,
d_{\nu}(B,B)=0
and
d_{\nu}(B,C)=d_{\nu}(C,B)
. The triangle inequality
d_{\nu}(A,B)+d_{\nu}(B,C)\geq d_{\nu}(A,C)
follows from the fact that

\begin{split}A\triangle C&=(A\setminus C)\cup(C\setminus A)\subseteq(A\setminus B\cup B\setminus C)\cup(C\setminus B\cup B\setminus A)\\
&=(A\setminus B\cup B\setminus A)\cup(C\setminus B\cup B\setminus C)=(A\triangle B)\cup(B\triangle C),\end{split}

so that
\nu_{N}(A\triangle C)\leq\nu_{N}(A\triangle B)+\nu_{N}(B\triangle C)
.

This pseudo-metric is related to the continuity of the set functions
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
on chains in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
or
{\mathcal{F}}
in the following sense.

Proposition 5.2
.

Consider a chain
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Then

1.

if
\inf\{d_{\nu}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}},A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}=0
, then
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})=\sup\{\nu^{+}(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
, and

2.

if
\inf\{d_{\nu}(A,\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}=0
, then
\nu^{-}(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})=\inf\{\nu^{-}(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
.

Moreover, if
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
and
\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, the converse of the first result holds, and if
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
and
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, the converse of the second result holds.

Proof:
For 1, first note
\sup\{\nu^{+}(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}\leq\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})
, since
\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})
for all
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
. To show the reverse inequality, fix
\epsilon>0
and choose
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that
d_{\nu}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}},A)<\epsilon
. By Proposition
2.6
(5),
d_{\nu}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}},A)\geq\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})-\nu^{+}(A)
, hence

\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})<\nu^{+}(A)+\epsilon\leq\sup\{\nu^{+}(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}+\epsilon.

Let
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
to obtain the first result. Then 2 follows by taking complements.

If
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
and
\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}}\setminus A)=\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})-\nu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
, by Proposition
2.6
(5). Taking infima gives

\inf\{d_{\nu}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}},A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}=\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})-\sup\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\},

implying the converse of 1. The converse of 2 follows by taking complements.
∎

The following theorem is the main result in this section, and it establishes that any chain
{\mathcal{T}}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
can be modified to produce a chain satisfying the conditions of Proposition

on all subchains
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. This technique will later be used to show that any integrable function

Theorem

Theorem 5.3

Given a chain

for all

\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle[A]=[B]

\displaystyle\iff\phi(A)=\phi(B)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle[A]<[B]

\displaystyle\iff\phi(A)\subset\phi(B),

3.

for all

(a)

\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}}))=\sup\{\nu(A):A\in\phi({\mathcal{S}})\}

(b)

\bigcap\phi({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}

for all

5.

if there exists a second map
\rho:{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\rho(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}
, then for all
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}
,

(a)

if
\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}}))=\sup\{\nu(A):A\in\rho({\mathcal{S}})\}
, then

\left(\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}},\mbox{ and }

(b)

if
\bigcap\rho({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}

\nu(\bigcap\rho({\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\rho({\mathcal{S}})\}
, then

\left(\bigcap\phi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcap\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}.

The construction of the map
\phi
involves first modifying the chain so that the conditions of Proposition
5.2
(1) hold for all subsets of the chain. To describe this construction, it will be convenient to introduce the following notation. Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}(A,B]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{C\in{\mathcal{C}}:A\subset C\subseteq B\}

to represent
sub-intervals
of a chain
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(N)
. Here
A,B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
but are not necessarily elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

It will also be convenient to define the
left end-points
of a chain
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
to be sets of the form
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}
, where
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
, such that:

1.

\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, and

2.

\exists\epsilon>0
such that for any
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
d_{\nu}(A,\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})<\epsilon\implies A\supseteq\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}
.

In other words, there is a “gap” of width at least
\epsilon
to the left of
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})
in
\nu({\mathcal{C}})
. A chain
{\mathcal{C}}
can have at most countably many left endpoints because there can be at most countably many disjoint sub-intervals in the interval
[0,1]
, corresponding to these gaps.

The subscripted
\cup
in Property 4 of the following lemma represents closure under unions, reprising the notation introduced in the paragraph before Proposition
3.1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider a countable chain
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(A)\neq\nu(B)
for distinct
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
. There exists a map
\psi:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}
such that:

1.

for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
\psi(A)\subseteq A
with
A\setminus\psi(A)\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

2.

for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
A\subseteq B\implies\psi(A)\subseteq\psi(B)
,

3.

for all
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
,

(a)

\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}}))=\sup\{\nu(A):A\in\psi({\mathcal{S}})\}
,

(b)

if
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
, then
\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\psi({\mathcal{S}})\}
,

4.

for all
A\in\psi({\mathcal{C}})_{\cup}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu(A)
, and

5.

if there exists a second map
\rho:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\rho(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
, then for any
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
with
\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}}))=\sup\{\nu(A):A\in\rho({\mathcal{S}})\}
,

\left(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Proof:

Without loss of generality, suppose
{\mathcal{C}}
contains its left end-points. Note the chain remains countable if its left end-points are added, and also retains the property
\nu(A)\neq\nu(B)
for distinct
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
. To see the latter claim, note that if a left end-point has the same charge as some
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
, then in fact
A
is already a left end-point of
{\mathcal{C}}
, and no new set need be added. The added end-points and their images under
\psi
can be discarded at the end of the construction, and the stated properties will be retained.

Arbitrarily order the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
as a sequence
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. (This assumes
{\mathcal{C}}
is infinite, but the proof that follows also applies if
{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, with minimal modification.) For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
B_{k}
to be the largest set in the sequence
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}
that is a proper subset of
C_{k}
, if such a set exists (that is,
B_{k}:=\bigcup\{C_{j}:j<k,C_{j}\subset C_{k}\}
). Otherwise, set
B_{k}:=\emptyset
.

Set
\psi_{0}(C)=C
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and assume inductively that
\psi_{k-1}({\mathcal{C}})\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. This is trivially true for
k=1
, since
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. Sequentially define
\psi_{k}
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

1.

Apply Algorithm 1 to decompose
A_{k}:=\psi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus\psi_{k-1}(B_{k})
into disjoint sets
A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
F_{k}\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

2.

For each
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, define

\psi_{k}(C):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi_{k-1}(C)\setminus F_{k}&\mbox{ if }C\in{\mathcal{C}}(B_{k},C_{k}]\\
\psi_{k-1}(C)&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Note
\psi_{k}(C)
differs from
C
by the removal of at most
k
null sets for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence
\psi_{k}({\mathcal{C}})\subset{\mathcal{F}}
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
\psi(C_{k}):=\psi_{k}(C_{k})
. Then
\psi(C_{k})\in{\mathcal{F}}
and Property 1 holds.

Also note that for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
A\subseteq B\implies\psi_{k}(A)\subseteq\psi_{k}(B)
. Moreover,
\psi_{j}(C_{k})=\psi_{k}(C_{k})
for all
j>k
. Hence Property 2 holds because if
C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}
, then

\psi(C_{j})=\psi_{\max\{j,k\}}(C_{j})\subseteq\psi_{\max\{j,k\}}(C_{k})=\psi(C_{k}).

Next note that for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\psi(C_{k})=\bigcup\{A_{j}^{\prime}:j\leq k,C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}\},

where the components of the union are disjoint. This is shown by induction. It is trivially true for
k=1
, since
\psi(C_{1})=A_{1}^{\prime}
. Given it is true for all
j<k
, then since
B_{k}\in\{\emptyset,C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}\}
,

\psi_{k-1}(B_{k})=\bigcup\{A_{j}^{\prime}:j\leq k-1,C_{j}\subseteq B_{k}\},

where the union is disjoint. Moreover,

\displaystyle\psi(C_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(C_{k})=\psi_{k-1}(C_{k})\setminus F_{k}=\psi_{k-1}(B_{k})\cup A_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup\{A_{j}^{\prime}:j\leq k,C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}\}

with
A_{k}^{\prime}
disjoint from
\psi_{k-1}(B_{k})
.

For all
k,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\nu_{N}(A_{k}^{\prime})\leq\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})
by Lemma
5.1
. Hence

\nu_{N}(\psi(C_{k}))=\sum_{j\leq k:C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}}\nu_{N}(A_{j}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{j\leq k:C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}}\nu(A_{j}^{\prime})=\nu(\psi(C_{k}))

by Proposition
2.6
(7). Property 3a thus follows by Proposition
2.6
(9).

To show 3b, first suppose
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}
is a left end-point of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Also note
\psi(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})\subseteq\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}})
, since
\psi(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})\subseteq\psi(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Hence

\displaystyle\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\psi(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{-}(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf\{\nu(\psi(A)):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\},

implying
\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\psi({\mathcal{S}})\}
. On the other hand, if
\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}
is not a left end-point of
{\mathcal{C}}
, then for any
\epsilon>0
there is
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
A\subset\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}
and
\nu(A)>\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})-\epsilon
. Hence

\displaystyle\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})-\epsilon

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\nu(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\psi(A))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{-}(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf\{\nu(\psi(A)):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\},

again implying
\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\psi({\mathcal{S}})\}
.

Consider any
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
, and define

A:=\bigcup\{\psi(C):C\in{\mathcal{S}}\}=\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}\psi(C_{k_{i}})

where
k_{1}
is the smallest positive integer
k
such that
\psi(C_{k})\subseteq A
,
k_{2}
is the smallest positive integer
k
such that
\psi(C_{1})\subset\psi(C_{k})\subseteq A
and so on. This sequence is infinite for

. It follows that
A=\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}A_{k_{j}}^{\prime}
,
where the union is disjoint, since for each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\psi(C_{k_{i}})=\cup_{j=1}^{i}A_{k_{j}}^{\prime}
is a disjoint union. Hence Property 4 holds because

\nu_{N}(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\nu_{N}(A_{k_{j}}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k_{j}}^{\prime})\leq\nu(A)

by Proposition
2.6
(8).

For 5, define a third map
\tau(A):=\psi(A)\cap\rho(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then since
\psi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
\rho(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
, one must have
\psi(A)\triangle\rho(A)\in{\mathcal{N}}
and then also
\tau(A)\triangle\psi(A)\in{\mathcal{N}}

2.6
(6). Moreover,
\rho(A)\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\tau(A)\in{\mathcal{F}}
for

, again by Proposition
2.6
(6). Now, for any
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
,

\displaystyle\nu^{+}\left(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})\setminus\bigcup\tau({\mathcal{S}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\nu\left(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})\setminus\tau(A)\right):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}}))-\sup\{\nu(\tau(A)):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}}))-\sup\{\nu(\psi(A)):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

using 3a. Noting
\bigcup\tau({\mathcal{S}})\subseteq\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})
, this implies

\left(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup\tau({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}.

If
\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}}))=\sup\{\nu(A):A\in\rho({\mathcal{S}})\}
, then one may apply a similar argument to

\left(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup\tau({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and hence

\left(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}.

One can now apply Lemma
5.1
twice - to a given chain and to the corresponding chain of complements - to obtain a chain that satisfies both the sufficient conditions of Proposition
5.2
(1) and (2): this strategy will be used to prove Theorem
5.3
below. The requirement that the chain be countable can also be removed, by an argument involving the following real analysis lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Totally ordered sets have the following properties.

1.

Any
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
contains a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
such that for
a\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
b,c\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
b\leq a\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon
.

2.

Consider a totally ordered set
{\mathcal{T}}
and a strictly increasing function
\mu:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then
{\mathcal{T}}
contains a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
such that for
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
B\leq A\leq C
and
\mu(C)-\mu(B)<\epsilon
.

Proof:

For 1, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
be a countable, dense subset of
{\mathcal{T}}
. (Such a subset exists since any subset of the reals is separable. Standard proofs of this invoke the axiom of countable choice.) Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}:\exists\epsilon>0\mbox{ with }(c-\epsilon/2,c)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\emptyset\},\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}:\exists\epsilon>0\mbox{ with }(c,c+\epsilon/2)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\emptyset\}.

Then
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
are both countable, since there cannot be an uncountable number of pairwise disjoint intervals of non-zero width contained in
\mathbb{R}
(each must contain a distinct rational). Thus
{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{C}}_{0}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is a countable set with the claimed property.

Claim 2 then follows by applying Claim 1 to
\mu({\mathcal{T}})
, and noting
\mu
is one-to-one and order preserving, as a consequence of being strictly increasing.
∎

Theorem
5.3
can now be proved as follows.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
:

Without loss of generality, suppose
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{T}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}
. (No generality is lost because for any chain
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, the chain
{\mathcal{S}}:={\mathcal{T}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}
has the property
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}
. Moreover, if the lemma holds for
{\mathcal{S}}
, it holds for
{\mathcal{T}}
.)

Let
[{\mathcal{C}}]
be the countable subchain of
[{\mathcal{T}}]:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{T}}\}
obtained by applying Lemma
5.2
(2) to the totally ordered set
[{\mathcal{T}}]
and the strictly increasing function
\nu:[{\mathcal{T}}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
\nu[A]:=\nu(A)
. Then let
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
be obtained by selecting exactly one element of
{\mathcal{T}}
from each of the equivalence classes in
[{\mathcal{C}}]
. (This implicitly invokes the axiom of countable choice, in general.)

Construct a map
\psi:{\mathcal{C}}^{c}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}
as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, and then a map
\psi^{\prime}:\psi({\mathcal{C}}^{c})^{c}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}
also as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
. Define
\phi^{\prime}(A):=\psi^{\prime}(\psi(A^{c})^{c})
for each
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

The map
\phi^{\prime}
inherits Properties 2, 3a, and 4 of Lemma
5.1
from
\psi^{\prime}
and
\psi
. Property 1 of that lemma must be weakened to
\phi^{\prime}(A)\triangle A
for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
, because
\phi^{\prime}
effectively adds a null set to
A
and then removes a null set. However, Property 3b of that lemma can be strengthened to the unconditional claim
\bigcap\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcap\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{S}})\}
for all
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
. This follows because
\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}}^{c})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup\psi({\mathcal{S}}^{c}))=\sup\{\nu(A):A\in\psi({\mathcal{S}}^{c})\}
, by Lemma
5.1
(3a) as it applies to
\psi
. Hence the condition of Lemma
5.1
(3b), as it applies to
\psi^{\prime}
, is satisfied for any
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
, that is
\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}^{c})^{c}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with

\nu(\bigcap\psi({\mathcal{S}}^{c})^{c})=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\psi({\mathcal{S}}^{c})^{c}\}.

Define

\phi(A):=\bigcup\{\phi^{\prime}(B):B\in{\mathcal{C}},[B]\leq[A]\}

for each
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Then
\phi
is an extension of
\phi^{\prime}
, because for any
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
[B]\leq[A]
, one must have
B\subseteq A
, since
{\mathcal{C}}
contains at most one element from each equivalence class. Hence
\phi(A)=\phi^{\prime}(A)
.

Note that for any
A,B\in{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle[A]=[B]

\displaystyle\implies\phi(A)=\phi(B)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle[A]<[B]

\displaystyle\implies\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(B).

This is immediate from the definition of
\phi
, and entails that
\phi
is order preserving.

To show 1, consider
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
. There exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
[B]\leq[A]\leq[C]
and
\nu(C)-\nu(B)<\epsilon/2
. If
[A]=[B]
then
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
\phi(A)=\phi^{\prime}(B)
. Moreover,
\phi^{\prime}(B)\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Hence
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Similarly, if
[A]=[C]
then
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Suppose
[B]<[A]<[C]
, implying
B\subset A\subset C
. Then
\phi^{\prime}(B)\subseteq\phi(A)\subseteq\phi^{\prime}(C)
, since
\phi
is order preserving, and
\nu(\phi^{\prime}(C))-\nu(\phi^{\prime}(B))<\epsilon/2
, using Proposition
2.6
(6). It follows that

\phi(A)\triangle A\subseteq(\phi^{\prime}(B)\triangle B)\cup(\phi^{\prime}(C)\setminus\phi^{\prime}(B))\cup(C\setminus B),

and

\nu^{+}(\phi(A)\triangle A)\leq\nu(\phi(B)\triangle B)+\nu(\phi^{\prime}(C))-\nu(\phi^{\prime}(B))+\nu(C)-\nu(B)<\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\phi(A)\triangle A\in{\mathcal{N}}
. This also implies
\phi({\mathcal{T}})\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, using Proposition
2.6
(6).

For 2, note

\phi(A)=\phi(B)\implies[\phi(A)]=[\phi(B)]\implies[A]=[B],

since
A\in[\phi(A)]
and
B\in[\phi(B)]
. This in turn gives

[A]<[B]\implies\phi(A)\neq\phi(B)\implies\phi(A)\subset\phi(B)

since it is already established that
[A]<[B]\implies\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(B)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\phi(A)\subset\phi(B)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\phi(A)\neq\phi(B)\mbox{ and }\phi(B)\not\subset\phi(A)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle[A]\neq[B]\mbox{ and }[B]\not<[A]

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle[A]<[B].

For 3a, consider
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. If
\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})=\phi(A)
for any
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
, then the result holds trivially, so assume
\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})\supset\phi(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
. But then for any
A\in{\mathcal{S}}
, there is
B\in{\mathcal{S}}
with
\phi(A)\subset\phi(B)\subset\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})
and
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
\phi(B)\subseteq\phi^{\prime}(C)\subset\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})
. Define
{\mathcal{B}}:=\{C\in{\mathcal{C}}:\phi^{\prime}(C)\subset\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})\}
, then
\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})=\bigcup\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{B}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
and

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\bigg(\bigcup\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{B}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\nu(\phi^{\prime}(C)):C\in{\mathcal{B}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\nu(\phi(A)):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}.

Property 3b follows by a similar argument.

For 4, consider
A\in\phi({\mathcal{T}})_{*}
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\epsilon>0
. Consider the case
A:=\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})
for some
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. If
\nu_{N}(A)=0
then trivially
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu(A)
, so assume
A
contains some element of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Then there is
B\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that
\nu_{N}(\phi(B))=\nu_{N}(A)
, since each element of
A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}
must be contained in some member of
\phi({\mathcal{S}})
. Moreover, there exists
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
[B]\leq[C]
and
\nu(C)-\nu(B)\leq\epsilon
. Putting this all together,

\nu_{N}(A)=\nu_{N}(\phi(B))\leq\nu_{N}(\phi(C))\leq\nu(\phi(C))\leq\nu(\phi(B))+\epsilon\leq\nu(A)+\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu(A)
.

On the other hand, if
A:=\bigcap\phi({\mathcal{S}})
for some
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
, then there is
B\in{\mathcal{S}}
with
\nu(\phi(B))-\nu(A)<\epsilon
, by Property 3b. Hence

\displaystyle\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(\phi(B))\big)+\big(\nu_{N}(\phi(B))-\nu(\phi(B))\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\nu(\phi(B))-\nu(A)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0+0+\epsilon,

where the first summand is less than or equal 0 because
A\subseteq\phi(B)
, and the second summand is less than or equal 0 by the first case. Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(A)
.

Property 5a follows by essentially the same argument as Lemma
5.1
(5), merely replacing
\psi
with
\phi
. Property 5b also follows by a similar argument, but using Property 3b instead of 3a.
∎

In the process of proving Theorem
5.3
, the following corollary emerges as an additional benefit. In essence, it establishes that any countable, pairwise disjoint collection of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
can be transformed into a similar collection on which
\nu
is countably additive. This corollary will later be used in the proofs of Theorems
6.4
and
8.1
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Consider pairwise disjoint sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. For each
k
, there exists
A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that

1.

A_{k}^{\prime}\subseteq A_{k}
with
F_{k}:=A_{k}\setminus A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

2.

A:=\cup_{k=j}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})
,

3.

\nu_{N}(A_{k}^{\prime})\leq\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})
for all
k,N\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu(A)
for all
N\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

4.

if there exists
\{A_{k}^{\prime\prime}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
such that

(a)

A_{k}\triangle A_{k}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and

(b)

\cup_{k=j}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\cup_{k=j}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k}^{\prime\prime})
,

then
\left(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime}\right)\triangle\left(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime\prime}\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Proof:
First consider the case
\nu(A_{k})>0
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then one may apply Lemma
5.1
to the chain
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
, where
B_{j}:=\cup_{k=1}^{j}A_{k}
for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
A_{1}^{\prime}=\psi(B_{1})
and
A_{k+1}^{\prime}:=\psi(B_{k+1})\setminus\psi(B_{k})
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Property 1 follows because the construction in the proof of Lemma
5.1
uses Algorithm 1 to remove the null set
F_{k}
from
A_{k}
to produce
A_{k}^{\prime}
. Property 2 follows from Lemma
5.1
(3a) with
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{C}}
. Property 3 is immediate from Lemma
5.1
(4). Property 4 follows from Lemma
5.1
(5), with
\rho(B_{j}):=\cup_{k=1}^{j}A_{k}^{\prime\prime}
for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Now consider the case
\nu(A_{k})=0
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For each such
k
, set
A_{k}^{\prime}:=\emptyset
and apply Lemma
5.1
as above to the remaining elements of
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
(ie. those with non-zero charge). Then Properties 1 and the first part of 3 hold for
A_{k}^{\prime}=\emptyset
, as for the sets with non-zero charge. Property 2 and the latter part of 3 are properties of
A:=\cup_{k=j}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime}
, to which sets with
A_{k}^{\prime}=\emptyset
make no contribution. Property 4 is also a property of
A
, and will therefore hold provided

\nu\bigg(\bigcup\{A_{k}^{\prime\prime}:\nu(A_{k})=0\}\bigg)=0.

This must be the case, otherwise condition 4b could not hold.
∎

6
The space
[{\mathcal{F}}]

This section reveals a useful property of the set
{\mathcal{F}}
, specifically that the quotient map
\xi:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
maps
{\mathcal{F}}
to a monotone class of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

Recall the notation

[{\mathcal{F}}]:=\{[A]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}:A\in{\mathcal{F}}\}.

The set function
\nu:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
induces a corresponding function on
[{\mathcal{F}}]
defined by

\nu[A]:=\nu(A),

This function is well defined, since if
[A]=[B]
and
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
B\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\nu(B)
by Properties 5 and 6 of Proposition
2.6
. Similarly the set functions
\nu^{+}:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow[0,1]
and
\nu^{-}:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow[0,1]
induce functions on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
defined by

\nu^{+}[A]:=\nu^{+}(A)\mbox{ and }\nu^{-}[A]:=\nu^{-}(A).

These set functions are well defined as a consequence of the following result.

Proposition 6.1
.

If
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
then
\nu^{+}(A)=\nu^{+}(B)
and
\nu^{-}(A)=\nu^{-}(B)
.

Proof:
For any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\nu_{N}(A\cup B)=\nu_{N}(A)+\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)=\nu_{N}(B)+\nu_{N}(A\setminus B).

Consequently,

\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(B)\rvert=\lvert\nu_{N}(A\setminus B)-\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)\rvert\leq\nu_{N}(A\setminus B)+\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)=\nu_{N}(A\triangle B).

But then

\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu_{N}(B)+\lvert\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(B)\rvert\leq\nu_{N}(B)+\nu_{N}(A\triangle B).

Taking the
\limsup
as
N\rightarrow\infty
gives

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)+\nu(A\triangle B)=\nu^{+}(B).

Similarly
\nu^{+}(B)\leq\nu^{+}(A)
and thus
\nu^{+}(A)=\nu^{+}(B)
. To show the corresponding result for
\nu^{-}
, note that
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
implies
A^{c}\triangle B^{c}\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and so

\nu^{-}(A)=1-\nu^{+}(A^{c})=1-\nu^{+}(B^{c})=\nu^{-}(B).

∎

Note
\nu^{+}[A]=\nu^{-}[A]
if and only if
[A]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, and if either statement holds then
\nu[A]=\nu^{+}[A]
. These properties are inherited from the corresponding set functions on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
.

As Proposition
6.1
and the preceding discussion suggest, many of the properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
carry over naturally to
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, in some cases with simplified or stronger statements. These are summarized in Proposition
6.3
below, the proof of which is straightforward and omitted. Note in particular that the final claim of Statement 5 below differs from the corresponding claim in Proposition
2.6
, because
\nu[A]=0\iff[A]=[\emptyset]
for
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Property 7 below refers to disjoint elements in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. This is conventional terminology for elements of an abstract Boolean algebra that have a meet of 0, but nevertheless the following definition may clarify the meaning of ‘disjoint’ in the present context.

Definition 6.2
.

Equivalence classes
[A],[B]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
are said to be disjoint if
[A]\wedge[B]=[\emptyset]
, or equivalently
A\cap B\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Note the two definitions are equivalent because
[A]\wedge[B]=[A\cap B]
, and the latter is equal to
[\emptyset]
if and only if
A\cap B\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Proposition 6.3
.

The collection
[{\mathcal{F}}]
and functions
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
have the following properties.

1.

[\emptyset],[{\mathbb{N}}]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, with
\nu[\emptyset]=0
and
\nu[{\mathbb{N}}]=1
.

2.

For all
[A]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,
[A]^{c}\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
with
\nu([A]^{c})=1-\nu[A]
.

3.

For all
[A],[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,
[A]\vee[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
if and only if
[A]\wedge[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, and if either is true then
\nu([A]\vee[B])=\nu[A]+\nu[B]-\nu([A]\wedge[B])
.

4.

If
[A],[B]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, then
\nu^{+}([A]\vee[B])\leq\nu^{+}[A]+\nu^{+}[B]
. If
[A]
,
[B]
, and
[A]\vee[B]
are all in
{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\nu([A]\vee[B])\leq\nu[A]+\nu[B]
.

5.

For
[A],[B]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
such that
[A]\leq[B]
,

(a)

\nu^{+}[A]\leq\nu^{+}[B]
,

(b)

\nu^{+}([B]-[A])\geq\nu^{+}[B]-\nu^{+}[A]
,

(c)

\nu^{-}[A]\leq\nu^{-}[B]
, and

(d)

\nu^{-}([B]-[A])\leq\nu^{-}[B]-\nu^{-}[A]
.

If in addition
[A],[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, then

(a)

\nu[A]\leq\nu[B]
,

(b)

[B]-[A]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,

(c)

\nu([B]-[A])=\nu[B]-\nu[A]
, and

(d)

\nu[A]=\nu[B]\iff[A]=[B]
.

6.

For
[A]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
,
\nu^{+}[A]=0\iff[A]=[\emptyset]
.

7.

For pairwise disjoint elements
[A_{1}],\ldots,[A_{K}]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,
\vee_{k=1}^{K}[A_{k}]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
with

\nu(\vee_{k=1}^{K}[A_{k}])=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu[A_{k}].

The properties related to countable additivity - Properties 8 to 10 of Proposition
2.6
- are not carried over to this setting because Theorem
6.4
below establishes something stronger: that
\nu
is countably additive on pairwise disjoint sequences in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
.

Theorem 6.4
.

For pairwise disjoint equivalence classes
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset[{\mathcal{F}}]
, the representative sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
can be chosen so that

1.

\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are pairwise disjoint, and

2.

A:=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

Moreover,
[A]
is the least upper bound of
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

Proof:
Consider pairwise disjoint
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset[{\mathcal{F}}]
. Then
A_{j}\cap A_{k}\in{\mathcal{N}}
for
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
j\neq k
, hence
A_{k}\cap(\cup_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j})\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
[A_{k}]=[A_{k}\setminus(\cup_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j})]
. Thus one may instead choose
\{A_{k}\setminus(\cup_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
to be the representative sets from each equivalence class to ensure Claim 1 holds. One may then use Corollary
5.4
to replace each representative set with a subset in the same equivalence class to ensure Claim 2 holds.

To see that
[A]
is the least upper bound of
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, first note
[A]
is an upper bound for this set because
A_{k}\subseteq A
for all
k
, and the quotient map respects the partial order. Suppose there is some other
B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
[A_{k}]\leq[B]
for all
k
. Define
A_{k}^{\prime}:=B\cap A_{k}
for each
k
, and
A^{\prime}:=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime}
. Then
[A^{\prime}]=[B\cap A]=[B]\wedge[A]
, giving
[A^{\prime}]\leq[B]
. Moreover, since both
[A]
and
[B]
are upper bounds for
[A_{k}]
, so is
[A^{\prime}]
, which implies
A_{k}\setminus A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Now, for any
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(A\setminus A^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(A\setminus\cup_{k=1}^{K}A_{k})+\nu^{+}(\cup_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\setminus A^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\cup_{k=K+1}^{\infty}A_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k}).

Letting
K\rightarrow\infty
gives
\nu^{+}(A\setminus A^{\prime})=0
and hence
[A]\leq[A^{\prime}]\leq[B]
.
∎

This theorem has the following useful corollary.

Corollary 6.5
.

[{\mathcal{F}}]
is a monotone class in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
on which
\nu
is countably additive.

Proof:
If
B_{1}\leq B_{2}\leq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, Theorem
6.4
implies the sequence has a supremum
B\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
. Similarly, if
C_{1}\geq C_{2}\geq\ldots
is a non-increasing sequence in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, then
C_{1}^{\prime}\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\leq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
with supremum
C^{\prime}\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, and then
C
is the infimum for
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. Thus
[{\mathcal{F}}]
is a monotone class. Countable additivity of
\nu
on
[{\mathcal{F}}]
follows from Theorem
6.4
(2).
∎

As a consequence of this corollary and the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras (Theorem
4.1
),
[{\mathcal{F}}]
contains the countably complete Boolean algebras generated by any Boolean algebra contained in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
. This fact is used in the proofs of Theorem
9.1
, Theorem
10.3
and Corollary
10.4
to construct complete and separable
L_{p}
spaces.

7
Cesàro limits as integrals

Up to this point, the paper has focused on binary sequences with Cesàro limits, but this and the remaining sections study general real-valued sequences with Cesàro limits. First, the definitions of
\nu_{N}
,
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
can be generalised as follows.

Definition 7.1
.

For any function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, define

\nu_{N}(f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n).

Also define

\nu^{+}(f):=\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(f),

and

\nu^{-}(f):=\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(f).

If the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
exists, or equivalently if
\nu^{+}(f)=\nu^{-}(f)
, define

\nu(f):=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(f)=\nu^{+}(f)=\nu^{-}(f).

Note
\nu_{N}(I_{A})=\nu_{N}(A)
,
\nu^{+}(I_{A})=\nu^{+}(A)
and
\nu^{-}(I_{A})=\nu^{-}(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Similarly,
\nu(I_{A})=\nu(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Some key concepts from the theory of charges will be required throughout what follows:
Peano-Jordan completion
,
outer charges
,
null functions
,
equivalence almost everywhere (a.e.)
and
dominance almost everywhere
. The following definitions are reproduced from
keith2022
. Similar definitions are standard in the literature on charges (see for example
bhaskararao1983
and
basile2000
).

Definition 7.2
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. The Peano-Jordan completion of
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is the charge space
(X,\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\overline{\mu})
where

\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\{A\subseteq X:\forall\epsilon>0,\exists B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}\mbox{ such that }B\subseteq A\subseteq C\mbox{ and }\mu(C\setminus B)<\epsilon\}

and

\overline{\mu}(A):=\sup\{\mu(B):B\subseteq A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}\}=\inf\{\mu(C):A\subseteq C,C\in{\mathcal{A}}\}.

A charge space is said to be Peano-Jordan complete if it is equal to its Peano-Jordan completion.

Peano-Jordan completion respects
{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu
in the following sense.

Proposition 7.3
.

Consider any field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. Then

1.

\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
,

2.

For all
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
,

\displaystyle\nu(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\nu(B):B\subseteq A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\nu(C):A\subseteq C,C\in{\mathcal{A}}\},\mbox{ and }

3.

({\mathbb{N}},\overline{{\mathcal{A}}},\nu)
is the Peano-Jordan completion of
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
.

Proof:
Given
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\epsilon>0
, choose
B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}\mbox{ such that }B\subseteq A\subseteq C\mbox{ and }\nu(C\setminus B)<\epsilon
. Then
\nu(B)=\nu^{-}(B)\leq\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(C)=\nu(C)
. Since
\nu^{+}(A)-\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu(C)-\nu(B)<\epsilon
, letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\nu^{+}(A)=\nu^{-}(A)
, and thus
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

For 2, given
A\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\epsilon>0
, choose
B,C\in{\mathcal{A}}
as before. Then
\nu(C)-\epsilon<\nu(B)\leq\nu(A)\leq\nu(C)<\nu(B)+\epsilon
. Taking
\epsilon
to
0
yields the result.

For 3, it is enough to note Statement 2 implies
\overline{\nu}=\nu
on
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
.
∎

Definition 7.4
.

A charge
\mu
on a field of subsets
{\mathcal{A}}
of a set
X
can be extended to an outer charge on
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
as follows

\mu^{*}(A):=\inf\{\mu(B):B\in{\mathcal{A}},A\subseteq B\}

for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X)
.

Definition 7.5
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. A null function is a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\mu^{*}(\{x\in X:\lvert f(x)\rvert>\epsilon\})=0

for all
\epsilon>0
.

Definition 7.6
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
be a charge space. Two functions
f,g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are said to be equal almost everywhere (abbreviated as
f=g
a.e.) if
f-g
is a null function. The function
f
is said to be dominated almost everywhere by
g
(abbreviated as
f\leq g
a.e.) if
f\leq g+h
, where
h
is a null function.

The
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
function spaces that are the focus of this paper can now be defined.

Definition 7.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a field of subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
and let
p\in[1,\infty)
. Define
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
to be the space consisting of all functions
h:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the following conditions hold.

1.

There exists a countable set
C\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

\{h^{-1}(y,\infty):y\in\mathbb{R}\setminus C\}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}.

2.

For all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
y\in(0,\infty)
such that

\nu^{+}(gI_{g^{-1}(y^{p},\infty)})<\epsilon,

where
g:=\lvert h\rvert^{p}
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}}):=K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})/\sim
, that is, the collection of equivalence classes of
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
under the equivalence relation
h_{1}\sim h_{2}\iff h_{1}=h_{2}
a.e., for
h_{1},h_{2}\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

The two conditions above resemble the characterisation of an integrable function in Theorem 3.9 of
keith2022
. The importance of the second condition can be seen in the following example. Let
f(k)=0
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and define

g(k):=\begin{cases}m&\mbox{ if $k=m^{2}$ is a perfect square}\\
0&\mbox{ if $k$ is not a perfect square}\end{cases}.

Then
f
and
g
differ only on the set of squares, which is a null set. Thus,
f=g
a.e. However, clearly
\nu(f)=0
, while
\nu(g)=\frac{1}{2}
, as can be shown in the following way. Given any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
m^{2}
be the largest perfect square that is no bigger than
k
. Since
g(k)=0
for
m^{2}<k<(m+1)^{2}
, it follows that
\nu_{(m+1)^{2}-1}(g)\leq\nu_{k}(g)\leq\nu_{m^{2}}(g)
. But then

\nu_{m^{2}}(g)=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}j=\frac{m(m+1)}{2m^{2}},

and

\nu_{(m+1)^{2}-1}(g)=\frac{1}{(m+1)^{2}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}j=\frac{m(m+1)}{2((m+1)^{2}-1)},

so
\lim_{m\to\infty}\nu_{m^{2}}(g)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\nu_{(m+1)^{2}-1}(g)=\frac{1}{2}
, and it follows that
\nu(g)=\frac{1}{2}
. Thus the analogy between Cesàro limits and integrals fails for some sequences, in the sense that two sequences that are equal almost everywhere may have different Cesàro limits. But the second condition in Definition
7.7
excludes
g
(and similar anomalies) from
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
for any field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, since
\lim_{N\to\infty}\nu_{N}(gI_{g^{-1}(y,\infty)})=\frac{1}{2}
for all
y\in(0,\infty)
.

Many of the properties of
L_{p}
spaces also hold for
K_{p}
spaces. To describe some of these, further definitions used in the theory of charges are required (again see
keith2022
;
bhaskararao1983
for more details).

Definition 7.8
.

A simple function on a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form
\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I_{A_{k}}
, for
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
, real numbers
c_{1},\ldots,c_{K}
and a partition of
X
into subsets
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
.

Definition 7.9
.

Let
f
and
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be real-valued functions on a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
. The sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is said to converge hazily to
f
, abbreviated as
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h,{\mathcal{A}}}f
or
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h}f
if
{\mathcal{A}}
is clear from the context, if for every
\epsilon>0
,

\mu^{*}(\{x:\lvert f_{n}(x)-f(x)\rvert>\epsilon\})\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. If
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of simple functions with
f_{n}\xrightarrow{h}f
, then
f
is said to be
T_{1}
-measurable. The set of all
T_{1}
-measurable functions on
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is denoted
L_{0}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

Definition 7.10
.

Any simple function on a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is integrable with integral

\int\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I_{A_{k}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\mu(A_{k}).

A general function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be integrable if there is a sequence of simple functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that:

1.

f_{n}\xrightarrow{h}f
, and

2.

\int\lvert f_{n}-f_{m}\rvert d\mu\rightarrow 0
as
n,m\rightarrow\infty
.

The integral is given by

\int fd\mu:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f_{n}d\mu.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
, the function space
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is the set of all
T_{1}
-measurable functions
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\lvert f\rvert^{p}
is integrable.

In what follows,
X={\mathbb{N}}
and
\mu
is the restriction of
\nu
to
{\mathcal{A}}
, hence
L_{0}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
will be abbreviated as
L_{0}({\mathcal{A}})
and
L_{p}({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
will be abbreviated as
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

Proposition 7.11
.

Consider a field
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. Let
r,p\in[1,\infty)
. Let
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and suppose
f
is a real-valued function on
{\mathbb{N}}
.

1.

K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
contains the simple functions with respect to

∎

As in the proof of Lemma 5.9 of

, define

\displaystyle A_{y}

Armed with this null modification, one of the main theorems of this paper can now be proved. Informally, the theorem establishes that
K_{p}
spaces are comprised of sequences for which Cesàro limits exist, that these limits determine a natural pseudonorm on
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and a norm on
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
, and that
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
can be isometrically embedded in
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

To state this theorem, the following notation is required. Let
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
denote the equivalence class of
h
in
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
(under equality almost everywhere), to distinguish it from the equivalence class
[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
of
h
in
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. In a similar manner, the pseudonorm of
h
in
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
will sometimes be denoted
\|h\|_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
and the norm of
[h]
in
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
will sometimes be denoted
\|[h]\|_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
, where needed to distinguish them from the pseudonorm and norm defined in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.13
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
be a field of sets and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then the following statements hold.

1.

K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is a vector subspace of
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

2.

For all
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
,
\nu_{N}(\lvert h\rvert^{p})
converges to
\int\lvert h\rvert^{p}d\nu
and
\nu_{N}(h)
converges to
\int hd\nu
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

3.

The function
\|\cdot\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}:K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by:

\|h\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}:=\left(\nu(\lvert h\rvert^{p})\right)^{1/p}

is a pseudonorm on
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

4.

For all
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
,

\|h\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}=\left(\int\lvert h\rvert^{p}d\nu\right)^{1/p}=\|h\|_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}.

That is, the embedding of
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
in
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is an isometry.

5.

For
h_{1},h_{2}\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
,
\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}=0
if and only if
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are equal a.e.

6.

The quotient space
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is a vector space with norm

\|[h]\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}:=\|h\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}.

The proof requires the following lemma, which establishes that Cesàro limits exist for all elements of
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
, not just those that satisfy the conditions of Lemma
7.1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Consider a field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. Then

1.

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{*}(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
,

2.

for any null function
h\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
,
\nu_{N}(h)
and
\nu_{N}(\lvert h\rvert)
converge as
N\rightarrow\infty
, with
\nu(h)=\nu(\lvert h\rvert)=0
,

3.

for any
f,g\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
with
f=g
a.e.

(a)

\nu^{+}(f)=\nu^{+}(g)
,

(b)

\nu^{-}(f)=\nu^{-}(g)
, and

(c)

\nu_{N}(f)
converges if and only if
\nu_{N}(g)
converges, in which case
\nu(f)=\nu(g)
.

Proof:

For 1, note for any
B\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
A\subseteq B
,
\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu(B)
. Hence

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\inf\{\nu(B):B\in{\mathcal{A}},A\subseteq B\}=\nu_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{*}(A).

For 2, it will be sufficient to prove the statement for
h\geq 0
, since then it applies to
h^{+}
and
h^{-}
, and thus to
h^{+}-h^{-}
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
h\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
, there is
y\in(0,\infty)
such that
\nu^{+}(hI_{h^{-1}(y,\infty)})<\epsilon/2
. Then

\displaystyle 0\leq\nu^{-}(h)\leq\nu^{+}(h)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(hI_{h^{-1}(0,\epsilon/2)})+\nu^{+}(hI_{h^{-1}(\epsilon/2,y)})+\nu^{+}(hI_{h^{-1}(y,\infty)})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}+y\nu^{+}(h^{-1}(\epsilon/2,y))+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon,

where
\nu^{+}(h^{-1}(\epsilon/2,y))\leq\nu^{*}(h^{-1}(\epsilon/2,\infty))=0
, since
h
is a null function. Let
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
to obtain the result.

For 3, note that if
f=g
a.e., then
h:=f-g
is a null function. Moreover,
h\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
by Proposition
7.11
(11). Thus
\nu^{+}(f)\leq\nu^{+}(g)+\nu(h)=\nu^{+}(g)
and
\nu^{-}(f)\geq\nu^{-}(g)+\nu(h)=\nu^{-}(g)
. Similarly,
\nu^{+}(g)\leq\nu^{+}(f)
and
\nu^{-}(g)\geq\nu^{-}(f)
, giving 3a and 3b, which together imply 3c.
∎

Theorem
7.13
can now be proved as follows.

Proof of Theorem
7.13
:

For 1, Proposition
7.11
(11) gives that

is a vector space, and Proposition

.

For 2, consider

. Then by Theorem

, there is

such that

a.e. with respect to

,
\nu_{N}(\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{p})
converges to

and
\nu_{N}(h^{\prime})
converges to

. But

, and thus Lemma

gives the result.

Properties 3 and 4 follow from 2, since

inherits the properties of a pseudonorm from

.

For 5, note

with

(see also Comment 1.5 of

Claim 6 follows from 5, since then the equivalence relation

∎

The next proposition lists additional properties of
K_{p}
spaces that are similar to familiar properties of
L_{p}
spaces. The proof of these is facilitated by Theorem
7.13
.

Proposition 7.14
.

Consider a field of subsets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, and let
f
and
g
be real-valued functions on
{\mathbb{N}}
.

1.

If
f,g\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
and
c,d\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\nu(cf+dg)=c\nu(f)+d\nu(g)
.

2.

If
f,g\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
and
f\leq g
a.e., then
\nu(f)\leq\nu(g)
.

3.

If
f,g\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
and
f=g
a.e. then
\nu(f)=\nu(g)
.

4.

(
Dominated Convergence Theorem I
) Suppose
g\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, and let
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of
T_{1}
-measurable functions on
{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert f_{k}\rvert\leq g
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
f_{k}\xrightarrow{h}f
if and only if
f\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and
\|f_{k}-f\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\rightarrow 0
.

5.

(
Dominated Convergence Theorem II
) Suppose
f,g\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, and let
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
satisfy
\lvert f_{k}\rvert\leq g
a.e. for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
f_{k}\xrightarrow{h}f
if and only if
\|f_{k}-f\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\rightarrow 0
.

Proof:

For 1, recall
cf+dg\in K_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
by Proposition
7.11
(11). The convergence of
\nu_{N}(f)
,
\nu_{N}(g)
and
\nu_{N}(cf+dg)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
follows from Theorem
7.13
(2), and then
\nu(cf+dg)=c\nu(f)+d\nu(g)
by the linearity of limits.

For 2, Theorem
7.13
(2) gives
\nu(f)=\int fd\nu\leq\int gd\nu=\nu(g)
. Property 3 then follows straightforwardly from Property 2.

For 4, note Proposition
7.11
(7) gives
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and Proposition
7.11
(5) gives
g\in L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. For the forward implication, Corollary 4.4.9 of
bhaskararao1983
gives that
f
is
T_{1}
-measurable, and then Proposition
7.11
(7)
gives
f\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\subseteq L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. Hence

\|f_{k}-f\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}=\|f_{k}-f\|_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\rightarrow 0.

where the first equality follows by Theorem
7.13
, and convergence to zero follows by the dominated convergence theorem for

spaces (Theorem 4.6.14 of
bhaskararao1983
). For the reverse implication, Theorem
7.13
gives
\|f_{k}-f\|_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}=\|f_{k}-f\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
for each

, hence dominated convergence gives
f_{k}\xrightarrow{h}f
. The proof of 5 is similar to that of Property 4.
∎

Theorem
7.13
provides an isometric embedding of
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
in
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
, and by implication, of
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
in
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. However, it leaves open the question of whether this embedding is surjective, and thus an isomorphism. The next theorem characterises fields of sets for which this embedding is surjective.

Theorem 7.15
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
be a field of sets and let
p\in[1,\infty)
.

1.

If
{\mathcal{N}}\subset\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
, then
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
with the isomorphism given by
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
for each
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

2.

The following statements are logically equivalent.

(a)

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with the isomorphism mapping
[f]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
to
[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, where
f
and
h
are as described in Theorem
7.12
.

(b)

\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
.

Proof:
For 1, Theorem
7.13
gives that
[h]_{K_{p}(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}})}
is an isometry, and Theorem
7.12
implies this map is surjective. Hence
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}})
. The result then follows by Proposition 1.8 of
basile2000
and Proposition
7.11
(2) (of this paper).

For 2, first suppose 2a. Then Statement 1 implies
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
, with the isomorphism given by
[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
for each
h\in K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
. But Theorem
7.12
gives
f=h
a.e. with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, hence
[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}=[f]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
. Chaining the isomorphisms gives
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with the isomorphism given by
[f]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[f]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, and then Theorem 5.10(2) of
keith2022
gives 2b.

Conversely, suppose 2b. Then Theorem 5.10(2) of
keith2022
gives
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with the isomorphism given by
[f]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[f]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
for each

. Apply Theorem
7.12
to

, then
[f]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}=[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
. Statement 1 then gives

, with the isomorphism mapping

. Inverting the latter isomorphism and chaining the isomorphisms gives 2a.
∎

Statement 2 of Theorem

identifies conditions under which

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
, but this requires augmenting the null sets to

Theorem 7.16
.

Consider a field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and

. Then

is a dense subspace of

. Moreover,

1.

if and only if
{\mathcal{N}}\subset\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
, and

2.

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with isomorphism
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
if and only if both of the following conditions hold:

(a)

\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, and

(b)

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
with isomorphism
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
.

Proof:
As argued in the proof of Theorem 5.10 in
keith2022
, for every simple function
s
with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, there is a simple function
s^{\prime}
with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
, such that
\|s-s^{\prime}\|_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}=0
. Recall
K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
contains the simple functions with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
(and therefore also the simple functions with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
) by Proposition
7.11
(1). Also note Theorem
7.13
(5) gives
\|s-s^{\prime}\|_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}=0
. Simple functions with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
are dense in
L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
by Theorem 4.6.15 of
bhaskararao1983
, and hence also dense in the subspace
K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
. But then so are simple functions with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
, implying
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is dense in
K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
.

To show 1, first note
{\mathcal{N}}\subset\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
if and only if
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
(Lemma 5.7 of
keith2022
). So suppose
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Proposition
7.11
(2) gives

K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))=K_{p}(\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})})=K_{p}(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}})=K_{p}({\mathcal{A}}).

Conversely, suppose
K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))=K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
, and consider
A\in\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. Then
I_{A}\in K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
by Proposition
7.11
(1), hence
I_{A}\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. By Definition
7.7
, there is
y\in(0,1)
such that
A=I_{A}^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

For 2, first suppose
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
with the isomorphism given by
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, and consider
A\in\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
. As in the proof of 1,
I_{A}\in K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
, hence
[I_{A}]\in{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
. By assumption, there exists
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
such that
[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}=[I_{A}]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, which implies
h=I_{A}
a.e. with respect to
\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. There exists some
y\in(0,1)
such that
I_{A}^{-1}(y,\infty)\triangle h^{-1}(y,\infty)\in{\mathcal{N}}
(by Theorem 3.11 of
keith2022
) and also such that
h^{-1}(y,\infty)\in\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
(by Theorem 3.4 of
keith2022
, since
h
is
T_{1}
-measurable with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
). Hence
A=I_{A}^{-1}(y,\infty)
differs from a set in
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
by a null set. This implies
A\in\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, by Lemma 5.3 of
keith2022
. Thus
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}\subseteq\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
. Moreover,
\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
, since
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
and
{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}
, giving
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
.

By Theorem 5.10 of
keith2022
,
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with isomorphism
[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, and then by Theorem
7.15
,

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}}).

Moreover, the first two isomorphisms map

[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}

for
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. Since
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\subseteq L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
(Proposition
7.11
(5)), the third isomorphism maps
[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, and hence its inverse maps
[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
. The combined isomorphism maps
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}
.

For the converse, 2(a) implies
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with isomorphism
[h]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
, by Theorem 5.10 of
keith2022
. Combining this with 2(b) gives
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
, with the isomorphism given by
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
. Theorem
7.15
gives
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
with isomorphism
[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}\mapsto[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
for
h\in K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
. Since
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\subseteq K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\subseteq L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
(by Proposition
7.11
(5)), the inverse of the latter isomorphism maps
[h]_{L_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}\mapsto[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
for each
h\in K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. Hence chaining these isomorphisms gives
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
with isomorphism
[h]_{K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[h]_{K_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))}
.
∎

Theorems
7.15
and
7.16
imply that many of the properties of
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces can be learned through study of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces. However, as several of the results in this section have demonstrated, care must be taken in adapting theorems for
L_{p}
spaces to obtain theorems for
K_{p}
spaces, when those theorems involve equality or dominance of functions almost everywhere (as many do). The
K_{p}
spaces are in general proper subsets of
L_{p}
spaces, and this may necessitate modifying the conditions or conclusions of key theorems when adapting them for
K_{p}
spaces.

The remainder of this paper focuses on the completeness and separability of
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces, and by implication the
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces isomorphic to them.

8
L_{p}
spaces over
\sigma
-fields on which
\nu
is countably additive

This short section considers the situation in which
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
is a measure space, that is,
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
\sigma
-field and
\nu
is countably additive. The
L_{p}
spaces in this case have a particularly simple form. Theorem
8.1
characterises those fields of sets for which this situation occurs, and Theorem
8.2
characterises functions that are measurable (in the standard measure theory sense) with respect to such a field.

Theorem 8.1
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is a
\sigma
-field. Then the following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu
is countably additive on
{\mathcal{A}}
.

2.

{\mathcal{A}}
is generated by a countable partition
{\mathcal{S}}
of
{\mathbb{N}}
(that is,
{\mathcal{A}}=\sigma({\mathcal{S}})
) such that
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{S}}}\nu(A)=1
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Suppose
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
is a measure space, where
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. Then measurable functions on
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
are of the form

f=\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{j}I_{A_{j}}+g,

where
J
may be finite or (countably) infinite,
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
A_{j}\in{\mathcal{A}}
with
\nu(A_{j})>0
for each
j
, the sets
\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
are pairwise disjoint, and
g
is non-zero only on a null set disjoint from
\cup_{j=1}^{J}A_{j}
.

The proofs of these two theorems take advantage of the fact
{\mathbb{N}}
is countable. The following two lemmas identify consequences of this for all
\sigma
-fields in
{\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Any
\sigma
-field
{\mathcal{A}}
comprised of subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
is closed under arbitrary unions (that is, including uncountable unions).

Proof:

Consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
and let
C:=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
. For each
n\in C
, there exists some
C_{n}\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
n\in C_{n}\subseteq C
. Hence
C=\cup_{n\in C}C_{n}
. Since
C
is countable, this is a countable union and hence
C\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Any
\sigma
-field
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is generated by a countable partition of
{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof:

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
A_{k}
be the intersection of all sets in
{\mathcal{A}}
that contain
k
. Then
A_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}
by Lemma
8.1
. The collection
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
generates a
\sigma
-field
\{\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}:{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}\}
that contains every element of
{\mathcal{A}}
and hence
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}:{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}\}
. For distinct
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, either
A_{j}=A_{k}
or
A_{j}\cap A_{k}=\emptyset
, otherwise
A_{j}\setminus A_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}
would be a proper subset of
A_{j}
containing
j
. Thus
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
forms a partition of
{\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

These properties of
\sigma
-fields over
{\mathbb{N}}
entail that requiring
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
to be a measure space imposes severe restrictions on the structure of
{\mathcal{A}}
. These are described in Theorem
8.1
, which can now be proved.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
:

(1\implies 2)
By Lemma
8.2
,
{\mathcal{A}}
is generated by a countable partition
{\mathcal{S}}
of
{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus
\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{S}}}\nu(A)=\nu({\mathbb{N}})=1
by the countable additivity of
\nu
on
{\mathcal{A}}
.

(2\implies 1)
Suppose
{\mathcal{S}}=\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. (This assumes
{\mathcal{S}}
is infinite, but the proof that follows also works for finite
{\mathcal{S}}
, with minor modifications.) Recall
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}:{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}\}
. Thus it is sufficient to show
\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\nu(A_{k})
for any
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
.

Apply Corollary
5.4
to obtain sets
\{A_{k}^{\prime}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\{F_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
such that for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

1.

A_{k}=A_{k}^{\prime}\cup F_{k}
,

2.

\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})=\nu(A_{k})
,

3.

\nu_{N}(A_{k}^{\prime})\leq\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and

4.

\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with

\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}F_{k})=\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\setminus\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime})=\nu({\mathbb{N}})-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})=0.

By Proposition
2.6
(9),
\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with

\nu(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}^{\prime})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\nu(A_{k}),

for any
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence

\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}=(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}^{\prime})\cup(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}F_{k})\in{\mathcal{F}}

with

\nu(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k})=\nu(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}A_{k}^{\prime})+\nu(\cup_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}F_{k})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{I}}}\nu(A_{k})

as required.
∎

As a consequence,
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces are rather simple when
({\mathbb{N}},{\mathcal{A}},\nu)
is a measure space, since all functions have the form described in Theorem
8.2
. This can be proved as follows.

Proof of Theorem
8.2
:

Let
f\in L_{0}({\mathcal{A}})
. Then the sets
\{f^{-1}(a)\}_{a\in\mathbb{R}}
form a partition of
{\mathbb{N}}
, and only countably many of them can be non-empty by Theorem
8.1
. Hence

f=\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{j}I_{A_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}b_{j}I_{B_{j}},

where
J
and
K
may be finite or infinite,
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}\cup\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}
, and
\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}\cup\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
are disjoint sets with
\nu(A_{j})>0
for each
j
and
\nu(B_{j})=0
for each
j
. But then
\cup_{j=1}^{K}B_{j}\in{\mathcal{N}}
by countable additivity.
∎

The
L_{p}
spaces comprised of such functions provide first examples of complete, separable
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces. For any
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
, these spaces are complete by Corollary 3.7 of
keith2022
. They are also separable, since the simple functions with rational coefficients form a countable, dense subset of
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
.

9
Completeness of
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})

This section characterises complete
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces. It contains two complementary theorems. The first of these embeds
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
in a conventional Lebesgue function space, and thus provides access to the familiar properties and theorems of Lebesgue integration for application to functions in
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. This is desirable, because although many of the properties and theorems of the Lebesgue integral have analogues in charge spaces (see
leader1953
,
appling1974
,
chen1976
,
karandikar1982
and
karandikar1988
for some examples), these analogous results typically have different or additional conditions that make them more difficult to use, or at least less familiar.

The first theorem and its proof refer to the concept of a
representation
of the Boolean quotient
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, that is, a Boolean isomorphism
\phi
mapping
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
to a field of subsets of some set
Y
. Stone’s representation theorem (originally proved in
stone1937
, but
givant2009
and
sikorski1969boolean
contain helpful expositions) is a fundamental result asserting that every abstract Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is isomorphic to a field of sets. More specifically,
{\mathcal{A}}
can be embedded in
{\mathcal{P}}(S_{{\mathcal{A}}})
, where
S_{{\mathcal{A}}}
is a set called the
Stone space
of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Stone spaces can be constructed in various equivalent ways, but the details are not needed here. It will be sufficient throughout what follows to work with an unspecified representation of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

The theorem and proof also refer to the set
[{\mathcal{F}}]
defined in Section
6
, and the set
\langle{\mathcal{F}}\rangle_{\phi}:=\{\langle A\rangle_{\phi}:A\in{\mathcal{F}}\}
, where
\langle A\rangle_{\phi}:=\phi[A]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Consider a field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
. Let
\phi:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(Y)
be a representation of the Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. Then

1.

\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])\subset\langle{\mathcal{F}}\rangle_{\phi}
,

2.

\big(Y,\overline{\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])},\overline{\nu}\big)
is a complete measure space,

3.

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\overline{\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])},\overline{\nu}\big)
is a Lebesgue function space,

4.

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is isometrically isomorphic to a dense subspace of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
, and

5.

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is complete if and only if
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
.

The proof uses the following lemma, which resembles a well known result sometimes set as an exercise for students of measure theory. Recall that the symbol ‘+’ in this context represents exclusive disjunction, that is
p+q=(p\wedge q^{\prime})\vee(p^{\prime}\wedge q)
, where
p
and
q
are elements of a Boolean algebra.

Lemma 9.1
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}
is a Boolean algebra, and
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
is a monotone class on which a non-negative, bounded, countably additive function
\mu
is defined. Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
be a sub-algebra. Then for any
B\in\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
such that
\mu(B+A)<\epsilon
.

Proof:

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the collection of elements
B\in{\mathcal{M}}
such that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
with
\mu(B+A)<\epsilon
. Let
\{B_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a non-decreasing sequence in
{\mathcal{B}}
and choose
\epsilon>0
. Set
B:=\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{k}
. Then there is
B_{k}
with
\mu(B+B_{k})<\epsilon/2
and
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
with
\mu(B_{k}+A)<\epsilon/2
. Hence
\mu(B+A)\leq\epsilon
, implying
{\mathcal{B}}
is a monotone class containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
, and hence
\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}
by the monotone class theorem (Theorem
4.1
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
9.1
:

First note
\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]\subset[{\mathcal{F}}]
with
\nu
countably additive on
\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]
, by Corollary
6.5
and the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras (Theorem
4.1
). Then
\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])\subset\langle{\mathcal{F}}\rangle_{\phi}
. Moreover
\big(Y,\overline{\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])},\overline{\nu}\big)
is a complete measure space by Lemma 4.1 of
keith2022
. Corollaries 3.5 and 3.6 of
keith2022
give that
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\overline{\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])},\overline{\nu}\big)
is a Lebesgue function space, which is therefore complete (Corollary 3.7 of
keith2022
). Proposition 1.8 of
basile2000
then gives
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\overline{\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])},\overline{\nu}\big)={\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
.

Define a map
\rho:{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
in two stages: first for equivalence classes of simple functions, then for general equivalence classes of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
. To define
\rho
for the equivalence class of a simple function
f=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I_{A_{k}}
, suppose without loss of generality that
\nu(A_{k})>0
and
c_{k}\neq 0
for each
k
, and that the
\{c_{k}\}
are distinct and in increasing order. (One can discard terms for which
\nu(A_{k})=0
or
c_{k}=0
, merge sets with equal coefficients and reorder to obtain a simple function with these properties in the same equivalence class
[f]
.) Then define

\rho[f]:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}[I_{\langle A_{k}\rangle}].

This mapping is well defined since if
[f]
contains another simple function
f^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{K^{\prime}}c_{k}^{\prime}I_{A_{k}^{\prime}}
(again with
\nu(A_{k}^{\prime})>0
and
c_{k}^{\prime}\neq 0
for each
k
and distinct
\{c_{k}^{\prime}\}
in increasing order) then

\sum_{k=1}^{K^{\prime}}c_{k}^{\prime}[I_{\langle A_{k}^{\prime}\rangle}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}[I_{\langle A_{k}\rangle}].

To see this, observe that since there are only finitely many values of
\{c_{k}\}
and
\{c_{k}^{\prime}\}
, for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
one must have

\nu^{*}(\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:f(n)\neq f^{\prime}(n)\})=\nu^{*}(\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\lvert f(n)-f^{\prime}(n)\rvert>\epsilon\})=0,

since
f
and
f^{\prime}
are equal a.e. But this is only possible if
K=K^{\prime}
,
c_{k}=c_{k}^{\prime}
, and
A_{k}\triangle A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
(hence
\langle A_{k}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle A_{k}\rangle
) for
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

The mapping
\rho
is an isometry for simple functions in the case
p\in[1,\infty)
, since

\displaystyle\|\rho[f]\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert c_{k}\rvert^{p}I_{\langle A_{k}\rangle}d\nu=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert c_{k}\rvert^{p}\nu\langle A_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert c_{k}\rvert^{p}\nu(A_{k})=\|f\|_{p}^{p}=\|[f]\|_{p}^{p}.

Likewise,
\rho
is an isometry for simple functions in the case
p=0
, where the metrics on
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}({\mathcal{A}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
are of the form described immediately following Definition 2.10 of
keith2022
. To see this, note that for simple functions
f,f^{\prime}\in L_{0}({\mathcal{A}})
, one can express
d(\rho[f],\rho[f^{\prime}])
and
d(f,f^{\prime})
as parallel functions of finite sets of real numbers of the form
\{\nu\langle A_{k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
\{\nu(A_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
respectively, and then use
\nu\langle A_{k}\rangle=\nu(A_{k})
for each
k
.

To define
\rho
for general
[f]\in{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
, note there is a sequence of simple functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
that converges hazily to
f
. Then

d(\rho([f_{n}]),\rho([f_{m}]))=d(f_{n},f_{m})\rightarrow 0

as
m,n\rightarrow 0
. Thus
\{\rho([f_{n}])\}_{n\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence in the metric space
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
, which is complete. Define
\rho([f])
to be the limit of this Cauchy sequence. Then
\rho
is well defined, since if there is some other sequence of simple functions
\{f_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
that converges to
f^{\prime}\in[f]
, it follows that

\displaystyle d(\rho([f_{n}]),\rho([f_{n}^{\prime}]))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(f_{n},f_{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(f_{n},f)+d(f,f^{\prime})+d(f_{n}^{\prime},f^{\prime}).

The middle summand is identically zero since
f\sim f^{\prime}
, and the other two terms converge to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Hence
\rho([f_{n}^{\prime}])
converges to the same limit as
\rho([f_{n}])
.

To show
\rho
is an isometry for general
[f]\in{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
in the case
p\in[1,\infty)
, note that for any
n
,

\displaystyle\lvert\|\rho[f]\|_{p}-\|[f]\|_{p}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lvert\|\rho[f]\|_{p}-\|\rho[f_{n}]\|_{p}\rvert+\lvert\|\rho[f_{n}]\|_{p}-\|[f_{n}]\|_{p}\rvert

\displaystyle+\lvert\|[f_{n}]\|_{p}-\|[f]\|_{p}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\rho[f]-\rho[f_{n}]\|_{p}+\|f-f_{n}\|_{p}

where the middle summand on the right hand side of the first inequality is identically zero since
\rho
is an isometry for simple functions. But the final line goes to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, the first summand goes to zero by dominated convergence (Theorem 4.6.14 of
bhaskararao1983
), noting
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
can be chosen to be dominated by
f
, using Theorem 3.4 of
keith2022
. Hence
\|\rho[f]\|_{p}=\|[f]\|_{p}
. For
p=0
and general
[f],[g]\in{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
,

\displaystyle d(\rho[f],\rho[g])

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(\rho[f],\rho[f_{n}])+d(\rho[f_{n}],\rho[g_{n}])+d(\rho[g_{n}],\rho[g])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(\rho[f],\rho[f_{n}])+d([f_{n}],[g_{n}])+d(\rho[g_{n}],\rho[g])

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d([f_{n}],[g_{n}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d([f],[g]).

By a similar argument,
d([f],[g])\leq d(\rho[f],\rho[g])
.

Lemma
9.1
implies any
[A]\in\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]
can be approximated arbitrarily closely by some
[A_{\epsilon}]\in[{\mathcal{A}}]
. Hence any simple function with respect to
\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])
can be approximated arbitrarily closely (in the
p
pseudo-norm or pseudo-metric
d
) by a simple function with respect to
\phi[{\mathcal{A}}]
. The simple functions with respect to
\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])
are dense in
L_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
by Theorem 4.6.15 of
bhaskararao1983
, hence so are the simple functions with respect to
\phi[{\mathcal{A}}]
. But then the equivalence classes of simple functions with respect to
\phi[{\mathcal{A}}]
are dense in
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
, that is, the images under
\rho
of the equivalence classes of simple functions with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
are dense in
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
. Consequently,
\rho({\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}}))
is dense in
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is complete. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
, since
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is dense in
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
. Conversely, suppose
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is complete because
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\big(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu\big)
is complete.
∎

The above theorem entails that
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
spaces, and the
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}
spaces to which they are isomorphic, can ultimately be embedded in conventional Lebesgue function spaces on a complete measure space. Alternatively, instead of being “mapped forward” using a representation
\phi
,
\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]
can be “mapped back” into
{\mathcal{F}}
, from which a complete
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
space on
{\mathbb{N}}
can be constructed.

Recall the definition
\xi(A):=[A]
for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Note
\xi^{-1}[{\mathcal{B}}]=\alpha({\mathcal{B}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
for any field of sets
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, by Lemma 5.3 of
keith2022
, since
\xi^{-1}[{\mathcal{B}}]=\{A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}}):A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}\mbox{ for some }B\in{\mathcal{B}}\}
.

Theorem 9.2
.

Consider a field of subsets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
. Define
\lambda({\mathcal{A}}):=\xi^{-1}(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])
. Then the following claims hold.

1.

\lambda({\mathcal{A}})\subset{\mathcal{F}}
.

2.

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))
is complete.

3.

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is isometrically isomorphic to a dense subspace of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))
, with the isomorphism mapping
[f]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[f]_{L_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))}
.

4.

The following statements are logically equivalent:

(a)

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is complete,

(b)

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
with isomorphism
[f]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[f]_{L_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))}
,

(c)

\overline{\lambda({\mathcal{A}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
, and

(d)

{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))
is complete and
\overline{\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})}=\alpha(\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})
.

Moreover, if any of 4(a)-(d) holds, then

{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}}))\cong{\mathcal{K}}_{p}(\alpha({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{N}})).

Proof:
As in the proof of Theorem
9.1
,
\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]\subset[{\mathcal{F}}]
, hence
\xi^{-1}(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])\subset{\mathcal{F}}
.

Since
\lambda({\mathcal{A}})/{\mathcal{N}}=[\lambda({\mathcal{A}})]=\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]
and
\nu
is countably additive on

,
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))
is complete by Theorem 4.2(2) of
keith2022
(noting

is complete if and only if

is complete by Theorem 3.4 of
basile2000
). Let

be a representation of the Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. By Theorem

,
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu)
and

is isometrically isomorphic to a dense subspace of

. Also note the former two isomorphisms map

and

to the same element of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(Y,\phi(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]),\nu)
, for each

, hence the latter isomorphism maps
[f]_{L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})}\mapsto[f]_{L_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))}
.

(4a\implies 4b)
Suppose
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is complete. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})\cong{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))
because
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
is isometrically isomorphic to a dense subspace of
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))
.

(4b\implies 4a)
This is immediate from the fact
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}(\lambda({\mathcal{A}}))
is complete.

(4b\implies 4c)
This follows by an argument familiar from the proof of Theorem
7.16
(2) (and also Theorem 5.10(2) of
keith2022
). Consider

basile2000

10
Separability of
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})

This section concerns sufficient conditions for
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
to be separable, that is, to have a countable dense subset. Even conventional Lebesgue function spaces are not separable in general; however, there is a well known sufficient condition, described in the following definition and proposition.

Definition 10.1
.

A charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is said to be separable if there is a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that for any
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there is
A_{\epsilon}\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
\mu(A\triangle A_{\epsilon})<\epsilon
.

Proposition 10.2
.

If a charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is separable, then
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is separable for
p\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
.

Proving the preceding lemma for measure spaces is sometimes set as an exercise for students of measure theory. The proof also applies with minimal modification to the
L_{p}
spaces constructed on a charge space, and involves constructing a countable dense subset of
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, comprised of simple functions with rational coefficients and indicator functions only for sets in the countable set
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
described in Definition
10.1
. Proving these simple functions are dense in
L_{p}(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is straightforward with the aid of Theorem 4.6.15 of
bhaskararao1983
.

One natural way to generate a field in
{\mathcal{F}}
for which the corresponding
L_{p}
and
K_{p}
spaces are complete and separable, is to start with a countable field
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, generate the countably complete Boolean algebra
\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}]
, and then map it back to the inverse image
\xi^{-1}(\sigma[{\mathcal{A}}])
. This strategy is implemented in the following theorem.

Theorem 10.3
.

Consider a field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. The following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

There is a chain
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that
[{\mathcal{A}}]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{T}})]
.

2.

There is a countable set
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that
[{\mathcal{A}}]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]
.

Moreover, if either statement holds then
{\mathcal{A}}
is separable.

The proof of Theorem
10.3
requires the following lemma, which provides several alternative characterisations of countable fields. The lemma requires the following definitions. Consider a Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
. A
partition of 1
is a collection
\Delta
of pairwise disjoint elements of
{\mathcal{A}}
with supremum 1. A partition of 1
\Delta_{1}
is said to be a
refinement
of a partition of 1
\Delta_{2}
, written
\Delta_{1}\leq\Delta_{2}
, if for every
p_{1}\in\Delta_{1}
there is
p_{2}\in\Delta_{2}
such that
p_{1}\leq p_{2}
.

Statement 4 of the following lemma uses the notation
\alpha({\mathcal{C}})
to represent the subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
generated by
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
, generalising notation used earlier in this paper for fields of sets.

Lemma 10.1
.

Consider a Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
. The following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

{\mathcal{A}}
is countable.

2.

{\mathcal{A}}
has a countable generating set.

3.

There is a non-decreasing sequence of finite subalgebras
\{{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
.

4.

There is a sequence
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of finite partitions of 1 such that
\Delta_{k+1}\leq\Delta_{k}
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
{\mathcal{A}}=\alpha(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k})
.

5.

{\mathcal{A}}
is generated by a countable chain
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof:

(1\implies 2)
This is trivial, since
{\mathcal{A}}
can be its own generating set.

(2\implies 3)
Let
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
be a countable generating set for
{\mathcal{A}}
. (Note
{\mathcal{C}}
may be finite, since
p_{j}
need not be distinct from
p_{k}
for
j\neq k
.) For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}=\alpha(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
. Then
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}_{2}\subseteq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence of finite subalgebras. Moreover,
{\mathcal{A}}=\alpha({\mathcal{C}})=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
, since each element of
{\mathcal{A}}
may be expressed in terms of elementary operations applied to a finite subset of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

(3\implies 4)
Since
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
is finite, the atoms of
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
form a finite partition
\Delta_{k}
of 1. Then each element of
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
is a finite disjunction of elements of
\Delta_{k}
, so
\alpha(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k})=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
. Also, since
{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}_{k+1}
, each element of
\Delta_{k}
is a disjunction of atoms of
{\mathcal{A}}_{k+1}
, so
\Delta_{k+1}\leq\Delta_{k}
.

(4\implies 5)
For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Delta_{k}:=\{\Delta_{k,1},\ldots,\Delta_{k,n_{k}}\}
, with
\{n_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
a non-decreasing sequence. Without loss of generality, suppose each partition is ordered so that the elements of
\Delta_{k+1}
that form
\Delta_{k,1}
by disjunction are enumerated first, then the elements of
\Delta_{k+1}
that form
\Delta_{k,2}
by disjunction, and so on. Then the set

{\mathcal{T}}:=\{\vee_{j=1}^{i}\Delta_{k,j}:k\in{\mathbb{N}},i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{k}\}\}

forms a countable chain in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Moreover,
\Delta_{k,j}
is a difference of elements of
{\mathcal{T}}
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{k}\}
, and every
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
is a finite disjunction of elements of
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k}
. Thus
\alpha(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k})=\alpha({\mathcal{T}})
.

(5\implies 1)
Each element of
{\mathcal{A}}
is formed by performing basic operations on a finite subset of
{\mathcal{T}}
, and thus the elements of
{\mathcal{A}}
can be systematically enumerated.
∎

Theorem
10.3
can now be proved as follows.

Proof of Theorem
10.3
:

(1\implies 2)
Given
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
, one may obtain a countable subchain
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
such that for any
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
there are
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
[B]\leq[A]\leq[C]
and
\nu(C)-\nu(B)<\epsilon
, as in the proof of Theorem
5.3
. Clearly
\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]\subseteq\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{T}})]
. Now consider
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and define
B_{0}:=\bigvee\{[B]:B\in{\mathcal{C}},[B]\leq[A]\}
and
C_{0}:=\bigwedge\{[C]:C\in{\mathcal{C}},[A]\leq[C]\}
, where
\bigvee
and
\bigwedge
represent supremum and infimum respectively. Note the supremum and infimum exist and are elements of
[{\mathcal{F}}]
by Theorem
6.4
. Moreover,
B_{0}\leq[A]\leq C_{0}
and
\nu(C_{0}-B_{0})<\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
. But this implies
B_{0}=[A]=C_{0}
, by Proposition
6.3
(5). Hence
[A]\in\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]
,
[{\mathcal{T}}]\subseteq\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]
and
[{\mathcal{A}}]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{T}})]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]
.

(2\implies 1)
Given a countable set
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
, Lemma
10.1
implies there is a countable chain
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}\subseteq[{\mathcal{A}}]
such that
\alpha({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime})=\alpha[{\mathcal{C}}]
. But then
\sigma(\alpha({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}))=\sigma(\alpha[{\mathcal{C}}])
. Suppose
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}:=\{[A_{k}^{\prime}]:k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
for some
\{A_{k}^{\prime}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
. (This implicitly invokes the axiom of countable choice.) Set
A_{1}:=A_{1}^{\prime}
so that trivially
[A_{1}]=[A_{1}^{\prime}]
and
\{A_{1}\}
forms a chain in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Inductively define for each
k\geq 2
the sets
B_{k}:=\bigcup\{A_{j}:j<k,[A_{j}]\leq[A_{k}^{\prime}]\}
and
C_{k}:={\mathbb{N}}\cap\bigcap\{A_{j}:j<k,[A_{j}]\geq[A_{k}^{\prime}]\}
. That is,
B_{k}
is the largest element of the finite sequence
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k-1}
with
[A_{j}]\leq[A_{k}^{\prime}]
or
\emptyset
if no such set exists, and
C_{k}
is the smallest element of that finite sequence with
[A_{j}]\geq[A_{k}^{\prime}]
or
{\mathbb{N}}
if no such set exists. Set
A_{k}:=(A_{k}^{\prime}\cup B_{k})\cap C_{k}
and note
A_{k}\setminus A_{k}^{\prime}\subseteq B_{k}\setminus A_{k}^{\prime}
and
A_{k}^{\prime}\setminus A_{k}\subseteq A_{k}^{\prime}\setminus C_{k}
. But then since
[B_{k}]\leq[A_{k}^{\prime}]\leq[C_{k}]
, one must have
B_{k}\setminus A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
A_{k}^{\prime}\setminus C_{k}\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
A_{k}\triangle A_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Thus
[A_{k}]=[A_{k}^{\prime}]
and
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}
forms a chain in
{\mathcal{A}}
. It follows that
{\mathcal{T}}:=\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
is a chain with
[{\mathcal{T}}]={\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
[{\mathcal{A}}]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{T}})]
, since
[\alpha({\mathcal{C}})]=\alpha[{\mathcal{C}}]=\alpha({\mathcal{T}}^{\prime})=\alpha[{\mathcal{T}}]=[\alpha({\mathcal{T}})]
.

If either statement holds, then Lemma
9.1
and Corollary
6.5
together imply that for any
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
\epsilon>0
there is
A_{\epsilon}\in\alpha({\mathcal{C}})
with
\nu([A]+[A_{\epsilon}])<\epsilon
. But then
\nu(A\triangle A_{\epsilon})<\epsilon
, implying
{\mathcal{A}}
is separable, since
\alpha({\mathcal{C}})
is countable.
∎

Corollary 10.4
.

If
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
is a field of sets and
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
is a chain such that
[{\mathcal{A}}]=\sigma[\alpha({\mathcal{T}})]
, then

11
Conclusion

A common strategy in mathematics is to study objects of interest in aggregrate, to uncover structural properties of the class that shed light on the inter-relationships between members. This has been a fruitful approach in probability, much of which is concerned with random variables and their various modes of convergence. Surprisingly, sequences with a Cesàro limit have not been studied in aggregrate, despite their obvious relevance to many topics in probability and analysis, particularly the study of ergodic processes. A discrete-time, real-valued ergodic process may be regarded as a map from a probability space into the space of real sequences with a Cesàro limit, with probability one. It is therefore appropriate to investigate the nature of that latter space.

This paper represents a first step in that direction. The space
{\mathcal{F}}
of binary sequences with a Cesàro limit has been studied in Sections
2
to
6
. The main structural property to emerge from this part of the paper is Corollary
6.5
, which establishes that
{\mathcal{F}}
can be factored to produce a monotone class
[{\mathcal{F}}]
. This finding is significant in light of the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras (Theorem
4.1
): it implies that algebras embedded in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
can be expanded to countably complete algebras in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
.

Spaces of more general sequences with a Cesàro limit are studied in Sections
7
to
10
. These spaces, dubbed
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces, are comprised of sequences with a kind of measurability property (Condition 1 of Definition
7.7
) with respect to a field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, and also a kind of integrability property (Condition 2 of Definition
7.7
). One of the main results in this second part of the paper is that
K_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces are vector spaces with a pseudonorm defined in terms of the Cesàro limit, and an isometric embedding into a
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
space. In particular, for sequences in these spaces, the Cesàro limit is equal to an integral. Conditions under which this embedding (or the induced isometric embedding of the normed vector space
{\mathcal{K}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
into
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
) is an isomorphism are identified in Theorems
7.15
and
7.16
.

Sections
8
,
9
and
10
identify conditions under which
L_{p}
spaces and the
K_{p}
spaces isomorhpic to them are complete and separable. Section
8
clarifies why
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
and
K_{p}(A)
are appropriately studied using finitely additive measure theory: the cases in which these reduce to (countably additive) measure spaces are of a highly specific form, but are nevertheless complete and separable. More generally, Theorems
9.1
and
9.2
provide complementary characterisations of complete
L_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces, the first in terms of an embedding in a conventional Lebesgue function space (constructed on a countably additive measure space), and the second in terms of an expansion of the underlying field
{\mathcal{A}}
to a field
\lambda({\mathcal{A}})
, based on Corollary
6.5
. Section
10
proposes a strategy for generating
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}({\mathcal{A}})
spaces that are Polish spaces (complete, separable metric spaces), using a chain of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
, such as that consisting of the inverse images of a
T_{1}
-measurable function. This is flagged as a potential topic for further research.

In the process of proving the results contained in this paper, an analytical tool dubbed
null modification
has been developed (a form of this technique is also described in the companion paper
keith2022
). The technique involves manipulating sets (Proposition
5.1
), chains (Theorem
5.3
), sequences (Corollary
5.4
) or functions (Theorem
7.12
) by adding and/or deleting null sets in such a manner as to imbue the modified object with desirable properties. This technique may be useful in other contexts.

The theory developed herein pertains to certain spaces of sequences with Cesàro limits, defined in terms of some field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. An interesting direction for future research is to study in aggregate the space of all sequences with Cesàro limits, or at least those that satisfy Definition
7.7
with the field
{\mathcal{A}}
replaced by the additive class
{\mathcal{F}}
. Spaces of this latter form are appropriately dubbed
K_{p}({\mathcal{F}})
spaces, and may form the subject of a future paper.
