A variance-sensitive Gaussian concentration inequality
Nguyen Tien Dung

Abstract
In this note, we obtain a Gaussian concentration inequality for a class of non-Lipschitz functions. In the one-dimensional case, our results supplement those established by Paouris and Valettas in [ 8 ] .



Keywords:
Concentration of measure, Gaussian vectors.

2010 Mathematics Subject Classification:
Primary 60E15.

1
Introduction

In the whole paper, let
Z=(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n})
be an
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian vector and
Z^{\prime}=(Z^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,Z^{\prime}_{n})
be an independent copy of
Z.
It is well known that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
L,
then we have the following concentration inequalities (see e.g.
[
3
,
10
]
), for all
t>0,

P\left(f(Z)-Ef(Z)\leq-t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2L^{2}}\right)

(1.1)

and

P\left(f(Z)-M\leq-t\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2L^{2}}\right),

(1.2)

where
M
is a median for
f(Z).
In a recent paper
[
8
]
, Paouris and Valettas pointed out that Gaussian concentration phenomenon also holds for the convex functions. More specifically, they got the following.

Theorem A.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with
E|f(Z)|<\infty
and let
M
be a median for
f(Z).
Then, it holds that

P\left(f(Z)-M\leq-tE(f(Z)-M)_{+}\right)\leq\Phi\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{32}t\right)\,\,\,\forall\,t>0.

(1.3)

As discussed in
[
8
]
, for any convex function
f
with
E|f(Z)|^{2}<\infty,
the inequality (
1.3
) implies the following estimates

P\left(f(Z)-M\leq-t\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi t^{2}}{1024{\rm Var}f(Z)}\right)\,\,\,\forall\,t>0

(1.4)

and

\displaystyle P\left(f(Z)-Ef(Z)\leq-t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi(t-\sqrt{{\rm Var}f(Z)})^{2}}{1024{\rm Var}f(Z)}\right)

\displaystyle<\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{1000{\rm Var}f(Z)}\right)\,\,\,\forall\,t>\sqrt{{\rm Var}f(Z)}.

(1.5)

Interestingly, the bound (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) depend on
{\rm Var}f(Z)
instead of Lipschitz constant
L.
Hence, those bound improve (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). Here the improvement lies in the fact that
{\rm Var}f(Z)\leq L^{2}
(by the Gaussian Poincaré inequality) and one can find many examples of
f
for which
{\rm Var}f(Z)\ll L^{2}.
For further results, Valettas proved in
[
14
]
that (
1.5
) is tight if the convex function
f
is not superconcentrated, some variance-sensitive concentration inequalities for the log-concave probability measures were obtained in
[
9
,
14
]
. We also refer to
[
1
,
11
]
for other concentration results.

In the present paper, our purpose is to obtain a multi-dimensional version of the variance-sensitive concentration inequality (
1.5
). Our main result is the following statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\cdots,d
be twice differentiable functions. For each
i,j,l=1,\cdots,d,
we define the functions

T_{ij}(z):=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right]d\alpha,\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and

T_{lij}(z):=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_{l}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right]d\alpha,\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Assume that

(i)
E\left[\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\right]<\infty
for any
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d}\geq 0,
and all derivatives of
f_{i}^{\prime}s
have subexponential growth at infinity,

(ii)
T_{lij}(z)\geq 0
for every
i,j,l=1,\cdots,d,
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then, for any
t=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},
we have

P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1},\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{d}^{2}}{2\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}}\right),

(1.6)

where
\Sigma
is covariance matrix of
(f_{1}(Z),\cdots,f_{d}(Z))
and
\|.\|_{\rm op}
denotes the operator norm.

Assume, further, that the inverse matrix
\Sigma^{-1}
exists, then the bound (
1.6
) can be improved to the following

P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1},\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t\,\Sigma^{-1}\,t^{\intercal}\right),

(1.7)

for any
t
satisfying
t\,\Sigma^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},
where
t^{\intercal}
is the transpose of
t.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is deferred to Section
2
. To see clearer our new contributions let us end up this Section with some remarks and examples.

Remark 1.1
.

Let
M_{i}
be a median for
f_{i}(Z),i=1,\cdots,d.
Taking into account the fact that
M_{i}-Ef_{i}(Z)\leq E|f_{i}(Z)-M_{i}|\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}f_{i}(Z)}
we can get concentration inequalities about the median. Indeed, for example, we obtain from (
1.6
) that

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\left(f_{1}(Z)-M_{1}\leq-t_{1},\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-M_{d}\leq-t_{d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1}+M_{1}-Ef_{1}(Z),\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}+M_{d}-Ef_{d}(Z)\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1}+\sqrt{{\rm Var}f_{1}(Z)},\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}+\sqrt{{\rm Var}f_{d}(Z)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(t_{1}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}f_{1}(Z)})^{2}+\cdots+(t_{d}-\sqrt{{\rm Var}f_{d}(Z)})^{2}}{2\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}}\right)

for all
t_{i}\geq\sqrt{{\rm Var}f_{i}(Z)},i=1,\cdots,d.
Furthermore, by a result of Kwapień
[
7
]
, if
f_{i}^{\prime}s
are convex functions then
M_{i}-Ef_{i}(Z)\leq 0
and hence,

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\left(f_{1}(Z)-M_{1}\leq-t_{1},\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-M_{d}\leq-t_{d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1},\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{d}^{2}}{2\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}}\right)\,\,\,\forall\,(t_{1},\cdots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.

Remark 1.2
.

In the one-dimensional case (
d=1
), the bound (
1.6
) reduces to

P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2{\rm Var}f_{1}(Z)}\right)\,\,\,\forall\,\,t\geq 0,

(1.8)

provided that
T_{111}(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
In particular, (
1.8
) holds true if
\frac{\partial f_{1}(z)}{\partial z_{i}}\geq 0
and
\frac{\partial^{2}f_{1}(z)}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}}\geq 0
for all
i,j=1,\cdots,n.
We note that the class of functions
f_{1}
satisfying
T_{111}(z)\geq 0
is not the same as the class of convex functions, see the examples provided by Tanguy in
[
13
, p. 981]
. Hence, our bound (
1.8
) supplements the bound (
1.5
).

Remark 1.3
.

The concentration inequality (
1.8
) was proved first in
[
6
]
, which is a draft version of the present paper. We also refer the reader to
[
13
, Sec. 5]
for discussions and a neater proof using semigroup operators.

Remark 1.4
.

In the multi-dimensional setting, an important application of Theorem
1.1
is to estimate the tail of the minimal component of non-Gaussian random vectors. Let
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\cdots,d
be twice differentiable functions. Assume that the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of
(h_{1}(Z),\cdots,h_{d}(Z))
is invertible and the functions
f_{i}=-h_{i},i=1,\cdots,d
satisfy the conditions
(i)
-
(ii)
of Theorem
1.1
. Then, for
(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d}):=\Sigma^{-1}\,(1,\cdots,1)^{\intercal},
the inequality (
1.7
) gives us

\displaystyle P\left(\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq d}(h_{i}(Z)-Eh_{i}(Z))>t\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t,\cdots,f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}(\theta_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{d})\right)\,\,\,\forall\,\,t\geq 0,

(1.9)

provided that
\theta_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{d}>0.
Note that (
1.9
) is similar to the relation (3.17) given in
[
4
]
where Chakrabarty & Samorodnitsky establish the precise asymptotic behaviour for the tail of the minimal component of the Gaussian vector.

Remark 1.5
.

Observe that

\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{u=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{u}}(z)\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial z_{u}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})+\sum\limits_{u=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{u}}(z)\frac{\partial^{2}f_{j}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{u}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right]d\alpha.

Hence, we can verify the condition
(ii)
of Theorem
1.1
by checking the following

(ii^{\prime})
For all
i=1,\cdots,d,

f_{i}
is coordinatewise non-decreasing (or non-increasing) and

\frac{\partial^{2}f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{u}}\geq 0\,\,\forall\,\,k,u=1,\cdots,n.

For example, the sum of log-normal random variables fulfills the condition
(ii^{\prime}).

Remark 1.6
.

Let
U=(U_{1},\cdots,U_{n})
be a Gaussian random vector (coordinates are not necessarily independent). It is well known that we can express
U=AZ,
where
A
is a matrix such that
AA^{\intercal}
is the covariance matrix of
U.
Hence, Theorem
1.1
can be extended to the functions of arbitrary Gaussian random vectors.

Example 1.1
.

Consider
h_{1}(Z):=Z_{1}+Z_{2}
and
h_{2}(Z):=Z_{1}-Z_{2},
we have

\Sigma=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&0\\
0&2\\
\end{array}\right).

Hence, the bound (
1.9
) becomes

P\left(\min\{Z_{1}+Z_{2},Z_{1}-Z_{2}\}>t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right)\,\,\,\forall\,\,t\geq 0.

(1.10)

On the other hand, we observe that
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
and
Z_{1}-Z_{2}
are independent Gaussian random variables. We obtain

\displaystyle P\left(\min\{Z_{1}+Z_{2},Z_{1}-Z_{2}\}>t\right)

\displaystyle=P(Z_{1}+Z_{2}>t)P(Z_{1}-Z_{2}>t)

\displaystyle=(1-\Phi(t/\sqrt{2}))^{2}\,\,\,\forall\,\,t\geq 0,

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable. Thus, in this simple example, our estimate (
1.10
) is sharp.

Example 1.2
.

In this example, we construct a class of random vectors satisfying Theorem
1.1
. Fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}=\{1,2,\cdots\},
define the set
\mathcal{P}
of the polynomials of the form

\mathcal{P}:=\bigg\{p(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}x^{2m_{i}},x\in\mathbb{R}:a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{+},i=1,\cdots,N\bigg\}

and denote by
\mathcal{Q}
the following set of non-negative random variables

\mathcal{Q}:=\bigg\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(Z_{i}):p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}\bigg\}.

We observe that, for any
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{+},
the function

z_{k}\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}^{2m_{1}-1}(\alpha z_{k}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z_{k}^{\prime})^{2m_{2}-1}\right]d\alpha

belongs to
\mathcal{P}
(this is due to the fact that
E[(Z_{k}^{\prime})^{2m-1}]=0
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
). Hence, it is easy to see that if the random variables
f_{i}(Z),f_{j}(Z)\in\mathcal{Q},
then
T_{ij}(Z)
also belongs to
\mathcal{Q}.
As a consequence, we can conclude that every vector
(f_{1}(Z),\cdots,f_{d}(Z))\in\mathcal{Q}^{d}
will satisfy both conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
of Theorem
1.1
.

2
Proofs

The key tool in our proof is the covariance identify formula for Gaussian functionals. We have

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable functions. Suppose that
f,g
and their derivatives have subexponential growth at infinity. Define

u(z):=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\frac{\partial g}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right]d\alpha,\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then, we have

{\rm Cov}(f(Z),g(Z))=E[u(Z)].

Proof.

Note that the subexponential growth condition ensures the existence of all expectations. Hence, the desired result follows directly from Lemma 2.1.4 in
[
2
]
and the routine approximation argument.
∎

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\cdots,d
be twice differentiable functions satisfying the conditions
(i)
-
(ii)
of Theorem
1.1
. Then, for any
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d}\geq 0,
we have

E\left[\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\right]\leq E\left[\exp\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right],

(2.1)

where
N=(N_{1},\cdots,N_{d})
is a Gaussian vector satisfying
E[N_{i}]=E[f_{i}(Z)]
and
E[N_{i}N_{j}]=E[f_{i}(Z)f_{j}(Z)]
for all
i,j=1,\cdots,d.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
N
is independent of
(f_{1}(Z),\cdots,f_{d}(Z))
and
E[N_{i}]=E[f_{i}(Z)]=0
for all
i=1,\cdots,d.
Consider the function

\varphi(t):=E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)+\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right],\,\,t\in[0,1].

This function is well-defined because of the condition
(i)
of Theorem
1.1
. We have, for
t\in(0,1),

\varphi^{\prime}(t)=E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)+\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right].

By the independence, we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}E\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right]E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\right]E\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right].

(2.2)

An application of Lemma
2.1
yields

E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right]=-\sqrt{t}E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)T(Z)\right],

(2.3)

where the function
T
is defined by

\displaystyle T(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\right)\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right)\right]d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right)\right]d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}T_{ij}(z),\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

On the other hand, by the integration by parts formula for Gaussian random variables (see, e.g. Appendix A.6 in
[
12
]
) we obtain

E\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right]=\sqrt{1-t}E\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right]\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\sigma_{ij},

(2.4)

where
\sigma_{ij}:={\rm Cov}(N_{i},N_{j})={\rm Cov}(f_{i}(Z),f_{j}(Z)).
By inserting (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) into (
2.2
) we get

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}E\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right]E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\left(T(Z)-\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\sigma_{ij}\right)\right].

We now observe that
{\rm Cov}(f_{i}(Z),f_{j}(Z))=E[T_{ij}(Z)]
for every
\,\,i,j=1,\cdots,d.
Hence,

E[T(Z)]=E\left[\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}T_{ij}(Z)\right]=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\sigma_{ij}.

So, once again, we can apply Lemma
2.1
and we obtain

\varphi^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\sqrt{t}}{2}E\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{1-t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right]E\left[\exp\left(-\sqrt{t}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\bar{T}(Z)\right],\,\,t\in(0,1),

where
\bar{T}
is defined by

\displaystyle\bar{T}(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\right)\frac{\partial T}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right]d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\right)\left(\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right)\right]d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}E\left[\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i,j,l=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{l}\frac{\partial f_{l}}{\partial z_{k}}(z)\frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial z_{k}}(\alpha z+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}Z^{\prime})\right)\right]d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i,j,l=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{l}T_{lij}(z),\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Consequently, the condition
(ii)
of Theorem
1.1
implies that
\bar{T}(z)\geq 0,
and hence,
\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leq 0,\,\,t\in(0,1).
So the claim (
2.1
) holds true because

E\left[\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\right]=\varphi(1)\leq\varphi(0)=E\left[\exp\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right].

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

The proof of Lemma
2.2
has some similarities with the proof of Slepian’s lemma, see e.g.
[
5
]
. The main difference lies in the fact that we applied Lemma
2.1
twice times. This is the key idea allowing us to handle non-Lipschitz functions.

Remark 2.2
.

The left hand side of (
2.1
) is the Laplace transformation of
(f_{1}(Z),\cdots,f_{d}(Z)),
and hence, the estimate (
2.1
) could be useful for other research problems.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Without loss of generality, we may and will assume that
Ef_{i}(Z)=0
for every
i=1,\cdots,d.
Then, by Markov’s inequality we have

\displaystyle P\big(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1},\cdots,

\displaystyle f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}\big)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\geq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}t_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq E\left[\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}f_{i}(Z)\right)\right]\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}t_{i}\right)

(2.5)

for all
t=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.

From (
2.1
) and (
2.5
) we obtain, for any
\theta=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},

\displaystyle P\big(f_{1}(Z)-Ef_{1}(Z)\leq-t_{1},\cdots,

\displaystyle f_{d}(Z)-Ef_{d}(Z)\leq-t_{d}\big)

\displaystyle\leq E\left[\exp\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}N_{i}\right)\right]\exp\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}t_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-t\,\theta^{\intercal}+\frac{1}{2}\theta\,\Sigma\,\theta^{\intercal}\right)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-t\,\theta^{\intercal}+\frac{1}{2}\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}\theta\,\theta^{\intercal}\right).

(2.7)

Taking the infimum over all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},
the optimal choice of
\theta
is
t/\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}.
So (
1.6
) follows from (
2.7
). Similarly, if
\Sigma^{-1}
exists, we obtain (
1.7
) from (
2.6
) by choosing
\theta=t\,\Sigma^{-1}.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is completed.
\square
