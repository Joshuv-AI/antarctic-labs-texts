An adaptive splitting method for the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process.
Cónall Kelly, Gabriel J. Lord

Abstract
. We propose a new splitting method for strong numerical solution of the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model. For this method, applied over both deterministic and adaptive random meshes, we prove a uniform moment bound and strong error results of order 1 / 4 1/4 in L 1 L_{1} and L 2 L_{2} for the parameter regime κ ​ θ > σ 2 \kappa\theta>\sigma^{2} . We then extend the new method to cover all parameter values by introducing a soft zero region (where the deterministic flow determines the approximation) giving a hybrid type method to deal with the reflecting boundary. From numerical simulations we observe a rate of order 1 1 when κ ​ θ > σ 2 \kappa\theta>\sigma^{2} rather than 1 / 4 1/4 . Asymptotically, for large noise, we observe that the rates of convergence decrease similarly to those of other schemes but that the proposed method making use of adaptive timestepping displays smaller error constants.



1.
Introduction

We introduce a novel splitting method for the strong numerical solution of the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process, which typically arises as a model of stochastic volatility or for the pricing of interest rate derivatives in finance; see Cox et al.,
[
9
]
. Similar equations arise in other application, for example the modelling of changes in the membrane voltage of a neuron, see
[
12
]
. The CIR process is given by the Itô-type stochastic differential equation (SDE) for
t\in[0,T]

(1)

dX(t)=\kappa\left(\theta-X(t)\right)dt+\sigma\sqrt{X(t)}dW(t),\quad\quad X(0)=X_{0}>0,

where
W(t)
is a Wiener process, and
\kappa
,
\theta
, and
\sigma
are positive parameters.
Solutions of (
1
) are almost surely (a.s.) non-negative. In general paths can achieve a value of zero but are reflected back into the positive half of the real line immediately. We propose a novel construct called the
soft zero
to ensure dynamic consistency in the neighbourhood of this reflecting bounday. Further, when the Feller condition

(2)

2\kappa\theta>\sigma^{2}

is satisfied solutions are a.s. positive.

Although no analytic solution is available,
X(t)
has
a non-central chi-square distribution (conditional upon
X(s)
for
0\leq s<t
), see
[
1
,
7
,
13
,
20
]
.
For Monte Carlo estimation, exact sampling from this known conditional distribution is feasible but computationally inefficient and potentially restrictive if the Wiener process of (
1
) is correlated, see Cozma & Reisinger
[
10
]
.
As a consequence the numerical simulation of (
1
) is an active topic of research, and techniques to handle the unbounded gradient of the diffusion coefficient near zero can be applied to more general equations.

Two significant challenges for a numerical scheme for CIR are to preserve positivity and to prove convergence with as high a rate as possible over as large a parameter range as possible. Upper bounds on the order of strong convergence are most often shown to hold in a restricted parameter range; see Table
1
, which gives a summary of some of the key methods, the known rates of convergence and parameter ranges where the theoretical rates are valid. We now discuss two main approaches for the strong approximation of (
1
) in the literature, the first is direct numerical simulation of (
1
), and the second is based on a Lamperti transformation.

For direct approximations of (
1
) that preserve positivity of the numerical solution see, for example, the discussions in
[
1
,
5
,
17
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
14
]
. The last of these represents a remarkable advance, showing that a broad class of Milstein-type methods over an equidistant mesh with a novel truncation in the neighbourhood of zero is convergent with rate across all parameter values.

General results on the strong convergence of methods to approximate (
1
) using equidistant evaluations of the Wiener process are in Hefter & Jentzen
[
15
]
, with extension to a class of path-dependent adaptive meshes in Hefter et al.,
[
16
]
. For equidistant meshes Euler- and Milstein-type discretizations are included, and it is shown that such a method has a convergence order of at best
\delta/2
where
0<\delta<2
is the dimension of the squared Bessel process associated with CIR.
In the general results of Hefter et al.,
[
16
]
, when applied to (
1
) with
\kappa\theta>2\sigma
it is proved that adaptive algorithms are subject to the same upper and lower bounds on the rate of convergence as equidistant grids, though the error constant may be different. For larger relative values of
\sigma
they state it is unclear whether adaptive algorithms can improve upon the optimal rate of convergence. In our work we examine adaptive discretizations of (
1
) and observe the same rates of convergence with equidistant vs adaptive steps. Howevever, in numerical experiments adaptivity allows us to better capture the dynamics of the process and to reduce the error constant, hence improving efficiency.

In Section
5
we compare our new splitting based method to several of these: the Milstein method of Hefter & Herzwurm
[
14
]
and the fully truncated method proposed by Lord et al.,
[
20
]
. The latter method is widely used in practice and was shown in
[
20
]
to be convergent in
L_{1}
but without a rate (results of this type are common for these Euler variants, see for example Higham & Mao
[
17
]
).
However recently Cozma & Reisinger
[
10
]
obtained strong order of convergence
1/2
in
L_{p}
under certain conditions. With
p=2
this gives convergence with rate
1/2
for
2\kappa\theta>3\sigma^{2}
.

The second main approach is to apply a Lamperti transformation to (
1
) and to numerically approximate the related process
Y=\sqrt{X}
. The SDE in
Y
has additive noise but a drift coefficient with a singularity in the neighbourhood of zero (see (
6
)).
We note that strong
L_{2}
convergence for the transformed equation yields
L_{1}
convergence for the original CIR process.
A fully implicit Euler discretisation over a uniform mesh that preserves positivity of solutions was proposed in Alfonsi
[
1
]
and shown to have uniformly bounded moments. A continuous time extension interpolating linearly between mesh points was shown to have strong
L_{p}
order of convergence
1/2
(up to a factor of
\sqrt{|\log({\Delta t})|}
) in Dereich et al.,
[
11
]
when
2\kappa\theta>p\sigma^{2}
, a continuous-time variant based on the same implicit discretisation was shown to have strong
L_{p}
convergence of order
1
when
4\kappa\theta>3p\sigma^{2}
in Alphonsi
[
3
]
. In Chassagneux et al.,
[
8
]
a variant which discretised the transformed SDE for
Y
with an explicit projection method was shown to give strong convergence of between order
1/6
and
1
for
\kappa\theta>\sigma^{2}
(see Table
1
). We consider in Section
5
the implicit method of
[
1
]
and the projection method of
[
8
]
.

In this article, we construct and analyse a new positivity preserving numerical method for (
1
) which is based on a splitting method applied to the Lamperti transform. This scheme falls outside the framework of Hefter & Herzwurm
[
14
]
as it fails to satisfy their
L_{p}
-Lipschitz continuity requirement in the absence of an equivalent truncation strategy. As an example of a splitting strategy it merits an independent error analysis. Over a class of meshes that includes both uniform and adaptive meshes we prove a uniform moment bound for the numerical scheme, which leads to a strong
L_{1}
convergence of order
{\Delta t}^{1/4}
for
\kappa\theta>\sigma^{2}
.
These results together then allow us to prove strong
L_{2}
convergence of the same order in that region. The parameter restriction arises from the need to control the first two inverse moments of the transformed SDE.

In common with other methods we observe in numerical experiments a far better rate of convergence for the proposed method than predicted by the analysis in the Feller regime.
In fact, with increasing
\sigma
we see higher rates up to when
\alpha:=(4\kappa\theta-\sigma^{2})/8=0
.
The scheme can be used without modification for
\alpha\geq 0
.
For
\alpha<0
we describe an extension of the adaptive version of the scheme in Section
2.4
that introduces the notion of a
soft zero
region where the noise component is switched off and the unperturbed solution is solved exactly, giving a hybrid method. The splitting method is seen to be competitive across all values of
\alpha
both in terms of the estimated rate of convergence and in that it consistently shows a small error constant. The
soft zero
approach means that for large time steps, by contrast with other methods, the solution is not simply projected to zero. We believe this to be a novel technique with broad applicability.

The structure of the article is as follows. In Section
2
we motivate the construction of the numerical scheme by describing the Lamperti transformed equation in variation of constants form, and set up the (potentially adaptive) mesh. In Section
3
we describe useful conditional moment and regularity bounds on the exact solution of the SDE (
1
). In Section
4
we present our three main results, all of which hold over a potentially adaptive mesh. First, we prove a uniform moment bound on a linear interpolant of the numerical scheme. Second, we prove an
L_{2}
-strong convergence result for the numerical discretisation of the Lamperti transformed scheme, which implies an
L_{1}
-strong convergence result for the untransformed scheme. This leads to the third main result, an
L_{2}
-strong convergence result for the untransformed scheme. In Section
2.4
we extend the scheme to the
\alpha<0
regime and prove that numerical steps taken in the “soft zero” region satisfy an appropriate mean-square consistency bound. In Section
5
we compare numerically our method to four methods, two from direct simulation of CIR and two derived from the Lamperti transformation.

2.
Mathematical Preliminaries

2.1.
The Cox-Ingersoll-Ross equation

For
t\geq 0
, the integral equation of (
1
) is given by

(3)

X(t)=X_{0}+\kappa\int_{0}^{t}(\theta-X(s))ds+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{X(s)}dW(s),

which can be written in variation of constants form as

(4)

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa t}X_{0}+\theta(1-e^{-\kappa t})+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\sqrt{X(s)}dW(s).

If we suppose that Feller’s condition (
2
) holds
we can equivalently consider the SDE with additive noise yielded by a Lamperti transformation. Letting
Y(t)=\sqrt{X(t)}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and

(5)

\alpha:=(4\kappa\theta-\sigma^{2})/8,\quad\beta:=\kappa/2,\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma:=\sigma/2,

we have, after an application of Itô’s formula, the SDE

(6)

dY(t)=(\alpha Y^{-1}(t)-\beta Y(t))dt+\gamma dW(t),\quad t\geq 0.

This in its turn may be written in variation of constants form as

(7)

Y(t)=e^{-\beta(t-t_{n})}Y(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n}}^{t}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\frac{\alpha}{Y(s)}ds+\gamma\int_{t_{n}}^{t}e^{-\beta(t-s)}dW(s).

Note that Feller’s condition as given in (
2
) may be expressed as
\alpha>\sigma^{2}/8
or equivalently
2\alpha>\gamma^{2}
.

We prove a uniform moment bound, as well as strong
L_{2}
-convergence with order at least
1/4
of numerical approximations to both (
1
) and to the transformed equation (
6
) under the following assumption, which implies the Feller condition (
2
):

Assumption 1
.

Let
\alpha>3\sigma^{2}/8
, or equivalently

\kappa\theta>\sigma^{2}.

Numerically, we examine strong
L_{2}
-convergence using a deterministic (uniform) mesh under the following parameter set, which is a superset of that defined in Assumption
1
:

Assumption 2
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
, or equivalently

4\kappa\theta\geq\sigma^{2}.

Finally we numerically investigate strong
L_{2}
-convergence using a random adaptive mesh under the following complementary parameter set:

Assumption 3
.

Let
\alpha<0
, or equivalently

4\kappa\theta<\sigma^{2}.

Note that Feller’s condition is necessary for Assumption
1
, and sufficient but not necessary for Assumption
2
. If Assumption
3
holds, Feller’s condition does not.

2.2.
The mesh

Our method may be implemented on either a deterministic or adaptive (random) mesh.
Therefore we introduce here a generic nonuniform mesh with nodes that may be selected randomly subject to certain measurability requirements, with a view to proving strong convergence under a minimal set of constraints on the mesh.

Consider the mesh
\{t_{0},t_{1}\ldots,t_{N}\}
on the interval
[0,T]
, where
t_{0}=0
and
t_{N}=T
. Points on the mesh are assumed to be distinct and may be selected in a way that may or may not be path dependent. For example the mesh may be deterministic (e.g. with uniform steps across trajectories) or random (e.g. in an adaptive manner by choosing
t_{n+1}
based upon the observed value of a discretisation at
t_{n}
). In the latter case
N
will be an
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variable, and in either case, we denote
{\Delta t}_{n+1}:=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
for all
n=0,\ldots,N-1
.

Definition 4
.

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the natural filtration of
W(t)
. Suppose that each member of
\{t_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\Delta t}_{i}\}_{n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}}
, with
t_{0}=0
, is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-stopping time. That is to say
\{t_{n}\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
If
\tau
is any
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-stopping time
\tau
then (see
[
22
]
)

\mathcal{F}_{\tau}:=\{B\in\mathcal{F}\,:\,B\cap\{\tau\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t},\,\text{ for all }\,t\geq 0\}.

This allows us to condition on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
at any point on the time-set
\{t_{n}\}_{n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}}
.

All our theoretical results apply on a mesh that satisfies the following:

Assumption 5
.

{\Delta t}_{n+1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable and
N<\infty
a.s. Moreover there exists a deterministic constant
{\Delta t}_{\max}
such that
{\Delta t}_{n}\leq{\Delta t}_{\max}
for all
n=1,\ldots,N
.

For our main convergence results we must additionally assume the following stronger conditions on the mesh:

Assumption 6
.

For each fixed
{\Delta t}_{\max}
, there exists

A.

a deterministic integer
N_{\max}<\infty
such that
N\leq N_{\max}
a.s;

B.

a constant
T_{\max}
, independent of
{\Delta t}_{\max}
and
N_{\max}
, such that

(8)

{\Delta t}_{\max}N_{\max}\leq T_{\max}.

Part B holds if, for example, there exists a minimum stepsize
{\Delta t}_{\min}
held in a fixed ratio to
{\Delta t}_{\max}
: see
[
18
,
19
]
. Whereas, Part A by itself, only requires that the number of steps taken over the entire interval of simulation has a deterministic upper bound. Moreover, both of Assumptions

and
6
are automatically satisfied if the mesh is constructed deterministically.
Only Part A of Assumption

is required to prove

-convergence directly for the transformed equation (
6

-convergence result for (
1

If an adaptive timestepping strategy is used the a.s. finiteness (Assumption
5
) or boundedness (Assumption
6
) of

would need to be confirmed.

Definition 7
.

For each
t\in[0,T]
, define the (potentially random) integer

such that

N^{(t)}:=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}\,:\,t_{n-1}<t\}.

Set
N:=N^{(T)}
and
t_{N}:=T
, so that

is always the last point on the mesh.

For any
t\in[0,T]
,

is almost everywhere (a.e.) the index of the right endpoint of the step that contains

, and by construction and Assumption
5
, we have
N^{(t)}\leq N<\infty
a.s, or
N^{(t)}\leq N\leq N_{\max}<\infty
a.s. if Assumption
6
Part A additionally holds.

If the mesh is constructed adaptively,
\Delta W_{n+1}:=W(t_{n+1})-W(t_{n})

X_{n}
, through which it depends on
\{W(s),\,s\in[0,t_{n}]\}
. Therefore
\Delta W_{n+1}
will not be independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
; indeed it is not necessarily normally distributed. Since
{\Delta t}_{n+1}
is a bounded
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-stopping time and

-measurable, then

is

-conditionally normally distributed, by Doob’s optional sampling theorem (see for example Shiryaev

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W(t_{n+1})-W(t_{n})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad a.s.;

2.3.
The splitting method

We start by considering the transformed SDE (
6
). The main approximation over a single step from
t_{j}
to
t_{j+1}
is based on the exact solution of the ODE

\frac{dz(t)}{dt}=\alpha z^{-1}(t),\quad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}],

which, when
z(t_{j})
is given, can be computed to be

z(t)=\sqrt{z(t_{j})^{2}+2\alpha(t-t_{j})},\quad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}].

This we combine with the exponential integrator based approximation of the OU-type SDE

dx(t)=-\beta x(t)dt+\gamma dW(t),\quad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}].

The method that results is equivalent to the Lie-Trotter composition of the exact flows of the following subequations

\displaystyle dY^{[1]}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(Y^{[1]}(t)\right)^{-1}dt,\quad Y^{[1]}(0)=Y_{0}^{[1]}

\displaystyle dY^{[2]}(t)

\displaystyle=\gamma dW(t),\quad Y^{[2]}(0)=Y_{0}^{[2]};

\displaystyle dY^{[3]}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta Y^{[3]}(t)dt,\quad Y^{[3]}(0)=Y_{0}^{[3]}.

Thus we obtain the approximation
Y_{n}
to
Y(t_{n})
of (
6
)

(9)

Y_{n+1}=e^{-\beta{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}+\gamma\Delta W_{n+1}\right),

where
\Delta W_{n+1}=W(t_{n+1})-W(t_{n})
. Note that changing the order of the splitting results in a method where we can not readily control the timestep to preserve positivity.

Our analysis requires a continuous form of this intermediate scheme defined over
t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
given by

(10)

\bar{Y}(t)=e^{-\beta(t-t_{n})}\left(Y_{n}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}ds\right)+e^{-\beta(t-t_{n})}\gamma\int_{t_{n}}^{t}dW(s).

It is straightforward to confirm that
\bar{Y}(t_{n})=Y_{n}
and
\bar{Y}(t_{n+1})=Y_{n+1}
.

The scheme for (
1
) is then defined by
X_{n+1}:=(Y_{n+1})^{2}
, so

(11)

X_{n+1}=e^{-2\beta{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(\sqrt{X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}+\gamma\Delta W_{n+1}\right)^{2},

or equivalently

(12)

X_{n+1}=e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}\right.\\
\left.+\sigma\sqrt{X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(\Delta W_{n+1}\right)+\sigma^{2}\frac{(\Delta W_{n+1})^{2}}{4}\right).

An immediate consequence of the construction is that values of the numerical solution
\{X_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
are non-negative for any
\alpha>0
.

Our results easily extend to a Strang-like splitting which results in only one extra term that is of higher order, and is given by

X_{n+1}=e^{-2\beta{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(\sqrt{X_{n}+\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}+\gamma\Delta W_{n+1}\right)^{2}+\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}.

We did not observe numerical evidence of significant advantage. Nonetheless there is scope for further investigation in this direction.

2.4.
Extension to
\alpha<0
: adaptivity and a soft zero

The scheme given by (
11
) contains the expression
\sqrt{X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}
. When
\alpha>0
this term is real and strictly positive for all
{\Delta t}_{n+1}>0
. To ensure that the square root is real and positive when
\alpha\leq 0
we can adapt the time step
{\Delta t}_{n+1}
to impose
X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}>0
by taking

(13)

{\Delta t}_{n+1}=\min\left\{0.95\frac{X_{n}}{2|\alpha|},{\Delta t}_{\max}\right\}.

However, it is insufficient to simply apply the scheme (
11
) over the adaptive mesh (
13
) for two reasons. First, since Feller’s condition does not hold, the boundary at zero may be achieved in this parameter regime, and in this case adaptivity is insufficient to maintain strict positivity of
{\Delta t}_{n+1}
. Second, our scheme is based on the transformed SDE (
6
) which is only well defined if solutions cannot attain zero (i.e. if Feller’s condition holds). Nonetheless the scheme itself is well defined if Feller’s condition is violated, but we must in that case treat numerical solutions in a neighbourhood of zero carefully.

We are thus motivated to introduce a
soft zero
region
[0,X_{\text{zero}}]
for some
X_{\text{zero}}>0
so that, when
X_{n}\in[0,X_{\text{zero}}]
, we approximate (
1
) by the deterministic ODE on
t\in[t_{n},s]
, given
u(t_{n})

\frac{du}{dt}=\kappa(\theta-u),\quad\text{with solution}\quad u(t)=e^{-\kappa(t-t_{n})}u(t_{n})+\theta\left(1-e^{-\kappa(t-t_{n})}\right).

We construct
X_{\text{zero}}
by a rescaling of
u(t_{n}+{\Delta t}_{\max})|_{u(t_{n})=0}
: for any
\rho>1
(we choose
\rho=2
in our numerical experiments) define

X_{\text{zero}}:=\rho^{-1}\theta(1-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{\max}})\leq\rho^{-1}2\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{\max}.

Note that as
{\Delta t}_{\max}\to 0
,
X_{\text{zero}}\to 0
.
When
X_{n}<X_{\text{zero}}
we set

(14)

{\Delta t}_{n+1}=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\log\left(\frac{X_{\text{zero}}-\theta}{X_{n}-\theta}\right)

so that
X_{n+1}=X_{\text{zero}}
.

Thus, when the numerical solution enters the
soft zero
region from a step of the splitting method (
12
), we take a single step of length
{\Delta t}_{n+1}
computed according to (
14
) after which we are guaranteed to have exited the
soft zero
region. The scheme then reverts to (
12
) unless the numerical solution again drops below
X_{\text{zero}}
. In this way we can preserve the drift dynamics of the underlying SDE when numerical solutions are close to zero, by contrast with a truncation or projection approach.

In Section
4.4
, Lemma
16
, we prove a local error estimate for the scheme when it operates in the
soft zero
region. However, further work is required to prove global convergence. There is scope to extend the application of this type of
soft zero
to preserve domain invariance for other SDEs in future work.

3.
Moment bounds for the continuous-time equation

The bounds in this subsection hold under either of Assumptions
2
and
3
.
We start by providing

(17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle X(t_{n})+\theta.

Proof.

Equation (

9
]

Furthermore we have the following:

Lemma 9

4.
Main results: moment bounds and strong convergence

4.1.
Moment bounds for the splitting scheme

Our first main result is to prove a uniform moment bound for the scheme (
12
) that applies over both deterministic and random meshes.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a solution of (
12
), suppose that the (potentially random) mesh values
\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\}
are selected so that Assumption
5
holds.
Then, where
N^{(t)}
is as given in Definition
7
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{N^{(t)}}}\right]\leq X_{0}+\kappa\theta T,\quad t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
and let
N^{(t)}
be as in Definition
7
. From the form of (
11
),
X_{n}>0
,
n=0,\ldots,N^{(t)}
, a.s.
Take the expectation of both sides, conditional upon
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
, to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{n+1}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}+\sigma\sqrt{X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\Delta W_{n+1}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[{(\Delta W_{n+1})^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\left(X_{n}+{\Delta t}_{n+1}\left(2\alpha+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}(X_{n}+\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle X_{n}+\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1},

where we have used the fact that
2\alpha+\sigma^{2}/4=\kappa\theta
at the third step and the final inequality holds since
\kappa\geq 0
,
e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\leq 1
.

Multiplying both sides by the indicator random variable
\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}
, we have that a.s.

\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{n+1}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}-X_{n}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}\leq\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}.

We now sum both sides over
n\in\mathbb{N}
and take expectations to get

(22)

\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{n+1}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}-X_{n}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}\right)}\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}}\right]=\kappa\theta T.

Appealing to the Dominated Convergence Theorem we can exchange the expectation and infinite sum on the LHS of (
22
) and

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{n+1}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-X_{n}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(X_{n+1}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}-X_{n}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{n=0}^{N^{(t)}-1}\left(X_{n+1}-X_{n}\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{N^{(t)}}}\right]-X_{0}.

Combining (
22
) and (
23
) gives the result.
∎

4.2.

L_{1}
for CIR

In this section we investigate the

-strong error of the continuous form of the Lamperti based scheme (

Theorem 13
.

Let
(Y(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a solution of (
6

\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a solution of (
9

\bar{Y}
the continuous version given by (

1

are selected so that Assumptions

and
6

(24)

\max_{n}{\Delta t}_{n}\leq{\Delta t}_{\max}\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\kappa}\right\}.

Then there exists a constant
C_{3}<\infty
such that

(25)

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{Y}({t})^{2}}\right]\leq C_{3}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{1/2},

where the error process

(26)

E_{Y}({t}):=Y(t)-\bar{Y}(t),\quad t\in[0,T],

and for each

(27)

\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{Y}({t_{n}})^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N\geq n\}}}\right]\leq C_{3}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{1/2}.

The condition
\kappa\theta>\sigma^{2}
in Assumption
1
implies Assumption
2
. Furthermore
L_{1}
convergence for the CIR scheme (
11
) follows by an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
, and let
N^{(t)}
be as given in Definition
7
. Using that

e^{-\beta(u-t_{n})}=e^{-\beta(u-s)}e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})},\quad t_{n}\leq s<u\leq t_{n+1},

the error process
E_{Y}({u})
satisfies

(28)

E_{Y}({u})=e^{-\beta(u-t_{n})}E_{Y}({t_{n}})\\
+\alpha\int_{t_{n}}^{u}e^{-\beta(u-s)}\left(\frac{1}{Y(s)}-\frac{e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}}{\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}\right)ds\\
+\gamma\int_{t_{n}}^{u}e^{-\beta(u-s)}\left(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}\right)dW(s),\quad u\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}].

We introduce the notation

\tilde{f}(s):=\frac{1}{Y(s)}-\frac{e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}}{\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}},

and re-write (
28
) as the SDE on
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]

dE_{Y}({u})=\left[\alpha\tilde{f}(u)-\beta E_{Y}({u})\right]du+\gamma\left[1-e^{-\beta(u-t_{n})}\right]dW(u).

We can apply the Itô formula to the vector-valued process
[E_{Y}({u}),Y(u)]^{T}
to derive,
for
u\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
,

E_{Y}({u})^{2}=E_{Y}({t_{n}})^{2}\\
+2\int_{t_{n}}^{u}\left(\alpha E_{Y}({s})\tilde{f}(s)-\beta E_{Y}({s})^{2}+\gamma^{2}(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})})^{2}\right)ds\\
+2\gamma\int_{t_{n}}^{u}E_{Y}({s})\left(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}\right)dW(s).

Now take expectations conditional upon
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
,

(29)

\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{Y}({u})^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]=\underbrace{E_{Y}({t_{n}})^{2}}_{=:I}+\underbrace{2\alpha\int_{t_{n}}^{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{Y}({s})\tilde{f}(s)}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]ds}_{=:II}\\
+\underbrace{2\gamma^{2}\int_{t_{n}}^{u}(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})})ds}_{=:III}-\underbrace{\beta\int_{t_{n}}^{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{Y}({s})^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]ds}_{=:IV},\quad u\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}],\quad a.s.

Note that the term
IV
is positive since
\beta>0
, and since it is subtracted from the RHS it can be omitted in any estimate from above. For

, by (
24
)
{\Delta t}_{\max}<1/\kappa
, and (
5
) (
\beta=\kappa/2
), so there exists

such that

(30)

and therefore

III\leq\kappa\gamma^{2}(u-t_{n})^{2},\quad u\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}].

It remains to bound

. There are two cases, each determined by the sign of
E_{Y}({s})=Y(s)-Y_{n}

Case 1:
When

e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}Y(s)-\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}\leq Y_{n}-\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}<0,

and

(31)

\displaystyle E_{Y}({s})\tilde{f}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(Y(s)-Y_{n}\right)\frac{\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}Y(s)}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|Y(s)-Y_{n}\right|\frac{e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}Y(s)-\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}

is negative for all
s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
.

Case 2:
Suppose
Y(s)-Y_{n}>0
. Then applying the inequality

\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}\leq Y_{n}+\sqrt{2\alpha(s-t_{n})}

\displaystyle E_{Y}({s})\tilde{f}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(Y(s)-Y_{n}\right)\frac{\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}Y(s)}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(Y(s)-Y_{n}\right)\frac{Y_{n}-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})}Y(s)+\sqrt{2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}},

for all
s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]

Y(s)
in the numerator and split into three terms:

\displaystyle E_{Y}({s})\tilde{f}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(Y(s)-Y_{n}\right)\left(\frac{Y_{n}-Y(s)+(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})})Y(s)+\sqrt{2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{-(Y(s)-Y_{n})^{2}}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}+\frac{(Y(s)-Y_{n})(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})})Y(s)}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}

\displaystyle E_{Y}({s})\tilde{f}(s)

\displaystyle\frac{-(Y(s)-Y_{n})^{2}}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}+\frac{(Y(s)-Y_{n})^{2}+(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})})^{2}Y(s)^{2}}{2Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}

\displaystyle\frac{(1-e^{-\beta(s-t_{n})})^{2}Y(s)}{2\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}}+\frac{\alpha(s-t_{n})}{Y(s)\sqrt{(Y_{n})^{2}+2\alpha(s-t_{n})}},\quad s\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}].

By (

(32)

Taking conditional expectation on both sides of (

(33)

4.3.
An error bound in

for the CIR splitting scheme

Our third main result provides an order of mean-square convergence for the scheme on a potentially random mesh, where we assume both parts of Assumption

Definition 14
.

Let
(X(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a solution of (
4
) and

Theorem 15

Let
(X(t))_{t\in[0,T]}

). Suppose that Assumptions
1

5

6

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{E}^{2}(t)}\right]\leq C_{4}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{1/2},

where
\mathcal{E}^{2}(t)
is as in Definition
14

Since our proof relies upon Theorem
13

24

41

If the mesh values are deterministic, the bound on the RHS of (

\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{2\kappa}\right\}

Proof.

Let
E_{n}
be as given in Definition
14
and subtract the approximation (
12
) from the variation of constants form of the true solution (
4
), evaluated at

, to get

\displaystyle E_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}E_{n}+\theta(1-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}})-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1}

\displaystyle+e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\sigma^{2}\left({\Delta t}_{n+1}-\Delta W_{n+1}^{2}\right)/4

\displaystyle+\sigma\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\left(e^{-\kappa(t_{n+1}-s)}\sqrt{X(s)}-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\sqrt{X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\right)dW(s)

\displaystyle A+B+C+D,

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}E_{n};

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\theta(1-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}})-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\kappa\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1};

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}}\sigma^{2}({\Delta t}_{n+1}-\Delta W_{n+1}^{2})/4;

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sigma\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\left(e^{-\kappa(t_{n+1}-s)}\sqrt{X(s)}-e^{-\kappa{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\sqrt{X_{n}+2\alpha{\Delta t}_{n+1}}\right)dW(s).

Squaring both sides of (

Taking

. By (

For
AB

\displaystyle\frac{\theta}{2}\kappa^{2}({\Delta t}_{n+1}^{3}+{\Delta t}_{n+1}E_{n}^{2}),\quad a.s.

where we used

in the last inequality.
For

where using the fact that (
41

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{n+1}^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\left(1-2\kappa(1-\kappa{\Delta t}_{\max}){\Delta t}_{n+1}+{\Delta t}_{n+1}\frac{\theta\kappa^{2}}{2}\right)E_{n}^{2}

\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{n+1}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-E_{n}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}\\

, a.s. for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{n=0}^{N^{(t)}-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{E_{n+1}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]-E_{n}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\{N^{(t)}\geq n+1\}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\left.+K_{7}{\Delta t}_{\max}+(3TK_{5}+4\sigma T_{\max}C_{3}){\Delta t}_{\max}^{1/2}\right)

4.4.
A local error bound for the scheme in the

Finally, we show that one step in the

preserves the local error from (

Lemma 16
.

Let
(X(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a solution of (
1
),
X_{n}<X_{\text{zero}}
,
X_{n+1}=X_{\text{zero}}
and
{\Delta t}_{n+1}
be given by (
14
). Suppose that either of Assumptions
2
or
3
holds, and suppose also that for
r,s>0

(51)

\mathbb{E}\left[{|X(t_{n})-X_{n}|}\right]\leq\tilde{C}_{n}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{r}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[{(X(t_{n})-X_{n})^{2}}\right]\leq\tilde{C}_{n}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{s}.

Then there exist constants
\tilde{C}_{n},\tilde{C}_{n+1}>0
, independent of
{\Delta t}_{\max}
, such that

\mathbb{E}\left[{(X(t_{n+1})-X_{\text{zero}})^{2}}\right]\leq\tilde{C}_{n+1}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{\min(s,r+1)}.

Proof.

By construction,

X_{n+1}=X_{\text{zero}}=X_{n}+\kappa\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(\theta-X_{n})ds.

Define
\mathrm{E}_{n+1}:=X(t_{n+1})-X_{\text{zero}}
and

\mathrm{E}_{n}:=X(t_{n})-X_{n}

then

\mathrm{E}_{n+1}=\mathrm{E}_{n}-\kappa\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(X(s)-X_{n})ds+\gamma\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\sqrt{X(s)}dW(s).

Squaring both sides, using the elementary inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3a^{2}+3b^{2}+3c^{2}
and a standard corollary of Jenzen’s inequality,

\mathrm{E}_{n+1}^{2}\leq 3\mathrm{E}_{n}^{2}+3\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(X(s)-X_{n})^{2}ds+3\gamma^{2}\left(\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\sqrt{X(s)}dW(s)\right)^{2}.

Adding in and subtracting out
X(t_{n})
in the first integral, then taking conditional expectation and applying the Itô Isometry, again in its conditional form, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n+1}^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\leq 3\mathrm{E}_{n}^{2}(1+2\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}^{2})\\
+6\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[{(X(s)-X(t_{n}))^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]ds\\
+3\gamma^{2}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[{X(s)}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]ds,\quad a.s.

Now by the bound (
16
) in the statement of Lemma
8
we find

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n+1}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\leq 3\mathrm{E}_{n}^{2}(1+2\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}^{2})\\
+6\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[{(X(s)-X(t_{n}))^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]ds\\
+3\gamma^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}(X(t_{n})+\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1}),\quad a.s.

For the last term we add in and subtract out
X_{n}
and use that
X_{n}\leq X_{\text{zero}}

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n+1}^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\\
\leq 3\mathrm{E}_{n}^{2}(1+2\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}^{2})+6\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[{(X(s)-X(t_{n}))^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]ds\\
+3\gamma^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}\mathrm{E}_{n}+3\gamma^{2}(X_{\text{zero}}+\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1}){\Delta t}_{n+1},\quad a.s.

For the second term it suffices to use the bound (
18
) in Lemma
9
to get

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n+1}^{2}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}\right]\leq 3\mathrm{E}_{n}^{2}(1+2\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}^{2})+24\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}^{2}M_{1,2}(1+X(t_{n})^{2})\\
+3\gamma^{2}{\Delta t}_{n+1}\mathrm{E}_{n}+3\gamma^{2}(X_{\text{zero}}+\theta{\Delta t}_{n+1}){\Delta t}_{n+1},\quad a.s.

Since
X_{\text{zero}}\leq\rho^{-1}\theta{\Delta t}_{\max}
we have, after taking the expectation and using (
15
) with
p=1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n+1}^{2}}\right]\leq 3\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n}^{2}}\right](1+2\kappa^{2}{\Delta t}_{\max}^{2})+24\kappa^{2}(M_{1,2}(1+M_{2})){\Delta t}_{\max}^{2}\\
+3\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{E}_{n}}\right]{\Delta t}_{\max}+3\gamma^{2}\theta(\rho^{-1}+1){\Delta t}_{\max}^{2}.

An application of the bounds (
51
) yields the result.
∎

5.
Numerical results

We compare our new splitting/adaptive method to four other methods in the literature. We let
{\Delta t}
denote a fixed time step and
\Delta W_{n+1}
be an increment of the Brownian motion.
As a reference solution we take
the Milstein method of
[
14
]
, which is known to converge strongly over all
\alpha>0
. For (
1
) this is

\displaystyle R_{1}:=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\sigma\sqrt{{\Delta t}}/2,\sqrt{\max\{\sigma^{2}{\Delta t}/4,X_{n}\}}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\Delta W_{n+1}\right\};

\displaystyle X_{n+1}=

\displaystyle\max\left\{R_{1}^{2}+{\Delta t}(\kappa(\theta-X_{n})-\sigma^{2}/4),0\right\}.

In our figures below we denote this method as
Milstein
.
We also consider the fully truncated method
(denoted
Fully Truncated
) proposed by
[
20
]
given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{n}+{\Delta t}\kappa(\theta-\max\{\widetilde{X}_{n},0\})+\sigma\sqrt{\max\{\widetilde{X}_{n},0\}}\Delta W_{n+1};

\displaystyle X_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{\widetilde{X}_{n+1},0\}.

Both these approaches maintain non-negativity of the numerical approximation by enforcing that the approximated solution is always greater or equal to zero. For larger
{\Delta t}
values this may then be enforced over a few consecutive steps. This is in contrast to
the
soft zero
approach that we take for
\alpha<0
, (
\sigma>2\sqrt{\kappa\theta}
) that, we believe, better mimics the dynamics of the underlying SDE (
1
), (see below).

The last two methods we compare to are
derived from the Lamperti transformation. First is the drift implicit method (denoted
Implicit

[
3
]

Y_{n+1}=\frac{Y_{n}+\gamma\Delta W_{n+1}}{2(1-\beta{\Delta t})}+\sqrt{\frac{(Y_{n}+\gamma\Delta W_{n+1})^{2}}{4(1-\beta{\Delta t})^{2}}+\frac{\alpha{\Delta t}}{1-\beta{\Delta t}}},

\alpha,\beta

\gamma

\alpha\leq 0
