Bochner-Riesz means for the Hermite and special Hermite expansions
Sanghyuk Lee, Jaehyeon Ryu

Abstract
. We consider the Bochner-Riesz means for the Hermite and special Hermite expansions and study their L p L^{p} boundedness with the sharp summability index in a local setting. In two dimensions we establish the boundedness on the optimal range of p p and extend the previously known range in higher dimensions. Furthermore, we prove a new lower bound on the L p L^{p} summability index for the Hermite Bochner-Riesz means in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , d ≥ 2 . d\geq 2. This invalidates the conventional conjecture which was expected to be true.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the Hermite operator

-\Delta+|x|^{2}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{2}+x_{i}^{2},\quad x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}),\quad d\geq 1

which is non-negative and selfadjoint with respect to the Lebesgue measure
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{H}
is given by the set

2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
. Here
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the set of nonnegative integers.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the Hermite polynomial
H_{k}(t)
on
\mathbb{R}
is given by Rodrigues’ formula
H_{k}(t)=(-1)^{k}e^{t^{2}}{d^{k}\over dt^{k}}\big(e^{-t^{2}}\big)
, and the
L^{2}
normalized Hermite functions

h_{k}(t):=(2^{k}k!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}H_{k}(t)e^{-t^{2}/2}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
form an orthonormal basis
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
In higher dimensions
the
d
-dimensional Hermite functions are given by the tensor products of
h_{k}
:

\Phi_{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}),\quad\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\ {\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.}

The Hermite operator and functions respectively represent the Hamiltonian and quantum states of the particle
for the quantum harmonic oscillator. The functions
\Phi_{\alpha}
can also be interpreted as basis functions for the bosonic Fock space via the Bargmann transform. For a detailed discussion regarding the matters, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
. The Hermite operator also appears in the representation theory of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{d}

(see for example
[
32
]
).

The set
\{\Phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
forms a complete orthonormal
system in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the functions
\Phi_{\alpha}
are eigenfunctions for
the Hermite operator with eigenvalue
2|\alpha|+d
where
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
.
Thus, for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have the Hermite expansion

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\langle f,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle\Phi_{\alpha}=\sum_{\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f,

where
\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the Hermite spectral projection given by

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f=\sum_{2|\alpha|+d=\lambda}\langle f,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle\Phi_{\alpha}.

1.1.
Bochner-Riesz means for the Hermite expansion

The Hermite expansion is convergent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
space, but when
d\geq 2
the expansion
\sum_{\lambda\leq N}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f
does not converge to
f
as
N\to\infty
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
unless
p=2
. This can be shown making use of the transplantation theorem due to
Kenig-Stanton-Tomas
[
16
]
and Fefferman’s counterexample for
L^{p}
boundedness of the ball multiplier
[
7
]
(also see
[
30
, Theorem 3.1.2]
). Thus we are naturally led to consider the Bochner-Riesz mean:

S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})f(x):=\Big(1-\frac{\mathcal{H}}{\lambda}\Big)_{+}^{\delta}f(x):=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}\Big(1-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}\Big)_{+}^{\delta}\Pi_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{H}}f.

The summability exponent
\delta
mitigates the influence of new summands
\Pi_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{H}}f
which enter into the summation as
\lambda
increases.
So, the operator
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
has more favorable behavior in perspective of
L^{p}
summability as
\delta
becomes larger.
The classical Bochner-Riesz problem is to determine the optimal summability order
\delta
for which
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(-\Delta)f
converges to
f
in
{L^{p}}
for a given
p\in[1,\infty]
.
When
d=2
, the problem was settled by Carleson-Sjölin
[
4
]
. In higher dimensions progress has been made,
however the problem is still left open.
See
[
25
,
28
,
19
]
and also see
[
9
,
33
]
for most recent results and references therein.

In this paper we are concerned with
{L^{p}}
convergence of the Hermite Bochner-Riesz means, that is to say, the problem of determining the optimal
\delta
for which
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})f
converges to
f
in
L^{p}
.
By the uniform boundedness principle, this problem is equivalent to that of characterizing the optimal
\delta
for which the estimate

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})\|_{p}\leq C

holds with a uniform constant
C
where
\|T\|_{p}:=\sup_{\|f\|_{p}\leq 1}\|Tf\|_{p}
.

When
d=1
, the problem is almost completely settled except some endpoint cases. Askey and Wainger
[
1
]
proved that (
1.1
) holds with
\delta=0
if and only if
4/3<p<4
.
When
p\leq 4/3
or
p\geq 4
, combining this with the result due to Thangavelu
[
29
]
, one can show that
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
is uniformly bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

if
\delta>\max\{\frac{2}{3}|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|-\frac{1}{6},0\}
. On the other hand, Thangavelu
[

showed that (
1.1
) fails to hold if
\delta<\max\{\frac{2}{3}|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|-\frac{1}{6},0\}
.
However, it looks that the estimate (
1.1
) (or its weaker variants) with
\delta=\frac{2}{3}|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}|-\frac{1}{6}
still remains open when
p<4/3
or
p>4
.

In higher dimensions, unlike one dimension, only partial results are known.
By the transplantation theorem due to Kenig, Stanton, and Tomas
[
16

, the bound (
1.1
) implies
that the classical Bochner-Riesz means

is uniformly bounded on

(1.2)

if the uniform bound (
1.1
) holds. This naturally leads to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let

. The uniform estimate (
1.1
) holds if and only if (
1.2

Karadzhov

The estimate plays the role of

[

which deduces the sharp

from the

, the range of

We refer the reader to

]
and references therein for more about the Hermite spectral projection operator. This means the approach in
[

]
relying on the

–

) when

To state our first result, we introduce some notations.
Let us set

\displaystyle p_{0}(d)=\begin{cases}\ 2\cdot\dfrac{3d+2}{3d-2}&\text{if }d\equiv 0\,(\text{mod }2),\\[8.61108pt]

\mathcal{D}(x,y):=1+(\langle x,y\rangle)^{2}-|x|^{2}-|y|^{2},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The following is our first result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that

are compact sets such that

\lambda

\|\chi_{E_{\lambda}}S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})\chi_{F_{\lambda}}\|_{p}\leq C,

\delta>\delta(d,p)
where
E_{\lambda}
,

denote the dilated set
\sqrt{\lambda}E
,
\sqrt{\lambda}F

,

From Theorem (

Corollary 1.3
.

Suppose that

Then for any compact set

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\int_{K}|S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})f(x)-f(x)|^{p}dx=0.

1.2.
Bochner-Riesz means for the special Hermite expansion

Now we consider the twisted Laplacian which is closely related to the Hermite operator.
The twisted Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d}
which is defined by

\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{1}{2}iy_{j}\big)^{2}+\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial{y_{j}}}+\frac{1}{2}ix_{j}\big)^{2}\Big),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

has the same discrete spectrum
2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
as
\mathcal{H}
. The associated eigenfunctions are the special Hermite functions
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
which
are given by the Fourier-Wigner transform of the Hermite functions.
Indeed, for any multi-index
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,

\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z):=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\Phi_{\alpha}(\xi-\frac{1}{2}y)\Phi_{\beta}(\xi+\frac{1}{2}y)d\xi,\quad z=x+iy.

Then it follows that
\mathcal{L}\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}=(2|\beta|+d)\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
, thus
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
with the eigenvalue
2|\beta|+d
and
the eigenspaces of
\mathcal{L}
are infinite dimensional.
Additionally,
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
satisfies
(-\Delta_{z}+\frac{1}{4}|z|^{2})\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}=(|\alpha|+|\beta|+d)\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
, which means
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
is an eigenfunction of the Hermite operator
-\Delta_{z}+\frac{1}{4}|z|^{2}
.
This is the reason that
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
is called the special Hermite function.

For
\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
, by
\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{L}}
we denote the projection to the eigenspace of
\mathcal{L}
with the eigenvalue
\lambda
, i.e.,

(1.5)

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{L}}f(z):=\sum_{\beta:2|\beta|+d=\lambda}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\langle f,\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}\rangle\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z),\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

Since
\{\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}\}_{\alpha,\beta}
is a orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, so one can expand
f
into the series of special Hermite functions. In fact,
we have

f(z)=\sum_{\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{L}}f(z),\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

As seen before in the case of Hermite expansion, by the transplantation in
[
16
]
and Fefferman’s counter example
[
7
]
)
this series fails to converge in
L^{p}
unless
p=2
. So, we need to consider the Bochner-Riesz mean for the special Hermite expansion which is defined by

S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{L})f(z)=\sum_{\mu\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}\Big(1-\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\Big)_{+}^{\delta}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{L}}f(z).

By the uniform boundedness principle, the
L^{p}
convergence of
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{L})f
for all
f\in L^{p}
is equivalent to the uniform estimate

(1.6)

\displaystyle\|S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{L})f\|_{p}\leq C\|f\|_{p}.

The problem has been studied by many authors. By the transplantation theorem in
[
16
]
we see
the estimate (
1.6
) implies
L^{p}
boundedness of the classical Bochner-Riesz operator in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Thus
(
1.6
) holds only if

\delta>\delta(2d,p)
,
p\neq 2
.
It seems to be plausible to conjecture that the uniform estimate (
1.6
) holds if
\delta>\delta(2d,p)
.
In
[
30
]
, Thangavelu verified the conjecture for
\frac{2d}{d-1}\leq p\leq\infty
.
Later, the range was extended to
\frac{2(3d+1)}{3d-2}<p\leq\infty
by Ratnakumar, Rawat, and Thangavelu
[
21
]
.
Further progress was made by Thangavelu
[
31
]
who showed
the local estimate

\|\chi_{E}S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{L})\|_{p}\leq C

holds provided that
\delta>\delta(2d,p)
and
\frac{2(2d+1)}{2d-1}<p\leq\infty
.
The corresponding global estimate was later established by Stempak and Zienkiewicz
[

The common key ingredient of the previous results is the

–

projection estimate of the form

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{L}}\|_{2\to p}\leq C\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}

The projection estimate (

Later, Koch and Ricci

Our approach

The proofs of Theorem
1.4
and
1.2
follow a similar strategy which is inspired by the recent works by Jeong and the authors
[
13
,
14
]
on the spectral projection operators

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{L}}
. First, we obtain a explicit expression for the kernels of the operators
S^{\delta}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})
,
S^{\delta}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L})

using the Schrödinger propagators

,
e^{-it\mathcal{L}}
(see (
2.1
), (
3.1
)) of which kernel representation is well known. Secondly, combining the expression of kernel with the method of stationary phase we obtain asymptotic expansions of the kernels. This reduces the matter to obtaining the sharp estimate for the oscillatory integral operators which satisfy the
Carleson-Sjölin condition

[
4
,
11
,
24
]
. In two dimensions this allows us to obtain the optimal results. However, in higher dimensions the Carleson-Sjölin condition alone is not enough, as was shown by Bourgain
[
3
]
, to give the sharp bound for
p<2(d+1)/(d-1)
(or
p<2(2d+1)/(2d-1)
). However, an additional ellipticity assumption on the
second fundamental form of the phase allows to get the bound on improved range. This observation was first made by one of the author
[
20
]
.
Thirdly, we show that the phases in the asymptotic expansion satisfy the ellipticity condition (see Lemma
3.10
and
2.13
).
We combine this with the results regarding the oscillatory integral operator
[
20
,
9
]
.
We refer the reader forward to Section

2.
Bochner-Riesz means for the Hermite expansion:

Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. We begin by obtaining an explicit expression of the kernel of the Bochner-Riesz means. For the
purpose we make use of Mehler’s formula for the Schrödinger propagator.

2.1.
Decomposition of
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})

We start by considering the Hermite-Schrödinger propagator
e^{-it\mathcal{H}}
which is the solution to
the Cauchy problem
(i\partial_{t}-\mathcal{H})u=0
and
u(0,x)=f(x)
.
The propagator
e^{-it\mathcal{H}}
can be expressed by the spectral projection operators
\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}
:

e^{-it\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}e^{-it\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}.

On the other hand, by virtue of Mehler’s formula we have an explicit expression of the kernel of
e^{-it\mathcal{H}}
.
It is well known that

(2.1)

\displaystyle e^{-it\mathcal{H}}f(x)=(2\pi i\sin 2t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{i\pi d/4}\int e^{\frac{i}{2}((|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})\cot{2t}-2\langle x,y\rangle\csc{2t})}f(y)dy

for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For example, see
[
30
]
and also see
[
22
]
for a detailed discussion regarding derivation of (
2.1
).

To study
L^{p}
boundedness of the Bochner-Riesz means we need to properly decompose the operator
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
.
Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([\frac{1}{4},1])
be a smooth bump function such that
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(2^{j}t)=1
for all
t>0
. Then, setting
\psi^{\delta}(t):=t^{\delta}\psi(t)
,
we denote

\displaystyle\quad\psi_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\psi^{\delta}(2^{-j}t),\quad j\geq 1,

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{\delta}_{+}\sum_{j\geq 0}\psi(2^{j}t),

so that
t^{\delta}=\sum_{1\leq 2^{j}\leq 4\lambda}2^{\delta j}\psi_{j}(t)
for
0\leq t\leq\lambda
. For any bounded continuous function
m
on
\mathbb{R}
, we define an operator
m(\mathcal{H})
by setting

m(\mathcal{H})=\sum_{\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}m(\lambda)\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}.

Then, since
S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})=\lambda^{-\delta}\big(\lambda-\mathcal{H}\big)_{+}^{\delta}
and since the above summation is taken over
the set
2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
, we can write

(2.2)

S_{\lambda}^{\delta}(\mathcal{H})=\lambda^{-\delta}\big(\lambda-\mathcal{H}\big)_{+}^{\delta}=\lambda^{-\delta}\sum_{1\leq 2^{j}\leq 4\lambda}2^{\delta j}\psi_{j}(\lambda-\mathcal{H}).

Though
\psi_{0}
is not smooth, we may assume
\psi_{0}
is smooth replacing it with a suitable smooth function because
\mathcal{H}
has the spectrum
2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
.
Thus, the proof of Theorem
1.2
is reduced to obtaining the sharp
L^{p}
estimate for each operator
\psi_{j}(\lambda-A)
.
That is to say, by (
2.2
) Theorem
1.2
follows if we show

(2.3)

\|\chi_{E_{\lambda}}\psi_{j}(\lambda-\mathcal{H})\chi_{F_{\lambda}}\|_{p}\lesssim(\lambda 2^{-j})^{\delta(d,p)},\quad 1\leq 2^{j}\leq 4\lambda

for
p>p_{0}(d)
.

We now relate the operators
\psi_{j}(\lambda-\mathcal{H})
to the propagator
e^{it\mathcal{H}}
via Fourier inversion.
In fact, for
\eta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
we have

{\mathchoice{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.25pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 6.25pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=4.30554pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=4.9653pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.25pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 6.25pt\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=4.30554pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=4.9653pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\textstyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 5.81944pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 5.81944pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=3.01389pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=4.12018pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptstyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 5.53241pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 5.53241pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=2.15277pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=3.7188pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}}(\lambda-\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\eta(t)e^{i{t}(\lambda-\mathcal{H})}dt.

Combining this with
(

{\mathchoice{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.25pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 6.25pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=4.30554pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=4.9653pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.25pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 6.25pt\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=4.30554pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=4.9653pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\textstyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 5.81944pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 5.81944pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=3.01389pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=4.12018pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptstyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 5.53241pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 5.53241pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=2.15277pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=3.7188pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\eta$}\crcr}}}}}(\lambda-\mathcal{H})(x,y)=C_{d}\int\eta(t/2)(\sin t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{i\phi_{\lambda}(t,x,y)}dt,

where

Instead of dealing with

for

\displaystyle[\zeta]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle:={\mathchoice{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 7.31482pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 7.31482pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=7.5pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=22.15279pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\zeta(2\cdot)$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 7.31482pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 7.31482pt\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=7.5pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=22.15279pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\textstyle\zeta(2\cdot)$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.56482pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 6.56482pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=5.25pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=18.47223pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptstyle\zeta(2\cdot)$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.06482pt\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}[-1.2]{\lower 6.06482pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=3.75pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=16.66676pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\zeta(2\cdot)$}\crcr}}}}}(\lambda-\mathcal{H})(\sqrt{\lambda}x,\sqrt{\lambda}y)

(2.12)

\displaystyle\,=C_{d}\int\zeta(t)(\sin t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{i\lambda\mathcal{P}_{\!\,\mathcal{H}}(t,x,y)}dt,

By scaling, the estimate (

(2.14)

\|\chi_{E}[\widehat{\psi_{j}}(\cdot/2)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\chi_{F}\|_{p}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{d}{2}}(\lambda 2^{-j})^{\delta(d,p)},\quad 1\leq 2^{j}\leq 4\lambda.

We occasionally use the following lemma which is a simple consequence of the formula (

) for the kernel.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,\infty))
. Then, for any measurable sets

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{E}[\zeta]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\,\chi_{F}\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\|\chi_{E}[\zeta(-\,\cdot)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\,\chi_{F}\|_{p},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{E}[\zeta]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\,\chi_{F}\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\|\chi_{E}[\zeta(\cdot+\pi)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\,\chi_{(-F)}\|_{p}.

Proof.

We observe that

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t+\pi,x,y)=\frac{\pi}{2}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,x,-y).

Using this, (

) and changing of variables, we have

(2.17)

\displaystyle[\zeta(-\cdot)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle=C_{1}\overline{[\zeta]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(x,y)},

(2.18)

\displaystyle[\zeta(\cdot+\pi)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle=C_{2}[\zeta]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(x,-y),

where

.
Thus, (
2.17

), (

In the expression (

{\nu_{j}^{l,k}}(t)=\widehat{\psi_{j}}(t/2){\nu^{l}(t-2k\pi),}

[\widehat{\psi_{j}}(\cdot/2)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}[\nu_{j}^{l,k}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

\displaystyle\|\chi_{E}[\eta_{\rho}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\chi_{F}\|_{p}\lesssim\begin{cases}\ \lambda^{-\frac{d}{2}}\rho,&\rho\leq\lambda^{-1},\\
\ \lambda^{-\frac{d}{2}}\lambda^{\delta(d,p)}\rho^{\delta(d,p)+1},&\rho>\lambda^{-1}.\end{cases}

Once we have Theorem

) is rather straightforward.
Assuming Theorem

Proof of (

Recalling (

By (

\|\chi_{E}[\nu_{j}^{l,k}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\chi_{F}\|_{p}\lesssim\begin{cases}\lambda^{-\frac{d}{2}}2^{-l}2^{j}(1+2^{j}|k|)^{-N},&4\lambda<2^{l},\\

Using (

) and taking summation over

2.2.
Further decomposition and reduction

From now on we extensively utilize the expression (
2.12
). To obtain estimates from the oscillatory integral we need to collect
some properties of the phase function
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Using (
2.13
), a computation
gives

(2.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\cos^{2}t-2\langle x,y\rangle\cos t+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}-1}{2\sin^{2}t}.

Since
\mathcal{D}(x,y)\geq c_{0}^{2}
for
(x,y)\in\mathfrak{D}(c_{0})
, we can factor

\cos^{2}t-2\langle x,y\rangle\cos t+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}-1=(\cos t-\langle x,y\rangle-\mathcal{D}(x,y))(\cos t-\langle x,y\rangle+\mathcal{D}(x,y))

One can easily see
-1<\langle x,y\rangle\pm\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)}<1
for
(x,y)\in\mathfrak{D}(c_{0})
because
|\langle x,y\rangle|<1
if
(x,y)\in\mathfrak{D}(c_{0})
.
Thus we can define smooth functions
S_{c}(x,y),

S_{*}(x,y)\in(0,\pi)
by setting

(2.23)

\displaystyle\cos S_{c}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle x,y\rangle+\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)},\quad(x,y)\in\mathfrak{D}(c_{0}),

(2.24)

\displaystyle\cos S_{*}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle x,y\rangle-\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)},\quad(x,y)\in\mathfrak{D}(c_{0}).

So, we may also write

(2.25)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,x,y)=-\frac{(\cos t-\cos S_{c}(x,y))(\cos t-\cos S_{*}(x,y))}{2\sin^{2}t}\,.

Also, since
\sin^{2}S_{c}(x,y)=(1-\langle x,y\rangle-\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)})(1+\langle x,y\rangle+\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)})
and
(1-\langle x,y\rangle)^{2}-\mathcal{D}(x,y)=|x-y|^{2}

(2.26)

\displaystyle\sin S_{c}(x,y)=|x-y|\bigg(\frac{1-\langle x,y\rangle+\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)}}{1+\langle x,y\rangle+\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

1\pm\langle x,y\rangle+\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x,y)}\gtrsim c_{0}

(x,y)\in\mathfrak{D}(c_{0})
.

S_{c}(x,y)\sim|x-y|
. A similar computation shows

.

,

2.5.
Carleson-Sjölin type operator

Let
A\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
and
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\supp A)
.
Let
T_{\lambda}[\phi,A]
denote the operator defined by

T_{\lambda}[\phi,A]f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}e^{i\lambda\phi(x,\xi)}A(x,\xi)f(\xi)d\xi.

We assume that the mixed Hessian
\partial_{\xi}\partial^{\intercal}_{x}\phi
has the maximal rank,
i.e.,

(C1)

\displaystyle\rank(\partial_{\xi}\partial^{\intercal}_{x}\phi(x,\xi))=d-1,\quad(x,\xi)\in\supp A

This means that the image of
\xi\to\partial_{x}\phi(x_{0},\xi)
is a smooth immersed surface in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The condition guarantees that, for any
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\supp A
, there is a unique vector
\nu(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
modulo
\pm
such that

\partial_{\xi}\langle\partial_{x}\phi(x_{0},\xi),\nu(x_{0},\xi_{0})\rangle\big|_{\xi=\xi_{0}}=0.

We further assume that the parameterized surface
\xi\to\partial_{x}\phi(x_{0},\xi)
has nonvanishing Gaussian curvature.
Equivalently, the second fundamental form of the surface parameterized by
\xi\to\partial_{x}\phi(x_{0},\xi)
is not singular,
i.e.,

\rank(\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\xi}^{\intercal}\langle\partial_{x}\phi(x_{0},\xi),\nu(x_{0},\xi_{0})\rangle\big|_{\xi=\xi_{0}})=d-1,\ \ (x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\supp A.

The conditions (
C1
) and (
2.5
) together are called the
Carleson-Sjölin condition
, and
if an oscillatory integral operator
T_{\lambda}[\phi,A]
satisfies both (
C1
) and (
2.5
), we say
T_{\lambda}[\phi,A]
is a
Carleson-Sjölin type operator
.
Concerning the Carleson-Sjölin type operators, the estimate of the form

(2.58)

\displaystyle\|T_{\lambda}[\phi,A]f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{d}{q}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})}

3

]

]
conjectured that (

and
\frac{1}{q}\leq\frac{d-1}{(d+1)p^{\prime}}

2

2

3.3.
The phase function
\Phi_{\!\mathcal{L}}(z,z^{\prime})

For

We occasionally denote

We compute the mixed Hessian
\partial_{z^{\prime}}\partial_{z}^{\intercal}\Phi_{\mathcal{L}}

(3.40)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{z^{\prime}}\partial_{z}^{\intercal}\Phi_{\!\mathcal{L}}&=\partial_{z^{\prime}}\partial_{z}^{\intercal}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})+\partial_{z^{\prime}}\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})\cdot\partial_{z}^{\intercal}\mathfrak{S}_{c}\\

Here we use
\ \partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})=0

From (

\displaystyle\partial_{z^{\prime}}\partial_{z}^{\intercal}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\cos\mathfrak{S}_{c}}{2\sin\mathfrak{S}_{c}}\,\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{S},

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{\intercal}\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}}{2\sin^{2}\mathfrak{S}_{c}},

\displaystyle\partial_{z^{\prime}}\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})

Since

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{S}_{c},z,z^{\prime})=\frac{4\cos\mathfrak{S}_{c}}{|z-z^{\prime}|},

\sup_{x\in B(x_{\ast},10^{2}\lambda^{-1/6})}\int_{Q}\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})(x,y)^{2}dy=\|\chi_{B(x_{\ast},10^{2}\lambda^{-1/6})}\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})\chi_{Q}\|_{2\to\infty}^{2}.

Thus (
4.3
) follows once we find a function
g
such that

(4.6)

\|\chi_{B(x_{\ast},10^{2}\lambda^{-1/6})}\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})\chi_{Q}g\|_{\infty}\gtrsim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{12}}\|g\|_{2}.

Let
k:=\frac{\lambda-d}{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then, we define the set of indices
J
by

J=\Big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:|\alpha|=k,\,{\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}}/{d}\leq\alpha_{i}\leq{2\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}}/{d},\,2\leq i\leq d\Big\}.

We note that
\lambda-2\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\lambda
for
\alpha\in J
. Thus, using Lemma
4.4
, we see

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda^{1/2}-20\lambda^{-1/6}}^{\lambda^{1/2}-10\lambda^{-1/6}}h_{\alpha_{1}}(t)^{2}dt\sim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}},\quad\int_{-\frac{\lambda^{-1/6}}{\sqrt{d}}}^{\frac{\lambda^{-1/6}}{\sqrt{d}}}h_{\alpha_{i}}(t)^{2}dt\sim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}},\quad 2\leq i\leq d

whenever
\alpha\in J
.
Denote
D=[-\frac{\lambda^{-\frac{1}{6}}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\lambda^{-\frac{1}{6}}}{\sqrt{d}}]^{d-1}
.
Since
|J|\sim\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{3}}
and
\Phi_{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})
, by using the estimates in the above we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in J}\int_{\lambda^{1/2}-20\lambda^{-1/6}}^{\lambda^{1/2}-10\lambda^{-1/6}}\int_{D}\Phi_{\alpha}(x_{1},x^{\prime})^{2}dx^{\prime}dx_{1}\sim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}}.

Thus there exists a point
\widetilde{x}_{*}\in[\lambda^{1/2}-20\lambda^{-1/6},\lambda^{1/2}-10\lambda^{-1/6}]\times D\subset B(x_{\ast},10^{2}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{6}})
such that
\sum_{\alpha\in J}\Phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\widetilde{x}_{*})\sim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}
.
Now we set

g(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in J}\Phi_{\alpha}(\widetilde{x}_{*})\Phi_{\alpha}(x).

We proceed to prove that
g
satisfies (
4.6
).
By orthogonality, we have
\|g\|_{2}=\big(\sum_{\alpha\in J}\Phi_{\alpha}(\widetilde{x}_{*})^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{12}}
, so (
4.6
)
follows if we show

(4.7)

\|\chi_{B(x_{\ast},10^{2}\lambda^{-1/6})}\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})\chi_{Q}g\|_{\infty}\gtrsim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}.

We write

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})\chi_{Q}g(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=k}\sum_{\beta\in J}\Phi_{\alpha}(x)\Phi_{\beta}(\widetilde{x}_{*})\int_{Q}\Phi_{\alpha}(y)\Phi_{\beta}(y)dy

\displaystyle=:\mathbf{I}(x)+\mathbf{II}(x),

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in J}\Phi_{\alpha}(x)\Phi_{\alpha}(\widetilde{x}_{*})\int_{Q}\Phi_{\alpha}(y)\Phi_{\alpha}(y)dy,

\displaystyle\mathbf{II}(x)=\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}\Phi_{\alpha}(x)\Phi_{\beta}(\widetilde{x}_{*})\int_{Q}\Phi_{\alpha}(y)\Phi_{\beta}(y)dy.

We first handle
\mathbf{I}
.
By the choice of
J
,
\lambda-2\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\lambda
and
{\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}}/{d}\leq\alpha_{i}\leq{2\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}}/{d}
for
i=2,\dots,d
. Thus, using Lemma
4.4
we see that

\int_{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}/200\leq|t|\leq\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}/100}h_{\alpha_{1}}(t)^{2}dt\sim 1,\quad\int_{|t|\leq 10^{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{6}}}h_{\alpha_{i}}(t)^{2}dt\sim 1,\quad 2\leq i\leq d

for any
\alpha\in J
. Thus, we have
\int_{Q}\Phi_{\alpha}(x)^{2}dx\gtrsim 1
for
\alpha\in J
, so
\mathbf{I}(x)
satisfies the estimate

|\mathbf{I}(x_{*})|\gtrsim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}

because
\sum_{\alpha\in J}\Phi_{\alpha}^{2}(x_{*})\sim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}
.
For
\mathbf{II}(x)
, we make use of the following formula

\int_{-l}^{l}h_{u}(t)h_{v}(t)dt=\frac{1+(-1)^{u+v}}{\sqrt{2}(u-v)}\big(\sqrt{u+1}\,h_{u+1}(l)h_{v}(l)-\sqrt{v+1}\,h_{u}(l)h_{v+1}(l)\big),\quad u\neq v.

See
[
13
, pp.24–25]
for its proof. By Lemma
4.4
it follows that
|h_{v}(10^{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{6}})|\lesssim e^{-c\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}}
for some
c>0
when
v\leq 2\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}d^{-1}
. Also, if
\alpha\neq\beta
, there exists at least one
i
’s such that
2\leq i\leq d
and
\alpha_{i}\neq\beta_{i}
because
|\alpha|=|\beta|=k
. Therefore, we have

\bigg|\int_{Q}\Phi_{\alpha}(y)\Phi_{\beta}(y)dy\bigg|\leq\prod_{i=2}^{d}\int_{-10^{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{6}}}^{10^{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{6}}}h_{\alpha_{i}}(t)h_{\beta_{i}}(t)dt\lesssim\lambda^{N}e^{-c\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}}

for some constants
c>0
and
N
. From Lemma
4.4
we see
\mathbf{II}(x_{*})=O(\lambda^{N}e^{-c\lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}})
.
Combining this and the lower bound for
\mathbf{I}(x_{*})
together with the estimate for
\|g\|_{2}
, we obtain
(
4.7
) taking
\lambda
large enough.
∎

4.2.
Proof of (
4.4
)

To show (
4.4
), we make use of the following Lemma, which we prove later.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
and
\mu\in[\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}},\frac{1}{4}]
.
Suppose that
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a eigenfunction of
\mathcal{H}
with the eigenvalue
\lambda
, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}h(x)=\lambda h(x)
. If

\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-2\mu)\leq|y_{0}|\leq\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-\mu),

then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we have

(4.8)

|\partial_{y}^{\alpha}h(y_{0})|\leq C(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(B(y_{0},2(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}}))}

with
C
independent of
\lambda
,
\mu
and
h
.

We start by recalling the estimate

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{A_{\lambda,0}}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\chi_{A_{\lambda,0}}\|_{1\to\infty}\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}},

which can be found in (
[
13
, p.79]
,
[
18
, p.375]
). Here
A_{\lambda,0}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:||x|-\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}|\leq 10^{3}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{6}}\}
.
This is equivalent to the estimate
|\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)|\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}
for any
x,y\in A_{\lambda,0}
.
Now we note that

(4.10)

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f_{\lambda}(x)=\int_{Q}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0},y)\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)dy.

Using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and orthogonality between the Hermite functions
\Phi_{\alpha}
, we get

|\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f_{\lambda}(x)|\leq\bigg(\int\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0},y)^{2}dy\int\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)^{2}dy\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\big(\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x_{0},x_{0})\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,x)\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By (
4.9
), we see that
|\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f_{\lambda}(x)|\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}
for every
x\in A_{\lambda,0}
.
Since
\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and trivially is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{H}
with the eigenvalue
\lambda
,
by Lemma
4.5
we see

|\nabla(\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f_{\lambda})(x)|\lesssim C\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{6}}

if
||x|-\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}|\leq 10^{2}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{6}}
.
On the other hand, from (
4.3
) and (
4.10
) we have
\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f_{\lambda}(x_{0})\gtrsim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}.

Thus, it follows that

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}f(x)\gtrsim\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{6}}

if
x\in B(x_{0},c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{6}})
with a sufficiently small
c>0
. We therefore get
(
4.4
). ∎

We now turn to the proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

To show (
4.8
) we may assume
\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(B(y_{0},2(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}}))}=1
.
Let
\varphi
be a smooth cutoff function such that
\supp\varphi\subset B(0,2)
and
\varphi\equiv 1
on
B(0,1)
.
Let us set
\phi_{y_{0}}(y)=\varphi(\sqrt{\lambda\mu}(y-y_{0}))
.
Then by inversion we write

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{\alpha}h(y_{0})=\partial_{x}^{\alpha}(\varphi_{y_{0}}h)(y_{0})=(2\pi)^{-d}\iint(i\xi)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\varphi_{y_{0}}(y)e^{i(y_{0}-y)\cdot\xi}h(y)dyd\xi.

Setting
\phi_{K}(\xi)=\varphi({\xi}/{K\sqrt{\lambda\mu}})
with a large positive constant
K
, we split the integral

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{\alpha}h(y_{0})=\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{I\!I},

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}

\displaystyle:=(2\pi)^{-d}\iint(i\xi)^{\alpha}{\phi_{K}}(\xi)\phi_{y_{0}}(y)e^{i(y_{0}-y)\cdot\xi}h(y)dyd\xi,

\displaystyle\mathrm{I\!I}

\displaystyle:=(2\pi)^{-d}\iint(i\xi)^{\alpha}(1-\phi_{K}(\xi))\phi_{y_{0}}(y)e^{i(y_{0}-y)\cdot\xi}h(y)dyd\xi.

For
\mathrm{I}
we have

|{\mathrm{I}}|\lesssim\iint|\xi|^{|\alpha|}|\varphi_{K}(\xi)\phi_{y_{0}}(y)h(y)|dyd\xi\leq C(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}.

Since
(|\xi|^{2}+\Delta_{y})h(y)=|\xi|^{2}+{|y|^{2}}-\lambda
, we may write

\mathrm{I\!I}=C\iint{(i\xi)^{\alpha}}\frac{(1-\varphi_{K}(\xi))\phi_{y_{0}}(y)}{|\xi|^{2}+y^{2}-\lambda}e^{i(y_{0}-y)\cdot\xi}(\xi^{2}+\Delta_{y})h(y)dyd\xi.

By integration by parts, this is equal to

C\iint\xi^{\alpha}(1-\varphi_{K}(\xi))A_{1}(y,\xi)e^{i(y_{0}-y)\cdot\xi}h(y)dyd\xi,

where

A_{1}(y,\xi):=\Delta_{y}\bigg(\frac{\phi_{y_{0}}(y)}{\xi^{2}+y^{2}-\lambda}\bigg)-2i\nabla_{y}\bigg(\frac{\phi_{y_{0}}(y)}{\xi^{2}+y^{2}-\lambda}\bigg)\cdot\xi.

We now note that
\xi^{2}+y^{2}-\lambda>\frac{K}{2}(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}
for
(y,\xi)\in{\supp(\phi_{y_{0}})\times\supp(\phi_{K})}
with a sufficiently large
K
and
|\partial_{y}^{\alpha}\phi_{y_{0}}(y)|\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. Thus, we have

|A_{1}(y,\xi)|\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}{|\xi|^{-1}}.

Repeating this by
N
times, we have

\mathrm{I\!I}=C_{N}\iint\xi^{\alpha}(1-{\phi_{K}}(\xi))A_{N}(y,\xi)e^{i(y_{0}-y)\cdot\xi}h(y)dyd\xi,

where
A_{N}
is a smooth function such that
\supp A_{N}\subset\supp(\phi_{y_{0}}\otimes\varphi_{K})
and
|A_{N}(y,\xi)|\lesssim|\xi|^{-N}(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{N}{2}}
.
Since
\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(B(y_{0},2(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}}))}=1
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathrm{I\!I}|\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{N}{2}}\int_{K\sqrt{\lambda\mu}\leq|\xi|}|\xi|^{|\alpha|-N}\int_{|y-y_{0}|\leq 2/\sqrt{\lambda\mu}}dyd\xi\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}.

Therefore, we obtain (
4.8
).
∎

4.3.
Proof of (
4.5
)

Let us set
\widetilde{x}_{0}=\lambda^{-1/2}x_{0}
.
Changing variables, we note that the estimate (
4.5
) is equivalent to

(4.11)

\|\chi_{\widetilde{Q}}\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{x}_{0},\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot)\|_{p}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{d-1}{3p}},

where

\widetilde{Q}=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:1/200\leq|x_{1}|\leq 1/100,\ |x_{i}|\leq 10^{2}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}},\ 2\leq i\leq d\,\big\}.

We recall the representation formula

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(\lambda-\mathcal{H})t}dt,

which can be easily shown by utilizing the Hermite expansion and the fact that
\lambda\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d
.
For example, see
[
13
, p.11]
for the detail. Using the cutoff functions
\nu^{l}
(see (
2.19
)) and scaling,
we can write

\Pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{x}_{0},\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}x)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}[\nu^{l}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y).

Thus, the desired estimate (
4.11
) follows if we show

\displaystyle\|[\nu^{0}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\widetilde{Q})}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{d-1}{3p}},

\displaystyle\|[\nu^{l}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\widetilde{Q})}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{d-1}{3p}-N}2^{l(\frac{d-2}{2}-N)},\quad l\geq 1.

We recall (
2.19
). Then, using the properties
(
2.17
)
and
(
2.18
), we see that the above estimates follow if we obtain

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|[\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\widetilde{Q})}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{d-1}{3p}},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\|[\psi(2^{l}\cdot)]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\widetilde{Q})}\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{d-1}{3p}-N}2^{l(\frac{d-2}{2}-N)},\quad l\geq 1.

Since
\widetilde{x}_{0}\in B((1-10^{2}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}})\mathbf{e}_{1},10^{2}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}})
, for
y\in\widetilde{Q}
we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)|=(1-|\widetilde{x}_{0}|^{2})(1-|y|^{2})+|\widetilde{x}_{0}|^{2}|y|^{2}-\langle\widetilde{x}_{0},y\rangle^{2}\leq 10^{3}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}.

We also have
|\langle\widetilde{x}_{0},y\rangle|\leq 10^{-2}
for
y\in\widetilde{Q}
. Thus,
from (
2.22
) we have

\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,\widetilde{x}_{0},y)=\frac{(\cos t-\langle\widetilde{x}_{0},y\rangle)^{2}-\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)}{2\sin^{2}t}\gtrsim 2^{2l}

if
t\in\supp\psi(2^{l}\cdot)
,
l\geq 1
since
\lambda
is assumed to be large. Recalling (
2.12
) we have

[{\psi(2^{l}\cdot)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)=C_{d}\int\psi(2^{l}t)(\sin t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{i\lambda\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,\widetilde{x}_{0},y)}dt.

Since
{|\partial_{t}^{n}(\psi(2^{l}t)(\sin t)^{-\frac{d}{2}})|\lesssim 2^{l(n+\frac{d}{2})}}
for any
n\geq 1
, applying Lemma
2.5

to
[\psi^{l}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)
, we obtain

|[\psi^{l}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)|\lesssim\lambda^{-N}2^{j(\frac{d-2}{2}-N)},\quad N\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, combining this with
|\widetilde{Q}|\sim\lambda^{-\frac{(d-1)}{3}}
, we get (
4.13
).

Let
t_{*}
be a number in
[0,\pi]
such that

\cos t_{*}=\langle\widetilde{x}_{0},y\rangle.

The point
t_{*}
can be contained in the support of
\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}
.
To show the estimate (
4.12
), we make additional decomposition away from
t_{\ast}
. Let us set

\psi_{l}(t)=\psi(2^{l}(t-t_{\ast}))+\psi(2^{l}(t_{\ast}-t)).

and also set

\psi_{\lambda}(t):=\sum_{2^{-l}\leq C\lambda^{-1/3}}\psi_{l}(t),

where
C
is a sufficiently large constant.
We make a further decomposition on
[\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)
as follows. Then we may write

\displaystyle[\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)

\displaystyle=[\psi_{\lambda}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)+\sum_{2^{-l}>C\lambda^{-1/3}}[\psi_{l}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y).

Since
\sin t\sim 1
on
t\in\supp(\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)})\subset[2^{-2},\pi-2^{-2}]
,

|[\psi_{\lambda}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)|\lesssim\int|\psi_{\lambda}(t)|dt\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}}.

Now we note that
(\cos t-\cos t_{\ast})^{2}\sim 2^{-2l}
on the support of
\psi_{l}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}
.
Since

\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,\widetilde{x}_{0},y)=\frac{(\cos t-\cos t_{\ast})^{2}-\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)}{2\sin^{2}t}
,
by (
4.14
)
we have

\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{H}}(t,\widetilde{x}_{0},y)\gtrsim 2^{-2l}

for
t\in\supp(\psi_{l}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)})
. So, Lemma
2.5
gives

[\psi_{l}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)\lesssim\lambda^{-N}2^{l(-1+3N)}
, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,

\sum_{2^{-l}>C\lambda^{-1/3}}|[\psi_{l}\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)|\lesssim\lambda^{-1/3}
.
Combining these estimate, we now get

|[\nu^{0}\chi_{(0,\pi)}]^{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{x}_{0},y)|\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{3}}.

Therefore, we get (
4.12
) because
|\widetilde{Q}|\sim\lambda^{-\frac{(d-1)}{3}}
. This completes the proof of (
4.5
).
∎
