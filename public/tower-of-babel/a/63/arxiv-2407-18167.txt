Ádám Kunos, Benoît Larose, David Emmanuel Pazmiño Pullas

Abstract
. Call a finite relational structure k k -Słupecki if its only surjective k k -ary polymorphisms are essentially unary, and Słupecki if it is k k -Słupecki for all k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . We present conditions, some necessary and some sufficient, for a reflexive digraph to be Słupecki. We prove that all digraphs that triangulate a 1-sphere are Słupecki, as are all the ordinal sums m ⊕ n m\oplus n ( m , n ≥ 2 m,n\geq 2 ). We prove that the posets ℙ = m ⊕ n ⊕ k \mathbb{P}=m\oplus n\oplus k are not 3-Słupecki for m , n , k ≥ 2 m,n,k\geq 2 , and prove there is a bound B ⁡ ( m , k ) B(m,k) such that ℙ \mathbb{P} is 2-Słupecki if and only if n > B ⁡ ( m , k ) + 1 n>B(m,k)+1 ; in particular there exist posets that are 2-Słupecki but not 3-Słupecki.



1.
Introduction

For our purposes, a digraph is a finite, non-empty set
A
together with a binary relation on
A
.
In this paper, all digraphs are assumed to be reflexive
, i.e. that the binary relation contains
(a,a)
for all
a\in A
. We study the surjective polymorphisms of these objects; these operations play an important role in the complexity of the related quantified constraint satisfaction problem (QCSP) and the surjective H-colouring problem, as do idempotent polymorphisms in the study of the associated CSP (see for instance
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
16
]
).

A digraph is
k
-
idempotent trivial
if all its
k
-ary idempotent polymorphisms are projections, and
idempotent trivial
if it is
k
-idempotent trivial for all
k
. Similarly, we say a digraph is
k
-
Słupecki
if all its
k
-ary surjective polymorphisms are essentially unary, and
Słupecki
if it is
k
-Słupecki for all
k
. Clearly a digraph is idempotent trivial if it is Słupecki. It is known that these properties respectively imply NP-hardness and PSPACE-hardness of the CSP and QCSP naturally associated to the digraph (see
[
10
]
and
[
16
]
). The Słupecki property has also been studied independently, see for example
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
.

The starting point of our investigation was to find some workable sufficient condition for a digraph to be Słupecki: in particular, we wanted to verify that all cycles of girth at least 4 have this property. There is a known sufficient condition for a digraph to be idempotent trivial that relies on the homotopy of a simplicial complex naturally associated to the digraph (see section
2
): if for some
n>0
the
n
-th homotopy group of the space is non-trivial, but the
n
-th homotopy group of any proper retract is trivial, and provided the identity is isolated in the digraph of endomorphisms, then the digraph is idempotent trivial
[
9
]
. It turns out that digraphs that triangulate
n
-spheres satisfy these properties, and thus are potentially Słupecki. At first glance, the most straightforward approach to show a structure is Słupecki is to pp-define the Słupecki relation from its basic relations (see Section
4
), and we manage to do this for a variety of digraphs triangulating
n
-spheres; however, this approach seems impractical for general cycles. In section
3
we present an alternative sufficient condition: provided the identity is isolated in the digraph of endomorphisms, if every onto polymorphism of the idempotent trivial digraph is a retraction, then the digraph is Słupecki (see Lemma
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
). In a companion paper, this result is used to prove that cycles of girth at least 4 are indeed Słupecki
[
6
]
. The slightly annoying technical condition on the identity endomorphism can be removed for several families of digraphs (Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
), but we provide an example of a Słupecki digraph that does not satisfy it (Lemma
3.8
).
However, we do not know if the condition can be removed from the statement of Lemma
3.2
.

As mentioned above, examining small digraphs triangulating spheres, it turns out that many are Słupecki, such as for instance, all digraphs that triangulate a 1-sphere (Theorem
4.11
), but in section
5
we provide an example of a small digraph triangulating a 2-sphere which isn’t Słupecki: indeed, the poset
2\oplus 2\oplus 2
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
5.2
, and is the suspension of a 4-cycle. Furthermore, we provide examples of posets that are 2-Słupecki but not 3-Słupecki (Theorem
5.5
); contrast this with the fact that all digraphs that are 2-idempotent trivial are idempotent trivial (see
[
17
]
).

We now outline the contents of the paper. In section
2
we introduce basic terminology and notation. In section
3
we present various sufficient and necessary conditions for a digraph to be Słupecki. The main result of that section, Theorem
3.3
, is a crucial tool for the main result of the paper
[
6
]
, namely that all cycles of girth at least 4 are Słupecki, and is also used in section
5
to provide some examples of Słupecki posets. In section
4
we prove that various digraphs that triangulate spheres are indeed Słupecki, via pp-definitions using gadgets. We also state the result that all digraphs that triangulate 1-spheres are Słupecki (Theorem
4.11
). In section
5
we consider posets that are ordinal sums of antichains; we exhibit small digraphs that triangulate spheres but are not Słupecki (Theorem
5.2
); we also provide examples of posets that are 3-Słupecki but not 2-Słupecki Theorem
5.5
. In section
6
we discuss various open questions that follow naturally from our results.

2.
Preliminaries: notation, definitions, etc.

2.1.
Reflexive digraphs

A
digraph

\mathbb{G}=\langle G;E\rangle
consists of a non-empty set
G
of
vertices
and a binary relation
E
on
G
; the pairs in
E
are called the
arcs
or
edges
of
\mathbb{G}
. It is
reflexive
if
(x,x)\in E
for all
x\in E
; an arc of the form
(x,x)
we call a
loop
. When we consider digraphs
\mathbb{G}
,
\mathbb{H}
, etc. we denote their set of vertices by
G
,
H
, etc. We sometimes write
u\rightarrow v
to mean that
(u,v)
is an arc of a digraph. Consider the undirected graph
\mathbb{G}_{Sym}
obtained from the digraph
\mathbb{G}
as follows: it has the same set of vertices
G
, and two vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent if one of
(x,y)
or
(y,x)
is an arc of
\mathbb{G}
. We say
\mathbb{G}
is
connected
if
\mathbb{G}_{Sym}
is connected. We say the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is
symmetric
if
x\rightarrow y
implies
y\rightarrow x
for all
x,y\in G
(i.e.
\mathbb{G}
is undirected). Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph and
x,y\in G
. We say that
x
and
y
are in the same strong component of
\mathbb{G}
if there exist
x=x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{t}=y
and
y=y_{0},y_{1},\cdots,y_{s}=x
such that
x_{i}\rightarrow x_{i+1}
for all
0\leq i\leq t-1
and
y_{j}\rightarrow y_{j+1}
for all
0\leq j\leq s-1
. Clearly every digraph is partitioned into strongly connected components. We say
\mathbb{G}
is
strongly connected
if it has exactly one strong component. We say that a digraph
\mathbb{H}
is
embedded
in a digraph
\mathbb{G}
if it is isomorphic to an induced subdigraph of
\mathbb{G}
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
be digraphs. A map
f:G\rightarrow H
is a
homomorphism
if it preserves arcs, i.e. if
(x,y)
is an arc of
\mathbb{G}
then
(f(x),f(y))
is an arc of
\mathbb{H}
. If
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
are digraphs and we write
f:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
it is understood that
f
is a homomorphism. We say
f:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
is an
embedding
if it is an isomorphism onto its image (and hence
\mathbb{H}
is embedded in
\mathbb{G}
). The
product

\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}
of two digraphs
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
is the usual product of relational structures, i.e. the digraph with set of vertices
G\times H
and arcs
((g_{1},h_{1}),(g_{2},h_{2}))
where
(g_{1},g_{2})
and
(h_{1},h_{2})
are arcs of
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
respectively; notice that
\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}
is reflexive if both
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
are reflexive. For every positive integer
k
, the product of
k
digraphs is defined is the obvious way;
\mathbb{G}^{k}
is the product of
\mathbb{G}
with itself
k
times. A
k
-ary
polymorphism
of
\mathbb{G}
is a homomorphism from
\mathbb{G}^{k}
to
\mathbb{G}
; the integer
k
is the
arity
of
f
;
f
is
idempotent
if
f(x,\dots,x)=x
for all
x\in G
. It is
essentially unary
if there exists some
1\leq i\leq k
and a homomorphism
g:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
such that
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=g(x_{i})
for all
x_{j}\in G
; it is a
projection
if furthermore the homomorphism
g
is the identity.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
. We say that the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is
k
-idempotent trivial if all its idempotent
k
-ary polymorphisms are projections. We say that the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial if it is
k
-idempotent trivial for all
k\geq 2
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
. We say that the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is
k
-Słupecki if all its surjective
k
-ary polymorphisms are essentially unary. We say the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is Słupecki if it is
k
-Słupecki for all
k\geq 2
.

It is easy to see that if a digraph is
k
-Słupecki for some
k\geq 3
then it is
(k-1)
-Słupecki. Note also that a Słupecki digraph is idempotent trivial.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
k\geq 0
. A path of length
k
is a digraph with vertex set
\{0,1,\dots,k\}
where for each
0\leq i\leq k-1
, one or both of the arcs
\{(i,i+1),(i+1,i)\}
is
present, and there are no other arcs.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
. An
n
-cycle is a digraph with vertex set
\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
where for each
0\leq i<n-1
, one or both of the arcs
\{(i,i+1),(i+1,i)\}
is present, one or both of the arcs
\{(n-1,0),(0,n-1)\}
is present, and there are no other arcs. The integer
n
is called the girth of the cycle.

We now discuss briefly the connection of reflexive digraphs to simplicial complexes: all details can be found in
[
13
]
, see also
[
9
]
. A digraph
\mathbb{P}
is a
poset
if its edge relation is reflexive, antisymmetric (if
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
are arcs then
x=y
) and transitive (if
(x,y)
and
(y,z)
are arcs so is
(x,z)
). We usually denote the relation on a poset by
\leq
. A poset
\mathbb{P}
is a
chain
or
totally ordered set
if for every
x,y\in P
either
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph. Define a simplicial complex
K(\mathbb{G})
as follows: a subset
S
of
G
is a simplex of
K(\mathbb{G})
if there exists a totally ordered set
\mathbb{P}
and a homomorphism from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{G}
whose image is
S
. We will call
K(\mathbb{G})
the
simplicial realisation of
\mathbb{G}
, and we will say that
\mathbb{G}

triangulates
a topological space
X
if
X
is homeomorphic to the geometric realisation of
K(\mathbb{G}).

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph. The
suspension of
\mathbb{G}
is the digraph obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by adding two vertices
u
and
v
that are adjacent to every vertex of
\mathbb{G}
(by a two-way edge) and nothing else (see Figure
1
). It turns out that the geometric realisation of the suspension of
\mathbb{G}
is homeomorphic to the suspension of the geometric realisation of
\mathbb{G}
(hence the name).

Figure 1.
A digraph and its suspension.

For posets we can refine the construction a bit. Given two posets
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, their
ordinal sum

\mathbb{P}\oplus\mathbb{Q}
is the poset obtained from the disjoint union of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
by adding the relations
p\leq q
for all
p\in P
and
q\in Q
. An
antichain
is a totally disconnected poset, i.e. with no edges except loops. In ordinal sums, we denote the
n
-element antichain simply by
n
. The
poset suspension of
\mathbb{P}
is the poset
\mathbb{P}\oplus 2
. It turns out that the geometric realisation of the suspension of
\mathbb{P}
is homeomorphic to the suspension of the geometric realisation of
\mathbb{P}
(hence the name).

3.
A sufficient condition

In this section we present various necessary and sufficient conditions for a digraph to be Słupecki. The main result, Theorem
3.3
, will be used in sections
4
and
5
and is a central tool in
[
6
]
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
,
\mathbb{H}
be digraphs. The digraph
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H})
is defined as follows: its vertices are the homomorphisms
\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
, and we have an arc
(f,g)
if
(f(x),g(y))
is an arc of
\mathbb{H}
whenever
(x,y)
is an arc of
\mathbb{G}
. It is easy to verify that if
f,g,h\in Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
and
f\rightarrow g
then
h\circ f\rightarrow h\circ g
(see Lemma 2.1 of
[
9
]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph such that the identity is an isolated loop in
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
. If
f:\mathbb{G}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
is an onto, essentially unary polymorphism then
f
is an isolated loop in
Hom(\mathbb{G}^{p},\mathbb{G})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the result for projections: indeed, if
f
is an onto essentially unary polymorphism of arity
p
then
f=\sigma\circ\pi
for some projection
\pi
and some automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{G}
. If
f\rightarrow g
then
\pi\rightarrow\sigma^{-1}\circ g
so if the result holds for projections then
\pi=\sigma^{-1}\circ g
and
f=g
; similarly if
g\rightarrow f
.

Suppose that
\pi\rightarrow f
in
Hom(\mathbb{G}^{p},\mathbb{G})
where
\pi
is a projection (the case
f\rightarrow\pi
is identical). Without loss of generality, suppose that
\pi
is the first projection.
Fix
a_{2},\dots,a_{p}\in G
and define
g(x)=f(x,a_{2},\dots,a_{p})
. Then it is clear that
id\rightarrow g
in
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
. Thus
g=id
and hence
f
is the first projection.

∎

For
i=1,\dots,p
let
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{G}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
denote the
i
-th projection.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph and let
f:\mathbb{G}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
be an onto polymorphism of arity at least 2. Then
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
:

(1)

there exists an embedding
e:\mathbb{G}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}^{p}
such that the restriction of
f
to
e(\mathbb{G})
is onto;

(2)

f
is essentially unary.

if
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial such that the identity is an isolated loop in
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
, the converse holds.

Proof.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
: suppose
f
is onto and depends only on its
i
-th variable; then the required embedding is
e:\mathbb{G}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}^{p}
defined by
e(x)=(a,a,\dots,a,x,a,\dots,a)
where
a
is any fixed vertex in
\mathbb{G}
and
x
appears in the
i
-th position.

Now assume that
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial and that the identity is an isolated loop in
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
: we prove

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. First notice that if
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial then it must be connected: one can easily construct a binary idempotent polymorphism which is a non-projection if
\mathbb{G}
is not connected.
Let
f:\mathbb{G}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
and
e:\mathbb{G}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}^{p}
be such that
f(e(\mathbb{G}))=\mathbb{G}
. It follows that
f\circ e=\sigma
for some automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{G}
. Define an operation
\phi:\mathbb{G}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
by

\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\sigma^{-1}f(\pi_{1}(e(x_{1})),\dots,\pi_{p}(e(x_{p}))).

Then
\phi(x,\dots,x)=\sigma^{-1}f(\pi_{1}(e(x)),\dots,\pi_{p}(e(x)))=\sigma^{-1}f(e(x))=x
and thus
\phi
is idempotent. Since
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial there exists some
i
such that
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=x_{i}
for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{p}
. We may suppose without loss of generality that
i=1
, and thus

f(\pi_{1}(e(x_{1})),\dots,\pi_{p}(e(x_{p})))=\sigma(x_{1})

for all
x_{i}
; in particular,
if we let
\mathbb{H}_{i}=\pi_{i}(e(\mathbb{G}))
for all
i=1,\dots,p
, we conclude that
\mathbb{H}_{1}=\mathbb{G}
. Let
k
be the largest index
i
such that
\mathbb{H}_{i}=\mathbb{G}
: notice that for each
1\leq i\leq k
,
\sigma_{i}=\pi_{i}\circ e
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{G}
. If
k=p
then we’ve shown that
f
depends only on its first variable. Otherwise, consider the map
\psi:\mathbb{G}^{p-k}\rightarrow Hom(\mathbb{G}^{k},\mathbb{G})
where
\psi(b_{k+1},\dots,b_{p})
is the homomorphism
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},b_{k+1},\dots,b_{p})
. Clearly
\psi
is a homomorphism, and since
\mathbb{G}^{p-k}
is connected, so is the image of
\psi
. Choose elements
c_{j}\in\mathbb{H}_{j}
,
k+1\leq j\leq p
. Then by the above identity we have that
\psi(c_{k+1},\dots,c_{p})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sigma(\sigma_{1}^{-1}(x_{1}))
; in particular
\tau=\psi(c_{k+1},\dots,c_{p})
is an essentially unary onto polymorphism.
By Lemma
3.1
, it is an isolated loop and hence
\psi
is a constant map with value
\tau
; in other words,
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\sigma(\sigma_{1}^{-1}(x_{1}))
for all
x_{j}
and we are done.
∎

We remark in passing that if the condition that
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial is dropped then the implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
in the previous result is not necessarily true (the construction of a binary non-projection idempotent polymorphism on a disconnected digraph easily shows this.)

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
\mathbb{G}
triangulate an
n
-sphere. If for every
p\geq 2
and every onto polymorphism
f:\mathbb{G}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
there exists an embedding
e:\mathbb{G}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}^{p}
such that the restriction of
f
to
e(\mathbb{G})
is onto then
\mathbb{G}
is Słupecki.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{G}
triangulates an
n
-sphere then the
n
-th homotopy group of the geometric realisation of
\mathbb{G}
is non-trivial, but every proper retract of
\mathbb{G}
has a contractible realisation and hence has trivial homotopy. It follows from Theorem 2.11 of
[
9
]
and Claim 1 in the proof of that same theorem that
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial and that the identity is alone in its connected component of
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
. We can then invoke Lemma
3.2
to conclude. ∎

For completeness’ sake, we state an analog of Lemma
3.2
when we only consider strong components. Write
f\sim g
to mean that
f
and
g
are in the same strong component of
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph such that the identity is alone in its strong component of
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
. If
f:\mathbb{G}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
is an onto, essentially unary polymorphism then
f
is alone in its strong component of
Hom(\mathbb{G}^{n},\mathbb{G})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the result for projections: indeed, if
f
is an onto essentially unary polymorphism of arity
n
then
f=\sigma\circ\pi
for some projection
\pi
and some automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{G}
. If
f\sim g
then
\pi\sim\sigma^{-1}\circ g
so if the result holds for projections then
\pi=\sigma^{-1}\circ g
and
f=g
.

Suppose that
\pi\sim f
in
Hom(\mathbb{G}^{n},\mathbb{G})
where
\pi
is a projection. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\pi
is the first projection.
Fix
a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in G
and define
g(x)=f(x,a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
. Then it is easy to see that
id\sim g
in
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
. Thus
g=id
and hence
f
is the first projection.

∎

Now we can state the analog of Lemma
3.2
for strongly connected digraphs: in this case we can weaken the technical condition on the identity.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a strongly connected digraph and let
f:\mathbb{G}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
be an onto polymorphism of arity at least 2. If
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial such that the identity is alone in its strong component of
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
, then (1) implies (2):

(1)

there exists an embedding
e:\mathbb{G}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}^{n}
such that the restriction of
f
to
e(\mathbb{G})
is onto;

(2)

f
is essentially unary.

Proof.

The proof is exactly the same as for Lemma
3.2
, except for two observations: (i) we replace the use of Lemma
3.1
by that of Lemma
3.4
and (ii) since
\mathbb{G}
is strongly connected, so is the image of the homomorphism
\psi
; since its image contains a surjective essentially unary polymorphism, this image must be constant by (i). ∎

We close this section with some observations on the conditions used in the previous lemmas. The technical condition that the identity should be an isolated loop is slightly vexing; it can be removed in many instances, but unfortunately not always.

We say the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is
intransitive
if its simplices all have dimension at most 1, i.e. there are no transitive triples in
\mathbb{G}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a poset or symmetric digraph or intransitive digraph. If
\mathbb{G}
is idempotent trivial then the identity is isolated in
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
.

As we argued earlier, we can assume that
\mathbb{G}
is connected if it is idempotent trivial.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
\mathbb{G}
is a poset, and
id
is not alone in its connected component. Then (see the comment following Lemma 2.10 in
[
15
]
) without loss of generality
id<f
where
f
differs from
id
only in one place, say
f(a)=b
; it is easy to see that in this case
b
is a unique upper cover of
a
in
\mathbb{G}
(i.e. if
a<c
then
b\leq c
); then define
\phi(x,y)=y
if
x\leq a
and
\phi(x,y)=f(y)
otherwise. It is easy to see that
\phi
is an idempotent polymorphism, and that it is not a projection.

(2) Suppose that
\mathbb{G}
is a symmetric digraph, and that
id
is not alone in its component, so without loss of generality let
id\rightarrow f
. By Lemma 2.2 of
[
15
]

4.
Some Słupecki Spheres

As we remarked earlier, digraphs that triangulate spheres are known to be idempotent trivial, and cycles of girth at least 4 are in fact Słupecki. In the next section we will provide examples of digraphs that triangulate 2-spheres that are
not
Słupecki; in the present section we provide examples of digraphs triangulating spheres that are Słupecki, using a technique involving gadgets. We also state for the record the result that digraphs that triangulate 1-spheres are Słupecki.

An
n
-ary operation
f
on a set
A

preserves
the
k
-ary relation
\theta\subseteq A^{k}
if applying
f
to the rows of any
k\times n
matrix whose columns are tuples in
\theta
yields a tuple in
\theta
.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
A
be an
n
-element set,
n\geq 2
. Then the operations on
A
preserving the relation

\theta=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}):|\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}|<n\}

are precisely the essentially unary operations and the non-surjective operations on
A
.

The set of operations preserving the above relation
\theta
is called the
Słupecki
clone; it is known to be a maximal clone, and it is easy to see that it contains all non-surjective operations and all unary operations. To show that a reflexive digraph is Słupecki, it is necessary and sufficient to prove that its polymorphisms preserve the relation
\theta
; it is well-known (see
[
14
]
) that this is equivalent to showing that there exists a primitive, positive definition of
\theta
in terms of the edge relation of the digraph. Or in more convenient terms for our purposes:

Lemma 4.2
.

The digraph
\mathbb{G}
is Słupecki if and only if there exists a digraph
\mathbb{K}
and vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of
\mathbb{K}
such that

\theta=\{(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{n})):f:\mathbb{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}\}.

If the above holds we say that the gadget
\mathbb{K}

pp-defines
the Słupecki relation
\theta
. It turns out that finding the gadget
\mathbb{K}
is fairly easy for various families of cycles (see below), but we do not know of a uniform gadget construction for all cycles.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph, and let
S\subseteq G
. If there exists a digraph
\mathbb{K}
, vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{k},u\in K
and a partial map

is pp-defined by
(\mathbb{K},f,u)

Theorem 4.4

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph. If there exists a digraph

is Słupecki.

(1)

for all

such that

(2)

5.
Ordinal sums

In this section we investigate the nature of surjective polymorphisms on posets of the form
m\oplus n
and
m\oplus n\oplus k
with
m,n,k\geq 2
.
These posets are known to be idempotent trivial
[
8
]
, and, hence, by Lemma
3.6
, the identity is an isolated loop in
Hom(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P})
.
These rather simple-looking posets will provide many examples of unexpected behaviour.
Namely, although
m\oplus n
is Słupecki, none of the posets
n\oplus m\oplus k
are; this includes the poset
2\oplus 2\oplus 2
which is the poset suspension of a 4-cycle and hence triangulates a 2-sphere. When considering homomorphisms between posets we often use the term
monotone
instead.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
m,n\geq 2
. Then
\mathbb{P}=m\oplus n
is Słupecki.

Figure 7.
The poset
3\oplus 4
. Arcs are all oriented bottom to top.

Proof.

We are going to use Lemma
3.2
in the
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
direction.
We have just observed that the two technical conditions, namely that
\mathbb{P}
is idempotent trivial and the identity is isolated, are fulfilled.
Let
f:\mathbb{P}^{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
(
s\geq 2
) be an onto polymorphism.
Finding the embedding
e
of Lemma
3.2
means finding an isomorphic copy of
\mathbb{P}
in
\mathbb{P}^{s}
on which
f
is onto.
That is what we are going to do.

Let
A
and
B
denote the set of minimal and maximal elements of
\mathbb{P}
, respectively.
We claim
f(A^{s})\supseteq A
.
Pick
a\in A
, then
f(x)=a
for some
x\in\mathbb{P}^{s}
, and, under
x
, there is a minimal element
y
of
\mathbb{P}^{s}
for which
f(y)=a
, and, from minimality, we have
y\in A^{s}
.
Dually,
f(B^{s})\supseteq B
.
Now we can choose two sets of elements
X_{A}\subseteq A^{s}
and
X_{B}\subseteq B^{s}
such that

|X_{A}|=|A|,\;|X_{B}|=|B|,\;f(X_{A})=A,\text{ and }f(X_{B})=B.

As the elements of
A^{s}
and
B^{s}
form two antichains such that the elements of
A^{s}
are all under those of
B^{s}
, the poset
X_{A}\cup X_{B}
is in fact an isomorphic copy of
\mathbb{P}
in
\mathbb{P}^{s}
on which

is onto.
We are done.
∎

The case of ordinal sums of three antichains is more interesting.
First some notation. Once

are fixed, we let
A=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{m-1}\}

and
C=\{c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1}\}
denote the minimal, middle and maximal elements of
\mathbb{P}=m\oplus n\oplus k
respectively.

We first show that
m\oplus n\oplus k
is not 3-Słupecki (and hence not Słupecki) by directly exhibiting a not essentially-unary ternary polymorphism.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
m,n,k\geq 2
. Then
\mathbb{P}=m\oplus n\oplus k
is not 3-Słupecki, and, hence, not Słupecki.

Proof.

We exhibit a polymorphism
f:\mathbb{P}^{3}\to\mathbb{P}
that is onto and not essentially unary: let

f(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}s,&\text{if }(x,y,z)=(s,s,s),\;\;\text{for }s\in A\cup C\\
b_{i},&\text{if }(x,y,z)=(a_{0},c_{0},b_{i}),\;\;(i=0,\dots,n-1)\\
a_{0},&\text{if }(x,y,z)<(a_{0},c_{0},b_{i}),\;\;(i=0,\dots,n-1)\\
c_{0},&\text{if }(x,y,z)>(a_{0},c_{0},b_{i}),\;\;(i=0,\dots,n-1)\\
b_{0},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

First, we have to verify that our function is well-defined.
There are, indeed, elements of
P^{3}
that are given values multiple times in the definition; we need to make sure that they are given the same value.
It is clear that this occurs for
(a_{0},a_{0},a_{0})
and
(c_{0},c_{0},c_{0})
which are given the values
a_{0}
and
c_{0}
respectively, at each of their occurrences in the definition, hence there is no conflict here.
Let
x^{\uparrow}=\{y:x\leq y\}
and similarly
x_{\downarrow}=\{y:x\geq y\}
.
The only other way this multiple assignment phenomenon can happen for
(x,y,z)\in P^{3}
is if it belongs to

(a_{0},c_{0},b_{i})^{\uparrow}\cap(a_{0},c_{0},b_{j})^{\uparrow}

(or its dual) for
i\neq j
.
On those intersections, we define our function to be
c_{0}
(
a_{0}
on its dual), hence this causes no conflict either, our function is well-defined.

It is clear that
f
is onto.

Let’s show that
f
is not essentially unary.
The fact that

c_{0}=f(a_{0},c_{0},c_{0})\neq f(a_{0},c_{0},a_{0})=a_{0}

shows that if
f
depends only on one variable it is the third;
if
f
depended only its third variable, then its surjectivity would imply that it would be injective on its third variable, which contradicts the fact that

f(a_{0},c_{0},a_{0})=f(a_{0},c_{0},a_{1})=a_{0}
.

Finally, the reader can easily verify that
f
is a polymorphism using that
f
is defined using upsets and downsets. ∎

Let us turn our attention to binary polymorphisms.
Surprisingly, 2-Słupeckiness of
m\oplus n\oplus k
depends on
m
,
n
, and
k
:
if
n
is small enough compared to
m
and
k
, then
m\oplus n\oplus k
is not 2-Słupecki, and otherwise, it is.
More precisely, the main result of the section, Theorem
5.5
, states that there is a bound
B(m,k)
for which the poset
m\oplus n\oplus k
is 2-Słupecki if and only if
n>B(m,k)+1
.

We start with a standalone, auxiliary result that we will make use of later:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
m,n,k\geq 2
, let
\mathbb{P}=m\oplus n\oplus k
and let
f:\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
be a monotone map.
Suppose
|B|>2
and that
f(B^{2})\subseteq B
has size at least 2.
Then there exist
b,b^{\prime}\in B
distinct,
\tau_{i}\in Aut(\mathbb{P})
,

, such that

(1)

\tau_{i}(x)=x
for all
x\in A\cup C
and all
i=0,1
;

(2)

(\tau_{0}\circ f\circ\sigma)(s,s)=s
for
s\in\{b,b^{\prime}\}
, where
\sigma(x,y)=(\tau_{1}(x),y)
.

Proof.

Notice that
Aut(\mathbb{P})
contains every transposition of pairs of elements in
B
(which fixes all other elements in
\mathbb{P}
.) We first prove the result in the case where the restriction of
f
to the diagonal of
B^{2}
has image of size at least 2: if
f(b,b)=u
and
f(b^{\prime},b^{\prime})=v
where
u\neq v
then choose
\tau_{0}
to be any automorphism of
\mathbb{P}
that sends
u
to
b
and
v
to
b^{\prime}
: then
(\tau_{0}\circ f)(s,s)=s
for
s\in\{b,b^{\prime}\}
and we are done. Otherwise, there exist some

such that
f(t,t)=z
for all
t\in B
, and some

and

such that
f(u,v)=w
. Let
\tau_{1}\in Aut(\mathbb{P})
swap
u
and
v
. Then
(f\circ\sigma)(t,t)=z
for any

and
(f\circ\sigma)(v,v)=f(\tau_{1}(v),v)=f(u,v)=w
. We conclude with the preceding argument.

∎

Now we define the bound
B(m,k)
of Theorem
5.5
.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
m,k\geq 2
be integers. We let
\mu(m,k)
denote the maximum of the function
\alpha\gamma+\beta\delta
where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
are integers satisfying
0<\alpha,\beta<m
,
0<\gamma,\delta<k
,
(m-\alpha)(m-\beta)\geq m-1
and
(k-\gamma)(k-\delta)\geq k-1
. Let
B(m,k)=\max(\mu(m,k),mk)
.

From the definition and the individual bounds for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\delta
, one sees easily that
mk\leq B(m,k)<2mk
and in particular that the function is well-defined; notice also that
B(m,k)\geq 4
for all
m,k\geq 2
.
We have verified numerically that
B(m,k)=mk
if
m,k\leq 11
. On the other hand,
B(12,12)=145
as shown by the choice
\alpha=\gamma=9
, and
\beta=\delta=8
, and
B(10,13)=134
.

Theorem 5.5
.

The poset
m\oplus n\oplus k
is 2-Słupecki if and only if
n>B(m,k)+1
.

We split the proof into two separate lemmas (Lemma
5.7
and Lemma
5.8
) according to the two directions of the statement.

Definition 5.6
.

Given any map
f:A^{2}\cup C^{2}\rightarrow A\cup C
such that
f(A^{2})\subseteq A
and
f(C^{2})\subseteq C
, for
T\in\{A,C\}
, let
l(T)
denote the set of all
s\in T
such that the map

is constant, and let
l(s)
denote the value of this map. Similarly let
r(T)
denote the set of all
t\in T
such that the map
x\mapsto f(x,t)
is constant, and let
r(t)
denote the value of this map.

First, we take on the
only if
direction.
We actually formulate its contrapositive:

Lemma 5.7
.

If
n\leq B(m,k)+1
, then
m\oplus n\oplus k
is not 2-Słupecki.

Proof.

With
\mathbb{P}=m\oplus n\oplus k
, we will exhibit an essentially binary polymorphism
f:\mathbb{P}^{2}\to\mathbb{P}
.
To do so, we start by defining
f
partially on
A^{2}
and
C^{2}
.
Let us denote the corresponding restrictions by
f_{A}
and
f_{C}
.

Claim 0.
There exist onto maps
f_{A}:A^{2}\rightarrow A
and
f_{C}:C^{2}\rightarrow C
such that there are exactly

pairs
(s,t)\in A\times C\cup C\times A
where both the maps

and

are constant, where, for convenience,
f
stands for the corresponding
f_{A}
or
f_{C}
.

Proof of Claim 0.
If
B(m,k)=mk
, then we define

to be the first projection on
A
and the second projection on
C
.
Clearly there are exactly
B(m,k)=mk
pairs
(s,t)\in A\times C
(and none in

) such that both the maps
x\mapsto f(x,t)
and
x\mapsto f(s,x)
are constant.
Otherwise,
B(m,k)=\alpha\gamma+\beta\delta
with

,
0<\gamma,\delta<k
.
By definition of

there exists a map

from
\{a_{\alpha},\dots,a_{m-1}\}\times\{a_{\beta},\dots,a_{m-1}\}
onto
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}\}
.
Then define

f(a_{i},a_{j})=\begin{cases}a_{0}&\mbox{ if $i<\alpha$ or $j<\beta$},\\

f

with image

.

By definition of

there are exactly
\alpha
values
a\in A
such that the map

is constant, and
\beta
values
a\in A
such that the map

is constant.
Similarly, we can define

on

to be onto

and such that there are exactly
\gamma
values

such that the map

is constant and
\delta

such that the map
x\mapsto f(c,x)
is constant.
In particular, this shows that there are exactly
B(m,k)
pairs
(s,t)\in(A\times C)\cup(C\times A)
such that both the map
x\mapsto f(x,t)
and
x\mapsto f(s,x)
are constant.

Now we will extend
f
to be a fully defined map on
P^{2}
.
Let
b_{0}\in B
; by our hypothesis and Claim 0, there exists a map
h:(l(A)\times r(C))\cup(l(C)\times r(A))\rightarrow B\setminus\{b_{0}\}
which is onto. We now define our map

as follows:

f(x,y)=\begin{cases}f_{A}(x,y)&\mbox{ if $(x,y)\in A^{2}$},\\
f_{C}(x,y)&\mbox{ if $(x,y)\in C^{2}$},\\
h(x,y)&\mbox{if $(x,y)\in(l(A)\times r(C))\cup(l(C)\times r(A))$},\\

Clearly

is onto; and since

implies

, there is some pair
(a,c)\in A\times C
such that

; since
f(a,a)\in A
and

,

depends on both variables. Finally we verify that

is monotone. It suffices to verify that
f(x,y)\leq f(u,v)
where

in

; and it is easy to see that this occurs if and only if

or

.

(1)

(a,a^{\prime})<(a,b)
: if

then

; otherwise

(2)

(3)

(a,b)<(a,c)

and

(4)

the remaining cases are dual to the previous ones (replacing

).

∎

It is time to prove the converse (the
if
direction of Theorem

).

Lemma 5.8
.

The poset
\mathbb{P}=m\oplus n\oplus k
is 2-Słupecki if
n>B(m,k)+1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}
admits a binary, not essentially unary onto polymorphism
f
; we want to show that
n\leq B(m,k)+1
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
n\geq 3
. Our goal is to obtain from
f
a binary, not essentially unary onto polymorphism with a very specific form.

Claim 1.

f(A^{2})\supseteq A
,
f(C^{2})\supseteq C
and
f(B^{2})\subseteq B
.

Proof of Claim 1.
The first statement is clear:
f
is onto, and every element of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is above some minimal element which is in
A^{2}
. The second statement is proved dually. It is then clear that
f(B^{2})\subseteq B
since an element
(b,b^{\prime})\in B^{2}
is above every element of
A^{2}
and below every element of
C^{2}
, so by our first two statements
a\leq f(b,b^{\prime})\leq c
for all
a\in A
and
c\in C
which shows that
f(b,b^{\prime})\in B
.

Claim 2.

We may assume that
f(C^{2})=C
and
f(A^{2})=A
.

Proof of Claim 2.
Suppose that
f(c_{i},c_{j})=y\not\in C
for some
i,j
. Suppose that no other pair is mapped to
y
by
f
. Then
f(s,t)<y
for all
s,t\in A\cup B
. Since
y\not\in C
this means that
f((A\cup B)^{2})=A
(using Claim 1). But
A\cup B
is connected and
A
is not, so this is impossible. Thus

f(u,v)=y
for some
(u,v)\neq(c_{i},c_{j})
. Define a map as follows:

g(s,t)=\begin{cases}c_{1}&\mbox{ if $(s,t)=(c_{i},c_{j})$},\\
f(s,t)&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is clear that
g
is monotone, onto, and since obviously
f<g
and the identity is alone in its connected component, we conclude by Lemma
3.1
that
g
depends on both variables. The argument for
A
is identical.

Claim 3.

Let
b\in B
. If
b\in f((A\times B)\cup(B\times A))\cup f((C\times B)\cup(B\times C))
then
b\in f(B^{2})
.

Proof of Claim 3.
Let
(x,y)\in(A\times B)\cup(B\times A)
with
f(x,y)=b
. Then
(x,y)
is comparable to a pair
(u,v)\in B^{2}
so
f(u,v)=b
(since
f(u,v)\in B
). Similarly if
(x,y)\in(C\times B)\cup(B\times C)
.

Claim 4.

There exists
(x,y)\in(A\times C)\cup(C\times A)
such that
f(x,y)\in B
.

Proof of Claim 4.
Otherwise, it follows from Claim 3 that
f(B^{2})=B
. By Claim 2 we can then find subsets
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
,
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
and
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of size
m,n,k
respectively such that
f(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}\cup C^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}
. But this gives an embedding
e
of
\mathbb{P}
into
\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
f(e(\mathbb{P}))=\mathbb{P}
, contradicting Lemma
3.2
.

Claim 5.

Let
b_{0}\in f(B^{2})
and let
(a,c)\in A\times C
.

(1)

If
f(a,c)\in B\setminus\{b_{0}\}
, then
(a,c)\in l(A)\times r(C)
;

(2)

If
f(c,a)\in B\setminus\{b_{0}\}
, then
(c,a)\in l(C)\times r(A)
.

Proof of Claim 5.
We prove (1) (the proof of (2) is identical). Let
f(b,b^{\prime})=b_{0}
where
b,b^{\prime},b_{0}\in B
. Then
f(b,c)\geq f(a,c),f(b,b^{\prime})
so
f(b,c)\in C
. On the other hand,
f(b,c)\leq f(x,c)
for all
x\in C
, thus
c\in r(C)
. We have
f(a,b^{\prime})\leq f(a,c),f(b,b^{\prime})
so
f(a,b^{\prime})\in A
. On the other hand,
f(a,b^{\prime})\geq f(a,x)
for all
x\in A
, thus
a\in l(A)
.

Claim 6.

|f(B^{2})|=1
.

Proof of Claim 6.
We consider two cases. First suppose that the restrictions
f_{A}
and
f_{C}
of
f
to
A^{2}
and
C^{2}
respectively depend on two different variables, without loss of generality suppose
f_{C}
depends on its first variable and
f_{A}
depends on its second. This precisely means there exist
a\in A\setminus l(A)
and
c\in C\setminus r(C)
. Choose any
b\in B
. Since
f(a,x)\leq f(a,c)\leq f(b,c)\leq f(y,c)
for all
x\in A
and all
y\in C
, we conclude that
f(a,c)
and
f(b,c)
must be in
B
(and equal). Now if
b^{\prime}\in B
arbitrary, we have
f(b,b^{\prime})\leq f(b,c)
and hence they must also be equal. Hence
f(B^{2})=\{f(a,c)\}
.

If the restrictions of
f
to
A^{2}
and
C^{2}
do not depend on different variables, it implies they are both essentially unary (and depend on the same variable.) By applying an appropriate
\tau\in Aut(\mathbb{P}^{2})
, we can then suppose that
f(x,x)=x
for all
x\in A\cup C
(notice that Claim 4 guarantees we still obtain an essentially binary operation.)
Suppose for a contradiction that
|f(B^{2})|\geq 2
. By Lemma
5.3
, we may assume that
f(s,s)=s
for
s\in\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
(again Claim 4 guarantees we may apply automorphisms and obtain an essentially binary operation). Let
\mathbb{R}
be the subposet of
\mathbb{P}
induced by
A\cup\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\cup C
.
It is isomorphic to
m\oplus 2\oplus k
and thus is idempotent trivial.
There is an obvious retraction
r
of
\mathbb{P}
onto
\mathbb{R}
, that fixes
A
and
C
pointwise and sends
B
onto
\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
.
Consider the restriction of
r\circ f
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: it is idempotent and hence a projection. In particular, for the pair
(x,y)
from Claim 4, we will have that
(r\circ f)(x,y)\in\{x,y\}
, but since
f(x,y)\in B
, this is a contradiction.

Claim 7.

|(l(A)\times r(C))\cup(l(C)\times r(A))|\leq B(m,k)
.

Proof of Claim 7.
Let
M=|(l(A)\times r(C))\cup(l(C)\times r(A))|
. If one of the values in
\{|r(A)|,|r(C)|,|l(A)|,|l(C)|\}
is equal to 0, then clearly
M\leq mk\leq B(m,k)
. So we may now assume that all 4 values are non-zero. If both
|r(A)|
and
|l(A)|
are non-zero it means that all the maps
x\mapsto f(a,x)
and
x\mapsto f(x,a)
that are constant must have the same value, i.e. there exists some
w\in A
such that
l(a)=r(a^{\prime})=w
for all
a\in l(A)
and all
a^{\prime}\in r(A)
. By Claim 2 it means that the restriction of
f_{A}
to
(A-l(A))\times(A-r(A))
has image
A-\{w\}
, and thus
(m-|l(A)|)(m-|l(B)|)\geq m-1
, and in particular,
|l(A)|,|r(A)|<m
. The same argument shows that the values
|l(C)|,|r(C)|
also satisfy the required inequalities of Definition
5.4
, and thus
M\leq\mu(m,k)\leq B(m,k)
.

We can now conclude the proof. Let
f(B^{2})=\{b_{0}\}
. By Claims 3, 5 and 6, any
b\in B\setminus\{b_{0}\}
must be in the image of
(l(A)\times r(C))\cup(l(C)\times r(A))
.
Hence by Claim 7
|B|-1\leq|(l(A)\times r(C))\cup(l(C)\times r(A))|\leq B(m,k)
and we are done.
∎

6.
Conclusion

We have presented various necessary and sufficient conditions for a reflexive digraph to be Słupecki. We’ve shown in particular that there exist posets that triangulate spheres that are idempotent trivial but not 2-Słupecki; and some idempotent trivial posets that are 2-Słupecki but not 3-Słupecki. Here now are some related questions:

(1)

Can the condition that the identity is isolated be removed in Lemma
3.2
?

(2)

If
\mathbb{G}
is strongly connected and idempotent trivial, is the identity alone in its
strong
component ? Notice that if this holds, then combining Lemmas
3.5
and
3.7
with Lemma
3.2
gives a positive answer to question (1).

(3)

What is the algorithmic complexity of recognising Słupecki (idempotent trivial) digraphs ?

(4)

An operation
f
is
conservative
if
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
for all
x_{i}
. It is known that a poset of size at least 3 is idempotent trivial if and only if all its idempotent binary polymorphisms are conservative
[
7
]
; hence a poset is idempotent trivial precisely when all its 2-element subsets are pp-definable by gadgets. Does this property hold for reflexive digraphs in general ? Also, although it is not true in general that one can always find gadgets that are trees, is it possible that an algorithm such as singleton arc-consistency (as described in
[
1
]
) might determine if all 2-element subsets are constructible ? If so, this might lead to interesting algorithms for recognising idempotent trivial posets.

(5)

the bound
B(m,k)
we introduced is intriguing from a combinatorial standpoint. Investigate.

(6)

Are there posets (or more generally digraphs) that are not Słupecki but
n
-Słupecki for arbitrary large
n
? What is the relationship to the size of the digraph ?

(7)

The idempotent trivial posets of height 1 are known
[
2
]
; they are the height 1 posets that are connected and such that no element has a unique upper nor unique lower cover. Are they all Słupecki ?
