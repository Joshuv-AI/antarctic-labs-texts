Non-Asymptotic Bounds for Adversarial Excess Risk under Misspecified Models
Changyu Liuabel=e1, mark]changyuliu@cuhk.edu.hk, Yuling Jiao ∗ abel=e2, mark]yulingjiaomath@whu.edu.cn, Junhui Wangabel=e3, mark]junhuiwang@cuhk.edu.hk, Jian Huangabel=e4, mark]j.huang@polyu.edu.hk

Abstract
We propose a general approach to evaluating the performance of robust estimators based on adversarial losses under misspecified models. We first show that adversarial risk is equivalent to the risk induced by a distributional adversarial attack under certain smoothness conditions. This ensures that the adversarial training procedure is well-defined. To evaluate the generalization performance of the adversarial estimator, we study the adversarial excess risk. Our proposed analysis method includes investigations on both generalization error and approximation error. We then establish non-asymptotic upper bounds for the adversarial excess risk associated with Lipschitz loss functions. In addition, we apply our general results to adversarial training for classification and regression problems. For the quadratic loss in nonparametric regression, we show that the adversarial excess risk bound can be improved over those for a general loss.

A deep learning model trained using such perturbed input data

Among the adversarial defense methods, adversarial training has been empirically shown to be successful

Although there has been significant progress
in developing methods for defending adversarial attacks,
theoretical understanding of adversarial robustness remains limited.
Pydi and Jog 2021a
;

Mustafa, Lei and Kloft 2022

Tu, Zhang and Tao 2019

However, the above work only considered well specified models, i.e., the underlying target function is assumed to belong to a class of neural network functions.
As well known in classical nonparametric method for classification and regression, the underlying target is the link function defined as

Therefore, a natural question is what are the properties of adversarial estimator under misspecified models, i.e., when the underlying target function is not an exact neural network function, but can only be approximated by neural networks. In this work, we provide theoretical guarantees for
deep adversarial training with misspecified models
by establishing non-asymptotic error bounds for
the adversarial excess risk, defined as the difference between the adversarial risk of adversarial estimator and the optimal adversarial risk.
We show that the adversarial excess risk
can be decomposed as

\text{Adversarial excess risk}\leq\mathcal{E}_{gen}+\mathcal{E}_{app},

where
\mathcal{E}_{gen}
represents the generalization error and
\mathcal{E}_{app}
represents the approximation error. The approximation error
\mathcal{E}_{app}
is due to model misspecification when the target function is not in the class of neural networks used in training. As summarized in Table
1
,
the existing related work has only considered the generalization error, assuming a well-specified model.
By investigating these two types of errors, we establish a non-asymptotic error bound on the adversarial excess risk in the more general setting that allows model misspecification.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

•

We establish non-asymptotic error bounds for the adversarial excess risks under misspecified models and use the feedforward neural networks (FNNs) with constraints on the Lipschitz property. The error bounds explicitly illustrate the influence of the adversarial attack level and can achieve the rate
O(n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)})
up to a logarithmic factor, where

\alpha
represents the smoothness level of the underlying target function and
d

is the dimension of input. The structure of the neural network is specified to show when the error rate can be achieved.

•

We also evaluate the adversarial estimator under natural risk and local worst-case risk. The result theoretically demonstrates that the adversarial robustness can hurt the accuracy in a general nonparametric setting.

•

We apply our general results to the classification and nonparametric regression problems in an adversarial setting
and establish non-asymptotic error bounds for the adversarial estimators under the adversarial classification risk and
L_{2}
-norm, respectively.

The results for error bounds for the adversarial estimator in different settings and measurements are summarized in Table

.

2
Problem setup

In this section, we introduce the definition of adversarial risk and present a basic setup of adversarial training. We also
lay the foundation for the theoretical analysis of adversarial training and establish some
basic properties of adversarial risk.

2.1
Adversarial risk

Suppose that
(X,Y)
follows an unknown distribution
P
over
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}.

For a loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\mapsto[0,\infty)
and a measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, the (population) natural risk is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\big[\ell(f(X),Y)\big].

To evaluate the performance of function
f
in the presence of adversarial attacks, the (population) adversarial risk is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\big[\sup_{X^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)}\ell(f(X^{\prime}),Y)\big],

where
B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})=\left\{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}:\|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.
Here we focus on
\ell_{\infty}
attack.
In Section
3
, we will show that the proposed analysis method can be easily extended to a general

\ell_{r}
attack.

2.2
Properties of adversarial risk

Adversarial risk has been widely considered in recent years for the goal of deriving adversarial robust estimator. To facilitate the analysis, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

\mathcal{Z}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}\times[-1,1]
and
\cup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
holds for
\varepsilon>0
.

The assumption
\cup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
is to guarantee that the estimation function class is well-defined under the adversarial setting. Our analysis can be easily extended to a more general setting, where
\mathcal{Z}
is bounded.
For a loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\mapsto[0,\infty)
, we define

\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sup_{u_{1}\neq u_{2}}\frac{|\ell(u_{1},y)-\ell(u_{2},y)|}{|u_{1}-u_{2}|}.

Assumption 2
.

The loss function is continuous and satisfies
\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)<\infty.

The assumption
\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)<\infty
is weaker than the Lipschitz continuity condition, since it only imposes restriction on the Lipschitz constant of
\ell(\cdot,y)
for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The assumption
is satisfied by many commonly used loss functions, such as the hinge loss and
\rho
-margin loss.

We first
show that the adversarial risk is well-defined in our
setting.
This is
necessary since the adversarial risk
may not be well-defined in general
Pydi and Jog 2021b
.
Specifically, we show
that the adversarial risk for a function
f
can be represented by a natural risk with the expectation taken over a shifted distribution. Moreover, the distance between the shifted distribution and the data generating distribution can be measured by the
\infty
-th Wasserstein distance.

Let
d_{\mathcal{Z}}
denote a metric over
\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2})=\|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}\|_{\infty}+|y_{1}-y_{2}|
for any
\bm{z}_{1}=(\bm{x}_{1},y_{1})
and
\bm{z}_{2}=(\bm{x}_{2},y_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
denote the space of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{Z}
. For
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
p
-th Wasserstein distance between two probability measures
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{p}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\mathbb{E}_{(Z_{1},Z_{2})\sim\pi}\big[d_{\mathcal{Z}}(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{p}\big]\Big\}^{1/p},

where
\Pi(P,Q)
denotes the collection of all probability measures on
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
with marginals
P
and
Q.
The

\infty
-th Wasserstein distance is defined to be the limit of
the
p
-th Wasserstein distances, which can also be characterized by

\displaystyle W_{\infty}(P,Q)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\esssup_{(Z_{1},Z_{2})\sim\pi}d_{\mathcal{Z}}(Z_{1},Z_{2}).

Since
W_{p}(P,Q)\leq W_{q}(P,Q)
for any
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty,
the
\infty
-th Wasserstein distance is stronger than any
p
-th Wasserstein distance. Similarly, for
p\in[1,\infty]
, we define the
p
-th Wasserstein distance over
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
based on the supremum norm, where
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the space of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds,
\ell
and
f
are continuous, there exists a measurable function
T^{\star}
satisfying
T^{\star}(\bm{z})\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})
such that
\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)=\ell(f(T^{\star}(\bm{z})),y).

Let the joint distribution of
(T^{\star}(Z),Y)
be denoted by
P^{\star}
, we have
W_{\infty}(P^{\star},P)\leq\varepsilon
and

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{R}_{P^{\star}}(f).

Lemma
1
shows that the adversarial risk is well-defined with respect to the shifted distribution
P^{\star}
.
With further analysis of this shifted distribution, we construct an equivalent relationship between the adversarial risk and the risk induced by the distribution perturbing adversary
(
Pydi and Jog 2021b
;
Pydi and Jog 2021a
)
. The result is given in Section
2.3
below.

To state the next lemma, we denote
the Lipschitz constant for a function
f
by

\operatorname{Lip}(f)=\sup_{\bm{x}_{1}\neq\bm{x}_{2}}\frac{|f(\bm{x}_{1})-f(\bm{x}_{2})|}{\|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}\|_{\infty}}.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
2
hold and
\operatorname{Lip}(f)<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)+\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\operatorname{Lip}(f)\varepsilon.

Lemma
2
shows that the adversarial robustness
is related to the Lipschitz constraint.

Cisse et al. 2017
;
Tsuzuku, Sato and
Sugiyama 2018
and
Bubeck and Sellke 2021

also showed that robustness is related to the Lipschitz constraint under different settings.

2.3
Relationship between adversarial risk and distribution perturbing risk

We further study the shifted distribution in Lemma
1

and construct a relationship between the adversarial risk and another kind of risk induced by a distributional adversarial attack defined below.

For any distribution
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, we denote its corresponding pair of variables by
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
, and let the conditional distribution of
\widetilde{X}
given
\widetilde{Y}=y
be denoted by
Q_{y}
for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The collection of distributions
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\Big\{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z}):

\displaystyle\text{ when }(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})\sim Q,\text{ then }\widetilde{Y}\sim P_{Y}\text{ and }W_{\infty}(Q_{y},P_{y})\leq\varepsilon,\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}\Big\},

where
P_{y}
is the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=y
and
P_{Y}
is the distribution of
Y.
Intuitively, it is helpful to think
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
as a collection of distributional adversarial attacks. For every
Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
, as it observing sample
(X,Y)
, with
y
denoting the value of
Y
, it perturbs
X
to
\widetilde{X}
such that
\widetilde{X}\sim Q_{y}
and lets
Q_{y}
lie in an uncertainty set around
P_{y}.
This kind of adversarial attack strategy is also known as distribution perturbing adversary
(
Pydi and Jog 2021b
;
Pydi and Jog 2021a
)
. The corresponding distribution perturbing risk is defined as
\sup_{Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f).

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds,
\ell
and
f
are continuous, then we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)=\sup_{Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f).

This theorem shows that the risks induced by the two different types of adversarial attack are equivalent under the smoothness condition. Similar results were constructed in
Pydi and Jog 2021b
;
Pydi and Jog 2021a
, while
Pydi and Jog 2021b
focused on the binary classification setting and
Pydi and Jog 2021a
considered the case when
\mathcal{Y}
is a discrete set of labels. From the proof of Theorem
1
, we
also show that
P^{\star}\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
, which directly implies
W_{\infty}(P^{\star},P)\leq\varepsilon.
Hence, it
shows a stronger relationship with
P
.

2.4
Adversarial training

Adversarial training
aims to
learn a target function
f^{\star}
that minimizes the adversarial risk.
In this work, we assume that
f^{\star}
belongs to a Hölder class.

Definition 1

(Hölder class)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha=r+\beta>0
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\beta\in(0,1]
. Let
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
denote the multi-index. The Hölder class
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\Big\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},&\max_{\|\bm{s}\|_{1}\leq r}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\partial^{\bm{s}}f(\bm{x})\right|\leq 1,\\
&\max_{\|\bm{s}\|_{1}=r}\sup_{\bm{x}_{1}\neq\bm{x}_{2}}\frac{\left|\partial^{\bm{s}}f(\bm{x}_{1})-\partial^{\bm{s}}f(\bm{x}_{2})\right|}{\|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}\|_{\infty}^{\beta}}\leq 1\Big\}.\end{split}

We let
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}=\left\{f:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}
denote the restriction of
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
[0,1]^{d}
.

Our target function
f^{\star}
is defined by

f^{\star}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon).

(1)

When only a finite sample
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}),i=1,\ldots,n\}
is available, we estimate
f^{\star}
by minimizing the empirical adversarial risk over a space of estimation functions
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, which can vary with
n
. Specifically, we aim to find an estimator
\widehat{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
that solves

\widehat{f}_{n}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f,\varepsilon),\quad\text{where }\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f,\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\sup_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X_{i})}\ell(f(X^{\prime}_{i}),Y_{i})\big].

Here
P_{n}
denotes the empirical distribution induced by the samples.
The function
\widehat{f}_{n}
is called an adversarial estimator.
Based on the relationship between Lipschitz constraints and adversarial robustness, we focus on the feedforward neural network with constraints on Lipschitz property.

2.5
Feedforward neural networks with norm constraints

A feedforward neural networks (FNN) can be represented in the form of

g=g_{L}\circ g_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{0},

where
g_{i}(\bm{x})=\sigma(A_{i}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{i})
and
g_{L}(\bm{x})=A_{L}\bm{x}
, with
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i+1}\times d_{i}}
and
\bm{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i+1}\times 1}
for
i=0,\dots,L-1
,
A_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}\times d_{L}}
, and
\sigma(x)=\max\{x,0\}
being the ReLU activation function (applied
component-wise). For simplicity of notation, we use
g_{\theta}
to emphasis that the FNN is parameterized by
\theta=(A_{0},\dots,A_{L},\bm{b}_{0},\dots,\bm{b}_{L-1})
.
The number
W=\max\{d_{1},\dots,d_{L}\}
and
L
are called the width and depth of the FNN, respectively. We let
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L)
denote the class of FNNs with width
W
and depth
L
. Additionally, we
define
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
as the subset of functions in
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L)
which satisfies the following norm constraint on the weight.

\kappa(\theta):=\|A_{L}\|\prod_{i=0}^{L-1}\max\{\|(A_{i},\bm{b}_{i})\|,1\}\leq K,

where the norm satisfies
\|A\|=\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\|A\bm{x}\|_{\infty}
.
Then for any
g_{\theta}\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
, we have

\operatorname{Lip}(g_{\theta})\leq\kappa(\theta)\leq K.

Therefore, the Lipschitz constants of the functions in
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
have an uniformly upper bound.

3
Non-asymptotic error bounds

In this section, we present our main results of non-asymptotic
bounds for the adversarial excess risk.
We also discuss the relationship between accuracy and adversarial robustness in the sense
that
more robustness can lead to less accurate upper bounds for the excess risk.

3.1
Non-asymptotic error bounds for adversarial excess risk

The adversarial estimator based on FNNs with norm constraints is defined by

\widehat{f}_{n}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f,\varepsilon).

(2)

We evaluate its performance via the adversarial excess risk

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon).

The adversarial excess risk is nonnegative. It
is a measure for evaluating the performance of an adversarial estimator for future data
using the optimal population adversarial risk as a benchmark.

To investigate the adversarial excess risk, we show it can be decomposed into (
3
),
where
\mathcal{E}_{gen}
represents the generalization error, which is the difference between the population adversarial risk and empirical adversarial risk, and
\mathcal{E}_{app}
represents the approximation error due to model misspecification, which measures the distance between the target function and the space of estimation functions that may not contain the target function. By investigating both
\mathcal{E}_{gen}
and
\mathcal{E}_{app}
, we establish a non-asymptotic error bound on the adversarial excess risk.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a Hölder space
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha=r+\beta\geq 1
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\beta\in(0,1]
. Let
\gamma=\lceil\log_{2}(d+r)\rceil
. Suppose Assumptions
1
–
2
hold. Suppose

W\geq c(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{(2d+\alpha)/(2d+2)}
for a constant
c>0
, and
L\geq 4\gamma+2
. Then for any adversarial estimator
\widehat{f}_{n}
in (
2
) and adversarial attack level
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{gen}+\mathcal{E}_{app},

(3)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{gen}

\displaystyle=K\varepsilon n^{-1}+WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log n}+n^{-\min\{1/2,\alpha/d\}}\log^{c(\alpha,d)}n,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{app}

\displaystyle=(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{-\alpha/(d+1)}.

Here
c(\alpha,d)=1
when
d=2\alpha
, and
c(\alpha,d)=0,
otherwise.

If we further select

K\asymp n^{(d+1)/(2d+3\alpha)}
and
WL\asymp n^{(2d+\alpha)/(4d+6\alpha)},
then we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)\lesssim n^{-(d+3\alpha-1)/(2d+3\alpha)}\varepsilon+n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\xi}.

(4)

where
\xi=\max\{1,\gamma\alpha/(d+1)\}
.

The upper bound (
3
)
is determined by the smoothness property of the Hölder space, the structure of the estimation function class
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
, the adversarial attack level
\varepsilon
, and sample size
n
.
There is a trade-off between the two errors. Specifically,
\mathcal{E}_{gen}
increases with the complexity of
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
, with larger
W,L
and
K
leading to a larger upper bound. On the other hand, as

K
becomes larger, the error
\mathcal{E}_{app}
decreases. To achieve the best error rate, we balance the trade-off between the two errors and show the rate can reach
n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}
up to a logarithmic factor for suitable chosen
\varepsilon
.

The proposed analysis
method
is applicable to
the settings with
different models, loss functions, estimation function classes, and adversarial attacks. For example, we can apply the method to the setting where a general
\ell_{r}
adversarial attack is used. The upper bounds on the corresponding generalization error and approximation error can
be obtained based on the results on Rademacher complexity of general adversarial loss function classes
(
Awasthi, Frank and Mohri 2020
;
Mustafa, Lei and Kloft 2022
)
and the results on approximation power of different estimation function classes such as the deep neural networks
(
Yarotsky 2018
;
Lu et al. 2021
;
Jiao, Wang and
Yang 2022
)
.

The result (
4
)
show how the bounds for the
adversarial excess risk
depends on the adversarial attack level
\varepsilon
,
where
\varepsilon
is allowed to vary with
n
. Let
e_{n}=n^{(d+2\alpha-1)/(2d+3\alpha)}.
When
\varepsilon=O(e_{n})
, the error rate can reach
n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}
up to a logarithmic factor. However, if
\varepsilon
grows faster than
e_{n}
, the error rate of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)
is dominated by the rate of
\varepsilon.
Moreover, the convergence of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)
cannot be guaranteed when
\varepsilon
grows faster than
n^{(d+3\alpha-1)/(2d+3\alpha)}.

As mentioned above, the error rate of the adversarial excess risk can reach
n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}
up to a logarithmic factor when
\varepsilon
is appropriately selected. Here we only require the Lipschitz property of the loss function. We will further show in Section
4.2
that the error rate can be improved to
n^{-2\alpha/(2d+5\alpha)}
up to a logarithmic factor when using the
quadratic loss, where the
improvement is due to an improved approximation error bound.

Some recent papers have studied the convergence properties of deep neural network under the excess risk (
5
), where the data are naturally unperturbed
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
;
Bauer and Kohler 2019
;
Jiao et al. 2021
)
. The results are generally established under certain smoothness assumption on the target function. And it is typically assumed that the target function is in a Hölder class with a smoothness index
\alpha.
The results show that the deep neural network estimation could achieve the optimal minimax rate
n^{-2\alpha/(d+2\alpha)}
established by
Stone 1982
. Though the structures of neural networks vary in these works, which include different choices of width, depth, and activation functions, they make no constraint on the Lipschitz property of neural networks.
Jiao, Wang and
Yang 2022
investigated the approximation properties of FNNs with norm constraints. Intuitively, a norm constrained neural network class would be smaller in size compared to an unconstrained neural network class with the same structure. Therefore, the benefit of the Lipschitz property comes at the cost of losing the approximation power, which would lead to larger approximation errors. This is demonstrated in
Jiao, Wang and
Yang 2022
, where the error rate of the excess risk only reaches the rate
n^{-\alpha/(d+2\alpha+1)}
up to a logarithmic factor.

3.2
Adversarial robustness can hurt accuracy

We also evaluate the performance of the adversarial estimator under some other risks.
We first consider the natural risk
and study the excess risk defined by

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f}_{n})=\mathcal{R}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n})-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{R}_{P}(f).

(5)

Corollary 1
.

Suppose the
conditions of Theorem
2
are satisfied and
\alpha\geq 1
,
then for any adversarial estimator
\widehat{f}_{n}
in (
2
), we have

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f}_{n})\lesssim n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\xi}+\varepsilon,

where
\xi=\max\{1,\gamma\alpha/(d+1)\}
.

Corollary
1
shows that
the upper bound for the excess risk of the adversarial estimator is not guaranteed to converge.
The increase in the upper bound for the excess risk becomes significant when the adversarial robustness reaches a certain level. Previous studies have mostly focused on specific scenarios and made certain assumptions on the data distribution. For instance,
Javanmard and
Soltanolkotabi 2022
;
Javanmard, Soltanolkotabi and
Hassani 2020
analyzed the trade-offs in linear regression and binary classification with linear classifier, assuming the data was normally distributed. However, a comprehensive analysis of this problem is still lacking. We provide an upper bound for the excess risk that increases with the adversarial robustness level. Our result sheds light on the theoretical understanding of the trade-offs, but a complete analysis also requires lower bounds for the excess risk.

We
now consider the local worst-case risk.
Specifically, the local worst-case risk with
1
-th Wasserstein distance is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{P,1}(f,\varepsilon)=\sup_{Q:W_{1}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f),

where the distribution
Q
runs over an uncertainty set around the data generating distribution
P
. The excess risk with respect to the local worst-case risk is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)=\mathcal{R}_{P,1}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\mathcal{R}_{P,1}(f,\varepsilon).

Corollary 2
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2
are satisfied and
\operatorname{Lip}(\ell)<\infty,

then for any adversarial estimator
\widehat{f}_{n}
in (
2
), we have

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)\lesssim n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\xi}+K\varepsilon,

where
\xi=\max\{1,\gamma\alpha/(d+1)\}
.

4
Examples

In this section, we consider the more specific settings of classification and regression and apply
Theorem
2
to classification and regression problems.

4.1
Classification

Suppose that
(X,Y)
follows an unknown distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,1\}
.
A basic goal of binary classification is to predict the label
Y,
when we only observe a predictor
X
in a
random pair
(X,Y)\sim P

A commonly used loss
function
is the classification loss

\ell_{\operatorname{class}}:\mathbb{R}\times\{-1,1\}\mapsto[0,\infty),

defined by
\ell_{\operatorname{class}}(u,y)=\mathbf{1}\big\{\operatorname{sign}(u)y\leq 0\big\},

where
\operatorname{sign}(u)=1
when
u\geq 0
, and
\operatorname{sign}(u)=-1
, otherwise.
Let
f:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a score function, and let the associated binary classifier be
\operatorname{sign}f(\cdot)
. The natural classification risk and the adversarial classification risk of the score function
f
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\mathbf{1}\big\{\operatorname{sign}f(X)\neq Y\big\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\big[\sup_{X^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)}\mathbf{1}\left\{\operatorname{sign}f(X^{\prime})\neq Y\right\}\big].

Let
\eta(\bm{x})=P(Y=1|X=\bm{x})
. Define
c_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}\{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}>2\varepsilon\}
and let the corresponding optimal transport cost
D_{\varepsilon}
be defined by

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}(P,Q)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\mathbb{E}_{(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\pi}\big[c_{\varepsilon}(X_{1},X_{2})\big].

The minimum value of the natural classification risk is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star}=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f)=\mathbb{E}\big[\min\{\eta(X),1-\eta(X)\}\big],

which is reached when
f
is the Bayes
classifier, i.e.,
f(\bm{x})=\operatorname{sign}(2\eta(\bm{x})-1)

(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
.
The minimum value of the adversarial classification risk
can be expressed as

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star}(\varepsilon)=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f,\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{T+1}\big[1-\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):Q\preceq TP_{0}}D_{\varepsilon}(Q,P_{1})\big],

where
P_{1}=P_{X|Y=1},P_{0}=P_{X|Y=-1},
and
T=P(Y=-1)/P(Y=1)

(
Pydi and Jog 2021b
, Theorem 6.2)
.

The natural classification loss and its adversarial counterpart are
non-smooth and non-convex. Many surrogate losses have been considered in the context of standard classification. We specifically focus on margin-based loss, where a margin loss function
\phi
exists such that the loss function satisfies
\ell(u,y)=\phi(uy),\ (u,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{-1,1\}.

In general, the margin loss is selected to have a property called
consistency, which is satisfied by a large family of convex losses
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
. However, the adversarial version of these margin losses may not show the same consistency properties with respect to the adversarial classification loss. Moreover,
Meunier et al. 2022
showed that no convex margin loss can be calibrated in the adversarial setting. Consequently, it is challenging to study consistency in the general adversarial setting.

Let
C_{\operatorname{class}}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\mathbf{1}\{f(\bm{x})<0\}\eta+\mathbf{1}\{f(\bm{x})\geq 0\}(1-\eta)
and
C_{\operatorname{class}}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})=\min\{\eta,1-\eta\}
. Let
C_{\phi}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\phi(f(\bm{x}))\eta+\phi(-f(\bm{x}))(1-\eta)
and
C_{\phi}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})=\inf_{\alpha}\phi(\alpha)\eta+\phi(-\alpha)(1-\eta).
Define
\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star}=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)
.

Assumption 3
.

For any
\eta\in[0,1],

\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and measurable function
f
,

C_{\phi}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\phi}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})\geq a(C_{\operatorname{class}}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class}}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x}))

holds for a positive constant
a
.

Assumption 4
.

There exist positive constants
c
and
b
such that

\phi(0)-C_{\phi}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})\geq b(1-C_{\operatorname{class}}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x}))

when
|\eta-1/2|>c.

Assumptions
3
and
4
can be satisfied by some common margin losses, such as the hinge loss.

Corollary 3
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2
are satisfied and
\phi
is a continuous decreasing margin function satisfying Assumptions
3
and
4
.
Assume
|\eta(\bm{x})-1/2|>c
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)=\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star}.

Then,

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star}(\varepsilon)\lesssim n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\xi}+\varepsilon.

For the case where there might be
\eta(\bm{x})=1/2
, we show that the natural classification risk of the adversarial estimator converges to
\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star}
when
\varepsilon
goes to
0.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2
are satisfied and
\phi
is a margin function satisfying Assumptions
3
. Assume
\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)=\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star}.
Then,

\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}(\widehat{f}_{n})-\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star}\lesssim n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\xi}+\varepsilon.

4.2
Regression

Consider a nonparametric regression model

Y=f_{0}(X)+\eta,

(6)

where
Y\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq[-1,1]
is a response,
X\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
is a

is an unknown regression function, and
\eta
is an unobservable error satisfying
\mathbb{E}(\eta|X)=0
and
\mathbb{E}(\eta^{2})<\infty.

Under model (

) and the
quadratic loss
\ell(u,y)=(u-y)^{2},
we denote the corresponding adversarial estimator (

) by
\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls}
.
We measure the distance between
\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls}
and
f_{0}

using the
L_{2}(P)
norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}:=\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}(P_{X})}
, that is,
\|f\|_{2}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|f(X)|^{2}}
.
First, we derive a new error bound for the adversarial excess risk when using the quadratic loss.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha=r+\beta\geq 1
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\beta\in(0,1]
. Let
\gamma=\lceil\log_{2}(d+r)\rceil
. Suppose Assumption

Suppose

If we select

, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls},\varepsilon)\lesssim n^{-2\alpha/(2d+5\alpha)}\log n^{\lambda}+n^{(d+1)/(2d+5\alpha)}\varepsilon,

where

Theorem

shows that the error rate of the adversarial excess risk of

. It improves the rate

given by Theorem

. This is because a better control of the approximation error can be obtained with the quadratic loss function.

.

Suppose the

are satisfied, then

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls}-f_{0}\|^{2}_{2}\lesssim n^{-2\alpha/(2d+5\alpha)}\log n^{\lambda}+n^{(d+1)/(2d+5\alpha)}\varepsilon.

5
Related work

There is a line of
work focusing on the analysis of the Rademacher complexity of adversarial loss function class

(
Yin, Kannan and Bartlett 2019
;
Khim and Loh 2018
;
Awasthi, Frank and Mohri 2020
;
Mustafa, Lei and Kloft 2022
)
.
Specifically,

Yin, Kannan and Bartlett 2019
investigated the adversarial Rademacher complexity of the linear models under perturbations measured in
\ell_{\infty}
norm.
The result was later generalized by
Khim and Loh 2018
and
Awasthi, Frank and Mohri 2020
to the cases where the perturbations were measured in a general
\ell_{r}
norm.

For neural network models,
Yin, Kannan and Bartlett 2019
;
Awasthi, Frank and Mohri 2020
investigated adversarial training
when the model was a
neural network with a single hidden layer.

In
Khim and Loh 2018
and
Mustafa, Lei and Kloft 2022
, deep neural networks were studied.
Khim and Loh 2018
proposed a tree transformation
to upper bound the adversarial loss function.
In
Mustafa, Lei and Kloft 2022
, the covering number of the adversarial loss function class was shown to be upper bounded by the covering number of a newly defined loss function class over an extended training set. The Rademacher complexity of the adversarial loss function class can then be obtained by analyzing the covering number of the new loss function class.

Lee and Raginsky 2018
and
Tu, Zhang and Tao 2019
transformed the adversarial learning problem into a DRO problem.
However, in all the aforementioned works, the authors did not consider the approximation error and only focused on the generalization error, see Table
1
.

The problem of the trade-offs between accuracy and adversarial robustness has been studied recently
(
Madry et al. 2018
;
Schmidt et al. 2018
;
Tsipras et al. 2019
;
Raghunathan
et al. 2019
;
Dobriban et al. 2020
;
Ma, Wang and Liu 2022
;
Xing, Zhang and Cheng 2021
;
Dan, Wei and Ravikumar 2020
)
.
The works of
Javanmard and
Soltanolkotabi 2022
and
Javanmard, Soltanolkotabi and
Hassani 2020
gave a precise theoretical characterization of the trade-offs in the linear regression and parametric binary classification problems under the Gaussian assumption.
For the adversarial training,
Javanmard, Soltanolkotabi and
Hassani 2020
characterized its trade-off curve by calculating the natural risk and adversarial risk of the adversarial estimator that was derived from different adversarial attack levels.
They found that
the adversarial training hurt the accuracy if robustness is pursued.
However, there is still a lack of systematic
theoretical understanding of the trade-offs in general nonparametric settings.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed a general approach
to evaluating the
generalization performance of
estimators based on adversarial training under misspecified models.
The adversarial risk is shown to be equivalent to the risk induced by a distributional adversarial attack under certain smoothness conditions. This shows that the adversarial training procedure is well-defined.
We have established non-asymptotic error bounds on the adversarial excess risk,
which achieve the rate
O(n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)})
up to a logarithmic factor for a
Lipschitz loss function and can be improved to
O(n^{-2\alpha/(2d+5\alpha)})
up to a logarithmic factor when using
the quadratic loss.
We have also theoretically demonstrated that adversarial robustness can hurt accuracy in a general nonparametric setting.

There are several interesting problems that deserve further study.
First, the Lipschitz type condition (e.g. Assumption
2
) plays an important role
in our analysis. However, this assumption is not satisfied for the important quadratic
loss function when the support of
Y
is unbounded. It would be interesting to relax
this assumption in the future.
Also, we have only considered robustness against adversarial examples in
X
. How to generalize the results to the case when there are also adversarial examples in both
X
and
Y

is an important problem for future work. Finally, a complete analysis of the trade-offs between accuracy and adversarial robustness requires the establishment of lower bounds for the adversarial excess risk.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}\big[\sup_{X^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)}\mathbf{1}{\{\operatorname{sign}f(X^{\prime})\neq Y\}}\big]

\displaystyle=\int C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta(\bm{x}),\bm{x},f)dP_{X},

where
C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\mathbf{1}{\{f(\bm{x}^{\prime})<0\}}\eta+\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\mathbf{1}{\{f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\geq 0\}}(1-\eta)
. For the surrogate loss
\phi
, the surrogate risk and adversarial surrogate risk are

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}[\phi(f(X)Y)]

\displaystyle=\int\phi(f(\bm{x}))\eta(\bm{x})+\phi(-f(\bm{x}))(1-\eta(\bm{x}))dP_{X}

\displaystyle=\int C_{\phi}(\eta(\bm{x}),\bm{x},f)dP_{X},

where
C_{\phi}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\phi(f(\bm{x}))\eta+\phi(-f(\bm{x}))(1-\eta)
, and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}[\sup_{X^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)}\phi(f(X^{\prime})Y)]

\displaystyle=\int\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\phi(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))\eta(\bm{x})+\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\phi(-f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))(1-\eta(\bm{x}))dP_{X}

\displaystyle=\int C_{\phi_{,}\varepsilon}(\eta(\bm{x}),\bm{x},f)dP_{X},

where
C_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\phi(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))\eta+\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\phi(-f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))(1-\eta)
.

Based on Proposition 2.2 of
Meunier et al. 2022
, we have

C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x}):=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\inf_{\alpha}\mathbf{1}{\{\alpha<0\}}\eta+\mathbf{1}{\{\alpha\geq 0\}}(1-\eta),

and

C^{\star}_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x}):=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}C_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)=\inf_{\alpha}\phi(\alpha)\eta+\phi(-\alpha)(1-\eta).

Then,
C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})=C_{\operatorname{class}}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})
and
C^{\star}_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x})=C^{\star}_{\phi}(\eta,\bm{x})
. For any continuous function
f
, let
a_{f,\varepsilon}=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}f(\bm{x}^{\prime})
and
b_{f,\varepsilon}=\inf_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}f(\bm{x}^{\prime}).

\displaystyle C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\mathbf{1}{\{f(\bm{x}^{\prime})<0\}}\eta+\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\mathbf{1}{\{f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\geq 0\}}(1-\eta)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}{\{b_{f,\varepsilon}<0\}}\eta+\mathbf{1}{\{a_{f,\varepsilon}\geq 0\}}(1-\eta)-\min\{\eta,1-\eta\}.

If
a_{f,\varepsilon},b_{f,\varepsilon}\geq 0
or
a_{f,\varepsilon},b_{f,\varepsilon}<0
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})=C_{\operatorname{class}}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class}}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x}).

If
a_{f,\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
b_{f,\varepsilon}<0
,

\displaystyle C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})=1-C_{\operatorname{class}}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x}).

For the adversarial surrogate loss, since
\phi
is a decreasing continuous function, we have

\displaystyle C_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\phi(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))\eta+\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\phi(-f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))(1-\eta)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\phi(b_{f,\varepsilon})\eta+\phi(-a_{f,\varepsilon})(1-\eta)-C^{\star}_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x})

\displaystyle\geq C_{\phi}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x}).

If
a_{f,\varepsilon},b_{f,\varepsilon}\geq 0
or
a_{f,\varepsilon},b_{f,\varepsilon}<0
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})\geq a(C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})).

If
a_{f,\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
b_{f,\varepsilon}<0
, when
|\eta-\frac{1}{2}|>c
, then

\displaystyle C_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})\geq\phi(0)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})\geq a(C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})).

Consequently, for any continuous function
f
, any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, if
|\eta-\frac{1}{2}|>c
, then

\displaystyle C_{\phi,\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\phi,\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})\geq a(C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}(\eta,\bm{x},f)-C_{\operatorname{class},\varepsilon}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{x})).

Since
|\eta(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}|>c
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)-\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)\geq a(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f,\varepsilon)-\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f)).

Recall
\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star}=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star}=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f)
. Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)-\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star}-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)+\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star}

\displaystyle\geq a(\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\operatorname{class},P}(f,\varepsilon)-\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{class},P}^{\star})-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)+\mathcal{R}_{P}^{\star},

which completes the proof.
∎

B

B.1
Proof of Lemma 2.3

Since
\ell,f
are continuous and Assumption 2.1 hold, there exists a constant
M>0
such that
|\ell(f(\bm{x}),y)|\leq M
for any
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{Z}.
For any
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},y),\bm{z}^{\prime}=(\bm{x}^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}
, we define a function
\varphi(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\mapsto[0,\infty)
by

\varphi(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})=M-\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y^{\prime}).

From the continuity of
\ell
and
f
, the function
\varphi
is continuous. Define a function
h(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by
h(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}\{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon,y=y^{\prime}\}
and let
F:\mathcal{Z}\mapsto 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
be defined by

F(\bm{z})=\big\{\bm{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}:h(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})\in\{1\}\big\}.

It follows that
F(\bm{z})=\{(\bm{x}^{\prime},y):\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\}=B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\times\{y\}.
Let
\psi:\mathcal{Z}\mapsto[0,\infty)
be defined by
\psi(\bm{z})=\inf_{\bm{z}^{\prime}\in F(\bm{z})}\varphi(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})
, that is,

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\big\{M-\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)\big\}

\displaystyle=M-\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y).

By the continuity of
\varphi,
the infimum in the above can be attained for any
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
. Therefore, it follows from Aumann’s measurable selection principle in Lemma
7
that
\psi(\bm{z})
is measurable and there exists a measurable function
K^{\star}:\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathcal{Z}
with
\psi(\bm{z})=\varphi(\bm{z},K^{\star}(\bm{z}))
. In addition, from
K^{\star}(\bm{z})\in F(\bm{z}),
we can denote
K^{\star}
by
K^{\star}(\bm{z})=(T^{\star}(\bm{z}),y),
where
T^{\star}
is measurable function satisfying
T^{\star}(\bm{z})\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}).
Combining these together, we show

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)=\ell(f(T^{\star}(\bm{z})),y).

Let the joint distribution of
(T^{\star}(Z),Y)
be denoted by
P^{\star}
, it follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}[\sup_{X^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)}\ell(f(X^{\prime}),Y)]=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P^{\star}}[\ell(f(X),Y)].

In addition,
W_{\infty}(P^{\star},P)\leq\esssup_{Z\sim P}d_{\mathcal{Z}}(K^{\star}(Z),Z)=\esssup_{Z\sim P}\|T^{\star}(Z)-X\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon,

which completes the proof.
∎

B.2
Proof of Lemma 2.4

Based on Lemma 2.3, with
Z=(X,Y)
, we show

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)-\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\ell(f(T^{\star}(Z)),Y)-\mathbb{E}\ell(f(X),Y)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\mathbb{E}|f(T^{\star}(Z))-f(X)|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\operatorname{Lip}(f)\varepsilon.

In addition, from
\ell(f(\bm{x}),y)\leq\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)
, we have

\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon).

Consequently, we show

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{R}_{P}(f)+\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\operatorname{Lip}(f)\varepsilon,

which completes the proof.
∎

C.1
Proof of Theorem 2.5

Based on Lemma 2.3, we consider the joint distribution
P^{\star}
of
(T^{\star}(Z),Y)
. For any given
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we study the conditional distributions
P_{y}
and
P^{\star}_{y}
. Specifically,
P_{y}
is the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=y
and
P^{\star}_{y}
is the conditional distribution of
T^{\star}(Z)
given
Y=y
. Then, we have

W_{\infty}(P^{\star}_{y},P_{y})\leq\esssup_{X\sim P_{y}}\|T^{\star}(X,y)-X\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

It shows that
P^{\star}\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Therefore,

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{R}_{P^{\star}}(f)\leq\sup_{Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f).

Next, we prove the reverse direction. For any
Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
, we denote the corresponding pair of variables by
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
, and let the conditional distribution of
\widetilde{X}
given
\widetilde{Y}=y
be denoted by
Q_{y}.
With a similar analysis method in the proof of
(
Pydi and Jog 2021a
, Theorem 1)
, the condition
W_{\infty}(Q_{y},P_{y})\leq\varepsilon
implies that there is a joint distribution
\pi
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
such that the marginals are
Q_{y}
and
P_{y}
and satisfies

\esssup_{(\widetilde{X},X)\sim\pi}\|\widetilde{X}-X\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

It follows that
\widetilde{X}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)
almost surely. This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{y}}\big[\sup_{X^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X)}\ell(f(X^{\prime}),y)\big]-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{X}\sim Q_{y}}\big[\ell(f(\widetilde{X}),y)\big]\geq 0.

Since
\widetilde{Y}
follows
P_{Y}
, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)\geq\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f)
holds for any
Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Hence,

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)\geq\sup_{Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f).

This together with the above result implies

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)=\sup_{Q\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f)
, which completes our proof.
∎

C.2
Proof of Theorem 3.1

Let
f_{0}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\min\big\{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f^{\star},\varepsilon),\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f_{0},\varepsilon)\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\},

where
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f_{0},\varepsilon).
For the error
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\quad+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where the errors are

I_{1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)
,
I_{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)
, and
I_{3}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f^{\star},\varepsilon).
For the error
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, based on
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f_{0},\varepsilon),
we have the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f_{0},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\quad+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f_{0},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f_{0},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq I_{1}+I_{4},

where
I_{4}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f_{0},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f_{0},\varepsilon)
.
We show the non-asymptotic upper bound for the adversarial excess risk by deriving N upper bound for each error term.

C.2.1
Bounds for
I_{1}
and
I_{4}

For any
f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
and
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{Z}
, define

\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z})=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)=\sup_{\bm{\delta}\in B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}),y).

Since
\widehat{f}_{n}
and
f_{0}
belong to the class
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P_{n}}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)\big\}.

and

\displaystyle I_{4}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f_{0},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f_{0},\varepsilon)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P_{n}}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)\big\}.

Let the random vector
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
consist of i.i.d. the Rademacher variables that are independent from the data. The Rademacher variable takes equal probability of being
1
or
-1
. Denote the samples by
Z_{1:n}=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, with
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i}).

Let
Z_{i}^{\prime}=(X_{i}^{\prime},Y_{i}^{\prime}),i=1,\dots,n
, be generated i.i.d. from
P
and be independent of
Z_{1:n}
. The sample
Z_{1:n}^{\prime}=\{Z_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is called as the ghost sample of
Z_{1:n}
. Then,

\begin{split}&\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}[\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)]\big\}\\
&=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}[\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)]\big\}\\
&=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}_{1:n}}[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}^{\prime})]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i})\big\}\\
&=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}_{1:n}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}^{\prime})-\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i})\big]\big\}\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}_{1:n}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\big\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}^{\prime})-\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i})\big]\big\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}_{1:n}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\big\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\big[\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}^{\prime})-\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i})\big]\big\},\end{split}

(8)

where the last equality holds since
\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}^{\prime})-\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i})
are symmetric random variables, for which they have the same distribution as
\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}^{\prime})-\tilde{\ell}(f,Z_{i}))

(
Vershynin 2018
, Chapter 6.4)
. Define the class of functions
\mathcal{L}_{n}
by

\mathcal{L}_{n}=\Big\{\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z}):\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\mid f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)\Big\}.

For a given set of samples
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}
from
\mathcal{Z}
, the empirical Rademacher complexity of class
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is defined by

\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Big\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z}_{i})\Big\}.

We analyze the Rademacher complexity following the method motivated from
Mustafa, Lei and Kloft 2022
.
For a given
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon)
, let
C_{B_{\varepsilon}}(\tau)
be a
(\tau,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
-cover of
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
with the smallest cardinality
M_{\tau}
. Denote the elements of
C_{B_{\varepsilon}}(\tau)
by
\bm{\delta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\delta}_{M_{\tau}}
.
It follows by Lemma
SM1.3
that

\log M_{\tau}\leq cd\log(\varepsilon\tau^{-1})

for a constant
c.

For any
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{Z}
, the continuity of
\ell
and
f
imply that there exists
\bm{\delta}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(0)
such that
\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z})=\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}),y)
. Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z})-\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y)

\displaystyle=\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}),y)-\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{j}|\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}),y)-\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y)|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\operatorname{Lip}(f)\min_{j}\|\bm{\delta}^{\prime}-\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\operatorname{Lip}(f)\tau.

Therefore, for any
f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{\sigma_{i}\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z}_{i})-\sigma_{i}\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})+\sigma_{i}\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)K\tau+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{\sigma_{i}\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})\big\}.

This leads to an upper bound of
\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{n})
as follows.

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Big\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})\Big\}+\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)K\tau.

Define the class

\mathcal{L}_{n,\tau}=\Big\{\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y):\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\mid f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)\Big\}.

Let
\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{L}_{n,\tau},L_{\infty}(P_{n}))
denote the covering number of the class
\mathcal{L}_{n,\tau}
under the data dependent
L_{\infty}
metric. Define
S_{nM_{\tau}}=\{\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}:i=1,\dots,n,j=1,\dots,M_{\tau}\}
.
For the data set
S_{nM_{\tau}}
, let
\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K),L_{\infty}(P_{nM_{\tau}}))
denote the covering number of the class
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
under the data dependent
L_{\infty}
metric.
For any
f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
, there exists
f^{\prime}
such that
\max_{i,j}|f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j})-f^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j})|\leq u
, which leads to

\displaystyle\max_{i}\big|\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})-\max_{j}\ell(f^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i,j}|\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})-\ell(f^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\max_{i,j}|f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j})-f^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)u.

Hence, we show

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{L}_{n,\tau},L_{\infty}(P_{n}))\leq\mathcal{N}(u/\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell),\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K),L_{\infty}(P_{nM_{\tau}})).

Suppose the functions in
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
are uniformly bounded, otherwise they can be truncated. Define the uniform covering number of
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(u,\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K),n)=\sup_{P_{n}}\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K),L_{\infty}(P_{n})),

where the supremum runs over all the data set of size
n
. Combining Lemmas
SM1.1
and
SM1.2, we derive

\log\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(u,\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L),n)\leq C_{1}W^{2}L^{2}\log(W^{2}L)\log(u^{-1}n)

for a constant
C_{1}.
It follows

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{L}_{n,\tau},L_{\infty}(P_{n}))\leq C_{2}W^{2}L^{2}\log(W^{2}L)\log(u^{-1}nM_{\tau})

for a constant
C_{2}.
Since the class
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
is bounded and
\ell
is continuous, there exists
B>0
such that
\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}|\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y)|\leq B
for any
f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
. From Lemma
SM1.4
and
\log M_{\tau}\leq cd\log(\varepsilon\tau^{-1})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Big\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\max_{j}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{j}),y_{i})\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big\{4\delta+12\int_{\delta}^{B}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{L}_{n,\tau},L_{\infty}(P_{n}))}{n}}du\Big\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big\{\delta+WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\cdot\int_{\delta}^{B}\big[\sqrt{\log(u^{-1})}+\sqrt{\log n}+\sqrt{\log M_{\tau}}\;\big]du\Big\}

\displaystyle\lesssim WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\big\{\sqrt{\log n}+\sqrt{\log(\varepsilon\tau^{-1})}\big\}.

Therefore,
by selecting
\tau
such that
\varepsilon\tau^{-1}=O(n),
we show

\displaystyle I_{1}\lesssim K\varepsilon n^{-1}+WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log n}.

Following a similar procedure, we have
I_{4}\lesssim K\varepsilon n^{-1}+WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log n}
.

C.2.2
Bound for
I_{2}

Define the approximation error by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\alpha},\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)\right)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\|f-\phi\|_{C([0,1]^{d})},

where
C([0,1]^{d})
is the space of continuous functions on
[0,1]^{d}
equipped with the supremum norm. There exists
\bar{f}\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
approximating the target function
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}
such that

\|f^{\star}-\bar{f}\|_{C([0,1]^{d})}=O(\mathcal{E}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\alpha},\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)\right)).

The difference between the empirical adversarial risks
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\bar{f},\varepsilon)
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\bar{f},\varepsilon)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big|\sup_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X_{i})}\ell(f^{\star}(X^{\prime}_{i}),Y_{i})-\sup_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X_{i})}\ell(\bar{f}(X^{\prime}_{i}),Y_{i})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in B_{\varepsilon}(X_{i})}\Big|\ell(f^{\star}(X^{\prime}_{i}),Y_{i})-\ell(\bar{f}(X^{\prime}_{i}),Y_{i})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\cdot\|f^{\star}-\bar{f}\|_{C([0,1]^{d})}.

Since
\widehat{f}_{n}
minimizes the empirical adversarial risk over the class
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)
, then

\displaystyle I_{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\bar{f},\varepsilon)+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\bar{f},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\bar{f},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\cdot\|f^{\star}-\bar{f}\|_{C([0,1]^{d})}.

The approximation error
\mathcal{E}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\alpha},\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)\right)
is investigated by
Jiao, Wang and
Yang 2022
and the result is given as follows.

Lemma 9
(
Jiao, Wang and
Yang 2022
Theorem 3.2).

Let
\gamma=\lceil\log_{2}(d+r)\rceil
. There exists
c>0
such that for any
W\geq c(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{(2d+\alpha)/(2d+2)}
and
L\geq 4\gamma+2
,

\mathcal{E}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\alpha},\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)\right)\lesssim(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{-\alpha/(d+1)}.

Therefore, we derive

\displaystyle I_{2}\lesssim(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{-\alpha/(d+1)}.

C.2.3
Bound for
I_{3}

For any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{Z}
, we define
\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z})=\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)
. The error
I_{3}
can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle I_{3}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P_{n}}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)\big\}.

Define the class
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}=\{\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z}):\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\mid f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\}
.
Let
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
consist of i.i.d. the Rademacher variables and be independent from the data. For any samples
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}
from
\mathcal{Z}
, we denote the empirical Rademacher complexity of the class
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
by

\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Big\{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z}_{i})\Big\}.

From
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, there exists a constant
B
such that
\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}|\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z})|\leq B
for any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}
. In addition, we have

\log\mathcal{N}\big(u,\mathcal{L}^{\alpha},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\leq\log\mathcal{N}\big(u/\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell),\mathcal{H}^{\alpha},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big).

This is because that for any
f
,
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}
satisfying
\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\leq u/\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)
, it follows

\displaystyle\big|\tilde{\ell}(f,\bm{z})-\tilde{\ell}(\tilde{f},\bm{z})\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(f(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)-\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\ell(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}^{\prime}),y)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}^{1}(\ell)\sup_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})}\big|f(\bm{x}^{\prime})-\tilde{f}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle\leq u.

From
Kolmogorov and
Tikhomirov 1959
,
\log\mathcal{N}\big(u,\mathcal{H}^{\alpha},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\lesssim u^{-d/\alpha}
holds. Hence,

\log\mathcal{N}\big(u,\mathcal{L}^{\alpha},L_{2}(P_{n})\big)\leq\log\mathcal{N}\big(u,\mathcal{L}^{\alpha},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\lesssim u^{-d/\alpha},

where
L_{2}(P_{n})
denotes the
L_{2}
metric generated by the samples.
It follows by Lemma
SM1.4
that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})\lesssim\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big\{4\delta+12\int_{\delta}^{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}(u,\mathcal{L}^{\alpha},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})}{n}}du\Big\}.

Let
\gamma=d/(2\alpha)
.
Thus, we show

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})\lesssim\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big(\delta+n^{-1/2}\int_{\delta}^{1}u^{-\gamma}du\Big).

When
\gamma>1,
by taking
\delta=n^{-\alpha/d}
, one has

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})\lesssim\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big(\delta+(\gamma-1)^{-1}n^{-1/2}(\delta^{1-\gamma}-1)\Big)\lesssim n^{-\alpha/d}.

When
\gamma=1,
by taking
\delta=n^{-1/2}
, one has

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})\lesssim\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big(\delta-n^{-1/2}\log\delta\Big)\lesssim n^{-1/2}\log n.

When
\gamma<1,
one has

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})\lesssim\inf_{\delta\geq 0}\Big(\delta+(1-\gamma)^{-1}n^{-1/2}(1-\delta^{1-\gamma})\Big)\lesssim n^{-1/2}.

Combining these cases together, we derive

\displaystyle\widehat{\Re}_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha})\lesssim n^{-\min\{1/2,\alpha/d\}}\log^{c(\alpha,d)}n,

where
c(\alpha,d)=1
if
d=2\alpha
, and
c(\alpha,d)=0,
otherwise. Following a similar analysis method in (
8
), we derive

\begin{split}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P_{n}}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}\tilde{\ell}(f,Z)\big\}\lesssim n^{-\min\{1/2,\alpha/d\}}\log^{c(\alpha,d)}n.\end{split}

Consequently, it follows

I_{3}\lesssim n^{-\min\{1/2,\alpha/d\}}\log^{c(\alpha,d)}n.

Let
\gamma=\lceil\log_{2}(d+r)\rceil
. Combining the results from Sections
C.2.1
–
C.2.3
, we show for any
W\geq c(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{(2d+\alpha)/(2d+2)}
and
L\geq 4\gamma+2
,

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)&=\max\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\}\\
&\leq\max\{I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},I_{1}+I_{4}\}\\
&\lesssim K\varepsilon n^{-1}+WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log n}\\
&\quad+(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{-\alpha/(d+1)}+n^{-\min\{1/2,\alpha/d\}}\log^{c(\alpha,d)}n,\end{split}

(9)

where
c(\alpha,d)=1
when
d=2\alpha
, and
c(\alpha,d)=0,
otherwise.
By selecting
K\asymp n^{(d+1)/(2d+3\alpha)}
, and
WL\asymp n^{(2d+\alpha)/(4d+6\alpha)}
, we have

\displaystyle WL\sqrt{\log(W^{2}L)}n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log n}+(K/\log^{\gamma}K)^{-\alpha/(d+1)}

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\max\{1,\gamma\alpha/(d+1)\}}.

This leads to

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)\lesssim K\varepsilon n^{-1}+n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\xi},

where
\xi=\max\{1,\gamma\alpha/(d+1)\}
. Hence, the result is proved.

C.4
Proof of Corollary 3.3

From Lemma 2.3, there exists
P^{\star}
such that
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{R}_{P^{\star}}(f)
, where
P^{\star}
is dependent on
f
and satisfies
W_{\infty}(P^{\star},P)\leq\varepsilon
. Since
W_{p}(P,Q)\leq W_{q}(P,Q)
for any
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty,
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)\leq\sup_{Q:W_{\infty}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f)\leq\sup_{Q:W_{p}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f),

for any
1\leq p\leq\infty.
It implies

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f,\varepsilon)\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\sup_{Q:W_{1}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f).

For a function
\psi:\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R},
the Lipschitz constant is defined by

\operatorname{Lip}(\psi)=\sup_{\bm{z}_{1}\neq\bm{z}_{2}}\frac{|\psi(\bm{z}_{1})-\psi(\bm{z}_{2})|}{d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2})}.

Let the 1-Lipschitz function class be defined by

\Psi_{\operatorname{Lip}}^{1}=\big\{\psi:\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R},\;\operatorname{Lip}(\psi)\leq 1\big\}.

The
1
-th Wasserstein distance
W_{1}
has an useful duality formula

\displaystyle W_{1}(Q,P)=\sup_{\psi\in\Psi_{\operatorname{Lip}}^{1}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}[\psi(Z)]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}[\psi(Z)]\Big\}.

It follows that for any
Q
satisfying
W_{1}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f)-\mathcal{R}_{P^{\star}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}[\ell(f(X),Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P^{\star}}[\ell(f(X),Y)]\leq 2\operatorname{Lip}(\ell)(\operatorname{Lip}(f)+1)\varepsilon.

It shows

\sup_{Q:W_{1}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(\widehat{f}_{n})\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n},\varepsilon)+2\operatorname{Lip}(\ell)(K+1)\varepsilon.

Let
r_{n}=n^{-\alpha/(2d+3\alpha)}\log n^{\max\{1,\gamma\alpha/(d+1)\}}
. From Theorem 3.1, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{Q:W_{1}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(\widehat{f}_{n})-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}(W,L,K)}\sup_{Q:W_{1}(Q,P)\leq\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(f)\leq r_{n}+2\operatorname{Lip}(\ell)(K+1)\varepsilon,

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem 4.5

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f^{\star},\varepsilon)&=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls},\varepsilon)+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\widehat{f}_{n}^{ls},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(\bar{f},\varepsilon)\\

\displaystyle\quad+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P_{n}}(f_{0},\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{P}(f_{0},\varepsilon)

A.1

shows that there exists
