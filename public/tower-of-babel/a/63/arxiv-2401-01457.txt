Flip Graphs on Self-Complementary Ideals of Chain Products
Serena An, Holden Mui

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a flip operation on self-complementary ideals of chain product posets and study the resulting flip graphs. We give asymptotics for the number of vertices in these graphs, compute their diameters, and give bounds for their radii. We also define similar flip operations on self-complementary ideals of the chain product [ 2 ​ r ] × [ 2 ​ r ] × [ 2 ​ r ] [2r]\times[2r]\times[2r] satisfying additional symmetries, and we achieve similar results for the resulting flip graphs.

Introduction

Self-complementary ideals of chain products originate from a generalization of maximal intersecting families. The graph of maximal intersecting families of a fixed set
S
is formed by taking all maximal intersecting families as vertices, and connecting two maximal intersecting families if they differ in exactly one set. Asymptotics for the number of maximal intersecting families are given in
[
BMMV13
]
; this paper also computes this vertex count exactly for all
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq 9
. The structure of this graph is studied in
[
LM97
]
, which computes bounds and some exact values for its diameter and radius. Another related graph is studied in
[
Mey95
]
, where maximal intersecting families that are the same under a permutation of
S
are mapped to the same vertex.

Because the poset of all subsets of a finite set of size
n
is isomorphic to an
n
-fold product of the two-element chain, this setup admits a natural generalization to chains of arbitrary length. In this setting, maximal intersecting families correspond to self-complementary ideals. The corresponding graph, which we refer to as a
flip graph
, is formed by taking all self-complementary ideals of a chain product as vertices and connecting two ideals if they differ in the minimum possible number of elements. Questions about the structure of the flip graph include vertex count bounds, diameter bounds, and radius bounds.

Additional types of flip graphs can be created by imposing symmetry restrictions on the self-complementary ideals that form the vertices of the flip graph. One such example is the flip graph on all cyclically symmetric self-complementary (CSSC) ideals of a product of three chains of equal length; two ideals are connected if they differ in exactly three elements. Another such example considers the set of all totally symmetric self-complementary (TSSC) ideals; in this setting, some edges need to be weighted differently to account for the fact that some vertices of the poset lie on axes of symmetry. The total number of vertices in these graphs are computed in
[
Kup94
]
and
[
And94
]
, respectively, via connections to plane partitions.

The vertex count for the flip graphs on cyclically symmetric and totally symmetric self-complementary ideals are known exactly; asymptotics are given for all three cases. The diameter of the flip graph is also known exactly in all three cases. 2
Preliminaries

2.1
Posets

A
poset

(P,\leq_{P})
is a set
P
with a binary relation
\leq_{P}
that satisfies the following three conditions for all
a,b,c\in P
:

•

a\leq_{P}a
(reflexivity),

•

a\leq_{P}b
and
b\leq_{P}a
imply
a=b
(antisymmetry), and

•

a\leq_{P}b
and
b\leq_{P}c
imply
a\leq_{P}c
(transitivity).

When the binary relation
\leq_{P}
is unambiguous, it is denoted with the symbol
\leq
. Similarly, posets are denoted with their base set for brevity.

A poset is
self-dual
if there exists an involution
\varphi:P\to P
such that

a\leq_{P}b\iff\varphi(b)\leq_{P}\varphi(a).

When referring to a self-dual poset, it is assumed that the map
\varphi
is given and fixed.

The
product

P_{1}\times\dots\times P_{n}
of a finite number of posets
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
is the poset on all tuples
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
with
a_{1}\in P_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in P_{n}
and the relation

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\leq_{P_{1}\times\dots\times P_{n}}(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\iff\text{$a_{1}\leq_{P_{1}}b_{1}$, $\dots$, $a_{n}\leq_{P_{n}}b_{n}$.}

Observe that a product of self-dual posets is self-dual; if
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
come with involutions
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}
, then the involution on
P_{1}\times\dots\times P_{n}
is given by

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\mapsto(\varphi_{1}(a_{1}),\dots,\varphi_{n}(a_{n})).

One example of a poset, and also the primary poset of importance in this paper, is a chain. Given a positive integer
\ell
, the
chain
with
\ell
elements is the poset with elements
\{1,2,\dots,\ell\}
and the binary relation given by the natural ordering on the integers. This poset is denoted with the notation
[\ell]
. Chains are self-dual since the involution
\varphi:\{1,\dots,\ell\}\to\{1,\dots,\ell\}
sending
i
to
\ell+1-i
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
reverses the natural ordering on the integers.

2.2
Ideals

Let
P
be a poset. An
ideal
of
P
is a subset
I\subseteq P
with the property that

\text{$a\in I$ and $b\leq a$}\implies b\in I

for all
a\in I,b\in P
. Given an ideal
I
, an element
a\in I
is
maximal
if there does not exist
b\in I
with
b\neq a
and
a\leq b
.

Remark
.

The standard definition of an ideal states that ideals are nonempty, but for our purposes, we will allow empty ideals.

Given a self-dual poset
P
, an ideal
I\subset P
is
self-complementary
if

a\in I\iff\varphi(a)\notin I.

Observe that if
I
is self-complementary, then
I\sqcup\varphi(I)=P
, where
\varphi(I)=\{\varphi(a)\mid a\in I\}
. In particular, only self-dual posets with an even number of elements can have self-complementary ideals.

If the poset
P
is a product of chains
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
for a sequence of positive integers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
, an ideal
I\subset P
is self-complementary if

(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in I\iff(\ell_{1}+1-a_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}+1-a_{d})\notin I.

If the chains that comprise the poset all have the same number of elements, it is possible for ideals to have additional types of symmetries. Let
\ell
be a positive integer; an ideal
I\subseteq[\ell]\times[\ell]\times[\ell]
is
cyclically symmetric
if

(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in I\implies\left(a_{\sigma(1)},a_{\sigma(2)},a_{\sigma(3)}\right)\in I

for all 3-cycles
\sigma\in S_{3}
. Similarly, an ideal
I\subseteq[\ell]\times[\ell]\times[\ell]
is
totally symmetric
if

(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in I\implies\left(a_{\sigma(1)},a_{\sigma(2)},a_{\sigma(3)}\right)\in I

for all permutations
\sigma\in S_{3}
.

2.3
Graphs

Given a graph
G
, let let
V(G)
denote its vertex set and let
v(G)=\left\lvert V(G)\right\rvert
denote its vertex count.

Let
G
be a connected, unweighted graph. The
distance
between two vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V(G)
is the minimum possible number of edges in any path connecting
v
and
v^{\prime}
. Similarly, let
G
be a connected, weighted graph with nonnegative edge weights. The
distance
between two vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V(G)
is the minimum possible sum of the edge weights along any path connecting
v
and
v^{\prime}
. Observe that the distance in a weighted graph with edge weights all equal to 1 coincides with the distance in the corresponding unweighted graph. The distance between two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
is denoted
\operatorname{dist}(u,v)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph that can be weighted or unweighted. The
eccentricity
of a vertex
v\in G
is the maximum distance between
v
and any vertex in
G
. The
diameter
of
G
is the maximum eccentricity of any vertex
v\in G
, and the
radius
of
G
is the minimum eccentricity of any vertex
v\in G
. The
center
of
G
is the set of all vertices with eccentricity equal to the radius. The
perimeter
of
G
is the set of all vertices with eccentricity equal to the diameter.

3
Self-Complementary Ideals

Given a self-dual poset
P
, the
flip graph on self-complementary ideals of
P
is the unweighted graph whose vertices are the self-complementary ideals of
P
, and whose edges connect self-complementary ideals
I,J\subset P
for which

\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert J\setminus I\right\rvert=1.

When the context is clear, this graph will simply be referred to as the
flip graph
. The graph is named this way because traversing an edge of the graph can be thought of as a “flip”: taking a maximal element
a
of some ideal and replacing it with its dual
\varphi(a)
.
Figure

4
shows the flip graph structure for the specific case
P=[2]\times[3]\times[4]
.

Figure 4:
The flip graph on self-complementary ideals of
[2]\times[3]\times[4]
.

The following lemma, although simple to prove, is important for establishing bounds on the distance between self-complementary ideals in the flip graph.

Lemma 3.0.1
.

Let
I
and
J
be self-complementary ideals of a self-dual poset
P
. Then the distance from
I
to
J
in the flip graph of
P
is
\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert
. In particular, the flip graph of any self-dual poset is connected.

Proof.

The distance from
I
to
J
must be at least
\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert
since each flip decreases
\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert
by at most 1. Thus, it suffices to give a path from
I
to
J
using exactly
\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert
flips. Indeed, repeatedly flipping a maximal element in
I\setminus J
works.
∎

Given a positive integer
d
and a sequence of positive integers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
, let
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
denote the flip graph on self-complementary ideals of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
.

3.1
Vertex Count

Consider the graph
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
for some sequence of positive integers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
. When
d\leq 3
, the exact vertex count of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
is known.

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
d\leq 3
be a positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of positive integers with even product.

•

If
d=1
, the number of vertices in
G(\ell_{1})
is 1.

•

If
d=2
, the number of vertices in
G(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
is
\displaystyle\binom{\left\lfloor\ell_{1}/2\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\ell_{2}/2\right\rfloor}{\left\lfloor\ell_{1}/2\right\rfloor}
.

•

If
d=3
, the number of vertices in
G(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
is

\left(\prod_{i_{1}=1}^{\left\lfloor\ell_{1}/2\right\rfloor}\prod_{i_{2}=1}^{\left\lceil\ell_{2}/2\right\rceil}\prod_{i_{3}=1}^{{\ell_{3}/2}}\frac{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}-1}{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}-2}\right)\left(\prod_{i_{1}=1}^{\left\lceil\ell_{1}/2\right\rceil}\prod_{i_{2}=1}^{\left\lfloor\ell_{2}/2\right\rfloor}\prod_{i_{3}=1}^{{\ell_{3}/2}}\frac{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}-1}{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}-2}\right),

assuming
\ell_{3}
is even.

Proof.

If
d=1
and
\ell_{1}
is even, there is only one self-complementary ideal.

If
d=2
, there is a bijection between self-complementary ideals of
[\ell_{1}]\times[\ell_{2}]
and rotationally symmetric up-left lattice paths from
(\ell_{1},0)
to
(0,\ell_{2})
. Observing that each rotationally symmetric path bijects to a up-left lattice path from
(\ell_{1},0)
to
(\left\lceil\ell_{1}/2\right\rceil,\left\lfloor\ell_{2}/2\right\rfloor)
gives the desired result. (An example of the bijection is given in
Figure

5
.)

If
d=3
, the result is given in
[
Sta86
]
.
∎

Determining exact answers for
d\geq 4
in general seems to be a difficult problem; even getting asymptotically tight results appears to be difficult due to the intractability of counting solid partitions. However, some statements about asymptotics can be made, depending on which parameters are fixed and which are varied.

Theorem 3.1.2
.

Let
d
be a positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d-1}
be a fixed sequence of positive integers. Then as
n
tends to infinity, we have

v(G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d-1},n))\sim Cn^{\left\lfloor\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}/2\right\rfloor}

for some constant
C
. (If
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}
is even, the asymptotic runs over all positive integers
n
. If
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}
is odd, the asymptotic runs over all even positive integers
n
.)

Proof.

Let
P
denote the poset

P=\begin{cases}[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]&\text{if $\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}$ even}\\
\left([\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\right)\setminus(\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{1}+1),\dots,\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{d-1}+1))&\text{if $\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}$ odd.}\end{cases}

It suffices to find asymptotics for the number of order-preserving functions
f:P\to\{0,\dots,n\}
satisfying
\varphi_{\{0,\dots,n\}}(f(a))=f(\varphi_{P}(a))
for all
a\in P
, where
\varphi_{\{0,\dots,n\}}
and
\varphi_{P}
denote the self-dual involution on
\{0,\dots,n\}
and
P
, respectively; this condition is equivalent to the corresponding ideal in
P\times[n]
being self-complementary. To do this, pick any self-complementary ideal
I\subset P
. Observe that
\left\lvert I\right\rvert=\left\lfloor\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}\right\rfloor
and that any function
g:I\to\{0,\dots,n\}
extends uniquely to a function
\tilde{g}:P\to\{0,\dots,n\}
satisfying
\varphi_{\{0,\dots,n\}}(\tilde{g}(a))=\tilde{g}(\varphi_{P}(a))
for all
a\in P
. (If
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}
is odd, then
\tilde{g}((\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{1}+1),\dots,\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{d-1}+1)))=\frac{1}{2}n
is forced.) An example of this extension is given in
Figure

6
.

Construct a function
h:I\to[0,1)
such that each of its
\left\lvert I\right\rvert
outputs are chosen uniformly and independently, and define
g:I\to\{0,\dots,n\}
via
a\mapsto\left\lfloor(n+1)h(a)\right\rfloor
; note that this uniformly selects a function from
I\to\{0,\dots,n\}
. In the limit of large
n
, the probability that
\tilde{g}
is order-preserving is asymptotically equal to the probability that
h
is order-preserving, since the probability that two outputs of
g
have the same floor tends to zero. If
C
denotes the probability that
h
is order-preserving, the total number of order-preserving functions
f:P\to\{0,\dots,n\}
satisfying
\varphi_{\{0,\dots,n\}}(\tilde{g}(a))=\tilde{g}(\varphi_{P}(a))
for all
a\in P
is asymptotic to
Cn^{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}=Cn^{\left\lfloor\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}/2\right\rfloor}
, as desired.
∎

For the next asymptotic result, the following crude bound will be needed.

Lemma 3.1.3
.

Let
n
and
d
be positive integers with
d\geq 2
. Then there are at most
4^{n^{d-1}}
ideals (not necessarily self-complementary) in
[n]^{d}
.

Proof.

Induct on
d
. For the base case of
d=2
, there are
\binom{2n}{n}\leq 4^{n}
such ideals. For the inductive step, observe that each layer of an ideal in
[n]^{d}
is an ideal in
[n]^{d-1}
. There are
d
such layers and at most
4^{n^{d-2}}
choices for each layer by the inductive step, so there are at most
\bigl(4^{n^{d-2}}\bigr)^{n}=4^{n^{d-1}}
ideals in
[n]^{d}
.
∎

Theorem 3.1.4
.

Let
d
be a fixed positive integer. As
n
tends to infinity, we have

\log v(G(\underbrace{n,\dots,n}_{\text{$d$ $n$'s}}))=\Theta(n^{d-1}).

(The asymptotic runs over all even positive integers
n
).

Proof.

The upper bound is given by
Lemma

3.1.3
. For the lower bound, it suffices to construct
2^{\Theta(n^{d-1})}
self-complementary ideals of
[n]^{d}
.

Consider the set

S=\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[n^{d}]\left|\medspace\begin{aligned} a_{d}&\leq\tfrac{1}{2}n\text{ and }a_{1}+\dots+a_{d}\leq d(\tfrac{1}{2}n+1)-1,\text{ or}\\
a_{d}&>\tfrac{1}{2}n\text{ and }a_{1}+\dots+a_{d}\leq d(\tfrac{1}{2}n)+1\end{aligned}\right.\right\}.

An example of this set is given in
Figure

7
.

Figure 7:
S
for the case
n=10
,
d=3
.

Let
M
be the set of maximal elements in
S
; observe that
\varphi(m)\in M
for each
m\in M
, where
\varphi
is the self-dual involution on
[n]^{d}
. Therefore, every subset
M^{\prime}\subset M
such that
M=M^{\prime}\sqcup\varphi(M^{\prime})
gives rise to a self-complementary ideal
S\setminus M^{\prime}
of
[n]^{d}
.
Bounding the size of
\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert M\right\rvert
gives

\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lvert M\right\rvert\geq\lvert\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq_{0}}^{d}\mid b_{1}+\dots+b_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}n-1\}\rvert=\binom{\tfrac{1}{2}n-1+d-1}{d-1}=\Theta(n^{d-1}),

since a suitable translation of the set
\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq_{0}}^{d}\mid b_{1}+\dots+b_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}n-1\}
is a subset of
M
.
This gives

2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert/2}\geq 2^{\Theta(n^{d-1})}

such self-complementary ideals, as desired.
∎

Remark
.

For
d=2
, the asymptotic is exactly

v(G(n,n))=\binom{n}{\frac{1}{2}n}\sim C\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

for
C=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\approx 0.798
. The logarithmic asymptotic is thus
\log v(G(n,n))\sim\log(2)n\approx 0.693n
. For
d=3
, the asymptotic is exactly

v(G(n,n,n))=\left(\prod_{i_{1}=1}^{n/2}\prod_{i_{2}=1}^{n/2}\prod_{i_{3}=1}^{n/2}\frac{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}-1}{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}-2}\right)^{2}\sim Cn^{-1/6}e^{9n}\left(\frac{9\sqrt[4]{3}}{8}\right)^{n^{2}}

for
C=2^{1/3}3^{-1/6}e^{2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-18}
. The logarithmic asymptotic is thus
\log v(G(n,n,n))\sim\log(9\sqrt[4]{3}/8)n^{2}\approx 0.392n^{2}
.
For

, the exact logarithmic asymptotic appears to be open.

3.2
Diameter

This section will compute the exact value of the diameter of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
for every sequence of positive integers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
x_{1}\geq\dots\geq x_{n}
and
y_{1}\geq\dots\geq y_{n}
be nonincreasing sequences of real numbers. Then

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\geq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right).

Proof.

This is an application of the rearrangement inequality. Alternatively, observe that

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}-x_{j})(y_{i}-y_{j})\geq 0

is the difference between the left hand side and the right hand side of the original inequality.
∎

Now, given a poset
P
and an ideal
I\subseteq P
, let
\mu(I)
denote the
density

\mu(I)=\frac{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}{\left\lvert P\right\rvert}
. The following lemma is effectively a “correlation inequality” and is a generalization of the Harris-Kleitman inequality
[
Har60
,
Kle66
]
.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

Let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of positive integers, and let
I
and
J
be ideals of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
. Then

\mu(I\cap J)\geq\mu(I)\mu(J)
.

Proof.

Induct on
d
. The base case of
d=1
follows from the inequality
\frac{\min(\left\lvert I\right\rvert,\left\lvert J\right\rvert)}{\ell_{1}}\geq\frac{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}{\ell_{1}}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert J\right\rvert}{\ell_{1}}
, since
\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert=\min(\left\lvert I\right\rvert,\left\lvert J\right\rvert)
in this case.

For the inductive step, let
I
and
J
be ideals of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
, and for each index
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell_{d}\}
define

\displaystyle I_{i}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\mid(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},i)\in I\},

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\mid(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},i)\in J\}.

Observe that both
I_{i}
and
J_{i}
are ideals of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]
for each index
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell_{d}\}
, and that
\mu(I_{1})\geq\dots\geq\mu(I_{\ell_{d}})
and
\mu(J_{1})\geq\dots\geq\mu(J_{\ell_{d}})
.
By the inductive hypothesis and
Lemma

3.2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mu(I\cap J)=\frac{1}{\ell_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{d}}\mu(I_{i}\cap J_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\ell_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{d}}\mu(I_{i})\mu(J_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{d}}\mu(I_{i})\right)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{d}}\mu(J_{i})\right)=\mu(I)\mu(J),

completing the induction.
∎

Corollary 3.2.3
.

Let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of positive integers with
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}
even. Then for any self-complementary ideals
I,J\subset[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
, we have

\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{4}\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}.

Proof.

Since
\mu(I)=\mu(J)=\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\frac{\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert}{\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}}=\mu(I\cap J)\geq\mu(I)d(J)=\frac{1}{4}

by
Lemma

3.2.2
.
∎

Corollary 3.2.4
.

Let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of positive integers, all of which are odd except for
\ell_{k}
. Then for any self-complementary ideals
I,J\subset[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
, we have

\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{4}(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}+\ell_{k})
.

Proof.

Induct on
d
. The base case of
d=1
is true because the flip graph on
[\ell_{1}]
has only one self-complementary ideal: the ideal with
\frac{1}{2}\ell_{1}
elements.

For the inductive step, suppose without loss of generality that
\ell_{d}
is odd. Consider the sets

\displaystyle I^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in I\mid a_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1)\}

\displaystyle J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in J\mid a_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1)\}.

and

\displaystyle I^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\mid(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1))\in I^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\mid(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1))\in J^{\prime}\right\}.

Observe that
I^{\prime\prime}
and
J^{\prime\prime}
are self-complementary ideals in
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]
, since the involution on the self-dual poset
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
maps elements with last coordinate
\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1)
to elements with last coordinate
\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1)
. Similarly,
I\setminus I^{\prime}
and
J\setminus J^{\prime}
are self-complementary ideals in
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\times([\ell_{d}]\setminus\{\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{d}+1)\})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert I^{\prime\prime}\cap J^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert+\left\lvert(I\setminus I^{\prime})\cap(J\setminus J^{\prime})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\tfrac{1}{4}(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}+\ell_{k})+\tfrac{1}{4}(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d-1}(\ell_{d}-1))

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}+\ell_{k})

by the inductive hypothesis and
Corollary

3.2.3
.
∎

Theorem 3.2.5
.

Let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of positive integers, and let
V
denote their product. The diameter of the flip graph on self-complementary ideals of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
is

\begin{cases}0&\text{if all of $\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}$ are odd,}\\
\frac{1}{4}V&\text{if at least two of $\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}$ are even, and}\\
\frac{1}{4}(V-\ell_{k})&\text{if $\ell_{k}$ is even and the rest are odd.}\end{cases}

Proof.

If all of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are odd, then no self-complementary ideals exist. Thus, the flip graph is empty, and the diameter is zero.

Now, suppose that at least two of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are even.

•

To prove that the distance between any two self-complementary ideals is at most
\frac{1}{4}V
, let
I
and
J
be self-complementary ideals in
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
. By
Lemma

3.0.1
and
Corollary

3.2.3
, the distance between
I
and
J
is

\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert I\right\rvert-\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}V-\tfrac{1}{4}V=\tfrac{1}{4}V.

•

Suppose that
i
and
j
are distinct indices for which both
\ell_{i}
and
\ell_{j}
are even. Then the self-complementary ideals

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid a_{i}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{i}\}

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid a_{j}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{j}\}

(see
Figure

8
for an example) satisfy

\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid\text{$a_{i}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{i}$ and $a_{j}>\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{j}$}\right\}\right\rvert=\tfrac{1}{4}V.

By
Lemma

3.0.1
, the distance between
I
and
J
is
\frac{1}{4}V
.

Therefore the diameter of the flip graph of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
is
\frac{1}{4}V
when at least two of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are even.

Finally, suppose that
\ell_{k}
is even and the rest are odd. If
d=1
, the result follows because the diameter of a one-vertex graph is zero; hence, suppose
d\geq 2
.

•

To prove that the distance between any two self-complementary ideals is at most
\frac{1}{4}(V-\ell_{k})
, let
I
and
J
be self-complementary ideals in
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
. By
Lemma

3.0.1
and
Corollary

3.2.4
, the distance between
I
and
J
is

\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert I\right\rvert-\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}V-\tfrac{1}{4}(V+\ell_{k})=\tfrac{1}{4}(V-\ell_{k}).

•

Without loss of generality, assume
\ell_{d}
is even, and let
I^{\prime}
be a self-complementary ideal of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\setminus\left\{\left(\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{1}+1),\dots,\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{d-1}+1)\right)\right\}
. Then the self-complementary ideals

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\left(I^{\prime}\times[\ell_{d}]\right)\cup\left\{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{1}+1),\dots,\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{d-1}+1),i\right)\mid i\in\{1,\dots,\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{d}\}\right\}

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid a_{d}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{d}\}

(see
Figure

9
for an example) satisfy

\displaystyle\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert I^{\prime}\times\{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{d}+1,\dots,\ell_{d}\}\right\rvert=\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{1}\dots\ell_{d-1}-1)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{d}=\tfrac{1}{4}(V-\ell_{d}).

By
Lemma

3.0.1
, the distance between
I
and
J
is
\frac{1}{4}(V-\ell_{d})
.
∎

3.3
Radius

This section will compute the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
for certain sequences of positive integers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
. The answer is known exactly in the specific case when both of the following conditions hold:

•

all of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are even, and

•

d
is odd or some
\ell_{i}
is divisible by 4.

Some of these results are generalizations of
[
Mey95
]
, which gives a tight radius bound for
[2]^{d}
when
d
is odd.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. Let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of even positive integers, and let
V
denote their product. Then the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
is at least

{\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V}.

Proof.

Let
r
denote the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
, and let
C
be any self-complementary ideal of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
contained in the center of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
. Additionally, for each index
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
let
I_{i}
be the self-complementary ideal

I_{i}=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid a_{i}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{i}\}.

Then

r\geq\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,d\}}\operatorname{dist}(C,I_{i})\geq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{dist}(C,I_{i})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert C\setminus I_{i}\right\rvert=\frac{1}{2}V-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert C\cap I_{i}\right\rvert.

Now, for each every tuple
(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, define

c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}=\left\lvert\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in C\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}>\ell_{1}/2}=t_{1},\dots,\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}>\ell_{d}/2}=t_{d}\}\right\rvert.

Since
C
is self-complementary, we have

c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}+c_{(1-t_{1},\dots,1-t_{d})}=\frac{V}{2^{d}}

for every tuple
(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.
Using this fact gives

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert C\cap I_{i}\right\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\
t_{i}=0\end{subarray}}c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}=\sum_{s=0}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\
t_{1}+\dots+t_{d}=s\end{subarray}}(d-s)c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}.

Using the equalities
c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}+c_{(1-t_{1},\dots,1-t_{d})}=\frac{V}{2^{d}}
for all
(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and the inequalities
c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}\leq\frac{V}{2^{d}}
for
t_{1}+\dots+t_{d}<\frac{1}{2}d
gives a bound of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert C\cap I_{i}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}d\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{(d-1)/2}\right)V&\text{if $d$ is odd}\\
d\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{(d-2)/2}\right)V&\text{if $d$ is even}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=d\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V

after collecting the binomial sums.
Therefore,

r\geq\frac{1}{2}V-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert C\cap I_{i}\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}V-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V

for all positive integers
d
.
∎

Remark
.

Equality in the above lemma can only occur when the inequality
c_{(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})}\leq\frac{V}{2^{d}}
is tight for all
(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
satisfying
t_{1}+\dots+t_{d}<\frac{1}{2}d
. An example of the equality case for the poset
[6]\times[8]\times[4]
is given in
Figure

11
.

The next theorem, known as the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem, is a famous result bounding the size of an intersecting family of
r
-element sets. It will give a tight bound for the radius in the case when all of the side lengths
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are even and the dimension
d
is odd.

Theorem 3.3.2

(Erdős-Ko-Rado)
.

Let
n
and
r
be nonnegative integers with
n\geq 2r
. Let
S
be an
n
-element set, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a set of
r
-element subsets of
S
for which every pair of sets in
\mathcal{F}
share an element. Then
\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{r-1}
.

Proof.

A beautiful proof by Katona is given in
[
Kat72
]
.
∎

Lemma 3.3.3
.

Let
d
be an odd positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of even positive integers with product
V
. Then the radius of the flip graph on
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
is at most

\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}\right)V.

Additionally, the self-complementary ideal

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}\leq\ell_{1}/2}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}\leq\ell_{d}/2}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

achieves this bound.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{d}=2
, since
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
can be partitioned into
\frac{V}{2^{d}}
copies of
[2]^{d}
, where the center of symmetry of each copy of
[2]^{d}
is the same as the center of symmetry of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
. An example of this partition can be found in
Figure

10
.

Figure 10:
The partition of
[2]\times[4]\times[6]
into six copies of
[2]^{3}
.

Let

C=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[2]^{d}\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}=1}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}=1}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}.

be a self-complementary ideal in
[2]^{d}
, and let
I
be any self-complementary ideal in
[2]^{d}
. By
Lemma

3.0.1
, it suffices to show that

\left\lvert I\setminus C\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}\right)2^{d}.

Observe that if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
and
(b_{1},\dots,b_{d})
are elements in
I
, then there is some index
i
for which
a_{i}=b_{i}=1
, since
I
is self-complementary. This means
I\setminus C
can be interpreted as an intersecting family of subsets of
\{1,\dots,d\}
by mapping each tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
to the set
\{i\in[d]\mid a_{i}=1\}
.
By
Theorem

3.3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert I\setminus C\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{(d-3)/2}\binom{d-1}{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(2^{d-1}-\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-1)}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)2^{d},

since every tuple in
I\setminus C
has fewer than
\frac{d}{2}
1’s. Lastly, the self-complementary ideal

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}\leq\ell_{1}/2}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}\leq\ell_{d}/2}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

achieves this bound because it corresponds to the equality cases in
Theorem

3.3.2
.
∎

Lemma 3.3.4
.

Let
d
be a positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of even positive integers with product
V
. Suppose
\ell_{d}
is divisible by 4. Then the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
is at most

\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V.

Additionally, if
d
is odd, then the self-complementary ideal

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}\leq\ell_{1}/2}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}\leq\ell_{d}/2}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

achieves this bound. Similarly, if
d
is even, then the self-complementary ideal

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}\leq\ell_{1}/2}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d-1}\leq\ell_{d-1}/2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lceil\tfrac{4a_{d}}{\ell_{d}}-1\bigr\rceil>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

achieves this bound.

Proof.

If
d
is odd, the result follows from
Lemma

3.3.3
, so assume henceforth that
d
is even and
a_{d}
is divisible by 4. It suffices to prove the result for
\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{d-1}=2
and
\ell_{d}=4
, since
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
can be partitioned into
\frac{V}{2^{d+1}}
copies of
[2]^{d-1}\times[4]
, where the center of symmetry of each copy of
[2]^{d-1}\times[4]
is the same as the center of symmetry of
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
, and if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},a_{d})
lies in a copy, then so does
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},\frac{1}{2}\ell_{d}+1-a_{d})
,
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},\frac{1}{2}\ell_{d}+a_{d})
, and
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},\ell_{d}+1-a_{d})
.

into six copies of
[2]\times[2]\times[4]
.

Let

C=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[2]^{d-1}\times[4]\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}=1}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d-1}=1}+\tfrac{4-a_{d}}{2}\geq\tfrac{d}{2}\}

be a self-complementary ideal in
[2]^{d-1}\times[4]
, and let
I
be any self-complementary ideal in
[2]^{d-1}\times[4]
. It suffices to show that

\left\lvert I\setminus C\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)2^{d+1}.

Let
\varphi:[2]^{d-1}\times[4]\to[2]^{d+1}
be the map given by

\textstyle(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},a_{d})\mapsto\begin{cases}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},1,1)&\text{if }a_{d}=1\\
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},1,2)&\text{if }a_{d}=2\\
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},2,1)&\text{if }a_{d}=3\\
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1},2,2)&\text{if }a_{d}=4\\
\end{cases}.

Observe that
\varphi
maps self-complementary ideals of
[2]^{d-1}\times[4]
to self-complementary ideals of
[2]^{d+1}
. Since
\varphi
maps
C
to an element in the center of
[2]^{d+1}
, we apply
Lemma

3.3.3
to obtain

\left\lvert I\setminus C\right\rvert=\left\lvert\varphi(I)\setminus\varphi(C)\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+2}}\binom{d}{\frac{1}{2}d}\right)2^{d+1}=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)2^{d+1}.\qed

Conjecture 3.3.5
.

Let
d
be an even positive integer. Then the radius of the flip graph of
[2]^{d}
is at most

\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)2^{d}\right\rceil.

Additionally, there exists a self-complementary ideal achieving this bound containing

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[2]^{d}\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}=1}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}=1}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

as a subset.

Remark
.

The truth of a specific case (
3.4.3
) of Chvátal’s conjecture (
3.4.1
) implies the truth of this conjecture. To see why, let
\mathcal{H}
be a uniform set of subsets of
\{1,\dots,d\}
, satisfying the conditions of
3.4.3
.
Let
\varphi
be the map from elements of
[2]^{d}
to subsets of
\{1,\dots,d\}
given by

(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\mapsto\{i\in[d]\mid a_{i}=1\},

and let
C=[2]^{d}\setminus\{\varphi^{-1}(A)\mid A\in\mathcal{H}\}
. Observe that
C
is an self-complementary ideal of
[2]^{d}
, since

•

for each subset
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
, exactly one of
A
or
\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus A
is contained in
\mathcal{H}
, and

•

\varphi
maps the order structure on
[2]^{d}
to the reverse inclusion structure on subsets of
\{1,\dots,d\}
(that is,
a\leq_{[2]^{d}}b\iff\varphi(a)\supseteq\varphi(b)
), so
C
is an ideal.

Now by
Lemma

3.0.1
, it suffices to show that

\left\lvert I\setminus C\right\rvert\leq\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)2^{d}\right\rceil

for any self-complementary ideal
I\subset[2]^{d}
. Indeed,

\left\lvert I\setminus C\right\rvert=\left\lvert\varphi(I)\setminus\varphi(C)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\varphi(I)\cap\mathcal{H}\right\rvert.

Since
I
is a self-complementary ideal, every pair of sets in
\varphi(I)
must share an element. By the hypothesis of the conjecture, we have

\left\lvert\varphi(I)\cap\mathcal{H}\right\rvert\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{4}\binom{d}{\frac{1}{2}d}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}d-1}\binom{d-1}{i-1}=\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)2^{d}\right\rceil.

Finally, observe that
C
contains

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[2]^{d}\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}=1}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}=1}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

as a subset, as desired.

Corollary 3.3.6
.

Assume
3.3.5
. Let
d
be a positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of even positive integers with product
V
. Suppose none of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are divisible by 4. Then the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
is at most

\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V\right\rceil.

Additionally, there exists a self-complementary ideal achieving this bound containing

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]\mid\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}\leq\ell_{1}/2}+\dots+\mathbbm{1}_{a_{d}\leq\ell_{d}/2}>\tfrac{1}{2}d\}

as a subset.

Proof.

Let
P
denote the poset
[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]
. For each index
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, define
c_{i}=\{\tfrac{1}{4}\ell_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{4}\ell_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\}
. Define the following sequence of subsets of
P
:

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\times c_{2}\times\dots\times c_{d-1}\times c_{d}

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=\{[\ell_{1}]\setminus c_{1}\}\times c_{2}\times\dots\times c_{d-1}\times c_{d}

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=[\ell_{1}]\times\{[\ell_{2}]\setminus c_{2}\}\times\dots\times c_{d-1}\times c_{d}

\displaystyle P_{d-1}

\displaystyle=[\ell_{1}]\times[\ell_{2}]\times\dots\times\{[\ell_{d-1}]\setminus c_{d-1}\}\times c_{d}

\displaystyle P_{d}

\displaystyle=[\ell_{1}]\times[\ell_{2}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d-1}]\times\{[\ell_{d}]\setminus c_{d}\}.

Observe that
P=P_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup P_{d}
. (An example of this partition is given in
Figure

13
.)

The partition of the poset
[2]\times[6]\times[10]
.

Let
C_{0}\subset P_{0}
be an ideal satisfying
3.3.5
. Similarly, let
C_{1}\subset P_{1},\dots,C_{d}\subset P_{d}
be ideals satisfying
Lemma

3.3.4
, which exist since each of
P_{1},\dots,P_{d}
have at least one dimension divisible by 4. Let
C=C_{0}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup C_{d}
.

C
is an ideal of

because the construction given in

implies that for every maximal element
a\in C_{i}
, the set
\{b\in P\mid b\leq a\}\subseteq I
and
\{b\in P\mid b>a\}\subseteq P\setminus I
. Furthermore, it is self-complementary because
C_{0},\dots,C_{d}
are all self-complementary. Therefore, for any self-complementary ideal
I\subset P
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(C,I)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}(C_{0},I\cap C_{0})+\dots+\operatorname{dist}(C_{d},I\cap C_{d})

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)\left\lvert P_{0}\right\rvert\right\rceil+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)\left\lvert P_{i}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)\left(\left\lvert P_{0}\right\rvert+\dots+\left\lvert P_{d}\right\rvert\right)\right\rceil

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\frac{1}{2}(d-2)}\right)V\right\rceil

by
3.3.5
and
Lemma

3.3.4
, since
I\cap C_{i}
is an self-complementary ideal of
C_{i}
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,d\}
by construction.
∎

Combining all previous results gives the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3.7
.

Let
d
be a positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be a sequence of even positive integers with product
V
. Suppose that
d
is odd or that at least one
\ell_{i}
is divisible by 4. Then the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
is

\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V.

Additionally, if
3.3.5
is true and there are no assumptions on
d
or any
\ell_{i}
being divisible by 4, then the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
is

\left\lceil\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}(d-1)\right\rfloor}\right)V\right\rceil=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor(d-1)/2\right\rfloor}\right)V&\text{if $d$ is not a power of 2}\\
\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\binom{d-1}{\left\lfloor(d-1)/2\right\rfloor}\right)V+\frac{1}{2}&\text{if $d$ is a power of 2,}\end{cases}.

Proof.

The lower bound in all cases is given by
Lemma

3.3.1
. The upper bound, assuming
d
is odd, is given by
Lemma

3.3.3
. The upper bound, assuming one dimension is divisible by 4, is given by
Lemma

3.3.4
. The upper bound, assuming nothing aside from the evenness of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
, is given by
Corollary

3.3.6
, which is true because
3.4.3
implies
3.3.5
, which implies
Corollary

3.3.6
.
∎

Now, we turn our attention towards the case when at least one of
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
is odd.

Lemma 3.3.8
.

Assume
3.3.5
. Given a sequence of positive integers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
, let
r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
denote the radius of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
. Suppose
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d^{\prime}}
are odd, and
\ell_{d^{\prime}+1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are even. Then for every positive integer
k
, we have

r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d^{\prime}})\leq\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}r(\{\ell_{i}-1\mid i\in S\}\cup\{\ell_{d^{\prime}+1},\dots,\ell_{d}\}).

Proof.

Let
P
denote
[\ell_{1}]\times\cdots\times[\ell_{d}]
.
Without loss of generality, assume
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d^{\prime}}
are odd, and
\ell_{d^{\prime}+1},\dots,\ell_{d}
are even. For each index
i\in\{1,\dots,d^{\prime}\}
, let
m_{i}=\{\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{i}+1)\}
. For each subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,d^{\prime}\}
, define

P_{S}=\left(\begin{cases}m_{1}&\text{if $1\in S$}\\
[\ell_{1}]\setminus m_{1}&\text{if $1\notin S$}\end{cases}\right)\times\dots\times\left(\begin{cases}m_{d^{\prime}}&\text{if $d^{\prime}\in S$}\\
[\ell_{d^{\prime}}]\setminus m_{d^{\prime}}&\text{if $d^{\prime}\notin S$}\end{cases}\right)\times[\ell_{d^{\prime}+1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}].

Observe that

[\ell_{1}]\times\dots\times[\ell_{d}]=\bigsqcup_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,d^{\prime}\}}P_{S}.

(An example of this partition is given in
Figure

14
.)

The partition of the poset
[3]\times[5]\times[8]
.

For each poset
P_{S}
, construct an element
C_{S}
of the center according to

,
Lemma

3.3.4
, or

3.3.6
.
Let

as a subset, and dually does not contain any element of

\left\lvert I\setminus P\right\rvert=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,d^{\prime}\}}(I\cap P_{S})\setminus C_{S}\leq\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}}r(\{\ell_{i}-1\mid i\in S\}\cup\{\ell_{d^{\prime}+1},\dots,\ell_{d}\})

by

An almost-exact answer is obtainable in the
d=3
case. In fact, the following is true:

Theorem 3.3.9
.

Let
d\leq 3
be a positive integer, and let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
be positive integers. Let
r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
and
d(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
denote the radius and diameter of
G(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
. Then

r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})\right\rceil,

unless
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\equiv(2,3,3)\pmod{4}
or permutations, in which case it is possible that

r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})=\tfrac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})+1.

Proof.

To prove the upper bound, we use
Theorem

3.2.5
and
Lemma

3.3.8
and split into cases.

•

If
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}
is odd, then
r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})=d(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})=0
.

•

If
d=1
and
\ell_{1}
is even, then
r(\ell_{1})=d(\ell_{1})=0
.

•

If
d=2
and
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
are both even, then

r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=\left\lceil(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{8})\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\right\rceil.

•

If
d=2
,
\ell_{1}
is even, and
\ell_{2}=2k+1
is odd, then

r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\leq r(\ell_{1},2k)+r(\ell_{1})=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}\ell_{1}\cdot 2k\right\rceil=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{4}(\ell_{1}\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})\right\rceil=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\right\rceil.

•

If
d=3
and
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}
are all even, then

r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})=(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{8})\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}=\tfrac{1}{8}\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}=\tfrac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}).

•

If
d=3
,
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
are even, and
\ell_{3}=2k+1
is odd, then

r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\leq r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},2k)+r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=\tfrac{1}{8}\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\cdot 2k+\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\right\rceil.

•

If
d=3
,
\ell_{1}=2k_{1}+1
and
\ell_{2}=2k_{2}+1
are odd, and
\ell_{3}
is even, then

\displaystyle r(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})

\displaystyle\leq r(2k_{1},2k_{2},\ell_{3})+r(2k_{1},\ell_{3})+r(2k_{2},\ell_{3})+r(\ell_{3})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{8}2k_{1}\cdot 2k_{2}\cdot\ell_{3}+\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}\cdot 2k_{1}\ell_{3}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}\cdot 2k_{2}\ell_{3}\right\rceil

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}(2k_{1}\cdot 2k_{2}+2k_{1}+2k_{2})\ell_{3}\right\rceil+\mathbbm{1}_{\text{$k_{1}$, $k_{2}$, $\ell_{3}/2$ odd}}

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\tfrac{1}{8}(\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}-\ell_{3})\right\rceil+\mathbbm{1}_{\text{$k_{1}$, $k_{2},\ell_{3}/2$ odd}}.

To prove the lower bound, observe that
r(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})\geq\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}d(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})\right\rceil
.
∎

This result illustrates the difficulty of obtaining a “nice” formula for the radius when
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
can be any sequence of positive integers.

3.4
Relation to Chvátal’s Conjecture

This section is essentially an addendum to the previous section about radius bounds, since some results from the previous section rely on the truth of conjectures stated here.

The following conjecture, known as Chvátal’s conjecture, can be thought of as an extension of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem (
Theorem

3.3.2
) to sets of subsets closed under taking subsets. If proven, it would imply tight radius bounds for flip graphs of all products of even-length chains.

Conjecture 3.4.1

(Chvátal)
.

Let
S
be a finite set, and let
\mathcal{D}
be a set of subsets of
S
closed under taking subsets. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a subset of
\mathcal{D}
for which every pair of sets in
\mathcal{F}
share an element. Then

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert\leq\max_{s\in S}\left\lvert\{A\in\mathcal{F}\mid s\in A\}\right\rvert.

Remark
.

This conjecture seems to be open in all but a small number of cases. According to
[
FKKK18
]
,
the intractability of this conjecture stems from the inability to choose a suitable
s
in the maximum, and that current progress is limited.
For the purposes of this paper, the conjecture only needs to be resolved in the case given in
3.4.3
.

Before the next conjecture is stated, consider the following definition. Let
d
be an even positive integer, and call a set
\mathcal{H}
of subsets of
S=\{1,\dots,d\}

uniform
if

•

every subset has size
\frac{1}{2}d
,

•

for every subset
A\subset S
of size
\frac{1}{2}d
, exactly one of
A
and
S\setminus A
is in
\mathcal{H}
, and

•

for every element
s\in S
,
s
appears in at most
\left\lceil\frac{1}{4}\binom{2d}{d}\right\rceil
subsets of
\mathcal{H}
.

Observe that if
d
is not a power of 2, then
\frac{1}{4}\binom{2d}{d}
is an integer, so every element must appear exactly
\frac{1}{4}\binom{2d}{d}
times. The existence of uniform subsets is given by
[
Mey95
]
in Lemma 13.

Conjecture 3.4.2
.

Let
d
be an even positive integer. There exists a uniform set of subsets
\mathcal{H}
such that if

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{H}\cup\{A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\mid\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\tfrac{1}{2}d-1\},

then every subset
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{D}
for which every pair of sets in
\mathcal{F}
share an element satisfies

\mathcal{F}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{4}\binom{d}{\frac{1}{2}d}\right\rceil+\binom{d-1}{\tfrac{1}{2}d-2}.

Remark
.

This conjecture is true for
d\leq 6
. For
d=2
, select
\mathcal{H}=\{\{1\}\}
. For
d=4
, select
\mathcal{H}=\{\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\}\}
. For
d=6
, select

\mathcal{H}=\{\{1,2,3\},\{1,2,4\},\{1,3,6\},\{1,4,5\},\{1,5,6\},\{2,3,5\},\{2,4,6\},\{2,5,6\},\{3,4,6\},\{3,4,5\}\}.

The fact that these choices of
\mathcal{H}
work for
d=2
and
d=4
is easy to verify. For
d=6
, we present two interesting proofs for
\left\lvert F\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{4}\binom{6}{3}+\binom{5}{1}=10
. However, these proofs seem difficult to generalize to larger values of
d
, because it appears that for large
d
the structure of uniform
\mathcal{H}
is not well-behaved.

Proof 1.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\{4,5\},\{1,2,3\},\{5,6\},\{1,2,4\},\{3,6\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\{2,3\},\{1,4,5\},\{2,6\},\{3,4,5\},\{1,6\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{\{4,6\},\{2,3,5\},\{1,4\},\{2,5,6\},\{1,3\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{4}

\displaystyle=\{\{2,5\},\{1,3,6\},\{2,4\},\{1,5,6\},\{3,4\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{5}

\displaystyle=\{\{3,5\},\{2,4,6\},\{1,5\},\{3,4,6\},\{1,2\}\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{2}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{3}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{4}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{5}

so

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{D}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{D}_{1}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{D}_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{D}_{3}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{D}_{4}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{D}_{5}\right\rvert\leq 2+2+2+2+2=10.\qed

Proof 2.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a subset of
\mathcal{D}
for which every pair of sets in
\mathcal{F}
share an element. For every subset
A\subseteq\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
, define

\mathbbm{1}_{A}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $A\in\mathcal{F}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Observe that

\mathbbm{1}_{123}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{45}+\mathbbm{1}_{46}+\mathbbm{1}_{56})\leq 1+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{45}\mathbbm{1}_{46}\mathbbm{1}_{56},

and analogous symmetric statements hold.
Summing this inequality over all sets in
\mathcal{H}
gives

\sum_{A\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\{1,\dots,6\}\\
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=2\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\leq 10+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,c\in\{1,\dots,6\}\\
\{1,\dots,6\}\setminus\{a,b,c\}\in\mathcal{H}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{ab}\mathbbm{1}_{ac}\mathbbm{1}_{bc}\leq 10+\tfrac{1}{2}.

Since the left hand side is an integer, it follows that

\sum_{A\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\{1,\dots,6\}\\
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=2\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\leq 10.\qed

Conjecture 3.4.3
.

Let
d
be an even positive integer. There exists a uniform subset
\mathcal{H}
such that if

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{H}\cup\{A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\mid\left\lvert A\right\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}d-1\},

then every subset
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{D}
for which every pair of sets in
\mathcal{F}
share an element satisfies

\mathcal{F}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{4}\binom{d}{\frac{1}{2}d}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}d-1}\binom{d-1}{i-1}.

Remark
.

This conjecture follows from
3.4.2
and
Theorem

3.3.2
after partitioning

\mathcal{D}=\left(\mathcal{H}\cup\{A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\mid\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\tfrac{1}{2}d-1\}\right)\cup\smash{\bigcup}_{i\in\{0,\dots,\frac{1}{2}d-2\}}\{A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\mid\left\lvert A\right\rvert=i\}.

This conjecture is also a specific case of Chvátal’s conjecture (
3.4.1
).

4
Cyclically Symmetric Self-Complementary Ideals

The flip graph on self-complementary ideals of a poset can be modified to accommodate additional symmetries the ideals may have. In particular, there are flip graphs whose vertices are the cyclically symmetric self-complementary (CSSC) ideals of a poset
P=[\ell]\times[\ell]\times[\ell]=[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
for an even positive integer
\ell=2r
.

Given two distinct CSSC ideals
I,J\subset P
,
I
and
J
differ by a
flip
if there exists a point
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in I
such that the replacements

\displaystyle(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle\mapsto(2r+1-a_{1},2r+1-a_{2},2r+1-a_{3})

\displaystyle(a_{2},a_{3},a_{1})

\displaystyle\mapsto(2r+1-a_{2},2r+1-a_{3},2r+1-a_{1})

\displaystyle(a_{3},a_{1},a_{2})

\displaystyle\mapsto(2r+1-a_{3},2r+1-a_{1},2r+1-a_{2})

yield
J
. The
flip graph on cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals of
P
is an unweighted graph whose edges are constructed between CSSC ideals of
P
that differ by a flip.
Figure

15
shows the flip graph structure for the specific case
P=[4]\times[4]\times[4]
.

Figure 15:
The flip graph on cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[4]\times[4]\times[4]
.

Observe that at least two of
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
must be distinct for the above flip to yield another ideal, because

\{(1,1,1),(2,2,2),\dots,(r,r,r)\}\subset I

for any self-complementary ideal
I\subset P
. Thus the points
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
,
(a_{2},a_{3},a_{1})
, and
(a_{3},a_{1},a_{2})
above are distinct, and we have

\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert J\setminus I\right\rvert=3

for any two CSSC ideals that differ by a flip. Observe that this condition is also sufficient: if CSSC ideals
I
and
J
satisfy
\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert J\setminus I\right\rvert=3
and
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in I\setminus J
, then flipping
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
,
(a_{2},a_{3},a_{1})
, and
(a_{3},a_{1},a_{2})
in
I
yields the ideal
J
. This readily implies the following lemma:

Lemma 4.0.1
.

Let
I
and
J
be cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
. Then the distance from
I
to
J
in the flip graph is
\frac{1}{3}\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert
.

Proof.

This is essentially the same as the proof of
Lemma

3.0.1
.
∎

Given a positive integer
r
, let
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
denote the flip graph on cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
. Additionally, for each tuple
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\{0,1\}^{3}
define

O_{t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]\mid t_{1}=\mathbbm{1}_{a_{1}>r},t_{2}=\mathbbm{1}_{a_{2}>r},t_{3}=\mathbbm{1}_{a_{3}>r}\}.

For ideals

let

denote their symmetric difference, equal to

. Observe that if

and

are CSSC ideals that differ by a flip, then

\left\lvert I\vartriangle J\right\rvert=6,

and for
t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}\in\{001,010,100,011,101,110\}

\left\lvert(I\vartriangle J)\cap O_{t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}}\right\rvert=1.

4.1
Vertex Count

The number of vertices in
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
, also equal to the number of cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
, was determined by Kuperberg in 1994.

Theorem 4.1.1
.

Let
r
be a positive integer. The number of vertices in
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
is

\left(\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}{\frac{(3j+1)!}{(r+j)!}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

This is given in
[
Kup94
]
.
∎

Corollary 4.1.2
.

As
r
tends to infinity, we have

v(G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r))\sim Cr^{-5/18}\left(\frac{27}{16}\right)^{r^{2}}

for some real number
C
. In particular,
\log v(G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r))\sim\log(27/16)r^{2}\approx 0.523r^{2}
.

Proof.

In fact,

C=\left(\frac{e^{\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}2^{1/4}\Gamma(2/3)}{3^{1/12}\Gamma(1/3)}\right)^{2/3}\approx 0.600.

This can be obtained using asymptotic formulas for the superfactorial numbers and triple factorial numbers.
∎

4.2

This section will compute the exact value of the diameter of
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
for all positive integers

.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

Let

be a positive integer. The diameter of
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
is at least
\frac{1}{3}(r-1)r(r+1)

Proof.

Let

denote the CSSC ideal

, and let

denote the CSSC ideal constructed as a union of three “pyramids” consisting of

squares as layers.
Figure

depicts these ideals in the case

By

4.0.1
, the distance between

and

in the flip graph is

\tfrac{1}{3}\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert=\left\lvert O_{011}\cap(I\setminus J)\right\rvert=1\cdot 2+2\cdot 3+\dots+(r-1)\cdot r=\tfrac{1}{3}(r-1)r(r+1).

Therefore, the diameter of the flip graph is at least
\frac{1}{3}(r-1)r(r+1)
.
∎

and

for the case

Theorem 4.2.2
.

Let

be a positive integer. The diameter of

\frac{1}{3}(r-1)r(r+1)

4.3
Radius

This section will compute the exact value of the radius of
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
for all positive integers
r
. We first prove two general lemmas about CSSC ideals.

Lemma 4.3.1
.

All cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals
I\subset[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
must contain the points
(i,i,2r+1-i)
for each integer
1\leq i\leq r
.

Proof.

If
(i,i,2r+1-i)\notin I
, then
(2r+1-i,2r+1-i,i)\in I
from
I
being self-complementary. Since
I
is cyclically symmetric, we also have
(i,2r+1-i,2r+1-i)\in I
, which is a contradiction because
2r+1-i>i
.
∎

Lemma 4.3.2
.

All cyclically symmetric self-complementary ideals
I\subset[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
must contain at least one of
(1,r,2r)
or
(r,1,2r)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(1,r,2r)\notin I
, so
(2r,r+1,1)\in I
, and thus
(r+1,1,2r)\in I
. Then
I
contains
(r,1,2r)
, as it is covered by
(r+1,1,2r)
.
The other case is similar.
∎

Next, we prove a lemma about ideals in two dimensions that will be used when inducting with 3-dimensional ideals.

Lemma 4.3.3
.

Let
r\geq 2
be a positive integer, and let
k\leq r-2
be a nonnegative integer.
Let
C
be the self-complementary ideal in
[r-k-1]\times[r+k]
given by
\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in[r-k-1]\times[r+k]\mid a_{1}+a_{2}\leq r\}
. (An example of
C
is given in
Figure

17
.)
Then for any ideal
I\subseteq[r-k-1]\times[r+k]
, not necessarily self-complementary, we have

\left\lvert C\vartriangle I\right\rvert\leq(k+1)+(k+2)+\cdots+(r-1)=\tfrac{1}{2}(r+k)(r-k-1).

Furthermore, equality holds only when
I
is the empty ideal or the entire poset.

Figure 17:
An example of
C
for the case
r=6
,
k=2
.

Proof.

For each integer
1\leq i\leq r-k-1
, define
p_{i}=\left\lvert\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in I\mid a_{1}=i\}\right\rvert
, so
0\leq p_{r-k-1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{1}\leq r+k
.
Since
C
contains
r-i
points with
a_{1}=i
for each integer
1\leq i\leq r-k-1
, we wish to upper-bound

\left\lvert C\vartriangle I\right\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{r-k-1}|p_{i}-(r-i)|.

We will pair up column
i
with column
r-k-i
and show that

|p_{i}-(r-i)|+|p_{r-k-i}-(k+i)|\leq r+k.

For the following, assume without loss of generality that
i\leq r-k-i
, so
0\leq p_{r-k-i}\leq p_{i}\leq r+k
.

For a fixed
p_{i}
, the maximum value of
|p_{i}-(r-i)|+|p_{r-k-i}-(k+i)|
obtained by varying
p_{r-k-i}
must occur at an extreme: when
p_{r-k-i}=p_{i}
or
p_{r-k-i}=0
. Similarly for a fixed
p_{r-k-i}
, the maximum value obtained by varying
p_{i}
occurs when
p_{i}=p_{r-k-i}
or when
p_{i}=r+k
. The maximum value now reduces to a finite case check:

•

Case 1:

p_{i}\neq p_{r-k-i}
.

Then we must have
p_{i}=r+k
and
p_{r-k-i}=0
, and

|(r+k)-(r-i)|+|0-(k+i)|=2k+2i\leq r+k,

by the assumption that
i\leq r-k-i
.

•

Case 2:

p_{i}=p_{r-k-i}
.

Setting
x=p_{i}=p_{r-k-i}
, the maximum value of
|x-(r-i)|+|x-(k+i)|
must occur at an endpoint of
x=0
or
x=r+k
, both of which give
r+k
.

Summing the inequalities
|p_{i}-(r-i)|+|p_{r-k-i}-(k+i)|\leq r+k
for
i=1
to
r-k-1
and dividing by 2 yields the desired conclusion of

\left\lvert C\vartriangle I\right\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(r+k)(r-k-1).

In order for equality to hold, we need
|p_{i}-(r-i)|+|p_{r-k-i}-(k+i)|=r+k
for all
1\leq i\leq\frac{r-k}{2}
. Note that equality cannot actually hold in Case 1 above, because that would require
i=r-k-i
, but then
p_{i}=p_{r-k-i}
, so Case 2 would apply instead. Thus, to achieve equality, we must apply Case 2 and have
p_{i}=p_{r-k-i}=0
or
p_{i}=p_{r-k-i}=r+k
for each integer
1\leq i\leq\frac{r-k}{2}
.

In particular, we have that
p_{1}=p_{r-k-1}=0
or
p_{1}=p_{r-k-1}=r+k
. This determines that either
p_{i}=0
for all
i
, or
p_{i}=r+k
for all
i
, giving that
I
is either the empty ideal or the entire poset.
∎

5
Totally Symmetric Self-Complementary Ideals

The definition of a flip graph can be modified to accommodate totally symmetric self-complementary (TSSC) ideals of a poset
P=[\ell]\times[\ell]\times[\ell]=[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
for an even positive integer
\ell=2r
.

Given two distinct TSSC ideals
I,J\subset P
,
I
and
J
differ by a
flip
if there exists a point
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in I
such that the replacements

(a_{\sigma(1)},a_{\sigma(2)},a_{\sigma(3)})\mapsto(2r+1-a_{\sigma(1)},2r+1-a_{\sigma(2)},2r+1-a_{\sigma(3)})

over all
\sigma\in S_{3}
yield
J
. The
flip graph on totally symmetric self-complementary ideals of
P
is a weighted graph whose edges are constructed between TSSC ideals of
P
that differ by a flip. If
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are distinct, connect
I
and
J
with an edge of weight one. If exactly two of
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are equal, connect
I
and
J
with an edge of weight two. Note that, like the CSSC ideal case, it is not possible for
a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}
.

Figure

26
shows the flip graph structure for the specific case
P=[6]\times[6]\times[6]
. Edges of weight one are denoted with a thinner line segment, and edges of weight two are denoted with a thicker line segment.

Figure 26:
The flip graph on totally symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[6]\times[6]\times[6]
.

and

\frac{1}{3}\left\lvert I\setminus J\right\rvert
.

.

, let

The number of vertices in
G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
, or the number of totally symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
, was determined by Andrews in 1994.

Theorem 5.1.1
.

Let
r
be a positive integer. The number of vertices in
G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
is

\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}{\frac{(3j+1)!}{(r+j)!}}.

Proof.

This is given in
[
And94
]
.
∎

Corollary 5.1.2
.

As
r
tends to infinity,

v(G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r))\sim Cr^{-5/36}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}\right)^{r^{2}}

for some real number
C
. In particular,
\log v(G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r))\sim\frac{1}{2}\log(27/16)r^{2}\approx 0.262r^{2}
.

Proof.

One can take

C=\left(\frac{e^{\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}2^{1/4}\Gamma(2/3)}{3^{1/12}\Gamma(1/3)}\right)^{1/3}\approx 0.775.

This is the square root of the result in
Corollary

4.1.2
because the vertex count of
G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
is the square root of the vertex count of
G_{C_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
.
∎

5.2
Diameter

The diameter of the
G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
is known exactly. To determine this exact value, first consider the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2.1
.

All totally symmetric self-complementary ideals
I\subset[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
must contain the points
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
that satisfy

•

2a_{1}\leq 2r+1
and
a_{2}+a_{3}\leq 2r+1
, or

•

2a_{2}\leq 2r+1
and
a_{3}+a_{1}\leq 2r+1
, or

•

2a_{3}\leq 2r+1
and
a_{1}+a_{2}\leq 2r+1
.

Proof.

If
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\notin I
, then
(2r+1-a_{1},2r+1-a_{2},2r+1-a_{3})\in I
, as are
(2r+1-a_{1},2r+1-a_{3},2r+1-a_{2})
,
(2r+1-a_{3},2r+1-a_{2},2r+1-a_{1})
, and
(2r+1-a_{2},2r+1-a_{1},2r+1-a_{3})
. If any of the above inequalities hold, then one of these three points covers
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
, a contradiction.
∎

r=5
in

This also means that the TSSC ideal with the fewest points in
O_{001}
contains precisely those points only and the remaining octants filled in accordingly. On the other hand, the TSSC ideal with the most points in
O_{001}
is the one that contains the entirety of
O_{001}
. The case of

In general for the
[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
case, the symmetric difference between these two TSSC ideals in
O_{001}
, which is also their distance in the flip graph, is
1^{2}+2^{2}+\cdots+(r-1)^{2}=\frac{1}{6}(r-1)r(2r-1)
.

Theorem 5.2.2
.

The diameter of
G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)
is
\frac{1}{6}(r-1)r(2r-1)
.

Proof.

It remains to show that for any two TSSC ideals
I
and
J
of
[2r]\times[2r]\times[2r]
, we have that
\frac{1}{6}\left\lvert I\vartriangle J\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{6}(r-1)r(2r-1)
. Since
\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert\geq 4r^{3}-3\cdot\frac{1}{6}(r-1)r(2r-1)
by
Lemma

5.2.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lvert I\vartriangle J\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert I\right\rvert+\left\lvert J\right\rvert-2\left\lvert I\cap J\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 4r^{3}+4r^{3}-2\left(4r^{3}-3\cdot\tfrac{1}{6}(r-1)r(2r-1)\right)

\displaystyle=(r-1)r(2r-1),

as desired.

Observe that equality holds when
|I\cap J|=4r^{3}-3\cdot\frac{1}{6}(r-1)r(2r-1)
, i.e. when the intersection of
I
and
J
is the ideal as depicted in
Lemma

5.2.1
. This can only occur when one ideal contains no points in
O_{110}
and the other ideal contains the minimum possible number of points in
O_{001}
, which are the two ideals depicted in
Figure

28
.
∎

5.3
Radius

Only a lower bound for the radius is known, which follows directly from the diameter bound (
Theorem

5.2.2
).

.

The radius of
G_{S_{3}}(2r,2r,2r)

\left\lceil\frac{1}{12}(r-1)r(2r-1)\right\rceil

Future Directions

Currently, there are three main open conjectures in this paper:

•

3.1.5
conjectures an asymptotic bound on the number of self-complementary ideals of
[\ell]^{n}
for a fixed even positive integer
\ell
, as
n
tends to infinity.

•

3.4.3
is a specific case of Chvátal’s conjecture. If true, it would imply an exact answer for the radius of the flip graph on self-complementary ideals of chain products.

•

5.3.1
conjectures that the radius of the flip graph on totally symmetric self-complementary ideals of
[\ell]^{3}
is given by the ceiling of half of the diameter.

In addition to the three conjectures above, there are also a number of other questions that can be asked about flip graph structures on self-dual posets. These include, but are not limited to, the following questions:

•

Is it possible to improve on
Theorem

3.1.4
to obtain more precise vertex count asymptotics for flip graphs on self-complementary ideals?

•

In this paper, the only graph properties studied were vertex count, diameter, and radius. Other properties of interest could be:

–

edge count and average degree

–

maximal degree, which is related to Sperner’s theorem

–

characterization of the center, and bounds on its size

–

characterization of the perimeter, and bounds on its size.
