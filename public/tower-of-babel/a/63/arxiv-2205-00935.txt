The Ruelle Invariant And Convexity In Higher Dimensions
J. Chaidez, O. Edtmair

Abstract
. We construct the Ruelle invariant of a volume preserving flow and a symplectic cocycle in any dimension and prove several properties. In the special case of the linearized Reeb flow on the boundary of a convex domain X X in R 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} , we prove that the Ruelle invariant Ru ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{Ru}(X) , the period of the systole c ⁡ ( X ) c(X) and the volume vol ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{vol}\left({X}\right) satisfy Ru ⁡ ( X ) ⋅ c ⁡ ( X ) ≤ C ⁡ ( n ) ⋅ vol ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{Ru}(X)\cdot c(X)\leq C(n)\cdot\operatorname{vol}\left({X}\right) Here C ⁡ ( n ) > 0 C(n)>0 is an explicit constant dependent on n n . As an application, we construct dynamically convex contact forms on S 2 ​ n − 1 S^{2n-1} that are not convex, disproving the equivalence of convexity and dynamical convexity in every dimension.



1.
Introduction

In
[
27
]
, Ruelle introduced his eponymous
Ruelle invariant

\operatorname{Ru}(Y,\phi)
of a flow
\phi:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y
on a
3
-manifold
Y
preserving a smooth measure
\mu
. This invariant is the integral of a function
\operatorname{ru}(\phi)
that (morally speaking) measures the linking of nearby trajectories of
\phi
in
Y
.

Figure 1.
The function
\operatorname{ru}(\phi)
at
y
measures the time-averaged linking of the length
T
trajectory
\gamma
starting at
y
and a nearby trajectory
\gamma^{\prime}
, as
T
goes to
\infty
.

Since its introduction, the Ruelle invariant has appeared in low-dimensional dynamics (cf. Gambaudo-Ghys
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
), bifurcation theory (cf. Parlitz
[
26
]
) and Sturm-Liouville theory (cf. Schulz-Baldes
[
28
,
29
]
). More recently, the Ruelle invariant has been applied very fruitfully to the study of 3-dimensional Reeb dynamics and 4-dimensional symplectic geometry
[
20
,
5
,
8
]
.

In our previous work
[
5
]
, we applied the Ruelle invariant to find the first examples of contact forms on the
3
-sphere that are dynamically convex in the sense of Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder
[
19
]
but not symplectically convex (see Definition
1.10
). This was a longstanding unsolved problem, and remains particularly impervious to more conventional modern methods in symplectic geometry such as Floer theory.

In this paper, we initiate the study of the Ruelle invariant in higher dimensional Reeb dynamics. Specifically, we construct a substantial generalization of the Ruelle invariant in
[
27
]
to symplectic cocycles of flows in any dimension. This generalization is related to previous ones such as the asymptotic Maslov index
[
7
]
. We then formulate and prove higher dimensional versions of results in
[
5
]
,
[
20
]
and
[
8
]
. In particular, we show that dynamical convexity and symplectic convexity are inequivalent in all dimensions by constructing toric counter-examples, generalizing a construction of Dardennes-Gutt-Zhang
[
8
]
from dimension four.

1.1.
Ruelle Invariant Of A Symplectic Cocycle

Let us begin by summarizing our construction of the Ruelle invariant and discussing its important formal properties.

Let
Y
be a compact manifold equipped with an autonomous flow
\phi:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y
and let
E\to Y
be a symplectic vector bundle. Also let
\rho:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y
denote the obvious projection.

Definition 1.1
.

A
symplectic cocycle

\Phi
on
E
for the flow
(Y,\phi)
is a symplectic bundle map

(1.1)

\Phi:\rho^{*}E\to\phi^{*}E\qquad\text{satisfying}\qquad\Phi(s+t,x)=\Phi(t,\phi(s,x))\Phi(s,x)

Fix a
\phi
-invariant Borel measure on
Y
, a symplectic cocycle
(E,\Phi)
with vanishing first Chern class
c_{1}(E)
and a homotopy class of trivialization
\tau:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}
of the determinant line bundle
\Lambda E
. Here we consider the complex determinant line bundle with respect to an auxiliary choice of compatible complex structure on
E
(see §
3.1
).

Theorem 1.2
.

There is a well-defined Ruelle density and Ruelle invariant, denoted respectively by

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau)\in L^{1}(Y,\mu)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\mu):=\int_{Y}\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau)\cdot\mu

Moreover, the Ruelle density and invariant satisfy the following properties.

(a)

(Covariance) If
\Psi:(E,\Phi)\to(E^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime})
is a symplectic cocycle isomorphism that maps
\tau
to
\tau^{\prime}
, then

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau)=\operatorname{ru}(\Phi^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\qquad\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\mu)=\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\mu)

(b)

(Direct Sum) If
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\oplus\Phi_{2}
is a direct sum of symplectic cocycles and
\tau=\tau_{1}\otimes\tau_{2}
, then

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi_{1}\oplus\Phi_{2},\tau_{1}\otimes\tau_{2})=\operatorname{ru}(\Phi_{1},\tau_{1})+\operatorname{ru}(\Phi_{2},\tau_{2})

(c)

(Linearity) If
a\mu+b\nu
is a positive combination of
\phi
-invariant Borel measures
\mu
and
\nu
, then

\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,a\mu+b\nu)=a\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\mu)+b\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\nu)

(d)

(Trivial Bundle) If
\Phi
is a symplectic cocycle on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the tautological trivialization
\tau_{\operatorname{std}}
, then

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau_{\operatorname{std}})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{q\circ\widetilde{\Phi}_{T}}{T}\qquad\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau_{\operatorname{std}})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{Y}q\circ\widetilde{\Phi}_{T}\cdot\mu

Here
q
is any rotation quasimorphism (see §
2.2
) and
\widetilde{\Phi}:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
is the lift of
\Phi
(regarded as a map
\mathbb{R}\times Y\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
) to the universal cover
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
.

The data needed to apply Theorem
1.2
arises in a fairly natural way for the dynamical systems that arise in symplectic geometry. Here are the main examples of interest.

Example 1.3

(Symplectic Flows)
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold with a compact symplectic submanifold
\Sigma\subset X
and let
V
be a complete symplectic vector field tangent to
\Sigma
.

The differential of the symplectic flow
\Phi
generated by
V
induces a symplectic cocycle

T\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times TX\to\Phi^{*}TX

The flow
\Phi
preserves
\Sigma
since
V
is tangent to
\Sigma
. Moreover,
\Sigma
is equipped with the natural invariant measure
\omega^{m}|_{\Sigma}
where
\operatorname{dim}(\Sigma)=2m
. Given a homotopy class of trivialization
\tau:\Lambda(TX)\simeq\mathbb{C}
along
\Sigma
, we thus acquire a Ruelle density and invariant via Theorem
1.2
.

\operatorname{ru}(T\Phi|_{\Sigma},\tau)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(T\Phi|_{\Sigma},\tau,\omega^{m}|_{\Sigma})

More generally, we only need to assume that the flow
\Phi
is defined near
\Sigma
and that
\Sigma
is a (not necessarily symplectic) submanifold equipped with an invariant measure
\mu
. This special case is discussed in
[
7
]
.

Example 1.4

(Hamiltonian Flows)
.

Let
X
be a compact symplectic manifold with boundary and let
H:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a Hamiltonian that is locally constant on
\partial X
. Assume that
c_{1}(X)=0
.

Then as a special case of Example
1.3
, we get a Ruelle invariant associated to
X
, the flow
\Phi^{H}
of
H
and a chosen homotopy class of trivialization
\tau
. We denote this by

\operatorname{Ru}(X,H,\tau)\qquad\text{or simply }\quad\operatorname{Ru}(X,H)\quad\text{if}\quad H^{1}(X;\mathbb{Z})=0

Example 1.5

(Reeb Flows)
.

Recall that a contact
(2n-1)
-manifold
(Y,\xi)
is a manifold equipped with a
(2n-2)
-plane field
\xi\subset TY
, called the contact structure, that is the kernel of a contact form
\alpha
. A contact form on
Y
is a
1
-form that satisfies

\ker(d\alpha)\subset TY\text{ is rank 1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha|_{\ker(d\alpha)}>0

Every contact form comes equipped with a natural Reeb vector field
R
, defined by

\alpha(R)=1\qquad\iota_{R}d\alpha=0

The flow
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y
of the Reeb vector field is simply called the Reeb flow of
Y
. Note that
\Phi
preserves
\alpha
and the natural volume form
\alpha\wedge d\alpha^{n-1}
. The contact structure
\xi
of
Y
is a symplectic vector bundle with symplectic form
d\alpha|_{\xi}
. Thus

(\xi,T\Phi|_{\xi})

has the structure of a symplectic cocycle. If
\xi
has vanishing first Chern class, we can choose a homotopy class of trivialization
\tau:\Lambda\xi\simeq\mathbb{C}
to acquire a Ruelle invariant, denoted in this case by

\operatorname{Ru}(Y,\alpha,\tau)\qquad\text{or simply}\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(Y,\alpha)\quad\text{if}\quad H^{1}(Y;\mathbb{Z})=0

1.2.
Ruelle Invariant Of Liouville Domains

In the case of Liouville domains, the Ruelle invariant yields a new symplectomorphism invariant (under some mild topological hypotheses).

Recall that a
Liouville domain

(X,\lambda)
is a compact symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
with a vector field
Z
and a symplectically dual
1
-form
\lambda=\iota_{Z}\omega
such that

\omega=d\lambda\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z\text{ points outward along }\partial X

The 1-form
\lambda
and the vector field
Z
are called the
Liouville form
and
Liouville vector field
of
X
. The
skeleton

\operatorname{Skel}(X)
of a Liouville domain
(X,\lambda)
is the set given by

\operatorname{Skel}(X)=\bigcap_{t<0}\Phi^{Z}_{t}(X)\quad\text{where}\qquad\Phi^{Z}\text{ is the flow generated by }Z

The boundary
\partial X
of a Liouville domain
X
is a contact manifold with contact form
\lambda|_{\partial X}
. Moreover,
X
admits a canonical Hamiltonian on the complement of the skeleton

H_{X}:X\setminus\operatorname{Skel}(X)\to(0,1]\qquad\text{characterized by}\qquad ZH_{X}=H_{X}\text{ and }H_{X}^{-1}(1)=\partial X

The level sets of
H_{X}
are canonically contactomorphic to
\partial X
and the Hamiltonian vector field of
H_{X}
agrees with the Reeb vector field of
\lambda|_{\partial X}
on each level. Note that
H_{X}
extends continuously to the skeleton as
H_{X}|_{\operatorname{Skel}(X)}=0
, but in general this extension is not differentiable.

Figure 2.
Some pictures of 2-dimensional Liouville domains. The skeleton is depicted in red and the Liouville vector field in blue.

Example 1.6

(Star-Shaped Domains)
.

A
star-shaped
domain
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
with smooth boundary
Y
is a domain such that

0\in\operatorname{int}(X)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{the radial vector field }\partial_{r}\text{ is transverse to }Y

A star-shaped domain is naturally a Liouville domain, via restriction of the standard symplectic form and Liouville vector field on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In standard coordinates, these are given by

\omega=\sum_{j=1}^{n}dx_{j}\wedge dy_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}x_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}+y_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}\qquad\text{where}\qquad z_{j}=x_{j}+i\cdot y_{j}

As with any Liouville domain, the restriction
\lambda|_{Y}
of the Liouville form
\lambda=\iota_{Z}\omega
is a contact form on the boundary
Y
. The Reeb vector field
R
on
Y
is given

R=\frac{J\nu}{\langle Z,\nu\rangle}\qquad\text{where }\nu\text{ is the normal vector field and }J\text{ is multiplication by }i

Strictly speaking, the Ruelle invariant of
H_{X}
is not well-defined since
H_{X}
is only defined away from
\operatorname{Skel}(X)
. However we can show that
\operatorname{ru}(X,H_{X})
is invariant under
Z
. Thus we can take

\operatorname{Ru}(X,H_{X}):=\lim_{E\to 0}\;\operatorname{Ru}(X_{E},H_{X})\qquad\text{where}\qquad X_{E}=H_{X}^{-1}[E,1]

By applying a standard argument using Grey stability (cf.
[
5
, Lemma 3.5]
or
[
8
, §3]
) along with Stokes theorem, one may prove the following result.

Lemma 1.7
.

Let
(X,\lambda)
be a Liouville domain with
H^{1}(X;\mathbb{Z})=H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})=0
. Then

\operatorname{Ru}(X,H_{X})=\operatorname{Ru}(\partial X,\lambda|_{\partial X})

Furthermore,
\operatorname{Ru}(X,H_{X})=\operatorname{Ru}(W,H_{W})
if
X
and
W
are symplectomorphic.

Thus the Ruelle invariant
\operatorname{Ru}(X)=\operatorname{Ru}(X,H_{X})
is a symplectic invariant of Liouville domains.

Example 1.8

(Toric Domains)
.

In the case of a toric domain, we can prove an explicit formula for the Ruelle invariant that generalizes the formulas appearing in
[
20
,
8
]
.

Let
X_{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth, star-shaped toric domain with moment region
\Omega\subset[0,\infty)^{n}
. Let
f_{\Omega}:[0,\infty)^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be the unique smooth function such that

f^{-1}_{\Omega}[0,1]=\Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i}x_{i}\cdot\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}=f_{\Omega}

Proposition 1.9
.

(Proposition
5.6
) The Ruelle invariant of
X_{\Omega}
is given by the following formula.

\operatorname{Ru}(X_{\Omega})=\sum_{i}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}\cdot\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

We will provide a review of toric domains and their Reeb dynamics in §
5
.

1.3.
Symplectic Convexity

Our main application of the Ruelle invariant is to distinguish symplectically convex domains from dynamically convex domains. Let us recall the former concept.

Definition 1.10
.

A star-shaped domain
X
is
symplectically convex
if it is symplectomorphic to a convex star-shaped domain
X^{\prime}
.

Convex domains and their contact boundaries have many special properties that distinguish them from ordinary star-shaped domains and arbitrary contact forms on the sphere, particularly in dimension four (cf.
[
19
,
31
,
18
]
).

In
[
5
]
, we demonstrated a new special property of the Ruelle invariant of convex star-shaped domains. To be precise, let
c(X)
denote the period of the systole of
\partial X
, i.e.

(1.2)

c(X):=\operatorname{min}\{T\;:\;T\text{ is the period of a closed Reeb orbit on }\partial X\}

Theorem 1.11
.

[
5
]
There are constants
C,c>0
such that, for any convex star-shaped domain
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}

c\cdot\operatorname{vol}(X)\leq\operatorname{Ru}(X)\cdot c(X)\leq C\cdot\operatorname{vol}(X)

Our second main result in this paper is the generalization of the upper bound in Theorem
1.11
.

Theorem 1.12
.

There is a constant
C(n)>0
such that any convex star-shaped domain
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfies

\operatorname{Ru}(X)\cdot c(X)\leq C(n)\cdot\operatorname{vol}(X)

Let us briefly sketch the proof, which is strategically similar to the proof of Theorem
1.11
in
[
5
]
.

Proof Sketch.
We start by observing that the tangent cocycle
T\Phi
induced by the Hamiltonian flow
\Phi
of
H_{X}
is generated by the Hessian of
H_{X}
, in the sense that

\frac{d}{dt}(T\Phi)(t,x)=J\circ A(\Phi(t,x))\circ T\Phi(t,x)\qquad\text{where}\qquad A(x)=\nabla^{2}H_{X}(t,x)

General properties of the rotation quasimorphism (see §
2.2
) imply a trace estimate for the Ruelle invariant (see Proposition
3.13
(e)) when the generator
A
is positive semi-definite, which is the case if
X
is convex. Thus we get

\operatorname{Ru}(X)\leq\frac{8n^{2}}{\pi}\cdot S(H_{X})\qquad\text{where}\qquad S(H_{X}):=\int_{X}\operatorname{tr}(A)\cdot\omega^{n}=\int_{X}\Delta H_{X}\cdot\omega^{n}

By analyzing the functional
S
, we prove (Proposition
4.10
) that if
X
and
W
are sandwiched, in the sense that
W\subset X\subset c\cdot W
for some constant
c\geq 1
, then

S(H_{X})\leq C(c,n)\cdot S(H_{W})\qquad\text{where $C(c,n)$ depends only on $n$ and $c$}

On the other hand, by the John ellipsoid theorem, we can find a standard symplectic ellipsoid
E
such that
E\subset X\subset 2n\cdot E
(after applying a symplectomorphism to
X
). For this ellipsoid, we have

c(X)\leq c(2n\cdot E)\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(X)\leq C^{\prime}(n)\cdot S(H_{2n\cdot E})\qquad\operatorname{vol}(2n\cdot E)\leq(2n)^{2n}\cdot\operatorname{vol}(X)

This reduces the proof to the statement that
S(H_{E})\cdot c(E)\leq C^{\prime\prime}(n)\cdot\operatorname{vol}(E)
for any standard ellipsoid
E
and a constant
C^{\prime\prime}(n)
depending on
n
. This is a simple calculation (Lemma
4.14
). ∎

We will carry our a detailed version of this proof (keeping track of constants) in §
4.4
.

Remark 1.13
.

Our proofs of Theorem
1.12
above and Theorem
1.11
in
[
5
]
are very similar.

The key difference is our use of the Laplacian integral in place of the total mean curvature of the contact boundary, which plays an almost identical role in
[
5
]
. A higher dimensional bound by some extrinsic curvature integral (cf.
[
5
, Lemma 3.11]
) would, likely, further simplify and improve the proof of Theorem
1.12
. At this time, we do not have a construction of the Ruelle invariant in higher dimensions that makes such a bound manifest.

Relatedly, the lower bound in Theorem
1.11
is likely true in higher dimensions. However, at this time, it is not clear how to adapt our methods to prove it. The proof in
[
5
]
relies on some delicate geometric reasoning specific to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Remark 1.14
.

The constant
C(n)
in Theorem
1.12
can be given explicitly as

C(n):=2^{2n+5}\cdot n^{2n+3}\cdot\operatorname{exp}(8n^{4})

We believe that this constant is far from optimal. In fact, in dimension 4, it is an inferior constant to the one produced in the upper bound of Theorem
1.11
in
[
5
]
.

1.4.
Dynamical Convexity

Symplectic convexity is a mysterious and fundamentally extrisic condition that nonetheless plays a fundamental role in the symplectic geometry of star-shaped domains. One is thus drawn naturally to the following problem.

Problem 1.15
.

Give a characterization of symplectic convexity in terms of symplectomorphism invariant properties, i.e. without referencing an embedding to
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

A prominent candidate criterion to resolve Problem
1.15
was introduced by Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder in their groundbreaking paper
[
19
]
. This characterization uses the lower-semicontinuous extension
\operatorname{LCZ}
(see §
2.3
) of the Conley-Zehnder index
\operatorname{CZ}
, which can be viewed as a sort of Floer-theoretic Morse index of a closed Reeb orbit.

Definition 1.16
.

A contact form on
\alpha
on
S^{2n-1}
is
dynamically convex
if

\operatorname{LCZ}(\gamma)\geq n+1\qquad\text{for every closed Reeb orbit }\gamma\text{ of }\alpha

Likewise, a star-shaped domain
(X,\lambda)
is dynamically convex if
(\partial X,\lambda|_{\partial X})
is.

Since
[
19
]
, dynamical convexity has been used as a key hypothesis for many results in symplectic geometry (cf.
[
32
,
33
,
2
,
3
,
14
,
10
,
21
]
). It is simple to check that every strictly positively curved convex domain is dynamically convex, but the converse has been open for more than 20 years.

Question 1.17
.

Is every dynamically convex contact form on
S^{2n-1}
also convex?

In dimension four, we resolved this problem in
[
5
]
by constructing examples of dynamically convex contact manifolds violating both bounds in Theorem
1.11
.

There was substantial evidence prior to
[
5
]
that the answer to Question
1.17
is no. For example, Abbondandolo-Bramham-Hryniewicz-Salomão proved in
[
1
]
that the weak Viterbo conjecture fails for dynamically convex domains. There is substantial evidence for the latter conjecture, especially in dimension four
[
6
]
, so the contact forms in
[
1
]
are likely not convex.

In higher dimensions, Ginzburg-Macarini
[
15
]
constructed examples of dynamically convex contact forms admitting an action of a finite group
G
that were not
G
-equivariantly isomorphic to a convex boundary with a similar
G
-action. However, their methods only apply when
G
is non-trivial, and thus do not answer Question
1.17
.

Theorem
1.12
can be used to resolve Question
1.17
in any dimension. In fact, using the results in
[
5
]
, Dardennes-Gutt-Zhang
[
8
]
introduced an elegant toric construction of non-convex, dynamically convex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
that is much simpler than the open book construction in
[
5
]
. Using a straight forward adaptation of their operation, we prove the following result.

Proposition 1.18
.

(Proposition
5.16
) Let
X_{\Omega}
be a star-shaped, concave toric domain. Then for any
C,\epsilon>0
, there is a smooth, star-shaped, concave moment region

\hat{\Omega}\supset\Omega

that satisfies the following properties

\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Omega})\leq\operatorname{vol}(X_{\hat{\Omega}})\leq\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Omega})+\epsilon\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(X_{\hat{\Omega}})\geq C\qquad c(X_{\Omega})\leq c(X_{\hat{\Omega}})

Smooth concave toric domains are examples of strictly monotone toric domains (see Definition
5.7
), which are all dynamically convex (Proposition
5.8
). Therefore, Proposition
5.16
resolves Question
1.17
as it implies the following corollary.

Corollary 1.19
.

There are dynamically convex contact forms on
S^{2n-1}
that are not symplectically convex.

2.
Symplectic Linear Algebra

In this section, we review background topics from symplectic linear algebra that will be required later in the paper.

Specifically, we discuss polar decompositions and rotation quasimorphisms, which are key ingredients in our construction of the Ruelle invariant in §
3
. We also discuss variants and properties of the Conley-Zehnder index, which will be needed in §
5
.

2.1.
Polar Decomposition

Recall that every matrix
A\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
admits a unique
polar decomposition
into a product
A=UP
where
U
is orthogonal and
P
is symmetric positive definite.

We can view the polar decomposition as pair of smooth maps between spaces of matrices.

(2.1)

\Phi=(U,P):\operatorname{GL}(n)\to\operatorname{O}(n)\times\operatorname{P}(n)

Here
\operatorname{O}(n)
and
\operatorname{P}(n)
are, respectively, the spaces of orthogonal matrices and symmetric matrices.

\operatorname{O}(n)=\{A\in\operatorname{GL}(n)\;:\;AA^{T}=\operatorname{Id}\}\qquad\text{with Lie algebra}\qquad\mathfrak{o}(n)=\{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(2n)\;:\;A+A^{T}=0\}

\operatorname{P}(n):=\{A\in\operatorname{GL}(n)\;:\;A=A^{T}\}\qquad\text{with tangent space}\qquad\mathfrak{p}(n):=\{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)\;:\;A=A^{T}\}

We will need an explicit integral expression for the derivative of the polar decomposition.

Lemma 2.1
.

The differential
TU:T\operatorname{GL}(n)\to T\operatorname{O}(n)
of the map
U:\operatorname{GL}(n)\to\operatorname{O}(n)
is given by

TU_{A}(UB)=U\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sP}(B-B^{T})e^{-sP}ds\quad\text{at}\quad A=UP

Proof.

Fix
A\in\operatorname{GL}(2n)
and let
A=UP
be the polar decomposition. Note that we can split the tangent space
T_{A}\operatorname{GL}(2n)
into a direct sum

T_{A}\operatorname{GL}(2n)=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}=U\cdot\mathfrak{o}(n)+U\cdot\mathfrak{p}(n)

That is, any matrix can be written as a sum
US+UT
where
S
is anti-symmetric and
T
is symmetric. Clearly, if
T\in\mathfrak{p}(n)
is a small symmetric matrix
T\in\mathfrak{p}(n)
, the unitary part of
U(P+T)
is
U
. Thus,
U\cdot\mathfrak{p}(n)
is the kernel of
TU_{A}
, and so

TU(UB)=TU_{A}(US)\qquad\text{where}\qquad S=\frac{1}{2}(B-B^{T})

Thus, we must compute
TU_{A}(US)
where
S
is the anti-symmetric part of
B
. Let

T\Phi_{A}(US)=(UM,N)\qquad\text{with}\qquad M\in\mathfrak{o}(n)\text{ and }N\in\mathfrak{p}(n)

denote the image of
US
under
T\Phi_{A}
. Essentially by definition,
M
and
N
are the unique matrices that satisfy

US=T(\Phi^{-1})_{U,P}(UM,N)=\frac{d}{dt}(Ue^{Mt}P+U(P+tN))=UMP+UN

Multiplying this equation by
U^{-1}
and taking the transpose, we acquire the two equations

S=MP+N\qquad\text{and}\qquad-S=-PM+N

The difference of these two equations is the well-known Lyupanov equation for
M
.

2S=\{M,P\}=MP+PM

This equation has an integral solution (cf.
[
24
, Thm 12.3.3 and Thm 13.1.1]
) given by

M=2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-Ps}Se^{-Ps}ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-Ps}(B-B^{T})e^{-Ps}ds

By construction of
M
, we have
TU_{A}(UB)=UM
, so this is the desired formula.
∎

We are, of course, mostly interested in the symplectic polar decomposition. Let
\Omega
denote the standard linear symplectic structure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, i.e.

\Omega=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{array}\right]

We abbreviate the group of linear symplectomorphisms on
(\mathbb{C}^{n},\Omega)
and its Lie algebra in the usual way.

\operatorname{Sp}(2n)=\{A\in\operatorname{GL}(2n)\;:\;A\Omega A^{T}=\Omega\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{sp}(2n)=\{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(2n)\;:\;A\Omega+\Omega A^{T}=0\}

Recall that
\mathfrak{sp}(2n)=\Omega\cdot\mathfrak{p}(2n)
or, in other words, that
A
is in the symplectic Lie algebra if and only if
\Omega A
is symmetric. We let

\operatorname{U}(n)=\operatorname{O}(2n)\cap\operatorname{Sp}(2n)

denote the unitary group on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. By standard linear algebra (cf.
[
25
, Ch. 2]
), the polar decomposition restricts to a map

(U,P):\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\to\operatorname{U}(n)\times(P(2n)\cap\operatorname{Sp}(2n))

The derivative formula in Lemma
2.1
implies an estimate for the trace of derivative of the polar decomposition. This will be a key ingredient for bounding the Ruelle invariant in §
3.2
.

Lemma 2.2

(Trace Estimate)
.

Let
A
be a symplectic matrix and let
\Omega S
be a symplectic Lie algebra element with
S
positive semi-definite. Then

|\operatorname{tr}{C}(TU_{A}(\Omega SA)\cdot U^{-1})|\leq 16n^{2}\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(S)

Proof.

First, note that we can compute the complex trace as a real trace, as follows.

(2.2)

\operatorname{tr}{C}(TU_{A}(\Omega SA)\cdot U^{-1})=2i\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(\Omega^{-1}\cdot TU_{A}(\Omega SA)\cdot U^{-1})

Thus it suffices to estimate the real trace of
\Omega^{-1}\cdot TU_{A}(\Omega SA)\cdot U^{-1}
. We may write
\Omega SA=UB
where
B=U^{T}\Omega SUP
and apply Lemma
2.1
to see that

TU_{A}(\Omega SA)=U\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sP}(U^{T}\Omega SUP+P^{T}U^{T}S\Omega U)e^{-sP}ds

We multiply on the left by
-\Omega
and on the right by
U^{-1}=U^{T}
to acquire the formula

(2.3)

\Omega^{T}\cdot TU_{A}(\Omega SA)\cdot U^{T}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{T}Ue^{-sP}U^{T}\Omega)S(UPe^{-sP}U^{T})+(\Omega^{T}Ue^{-sP}PU^{T}\Omega)\Omega^{T}S\Omega(Ue^{-sP}U^{T})ds

The matrices
S,P
and
e^{-sP}
are all positive definite and
e^{-sP}
has eigenvalues between
0
and
1
. Thus, we may estimate the integrand on the righthand side as follows.

|\operatorname{tr}{R}((\Omega^{T}Ue^{-sP}U^{T}\Omega)S(UPe^{-sP}U^{T})+(\Omega^{T}Ue^{-sP}PU^{T}\Omega)\Omega^{T}S\Omega(Ue^{-sP}U^{T}))|

\leq|\operatorname{tr}{R}((\Omega^{T}Ue^{-sP}U^{T}\Omega)S(UPe^{-sP}U^{T}))|+|\operatorname{tr}{R}((\Omega^{T}Ue^{-sP}PU^{T}\Omega)\Omega^{T}S\Omega(Ue^{-sP}U^{T}))|

\leq 2\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(e^{-sP})\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(S)\cdot\operatorname{tr}(Pe^{-sP})\leq 4n\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(S)\cdot\operatorname{tr}(Pe^{-sP})

Therefore, we have

|\operatorname{tr}{R}(\Omega^{T}\cdot TU_{A}(\Omega SA)\cdot U^{T})\leq 4n\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(S)\cdot\big(\int_{0}^{\infty}Pe^{-sP}ds\big)=4n\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(S)\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(I_{2n})=8n^{2}\cdot\operatorname{tr}{R}(S)

We can plug this estimate into (
2.2
) to acquire the desired result. ∎

2.2.
Rotation Quasimorphisms

The rotation quasimorphism is a certain (equivalence class of) quasimorphism on the universal cover
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
of
\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
. Let us recall the relevant definitions.

Definition 2.3
.

A
quasimorphism

q:G\to\mathbb{R}
from a group
G
is a map that satisfies

(2.4)

|q(gh)-q(g)-q(h)|<C\qquad\text{for all }g,h\in G\text{ and some }C>0\text{ independent of $g,h$}

Two quasimorphisms
q
and
q^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if
|q-q^{\prime}|
is bounded, and
q
is
homogeneous
if

q(g^{k})=k\cdot q(g)\qquad\text{for any}\qquad g\in G\qquad\text{and}\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}

The universal cover of the symplectic group possesses a canonical homogeneous quasimorphism, due to the following result of Salamon-Ben Simon
[
30
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
30
]
, Thm 1)
.

There exists a unique homogeneous quasimorphism

\rho:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\to\mathbb{R}

that restricts to the lift of the complex determinant
\operatorname{det}{C}
on
\operatorname{U}(n)
. That is, the diagram

(2.5)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\operatorname{U}}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{U}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U(1)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{exp}(2\pi i\cdot)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{det}{C}}

commutes.

Definition 2.5
.

A
rotation quasimorphism

q:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\to\mathbb{R}
is a quasimorphism that is equivalent to the quasimorphism
\rho
in Theorem
2.4
.

We will use two representatives of this equivalence class of quasimorphisms. The first is defined using the complex determinant of the unitary part of the polar decomposition.

Example 2.6
.

[
4
]
The
determinant quasimorphism

r:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\to\mathbb{R}
is the lift of the composition

(2.6)

\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\xrightarrow{U}\operatorname{U}(n)\xrightarrow{\text{det}{C}}\operatorname{U}(1)\simeq\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

Lemma 2.7
.

[
4
,
9
]
There exists a constant
C>0
such that

|r(\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Psi})-r(\tilde{\Phi})-r(\tilde{\Psi})|<C\qquad\text{for all}\qquad\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\Psi}\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)

The second uses the eigenvalues of
A
, and appears in formulations of the Conley-Zehnder index.

Example 2.8
.

[
16
]
The
eigenvalue quasimorphism

e:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\to\mathbb{R}
is the lift of the map

\underline{e}:\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\to U(1)

defined as follows. Let
A
be a symplectic matrix. For each eigenvalue
\lambda\in U(1)\setminus\{\pm 1\}
with generalized complex eigenspace
V(\lambda)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2n}
, consider the real quadratic form

Q(A,\lambda):V(\lambda)\otimes V(\lambda)\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{given by}\qquad Q(A,\lambda)(v\otimes w)=\operatorname{Im}(\omega(v,\bar{w}))

Let
m(A,\lambda)
be the maximal real dimension of a real subspace of
V(\lambda)
on which
Q(A,\lambda)
is positive definite. Finally, let
n(A)
denote the sum of the complex dimensions of the generalized eigenspaces of
A
with negative real eigenvalues. Then

(2.7)

\underline{e}(A)=(-1)^{n(A)/2}\cdot\prod_{\lambda\in U(1)\setminus\{\pm 1\}}\lambda^{m(A,\lambda)/2}\in U(1)

Note that if
A
has no eigenvalues in
(U(1)\cup(-\infty,0))\setminus\{1\}
, then
\underline{e}(A)
is
1
by convention.

Proposition 2.9

(Trace Estimate)
.

Let
A:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
be a path of symplectic matrices with
A_{0}=\operatorname{Id}
and let

S_{t}:=-\Omega\frac{dA}{dt}A_{t}^{-1}

Suppose that
S
is positive semi-definite. Then the determinant quasimorphism
r
(see Example
2.6
) satisfies

r(A)\leq\frac{8n^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{tr}(S_{t})dt

where we regard
A
as an element of the universal cover
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
.

Proof.

Let
U
be the unitary part of
A
. The rotation quasimorphism on
A
is given by

r(A)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\frac{d}{dt}(\text{det}{C}(U_{t}))}{\text{det}{C}(U_{t})}dt=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\text{tr}{C}(\frac{dU_{t}}{dt}U_{t}^{-1})dt

The trace estimate in Lemma
2.2
implies that the trace above can be estimated as

|\text{tr}{C}(\frac{dU_{t}}{dt}U_{t}^{-1})|\leq 16n^{2}\cdot\operatorname{tr}(S_{t})\qquad\text{and thus}\qquad|r(A)|\leq\frac{8n^{2}}{\pi}\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{tr}(S_{t})ds\qed

2.3.
Conley-Zehnder Index

The Conley-Zehnder index is an invariant of certain non-degenerate elements of the universal cover of the symplectic group.

Recall that a symplectic matrix
A\in\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
is
non-degenerate
if none of its eigenvalues is equal to
1
. That is

\operatorname{det}(A-\operatorname{Id})\neq 0

We let
\operatorname{Sp}_{\star}(2n)\subset\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
denote the open set of non-degenerate symplectic matrices and
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}_{\star}(2n)
denote its inverse image in the universal cover.

Theorem 2.10
.

(cf.
[
16
]
) There is a unique continuous map, called the Conley-Zehnder index, of the form

\operatorname{CZ}:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}_{\star}(2n)\to\mathbb{Z}\qquad\text{for each dimension $n$}

that satisfies the following list of axioms.

(a)

(Naturality)
\operatorname{CZ}
is invariant under conjugation.

\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}\widetilde{\Phi}\widetilde{\Psi}^{-1})=\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})

(b)

(Direct Sum)
\operatorname{CZ}
is additive under direct sum.

\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Phi}\oplus\widetilde{\Psi})=\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Psi})+\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})

(c)

(Maslov Index) If
\widetilde{\Psi}\in\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Sp}(2n))
is an element of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
starting and ending on
\operatorname{Id}
, then

\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}\widetilde{\Phi})=2\mu(\widetilde{\Psi})+\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})

Here
\mu
is the Maslov index of the loop (cf.
[
25
]
).

(d)

(Signature) Let
\widetilde{\Psi}\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}_{\star}(2n)
be the homotopy class of the path
t\mapsto\exp(2\pi\Omega At)
for
t\in[0,1]
, where
A
is a non-degenerate symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda
of norm less than
1
. Then

\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Psi})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{sign}(A)\qquad\text{where $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)$ is the signature.}

There are a number of inequivalent ways to extend the Conley-Zehnder from
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}_{\star}(2n)
to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)

[
16
,
14
]
. We are primarily interested in the following extension.

Definition 2.11
.

The
lower semi-continuous Conley-Zehnder index
is the map

\operatorname{LCZ}:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\to\mathbb{Z}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})=\inf\big\{\liminf_{j\to\infty}\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{j})\;:\;\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\text{ with }\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}\to\widetilde{\Phi}\big\}

Evidently,
\operatorname{CZ}
extends
\operatorname{LCZ}
in the sense that
\operatorname{CZ}=\operatorname{LCZ}
on
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}_{\star}(2n)
and some axioms of
\operatorname{CZ}
survive as properties of
\operatorname{LCZ}
. We record these properties, along with a key lower bound, below.

Lemma 2.12
.

The lower semi-continuous Conley-Zehnder index
\operatorname{LCZ}
has the following properties.

(a)

(Naturality)
\operatorname{LCZ}
is invariant under conjugation.

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}\widetilde{\Phi}\widetilde{\Psi}^{-1})=\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})

(b)

(Direct Sum)
\operatorname{LCZ}
is additive under direct sum.

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Phi}\oplus\widetilde{\Psi})=\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi})+\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})

(c)

(Maslov Index) If
\widetilde{\Psi}\in\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Sp}(2n))
is an element of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
starting and ending on
\operatorname{Id}
, then

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}\widetilde{\Phi})=2\mu(\widetilde{\Psi})+\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})

(d)

(Lower Bound) Let
\rho:\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)\to\mathbb{R}
be the homogeneous rotation quasi-morphism. Then

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Phi})\geq 2\cdot\rho(\widetilde{\Phi})-n

The naturality and Maslov index properties follow immediately from the same properties of
\operatorname{CZ}
. The direct sum property is
[
14
, Lemma 4.3, p. 45]
and the lower bound is given in
[
14
, Eq. 4.6, p. 43]
. Note that the lower bound in
[
14
]
is stated in terms of the mean index
\hat{\mu}
(see
[
14
, p. 41]
).

As an example, we calculate
\operatorname{LCZ}
in the case of paths in
U(1)
. We will use this calculation in §
5
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\widetilde{u}(\theta)\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2)
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
be the homotopy class of the path

[0,1]\to\operatorname{U}(1)\subset\operatorname{Sp}(2)\qquad\text{where}\qquad t\mapsto\operatorname{exp}(2\pi i\theta\cdot t)

Then
\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{u}(\theta))
is given by
2\lceil\theta\rceil-1
. As a special case, we have
\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\operatorname{Id}}_{2})=-1
.

Proof.

By the signature property in Theorem
2.10
, we can directly compute that

\operatorname{CZ}(\widetilde{u}(\theta))=-1\text{ if }\theta\in(-1,0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{u}(\theta))=-1\text{ if }\theta=0

Since we can write
\theta=\lceil\theta\rceil+r
for
r\in(-1,0]
, the Maslov index property then implies that

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{u}(\theta))=\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{u}(\lceil\theta\rceil)\cdot\widetilde{u}(r))=2\mu(\widetilde{u}(\lceil\theta\rceil))+\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{u}(r))=2\lceil\theta\rceil-1\qed

2.4.
Indices Of Orbits

We conclude this section by discussing the Conley-Zehnder index of Hamiltonian and Reeb orbits.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
X
be a symplectic manifold with
c_{1}(TX)=0
and let
H:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a Hamiltonian. The (lower semi-continuous) Conley-Zehnder index

\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H;\gamma)\qquad\text{or simply}\qquad\operatorname{LCZ}(\gamma)

of a contractible periodic Hamiltonian orbit
\gamma:[0,T]\to X
is defined as follows. Let
T\Phi
be the differential of the Hamiltonian flow. Choose a disk
\Sigma\subset X
bounded by
\gamma
and a trivialization
\tau:TX|_{\Sigma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
\widetilde{\Psi}_{\tau}\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
be the homotopy class of the path

(2.8)

\Psi_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\qquad\text{given by}\qquad\Phi_{\tau}(t)=\tau_{\gamma(t)}\circ T\Phi(t,z)\circ\tau_{\gamma(0)}^{-1}\in\operatorname{Sp}(2n)

We define
\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H;\gamma)
to be
\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{\tau})
. Since
c_{1}(TX)=0
, this is independent of
\Sigma
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
(Y,\xi)
be a closed contact manifold with
c_{1}(\xi)=0
and let
\alpha
be a contact form. The (lower semi-continuous) Conley-Zehnder index

\operatorname{LCZ}(Y,\alpha;\gamma)\qquad\text{or simply}\qquad\operatorname{LCZ}(\gamma)

of a contractible periodic Reeb orbit
\gamma:[0,T]\to Y
is defined as follows. Let
T\Phi|_{\xi}
be the differential of the Reeb flow restricted to
\xi
. Choose a disk
\Sigma
bounded by
\gamma
and a trivialization
\tau:\xi|_{\Sigma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
. Let
\widetilde{\Psi}_{\tau}\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n-2)
be the homotopy class of the path

(2.9)

\Psi_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\qquad\text{given by}\qquad\Phi_{\tau}(t)=\tau_{\gamma(t)}\circ T\Phi|_{\xi}(t,y)\circ\tau_{\gamma(0)}^{-1}

We define
\operatorname{LCZ}(Y,\alpha;\gamma)
to be
\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{\tau})
. Since
c_{1}(TX)=0
, this is independent of
\Sigma
.

In the case of a Liouville domain, these two versions of
\operatorname{LCZ}
can be related.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
(X,\lambda)
be a Liouville domain with boundary
(Y,\xi)
. Fix an contractible loop

\gamma:[0,T]\to Y=\partial X\qquad\text{with}\qquad\gamma(0)=\gamma(T)

that is an orbit of the canonical Hamiltonian
H_{X}
, or equivalently a Reeb orbit of
\lambda|_{Y}
. Then

\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H_{X};\gamma)=\operatorname{LCZ}(Y,\lambda|_{Y};\gamma)-1

Proof.

Let
Z
and
R
denote the Liouville and Hamiltonian vector field, respectively. Also, we adopt the shortened notation
H=H_{X}
. Note that we have a splitting

TX=\xi^{\omega}\oplus\xi=\operatorname{span}(Z,R)\oplus\xi\simeq\mathbb{C}\oplus\xi

Now choose a disk
\Sigma\subset Y
bounded by
\gamma
and let
\tau:\xi|_{\Sigma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
be the unique isotopy class of trivialization of
\xi|_{\Sigma}
. Then we may form a trivialization
\sigma:TX|_{\Sigma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
as the direct sum

\sigma=\tau_{\operatorname{std}}\oplus\tau:TX\simeq\mathbb{C}\oplus\xi\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\tau_{\operatorname{std}}:\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{C}\text{ is the tautological trivialization}

The flow
\Phi
of
H
preserves
Z
and
R
. Indeed,
R
generates
\Phi
, and since
ZH=H
we have

dH=\mathcal{L}_{Z}(dH)=\mathcal{L}_{Z}(\iota_{R}\omega)=\iota_{[Z,R]}\omega+\iota_{R}\mathcal{L}_{Z}\omega=\iota_{[Z,R]}\omega+dH\qquad\text{and so}\qquad[R,Z]=0

Thus the paths
\Psi_{\sigma}:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
in (
2.8
) and
\Psi_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n-2)
in
(\ref{eqn:linearized_flow_Reeb})
are related by

\Psi_{\sigma}=\operatorname{Id}_{2}\oplus\Psi_{\tau}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\operatorname{Id}_{2}:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2)\text{ is the constant path}

By Lemma
2.13
, we have
\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\operatorname{Id}}_{2})=-1
. Thus we have

\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H;\gamma)=\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{\sigma})=\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\operatorname{Id}}_{2})+\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{\tau})=-1+\operatorname{LCZ}(Y,\alpha;\gamma)\qed

As a corollary, we have a different characterization of dynamical convexity in terms of the Hamiltonian flow of the canonical Hamiltonian.

Corollary 2.17
.

A star-shaped domain
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is dynamically convex if and only if the closed orbits
\gamma
of the canonical Hamiltonian
H_{X}
satisfy

\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H_{X};\gamma)\geq n

3.
Ruelle Density And Invariant

In this section, we construct the Ruelle invariant of a symplectic cocycle of a flow on a compact manifold, and demonstrate its basic properties.

3.1.
Rotation Function

For the rest of the section, we fix a flow and a symplectic cocycle.

\phi:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to Y\qquad\Phi:\rho^{*}E\to\phi^{*}E

We also fix a
\phi
-invariant Borel measure
\mu
. Our construction of the Ruelle invariant requires an auxilliary family of maps

\tilde{u}_{T}:Y\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{also denoted by}\qquad\tilde{u}_{T}(\Phi;J,\tau):Y\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{for }T\in[0,\infty)

depending on a choice of complex structure
J
and trivialization
\tau
. We refer to
\tilde{u}_{T}
as the
rotation function
at time
T
. The goal of this subsection is to define the rotation function and prove some basic properties.

Let us, first, recall the definitions of the various auxilliary data required to build
\tilde{u}_{T}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A (compatible)
complex structure

J
on
E
is an bundle map
J:E\to E
such that

J^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\omega(J-,-)\text{ is a fiberwise inner product}

A choice of compatible complex structure
J
gives
E
the structure of a Hermitian vector bundle. Standard results in algebraic topology (cf.
[
25
]
) state that the space
\mathcal{J}(E,\omega)
of compatible complex structures on
E
is contractible. Moreover, any two choices
J,J^{\prime}
of such complex structures yield isomorphic Hermitian vector bundles
(E,\omega,J)\simeq(E,\omega,J^{\prime})
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
determinant bundle

\Lambda E
of
(E,J)
is the maximal wedge power of
E
as a complex vector bundle. That is

\Lambda E:=\wedge^{k}\big(E\big)\qquad\text{where}\qquad k=\operatorname{rank}{C}(E)

A
trivialization

\tau:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}
is a unitary bundle map to the trivial bundle.

The determinant bundle of
E
is independent of
J
up to (homotopically unique) isomorphism. In particular, the set of homotopy classes of trivialization

\mathcal{T}(\Lambda E):=\{\text{trivializations }\tau:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}\}\;\big/\text{ homotopy }

is well-defined, without reference to a specific choice of
J
. The determinant bundle admits a trivialization if and only if
c_{1}(E)=c_{1}(\Lambda E)=0
. Furthermore, the space of trivializations is naturally a torsor over
[Y,S^{1}]\simeq H^{1}(Y;\mathbb{Z})
.

We are now ready to proceed with the construction of the rotation function.

Construction 3.3
.

Choose a compatible complex structure
J
on
E
and an explicit unitary trivialization
\tau:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}
in the chosen class. Start by taking the polar decomposition of
\Phi

\Phi:\rho^{*}E\xrightarrow{P}\rho^{*}E\xrightarrow{U}\phi^{*}E

Here
P
is self-adjoint and
U
is unitary with respect to
\omega
and
J
. The determinant
\Lambda U
of
U
and the trivialization
\tau
determine a unitary bundle map

u:\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{\tau^{-1}}\rho^{*}\Lambda E\xrightarrow{\Lambda U}\phi^{*}\Lambda E\xrightarrow{\tau}\mathbb{C}\quad\text{or equivalently a map}\quad u:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to U(1)

The map
u
sends
0\times Y
to
1\in U(1)
, and is therefore null-homotopic. Thus there is a unique lift

\tilde{u}:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\exp(2\pi i\cdot\tilde{u})=u\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{u}|_{0\times Y}=0

The rotation function
\tilde{u}_{T}
is simply this lift at time

\tilde{u}(T,-)
.

We may view these maps as a version of the rotation quasimorphism applied pointwise in
Y
to
\Phi
. To make this precise, it will be helpful to fix some notation.

Notation 3.4
.

Given a trajectory
\gamma:[0,T]\to Y
of
\phi
and a trivialization
\Xi:E|_{\gamma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we let

\Phi_{\Xi}:[0,T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\qquad\text{denote the map}\qquad\Phi_{\Xi}(t):=\Xi(t)\circ\Phi(t,x)\circ\Xi(0)^{-1}

Furthermore, we let
\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}
denote the unique lift of
\Phi_{\Xi}
to the universal cover satisfying
\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}(0)=\tilde{\operatorname{Id}}
.

Lemma 3.5

(Quasimorphism)
.

Let
\gamma:[0,T]\to Y
be a trajectory of
\phi
with
\gamma(0)=X
and let
\Xi:E|_{\gamma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a unitary trivialization of
E
over
\gamma
such that the map
\Lambda\Xi:\Lambda E|_{\gamma}\simeq\mathbb{C}
is
\tau|_{\gamma}
. Then

\tilde{u}_{T}(x)=r(\widetilde{\Phi}_{\Xi})

Proof.

Since
\Xi
is unitary, the unitary parts
U_{\Xi}
of
\Phi_{\Xi}
and
U
of
\Phi
are related by

U_{\Xi}(t):=\Xi(t)\circ U(t,x)\circ\Xi(0)^{-1}\in\operatorname{Sp}(2n)

The map on the determinant bundle
\Lambda\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}
induced by

is simply the determinant over
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, we have

\operatorname{det}{C}(U_{\Xi}(t))=\Lambda\Xi(t)\circ\Lambda U(t,x)\circ\Lambda\Xi(0)^{-1}=\tau(\phi(t,x))\circ\Lambda U(t,x)\circ\tau^{-1}(x)=u(t,x)

In particular, the maps
r\circ\widetilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{u}(x):[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
are both lifts of the same map

that are

at

. This implies that they agree, proving the result. ∎

The rotation functions at time

essentially define a sub-additive process in the sense of Kingman

Definition 3.6
.

A

Lemma 3.7
.

The family of maps
\tilde{u}_{T}
are a sub-additive process for
(Y,\phi)
and
\mu
.

Proof.

We verify the properties in Definition
3.6

\gamma:[0,S+T]\to Y
of
\phi
with
\gamma(0)=x
, and choose a unitary trivialization
\Xi:E|_{\gamma}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
inducing the trivialization
\tau:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}
. Define

(3.1)

\Phi_{\Xi}:[0,S+T]\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\qquad\Phi_{\Xi}(t):=\Xi(t)\circ\Phi(t,x)\circ\Xi(0)^{-1}

Let
\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}:[0,S+T]\to\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
denote the lift to the universal cover. Then by Lemma

and the quasimorphism property of

such that

(3.2)

\tilde{u}_{S+T}(x)=r(\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}(S+T))\leq r(\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}(S))+r(\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}(S+T)\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}(S)^{-1})+C

Clearly
\tilde{u}_{S}(x)=r(\tilde{\Phi}_{\Xi}(S))
by Lemma
3.5

\Phi_{\Xi}(S+t)\Phi_{\Xi}(S)^{-1}=\Xi(t)\circ\Phi(S+t,x)\circ\Phi(S,x)\circ\Xi(s)=\Xi(S+t)\circ\Phi(t,\phi_{S}(t))\circ\Xi(S)^{-1}

Thus Lemma

). To see the second property, note that if

\int_{Y}\tilde{u}_{T}\cdot\mu\geq\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\int_{Y}\phi^{*}_{k}\tilde{u}_{1}\cdot\mu+\int_{Y}\phi^{*}_{m}\tilde{u}_{S}\cdot\mu-cm\geq(-c+M\cdot\mu(Y))\cdot T

Here

and

∎

Proposition 3.11
.

The limit of
\frac{\tilde{u}_{T}}{T}
as

and the choice of representative of
\tau
.

Proof.

For convenience, we fix the following notation for this proof.

g(\Phi,J,\tau):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\tilde{u}_{T}(\Phi,J,\tau)}{T}\in L^{1}(Y,\mu;\mathbb{R})

To show that the limit depends only on the isotopy class of
\tau
, let
\sigma:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}
and
\tau:\Lambda E\simeq\mathbb{C}
be isotopic unitary trivializations. Then we have

\sigma=f\tau\qquad\text{where}\qquad f:X\to U(1)\qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad[f]=0\in[Y,U(1)]

Since

is null-homotopic,

admits a lift
\tilde{f}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
via the covering map
\exp(2\pi i\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to U(1)

(3.6)

u_{T}(\Phi,J,\sigma)=(f\circ\phi)u_{T}(\Phi,J,\tau)f^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{u}_{T}(\Phi,J,\sigma)=\tilde{f}\circ\phi+\tilde{u}_{T}(\Phi,J,\tau)-\tilde{f}

To prove independence of

3.2.
Construction Of Invariant

We are now ready to give a precise definition of the Ruelle density and invariant. Choose a complex structure

Definition 3.12
.

The

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\tilde{u}_{T}}{T}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau):=\int_{Y}\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau)\cdot\mu

Proposition 3.13
.

The Ruelle density and the Ruelle invariant satisfy the following formal properties.

(a)

(Covariance) If
\Psi:(E,\Phi)\to(E^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime})
is a symplectic cocycle isomorphism that maps
\tau
to
\tau^{\prime}
, then

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau)=\operatorname{ru}(\Phi^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\qquad\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\mu)=\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\mu)

(Direct Sum) If

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi_{1}\oplus\Phi_{2},\tau_{1}\otimes\tau_{2})=\operatorname{ru}(\Phi_{1},\tau_{1})+\operatorname{ru}(\Phi_{2},\tau_{2})

(c)

(Linearity) If
a\mu+b\nu
is a positive combination of
\phi
-invariant Borel measures
\mu
and
\nu
, then

\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,a\mu+b\nu)=a\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\mu)+b\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau,\nu)

(d)

(Trivial Bundle) If
\Phi
is a symplectic cocycle on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the tautological trivialization
\tau_{\operatorname{std}}
, then

\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau_{\operatorname{std}})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{q\circ\widetilde{\Phi}_{T}}{T}\qquad\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(\Phi,\tau_{\operatorname{std}})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{Y}q\circ\widetilde{\Phi}_{T}\cdot\mu

is any rotation quasimorphism (see §

) and

\Phi
(regarded as a map
\mathbb{R}\times Y\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
) to the universal cover
\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
.

Proof.

These properties are more or less immediate from the properties of
\tilde{u}_{T}
. We discuss each proof separately below.

Covariance.
This is immediate since we can assume (by choice of
J^{\prime}
and
\tau^{\prime}
) that
\Psi
is unitary.

Direct Sum.
Choose explicit complex structures
J_{i}
and unitary trivializations
\tau_{i}:\Lambda E_{i}\simeq\mathbb{C}
. We adopt the notatation

E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}\qquad\Phi=\Phi_{1}\oplus\Phi_{2}\qquad J=J_{1}\oplus J_{2}\qquad\tau=\tau_{1}\otimes\tau_{2}

The unitary part

Linearity.
This follows from the fact that
\operatorname{ru}(\Phi,\tau)\in L^{1}(Y,\mu)\cap L^{1}(Y,\nu)
and the linearity of integration against measures.

Trivial Bundle.
Clearly, it suffices to prove the result for the rotation quasimorphism

\tilde{\Phi}:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n))
denote the lift of
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times Y\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)

4.1.

We start by discussing linear tensor fields, i.e. tensor fields on a (Riemannian) manifold that are conformal with respect to a vector field. Let

Definition 4.1
.

A vector field

X\simeq(-\infty,0]\times\partial X

Definition 4.2

A tensor field

\mathcal{L}_{V}\Psi=a\cdot\Psi

We will need some elementary properties of linear tensor fields, which we record in the following lemma. The proofs are simple and left to the reader.

Lemma 4.3
.

V
-linear tensor fields on
M
have the following properties.

(a)

(Linearity) If
\Phi

\Psi

V
-linear tensor fields of slope

(b)

(Tensor Product) If
\Phi

\Phi\otimes\Psi\qquad\text{is $V$-linear of slope $a+b$}

(c)

(Integral) If

(d)

(Derivative) If

We will be primarily interested in

V
-linear metric

of slope
1

To start, we note that
V
is compatible with the covariant derivative and metric volume.

Lemma 4.4

(Covariant Derivative)
.

The covariant derivative
\nabla
of the metric

\mathcal{L}_{V}(\nabla\Psi)=\nabla(\mathcal{L}_{V}\Psi)\qquad\text{for any tensor field}\qquad\Psi

\langle\nabla_{U}V,W\rangle+\langle U,\nabla_{W}V\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle U,W\rangle\qquad\text{for any pair of vector-fields}\qquad U,W

Thus
\nabla\Psi
is
V
-linear of slope
a
if
\Psi
is
V
-linear of slope
a
.

Proof.

For the first formula, let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times M\to M
be the flow of
V
. Then

\Phi_{t}^{*}g=e^{t}g

Metrics differing by a constant conformal factor have identical covariant derivatives. Therefore

\mathcal{L}_{V}(\nabla\Psi)=\frac{d}{dt}(\Phi^{*}_{t}(\nabla\Psi))|_{t=0}=\frac{d}{dt}(\nabla(\Phi^{*}_{t}\Psi))|_{t=0}=\nabla\frac{d}{dt}(\Phi^{*}_{t}\Psi)|_{t=0}=\nabla(\mathcal{L}_{V}\Psi)

For the second formula, let
U
and
W
be arbitrary
V
-linear vector fields of slope

. Since the metric connection is torsion free,

and

\nabla_{U}V=\nabla_{V}U+[U,V]=\nabla_{V}U\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla_{W}V=\nabla_{V}W+[W,V]=\nabla_{V}W

Moreover,
\langle U,W\rangle
is slope

and

\langle U,W\rangle=\nabla_{V}\langle U,W\rangle=\langle\nabla_{V}U,W\rangle+\langle U,\nabla_{V}W\rangle=\langle\nabla_{U}V,W\rangle+\langle U,\nabla_{W}V\rangle

Since

and

Lemma 4.5

(Volume Form)
.

The metric volume form

-linear of slope

Proof.

We briefly adopt the notation

Taking the derivative at

Lemma 4.7

\langle W,\nabla H\rangle=\nabla_{W}H=\nabla_{W}\langle\nabla H,V\rangle=\langle W,\nabla^{2}H(V)\rangle+\langle W,\nabla_{\nabla H}V\rangle

The self-adjoint part of
\nabla V
is
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{Id}
by Lemma
4.4
. Since
\nabla V
is assumed to be self-adjoint, we thus conclude that

\langle W,\nabla H\rangle=\langle W,\nabla^{2}H(V)\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle W,\nabla H\rangle\text{ for all }W\qquad\text{and thus}\qquad 2\cdot\nabla^{2}H(V)=\nabla H

Finally, note that if
\nabla^{2}H
is positive definite, then we know that

\langle\nabla^{2}H(U),\nabla^{2}H(U)\rangle\leq\operatorname{tr}(\nabla^{2}H)\cdot\langle\nabla^{2}H(U),U\rangle=\Delta H\cdot\langle\nabla^{2}H(U),U\rangle\qquad\text{for any vector field }U

Applying this inequality to the Hessian
\nabla^{2}H
and the formula
2\cdot\nabla^{2}H(V)=\nabla H
, we find that

|\nabla H|^{2}\leq 4\cdot|\nabla^{2}H(V)|^{2}\leq 4\cdot\Delta H\cdot\langle V,\nabla^{2}H(V)\rangle=2\cdot\Delta H\cdot\langle V,\nabla H\rangle=2H\cdot\Delta H\qed

4.2.
Laplacian Functional

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold with a cylindrical vector field
V
such that
g
is
V
-linear of slope
1
. Consider the space of
V
-linear functions

\Gamma(M;V):=\{h\in C^{\infty}(M;\mathbb{R})\;:\;Vh=h\}

There is a convex open subset
U(M;V)\subset\Gamma(M;V)
consisting of positive functions.

U(M;V):=\{H\in\Gamma(M;V)\;:\;H>0\}

Note that the sub-level set
X=H^{-1}(-\infty,1]
(or equivalently,
H^{-1}(0,1]
) is a cylindrical domain for any
H\in U(M;V)
. The purpose of this section is to study the following functional on
U(M;V)
.

S:U(M;V)\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{given by}\qquad S(H):=\int_{X}\Delta H\cdot\mu_{g}\qquad\text{with}\qquad X:=H^{-1}(-\infty,1]

We begin by computing a useful formula for the variation of
S
.

Proposition 4.8

(Variation)
.

The variation
\delta S
of the functional
S:U(M;V)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\delta S_{H}(h)=\int_{\partial X}h\cdot(\frac{\operatorname{dim}(M)+2}{2}\cdot|\nabla H|^{2}-2\Delta H)\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

Proof.

Fix a function
H\in U(M;V)
and a tangent vector
h\in\Gamma(M;V)
. We set

H_{t}:=H+t\cdot h\qquad\text{and}\qquad X_{t}:=H_{t}^{-1}(-\infty,1]

The variation
\delta S_{H}(h)
of
S
along
h
is the time derivative of
S(H_{t})
at
t=0
.

\delta S_{H}(h)=\frac{d}{dt}\big(\int_{X_{t}}\Delta H_{t}\cdot\mu_{g}\big)|_{t=0}=\int_{X}\Delta\big(\frac{dH_{t}}{dt}\big)|_{t=0}\cdot\mu_{g}+\int_{\partial X}\Delta H\cdot\big(\iota_{\frac{dX}{dt}}\mu_{g}\big)|_{t=0}

Here
\frac{dX}{dt}
is the variation of
X_{t}
at
t=0
, i.e. a vector field along
\partial X
given as
\frac{d\Psi}{dt}
for a family of parametrizations
\Psi_{t}:\partial X\simeq\partial X_{t}
. Note that this depends on
\Psi
, but
\iota_{\frac{dX}{dt}}\mu_{g}|_{\partial X}
does not.

Lemma 4.9
.

Under a specific parametrization of
\partial X_{t}
, the variation
\frac{dX}{dt}
of
X_{t}
at
t=0
is given by

\frac{dX}{dt}=-h\cdot V

Proof.

Recall that the flow
\Phi
of
V
determines a diffeomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{R}_{r}\times\partial X\simeq M
. In these coordinates,
V=\partial_{r}
and
H=e^{r}
. Furthermore,
H_{t}=f_{t}\cdot e^{r}
where

f_{t}:\partial X\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad f_{0}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{df}{dt}=h\cdot e^{-r}=h\cdot H^{-1}

For small
t
, the boundary
\partial X_{t}
may be parametrized via

\Psi_{t}:\partial X\to\mathbb{R}\times\partial X\qquad\text{with}\qquad\Psi_{t}(x)=(-\log(f_{t}(x)),x)

The variation of the boundary
\frac{dX}{dt}
under the parametrization
\Psi_{t}
is thus

\frac{dX}{dt}=-\frac{df}{dt}|_{t=0}\cdot f_{0}^{-1}\cdot\partial_{r}=-h\cdot V\qed

Returning to the proof of Proposition
4.8
, we apply our formulas for the variations of
X_{t}
and
H_{t}
to acquire the following expression.

(4.1)

\delta S_{H}(h)=\int_{X}\Delta h\cdot\mu_{g}-\int_{\partial X}h\cdot\Delta H\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

We now proceed to analyze the first integral in (
4.1
). Using the divergence theorem and the fact that
|\nabla H|\cdot\nu=\nabla H
on any regular level set of
H
, we may write

(4.2)

\int_{X}\Delta h\cdot\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\langle\nu,\nabla h\rangle\cdot\iota_{\nu}\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\langle\frac{\nabla H}{|\nabla H|},\nabla h\rangle\cdot\iota_{\nu}\mu_{g}

Next, we note that
|\nabla H|\cdot\langle V,\nu\rangle=1
and
\langle V,\nu\rangle\cdot\iota_{\nu}\mu_{g}=\iota_{V}\mu_{g}
on
\partial X
. Therefore

(4.3)

\int_{\partial X}\langle\frac{\nabla H}{|\nabla H|},\nabla h\rangle\cdot\iota_{\nu}\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\langle\nabla H,\nabla h\rangle\cdot\langle V,\nu\rangle\cdot\iota_{\nu}\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\langle\nabla H,\nabla h\rangle\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

Using the Leibniz rule for the covariant derivative
\nabla
, we thus find that

(4.4)

\int_{X}\Delta h\cdot\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\langle\nabla H,\nabla h\rangle\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\operatorname{div}(h\cdot\nabla H\rangle)\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}-\int_{\partial X}h\cdot\Delta H\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

Now focus on the first integral on the righthand side. Since
\nabla H
is linear of slope
0
and
h
is slope
1
, the divergence
\operatorname{div}(h\cdot\nabla H)
is linear of slope
1
. Therefore

\operatorname{div}(h\cdot\nabla H)\cdot\mu_{g}\qquad\text{is a linear volume form of slope}\quad\frac{\operatorname{dim}(M)+2}{2}

Thus we apply Lemma
4.3
(c) to find that

(4.5)

\int_{\partial X}\operatorname{div}(h\cdot\nabla H)\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}=\frac{\operatorname{dim}(M)+2}{2}\cdot\int_{X}\operatorname{div}(h\cdot\nabla H)\cdot\mu_{g}

Finally, we once more apply Stokes’ theorem to see that

(4.6)

\int_{X}\operatorname{div}(h\cdot\nabla H)\cdot\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}\langle\nu,h\cdot\nabla H\rangle\cdot\iota_{\nu}\mu_{g}=\int_{\partial X}h\cdot|\nabla H|^{2}\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

Combining the formulas (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), and plugging the result into (
4.4
), we find that

(4.7)

\int_{X}\Delta h\cdot\mu_{g}=\frac{\operatorname{dim}(M)+2}{2}\cdot\int_{\partial X}h\cdot|\nabla H|^{2}\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}-\int_{\partial X}h\cdot\Delta H\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

Plugging (
4.7
) into (
4.1
) concludes the proof.
∎

By applying the variational formula in Proposition
4.8
, we can deduce a sandwiching property.

Proposition 4.10

(Sandwich Estimate)
.

Let
G,H:M\to\mathbb{R}
be maps in
U(M;V)
. Suppose that

\nabla V:TM\to TM\qquad\text{is self-adjoint}

\nabla^{2}G\text{ and }\nabla^{2}H\text{ are positive semi-definite}\qquad\text{and}\qquad G\leq H\leq L\cdot G\text{ for a constant }L\geq 1

Then
S(G)
bounds
S(H)
from above, up to a constant dependent on
L
and the dimension
d
of
M
.

S(H)\leq C(L,d)\cdot S(G)\qquad\text{where}\qquad C(L,d)=\operatorname{exp}(\frac{1}{2}\cdot Ld^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad d=\operatorname{dim}(M)

Proof.

Consider the family of functions and domains parametrized by
[0,1]_{t}
, given by

F_{t}=(1-t)\cdot H+t\cdot G=H+t\cdot(G-H)\qquad\text{and}\qquad X_{t}=F_{t}^{-1}(-\infty,1]

Due to our hypotheses on
G
and
H
,
F_{t}
and
X_{t}
have the following properties.

\nabla^{2}F_{t}\geq 0\qquad F_{s}\geq F_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{s}\subset X_{t}\qquad\text{for }s\leq t

On
X_{1}
, we can bound the time derivative of
F
from below as follows.

\frac{dF_{t}}{dt}=-|G-H|\geq-H\geq-\underset{X_{1}}{\operatorname{max}}(H)\geq-L\cdot\underset{X_{1}}{\operatorname{max}}(G)=-L

Moreover, by Lemma
4.7
, we know that

2\cdot\Delta F_{t}\geq|\nabla F_{t}|^{2}\quad\text{on}\quad\partial X_{t}=F_{t}^{-1}(1)

Now we apply the formula for the variation of
S
derived in Proposition
4.8
.

\frac{d}{dt}(S(F_{t}))|_{t=s}=\int_{\partial X_{s}}\frac{dF}{dt}\cdot(\frac{d+2}{2}\cdot|\nabla F_{s}|^{2}-2\cdot\Delta F_{s})\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}\geq-Ld\cdot\int_{\partial X_{s}}\Delta F_{s}\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}

Now note that by Corollary
4.6

\Delta H
is
V
-linear of slope
0
. Therefore,
\Delta H\cdot\mu_{g}
is a volume form of slope
d/2
, and so by Lemma
4.5
we have

\int_{\partial X_{s}}\Delta F_{s}\cdot\iota_{V}\mu_{g}=\frac{d}{2}\cdot\int_{X_{s}}\Delta F_{s}\cdot\mu_{g}=\frac{d}{2}\cdot S(F_{s})

Therefore, we acquire the following differential inequality for
S(F_{s})
.

\frac{d}{dt}(S(F_{t}))\geq-\frac{Ld^{2}}{2}\cdot S(F_{t})

Integrating this inequality from
0
to
1
, we obtain the desired result.

C(L,d)\cdot S(G)=\exp(\frac{Ld^{2}}{2})\cdot S(F_{1})\geq S(F_{0})=S(H)\qed

4.3.
Standard Ellipsoids

The prototypical star-shaped, convex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are standard ellipsoids. Here we review some facts about these domains that we will need for Theorem
1.12
.

Definition 4.11
.

The
standard ellipsoid

E
with symplectic widths
a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{n}
is the sub-level set

E=H_{E}^{-1}(-\infty,1]\qquad\text{with}\qquad H_{E}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{given by}\qquad(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=\pi\cdot\sum_{i}\frac{|z_{i}|^{2}}{a_{i}}

Every ellipsoid in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is symplectomorphic (via an affine symplectomorphism) to a standard one. Moreover, any convex body can be sandwiched between an ellipsoid and its scaling, as stated by John’s ellipsoid theorem.

Theorem 4.12

(John Ellipsoid)
.

[
22
]
Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex domain. Then there exists a unique ellipsoid
E
of maximal volume in
K
. Furthermore, if
c\in E
is the center of
E
then

E\subset K\subset c+n(E-c)

In
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we can assume that the John ellipsoid is standard after applying a symplectomorphism.

Lemma 4.13
.

[
5
, Cor. 3.6]
Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a convex domain. Then there is an affine symplectomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
and a standard ellipsoid
E
such that

E\subset\Phi(X)\subset 2n\cdot E

For ellipsoids, most of the geometric quantities that appear in the proof of Theorem
1.12
can be computed explicitly. We record the results of that computation.

Lemma 4.14

(Ellipsoid Quantities)
.

Let
E
be a standard ellipsoid with symplectic widths
a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{n}
. Then the systole period, Laplacian integral and metric volume of
E
are given by

c(E)=a_{1}\qquad S(H_{E})=\frac{4\pi}{n!}\cdot\big(\sum_{i}\frac{1}{a_{i}}\big)\cdot\prod_{i}a_{i}\qquad\operatorname{vol}_{g}(E)=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\prod_{i}a_{i}

In particular, these quantities obey the following inequalities.

4\pi\cdot\operatorname{vol}_{g}(E)\leq c(E)\cdot S(H_{E})\leq 4\pi n\cdot\operatorname{vol}_{g}(E)

Proof.

The formula for
c(E)
is standard (cf.
[
17
]
). To derive the volume, note that
E=A^{-1/2}(B^{2n})
where
A
is the diagonal Hermitian matrix with
H_{E}(z)=\langle z,Az\rangle
. Therefore

\operatorname{vol}(E)=\operatorname{det}(A)^{-1/2}\cdot\operatorname{vol}(B^{2n})=\Big(\prod_{i}\big(\frac{\pi}{a_{i}}\big)^{2}\Big)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{\pi^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\prod_{i}a_{i}

To compute
S(H_{E})
, we note that
\Delta H_{E}
is constant and given by

\Delta H_{E}=4\pi\cdot\sum_{i}\frac{1}{a_{i}}\qquad\text{so that}\qquad S(H_{E})=\Delta H_{E}\cdot\operatorname{vol}_{g}(E)

Finally, to prove the claimed inequality it suffices to note that
4\pi\leq\Delta H_{E}\cdot c(E)\leq 4\pi n
. Indeed, by the formulas already derived, we have

\Delta H_{E}\cdot c(E)=4\pi\cdot\sum_{i}\frac{a_{1}}{a_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 1\leq\sum_{i}\frac{a_{1}}{a_{i}}\leq n\qed

4.4.
Proof Of Main Estimate

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.12
.

Proof.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a convex, star-shaped domain. By Lemmas
4.13
and
1.7
, we may assume without loss of generality that there is a standard ellipsoid
E
such that

(4.8)

\frac{1}{2n}\cdot E\subset X\subset E

Note that since the systole period is a symplectic capacity on convex domains (cf.
[
17
]
), we have

c(X)\leq c(E)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{vol}(E)\leq(2n)^{2n}\cdot\operatorname{vol}(X)

Now let
T\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times X\to X
denote the symplectic cocycle induced by the Hamiltonian flow
\Phi
of
H_{X}
. This cocycle is generated by the Hessian, i.e.

\frac{d}{dt}\big(T\Phi(t,x))=\Omega\circ\nabla^{2}H_{X}(\Phi(t,x))\circ T\Phi(t,x)

where
\Omega
is the matrix representing multiplication by
i
. Convexity of
X
implies that
\nabla^{2}H_{X}
is positive semi-definite. Thus we may apply the trace estimate, Lemma
3.14
, and conclude that

\operatorname{Ru}(X)\leq\frac{8n^{2}}{\pi}\cdot\int_{X}\operatorname{tr}\big(\nabla^{2}H_{X}\big)\cdot\omega^{n}=\frac{8n^{2}}{\pi}\cdot n!\cdot\int_{X}\Delta H_{X}\cdot\mu_{g}=\frac{8n^{2}}{\pi}\cdot n!\cdot S(H_{X})

The inclusions (
4.8
) imply that

H_{E}\leq H_{X}\leq H_{\frac{1}{2n}\cdot E}=(2n)^{2}\cdot H_{E}

Now we apply the sandwiching estimate for
S
derived in Proposition
4.10
. Indeed,
M=\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus 0
has a cylindrical vector field
Z
(the standard Liouville vector field) and the standard metric is
Z
-linear of slope
1
. Moreover,
Z
satisfies

\nabla Z=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{Id}

We may therefore apply Proposition
4.10
to find that

S(H_{X})\leq C((2n)^{2},2n)\cdot S(H_{E})\qquad\text{where}\qquad C((2n)^{2},2n)=\exp(8n^{4})

Finally, combing the estimates above and applying Lemma
4.14
, we find that

c(X)\cdot\operatorname{Ru}(X)\leq\frac{8n^{2}\cdot n!}{\pi}\cdot\operatorname{exp}(8n^{4})\cdot c(E)\cdot S(H_{E})

\leq 32n^{3}\cdot n!\cdot\operatorname{exp}(8n^{4})\cdot\operatorname{vol}(E)\leq 32n^{3}\cdot(2n)^{2n}\cdot\operatorname{exp}(8n^{4})\cdot\operatorname{vol}(X)

This proves the inequality for the constant
C(n)
given by

C(n)=2^{2n+5}\cdot n^{2n+3}\cdot\operatorname{exp}(8n^{4})\qed

5.
Ruelle Invariant Of Toric Domains

In this section, we compute the Ruelle invariant of toric domains in any dimension and explain the higher-dimensional examples of non-convex, dynamically convex domains.

5.1.
Star-Shaped Toric Domains

We begin by recalling the basics of toric domains.

Remark 5.1
.

We recommend Gutt-Hutchings
[
17
]
for a detailed treatment. Also see
[
18
]
.

Consider
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the Hamiltonian action by
T^{n}\simeq(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}
induced by the
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
-action.

T^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}\qquad\text{given by}\qquad\theta\cdot z=(e^{2\pi i\theta_{1}}z_{1},\dots,e^{2\pi i\theta_{n}}z_{n})

This
standard torus action
is generated by the following moment map.

\mu:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to[0,\infty)^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\qquad\text{given by}\qquad\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{i}(z)=\pi\cdot|z_{i}|^{2}

One can extend
\mu
to a symplectomorphism on the free region of the action of the form

(\mu,\theta):(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n}\simeq(0,\infty)^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}\simeq(0,\infty)^{n}\times T^{n}

Here
(0,\infty)^{n}\times T^{n}
has symplectic form
\sum_{i}d\mu_{i}\wedge d\theta_{i}
.

Definition 5.2
.

The
toric domain

X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
moment region

\Omega\subset[0,\infty)^{n}
is the
T^{n}
-invariant domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
given by
X=\mu^{-1}(\Omega)
. It is conventional to use the notation
X_{\Omega}
for
X
.

We are interested in toric domains that are also star-shaped. In the coordinates
(\mu,\theta)
, the Liouville vector field
Z
and the Liouville form
\lambda
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are given by

Z=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\partial_{\mu_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}d\theta_{i}

Thus
X_{\Omega}
is a star-shaped domain if and only if
\Omega
is star-shaped with respect to
0
and

\partial_{+}\Omega:=\mu(\partial X_{\Omega})\subset\partial\Omega\qquad\text{is transverse to }\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\mu_{i}\partial_{\mu_{i}}

The canonical Hamiltonian of a star-shaped toric domain
X
, its corresponding vector field and its Hamiltonian all possess nice toric formulas. We record these in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X
be a star-shaped toric domain with moment region
\Omega
. Then

(a)

The canonical Hamiltonian
H_{X}
is given by
H_{X}=f_{\Omega}\circ\mu
where

f_{\Omega}:[0,\infty)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\cdot\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}=f_{\Omega}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{\Omega}^{-1}(1)=\partial_{+}\Omega

(b)

The Hamiltonian vector field
V_{X}
of
H_{X}
is given by

V_{X}=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}\circ\mu\cdot\partial_{\theta_{i}}

(c)

The Hamiltonian flow of
H_{X}
is given (in standard coordinates on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
) as

\Phi(t,z)=U(t,\mu)z

Here
U(t,\mu)
is a diagonal, unitary matrix dependent only on
t
and
\mu(z)
, with diagonal entries

u_{j}(t,\mu)=\exp\big(2\pi it\cdot\partial_{j}f_{\Omega}(\mu)\big)\qquad\text{for}\qquad i=1,\dots,n

(d)

The differential of the Hamiltonian flow of
H_{X}
is given (in standard coordinates on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
) as

T\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)\qquad\text{where}\qquad T\Phi(t,z)=U(t,\mu)Q(t,z)

Here
Q(t,z)=\operatorname{Id}+t\cdot M(z)
where
M(z)
is a nilpotent matrix.

Proof.

To see (a), note that the toric formula for
Z
implies that
F=f_{\Omega}\circ\mu
satisfies
ZF=F
and
F^{-1}(1)=\mu^{-1}(\partial_{+}\Omega)=\partial X
. Thus
H_{X}=F
since these properties uniquely determine
H_{X}
. (b) and (c) are immediate from (a). To deduce (d), differentiate (c) to acquire the formula

T\Phi(t,z)=U(t,\mu(z))(\operatorname{Id}+t\cdot M(z))\qquad\text{where}\qquad M(z)v=2\pi i\cdot D(z)\circ\nabla^{2}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z))\circ d\mu(v)

Here
D(z)
is the diagonal matrix with entries
z_{i}
. Note that
Q(t,z)=\operatorname{Id}+t\cdot M(z)
is symplectic for every
t
and fixed
z
. By Lemma
5.4
,
M(z)
is nilpotent and
Q(t,z)
has all
1
eigenvalues.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
Q:\mathbb{R}\to\operatorname{Sp}(2n)
be a path of symplectic matrices of the form

Q(t)=\operatorname{Id}+tM\qquad\text{where}\qquad M\text{ is $t$ independent}

Then
M
is nilpotent and the eigenvalues of
Q(t)
are
1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
J
be the matrix for the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Then for all
t
, we have

(\operatorname{Id}+tM)J(\operatorname{Id}+tM)^{T}=J\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad MJ+JM^{T}=MJM^{T}=0

Combining the last two formulas, we find that
M^{2}J=-MJM^{T}=0
so that
M^{2}=0
. Thus
M
is nilpotent and has eigenvalues
0
. Hence
1
is the only eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Id}+tM
.
∎

We now calculate the Ruelle invariant of a star-shaped toric domain in any dimension.

Remark 5.5
.

Our formula specializes to the formulas in
[
20
,
8
]
in dimension four. However, our calculation differs from both of
[
20
,
8
]
and utilizes the properties of the Ruelle invariant in §
3
.

Proposition 5.6

(Toric Ruelle)
.

The Ruelle density
\operatorname{ru}(X_{\Omega})
of a star-shaped toric domain
X_{\Omega}
is given by

\operatorname{ru}(X_{\Omega})(z)=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z))

In particular, the Ruelle invariant of
X_{\Omega}
is given by

\operatorname{Ru}(X_{\Omega})=\sum_{i}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}\cdot\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

Proof.

We consider the lift to the universal cover of the differential
T\Phi
of the Hamiltonian flow of the canonical Hamiltonian. By Lemma
5.3
, we may write

\widetilde{T\Phi}(t,z)=\widetilde{U}(t,\mu(z))\widetilde{Q}(t,z)

By Proposition
3.13
(c) and the quasi-morphsim property for
r
, we can calculate the Ruelle density as the limit

\operatorname{ru}(X_{\Omega})(z)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{r(\widetilde{T\Phi}(T,z))}{T}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{r(\widetilde{U}(T,\mu(z)))}{T}+\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{r(\widetilde{Q}(T,z))}{T}

Since
\widetilde{U}
is already unitary and diagonal, so we see that

\frac{r(\tilde{U}(t,z))}{T}=\frac{1}{2\pi iT}\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{tr}{C}(\frac{dU}{dt}(t,\mu(z))\cdot\widetilde{U}^{-1}(t,\mu(z)))dt=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i}\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z))dt=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z))

It now suffices to show that
r(\widetilde{Q}(T,z))/T\to 0
as
T\to\infty
. Since the determinant quasimorphism
r
and eigenvalue quasimorphism
e
are equivalent (see §
2.2
), we have

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{r(\widetilde{Q}(T,z))}{T}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{e(\widetilde{Q}(T,z))}{T}

Moreover, by Lemma
5.4
,
Q(t)
has all its eigenvalues equal to
1
for all
t
. In particular, it has no eigenvalues on
(U(1)\cup(-\infty,0))\setminus\{1\}
. Thus we conclude that

\underline{e}(Q(t))=1\in U(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad e(\widetilde{Q}(t))=0\qquad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\qed

5.2.
Monotone Toric Domains

In
[
18
]
, Gutt-Hutchings-Ramos introduced the notion of a (strictly) monotone toric domain.

Definition 5.7
.

A star-shaped, toric domain
X_{\Omega}
is
strictly monotone
if either of the following equivalent conditions are satisfied.

(a)

the unit normal vector-field
\nu_{\Omega}:\partial_{+}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
pointing outward from
\Omega
satisfies

\nu_{\Omega}(x)\in(0,\infty)^{n}\text{ for each }x\in\partial_{+}\Omega

(b)

the gradient of the canonical function
f_{\Omega}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\nabla f_{\Omega}(x)\in(0,\infty)^{n}\text{ for each }x\in\Omega\setminus 0

In dimension four, a star-shaped toric domain is monotone if and only if it is dynamically convex
[
18
, Prop. 1.8]
. We generalize this result to higher dimensions, in one direction.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
X
be a strictly monotone toric domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then
X
is dynamically convex.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a closed orbit of the Hamiltonian flow
\Phi
of
H_{X}
starting at
z\in\partial X
with period
T
. We may assume (without loss of generality) that

\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-m}\qquad\text{where}\qquad z_{j}=0\text{ if and only if }j=m+1,\dots,n

By Corollary
2.17
, it suffices to show that the lower semicontinuous Conley-Zehnder index of
\gamma
as a periodic orbit of
H_{X}
is bounded below by
n
. That is,
\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H_{X};\gamma)\geq n
.

We start by analyzing the differential
T\Phi
along
\gamma
. By Lemma
5.3
(c) we may write

\Phi(t,z)=U(t,\mu(z))z\qquad\text{and}\qquad T\Phi(t,z)=U(t,\mu(z))Q(t,z)

Here
U(t,\mu(z))
is a diagonal matrix with unit complex entries

u_{j}(t,\mu(z))=\operatorname{exp}(2\pi it\cdot\partial_{j}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z)))

Note that the flow
\Phi
preserves the symplectic subspace
\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus 0
. Thus the differential preserves
\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus 0
and
(\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus 0)^{\omega}=0\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-m}
and there is a block decomposition

T\Phi=T\Phi_{1}\oplus T\Phi_{2}\qquad\text{with respect to the splitting }\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-m}\text{ along }\gamma

Since
U
also decomposes in block form, it follows that we have a block decomposition

U=U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q=Q_{1}\oplus Q_{2}

A direct analysis of
Q
shows that
Q_{2}(t,z)=\operatorname{Id}
. Indeed,
Q(t,z)=\operatorname{Id}+t\cdot M(z)
where

M(z)v=2\pi i\cdot D(z)\circ\nabla^{2}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z))\circ d\mu(v)

Here
D(z)
is the diagonal matrix with entries
z_{j}
. Since
z_{j}=0
for
j=m+1,\dots,n
, we can conclude that the lower block of
M(z)
vanishes, and so
Q_{2}(t,z)=\operatorname{Id}
. Finally, note that the period
T
of
\gamma
must satisfy

T\cdot\partial_{j}f_{\Omega}(\mu(z))\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\qquad\text{for each}\qquad j=1,\dots,m

Thus, the upper block
U_{1}
of
U
satisfies
U_{1}(T,\mu(z))=\operatorname{Id}
, and is a closed loop in
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
.

Now estimate the lower semi-continuous Conley-Zehnder index
\operatorname{LCZ}
of the lift
\widetilde{T\Phi}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\widetilde{\operatorname{Sp}}(2n)
at
(T,z)
. By the above discussion, we may write

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{T\Phi}(T,z))=\operatorname{LCZ}\left(\widetilde{U}_{1}(T,z)\widetilde{Q}_{1}(T,z)\oplus\widetilde{U}_{2}(T,z)\right)

By the additivity and Maslov index properties of
\operatorname{LCZ}
(see Lemma
2.12
(b)-(c)), we have

(5.1)

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{T\Phi}(T,z))=2\mu(\widetilde{U}_{1}(T,z))+\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{Q}_{1}(T,z))+\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{U}_{2}(T,z))

To bound the first and third term, note that
U
is a diagonal unitary matrix, so we may write

\widetilde{U}_{1}(T,z)=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{u}_{j}(T,z)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{U}_{2}(T,z)=\bigoplus_{j=m+1}^{n}\widetilde{u}_{j}(T,z)

By the direct sum property of the Maslov index
[
25
, Thm. 2.2.12]
and the Conley-Zehnder index (see Lemma
2.12
(b)), along with the calculation of
\operatorname{LCZ}
in Lemma
2.13
, we may thus write

(5.2)

\mu(\widetilde{U}_{1}(T,z))=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu(\widetilde{u}_{j}(T,z))\geq m

(5.3)

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{U}_{2}(T,z))=\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{u}_{j}(T,z))\geq n-m

For the second term, note that
\widetilde{Q}_{1}(T,z)
satisfies
e(\widetilde{Q}_{1}(T,z)^{k})=e(\widetilde{Q}_{1}(kT,z))=0
where
e
is the eigenvalue quasimorphism in Example
2.8
. Thus the homogenization
\rho
of
e
, which is the unique homogeneous rotation quasimorphism, is also
0
on
\widetilde{Q{}}_{1}(T,z)
. Then by Lemma
2.12
(d)

(5.4)

\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{Q}_{1}(T,z))\geq\rho(\widetilde{Q}_{1}(T,z))-m=-m

By plugging (
5.2
-
5.4
) into (
5.1
), we acquire the desired lower bound.

\operatorname{LCZ}(X,H_{X};\gamma)=\operatorname{LCZ}(\widetilde{T\Phi}(T,z))\geq 2m-m+(n-m)=n\qed

Remark 5.9
.

Although we will not require this property later in the paper, Proposition
5.6
implies that the Ruelle invariant of a strictly monotone domain is always positive.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
X
be a strictly monotone, star-shaped toric domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then

\operatorname{ru}(X)>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(X)>0

5.3.
Concave Toric Domains

We are interested in the following sub-class of monotone domains.

Definition 5.11
.

A star-shaped toric domain
X_{\Omega}
is
concave
if the complement of
\Omega
is convex.

Lemma 5.12
.

A smooth concave toric domain
X_{\Omega}
is strictly monotone, and thus dynamically convex.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\langle\nu_{\Omega}(x),e_{i}\rangle>0
for each unit basis vector
e_{i}
and every
x\in\partial_{+}\Omega
.

To prove this, let
K
be the closure of
[0,\infty)^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Note that
\partial_{+}\Omega
is a properly embedded smooth hypersurface in
[0,\infty)^{n}
with
\partial_{+}\Omega\subset\partial K
. Moreover, the outward unit normal
\nu_{\Omega}
to
\partial_{+}\Omega
is normal and inward pointing along
\partial K
. Since
K
is convex, this implies that

(5.5)

\langle\nu_{\Omega}(x),w-x\rangle\geq 0\qquad\text{for any $w\in K$ and $x\in\partial_{+}\Omega$}

Since
\Omega
is compact,
K
contains the scaling
c\cdot e_{i}
for every
i=1,\dots,n
and all
c>0
sufficiently large. Thus (
5.5
) implies that, for any
x\in\partial_{+}\Omega
, we have

\langle\nu_{\Omega}(x),e_{i}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{c}\cdot\langle\nu_{\Omega}(x),x\rangle\qquad\text{for all $c>0$ sufficiently large}

To finish the proof, note that
\langle x,\nu_{\Omega}(x)\rangle>0
for any
x\in\partial_{+}\Omega
since

\langle\nu_{\Omega}(x),x\rangle=|\nabla f_{\Omega}(x)|^{-1}\cdot\langle x,\nabla f_{\Omega}(x)\rangle=|\nabla f_{\Omega}(x)|^{-1}\cdot f_{\Omega}(x)=|\nabla f_{\Omega}(x)|^{-1}>0\qed

We will need a formula for the minumum period of a Reeb orbit on the boundary of a concave toric domain given by Gutt-Hutchings
[
17
]
. Given a subset
S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, we adopt the notation

(0,\infty)^{S}:=\{x\in[0,\infty)^{n}\;:\;x_{i}\neq 0\text{ if and only if }i\in S\}

Given a star-shaped
\Omega\subset[0,\infty)^{n}
, we also let
\Omega_{S}\subset\Omega
and
\partial_{+}\Omega_{S}\subset\partial_{+}\Omega
be the subsets

\Omega_{S}:=\Omega\cap(0,\infty)^{S}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{+}\Omega_{S}:=\partial_{+}\Omega\cap\Omega_{S}

Definition 5.13
.

The
bracket

[-]_{\Omega}
of a concave, star-shaped moment region
\Omega
is the function

[-]_{\Omega}:[0,\infty)^{n}\to[0,\infty)\qquad\text{given by}\qquad[v]_{\Omega}:=\operatorname{min}\{\langle x,v\rangle\;:\;x\in\partial_{+}\Omega_{S}\}\qquad\text{if }v\in(0,\infty)^{S}

Lemma 5.14
.

[
17
, §2.3, p. 22]
Let
X_{\Omega}
be a concave, star-shaped toric domain. Then

c(X_{\Omega})=\operatorname{min}\{[v]_{\Omega}\;:\;v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\setminus 0\}

Note that, if
\Omega
and
\Xi
are concave, star-shaped moment regions with
\Omega\subset\Xi
, then
[-]_{\Omega}\leq[-]_{\Xi}
. Thus, as a corollary of Lemma
5.14
we have

Corollary 5.15
.

If
X
and
X^{\prime}
are concave, star-shaped and toric and
X\subset X^{\prime}
, then
c(X)\leq c(X^{\prime})
.

5.4.
Counter-Examples

We conclude this paper by constructing new non-convex, dynamically convex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by generalizing the strain operation of Dardennes-Gutt-Zhang
[
8
]
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
X_{\Omega}
be a star-shaped, concave toric domain. Then for any
C,\epsilon>0
, there is

\text{a smooth, star-shaped, concave moment region}\quad\hat{\Omega}\supset\Omega

that satisfies the following properties

\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Omega})\leq\operatorname{vol}(X_{\hat{\Omega}})\leq\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Omega})+\epsilon\qquad\operatorname{Ru}(X_{\hat{\Omega}})\geq C\qquad c(X_{\Omega})\leq c(X_{\hat{\Omega}})

Proof.

We start by fixing some notation. Fix a large
B>0
such that the moment region

\Xi:=\big\{x\in[0,\infty)^{n}\;:\;B^{-1}\cdot x_{2}+\dots+B^{-1}\cdot x_{n}\leq 1\big\}\qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad\Omega\subset\Xi

Also let
\Delta
denote the moment region for a very flat ellipsoid, given by

\Delta:=\big\{x\in[0,\infty)^{n}\;:\;A^{n}\cdot x_{1}+A^{-1}\cdot x_{2}+\dots+A^{-1}\cdot x_{n}\leq 1\big\}

Here
A
is a positive constant that we will specify below. The volume and Ruelle density of
X_{\Delta}
can be calculated as

\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Delta},\omega)=\operatorname{vol}(\Delta)=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot A^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{ru}(\mathbb{C}^{n},H_{\Delta})=A^{n}+(n-1)\cdot A^{-1}

Moreover, the volume of
\Delta\setminus\Xi
can be estimated as

\operatorname{vol}(\Delta\setminus\Xi)=\operatorname{vol}(\Delta)-\operatorname{vol}(\Xi\cap\Delta)\geq\frac{1}{n!}\cdot A^{-1}-A^{-n}\cdot B^{n-1}

Now let
\hat{\Omega}
be a smooth, star-shaped, concave moment region given by a concave smoothing of the union
\Omega\cup\Delta
such that

\Omega\cup\Delta\subset\hat{\Omega}\subset(1+\frac{1}{A})\cdot\Omega\cup\Delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\Omega}\setminus\Xi=\Delta\setminus\Xi

The only non-trivial bounds are the volume upper bound and the Ruelle invariant lower bound. For the volume bound, we note that for sufficiently large
A
, we have

(5.6)

\operatorname{vol}(\hat{\Omega})\leq(1+\frac{1}{A})^{n}\cdot\operatorname{vol}(\Omega\cup\Delta)\leq(1+\frac{1}{A})^{n}\cdot(\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)+\frac{A^{-1}}{n!})\leq\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)+\epsilon

For the Ruelle bound, we note that the Ruelle density of
X_{\hat{\Omega}}
, given by

\operatorname{ru}(\mathbb{C}^{n},X_{\hat{\Omega}})=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}f_{\hat{\Omega}}\geq 0

which is positive since
\hat{\Omega}
is monotone. Moreover, we have

f_{\hat{\Omega}}=f_{\Delta}\qquad\text{on}\qquad\hat{\Omega}\setminus\Xi=\Delta\setminus\Xi

\operatorname{Ru}(X_{\hat{\Omega}})=\sum_{i}\int_{\hat{\Omega}}\partial_{i}f_{\hat{\Omega}}\cdot\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\geq\sum_{i}\int_{\Delta\setminus\Xi}\partial_{i}f_{\Delta}\cdot\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}=(\frac{1}{n!}\cdot A^{-1}-A^{-n}\cdot B^{n-1})\cdot(A^{n}+(n-1)\cdot A^{-1})

For sufficiently large
A
, we can thus acquire
\operatorname{Ru}(X_{\hat{\Omega}})\geq C
. This concludes the proof. ∎
