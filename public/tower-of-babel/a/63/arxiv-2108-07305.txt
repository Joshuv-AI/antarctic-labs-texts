Flavors of Magnetic Noise in Quantum Materials
Shu Zhang, Yaroslav Tserkovnyak

Abstract
The complexity of electronic band structures in quantum materials offers new charge-neutral degrees of freedom stable for transport, a promising example being the valley (axial) degree of freedom in Weyl semimetals (WSMs). A noninvasive probe of their transport properties is possible by exploiting the frequency dependence of the magnetic noise generated in the vicinity of the material. In this work, we investigate the magnetic noise generically associated with diffusive transport using a systematic Langevin approach. Taking a minimal model of magnetic WSMs for demonstration, we show that thermal fluctuations of the charge current, the valley current, and the magnetic order can give rise to magnetic noise with distinctively different spectral characters, which provide a theoretical guidance to separate their contributions. Our approach is extendable to the study of magnetic noise and its spectral features arising from other transport degrees of freedom in quantum materials.



Introduction.
—Many recently discovered novel quantum materials are featured by the complexity of their electronic band structures, as a result of spin-orbit coupling
Hasan and Kane 2010
, magnetic order
Watanabe et al. 2018
, twist engineering
Zou et al. 2018
, etc. These structures may exhibit new degrees of freedom stable for transport, in addition to charge and spin, due to the protection either by symmetry or topology, which can be explored for the next-generation information devices. One prominent example is the valley degree of freedom present in some hexagonal two-dimensional semiconductors or Weyl semimetals (WSMs), the possibility of manipulating which gives rise to the field of valleytronics
Schaibley et al. 2016
.

Transport is generally noisy. As the Johnson-Nyquist noise (thermally excited electric currents) is important in electronic devices, understanding the generation of noise from these new degrees of freedom is of practical relevance in spintronic or valleytronic devices. At the same time,
the electromagnetic noise emitted by a material into its environment encodes rich information about its intrinsic excitation dynamics and transport properties.
For example,
the magnetic noise in the vicinity of a conductor
is directly related to its impedance
Johnson 1928
;
Varpula and Poutanen 1984
.
The recent development of magnetic noise spectroscopy using single qubits, especially the nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond
Taylor et al. 2008
, has provided a nanoscale probe to access such information noninvasively, and with high frequency resolution
Kolkowitz et al. 2015
;
Agarwal et al. 2017
;
Ariyaratne et al. 2018
.
NVs
have also turned out to be useful in the study of magnetic insulators
Van der Sar et al. 2015
;
Du et al. 2017
;
Casola et al. 2018
;
Chatterjee et al. 2019
by probing the magnetic noise generated by spin excitations.
While thermodynamic valley fluctuations have been accessed by optical methods
Goryca et al. 2019
, the noise associated with valley transport in low-frequency regimes is so far rarely explored.

In this work, we offer a qualitative perspective to the study of the magnetic noise in quantum materials, which can be flavorful due to the presence of various transport degrees of freedom, focusing on their generic diffusive aspect. To this end, we take an example of a magnetic WSM, which naturally involves three sources of noise, namely, charge, spin, and valley. Intriguingly, each flavor can contribute a distinct spectral character, as shown in Fig.

. Our perspective can be extended to consider other pseudospin degrees of freedom in general and may inspire future experimental work in the NV probe of quantum materials in light of its advantage in frequency resolution in the GHz regime.

WSMs are a family of topological quantum materials promising for valleytronic applications,
because the band crossing at Weyl points is topologically protected, and the valley relaxation time can be very long in a clean system

Magnetic WSMs, such as those in magnetic Heusler compounds

The interplay between charge, valley, and spin

The detection of valley transport often relies on the conversion from valley excitations to optic or electric signals, for instance, with the help of the chiral anomaly effect in a nonlocal geometry

In magnetic WSMs, however, this is not totally unambiguous due to the presence of spin excitations, which usually have a long diffusion length as well.
The magnetic noise spectroscopy can serve as a direct probe of the intrinsic transport properties in the absence of external perturbations and electric contacts.
As we will show, it is possible to distinguish the valley and spin contributions to the magnetic noise based on their spectral characteristics.

Main concepts.
—We first briefly summarize our conceptual understanding of why the three flavors contribute differently, even all under a diffusive treatment.
Focusing on the scenario with a nonvanishing carrier density, where the electric charge density fluctuations are screened by Coulomb interactions, transverse fluctuations of the charge current dominate the charge channel. In contrast, longitudinal fluctuations of the valley current are important due to its charge neutrality and hence the absence of screening
of the axial charge density. In our model (see below),
the Weyl nodes are induced
by the broken time-reversal symmetry associated with the magnetic order, which
dictates the response of valley currents to magnetic fields and thus the generation of magnetic noise by valley fluctuations.
Consequently, only the longitudinal component of the valley current generates magnetic fields in the environment: The valley current
\mathbf{j}^{v}
behaves as a magnetization, and determines a “magnetic charge” distribution in the bulk
\rho_{M}\propto-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{v}
, while that on the surface vanishes due to the boundary condition
\sigma_{M}\propto\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{v}=0
. In the spin channel, however, the spin density (rather than the spin current) plays such a role. See Fig.
1
for comparison.

Model.
—The following minimal model is considered for a magnetic WSM with four bands

Valley.
—We next apply this approach to the valley degree of freedom.
Different from the charge current, the valley fluctuations are neutral and thus compressible, which, as we have pointed out, turns out to be crucial for generating magnetic noise.

Discussion.
—It should be remarked that the valley noise derived here is based on a specific model (
1
), as a demonstration of our formalism. The resulted prediction is not universal for all WSMs. Different symmetry assumptions about spin and parity in the band model can lead to a different coupling scheme of the valley degree of freedom to magnetic fields. The frequency and distance dependence of the magnetic noise can therefore also reflect the applicability of the effective theories.
For specific materials, one may need to construct the coupling scheme from first-principles calculations.

We have considered the magnetic noise primarily driven by the diffusive transport, neglecting the valley and spin relaxation effects. This applies to NV distances much smaller than the corresponding diffusion lengths (while larger than the mean free paths), which is often the case in a clean material. A finite valley relaxation time modifies the valley diffusion equation, and in our Langevin approach, is accompanied by an intervalley fluctuation of the axial charge density. Likewise, spin density relaxation and fluctuation can be added to the Cahn-Hilliard model. We refer to the discussion in the supplemental material
sup
.
Furthermore, our treatment focuses on the low-frequency regime, where dynamic effects of the demagnetization fields, such as the Faraday induction, are negligible.

The Fermi arc states are universal in WSMs and are generally dissipative
Kourtis et al. 2016
;
Gorbar et al. 2016
.
The magnetic noise they generate, which is not detected in our choice of geometry, may also be interesting to look into, by placing the NV near material surfaces parallel to the magnetic order
\mathbf{M}
.
The Fermi-arc contribution may be recognized by its distance dependence
Burrello et al. 2019
, which is different from the bulk one.
For the spin channel, the change in the surface orientation relative to the order parameter would acquire a geometric factor
Guslienko and Slavin 2011
. It would also be straightforward to account for an anisotropic electric conductivity under our framework for practical application.

Going beyond the diffusive regime, a nanoscale probe such as NV can also probe the ballistic and perhaps the hydrodynamic transport of charge or spin, when the NV to material distance is smaller than or comparable to the electron or magnon mean free paths
Kolkowitz et al. 2015
;
Fang et al. 2022
. The spectral analysis of magnetic noise would also be particularly useful in the nonequilibrium regime, for example, in the presence of a steady spin or valley current, which may provide a cleaner detection of the corresponding nonlinear responses.

Appendix A
I. Transport

A.1
A. Charge

We take a stochastic approach and study the response of the charge current to a Langevin noise, and verify that our method reproduces the known result for the Johnson-Nyquist noise from the thermal fluctuations of electric charge current in a metal.
The charge transport obeys the continuity equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{c}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\mathbf{j}^{c}}=0.

(S.2)

In the low frequency limit, the charge density fluctuation is efficiently screened by Coulomb interactions. We thus consider the transverse current fluctuations only,
\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\mathbf{j}^{c}}=0

{\mathbf{j}^{c}}=-\sigma\bm{\nabla}\mu^{c}+\bm{\epsilon}^{c}

\sigma

\langle\epsilon^{c}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\epsilon^{c}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2\sigma k_{B}T\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

We find the response of the charge current
{\mathbf{j}^{c}}
to the thermal noise
\bm{\epsilon}^{c}
by solving the following differential equation

\displaystyle\sigma\bm{\nabla}^{2}\mu^{c}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{c}.

(S.3)

{\mathbf{j}^{c}}=\bm{\epsilon}^{c}-\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{c}/k^{2}
,
which gives the familiar transverse current correlation function

\displaystyle\langle j^{c}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)j^{c}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\left[\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta(\Delta\mathbf{r})+\frac{\delta_{ii^{\prime}}|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^{2}-3\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i}\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i^{\prime}}}{4\pi|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^{5}}\right]2\sigma k_{B}T\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(S.4)

where
\Delta\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
.

Here, since the current does not flow out of the material, we solve Eq. (
S.3
) in the half space
\Omega
with the Neumann boundary condition

\sigma\partial_{z}\mu^{c}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big|_{z=0}=\epsilon^{c}_{z}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big|_{z=0}
.
The Green function for the Laplacian with the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is [the time dependent factor is simply
\delta(t-t^{\prime})
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{L}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}+(z+z^{\prime})^{2}}}\right].

(S.5)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu^{c}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\mathcal{G}_{L}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)}{\sigma}-\int_{\partial\Omega}d^{2}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\mathcal{G}_{L}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\epsilon^{c}_{z}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)}{\sigma},

(S.6)

and integrating by parts

\displaystyle{\mathbf{j}^{c}}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{r},t)+\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{G}_{L}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(S.7)

Using the Fourier transform of the Green function (
S.5
) in the
x
and
y
directions with
\mathbf{R}=(x,y,0)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{L}(\mathbf{K},z,z^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2K}\left(e^{-K|z-z^{\prime}|}+e^{-K|z+z^{\prime}|}\right),

(S.8)

we take the Fourier transfrom of
{\mathbf{j}^{c}}(\mathbf{r},t)
in the following form,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{j}^{c}}(\mathbf{K},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{Kz}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\,e^{-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{j}^{c}}(\mathbf{r},t)

(S.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{Kz}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\,e^{-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,\left[\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{r},t)-\epsilon_{z}^{c}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2K}\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz^{\prime}\,e^{Kz^{\prime}}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\,e^{-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\cdot\left(\mathbf{K}\bm{\kappa}^{*}\frac{1}{K}-\bm{\kappa}\bm{\kappa}z^{\prime}\right),

where
\bm{\kappa}=\mathbf{K}+iK\hat{\mathbf{z}}
.
The second term in the square brackets comes from the Dirac
\delta
-function in
\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}}G^{c}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
.

In the magnetostatic limit, where the wavelength of the electromagnetic field and the skin depth are both much larger than the NV distance
d
, the Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field generated by the electric charge current distribution,

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{c}(\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{c}\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}\,{\mathbf{j}^{c}}(\mathbf{r},t)\times\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}}-\mathbf{r}|}

(S.10)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{c}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{2\pi}{K}e^{-Kd}(i\bm{\kappa})\times{\mathbf{j}^{c}}(\mathbf{K},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{c}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{2\pi}{K}e^{-Kd}\left[i\bm{\kappa}\times\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{K},t)+\frac{1}{K}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\mathbf{K})\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{K},t)\cdot\bm{\kappa}\right],

where
\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{K},t)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{Kz}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\,e^{-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{c}(\mathbf{r},t)
. We have used

\bm{\kappa}\times\bm{\kappa}^{*}=i2K\mathbf{z}\times\mathbf{K}
and

\bm{\kappa}\times\bm{\kappa}=0
. This result reproduces that in Ref.
Henkel 2005
derived from Maxwell equations with proper boundary conditions, and is consistent with the approach of finding the Fresnel coefficients
Ford and Weber 1984
;
Henkel et al. 1999
.
Finally, we obtain the magnetic noise tensor contributed from charge fluctuations, according to Eq. (
3
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{c}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e}{c}\right)^{2}\int d^{2}\mathbf{K}\frac{2\sigma k_{B}T}{2K^{3}}e^{-2Kd}\left(\epsilon^{ijk}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k}\kappa_{j}\kappa_{j^{\prime}}^{*}+\frac{i}{K}\epsilon^{ijk}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}zk^{\prime}}\kappa_{j}K_{k^{\prime}}\kappa_{k}^{*}-\frac{i}{K}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\epsilon^{izk}\kappa^{*}_{j^{\prime}}K_{k}\kappa_{k^{\prime}}+2\epsilon^{izk}\epsilon^{i^{\prime}zk^{\prime}}K_{k}K_{k^{\prime}}\right)

(S.11)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e}{c}\right)^{2}\int dKe^{-2Kd}2\pi\sigma k_{B}T\Delta_{ii^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{2}k_{B}T\sigma}{c^{2}d}\Delta_{ii^{\prime}},

where the tensor
\Delta=\text{diag}(1/2,1/2,1)
and Einstein summation is taken over repeated indices.

A.2
B. Valley

Following the same approach, we next look into the contribution from the valley current. We first focus on the particle conservation in a single valley
p
, assuming the interaction and scattering between the two Weyl nodes are weak. We do not consider the chiral-anomaly related perturbations here.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{p}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{p}=0,

(S.12)

where

\rho^{p}=(\nu/2)\mu^{p}
and

\mathbf{j}^{p}=-(\sigma/2)\bm{\nabla}\mu^{p}+\bm{\epsilon}^{p}
. Here
\nu
refers to the total density of states at the Fermi surface, including both valleys.
Assuming no correlation between
\bm{\epsilon}^{+}
and
\bm{\epsilon}^{-}
,
\langle\epsilon^{p}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\epsilon^{p^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma k_{B}T\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
The differential equation to be solved is then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu^{p}-D\bm{\nabla}^{2}\mu^{p}=-\frac{2}{\nu}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{p}

(S.13)

where the diffusion coefficient
D=\sigma/\nu
, as is consistent with the Einstein relation and the Drude law. The boundary condition
j_{z}^{p}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big|_{z=0}=0
is similar to the case with charge. We use the Green function for the diffusion equation satisfying the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, and its Fourier transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{D}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})=\frac{\Theta(t-t^{\prime})}{[4\pi D(t-t^{\prime})]^{3/2}}e^{-[(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}]/4D(t-t^{\prime})}\left(e^{-(z-z^{\prime})^{2}/4D(t-t^{\prime})}+e^{-(z+z^{\prime})^{2}/4D(t-t^{\prime})}\right),

(S.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{D}(\mathbf{K},z,z^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{2DaK}\left(e^{-aK|z-z^{\prime}|}+e^{-aK|z+z^{\prime}|}\right),

where
\Theta(t-t^{\prime})
is the Heaviside step function. Here, the dimensionless parameter
a=\sqrt{-i\omega/DK^{2}+1}
carries the frequency dependence in the following context, and is evaluated in the branch with
\text{Re}\,a>0
.
The current distribution is therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}^{p}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{\epsilon}^{p}(\mathbf{r},t)-D\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{p}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{G}_{D}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime}),

(S.15)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}^{p}(\mathbf{K},\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{Kz}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\int dt\,e^{i\omega t-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{j}^{p}(\mathbf{r},t)

(S.16)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{Kz}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\int dt\,e^{i\omega t-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,\left[\bm{\epsilon}^{p}(\mathbf{r},t)-{\epsilon}_{z}^{p}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2aK^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz^{\prime}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\int dt\,e^{i\omega t^{\prime}-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}\,\bm{\epsilon}^{p}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot\left[{\textbf{\textkappa}}^{\star}{\textbf{\textkappa}}^{\star}\frac{e^{Kz^{\prime}}}{a+1}+\textbf{\textkappa}\textbf{\textkappa}\frac{e^{Kz^{\prime}}-e^{aKz^{\prime}}}{a-1}+\textbf{\textkappa}{\textbf{\textkappa}}^{\star}\frac{e^{aKz^{\prime}}}{a+1}\right],

where

\textbf{\textkappa}=\mathbf{K}+iaK\hat{\mathbf{z}}
, and
\textbf{\textkappa}^{\star}=\mathbf{K}-iaK\hat{\mathbf{z}}
.

The valley current density behaves like a local magnetization in generating the magnetic field:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{v}(\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{v}\mu_{B}}{v}\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}\left(-\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}}-\mathbf{r}|}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{j}^{v}}(\mathbf{r},\omega)

(S.17)

\displaystyle=-\frac{g_{v}\mu_{B}}{v}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{2\pi}{K}e^{-Kd}\bm{\kappa}\bm{\kappa}\cdot\left[\mathbf{j}^{+}(\mathbf{K},\omega)-\mathbf{j}^{-}(\mathbf{K},\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{g_{v}\mu_{B}}{v}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{2\pi}{K}e^{-Kd}\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\int dt\,e^{i\omega t-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,\bm{\kappa}\left(e^{Kz}\bm{\kappa}-\frac{e^{aKz}}{a}\textbf{\textkappa}\right)\cdot\left[\bm{\epsilon}^{+}(\mathbf{r},t)-\bm{\epsilon}^{-}(\mathbf{r},t)\right],

where
g_{v}
is the effective g factor describing the coupling of the valley degrees of freedom to an external magnetic field and
v
is the Fermi velocity. We have used
\bm{\kappa}\cdot\textbf{\textkappa}=-K^{2}(a-1)
and
\bm{\kappa}\cdot\textbf{\textkappa}^{\star}=K^{2}(a+1)
.
With the help of

\displaystyle\Big\langle\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{c_{1}z}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\int dt\,e^{-i\omega t+i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,\epsilon^{p}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz^{\prime}\,e^{{c_{2}}^{*}z^{\prime}}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\int dt^{\prime}\,e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}-i\mathbf{K}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}\epsilon^{p^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rangle

(S.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma k_{B}T}{c_{1}+{c_{2}}^{*}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

we obtain the magnetic noise tensor generated by valley fluctuations

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{v}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle B_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}},\omega)B_{i^{\prime}}^{*}(\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}},\omega)\rangle

(S.19)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g_{v}\mu_{B}}{v}\right)^{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{K}e^{-2Kd}2\sigma k_{B}T\kappa_{i}{\kappa}_{i^{\prime}}^{*}\left[1+\frac{1+aa^{*}}{(a+a^{*})aa^{*}}-\frac{1}{a^{*}}-\frac{1}{a}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g_{v}\mu_{B}}{v}\right)^{2}\frac{4\pi k_{B}T\sigma}{d^{3}}\Lambda_{ii^{\prime}}\int d\xi\,\xi^{2}e^{-2\xi}I^{v}(\xi,\zeta),

where

\displaystyle I^{v}(\xi,\zeta)=1+\frac{1+aa^{*}}{(a+a^{*})aa^{*}}-\frac{1}{a^{*}}-\frac{1}{a},

(S.20)

with dimensionless quantities

\xi=Kd
,

\zeta=\omega d^{2}/D
, and
a=\sqrt{-i\zeta/\xi^{2}+1}
.
The anisotropy tensor
\Delta
is the same as in
\mathcal{B}^{c}
.

A.3
C. Spin

We invoke the Cahn-Hilliard model
Cahn and Hilliard 1958
to describe the Langevin dynamics of the longitudinal spin density
s_{z}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s_{z}-D^{s}\bm{\nabla}^{2}s_{z}=\epsilon^{s},

(S.21)

where
D^{s}
is the spin diffusion constant and
\epsilon^{s}
is a scalar Gaussian white noise with the correlation function

\langle\epsilon^{s}(\mathbf{r},t)\epsilon^{s}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=-2\sigma^{s}k_{B}T\bm{\nabla}^{2}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, containing the Laplacian operator. The spin conductivity
\sigma^{s}
is related to the spin diffusion constant

D^{s}=\sigma^{s}/\chi_{0}

by the static longitudinal spin susceptibility
\chi_{0}
.
The boundary condition
j^{s}_{z}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big|_{z=0}=0
is specified in terms of the spin current

\mathbf{j}^{s}=-D^{s}\bm{\nabla}s_{z}
. The solution is

\displaystyle s_{z}(\mathbf{r},t)=\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathcal{G}_{D}^{s}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})\,\epsilon^{s}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(S.22)

The Green function
\mathcal{G}_{D}^{s}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})
is simply Eq. (
S.14
) with
D\rightarrow D^{s}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{D}^{s}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})=\frac{\Theta(t-t^{\prime})}{[4\pi D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})]^{3/2}}e^{-[(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}]/4D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})}\left(e^{-(z-z^{\prime})^{2}/4D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})}+e^{-(z+z^{\prime})^{2}/4D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})}\right)

(S.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{D}^{s}(\mathbf{K},z,z^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{2DbK}\left(e^{-bK|z-z^{\prime}|}+e^{-bK|z+z^{\prime}|}\right),

where
b=\sqrt{-i\omega/D^{s}K^{2}+1}
.
Taking the Fourier transform,

\displaystyle s_{z}(\mathbf{K},\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\,e^{Kz}\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\int dt\,e^{i\omega t-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}s_{z}(\mathbf{r},t)

(S.24)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{i\omega t^{\prime}-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}\,\frac{1}{D^{s}bK^{2}}\frac{be^{Kz^{\prime}}-e^{bKz^{\prime}}}{(b+1)(b-1)}\epsilon^{s}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

The spin density generates stray fields via the demagnetization kernel,

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{s}(\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}},\omega)

\displaystyle=g_{s}\mu_{B}\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}\left(-\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{r}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_{\text{NV}}-\mathbf{r}|}\right)\cdot s_{z}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{\mathbf{z}}

(S.25)

\displaystyle=g_{s}\mu_{B}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{2\pi}{K}e^{-Kd}\bm{\kappa}\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\int d^{2}\mathbf{R}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dte^{i\omega t-i\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{1}{iD^{s}bK}\frac{be^{Kz}-e^{bKz}}{(b+1)(b-1)}\epsilon^{s}(\mathbf{r},t).

Using the correlation relation of the Gaussian noise, we obtain the magnetic noise tensor

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{s}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=(g_{s}\mu_{B})^{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{K}e^{-2Kd}\kappa_{i}\kappa^{*}_{i^{\prime}}\frac{\sigma^{s}k_{B}T}{iD^{s}\omega}\left[\frac{1}{b(b+1)}-\frac{1}{b^{*}(b^{*}+1)}\right]

(S.26)

\displaystyle=\left(g_{s}\mu_{B}\right)^{2}\frac{2\pi k_{B}T\chi_{0}}{\omega d^{3}}\Lambda_{ii^{\prime}}\int d\xi\,\xi^{2}e^{-2\xi}I^{s}(\xi,\eta),

where
g_{s}
is the g factor of localized spins, and

\displaystyle I^{s}(\xi,\eta)=-i\left[\frac{1}{b(b+1)}-\frac{1}{b^{*}(b^{*}+1)}\right]

(S.27)

with
\xi=Kd
,
\eta=\omega d^{2}/D^{s}
and
b=\sqrt{-i\eta/\xi^{2}+1}
.

Appendix B
II. Relaxation

B.1
A. Spin

In the Langevin approach, the spin relaxation can be handled by replacing the correlation functions of the Gaussian noise by

\langle\widetilde{\epsilon}^{s}(\mathbf{r},t)\widetilde{\epsilon}^{s}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2k_{B}T\chi_{0}(1/\tau^{s}-D^{s}\bm{\nabla}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
in

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau^{s}}\right)s_{z}-D^{s}\bm{\nabla}^{2}s_{z}=\widetilde{\epsilon}^{s},

(S.28)

where
\tau^{s}
is the spin relaxation time.
This is because the consideration of the spin relaxation has to be accompanied by a spin density fluctuation, as dictated by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
Here, the Green function and its Fourier transform are, respectively,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{s}_{D}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})=\frac{\Theta(t-t^{\prime})}{[4\pi D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})]^{3/2}}e^{-[(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}]/4D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})}\left(e^{-(z-z^{\prime})^{2}/4D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})}+e^{-(z+z^{\prime})^{2}/4D^{s}(t-t^{\prime})}\right)e^{-(t-t^{\prime})/\tau^{s}},

(S.29)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{s}_{D}(\mathbf{K},z,z^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{2D^{s}\widetilde{b}K}\left(e^{-\widetilde{b}K|z-z^{\prime}|}+e^{-\widetilde{b}K|z+z^{\prime}|}\right),

where
\widetilde{b}=\sqrt{(-i\omega+1/\tau^{s})/D^{s}K^{2}+1}
.
The magnetic noise thus becomes

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{s}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=(g_{s}\mu_{B})^{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{K^{3}}e^{-2Kd}\kappa_{i}\kappa^{*}_{i^{\prime}}\frac{2k_{B}T\chi_{0}}{D^{s}}\frac{(1+\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{b}^{*})(\widetilde{b}+1)(\widetilde{b}^{*}+1)+[2(\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{b}^{*}+1)^{2}+\widetilde{b}\widetilde{b}^{*}(\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{b}^{*})]/2K^{2}D^{s}\tau^{s}}{\widetilde{b}\widetilde{b}^{*}(\widetilde{b}+1)^{2}(\widetilde{b}^{*}+1)^{2}(\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{b}^{*})}

(S.30)

\displaystyle=(g_{s}\mu_{B})^{2}\frac{\pi k_{B}T\chi_{0}}{D^{s}}\Delta_{ii^{\prime}}\int dKe^{-2Kd}\,\left[\frac{2+\widetilde{b}}{\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{b}+1)^{2}}+\frac{2+\widetilde{b}^{*}}{\widetilde{b}^{*}(\widetilde{b}^{*}+1)^{2}}\right].

In the limit
1/\tau^{s}\rightarrow 0
,
\text{Re}\left[(2+\widetilde{b})/\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{b}+1)^{2}\right]\rightarrow\text{Im}\left[(2D^{s}K^{2}/\omega)/b(b+1)\right]
, we arrive at the same result as in Eq. (
S.26
). In the opposite limit
\tau^{s}\rightarrow 0
,
(2+\widetilde{b})/\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{b}+1)^{2}\approx D^{s}K^{2}\tau^{s}
,
\mathcal{B}^{s}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)\rightarrow\left(g_{s}\mu_{B}\right)^{2}\pi k_{B}T\chi_{0}\tau^{s}\Delta_{ii^{\prime}}/2d^{3}
is independent of
D^{s}
, as expected from the relaxation dominated equation of motion.

Alternatively, the noise can be computed from the dynamic spin susceptibility, with the help of the fluctuation dissipation theorem. We derive dynamic spin susceptibility from the diffusion equation for the longitudinal spin dynamics

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s_{z}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{s}=-\frac{\chi_{0}}{\tau^{s}}\mu^{s},

(S.31)

where

\mathbf{j}^{s}=-\sigma^{s}\bm{\nabla}\mu^{s}
is the spin current,
\mu^{s}=s_{z}/\chi_{0}-h
is the spin chemical potential, and
h
is a force thermodynamically conjugate to
s_{z}
, given by the external magnetic field. The spin distributionr relaxes towards
\chi_{0}h
(
\mu^{s}=0
in equilibrium), characterized by the spin relaxation time
\tau^{s}
.
The differential equation

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau^{s}}\right)\mu^{s}-D^{s}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\mu^{s}=-\partial_{t}h.

(S.32)

again has the boundary condition
\partial_{z}\mu^{s}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big|_{z=0}=0
.
From the solution of
\mu^{s}(\mathbf{r},t)
, we have

\displaystyle s_{z}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\chi_{0}\int_{\Omega}d^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\left[\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{D}^{s}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})\partial_{t^{\prime}}\right]h(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

(S.33)

which defines the longitudinal susceptibility
\chi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};t,t^{\prime})
.
The Fourier transform of the dynamic spin susceptibility is therefore

\displaystyle\chi(z,z^{\prime},\mathbf{K},\omega)=\chi_{0}\left[\delta(z-z^{\prime})+\frac{i\omega}{2D^{s}\widetilde{b}K}\left(e^{-\widetilde{b}K|z-z^{\prime}|}+e^{-\widetilde{b}K|z+z^{\prime}|}\right)\right].

(S.34)

The spin induced magnetic noise is then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{s}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(g\mu_{B}\right)^{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{K}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{K^{2}}e^{-2Kd}\kappa_{i}{\kappa}_{i^{\prime}}^{*}\kappa_{j}{\kappa}_{j^{\prime}}^{*}\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz^{\prime}\,e^{K(z+z^{\prime})}\,\frac{2k_{B}T}{\omega}\text{Im}\chi(z,z^{\prime},\mathbf{K},\omega)

(S.35)

\displaystyle=\left(g\mu_{B}\right)^{2}\frac{2\pi k_{B}T\chi_{0}}{D^{s}}\Delta_{ii^{\prime}}\int dKe^{-2Kd}\,\text{Re}\left[\frac{2+\widetilde{b}}{\widetilde{b}(\widetilde{b}+1)^{2}}\right],

which reproduces the result (
S.26
) from the Langevin approach.

B.2
B. Valley

In the same spirit, we consider the following diffusion and relaxation of the valley degree of freedom

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau^{v}}\right)\rho^{v}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{v}=\widetilde{\epsilon}^{v},

(S.36)

where

\rho^{v}=(\nu/2)\mu^{v}
,
\nu
is the total density of states at the Fermi surface,
\mu^{v}=(\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}
)/2 is the valley chemical potential,

\mathbf{j}^{v}=-(\sigma/2)\bm{\nabla}\mu^{v}+\bm{\epsilon}^{v}
, and
\tau^{v}
is the valley relaxation time. The intravalley noise has the same correlation as before
\langle\epsilon^{v}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\epsilon^{v}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2\sigma k_{B}T\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and we also account for an intervalley fluctuation of the axial charge density
\langle\widetilde{\epsilon}^{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\rangle=0
and
\langle\widetilde{\epsilon}^{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\widetilde{\epsilon}^{v}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=(\nu/\tau^{v})2k_{B}T\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
The differential equation to be solved is then

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau^{v}}\right)\mu^{v}-D\bm{\nabla}^{2}\mu^{v}=\frac{2}{\nu}(-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{v}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{v}),

(S.37)

with the Neumann boundary condition. The Green function in this case is
