Critical lengths of Steklov eigenvalues of hypersurfaces of revolution in Euclidean space
Antoine Métras, Léonard Tschanz

Abstract
. We study the Steklov problem on hypersurfaces of revolution with two boundary components in Euclidean space. In [ 10 ] , the phenomenon of critical length, at which a Steklov eigenvalue is maximized, was exhibited and multiple questions were raised. In this article, we conjecture that, in any dimension, there is a finite number of infinite critical length. To investigate this, we develop an algorithm to efficiently perform numerical experiments, providing support to our conjecture. Furthermore, we prove the conjecture in dimension n = 3 n=3 and n = 4 n=4 . Keywords: Spectral geometry, Steklov problem, hypersurfaces of revolution, numerical experiments.



1.
Introduction

Let
(M,g)
a smooth compact connected Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
with smooth boundary
\Sigma
, the Steklov problem on
(M,\Sigma)
consists in finding the real numbers
\sigma
and the functions
f:M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta f=0\mbox{ in }M\\
\partial_{\nu}f=\sigma f\mbox{ on }\Sigma,\end{array}\right.

where
\nu
denotes the outward normal on
\Sigma
. Such a
\sigma
is called a Steklov eigenvalue of
(M,g)
. It is well known that the Steklov spectrum forms a discrete sequence
0=\sigma_{0}(M,g)<\sigma_{1}(M,g)\leq\sigma_{2}(M,g)\leq\ldots\nearrow\infty
, where each eigenvalue is repeated with its multiplicity, which is finite.

Since we are interested in upper bounds for the Steklov eigenvalues of a family of manifolds, we have to add some geometric constraints on the manifolds. Indeed,
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
states that if
(M,g)
is a compact connected manifold of dimension
n\geq 3
with boundary
\Sigma
, then there exists a family of Riemannian metrics
(g_{\varepsilon})
conformal to
g
and that agrees with
g
on
\Sigma
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(M,g)\longrightarrow\infty\;\mbox{ as }\;\varepsilon\longrightarrow 0.

A class of manifolds that was recently investigated consists of warped products of the form
[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, where
L
is a positive real number and
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is the unit sphere of dimension
n-1
, see for instance
[
7
,
8
,
11
,
12
]
. Some authors studied the particular case where these warped products manifolds can be seen as hypersurfaces of revolution in Euclidean space, see
[
3
,
4
,
10
]
. In this article, we will focus in these hypersurfaces of revolution in Euclidean space. Let us start by recalling what they are.

Definition 1
.

A
n
-dimensional compact hypersurface of revolution
(M,g)
in Euclidean space with two boundary components
is the warped product
M=[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
endowed with the Riemannian metric

\displaystyle g(r,p)=dr^{2}+h^{2}(r)g_{0}(p),

where

(1)

The metric
g_{0}
is the canonical one on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,

(2)

The function
h:[0,L]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is smooth and satisfies
|h^{\prime}(r)|\leq 1
for all
r\in[0,L]
.

This assumption on
h
is a consequence of the fact that the hypersurface lives in Euclidean space, see
[
3
, Section
3
]
for more details.

As it was done in
[
3
,
4
,
10
]
, in this article we will only consider hypersurfaces of revolution whose boundary components are isometric to a unit
(n-1)
-sphere. Therefore, we will always assume that
h(0)=h(L)=1
.

If
M=[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
h:[0,L]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
satisfy the properties above, we say that
M
is a
hypersurface of revolution
, that
g(r,p)=dr^{2}+h^{2}(r)g_{0}(p)
is a
metric of revolution on
M
induced by
h
and we call the number
L
the
meridian length
of the manifold
M
.

We refer to
[
10
]
for an overview of what is already known concerning upper and lower bounds for the Steklov spectrum of hypersurfaces of revolution. Here, because we want to investigate
[
10
, Question 22]
, we will focus on the upper bounds. As such, the meridian length
L
plays an important role. Indeed, it was shown in
[
10
]
that when maximizing the
k
-th Steklov eigenvalue, for each given meridian length
L
, a sharp upper bound exists depending only on
L
. The problem is then reduced into studying this upper bound in function of
L
. One can show that for some value of
k
, the global maximum is achieved for some finite number
L_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, while for other
k
, the supremum is achieved at infinity. This motivates the following definitions.

Definition 2
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 1
be integers and
L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.

(1)

We write
B_{n}^{k}(L)<\infty
for the sharp upper bound for the
k
th eigenvalue of a hypersurface of revolution of dimension
n
and meridian length
L
.

(2)

We write
B_{n}^{k}:=\sup_{L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}}\{B_{n}^{k}(L)\}
.

(3)

We say that
L_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is a finite critical length associated with
k
, and that
k
has a finite critical length, if we have
B_{n}^{k}=B_{n}^{k}(L_{k})
.

(4)

We say that
k
has a critical length at infinity, or an infinite critical length, if it satisfies
B_{n}^{k}=\lim_{L\to\infty}B_{n}^{k}(L)
.

When we look at the literature, we can see that the phenomenon of finite/infinite critical lengths is intriguing. Indeed,

(1)

In dimension
n=2
:

(a)

All surfaces of revolution of
\mathbb{E}^{3}
with one boundary component isometric to the unit circle are isospectral to the unit disk
[
3
, Proposition 1.10]
. Therefore, for any fixed
k
, all meridian lengths
L
are critical for
k
in the sense that all of them achieve the maximum;

(b)

In the case of surfaces of revolution with two boundary components,
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
states that
k=2
has an infinite critical length while every other eigenvalues have a finite critical length.

(2)

In dimension
n\geq 3
:

(a)

If
(M,g)
is a hypersurface of revolution of the Euclidean space with one boundary component isometric to the unit sphere, then for all
k\geq 1
, the
k
th eigenvalue has an infinite critical length, see
[
4
, Proof of Theorem 1]
;

(b)

If
(M,g)
is a hypersurface of revolution of the Euclidean space with two boundary components each isometric to the unit sphere, the set of eigenvalues which have a finite critical length is non empty. Indeed,
[
10
, Corollary 4]
guarantees that
k=1
has a finite critical length. Moreover, the set of eigenvalues which have a critical length at infinity is also non empty. Indeed, in
[
10
, Section 6.1]
it was shown that
k=2
has an infinite critical length.

This consideration naturally calls for further research on finite/infinite critical lengths in the case
n\geq 3
and with two boundary components.

We state here
[
10
, Theorem 9]
:

Theorem 3
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then there exist infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
which have a finite critical length associated with them.
Moreover, if we call
(k_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{N}
the increasing sequence of such
k
and if we call
(L_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
the associated sequence of finite critical lengths, then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}L_{i}=0.

This result immediately raises the following open question (
[
10
, Question 22]
):

Given
n\geq 3
, are there finitely or infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k
has a critical length at infinity?

To investigate this question, we developed the following tool:

Theorem 4
.

Let
(M=[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g)
be a hypersurface of revolution in Euclidean space with two boundary components each isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, with
n\geq 3
, and let
k\geq 1
. Then there exists an algorithm, called
extension process
, that computes the sharp upper bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
, depending only on
n,k
and
L
, and shows that the upper bound is never achieved :

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)<B_{n}^{k}(L).

Moreover, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we construct a metric of revolution
g_{\varepsilon}
on
M
such that
\sigma_{k}(M,g_{\varepsilon})>B_{n}^{k}(L)-\varepsilon
.

Using this result, we obtain two corollaries:

Corollary 5
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 1
. Then there exists a bound
B_{n}^{k}<\infty
such that for all hypersurfaces of revolution
(M,g)
in Euclidean space with two boundary components each isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)<B_{n}^{k},

given by

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}:=\sup\{B_{n}^{k}(L):L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\}.

Moreover, this bound is sharp: for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a hypersurface of revolution
(M_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
such that
\sigma_{k}(M_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})>B_{n}^{k}-\varepsilon
.

Corollary 6
.

Let
(M_{i},g_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a family of hypersurfaces of revolution in Euclidean space with two boundary components each isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, where
M_{i}=[0,L_{i}]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
n\geq 3
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us suppose that
L_{i}\underset{i\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M_{i},g_{i})\underset{i\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Remark 7
.

Corollary
6
is already contained in Proposition 3.3 of
[
3
]
.

In this paper we do not answer the open question completely (we do so in the case of dimension
n=3
and
n=4
) but we perform some numerical experiments to clarify the phenomenon of finite / infinite critical lengths. Here is an overview of the experiments results:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cl}n&\mbox{Result of the program investigations}\\
\hline\cr 3&\mbox{From $k=18$ to $k=45^{\prime}601$, only finite critical lengths found.}\\
4&\mbox{From $k=408$ to $k=47^{\prime}641$, only finite critical lengths found.}\\
5&\mbox{From $k=8^{\prime}400$ to $k=195^{\prime}423$, only finite critical lengths found.}\\
6&\mbox{From $k=21^{\prime}112$ to $k=610^{\prime}973$, only finite critical lengths found.}\end{array}

Therefore, we propose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 8
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Then there exists a constant
K=K(n)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\geq K
, the
k
th eigenvalue has an associated finite critical length.

While investigating other questions and topics, Fan et al. proved this conjecture in the special case of dimension
2
, see
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
. This conjecture is still to be proved or refuted in general, but in this article, we are able to prove it in the case of dimensions
n=3
and
n=4
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
n\in\{3,4\}
. Then there exists a constant
K(n)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\geq K
, the
k
th eigenvalue has a finite critical length.

In
Section

2
, we recall the context and fix some notation we will use throughout the paper. In

3
, we describe the extension process and prove
5
and
6
, so that we can formulate the question and the conjecture properly in

. In

, namely we solve the question for the case of dimension

, then we add on these plots the mixed Steklov-Dirichlet and Steklov-Neumann eigenvalues in Appendix

.

2.
Hypersurfaces of revolution and mixed problems

As explained in
[
10
]
, maximizing the Steklov eigenvalues of hypersurfaces of revolution is deeply linked to the comprehension of the mixed Steklov-Dirichlet and Steklov-Neumann problem on annular domains. We recall what these mixed problems are in this section, as well as recalling what these links are.

2.1.
Characterization of the Steklov eigenvalues and eigenfunctions

For
(M,g)
a Riemannian manifold with smooth boundary
\Sigma
, we can characterize its
k
th Steklov eigenvalue by:

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)=\min\left\{R_{g}(f):f\in H^{1}(M),\;f\perp_{\Sigma}f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k-1}\right\},

where

\displaystyle R_{g}(f)=\frac{\int_{M}|\nabla f|^{2}dV_{g}}{\int_{\Sigma}|f|^{2}dV_{\Sigma}}

is the Rayleigh quotient and

\displaystyle f\perp_{\Sigma}f_{i}\iff\int_{\Sigma}ff_{i}dV_{\Sigma}=0,

with the
f_{i}
being
\sigma_{i}
-eigenfunctions,
i=1,\dots,k-1
.

Similarly, we have the usual Min-Max characterization

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)=\min_{S\in S_{k+1}}\max_{f\in S\backslash\{0\}}\left\{R_{g}(f)\right\},

where
S_{k+1}
is the family of all subspaces of dimension
k+1
of
H^{1}(M)
.

In the special setting of this article, where
(M,g)
is a hypersurface of revolution, the corresponding eigenfunctions have a special expression. We denote by
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\nearrow\infty
the spectrum of the Laplacian on
(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g_{0})
and we consider
(S_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions associated with it.

Proposition 10
.

Let
(M,g)
be a hypersurface of revolution as above. Then each eigenfunction on
M
can be written as
f_{l}(r,p)=u_{l}(r)S_{j}(p)
, where
u_{j}
is a smooth function on
[0,L]
.

This expression for the eigenfunctions is true in the more general case of warped product manifolds (and therefore for hypersurfaces of revolution) and it is often used, see for example
[
5
, Remark 1.3]
,
[
6
, Lemma 3]
,
[
9
, Proposition
3.16
]
or
[
11
, Proposition
9
]
.

2.2.
Mixed problems on annular domains

Let
\mathbb{B}_{1}
and
\mathbb{B}_{R}
be the balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
R>1
and
n\geq 3
, centered at the origin. The annulus
A_{R}
is defined as follows:
A_{R}=\mathbb{B}_{R}\backslash\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{1}
. We say that this annulus is of inner radius
1
and outer radius
R
. This particular kind of domains shall be useful in this article.

For such domains, it is possible to compute explicitly
\sigma_{(k)}^{D}(A_{R})
, which is the
(k)
th eigenvalue of the Steklov-Dirichlet problem on
A_{R}
, counted without multiplicity.

We state here Proposition
4
of
[
4
]
:

Proposition 11
.

For
A_{R}
as above, consider the Steklov-Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta f=0&\mbox{ in }A_{R}\\
\partial_{\nu}f=\sigma f&\mbox{ on }\partial\mathbb{B}_{1}\\
f=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\mathbb{B}_{R}.\end{array}\right.

Then, for
k\geq 0
, the
(k)
th eigenvalue (counted without multiplicity) of this problem is

\displaystyle\sigma_{(k)}^{D}(A_{R})=\frac{(k+n-2)R^{2k+n-2}+k}{R^{2k+n-2}-1}.

It is also possible to get the expression of the eigenfunctions of the Steklov-Dirichlet problem on an annular domain.

Lemma 12
.

Each eigenfunction
\varphi_{l}
of the Steklov-Dirichlet problem on the annulus
A_{R}
can be expressed as
\varphi_{l}(r,p)=\alpha_{l}(r)S_{l}(p)
, where
S_{l}
is an eigenfunction for the
l^{th}
harmonic of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Similarly we can compute explicitly
\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(A_{R})
, which is the
(k)
th eigenvalue of the Steklov-Neumann problem on
A_{R}
, counted without multiplicity.

We state now Proposition
5
of
[
4
]
:

Proposition 13
.

For
A_{R}
as above, consider the Steklov-Neumann problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta f=0&\mbox{ in }A_{R}\\
\partial_{\nu}f=\sigma f&\mbox{ on }\partial\mathbb{B}_{1}\\
\partial_{\nu}f=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\mathbb{B}_{R}.\end{array}\right.

Then, for
k\geq 0
, the
(k)
th eigenvalue (counted without multiplicity) of this problem is

\displaystyle\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(A_{R})=k\frac{(k+n-2)(R^{2k+n-2}-1)}{kR^{2k+n-2}+k+n-2}.

In the same manner as before, we have the following:

Lemma 14
.

Each eigenfunction
\phi_{l}
of the Steklov-Neumann problem on the annulus
A_{R}
can be expressed as
\phi_{l}(r,p)=\beta_{l}(r)S_{l}(p)
, where
S_{l}
is an eigenfunction for the
l^{th}
harmonic of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

2.3.
Multiplicity of the eigenvalues

Since we will have to deal with several problems and the multiplicity of the eigenvalues, we start by giving some notation, summarized in the table below:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l|cc}\mbox{Problem}&k\mbox{th eigenvalue}&k\mbox{th eigenfunction}\\
\hline\cr\mbox{Laplace}&\lambda_{k}&S_{k}\\
\mbox{Steklov}&\sigma_{k}&f_{k}=u_{k}S_{j}\\
\mbox{Steklov-Dirichlet}&\sigma_{k}^{D}&\varphi_{k}=\alpha_{k}S_{k}\\
\mbox{Steklov-Neumann}&\sigma_{k}^{N}&\phi_{k}=\beta_{k}S_{k}\end{array}

We also write
\lambda_{(k)},\sigma_{(k)},\sigma_{(k)}^{D},\sigma_{(k)}^{N}
for the
(k)
th eigenvalue counted without multiplicity.

In the case of the classical Laplacian problem
\Delta S=\lambda S
on
(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g_{0})
, it is known that the set of eigenvalues is
\{\lambda_{(k)}=k(n+k-2):k\geq 0\}
, see
[
1
, Page 160-162]
. Besides, the multiplicity
m_{0}
of
\lambda_{(0)}=0
is
1
and the multiplicity of
\lambda_{(k)}
is

(3)

\displaystyle m_{k}:=\frac{(n+k-3)(n+k-4)\ldots n(n-1)}{k!}(n+2k-2).

Thus, given
k\geq 0
, there are
m_{k}
independent functions
S_{k}^{1},\ldots,S_{k}^{m_{k}}
which satisfy

\displaystyle\Delta S_{k}^{i}=\lambda_{(k)}S_{k}^{i},\mbox{ for all }i=1,\ldots,m_{k}.

Moreover, for both the Steklov-Dirichlet and Steklov-Neumann problems on annular domains, the multiplicity of the
(k)
th eigenvalue is exactly
m_{k}
, as stated by
[
4
, Proposition 3]
.

Hence, using the notation above, given
k\geq 0
,

(1)

There are exactly
m_{k}
linearly independent Steklov-Dirichlet eigenfunctions associated with
\sigma_{(k)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})
, that can be written
\varphi_{k}^{i}(r,p)=\alpha_{k}(r)S_{k}^{i}(p),\;i=1,\ldots,m_{k}
.

(2)

There are exactly
m_{k}
linearly independent Steklov-Neumann eigenfunctions associated with
\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
, that can be written
\phi_{k}^{i}(r,p)=\beta_{k}(r)S_{k}^{i}(p),\;i=1,\ldots,m_{k}
.

3.
Extension process

It is natural to wonder if we can get a general formula giving a sharp upper bound for the
k
th Steklov eigenvalue of
(M,g)
, depending on
L
and
n
or even only on
n
.
The expression for such a general bound is difficult to obtain. However, one can introduce a process, that we call
extension process
, leading to a sharp bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
such that
\sigma_{k}(M,g)<B_{n}^{k}(L)
. This process is the following.

Let
(M=[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},g)
be a hypersurface of revolution and let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. We define

\displaystyle l_{0}:=\max\left\{l\in\mathbb{N}:\sum_{i=0}^{l}m_{i}\leq k\right\}.

Let us now consider the finite set

\displaystyle E:=\left\{\sigma_{(0)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2}),\ldots,\sigma_{(l_{0})}^{D}(A_{1+L/2}),\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}),\ldots,\sigma_{(l_{0}+1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})\right\}.

One can rearrange this set in ascending order, i.e we choose
\pi\in Sym(E)
such that the finite sequence

\displaystyle\left(\pi(\sigma_{(0)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})),\ldots,\pi(\sigma_{(l_{0})}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})),\pi(\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})),\ldots,\pi(\sigma_{(l_{0}+1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}))\right)

is increasing. For practical reasons, we rename this sequence

\displaystyle\left(\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{l_{0}},\nu_{l_{0}+1},\ldots,\nu_{2l_{0}+1}\right).

Let us now consider the corresponding multiplicities. For
i\in\{0,\ldots,2l_{0}+1\}
, we write
\mu_{i}
for the multiplicity of
\nu_{i}
. This gives us a finite sequence

\displaystyle(\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{l_{0}},\mu_{l_{0}+1},\ldots,\mu_{2l_{0}+1}).

We define

\displaystyle l_{1}:=\min\left\{l\in\{0,\ldots,2l_{0}+1\}:\sum_{i=0}^{l}\mu_{i}\geq k\right\},

and we will prove that

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}(L):=\nu_{l_{1}}

is a sharp upper bound for
\sigma_{k}(M,g)
.

Example 15
.

We take the case
n=5
and
L=1
, and we choose
k=127
. Using the formula

\displaystyle m_{i}=\frac{(n+i-3)(n+i-4)\ldots n(n-1)}{i!}(n+2i-2),

we have
m_{0}=1,\;m_{1}=5,\;m_{2}=14,\;m_{3}=30,\;m_{4}=55,\;m_{5}=91
.
Therefore,
\sum_{l=0}^{4}m_{l}=105\leq k=127
, but
\sum_{l=0}^{5}m_{l}=196>k
, which means that we have
l_{0}=4
.

We consider the set

\displaystyle E=\{

\displaystyle\sigma_{(0)}^{D}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(1)}^{D}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(2)}^{D}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(3)}^{D}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(4)}^{D}(A_{3/2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(2)}^{N}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(3)}^{N}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(4)}^{N}(A_{3/2}),\sigma_{(5)}^{N}(A_{3/2})\}.

Using Propositions
11
and
13
, we can determine the value of the
10
numbers belonging to the set
E
, and arrange them in an ascending order. We get

\displaystyle(

\displaystyle\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{0}\approx 2.27,\sigma_{(2)}^{N}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{1}\approx 4.11,\sigma_{(0)}^{D}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{2}\approx 4.26,

\displaystyle\sigma_{(1)}^{D}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{3}\approx 4.76,\sigma_{(2)}^{D}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{4}\approx 5.44,\sigma_{(3)}^{N}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{5}\approx 5.55,

\displaystyle\sigma_{(3)}^{D}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{6}\approx 6.24,\sigma_{(4)}^{N}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{7}\approx 6.78,\sigma_{(4)}^{D}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{8}\approx 7.13,

\displaystyle\sigma_{(5)}^{N}(A_{3/2})=:\nu_{9}\approx 7.89).

Writing
\mu_{i}
for the multiplicity of
\nu_{i}
, we can associate to
(\nu_{i})_{i=0}^{9}
the sequence
(\mu_{i})_{i=0}^{9}
. We get

\displaystyle(\mu_{0}=5,\mu_{1}=14,\mu_{2}=1,\mu_{3}=5,\mu_{4}=14,\mu_{5}=30,\mu_{6}=30,\mu_{7}=55,\mu_{8}=55,\mu_{9}=91).

We determine the value of
l_{1}
: since
\sum_{i=0}^{6}\mu_{i}=99<k
and
\sum_{i=0}^{7}\mu_{i}=154\geq k
, we have
l_{1}=7
.

We have a sharp upper bound for
\sigma_{127}([0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{4},g)
, given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{127}([0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{4},g)<B_{5}^{127}(1)=\nu_{7}=\sigma_{(4)}^{N}(A_{3/2})\approx 6.78.

One can then vary the value of
L
in order to find a sharp upper bound
B_{5}^{127}(L)
, that we can represent in an axis system, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Representation of the case
n=5
. The decreasing blue curves represent
L\longmapsto\sigma_{(i)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})
for
i=0,\ldots,4
while the increasing green curves represent
L\longmapsto\sigma_{(i)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
for
i=1,\ldots,5
. The solid red curve is the sharp upper bound
B_{5}^{127}(L)

Let us prove that
B_{n}^{k}(L)
is indeed the bound that we were looking for.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We will use the same strategy that was already used in the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 3]
. Let us start by showing that
\sigma_{k}(M,g)<B_{n}^{k}(L)
.

We consider
\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{l_{1}}
and the
k_{1}+1
independent eigenfunctions
\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{k_{1}}
associated, where
\nu_{i}
is either a Steklov-Dirichlet or Steklov-Neumann eigenvalue on
A_{1+L/2}
.
Note that by construction, we have
k_{1}+1\geq k
.

For each
i=0,\ldots,k_{1}
the function

\displaystyle\xi_{i}:[0,\frac{L}{2}]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},

is either a Steklov-Dirichlet or Steklov-Neumann eigenfunctions, dependending on the nature of
\nu_{i}
, hence we split the proof into two cases:

(1)

Let us suppose that
\xi_{i}
is a Steklov-Dirichlet eigenfunction. Then
\xi_{i}(L/2)=0
and we can extend
\xi_{i}
to a new function

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{i}:[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(r,p)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\xi_{i}(r,p)&\mbox{ if }0\leq r\leq L/2\\
-\xi_{i}(L-r,p)&\mbox{ if }L/2\leq r\leq L.\end{array}\right.

The function
\tilde{\xi}_{i}
is continuous.

(2)

Let us suppose that
\xi_{i}
is a Steklov-Neumann eigenfunction. Then we can extend
\xi_{i}
to a new function

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{i}:[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(r,p)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\xi_{i}(r,p)&\mbox{ if }0\leq r\leq L/2\\
\xi_{i}(L-r,p)&\mbox{ if }L/2\leq r\leq L.\end{array}\right.

The function
\tilde{\xi}_{i}
is continuous.

One can check that all these functions
\tilde{\xi}_{i}
are two by two orthogonal, therefore

\dim\,\mathrm{Span}(\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{k_{1}})=k_{1}+1\geq k.

Now, using the Min-Max characterization (
2
) of the Steklov eigenvalues, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{R_{g}(\tilde{\xi}_{i})\right\}<\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{R_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{\xi}_{i})\right\},

where
\tilde{g}=dr^{2}+\tilde{h}^{2}g_{0}
is given by a symmetric
\tilde{h}
satisfying

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(r)=\begin{cases}1+r&\text{if }0\leq r\leq m-1,\\
1+L-r&\text{if }L-m+1\leq r\leq L,\\
\frac{1+L/2+m}{2}&\text{if }r=\frac{L}{2},\end{cases}

and
h(r)>m
for
r\in(m-1,L-m+1)
,
where
m=\max_{r\in[0,L]}h(r)
. The inequality between Rayleigh quotients is then a consequence of the fact that, by construction,
\tilde{h}(r)\geq h(r)
for all
r
(see the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 2]
for details).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{R_{g}(\tilde{\xi}_{i})\right\}<\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{R_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{\xi}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{\frac{\int_{0}^{L}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left((\partial_{r}\tilde{\xi}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{h}(r)^{2}}|\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\xi}_{i}|^{2}\right)\tilde{h}(r)^{n-1}dV_{g_{0}}dr}{\int_{\Sigma}\tilde{\xi}_{i}^{2}(0,p)dV_{\Sigma}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{\frac{2\times\int_{0}^{L/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left((\partial_{r}\tilde{\xi}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{h}(r)^{2}}|\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\xi}_{i}|^{2}\right)\tilde{h}(r)^{n-1}dV_{g_{0}}dr}{2\times\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\tilde{\xi}_{i}^{2}(0,p)dV_{g_{0}}}\right\}

\displaystyle<\max_{i=0,\ldots,k_{1}}\left\{\frac{\int_{0}^{L/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left((\partial_{r}\xi_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{(1+r)^{2}}|\tilde{\nabla}\xi_{i}|^{2}\right)(1+r)^{n-1}dV_{g_{0}}dr}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\xi_{i}^{2}(0,p)dV_{g_{0}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{L/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left((\partial_{r}\xi_{k_{1}})^{2}+\frac{1}{(1+r)^{2}}|\tilde{\nabla}\xi_{k_{1}}|^{2}\right)(1+r)^{n-1}dV_{g_{0}}dr}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\xi_{k_{1}}^{2}(0,p)dV_{g_{0}}}\quad(\mbox{by construction})

\displaystyle=\nu_{l_{1}},

where we use the symmetry of
\tilde{g}
on the third line and the second strict inequality comes from the existence of a continuum of points
r\in[0,L/2]
such that
\tilde{h}(r)<1+r
.

Let
B:=\nu_{l_{1}}
, we will prove that
B
is the desired sharp upper bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
. It remains to show that
B
is sharp, i.e for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a metric of revolution
g_{\varepsilon}
on
M
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g_{\varepsilon})>B-\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
M=[0,L]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and let
g_{\varepsilon}(r,p)=dr^{2}+h_{\varepsilon}^{2}(r)g_{0}(p)
be a metric of revolution on
M
such that

(1)

For all
r\in[0,L]
, we have
h_{\varepsilon}(r)=h_{\varepsilon}(L-r)
(i.e
h_{\varepsilon}
is symmetric).

(2)

For all
r\in[0,L/2-\delta]
, we have
h_{\varepsilon}(r)=(1+r)
, with
\delta
small enough to guarantee that for all
r\in[0,L/2]
, we have
\max\{(1+r)^{n-3}-h_{\varepsilon}(r)^{n-3},(1+r)^{n-1}-h_{\varepsilon}(r)^{n-1}\}<\frac{\varepsilon}{B}=:\varepsilon^{*}
.

We write
f_{k}
a Steklov eigenfunction associated with
\sigma_{k}(M,g_{\varepsilon})
. Because
g_{\varepsilon}
is symmetric, we can choose
f_{k}
symmetric or anti-symmetric. Writing

\displaystyle R_{A_{1+L/2}}(f_{k}):=\frac{\int_{0}^{{L}/2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left((\partial_{r}f_{k})^{2}+\frac{1}{(1+r)^{2}}|\tilde{\nabla}f_{k}|^{2}\right)(1+r)^{n-1}dV_{g_{0}}dr}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(f_{k})^{2}(0,p)dV_{g_{0}}},

it is an easy computation to check that

\displaystyle R_{A_{1+L/2}}(f_{k})<\sigma_{k}(M,g_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon.

We once again split the proof into two cases:

(1)

Let us suppose that
f_{k}
is anti-symmetric, i.e we have
f_{k}(r,p)=u_{k}(r)S_{l}(p)
with
u_{k}
anti-symmetric and
B=\sigma_{j}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})
for a certain
j\leq l
. Then we can check that

\displaystyle f_{k}(L/2,p)=0\mbox{ and }\int_{\{0\}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\varphi_{i}f_{k}dV_{g_{0}}=0\mbox{ for all }i=0,\ldots,l-1.

(2)

Let us suppose that
f_{k}
is symmetric, i.e we have
f_{k}(r,p)=u_{k}(r)S_{l}(p)
with
u_{k}
symmetric and
B=\sigma_{j}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
for a certain
j\leq l
. Then we can check that

\displaystyle\partial_{r}f_{k}(L/2,p)=0\mbox{ and }\int_{\{0\}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\phi_{i}f_{k}dV_{g_{0}}=0\mbox{ for all }i=0,\ldots,l-1.

In both cases, we can use
f_{k}|_{[0,L/2]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
as a test function for
B
, and we get

\displaystyle B<R_{A_{1+L/2}}(f_{k})<\sigma_{k}(M,g_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon.

Therefore,
B=\nu_{l_{1}}
is indeed the sharp upper bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
.

∎

From this statement, let us prove Corollary
5
.

Proof.

Let
k\geq 1
. We want to show the existence of a bound
B_{n}^{k}<\infty
such that for all hypersurfaces of revolution
(M,g)
of dimension
n\geq 3
, we have
\sigma_{k}(M,g)<B_{n}^{k}
. A way to do it is the analyse to function
L\longmapsto B_{n}^{k}(L)
, and defining
B_{n}^{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}:=\sup\{B_{n}^{k}(L):L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\}.

By construction, we have
\sigma_{k}(M,g)<B_{n}^{k}
. We only have to show that
B_{n}^{k}
is finite. Indeed, for all
L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}(L)<\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(L).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sup\{B_{n}^{k}(L):L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}):L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\to\infty}\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})

\displaystyle=n+k-2.

Now we can prove that
B_{n}^{k}
is a sharp upper bound, that is for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a hypersurface of revolution
(M_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
such that
\sigma_{k}(M_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})>B_{n}^{k}-\varepsilon
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists
L^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that
B_{n}^{k}-B_{n}^{k}(L^{*})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. We define
M_{\varepsilon}:=[0,L^{*}]\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Thanks to Theorem
4
, there exists a metric of revolution
g_{\varepsilon}
on
M_{\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})>B_{n}^{k}(L^{*})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}>B_{n}^{k}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=B_{n}^{k}-\varepsilon.

∎

From Theorem
4
, let us prove Corollary
6
.

Proof.

Let us notice that given
j\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to 0}\sigma_{(j)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})=\infty\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lim_{L\to 0}\sigma_{(j)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})=0.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
L_{0}>0
be small enough so that
\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{1+L_{0}/2})<\ldots<\sigma_{(k)}^{N}(A_{1+L_{0}/2})<\varepsilon
. Let
0<L<L_{0}
. Then the finite set
E=E(n,k,L)
can be ordered and
\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{l_{0}+1}
are equal respectively to
\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}),\ldots,\sigma_{(l_{0}+1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
, where
l_{0}+1<k
.

Hence
\nu_{l_{1}}\in\{\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}),\ldots,\sigma_{(l_{0}+1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})\}
and therefore, writing

,

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(M,g)<\nu_{l_{1}}\leq\max\{\sigma_{(1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}),\ldots,\sigma_{(l_{0}+1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})\}<\varepsilon.

∎

4.
Question and conjecture

We now investigate if the sharp upper bound
B_{n}^{k}
is achieved by a finite critical length
L_{k}
or an infinite one. In particular, we are interested to know if the infinite critical length happens only finitely often. As the question is complex, we used a program that, given a dimension
n
and an integer
k
, checks if the eigenvalues with index smaller or equal to
k
have finite or infinite critical length. The code for this program is given in Appendix
C
.

Such a program requires quite some computation time to run, at least if we want to check many eigenvalues.
Here are some considerations used in order to optimize the program.

Definition 16
.

Given a dimension
n\geq 3
, we say that
k\in\mathbb{N}
is a
diagnosis eigenvalue
if
k=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}2m_{j}
for a certain integer
i
.

This definition is motivated by the following lemma:

Lemma 17
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
k:=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}2m_{j}
be the
i
th diagnosis eigenvalue. Let us suppose that
k
has an associated finite critical length. Then, for each
k^{\prime}\in\{k,\dots,k+2m_{i}-1\}
, the eigenvalue
k^{\prime}
has an associated finite critical length.

Proof.

Since
k=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}2m_{j}
, there exists
C>0
such that
B_{n}^{k}(L)=\sigma_{(i)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
for all
L>C
. Moreover, since
L\longmapsto\sigma_{(i)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
is strictly increasing, then the critical length
L_{k}
(which is finite by assumption) satisfies
L_{k}\leq C
.

Let us fix
L^{*}>C
. Then for each
k^{\prime}
such that
k\leq k^{\prime}<k+m_{i}
, we have
B_{n}^{k^{\prime}}(L^{*})=\sigma_{(i)}^{N}(A_{1+L^{*}/2})
. Moreover, we have
B_{n}^{k^{\prime}}(L_{k})\geq B_{n}^{k}(L_{k})
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{(i)}^{N}(A_{1+L^{*}/2})<B_{n}^{k}(L_{k})\leq B_{n}^{k^{\prime}}(L_{k})\leq\sup_{L\in(0,C]}B_{n}^{k^{\prime}}(L),

and
k^{\prime}
has a finite critical length.

Finally, for
k^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle k+m_{i}\leq k^{\prime}<k+2m_{i},

we have
B_{n}^{k^{\prime}}(L)=\sigma_{(i)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})
for all
L>C
. Moreover, since
L\longmapsto\sigma_{(i)}^{D}(A_{1+L/2})
is decreasing, we know that
k^{\prime}
has a finite critical length.

Therefore, for all
k^{\prime}\in\{k,\dots,k+2m_{i}-1\}
, the eigenvalue
k^{\prime}
has an associated finite critical length.

∎

Example 18
.

Let
n=5
. The sequence of diagnosis eigenvalues is

\displaystyle(2,12,40,100,210,392,672,1^{\prime}080,1^{\prime}650,2^{\prime}420,3^{\prime}432,4^{\prime}732,6^{\prime}370,8^{\prime}400,10^{\prime}880,\ldots).

The lemma allows us to state that in dimension
5
,
if
the
14
th diagnosis eigenvalue
k=8^{\prime}400
has an associated finite critical length,
then
for each
k^{\prime}\in\{8^{\prime}400,\dots,10^{\prime}879\}
, the
k^{\prime}
th eigenvalue has an associated finite critical length.

Therefore, it is relevant to consider only these diagnosis eigenvalues to save time while checking what could be the answer to our open question.

Here are some of the results obtained by our program:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}n&i&\mbox{Result of the program's investigations}\\
\hline\cr 3&150&\mbox{From $k=18$ to $k=45^{\prime}601$, only finite critical lengths found.}\\
4&40&\mbox{From $k=408$ to $k=47^{\prime}641$, only finite critical lengths found.}\\
5&30&\mbox{From $k=8^{\prime}400$ to $k=195^{\prime}423$, only finite critical lengths found.}\\
6&25&\mbox{From $k=21^{\prime}112$ to $k=610^{\prime}973$, only finite critical lengths found.}\end{array}

We draw attention to the fact that the program
does not say
that in dimension
3
, the eigenvalue number
17
has a critical length at infinity, it only says that it has not found any infinite critical length above
17
.
Actually, in dimension
n=3
, we have not found any infinite critical length associated with the eigenvalue
k
, if
k>8
.

These numerical results motivate
8
:

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer. Then there exists
K=K(n)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\geq K
, the
k
th eigenvalue has an associated finite critical length.

If this conjecture were to be proven, it would then be interesting to study the function

n\longmapsto K_{min}(n),

where
K_{min}(n)
is the smallest integer in the conjecture. From our numerical experiments, it seems that this function,
if it exists
, grows quickly with
n
.

5.
Proof of the conjecture in low dimension

Notation.
If the context is clear, we write
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{D/N}(L)
instead of
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{D/N}(A_{1+L/2})
to streamline the notations.

The goal of this section is to prove
9
.
The idea of the proof is simple: as explained in previous sections, the bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
can be obtained by looking at Steklov-Neumann
\sigma^{N}
and Steklov-Dirichlet
\sigma^{D}
eigenvalues on an annulus. So suppose that for a given
k
, the bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
is achieved by some
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}(L)
for
L
near 0.
We know the curve
L\mapsto\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}(L)
will intersect the curves of
\sigma_{(0)}^{D}(L),\sigma_{(1)}^{D}(L),\dots,\sigma_{(\kappa-1)}^{D}(L)
.
Hence looking at the multiplicities carried by each curve, one can see that for large enough
L
, the bound
B_{n}^{k}(L)
cannot continue to be achieved by
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}(L)
and must be achieved by a lower curve.
Applying this reasoning to more curves
\sigma^{N}(L)
, we will obtain an upper bound on the index of the curve
\sigma^{N}
which can be achieved for large
L
(c.f. Lemma
21
).
This allows us to obtain an upper bound for
B_{n}^{k}(L)
when
L\to\infty
.
Meanwhile, looking at the intersection of
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}(L)
and
\sigma_{(0)}^{D}(L)
, we obtain a lower bound for
B_{k}^{n}
(Lemma
19
).
In dimensions
n=3
or
n=4
, this bound is bigger than the one achieved at infinity allowing us to conclude that we must have a finite critical length.

We recall that the multiplicity
m_{k}
of the eigenvalue
\sigma_{(k)}^{N/D}(A_{1+L/2})
is given by

\displaystyle m_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+k-3)!}{(n-2)!k!}(n+2k-2)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+n-3)(k+n-4)\dots(k+2)(k+1)}{(n-2)!}(2k+n-2).

We first prove the following lemma:

Lemma 19
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
, let
b_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}
be the value such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})=b_{\kappa}=\sigma_{0}^{D}(A_{1+L/2}).

Let
c_{0}\approx 0.83
be the unique positive solution of the equation
\frac{1+c}{1-c}=e^{2/c}
.
Then for any
0<c<c_{0}
, there exists
\kappa_{0}(c)
such that for all
\kappa\geq\kappa_{0}
,

\displaystyle b_{\kappa}\geq c\kappa.

Proof.

Since
\sigma_{0}^{D}
and
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}
are monotone in
L
, we can invert them and give an expression for
(\sigma_{0}^{D})^{-1}
and
(\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N})^{-1}
. We get

\displaystyle(\sigma^{D}_{0})^{-1}(y)=\left(1+\frac{n-2}{y-(n-2)}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-2}}

and

\displaystyle(\sigma^{N}_{(\kappa)})^{-1}(y)=\left(\frac{1+\frac{y}{\kappa}}{1-\frac{y}{\kappa+n-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\kappa+n-2}}.

With the notation
y_{\kappa}=c\kappa
, we have to show that

\displaystyle(\sigma^{N}_{(\kappa)})^{-1}(y_{\kappa})\leq(\sigma^{D}_{0})^{-1}(y_{\kappa})

if
\kappa
is large enough. Here we use that
\sigma^{N}(\kappa)
is increasing and
\sigma^{D}_{0}
is decreasing.

Therefore, we need to show that for large enough
\kappa
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1+\frac{y_{\kappa}}{\kappa}}{1-\frac{y_{\kappa}}{\kappa+n-2}}\leq\left(1+\frac{n-2}{y_{\kappa}-(n-2)}\right)^{\frac{2\kappa+n-2}{n-2}}.

We have for the left-hand-side

\displaystyle\frac{1+\frac{c\kappa}{\kappa}}{1-\frac{c\kappa}{\kappa+n-2}}=\frac{1+c}{1-c}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\kappa}),

and for the right-hand-side

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{n-2}{c\kappa-(n-2)}\right)^{\frac{2\kappa+n-2}{n-2}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2\kappa+n-2}{n-2}\log\left(1+\frac{n-2}{c\kappa-(n-2)}\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2\kappa+n-2}{n-2}\left(\frac{n-2}{c\kappa-(n-2)}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}})\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2}{c}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\kappa})}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2}{c}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\kappa}).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\frac{1+\frac{c\kappa}{\kappa}}{1-\frac{c\kappa}{\kappa+n-2}}=\frac{1+c}{1-c}<e^{\frac{2}{c}}=\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{n-2}{c\kappa-(n-2)}\right)^{\frac{2\kappa+n-2}{n-2}},

since we assumed
0<c<c_{0}
.

∎

Remark 20
.

This lemma holds whatever the dimension
n\geq 3
. Moreover, the value of
c_{0}\approx 0.83
is independent of the dimension.

Lemma 21
.

Let
c_{0}\approx 0.83
be the unique positive solution of the equation
\frac{1+c}{1-c}=e^{2/c}
. For
n=3
or
n=4
, there exists
\kappa_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\kappa\geq\kappa_{1}
,

\displaystyle m_{\kappa}+m_{\kappa-1}+\dots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor}<m_{0}+m_{1}+\dots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}.

Proof.

Let us suppose
n=3
. Then we have
m_{i}=2i+1
, therefore

\displaystyle m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\kappa}=1+3+\ldots+(2\kappa+1)=(\kappa+1)^{2},

and

\displaystyle m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}=\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor^{2}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{m_{0}+\ldots+m_{\kappa}}{m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}}=\frac{(\kappa+1)^{2}}{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor^{2}}\underset{\kappa\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{c_{0}^{2}}<2.

Therefore, there exists
\kappa_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\kappa\geq\kappa_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle m_{\kappa}+m_{\kappa-1}+\dots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor}<m_{0}+m_{1}+\dots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}.

Let us suppose
n=4
. Then we have
m_{i}=(i+1)^{2}
, therefore

\displaystyle m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\kappa}=\frac{(\kappa+1)(\kappa+2)(2\kappa+3)}{6}

and

\displaystyle m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}=\frac{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor(\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor+1)(2\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor+1)}{6}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\kappa}}{m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}}\underset{\kappa\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{c_{0}^{3}}<2.

Therefore, there exists
\kappa_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\kappa\geq\kappa_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle m_{K}+m_{\kappa-1}+\dots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor}<m_{0}+m_{1}+\dots+m_{\lfloor c_{0}\kappa\rfloor-1}.

∎

Remark 22
.

In dimension
n\geq 5
, the previous lemma does not hold. Indeed, one can see that
m_{i}
is polynomial of degree
(n-2)
,
m_{i}=\frac{2i^{n-2}}{(n-2)!}+\mathcal{O}(i^{n-3})
. Hence, if we search for the best
c
such that the lemma hold, we must solve for
c
and in the limit
\kappa\to\infty
, the equation

\displaystyle\frac{2\kappa^{n-1}}{(n-1)(n-2)!}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{n-2})=\sum_{i=0}^{\kappa}m_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{c\kappa}m_{i}=\frac{2(c\kappa)^{n-1}}{(n-1)(n-2)!}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{n-2}).

This gives
c=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
. As
n\to\infty
,
c\to 1
and in fact for
n\geq 5
,
c>c_{0}
.

Lemma 23
.

For
n\in\{3,4\}
, there exists a
K_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\geq K_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}=\sup_{L\in(0,\infty)}\{B_{n}^{k}(L)\}>\lim_{L\to\infty}B_{n}^{k}(L).

Proof.

Let
n\in\{3,4\}
. Let
\kappa\geq K_{3}:=\max\{\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1}\}
, where
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}
are as in the preceding lemmas. Let
k
be such that

\displaystyle m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{\kappa-1}<k\leq m_{0}+m_{1}+\ldots m_{\kappa}.

Then, for
L
small enough, we have

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}(L)=\sigma_{\kappa}^{N}(A_{1+L/2}).

Moreover,

\displaystyle k\leq m_{0}+\ldots+m_{\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor-1}+m_{\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor}+\ldots+m_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem : tech 2}}}{{<}}2\cdot\left(m_{0}+\ldots+m_{\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor-1}\right).

Therefore, for
L
large enough, we have

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}(L)\leq\sigma_{\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor-1}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})\underset{L\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor+n-3,

as shown in
Figure

2
.

Figure 2.
Before the intersections (the region covered by the interrogation mark), the sharp upper bound is given by
\sigma_{(\kappa)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
and after, an upper bound (non necessarily sharp) is given by
\sigma_{(\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor-1)}^{N}(A_{1+L/2})
, which is lower than
b_{\kappa}
.

Moreover,
Lemma

19
allows us to state that
b_{\kappa}\geq c_{0}\kappa>\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor+n-3
because
\kappa
is large enough. Therefore,

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}\geq b_{\kappa}\geq c_{0}\kappa>\lfloor 0.8\kappa\rfloor+n-3,

and hence

\displaystyle B_{n}^{k}=B_{n}^{k}(L_{k})

for a certain
L_{k}
finite, which means that
k
has a finite critical length.

∎
