Not All Learnable Distribution Classes are Privately Learnable Thanks: Authors are listed in alphabetical order.
Mark Bun, Gautam Kamath, Argyris Mouzakis, Vikrant Singhal

Abstract
We give an example of a class of distributions that is learnable up to constant error in total variation distance with a finite number of samples, but not learnable under OPEN ( ε , δ CLOSE ) \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right) -differential privacy with the same target error. This weakly refutes a conjecture of Ashtiani.

Introduction

Given samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
belonging to some class of distributions
\mathcal{H}
, can we output a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that is close to
\mathcal{D}
in total variation distance?
This problem, known as
distribution learning
or
density estimation
, has enjoyed significant study by a number of communities, including Computer Science, Statistics, and Information Theory (see, e.g.,
[
DL01
,
KMR+94
,
DDS12
,
ABDH+20
]
).

A recent line of work studies distribution learning under
differential privacy

[
DMNS06
]
, giving sample complexity bounds for several classes of interest.
However, many of these algorithms are ad hoc, exploiting idiosyncrasies of the class of interest (see, e.g.,
[
KV18
,
KLSU19
]
).
Recent efforts have succeeded in weakening assumptions and designing increasingly general learning algorithms and frameworks (see, e.g.,
[
LKO22
,
KMS+22b
,
AL22
,
KMV22
,
AAL23a
]
).
It is natural to wonder how far this agenda can be pushed – what are the limits of private learning?
Specifically, we consider the following question:

Question 1.1
.

Is every learnable class of distributions
\mathcal{H}
also learnable under the constraint of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-differential privacy?

The answer is known to be “no” under the stronger constraint of
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP (i.e.,
pure
DP).
Bun, Kamath, Steinke, and Wu
[
BKSW19
]
showed that the covering and packing numbers of a distribution class
\mathcal{H}
give sample complexity upper and lower bounds, respectively, for learning the class
\mathcal{H}
.
Consequently, this immediately gives separations between learning and
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP learning.

1

1

The simplest natural example is the class of univariate unit-variance Gaussians with unbounded mean.

However, they do not prove any sample complexity lower bounds for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP (i.e.,
approximate
DP) learning, leaving open the possibility that every learnable distribution class is privately learnable.

On the related task of PAC learning of
functions
, a rich line of work shows that there exist strong separations between non-private learning and private learning, under both
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP
[
BBKN14
,
FX15
]
and
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
[
BNSV15
,
ALMM19
,
BLM20
]
.
In particular, for approximate DP, learnability is characterized by the Littlestone dimension, rather than the VC dimension as in the non-private setting.
However, given substantial differences in the setting, it is unclear whether these separations have any implications for private distribution learning.

At a July 2022 workshop at the Fields Institute, Ashtiani explicitly conjectured an affirmative answer to Question
1.1
: every learnable class of distributions is privately learnable
[
Ash22
]
.
Indeed, as mentioned before, the community (including contributions by Ashtiani, as well as others) has designed increasingly generic algorithms for private distribution learning
[
AL22
,
TCK+22
,
AAL23a
]
, often depending only on a non-private learner in a black-box manner.

We weakly refute Ashtiani’s conjecture.
We give an explicit class of distributions which is learnable up to constant error from a constant number of samples, but is not privately learnable with the same error guarantee with any finite number of samples.

2

A strong refutation would require showing that the class considered is learnable with a finite number of samples for
any
target error, which we do not show in the paper.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal version of Theorem
3.8
)
.

There exists a class of distributions
\mathcal{H}
such that, for an absolute constant
c
:

1.

There exists an algorithm which, given
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
samples from any distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{H}
, outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\underset{}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}}}\right)\leq c}}\right]\geq 0.9
.

2.

Any
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism that attains the same accuracy guarantee needs an infinite number of samples.

We use a “trapdoor” construction, where the class of distributions consists of mixtures over two components.
The components are entangled, in the sense that they share the same set of parameters.
The first component encodes a “key” that makes it possible to identify the other component.
The second component is hard to learn individually, even without privacy.
In our setting, the first component will be a binary product distribution over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}
, whereas the other component will be a distribution over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}
.
However, we stress that
d
will not be fixed a-priori, in the sense that our class will include distributions where
d
can be any positive integer greater than
1
.
3
3

3

We focus on the
d\geq 2
case because, for
d=1
, the two components will have overlapping supports.

The construction will be done in a way that the mixing weight will significantly favor the second component, but samples drawn from it will give very little information about the overall distribution.
Eventually, the hardness in the private setting will be a consequence of reducing from lower bounds for private mean estimation of binary product distributions (in the appropriate error metric).
We note that conceptually-similar (but technically quite different) trapdoor constructions have recently been used to show lower bounds for PAC learning
[
LBD23
]
and robust learnability
[
BDBKL23
]
.

Preliminaries

General Notation.

We denote the set of all non-zero integers by
\mathbb{Z}^{*}
.
Additionally, given a set
S
, we define
S^{i}
to be the
i
-fold Cartesian product of the set with itself
.
We use the notation
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2,\dots,n}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a\pm R}}\right]\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a-R,a+R}}\right]
.
Also, for convenience, we will use the notations like
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right)^{n}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}}}\right)^{n}\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{n\times d}
.
We use
\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
to denote a Bernoulli distribution with probability of success
p
.
Furthermore, given any set
S
, we denote the set of all distributions over that set by
\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
.
For any distribution
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\bigotimes n}
denotes the
product measure
where each
marginal distribution
is
\mathcal{D}
.
Thus, if we are given
n
independent samples from
\mathcal{D}
, we write
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}^{\bigotimes n}
.
Also, depending on the context, we may use capital Latin characters like
X
to denote either an individual sample from a distribution or a collection of samples
X\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}}\right)
.
To denote the
j
-th component of a vector, we will use a subscript (e.g.,
X_{j}
, if the vector is
X
).
Given a pair of distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}
over a space
\mathcal{X}
, their TV-distance is defined as
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}}}\right)\coloneqq\sup\limits_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{D}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)-\mathcal{D}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)}}\right|
.
If
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
are discrete, it holds that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{D}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\mathcal{D}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|
.

We conclude this section by introducing the definition of differential privacy and its
closure under post-processing
property.

Definition 2.1

(Differential Privacy (DP)
[
DMNS06
]
)
.

A mechanism
M\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
is said to satisfy
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-differential privacy (
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP) if for every pair of neighboring datasets
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
(i.e., datasets that differ in exactly one entry), we have:

\underset{M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)\in Y}}\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\underset{M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X^{\prime}}}\right)\in Y}}\right]+\delta,~~~\forall~Y\subseteq\mathcal{Y}.

When
\delta=0
, we say that
M
satisfies
\varepsilon
-differential privacy or pure differential privacy.

Lemma 2.2

(Post Processing
[
DMNS06
]
)
.

If
M\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP, and
P\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
is any randomized function, then the algorithm
P\circ M
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP.

3
The Construction and Proofs

We define the class of distributions
\mathcal{H}_{w,d}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{D}_{w,d,p}\colon p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}}}\right\}\subseteq\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)
, where each
\mathcal{D}_{w,d,p}
has pmf
q_{w,d,p}
with:

q_{w,d,p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\coloneqq\begin{cases}w,&\prod\limits_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}p_{j}^{x_{j}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-p_{j}}}\right)^{1-x_{j}},\forall x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\\
\frac{1-w}{d},&p_{1}^{\frac{1+x}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-p_{1}}}\right)^{\frac{1-x}{2}},\forall x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1}}\right\}\\
\frac{1-w}{d},&p_{2}^{\frac{1+\frac{x}{2}}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-p_{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1-\frac{x}{2}}{2}},\forall x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 2}}\right\}\\
&\vdots\\
\frac{1-w}{d},&p_{d}^{\frac{1+\frac{x}{d}}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-p_{d}}}\right)^{\frac{1-\frac{x}{d}}{2}},\forall x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm d}}\right\}\end{cases}.

Simply put, each
\mathcal{D}_{w,d,p}
is a mixture of
d+1
components.
The first component has mixing weight
w
and is a binary product distribution over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}
with probability vector
p
.
Each of the remaining components has mixing weight
\frac{1-w}{d}
and is a binary distribution that takes the value
j
with probability
p_{j}
and the value
-j
with probability
1-p_{j}
.
Note, in particular, that the probability vector
p
is shared for both components of the distribution.
In this context, the first component can be seen as the “key” to learning the distribution, because a single sample from it reveals information about the whole parameter vector, in contrast to the last
d
components which, taken together, play the role of the “hard distribution”, since a sample from it reveals information about only one component of the parameter vector.
Our goal will be to use
\mathcal{H}_{w}\coloneqq\bigcup\limits_{d\geq 2}\mathcal{H}_{w,d}
as the class that will lead to the separation.
Specifically, we will show that the sample complexity of privately learning each
\mathcal{H}_{w,d}
is dimension-dependent.
As
d
grows, the sample complexity will approach infinity.
At this point, we note that lower bounds shown for individual classes
\mathcal{H}_{w,d}
are also lower bounds for
\mathcal{H}_{w}
which, combined with our previous observation, implies that it’s impossible to learn
\mathcal{H}_{w}
with a finite number of samples.

Suppose that our target error is denoted by
\alpha
.
Our proof will focus on an instance of
\mathcal{H}_{w}
with
w=\frac{\alpha}{2}
.
Specifically, focusing on the sub-class
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
for
d\geq 2
, we will first show a lower bound of
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\sqrt{\alpha}\varepsilon}}}\right)
for density estimation up to error
\alpha
with probability of success
0.9
for this class under
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP (Corollary
3.5
), and then argue that the non-private sample complexity for the same task is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{3}}}}\right)
(Lemma
3.7
).
We conclude by formally establishing the desired separation in Theorem
3.8
.

We start by showing the lower bound under privacy.
Doing so involves an argument which establishes a reduction from parameter estimation for binary product distributions to density estimation for the class
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
.
Formulating the reduction first necessitates showing how a mechanism that performs density estimation for the class
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
can be used to construct a mechanism that estimates the parameter
p
of distributions in this class.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2,p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
, and
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\otimes n}
.
If
M\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
is an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism that outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\alpha\leq 1
, then it is possible to output a
\widehat{p}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
such that
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{1}}}\right]\leq 2d\alpha
, while preserving
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP.

Proof.

We observe that all the distributions in the class are mixtures with two components that have disjoint supports, and that the mixing weights are the same for all distributions.
As a consequence, given a pair
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
, we have the following for the corresponding distributions:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p_{1}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p_{2}}}}\right)=\frac{\alpha}{2}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigotimes\limits_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{1,j}}}\right),\bigotimes\limits_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{2,j}}}\right)}}\right)+\frac{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|p_{1}-p_{2}}}\right\|_{1}.

(1)

Based on the above, if we have a distribution
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\equiv\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,\widehat{p}}
, such that
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\alpha
, it must always be the case that
\frac{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}{d}\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{1}}}\right]\leq\alpha\implies\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{1}}}\right]\leq\frac{d\alpha}{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\leq 2d\alpha
.
Thus, all we have to do is identify the probability vector
\widehat{p}
that corresponds to
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
and output it, while privacy is preserved thanks to Lemma
2.2
.
∎

To complete the reduction, we need to show how, given a mechanism that performs density estimation for the class
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
, it is possible to use it to perform
\ell_{1}
-parameter estimation for binary product distributions.
This is done in the following lemma:

Lemma 3.2
.

For
d\geq 2
, let
P
be a binary product distribution over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}
with mean vector
p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
, and let
X\sim P^{\otimes n}
.
If any
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism
T\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{n\times d}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
with
\underset{X,T}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{1}}}\right]\leq 2d\alpha
requires at least
n\geq n_{0}
samples, the same sample complexity lower bound holds for any
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism
M\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
that satisfies
\underset{Y,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right),\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\alpha\leq 1
, where
Y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\otimes n}
.

Proof.

To establish our result, it suffices to show that estimating the parameter vector of
P
can be transformed into an instance of density estimation for distributions in
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
, implying that lower bounds for the former problem also apply to the latter.
To do so, we assume we have an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism
M\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
with
\underset{Y,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right),\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\alpha\leq 1
for
Y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\otimes n}
.
We will show how to use this mechanism to construct an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism
T\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{n\times d}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
with
\underset{X,T}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{1}}}\right]\leq 2d\alpha
for
X\sim P^{\otimes n}
.

The crux of the argument involves proving that, given a dataset
X\sim P^{\otimes n}
, it is possible to generate a dataset
Y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\otimes n}
.
The mechanism
T
will consist of this sampling step (pre-processing), and an application of
M
over the resulting dataset.
Appealing to Lemma
3.1
suffices to establish that
T
will have the desired accuracy guarantee, so the rest of the proof is devoted to describing the sampling process.

Given any datapoint
X_{i}
, we set
Y_{i}
equal to it with probability
\frac{\alpha}{2}
, or, with probability
1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
, we choose one of the coordinates of
X_{i}
uniformly at random (say the
j
-th coordinate).
If the
j
-th coordinate of
X_{i}
is equal to
1
, we set
Y_{i}=j
.
Otherwise, we set
Y_{i}=-j
.
The resulting dataset
Y
will follow the desired distribution.
We stress that this process preserves privacy guarantees, because changing a point of
X
can result in at most one point of
Y
changing (conditioned on the randomness involved in the conversion of
X
to
Y
).
∎

At this point, we recall the following result from
[
KLSU19
]
:

Proposition 3.3
.

[Lemma
6.2
from
[
KLSU19
]
]
Let
p
be any vector in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
, and let
X\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}}}\right)
be a dataset consisting of
n
independent samples from a binary product distribution
P
over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}
with mean
p
.
If
M\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{n\times d}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
is an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism with
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
and
\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}}}\right)
that satisfies
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]\leq\alpha^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right),\forall p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
, it must hold that
n\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\alpha\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

While phrased in terms of mechanisms with mean-squared-error guarantees, the above result also implies a bound for
\ell_{1}
-estimation.
The connection is described in the following lemma:

Lemma 3.4
.

For
d\geq 2
, an absolute constant
C_{\ref{c}}>0
, and any
\alpha\leq C_{\ref{c}}
, consider the class of distributions
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
.
Let
p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
, and let
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\otimes n}
.
If
M\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
is an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism with
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
and
\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}}}\right)
that outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\alpha,\forall p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
, it must hold that
n\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\alpha}\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

Proof.

We recall the inequality
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|_{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|_{1},\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This is a consequence of Hölder’s inequality, but can also be shown in an elementary way by remarking that:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}x_{i}^{2}\leq\max\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x_{i}}}\right|}}\right\}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x_{i}}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|_{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|_{1}.

Now, let
X
be a dataset of size
n
that has been drawn i.i.d. from a binary product distribution
P
with mean vector
p
, and let
T\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{n\times d}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d}
be an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism with
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right],\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}}}\right)
that satisfies
\underset{X,T}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{1}}}\right]\leq 2d\alpha
.
We have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1
which, by an application of the above inequality, yields
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{1}
.
This implies that
T
satisfies the guarantee
\underset{X,T}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)-p}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]\leq 2d\alpha
.
Consequently, the lower bound of Proposition
3.3
applies to
T
if we set
\alpha\to\sqrt{2d\alpha}
.
Then, appealing to Lemma
3.2
completes the proof.
∎

The lower bound of Lemma
3.4
also holds for mechanisms that achieve the accuracy guarantee
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)\leq\alpha}}\right]\geq 0.9
, albeit at the cost of getting a result that’s weaker by a log-factor.
The argument is sketched in the proof of Theorem
6.1
of
[
KLSU19
]
, so we point readers there and do not repeat it here.
The resulting sample complexity bound is
n\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\sqrt{\alpha}\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

We summarize the above remarks in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

For
d\geq 2
, an absolute constant
C_{\ref{c}}>0
, and any
\alpha\leq C_{\ref{c}}
, consider the class of distributions
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
.
Let
p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
, and let
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\otimes n}
.
If
M\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
is an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism with
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
and
\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}}}\right)
that outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)\leq\alpha}}\right]\geq 0.9,\forall p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}}}\right]^{d}
, it must hold that
n\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\sqrt{\alpha}\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

Remark 3.6
.

While the lower bounds in the above statements are phrased in terms of
proper learners
, they also imply the same bounds against
improper learners
.
If computation is not a concern, an improper learner can be converted to a proper one by enumerating over all densities in the class and projecting to whichever one is closest with respect to the TV-distance.
Since the TV-distance satisfies the triangle inequality, this can lead to the error increasing by a factor of
2
.

We now proceed to argue that the non-private sample complexity of proper density estimation with respect to the TV-distance for the class
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
is independent of
d
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For
d\geq 2
, there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
which, given a dataset
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}^{\bigotimes n}
of size
n=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\alpha^{3}}}}\right)
, outputs a distribution
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\equiv\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,\widehat{p}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d}
such that:

\underset{X}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,\widehat{p}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)\leq\alpha}}\right]\geq 1-\beta.

Proof.

By (
1
), we have:

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,\widehat{p}},\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}}}\right)=\frac{\alpha}{2}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigotimes\limits_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{p}_{j}}}\right),\bigotimes\limits_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{j}}}\right)}}\right)+\frac{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{1}.

Based on the above, in order to attain error
\alpha
in TV-distance, it suffices to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
estimate
\bigotimes\limits_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]}\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{j}}}\right)
up to error
1
in TV-distance, and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
estimate the vector
p
up to error
\frac{d\alpha}{2}
in
\ell_{1}
-distance.
Statement
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
holds trivially, since all distributions are at TV-distance
1
from each other, so we focus on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
.

For
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
, it holds that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{2}
, so it suffices to have a
\widehat{p}
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}\alpha}{2}
.
Assume, now, that we are given
m
samples drawn i.i.d. from a binary product distribution, and that we want to estimate its parameter vector within
\ell_{2}
-error
\alpha
with probability at least
1-\frac{\beta}{2}
.
It is a folklore fact that
m=\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d+\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\alpha^{2}}}}\right)
samples, are both necessary and sufficient for this task, with the bound being attained by taking the sample mean.
Thus, setting
\alpha\to\frac{\sqrt{d}\alpha}{2}
yields
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d+\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{d\alpha^{2}}}}\right)
, which is dominated by
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\alpha^{2}}}}\right)
.
Consequently, in order to get
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{p}-p}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}\alpha}{2}
in our setting, it suffices to have
m=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\alpha^{2}}}}\right)
samples from the first component (the binary product distribution).
For that reason, assume that, for each datapoint
X_{i}
we draw from
\mathcal{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{2},d,p}
, we have an associated random variable
Z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\right)
which becomes
1
if
X_{i}
comes from the first component.
We assume now that we have
n
samples with
\frac{n\alpha}{2}\geq m
.
We will show that
n=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\alpha^{3}}}}\right)
suffices to ensure that the event
\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}Z_{i}<m
doesn’t happen, except with probability at most
\frac{\beta}{2}
.
The Hoeffding bound implies that:

\underset{}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}Z_{i}<m}}\right]\leq\underset{}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}Z_{i}-\frac{n\alpha}{2}}}\right|\geq\frac{n\alpha}{2}-m}}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\alpha-2m}}\right)^{2}}{2n}}.

To ensure that the above is upper-bounded by
\frac{\beta}{2}
, it suffices to have
n\geq\frac{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\alpha m+\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\beta}}}\right)}}\right)}{\alpha^{2}}=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\alpha^{3}}}}\right)
.
By a union bound, the total probability of failure is upper-bounded by
\beta
, completing the proof.
∎

We are now ready to establish our main result.

Theorem 3.8
.

Given any
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
, we have:

1.

There exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}\colon\bigcup\limits_{d\geq 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
which, given
n=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
samples drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
, outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
such that
\underset{X}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}}\right]\geq 0.9
.

2.

For finite
n
, there exists no
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism
M\colon\bigcup\limits_{d\geq 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
with
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right],\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}}}\right)
which, given a dataset
X\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}
, outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
that satisfies
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}}\right]\geq 0.9
.

Proof.

Let a (potentially adversarially chosen)
\mathcal{D}\equiv\mathcal{D}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}},d,p}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}},d}
be our ground truth.

Without privacy constraints, all the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has to do is look at the samples to identify the number of components
d
, and then calculate the sample mean corresponding to the samples from the first component (as we did in the proof of Lemma
3.7
).
The desired guarantee is immediate by the guarantees of that lemma.

Under privacy, we will establish our result by contradiction.
Assume that, for some finite
n
, there exists an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP mechanism
M\colon\bigcup\limits_{d\geq 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}^{d}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm d}}\right\}}}\right)^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
with
\varepsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right],\delta=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}}}\right)
which, given
X\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}
, outputs a
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
such that
\underset{X,M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}}\right]\geq 0.9
.
We note that
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
might not be in
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}},d}
(since the output range is assumed to be the entire
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}}}
).
However, working as we described in Remark
3.6
, we can round the output to an element of
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}C_{\ref{c}},d}
, with the TV-distance between the resulting distribution and the ground truth now being
C_{\ref{c}}
(the privacy guarantee is preserved thanks to Lemma
2.2
).
Then, by Corollary
3.5
, it must be the case that
n\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}}}\right)
.
This must hold for every
d\geq 2
, so taking
d\to\infty
leads to a contradiction.
∎
