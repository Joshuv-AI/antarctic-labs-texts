Graphical tools for detection and control of selection bias with multiple exposures and samples
Patrick M. Schnell, Eben Kenah

Abstract
Among recent developments in definitions and analysis of selection bias is the potential outcomes approach of Kenah ( Epidemiology , 2023), which allows non-parametric analysis using single-world intervention graphs, linking selection of study participants to identification of causal effects. Mohan & Pearl ( JASA , 2021) provide a framework for missing data via directed acyclic graphs augmented with nodes indicating missingness for each sometimes-missing variable, which allows for analysis of more general missing data problems but cannot easily encode scenarios in which different groups of variables are observed in specific subsamples. We give an alternative formulation of the potential outcomes framework based on conditional separable effects and indicators for selection into subsamples. This is practical for problems between the single-sample scenarios considered by Kenah and the variable-wise missingness considered by Mohan & Pearl. This simplifies identification conditions and admits generalizations to scenarios with multiple, potentially nested or overlapping study samples, as well as multiple or time-dependent exposures. We give examples of identifiability arguments for case-cohort studies, multiple or time-dependent exposures, and direct effects of selection.



Keywords:
causal inference, selection bias, conditional separable effects, directed acyclic graph, single world intervention graph

1
Introduction

Selection bias is a fundamental threat to valid epidemiologic inferences.
Recently, significant attention has returned to detecting and controlling selection bias using graphical tools similar to those widely used to analyze confounding.

Hernán et al., (2004)
present a structural approach via directed acyclic graphs (DAGs,
Greenland et al., (1999)
) and show that selection bias occurs when (but not only when) conditioning on selection into a sample opens a non-causal path from exposure to outcome.
However, the structural approach can only detect selection bias that can occur in the absence of a causal effect of exposure on outcome
(
Greenland,, 1977
;
Kenah,, 2023
)
.

Lu et al., (2022)
also use DAGs to distinguish between failures of internal validity (collider bias, or “type 1 selection bias”) and external validity (generalizability bias, or “type 2 selection bias”).

Sjölander, (2023)
notes that a weakness of this approach is that its relationship to potential outcomes and associated exchangeability assumptions is opaque as they do not appear in the DAGs.

Kenah, (2023)
proposes a definition of selection bias in terms of potential outcomes which captures selection bias under both the structural definition of
Hernán et al., (2004)
and the traditional difference-in-measures definition and allows for the simultaneous analysis of confounding and selection bias using single-world intervention graphs (SWIGs,
Richardson and Robins, (2013)
).

Sjölander, (2023)
uses counterfactual graphs
(
Shpitser and Pearl,, 2007
)
to contrast outcome-associated selection (where there is an open non-causal path from the outcome to selection) and outcome-influenced selection (where at least one open path from the outcome to selection is causal), and shows that causal effects in the selected population are identifiable under the former but not the latter.
Further, there is no information about causal effects in the full population under outcome-associated selection, but, as is shown by
Kenah, (2023)
, under outcome-influenced sampling the causal odds ratio in the source population is identifiable.

Sjölander, (2023)
does not consider the impact of other sources of bias, including confounding.

Hernán et al., (2004)
,
Kenah, (2023)
, and
Sjölander, (2023)
consider identifiability in a single selected population or the source population from a single sample in which all variables are fully observed.

Mohan and Pearl, (2021)
develop the concept of an m-graph which provides for each partially missing variable
V
a missingness indicator
H_{V}
(for “hidden” in our notation) and a proxy variable
V^{*}
defined as
V
if
H_{V}=0
and some placeholder
\eta
if
H_{V}=1
.
Classification of missing data mechanisms into missing completely at random, missing not at random, and a variation of missing at random are expressible in terms of unconditional and conditional independencies among fully observed, partially observed, and fully unobserved variables, along with missingness indicators.
Recoverability (a broadening of “identifiability” to apply to non-causal relationships) results are also presented in terms of such independencies, which can be evaluated from m-graphs.
The m-graph approach allows for analysis of general missing data problems, and it can be adapted to situations in which all variables are either observed or unobserved simultaneously (as in selection).
However, it cannot easily encode scenarios in which different groups of variables are observed in specific subsamples.

Transportability literature (e.g.,
Bareinboim and Pearl, (2013)
) considers inferences made about one population from another, and often considers cases with two populations in which different sets of variables are observed, or there are slightly different causal structures (e.g., randomized versus non-randomized exposures).
One common scenario is extension of inferences from a randomized trial to a target population
(
Dahabreh and Hernán,, 2019
)
.

Our objective is to provide graphical tools to aid in detection and control of selection bias among multiple samples in which different sets of variables may be observed.
We do so by revisiting the potential outcomes approach of
Kenah, (2023)
through the lens of separable effects
(
Robins et al.,, 2022
;
Stensrud et al.,, 2022
;
Stensrud et al.,, 2023
)
, a recent development in causal mediation analysis that helps to clarify when conditioning on (possibly post-exposure) variables does not irrecoverably obscure the causal effect of exposure.
In doing so, we clarify and relax the identifiability conditions of
Kenah, (2023)
.
Our approach also allows for analysis of selection bias in the presence of time-dependent or otherwise multiple exposures beyond the simple case of monotone censoring
(
Robins et al.,, 2000
;
Hernán et al.,, 2001
)
.

Two obstacles pose difficulties for applying m-graphs directly to the type of problem we consider.
First, we wish to work primarily with single world intervention graphs (or another causal graph that represents potential outcomes explicitly) to facilitate the analysis of causal selection problems in terms of potential outcomes.
The interpretation and use of missingness and proxy variables for potential outcomes is unclear because, even without missingness as usually conceptualized, the observability of potential outcomes is determined by the actual exposure.
Second, m-graphs excel at encoding unconstrained independencies between missingness and other variables, but they struggle to encode more complex relationships.

Such a scenario requires two m-graphs to properly encode (Figure

3.2
Clarification of the analytic case-control condition

The case-control isolation condition clarifies the function of the secondary independence condition (
4
) that
\boldsymbol{W}^{a}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{X})
in the analytic case-control condition.
The analytic case-control condition includes the independence
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
, which conditions on the observed variable
Y
not appearing on our separated SWIGs.
The following lemma, proved in Appendix
A.5
, connects a conditional independence relationship between observed variables
S
and
Y
to one between the potential variables

and
Y^{b}
appearing on the separated SWIG:

Lemma 1

(Observable conditioning)
.

Under Condition
5
(case-control isolation) that all causal paths from
B
to
S
pass through
Y
, for any (possibly empty) set of variables
\boldsymbol{K}
not containing descendants of
b
, we have
S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y^{b},\boldsymbol{K})
if and only if
S\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{K})
.

Then, under case-control isolation, if we can find a suitable set of variables
\boldsymbol{K}
not containing descendants of
b
so that
S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y^{b},\boldsymbol{K})
, we can equate the distributions
[A\;|\;Y,\boldsymbol{K}]\sim[A\;|\;Y,\boldsymbol{K},S=1]
.
The exposure odds ratio is equal to the (observed, not necessarily causal) outcome odds ratio, so if exchangeability holds given
\boldsymbol{K}
we can identify the population causal odds ratio.
We collect the necessary conditions into the following analytic case-control condition with relaxed non-descent conditions expressed in terms of our
b
-SWIG approach.

Condition 7

(Separable case-control condition)

The case-control isolation condition holds.

\displaystyle A\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}

\displaystyle\;|\;\boldsymbol{K},

(9)

\displaystyle S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A

\displaystyle\;|\;Y^{b},\boldsymbol{K}.

(10)

The above pair of independencies is equivalent to

Theorem 1

(Identification between samples)
.

A functional
\psi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;\middle|\;S_{0}=1\right)
of the distribution of
\left[\boldsymbol{V}\;|\;S_{0}=1\right]
is identifiable if the components of the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be written as

\theta_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{jL}\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{V}_{jR},S_{0}=1\right),

(12)

where

\theta_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{jL}\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{V}_{jR},S_{j}=1\right)

(13)

is identifiable and

\displaystyle S_{0}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{V}_{jL}

\displaystyle\;|\;(\boldsymbol{V}_{jR},S_{j}=1),

(14)

\displaystyle S_{j}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{V}_{jL}

\displaystyle\;|\;(\boldsymbol{V}_{jR},S_{0}=1).

(15)

We allow

\boldsymbol{V}_{jL}
, and
\boldsymbol{V}_{jR}

\theta_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{jL}\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{V}_{jR},S_{j}=1,S_{0}=1\right)

5
Conclusion

Using the concept of separability
(
Robins et al.,, 2022
;
Stensrud et al.,, 2022
;
Stensrud et al.,, 2023
)
, we have developed an alternative formulation of the potential outcomes approach to selection bias of
Kenah, (2023)
.
As illustrated by examples, this new formulation enables nonparametric analysis of selection bias even in the case of multiple or time-dependent exposures, complex relationships between samples in which different sets of variables are observed, or causal effects of selection on the outcome.
Though not shown, it is also straightforward to deduce from the separable cohort and case-control conditions the results of
Mathur and Shpitser, (2024)
for identifiability of the average treatment effect in the selected population, and the distinction of
Sjölander, (2023)
between identifiability under outcome-associated selection and outcome-influenced selection.
The approach is flexible and multiple paths may be available to establishing identifiability of causal effects.
The overall strategy is straightforward:

1.

Beginning with the DAG representing the causal system, add selection variables indicating membership into each sample;

2.

Construct the
b
-SWIG such that totality is satisfied, applying graph transformations to multiple exposures in reverse topological order;

3.

Optionally evaluate the cohort and case-control isolation conditions to narrow the space of possibly identifiable estimands;

4.

Attempt to express the quantity of interest in terms satisfying the conditions of Theorem
1
.

Only the final step may require cleverness, as it is not always obvious whether or how such an expression can be constructed, such as in our example of identifying causal risks from a case-cohort study.
However, the separable cohort and case-control conditions, or the g-formula, may often be useful starting points, and successful establishment of identifiability by this approach constructs a template for a plug-in estimator.
Development of additional shortcuts and heuristics, as well as formal derivations of sequential versions of these cohort and case-control conditions, would be useful avenues for future work.

A.2
Proofs of sufficiency of separable cohort and case-control conditions

Suppose the following conditions hold:

1.

Totality:

Y^{b}=Y^{a}
;

2.

Cohort isolation:

S^{b}=S
;

3.

Separable cohort condition:

(S^{b},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
.

By totality and cohort isolation, the separable cohort condition may be written
(S,A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
.
Then
\mathbbm{P}[Y^{a}=y]=\mathbbm{P}[Y^{a}=y\;|\;A=a,S=1]=\mathbbm{P}[Y=y\;|\;A=a,S=1]
by the separable cohort condition and consistency.
Alternatively, working entirely from the
b
-SWIG,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}[Y=y\;|\;A=b,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k},S=1]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}[Y^{b}=y\;|\;A=b,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k},S=1],

(A.7)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}[Y^{b}=y\;|\;A=b,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}],

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}[Y^{b}=y\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}].

By totality,
Y^{b}=Y^{a}
.
If
\boldsymbol{K}
can be decomposed into
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
such that
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{W}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
, then the potential outcome distribution in the final line can be equated to one conditioning only on
\boldsymbol{X}
instead of
\boldsymbol{K}
.
If the decomposition satisfies
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
and
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{W}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{X})
then the substitution of
\boldsymbol{X}
for
\boldsymbol{K}
may be made at the penultimate line and carried through.

Now suppose instead that the following conditions hold:

1.

Totality:

Y^{b}=Y^{a}
;

2.

Case-control isolation:
All causal paths from
B
to
S
pass through
Y
;

3.

Separable case-control condition:

(S^{b},Y^{b})\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
.

By Lemma
1
(which assumes case-control isolation), Bayes’ Theorem, consistency, and exchangeability (implied by the separable case-control condition),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbbm{P}[A=1\;|\;Y=1,K=k,S=1]/\mathbbm{P}[A=0\;|\;Y=1,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k},S=1]}{\mathbbm{P}[A=1\;|\;Y=0,K=k,S=1]/\mathbbm{P}[A=0\;|\;Y=0,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k},S=1]}

(A.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{P}[A=1\;|\;Y=1,K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[A=0\;|\;Y=1,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]}{\mathbbm{P}[A=1\;|\;Y=0,K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[A=0\;|\;Y=0,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{P}[Y=1\;|\;A=1,K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[Y=0\;|\;A=1,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]}{\mathbbm{P}[Y=1\;|\;A=0,K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[Y=0\;|\;A=0,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]},

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{P}[Y^{1}=1\;|\;A=1,K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[Y^{1}=0\;|\;A=1,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]}{\mathbbm{P}[Y^{0}=1\;|\;A=0,K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[Y^{0}=0\;|\;A=0,\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]},

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{P}[Y^{1}=1\;|\;K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[Y^{1}=0\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]}{\mathbbm{P}[Y^{0}=1\;|\;K=k]/\mathbbm{P}[Y^{0}=0\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{k}]}.

By totality,
Y^{1}
equivalently represents
Y^{b=1}
or
Y^{a=1}
, and similarly for
Y^{0}
.

If
\boldsymbol{K}
can be decomposed into
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
with
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp W\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
, then the causal odds ratio conditional on
\boldsymbol{K}
in the final line above may be further equated to the causal odds ratio conditional on
\boldsymbol{X}
only.
If the decomposition satisfies
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
and
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{W}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{X})
then the substitution may be made at the penultimate line and carried through.

A.3
Equivalence of conditions with strict non-descent requirements

A.3.1
Cohort conditions

The analytic cohort condition requires
(S^{a},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
with
\boldsymbol{X}
containing no descendants of
A
.
The separable cohort condition requires
(S^{b},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
, where
\boldsymbol{K}
contains no descendants of
b
(whose descendants are a subset of those of
A
).
If we require
\boldsymbol{K}
to contain no descendants of
A
, then to show that the conditions are equivalent, we must show that
(S^{b},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
if and only if
(S^{a},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
.
We divide this task into establishing the equivalence of
A\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
and
A\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
, then the equivalence of
S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{K})
and
S^{a}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{K})
.

To show that
A\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
if and only if
A\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
, note that, conditioning on
\boldsymbol{K}
, there are one-to-one correspondences among the following sets:

1.

Open paths between
A
and
Y^{a}
on the
a
-SWIG
\mathcal{G}^{a}
,

2.

Open back-door paths from
Y
into
A
on the DAG
\mathcal{G}
,

3.

Open back-door paths from
Y
into
A
on the separated DAG
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}
,

4.

Open back-door paths from
Y
into
B
on
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}
,

5.

Open paths between
B
and
Y^{b}
on the
b
-SWIG
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}^{b}
.

Thus the equivalence.

To show that
S\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{K})
if and only if
S^{a}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{K})
, note that, conditioning on
(A,\boldsymbol{K})
, there are one-to-one correspondences among the following sets:

1.

Open paths between
S^{a}
and
Y^{a}
on the
a
-SWIG
\mathcal{G}^{a}
,

2.

Open paths between
S
and
Y
on the DAG
\mathcal{G}
,

3.

Open paths between
S
and
Y
on the separated DAG
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}
,

4.

Open paths between
S
and
Y^{b}
on the
b
-SWIG
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}^{b}
.

Thus the equivalence.

A.3.2
Case-control conditions

The analytic case-control condition requires
(\boldsymbol{W}^{a},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
with
\boldsymbol{X}
containing no descendants of
A
and
\boldsymbol{W}
containing no descendants of
Y
with
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
.
The separable case-control condition requires case-control isolation (all causal paths from
B
to
S
pass through
Y
) and
(Y^{b},S^{b})\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;\boldsymbol{K}
.

The two conditions imply different identifiability results: The analytic condition implies that the source population causal odds ratio given
\boldsymbol{X}
is equal to the exposure odds ratio given
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
in the selected population, while the separable condition implies that the source population causal odds ratio given
\boldsymbol{K}
is equal to the exposure odds ratio given
\boldsymbol{K}
in the selected population.
As shown in the proof of sufficiency of the separable case-control condition, if we require
\boldsymbol{K}=(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
with
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
and
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{W}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{X})
, then we get the result implied by the analytic case-control condition.
We wish to show that if we also require that
\boldsymbol{X}
contains no descendants of
A
and
\boldsymbol{W}
contains no descendants of
Y
, it is equivalent to the analytic case-control condition.

First, note that
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
and
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp W\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{X})
if and only if
(\boldsymbol{W},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
, which is equivalent to the requirement
(\boldsymbol{W}^{a},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
in the analytic case-control condition.
This equivalence follows from the same argument that establishes the equivalence of
(S^{b},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
and
(S^{a},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{a}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
in the cohort conditions (where
S^{b}=S
due to cohort isolation).
It remains is to show that given the requirements of the previous paragraph, case-control isolation and
(Y^{b},S^{b})\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
together are equivalent to
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
.
The independence
(Y^{b},S^{b})\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
is equivalent to
S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y^{b},\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
and
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
.
Because
(\boldsymbol{W},A)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{b}\;|\;\boldsymbol{X}
, the latter is already implied by both the analytic and separable case-control conditions.
Thus the only thing left to show that case-control isolation and
S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y^{b},\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
together are equivalent to
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
.

We now show by contradiction that
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
implies case-control isolation under previously-established requirements.
Suppose that case-control isolation does not hold.
Then there exists a causal path from
B
to
S
that does not go through
Y
and therefore is not closed by conditioning on
Y
.
Since we have established that
Y^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{W}\;|\;(A,\boldsymbol{X})
and
\boldsymbol{X}
does not contain any descendants of
A
,
W
cannot contain a descendant of
B
and thus cannot close any causal paths from
B
to
S
(nor can
\boldsymbol{X}
).
Thus there is an open causal path between
B
and
S
(which implies one from
A
to
S
) that does not pass through
Y
and remains after the node-splitting operation that yields
\mathcal{G}^{y}
.
But then
S^{y}\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
, which is a contradiction.
Therefore
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
implies case-control isolation.

Finally, we show that
S^{b}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y^{b},\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
implies
S^{y}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\;|\;(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
under case-control isolation.
Note that there are one-to-one correspondences among the following sets (regardless of case-control isolation):

1.

Open paths betwen
A
and
S^{y}
on the
y
-SWIG
\mathcal{G}^{y}
, conditioning on
(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
;

2.

Open paths between
A
and
S
on DAG
\mathcal{G}
, conditioning on
(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
;,

3.

Open paths between
A
and
S
on the separated DAG
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}
, conditioning on
(Y,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
.

Under case-control isolation, the final set above has a one-to-one correspondence with open paths between
A
and
S^{b}
on
\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{ex}}^{b}
after conditioning on
(Y^{b},\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{W})
, because any path from
A
to
S
broken by the node-splitting operation on
B
must be a causal path not passing through
Y
(which is prohibited by case-control isolation).
Thus the equivalence.

from

\mathcal{G}^{a}

.
