1.
Introduction

For an integer
r\geq 2
, we say an integer
n
is
r
-free if it has no factor
d>1
which is an
r
-th power. In 1998 Brüdern, Granville, Perelli, Vaughan and Wooley
[
2
]
studied bounds of the
L_{1}
norm for the exponential sum over
r
-free numbers and gave the first nontrivial bounds. In 2001 Balog and Ruzsa
[
1
]
improved on the bounds and gave the best possible bound for the
L_{1}
norm of the exponential sum over
r
-free numbers.

Let
r\geq 2
be fixed and
a_{n}
be the characteristic function of the
r
-free integers, that is

a_{n}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n\text{ is }r\text{-free},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(1.1)

Balog and Ruzsa
[
1
]
proved that

Theorem 1.1

(Balog-Ruzsa)
.

Let
N\geq 2
. Then

N^{\frac{1}{r+1}}\ll\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha\ll N^{\frac{1}{r+1}}

The most interesting case is
r=2
, which is related to the squarefree numbers and the Möbius function
\mu(n)
. As a corollary their theorem implies that

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N\geq 2
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu(n)e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha\gg N^{\frac{1}{6}}.

In this paper, we will give a short interval version of the Balog-Rusza theorem. We are most interested in the lower bound of the
L_{1}
norm of the exponential sum over squarefree numbers and the exponential sum of the Möbius function, and obtain the following results.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
N\geq H\geq N^{\frac{9}{17}+\varepsilon}
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|<H}\mu(n)e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha\gg H^{\frac{1}{6}}.

Theorem
1.3
is essentially a corollary of the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
N\geq H\geq N^{\frac{9}{17}+\varepsilon}
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|<H}\mu^{2}(n)e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha\gg H^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(1.2)

We will prove Theorem
1.4
in Section
4
and show how Theorem
1.4
implies Theorem
1.3
in the end of this section.
As a short interval analog of Balog-Ruzsa theorem we also get the following theorem

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and

N\geq N\geq\begin{cases}N^{\frac{18}{29}+\epsilon}&\text{if }r=2,\\
N^{\frac{r+1}{2r}+\epsilon}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1.3)

Then

H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}\ll\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}a_{n}e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha\ll H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}.

Remark
.

The result for
r\geq 3
follows from Balog-Ruzsa’s arguments. However, for
r=2
, our results are much stronger than the trivial result
\frac{r+1}{2r}=\frac{3}{4}=0.75
. For
r=2
, we note that our exponent is
\frac{9}{17}=0.529\dots
in lower bound case, and
\frac{18}{29}=0.620\dots
in upper bound case.

Besides, for
r=2
, Theorem
1.4
gives the lower bound (
1.2
) for shorter intervals than Theorem
1.5
. Thus from Theorem
1.5
we can not get Theorem
1.3
.

Balog-Ruzsa’s argument utilizes the Fejér Kernel which has many good properties (see
[
8
]
) and has many applications in number theory (see
[
7
]
).

Let
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
. The Fejér Kernel
F(\alpha)
is the Cesàro mean of the Dirichlet Kernel
D_{N}(\alpha)=\sum_{|n|\leq N}e(n\alpha)
and is defined by

F(\alpha):=\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left(1-\frac{|n|}{N}\right)e(n\alpha)=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi N\alpha)}{N\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)}\leq\min\left\{N,\frac{1}{N\|\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}.

(1.4)

For the short interval case, we need to introduce a short interval version of the Fejér Kernel which is motivated by the perspective of the Fourier analysis. For convenience we first introduce some notions in Fourier analysis.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
be finite. For
f:A\to\mathbb{C}
, we define the Fourier transform
\hat{f}:{\mathbb{T}}\to\mathbb{C}
of
f
by

\hat{f}(\alpha)=\sum_{n\in A}f(n)e(n\alpha).

If
f,g:A\to\mathbb{C}
, we define the convolution of
f
and
g
to be

f\ast g(n)=\sum_{x+y=n}f(x)g(y),

A basic property involving Fourier transform and convolution is that

\widehat{f\ast g}(\alpha)=\hat{f}(\alpha)\hat{g}(\alpha).

Similarly, we define the convolution of the Fourier transform as

\hat{f}\ast\hat{g}(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\hat{f}(\alpha-\beta)\hat{g}(\beta)d\beta=\widehat{fg}(\alpha).

(1.5)

Now the Fejér Kernel
F(\alpha)
can be described through the “Fourier language”.

\displaystyle F(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\widehat{{\bf 1}_{[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2})}*{\bf 1}_{(-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}]}}=\frac{1}{N}\hat{{\bf 1}}_{[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2})}\bar{\hat{{\bf 1}}}_{[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{n\in(-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}]}e(n\alpha)\right|^{2}=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi N\alpha)}{N\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)}\ll\min\left\{N,\frac{1}{N\|\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}.

(1.6)

Let us introduce some properties of the difference of two Fejér Kernels, which will be used in Section
5
to prove the upper bound case of Theorem
1.5
.
By the difference of the two Fejér Kernels, we mean

\frac{1}{K}\left(\widehat{{\bf 1}_{(-\frac{N+K}{2},\frac{N+K}{2}]}*{\bf 1}_{[-\frac{N+K}{2},\frac{N+K}{2})}}-\widehat{{\bf 1}_{(-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}]}*{\bf 1}_{[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2})}}\right).

(1.7)

From (
1.4
) and (
1.6
) one can easily get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\left(\widehat{{\bf 1}_{(-\frac{N+K}{2},\frac{N+K}{2}]}*{\bf 1}_{[-\frac{N+K}{2},\frac{N+K}{2})}}-\widehat{{\bf 1}_{(-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}]}*{\bf 1}_{[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2})}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|n|\leq N+K}\min\left\{1,\frac{N+K-|n|}{K}\right\}e(n\alpha)=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi(N+K)\alpha)-\sin^{2}(\pi K\alpha)}{K\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi(2N+K)\alpha)\sin(\pi K\alpha)}{K\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)}\ll\min\left\{N+K,\frac{1}{\|\alpha\|},\frac{1}{K\|\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}

(1.8)

Now we define our short interval version of the Fejér Kernel
F_{H}(\alpha)
, For convenience we can assume that both
N
and
H
are even integers. We write

F_{H}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{H}\widehat{{\bf 1}_{(\frac{N-H}{2},\frac{N+H}{2}]}\ast{\bf 1}_{[\frac{N-H}{2},\frac{N+H}{2})}}(\alpha).

(1.9)

Similar to (
1.6
), our
F_{H}(\alpha)
also has two explicit formulas and an upper bound. Namely,

F_{H}(\alpha)=e(N\alpha)\sum_{|h|\leq H}\left(1-\frac{h}{H}\right)e(h\alpha)=\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}\left(1-\frac{|n-N|}{H}\right)e(n\alpha)

(1.10)

and then

\displaystyle F_{H}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H}e(N\alpha)\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi H\alpha)}{\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)}

(1.11)

\displaystyle\ll\min\left\{H,\frac{1}{H\|\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}.

(1.12)

Remark
.

By using this
F_{H}
we can reserve almost all properties from Fejér Kernel, but from (
1.11
) we can see that this
F_{H}
is not always real, which might be the only property it loses from Fejér Kernel.

Recall the definition of
a_{n}
from (
1.1
) and let
g_{1}(\alpha)=\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}a_{n}e(n\alpha)
.
Before proving our results, we should notice that
\|g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}
and
\|F_{H}\ast g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}
are comparable. Namely, we have

\|F_{H}\ast g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|F_{H}(\alpha-\beta)||g_{1}(\beta)|d\beta d\alpha\leq\|g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}\|F_{H}\|_{L_{1}}\ll\|g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}.

(1.13)

The last inequality immediately follows from (
1.11
). Note that
a_{n}=\mu^{2}(n)
when
r=2
in (
1.1
). Thus, for Theorem
1.4
and the lower bound case of Theorem
1.5
, it suffices to find a lower bound for
\|F_{H}\ast g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}
. The above idea can also be used to deduce that Theorem
1.3
impies Theorem
1.4
.

Proof that Theorem
1.4
implies Theorem
1.3
.

Define

\mu_{H}(n):=\mu(n){\bf 1}_{|n-N|<H}

Then we have

\widehat{\mu_{H}}(\alpha)=\sum_{|n-N|<H}\mu(n)e(n\alpha),

and by (
1.5
) we have

\widehat{\mu^{2}_{H}}(\alpha)=\widehat{\mu_{H}}*\widehat{\mu_{H}}(\alpha).

Similar to (
1.13
), we have

\|\widehat{\mu^{2}_{H}}\|_{L_{1}}=\|\widehat{\mu_{H}}*\widehat{\mu_{H}}\|_{L_{1}}\ll\|\widehat{\mu_{H}}\|_{L_{1}}^{2}.

Now by Theorem
1.4
,
\|\widehat{\mu^{2}_{H}}\|_{L_{1}}\gg H^{1/3}
, and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 2.5

(van der Corput bounds)
.

Suppose that
(p,q)
is an exponent pair defined by
[
5
, (8.57) and (8.58)]
,
f(x)
behaves like a monomial so that

|f^{(j)}(x)|\asymp_{j}FM^{-j}

for every
j\geq 0
, any
x\in[M,2M]
and some
F\geq M
. We define
\psi(x):=\{x\}-\frac{1}{2}
and let
I=[a,b]\subset[M,2M]
. Then

\sum_{m\in I}\psi(f(m))\ll F^{\frac{p}{p+1}}M^{\frac{1+2q}{2(p+1)}+\epsilon}

for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

By
[
3
, Theorem A.6]
, there exist coefficients
\beta(r)\ll|r|^{-1}
such that

\psi(x)\leq R^{-1}+\sum_{1\leq|r|\leq R}\beta(r)e(rx).

Thus,

\sum_{m\in I}\psi(f(m))\ll MR^{-1}+\sum_{1\leq r\leq R}\frac{1}{r}\left|\sum_{m\in I}e(rf(m))\right|.

By
[
5
, (8.58)]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in I}\psi(f(m))

\displaystyle\ll MR^{-1}+\sum_{1\leq r\leq R}\frac{1}{r}(rFM^{-1})^{p}M^{q+\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\ll MR^{-1}+F^{p}M^{q-p+\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon^{\prime}}R^{p}.

We choose
R
so that
R^{p+1}=M^{\frac{1}{2}+p-q-\epsilon^{\prime}}F^{-p}
, and the claim follows.
∎

Remark
.

We will only need the lemma for the exponent pair
(p,q)=\left(\frac{2}{7},\frac{1}{14}\right)

3.
Key lemmas

We recall that
a_{n}=\sum_{d^{r}\mid n}\mu(d)
. Let
z>y\geq 1
be any real numbers. For
n\neq 0
we define
c_{n}=c_{n}(y,z)
as a middle part of the above sum, namely

c_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{r}\mid n\\
y<d\leq z\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

(3.1)

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
1\leq K<N
and
1\leq y<z
we have

\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}|c_{n}|^{2}\ll Ky^{1-r}+N^{1/r+\epsilon}.

(3.2)

Furthermore, for
r=2
and
y\leq K^{1/2-\epsilon}
, we have

\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}|c_{n}|^{2}\ll Ky^{-1}+N^{\frac{12}{29}+\epsilon}y^{-\frac{10}{29}}.

(3.3)

Proof.

In fact (
3.2
) is
[
1
, Lemma 1]
, so we just need to prove (
3.3
). For
r=2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}|c_{n}|^{2}\leq\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{2}\mid n\\
y<d\leq z\end{subarray}}1)^{2}=\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}^{2},d_{2}^{2}\mid n\\
y<d_{i}\leq z\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}\sum_{h^{2}\mid n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(hd_{1}^{\prime})^{2},(hd_{2}^{\prime})^{2}\mid n\\
(d_{1}^{\prime},d_{2}^{\prime})=1\\
y<hd^{\prime}_{i}\leq z\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{N-K<n\leq N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=h^{2}d_{1}^{2}d_{2}^{2}a\\
y<hd_{i}\leq z\end{subarray}}1

(3.4)

So we only need to show that (
3.4
) is
\ll Ky^{-1}+N^{\frac{12}{29}+\epsilon}y^{-\frac{10}{29}}
. We first split

into dyadic ranges, so we consider the sum over
d_{i}\sim D_{i}
and

with

and then add those sums later.

Before proving the upper bound for (

D_{i}
and

D_{2}\gg D_{1}

For convenience we can assume that neither

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N-K<h^{2}d_{1}^{2}d_{2}^{2}a\leq N\\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1+\varepsilon-iT_{0}}^{1+\varepsilon+iT_{0}}\frac{N^{s}-(N-K)^{s}}{s}\zeta(s)P(2s)ds+O(N^{\epsilon}),

\displaystyle\max_{\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1+\varepsilon}\frac{N^{\sigma}}{T_{0}}T_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}(HD_{1}D_{2})^{1-2\sigma}\ll N^{\epsilon}.

Similarly,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}-iT_{0}}^{1+\varepsilon-iT_{0}}\frac{N^{s}-(N-K)^{s}}{s}\zeta(s)P(2s)ds\ll N^{\epsilon}

\epsilon>0
be a small fixed constant. By (
4.11
) and (
4.16
) we have

|{\mathcal{Y}}_{d}|=|{\mathcal{X}}_{d}|-|{\mathcal{Z}}_{d}|\gg\frac{d^{r}}{H}\gg|{\mathcal{X}}_{d}|,\quad\text{ for every }\quad d\leq\epsilon y.

(4.17)

Let

{\mathcal{Y}}=\bigcup_{d\leq\epsilon y}{\mathcal{Y}}_{d}.

We have

|{\mathcal{Y}}|\leq\sum_{d\leq\epsilon y}|{\mathcal{X}}_{d}|\leq\frac{(\epsilon y)^{r+1}}{H}=\epsilon^{r+1}.

(4.18)

By (
4.1
), Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Parseval’s identity, we have

\displaystyle\|F*g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}|F*g_{1}(\alpha)|d\alpha\geq\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}|F*g_{1}(\alpha)|d\alpha

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}|g_{2}(\alpha)|d\alpha-\left(|{\mathcal{Y}}|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}|c_{n}(y,z)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.19)

By (
4.10
), (
4.17
) and Lemma
4.2
,

\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}|g_{2}(\alpha)|d\alpha\gg\sum_{d\leq\epsilon y}\frac{H}{d^{r}}|{\mathcal{Y}}_{d}|\gg\epsilon y\gg\epsilon H^{\frac{1}{r+1}},

and by Lemma
3.2
and (
4.18
)

\left(|{\mathcal{Y}}|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}|c_{n}(y,z)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll\epsilon^{\frac{r+1}{2}}H^{\frac{1}{r+1}},

when
H
satisfies the condition of
K
in Lemma
3.2
.
Combining these with (
4.19
) implies, when
\epsilon>0
is small enough,
\|F_{H}*g_{1}\|_{L_{1}}\gg H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}
. As explained in the beginning of this section, this completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
and the proof of the lower bound case of Theorem
1.5
.

5.
Proof of the upper bound case of theorem
1.5

In this section, we will prove the upper for the
L_{1}
norm of the exponential sum over
r
-free numbers. This proof does not involve
F_{H}
. Instead we involve difference of two Fejér Kernels, see (
1.7
), and the argument is essentially same as Balog-Ruzsa’s.

Let
D=H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}
, and let
S
be such that
2^{S-1}<D\leq 2^{S}
. Note that

S\leq\frac{1}{r+1}\log_{2}H+1.

Recall (
3.1
) and decompose
a_{n}
as follows

a_{n}=\sum_{s=1}^{S}c_{n}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})+c_{n}(D,N^{\frac{1}{r}}).

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}a_{n}e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}c_{n}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}c_{n}(D,N^{\frac{1}{r}})e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha.

(5.1)

The second term can be estimated as in the proof of the lower bound case: The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Parseval’s identity and Lemma
3.2
imply that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}c_{n}(D,N^{1/r})e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha\ll H^{1/(r+1)}.

(5.2)

The remaining task is to estimate the first term. Let
K=H2^{-rs}
and rewrite

\displaystyle\sum_{|n-N|\leq H}c_{n}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})e(n\alpha)=\sum_{|n|\leq H}c_{n+N}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})e((n+N)\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|n|\leq H+K}\min\left\{1,\frac{H+K-|n|}{K}\right\}c_{N+n}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})e((n+N)\alpha)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{H<|n|\leq H+K}\frac{H+K-|n|}{K}c_{n+N}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})e((n+N)\alpha)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}(s)+\Sigma_{4}(s).

Before estimating each term, let us explain the motivation of this decomposition. The coefficients in
\Sigma_{3}(s)
comes from the difference of two Fejér Kernels. By involving this difference of Fejér Kernels we can make the coefficient “smooth”, which is useful in the later integral. However, the payoff is that we should add the “tails”–
\Sigma_{4}(s)
from
H
to
H+K
. Fortunately, we can control the “tails” using Lemma
3.1
.

Let us estimate
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Sigma_{4}(s)
first. In fact by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Parseval’s identity

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\Sigma_{4}(s)|d\alpha\leq\left(\sum_{H<|n|\leq H+K}|c_{n+N}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{N+H<n\leq N+H+K}+\sum_{N-H-K\leq n<N-H}|c_{n}(D2^{-s},D2^{-(s-1)})|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.3)

For
r=2
, we have

D2^{-s}=H^{\frac{1}{3}}2^{-s}\leq H^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}2^{-s}\leq(H2^{-2s})^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}=K^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon},

which implies that (
5.3
) satisfies the condition of Lemma
3.1
. By Lemma
3.1
, (
5.3
) is

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(K(D2^{-s})^{1-r}+N^{\frac{12}{29}+\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}&r=2\\
(K(D2^{-s})^{1-r}+N^{\frac{1}{r}+\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\begin{cases}H^{\frac{1}{3}}2^{-\frac{s}{2}}+N^{\frac{6}{29}+\epsilon}&r=2\\
H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}2^{-\frac{s}{2}}+N^{\frac{1}{2r}+\epsilon}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Thus when
H
satisfies (
1.3
), we have

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\Sigma_{4}(s)|d\alpha\ll H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}

(5.4)

The rest argument for estimating the contribution of the
\Sigma_{3}(s)
part is same as Balog-Ruzsa’s (2.11), but to make paper self-contained, we write down it.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
H\geq 0,K\geq 1,1\leq d\leq H+K
and
M
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|n|\leq H+K\\
n\equiv M\ ({\rm{mod}}\ d)\end{subarray}}\min\left\{1,\frac{H+K-|n|}{K}\right\}e(n\alpha)\ll\min\left\{\frac{H+K}{d},\frac{1}{\|d\alpha\|},\frac{d}{K\|d\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}

Proof.

See
[
1
, (2.5)]
.
∎

Recall the definition of
c_{n}
in (
3.1
). By the above lemma, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\Sigma_{3}(s)|d\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{|n|\leq H+K}\min\left\{1,\frac{H+K-|n|}{K}\right\}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{r}\mid(n+N)\\
D2^{-s}<d\leq D2^{-(s-1)}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)e((n+N)\alpha)\right|d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{D2^{-s}<d\leq D2^{-(s-1)}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|n|\leq H+K\\
n\equiv-N\ ({\rm{mod}}\ d^{r})\end{subarray}}\min\left\{1,\frac{H+K-|n|}{K}\right\}e(n\alpha)\right|d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{D2^{-s}<d\leq D2^{-(s-1)}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\min\left\{\frac{H+K}{d^{r}},\frac{1}{\|d^{r}\alpha\|},\frac{d^{r}}{K\|d^{r}\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{D2^{-s}<d\leq D2^{-(s-1)}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\min\left\{\frac{H}{d^{r}},\frac{1}{\|\alpha\|},\frac{d^{r}}{K\|\alpha\|^{2}}\right\}d\alpha

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{D2^{-s}<d\leq D2^{-(s-1)}}\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{d^{r}}{H}}\frac{H}{d^{r}}d\alpha+\int_{\frac{d^{r}}{H}}^{\frac{d^{r}}{K}}\frac{1}{\alpha}d\alpha+\int_{\frac{d^{r}}{K}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{d^{r}}{K\alpha^{2}}d\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{D2^{-s}<d\leq D2^{-(s-1)}}\left(1+\log\frac{H}{K}\right)\ll s2^{-s}D.

So it is easy to see that

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\Sigma_{3}(s)|d\alpha\ll H^{\frac{1}{r+1}}

(5.5)

Hence, the upper bound case of Theorem
1.5
follows from (
5.1
), (
5.2
), (
5.5
) and (
5.4
).
