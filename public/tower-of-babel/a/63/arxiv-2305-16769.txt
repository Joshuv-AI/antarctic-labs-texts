ASEP proofs of some partition identities and the blocking stationary behaviour of second class particles
Daniel Adams, Márton Balázs, Jessica Jay

Abstract
. We give probabilistic proofs to well-known combinatorial identities; the Durfee rectangles identity, Euler’s identity and the q q -Binomial Theorem. We use the asymmetric simple exclusion process on ℤ \mathbb{Z} under its natural product blocking measure to achieve this. The results we derive also allow us to determine the stationary distribution of second class particles in the blocking scenario.



1.
Introduction

The Asymmetric Simple Exclusion Process (ASEP) is a classic nearest neighbour interacting particle system on
\mathbb{Z}
, introduced in 1970 by Spitzer
[
18
]
. In 1976, Liggett
[
16
]
showed that ASEP has reversible stationary measures known as blocking measures. These blocking measures have a special algebraic structure which allowed Balázs and Bowen
[
5
]
to derive a probabilistic proof of the Jacobi triple product identity. In that paper the authors considered ASEP and its equivalent description in terms of the Asymmetric Zero-Range Process. Comparing the blocking measures for each process led to their result. In 2022, this method was generalised by Balázs, Fretwell and Jay
[
6
]
, where new identities of combinatorial significance were proved by considering blocking measures of more general particle systems.

Here we return to ASEP only, this time we extend the previous work of
[
5
]
by determining the distribution of the number of particles in specific regions of the integer lattice. This way we give purely probabilistic proofs to other well-known and non-trivial combinatorial identities. The ones we cover are the Durfee rectangles identity, Euler’s identity and the
q
-Binomial Theorem, each fundamental to the theory of integer partitions. Probabilistic methods are certainly not the common way of proving such identities, which gives a unique perspective to this paper.

Attractive interacting particle systems, like ASEP, give rise to coupling methods from which the notion of second class particles arises. The coupled particle systems are referred to as a multi-species particle system. Stationary measures of such systems have drawn lots of interest, for examples in the translation invariant case see Angel
[
4
]
, Ferrari and Martin
[
13
]
and also, in terms of last passage percolation, Fan and Seppäläinen
[
11
]
.

Informally, a two-species ASEP can be described in the following way. Both species of particles attempt to evolve as the single species ASEP would, i.e. obeying the exclusion rule within their own species. The two species can interact as follows, a first class particle may jump into the space of a second and hence swap position with the second class particle. However if a second class particle attempts to jump into the space of a first class particle the jump is blocked.

The quantities we define and the combinatorial identities we prove allow us to give an explicit formula for the distribution of positions for a given number of second class particles. To the best of our knowledge, the distribution of second class particles under the blocking measure has not been determined before. From private communications, the authors understand that similar results will be extracted from arguments involving the Mallows measure by A. Bufetov and K. Chen in a forthcoming paper. With the use of a second class particle we also give an alternative proof to the distribution of the number of particles on the half infinite line.

The second class particle arguments rely on an interpretation of the two species ASEP as a single species ASEP with a second ASEP on the particle labels using the first as a dynamic background. It turns out that both these ASEPs are in their stationary blocking distributions, which allows us to perform calculations. Both for the distribution of the number of particles and positions of second class particles, we use probabilistic arguments that lead to various recursions. Finding the solutions of these gives rise to our proofs. For the Durfee rectangles identity we also exploit the well-known symmetry between particles and holes in ASEP.

1.1.
Notation

Throughout the paper we will use the
q
-Pochhammer symbol as well as the
q
-Binomial coefficient. For some
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the
q
-Pochhammer symbol is given by

(a;q)_{n}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^{i})
.
Also we set,
(a;q)_{0}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=1
and
(a;q)_{\infty}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{i})
.
The
q
-Binomial coefficient is then,

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
m\end{bmatrix}_{q}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\frac{(q;q)_{n}}{(q;q)_{m}(q;q)_{n-m}}=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{(1-q^{n-i})}{(1-q^{i+1})}.

1.2.
Results

1.2.1.
Combinatorial Identities

In Section
3.2
, we look at the symmetry between particles and holes in ASEP under its blocking measure. Using this symmetry we prove a well-known combinatorial identity, the Durfee rectangles identity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Durfee Rectangles Identity
(for example see equation (4) in Gessel
[
14
]
)

For
q\in(0,1)
and any fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}=\sum\limits_{k=\max\{-n,0\}}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(n+k)}}{(q;q)_{n+k}(q;q)_{k}}.

Combinatorially this identity identity says that for a fixed integer
n
, any integer partition has a maximal rectangle of side lengths
n+k
and
k
(for some
k\geq\max\{-n,0\}
) in its Ferrers diagram known as its Durfee rectangle. When
n=0
, this identity is the well-known Durfee square identity (for example see Andrews and Eriksson
[
3
]
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Euler’s Identity
(for example see equation E1 in Andrews
[
1
]
)

For
q\in(0,1)
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}z^{k}}{(1-q)(1-q^{2})...(1-q^{k})}=(-z;q)_{\infty}.

In combinatorics, this identity gives equivalent ways of writing the generating function for integer partitions into distinct parts. In a general term of the expansion, the power of
z
gives the number of parts in the partition.

Theorem 1.3
.

q
-Binomial Theorem
(Heine
[
15
]
, also Andrews
[
2
]
)

For
q\in(0,1)
,
z\in\mathbb{R}
and any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m}q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}z^{k}\begin{bmatrix}m\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}=(-z;q)_{m}.

This identity gives equivalent forms of the generating function for integer partitions into distinct parts of size at most
m
. Again here in a general term in the expansion, the power of
z
is the number of parts in the partition. Euler’s identity can be thought of as the limiting identity when we take
m\rightarrow\infty
in the
q
-Binomial theorem.

The proofs we give here are purely probabilistic using ASEP and give natural probabilistic interpretations to these combinatorial identities.

1.2.2.
Distributional results for single and two species ASEP

We denote the family of product blocking measures for ASEP by
\underline{\mu}^{c}
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, these measures are written in terms of the asymmetry parameter for the process,
q\in(0,1)
. We also let

N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})
denote the number of particles at and to the left of site
m
in the state
\underline{z}
. We will derive the distribution under this blocking measure of a given number of particles on a half infinite volume.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k(c-m)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}(-q^{c-m};q)_{\infty}}.

We also consider the distribution under the blocking measure of a given number of particles in a finite volume (see Lemma
3.2
).

Using the basic coupling of two ASEPs,
\{\underline{\eta},\underline{\xi}\}
, we find the probability that a second class particles is at a given site. At any time,
\underline{\eta}
and
\underline{\xi}
differ in exactly
d>0
places (at these sites
\xi_{i}>\eta_{i}
), these differences define
d
second class particles.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume that the coupled system is stationary and
\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c}
. For a given
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we find a second class particle at site
m
with probability,

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{m}>\eta_{m})=\frac{(1-q^{d})q^{c-m}}{(1+q^{c-m})(1+q^{c+d-m})}.

We can also find the stationary distribution of the
d
second class particle positions process,

Theorem 1.6
.

Assuming that the coupled system is stationary and
\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c}
, the stationary distribution of
\underline{X}
, the position of
d>0
second class particles, is given by,

\mathbb{P}(\underline{X}=\underline{m})=\frac{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1-q^{i})\cdot q^{dc-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}(1+q^{c+d-j-m_{j}})(1+q^{c+d+1-j-m_{j}})},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all }m_{1}<m_{2}<...<m_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Of course, when
d=1
Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
are the same. Then, in this case, the position of a single second class particle under
\underline{\mu}^{c}
is distributed according to
-Y
where
Y
is a discrete logistic with parameters
q
and
-c
(introduced by Chakraborty and Chakravarty in 2016
[
9
]
).

In order to prove Theorem
1.6
, we first condition on the label process for the second class particles. The label of a particle is defined to be the number of particles to its left, which by the blocking nature of the process will be finite. The label process can be viewed as the positions of particles in an asymmetric simple exclusion process on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with exactly
d
particles (meaning closed left boundary). Then we split states up into the sites to the left of site
m_{1}
and for
j\in\{1,...,d-1\}
the sites between
m_{j}
and
m_{j+1}
. From here we use the distributional results for particles in half infinite and finite volumes along with Euler’s identity and the
q
-Binomial Theorem to complete the proof.

2.
Interacting particle systems with product blocking measure

2.1.
Blocking Family

Let us recall the blocking family of particle systems on
\mathbb{Z}
as introduced by Balázs and Bowen in
[
5
]
, using the notation as in the paper of Balázs, Fretwell and Jay
[
6
]
. We consider nearest neighbour exclusion type particle systems on some state space. The state space we consider is some countable
\Omega\subseteq I^{\Lambda}
, where:

•

\Lambda\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{i:\ell-1<i<\mathfrak{r}+1\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, for some (possibly infinite)
-\infty\leq\ell\leq\mathfrak{r}\leq\infty
, defines the set of sites

•

I\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{z:\omega^{\textrm{min}}-1<z<\omega^{\textrm{max}}+1\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, for some (possibly infinite)
-\infty\leq\omega^{\textrm{min}}\leq 0<\omega^{\textrm{max}}\leq\infty
, defines the set of possible particle configuration per site.

We consider the following functions
N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}},N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}:I^{\Lambda}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
for any
m\in\Lambda
defined by,

N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}(\underline{\eta})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{m}(\eta_{i}-\omega^{\textrm{min}})\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 14.22636ptN_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}(\underline{\eta})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\sum\limits_{i=m+1}^{\mathfrak{r}}(\omega^{\textrm{max}}-\eta_{i})

(this can be thought of as just the number of ‘particles’ to the left and ‘holes’ to right of
m+\frac{1}{2}
whenever
\omega^{\text{min}}
and
\omega^{\text{max}}
are finite). When
m=0
we simplify notation to
N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}
and
N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}
.

Now we can define the state space as follows:

•

if
\ell\leq 0\leq\mathfrak{r}
then,

\Omega\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{\eta}\in I^{\Lambda}:(\ell>-\infty\textrm{ or }N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}(\underline{\eta})<\infty)\textrm{ and }(\mathfrak{r}<\infty\textrm{ or }N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}(\underline{\eta})<\infty)\}

•

if
\mathfrak{r}<0
then,

\Omega\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{\eta}\in I^{\Lambda}:\ell>-\infty\textrm{ or }N_{\mathfrak{r}+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}(\underline{\eta})<\infty\}

•

if
\mathfrak{\ell}>0
then,

\Omega\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{\eta}\in I^{\Lambda}:\mathfrak{r}<\infty\textrm{ or }N_{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}(\underline{\eta})<\infty\}

Let
\underline{\eta}^{(i,j)}
denote the state reached from
\underline{\eta}\in\Omega
by a particle jumping from site
i
to site
j
(when this is possible), that is,

\left(\underline{\eta}^{(i,j)}\right)_{k}=\begin{cases}\eta_{k}&\textrm{if }k\neq i,j\\
\eta_{i}-1&\textrm{if }k=i\\
\eta_{j}+1&\textrm{if }k=j.\end{cases}

The systems we consider evolve according to the following Markov generators. In the bulk (sites
\ell\leq i\leq\mathfrak{r}-1
) the generator is of the form,

\left(L^{\textrm{bulk}}\varphi\right)(\underline{\eta})=\sum\limits_{i=\ell}^{\mathfrak{r}-1}\left\{p(\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1})\left(\varphi(\underline{\eta}^{(i,i+1)})-\varphi(\underline{\eta})\right)+q(\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1})\left(\varphi(\underline{\eta}^{(i+1,i)})-\varphi(\underline{\eta})\right)\right\}

for some cylinder function
\varphi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and right/left jump rates
p,q:I^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
. When either
\ell>-\infty
or
\mathfrak{r}<\infty
we consider open infinite type boundaries with boundary generators given by,

\displaystyle\left(L^{\ell}\varphi\right)(\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle=p_{\ell}(\eta_{\ell})\left(\varphi(\underline{\eta}^{(\ell-1,\ell)})-\varphi(\underline{\eta})\right)+q_{\ell}(\eta_{\ell})\left(\varphi(\underline{\eta}^{(\ell,\ell-1)})-\varphi(\underline{\eta})\right)

\displaystyle\left(L^{\mathfrak{r}}\varphi\right)(\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle=p_{\mathfrak{r}}(\eta_{\mathfrak{r}})\left(\varphi(\underline{\eta}^{(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{r}+1)})-\varphi(\underline{\eta})\right)+q_{\mathfrak{r}}(\eta_{\mathfrak{r}})\left(\varphi(\underline{\eta}^{(\mathfrak{r}+1,\mathfrak{r})})-\varphi(\underline{\eta})\right).

Here
\underline{\eta}^{(\ell-1,\ell)}
and
\underline{\eta}^{(\mathfrak{r}+1,\mathfrak{r})}
denote the states reached from a particle entering via the boundary with
p_{\ell}
and
q_{\mathfrak{r}}
being the corresponding jump rates. Similarly
\underline{\eta}^{(\ell,\ell-1)}
and
\underline{\eta}^{(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{r}+1)}
denote the states reached from a particle leaving the system via the boundary with jump rates
q_{\ell}
and
p_{\mathfrak{r}}
.

Particle systems of this type are members of the blocking family if they satisfy the blocking conditions:

(
B{1}
)

For
-\infty<\omega^{\textrm{min}}<\omega^{\textrm{max}}<\infty
we have that

p(\omega^{\textrm{min}},\cdot)=p(\cdot,\omega^{\textrm{max}})=q(\omega^{\textrm{max}},\cdot)=q(\cdot,\omega^{\textrm{min}})=0.

If
\ell>-\infty
,

q_{\ell}(\omega^{\textrm{min}})=p_{\ell}(\omega^{\textrm{max}})=0

and if
\mathfrak{r}<\infty
,

q_{\mathfrak{r}}(\omega^{\textrm{max}})=p_{\mathfrak{r}}(\omega^{\textrm{min}})=0.

(
B{2}
)

The system is attractive; that is
p(\cdot,\cdot)
is non-decreasing in the first variable and non-increasing in the second whilst
q(\cdot,\cdot)
is non-increasing in the first variable and non-decreasing in the second. If
\ell>-\infty
,
p_{\ell}
is non-increasing and
q_{\ell}
non-decreasing. If
\mathfrak{r}<\infty
,
p_{\mathfrak{r}}
is non-decreasing and
q_{\mathfrak{r}}
non-increasing.

(
B{3}
)

There exist
p_{\text{asym}},q_{\text{asym}}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\frac{1}{2}<p_{\textrm{asym}}=1-q_{\textrm{asym}}\leq 1
, and functions
f:I\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and
s:I\times I\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that,

p(y,z)=p_{\textrm{asym}}\cdot s(y,z+1)\cdot f(y)\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 14.22636ptq(y,z)=q_{\textrm{asym}}\cdot s(y+1,z)\cdot f(z).

If
\omega^{\textrm{min}}
is finite then
f(\omega^{\textrm{min}})=0
, and if
\omega^{\textrm{max}}
is finite we extend the domain of
s
and require that
s(\omega^{\textrm{max}}+1,\cdot)=s(\cdot,\omega^{\textrm{max}}+1)=0
. (Note that attractivity implies that
s
is non-increasing in both of its variables and
f
is non-decreasing).

Particle systems which satisfy these conditions will in general have many stationary distributions. In particular, they have reversible stationary product blocking measures.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
5
]
, Theorem
3.1
)

Set,

c^{\text{min}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\begin{cases}-\infty&\text{ when }\omega^{\text{max}}<\infty\\
-\frac{\lim\limits_{z\rightarrow\infty}\ln{f(z)}}{(\ln{p}-\ln{q})}&\text{ when }\omega^{\text{max}}=\infty\end{cases}\qquad c^{\text{max}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{ when }\omega^{\text{min}}>-\infty\\
-\frac{\lim\limits_{z\rightarrow-\infty}\ln{f(z)}}{(\ln{p}-\ln{q})}&\text{ when }\omega^{\text{min}}=-\infty.\end{cases}

Take
c^{\text{min}}<c<c^{\text{max}}
. If
\ell>-\infty
then assume that
p_{\ell}
and
q_{\ell}
are such that,

\frac{q_{\ell}(z+1)}{p_{\ell}(z)}=\frac{f(z+1)}{\left(\frac{p_{\text{asym}}}{q_{\text{asym}}}\right)^{\ell-c}}.

Similarly, if
\mathfrak{r}<\infty
then assume that
p_{\mathfrak{r}}
and
q_{\mathfrak{r}}
are such that,

\frac{p_{\mathfrak{r}}(z+1)}{q_{\mathfrak{r}}(z)}=\frac{f(z+1)}{\left(\frac{p_{\text{asym}}}{q_{\text{asym}}}\right)^{\mathfrak{r}-c}}.

Then there is a product reversible stationary blocking measure
\underline{\mu}^{c}
on
\Omega
, given by the marginals

\mu_{i}^{c}(z)=\frac{1}{Z_{i}^{c}}\frac{\left(\frac{p_{\textrm{asym}}}{q_{\textrm{asym}}}\right)^{(i-c)z}}{f(z)!}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{ for }i\in\Lambda\text{ and }z\in I,

where
Z_{i}^{c}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\sum\limits_{z=\omega^{\text{min}}}^{\omega^{\text{max}}}\frac{\left(\frac{p_{\text{asym}}}{q_{\text{asym}}}\right)^{(i-c)z}}{f(z)!}
is the normalising factor and
f(z)!\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{y=1}^{z}f(y)&\textrm{for }z>0\\
1&\textrm{for }z=0\\
\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{y=z+1}^{0}f(y)}&\textrm{for }z<0.\end{cases}

Remark
.

By definition of the state space
\Omega
and by a Borel-Cantelli argument for
\underline{\mu}^{c}
we see that any state of a process in the Blocking family has a left most particle and right most hole a.s.

2.2.
Ergodic Decomposition

When either
\ell
or
\mathfrak{r}
are finite we see that the state space
\Omega
is irreducible. This is not true in the doubly infinite case, but we can decompose
\Omega
into irreducible components in the following way.

Let us concentrate on the case when
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
and
I=\{\omega^{\text{min}},...,\omega^{\text{max}}\}
for some

-\infty<\omega^{\text{min}}\leq 0<\omega^{\text{max}}<\infty
. Let
\tilde{q}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\frac{q_{\text{asym}}}{p_{\text{asym}}}<1
, then we can write the one parameter family of stationary blocking measures as,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\underline{\eta})=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{0}\frac{\tilde{q}^{-(i-c)(\eta_{i}-\omega^{\text{min}})}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{min}}(i-c)}f(\eta_{i})!\cdot Z_{i}^{c}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(\omega^{\text{max}}-\eta_{i})(i-c)}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{max}}(i-c)}f(\eta_{i})!\cdot Z_{i}^{c}}.

It is clear to see that for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
the quantity,

N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})=\sum\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}(\omega^{\text{max}}-\eta_{i})-\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{m}(\eta_{i}-\omega^{\text{min}})

is finite by definition and is conserved by the dynamics of the process. As before when
m=0
we simplify notation to
N
.

Using this fact, we see that the state space decomposes into irreducible components,

\Omega=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Omega^{n}\hskip 5.69054pt\text{ where }\hskip 5.69054pt\Omega^{n}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{\eta}\in\Omega:N(\underline{\eta})=n\}.

We note that the left shift operator,
\tau
such that
(\tau\underline{\eta})_{i}=\eta_{i+1}
, defines a bijection
\Omega^{n}\xrightarrow{\tau}\Omega^{n-(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})}
(i.e if
\underline{\eta}\in\Omega^{n}
then,
N(\tau\underline{\eta})=n-(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})
).

We can then find the unique stationary distribution on
\Omega^{n}
,

\underline{\nu}^{n}(\cdot)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\underline{\mu}^{c}(\cdot|N(\cdot)=n)=\frac{\underline{\mu}^{c}(\cdot)\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\in\Omega^{n}\}}{\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n\})},

where
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n\})
is found as the solution to the recurrence relation given in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
5
]
, Lemma
6.1
and Corollary
6.2
)

The following relation holds,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\tau\underline{\eta})=\tilde{q}^{c(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})-N(\underline{\eta})}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\underline{\eta}).

This gives the recursion,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n\})=\tilde{q}^{n-c(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n-(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})\}).

2.3.
Distributional Results

We will now prove some distributional results for our processes in blocking measure. Here we assume that
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
and
I=\{\omega^{\textrm{min}},...,\omega^{\text{max}}\}
for some
-\infty<\omega^{\textrm{min}}\leq 0<\omega^{\text{max}}<\infty
. Firstly, we see that if we shift the entire system this corresponds to a shift in the parameter
c
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\underline{\eta}\in\Omega

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\tau\underline{\eta})=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\underline{\eta}).

Proof.

Writing the blocking measure compactly we have,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\tau\underline{\eta})=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-i)(\tau\underline{\eta})_{i}}}{f((\tau\underline{\eta})_{i})!\cdot Z_{i}^{c}}=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-i)\eta_{i+1}}}{f(\eta_{i+1})!\cdot Z_{i}^{c}}=\prod\limits_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-(j-1))\eta_{j}}}{f(\eta_{j})!\cdot Z_{j-1}^{c}}=\prod\limits_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{((c+1)-j)\eta_{j}}}{f(\eta_{j})!\cdot Z_{j}^{c+1}}=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\underline{\eta}).

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(1)

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

(2)

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

(3)

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

Proof.

Firstly we note that for any
r\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{r+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{r}(\eta_{i+1}-\omega^{\text{min}})=\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{r+1}(\eta_{i}-\omega^{\text{min}})=N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{r+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{r+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=r+1}^{\infty}(\omega^{\text{max}}-\eta_{i+1})=\sum\limits_{i=r+2}^{\infty}(\omega^{\text{max}}-\eta_{i})=N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{r+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle N_{r+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau\underline{\eta})

\displaystyle=N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{r+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau\underline{\eta})-N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{r+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau\underline{\eta})=N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{r+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})-N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{r+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta})=N_{r+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{\eta}).

So we have that,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c}

\displaystyle(\{\underline{z}:N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau^{-1}\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{y}:N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{y})=k}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\tau\underline{y})=\sum\limits_{\underline{y}:N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{y})=k}\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\underline{y})=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

Similar arguments hold for
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})
and
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})
.
∎

We can also give results where we only change the parameter
c
and not the quantity we wish to understand the probability of.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}
we have that,

(1)

\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{\tilde{q}^{-k-\omega^{\text{min}}(m+1-c)}}{Z_{m+1}^{c}}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

(2)

\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\tilde{q}^{k+\omega^{\text{max}}(m+1-c)}Z_{m+1}^{c}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

(3)

\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\tilde{q}^{k+(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})(m+1-c)}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

Proof.

(1)

We have that,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\prod\limits_{j=-\infty}^{m}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-1-j)z_{j}}}{f(z_{j})!\cdot Z_{j}^{c-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\prod\limits_{j=-\infty}^{m}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-1-j)(z_{j}-\omega^{\text{min}})}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{min}(j-c+1)}f(z_{j})!\cdot Z_{j+1}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\frac{\tilde{q}^{-\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{m}(z_{j}-\omega^{\text{min}})}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{min}}(m+1-c)}Z_{m+1}^{c}}\prod\limits_{j=-\infty}^{m}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-j)(z_{j}-\omega^{\text{min}})}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{min}}(j-c)}f(z_{j})!\cdot Z_{j}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{q}^{-k-\omega^{\text{min}}(m+1-c)}}{Z_{m+1}^{c}}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

(2)

We have that,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\prod\limits_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(c-1-j)z_{j}}}{f(z_{j})!\cdot Z_{j}^{c-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\prod\limits_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(\omega^{\text{max}}-z_{j})(j-c+1)}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{max}}(j-c+1)}f(z_{j})!\cdot Z_{j}^{c-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{z}:\penalty\ N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k}\tilde{q}^{\sum\limits_{j=m+1}^{\infty}(\omega^{\text{max}}-z_{j})}\cdot\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{max}}(m+1-c)}Z_{m+1}^{c}\prod\limits_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{q}^{(\omega^{\text{max}}-z_{j})(j-c)}}{\tilde{q}^{\omega^{\text{max}}(j-c)}f(z_{j})!\cdot Z_{j}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{q}^{k+\omega^{\text{max}}(m+1-c)}Z_{m+1}^{c}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

(3)

We notice that,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}\cap\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{1}\}\cap\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{2}\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}:\\
k_{2}-k_{1}=k\end{subarray}}\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{1}\}\cap\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{2}\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}:\\
k_{2}-k_{1}=k\end{subarray}}\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{1}\})\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{2}\}).

Using (a) and (b) we have that,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}:\\
k_{2}-k_{1}=k\end{subarray}}\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{1}\})\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c-1}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{2}\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}:\\
k_{2}-k_{1}=k\end{subarray}}\frac{\tilde{q}^{-k_{1}-\omega^{\text{min}}(m+1-c)}}{Z_{m+1}^{c}}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{1}\})\cdot\tilde{q}^{k_{2}+\omega^{\text{max}}(m+1-c)}Z_{m+1}^{c}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{2}\})

\displaystyle=\tilde{q}^{k+(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})(m+1-c)}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}:\\
k_{2}-k_{1}=k\end{subarray}}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{1}\})\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k_{2}\})

\displaystyle=\tilde{q}^{k+(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})(m+1-c)}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

∎

3.
The asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP)

For the rest of the paper we will focus on a particular member of the blocking family, the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP) on the state space,

\Omega\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}:N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}(\underline{\eta}),N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}(\underline{\eta})<\infty\}.

That is the interacting particle system on
\Omega
which evolves according to nearest neighbour particle jumps happening with jump rates,

p(z_{i},z_{i+1})=\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 1\}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636ptq(z_{i},z_{i+1})=q\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 1\},

for some
q\in(0,1)
. Blocking measures for ASEP were first found by Liggett in 1976
[
16
]
and by the general form of blocking measures from Balázs and Bowen
[
5
]
, we know that the natural product blocking measure for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
is given by,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\underline{\eta})=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-(i-c)\eta_{i}}}{1+q^{-(i-c)}}=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{0}\frac{q^{-(i-c)\eta_{i}}}{1+q^{-(i-c)}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(i-c)(1-\eta_{i})}}{1+q^{(i-c)}}.

We also have the unique stationary measure on the irreducible component,
\Omega^{n}
, of the state space for each
n
. The results of
[
16
]
gives us that,

(1)

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n\})=\frac{q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-nc}}{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}-\ell c}},

and then,

\underline{\nu}^{n}(\underline{\eta})=\frac{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}-\ell c}}{q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-nc}}\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{0}\frac{q^{-(i-c)\eta_{i}}}{1+q^{-(i-c)}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(i-c)(1-\eta_{i})}}{1+q^{i-c}}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{N(\underline{\eta})=n\}.

3.1.
Distributional Results

We will now give some distributional results for ASEP; in particular we focus on the functions
N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}
and
N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}

In
[
5
]
Balázs and Bowen gave the distribution under
\underline{\mu}^{c}
of the conserved quantity about the site
0
, that is
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n\})
(given in equation (
1
)). By considering shifts of ASEP states we can find the distribution of the conserved quantity about any site
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}=n\})=\frac{q^{\frac{(n+m)(n+m+1)}{2}-(n+m)c}}{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}-\ell c}}

Proof.

As we saw in the proof of Corollary
2.4
,
N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=N_{m-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau\underline{z})
. Thus we have that,
N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=N(\tau^{m}\underline{z})
. And as we have seen before, a left shift decreases the conserved quantity by
(\omega^{\text{max}}-\omega^{\text{min}})=1
and so,
N(\tau^{m}\underline{z})=N(\underline{z})-m
. Then, by (
1
) we have that,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}=n\})=\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N=n+m\})=\frac{q^{\frac{(n+m)(n+m+1)}{2}-(n+m)c}}{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}-\ell c}}.

∎

We now consider the probability under the blocking measure that there is some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
particles to left of a site
m\in\mathbb{Z}
(inclusive).
See
1.4

Proof.

Conditioning on site
m
we have that (for
k\geq 1
),

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k|z_{m}=0\})\mu^{c}_{m}(0)+\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k|z_{m}=1\})\mu_{m}^{c}(1)

\displaystyle=\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})\frac{1}{1+q^{c-m}}+\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})\frac{q^{c-m}}{1+q^{c-m}}.

We want to look at
N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})
not
N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})
so we use the result of Corollary
2.4
:

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})\}=k)=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})\}=k).

Hence,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})\frac{1}{1+q^{c-m}}+\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})\frac{q^{c-m}}{1+q^{c-m}}.

By Lemma
2.5
,

\underline{\mu}^{c+1}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=q^{k}(1+q^{c-m})\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

And so we have,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})q^{k}+\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})q^{c-m+k-1}.

Giving the recurrence relation,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k-1+c-m}}{1-q^{k}}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})

which we solve by,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\frac{q^{i-1+c-m}}{1-q^{i}}\right)\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=0\})

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\frac{q^{i-1+c-m}}{1-q^{i}}\cdot\prod\limits_{j=-\infty}^{m}\frac{1}{1+q^{c-j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k(c-m-1)+\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}(-q^{c-m};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k(c-m)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}(-q^{c-m};q)_{\infty}}.

∎

Remark
.

An alternative proof of this result by considering two coupled ASEPs can be given. We will see this in Section
4.4
.

We also consider the probability under the blocking measure that there is some
k
particles in between two sites.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
m_{1}+1<m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z},c\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\{0,1,...,\hat{m}_{2}\}
where
\hat{m}_{2}:=m_{2}-m_{1}-1
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}.

Proof.

Firstly, let us consider the cases where
k=0
or
k=\hat{m_{2}}
. These cases are directly computable since there is only 1 state in each case for which
N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k
. If
k=0
then the state is such that all the sites in
[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]
are empty and so,

(2)

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=0\})=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}-1}(1+q^{c-i})}=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\hat{m}_{2}}(1+q^{c-m_{2}+i})}=\frac{1}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}.

If
k=\hat{m}_{2}
then the state is such that all the sites in
[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]
are full and so,

(3)

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=\hat{m}_{2}\})=\prod\limits_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}-1}\frac{q^{c-i}}{(1+q^{c-i})}=\frac{q^{\hat{m}_{2}(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{\hat{m}_{2}(\hat{m}_{2}-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}.

Now, let us consider
k\in\{1,...,\hat{m}_{2}-1\}
, we see that

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-

\displaystyle N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k|z_{m_{2}-1}=0\})\cdot\mu^{c}_{m_{2}-1}(0)

\displaystyle+\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k|z_{m_{2}-1}=1\})\cdot\mu^{c}_{m_{2}-1}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-2+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})\cdot\frac{1}{1+q^{c-m_{2}+1}}

(4)

\displaystyle+\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-2+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})\cdot\frac{q^{c-m_{2}+1}}{1+q^{c-m_{2}+1}}.

We now show that
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}
satisfies (
4
). This formula is inspired by a combinatorial argument using integer partitions. As additional material this argument is given in Appendix
B
but those parts are not needed for any of the proofs in this article, since these are self-contained and purely probabilistic.

Start with the RHS of (
4
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+q^{c-m_{2}+1}}\left(\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-2+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})+q^{c-m_{2}+1}\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-2+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt=\frac{1}{1+q^{c-m_{2}+1}}\left(\frac{q^{k(c+2-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+2};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}-1}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}-1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}+q^{c-m_{2}+1}\frac{q^{(k-1)(c+2-m_{2})+\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+2};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}-1}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}-1\\
k-1\end{bmatrix}_{q}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}\left(q^{k}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}-1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}+\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}-1\\
k-1\end{bmatrix}_{q}\right).

Using the
q
-binomial analogue of Pascal’s identity (Lemma
A.1
) we have that,

q^{k}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}-1\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}+\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}-1\\
k-1\end{bmatrix}_{q}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}.

Thus (
4
) holds with
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}
.

To complete the proof we first notice that the boundary conditions given by (
2
) and (
3
) conform with the solution above and show that with the recursion (
4
) the distribution is completely fixed. To find the value of
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})
at a point
(\hat{m_{2}},k)
the recursion (
4
) requires values at the points
(\hat{m_{2}}-1,k)
and
(\hat{m_{2}}-1,k-1)
. Using the recursion iteratively we arrive to the boundary points
(x,x)
and
(x,0)
for any
x\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. The former case is handled by (
3
) and the latter by (
2
).
∎

3.2.
Symmetry of ASEP

In the previous section we considered the function
N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}
, the number of particles to the left of a site. Now we consider the distribution under the blocking measure of the function
N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}
. To do this we notice that there is a symmetry between particles and holes in ASEP, this symmetry is made precise in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\underline{\eta}
be an ASEP with right drift and blocking measure (for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
),

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\underline{\eta})=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-(i-c)\eta_{i}}}{1+q^{-(i-c)}}.

Consider the process given by
\hat{\underline{\eta}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\underline{1}-\underline{\eta}
. This process is an ASEP with left drift on the state space

\hat{\Omega}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i},\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{0}(1-z_{i})<\infty\}

and has stationary measure (for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
),

\hat{\underline{\mu}^{c}}(\hat{\underline{\eta}})=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(i-c)\hat{\eta_{i}}}}{1+q^{i-c}}.

Then for any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\hat{\underline{\mu}}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})

where
N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\sum\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}z_{i}
.

Proof.

By definition of
\hat{\underline{\eta}}
, particles in
\hat{\underline{\eta}}
move like holes in
\underline{\eta}
. That is, particles jump to the left at rate
1
and to the right at rate
q
, unless the jump is blocked. Thus, the probability there is a particle at site
i
, is exactly the probability that there isn’t in
\underline{\eta}
and similarly for when there isn’t a particle in
\hat{\underline{\eta}}
. So we have that
\hat{\underline{\mu}}^{c}=\bigotimes\limits_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\mu}_{i}^{c}
given by,

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{c}_{i}(1)

\displaystyle=\mu^{c}_{i}(0)=\frac{1}{1+q^{-(i-c)}}=\frac{q^{i-c}}{1+q^{i-c}}

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{i}^{c}(0)

\displaystyle=\mu^{c}_{i}(1)=\frac{q^{-(i-c)}}{1+q^{-(i-c)}}=\frac{1}{1+q^{i-c}}

is reversible stationary for this ASEP with left drift.
Hence if we define,
N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\sum\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}z_{i}
, it immediately follows that for any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\hat{\underline{\mu}}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\}).

∎

We will now see that considering particles to the right of say
m+\frac{1}{2}
in
\hat{\underline{\eta}}
is similar to considering particles to the left of
m+\frac{1}{2}
in
\underline{\eta}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\hat{\underline{\mu}}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\underline{\mu}^{2m+1-c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\}).

Proof.

We have that,

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\mu}}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{z}\in\hat{\Omega}:\\
\sum\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}z_{i}=n\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\hat{\mu}_{i}^{c}(z_{i})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{z}\in\hat{\Omega}:\\
\sum\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}z_{i}=n\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\hat{\mu}_{m+1+i}^{c}(z_{m+1+i})

and

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{2m+1-c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{z}\in\Omega:\\
\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{m}z_{i}=n\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{m}\mu_{i}^{2m+1-c}(z_{i})=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{z}\in\Omega:\\
\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{m}z_{i}=n\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{m-i}^{2m+1-c}(z_{m-i}).

We see that for
i\geq 0
the marginals
\mu^{2m+1-c}_{m-i}
and
\hat{\mu}^{c}_{m+1+i}
agree, for
z\in\{0,1\}

\mu^{2m+1-c}_{m-i}(z)=\frac{q^{-(m-i-(2m+1-c))z}}{1+q^{-(m-i-(2m+1-c))}}=\frac{q^{(m+1+i-c)z}}{1+q^{m+1+i-c}}=\hat{\mu}^{c}_{m+1+i}(z).

Thus for any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\hat{\underline{\mu}}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\underline{\mu}^{2m+1-c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\}).

∎

By combining Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
we have the following result.

Corollary 3.5
.

For any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\underline{\mu}^{2m+1-c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=n\})=\frac{q^{n(m-c)+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(q;q)_{n}(-q^{1+m-c};q)_{\infty}}.

By considering the symmetry between particles and holes for the asymmetric simple exclusion process we can give a probabilistic proof to a well-known identity from the theory of integer partitions, the Durfee rectangles identity.

Proof.

It is clear by definition of

\displaystyle\frac{q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-nc}}{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}-\ell c}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=\max\{-n,0\}}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-(n+k)c+\frac{(n+k)(n+k+1)}{2}}}{(-q^{1-c};q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{n+k}}\cdot\frac{q^{k(c-1)+\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c};q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{k}}

4.
The asymmetric simple exclusion process with second class particles

Now we consider the asymmetric simple exclusion process with 2 species of particles, first and second class particles. Both species of particles evolve according to ASEP dynamics obeying the exclusion rule within their own class, with first class particles viewing second class as ‘holes’. That is if,

•

a second class particle tries to jump into a space occupied by a first class particle the jump is blocked and nothing changes

•

a first class particle tries to jump into a space occupied by a second class particle it takes that space and the two particles swap positions.

To construct an ASEP with first class particles and some fixed
d>0
second class particles we use the basic coupling method. This idea of defining second class particles via a basic coupling is similar to the work of Ferrari, Kipnis and Saada
[
12
]
and later work of Tracy and Widom
[
19
]
as well as Balázs and Seppäläinen
[
7
]
(also work of Liggett
[
16
]
in 1976 considered coupling for simple exclusion processes).

We will study the behaviour of these second class particles under the blocking measure.

4.1.
Constructing a Basic Coupling of Two ASEPs

To construct a basic coupling we start with an ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
,
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}
, and also a process on the particle labels. For some given
d>0
, this label process is defined by an ASEP on the half infinite line
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with exactly
d
particles having left drift,
\{\underline{\gamma}(t)\}
. The dynamics of
\{\underline{\gamma}(t)\}
is restricted by the state of
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}
, such that a particle jump over the edge
(i,i+1)
in
\underline{\gamma}(t)
can only occur if the particles
i
and
i+1
in
\underline{\xi}(t)
are nearest neighbours. Lastly we construct a second ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
,
\{\underline{\eta}(t)\}
, by at each time removing the particles corresponding to the label process from
\underline{\xi}(t)
. We see that this defines a basic coupling of two ASEPs with the second class particles being defined as the particles corresponding to the label process. We make this procedure precise below.

4.1.1.
ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}

We consider an asymmetric simple exclusion process on ,

\Omega\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}:N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}(\underline{\eta}),N^{\overset{\rightarrow}{h}}(\underline{\eta})<\infty\}
, under the blocking measure
\underline{\mu}^{c}
(as in Section
3
) At time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
we will denote the state of the process by
\underline{\xi}(t)\in\Omega
.

At any time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
we attach to the state
\underline{\xi}(t)
a labelling of the particles. That is, we enumerate the particles in
\underline{\xi}(t)
from left to right starting from
0
for the left most particle and increasing as we go to the right. So a particle’s label is simply the number of particles to its left. When we consider second class particles we will be interested in the label of only
d>0
certain particles.

4.1.2.
ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with exactly
d
particles (Label process)

Alongside the asymmetric simple exclusion process
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
we also consider an ASEP on the half infinite integer line, with exactly
d>0
particles moving with a left drift. That is, an interacting particle system on the state space

\hat{\Omega}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}:\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}z_{i}=d\}
,
which evolves according to nearest neighbour particle jumps with jump rates, (for
i\geq 0
)

\hat{p}(z_{i},z_{i+1})=q\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 1\}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{q}(z_{i},z_{i+1})=\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 1\}.

Note that by the definition of the state space
\hat{\Omega}
any state has exactly
d
particles and so there is a closed boundary to the left of site
0
. At time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
we will denote the state of this process by
\underline{\gamma}(t)
.

Let
\underline{y}(t)=(y_{1}(t),...,y_{d}(t))
, with
0\leq y_{1}(t)<y_{2}(t)<...<y_{d}(t)
, be the vector of sites where the particles are in
\underline{\gamma}(t)
, i.e.
\forall j\in\{1,..,d\}
,

y_{j}(t)=i\text{ if }\gamma_{i}=1\text{ and }\sum\limits_{k=0}^{i}\gamma_{k}=j.

In fact, we will consider a restriction of the process
\{\underline{\gamma}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
by conditioning on the process
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
. We say that a particle jump which is possible over the edge
(i,i+1)
in the half-infinite ASEP can only happen if the particles with labels
i
and
i+1
are nearest neighbours at that time in the ASEP on
\Omega
. So, we adjust the jump rates
\hat{p}
and

Lemma 4.1
.

is an asymmetric simple exclusion process. Since
\underline{\xi}(t)

with the same jump rate (

to the left and
1

\underline{\xi}(t)

\underline{\eta}(t)
the particle at site

jumps to the left with rate equal to a right jump in
\{\underline{\gamma}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
so with rate
q
.

–

Similarly suppose that the particle at site

in
\underline{\gamma}(t)
jumps left (meaning particle
i-1
and
i

\underline{\xi}(t)
say at sites

and

\underline{\xi}(t)

\underline{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle p(\eta_{i}(t),\eta_{i+1}(t))=\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i}(t)\neq 0\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i+1}(t)\neq 1\}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{i}\xi(t)}(t)\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{i}\xi(t)-1}(t)\neq 1\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i+1}(t)=1\}

\displaystyle q(\eta_{i}(t),\eta_{i+1}(t))=q\Big(\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i+1}(t)\neq 0\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i}(t)\neq 1\}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{i-1}\xi(t)}(t)\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{i-1}\xi(t)+1}(t)\neq 1\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{i+1}(t)=1\}\Big)

4.2.
Distributional results for the basic coupling

Let us now look at some distributional results for the process with second class particles given we know the distribution of the ASEP with only first class particles.

Lemma 4.2
.

With the relation
\underline{\eta}=\underline{\xi}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\underline{\delta}_{X_{j}}
, when we reach stationarity for the coupling, we have that,

\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\nu}^{n}\hskip 2.84526pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 2.84526pt\underline{\eta}\sim\underline{\nu}^{n+d}.

Proof.

This is clear by definition of
\underline{\nu}^{n}
and since,

\displaystyle N(\underline{\xi})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-\xi_{i})-\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{0}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-\{\underline{\eta}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\underline{\delta}_{X_{j}}\}_{i})-\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{0}\{\underline{\eta}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\underline{\delta}_{X_{j}}\}_{i}

\displaystyle=N(\underline{\eta})-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}(\underline{\delta}_{X_{j}})_{i}

\displaystyle=N(\underline{\eta})-d.

Since
\underline{\nu}^{n}
is the unique stationary distribution for the marginal process conditioned on having conserved quantity
n
it holds that
\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\nu}^{n}\hskip 2.84526pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 2.84526pt\underline{\eta}\sim\underline{\nu}^{n+d}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

With the relation
\underline{\eta}=\underline{\xi}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\underline{\delta}_{X_{j}}
, when we reach stationarity for the coupling we have that,

\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c-d}\hskip 2.84526pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 2.84526pt\underline{\eta}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c}.

Proof.

First let us show that
\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c-d}\Rightarrow\underline{\eta}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c}
. Assume
\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c-d}
and consider,

\mathbb{P}(\underline{\eta}=z)=\mathbb{P}\left(\underline{\eta}=\underline{z},N(\underline{\eta})=N(\underline{z})\right)=\underline{\nu}^{N(\underline{z})}(\underline{z})\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(N(\underline{\eta})=N(\underline{z})\right).

Let
\tau
denote the left shift
(\tau\underline{y})_{i}=y_{i+1}
, by Lemma
2.3
we have that
\underline{\mu}^{c}(\tau^{j}\underline{y})=\underline{\mu}^{c+j}(\underline{y})
, and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(\underline{\eta})=N(\underline{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N(\underline{\xi})=N(\underline{z})-d\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{y}:N(\underline{y})=N(\underline{z})-d}\underline{\mu}^{c-d}(\underline{y})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{u}:N(\underline{u})=N(\underline{z})}\underline{\mu}^{c-d}(\tau^{d}\underline{u})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\underline{u}:N(\underline{u})=N(\underline{z})}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\underline{u})

\displaystyle=\underline{\mu}^{c}\left(\{N(\underline{\eta})=N(\underline{z})\}\right).

Hence,
\mathbb{P}(\underline{\eta}=z)=\underline{\nu}^{N(\underline{z})}(\underline{z})\cdot\underline{\mu}^{c}\left(\{N(\underline{\eta})=N(\underline{z})\}\right)=\underline{\mu}^{c}(\underline{z})
.
By a similar argument we can show that
\underline{\eta}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c}\Rightarrow\underline{\xi}\sim\underline{\mu}^{c-d}
.
∎

4.3.
Stationary Distribution for the Label Process

Let us now look at stationarity for the label process for the second class particles,
\underline{x}(t)
. By construction,
\underline{x}(t)
gives the positions of the particles in a half infinite ASEP with exactly
d
particles,
\underline{\gamma}(t)
, at time
t
. The evolution of this half infinite ASEP depends on the state of the ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
,
\underline{\xi}(t)
, as previously described.

The stationary distribution for the label process
\{\underline{x}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
is as given below, where we use the notation,
Z^{d}_{+}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\,=\{\underline{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:0\leq x_{1}<x_{2}<...<x_{d}\}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The process
\underline{x}(t)
has reversible stationary distribution
\underline{\pi}
where,

\underline{\pi}(\underline{x})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-q^{i}\right)\cdot q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for any }\underline{x}\in Z_{+}^{d}.

Proof.

(We adapt the proofs of Balázs and Seppäläinen
[
7
]
, Lemma 4.1 and also
[
8
]
, Proposition 3.1).

First we show that,
\sum\limits_{\underline{x}\in Z_{+}^{d}}\underline{\pi}(\underline{x})=1
.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\underline{x}\in Z_{+}^{d}}\underline{\pi}(\underline{x})

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1-q^{i})q^{-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot\sum\limits_{\underline{x}\in Z_{+}^{d}}q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1-q^{i})q^{-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot\sum\limits_{x_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{x_{2}=x_{1}+1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum\limits_{x_{d}=x_{d-1}+1}^{\infty}q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1-q^{i})q^{-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot\sum\limits_{x_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum\limits_{x_{d-1}=x_{d-2}+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-2}x_{i}+2x_{d-1}+n}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1-q^{i})q^{-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot\sum\limits_{x_{1}=0}^{\infty}q^{dx_{1}}\sum\limits_{n_{d-1}=1}^{\infty}q^{(d-1)n_{d-1}}\cdots\sum\limits_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}q^{n_{1}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1-q^{i})q^{-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-q^{d}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\frac{q^{i}}{1-q^{i}}

\displaystyle=q^{-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}i}=1.

Let’s consider the label process without the restrictions on the evolution given by the underlying asymmetric exclusion process on
\mathbb{Z}
,
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
. That is the process
\underline{y}(t)
, which gives the positions of the particles in an ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with exactly
d
particles and closed left boundary,
\{\underline{\gamma}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{t\geq 0}}
. The jump rates for this process are (for
i\geq 0
),

\hat{p}(z_{i},z_{i+1})=q\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 1\}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{q}(z_{i},z_{i+1})=\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 1\}.

For
j\in\{1,..,d\}
, let
\underline{e}_{j}
be the
d
long vector such that
(\underline{e}_{j})_{k}=\mathbb{I}\{k=j\}
. When the
j^{\text{th}}
particle jumps left then its position is decreased by 1,
\underline{y}(t)\mapsto\underline{y}(t)-\underline{e}_{j}
. Similarly, if the
j^{\text{th}}
particle jumps right then its position increases by 1,
\underline{y}(t)\mapsto\underline{y}(t)+\underline{e}_{j}
.

We know show that,

\underline{\pi}(\underline{y})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-q^{i}\right)\cdot q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for any }\underline{y}\in Z_{+}^{d},

is stationary for
\{\underline{y}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{t\geq 0}}
. To do this we check that detailed balance holds; that is for any
\underline{y}\in Z_{+}^{d}
and
j\in\{1,...,d\}
, we check that

\underline{\pi}(\underline{y})q\mathbb{I}\{z_{i}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z_{i+1}\neq 1\}=\underline{\pi}(\underline{y}+\underline{e}_{j})\mathbb{I}\{z^{(i,i+1)}_{i+1}\neq 0\}\mathbb{I}\{z^{(i,i+1)}_{i}\neq 1\},

where
\underline{z}\in\hat{\Omega}
is the current state of
\{\underline{\gamma}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
with the
j^{\text{th}}
particle at site
i\geq 0
. This trivially holds when a jump is not possible over the edge
(i,i+1)
. Now consider the case when
z_{i}=1
and
z_{i+1}=0
, then

\displaystyle\underline{\pi}(\underline{y})q

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-q^{i}\right)\cdot q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\cdot q

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-q^{i}\right)\cdot q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}+1-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-q^{i}\right)\cdot q^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}(\underline{y}+\underline{e}_{j})_{i}-\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}

\displaystyle=\underline{\pi}(\underline{y}+\underline{e}_{j}).

Now we re-introduce the restriction coming from the process
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
. Consider an approximating process
\underline{x}^{m}(t)
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
which has the same initial value,
\underline{x}^{m}(0)=\underline{x}(0)
. The process
\{\underline{x}^{m}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
evolves so that the underlying process
\{\underline{\xi}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
only restricts its motion between states in the range
\{\underline{x}\in Z_{+}^{d}:x_{d}<m\}
. We couple the processes together so that
\underline{x}(t)=\underline{x}^{m}(t)
until the first time that one of them exits the range
\{\underline{x}\in Z_{+}^{d}:x_{d}<m\}
.

Suppose we fix
m
for now. Let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<...
be a partition of time such that
t_{j}\nearrow\infty
and the state of the process
\underline{\xi}(t)
on the sites
(-\infty,j_{m}(t_{i})]
(where
j_{m}(t_{i})
is the site of the
m^{\text{th}}
particle at time
t_{i}
) is constant on each time interval
t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1})
. Then on each time interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
the process
\underline{x}^{m}(t)
is a continuous time Markov chain with jump rates, (for
k\in\{1,...,d\}
) when
\underline{x}+\underline{e}_{k}\in Z^{+}_{d}
,

\rho(\underline{x},\underline{x}+\underline{e}_{k})=\begin{cases}q\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}+1}=0\}\left(\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{I}\left\{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{j-1}\xi_{\ell}(t)=k\right\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{j}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{j+1}=1\}\right)&\text{ if }x_{k}\leq m\\
q\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}+1}=0\}&\text{ if }x_{k}>m,\end{cases}

with this rate being
0
if
\underline{x}+\underline{e}_{k}\notin Z^{+}_{d}
; and when
\underline{x}-\underline{e}_{k}\in Z^{+}_{d}
,

\rho(\underline{x},\underline{x}-\underline{e}_{k})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}-1}=0\}\left(\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{I}\left\{\sum\limits_{\ell=-\infty}^{j-1}\xi_{\ell}(t)=k-1\right\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{j}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{j+1}=1\}\right)&\text{ if }x_{k}\leq m+1\\
\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}}(t)=1\}\mathbb{I}\{\gamma_{x_{k}-1}=0\}&\text{ if }x_{k}>m+1,\end{cases}

with this rate being
0
when
\underline{x}-\underline{e}_{k}\notin Z^{+}_{d}
.

Since in the time intervals
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
the background giving the restriction of
\{\underline{y}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
to
\{\underline{x}^{m}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
is fixed we are in the setting of Proposition 5.10 of Liggett [II.,
[
17
]
] and so on each interval
t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1})
the measure
\underline{\pi}
is also reversible stationary for
\underline{x}^{m}(t)
. Thus
\underline{\pi}
is reversible stationary for
\{\underline{x}^{m}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
.

The coupling between
\{\underline{x}^{m}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
and
\{\underline{x}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
gives us that
\underline{x}^{m}(t)\xrightarrow[m\rightarrow\infty]{}\underline{x}(t)
a.s. , thus the measure
\underline{\pi}
is also reversible stationary for the process
\{\underline{x}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
.
∎

.

Since, either the second class particle is to the right of the site

or it is to the left of the site

We want the measures above to all have the same parameter so we look to change

to

Hence (

) becomes,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{-k}}{1+q^{c-1-m}}\underline{\mu}^{c}((\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})\cdot\mathbb{P}(x\geq k)\\
+\frac{q^{-(k+1)}}{1+q^{c-1-m}}\underline{\mu}^{c}((\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k+1\})\cdot\mathbb{P}(x\leq k)

By Theorem
4.4

,

\mathbb{P}(x\geq k)=\sum\limits_{i=k}^{\infty}(1-q)q^{i}=q^{k}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{P}(x\leq k)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}(1-q)q^{i}=1-q^{k+1}

thus we have that,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{\underline{\mu}^{c}((\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})}{1+q^{c-1-m}}+\frac{q^{-(k+1)}-1}{1+q^{c-1-m}}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k+1\}).

Rearranging gives the following recurrence,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k+1\})=\frac{q^{c-1-m}}{q^{-(k+1)}-1}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\}).

We now solve this recurrence,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{c-1-m}}{q^{-k}-1}\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k-1\})

We can calculate

and so we have that,

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k(c-1-m)}(-1)^{k}}{\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-q^{-(k-j)})}\prod\limits_{j\leq m}\frac{1}{1+q^{c-j}}

Future directions and open questions

As we saw in Section

, natural questions for the particle system can lead to probabilistic proofs of well-known combinatorial identities. The authors are exploring further directions.

In the paper of Balázs, Fretwell and Jay

With this in mind, perhaps by considering the number of particles to the left of some site or within a finite range or symmetry between holes and particles for these more general blocking systems we would see GFP versions of the Euler,

In the paper Balázs and Bowen

where the parts are the number of left jumps each particle is away from its position in
\underline{\tilde{\omega}}
. So the partitions that these states correspond to are partitions of
s
with up to
k
parts. Then
\sum\limits_{s=0}^{\infty}q^{s}\hat{g}(k,s)
is the generating function for such partitions, by conjugation of partitions we know that
\sum\limits_{s=0}^{\infty}q^{s}\hat{g}(k,s)=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{k}}
(for example see Section 6.2 of Andrews and Eriksson
[
3
]
). Now we find,

\underline{\mu}^{c}|_{(-\infty,m]}(\tilde{\underline{\omega}})=\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{m}\mu^{c}_{i}(\tilde{\omega}_{i})=\frac{\prod\limits_{i=m-k+1}^{m}q^{c-i}}{\prod\limits_{i=-\infty}^{m}(1+q^{c-i})}=\frac{q^{k(c-m)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(1+q^{c-m+i}}=\frac{q^{k(c-m)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m};q)_{\infty}}.

Putting this together we have that,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N_{m+\frac{1}{2}}^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k(c-m)+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}(-q^{c-m};q)_{\infty}}.

∎

Alternative proof of Lemma
3.2
.

For some
k\in\{0,...,\hat{m}_{2}\}
define a state
\underline{\omega}
on
[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]
such that,

\omega_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{for }i\in\{m_{2}-k,...,m_{2}-1\}\\
0&\text{for }i\in\{m_{1}+1,...,m_{2}-k-1\}.\end{cases}

We see that for any state
\underline{z}
defined on
[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]
there is some minimal number of right particle jumps needed to get to
\underline{\omega}
; that is
\underline{z}
is some
s
left jumps away from
\underline{\omega}
.
Thus, since a left jump changes the measure by a factor of
q
,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\underline{\mu}^{c}|_{[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]}(\underline{\omega})\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k(\hat{m_{2}}-k)}q^{s}g(k,s)

where
g(k,s)
counts the number of states on
[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]
that are
s
jumps away from
\underline{\omega}
. The probability under
\underline{\mu}^{c}|_{[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]}
of the state
\underline{\omega}
is,

\underline{\mu}^{c}|_{[m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1]}(\underline{\omega})=\prod\limits_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}-1}\mu^{c}_{i}(\omega_{i})=\frac{\prod\limits_{i=m_{2}-k}^{m_{2}-1}q^{c-i}}{\prod\limits_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}-1}(1+q^{c-i})}=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k-1}i}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\hat{m}_{2}}(1+q^{c-m_{2}+i})}=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}.

A state that is
s
left jumps away from
\underline{\omega}
can be viewed as a partition of
s
where the parts are the number of left jumps each particle is away from its position in
\underline{\omega}
. So the partitions which these states correspond to are partitions of
s
with up to
k
parts of size up to
\hat{m}_{2}-k
. Hence
\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k(\hat{m}_{2}-k)}q^{s}g(k,s)
is the generating function for these such partitions which can be written in the following way (for example see Section 7.2 of Andrews and Eriksson
[
3
]
),

\sum\limits_{s=0}^{k(\hat{m}_{2}-k)}q^{s}g(k,s)=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{m}_{2}\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}=\frac{(q;q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{\hat{m}_{2}-k}}

Putting all this together we find that,

\underline{\mu}^{c}(\{N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})-N^{\overset{\leftarrow}{p}}_{m_{1}+\frac{1}{2}}(\underline{z})=k\})=\frac{q^{k(c+1-m_{2})+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}(q;q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}}{(-q^{c-m_{2}+1};q)_{\hat{m}_{2}}(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{\hat{m}_{2}-k}}

∎
