Abstract
. Context sensitivity is essential for achieving the precision in inter-procedural static analysis. To be (fully) context sensitive, top-down analysis needs to fully inline all statements of the callees at each callsite, leading to statement explosion. Compositional analysis, which inlines summaries of the callees, scales up but often loses precision, as it is not strictly context sensitive. We propose a compositional and strictly context sensitive framework for static analysis. This framework is based on one key observation: a compositional static analysis often loses precision only on some critical statements that need to be analyzed context sensitively. Our approach hybridly inlines the critical statements and the summaries of non-critical statements of each callee, thus avoiding the re-analysis of non-critical ones. In addition, our analysis lazily summarizes the critical statements, by stopping propagating the critical statements once the calling context accumulated is adequate. Hybrid Inlining can be as precise as context sensitive top-down analysis. We have designed and implemented a pointer analysis based on this framework. It can analyze large Java programs from the Dacapo benchmark suite and industry in minutes. In our evaluation, compared to context insensitive analysis, Hybrid Inlining just brings 65% and 1% additional time overhead on Dacapo and industrial applications respectively.



1.
Introduction

Context sensitivity is essential for the precision of inter-procedural static analysis.
There are two ways to achieve inter-procedural analysis: top-down and compositional (i.e., bottom-up).
They both fall short with respect to context sensitivity in analyzing software at industrial scale.

To be context sensitive, a top-down analysis needs to inline explicitly or implicitly the statements of the called procedures at each callsite.
Given a large industrial software application, inlining all its procedures often results in statement explosion, especially when polymorphism is heavily used.
A compromise is to limit context sensitivity, such as
k
-callsite sensitivity
(
Shivers 1988
)
and
k
-object sensitivity
(
Milanova et al. 2005
)
.
However, this results in precision loss
(
Mangal et al. 2014
)
.
Scalability with high context sensitivity remains challenging even though recent studies have made significant progress with techniques like parallelization
(
Albarghouthi et al. 2012
)
, demand driven
(
Sridharan et al. 2005
;
Arzt et al. 2014
;
Heintze and Tardieu 2001
)
, selective context sensitivity
(
Jeong et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Lu and Xue 2019
)
, and incremental analysis
(
Shang et al. 2012
)
.

A compositional, i.e., bottom-up approach
(
Cousot and Cousot 2002
)
starts from the leaf procedures of the call graph, and inlines the summaries of the called procedures at each callsite.
Since each procedure is analyzed only once to obtain its summary, it is much easier for the
analysis to scale up.
However, the summaries are computed with all possible input states (i.e.,
top
) as a precondition. Inlining such summaries often leads to less precise results than inlining statements.
This makes compositional analysis non-strictly context sensitive.
Some works tackle context sensitivity with subtle designs of summaries
(
Dillig et al. 2011
;
Feng et al. 2015
;
Calcagno et al. 2009
;
Nystrom et al. 2004
;
Lattner et al. 2007
)
.
However, these works either are specific to a kind of properties or introduce disjunction which brings potential disjunctive explosion.

In this paper, we propose a compositional and context sensitive static analysis framework called
Hybrid Inlining
. Our key observation is that the summarization process in a compositional static analysis is imprecise only on some statements due to the absence of contexts, referred to as

critical
statements.
In other words, the summarization of non-critical statements with/without contexts generates the same postcondition for any precondition.
At each callsite, our framework hybridly inlines the critical statements (for context sensitivity) and the summaries of non-critical statements (for performance) of the called procedures.
Thus, the critical statements are propagated from callees to the contexts in their callers.
The propagation of a critical statement stops at a callsite where the context is
adequate
(since no further precision improvement with more propagation is possible).
Then the critical statement is summarized.
Compositional analysis with Hybrid Inlining can be as precise as an
\infty
-callsite sensitive top-down analysis.
Compared with conventional compositional context insensitive analysis, the non-critical statements are still analyzed once and for all, but the critical statements are analyzed multiple times, depending on the distinct contexts they encounter.

The key difference of our approach with all other compositional analyses
(
Dillig et al. 2011
;
Feng et al. 2015
;
Calcagno et al. 2009
;
Nystrom et al. 2004
;
Lattner et al. 2007
)
is that, our approach is about
when
to summarize the statements in a program, rather than
how
to summarize them.

Our approach can be seen as an application of selective context sensitivity on compositional analysis. While the idea of selective context sensitivity has been well explored in top-down analysis
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Lu and Xue 2019
)
, this paper is the first attempt on compositional analysis. Thanks to the fact that compositional analysis can summarize each statement without the context, our approach enables fine-grained selective context sensitivity in the level of statements rather than the level of procedures
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Lu and Xue 2019
)
. Another advantage of our approach is that the propagation of critical statements stops whenever the context is adequate, not necessarily at the root procedures. This avoids unnecessary propagation without sacrificing the ability to allow individual critical statements to be propagated far.

We have designed and implemented a non-trivial compositional pointer analysis for Java programs.
While compositional analysis is known for easy scalability, Hybrid Inlining enhances it with context sensitivity at almost no cost on performance.
In practice, the compositional and context sensitive pointer analysis can analyze large programs from Dacapo
(
Blackburn et al. 2006
)
and industry in minutes on a personal laptop.

In this paper, we make the following three key contributions:

•

We propose a Hybrid Inlining framework for performing compositional analysis, which brings context sensitivity at little cost on performance.

•

We instantiate it with a non-trivial pointer analysis.

•

We share our experience in applying the pointer analyses to 10 open source benchmarks from Dacapo and 10 real industrial large benchmarks. With Hybrid Inlining, the context sensitive analysis runs on large programs as fast as context insensitive analysis.

Comparison with conventional compositional approaches
.
Compared with the conventional compositional context insensitive analysis, Hybrid Inlining achieves context sensitivity at some additional performance overhead on analyzing the critical statement
tx
.
foo
(

Another way to make compositional analysis context sensitive is via disjunction. Many approaches e.g., multiple summaries and disjunctive abstract states
(
Dillig et al. 2011
;
Feng et al. 2015
;

)
fall into this category.
With this approach,

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{ if }\{tx\supseteq\{l_{Y}\}\}\in\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}\mapsto\mathfrak{a}\cup\{ret@foo\supseteq\mathnormal{par}_{1}@\mathnormal{foo}\}\\
\text{ if }\{tx\supseteq\{l_{Z}\}\}\in\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}\mapsto\mathfrak{a}\cup\{ret@foo\supseteq\{l_{14}\}\}\\
\end{array}

where

(resp.

) denotes an allocation site of object
Y
(resp.
Z
). This summary can achieve the same precision as Hybrid Inlining. However, the main limitation
(
Li et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2014
)
of these approaches is well-known.
The size of disjuncts increases exponentially in terms of the number of virtual invocation statements. Suppose that class

has a total of 10 different implementations and the procedure

Sometimes, it is just impossible to generate disjunction, because the number of possibilities are unbounded. Section
4.1
shows such an example.

3.
Methodology

In this section, we formalize the main idea of our framework. We first consider an application of Hybrid Inlining on flow sensitive analysis and then move to flow insensitive analysis.

We define our formalization on a simple yet general language:

\mathtt{P}:=\mathtt{stmt}\mid\mathtt{P}\circ\mathtt{P}\mid\mathtt{P}+\mathtt{P}\mid\mathtt{P}^{*}

In this language, a program
\mathtt{P}
can be an atomic statement
\mathtt{stmt}
(including assignments, condition tests, procedure invocations, etc.), a non-deterministic choice
\mathtt{P}+\mathtt{P}
, a sequential composition
\mathtt{P}\circ\mathtt{P}
or an iteration
\mathtt{P}^{*}
. We define the set of concrete states as
\mathchar 265\relax
and the concrete semantics of a program
\mathtt{P}
as
\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket:2^{\mathchar 265\relax}\Rightarrow 2^{\mathchar 265\relax}
. We denote the set of all statements as
\mathbb{S}
. We do not include
store
s and
load
s in this language since our framework can be applied on any analysis regardless of field sensitivity. In Section
4
, we will instantiate our framework to a field sensitive pointer analysis.

3.1.
Hybrid Inlining on Flow Sensitive Analysis

We first formalize a general top-down analysis and a general compositional analysis, and then show to combine the ideas behind them with Hybrid Inlining.

3.1.1.
A Top-down Flow Sensitive Analysis

This can be characterized by a set of abstract states
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A}
, an abstract transfer function
\mathbf{trans}^{t}:(\mathbb{S}\times\mathfrak{A})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A})
on atomic statements, and a join operator
\sqcup^{t}:\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
.
An abstract state
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A}
is connected to a set of concrete states via a concretization function
\gamma:\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow 2^{\mathchar 265\relax}
. Two special abstract states are
top

\top\in\mathfrak{A}
and
bottom

\bot\in\mathfrak{A}

such that
\gamma(\top)=\mathchar 265\relax
and
\gamma(\bot)=\emptyset
. The abstract transfer function
\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})
over-approximates the concrete semantics on statements:

\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}))\supseteq\llbracket\mathtt{stmt}\rrbracket(\gamma(\mathfrak{a}))
. The join operator over-approximates the union of the two sets of concrete states:
\gamma(\mathfrak{a}_{0}\sqcup^{t}\mathfrak{a}_{1})\supseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{a}_{0})\cup\gamma(\mathfrak{a}_{1})
.

The abstract semantics
\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}:\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
of a top-down flow sensitive analysis can be defined as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\llbracket\mathtt{stmt}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})&:=&\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{P}_{0}\circ\mathtt{P}_{1}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})&:=&\llbracket\mathtt{P}_{1}\rrbracket^{t}(\llbracket\mathtt{P}_{0}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a}))\\
\llbracket\mathtt{P}_{0}+\mathtt{P}_{1}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})&:=&\llbracket\mathtt{P}_{0}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})\sqcup^{t}\llbracket\mathtt{P}_{1}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{P}^{*}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})&:=&\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\lambda\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}.(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\sqcup^{t}\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})))\\
\end{array}

This definition of top-down flow sensitive is standard.
Here,
\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathfrak{a}}
denotes a process for computing the least fix-point with
\mathfrak{a}
as the initial state. Its soundness is obvious such that

\gamma(\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a}))\supseteq\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket(\gamma(\mathfrak{a}))

Example 3.1.

A popular flow sensitive analysis is the Polyhedra domain
(
Cousot and Halbwachs 1978
)
. Its abstract states are defined as linear inequalities on variables, such as:

a_{0}+\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}\leq 0

where
a_{i}
represents a constant and
x_{i}
represents a program variable. For the transfer functions and the join operator in the Polyhedra domain, we refer to
(
Cousot and Halbwachs 1978
)

3.1.2.
Strict Context Sensitivity

We define strict context sensitivity on procedure invocation statements
\mathtt{call\;proc}
. We write
\mathchar 256\relax:\mathtt{proc}\mapsto\mathtt{P}
to map a procedure
\mathtt{proc}
to its statements. The abstract semantics is strictly context sensitive if for all procedure invocation statements, we have:

\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{call\;proc})(\mathfrak{a})=\llbracket\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc})\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})

This requires the transfer function of an invocation statement to be as precise as re-analyzing the body of the procedure for each distinct precondition. In top-down analysis, this is almost equivalent to analyzing a fully inlined program. Thus, even though strict context sensitivity is essential for precision, it is hardly practical due to serious performance concerns.

3.1.3.
Compositional Flow Sensitive Analysis

Unlike top-down analysis, compositional analysis is built on abstract summaries rather than abstract states. Abstract summaries are usually relations on abstract states mapping preconditions to postconditions. A conventional summary can be defined as
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}:\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
. A conventional compositional analysis is characterized by the following operators:

•

The abstract transfer function
\mathbf{trans}^{c}:(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathbb{S})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
summarizes a statement in
\mathbb{S}
to a summary in
\mathfrak{C}
with a context in
\mathfrak{A}
. The context represents a condition that must be satisfied by the precondition;

•

A compositional operator
\mathbf{comp}^{c}:(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{C}\times\mathfrak{C})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
summarizes the effect of composition operation "
\circ
"; and

•

A join Operator
\mathbf{join}^{c}:(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{C}\times\mathfrak{C})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
summarizes the effect of non-deterministic choice "
+
".

A compositional analysis first summarizes a procedure as an abstract summary
\mathfrak{c}
and then applies it on any precondition
\mathfrak{a}
to compute the postcondition
\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})
. In the summarization process, a more precise context can often lead to a more precise summary. For instance, for the abstract transfer function
\mathbf{trans}^{c}
, let
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
be two contexts and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime\prime}
a precondition, the following always holds:

\forall\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}^{\prime},\mathfrak{a}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{A},\gamma(\mathfrak{a})\supseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})\supseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime\prime})\implies\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathfrak{a},\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime\prime}))\supseteq\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime},\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime\prime}))

As summarization with appropriate contexts can bring precision, a compositional analysis has to start with
\top
to fit into any precondition. We define the summarization process
\mathbf{sum}^{c}
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{stmt})&:=&\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})\\
\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}_{0}\circ\mathtt{P}_{1})&:=&\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}_{1}),\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}_{0}))\\
\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}_{0}+\mathtt{P}_{1})&:=&\mathbf{join}^{c}(\top,\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}_{0}),\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}_{1}))\\
\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}^{*})&:=&\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P})}(\lambda\mathfrak{c}.\mathbf{join}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c},\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}),\mathfrak{c})))\\
\end{array}

With
\mathbf{sum}^{c}
(which is straightforward), the abstract semantics of this compositional analysis is:

\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{c}(\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P})(\mathfrak{a})

This abstract semantics is sound if the abstract operators are sound in the following sense:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},&\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}))\supseteq\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}))\supseteq\llbracket\mathtt{stmt}\rrbracket\gamma(\mathfrak{a})\\
\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\forall\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1}\in\mathfrak{C},&\gamma(\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathfrak{c}_{0})(\mathfrak{a})))\supseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{c}_{1}(\mathfrak{c}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})))\\
\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\forall\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1}\in\mathfrak{C},&\gamma(\mathbf{join}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1})(\mathfrak{a}))\supseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{c}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})\sqcup^{t}\mathfrak{c}_{1}(\mathfrak{a}))\end{array}

We assume that
\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}))\supseteq\gamma(\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}))
, indicating that the transfer function from top-down analysis is at least as precise as the corresponding compositional analysis. This is a valid assumption, because, otherwise, we can just define
\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})
as
\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})
.

Recall that
\mathchar 256\relax:\mathtt{proc}\mapsto\mathtt{P}
maps a procedure
\mathtt{proc}
to its statements. The transfer function for procedure invocation is defined to be:

\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{call\;proc}):=\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc}))
.

From this definition, we can see that compared to top-down analysis, compositional analysis just needs to compute the summary
\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc}))
of each procedure once and for all. This ensures that its analysis time increases almost linearly as the size of the program
being analyzed. This represents a significant performance advantage of compositional analysis over top-down analysis.

Our definition is a standard abstract relation based compositional analysis.

Example 3.2.

A compositional version of the Polyhedra domain has been proposed in
(
Cousot and Cousot 2002
)
. It uses linear inequalites (same as abstract states) to store summaries. To represent the relations of a preconditions and its corresponding postcondition, their variables are renamed differently. For instance, to summarize a simple assignment
x = x + 1
, the summary is
x^{o}-x^{i}-1\leq 0\wedge x^{i}-x^{o}-1\leq 0
, where
x^{i}
(resp.
x^{o}
represents a variable from the precondition (resp., postcondition)). The composition and join operators in the compositional Polyhedra analysis just reuse the
meet
operator and the join operator in the top-down Polyhedra domain, respectively. For more details, we refer to
(
Cousot and Halbwachs 1978
)
and
(
Cousot and Cousot 2002
)
.

However, despite its performance advantage, compositional analysis is much less explored than top-down analysis in practice. This is because it is difficult to design a compositional analysis that is as precise as its top-down counterpart, especially with respect to strict context sensitivity.

3.1.4.
Difficulties in Designing Strictly Context Sensitive Compositional Analysis

Following the definition of strict context sensitivity, a compositional analysis is strict context sensitive iff:

\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc}))(\mathfrak{a})=\llbracket\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc})\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})

Therefore, for any precondition, applying the abstract summary of a procedure will produce the same postcondition as re-analyzing its body. To achieve this, the transfer functions
\mathbf{trans}^{c}
, the composition operator
\mathbf{comp}^{c}
and the join operator
\mathbf{join}^{c}
cannot lose precision. For any statement
\mathtt{stmt}
and abstract summaries
\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
the following three conditions must be thus satisfied:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt})=true&\text{iff}&\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})=\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})\\
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{comp}^{c}}(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1})=true&\text{iff}&\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathfrak{c}_{0})(\mathfrak{a}))=\mathfrak{c}_{1}(\mathfrak{c}_{0}(\mathfrak{a}))\\
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1})=true&\text{iff}&\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{join}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1})(\mathfrak{a})=\sqcup^{t}(\mathfrak{c}_{0}(\mathfrak{a}),\mathfrak{c}_{1}(\mathfrak{a}))\end{array}

It is very difficult (if not impossible) to design a compositional analysis satisfying all three conditions on non-trivial properties. There are two main difficulties: (1) a domain of computer representable abstract summaries (i.e., sets of symbolic relations) supporting composition and join is harder to design than abstract states in top-down analysis, and
(2) the behaviors of statements can be so complex that summarizing them without contexts can hardly keep all behaviors in the resulting summary. For instance, a compositional Polyhedra domain cannot precisely summarize any non-linear statements. In the case of a non-linear assignment
\mathtt{z=x*y}
, the compositional Polyhedra domain can only strongly update
\mathtt{z}
by
(-\infty,+\infty)
. On the other hand,
a top-down Polyhedra analysis can infer much more precise postcondition given a suitable precondition. If the precondition is
\mathtt{x}=2\wedge\mathtt{y}=2
, for example,

\mathtt{z}
can be
strongly updated to be 4.

Due to these two difficulties (especially the second one), compositional analysis is used relatively less often than top-down analysis in practice.
Conventional compositional analysis often gives up strict context sensitivity, or as in many prior works
(
Dillig et al. 2011
;
Feng et al. 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2014
)
, addressing the second difficulty with disjunctive summaries, can easily give rise to disjunctive explosion.

3.1.5.
Hybrid Inlining for Flow Sensitive Analysis

In this paper, we propose to make conventional compositional analysis strictly context sensitive without introducing disjunction. The approach is centered on one key observation: given a conventional compositional analysis, the predicate
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt})
evaluates to
False
only on some statements, which
are called
critical
statements.

We say that a statement
\mathtt{stmt}
is
critical
if
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt})
does not hold:

\exists\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})\supset\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})

As mentioned earlier, the non-linear statements are
critical
for the compositional Polyhedra domain. Similarly, we say that
a statement is

non-critical
if
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt})
holds:

\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})=\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})

All linear assignments are non-critical for the compositional Polyhedra domain. For instance, the statement
\mathtt{x=x+3}
can be summarized as
0\leq x^{o}-x^{i}-3\leq 0
. Given any precondition, this summary can generate the same postcondition as the top-down Polyhedra domain.

With this observation, we propose to treat critical and non-critical statements differently during the summarization. Unlike top-down analysis that inlines all statements of a callee and compositional analysis that inlines the summaries of all statements of the callee, our approach inlines all critical statements and the summaries of all non-critical statements in the callee. We achieve this with a

hybrid summary

\mathfrak{h}
, which is defined as follows:

\mathfrak{h}:=\mathtt{stmt}\mid\mathfrak{c}\mid\mathfrak{h}\circ\mathfrak{h}\mid\mathfrak{h}+\mathfrak{h}\mid\mathfrak{h}^{*}

A hybrid summary is actually a complete procedure body with all non-critical statements summarized, computed with a function
\mathbf{sum}^{h}
that summarizes any
\mathtt{P}
to a hybrid summary
\mathfrak{h}
.

On atomic statements,
\mathbf{sum}^{h}
just summarizes them with
\mathbf{trans}^{c}
if they are non-critical and keep them as they are if they are critical. This is defined as follows:

\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{stmt}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt}),&if\;\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt})\\
\mathtt{stmt},&otherwise.\end{array}\right.

On program composition
\mathtt{P}_{0}\circ\mathtt{P}_{1}
, if both
\mathtt{P}_{0}
and
\mathtt{P}_{1}
can be summarized into abstract relations in
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathbf{comp}^{c}
does not lose precision on them, they are summarized with
\mathbf{comp}^{c}
. Otherwise,
\mathbf{sum}^{h}
just keeps this operation in the hybrid summary:

\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0}\circ\mathtt{P}_{1}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1}),\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0})),&if\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0})\in\mathfrak{C}\wedge\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1})\in\mathfrak{C}\\
\wedge\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{comp}^{c}}(\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0}),\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1}))\end{array}\\
\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0})\circ\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1}),&otherwise.\\
\end{array}\right.

The summarization of non-deterministic choice and iteration follows the same principle:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0}+\mathtt{P}_{1}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{join}^{c}(\top,\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0}),\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1})),&if\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0})\in\mathfrak{C}\wedge\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1})\in\mathfrak{C}\\
\wedge\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}(\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0}),\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1}))\end{array}\\
\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{0})+\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}_{1}),&otherwise.\\
\end{array}\right.\\
\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}^{*}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P})}(\lambda\mathfrak{c}.\mathbf{join}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c},\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c},\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}))),&if\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P})\in\mathfrak{C}\wedge(\forall\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C},\\
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}(\mathfrak{c},\mathbf{comp}^{c}(\top,\mathfrak{c},\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}))\\
\quad\wedge\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{comp}^{c}}(\mathfrak{c},\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}))))\end{array}\\
\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P})^{*},&otherwise.\\
\end{array}\right.\end{array}

Now we define the abstract semantics with hybrid inlining. Our hybrid summary contains both statements and abstract summaries. As a result, we rely on an operator,
\mathbf{apply}^{h}
defined
straightforwardly below, to compute the postcondition with respect to a given precondition:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathtt{stmt},\mathfrak{a})&:=&\llbracket\mathtt{stmt}\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})\\
\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{a})&:=&\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})\\
\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}_{0}\circ\mathfrak{h}_{1},\mathfrak{a})&:=&\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}_{0},\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}_{1},\mathfrak{a}))\\
\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}_{0}+\mathfrak{h}_{1})(\mathfrak{a})&:=&\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}_{0},\mathfrak{a})\sqcup^{t}(\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}_{1},\mathfrak{a})\\
\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}^{*},\mathfrak{a})&:=&\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\lambda\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}.(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\sqcup^{t}\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})))\\
\end{array}

where
\mathbf{apply}^{h}
just applies abstract relations from the compositional domain and the abstract operators from the top-down domain as needed. The abstract semantics with hybrid inlining is:

\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{h}(\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}),\mathfrak{a})

Theorem 3.3.

The abstract semantics
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{h}
of the hybrid domain is sound if the
abstract semantics for both the underlying compositional domain
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{c}
and top-down domain
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{t}
are sound.

Theorem 3.4.

The abstract semantics
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{h}
of the hybrid domain is strictly context sensitive.

Example 3.5.

To apply Hybrid Inlining to the Polyhedra domain, we just need to implement the three predicates:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{comp}^{c}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}
.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}
evaluates all non-linear/linear statements to
false
/
true
. This can be implemented
with a simple syntactic check.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}
is always
true
, since the compositional operator (i.e., the
meet
operator from top-down Polyhedra) loses no precision.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}
evaluates to
true
when one operand represents a subset of concrete sets of the other. This can be implemented with the
comparison
operator from
(
Cousot and Halbwachs 1978
)
.

3.1.6.
Context Ready Predicate

In the aforementioned algorithm, critical statements are propagated to the root procedure and abstracted with the top-down analysis. However, this is not always necessary. We define the value set of a hybrid summary as
\mathbf{val}(\mathfrak{h}):=\{\mathfrak{a}\mid\exists\mathfrak{a}^{\prime},\mathfrak{a}=\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})\}
. During the process of computing the summary
\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathfrak{h}\circ\mathtt{stmt})
, the context of
\mathtt{stmt}
is actually
\mathbf{val}(\mathfrak{h})
. Thus,
\mathtt{stmt}
can be re-summarized by
\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathbf{val}(\mathfrak{h}),\mathtt{stmt})
.
As the hybrid summary grows in size, the context
\mathbf{val}(\mathfrak{h})
becomes more and more precise. For instance, the context
\mathbf{val}(\mathfrak{h}_{0}\circ\mathfrak{h}_{1})
for
\mathtt{stmt}
in
\mathfrak{h}_{0}\circ\mathfrak{h}_{1}\circ\mathtt{stmt}
is more precise than
\mathbf{val}(\mathfrak{h}_{1})
in
\mathfrak{h}_{1}\circ\mathtt{stmt}
.
We define a
context ready predicate

\mathchar 264\relax:\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow\{true,false\}
which evaluates whether the current context is enough for the summarization of the critical statements.
Our analysis can summarize the critical statements when the context is ready without propagating them to the root procedure. Thus, such context ready predicates limit the propagation of critical statements. They can be used to trade-off between performance and precision. However, if we define a context ready predicate as being also
adequate
, then no precision will be lost. An adequate predicate should satisfy the following condition:

\textbf{if }\mathchar 264\relax(\mathfrak{a})=true\textbf{ then }\forall\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A},\gamma(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})\subseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{a})\implies\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathfrak{a},\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})=\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})

An adequate predicate evaluates to
true
when the context is precise enough so that
\mathbf{trans}^{c}
loses no precision. As the critical statements propagate, their context can only be more and more precise. Thus, our approach does not need to propagate them unnecessarily.
For instance, the critical statement
\mathtt{z=x*y}
can be safely summarized if the s statement is
\mathtt{x=3}
is encountered during the propagation. In this case
\mathtt{x=3\circ z=x*y}
is
summarized precisely into
x=3\wedge z=3*y
.

Example 3.6.

In the compositional Polyhedra domain, there can be multiple adequate predicates
\mathchar 264\relax
for non-linear statements (e.g.,
x=y*z
). There are two cases.
One is that the variables in the right-hand side value expressions are confined by the same lower/upper bound (e.g.,
a\leq y\leq a
, where
a
is a constant). Another is that the variables in the right-hand side value expressions are not accessible in the precondition and have no relational constraints with other accessible variables.

Our context ready predicates can be applied to the composition and join operators in the same way as abstract transfer functions.

Theorem 3.7.

Hybrid inlining with adequate predicates are sound and strictly context sensitive.

Adequate predicates are actually important for the performance of hybrid inlining. Many industrial applications can easily have call stacks representing more than 100 callsites. Propagating all critical statements to the root procedures will cause unnecessary performance loss. In addition, hybrid inlining with adequate predicates induces a compositional analysis, rather than a hybrid top-down and compositional analysis, because it does not need the knowledge of the call string from the root procedures when summarizing a procedure. This makes it possible to reap the
benefits of compositional analysis, by, e.g., parallelizing and incrementalizing our
analysis easily. However, sometimes there can be too many critical statements. We can limit the maximal steps each critical statement can propagate as
k
(similar to
k
-callsite-sensitivity in top-down analysis). We call it
k
-
limit
Hybrid Inlining.

3.1.7.
Analysis Process and Recursion Handling

Our analysis is simple, as it just summarizes all reachable procedures with
\mathbf{sum}^{h}
and then evaluates all the root procedures with
\mathbf{apply}^{h}
. However, as revealed by the algorithm of
\mathbf{sum}^{h}
, the summarization of a procedure needs to use the hybrid summaries in its called procedures. Since all procedures are analyzed in a random order (unlike in other works, our analysis does not build a call graph via a pre-analysis), it can happen that some callees are not summarized yet when a caller is to be summarized. In this case, our analysis just turns to summarize all the callees first,
by adopting a simple depth-first search algorithm. However, we need to specifically handle call string recursion to ensure the termination of our algorithm. During the depth-first search, our algorithm maintains the accumulated call string. Once recursion is detected on the call string,
our analysis will treat it by unrolling it with a factor of
n
.

Our approach for handling recursion has two advantages: (1) it is simple to implement; and (2) its precision is comparable to an
(n-1)\times l
callsite sensitive top-down analysis at each recursion site, where
l
is the length of the recursive call string; In practice, we set
n=2
in order to strike a trade-off between precision and performance.

3.1.8.
Application Scope and Limitations

Our Hybrid Inlining framework is general and can be applied to any top-down analysis within the scope of Section
3.1.1
and any compositional analysis within the scope of Section
3.1.3
regardless of the underlying programming language used. All the technical definitions in Section
3.1.1

(
Cousot and Cousot 1977
)
and in Section
3.1.3

(
Sharir et al. 1978
)
are classic. To the best of our knowledge, most popular top-down and compositional flow sensitive analyses fall into these categories.

It is easy to integrate a specific analysis with Hybrid Inlining, which reuses the abstract operators from the underlying top-down analysis and compositional analysis. The additional work just involves defining the predicates
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{comp}^{c}}
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}
and
\mathchar 264\relax
for the underlying analysis, which determines whether a statement or an operator should be propagated or summarized. Such predicates are easy to implement as shown for the non-trivial abstract domain Polyhedra.

We do have one concern on Hybrid Inlining for flow sensitive analysis: the memory occupation of hybrid summaries can be large. Consider a program with three statements
\mathtt{stmt}_{0}\circ\mathtt{stmt}_{1}\circ\mathtt{stmt}_{2}
. It can be summarized to
\mathfrak{c}_{0}\circ\mathtt{stmt}_{1}\circ\mathfrak{c}_{2}
when
\mathtt{stmt}_{1}
is critical. In many abstract domains, an abstract summary
\mathfrak{c}_{0}
usually takes much larger memory than
\mathtt{stmt}_{0}
. If a program contains many critical statements, the memory occupation can become a serious concern. However, this concern can be adequately addressed in flow insensitive analysis, which will be described below.

3.2.
Flow Insensitive Analysis

Applying Hybrid Inlining on flow insensitive analysis follows the same principle on flow sensitive analysis. For simplicity, we reuse most of the notations from those on flow sensitive analysis.

3.2.1.
Top-Down Flow-Insensitive Analysis

This can be specified by the abstract transfer functions
\mathbf{trans}^{t}:(\mathbb{S}\times\mathfrak{A})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
, which over-approximate the behaviors of program statements and a join operator
\sqcup^{t}:\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
that over-approximates the union of sets of concrete states.
A flow insensitive analysis sees a procedure as a set of statements.
We define a function
\mathchar 259\relax
to map a program
\mathtt{P}
to the set of statements
\mathcal{S}
contained in
\mathtt{P}
.

The abstract semantics of the top-down flow insensitive analysis is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}=\mathbf{lfp}_{\bot}(\lambda\mathfrak{a}.\bigsqcup\nolimits^{t}\limits_{\mathtt{stmt}\in\mathchar 259\relax(\mathtt{P})}\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}))\end{array}

This standard definition is sound if its transfer functions and join operator are sound. The formal definition of soundness is similar to that of flow sensitive analysis and thus omitted.

The strict context sensitivity on flow insensitive analysis is also similar.
Recall that the function
\mathchar 256\relax:\mathtt{proc}\mapsto\mathtt{P}
maps a procedure
\mathtt{proc}
to its statements. The abstract semantics is strictly context sensitive if the following condition holds for all procedure invocation statements:

\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{call\;proc})(\mathfrak{a})=\llbracket\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc})\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})
.

As in the case of flow sensitive analysis, a top-down flow insensitive analysis also needs a
full inlining policy to achieve strict context sensitivity. This brings serious performance concerns.

3.2.2.
Compositional and Flow Insensitive Analysis

A conventional compositional and flow insensitive analysis can be characterized by a set of abstract summaries
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}:\mathfrak{A}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
, an abstract transfer function
\mathbf{trans}^{c}:(\mathbb{S}\times\mathfrak{A})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
, and a join Operator
\sqcup^{c}:(\mathfrak{C}\times\mathfrak{C})\Rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
. These operators are similar to those in flow sensitive analysis. The only difference is that the join operator
\sqcup^{c}
does not take any context as input any more. Instead, it always assumes the context to be
\top
. We will see the reason for this later. Compositional analysis comes with a summarization function
\mathbf{sum}^{c}
that maps a program to an abstract relation, which is defined below:

\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}):=\bigsqcup^{c}_{\mathtt{stmt}\in\mathchar 259\relax(\mathtt{P})}(\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt}))

The abstract semantics of this compositional analysis is defined as:

\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{c}(\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{P}))

The summarization of procedure invocation statement is defined as:

\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathtt{call\;proc}):=\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc}))
.

Compositional flow insensitive analysis just needs to compute the summary
\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc}))
for each procedure once and for all, by sharing
the same performance advantage as in the case of
compositional flow sensitive analysis.

Example 3.8.

The Andersen-style pointer analysis discussed in Section
2.1
falls into the category of compositional, flow insensitive and context insensitive analysis defined in this section.

A compositional flow insensitive analysis is strictly context sensitive if the following holds:

\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{sum}^{c}(\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc}))(\mathfrak{a})=\llbracket\mathchar 256\relax(\mathtt{proc})\rrbracket^{t}(\mathfrak{a})

This condition holds if for any statement
\mathtt{stmt}
and abstract summaries
\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
the following two conditions must be satisfied:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt}):=\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\top,\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})=\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a})

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1}):=\forall\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A},(\mathfrak{c}_{0}\sqcup^{c}\mathfrak{c}_{1})(\mathfrak{a})=\mathfrak{c}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})\sqcup^{t}\mathfrak{c}_{1}(\mathfrak{a})

The conditions for strict context sensitivity in compositional flow insensitive analysis are similar to those in compositional flow sensitive analysis. As the process of summarization must not lose any precision, the same challenges exist as in the case of flow sensitive analysis. However, Hybrid Inlining on flow insensitive analysis can be more friendly on memory consumption.

3.2.3.
Hybrid Inlining for Flow Insensitive Analysis

In flow insensitive analysis, a hybrid summary
\mathfrak{h}
is defined as a pair consisting of an abstract summary
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and a set of critical statements
\mathcal{S}\in 2^{\mathbb{S}}
to be propagated:

\mathfrak{h}:=(\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S})

Unlike in the case of flow sensitive analysis, a hybrid summary in flow insensitive analysis does not keep the program structure. We define the join operator
\sqcup^{h}
for hybrid summaries as follows:

(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathcal{S}_{0})\sqcup^{h}(\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1}):=(\mathfrak{c}_{0}\sqcup^{c}\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1})

The join operator
\sqcup^{h}
just joins the two hybrid summaries component-wise.

Hybrid summaries are computed with the following summarization function:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{stmt})&:=&\left\{\begin{aligned} (\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathtt{stmt}),\emptyset),\qquad&if\;\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}^{c}}(\mathtt{stmt})\\
(\bot,\{\mathtt{stmt}\}),\qquad&otherwise.\end{aligned}\right.\\
\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P})&:=&\bigsqcup^{h}_{\mathtt{stmt}\in\mathchar 259\relax(\mathtt{P})}\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{stmt})\end{array}

The summarization function
\mathbf{sum}^{h}
just summarizes non-critical statements and keeps the critical statements in the hybrid summary. For a program
\mathtt{P}
, it just keeps applying the join operator
\sqcup^{h}
to the summarization of each statement in
\mathchar 259\relax(\mathtt{P})
. Here, we do not delay the application
\sqcup^{h}
even if
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{join}^{c}}
does not hold. This may lead to precision loss, which trades precision for performance.

We define
\mathbf{apply}^{h}
to compute the postcondition of a hybrid summary for any precondition:

\mathbf{apply}^{h}((\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S}),\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{lfp}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\lambda\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}.(\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})\sqcup^{t}\bigsqcup^{t}_{\mathtt{stmt}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})))

The abstract semantics of hybrid inlining on flow insensitive analysis is defined as follows:

\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{h}(\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{P}),\mathfrak{a})

3.2.4.
Context Ready Predicates

As in the case of flow sensitive analysis, it is not necessary to
propagate critical statements to the root procedures. The context for a hybrid summary
(\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S})
is
\mathfrak{a}=\mathbf{apply}^{h}((\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S}),\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}(\bot))
, where
\mathtt{P}
represents the program that is not summarized in
(\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S})
. This context represents all possible preconditions for the given hybrid summary
(\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S})
. It is more precise than
\top
. Thus, if we summarize the critical statements in
\mathcal{S}
with
\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathbf{apply}^{h}(\mathtt{stmt},(\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S}),\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}(\bot)))
, we can gain some context sensitivity compared to conventional compositional analysis.
We define a context ready predicate
\mathchar 264\relax(\mathtt{stmt},\mathfrak{a})
, which takes as input a statement and the current context and produces as output
true
if the current context is enough for the underlying specific analysis.

Let
\mathfrak{a}_{c}=\mathbf{apply}^{h}((\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S}),\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}(\bot))
. Then we can
define the following
\mathbf{ready}
operator to summarize eagerly the critical statements as follows:

\mathbf{ready}(\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S}):=(\mathfrak{c}\sqcup^{c}\bigsqcup^{c}\{\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathfrak{a}_{c},\mathtt{stmt})\mid\mathtt{stmt}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mid\mathchar 264\relax(\mathfrak{a}_{c})}\},\mathcal{S}_{\mid\neg\mathchar 264\relax(\mathfrak{a}_{c})})

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mid\mathchar 264\relax(\mathfrak{a}_{c})}=\{\mathtt{stmt}\in\mathcal{S}\mid\mathchar 264\relax(\mathtt{stmt},\mathfrak{a}_{c})=true\}
represents all statements on which the current context is enough. The remaining statements are represented by
\mathcal{S}_{\mid\neg\mathchar 264\relax(\mathfrak{a}_{c})}
.

With
\mathbf{ready}
, the summarization of an invocation statement can be redefined as:

\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathtt{callproc}):=\mathbf{ready}(\mathbf{sum}^{h}(\mathchar 256\relax(proc)))

This will allow us to summarize the critical statements wherever their accumulated context is enough. We can also define an adequate context predicate as:

\textbf{if }\mathchar 264\relax(\mathfrak{a})=true\textbf{ then }\forall\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A},\gamma(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})\subseteq\gamma(\mathfrak{a})\implies\mathbf{trans}^{c}(\mathfrak{a},\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})=\mathbf{trans}^{t}(\mathtt{stmt})(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})

Theorem 3.11.

Hybrid inlining with adequate context predicates is sound and as precise as a version of hybrid inlining that propagates all critical statements to the root procedures.

The exact context
\mathfrak{a}_{c}=\mathbf{apply}^{h}((\mathfrak{c},\mathcal{S}),\llbracket\mathtt{P}\rrbracket^{t}(\bot))
may be hard to know during the analysis. However, for a specific analysis given, some other information can help decide the evaluation of
\mathchar 264\relax
. For instance, recall the example discussed in Section
2
. Without any knowledge of the exact context, our analysis can still decide that the context is adequate because the receiver
tx
and all its aliased variables are not accessible outside the scope of
bar

3.2.5.
Application Scope

Hybrid Inlining can be applied in any flow insensitive analysis that falls into the category defined in Section
3.2
, which is very general.

Compared to hybrid inlining on flow sensitive analysis, hybrid inlining on flow insensitive has one advantage: the representation
(\mathfrak{a},\mathcal{S})
is much more concise, with only one summary and a set of statements. This alleviates the memory occupation concern significantly.

4.
Instantiation on A Pointer Analysis

We instantiate our framework on pointer analysis, which is fundamental for many client analyses. However, compared to its top-down counterpart, compositional pointer analysis is much less explored in the literature. Thus, we design a compositional and flow insensitive pointer analysis first and then show how to apply Hybrid Inlining on it. The pointer analysis follows conventional Andersen’s style, but with a special constraint solving process so as to be compositional.

4.3.

To apply Hybrid Inlining, following the framework in Section
3.2

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}}
(to identify the critical statements). In this pointer analysis, the two kinds of critical statements can be easily identified with syntactic pattern matching and class hierarchy.

Defining
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{trans}}
is enough to apply
k

k

\mathchar 264\relax

\mathchar 264\relax_{a}
for statements involving index access (i.e.
\mathtt{lv}[v]

\mathchar 264\relax_{a}(\mathtt{lv}[v]=\mathtt{expr},\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{pt}(v,\mathfrak{a})\cap\mathbf{free}(\mathfrak{a})=\emptyset

where
\mathbf{free}(\mathfrak{a})
denotes the set of variables that are accessible outside the current procedure. This predicate indicates that the propagation stops when the points-to set of the index variable
v
does not contain any free variables, i.e., it cannot change any more. Thus, this context is adequate. Note that the variables in the critical statements in the hybrid summary are also free variables.

We also need an adequate context predicate
\mathchar 264\relax_{a}
for virtual invocation statements (i.e.
\mathtt{lv}=\mathtt{proc}(\mathtt{lv}_{0-n})
), which is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathchar 264\relax_{a}(\mathtt{lv}=\mathtt{proc}(\mathtt{lv}_{0-n}),\mathfrak{a}):=\mathbf{pt}(\mathtt{lv}_{0},\mathfrak{a})\cap\mathbf{free}(\mathfrak{a})=\emptyset

\displaystyle\vee|\mathbf{dispatch}(\mathtt{proc},\mathfrak{a})|=1

\mathtt{lv}_{0}
contains free variables. It also checks whether the callsite is monomorphic, which can happen when the type information gathered on the receiver has indicated such a unique implementation. This condition is adequate if the analysis does not consider error states (when, e.g., the receiver points to
null

5.
Evaluation

We have implemented our compositional pointer analysis in Java based on GraalVM
(
Oracle 2021
)
, which transforms Java bytecode to an SSA-like graph representation. That alleviates the impression brought by flow insensitivity of our pointer analysis. It is very simple to apply Hybrid Inlining. We just need to add some conditions for each kind of critical statements to decide whether they should be propagated or summarized. We have implemented Hybrid Inlining with both
k
-limit propagation steps and adequate context predicates. We evaluate our approach by addressing the following research questions:

RQ1:

What are the benefits of applying Hybrid Inlining on conventional compositional analysis with respect to precision and performance?

RQ2:

Why does Hybrid Inlining achieve such benefits?

RQ3:

What is the practical significance of our work when applied in the industry?

To our best knowledge, our approach is the
first
work on selective context sensitivity for compositional analysis. We take some selective context sensitivity works
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Lu et al. 2021
)
for top-down analysis implemented in
(
He et al. 2022
)
as a comparison. It is worth mentioning that, we do not intend to demonstrate which approach is better via this comparison, because their approaches and ours are built on very different grounds (top-down analysis vs compositional analysis). The comparison is conducted only to help evaluate the benefits of our work.

6.
Related Work

Context sensitivity on inter-procedural analysis has been an important research topic for many years. In this section, we briefly review some of the past research efforts on this. Inter-procedural analysis can be classified into top-down analysis and compositional analysis.

Top down analysis
. Inlining is a conventional way to perform inter-procedural analysis
(
Cousot and Cousot 1977
)
. However, full inlining can hardly scale up. Thus, calling contexts are often limited. For instance, in
k
-CFA
(
Shivers 1988
;
Grove et al. 1997
)
, the length of a call string is limited to be
k
. Various representations for adopting limited contexts have been proposed, including
k
-object
(
Milanova et al. 2005
;
Smaragdakis et al. 2011
)
,
k
-type
(
Smaragdakis et al. 2011
)
, and
k
-this
(
Lundberg et al. 2009
)
. A popular way for implementing such an analysis is to use BDDs
(
Whaley and Lam 2004
;
Berndl et al. 2003
)
and Datalog
(
Bravenboer and Smaragdakis 2009
)
.
Some work models inter-procedural analysis as a reachability problem
(
Reps et al. 1995
;
Sagiv et al. 1996
;
Zhang and Su 2017
)
, which can be solved
with a pushdown system
(
Reps et al. 2005
)
. Their work has been applied to various non-trivial analyses like pointer analysis
(
Späth et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Xu et al. 2009
;
Chatterjee et al. 2017
)
and taint analysis
(
Arzt et al. 2014
)
with field sensitivity.

Given the large size of current industrial software, top-down inter-procedural analysis can hardly scale up with high context sensitivity (i.e. a relatively large
k
). Many optimizations have been considered, including parallization
(
Albarghouthi et al. 2012
)
, demand driven analysis
(
Sridharan et al. 2005
;
Arzt et al. 2014
;
Heintze and Tardieu 2001
)
, selective context sensitivity
(
Jeong et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Lu and Xue 2019
)
, incremental analysis
(
Shang et al. 2012
)
. Another useful optimization
(
Wang et al. 2017
;
Zuo et al. 2021
)
utilizes disks to store the facts generated.

Compositional analysis
. Compositional analysis has been shown to be scalable in the industry
(
Calcagno et al. 2009
;
Distefano et al. 2019
)
, since it allows every procedure to be analyzed only once and facilitates
both incrementalization
(
Calcagno et al. 2009
)
and parallelization
(
Shi and Zhang 2020
)
. A theoretical investigation has been made in
(
Cousot and Cousot 2001
)
. Some recent advances fall mainly on designing new symbolic abstract domains to summarize pointer information
(
Dillig et al. 2011
;
Whaley and Rinard 1999
;
Chatterjee et al. 1999
;
Nystrom et al. 2004
)
and shape properties
(
Gulavani et al. 2011
;
Illous et al. 2020
)
. Symbolic numeric domains like Polyhedra
(
Cousot and Halbwachs 1978
)
can also enable compositional analysis. Compositional analyses
(
Nystrom et al. 2004
;
Shi et al. 2018
;
Illous et al. 2020

7.
Conclusion

We present a Hybrid Inlining framework, which inlines both the critical statements and the summarization of non-critical statements at each callsite. It combines the advantages of top-down analysis (context sensitivity) and compositional analysis (scalability). Our work represents the first attempt to apply selective context sensitivity on compositional analysis. What is more, our approach can assign context sensitivity in the level of statements, rather than procedures. This fine-grained context sensitivity alleviates performance loss. In our evaluation, the pointer analysis instantiated from our framework can accomplish context sensitivity with little performance loss (65% additional time overhead on Dacapo and 1% on industrial applications).
