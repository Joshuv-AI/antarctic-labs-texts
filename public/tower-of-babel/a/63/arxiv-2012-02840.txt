Ranking-based Convolutional Neural Network Models for Peptide-MHC Binding Prediction
Ziqi Chen 1 , Martin Renqiang Min 2 and Xia Ning 1,3,4

Abstract
T-cell receptors can recognize foreign peptides bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class-I proteins, and thus trigger the adaptive immune response. Therefore, identifying peptides that can bind to MHC class-I molecules plays a vital role in the design of peptide vaccines. Many computational methods, for example, the state-of-the-art allele-specific method 𝙼𝙷𝙲𝚏𝚕𝚞𝚛𝚛𝚢 \mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits , have been developed to predict the binding affinities between peptides and MHC molecules. In this manuscript, we develop two allele-specific Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based methods named 𝙲𝚘𝚗𝚟𝙼 \mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits and 𝚂𝚙𝙲𝚘𝚗𝚟𝙼 \mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits to tackle the binding prediction problem. Specifically, we formulate the problem as to optimize the rankings of peptide-MHC bindings via ranking-based learning objectives. Such optimization is more robust and tolerant to the measurement inaccuracy of binding affinities, and therefore enables more accurate prioritization of binding peptides. In addition, we develop a new position encoding method in 𝙲𝚘𝚗𝚟𝙼 \mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits and 𝚂𝚙𝙲𝚘𝚗𝚟𝙼 \mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits to better identify the most important amino acids for the binding events. We conduct a comprehensive set of experiments using the latest Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) datasets. Our experimental results demonstrate that our models significantly outperform the state-of-the-art methods including 𝙼𝙷𝙲𝚏𝚕𝚞𝚛𝚛𝚢 \mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits with an average percentage improvement of 6.70% on AUC and 17.10% on ROC5 across 128 alleles. \helveticabold 1 Keywords: deep learning, prioritization, peptide vaccine design, convolutional neural networks, attention

Introduction

Immunotherapy, an important treatment of cancers, treats the disease by boosting patients’ immune systems to
kill cancer cells
Waldman
et al. 2020
;
Esfahani et al. 2020
;
Couzin-Frankel 2013
;
Mellman
et al. 2011
.
To trigger patients’ adaptive immune responses, Cytotoxic T cells, also known as CD8+ T-cells, have to recognize
peptides presented on the cancer cell surface
Blum
et al. 2013
;
Valitutti et al. 1995
.
These peptides are fragments derived from self-proteins or pathogens by proteasomal proteolysis within the cell.
To have the peptides presented on the cell surface to be recognized by CB8 receptors,
they need to be brought from inside the cells to the cell surface, typically through binding with and transported by
major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class-I molecules.
To mimic natural occurring proteins from pathogens, synthetic peptide vaccines are developed
for therapeutic purposes
Purcell
et al. 2007
.
Therefore, to design successful peptide vaccines, it is critical to identify and study peptides that can bind with MHC
class-I molecules.

Many computational methods have been developed to predict the binding affinities between peptides and MHC class-I
molecules
O’Donnell et al. 2018
;
Han and Kim 2017
.
These existing computational methods can be categorized into two types: allele-specific methods and pan methods.
Allele-specific methods train one model for one allele such that the model can capture binding patterns specific to the allele, and thus it is better
customized to that allele
Lundegaard et al. 2008
;
O’Donnell et al. 2018
.
Pan methods train one model for all the alleles at a same time, and thus the information across different alleles can be shared and
integrated into a general model
Jurtz et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2018
.
These existing methods can achieve significant performance on the prediction of binding affinities.
However, most existing methods formulate the prediction problem as to predict the exact binding affinity values (e.g., IC
50
values) via regression.
Such formulations may suffer from two potential issues. First of all, they tend to be sensitive to the measurement errors when the measured IC
50

values are not accurate. In addition, many of these methods use ranking-based measurement such as Kendall’s Tau correlations
to measure the performance of regression-based methods
Bhattacharya et al. 2017
;
O’Donnell et al. 2020
.
This could lead to sub-optimal solution as small regression errors do not necessarily correlate
to large Kendall’s Tau.
Therefore, these methods are limited in their capability of prioritizing the most possible peptide-MHC pairs of high binding affinities.

In this study, we formulate the problem as to prioritize the most possible peptide-MHC binding pairs via ranking based learning.
We propose three ranking-based learning objectives such that through optimizing these objectives, we impose peptide-MHC pairs of high binding affinities ranked higher than
those of low binding affinities.
Coupled with these objectives, we develop two allele-specific Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based methods
with attention mechanism, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
.

\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
extracts local features of peptide sequences using 1D convolutional layers, and learns the importance of different positions in peptides
using self-attention mechanism.
In addition to the local features used in
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
,

\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
represents the peptide sequences at different granularity levels by leveraging both global and local features
of peptide sequences.
We also develop a new position encoding method together with self-attention mechanism so as to differentiate amino acids at different positions.
We compare the various combinations of model architectures and objective functions of our methods with the
state-of-the-art baseline
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits

O’Donnell et al. 2018
on IEDB datasets
Vita et al. 2018
.
Our experimental results demonstrate that our models significantly outperform the state-of-the-art
methods with an average percentage improvement of 6.70% on AUC and
17.10% on ROC5 across 128 alleles.

We summarize our contributions below:

•

We formulate the problem as to optimize the rankings of peptide-MHC pairs instead of predicting the exact binding affinity values.
Our experimental results demonstrate that our ranking-based learning is able to significantly improve the performance of
identifying the most possible peptide-MHC binding pairs.

•

We develop two allele-specific methods
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
with position encoding and self attention, which
enable a better learning of the importance of amino acids at different positions in determining peptide-MHC binding.

•

We incorporate both global and local features in
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
to better capture and learn from different granularities of
peptide sequence information.

•

Our methods outperform the state-of-the-art baseline
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
on IEDB datasets
O’Donnell et al. 2018
in prioritizing the most
possible peptide-MHC binding pairs.

3
Literature Review

The existing computational methods for peptide-MHC binding prediction can be generally classified into two
categories: linear regression-based methods and deep learning (DL)-based methods. Below, we present a literature
review for each of the categories, including the key ideas and the representative work.

3.1
Peptide Binding Prediction via Linear Regression

Many early developed methods on peptide-MHC binding prediction are based on linear regression.
For example, Peters
et al.

Peters and Sette 2005
proposed a method named Stabilized Matrix Method
(
\mathop{\mathtt{SMM}}\limits
), which applied linear regression to predict the binding affinities from one-hot encoded
vector representation of peptide sequences.
Kim
et al.

Kim
et al. 2009
derived a novel amino acid similarity matrix named Peptide:MHC Binding Energy
Covariance (PMBEC) matrix and incorporated it into the
\mathop{\mathtt{SMM}}\limits
approach to improve the performance of

\mathop{\mathtt{SMM}}\limits
. In PMBEC, each amino acid is represented by its
covariance of relative binding energy contributions with all other amino acids.
Some recent work
Bonsack et al. 2019
;
Zhao and Sher 2018
demonstrates these linear regression-based methods are
inferior to DL-based methods,
and therefore, in our work, we focus on DL-based methods.

3.2
Peptide Binding Prediction via Deep Learning

The DL-based models can be categorized into allele-specific methods and pan methods.
Allele-specific methods train a model for each allele and learn the binding patterns of each allele separately.
Instead, pan methods train a model for all alleles to learn all the binding patterns together within one model.
Both the methods use similar encoding methods such
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
encoding,
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
encoding and
\mathop{\mathtt{Word2Vec}}\limits

Goldberg and Levy 2014
.

3.2.1
Allele-Specific Deep Learning Methods

Among these allele-specific methods, Lundegaard
et al.

Lundegaard et al. 2008
proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{NetMHC3.0}}\limits
that takes
the embeddings of peptide sequences as input, and they
applied neural networks with one hidden layer to predict peptide-MHC binding for peptides of fixed length.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{NetMHC3.0}}\limits
, the hidden layer is a fully-connected (FC) layer, and learns the
global features of peptide sequences such as the position and types of specific amino acids.
Andreatta
et al.

Andreatta and
Nielsen 2015
extended
\mathop{\mathtt{NetMHC3.0}}\limits
to
\mathop{\mathtt{NetMHC4.0}}\limits
by padding
so that the model can handle peptides of variable length.
Kuksa
et al.

Kuksa
et al. 2015
developed two
nonlinear high-order methods including high-order neural networks (
\mathop{\mathtt{HONN}}\limits
) pre-trained with high-order semi-restricted
Boltzmann machine (RBM), and high-order kernel support vector machines (
\mathop{\mathtt{hkSVM}}\limits
).
Both the high-order RBMs and the high-order kernel are designed to capture the direct strong
high-order interactions between features.
Bhattacharya
et al.

Bhattacharya et al. 2017
developed a deep recurrent neural network
based on gated recurrent units (GRUs) to
capture the sequential features from peptides of various length.
Vang
et al.

Vang and Xie 2017
applied two layers of 1D convolution on the embeddings of peptide sequences
so as to learn local binding patterns existing in each
k
-mer amino acids.
O’Donnell
et al.

O’Donnell et al. 2018
designed a deep model named
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
with locally-connected layers.
This locally-connected layer is used to learn the position-specific local features from
the peptide sequences. MHCflurry has been demonstrated to achieve better or similar performance compared with most of
The other prediction methods
Boehm et al. 2019
;
Zhao and Sher 2018
.

3.2.2
Pan Deep Learning Methods

Nielsen
et al.
Nielsen and
Andreatta 2016
developed a DL-based pan method named NetMHCpan3.0.
This method takes the embedding of pseudo MHC sequences and peptide sequences as input, and then applies an ensemble of neural networks to
predict the binding affinities of peptide-MHC pairs.
Jurtz
et al.

Jurtz et al. 2017
extended NetMHCpan3.0 to NetMHCpan4.0 by training the model on both
binding affinity data and eluted ligand data.
Their model shares a hidden layer among two kinds of data and applies two different output layers to predict binding
affinities and eluted ligands, respectively, for peptide-MHC pairs.
Phloyphisut
et al.

Phloyphisut et al. 2019
developed a deep learning model, which uses
GRUs to learn the embeddings of peptides, and a FC layer to learn the embeddings
of alleles. The two types of embeddings are then concatenated to predict peptide-MHC binding probabilities.
Han
et al.

Han and Kim 2017
encoded peptide-MHC pairs into image-like array (ILA) data and applied
deep 2D convolutional neural networks to extract the possible peptide-MHC interactions from the ILA data.
Hu
et al.

Hu et al. 2018
combined a deep convolutional neural network with an attention module.
They applied multiple convolutional layers to extract features of different levels.
The extracted features are integrated with the features learned from attention mechanism
and fed into the output layer to predict binding affinities of peptide-MHC pairs.
O’Donnel
et al.

O’Donnell et al. 2020
developed a pan-allele binding affinity predictor
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry2.0}}\limits
BP and an allele-independent antigen
presentation predictor
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry2.0}}\limits
AP to calculate the presentation scores of peptide-MHC pairs.
Their binding affinity predictor includes upstream and downstream residues of peptides from their source proteins to improve
the performance of models.
Note that
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry2.0}}\limits
is a pan method and requires source proteins of peptides.
Therefore, we do not compare our methods with
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry2.0}}\limits
.

6.1.1
Peptide Representation in
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits

In
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
, we first represent each amino acid,
denoted as
\mbox{$\mathop{a}\limits$}_{j}
, in a peptide sequence, denoted as
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}=[\mbox{$\mathop{a}\limits$}_{1},...,\mbox{$\mathop{a}\limits$}_{j},...,\mbox{$\mathop{a}\limits$}_{n}]

The first information encodes the type of amino acids using BLOSUM62 matrix or
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits

The details about the encoding of amino acids are
described in Section
7.1.1

that different positions of peptides contribute differently to their binding to alleles.
In particular, each position of the peptide sequences, regardless of
which amino acid is at the position, will have two position vectors: for the
j

\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{o}}\limits$}_{j}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{o}}\limits$}_{-j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mbox{$\mathop{d_{o}}\limits$}\times 1}

Thus, each amino acid
\mbox{$\mathop{a}\limits$}_{j}
is represented as a feature vector

\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{f}}\limits$}_{j}=[\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{e}}\limits$}_{j};\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{o}}\limits$}_{j};\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{o}}\limits$}_{-j}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mbox{$\mathop{d_{e}}\limits$}+2\mbox{$\mathop{d_{o}}\limits$})\times 1}
,
where
\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{e}}\limits$}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mbox{$\mathop{d_{e}}\limits$}\times 1}

is
\mbox{$\mathop{a}\limits$}_{j}
’s embedding with an encoding method in Section

,

Model Architecture of
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits

The
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits

a fully-connected layer as demonstrated in Figure

.
The 1D convolutional layer takes the peptide feature matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mbox{$\mathop{d_{e}}\limits$}+2\mbox{$\mathop{d_{o}}\limits$})\times n}

feature patterns from the peptide sequences via 1D convolution using
\mathop{d_{r}}\limits

(\mbox{$\mathop{d_{e}}\limits$}+2\mbox{$\mathop{d_{o}}\limits$})\times k

The output of the 1D convolutional layer is an embedding matrix
\mbox{$\mathop{R}\limits$}=[\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{r}}\limits$}_{1},...,\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{r}}\limits$}_{(n-k+1)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\mbox{$\mathop{d_{r}}\limits$}\times(n-k+1)}

\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{r}}\limits$}_{i}

represents the embedding of the
i
-th
k
-mer
out of the
\mathop{d_{r}}\limits

Batch normalization is applied to fix the mean and variance of the embedding matrix
\mathop{R}\limits
in each batch.
After batch normalization, rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation is applied as the non-linear activation function on the
embedding matrix.
Then, we apply the self-attention mechanism

to convert the embedding matrix into an embedding vector
\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits
for the input peptide as follows.
First, the weight
w_{i}
for
i
-th
k

w_{i}=\frac{\exp(a_{i})}{\sum_{j}\exp(a_{j})},~~{a_{i}=\boldsymbol{v}\tanh(W\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{r}}\limits$}_{i}+\boldsymbol{b})},

(2)

where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{\mbox{$\mathop{d_{a}}\limits$}\times\mbox{$\mathop{d_{r}}\limits$}}
,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mbox{$\mathop{d_{a}}\limits$}\times 1}
and

and
\mathop{d_{a}}\limits

With the weight
w_{i}
on each

\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits$}=\sum_{i}^{n-k+1}{w_{i}\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{r}}\limits$}_{i}}.

(3)

The embedding vector
\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mbox{$\mathop{d_{r}}\limits$}\times 1}
of the input peptide is then fed into the fully-connected layer to predict
peptide binding at the output layer.

.

6.2
Convolutional Neural Networks with Global Kernels and Attention Layers (
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
)

We further develop
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
into a new model with global kernels, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
as in
Figure
1

The use of global kernels is inspired by Bhattacharya

As
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits

In order to use global kernels, we pad the peptide sequences of various lengths to length 15,

\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits

More details about padding are available later in Section
7.1.2
.
The padded peptide sequences will be encoded into a feature matrix
F_{G}

in the same way as in
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
, except that the position embeddings are not included
because the global kernels will overwrite the local information after the convolution.

Given the input
F_{G}
, the convolution using
d_{g}
global kernels will generate a vector
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}\times 1}
.
We concatenate
\boldsymbol{g}
and
\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits
as in
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
(i.e., the embedding vector calculated from local kernels)
to construct a local-global embedding vector
\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits$}^{\prime}=[\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits$};\boldsymbol{g}]
for the input peptide
sequence and feed
\mbox{$\mathop{\boldsymbol{c}}\limits$}^{\prime}

6.3
Loss Functions

We propose three pair-wise hinge loss functions, denoted as
\text{H}_{v}
,
\text{H}_{l}
and
\text{H}_{i}
, respectively.
We will compare these loss functions with the widely used mean-square loss function
O’Donnell et al. 2018
,
denoted as
MS
, in learning peptide bindings.

6.3.1
Hinge Loss Functions for Peptide Binding Ranking

We first evaluate the hinge loss as the loss function in conjunction with various model architectures.
The use of hinge loss is inspired by the following observation.
We noticed that in literature, peptide-MHC binding prediction is often formulated into either a regression
problem, in which the binding affinities between peptides and alleles are predicted, or a classification problem,
in which whether the peptide will bind to the allele is the target to predict.
However, in practice, it is also important to first prioritize the most promising peptides with acceptable binding
affinities for further assessment, whereas regression and classification are not optimal for prioritization.
Besides, recent work has already employed several evaluation metrics on top ranked peptides, for example,

Zeng and Gifford 2019
evaluated the performance through the true positive rate at 5% false positive rate,
which suggests the importance of top-ranked peptides in addition to accurate affinity prediction.
All of these inspire us to consider ranking based formulation for peptide prioritization.

Given two normalized binding affinity values
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{j}
of any two peptides
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}

with respect to an allele,
the allele-specific pair-wise ranking problem can be considered as to learn a scoring function

\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\cdot)
, such that

\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})>\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}),\text{ if }\ \mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}>\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{j}.

(4)

Please note that
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})
is a score for peptide
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}
, which is not necessarily close to the binding affinity

\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
, as long as it reconstructs the ranking structures among all peptides.
This allows the ranking based formulation more flexibility to identify the most promising peptides without accurately estimating
their binding affinities.
To learn such scoring functions, hinge loss is widely used, and thus we develop three hinge loss functions to emphasize
different aspects during peptide ranking.

Value-based Hinge Loss Function

The first hinge loss function, denoted as
\text{H}_{v}
, aims to well rank peptides with significantly different binding
affinities.
Given two peptides
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}
, this hinge loss function is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\text{H}_{v}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})=

\displaystyle\max(0,c+(\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{j})-(\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})),\text{ where }{\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}>\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j}},

(5)

where
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}
denotes the binding level of peptide
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}
according to the Table
1
;
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}>\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j}
denotes that
the binding level of peptide
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}
is higher than the peptide
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}
;

\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{j}
are the ground-truth normalized binding affinities of
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}
, respectively;

c>0
is a pre-specified constant to increase the difference between two predicted scores.

\text{H}_{v}
learns from two peptides of different binding levels and defines a margin value between two peptides as the difference of their
ground-truth binding affinities
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{j}
plus a constant
c
.
If two peptides
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
are on different binding levels
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}>\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j}
, and the difference of their predicted scores is smaller than the
margin
c+(\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{j})
,
this pair of peptides will contribute to the overall loss; otherwise, the loss of this pair will be 0.
Note that
\text{H}_{v}
is only defined on peptides of different binding levels.
For the peptides with the same or similar binding affinities,
\text{H}_{v}
allows incorrect ranking among them.

Level-based Hinge Loss Function

Instead of ranking with respect to the margin as in
\text{H}_{v}
,
we relax the ranking criterion and use a margin according to the difference of binding levels (Table
1
).
Thus, the second hinge loss, denoted as
\text{H}_{l}
, is defined as follows:

\mbox{$\text{H}_{l}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})=\max(0,r\times(\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j})-(\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}))),\text{where }\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}>\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j},

(6)

where
r>0
is a constant.
Given a pair of peptides in two different binding levels, similar to
\text{H}_{v}
,
\text{H}_{l}
requires that if the difference of their predicted scores is
smaller than a margin, this pair of peptides will contribute to the overall loss; otherwise, the loss of these two peptides will be 0.
However, unlike
\text{H}_{v}
, the margin defined in
\text{H}_{l}
depends on the difference of binding levels between two peptides (i.e.,
r\times(\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j})
).
Therefore, in
\text{H}_{l}
, the margin values of all the peptides
(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{1},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{2},\cdots,\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{n})
on the level
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}

to any other peptides on the level
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j}
will be the same (i.e.,
r\times(\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j})
).
Note that
\text{H}_{l}
is defined on peptides of different binding levels, and thus also allows incorrect ranking among peptides of same
binding levels as in
\text{H}_{v}
; the difference with
\text{H}_{v}
is on how the margin is calculated.

Constrained Level-based Hinge Loss Function

The third hinge loss function
\text{H}_{i}
extends
\text{H}_{l}
by adding a constraint that two peptides of a same binding level
can have similar predicted scores.
This hinge loss is defined as follows:

\mbox{$\text{H}_{i}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\max(0,r\times(\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j})-(\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}))),&\text{ if }\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}>\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j},\\
&\max(0,|\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})|-r),&\text{ if }\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}=\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j}.\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

Given a pair of peptides on a same binding level, the added constraint (the case if
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}=\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{j}
) requires that if the absolute difference

|\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})|
is smaller than the pre-specified margin
r
, the loss will be zero;
otherwise, this pair will have non-zero loss.
The constraint on the absolute difference allows incorrect ranking among peptides on a same binding
level as long as their predicted scores are similar.

6.3.2
Mean-Squares Loss

We also compare a mean-squares loss function, denoted as
MS
, proposed in
O’Donnell et al. 2018
;
Paul et al. 2019
,
to fit the entries without exact binding affinity values as below:

\displaystyle\mbox{MS}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i})^{2}\quad&&\text{if }\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}\ \text{is quantitative, }\\
&(\max(0,\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})-\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}))^{2}\quad&&\text{if }\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}\ \text{is qualitative and }l_{i}=1\text{ (i.e., negative binding)},\\
&(\max(0,\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})))^{2}\quad&&\text{if }\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}\ \text{is qualitative and }l_{i}>1\text{ (i.e., positive binding)},\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

where “
\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}\ \text{is quantitative}
" denotes that the peptide-MHC entry
\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}
is associated with an exact binding affinity value
x_{i}
.
In this case, the
MS
loss is calculated as the squared difference between the predicted score
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})
and
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
(
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
is
normalized from
x_{i}
as in Equation
1
).
In Equation
8
,
“
\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}
is qualitative" denotes that
\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}
is associated with a binding level
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}
instead of a binding affinity value (Table
1
).
In this case,
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
is normalized from the binding level thresholds (i.e.,
x_{i}\in\{100,500,1000,5000\}
in calculating
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
in Equation
1
).
When qualitative
\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}
has
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}=1
, that is, the peptide does not bind to the allele and the binding affinity is low (i.e., large binding affinity value),
the predicted score
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})
should be small enough compared to
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
in order not to increase the loss.
When quantitative
\mbox{$\mathop{m}\limits$}_{i}
has
\mbox{$\mathop{l}\limits$}_{i}>1
, that is, the peptide binds to the allele with reasonably high binding affinity (i.e., small binding affinity value),
the predicted score
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})
should be large enough compared to
\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}
in order not to increase the loss.

Note that in
MS
, the predicted score
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
needs to be normalized into range
[0,1]
. This is because
\mathop{b}\limits
is in
range
[0,1]
(Equation
1
) so that
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
needs to be in the same range and thus neither
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
nor
\mathop{b}\limits

will dominate the squared errors due to substantially large or small values.
However, in the three hinge loss functions (Equation
5
,
6
and
7
), the potential different
range between
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
and
\mathop{b}\limits
or
\mathop{l}\limits
could be accommodated by the constant
c
(Equation
5
) or
r

(Equation
6
and
6
), respectively.
In
MS
, we use sigmoid function to normalize
\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
.

7
Experimental Settings

7.1
Baseline methods

7.1.1
Encoding Methods

Encoding methods represent each amino acid with a vector. Popular encoding methods used by the previous works
include
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
encoding
O’Donnell et al. 2018
;
Jurtz et al. 2017
;
Nielsen and
Andreatta 2016
,
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
encoding
Phloyphisut et al. 2019
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2017

and
\mathop{\mathtt{Word2Vec}}\limits
embedding method
Vang and Xie 2017
.

\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
encoding utilizes the BLOSUM62 matrix
Henikoff and
Henikoff 1992
,
which measures the evolutionary divergence information among amino acids.
We use the
i
-th row of the
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
matrix as the feature of
i
-th amino acid.

\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
encoding represents the
i
-th natural amino acid with an one-hot vector, in which
all elements are ‘0’ except the
i
-th position as ‘1’.

\mathop{\mathtt{Word2Vec}}\limits
learns the embeddings of amino acids from their contexts in protein sequences or peptide sequences.
This embedding method requires learning on a large corpus of amino acid sequences,
and is much more complicated than
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
.
However, it is demonstrated
Phloyphisut et al. 2019

that
\mathop{\mathtt{Word2Vec}}\limits
embedding method is comparable to
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
encoding method, and
therefore, we use
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
encoding and
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
encoding, but not
\mathop{\mathtt{Word2Vec}}\limits
.
Besides the above encoding methods, we also evaluate another deep encoding method, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
,
in which the encoding of each amino acid is learned during the training process.

\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
encoding is not deterministic and is learned during the training process;
the dimension of embedding vector needs to be specified as a predefined hyper-parameter.
We also combine different representations of amino acid generated by the above three encoding methods. These combinations include

\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
+
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
+
\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
+
\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
+
\mathop{\mathtt{Onehot}}\limits
+
\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
, where
“+" represents concatenation of the embeddings of amino acid from different encoding methods.

7.1.2
Baseline Method - Local Connected Neural Networks
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits

O’Donnell et al. 2018
is a state-of-the-art deep model with locally-connected layers
for peptide binding prediction.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
, all peptides of length 8 to 15 are padded into length 15 by keeping the first and last four
residues and inserting the padding elements in the middle (e.g., "GGFVPNMLSV" is padded to "GGFVXXPNXXXMLSV").
The padded sequences are encoded into a feature matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{15\times 20}
using
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
encoding method.

\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
employs locally-connected layers to extract local feature patterns for each
k
-mers in peptide sequences.
Unlike CNN using common filters across all
k
-mer residues in peptides, locally-connected layers apply local filters for each
k
-mer
to encode the position-specific features.
The encoded feature embeddings for all
k
-mers are then concatenated into a vector, and fed into the fully-connected layer for binding prediction.
To the best of our knowledge,
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
is one of the best neural network model for allele-specific peptide
binding prediction problem.

7.2
Batch Generation

For models with
MS
as the loss function,
we randomly sample a batch of peptides as the training batch.
For models with the proposed pair-wise hinge loss functions (
\text{H}_{v}
,
\text{H}_{l}
,
\text{H}_{i}
), to reduce
computational costs, we construct pairs of peptides for each training batch from a sampled batch of peptides. Specifically, for
\text{H}_{v}
and
\text{H}_{l}
, each pair consists of two peptides from different binding levels;
and for
\text{H}_{i}
, the constructed pairs can consist of two peptides from the same or different binding levels.

7.3
Model Training

We use 5-fold cross validation to tune the hyper-parameters of all methods through a grid search approach.
For each allele, we run the grid search algorithm to find the optimal hyper-parameters
for the allele-specific model.
We apply stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to optimize the loss functions.
We use 10% of the training set as a validation set. This validation set is applied to adjust the learning rate dynamically
and determine the early stopping of training process.
We set the dimension of
\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
encoding method as 20, which is equal to the dimension of
\mathop{\mathtt{BLOSUM}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{Deep}}\limits
encoding method.
We also set both the constant
c
in
\text{H}_{v}
and the constant
r
in
\text{H}_{l}
and
\text{H}_{i}
as
0.2
.

7.4
Evaluation Metrics

We use 4 types of evaluation metrics, including average rank (
\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}
), hit rate (
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}
),
area under the roc curve (
\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}
), and
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}
, to evaluate the performance of the various model
architectures and loss functions.
Both
\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}
and
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}
metrics are employed to measure the effectiveness of our model on the prioritization of promising
peptides.
Specifically,
\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}
metric measures the average overall rankings of promising peptides;

\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}
metric measures the ratio of promising peptides ranked at top;

\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}
metric measures the possibility that positive peptides are ranked higher than negative peptides;

\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}
metric measures the ratio of positive peptides that are prioritized higher than top-
n
false positive peptides.
We denote
\mathop{s}_{i}
as the rank of peptide
\mathop{p}_{i}
based on their predicted scores,

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{{h}}
as the set of peptides with binding affinities
smaller than
\mathop{h}\limits
(e.g.,
\mbox{$\mathop{h}\limits$}=500\text{nM}
). Then
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{h}\limits$}}}
(e.g.,
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}
) is defined as follows,

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}$}_{h}=\frac{\sum_{{p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{{h}}}}\mbox{$\mathop{s}_{i}$}}{|\mathcal{P}_{{h}}|},\text{ where }\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{{h}}=\{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i}|\ \forall\mbox{$\mathop{b}\limits$}_{i}<\mbox{$\mathop{h}\limits$}\},

(9)

where
|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{{h}}|
is the size of
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{{h}}
. Smaller values of
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{h}\limits$}}}
indicate that
promising peptides are ranked higher in general, and thus better model performance.

The hit rate
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}$}_{h}
(e.g.,
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}_{\scriptsize{500}}}
) is defined as follows,

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}$}_{h}=\frac{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{t}\cap\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{h}|}{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{h}|},\text{ where }\mbox{$\mathop{t}\limits$}=|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{h}}|

(10)

where
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{t}
denotes the set of peptides with predicted scores ranked at top
\mathop{t}\limits
.
Larger values of
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}$}_{h}
indicate that more promising peptides are prioritized to top-
\mathop{t}\limits
by the model,
and thus better performance.

We use
\mbox{$\mathop{h}\limits$}=500\text{nM}
as the threshold to distinguish positive peptides and negative peptides, and apply two metrics for classification to
evaluate the model performance. The first classification metric
\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}
is
calculated as below,

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}$}=\frac{1}{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}|(|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}|-|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}|)}\sum_{i=1}^{\tiny{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}|}}\sum_{j=1}^{\tiny{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}|-|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}|}}\mathbbm{1}(\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})>\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j})),

(11)

where
\mathop{\mathcal{P}}
is the set of all peptides, and
|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}|
is the number of peptides in the dataset;

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}
is the set of all positive peptides, and
|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{\scriptsize{500}}|
is the number of positive peptides;

\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is an indicator function (
\mathbbm{1}(x)=1
if
x
is true, otherwise 0).
Larger values of
\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}
indicate that positive peptides are more likely to be ranked higher than negative peptides.

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}$}_{t}
(e.g.,
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}_{\scriptsize{5}}}
) score is the area under the roc curve up to
t
false positives.

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}$}_{t}
is calculated as below.

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}$}_{t}=\frac{1}{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{500}|\mbox{$\mathop{t}\limits$}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tiny{|\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{P}}$}_{500}|}}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathbbm{1}(\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{i})>\mbox{$\mathop{S}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}_{j}))

(12)

Larger values of
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}$}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{t}\limits$}}}
indicate that the model can prioritize more positive peptides up to first
\mathop{t}\limits
false positive peptides.
We use 7 metrics constructed from the above 4 types of metrics to evaluate the model performance. These 7 metrics include

\mathop{\mathtt{AR}_{\scriptsize{100}}}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}_{\scriptsize{100}}}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{AR}_{\scriptsize{500}}}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{HR}_{\scriptsize{500}}}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}_{\scriptsize{5}}}
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}_{\scriptsize{10}}}
.

In order to compare the models with respect to one single metric in a holistic way,
we define a hybrid metric
\mathop{H}\limits
by combining all the evaluation metrics .
Given a model trained with a set of hyper-parameters
\mathcal{Y}
,
we denote its performance on metric “
\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits
” (
\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits
=
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}$}_{h}
,
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{HR}}$}_{h}
,
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AUC}}$}_{h}
,
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{ROC}}$}_{h}
)
as
\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$}(\mathcal{Y})
,
and the best metric value as
\text{best}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$})=\max_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$}(\mathcal{Y}))
.
Then, the hybrid metric
\mathop{H}\limits
for a model with hyper-parameters
\mathcal{Y}
is defined as below,

\mbox{$\mathop{H}\limits$}(\mathcal{Y})=\sum_{\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$}}{\frac{\mathbb{I}(\downarrow\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$})\times(\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$}(\mathcal{Y})-\text{best}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$}))}{\text{best}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$})}},

(13)

where
\mathbb{I}(\downarrow\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$})
is an identity function:

\mathbb{I}(\downarrow\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$})=+1
if smaller values on metric
\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits
indicate better performance;

\mathbb{I}(\downarrow\mbox{$\mathop{\text{mtrc}}\limits$})=-1
otherwise.
For metrics
\mathop{\mathtt{AR}_{\scriptsize{100}}}
and
\mathop{\mathtt{AR}_{\scriptsize{500}}}
, a smaller value represents a better model performance.

extends
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
with global kernels to extract global features from the entire peptide sequences.
The better performance of
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
than that of
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
indicates that global features could capture useful information from entire peptide sequences, which are typically short,
for binding prediction.
In addition,
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
outperforms
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
on average.
The difference between
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
is that
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
learns position-specific features via position-specific kernels,
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
learns local features via kernels that are common to all the locations.
As demonstrated in other studies
O’Donnell et al. 2018
that certain positions of peptides are more critical for their binding to alleles,
the better performance of
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
over
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
could be attributed to its position-specific feature learning capablity.
Moreover, since the peptide sequences are usually short (8-15 amino-acid long), it is very likely that these short sequences do not have
strong local patterns, and thus
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
could not capture a lot of useful local information.
In comparison with
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
integrates both local features via its
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
component and global features via
global kernels. Such integration could enable
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
to capture global information as compensation to local features, and thus
to improve model performance.

Loss Function Comparison

Table

also demonstrates that

\text{H}_{v}
is the most effective loss function in combination with each of the learning architectures, and
all three hinge loss functions
\text{H}_{v}
,
\text{H}_{l}
and
\text{H}_{i}
can outperform the
MS
loss function.
For example, for
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
, the performance improvement
of
\text{H}_{v}
,
\text{H}_{l}
,
\text{H}_{i}
and
MS
in terms of
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{AR}}$}_{100}
follows the order

\mbox{$\text{H}_{v}$}(11.58\%)>\mbox{$\text{H}_{i}$}(10.01\%)>\mbox{$\text{H}_{l}$}(8.97\%)>\mbox{MS}(8.66\%)
, compared with the baseline
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
with
MS
.
This trend is also consistent for
\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
.
The better performance of
\text{H}_{v}
may be due to the use of a margin in the loss function that is determined by the
binding affinity values (Equation
5
).
This value-based margin could enforce granular ranking among peptides even when they are from a same binding level.
In
\text{H}_{l}
(Equation
6
) and
\text{H}_{i}
(Equation
7
),
the margins are determined based on the levels of the binding affinities. While
\text{H}_{l}
and
\text{H}_{i}
can still produce
ranking structures of peptides according to their binding levels, they may fall short to differentiate peptides of a same binding level.

All the three hinge loss functions
\text{H}_{v}
,
\text{H}_{l}
and
\text{H}_{i}
outperform
MS
across all the model architectures.
This might be due to two reasons.
First, the pairwise hinge loss functions are less sensitive to the imbalance of different amounts of peptides,
either strongly binding or weakly/non-binding, by sampling and constructing pairs from respective peptides.
Thus, the learning is not biased by one type of peptides, and the models can better
learn the difference among different types of peptides, and accordingly produce better ranking orders of peptides.
Second, the pairwise hinge loss functions can tolerate insignificant measurement errors to some extent.
All the three hinge loss functions do not consider pairs of peptides with similar binding affinities.
This enables our models to be more robust and tolerant to noisy data due to the measurement inaccuracy of binding affinities.

9
Conclusions

Our methods contribute to the study of peptide-MHC binding prediction problem in two ways.
First, instead of predicting the exact binding affinities values as in the existing methods, we
formulate the problem as to prioritize most possible peptide-MHC binding pairs via a ranking-based learning.
We developed three pairwise ranking-based learning objectives for such prioritization, and the corresponding
learning methods that impose the peptide-MHC pairs of higher binding affinities ranked above those with lower
binding affinities with a certain margin.
Our experimental results in comparison with the state-of-the-art regression based methods demonstrate the
superior prediction performance of our methods in prioritizing and identifying the most likely binding peptides.
In addition to the the learning objectives, we also developed two convolutional neural network-based model architectures

\mathop{\mathtt{ConvM}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{SpConvM}}\limits
, which incorporate a new position encoding method and attention mechanism that
differentiate the importance of amino acids at different positions in determining peptide-MHC binding.
Our experiments show that the learned important positions and amino acids for allele HLA-A-0201 conform to the biological
understanding of the allele.
Our experimental results also demonstrate that our model architectures can achieve superior or at least comparable performance
with the state-of-the-art allele-specific baseline
\mathop{\mathtt{MHCflurry}}\limits
.
