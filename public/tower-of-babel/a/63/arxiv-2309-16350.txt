Intrinsic Hölder spaces for fractional kinetic operators
Maria Manfredini, Stefano Pagliarani, Sergio Polidoro

Abstract
We introduce anisotropic Hölder spaces useful for the study of the regularity theory for non local kinetic operators ℒ \mathscr{L} whose prototypical example is ℒ u ( t , x , v ) = ∫ ℝ d C d , s | v − v ′ | d + 2 ​ s ( u ( t , x , v ′ ) − u ( t , x , v ) ) d v ′ + ⟨ v , ∇ x ⟩ + ∂ t , ( t , x , v ) ∈ ℝ × ℝ 2 ​ d . \mathscr{L}u(t,x,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{C_{d,s}}{|v-v^{\prime}|^{d+2s}}(u(t,x,v^{\prime})-u(t,x,v))\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}+\langle v,\nabla_{x}\rangle+\partial_{t},\quad(t,x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}. The Hölder spaces are defined in terms of an anisotropic distance relevant to the Galilean geometric structure on ℝ × ℝ 2 ​ d {\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d} the operator ℒ \mathscr{L} is invariant with respect to. We prove an intrinsic Taylor-like formula, whose reminder is estimated in terms of the anisotropic distance of the Galilean structure. Our achievements naturally extend analogous known results for purely differential operators on Lie groups.



Keywords
: Fractional kinetic operators, Kolmogorov operators, Hörmander’s
condition, Hölder spaces, Taylor formula.

1
Introduction

We consider Hölder spaces and Taylor-like formulas useful for the study of the regularity theory of the solutions to
\mathscr{L}u=f
, being
\mathscr{L}
a non local kinetic operator of the form

\mathscr{L}u(t,x,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}K(t,x,v,v^{\prime})(u(t,x,v^{\prime})-u(t,x,v))\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}+Yu,\qquad(t,x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

(1.1)

where

Y=\langle v,\nabla_{x}\rangle+\partial_{t},\qquad v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(1.2)

The integral part is of order
2s
, with
s\in]0,1[
, in the following sense

\frac{c^{-}}{|v-v^{\prime}|^{d+2s}}\leq K(t,x,v,v^{\prime})\leq\frac{c^{+}}{|v-v^{\prime}|^{d+2s}},\qquad(t,x,v,v^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3d},

for some positive constants
c^{-}
and
c^{+}
. One notable particular instance of
\mathscr{L}
is the fractional kinetic Fokker–Planck operator

\mathscr{L}_{s}=(-\Delta_{v})^{s}+Y,

(1.3)

which is the operator in (
1.1
) with

K(t,x,v,v^{\prime})=\frac{C_{d,s}}{|v-v^{\prime}|^{d+2s}},

(1.4)

for a suitable positive constant
C_{d,s}
. Note that
\mathscr{L}_{s}=(-\Delta_{v})^{s}+Y
is also related to the infinitesimal generator of an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process, with
\alpha=2s
.

In the purely diffusive setting, which can be seen as the limiting case
s=1
,
\mathscr{L}_{1}=-\Delta_{v}+Y
is a differential hypoelliptic operator. This means that every distributional solution
u
to the equation
-\Delta_{v}u+Yu=f
is a smooth function whenever
f
is smooth. Indeed, setting the primary vector fields

Z_{i}:=\partial_{v_{i}},\qquad i=1,\cdots,d,

(1.5)

we obtain that
\mathscr{L}_{1}
writes in the form
\mathscr{L}_{1}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}+Y
and the system
Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{d},Y
satisfies the so-called Hörmander condition, namely

\text{rank }\text{Lie}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d},Y)=2d+1.

(1.6)

This is a straightforward consequence of

[\partial_{v_{j}},Y]=\partial_{x_{j}},\qquad j=1,\dots,d.

(1.7)

We emphasize that the regularity properties of the Hörmander’s operators depend on a non-Euclidean underlying structure (see the survey
Anceschi and
Polidoro 2020
). In the setting of the kinetic operator
\mathscr{L}
, this structure agrees with the Galilean translation (see (
2.10
) below).

In this work we rely on the geometric structure introduced for the differential operator
\mathscr{L}_{1}
, in order to study the fractional operator
\mathscr{L}_{s}
for
0<s<1
. We give a definition of
intrinsic Hölder spaces
, which extends the one indroduced by
Pagliarani
et al. 2016
, and we prove a Taylor polynomial approximation of a function
f
belonging to this Hölder space.

We conclude this section with some remarks about the intrinsic Hölder spaces and the applications of our main results. These spaces are
anisotropic
, as the variables
v
and
x
in the Galileian group have a different role. Moreover, the definition of
intrinsic Hölder spaces
is based on the non-Euclidean quasi-distance (
2.18
) of the underlying Galileian group. Remark
2.8
contains a brief discussion on our definition of anisotropic Hölder spaces compared with other definition present in the literature.
In the purely differential setting, such Hölder spaces were studied by several authors, and a characterization relating the regularity along the vector fields
\partial_{v_{1}},\dots,\partial_{v_{d}},Y
to the existence of appropriate instrinsic Taylor formulas was given by
Pagliarani
et al. 2016
. We refer to the articles
Bonfiglioli 2009
and
Arena
et al. 2010
for similar Taylor formulas on homogeneous groups. The Taylor approximation of a solution to a PDE is a useful tool in the proof of Schauder estimates. We refer to
Polidoro
et al. 2022
, where the regularity of classical solutions to degenerate Kolmogorov equations is obtained by using the method introduced in
Wang 2006
for uniformly elliptic and parabolic equations. We also recall the article
Imbert and
Mouhot 2021
where Taylor approximation results and Schauder estimates for kinetic equations are proved.
In the fractional setting, Schauder estimates for the solutions to
\mathscr{L}u=0
have been recently proved by
Imbert and
Silvestre 2021
, in suitable Hölder spaces that take into account the intrinsic geometry of the Galilean group. We emphasize that the main results of this note do apply to the operators
\mathscr{L}
considered in the aforementioned reference.

Our main results also apply to a non-linear non-local version of (
1.3
), that is

\mathscr{L}_{s,p}=(-\Delta_{v})^{s}_{p}+Y,

(1.8)

considered for
p\in]1,\infty[
and
s\in]0,1[
in
Anceschi and
Piccinini 2023
. In this case, the kernel
K
in (
1.1
) also depends on the unknown function
u
and the term
(-\Delta_{v})_{p}^{s}u
takes the following form

(-\Delta_{v})^{s}_{p}u(t,x,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{C_{d,p,s}}{|v-v^{\prime}|^{d+ps}}|u(t,x,v)-u(t,x,v^{\prime})|^{p-2}\big(u(t,x,v)-u(t,x,v^{\prime})\big)\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}.

(1.9)

We finally remark that not only anisotropic spaces of Hölder continuous functions have been considered in literature for the study of kinetic equations of the form
\mathscr{L}u=f
. Indeed,
Pascucci and
Pesce 2022
prove intrinsic Taylor expansion for anisotropic Sobolev-Slobodeckij spaces, and prove continuous embeddings into Lorentz and intrinsic Hölder spaces.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 contains some recalls on the non-Euclidean geometry relevant to the kinetic operator
\mathscr{L}_{1}
. Note that the study of the non local operator
\mathscr{L}_{s}
relies on the same geometric structure. In Section 2 the notion of intrinsic regularity and intrinsic Hölder spaces and the statement of Theorem
2.7
, which is the main results of this note, are given. Some examples illustrate the meaning of the definitions and the main results. Section 3 contains some preliminaries the the proof of the main results, which is given in Section 4. Section 5 contains a local version of our main results, which generalizes Theorem
2.7
in that it applies to the more general geometric framework of the
non-homogeneous Lie groups
.

2
Hölder spaces and Taylor polynomials

In this part we fix some notation for the geometric structure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d+1}
that will be used in this work. We recall that, remarkably,
\mathscr{L}_{s}
in (
1.3
) has the property of being invariant with respect to left translations in the group

\left({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},\circ\right)
, where the non-commutative group law “
\circ
” is defined by

z_{1}\circ z_{2}=\left(t_{1}+t_{2},x_{1}+x_{2}+t_{2}v_{1},v_{1}+v_{2}\right),\qquad z_{1}=(t_{1},x_{1},v_{1}),z_{2}=(t_{2},x_{2},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

(2.10)

Precisely, we have

\big(\mathscr{L}u^{(z_{1})}\big)(z_{2})=(\mathscr{L}u)(z_{1}\circ z_{2}).

(2.11)

where

u^{(z_{1})}(z_{2})=u(z_{1}\circ z_{2}).

(2.12)

This translation property is often referred to as “Galilean” change of coordinate and is very useful in kinetic theory. A systematic study of the PDEs theory on this group started in
Lanconelli and
Polidoro 1994
in the limiting case
s=1
. Notice that
({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},\circ)
is a group with the identity and the inverse elements

{\text{Id}=(0,0,0)},\qquad(t,x,v)^{-1}=\left(-t,tv-x,-v\right).

(2.13)

Moreover,
\mathscr{L}_{s}

is homogeneous of degree
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}=2s
with respect to the
dilations
\left(D_{\lambda}\right)_{\lambda>0}
on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
given by

D_{\lambda}=\textrm{diag}\big(\lambda^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}},\lambda^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}I_{d},\lambda I_{d}\big),

(2.14)

where
I_{d}
is the
(d\times d)
identity matrix, i.e.

\big(Yu^{(\lambda)}\big)(z)=\lambda^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}(Yu)(D_{\lambda}z),\qquad z=(t,x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},\ \lambda>0,

(2.15)

where

u^{(\lambda)}(z)=u\big(D_{\lambda}(z)\big).

(2.16)

Note that the exponent
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}
in (
2.14
) equals
ps
in the case of non-local operator
\mathscr{L}_{s,p}
in (
1.8
), hence we don’t impose any restrition to the choice of
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}\in]0,+\infty[
.

In the sequel we will say that
Y
has formal degree
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}
and that the vector fields
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
in (
1.5
) have formal degree
1
, in accordance with the terminology adopted in
(
Bonfiglioli 2009
, Section 2.4)
.

Notice that the triplet
\left(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\circ,D_{\lambda}\right)
forms a homogeneous group. Indeed, it is
well defined the so-called homogeneous norm:

\left\|{(t,x,v)}\right\|=|t|^{\frac{1}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}}+|x|^{\frac{1}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}}+|v|,

(2.17)

and we consider the quasi-distance

d(z_{1},z_{2}):=\left\|{z_{2}^{-1}\circ z_{1}}\right\|,\qquad z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

(2.18)

The following properties directly follow from the definition of the quasi-distance

d(D_{\lambda}(z_{1}),D_{\lambda}(z_{2}))=\lambda\,d(z_{1},z_{2}),\qquad d(z\circ z_{1},z\circ z_{2})=d(z_{1},z_{2})

(2.19)

for every
z,z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and for every
\lambda>0
. Note that
d
is said
quasi-distance
as the following weaker form of triangular inequality holds for it: there exists a constant
\kappa\geq 1
such that

d(z_{1},z_{3})\leq\kappa\left(d(z_{1},z_{2})+d(z_{2},z_{3})\right),\qquad z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

(2.20)

We recall that in
Imbert and
Silvestre 2021
it is considered the
equivalent
distance

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big\{\max\big(|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\frac{1}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}},|x_{1}-x_{2}-w(t_{1}-t_{2})|^{\frac{1}{1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}},|v_{1}-w|,|v_{2}-w|\big)\big\},\qquad z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

(2.21)

We next introduce the notions of intrinsic regularity and intrinsic Hölder space. Let
X
be a
Lipschitz vector field on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. For any
z\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we denote by
{\tau}\mapsto e^{{\tau}X}(z)
the
integral curve of
X
defined as the unique solution to

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{d{\tau}}e^{{\tau}X}(z)=X\left(e^{{\tau}X}(z)\right),\qquad&{\tau}\in{\mathbb{R}},\\
e^{{\tau}X}(z)|_{{\tau}=0}=z.\end{cases}

(2.22)

In particular, for a vector
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we set
Z_{h}:=h_{1}Z_{1}+\dots+h_{d}Z_{d}
and we find
we have

e^{{\tau}Z_{h}}(t,x,v)=(t,x,v+{\tau}h),\qquad e^{{\tau}Y}(t,x,v)=(t+{\tau},x+{\tau}v,v),\qquad\tau\in{\mathbb{R}},

(2.23)

for any
(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.

Next we recall the general notion of Lie differentiability and Hölder regularity.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a Lipschitz vector field and
u
be a real-valued function defined in a neighborhood of

z\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. We say that
u
is
X
-differentiable
in
z
if the function
{\tau}\mapsto u\left(e^{{\tau}X}(z)\right)
is differentiable in
0
. We will refer to the function
z\mapsto Xu(z):=\frac{d}{d{\tau}}u\left(e^{{\tau}X}(z)\right)\big|_{{\tau}=0}
as
X
-Lie derivative of
u
, or
simply
Lie derivative of
u
when the dependence on the field
X
is clear from the
context.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
u:{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, for
a\in]0,1]
, we say that
u\in C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,d
, if

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\atop{\tau}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left|u\left(e^{{\tau}Z_{i}}(z)\right)-u(z)\right|}{|{\tau}|^{{\alpha}}}<\infty.

(2.24)

Moreover, for
\alpha\in]0,{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
, we say that
u\in C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}
if

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\atop{\tau}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left|u\left(e^{{\tau}Y}(z)\right)-u(z)\right|}{|{\tau}|^{\frac{{\alpha}}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}}}<\infty.

(2.25)

Let us introduce the set

\mathcal{I}=\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\mid\alpha=k+j{\mathbf{\vartheta}}\,\text{ with }k,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}.

(2.26)

As we shall see in the sequel,
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
represents the regularity indices for which there is a
jump
in the regularity of a function
u
, meaning that new derivatives along
Z_{i}
or/and
Y
appear.
In particular, the fllowing statements are true.

-

If
\alpha\in]0,1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
there are no derivatives with respect to any of the vector fields
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
or
Y
.

-

If
\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
, there are two cases: if
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}<1
, then only derivatives along
Y
appear, up to order
j
with
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}j<\alpha
; if
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}>1
, then only derivatives along the vector fields
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
appear, up to order
k
with
k<\alpha
. Of course, if
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}=1
, the interval is empty and all the fields have the same formal degree (the gradation is the same as in the Heisenberg group).

-

If
{\alpha}>1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}
, then there exist derivatives along
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
and
Y
.

-

In Theorem
2.7
we show that, if
{\alpha}>1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}}
, then the derivatives
\partial_{x_{j}}
also appear for
i=1,\dots,d
.

Now we define the intrinsic Hölder spaces on the homogeneous group
\left(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\circ,D_{\lambda}\right)
, by extending the definitions of Hölder spaces given in
Pagliarani
et al. 2016
.
Namely, our procedure is recursive with respect to the (ordered) indices in
\mathcal{I}
. In particular, in the second step, which is for
\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
, the definition splits in two different cases depending on whether
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}<1
or
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}>1
. If
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}=1
,
\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
is empty and one moves on to the third step.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
u:{{\mathbb{R}}\times}{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\alpha}>0
. Then:

i)

if
\alpha\in]0,1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
,
u\in C^{{\alpha}}
if the semi-norm

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}}:=\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}}

(2.27)

is finite;

ii)

if
\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
,

u\in C^{{\alpha}}
if the semi-norm

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}}:=\begin{cases}\left\|{Yu}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}},\qquad\text{if }{\mathbf{\vartheta}}<1\\
\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{Z_{i}u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}},\qquad\text{if }{\mathbf{\vartheta}}>1\end{cases}

(2.28)

is finite. If
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}=1
,
\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
is empty and this case can be skipped;

iii)

if
\alpha>1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}
,
u\in C^{{\alpha}}
if the semi-norm

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}}:=\left\|{Yu}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{Z_{i}u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}}

(2.29)

is finite.

I_{8}={\delta}\,[Z_{i},Y]u(z)+\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{k>0,j>0\atop{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1<m^{\prime}({\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1)+(j^{\prime}+j){\mathbf{\vartheta}}+k^{\prime}+k<\alpha}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\delta}^{\frac{m^{\prime}({\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1)+(j^{\prime}+j){\mathbf{\vartheta}}+k^{\prime}+k}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}}\big(\bar{c}_{m^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}jk}\partial_{x_{i}}^{m^{\prime}}Y^{j^{\prime}}Z_{i}^{k^{\prime}+k}Y^{j}u+\widetilde{c}_{m^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}jk}\partial_{x_{i}}^{m^{\prime}}Y^{j^{\prime}}Z_{i}^{k^{\prime}}Y^{j}Z_{i}^{k}u\big)(z),

(4.72)

where every constant
\bar{c}_{m^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}jk}
and
\widetilde{c}_{m^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}jk}
is obtained by collecting the coefficients of the Taylor polynomials of the
v_{jk}
functions. As a consequence of the fact that
\frac{m^{\prime}({\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1)+(j^{\prime}+j){\mathbf{\vartheta}}+k^{\prime}+k}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}>1
for every term of the above sum, we finally obtain

\frac{I_{8}}{{\delta}}\to[Z_{i},Y]u(z)\qquad\text{as}\quad{\delta}\to 0.

(4.73)

Moreover, since
1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}}<\alpha\leq 2(1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}})
, (
4.65
), (
4.66
) and (
4.71
) yield

\frac{I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}}{{\delta}}\to 0,\qquad\frac{I_{5.1}+I_{6.1}}{{\delta}}\to 0,\qquad\frac{I_{7}}{{\delta}}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\quad{\delta}\to 0.

(4.74)

The proof of (
4.61
) follows from (
4.73
) and (
4.74
).

5
Extensions: non-homogeneous and local case

In some applications, the operator
Y
in (
1.1
) appears in a more general form than (
1.2
), namely

Y=\big\langle B(x,v)^{\top},\nabla_{(x,v)}\big\rangle+\partial_{t},\qquad(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},

(5.75)

with
B
being a
(2d\times 2d)
-matrix with real entries that admits the following block decomposition:

B=\left(\begin{matrix}B_{11}&B_{12}\\
B_{21}&B_{22}\end{matrix}\right),

(5.76)

where each block is a
(d\times d)
-matrix and
B_{12}
has rank
d
. For instance, in the kinetic model originally introduced in (
Langevin 1908
), the term
B_{12}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix, while
B_{22}
is non-null and depends on the viscosity of the liquid. In mathematical finance,
B_{22}
may represent an interest rate in the pricing of path-dependent derivatives.

Note that the Hörmander condition (
1.6
) is still satisfied: in particular, (
1.7
) becomes

B_{12}\nabla_{x}=[\nabla_{v},Y]-B_{22}\nabla_{v}.

(5.77)

The integral curve of the vector field
Y
now reads as

e^{{\tau}Y}(t,x,v)=\big(t+{\tau},e^{{\tau}B}(x,v)^{\top}\big),\qquad(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\quad{\tau}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(5.78)

Accordingly, the relevant non-commutative group law “
\circ
” in (
5.79
) is replaced by

z_{1}\circ z_{2}=\left(t_{1}+t_{2},(x_{2},v_{2})^{\top}+e^{t_{2}B}(x_{1},v_{1})^{\top}\right),\qquad z_{1}=(t_{1},x_{1},v_{1}),z_{2}=(t_{2},x_{2},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

(5.79)

Notice that
({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},\circ)
remains a group, with the identity and the inverse elements that now read as

{\text{Id}=(0,0,0)},\qquad(t,x,v)^{-1}=\big(-t,-e^{-tB}(x,v)^{\top}\big).

(5.80)

In particular, we have

(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})^{-1}\circ(t,x,v)=\big(t-t_{0},(x,v)^{\top}-e^{(t-t_{0})B}(x_{0},v_{0})^{\top}\big).

(5.81)

Despite the fact that
\mathscr{L}_{s}
is no longer homogeneous with respect to the dilations
\left(D_{\lambda}\right)_{\lambda>0}
in (
2.14
), the homogeneous norm (
2.17
) remains well behaved with respect to the stratification induced by the Hörmander condition, in particular by (
5.77
).

A local version of Theorem
2.7
can be proved in this more general setting, with the Hölder spaces
C^{{\alpha}}
begin exactly as in Definition
2.3
. However, it is more natural to localize the definition of Hölder spaces as well. We follow the approach in
Pagliarani
et al. 2016
,
Pagliarani and
Pignotti 2017
for the case
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}=2
. Let
{\Omega}
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. For any
z\in{\Omega}
we set

{\delta}_{z}:=\sup\left\{\bar{{\delta}}\in\,]0,1]\mid e^{{\delta}Z_{1}}(z),\cdots,e^{{\delta}Z_{d}}(z),e^{{\delta}Y}(z)\in{\Omega}\text{ for any }{\delta}\in[-\bar{{\delta}},\bar{{\delta}}]\right\}.

If
{\Omega}_{0}
is a bounded domain with
\overline{{\Omega}}_{0}\subseteq{\Omega}
, we set

{\delta}_{{\Omega}_{0}}:=\min_{z\in\overline{{\Omega}}_{0}}{\delta}_{z}.

Note that
{\delta}_{{\Omega}_{0}}\in\,]0,1]
. Now we first localize Definition
2.2
of Hölder regularity along the Hörmander fields.

Definition 5.13
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, for
a\in]0,1]
, we say that
u\in C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}({\Omega})
,
i=1,\dots,d
if, for any
bounded domain
{\Omega}_{0}
with
\overline{{\Omega}}_{0}\subseteq{\Omega}
, we have

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}({\Omega}_{0})}:=\sup_{z\in{\Omega}_{0}\atop 0<|{\tau}|<{\delta}_{{\Omega}_{0}}}\frac{\left|u\left(e^{{\tau}Z_{i}}(z)\right)-u(z)\right|}{|{\tau}|^{{\alpha}}}<\infty.

(5.82)

Moreover, for
\alpha\in]0,{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]
, we say that
u\in C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}(\Omega)
if, for any
bounded domain
{\Omega}_{0}
with
\overline{{\Omega}}_{0}\subseteq{\Omega}
, we have

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}({\Omega}_{0})}:=\sup_{z\in{\Omega}_{0}\atop 0<|{\tau}|<{\delta}_{{\Omega}_{0}}}\frac{\left|u\left(e^{{\tau}Y}(z)\right)-u(z)\right|}{|{\tau}|^{\frac{{\alpha}}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}}}<\infty.

(5.83)

With Definition
5.13
at hand, we can now localize the notion of intrinsic Hölder spaces
C^{{\alpha}}
with the following definition, which is completely analogous to Definition
2.3
and thus is written in a more compact form for sake of brevity.

Definition 5.14
.

Let
u:{\Omega}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\alpha}>0
. Then
u\in C^{{\alpha}}({\Omega})
if, for any
bounded domain
{\Omega}_{0}
with
\overline{{\Omega}}_{0}\subseteq{\Omega}
, the semi-norm defined recursively as

\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}({\Omega}_{0})}:=\begin{cases}\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}({\Omega}_{0})}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}({\Omega}_{0})}&\quad\text{if }\alpha\in]0,1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}}],\\
\left\|{Yu}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}({\Omega}_{0})}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Z_{i}}({\Omega}_{0})}&\quad\text{if }\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]\text{ and }{\mathbf{\vartheta}}<1,\\
\left\|{u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{Y}({\Omega}_{0})}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{Z_{i}u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}({\Omega}_{0})}&\quad\text{if }\alpha\in]1\wedge{\mathbf{\vartheta}},1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}]\text{ and }{\mathbf{\vartheta}}>1,\\
\left\|{Yu}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}({\Omega}_{0})}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|{Z_{i}u}\right\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}({\Omega}_{0})}&\quad\text{if }\alpha>1\vee{\mathbf{\vartheta}}\end{cases}

(5.84)

is finite.

Remark 5.15
.

It is easy to see that
C^{{\alpha}}({\Omega})
is a decreasing family in
{\alpha}
, meaning that
C^{{\alpha}}({\Omega})\subset C^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}({\Omega})
for any
{\alpha}>{\alpha}^{\prime}>0
.

We now have the following local version of Theorem
2.7
. Before, for
u\in C^{{\alpha}}({\Omega})
, we define its intrinsic Taylor polynomial centered at
z_{0}=(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})
as

T_{\alpha}u(z_{0};z):=\!\!\sum_{{0\leq{\mathbf{\vartheta}}k+(1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}})|\gamma|+|\beta|<\alpha}}\!\!\frac{Y^{k}{\partial_{v}^{\beta}\partial_{x}^{\gamma}}}{k!\,\gamma!\,\beta!}\big((t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})^{-1}\circ(t,x,v)\big)^{(k,\gamma,\beta)},\qquad z=(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},

(5.85)

with
\circ
as in (
5.81
), and where we adopted the multi-product notation

(s,h,y)^{(k,\gamma,\beta)}=s^{k}h_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots h_{d}^{\gamma_{d}}y_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots y_{d}^{\beta_{d}},\qquad(s,h,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

(5.86)

Note that the term
polynomial
here is slightly abused, as
T_{\alpha}u(z_{0};z)
is not necessarily a polynomial in the time-increment.

Theorem 5.16
.

For any
\alpha>0
and for any
u\in C^{{\alpha}}({\Omega})
the following statements are true:

Hölder spaces characterization.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
\gamma,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
with
{0\leq{\mathbf{\vartheta}}k+(1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}})|\gamma|+|\beta|<\alpha}
, the derivatives

\ Y^{k}\partial_{v}^{\beta}\partial_{x}^{\gamma}u
exist on
{\Omega}
and

\|Y^{k}\partial_{v}^{\beta}\partial_{x}^{\gamma}u\|_{C^{\alpha-{\mathbf{\vartheta}}k-(1+{\mathbf{\vartheta}})|\gamma|-|\beta|}({\Omega}_{0})}\leq\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}({\Omega}_{0})}

(5.87)

for any
bounded domain
{\Omega}_{0}
with
\overline{{\Omega}}_{0}\subseteq{\Omega}
.

Taylor formula.

For any
{\zeta}_{0}\in\Omega
, there exist two bounded domains
U,\Omega_{0}
such that
\zeta_{0}\in\overline{U}\subset\overline{\Omega_{0}}\subset{\Omega}
, and a positive constant
c
, only dependent on
B
,
\alpha
and
U
, such that

\left|u(z)-T_{\alpha}u(z_{0};z)\right|\leq c\|u\|_{C^{{\alpha}}(\Omega_{0})}\|z_{0}^{-1}\circ z\|^{{\alpha}},\qquad z,z_{0}\in U.

(5.88)

In the homogeneous case, namely when the blocks
B_{11}
,
B_{21}
,
B_{22}
are null, the proof of Theorem
5.16
is substantially identical to that of Theorem
2.7
with the only additional complexity being that
U
and
\Omega_{0}
have to be chosen in a way that all the integral curves employed to connect
z_{0}
and
z
are contained in
\Omega
. On the other hand, in the general non-homogeneous case, there is a substantial additional difficulty stemming from the fact that the discretization of (
5.77
) with the integral curves of
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
and
Y
is more involved than in the homogeneous case. In the rest of the section we give an account of this additional complexity and recall a result from
Pagliarani and
Pignotti 2017
that allows to overcome it.

Let
z=(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and recall the points
z_{2}
,
z_{3}
,
z_{4}
as defined in (
3.38
), namely

z_{2}=e^{\tau Z_{w}}(t,x,v),\qquad z_{3}=e^{\tau^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}Y}(z_{2}),\qquad z_{4}=e^{-\tau Z_{w}}(z_{3}),

(5.89)

and set

z_{5}=e^{-\tau^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}}Y}(z_{4}),\qquad z_{6}=e^{-\tau^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}Z_{B_{22}w}}(z_{5}).

(5.90)

A direct computation shows that

z_{6}=\big(t,x+\tau^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}B_{12}w,v\big)-{\tau}^{2{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}\bigg(0,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}{\tau}^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}n}}{(n+2)!}B^{n+2}(0,w)^{\top}\bigg),\qquad{\tau}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(5.91)

Assume that our aim is to move along the
x
variable only, by an increment
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If we adjust the leading order increment in (
5.91
) by choosing
w=B_{1,2}^{-1}h/|h|
and
{\tau}=|h|^{\frac{1}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}}
(recall that
B_{1,2}
has maximum rank),
then we are off by an error term of order
{\tau}^{2{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}
. Note that this error involves both the velocity and position variables as the blocks of
B
are all non-null. Therefore, if we make a further correction in the velocity variables by considering

\displaystyle g_{w,{\tau}}(z):=e^{\tau^{2{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}Z_{w^{\prime}}}(z_{6}),\qquad\text{with }\quad w^{\prime}=w^{\prime}({\tau},w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}{\tau}^{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}n}}{(n+2)!}B^{n+2}_{22}w,

(5.92)

we fix the velocity variables but the increment in the position variables still differs from
h
by an error term of order
{\delta}^{2{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}
. The next lemma allows to connect
z=(t,x,v)
to
(t,x+h,v)
moving along the curves above, while keeping
w
bounded and
|{\tau}|
controlled in terms of
|h|^{\frac{1}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}}
.
It concides with
(
Pagliarani and
Pignotti 2017
, Lemma 3.2)
, which has been proved for
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}=2
. We recall this result without proof, as dealing with a general
{\mathbf{\vartheta}}>0
requires no modification.

Lemma 5.17
.

There exists
{\varepsilon}>0
, only dependent on
B
, such that: for any
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|h|\leq{\varepsilon}
, there exist
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\tau\geq 0
with

|w|=1,\qquad|{\tau}|\leq\frac{2}{\left\|{B_{12}}\right\|}|h|^{\frac{1}{{\mathbf{\vartheta}}+1}},

(5.93)

such that

g_{w,{\tau}}(z)=(t,x+h,v),\qquad z=(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},

(5.94)

where
g_{w,{\tau}}(z)
is as in (
5.92
).

With Lemma
5.17
at hand, which plays the role of identity (
3.39
) that we had in the case
Y=\langle v,\nabla_{x}\rangle+\partial_{t}
, the proof of Theorem
5.16
is essentially the same as in the homogeneous case.
