Demonstration of Metaplectic Geometrical Optics for Reduced Modeling of Plasma Waves Preprint: APS/123-QED
Rune Højlund Marholt, Mads Givskov Senstius, Stefan Kragh Nielsen

Abstract
(Published in Physical Review E on 27 August 2024, Ref. Højlund Marholt et al. 2024 . This is the article version before passing final review and without the edits from Physical Review E.) The WKB approximation of geometrical optics is widely used in plasma physics, quantum mechanics and reduced wave modeling in general. However, it is well-known that the approximation breaks down at focal and turning points. In this work we present the first unsupervised numerical implementation of the recently developed metaplectic geometrical optics framework, which extends the applicability of geometrical optics beyond the limitations of WKB, such that the wave field remains finite at caustics. The implementation is in 1D and uses a combination of Gauss-Freud quadrature and barycentric rational function inter- and extrapolation to perform an inverse metaplectic transform numerically. The capabilities of the numerical implementation are demonstrated on Airy’s and Weber’s equation, which both have exact solutions to compare with. Finally, the implementation is applied to the plasma physics problem of linear conversion of X-mode to electron Bernstein waves at the upper hybrid layer and a comparison is made with results from fully kinetic particle-in-cell simulations. In all three applications we find good agreement between the exact results and the new reduced wave modeling paradigm of metaplectic geometrical optics.



I
Introduction

Ray tracing methods based on geometrical optics are widely used for reduced wave modeling in general and in fusion plasmas in particular (Refs.
Tracy et al. 2012
;
Batchleor et al. 1980
). Within plasma physics examples of applications include various beam based diagnostics (Refs.
Donné 1995
;
Nielsen et al. 2008
), electron cyclotron resonance heating and current drive (Ref.
Prater et al. 2008
), as well as suppression of instabilities (Ref.
Nagasaki et al. 2005
). Unfortunately, the underlying eikonal (WKB) approximation behind geometrical optics theory typically breaks down at reflection points and focal points. Such critical points are in general called caustics. Caustics are common in plasma physics and they often occur in critical regions for mode coupling, which makes them essential for various diagnostics and advanced mode coupling schemes, Refs.
Preinhaelter and Kopecký 1973
;
Ram and Schultz 2000
. Waves typically experience a natural amplification or swelling near caustics which means that several nonlinear effects can become particularly important
White and Chen 1974
;
Karney and Bers 1977
. Calculating the impact of these nonlinear wave effects relies crucially on the amplitude of the wave field at the caustics, Ref.
Porkolab and Chang 1978
. However, the breakdown of the eikonal approximation means that the wave amplitude obtained from geometrical optics erroneously diverges. For this reason, many wave phenomena are challenging to model properly using reduced models based on geometrical optics and one must instead resort to computationally expensive full-wave codes
Aleynikov and Marushchenko 2019
;
Köhn-Seemann et al. 2023
.

In the recent works of Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2019
;
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Lopez and Dodin 2021
;
Donnelly et al. 2021
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
;
Lopez and Dodin 2022
, Lopez et al. proposed a new method known as metaplectic geometrical optics for reinstating the validity of geometrical optics in caustic regions. metaplectic geometrical optics takes a geometrical solution strategy by recognizing that the singularities in geometrical optics arise whenever the projection of a ray trajectory from phase space to position space is singular. This is not unlike the widely used Maslov method (Refs.
Maslov and Fedoriuk 1981
;
Keller 1985
) which works by evolving the wave field in Fourier space which effectively corresponds to representing the ray trajectory in a rotated phase space, thereby eliminating some projection singularities. The metaplectic geometrical optics method takes the Maslov method a step further by continuously applying a metaplectic transform, which corresponds to a continuous rotation of phase space in a manner such that the projection of the ray trajectory to the rotated position space is always well-defined locally. In Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2019
;
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Lopez and Dodin 2021
;
Donnelly et al. 2021
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
;
Lopez and Dodin 2022
, Lopez et al. developed and demonstrated the metaplectic geometrical optics method analytically on a few well-chosen key examples, but the method remained to be implemented in a fully automated numerical code. In this work we have developed a first of its kind automated 1D implementation of the metaplectic geometrical optics framework which differs from previous semi-analytical demonstrations of metaplectic geometrical optics by calculating all quantities fully numerically including the inverse metaplectic transform which we shall return to in
section

III
. The code is openly accessible on a repository on GitHub, Ref.
Rune Højlund 2024
. As we shall explain in
section

IV.4
, a particular challenge in evaluating the inverse metaplectic transform is how to analytically continue a numerical field from the real to the complex domain. This sub problem has not been addressed in the previous literature, but we suggest it can be accomplished using barycentric rational interpolation. We demonstrate the results of this newly proposed method in
section

V
.

In the following, we first present the main ideas and analytical foundations of geometrical optics and metaplectic geometrical optics in
sections

II
and
III
. In
section

IV
we then describe the numerical details of our implementation of metaplectic geometrical optics, for which evaluating the inverse metaplectic transform is the main challenge and is achieved with barycentric rational interpolation and Gauss-Freud quadrature. In
sections

V.1
and
V.2
we present results of applying the method to Airy’s equation and Weber’s equation, which are fundamental wave physics problems with known exact analytical solutions as well as analytical metaplectic geometrical optics solutions. Finally, in
section

V.3
we apply the metaplectic geometrical optics code to a caustic occurring in plasma physics when electromagnetic X-mode couples to electron Bernstein waves at the upper hybrid layer to showcase a less idealized application. The metaplectic geometrical optics results are compared to results from fully kinetic particle-in-cell simulations.

II
Geometrical Optics

In this work we consider time-stationary scalar linear wave equations of the integrodifferential form:

\displaystyle\int\differential{\vb{x}}^{\prime}D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\psi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=0,

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}
is the position coordinate,
D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
is the wave operator kernel and
\psi(\mathbf{x})
is some wave field such as a scalar electric field or a quantum mechanical wave function. To the best of our knowledge, the metaplectic geometrical optics theory has not yet been generalized to time-dependent, vector-valued waves, but this is not an intrinsic limitation to the theory. Also note that our first iteration of a numerical implementation is only in 1D, i.e.
\mathbf{x}=x\in\mathbb{R}
. However, we use vector notation in
sections

II
and
III
, since Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2019
;
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Lopez and Dodin 2021
;
Donnelly et al. 2021
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
;
Lopez and Dodin 2022
already have derived the metaplectic geometrical optics theory in multiple dimensions.

The full integrodifferential wave equation in eq.
1
can be simplified by assuming the wave field to be of the eikonal form:

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})=\phi(\mathbf{x})e^{i\theta(\mathbf{x})}

(2)

Here
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is the envelope and
\theta(\mathbf{x})
is the phase. Within the theory of geometrical optics the envelope
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is assumed to vary much more slowly compared to the phase such that high order derivatives of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
can be neglected. This can also be expressed in the eikonal parameter which is assumed small:

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{1}{kL}\ll 1,

(3)

where
k\sim\absolutevalue{\p_{\vb{x}} \theta}
is the characteristic scale of variation of the phase and
1/L\sim\absolutevalue{\p_{\vb{x}} \phi}
is the envelope variation scale. The eikonal approximation of
eq.

3
is also known as the WKB (after Wentzel, Kramers and Brillouin) or the LG approximation (after Liouville and Green) (Ref.
(
Tracy et al. 2012
, p. 22)
) and a medium satisfying the eikonal criterion is said to be weakly inhomogenous (Ref.
Mazzucato 2014
). To first order in
\epsilon
the full wave equation of (
1
) can be simplified to the geometrical optics equations (Refs.
Tracy et al. 2012
;
Dodin et al. 2019
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\quantity[\vb{x}, \vb{k}\qty(\vb{x})]=0.

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\quantity(\vb{x})\cdot\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\quantity(\vb{x})+\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\p_{\vb{x}}\cdot\vb{v}\qty(\vb{x})]\phi\quantity(\vb{x})=0.

(4b)

where
\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})
are respectively the local wave number and group velocity defined by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\theta\quantity(\vb{x}),\quad\mathbf{v}\quantity(\vb{x})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}-\partial_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{D}\quantity(\vb{x}, \vb{k})\biggr\rvert_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}\quantity(\vb{x})}.

(5)

Here
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})
is the dispersion symbol which is a function of phase space coordinates
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})^{T}
and is assumed to be real implying that we neglect dissipation. To be clear:
\mathbf{k}
is generally any wave vector, whereas the
local wave vector

\mathbf{k}\quantity(\vb{x})
is a specific function of
\mathbf{x}
for which
eq.

4a
is satisfied. There are multiple paths for deriving the geometrical optics equations, eq. (
4
). A modern approach found in e.g. Refs.
Dodin et al. 2019
;
Tracy et al. 2012
is to use Weyl symbol calculus which makes it possible to approximate the wave equation’s differential operator by Taylor expanding its Weyl symbol in the eikonal parameter, eq. (
3
). In this approach
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})
is found as the Wigner-Weyl transform of the wave operator (Ref.
Dodin et al. 2019
;
Zhou and Dodin 2019
). Please refer to Ref.
Dodin et al. 2019
for a full derivation.

II.1
Ray Tracing

The geometrical optics equations can be solved by finding phase space trajectories,
\mathbf{z}(\bm{\tau})=(\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau}),\mathbf{k}(\bm{\tau}))^{T}
satisfying the local dispersion relation, eq. (
4a
). Such trajectories are called rays. Here
{\bf\it\tau}=\quantity(\tau_1, \vb*{\tau_{\perp}})^{T}
where
\tau_{1}
is a longitudinal time parameter and
{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp}=\quantity(\tau_2, \tau_3)^{T}=\quantity(x_2^{(0)}, x_3^{(0)})^{T}
are the perpendicular initial coordinates of the ray. Given an initial condition
\mathbf{z}(0,\bm{\tau}_{\perp})=(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{k}_{0})^{T}
, a ray can be found from Hamilton’s ray equations (Ref.
Tracy et al. 2012
):

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\mathbf{x}(\tau_{1})=-\partial_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})

(6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\mathbf{k}(\tau_{1})=\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})

(6b)

The dispersion symbol plays the role of the Hamiltonian. In a numerical setting in multiple dimensions, we can launch a finite family of rays on a discrete
{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp}
-grid all with the same initial
x_{1}
-position. Thereby, we span out a region of phase space
\mathbf{z}(\bm{\tau})
parameterized by
\bm{\tau}\in U,U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N
is the number of spatial dimensions.

The mapping
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau})\mapsto\mathbf{z}(\bm{\tau})=\quantity(\vb{x}(\boldsymbol{\tau}), \vb{k}\qty[\vb{x}(\boldsymbol{\tau})])^{T}
from
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau})
to the graph of the local wave vector is called a lift. Conversely, the inverse map from
\mathbf{z}(\bm{\tau})
to
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau})
is a projection. The set of points
\quantity{\vb{z}(\vb*{\tau}) \, | \, \boldsymbol{\tau} \in U}
is an
N
-dimensional Lagrange manifold which we call the ray manifold.

II.2
Field Construction

Having found a ray
\mathbf{z}({\bf\it\tau})
satisfying
eq.

4a
, the corresponding phase and amplitude of the eikonal field can be found by solving
eqs.

4b
and
5
with the explicit solutions:

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{\tau})=\phi(0,\bm{\tau}_{\perp})\sqrt{\frac{j(0,\bm{\tau}_{\perp})}{j(\bm{\tau})}},

(7a)

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{where}\quad j(\bm{\tau})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\mathrm{det}[\partial_{\bm{\tau}}\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau})].

(7b)

\displaystyle\theta\quantity(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\theta(0,\bm{\tau}_{\perp})+\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\differential{\tau_1}\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{T}(\bm{\tau})\mathbf{k}\quantity(\boldsymbol{\tau})

(8)

Here, det[
\cdot
] is the determinant of
\cdot
and the dot is differentiation with respect to the first coordinate
\tau_{1}
. These are the essential equations of the geometrical optics method. First, trace a set of ray phase space trajectories using eq. (
6
) to obtain the ray manifold
\quantity{\qty(\vb{x}(\boldsymbol{\tau}), \vb{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))^T}
. Then, for each ray, determine
\phi\quantity(\boldsymbol{\tau})
from eq. (
7
) and
\theta\quantity(\boldsymbol{\tau})
from eq. (
8
). If we assume that
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau})
has a well-defined inverse in
{\bf\it\tau}(\mathbf{x})
, the final field as a function of position simply is:

\displaystyle\psi\quantity(\vb{x})=\psi\quantity[\boldsymbol{\tau}\qty(\vb{x})]=\phi\quantity[\boldsymbol{\tau}\qty(\vb{x})]e^{i\theta\quantity[\boldsymbol{\tau}\qty(\vb{x})]},

(9)

II.3
The Caustic Problem

For
eq.

9
above to be meaningful, the map
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\tau})
needs to be bijective such that it is invertible. This is not satisfied near turning and focal points which in both cases cause rays to cross. This caustic problem is also reflected in
eq.

7
which diverges for
j\quantity(\boldsymbol{\tau})\to 0
. In 1D the caustic breakdown occurs if the slope
\partial_{x}k(x)
goes to infinity and therefore the local wave number function
k(x)
does not have an explicit representation at the caustic point. In general we shall speak of the points where
j(\bm{\tau})=0
as projection singularities. Within the geometrical optics approximation the wave field diverges exactly where the projection of the ray manifold becomes singular (Ref.
(
Tracy et al. 2012
, p. 147)
).

III
Metaplectic Geometrical Optics

metaplectic geometrical optics proposes to solve the caustic problem by continuously rotating the phase space coordinates along the ray, such that the ray manifold always has an explicit representation in the new rotated phase space coordinates. The coordinate rotations are accomplished with symplectic transforms while the corresponding transformations of the eikonal fields are accomplished with metaplectic transforms. For convenience and readability, we state in the following a few essential results on symplectic and metaplectic transforms needed to present metaplectic geometrical optics. The reader is encouraged to consult Refs.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
;
Goldstein et al. 2002
;
Tracy et al. 2012
for a more elaborate discussion on these topics.

III.1
Symplectic Transforms

Consider linear transformations of phase space coordinates of the general form:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{K}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\mathsfbfit{S}\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{k}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathsfbfit{S}
is a
2N\times 2N
matrix and
\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}=\quantity(\vb{X},
\vb{K})^{T}
are the new phase space coordinates. Within metaplectic geometrical optics we impose two constraints on the transformation matrix
\mathsfbfit{S}
. First, the linear transformations must be canonical, i.e. they must preserve Hamilton’s equations. It can be shown (see either of Refs.
Goldstein et al. 2002
;
Tracy et al. 2012
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
), that this is satisfied if and only if
\mathsfbfit{S}
is symplectic such that it satisfies the equation:

\displaystyle\mathsfbfit{S}\mathsfbfit{J}_{2N}\mathsfbfit{S}^{T}=\mathsfbfit{J}_{2N},\hskip 11.49994pt\mbox{where}\hskip 11.49994pt\mathsfbfit{J}_{2N}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
\mat{0}_N & \mat{I}_N \\
- \mat{I}_N & \mat{0}_N\endlx@physics@matrix).

(18)

Here
\mathsfbfit{J}_{2N}
is known as the symplectic matrix and it is composed as a block matrix of the
N\times N
zero matrix,
\mathsfbfit{0}_{N}
, and the
N\times N
identity matrix,
\mathsfbfit{I}_{N}
.
Furthermore, we constrain ourselves to only consider rotations, why
\mathsfbfit{S}
must be orthonormal:

\displaystyle\mathsfbfit{S}^{T}=\mathsfbfit{S}^{-1}.

(19)

The orthonormality and symplecticity requirements restricts
\mathsfbfit{S}
to be of the block form:

\displaystyle\mathsfbfit{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsfbfit{A}&\mathsfbfit{B}\\
-\mathsfbfit{B}&\mathsfbfit{A}\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

where
\mathsfbfit{A},\mathsfbfit{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.

Generator Formalism

As an alternative to

III.3
Metaplectic Transforms

For each orthosymplectic transformation
\mathbf{z}\mapsto\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}
of phase space coordinates there exists a corresponding metaplectic transformation which defines how the field transforms from the old coordinate representation
\psi(\mathbf{x})
to the new coordinate representation
\Psi(\mathbf{X})
. Assuming
\mathsfbfit{B}
to be invertible it is possible to show, that the representation of the metaplectic transform of
\psi(\mathbf{x})
in the new
\mathbf{X}
-space is (Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
):

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{X})=\alpha\int\differential{\vb{x}}e^{-iF_{1}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{x})}\psi(\mathbf{x}),

(27)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
F_{1}
is the first generating function from
eq.

21
. The metaplectic transform must be unitary and this determines
\alpha
up to a sign. However, this leaves us with an overall sign ambiguity such that for each symplectic transformation
\mathsfbfit{S}
there are two metaplectic transformations (see Ref.
(
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
, p. 47-48)
and Ref.
(
Tracy et al. 2012
, p. 470)
for a detailed discussion). The final integral form of the metaplectic transform of
\psi(\mathbf{x})
is (Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
):

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{X})\!=\!\frac{\pm e^{\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathsfbfit{A}\mathsfbfit{B}^{-1}\mathbf{X}}}{\scriptstyle\quantity(2 \pi i)^{N/2}\sqrt{\det\mathsfbfit{B}}}\!\int\!\!\differential{\vb{x}}\psi(\mathbf{x})e^{i\quantity(\frac{1}{2} \vb{x} \mat{B}^{-1} \mat{A} \vb{x} - \vb{x}^T \mat{B}^{-1} \vb{X} )}.

Here
\mathsfbfit{S}
is already assumed to be of the orthosymplectic form of
eq.

20
. Under the same assumption, the inverse metaplectic transform, which maps
\Psi(\mathbf{X})
to the old representation, can be shown to be (Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
):

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})\!=\!\frac{\pm e^{-\frac{i}{2}\mathbf{x}\mathsfbfit{B}^{-1}\mathsfbfit{A}\mathbf{x}}}{\scriptstyle\quantity(-2 \pi i)^{N/2}\sqrt{\det\mathsfbfit{B}}}\!\int\!\!\differential{\vb{X}}\Psi(\mathbf{X})e^{i\quantity(-\frac{1}{2} \vb{X}^T \mat{A} \mat{B}^{-1} \vb{X} + \vb{x}^T \mat{B}^{-1} \vb{X} )}

(28)

Note how the metaplectic transform reduces to a Fourier transform in the special case where
\mathsfbfit{A}=\mathsfbfit{0}_{N}
and
\mathsfbfit{B}=\mathsfbfit{I}_{N}
. In particular, a 1D Fourier transform is the metaplectic transform corresponding to a
90^{\circ}
rotation in phase space.

III.4
Geometrical Optics in Rotated Phase Space

Importantly, Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
shows that under a symplectic transformation the geometrical optics equations,
eq.

4
, will carry over to the new position space. To arrive at this result Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
consider an eikonal form of the metaplectically transformed field in the new position space:

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{X})=\Phi(\mathbf{X})\exp[i\Theta({\bf\it X})].

(29)

Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
then impose the eikonal assumption, but this time in the rotated phase space reference, i.e.
\absolutevalue{\p_{\vb{X}} \Phi}\ll\absolutevalue{\p_{\vb{X}} \Theta}
. To lowest order in the eikonal parameter the geometrical optics equations then take the familiar form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\quantity[ \mat{S}^{-1} \Zs(\vb{X}) ]=0

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{X})\cdot\partial_{\mathbf{X}}\Phi(\mathbf{X})+\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\p_{\vb{X}} \cdot\vb{V}(\vb{X})]\Phi(\mathbf{X})=0,

(30i)

where
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{X})
is the group velocity in the new phase space coordinates. Furthermore, Ref.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
shows that the manifold in rotated phase space is simply obtained by transforming the original representation of the manifold:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}\quantity(\vb*{\tau})=\quantity(\begin{array}{cc}
\vb{X}(\vb*{\tau})\\
\vb{K}(\vb*{\tau})
\end{array})=\mathsfbfit{S}\mathbf{z}({\bf\it\tau}).

Therefore, in complete analogy with

,

and

, the new envelope and phase will have the explicit solutions in rotated phase space when away from caustics:

\displaystyle\Phi({\bf\it\tau})=\Phi(0,{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})\sqrt{\frac{J(0,{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})}{J({\bf\it\tau})}},

(41a)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad J({\bf\it\tau})=\det[\partial_{{\bf\it\tau}}\mathbf{X}({\bf\it\tau})].

(41b)

\displaystyle\Theta\quantity(\vb*{\tau})=\Theta(0,{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})+\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\differential{\tau_1}\dot{\mathbf{X}}^{T}({\bf\it\tau})\mathbf{K}\quantity(\vb*{\tau})

III.5
Review of metaplectic geometrical optics

Having presented the essentials on symplectic and metaplectic transforms, we will now present a review of the metaplectic geometrical optics method as it was developed in Refs.
Lopez and Dodin 2019
;
Lopez and Dodin 2020
;
Donnelly et al. 2021
;
Lopez and Dodin 2021
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
;
Lopez and Dodin 2022
. This summary is in particular based on Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
, which the reader is encouraged to consult for a full derivation of the theory. The core idea of metaplectic geometrical optics is presented in FIG.
1
. The method consists of the following five steps:

1.

Trace a set of rays to obtain a rendering of
\mathbf{z}({\bf\it\tau})=\quantity(\vb{x}(\vb*{\tau}), \vb{k}(\vb*{\tau}))^{T}
.

2.

For each point
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
on the ray manifold determine a symplectic transformation
\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}
which rotates the ray manifold representation into the new phase space coordinates:

\mathbf{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}}_{\mathbf{t}}({\bf\it\tau})=\quantity(\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb*{\tau}), \vb{K}_{\vb{t}}(\vb*{\tau}))^{T}=\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{z}({\bf\it\tau}).

The construction of
\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}
is given explicitly in
eqs.

43
,
44
,
45
and
46
and is designed such that the new position coordinate axes are all tangent to the ray manifold at
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
.

3.

For each point
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
on the ray manifold, solve the geometrical optics phase and envelope equations in the new phase space to obtain function values of
\Psi\quantity(\vb{X}_{\vb{t}})
for all
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}({\bf\it\tau})
-points.

4.

Ensure continuity of the solution by using a near identity metaplectic transform to connect the initial conditions of the fields in rotated phase spaces together.

5.

For each point
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
on the ray manifold, inverse metaplectic transform
\Psi\quantity(\vb{X}_{\vb{t}})
to obtain
\psi(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{t}))
. Sum up the field contributions from all branches of the ray manifold.

The five steps are illustrated in FIG.
1
. Note that there is a new transformation for each
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
and this is reflected in the notation. For instance
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})
is a function of
\bm{\tau}
for a given fixed
\mathbf{t}
, while
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
is this function evaluated at
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
. Finally we denote an arbitrary coordinate set in the rotated position space as just
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}
and later on we shall allow
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}
to be complex so that we can analytically continue
\Psi(\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}})
. Also note how the inverse metaplectic transform maps the function
\Psi\quantity(\vb{X}_{\vb{t}})
defined on the entire
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}
domain onto a single point. In the following we present a summary of the equations resulting from the five steps above.

Figure 1
:
Conceptual illustration of the 5 steps of MGO. 1) Trace rays, 2) determine symplectic transformation
\mathsfbfit{S}_{t}
, 3) Solve geometrical optics in rotated phase space, 4) link the solutions together using metaplectic transforms such that the final solution is continuous 5) Inverse metaplectic transform the fields and add up the solutions from different branches.

Assume that the incoming wave field is defined on the boundary at position
x_{1}
:

\displaystyle\psi_{\text{in}}(\bm{\tau}_{\perp})=\phi(0,\bm{\tau}_{\perp})e^{i\theta(0,\bm{\tau}_{\perp})}.

(42)

Furthermore, assume that we have an initial
\mathbf{z}_{0}
satisfying the local dispersion relation, i.e.
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_{0})=0
. The first step of the procedure is to evolve the rays using
eq.

6
to obtain
\mathbf{z}(\bm{\tau})
. Next, for all ray parameters
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{t}
, we need to determine a symplectic rotation matrix
\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}
such that the new position space coordinate axes span the tangent space of the ray manifold at
{\bf\it\tau}=\mathbf{t}
. This is accomplished by defining the unit vector

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{T}}_{1}(\mathbf{t})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\partial_{\tau_{1}}\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{t})/\norm{\p_{\tau_1} \vb{z}(\vb{t})},

(43)

and then defining
\check{\mathbf{T}}_{2}(\mathbf{t}),\cdots,\check{\mathbf{T}}_{N}(\mathbf{t})
by Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization of the Jacobian matrix
\quantity[\p_{\vb*{\tau} }\vb{z}(\vb*{\tau})]^{T}
(Ref.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
). From the set of tangent vectors one can also define a symplectically dual set of normal vectors:

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{N}}_{j}(\mathbf{t})=-\mathsfbfit{J}_{2N}\check{\mathbf{T}}_{j}(\mathbf{t}).

(44)

Thereby the symplectic transformation which maps
\mathbf{x}
to the tangent space of the ray manifold is determined by inverting the following matrix (Ref.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
):

\displaystyle\mathsfbfit{S}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\big\uparrow&&\big\uparrow&\big\uparrow&&\big\uparrow\\
\check{\mathbf{T}}_{1}(\mathbf{t})&\cdots&\check{\mathbf{T}}_{N}(\mathbf{t})&\check{\mathbf{N}}_{1}(\mathbf{t})&\cdots&\check{\mathbf{N}}_{N}(\mathbf{t})\\
\big\downarrow&&\big\downarrow&\big\downarrow&&\big\downarrow\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

As a result, the symplectic transformation matrix is now on the orthonormal form of
eq.

20
:

\displaystyle\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{t}}&\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}}\\
-\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}}&\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{t}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(46)

From
\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}
we rotate the manifold representation using
section

III.4
:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})\\
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\mathsfbfit{S}_{\mathbf{t}}\,{\mathsfbfit{z}(\bm{\tau})}.

By construction, this rotation ensures that the new position coordinate axes are now tangent to the rotated manifold and therefore the manifold always has an explicit representation locally. In other words, in a neighborhood of
{\bf\it\tau}=\mathbf{t}
the wave field is free from caustics and in this neighborhood it will be justified to assume the field to be of the eikonal form of
eq.

29
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb*{\tau})]=\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb*{\tau})]\exp(i \Theta_{\vb{t}} \qty[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb*{\tau})] ).

(55)

The envelope and phase field in the rotated frame is calculated using
eq.

41
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})]=\sqrt{\frac{J_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})}{J_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})}},

(56a)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad J_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})=\det[\partial_{\bm{\tau}}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\tau})].

(56b)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})]=\int_{t_{1}}^{\tau_{1}}\differential{\xi}\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}(\xi,{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{t}}(\xi,{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp}).

(57)

Note, how the field in rotated phase space is renormalized such that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb{t})]=1,\quad\Theta_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb{t})]=0

(58)

This renormalization choice is convenient, since it allows us to calculate
\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}
and
\Theta_{\mathbf{t}}
independently for each
\mathbf{t}
. To adjust for this renormalization and ensure continuity of the final wave field, a near identity metaplectic transform prefactor,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{t}}
, will be multiplied to the final wave field.

The contribution from
\Psi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})]
then needs to be brought back to the original frame. Apart from constants which we will absorb into an metaplectic geometrical optics prefactor,
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{\tau}_{i}}
, the inverse metaplectic transform from
eq.

28
is given by:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mathbf{t}}=\int\differential{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\epsilon})\exp[if_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\epsilon})]

(59a)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\epsilon})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\Phi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[
\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb{t}) + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}
],

(59b)

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\epsilon})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\Theta\quantity[
\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}(\vb{t}) + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}
]-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t}),

(59c)

with
{\bf\it\epsilon}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{t})
. In section
IV.3
we give details on how we calculate the integral, which in our current 1D implementation is only an integral over the real line. If however
\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}}
is singular, we must instead use the form:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mathbf{t}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\differential{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\rho}

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathbf{t}}\quantity[
\mat{L}_{S}\left(\begin{array}{c}
\vb{X}_{\vb{t}}^\rho(\vb{t})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\rho\\
\mat{a}_{\varsigma\varsigma}\cdot\vb{x}_{\varsigma}(\vb{t})
\end{array}\right)
]

(60)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{i}{2}\bm{\epsilon}_{\rho}^{T}\mathsfbfit{a}_{\rho\rho}\mathsfbfit{\Lambda}_{\rho\rho}^{-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{\rho}-i\bm{\epsilon}_{\rho}^{T}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{t}}^{\rho}(t)\right].

The quantities
\mathsfbfit{L}_{S},\mathsfbfit{a}_{\varsigma\varsigma},\mathsfbfit{\Lambda}_{\rho\rho}
are defined through a singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}}
as explained in
section

III.2
.

The final solution to the wave field is obtained by summing up the contributions from all branches:

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\psi(\bm{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{x})),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\psi\quantity[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_i(\vb{x}) ]=\mathcal{N}_{\bm{\tau}_{i}}\Upsilon_{\bm{\tau}_{i}},

(61)

where
b
denotes the number of branches and the prefactor
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{t}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{t}}=\frac{A({\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})\exp[i\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\differential{\tau_1}\mathbf{k}^{T}(\tau_{1},{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})\dot{\mathbf{x}}(\tau_{1},{\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})]}{(-2\pi i)^{\rho/2}e^{i\varphi/2}\sqrt{\det\mathsfbfit{\Lambda}_{\rho\rho}\det\mathsfbfit{a}_{\varsigma\varsigma}\det\mathsfbfit{R}_{\mathbf{t}}}},

Here
\varphi(\mathbf{t})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\arg(\mathrm{det}(\mathsfbfit{B}_{\mathbf{t}}))
. In our 1D implementation we require that
\varphi(t)
must be monotonically increasing for increasing
t
.
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{t}}
combines the prefactor from the inverse metaplectic transform and the analytic form of the near identity metaplectic transform into a single analytic expression which may be evaluated independently from the integral (Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
). Note that our definition of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{t}}
is formulated slightly different from Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
. First, we have defined
\varphi(t)
as the argument of
\mathrm{det}(\mathsfbfit{B}_{\mathbf{t}})
. Since
\mathrm{det}(\mathsfbfit{B}_{\mathbf{t}})\in\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi(t)\in\quantity{n\pi\,|\, n \in\ZZ}
. By construction,
\varphi
must be monotonically increasing as a function of
t_{1}
to avoid crossing branch cuts in the square root
\sqrt{\mathrm{sign}\,(\mathrm{det}(\mathsfbfit{B}_{\mathbf{t}}))}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}e^{i\varphi/2}
. This is directly related to the sign ambiguity of the metaplectic transform discussed in
section

III.3
. Second, as opposed to Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
we define the non-zero singular values in
\mathsfbfit{\Lambda}_{\rho\rho}
to always be positive since this is a customary convention for singular value decomposition (Ref.
(
Jorge Nocedal and Stephen J. Wright 2006
, p. 604)
). Finally, we determine the amplitude constant
A({\bf\it\tau}_{\perp})
by matching the final metaplectic geometrical optics field
\psi(\mathbf{x})
to the true field at some
x_{1}
-coordinate, where the true field is assumed to be known in the problem. Note also, that
\mathsfbfit{R}_{\mathbf{t}}
is the upper triangular matrix from a QR decomposition of the Jacobian matrix
\partial_{{\bf\it\tau}}\mathbf{z}({\bf\it\tau})
. This is not to be confused with the
\mathsfbfit{R}_{S}
from the singular value decomposition of
\mathsfbfit{B}_{\mathbf{t}}
.

IV
Numerical Details

From this point onward we now restrict the position to be 1D, i.e.
N=1
. This reflects the current state of the numerical implementation and the three examples on which we apply the code.

IV.1
Obtaining the Ray Manifold

We obtained the ray manifold by integrating Hamilton’s ray equations in
eq.

6
numerically using the initial value problem solver from the SciPy Library (Ref.
The SciPy Community 2024
) which uses a Runge-Kutta scheme. The dispersion symbol,
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})
, depends on the particular physical system but its analytical form is known in examples below. To obtain the right hand side of
eq.

6
we used automatic differentiation with PyTorch, Ref.
Paszke et al. 2019
. The initial value problem solver calculated the solution at discrete points on the ray manifold, and points at equidistant
{\tau}
-values were then interpolated with a quartic polynomial. In addition to integrating the equations forward in
{\tau}
from some initial values, we also integrate backwards by a smaller amount to get some ghost points on the manifold preceding our initial values. This makes the later step when we perform the inverse metaplectic transform numerically more robust.

IV.2
Determining Time Derivatives, the Symplectic Transformation Matrix and the Eikonal Fields

We used a finite central difference scheme with second order precision based on the discrete known points of
\mathbf{z}({\tau})
to calculate the Jacobian
j(\tau)=\partial_{{\tau}}\mathbf{z}({\tau})
and its’ counter parts in the different rotated phase spaces
J_{t}(\tau)=\partial_{{\tau}}\mathbf{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\displaystyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\textstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\textstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathbf{Z}$\hss\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{{--}}$}\crcr}}{}}_{t}}({\tau})
. As an alternative, one could have used the right hand side of
eq.

6
and its’ symplectically transformed analogue. However, since we used a fine
\tau
resolution in all examples presented below, we expect the difference between these methods to be negligible compared to the larger sources of error of the metaplectic geometrical optics method.

From
j(\tau)
we calculated
\mathsfbfit{S}_{{t}}
at all points along the ray using
eqs.

43
,
44
and
45
. For each point
\tau=t
, the eikonal envelope,
\Phi_{t}(\tau)
, was readily calculated using
J_{t}(\tau)
and
eq.

56
where we restricted the calculation to only include points on the current branch in rotated phase space. Note, we define a branch as a connected interval with constant sign of
J_{t}(\tau)
. For the eikonal phase,
\Theta_{t}(\tau)
, in
eq.

57
, we used numerical trapezoidal integration. With a fine
\tau
resolution and well behaved
\dot{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{k}
the numerical error associated with this is expected to be negligible. Alternatively, the phase could have been calculated by integrating the
\tau
derivative of
eq.

57
as an ordinary differential equation coupled to Hamilton’s ray equations.

IV.3
Steepest Descent Method for the Inverse Transform

From the eikonal fields,
\Phi_{t},\Theta_{t}
in rotated phase space we arrive at the inverse metaplectic transform integral of
eq.

59
:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{{t}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{\epsilon}\Phi_{{t}}(\epsilon)e^{if_{{t}}(\epsilon)},

(63)

where
{\epsilon}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}X_{t}-X_{t}(t)
. Due to the oscillatory term,
e^{if_{{t}}(\epsilon)}
, the main contribution to the integral will be from the vicinity of the saddle point where
\partial_{{\epsilon}}f_{{t}}(\epsilon)=0
. Note, that by construction of
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
in
eq.

59c
, the phase factor has a saddle point exactly at
\epsilon=0
. Attempting to evaluate the integral along the real line by simply using the trapezoidal rule will cause erroneous numerical cancellations due to the oscillatory behavior. Instead we have followed Refs.
Donnelly et al. 2021
;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022
which propose using the method of steepest descent and Gauss-Freud quadrature. This section explains the approach.

The steepest descent method utilizes that we may deform the integral to a new contour
\gamma(l)=\epsilon\in\mathbb{C}
in the complex plane. This deformation of the integration path is allowed provided that the contributions to the integral as
|\epsilon|\to\infty
vanishes and provided no singularities of the integrand
\Phi_{{t}}(\epsilon)e^{if_{{t}}(\epsilon)}
are crossed when changing the contour path (Ref.
(
Gil et al. 2007
, p. 158)
). From
eq.

56
we see that
\Phi_{{t}}(\epsilon)
will only have singularities along the real line (at the caustics in rotated phase space). These caustics are not crossed anew by a deformation of the contour. From the definition of
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
in
eq.

59c
we see that
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
is an entire function provided the eikonal phase
\Theta_{{t}}(\epsilon)
is entire. Thus we can assume that a deformation of the integration path is in general possible. The integral is therefore:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{{t}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\differential{\epsilon}\Phi_{{t}}(\epsilon)e^{if_{{t}}(\epsilon)},

(64)

The new integration contour
\mathcal{C}_{0}
will be chosen as the path passing through the saddle point
\epsilon=0
which has the steepest descent of
\absolutevalue{e^{i f_{{t}}(\epsilon)}}
when moving away from the saddle point. This ensures that the integral quickly converges. This is the same as finding the steepest descent of
-\Im{f_{{t}}(\eg)}
. Note, that
\Phi_{{t}}(\epsilon)
is assumed to vary much more slowly than
e^{if_{\mathbf{t}}(\epsilon)}
, so we only consider the behavior of
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
to be relevant in choosing the optimal integration contour.

IV.3.1
Steepest Descent Directions without Degeneracy

A simple analysis reveals the directions of steepest descent for a non-degenerate saddle point where
f_{t}^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
. By construction of
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
in
eq.

59c
we have:

\displaystyle f_{{t}}(0)=0,\quad f_{{t}}^{\prime}(0)=0

(65)

If
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
we may therefore approximate
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
in the vicinity of
\epsilon=0
as:

\displaystyle if_{{t}}(\epsilon)\approx i\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{{t}}(0)\epsilon^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{f''_{{t}}(0)}\absolutevalue{\eg}^{2}e^{i\quantity(\pi/2 + \alpha+ 2\sigma)},

(66a)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\alpha\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\arg f^{\prime\prime}_{{t}}(0),\quad\sigma\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\arg\epsilon.

(66b)

The exponential has
\cos(\pi/2 + \alpha+ 2\sigma)
as the real part. Therefore
\absolutevalue{e^{i f_{{t}}(\epsilon)}}
is minimized when the cosine is
-1
, i.e. in the two directions where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\pm}=-\frac{\arg f^{\prime\prime}_{{t}}(0)}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}

(67)

Since the cosine is
-1
in these directions and since
f_{{t}}(0)=0
, the real part of
f_{{t}}(\epsilon)
will be
0
in these directions meaning that
e^{if_{{t}}(\epsilon)}
will be free from oscillations if evaluated along the steepest descent direction. A similar analysis for the degenerate case where
f^{\prime\prime}_{{t}}(0)=0,f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{{t}}(0)\neq 0
gives 3 steepest descent directions and so forth for higher degeneracy orders.

IV.3.2
Gauss-Freud Quadrature

Now, assume we have found a parameterization
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
of the contour such that the contour integral is:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{{t}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{l}\gamma^{\prime}(l)\Phi_{{t}}\quantity[\gamma(l)]e^{if_{{t}}\quantity[\gamma(l)]}

(68)

Further, assume that the saddle point is reached at
l=0
, i.e.
\gamma(0)=0
. If the saddle point is degenerate,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
may have a kink at the saddle point and it is therefore convenient to split
\gamma
into two functions:

\displaystyle\gamma(l)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\begin{cases}\gamma_{-}(l)\quad l\leq 0\\
\gamma_{+}(l)\quad l>0\end{cases}.

(69)

The contour integral can then be written as a single integral from
0
to
\infty
:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{{t}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{l}\bigg(

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}_{-}(-l)\Phi_{{t}}\quantity[\gamma_{-}(-l)]e^{if_{{t}}\quantity[\gamma_{-}(-l)]}

(70)

\displaystyle+\gamma^{\prime}_{+}(l)\Phi_{{t}}\quantity[\gamma_{+}(l)]e^{if_{{t}}\quantity[\gamma_{+}(l)]}\bigg)

(71)

Using Gauss-Freud quadrature, an integral of the form above can be approximated as a finite sum:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{l}h(l)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\frac{h(l_{j})}{\omega(l_{j})},\quad\text{where}

(72)

\displaystyle\omega(l)=e^{-l^{2}}.

(73)

Please refer to Refs.
Donnelly et al. 2021
;
Gil et al. 2007
for more details on the Gauss-Freud quadrature method. To reduce the error in the quadrature approximation, we need
h(l)/\omega(l)
to be well-approximated by a
2n-1
degree polynomial (Ref.
Gil et al. 2007
). We shall therefore introduce a constant scaling,
\lambda
, of the parameterization, such that the integral becomes:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{{t}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{l}h(l),\quad\mbox{where}\quad h(l)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}h_{-}(l)+h_{+}(l),

(74)

\displaystyle h_{\pm}(l)=\lambda\quantity(
\gamma'_{\pm}(\pm\lambda l)
\Phi_{{t}} \qty[\gamma_{\pm}(\pm\lambda l)]
e^{i f_{{t}}\qty[\gamma_{\pm}(\pm\lambda l)]}
).

(75)

To choose
\lambda
we note that
f_{t}\quantity[\gamma(l)]
is purely imaginary and increasing and then Taylor expand the imaginary part around
l=0
:

\displaystyle f_{t}\quantity[\gamma(l)]=i\quantity[ \qty(\frac{l}{\lambda_2})^2 + \qty(\frac{l}{\lambda_3})^3 + \cdots]

(76a)

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad\lambda_{m}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{m!}\p_{l}^m \Im{f_t\qty[\gamma(l)]}\at{l=0}}^{-1/m}

(76b)

The absolute value is included to stress that we assume the
m
’th derivative of
\imaginary f_{t}\quantity[\gamma(l)]
to be positive. We propose defining the global length scale as
\lambda=\lambda_{m}
, where
m
is the lowest positive integer for which
\lambda_{m}\leq\lambda_{m+1}
. With this definition, we will approximately have (in the vicinity of the saddle point):

\displaystyle f_{t}\quantity[\gamma(\lambda l)]\approx il^{m}

(77)

And thereby

\displaystyle\frac{h_{\pm}(l)}{\omega(l)}\approx\lambda\gamma_{\pm}^{\prime}(\pm\lambda l)\Phi_{t}\quantity[\gamma_{\pm} \qty(\pm\lambda l)]e^{l^{2}-l^{m}}

(78)

The Gauss Freud quadrature is therefore appropriate if
\Phi_{t}(l)e^{l^{2}-l^{m}}
is well-approximated by a
(2n-1)
degree polynomial, where
m
is the order of degeneracy. This should work especially well for non-degenerate saddle points, where
m=2
.

IV.3.3
Steepest Descent Angle Update Algorithm

Equation

67
is only accurate far from caustics, since the saddle point becomes degenerate at the caustic. Instead of using
eq.

67
we have therefore implemented an algorithm similar to, but slightly different from, the angle update algorithm of Ref.
Donnelly et al. 2021
. First, we assume that the directions of the contours are unchanged as we move away from the saddle point such that

\displaystyle\gamma(l)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\begin{cases}|l|e^{i\sigma_{-}}\quad l\leq 0\\
|l|e^{i\sigma_{+}}\quad l>0\end{cases}.

(79)

We start an iteration on each branch of the manifold at the
\tau
-value with maximal value of
\absolutevalue{j(\tau)}
. This is to ensure that we are as far from the caustics as possible such that we can use
eq.

67
as our initial values of
\sigma_{\pm}
. Then, for each point on the ray manifold we minimize
-\mathrm{Im}\{f(\epsilon)\}
on a circle of radius
L
and select the closest minimal loci
\sigma_{\pm}
relative to the
\sigma_{\pm}
-values found at the previous
\tau
step. In doing so, we avoid
\sigma_{+}=\sigma_{-}
which would not be a valid deformation of the contour. For the radius of the circle, we choose
L=l_{1}\lambda
where
l_{1}
is the lowest order node in the Gauss-Freud quadrature.

IV.4
Analytic Continuation to the Complex Plane

To evaluate the integrand along the steepest descent contour we need to know the values of the integrand in the complex domain. This is no problem in an analytic implementation of metaplectic geometrical optics, but in a numerical treatment we only know the function values of
\Psi_{{t}}\quantity[\epsilon+ {X}_{{t}}({t})]
on the real domain after having calculated the phase and envelope using
eqs.

56
and
57
. To solve this problem, we use barycentric rational interpolation (see Refs.
Berrut and Klein 2014
;
Nakatsukasa et al. 2018
;
Hofreither 2021
;
Trefethen 2023
) of the numerical signal of
\Psi_{{t}},f_{{t}}
. In a barycentric rational interpolation, a function
f(z)
of a complex variable
z
is represented as the ratio of two partial fractions:

\displaystyle r(z)=\frac{n(z)}{d(z)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{w_{j}f_{j}}{z-z_{j}}\bigg/\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{w_{j}}{z-z_{j}},

(80)

where
f_{j}=f(z_{j})
are known sampled values of the function and
w_{j}
are weights which must be chosen. In this form, one can see that
r\to f_{j}
for
z\to z_{j}
why defining
r(z_{j})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}f_{j}
for all
j
is meaningful such that
r(z)
is continuous and takes the sampled values at all interpolation points. Multiplying the nominator and denominator with the node polynomial
\ell
:

\displaystyle\ell(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\quantity(z - z_j),

(81)

shows that the barycentric representation is in fact a rational function, i.e. a quotient of two polynomials, Ref.
Nakatsukasa et al. 2018
. The barycentric form is less prone to numerical overflow compared to the raw rational form, Ref.
Berrut and Klein 2014
. A well known special case of barycentric interpolation is Lagrange polynomial interpolation, which uses
w_{j}=\prod_{k\neq j}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{-1}
. Rather than this choice of weights, we use the recently proposed adaptive Antoulas-Anderson algorithm to find the best interpolation. The algorithm is described in Ref.
Nakatsukasa et al. 2018
and the Python implementation which we use is due to Refs.
Hofreither 2021
;
Hofreither, Clemens 2024
. Although the algorithm generally performs well when interpolating, the precision when extrapolating an oscillatory function is generally acceptable one wavelength away from the interpolation domain, Ref.
Trefethen 2023
. An issue in using the adaptive Antoulas-Anderson algorithm for extrapolation is however that we only know the function values in a limited interval
\epsilon\in[a,b]
along the real line. Thus we are not guaranteed to have data in a sufficiently large radius around
\epsilon=0
for an interpolation to be valid. Furthermore, there may also be caustics at
\epsilon\neq 0
in the rotated frame which can cause numerical challenges. With these considerations, we pick a maximal value
M
beyond which we do not extrapolate. The value is chosen based on the wave number in the rotated phase space such that

\displaystyle M=\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}+\frac{\pi}{K_{t}(t)},

(82)

where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{max}}\mathrel{=\mkern-0.25mu:}\mathrm{min}(|a|,|b|)
. The maximal number of quadrature nodes we use is 10, but we choose the highest number of nodes,
n
, such that
\lambda l_{n}<M
, where
l_{n}
is the position of the
n
’th quadrature node. In the worst case where even the first quadrature node is outside the extrapolation domain, i.e.
\lambda l_{1}<M
, we instead change the scaling
\lambda
to make the nodes fit inside the extrapolation domain. There is a trade-off here; more nodes improves the accuracy of the integral, but at the same time requires the barycentric rational interpolation to be valid in a larger radius in the complex domain. The choices above are an attempt to balance these considerations in an automated fashion.

IV.5
Constructing the field

Once the inverse metaplectic transform has been applied, the branches of different sign of
j(\tau)
must be superimposed in order to produce the total field. As different branches may be known at different positions and with varying resolution, we here apply interpolation once again to evaluate the different branches in the same points. For this purpose we use a linear interpolation scheme on the different branches of the wave field in the original frame.

V
Results

Having outlined the theory of metaplectic geometrical optics and the numerical details of the implementation we will now proceed to showcase metaplectic geometrical optics in action. The only type of caustic occurring in 1D is the fold caustic and we shall see three examples of this. First we examine the Airy and Weber equation. Both problems are fundamental examples of caustics and have been treated analytically in the previous work by Ref.
Lopez and Dodin 2020
. Lastly, we demonstrate the numerical implementation on a fold caustic in connection with X-B mode coupling in a hot magnetized plasma. This problem has not previously been treated using metaplectic geometrical optics so we compare the results with particle-in-cell simulations.

Insertion of a plane wave ansatz or alternatively taking the Wigner-Weyl transform of the wave operator gives us the local dispersion relation for Airy’s equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x,k)=-k^{2}-x=0

A longer analytical analysis of the problem using the formulas from

\displaystyle f_{t}(\epsilon)=\Theta[X_{t}(t)+\epsilon]+k(t)\epsilon^{2}-\dfrac{k^{2}(t)}{\vartheta_{t}}\epsilon,

As our next example we consider Weber’s equation:

\displaystyle(2E_{\nu}+\partial_{x}^{2}-x^{2})\psi(x)=0,

(89)

where
E_{\nu}=\nu+1/2
and
\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Weber’s equation describes the quantum harmonic oscillator, with
E_{\nu}
being the energy associated with the mode number
\nu
. In a plasma physics context, this could be an O-mode inside a non-monotonic density profile, where it meets a cutoff on either side of the peak. These cutoffs are caustics and are found at

. After a Wigner-Weyl transform, the dispersion symbol is found to be (Ref.
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x,k)=(2E_{\nu}-k^{2}-x^{2})=0.

(90)

, as

\displaystyle\psi_{\nu}(x)=\dfrac{\pi^{-1/4}}{\sqrt{2^{\nu}\nu!}}H_{\nu}(x)e^{-x^{2}/2},

(91)

Again, we also cite the analytical results from Ref.

;
Nicolas Alexander Lopez 2022

8
:

\begin{gathered}f_{t}(\epsilon)=\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\sqrt{2E_{\nu}-\epsilon^{2}}+E_{\nu}\sin^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2E_{\nu}}}\right)\\
-\;\dfrac{\tan(2t)}{2}\epsilon^{2}-\sqrt{2E_{\nu}}\epsilon.\end{gathered}

(92)

For the numerical solutions, we follow a very similar procedure to Airy’s equation. This time, we initiate the ray tracing at
x=x_{0}=-R_{\nu}
, since
R_{\nu}=\sqrt{2E_{\nu}}
is the radius of the oscillation. The initial condition is chosen to match the exact solution at
x=x_{0}
. We use a ”ghost margin” of 16 % such that we trace 16 % of the number of time points at each side of the ray trace for ghost points. We show the result of the fundamental mode in FIG.
6

x=0

\sim 10\%
of the exact solution at all points. For higher mode numbers shown in FIG.
7

\sqrt{2\nu+1}

Nakatsukasa et al. 2018

We note that analytical results using the metaplectic geometrical optics method in Ref.
Lopez and Dodin 2020

V.3
X-B mode coupling in ASDEX Upgrade

In the final example, we apply the code to the problem of X-B coupling where X-mode couples to an electron Bernstein wave at the upper hybrid layer in a hot magnetized plasma. In this phenomenon a forward propagating electromagnetic wave is coupled to a backward propagating electrostatic wave. X-B mode coupling occurs when generating electron Bernstein waves for heating and current drive (Ref.
Laqua et al. 1997
;
Guo et al. 2022
) and for wave trapping related to low threshold two-plasmon decay instabilities (Ref.
Clod et al. 2024
). The nomenclature of what is meant by X-B and the different waves vary across the literature. We refer to the forward propagating wave as X-mode, the backward propagating wave as the electron Bernstein wave and the turning point as the upper hybrid layer. Unlike the cold O- and X-modes, the X-mode and the electron Bernstein wave are described by the same dispersion symbol and are simply two different parts of the same ray trajectory with different physical wave characteristics. The turning point is a fold caustic and the phase space trajectory around this point is similar to that of the cutoff in Airy’s equation except that the caustic occurs at non-zero
k
and the in- and outgoing branches describe different types of waves. This problem has not previously been treated analytically with metaplectic geometrical optics and is unlikely to ever be as the kinetic dispersion relations for magnetized plasmas are rather complicated. In place of a comparison with analytical theory, we will make use of 1D fully kinetic particle-in-cell simulations of X-B coupling at the upper hybrid layer. We take parameters inspired by the
Axially Symmetric Divertor Experiment Upgrade which is a medium sized tokamak equipped with several gyrotrons used for electron cyclotron resonance heating and current drive as well as for collective Thomson scattering diagnostics, Refs.
Kudlacek et al. 2024
;
Hansen et al. 2019
. For these parameters the upper hybrid layer of the gyrotrons is found between the fundamental and second harmonic electron cyclotron frequency.

For the particle-in-cell simulations, we use the particle-in-cell code EPOCH, Ref.
Arber et al. 2015
. It is a low power simulation that has previously been used in a study of parametric decay in Axially Symmetric Divertor Experiment Upgrade, Ref.
Senstius et al. 2020
, with 1 spatial and 3 velocity dimensions. It is a deuterium plasma with a linear density profile of
n_{\mathrm{e}}(x)=n_{\mathrm{i}}(x)=5.4\times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}-x\,2.0\times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{mm}^{-4}
, where subscript e and i refer to electrons and deuterons, with a uniform temperature profile of
T_{\mathrm{e}}=T_{\mathrm{i}}=300
eV and a magnetic field of
B=3.35
T, pointing perpendicular to the
x
-direction. The domain is 0 mm
\leq x\leq
13.5 mm with 1666 grid points and
6\times 10^{4}
macroparticles per grid point, corresponding approximately to 64 gridpoints per wavelength at the upper hybrid layer. The particle boundary conditions replace lost particles at the boundaries with new thermally distributed ones. The field boundary conditions are open with a
\omega/(2\pi)=105
GHz X-mode polarized wave of intensity
I=7\times 10^{7}\;\mathrm{W/m^{2}}
at the
x=0
boundary. With these parameters, the upper hybrid layer is found in the vicinity of
x=
12 mm and an excerpt of the longitudinal electric field can be seen in FIG.
11
. An interference pattern in space can be seen in the figure and the field is seen to peak at a finite value close to
x=
12 mm. The field appears to have a simple harmonic time dependence on the shown time scale.

To model this with geometrical optics and metaplectic geometrical optics, we use the following dispersion relation (Refs.
Senstius et al. 2024
;
Crawford and Tataronis 1965
;
Hansen 2019
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x,k,\omega)=K_{1}(x,k,\omega)k^{2}-\left(\dfrac{\omega}{c}\right)^{2}\left(S^{2}-D^{2}\right)

(93)

\displaystyle K_{1}(x,k,\omega)=1+\dfrac{\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}^{2}}{\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}^{2}}\exp(-\lambda)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{\psi}\dfrac{\sin\left(\psi\left(\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}}\right)\right)\sin(\psi)\exp(-\lambda\cos(\psi))}{\sin\left(\pi\left(\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}}\right)\right)},

(94)

where
S=1-\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}^{2}/(\omega^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}^{2})
and
D=\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}^{2}/(\omega(\omega^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}^{2}))
are the Stix sum and difference parameters,
\lambda=k^{2}v_{\mathrm{Te}}^{2}/(2\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}^{2})
is a normalized squared wavenumber,
\omega_{\mathrm{ce}}=eB/m_{\mathrm{e}}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}=\sqrt{e^{2}n_{e}/(\epsilon_{0}m_{\mathrm{e}})}
are the electron cyclotron and plasma frequencies, and
v_{\mathrm{Te}}=\sqrt{2T_{\mathrm{e}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
is the electron thermal speed. Note that we are neglecting contributions from the ions because we are considering a wave in the electron frequency range and the large mass ratio renders the ion contributions insignificant. As has been reported earlier in the literature, the theoretical dispersion relation does not perfectly match what is observed in EPOCH
Senstius et al. 2021
. The reasons could include numerical dispersion and that non-local and nonlinear effects are neglected in the linear dispersion relation but not in EPOCH.

As mentioned earlier, non-local and nonlinear effects are neglected even though the upper hybrid layer is often associated with nonlinear effects due, in part, to the caustic. Still, our numerical implementation of metaplectic geometrical optics is capable of capturing the features of the PIC wave field, provided that the dispersion manifold can be determined sufficiently well.

VI
Conclusion and discussion

We have presented the first unsupervised numerical implementation of metaplectic geometrical optics, relying only on a 1D discrete phase space trajectory produced by a ray tracer. The code needs no additional information to the ray tracer and reconstructs the wave field with good agreement, and importantly without singularities, for the bench marking examples of Airy’s and Weber’s equation. The numerical solutions display minor deviations near the caustics with a relative error within 10 % in the worst case example in Weber’s equation. This discrepancy may be attributed to the numerical integration method used to evaluate the inverse metaplectic transform or to an approximation of the near identity metaplectic transform. The contour integrals are performed using Gauss-Freud quadrature and a barycentric rational function fit, which reconstructs the target functions with great precision and does a decent job extrapolating to the complex domain. A problem in the current implementation is that the barycentric rational interpolation suffers from Froissart doublets. An approach to remove these doublets is proposed in Ref.
Nakatsukasa et al. 2018
, but not yet implemented in the numerical implementation presented here. Though there is room for optimization, our numerical implementation reconstructs the wave field in seconds to a few minutes on a modern laptop (MacBook Pro 2019) depending on the resolution needed for the problem (about 30 seconds for Airy’s equation with 700 ray tracing points and 1.5 minutes for the X-B example with 2500 ray tracing points). The numerical implementation is fully automated and the implementation can in principle be extended to more spatial dimensions in future work. However, it remains an unresolved problem to generalize the steepest-descent methods used in 1D in this work to multidimensional integrals. Importantly, the metaplectic geometrical optics algorithm is agnostic to the type of linear wave equation, though the current formulation of metaplectic geometrical optics is reserved to time-invariant scalar waves.

When applied to the problem of X-B mode coupling in a magnetized fusion plasma, the numerical implementation of metaplectic geometrical optics is capable of achieving good agreement with particle-in-cell simulations, with similar or better performance than geometrical optics and crucially without the divergent behavior at the caustic. This opens up for applications in reduced modeling of phenomena which depend nonlinearly on the electric field intensity such as three-wave interactions and stochastic heating.
