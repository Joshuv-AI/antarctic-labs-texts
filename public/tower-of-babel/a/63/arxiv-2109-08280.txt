Gaussian discrepancy: a probabilistic relaxation of vector balancing


Abstract
We introduce a novel relaxation of combinatorial discrepancy called Gaussian discrepancy , whereby binary signings are replaced with correlated standard Gaussian random variables. This relaxation effectively reformulates an optimization problem over the Boolean hypercube into one over the space of correlation matrices. We show that Gaussian discrepancy is a tighter relaxation than the previously studied vector and spherical discrepancy problems, and we construct a fast online algorithm that achieves a version of the Banaszczyk bound for Gaussian discrepancy. This work also raises new questions such as the Komlós conjecture for Gaussian discrepancy, which may shed light on classical discrepancy problems.

Introduction and overview of the paper

In this work we introduce a probabilistic relaxation of the classical combinatorial discrepancy problem that we call
Gaussian discrepancy
. In this section, we first briefly survey discrepancy theory and formally define our relaxation. Then we discuss our main results which consist of (i) sharp comparisons of Gaussian discrepancy to previously studied relaxations, and (ii) a fast algorithm for online Gaussian discrepancy. We conclude this overview with some open problems and an outline of the remainder of the paper.

1.1
Discrepancy theory and Gaussian discrepancy

1.1.1
Background on combinatorial discrepancy

Discrepancy theory is a rich area of mathematics which has both inspired the development of novel tools for its study, and found numerous applications in a variety of fields such as combinatorics, computer science, geometry, optimization, and statistics; see the textbooks
Matoušek 1999
;
Chazelle 2000
.
In one of its most fundamental forms, discrepancy asks the following question: given an
m\times n
matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, determine the value of the
discrepancy
of
A
, defined as

\displaystyle\disc(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\sigma\in{\{\pm 1\}}^{n}}{\lVert A\sigma\rVert_{\infty}}\,=\min_{\sigma\in{\{\pm 1\}}^{n}}{\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{\lvert(A\sigma)_{i}\rvert}}\,.

(1)

This question can be interpreted in terms of
vector balancing
: if
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}
denote the columns of
A
, then we are looking for a
signing
, that is, a vector of signs
\sigma\in{\{\pm 1\}}^{n}
, that makes the signed sum
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}v_{j}
have small entries.

A seminal result in this area, due to
Spencer 1985
and independently
Gluskin 1989
(see also
Giannopoulos 1997
), states that

\disc(A)\leq 6\sqrt{n}

(2)

when
m=n
and the entries of
A
are bounded in magnitude by
1
. This remarkable result is the best possible, up to the constant factor
6
, and should be compared with the discrepancy incurred by a signing chosen uniformly at random which is of order
\Theta(\sqrt{n\log n})
. A far-reaching extension of this result is the
Komlós conjecture

(
Spencer 1985
)
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Komlós conjecture)
.

There exists a constant
K>0
such that for any matrix
A
whose columns have Euclidean norm at most
1
, it holds that

\disc(A)\leq K\,.

This conjecture remains one of the most important open problems in the field, and the best known bound for the Komlós problem, due to
Banaszczyk 1998
, yields
\disc(A)=O(\sqrt{\log(m\land n)})
.
1
1

1

Here
m\land n=\min\{m,n\}
.
The Komlós conjecture contains as a special case the long-standing Beck–Fiala conjecture which states that if
A
has
t
-sparse columns in
\{0,1\}^{m}
, then
\disc(A)=O(\sqrt{t})

(
Beck and Fiala 1981
)
.

The original proofs of these results are non-constructive in the sense that they do not readily yield efficient algorithms for computing signings which achieve these discrepancy upper bounds. In the last decade, considerable effort was devoted to matching these upper bounds
algorithmically
. Starting with the breakthrough work of
Bansal 2010
, there are now a number of algorithmic results matching Spencer’s bound
(
Lovett and Meka 2012
;
Harvey et al. 2014
;
Levy et al. 2017
;
Rothvoss 2017
;
Eldan and Singh 2018
)
. The task of making Banaszczyk’s bound algorithmic was more challenging, and it was settled in the last few years in a line of works
(
Bansal et al. 2016
;
Levy et al. 2017
;
Bansal et al. 2018
;
Dadush et al. 2019
)
.

Recently,
online
discrepancy minimization
(
Bansal and Spencer 2020
;
Bansal et al. 2020
;
Alweiss et al. 2021
;
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
has seen increasing interest and has led to a new perspective on Banaszczyk’s result.
In the
oblivious
online setting, an adversary picks in advance vectors
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, each with Euclidean norm at most
1
. During each round
t=1,\dotsc,T
, the algorithm receives the vector
v_{t}
, and it must output a sign
\sigma_{t}\in\{\pm 1\}
. The goal of the algorithm is to minimize the maximum discrepancy incurred at any time,
i.e.
the quantity
\max_{t\in[T]}{\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}v_{s}\rVert_{\infty}}
.
Alweiss et al. 2021
conjecture the following online version of the Banaszczyk bound.

Conjecture 1.2

(Online Banaszczyk)
.

There exists a randomized algorithm for online balancing in the oblivious adversarial setting that with high probability achieves the bound

\max_{t\in[T]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}v_{s}\Bigr\rVert_{\infty}}\lesssim\sqrt{\log(mT)}\,,

for any sequence
v_{1},\ldots,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of vectors with Euclidean norm at most
1
.

This would be nearly optimal, as a lower bound of
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{\log T})
is established in
Bansal et al. 2020
.

1.1.2
Gaussian discrepancy and the coupling perspective

Motivated by the aforementioned longstanding conjectures, recent algorithmic progress, and the goal of shedding new light on the classical discrepancy minimization problem, in this work we introduce a novel relaxation called
Gaussian discrepancy
. Our route to Gaussian discrepancy is through an alternative perspective on the discrepancy objective (
1
) based on couplings of random variables.

Recall that a Rademacher random variable is distributed uniformly on
\{\pm 1\}
. A
coupling
of Rademacher random variables is a random vector for which each marginal distribution is Rademacher. Since a signing
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and its negative
-\sigma
achieve the same discrepancy, the uniform distribution on the set of optimal signings furnishes an
optimal
Rademacher coupling that minimizes the right-hand-side of (
1
) in expectation. Precisely, it holds that

\displaystyle\disc(A)=\min\Bigl\{\E\lVert A\sigma\rVert_{\infty}:\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{j}=-1)=\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{j}=+1)=\frac{1}{2}~\text{for all}~j\in[n]\Bigr\}\,,

(3)

where the minimization above is over couplings of Rademacher random variables.
2
2

2

Such a minimization can be seen as an instance of a multimarginal optimal transport problem
(
Pass 2015
;
Altschuler and Boix-Adserà 2021
)
.

The coupling perspective plays an important role in discrepancy theory and its applications. The recent algorithmic proof of Banaszczyk’s theorem by
Bansal et al. 2018
relies on the equivalence between Banaszczyk’s theorem
3
3

3

More precisely, in this statement we refer to the general result of
Banaszczyk 1998
about balancing vectors to lie in a convex body
K
; the case where
K
is the scaled
\ell_{\infty}
ball corresponds to the combinatorial discrepancy.
and the existence of sub-Gaussian distributions supported on
\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}v_{j}\}_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}
, as established by
Dadush et al. 2019
. Their algorithm, known as the
Gram-Schmidt walk
, focuses on correlating the entries of
\sigma
in order to control the sub-Gaussian constant of
A\sigma
. Further, in applications of discrepancy theory such as randomized control trials, it is important to output not only a single signing but rather a
distribution
over low-discrepancy signings for the purpose of inferring treatment effects
(
Krieger et al. 2019
;
Turner et al. 2020
;
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
.

To construct our relaxation, we replace the Rademacher distribution with the standard Gaussian distribution in the coupling interpretation (
3
) of combinatorial discrepancy. The
Gaussian discrepancy
is defined to be

\displaystyle\discG(A)=\min\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}\lVert Ag\rVert_{\infty}:g_{j}\sim\normal(0,1)~\text{for all}~j\in[n]\,,\;g_{1},\dotsc,g_{n}~\text{jointly Gaussian}\Bigr\}\,,

(4)

where
\NN(0,1)
denotes the standard normal distribution. Equivalently, the minimization is over covariance matrices
\Sigma
for the random vector
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
that lie in the
elliptope

\mathcal{E}_{n}
, the set of all positive semidefinite matrices whose diagonal is the all-ones vector. Unlike Rademacher couplings which require, a priori, an exponential number of parameters to describe, joint Gaussian couplings admit a compact description that is completely determined by the covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Let us record the simple observation that Gaussian discrepancy is indeed a relaxation of combinatorial discrepancy.

Proposition 1.3
.

For any matrix
A
, it holds that

\discG(A)\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\disc(A)\,.

Proof 1.4
.

Given an optimal signing
\sigma
for
\disc(A)
, let
g_{j}=\sigma_{j}\xi
for
j\in[n]
, where
\xi
is a standard Gaussian (equivalently, the covariance matrix of
g
is
\Sigma=\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf{T}}
).
Then,

\displaystyle\discG(A)

\displaystyle\leq\E\lVert Ag\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert A\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\E\lvert\xi\rvert=\sqrt{2/\pi}\disc(A)\,.

As a consequence, results on ordinary discrepancy, such as Spencer’s theorem and Banaszczyk’s theorem, immediately translate into bounds on the Gaussian discrepancy.

, while for matrices

1.3
Results

1.3.1
Comparisons between relaxations of discrepancy

In this section we develop an understanding of Gaussian discrepancy by comparing it to the
vector discrepancy
and
spherical discrepancy
relaxations of combinatorial discrepancy. Let us first define these notions and describe some results known about them.

The
vector discrepancy
relaxation replaces the signs
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}
in combinatorial discrepancy (
1
) with unit vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\in S^{n-1}
. Formally,

\displaystyle\discv(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\Bigl\{\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}:u_{1},\dotsc,u_{n}\in S^{n-1}\Bigr\}\,.

(5)

This problem is recast as a semidefinite program over the elliptope by constructing the Gram matrix,
\Sigma_{i,j}\coloneqq\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle
for
i,j\in[n]
, of the unit vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
; see
Nikolov 2013
for more details.

Given a signing
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
u\in S^{n-1}
, we can associate to it the unit vectors
u_{j}=\sigma_{j}u,\,j\in[n]
. From this we see that vector discrepancy is indeed a relaxation of discrepancy:

\discv(A)\leq\disc(A)\,.

Vector discrepancy has been highly influential in discrepancy theory. It led to the initial algorithm of
Bansal 2010
for Spencer’s theorem, which uses a random walk guided by vector discrepancy solutions
(
Bansal et al. 2016
, see also)
, as well as the recent constructive proof of Banaszczyk’s theorem
(
Bansal et al. 2018
)
.
4
4

4

Bansal et al. 2018
remark that their Gram-Schmidt algorithm was “…inspired by the constructive proof of
Dadush et al. 2019
for the existence of solutions to the Komlós vector coloring program of Nikolov.”
Vector discrepancy has also been studied in its own right in the work of
Nikolov 2013
which provides an in-depth analysis of this relaxation using SDP duality.

Spherical discrepancy
was more recently introduced by
Jones and McPartlon 2020
. It is obtained by relaxing the space of solutions in (
1
) from
{\{\pm 1\}}^{n}
to the sphere of radius
\sqrt{n}
. Formally,

\displaystyle\discs(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{x\in\sqrt{n}\,S^{n-1}}{\lVert Ax\rVert_{\infty}}\,.

(6)

Jones and McPartlon 2020
prove sharp bounds on spherical discrepancy in the setting of Spencer’s theorem (
\lvert A_{i,j}\rvert\leq 1
for all
i,j
) and the Komlós conjecture (
\lVert A_{:,j}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
for all
j\in[n]
) and apply their results to derive lower bounds for certain sphere covering problems.

With these definitions in hand, it is natural to wonder about the relationships between the various relaxations of discrepancy. For example, which relaxation gives the best approximation to combinatorial discrepancy? Our first result, whose proof we defer to Section
2
, provides a unifying probabilistic perspective on the three relaxations of discrepancy and leads to a straightforward comparison.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any matrix
A
, it holds that

\displaystyle\discv(A)

\displaystyle\asymp\min\bigl\{

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[m]}\E\lvert{(Ag)}_{i}\rvert

\displaystyle\bigm|g\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)\,,\;\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,,\;\diag\Sigma=\mathbf{1}_{n}\bigr\}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\discs(A)

\displaystyle\asymp\min\bigl\{

\displaystyle\E\max_{i\in[m]}{\lvert{(Ag)}_{i}\rvert}

\displaystyle\bigm|g\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)\,,\;\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,,\;\tr\Sigma=n\bigr\}\,.

(8)

Here,
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
is the set of symmetric positive semidefinite
n\times n
matrices.

For comparison, we recall that

\displaystyle\discG(A)

\displaystyle=\min\bigl\{\E\max_{i\in[m]}{\lvert{(Ag)}_{i}\rvert}\bigm|g\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)\,,\;\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,,\;\diag\Sigma=\mathbf{1}_{n}\bigr\}\,.

Hence,
\discv
relaxes the objective of Gaussian discrepancy by moving the maximum over rows outside of the expectation, whereas
\discs
relaxes the constraint of Gaussian discrepancy from
\diag\Sigma=\mathbf{1}_{n}
to
\tr\Sigma=n
.
In comparison, from Proposition
1.3
, the usual definition of discrepancy can be understood as
adding
a constraint to Gaussian discrepancy, namely that
\rank\Sigma=1
. The following relationship between the notions of discrepancy is an immediate consequence.

Corollary 1.6
.

For any matrix
A
,

\displaystyle\discv(A)\vee\discs(A)

\displaystyle\lesssim\discG(A)\lesssim\disc(A)\,.

In words, Gaussian discrepancy is a tighter relaxation of discrepancy than vector discrepancy and spherical discrepancy. Moreover, we show via a suite of examples in Section
2
that none of the inequalities in Corollary
1.6
can be reversed up to constant factor and that spherical discrepancy and vector discrepancy are incomparable in general.

Although Gaussian discrepancy is always larger than the vector discrepancy, the two relaxations are in fact closely related. Indeed, their common feasible set is the elliptope, which consists of the Gram matrices of collections of unit vectors or equivalently the covariance structures of feasible Gaussian couplings. The connection between these two relaxations yields our main tools for bounding Gaussian discrepancy as well as an
O(\sqrt{\log m})
-factor approximation algorithm for computing Gaussian discrepancy.

Our main inequality for controlling Gaussian discrepancy relies on a notion of
rank-constrained vector discrepancy
, defined as follows. For every rank
r\in[n]
, let

\displaystyle\discv_{r}(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\Bigl\{\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}:u_{1},\dotsc,u_{n}\in S^{r-1}\Bigr\}\,.

(9)

Equivalently we require the Gram matrix
\Sigma\coloneqq{(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)}_{i,j\in[n]}
to have rank at most
r
. Since a rank-
1
matrix
\Sigma
corresponds precisely to a signing, note that

\displaystyle\discv(A)=\discv_{n}(A)

\displaystyle\leq\discv_{n-1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\discv_{1}(A)=\disc(A)\,.

The next result compares Gaussian discrepancy with this rank-constrained problem and is a crucial ingredient in our study of online Gaussian discrepancy.

Proposition 1.7
.

For any
r\in[n]
, it holds that
\discG(A)\leq\sqrt{r}\discv_{r}(A).

Proof 1.8
.

Let
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{n}\in S^{r-1}
be an optimal solution for
\SDP_{r}(A)
. Then, if
\xi
denotes a standard Gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
, we can define
g_{j}\coloneqq\langle u_{j},\xi\rangle
for
j\in[n]
, and it can be easily checked that this is feasible for the definition of Gaussian discrepancy.
Hence,

\displaystyle\discG(A)\leq\E\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lvert\Bigl\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j},\xi\Bigr\rangle\Bigr\rvert}\leq\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}\E\lVert\xi\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{r}\SDP_{r}(A)\,.

(10)

The last inequality uses the standard probabilistic fact that
\E\lVert\xi\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{r}
.
When
r=1
, this can be improved to
\E\lvert\xi\rvert=\sqrt{2/\pi}
, which recovers Proposition
1.3
.

Applying a similar strategy but using the union bound instead of Cauchy–Schwarz shows that vector discrepancy upper bounds Gaussian discrepancy up to a logarithmic term.

Proposition 1.9
.

It holds that

\discG(A)\lesssim\sqrt{\log(2m)}\,\discv(A).

Proof 1.10
.

Let
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an optimal solution for
\SDP_{n}(A)
, and let
\xi\sim\NN(0,I_{n})
. As in the proof of Proposition
1.7
,

\displaystyle\discG(A)

\displaystyle\leq\E\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lvert\Bigl\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j},\xi\Bigr\rangle\Bigr\rvert}\,.

The random variable
\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j},\xi\rangle
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Thus the standard maximal inequality for sub-Gaussian random variables
(see, e.g.,
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Theorem 2.5)
yields

\displaystyle\discG(A)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\ln(2m)}\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}=\sqrt{2\ln(2m)}\SDP_{n}(A)\,.

(11)

We record three useful consequences of Proposition
1.9
. When combined with Theorem
1.5
it implies that Gaussian discrepancy is approximated by vector discrepancy up to a logarithmic factor. Since vector discrepancy admits an SDP formulation, it can be computed in polynomial time using methods from convex optimization
(
Nesterov 2018
)
, and in turn this yields our claimed
O(\sqrt{\log m})
-factor approximation algorithm for Gaussian discrepancy.

Second, Proposition
1.9
allows us to draw a new connection between spherical discrepancy and vector discrepancy. Combining this result with Corollary
1.6
and Proposition
1.7
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.11
.

For any matrix
A
, it holds that

\discs(A)\lesssim\sqrt{n\land\log m}\discv(A).

As described in detail in Section
2
, this inequality is tight and also may not be reversed — in fact it is possible to have
\discs(A)=0
while
\discv(A)>0
(see Example
2.4
). We remark that this sharp result falls out naturally with Gaussian discrepancy as a mediator between spherical and vector discrepancy.

Finally, Proposition
1.9
leads to a simple algorithm that achieves Banaszczyk’s bound for Gaussian discrepancy. The main result of
Nikolov 2013
states that the Komlós conjecture holds for vector discrepancy. Hence, a solution to the SDP formulation of vector discrepancy yields a feasible covariance matrix for Gaussian discrepancy with objective value
O(\sqrt{\log m})
.
5
5

5

This can be further refined to
O(\sqrt{\log(m\wedge n)})
using standard reductions.
Note that since Gaussian discrepancy is a relaxation, existing approaches for combinatorial discrepancy also achieve Banaszczyk’s bound for Gaussian discrepancy with much more involved algorithms
(
Bansal et al. 2016
;
Levy et al. 2017
;
Bansal et al. 2018
)
.

This raises the questions of whether or not the Komlós conjecture (Conjecture
1.1
) or online Banaszczyk (Conjecture
1.2
) can be proved for Gaussian discrepancy. More broadly, it is natural to ask whether unresolved conjectures about combinatorial discrepancy can be solved for a given relaxation. Indeed,
Nikolov 2013
and
Jones and McPartlon 2020
establish the Komlós conjecture for vector and spherical discrepancy, respectively. While we are not able to establish a Gaussian version of the Komlós conjecture, it serves as a tantalizing question for further study; see Section
1.4
for further discussion. On the other hand, our main algorithmic result establishes a Gaussian discrepancy variant of the online Banaszczyk bound, as discussed in the next section.

1.3.2
Online Banaszczyk bound for Gaussian discrepancy

We begin by recalling the setting of online discrepancy minimization with an oblivious adversary. First, an adversary picks vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{T}
of
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
. Then, in each round
t=1,2,\dots,T
the vector
v_{t}
is revealed to the algorithm, which must output a sign
\sigma_{t}\in\{\pm 1\}
. The aim of the algorithm is to minimize the maximum discrepancy incurred, i.e.
\max_{t\in[T]}{\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}v_{s}\rVert_{\infty}}
. Equivalently, in each round the algorithm is required to choose a
vector
of signs
\sigma^{(t)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{t}
that obeys the following
consistency condition
:

\sigma^{(t)}_{[t-1]}=\sigma^{(t-1)}\,,

(12)

where
x_{[t-1]}
denotes the restriction of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
to the first
t-1
coordinates. That is, the new signing
\sigma^{(t)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{t}
chosen in round
t
is forbidden from changing the signs specified in previous rounds.

The latter formulation motivates our Gaussian discrepancy variant of the online discrepancy problem. At each round
t
, we require the algorithm to output a
t
-dimensional correlation matrix
\Sigma^{(t)}
which satisfies a consistency condition analogous to (
12
), namely

\Sigma^{(t)}_{[t-1]\times[t-1]}=\Sigma^{(t-1)}\,.

(13)

Thus after round
t
, the correlations between the first
t
coordinates must remain fixed. The aim is still to minimize the maximum discrepancy incurred, i.e.
\max_{t\in[T]}\E\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s}v_{s}\rVert_{\infty}
, where
g=(g_{1},\dotsc,g_{T})\sim\normal(0,\Sigma^{(T)})
. A simple argument based on the Cholesky decomposition (see Appendix
A
for details) shows that any such sequence
\Sigma^{(1)},\dotsc,\Sigma^{(T)}
can be realized as a sequence of Gram matrices of initial segments of a sequence of unit vectors
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{T}\in\ell_{2}
.
Hence, it is equivalent to require that the algorithm output a unit vector
u_{t}\in\ell_{2}
at each round
t
, and then set
\Sigma^{(t)}_{i,j}=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle_{\ell_{2}}
. We arrive at the following formulation of online Gaussian discrepancy. As in online combinatorial discrepancy, we allow the algorithm to be randomized.

Problem 1.12

(Online Gaussian discrepancy with oblivious adversary)
.

An adversary selects vectors
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{T}
with
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
in advance. At each round
t\in[T]
, the algorithm observes
v_{t}
and outputs a random unit vector
u_{t}\in\ell_{2}
. The algorithm aims to minimize

\discG(v_{1},\ldots,v_{T};\Sigma^{(T)})=\max\limits_{t\in[T]}\E\Bigl\lVert\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}g_{s}v_{s}\Bigr\rVert_{\infty},\quad g\sim\NN(0,\Sigma^{(T)})

with high probability over the random correlation matrix
\Sigma_{i,j}^{(T)}=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle_{\ell_{2}}
.

We show in Appendix
A
that, without loss of generality, it suffices to consider
u_{t}
whose support is a subset of the first
t
coordinates.

One strategy for generating feasible couplings in each round is to first fix a
rank
parameter
r\geq 1
in advance and output a unit vector
u_{t}\in S^{r-1}
in each round. Our main result is an algorithm of this form that solves the Gaussian discrepancy variant of the online Banaszczyk conjecture (Conjecture
1.2
).

Theorem 1.13

(Online Banaszczyk bound for Gaussian discrepancy)
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote vectors of
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
selected in advance by an adversary. For all positive integers
r\geq 2
, there is a randomized online algorithm that with probability at least
1-\delta
outputs
u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}\in S^{r-1}
such that

\discG(v_{1},\ldots,v_{T};\Sigma^{(T)})=O\bigl(\sqrt{\log(mT/\delta)}\bigr),

where
\Sigma^{(T)}_{i,j}=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle
. The algorithm runs in time
O(mr)
per round.

The proof of Theorem
1.13
is the main content of Section
3
. If we could prove this theorem with
r=1
, then the online Banaszczyk problem (Conjecture
1.2
) would follow from the proof of Proposition
1.3
. Unfortunately, there is an obstruction preventing us from considering
r=1
, as described further below. Nevertheless, Theorem
1.13
shows that we are ‘one rank away’ from establishing Conjecture
1.2
.

Our algorithm is based on an intriguing idea in the recent paper
Liu et al. 2022
: namely, if one can find a Markov chain on
\mathbb{R}
whose increments take values in
\{\pm 1\}
and whose stationary distribution is a Gaussian, then it is possible to construct an algorithm, which they call the
Gaussian fixed-point walk
, with the property that each partial sum
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}v_{s}
is the difference of two Gaussian vectors. The online Banaszczyk bound would then follow from a union bound. However,
Liu et al. 2022
exhibit a
parity obstruction
which implies no such Markov chain exists on
\mathbb{R}
, and this leads them to consider instead Markov chains whose increments lie in
\{0,\pm 1\}
or
\{\pm 1,2\}
(and hence the resulting algorithms output partial colorings or improper colorings). We show that an analogous Markov chain
does
exist on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
for any
r\geq 2
whose increments lie in
S^{r-1}
and whose stationary distribution is a Gaussian with independent and identically distributed coordinates (see Figure
1
). Working with higher ranks
r\geq 2
avoids certain technical complications resulting in the partial coloring version derived by
Liu et al. 2022
and allows for a simple algorithm and analysis.

We note that the guarantee of Theorem

can be shown to be tight by using standard Gaussian estimates. In particular, taking
r>2
in our algorithm provably does not improve the Gaussian discrepancy.

Interestingly, our algorithm also has consequences for the previously unexplored problem of online vector discrepancy, and for this problem, choosing
r>2
offers a substantial improvement. Perhaps surprisingly, Theorem
1.14
below shows that the Komlós conjecture, with sharp constant, is attainable for vector discrepancy even in the oblivious online setting. In contrast,
Bansal et al. 2020
exhibit an
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{\log T})

3.7
proved in Section

Theorem 1.14

(Online Komlós bound for vector discrepancy)
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote vectors of
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
selected in advance by an adversary. Fix
\delta,\varepsilon>0
.
When run with rank
r=\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(mT/\delta))
, the algorithm outputs
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{T}\in S^{r-1}
online with

\max_{t\in[T]}\,\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}{(v_{s})}_{i}\,u_{s}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}\leq 1+\varepsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
. The algorithm runs in time
O(m\varepsilon^{-2}\log(mT/\delta))
per round.

As a corollary of the above theorem, we obtain a new proof of the vector Komlós theorem of
Nikolov 2013
, which states that
\discv(A)\leq 1
for all matrices
A
whose columns have
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
.
Since the vector discrepancy of the identity matrix
I_{T}
is
1
, our result is essentially sharp.

Although we present our algorithm for the online setting, it yields new algorithmic implications for the offline setting as well. In particular, our runtime is nearly linear in the input size
mT
for moderate values of the approximation parameter
\varepsilon
. This improves on the time complexity of previous algorithms for vector Komlós such as off-the-shelf SDP solvers, which require
\widetilde{O}({(m\lor T)}^{3.5}\log(1/\varepsilon))
arithmetic operations
(
Nesterov 2018
)
, and an iterative approach of
Dadush et al. 2019
that naïvely runs in time
O(T^{3}\,(m\lor T))
due to costly matrix inversion steps.
6
6

6

We do not take into account fast matrix multiplication for these runtime estimates. We also note that an SDP solver would in general yield the stronger guarantee
\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{T}{(v_{t})}_{i}\,u_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq{\discv(A)}^{2}+\varepsilon
for
1\leq i\leq m
.

Furthermore, setting
\varepsilon=1
in Theorem
1.14
implies that the Komlós conjecture holds for rank-constrained vector discrepancy with
r=O(\log(mT))
. To the best of our knowledge this result was previously unknown even in an offline setting.

1.4
Open problems

Our work leads to some natural open questions which we briefly describe below.

1.

Komlós conjecture for Gaussian discrepancy
. Does there exist a constant
K_{\rm G}>0
such that
\discG(A)\leq K_{\rm G}
for any matrix
A
whose columns have
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
?

Since Gaussian discrepancy is a relaxation, solving this conjecture is a natural prerequisite for establishing the original Komlós conjecture (Conjecture
1.1
). It also leads to a related problem: prove that the Komlós conjecture for Gaussian discrepancy implies the Komlós conjecture.

2.

Rounding Gaussian discrepancy solutions.
Does there exist an efficient rounding scheme to convert Gaussian discrepancy solutions to low-discrepancy signings? For example, in the Komlós setting, does there exist a polynomial-time computable function
f
from covariance matrices to signings, such that

\lVert Af(\Sigma)\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim\E_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\lVert Ag\rVert_{\infty}+O(1)\,\,,

for all matrices
A
with
\max_{j\in[n]}{\lVert A_{:,j}\rVert_{2}}\leq 1
?

In Appendix
B
, we show that two simple rounding schemes (Goemans–Williamson rounding and a PCA-based rounding) are not effective in the setting of Spencer’s theorem and the Komlós conjecture.

3.

Computational tractability of Gaussian discrepancy
. Given a matrix, can we compute its Gaussian discrepancy exactly or approximately in polynomial time? In particular, is it NP-hard to approximate Gaussian discrepancy up to a constant factor?
We note that hardness of approximation results are already known for combinatorial discrepancy
(
Charikar et al. 2011
)
and spherical discrepancy
(
Jones and McPartlon 2020
)
.

4.

Achieving Banaszczyk’s bound online
. Finally, we mention that the question of achieving Banaszczyk’s bound online for combinatorial discrepancy, as originally posed by
Alweiss et al. 2021
, is still open.

1.5
Organization of the paper

In Section
2.1
, we give the proof of our main comparison result between the relaxations of discrepancy (Theorem
1.5
). In Section
2.2
, we provide a suite of examples showing that the inequalities given in Section
1.3.1
are sharp. Finally, in Section
3
, we present our algorithm and prove that it achieves the online Banaszczyk bound for Gaussian discrepancy.

2
Comparisons between relaxations of discrepancy

In this section we prove Theorem
1.5
and give a number of illustrative examples that highlight the differences between the various relaxations of discrepancy. These examples show that none of the inequalities in Corollary
1.6
can be reversed in general (at least with constants independent of
m
and
n
). Moreover, our results in this section give evidence for the following assertion: vector discrepancy and spherical discrepancy each capture distinct aspects of the original discrepancy problem, namely SDP solutions are “aligned with the coordinate axes”, whereas spherical discrepancy solutions are “low rank”. Gaussian discrepancy appears to capture both of these aspects simultaneously.

This assertion can already be partially justified by observing that the feasible covariance matrices
\Sigma
for vector discrepancy satisfy
\diag\Sigma=\mathbf{1}_{n}
, i.e. the variance along each of the coordinate axes is fixed to be
1
. On the other hand, from the proof of Theorem
1.5
below, we see that a spherical discrepancy solution
x
gives rise to the covariance matrix
\Sigma\coloneqq xx^{\mathsf{T}}
, which is indeed low rank but is not guaranteed to have unit variance along each coordinate axis.

2.1
Proof of main comparison result

We repeatedly use the following useful facts about log-concave distributions. Note that any Gaussian random variable has a log-concave distribution.

Lemma 2.1

(
Alonso-Gutiérrez and Bastero 2015
)
.

Let
X
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a log-concave distribution, and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be any seminorm.

1.

For any
p>1
, it holds that
{\E[\lVert X\rVert^{p}]}^{1/p}\lesssim p\E\lVert X\rVert
, where the implied constant is universal.

2.

For any
t>0
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}\{\lVert X\rVert\leq t\E\lVert X\rVert\}\lesssim t
, where the implied constant is universal.

Proof 2.2

(Proof of Theorem
1.5
)
.

Vector discrepancy
: Given
\Sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
, let
g^{\Sigma}
denote a centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and write
UU^{\mathsf{T}}=\Sigma
with
u_{j}
denoting the rows of
U
. Observe that

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[m]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}u_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\max_{i\in[m]}{\langle A_{i,:},\Sigma A_{i,:}\rangle}}\,.

(14)

Therefore

\displaystyle\discv(A)=\min_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\sqrt{\max_{i\in[m]}{\langle A_{i,:},\Sigma A_{i,:}\rangle}}

\displaystyle=\min_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\sqrt{\max_{i\in[m]}\E\Bigl[\Bigl\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}g^{\Sigma}_{j}\Bigr\rvert^{2}\Bigr]}

\displaystyle\overset{(\star)}{\asymp}\min_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\max_{i\in[m]}\E\Bigl\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}g^{\Sigma}_{j}\Bigr\rvert=\min_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\max_{i\in[m]}\E\lvert{(Ag^{\Sigma})}_{i}\rvert\,,

where
(\star)
uses Lemma
2.1
and Cauchy–Schwarz.

Spherical discrepancy
: Let us temporarily denote

\displaystyle\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathsf{disc}_{\mathsf{s}}}}(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{\E\lVert Ag\rVert_{\infty}:g\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)\,,\;\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,,\;\tr\Sigma=n\}\,.

Let
g
be a Gaussian that achieves the minimum in the definition of
\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathsf{disc}_{\mathsf{s}}}}(A)
.
Then, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lVert Ag\rVert_{\infty}\geq 3\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathsf{disc}_{\mathsf{s}}}}(A)\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}\,.

(15)

From Lemma
2.1
, we further have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lVert g\rVert_{2}\leq c\E\lVert g\rVert_{2}\}\leq\frac{1}{3}\,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\E\lVert g\rVert_{2}\gtrsim\sqrt{\E\lVert g\rVert_{2}^{2}}

(16)

for some constant
c>0
. From (
16
) and (
15
), there exists a realization
\mathsf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
g
with

\lVert A\mathsf{g}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 3\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathsf{disc}_{\mathsf{s}}}}(A)\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert\mathsf{g}\rVert_{2}\geq c\E\lVert g\rVert_{2}\gtrsim\sqrt{\E\lVert g\rVert_{2}^{2}}=\sqrt{n}\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle\discs(A)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\lVert A\,\frac{\sqrt{n}\,\mathsf{g}}{\lVert\mathsf{g}\rVert_{2}}\bigr\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\lVert\mathsf{g}\rVert_{2}}\,\lVert A\mathsf{g}\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathsf{disc}_{\mathsf{s}}}}(A)\,.

Conversely, let
x
be an optimal solution for
\discs(A)
and let
\xi
be a standard Gaussian variable on
\mathbb{R}
, so
\xi x\sim\normal(0,xx^{\mathsf{T}})
and
\tr(xx^{\mathsf{T}})=\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}=n
.
Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathsf{disc}_{\mathsf{s}}}}(A)\leq\E\lVert A(\xi x)\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\E\lvert\xi\rvert\,\lVert Ax\rVert_{\infty}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\discs(A)\,,

which establishes the converse bound.

2.2
Tightness of the comparison results

Our first example demonstrates the sharpness of Corollary
1.11
with respect to both
m
and
n
.

Example 2.3
.

For this example we first consider an infinitely tall matrix
A
.
Let the rows of
A
consist of all unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By taking the vector discrepancy solution
\Sigma=I_{n}
and using (
14
), we see that

\displaystyle\discv(A)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\max_{a\in S^{n-1}}{\langle a,\Sigma a\rangle}}=1\,.

(In fact it is not hard to see that this is an equality.)
However, for any
x\in\sqrt{n}\,S^{n-1}
we have

\displaystyle\max_{a\in S^{n-1}}{\lvert\langle a,x\rangle\rvert}

\displaystyle=\lVert x\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{n}\,,

which shows that
\discs(A)=\sqrt{n}
. Thus, the spherical discrepancy can be much larger than vector discrepancy.

Although this example used an infinitely tall matrix
A
, we can modify it by taking the rows of
A
to consist of a
1/2
-net of
S^{n-1}
.
Then, the number of rows of
A
can be taken to be
m=\exp(\Theta(n))
, and a standard argument involving nets
(see the proof of
Vershynin 2018
, Lemma 4.4.1)
shows that we still have
\discv(A)\leq 1
and
\discs(A)\gtrsim\sqrt{n}
. In particular, this shows that the bound

\displaystyle\discs(A)

\displaystyle=O(\sqrt{n\wedge\log m})\discv(A)

obtained in Corollary
1.11
is sharp with respect to both
m
and
n
.

The next example shows that Corollary
1.11
cannot be reversed, even with a constant depending on
m
and
n
, so that vector discrepancy cannot in general be controlled by spherical discrepancy.

Example 2.4
.

Let
v\in S^{n-1}
be a unit vector and suppose that the rows of
A
consist of all vectors in

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\coloneqq S^{n-1}\cap v^{\perp}\,,

the set of unit vectors which are orthogonal to
v
(as in the preceding example, this example can also be modified to a matrix with finitely many rows).
Then,
\discs(A)\leq\sqrt{n}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\lvert\langle a,v\rangle\rvert}=0
.
On the other hand, we claim that
\discv(A)>0
for most choices of
v
.
Suppose to the contrary that there exist unit vectors
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
witnessing the fact that
\discv(A)=0
.
Then, for each
k\in[n]
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}u_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}}\geq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\Bigl\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}u_{j}[k]\Bigr\rvert}=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\lvert\langle a,u_{\bullet}[k]\rangle\rvert}=\lVert\proj_{v^{\perp}}u_{\bullet}[k]\rVert_{2}\,,

where
u_{\bullet}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the vector
{(u_{j}[k])}_{j=1}^{n}
.
The inequality implies that
u_{\bullet}[k]
is a multiple of
v
, i.e., there exists a scalar
c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u_{j}[k]=c_{k}v_{j}
for all
j\in[n]
.
Since
u_{j}
is a unit vector,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}=v_{j}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}\,,\qquad\text{for all}~j\in[n]\,.

In order for this to hold, each coordinate of
v
must have the same magnitude, i.e.
v
must be a signing (scaled by
n^{-1/2}
).
Hence, for most directions
v
we have
\discs(A)=0
but
\discv(A)>0
.

This shows in particular that there does not exist
C>0
such that
\discv(A)\leq C\discs(A)
for all
m\times n
matrices
A
, even if the constant
C
is allowed to depend on
m
and
n
.

The two preceding examples show that the statements
\discv\leq C_{1}\discs
and
\discs\leq C_{2}\discv
do
not
hold with universal constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. In particular, since
\discv\vee\discs\lesssim\discG
by Corollary
1.6
, it also implies that the statements
\discG\leq C_{1}^{\prime}\discv
and
\discG\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\discs
also do
not
hold with universal constants
C_{1}^{\prime}
and
C_{2}^{\prime}
.

We give another example to show that in general, Gaussian discrepancy can indeed be smaller than combinatorial discrepancy.

Example 2.5
.

Consider the case when
m=1
, so that
A
consists of a single row, and assume that the entries of
A
are i.i.d. standard Gaussians.
Then, the discrepancy of
A=(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n})
is usually referred to as the
number balancing
problem.

Let us suppose that
n
is a multiple of
3
, and divide the
n
coordinates into
3
groups consisting of
n/3
coordinates each. Due to concentration of measure, the sums

\ell_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n/3}{\lvert a_{i}\rvert}\,,\qquad\ell_{2}\coloneqq\sum_{i=n/3+1}^{2n/3}{\lvert a_{i}\rvert}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ell_{3}\coloneqq\sum_{i=2n/3+1}^{n}{\lvert a_{i}\rvert}

will concentrate around their expected value
\sqrt{2/\pi}\,n/3
. In particular, with high probability we will have
2\max_{i=1,2,3}\ell_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\ell_{i}
, which says that
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
, and
\ell_{3}
form the side lengths of a triangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If we let
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote unit vectors corresponding to the sides of these triangles, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n/3}{\lvert a_{i}\rvert\,u_{1}}+\sum_{i=n/3+1}^{2n/3}{\lvert a_{i}\rvert\,u_{2}}+\sum_{i=2n/3+1}^{n}{\lvert a_{i}\rvert\,u_{3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n/3}{a_{i}\,(\sgn a_{i})\,u_{1}}+\sum_{i=n/3+1}^{2n/3}{a_{i}\,(\sgn a_{i})\,u_{2}}+\sum_{i=2n/3+1}^{n}{a_{i}\,(\sgn a_{i})\,u_{3}}\,.

This shows that with high probability,
\discv_{2}(A)=0
and hence
\discG(A)=0
by Proposition
1.7
.

On the other hand, it is well-known that
\disc(A)=\Theta(\sqrt{n}\,2^{-n})>0

(
Karmarkar et al. 1986
;
Costello 2009
;
Turner et al. 2020
, see, e.g.,)
. In particular, there does not exist a constant
C>0
(even if the constant is allowed to depend on
m
and
n
) such that
\disc(A)\leq C\discG(A)
.

In summary, if we require the constants to be universal, then in general none of the inequalities in Corollary
1.6
can be reversed, and furthermore vector discrepancy and spherical discrepancy are incomparable.

In this section, we have argued that vector discrepancy and spherical discrepancy capture distinct aspects of the original discrepancy problem, and can therefore be viewed as complementary.
It is then natural to ask, whether a
combination
of vector discrepancy and spherical discrepancy can control Gaussian discrepancy.
This is also motivated by the results of
Nikolov 2013
and
Jones and McPartlon 2020
that prove the Komlós conjecture for vector discrepancy and spherical discrepancy respectively; hence, a control on the Gaussian discrepancy in terms of these two notions would imply the Komlós conjecture for Gaussian discrepancy as well.
We however resolve this question negatively via the following example.

Example 2.6
.

Let the rows of
A
consist of all unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
orthogonal to
e_{1}
.
Then, similarly to the previous examples, we have
\discv(A)\leq 1
and
\discs(A)=0
.
On the other hand, for any feasible Gaussian coupling
g
,

\displaystyle\E\lVert Ag\rVert_{\infty}=\E\max_{a\in S^{n-1}\cap e_{1}^{\perp}}{\lvert\langle a,g\rangle\rvert}=\E\lVert\proj_{e_{1}^{\perp}}g\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\star)}{\gtrsim}\sqrt{\E[\lVert(0,g_{2},\dotsc,g_{n})\rVert_{2}^{2}]}=\sqrt{n-1}\,,

where the inequality
(\star)
uses Lemma
2.1
.
Hence,
\discG(A)\gtrsim\sqrt{n-1}
.
This in fact shows that there is no non-trivial function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
which is independent of both
m
and
n
, such that

\displaystyle\discG(A)\leq f\bigl(\discv(A),\discs(A)\bigr)

for all matrices
A
.

3
Gaussian fixed-point walk in higher dimensions

3.1
High-level overview

We begin by summarizing the Gaussian fixed-point walk as introduced in
Liu et al. 2022
. Recall the setting of online discrepancy minimization with oblivious adversary: the adversary chooses in advance vectors
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and at each time step
t\in[T]
, the vector
v_{t}
is revealed to the algorithm, upon which it must then choose a sign
\sigma_{t}\in\{\pm 1\}
.
The loss incurred by the algorithm is the maximum discrepancy
\max_{t\in[T]}{\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}v_{s}\rVert_{\infty}}
.

Let
w_{t}\coloneqq w_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s}v_{s}
denote the partial sum vector with initialization
w_{0}\sim\NN(0,I_{m})
. The idea behind the Gaussian fixed-point walk is to choose the signs
\sigma_{t}
to ensure that
w_{t}\sim\normal(0,I_{m})
for all
t\in[T]
(observe that if this holds, then the loss
\max_{t\in[T]}{\lVert w_{t}-w_{0}\rVert_{\infty}}
incurred by the algorithm is easily controlled via a union bound).
Towards this end, we may assume as an inductive hypothesis that
w_{t-1}\sim\normal(0,I_{m})
.
Then, we may write

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle={\underbrace{\proj_{v_{t}^{\perp}}w_{t-1}}_{\sim\NN(0,I_{m}-v_{t}v_{t}^{\perp})}}+{[{\underbrace{\proj_{v_{t}}w_{t-1}}_{\sim\NN(0,v_{t}v_{t}^{\perp})}}+\sigma_{t}v_{t}]}\,.

If we choose
\sigma_{t}
independently of
\proj_{v_{t}^{\perp}}w_{t-1}
, then the two terms in the above decomposition are independent. Moreover, if we could choose
\sigma_{t}
such that
\proj_{v_{t}}w_{t-1}+\sigma_{t}v_{t}\sim\normal(0,v_{t}v_{t}^{\perp})
, then
w_{t}\sim\NN(0,I_{m})
. These considerations lead to the problem of finding a one-dimensional Markov chain (on
\spn v_{t}
) whose increments belong to
\{\pm v_{t}\}
, and whose stationary distribution is
\NN(0,v_{t}v_{t}^{\perp})
. However, a parity argument given in
Liu et al. 2022
shows that such a Markov chain does not exist, even if we allow for changing the variance of the Gaussian from
1
.

To deal with this issue,
Liu et al. 2022
show that such Markov chains exist if we allow the increments to take values in
\{0,\pm v_{t}\}
or
\{\pm v_{t},2v_{t}\}
, which leads to the algorithm outputting partial or improper colorings. If the variance of the Gaussian is set to be sufficiently large, then they can further argue that the algorithm outputs an actual signing with high probability, but the large variance of the Gaussian prevents them from establishing an online Banaszczyk result.

Our contribution in this section lies in adapting the idea above to online Gaussian discrepancy, by constructing a Markov chain in
\mathbb{R}^{r},\,r\geq 2
whose stationary distribution is a Gaussian, and whose increments lie in on the unit sphere; this is given in Section
3.2
. The algorithm and analysis are then given in Section
3.3
.

3.2
Construction of the Markov chain

Our goal in this section is to construct a Markov chain on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
for
r\geq 2
whose increments are unit vectors and whose stationary distribution is
\NN(0,\sigma^{2}I_{r})
for some
\sigma^{2}
. In this and subsequent sections,
\sigma
is used to denote the standard deviation (and in particular should not be confused with a signing).

The density of the
\chi
-distribution with
r
degrees of freedom is

\chi_{r}(s)=\frac{1}{2^{r/2-1}\,\Gamma(r/2)}\,s^{r-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\one\{s\geq 0\}\,.

Hence, the density of
\lVert g\rVert_{2}
where
g\sim\NN(0,\sigma^{2}I_{r})
is

\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)=\frac{1}{2^{r/2-1}\,\Gamma(r/2)}\,\frac{s^{r-1}}{\sigma^{r}}\,e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\one\{s\geq 0\}\,.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, and define the following sets:

\displaystyle S_{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}=1~\text{and}~\lVert y\rVert_{2}=\lVert x\rVert_{2}\}\,,

\displaystyle S_{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}=1~\text{and}~\lVert y\rVert_{2}=1-\lVert x\rVert_{2}\}\,.

Since
\lVert y\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\,\langle y-x,x\rangle
, if
\lVert y-x\rVert_{2}=1
then from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle{(\lVert x\rVert_{2}-1)}^{2}\leq\lVert y\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq{(\lVert x\rVert_{2}+1)}^{2}\,.

Conversely, if
s\geq 0
satisfies
{(\lVert x\rVert_{2}-1)}^{2}\leq s^{2}\leq{(\lVert x\rVert_{2}+1)}^{2}
, then there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
\lVert y-x\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert y\rVert_{2}=s
. From this, we deduce that

\displaystyle\bigl[S_{x}\neq\varnothing\quad\text{if}\quad\lVert x\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigr]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigl[S_{x}^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\quad\text{if}\quad\lVert x\rVert_{2}\leq 1\bigr]\,.

Further note that if
0<\lVert x\rVert_{2}\leq 1
, then
S^{\prime}_{x}
consists of a single element given by
y=(\frac{\lVert x\rVert_{2}-1}{\lVert x\rVert_{2}})x
, while
S^{\prime}_{0}=S^{r-1}
is the unit sphere.

The Markov chain transitions from
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with norm
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=s
to the next point according to the following rules.

1.

If
0\leq s<\frac{1}{2}
, move to a point chosen uniformly at random in
S_{x}^{\prime}
.

2.

If
\frac{1}{2}\leq s<1
:

(a)

with probability
\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)}
, move to a point chosen uniformly at random in
S_{x}^{\prime}
.

(b)

with probability
1-\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)}
, move to a point chosen uniformly at random in
S_{x}
.

3.

If
s\geq 1
, then move to a point chosen uniformly at random in
S_{x}
.

Symbolically, this defines a transition kernel
q(x,\cdot)
described explicitly as follows. Let
\mathsf{P}_{x}
(respectively
\mathsf{P}_{x}^{\prime}
) denote the uniform distribution on
S_{x}
(respectively
S_{x}^{\prime}
).
Then:

q(x,\cdot)=\begin{cases}\mathsf{P}_{x}^{\prime}\,,&\text{if}\,\,0\leq s<\frac{1}{2}\\[3.00003pt]
\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)}\,\mathsf{P}_{x}^{\prime}+(1-\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)})\,\mathsf{P}_{x}\,,&\text{if}\,\,\frac{1}{2}\leq s<1\\
\mathsf{P}_{x}\,,&\text{if}\,\,s\geq 1.\end{cases}

(17)

This Markov chain is only well-defined if
\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\geq\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)
for all
0\leq s<1/2
.
The following lemma shows that this holds if and only if
\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r-1}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The inequality
\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\geq\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)
holds for all
s\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
if and only if
\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r-1}}
.

Proof 3.2
.

Recall that
\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\propto s^{r-1}\exp(-s^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
, so that the mode is at
\sigma\sqrt{r-1}
(this can be derived via elementary calculus, since the density is log-concave).
In particular, if
\sigma<1/(2\sqrt{r-1})
, then the mode lies in
(0,1/2)
, so the desired property cannot hold.

Conversely, suppose
\sigma\geq 1/(2\sqrt{r-1})
.
We want to show, for all
s\in[0,1/2]
,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\overset{!}{\geq}\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}={\bigl(\frac{s}{1-s}\bigr)}^{r-1}\exp\bigl(-\frac{s}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigr)\,.

Taking logarithms and rearranging, we want

\displaystyle\psi(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(r-1)\log\frac{s}{1-s}-\frac{s}{\sigma^{2}}\overset{!}{\leq}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,.

Differentiating, we obtain

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle\geq(r-1)\,\bigl(\frac{1}{s\,(1-s)}-4\bigr)\geq 0\,,

so
\psi
is increasing.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{[0,1/2]}\psi

\displaystyle=\psi\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\bigr)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}

as desired.

With this result in hand, we verify that the Markov chain construction has the correct stationary distribution.

Proposition 3.3
.

The Markov transition kernel
q
on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
defines a Markov chain with unit length increments and stationary distribution
\NN(0,\sigma^{2}I_{r})
provided that
\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r-1}}
.

Proof 3.4
.

We represent a point in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
in spherical coordinates, i.e., as a pair
(s,\theta)
with
s\geq 0
and
\theta\in S^{r-1}
.
Let
\pi\coloneqq\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}\otimes\lambda
, where
\lambda
is the uniform distribution on
S^{r-1}
.
Via a change of variables, it suffices to check that for every bounded measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{r-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\iint f(s^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}\theta)\,q\bigl((s,\theta),(\mathrm{d}s^{\prime},\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\bigr)=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi\,.

We rewrite the LHS of this equation as

\displaystyle\iint f(s^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}\theta)\,q\bigl((s,\theta),(\mathrm{d}s^{\prime},\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl[\iint f(s^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,q\bigl((s,\theta),(\mathrm{d}s^{\prime},\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\bigr)\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1/2}+\int_{1/2}^{1}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\Bigr)\cdots\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s={\rm I}+{\rm II}+{\rm III}\,,

and examine each of the three terms separately.

For the first term,

\displaystyle{\rm I}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}\Bigl[\iint f(1-s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,T_{\#}\mathsf{P}_{(s,\theta)}^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

where
T:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to S^{r-1}
is the map
x\mapsto x/\lVert x\rVert_{2}
.
Due to the rotational symmetry we have
\int\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta)T_{\#}\mathsf{P}^{\prime}_{(s,\theta)}(\cdot)=\lambda(\cdot)
, so that evaluating the integral over
\theta
yields:

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}\Bigl[\int f(1-s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{1/2}^{1}\Bigl[\int f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

For the second term, a similar argument yields

\displaystyle{\rm II}

\displaystyle=\int_{1/2}^{1}\Bigl[\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)}\iint f(1-s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,T_{\#}\mathsf{P}_{(s,\theta)}^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad{}+\bigl(1-\frac{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)}{\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)}\bigr)\iint f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,T_{\#}\mathsf{P}_{(s,\theta)}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{1/2}^{1}\Bigl[\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)\int f(1-s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})+\bigl(\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)-\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)\bigr)\int f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}\Bigl[\int f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad{}+\int_{1/2}^{1}\Bigl[\int f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\bigl(\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)-\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(1-s)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Finally, the third term is

\displaystyle{\rm III}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\Bigl[\iint f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,T_{\#}\mathsf{P}_{(s,\theta)}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{1}^{\infty}\Bigl[\int f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Putting it together, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm I}+{\rm II}+{\rm III}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl[\int f(s,\theta^{\prime})\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\chi_{r,\sigma^{2}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi

which verifies the stationarity of
\pi
.

For the details of how to sample from the Markov kernel (
17
) see Appendix
C
.

3.3
Algorithm and analysis

We develop some notation needed for our algorithm. Let
\sigma_{\star,r}=1/(2\sqrt{r-1})
; recall that this is the smallest standard deviation parameter so that for
\sigma^{2}\geq\sigma_{\star,r}^{2}
, the transition probabilities (
17
) are well-defined. For
\sigma^{2}\geq\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}
, we let
q_{\sigma^{2}}
denote the corresponding Markov transition kernel with stationary distribution
\NN(0,\sigma^{2}I_{r})
as shown in Proposition
3.3
.
Also, we let
\normal(0,\sigma^{2}I_{m\times r})
denote the law of an
m\times r
matrix whose entries are i.i.d.
\normal(0,\sigma^{2})
.

{algorithm}

[H]

Rank-
r
Gaussian Fixed-Point Walk

Input

v_{1},\ldots,v_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
\ell_{2}
-norm at most
1

Initialize

W_{0}\sim\NN(0,\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}\,I_{m\times r})

\For
t=1,\dotsc,T

Define
V_{t}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
to be the matrix whose
k
-th column is
v_{t}
and remaining entries are
0
.

\sigma_{t}\leftarrow\sigma_{\star,r}/\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}

z_{t}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}W^{\mathsf{T}}_{t-1}v_{t}=\frac{1}{\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\,{(\langle W_{t-1},\,V_{t}^{(k)}\rangle)}_{k\in[r]}

z_{t}^{\prime}\leftarrow
sample from
q_{\sigma_{t}^{2}}(z_{t},\cdot)

u_{t}\leftarrow z_{t}^{\prime}-z_{t}

W_{t}\leftarrow W_{t-1}+v_{t}u_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}=W_{t-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}u_{t}[k]\,V_{t}^{(k)}

Output

u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\max\limits_{t\in[T]}{\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}}\leq 1
, then for all
t\in[T]
, the partial sum
W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
of Algorithm
3.3
is distributed as
\NN(0,\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}\,I_{m\times r})
.

Proof 3.6
.

We prove this by induction, so suppose that
W_{t-1}\sim\NN(0,\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}\,I_{m\times r})
.
Let
S_{t}
be the subspace
S_{t}=\spn\{V_{t}^{(k)}:k\in[r]\}
and observe that

W_{t-1}=\proj_{S_{t}^{\perp}}W_{t-1}+\proj_{S_{t}}W_{t-1}=\proj_{S_{t}^{\perp}}W_{t-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}z_{t}[k]\,V_{t}^{(k)}\,.

Using standard facts about Gaussian random variables, these two components are independent and
z_{t}\sim\NN(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{r})
(recall the definition of
\sigma_{t}^{2}\coloneqq\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}/\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}
). Also, the sampling
z_{t}^{\prime}\sim q_{\sigma_{t}^{2}}(z_{t},\cdot)
is well-defined because
\sigma_{t}\geq\sigma_{\star,r}
by the assumption that
\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Next,

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle=W_{t-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}u_{t}[k]\,V_{t}^{(k)}=\underbrace{\proj_{S_{t}^{\perp}}W_{t-1}}_{=:W_{t}^{(1)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{r}(z_{t}+u_{t})[k]\,V_{t}^{(k)}}_{=:W_{t}^{(2)}}\,.

Note that
z_{t}^{\prime}=z_{t}+u_{t}\sim\NN(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}I_{r})
and
\lVert u_{t}\rVert_{2}=1
by Proposition
3.3
. Hence,
W_{t}^{(2)}
is distributed as a centered Gaussian on
S_{t}
with variance
\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}
. Moreover,
W_{t}^{(1)}
is independent of
W_{t}^{(2)}
: by the construction of our Markov chain, the distribution of
z_{t}^{\prime}=z_{t}+u_{t}
is a function only of
z_{t}
, and
z_{t}
is independent of
W_{t}^{(1)}
as noted above. Thus
W_{t}\sim\NN(0,\sigma^{2}_{\star,r}\,I_{m\times r})
.

The following guarantee for Algorithm
3.3
is an easy consequence. For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
, note that the
2\to\infty
norm satisfies
\lVert M\rVert_{2\to\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\lVert M_{i,:}\rVert_{2}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

If
\max\limits_{t\in[T]}{\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{2}}\leq 1
, then Algorithm
3.3
outputs
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with the following properties.
In expectation,

\displaystyle\E\max_{t\in[T]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}u_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr\rVert_{2\to\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(mT)}{r-1}}+\sqrt{\frac{r}{r-1}}\,.

Also, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[T]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}u_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr\rVert_{2\to\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2mT/\delta)}{r-1}}+\sqrt{\frac{r}{r-1}}\,.

Finally, the time complexity of Algorithm
3.3
is
O(mrT)
.

Proof 3.8
.

Note that
\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}u_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}=W_{t}-W_{0}
, where
W_{0},W_{t}\sim\normal(0,\sigma_{\star,r}^{2}\,I_{m\times r})
by Proposition
3.5
, so that
\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}u_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{2\to\infty}\leq\lVert W_{0}\rVert_{2\to\infty}+\lVert W_{t}\rVert_{2\to\infty}
.
Since
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2\to\infty}
is the maximum
\ell_{2}
norm of a row, then
\max_{t\in[T]}{\lVert W_{t}\rVert_{2\to\infty}}
can be written as a maximum over
mT
(not necessarily independent) random variables
Y_{i}
, where each
Y_{i}
is a norm of a
\normal(0,\sigma_{\star,r}^{2}\,I_{r})
random variable. On the other hand, Gaussian concentration of Lipschitz functions
(
Boucheron et al. 2013
, see Theorem 5.5 of)
implies that each
Y_{i}\in\mathsf{subG}(\sigma_{\star,r}^{2})
, whereas
\E Y_{i}\leq\sigma_{\star,r}\sqrt{r}
.
A similar statement holds for
W_{0}
.

For the expectation bound, a standard sub-Gaussian bound yields

\displaystyle\E\max_{t\in[T]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}u_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr\rVert_{2\to\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\E\max_{i\in[mT]}Y_{i}\leq 2\E\max_{i\in[mT]}{(Y_{i}-\E Y_{i})}+2\sigma_{\star,r}\sqrt{r}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{\star,r}\,\{\sqrt{2\log(mT)}+\sqrt{r}\}\,,

and the result follows from
\sigma_{\star,r}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r-1}}
.
The proof of the high-probability bound also uses standard facts about sub-Gausssian random variables and is omitted.

As mentioned in Section
1.3
, our online vector Komlós result (Theorem
1.14
) is an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.7
. Interestingly, Theorem
3.7
also implies that scaling
\sigma_{\star,r}^{2}\sim\frac{1}{4r}
of our variance parameter is asymptotically optimal in that no sequence of Markov chains, defined on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
for
r\to\infty
with unit length increments and Gaussian stationary distribution, can achieve a smaller asymptotic variance. Indeed, a smaller asymptotic variance would lead to an improvement of Theorem
3.7
, which would further yield an improvement of the bound
\discv(A)\leq 1
of
Nikolov 2013
. However, the example of the identity matrix shows that this is not possible. We do not know if our variance parameter
\sigma_{\star,r}^{2}=\frac{1}{4\,(r-1)}
can be improved for any finite
r
.

We are now ready to prove our main result, the online Banaszczyk bound for Gaussian discrepancy.

Proof 3.9

(Proof of Theorem
1.13
)
.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{T}
denote the output of Algorithm
3.3
with rank parameter
r\geq 2
, and let
\Sigma^{(T)}_{i,j}=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle_{\ell_{2}}
denote the corresponding Gram matrix. As a consequence of Theorem
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle\max\limits_{t\in[T]}{\Bigl\lVert\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}v_{s}u_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr\rVert_{2\to\infty}}\lesssim 1\lor\sqrt{\frac{\log(mT/\delta)}{r}}

(18)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. If
r\geq\log(mT/\delta),
then combining (
18
) with (
11
) from the proof of Proposition
1.9
implies that

\discG(v_{1},\ldots,v_{T};\Sigma^{(T)})\lesssim\sqrt{\log(2m)}

with probability at least

. If

, then combining (

) with (

Appendix A
Equivalence of online discrepancy formulations

In Section
1.3.2
we introduced the online Gaussian discrepancy problem, and described two formulations: one in terms of correlation matrices, and one in terms of unit vectors in
\ell_{2}
. We now show that the two approaches are equivalent, so that the description of Problem
1.12
is without loss of generality.

It will be useful to define the
Cholesky decomposition
of a PSD matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We define
\mathsf{Ch}(\Sigma)
to be the unique matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that is lower triangular, has exactly
r=\mathsf{rank}(\Sigma)
positive diagonal elements, has
n-r
columns consisting entirely of zeros, and satisfies

\Sigma=LL^{\mathsf{T}}\,.

We refer the reader to
Gentle 2012
for details regarding existence and uniqueness.

Proposition A.1
.

The following hold.

1.

Let
{(\Sigma^{(t)})}_{t\geq 1}
be a stream of consistent (in the sense of (
13
)) correlation matrices. Then one can output unit vectors
{(u_{t})}_{t\geq 1}
in
\ell_{2}
online, such that
\Sigma^{(t)}
is the Gram matrix of
u_{1},\dots,u_{t}
for each
t\geq 1
. Moreover, only the first
t
coordinates of
u_{t}
need be non-zero.

2.

Let
{(u_{t})}_{t\geq 1}
be a stream of unit vectors in
\ell_{2}
. Then one can output consistent (in the sense of (
13
)) correlation matrices
{(\Sigma^{(t)})}_{t\geq 1}
online, such that
\Sigma^{(t)}
is the Gram matrix of
u_{1},\dots,u_{t}
for each
t\geq 1
.

Proof A.2
.

Note that the second assertion is immediate after defining
\Sigma^{(t)}_{i,j}=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle_{\ell_{2}}
for all
i,j\in[t]
and
t\geq 1
. Now, we turn to the proof of the first assertion. For convenience, we will write
\iota_{n}
for the inclusion map from
\mathbb{R}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
k\leq n
, with action
\iota_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},0,\dots,0)
. Let
\tilde{u}_{t}={\mathsf{Ch}(\Sigma^{(t)})}_{t,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
be the last row of
\mathsf{Ch}(\Sigma^{(t)})
for each
t\geq 1
, and let
u_{t}=\iota_{\infty}(\tilde{u}_{t})
. We claim that the sequence
{(u_{t})}_{t\geq 1}
satisfies the requirements of the assertion. Clearly only the first
t
coordinates of
u_{t}
can be nonzero. Thus, it remains to show that
\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle_{\ell_{2}}=\Sigma^{(t)}_{i,j}
for all
i,j\in[t],\,t\geq 1
.

Define
L^{(t)}=\mathsf{Ch}(\Sigma^{(t)})
and
r_{t}=\mathsf{rank}(\Sigma^{(t)})
. It is sufficient to show that for all
t\geq 1
, the rows of
L^{(t)}
are
\iota_{t}(\tilde{u}_{1}),\ldots,\iota_{t}(\tilde{u}_{t})
. This is true for
t=1
by construction. We proceed by induction and suppose that the claim is true for round
t-1
. Let
L^{\prime}
denote the principal minor of
L^{(t)}
corresponding to the first
t-1
indices. It suffices to show that
L^{\prime}=L^{(t-1)}
by the inductive assumption.

Both
L^{(t-1)}
and
L^{\prime}
are lower triangular by construction. Next, by consistency and the definition of the Cholesky decomposition, we have

L^{(t-1)}\,(L^{(t-1)})^{\mathsf{T}}=\Sigma^{(t-1)}=\Sigma^{(t)}_{[t-1]\times[t-1]}=L^{\prime}\,{(L^{\prime})}^{\mathsf{T}}\,.

Therefore,
\mathsf{rank}(L^{(t-1)})=\mathsf{rank}(L^{\prime})=r_{t-1}
. Since columns of
L^{(t)}
with non-positive diagonal elements contain all zeros, it follows that
L^{\prime}
has
r_{t-1}
positive elements on its diagonal. Consequently,
L^{\prime}
also has
t-1-r_{t-1}
columns consisting of all zeros. We conclude that
L^{\prime}=\mathsf{Ch}(\Sigma^{(t-1)})=L^{(t-1)}
, as desired.

Appendix B
Failure of some rounding schemes

As a preliminary, we refer to a univariate function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as a
sign function
if

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if }x>0\\
-1\quad&\text{if }x<0\end{cases}

and
f(0)\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Given a vector
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
\sgn(\xi)
denote a function given by the entrywise application of sign functions to the coordinates of
\xi
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the correlation matrix achieving the optimal Gaussian discrepancy. In this section, we consider two ways of rounding
\Sigma
to a signing. The Goemans–Williamson rounding
(
Goemans and Williamson 1995
)
is defined by drawing a random vector
\xi\sim\NN(0,\Sigma)
and setting

\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\sgn(\xi)\,.

The PCA rounding is defined by taking a top eigenvector
v_{1}(\Sigma)
of
\Sigma
and setting

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\sgn v_{1}(\Sigma)\,.

Note that when
\Sigma=\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf{T}}
has rank
1
, then
\sigma\in{\{\pm 1\}}^{n}
and both of these rounding schemes recover
\sigma
(up to a global sign flip).

In this section, we show that in the setting of both Spencer’s theorem and the Komlós conjecture, these rounding schemes are not very effective. Concretely, for both of these settings, we construct a random matrix that has a planted Gaussian discrepancy solution with objective value zero. However, rounding this solution with either Goemans–Williamson or PCA rounding results in a value of the combinatorial discrepancy that is much larger than the guarantees obtained by direct application of Spencer’s theorem or Banaszczyk’s theorem.

First let us construct our planted Gaussian discrepancy solution. For
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
let
U
denote the
n\times 2
matrix whose
j
-th row is
u_{j}=(\cos(\frac{2\pi j}{n}),\sin(\frac{2\pi j}{n}))
, and let
\Sigma=UU^{\mathsf{T}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Sigma=\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathsf{T}}+\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\,,

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{c}_{j}\coloneqq\cos(\frac{2\pi j}{n})
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{j}\coloneqq\sin(\frac{2\pi j}{n})
. Given a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the shift operator
s:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
s(v)=(v_{2},\dotsc,v_{n},v_{1})
and let
w=(\mathbf{1}_{n/2},-{\mathbf{1}_{n/2}})
. The lemma below verifies that the Goemans–Williamson and PCA roundings of
\Sigma
are close to a vector in
\{w,s(w),\ldots,s^{n-1}(w)\}
.

Lemma B.1
.

For
\Sigma
defined as in
(
19
)
and
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}}(\Sigma),\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}(\Sigma)\}
, the following event holds with probability
1
: there exists
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
,
i\neq j\in[n]
, and
a,b\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
such that

s^{k}(w)-\sigma=ae_{i}+be_{j}.

Proof B.2
.

For the Goemans–Williamson rounding, in fact it holds that

\mathbb{P}\big[\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}}(\Sigma)\in\{w,s(w),\ldots,s^{n-1}(w)\}\big]=1.

To see this, observe that
\xi=Ug
where
g\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is distributed as standard normal. Thus
\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}}(\Sigma)_{j}=1
iff
\langle g,u_{j}\rangle>0
. Since the points
u_{j}
are equidistributed on the circle
S^{1}
, the claim follows by rotational invariance of the standard normal.

Let us now turn to the PCA-rounding scheme. Since
\boldsymbol{c}\perp\boldsymbol{s}
, the top eigenspace (corresponding to eigenvalue
n/2
) of
\Sigma
is
\spn\{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}\}
. Without loss of generality suppose that our rounding scheme chooses

v_{1}(\Sigma)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\,\boldsymbol{c}+\frac{b}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\,\boldsymbol{s}

as the top eigenvector of
\Sigma
for some
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
. Let
\theta
be such that

\displaystyle(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\,(a,b)\,.

Then we have

v_{1}(\Sigma)=\Bigl(\cos\bigl(\theta-\frac{2\pi j}{n}\bigr)\Bigr)_{j\in[n]}\,.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}(\Sigma)=\sgn(v_{1}(\Sigma))
. We consider two cases. First suppose that
v_{1}(\Sigma)_{j}=0
for some
j\in[n]
. Then it holds that
\theta\in\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{2\pi}{n}\mathbb{Z}
. Let
j^{*}\in[n]
denote the smallest positive integer such that
\theta+\frac{2\pi j^{*}}{n}\in\frac{\pi}{2}+\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Recalling that
n
is even, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}=\begin{cases}\pm 1&\quad\text{if}~j\in(j^{*},j^{*}+n/2)\\
\mp 1&\quad\text{if}~j\in[n]\backslash[j^{*},j^{*}+n/2],\end{cases}

and
\sigma_{j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
if
j\in\{j^{*},j^{*}+n/2\}
. Here we use the properties of the cosine and sign functions. By inspection, the conclusion of the lemma holds in this case.

If on the other hand,
v_{1}(\Sigma)_{j}\neq 0
for all
j\in[n]
, then in fact we have

\sigma\in\{w,s(w),\ldots,s^{n-1}(w)\},

by the fact that
n
is even and the properties of the cosine and sign functions. The lemma follows.

Next, we construct a family of distributions
\mathfrak{p}_{m,n}
on
m\times n
matrices such that the feasible Gaussian coupling
g\sim\NN(0,\Sigma)
attains zero Gaussian discrepancy. Let
\Sigma^{\perp}=I_{n}-\frac{1}{\lVert\boldsymbol{c}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathsf{T}}-\frac{1}{\lVert\boldsymbol{s}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathsf{T}}
and write
A\sim\mathfrak{p}_{m,n}
for the random
m\times n
matrix with rows

g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\NN(0,\Sigma^{\perp})\,.

The rows of
A
are isotropic Gaussians on the orthogonal complement of
\spn\{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}\}
. Thus in particular
AU=0
almost surely so that
\discv_{2}(A)=0
and
\discG(A)=0
almost surely.

We prove an intermediate lemma that lower bounds the combinatorial discrepancy incurred by
\sigma\in\{w,s(w),\ldots,s^{n-1}(w)\}
.

Lemma B.3
.

If
A\sim\mathfrak{p}_{m,n}
,
then there exists a constant
c>0
such that for
n
sufficiently large and for all
\tau\geq 2
,

\mathbb{P}\big[\min_{0\leq k\leq n-1}\lVert As^{k}(w)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau\big]\leq n\exp\Bigl[-m\exp\bigl(-\frac{c\tau^{2}}{\,n}\bigr)\Bigr]\,.

Proof B.4
.

We record some elementary calculations that will be useful later:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\cos^{2}\bigl(\frac{2\pi j}{n}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sin^{2}\bigl(\frac{2\pi j}{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}\cos\bigl(\frac{2\pi j}{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=0

(21)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}\sin\bigl(\frac{2\pi j}{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}

(22)

The identity in the first line is an elementary fact from complex variables, and the limits above are a consequence of Riemann integration.

Observe that for all
1\leq k\leq n-1
,

\Bigl\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr\rVert_{2}=\Bigl\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}{s^{k}(w)}_{i}u_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr\rVert_{2}\,

by the unitary invariance of the
\ell_{2}
-norm. Therefore, for all
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq k\leq n-1
,

\langle g_{i},s^{k}(w)\rangle\sim\NN(0,w^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{\perp}w)\,.

Next,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lVert\boldsymbol{c}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{c},w\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n/2}\,\Bigl\{2\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}\cos\bigl(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\bigr)\Bigr\}^{2}=o(n)\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lVert\boldsymbol{s}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{s},w\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n/2}\,\Bigl\{2\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}\sin\bigl(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\bigr)\Bigr\}^{2}=\frac{8n}{\pi^{2}}+o(n)\,,

(24)

by (
20
), (
21
), and (
22
). We have that

w^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{\perp}w=\underbrace{\bigl(1-\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\bigr)}_{=:c_{0}>0}\,n+o(n)\,.

Thus the inner product of a row
g_{i}
of
A
with an output of either Goemans–Williamson or PCA rounding of
\Sigma
is a centered Gaussian with variance roughly
c_{0}n
.

Next we lower bound
\lVert A\sigma\rVert_{\infty}
for
\sigma\in\{w,s(w),\ldots,s^{n-1}(w)\}
using the first moment method.
Define the random variable

S=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\one\{\lVert As^{k}(w)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau\}

that counts the number of shifts of
w
attaining discrepancy
\tau
. The Markov inequality implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\exists k:\lVert As^{k}(w)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau]=\mathbb{P}[S\geq 1]\leq\E S.

(25)

By our work above, all of the vectors
As^{k}(w)
have the same distribution and have independent coordinates. Using this fact and standard Gaussian estimates, for
\tau\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\E S

\displaystyle=n\,\mathbb{P}[\lVert Aw\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau]=n\,{\mathbb{P}_{g\sim\NN(0,w^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{\perp}w)}[\lvert g\rvert\leq\tau]}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq n\,\Bigl\{1-\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\,\langle w,\Sigma^{\perp}w\rangle}\Bigr)\Bigr\}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\Bigl[-m\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\,(c_{0}+o(1))\,n}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,.

(26)

The lemma statement follows from (
25
) and the previous display.

Lemma
B.3
allows us to demonstrate the poor performance of the rounding schemes considered in this section in two well-studied settings.

Proposition B.5
.

Let
A\sim\mathfrak{p}_{m,n}
and define
A^{\prime}=\frac{1}{c\sqrt{\log n}}\,A
for a sufficiently large absolute constant
c>0
. Let
\Sigma
be defined as in (
19
), and let
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}},\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}\}
. Then

\discG(A^{\prime})=0=\E\lVert A^{\prime}g\rVert_{\infty}\quad\text{if}\,\,g\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)\,,

and the following hold with high probability as
n\to\infty
:

•

(Spencer setting)
If
m=\frac{n}{\log n}
, then
\max_{i\in[m],\;j\in[n]}{\lvert A^{\prime}_{i,j}\rvert}\leq 1
, and

\disc(A^{\prime})\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log n}}\ll\sqrt{n}\lesssim\lVert A^{\prime}\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\,.

•

(Komlós setting)
If
m=10\log n
, then
\max_{j\in[n]}{\lVert A^{\prime}_{:,j}\rVert_{2}}\leq 1
, and

\disc(A^{\prime})\lesssim\sqrt{\log\log n}\ll\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log n}}\lesssim\lVert A^{\prime}\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\,.

Proof B.6
.

Recall that
\E\lVert A^{\prime}g\rVert_{\infty}=0
holds for
g\sim\normal(0,\Sigma)
by construction.

Next we verify the high probability bounds on the size of the entries of
A^{\prime}
. The bound on the size of the largest entry of
A^{\prime}
in the Spencer setting follows from the union bound and tail bounds for the standard Gaussian distribution. Now, for the Komlós setting, since
\Sigma^{\perp}\preceq I_{n}
, it follows that
\Var(A_{i,j})\leq 1
for all
i,j
. Let
\tau_{j}\leq 1
denote the common variance of the entries in column
j
. We have that
\lVert A_{:,j}\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{\tau_{j}m}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
1

(see e.g.
Vershynin 2018
, Chapter 3)
. Applying the sub-Gaussian tail bound and the union bound over
1\leq j\leq n
implies that
\max_{j\in[n]}{\lVert A_{:,j}\rVert_{2}}\lesssim\sqrt{\log n}
, as desired.

Given the previous bounds on the entries of
A^{\prime}
, the upper bounds on
\disc(A^{\prime})
follow immediately from Spencer’s theorem and Banaszczyk’s bound, respectively. To finish, we verify the lower bounds on
\lVert A^{\prime}\sigma\rVert_{\infty}
when
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}},\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}\}
. By Lemma
B.1
, it holds with probability
1
that for
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}},\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}\}
,

s^{k}(w)-\sigma=ae_{i}+be_{j}

for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\},i\neq j\in[n],
and
|a|,|b|\leq 2
. By the union bound over this event and the event that
\max_{i,j}|A^{\prime}_{i,j}|\leq 1
, it holds with high probability that

\displaystyle\|A^{\prime}(\sigma-s^{k}(w))\|_{\infty}\leq 4,

(27)

for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, by the triangle inequality. By the union bound again over the event (
27
) and the event of Lemma
B.3
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|A^{\prime}\sigma\|_{\infty}\geq\min_{0\leq k\leq n-1}\|A^{\prime}s^{k}(w)\|_{\infty}-4\gtrsim\begin{cases}\sqrt{n}\quad&\text{if }m=\frac{n}{\log n}\\
\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log n}}\quad&\text{if }m=10\log n\\
\end{cases}.

Precisely, for
m=\frac{n}{\log n}
, we set
\tau=c^{\prime}\sqrt{n\log n}
in Lemma
B.3
, and for
m=10\log n
, we set
\tau=c^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{n}
, for sufficiently small absolute constants
c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}>0
.

Furthermore, when
m=\frac{n}{\log n}
, a signing chosen uniformly at random already achieves discrepancy
O(\sqrt{n})
for the matrix
A^{\prime}
with high probability. This indicates that in the Spencer setting our rounding schemes
\sigma_{\mathsf{GW}}
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{PCA}}
fail to ‘beat the union bound’. We justify this below for completeness.

Lemma B.7
.

With high probability over the random matrix
A\sim\mathfrak{p}_{m,n}
, a uniformly random sign
\sigma\sim\operatorname{unif}(\{\pm 1\}^{n})
satisfies

\E[\lVert A^{\prime}\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\mid A^{\prime}]\lesssim\sqrt{n}\,,

where
A^{\prime}=\frac{1}{c\sqrt{\log n}}A
as before.

Proof B.8
.

By standard concentration results
(see, e.g.,
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Theorem 5.5)
and using the fact that
\Var(A_{i,j})\leq 1
for all
i,j
, it holds for fixed
i
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\lVert A_{i,:}\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{n}\geq t]\leq\exp(-t^{2}/2)\,.

Thus the union bound implies that the event
E=\{\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{\lVert A_{i,:}\rVert_{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{n}\}
holds with probability at least
1-me^{-n/2}
. Conditionally on the matrix
A
, the random variable
\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{i,j}\sigma_{j}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\lVert A_{i,:}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, so the maximal inequality for sub-Gaussian random variables
(see, e.g.,
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Theorem 2.5)
yields

\displaystyle\E[\lVert A\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\mid A]

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{\lVert A_{i,:}\rVert_{2}}\bigr)\,\sqrt{2\log(2m)}\leq 2\sqrt{2n\log\frac{2n}{\log n}}=O(\sqrt{n\log n})

with probability at least
1-me^{-n/2}
, as desired.

Appendix C
Sampling from the Markov chain

To implement the Gaussian fixed point walk in dimension
r>1
, we need to be able to sample from the Markov kernel (
17
). Clearly, to this end it is sufficient to solve the following problem.

Problem C.1
.

Given a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and a target radius
s\geq 0
, output a uniformly random point from

S\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\,:\,\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}=1,\,\lVert y\rVert_{2}=s\}\,,

(28)

provided
S\neq\varnothing
.

Let
\delta=y-x
. Equivalently, we need to sample from the set of
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert x+\delta\rVert_{2}=s
. Expanding and rearranging the latter, we obtain

\bigl\langle\frac{x}{\lVert x\rVert_{2}},\delta\bigr\rangle=\frac{s^{2}-\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}-1}{2\,\lVert x\rVert_{2}}\,.

The set of such
\delta
can be parametrized as
\delta=\lambda x/\lVert x\rVert_{2}+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\,w
where
w
is a unit vector orthogonal to
x
, and
\lambda=(s^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}_{2}-1)/(2\lVert x\rVert_{2})
. Uniformly sampling such a
w
is easy: given a standard Gaussian
g\sim\normal(0,I_{r})
, project it onto
x^{\perp}
and normalize:

w=\frac{(I_{r}-xx^{\mathsf{T}}/\lVert x\rVert_{2})\,g}{\lVert(I_{r}-xx^{\mathsf{T}}/\lVert x\rVert_{2})\,g\rVert_{2}}.

(29)

Putting it all together, we obtain the following algorithm.

{algorithm}

[H]

Sampling from the Markov kernel (
17
)

Input:
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
s\geq 0
.

Set
\lambda=(s^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}_{2}-1)/(2\lVert x\rVert_{2})
.

\If
\lambda\notin[0,1]

Output:
S
is empty.

\Else
Draw
w
according to (
29
).

Set
\delta=\lambda x/\lVert x\rVert_{2}+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\,w
.

Output:
x+\delta
.
