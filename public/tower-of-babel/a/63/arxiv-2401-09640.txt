Blackout Mitigation via Physics-guided RL
Anmol Dwivedi, Santiago Paternain, Ali Tajer

Abstract
This paper considers the sequential design of remedial control actions in response to system anomalies for the ultimate objective of preventing blackouts. A physics-guided reinforcement learning (RL) framework is designed to identify effective sequences of real-time remedial look-ahead decisions accounting for the long-term impact on the system’s stability. The paper considers a space of control actions that involve both discrete-valued transmission line-switching decisions (line reconnections and removals) and continuous-valued generator adjustments. To identify an effective blackout mitigation policy, a physics-guided approach is designed that uses power-flow sensitivity factors associated with the power transmission network to guide the RL exploration during agent training. Comprehensive empirical evaluations using the open-source Grid2Op platform demonstrate the notable advantages of incorporating physical signals into RL decisions, establishing the gains of the proposed physics-guided approach compared to its black-box counterparts. One important observation is that strategically removing transmission lines, in conjunction with multiple real-time generator adjustments, often renders effective long-term decisions that are likely to prevent or delay blackouts.

Introduction

Widespread power system failures are partly due to congestion led by excessive electricity load demands.
Increased and sustained congestion gradually stresses the system over time, rendering anomalies (e.g., failures) that can potentially escalate
[
1
]
if left unchecked. In such circumstances, system operators take real-time remedial actions to re-stabilize the system. Widely-used examples of such actions include adjusting generation
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
and altering network’s topology
[
6
,

,
8
,

. Choosing these actions involves balancing two opposing action impacts. On the one hand, an action can ensure safeguards for specific system components (e.g., adjusting flow in a line to prevent overflow). Such actions have
quick
impacts but target only specific components’ stability without regard for their inadvertent consequences. On the other hand, an action can ensure overall network stability. Such
look-ahead
actions have delayed impact but ensure higher robustness. Therefore, to balance these opposing impacts, forming
quick
remedial control decisions with a
look-ahead
approach is crucial for ensuring a secure and reliable power system operation.

Reinforcement Learning Framework:
In this paper, we formalize a
physics-guided
reinforcement learning (RL) framework to form real-time decisions to alleviate transmission line overloads over long horizons. We focus on both
discrete
-valued transmission line-switching decisions (i.e., line reconnections and removals) and
continuous
-valued generator adjustments as the control decisions of interest. In this framework, finding an optimal sequence of decisions faces three key challenges. First, the cardinality of the decision space grows exponentially with the number of components and control horizon. Second, the decisions are temporally dependent, as system operational constraints limit the frequency of component interventions to maintain system security. Finally, we have a hybrid of continuous and discrete actions, quantifying the impacts of which have distinct natures.

RL Methodology:

Our RL approach is guided by
two power-flow sensitivity metrics: the line outage distribution factor and the power transfer distribution factor
[
10
]
. Such physics-guided decisions facilitate more structured exploration during training RL agents and effective exploration of diverse topological configurations and generation adjustment decisions.
Our extensive evaluations on Grid2Op
[
11
]
– a platform specialized for implementing RL in power transmission systems – confirm that
physics-guided RL decisions significantly outperform the counterpart black-box RL exploration strategies.
Before specifying our approach, we review the existing literature relevant to this paper’s scope. Specifically, there exist two overarching approaches for devising real-time remedial decisions: model-based and data-driven, which we review next.

Model-based Approaches:
Model-based techniques such as model predictive control (MPC) focus on analytically approximating the system model and formulating a multi-horizon optimization objective to predict future states and devise control decisions. For instance, MPC approaches to optimally coordinate load shedding, capacitor switching, and transformer tap changes for long-term voltage stability are studied in
[
12
,
13
,
14

, and the studies in
[

4

alleviate line overloads by optimally coordinating generation adjustment and energy storage schedules. While model-based approaches offer increased control accuracy by ensuring adherence to system constraints, they require access to an accurate system model, which can be difficult for highly complex systems. Furthermore, characterizing optimal policies becomes particularly challenging when coordinating decisions of different natures, such as discrete line-switching and continuous generation adjustments. Such hybrid decision spaces pose analytical challenges to optimizing decisions within a unified optimization objective.

Data-driven Approaches:
In another direction, there are data-driven techniques, such as deep RL. In these approaches, the objective is to learn decision policies directly via sequentially interacting with the system model.
For example, model-free RL algorithms have been widely employed to efficiently address various challenges in power systems, including system stability control

Deep RL Approaches:
There exist several deep RL approaches designed for
transmission line overload management. These approaches can be categorized based on the nature of their decisions. For instance, the study in
[
23
]
focuses on a subset of bus-split topological actions and proposes a guided RL exploration training approach that selects control actions guided by the highest

[
27
]
pre-selects
effective
topological actions via extensive power-grid simulations and subsequently employs policy gradient-based RL algorithms using the pre-selected action space to find a decision policy. The study in
[
28

proposes a curriculum-based approach for training RL agents, emphasizing the significance of integrating domain knowledge into RL frameworks. Building upon this work,
[

Our contributions:
The common aspect of all the existing RL approaches discussed earlier is that (i) they focus entirely on bus-splitting actions, and (ii) they do not incorporate any physical power system signals to guide exploration decisions. The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

•

Physics-guided:
Our approach is physics-guided and leverages
sensitivity factors
to structure exploration during training. The experiments establish the gains of explicitly integrating network topology information into RL. This also alludes to the potential for broader applicability to other topological control actions, such as bus-splitting.

•

Active line-switching:
Active line switching and particularly line
removal
actions, have been unexploited in designing action spaces. This is perhaps primarily due to concerns that line removal may decrease power transfer capabilities, thereby increasing the risk of cascades. However, we demonstrate that while line removals may momentarily decrease transfer capabilities,
strategically
removing lines over a planned horizon can prevent or delay cascades, proving to be an effective remedial action.

•

Hybrid action space:
Our framework
jointly
optimizes continuous generator dispatch and discrete line switching within a single RL agent. This integration, despite its modeling and optimization challenges due to mixed action spaces, demonstrably improves power grid resilience by drastically increasing the system’s survival time. We note that adopting a hybrid action space is also investigated in
[
31
]
. Nevertheless, its distinction is that it decouples the decisions, designing the discrete and continuous (generation adjustment) independently.

2
Problem Formulation

2.1
System Model

Consider a power transmission system consisting of
N
buses indexed by
[N]\triangleq\{1,\dots,N\}
and
L
transmission lines indexed by
[L]\triangleq\{1,\dots,L\}
.
We denote the power and current flows in line
\ell\in[L]
at the discrete-time instant
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
by
F_{\ell}[n]
and
A_{\ell}[n]
, respectively. Accordingly, we define

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[F_{1}[n],\dots,F_{L}[n]]^{\top},\ \mathbf{A}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[A_{1}[n],\dots,A_{L}[n]]^{\top}\ .

Each line
\ell\in[L]
is constrained with maximum power and current flows, denoted by
F_{\ell}^{\sf max}
and
A_{\ell}^{\sf max}
, respectively.
The transmission system has
G
dispatchable generators serving
D
loads. The generators output are injected in buses
\mathcal{G}\subseteq[N]
indexed by
\mathcal{G}\triangleq\{g_{j}:j\in[G]\}
and the loads draw power from buses
\mathcal{D}\subseteq[N]
indexed by
\mathcal{D}\triangleq\{d_{k}:k\in[D]\}
. We denote the real-power injected by generator
g_{j}\in[G]
by
G_{j}[n]
(in MWs) and the real-power demand by load
d_{k}\in[D]
(in MWs) by
D_{k}[n]
. For all other buses
i\in[N]
not connected to a generator or a load component we have
G_{i}[n]=0
and
D_{i}[n]=0
. Accordingly, we define generator and load vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[G_{1}[n],\dots,G_{N}[n]]^{\top},\ \mathbf{D}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[D_{1}[n],\dots,D_{N}[n]]^{\top}.

We refer to the net-power injection in bus
i\in[N]
by
\mathbf{P}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathbf{G}[n]-\mathbf{D}[n]
. We assume that each line
\ell\in[L]
is specified by
F
features (e.g., flows and status) and each bus
i\in[N]
is specified by
H
features (e.g., demand and generation). For compact representation, we concatenate all these features and represent them by a system state vector, which at time
n
is denoted by
\mathbf{X}[n]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\cdot N+F\cdot H}
. Finally, we define the
operational
status of each line
\ell\in[L]
as a binary variable
\Omega_{\ell}[n]
such that
\Omega_{\ell}[n]=1
if line
\ell
is operational at time
n
, and otherwise
\Omega_{\ell}[n]=0
.
We denote the set of lines operational at
n
by
\mathcal{L}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\ell\in[L]\;:\;\Omega_{\ell}[n]=1\}
.

2.2
Blackout Mitigation Model

Transmission systems are constantly prone to being stressed by adverse internal (e.g., line failures) and external (e.g., excessive demand) conditions. Without proper remedial actions, sustained stress can lead to failures, further compounding the stress and resulting in more failures. Prolonged periods of such cascading failures can lead to circumstances in which the system cannot meet the load demand. We refer to such circumstances as
blackouts
. Minimizing the likelihood of such blackouts necessitates devising actions that can swiftly react to emerging system stress to confine its impact.

To mitigate the adverse consequences of cascading failures in the network, our objective is to maximize the system’s survival time (ST). The ST of the system is defined as the first instance at which a system blackout occurs
[
11
,
23
,
25
]
.
Maximizing the ST is facilitated by the following two key sets of actions that aim to reduce stress via controlling line flows.

2.2.1
Line-Switching Actions

Line-switching decisions pertain to removing or reconnecting lines to alleviate overloads. For each line
\ell
at a given time
n
, we define the binary decision variable
W_{\ell}[n]\in\{0,1\}
such that
W_{\ell}[n]=0
indicates removing line
\ell
while
W_{\ell}[n]=1
indicates reconnecting it. Accordingly, we define the decision vector at time
n
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{\sf line}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[W_{1}[n],\dots,W_{L}(n)]^{\top}\ .

(1)

Such line-switching decisions have operational constraints for implementing them. Specifically, we consider two constraints for line failures and line-switching decisions. First, once we apply a line-switching decision on a line, a mandated downtime period
\tau_{\rm D}
must elapse before this line can be again considered for a line-switching decision. Secondly, when a line fails naturally (e.g., due to severe overloads), it can be reconnected only after a mandated downtime period of
\tau_{\sf F}
, where
\tau_{\rm F}\gg\tau_{\rm D}
. Furthermore, different lines have different line-switching costs. We define
c^{\sf line}_{\ell}
as the cost of line-switching for line
\ell
. Hence, the system-wide cost of line-switching over a horizon
T
is

C_{\sf line}(T)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c^{\sf line}_{\ell}\cdot W_{\ell}[n]\ .

(2)

2.2.2
Generator Adjustments

Alongside line-switching, real-time generator injection adjustments are also instrumental in alleviating overloads. We denote the incremental adjustment to the injection of generator
g_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
at time
n
by
\Delta G_{j}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}G_{j}[n+1]-G_{j}[n]

and accordingly, define

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{G}_{\sf gen}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[\Delta G_{1}[n],\dots,\Delta G_{N}[n]]^{\top}\ .

(3)

Such adjustments are constrained by generation ramping constraints, enforcing a maximum adjustment level on each generator. We denote the maximum ramp-rate associated with generator
g_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
by
\Delta G_{j}^{\sf max}
. Finally, we consider a linear generation cost such that the per-unit cost of adjusting the generation
g_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
is
c^{\sf gen}_{j}
(in
\$
/MW).

Therefore, the cumulative system-wide cost incurred by generation adjustments is

C_{\sf gen}(T)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{G}c^{\sf gen}_{j}\cdot\Delta G_{j}[n]\ .

(4)

2.3
Maximizing Survival Time

Our objective is to constantly monitor the system and, when there is evidence of mounting stress (e.g., imminent overflows), initiate a series of flow control decisions (line switching and generation adjustment) that maximizes the survival of the system over a horizon of at least
T
.
Such decisions are highly constrained with (i) the decision costs
C_{\rm line}(T)
and
C_{\rm gen}(T)
, and (ii) system operational constraints of generation ramping and line-switching downtime periods
\tau_{\rm N}
and
\tau_{\rm F}
.

To quantify the system’s survival time over a horizon
T
, denoted by ST
(T)
, we use a proxy that quantifies and tracks the risk margins of the transmission lines. Minimizing such risk margins minimizes the possibilities of line overloads, a key factor that contributes to maximizing ST
(T)
.
Specifically, for each line
\ell\in[L]
at time
n
we define the
risk margin
as

\displaystyle\rho_{\ell}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\frac{A_{\ell}[n]}{A_{\ell}^{\sf max}}\ ,

(5)

based on which the transmission line
\ell
is
overloaded
when
\rho_{\ell}[n]\geq 1
. Accordingly, we define the risk margin vector
\boldsymbol{\rho}[n]\triangleq[\rho_{1}[n],\dots,\rho_{L}[n]]^{\top}
. Assessing the ST
(T)
based on these risk margins is motivated by the fact that the system’s susceptibility to disruptions stemming from various conditions may result in line failures. Following a failure, the line risk margins
\rho_{\ell}[n]
associated with the healthy lines can be potentially compromised, rendering additional overloads within the system and raising the possibility of a cascading effect. Hence, minimizing the aggregate risk margins of all lines over a horizon decreases the risk of overloading healthy lines, which in turn increases the likelihood of a higher ST.

We use risk margins to determine when the system is in a
critical
state, i.e., has reached a level that requires remedial interventions. This is specified by the rule

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[L]}\quad\rho_{i}[n]\geq\eta\ .

(6)

When the condition in (
6
) is satisfied, subsequently, our objective is to sequentially form the decisions
\bar{\mathbf{W}}_{\rm line}\triangleq\{\mathbf{W}_{\sf line}[n]:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
and
{\Delta\bar{\mathbf{G}}_{\rm gen}}\triangleq\{\Delta\mathbf{G}_{\sf gen}[n]:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
such that they maximize the survival time of the system subject to the controlled decision costs and specified operational constraints. These decisions can be obtained by solving

\mathcal{P}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min_{\{\bar{\mathbf{W}}_{\rm line},{\Delta\bar{\mathbf{G}}_{\rm gen}\}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{T}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\rho_{\ell}[n]\\
{\rm s.t.}&C_{\rm gen}(T)\leq\beta_{\rm gen}\\
&C_{\rm line}(T)\leq\beta_{\rm line}\\
&\mbox{\footnotesize Operational constraints in}~\ref{sec: Line-Switch Decisions},~\ref{sec: Generator Adjustment Decisions}\end{array}\right.\ .

(7)

In (
7
),
\beta_{\rm gen}
controls the total allowable cost for interventions on generator outputs and while
\beta_{\rm line}
restricts the total cost associated with line-switching operations. These ensure that the actions are economically viable within the limits set by the system operator. Furthermore, the operational constraints in Section
2.2.1
ensure that the decisions to switch transmission lines are compatible with maintaining system security, and the constraints in Section
2.2.2
mandate that generation adjustments
\Delta\mathbf{G}_{\sf gen}[n]
adhere to predefined ramp rates.

While solving
\mathcal{P}
dynamically over time, whenever the system’s risk is deemed small enough that remedial actions are not necessary, we terminate our line-switching and generation adjustment decisions, and the system returns to its normal operation. To formalize this, we use the rule
\max_{i\in[L]}\quad\rho_{i}[n]\leq\nu

to determine when to terminate the remedial actions. To ensure timely remedial control actions, it is common to set
\eta\in[0.9,1.0]
and
\nu\in[0,0.9]
. We note that solving
\mathcal{P}
assumes the fulfillment of demand
\mathbf{D}[n]
at all times
n
without resorting to load-shedding as a possible control action. This is a deliberate choice that emphasizes the entire focus on the optimal design of other actions (line-switching and generation adjustment) while ensuring that the system demand is met.

3
Blackout Mitigation as an MDP

Sequentially finding the decisions
that maximize the ST faces two broad types of challenges.

1. Temporally Dependent Decisions:
The first pertains to quantifying the impact of the current decision on future decisions. For instance, at time
n=1
, the system receives the demand
\mathbf{D}[1]
and itcan decide about control actions
\mathbf{W}_{\sf line}[1]
or
\Delta\mathbf{G}_{\sf gen}[1]
, based on their anticipated impact on maximizing ST. Forming these decisions
faces two potentially opposing impacts. The first pertains to its
immediate
adjustments of risk margins
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\rho_{\ell}[2]
in the context of meeting demand
\mathbf{D}[2]
, while the second considers the lasting influence on all
future
risk margins in accommodating upcoming demands
\{\mathbf{D}[n]\}_{n=3}^{T}
. For instance, a line-switching decision affects the future availability of the lines for switching, which are constrained by downtime operational constraints
\tau_{\rm F}
and
\tau_{\rm D}
.

2. Computational Complexity:
The complexity of identifying optimal line-switching (discrete) and generation adjustments (continuous) decisions grows exponentially with the number of lines
L
, the number of generators
G
, and the target horizon
T
. This complexity is further compounded by the need to adhere to operational constraints at all times. To address the challenges of solving (
7
), we design a
sequential
, agent-based, and physics-guided RL algorithm.

3.1
Modeling Failure Mitigation as an MDP

At any instance
n
, we have access to the system’s states
\{\mathbf{X}[m]:m\in[n]\}
, based on which we determine the line-switching and generation adjustment decisions. Implementing these remedial decisions results in partly deterministic outcomes – reflecting the influence of the implemented decision on the system state – and partly stochastic, representing changes arising from the randomness associated with unknown future demands. Hence, we employ a Markov decision process (MDP) to effectively model the stochastic interactions between the system’s states and our remedial control actions over time. Such a model is formally characterized by the MDP tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},{\mathbb{P}},\mathcal{R},\gamma)
, specified next.

3.1.1
MDP State Space
\mathcal{S}

Based on the system’s state
\mathbf{X}[n]
, which captures the line and bus features, we denote the MDP state at time
n
by
\mathbf{S}[n]
, which is defined as a moving window of the states of length
\kappa
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\left[\mathbf{X}[n-(\kappa-1)],\dots,\mathbf{X}[n]\right]^{\top}\ ,

(8)

where the state space is
\mathcal{S}={\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa\cdot(L\cdot N+F\cdot H)}
. Leveraging the temporal correlation of demands, decisions based on the MDP state
\mathbf{S}[n]
help predict future load demands.

3.1.2
Action Space
\mathcal{A}

We denote the action space by
\mathcal{A}\triangleq\mathcal{A}_{\sf line}\bigcup\mathcal{A}_{\sf gen}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{\sf line}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\sf gen}
are the spaces of line-switching and generation adjustment actions, respectively.

Action space
\mathcal{A}_{\sf line}
includes two actions for each line
\ell\in[L]
associated with
reconnecting
and
removing
it. Besides these
2L
actions, we also include a
do-nothing
action to accommodate the instances at which (i) the mandated downtime period
\tau_{\rm D}
makes all line-switch actions operationally infeasible; or (ii) the system’s risk is sufficiently low. This allows the model to determine the MDP state at time
n+1
solely based on the system dynamics driven by changes in load demand
\mathbf{D}[n+1]
.
In action space
\mathcal{A}_{\sf gen}
, incremental generator adjustments
\Delta G_{j}[n]

are
continuous
. To harmonize these actions with the discrete set
\mathcal{A}_{\sf line}
, we discretize generator adjustment decisions
\Delta\mathbf{G}_{\rm gen}[n]
in (
3
) for each
g_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
when solving
\mathcal{P}
in (
7
). To ensure that the discretized outputs satisfy ramping constraints
\Delta G_{j}^{\rm max}
, we select

\Delta\mathbf{G}_{\rm gen}[n]
such that

\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{G}_{j}\in\{-\delta,\;0,\;+\delta\}\ ,\;\;\;\delta=\min\{\Delta G_{j}^{\rm max}:g_{j}\in\mathcal{G}\}\ .

(9)

Choosing
\delta
larger than the choice in (
9
) excludes the generators with ramping constraints smaller than
\delta
from actively participating in the generator decisions. In contrast, choosing a smaller value for
\delta
excludes larger adjustments that satisfy the ramping constraints and can expedite directing the flows in the desired trajectories.
Furthermore, to ensure that generation adjustments are operationally viable at all times
n
, we design each action
a[n]\in\mathcal{A}_{\sf gen}
as a
feasible
combination involving adjustments to the injections of
multiple
generators.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{G}\;\Delta\tilde{G}_{j}[n]=0\ ,\;{\rm where}\;\;\Delta\tilde{G}_{j}[n]\in\{-\delta,\;0,\;+\delta\}\ .

(10)

3.1.3
Stochastic Transition Dynamics
{\mathbb{P}}

After an action
a[n]\in\mathcal{A}
is taken at time
n
, the MDP state
\mathbf{S}[n]
transitions to the next state
\mathbf{S}[n+1]
according to an unknown transition probability kernel
{\mathbb{P}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}[n+1]\sim{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{S}\;|\;\mathbf{S}[n],a[n])\ ,

(11)

where
{\mathbb{P}}
captures the system dynamics influenced by both the random future load demand and the implemented action
a[n]
.

3.1.4
Reward Dynamics
\mathcal{R}

To capture the immediate effectiveness of taking an action
a[n]\in\mathcal{A}
in any given MDP state
\mathbf{S}[n]
, we define an instant reward function

\displaystyle r[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(1-\rho_{\ell}^{2}[n]\right)-\mu_{\sf gen}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{G}c^{\sf gen}_{j}\cdot\Delta\tilde{G}_{j}[n]\right)-\mu_{\sf line}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c^{\sf line}_{\ell}\cdot W_{\ell}[n]\right)\ ,

(12)

which is the decision reward associated with transitioning from MDP state
\mathbf{S}[n]
to
\mathbf{S}[n+1]
, where the constants
\mu_{\sf gen}
and
\mu_{\sf line}
are associated with the cost constraints
\beta_{\rm gen}
and
\beta_{\rm line}
introduced in (
7
), respectively. The inclusion of parameters
(\mu_{\sf gen},\mu_{\sf line})
allows us to flexibly model different cost constraints, reflecting diverse economic considerations in power systems. Greater values for the parameters
\mu_{\sf gen}
and
\mu_{\sf line}
in (
12
) promote solutions that satisfy stricter cost requirements. To avoid tuning these parameters manually one can resort to primal-dual methods
[
32
]
, where the parameters
(\mu_{\sf gen},\mu_{\sf line})
can be adapted based on the extent of constraint violation.

For any decision-making policy
\pi
, the aggregate reward when the system is initiated at state
\mathbf{S}[1]=\mathbf{S}
can be characterized by the state-action value function, given by

\displaystyle Q_{\pi}(\mathbf{S},a)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{T}\gamma^{n}r[n]\;\Big|\;\mathbf{S}[1]=\mathbf{S},a[1]=a,a[n]_{n\geq 2}=\pi(\mathbf{S}[n])\right]\ ,

(13)

where
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is the discount factor that decides how much future rewards are favored over instant rewards.
Hence, finding an
optimal
decision policy
\pi^{*}
can be found by solving
[
33
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}:\quad\pi^{*}(\mathbf{S})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\argmax_{\pi}\;Q_{\pi}(\mathbf{S},\pi(\mathbf{S}))\ .

(14)

4
Physics-guided RL Solution

4.1
Motivation

Determining an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
requires knowing the transition dynamics, captured by
{\mathbb{P}}
, for the high-dimensional MDP state space
\mathbf{S}\in\mathcal{S}
.
When an accurate system dynamics model is available, model-based approaches (e.g., dynamic programming) can be effective for determining an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
. However, obtaining accurate models can be computationally prohibitive due to extensive offline simulator interactions, especially as the system size scales up. In such cases,
model-free
RL is effective in finding an optimal policy without explicitly learning the system model. A crucial aspect in the design of such algorithms is a comprehensive exploration of the MDP state space
\mathcal{S}
to learn the expected decision utilities, e.g., to obtain accurate
Q
-value estimates.

For addressing these,
Q
-learning with function approximation
[
34
]
serves as an effective value-based
model-free off-policy
RL approach

[
35
,
36
]
. Such methods entail dynamically updating a behavior policy
\pi
, informed by a separate exploratory policy such as
\epsilon
-greedy
[
36
]
.
While
Q
-learning algorithms with random
\epsilon
-greedy exploration are effective in many domains
[
34
]
, their application to power systems encounters a significant limitation. Specifically, without properly selecting exploration actions, a generic exploration policy often inadvertently selects actions that quickly lead to severe overloads and, consequently, blackouts. This results in the agent interacting with the MDP for significantly fewer time steps, specifically instances when
\max_{i\in[L]}\rho_{i}[n]\geq\eta
, as the exploration process terminates upon reaching such critical MDP states, preempting a comprehensive exploration of the state space
\mathcal{S}
(more details in Section
5.3
(a)). Consequently, this limitation adversely affects the accuracy of
Q

4.2
Action Mapping via Sensitivity Factors

Random exploratory actions,
particularly
those involving the network topology
\mathcal{A}_{\sf line}
over long horizons
T
, lead to
rapid
system-wide failures
[
25
,
23
]
. This is because topological actions force an abrupt change in the system state by redistributing flows after a network topological change, compromising risk margins
\rho_{\ell}
and exposing the system to potential cascading failures. This prevents the agent from exploring a diverse set of MDP states that are significantly different from the initial state
\mathbf{S}[1]
, in turn, limiting action diversity, and subsequently, adversely impacting the learned policy
\pi
. To address this, we leverage power-flow
sensitivity factors

[
10

to guide the selection of the exploration decisions.

Sensitivity factors facilitate expressing the mapping between MDP states
\mathcal{S}
and actions
\mathcal{A}
derived by linearizing the system about the current operating point. Their primary function is to recursively approximate line flows based on power injection fluctuations.
This is facilitated through linearizing power-flow equations
to effectively assess the impact of an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
on all line flows
F_{\ell}
and, consequently, the reward
r\in\mathcal{R}
. Two distinct types of sensitivity factors can be characterized based on action
a\in\mathcal{A}
under consideration.

DQN Architecture

Our DQN architecture consists of a fully-connected NN, parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, that takes as input a critical MDP state
\mathbf{S}[n]
and
predicts
two key quantities of interest: (i) a state-value function
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n])
and, (ii) an advantage function
A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n],a[n])
that quantifies the advantage of taking action
a[n]
in MDP state
\mathbf{S}[n]
.
Q
-values (
13
) for each action
a[n]\in\mathcal{A}
are then predicted via

\displaystyle Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n],a[n])\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n],a[n])+V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n])\ .

(18)

This
dueling
NN architecture
[
24
]
takes
\mathbf{S}[n]
as it’s input, denoted by DQN
{}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n])
, and outputs an advantage vector
\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n],\cdot)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
along with a state-value function
V_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n])
implicitly predicting
\mathbf{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[n],\cdot)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
via (
18
).

Experience Replay

To enhance the learning efficiency of
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
via DQN
θ
, we employ a
prioritized
experience buffer
[
38
]
that stores past experience according to a
priority
encountered during the agent’s interaction with the power system. The past experience is captured by the tuples
(\mathbf{S}[n],a[n],r[n],\mathbf{S}[n+1],{\sf end}(\mathbf{S}[n+1]))
, where
{\sf end}(\mathbf{S})
is a boolean value such that when it is true, it indicates that MDP state
\mathbf{S}
is the terminal state. The prioritization of past experience tuples, based on the magnitude of temporal-difference errors, when sampling mini-batches for DQN
θ
training contributes to more effective learning of behavior strategies
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
influenced by
\boldsymbol{\theta}

[
38
]
.

Training the DQN
θ

To ensure training stability, we employ the standard approach of splitting the task of predicting and evaluating
Q
-values via two separate NNs
[
34
]
: a behavior DQN
θ
and a target DQN
{}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}}
parameterized by a distinct set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}
, respectively. Under critical states
\mathbf{S}[n]
, the agent samples

(\mathbf{S}[i],a[i],r[i],\mathbf{S}[i+1],{\sf end}(\mathbf{S}[i+1]))

{}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}}
is

to predict

-values (
18
). Subsequently,

-values are employed to compute a

\displaystyle t[i]=r[i]+\gamma\cdot(1-{\sf end}(\mathbf{S}[i+1]))\cdot\max_{a}Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}}(\mathbf{S}[i+1],a)\ ,

(19)

which is further used to update the behavior DQN
θ

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}-\alpha_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[\;t[i]-Q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S}[i],a[i])\;\right]^{2}\ ,

(20)

where
\alpha_{n}
denotes the learning rate and

denotes the current training iteration. We use the Adam optimizer
[
39
]
to perform the gradient update (
20

\alpha_{n}
, and update the target DQN
{}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}}

by following a
soft

5.2
Evaluation Criteria

Performance Metrics:
A key performance metric is the agent’s survival time ST
(T)
, averaged across all test set scenarios for
T=8062
. We explore factors influencing ST through analyzing action diversity and track
unique
control actions per scenario. Furthermore, we quantify the fraction of times each of the following three possible actions are taken: “do-nothing,” “line-switch
\mathcal{A}_{\sf line}
,” and “generator adjustment
\mathcal{A}_{\sf gen}
”. Since the agent takes remedial actions only under critical states associated with critical time instances
n
, we report action decision fractions that exclusively stem from these critical states, corresponding to instances when
\rho_{\ell_{\sf max}}[n]\geq\eta
. We also note that monthly load demand variations
\mathbf{D}[n]
influence how frequently different MDP states
\mathbf{S}[n]
are visited. This results in varying control actions per scenario. To form an overall insight, we report the
average
percentage of actions chosen across all test scenarios. Note that the reward function
r[n]
in (
12
) is characterized by its dependence on parameters
(\mu_{\sf gen},\mu_{\sf line})
, designed to accommodate control action costs
\beta_{\sf line}
and
\beta_{\sf gen}
associated with line-switch and generator adjustments, respectively. Consequently, the choice of
(\mu_{\sf gen},\mu_{\sf line})
directly influences performance metrics. To illustrate the benefits of physics-guided exploration, we showcase how performance metrics vary as a function of
(\mu_{\sf gen},\mu_{\sf line})
, providing a comprehensive understanding of the agent’s adaptive decision-making for different cost scenarios.

Baseline Agents:
For the chosen performance metrics, we consider five alternative baselines: (i)
{\sf Do\text{-}Nothing}
agent consistently opts for the “do-nothing” action across all scenarios, independent of the system-state
\mathbf{X}[n]
; (ii)
{\sf Re\text{-}Connection}
agent decides to “re-connect” a disconnected line that greedily
maximizes
the reward estimate
\tilde{r}[n]
(
12
) at the current time step
n
. In cases where reconnection is infeasible due to line downtime constraints or when no lines are available for reconnection, the
{\sf Re\text{-}Connection}
agent defaults to the “do-nothing” action for that step; (iii)
milp_agent
[
41
]
agent strategically minimizes over-thermal line margins using line switching actions
\mathcal{A}_{\rm line}
by formulating the problem as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP); (iv)
OptimCVXPY
[
42
]
agent redispatches the generators to proactively minimize thermal limit margins by formulating a continuous optimization problem. For comparisons, agents
milp_agent
and
OptimCVXPY
are activated only under critical states, i.e., when
\rho_{\ell_{\sf max}}[n]\geq\eta
; and (v) RL + Random Explore baseline agent: we employ a DQN
θ
network with a
tailored
random
\epsilon_{n}
-greedy exploration policy during agent training. Specifically, similar to Algorithm
3
, the agent first constructs a legal action set
\mathcal{A}^{\sf legal}_{\sf line}[n]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{\ell\in[L]:\tau_{\rm D}=0,\tau_{\rm F}=0\}
from
\mathbf{X}[n]
at critical times. In contrast to Algorithm
3
, however, this agent chooses a
random
legal action in the set
a[n]\in\mathcal{A}^{\sf legal}_{\sf line}[n]\bigcup\mathcal{A}_{\sf gen}
(instead of using
\mathcal{R}^{\sf eff}_{\sf line}[n]
). In the Grid2Op 36-bus system, using this random exploration policy, we train the DQN
θ
for
20
hours of repeated interactions with the Grid2Op simulator for each
\mu_{\sf line}\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5\}
. We report results associated with the
best
model
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and refer to the best policy obtained following this random
\epsilon_{n}
-greedy exploration by
\pi^{\sf rand}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mu_{\sf line})
. Similarly, in the IEEE 118-bus system, we train the DQN
θ
model for 15 hours of repeated interactions for
\mu_{\sf line}=0
. We present the results for the Grid2Op 36-bus system in the next two subsections and present their counterpart results to verify scalability in Subsection
5.5
.

5.6
Discussions

5.6.1
Scalability

We enhance network resilience by primarily altering the network topology as a preventive measure to alleviate overflows, which necessitates the re-computation of the LODFs, which has a time complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. It is noteworthy that this re-computation arises only under critical states, specifically when
\rho_{\ell_{\sf max}}[n]\geq\eta
, which occurs infrequently. Additionally, these re-computations are confined to the agent training phase. Once the neural network weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
have been trained to parameterize the policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, sensitivity factor computations are not needed at inference time.

5.6.2
Choice of RL Algorithm

One takeaway of this paper is to showcase how physical signals and domain knowledge can be integrated into RL frameworks. This establishes the benefits of integrating domain knowledge into
\epsilon
-greedy based RL algorithms for a more directed exploration in complex environments. The same idea of creating a physics-guided exploration is broadly applicable to other advanced RL algorithms, such as policy gradients. Rather than randomly initializing the policy network’s weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, pre-training the network with the ultimate objective of maximizing the action probabilities associated with the effective action set
\mathcal{R}^{\sf eff}_{\sf line}[n]\subseteq\mathcal{A}
will render the agent sensitivity factor-aware during exploration. It is noteworthy that the broader applicability of this framework to other RL algorithms is also underscored by the recent study in
[
29
]
, demonstrating improved convergence by incorporating hop-based electrical distance into MCTS.

5.6.3
Potential Applications

Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a physics-guided RL framework to determine sequences of effective real-time remedial control decisions for blackout mitigation. The approach, focused on transmission line-switches and generator adjustments, utilizes linear sensitivity factors to enhance RL exploration during agent training. Simulations on the open-source Grid2Op platform demonstrate that strategically removing transmission lines, alongside multiple real-time generator adjustments, provides a more effective long-term remedial control strategy. Comparative analyses on the Grid2Op 36-bus and the IEEE 118-bus networks highlight the superior performance of our framework against relevant baselines.
