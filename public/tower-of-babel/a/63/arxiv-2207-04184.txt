Koopman-Model Predictive Control with Signal Temporal Logic Specifications for Temperature Regulation of a Warm-Water Supply System Thanks: Corresponding author: Yoshihiko Susuki ( susuki.yoshihiko.5c@kyoto-u.ac.jp )
Ryo Miyashita, Yoshihiko Susuki, Atsushi Ishigame

Abstract
Control of warm-water supply for dialysis treatment in a hospital environment is typical of safety-critical control problems. In order to guarantee the continuity of warm-water supply satisfying physical specifications for a wide range of operating conditions, it is inevitable to consider the nonlinearity involved in a dynamic model of a warm-water supply system for the control design. In this paper, we propose to incorporate control specifications described by signal temporal logic, which is a temporal logic with semantics over finite-time signals in formal methods, into the so-called Koopman-Model Predictive Control (MPC) as a novel technique of nonlinear MPC based on the Koopman operator framework for nonlinear systems. This enables us to generate a sequence of optimal inputs such that the controlled state of a nonlinear system can satisfy the specifications. The proposal is applied to the temperature regulation of warm-water supply, and its effectiveness is established numerically.

Introduction

Design of energy control systems in medical institutions is typical of safety-critical control problems in the modern aging society.
In
[
1
]
, the second author of the present paper introduced a problem of control for warm-water supply in a safety-critical hospital environment.
A system model of warm-water supply based on the practical hospital for dialysis treatment in Japan was addressed.
In the present paper, we focus on the same control problem and report a new control design by a combination of techniques from nonlinear Model Predictive Control (MPC) and formal synthesis.

The formal synthesis, in which the goal is to synthesize or control a finite-state transition system from a temporal logic specification, has attracted interest with connection to control of dynamical systems: see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
]
.
Temporal logics have been widely utilized for specifying desired behaviors of the transition system
[
2
]
.
Temporal logics with semantics over finite-time signals, such as metric temporal logic
[
4
]

and Signal Temporal Logic (STL)
[
5
,
6
]
, have been connected to the so-called optimal control of dynamical systems such as MPC with STL specifications for plants with LTI dynamics
[
7
,
8
]
.

In the preliminary report
[
9
]
,
we focused on the optimization-based method
[
7
,
8
]
for the control of warm-water supply dynamics.
The dynamics were originally modeled by a nonlinear dynamic model
[
10
]
, and thus an LTI model was derived through linearization around a nominal operating point (that is, stable equilibrium point of that model) and used for the control design.
The method was effective for certain initial conditions, but it showed limitations in a situation where the state of the nonlinear model is far from the nominal operating point.
Precisely speaking, for this situation the optimization does not often converge.
It is thus necessary to perform the control design for a wider range of operating conditions in order to guarantee the safety of warm-water supply.

In the present paper, we report a new control design of the warm-water supply system by considering the nonlinearity of the dynamic model.
The main contributions of this paper are two-fold.
First, to consider the nonlinearity for the design, we propose to incorporate control specifications described by STL into the so-called Koopman-MPC
[
11
]
.
The Koopman-MPC is a novel technique of nonlinear MPC based on the Koopman operator framework
[
12
]
for nonlinear dynamical systems and is used in various applications such as power grid
[
13
]
.
The main idea of Koopman-MPC is to utilize a linear model (called linear predictor) that predicts the time evolution of the state of a target nonlinear system. The linear predictor is mathematically guided by the action of the Koopman operator defined for the target nonlinear system and can be estimated directly from time-series data on the state as well as input (see Sections 3.2 and 4.2), which are termed as Koopman linearization.
The incorporation of STL specifications into Koopman-MPC is novel to the best of the authors’ survey.
Second, we numerically show its effectiveness for the control design of warm-water supply by applying it to the nonlinear model of the system.
A procedure of the control design is presented, for example, the choice of observable functions to derive the linear predictor.
This paper is a refined version of the non-reviewed proceeding
[
14
]
.

2.1
System model and control specifications

Figure
1
shows the warm-water supply system which we consider in this paper.
The system is related to the actual medical institution
[
15
]
and consists of one heat pump, two pipes, and one tank.
The heat pump aims to raise the temperature of water.
The pipes flow warm-water between the heat pump and the tank.
The tank stores warm-water and supplies it to the load assumed to be connected to the upper part of the tank.
The load is supposed to be ideal, and its characteristics can be neglected.
Also, to consider temperature gradient of water inside the tank, we use the three-layer model in
[
16
]

Between nearest layers, the exchange of heat is considered.

In this paper, we introduce two control specifications based on practical constraints of the system.
Dialysis treatment requires warm water of about the temperature of human skin (about
37\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
)
[
15
]
.
Based on this, the first control specification is to keep the temperature of the third layer of the tank, which will be regarded as the output of a nonlinear dynamic model, above
40\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
.
The value is set by considering the temperature drop from the third layer to the (ideal) load.
The second control specification is based on the operating range of the heat pump as described in
[
17
]
, where its electricity consumption ranges from
80\%
of the rated to the rated itself.
The rated is set to
26.5\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{W}$
based on actual measurement data of the heat pump in the hospital.

3
Koopman-MPC with STL specifications

This section is devoted to the first contribution of this paper—to propose the Koopman-MPC with STL specifications.
First of all, we briefly explain the STL and Koopman-MPC, and then describe our idea of how to incorporate a control specification described by STL into the formulation of Koopman-MPC.

3.1
STL (Signal Temporal Logic)

STL is a temporal logic that allows the specification of properties of real-valued signals and the automatic generation of monitors for testing these properties

[
5
,
6
]
.
STL formulas are defined recursively in
[
7
,
8
]
as follows:

\phi::=\mu~|~\lnot\mu~|~\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2}~|~\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2}~|~\square_{[a,b]}\phi~|~\phi_{1}{\cal U}_{[a,b]}\phi_{2},

(2)

where
\mu
is a predicate whose value is determined by the sign of a function of an underlying signal, and
\phi
is an STL formula.
We here use the usual logical operators such as

\lnot
(negation),
\land
(conjunction),
\lor
(disjunction), and
\to
(implication).
The temporal logic operators are
\square_{[a,b]}\phi
(always: if the property defined by
\phi
holds (that is,
\phi
is true (
\top
)) at any time between
a
and
b
) and
\phi_{1}{\cal U}_{[a,b]}\phi_{2}
(until: if
\phi_{1}
holds at any time before
\phi_{2}
holds, and
\phi_{2}
holds at some onset between
a
and
b
).
Additionally, the operator

\Diamond_{[a,b]}\phi:=\top{\cal U}_{[a,b]}\phi
is defined (eventually: if
\phi
holds at some onset from
a
to
b
).
For details including its semantics and boundedness property, see
[
7
,
8
]
.

3.2
Koopman-MPC

The Koopman-MPC is the MPC for nonlinear dynamical systems
using linear predictors based on the so-called Koopman linearization
[
11
]
.
A main idea of constructing an accurate linear predictor is to introduce a mapping from the state space of a target nonlinear system to a space with dimension sufficiently higher than that of the original system.
To make this concrete, a function:
\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is introduced from the state space
\mathbb{X}
of the nonlinear system (
1
) to
\mathbb{C}
, called the
observable
.
A linear predictor is estimated using a set of observables,
\psi_{i}:i=1,\ldots,N
, where
N
is sufficiently larger than the dimension of
\mathbb{X}
.
This estimation can be performed based on time-series data of the state
\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{x}(kh)
, input
u_{k}=u(kh)
, and disturbance
w_{k}=w(kh)
for (
1
) under the sampling period
h
, and is explained in Section
4.2
.
An advantage of the Koopman linearization is that it can work globally in the state space
\mathbb{X}
, unlike the conventional local linearization around a particular state such as an equilibrium point.
The linear predictor (
3
) for (
1
) is derived in discrete-time form:

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bm{z}_{k+1}&=&{\mathsf{A}}\bm{z}_{k}+\bm{b}_{u}u_{k}+\bm{b}_{d}w_{k}\\
\hat{\bm{x}}_{k}&=&{\mathsf{C}}\bm{z}_{k}\end{array}\right\},

(3)

where
k
is the discrete time.
The state
\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
is defined by the set of observables
\psi
as
\bm{z}\coloneqq\bm{\psi}(\bm{x})=\left[\psi_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,\psi_{N}(\bm{x})\right]^{\rm T}
(
\rm T
stands for the transpose operation).
The output
\hat{\bm{x}}
is the predicted value of the sampled
\bm{x}
.
The coefficient matrices
{\sf A},\bm{b}_{u},\bm{b}_{d},{\sf C}
are determined through the data-based estimation.

The Koopman-MPC controller then solves at each time step
k
of the closed-loop operation the following optimization problem:

\left.\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle{\minimize_{u_{0},\ldots,u_{N_{\rm{p}}-1}}}&{\it{J}}\left(\left(u_{i}\right)_{i=0}^{N_{\rm{p}}-1},\left(\bm{z}_{i}\right)_{i=0}^{N_{\rm{p}}-1}\right)\\
\text{subject to}&\bm{z}_{i+1}={\mathsf{A}}\bm{z}_{i}+\bm{b}_{u}u_{i}+\bm{b}_{d}w_{i}\\
&u_{\text{min}}\leq u_{i}\leq u_{\text{max}}\\
&\bm{z}_{\text{min}}\leq\bm{z}_{i}\leq\bm{z}_{\text{max}}\\
&(i=0,\ldots,N_{\rm{p}}-1)\\
\text{parameter}&\bm{z}_{0}=\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}_{k})\end{array}\right\},

(4)

where
N_{\rm{p}}
is the number of prediction steps (horizon), and
J
is a cost function.
For an optimizer at time step
k
, denoted by
u^{\star}_{0},u^{\star}_{1},\ldots,u^{\star}_{N_{\rm{p}-1}}
, the initial control
u^{\star}_{0}
is used at the time step
k
.
Note that because (
1
) is in continuous-time form, we apply the above input to (
1
) using the zeroth-order hold.

3.3
Proposed method: Koopman-MPC with STL specifications

In this paper, we propose to incorporate an STL specification with the Koopman-MPC.
The controller solves the following optimization problem:

\left.\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle{\minimize_{u_{0},\ldots,u_{N_{\rm p}-1}}}&{\it{J}}\left(\left(u_{i}\right)_{i=0}^{N_{\rm{p}}-1},\left(\bm{z}_{i}\right)_{i=0}^{N_{\rm{p}}-1}\right)\\
\text{subject to}&\bm{z}_{i+1}={\mathsf{A}}\bm{z}_{i}+\bm{b}_{u}u_{i}+\bm{b}_{d}w_{i}\\
&u_{\text{min}}\leq u_{i}\leq u_{\text{max}}\\
&\bm{z}_{\text{min}}\leq\bm{z}_{i}\leq\bm{z}_{\text{max}}\\
&(i=0,\ldots,N_{\rm{p}}-1)\\
&\bm{z}_{0},u_{0}\vDash\bm{\phi}\\
\text{parameter}&\bm{z}_{0}=\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}_{k})\end{array}\right\},

(5)

where
\bm{\phi}
stands for STL formulas corresponding to control specifications.
The key inclusion
\bm{z}_{0},u_{0}\vDash\bm{\phi}
implies that
\bm{z}_{0},u_{0}
satisfy the STL formulas
\bm{\phi}
at time step
k
of the closed-loop operation.
The problem (
5
) except for the parameter linked to the original nonlinear system is basically same as that for the MPC with STL specifications for discrete-time LTI systems
[
7
,
8
]
.
In these, the system dynamics as well as the STL formulas are automatically encoded to Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) constraints.
The MILP encoding is available from the MATLAB toolbox
BluSTL

[
8
]
, by which the Koopman-MPC with STL specifications can be implemented.

4
Application to warm-water supply system

This section is devoted to the second contribution of this paper—to apply the Koopman-MPC with STL specifications to the control problem of the warm-water supply system.

4.1
Description of control specifications with STL

We describe the control specifications described in Section
2.1
as STL formulas.
For simplicity of the presentation, all units in the STL formulas are omitted.
The first specification of the water temperature
y_{k}=y(kh)
, denoted as
\phi_{1}
, is given by

\phi_{1}=\square_{[420,{\rm{end}}]}\left(y_{k}\geqq 40\right),

(6)

where ‘end’ stands for the finite, final time of closed-loop operation.
Eq. (
6
) guarantees the supply of warm water with temperature larger than and equal to
40\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
.
The prescribed time in the formula,
420\,$\mathrm{s}$
, is set by taking into account a transient duration for the water temperature to rise.
The second specification
\phi_{2}
for the power input to the heat pump is given by

\phi_{2}=\square_{[0,{\rm{end}}]}\bigl[\left\{(u_{k}>0.001)\land(u_{k}<0.01)\right\}\lor\left\{(u_{k}\geqq 21.2)\land(u_{k}\leqq 26.5)\right\}\bigr].

(7)

At any time, the heat pump will consume
0.001\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{W}$
to
0.01\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{W}$
, that is,
0\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{W}$
of power when it stops, and it will also consume
21.2\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{W}$
to
26.5\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{W}$
of power when it runs.

4.2
Setting of Koopman-MPC

Here, we explain the estimation of linear predictor for the warm-water supply system.
For this, the following set of observables
\bm{\psi}
is introduced in which all nonlinear terms of the state in the original nonlinear model (
1
) are taken out, referring to
[
18
]
:

\bm{\psi}(\bm{x})=[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6},x_{3}^{2},x_{4}^{2},x_{5}^{2},x_{3}^{2}x_{4},x_{3}x_{4}^{2},x_{4}^{2}x_{5},x_{4}x_{5}^{2},x_{3}^{3},x_{4}^{3},x_{5}^{3}]^{\rm T}.

(8)

The estimation procedure is based on
[
11
]
and summarized below.
The initial dataset (
9
) with number
K=10000
is introduced as

\left.\begin{aligned} \mathsf{X}&:=[\bm{x}^{(1)}_{0},\bm{x}^{(2)}_{0},\ldots,\bm{x}^{(K)}_{0}]\\
\bm{u}&:=[u^{(1)}_{0},u^{(2)}_{0},\ldots,u^{(K)}_{0}]\\
\bm{w}&:=[1,1,\ldots,1]w_{0}\end{aligned}\right\},

(9)

where every element of
\bm{x}^{(i)}_{0}
is commonly set at a uniform random number in
[10,40]
, and
u^{(i)}_{0}
is set at either a uniform random number in
[21.2,26.5]
or
0
.
The probability of appearance of
0
is
20\%
.
The disturbance is common for all the initial conditions, and
w_{0}
is set at
10
.
For each
\bm{x}^{(i)}_{0}
, its one-step (
h
) progress is computed using
u^{(i)}_{0}
and
w_{0}
, denoted as
\bm{x}^{(i)}_{1}
, and the following dataset is additionally introduced:

{\sf X}^{\prime}:=[\bm{x}^{(1)}_{1},\bm{x}^{(2)}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}^{(K)}_{1}].

(10)

The coefficient matrices of the linear predictor are then estimated with

\left.\begin{aligned} &=\mathsf{X}^{\prime}_{\rm{lift}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{X}_{\rm{lift}}\\
\bm{u}\\
\bm{w}\end{bmatrix}^{{\dagger}}\\
\mathsf{C}&=\mathsf{X}\mathsf{X}^{{\dagger}}_{\rm{lift}}\end{aligned}\right\},

(11)

where
{\dagger}
is the pseudo-inverse of a matrix, and
\mathsf{X}_{\rm{lift}}
and
\mathsf{X}^{\prime}_{\rm{lift}}
are defined through
\bm{\psi}
as

\left.\begin{aligned} \mathsf{X}_{\rm{lift}}&=\bm{\psi}({\sf X}):=[\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{(1)}_{0}),\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{(2)}_{0}),\ldots,\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{(K)}_{0})]\\
\mathsf{X}^{\prime}_{\rm{lift}}&=\bm{\psi}({\sf X}^{\prime}):=[\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{(1)}_{1}),\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{(2)}_{1}),\ldots,\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{(K)}_{1})]\\
\end{aligned}\right\}.

Next, we explain the setting of MPC.
The cost function
J
is set so that we minimize in a fixed horizon
N_{\rm p}
the error between the temperature of the output water (in the third layer of tank) and the reference temperature of
40\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
, and the power input to the heat pump.
The cost function is

{\it{J}}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathrm{p}}-1}\left\{Q\left(y_{i+1}-40\right)^{2}+Ru_{i}^{2}\right\},

(12)

where
Q
and
R
are positive weights.
For the following simulations, the time step of the optimization is set at
h=60\,$\mathrm{s}$
to prevent chattering, and
the horizon
N_{\rm{p}}
is set at
10
(that is,
10h=600\,$\mathrm{s}$
).
The two weights are set as
Q=1
and
R=10
, implying that we intend to keep the power input as low as possible.
The final time of closed-loop simulation is set as
1200\,$\mathrm{s}$
, which is as long enough time from the first specification of the continuity of warm-water supply.
This is relevant in practical situations.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we formulated the Koopman-MPC with STL specifications and applied it to the control problem of the warm-water supply system.
This paper showed that the Koopman-MPC with STL specification guarantees a wider set of feasible initial states of the safe supply operation by comparison with the linear MPC with STL specifications.

Future directions are summarized as follows.
Without assuming that the outside temperature is constant, we will incorporate time-series prediction of disturbance into the model estimation.
Since the performance of the linear predictor depends on the choice of the observables
\bm{\psi}
, we examine to improve the control performance by selecting a different set of observables.
In addition, we examine another STL specification such as suppression of output temperature fluctuation from the viewpoint of dialysis treatment.
