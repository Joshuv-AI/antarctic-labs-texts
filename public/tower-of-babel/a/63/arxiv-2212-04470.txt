On the Mean Square Error Optimal Estimator in One-Bit Quantized Systems
Benedikt Fesl, Michael Koller, Wolfgang Utschick

Abstract
This paper investigates the mean square error (MSE)-optimal conditional mean estimator (CME) in one-bit quantized systems in the context of channel estimation with jointly Gaussian inputs. We analyze the relationship of the generally nonlinear CME to the linear Bussgang estimator, a well-known method based on Bussgang’s theorem. We highlight a novel observation that the Bussgang estimator is equal to the CME for different special cases, including the case of univariate Gaussian inputs and the case of multiple pilot signals in the absence of additive noise prior to the quantization. For the general cases we conduct numerical simulations to quantify the gap between the Bussgang estimator and the CME . This gap increases for higher dimensions and longer pilot sequences. We propose an optimal pilot sequence, motivated by insights from the CME , and derive a novel closed-form expression of the MSE for that case. Afterwards, we find a closed-form limit of the MSE in the asymptotically large number of pilots regime that also holds for the Bussgang estimator. Lastly, we present numerical experiments for various system parameters and for different performance metrics which illuminate the behavior of the optimal channel estimator in the quantized regime. In this context, the well-known stochastic resonance effect that appears in quantized systems can be quantified.

Despite this extensive variety of channel estimation algorithms, there is so far no detailed discussion about the
CME

CME
is well-known to be the minimizer for the
MSE

CME
is the optimal predictor for all Bregman loss functions, of which the

[
41

On the one hand, there are approximations of the

[
42

, or with neural networks that are trained on unquantized data
[

On the other hand, the

[

[
43

. However, to our knowledge, there exists no general discussion about the multivariate case with correlated channel entries, or with longer pilot sequences that can not be optimally decorrelated.

In

Contributions:
We discuss the
CME

We finally discuss further performance metrics such as the cosine similarity and a lower bound on the achievable rate for a matched filter.

II
Problem Formulation

We consider the following generic system equation

{\bm{R}}=Q({\bm{Y}})=Q({\bm{h}}{\bm{a}}^{\operatorname{T}}+{\bm{N}})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}

(1)

where
{\bm{R}}=[{\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{r}}_{M}]
contains
M
quantized observations of the vector of interest
{\bm{h}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
with the known pilot vector
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
that fulfills the power constraint
\|{\bm{a}}\|_{2}^{2}=M
. We denote by
{\bm{Y}}
the unquantized received signal.
Let the vector
{\bm{h}}
be a zero-mean Gaussian
RV
, i.e.,
{\bm{h}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}})
.
The desired signal is distorted with additive zero-mean Gaussian noise
{\bm{N}}=[{\bm{n}}_{1},{\bm{n}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{n}}_{M}]
where
{\bm{n}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{n}}})
and is afterwards quantized with the complex-valued one-bit quantization function

Q(\cdot)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\operatorname{sign}(\Re(\cdot))+{\operatorname{j}}\operatorname{sign}(\Im(\cdot))\right)

(2)

which is applied element-wise to the input vector/matrix. The system model in (
1
) can be equivalently described in its (column-wise) vectorized form as

{\bm{r}}=Q({\bm{y}})=Q({\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}+{\bm{n}})\in\mathbb{C}^{NM}

(3)

with
{\bm{A}}={\bm{a}}\otimes\eye
,
{\bm{r}}=\operatorname{vec}({\bm{R}})
,
{\bm{y}}=\operatorname{vec}({\bm{Y}})
, and
{\bm{n}}=\operatorname{vec}({\bm{N}})
.
The system model describes, e.g., a
single-input multiple-output (SIMO)
wireless communication scenario where a base station equipped with
N
antennas receives
M
pilot signals from a single-antenna user terminal. The
ADCs
at the receiver have one-bit resolution, which is modeled by the quantization function in (
2
).
Although we especially focus on the application of channel estimation in this work, the analysis is not limited to this instance and might yield insights to various applications such as lossy compression
[
2
]
, wireless sensor networks
[
3
]
, audio coding
[
4
]
, or control theory
[
45
]
,
to name a few.

The overall goal is to estimate the signal
{\bm{h}}
in an optimal sense where the vector
{\bm{a}}
is known and the channel and noise are both zero-mean Gaussian with known covariances. The optimality depends on the metric of choice, however, the most frequently used criterion in the context of channel estimation is the
MSE
between the true channel
{\bm{h}}
and its estimate
\hat{{\bm{h}}}
, i.e.,

\text{MSE}={\operatorname{E}}[\|{\bm{h}}-\hat{{\bm{h}}}\|_{2}^{2}]

(4)

for which the
CME
, defined as

\hat{{\bm{h}}}={\operatorname{E}}[{\bm{h}}|{\bm{r}}]=\int{\bm{h}}{\bm{f}}_{{\bm{h}}|{\bm{r}}}({\bm{h}}|{\bm{r}}){\operatorname{d}}{\bm{h}},

(5)

is known to yield optimal estimates.
The
CME
is generally not available since it requires full knowledge of the conditional
PDF

f_{{\bm{h}}|{\bm{r}}}
. Furthermore, even if the
PDF
is known, the
CME
does generally neither have a closed-form solution nor is it feasible to compute numerically in real-time systems. For this reason, the
CME
is usually approximated by sub-optimal approaches with a practically reasonable trade-off between performance and complexity, e.g., using the linear
MMSE
estimator. In a jointly Gaussian setting, the linear
MMSE
estimator is well-known to be the
CME
. However, in the quantized case, this statement is no longer true in general.
Nonetheless, for a zero-mean jointly Gaussian input, the Bussgang decomposition, based on Bussgang’s theorem
[
33
]
, can be leveraged to design a linear
MMSE
estimator in the quantized case. The Bussgang estimator is sub-optimal in general due to its linearity. However, up to now there has been no analysis of the connection between the Bussgang estimator and the
CME
in the case of one-bit quantization.

In this work, we investigate the
MSE
optimality of the Bussgang estimator and identify cases in which the Bussgang estimator is equal to the
CME
and we show when the Bussgang estimator differs from the
CME
. To this end, after revising the Bussgang estimator, we derive the
CME
for one-bit quantization in the multivariate case. Simulation results based on the derived quantities verify the discussion.

III
Bussgang Estimator

In this section, we briefly revise the linear
MMSE
estimator based on the Bussgang decomposition which is a direct consequence of Bussgang’s theorem
[
33
]
.
In particular, the Bussgang decomposition implies that the system in (
3
) can be written as a linear combination of the desired signal part and an uncorrelated distortion
{\bm{q}}
as

{\bm{r}}=Q({\bm{y}})={\bm{B}}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}+{\bm{q}},

(6)

where
{\bm{B}}
is the Bussgang gain that can be obtained from the linear
MMSE
estimation of
{\bm{r}}
from
{\bm{y}}
as

{\bm{B}}={\bm{C}}_{{\bm{r}}{\bm{y}}}{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}}^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\operatorname{diag}({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}})^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(7)

cf.
[
46
, Sec. 9.2]
,
and where the distortion term
{\bm{q}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{n}}+{\bm{\eta}}
contains both the
AWGN

{\bm{n}}
and the quantization noise
{\bm{\eta}}
.
As the statistically equivalent model (
6
) is linear, one can formulate the linear
MMSE
estimator

\hat{{\bm{h}}}_{\text{buss}}={\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}{\bm{r}}}{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{r}}}^{-1}{\bm{r}}.

(8)

The cross-correlation matrix between the channel and the received signal is calculated as

{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}{\bm{r}}}={\operatorname{E}}[{\bm{h}}({\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}+{\bm{q}})^{\operatorname{H}}]={\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{A}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}^{\operatorname{H}}

(9)

which follows from the fact that the noise term
{\bm{q}}
is uncorrelated with the channel
{\bm{h}}
, see
[
12
, Appendix A]
.
The auto-correlation matrix
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{r}}}
is calculated via the so-called arcsine law
[
47
]
as

{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{r}}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\arcsin\left({\bm{\Psi}}\Re({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}}){\bm{\Psi}}\right)+{\operatorname{j}}\arcsin\left({\bm{\Psi}}\Im({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}}){\bm{\Psi}}\right)\right)

(10)

where
{\bm{\Psi}}=\operatorname{diag}({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
Further reading on the Bussgang estimator can be found in
[
12
,
37
]
.

In
[
40
]
, it is discussed that the Bussgang estimator is equal to a two-stage estimator that first computes the
CME
of the product
{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}
of the pilot matrix and the channel, and afterwards recovers the channel
{\bm{h}}
. Since the resulting error after the first step is non-Gaussian, the second step is non-optimal from the
CME
perspective. In the following, we discuss cases in which the Bussgang estimator is indeed equal to the
CME
, and we show the differences between the
CME
and the Bussgang estimator in the general case.

IV
Conditional Mean Estimator

In this section, we derive the
CME
for one-bit quantization for the general system in (
3
). Afterwards, we discuss the
CME
for different simplifications, i.e., with and without
AWGN
or multiple pilots, and for the uni- as well as multivariate case, where these cases are differently combined to provide a general overview. For each instance, we discuss possible closed-form solutions of the
CME
and its relationship to the Bussgang estimator, where we identify cases in which equality between these estimators holds.
We start by rewriting the
CME
from (
5
) as

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[{\bm{h}}|{\bm{r}}]

\displaystyle=\int{\bm{h}}\frac{p_{{\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}}}({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})f_{{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}})}{p_{{\bm{r}}}({\bm{r}})}\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pt{\bm{h}}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{{\bm{r}}}({\bm{r}})}\int{\bm{h}}f_{{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}})p_{{\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}}}({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pt{\bm{h}}

(12)

by making use of Bayes’ theorem. It is known that
f_{{\bm{h}}}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}})
. In the following, we also find expressions for the prior and conditional probabilities.
We first define the set of all vectors
{\bm{y}}
which are mapped onto a given observation
{\bm{r}}
as

\overline{Q}({\bm{r}})=\left\{{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN}:Q({\bm{y}})={\bm{r}}\right\}.

(13)

Integrating the
PDF

f_{{\bm{y}}}
over this set yields the prior probability of observing the respective quantized observation, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{r}}}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{Q}({\bm{r}})}f_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{y}})\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pt{\bm{y}},

(14)

where due to the joint Gaussianity we have
f_{{\bm{y}}}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}})
with
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{A}}^{\operatorname{H}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{n}}}
, cf. (
3
).
The conditional probability
p_{{\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}}}({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})
is found similarly, however, now the integrand changes to
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{A}}{\bm{h}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{n}}})
, where
{\bm{h}}
is no longer a
RV
:

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}}}({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})=\int_{\overline{Q}({\bm{r}})}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{n}}})\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pt{\bm{x}}.

(15)

Plugging (
14
) and (
15
) into (
12
) yields a generally computable expression of the
CME
for the model in (
3
).

IV-A

Numerical Integration

For the most general case, the integrals in (
12
), (
14
), and (
15
) do neither have a closed-form solution, nor can they be simplified. In this case, the
CME
has to be found by numerical integration. However, the number of solutions for the discrete number of possible observations
{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN}
grows exponentially with
4^{MN}
, not to mention the increasing complexity for each individual solution.
Since in (
14
) and (
15
) we have integrals over Gaussian
PDFs
, there exist computationally efficient algorithms. In this paper, the numerical integration of the Gaussian density is based on an algorithm from
[
48
]
which first transforms the integral into an integration over the unit hypercube by combining a Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix with a priority ordering of the integration variables. The transformed integral is then approximated based on a Monte Carlo method which is implemented in
[
49
]
.
In the following, we discuss special instances of the general model (
3
) that allow for a simplified computation of the
CME
(
12
).

IV-B

Univariate Case with a Single Observation

In the following, the case of a scalar
N=1
system with a single pilot observation, i.e.,
M=1
and
a=1
, is considered.
Since the quantization function in (
2
) is applied independently to the real and imaginary part of its input, deriving only the real part formula of the
CME
is sufficient here since the imaginary part is computed analogously.
To this end we consider
h_{\Re}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}/2)
,
n_{\Re}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\eta^{2}/2)
, and for the ease of notation we define
r_{\Re}=\operatorname{sign}(h_{\Re}+n_{\Re})
.

Because of the symmetry of the zero-mean Gaussian density, we get
p_{r_{\Re}}(r_{\Re})=1/2
.
Furthermore, the integral in (
15
) can be computed in closed-form to, cf.
[
50
]
,

\displaystyle p_{r_{\Re}|h_{\Re}}(r_{\Re}=1|h_{\Re})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}(x;h_{\Re},\eta^{2}/2)\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5ptx

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{h_{\Re}}{\eta}\right)\right),

(17)

\displaystyle p_{r_{\Re}|h_{\Re}}(r_{\Re}=-1|h_{\Re})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathcal{N}(x;h_{\Re},\eta^{2}/2)\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5ptx

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{h_{\Re}}{\eta}\right)\right).

(19)

We can summarize (
17
) and (
19
) as

p_{r_{\Re}|h_{\Re}}(r_{\Re}|h_{\Re})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+r_{\Re}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{h_{\Re}}{\eta}\right)\right).

(20)

Plugging this result into (
12
) yields, cf.
[
51
, eq. (2.7.2.4)]
,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{E}}[h_{\Re}|r_{\Re}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}hf_{h_{\Re}}(h)\left(1+r_{\Re}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{h}{\eta}\right)\right)\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pth

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\eta^{2}}}r_{\Re}.

(22)

This can now easily be extended to the complex case with
h\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
n\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\eta^{2})
, and
r=Q(h+n)
which reads as

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[h|r]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\eta^{2}}}r.

(23)

The closed-form
MSE
from (
4
) is then computed to

\displaystyle\operatorname{MSE}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\eta^{2}}\right).

(24)

Interestingly, if we evaluate the Bussgang estimator in (
8
), we get the exact same
CME
and
MSE
expressions. To conclude, for this case the Bussgang estimator is indeed the
CME
.
Note that in the multivariate case with an identity pilot matrix
{\bm{A}}=\eye
and uncorrelated channels
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}=\operatorname{diag}({\bm{c}}_{\bm{h}})
as well as uncorrelated noise
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{n}}}=\operatorname{diag}({\bm{c}}_{{\bm{n}}})
, the
PDFs
in (
14
) and (
15
) can be decomposed into the product of their marginals. Thus, the
CME
from (
23
) can be applied element-wise and is therefore also equal to the Bussgang estimator.
The same expression as in (
23
) was found in
[
43
]
, more generally for non zero-mean channels. However, the Bussgang theorem from
[
33
]
can not be applied for non zero-mean
RVs
and thus, the equality does not hold in general.

Additionally, we can evaluate the well-known linear
MMSE
estimator for unquantized observations where it is the
CME
. There, the closed-form
MSE
is simply

\text{MSE}=\sigma^{2}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\eta^{2}}\right).

(25)

We can now compute the necessary
SNR
for the unquantized system to achieve the same
MSE
as the one-bit quantized system without
AWGN
which we will analyze later in the numerical experiments section.

IV-C

Multivariate Noiseless Case with a Single Observation

In this case, we consider
M=1
with
{\bm{n}}={\bm{0}}
. Similarly as in
Section

IV-B
, it is sufficient to derive only the real part of the
CME
. To this end, we consider
{\bm{r}}_{\Re}=\operatorname{sign}({\bm{h}}_{\Re})
with
{\bm{h}}_{\Re}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}})
where
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a full matrix. We abuse the notation for
\overline{Q}({\bm{r}}_{\Re})=\{{\bm{h}}_{\Re}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\operatorname{sign}({\bm{h}}_{\Re})={\bm{r}}_{\Re}\}
.
In the following Theorem
1
, a simplified expression of the general
CME
(
12
) is derived whose advantage is discussed afterwards.

Theorem 1
.

The
CME
for the above system can be computed element-wise as

\displaystyle\left[\operatorname{E}[{\bm{h}}_{\Re}|{\bm{r}}_{\Re}]\right]_{i}=\frac{1}{p_{{\bm{r}}_{\Re}}({\bm{r}}_{\Re})}\sum_{n=1}^{N}[{\bm{r}}_{\Re}]_{n}[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}]_{i,n}

IV-D

Univariate Noiseless Case with Multiple Observations

In this case, we consider
N=1

{\bm{n}}={\bm{0}}
, i.e., the system from (

{\bm{r}}=Q({\bm{a}}h)
with

Note that in this case it is not sufficient to compute the real-valued

.
Opposite to the unquantized case, having multiple pilot observations of the same realization is helpful in the context of quantization as more information can be recovered.
Because the amplitude of the received signal is lost after the one-bit quantizer, having varying amplitude in the pilot sequence
{\bm{a}}
has no influence on the observed signal.
However, one can introduce phase shifts in order to increase the obtained information.

In the following, we discuss the optimal pilot sequence and the resulting

based on it. We start by rewriting the

in terms of the magnitude and phase of the channel

[

\displaystyle{\operatorname{E}}[\alpha{\operatorname{e}}^{{\operatorname{j}}\theta}|{\bm{r}}]={\operatorname{E}}[\alpha|{\bm{r}}]\hskip 1.0pt{\operatorname{E}}[{\operatorname{e}}^{{\operatorname{j}}\theta}|{\bm{r}}]={\operatorname{E}}[\alpha]\hskip 1.0pt{\operatorname{E}}[{\operatorname{e}}^{{\operatorname{j}}\theta}|{\bm{r}}],

(28)

since the magnitude information is lost due to the one-bit quantization and its estimate is thus independent of the received signal.
Therefore, we can set the magnitude of each pilot to one without loss of generality in order to fulfill the power constraint
\|{\bm{a}}\|_{2}^{2}=M
.

\pi/2

\pi/2

{\bm{r}}

\phi_{\text{low}}({\bm{r}})

\phi_{\text{high}}({\bm{r}})

V
Different Performance Metrics

V-A

Cosine Similarity

The amplitude of the received signal is lost due to the one-bit quantization. For this reason, one might solely be interested in how well an estimator can recover the angle of the signal of interest. A measure for the difference of the angle
\psi
between the true and estimated channel is the
cosine similarity
which we define as

\cos\psi=\frac{\Re({\bm{h}}^{\operatorname{H}}\hat{{\bm{h}}})}{\|{\bm{h}}\|\|\hat{{\bm{h}}}\|}.

(41)

Note that the cosine similarity lies between
-1
(opposite direction) and
1
(same direction).

V-B

Achievable Rate Lower Bound

The achievable rate is of great interest in quantized systems
[
12
,
13
]
.
For our considerations, we are aiming at a comparison between the
CME
and the Bussgang estimator via a lower bound on the corresponding achievable rate of a respective data transmission system that is taking the
CSI
mismatch into account. To this end, after estimating the channel with the pilot transmission in (
3
), the data symbol
s
is transmitted over the same channel, i.e.,

{\bm{r}}=Q({\bm{h}}s+{\bm{n}})={\bm{B}}{\bm{h}}s+{\bm{q}}

(42)

where in the second equation the linearized model with Bussgang’s decomposition is used where
{\bm{q}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{n}}+{\bm{\eta}}
. We make the worst-case assumption that the aggregated noise is Gaussian, i.e.,
{\bm{q}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{q}}}={\bm{C}}_{{\bm{r}}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{B}}^{\operatorname{H}})
, cf.
[
54
]
, and use a matched filter

{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}=\hat{{\bm{h}}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}^{\operatorname{H}}({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{r}}}-{\bm{B}}{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{B}}^{\operatorname{H}})^{-1}

(43)

at the receiver. Note that the variance of the data symbol
s
is assumed to be one without loss of generality. We further assume that the
SNR
is the same during pilot and data transmission.
As in
[
55
]
, we split up the received data symbol as

\displaystyle\hat{s}=\underbrace{{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}\hat{{\bm{h}}}s}_{\text{desired signal}}+\underbrace{{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}{\bm{\epsilon}}s}_{\text{estimation error}}+\underbrace{{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}{\bm{q}}}_{\text{noise}}

(44)

where
{\bm{\epsilon}}={\bm{h}}-\hat{{\bm{h}}}
denotes the channel estimation error. Thus, we can evaluate a lower bound on the achievable rate, cf.
[
55
, eq. (20)]
, as

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle={\operatorname{E}}\left[\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{|{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}\hat{{\bm{h}}}|^{2}}{|{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}{\bm{\epsilon}}|^{2}+{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{B}}{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{q}}}{\bm{B}}^{\operatorname{H}}{\bm{g}}_{\text{MF}}}\right)\right]

(45)

with Monte Carlo simulations.

Numerical Results

In this section, we perform numerical experiments that verify our theoretical derivations and allow for quantifying the performance gap between the Bussgang estimator and the
CME
in the general case.
We consider a diagonal noise covariance
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{n}}}=\eta^{2}\eye
. For the case of
N=1
, we choose without loss of generality the variance of the channel to
\sigma^{2}=1
. In the multivariate case, we randomly sample channel covariance matrices with the following procedure, cf.
[

]
.
We first generate a matrix

where both the real and imaginary parts of all entries are i.i.d. as
\mathcal{U}(0,1)

[{\bm{\xi}}]_{m}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
.
Afterwards, we normalize each covariance matrix by first dividing by its trace and afterwards multiplying by its dimension, such that
\operatorname{tr}({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{h}}})=N
.
For each covariance matrix we draw a single channel sample. This procedure averages out all possible structural features of the covariance matrix. For all simulations we draw a total of
10{,}000

With the property

and

(46)

The

from (
4
) is normalized by

.

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we have investigated the
CME
for one-bit quantized systems in a jointly Gaussian setting. We derived a novel closed-form solution for multiple pilot observations and a transformation into a computationally efficient expression where only Gaussian integrals have to be evaluated for the case without
AWGN
.
Additionally, we have proposed an
MSE
-optimal pilot sequence which we motivated by the structure of the
CME
. It turned out that a successive shift of the transmit signals’ angle is optimal which was already proposed in prior work with a different motivation in the context of deep learning.

We analyzed the well-known Bussgang estimator and showed the equivalence of the Bussgang estimator and the
CME
in the univariate case with a single pilot observation, which extends to the multivariate case when there is no correlation between the channel or noise entries. In addition, we showed that the closed-form
MSE
terms of the Bussgang estimator and the
CME
are equal in the case of multiple pilots without
AWGN
. To this end, we found a closed-form expression for the inverse of the covariance matrix of the quantized receive signal which is derived with the arcsine law.
For the general case where numerical integration is necessary, we found that the performance gap between the Bussgang estimator and the
CME
increases for higher dimensions, longer pilot sequences, or higher
SNR
values.
The findings of this work may facilitate extensions to more general input distributions, e.g.,
Gaussian mixture models (GMMs)
, different nonlinearities such as the clipper function instead of the one-bit quantization, or to quantizers with higher resolutions.

Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

We first observe that with the procedure in Algorithm
1
the angle domain can be split into at most
4M
regions by choosing
\psi_{m}\neq 0

\forall m=2,...,M
because any of the four quadrants can be divided into
M
distinct regions which are identified via (
32
).
Now, we write the
CME
as
\hat{h}=\hat{\alpha}{\operatorname{e}}^{{\operatorname{j}}\hat{\theta}}
such that we can reformulate the
MSE
as

\displaystyle{\operatorname{E}}[|\alpha{\operatorname{e}}^{{\operatorname{j}}\theta}-\hat{\alpha}{\operatorname{e}}^{{\operatorname{j}}\hat{\theta}}|^{2}]

\displaystyle={\operatorname{E}}[\alpha^{2}+\hat{\alpha}^{2}-2\alpha\hat{\alpha}\cos(\theta-\hat{\theta})]

(52)

by using the cosine law. Since we are solely interested in the phase estimate for the design of the optimal pilot sequence and the cosine is monotonically decreasing between
[0,\pi/2)
, the minimization of the
MSE
between the phases
{\operatorname{E}}[(\theta-\hat{\theta})^{2}]
is equivalent to the minimization of the
MSE
from (
52
) for fixed magnitudes.
Note that because of the uniform distribution
f_{\theta}
the
CME

\hat{\theta}
is exactly the midpoint between the boundary angles which can also be seen later in Theorem
3
.
The optimization of the pilot sequence is thus given as

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{a}}}~{\operatorname{E}}[(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bm{a}}))^{2}]

(53)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bm{a}}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bm{a}}))^{2}f_{\theta}(\theta)\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pt\theta

(54)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bm{a}}}\sum_{i=1}^{4M-1}\int_{\phi_{i}}^{\phi_{i+1}}(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bm{a}}))^{2}f_{\theta}(\theta)\operatorname{d}\hskip-1.5pt\theta

(55)

where in (

4M

32

\phi_{1}<\dots<\phi_{4M}
where
\phi_{1}=0
and
\phi_{4M}=2\pi
.
The optimization problem in (

[

\psi_{m}=\frac{\pi(m-1)}{2M}~\forall m=1,...,M
