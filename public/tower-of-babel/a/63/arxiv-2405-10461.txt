Prediction in Measurement Error Models
Fei Jianglabel=e1]fei.jiang@ucsf.edu, Yanyuan Malabel=e2]yzm63@psu.edu

Abstract
We study the well known difficult problem of prediction in measurement error models. By targeting directly at the prediction interval instead of the point prediction, we construct a prediction interval by providing estimators of both the center and the length of the interval which achieves a pre-determined prediction level. The constructing procedure requires a working model for the distribution of the variable prone to error. If the working model is correct, the prediction interval estimator obtains the smallest variability in terms of assessing the true center and length. If the working model is incorrect, the prediction interval estimation is still consistent. We further study how the length of the prediction interval depends on the choice of the true prediction interval center and provide guidance on obtaining minimal prediction interval length. Numerical experiments are conducted to illustrate the performance and we apply our method to predict concentration of A ​ β ​ 1 − 42 A\beta 1-42 in cerebrospinal fluid in an Alzheimer’s disease data.

Introduction

Prediction in measurement error models is a notoriously difficult problem.
In fact, prediction has been largely avoided in the measurement error
literature except for a few works
(
Ganse et al., 1983
;
Buonaccorsi, 1995
;
Carroll et al., 2009
;
Datta et al., 2018
)
in special cases,
because the difficulties involved are fundamental and
almost impossible to overcome. Instead, a different notion of
prediction is sometimes used, where although the observed
data contain measurement errors, the “prediction” is conducted for
observed covariates that are error free, thus changing the true notion of
prediction
(
Zhang et al., 2019
;
Zhang et al., 2021
)
.

The difficulty involved in prediction for measurement error models is
rooted in the problem setting itself, in that the task of prediction
involves issues beyond the model estimation itself.
Indeed, even if
the measurement error model is completely known without any unknown
parameters, prediction is still unachievable. To see this, assume we
have response variable
Y
and covariate
X
that is subject to
measurement error. Instead of
X
, we observe
W
, which is linked to

X
via
W=X+U
. A typical and simplest measurement error model specifies the
dependence of
Y
on
X
up to some unknown parameters, say
{\bm{\beta}}
,
and the relation between
W
and
X
. Given independent and
identically distributed (iid) observations
(W_{i},Y_{i}),i=1,\dots,n
, various methods have been developed to estimate the unknown
parameter
{\bm{\beta}}
. The goal of prediction in the measurement
error model context
is to further estimate
Y_{n+1}
based on a
new
W_{n+1}
, while making use of the link between
Y
and

X
and the link between
W
and
X
. This implies that we are
obliged to study the
dependence of
Y
on
W
, which is not readily available even though we
can estimate
{\bm{\beta}}
. To see this,
f_{Y,W}(y,w)=\int f_{Y\mid X}(y,x,{\bm{\beta}})f_{W\mid X}(w,x)f_{X}(x)d\mu(x)
, and without the knowledge
of the probability density function (pdf)
f_{X}(x)
, even after we obtain the estimation
for
{\bm{\beta}}
, we still
cannot obtain
f_{Y\mid W}(y,w)
. Here, we use
f_{Y\mid W}(y,w)
to
denote the conditional pdf of
Y
given
W
, and similarly for other
notations. Of course, as often raised by people who
dismiss measurement error literatures, one can
always directly study the relation between the response
Y
and the
errored covariates
W
. Although it is certainly a
natural and valid idea, this approach gives up the existing
knowledge on the relation between the response and the original
covariate
X
, i.e. the model
f_{Y\mid X}(y,x,{\bm{\beta}})
, hence giving up any inherent
relation between
Y
and
W
as well. Indeed, the
information contained in a completely unspecified function
f_{Y\mid W}(y,w)

and a partially specified function
\int f_{Y\mid X}(y,x,{\bm{\beta}})f_{W\mid X}(w,x)f_{X}(x)d\mu(x)
is not the same, even
though on the surface both contain a single nonparametric component.
In fact, in our
numerical experiments, we found
that taking into account the
existing relation
f_{Y\mid X}(y,x,{\bm{\beta}})
improves the prediction
quite substantially in
comparison to a direct approach to predict
Y
based on
W
.

In this work, instead of
providing a
single prediction value, we consider a prediction interval, or more
generally a prediction set, so that the probability of a future
observation belongs to this set is at least some pre-specified
value. Naturally, if we lower this probability level, the prediction interval will
shrink. Ideally, if the probability level is approaching zero, then the interval
will approach a single point which will be the classic prediction
value.

While the general idea is simple and intuitive, its treatment in
measurement error problems has its own unique features and properties,
which makes the problem interesting and far from being straightforward.
We propose a basic procedure of constructing a prediction
interval (Section

3.1
), derive the efficient (Section
3.2
) and local
efficient (Section
3.3
) influence functions for estimating the
boundary values of the prediction
interval. We also study
how to achieve the best prediction performance when the relationship between
Y

and
W
is directly taken into account (Section
5.1
) and
when the measurement error in
W
is ignored (Section
5.2
).
As far as we are aware, this is the first
prediction method in the measurement error model framework, where the
characteristic of measurement errors is taken into account, and the
prediction is honestly performed under the same data feature for the
future observation as for the observed data. It is also the pioneering
work that studies the variability of the prediction interval, which
provides information about the reliability of a prediction.

2
Model, goal and problem formulation

2.1
Model and goal

Consider the classical measurement error model framework where

f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})
is given up to the unknown parameter

{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
and the observations are
{\bf O}_{i}\equiv(W_{i},{\bf Z}_{i},Y_{i}),i=1,\dots,n
. Here,
{\bf Z}_{i}
is
the error free covariate,
W_{i}
is an errored version of the
error prone covariate
X_{i}
.
We allow any relation
f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})
here, while in practice,

W
often only depends on
X
, for example,
W_{i}=X_{i}+U_{i}
for a random
noise
U_{i}
,
and sometimes the dependence is completely known.
In this case,

{\bm{\beta}}_{2}
vanishes. Let
{\bm{\beta}}=({\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{\rm T},{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in{{\cal R}}^{p}
and assume

{\bm{\beta}}
is identifiable.
Our original goal is to predict
Y_{n+1}
given a new observation

(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
. Practically, we aim to predict a prediction set
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
so that
\hbox{pr}\{Y_{n+1}\in C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})\}\geq 1-\alpha

for a predetermined
\alpha
, such as
\alpha=0.05
. A familiar form
of
C(w,{\bf z})
is
[m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-c_{\alpha}s(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}),m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})+c_{\alpha}s(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})]
, following the practice of
constructing confidence intervals, where
m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
and

s(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
are the estimated conditional mean and conditional standard
deviation of
Y
given
w,{\bf z}
, and
c_{\alpha}
is a constant that is
often the
(1-\alpha/2)
th standard normal quantile in large samples.

Remark 1
.

We made an assumption that
{\bm{\beta}}
is identifiable and all the subsequent
results will build on this assumption. Establishing the
identifiability of
{\bm{\beta}}
often
has to be done case by case based on the specific model,
although many works exist in an attempt to establish
identifiability in more general situations
(
Hu & Schennach, 2008
;
Hu & Schennach, 2013
;
Hu et al., 2015
;
Hu et al., 2016
;
Hu & Sasaki, 2017
;
Hu & Shiu, 2018
;
Hu et al., 2022
)
.

To see
why a general result is difficult to obtain, consider the simple
linear regression model with normal additive error, i.e.

Y=\beta_{c}+\beta_{x}X+\beta_{{\bf z}}^{\rm T}{\bf Z}+\epsilon
,
W=X+U
, where

\epsilon,U
are both normal with zero mean and variances
\sigma^{2},\sigma_{U}^{2}
respectively. This corresponds to
f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z})=\phi\{(y-\beta_{c}-x\beta_{x}-{\bf z}^{\rm T}{\bm{\beta}}_{{\bf z}})/\sigma\}/\sigma
and
f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})=\phi\{(w-x)/\sigma_{U}\}/\sigma_{U}
.
It is known that in this very simple
situation, if both
\sigma_{U}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
are unknown, hence

{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=(\beta_{c},\beta_{x},{\bm{\beta}}_{{\bf z}}^{\rm T},\sigma)^{\rm T}
,

\beta_{2}=\sigma_{U}
, then the problem is not identifiable. However, as
soon as
\sigma
is known, i.e.,

{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=(\beta_{c},\beta_{x},{\bm{\beta}}_{{\bf z}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
,
\beta_{2}=\sigma_{U}
, then the
problem is identifiable. Thus, in practice, it is important to first
inspect the problem to ensure identifiability before proceeding to
perform estimation and prediction. In the situation when the problem
is not identifiable, then one needs to collect additional information
such as repeated measurements or instrumental variables to achieve identifiability.
For example, in the normal additive error case with two repeated
measurements, we can use
W_{i1},W_{i2},i=1,\dots,n
to estimate
\sigma_{U}
, and then treat

W=(W_{1}+W_{2})/2
with known error variance, which leads to the case of

{\bm{\beta}}={\bm{\beta}}_{1}
in the discussion above. In the case of instrumental
variable, say
S
which has the relation to
X
captured by
f_{X\mid S,{\bf Z}}(x,s,{\bf z},{\bm{\alpha}})
, then we can consider the expanded model

f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z},S}(y,x,{\bf z},s,{\bm{\beta}}_{1})=f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})
and
f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z},S}(w,x,{\bf z},s,{\bm{\beta}}_{2},{\bm{\alpha}})=f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})f_{X\mid S,{\bf Z}}(x,s,{\bf z},{\bm{\alpha}})
,
where we treat
({\bf Z}^{\rm T},S)^{\rm T}
as the new error free covariate

{\bf Z}
, and treat
({\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{\rm T},{\bm{\alpha}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
as the new
{\bm{\beta}}_{2}
in
this expanded model.

2.2
Estimation of
{\bm{\beta}}

It is natural to believe that to perform reasonable prediction, we
would first need to estimate
{\bm{\beta}}
.
For such a simple, purely parametric measurement error model,
estimation of
{\bm{\beta}}
is actually not straightforward. The difficulty lies in
the presence of the conditional pdf of
X
given
{\bf Z}
,
denoted as
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
, in practically all estimation
approaches. Unfortunately,
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
is
unknown, difficult to model and even more difficult to
estimate
(
Carroll & Hall, 1988
;
Fan, 1991
)
, hence handling

\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
itself or any quantities dependent on it becomes a very
thorny issue. Fortunately,

Tsiatis & Ma (2004)
eventually bypassed this issue and
prescribed an estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}
. Their method requires a subjectively
posited conditional pdf
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
which does not have to
be equal to or even approximate the true
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
that governs
the data generation procedure. Specifically, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

solves an estimating equation of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf O}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})={\bf 0}
, where
{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}
is the
efficient score of
{\bm{\beta}}
computed under the posited
conditional pdf
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
. To give a more explicit description of

{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
, we write out the detailed construction below.

1.

Posit a working conditional pdf model for
X
given
{\bf Z}
, denote
it
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
.

2.

Compute the working score function

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})=\frac{\partial{\rm log}\int f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})d\mu(x)}{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}.

3.

Solve the integral equation
E[E^{*}\{{\bf a}^{*}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})\mid{\bf O}\}\mid X,{\bf Z}]=E\{{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\mid X,{\bf Z}\}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\int\frac{\int{\bf a}^{*}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})d\mu(x)}{\int f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})d\mu(x)}

\displaystyle\times f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})d\mu(w,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}(y,w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})d\mu(w,y)

to obtain
{\bf a}^{*}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
.

4.

Compute
{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})={\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})-E^{*}\{{\bf a}^{*}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})\mid{\bf o}\}
.

Remark 2
.

In the algorithm above, the only step that may appear nonstandard is
the integral equation solving step. This is a fredholm equation of the
first type and is well studied in numerical analysis
(
Kress, 1999
)
. In
practice, we can simply discretize the problem by writing

a_{i}={\bf a}^{*}(x_{i},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
, and converting the problem into a linear
system solving problem of the form
{\bf A}{\bf a}={\bf b}
, where
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})^{\rm T}
,
{\bf b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})^{\rm T}

with

\displaystyle b_{i}=\int{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}^{*}(y,w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x_{i},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x_{i},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})d\mu(w,y),

and
{\bf A}
is a
m\times m
matrix with

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x_{j},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x_{j},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})\eta_{1}^{*}(x_{j},{\bf z})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x_{j},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x_{j},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})\eta_{1}^{*}(x_{j},{\bf z})}

\displaystyle\times f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x_{i},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x_{i},{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})d\mu(w,y).

The linear system is solved at each different
{\bf z}
value to yield

{\bf z}
-specific
{\bf a}
. Because
m
is often chosen to be small (8-15 in
our experience), the linear system is very easy to solve hence the
overall computation is very fast.

2.3
Prediction problem formulation

After obtaining the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{*}
from
solving
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf O}_{i},{\bm{\beta}})={\bf 0}
,
we proceed to consider constructing a prediction interval.
A classical prediction interval is often of the form
C(w,{\bf z})=[m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})-\zeta,m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})+\zeta]
, where
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
is the
center of the prediction interval and
2\zeta
is the length.
Here, we fix
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
and consider how to obtain
\zeta
.
In other words,
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
is a pre-specified function. For example,
we can imagine

m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})=E(Y\mid w,{\bf z})
if it had been obtainable. We will
study different choices of
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
in more detail in Section
3
.

Let
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})\equiv|y-m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})|
be the distance of
y
to the
center of the prediction interval. Note that

r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
is fully specified once
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
is
fixed, and it can be viewed as regression error or residual if we indeed have

m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})=E(Y\mid w,{\bf z})
.
If we restrict ourselves to the prediction intervals with its center

m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
fixed, then
we can equivalently rewrite the prediction problem as
searching for
\zeta
so that
\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}=1-\alpha
.

3
Performing prediction

3.1
Interval prediction is an estimation problem of
\zeta

At the end of Section
2.3
,
by fixing the functional form of the center of the prediction
interval, we have converted the problem of finding the prediction
region (interval)
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})

to the problem of finding
\zeta
so that

\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}=1-\alpha
.
Although we motivated the
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
function by considering it
as a regression error or residual, the choice of
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
can be
arbitrary. Our formulation of the prediction is also considered in the
conformal prediction literature
and
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
is officially termed conformal score
there
(
Lei et al., 2018
)
. In fact, given an
arbitrary conformal score
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
, we can always define
a prediction region
C(w,{\bf z})
by letting
C(w,{\bf z})\equiv\{y:r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\hbox{pr}\{Y_{n+1}\in C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})\}=\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}=1-\alpha.

To find the prediction region
C(w,{\bf z})
, we only need to find

\zeta
. Note that here
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
can be any pre-specified conformal
score including but not limited to the form of
|y-m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})|
.

An important discovery we make here is to realize that finding
\zeta

such that
\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}=1-\alpha
is a semiparametric estimation problem, where
{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z}),\eta_{2}({\bf z})
are unknown parameters and
\zeta
is a
functional of
{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
implicitly defined.
Here,
\eta_{2}({\bf z})
denotes the marginal pdf
of
{\bf Z}
.
We exploit this view point below to develop procedures to assess

\zeta
differently from any existing literature including conformal
prediction. In the following, we first derive the efficient influence
function of
\zeta
under the ideal setting where the joint
distribution of
X,{\bf Z}
are correctly specified. This
leads to the local efficient
influence function of
\zeta
through replacing the true joint
distribution of
X,{\bf Z}
by a working model. The estimator for

\zeta
is then
obtained by finding the root of the sum of the local efficient
influence functions. We finally study the asymptotic properties of
the estimator of
\zeta
.

3.2
Efficient influence function of
\zeta

In the semiparametrics literature, an important approach to
construct efficient estimator for a parameter is through finding its
efficient influence function. To this end,
to perform efficient estimation of
\zeta
, we note that the
likelihood of a single observation
{\bf o}
can be written as

f_{\bf O}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=\eta_{2}({\bf z})\int\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}}_{2})d\mu(x),

and
\zeta
depends on
{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
implicitly through

\displaystyle\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta}f_{\bf O}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})d\mu({\bf o})=1-\alpha.

We then derive the efficient influence
function for estimating
\zeta
, which we denote
\phi
. We find that

Proposition 1
.

The efficient influence function of
\zeta
is

\displaystyle\phi({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf c}^{\rm T}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})+\frac{E\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}+(1-\alpha)-E[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}]}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]},

where

\displaystyle{\bf c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[E\left\{{\bf S}_{\rm eff}({\bf O}){\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{\rm T}({\bf O})\right\}\right]^{-1}\left(\frac{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}{\partial r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})}/{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}]-E[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})]}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]}\right.

(1)

\displaystyle\left.-E\left[E\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}E\{{\bf a}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})\mid{\bf O}\}\right]\right),

with
{\bf a}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
the same as
{\bf a}^{*}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
defined in Section

2.2
under the true
posited model
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
,
and
a_{1}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
satisfies

\displaystyle E\left[E\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}\mid X,{\bf Z}\right]=E\left[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}\right]-E\left[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid X,{\bf Z}\right].

(2)

Because the proof of Proposition
1
is lengthy and
involved, we provide the detailed derivation and proofs in
Appendix
A.1
. In a nutshell, we first find the tangent space
of the model, which is formed by the tangent spaces
associated with the parameters
{\bm{\beta}}
,
\eta_{1}
and

\eta_{2}
. We then identify the space of all the influence
functions. We finally identify the intersection of the influence
function family and the tangent space to find the efficient influence
function.

Note that (
2
) uniquely determines
E\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}

because the efficient influence function
\phi({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
is unique.
Specifically, through solving (
2
) we obtain
a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
,
which is not necessarily unique, and then we form

E\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}/E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]
, which is the
orthogonal projection of the
efficient influence function
\phi({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
onto the nuisance tangent space
corresponding to
\eta_{1}
,
i.e., the space spanned by all the score functions of
all the parametric submodels of
\eta_{1}
,
hence is a unique function.

3.3
Locally efficient influence function and the estimation of
\zeta

As expected, the efficient score involves both
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
and

\eta_{2}({\bf z})
, which are unknown. While
\eta_{2}({\bf z})
does not involve
unobservable variable hence is standard to estimate, it is not
practical to estimate
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
. Fortunately,
we discover that we can use two
possibly misspecified working models
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z}),\eta_{2}^{\star}({\bf z})
, and the
resulting
\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)

still has mean zero. This is
similar to the construction of the locally
efficient score
{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}
for estimating
{\bm{\beta}}
, which is robust to

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
. Note that to distinguish functions and operations
affected by each working model, we used two slightly different
notations
∗
and
⋆
to denote the two different working models.
Specifically, let

\displaystyle\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf c}^{*\star\rm T}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})+\frac{E^{*}\{a_{1}^{*}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}+(1-\alpha)-E^{*}[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}]}{E^{*\star}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]},

where
a_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
is a function that satisfies

\displaystyle E\left[E^{*}\{a_{1}^{*}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}\mid X,{\bf Z}\right]=E^{*}\left[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}\right]-E\left[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid X,{\bf Z}\right],

(4)

and

\displaystyle{\bf c}^{*\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[E^{*\star}\left\{{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf O}){\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*\rm T}({\bf O})\right\}\right]^{-1}\left(-E^{*\star}\left[E^{*}\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}E^{*}\{{\bf a}^{*}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})\mid{\bf O}\}\right]\right.

(5)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{E^{*\star}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}{\partial r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})}/{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}]-E^{*\star}[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})]}{E^{*\star}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]}\right).

We can see that

\displaystyle E\{\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid X,{\bf Z}\}=0+\frac{(1-\alpha)-E\left[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid X,{\bf Z}\right]}{E^{*\star}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]},

hence indeed
E\{\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\}=0
.

In fact, we can avoid positing a working model
\eta_{2}^{\star}({\bf z})

by estimating all the marginal
expectations involved in
\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
with sample average, i.e.
we can construct

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}^{*}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)=\widehat{\bf c}^{*\rm T}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})+\frac{E^{*}\{a_{1}^{*}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}+(1-\alpha)-E^{*}[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}]}{\widehat{E}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]},

(6)

where
a_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
still satisfies (
4
),
and

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf c}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{E}\left\{{\bf S}_{\rm eff}({\bf O}){\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{\rm T}({\bf O})\right\}\right]^{-1}\left(\frac{\widehat{E}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}\frac{\partial r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})}{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}]-\widehat{E}[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})]}{\widehat{E}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\widehat{E}\left[E^{*}\{a_{1}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}\}E^{*}\{{\bf a}(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})\mid{\bf O}\}\right]\right).

We can easily verify that

\displaystyle E\{\widehat{\phi}^{*}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid X,{\bf Z}\}=0+\frac{(1-\alpha)-E\left[I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid X,{\bf Z}\right]}{\widehat{E}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]},

hence
\widehat{\phi}^{*}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
still has mean
zero.

Finally, because the values of
c^{*\star}
and

E^{*\star}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]
does not affect the mean of

\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
, we can actually replace them by an arbitrary vector
and constant respectively to retain its mean zero property.

We also want to point out that given we already have an estimator

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
based on the procedure described in Section

2.2
, it is reasonable to insert
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
into

\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
. This directly leads to the estimating equation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi^{*\star}({\bf O}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{E^{*}\{a_{1}^{*}(X_{i},{\bf Z}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}_{i}\}+(1-\alpha)-E^{*}[I\{r({\bf O}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}_{i}]}{\widehat{E}[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\}]}=0,

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(E^{*}\{a_{1}^{*}(X_{i},{\bf Z}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\mid{\bf O}_{i}\}+(1-\alpha)-E^{*}[I\{r({\bf O}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid{\bf Z}_{i}]\right)=0,

(7)

and it does not involve the model
\eta_{2}({\bf z})
.
The estimating equation (
7
) in combination with (
4
)
suggests that for the purpose of estimating
\zeta
, we do not need to
concern ourselves with handling
\eta_{2}({\bf z})
.

3.4
Locally efficient interval prediction and its properties

As we have discussed,
the fact that
E\{\phi^{*\star}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)\}=0

implies that we can perform estimation of
\zeta
under the posited
models
\eta_{1}^{*},\eta_{2}^{\star}
, by solving

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi^{*\star}({\bf o}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)=0,

where
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
is a consistent estimator of
{\bm{\beta}}
. Let
the resulting estimator be
\widetilde{\zeta}
. This
is a locally efficient estimator of
\zeta
and it
serves as an alternative procedure to the conformal
prediction procedure in assessing
\zeta
.
Alternatively, we can use
\widehat{\phi}^{*}({\bf o}_{i},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
to construct
estimating equations
instead of using
\phi^{*\star}({\bf o}_{i},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
. Let the resulting
estimator be
\widehat{\zeta}
. In practice, unless we have very good
knowledge on the distribution of
{\bf Z}
, we recommend this
procedure due to its computational convenience. Further, if the
estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
happens to be
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
, i.e. the estimator that
satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}^{*}({\bf o}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})={\bf 0}
, then
\widetilde{\zeta}
and

\widehat{\zeta}
are identical and they both solve (
7
).

Interestingly, to perform
conformal prediction using the locally efficient estimators
\widetilde{\zeta}

and
\widehat{\zeta}
, we do not
need to engage the potential new observation in estimating
{\bm{\beta}}
,
and we do not require data splitting either. In contrast, engaging
potential new data or data splitting is a key requirement in any
conformal prediction procedures. We next provide the
theoretical properties of
\widetilde{\zeta}
and
\widehat{\zeta}
, with the proofs
given in Appendix
A.2
. We also show that the resulting
prediction probability indeed approximates the target value by
providing the bound on the difference in Theorem
2
,
with its proof given in Appendix
A.4
.

.

Let
\widehat{\zeta}

3.5
Conformal prediction estimator of
\zeta

For completeness and to facilitate comparison, we now briefly
summarize the
estimation of
\zeta
in the conformal prediction literature. The
conformal prediction literature includes two general approaches. One
approach involves engaging a potential new observation
W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1},Y_{n+1}=y
and conducting estimation of
{\bm{\beta}}
under each
potential
y
value, hence is computationally intensive and less often
used. The other approach requires data splitting and is easy to
implement, hence is more often recommended as we explain in detail
below.

We randomly
split the data into
two parts, with sizes
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
. One part is used to estimate

{\bm{\beta}}
. Let the size of this
part be
n_{2}
and the data be
{\bf O}_{n_{1}+1},\dots{\bf O}_{n}
. Typically,

n_{2}=o(n)
. Let the estimator be
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
.
The other part of size
n_{1}
is used to
estimate
\zeta
via solving
n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}I\{\zeta-r({\bf o}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})>0\}=(1-\alpha)
. Let the resulting estimator
be
\widecheck{\zeta}
.
To obtain the asymptotic properties of the resulting estimator

\widecheck{\zeta}
, we note that solving the equation is
equivalent to minimizing

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\rho_{1-\alpha}\{r({\bf o}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-\zeta\}
, where

\rho_{1-\alpha}(u)\equiv u\{1-\alpha-I(u<0)\}
is the check
function. So we directly use the standard quantile regression
asymptotic results to obtain

n_{1}^{1/2}(\widecheck{\zeta}-\zeta)\to N\{0,\alpha(1-\alpha)(E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}])^{-2}\}

in distribution, where we used
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\to{\bm{\beta}}
. Further note that

n_{1}/n\to 1
, hence
we get
n^{1/2}(\widecheck{\zeta}-\zeta)\to N\{0,\alpha(1-\alpha)(E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}])^{-2}\}
. For completeness, we also provide a more detailed derivation of the
results in Appendix
A.5
.

Compared to the locally efficient estimators

\widehat{\zeta}
, we will see that
\widecheck{\zeta}
generally leads to larger
variability. In fact, when
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})=\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})
, it is
certain that
\widehat{\zeta}
is efficient hence has the smallest
variability. In our numerical implementations, we find that even when

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
is dramatically misspecified,
\widehat{\zeta}
still
performs similarly to the efficient estimator hence is
more efficient than
\widecheck{\zeta}
.

4
Shortest prediction interval

We have fixed the center of the prediction interval
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
and
focused on estimating
\zeta
so far. As long as the functional form
of
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
is fixed, the true value of
\zeta
is well defined
hence the prediction interval length
2\zeta
is determined. Different
estimators of
\zeta
only leads to different estimated values of the
prediction interval length. Hence, our next question is: what choice of

m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
will lead to the shortest prediction interval,
i.e. the smallest
\zeta
?

To this end, note that

\displaystyle 1-\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{pr}\{Y_{n+1}\in[m(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})-\zeta,m(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})+\zeta]\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[F_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}\{m(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})+\zeta\}-F_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}\{m(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})-\zeta\}],

hence we need to choose
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
so that at any
(w,{\bf z})
,
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})

is the center of the length
2\zeta
interval so that the area under
the conditional pdf
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}({\bf o})
curve restricted to this
interval is the
largest among all choices of length
2\zeta
intervals. Figure

1

provides an illustration of these intervals.
This interval is what is known as the highest density
interval in the Bayesian statistics literature.
Obviously, when
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}({\bf o})
is a symmetric
unimodal function of
y
,
m(w,{\bf z})
is the mode/mean.
However, interestingly, the optimal
m(w,{\bf z})
is not necessarily the mean
function
E(Y\mid w,{\bf z})
in general. In fact,
we cannot give more descriptive statement than the above in
general.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the intervals with the
largest area under the conditional pdf curves.

Of course, in practice, we are faced
with the issue that
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}({\bf o})
is unknown. We suggest to
compute
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf o},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
instead, and then identify the
optimal
m(W,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})
based on
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf o},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
using an
iterative procedure. Specifically, we select an initial
m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
,
for example
m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})=E^{*}(Y\mid W,{\bf Z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
. We then estimate

\zeta
. Based on the
estimator
\widehat{\zeta}
and
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf o},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
, we identify the
optimal
m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
as an updated choice of
m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
. We repeat the
procedure of estimating
\zeta
and updating
m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
,
until
\widehat{\zeta}
does not decrease sufficiently large for our
application purpose.

Clearly, the whole procedure of identifying the optimal

m(w,{\bf z},\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
relies on the posited model

\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)
. If we wish, we can choose several candidate models
and retain the result associated with the smallest
\widehat{\zeta}
. We do
not need to adjust for multiple testing here, since each
probability statement

1-\alpha=\hbox{pr}\{Y_{n+1}\in C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})\}
, regardless
the posited
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
, is valid.

5
Alternative prediction methods

We now prescribe two alternative ways for prediction in the
measurement error models. The first method is a direct approach, where
the relation between
Y
and
X
is completely bypassed. Instead, one
directly inspects the relation between
Y
and
W,{\bf Z}
for
prediction. The second approach is a naive approach, where one treats

W
as if it is
X
. In the prediction interval context, we show that
both methods are consistent, although they have drawbacks in
comparison to the semiparametric method.

5.1
Direct prediction

Because our goal is to predict
Y
based on
W,{\bf Z}
, which are
observed in our data, so a direct approach is to establish the
relation between
Y
and
W,{\bf Z}
and perform prediction.
In this sense, the measurement error issue is completely dismissed.
Specifically, in the direct prediction approach, we assume

Y=m(w,{\bf z})+\epsilon
and we estimate
m(w,{\bf z})
nonparametrically to
form the residuals
r({\bf O})
. We then find the prediction interval by
estimating
\zeta
based
on the same relation
\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O})<\zeta\}=1-\alpha
.
In Appendix
A.6
, we show that in this approach, the
efficient influence function for estimating
\zeta
is

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm direct}({\bf O},\zeta)=\frac{(1-\alpha)-I\{r({\bf O},m)<\zeta\}-\epsilon E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},m)\}\mid W,{\bf Z}]}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O})\}]}.

We subsequently estimate
\zeta
via solving

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\rm direct}({\bf O}_{i},\zeta)=0
.

5.2
Naive prediction

A naive approach in measurement error models is to treat
W
as if it
were
X
and perform the standard analysis. While it is known that
this will lead to an inconsistent estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}
, this is
nevertheless a valid method for prediction interval
construction. Specifically, we can estimate
{\bm{\beta}}
by the usual
regression methods, and then form the prediction interval by
estimating
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\rm naive}({\bf O}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)=0
.
In Appendix
A.7
, we show that the
efficient influence function for estimating
\zeta
is

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm naive}({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}},\zeta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\alpha-I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]}+\epsilon\frac{E[\epsilon I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}\mid W,{\bf Z}]}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]E(\epsilon^{2}\mid W,{\bf Z})}

\displaystyle+\epsilon{\bf m}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})^{\rm T}\left[E\left\{\frac{{\bf m}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}(W,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})^{\otimes 2}}{\hbox{var}(\epsilon^{2}\mid W,{\bf Z})}\right\}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\times E\left(\frac{\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}{\partial r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})}/{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]}+\frac{\epsilon I\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}{\bf m}_{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}(W,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})}{E[\delta\{\zeta-r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})\}]E(\epsilon^{2}\mid W,{\bf Z})}\right).

6
Simulation studies

In our first simulation, we let
{\bf Z}=(1,Z_{1},Z_{2})^{\rm T}
, where
Z_{1}\sim U(0,1)
,

We simulate the data with sample sizes

and
n=500
respectively.

methods to perform prediction.
In all these methods,
we use

.

in each group.

•

m1s:

. We use semiparametric method to

{\bm{\beta}}
and
\zeta
.

•

m1c:

{\bm{\beta}}
and conformal prediction to estimate
\zeta
.

•

m2s:
r({\bf o},m)=|y-\widehat{E}(Y|w,{\bf z})|

m

and the direct method in Section

to
estimate
\zeta
.

•

m2c:
r({\bf o},m)=|y-\widehat{E}(Y|w,{\bf z})|

\zeta
.

•

m3s:
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})=|y-m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})|

.

to

.

We can see that methods

r({\bf o},m)
,
while m2s subsequently uses semiparametrics to estimate
\zeta
,
m2c uses the conformal prediction to do so. Please see Section
A.6

in the Appendix for the details on m2s. Note that methods
m2s and m2c correspond to the approach by those who hold
the view point that measurement error problems do not need to be
treated as long as one directly study the relation between the
response and the observed variables.
Finally, methods m3s and m3c both ignore the presence of the measurement
error and treat
W
as
X
, so are naive methods.
m3s and m3c also differ in
terms of whether prediction is carried out using semiparametric or
conformal prediction, please see Section
A.7

for the details in the semiparametric method.
In implementing all the methods that require a
working model
\eta^{*}
, we adopted the correct
distribution in Simulation 1. Specifically,
we selected 30 grid points
(x_{j},j=1,\ldots,30)
on the support of
X
, and
let the weights be
\eta^{*}(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{30}I(x_{i}=x_{j})\eta_{{\bf x}}(x_{j})/\sum_{j=1}^{30}\eta_{{\bf x}}(x_{j})
, where
\eta_{{\bf x}}
is the density of the
scaled and shifted

{\rm Beta}(2,2)
distribution.

Based on the
n
estimated 90% prediction intervals,
we computed the coverage probability
(CP) and the average length (LPI), and provided the box-plots from the 100
simulations in Figures
2

and
3
. Further, in Table
1
, we
present the mean and standard deviation of the 100 CPs, as well as
the mean and standard deviation of the 100
prediction interval lengths.
We can see that in general, all methods provide coverage close to
the nominal level 90%, hence are all consistent.

In most cases, the prediction intervals of all the consistent estimators
tend to be the shortest in m1s and m1c, where we used the model information.
They are the largest in m3c, where the measurement error issue is naively
ignored, and are in the middle for m2s and m2c, when the model
information is completely ignored.
Within each of the three method classes, the
semiparametric approach leads to better performance, in that the box
is generally narrower, reflecting smaller variability. Among all six
methods, it is quite clear that m1s has the best performance, in terms
of its good coverage, short length and small variabilities.
The observed small variability agrees with our theory, because
when the working model is correct, under the same residual form, the
semiparametric method provides the most efficient estimation of the
prediction interval. Further, as we have pointed out,
among the three semiparametric methods
m1s, m2s, m3s, we can see that m1s has the best performance in
general. This indicates that it is beneficial to make use of the model
information and to take into account the measurement error issue.

Real data analysis

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia and
is characterized by accumulation of amyloid-
\beta
(
A\beta
)
plaques in the earliest phase of the disease

(
Masters & Bateman, 2015
;
Scheltens et al., 2016
)
. Two established
methods for detecting the presence of
A\beta
pathology are
reduced concentrations of
A\beta 1-42
(
A\beta 42
) in
cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and increased retention of
A\beta

positron emission tomography (PET) tracers

(
Mattsson et al., 2017
)
. It is often
assumed that CSF
A\beta 42
and
A\beta
PET can be used
interchangeably, because there are mounting evidences showing that
PET and CSF biomarkers are strongly associated

(
Schipke et al., 2017
;
Leuzy et al., 2016
;
Palmqvist et al., 2015
)
.
It is natural to ask how accurate it
would be if we use one marker, for example
A\beta
PET to predict
the other. Ideally, if one perfectly predicts the other, patients will no
longer need to take multiple examinations, which
reduces the chance of side
effect and lowers patients’ psychological stress. However, prediction based on
neuroimage data is challenging because neuroimage data
are often subject to measurement errors resulting from the data acquisition and
processing steps.

We utilize the prediction interval methods m1s–m3c discussed in
Section
6
to study the performance of using the standardized
uptake value ratio (SUVR) of florbetapir (radiotracer) from the
A\beta
PET
to predict the

A\beta 42
from CSF. We download the preprocessed CSF

(
Shaw et al., 2016
)
and florbetapir PET

(
Landau et al., 2021
)
data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging
Initiative (ADNI) phase 2/Go study, from which we obtain a
subsample with
A\beta 42
values from the CSF tests that were taken
within 30 days of the PET
scans. Furthermore, we only retain the healthy subjects and the subjects with
Alzheimer’s disease who had their diagnoses
within 30 days of the CSF tests. After removing missing data, we
obtain 669
subjects (48.5% female) with an average age of 74.5 years, where
388 are classified as CSF negative subjects at
the risk of cognitive impairment, and among which
220 are subjects with AD. Let
Y
be
the logarithm of
A\beta 42
from CSF,
W
be the SUVR from PET,
{\bf Z}=\{1,{\rm gender},{\rm log}({\rm age})\}^{\rm T}
. The scatter plot
in the left panel of Figure
6

indicates an approximate
quadratic relationship between
Y
and
W
, therefore we
assume
m(X,{\bf Z},{\bm{\beta}})=(X,X^{2}){\bm{\beta}}_{1}+{\bf Z}^{\rm T}{\bm{\beta}}_{2}
,
where
X
is the underlying error free covariate.
The box plots in the right panel of
Figure

6
indicate that there is a significant
difference in CSF values
between healthy (199.748
\pm
51.30) and AD groups (135.465
\pm

37.57), with the
p-value from a student t-test to be less than 0.0001.

We randomly sample two thirds of the subjects to construct
the prediction interval and
evaluate the coverage probability on the remaining one third. We
repeat
the above cross validation process 100 times to
compare the performance of m1s–m3c. In m1s and
m1c, we choose

\eta^{*}
to be a normal probability density function and assign 30
positive masses evenly spaced on
(\widehat{\mu}_{x}-r_{1}\widehat{\sigma}_{x},\widehat{\mu}_{x}+r_{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{x})
. Here
\widehat{\mu}_{x}
is the estimated mean of
X

calculated by the sample average of
W
, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}
is the
estimated variance of
X
calculated by the sample variance
of
W
minus
\sigma_{U}^{2}
. We set
\sigma_{U}
to be 10% of the
standard deviation of
W
based on empirical knowledge and experience.
Furthermore, following
Romano et al. (2019)
we calibrate
the tuning parameters

\sigma_{\epsilon},r_{1},r_{2}
(in m1s, m1c) and the kernel bandwidths (in
m2s, m2c) to allow the coverage probabilities in the test samples to
achieve the nominal 90% level on average in the cross
validation.
Figure
7
indicates that all
six methods are consistent, which yield the coverage probabilities
close to
90%, while the semiparametric methods generally have much smaller
variations in CP and PI.

Discussion

Many interesting problems are worth further research in the prediction
issue for measurement error models. We highlight some here.

We have used the method in
Tsiatis & Ma (2004)
as the
estimator due to its generality and root-
n
rate. Other estimation
methods can certainly be used. When the original
model is nonparametric and semiparametric, various other methods
exist based on deconvolution or Sieve
(
Chen et al., 2008
;
Chen et al., 2009
;
Carroll et al., 2010
)
,
and some even handle high dimensional covariates

(
Jiang & Ma, 2022
;
Jiang et al., 2023
;
Jiang et al., 2024
)
. See

Li & Ma (2024)
for a recent review. These methods can
also be incorporated with the prediction procedures proposed here. When different
estimators are combined with the prediction interval construction,
their subsequent analysis will be different and some could be
challenging.

As we have mentioned, more general choice of the conformal score

r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
is possible
beyond the form
|y-m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})|
. Under the general conformal score

r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
, one can also ask the question: what choice of the
conformal score will lead to the smallest prediction set

C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
, where it satisfies
\hbox{pr}\{Y_{n+1}\in C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})\}=\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}=1-\alpha
.
Note that as soon as the functional form of

r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
is chosen,
different estimators of
{\bm{\beta}}
and
\zeta

only affect the estimated version of
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
. So
identifying the smallest conformal set
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
is not a
statistical problem, but a mathematical problem, which involves
inverting

r({\bf O}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta
to obtain
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
and finding
the optimal
r
which yields a smallest
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
under a
predefined measure.
Unfortunately, this is a very difficult question to answer in
general. To see the potential difficulties, as an example, if we have
set
r({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})=-{\rm log}f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
, then we get

\displaystyle 1-\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{pr}\{-{\rm log}f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf O}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[\hbox{pr}\{-{\rm log}f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf O}_{n+1},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta\mid W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1}\}].

From here, we cannot further conclude that
\zeta
is
directly linked to the size of
C(W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1})
, unless we assume
special features of
f_{Y\mid W,{\bf Z}}^{*}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})
as a function of

y
. Thus we suspect this problem has to be studied case-by-case in
specific concrete models.

Lastly, we comment on the original issue of predicting a single response

Y_{n+1}
given
W_{n+1},{\bf Z}_{n+1}
.
Indeed, at any
m(w,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})
, we can estimate the prediction
interval associated with any
\eta_{1}^{*}(x,{\bf z})
. Thus, we can use the
smallest non-empty
intersection of any of these intervals as a most aggressive prediction
interval. Now if we consider an increasing series of
\alpha
values
that approach 1, we can obtain a series of
intervals, which approximates the single prediction value. This of
course does not necessarily lead to a single value. In fact, we cannot guarantee
that the interval length will approach zero.

Appendix

A.1
Proof of Proposition
1
: the efficient influence function for
\zeta

Let the dimension of
{\bm{\beta}}
be
d_{\bm{\beta}}
.
Let
f_{X\mid{\bf Z}}(x,{\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{1}),f_{\bf Z}({\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{2})
be parametric sub-models
of
\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z}),\eta_{2}({\bf z})
. We can easily see that the score
functions are

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{{\bm{\beta}}}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm log}\int\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})\eta_{2}({\bf z})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int{\partial{\rm log}f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})}/{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}\eta_{1}({\bf x},{\bf z})\eta_{2}({\bf z})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}{\int\eta_{2}({\bf z})\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\frac{\partial{\rm log}f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,X,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})}{\partial{\bm{\beta}}}\mid{\bf O}={\bf o}\right\},

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{\bm{\gamma}_{1}}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm log}\int\eta_{2}({\bf z})f_{X\mid{\bf Z}}(x,{\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}{\partial\bm{\gamma}_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int{\partial{\rm log}f_{X\mid{\bf Z}}(x,{\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{1})}/{\partial\bm{\gamma}_{1}}\eta_{2}({\bf z})f_{X\mid{\bf Z}}(x,{\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{1})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}{\int\eta_{2}({\bf z})\eta_{1}(x,{\bf z})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left\{\frac{\partial{\rm log}f_{X\mid{\bf Z}}(X,{\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{1})}{\partial\bm{\gamma}_{1}}\mid Y=y,W=w,{\bf Z}={\bf z}\right\},

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{\bm{\gamma}_{2}}({\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm log}\int\eta_{1}({\bf x},{\bf z})f_{\bf Z}({\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{2})f_{W\mid X,{\bf Z}}(w,x,{\bf z})f_{Y\mid X,{\bf Z}}(y,x,{\bf z},{\bm{\beta}})dx}{\partial\bm{\gamma}_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm log}f_{\bf Z}({\bf z},\bm{\gamma}_{2})}{\partial\bm{\gamma}_{2}}.

Thus, the tangent space
{\cal T}
, which is the span of all
the score functions
derived above associated with all the possible parametric submodels, is

{\cal T}=\Lambda_{\bm{\beta}}+(\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2})=\Lambda_{\rm eff}\oplus\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
,
where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf c}^{\rm T}{\bf S}_{\bm{\beta}}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}}):{\bf c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\beta}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[E\{a(X,{\bf z})\mid{\bf O}={\bf o}\}:E\{a(X,{\bf z})\mid{\bf z}\}=0\right]

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[a({\bf z}):E\{a({\bf Z})\}=0],

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf c}^{\rm T}{\bf S}_{\rm eff}({\bf o},{\bm{\beta}}):{\bf c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\beta}}}\right\}.

Here, we rewrite
\Lambda_{\bm{\beta}}+(\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2})
into

\Lambda_{\rm eff}\oplus\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
to ease the utility of

{\cal T}
later when we compute the efficient influence function.

Next, we compute the derivatives of
\zeta
with respect to
the parameters
{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2}
associated with an arbitrary
parametric submodel.
Consider

\displaystyle 1-\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{pr}\{r({\bf O},{\bm{\beta}})<\zeta({\bm{\beta}},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\}

\displaystyle=
