ALLIANCE: Spectral solver for kinetic plasma turbulence
Evgeny A. Gorbunov, Bogdan Teaca

Abstract
The ALLIANCE 1 1 1 ALLIANCE - spectrAL soLver for kInetic plAsma turbuleNCE code is developed to solve a new set of four-dimensional electromagnetic drift-kinetic equations in slab geometry [ 1 ] . The nonlinear equations are useful for the study of magnetized plasma systems at scales comparable to, or larger than the ion gyroradius. In particular, it is suited for the study the kinetic turbulent cascade in astrophysical plasma, while preserving finite Larmor radius effects at the fluid-kinetic transition. The equations solved are in spectral Fourier-Laguerre-Hermite form, a pseudo-spectral approach is used for the nonlinear terms, and the code is parallelised over multiple directions. After a presentation of the code, validation runs are shown, and benchmarks for serial and parallel computations are presented.



1
Introduction

In strongly magnetized plasma, gyrokinetic (GK) formalisms
[
2
]
are employed for the study of fusion in laboratory
[
3
,
4
]
and kinetic Alfvén wave turbulence in astrophysical conditions
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Obtained from a Maxwell-Vlasov system of kinetic equations
[
8
]
, one of the basic assumptions in deriving the gyrokinetic formalism is that the fast gyration of the charged particles can be integrated out of the equations of motions, thus reducing the dimensionality of the problem from six to five dimensions. The impact of polarization effects due to the fast gyromotion are taken into account via gyroaveraging operators, which in Fourier space are represented by a simple product with Bessel functions. The physical meaning behind the gyroaveraging operation is to take into account finite Larmor radius (FLR) of the gyrating particles.

GK formalisms have been effectively used for the study of astrophysical plasma turbulence at scales (
\ell\sim 1/k
) of the size of the ion gyroradius (i.e.
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\approx 1
) and smaller. At these scales, the plasma is strictly kinetic and fluid descriptions are insufficient to capture the relevant linear and nonlinear phase mixing. However, since the nonlinear mixing of velocity structures becomes weak at scales above the ion gyroradius, GK equations are becoming computationally expensive considering the relevant physics being solved. Thus, simplified models have been proposed
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
for scales much larger than the ion gyroradius (
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\gg 1
) in an attempt to understand the effective fluid-kinetic system in astrophysical conditions. Such approach is helped by the fact that the ratio
l_{\parallel}/l_{\perp}
between parallel and perpendicular characteristic scales for solar wind, for example, is large
[
13
]
(with
l_{\parallel}\approx 1
AU, and
l_{\perp}=\rho_{i}\approx 10^{2}
km
[
14
]
). Moreover, with in-situ solar wind observations
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
and numerical simulations
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
showing the existence of a so-called spectral "knee", characterized by a spectral break for different quantities at the
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\approx 1
scale, a simplified set of equations that account for this transition at least qualitatively is also desired.

The exact mechanism for the ion Larmor radius transition is still under scrutiny, and requires a model that can allows us to study this and the fluid-kinetic transition in detail, while being more computationally effective compared to GK. In order to study the scales above the
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\approx 1
in a meaningful way and with as little limiting assumptions as possible, one can employ a Fourier-Laguerre-Hermite decomposition
[
21
]
, keep the relevant FLR dynamics that become relevant at
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\approx 1
by taking into account by the first two (0th and 1st) Laguerre moments of the gyrokinetic distribution function, and only then apply the drift-kinetic limit
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\ll 1
. The resulting set of electromagnetic drift-kinetic equations are four-dimensional, with three spatial directions and a parallel velocity one. This new set of four-dimensional electromagnetic drift-kinetic equations in slab geometry that accounts for FLR effects was derived by the authors in
[
1
]
.

In order to solve this drift-kinetic system, the new code
ALLIANCE
was developed and is presented here.
ALLIANCE
evolves in time the first two Laguerre moments of the GK distribution function, with the nonlinear terms being solved using a pseudo-spectral approach for the spatial directions, and the parallel velocity direction being captured via a Hermite decomposition. Such spectral formulation allows for to study of kinetic turbulent cascade in the spatial directions while accounting for parallel velocity mixing, therefore providing a link between fluid and kinetic turbulence.

The paper is organized as follows: in section
2
, we introduce the drift-kinetic equations in the form solved numerically, mentioning all the necessary preliminary information needed to give the reader a clear understanding of the physical system. Next, in section
3
, we talk about the numerical implementation of the equations, such as parallelization and numerical schemes. In section
4
we present initial simulation results, and several numerical tests to validate the code. Single processor and multiprocessor benchmarks are given in
5
. Last, we discuss the results, and propose the possible usage of the code, as well as further developments in section
6
.

2
Drift-kinetic model of plasma

In this section, we present the equations which are solved by
ALLIANCE
. Here, we do not provide the comprehensive derivation of the equations, which can be found in
[
1
]
, and list only the minimum information required for understanding the model used. First, we consider a plasma with particles of species
s
, influenced by a straight strong magnetic guide field, acting in the
z
-direction,
\mathbf{B}=B_{0}\mathbf{\hat{z}}
. For a GK system, the particle perturbed distribution function for species
s
can be found as
\delta f_{s}=-q_{s}\phi F_{s}/T_{s}+h_{s}
, with
h_{s}(\mathbf{R},v_{\parallel},\mu)
being the non-adiabatic part of the GK distribution function.
Using a spectral formulation
[
21
]
, it can be represented by its Laguerre-Hermite moments
h^{m}_{l,s}
as

h_{s}(\mathbf{k},v_{\parallel},\mu)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}\psi^{l}\left(\mu B_{0}\right)\xi^{m}\left(v_{\parallel}\right)h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})

where
\psi^{l}
, and
\xi^{m}
are Laguerre and Hermite functions, respectively
[
21
]
. For a drift-kinetic tendency, seen here as
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}<1
rather than the stringent limit condition
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}\ll 1
, all the high Laguerre moments
l\geq 2
can be omitted as negligibly small. Such simplification preserve finite Larmor radius (FLR) effects, while significantly simplifying the gyrokinetic system
[
1
]
.
The gyrokinetic distribution function moments are related to the gyrocenter distribution function moments via the relation

g^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})=h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})-\frac{q_{s}}{T_{s}}(\chi_{s}^{\phi}+\chi_{s}^{B})\delta_{m0}\delta_{l0}-\frac{q_{s}}{T_{s}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\chi_{s}^{A}\delta_{m1}\delta_{l0}-\frac{q_{s}}{T_{s}}\chi_{s}^{B}\delta_{m0}\delta_{l1}.

(1)

The difference between the
g_{s}
and
h_{s}
can be seen as following: the
g_{s}
describes the point-like particle gyrocentres, while
h_{s}
is the distribution function of the charged rings (see C.5 in
[
11
]
). Relation (
1
) involve gyrokinetic potential functions,

\displaystyle\chi^{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{s00}\phi(\mathbf{k})\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\chi^{B}_{s}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{qB_{0}}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{s10}B_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\chi^{A}_{s}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-v_{T_{s}}\mathcal{J}_{s00}A_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(4)

which are computed via the electromagnetic fields.
Electromagnetic fields are represented by electrostatic potential
\phi(\mathbf{k})
,
parallel magnetic field

B_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})

and parallel component of the vector potential

A_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})
given as:

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}\mathcal{J}_{s00}h^{0}_{0,s}\bigg/\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}^{2}n_{s}}{T_{s}},

(5)

\displaystyle B_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})=-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{s}\frac{n_{s}T_{s}}{B_{0}}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{s10}\left(h^{0}_{0,s}+h^{0}_{1,s}\right),

(6)

\displaystyle A_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\beta}{2k_{\perp}^{2}}\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}v_{T_{s}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{J}_{s00}h^{1}_{0,s}.

(7)

In terms of
g^{m}_{l,s}
the equations for the electromagnetic fields becoming more cumbersome,

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{k})=\frac{2bI_{\phi}(g)-\beta cI_{B}(g)}{2ab+\beta c^{2}},

(8)

\displaystyle B_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})=-\beta\frac{aI_{B}(g)+cI_{\phi}(g)}{2ab+\beta c^{2}}.

(9)

Here, we introduced a number of parameters for ease of notation,

\displaystyle I_{\phi}(g)=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}\mathcal{J}_{s00}g_{0,s}^{0},

(10)

\displaystyle I_{B}(g)=\sum_{s}\frac{n_{s}T_{s}}{B_{0}}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{s10}\left(g_{0,s}^{0}+g_{1,s}^{0}\right),

(11)

\displaystyle a={\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}^{2}n_{s}}{T_{s}}}(1-{\mathcal{J}_{s00}^{2}}),

(12)

\displaystyle b=1+\beta\sum_{s}\frac{n_{s}T_{s}}{B_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{s10}^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle c=\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}n_{s}}{B_{0}}\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{s10}\mathcal{J}_{s00}.

(14)

The relation for
A_{\parallel}
is also modified, and now expressed via
g_{0,s}^{1}
:

A_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}v_{Ts}\mathcal{J}_{s00}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}g_{0,s}^{1}}{k_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}\sum_{s}\frac{q_{s}^{2}n_{s}v_{Ts}^{2}}{T_{s}}\mathcal{J}_{s00}^{2}}\,.

(15)

The FLR effects in eqs.(
2

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{s00}=e^{-b_{s}/2},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{s10}=\left[\left(1-e^{-b_{s}/2}\right)\frac{2}{b_{s}}\right],

(17)

The tilde notation in eq.(

) is used in order to make a clear distinction between the zeroth Laguerre moment of a first order Bessel function
J_{0}(\sqrt{2\mu B_{0}b_{s}})=\sum_{l}\psi_{l}\mathcal{J}_{s0l}
and the zeroth Laguerre moment of the function
2J_{1}(\sqrt{2\mu B_{0}b_{s}})/\sqrt{2\mu B_{0}b_{s}}=\sum_{l}\psi_{l}\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{s1l}
(see
[
1
]

The Bessel function moments (
16

17
) depend on the product
b_{s}=(k_{\perp}\rho_{s})^{2}
, with
\rho_{s}
being the gyration radius of the particle of the species
s

1

17

h^{m}_{l,s}
(

g^{m}_{l,s}

Having established the connection between the
g^{m}_{l,s}
and
h^{m}_{l,s}
moments, we can now introduce the evolution equations in the drift-kinetic limit. While it is possible to propagate in time the moments of gyrokinetic distribution function
h^{m}_{l,s}
, it is more numerically convenient to solve the equations for
g^{m}_{l,s}
- a preferred choice in other codes relying on the GK expansion
[
22
]
. The evolution of the
g^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})
in the drift kinetic limit with
m=\overline{0,M}
Hermite moments, and
l={\{0,1\}}
Laguerre moments is governed by the
M\times 2
system of equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{{g}}^{m}_{0,s}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{0,s}^{m}[h]+\mathcal{L}_{0,s}^{m}[h]+\mathcal{C}_{0,s}^{m}[g]

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g^{m}_{1,s}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{1,s}^{m}[h]+\mathcal{L}_{1,s}^{m}[h]+\mathcal{C}_{1,s}^{m}[g]

(19)

Linear terms describe the local coupling of Hermite moments of
h^{m}_{l,s}
, which include thermal velocities
v_{Ts}=\sqrt{2T_{s}/m_{s}}
:

\displaystyle L_{l,s}^{m}[h]=-ik_{z}v_{T_{s}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{2}}h^{m+1}_{l,s}+\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}}h^{m-1}_{l,s}\right),

(20)

and are dictating parallel dynamics along the unperturbed magnetic guide field
k_{\parallel}=k_{z}
, as well as responsible for development of linear phase mixing in
v_{\parallel}
direction
[
23
]
.
Nonlinear interactions have the following form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{0,s}^{m}[h]=-\frac{1}{B_{0}}\Bigg[\left\{\chi_{s}^{\phi}+\chi_{s}^{B},h^{m}_{0,s}\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{2}}\left\{\chi_{s}^{A},h_{0,s}^{m+1}\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}}\left\{\chi_{s}^{A},h^{m-1}_{0,s}\right\}+\left\{\chi_{s}^{B},h^{m}_{1,s}\right\}\Bigg],

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1,s}^{m}[h]=-\frac{1}{B_{0}}\Bigg[\left\{\chi_{s}^{\phi}+\chi_{s}^{B},h^{m}_{1,s}\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{2}}\left\{\chi_{s}^{A},h_{1,s}^{m+1}\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}}\left\{\chi_{s}^{A},h^{m-1}_{1,s}\right\}+\left\{\chi_{s}^{B},h^{m}_{0,s}\right\}+2\left\{\chi_{s}^{B},h^{m}_{1,s}\right\}\Bigg],

(22)

and are expressed in terms of Poisson brackets,

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\right\}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial\mathcal{B}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial\mathcal{B}}{\partial x},

(23)

describing the perpendicular dynamics of the system,
\mathbf{k}_{\perp}=\{k_{x},k_{y}\}
. Here, nonlinear interactions are mediated by the electromagnetic fields, which enter the entering nonlinear terms in form of the gyrokinetic potentials.

Lastly,
ALLIANCE
incorporates a simple collisional term, which has the only purpose to remove energy from small scales. It’s form is given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{l,s}^{m}[g]=-\left(\nu_{k_{\perp}}k^{2\gamma_{k_{\perp}}}_{\perp}+\nu_{k_{\parallel}}k^{2\gamma_{k_{\parallel}}}_{z}+\nu_{m}m^{\gamma_{m}}\right)g^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k}).

(24)

Collision frequencies in
k_{\perp},k_{\parallel}
and
m
directions are set by
\nu_{k_{\perp}},\nu_{k_{\parallel}},\nu_{m}
. Parameters
\gamma_{k_{\perp}},\gamma_{k_{\parallel}},\gamma_{m}
are provided by the user and are used to define the localization of the dissipation range, and
k_{\perp}=\left(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.
The chosen collision operator (or similar) has been used before in works
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
. It should be noted that when
\gamma_{m}=1
, the collisions in Hermite space become equivalent to Lenard-Bernstein collision operator. As has been shown in
[
27
]
, such collision operator can impact the dynamics of the system significantly. In future works, other collision operators can be implemented as needed, such as in works
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, or anomalous dissipation models (i.e. sub-grid-scale models for the nonlinear terms) can be considered
[
31
,
32
]
.

It should be noted that since collisional operators can introduce velocity space mixing effects depending on their explicit form, for finite collisional frequency cases in particular (i.e. collisional terms become comparable in intensity with the other linear and nonlinear terms), one must take care that any such model (e.g.
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
) is consistent with the approximations used to derive the overall set of drift-kinetic equations. If collisional terms deemed necessary for a particular project violate the approximations made , a GK or full kinetic formalism may be needed
[
33
]
and
ALLIANCE
is not the suitable tool in that case.

3
Numerical implementation

We present next the numerical implementation of our drift-kinetic system. The gyrokinetic distribution function as well as the gyrocenter distribution function moments are represented in memory by 6-dimensional arrays,
g(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z},m,l,s)
and
h(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z},m,l,s)
. Note that in addition to the three wave-space coordinates
(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
and the Hermite coordinate (
m
), which can all be arbitrary large, the Laguerre coordinate is hard-coded to at most the two elements (i.e.
l=0
and
l=1
) needed by the drift-kinetic model. Moreover, the species index
s
track usually the electrons and a single ion species plasma. The data management of the arrays is done with these aspects in mind. The electromagnetic fields are 3-dimensional, with only wave space coordinates
(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
, and the gyrokinetic potentials
\chi(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z},s)
are 4-dimensional. The simulation data is stored on the disk using Hierarchical Data Format (HDF)
[
34
]
.

The choice of space discretization is discussed in the next subsection. Periodic boundaries are assumed for the spatial direction, resulting in a wave-space representation, while a Hermite polynomial representation is used to discretize the parallel velocity direction.

3.1
Space discretization

Given a simulation spatial box boundaries
\{L_{x},L_{y},L_{z}\}
and a spatial resolution
\{N_{x},N_{y},N_{z}\}
, the wave space discretized with smallest wave numbers
\{\Delta k_{x},\Delta k_{y},\Delta k_{z}\}=\{\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}},\frac{2\pi}{L_{y}},\frac{2\pi}{L_{z}}\}
. The
i
-th wave number is then computed as

\displaystyle k^{i}_{x}=i_{x}\Delta k_{x},\,i_{x}=\left(-\frac{N_{x}}{2}+1,...,\frac{N_{x}}{2}\right)\,,

(25)

\displaystyle k^{i}_{y}=i_{y}\Delta k_{y},\,i_{y}=\left(-\frac{N_{y}}{2}+1,...,\frac{N_{y}}{2}\right)\,,

(26)

\displaystyle k^{i}_{z}=i_{z}\Delta k_{z},\,i_{z}=\left(0,...,\frac{N_{z}}{2}\right)\,.

(27)

A reality condition
f(\mathbf{k})=f^{*}(-\mathbf{k})
is taken into account in
k_{z}
direction, allowing to perform computations only for the data above
k_{z}\geq 0
plane. The real discrete forward and inverse Fourier transforms for a function
f(\mathbf{r})
are

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}f(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\widehat{f}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}.

(29)

3.1.1
Fast Fourier Transforms

The nonlinear terms (
21
),(
22
) are computed in real space, which makes it necessary to perform
(2\times N_{m}\times N_{s})
fast Fourier transforms of size
(N_{x}\times N_{y}\times N_{z}/2)
.
The nonlinear terms are dealiased using the 2/3 rule, which must be taken into account when initializing the system size.
That is, the nonlinear term computed in real space on the grid of size
(N_{x},N_{y},N_{z})
. Dealiasing is then performed in
(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
space, with
k_{x_{max}}=2\pi N_{x}/3L_{x},k_{y_{max}}=2\pi N_{y}/3L_{y},k_{z_{max}}=2\pi N_{z}/3L_{z}
.
FFTs are treated by parallel
FFTW
library
[
35
]
, using
fft_mpi_many
real FFT routine. In order to save the device memory and the computational time, the transforms are performed in-place, and give transposed (
(y,x,z)
instead of
(x,y,z)
) real output. FFTs in
ALLIANCE
are implemented for 6D distribution function moments, electromagnetic fields and gyrokinetic potentials. Another application of the FFTs in
ALLIANCE
is to save the real data on the disk.

3.2
Time integration

3.2.1
RK4

Equations (
18
)-(
19
) are propagated in time using the simple order 4 explicit Runge-Kutta scheme. Denoting the RHS of (
18
)-(
19
) as
\mathcal{R}^{m}_{l}[g(\tau),h(\tau)]
at the time
\tau
, we remind the reader the generic algorithm:

\displaystyle K_{1}=\mathcal{R}^{m}_{l}[g(\tau),h(\tau)]\,,

(30)

\displaystyle K_{2}=\mathcal{R}^{m}_{l}\left[g(\tau)+\Delta t\frac{K_{1}}{2},h(\tau)+\Delta t\frac{K_{1}}{2}\right]\,,

(31)

\displaystyle K_{3}=\mathcal{R}^{m}_{l}\left[g(\tau)+\Delta t\frac{K_{2}}{2},h(\tau)+\Delta t\frac{K_{2}}{2}\right]\,,

(32)

\displaystyle K_{4}=\mathcal{R}^{m}_{l}\left[g(\tau)+\Delta tK_{3},h(\tau)+\Delta tK_{3}\right]\,,

(33)

\displaystyle g(\tau+\Delta t)=g(\tau)+\frac{1}{6}\left(K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3}+K_{4}\right)\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\tau=\tau+\Delta t\,.

(35)

In case of the linear simulations, size of the time step
\Delta t
remains constant throughout the run. The necessary requirement for the simulation to be stable and converge, which means that all the eigenvalues of the linear operator (
20
) and, if present, eigenvalues of the collision operator (
24
) must be localized inside the stability region of the numerical method, i.e.
|1+\lambda\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda\Delta t\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}(\lambda\Delta t)^{3}+\frac{1}{24}(\lambda\Delta t)^{4}|\leq 1
, where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the linear or collision operators. An a-priory eigenvalue analysis can be performed by the user.

One of the possible ways to estimate the linear time step constraint, one can use Gershgorin circle theorem. For the linear operator given by eq.(
20
), the maximum absolute value of an eigenvalue
\lambda_{max}
in the system can be estimated as
|\lambda_{max}|\leq\max{(k_{z})}\max{(v_{Ts})}\left[\sqrt{(N_{m}+1)/2}+\sqrt{N_{m}/2}\right]
. The latter can be used to derive
\Delta t
from the stability region constraint of the chosen numerical method.

3.2.2
Adaptive time stepping

In case of nonlinear simulations, the situation is more complex, and the usage of the adaptive time step size is required. In this case, we adapt Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition, widely used for the nonlinear simulations. In order to use the CFL condition, we first rewrite the Poisson brackets in the form

\displaystyle\{A,B\}=\hat{e}_{z}\times\nabla A\cdot\nabla B,

(36)

so we can introduce the following advective velocities:

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{A}=\frac{1}{B_{0}}\hat{e}_{z}\times\nabla\chi^{A},

(37)

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\phi}=\frac{1}{B_{0}}\hat{e}_{z}\times\nabla\chi^{\phi},

(38)

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{B}=\frac{1}{B_{0}}\hat{e}_{z}\times\nabla\chi^{B},

(39)

The CFL condition is now can be written as

\Delta t=\frac{C}{\max(|{\bf u}_{x}|)/\Delta x+\max(|{\bf u}_{y}|)/\Delta y},

(40)

with the Courant number
C=0.5
and the maximum drift velocity chosen as

|{\bf u}|=\max\left\{|{\bf u}^{\phi}|,|{\bf u}^{A}|,|{\bf u}^{B}|\right\}.

(41)

Depending on the physical parameters, nonlinear or linear time step constraint can dominate. However, for the solar wind parameters at 1 AU (
\beta=T_{i}/T_{e}=1
) for proton-electron plasma, one can expect the linear time step constraint to dominate.

[

(i.e. truncated at

), making the choice of

direction, and each processor with the same

During the simulation run, two operations require the exchange of data between processors. The first one is the computation of the linear term, which requires the exchange of border cells in the Hermite direction. If the processor with coordinates
(i_{x},i_{m})
has access to the data slice spanning between the Hermite moments

, then it will send the slice of the distribution function

to the processor
\{i_{x},(i+1)_{m}\}
, and
g(\mathbf{k},M^{i}_{min},l,s)
to the processor
\{i_{x},(i-1)_{m}\}
, in order to compute the linear term. The same communication is performed when computing the nonlinear term, in order to obtain the contribution to the
m
-space exchange due to
\chi^{A}
.
Another data exchange is performed in
k_{x}
during the FFT, and is treated by
FFTW
library.

3.4
Energy injection

When the collision terms are active, if one desires to compensate for the energy dissipation throughout the simulation run, a "forcing" mechanism is needed to inject energy in the system.
ALLIANCE
incorporates a simple forcing mechanism, that ensures constant power
\epsilon
injection throughout the simulation run. First, we define the free energy as in
[
1
]

\displaystyle W=\sum_{s}T_{s}n_{s}\sum_{l}\sum_{k}g^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})\left(h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})\right)^{*}

(42)

And we define the forcing operator as following

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}=\mathcal{F}[h],

(43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[h]=\begin{cases}\alpha h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})\delta_{l,0}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}},&\text{if }k^{f}_{min}<|k_{\perp}|\leq k^{f}_{max}\\
\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

(44)

The forcing of such form only excites zeroth Laguerre moment of the distribution function within the shell
k^{f}_{min},k^{f}_{max}
and the Hermite moment
m^{\prime}
. The user of the code decides on the forcing shell boundaries, and which Hermite moment he desires to force. We now, however, need to define a parameter
\alpha
, a local forcing amplitude at point
(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z},m)
. To do so, we multiply (
43
) by
T_{s}n_{s}h_{l,s}^{m*}
, sum over all the phase space coordinates and species, and obtain the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{dW(t)}{dt}=\alpha\sum_{s}T_{s}n_{s}\sum_{l}\sum_{m}\sum_{k}|h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\delta_{l,0}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}

(45)

using the relation (
42
). Now, considering the constant power injection

\displaystyle\frac{dW(t)}{dt}=\epsilon

(46)

we obtain the relation for the local forcing amplitude as

\displaystyle\alpha=\epsilon\left[\sum_{s}T_{s}n_{s}\sum_{k}|h^{m^{\prime}}_{0,s}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\right]^{-1}.

(47)

Naturally, different forcing mechanisms can also be implemented by the user.

3.5
Free energy diagnostics

ALLIANCE
outputs the full data of the distribution function moments as well as the electromagnetic fields, both in the position and wave space. However, a significant disk space required to store the 6-dimensional array, and the three 3-dimensional arrays makes the frequent output of the raw simulation data unfavourable. Therefore, several reduced-data diagnostics are implemented in
ALLIANCE
, based on the analysis of the free energy, and are computed in the real-time during the simulation run. As in works
[
5
,
36
,
37
]
, the free energy can be computed from the gyrokinetic distribution function and the electromagnetic fields as

\displaystyle W=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\sum_{m}W_{h}(\mathbf{k},m)-W_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})+W_{B_{\parallel}}(\mathbf{k})+W_{B_{\perp}}(\mathbf{k})\right]\,,

(48)

which includes the contributions from the free energy density functions
W_{h}(\mathbf{k},m)=\sum_{s}W_{h,s}(\mathbf{k},m)
and the fields defined as

\displaystyle W_{h,s}(\mathbf{k},m)=T_{s}n_{s}\sum_{l}\frac{|h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{2}\,,

(49)

\displaystyle W_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{s}\frac{q^{2}_{s}n_{s}|\phi(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{2T_{s}}\,,

(50)

\displaystyle W_{B_{\parallel}}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{|B_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{8\pi}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle W_{B_{\perp}}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{k^{2}_{\perp}|A_{\parallel}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{8\pi}\,.

(52)

We define several diagnostics for the free energy.

\bullet
Free energy channels
: we compute each contribution to the free energy from the free energy spectral-density functions (
49
)-(
52
) as
W_{h}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{m}W_{h}(\mathbf{k},m)
,
W_{\phi}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}W_{\phi}(\mathbf{k})
,
W_{B_{\perp}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}W_{B_{\perp}}
.

\bullet
Free energy spectra
:
We also compute spectral quantities of each free energy contribution in
k_{\perp}
. Each

\displaystyle W^{spec}_{\phi,B_{\parallel},B_{\perp}}(k_{\perp})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{k}_{shell}}\frac{1}{N_{shell}}W_{\phi,B_{\parallel},B_{\perp}}(\mathbf{k}),

(53)

and for for the entropic contribution of the
h^{m}_{ls}(\mathbf{k})
to the free energy the two-dimensional spectra is computed in
(k_{\perp},m)
space as

\displaystyle W^{spec}_{h}(k_{\perp},m)=\sum_{s}T_{s}n_{s}\sum_{l}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{k}_{shell}}\frac{1}{N_{shell}}\frac{|h^{m}_{l,s}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{2},

(55)

and the total free energy spectra is computed as sum over the individual contributions

\displaystyle W^{spec}(k_{\perp})=\sum_{m}W^{spec}_{h}(k_{\perp},m)-W^{spec}_{\phi}(k_{\perp})+W^{spec}_{B_{\parallel}}(k_{\perp})+W^{spec}_{B_{\perp}}(k_{\perp})

(57)

There are two options to the spectral shell initialization in
ALLIANCE
. The first one utilizes equidistant shells, with the shell boundaries
k^{i}_{shell}
and the shell centres
k^{i}_{center}
computed as

\displaystyle k^{i}_{shell}=k_{0}\cdot i,\quad i\in\{0,N_{shells}\},

(59)

\displaystyle k^{i}_{center}=\frac{1}{2}\left(k^{i}_{shell}+k^{i+1}_{shell}\right),

(60)

The amount of the wave vectors falling inside one shell is then computed for the each shell. For this choice, size of the unit shell
k_{0}
is provided by user, and a number of shells
N_{shells}
is computed automatically from the size of the simulation box. Second choice utilizes the logarithmically spaced shells, each computed using the golden ratio relation

\displaystyle k^{i}_{shell}=\varphi^{\frac{i}{2}}\cdot k_{0},\quad i\in\{0,N_{shells}\}

(61)

\displaystyle\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},

(62)

For this choice of the shell spacing, user also have to provide only size of the unit shell
k_{0}
.

\bullet
Nonlinear flux spectra
:
Nonlinear flux spectra uses the same shell disctretization of the wave space as any other spectral computation in the code. In fact, the nonlinear flux of the free energy through scale
k_{c}
is computed as

\displaystyle\Pi(k_{c})=\frac{1}{N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}}\sum_{s}T_{s}n_{s}\sum_{l}\sum_{m}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}N^{m}_{l,s}[h][{h^{m*}_{l,s}}]^{\leq}_{k^{c}_{\perp}}(\mathbf{k})

(64)

The operator
[*]^{\leq}_{k^{c}_{\perp}}
denotes sharp low-pass filtering
[
36
]
over the cut-off wave number
k^{c}_{\perp}
. In order to obtain the nonlinear flux,
ALLIANCE
computes the nonlinear term only once during the function call, and then performs several computations of
[{h^{m}_{l,s}}]^{\leq}_{k^{c}_{\perp}}(\mathbf{k})
. It is should be noted, however, that it is possible to compute the nonlinear flux in the position space as well, as it was done in work
[
36
]
.

\bullet
Dissipative and integral scale lengths
: we compute dissipative and integral length scales as

\displaystyle L_{int}=2\pi\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{k}}1/k_{\perp}W^{spec}(k_{\perp})}{\sum_{\mathbf{k}}W^{spec}(k_{\perp})}\,,

(65)

\displaystyle L_{dis}=2\pi\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{k}}k^{2\gamma_{\perp}-1}_{\perp}W^{spec}(k_{\perp})}{\sum_{\mathbf{k}}k^{2\gamma_{\perp}}_{\perp}W^{spec}(k_{\perp})}\,.

(66)

Such estimates allow to form an assumption on the largest length scale of the system and the scale where the dissipation peeks in intensity.

In this section, we investigate the performance of the

5.2
Parallel scalability

The parallel performance of

(b). As for the strong scaling, performance degrades up to 64 processors due to increase of inter-processor communications, but from 64 up to 256 there is no significant change in the wall-clock time.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, the new pseudo-spectral code
ALLIANCE
was presented. The code integrates numerically the new set of the drift-kinetic equations
[
1
]
. Several diagnostics that are implemented have been presented. To facilitate the study of the turbulent cascade, the simple forcing and dissipation mechanisms that are implemented have been presented. The structure of the code, however, allows for an easy modification of the form of those mechanisms, as needed. Initial test runs, both linear and nonlinear were performed, showing the ability of the model to capture the linear phase mixing and allow for turbulence development.

To solve the equations,
ALLIANCE
uses explicit Runge-Kutta of order 4 scheme, allowing excellent accuracy for both linear and nonlinear terms. For the linear simulations, the error scales as
\propto dt^{5}
. For the nonlinear term, the CFL condition is employed to ensure numerical stability of the nonlinear simulations.

The algorithm demonstrates ideal single processor scaling, showing expected asymptotic behaviour with increase of the system size for all the dimensions.
ALLIANCE
also demonstrates great parallel performance, for which further optimizations will be considered. Moreover, possible further developments of the
ALLIANCE

include transferring of the core functions on GPU similar to other modern codes
[
26
]
, as well as using pencil domain decomposition for FFT transforms in the future to allow better scalability at large supercomputing clusters (
>10^{3}
processors). One of the advantages of the
ALLIANCE
is, however, that it is being highly portable, with ability to run on a large range of machines, from personal computers to high-performance supercomputing clusters.

The equations used at the core of
ALLIANCE
are specifically designed to be a link between kinetic and fluid description for plasma, thus allowing to study different physical phenomena. The pseudo-spectral approach used along with Hermite decomposition of the parallel velocity direction allows to study kinetic turbulent cascade and its interplay with linear phenomena such as Landau damping, and it can facilitate fundamental studies similar to
[
40
,
19
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
, which can offer insight into the fundamental nonlinear structure of magnetized plasma turbulence, especially when a specific scale (i.e.
k_{\perp}\rho_{i}=1
) has a privileged nonlinear impact. The
ALLIANCE
code can also help to shed light on the knee break in the solar wind spectra, it can be used to study how large-scale dynamics of plasma affects micro scales, or how fluid constraints at large scales impact the fluid-kinetic turbulent transition of plasma (seen as the point when velocity space dynamics become important).

Apart from this, the connection between gyrofluid moments and the gyrokinetic distribution moments provided here allows to use
ALLIANCE
to model gyrofluid system, provided with appropriate closures for the system. Thus, using
ALLIANCE
