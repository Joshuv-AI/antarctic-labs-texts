Sharp concentration phenomena in high-dimensional Orlicz balls
Lorenz Frühwirth, Joscha Prochno

Abstract
In this article, we present a precise deviation formula for the intersection of two Orlicz balls generated by Orlicz functions V V and W W . Additionally, we establish a (quantitative) central limit theorem in the critical case and a strong law of large numbers for the " W W -norm" of the uniform distribution on 𝔹 ( n , V ) \mathbb{B}^{(n,V)} . Our techniques also enable us to derive a precise formula for the thin-shell concentration of uniformly distributed random vectors in high-dimensional Orlicz balls. In our approach we establish an Edgeworth-expansion using methods from harmonic analysis together with an exponential change of measure argument.

Introduction & Main results

Let
n\in\N,1\leq p<\infty
and let
\mathbb{D}^{(n,p)}
be the volume normalized
\ell_{p}
-ball in
\R^{n}
. In
[
46
]
Schechtman and Schmuckenschläger studied the asymptotic behavior of the intersection of
\mathbb{D}^{(n,p)}
with an appropriately scaled
\ell_{q}
-ball and found the threshold behavior

\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{D}^{(n,p)}\cap t\mathbb{D}^{(n,q)}\right)\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\begin{cases}0,&t<A_{p,q}^{-1}\\
1,&t>A_{p,q}^{-1},\end{cases}

(1)

where
A_{p,q}\in(0,\infty)
is an explicit constant. Their proof mainly relied on a probabilistic representation of the uniform distribution on
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls obtained by Schechtman and Zinn
[
47
]
and independently by Rachev and Rüschendorf
[
44
]
). More precisely, for
X^{(n,p)}\sim\mathbb{D}^{(n,p)}
, we can write

X^{(n,p)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U^{1/n}\frac{\left(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\right)}{||\left(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\right)||_{p}},

(2)

where
U\sim\U([0,1])
and
(Y_{i})_{i\in\N}
are independent and identically distributed (iid) with Lebesgue density

\R\ni x\mapsto\frac{1}{2p^{1/p}\Gamma(1+1/p)}e^{-|x|^{p}/p}.

It is then possible to represent the volume in (
1
) as the probability that a random vector, which is uniformly distributed in the (normalized)
\ell_{p}^{n}
-ball, has
\ell_{q}
-norm smaller than
t
. The probabilistic representation of
X^{(n,p)}
allows us to work with independent random variables and thus the limit in (
1
) is essentially an application of the strong law of large numbers. The threshold case
t=A_{p,q}
first remained an open problem before Schmuckenschläger proved a central limit theorem for the
\ell_{q}
-norm of a point chosen randomly in an
\ell_{p}^{n}
-ball
[
49
]
. The existence of a probabilistic representation as in (
2
) gave rise to the rich and flourishing theory of high-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-geometry, which was intensively studied over the past 15 years (we refer the interested reader to the surveys
[
41
,
43
]
). One aim of the present paper is to generalize the Schechtman–Schmuckenschläger result from (
1
) in a different direction. All mentioned results above heavily use the representation in (
2
), which is limited to
\ell_{p}
-balls. Here we want to study concentration phenomena like (
1
) for the intersection of so-called Orlicz balls. Typically, taking a symmetric and convex function
V:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
V(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
and some
R\in(0,\infty)
, the associated Orlicz ball with "radius"
R
is defined as

\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}:=\left\{x\in\R^{n}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})\leq nR\right\};

(3)

in this work we deal with a slightly more restrictive notion of an Orlicz function (see Definition
2.1
and Remark
2.2
). Orlicz spaces serve as natural generalizations of
\ell_{p}
-spaces and are fundamental elements within the class of symmetric Banach sequence spaces. They have been intensively studied
in the functional analysis literature and we direct the interested reader to
[
20
,
26
,
35
,
36
,
50
,
42
,
45
]
and the references cited therein. In
[
22
]
, using the maximum entropy principle from statistical mechanics and elements from (sharp) large deviations theory, Kabluchko and Prochno were able to bypass the non-existence of a classical Schechtman–Schmuckenschläger-type probabilistic representation and computed the asymptotic volume of high-dimensional Orlicz balls; independently similar problems were considered by Barthe and Wolff in
[
8
]
with a view towards confirming the Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits (KLS) spectral gap conjecture. Although not stated explicitly it follows from the proof of
[
22
, Theorem B]
that the convergence rate is exponential. In our Theorem
A
we are able to present a precise version of
[
22
, Theorem B]
, which also includes lower-order terms and could not be obtained by the approach developed in
[
22
]
. The study of moderate and large deviations principles in the realm of asymptotic geometric analysis has been relatively recent, pioneered by Gantert, Kim, and Ramanan in
[
18
]
, and further developed by Prochno, Kabluchko, and Thäle in
[
25
]
. In their recent work
[
4
]
, Alonso-Gutiérrez, Prochno, and Thäle uncovered a connection between the investigation of moderate and large deviations for isotropic log-concave random vectors and the famous KLS conjecture. Unlike central limit theorems, (sharp) large deviation principles are highly sensitive to the distribution of the underlying random variables.
Although large deviations principles (LDPs), unlike concentration results or large deviation upper bounds, precisely identify the asymptotic exponential decay rate and allow for the identification of conditional limit laws (see
[
16
,
29
]
), they have a notable drawback. In general, LDPs only provide approximate estimates of the probability that a sequence of random variables lies in an atypical region. Specifically, they characterize the limit of the logarithms of the deviation probabilities as the dimension
n
tends to infinity. Sharp LDPs, on the other hand, not only provide the precise exponential decay but also include additional subexponential factors, offering more accurate estimates and are more in the spirit of asymptotic geometric analysis.

While numerous articles investigate the asymptotic behavior of high-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-balls and spheres (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
17
,
18
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
27
,
29
]
), the study becomes more nuanced and delicate when extending to high-dimensional Orlicz balls. This can be seen in the work of Kabluchko and Prochno
[
22
]
and the complexity stems from the absence of a probabilistic representation for the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}
and because general Orlicz functions are not homogenous. The former problem can be resolved by employing suitable Gibbs measures
\mu_{\alpha}
, distributed according to the Lebesgue density

\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}(x)=\frac{e^{\alpha V(x)}}{Z_{\alpha}},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\mathbb{R},

where, in statistical mechanics terminology,
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)
is referred to as the inverse critical temperature, and
Z_{\alpha}
is the partition function, i.e., the normalizing constant; this approach was initiated in
[
22
]
and has led to successful breakthroughs in addressing geometric questions within high-dimensional Orlicz spaces by probabilistic means (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
16
,
21
,
28
,
29
]
).
Turning to sharp large deviations, those can be traced back to Bahadur and Rao
[
7
]
and Petrov
[
40
]
(see also
[
15
]
). Essentially, they used an exponential change of measure to identify the exponential decay of the probability that the empirical mean of iid random variables exceeds their common mean. The residual term can then be approximated using a first-order Edgeworth expansion (see, e.g.,
[
40
, p. 293]
). The proof of Theorem
A
adopts a similar approach, but encounters certain challenges in establishing an Edgeworth expansion for the resulting sum of random variables (see Section
3.2
and the discussion therein). After identifying the precise asymptotic formula for the Schechtman–Schmuckenschläger ratio (
1
) in the case of Orlicz balls, one may raise questions about its behavior in the critical case; note that this question could not be addressed by the methods in
[
22
]
. This inquiry is addressed in our Theorem
B
, where we demonstrate that the normalized volume intersection for high-dimensional Orlicz balls adheres to a quantitative Central Limit Theorem with a speed of order
n^{-1/2}
, analogous to the classical Berry–Esseen theorem.

Our techniques also allow to give a precise description of the so-called thin-shell concentration of high-dimensional Orlicz balls (see Theorem
C
). Roughly speaking, for small values of
\delta\in(0,\infty)
, we provide a precise formula for

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\|X^{(n,V)}\|_{2}^{2}}{n}-m\right|\geq\delta\right],

(4)

where
X^{(n,V)}
follows a uniform distribution on an Orlicz ball generated by the Orlicz function
V
. Estimating probabilities of the form (
4
) has attracted considerable attention since the works of Sudakov
[
51
]
, Diaconis and Freedman
[
14
]
, von Weizsäcker
[
52
]
, and Antilla, Ball, and Perissinaki
[
6
]
. We also highlight the breakthrough result by Klartag
[
30
]
, who proved that the marginals of an isotropic random vector are close to a Gaussian distribution. In
[
6
]
, it was established that any isotropic (with mean
0
and identity covariance matrix) random vector
X^{(n)}\in\R^{n}
has (approximately) Gaussian marginals if
\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n}
concentrates around
1
. In other words,
||X^{(n)}||_{2}
concentrates in a thin-shell with radius
\sqrt{n}
. This principle has led to the thin-shell width conjecture, which proposes that there exists an absolute constant
C\in(0,\infty)
such that for every
n\in\N
and every
isotropic random vector
X^{(n)}\in\R^{n}
one has
\mathbb{E}\left[\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}-\sqrt{n}\right]^{2}\leq C
. This is equivalent to the famous variance conjecture, first formally proposed in
[
10
]
(see also
[
1
]
). It asserts the existence of an absolute constant
C\in(0,\infty)
such that for any isotropic random vector
X^{(n)}\in\R^{n}
, it holds that
\mathbb{V}\left[\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq Cn
. The variance conjecture has been successfully approached for various important examples of isotropic convex bodies (see, e.g.,
[
53
]
,
[
34
]
and the references cited therein). The variance conjecture is a special case of the renowned KLS conjecture, which roughly says that, up to a universal constant
C\in(0,\infty)
independent of the dimension
n
, the best way to divide a convex body into two pieces of equal mass in order to minimize their interface is a hyperplane bisection. For a long time the best known bound was obtained by Bourgain
[
11
,
12
]
where it is proven that
C=O\left(n^{1/4}\log n\right)
. A few years ago Chen
[
13
]
accomplished a breakthrough by obtaining a subpolynomial bound for the KLS constant. This work led to great further improvements (e.g., by Klartag and Lehec
[
32
]
) with the currently best known estimate for the KLS constant of
O(\sqrt{\log n})
obtained by Klartag in
[
31
]
(see also the recent survey
[
33
]
for a more comprehensive discussion on the KLS conjecture and related topics).

Returning to thin-shell concentration estimates, we mention that in 2018, Lee and Vempala
[
37
]
proved that for
t\in(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}\right]}-1\right|\geq t\right]\leq e^{-c\min\left\{t,t^{2}\right\}\sqrt{n}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}\left[\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}-\sqrt{n}\right]^{2}\leq C\sqrt{n};

another estimate of a similar order was obtained by Guédon and Milman
[
19
]
(see also
[
39
]
,
[
48
]
, and
[
2
]
for more precise estimates in case of
\ell_{p}
-balls). More recently, Prochno and Alonso-Gutierrez
[
3
]
, using a moderate deviations perspective, were able to provide precise estimates for probabilities of the form

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}^{2}}{nL_{Z}^{2}}-1\right|\geq t_{n}\right],

where
X^{(n)}
is uniformly distributed in a high-dimensional Orlicz ball
\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}
,
L_{Z}^{2}
is the asymptotic value of the isotropic constant (see also
[
3
, Theorem A]
) and
(t_{n})_{n\in\N}
is a sequence that grows to infinity slower than
\sqrt{n}
. Their approach requires the Orlicz function
V
to grow sufficiently fast; more precisely, they assumed that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{V(x)}{x^{2}}=\infty.

In our Theorems
A
and
C
, we are able to weaken this assumption and we only require
V
to grow at least as fast as
x\mapsto x^{2}
(see also Assumption
A
).

1.1
Main results

Before stating our main results, Theorem
A
,
B
and
C
, we introduce a few quantities. An Orlicz function is a symmetric and convex function
V:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
V(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
, which is essentially
\mathcal{C}_{2}
(see Section
2.2
for a precise definition). The associated Orlicz ball
\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}
with radius
R\in(0,\infty)
in
\R^{n}
is then defined as in (
3
). For
n\in\N
and
R\in(0,\infty)
, let
X^{(n,V)}\sim\UOrlR
. Then, as shown in Theorem
B
, for any (linearly independent) Orlicz functions
V
and
W
, the almost sure limit

m:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)\in(0,\infty)

(5)

exists.

Let
V,W:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be two Orlicz functions and, for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\R^{2}
, we define a measure
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
via the Lebesgue density

\frac{d\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{dx}=\frac{e^{\alpha V(x)+\beta W(x)}}{Z_{\alpha,\beta}},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\R,

provided
Z_{\alpha,\beta}:=\int_{\R}e^{\alpha V(x)+\beta W(x)}dx<\infty
. Gibbs measures of this or a similar form have proven to be very useful when dealing with the asymptotic behavior of high-dimensional Orlicz balls (for more background on the maximum entropy principle for Orlicz spaces, we refer to Section 1.2 in
[
22
]
). Moreover, we define
\varphi:\R^{2}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
with

\varphi(\alpha,\beta):=\log Z_{\alpha,\beta}.

Let
\I:\R^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
be the Legendre transform of
\varphi
, i.e., for
(x,y)\in\R^{2}
, we set

\I(x,y):=\sup_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\R^{2}}\left[\alpha x+\beta y-\varphi(\alpha,\beta)\right],

(6)

which is well defined since
\varphi
is convex.

Assumption A
.

Let
V,W:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be two Orlicz functions. We say
V
and
W
satisfy Assumption
A
, if

\liminf_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{V(x)}{W(x)}>0

Assumption
A
essentially means that
W
grows at most as fast as
V
.

Assumption B
.

Let
V,W:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be two Orlicz functions. We say
V
and
W
satisfy Assumption
B
, if

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{V(x)}{W(x)}=\infty.

Assumption
B
requires
V
to grow much faster than
W
and we note that Assumption
B
implies
A
. We highlight that similar conditions to Assumption
B
were already imposed in, e.g.,
[
3
,
29
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

For example, Assumption
B
is satisfied for
V(x)=|x|^{p}
and
W(x)=|x|^{q}
if
p>q\geq 1
. The Orlicz functions
V(x):=x^{4}+x^{2}
and
W(x):=x^{4}
satisfy Assumption
A
, but not Assumption
B
(see also Remark
3.4
, where we demonstrate the necessity of distinguishing between Assumptions
A
and
B
!).

In the theory of large deviations, functions as
\I
from (
6
) are usually referred to as good rate functions (GRF). This means that all level sets of
\I
are compact.

Theorem A
.

(i)
Let
R\in(0,\infty)
and
V,W:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be linearly independent Orlicz functions that satisfy Assumption
A
. Then there exists an
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
t\in(m,m+\varepsilon)
, we have

\frac{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\cap\mathbb{B}_{t}^{(n,W)}\right)}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}=1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma}e^{-n\I(R,t)}(1+o(1)),

(7)

where
m\in(0,\infty)
is the constant from (
5
),
\I
is the GRF from (
6
) and
\sigma=\sigma(V,W,R,t)
is an explicit positive quantity.

(ii)
If
V,W:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfy Assumption
B
, then (
7
) holds for all
t\in(m,W(V^{-1}(R))
.

Remark 1.2
.

We observe that in the case of
W(x):=|x|^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
, we can express

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\left|X^{(n,V)}\right|\right|_{p}^{p}\geq t\right]

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\cap\mathbb{B}_{t}^{(n,W)}\right)}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}.

Hence, Theorem
A
generalizes
[
27
, Theorem 11]
and provides a refinement of
[
22
, Theorem B]
. In several related works (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
38
]
) the admissible values of
t
are non-explicit. In Theorem
A

(ii)
we can identify the explicit interval
(m,W(V^{-1}(R)))
such that the sharp asymptotic formula given in (
7
) holds for all values of
t
in that interval. We consider this to be a non-trivial feature of our result (see the proof of Lemma
3.2
).

As in case of
\ell_{p}
-balls, we see that the "
W
-norm" of a uniform distribution on a
V
-Orlicz ball
\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}
concentrates around a critical value
m\in(0,\infty)
. The following theorem shows that this concentration happens almost surely and, under a suitable normalization, we obtain a Berry–Esseen-type result, i.e., a quantitative central limit theorem. We denote the Kolmogoroff distance by
d_{\text{Kol}}
(see Equation (
11
) for a formal definition).

Theorem B
.

(Strong law of large numbers and CLT)

Let
V,W:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be linearly independent Orlicz functions. For
n\in\N
and
R\in(0,\infty)
, let
\UOrl\sim\UOrlR
, then the limit

m=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)

(8)

exists almost surely with
m=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha^{*},0}}[W(X)]
, where
\alpha^{*}\in(0,\infty)
is chosen such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha^{*},0}}[V(X)]=R
. Moreover, we can even give a quantitative version of the limit above: there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

d_{\text{Kol}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right],Z\right)\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}},\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\N,

(9)

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{*}^{2})
and
\sigma_{*}^{2}\in(0,\infty)
is a different quantity as in Theorem
C
.

Moreover, the result remains valid if we choose
V(x)=|x|^{p}
and
W(x)=|x|^{q}
for some
p,q\in(0,1)
with
p\neq q
.

Remark 1.3
.

It’s worth noting that Theorem
B
holds true for functions
V(x)=|x|^{p}
and

, where

). We anticipate that the techniques developed in Section

could apply to a broader class of functions beyond those considered in Theorem

, which could be of independent interest.

\frac{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\cap\mathbb{B}_{m}^{(n,W)}\right)}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{2},

where

is the quantity from (

Another valuable application of our techniques is the following thin-shell concentration result, which complements

Theorem C
.

(Sharp thin-shell concentration)

Let
R\in(0,\infty)
,

2.1
Notation

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable and
\mu
be some distribution. If
X
is distributed according to

. We denote the Dirac-measure in some point

\delta_{x}

. Let

\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\Sigma\right)
. Throughout this article we will only see the cases
k=1
or
k=2
. Let
X
and
Y
be random variables. The
Kolmogoroff distance
between
X
and
Y
is defined as

d_{\text{Kol}}(X,Y):=\sup_{t\in\R}\left|\mathbb{P}\left[X\leq t\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[Y\leq t\right]\right|.

(11)

Let
I\subseteq\R
be an interval with endpoints
a,b
. In Section
3.2
we will frequently use the notation
|I|=b-a
to indicate the length of

. Let
A\subseteq\R^{d}

.
Let

be an open non-empty set and

, the gradient of

(b_{n})_{n\in\N}

2.2
Orlicz functions & Gibbs measures

Definition 2.1
.

A symmetric, convex function
V:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
V(x)=0
iff
x=0
is called an Orlicz function if
V
is two times continously differentiable. In fact, we only need that
V
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
except of countably many points that do not accumulate anywhere. We recall the definition of the associated Orlicz ball
\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}
of radius
R\in(0,\infty)
with

\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}=\left\{x\in\R^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})\leq Rn\right\}.

Remark 2.2
.

Our notion of an Orlicz function is slightly more restrictive than usual, since we require that
V
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
except of a discrete set of points. Often (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
22
]
)
V
is assumed to be just convex, which by Alexandrov’s theorem, implies that
V
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
almost everywhere. The set of points where
V
is not
\mathcal{C}^{2}
could still be dense. Here we exclude such examples. We believe that this assumption could be overcome with a significant amount of technical work in Section
3.2
, which we have chosen not to undertake.

The uniform distribution of the intersection of two Orlicz balls
\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}
and
\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,W)}
is naturally related to two-factor Gibbs measures
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
defined via the Lebesgue density

\frac{d\mu_{\alpha,\beta}}{dx}(x)=\frac{e^{\alpha V(x)+\beta W(x)}}{Z_{\alpha,\beta}},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\R,

(12)

where

Z_{\alpha,\beta}=\int_{\R}e^{\alpha V(x)+\beta W(x)}dx

is the normalizing constant and
(\alpha,\beta)\in\R^{2}
are chosen in a way such that
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
exists. For instance, if we choose
\alpha=-1
and
\beta=0
, we always have that
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
exists since
V
is convex as Orlicz function.

3.2
Establishing an Edgeworth expansion

In this subsection we prove that, for linearly independent
V,W
, the sequence of random variables

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(V(X_{i}),W(X_{i})\right),

(16)

where
(X_{i})_{i\in\N}
is an iid sequence with
X_{1}\sim\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
, satisfies an Edgeworth expansion. To that end, we need to establish a Cramér-type bound for the characteristic function
\varphi:\R^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of (
16
) given by

\varphi(t,s):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha,\beta}}\left[\exp\left(itV(X)+isW(X)\right)\right],\hskip 10.00002pt(t,s)\in\R^{2}.

In the proof of Lemma
3.10
we show that

\limsup_{||(t,s)||_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\left|\varphi(t,s)\right|=\limsup_{||(t,s)||_{2}\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,\beta}}\int_{\R}e^{\alpha V(x)+\beta W(x)}e^{itV(x)+isW(x)}dx\right|<1.

(17)

In the parlance of probabilistic number theory, (
17
) is satisfied if the oscillatory integral given by the characteristic function
\varphi
shows at least a very small cancellation. Showing (
17
) is not straight forward, since our sequence in (
16
) is a sequence of random vectors in
\R^{2}
. In the classical theory (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Section 2]
), we would like to see that the distribution of

\left(V(X_{1}),W(X_{1})\right),\hskip 10.00002ptX_{1}\sim\mu_{\alpha,\beta},

has a non-degenerate (w.r.t. the
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure) component. Since this particular sequence is concentrated on the
1
-dimensional manifold

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\left(V(x),W(x)\right)|x\in\R\right\},

the distribution of the random vector in (
16
) is degenerated in the
2
-dimensional sense. So we need to tackle the limit in (
17
) directly.
The classical tool to deal with such integrals is Van der Corput’s lemma (see Proposition
3.6
). In our case, we are dealing with a phase function of the form
tV+sW
, where
(t,s)\in\R^{2}
and
V,W
are either Orlicz functions or
V(x)=|x|^{p}
and
W(x)=|x|^{q}
with
p,q\in(0,1)
and
p\neq q
. In many cases, the function
x\mapsto tV(x)+sW(x)
lacks the necessary smoothness and may also have many inflection points over
\R
. Thus, we are not able to directly apply Van der Corput’s lemma. Our idea is to find a family of intervals
J=J(t,s)\subseteq\R
such that the function
tV+sW
restricted on
J
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and is strictly monotone and has no inflection points on
J
. On such an interval we can apply Van der Corput’s lemma to see at least some amount of cancellation in (
17
). In order for everything to work out nicely, we need to impose more regularity on
V
and
W
, which is the reason for our notion of an Orlicz function.

Proposition 3.6
.

(Van der Corput’s lemma)

Let
J=(a,b)\subseteq\R
be an interval and
f:J\rightarrow\R
be a convex or concave function which is continuously differentiable on
J
and assume that

for all
x\in J
. Moreover, let

\mathcal{C}^{1}(J)

such that for all

\left|\int_{a}^{b}\phi(x)e^{i\lambda f(x)}dx\right|\leq c\left(\max_{x\in J}|\phi(x)|+\int_{J}|\phi^{\prime}(x)|dx\right)\frac{1}{|\lambda|}.

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{-1}\right).

This implies that

\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{0}e^{\sqrt{n}\alpha x+\sqrt{n}\beta y}d\left(n^{-j/2}F_{j}(x,y)\right)=o(n^{-1}).

This establishes the first claim. Now let
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)
and
\beta=0
. Then, again, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha,0}}\left[\left|\left|\left(V(X),W(X)\right)\right|\right|_{2}^{2}\right]<\infty,

allowing us to write

F_{n}(x,y)=G^{\Sigma}(x,y)+n^{-1/2}F_{1}(x,y)+R_{n}(x,y),

with
R_{n}(x,y)=O(n^{-1})
, uniformly in
(x,y)\in\R^{2}
and the density of
F_{j}
is of the form

g^{\Sigma}(x,y)\pi_{1}(x,y)
with
\pi_{1}
having degree
3
and
g^{\Sigma}
is the density of a bivariate normal distribution with mean
0
and covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\R^{2\times 2}
. As before, we get

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{\alpha\sqrt{n}x}dF_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{\alpha\sqrt{n}x}dG^{\Sigma}(x,y)+\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{\alpha\sqrt{n}x}d\left(n^{-1/2}F_{1}(x,y)\right)+O(n^{-1}).

We further find constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
with

\left|\pi_{1}(x,y)g^{\Sigma}(x,y)\right|\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})}.

This implies

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{\alpha\sqrt{n}x}d\left(n^{-1/2}F_{1}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle\ll n^{-1/2}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha\sqrt{n}x}e^{-c_{2}x^{2}}\underbrace{\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-c_{2}y^{2}}dy}_{\ll 1}dx

\displaystyle\ll n^{-1/2}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha\sqrt{n}x}dx

\displaystyle\ll n^{-1}.

∎

The next lemma gives an estimate for the asymptotic volume of a high dimensional Orlicz ball. Such a result was first obtained in
[
22
, Theorem A]
, where the error term was of order
o(1)
. For the proof of Theorem
B
, we need an error of order
O(n^{-1/2})
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
R\in(0,\infty)
and
V:\R\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an Orlicz function. Then the asymptotic volume of
\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}
is given by

\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)=\frac{e^{-\alpha_{*}nR}Z_{\alpha_{*},0}^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}|\alpha_{*}|}\left(1+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\right).

Remark 3.13
.

We note that Lemma
3.12
is a special case of
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
. Nevertheless, in the following, we present an alternative proof, resting on the methods developed in this article.

Proof.

(Lemma
3.12
)
We have

\displaystyle\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\R^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}}(x)dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=Z_{\alpha_{*},0}^{n}\int_{\R^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})\leq nR\right]}\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha_{*},0}^{n}}e^{(\alpha_{*}-\alpha_{*})\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=Z_{\alpha_{*},0}^{n}e^{-n\alpha_{*}R}\int_{x=-\infty}^{0}\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha\sqrt{n}x}dF_{n}(x,y),

where
F_{n}
is the distribution function of the vector

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(V(X_{i})-R,W(X_{i})-m\right),

with
(X_{i})_{i\in\N}
being an iid sequence all distributed according to
\mu_{\alpha_{*},0}
. By Lemma
3.10
together with Lemma
3.11
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{x=-\infty}^{0}\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha\sqrt{n}x}dF_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x=-\infty}^{0}\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha\sqrt{n}x}dG^{\Sigma}(x,y)+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{*}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{*}}}dx+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha_{*}x}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2n\sigma_{*}}}dx+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha_{*}x}dx+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha_{*}x}\left(e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2n\sigma_{*}}}-1\right)dx+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}|\alpha_{*}|}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha_{*}x}\left(e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2n\sigma_{*}}}-1\right)dx+O(n^{-1}).

We take a closer look at the second integral in the expression above, where we get (we use the estimate
|e^{t}-1|\leq|t|\max\{e^{t},1\}
for
t\in\R
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}}\left|\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\alpha_{*}x}\left(e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2n\sigma_{*}}}-1\right)dx\right|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{\alpha_{*}x}\underbrace{\max\left\{1,e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2n\sigma_{*}}}\right\}}_{\leq 1}dx

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Proof.

(Theorem
A
)
We only prove
(i)
, since the proof of
(ii)
can be carried out in a similar way. By Lemma
3.3
, there exists an
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all
t\in(m,m+\varepsilon)
, we find a pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in(-\infty,0)\times(0,\infty)
with

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
t\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha,\beta}}\left[V(X)\right]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha,\beta}}\left[W(X)\right]\end{array}\right).

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\N}
be an iid sequnce of random variables with
X_{1}\sim\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
. Then we can introduce the following sequence of centered random variables

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i},Z_{i}\right):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(V(X_{i})-R,W(X_{i})-t\right),\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\N.

We denote the distribution function of this sequence by
F_{n}
and recall that
F_{n}
has an Edgeworth expansion by Lemma
3.10
. We now get

\displaystyle\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}\cap\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,W)}_{t}\right)^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\R^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,W)}_{t}\right)^{c}}(x)dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=Z_{\alpha,\beta}^{n}\int_{\R^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})\leq nR\right]}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}W(x_{i})>nt\right]}\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,\beta}^{n}}e^{(\alpha-\alpha)\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})+(\beta-\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{n}W(x_{i})}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=Z_{\alpha,\beta}^{n}e^{-n\alpha R-n\beta t}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{1}_{\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\leq 0\right]}\mathbb{1}_{\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}>0\right]}e^{\sqrt{n}\alpha\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-R)+\sqrt{n}\beta\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-t)}\right]

\displaystyle=Z_{\alpha,\beta}^{n}e^{-n\alpha R-n\beta t}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{0}e^{-\sqrt{n}\beta x-\sqrt{n}\alpha y}dF_{n}(x,y).

By Lemma
3.10
, we can write

F_{n}(x,y)=G^{\Sigma}(x,y)+\sum_{j=1}^{2}n^{-j/2}F_{j}(x,y)+R_{n}(x,y),

where
G^{\Sigma}
is the distribution function of a normal distribution with mean
0
and variance matrix
\Sigma\in\R^{2\times 2}
and
R_{n}(x,y)=o(n^{-1})
, uniformly in
(x,y)\in\R^{2}
. By Lemma
3.11
we get

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{0}e^{-\sqrt{n}\beta x-\sqrt{n}\alpha y}dF_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=0}^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{n}\beta x+\sqrt{n}\alpha y}dG^{\Sigma}(x,y)+o(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=0}^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{n}(\beta x-\alpha y)}\frac{1}{2\pi|\Sigma|^{1/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x,y)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dxdy+o(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi n|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x+\alpha y}\underbrace{e^{-\frac{1}{2n}(x,y)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}}_{=:f_{n}(x,y)}dxdy+o(n^{-1}).

We now observe that
f_{n}(x,y)\leq 1
for all
(x,y)\in\R^{2}
and for all
n\in\N
. Since
e^{-\beta x+\alpha y}
is integrable, the dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi n|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x+\alpha y}f_{n}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi n|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x+\alpha y}dxdy+o(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi n(-\alpha)\beta|\Sigma|^{1/2}}+o(n^{-1}).

By Lemma
3.12
, it holds that

\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}\right)=e^{-\alpha^{*}nR}Z_{\alpha^{*},0}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}|\alpha_{*}|}\left(1+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\right),

where
\sigma_{*}^{2}=\mathbb{V}_{\mu_{\alpha_{*},0}}\left[V(X)\right]
. This implies that

\frac{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}\cap\mathbb{B}^{(n,W)}_{t}\right)}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}\right)}=1-e^{-n\left(\varphi(\alpha^{*},0)-\varphi(\alpha,\beta)+(\alpha-\alpha^{*})R+\beta t\right)}\frac{|\alpha_{*}|}{\sqrt{2\pi n}|\alpha|\beta\bar{\sigma}}\left(1+o(1)\right),

where

\bar{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{*}^{2}}{|\Sigma|}

is the second entry in the diagonal of
\Sigma^{-1}
. Since, by construction,

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\\
\beta\end{array}\right)=\left(\nabla\varphi\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
t\end{array}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{*}\\
0\end{array}\right)=\left(\nabla\varphi\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
m\end{array}\right),

we recover the definition of
\I
and thus the estimate in (
7
).

∎

Proof.

(Theorem
C
)
We prove a more general result, where instead of the
\ell_{2}
-norm we work with another Orlicz function
W
, such that
V
and
W
satisfy Assumption
A
.
We define
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
to be the largest constant such that, for any
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, we find pairs
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})\in(-\infty,0)\times(0,\infty)
and
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})\in(-\infty,0)^{2}
with

\nabla\varphi(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
m+\delta\end{array}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\varphi(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
m-\delta\end{array}\right).

(22)

This is possible by Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.5
. We now obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right|\geq\delta\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)\geq m+\delta\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)\leq m-\delta\right].

Analogously to the proof of Theorem
A
, we now get

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right|\geq\delta\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{1}}e^{-n\mathbb{I}(R,m+\delta)}(1+o(1))+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{2}}e^{-n\mathbb{I}(R,m-\delta)}(1+o(1)),

where
\mathbb{I}
is the same GRF as in Theorem
A
and

\sigma_{i}^{2}:=\mathbb{V}_{\mu_{\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}}}\left[V(X)\right],\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2.

The claim now follows by defining

\tilde{\sigma}:=\begin{cases}\sigma_{1},&\text{if}\quad\mathbb{I}(R,m+\delta)>\mathbb{I}(R,m-\delta)\\
\sigma_{2},&\text{if}\quad\mathbb{I}(R,m+\delta)<\mathbb{I}(R,m-\delta)\\
\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}},&\text{if}\quad\mathbb{I}(R,m+\delta)=\mathbb{I}(R,m-\delta).\end{cases}

∎

Proof.

(Theorem
B
)
We start with the proof of the Berry–Esseen result, where we recall that there exists an
\alpha_{*}\in(-\infty,0)
such that

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha_{*},0}}[V(X)]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha_{*},0}}[W(X)]\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
m\end{array}\right).

This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right]\leq t\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}\int_{\R^{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}}(x)\mathbb{1}_{\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(W(x_{i})-m\right)\leq t\right]}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\alpha_{*}}^{-n}e^{n\alpha_{*}R}}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}\int_{\R^{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[V(x_{i})-R\right]\leq 0\right]}\mathbb{1}_{\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[W(x_{i})-m\right]\leq t\right]}e^{\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[V(x_{i})-R\right]}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\alpha_{*}}^{-n}e^{n\alpha_{*}R}}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}\int_{x=-\infty}^{0}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{\sqrt{n}\alpha_{*}x}dF_{n}(x,y),

where
F_{n}
is the distribution function of the vector

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(V(X_{i})-R,W(X_{i})-m\right),

with
(X_{i})_{i\in\N}
being an iid sequence with
X_{1}
being distributed according to
\mu_{\alpha_{*},0}
. By Lemma
3.12
, it holds that

\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}^{(n,V)}_{R}\right)=e^{-\alpha^{*}nR}Z_{\alpha^{*},0}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\sigma_{*}|\alpha_{*}|}(1+O(n^{-1/2})).

Together with Lemma
3.11
we now get (let
\Sigma=(\sigma_{i,j})_{i,j=1,2}
and
\Sigma^{-1}=(\rho_{i,j})_{i,j=1,2}
and recall the identity
\rho_{2,2}=\frac{\sigma_{2,2}}{|\Sigma|}
)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right]\leq t\right]&=\frac{\sqrt{n}|\alpha_{*}|\sigma_{*}}{\sqrt{2\pi}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-\sqrt{n}\alpha_{*}x}e^{-(x,y)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dxdy+O(n^{-1/2})\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{\rho_{2,2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-x}e^{-\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},y\right)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dydx+O(n^{-1/2}).\end{split}

(23)

We take a closer look at the integral above, where we get

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-x}e^{-\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},y\right)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},y\right)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dydx+\int_{x=\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},y\right)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}\rho_{1,1}}{2n}-\frac{xy\rho_{2,1}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}\right)dydx+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\pm\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}\right)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}\rho_{1,1}}{2n}-\frac{xy\rho_{2,1}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}\right)dydx

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}\right)dydx+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right).

We aim to show that the first integral in the expression above is of order
O(n^{-1/2})
. We recall the trivial estimate

\left|e^{t}-1\right|\leq|t|\left(1+e^{t}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\R.

This yields (we use that
\left|\frac{x^{2}\rho_{1,1}}{n}+\frac{xy\rho_{2,1}}{\sqrt{n}}\right|\ll\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}(|y|+1)
)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}\rho_{1,1}}{2n}-\frac{xy\rho_{2,1}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)-1\right]dydx\right|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}xe^{-x}\underbrace{\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}(|y|+1)e^{-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}-\frac{xy}{\sqrt{n}}}dy}_{\ll 1}dx

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{n}}xe^{-x}dx

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

From that we infer

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-x}e^{-\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},y\right)\Sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)}dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{-x}\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}}dydx+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{y=-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{y^{2}\rho_{2,2}}{2}}dy+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right).

Combining this estimate with Equation (
23
) gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right]\leq t\right]

\displaystyle=\phi(t)+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right),

where
\phi
is the cumulative distribution function of a normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
\rho_{2,2}^{-1}
.

Next we prove the strong law of large numbers using the convergence Borel–Cantelli lemma. To that end, let
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
, where we will show that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right|>\varepsilon\right]<\infty.

Let
n\in\N
be large, where we have (using an analogous argument as in the first part of this proof)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right|>\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}dF_{n}(x,y)\left(1+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}dF_{n}(x,y).

Since

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\alpha_{*},0}}\left[\left|\left|\left(V(X),W(X)\right)\right|\right|_{2}^{3}\right]<\infty,

we can write

F_{n}(x,y)=G^{\Sigma}(x,y)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}n^{-j/2}F_{j}(x,y)+R_{n}(x,y),

with
R_{n}(x,y)=o(n^{-3/2})
, uniformly in
(x,y)\in\R^{2}
and the density of
F_{j}
is of the form

g^{\Sigma}(x,y)\pi_{j}(x,y)
with
\pi_{j}
having degree
3j
. This implies

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}dF_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}dG^{\Sigma}(x,y)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}n^{-j/2}\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}dF_{j}(x,y)+o(n^{-3/2})

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}dG^{\Sigma}(x,y)+o(n^{-3/2})

\displaystyle\ll\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x}\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-c(x^{2}+y^{2})}dydx+o(n^{-3/2})

\displaystyle\ll\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sqrt{n}x-cx^{2}}e^{-c^{\prime}n}dx+o(n^{-3/2})

\displaystyle=o(n^{-3/2}),

where, in the second last line, we used that

\int_{|y|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon}e^{-cy^{2}}dy\ll e^{-c^{\prime}n},

for some constant
c^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
. This implies that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right|>\varepsilon\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}o(n^{-3/2})<\infty.

∎

Proof.

(of Corollary
1.4
)
We recall that

\displaystyle\frac{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\cap\mathbb{B}_{m}^{(n,W)}\right)}{\vol_{n}\left(\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)\leq m\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[W\left(X_{i}^{(n,V)}\right)-m\right]\leq 0\right],

where
X^{(n,V)}
is uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{B}_{R}^{(n,V)}
. The last probability in the expression above converges to
\frac{1}{2}
