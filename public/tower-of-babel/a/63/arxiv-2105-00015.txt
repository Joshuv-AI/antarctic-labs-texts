Dynamics of Open Quantum Systems I, Oscillation and Decay
Marco Merkli

Abstract
We develop a framework to analyze the dynamics of a finite-dimensional quantum system S {\rm S} in contact with a reservoir R {\rm R} . The full, interacting SR {\rm S}{\rm R} dynamics is unitary. The reservoir has a stationary state but otherwise dissipative dynamics. We identify a main part of the full dynamics, which approximates it for small values of the SR {\rm S}{\rm R} coupling constant, uniformly for all times t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . The main part consists of explicit oscillating and decaying parts. We show that the reduced system evolution is Markovian for all times. The technical novelty is a detailed analysis of the link between the dynamics and the spectral properties of the generator of the SR {\rm S}{\rm R} dynamics, based on Mourre theory. We allow for SR {\rm S}{\rm R} interactions with little regularity, meaning that the decay of the reservoir correlation function only needs to be polynomial in time, improving on the previously required exponential decay. In this work we distill the structural and technical ingredients causing the characteristic features of oscillation and decay of the SR {\rm S}{\rm R} dynamics. In the companion paper [ 27 ] we apply the formalism to the concrete case of an N N -level system linearly coupled to a spatially infinitely extended thermal bath of non-interacting Bosons.

Introduction

The fundamental evolution equation of quantum theory is the Schrödinger equation, which governs the dynamics of
closed
quantum systems, isolated from their surroundings. In contrast, when a system is interacting with its surroundings, then the evolution equation for the degrees of freedom of the system alone have to be deduced from other fundamental principles. One approach is to view the system plus its surroundings
together
as a closed system, meaning that they evolve according to the Schrödinger equation involving both, the degrees of freedom of the system and those of the surroundings. This amounts to a huge number of variables, even if the system itself is small. The evolution equation for the system is obtained by “tracing out” the degrees of freedom of the environment, and its solution is called the reduced system dynamics. Generically, this situation is described as follows. The Hilbert space of states is

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R},

(1.1)

describing the full complex into a partition of a system part
{\rm S}
and a reservoir part
{\rm R}
. Traditionally, one calls the degrees of freedom making up the surroundings of
{\rm S}
a
reservoir
(a thermal reservoir if one has in mind a big system in thermal equilibrium). The total system (
{\rm S}+{\rm R}
) being closed, the dynamics of an initial (time
t=0
)
{\rm S}{\rm R}
density matrix
\rho(0)
is given by

\rho(t)=e^{-{\rm i}tH}\rho(0)e^{{\rm i}tH},

in accordance with the Schrödinger equation – also called the von-Neumann equation in the case of density matrices. Here,
H
is the total Hamiltonian, which is the sum

H=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm R}+\lambda V

(1.2)

of individual system and reservoir Hamiltonians plus an interaction term
\lambda V
, where
\lambda
is a
coupling constant
. The operators
H_{\rm S}
and
H_{\rm R}
act non-trivially only on the system and reservoir factors of
\mathcal{H}
, while
V
mixes up those factors. Typically
V=A\otimes B
(or a sum of such terms), where
A
and
B
are system and reservoir operators, respectively. The reduced system density matrix at time
t
is given by

\rho_{\rm S}(t)={\rm tr}_{\rm R}\big(e^{-{\rm i}tH}\rho(0)e^{{\rm i}tH}\big),

(1.3)

where
{\rm tr}_{\rm R}
denotes the partial trace over the reservoir part of
\mathcal{H}
. For
\lambda=0
, the dynamics is uncoupled,
e^{-{\rm i}tH}=e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm S}}\otimes e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm R}}
and each factor (subsystem) evolves individually and independently. The dynamics is usually known explicitly in the uncoupled case, and the challenge is to find out what happens when
\lambda\neq 0
. The idea then is, usually, to carry out perturbation theory for small
\lambda
. If the initial state is not correlated,
\rho(0)=\rho_{\rm S}(0)\otimes\rho_{\rm R}(0)
, then the map

V_{t}:\rho_{\rm S}(0)\mapsto\rho_{\rm S}(t)

is well defined. It is the flow, mapping an arbitrary system initial state to its state at time
t\geq 0
. For
\lambda=0
, we have
V_{t}=e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}}
, where
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}\rho=-{\rm i}[H_{\rm S},\rho]
. However, for
\lambda\neq 0
, generically,
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
become entangled during the evolution, and this prevents
V_{t}
from having the above simple structure. Namely,
V_{t}
cannot be written as
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
for any
{\mathcal{L}}
; this means that
V_{t}
is not a semigroup in
t
any longer (we call
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
a semigroup instead of a group, if we restrict the values of
t
to
t\geq 0
). Apart from encoding the Markovian characteristics, any dynamics of the form
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
has the practical advantage that the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the operator
\mathcal{L}
encode the dynamical behaviour. Spectral theory thus becoms a main tool in the analysis of such a Markovian dynamics.

Markovian approximation.
Even though the flow
V_{t}
is not of exponential (semigroup) form, one expects that under suitable conditions,
V_{t}
can be
approximated
by a semigroup,

V_{t}\approx e^{t{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)},

with
{\mathcal{L}}(0)={\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}
. This is called the
Markovian approximation

[
5
,
36
]
. The approximate evolution equation, which reads in differential form

\dot{\rho}_{\rm M}(t)={\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)\rho_{\rm M}(t),

(1.4)

is the ubiquitous
Markovian master equation
. On physical grounds, its validity is plausible under two main assumptions: Firstly, the reservoir loses its memory quickly and secondly, the reservoir is ‘large’ and the interaction is weak, so as to guarantee that the reservoir dynamics is not affected much by the inteaction with the system. These assumptions, also called the Markov- and Born approximations, respectively, are quantified by the decay of the reservoir correlation function (quick memory loss) and a smallness condition on
\lambda
(weak coupling). The main two issues are

(a)

How to construct
{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)
and

(b)

How to estimate the error made by the Markovian approximation.

The generator
{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)
has to be constructed starting from the full
{\rm S}{\rm R}
dynamics (interacting Hamiltonian
H
as above) and reducing the evolution to the system component alone by tracing out the reservoir degrees of freedom. The literature on this topic is enourmous, and there have been many proposals for
{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)
. Eventually, the so-called Davies generator
[
9
,
10
]

{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\rm S}+\lambda^{2}K

(1.5)

emerged as the ‘right’ generator. Here,
K
is an operator describing the effect of
{\rm R}
on
{\rm S}
, which is explicitly determined by the interaction operator
V
and the reservoir correlation function. The approximate Markovian dynamics
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
is a so-called CPTP semigroup
[
1
,
6
]
(completely positive, trace preserving), which guarantees, in particular, that the approximate solution
\rho_{\rm M}(t)
is a (positive definite, normalized) density matrix for all
t
. For other generators proposed in the literature, this is not the case
[
13
]
. Of course, the Davies generator is not the remedy for all ailments. There are plenty of interesting situations where the usual Davies generator (
1.5
) does not lead to a good approximation. An important class of examples are systems with small energy level spacings, for which a different generator has to be used
[
32
,
22
,
40
,
33
,
35
,
29
,
30
]
.

1.1
Goals

We have two goals:

G1

Identify a general framework for the perturbation theory of the dynamics of a unitarily evolving total system-reservoir complex. Isolate the dominant term of the total dynamics for small coupling. Derive from it the open system dynamics. Do this with weak regularity requirements on the interaction between the parts.

G2

Show how a widely used model of open quantum systems, an
N
-level system coupled linearly to a spatially infinitely extended system of non-interacting Bosons in thermal equilibrium, fits the general framework. Show the validity of the Markovian master equation for this model, under weak regularity conditions.

We achieve the first goal in the current manuscript, while the second one is addressed in the companion paper
[
27
]
. The setup for G1 is more general than the usual setup found in the open quantum systems literature. This is motivated by us trying to distill the essential ingredients that imply characteristic oscillation and decay features of the total dynamics, as presented in our main result, Theorem
3.1
. The companion paper
[
27
]
can be read independently of the current work, and the concrete model considered there will be more familiar to many researchers in the field. We hope that a reader finding the current manuscript somewhat abstract may still consult
[
27
]
for the application part, which presents new results on this classical family of models.

We consider a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, of the form (
1.1
), carrying a unitary dynamics
e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}
, with a self-adjoint generator (compare to (
1.2
))

L_{\lambda}=L_{\rm S}+L_{\rm R}+\lambda I,

were
L_{\rm S}
and
L_{\rm R}
are called the system and reservoir Liouville operators, acting non-trivially only on the respective factors of
\mathcal{H}
,
\lambda
is a coupling constant and
I
is an interaction operator. Our assuptions are expressed mathematically precisely in (A1)-(A5), Section
2
. The main two assumptions are described as follows.

•

We assume that the reservoir dynamics has a
unique stationary state
. In other words, the reservoir Liouvillean
L_{\rm R}
has a unique eigenvalue. This eigenvalue is zero and non-degenerate. The unique stationary state (eigenvector) is denoted by
\Omega_{\rm R}
.

•

We assume that away from the reservoir stationary state, the total dynamics is
dissipative
. By this we mean that for vectors
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
P_{\rm R}\phi=P_{\rm R}\psi=0
, where
P_{\rm R}=\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}}\otimes|\Omega_{\rm R}\rangle\langle\Omega_{\rm R}|
is the projection onto the reservoir stationary state, we have

|\langle\phi,e^{{\rm i}t\bar{L}_{\lambda}}\psi\rangle|\sim t^{-k}\mbox{\qquad for large $t$, for some $k\geq 2$.}

(1.6)

Here,
\bar{L}_{\lambda}
denotes the operator
L_{\lambda}
restricted to the range of
P_{\rm R}^{\perp}=\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}-P_{\rm R}
(the space of vectors
\psi
that have a vanishing overlap with the reservoir stationary state
\Omega_{\rm R}
).

How do we phrase (
1.6
) precisely? This is done by representing
e^{{\rm i}t\bar{L}_{\lambda}}
by the Fourier-Laplace transform of the resolvent operator
(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-z)^{-1}
,

e^{{\rm i}t\bar{L}_{\lambda}}=-\tfrac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{{\rm i}tx}(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-x-{\rm i}0_{+})^{-1}dx
. Using
e^{{\rm i}tx}=\frac{1}{\ it}\tfrac{d}{dx}e^{{\rm i}tx}
and integrating by parts with respect to
x
gives

e^{{\rm i}t\bar{L}_{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{t}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{{\rm i}tx}(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-x-{\rm i}0_{+})^{-2}dx.

Stronger decay in
t
involves the Fourier-Laplace transform of higher powers
(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-x-{\rm i}0_{+})^{-1}
(repeat the integration by parts). To estimate such integrals, we require some powers of the resolvent
(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-z)^{-1}
to be bounded in
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}=\{z:{\rm Im}z<0\}
(weakly on a suitable set of vectors). We give a rigorous discussion of this point below, after (
2.26
). Our method requires decay
\sim t^{-2}
, which results in the assumption that
\partial^{j}_{z}(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-z)^{-1}
is bounded in
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
, for
j=0,1,2
, see (
2.6
).

•

Further conditions: We assume some non-degeneracy of the eigenvalues of
L_{\lambda}
and some suitable relative boundedness conditions on the interaction operator
I
(see (A1)-(A5)).

These further conditions, given in detail in Section
2
, are less fundamental (and can be weakened, or entirely removed).

We now explain what we mean by
weak regularity requirement
on the interaction, stated in the Goals G1, G2 above. A result similar to Theorem
3.1
(which implies the validity of the Markovian approximation) was proven in
[
21
,
26
]
under an
analyticity condition
on the reduced resolvent. Instead of requiring the first three derivatives of
(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-z)^{-1}
to be (weakly) bounded in
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
, it was assumed in those works that
(\bar{L}_{\lambda}-z)^{-1}
has an analytic extension as
z
moves from the lower complex plane across the real line into the upper complex plane. The difference between the two cases is not merely a technical issue, as the regularity determines physical features. For instance, the stronger analyticity condition implies that overlaps (
1.6
), as well as the reservoir correlation function, decay
exponentially quickly
in
t
. Improving the ‘analytic deformation theory’ used in
[
20
,
21
,
26
]
to the weaker regularity regime considered in the current paper and in
[
27
]
, is not totally easy. It is done using ‘Mourre theory’, which is more delicate and technically trickier than the analytic theory. This improvement is worth pursuing as it sheds light on the question of how quickly the reservoir has to lose its memory in order for the Markovian approximation still to hold. The current work and
[
27
]
show that polynomial decay of the memory (reservoir correlation function) suffices. Incidentally, this polynomial memory loss still drives an exponentially quick approach of the system to its final state (c.f. Theorem
3.1
). It is well known that if the memory loss is not quick enough, then the Markovian approximation is not valid
[
35
,
37
,
23
]
. We plan on extending our methods to this case in the future.

Remarks in view of applications.

(1)

In applications
[
27
,
28
]
, we need, in general, to describe mixed states (such as equilibrium states). The Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
considered in the current paper does not, in general, describe pure states. Rather,
\mathcal{H}
here is the space of purification (or, vectorization) of mixed states in question. Similarly,
L_{\lambda}
is the
Liouville
operator, describing the dynamics expressed in the purification Hilbert space. This setup allows us to treat mixed and pure states on the same footing.

Example.
The system equilibrium state of a qubit with Hamiltonian
H_{\rm S}=\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}\sigma_{z}=\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}|\!\uparrow\rangle-\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}|\!\downarrow\rangle
is the mixed state
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}\propto e^{-\beta H_{\rm S}}
. Its purification is given by
\Psi_{\beta,{\rm S}}\propto e^{-\beta\omega_{0}/4}|\!\uparrow\rangle\otimes|\!\uparrow\rangle+e^{\beta\omega_{0}/4}|\!\downarrow\rangle\otimes|\!\downarrow\rangle
, a normalized vector in the
purification space

{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}={\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.
The Liouvillean is
L_{\rm S}=H_{\rm S}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}\otimes H_{\rm S}
. The key link between
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}
and its purification
\Psi_{{\rm S},\beta}
is that for all observables
A
and times
t
, the expectations can be expressed as,

{\rm tr}_{{\mathbb{C}^{2}}}(e^{-{\rm i}tH_{\rm S}}\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}e^{{\rm i}tH_{\rm S}}A)=\langle e^{-{\rm i}tL_{\rm S}}\Psi_{{\rm S},\beta},(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\mathbb{C}^{2}}})e^{-{\rm i}tL_{\rm S}}\Psi_{{\rm S},\beta}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}}.

Given
\rho_{{\rm S},\beta}
and
H_{\rm S}
, neither
\Psi_{{\rm S},\beta}
nor
L_{\rm S}
are unique – but there are standard choices for them
[
4
,
26
,
3
,
18
,
12
]
. The reservoir we consider in applications
[
27
,
28
]
consists of non-interacting Bose particles in infinite position space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. They are in a state of thermal equilibrium at temperature
1/\beta>0
, determined by Planck’s black body momentum density distribution: there are
(e^{-\beta|k|}-1)^{-1}d^{3}k
particles having momentum
k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in a volume
d^{3}k
centered around
k
, for each unit volume in position space. Usual Bosonic Fock space
{\mathcal{F}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k))
does not accommodate this state, as any state
\psi\in{\mathcal{F}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k))
(or denisty matrix) describes finitely many particles, which results in a vanishing particle density at infinite volume! However, one can find a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
(turning out to be the tensor product of two copies of Fock space) and a vector
\Omega_{\rm R}
(turning out to be the tensor product of two vacua) which do represent the equilibrium state at positive density. The details of this construction are not the topic here, they can be found in textbook style in
[
31
]
(see also
[
26
]
, or the original paper
[
2
]
). In the mathematical literature, representing a mixed state as a normalized vector in a Hilbert space is known as the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction (GNS); in the physics and chemistry literature it is also sometimes called the
thermofield
method.

(2)

We assume that
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}<\infty
. However, the decay (
1.6
) necessitates that
\bar{L}_{\lambda}
does not have any eigenvalues, for otherwise clearly the overlap in (
1.6
) would be independent of time on eigenvectors. This means that the spectrum of
\bar{L}_{\lambda}
must be continuous. In particular, the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
has to be infinite dimensional.

Example.
In the case of a reservoir of infinitely extended Bose particles, continuous energy spectrum arises due to the infinite volume limit. We prove in Theorem 3.1 of
[
27
]
that (
1.6
) holds for this reservoir. The proof is based on the fact that the estimate holds for
\bar{L}_{\lambda}
replaced by
\bar{L}_{0}
, together with a suitable perturbation theory in
\lambda
.

1.2
Explanation of the main result

Our main result is Theorem
3.1
. It states that for coupling constants
\lambda
small enough, we have the expansion

e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}=e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}\otimes P_{\rm R}+P^{\perp}_{\rm R}e^{{\rm i}t\bar{L}_{\lambda}}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}+R(\lambda,t),

(1.7)

valid for all
t\geq 0
. Here,
P_{\rm R}=\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}}\otimes|\Omega_{\rm R}\rangle\langle\Omega_{\rm R}|\equiv|\Omega_{\rm R}\rangle\langle\Omega_{\rm R}|
is the projection onto the reservoir stationary state
\Omega_{\rm R}
and
P_{\rm R}^{\perp}=\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}-P_{\rm R}
. The operator on the left side,
e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}
, is the propagator of the full, unitary
{\rm S}{\rm R}
evolution. The first term on the right side describes a non-trivial evolution of
{\rm S}
, generated by an operator
M(\lambda)
, while
{\rm R}
is projected onto the stationary state
\Omega_{\rm R}
. This part of the dynamics is Markovian. The second term on the right side describes the dynamics of states (vectors) in the range of
P^{\perp}_{\rm R}
, so states with vanishing overlaps with the reservoir stationary state
\Omega_{\rm R}
. Finally,
R(\lambda,t)
is a remainder term. The equality (
1.7
) is understood in the weak sense, that is, when
\langle\phi,\cdot\,\psi\rangle
is applied to both sides, for suitable vectors
\psi,\phi\in\mathcal{H}
belonging to a dense set. This set of vectors includes all uncorrelated
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states in which the system is in any state and the reservoir is in the stationary state
\Omega_{\rm R}
. However, the set also includes a wide class of correlated initial
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states; this aspect is exploited in
[
28
]
to show the validity of the Markovian approximation even for correlated initial states. In Theorem
3.1
,

•

We find the detailed structure of
e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}
. It consists of explicit oscillating terms and explicit decaying terms with decay rates
\propto 1/\lambda^{2}
.

•

We show that the dissipative term decays polynomially in time,

|\langle\phi,e^{{\rm i}t\bar{L}_{\lambda}}\psi\rangle|\leq C/(1+t^{2}),\qquad\mbox{for $\phi,\psi\in{\rm Ran}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}$.}

•

We show that the remainder satisfies
|\langle\phi,R(\lambda,t)\psi\rangle|\leq C|\lambda|^{1/4}
for all
t\geq 0
.

We apply our main result (
1.7
) to the concrete class of open quantum systems explained in goal G2 above in
[
27
,
28
]
.

1.3
New results in the theory of open quantum systems

We verify in
[
27
]
that the assumptions (A1)-(A5) of Section
2
below are satisfied, and hence that Theorem
3.1
holds, for a standard class of open quantum systems described in goal G2 above. For this model, the generator
M(\lambda)
is just the Davies generator
\mathcal{L}
, (
1.5
), as shown in
[
26
]
, and Theorem
3.1
yields the following new results.

1.

Proof of the validity of the Markov approximation for all times
[
27
]
.

Davies
[
9
,
10
]
showed the validity of the approximation in the
ultra-weak coupling limit
.
1
1

1

In the mathematical literature, the term ‘weak coupling limit’, or ‘Van-Hove limit’ is used for this regime.
Namely, for any
a>0
,

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\ \sup_{0\leq\lambda^{2}t<a}\|V_{t}-e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}\|=0,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the norm of super operators and
\mathcal{L}
is the Davies generator (
1.5
). This means that
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
is a good approximation of the true system dynamics
V_{t}
for small values of
\lambda
,
but only for times up to
t\sim\lambda^{-2}
. In particular, Davies’ result does not guarantee that the asymptotic system dynamics is approximated by the Markovian master equation. Our results overcomes this defect. Namely, we show in
[
27
]
that there is a
\lambda_{0}>0
such that if
|\lambda|<\lambda_{0}
, then

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|V_{t}-e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}\|\leq C|\lambda|^{1/4},

(1.8)

for a constant
C
independent of
\lambda,t
.

We can rephrase the result (
1.8
). Let
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
be the system density matrix (
1.3
) and let
\rho_{\rm M}(t)
be the solution of the Markovian master equation (
1.4
), with
{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)={\mathcal{L}}
the Davies generator, and with equal initial conditions,
\rho_{\rm S}(0)=\rho_{\rm M}(0)
. Then (
1.8
) asserts that

\|\rho_{\rm S}(t)-\rho_{\rm M}(t)\|_{1}\leq C|\lambda|^{1/4},\qquad\forall t\geq 0,

where
\|\sigma\|_{1}={\rm tr}_{\rm S}(|\sigma|)
is the trace norm and
C
is a constant independent of the initial condition.

2.

Proof of the validity of the Born approximation
[
28
]
.

Our main result (
1.7
) gives an expansion of the full, interacting
{\rm S}{\rm R}
dynamics. When reduced to the system dynamics alone, it results in (
1.8
). However, when analyzed in full, (
1.7
) is shown in
[
28
]
to yield the following results.

–

For uncorrelated initial states
\rho(0)=\rho_{\rm S}(0)\otimes|\Omega_{\rm R}\rangle\langle\Omega_{\rm R}|
, where
{\rm S}
is in any state and
{\rm R}
is in equilibrium, the reservoir stays in equilibrium during the coupled evolution up to an error of
O({|\lambda|^{1/4}})
, for all times
t\geq 0
. This is a proof of the validity of the Born approximation, for all times.

–

For a large class of correlated initial
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states, the correlations decay polynomially in time. After this decay has happened, the reservoir is in thermal equilibrium and the system evolves according to the Markovian dynamics generated by the Davies generator, up to errors
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
, uniformly in times
t\geq 0
.

1.4
Relation to earlier work

It is not our aim to present a detailed discussion of the huge literature on the dynamics of open quantum systems, as the goal of the current manuscript is the construction of a general mathematical framework. Let us rather discuss some mathematically rigorous works related to ours.

The spectral methods developed here have a certain similarity with the theory of metastable states in many-body quantum theory. There, the Hilbert space does not have the structure (
2.1
), but rather,
L_{0}
is the kinetic energy operator of
N
particles and
I
is an (interaction) potential. In this Schrödinger operator setup, initial states close to an eigenstate of
L_{0}
stay bound (spatially localized) for a long time under the evolution
e^{-{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}
, but eventually decay for large times
[
38
,
16
,
17
]
. It is intuitively plausible, quite generally, that eigenvectors of
L_{0}
associated to an unstable eigenvalue
e
, describing a bound states of a quantum system for
\lambda=0
, turn into ‘almost-bound’ states for small
\lambda\neq 0
. This phenomenon, and some related mathematical tools (complex spectral deformation and Mourre theory, resolvent representation of the propagator), are common to the many-body Schrödinger and the open system setups.

The dynamics of an
N
-level system coupled linearly to a spatially infinitely extended reservoir of non-interacting Bose particles (as we will consider as an application of the current results in
[
27
,
28
]
) has been investigated in detail before. It is based on the representation of the infinite volume equilibrium state as a vector in a purification Hilbert space, which was first constructed in the early sixties in
[
2
]
. However, it was not exploited to analyze the dynamics of open systems until the pioneering papers
[
18
,
3
]
developed the spectral approach. In these works, the phenomenon of
Return to Equilibrium (RtE)
was proven: States ‘close’ to the (coupled)
{\rm S}{\rm R}
equilibrium state are driven to equilibrium in the long time limit. In contrast with usual open system dynamics results, RtE is a result about the full system-reservoir dynamics, not only about the reduced system dynamics. Averages of reservoir observables also converge to the equilibrium values. While
[
18
,
3
]
based their analysis on analytic deformation methods, a Mourre theory version for RtE was developed in
[
15
,
25
]
. A bit later, in
[
24
]
, the formalism used to show RtE was refined and a detailed description of the dynamics of each system density matrix element was obtained. Then in
[
20
,
21
,
26
]
is was shown that the main term of the reduced system dynamics is a completely positive trace preserving semigroup. Under general assumptions, the generator of the semigroup is the Davies generator, as is shown in
[
26
]
.

In terms of technique, the paper
[
20
]
, where Mourre theory is used to analyze the dynamics, is closest to the present work. However, as explained in discussion point (v) after Theorem
3.1
, the approach of
[
20
]
is not suited to show the Markovian approximation. In the present work, we make substantial changes to the method of
[
20
]
, changes which we explain in Section
4
.

Further rigorous work.
Establishing the validity of Markovian master equations is an active research field. In
[
32
,
22
]
, the authors develop a so-called coarse-grained Master equation (CGME) and they compare its validity to that of the Davies and the Redfield master equations. They show that the CGME combines the advantages of the other two, but without incorporating their disadvantages. Namely, the CGME is a good approximation of the true system dynamics regardless of the system level spacing (Davies cannot do this) and the equation is CPTP (Redfield is not). However, the error bounds for neither of the three Master equations are uniform in time; the error of the CGME is
\propto O(|\lambda|e^{c\lambda^{2}t})
for some constant
c
(linked explicitly to the fastest system decoherence time scale). In a similar vein,
[
40
,
33
]
develop a Master equation, again a CPTP equation, which describes the approximation of the true system dynamics for arbitrarily small system level spacings, but again with remainders guaranteed to be small only for finite times. In
[
33
]
the authors also provide a bound on the error generated by the Born approximation, for finite times. We have applied our resonance theory to systems with almost degenerate system levels in
[
29
,
30
]
and shown that almost degeneracy leads to a separation of time scales in the system dynamics. The papers
[
29
,
30
]
assume the stronger regularity (analyticity), but we do not see any obstacle to adapting the general theory for weak regularity developed here to the case of degenerate or almost degenerate system levels.

2
Approach and assumptions

The purpose of this section is to define the setup and state precisely the assumptions we make. The core of our overall strategy is to link the dynamics to the spectral data of the generator
L_{\lambda}=L_{0}+\lambda I
. A basic mechanism producing irreversible dynamics, or decay, is the disappearence (instability) of eigenvalues of
L_{0}
under the perturbation
\lambda I
. The instability occurs because the eigenvalues of
L_{0}
are embedded in continuous spectrum. Their fate after perturbation is analyzed using ‘singular perturbation theory’. We explain the main ideas of it in Section
2.2
. In Section
2.3
we explain how stable and unstable eigenvalues of
L_{\lambda}
affect the propagator
e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}
.

Instead of making a list of assumptions, we are trying to proceed in an
in
ductive manner: We explain the main strategy of our approach, and while doing so, the assumptions should emerge naturally.

2.1
Basic properties of the Liouville operator

We consider a bipartite Hilbert space

2.2
Instability of embedded eigenvalues and how to track them

If

eP_{e}+\lambda P_{e}IP_{e}-\lambda^{2}P_{e}I(L_{0}-e)^{-1}P^{\perp}_{e}IP_{e}+O(\lambda^{3}).

Each term in the expansion (

then the reduced resolvent

being self-adjoint. We may then intuit that for small nonzero

(2.12)

Here,
(L_{0}-e+{\rm i}0_{+})^{-1}

3
Main result

Our main result is Theorem
3.1
below. It involves a reduced system dynamics
e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}
, and we explain the generator
M(\lambda)
now. The simplicity of the spectrum assumed in (A5) implies that the level shift operators
\Lambda_{e}
is diagonalizable,

\Lambda_{e}=\sum_{s=1}^{m_{e}}a_{e}^{(s)}Q_{e}^{(s)}.

(3.1)

The
a_{e}^{(s)}
are the eigenvalues and the
Q_{e}^{(s)}
are the rank-one spectral projections, satisfying the disjointness and completeness relations

Q_{e}^{(s)}Q_{e^{\prime}}^{(s^{\prime})}=Q_{e}^{(s)}\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}\delta_{e,e^{\prime}}\quad\mbox{
and}\quad\sum_{s=1}^{m_{e}}Q_{e}^{(s)}=P_{e}.

Given an eigenvalue
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
of
L_{0}
, we partition the indices
s=1,\ldots,m_{e}
into the oscillating and decaying classes

{\mathcal{S}}_{e}^{\rm osc}=\big\{s:a_{e}^{(s)}\in{\mathbb{R}}\big\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\mathcal{S}}_{e}^{\rm dec}=\big\{s:{\rm Im}a_{e}^{(s)}>0\big\}.

(3.2)

We define the operators

M(\lambda)=\bigoplus_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}M_{e},\qquad M_{e}=\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{e}^{\rm dec}}\big(e+\lambda^{2}a_{e}^{(s)}\big)Q_{e}^{(s)}+\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm osc}_{e}}E_{e}^{(s)}(\lambda)Q_{e}^{(s)}.

(3.3)

Since
Q_{e}^{(s)}=Q_{e}^{(s)}P_{\rm R}=P_{\rm R}Q_{e}^{(s)}
we view each
M_{e}
as an operator on
{\rm Ran}P_{{\rm S},e}
, the eigenspace of
L_{\rm S}
associated to the eigenvalue
e
. The operator
M(\lambda)
is then also an operator acting on the system Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}
alone. By construction,
M(\lambda)
and
L_{\rm S}
commute, and
M(0)=L_{\rm S}
.

Theorem 3.1
(Resonance expansion of propagator)

There is a constant
c_{0}>0
such that if
0<|\lambda|<c_{0}
, then we have weakly on
{\mathcal{D}}
,

e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}=e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}\otimes P_{\rm R}+P^{\perp}_{\rm R}e^{{\rm i}tP^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}+R(\lambda,t),

(3.4)

where the operator
R(\lambda,t)
satisfies

\big|\langle\phi,R(\lambda,t)\psi\rangle\big|\leq C|\lambda|^{1/4}\,K(\phi,\psi),\qquad\forall\phi,\psi\in{\mathcal{D}},

(3.5)

for constants
C
and
K
independent of
t\geq 0
.

If interested in
\langle\phi,e^{-{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}\psi\rangle
,
t\geq 0
, then it suffices to take the adjoint of (
3.4
). The choice of the sign in the exponent on the left side of (
3.4
) is motivated by the application of our result to the reduced system dynamics,
[
27
]
. The generator
M(\lambda)
is defined in its diagonalized form (
3.3
) and so
e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}
is easily obtained from the functional calculus: The equality (
3.4
) means that for all
\phi,\psi\in{\mathcal{D}}
,

\displaystyle\langle\phi,e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}\Big[\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{e}^{\rm dec}}e^{{\rm i}t(e+\lambda^{2}a_{e}^{(s)})}\big\langle\phi,Q_{e}^{(s)}\psi\big\rangle+\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{e}^{\rm osc}}\,e^{{\rm i}tE_{e}^{(s)}(\lambda)}\big\langle\phi,Q_{e}^{(s)}\psi\big\rangle\Big]

(3.6)

\displaystyle+\langle\phi,P^{\perp}_{\rm R}e^{{\rm i}tP^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\rangle+\langle\phi,R(\lambda,t)\psi\rangle.

Discussion of Theorem
3.1

(i)

The operator
M(\lambda)
generally
contains all orders of
\lambda
in the oscillatory parts
E_{e}^{(s)}(\lambda)
, see (
3.3
). It is clear that we need to include the
exact
phases (eigenvalues
E_{e}^{(s)}(\lambda)
of
L_{\lambda}
to all orders in
\lambda
) in the approximate dynamics on the right side of (
3.4
), for otherwise we have diverging phase differences, and we cannot get an approximation small in
\lambda
for all times. However, in the decaying terms, we can truncate the exponents at the second order in
\lambda
, keeping only
e+\lambda^{2}a_{e}^{(s)}
, and the time decay still allows us a control uniform in
t
.

(ii)

On the finite-dimensional part, the dynamics
e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}\otimes P_{\rm R}
on the right side of (
3.4
), there are terms oscillating in time,
\propto e^{{\rm i}tE_{e}^{(s)}(\lambda)}
, and terms decaying in time,
\propto e^{{\rm i}t(e+\lambda^{2}a_{e}^{(s)})}
. The decay is exponential, with rates
\lambda^{2}{\rm Im}a_{e}^{(s)}>0
, since
|e^{{\rm i}t(e+\lambda^{2}a_{e}^{(s)})}|=e^{-\lambda^{2}t{\rm Im}a_{e}^{(s)}}
. On the infinite-dimensional part (
i.e.
, on
{\rm Ran}P_{\rm R}^{\perp}
), the dynamics is also decaying, but only at a polynomial rate
1/t^{2}
, as per (
2.8
).

(iii)

The asymptotic dynamics for times larger than all the life times
(\lambda^{2}{\rm Im}a_{e}^{(s)})^{-1}
and large enough so that the dissipative term
\langle\phi,P^{\perp}_{\rm R}e^{{\rm i}tP^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\rangle\sim t^{-2}
has decayed (
c.f.
(
2.8
)), is given by
e^{{\rm i}tL_{\lambda}}\approx e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}\otimes P_{\rm R}
. If
E_{e}^{(s)}(\lambda)=0
is the only eigenvalue of
L_{\lambda}
, then the dynamics relaxes to the final state
Q_{e}^{(s)}\otimes P_{\rm R}
modulo an error
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
.

(iv)

We may call
e^{{\rm i}tM(\lambda)}\otimes P_{\rm R}
and
P^{\perp}_{\rm R}e^{{\rm i}tP^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}
of (
3.4
) the quasi-static and dissipative parts, respectively. The decomposition (
3.4
) is reminiscent of the structure of solutions of dispersive partial differential equations, which split into a dispersive wave plus a part converging towards an invariant manifold
[
34
,
39
]
. In our case, after the decay of the dissipative term, the dynamics first approaches the quasi-static manifold spanned by the
Q_{e}^{(s)}
for all
e,s
. The orbits stay close to the quasi-static manifold for times up to
\sim 1/\lambda^{2}
, after which the dynamics moves to the final, invariant manifold spanned, modulo
O(|\lambda|^{1/4})
, by the
Q_{e}^{(s)}\otimes P_{\rm R}
with
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
and
s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{e}^{\rm osc}
.

(v)

The approach we take to prove Theorem
3.1
is similar to
[
20
]
. In that paper, an expansion of the dynamics was given as a sum of a main part plus a remainder, similar to (
3.4
). The remainder of
[
20
]
converges to zero in the limit of large times, but it is not shown to be small in
\lambda
. In the current work, the remainder is small in
\lambda
for all times (
3.5
), but it does not converge to zero for large times. This means, incidentally, that the main term of the current work has a simpler form than the one of
[
20
]
. Indeed, the current main term is a second order approximation along the decaying terms, with
s\in{\mathcal{S}_{\rm dec}}
, of that of
[
20
]
. The result of
[
20
]
is not suitable for proving the validity of the Markovian approximation, but the current result here is.

For vectors of the form

We introduce a new parameter

Proposition 4.3

If
\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}/\eta\prec 1
, then we have

\displaystyle\Big|J_{\infty}(t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle dz-\langle\phi,P^{\perp}_{\rm R}e^{{\rm i}tP^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\ \lambda^{2}e^{wt}\Big[\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{\eta}+\frac{g\varkappa_{1}}{(\eta+\vartheta)^{2}}+\frac{g\varkappa^{2}_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta^{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\vartheta\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}(\eta+\vartheta+|\lambda|)+\alpha\kappa}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{g}\big(1/\eta+1/g\big)\Big)\Big]\|\phi\|\,\|\psi\|

\displaystyle+\eta e^{wt}C_{1}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle+e^{wt}\Big(\frac{|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}+\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}+\frac{|\lambda|^{3}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\Big[1+\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta}\Big]\Big){\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)

\displaystyle+e^{wt}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\Big[1+\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}(1+1/\eta)\Big]\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\phi)\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)

\displaystyle+e^{wt}\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}{\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\|\phi\|\Big(\|P_{\rm R}I\|\|P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\|+\max_{j}C_{1}\big(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\psi\big)\Big)

\displaystyle+e^{wt}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\eta}{\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\phi)\Big(\|P_{\rm R}I\|\|P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\|+\max_{j}C_{1}\big(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\psi\big)\Big).

(4.85)

In the last line, we use the notation
\bar{L}_{\lambda}\equiv P^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\upharpoonright_{{\rm Ran}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}
.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.
Consider two adjacent eigenvalues
e<e^{\prime}
of
L_{\rm S}
and set

{\mathcal{G}}^{e}_{\infty}=\{x-{\rm i}w:e+\eta\leq x\leq e^{\prime}-\eta\}.

(4.86)

We introduce a new small parameter
\vartheta>0
and set
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}=\{x-{\rm i}w:e+\eta\leq x\leq e+\eta+\vartheta\}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}=\{x-{\rm i}w:e+\eta+\vartheta\leq x\leq e+\tfrac{e^{\prime}-e}{2}\}
, as depicted in Fig.
4
.

We use the Feshbach decomposition (
4.3
) with
Q=P_{\rm R}=|\Omega_{\rm R}\rangle\langle\Omega_{\rm R}|
and accordingly, we get three contributions to the resolvent as in (
4.23
),

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}e^{{\rm i}tz}R_{z}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}e^{{\rm i}tz}{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}dz+\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}e^{{\rm i}tz}{\mathfrak{B}}(z)dz+\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}e^{{\rm i}tz}R^{P_{\rm R}}_{z}dz

(4.87)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle D^{\prime}_{1}+D^{\prime}_{2}+D^{\prime}_{3}.

Estimating
D^{\prime}_{1}
of (
4.87
).
The main bound we prove is given in (
4.95
). We have, in the sense of operators acting on
{\rm Ran}P_{\rm R}
,

{\mathfrak{F}}(z)=P_{\rm R}\big(L_{\rm S}-z-\lambda^{2}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}I\big)P_{\rm R}=\big(\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}-\lambda^{2}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\big)(L_{\rm S}-z).

(4.88)

For
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}
,
\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\leq 1/\eta
and by (
5.2
) we get
\|\lambda^{2}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\prec\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}

and so we get from (
4.88
), since
\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\prec 1
,

{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}=(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\big[\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}+\lambda^{2}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}+S_{4}\big],\qquad\|S_{4}\|\prec\frac{\varkappa^{2}_{1}\lambda^{4}}{\eta^{2}}.

(4.89)

We consider now
z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
(the region closer to
e
than to
e^{\prime}
, see Fig. 4). By inserting
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{\rm S}=P_{{\rm S},e}+P_{{\rm S},e}^{\perp}
, using that
P_{e}=P_{{\rm S},e}\otimes P_{\rm R}
and that
\|P_{{\rm S},e}^{\perp}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\leq 2/g
(see (
2.17
) for the gap
g
), we estimate the term in (
4.89
) involving the resolvent
R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}
as follows,

\Big\|\lambda^{2}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(e-z)^{2}}P_{e}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{e}\Big\|\prec\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{g}\big(1/\eta+1/g\big).

(4.90)

By (
5.2
) we have
\|P_{e}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{e}-P_{e}I(R_{e}^{P_{\rm R}}|_{\lambda=0})IP_{e}\|\prec\varkappa_{1}(|z-e|+|\lambda|)
. Thus, taking into account the definition (
2.12
) of the level shift operator
\Lambda_{e}
and using that
P^{\perp}_{\rm R}IP_{e}=P^{\perp}_{e}IP_{e}
, we have

\|P_{e}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{e}-\Lambda_{e}\|\prec\varkappa_{1}\big(|z-e|+|\lambda|\big).

(4.91)

The reason for introducing the new small parameter
\vartheta
(see Fig. 4), is that for
z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
, the right hand side of (
4.91
) is bounded above by
\varkappa_{1}(\eta+\vartheta+|\lambda|)
. It then follows from (
4.91
) and (
4.90
) that
\forall z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
,

\|\lambda^{2}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\prec\frac{\lambda^{2}\|\Lambda_{e}\|}{\eta^{2}}+\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}\Big[\frac{\eta+\vartheta+|\lambda|}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{1}{g}\big(1/\eta+1/g\big)\Big]

and hence, as
\|\Lambda_{e}\|\prec\alpha\kappa
, we get

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\lambda^{2}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}dz\Big\|

(4.92)

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\vartheta e^{wt}\Big[\frac{\varkappa_{1}(\eta+\vartheta+|\lambda|)+\alpha\kappa}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{g}\big(1/\eta+1/g\big)\Big].

Next we find a bound for the integral on the left side of (
4.92
) with
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
replaced by
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
. For
z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
, we have
\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\leq(\eta+\vartheta)^{-1}
and so it follows from (
5.2
) that
\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\prec\varkappa_{1}(\eta+\vartheta)^{-2}
. Consequently, since
|{\mathcal{D}}_{2}|<g/2
, we get

\Big\|\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{2}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\lambda^{2}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}dz\Big\|\prec\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}ge^{wt}}{(\eta+\vartheta)^{2}}.

(4.93)

We now combine (
4.92
) and (
4.93
) with (
4.89
),

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\,{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}dz-\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}tz}}{L_{\rm S}-z}dz\Big\|

(4.94)

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}e^{wt}\Big[\frac{g\varkappa_{1}}{(\eta+\vartheta)^{2}}+\frac{g\varkappa^{2}_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta^{3}}+\vartheta\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}(\eta+\vartheta+|\lambda|)+\alpha\kappa}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{g}\big(1/\eta+1/g\big)\Big)\Big].

For
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}
lying closer to
e^{\prime}
than
e
(
i.e.
,
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}\backslash({\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2})
) the analysis is the same, as only the distance from
x
to the nearest eigenvalue of
L_{\rm S}
plays a role in the estimates. We conclude that the bound (
4.94
) holds with
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
replaced by
{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}
in the integrals on the left side:

\displaystyle\Big\|D^{\prime}_{1}-\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}tz}}{L_{\rm S}-z}dz\Big\|

(4.95)

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}e^{wt}\Big[\frac{g\varkappa_{1}}{(\eta+\vartheta)^{2}}+\frac{g\varkappa^{2}_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta^{3}}+\vartheta\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}(\eta+\vartheta+|\lambda|)+\alpha\kappa}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{g}\big(1/\eta+1/g\big)\Big)\Big].

Estimating
D^{\prime}_{2}
of (
4.87
).
Our main etimate is given in (
4.102
). According to (
4.5
), the term
{\mathcal{B}}(z)
gives three contributions. The ones involving only one resolvent
R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}
are all estimated in the same way as follows. We have for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
(use (
2.6
)),

\big|\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle\big|\prec\max_{j}\big|\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R}\rangle\big|\,C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi).

(4.96)

According to (
4.89
), the main term of
{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}
is
(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}
the norm of which is bounded above by
1/\eta
for
z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
and by
1/(\eta+\vartheta)
for
z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
. Then,

\Big|\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\lambda IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz\Big|\prec e^{wt}\Big(\frac{|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}+\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}\Big)\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi).

(4.97)

The terms in (
4.89
), which are of order two and higher in
\lambda
, are estimated as

\Big\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\big[\lambda^{2}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}+S_{4}\big]\Big\|\prec\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\Big[1+\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta}\Big]

(4.98)

for
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}
. It follows from (
4.89
), (
4.97
) and (
4.98
) that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}\lambda IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz\Big|\prec

\displaystyle\ e^{wt}\Big(\frac{|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}+\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}+\frac{|\lambda|^{3}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\Big[1+\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta}\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\times\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi).

(4.99)

The same upper bound is achieved for the term involving
R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\lambda IP_{\rm R}{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}
in (
4.5
). To deal with the term in (
4.5
) involving the resolvent twice, we use the bound

\lambda^{2}\big|\langle\phi,R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}I{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}IP_{\rm R}R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle\big|\prec\lambda^{2}\|{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\phi)C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi).

(4.100)

From (
4.89
),
\|{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}\|\prec\frac{1}{\eta}[1+\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}/\eta]
for
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}
, which gives, combined with (
4.100
) and (
4.99
) the result

\displaystyle\big|\langle\phi,D^{\prime}_{2}\psi\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{B}}(z)\psi\rangle dz\Big|

(4.101)

\displaystyle\prec\ e^{wt}\Big(\frac{|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}+\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}+\frac{|\lambda|^{3}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\Big[1+\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta}\Big]\Big){\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{wt}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\Big[1+\frac{\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}}{\eta}\Big]\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\phi)C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi).

(4.102)

Estimating
D^{\prime}_{3}
of (
4.87
).
Recalling the definition (
4.86
) of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}
and using (
2.6
), we extend the integration by an amount of
2\eta
to
e\leq x\leq e^{\prime}
,

\langle\varphi,D^{\prime}_{3}\psi\rangle=\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{e}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz=\int_{e}^{e^{\prime}}e^{{\rm i}t(x-{\rm i}w)}\langle\phi,R_{x-{\rm i}w}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dx+S_{5},

(4.103)

with

\|S_{5}\|\prec\eta e^{wt}C_{1}(\phi,\psi).

(4.104)

Estimates on the infinite parts of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}
.
So far in this section, we have dealt with all
z
between any two eigenvalues of
L_{\rm S}
, see Figs. f1 and f3. Let
e_{+}
and
e_{-}
be the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
L_{\rm S}
, respectively and set

{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}=\{x-{\rm i}w:x\geq e_{+}+\eta\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}=\{x-{\rm i}w:x\leq e_{-}-\eta\}.

(4.105)

We choose
Q=P_{\rm R}
for the projection in the Feshbach decomposition (
4.3
). We use (
4.88
) to expand

{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}=(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\sum_{n\geq 0}\lambda^{2n}\big[P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\big]^{n},

(4.106)

which converges for
\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\prec 1
. For
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}
, we have
\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\|\leq 1/\eta
and so, since
\lambda^{2}\varkappa_{1}/\eta\prec 1
, the series (
4.106
) converges uniformly in
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}
an uniformly in
w
. Splitting off the first term in the series (
4.106
) gives

\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}}e^{{\rm i}tz}{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}dz=\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}tz}}{L_{\rm S}-z}dz+{\mathcal{I}},

(4.107)

where (
z=x-{\rm i}w
)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{I}}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{wt}\int_{e_{+}+\eta}^{\infty}\big\|(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\sum_{n\geq 1}[\lambda^{2}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}]^{n}\big\|dx

(4.108)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{wt}\int_{e_{+}+\eta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x-e_{+}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(C\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2})^{n}}{(x-e_{+})^{n}}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{wt}\sum_{n\geq 1}(C\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2})^{n}\int_{\eta}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{n+1}}=e^{wt}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\Big(\frac{C\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{wt}\frac{C\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\sum_{n\geq 0}\Big(\frac{C\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\Big)^{n}\prec e^{wt}\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta},

since
\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}\prec 1
. The analysis and estimates for
z\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}
are the same.
We conclude that

\Big\|\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}}e^{{\rm i}tz}{\mathfrak{F}}(z)^{-1}dz-\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}tz}}{L_{\rm S}-z}dz\Big\|\prec e^{wt}\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{2}}{\eta}.

(4.109)

Next we need to estimate
\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{B}}(z)\psi\rangle dz
, where
{\mathfrak{B}}(z)
is the sum of three terms according to (
4.5
). In each term, we use (
4.106
) to split off the main part. Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{B}}(z)=

\displaystyle-\lambda(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}-\lambda R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}+{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}(z),

(4.110)

which defines the quantity
{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}(z)
. Proceeding as in the derivation of (
4.108
), we get

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}}

\displaystyle e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}(z)\psi\rangle dz\Big|

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\quad e^{wt}\frac{\varkappa_{1}|\lambda|^{3}}{\eta}{\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{wt}\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{4}}{\eta}\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega,\psi)\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega,\phi).

(4.111)

Now we analyze the integral associated to the first term in (
4.110
), which is given by
-\lambda\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz
. We split the integration domain into
e_{+}+\eta\leq x\leq e_{+}+\eta+\vartheta
and
x\geq e_{+}+\eta+\vartheta
. On the compact domain, the integral has the bound
\prec e^{wt}\frac{|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)
, where the factor
1/\eta
is due to the resolvent
(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}
and
\vartheta
is the length of the interval of integration. On the infinite integration domain, we need to control the integrability for large
x
. Denoting
\bar{L}_{\lambda}\equiv P^{\perp}_{\rm R}L_{\lambda}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\upharpoonright_{{\rm Ran}P^{\perp}_{\rm R}}
, we have

R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}=(z+{\rm i})^{-1}\big[-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}+R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\big]

(4.112)

so that (
z=x-{\rm i}w
)

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{e_{+}+\eta+\vartheta}^{\infty}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,

\displaystyle(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz\Big|

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\ e^{wt}\|\phi\|\Big(\|P_{\rm R}I\|\|P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\|+\max_{j}C_{1}\big(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\psi\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\times\int_{e_{+}+\eta+\vartheta}^{\infty}\ \frac{dx}{|e_{+}-z||z+{\rm i}|}.

(4.113)

The last integral is
\prec(\eta+\vartheta)^{-1}
, thus

\displaystyle\Big|\lambda\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,

\displaystyle(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}P_{\rm R}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz\Big|

\displaystyle\prec\

\displaystyle e^{wt}\frac{|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)

\displaystyle+e^{wt}\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}\|\phi\|\Big(\|P_{\rm R}I\|\|P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\|+\max_{j}C_{1}\big(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\psi\big)\Big).

(4.114)

Of course, we get the same upper bound for
-\lambda\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle dz
(see (
4.110
)), with
\phi
and
\psi
exchanged on the right side of (
4.114
). Finally, we estimate

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}}e^{{\rm i}tz}\langle\phi,\,

\displaystyle R_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}IP_{\rm R}(L_{\rm S}-z)^{-1}IR_{z}^{P_{\rm R}}\psi\rangle dz\Big|

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\ e^{wt}\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\phi)\Big(\|P_{\rm R}I\|\|P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\|+\max_{j}C_{1}\big(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\psi\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\times\int_{e_{+}+\eta}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{|e_{+}-z||z+{\rm i}|}.

(4.115)

The last integral is
\prec 1/\eta
. Collecting the bounds (
4.111
), (
4.114
) and (
4.115
) and using them in (
4.110
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{G}}_{\infty}^{-}}\langle\phi,{\mathfrak{B}}(z)\psi\rangle dz\Big|

\displaystyle\prec e^{wt}\frac{\varkappa_{1}|\lambda|^{3}+|\lambda|\vartheta}{\eta}{\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\|\phi\|\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},\psi)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{wt}\frac{\varkappa_{1}\lambda^{4}}{\eta}\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega,\psi)\max_{j}C_{1}(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega,\phi)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{wt}\frac{|\lambda|}{\eta+\vartheta}{\mathfrak{S}}_{\phi\leftrightarrow\psi}\|\phi\|\Big(\|P_{\rm R}I\|\|P^{\perp}_{\rm R}\psi\|+\max_{j}C_{1}\big(I\varphi_{j}\otimes\Omega_{\rm R},(\bar{L}_{\lambda}+{\rm i})\psi\big)\Big)

6.5
) holds when applied to vectors of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\chi\end{pmatrix}
with
\chi\in{\rm Dom}(Q^{\perp}HQ^{\perp})
. Moreover, each single product operation of the matrices in the threefold matrix product in (
6.5
) makes sense individually (is well defined, when the right side is applied to vectors of the indicated form).

Due to the assumption of the theorem,
QHQ^{\perp}R_{z}^{Q}Q^{\perp}HQ
has a limit when
z=E-{\rm i}\epsilon
with
\epsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}
. Call this limit
QHQ^{\perp}(\bar{H}-E)^{-1}Q^{\perp}HQ
(the overlined
\bar{H}
indicates that
H
is restricted to the range of
Q^{\perp}
). Then
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}}{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E-{\rm i}\epsilon;Q)=Q(H-E-HQ^{\perp}(\bar{H}-E)^{-1}Q^{\perp}H)Q\equiv{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E;Q)
, where the last symbol is again a definition. Suppose that
\varphi=Q\varphi
is such that
{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E;Q)\varphi=0
. For
\epsilon>0
, define
\chi_{\epsilon}=-Q^{\perp}R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}Q^{\perp}HQ\varphi
. Then applying equation (
6.5
) with
z=E-{\rm i}\epsilon
gives

(H-E+{\rm i}\epsilon)(\varphi+\chi_{\epsilon})={\mathfrak{F}}(H-E+{\rm i}\epsilon;Q)\varphi\longrightarrow 0\quad\mbox{as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}$.}

(6.6)

We show below that
\chi_{\epsilon}
has a limit
\chi
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}
. It then follows from (
6.6
) that
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}}(H-E)(\varphi+\chi_{\epsilon})=0
. Since
H
is a closed operator, the vector
\varphi+\chi
is in the domain of
H
and
(H-E)(\varphi+\chi)=0
.

We now show that
\chi_{\epsilon}
is Cauchy. This part of the analysis is inspired by [DJ], Theorem 3.8. Denote by
p
the spectral projection of
{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E;Q)
associated to the eigenvalue
0
. In particular,
p\varphi=\varphi
. Since
{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E;Q)
is a dissipative operator and
0
is on the boundary of its numerical range,
0
is a semisimple eigenvalue and
p
is the orthogonal projection onto the kernel of
{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E;Q)
, in particular,
p^{*}=p
.
First we show that
z\mapsto pHR_{z}^{Q}Hp
is a continuously differentiable function of
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\cup{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\{E\}
. (Note:
QHR_{z}^{Q}HQ
, without the restriction to the range of
p
, is not even continuous at
E
, because the limits coming from
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
do not coincide.) We point out that
z\mapsto QHQ^{\perp}R_{z}^{Q}Q^{\perp}HQ
is continuously differentiable on
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\{E\}
, which follows by taking the adjoint in the assumption of the theorem. Next, we have
p{\mathfrak{F}}(H-E;Q)p=0
and hence
p(H-E)p=pHR^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{+}}Hp
, and taking the adjoint gives
p(H-E)p=pHR^{Q}_{E+{\rm i}0_{+}}Hp
. So we have

pHR^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{+}}Hp=pHR^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{-}}Hp=p(H-E)p,

(6.7)

which shows that
z\mapsto pHR^{Q}_{z}Hp
is continuous at
z=E
and its value at
z=E
is self-adjoint. Next, let
\gamma(\tau)
,
\tau\in[-1,1]
, be a smooth curve in
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\cup\{E\}
which is tangent to the point
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
, satisfying
\gamma(0)=E
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=1
. Then
\tau\mapsto pHR^{Q}_{\gamma(\tau)}Hp
is continuously differentiable at
\tau=0
and
\frac{d}{d\tau}|_{\tau=0}\,{\rm Im}\,pHR_{\gamma(\tau)}^{Q}Hp={\rm Im}\,pH(R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{+}})^{2}Hp
. On the other hand, this last derivative has to be equal to zero for the following reason. For all
\tau
we have
{\rm Im}\,pHR_{\gamma(\tau)}^{Q}Hp\leq 0
and from (
6.7
),
{\rm Im}\,pHR_{\gamma(\tau)}^{Q}Hp=0
at
\tau=0
. If
\frac{d}{d\tau}|_{\tau=0}\,{\rm Im}\,pHR_{\gamma(\tau)}^{Q}Hp
was
>0
or
<0
this would contradict the fact that
{\rm Im}\,pHR_{\gamma(\tau)}^{Q}Hp\leq 0
. Since this derivative vanishes, we get
{\rm Im}\,pH(R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{+}})^{2}Hp=0
, so
pH(R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{+}})^{2}Hp
is self-adjoint, which means that
pH(R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{+}})^{2}Hp=pH(R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}0_{-}})^{2}Hp
. So
z\mapsto pHR^{Q}_{z}Hp
is continuously differentiable on
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\cup{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\{E\}
.

We now use this regularity property to show that
\chi_{\epsilon}
converges. Let
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. We have

\big\|(R_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}^{Q}-R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}})HQ\varphi\big\|^{2}=\left\langle{\varphi},{QH(R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon}^{Q}-R^{Q}_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}})(R_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}^{Q}-R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}})HQ\varphi}\right\rangle.

(6.8)

From the resolvent identity,
R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}^{Q}=\frac{1}{{\rm i}(\epsilon^{\prime}+\epsilon)}(R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}-R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}^{Q})
and similarly for the other three terms on the right side of (
6.8
). Therefore, (also using that
pQ=Qp=p
),

\displaystyle\big\|(R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon}^{Q}-R^{Q}_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}})HQ\varphi\big\|^{2}

(6.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\varphi},{pH\tfrac{R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}-R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon}^{Q}}{2{\rm i}\epsilon}Hp\varphi}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\varphi},{pH\tfrac{R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon}-R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}^{Q}}{{\rm i}(\epsilon^{\prime}+\epsilon)}Hp\varphi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle{\varphi},{pH\tfrac{R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}-R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon}^{Q}}{{\rm i}(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})}Hp\varphi}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\varphi},{pH\tfrac{R^{Q}_{E-{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}-R_{E+{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}^{Q}}{2{\rm i}\epsilon^{\prime}}Hp\varphi}\right\rangle.

(6.10)

Since
z\mapsto pHR_{z}^{Q}Hp
is a continuously differentiable function of
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}\cup{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup\{E\}
, the right hand side of (
6.10
) converges to zero as
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
. Thus
\chi_{\epsilon}
converges.
\Box

Conclusion

In this paper we consider the evolution of a finite-dimensional system coupled to a reservoir. The coupling is small, but fixed. The reservoir is assumed to have a unique stationary state and otherwise dissipative dynamics. We develop a method to separate the total, unitary evolution of a
{\rm S}{\rm R}
complex into a Markovian part, a dissipative part, plus a remainder. The Markovian part describes the system dynamics with the reservoir in its stationary state and contains explicit oscillating (in time) and exponentially decaying terms. The dissipative term governs the dynamics on
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states which do not have any overlap with the system stationary state. This term decays polynomially in time. The remainder term is small in the coupling,
uniformly for all
t\geq 0
. The strengths of our results are:

-

The decomposition is derived mathematically rigorously, with a controlled remainder which is small in the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
coupling, for all times
t\geq 0
.

-

We can treat
{\rm S}{\rm R}
interactions with significantly less regularity than previously needed. In terms of the reservoir correlation function, this means that only polynomial decay is required (versus exponential decay in previous rigorous works).

-

We analyze the whole
{\rm S}{\rm R}
dynamics, not only the reduced system dynamics and moreover, the initial
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states do not need to be of product form (they can be classically correlated or entangled).

-

Our method applies to a widely used class of open systems: An
N
-level system coupled linearly to a spatially infinitely extended reservoir of thermal, non-interacting Bose particles. Detailed results on this model are presented in
[
27
,
28
]
. In particular, it is shown there that the system dynamics is well approximated by the Markovian master equation for all times
t\geq 0
, even for initially correlated
{\rm S}{\rm R}
states. It is also shown there that the Born approximation is valid for all times.
