The present work originates from the interplay between these concepts and higher-spin gravity theories. Indeed, despite heavy constraints imposed by no-go theorems on interacting higher-spins in Minkowski spacetime (see

Another key ingredient of this work is the mathematical language of curved Carrollian geometry

Despite this large body of existing knowledge, the line of thought pursued here appears to be new. Namely, inspired by the key role of the singleton—also known as the “Rac”
[
26
]
—in higher-spin AdS/CFT, we study the asymptotic kinematics of a massless scalar field near null infinity
[
58
,
59
,
60

and use it to define higher-spin BMS algebras based on Carrollian geometry. Our paper is thus a step towards flat higher-spin holography, understood as an analogue of the holographic dictionary between a scalar bulk field with critical mass in AdS and the higher-spin symmetry of its boundary data. In practice, the analysis is carried out in arbitrary bulk spacetime dimension
d+2
, involving
d
-dimensional celestial spheres
S^{d}
, and yields two inequivalent Minkowskian analogues of the usual singleton:

1.

The Wick-rotated Rac (WRac)
consists of time-independent field configurations at null infinity that stem from “overleading” solutions of the d’Alembert equation. It is essentially obtained upon replacing
\mathfrak{so}(d,2)
by
\mathfrak{so}(d+1,1)
in the group-theoretic definition of the Rac
[
26
]
and should therefore look familiar to higher-spin experts, as it is formally described by the same equations up to proper changes of signature. In fact, it shares several important qualitative features with the Rac—it has no bulk degrees of freedom and lives on the conformal boundary—save for one cardinal property: it is
not
unitarisable. Another unsatisfactory feature of the WRac is that is
not
a faithful representation of BMS (nor of Poincaré); only the Lorentz subgroup is represented faithfully.

2.

The Sachs module

[
4
]
consists of time-dependent configurations on null infinity, determined by radiative solutions of the d’Alembert equation. It seems to be the best candidate for an analogue of the Rac in flat spacetime, furnishing a representation of Poincaré and BMS that is both faithful and unitary
[
4
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
. Its main weakness is its generality: as we shall see, it furnishes a unitary representation not only of BMS, but of the immensely larger group of all Carrollian bundle automorphisms.

The peculiarities of these two setups suggest that flat higher-spin holography must be quite different from its AdS cousin. We shall proceed nonetheless and define new Carrollian higher-spin symmetries by considering algebras of differential operators on null infinity that preserve these structures. The WRac will thus yield an algebra isomorphic to the standard one of bosonic higher-spin gravity
[
67
,
68
]
on a de Sitter spacetime in one less dimension, signalling that it is “too small” to provide a suitable starting point for higher-spin gravity in Minkowski spacetime since all supertranslations are quotiented out. On the other hand, the symmetry algebra of the Sachs module will define a novel candidate higher-spin extension of BMS, whose spectrum of generators turns out to contain the BMS Killing tensors obtained in
[
38
]
from the asymptotic symmetries of free massless higher-spin fields. Since the space of symmetries encountered in
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
only had a vector structure, our present work may be seen as a way to endow it with a Lie bracket. A corollary of this proposal is also a sharp distinction between symmetry algebras stemming from distinct choices of fall-off conditions; choosing the “correct” higher-spin BMS algebra for a putative higher-spin theory around Minkowski spacetime requires the selection of certain preferred fall-offs.

The paper is organised as follows. We start in section
2
by writing the asymptotic solution of the d’Alembert equation near null infinity. Its leading piece then yields either the WRac, or the Sachs module of the BMS group. In particular, we extend the Sachs Hermitian form to any dimension and relate it to the standard Poincaré-invariant one for scalar massless unitary representations. Section
3
is devoted to a detailed, self-contained review of Carrollian geometry and its relation to (generalised) BMS symmetry. This also allows us to write the Sachs inner product in a coordinate-independent way such that invariance under bundle automorphisms becomes manifest. We stress that sections
2
–
3
are devoid of higher spins and may be of general interest to anyone working on asymptotic symmetries and flat holography. Finally, section
4
is devoted to the definition of higher-spin extensions of BMS based on Carrollian geometry and the two aforementioned representations (WRac and Sachs). We briefly conclude with a discussion on the fall-offs and spectra of bulk gauge fields needed for a putative higher-spin theory to admit these algebras as asymptotic symmetries.

2
BMS action on massless scalars

In this section, we solve d’Alembert’s equation
\square\Phi=0
as an asymptotic series in the inverse distance away from the origin in Bondi coordinates, and find that the space of solutions supports a linear action of the BMS algebra. We then briefly recall certain definitions pertaining to the transformations of densities on manifolds under diffeomorphisms, before identifying the densities that occur in the asymptotic expansion of scalars. Thus, an “overleading” choice of fall-offs naturally leads to the WRac solution mentioned in the
introduction
, while radiative fall-offs are such that the field’s leading component defines the Sachs module. Related considerations have been put forward in
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
69
]
in the context of asymptotic symmetries. The Carrollian perspective on these results is relegated to section
3
, while their higher-spin implications will be treated in section
4
.

2.1
Asymptotic scalar dynamics

Here we briefly recall the expression of Poincaré and BMS generators in Bondi coordinates, then study the asymptotic expansion of solutions of the d’Alembert equation near null infinity. This will motivate the existence of two distinguished kinds of boundary data: the first, time-dependent but unconstrained, will eventually lead to the Sachs module. The other, time-independent but singular, will yield a WRac.

Asymptotic d’Alembert equation.

Now let

{\cal L}=\sqrt{g}\,\nabla_{\mu}\Phi^{*}\nabla^{\mu}\Phi

Wick-rotated Rac (WRac)

The remainder of this section is devoted to a detailed group-theoretic analysis of these leading and subleading components: following a brief aside (section

) on densities and weights, the WRac is discussed in section

2.2
An interlude on densities and weights

This work is concerned with transformation laws of various (mostly scalar) fields on celestial spheres under the BMS group, and in particular under celestial diffeomorphisms or conformal maps, so it is crucial to fix our conventions in that respect. What follows therefore spells out our definitions for Radon densities, volumic densities, conformal densities, conformal primaries, and the accompanying terminology.

A word of caution may be in order here. The abstract content of this subsection may seem off-putting to physicists, especially as we start with some measure-theoretic concepts that are not normally covered in the physics literature. However, to the extent that our first objective is to understand BMS transformations of the
\phi_{n}
’s in the expansion (
10
), it is essential to define an action of diffeomorphisms on densities when the underlying metric (
e.g.
that of a celestial sphere) is not flat. This problem turns out to require a bit of mathematical machinery that, to our knowledge, has been overlooked in the literature on BMS; we attempt to fill that gap here, as it will even play a Carrollian role in section
3.4
. (For an introduction to measure theory, see
e.g.

[
75
]
.)

Radon densities.

Consider an orientable
d
-dimensional manifold
\mathscr{M}
and endow it with a measure
\mu
.
4
4

4

For any measurable set
\mathscr{N}\subseteq\mathscr{M}
, one writes
\mu(\mathscr{N}):=\int_{\mathscr{N}}\text{d}\mu
.
The latter is equivalent to a volume form
\varepsilon
,
i.e.
a nowhere-vanishing top form; given local coordinates
x^{a}=(x^{1},\cdots,x^{d})
, any such form reads
5
5

5

We abuse notation slightly by using the same letter
\varepsilon
to denote both the volume form
\varepsilon
and its “density function”
\varepsilon(x)
. We also write
\text{d}\mu
for the volume form corresponding to the measure
\mu
. This notation is common but rather abusive since this differential form is
not
exact in general.

\text{d}\mu=\varepsilon=\varepsilon(x)\,\text{d}^{d}x

(28)

where
\varepsilon(x)
is some strictly positive function on
\mathscr{M}
and
\text{d}^{d}x:=\text{d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\text{d}x^{d}
. This applies in particular when
\mathscr{M}
carries a metric
g_{ab}
, in which case
\varepsilon(x)=\sqrt{g(x)}:=\sqrt{\det g_{ab}(x)}
.

Now suppose some group acts on
\mathscr{M}
by orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms
x\to x^{\prime}=F(x)
. In general, such transformations deform the measure in the sense that
F^{*}\varepsilon\neq\varepsilon
: the pullback by
F
need not preserve the volume form. However, it is certainly true that
F^{*}\varepsilon=\rho_{F}\,\varepsilon
(or, equivalently, in coordinates
\text{d}\mu\big(F(x)\big)=\rho_{F}(x)\,\text{d}\mu(x)
) for some positive function
\rho_{F}(x)
known as the
Radon-Nikodym derivative
of
\mu

[
76
, sec. 18.4]
, proportional to the Jacobian of
F
. It is indeed straightforward to compute
\rho_{F}
for any diffeomorphism acting on a volume form: writing the diffeomorphism as
x\to F(x)
, eq. (
28
) yields

\rho_{F}(x)=\frac{\text{d}\mu\big(F(x)\big)}{\text{d}\mu(x)}=\frac{\varepsilon\big(F(x)\big)}{\varepsilon(x)}\,\bigg|\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\bigg|\,,

(29)

where
|\partial F/\partial x|:=|\det(\partial x^{\prime a}/\partial x^{b})|
is the Jacobian of the map
x\to x^{\prime}=F(x)
in local coordinates. It is then straightforward to verify the cocycle property
\rho_{F_{1}\circ F_{2}}=F_{2}^{*}(\rho_{F_{1}})\,\rho_{F_{2}}

[
77
, sec. 3.2.3]
, either from the pullback definition
F^{*}\varepsilon=\rho_{F}\,\varepsilon
, or from the coordinate expression (
29
). Again, all this applies to measures induced by metrics, in which case

\rho_{F}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{g\big(F(x)\big)}{g(x)}}\,\,\bigg|\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\bigg|\,.

(30)

We stress that the presence of the same metric
g
in both the numerator and the denominator of (
30
) is not a typo: the metric was
not
transformed with the usual transformation law under the diffeomorphism
x\to x^{\prime}
because the measure density
\varepsilon(x)=\sqrt{g(x)}
is understood as being fixed in the present setting. In fact, had the metric been transformed following the usual formula

g^{\prime}_{ab}(x^{\prime})=\frac{\partial x^{c}}{\partial x^{\prime a}}\frac{\partial x^{d}}{\partial x^{\prime b}}\,g_{cd}(x)\,,

(31)

the naïve candidate
\sqrt{g^{\prime}(x^{\prime})/g(x)}\,|\partial x^{\prime}/\partial x|
would have equalled one. By contrast, the actual Radon-Nikodym derivative (
30
) is typically a highly non-trivial function on
\mathscr{M}
.

Having fixed the terminology, we are now ready to state the following definition: a
Radon density
with weight
w
on
\mathscr{M}
is a function
\phi(x)
that transforms under any diffeomorphism
F:x\to x^{\prime}
as
\phi\to F\cdot\phi:=(\rho_{F^{-1}})^{\texttt{w}}\,\phi\circ F^{-1}
. More explicitly, writing
F\cdot\phi:=\phi^{\prime}
and using
\rho_{F^{-1}}\circ F=\rho_{F}^{-1}
, the transformation law reads

\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\rho_{F}(x)^{-\texttt{w}}\,\phi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{s12t}}}}{{=}}\left(\frac{g(x)}{g(x^{\prime})}\right)^{\texttt{w}/2}\bigg|\,\det\left(\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{b}}\right)\,\bigg|^{-{\texttt{w}}}\,\phi(x)\,.

(32)

Here we chose to write the Radon-Nikodym derivative (
30
) only for the special case where the measure on
\mathscr{M}
is inherited from a metric; this is by far the most relevant situation we will encounter below. In particular, it is essential for later reference to work out the action of vector fields on densities from the infinitesimal version of eq. (
32
): letting
x^{\prime a}=x^{a}+\epsilon X^{a}(x)
and defining
\delta\phi:=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)
, the expansion of (
32
) yields

\delta\phi={\cal L}_{X}\phi+\texttt{w}\,\nabla_{a}X^{a}\,\phi

(33)

where
{\cal L}_{X}\phi:=X^{a}\partial_{a}\phi
and
\nabla_{a}X^{a}
is the covariant divergence of the vector field
X
. More generally, for an arbitrary volume form
\varepsilon
on
\mathscr{M}
, the factor
\nabla_{a}X^{a}
would be replaced by the divergence
\text{div}(X)
defined thanks to the Lie derivative

{\cal L}_{X}\varepsilon=\text{div}(X)\,\varepsilon\,.

(34)

In coordinates, it follows from (
28
) that
\text{div}(X)=\varepsilon(x)^{-1}\partial_{a}\big(\varepsilon(x)X^{a}(x)\big)=\partial_{a}X^{a}(x)+X^{a}(x)\,\partial_{a}\big(\ln\varepsilon(x)\big)
. For a scalar field (
\texttt{w}=0
), eq. (
33
) reduces to
\delta\phi=X^{a}\partial_{a}\phi
. An example of scalar density with non-zero weight is provided by (super)translations, whose weight
\texttt{w}=-1/d
is visible in the BMS bracket (
6
). More generally, we shall see in section
2.4
that eqs. (
32
)–(
33
) provide the basic form of the BMS transformation law of the
\phi_{n}
’s in the expansion (
10
).

One readily verifies that the definition (
32
) furnishes a representation of the group of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms of
\mathscr{M}
, in the sense that
F_{1}\cdot(F_{2}\cdot\phi)=(F_{1}\circ F_{2})\cdot\phi
. Equivalently, the commutator of two transformations of the form (
33
) generated by
X,Y
is itself of the form (
33
) generated by the Lie bracket
[X,Y]
. The vector space carrying this representation is the set
C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
of all smooth functions on
\mathscr{M}
. In what follows we denote this space by
C^{\infty}_{\texttt{w}}(\mathscr{M})
to stress the weight
w
; in particular,
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathscr{M})=C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
is the usual space of scalar fields on
\mathscr{M}
. Note that the generalisation to tensor-valued densities is straightforward: simply add the appropriate Jacobian matrices on the right-hand side of (
32
). In terms of infinitesimal transformations, eq. (
33
) then remains valid with
{\cal L}_{X}\phi
the usual Lie derivative of a tensor field.

We stress that the transformation laws (
32
)–(
33
) are the most important ones for our purposes, and for BMS generally: all densities in sections
3
–
4
will be Radon densities in the sense defined here, unless explicitly stated otherwise. Accordingly, the hasty reader may skip the next few paragraphs and go straight to section
2.3
. For now, we present a few related notions of densities, both for completeness and for comparison with other patches of the literature.

Volumic densities.

By contrast with Radon densities, a
(volumic) density
with weight
w
is a scalar field
\varphi(x)
whose transformation law reads
6
6

6

The adjective “volumic” is very much non-standard. We use it only in this subsection, for the sole purpose of clearly distinguishing inequivalent concepts of densities: Radon, volumic, and conformal.

\varphi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\bigg|\,\det\left(\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{b}}\right)\,\bigg|^{-{\texttt{w}}}\,\varphi(x)\,.

(35)

More generally, a tensor-valued (volumic) density of weight
w
is a tensor field whose usual transformation law under reparametrisations involves an extra Jacobian factor to the power
w
, exactly as in the scalar definition (
35
). The corresponding infinitesimal transformation law is given by (
33
) except that the divergence of
X
on the right-hand side now involves standard derivatives instead of covariant ones:

\delta\varphi={\cal L}_{X}\varphi+\texttt{w}\,\partial_{a}X^{a}\,\varphi\,,

(36)

where
{\cal L}_{X}
is the Lie derivative along
X

\varphi

\texttt{w}=0
) it reduces to
\delta\varphi=X^{a}\partial_{a}\varphi
. Scalar volumic densities of weight
\texttt{w}=1
on a manifold
\mathscr{M}
(not necessarily orientable) are the objects that can be integrated over
\mathscr{M}
in a coordinate-independent way.

It is clear from these definitions that volumic densities and Radon densities are closely related notions: their transformations (
32
) and (
35
) are identical save for a slightly different convention in handling the metric-induced measure. This is why we denote their weights by the same symbol
w
. Note that the transformation law (
35

any
manifold, with or without metric or measure. This is perhaps the reason why volumic densities—normally just called
densities

—are more familiar in the literature.

Conformal densities.

Independently of a density’s behaviour under diffeomorphisms, one can also define a notion of weight under Weyl transformations. The transformation law of a (scalar or tensor)
conformal density

\psi
of conformal weight
w
under Weyl transformations is given by

g_{ab}(x)\to g^{\prime}_{ab}(x)=\Omega^{2}(x)\,g_{ab}(x)\,,\qquad\psi^{\prime}(x)\,=\,\Omega(x)^{w}\,\psi(x)\,.

(37)

This is the notion of weight mentioned below the GJMS operator (
17
).

Note that a field may well be a volumic density and a conformal density simultaneously. For instance, the metric
g_{ab}
is a tensor density of volumic weight zero (since it is a covariant tensor field whose transformation law under diffeomorphisms is (
31
)) and conformal weight two (since its transformation law under Weyl transformations is (
37
)). Similarly, the volume density
\sqrt{g}
on a manifold of dimension
d
is a scalar volumic density with weight
\texttt{w}=1
in the sense of eqs. (
31
) and (
35
), and conformal weight
w=d
in the sense of (
37
).

Conformal primaries.

Finally, one may compose a Weyl transformation
g_{ab}\to\Omega^{2}g_{ab}
and a conformal map
x\to x^{\prime}
such that the metric remains unchanged:
g^{\prime}_{ab}(x^{\prime})=g_{ab}(x)
, thereby fixing the Weyl parameter to
\Omega(x)=|\det(\partial x^{\prime}/\partial x)|^{1/d}
. As a result, the corresponding conformal transformation law of a scalar density
\chi
with volumic weight
w
(in the sense of (
35
)) and conformal weight
w
(in the sense of (
37
)) reads

x\to x^{\prime},\qquad\chi(x)\to\chi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\bigg|\,\det\left(\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{b}}\right)\,\bigg|^{-\texttt{w}+w/d}\chi(x).

(38)

The same would hold for a tensor density up to additional Jacobian matrices. This is by definition the transformation law of a
conformal primary
under a conformal transformation. In particular, on a conformally flat manifold, we say that
\chi
has
scaling dimension

\Delta
if it transforms under dilations as

x\to x^{\prime}=\lambda x\,,\qquad\chi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\,=\,\lambda^{-\Delta}\,\chi(x)\,,

(39)

with
\lambda>0
. Note that the scaling dimension of a tensor field is not independent of its ranks and weights: if
\chi
is
r

s

w
, and a conformal density of weight
w
, then its scaling dimension is
\Delta=d\,\texttt{w}-w+s-r

For instance, the GJMS operator (
17
) is conformally covariant when it acts on a conformal density of weight
w=n-\tfrac{d}{2}
; in particular, on the conformally flat sphere
S^{d}
, such a density is a conformal primary of scaling dimension
\Delta=\tfrac{d}{2}-n
. Another example is provided by the fluctuation
h_{ab}
of the metric around a conformally flat background, which has rank

and is a primary field of scaling dimension zero (since

On the celestial sphere, both the metric and its determinant are conformal primary fields of scaling dimension
\Delta=0

10
); indeed, our notation in (
39

10

\phi_{0}
will turn out to have scaling dimension
\Delta

2.4
The unitary Sachs module

3
Carrollian geometry and BMS groups

Our approach has been pragmatic so far: starting from the massless field equation (
7
), we found that its space of radiative solutions carries a unitary representation of the BMS group. In order to relate this structure to higher-spin transformations, we now adopt a more intrinsic perspective based on Carrollian geometry. The latter may be seen as an ultra-relativistic limit of Lorentzian geometry when the speed of light vanishes and the metric becomes degenerate due to the appearance of one vanishing eigenvalue. This motivates the definition of automorphisms analogous to “isometries” or “conformal maps”, except that they must preserve this null direction. Accordingly, we now review the description of BMS transformations as Carrollian conformal maps, following the intrinsic (
i.e.
purely from the boundary) and geometric (
i.e.
global and coordinate-free) approach of Penrose
[
48
]
, translated in Carrollian language in
[
46
]
. We start with generalities on principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundles, their automorphisms and connections; we then introduce Carrollian spacetimes, isometries and conformal maps; finally, we define Carrollian volume forms and their symmetries to revisit the Sachs module (
51
) from a Carrollian viewpoint.

We stress that the language reviewed here will be crucial in section
4
, as it simplifies computations whose expression in coordinates is otherwise unwieldy. Still, the presentation will unavoidably be quite mathematical, so we have attempted to frame it in a pedagogical manner accessible to theoretical physicists. It is self-contained, save for basic notions of differential geometry and fibre bundles that are not reviewed in any depth; we refer
e.g.
to
[
93

94
,
95
]
for the necessary background. A pedagogical introduction to Carrollian conformal geometry is also provided

in
[

\bar{F}\circ\pi=\pi\circ F\,,

(64)

ensuring that
F
maps fibres on fibres (see
e.g.

[
93
]
). In adapted coordinates
(u,x^{a})
, it takes the form of a smooth transformation

(u,x)\to F(u,x)=\big(u^{\prime}(u,x),x^{\prime}(x))

(65)

where
x\to x^{\prime}(x)
is any diffeomorphism of the base space
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
and
u^{\prime}
is any function of
(u,x)
; see the examples in fig.
3
. Vector fields whose flow consists of such maps are precisely the projectable ones defined above, while super-projectable vector fields generate diffeomorphisms (
65
) for which
u^{\prime}=f(x)u+\alpha(x)
with
f(x)>0
. Similarly, we could have defined
principal bundle
automorphisms as bundle automorphisms
F:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
that are equivariant with respect to the action of
\mathbb{R}
on
\mathscr{M}
and thus leave the fundamental vector field invariant in the sense that
F_{*}(\xi)=\xi
. In the notation (
65
), one then has
u^{\prime}=u+\alpha(x)
. The corresponding vector fields are invariant, as defined above.

The reason we avoided this route (from finite transformations to their infinitesimal counterparts) is due to the standard recipe for higher-spin extensions of symmetries, which consists in first considering infinitesimal spacetime symmetries realised as first-order differential operators, then allowing for higher-order operators by turning to the enveloping algebra. It is indeed this approach that will be pursued in section
4
, and it exclusively involves infinitesimal generators in Lie algebras as opposed to finite transformations in Lie groups.

Basic and invariant tensor fields.

The notion of invariance, and other similar constraints, is readily extended to covariant tensor fields (
e.g.
differential forms) instead of vectors. Accordingly, a covariant tensor field
T_{\mu\nu\cdots}
on
\mathscr{M}
is called
invariant
if
{\cal L}_{\xi}T=0
,
horizontal
if any of its contractions with
\xi
vanishes, and
basic
if it is both invariant and horizontal. In this last case,
T
is the pullback by the projection (
59
) of a tensor
\bar{T}
on the base manifold
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
; in other words,
T
is basic iff
T=\pi^{*}\bar{T}
.

For example, a function
f
on
\mathscr{M}
is invariant if
{\cal L}_{\xi}f=0
; in local coordinates
(u,x^{a})
, it is any time-independent function
f(x)
. Similarly, a one-form
A
on
\mathscr{M}
is invariant if its components in adapted coordinates are
u

i.e.
if
A=f(x)\,\text{d}u+A_{a}(x)\,\text{d}x^{a}
); it is horizontal if its
u
component vanishes, so basic one-forms read
A=A_{a}(x)\text{d}x^{a}
in local coordinates. Other examples of basic tensor fields will be provided by Carrollian metrics in section
3.3
.

3.2
Ehresmann connections as Carrollian clocks

The concepts presented so far in section
3
apply to any principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle, without any Carrollian spacetime interpretation save for time translations. We now push this intuition much further by defining Carrollian clocks and horizontal surfaces, all based on a choice of Ehresmann connection on
\mathscr{M}
. This eventually leads to the notion of Euler vector fields, and it will allow us to write the algebras (
62
) as semi-direct sums that start to resemble BMS. The spacetime picture will later be further completed by the addition of a Carrollian metric in section
3.3
. We refer
e.g.
to
[
97
,
56
,
55
]
for more on the natural appearance of Ehresmann connections in Carrollian geometry.

Carrollian clocks.

Let
\mathscr{M}\twoheadrightarrow\bar{\mathscr{M}}
be a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle with fundamental vector field
\xi
, and let
p\in\mathscr{M}
be a point. Then a non-zero vector
v
tangent to
\mathscr{M}
at
p
is
time-like
if it is vertical,
i.e.
if
v\propto\xi_{p}
; otherwise
v
is
space-like
. Thus the space of time-like vectors has measure zero within the set of all tangent vectors on a Carrollian manifold, which should be contrasted with Lorentzian manifolds where time-like vectors are as “generic” as space-like ones.

Now, one typically wishes to have a global prescription splitting any tangent vector, anywhere in
\mathscr{M}
, as the sum of a time-like piece and a space-like “horizontal” one. This can be achieved with an Ehresmann connection on
(\mathscr{M},\xi)
,
i.e.
an invariant one-form
A\in\Omega^{1}(\mathscr{M})
such that
i_{\xi}A=1
everywhere. In the Carrollian context, such a connection may be called a
Carrollian clock
, since the normalization
i_{\xi}A=1
suggests the interpretation of
A
as a field of clocks “dual” to the fundamental vector field
\xi
that generates time translations. In adapted coordinates
(u,x^{a})
such that
\xi=\partial_{u}
, any clock reads
A=\text{d}u+A_{a}(x)\text{d}x^{a}
with arbitrary time-independent components
A_{a}(x)
.

The standard terminology of connections and their curvature carries over to the Carrollian realm: a clock is
locally synchronised
if the connection
A
is flat (
\text{d}A=0
), and it is
globally synchronised
if
A=\text{d}f
is exact. Note that the curvature
\text{d}A
is a basic two-form.
15
15

15

i_{\xi}(\text{d}A)={\cal L}_{\xi}A=0
and
{\cal L}_{\xi}(\text{d}A)=\text{d}i_{\xi}\text{d}A=0
thanks to Cartan’s magic formula
{\cal L}_{\xi}=\text{d}\circ i_{\xi}+i_{\xi}\circ\text{d}
.
This is actually manifest in adapted coordinates, where
\text{d}A=\partial_{[a}A_{b]}(x)\,\text{d}x^{a}\wedge\text{d}x^{b}
only involves the base form
A_{a}(x)\text{d}x^{a}
. In this sense, the only obstruction that may prevent a locally synchronised clock from being globally synchronisable occurs if the first de Rham class of
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
(
i.e.
its first Betti number) happens to be non-trivial. The total space
\mathscr{M}
plays no role for this issue, in accordance with our comment above that all principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundles are trivial.

Horizontal vectors and simultaneity.

Given a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle
(\mathscr{M},\xi)
with an Ehresmann connection
A
, a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})
is
horizontal
if
i_{X}A=0
. The condition
i_{\xi}A=1
then ensures that any horizontal vector is space-like; in adapted coordinates, any horizontal vector reads
X=X^{a}(u,x)\big(\partial_{a}-A_{a}(x)\partial_{u}\big)
with arbitrary components
X^{a}(u,x)
. The notion of horizontality also allows us to define a linear lift
\ell
from vector fields on the base
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
to invariant horizontal vector fields on
\mathscr{M}
:

\ell:\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{X}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{M}):\bar{X}\mapsto\ell(\bar{X})\,,

(66)

where
X=\ell(\bar{X})
is the unique invariant and horizontal vector field such that
\pi_{*}(X)=\bar{X}
. In adapted coordinates, this amounts to the “minimal coupling” prescription
\ell:X^{a}(x)\,\partial_{a}\mapsto X^{a}(x)\big(\partial_{a}-A_{a}(x)\partial_{u}\big)
. Note that the lift (
66
) is a morphism of Lie algebras iff the connection is flat; this is most easily verified in adapted coordinates.

Suppose now that one picks a point in
\mathscr{M}
. The set of horizontal vectors at that point is a
d
-dimensional subspace of the
(d+1)
-dimensional tangent space at that point, so the Carrollian clock
A
actually defines a distribution of
d
-planes in
\mathscr{M}
. Each such plane is a local choice of “absolute space”. When the clock is locally synchronised (
\text{d}A=0
), the distribution turns into a foliation of
\mathscr{M}
thanks to the Frobenius theorem (see
e.g.

[
94
, Chap. 11]
for details). If, in addition, the clock is globally synchronised (
A=\text{d}f
), then horizontal vector fields are those that solve
{\cal L}_{X}f=0
. In adapted coordinates, one has
f(u,x)=u+g(x)
and horizontal vectors read
X=X^{a}(u,x)(\partial_{a}-\partial_{a}g(x)\partial_{u})
, so the leaves of the foliation are submanifolds specified by an equation of the form
u=\text{cst}-g(x)
. This defines a global
Carrollian time

u^{\prime}:=f(u,x)=u+g(x)
.

To conclude, suppose one has chosen
two
clocks
A
and
A^{\prime}
. Then their difference
A^{\prime}-A
is necessarily basic. Accordingly, any change of clock mapping
A\to A^{\prime}=A+\pi^{*}\bar{B}
with
\bar{B}\in\Omega^{1}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
is called a local
Carrollian boost
. In terms of the distribution of space-like
d
-planes specified by a clock, the boost’s effect is to locally tilt the planes,
i.e.
to locally change the notion of simultaneity. We will use such boosts in section
3.4
to prove that invariant volume forms on
\mathscr{M}
induced by a volume form on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
are independent of one’s choice of Carrollian clock.

Euler vector fields.

The notion of horizontality defined by a clock will soon allow us to unveil the semi-direct structure of projectable and invariant vector fields introduced in section
3.1
. In order to also include super-projectable vector fields in this analysis, an aside is now needed on Euler vector fields. Namely, let
A=\text{d}f
be a globally synchronised clock and let
\Sigma\subset\mathscr{M}
be the corresponding leaf at Carrollian time
f=0
. Then an
Euler vector field
is a super-projectable vector field
\eta
on
\mathscr{M}
such that

{\cal L}_{\eta}\xi=-\xi\,,\qquad{\cal L}_{\eta}A=+A\,,\qquad(i_{\eta}A)|_{\Sigma}=0\,.

(67)

Any such vector field generates dilations of Carrollian time. Indeed, in adapted coordinates, it reads
\eta=u\partial_{u}+X^{a}(x)\partial_{a}
with arbitrary components
X^{a}(x)
. Euler vector fields are thus not unique; in fact the difference between two Euler vector fields
\eta,\eta^{\prime}
is an invariant horizontal vector field
Y:=\eta^{\prime}-\eta
since
{\cal L}_{\xi}Y={\cal L}_{\xi}\eta^{\prime}-{\cal L}_{\xi}\eta=0
and
i_{Y}A\big|_{\Sigma}=i_{\eta^{\prime}}A\big|_{\Sigma}-i_{\eta}A\big|_{\Sigma}=0
(the latter equality extends to
\mathscr{M}
since
A
and
Y
are both invariant). It is natural to fix this ambiguity by calling
canonical Euler vector field
the unique Euler vector field
\eta
such that
{\cal L}_{\eta}X=0
for any invariant horizontal vector field
X\in\ell\big(\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\big)
, where
\ell
is the horizontal lift (
66
). The canonical Euler vector field simply reads
\eta=u\,\partial_{u}
in adapted coordinates.

Together, the fundamental vector field
\xi
and any Euler vector field
\eta
span the affine algebra
\mathfrak{igl}(1)
of the real line, where
\xi
and
\eta
respectively generate fibrewise translations and dilations. In fact, a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle endowed with a global section
\Sigma
can be identified with a line bundle (
i.e.
a vector bundle with one-dimensional fibre) with zero section
\Sigma
, in which case there is a canonical action of the affine algebra
\mathfrak{igl}(1)
on each fibre. This affine structure will now allow us to write the algebra of super-projectable vector fields as a semi-direct sum.

Algebras of (super-)projectable and invariant vector fields.

The arsenal of clocks and horizontal vectors allows us to gain some insight in the structure of the algebras (
62
) defined above; as it happens, this will also eventually provide the semi-direct structure of BMS transformations. Accordingly, note first that all three algebras
\mathfrak{X}_{\text{pro}}(\mathscr{M})
,
\mathfrak{X}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})
and
\mathfrak{X}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{M})
of section
3.1
admit an ideal consisting of vertical vector fields. This ideal is isomorphic to
C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
in the projectable case, since any vertical vector field reads as
f\,\xi
in terms of some function
f
on
\mathscr{M}
; it is isomorphic to
\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})\,|\,{\cal L}^{2}_{\xi}f=0\}\cong C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\otimes\mathfrak{igl}(1)
in the super-projectable case, where
\mathfrak{igl}(1)
is spanned by the fundamental vector field
\xi
and the canonical Euler vector field
\eta
; and it is isomorphic to
C_{\text{inv}}^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})\,|\,{\cal L}_{\xi}f=0\}\cong C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
in the invariant case. It follows that the quotients

\frac{\mathfrak{X}_{\text{pro}}(\mathscr{M})}{C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})}\cong\frac{\mathfrak{X}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})}{C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\otimes\mathfrak{igl}(1)}\cong\frac{\mathfrak{X}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{M})}{C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})}\cong\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})

(68)

are Lie algebras, all three isomorphic to the algebra
\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
of vector fields on the base. It is tempting to deduce from this the semi-direct sum expressions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{\text{pro}}(\mathscr{M})

\displaystyle\cong\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathscr{M})\,,

(69)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})

\displaystyle\cong\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus\big(C^{\infty}_{0}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\otimes\mathfrak{igl}(1)\big)\,,

(70)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{M})

\displaystyle\cong\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus C^{\infty}_{0}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\,,

(71)

where the subscript in
C^{\infty}_{0}
indicates that
\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
acts on functions via the Lie derivative,
i.e.
according to eqs. (
33
)–(
36
) with
\texttt{w}=0
. Indeed, the decompositions (
69
)–(
71
) are correct but
not
canonical, as they require a choice of flat connection
A
. To see this, consider projectable vector fields for definiteness: in order to write any such vector field
X
as a pair
(\bar{X},f)\in\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
, one needs both the projection
\bar{X}=\pi_{*}(X)

and
a horizontal lift (
66
) that defines the function
f
by
f\,\xi:=X-\ell(\pi_{*}(X))
. Conversely, the same data is needed in order to associate with any pair
(\bar{X},f)\in\mathfrak{X}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
a vector field on
\mathscr{M}
via
X=\ell(\bar{X})+f\xi
. This lift only exists once a clock has been chosen, and it is a Lie algebra morphism only if the clock is synchronisable. This is why we could not yet write the isomorphisms (
69
)–(
71
) in section
3.1
.

The expressions (
69
)–(
71
) also provide information on the groups of automorphisms generated by projectable, super-projectable and invariant vector fields. These groups are semi-direct products, and they respectively read

\text{Diff}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\ltimes C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathscr{M}),\qquad\text{Diff}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\ltimes\big\{\text{Maps }\bar{\mathscr{M}}\to\text{IGL(1)}\big\},\qquad\text{Diff}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\ltimes C_{0}^{\infty}(\bar{\mathscr{M}}).

(72)

The corresponding transformations in coordinates
(u,x^{a})
were written around (
65
). These semi-direct products manifestly resemble the BMS group, but we are not quite there yet. First, BMS vector fields in (
2
)–(
4
) are super-projectable but not invariant, so they generate automorphisms of null infinity seen as a fibre bundle, but not as a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle. In this sense, the first two groups in (
72
) are too large, while the third is overly restrictive. Second, the Abelian normal subgroups in (
72
) consist of functions on
\mathscr{M}
or
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
(as in the “warped Virasoro group” where
\bar{\mathscr{M}}=S^{1}

[
98
,
99
]
), while the Abelian subgroup needed for BMS consists of densities with a suitable weight: recall the bracket (
6
) or the weight
-1/d
appearing in the BMS action in (
45
). Defining BMS thus requires an extra, conformal, structure, to which we now turn.

3.3
BMS as conformal Carroll

Having reviewed the basics of principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundles, we now consider genuine Carrollian physics by endowing these bundles with degenerate metrics. This will lead to Carrollian isometries whose natural generalisation
[
46
,
47
]
will allow us to introduce the Carrollian cousins of conformal groups, and in particular BMS algebras. The groups obtained in this way generally have no infinite-dimensional non-Abelian factor, save for exceptional circumstances (one- or two-dimensional celestial spheres) where conformal generators admit an infinite-dimensional enhancement. The generalisation to non-conformal diffeomorphisms of the base and “generalised” BMS
[
15
,
16
]
will be treated in section
3.4
.

Carrollian spacetimes.

An (invariant)
Carrollian metric
is a symmetric, covariant, rank-two tensor field
\gamma_{\mu\nu}
on
\mathscr{M}
which is basic (
{\cal L}_{\xi}\gamma=0
and
\gamma(\xi,\cdot)=0
), positive semi-definite (
\gamma(X,X)\geqslant 0
) and whose radical is spanned by
\xi
(
i.e.

\gamma(X,\cdot)=0
iff
X
is vertical). Since it is basic, it can be written as the pullback
\gamma=\pi^{*}g
of a Riemannian (non-degenerate, positive-definite) metric
g
on the base
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
. Thus, in adapted coordinates
(u,x^{a})
with
\xi=\partial_{u}
, a Carrollian metric reads

\gamma=g_{ab}(x)\,\text{d}x^{a}\,\text{d}x^{b}

(73)

where
g_{ab}
can be any Riemannian metric on the base. A triplet
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
consisting of a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle
\mathscr{M}
with fundamental vector field
\xi
and a Carrollian metric
\gamma
will be called a
Carrollian spacetime
. A typical example of Carrollian metric is again provided by null infinity, whose metric can be read off from the limit
r\to\infty
of the Minkowski metric (
1
) restricted to a surface with constant
r
. The result is nothing but eq. (
73
),
i.e.
the pullback by the projection (
60
) of the metric on a celestial sphere, up to a divergent normalisation.

Note that the invariance condition on the Carrollian metric may be relaxed in full generality
[
47
]
, which is crucial in certain cases. For instance, the induced metric on a light-cone in Minkowski spacetime is not invariant, although it obeys all the other axioms above. Moreover, in Penrose’s approach the metric structure at null infinity is only defined up to a conformal factor, so a more general definition may seem in order (see
e.g.

[
49
, sec. 2]
). In practice the restriction to
invariant
metrics at null infinity adopted here entails no loss of generality (see
e.g.

[
51
, sec. II.B.2]
or
[
100
, sec. II]
), so we stick to it.

A final comment: in contrast to Riemannian geometry, there is no unique torsionless affine connection compatible with a given Carrollian structure
(\xi,\gamma)
—the reason essentially lies in the fact that the metric is not invertible. This is yet another instance where the usefulness of Ehresmann connections becomes manifest in Carrollian geometry: a unique notion of parallel transport on a Carrollian spacetime
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
becomes privileged only once it is further endowed with a clock
A
,
cf.
the “special” connection in
[
97
, app. A]
. This is not crucial for our purposes, so we will not dwell on that point.

Carrollian isometries.

Given a Carrollian spacetime
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
, its
Carrollian isometries
are principal bundle automorphisms that preserve the Carrollian metric. Any such automorphism projects on an isometry of the base
(\bar{\mathscr{M}},g)
. Thus, a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})
generates Carrollian isometries iff it is invariant and satisfies in addition the Killing equation on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
, that is,

{\cal L}_{X}\xi=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\cal L}_{\bar{X}}g=0\,.

(74)

In adapted coordinates
(u,x^{a})
, any such vector field reads
X=X^{a}(x)\,\partial_{a}+\alpha(x)\,\partial_{u}
, where
\bar{X}=X^{a}(x)\,\partial_{a}
and
\alpha(x)
are, respectively, a Killing vector field and an arbitrary function on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
(recall eq. (
63
)). The algebra of Carrollian isometry generators thus has a structure of semi-direct sum

\mathfrak{isom}(\mathscr{M})\cong\mathfrak{isom}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus C^{\infty}_{\texttt{w}}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})

(75)

provided a synchronised clock has been chosen to define a horizontal lift (
66
). Note that the weight
w
of fibrewise translations under diffeomorphisms of
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
is arbitrary in (
75
), since the Killing equation in (
74
) implies
\nabla_{a}X^{a}=0
so that the coefficient of
w
in the transformation laws (
33
)–(
36
) vanishes. The simplest choice is
\texttt{w}=0
, but we will soon see that conformal invariance yields instead
\texttt{w}=-1/d
, as in the BMS bracket (
6
).

The Carrollian isometry group with Lie algebra (
75
) has a structure of semi-direct product
\text{Isom}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\ltimes C^{\infty}_{\texttt{w}}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
. The BMS group to be introduced now will extend it, in the same way that conformal maps extend standard isometries.

Carrollian conformal structure.

An equivalence class
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\gamma])
of Carrollian spacetimes, with respect to the equivalence relation

(\xi,\gamma)\sim(\Omega^{-1}\,\xi\,,\Omega^{2}\,\gamma)

(76)

where
\Omega=\pi^{*}\bar{\Omega}
is any positive invariant function, is called a
Carrollian conformal structure
.
16
16

16

It is also called a
universal structure
in Penrose’s approach to asymptotically flat spacetimes, since it is the natural kinematical structure at null infinity; see
e.g.

[
51
, sec. II.B.2]
or
[
100
, sec. II]
.
Its projection on the base defines a conformal structure
(\bar{\mathscr{M}},[g])
for the class of Riemannian metrics
g^{\prime}\sim\overline{\Omega}^{2}\,g
. The key point of (
76
) is to link the conformal weight of
\gamma
to that of
\xi
in such a way that
\gamma\otimes\xi\otimes\xi
be “Weyl-invariant”. This will eventually fix the weight
\texttt{w}=-1/d
of BMS supertranslations under celestial diffeomorphisms.

Note that one could also define a “Lifschitz” scaling where fundamental vector fields are identified as
\xi\sim\Omega^{-z}\,\xi
for some exponent
z\in\mathbb{R}
instead of (
76
)
[
47
]
. This leads to a different weight of “supertranslations” under diffeomorphisms of the base space and no longer corresponds to the structure normally encountered in asymptotically flat gravity, but it is conceivable that it would find applications elsewhere, similarly to Lifschitz symmetries in generalisations of AdS/CFT
[
101
,
102
]
. We will not study this situation in depth, but we shall from time to time mention the generalisation of conformal (BMS) results to the Lifschitz case since our geometrical approach makes the extension straightforward.

A bundle automorphism of
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
that preserves a given Carrollian conformal structure, so that
\xi^{\prime}=\Omega^{-1}\,\xi
(rescaling of Carroll time) and
\gamma^{\prime}=\Omega^{2}\,\gamma
(local rescaling of the metric) is a
Carrollian conformal map
.
17
17

17

To be precise, if
F:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
is the automorphism, we write the transformed fundamental vector field and metric as
\xi^{\prime}:=F_{*}\xi
and
\gamma^{\prime}:=(F^{-1})^{*}\gamma
.
Its projection on the base is a conformal transformation of
(\bar{\mathscr{M}},g)
, with
g^{\prime}=\overline{\Omega}^{2}\,g
. (One could similarly define a Carrollian Lifschitz map by replacing the first condition with
\xi^{\prime}=\Omega^{-z}\,\xi
.)

Carrollian conformal vector fields.

Having chosen a Carrollian conformal structure
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\gamma])
, pick a representative in the equivalence class (
76
),
i.e.
a Carrollian spacetime
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
. Then any projectable vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})
such that

{\cal L}_{X}\xi=-f\,\xi\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\cal L}_{X}\gamma=2f\,\gamma\,,

(77)

for some function
f
on
\mathscr{M}
, is a
Carrollian conformal vector field
that generates Carrollian conformal maps of
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
. One can check that
X
is also a Carrollian conformal vector field for any representative
(\xi^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
in the equivalence class (
76
),
i.e.

{\cal L}_{X}\xi^{\prime}=-f^{\prime}\,\xi^{\prime}
and
{\cal L}_{X}\gamma^{\prime}=2f^{\prime}\,\gamma^{\prime}
with
f^{\prime}=f+{\cal L}_{X}(\ln\Omega)
. Note that the conditions (
77
) imply
{\cal L}_{\xi}f=0
since
\gamma
is basic by definition, so
f=\pi^{*}\bar{f}
is invariant and
X
is automatically super-projectable. The projection
\bar{X}=\pi_{*}(X)
is a conformal Killing vector field of
(\bar{\mathscr{M}},g)
, since the condition
{\cal L}_{X}\gamma=2f\,\gamma
projects to the conformal Killing equation
{\cal L}_{\bar{X}}g=2\bar{f}g
where
\bar{f}=\tfrac{1}{d}\nabla\cdot\bar{X}

{\cal L}_{X}\xi=-f\,\xi\,,\qquad{\cal L}_{\bar{X}}g=2\bar{f}\,g\,,

Carrollian conformal Killing equations
of
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\gamma])

The structure of Carrollian conformal vector fields is most easily revealed in adapted coordinates

\alpha(x)

X=X^{a}(x)\partial_{a}+\big(\alpha(x)+\tfrac{u}{d}\nabla_{a}X^{a}(x)\big)\partial_{u}

(79)

for some conformal Killing vector
\bar{X}=X^{a}(x)\partial_{a}
of the base manifold. The coincidence with the components of BMS vector fields (

\mathfrak{conf}(\mathscr{M})\cong\mathfrak{conf}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\inplus C^{\infty}_{\texttt{w}}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\qquad\text{with}\qquad\texttt{w}=-1/d,

(80)

where the subscript
w

are densities with weight
\texttt{w}=-1/d
under diffeomorphisms of
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
, in the sense of eq. (

Note for completeness that the “Lifschitz” generalisation of the above definitions is immediate: since the identification is now declared to be
\xi\sim\Omega^{-z}\xi

Example: BMS algebras.

The definition of Carrollian conformal structures is motivated by the example of null infinity

at the boundary of asymptotically flat spacetimes, whose Carrollian conformal maps are really BMS transformations

48

\mathfrak{bms}_{d+2}\cong\mathfrak{conf}(S^{d})\inplus C^{\infty}_{\texttt{w}}(S^{d})\qquad\text{with}\qquad\texttt{w}=-1/d.

(81)

Fibrewise translations

spanning the Abelian ideal

supertranslations
, where the function

\mathfrak{conf}(S^{d})\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathfrak{so}(d+1,1)&\text{for}\quad d\geqslant 3,\\
\mathfrak{X}(S^{1})\oplus\mathfrak{X}(S^{1})&\text{for}\quad d=2,\\
\mathfrak{X}(S^{1})&\text{for}\quad d=1.\end{array}\right.

(82)

The terminology is justified by the fact that supertranslations (respectively superrotations) extend finite-dimensional bulk spacetime translations (respectively rotations and boosts). A minor subtlety is that the embedding

BMS algebras as defined in (

3.4
Carrollian volumes and generalised BMS

16

92

Carrollian measured spaces.

Let
(\mathscr{M},\xi)
be a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle and assume
\mathscr{M}
is orientable. Then an
invariant volume form
on
(\mathscr{M},\xi)
is a nowhere-vanishing top form
\varepsilon\in\Omega^{d+1}(\mathscr{M})
such that
{\cal L}_{\xi}\varepsilon=0

(u,x^{a})
, any invariant volume form reads
\varepsilon=\pm\varepsilon(x)\,\text{d}u\wedge\text{d}^{d}x

\varepsilon(x)
is strictly positive on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
. A triplet
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
made of a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle with orientable total space
\mathscr{M}
and fundamental vector field
\xi

\varepsilon

Carrollian measured space
since the volume form is really a measure (recall section
2.2

It is intuitively clear that a Carrollian measured space
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
induces a volume form on the base manifold
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
. This is not as trivial as for Carrollian metrics: the contraction
{\cal V}:=i_{\xi}\varepsilon
does not vanish, so
\varepsilon
is not basic and cannot be written as the pullback of a form on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
. Note, however, that
{\cal V}\in\Omega^{d}(\mathscr{M})
is closed (
\text{d}{\cal V}=0
) thanks to the Cartan formula along with the invariance condition
{\cal L}_{\xi}\varepsilon=0
; also note that it is horizontal (
i_{\xi}{\cal V}=0
) since
i_{\xi}
is nilpotent. It follows that
{\cal V}
is basic and uniquely determined by a volume form
\bar{\varepsilon}\in\Omega^{d}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
on the base manifold:

{\cal V}\,:=\,\pi^{*}\bar{\varepsilon}\,\in\,\Omega^{d}(\mathscr{M})\,.

(83)

In adapted coordinates where
\varepsilon=\pm\varepsilon(x)\,\text{d}u\wedge\text{d}^{d}x
, one simply has
{\cal V}=\pm\varepsilon(x)\,\text{d}^{d}x
. Combined with the notion of clocks introduced in section
3.2
, this elementary observation will now allow us to define volume forms on Carrollian manifolds starting from a volume form on the base manifold.

Inducing volume forms from the base.

Let
\bar{\varepsilon}
be a volume form on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
and define
{\cal V}
by (
83

\mathscr{M}
is endowed with an Ehresmann connection

\varepsilon:=A\wedge{\cal V}
on
\mathscr{M}

{\cal L}_{\xi}\varepsilon={\cal L}_{\xi}A\wedge{\cal V}+A\wedge{\cal L}_{\xi}{\cal V}=0

A

are both invariant). The volume form thus obtained is independent of the choice of connection, since any two Ehresmann connections differ by a horizontal form, and any horizontal
(d+1)

(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
from the (equivalent) data
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\bar{\varepsilon})
made of a principal
\mathbb{R}
-bundle
(\mathscr{M},\xi)
and a volume form
\bar{\varepsilon}
on the orientable base space
\bar{\mathscr{M}}

For example, consider a Carrollian spacetime
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
that projects down to a Riemannian space
(\bar{\mathscr{M}},g)

\bar{\mathscr{M}}
determines its canonical volume form
\bar{\varepsilon}:=*1\in\Omega^{d}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})

\varepsilon=A\wedge{\cal V}

Carrollian volume form

induced by

\text{d}u\wedge\text{d}^{d}x\,\sqrt{g(x)}
.

It is useful, for future reference, to know how bundle automorphisms affect invariant volume forms. Namely, let
F:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
be an automorphism, so that it admits a projection
\bar{F}:\bar{\mathscr{M}}\to\bar{\mathscr{M}}
satisfying (

F^{*}{\cal V}=\pi^{*}(\rho_{\bar{F}})\,{\cal V}

(84)

in terms of the Radon-Nikodym derivative (

\bar{F}

2.2
.

Campiglia-Laddha structures.

We are finally ready to define the equivalence class of Carrollian measured spaces that generalises the conformal class (
76
) and will eventually lead to generalised BMS transformations. Thus, we call
Campiglia-Laddha structure
an equivalence class
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\varepsilon])
of Carrollian measured spaces, with respect to the relation

(\xi,\varepsilon)\sim(\Omega^{-1}\,\xi\,,\Omega^{d+1}\,\varepsilon)

(85)

where
\Omega=\pi^{*}\bar{\Omega}
is any positive invariant function. These conditions can equivalently be expressed in terms of the volume form
\bar{\varepsilon}
on the base, namely

(\xi,\bar{\varepsilon})\sim(\Omega^{-1}\,\xi\,,\bar{\Omega}^{d}\bar{\varepsilon})\,.

(86)

To the best of our knowledge, the first discussion of such equivalence classes in their own right, characterising generalised BMS transformations, appeared in
[
15
, sec. 4.1]
for
d=2

In particular, any Carrollian conformal structure
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\gamma])
yields a canonical Campiglia-Laddha structure
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\varepsilon])
by virtue of the above construction of the Carrollian volume form, since
A\sim\Omega A
(due to the condition
i_{\xi}A=1
) and
{\cal V}\sim\Omega^{d}{\cal V}
(due to
\bar{\varepsilon}\sim\bar{\Omega}^{d}\bar{\varepsilon}
).

In contrast to conformal structures, the extension of the equivalence (
85

\xi\sim\Omega^{-z}\,\xi
also requires a modification of the relation imposed on Carrollian volumes. This is because the normalisation condition
i_{\xi}A=1
for Carrollian clocks requires
A\sim\Omega^{z}A
in the Lifschitz case, which in turn implies that the volume form must now be identified as
\varepsilon\sim\Omega^{d+z}\,\varepsilon
instead of the second condition in (
85

76

3.3
for the conformal case, so we will not dwell on this extension of the formalism.

Similarly to section
3.3

\mathscr{M}
that preserves a given Campiglia-Laddha structure
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\varepsilon])

\xi^{\prime}=\Omega^{-1}\,\xi

generalised conformal map
.

19

19

With the notation of footnote

\varepsilon^{\prime}:=(F^{-1})^{*}\varepsilon

Generalised conformal vector fields.

Again mimicking section

Example: Generalised BMS.

It is immediate to apply the definition of generalised conformal maps and vector fields to null infinity

Carrollian Hermitian form.

Consider a Carrollian measured space
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
and define the basic form

\bar{\varepsilon}

\bar{\mathscr{M}}

\mathscr{M}

\phi^{*}\text{d}\psi\wedge{\cal V}=(\phi^{*}\,{\cal L}_{\xi}\psi)\,\varepsilon\,,

{\cal L}_{\xi}\psi=i_{\xi}(\text{d}\psi)

21

21

Indeed,
{\cal L}_{\xi}\psi\,\varepsilon=i_{\xi}(\text{d}\psi)\wedge\varepsilon=-\text{d}\psi\wedge i_{\xi}\varepsilon=-\text{d}\psi\wedge{\cal V}

Note in passing that the Hermitian form (

Carrollian densities.

Having defined Carrollian measured spaces, it is straightforward to define notions of densities analogous to those of section
2.2
. Let therefore
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
be a Carrollian measured space with base volume form
\bar{\varepsilon}
and let
F:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
be a bundle automorphism so that the identity (

with weight

\mathscr{M}
that transforms under any bundle automorphism

according to
\phi\to\phi^{\prime}:=F\cdot\phi
with

F\cdot\phi:=(\pi^{*}\rho_{\bar{F}^{-1}})^{\texttt{w}}\,\,(F^{-1})^{*}\phi\,,

(96)

where
\rho_{\bar{F}^{-1}}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative (

) of the base volume form
\bar{\varepsilon}
under
\bar{F}^{-1}
. This applies in particular to any generalised conformal map, in which case
\varepsilon^{\prime}=(F^{-1})^{*}\varepsilon=\Omega^{d+1}\,\varepsilon

\xi^{\prime}=F_{*}\xi=\Omega^{-1}\xi

in CFT. This is manifest in adapted coordinates

, where any bundle automorphism takes the form (
65

\phi^{\prime}\big(u^{\prime}(u,x),x^{\prime}(x)\big)=\rho_{\bar{F}}(x)^{-\texttt{w}}\,\phi(u,x)\,,

which is nothing but the generalisation of the BMS transformation law (

) to any bundle automorphism, with
\texttt{w}=\Delta/d
. Indeed, eqs. (
46
) and (

The infinitesimal transformation corresponding to eq. (

\delta\phi={\cal L}_{X}\phi+\texttt{w}\,\text{div}(\bar{X})\,\phi

where the divergence is defined by (

Manifest generalised BMS symmetry of the Sachs form.

We showed in section

4
Higher-spin BMS algebras

Having established our playground, we now turn to the construction of higher-spin extensions of (generalised) BMS algebras. Our strategy will be the same in all cases and follows a standard higher-spin pattern now applied to Carrollian structures:

1.

Start by considering suitable Lie algebras of vector fields on a Carrollian manifold—typically the Carrollian conformal Killing vector fields (standard BMS) of section
3.3

3.4

2.

Build the corresponding, much larger, universal enveloping algebras (“
\mathscr{U}

3.

In each case,

admits an ideal (an annihilator “

all powers of the conformal Laplacian in the case of the WRac). Mod out this annihilator to obtain higher-spin algebras of the general form
\mathscr{U}/\mathscr{U}_{0}

51

As announced in the

2
: the WRac and the Sachs module, respectively studied in sections
4.1

[

[

from a genuine Minkowskian limit of the Rac in AdS space. This suggests that more work is required in order to understand the relation between scalar kinematics at infinity, Carrollian geometry, and flat limits—an issue that we will not address here.

Note that we will rely heavily on the conventions and Carrollian language of section
3

e.g.

Homogeneous differential operators of orders zero and one in the partial derivatives are, respectively, functions (acting by local multiplication) and vector fields (acting by local derivation), while operators of order

4.1
Higher symmetries of the WRac

Recall from section
2
that a WRac is an “overleading” solution of the d’Alembert equation (
7

40

\Phi(r,u,x)\sim r^{1-d/2}\phi(x)
and satisfies the Yamabe equation (
41

\phi(x)
is generally singular; it is nevertheless natural to study it from a higher-spin perspective. Accordingly, we now build the higher-spin algebra obtained by (i) extending the conformal BMS vector fields of section
3.3
to higher-order differential operators, (ii) quotienting them by all time derivatives (owing to
\dot{\phi}=0
in eqs. (
14

41

This oversimplification ultimately rules out the WRac as a well-behaved analogue of the usual singleton, and justifies the later study of radiative scalars (section
4.2
).

The plan is as follows. We start by rephrasing the Carrollian conformal Killing equations (

41

[
67
,

111

\dot{\phi}=0
leads to the definition of vertical operators and their modding out of the higher-spin symmetry algebra; the latter eventually reduces (after a further quotient by trivial Laplacian symmetries) to the Eastwood-Vasiliev algebra
[

that would be relevant in bosonic higher-spin theories on de Sitter space
[

for which the present Minkowski spacetime would play the role of ambient space, dS

Proposition: Carrollian conformal vector fields as operators.

Let
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\gamma)
be a Carrollian spacetime as defined at the end of section

X
that satisfies eqs. (

[\xi,D\,]=f\,\xi\,,\qquad[\Delta,\bar{D}\,]=2\bar{f}\,\Delta\,,

(99)

\xi

(

the Yamabe operator in eq. (
41

is unique up to an additive constant in the zeroth-order part of

78

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})
be a vector field. Any one of its completions to a first-order differential operator reads
D=X+h
for some function
h\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
. Our goal is to show that

can be chosen such that the commutators (
99

X
satisfies eqs. (
77
)–(
78

[\xi,X+h]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{s20}}}}{{=}}f\xi+{\cal L}_{\xi}h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}f\xi
. This holds iff
{\cal L}_{\xi}h=0
,
i.e.
whenever
h=\pi^{*}\bar{h}
is an invariant function. Then
\bar{D}=\bar{X}+\bar{h}
is well-defined and the second condition in (
99

\tfrac{2}{d}\nabla_{a}\bar{X}^{a}\,\nabla^{2}+(2\nabla^{a}\bar{h}+\nabla^{2}\bar{X}^{a})\partial_{a}+(\nabla^{2}\bar{h}+\tfrac{d-2}{4(d-1)}\bar{X}^{a}\partial_{a}{\cal R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}2\bar{f}(\nabla^{2}-\tfrac{d-2}{4(d-1)}{\cal R})\,.

(100)

Here the leading symbols of both sides automatically coincide for
\bar{f}=\nabla_{a}\bar{X}^{a}/d
, which is the standard factor on the right-hand side of the conformal Killing equation in (
78
). It only remains to solve the first- and zeroth-order terms of (

2\nabla^{a}\bar{h}+\nabla^{2}\bar{X}^{a}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tfrac{4(d-1)}{d-2}\nabla^{2}\bar{h}+\bar{X}^{a}\partial_{a}{\cal R}+\tfrac{2}{d}\nabla_{a}\bar{X}^{a}{\cal R}=0\,.

(101)

The first of these conditions sets
\bar{h}=(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{d})\nabla_{a}\bar{X}^{a}+\text{cst}

\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{d}=\frac{d-2}{2d}

cf.

2.3

is unique up to an additive constant.
∎

Note that the explicit computations (

We have seen around eq. (

Vertical symmetries.

It is now time to turn to the ideal that will eventually be modded out of the WRac higher-spin symmetry algebra. This ideal will consist of two pieces: vertical symmetries and “trivial” Laplacian symmetries. We begin with the former. Accordingly, a differential operator

The quotient of the algebra

Proposition: Super-projectable operators.

The associative algebra
{\cal D}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})
of super-projectable differential operators is a tensor product

of order

D=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{k}D_{m}\circ\eta^{m}\,,\qquad\text{where}\qquad D_{m}\in{\cal D}^{k-m}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{M})\,.

that (

).

Proof.

The structure of the algebra (

) is an immediate consequence of the expression (
70
) for the algebra of super-projectable vector fields. Thus it only remains to prove the decomposition (
112
); we do this by induction and in adapted coordinates where
\xi=\partial_{u}
and
\eta=u\,\partial_{u}
, since computations involving differential operators are always local. Accordingly, suppose

is an invariant function, which trivially satisfies (

with

). The inductive definition thus becomes a first-order differential equation in

where the

We are now ready to investigate the algebra of super-projectable higher symmetries of the Sachs form. This will involve a notion of Hermiticity, eventually producing a Lie subalgebra of (

Higher symmetries of the Sachs module.

Consider a Campiglia-Laddha structure
(\mathscr{M},[\xi,\varepsilon])
. Pick a representative
(\xi,\varepsilon)
and introduce the non-degenerate Hermitian form

(\phi|\psi)\,:=\,\int_{\mathscr{M}}\phi^{*}\,\psi\,\varepsilon\qquad\forall\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M},\mathbb{C})\,,

(114)

which is nothing but the standard inner product of complex-valued “wavefunctions” on
\mathscr{M}
with volume form
\varepsilon
. The Carrollian inner product (
94
) can then be written as

\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\,=\,i\,(\phi|\xi|\psi)\,.

(115)

Hermitian conjugation with respect to (
114
) will be denoted by a dagger,
i.e.

(D^{\dagger}\phi,\psi):=(\phi,D\psi)
for any differential operator
D\in{\cal D}(\mathscr{M})
. Thus, the Hermitian conjugate of a function is just its complex conjugate, while that of a vector field
X
is

X^{\dagger}=-X^{*}-\text{div}(X^{*})\,,

(116)

with the divergence defined by (
34
) in terms of the invariant volume form. In the particular case of a Carrollian spacetime, this divergence simply reads
\text{div}(X)=\partial_{u}X^{u}+\nabla_{a}X^{a}
in adapted coordinates
(u,x^{a})
.

We now define a
higher symmetry of the Carrollian Hermitian form
(
94
)–(
115
) (or a
Hermitian symmetry
for short) as being a differential operator
D\in{\cal D}(\mathscr{M})
such that the infinitesimal transformations
\delta\phi=iD\phi
and
\delta\psi=iD\psi
preserve
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle
for all
\phi,\psi
. The relation (
115
) shows that this requirement is equivalent to the
Hermiticity condition

\xi\circ D=D^{\dagger}\circ\xi\,.

(117)

For instance, a zeroth order operator is Hermitian in the above sense if it is a real invariant function. And a first-order operator
\hat{X}=X+h
is Hermitian if
X
is a purely imaginary projectable vector field while
\text{Im}(h)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{div}\,X
and
\text{Re}(h)
is an invariant function. Thus we recover, as expected, the statement of the end of section
3.4
that
all
bundle automorphisms preserve the Carrollian Hermitian form provided wavefunctions are Carrollian densities with weight
\texttt{w}=1/2
. This includes all super-projectable vector fields, which in turn includes all generalised conformal vector fields and of course all Carrollian conformal vector fields. Also note that, given a Carrollian clock, there exists a large collection of Euler vector fields
\eta
such that
\xi\circ\eta=-\eta^{\dagger}\circ\xi
: in adapted coordinates, they take the form
\eta=u\,\partial_{u}+X^{a}(x)\partial_{a}
with
\bar{X}=X^{a}(x)\partial_{a}
divergenceless with respect to
\bar{\varepsilon}
. In the case of null infinity, the higher symmetries of the Carrollian Hermitian form (
51
) will be called
higher symmetries of the Sachs module
; they include, in particular, higher-order extensions of generalised BMS generators.

Hermitian higher symmetries span a real Lie algebra with Lie bracket
i\times
commutator. In contrast to section
4.1
, the algebra is

associative because the composition of two Hermitian operators is generally not Hermitian. Given a representative Carrollian measured space
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
endowed with a synchronised clock, this algebra is completely characterised as follows:

Proposition: All Hermitian symmetries.

The Lie algebra
{\cal H}_{\text{sym}}(\mathscr{M})
of all higher symmetries of the Carrollian Hermitian form (
94
)–(
115
) is isomorphic to the semi-direct sum

{\cal H}_{\text{sym}}(\mathscr{M})\cong{\cal H}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\,\inplus\,{\cal H}_{\text{vsym}}(\mathscr{M})\,

(118)

of the Lie algebra
{\cal H}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
of differential operators on the base that are Hermitian with respect to

(\bar{\phi}|\bar{\psi})\,:=\,\int_{\bar{\mathscr{M}}}\bar{\phi}^{*}\,\bar{\psi}\,\bar{\varepsilon}\qquad\forall\bar{\phi},\bar{\psi}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathscr{M}}),

(119)

and the Lie ideal
{\cal H}_{\text{vsym}}(\mathscr{M})
of all vertical higher symmetries. More precisely, any higher symmetry

decomposes as
D=E\circ\xi+\ell(\bar{H})
where
E
is antihermitian with respect to (
114
) while
\bar{H}
is hermitian with respect to (
119
), and
\ell
is the horizontal lift (
105
).

Proof.

To start, consider a vertical differential operator
D=E\circ\xi
. In order for it to satisfy (
117
), one needs
\xi\circ E\circ\xi=-\xi\circ E^{\dagger}\circ\xi
, hence
E^{\dagger}=-E
since
\xi
is nowhere vanishing. Now check that vertical symmetries span an ideal by computing
D_{3}:=i[D_{1},D_{2}]
for
D_{1}=A\circ\xi
a vertical symmetry and
D_{2}
any symmetry, which shows that
D_{3}=i(A\circ D_{2}^{\dagger}-D_{2}\circ A)\circ\xi
is also a vertical symmetry. Thus the quotient
{\cal H}_{\text{sym}}(\mathscr{M})/{\cal H}_{\text{vsym}}(\mathscr{M})
is a Lie algebra. Given a horizontal lift, one can consider representatives that are invariant horizontal differential operators. Moreover, the Hermitian conjugations with respect to (
114
) and (
119
) commute with the horizontal lift:
26
26

26

For simplicity, we slightly abuse notation by using the same symbol for both conjugations.

{}^{\dagger}\circ\ell=\ell\circ{}^{\dagger}
, as is obvious in coordinates. Thus the condition (
117
) for the invariant horizontal lift
D=\ell(\bar{D})
of a differential operator
\bar{D}
on the base is equivalent to the hermiticity condition
\bar{D}^{\dagger}=\bar{D}
on the base, and the isomorphism (

) follows.
∎

It is clear from this result that the algebra of all higher symmetries is huge, so some criterion is needed to restrict attention to those symmetries that are actually relevant. Here we adopt the point of view that the “interesting” generators are super-projectable (which includes generalised conformal transformations), for which the following holds:

Proposition: Super-projectable Hermitian symmetries.

Let
(\mathscr{M},\xi,\varepsilon)
be a Carrollian measured space endowed with a global section defining the canonical Euler vector field
\eta
. Then, the Lie algebra
{\cal H}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})
of super-projectable higher symmetries of the Carrollian Hermitian form (
94
)–(
115
)
is a tensor product

{\cal H}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})\cong{\cal H}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\otimes U_{+}\big(\mathfrak{igl}(1)\big)\,

(120)

where
{\cal H}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})
is the Lie algebra of differential operators on the base that are Hermitian with respect to (
119
), while
U_{+}\big(\mathfrak{igl}(1)\big)
is the real form of the universal enveloping algebra which is spanned by Weyl-ordered polynomials in
i\,\xi
and
i\,\eta
. Thus, as a vector space,
{\cal H}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{M})
is isomorphic to the tensor product
\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(\odot T\bar{\mathscr{M}})\otimes\mathbb{R}[i\xi,i\eta]
where the subscript in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}
stresses that one only considers real symmetric multivector fields.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be the horizontal lift (
105
). Using the decompositions (
106
)–(
112
), any super-projectable differential operator
D
can be written as

D=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{k}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k-m}\ell\big(\bar{D}_{m,n}\big)\circ\big[\,(i\eta)^{m}\circ(i\xi)^{n}+\cdots\big]\,,\qquad\bar{D}_{m,n}\in{\cal D}^{k-m-n}(\bar{\mathscr{M}})\,,

(121)

where the dots stand for terms that enforce Weyl-ordering of the polynomial in
i\eta
and
i\xi
. What is the Hermitian conjugate of (
121
)? To answer this, note first that
\eta
and
\xi
commute with any invariant horizontal differential operator by definition. Also recall that the Hermitian conjugations with respect to (
114
) and (

) commute with the horizontal lift:
{}^{\dagger}\circ\ell=\ell\circ{}^{\dagger}
. The Hermitian conjugate of (

D^{\dagger}=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{k}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k-m}\ell\big(\bar{D}^{\dagger}_{m,n}\big)\circ\Big[\,\big((i\eta)^{\dagger}\big)^{m}\circ\big((i\xi)^{\dagger}\big)^{n}+\cdots\big]\,,

(122)

where the dots still stand for Weyl-ordering terms. Now using the fact that
i\eta
and

are higher symmetries of (

), the Hermiticity condition (

) becomes

\sum\limits_{m=0}^{k}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k-m}\ell\big(\bar{D}^{\dagger}_{m,n}-\bar{D}_{m,n}\big)\circ\xi\circ\big[\,(i\eta)^{m}\circ(i\xi)^{n}+\cdots\big]=0\,.

(123)

This implies that
\ell\big(\bar{D}^{\dagger}_{m,n}-\bar{D}_{m,n}\big)=0
for all

, which requires
\bar{D}^{\dagger}_{m,n}=\bar{D}_{m,n}
since the horizontal lift

). ∎

This concludes the characterisation of Carrollian higher-spin symmetries in full generality. From now on, and until the end of this work, we focus on the application of these ideas to BMS symmetry at null infinity.

Proposition: Higher-spin extensions.

Any real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
in the hierarchy (
92
) admits a higher-spin extension
\mathfrak{hg}
, built as the real Lie subalgebra of higher symmetries of the Sachs module spanned by Weyl-ordered products of the generators of
\mathfrak{g}
. The hierarchy (
92
) then extends to the following inclusions:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\mathfrak{hiso}(d+1,1)&\subset&\mathfrak{hbms}_{d+2}&\subseteq&\mathfrak{hgbms}_{d+2}&\subset&{\cal D}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{I}_{d+1})\\
\cup&&\cup&&\cup&&\cup\\
\mathfrak{iso}(d+1,1)&\subset&\mathfrak{bms}_{d+2}&\subseteq&\mathfrak{gbms}_{d+2}&\subset&\mathfrak{X}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{I}_{d+1})\end{array}

(124)

Proof.

The proof is constructive and applies to any Lie algebra, so we start by spelling it out in full generality before applying it to the case of interest here. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of a real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and denote the corresponding universal enveloping algebras by
U(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
and
U(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})
(the former is a real form of the latter). Let also
\iota
be an involution (
i.e.
an involutive antilinear antiautomorphism) of
U(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})
, and call
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
the eigenspace of
\iota
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
, respectively, so that
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathfrak{g}_{+}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-}
as real vector spaces. Now pick a basis
\{T_{i}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
whose elements are eigenvectors of
\iota
with eigenvalue one, yielding
\mathfrak{g}_{+}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{T_{i}\}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-}=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\text{i}\,T_{i}\}
. Since
\mathfrak{g}_{+}
is a real Lie algebra with
i\times
commutator as Lie bracket, we shall assume that
\mathfrak{g}_{+}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
. It only remains to extend this to enveloping algebras: let
U_{\pm}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})\subset U(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})
denote the eigenspace of
\iota
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
(respectively), so that
U_{+}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})
endowed with the bracket
i\times
commutator is a real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{hg}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathfrak{hg}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a Lie subalgebra. The Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem then ensures that
U(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})=\text{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{T_{i_{1}}\cdots T_{i_{r}}\}
is spanned by Weyl-ordered products of generators with complex coefficients. Thus
U_{+}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}})=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{T_{i_{1}}\cdots T_{i_{r}}\}
, which proves that
\mathfrak{hg}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the real Lie algebra spanned by Weyl-ordered products of generators with real coefficients.

In the specific examples above, the involution
\iota
is Hermitian conjugation with respect to the Carrollian inner product (
115
):
\langle\phi,D\psi\rangle=\langle\,\iota(D)\phi,\psi\rangle
, and the proof applies in exactly the same way from that point on. A minor subtlety is that the higher-spin extensions considered in the proposition are
not
actually universal enveloping algebras, but quotients thereof (by the annihilator on the Sachs module). The proof applies nevertheless since, by definition, the higher-spin extensions in the proposition are spanned by Weyl-ordered products of generators.
∎

Generalised BMS higher-spin algebra.

Recall that any generalised conformal vector field
X
is super-projectable and admits an extension to a first-order symmetry of the Sachs module. An analogous property remains true for Weyl-ordered polynomials of such first-order symmetries, so
\mathfrak{gbms}_{d+2}
is a Lie subalgebra of the Lie algebra
{\cal H}_{\text{spro}}(\mathscr{I}_{d+1})
of all super-projectable symmetries of the Sachs module.

The
\mathfrak{gbms}_{d+2}
subalgebra can be characterised in detail by focussing on the underlying vector space. Indeed, as a vector space, the Lie algebra (
90
)-(
91
) of generalised conformal vector fields is isomorphic to the space (
71
) of invariant vector fields because the time-dependence of generalised conformal vector fields is entirely fixed by data on
\bar{\mathscr{M}}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{gbms}_{d+2}\cong\mathfrak{X}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{I}_{d+1})
as vector spaces. This remains true for Weyl-ordered products of generalised conformal vector fields: as vector spaces, the higher-spin extension of the generalised BMS algebra is isomorphic to the algebra (
104
) of invariant differential operators on null infinity, that is,
\mathfrak{hgbms}_{d+2}\cong{\cal D}_{\text{inv}}(\mathscr{I}_{d+1})
. One can also see this in local coordinates, where any element
D
of
\mathfrak{hgbms}_{d+2}
is uniquely determined by an invariant differential operator

D_{0}\,=\,\sum_{r,q\geqslant 0}K^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}_{(q)}(x)\nabla_{a_{1}}\cdots\nabla_{a_{r}}\partial^{q}_{u}\,,

(125)

in the sense that
D_{0}
admits a unique completion of the form
D=D_{0}+{{\cal O}}(u)
such that
D\in\mathfrak{hgbms}_{d+2}
. We shall return to this type of expression in section
4.3
, where
K
’s will be interpreted as residual gauge parameters in a higher-spin gravity theory. Note for later purposes that
K^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}_{(q)}(x)
has scaling dimension
-(r+q)
, as follows from elementary dimensional analysis.

Minkowski higher-spin algebras.

The real Lie algebra spanned by Hermitian differential operators on Minkowski spacetime that commute with the d’Alembertian operator was first discussed in
[
113
]
, and dubbed
off-shell Minkowski higher-spin algebra
in
[
114
]
because it is the “flat limit” (really an Inönü-Wigner contraction) of the off-shell AdS higher-spin algebra introduced in
[
68
]
. (See
e.g.
Proposition 2 and Section 3.2.3 in
[
114
]
for a proof.) It is spanned by all Weyl-ordered products of Killing vector fields spanning the Poincaré algebra (see
[
114
, Cor. 6]
). By construction, the d’Alembertian operator is a central element in this algebra, so all products of the d’Alembertian with elements of the off-shell Minkowski higher-spin algebra span an ideal. The quotient of the off-shell Minkowski higher-spin algebra by this ideal will be called the
partially-massless Minkowski higher-spin algebra
since it is a flat limit
[
41
, sec. 6.1.2]
of the partially-massless AdS higher-spin algebra introduced in
[
115
]
. It can be seen as the realisation of off-shell Minkowski higher-spin algebra on the space of solutions of the d’Alembert equation, since the ideal that is quotiented out corresponds to the operators annihilating the solutions of the d’Alembert equation. We can now identify this structure in the first column of (
124
):

Proposition: Higher-spin algebra on Minkowski.

The partially-massless Minkowski higher-spin algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1,1}
is isomorphic to the Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{hiso}(d+1,1)
of higher symmetries of the Sachs module on
\mathscr{I}_{d+1}
.

Proof.

The Poincaré algebra is a subalgebra
\mathfrak{iso}(d+1,1)\subset\mathfrak{bms}_{d+2}
of the Lie algebra of Carrollian conformal vector fields of null infinity
\mathscr{I}_{d+1}
. It consists of Carrollian conformal vector fields that can be extended to Killing vector fields in the interior of Minkowski spacetime. Accordingly, one can extend Weyl-ordered products of Carrollian conformal vector fields generating
\mathfrak{iso}(d+1,1)
to Weyl-ordered products of Killing vector fields in Minkowski spacetime. This defines an injective linear map sending higher symmetries of the Sachs module to differential operators on compactified Minkowski spacetime. (To prove injectivity, note that the kernel vanishes since the restriction of a vanishing differential operator on compactified Minkowski spacetime obviously vanishes on the conformal boundary.) Furthermore, the map is invertible since Weyl-ordered products of Killing vector fields on Minkowski spacetime acting on the space of solutions of the d’Alembert equation span the partially-massless Minkowski higher-spin algebra; in particular, their action on radiative solutions (
i.e.
on the Sachs module (
26
) seen as on-shell fields in Minkowski spacetime) induces their action on the corresponding boundary data (
i.e.
the Sachs module seen as fields at null infinity). It follows that Weyl-ordered products of Carrollian conformal vector fields on
\mathscr{I}_{d+1}
that span the Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{iso}(d+1,1)\subset\mathfrak{bms}_{d+2}
form a Lie algebra isomorphic to the partially-massless Minkowski higher-spin algebra.
∎

Asymptotic Killing tensors.

The starting point of

and depth

The issue, then, is to understand to what extent fall-off conditions force

In what follows, we restrict ourselves to the two extreme classes of fall-offs—the “strong” and the “much weaker” ones—and attempt to match their asymptotic Killing tensors with the two candidate algebras of asymptotic symmetries obtained at the end of section
4.2
. (The WRac is discarded from the get-go since it fails to capture supertranslations.) In short, the matching would be spot on if it weren’t for trace conditions.

Higher-spin algebras of asymptotic symmetries.

Our two candidate algebras of asymptotic symmetries are, respectively, the partially-massless Minkowski algebra and the generalised BMS higher-spin algebra. They are, respectively, higher-spin extensions of Poincaré and generalised BMS algebras. As vector spaces, they are spanned by some symmetric tensor fields
K^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}_{(q)}(x)
on null infinity, as in (
125
). The only discrepancy with the residual gauge parameters in (
126
) is that the latter are traceless, while they are traceful for the higher-spin generalised BMS algebra,
cf.
(
125
).

Trace conditions are a recurring problem of tentative higher-spin algebras in Minkowski spacetime (see
e.g.

[
114
,
41
]
), because they preclude the interpretation of traceful tensors as algebras of global symmetries of massless gauge fields. This suggests to look for “exotic” higher-spin gravity theories whose spectra contain extra propagating fields beyond the usual tower of Fronsdal massless fields of all spins. A tantalising option would be an “unconstrained” higher-spin theory with higher-derivative equations (see
e.g.

[
116
,
117
]
and references therein) since, naïvely, its global symmetries are traceful Killing tensors on Minkowski spacetime, suggesting that asymptotic symmetries are similarly traceful tensors at null infinity. However, determining rigorously the spectrum of such symmetries and the underlying degrees of freedom is a subtle issue for higher-derivative gauge theories, and it remains to be achieved in complete generality.

Another way out was suggested in
[
41
, sec. 6.2]
:
the collection of generators of the partially-massless Minkowski algebra precisely matches the spectrum of global symmetries for a tower of higher-spin gauge fields of all spin
s=1,2,3,\ldots
and all odd depths
t=1,3,5,\ldots
around Minkowski spacetime; see
e.g.

[
82
,
115
]
for the (A)dS counterparts of such a matching. To be precise, in Minkowski spacetime such gauge fields are only “partially-massless-like” (in the sense of
[
41
]
). Unfortunately, exotic theories on Minkowski spacetime including such partially-massless-like fields do not seem to be unitary; in fact, partially-massless fields are only unitary on dS, not on AdS
[
118
]
. This second option is thus problematic in its own way.

The important questions raised by trace conditions will be left for future investigations. Nevertheless, it is tempting to conjecture that an exotic higher-spin gravity around Minkowski space, whose spectrum is a tower of partially-massless-like fields of all spins and all odd depths, admits
as algebra of asymptotic symmetries the partially-massless Minkowski (respectively, generalised BMS) higher-spin algebra for suitable fall-off conditions that generalise to higher depths
the strong fall-offs in
[
37
]
(respectively, the weak ones in
[
40
]
) of the massless case. It would be fascinating to see such an asymptotic symmetry computation carried out explicitly.
