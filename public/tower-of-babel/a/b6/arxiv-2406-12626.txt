Abstract
. We show that irreducible strongly continuous representations of SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}) on Hilbert spaces are admissible, modulo the recently proposed solution of the invariant subspace problem on Hilbert spaces.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 22E46; Secondary: 22E45, 22E47

1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper,
G
stands for the noncompact semisimple Lie group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
K
for its compact subgroup
\mathrm{SO}(2)
.
We define a representation
\rho
of
G
on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
to be
irreducible
if
\mathcal{X}
contains no nontrivial
closed

\rho(G)
-invariant subspaces
(this is usually called topologically irreducible), and an irreducible representation
\rho
of
G
on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
is said to be
admissible
if
\rho|_{K}
, its restriction to
K
, decomposes as a topological direct sum of characters of
K
, in which no character appears more than once.
We are going to prove the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the invariant subspace problem for Hilbert spaces has a positive solution.
Then every irreducible representation of
G
on a Hilbert space is admissible.

We give the proof towards the end of this note.
Our analysis focusses on the Banach algebra
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
of functions
f
with the invariance and integrability properties

f(k_{\theta}xk_{\phi})=e^{-im(\theta+\phi)}f(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{G}\left\|x\right\|^{\alpha}\left|f(x)\right|\,dx<\infty

(the notation will be explained in Section 2 below, together with other background material).
In Section 3 we review the theory of
m
-spherical functions on
G
and the associated spherical transformation; our main contribution
is a theorem that ensures that functions with the
K
-equivariance property above and whose
m
-spherical transforms behave well belong to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
.
Section 4 uses spectral theory and the results of Sections 2 and 3 to show that there exists
f_{m}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
that generates all
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
; this key result is in the spirit of some work of R. Gangolli
[
14
]
. Finally in Section 5 we use the function
f_{m}
of Section 4 and the putative solution of the invariant subspace problem on Hilbert spaces to prove our main theorem.

In the rest of this introduction, we explain the significance of Theorem 1.1.
In the early period of the development of the representation theory of semisimple Lie groups, between 1945 and 1975, say, representations on Hilbert spaces and on Banach spaces were considered; see, for instance,
[
24
, Chap 4]
.
It was shown that irreducible
unitary
representations on Hilbert spaces are admissible, which implies in particular that they may be effectively studied and classified by passing to the Lie algebra of the group in question, but the situation with Banach representations was unclear, though it was known that there were some pathological examples of reducible Banach representations (see below for an example).
In 1988, W. Soergel
[
22
]
finally constructed an example of an irreducible representation of
G
on a Banach space that was not admissible.

Soergel’s construction used the resolution of the invariant subspace problem.
This problem, whether every nontrivial operator on a complex Banach space of dimension greater than
1
admits a nontrivial invariant subspace, was first resolved in the negative by P. Enflo
[
9
]
, and later shorter solutions were produced by C.J. Read
[
19
,
20
]
.
All the examples produced were operators on Banach spaces that are rather different from Hilbert spaces, so it was not clear whether Soergel’s construction could be applied to Hilbert space representations or not.
What was clear, however, was that if
T
is a bounded linear operator on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
with no nontrivial invariant subspaces, then nonadmissible irreducible representations can be constructed on the Banach space
L^{2}(G/P,\mathcal{X})
; here
P
is a minimal parabolic subgroup of
G
.
Hence while we have a relatively simple classification of all the irreducible unitary representations, for general Banach space representations, a similar classification is probably impossible.

In the last year, two solutions have been proposed to the invariant subspace problem on Hilbert spaces, by Enflo
[
10
]
and by C. W. Neville
[
18
]
, both affirming that every bounded operator on a Hilbert space of dimension greater than
1

does
admit a nontrivial invariant subspace.
We do not propose to confirm the correctness of these papers here.
Rather, the aim of this note is to take this proposed solution as a starting point and to then show that it implies that every irreducible representation of
G
on a Hilbert space
is
admissible.
At this point, it is not clear whether the invariant subspace problem has a positive or negative solution when the Banach space under consideration is “better than reflexive but worse than Hilbertian”, for instance if
\mathcal{X}
is an
L^{p}
space where
1<p<\infty
.
However, our arguments, coupled with those of Soergel
[
22
]
, imply that the admissibility of representations of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and the invariant subspace problem are intimately related.

Together with our previous paper
[
2
]
, our result also shows that a classification of irreducible uniformly bounded representations of
G
on Hilbert spaces is possible; every such representation is equivalent (via conjugation by a bounded operator with bounded inverse) to one of the irreducible uniformly bounded representations described by R. A. Kunze and E. M. Stein
[
16
]
, and the Kunze–Stein representations are minimal representatives of the equivalence classes.

2.
Definitions and preliminary results

The early work on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is due to V. Bargmann
[
3
]
.
L. Ehrenpreis and F. Mautner
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
used Bargmann’s work later to study analysis on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
in detail.
Most of what we write in this section would have been known to these authors.

The group
G

Recall that
G=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and
K=\mathrm{SO}(2)
.
The Iwasawa decomposition is that every
x
in
G
may be written uniquely in the form
n_{t}a_{r}k_{\theta}
, where
r,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\mod 2\pi
; here

\begin{gathered}k_{\theta}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=\exp\left(\theta\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\right)\\
a_{r}:=\begin{pmatrix}e^{r}&0\\
0&e^{-r}\end{pmatrix}=\exp\left(r\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right)\\
n_{t}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
t&1\end{pmatrix}=\exp\left(t\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\right)\ .\end{gathered}

According to the Cartan decomposition, every element of
G
may be written in the form
k_{\theta}a_{r}k_{\phi}
, where
r\geq 0
; if
x\in K
then
r=0
and there are many choices for
k_{\theta}
and
k_{\phi}
; otherwise, the decomposition is unique up to changes in
\theta
and
\phi
by adding (or subtracting)
\pi
to both or
2\pi
to either.
In these coordinates,

dx=2\sinh(2r)\,dr\,d\varphi\,d\psi.

Clearly

2\sinh(2r)\sim\begin{cases}4r&\text{as $r\to 0$}\\
e^{2r}&\text{as $r\to\infty$}.\end{cases}

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
is spanned by the elements
E
,
H
and
Q
, given by

E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix};

we define the Casimir operator
\Omega
by

4\Omega:=H^{2}+Q^{2}-E^{2}.

Representations of
G

Throughout, we use the word
representation
to mean a representation
\rho
of
G
on a topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
by continuous operators whose inverse is also continuous, which is strongly continuous, in the sense that
x_{n}\to x
in
G
implies that
\rho(x_{n})\xi\to\rho(x)\xi
in
\mathcal{V}
for all
\xi\in\mathcal{V}
.
If
\mathcal{V}
is a Hilbert space or a Banach space, we call
\rho
a Hilbert or a Banach representation.
For
x\in G
, we write
\|x\|
for the norm of
x
as a linear operator on the
2
-dimensional real Hilbert space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
It is evident that
\|x\|=\|x^{-1}\|
and
\|x_{1}x_{2}\|\leq\|x_{1}\|\|x_{2}\|
for all
x,x_{1},x_{2}
in
G
, and that if
x
has Cartan decomposition
ka_{r}k^{\prime}
, then
\|x\|=\|a_{r}\|=e^{r}
.
Norms in other spaces are indicated by a subscript, for example,
\left\|\rho(x)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}
indicates the norm of
\rho(x)
in the Banach spaces of bounded linear operators on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\rho
is a representation of
G
on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.
Then there exist constants
\alpha
in
[0,\infty)
and
C
in
[1,\infty)
such that

(2.1)

\|\rho(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq C\|x\|^{\alpha}\qquad\forall x\in G.

Proof.

Take
\xi
in
\mathcal{X}
.
Since
x\mapsto\|\rho(x)\xi\|_{\mathcal{X}}
is continuous on
G
, it is bounded on compact sets.
The Banach–Steinhaus theorem implies that
\|\rho(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}
is bounded on compact sets in
G
.

If
j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
e^{j-1}\leq\|x\|\leq e^{j}
, then we may write
x=x_{1}\dots x_{j}
, where
\|x_{i}\|\leq e
; indeed, if
x=ka_{r}k^{\prime}
, then we may take
x_{1}
to be
ka_{r/j}
,
x_{2}
, …,
x_{j-1}
to be
a_{r/j}
, and
x_{j}
to be
a_{r/j}k^{\prime}
.
Hence

\|\rho(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\|\rho(x_{1})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\dots\|\rho(x_{j})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\max\{\|\rho(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}:\|x\|\leq e\}^{j};

the result follows with
C
equal to
\max\{\|\rho(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}:\|x\|\leq e\}
and
\alpha
equal to
\log C
.
∎

A representation satisfying (
2.1
) is said to be of growth rate
\alpha
.

We now consider the restriction to
K
of a representation
\rho
of
G
on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define the closed subspace
\mathcal{X}_{m}
of
\mathcal{X}
by

\mathcal{X}_{m}:=\{\xi\in\mathcal{X}:\rho(k_{\theta})\xi=e^{im\theta}\xi\},

and
\mathcal{P}_{m}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{m}
by

\mathcal{P}_{m}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-im\theta}\rho(k_{\theta})\,d\theta.

Then
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is a bounded projection; indeed,

\left\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left\|\rho(k_{\theta})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\,d\theta\leq\max\{\left\|\rho(k_{\theta})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}:k_{\theta}\in K\}.

The algebra
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}

Let
L^{1}(G)_{m}
and
L^{2}(G)_{m}
be the subsets of
L^{1}(G)
and
L^{2}(G)
of functions
f
such that

(2.2)

f(k_{\theta}xk_{\phi})=e^{-im(\theta+\phi)}f(x)\qquad\forall k_{\theta},k_{\phi}\in K\quad\forall x\in G.

The spaces
L^{1}(G)_{m}
and
L^{2}(G)_{m}
are obviously closed subspaces of
L^{1}(G)
and
L^{2}(G)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The space
L^{1}(G)_{m}
, equipped with convolution and the
L^{1}(G)
-norm, is a commutative Banach algebra.

Proof.

See
[
17
, Lemma 3, p. 21]
or
[
23
, Proposition 1, p. 290]
.
∎

For
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
, let
\omega_{\alpha}
, or just
\omega
, be the function
x\mapsto\|x\|^{\alpha}
.
Then

\omega(x)\in[1,\infty),\qquad\omega(x)=\omega(x^{-1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\omega(x_{1}x_{2})\leq\omega(x_{1})\omega(x_{2})

for all
x,x_{1},x_{2}\in G
.
A continuous function satisfying these conditions is called a weight.
We write
L^{1}(G,\omega)
for the subset of
L^{1}(G)
of functions
f
such that

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}:=\int_{G}\left|f(x)\right|\omega(x)\,dx<\infty.

Then
L^{1}(G,\omega)
is a subspace of
L^{1}(G)
that is closed under convolution, and equipped with its own norm, is a Banach algebra (often called a Beurling algebra).
Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\|f*g\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{G}\left|f\right|*\left|g\right|(x)\omega(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{G}\left|f(y)\right|\left|g(y^{-1}x)\right|\omega(y)\omega(y^{-1}x)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}.

Evidently
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a subspace of
L^{1}(G,\omega)
, no matter what weight is considered.
We add the subscript
m
to both
L^{1}(G,\omega)
and
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
to indicate the subspaces of functions that satisfy (
2.2
).
These are subalgebras of the convolution algebra
L^{1}(G)_{m}
.

The continuous multiplicative linear functionals on the commutative algebras
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
were determined by Ehrenpreis and Mautner
[
7
]
, and are called
m
-spherical functions.
Every continuous multiplicative linear functional on
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
restricts to a continuous multiplicative linear functional on the dense subspace
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
, and hence is the continuous extension to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
of one of the known
m
-spherical functions.
The multiplicative linear functionals on the Banach algebras
L^{1}(G)_{m}
, with no weight, were first studied by Ehrenpreis and Mautner
[
8
, Lemma 7.2]
, and further analysed in
[
21
,
23
]
.
We will recall some properties of
m
-spherical functions and extend some of the results of these authors to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
in Section 3.

The algebras
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
do not have an identity, but the larger space
M(G,\omega)_{m}
of suitably bounded complex measures with the (measure theoretic version of the) invariance property (
2.2
) contains the measure
\mu_{m}
:

\mu_{m}(f)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(k_{\theta})e^{-im\theta}\,d\theta,

which has the properties that
\mathcal{P}_{m}=\rho(\mu_{m})
and

\mu_{m}*f=f*\mu_{m}=f\qquad\forall f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}.

If
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an approximate identity in the algebra
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
, then
(\mu_{m}*f_{n}*\mu_{m})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an approximate identity in the algebra
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
that consists of smooth compactly supported functions.
By considering approximate identities, we may show that when
g\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
, the norm of the operator
f\mapsto g*f
on
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
is equal to
\left\|g\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}
.

Irreducible representations

By Lemma
2.1
, a Banach representation
\rho
of
G
has a growth rate
\alpha
.
We take
\omega(x)
to be
\|x\|^{\alpha}
, and then
\rho
induces a representation, also denoted by
\rho
, of
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
on
\mathcal{X}_{m}
by integration.
Since
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
contains an approximate identity of smooth functions, the subspace
\mathcal{X}_{m}^{\infty}
of all smooth vectors in
\mathcal{X}_{m}
is dense in
\mathcal{X}_{m}
.
The space
\mathcal{X}_{m}^{\infty}
is also the subspace of
\mathcal{X}^{\infty}
, the space of smooth vectors in
\mathcal{X}
, of vectors satisfying (
2.2
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\rho
be an irreducible representation of
G
on the Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.
Then the restriction of
\rho
to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
is an irreducible representation of
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
on
\mathcal{X}_{m}
.

Proof.

To prove our claim, it suffices to take
\xi
in
\mathcal{X}_{m}\setminus\{0\}
and
\eta
in
\mathcal{X}_{m}
and show that there is a sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
such that
\rho(h_{n})\xi\to\eta
as
n\to\infty
.

Since
\rho
is an irreducible representation of
G
, its integrated version is an irreducible representation of
L^{1}(G,\omega)
and there exists a sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
L^{1}(G,\omega)
such that
\lim_{n}\rho(g_{n})\xi=\eta
in
\mathcal{X}
.
Then
h_{n}:=\mu_{m}*g_{n}*\mu_{m}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
, and

\eta=\mathcal{P}_{m}\eta=\lim_{n}\mathcal{P}_{m}\rho(g_{n})\xi=\lim_{n}\mathcal{P}_{m}\rho(g_{n})\mathcal{P}_{m}\xi=\lim_{n}\rho(\mu_{m}*g_{n}*\mu_{m})\xi,

so we are done.
∎

Admissible representations

The representation
\rho
of
G
gives rise to a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{X}_{m}^{\infty}
(the algebraic sum): for
X\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\xi\in\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{X}_{m}^{\infty}
,

\rho(X)\xi=\frac{d}{dt}\rho(\exp(tX))\xi|_{t=0};

we extend
\rho
to a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
by linearity.

We say that
\rho
is an admissible representation when each
\mathcal{X}_{m}
is finite dimensional.
This means that
\mathcal{X}_{m}
coincides with
\mathcal{X}_{m}^{\infty}
, and the algebraic direct sum
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{X}_{m}
is then a space on which both
\mathfrak{g}
and
K
act, with an obvious compatibility condition between the two actions.
When an additional finite generation condition is satisfied, which is automatic for an irreducible representation, such a space is known as a Harish-Chandra module.
Conversely, every Harish-Chandra module arises from a representation (though this representation need not be irreducible).
Indeed, the Casselman–Wallach theorem shows that there is always a continuous Fréchet space representation of
G
associated to any Harish-Chandra module, and P. Delorme and S. Souaifi
[
5
]
later showed that we may take the Fréchet space to be a Hilbert space; see
[
4
]
for more information.

Pathological representations

There are natural representations of
G
acting on
C(T)
and
L^{\infty}(T)
, the spaces of continuous and bounded measurable functions on the circle
T
.
It is easy to construct pathological representations of
G
, which are weakly but not strongly continuous, by considering the quotient representation on
L^{\infty}(T)/C(T)
.

The next lemma shows that irreducible Banach representations are not too pathological.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
\rho
is an irreducible representation of
G
on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.
Then
\mathcal{X}
is separable.

Proof.

Take
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
.
The closure of the
G
-invariant subspace
\{\rho(f)\xi:f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)\}
of
\mathcal{X}
is
G
-invariant and separable, and must coincide with
\mathcal{X}
if
\rho
is irreducible.
∎

3.
The generalised spherical transformation

Consider the subalgebra
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
of the commutative Banach algebra
L^{1}(G)_{m}
.
The multiplicative linear functionals on
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
are given by integration against generalised spherical functions. We denote by
\phi_{m,s}
, where
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the (generalised)
m
-spherical function such that

\phi_{m,s}(x):=\int_{K}f_{m,s}(k^{-1}xk)\,dk,

where
f_{m,s}(k_{\theta}a_{r}n_{t})=e^{-im\theta}\,e^{r(1-2s)}
.

It is easily checked that
|\phi_{m,s}|\leq\phi_{0,\left|\operatorname{Re}s\right|}
, and that

\Omega\phi_{m,s}=\left(s^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\phi_{m,s}.

The (generalised)
m
-spherical transform of
f
in
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
is defined to be the
function
\hat{f}
given by

\hat{f}(s):=\int_{G}f(x)\,\phi_{m,s}(x^{-1})\,dx\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{C}.

Spherical functions corresponding to the same eigenvalues are equal, so
\phi_{m,s}=\phi_{m,-s}
, and therefore
\hat{f}(s)=\hat{f}(-s)
.
It has been observed by many authors
[
3
,
15
,
21
]
that the
m
-spherical functions may be expressed in terms of hypergeometric functions or Jacobi functions. Indeed,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\phi_{m,s}(a_{r})

\displaystyle=(\cosh r)^{-m}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\tfrac{1-m}{2}-s,\tfrac{1-m}{2}+s;1;-\sinh^{2}r\right)

\displaystyle=(\cosh r)^{-1-2s}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\tfrac{1+m}{2}+s,\tfrac{1-m}{2}+s;1;\tanh^{2}r\right)

\displaystyle=(\cosh r)^{-1+2s}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\tfrac{1-m}{2}-s,\tfrac{1+m}{2}-s;1;\tanh^{2}r\right).

These identities imply that

\phi_{m,s}(a_{r})=\phi_{-m,s}(a_{r})=\phi_{m,-s}(a_{r})
.

When
\delta\geq 0
, define the closed vertical strip
\mathbb{S}_{\delta}
and the finite set
\mathbb{D}_{m}
in the complex plane by

\mathbb{S}_{\delta}:=\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\left|\operatorname{Re}(s)\right|\leq\delta/2\right\}

and

\mathbb{D}_{m}:=\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\,:\,2|s|\in|m|-1-2\mathbb{N}\right\}\ \qquad\text{where $\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\ldots\}$}.

When
s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}
, depending on the sign of
s
, the hypergeometric function in the second or third line of (
3.1
) is a polynomial
of degree
(|m|-1)/2-|s|
.
Moreover, as
r\to\infty

|\phi_{m,s}(a_{r})|\lesssim\begin{cases}(1+r)\,e^{-r}&\text{ if }s=0\\
e^{(2\left|\operatorname{Re}(s)\right|-1)r}&\text{ if }s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{m},s\neq 0\\
e^{(-2|s|-1)r}&\text{ if }s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\ .\end{cases}

Take
\omega(x)
to be
\|x\|^{\alpha}
.
Then
x\mapsto|\phi_{m,s}(x)|\,\omega^{-1}(x)
is bounded when
s
is in
\mathbb{S}_{\alpha+1}\cup\mathbb{D}_{m}
and therefore we can extend the map
f\mapsto\hat{f}(s)

continuously to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
.
If
f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}

has compact support, then
\hat{f}
is an entire function and for all
\delta>0

(3.2)

\sup_{s\in\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}\bigl|\hat{f}(s)\bigr|\leq C_{\delta}.

Moreover if
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(G)
then
\hat{f}
decays rapidly in
S_{\delta}
, for every
\delta>0
.

Finally, we note that if
s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}
, then
\phi_{m,s}
is in
L^{2}(G)_{m}
,
and if
s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\alpha+1}
, then
\phi_{m,s}
is in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
.
Indeed,

(3.3)

\|\phi_{m,s}\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2|s|-1}\,e^{\alpha r}\,e^{2r}\,dr\leq\frac{1}{2|s|-\alpha-1}.

The asymptotic behaviour of the spherical functions for large
r
was studied in
[
15
, p. 53]
and
[
21
, Lemma 4.7]
.
Define the (generalised) Harish-Chandra function
\mathbf{c}_{m}
by the rule

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{m}(s):=\frac{2^{1-2s}\,\Gamma\left(2s\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1+2s+m}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1+2s-m}{2}\right)}\ .

Then the function
\mathbf{c}_{m}^{-1}
is meromorphic with simple poles at the points
s
with

2s\in-|m|-1-2\mathbb{N}\qquad\text{or}\qquad 0<2s\in|m|-1-2\mathbb{N}\ .

Moreover

(3.4)

|\mathbf{c}_{m}(s)^{-1}|\sim|s|^{1/2}\qquad\text{as $s\to\infty$ in $\mathbb{S}_{\delta}$}.

When
2s
is not an integer and
r>0
, using
[
11
, 2.10 (3)]
, we may write

(3.5)

\phi_{m,s}(a_{r})=\mathbf{c}_{m}(s)\,\Phi_{m,s}(a_{r})+\mathbf{c}_{m}(-s)\,\Phi_{m,-s}(a_{r}),

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{m,s}(a_{r})

\displaystyle=(2\cosh r)^{2s-1}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\tfrac{1-m}{2}-s,\tfrac{1+m}{2}-s;1-2s;\cosh^{-2}r\right)

\displaystyle=e^{(2s-1)r}(1+\nu_{m}(s,r)),

and where the function
s\mapsto\nu_{m}(s,r)
is holomorphic in
\operatorname{Re}(s)<1
and, for all fixed
\sigma>0
, satisfies

(3.6)

\sup_{\operatorname{Re}(s)\leq 0}|\nu_{m}(s,r)|\leq C_{m}\qquad\forall r\geq\sigma.

The positive-definite
m
-spherical functions were determined in
[
23
]
.
The spherical functions with
s
in
\mathbb{D}_{m}

correspond to the representations in the discrete series.

The following inversion formulas for
f
in
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
are proved in
[
23
,
15
]
:

(3.7)

f(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(i\lambda)\,\phi_{m,i\lambda}(x)\,|\mathbf{c}_{m}(i\lambda)|^{-2}\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}}|s|\,\hat{f}(s)\,\phi_{m,s}(x);

and if
\delta\geq 0
,
\delta\notin\mathbb{D}_{m}
,
x\neq e
, then

(3.8)

f(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(\delta+i\lambda)\,\mathbf{c}_{m}(\delta+i\lambda)^{-1}\,\Phi_{-\delta-i\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{2\delta}}|s|\,\hat{f}(s)\,\phi_{m,s}(x).

Formula (
3.7
), which is well known, can be easily extended to
f\in L^{1}(G)_{m}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(i\lambda)|\,|\mathbf{c}_{m}(i\lambda)|^{-2}\,d\lambda<\infty.

Koornwinder proved formula (
3.8
) for
\delta>m/2
and stated that (
3.7
) follows.
We show now how (
3.7
) implies (
3.8
) because we need this argument for the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Notice that
\mathbf{c}_{m}(-i\lambda)=\overline{\mathbf{c}_{m}(i\lambda)}

for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence
by (
3.5
) and (
3.7
), since
\hat{f}
is even, when
r>0
we obtain

\displaystyle f(a_{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(i\lambda)\,\mathbf{c}_{m}(-i\lambda)\,\Phi_{m,-i\lambda}(a_{r})\,|\mathbf{c}_{m}(i\lambda)|^{-2}\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}}|s|\,\hat{f}(s)\,\phi_{m,s}(a_{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(i\lambda)\,\mathbf{c}_{m}(i\lambda)^{-1}\,\Phi_{m,-i\lambda}(a_{r})\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}}|s|\,\hat{f}(s)\,\phi_{m,s}(a_{r}).

Let
\delta\in(0,+\infty)\setminus\mathbb{D}_{m}
.
We shift the contour of integration,
taking care of the simple poles of the function
\mathbf{c}_{m}^{-1}
,
whose residue at
s_{j}=\frac{|m|-1}{2}-j>0
is

(-1)^{j}\binom{|m|-j}{j}\frac{1}{2^{2-|m|+2j}}
. Combining this fact
with
[
12
, 10.8 (16)]
, we obtain

\mathrm{Res}(\mathbf{c}_{m}^{-1}(s)\Phi_{m,-s},s=s_{j})=\frac{s_{j}}{2}\,\phi_{m,s_{j}}\ .

Applying the Cauchy theorem gives (
3.8
).

Here is our multiplier theorem.
The reader who is interested in sharp results will notice that the conditions here are stronger than necessary.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\delta>\alpha+1
and
\psi\colon\mathbb{S}_{\delta}\cup\mathbb{D}_{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be even and holomorphic in
\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{\delta}
.
If

M_{\delta}(\psi)=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}\,(1+|s|)^{3}|\psi(s)|<\infty,

then there exists a unique function
f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
such that
\hat{f}=\psi
, and

\|f\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\leq C_{m}\,\Bigl(M_{\delta}(\psi)+\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}\frac{|s\,\psi(s)|}{2|s|-\alpha-1}\,\,\Bigr).

Proof.

According to the inversion formula (
3.7
), we take

f(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(i\lambda)\,\phi_{m,i\lambda}(x)\,|\mathbf{c}_{m}(i\lambda)|^{-2}\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}}|s|\,\psi(s)\,\phi_{m,s}(x)\ ,

and let
\chi
be the characteristic function of
\{x\in G\,:\,\|x\|\leq 1\}
.

Since spherical functions are locally bounded and
|s\,\psi(s)|\leq M_{\delta}(\psi)
if
s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\delta}
, we deduce from (
3.4
) that

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\,M_{\delta}(\psi)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,(1+|\lambda|)^{-2}\,\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}}|s\,\psi(s)|

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl(M_{\delta}(\psi)+\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}|s\,\psi(s)|\,\Bigr),

so
f
is bounded when
\|x\|\leq 1
, and
\|f\chi\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}
satisfies the required norm estimate.

We now estimate
|f(x)|
when
\|x\|
is large. Since
f
is of type
m
, it suffices to consider

|f(a_{r})|
for large
r
.

Let
t\in(\frac{\alpha+1}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})\setminus\mathbb{D}_{m}
. Our hypotheses on
\psi
allow us to apply Cauchy’s theorem as before, and we obtain

f(a_{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(t+i\lambda)\,\mathbf{c}_{m}(t+i\lambda)^{-1}\,\Phi_{m,-t-i\lambda}(a_{r})\,d\lambda+\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{2t}}|s|\,\psi(s)\,\phi_{m,s}(a_{r}).

Recall from (
3.3
) that the spherical functions
\phi_{m,s}
with
s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\alpha+1}
lie in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
, so the finite sum is easily handled. Moreover, by (
3.4
) and (
3.6
),

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(t+i\lambda)\,\mathbf{c}_{m}(t+i\lambda)^{-1}\,\Phi_{m,-t-i\lambda}(a_{r})\,d\lambda\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{-(1+2t)r}\,\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(t+i\lambda)\,\mathbf{c}_{m}(t+i\lambda)^{-1}\,(1+\nu_{m}(-t-i\lambda,r))\,e^{-2i\lambda r}\,d\lambda\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C_{m}\,e^{-(1+2t)r}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(t+i\lambda)|\,(1+|\lambda|)^{1/2}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C_{m}\,e^{-(1+2t)r}\,M_{\delta}(\psi).

The theorem follows by integration in Cartan coordinates:

\displaystyle\|f(1-\chi)\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\,M_{\delta}(\psi)\,\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-(1+2t)r}\,\sinh(2r)\,e^{\alpha r}\,dr

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\,M_{\delta}(\psi)\,\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-(2t-\alpha-1)r}\,dr,

as required.
∎

4.
Spectral theory and the spherical transformation

Let
\Omega_{m}
be the restriction of the differential operator
\Omega
to
L^{2}(G)_{m}
.
We take the weight
\omega
to be
\omega_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
, and define

\alpha_{m}
to be an arbitrary real number strictly greater than

\tfrac{1}{4}\max\left\{\alpha^{2}+2\alpha,m^{2}-2|m|\right\}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists a “heat semigroup”
(h_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}
of functions in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
that satisfies the conditions

h_{s}*h_{t}=h_{s+t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|h_{t}\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim e^{\alpha_{m}t}

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and, setting
u(\cdot,t)=f*h_{t}
for
f\in L^{2}(G)_{m}
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(\cdot,t)+\Omega_{m}u(\cdot,t)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad u(\cdot,t)\to f\quad\text{as $t\to 0$}.

Proof.

We define
h_{t}\in L^{2}(G)_{m}
by its spherical transform:

\hat{h}_{t}(s):=\exp(t(s^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}))\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{C}.

If
s=u+iv
, then
|\hat{h}_{t}(u+iv)|=\exp(t(u^{2}-v^{2}-1/4))
, so
\hat{h}_{t}
is bounded and decays exponentially as we approach infinity in every strip
\mathbb{S}_{\delta}
.
Theorem
3.1
ensures that
h_{t}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)
.
The semigroup property and the differential equation follow from the Fourier analysis.

It now suffices to show the norm estimate, for once this is established, the convergence of
u(\cdot,t)
as
t\to 0
also follows.
The norm estimate follows from Theorem
3.1
:

\displaystyle\|h_{t}\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl(\sup_{s\in\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}\,(1+|s|)^{3}|e^{t(s^{2}-1/4)}|+\sum_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}\frac{|s|e^{t(s^{2}-1/4)}}{2|s|-\alpha-1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\Bigl(e^{t(\delta^{2}-1)/4}+\max_{s\in\mathbb{D}_{m}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}|e^{t(s^{2}-1/4)}|\Bigr),

as claimed.
∎

By improving the multiplier theorem, we could improve the
e^{\alpha_{m}t}
term in Theorem
4.1
when
|m|
is small to a polynomial term, but we do not bother to do so here.

We define
\beta_{m}
to be
(\alpha_{m}+1/4)^{1/2}
and
\gamma_{m}
to be
(\alpha_{m}+5/4)^{1/2}
.

We are going to use Fourier analysis, and it is convenient to work in the Fourier variable.
For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{2\beta_{m}}
, we define the function
r_{z}
in
L^{2}(G)_{m}
by

(4.1)

\hat{r}_{z}(s):=(z^{2}-s^{2})^{-1}\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{S}_{2\beta_{m}}.

Lemma 4.2
.

The mapping
z\mapsto r_{z}
of (
4.1
) is continuous from
\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}
to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
,

(4.2)

\left\|r_{\zeta}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim(\zeta^{2}-\beta_{m}^{2})^{-1}\qquad\forall\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\beta_{m},\beta_{m}],

and

(4.3)

\left\|r_{z}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim(1+|z|)^{4}\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}.

Further, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R},\ |\zeta|\to\infty}\left\|(\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*N}*f-f\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\to 0\qquad\forall f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m},

where
f^{*N}
denotes the
N
-fold convolution power of
f
.

Proof.

We show first that, if
\left|\operatorname{Re}z\right|\geq\gamma_{m}
and
\left|\operatorname{Re}\zeta\right|\geq\gamma_{m}
, then
r_{z}-r_{\zeta}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
and

(4.4)

\left\|r_{z}-r_{\zeta}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim|\zeta^{2}-z^{2}|(1+|z|)^{2}(1+|\zeta|)^{2}.

Then we shall prove (
4.2
).
Once we have done so, we may take
\zeta
in (
4.4
) to be
\gamma_{m}
, and (
4.3
) follows immediately.

To prove (
4.4
), we apply the multiplier theorem.
By definition,

\hat{r}_{z}(s)-\hat{r}_{\zeta}(s)=\frac{1}{z^{2}-s^{2}}-\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}-s^{2}}=\frac{\zeta^{2}-z^{2}}{(z^{2}-s^{2})(\zeta^{2}-s^{2})}\,.

There is a constant
C
such that, if
\left|\operatorname{Re}s\right|\leq\beta_{m}
and
\left|\operatorname{Re}z\right|\geq\gamma_{m}
, then

\left|\frac{1}{z-s}\right|\leq C\frac{1+|z|}{1+|s|}\,,

whence

\left|\frac{1}{(z^{2}-s^{2})(\zeta^{2}-s^{2})}\right|\leq C^{4}\frac{(1+|z|)^{2}(1+|\zeta|)^{2}}{(1+|s|)^{4}}\,.

Now (
4.4
) follows from the multiplier theorem.

To prove (
4.2
), we consider the operator
\Omega_{m}
, which is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(G)_{m}
with spectrum contained in
(-\infty,\alpha_{m}]
, and may be treated using spectral theory.
When
\lambda>\alpha_{m}
, the resolvent operator
R_{\lambda}
may be defined using the formula

R_{\lambda}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}e^{t\Omega_{m}}\,dt=(\lambda-\Omega_{m})^{-1}.

Then the convolution kernel
\ell_{\lambda}
of
R_{\lambda}
is given by

\ell_{\lambda}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}h_{t}\,dt.

By Theorem
4.1
, the heat kernels
h_{t}
lie in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
and
\left\|h_{t}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim e^{\alpha_{m}t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
It follows that
\ell_{\lambda}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
and

\left\|\ell_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}e^{\alpha_{m}t}\,dt=\frac{1}{\lambda-\alpha_{m}}\,

for all
\lambda\in(\alpha_{m},\infty)
.
Taking spherical transforms, we see that

\hat{\ell}_{\lambda}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\hat{h}_{t}(s)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\exp(t(s^{2}-1/4))\,dt=(\lambda+\tfrac{1}{4}-s^{2})^{-1}.

Comparing this formula with (
4.1
), we see that
r_{z}=\ell_{\lambda}
when
z^{2}=\lambda+\tfrac{1}{4}
.
Consequently
r_{z}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
if
z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\beta_{m},\beta_{m}]
, and

\left\|r_{z}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim\frac{1}{z^{2}-\beta_{m}^{2}}\,.

The last part of the lemma is easy.
Suppose that
f
is in
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)_{m}
, so that
\hat{f}
is entire and has rapid decay in every strip
S_{\delta}
.
Since

\left((\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*N}*f-f\right)\hat{\phantom{m}}(s)=\left(\frac{1}{1-s^{2}/\zeta^{2}}\right)^{N}\hat{f}(s)-\hat{f}(s),

the multiplier theorem (Theorem
3.1
) shows that

\lim_{\zeta\to+\infty}\left\|(\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*N}*f-f\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}=0.

Since the norms
\left\|(\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*N}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}
are uniformly bounded for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\gamma_{m},\gamma_{m}]
, this extends to all
f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
by density.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

There exists
f_{m}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
such that the closed subalgebra of
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
generated by
f_{m}
is all
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be the closed subalgebra of
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
generated by the resolvent function
r_{\gamma_{m}}
; we are going to show that
A=L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
.

We recall a standard argument that the resolvent depends analytically on the parameter
\lambda
and the resolvent set is open.
We recall that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the space of bounded linear operators on the Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.

If
R_{\lambda_{0}}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m})
, and
|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|<1/\left\|R_{\lambda_{0}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m})}
, then

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda-\Omega_{m}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)^{n}}{(\lambda_{0}-\Omega_{m})^{n+1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)^{n}R^{n+1}_{\lambda_{0}},

and this last series converges absolutely in
\mathcal{L}(L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m})
, so
R_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m})
.
It also follows that when the resolvent operator
R_{\lambda_{0}}
is given by convolution with an element of
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
, so is
R_{\lambda}
, and when
R_{\lambda_{0}}
is given by convolution with an element of
A
, so is
R_{\lambda}
.
Then the set
S:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}:r_{z}\in A\}
is open in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}
.
The set
S
is also closed in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}
, since
r_{z_{j}}\to r_{z}
in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
if
z_{j}\to z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}
by Lemma
4.2
, and is symmetric about
0
; hence
S=\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}
.

Take
f\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
; we must show that
f\in A
.
From the Cartan decomposition,
f
may be approximated arbitrarily closely in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
by elements
g_{n}
with compact support, and so it suffices to show that
g_{n}\in A
.
By (
3.2
)
\hat{g}_{n}
is uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{S}_{2\gamma_{m}}
.
By Lemma
4.2
,
g_{n}
can be approximated arbitrarily well in
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
by elements of the form
(\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*4}*g_{n}
by taking
\zeta
large in
\mathbb{R}
, so it suffices to treat
(\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*4}*g_{n}
; it is evident that
((\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*4}*g_{n})\hat{\phantom{m}}(s)
vanishes like
(1+|s|)^{-8}
at infinity in the strip
\mathbb{S}_{2\beta_{m}}
.
Let
h=(\zeta^{2}r_{\zeta})^{*4}*g_{n}
. If we can show that
h\in A
, then
f\in A
by a limiting argument.

The Cauchy integral formula and the evenness of
\hat{h}
imply that

\displaystyle\hat{h}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\hat{h}(z)\left(\frac{1}{z-s}+\frac{1}{z+s}\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}z\hat{h}(z)\frac{1}{z^{2}-s^{2}}\,dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}z\hat{h}(z)\,\hat{r}_{z}(s)\,dz\qquad\forall s\in\operatorname{Int}\mathbb{S}_{2\beta_{m}};

here
\Gamma
is the contour given by
z=\gamma_{m}+iy
where
y
varies from
-\infty
to
\infty
in
\mathbb{R}
.
Hence

h=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}z\hat{h}(z)\,r_{z}\,dz,

and since
r_{z}\in A
and
\left\|r_{z}\right\|_{L^{1}(G,\omega)}\lesssim(1+|\operatorname{Im}z|)^{4}
while
|\hat{h}(z)|\lesssim(1+|\operatorname{Im}z|)^{-8}
, the last integral
converges as an
A
-valued Bochner integral, and hence
h\in A
, as required.
∎

5.
Irreducible Hilbert representations

Suppose that we have an irreducible Hilbert representation
\rho
of
G
on
\mathcal{H}
.
We shall show that
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{m})\leq 1
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that all bounded operators on a complex Hilbert space of dimension greater than
1
admit a nontrivial closed invariant subspace.
If
\rho
is an irreducible representation of
G
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{m})\leq 1
for all
m
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be a representation of
G
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
As argued in Section
2
, there exists
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
such that
\|\rho(x)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}\leq C\|x\|^{\alpha}:=C\omega(x)
for all
x\in G
.

With a view to reaching a contradiction, suppose that
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{m})>1
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
By Lemma
2.3
, the restriction to
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
of the integrated representation of
L^{1}(G,\omega)
acts irreducibly on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.
Take
f_{m}\in L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
that generates
L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m}
, whose existence in proved in the previous section.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{m}^{\circ}
be a nontrivial closed invariant subspace for
\rho(f_{m})
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.
Then
\mathcal{H}_{m}^{\circ}
is a nontrivial closed invariant subspace for
\rho(L^{1}(G,\omega)_{m})
on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, which is our desired contradiction.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Each irreducible uniformly bounded representation
\rho
of
G
on a Hilbert space is equivalent to one of the uniformly bounded representations of Ehrenpreis and Mautner
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
and Kunze and Stein
[
16
]
.

Proof.

Our theorem implies that
\rho
is admissible.
The classification of admissible representations (see, for instance,
[
13
, Chapter 2]
) then implies the affirmation.
∎

6.
Final remarks

We have seen that every irreducible Hilbert space representation of
G
is admissible, modulo the solution to the invariant subspace problem.
Conversely, by the embedding theorem of Delorme and Souafi
[
5
]
, every irreducible Harish-Chandra module of every seimsimple Lie group can be globalised to a Hilbert representation.
Our arguments may extend to other semisimple Lie groups.
However, the extension seems to require a version of the invariant subspace problem that deals with families of operators, and this does not seem to have been considered.
