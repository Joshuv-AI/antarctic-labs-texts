Nonequilibrium Dynamics of the Chiral Quark Condensate under a Strong Magnetic Field
Gastão Krein, Carlisson Miller

Abstract
Strong magnetic fields impact quantum-chromodynamics (QCD) properties in several situations; examples include the early universe, magnetars, and heavy-ion collisions. These examples share a common trait: time evolution. A prominent QCD property impacted by a strong magnetic field is the quark condensate, an approximate order parameter of the QCD transition between a high-temperature quark-gluon phase and a low-temperature hadronic phase. We use the linear sigma model with quarks to address the quark condensate time evolution under a strong magnetic field. We use the closed time path formalism of nonequilibrium quantum field theory to integrate out the quarks and obtain a mean- field Langevin equation for the condensate. The Langevin equation features dissipation and noise kernels controlled by a damping coefficient. We compute the damping coefficient for magnetic field and temperature values achieved in peripheral relativistic heavy-ion collisions and solve the Langevin equation for a temperature quench scenario. The magnetic field changes the dissipation and noise pattern by increasing the damping coefficient compared to the zero-field case. An increased damping coefficient increases fluctuations and time scales controlling condensate’s short-time evolution, a feature that can impact hadron formation at the QCD transition. The formalism developed here can be extended to include other order parameters, hydrodynamic modes, and system’s expansion to address magnetic field effects in complex settings as heavy-ion collisions, the early universe, and magnetars.

Introduction

Strong magnetic fields impact prominent quantum-chromodynamics (QCD) phenomena,
notably those associated with QCD’s approximate chiral symmetry in the light-quark
sector. Special in this respect is the impact on the chiral condensate, as revealed
by recent lattice QCD calculations
D’Elia 2013
;
Endrödi 2014
;
Ding et al. 2020
.
The chiral condensate is an approximate order parameter for the finite
temperature QCD transition between a high-temperature quark-gluon phase (QGP) and
a low-temperature hadronic phase. The transition likely qualifies as a crossover
(not a phase transition), in that the chiral condensate is nearly zero in the QGP
phase, and nonzero in the hadronic phase, with a rapid change (not a jump) around
the pseudocritical temperature
T_{\rm pc}\simeq 150
MeV
Aoki et al. 2006
. Such
a rapid change in the condensate’s value is key to our understanding of how
protons and neutrons (and other light-flavor hadrons) acquire their masses from
almost massless quarks and gluons
Wilczek 2008
;
Roberts 2020
.
Phenomenologically, QCD matter under strong magnetic fields occurs in different
settings, to name three of great current interest: the early
universe
Vachaspati 1991
;
Grasso and Rubinstein 2001
, magnetars
Kouveliotou et al. 1998
;
Duncan and Thompson 1992
, and relativistic heavy-ion collisions
Rafelski and Muller 1976
;
Kharzeev et al. 2008
. Magnetized QCD matter in those settings evolves in time, albeit
under very different time scales. A QGP to hadron transition occurring under
such circumstances typifies a nonequilibrium phase change problem. In this paper,
we present a first study of such a dynamical transition in magnetized QCD matter;
to wit: we study the nonequilibrium dynamics of the chiral condensate under a strong
magnetic field.

Magnetic field strengths and space-time scales in this study concern the phenomenology
related to high-energy heavy-ion collision experiments. Relativistic heavy-ion collisions
produce QCD matter of deconfined quarks and gluons, the quark-gluon plasma (QGP). Noncentral collisions produce the QGP under strong magnetic fields
Rafelski and Muller 1976
;
Kharzeev et al. 2008
;
for example, noncentral Pb-Pb collisions at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) can produce fields
of strengths as large as
Skokov et al. 2009

eB=15m^{2}_{\pi}
. We conduct our study within
the perspective
of a standard three-stage scenario of QGP’s time evolution
Jacak and Muller 2012
;
Shuryak 2017
;
Pasechnik and Šumbera 2017
;
Braun-Munzinger et al. 2016
: (1) quarks and gluons are freed from
the protons and neutrons of the colliding ions and form (2) hot matter that expands
hydrodynamically until it (3) cools up to a temperature
T\sim 150
MeV, and finally
disassembles into hadrons. More specifically, we work within the perspective that
local thermodynamic properties as temperature and order parameter acquire physical
meaning.

We address magnetic field effects on the chiral condensate dynamics with Langevin
field equations, equations widely used in field theory treatments of dynamical phase
transitions
Goldenfeld 1992
;
Onuki 2002
. A prototype dynamical transition
addressed by these equations is the one of a temperature quench in a spin system, in that
a sudden drop in the system’s temperature takes the system out from a spin-disordered
phase and drives it irreversibly toward a spin-ordered phase. The quench-induced transition
just described resembles, albeit with differences, the heavy-ion collision evolution
across the crossover from a quark-gluon phase, in which the condensate is very small,
toward a hadron-dominated phase, in which the condensate ultimately reaches its vacuum
value. Indeed, for zero magnetic field, there is a vast literature on the use of Langevin field
equations in this context—Refs.
Rajagopal and Wilczek 1993
;
Bedaque and Das 1993
;
Greiner and Muller 1997
;
Biro and Greiner 1997
;
Rischke 1998
;
Xu and Greiner 2000
;
Fraga and Krein 2005
;
Boyanovsky et al. 2006
;
Farias et al. 2007
;
Nahrgang et al. 2011
;
Nahrgang et al. 2012
;
Nahrgang et al. 2013
;
Singh et al. 2011
;
Krein 2012
;
Cassol-Seewald et al. 2012
;
Singh et al. 2013
;
Herold et al. 2013
;
Singh et al. 2013
;
Bluhm et al. 2019
;
Wu et al. 2019
are a sample of this literature. The Langevin
equations featured in that literature are either postulated on phenomenological
grounds

;
Fraga and Krein 2005
;
Singh et al. 2013
;
Krein 2012
;
Cassol-Seewald et al. 2012
;
Wu et al. 2019
,
or derived from a microscopic model through a coarse-graining procedure
Rajagopal and Wilczek 1993
;
Bedaque and Das 1993
;
Greiner and Muller 1997
;
Rischke 1998
;
Xu and Greiner 2000
;
Boyanovsky et al. 2006
;
Farias et al. 2007
;
Nahrgang et al. 2011
;
Nahrgang et al. 2012
;
Nahrgang et al. 2013
;
Singh et al. 2011
;
Herold et al. 2013
;
Singh et al. 2013
;
Bluhm et al. 2019
. We follow the
latter approach.

We extend the semiclassical approach of Ref.
Nahrgang et al. 2011
to include
magnetic field effects on the chiral condensate dynamics. In that approach,
the condensate dynamics is governed by a Langevin field equation derived from a semiclassical
two-particle irreducible (2PI) effective action. The effective action, computed with the time
path formalism of nonequilibrium quantum field theory
Calzetta and Hu 2008
;
Bellac 2011
,
refers to the Gell-Mann–Levy linear sigma model
Gell-Mann and Levy 1960
with quarks (LSMq).
The LSMq features degrees of freedom associated with the long-wavelength QCD
chiral physics: constituent quarks, pseudoscalar-isoscalar mesons (pions, pseudo-Goldstone bosons),
and a scalar-isoscalar meson (the quark condensate). The model does not describe quark
confinement.
Despite of this limitation, the model describes many of the equilibrium, time-independent
magnetic field effects on the QCD equation of state, phase structure and chiral
condensate
Fraga and Mizher 2008
;
Ayala et al. 2009
;
Frasca and Ruggieri 2011
;
Andersen and Khan 2012
;
Andersen and Tranberg 2012
;
Ruggieri et al. 2013
;
Fraga et al. 2014
;
Kamikado and Kanazawa 2014
;
Ruggieri et al. 2014
;
Ayala et al. 2014
;
Ayala et al. 2015
;
Andersen et al. 2015
;
Ayala et al. 2015

brought out by lattice calculations. We direct the reader to Refs.
Gatto and Ruggieri 2013
;
Ayala et al. 2015
;
Miransky and Shovkovy 2015
;
Andersen et al. 2016
for reviews with additional references on works
employing the LSMq and also other models.

This first study aims primarily to get insight into how a strong magnetic field affects
condensate dynamics. To fulfil this aim, we simplify the analysis by omitting physical
effects peculiar to a heavy-ion collision. We address the omissions and ensuing
consequences in the course of the presentation of our work. Besides, we seek an analytical
understanding and avoid, whenever possible numerical calculations. Notwithstanding the
simplifications, our study brings new insight into a complex problem that offers enormous
opportunities to learn about QCD matter.

We organize the presentation of the paper as follows. In the next section, we define
the chiral quark model upon which we base our study and summarize its main features.
In Section
3
we define the effective action and use the closed time path
formalism to derive an equation of motion for the condensate, a Langevin equation
featuring dissipation and noise kernels. The latter require the magnetized thermal
quark propagator in the real time formalism. We derive the propagator
in Section
4
. We complete the calculation of the the damping and
noise kernels in Section
5
. We present explicit numerical results in
Section
6
and conclude in Section
7
.

2
The model

We present the main ingredients of the model upon which we base our study of magnetic field
effects on the chiral condensate dynamics. The condensate dynamics is governed by a Langevin field
equation derived from a semiclassical two-particle irreducible (2PI) effective action
Nahrgang et al. 2011
.
The effective action builds on effective degrees of freedom associated with the long wavelength chiral physics
described by a Lagrangian featuring the approximate
SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}
symmetry of QCD. The Lagrangian
is that of the Gell-Mann–Levy linear sigma model
Gell-Mann and Levy 1960
, in which quarks replace
the nucleons of the original model. As in the Lagrangian of the original model, a fermion
isodoublet field,
q=(u,d)^{T}
, representing the light
u
and
d
quarks, Yukawa-couples to
pseudoscalar-isotriplet pion
\bm{\pi}
field and a scalar-isoscalar
\sigma
field.
The Lagrangian density of the linear sigma model with quarks (LSMq) is given by

{\mathcal{L}}=\bar{q}[i\not{\partial}-g(\sigma+i\gamma_{5}{\bm{\tau}}\cdot\bm{\pi})]q+\frac{1}{2}\left[\partial_{\mu}\sigma\partial^{\mu}\sigma+\partial_{\mu}\bm{\pi}\cdot\partial^{\mu}\bm{\pi}\right]-U(\sigma,\bm{\pi}),

(1)

where
U(\sigma,\bm{\pi})
is the potential

U(\sigma,\bm{\pi})=\frac{\lambda}{4}(\sigma^{2}+\bm{\pi}^{2}-v^{2})^{2}-h_{q}\sigma-U_{0},

(2)

where
U_{0}
is an arbitrary constant setting the zero of
U(\sigma,\bm{\pi})
.
We use the metric signature
g^{\mu\nu}=(1,-1,-1,-1)
and the Bjorken-Drell
Bjorken and Drell 1965

conventions for the Dirac
\gamma^{\mu}
matrices, for which
\{\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}\}=2g^{\mu\nu}
.

For
h_{q}=0
, the Lagrangian density is invariant under chiral
SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}

transformations. This symmetry can break spontaneously, in that
\sigma
acquires a nonzero vacuum
expectation value
\langle\sigma\rangle=v\neq 0
, whereas
\langle\bm{\pi}\rangle=0

due to parity.
For
h_{q}\neq 0
, the term
{\cal L}^{\rm LSM}_{SB}=h_{q}\sigma
breaks the
symmetry explicitly and plays the role of the symmetry-breaking quark mass term
in the QCD Lagrangian,

{\cal L}^{\rm QCD}_{m}=-m\bar{q}q
. Equality between the (vacuum or thermal)
expectation values of
{\cal L}^{\rm LSM}_{SB}
and
{\cal L}^{\rm QCD}_{m}
implies

m\,\langle\bar{q}q\rangle_{\rm QCD}=-h_{q}\langle\sigma\rangle
, and
establishes the physical correspondence between
\langle\sigma\rangle
and the quark
condensate in QCD—Ref.
Koch 1997
presents a didactic review on this
and other topics relating the LSM and QCD.
One can fit the parameters of the
model to chiral physics observables—a fit at the classical level, for example, sets
the parameters as:
h_{q}=f_{\pi}m^{2}_{\pi}
,
v^{2}=f^{2}_{\pi}-m^{2}_{\pi}/\lambda^{2}
,

m^{2}_{\sigma}=2\lambda^{2}f^{2}_{\pi}+m^{2}_{\pi}
, and
m_{q}=g\langle\sigma\rangle
.
Here
f_{\pi}
and
m_{\pi}
are the pion weak-decay constant and mass,
m_{\sigma}
the

\sigma
-meson mass, and
m_{q}
the constituent quark mass.
We chose
U_{0}
such that
U(0,0)=0
(Ref. [29] chooses
U_{0}

such that
U(f_{\pi},0)=0
).

The parameter
g
plays a very important role in the model’s equilibrium thermodynamics.
For example, when solving the model in the mean field approximation for zero
baryon chemical potential, one obtains a first order transition at a
temperature
T\simeq 123
MeV with
g=5.5
, a second order transition at

T\simeq 140
MeV with
g=3.63
, and a crossover at
T\simeq 150
MeV with

g=3.3
. We restrict our study of the condensate dynamics to the situation of a
crossover, the situation seemingly relevant for QCD. The model the has also been
used to study equilibrium, time-independent
magnetic field effects on the QCD equation of state, phase structure and chiral
condensate—for references, we direct the reader to
Refs.
Fraga and Mizher 2008
;
Ayala et al. 2009
;
Frasca and Ruggieri 2011
;
Andersen and Khan 2012
;
Andersen and Tranberg 2012
;
Ruggieri et al. 2013
;
Fraga et al. 2014
;
Kamikado and Kanazawa 2014
;
Ruggieri et al. 2014
;
Ayala et al. 2014
;
Ayala et al. 2015
;
Andersen et al. 2015
;
Ayala et al. 2015
and the reviews in
Refs.
Gatto and Ruggieri 2013
;
Ayala et al. 2015
;
Miransky and Shovkovy 2015
;
Andersen et al. 2016
.

We derive the LSMq effective action within the semiclassical framework developed for zero magnetic
field in Ref.
Nahrgang et al. 2011
. In that framework, the long wavelength (soft) modes control the

\sigma
field dynamics, with the quarks providing a heat bath. In the present case, this means
that the quarks are in equilibrium at some local temperature and local magnetic field. The
magnetic field enters the LSMq Lagrangian by replacing in Eq. (
1
)
\partial_{\mu}

by
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+iqA_{\mu}
, where
q
stands for the (quark or pion) electric
charge and
A_{\mu}
the electromagnetic vector field. We neglect pion fields in this first study
but discuss in Section
6
their possible implications on our results. In this
semiclassical framework, the magnetic field is a background field, not a dynamical degree of
freedom. The effective action is then a functional of the
\sigma(x)
mean field and of the
magnetic-field dependent quark propagator
S(x,y)
. We denote the effective action by

\Gamma[\sigma,S]
.

3
The effective action and Langevin equation

We summarize the main steps in the derivation of the Langevin equation for the
\sigma
mean field
from an effective action using the closed time path (CTP) formalism
Calzetta and Hu 2008
;
Bellac 2011
.
In the CTP formalism, one evolves the fields over the Schwinger-Keldysh contour, an oriented
time path
{\cal C}={\cal C}_{+}\cup{\cal C}_{-}
, in that the time variable
t
runs from an
initial time
-\tau
to a time
\tau
along
{\cal C}_{+}
and going back to
-\tau
along

{\cal C}_{-}
. One identifies fields on
{\cal C}_{+}
with an index
+
, whereas those on

{\cal C}_{-}
with
-
, i.e.
\sigma^{a}(x)
and
S^{ab}(x,y)
with
a=\pm
. A time instant
on
C_{-}
is posterior to any time instant on
C_{+}
. The fields on
{\cal C}_{+}
and those
on
{\cal C}_{-}
are not independent fields; they couple through a

CTP boundary condition
in that they coincide at large
\tau
for all values of the spatial
coordinate
Calzetta and Hu 2008
. To set notation and make the paper self-contained, we mention
that we set the speed of light
c
, the reduced Planck constant
\hbar=h/2\pi
, and the
Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
to unity, and define
S^{ab}(x,y)
as

\displaystyle S^{++}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle q(x)\overline{q}(y)\rangle\,\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})-\langle\overline{q}(y)q(x)\rangle\,\theta(y^{0}-x^{0}),

(3)

\displaystyle S^{--}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle q(x)\overline{q}(y)\rangle\,\theta(y^{0}-x^{0})-\langle\overline{q}(y)q(x)\rangle\,\theta(x^{0}-y^{0}),

(4)

\displaystyle S^{+-}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{<}(x,y)=-\langle\overline{q}(y)q(x)\rangle,

(5)

\displaystyle S^{-+}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{>}(x,y)=\langle q(x)\overline{q}(y)\rangle,

(6)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle
stands for averaging with respect to a density matrix specifying
the initial state. The propagator
S^{++}(x,y)
is nothing else the causal Feynman propagator
and
S^{--}(x,y)
the corresponding anti-causal propagator; from
the above definitions, one has:

\displaystyle S^{++}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{-+}(x,y)\,\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})+S^{+-}(x,y)\,\theta(y^{0}-x^{0}),

(7)

\displaystyle S^{--}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{+-}(x,y)\,\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})+S^{-+}(x,y)\,\theta(y^{0}-x^{0}).

(8)

The semiclassical action is given by

\Gamma[\sigma,S]=\Gamma_{\rm cl}[\sigma]+i\,{\rm Tr}\ln S-i\,{\rm Tr}\left(i\not{D}-m_{0}\right)S+\Gamma_{2}[\sigma,S],

(9)

where
\Gamma_{\rm cl}
is the classical action,
m_{0}=g\sigma_{0}
, and
\Gamma_{2}[\sigma,S]

contains the sum of 2PI diagrams. Here,
{\rm Tr}
stands for a spatial integration over the
Schwinger-Keldysh contour and sums over Dirac, color and flavor indices. Although one deals
with two fields,
\sigma^{+}
and
\sigma^{-}
, as mentioned above they are not independent, there
is a single mean field
\sigma(x)
, and a single equation of motion
Calzetta and Hu 2008
:

\frac{\delta\Gamma[\sigma,S]}{\delta\sigma^{+}(x)}{\Biggl|}_{\sigma^{-}=\sigma^{+}=\sigma}=-\frac{\delta\Gamma[\sigma,S]}{\delta\sigma^{-}(x)}{\Biggl|}_{\sigma^{+}=\sigma^{-}=\sigma}=0.

(10)

We need also the equation of motion for
S^{ab}(x,y)
:

\frac{\delta\Gamma[\sigma^{+},\sigma^{-},S]}{\delta S^{ab}(x,y)}=0,

(11)

or, equivalently:

\left(i\not{D}-m_{0}\right)S^{ab}(x,y)-\int_{\cal C}d^{4}z\,\frac{\delta\Gamma_{2}[\sigma,S]}{\delta S^{ac}(x,z)}\,S^{cb}(z,y)=i\delta^{ab}\delta^{(4)}(x-y),

(12)

here
{\cal C}
indicates that the integration runs over the Schwinger-Keldysh contour. Only one 2PI
diagram contributes to
\Gamma_{2}[\sigma,S]
, a single one-loop diagram that involves the trace over
the magnetic field dependent quark propagator, namely:

\Gamma_{2}[\sigma,S]=g\int_{\cal C}d^{4}x\,{\rm tr}_{CDF}\left[S^{++}(x,x)\sigma^{+}(x)+S^{--}(x,x)\sigma^{-}(x)\right],

(13)

where
{\rm tr}_{Dcf}
indicates trace over Dirac, color and flavor indices.

Replacing Eq. (
13
) into Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
), the last two terms in
Eq. (
9
) cancel; but to complete the derivation of
\Gamma[\sigma,S]
, one still
needs to solve Eq. (
12
) for
S^{ab}
. However, to solve Eq. (
12
) for
S^{ab}
is
not an easy task, even for the zero magnetic field case due to the spatiotemporal dependence
of
\sigma(x)
. Fortunately, the problem with magnetic field
is still tractable within the spirit of the semiclassical approach we use here. Specifically, by
assuming that long wavelength modes dominate the
\sigma(x)
dynamics
Nahrgang et al. 2011
,
in that dynamical fluctuations
\delta\sigma
build on a
\sigma_{0}
background mean field,
with
\sigma_{0}
governed by a locally equilibrated quark heath bath described by a thermomagnetic quark propagator
S_{\rm thm}
. The propagator
S_{\rm thm}
depends on a local temperature
and magnetic field, quantities that also drive a spatiotemporal dependence for the
\sigma_{0}

mean field. In practice, this amounts to split
\sigma^{a}(x)
as follows:

\sigma^{a}(x)=\sigma^{a}_{0}(x)+\delta\sigma^{a}(x),

(14)

and write
S^{ab}
as a functional power series in
\delta\sigma^{a}(x)
, with
S^{ab}_{\rm thm}

the zeroth order term:

S^{ab}(x,y)=S^{ab}_{\rm thm}(x,y)+\delta S^{ab}(x,y)+\delta^{2}S^{ab}(x,y)+\cdots.

(15)

When one replaces these expansions into Eq. (
12
) and takes into account Eq. (
13
),
one determines
\delta S^{ab}(x,y)
,
\delta^{2}S^{ab}(x,y),\cdots
recursively.
Specifically, the zeroth order propagator
S^{ab}_{\rm thm}
obeys the equation

\left[i\not{D}-m_{0}-g\,\sigma_{0}(x)\right]S^{ab}_{\rm thm}(x,y)=-i\delta^{ab}\delta^{(4)}(x-y),

(16)

whereas the fluctuating contributions, up to the second order in
\delta\sigma
, read:

\displaystyle\delta S^{ab}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ig\int_{SK}d^{4}z\,S^{ac}_{\rm thm}(x,z)\delta\sigma^{c}(z)S^{cb}_{\rm thm}(z,y),

(17)

\displaystyle\delta^{2}S^{ab}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g^{2}\int_{SK}d^{4}zd^{4}z^{\prime}\,S^{ac}_{\rm thm}(x,z)\delta\sigma^{c}(z)S^{cd}_{\rm thm}(z,z^{\prime})\delta\sigma^{d}(z^{\prime})S^{db}_{\rm thm}(z^{\prime},y).

(18)

Equation (
16
) evinces the role played by the
\sigma_{0}(x)
background field,
it gives quarks a local effective mass
m_{q}(x)=g\sigma_{0}(x)
determined by local
temperature and magnetic field. To obtain the equation of motion
for the mean field, one can now replace Eqs. (
14
)-(
18
)
into Eq. (
9
) and trail the steps in Ref.
Nahrgang et al. 2011
. Although the
magnetic field introduces new features into the Langevin dynamics, the generic form of the
equation is the same as for zero magnetic field, in that
S^{ab}_{\rm thm}
contains all
the effects of the magnetic field on the
\sigma
dynamics. Therefore, for now, we
do not need the explicit expression for
S^{ab}_{\rm thm}
to write down the Langevin
equation—we obtain the explicit form of
S^{ab}_{\rm thm}
in the following
section.

But before writing down the Langevin equation for the
\sigma
mean field, we comment on
two points in
the derivation of the equation, namely: the lack of independence of the fields on
{\cal C}_{+}

from those on
{\cal C}_{-}
, and the appearance of the noise source in the
\delta\sigma
equation
of motion. To account for the first point, one performs a change of basis
Calzetta and Hu 2008
,
a.k.a. Keldysh rotation
Kamenev 2011
. We apply the Keldysh rotation to
\sigma=\sigma_{0}+\delta\sigma
, which implies for the fluctuating
\delta\sigma
field needed here:

\delta\bar{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta\sigma^{+}(x)+\delta\sigma^{-}(x)\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\Delta\sigma(x)=\delta\sigma^{+}(x)-\delta\sigma^{-}(x).

(19)

This transformation makes transparent the physics behind the doubling of fields: it
reflects the need for both
response
(
\Delta\sigma
) and
fluctuating

(
\delta\bar{\sigma}
) fields to describe time-dependent fluctuating phenomena
Martin et al. 1973
.
The second point refers to the fact that
\Gamma[\sigma,S]
contains an imaginary part associated
with dissipation, a feature that obstructs the straightforward variation implied by
Eq. (
10
). A way to obtain a real action uses the Feynman-Vernon
trick
Feynman and Vernon 1963
, in that one replaces the imaginary part of
the action by a noise source coupling linearly to the field; this turns the equation
of motion into a
stochastic equation—we refer to the book of Ref.
Calzetta and Hu 2008

for a thorough discussion on this and other aspects of the CTP formalism. In summary, after using
Eqs. (
14
)-(
18
) into Eq. (
9
) and rewriting the action in terms of
the Keldysh-rotated fields, replacing the resulting imaginary part in the action by a noise source,
and varying w.r.t.
\Delta\sigma
and setting
\bar{\sigma}(x)=\sigma(x)
as implied by
Eq. (
10
), one obtains a stochastic differential equation for
\sigma(x)
,
namely
Nahrgang et al. 2011
:

\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}\sigma(x)+\frac{\delta U[\sigma]}{\delta\sigma(x)}+g\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})-D_{\sigma}(x)=\xi_{\sigma}(x),

(20)

where
\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})
is the scalar density:

\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})=\mathrm{tr}_{Dcf}\,S_{thm}^{++}(x,x),

(21)

and
D_{\sigma}(x)
the
dissipation kernel
:

D_{\sigma}(x)=ig^{2}\int d^{4}y\,\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\,M(x,y)\,\delta\bar{\sigma}(y),

(22)

with

M(x,y)=\mathrm{tr}_{Dcf}\left[S^{+-}_{thm}(x,y)S^{-+}_{thm}(y,x)-S^{-+}_{thm}(x,y)S^{+-}_{thm}(y,x)\right],

(23)

and
\xi_{\sigma}(x)
is a colored-noise field with the properties:

\langle\xi_{\sigma}(x)\rangle_{\xi}=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\langle\xi_{\sigma}(x)\xi_{\sigma}(y)\rangle_{\xi}=N(x,y),

(24)

with the
noise kernel

N(x,y)
given by:

N(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}\mathrm{tr}_{Dcf}\left[S^{+-}_{thm}(x,y)S^{-+}_{thm}(y,x)+S^{-+}_{thm}(x,y)S^{+-}_{thm}(y,x)\right].

(25)

In Eq. (
24
),
\langle\cdots\rangle_{\xi}
means functional average
with the probability distribution

P[\xi]=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\,\xi(x)N^{-1}(x,y)\,\xi(y)\right].

(26)

Our summary on the the derivation of the Langevin equation ends here. To proceed with the study
of magnetic field effects on the
\sigma
dynamics, we need the explicit form of the thermomagnetic
quark propagator
S^{ab}_{thm}
—we derive
S^{ab}_{thm}
in the next
section.

4
The thermomagnetic quark propagator

One can obtain the CTP thermomagnetic quark propagator at temperature
T
from the corresponding

T=0
propagator through a Bogoliubov transformation, much in the same way as done in thermofield
dynamics (TFD)
Loewe and Rojas 1992
;
Elmfors et al. 1993
;
Hasan et al. 2017
;
Rath and Patra 2017
.
Let
\overline{S}_{m}(x,y)

be the causal, zero temperature quark propagator in a constant magnetic field of strength
B

pointing along the
\hat{{\bm{z}}}
direction.
\overline{S}_{m}(x,y)
can be written as the product of a
gauge-dependent
Schwinger phase
\phi(x,y)
and a gauge-independent, translation invariant propagator

S_{m}(x-y)
, namely
Schwinger 1951
:

\displaystyle\overline{S}_{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\langle q(x)\bar{q}(y)\rangle-\theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\rangle\bar{q}(y)\psi(y)\rangle=S^{++}_{m}(x,y)

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})S^{-+}_{m}(x,y)+\theta(y^{0}-x^{0})S^{+-}_{m}(y,x)

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(x,y)\,S_{m}(x-y)=\phi(x,y)\,\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}\,S^{++}_{m}(p).

(29)

The phase factor is irrelevant for us since it cancels out in all terms appearing in the
Langevin equation in Eq. (
20
) due to the properties
\phi(x,z)\phi(z,y)=\phi(x,y)

and
\phi(x,x)=1
. We can also choose the symmetric gauge,
A_{\mu}=(0,-y,x,0)\,B/2
, for which
\phi(x,y)=1
. In any case, one can focus on the translation invariant piece of the
propagator which, from now on will be the object of interest.

We use the Landau level representation of
S_{m}(p)
and work with the lowest level contribution, the
dominant contribution for strong fields. The lowest Landau level (LLL) contribution to
S_{m}(p)
can
be written as
Miransky and Shovkovy 2015
:

S^{++}_{m}(p)=i\,e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,\frac{2\left(\not{p}_{\parallel}+m_{q}\right)}{p_{\parallel}^{2}-m^{2}_{q}+i\epsilon}\,P_{+},

(30)

where
{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}=p^{2}_{x}+p^{2}_{y}
,
p_{\parallel}^{2}=p_{0}^{2}-p_{z}^{2}
,

\not{p}_{\parallel}=\gamma^{0}p_{0}-\gamma^{3}p_{z}
, and

P_{+}=[1+i\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}{\rm sign}(qB)]/2
. The presence of the operator
P_{+}
in
Eq. (
30
) reflects the spin-polarized nature of the lowest Landau level, as

P_{+}
projects out
one of the two spin directions. From this result, one obtains the off-diagonal CTP components

S^{+-}_{m}
and
S^{-+}_{m}
by using Eqs. (
7
) and (
28
) and the identity:

\displaystyle i\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dp_{0}\frac{\not{p}_{\parallel}+m_{q}}{p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q}+i\epsilon}\,e^{-ip_{0}(x^{0}-y^{0})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dp_{0}(\not{p}_{\parallel}+m_{q})2\pi\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\theta(p_{0})\,e^{-ip_{0}(x^{0}-y^{0})}

(31)

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt+\,\theta(y^{0}-x^{0})\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dp_{0}(\not{p}_{\parallel}+m_{q})2\pi\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\theta(-p_{0})e^{-ip_{0}(x^{0}-y^{0})}.

Therefore, the CTP components
S^{ab}_{m}(p)
of the zero-temperature propagator can be written as:

\displaystyle S^{++}_{m}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)\,\frac{i}{p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q}+i\epsilon},

(32)

\displaystyle S^{+-}_{m}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)\,2\pi\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\,\theta(-p_{0}),

(33)

\displaystyle S^{-+}_{m}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)\,2\pi\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\,\theta(p_{0}),

(34)

\displaystyle S^{--}_{m}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)\,\frac{-i}{p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q}-i\epsilon},

(35)

where, to lighten the notation, we defined

A(p)=2(\not{p}_{\parallel}+m_{q})\,P_{+}=(\not{p}_{\parallel}+m_{q})\left[1+i\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}{\rm sign}(qB)\right].

(36)

One obtains the thermal propagator
S^{ab}_{thm}(p)
from
S^{ab}_{m}(p)
through the
Bogoliubov transformation,

\begin{pmatrix}S^{++}_{thm}(p)&S^{+-}_{thm}(p)\\[5.69046pt]
S^{-+}_{thm}(p)&S^{--}_{thm}(p)\end{pmatrix}=V_{CTP}(T,p)\begin{pmatrix}S^{++}_{m}(p)&S^{+-}_{m}(p)\\[5.69046pt]
S^{-+}_{m}(p)&S^{--}_{m}(p)\end{pmatrix}V_{CTP}(T,p).

(37)

A possible CTP transformation matrix
V_{CTP}(T,p)
is the following:

V_{CTP}(T,p)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sinh|p_{0}|/T}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{|p_{0}|/2T}&-e^{-|p_{0}|/2T}\\[5.69046pt]
-e^{-|p_{0}|/2T}&e^{|p_{0}|/2T}\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

V_{CTP}
is the fermionic counterpart to the bosonic Bogoliubov transformation
matrix in Ref.
Das et al. 2018
, denoted
U_{CT}(T,p)
in that reference.
The individual
S^{ab}_{thm}(p)
components are then given by:

\displaystyle S^{++}_{thm}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)\left[\frac{i}{p_{\parallel}^{2}-m^{2}_{q}+i\epsilon}-2\pi n_{F}(p_{0})\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\right],

(39)

\displaystyle S^{+-}_{thm}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)2\pi\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\left[\theta(-p_{0})-n_{F}(p_{0})\right],

(40)

\displaystyle S^{-+}_{thm}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)2\pi\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\left[\theta(p_{0})-n_{F}(p_{0})\right],

(41)

\displaystyle S^{--}_{thm}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}}/{|q_{f}B|}}\,A(p)\left[\frac{-i}{p_{\parallel}^{2}-m^{2}_{q}-i\epsilon}-2\pi n_{F}(p_{0})\delta(p^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\right],

(42)

where
n_{F}(p_{0})
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution:

n_{F}(p_{0})=\frac{1}{e^{|p_{0}|}/T+1}.

(43)

Here,
q_{u}=2e/3
,
q_{d}=-e/3
, and
e=1/\sqrt{137}

(we use Gaussian units).
We note that one obtains
the same result for
S^{ab}_{thm}
with the more standard TFD Bogoliubov transformation,
by multiplying the off-diagonal elements of TFD propagator,
S^{12}(p)
and
S^{21}(p)
,
by
e^{-p_{0}/2T}
and
e^{+p_{0}/2T}
, respectively. The diagonal elements of CTP and TFD
propagators are the same, of course.

We note that the LLL approximation is suitable for strong magnetic fields only. Therefore, one
cannot extrapolate
B\neq 0
results to recover
B=0
results. Such an extrapolation
is possible when performing the sum over all Landau levels or using an alternative
representation of the propagator—see, for example, Appendix A of
Ref.
Miransky and Shovkovy 2015
.

This completes the derivation of
S^{ab}(x,y)
. In the next section we compute the different
pieces entering the Langevin equation in Eq. (
20
), namely, the scalar density
\rho_{s}

and the dissipation
D(x)
and noise
N(x,y)
kernels. As mentioned before, our interest in
on the long-wavelength physics of the
\sigma
mean field dynamics, thereby we neglect vacuum
contributions to these quantities.

5
The scalar density, dissipation and noise kernels

We start with the scalar density
\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})
. Although we have flavor symmetry at the level
of the quark masses,
m_{u}=m_{d}=m_{q}=g\sigma_{0}
, we still need to make explicit the flavor
content of the propagator because of the quark electric charges. After taking the trace over Dirac,
color and flavor indices, one can write
\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})
as the sum of two
contributions
Menezes et al. 2009
;
Farias et al. 2014
,
\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})=\rho^{B}_{s}(\sigma_{0})+\rho^{BT}_{s}(\sigma_{0})
,
where
\rho^{B}_{s}(\sigma_{0})
depends only on
B
:

\rho^{B}_{s}(\sigma_{0})=-\frac{N_{c}}{2\pi^{2}}\,m_{q}\,\sum_{f=u,d}|q_{f}B|\left[\ln\Gamma(x_{f})-\frac{1}{2}\ln 2\pi+x_{f}-\frac{1}{2}(2x_{f}-1)\ln x_{f}\right],

(44)

and
\rho^{BT}_{s}(\sigma_{0})
that depends on
B
and
T
:

\rho^{BT}_{s}(\sigma_{0})=-\frac{N_{c}}{\pi^{2}}\,m_{q}\left(|q_{u}B|+|q_{d}B|\right)\int^{\infty}_{0}dp_{z}\,\frac{n_{F}(E_{q}(p_{z}))}{E_{q}(p_{z})},

(45)

where
N_{c}=3
is the number of colors,
x_{f}=m^{2}_{q}/2|q_{f}B|
,
\Gamma(x)
the Euler gamma
function, and
E_{q}(p_{z})=\sqrt{p^{2}_{z}+m^{2}_{q}}
.

Next, we consider the dissipation
D(x)
and noise
N(x,y)
kernels , Eqs. (
22
)
and (
25
). To compute
D(x)
, we need the function
M(x,y)
, given in
Eq. (
23
). The Schwinger phase
\phi(x,y)
cancels out in Eq. (
22
); as a result,

M(x,y)
becomes a function of
x-y
:

\displaystyle M(x-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{f}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,e^{-i(p-q)\cdot(x-y)}\,\mathrm{tr}_{Dc}\left[S^{+-}_{thm}(p)S^{-+}_{thm}(q)-S^{-+}_{thm}(p)S^{+-}_{thm}(q)\right]

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}\,M(p),

where

\displaystyle M(p)=\sum_{f=u,d}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\mathrm{tr}_{Dc}\left[S^{+-}_{thm}(p+q)S^{-+}_{thm}(q)-S^{-+}_{thm}(p+q)S^{+-}_{thm}(q)\right]_{f}\equiv\sum_{f=u,d}M_{f}(p).

(47)

The Schwinger phase also cancels out in Eq. (
25
) and
N(x,y)=N(x-y)

can be written as:

\displaystyle N(x-y)=\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}\,N(p),

(48)

where

N(p)=-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}\sum_{f=u,d}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\mathrm{tr}_{Dc}\left[S^{+-}_{thm}(p+q)S^{-+}_{thm}(q)+S^{-+}_{thm}(p+q)S^{+-}_{thm}(q)\right]_{f}\equiv\sum_{f=u,d}N_{f}(p).

(49)

Next, we use
M
’s translation invariance to write the dissipation kernel
D(x)

as
Nahrgang et al. 2011
:

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(t,{\bm{x}})=ig^{2}\int\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,M(p_{0},{\bm{p}})\int d^{3}ydy^{0}\,\theta(x^{0}-y^{0})e^{-ip_{0}(x^{0}-y^{0})+i{\bm{p}}\cdot({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})}\,\delta\sigma(y^{0},{\bm{y}})

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ig^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{i{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{dp_{0}}{2\pi}\,M(p_{0},{\bm{p}})\,\int^{\infty}_{0}d\tau\,e^{-ip_{0}\tau}\,\delta\sigma(t-\tau,{\bm{p}}).

Here, we made the change of variable
x^{0}-y^{0}=\tau
and
defined the spatial Fourier transform of the
\sigma
mean field:

\delta\sigma(t-\tau,{\bm{p}})=\int d^{3}y\,e^{-i{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{y}}}\,\delta\sigma(t-\tau,{\bm{y}}).

(51)

Eq. (
50
) exposes the presence of memory in the
\sigma
dynamics,
in that the value of
\sigma
at time
t
depends upon the values of
\sigma
at earlier
times
t-\tau
. This feature imposes technical difficulties to the analysis of the Langevin
equation as it requires numerical techniques to proceed. To maintain the pace with an analytically
tractable analysis, we follow Refs.
Greiner and Muller 1997
;
Rischke 1998
;
Nahrgang et al. 2011
and use
a
linear harmonic approximation
, whereby the dynamics memory is captured by soft-mode
harmonic oscillations around the mean field
\sigma_{0}(t,{\bm{p}})
. This approximation amounts to assume
an harmonic
\tau
dependence for
\bar{\sigma}(t-\tau,{\bm{p}})
, namely:

\displaystyle\sigma(t-\tau,{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(t)\cos(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)+b(t)\sin(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(t,{\bm{p}})\cos(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)-\frac{1}{E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})}\,\sin(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)\,\frac{\partial\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(t,{\bm{p}})+\delta\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(t,{\bm{p}})+\left\{\sigma_{0}(t,{\bm{p}})[\cos(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)-1]-\frac{1}{E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})}\,\sin(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)\,\frac{\partial\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})}{\partial t}\right\},

where
E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\approx\sqrt{{\bm{p}}^{2}+m^{2}_{\sigma}}
is a characteristic soft-mode
frequency, where
m_{\sigma}
is the
\sigma
field mass. The functions
a(t)
and
b(t)
were
determined using as initial conditions
\sigma(t-\tau,{\bm{p}})|_{\tau=0}=\sigma_{0}(t,{\bm{p}})
and

\partial\sigma(t-\tau,{\bm{p}})/\partial\tau|_{\tau=0}=-\partial\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})/\partial t
.
The first term within the curly brackets in Eq. (
52
), being linear in
\sigma_{0}
is a
leading-order correction to
g\rho_{s}
and, since
\cos(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\tau)-1
oscillates
around zero, it is neglected; as such, one obtains for
D(t,{\bm{x}})
:

\displaystyle D(t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{i{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,\eta({\bm{p}})\,\frac{\partial\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})}{\partial t}.

(53)

where
\eta({\bm{p}})
is the momentum-dependent
damping coefficient
:

\eta({\bm{p}})=g^{2}\frac{1}{2E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})}M({\bm{p}}).

(54)

To lighten the notation, we denoted
\eta(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}),{\bm{p}})
by
\eta({\bm{p}})
and

M(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}),{\bm{p}})

by
M({\bm{p}})
—from this point on, this notation will be used throughout the paper.

The harmonic approximation rendered the dissipation kernel local in time and
in a form appropriate to work with the Langevin equation in momentum-space:

\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})}{\partial t^{2}}+{\bm{p}}^{2}\,\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})+\eta({\bm{p}})\,\frac{\partial\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})}{\partial t}+F_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}})=\xi_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}}),

(55)

where
\eta({\bm{p}})
was defined in Eq. (
54
), and

\displaystyle F_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}})=\int d^{3}x\,e^{-i{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,\left[\frac{\delta U[\sigma]}{\delta\sigma(t,{\bm{x}})}+g\,\rho_{s}(\sigma_{0})\right].

(56)

The momentum space colored noise field has zero mean
\langle\xi_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}})\rangle_{\xi}=0

and correlation:

\langle\xi_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}})\xi_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}})\rangle_{\xi}=(2\pi)^{3}\delta({\bm{p}}+{\bm{p}}^{\prime})N(t-t^{\prime},{\bm{p}}),

(57)

where

N(t-t^{\prime},{\bm{p}})=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{dp_{0}}{2\pi}\,e^{-ip_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,N(p_{0},{\bm{p}}).

(58)

Although Eq. (
55
) involves colored noise, it can be solved efficiently by iteration
on a discrete momentum lattice using fast Fourier transformation to switch back and forth between
coordinate space and momentum space to compute the nonlinear term

F_{\sigma}(t,{\bm{p}})

Cassol-Seewald et al. 2008
. As our aim is to get analytic understanding
as much as possible, we leave for a future publication the study
of numerical solutions of Eq. (
55
). But we need to simplify further the
analysis to proceed
with an analytical treatment. A common simplification restricts the dynamics to a constant soft-mode
frequency
E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}})\approx\sqrt{{\bm{p}}^{2}+m^{2}_{\sigma}}\approx m_{\sigma}

Rischke 1998
;
Nahrgang et al. 2012
;
Herold et al. 2013
;
Bluhm et al. 2019
. We adopt another simplification, one motivated by the
dimensional reduction brought out by the magnetic field: we restrict the dynamics to the plane
orthogonal to the magnetic field, namely
\sigma(t,{\bm{p}})=\sigma(t,p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})\rightarrow\sigma(t,p_{x},p_{y},p_{z}=0)\equiv\sigma(t,{\bm{p}}_{\perp})
. Therefore, we need to compute the kernel
M_{f}({\bm{p}})
for

{\bm{p}}=({\bm{p}}_{\perp},p_{z}=0)
. We use Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
) into Eq. (
47
),
take the traces over Dirac and color indices and integrate over the transverse momentum
{\bm{q}}_{\perp}
,
to obtain for
M_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp},p_{z}=0)\equiv M_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})

the result:

\displaystyle M_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2N_{c}}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi|q_{f}|B}{2}\right)e^{-{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}/2|q_{f}B|}\;I_{M}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})),

(59)

where
I_{M}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp}))
is the integral

\displaystyle I_{M}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dq_{z}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dq_{0}\;\Bigl\{\delta((p+q)^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\delta(q^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})\left[q_{0}\left(p_{0}+q_{0}\right)-q_{z}\left(p_{z}+q_{z}\right)+m^{2}_{q}\right]

(60)

\displaystyle\times\,\Bigl(\left[\theta(q_{0})-n_{F}(q_{0})\right]\left[\theta(-p_{0}-q_{0})-n_{F}(p_{0}+q_{0})\right]

\displaystyle-\left[\theta(-q_{0})-n_{F}(q_{0})\right]\left[\theta(p_{0}+q_{0})-n_{F}(p_{0}+q_{0})\right]\Bigr)\Bigr\}_{p_{0}=E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}.

Here and in the following we suppress the explicit reference to the fact that
p_{z}=0

in
{\bm{p}}
-dependent
functions. We first use the delta function
\delta(q^{2}_{\parallel}-m^{2}_{q})=\delta(q^{2}_{0}-q^{2}_{z}-m^{2}_{q})
to
integrate over
q_{0}
, then use the other delta function to integrate over
q_{z}
to obtain:

\displaystyle I_{M}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1-2n_{F}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)\right]\left(\frac{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}{4E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}\right)\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dq_{z}\;\delta(E_{q}(q_{z})-E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1-2n_{F}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)\right]\left(\frac{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}{4E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}\right)\frac{2E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}{\sqrt{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1-2n_{F}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)\right]\frac{1}{2E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}\sqrt{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}.

Therefore:

M_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})=\frac{N_{c}}{\pi}\left(|q_{f}|B\right)\left[1-2n_{F}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)\right]\frac{1}{2E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}\sqrt{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}\;e^{-{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}/2|q_{f}B|}.

(62)

From this, one obtains for the momentum-dependent noise coefficient
\eta({\bm{p}}_{\perp})
:

\eta({\bm{p}}_{\perp})=g^{2}\frac{N_{c}}{4\pi}\left[1-2n_{F}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)\right]\frac{1}{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}\sqrt{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}\sum_{f=u,d}|q_{f}B|\;e^{-{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}/2|q_{f}B|}.

(63)

Next, we compute the noise kernel
N(x,y)
with the same simplifications used for
M(x)
.
We use Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
) into Eq. (
25
), take the traces
over Dirac and color indices, and integrate over the transverse momentum
{\bm{q}}_{\perp}

to obtain:

\displaystyle N_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}\frac{2N_{c}}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi|q_{f}|B}{2}\right)e^{-{\bm{p}}^{2}_{\perp}/2|q_{f}B|}\;I_{N}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})),

(64)

with

I_{N}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp}))=-\left[1-2n_{F}(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2)\right]\coth(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2T)\frac{1}{2E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})}\sqrt{E^{2}_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})-4m^{2}_{q}}.

(65)

Taking into account Eq. (
62
), one can write:

N_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})=\frac{1}{2}\,g^{2}\coth(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2T)\,M_{f}({\bm{p}}_{\perp}).

(66)

Therefore, after summing over flavor and using the result in Eq. (
63
), one can
write for the momentum space noise kernel
N({\bm{p}}_{\perp})
:

N({\bm{p}}_{\perp})=\eta({\bm{p}}_{\perp})\,E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})\,\coth(E_{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{\perp})/2T).

(67)

Finally, replacing this result into Eq. (
58
), the
p_{0}
integration leads to the Dirac
delta
\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and
\xi_{\sigma}
becomes a white noise field.

This concludes the derivation of the main ingredients entering the Langevin equation:
\rho_{s}
,

D(x)

Continuing with the aim of gaining analytic understanding, we consider
\sigma
’s dynamics
in a temperature quench scenario. Before continuing, we spell out the required
simplifications here. We neglect expansion of the system. Expansion is perhaps
the most relevant trait of a heavy-ion collision that needs to be taken into account when
simulating a real laboratory event. But such a simulation is out of the scope of this work.
We also assume a constant magnetic field in the course of the condensate evolution. As such,
we do not consider the complex magnetohydrodynamics that governs the magnetic field in the
medium expansion course. The magnetic field weakens as the system expands, but it also
induces electric currents that can sustain a magnetic field of sizeable strength while
the system exists
Tuchin 2013
;
Gursoy et al. 2014
;
Tuchin 2016
. This feature, to
some extent, justifies the assumption of a constant field. Finally, we do no consider
reheating, i.e. energy transfer between the condensate and the background. Reheating
changes the local temperature of the background and, as for zero magnetic fields,
can effect the dynamics
Nahrgang et al. 2012
. We reserve for a separate study the
inclusion of the neglected effects.

In a quench scenario, a sudden drop in the temperature
drives the system out of a high temperature phase, in which
\sigma\approx 0
, and forces
the system to evolve to a lower temperature phase in which
\sigma\neq 0
. One gets insight
on how a nonzero
B
impacts such a quench by examining the time scale controlling the short-time
dynamics. That time scale, which we denote by
\tau_{s}
, determines how quickly the system
leaves the initial state. It depends, of course, on
\eta
, and also on the nature of
the lower temperature phase, which the magnetic field affects as well. This interplay
between
\eta
and the nature of the low temperature phase in a quench scenario is well
known
Goldenfeld 1992
;
Onuki 2002
. We take as
lower temperature phase
one
around the
B=0
pseudocritical temperature; that is, at
t=0
the system is brought
to one of the local maxima of the
V_{\rm eff}
in Fig.
1
.

\sigma\approx 0

7
Conclusions and perspectives

We studied the impact of a strong magnetic field on the chiral quark condensate dynamics.
We built on the semiclassical framework developed for zero magnetic field developed
in Ref.
Nahrgang et al. 2011
. That framework bases the dynamics on a mean field
Langevin equation derived from a microscopic chiral quark model. We extended that
Langevin equation to include the effects of a magnetic field. The Langevin
equation we derived features damping and noise modified by the magnetic field.
Damping and noise reflect the condensate’s interactions with an
effective magnetized quark background in local thermal equilibrium. The background
results from integrating out quarks from a mean-field effective action defined by
the linear sigma model. To integrate out quarks, we used the closed time path
formalism of nonequilibrium quantum field theory. We obtained numerical results using
values of magnetic field strengths and space-time scales related to high-energy
heavy-ion collision experiments. We presented results for the short-time condensate
dynamics under temperature quenches. The quenches we used were from a high temperature,
for which the condensate is zero, to lower temperatures close to the zero magnetic field
crossover temperature,
T\sim 150
MeV. The results we showed revealed that the magnetic
field changes the dissipation pattern as compared to the zero magnetic field case,
retarding condensate’s short-time evolution substantially, a feature that can impact
hadron formation at the QCD transition.

Our study was a first incursion into a complex many-body problem. Our primary aim in
this study was to get insight into how a strong magnetic field affects
condensate dynamics. We simplified the analysis, and sought an analytical
understanding whenever possible. We also omitted physical effects peculiar to a
heavy-ion collision. As such, before one can draw conclusions on phenomenological
consequences in a realist heavy-ion setting, one needs to extend the theoretical
framework to include the omitted features. These include pions, expansion,
reheating, magnetohydrodynamics modes, and coupling to other order parameters.
As in the case of zero magnetic field
Nahrgang et al. 2011
, the formalism
developed here is flexible enough to tackle the more complex problem.
Another extension of our study is to incorporate a confinement mechanism.
A possibility is to couple a color dielectric field
to the chiral
\sigma
and
{\bm{\pi}}
fields of the LSMq,
a possibility very much explored in the context of bag and soliton
models
Birse 1990
. Such models can be extended to include
explicit gluon degrees of freedom to realize dynamical chiral symmetry breaking
and describe asymptotic freedom
Krein et al. 1988
;
Krein et al. 1991
.

The framework developed in this paper can be adapted to study magnetic field effects
on the QCD phase transition in the early universe and in the interior of magnetized
compact stars (magnetars). Several mechanisms of strong magnetic field
generation in the early universe have been suggested
Vachaspati 1991
;
Grasso and Rubinstein 2001
;
a very recent, connected with the QCD phase transition, involves the collapse
of domain walls related to the confinement order parameter
Atreya and Sanyal 2018
.
A marked difference between the early universe and
heavy-ion collision settings concerns the rate of change of the temperature
d\ln T/dt

during expansion of the system. In the early universe, this rate is given by Hubble
constant
H\sim 10^{-18}~s^{-1}
, which is much slower than that in a heavy-ion collision. Therefore, the primordial chiral condensate evolves in a slowly
changing effective potential as the system expands. Such an evolution characterizes an
annealing scenario for the phase change, rather than of a quench, but it can be studied
equally well with the Langevin equation framework of the present paper
Gavin and Muller 1994
.
Regarding magnetars, the inner-core magnetic field can reach strengths varying
between
eB\simeq m^{2}_{\pi}
and
eB\simeq 50m^{2}_{\pi}

Ferrer and Hackebill 2019
.
In this setting, the temperatures are very low, lower than 50 MeV, and the phase conversion is driven by high
baryon density. An issue of interest relates to the time scales associated with the
phase conversion during the early stages of the magnetar formation after
a core-collapsing supernova process. In this case, the
formalism used in this paper needs to be extended to nonzero baryon density
Kroff and Fraga 2015
.
