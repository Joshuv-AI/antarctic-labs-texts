Fidelity and Overlap of Neural Quantum States: Error Bounds on the Monte Carlo Estimator
Tomasz Szołdra

Abstract
Overlap between two neural quantum states can be computed through Monte Carlo sampling by evaluating the unnormalized probability amplitudes on a subset of basis configurations. Due to the presence of probability amplitude ratios in the estimator, which are possibly unbounded, convergence of this quantity is not immediately obvious. Our work provides a derivation of analytical error bounds on the overlap in the Monte Carlo calculations as a function of their fidelity and the number of samples. Special case of normalized autoregressive neural quantum states is analyzed separately.



I
Introduction

Numerical simulation of many-body quantum systems on classical hardware poses a challenge due to high dimensionality of the quantum Hilbert space, growing exponentially with the system size. Exact diagonalization of many-body problems in the full Hilbert space can only be done for relatively small systems of several degrees of freedom due to memory and computational time limitations
Sandvik et al. 2010
. Multiple approaches to solve these problems in an approximate way have been developed to date. Among them are Quantum Monte Carlo (
QMC
) methods
Hirsch et al. 1982
;
Ceperley and Alder 1986
;
Troyer and Wiese 2005
and tensor networks (
TN
)
White 1992
(for reviews see
Schollwöck 2011
;
Orús 2014
), which have proven successful for certain classes of low-dimensional systems with short-range entanglement. Nevertheless, TN have limitations regarding the representable space of states and a high computational cost for tensor contractions.

A possible way of solving the many-body problem is the use of Neural Quantum States (
NQS
), a variational neural network Ansatz for a quantum wavefunction, first introduced in
Carleo and Troyer 2017
. The neural network maps configurations into complex probability amplitudes. Due to nonlinearities in the activation functions in the network this Ansatz can represent a larger class of states than tensor networks
Sharir et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2023
with fewer parameters (see, however,
Lin and Pollmann 2022
;
Passetti et al. 2023
and in particular
Denis et al. 2023
). Parameters of the network are optimized variationally by Monte Carlo (
MC
) sampling of the configurations (see e.g.
Dawid et al. 2022
for a pedagogical introduction). The difficulty now shifts towards costly MC sampling and navigation on the complicated optimization landscape of parameters, rather than mere expressibility of the Ansatz. Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that NQS can be used to find ground states of 1- and 2-dimensional spin systems
Sharir et al. 2020
;
Hibat-Allah et al. 2020
, perform unitary time evolution
Carleo and Troyer 2017
;
Carleo et al. 2017
;
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
, learn physical wavefunction from experimental data
Torlai et al. 2018
or simulate open systems in contact with reservoir
Yoshioka and Hamazaki 2019
;
Carrasquilla et al. 2019
;
Vicentini et al. 2019
;
Hartmann and Carleo 2019
;
Luo et al. 2021
;
Reh et al. 2021
.

Quantum fidelity and overlap are fundamental quantities used for measuring the distance between quantum states. They can be used e.g. to iteratively find excited states starting from the groundstate (see
Choo et al. 2018
for excited state calculation in NQS), map out phase diagrams
Zhou et al. 2008
;
Damski 2015
, or to verify how far the quantum state prepared in the lab is from the desired one
Flammia and Liu 2011
. In the context of approximate numerical Ansätze, such as NQS, calculation of fidelity between the approximate and exact state, although accessible only for small systems, gives a quantitative estimate on the quality of the Ansatz. It is also a useful tool in diagnosis of the unitary time evolution algorithms, as performed in
Hofmann et al. 2022
. Not all numerical methods of simulating many-body systems allow for a direct, efficient calculation of fidelity - see eg. algorithm introduced in
Zhou et al. 2008
for 2-dimensional tensor networks.

Although the calculation of fidelities has already been successfully performed with NQS
Choo et al. 2018
;
Hofmann et al. 2022
;
Sinibaldi et al. 2023
in the standard Monte Carlo sampling fashion, and it has recently been rigorously proven it
can
be computed with a finite number of samples
Havlicek 2023
, in this paper we perform a detailed study of the error of such an estimate. Our calculations yield simple, analytical error bounds on both fidelity and overlap calculated with NQS via Monte Carlo sampling, which in some settings are tighter than those of Ref.
Havlicek 2023
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
we establish the notation and pedagogically revise how one calculates the overlap and fidelity for neural quantum states through Monte Carlo sampling. Section
III
is devoted to the derivation of the error bounds for both of these quantities. We separately treat the case of normalized states, eg. autoregressive neural networks (
ARNN
), for which sampling from only a single network is required, and the general case of unnormalized probability amplitudes. In Section
IV
we numerically benchmark the error bounds for a physical system. We conclude in Section
V
.

II
Overlap and fidelity

Figure 1:
Scheme of the fidelity and overlap measurement algorithm. If both states are normalized, e.g., they are represented by an autoregressive Ansatz, sampling from only one of the NQS becomes sufficient, see text.

Assume that we are given two NQSs, parametrized by variational parameters
\theta,\gamma
, respectively, that encode approximations of two quantum states

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\frac{\psi_{\theta}(s)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}}\ket{s},\quad\mathcal{N}_{\psi}\equiv\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi},

(1)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\frac{\phi_{\gamma}(s)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}}\ket{s},\quad\mathcal{N}_{\phi}\equiv\innerproduct{\phi}{\phi},

(2)

where
\{\ket{s}\}_{s}
are basis vectors of the Hilbert space with
\innerproduct{s}{s'}=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}
. For example, for
L
spins-1/2,
s=s_{1}\dots s_{L}
with
s_{i}=\uparrow,\downarrow
and the number of basis elements is
2^{L}
. We consider a general case of NQS with
\mathcal{N}_{\psi},\mathcal{N}_{\phi}
not necessarily equal to
1
. Some of our results obtained for
\mathcal{N}_{\psi}=\mathcal{N}_{\phi}=1
, e.g. for ARNN, are analyzed separately.
We calculate the overlap

\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}=\sum_{s}\frac{\psi_{\theta}(s)\phi^{*}_{\gamma}(s)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}}

(3)

and fidelity
\mathcal{F}=\left|\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}\right|^{2}
. As we are dealing with NQS, we can (i) evaluate amplitudes
\psi_{\theta}(s)
,
\phi_{\gamma}(s)
efficiently given configuration
s
, (ii) generate
n
samples of configurations
\{s^{(1)}\dots s^{(n)}\}
coming from probability distributions
\left|\psi_{\theta}(s)\right|^{2}/\mathcal{N}_{\psi}
and
\left|\phi_{\gamma}(s)\right|^{2}/\mathcal{N}_{\phi}
.

Although terms (
3
) are accessible, naive evaluation of the network on all basis elements is intractable for large systems due to the dimension of the Hilbert space growing exponentially with the system size. The normalization factors
\mathcal{N}_{\psi},\mathcal{N}_{\phi}
also require a summation of exponentially many terms. However, one can use a standard trick for the MC calculation of operator expectation values with NQS
Carleo and Troyer 2017
, i.e., rewrite Eq. (
3
) as:

\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}}\sum_{s}\frac{\left|\phi_{\gamma}(s)\right|^{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}Z(s)

(4)

where
Z(s)=\psi_{\theta}(s)/\phi_{\gamma}(s)
. Then
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}}\mathbb{E}\left(Z(s)\right)
, with configurations
s
drawn from the probability distribution
\left|\phi_{\gamma}(s)\right|^{2}/\mathcal{N}_{\phi}
.
To estimate
\mathbb{E}\left(Z\right)
one generates
n_{1}
samples
\{s^{(1)},\dots s^{(n_{1})}\}
according to this probability distribution and constructs an empirical MC estimator

Y_{1}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}Z(s^{(i)}).

(5)

It is clear that
\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(Z\right)
.

If both states are normalized with
\mathcal{N}_{\psi}=\mathcal{N}_{\phi}=1
, e.g. they are represented by an autoregressive Ansatz, one can immediately read off the overlap
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)
.

However, for unnormalized states, the preceding factor of
\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}/\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}
remains unknown.
This issue can be circumvented by computing

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}}\sum_{s}\frac{\left|\psi_{\theta}(s)\right|^{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}W(s),

(6)

where
W(s)=\phi_{\gamma}(s)/\psi_{\theta}(s)
and we used the same trick as in Eq. (
4
) but for
\psi_{\theta}(s)
.
In the similar way as before, we can generate
n_{2}
samples
\{s^{\prime(1)},\dots s^{\prime(n_{2})}\}
but now from
from
\left|\psi_{\theta}(s)\right|^{2}/\mathcal{N}_{\psi}
and define an estimator

Y_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}W(s^{\prime(i)}).

(7)

Of course,
\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(W\right)
, and fidelity reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{2}\right),

i.e., normalization factors cancel out, while
\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{2}\right)
as variables
Y_{1}
,
Y_{2}
are independent because they come from two different random processes. Using this result for the overlap, we can write

\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}=\sqrt{\left|\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{2}\right)\right|}e^{i\alpha},\quad\alpha=\arg\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right).

(9)

Note that the definition of the complex phase
\alpha
involves only
Y_{1}
because a ”symmetric” formula
{\alpha=\left(\arg\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)-\arg\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}\right)\right)/2}
would be defined up to mod
\pi
.

III
Error bounds

We provide an upper bound on the number of samples
n_{1},n_{2}
(or only
n_{1}
for the case of normalized states) required to estimate
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
and
\mathcal{F}
with a fixed additive error and failure probability
\delta
, see Fig.
2
. The bounds are calculated using the framework first employed in
Flammia and Liu 2011
in the context of measuring fidelities in quantum circuits through Pauli measurements.

III.1
Normalized states

For simplicity, we first consider the case of normalized states
\mathcal{N}_{\psi}=\mathcal{N}_{\phi}=1
, e.g. encoded using ARNN architectures, which generate configurations site-by-site by implementing conditional probabilities
P(s_{i}|s_{i-1},\dots s_{1})

Wu et al. 2019
,

P(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{L})=\Pi_{i=1}^{L}P(s_{i}|s_{i-1},\dots s_{1}).

(10)

We use the Chebyshev’s inequality

\Pr\left(\left|Y_{1}-\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)\right|\geq\sigma k\right)\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}},\quad\sigma=\sqrt{\text{Var}~Y_{1}},

(11)

which holds for any probability distribution of the random variable
Y_{1}
with finite first and second moments. It states that the probability of a single realization of
Y_{1}
achieving a value of
k
standard deviations away from the mean is smaller than
1/k^{2}
. In our case,
{\text{Var}~Y_{1}=(\text{Var}~Z)/n_{1}}
, see Eq. (
5
), while

\displaystyle\text{Var}~Z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(ZZ^{*}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(Z\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{*}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\left|\phi_{\gamma}(s)\right|^{2}\frac{\left|\psi_{\theta}(s)\right|^{2}}{\left|\phi_{\gamma}(s)\right|^{2}}-\mathcal{F}=1-\mathcal{F},

where we used
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}=\mathbb{E}\left(Z\right)
and normalization of
\ket{\psi}
. Setting
k=1/\sqrt{\delta}
we obtain

\Pr\left(\left|Y_{1}-\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\delta,

(13)

where

\varepsilon=\sqrt{\frac{1-\mathcal{F}}{n_{1}\delta}}.

(14)

That is, without any prior knowledge about the fidelity
\mathcal{F}
, assuming the worst-case scenario of orthogonal states with
\mathcal{F}=0
, we need at least
n_{1}=1/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta)
samples from
\left|\phi_{\gamma}(s)\right|^{2}
in order for the estimate
Y_{1}
to fall within a complex disk of radius
\varepsilon
around the true value
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
with probability
1-\delta
, see Fig.
2
a. Notice that the required number of samples is independent of any properties of the state, system size and any system details such as local Hilbert space and spatial dimension.
We also note that although the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that the distribution of
Y_{1}
asymptotically converges to the normal distribution on the complex plane
{N\left(\mu=\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi},~\sigma=\sqrt{(1-\mathcal{F})/n_{1}}\right)}
for
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
, the rate of convergence with
n_{1}
depends on the details of the probability distribution of
Z(s^{(i)})
and should be computed separately for each pair of quantum states
\ket{\psi}
,
\ket{\phi}
. On the contrary, the Chebyshev’s inequality holds without the assumption of the distribution of
Y_{1}
.

Error bounds of
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
in case
\mathcal{N}_{\psi}=\mathcal{N}_{\phi}=1
, eg. for autoregressive NQS. a) Star represents the true value of
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
which can be calculated if all amplitudes of both wave functions
\psi_{\theta}(s)
,
\phi_{\gamma}(s)
are known. Estimates
Y_{1}
, represented by dots, fall into the circle of radius
\varepsilon
around
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
with probability
1-\delta
.

The fidelity could in principle be estimated by
{\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{*}\right)}
. We were unable to provide bounds for its variance as this expression contains terms
\mathbb{E}\left((ZZ^{*})^{2}\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left(ZZ^{*}Z\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{*}\right)
which do not simplify as in Eq. (
12
). On the other hand, the interval in which we expect to find the estimate of
\mathcal{F}
follows directly from the error in overlap,

\Pr\left(\left||Y_{1}|^{2}-\mathcal{F}\right|\geq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{\mathcal{F}}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\leq\delta,

(15)

where
\varepsilon
was previously defined in Eq. (
13
). This is obtained from

\displaystyle||Y_{1}|^{2}-\mathcal{F}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle||(\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}+e^{i\varphi}\varepsilon)|^{2}-\mathcal{F}|

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon|2\real\left(\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}e^{-i\varphi}\right)+\varepsilon|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon(2\sqrt{\mathcal{F}}+\varepsilon)

where we used
-\sqrt{\mathcal{F}}\leq\real\left(\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}e^{-i\varphi}\right)\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{F}}
and
e^{-i\varphi}
is an arbitrary complex phase.

For large
n_{1}\delta\gg 1
the fidelity error attains a maximum at
\mathcal{F}=0.5
. Minima of the error occur at
\mathcal{F}=0
and
\mathcal{F}=1
. At
\mathcal{F}=1
the error reaches
0
, while for
\mathcal{F}=0
it converges to
1/(n_{1}\delta)
.

III.2
General case

The same technique is directly generalized to unnormalized states.
First, we derive an error bound for the MC estimate
Y_{1}Y_{2}
of fidelity
\mathcal{F}
through Chebyshev’s inequality (
11
). Using the independence of the variables
Y_{1}
,
Y_{2}
, and Eq. (
12
), we find that

\text{Var}~Y_{1}Y_{2}=\mathcal{F}(1-\mathcal{F})\frac{n_{1}+n_{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}+\frac{(1-\mathcal{F})^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}.

(17)

Full derivation is presented in the Appendix
A
.

Given the total number of samples
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
, choosing equal sampling
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
minimizes the variance. Plugging
k=1/\sqrt{\delta}
into the Chebyshev’s inequality for
Y_{1}Y_{2}
in Eq. (
11
), and using the variance formula (
17
), for the optimal choice of
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
samples we obtain

\Pr\left(\left|Y_{1}Y_{2}-\mathcal{F}\right|\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\leq\delta,

(18)

where

\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{F}(1-\mathcal{F})+\frac{(1-\mathcal{F})^{2}}{n}}{n\delta}}.

(19)

For a large number of samples
n\gg 1
, the minimal error
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is obtained for
\mathcal{F}=0
and
\mathcal{F}=1
, while
{\mathcal{F}=1/2}
maximizes the error. Let us compare this result to the case of single-state sampling with the same number
n_{1}=n
of samples.

Taylor expansion of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
as a function of
1/n
around zero gives

\varepsilon^{\prime}\approx 2\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{F}(1-\mathcal{F})}{n\delta}}+\frac{(1-\mathcal{F})^{2}}{n^{2}\delta}.

(20)

This means that the leading term is the same as in Eq. (
15
), while a higher-order correction to the error is smaller here by a factor of
\sim n/(1-\mathcal{F})
. However, in practice one always uses
n\gg 1
so there are no advantages of using one or the other method, irrespective of the fidelity value
\mathcal{F}
.

Our error bound can be compared to that of Ref.
Havlicek 2023
, where an robust estimator based on the calculation of the median of multiple estimates of means of
Y_{1}Y_{2}
is considered. With a total of
n
samples, i.e.
n
queries of the amplitude ratios, the fidelity is estimated up to
\varepsilon_{\text{med}}
additive error with failure probability
\delta=\exp\left(-n\varepsilon_{\text{med}}^{2}/64\right)
. It is straightforward to show that at the same failure probability
\delta
, our error bound given by Eq. (
19
) is smaller,
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon_{\text{med}}
, if the condition

\mathcal{F}(1-\mathcal{F})+\frac{(1-\mathcal{F})^{2}}{n}<-16~\delta\ln{\delta}

(21)

is met. In the limit of large
n\gg 1
, this is always true if
0.00263\lessapprox\delta\lessapprox 0.984)
since the l.h.s. is always less or equal
0.25
. The inequality is also satisfied in the interesting cases when states are either close to each other,
\mathcal{F}\approx 1
, for any
n
, or when they are orthogonal,
\mathcal{F}=0
, for
n
dependent on the chosen
\delta
.

Having the fidelity interval, we can read off the error bounds for the overlap defined by Eq. (
9
). Since, with probability
1-\delta
,
|Y_{1}Y_{2}|
lies in the interval
[\mathcal{F}-\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathcal{F}+\varepsilon^{\prime}]
, the estimator
\sqrt{|Y_{1}Y_{2}|}
for
\left|\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}\right|
lies in the interval
[\min(0,\sqrt{\mathcal{F}-\varepsilon^{\prime}}),\max(1,\sqrt{\mathcal{F}+\varepsilon^{\prime}}]
with the same probability.

The error in the complex phase
\alpha
is due to the uncertainty of the phase in
Y_{1}
. With probability
1-\delta
,
Y_{1}
lies in a disk of radius
r=\varepsilon^{\prime}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}/\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}
with a center at
\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}/\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
. The disk spreads over complex angles
[\alpha-\Delta\alpha,\alpha+\Delta\alpha]
, and simple trigonometry gives

\displaystyle\Delta\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arcsin\frac{r}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}/\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}\left|\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}\right|}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arcsin\frac{2\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(1-\mathcal{F})+\frac{(1-\mathcal{F})^{2}}{n}}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}n\delta}}.

(If the argument under
\arcsin
is greater than
1
, we separately define the angle
\Delta\alpha
as
\pi
, so that
\alpha
remains undefined). Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the MC estimator of
\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
lies in the region presented in Fig.
3
.

IV
Numerical benchmarks

IV.1
Method

We numerically validate the analytical error bounds in the following way. To simulate a generic, entangled state
\ket{\psi(0)}
we consider a 1-dimensional chain of
L
spins-1/2 and a neural network variational Ansatz with random weights (see eg.
Sun et al. 2022
for the properties of restricted Boltzmann machine NQS with Gaussian weights). In order to measure the error as a function of fidelity, we scan a range of fidelities by evolving this state in time to
\ket{\psi(t)}=\exp(-i H t)\ket{\psi(0)}
with the Hamiltonian of the transverse field Ising model,

H=-\sum_{i=1}^{L}J\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}+g\sigma_{i}^{x},

(23)

with periodic boundary conditions
{\sigma_{L+1}^{z}\equiv\sigma_{1}^{z}}
,
{J=1}
and
{g=-0.5}
. Time evolution up to time
t=2
is performed using the time-dependent Variational Monte Carlo (tVMC) method
Carleo and Troyer 2017
;
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
;
Gutiérrez and Mendl 2022
. Our codes are available at
github.com/tszoldra/jVMC_overlap
. We use the TDVP
Haegeman et al. 2011
algorithm, implemented in jVMC 1.2.4
Schmitt and Reh 2022a
;
Schmitt and Reh 2022b
framework, with Euler integrator with timestep
0.001
, regularization cutoff in the pseudoinverse of the quantum Fisher matrix
\epsilon_{\text{pinv}}=10^{-6}
and
8192
MC samples for each timestep (see
Schmitt and Reh 2022a
for details on regularization techniques). We do not care how well the variational Ansatz approximates the actual time-evolved physical wave function as we are only interested in the decrease of fidelity between the time-evolved and the initial state.

The error of the overlap estimation is measured by comparing the Monte Carlo estimate with the exact summation over all
2^{L}
basis states as in Eq. (
3
) which could be done for the moderate system sizes
L\leq 32
. Cases of autoregressive and unnormalized neural networks are analyzed separately below. All results are produced using
n=65536
samples (in ARNN case,
n_{1}=n
, in general case
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
) and are averaged over
10
random initial states (random weights of the neural network) and
100
independent MC calculations of the overlap or fidelity. Error bars denote an estimate of one standard deviation for a single sample, i.e., a deviation one may expect when computing a single estimate of the overlap or fidelity using
n
samples in total.

Our ARNN Ansatz is a densely connected recurrent neural network
Hibat-Allah et al. 2020
with hidden size
16
and depth
4
, yielding
1090
real variational parameters in total, and an exponential linear unit (ELU)
Clevert et al. 2016
activation function. Random initial weights come from a uniform distribution on the interval
[0,1]
, rescaled by the square root of the average number of inputs and outputs in a given layer. Due to the autoregressive property, MC sampling of spin configurations from this network is exact, i.e. MC samples are perfectly uncorrelated. Fig.
4
shows exemplary time evolutions of an exact overlap
\innerproduct{\psi(t)}{\psi(0)}
on the complex plane.

. Random initial weights have real and complex parts uniformly distributed on the interval

. We sample configurations from this NQS using Markov chain Monte Carlo Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with random spin flip as a step proposal,
L
Monte Carlo steps for a single sweep, and
25
sweeps for an initial ”burn-in”/thermalization.

V
Summary

In this Article we studied the convergence properties of the Monte Carlo estimate of the quantum overlap and fidelity. By analytically computing the variance of the MC estimator and utilizing the Chebyshev’s inequality, we were able to show that the error is explicitly given only by a total number of Monte Carlo samples and fidelity between the quantum states in consideration. We have verified our bounds on the error by comparing the resulting fidelity with the full summation over the Hilbert space up to dimension
2^{32}\approx 4.3\cdot 10^{9}
.

In the case of autoregressive states, only one of the NQS can be sampled from for the overlap estimation. This feature can be used for example if one wants to compute fidelity between an autoregressive NQS and a Matrix Product State and sample only the former to possibly avoid expensive tensor network contractions (see also
Sandvik and Vidal 2007
for Monte Carlo sampling of tensor networks).

We identified two regimes in which the error of fidelity estimate goes down. A small error for orthogonal states around
\mathcal{F}\approx 0
may explain the remarkable efficiency of the excited states search algorithm
Choo et al. 2018
. A small error at
\mathcal{F}\approx 1
is also a good sign for a class of algorithms that are supposed to variationally optimize states with low infidelity.

Note
In the late stage of preparation of this manuscript, I became aware of recently published Ref.
Sinibaldi et al. 2023
. Authors estimate the infidelity of NQS using a technique of Control Variates which gives a lower relative MC error than a standard estimate when operating around
\mathcal{F}\approx 1
. Better convergence of this estimate is used to construct an efficient
projected
tVMC unitary time evolution algorithm. Authors demonstrate that the natural measure of the MC estimate quality is the signal-to-noise ratio, defined as the quantity of interest in units of its standard deviation. It is shown that a direct MC estimate of infidelity
1-\mathcal{F}
close to
0
through an algorithm described here would require a diverging number of
n\sim 1/(1-\mathcal{F})
samples in order to conserve the signal-to-noise ratio and have a sufficient accuracy for performing the variational minimization. Authors also derive a variance formula which is a special case of Eq. (
17
) in the limit
\mathcal{F}\rightarrow 1
,
n\gg 1
.

Appendix A
Calculation of the fidelity variance

Variance of fidelity estimate
Y_{1}Y_{2}
is defined as

\text{Var}~Y_{1}Y_{2}=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{2}Y_{1}^{*}Y_{2}^{*}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}^{*}Y_{2}^{*}\right).

(24)

Using the independence of random variables
Y_{1},Y_{2}
, this boils down to

\displaystyle\text{Var}~Y_{1}Y_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{*}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}Y_{2}^{*}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}^{*}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}^{*}\right).

Notice that

\text{Var}~Y_{1}=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{*}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}^{*}\right)=\frac{\text{Var}~Z}{n_{1}}

(27)

and for unnormalized states

\text{Var}~Z=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}(1-\mathcal{F}),

(28)

see Eq. (
12
) in the main text. Since
\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(Z\right)=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}\innerproduct{\phi}{\psi}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{1}^{*}\right)+\frac{\text{Var}~Z}{n_{1}}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}\left(\mathcal{F}+\frac{1-\mathcal{F}}{n_{1}}\right).

Similarly,

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{2}Y_{2}^{*}\right)=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\phi}}{\mathcal{N}_{\psi}}\left(\mathcal{F}+\frac{1-\mathcal{F}}{n_{2}}\right).

(30)

Thus,

\displaystyle\text{Var}~Y_{1}Y_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{F}+\frac{1-\mathcal{F}}{n_{1}}\right)\left(\mathcal{F}+\frac{1-\mathcal{F}}{n_{2}}\right)\!-\!\mathcal{F}^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(1-\mathcal{F})\frac{n_{1}+n_{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}+(1-\mathcal{F})^{2}\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}.

Both terms in the variance are mimimized by choosing an equal number of samples
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
.
