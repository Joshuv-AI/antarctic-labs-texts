Robust Generative Learning with Lipschitz-Regularized α \alpha -Divergences Allows Minimal Assumptions on Target Distributions
Ziyu Chen, Hyemin Gu, Markos A. Katsoulakis, Luc Rey-Bellet, Wei Zhu

Abstract
This paper demonstrates the robustness of Lipschitz-regularized α \alpha -divergences as objective functionals in generative modeling, showing they enable stable learning across a wide range of target distributions with minimal assumptions. We establish that these divergences remain finite under a mild condition—that the source distribution has a finite first moment—regardless of the properties of the target distribution, making them adaptable to the structure of target distributions. Furthermore, we prove the existence and finiteness of their variational derivatives, which are essential for stable training of generative models such as GANs and gradient flows. For heavy-tailed targets, we derive necessary and sufficient conditions that connect data dimension, α \alpha , and tail behavior to divergence finiteness, that also provide insights into the selection of suitable α \alpha ’s. We also provide the first sample complexity bounds for empirical estimations of these divergences on unbounded domains. As a byproduct, we obtain the first sample complexity bounds for empirical estimations of these divergences and the Wasserstein-1 metric with group symmetry on unbounded domains. Numerical experiments confirm that generative models leveraging Lipschitz-regularized α \alpha -divergences can stably learn distributions in various challenging scenarios, including those with heavy tails or complex, low-dimensional, or fractal support, all without any prior knowledge of the structure of target distributions.



Key words:
probability divergences, Lipschitz regularization, generative modeling, heavy tails, manifolds, attractors

Introduction

In generative modeling, the goal is to create new samples that resemble those from an unknown data distribution by designing algorithms that minimize a probability divergence or metric between the generated distribution and the target distribution. However, the diverse characteristics of real-world data distributions—such as heavy tails, low-dimensional structures, manifold constraints, or fractal-like supports—introduce significant challenges in the training of generative models. These challenges are manifested as instabilities, reduced robustness, and a need for specialized architectures, as standard generative frameworks struggle to adapt to complex data structures. Addressing these issues is essential for developing models that are not only accurate but also robust across a wide range of scenarios for the target distribution.

Features such as heavy-tailed distributions arise in various fields, including extreme events in ocean waves
[
17
]
, floods
[
36
]
, social sciences
[
45
,
28
]
, human activities
[
30
,
56
]
, biology
[
31
]
, and computer science
[
48
]
. Learning to generate heavy-tailed distributions has been explored with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). However, GANs based on Integral Probability Metrics (IPMs), such as the Wasserstein-1 metric, may struggle to learn these distributions without additional tail estimation strategies
[
18
,
24
,
1
]
. This limitation arises because the Wasserstein-1 metric between two distributions becomes infinite when one lacks a finite first moment, and accurately estimating tail behavior often requires extensive data from that tail, which may be difficult to obtain. Consequently, capturing discrepancies between distributions with a metric that remains finite, is stable to compute, and is less sensitive to the need for extensive tail data is essential for stable and effective learning.

On the other hand, many empirical results suggest that real-world data, such as images, exhibit low-dimensional structures
[
46
]
.
While there are theoretical guarantees for GANs to learn distributions with low-dimensional support
[
29
,
23
]
, recent works on flow-based models, such as continuous normalizing flows (CNFs), neural ODEs, and score-based diffusion models, often rely on density assumptions
[
10
,
33
]
. These models can struggle to learn low-dimensional structures without additional regularization or specific architectures, such as autoencoders (see
Section

7
). This limitation arises because their performance is typically evaluated using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) or
f
-divergences, which require absolute continuity between probability measures. Thus, it is crucial to select a divergence that remains flexible and inherently compatible with the structure of the data distribution.

In this work, we demonstrate that the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergence, as proposed in
[
16
,
5
]
, is a suitable objective functional for generative modeling with minimal assumptions on the target distribution, denoted by
Q
from now on. First, we revisit the definition of the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergence between two distributions
P
and
Q
defined as:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\coloneqq\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right\},

(1)

where
\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the class of
L
-Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; see more details in
Section

3
.
In particular, we show that the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences are suitable for stably learning a broad range of distributions from three perspectives:

•

Finiteness.
The objective of generative modeling using (
1
) can be formulated as
\min_{\theta}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{\theta}\|Q)
, where
P_{\theta}
is the generated distribution parametrized by
\theta
and
Q
is the target distribution. Thus, the divergence needs to be finite. We prove that these divergences remain finite whenever the generated distribution has a finite first moment, with no assumptions necessary on the target distribution
Q
.
When both distributions have power-law-decay densities, we provide sufficient and necessary conditions for the divergences to be finite. Notably, the Lipschitz-regularized KL divergences require minimal assumptions on both the tails of the generated and the target distributions.

•

Existence of variational derivatives.
To find the optimal parameter
\theta
in the optimization
\min_{\theta}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{\theta}\|Q)
, one often uses gradient-based algorithms. Formally, the gradient of
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{\theta}\|Q)
in terms of
\theta
can be evaluated as

\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{\theta}\|Q)=\int\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}(P_{\theta}(x))\cdot\nabla_{\theta}P_{\theta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(2)

therefore it is essential that the variational derivative
\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}
is well-defined. We prove that these divergences have well-defined variational derivatives for any target distribution
Q
, given
P
has a finite first moment. This is a crucial property for stable optimizations in generative learning and the associated gradient flows, and it illustrates that algorithms using this class of divergences can stably learn distributions without extensive prior knowledge of the tail behavior or density formulation of the target. In contrast, those using divergences without Lipschitz regularization generally can fail to learn (see
Section

7
).

•

Convergence of empirical estimations.
As distributions are only accessible through their finite samples, it is important to know how fast the divergence between their empirical measures converges to the true value of the divergence. We prove the first result of empirical estimations of this class of divergences on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and as a byproduct of the proof, we offer the first sample complexity bounds for empirical estimations of the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences and the Wasserstein-1 metric with group invariance on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with sub-Weibull assumptions. The key to these results is the Lipschitz regularization, without which we cannot prove such bounds.

Related work

Generative models for heavy-tailed distributions.

Although heavy-tailed distributions are common, there are few results to date in their generative modeling, primarily using GANs. For example,
[
54
]
generates heavy-tailed financial time series data by logarithmically transforming the data and then exponentiating the output, which does not produce truly heavy-tailed distributions. In a different approach, GANs are used for cosmological analysis
[
18
]
, sharing a similarity with Pareto GANs
[
24
]
in their use of a heavy-tailed latent variable. However, both papers require accurate estimations of the tail decay rate for each
marginal distribution. EV-GANs
[
1
]
use neural network approximations of the quantile function to encode the tail decay rate in an asymptotic sense, which is essentially also a tail estimation approach. We note that the focus of our work is to devise appropriate divergences as objective functionals for comparing and learning heavy-tailed distributions stably,
without
prior knowledge of the tail behavior.

Generative models for distributions with low-dimensional structures.
In
[
29
,
23
]
it is rigorously shown that IPM-GANs are able to learn distributions with low-dimensional support. There are some other generative models that learn high-dimensional distributions from the low-dimensional latent space provided by auto-encoders
[
53
,
34
]
, such as Bidirectional GANs
[
15
]
, Variational Auto-Encoders
[
27
]
and Generalized Denoising Auto-Encoders
[
4
]
. However, it is not clear if the low-dimensional latent space matches the low-dimensional structure of the data distribution and no convergence guarantees have been provided, and these results are largely empirical.

Empirical estimations of divergences.

[
47
,
39
,
50
]
estimate
f
-divergences using various assumptions and estimators, and
[
14
]
considers in particular the
\alpha
-divergences. However, these studies either make additional structural assumptions or consider light tails or without establishing a convergence rate of the estimation. Recently,
[
35
,
32
]
studied the convergence rate of entropic optimal transport and optimal transport with smooth costs. While our proof of the convergence rate of the empirical estimations of the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences is inspired by these works, the structure inherited from the
\alpha
-divergences in our study requires different, non-trivial treatment due to the nonlinear and asymmetric variational form, particularly as we consider even heavier tails. When the distributions are invariant to some group actions,
[
11
]
shows that empirical estimations of the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences and the Wasserstein-1 metric enjoy a faster convergence using symmetry-informed estimators on bounded domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and later
[
51

extends the result to closed Riemannian manifolds with group symmetry only for Sobolev-IPMs that are symmetric.

Lipschitz-regularized divergences.
The class of Lipschitz-regularized
f
-divergences was first proposed in
[
16
]
in the context of Lipschitz-regularized KL-divergences with its first variation formula, under the assumptions that both the source and the target distributions have finite first moments. Later,
[
5
]
generalized it to the class of Lipschitz-regularized
f
-divergences and observed that GANs optimizing Lipschitz-regularized
f
-divergences outperform those optimizing either the Wasserstein-1 metric or the
f
-divergences in learning heavy-tailed distributions.
In
[
21
]
, under the assumption that
Q
has a finite first moment, the gradient flows of the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences were introduced, using the variational derivatives to define a corresponding generative particle algorithm, outperforming other generative models in scarce and high-dimensional data regimes. In this paper, we provide the first theoretical explanations, not only for learning heavy-tailed distribution but also for learning distributions with manifold or fractal support, essentially making the generative modeling agnostic to the target data assumptions.

Background

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A map
D:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to[0,\infty]
is called a
divergence
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if

D(P,Q)=0\iff P=Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(3)

hence providing a notion of “distance” between probability measures. In particular, the class of
\alpha
-divergences
[
2
,
22
]
, denoted by
D_{\alpha}
, which is a sub-class of
f
-divergences
[
12
]
, is defined as

D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q},\quad\text{if}~P\ll Q,

(4)

where
f_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{x^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}
, with
\alpha>0
and
\alpha\neq 1
, and
P\ll Q
means
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
. When
P
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
, we write
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\infty
. In the limiting case as
\alpha\to 1
, one recovers the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence with
f_{\text{KL}}(x)=x\ln x
. The
\alpha
-divergence can be equivalently formulated in its dual form
[
41
,
5
]
as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right\},

(5)

where
\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the set of bounded measurable functions and
f_{\alpha}^{*}
is the convex conjugate (Legendre transform) of
f_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}^{*}(y)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{-1}(\alpha-1)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}y^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\mathbf{1}_{y>0}+\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)},&\alpha>1,\\
\infty\mathbf{1}_{y\geq 0}+\left(\alpha^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\left|y\right|^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}\right)\mathbf{1}_{y<0},&\alpha\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(6)

Compared to (
5
), the formulation of the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences in (
1
) can be viewed as imposing Lipschitz regularization on the space of test functions in the variational form of
\alpha
-divergences. In our work, we focus on the case when
\alpha>1
or
\alpha=1
(corresponding to the KL divergence). It has been proved in
[
5
]
that the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergence defined in (
1
) has an equivalent primal formulation

D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)=\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{D_{\alpha}(\eta\|Q)+L\cdot W_{1}(P,\eta)\},

(7)

where
W_{1}
is the Wasserstein-1 metric. One can easily verify that
D_{\alpha}^{L}
satisfies the conditions for being a divergence using (
7
). (
7
) can be viewed as the infimal convolution between the
\alpha
-divergence and the Wasserstein-1 metric. Though (
1
) is more often used in generative modeling as training objectives, its primal formulation is also theoretically very important.
For example, we have from (
7
) that

D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\leq\min\{D_{\alpha}(P\|Q),L\cdot W_{1}(P,Q)\}.

(8)

In practical tasks, such as in generative modeling, we estimate the divergence from finite samples of
P
and
Q
, where the absolute continuity assumption in (
4
) typically no longer holds. Meanwhile,
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
is always finite if
P
and
Q
are discrete measures of finitely many points with possibly different support since
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\leq L\cdot W_{1}(P,Q)<\infty
by (
8
).

The following example shows that we can have a strict inequality in (
8
).

Example 1
.

Let
P
and
Q
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}
such that

p(x)=(1+\delta)x^{-(2+\delta)}\mathbf{1}_{x\geq 1},\quad q(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{0\leq x<1}+\frac{1}{x^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{x\geq 2}.

Then neither
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)
nor
W_{1}(P,Q)
is finite for any
\alpha>1,\delta>0
, while
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)<\infty
.

Proof.

Since
P
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
, we have
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\infty
; applying the cumulative distribution function formula for the 1-dimensional Wasserstein-1 distance, it is straightforward to see
W_{1}(P,Q)=\infty
as
Q
does not have a finite first moment. Consider the formula (
7
) and in particular, we design the intermediate probability measure as

d\eta=(1+\delta)2^{1+\delta}x^{-(2+\delta)}\mathbf{1}_{x\geq 2}.

Then we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\eta\|Q)=\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{(1+\delta)^{\alpha}2^{\alpha(1+\delta)}x^{-\alpha\delta}-1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{x^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}<\infty,

and

\displaystyle W_{1}(P,\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}\int_{1}^{y}(1+\delta)x^{-(2+\delta)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}dy

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{2}^{\infty}\left|\int_{1}^{y}(1+\delta)x^{-(2+\delta)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{2}^{y}(1+\delta)2^{1+\delta}x^{-(2+\delta)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right|dy

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}1-y^{-(1+\delta)}dy+\int_{2}^{\infty}\left|(1-y^{-(1+\delta)})-(1-2^{1+\delta}y^{-(1+\delta)})\right|dy

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}1-y^{-(1+\delta)}dy+\int_{2}^{\infty}(2^{1+\delta}-1)y^{-(1+\delta)}dy<\infty.

Therefore,
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\leq D_{\alpha}(\eta\|Q)+L\cdot W_{1}(P,\eta)<\infty.

∎

Example

1
is not a special example when
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
is finite but neither
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)
nor
W_{1}(P,Q)
is finite. In fact,
D_{\alpha}^{L}
can be applied to much wider situations. As we will see in
Theorem

4.1
and its proof, the Lipschitz regularization plays a key role. For the rest of the paper, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that have a finite
k
-th moment,
k\geq 1
and we assume that
k
can be a non-integer; we also denote by
\mathcal{P}_{<k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that have a finite
s
-th moment for any
s<k
.

4
Finiteness and variational derivatives of
D_{\alpha}^{L}

In generative modeling, the goal is to approximate a target data distribution
Q
by a generated distribution
P_{g_{\theta}}
, where
g_{\theta}
is typically a neural net parametrization. A specific divergence between the target and the generated distributions is often chosen as the loss function. We want to build the best approximation
P_{g_{\theta^{*}}}
of
Q
using the optimization of a probability divergence or metric:

g_{\theta^{*}}=\arg\min_{g_{\theta}\in\mathcal{G}}D\left(P_{g_{\theta}},Q\right)\approx Q\,,

(9)

where
\mathcal{G}
is a family of neural nets with certain constraints on the parameters
\theta
. To optimize or minimize this loss, it is essential to ensure that the loss function or divergence is
finite
. In
Section

4.1
, we first demonstrate that when
P
has a finite first moment,
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
remains finite without requiring any assumptions on
Q
. In
Section

4.2
, assuming
P
and
Q
have densities and tails, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions for
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
to be finite.

4.1
Minimal assumptions on the target
Q

We make the following assumption on
P
and
Q
for this subsection.

Assumption 1
.

Let
P
and
Q
be arbitrary probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In addition, we assume that
P
has a finite first moment, that is
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We show in the following theorem that
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
is finite whenever
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
without any assumption on
Q
. This includes cases when
Q
has heavy tails, even without a finite first moment, and when
Q
is supported on a low-dimensional manifold and does not have a density.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
\alpha\geq 1
(
\alpha=1
refers to the KL) and
P,Q
satisfy
1
, namely
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)<\infty
.

The key is the Lipschitz regularization, without which the result will not be true; see the proof below.

Proof.

We first prove the case when
\alpha>1
. Let
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\int\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R)}\left\{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)}\left\{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}

\displaystyle\coloneqq I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
, by
Lemma

B.1
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}+\left(\alpha^{-1}(\alpha-1)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}C^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}+\alpha^{-1}(\alpha-1)^{-1}\right)\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}<\infty,

where
C=(\alpha-1)^{-1}\left(\frac{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}\right)^{\alpha-1}+2LR
.

Now we prove that
I_{2}<+\infty
. Let
M(\gamma)=\sup_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert=R}\left|\gamma(x)\right|
, where
\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)
. We show that there exists some
\overline{M}>0
such that

I_{2}=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\left\{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\},

(10)

where

\mathcal{G}=\left\{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R):M(\gamma)\leq\overline{M}\right\}.

Indeed, we have for any
\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}

\displaystyle=\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq 2R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq 2R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}+\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq 2R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle\leq(M(\gamma)+LR)\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}f_{\alpha}^{*}(M(\gamma)-3LR)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq 2R}\left(M(\gamma)+LR+L\left\lVert x\right\rVert\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle=LR\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}+L\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq 2R}\left\lVert x\right\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}+M(\gamma)\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle\quad-f_{\alpha}^{*}(M(\gamma)-3LR)\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q},

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
\gamma(x)
is
L
-Lipschitz and that for any
x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R
, we have
\left|\gamma(x)-M(\gamma)\right|\leq L(R+\left\lVert x\right\rVert)
. The first two terms are finite and are independent of
\gamma
since
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For the difference between the last two terms, we have

\lim_{M(\gamma)\to+\infty}M(\gamma)\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-f_{\alpha}^{*}(M(\gamma)-3LR)\int_{R\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert<2R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}=-\infty,

since the exponent of
x
in
f_{\alpha}^{*}(x)
is
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}>1
. This indicates that the supremum in
I_{2}
should be taken over
\gamma
such that
M(\gamma)\leq\overline{M}
for some
\overline{M}>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\left\{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left(LR+L\left\lVert x\right\rVert+M(\gamma)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left(LR+L\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\overline{M}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}<\infty.

For
\alpha=1
, we bound
I_{1}
using
Lemma

B.2
, and the bound for
I_{2}
can be derived exactly in the same way as for
\alpha>1
by replacing
f_{\alpha}^{*}
by
f_{\text{KL}}^{*}
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Theorem

4.1
,
Lemma

B.1
and
Lemma

B.2
indeed work for any Lipschitz-regularized
f
-divergences, if
f^{*}
, the convex conjugate of
f
, is bounded below and superlinear, i.e.,
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f^{*}(x)}{x}=\infty
.

Remark 2
.

Theorem

4.1
has important implications in generative modeling that one can learn a data distribution
Q
, without any prior knowledge of whether
Q
has heavy tails (even without a finite first moment) or lies on a low-dimensional manifold such that
Q
does not have a density, whenever
P
has a finite first moment, which is a very weak assumption; for example,
P
can start with the Gaussian which is very easy to sample from. In this sense, the generative learning task can be agnostic to the structure of the data distribution using Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences as the objective functionals.

In what follows, we discuss the applicability of two generative models based on
Theorem

4.1
. Their numerical implementations can be seen in several numerical examples in
Section

7
.

Lip-
\alpha
-GANs

GANs based on the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences, abbreviated as Lip-
\alpha
-GANs, can be formulated as

\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D_{\alpha}^{L}(g_{\sharp}P\|Q)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{g_{\sharp}P}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right\},

(11)

where
P
is the initial source distribution, typically chosen as a Gaussian, and
Q
is the target data distribution, and
\mathcal{G}
is the class of generators, and
g_{\sharp}P
is the push-forward measure of
P
by the map
g
.
Theorem

4.1
informs us that we can learn any probability measure
Q
if
g_{\sharp}P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; for example, the generator can be realized using a ReLU network with a Gaussian source distribution as
P
. Key to obtaining the optimal generator is calculating the gradient of the loss relative to generator parameters, shown by the chain rule (Regarding the chain rule calculation (
12
), we also refer to a related formal calculation in Sec. 3.3 of
[
40
]
):

\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{g_{\theta}}\|Q)=\int\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}(P_{g_{\theta}}(x))\cdot\nabla_{\theta}P_{g_{\theta}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(12)

where
\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}
is the variational derivative or the first variation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
, formally defined in
Theorem

4.2
. Therefore, even with a well-designed neural network architecture for the generator
g_{\theta}
, a robust and well-defined variational derivative
\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}(P_{g_{\theta}}(x))
is crucial for stable and effective optimization in the parameter
\theta
because it directly impacts the parameter gradient
\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{g_{\theta}}\|Q)
via (
12
), otherwise computing
\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{g_{\theta}},Q)
could become unstable, leading to erratic parameter updates that hinder convergence. While GANs use discriminators rather than explicit variational derivatives,
Theorem

4.2
shows that the finiteness of a variational derivative can provide mathematical insight into GAN training. On the other hand, we should note that

•

The variational derivative does not exist in general for the Wasserstein-1 metric alone (as is used in WGANs). For example, let
P=\delta_{x_{1}}
and
Q=\delta_{x_{2}}
be two Dirac delta distributions centered at points
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}
with the usual distance function. Then the variational derivative in the sense of
Theorem

4.2
has a discontinuity:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\left|x_{1}-x_{2}+\epsilon v\right|\Big|_{\epsilon=0}=\begin{cases}v,&\text{if }x_{1}-x_{2}>0,\\
-v,&\text{if }x_{1}-x_{2}<0.\end{cases}

•

Unregularized
f
-divergences (such as the KL-divergence) may yield large variational derivatives when
P_{g_{\theta}}
and
Q
do not overlap significantly, potentially causing gradient spikes. This instability can lead to large, uncontrolled updates in
\theta
, which might result in mode collapse or oscillations in GAN training. In contrast, the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences always have well-defined variational derivatives by
Theorem

4.2
.

Gradient flows of
D_{\alpha}^{L}

To further illustrate the significance of
Theorem

4.1
, we provide perspectives from the Wasserstein gradient flows of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
for a feasible distribution learning task. As a particular case of the Lipschitz-regularized gradient flows proposed in
[
21
]
, the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences can be used to construct gradient flows of the form

\partial_{t}P_{t}=\text{div}\left(P_{t}\nabla\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{t}\|Q)}{\delta P_{t}}\right),

(13)

for an initial source probability measure
P_{0}
and a target measure
Q
, where
\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}
is the first variation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
, defined in
Theorem

4.2
. This type of gradient flows was inspired by the gradient flows in the 2-Wasserstein space of probability measures in
[
25
,
44
]
.
In
[
21
]
, the first variation form of
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
is proved under the assumption that both
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In
Theorem

4.2
, we extend it to the case when we only require
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
but impose no assumptions on
Q
. This corresponds to the condition in
Theorem

4.1
. The key to the extension is our
Lemma

E.3
and the proof can be found in
Appendix

D
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Under
1
, namely
P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
Q
can be any probability measure, we define

\gamma^{\star}\coloneqq\argmax_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right\},

(14)

where the optimizer
\gamma^{\star}\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
exists, and is defined on
\text{supp}(P)\cup\text{supp}(Q)
, and is unique. Subsequently, we can extend
\gamma^{\star}
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the same Lipschitz constant. Let
\rho
be a signed measure of total mass 0 and let
\rho=\rho_{+}-\rho_{-}
, where both
\rho_{\pm}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are nonnegative and mutually singular. If
P+\epsilon\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right)=\int\gamma^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho},

(15)

and we write

\frac{\delta D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)}{\delta P}(P)=\gamma^{\star}.

(16)

As a result,
Theorem

4.2
provides a reformulation of (
13
) as in
[
21
]
:

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}P_{t}+\text{div}(P_{t}v_{t}^{L})=0,\quad P_{0}=P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
&v_{t}^{L}=-\nabla\gamma^{\star}_{t},\quad\gamma^{\star}_{t}=\argmax_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right\}.\end{split}

(17)

Moreover, Theorem 2 in
[
21
]
tells us that if
P_{t}
is sufficiently smooth, then we have

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{t}\|Q)=-I_{\alpha}(P_{t}\|Q)\leq 0,

(18)

where
I_{\alpha}(P_{t}\|Q)
is the Lipschitz-regularized Fisher Information:

I_{\alpha}(P_{t}\|Q)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[\left|\nabla\gamma^{\star}_{t}\right|^{2}].

Then for any
T\geq 0
, we have

D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{T}\|Q)=D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{0}\|Q)-\int_{0}^{T}I_{\alpha}(P_{s}\|Q)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s}\leq D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{0}\|Q)\,.

(19)

Therefore, the finiteness and the variational derivative of
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{0}\|Q)
are crucial to launch the gradient flow. While the convergence of the gradient flow is also important, we do not address its PDE theory in this work, but rather its feasibility to learn any distribution
Q
.

4.2
When
P
and
Q
have densities and heavy tails

In this subsection, we show that
D_{\alpha}^{L}
is applicable to comparing heavy-tailed distributions, by providing necessary and sufficient conditions that relate the tail behaviors of
P
and
Q
with
\alpha
. This also provides insights into the selection of suitable
\alpha
’s. For this purpose, including cases when
P\notin\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
–compare to
Theorem

4.1
–we make the following assumptions on
P
and
Q
.

Assumption 2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose densities
p(x)
and
q(x)
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. However,
P
and
Q
are not necessarily absolutely continuous with respect to each other on some bounded subset.

Definition 4.3
.

For a pair of distributions
(P,Q)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say they are of heavy-tail
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
,
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>d
, if there exists some
R>0
, such that

p(x)\asymp\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{1}},\quad q(x)\asymp\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{2}},

for
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R
. That is, there exist constants
0<c_{p,1}\leq c_{p,2}
and
0<c_{q,1}\leq c_{q,2}
such that

c_{p,1}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{1}}\leq p(x)\leq c_{p,2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{1}},\quad c_{q,1}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{2}}\leq q(x)\leq c_{q,2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{2}},

for
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R
.

Then we prove the following necessary and sufficient conditions on the tail behaviors of
(P,Q)
for
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
to be finite. The proof makes extensive use of the variational formula (
1
) and Lipschitz regularization and is provided in
Appendix

C
.

Theorem 4.4

(Necessary and sufficient conditions for
D_{\alpha}^{L}<\infty
,
\alpha>1
)
.

Suppose
\alpha>1
, and
(P,Q)
are distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of heavy-tail
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
. Then

D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)<\infty
if and only if one of the following two conditions holds:

(i)
d<\beta_{1}\leq d+1
and
\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}<\frac{\beta_{1}-d}{\alpha-1}
;

(ii)
\beta_{1}>d+1
.

Remark 3
.

We can relax the assumption in
Definition

4.3
to allow different tail behavior in different directions as follows. Let
\Omega_{k}
be a finite partition of the spherical coordinates
[0,\pi]^{d-2}\times[0,2\pi)
, where each
\Omega_{k}
has non-zero Lebesgue measure of
[0,\pi]^{d-2}\times[0,2\pi)
. We can assume that
p(x)\asymp\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{1,k}}
and
q(x)\asymp\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{-\beta_{2,k}}
on each
\Omega_{k}
. Then the
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)<\infty
if and only if
\beta_{1,k}
and
\beta_{2,k}
satisfy one of the conditions of
Theorem

4.4
on each
\Omega_{k}
. The proof is the same as that of
Theorem

4.4
constrained on each
\Omega_{k}
. This relaxation can be adopted in the same way for
Theorem

4.5
and
Corollary

4.6
.

For the Lipschitz-regularized KL-divergence, we have the following result whose proof can be found in
Appendix

C
.

Theorem 4.5

(Necessary and sufficient conditions for
D_{\text{KL}}^{L}<\infty
)
.

Suppose
\alpha=1
(the KL case), and
(P,Q)
are distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of heavy-tail
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
, then

D_{\text{KL}}^{L}(P\|Q)<\infty
for any
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>d
.

Remark 4
.

Since
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>d
are the minimal assumptions for
P
and
Q
to be probability distributions,
Theorem

4.5
suggests that using the Lipschitz-regularized KL-divergence is the most robust choice, as it can be agnostic to both the tails of
P
and
Q
, compared to the conditions in
Theorem

4.4
.

In cases where both
P
and
Q
lie on a low-dimensional submanifold, we have the following corollary. The proof can be found in
Appendix

C
.

Corollary 4.6

(Necessary and sufficient conditions on embedded submanifolds)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
d^{*}
-dimensional smooth embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via an
L^{*}
-Lipschitz embedding
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathcal{M}=\varphi(\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}})
for
d^{*}<d
. Suppose
(P,Q)
are of heavy-tail
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
on
\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
, and let

and

be their push-forward distributions on
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,
p_{\mathcal{M}}=p\circ\varphi^{-1}
and

. Then the Lipschitz-regularized

-divergence between

and

, defined as

D_{\alpha}^{L}(p_{\mathcal{M}}\|q_{\mathcal{M}})=\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathcal{M}}}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathcal{M}}}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right\},

is finite if and only if one of the following two conditions holds for

:

(i)
d^{*}<\beta_{1}\leq d^{*}+1
and
\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}<\frac{\beta_{1}-d^{*}}{\alpha-1}
;

(ii)
\beta_{1}>d^{*}+1
;

and
D_{\alpha}^{L}(p_{\mathcal{M}}\|q_{\mathcal{M}})<\infty
for any
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>d^{*}
if

.

Remark 5
.

The Lipschitz condition on the embedding
\varphi
is necessary to guarantee that the tails of

and

do not become heavier than those of

and

.

5
Lipschitz regularization implies finite-sample estimation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

In practice, we only have finite i.i.d. samples drawn from
P
and
Q
. We denote by
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
and
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
the i.i.d. samples from
P
and
Q
, with empirical distributions
P_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
Q_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{j}}
, respectively. Thus it is essential to provide guarantees for how fast
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{m}\|Q_{n})
convergences to
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
in average. This type of convergence rate for the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences has been proved in
[
11
]
on bounded domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, we derive the first result of the convergence of the finite-sample estimations on the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, under certain tail conditions. The result for
d\geq 3
is stated below, with its proof deferred to
Appendix

E
. The results for
d=1,2
can be found as
Proposition

E.5
and
Proposition

E.6
in
Appendix

E
.

Theorem 5.1

(Finite sample estimation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Assume
d\geq 3
. For
\alpha>1
, let
P
and
Q
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
P\in\mathcal{P}_{<\beta_{1}-d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{<\beta_{2}-d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\beta_{1}>3d
and
\beta_{2}>5d
. Suppose
\alpha
satisfies
\frac{2d\alpha}{\alpha-1}<\beta_{1}-d
and
\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}<\frac{\beta_{2}}{d}-3
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left|D_{\alpha}^{L}(P_{m}\|Q_{n})-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{m^{1/d}}+\frac{C_{2}}{n^{1/d}},

(20)

where
C_{1}
depends on
M_{\frac{d}{d-1}}(P)
and
C_{2}
depends on
M_{\frac{2d\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(P)
,
M_{\frac{2d\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(Q)
, and
M_{dr_{2}}(Q)
for any
2+\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}<r_{2}<\frac{\beta_{2}}{d}-1
. Here, we use
M_{r}(P)
to denote the
r
-th moment of
P
. Both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are independent of
m,n
, but they depend on
L
such that
C_{1},C_{2}\to\infty
when
L\to\infty
.

Remark 6
.

The key to proving
Theorem

5.1
is to leverage the Lipschitz condition of the test functions in the variational form (
1
).

6
Finite-sample estimations of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
and
W_{1}
with group symmetry on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Based on
Theorem

5.1
and its proof, we are able to consider one special situation when the distributions are invariant with respect to some group symmetry and to provide convergence results for the empirical estimations of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
with group symmetry in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Empirical estimations of divergences with group symmetry have been studied in
[
11
,
51
]
on bounded domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or on closed Riemannian manifolds. Here we provide the first sample complexity bound with group symmetry on unbounded domains, in particular, for
D_{\alpha}^{L}
and later for
W_{1}
in this section. Before presenting the theorems, we first briefly review the related concepts of group symmetry. Readers of interest can refer to
[
6
,
11
,
51
]
for more details. All the proofs in this section can be found in
Appendix

E
.

A
group
is a set
G
equipped with a group product satisfying the axioms of associativity, identity, and invertibility. Given a group
G
and a set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a map
\theta:G\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is called a
group action on
\mathcal{X}
if
\theta_{g}\coloneqq\theta(g,\cdot):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is an automorphism on
\mathcal{X}
for all
g\in G
, and
\theta_{g_{2}}\circ\theta_{g_{1}}=\theta_{g_{2}\cdot g_{1}}
,
\forall g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. By convention, we will abbreviate
\theta(g,x)
as
gx
. We make the following assumptions on
G
.

Assumption 3
.

For any
g\in G
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\theta_{g}(x)=A_{g}\cdot x
, for some unitary matrix
A_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

A function
\gamma:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
G
-invariant
if
\gamma\circ\theta_{g}=\gamma,\forall g\in G
. Let
\Gamma
be a set of measurable functions
\gamma:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
; its subset,
\Gamma_{G}
, of
G
-invariant functions is defined as

\Gamma_{G}\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\circ\theta_{g}=\gamma,\forall g\in G\}.

(21)

On the other hand, a probability measure
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is called
G
-invariant
if
P=(\theta_{g})_{\sharp}P,\forall g\in G
, where
(\theta_{g})_{\sharp}P\coloneqq P\circ(\theta_{g})^{-1}
is the push-forward measure of
P
under
\theta_{g}
. We denote the set of all
G
-invariant distributions on
\mathcal{X}
as
\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):P~\text{is}~G\text{-invariant}\}
. For
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathcal{X})
,
[
11
]
proposes the following symmetry-informed estimator

D_{\alpha}^{L,G}(P_{m}\|Q_{n})\coloneqq\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{\mathbb{E}_{P_{m}}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q_{n}}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\}

(22)

for
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
, where
\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that consists of
G
-invariant
L
-Lipschitz functions. It is shown in Theorem 4.6 in
[
6
]
that when
P_{m},Q_{n}
are replaced by
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathcal{X})
in (
22
), we have
D_{\alpha}^{L,G}(P\|Q)=D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)
; that is, the divergence value between
P
and
Q
does not change if the supremum is taken over
\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
when both
P
and
Q
are
G
-invariant.

In particular, we consider the case when both
P
and
Q
are sub-Weibull, defined as follows.

Definition 6.1

(sub-Weibull distributions)
.

We call a distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
sub-Weibull, if

\Pr(x\sim P:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq r)\leq a\exp(-br^{1/\theta})~\text{for all }r>0,~\text{for some }a,b,\theta>0.

(23)

Remark 7
.

Sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential distributions are special examples of sub-Weibull distributions.

The following definition of intrinsic dimension is adopted from the capacity dimension from
[
26
]
.

Definition 6.2
.

The intrinsic dimension of a bounded
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, denoted by
dim(\mathcal{X})
, is defined as

\dim(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon)}{\log\epsilon},

(24)

where
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon)
is the covering number of
\mathcal{X}
with
\epsilon
-balls in the standard Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For example, if
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
has nonempty interior, then
\dim(\mathcal{X})=D
; if
\mathcal{X}
is a
d
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, then
\dim(\mathcal{X})=d
.

We have the following theorem for the empirical estimation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
with group symmetry on unbounded domains.

Theorem 6.3

(Finite sample estimation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
with finite group symmetry)
.

For
\alpha>1
, let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathcal{X})
for some
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
G
satisfies
3
. Suppose the quotient space
\mathcal{X}/G
is connected, and for any bounded
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}/G
with nonempty interior with respect to the subspace topology (

\dim(\mathcal{X}_{0})=d^{*}\geq 3
. Let
\left|G\right|<\infty
be the cardinality of

, and we further assume that both
P
and

are sub-Weibull on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left|D_{\alpha}^{L,G}(P_{m}\|Q_{n})-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{(\left|G\right|m)^{1/d^{*}}}+\frac{C_{2}}{(\left|G\right|n)^{1/d^{*}}},

(25)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
depends on
M_{d}(P)
,
M_{d}(Q)
. Both

and

are independent of

and
G
.

When

is a continuous group, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 6.4

(Finite sample estimation of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
with infinite group symmetry)
.

For
\alpha>1
, let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathcal{X})
for some
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
G
satisfies
3
. Suppose the quotient space
\mathcal{X}/G
is connected, and for any bounded

\alpha

-proximal regularization. One may consider to replace the

-proximal regularization with a

(30)

where
W_{2}
is the Wasserstein-2 distance. In
Section

7.1
, we introduce the generative models used and explain how their learning objectives relate to
\alpha
-divergences with
W_{1}
or
W_{2}
proximals. We illustrate our points with four examples. In
Section

7.2
, we compare the effects of incorporating
W_{1}

Section

7.3
, we show the importance of Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences when learning distributions with low-dimensional structures with an example of learning a strange attractor from the Lorenz 63 model. In
Section

7.4
, we present the task of learning an anisotropic heavy-tailed distribution embedded in a high-dimensional space and the results highlight that the Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences make generative learning agnostic to heavy-tailed and manifold assumptions.
We use Gaussian priors for all our experiments, and the implementation details including the network architectures can be found in

7.3
Learning attractors of chaotic dynamical systems

Strange attractor from Lorenz 63 example

The Lorenz 63 model is renowned for its strange attractor, which exhibits a complex structure characterized by a non-integer Hausdorff dimension. In this example, we use various generative models to learn the geometric shape of the attractor, without accounting for its underlying dynamics.
The target dataset
\mathcal{T}
for the generative models consists of
N=5000
positions, defined as:

\mathcal{T}=\{\mathbf{x}(t_{i})=(x_{1}(t_{i}),x_{2}(t_{i}),x_{3}(t_{i})):t_{i}\sim\text{Unif}([9900,10000])\}_{i=1}^{N}
where
(x_{1}(t_{i}),x_{2}(t_{i}),x_{3}(t_{i}))

a=10,b=28,c=8.3

generated samples across various generative models for this example.

Because the generated samples lack time labels, the dynamics cannot be directly observed.

(a)

Mean square sum of the errors (MSE)

8
Conclusions and discussions

In this paper, we prove that Lipschitz-regularized
\alpha
-divergences, introduced in previous works, enable robust and stable learning for target distributions with minimal assumptions. In particular, we prove that these divergences are always finite and have a well-defined variational derivative when the first input distribution has a finite first moment. We also prove the sufficient and necessary conditions for the divergence to be finite when both distributions have power-law-decay tails. A first convergence rate of the finite-sample estimations of these divergences on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is proved. As a result, we derive the first sample complexity bounds for the empirical estimations of
D_{\alpha}^{L}
and
W_{1}
with group symmetry on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Numerical simulations further confirm the robustness of these divergences, showing that they significantly improve the learning process across a range of challenging scenarios, such as heavy-tailed distributions or distributions supported on low-dimensional manifolds or fractals.

Some future directions are unexplored in this work. First, it is not clear if there is an optimal
\alpha
or if the
\alpha
should be chosen adaptively to make the learning more efficient. Second, the PDE theory of the Lipschitz-regularized gradient flow is not established, and the convergence of the gradient flow is an important topic and may require some new functional inequalities. Lastly,
Theorem

5.1
is not sharp, and a sharp convergence bound will help better understand this class of divergences and further derive better generalization bounds for algorithms based on this class of divergences.

, to represent probability distributions as well as the density functions associated with them. Whether
a character refers to a probability distribution or a density should be clear from the context.

Appendix B

Theorem

We first provide a lemma that generalizes Lemma A.12 in
[
11
]
.

Lemma B.1
.

For
\alpha>1
and any non-negative measures
P
and
Q
defined on some bounded
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with non-zero integrals,
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{L}(\Omega)
, we have

\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\},

(35)

where

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\Omega):\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq(\alpha-1)^{-1}\left(\frac{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}\right)^{\alpha-1}+L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)\right\}.

Proof of
Lemma

B.1
.

For any fixed
\gamma\in\Gamma
, define

h(\nu)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\gamma(x)+\nu\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)+\nu]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}.

Since
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)-\inf_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)\leq L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
,
interchanging the integration with differentiation is allowed by the dominated convergence theorem:

h^{\prime}(\nu)=\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}^{*\prime}(\gamma+\nu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q},

where

f_{\alpha}^{*\prime}(y)=(\alpha-1)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}y^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\mathbf{1}_{y>0}.

(36)

If
\inf_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)>(\alpha-1)^{-1}\left(\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}\right)^{\alpha-1}
, then
h^{\prime}(0)<0
. So there exists some
\nu_{0}<0
such that
h(\nu_{0})>h(0)
. This indicates the supremum on the left side of (
35
) is attained only if
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)\leq(\alpha-1)^{-1}\left(\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}\right)^{\alpha-1}+L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, if
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)<0
, then there exists
\nu_{0}>0
that satisfies
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)+\nu_{0}<0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\gamma(x)+\nu_{0}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)+\nu_{0}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\gamma(x)+\nu_{0}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle>\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}.

This indicates that the supremum on the left side of (
35
) is attained only if
\inf_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)\geq-L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
. Therefore, we have that the supremum on the left side of (
35
) is attained only if
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq(\alpha-1)^{-1}\left(\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}\right)^{\alpha-1}+L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
.
∎

For the Lipschitz-regularized KL-divergence, we have the following lemma similar to
Lemma

B.1
.

Lemma B.2
.

For the KL case, i.e.,
f_{\text{KL}}^{*}(y)=e^{y-1}
and any non-negative measures
P
and
Q
defined on some bounded
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with non-zero integrals,
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{L}(\Omega)
, we have

\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\text{KL}}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\text{KL}}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\},

(37)

where

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\Omega):\ln\frac{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}+1-L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)\leq\gamma\leq\ln\frac{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}+1+L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)\right\}.

Proof.

For any fixed
\gamma\in\Gamma
, define

h(\nu)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\gamma(x)+\nu\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\text{KL}}^{*}[\gamma(x)+\nu]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}.

Since
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)-\inf_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)\leq L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
,
interchanging the integration with differentiation is allowed by the dominated convergence theorem:

h^{\prime}(\nu)=\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}-\int_{\Omega}f_{\text{KL}}^{*\prime}(\gamma+\nu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}.

If
\inf_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)>\ln\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}+1
, then
h^{\prime}(0)<0
. So there exists some
\nu_{0}<0
such that
h(\nu_{0})>h(0)
. This indicates the supremum on the left side of (
37
) is attained only if
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)\leq\ln\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}+1+L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, if
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)<\ln\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}+1
, then
h^{\prime}(0)>0
. So there exists some
\nu_{0}>0
such that
h(\nu_{0})>h(0)
.
This indicates that the supremum on the left side of (
37
) is attained only if
\inf_{x\in\Omega}\gamma(x)\geq\ln\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}}+1-L\cdot\text{diam}(\Omega)
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of
Theorem

4.4
and
Theorem

4.5

Proof of
Theorem

4.4
.

1. Sufficiency
.

Let
\Gamma=\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\int\gamma(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R)}\left\{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\gamma(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)}\left\{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right\}

\displaystyle\coloneqq I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
, by Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\left(\alpha^{-1}(\alpha-1)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}C^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}+\alpha^{-1}(\alpha-1)^{-1}\right)\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}<\infty,

where
C=(\alpha-1)^{-1}\left(\frac{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}}{\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}}\right)^{\alpha-1}+2LR
.

For
I_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}p(x)\left(\gamma(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

(i) If
d<\beta_{1}\leq d+1
and
\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}<\frac{\beta_{1}-d}{\alpha-1}
:

Note that the set of bounded
L
-Lipschitz functions on
\{x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R\}
is a subset of
\mathcal{M}_{b}(x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)
, and the supremum over all the
L
-Lipschitz functions can be obtained by taking the supremum over all the bounded
L
-Lipschitz functions. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)}\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}p(x)\left(\gamma(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)}\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}p(x)\left(\gamma(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\gamma(x)]\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\left(\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]^{\alpha}-1\right)q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{\alpha(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}<\infty,

since
\alpha(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{2}=(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{1}<-d
, and the equality is due to the dual formula (
5
).

(ii) If
\beta_{1}>d+1
: the proof follows that of
Theorem

4.1
.

2. Necessity
.

Suppose
\beta_{1}\leq d+1
and
\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}\geq\frac{\beta_{1}-d}{\alpha-1}
. We split
\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}\geq\frac{\beta_{1}-d}{\alpha-1}
into two cases.

(i) If
\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}
:

Let
\widehat{\gamma}(x)=\tau\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, where
\tau\in(0,L]
is to be determined. Then we have
\widehat{\gamma}\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Using this
\widehat{\gamma}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle\geq\int\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

It is straightforward that the first integral over
\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R
is finite. For the latter one, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left(\tau\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{1-\beta_{1}}-\tau^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}-\beta_{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

We need to show the right-hand side is infinite. First, since
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}>1
, we can choose
\tau
sufficiently small such that
\tau>\tau^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
. Moreover, by the assumption, we have
1-\beta_{1}\geq-d
and
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}-\beta_{2}\leq 1-\beta_{1}
, so that we have

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left(\tau\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{1-\beta_{1}}-\tau^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}-\beta_{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}=\infty,

and thus
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)=\infty
.

(ii) If
\frac{\beta_{1}-d}{\alpha-1}\leq\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}<\frac{1}{\alpha-1}
:

Define

\widehat{\gamma}(x)=\begin{cases}\tau R^{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})},&\text{if }\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R;\\
\tau\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})},&\text{if }\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R,\end{cases}

where
\tau\in(0,L]
is to be determined. Since in this case we have
(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})(\alpha-1)<1
, we have
\widehat{\gamma}(x)\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if we pick
R
sufficiently large which is independent of
\tau\leq L
. Using this
\widehat{\gamma}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle\geq\int\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R}\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)-f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

By the definition of
\widehat{\gamma}
, we know that the first integral over
\left\lVert x\right\rVert<R
is finite. For the latter one, we have in this case

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\widehat{\gamma}(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\alpha}^{*}[\widehat{\gamma}(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left(\tau\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{1}}-\tau^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{1}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

We show the right-hand side is infinite. Again, we can choose
\tau
sufficiently small such that
\tau>\tau^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
. On the other hand, by the assumption in this case, we have
(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{1}\geq-d
, so that we have

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\left(\tau\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{1}}-\tau^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})-\beta_{1}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}=\infty,

hence
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)=\infty
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.5
.

Same as in the beginning of the proof of
Theorem

4.4
, we can split
D_{\text{KL}}^{L}(P\|Q)
into
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, where
I_{1}
is bounded by

with appropriate

.

For

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)}\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\text{KL}}^{*}[\gamma(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(x:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R)}\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}\gamma(x)p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R}f_{\text{KL}}^{*}[\gamma(x)]q(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

Appendix D
Proof of
Theorem

Proof of
Theorem

4.2
.

The existence and uniqueness of
\gamma^{\star}
follow from Theorem 4.9 in
[
16
]
and Theorem 25 in
[
5
]
. We extend
\gamma^{\star}
from
\text{supp}(P)\cup\text{supp}(Q)
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\gamma^{\star}(y)=\sup_{x\in\text{supp}(P)\cup\text{supp}(Q)}\{\gamma^{\star}(x)+L\left|x-y\right|\}.

(38)

And it is a well-known result that
\gamma^{\star}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\gamma^{\star}=\sup_{h}\{h(x):h\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),h(y)=\gamma^{\star}(y),\forall y\in\text{supp}(P)\cup\text{supp}(Q)\}.

(39)

We need to show that

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0+}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right)\geq\int\gamma^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho},

(40)

and

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0+}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right)\leq\int\gamma^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

(41)

If
P+\epsilon\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then by
Theorem

4.4
(condition (ii)),
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)<\infty
and thus we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P+\epsilon\rho}[\gamma]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma)]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{P+\epsilon\rho}[\gamma^{\star}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma^{\star})]

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}+\mathbb{E}_{P}[\gamma^{\star}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma^{\star})]

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}+D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q).

Thus, we have

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0+}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right)\geq\int\gamma^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

(42)

To prove the other direction, we define
F(\epsilon)=D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)
. Then by Theorem 18 in
[
5
]
,
F(\epsilon)
is convex, lower semi-continuous
and finite on
[0,\epsilon_{0}]
for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
. Due to the convexity of
F
, it is differentiable on
(0,\epsilon_{0})
except for a countable number of points. If
\gamma_{\epsilon}^{\star}
is the optimizer for
D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)
, similar to (
42
), we have for
\delta>0
sufficiently small

D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+(\epsilon+\delta)\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)\geq\delta\int\gamma_{\epsilon}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho},

(43)

and

D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+(\epsilon-\delta)\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)\geq-\delta\int\gamma_{\epsilon}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

(44)

If
F
is differentiable at
\epsilon
, this implies that

\displaystyle\int\gamma_{\epsilon}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\left(D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+(\epsilon+\delta)\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)\right)

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\left(D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon\rho\|Q)-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+(\epsilon-\delta)\rho\|Q)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int\gamma_{\epsilon}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

Consequently,

F^{\prime}(\epsilon)=\int\gamma_{\epsilon}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

(45)

Let
F^{\prime}_{+}(0)
be the right derivative at
\epsilon=0
, i.e.,
F^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}(F(\epsilon)-F(0))
. By convexity, for any sequence
\epsilon_{n}
such that
F
is differentiable at
\epsilon_{n}
and
\epsilon\searrow 0
, we have

F^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{\prime}(\epsilon_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

(46)

We write
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}K_{m}
with
K_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
being compact set and
K_{m}\subset K_{m+1}
. The optimizers
\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}
are unique. Moreover, by
Lemma

E.3
, they satisfy
\left|\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}(x)\right|\leq L(\left|x\right|+R)+M_{n}
, where

M_{n}=\inf_{M}\left\{(M+LR)+L\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|x\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P}+\epsilon_{n}L\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|x\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}<f_{\alpha}^{*}(M-3LR)\int_{\left|x\right|<2R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}\right\}

(47)

where
R>0
is fixed for all
n
such that
\int_{\left|x\right|<2R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{Q}>0
. Thus, by the linear dependence on
\epsilon_{n}
on the left side inside the infimum in (
47
), we have
M_{n}\leq\overline{M}
for all sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, the sequence
\{\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}\}
is equibounded and equicontinuous on
K_{m}
. By the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, there exists a subsequence of
\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}
that converges uniformly in
K_{m}
. Using diagonal argument, by taking subsequences sequentially along
\{K_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
we
conclude there exists a subsequence such that
\gamma_{\epsilon_{n_{k}}}^{\star}
converges uniformly in any
K_{m}
and thus
\gamma_{\epsilon_{n_{k}}}^{\star}
converges pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\gamma_{0}^{\star}
be the limit and for simplicity we also denote by
\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}
the convergent subsequence. Thus, given
\rho_{\pm}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have by dominated convergence

F_{+}^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma_{0}^{\star}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}.

(48)

By the lower semi-continuity of
D_{\alpha}^{L}(\cdot\|Q)
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}D_{\alpha}^{L}(P+\epsilon_{n}\rho\|Q)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P+\epsilon_{n}\rho}[\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star})]\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P+\epsilon_{n}\rho}[\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star}]-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\gamma_{0}^{\star}]-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma_{\epsilon_{n}}^{\star})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[\gamma_{0}^{\star}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{\alpha}^{*}(\gamma_{0}^{\star})]

\displaystyle\leq D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q),

where in the third equality we use the dominated convergence theorem and in the last inequality we apply the Fatou’s lemma. Thus we have
\gamma_{0}^{\star}=\gamma^{\star}

P-
a.s., and
\gamma_{0}^{\star}\leq\gamma^{\star}
for all

. It can be shown (as in the beginning of the proof of Theorem 1 in
[

is absolutely continuous with respect to

(49)

Thus, (

To prove
Theorem

5.1
, we need a few lemmas. Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be i.i.d. samples of distribution
P
, and
P_{m}
be the corresponding empirical distributions. We define
L_{2}(P_{m})
the metric between any functions
f,g
as
L_{2}(P_{m})(f,g)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|f(x_{i})-g(x_{i})\right|^{2}}
.

Lemma E.1

(Metric entropy with empirical measures)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
\xi=\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{m}\}
be a set of independent random variables that take values on
\{-1,1\}
with equal probabilities (also known as Rademacher variables). Suppose
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are i.i.d. samples of distribution
P
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(x_{i})\right|\leq\inf_{0<\theta<M_{X}}\left(4\theta+\frac{12}{\sqrt{m}}\int_{\theta}^{M_{X}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta,L_{2}(P_{m}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\delta}\right),

where
M_{X}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|f(x_{i})\right|^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be an arbitrary positive integer and
\delta_{k}=M_{X}\cdot 2^{-(k-1)}
,
k=1,\dots,N
, with
M_{X}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|f(x_{i})\right|^{2}}
. Let
V_{k}
be the cover achieving
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta_{k},L_{2}(P_{m}))
, and denote
\left|V_{k}\right|=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta_{k},L_{2}(P_{m}))
. For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, let
\pi_{k}(f)\in V_{k}
, such that

\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|f(x_{i})-\pi_{k}(f)(x_{i})\right|^{2}}\leq\delta_{k}.

(50)

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(x_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\left(f(x_{i})-\pi_{N}(f)(x_{i})\right)\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\left(\pi_{j+1}(f)(x_{i})-\pi_{j}(f)(x_{i})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\pi_{1}(f)(x_{i})\right|.

For the third term, observe that it suffices to take
V_{1}=\{0\}
so that
\pi_{1}(f)
is the zero function and the third term vanishes. The first term can be bounded using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\left(f(x_{i})-\pi_{N}(f)(x_{i})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}(\xi_{i})^{2}}\sqrt{\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(f(x_{i})-\pi_{N}(f)(x_{i})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{N}.

To handle the middle term, for each
j
, let
W_{j}=\{\pi_{j+1}(f)-\pi_{j}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. We have
\left|W_{j}\right|\leq\left|V_{j+1}\right|\left|V_{j}\right|\leq\left|V_{j+1}\right|^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\left(\pi_{j+1}(f)(x_{i})-\pi_{j}(f)(x_{i})\right)\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{w\in W_{j}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}w(x_{i})\right|.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in W_{j}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w(x_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\pi_{j+1}(f)(x_{i})-\pi_{j}(f)(x_{i})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\pi_{j+1}(f)(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}}+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(f(x_{i})-\pi_{j}(f)(x_{i})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{m}\delta_{j+1}+\sqrt{m}\cdot\delta_{j}

\displaystyle=3\sqrt{m}\delta_{j+1}.

By the Massart finite class lemma (see, e.g.
[
38
]
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{w\in W_{j}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}w(x_{i})\right|\leq\frac{3\sqrt{m}\delta_{j+1}\sqrt{2\ln\left|W_{j}\right|}}{m}\leq\frac{6\delta_{j+1}\sqrt{\ln\left|V_{j+1}\right|}}{\sqrt{m}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}f(x_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{N}+\frac{6}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta_{j+1}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta_{j+1},L_{2}(P_{m}))}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{N}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\delta_{j}-\delta_{j+1})\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta_{j},L_{2}(P_{m}))}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{N}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{m}}\int_{\delta_{N+1}}^{M_{X}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta,L_{2}(P_{m}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\delta}.

Finally, select any
\theta\in(0,M_{X})
and let
N
be the largest integer with
\delta_{N+1}>\theta
, (implying
\delta_{N+2}\leq\theta
and
\delta_{N}=4\delta_{N+2}\leq 4\theta
), so that

\delta_{N}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{m}}\int_{\delta_{N+1}}^{M_{X}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta,L_{2}(P_{m}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\delta}\leq 4\theta+\frac{12}{\sqrt{m}}\int_{\theta}^{M_{X}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\delta,L_{2}(P_{m}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\delta}.

∎

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle J_{1}+J_{2}

Lemma E.4

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\inf_{\theta>0}\left(4\theta+\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{\theta}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,M},\delta,L_{2}(Q_{n}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\inf_{\theta>0}\left(4\theta+\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}K(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}}\Lambda_{2}^{d/4}\int_{\theta}^{\infty}\delta^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\inf_{\theta>0}\left(4\theta+\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}K(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}}\Lambda_{2}^{d/4}\cdot\frac{2}{2-d}\theta^{1-d/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left(4n^{-\frac{1}{d}}+12K(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}}\Lambda_{2}^{d/4}\cdot\frac{2}{2-d}n^{-\frac{1}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=4n^{-\frac{1}{d}}+\frac{24K}{2-d}n^{-\frac{1}{d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left[(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}}\Lambda_{2}^{d/4}\right]

where we pick
\theta=n^{-\frac{1}{d}}
. By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left[(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}}\Lambda_{2}^{d/4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{(\alpha-1)}}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{Y}\Lambda_{2}^{d/2}}.

Notice that
\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}(M+L)^{\frac{d\alpha}{(\alpha-1)}}
is bounded by Lemma
E.4
and the bound depends on
M_{\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(p)
and
M_{\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(q)
. By Jensen’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{E}_{Y}\Lambda_{2}^{d/2}\leq(\mathbb{E}_{Y}\Lambda_{2}^{d})^{1/2}
. And we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y}\Lambda_{2}^{d}=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lVert y_{j}\right\rVert^{\hat{\beta}_{2}}\right)^{d}\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lVert y_{j}\right\rVert^{\hat{\beta}_{2}d}\right)=M_{\hat{\beta}_{2}d}(q),

where the inequality follows Jensen’s inequality. Combining all these bounds, we obtain the result as in the statement of the theorem.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.3
.

The proof is very similar to that of

, therefore we only outline the improvement we can obtain. First, same as the beginning of the proof of Theorem 4.8 in

to

by invariance, so that we focus on Lipschitz functions on

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left|D_{\alpha}^{L,G}(P_{m}\|Q_{n})-D_{\alpha}^{L}(P\|Q)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left|\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathcal{X})\\

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathcal{X})\\

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathcal{X})\\

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\text{Lip}_{L}^{G}(\mathcal{X}/G)\\

where

on

improved to

on the group and the partition

’s are circular about the origin, the Lebesgue measure induces a reduction by a factor of
