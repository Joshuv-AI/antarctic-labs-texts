Abstract
The synthetic control method is often applied to problems with one treated unit and a small number of control units. A common inferential task in this setting is to test null hypotheses regarding the average treatment effect on the treated. Inference procedures that are justified asymptotically are often unsatisfactory due to (1) small sample sizes that render large-sample approximation fragile and (2) simplification of the estimation procedure that is implemented in practice. An alternative is permutation inference, which is related to a common diagnostic called the placebo test. It has provable Type-I error guarantees in finite samples without simplification of the method, when the treatment is uniformly assigned. Despite this robustness, the placebo test suffers from low resolution since the null distribution is constructed from only N N reference estimates, where N N is the sample size. This creates a barrier for statistical inference at a common level like α = 0.05 \alpha=0.05 , especially when N N is small. We propose a novel leave-two-out procedure that bypasses this issue, providing O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) reference estimates while still maintaining the same finite-sample Type-I error guarantee under uniform assignment for a wide range of N N that is common in applications. Unlike the placebo test whose Type-I error always equals the theoretical upper bound, our procedure often achieves a lower unconditional Type-I error than theory suggests; this enables useful inference in the challenging regime when α < 1 / N \alpha<1/N . Empirically, our procedure achieves a higher power when the effect size is reasonably large and a comparable power otherwise. We generalize our procedure to non-uniform assignments and show how to conduct sensitivity analysis. From a methodological perspective, our procedure can be viewed as a new type of randomization inference different from permutation or rank-based inference, which is particularly effective in small samples.

Suppose we observe data with

\norm{X_I - X_0 W}_{V}=\sqrt{(X_{I}-X_{0}W)^{\top}V(X_{I}-X_{0}W)}

The standard inference procedure in the design-based framework is the placebo test. It was introduced in

, such as the widely used RMSPE statistic defined in (
6

p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left\{R_{I}\leq R_{i}\right\}.

(2)

The standard argument for permutation tests shows that
p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}
is a valid
p
-value.

Small sample issues of the placebo test.

The
p
-value obtained from such tests are coarse: they take values in the grid
\left\{\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},\dots,1\right\}.
When
N
is relatively small like in the premier applications of the synthetic control method

AG03
,
ADH10
,
ADH15
]
or those examples in Appendix

, the smallest value
1/N
of the

-value may give statistical significance, while the
2/N
may switch this conclusion. This low level of granularity hinders the application of the placebo test and related inference methods in practice. In some applications, the value of
1/N
may be too large to even reject at a pre-specified level
\alpha
. For example, when
\alpha=0.05
,
\alpha<1/N
in both
[
AG03
]
and
[
ADH15
]
. Due to a desire to control size at traditional levels like
\alpha\in\left\{0.01,0.05,0.10\right\}
and generally small datasets,
N\alpha<3
is typical for a variety of applications. This includes state/province/prefecture-level analysis
[
ADH10
,

,

,
DAW19
,

,

[

]
, and manufacturer-level analysis
[

]
. Even in country-level or county/city-level analysis where the donor pool appears to be large, the number of comparable control units may be small due to restrictions based on geographical proximity
[

,

]

[

,

]

[

,

]
, which can result in a fair drop in the sample size.

More importantly, the placebo test is powerless in small data settings where
\alpha<1/N
. For each unit
i
and time
t
, let
(Y_{it}(1),Y_{it}(0))
denote the pair of potential outcomes had the unit
i
been treated and not treated at time
t
, respectively. Further, let
\mathcal{Y}=\left\{(Y_{it}(1),Y_{it}(0)):i\in[N],t\in[T]\right\}
denote the realizations of all potential outcomes and assume they take distinct values almost surely. Then given
I
is uniformly assigned,
p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}
is distributed uniformly on the set
\left\{1/N,2/N,\dots,1\right\}
under the sharp null
H_{0}:Y_{It}(1)=Y_{It}(0)
for all
t
. Thus, the conditional Type-I error is

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})=\frac{\lfloor N\alpha\rfloor}{N}.

Averaging over
\mathcal{Y}
, the unconditional Type-I error is

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}\leq\alpha)=\frac{\lfloor N\alpha\rfloor}{N}.

As a result, when
\alpha<1/N
, the placebo test has zero size. Proposition
B.1
extends this argument to show that the placebo test has no power in this setting against a natural class of alternatives.

Can we develop a valid
p
-value which has power in these
\alpha<1/N
settings? One useful remedy is to define an approximate version of
p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}
by not comparing with the treated unit itself:

p_{\text{{\tiny app-placebo}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq I}\mathbb{I}\left\{R_{I}\leq R_{i}\right\}=p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}-\frac{1}{N}.

(3)

Clearly, its conditional and unconditional Type-I errors are both
(\lfloor N\alpha\rfloor+1)/N
, which is strictly larger than
\alpha
. The textbook solution to resolve this dilemma (e.g.,
[
LR05
]
) is to use a randomized
p
-value
p_{\text{{\tiny exact-placebo}}}-U/N
where
U
is an independent draw from a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. While this randomized
p
-value has an exact Type-I error control and allows to reject the null even when
\alpha<1/N
, it offers a cheap way of
p
-hacking – the researcher can simply choose a random seed that generates a large
U
without revealing its value. Taking this strategic behavior to the extreme, the randomized
p
-value is no different from the approximate placebo
p
-value defined in (
3
). Aside from the
p
-hacking incentive, the approximate
p
-value defined in (
3
) rescaled by
N/(N-1)
is sometimes used implicitly in the sense that significance is claimed when
p_{\text{{\tiny app-placebo}}}=0
, i.e., the treated unit has the highest summary statistic.

For the aforementioned reasons, the approximate placebo is unsatisfactory. Is there any alternative we may use? Unfortunately, no conditionally finite-sample-valid non-randomized
p
-value has power here, as Proposition
B.1
in Appendix
B.1
shows. However, this does not imply unconditional impossibility.
In applications of synthetic controls, it is reasonable to treat potential outcomes as random. Thus, it suffices to seek an unconditionally-valid p-value.
One way to get useful inference is to reject if the
p
-value
p(I)\leq 1/N
, and hope that
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq 1/N|\mathcal{Y})=0
for some
\mathcal{Y}
. Averaged over different datasets
\mathcal{Y}
, we may hope for an unconditionally valid
p
-value. For example, when
1/2N<\alpha<1/N
, if
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})
takes values
0
and
1/N
both with probability
1/2
, then the unconditional Type-I error
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq\alpha)=1/2N<\alpha
. Neither the exact nor the approximate placebo test has this property because their conditional Type-I errors are both constant regardless of
\mathcal{Y}
. Thus, we cannot achieve this goal by modifying the standard permutation test.

1.1
Contributions

We present the Leave-Two-Out (LTO) placebo test, which gives up conditional validity for a shot at unconditional validity. The LTO placebo test is a non-randomized approximate
p
-value which can be seen as a leave-two-out extension of the usual placebo test, with a user-specified statistic. The LTO placebo test has several advantages over the approximate placebo inference method.

•

Non-randomized inference when
N\alpha
is small.
The LTO placebo test has the same
conditional
Type-I error guarantee as the approximate placebo test (
3
) for a wide range of
N,\alpha
seen in practice. It is true in particular when
N\alpha\leq 1
or
N\alpha<3.9
if further
N>10
. Note that the latter case covers all examples listed above. This is proved in Theorem
2.1
. In addition, we provide empirical evidence that the LTO placebo test often achieves a much lower Type-I error than our theory indicates. As a result, unlike the approximate placebo test (
3
), the LTO placebo test can be
unconditionally valid
, and often matches the unconditional Type I error of the exact placebo test.

•

Power Improvements.
We empirically observe power gain compared to
the approximate placebo test when the effect size is large, and comparable power for intermediate effect sizes. We also observe strict power gain for all effect sizes compared to the exact placebo test and randomized placebo test even when
\alpha>1/N
.
Theoretically, we extend the classical notion of consistent tests to the fixed-
N
setting, and prove that the LTO placebo test is uniformly consistent while the approximate placebo test fails to be when
\alpha<1/N
.

•

Granularity Improvements.
By leaving multiple data points out at a time in the same procedure, the leave-two-out
p
-value lies on a much more refined scale than
\left\{\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},\dots,\frac{N-1}{N},1\right\},
solving granularity problems of the placebo test.

In addition, we discuss a weighted variant of the LTO placebo test under non-uniform assignment probabilities, a sensitivity analysis for the procedure, other versions of LTO placebo tests, and leave-
r
-out extensions for
r>2
.

Example: California Proposition 99 Dataset

In this section, we consider a semisynthetic simulation using the California proposition 99 example of
[
ADH10
]

[
ADH10
]

We construct a semisynthetic version of the data, where California is removed from the dataset. A subsample of
N=30

38

I

\tau

Y_{I,t}

\tau
. A range of values of
\tau

(0,-1,-2,-3)

In the first setting, we take
\alpha=0.02

4.1

On the other hand, the Type-I error of the approximate placebo test is always fixed. We do not present the exact placebo test because it is powerless. Simultaneously, the power of the LTO placebo test is better than that of the approximate placebo test when the effect size is large. When the effect size is intermediate, however, the approximate placebo test has higher power, but not by much. In Section
2.2
, we propose a more powerful test, which has nearly identical power as the approximate placebo test in this setting, by modifying the LTO p-value for any given
\alpha

Leave-Two-Out Placebo Test

Consider a design-based setup similar to that of the placebo test. We observe a panel
Y_{it}
of
N
units,
T
time periods, where the
I
th unit is treated from time
T_{0}+1,\dots,T
. In potential outcomes, the data is given as:

\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1,1}(0)&Y_{1,2}(0)&\dots&Y_{1,T_{0}+1}(0)&\dots&Y_{1,T-1}(0)&Y_{1,T}(0)\\
Y_{2,1}(0)&Y_{2,2}(0)&\dots&Y_{2,T_{0}+1}(0)&\dots&Y_{2,T-1}(0)&Y_{2,T}(0)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
Y_{I,1}(0)&Y_{I,2}(0)&\dots&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y_{I,T_{0}+1}(1)}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dots}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y_{I,T-1}(1)}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y_{I,T}(1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
Y_{N,1}(0)&Y_{N,2}(0)&\dots&Y_{N,T_{0}+1}(0)&\dots&Y_{N,T-1}(0)&Y_{N,T}(0)\end{bmatrix}.

We assume that the treatment index
I
is uniformly assigned across the
N
units. Synthetic control methods estimate the average treatment effect on the treated,
\tau_{I,t}=Y_{I,t}(1)-Y_{I,t}(0)
, by using the synthetic control
\hat{Y}_{I,t}
to estimate the control counterfactual. Following the literature
[
ADH10
,
ADH15
,
FP18
]
, the primary inference task of interest is to test the sharp null hypothesis

H_{0}:Y_{i,t}(1)=Y_{i,t}(0),\quad\forall i,\forall t\geq T_{0}+1

(4)

Extensions to sharp nulls of the form
H_{0}:Y_{i,t}(1)=Y_{i,t}(0)+\tau_{i,t}
for known quantities
\tau_{i,t},i=1,\dots,N,
and
t\geq T_{0}+1
can also be handled.

2.1
Naive LTO Placebo Test

Our LTO placebo test procedure works as follows:

1.

For every pair of distinct units
(i,j)
from
1,\dots,N
and unequal to
I
, create the synthetic control with control units
1,\dots,N
excluding points
i,j,I
to obtain
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{k}^{-(i,j,I)},k\in\left\{i,j,I\right\},
the vector of synthetic control outcomes for unit
k
using controls
[N]\setminus\left\{i,j,I\right\}.
For some statistic
S(\cdot,\cdot),
define the residual

R_{i,j,I;k}=|S(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{-(i,j,I)}_{k})|,

(5)

where
k\in\left\{i,j,I\right\}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{k}
is the observed data for unit
k.

A common choice is the post-period to pre-period Ratio of Mean Square Prediction Error (RMSPE) statistic proposed in
[
ADH15
]
:

S_{{\tiny\text{ratio-RMSPE}}}(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{k})=\frac{S_{{\tiny\text{post-RMSPE}}}(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{k})}{S_{{\tiny\text{pre-RMSPE}}}(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{k})}=\frac{\sum_{t=T_{0}+1}^{T}(Y_{k,t}-\hat{Y}_{k,t})^{2}/(T-T_{0})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}(Y_{k,t}-\hat{Y}_{k,t})^{2}/T_{0}}.

(6)

2.

For every pair of indices in the first step, compute the Leave-Two-Out residual

R_{i,j,I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LTO}}}:=\max(R_{i,j,I;i},R_{i,j,I;j}).

Denote the event that
I
’s residual is larger than that of
i,j
by
\mathbb{I}\left\{I\succ i,j\right\}
, so that

\mathbb{I}\left\{I\succ i,j\right\}:=\mathbf{1}\left\{R_{i,j,I;I}>R_{i,j}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LTO}}}\right\}.

3.

Compute the quantity

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}:=\frac{1}{(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[N]\setminus I\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{I}\left\{I\not\succ i,j\right\},

(7)

which functions as an approximate
p
-value under the null hypothesis.

Remark 2.1
.

Thinking of the LTO placebo test
p
-value in terms of tournaments is useful. Consider a tournament between all of the
N
units, where matches are held for every triple of units. A unit
i
wins a match amongst units
i,j,k
if its residual
R_{i,j,k;i}
is the largest. Then
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
after renormalization counts the number of matches in which unit
I
did not win.

By approximate
p
-value, we mean that the LTO
p
-value satisfies the following Type-I error guarantee.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
N\geq 3
. Then the following results hold.

(a)

When

Proof Sketch of Theorem
2.2
.

The proof of the coverage guarantee relies on analyzing combinatorial structures related to tournaments. We present the proof here in order to introduce the main ideas of the analysis; it is an interesting extension of the proof of coverage in the Jackknife+
[
BCRT21
]
. Let us recall the notation

\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j):=\mathbf{1}\left\{R_{i,j,k;k}>\max(R_{i,j,k;i},R_{i,j,k;j})\right\}.

Call a unit
k

strange
if

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[N]\setminus k\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)\geq(1-\alpha)(N-1)(N-2).

Intuitively, the LTO
p
-value can be seen as the normalized rank of the unit
I
in a tournament where matches are held between every triple of distinct units
i,j,k
. The winner of the match is the unit with the largest residual. We call a unit
strange
if it wins too many matches, quantified above. In such a tournament, not everyone can win too often; following this intuition, we upper bound the number of strange units.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of strange units, and let
s:=|\mathcal{S}|
. Summing the definition of strangeness on both sides, we see that

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i,j\in[N],i\neq j}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)\geq(1-\alpha)s(N-1)(N-2).

The sum on the left hand side may be split into three terms:

\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq j\neq k}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(I)}}+\overbrace{2\sum_{i,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq k,j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(II)}}+\overbrace{\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{c},i\neq j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(III)}},

where
i\neq j\neq k
is shorthand notation for mutually distinct
i,j,k
.
For the first sum, note by renaming the labels that

\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq j\neq k}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq j\neq k}\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq j\neq k}\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i).
Thus

\text{(I)}\leq\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq j\neq k}\frac{1}{3}\left(\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)+\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i)\right)\leq s(s-1)(s-2)/3.

Crucially, we use the fact that there can be at most one winner in a triple comparison:
\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)+\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i)\leq 1
.
1
1

1

We allow for zero winners in the triple comparison if there are ties in the residuals.
For the second sum, swap the naming of the
i,k
labels and use the bound
\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,k)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ k,j)\leq 1
to see that

\text{(II)}\leq 2\sum_{i,k\in\mathcal{S},i\neq k,j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,k)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ k,j)\right)\leq s(s-1)(N-s).

Finally, in the last sum, we use the naive bound
\text{(III)}\leq s(N-s)(N-1-s).

Combining these bounds and working through algebra, we arrive at a quadratic inequality in the variable
\beta:=s/N
:

\frac{\beta^{2}}{3}-\beta(1-\frac{1}{N})-\frac{4}{3N^{2}}+\frac{1}{N}+\alpha(1-\frac{1}{N})(1-\frac{2}{N})\geq 0.

We can solve this by quadratic formula, yielding that, for any
\alpha<2/3
,

\beta\leq f(N,\alpha).

(10)

To conclude, under uniform treatment we have
\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq\alpha)=\mathbb{P}(I\in\mathcal{S})=\beta.
The bound in equation (
10
) shows that
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
is an approximate
p
-value. A full proof with further computational details may be found in Appendix
B
.

2.2
Improved LTO Placebo test

Notice that in defining the notion of strange unit, we have great flexibility in choosing the threshold. Call a unit
k

c
-
strange
if

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[N]\setminus k\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)\geq(1-\alpha-c)(N-1)(N-2).

This results in a new
p
-value
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}-c
. By Theorem
2.2
,

\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}-c\leq\alpha)=\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq\alpha+c)\leq\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha+c)\rfloor}{N}.

As a result, for any
c>0
such that

\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha+c)\rfloor}{N}=\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha)\rfloor}{N},

the adjusted
p
-value
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}-c
has the same bound on the rejection probability as the naive LTO
p
-value
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
but is strictly more powerful than the latter, since it is strictly smaller. This motivates the improved LTO placebo
p
-value.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\delta
be any positive number (e.g.,
\delta=10^{-10}
). Define

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)=p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}-c(N,\alpha)+\delta,

(11)

where

c(N,\alpha)=\min\{c:f(N,\alpha+c)=(\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha)\rfloor+1)/N\}.

Then

\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)\leq\alpha)\leq\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha)\rfloor}{N}.

The power improvement is enabled by the discrete Type-I error bound. Since the improved LTO placebo test has the same Type-I error bound as the naive one, Theorem
2.1
applies to
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)
. As a result,
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)
has the same theoretical guarantee as
p_{\text{{\tiny app-placebo}}}
when
N\alpha<4(1-2/(N-1)(N-2))
. Note that
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)
depends on
\alpha
and hence, unlike
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
, cannot be viewed as a
p
-value. It can only be used for testing
H_{0}
at level
\alpha
.

While the expression of
c(N,\alpha)
is complicated, we can easily compute it by a binary search. For the Proposition 99 dataset,
N=39
,
c(N,0.02)=0.006
and
c(N,0.05)=0.002
. For the application in
[
AG03
]
,
N=17
and
c(N,0.05)=0.0125
.

Further power improvement by enforcing decision monotonicity.

One undesirable feature of
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)
is that the procedure is not decision-monotonic in
\alpha.
That is, if the test based on
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)
rejects at some level
\alpha
, the test using
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha^{\prime})
may not reject at an
\alpha^{\prime}
with
\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
. This is due to the step-like behavior of the Type-I error upper bound.

We can enforce decision monotonicity and also improve power of the procedure by considering instead

I_{\alpha}=\max_{\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha}\mathbf{1}\left\{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha^{\prime})\leq\alpha^{\prime}\right\},

rejecting at level
\alpha
if
I_{\alpha}=1
. Clearly this procedure is more powerful than rejecting only when
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)\leq\alpha
. The procedure also controls Type-I error. To see this,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I_{\alpha}=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\exists\alpha^{\prime}:p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha^{\prime})\leq\alpha^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}(\exists\alpha^{\prime}:p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq\alpha^{\prime}+c(N,\alpha^{\prime})-\delta),

The last line may be written

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq\max_{\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha}\alpha^{\prime}+c(N,\alpha^{\prime})-\delta\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq g(\alpha)+c(N,g(\alpha))-\delta\right),

where
g(\alpha)=\operatorname{argmax}_{\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha}\alpha^{\prime}+c(N,\alpha^{\prime})-\delta.
The latter is equal to
\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(g(\alpha))\leq g(\alpha))\leq\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,g(\alpha))\rfloor}{N}\leq\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha)\rfloor}{N}
, which is the same Type-I error bound as using just
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha).
We do not use this procedure in our simulations, leaving this method for theoretical interest.

2.3
Related Work

The existing literature on synthetic controls is vast: see the survey article
[
Aba21
]
for a review. Accordingly, there has been much work addressing inference for synthetic controls. Among the first inferential procedures for synthetic controls was the placebo test, first described in
[
AG03
]
, and formalized by Firpo and Possebom
[
FP18
]
into a permutation test. They work in the same design-based framework where the probability of treatment is uniform. Several extensions are made, including to a non-equally weighted inference procedure which is useful for sensitivity analysis.

Other works have also considered the design-based setup and randomization or permutation-based inference. See
[
BISW21
]
for a design-based analysis of the synthetic controls procedure under a similar uniform treatment assumption, and also a random treatment time assumption.
[
ST21
]
gives a related randomization inference procedure in a staggered adoption setting where there are multiple treated units, under the additional assumption that the time at which each unit adopts the treatment that follows a Cox proportional hazards model. In general, design-based inference proves to be a powerful tool for panel data analysis
[
AILL21
,
RR20
,
AAIW20
,
AI22
,
RS23
,
Xu23
]
.

Inference procedures have also been developed assuming underlying factor model structure and related time series assumptions on the data.
[
CWZ21
]
develop an inference procedure assuming a regularity condition on the residual process in time. The paper contains an extensive discussion on the various choices for creating the counterfactual or synthetic control. Another line of research
[
CFT21
,
CFPT22
]
provides prediction intervals via non-asymptotic concentration bounds, under stationarity and weak dependence time series assumptions on the data. See also
[
HS17
,
ASS18
,
AAH+21
,
AH23
,
Li20
,
CWZ18
]
for related asymptotic analyses of the synthetic-control type estimators under time series assumptions.

For many of these results, consistency of the estimator and the resulting inference procedure is shown in the limit as the number of controls and units goes to infinity. Although many of these works offer finite sample bounds, it is difficult to contextualize these results when the applications of synthetic controls are usually small
N
, small
T
settings. Furthermore, many of these asymptotic results require simplifications on the synthetic control procedure to carry out analysis. The LTO placebo test can use other counterfactual models beyond the standard synthetic control method, and we require no conceptual simplifications to do inference. As a result, the LTO procedure may be used with generalizations of the original synthetic control procedure such as those outlined in
[
DI16
,
ASS18
,
CWZ21
,
AAH+21
,
Gun23
]
. Furthermore, it can be applied with non-synthetic-control methods developed for inference with few clusters; see Section
6.4
for further discussion.

Non-Uniform Assignments

3.1
Weighted LTO placebo test

Outside of experimental settings, the assumption of uniform treatment is questionable. To handle non-uniform treatment assignments, we first propose a weighted analog of the LTO
p
-value. Suppose that the probability of treating unit
k
is given by
\pi_{k},k=1,\dots,N
. In some applications, these probabilities can be estimated. For example, when assessing the effect of earthquakes
[
CGNP13
,
dIN15
]
, the probability of earthquake in each region can be estimated via statistical seismology
[
VJBZZ05
]
. Then the following weighted approximate
p
-value generalizes the LTO
p
-value of Section
2
.

Definition 3.1

(Weighted LTO
p
-values)
.

\displaystyle p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\\
j,k\neq I\end{subarray}}\frac{\pi_{j}\pi_{k}}{(1-\pi_{I})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq I}\pi_{l}^{2}}\mathbf{I}\{I\not\succ j,k\}

Note that
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)=p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
when
\pi_{i}=1/N
for all
i
.
We prove the following upper bound for the Type-I error of
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)
as the value of an integer programming problem. The proof is deferred to Appendix
B.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)\leq\alpha)
is upper bounded by the value of the following integer programming problem:

\displaystyle\max\,\,

\displaystyle L_{1}(z)

s.t.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}L_{1}(z)^{3}-L_{1}(z)^{2}+L_{1}(z)\left[L_{2}(z)+\alpha\left(1-\sum_{l=1}^{N}\pi_{l}^{2}\right)\right]\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\alpha\right)L_{3}(z)-(1-2\alpha)L_{2}(z),

\displaystyle L_{k}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}^{k}z_{i},\,\,z_{i}\in\{0,1\},\,\,i=1,\ldots,N,\,\,k=1,2,3.

When
\pi_{i}\equiv 1/N
, it can be shown via tedious algebra that Theorem
3.1
recovers Theorem
2.2
. In the general non-uniform case, while the bound is given by a complicated nonconvex integer programming problem, it can be solved approximately by off-the-shelf solvers when
N
is small, e.g.,
N<50
.

3.2
Sensitivity analysis

When assignment probabilities are not controlled by the researcher, it is useful to have procedures to analyze sensitivity of our approach to the uniform assignment assumption. The goal of sensitivity analysis is to answer the question: by how much would our statistical conclusions change if the treatment were non-uniform? Phrased another way, by how much would the treatment probabilities have to change to overturn a conclusion of statistical significance? Firpo and Possebom
[
FP18
]
give an approach to do sensitivity analysis for the exact placebo test under non-uniform treatments. We can extend their approach to the naive LTO placebo test using the weighted LTO placebo
p
-values as the building block. The procedure is as follows.

1.

Compute
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
. If
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}>\alpha
, claim insignificance; otherwise, proceed with the following steps.

2.

Fix a
\Gamma\in[1,N].
Solve the optimization problem

\displaystyle\text{maximize}_{\pi}\ p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\pi_{i}\in\left[\frac{1}{\Gamma N},\frac{\Gamma}{N}\right]\,\,\forall i,\,\,\,\text{and }\sum_{i}\pi_{i}=1.

(13)

3.

Find the smallest value of
\Gamma
by a binary search such that the solution to the above optimization problem is greater than
\alpha.

Remark 3.1
.

The choice of the bound
[\frac{1}{\Gamma N},\frac{\Gamma}{N}]
is not essential. We can handle any bounds on
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{N})
defined by a single parameter. For example, the uncertainty set considered in the sensitivity analysis of
[
FP18
]
can be equivalently formulated as
\max_{i}\pi_{i}/\min_{i}\pi_{i}\leq\Gamma
for some
\Gamma\geq 1
.

By Theorem
3.1
, it remains to further add the constraint (
13
) into the integer programming problem. By carefully analyzing it, we end up with a much simpler result. The proof is presented in Appendix
B.3
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
\alpha\leq 1/3
,
\Gamma\leq N/4
, and
\pi_{i}\in[\frac{1}{\Gamma N},\frac{\Gamma}{N}]
for all
i
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)\leq\alpha)\leq\frac{3-\frac{3\Gamma}{N}-\sqrt{9(1-\frac{\Gamma}{N})^{2}-12\left(-\frac{4\Gamma^{2}}{3N^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma}{N}+\alpha\left(1-\frac{2\Gamma+1}{N}+\frac{2\Gamma^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)\right)}}{2}.

When
\Gamma=1
, Theorem
3.2
recovers Theorem
2.2
. When
N
is large, the bound has the same limit
\frac{3-\sqrt{9-12\alpha}}{2}
as obtained for the
\Gamma=1
setting. Unlike Theorem
2.2
, we cannot sharpen it by taking advantage of the discreteness of the Type-I error because
\pi_{i}
can take a range of values.

Computational Details.

The optimization problem in step 2) may pose challenges. To optimize this problem, we use the following trick. Fixing a
\Gamma
, let
B_{\Gamma}
be the set of
\pi
satisfying the constraints in Equation (
12
). We only need to check if
\max_{\pi\in B_{\Gamma}}p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)
is greater than
\alpha
. This is equivalent to checking that

\max_{\pi\in B_{\Gamma}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\neq I\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}\pi_{j}\pi_{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{I\not\succ j,k\right\}-\alpha\left((1-\pi_{I})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq I}\pi_{l}^{2}\right)>0

The optimization problem can be written as a simple quadratic program. Namely, we must check whether

\max_{\pi\in B_{\Gamma}}\pi^{\top}A\pi+2\alpha\pi^{\top}e_{I}>\alpha

where
A:=(G+\alpha I_{N}-2\alpha e_{I}e_{I}^{\top})
, with
G\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a zero-one matrix with entries
G_{jk}=\mathbb{I}\left\{I\not\succ j,k\right\},
whenever
j
and
k
do not equal
I
and zero otherwise. Here
e_{I}
is the standard basis vector for index
I.
If the goal is to calculate
\max_{\pi\in B_{\Gamma}}p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)
, we can do a binary search over
c\in[0,1]
that satisfy
\max_{\pi\in B_{\Gamma}}p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)>c
, and apply the same trick as above.

The objective is not necessarily concave as
A
is not guaranteed to be positive semidefinite. As it turns out, the resulting optimization problem is a fundamental NP-hard problem known as
quadratic nonconvex programming
with box constraints. In practice, non-convex quadratic programming problems are solved using heuristics such as branch-and-bound techniques. As the dimensionality of the problems that we are solving is rather small, we expect off-the-shelf solvers should do well on this problem. Moreover, whenever the computed LTO
p
-value is small, the matrix
G+I_{N}-2e_{I}e_{I}^{\top}
is usually rather sparse, which would help with computation.

4
Simulation Results

Thus far we have explored theoretical properties of the LTO placebo tests. In this section, we compare empirical properties of the LTO placebo tests on semisynthetic examples based on the California Proposition 99 on tobacco consumption
[
ADH10
]
, the Basque Country terrorism on local economic prosperity
[
AG03
]
, and the German Reunification on West Germany’s GDP.
[
ADH15
]
. All simulation results rely on the R package
Synth

[
ADH11
]
, and may be found at
https://github.com/tsudijon/LeaveTwoOutSCI
.

The structure of the semisynthetic simulations is the same for each dataset. Firstly, we subsample
N
units (smaller than the size of the original data) from each dataset, excluding the original treated unit. As described briefly in the introduction, on each subsample of the data, a unit is chosen uniformly at random to be treated. For each treated unit
I
, we posit a time-invariant treatment effect
\tau
, which we add on to the observed outcomes
Y_{I,t}
following
T_{0}
. A range of values of
\tau
are used, corresponding approximately to
(0,-1,-2,-3)
times the standard deviation of the outcome variable measured at
T_{0}.
For each subsample, several methods are compared in terms of power: the exact placebo, approximate placebo, randomized placebo, the naive LTO, and powered LTO.

The setting for
\tau=0
amounts to comparing the Type-I errors for all methods. We compare the methods in two regimes: one in which
\alpha<1/N
and one in which
\alpha\geq 1/N
. In the
\alpha<1/N
regime case we omit the results for the exact placebo, which will be powerless in this setting. Each method uses the widely-adopted RMSPE statistic defined in (
6
), and the synthetic controls are constructed in ways which follow the original analyses of each dataset. Finally, we average the powers and Type-I errors for all methods over 20 Monte Carlo subsamples of the original dataset, and report the resulting mean. This is an estimate of the unconditional Type-I error and power of each method.

Broadly, we observe the following empirical results. The LTO procedures demonstrate higher power in large signal to noise ratios, compared against the three placebo
p
-values. In intermediate signal to noise ratios, the naive LTO procedure often has lower or comparable power than the placebo procedures, but the powered LTO fares better. In the
\alpha<1/N
setting, we observe that the unconditional Type-I errors of the LTO methods are below
\alpha
in all cases, whereas that of the approximate placebo
p
-value is always
1/N
. This suggests that the upper bound on the conditional Type-I error established in Theorem
2.2
is not tight, and in practice, the LTO procedures often satisfy Type-I error constraints. The randomized placebo test does control the Type-I error, but its power is quite poor relative to the other procedures.

5
Extensions

We discuss several extensions of the LTO placebo test. The first defines the notion of strange point in a different way, while the second leaves
r
points out at a time.

5.1
Rank-sum LTO placebo test

Instead of labeling a unit as strange if it wins too many matches, we can consider a unit to be strange if its rank across all matches is too large. This leads to a variant of the procedure which has similar coverage guarantees as the original Jackknife+ procedure; see Theorem 1 of
[
BCRT21
]
. Define
\rank\left\{I;I,i,j\right\}:=\mathbb{I}\left\{I\succ j\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left\{I\succ i\right\},
where in this context,
\left\{I\succ j\right\}:=\left\{R_{I,i,j;I}>R_{I,i,j;j}\right\}
and similarly for
\left\{I\succ i\right\}
. We can define a
p
-value

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}:=\frac{1}{(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[N]\setminus I\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}(2-\rank\left\{I;I,i,j\right\}),

Using a similar argument as in Theorem
2.2
, we derive an upper bound on the Type-I error of
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}
. The proof is presented in Appendix
B.4
.

Theorem 5.1

(Rank-sum LTO placebo test)
.

The Type-I error of
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}
has the following bound

\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}\leq\alpha)\leq\frac{\lfloor(N-1)\alpha\rfloor+1}{N}.

The bound is equal to or
1/N
larger than that of
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}
and
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{powered-LTO}}}(\alpha)
. In particular, when
\alpha<1/(N-1)
, all three choices have Type-I error controlled at
1/N
, as shown in Theorem
2.1
. However, it is easy to see that
2-\rank\left\{I;I,i,j\right\}\geq\mathbb{I}\{I\not\succ i,j\}
and thus
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}\leq p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}
almost surely. Thus,
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}
is less favorable because it always gives more conservative inference.

5.2
Leave-
r
-out placebo test

We may consider an analogous procedure for the placebo test where
r
-tuples of data points are left out. The analysis of the method in this setting clarifies the essential features of the LTO case.

Extending the LTO procedure (Remark
2.1
), we consider a tournament between all
N
units where matches are held for every
(r+1)
units. For each match among
\{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
, we leave out all participants and construct synthetic controls for each of them. The score
R_{\{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\};i_{j}}
of participant
i_{j}
is defined via the same form as (
5
). For example, we can calculate the RMSPE by using
[N]\setminus\{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
as the donor pool.
We say
i_{0}
wins a match among
\{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
, denoted by
i_{0}\succ i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
, if
R_{\{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\};i_{0}}>\max_{j\in[r]}R_{\{i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\};i_{j}}
.
Extending the LTO p-value, define

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LRO}}}=\frac{(N-r-1)!}{(N-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\in[N]\setminus I\\
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\text{ mutually distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{I}\{I\not\succ i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}.

(14)

Anagolously, we can also define the rank-sum LRO p-value:

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LRO}}}=\frac{(N-r-1)!}{(N-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\in[N]\setminus I\\
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\text{ mutually distinct}\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{r}\left(r-\rank\left\{I;I,i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right\}\right),

(15)

where
\rank\left\{I;I,i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\right\}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{I}\{I\succ i_{j}\}
and, in this context,
I\succ i_{j}
iff
R_{\{I,i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\};I}>\max_{j\in[r]}R_{\{I,i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\};i_{j}}
. Note that they recover
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LTO}}}
and
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LTO}}}
when
r=2
. Above, the normalization factor
2/r
is chosen to make the Type-I error to converge to
\alpha
when
N
tends to infinity, as evidenced by Theorem
5.3
below.

The following two results generalize Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
5.1
to
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LRO}}}
and
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LRO}}}
, respectively. Both proofs are presented in Appendix
B.5
. Via tedious algebra, we can show that they recover Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
5.1
when
r=2
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

s\mapsto A_{N,r}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\left[\binom{N}{r+1}-\binom{N-s}{r+1}\right].

Then
A_{N,r}(\cdot)
is decreasing on
[N]
and, for any
\alpha<r/(r+1)
,

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LRO}}}\leq\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{N}\max\left\{k:A_{N,r}(k)\geq(1-\alpha)\binom{N-1}{r}\right\}.

Theorem 5.3
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{rs-LRO}}}\leq\alpha)\leq\frac{\lfloor(N-1)\alpha\rfloor+1}{N}.

6
Conclusion and discussion

We propose a novel leave-two-out placebo test for the synthetic control method under uniform assignments. Compared to the standard placebo test based on permutation tests, the LTO placebo test has two advantages. First, it has the same theoretical conditional Type-I error guarantee for practical
(N,\alpha)
pairs; further, it often achieves a strictly lower unconditional Type-I error in practice, unlike the standard placebo test for which the guarantee is tight. In particular, this permits valid inference even in the challenging
\alpha<1/N
case. Second, we find empirically that it has higher power for moderately large signal-to-noise ratio and comparable power otherwise. In most of our experimental settings, the powered-LTO placebo test almost Pareto-improves the standard placebo test.

While the design assumption is strong, it does not require large sample size and/or long time series, which are crucial for outcome-based approaches but uncommon in applications of the synthetic control method. Moreover, unlike most outcome-based approaches, the LTO placebo test does not artificially simplify the important steps, like weight matrix estimation and model selection, in the construction of synthetic controls. On the other hand, we propose a weighted variant of the LTO placebo test and a sensitivity analysis to account for non-uniform assignments. Below we discuss a few other related issues.

6.1
In-time placebo test

The placebo tests discussed in previous sections all exploit variation in which units are treated. An alternative approach is to exploit the variation of treatment timing. In the literature, in-time placebo tests are often performed by choosing a pre-treatment period, pretending it to be the time when the treatment occurs, and running the same synthetic control procedure to eyeball check if the treated unit differs from its synthetic control between the fake and actual treatment time
[
FHR13
,
ADH15
,
CGMS17
,
CEL20
,
Cha23
]
. To formalize it into a rigorous statistical inference procedure, we can follow
[
FP18
]
verbatim by assuming the treatment time
T_{0}
is uniform in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
for some
1\leq t_{0}<t_{1}\leq T
. Then the standard and LTO placebo tests can both be directly applied in this setting. For in-time placebo tests, the small sample issue is arguably more pronounced because it is typically only reasonable to choose a small time window
[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Based on the promising performance of LTO placebo tests in unit-level placebo tests when the sample size is small, we expect they continue to excel for in-time placebo tests, though we leave the formal investigation for future work.

6.2
Testing non-sharp null and constructing confidence regions

In previous sections we focus on the sharp null (
4
). Following
[
FP18
,
CWZ21
]
, it is straightforward to test any postulated effect vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{T-T_{0}}
:

H_{0}(\theta):\tau_{I,T_{0}+1}=\theta_{1},\tau_{I,T_{0}+2}=\theta_{2},\ldots,\tau_{I,T}=\theta_{T-T_{0}}.

In fact, by subtracting
\theta_{j}
from
Y_{I(T_{0}+j)}(1)
, we are back to the case of sharp null. Phrased differently, we can redefine the RMSPE
R_{i,j,I;I}(\theta)
for the treated unit within the triple
\{i,j,I\}
by replacing
Y_{It}
by
Y_{It}-\theta_{t-T_{0}}
for
t=T_{0}+1,\ldots,T
and reject
H_{0}(\theta)
if
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}(\theta)\leq\alpha
, where

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}(\theta):=\frac{1}{(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[N]\setminus I\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{I}\left\{R_{i,j,I;I}(\theta)\leq\max(R_{i,j,I;i},R_{i,j,I;j})\right\},

(16)

Note that the other RMSPEs remain the same because the synthetic control weights only depend on pre-treatment outcomes which are unchanged.

By inverting the test, we can obtain a confidence region for
(\tau_{I,T_{0}+1},\tau_{I,T_{0}+2},\ldots,\tau_{I,T})
:

\mathcal{C}_{1-\alpha}=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{T-T_{0}}:p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}(\theta)>\alpha\right\}.

When the RMSPE is used, by (
16
), there exists
A_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{T-T_{0}}
and
b_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i,j\in[N]\setminus I
such that

\mathcal{C}_{1-\alpha}=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{T-T_{0}}:\sum_{i,j\in[N]\setminus I}\mathbb{I}\left\{\|\theta-A_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}\leq b_{ij}\right\}>\alpha(N-1)(N-2)\right\}.

Each indicator gives a ball and
\mathcal{C}_{1-\alpha}
is an union of intersections of these balls that lie in at least
\alpha(N-1)(N-2)
balls. By Theorem
2.2
,

\mathbb{P}\Big((\tau_{I,T_{0}+1},\tau_{I,T_{0}+2},\ldots,\tau_{I,T})\in\mathcal{C}_{1-\alpha}\Big)\geq 1-\frac{\lfloor Nf(N,\alpha)\rfloor}{N}.

6.3
Consistency of LTO and inconsistency of approximate placebo test

Power analysis is challenging in our setting as it typically relies on large sample approximation that tends to be uninformative when
N
and
T
are small, particularly given the complexity of the synthetic control procedure. In this section, we make an attempt to explain the empirically observed power improvement over the approximate placebo test.

In the classical asymptotic regime where
N
tends to infinity, a test is consistent if its power converges to
1
for any fixed alternative. Qualitatively, consistency ensures that the null hypothesis will be rejected with high probability as the problem becomes sufficiently easy.
We migrate the notion of consistency to the fixed-
N
setting by defining a test to be consistent if the power converges to
1
as the signal strength under the alternative grows to infinity. Consistency serves as a minimal requirement for a desirable test. We show that, if
\alpha<1/N
, the LTO placebo test is uniformly consistent, whereas the approximate placebo test is not. In fact, the latter may have nearly zero power even with an arbitrarily large signal strength.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
denote the distribution of
(Y_{i,t}(0))_{i\leq N,t\leq T}
. Consider an alternative hypothesis parametrized by a single parameter
\eta>0
:

H_{1,\eta}:Y_{i,t}(1)=Y_{i,t}(0)+\eta\tau_{i,t}

for some deterministic matrix
\tau=(\tau_{i,t})_{i\leq N,t\leq T}\in\Gamma
where
\Gamma
includes all matrices
\tau
with
\min_{i}\sum_{t}\tau_{it}^{2}\geq 1
. Assume
N,T
are fixed and
\alpha<1/N
. Consider the LTO and approximate placebo tests based on the post-RMSPE statistic defined in (
6
). Then, for any tight collection of distributions
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N\times T}
,

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow\infty}\inf_{G\in\mathcal{G},\tau\in\Gamma}\mathbb{P}_{G,\tau,H_{1,\eta}}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq\alpha)=1,

Furthermore, there exists infinitely many pairs
(G,\tau)\in\mathcal{G}\times\Gamma
that do not vary with
\eta
such that

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{G,\tau,H_{1,\eta}}(p_{\text{{\tiny app-placebo}}}\leq\alpha)\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}=0&\text{ if $N$ is even}\\
\leq 1/N&\text{if $N$ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

A proof may be found in Section
B.6
.

6.5
Leave-two-out test as a new type of randomization inference

Classical randomization inference
[
Fis37
,
Rom89
,
Rom90
,
Din17
]
exploits exchangeability, or group invariance more generally, by comparing the test statistic for the observed data with those for permuted or group-transformed data. Despite a century of development, nearly all randomization inference procedures are variants of this generic form with different choices of test statistics or group of transformations. The LTO placebo tests, or the more general leave-
r
-out tests, are new types of randomization inference that move beyond classical permutation or rank-based inference.

Our tests generalize the Jackknife+
[
BCRT21
]
, which also differs from classical randomization inference. It is an extension of conformal prediction
[
VGS05
]
and the Jackknife procedure
[
Wu86
]
to construct prediction intervals with guaranteed coverage under exchangeability.
Let
Z_{1}=(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,Z_{N}=(X_{N},Y_{N})
be exchangeable observations where
X_{i}
denotes a set of covariates and
Y_{i}
denotes the outcome. The goal is to construct a prediction interval
\hat{C}(X_{N+1})
such that
\mathbb{P}(Y_{N+1}\in\hat{C}(X_{N+1}))\geq 1-\alpha
assuming
Z_{N+1}=(X_{N+1},Y_{N+1})
is exchangeable with
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\}
. Jackknife+ essentially inverts a leave-one-out test:

p(Y_{N+1})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left\{R_{i,N+1;N+1}\leq R_{i,N+1;i}\right\},

where
R_{i,j;j}=|Y_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{-i,-j}(X_{j})|
where
\hat{\mu}_{-i,-j}(\cdot)
can be any model fit on
(Z_{k})_{k\in[N]\setminus\{i,j\}}
. However, Jackknife+ suffers from the same granularity issue as the standard placebo tests because it can only take multiples of
1/N
.

Another exception is the prediction inference of model transfer errors by
[
AFL+23
]
. They consider a setting with data from
N
exchangeable training domains (e.g., countries), denoted as
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}
. For a given economic or machine learning model and a pair of domains
Z_{i},Z_{j}
, let
e(Z_{i},Z_{j})
denote the accuracy (e.g., the mean square error) of the model fit on the
i
-th domain and evaluated on the
j
-th domain. Let
Z_{N+1}
denote an unobserved target domain that is exchangeable with
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\}
. The goal is to construct a prediction interval for the random transfer error
e(Z_{I},Z_{n+1})
where
I
is chosen from
[N]
uniformly at random. Their interval is constructed based on quantiles of
\{e(Z_{i},Z_{j}):i,j\in[N],i\neq j\}
even if they are no longer exchangeable as in classical randomization inference due to the bivariate structure.

•

Resulting Issues with Placebo Inference
. The predominant robustness check / inference methodology used among papers in the survey was the unit-level placebo test. Only a few papers did not use the placebo procedure. Moreover, some authors note that the placebo-based
p
-value cannot be created when using small levels
\alpha

[

]

p

0.04
[…]. Thus, the coefficients of interest in this study cannot be significant at a 1% level of significance.” Newiak and Williams
[

]
remark similarly: “Typically, however, there are not many countries in the control group for which we can construct counterfactuals with a fit that is at least as good as for the treated country, which limits the informativeness of this exercise (and prevents us from calculating meaningful p-values).”

•

B.1
An impossibility result on conditionally valid p-values

Proposition
B.1
gives the formal statement of the impossibility result mentioned in Section
1
.

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose
I
is uniform on
[N]
and let
\alpha<1/N
. Then any conditionally finite-sample-valid, non-randomized
p
-value
p(I)
has zero power:
\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})=0,
for any alternative
H_{1}:\tau_{i,T_{0}+1}=\theta_{1},\ldots,\tau_{i,T}=\theta_{T-T_{0}}
, for all
i=1,\dots,N
.

Here we write
\tau_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}(1)-Y_{i,t}(0)
and recall that
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the set of control potential outcomes.

Proof of Proposition
B.1
.

Let
p(I)
be such a
p
-value, written as a function of the index of treated unit
I
. We may also write
p(I,\mathcal{D})
to emphasize the panel dataset
\mathcal{D}
that is used to create the
p
-value. Let
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}
be equal to the panel dataset of control potential outcomes, with the vector
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{T-T_{0}})
added on the unit
i
in the post-treatment periods. Under
H_{1},
the treated unit
I
has observed data
Y_{I,t}=Y_{I,t}(0)+\theta_{t}
, while for all other units
i
, we observe
Y_{i,t}(0).
Conditioning on the value of
I,

\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbf{1}\left\{p(i,\mathcal{D}^{(i)})\leq\alpha\right\}.

(17)

Now, consider the conditional rejection probability under the sharp null
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y}).
This latter probability can be written as
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(I\in\mathcal{A})
for some event
\mathcal{A}\subset[N]
. When
I
is uniform on
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})
belongs to the set
\left\{0/N,1/N,\dots,N/N\right\}
. If
\alpha<1/N,\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})=0
because
p
is conditionally valid. Thus

0=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left\{p(i,\mathcal{Y})\leq\alpha\right\}

(18)

for every choice of
\mathcal{Y}
. Summing the above over
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left\{p(i,\mathcal{D}^{(j)})\leq\alpha\right\}=0,

which implies

\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(p(I)\leq\alpha|\mathcal{Y})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbf{1}\left\{p(i,\mathcal{D}^{(i)})\leq\alpha\right\}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left\{p(i,\mathcal{D}^{(j)})\leq\alpha\right\}=0,

(19)

as claimed, since all summands are non-negative.
∎

Full Proof of Thm.
2.2
.

Let us start with the decomposition of the sum outlined in Section
2
:

\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(I)}}+\overbrace{2\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(II)}}+\overbrace{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(III)}}.

For the first sum, note by renaming the labels that

\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i).
Thus

\text{(I)}\leq\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{3}\left(\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)+\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i)\right)\leq s(s-1)(s-2)/3.

For the second sum, swap the naming of the
i,k
labels and use the bound
\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ k,j)\leq 1
to see that

\text{(II)}\leq 2\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ k,j)\right)\leq s(s-1)(N-s).

Finally, in the last sum, we use the naive bound
\text{(III)}\leq s(N-s)(N-1-s).

Combining these bounds, canceling a factor of
s,
we find that

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)(N-1)(N-2)

\displaystyle\leq(s-1)(s-2)/3+(s-1)(N-s)+(N-s)(N-1-s)

\displaystyle=(s-1)(s-2)/3+(N-s)(N-2)

(20)

The right hand side reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}s^{2}+\frac{2}{3}-s(N-1)-2N+N^{2}.

Thus

\frac{1}{3}s^{2}+\frac{2}{3}-s(N-1)-2N+N^{2}-(N^{2}-3N+2)+\alpha(N-1)(N-2)\geq 0,

which reduces to

\frac{s^{2}}{3}-\frac{4}{3}-s(N-1)+N+\alpha(N-1)(N-2)\geq 0

Introducing
\beta=s/N
, we may reduce to the quadratic inequality

\frac{\beta^{2}}{3}-\beta(1-\frac{1}{N})-\frac{4}{3N^{2}}+\frac{1}{N}+\alpha(1-\frac{1}{N})(1-\frac{2}{N})\geq 0.

This is a parabola that opens upwards. When
\beta=1
, since
\alpha<2/3
, the value is upper bounded by

\frac{1}{3}-1+\frac{2}{N}-\frac{4}{3N^{2}}+\frac{2}{3}(1-\frac{1}{N})(1-\frac{2}{N})=0.

Thus,
\beta
must be upper bounded by the smaller root of the quadratic function. On the other hand, when
\beta=1/N
, the value is
\alpha(1-1/N)(1-2/N)>0
. Thus, the smaller root is positive. We can solve this root by quadratic formula:

\beta\leq\frac{3-\frac{3}{N}-\sqrt{9(1-\frac{1}{N})^{2}-12\left(-\frac{4}{3N^{2}}+\frac{1}{N}+\alpha(1-\frac{1}{N})(1-\frac{2}{N})\right)}}{2}.

(21)

To conclude, we note that
\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{naive-LTO}}}\leq\alpha)=\mathbb{P}(I\in\mathcal{S})=\beta.
The bound in equation (
10
) completes the proof.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We first analyze the refined weighted
p
-value. Because
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j\\
i,j\neq I\end{subarray}}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}=(1-\pi_{I})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq I}\pi_{l}^{2}
, the event that the
p
-value is less than or equal to
\alpha
is the same as the event that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\neq I\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}{(1-\pi_{I})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq I}\pi_{l}^{2}}\mathbb{I}\left\{I\succ i,j\right\}\geq 1-\alpha

Define a unit
k
to be strange if it satisfies the above bound in place of
I,
i.e.,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\neq k\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}{(1-\pi_{k})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq k}\pi_{l}^{2}}\mathbb{I}\left\{k\succ i,j\right\}\geq 1-\alpha

Call
\mathcal{S}
the set of strange points and
s=|\mathcal{S}|
. Let
\beta=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}=\mathbb{P}(p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{w-LTO}}}(\pi)\leq\alpha)
. Then multiply on both sides by
\pi_{k}((1-\pi_{k})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq k}\pi_{l}^{2})
and sum over
k\in\mathcal{S}
to obtain the inequality

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\neq k\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\pi_{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{I\succ j,k\right\}\geq(1-\alpha)\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}(1-\pi_{k})^{2}-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S},l\neq k}\pi_{k}\pi_{l}^{2}\right).

(22)

Throughout the rest of the proof, we omit the terms
i\neq j,
and
i,j\neq k
below the summations for notational convenience. That is, all summations over two or more indices requires all indices to be mutually distinct. The left hand side of the above may be split into three terms. The sums are over distinct indices.

\displaystyle\overbrace{\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(I)}}+\overbrace{2\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(II)}}+\overbrace{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)}^{\text{(III)}}.

For sum (I), split the sum into three copies of itself, and permute the labels cyclically. That is, by switching the indexing labels, we note
\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i)
. Next, because at most one of the residuals
R_{i,j,k;\cdot}
can be the largest,
\mathbb{I}(i\succ j,k)+\mathbb{I}(j\succ k,i)+\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)\leq 1.
This leads to the bound

\displaystyle\text{(I)}\leq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\pi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(\beta^{3}-3\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2}\pi_{j}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{3}\right)=\frac{1}{3}\left(\beta^{3}-3\beta\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{3}\right)

For the second sum (II), switching the labels of
i,k
gives the identity

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\mathbb{I}(i\succ k,j).

Bounding the indicator sum
\mathbb{I}(k\succ i,j)+\mathbb{I}(i\succ k,j)
by one, we have the bound

\text{(II)}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{k}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}=\left(\beta^{2}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2}\right)(1-\beta).

Lastly, we bound (III) by the naive bound; replacing the indicator by the constant
1
, the sum can be bounded by
\beta[(1-\beta)^{2}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{i}^{2}].

Summing these bounds, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\beta^{3}-\beta^{2}+\beta\left(1-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\pi_{i}^{2}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{3}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}(1-\pi_{k})^{2}-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S},l\neq k}\pi_{k}\pi_{l}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left(\beta-2\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}^{2}+2\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{k}^{3}-\beta\sum_{l=1}^{N}\pi_{l}^{2}\right).

After some simplification, the resulting bound gives

\frac{1}{3}\beta^{3}-\beta^{2}+\beta\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2}+\alpha\left(1-\sum_{l=1}^{N}\pi_{l}^{2}\right)\right]\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\alpha\right)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{3}-(1-2\alpha)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2}.

(23)

The proof is completed by replacing
\beta,\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{2},\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}^{3}
with
L_{1}(z),L_{2}(z),L_{3}(z)
, respectively.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\pi_{\ell}^{2}\geq 1/N
. Thus, (
23
) implies that

\frac{1}{3}\beta^{3}-\beta^{2}+\beta\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}\left[\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\alpha\right)\pi_{i}^{2}-(1+\beta-2\alpha)\pi_{i}\right].

Let
g(x)=\left(4/3-2\alpha\right)x^{2}-(1+\beta-2\alpha)x
. Then
g^{\prime}(x)=2\left(4/3-2\alpha\right)x-(1+\beta-2\alpha)
. Since
\beta\geq 0
and
\alpha\leq 1/3
, for any
x\leq 1/4
,
g^{\prime}(x)\leq 2/3-\alpha-(1-2\alpha)=\alpha-1/3\leq 0
. Thus,
g(x)
is decreasing on
[0,1/4]
. Since
\Gamma\leq N/4
,
\pi_{i}\leq 1/4
. Using
\pi_{i}\leq\frac{\Gamma}{N}
, we find that

\frac{1}{3}\beta^{3}-\beta^{2}+\beta\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{i}g\left(\frac{\Gamma}{N}\right)=g\left(\frac{\Gamma}{N}\right)\beta.

Dividing both sides by
\beta
, we have that

\frac{1}{3}\beta^{2}-\beta+\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\alpha\right)\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{N^{2}}-(1+\beta-2\alpha)\frac{\Gamma}{N}

(24)

\Longrightarrow\frac{1}{3}\beta^{2}-\left(1-\frac{\Gamma}{N}\right)\beta+\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}-\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\alpha\right)\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{N^{2}}+(1-2\alpha)\frac{\Gamma}{N}\geq 0.

Again, this is a parabola that opens upwards. When
\beta=1
, the LHS is equal to

-\frac{2}{3}+\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}+\tilde{g}\left(\frac{\Gamma}{N}\right),\,\,\text{where }\tilde{g}(x)=(2-2\alpha)x-\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\alpha\right)x^{2}.

For any
x\leq 1/4
,
\tilde{g}^{\prime}(x)=(2-2\alpha)-2(4/3-2\alpha)x>(2-2\alpha)-(4/3-2\alpha)>0
. Thus,
\tilde{g}(x)
is increasing on
[0,1/4]
and

-\frac{2}{3}+\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}+\tilde{g}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=-\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{N-1}{N}-\frac{3}{8}\right)\alpha\leq-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{5\alpha}{8}<0,

where the last inequality uses the condition
\alpha\leq 1/3
. As a result, (
24

\beta
is upper bounded by the smaller root, i.e.,

\beta\leq\frac{3-\frac{3\Gamma}{N}-\sqrt{9(1-\frac{\Gamma}{N})^{2}-12\left(-\frac{4\Gamma^{2}}{3N^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma}{N}+\alpha\left(1-\frac{2\Gamma+1}{N}+\frac{2\Gamma^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)\right)}}{2}.

∎

26

\mathcal{S}\cap\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}

(27)

Swapping the naming of

B.7
Minor lemmas

Lemma B.1
.

For any integers
N>r>0
, the sequence

s\mapsto A_{N,r}(s):=\frac{1}{s}\left[\binom{N}{r+1}-\binom{N-s}{r+1}\right]

is decreasing.

Proof.

Observe the two following identities on binomial coefficients:

\displaystyle\binom{N-s}{r+1}=\sum_{k=r}^{N-1-s}\binom{k}{r}

\displaystyle s\binom{N-1-s}{r}\leq\sum_{k^{\prime}=N-s}^{N-1}\binom{k^{\prime}}{r}.

The first is true because of the hockey-stick identity, and the second is by monotonicity. Summing these inequalities, we find

\binom{N-s}{r+1}+s\binom{N-1-s}{r}\leq\sum_{k=r}^{N-1}\binom{k}{r}=\binom{N}{r+1},

which is equivalent to

\binom{N-s}{r+1}+s\left[\binom{N-s}{r+1}-\binom{N-1-s}{r+1}\right]\leq(s+1)\binom{N}{r+1}-s\binom{N}{r+1}.

Rearranging,
A_{N,r}(s)\geq A_{N,r}(s+1)
.
∎
