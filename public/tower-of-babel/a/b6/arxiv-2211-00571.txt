Simplicial distributions, convex categories and contextuality
Aziz Kharoof, Cihan Okay

Abstract
The data of a physical experiment can be represented as a presheaf of probability distributions. A striking feature of quantum theory is that those probability distributions obtained in quantum mechanical experiments do not always admit a joint probability distribution, a celebrated observation due to Bell. Such distributions are called contextual. Simplicial distributions are combinatorial models that extend presheaves of probability distributions by elevating sets of measurements and outcomes to spaces. Contextuality can be defined in this generalized setting. This paper introduces the notion of convex categories to study simplicial distributions from a categorical perspective. Simplicial distributions can be given the structure of a convex monoid, a convex category with a single object, when the outcome space has the structure of a group. We describe contextuality as a monoid-theoretic notion by introducing a weak version of invertibility for monoids. Our main result is that a simplicial distribution is noncontextual if and only if it is weakly invertible. Similarly, strong contextuality and contextual fraction can be characterized in terms of invertibility in monoids. Finally, we show that simplicial homotopy can be used to detect extremal simplicial distributions refining the earlier methods based on Čech cohomology and the cohomology of groups.

Introduction

Physical experiments are probabilistic: When a measurement is performed, the corresponding outcome occurs with a certain probability.
Quantum theory
comes with an additional constraint prohibiting certain measurements from being performed jointly.
Therefore in a quantum mechanical experiment, the data that describes the outcome probabilities consists of a family of probability distributions indexed over subsets of measurements that are allowed to be performed jointly.
More precisely, this family is a presheaf of probability distributions since the restriction of probabilities to a smaller subset of measurements, also called marginalization, is compatible.
A striking phenomenon in physics, known as Bell’s nonlocality and its generalization called contextuality, can be expressed as the nonexistence of a joint probability distribution over the set of all measurements that marginalizes to the distributions of the restricted set of measurements obtained from the experiment.
Such a joint distribution always exists in classical theories.
In particular, a joint distribution provides a model where all
measurement outcomes are assigned before the measurement takes place, and the measurement probabilities are obtained by considering all such global assignments with a certain probability.
It is a celebrated result of Bell
[
Bel64
]
that in quantum theory, the joint distribution does not always exist, i.e., there are contextual families of distributions.
Another celebrated result due to Kochen–Specker
[
KS67
]
proves a similar result by showing the impossibility of the global assignments of outcomes in quantum theory. The latter demonstrates a stronger version of contextuality.

There are various approaches to formalizing contextuality.
The presheaf approach is introduced in
[
AB11
]
.
The ingredients in this approach are (1) a simplicial complex
\Sigma
whose vertices represent the set of all measurements, and its simplices are those that can be jointly performed, and (2) a finite set of outcomes for the measurements.
For the outcome set, we don’t lose any generality by considering the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
of integers modulo
d
.
A distribution on
(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{d})
is a presheaf of distributions, i.e., a family
(p_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Sigma}
together with a compatibility condition.
Each
p_{\sigma}
is a distribution on the set of all functions
\sigma\to\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.
More formally, let
D_{R}
denote the distribution monad on the category of sets
[
Jac10
]
, where
R
is a commutative semiring.
Then
p_{\sigma}
belongs to
D_{R}(\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{\sigma})
where
R=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the semiring of nonnegative reals.
The presheaf approach uses tools from Čech cohomology to study contextuality.
Furthermore, this systematic study introduces degrees of contextuality, such as strong contextuality and
the more refined measure of contextuality known as the contextual fraction.
Another approach to contextuality is the topological approach of
[
ORBR17
]
based on techniques from the cohomology of groups.
This approach introduces cohomology classes that can detect strong contextuality but fail to capture weaker versions, e.g., the famous Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) scenario
[
CHSH69
]
.
In
[
OKI22
]
both approaches are unified under the theory of simplicial distributions.
This theory goes beyond the usual assumption that measurements and outcomes are represented by finite sets. In this framework, one can study distributions on spaces of measurements and outcomes, where a space is represented by a simplicial set.
Simplicial sets are combinatorial objects more expressive than simplicial complexes. They are fundamental objects in modern homotopy theory
[
GJ09
]
.
A simplicial distribution is defined on a pair
(X,Y)
of simplicial sets, where
X
represents the measurements and
Y
the outcomes.
The distribution monad can be elevated to a monad on the category of simplicial sets. Given
Y
, one can define another simplicial set
D_{R}(Y)
whose simplices are distributions on the set of simplices of
Y
.
A simplicial distribution is a morphism of simplicial sets

p:X\to D_{R}(Y).

In this paper, we study simplicial distributions from a categorical perspective.

For a semiring
R
the algebras of the distribution monad
D_{R}:\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
are called
R
-convex sets. This notion generalizes the usual notion of convexity for
R=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Let
\mathbf{Conv}_{R}
denote the category of
R
-convex sets.
Every monad has an associated Kleisli category.
In the case of
D_{R}
, the morphisms of the Kleisli category
s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
are the simplicial distributions, i.e., for simplicial sets
X,Y
the set
\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}(X,Y)
of morphisms is given by simplicial set morphisms
X\to D_{R}(Y)
.
Let
s\mathbf{Conv}_{R}
denote the category of simplicial
R
-convex sets. The main examples of such simplicial objects are
D_{R}(Y)
for some simplicial set
Y
.

Proposition 1.1
.

The functor sending a pair
(X,Y)
of simplicial sets to the set
s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y)
of simplicial set morphisms restricts
to a functor

s\mathbf{Set}(-,-):s\mathbf{Set}^{op}\times s\mathbf{Conv}_{R}\to\mathbf{Conv}_{R}

The main application of this result is to the set of simplicial distributions. By this result the set
s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))
is an
R
-convex set.
Contextuality for simplicial distributions is defined using a comparison map

\Theta_{X,Y}:D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y))\to s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y)).

Under this map, the delta distribution at a simplicial set morphism
\varphi:X\to Y
is sent to the simplicial distribution given by the composition
X\xrightarrow{\varphi}Y\xrightarrow{\delta_{Y}}D_{R}(Y)
, called a deterministic distribution.
Since the target is
R
-convex,
\Theta_{X,Y}
is the unique extension to the free
D_{R}
-algebra, the domain of the map.
A simplicial distribution is called contextual if it lies in the image of
\Theta_{X,Y}
; otherwise called noncontextual.
We show in Section
2.4
how to realize a presheaf of distributions as a simplicial distribution.
In Theorem
2.18
we show that the notion of contextuality for simplicial distributions specializes to the notion for presheaves of distributions originally introduced in
[
AB11
]
.

A category-theoretic point of view suggests lifting our analysis from the level of morphism sets to the level of categories.
For this, we introduce the notion of convex categories.
The first step is to lift
D_{R}
to a monad on the category
\mathbf{Cat}
of (locally small) categories (Corollary
3.7
).
Then we define an
R
-convex category as a
D_{R}
-algebra in
\mathbf{Cat}
.
Every category enriched over the category of
R
-convex sets is an
R
-convex category. However, the converse does not always hold. The prominent example of a convex category is the Kleisli category
\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
, and its simplicial version
s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
, which are not enriched over
\mathbf{Conv}_{R}
.
We can think of
\Theta_{X,Y}
assembled into a morphism in
\mathbf{ConvCat}_{R}
from the free
R
-convex category to the Kleisli category, both of which obtained from the category of simplicial sets:

\Theta:D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set})\to s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}.

For outcome spaces which also have a group structure the convex set of simplicial distributions can be given a monoid structure.
Our categorical framework combined with this monoid structure has interesting applications connecting contextuality to a weak notion of invertibility in convex monoids.
As we show in Section
2.4
any presheaf of distribution can be realized as a simplicial distribution

p:X\to D_{R}(N\mathbb{Z}_{d}),

where
N\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is the nerve of the additive group
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.
The simplicial set
N\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is, in fact, a simplicial group.
The group structure on the nerve induces a monoid structure on the convex set
s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(N\mathbb{Z}_{d}))
of simplicial distributions.
We remark that this monoid structure is new and has not been investigated in the study of contextuality.
More precisely,
s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(N\mathbb{Z}_{d}))
is a convex monoid, i.e., a convex category with a single object.
For a convex monoid
M
, with the map
\pi^{M}:D_{R}(M)\to M
giving the
D_{R}
-algebra structure, an element
m\in M
is called weakly invertible if it lies in the image of the composite
D_{R}(M^{*})\xhookrightarrow{D_{R}(i_{M})}D_{R}(M)\xrightarrow{\pi^{M}}M
, where
i_{M}:M^{*}\hookrightarrow M
is the inclusion of the units.
Our main result connects weak invertibility to noncontextuality.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
R
be a
zero-sum-free, integral semiring.
Given a simplicial set
X
and a simplicial group
Y
, a distribution
p\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))
is noncontextual if and only if
p
is weakly invertible.

Moreover, we introduce the notion of strong invertibility, a monoid-theoretic description of strong contextuality. We also introduce a degree of invertibility called invertible fraction which generalizes the notion of noncontextual fraction introduced in
[
AB11
,
ABM17
]
.
Simplicial distributions are formulated using the theory of simplicial sets.
It is a natural question to understand the role of homotopy in the context of simplicial distributions.
Embedding presheaves of distributions to our simplicial framework makes homotopical tools available for the study of contextuality.
In Corollary
5.23
we show that simplicial homotopy can be used to detect extremal distributions, a question of fundamental importance in the study of polytopes of distributions; see
[
Pit89
,
BLM+05
,
JM05
,
ABK+16
]
.

Our paper is organized as follows: In Section
2.1
we recall basics from convex sets and in Section
2.2
we introduce simplicial distributions. Proposition
1.1
(Proposition
2.15
) is proved in Section
2.3
. In Section
2.4
, we show how to describe a presheaf of distributions as a simplicial distribution.
In this section, we also provide examples of simplicial distributions, such as the CHSH scenario (Example
2.22
). Convex categories are introduced in Section
3.1
. In Section
3.2
we show that the Kleisli category
s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
is a convex category.
Weak invertibility, strong invertibility and invertible fraction are introduced in Sections
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
; respectively. Our main result Theorem
1.2
(Theorem
5.8
) is proved in Section
5.1
. The relationship between strong invertibility and strong contextuality is studied in Section
5.2
.
Extremal simplicial distributions and the role of simplicial homotopy are discussed in Section

.

2.1.1
Convex sets

Main examples of interest are the semiring of nonnegative reals
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and the Boolean algebra
\mathbb{B}=\{0,1\}
.
The
distribution monad

D_{R}:\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
is defined as follows:

•

For a set
X
the set
D_{R}(X)
of
R
-distributions on
X
consists of functions
p:X\to R
of finite support, i.e.,
|\{x\in X:\,p(x)\neq 0\}|<\infty

\sum_{x\in X}p(x)=1
.

•

Given a function
f:X\to Y
the function
D_{R}(f):D_{R}(X)\to D_{R}(Y)
is defined by

p\mapsto\left(y\mapsto\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}p(x)\right).

The structure maps of the monad are given as follows:

•

\delta_{X}:X\to D_{R}(X)
sends
x\in X
to the delta distribution

\delta^{x}(x^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&x^{\prime}=x,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

•

\mu_{X}:D_{R}^{2}(X)\to D_{R}(X)
sends a distribution
P
to the distribution

D_{R}(P)(x)=\sum_{p\in D_{R}(X)}P(p)p(x).

2.1.2
Real convex sets

We recall the definition of real convex sets from
[

consists of a set X together with a ternary operation

\langle-,-,-\rangle:[0,1]\times X\times X\to X

satisfying the following requirements, for

\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
and

:

1.

\langle\alpha,x,y\rangle=\langle 1-\alpha,y,x\rangle
.

2.

\langle\alpha,x,x\rangle=x
.

3.

\langle 0,x,y\rangle=y
.

4.

If
\alpha+(1-\alpha)\beta\neq 0
, then

\langle\alpha,x,\langle\beta,y,z\rangle\rangle=\langle\alpha+(1-\alpha)\beta,\langle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+(1-\alpha)\beta},x,y\rangle,z\rangle.

Given a real convex set

instead of
\langle\alpha,x,y\rangle
.
A morphism of convex sets
is
an function
f:X\to Y
satisfying

f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)x^{\prime})=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(x^{\prime}).

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
and

. This yields the category of real convex sets denoted by

\mathbf{Conv}
.

(

)

Conversely, an

p_{n}(x)=\sum_{\varphi\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y)}d(\varphi)\delta^{\varphi_{n}(x)}.

Then
\psi:X\to Y
with

will belong to the support of

since

p_{n}(x)(\psi_{n}(x))=\sum_{\varphi\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y)}d(\varphi)\delta^{\varphi_{n}(x)}(\psi_{n}(x))=d(\psi)+\sum_{\varphi\neq\psi}d(\varphi)\delta^{\varphi_{n}(x)}(\psi_{n}(x))\neq 0.

∎

For simplicial probability distributions one can introduce a degree of contextuality generalizing the notion introduced in
[
AB11
, Subsection 6.1]
.

Definition 2.13
.

The
noncontextual fraction
of a simplicial probability distribution

, which is denoted
by
\operatorname{NCF}(p)
, is defined to be the supremum of
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

where
q
and

noncontextual.

of

2.4
Simplicial distributions from presheaves of distributions

Simplicial presheaf of distributions can be constructed from the following data:

•

A simplicial complex
\Sigma
.

•

The ring
\mathbb{Z}_{d}=\{0,1,\cdots,d-1\}
of integers mod
d\geq 2
.

We will write
\Sigma_{0}
to denote the set of vertices of the simplicial complex. We will think of
\Sigma
as a category
\mathbf{C}_{\Sigma}
whose objects are the simplices
\sigma\in\Sigma
and morphisms are inclusions
\sigma\hookrightarrow\sigma^{\prime}
.
An element
p
of the inverse limit of the composite functor

\mathbf{C}_{\Sigma}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{-}}\mathbf{Set}\xrightarrow{D_{R}}\mathbf{Set}

is called an
R
-distribution on
(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{d})
(also known as an
empirical model
).
Let
V\subset U
be two subsets of
\Sigma_{0}
and
i:V\hookrightarrow U
denote the inclusion map.
For
p\in D_{R}(\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{U})
we will write
p|_{V}
for the distribution
D_{R}(i^{*})(p)\in D_{R}(\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{V})
, where
i^{*}:\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{U}\to\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{V}
is the restriction map.
Then, a presheaf of distributions consists of a tuple
p=(p_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Sigma}
of distributions
p_{\sigma}\in D_{R}(\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{\sigma})
such that

p_{\sigma}|_{\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}}=p_{\sigma^{\prime}}|_{\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}},\;\;\forall\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma,

We write
D_{R}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{d})

for the set of
R
-distributions on
(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{d})
.
For more details see
[
AB11
]
.

For a set
U
let
\Delta_{U}
denote the simplicial set whose
n
-simplices are given by the set
U^{n+1}
and
the simplicial structure maps are given by

\displaystyle d_{i}(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle s_{j}(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{j-1},x_{j},x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{n}).

For the next definition we will use the following notation: For
p:X\to D_{R}Y
and
\sigma\in X_{n}
we write
p_{\sigma}
for the distribution on
D_{R}Y_{n}
.

Construction 2.17

(Realization)
.

Given
(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{d})
we consider a pair of simplicial sets
(X_{\Sigma},Y)
where
X_{\Sigma}
is the subsimplicial set of
\Delta_{\Sigma_{0}}
whose
n
-simplices are given by

We define a function

by sending

to the simplicial distribution

\real(p):X_{\Sigma}\to D_{R}(\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}_{d}})

defined by

\real(p)_{(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})}(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})=p_{\{x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}}(s:x_{i}\mapsto a_{i}).

.

Let

be an

-distribution on

is noncontextual if and only if there exists

For

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}(X_{\Sigma},\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}_{d}}))}

3
Convex categories

In this paper by a category we mean a locally small category in the sense that the collection of morphisms between any two objects forms a set. Let
\mathbf{Cat}
denote the category of (locally small) categories. In this section we introduce the notion of convex categories by upgrading the distributions functor
D_{R}
to a monad on
\mathbf{Cat}
.

3.1
Convex categories

Let
X
and
Y
be sets. We define a multiplication map

m_{X,Y}:D_{R}(X)\times D_{R}(Y)\to D_{R}(X\times Y)
, by sending

(p,q)
to the distribution
p\cdot q
given by

(20)

(p\cdot q)(x,y)=p(x)q(y),\;\;x\in X,\;y\in Y.

This is a well-defined distribution since

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}(p\cdot q)(x,y)=\sum_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}p(x)q(y)=\sum_{x\in X}p(x)\sum_{y\in Y}q(y)=1

and
the support of
p\cdot q
is
a subset of the finite set

\{(x,y):\,p(x)\neq 0,\;q(y)\neq 0\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The map
m_{X,Y}
is a section of
D_{R}(r_{1})\times D_{R}(r_{2}):D_{R}(X\times Y)\to D_{R}(X)\times D_{R}(Y)
, where

r_{1}:X\times Y\to X
and
r_{2}:X\times Y\to Y
are the canonical projection maps.

Proof.

Given
(p,q)\in D_{R}(X)\times D_{R}(Y)
,
we have

(D_{R}(r_{1})(p\cdot q))(x)=\sum_{y\in Y}(p\cdot q)(x,y)=\sum_{y\in Y}p(x)q(y)=p(x).

Similarly
D_{R}(r_{2})(p\cdot q)=q
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a category. For objects
X,Y,Z
of
\mathbf{C}
we define a map

(21)

\ast:D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(X,Y))\times D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(Y,Z))\to D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(X,Z)),\;\;{(p,q)\mapsto q\ast p},

to be the composite of

m_{C(X,Y),C(Y,Z)}
with
D_{R}(\circ)
,
where
\circ
is the composition in
\mathbf{C}
.
So we have

(22)

(q\ast p)(f)=\sum_{\,g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f}q(g_{2})p(g_{1}),\;\;\;\forall f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Z).

where the sum runs over morphisms
g_{1}:X\to Y
and
g_{2}:Y\to Z
such that
g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f
in
\mathbf{C}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given
X,Y,Z
, objects in a category
\mathbf{C}
, and given morphisms

p=\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}\alpha_{f}\delta^{f}\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(X,Y))
and

q=\sum_{g\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\beta_{g}\delta^{g}\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(Y,Z))
we have

\left(\sum_{g\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\beta_{g}\delta^{g}\right)\ast\left(\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}\alpha_{f}\delta^{f}\right)=\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y),\,g\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\beta_{g}\alpha_{f}\,\delta^{g\circ f}.

In particular,

\delta^{g}\ast\delta^{f}=\delta^{g\circ f}
.

Proof.

Given
h\in\mathbf{C}(X,Z)
we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{g\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\beta_{g}\delta^{g}\right)\ast\left(\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}\alpha_{f}\delta^{f}\right)(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=h}\left(\sum_{g\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\beta_{g}\delta^{g}\right)(h_{2})\cdot\left(\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}\alpha_{f}\delta^{f}\right)(h_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=h}\beta_{h_{2}}\cdot\alpha_{h_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h_{1}\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y),\,h_{2}\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\beta_{h_{2}}\alpha_{h_{1}}\delta^{h_{2}\circ h_{1}}(h).

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a category. Consider the data consisting of

•

the collection of objects of
\mathbf{C}
,

•

for objects
X,Y
of
\mathbf{C}
the set
D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(X,Y))
of morphisms.

This data together with Equation (
22
) as the composition map specifies a category
D_{R}(\mathbf{C})
.

Proof.

The identity morphism in
D_{R}(\mathbf{C})(X,X)
is given by the delta distribution
\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{X}}
.
Given
p\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C})(X,Y)
and
f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)
we have

(\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{Y}}\ast p)(f)=\sum_{g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f}\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{Y}}(g_{2})p(g_{1})=\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{Y}}(\operatorname{Id}_{Y})p(f)=p(f).

Similarly, we have
p\ast\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{X}}=p
.
For associativity we check that for

s\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C})(X,Y)
,
q\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C})(Y,Z)
and
p\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C})(Z,W)
we have

\displaystyle((p\ast q)\ast s)(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f}(p\ast q)(g_{2})s(g_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f}\left(\sum_{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=g_{2}}p(h_{2})q(h_{1})\right)s(g_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f}\sum_{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=g_{2}}p(h_{2})q(h_{1})s(g_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{h_{2}\circ h_{1}\circ g_{1}=f}p(h_{2})q(h_{1})s(g_{1}).

A similar calculation shows that
(p\ast(q\ast s))(f)
gives the same result.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Given a functor
F:\mathbf{C}_{1}\to\mathbf{C}_{2}
in
\mathbf{Cat}
let us define the following maps:

•

D_{R}(F):\operatorname{Obj}(D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{1}))\to\operatorname{Obj}(D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{2}))
defined as the map
F
between the objects of the categories.

•

D_{R}(F)_{X,Y}:D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{1})(X,Y)\to D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{2})(FX,FY)
defined by applying
D_{R}
functor to the map
F_{X,Y}
between the morphism sets.

Then these maps specify a functor
D_{R}(F):D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{1})\to D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{2})
.

Proof.

Given
X\in\operatorname{Obj}(D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{1}))
, we have

D_{R}(F)(\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{X}})(f)=\sum_{F(g)=f}\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{X}}(g)=\begin{cases}1&f=\operatorname{Id}_{F(X)}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Therefore
D_{R}(F)(\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{X}})=\delta^{\operatorname{Id}_{F(X)}}
.
Now given
p\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{1})(Y,Z)
,
q\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C}_{1})(X,Y)
and
f\in\mathbf{C}_{2}(FX,FZ)
, then

\displaystyle D_{R}(F)(p\ast q)(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F(g)=f}(p\ast q)(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F(g)=f}\sum_{{f_{2}\circ f_{1}=g}}p(f_{2})\cdot q(f_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{F(f_{2}\circ f_{1})=f}p(f_{2})\cdot q(f_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{F(f_{2})\circ F(f_{1})=f}p(f_{2})\cdot q(f_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=f}}\sum_{F(f_{2})=h_{2},\,F(f_{1})=h_{1}}p(f_{2})\cdot q(f_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=f}}\left(\sum_{{F(f_{2})=h_{2}}}p(f_{2})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{{F(f_{1})=h_{1}}}q(f_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{h_{2}\circ h_{1}=f}}D_{R}(F)(p)(h_{2})\cdot D_{R}(F)(q)(h_{1})

\displaystyle=(D_{R}(F)(p)\ast D_{R}(F)(q))(f).

∎

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in D_{R}(\mathbf{C}(X,Z))}\sum_{p_{2}\ast p_{1}=q}Q_{2}(p_{2})\cdot Q_{1}(p_{1})\sum_{g_{2}\circ g_{1}=f}p_{2}(g_{2})\cdot p_{1}(g_{1})

\displaystyle=(\mu_{\mathbf{C}}(Q_{2})\ast\mu_{\mathbf{C}}(Q_{1}))(f).

The naturality of

and

Proposition 3.12

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a category such that the set
\mathbf{C}(X,Y)
of morphisms is a real convex set for all objects

of
\mathbf{C}

Let
\pi^{\mathbf{C}}:D(\mathbf{C})\to\mathbf{C}
be defined as in

is the corresponding structure map of

when regarded

Then the following statements are equivalent.

1.

(\mathbf{C},\pi^{\mathbf{C}})
is an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-convex category.

2.

For objects

of
\mathbf{C}
, morphisms
f_{i}\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)
,
g_{i}\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)
, and numbers
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in[0,1]
, where

, satisfying

\left(\sum_{i}\beta_{i}g_{i}\right)\circ\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}f_{i}\right)=\sum_{i,j}\beta_{j}\alpha_{i}\,g_{j}\circ f_{i}.

3.

For objects
X,Y,Z
of
\mathbf{C}
, morphisms
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)
,
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)

\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
we have the following equality:

\displaystyle(\beta g_{1}+(1-\beta)g_{2})

\displaystyle\circ(\alpha f_{1}+(1-\alpha)f_{2})

\displaystyle=\beta\alpha g_{1}\circ f_{1}+\beta(1-\alpha)g_{1}\circ f_{2}+(1-\beta)\alpha g_{2}\circ f_{1}+(1-\beta)(1-\alpha)g_{2}\circ f_{2}.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
(1)
implies
(2)
. Let
X,Y,Z\in\operatorname{Obj}(\mathbf{C})
,
f_{i}\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)
,
g_{i}\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)
,
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in[0,1]
, where
1\leq i\leq k
and

\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}=1
.
Using Lemma
3.2
and the functoriality of
\pi^{\mathbf{C}}
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\beta_{j}\alpha_{i}\,g_{j}\circ f_{i}

\displaystyle=\pi^{\mathbf{C}(X,Z)}\left(\sum_{i,j}\beta_{j}\alpha_{i}\delta^{g_{j}\circ f_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi^{\mathbf{C}(X,Z)}\left((\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\delta^{g_{j}})\ast(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\delta^{f_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle=\pi^{\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}\left(\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\delta^{g_{j}}\right)\circ\pi^{\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\delta^{f_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j}\beta_{j}g_{j}\right)\circ\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}f_{i}\right).

Conversely, assume that
(2)
holds. By

3.9
it is enough to prove that
\pi^{C}
is a functor. Given

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathbf{C}(X,Z)}(q\ast p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Z)}(q\ast p)(f)f

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in\mathbf{C}(X,Z)}\left(\sum_{g_{1}\circ g_{1}=f}q(g_{2})p(g_{1})\right)f

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{1}\in C(X,Y),\,g_{2}\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}q(g_{2})p(g_{1})g_{2}\circ g_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{g_{2}\in\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}q(g_{2})g_{2}\right)\circ\left(\sum_{g_{1}\in\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}p(g_{1})g_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi^{\mathbf{C}(Y,Z)}(q)\circ\pi^{\mathbf{C}(X,Y)}(p)

Finally, note that
(3)
is a special case of
(2)
and one can get
(2)
from
(3)
by induction.
∎

We will write
\mathbf{ConvCat}
for the category
\mathbf{ConvCat}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}

and refer to the objects of this category as real convex categories.
Note that Proposition
3.12

implies that
the category of
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces with the canonical convex structure on the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space of linear maps is a real convex category.

3.2
Kleisli category as a convex category

Recall that the Kleisli category
\mathbf{C}_{T}
of a monad
T:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
has the same objects as
\mathbf{C}
and for objects
X,Y
its morphisms are given by
\mathbf{C}(X,TY)
. For an object
X
the identity morphism in
\mathbf{C}_{T}(X,X)
is given by
\delta_{X}:X\to TX
. The composition of two morphisms
f:X\to TY
and
g:Y\to TZ
is defined by

g\diamond f:X\xrightarrow{f}TY\xrightarrow{T(g)}T(TZ)\xrightarrow{\mu_{Z}}TZ.

We are interested in the Kleisli category of the distribution monad
D_{R}:s\mathbf{Set}\to s\mathbf{Set}
.
In this case the composition can be explicitly written as

(q\diamond p)_{n}(x)=\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}p_{n}(x)(y)q_{n}(y),

where
p\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))
,
q\in s\mathbf{Set}(Y,D_{R}(Z))
and
x\in X_{n}
.

Proposition 3.13
.

The Kleisli category
s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
is an
R
-convex
category.

Proof.

The map
\pi:D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}})\to s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
which employs
s\mathbf{Set}_{D_{R}}
with the structure of an
R
-convex category is defined to be identity on the collection of objects. For simplicial sets
X,Y
the map

\pi_{X,Y}:D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y)))\to s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))

is defined to be
\pi^{s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))}

(see Equation (
9
) and Proposition
2.15
).
We need to show, as a consequence Proposition
3.9
, that
\pi
is a functor. That is, for all
P\in D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}Y))
and
Q\in D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}(Y,D_{R}Z))
we have
\pi(Q\ast P)=\pi(Q)\diamond\pi(P)
.
This follows from the following computation: For
x\in X_{n}
we have

\displaystyle\pi(Q\ast P)_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Z))}(Q\ast P)(p)p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Z))}\sum_{q^{\prime}\diamond q=p}Q(q^{\prime})P(q)p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y)),\,q^{\prime}\in s\mathbf{Set}(Y,D_{R}(Z))}Q(q^{\prime})P(q)(q^{\prime}\diamond q)_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}P(q)Q(q^{\prime})\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}q_{n}(x)(y)q^{\prime}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}\sum_{q\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))}P(q)q_{n}(x)(y)\sum_{q^{\prime}\in s\mathbf{Set}(Y,D_{R}(Z))}Q(q^{\prime})q^{\prime}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}\pi(P)_{n}(x)(y)\pi(Q)_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=(\pi(Q)\diamond\pi(P))_{n}(x).

∎

Let
F_{T}:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}_{T}
denote the functor defined as follows:

•

\operatorname{Obj}(\mathbf{C})\to\operatorname{Obj}(\mathbf{C}_{T})
is the identity functor.

•

(\delta_{Y})_{\ast}
, that is

is sent to the composite

(see

, Lemma 5.2.11]
).
In fact, the monad

arises from this adjunction.
In the following we will consider the free convex category

, and

, which is also a convex category (Proposition

).
The

Proposition 3.14
.

The transpose of the functor

4
Convex monoids

In Section
3
we introduced the notion of a convex category. Now, in this section we will specialize to convex monoids, that is convex categories with a single object. We will introduce a weak notion of invertibility for convex monoids.
This definition is inspired by the definition of noncontextuality for simplicial distributions. Later in Section
5
we will see that the two notions coincide for cases of interest. For real convex monoids we introduce the notion of invertible fraction to quantify the closeness of an element to being invertible.

The monad
D_{R}
acting on
\mathbf{Cat}
restricts to a monad on the category
\mathbf{Mon}
of monoids.
A
D_{R}
-algebra
(M,\pi^{M})
over this monad is called an
R
-convex monoid
.
By Proposition
3.9
this is equivalent to saying that
(M,\pi^{M})
is an
R
-convex set and the map
\pi^{M}:D_{R}(M)\to M
is a homomorphism of monoids.
We will write
\mathbf{ConvMon}_{R}
for the category of
R
-convex monoids. Proposition
3.12
specializes to give the following result.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
(M,\cdot)
be a monoid which is also a real convex set.
Let
\pi^{M}
denote
the structure map of
M
when regarded as an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-convex set.
Then the following statements are equivalent.

1.

(M,\pi^{M})
is an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-convex monoid.

2.

For
m_{i},n_{i}\in M
and

\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in[0,1]
, where
1\leq i\leq k

and
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}=1
,
we have

\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}m_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{i}\beta_{i}n_{i}\right)=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\,m_{i}\cdot n_{j}

3.

For
m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2}\in M
and

\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
we have the following equality

\displaystyle(\alpha m_{1}+(1-\alpha)m_{2})

\displaystyle\cdot(\beta n_{1}+(1-\beta)n_{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha\beta m_{1}\cdot n_{1}+\alpha(1-\beta)m_{1}\cdot n_{2}+(1-\alpha)\beta m_{2}\cdot n_{1}+(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)m_{2}\cdot n_{2}.

Remark 4.2
.

By part (3) of Corollary
4.1
one can see that the notion of a convex monoid given in
[
RR17
, Definition 9]

is a special case of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-convex monoid.

As in the case of categories, from now on we will write

\mathbf{ConvMon}
for
\mathbf{ConvMon}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
and refer to the objects of this category as real convex monoids.

Example 4.3
.

A semiring
R
can be given the structure of an
R
-convex set by defining

\pi^{R}:D_{R}(R)\to R

as follows:

\pi^{R}(p)=\sum_{x\in R}p(x)x.

Since
R
is commutative
\pi^{R}
is a homomorphism of monoids, hence
R
becomes an
R
-convex monoid.

Example 4.4
.

The set
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers is a convex set, and
with the following product

x_{1}\vartriangle x_{2}:=x_{1}x_{2}+(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2}),\;\;\;\;x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

is a monoid with
1
as the identity.
In addition, part
(3)
of Corollary
4.1
holds, hence
(\mathbb{R},\pi^{\mathbb{R}})
is a
real convex monoid.
Note that
([0,1],\pi^{\mathbb{R}},\vartriangle)
is a real subconvex monoid of
(\mathbb{R},\pi^{\mathbb{R}},\vartriangle)
.

Example 4.5
.

The set of continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
is a real
convex set. With the composition operation it is a monoid, but not a real convex monoid.

4.1
Weak invertibility

In this section, we will introduce the notion of weak invertibility for monoids. We begin by relating monoids to groups. For a monoid
M
let
I(M)
denote the subset of invertible elements. This construction defines a functor
I:\mathbf{Mon}\to\mathbf{Grp}
, which turns out to be the right adjoint of the inclusion functor
j:\mathbf{Grp}\to\mathbf{Mon}
; see
[
Lei14
, Example 2.1.3 (d)]
. The composition of the two adjunctions

j:\mathbf{Grp}\dashv\mathbf{Mon}:I
and
D_{R}:\mathbf{Mon}\dashv\mathbf{ConvMon}_{R}:U

gives us
the following adjunction

(24)

D_{R}:\mathbf{Grp}\dashv\mathbf{ConvMon}_{R}:I.

For simplicity, we will use the notation
M^{\ast}

instead of
I(M)
.

Let
i_{M}:M^{\ast}\to M
denote the inclusion map.
We will consider
D_{R}(M^{*})
as a subset of
D_{R}(M)
via the map
D_{R}(i_{M})
.
The restriction of
\pi^{M}:D_{R}(M)\to M
to
D_{R}(M^{*})
will be denoted by
\tilde{\pi}^{M}
.

Definition 4.6
.

An element
m\in M
is called

weakly invertible
if it lies in the image of
\tilde{\pi}^{M}:D_{R}(M^{*})\to M
.

In particular, every invertible element is weakly invertible.

Example 4.7
.

In Example
4.3
if we let
R=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
then
0
is not weakly invertible since for

p\in D((\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{\ast})
we have

\tilde{\pi}^{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}(p)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}p(x)x>0.

Example 4.8
.

The invertible elements of the subconvex
monoid
([0,1],\pi^{\mathbb{R}},\vartriangle)
(see Example
4.4
) are
0
and
1
. For an element
x\in[0,1]
we have the distribution
p=x\delta_{1}+(1-x)\delta_{0}\in D([0,1]^{\ast})
that satisfies
\pi^{\mathbb{R}}(p)=x
.
Therefore every element of
[0,1]
is weakly invertible.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
n\in M^{\ast}
and
m\in M
. Then
m
is weakly invertible if and only if
n\cdot m
is weakly invertible.

Proof.

Suppose that
p\in D_{R}(M^{\ast})
with
\tilde{\pi}^{M}(p)=m
.
Then we have

(25)

{\tilde{\pi}}^{M}(\delta^{n}\ast p)={\tilde{\pi}}^{M}(\delta^{n})\cdot{\tilde{\pi}}^{M}(p)=n\cdot m

In Equation (
25
) we used the fact that
{\tilde{\pi}}^{M}(\delta^{n})=\pi^{M}(D_{R}(i_{M})(\delta^{n}))=\pi^{M}(\delta^{n})=n
. This shows that if
m
is weakly invertible then
n\cdot m
is weakly invertible. Conversely,
if
n\cdot m
is weakly invertible then applying this observation to the product
n\cdot m
(instead of
m
) we obtain that
n^{-1}\cdot(n\cdot m)=m
is weakly invertible.
∎

Next, we provide a criterion for weak invertibility in pull-backs of convex monoids. This is a version for convex monoids of a “gluing result” for simplicial distributions
[
OKI22
, Lemma 4.4]
; see also
[
FF16
, Section 3.4]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
R
be a division semiring which is also zero-sum-free (see
[
Hut81
]
).
Consider the following diagram in
\mathbf{Set}
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}

Then the induced map
D_{R}(X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2})\to D_{R}(X_{1})\times_{D_{R}(Y)}D_{R}(X_{2})
is surjective.

Proof.

Given
(p_{1},p_{2})\in D_{R}(X_{1})\times_{D_{R}(Y)}D_{R}(X_{2})
, we define

p:X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2}\to R

as follows

p(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}\frac{p_{1}(x_{1})p_{2}(x_{2})}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))}&\text{if}~D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))\neq 0\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))=D_{R}(f_{2})(p_{2})(f_{2}(x_{2}))

because of the compatibility of
p_{1},p_{2}
and
x_{1},x_{2}
.
First, we verify that
p\in D_{R}(X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2}}p(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2}\,,\,D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))\neq 0}\frac{p_{1}(x_{1})p_{2}(x_{2})}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}\in X_{1}\,,\,D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))\neq 0}\;\;\sum_{x_{2}\in X_{2}\,,\,f_{2}(x_{2})=f_{1}(x_{1})}\frac{p_{1}(x_{1})p_{2}(x_{2})}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}\in X_{1}\,,\,D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))\neq 0}\frac{p_{1}(x_{1})}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))}\;\;\sum_{x_{2}\in X_{2}\,,\,f_{2}(x_{2})=f_{1}(x_{1})}p_{2}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}\in X_{1}\,,\,D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))\neq 0}\frac{p_{1}(x_{1})}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))}\;D_{R}(f_{2})(p_{2})(f_{1}(x_{1}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}\in X_{1}\,,\,D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))\neq 0}p_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}\in X_{1}}p_{1}(x_{1})=1.

In the last line we use the following fact: Since
R
is a zero-sum-free semiring,

D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x_{1}))=0
implies
p_{1}(x_{1})=0
.

Let
r_{j}:X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2}\to X_{j}
, where
j=0,1
, be the projection maps.
We will show that
D_{R}(r_{1})(p)=p_{1}
.
Let

x\in X_{1}
. If
D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x))\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle D_{R}(r_{1})(p)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f_{2}(x_{2})=f_{1}(x)}p(x,x_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{f_{2}(x_{2})=f_{1}(x)}\frac{p_{1}(x)p_{2}(x_{2})}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}(x)}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x))}\sum_{f_{2}(x_{2})=f_{1}(x)}p_{2}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}(x)}{D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x))}D_{R}(f_{2})(p_{2})(f_{1}(x))=p_{1}(x).

If
D_{R}(f_{1})(p_{1})(f_{1}(x))=0
, then as before
p_{1}(x)=0
.
We also have

D_{R}(r_{1})(p)(x)=\sum_{f_{2}(x_{2})=f_{1}(x)}p(x,x_{2})=0.

Similarly, one can show that
D_{R}(r_{2})(p)=p_{2}
.

∎

4.2
Strong invertibility

We introduce a stronger version of invertibility akin to strong contextuality (Definition

Definition 4.13
.

Given an
R

the

is the following subset of

\operatorname{Isupp}(m)=\{m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}:\,\exists P\in D_{R}(M)\text{ such that }\pi(P)=m\text{ and }P(m^{\prime})\neq 0\}

is

.

To study the properties of strong invertibility we will restrict to a special class of semirings.

Definition 4.14
.

A semiring
R
is called

if

implies
a=0
or
b=0
if for all

(see
[
Jac09
, Definition 7]
).

In this section the semiring

will be zero-sum-free (Definition
2.11
) and integral.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
R
be a zero-sum-free, integral semiring, and
M
be an
R
-convex monoid.
For
m_{1},m_{2}\in M
we have

\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1})\cdot\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2}).

Furthermore, if
m_{1}
invertible, then

m_{1}\cdot\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{2})=\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})
.

Proof.

Given
n_{1}\in\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1})
,
n_{2}\in\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{2})
there exists
P_{1},P_{2}\in D_{R}(M)
such that

\pi(P_{i})=m_{i}
and
P_{i}(n_{i})\neq 0
. Therefore

\pi(P_{1}\ast P_{2})=m_{1}\cdot m_{2}
and

P_{1}\ast P_{2}(n_{1}\cdot n_{2})=\sum_{x_{1}\cdot x_{2}=n_{1}\cdot n_{2}}P_{1}(x_{1})P_{2}(x_{2})=P_{1}(n_{1})P_{2}(n_{2})+\cdots\neq 0.

This means that
n_{1}\cdot n_{2}\in\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})
.

Suppose now that
m_{1}\in M^{\ast}
. Then

m_{1}\cdot\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1})\cdot\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})
.
Therefore
\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})=m_{1}\cdot m_{1}^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})\subseteq m_{1}\cdot\operatorname{Isupp}(m_{2})
.
∎

Corollary 4.16
.

Let
R
be a zero-sum-free, integral semiring, and

be an

-convex monoid.

and

Then the following are equivalent:

2.

is strongly non-invertible.

3.

is strongly non-invertible.

.

4.3
Invertible fraction

We now give the definition of the invertible fraction for real convex monoids.

Definition 4.19
.

Let
M
be a real convex monoid.
The
invertible
fraction
of
m\in M
, denoted by
\operatorname{IF}(m)
, is the supremum of

\{\sum_{m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}}P(m^{\prime}):\,P\in D_{R}(M)\text{ such that }\pi(P)=m\}.

The non-invertible fraction of
m

is defined to be

\operatorname{NIF}(m)=1-\operatorname{IF}(m)
.

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
M
be a real convex monoid. An element
m\in M
is
strongly non-invertible
if and only if
\operatorname{IF}(m)=0
.

Proof.

The element
m
is strongly non-invertible if and only if
\operatorname{Isupp}(m)=\emptyset
. The set
\operatorname{Isupp}(m)
is empty if and only if
every
P\in D(M)
such that
\pi(P)=m
satisfies

P(m^{\prime})=0
for all
m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}
.
This is equivalent to every
P\in D(M)
such that
\pi(P)=m
satisfying

\sum_{m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}}P(m^{\prime})=0
.
This means that
\operatorname{IF}(m)=0
.
∎

Proposition 4.21
.

For
m_{1},m_{2}\in M
we have

\operatorname{IF}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})\geq\operatorname{IF}(m_{1})\cdot\operatorname{IF}(m_{2}).

Proof.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in D_{R}(M)
such that
\pi(P_{i})=m_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Then

\pi(P_{1}\ast P_{2})=m_{1}\cdot m_{2}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}}(P_{1}\ast P_{2})(m^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}}\sum_{m^{\prime}_{1}\cdot m^{\prime}_{2}=m^{\prime}}P_{1}(m^{\prime}_{1})P(m^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}_{1}\cdot m^{\prime}_{2}\in M^{\ast}}P_{1}(m^{\prime}_{1})P_{2}(m^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2}\in M^{\ast}}P_{1}(m^{\prime}_{1})P_{2}(m^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}_{1}\in M^{\ast}}P(m^{\prime}_{1})\sum_{m^{\prime}_{2}\in M^{\ast}}P(m^{\prime}_{2}).

Therefore
\operatorname{IF}(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})\geq\operatorname{IF}(m_{1})\cdot\operatorname{IF}(m_{2})
.
∎

Corollary 4.22
.

Given
n\in M^{\ast}
and
m\in M
we have
\operatorname{IF}(n\cdot m)=\operatorname{IF}(m)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.21
we have
\operatorname{IF}(n\cdot m)\geq\operatorname{IF}(n)\operatorname{IF}(m)=\operatorname{IF}(m)
.
On the other hand,
m=n^{-1}\cdot(n\cdot m)
. Therefore
\operatorname{IF}(m)\geq\operatorname{IF}(n\cdot m)
.
∎

Proposition 4.23
.

Given a morphism
f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
in
\mathbf{ConvMon}
and
m\in M
we have

\operatorname{IF}(f(m))\geq\operatorname{IF}(m)

Proof.

For
P\in D(M_{1})
such that
\pi^{M_{1}}(P)=m
we have

\pi^{M_{2}}(D(f)(P))=f(\pi^{M_{1}}(P))=f(m)
and

\sum_{n^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}_{2}}D(f)(P)(n^{\prime})=\sum_{n^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}_{2}}\sum_{f(m)=n^{\prime}}P(m)\geq\sum_{m^{\prime}\in M_{1}^{\ast}}P(m^{\prime}).

∎

Proposition 4.24
.

For
m\in M
and
\pi(P)=m
we have

\operatorname{IF}(m)\geq\sum_{x\in M}P(x)\operatorname{IF}(x).

Proof.

Let us write

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}\in M^{\ast}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(m_{i})P_{i}(m^{\prime})

Remark 4.25
.

Theorem

Proposition 4.26

Let

For

(30)

\{\alpha\in[0,1]:\,m_{1}\text{ is weakly invertible},\;m_{2}\in M\text{ such that }m=\alpha m_{1}+(1-\alpha)m_{2}\,\}.

Then

(31)

For
\alpha\in A
,
there exists

and

By Proposition
2.3
the structure map

is a morphism in
\mathbf{Conv}
.
Therefore we obtain

Observe that

Note that

5
Contextuality and invertibility

Recall that a simplicial distribution

In this section we establish the equivalence of the notions of noncontextuality and weak invertibility.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X
be
a simplicial set
and
Y
be a simplicial monoid.
Then the map

\Theta_{X,Y}:D_{R}(s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y))\to s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))
is a homomorphism of monoids.

Proof.

Given

,
and

,

, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(p\ast q)_{n}(x)(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varphi\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y):\,\varphi_{n}(x)=y}(p\ast q)(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varphi_{n}(x)=y}\sum_{\psi\cdot\psi^{\prime}=\varphi}p(\psi)q(\psi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\psi_{n}(x)\cdot\psi^{\prime}_{n}(x)=y}p(\psi)q(\psi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1}\cdot y_{2}=y}~\sum_{\psi_{n}(x)=y_{1},\,\psi^{\prime}_{n}(x)=y_{2}}p(\psi)q(\psi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1}\cdot y_{2}=y}\left(\sum_{\psi_{n}(x)=y_{1}}p(\psi)\right)\left(\sum_{\psi^{\prime}_{n}(x)=y_{2}}q(\psi^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1}\cdot y_{2}=y}\Theta(p)_{n}(x)(y_{1})\cdot\Theta(q)_{n}(x)(y_{2})

\displaystyle=(\Theta(p)_{n}(x)\ast\Theta(q)_{n}(x))(y)

\displaystyle=(\Theta(p)\cdot\Theta(q))_{n}(x)(y).

∎

Y
be a simplicial group. For
p\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D(Y))
we have

\operatorname{Isupp}(p)=(\delta_{Y})_{\ast}(\operatorname{supp}(p)).

Proof.

A distribution
q\in\operatorname{Isupp}(p)
is invertible, and by
Lemma
5.7
there exists
\varphi\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y)
such that

q=\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi
.
Also there exists

Q\in D(s\mathbf{Set}(X,D(Y))
such that
\pi^{s\mathbf{Set}(X,D(Y)}(Q)=p
and

Q(\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi)=Q(q)>0
.
Using Equation (
10
), for
x\in X_{n}
we obtain

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)(\varphi_{n}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{p^{\prime}\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))}Q(p^{\prime})\,p_{n}^{\prime}(x)(\varphi_{n}(x))

\displaystyle\geq Q(\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi)(\delta_{Y_{n}}\circ\varphi_{n}(x))(\varphi_{n}(x))

\displaystyle=Q(\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi)\delta^{\varphi_{n}(x)}(\varphi_{n}(x))

\displaystyle=Q(\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi)>0.

Therefore
\varphi\in\operatorname{supp}(p)
and

q\in(\delta_{Y})_{\ast}(\operatorname{supp}(p))
.

For the converse inclusion, we will show that
\delta_{Y}\circ\psi\in\operatorname{Isupp}(p)
for
\psi\in\operatorname{supp}(p)
.
We define

\alpha=\min\{p_{n}(x)(\psi_{n}(x)):\,x\in X_{n},\,n\geq 0\}.

Observe that
\alpha>0
since
X
has finitely many simplices
and for
x\in X_{n}
we have both

p_{n+1}(s_{i}(x))(\psi_{n+1}(s_{i}(x)))\;\;\text{ and }\;\;p_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))(\psi_{n-1}(d_{j}(x)))

are greater than or equal to
p_{n}(x)
.
If
\alpha=1
, which implies that
p=\delta_{Y}\circ\psi
, then

\delta_{Y}\circ\psi\in\operatorname{Isupp}(p)
.
Then let us suppose that
\alpha<1
. For
n\geq 0
, we define
q_{n}:X_{n}\to D_{R}(Y_{n})
by

q_{n}(x)=\frac{p_{n}(x)-\alpha\delta^{\psi_{n}(x)}}{1-\alpha},\;\;\;x\in X_{n}.

We need to verify that indeed
q_{n}(x)\in D_{R}(Y_{n})
:
Observe that for
y\in Y_{n}
, we have

q_{n}(x)(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{p_{n}(x)(\psi_{n}(x))-\alpha}{1-\alpha}&\text{if}~y=\psi_{n}(x),\\
\frac{{p}_{n}(x)(y)}{1-\alpha}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By definition of
\alpha
, we have
q_{n}(x)(y)\geq 0

for all
x\in X_{n}
,
y\in Y_{n}
.
In addition, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}q_{n}(x)(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}\frac{p_{n}(x)(y)-\alpha\delta^{\psi_{n}(x)}(y)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}p_{n}(x)(y)-\alpha\sum_{y\in Y_{n}}\delta^{\psi_{n}(x)}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}(1-\alpha)=1.

Next we prove that the collection of maps
\{q_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
form a simplicial set map
q:X\to D_{R}(Y)
.
Given
x\in X_{n}
and
y\in Y_{n-1}
, we will show that

(D_{R}(d_{j})\circ q_{n}(x))(y)=(q_{n-1}\circ d_{j}(x))(y)
.
We begin with the case that
y\neq\psi_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))
: We have

\displaystyle q_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))(y)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}D(d_{j})(p_{n}(x))(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y}\frac{p_{n}(x)(y^{\prime})}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y}q_{n}(x)(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=D_{R}(d_{j})(q_{n}(x))(y).

In the fourth line we used the observation that
y^{\prime}
with
d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y
satisfies
y^{\prime}\neq\psi_{n}(x)
,
otherwise we would have

y=d_{j}(y^{\prime})=d_{j}(\psi_{n}(x))=\psi_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))
.
Next assume that

y=\psi_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))=d_{j}(\psi_{n}(x))
. Then we have

\displaystyle q_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n-1}(d_{j}(x))(y)-\alpha}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{R}(d_{j})(p_{n}(x))(y)-\alpha}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y}p_{n}(x)(y^{\prime})-\alpha}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n}(x)(\psi_{n}(x))-\alpha}{1-\alpha}+\sum_{d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y,\,y^{\prime}\neq\psi_{n}(x)}\frac{p_{n}(x)(y^{\prime})}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=q_{n}(x)(\psi_{n}(x))+\sum_{d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y,\,y^{\prime}\neq\psi_{n}(x)}q_{n}(x)(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{j}(y^{\prime})=y}q_{n}(x)(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=D_{R}(d_{j})(q_{n}(x))(y).

Now we define
Q\in D(s\mathbf{Set}(X,D(Y)))
by

Q(p^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1-\alpha&\text{if}~p^{\prime}=q\\
\alpha&\text{if}~p^{\prime}=\delta_{Y}\circ\psi\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
x\in X_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{s\mathbf{Set}(X,D(Y))}(Q)_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)q_{n}(x)+\alpha(\delta_{Y_{n}}\circ\psi_{n})(x)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\frac{p_{n}(x)-\alpha\delta^{\psi_{n}(x)}}{1-\alpha}+\alpha\delta^{\psi_{n}(x)}

\displaystyle=p_{n}(x).

We showed that
\pi^{s\mathbf{Set}(X,D(Y))}(Q)=p
, while

Q(\delta_{Y}\circ\psi)=\alpha\neq 0
, which implies that

\delta_{Y}\circ\psi\in\operatorname{Isupp}(p)
.
∎

Definition 5.13

Proposition 5.14
.

Let

be a zero-sum-free, integral semiring.
For simplicial sets
X,Y
every deterministic distribution in

is a vertex.

Proof.

Let

. Suppose that we have

such that

\pi^{s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))}(Q)=\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi

Given

, not equal to
\delta_{Y}\circ\varphi

and

such that

.

.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let

be a zero-sum-free semiring and

be a morphism in

is a vertex,

5.3.1
Homotopic simplicial distributions

Simplicial homotopy can be used to capture strongly contextual simplicial distributions and vertices.
We begin with a preliminary observation.

Lemma 5.19
.

Let
f:Z\to X
be a simplicial set map, and
p\in s\mathbf{Set}(X,D_{R}(Y))
.

1.

f^{\ast}(\operatorname{supp}(p))\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(f^{\ast}(p))
.

2.

If
\operatorname{supp}(f^{\ast}(p))\cap f^{\ast}(s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y))=\emptyset
then
p
is strongly contextual.

Proof.

Given
\varphi\in\operatorname{supp}(p)
and
x\in Z_{n}
, we have

f^{\ast}(p)_{n}(x)(f^{\ast}(\varphi)_{n}(x))=(p_{n}\circ f_{n})(x)(\varphi_{n}\circ f_{n}(x))=p_{n}(f_{n}(x))(\varphi_{n}(f_{n}(x)))\neq 0.

This proves part (1).
If
\varphi\in\operatorname{supp}(p)
then by part (1) we have

f^{\ast}(\varphi)\in\operatorname{supp}(f^{\ast}(p))
. This means that

f^{\ast}(\varphi)\in\operatorname{supp}(f^{\ast}(p))\cap f^{\ast}(s\mathbf{Set}(X,Y))
, proving part (2).
∎

Part (2) of Lemma
5.19
is a key observation also used in arguments involving simplicial cohomology

and Čech cohomology
[
ABK+
]
