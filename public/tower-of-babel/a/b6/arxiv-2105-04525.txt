1.
Introduction

This paper is motivated by the theory of integer programming.
Given a
constraint matrix

A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and vectors
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, a fundamental problem of integer programming is to find an integer vector
x
in the polyhedron
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon Ax\leq b\}
that maximizes the inner product
c^{T}x
.
In general, one cannot expect to optimize an integer program efficiently, but better efficiency is possible if the constraint matrix has special structure.

Given a positive integer
\Delta
, an integer matrix
A
is
\Delta
-modular
if the determinant of each
\rank(A)\times\rank(A)
submatrix of
A
has absolute value at most
\Delta
.
A
1
-modular matrix is
unimodular
, and integer programs with a unimodular matrix can be optimized in strongly polynomial time, because every vertex of the polytope
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon Ax\leq b\}
is integral
[
10
]
.
This fact has led to considerable interest in the complexity of integer programming with a
\Delta
-modular constraint matrix (see, for example,
[
15
]
and
[
1
]
).
This complexity is unknown when
\Delta\geq 3
, but a recent breakthrough of Artmann, Weismantel, and Zenklusen
[
2
]
shows that integer programs with a
2
-modular, or
bimodular
, constraint matrix can be optimized in strongly polynomial time.
In view of this result, the structural properties of
2
-modular matrices are of significant interest.
A well-known result of Heller
[
9
]
shows that a rank-
r
unimodular matrix has at most
{r+1\choose 2}
nonzero, pairwise non-parallel columns.
We prove the analogous result for
\Delta=2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each sufficiently large integer
r
, the maximum number of nonzero, pairwise non-parallel columns of a rank-
r

2
-modular matrix is
{r+2\choose 2}-2
.

This was proven independently by Lee et al.
[
11
]
, who showed that this bound holds for all
r
.
The previous best upper bound was
{r+1\choose 2}+C\cdot r
for a large constant
C
, due to Geelen, Nelson, and Walsh
[
6
]
.

1.1
is tight, for example, for the matrices

We prove Theorem
1.1
using matroids.
For each positive integer
\Delta
, let
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
denote the class of matroids with a representation over
\mathbb{R}
as a
\Delta
-modular matrix.
We say that such a matroid is
\Delta
-modular
.
An integer matrix
A
is
totally
\Delta
-modular
if the determinant of every submatrix of
A
has absolute value at most
\Delta
.
We define
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta}
to be the class of matroids with a representation over
\mathbb{R}
as a totally
\Delta
-modular matrix.
Clearly
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
for each
\Delta\geq 1
, because every totally
\Delta
-modular matrix is also
\Delta
-modular.
Since every unimodular matrix is row-equivalent to a totally unimodular matrix, the classes
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
are in fact equal.

The class
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is the class of
regular matroids
.
Tutte
[
14
]
proved that this class coincides with the class of matroids representable over every field, and that there are exactly three minor-minimal matroids not in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
For
\Delta\geq 2
, the class
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is not very well-studied.
It is proved in
[
6
]
that
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is minor-closed, and that every matroid in
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is representable over every field with characteristic greater than
\Delta
.
In particular, every matroid in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is representable over
\GF(3)
and
\GF(5)
; such a matroid is called
dyadic
.

In view of Tutte’s results and the fundamental significance of unimodular matrices, there are several natural open questions concerning
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
.

Problem 1.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a positive integer.

(i)

What is the maximum size of a simple rank-
r
matroid in
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
, and which matroids attain this maximum?

(ii)

What are the minor-minimal matroids that are not in
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
?

(iii)

Is
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta}
equal to
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
?

(iv)

Is
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta}
closed under duality?

Theorem
1.1
shows that the maximum size of a simple rank-
r

2
-modular matroid is
{r+2\choose 2}-2
, if
r
is sufficiently large, and the vector matroids of
A_{r}
and
A^{\prime}_{r}
show that there are at least two matroids that attain this maximum.
In order to prove Theorem

, we show that

and

have affirmative answers for

, and we find several excluded minors for

, including

,

,

, and

.
In fact, we prove the following result, which is stronger than Theorem
1.1
.
A
line
in a matroid is a rank-
2
flat, and a line is
long
if it contains at least three
points
, that is, rank-one flats.
The matroid
R_{9}
is the ternary Reid geometry
[
12
, p. 654]
, which is the simple rank-
3
matroid consisting of long lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
with a common intersection point

so that
|L_{1}|=|L_{2}|=4
,
|L_{3}|=3
, and both elements in
L_{3}-\{x\}
are on four long lines.

Theorem 1.3
.

For each sufficiently large integer
r
, the maximum size of a simple rank-
r
matroid with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4},R_{9}\}
is
{r+2\choose 2}-2
.

While we make progress on Problem
1.2
for
\Delta=2
, it is surprisingly difficult for
\Delta\geq 3
.
For fixed
\Delta\geq 3
, the current best upper bound on the maximum size of a simple rank-
r
matroid in
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is
{r+1\choose 2}+C\cdot r
for a large constant

, proved by Geelen, Nelson, and Walsh
[

.
Recently, Lee et al.
[

proved an upper bound of

Both of these are recent additions to a long sequence of previous results; we direct the reader to

for more details.
However, it is unclear what the correct bound should be for

.
It is also unclear in general what the rank-

.
On a more positive note, it follows from a result of D’Adderio and Moci

is closed under duality for all positive

.
In Theorem

However, for

it is unknown whether

, and whether

is closed under duality.
Some of the difficulties of these open problems are discussed in Section

.
After introducing some preliminaries in Section

.
The proofs of Theorems

2.
Preliminaries

We follow the notation of
[
12
]
, unless otherwise stated.
For a matroid
M
, we write
|M|
and
\varepsilon(M)
for, respectively,
|E(M)|
and the number of points of
M
.
For an integer
\ell\geq 2
, we denote by
\mathcal{U}(\ell)
the class of matroids with no
U_{2,\ell+2}
-minor.

We first describe a construction that shows how the vector matroids of
A_{r}
and
A^{\prime}_{r}
are related to
M(K_{r+2})
.
If a matroid
M
is obtained from a matroid
N
by deleting a non-empty subset
T
of
E(N)
, then
N
is an
extension
of
M
.
If
|T|=1
, then
N
is a
single-element extension
of
M
, and if
|T|=k
for
k\geq 2
, then
N
is a
k
-element extension
of
M
.
The single-element extensions of a matroid
M
are in one-to-one correspondence with the
modular cuts
of
M
, which are the sets
\mathcal{F}
of flats with the following two properties:

(i)

If
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
F^{\prime}
is a flat of
M
containing
F
, then
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

If
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
r_{M}(F_{1})+r_{M}(F_{2})=r_{M}(F_{1}\cup F_{2})+r_{M}(F_{1}\cap F_{2})
, then
F_{1}\cap F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
.

A pair of flats that satisfies (ii) is a
modular pair
.
A modular cut
\mathcal{F}
is
proper
if it does not contain all flats of
M
.
The modular cut of
M
corresponding to a single-element extension
N
of
M
by an element
e
is the set of flats of
M
that span
e
in
N
.
Conversely, Crapo
[
3
]
showed that, for every modular cut
\mathcal{F}
of
M
, there is a unique single-element extension
N
of
M
by an element
e
for which
\mathcal{F}
is precisely the set of flats of
M
that span
e
in
N
(see
[
12
, Theorem 7.2.3]
).
Given a set
\mathcal{F}
of flats of
M
, the modular cut of
M

generated
by
\mathcal{F}
is the intersection of all modular cuts of
M
that contain every flat in
\mathcal{F}
.
If
N
is a single-element extension of
M
by an element
e
corresponding to the modular cut generated by a single flat
F
, then we say that
e
has been
freely added
to
F
.

A matroid
M^{\prime}
is an
elementary projection
or an
elementary quotient
of
M
if there is a single-element extension
N
of
M
by an element
e
so that
M^{\prime}=N/e
.
Thus, every elementary projection of
M
corresponds to a modular cut of
M
.
We prove in the following section that the vector matroids of the matrices
A_{r}
and
A^{\prime}_{r}
are both simplifications of elementary projections of
M(K_{r+2})
.

Two classes of matroids arise in the proof of Theorem
1.3
: spikes and Reid geometries.
A
spike
is a simple matroid
S
with an element
t
so that the simplification of
S/t
is a circuit, and each parallel class of
S/t
has size two.
The element
t
is a
tip
of
S
.
A spike
S
may have several elements that can serve as a tip.
For example, the Fano plane is a spike, and every element can serve as a tip.
However, a spike of rank at least four has a unique tip.
We will also make use of the fact that spikes are not graphic (see
[
12
, p. 662]
).

A
Reid geometry
is a simple rank-
3
matroid
N
consisting of long lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
with a common intersection point
x
so that
|L_{1}|=|L_{2}|
and
|L_{3}|=3
, while both elements in
L_{3}-\{x\}
are on
|L_{1}|
long lines of
N
.
If
|L_{1}|=3
, then
N
is the Fano plane
F_{7}
.
If
|L_{1}|=4
, then
N
is the ternary Reid geometry
R_{9}
.

3.
The Maximum-Sized Matroids

In this section, we describe two matroids that meet the bound of Theorem
1.3
.
We first show that the matrices
A_{r}
and
A^{\prime}_{r}
are in fact
2
-modular.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each integer
r\geq 2
, the matrix
A_{r}
is
2
-modular.

Proof.

Let
r
be minimal so that the lemma is false.
Then
r>2
.
Let
A
be an
r\times r
submatrix of
A_{r}
so that
|\det(A)|>2
.
If
A
has a row with exactly one nonzero entry, then the determinant of
A
is
\pm 1
times the determinant of an
(r-1)\times(r-1)
submatrix of
A_{r-1}
, and so
|\det(A)|\leq 2
, by the minimality of
r
.
Then each row of
A
has at least two nonzero entries, and, since each column of
A_{r}
has at most two nonzero entries, this implies that
A
has precisely two nonzero entries in each row and column.
By permuting rows and columns of
A
and multiplying some rows and columns of
A
by
-1
, we may obtain a matrix

A^{\prime}
for which all but at most one column has nonzero entries
1
and
-1
.
If
A^{\prime}
has no column with two entries equal to
1
, then
|\det(A^{\prime})|\leq 1
.
Otherwise, by expanding along a column with two ones, we see that the determinant of
A
is the sum or difference of the determinants of two matrices with determinant in
\{-1,0,1\}
.
In either case,
|\det(A^{\prime})|\leq 2
.
But
|\det(A^{\prime})|=|\det(A)|
, so this contradicts the choice of
A
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For each integer
r\geq 2
, the matrix
A^{\prime}_{r}
is
2
-modular.

Proof.

For each integer
r\geq 3
, let
H_{r}=[I_{r}|D_{r}|v]
, where
v=[1,-1,-1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
.
Then
A^{\prime}_{r}
can be obtained from
H_{r+1}
by pivoting on the first entry of the last column, deleting the first row and last column, and then deleting two columns.
This implies that each
r\times r
submatrix of
A^{\prime}_{r}
has the same determinant as an
(r+1)\times(r+1)
submatrix of
H_{r+1}
, so it suffices to show that
H_{r}
is
2
-modular for each
r\geq 3
.

Let
A=[A^{\prime}|v]
be an
r\times r
submatrix of
H_{r}
.
Let
A_{1}=[A^{\prime}|e_{1}-e_{2}]
and
A_{2}=[A^{\prime}|e_{3}]
, where
e_{i}
is the
i^{th}
unit vector.
Then
|\det(A_{1})|\leq 1
and
|\det(A_{2})|\leq 1
, since each is a submatrix of
[I_{r}|D_{r}]
.
Since the determinant is linear in the last column and
v=(e_{1}-e_{2})-e_{3}
, we have
\det(A)=\det(A_{1})-\det(A_{2})
, so
|\det(A)|\leq 2
.
∎

The vector matroids of the matrices
A_{r}
and
A^{\prime}_{r}
are both simplifications of elementary projections of
M(K_{r+2})
.
For each integer
r\geq 2
, the matroid
T_{r}
is the simplification of the matroid obtained by projecting
M(K_{r+2})
by an element freely added to a
3
-point line.
The matrix
A_{r}
is a well-known representation of
T_{r}

[
13
]
, so
T_{r}
is a
2
-modular matroid by Lemma
3.1
.
Note that
|T_{r}|=|M(K_{r+2})|-2={r+2\choose 2}-2
.
We now define another single-element extension of
M(K_{r+2})
, by its corresponding modular cut.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be
2
-point lines of
M(K_{r+2})
so that
L_{1}\cup L_{2}
is a
4
-element circuit.
Then the set of flats of
M(K_{r+2})
that contain
L_{1}
or
L_{2}
is a proper modular cut.
Moreover, the corresponding single-element extension of
M(K_{r+2})
is represented over both
\mathbb{R}
and
\GF(3)
by the matrix
[I_{r+1}|D_{r+1}|v]
, where
v=[1,-1,-1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
.

Proof.

Note that the matrix
H_{r+1}=[I_{r+1}|D_{r+1}|v]
is a submatrix of
A^{\prime}_{r+1}
, and is thus
2
-modular, by Lemma
3.2
.
Therefore, the vector matroids of
H_{r+1}
over
\mathbb{R}
and
\GF(3)
are isomorphic; let
M
denote this matroid.
In the matroid
M
, let
e
label the last column of
H_{r+1}
.
Then
M\!\setminus\!e
is
M(K_{r+2})
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of flats of
M\!\setminus\!e
that span
e
in
M
.
Note that
\mathcal{F}
contains no
1
-element flat, because
M
is simple.
Let
a,b,c,d
be the labels of the columns
[1,-1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
,
[0,1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
,
[0,0,1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
, and
[1,0,-1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
, respectively, of
H_{r+1}
.
Then
\{a,c,e\}
,
\{b,d,e\}
, and
\{a,b,c,d\}
are circuits of
M
, and so
\{a,c\}
and
\{b,d\}
are flats of
M(K_{r+2})
in
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
F_{0}
denote the
6
-element flat of
M(K_{r+2})
spanned by
\{a,b,c,d\}
; then
M(K_{r+2})|F_{0}\cong M(K_{4})
.
It is straightforward to check that
\{a,c,e\}
and
\{b,d,e\}
are the only long lines of

that contain
e
.

Suppose that there is a flat
F\in\mathcal{F}
that does not contain
\{a,c\}
or
\{b,d\}
.
Since
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
F_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
, the flat

is in

as well, by property (ii) of modular cuts.
This flat

has rank less than three, since it does not contain
\{a,c\}
or
\{b,d\}
.
It has size at least two, since

is simple.
But
\{a,c,e\}
and
\{b,d,e\}
are the only long lines of

that contain

, which contradicts the existence of

.
Thus,
\mathcal{F}
is precisely the set of flats that contain
\{a,c\}
or
\{b,d\}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is proper, the proposition holds.
∎

For each integer
r\geq 2
, let
T^{\prime}_{r}
denote the simplification of the matroid obtained by projecting

by an element corresponding to the modular cut of Proposition

.
It is not hard to see that the matrix

.
Note that

.
The matroids

and

are likely the unique simple rank-

-modular matroids of size

4.
Duality and Excluded Minors

In this section, we find several excluded minors for
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, show that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{2}=\mathcal{M}_{2}
, and present a proof showing that
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is closed under duality for each positive integer
\Delta
.
We make use of the following result.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A
be a
2
-modular representation of a rank-
r
matroid
M
, and let
e
be a non-loop of
M
that labels the first column of
A
.
Then there is a
2
-modular matrix
A^{\prime}
of the form
[I_{r}|X]
whose first column is labeled by
e
such that
A^{\prime}
is obtained from
A
by elementary row operations, column swaps, and dividing rows by two.

Proof.

We may assume that
e
labels the first column of
A
, that
A
has
r
rows, and that
r\geq 2
.
Let
B
be a basis of
M
containing
e
, and let
A_{B}
be the corresponding submatrix of
A
.
By performing elementary row operations, we may transform
A
into another
2
-modular matrix in which
A_{B}
is in Hermite normal form.
If each entry on the diagonal of
A_{B}
is
1
, then the lemma holds.
Otherwise, since
A
is
2
-modular, exactly one entry on the diagonal of
A_{B}
is equal to
2
and all the rest are equal to
1
.
We may assume that the row of
A
with the entry
2
on the diagonal of
A_{B}
has an entry in
\{-1,1\}
, or else we may divide this row by
2
.
By pivoting on that entry and swapping columns, we obtain a
2
-modular matrix of the form
[I_{r}|X]
, where the first column is labeled by
e
.
∎

Since row operations do not change the vector matroid, Lemma
4.1
implies that
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is closed under duality (see
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.8]
).
Also, since every
\Delta
-modular matrix of the form
[I_{r}|X]
is in fact totally
\Delta
-modular, Lemma
4.1
implies that
\mathcal{M}_{2}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.
Unfortunately, the analogue of Lemma
4.1
is false for
\Delta
-modular matrices with
\Delta\geq 3
.
For example, the matrix

\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
0&2&3\\
\end{bmatrix}

with the first column labeled by
e
is
3
-modular, but is not row-equivalent to a matrix with an
I_{2}
-submatrix that uses the first column.
This is the main difficulty in showing that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta}=\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
for
\Delta\geq 3
.

Next we present a proof showing that
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is closed under duality.
This follows from a result of D’Adderio and Moci concerning arithmetic matroids
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]
, but we present an elementary proof due to Marcel Celaya (private communication).
We also thank the anonymous referee for informing us that this result is in fact known.

We first develop some notation.
Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be a matrix with columns indexed by a set
E
.
For every
X\subseteq E
, we write
A_{X}
for the submatrix of
A
consisting of the columns indexed by
X
.
If
A
has full row-rank, then we write
\gcd(A)
for the greatest common divisor of the determinants of the
m\times m
submatrices of
A
.
We write
[n]
for
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

Theorem 4.2

(D’Adderio, Moci)
.

For each positive integer
\Delta
, the class
\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}
is closed under duality.

Proof.

Let
M
be a
\Delta
-modular matroid with ground set
[n]
, and let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
be a
\Delta
-modular representation of
M
with full row rank.
We first show that there is a matrix
C\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n-m)\times n}
with
\gcd(C)=1
whose rows form a basis of
\ker(A)
.
Let
C_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n-m)\times n}
be a matrix whose rows form a basis of
\ker(A)
.
Let
P\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n-m)\times(n-m)}
and
Q\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
be unimodular matrices so that
PC_{1}Q
is the Smith normal form of
C_{1}
.
Then
PC_{1}Q=[D|0]
, where
D\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n-m)\times(n-m)}
is an invertible diagonal matrix.
Then
D^{-1}PC_{1}Q=[I|0]
is an integer matrix with
\gcd(D^{-1}PC_{1}Q)=1
.
Since
Q
is unimodular, so is
Q^{-1}
, and therefore
(D^{-1}PC_{1}Q)Q^{-1}=D^{-1}PC_{1}
is an integer matrix.
Clearly the Smith normal form of
D^{-1}PC_{1}
is
D^{-1}PC_{1}Q=[I|0]
.
Since a matrix
B
with full row-rank has
\gcd(B)=1
if and only if the Smith normal form of
B
is
[I|0]
, it follows that
\gcd(D^{-1}PC_{1})=1
.
Therefore,
D^{-1}PC_{1}
is an integer matrix row-equivalent to
C_{1}
with
\gcd(D^{-1}PC_{1})=1
, so we may take
C=D^{-1}PC_{1}
.

We will show that
C
is a
\Delta
-modular representation of
M^{*}
.
For each set
X\subseteq[n]
, we write
\bar{X}
for
[n]-X
.
Note that
A
and
C
both have columns indexed by
[n]
, and that
B\subseteq[n]
is a basis of
M
if and only if
\bar{B}
is a basis of
M^{*}
.
By rearranging columns, we may assume that
B=[m]
is a basis of
M
.
Then
A_{B}^{-1}A=[I_{m}|A_{B}^{-1}A_{\bar{B}}]
.
Since
A_{B}^{-1}A
is row-equivalent to
A
, it represents
M
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
C^{\prime}=[(A_{B}^{-1}A_{\bar{B}})^{T}|-I_{n-m}]
.
Then by Theorem 2.2.8 in
[
12
]
,
C^{\prime}
represents
M^{*}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Also, the rows of
C^{\prime}
form a basis of
\ker(A_{B}^{-1}A)=\ker(A)
, so
C^{\prime}
is row-equivalent to
C
, and thus
C
represents
M^{*}
as well.
Moreover, since
C^{\prime}
and
C
are row-equivalent, there exists an invertible rational
(n-m)\times(n-m)
matrix
Q
such that
C^{\prime}=QC
.
Then each set
B_{1}\subseteq E(M)
of size
m
satisfies
\det(C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}_{1}})=\det(Q)\det(C_{\bar{B}_{1}})
.

Let
B_{0}
be a basis of
M
obtained from
B
by a basis exchange, so
B_{0}=(B\cup\{j\})-\{i\}
for some
i\in B
and
j\notin B
.
Then the matrix
C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}_{0}}
is obtained from
-I_{n-m}
by replacing the
(j-m)^{th}
column with the
i^{th}
column of
(A_{B}^{-1}A_{\bar{B}})^{T}
, so

(1)

\displaystyle\det(C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}_{0}})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-m-1}(A_{B}^{-1}A_{\bar{B}})^{T}_{j-m,i}

(2)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-m-1}(A_{B}^{-1}A_{\bar{B}})_{i,j-m}

(3)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-m-1}(A_{B}^{-1}A)_{i,j}.

Furthermore, the system
(A_{B})x=A_{\{j\}}
has unique solution
A_{B}^{-1}A_{\{j\}}=(A_{B}^{-1}A)_{\{j\}}
, so by Cramer’s rule, the
i^{th}
entry of the solution
(A_{B}^{-1}A)_{\{j\}}
is

\displaystyle(A_{B}^{-1}A)_{i,j}=\frac{\det(A_{B_{0}})}{\det(A_{B})}.

Combined with (3), this implies that

(4)

\displaystyle\det(C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}_{0}})=(-1)^{n-m-1}\frac{\det(A_{B_{0}})}{\det(A_{B})}.

Since
C^{\prime}=QC
and
\det(C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}})=(-1)^{n-m}
, (4) implies that

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{\det(C_{\bar{B}_{0}})}{\det(C_{\bar{B}})}=\frac{\det(C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}_{0}})}{\det(C^{\prime}_{\bar{B}})}=-\frac{\det(A_{B_{0}})}{\det(A_{B})}.

Since every basis of
M
can be obtained from any other basis by a sequence of basis exchanges, (5) implies that each pair
(B_{1},B_{2})
of bases of
M
satisfies

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{\det(C_{\bar{B}_{1}})}{\det(C_{\bar{B}_{2}})}=\pm\frac{\det(A_{B_{1}})}{\det(A_{B_{2}})}.

Suppose that some basis
B_{1}
of
M
satisfies
\det(C_{\bar{B}_{1}})=c\cdot\det(A_{B_{1}})
for some
c\in\mathbb{Q}
with
|c|>1
.
Then (6) implies that each other basis
B_{2}
of
M
satisfies
\det(C_{\bar{B}_{2}})=\pm c\cdot\det(A_{B_{2}})
.
But then
c\cdot\gcd(A)
is an integer with absolute value greater than
1
which divides
\gcd(C)
, a contradiction.
Thus, each basis
B
of
M
satisfies
|\det(C_{\bar{B}})|\leq|\det(A_{B})|\leq\Delta
, so
C
is
\Delta
-modular.

∎

We now turn our attention back to

.
To find excluded minors for
\mathcal{M}_{2}

is dyadic.

,

.

Proposition 4.4
.

Proof.

Suppose

.
Each single-element contraction of

.

Find the unique rank-

, and is
type-
(b)
if the extension of
M|X
by
e
corresponds to the modular cut generated by a pair of
2
-point lines of
M|X
whose union is a circuit.
Proposition
5.2
says that if
M
is simple and has no minor isomorphic to
U_{2,5}
,
F_{7}
, or
R_{9}
, then every element in
E(M)-X
is type-
(a)
or type-
(b)
.
Also note that
e
is type-
(a)
if and only if
\si((M/e)|X)\cong T_{r(M)-1}
, and
e
is type-
(b)
if and only if
\si((M/e)|X)\cong T^{\prime}_{r(M)-1}
.

6.
Two-Element Extensions

We now analyze the structure of a
2
-element extension of a clique, under the additional assumption that
U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}
is excluded as a minor.
In a matroid
M
, for sets
A,B\subseteq E(M)
, we write
\sqcap(A,B)
for
r_{M}(A)+r_{M}(B)-r_{M}(A\cup B)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
M
be a simple matroid of rank at least five with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}
, and with a set
X
so that
M|X\cong M(K_{r(M)+1})
.
If
e,f\in E(M)-X
so that
e
is type-
(a)
and
f
is type-
(b)
, then there is a set
Z\subseteq X
so that
M|Z\cong M(K_{4})
and
e,f\in\cl_{M}(Z)
.

Proof.

Since
e
is a type-
(a)
element, there is a
3
-point line
L_{1}
of
M|X
so that
e
is freely added to
L_{1}
.
By Proposition
3.3
, since
f
is a type-
(b)
element, there are
2
-point lines
L_{2}
and
L_{2}^{\prime}
of
M|X
so that
L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime}
is a
4
-element circuit, and a flat
F
of
M|X
spans
f
if and only if
F
contains
L_{2}
or
L_{2}^{\prime}
.

We may assume that
\cl_{M}(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})
does not contain
e
otherwise the lemma holds.
Thus
\sqcap(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime},L_{1})\neq 2
.
If
\sqcap(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime},L_{1})=0
, then
L_{1}\cup\{e\}
and
\cl_{M|X}(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})
are skew rank-
2
sets in
M/f
each with four points, so
M
has
U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}
as a minor, a contradiction.
We deduce that
\sqcap(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime},L_{1})=1
.
Hence there is some
Z\subseteq X
such that
L_{1}\cup L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq Z
and
M|Z\cong M(K_{5})
.
Let
Z\subseteq Y\subseteq X
so that
M|Y\cong M(K_{6})
.
We may assume that the ground set of
M|Y
is the edge set of the complete graph with vertex set
\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}
, and that the ground set of
M|Z
is the edge set of the complete graph with vertex set
\{0,1,2,3,4\}
.
As
\sqcap(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime},L_{1})=1
, we may assume that
L_{1}=\{01,02,12\}
, and
L_{2}=\{13,24\}
.

Suppose that
\{z,e,f\}
is a circuit of
M
for some
z\in Z
.
Then either
z
meets precisely one of
0
,
1
, or
2
, or
z=34
.
In the first case,
L_{1}\cup\{z\}
spans an
M(K_{4})
-restriction of
M|X
, so
f
is spanned by a plane of
M|X
that does not contain
L_{2}
or
L_{2}^{\prime}
, a contradiction.
If
z=34
, then
e\in\cl_{M}(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})
, a contradiction.
Thus, no such element
z
exists.

Assume that
L_{2}^{\prime}=\{14,23\}
.
As
e\notin\cl_{M}(L_{2}\cup L_{2}^{\prime})
, the elements
13,14,12,34
are on distinct lines of
M/e
through
f
.
Moreover, each element of
(M/e)|(Z\cup\{f\})
is on one of these four lines, or else
M/\{e,f\}
has a
U_{2,5}
-restriction.
The sets
\{f,13,24\}
and
\{f,14,23\}
have rank two in
M/e
, and do not span any element in
\{03,04,34\}
, otherwise
e
is spanned by a plane of
M|Z
that does not contain
L_{1}
, a contradiction.
This means that each element in
\{03,04,34\}
is spanned in
M/e
by either
\{f,12\}
or
\{f,34\}
.
Since
r_{M/e}(\{03,04,34\})=2
, this implies that
\{03,04,34\}
is spanned in
M/e
by
\{f,34\}
, so
\{03,04,34,f\}
is a
U_{2,4}
-restriction of
(M/e)|(Z\cup\{f\})
.
But
\{01,03,04,05\}
is a basis of
(M/e)|Y
, and
\{01,05,15,25\}
is a
U_{2,4}
-restriction of
M/e
, since it spans
L_{1}
in
M
.
Thus,
M/e
has a
(U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4})
-restriction, a contradiction.

Now assume that
L_{2}^{\prime}=\{12,34\}
.
Let
N=(M/e)|(Z\cup\{f\})
, and let
N^{\prime}=N\!\setminus\!\{01,02\}
.
Then
N^{\prime}
is a simple rank-
3
matroid on nine elements.
The sets
\{12,03,13,23\}
and
\{12,04,14,24\}
are lines of
N^{\prime}
because they span
e
in
M
, and the set
\{12,34,f\}
is a line of
N^{\prime}
because it is a line of
M
that does not span
e
.
But then
N^{\prime}
has a
U_{2,5}
-minor or
N^{\prime}\cong R_{9}
, by Lemma
5.1
, a contradiction.
∎

The following lemma imposes structure on a different
2
-element extension of a clique.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
M
be a simple matroid of rank at least six with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}
and with a set
X
so that
M|X\cong M(K_{r(M)+1})
.
Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be distinct
4
-element circuits of
M|X
so that, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, there is an element
e_{i}\in E(M)-X
spanned by both
2
-point lines of
M|X
contained in
C_{i}
.
Then
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=2
, and there is a set
Z\subseteq X
so that
M|Z\cong M(K_{5})
and
C_{1}\cup C_{2}\subseteq Z
.

Proof.

We may assume that
E(M)=X\cup\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
.
Note that
e_{1}\neq e_{2}
, and that
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are both type-
(b)
.
Let
x
and
y
be distinct elements in
\cl_{M|X}(C_{1})-C_{1}
.
Then either
x
or
y
is not in
\cl_{M|X}(C_{2})
, or
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are contained in an
M(K_{4})
-restriction of
M|X
and the lemma follows.
Assume that
x\notin\cl_{M|X}(C_{2})
.
The matroid
(M/x)|(X-\{x\})
has a spanning-clique restriction, and
e_{2}
is spanned by both
2
-point lines of
(M/x)|(X-\{x\})
contained in
C_{2}
.
Moreover, the simplification of
(M/x)|(C_{1}\cup\{y\})
is a
3
-point line,
L
, that spans
e_{1}
in
M/x
.
Then
L\cup C_{2}
is contained in an
M(K_{4})
-restriction of
M/x
, by Lemma
6.1
.
Since
C_{1}
spans
x
in
M
, this implies that there is a set
Z\subseteq X
so that
M|Z\cong M(K_{5})
and
C_{1}\cup C_{2}\subseteq Z
.

We now prove that
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=2
.
We may assume that the ground set of
M|Z
is the edge set of the complete graph with vertex set
\{0,1,2,3,4\}
.
Assume that
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|\neq 2
.
Then
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=1
since
K_{5}
has no edge-disjoint
4
-cycle pairs.
Up to relabeling vertices, we may assume that
C_{1}=\{01,12,23,03\}
and
C_{2}=\{01,14,42,02\}
.

Suppose there is an element
z\in Z
for which
\{z,e_{1},e_{2}\}
is a circuit of
M
.
Assume
z
is in
\cl_{M}(C_{1})
or
\cl_{M}(C_{2})
.
Then
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
spans both
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
.
Since
(\cl_{M}(C_{1}),\cl_{M}(C_{2}))
is a modular pair of flats of
M
whose intersection contains
\{01,12,02\}
and
e_{1}
or
e_{2}
, we deduce that
e_{1}
or
e_{2}
is spanned by
\{01,12,02\}
, a contradiction.
We conclude that
z\notin\cl_{M}(C_{1})\cup\cl_{M}(C_{2})
, and so
z=34
.
The set
\cl_{M}(\{12,03,z\})
contains neither
\{01,24\}
nor
\{02,14\}
, and thus
e_{2}\notin\cl_{M}(\{12,03,z\})
because
e_{2}
is type-
(b)
.
This implies that
e_{2}\notin\cl_{M/z}(\{12,03\})
.
But
e_{1}\in\cl_{M/z}(\{12,03\})
because
e_{1}\in\cl_{M}(\{12,03\})
, and so
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are not parallel in
M/z
, a contradiction.

The set
\{01,02,03,13\}
is a
U_{2,4}
-restriction of
M/e_{1}
.
Since
01
and
23
are parallel in
M/e_{1}
and
\{e_{2},01,24\}
is a line of
M
and
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
spans no element of
X
, the set
\{01,24,34,e_{2}\}
is a
U_{2,4}
-restriction of
M/e_{1}
.
Since
\{01,04,14\}
is a
U_{2,3}
-restriction of
M/e_{1}
and
M/e_{1}\!\setminus\!\{12,23\}
is simple, Lemma
5.1
implies that
M/e_{1}
restricted to the union of the three sets
\{01,02,03,13\}
,
\{01,24,34,e_{2}\}
, and
\{01,04,14\}
is either isomorphic to
R_{9}
, or has a
U_{2,5}
-minor, a contradiction.
∎

7.
k
-Element Extensions

In this section, we place structure on
k
-element extensions of a clique with
k\geq 3
, in three cases.
We say that a point
x
of a simple matroid
M
is
special
if it is on at least two
4
-point lines, is a tip of at least two spike restrictions of
M
, or is a tip of a spike restriction of
M
and is on a
4
-point line.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
M
be a simple matroid with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9}\}
, and with a set
X
so that
M|X\cong M(K_{r(M)+1})
.
If
|E(M)-X|\leq 3
, then
M
has at most
21
special points.

Proof.

If
r(M)\leq 2
, then
|E(M)|\leq|X|+|E(M)-X|\leq 6
and therefore
M
has at most
6
special points, so we may assume that
r(M)\geq 3
.
Since
|E(M)-X|\leq 3
and
M
has no
U_{2,5}
-restriction, the points in
E(M)-X
span at most three lines, each of which spans at most two points in
X
.
By Proposition
5.2
, there are at most
12
elements in
X
that are on a line of
M|X
that spans an element of
E(M)-X
.
Thus, there are at most
18
elements in
X
that are on a long line of
M
that is not a long line of
M|X
.
Because no spike is graphic,
M|X
has no spike restriction.
Therefore, at most
18
elements in
X
are a tip of a spike or on a
4
-point line.
Since
|E(M)-X|\leq 3
, it follows that
M
has at most
21
special points.
∎

We must work harder when
|E(M)-X|
is not bounded.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
M
be a simple matroid with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}
, and with a set
X
so that
M|X\cong M(K_{r(M)+1})
.
If
|E(M)-X|\geq 4
and each element in
E(M)-X
is type-
(a)
, then
M
has exactly one special point.
Moreover, this point is in every
3
-point line of
M|X
which spans an element in
E(M)-X
.

Proof.

We may assume that the ground set of
M|X
is the edge set of the complete graph with vertex set
\{0,1,2,\dots,r(M)\}
.
Since each point in
E(M)-X
is type-
(a)
, each is freely added to a
3
-point line of
M|X
.
Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of
3
-point lines of
M|X
that span an element of
E(M)-X
.

7.2.1
.

The lines in
\mathcal{L}
intersect in a common point.

Proof.

Suppose that the lines in
\mathcal{L}
do not intersect in a common point.
Since
M
has no
(U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4})
-minor, each pair of lines in
\mathcal{L}
has a common element.
Then since
|E(M)-X|\geq 4
,
M
has a restriction
N
that can be obtained from
M(K_{4})
by freely adding a point to each long line.
Let
z
be an element of this
M(K_{4})
-restriction.
Then
z
is on at least two
4
-point lines of
N
.
Suppose
z
is on three or more long lines of
N
.
Then by Lemma
5.1
,
N
has a minor isomorphic to
R_{9}
or
U_{2,5}
, a contradiction.
Therefore
z
is on exactly two long lines of
N
.
But since
|N|=10
and
N
has no
U_{2,5}
-restriction, this implies that
N/z\cong U_{2,5}
, a contradiction.
∎

Without loss of generality, we may assume that the lines in
\mathcal{L}
intersect in the point
x=01
.
Since
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 4
,
x
is special.
We will show that
x
is the unique special point of
M
.
Note that the points of
(M/x)|(X-\{x\})
of size at least two form a basis of
\si((M/x)|(X-\{x\}))
; this implies that
E(M)-X
is independent in
M
.

7.2.2
.

Let
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(M)-X
, and, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
L_{i}
be a line in
\mathcal{L}
that spans
e_{i}
.
If
z\in X
so that
\{z,e_{1},e_{2}\}
is a circuit of
M
, then
z
is the unique element in
\cl_{M|X}(L_{1}\cup L_{2})-(L_{1}\cup L_{2})
.
Moreover, every
4
-point line of
M
contains exactly three elements of
X
including
x
.

Proof.

Certainly
z\in\cl_{M}(L_{1}\cup L_{2})
since
\{z,e_{1},e_{2}\}
is a circuit.
If
z\in L_{1}
, then
L_{1}
spans
e_{2}
.
But then
L_{1}
is a flat of
M|X
that spans
e_{2}
but does not contain
L_{2}
, which contradicts Proposition
5.2
(i).
Thus,
z\notin L_{1}
, and, by symmetry,
z\notin L_{2}
.
Since
|\cl_{M|X}(L_{1}\cup L_{2})|=6
and
|L_{1}\cup L_{2}|=5
, the element
z
is unique.
As
E(M)-X
is independent, it follows that each
4
-point line of
M
contains exactly three elements of
X
including
x
.
∎

By
7.2.2
, if an element
y
of
X
is on a line in
\mathcal{L}
, then each long line of
M
through
y
is spanned by a long line of
M|X
through
y
.
As no spike is graphic,
y
is not a tip of a spike restriction of
M
.
Moreover, if
y\neq x
, then
y
is on exactly one
4
-point line of
M
, so
X
contains at most one special point of
M
that is on a line in
\mathcal{L}
.

If an element
y
of
X
is not on a line in
\mathcal{L}
, then
y
is not on a
4
-point line, since
E(M)-X
is independent.
By
7.2.2
, the element
y
is on at most one more long line in
M
than in
M|X
, which implies that
y
is a tip of at most one spike restriction of
M
.
Thus,
y
is not a special point.
Therefore,
M
has at most one special point in
X
, namely
x
.

Now, let
e\in E(M)-X
.
We will show that
e
is not special.
We may assume that
e
is freely added to the
3
-point line
L^{\prime}=\{01,02,12\}
of
M|X
.
By
7.2.2
, each long line of
M
through
e
contains an element of
X
that uses the vertex
2
.
Let
T
be a transversal of the points of
M/e
so that each element in
T
is in
X
and uses the vertex
2
; then
T
is independent in
M|X
.
Also,
T
contains at most one of
02
and
12
, and so
T
does not span
L^{\prime}
in
M|X
.
Since
e
is freely added to
L^{\prime}
, this implies that
T
does not span
e
in
M
.
Since
T
is independent in
M
and does not span
e
, the element
e
is not a tip of a spike restriction of
M
.
Since
e
is on exactly one
4
-point line of
M
, the element
e
is not special.
Thus,
x
is the unique special point of
M
, so the lemma holds.
∎

We need an analogous lemma for a different case.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
M
be a simple matroid with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}
, and with a set
X
so that
M|X\cong M(K_{r(M)+1})
.
If there is a
3
-point line
\{x,y,z\}
of
M|X
so that each element in
E(M)-X
is either freely added to
\{x,y,z\}
, or is spanned by both
2
-point lines of
M|X
in some
4
-element circuit of
M|X
containing
x
and
y
,
then
M
has at most two special points.

Proof.

Let
L=\{x,y,z\}
.
We may assume that the ground set of
M|X
is the edge set of the complete graph with vertex set
\{0,1,2,\dots,r(M)\}
, and that
(x,y,z)=(01,02,12)
.
By Proposition
5.2
, each element in
E(M)-X
that is not freely added to
L
is type-
(b)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of circuits
C
of
M|X
for which both
2
-point lines of
M|X
in
C
span a common element of
E(M)-X
.
By hypothesis, each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
contains
x
and
y
.

The points of
(M/x)|(X-\{x\})
of size at least two form a basis of
\si((M/x)|(X-\{x\}))
because they all use the composite vertex obtained by identifying
0
and
1
.
This implies that
E(M)-X
is independent in
M
.
Since
E(M)-X
is independent, there is at most one
4
-point line of
M
, namely
\cl_{M}(L)
.

7.3.1
.

Let
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(M)-X
so that neither is freely added to
L
, and, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
C_{i}
be a circuit in
\mathcal{C}
that spans
e_{i}
.
If
w\in X
so that
\{w,e_{1},e_{2}\}
is a circuit of
M
, then
w
is the unique element in
\cl_{M|X}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})-(\cl_{M|X}(C_{1})\cup\cl_{M|X}(C_{2}))
.

Proof.

Certainly
w\in\cl_{M}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})
, since
\{w,e_{1},e_{2}\}
is a circuit.
If
w\in\cl_{M|X}(C_{1})
, then
\cl_{M|X}(C_{1})
is a flat of
M|X
that spans
e_{2}
, but does not contain either
2
-point line of
M|X
contained in
C_{2}
, which contradicts that
e_{2}
is of type-
(b)
.
Thus,
w\notin\cl_{M}(C_{1})
, and, by symmetry,
w\notin\cl_{M}(C_{2})
.
As
C_{1}\cap C_{2}=\{x,y\}
, it follows that
|\cl_{M|X}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})|=10
and
|\cl_{M|X}(C_{1})\cup\cl_{M|X}(C_{2})|=9
.
Thus, the element
w
is unique.
∎

The following deals with a slightly different case.

7.3.2
.

Let
e\in E(M)-X
be freely added to
L
, and let
f\in E(M)-(X\cup e)
be spanned by a circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
If
w\in X
so that
\{w,e,f\}
is a circuit of
M
, then
w
is the unique element in
\cl_{M|X}(C)-(L\cup C)
.

Proof.

Certainly
w\in\cl_{M|X}(C)
, since
C
spans
L
and
\{w,e,f\}
is a circuit.
If
w\in L
, then
\cl_{M}(L)
contains
\{x,y,z,e,f\}
, a contradiction.
If
w\in C
, then there is a
2
-point line of
M|X
that contains
w
and spans
e
but does not contain
L
, which contradicts the fact that
e
is freely added to
L
.
Since
|\cl_{M|X}(C)|=6
and
|L\cup C|=5
, the element
w
is unique.
∎

If

8.
The Spanning-Clique Case

We prove the following result for matroids with a spanning-clique restriction.
Recall that a point
x
of a simple matroid

is
special
if it is on at least two
4
-point lines, is a tip of at least two spike restrictions of
M
, or is a tip of a spike restriction of
M
and is on a
4
-point line.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
M
be a simple matroid of rank at least six with a spanning-clique restriction.
If
M
has no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}
, then

(i)

|M|\leq{r(M)+2\choose 2}-2
, and

(ii)

M
has at most
21
special points.

Proof.

Let
M|X\cong M(K_{r(M)+1})
.
Note that
|M|\leq{r(M)+2\choose 2}-2
if and only if
|E(M)-X|\leq r(M)-1
.
By Proposition
5.2
, each element of
E(M)-X
is either freely added to a
3
-point line of
M|X
, or corresponds to the modular cut generated by a pair of
2
-point lines of
M|X
whose union is a circuit.
Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of
3
-point lines of
M|X
that span an element in
E(M)-X
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of
4
-element circuits
C
of
M|X
for which both
2
-point lines of
M|X
contained in
C
span a common element of
E(M)-X
.

Clearly each line in
\mathcal{L}
spans exactly one element of
E(M)-X
, or else
M
has a
U_{2,5}
-restriction.
If there is a pair
\{L,L^{\prime}\}
of
2
-point lines
L
and
L^{\prime}
of
M|X
whose union is a circuit so that
L
and
L^{\prime}
each span elements
e
and
f
in
E(M)-X
, then
L\cup\{e,f\}
and
L^{\prime}\cup\{e,f\}
are distinct lines of
M
that intersect in two points, a contradiction.
Thus,
|E(M)-X|=|\mathcal{L}|+|\mathcal{C}|
.

We first deal with the case when two circuits in
\mathcal{C}
span each other.

8.1.1
.

If there are distinct circuits
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in
\mathcal{C}
whose union is contained in an
M(K_{4})
-restriction of
M|X
, then
|E(M)-X|\leq 3
, and
(i)
and
(ii)
hold.

Proof.

For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
e_{i}
be the element of
M
that is spanned by each
2
-point line of
M|X
contained in
C_{i}
.
Since
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is contained in an
M(K_{4})
-restriction of
M|X
, there is a line
\{x,y\}
of
M|X
contained in both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}\neq\varnothing
, and let
L\in\mathcal{L}
.
Then
L\subseteq C_{1}\cup C_{2}
, or else either
(L,C_{1})
or
(L,C_{2})
violates Lemma
6.1
.
Either
x
or
y
is in
L
, since every triangle of
K_{4}
intersects every size-
2
matching; assume without loss of generality that
x\in L
.
But then
x
is on the
4
-point lines
\cl_{M}(L)
and
\{x,y,e_{1},e_{2}\}
, and also a
3
-point line other than
L
in

, a contradiction to Lemma
5.1
.
Thus,
\mathcal{L}=\varnothing
.

If there is some circuit
C_{3}\in\mathcal{C}
that is not contained in
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
,
then there are at most two elements of
C_{3}
in
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
.
Since
|C_{1}\cap C_{3}|=2
and
|C_{2}\cap C_{3}|=2
by Lemma
6.2
, this implies that
C_{3}
contains
\{x,y\}
.
But the only
4
-element circuits of
M|X
that contain
\{x,y\}
are
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
Thus, each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
is contained in
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
.
Since
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is contained in an
M(K_{4})
-restriction of
M|X
, it follows that
|\mathcal{C}|\leq 3
.
Then
|E(M)-X|\leq 3
so
(i)
holds, and
(ii)
holds by Lemma
7.1
.
∎

Next we treat the case when the lines in
\mathcal{L}
do not share a common element.

8.1.2
.

If
\mathcal{L}
contains a set of three lines that do not intersect in a common point, then
|E(M)-X|=3
, and
(i)
and
(ii)
hold.

Proof.

Since
M
has no
(U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4})
-minor, each pair of lines in
\mathcal{L}
share a common element.
Since the lines in
\mathcal{L}
have no common point of intersection, there is a set
Z\subseteq X
so that
M|Z\cong M(K_{4})
and
Z
contains each line in
\mathcal{L}
.
Suppose
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 4
.
Then Lemma
7.2
shows that the lines in
\mathcal{L}
intersect in a common element, a contradiction.
Thus
|\mathcal{L}|=3
, and so there are three elements

that are each on two lines in
\mathcal{L}
.

Suppose
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 1
.
By Lemma
6.1
, each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
is contained in
Z
, and so each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
contains one of

, since
|Z|=6
.
But then one of

is on two
4
-point lines and a

-point line of

, so

has an

-restriction or a

-minor by Lemma

.
This is a contradiction, so
\mathcal{C}=\varnothing
, and so
|E(M)-X|=3
.
Then
|E(M)-X|\leq 3
so
(i)
holds, and
(ii)
holds by Lemma
7.1
.
∎

We now consider four cases, depending on the size of
\mathcal{L}
.
Suppose first that
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 3
.
By
8.1.2
, the lines in
\mathcal{L}
share a common element
x
; thus,
|\mathcal{L}|\leq r(M)-1
.
Since
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 3
, the set
\mathcal{C}
is empty, or else there is a line
L\in\mathcal{L}
and a circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}
so that
(L,C)
violates Lemma
6.1
.
Then
|E(M)-X|=|\mathcal{L}|\leq r(M)-1
, and
(i)
holds.
Also,
(ii)
holds by Lemma
7.2
if
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 4
and by Lemma
7.1
if
|\mathcal{L}|=3
.

Next assume that
|\mathcal{L}|=2
.
The union of the two lines in
\mathcal{L}
is contained in an
M(K_{4})
-restriction

of

, and by Lemma
6.1
, each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
is contained in

.
By Lemma

, the element

that is on both lines in
\mathcal{L}
is in no circuit in
\mathcal{C}
.
This implies that
|\mathcal{C}|\leq 1
, and so
|E(M)-X|\leq 3\leq r(M)-1
, and
(ii)
holds by Lemma
7.1
.

Now suppose that
|\mathcal{L}|=1
.
Let
L\in\mathcal{L}
, and let
e
be the element of

that is spanned by

.
By
8.1.1
, we may assume that each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
spans at most one element of

other than

.
By Lemma

, each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
contains two elements of

.
If, for two different circuits

and

in
\mathcal{C}
, we have

, then, as

, we see that

, which contradicts Lemma

.
Thus, there are elements

and

in

that are contained in each circuit in

.
This implies that

, and so

.
Also,
(ii)
holds by Lemma

.

Finally, suppose that
|\mathcal{L}|=0
.
By

, we may assume that each

-restriction of

spans at most one element of

.
By Lemma

, each pair of these

-restrictions has a common triangle.
If there is no shared triangle among all of these restrictions, then there are exactly three

-restrictions of

that span an element of

, and they are contained in an

-restriction of

.
Then
|E(M)-X|=3
, so
(i)
and
(ii)
hold, using Lemma

.
Otherwise, there is a common triangle

-restriction that spans an element of

.
By the same reasoning at the end of the previous case, there are elements

and

in
L
that are contained in each circuit in
\mathcal{C}
, and so
(i)
and
(ii)
hold.
∎

9.
The Proofs of the Main Results

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.1
.
We use two results of Geelen, Nelson, and Walsh from
[
6
]
.
To state these results, we need a notion of matroid connectivity.
A
vertical
j
-separation
of a matroid
M
is a partition
(X,Y)
of
E(M)
so that
r(X)+r(Y)-r(M)<j
and
\min(r(X),r(Y))\geq j
.
A matroid
M
is
vertically
k
-connected
if it has no vertical
j
-separation with
j<k
.
The first result
[
6
, Theorem 6.1.3]
finds structure in a minimal counterexample.
The second, which is a consequence of Theorem 7.6.1 in
[
6
]
, allows us to move from a clique minor to a spanning-clique restriction.

Theorem 9.1
.

There is a function
r_{\ref{new reduction}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{Z}
so that, for all integers
\ell\geq 2
and
t,s\geq 1
, and any real polynomial
p(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
with
a>0
, if
M\in\mathcal{U}(\ell)
satisfies
r(M)\geq r_{\ref{new reduction}}(a,b,c,\ell,t,s)
and
\varepsilon(M)>p(r(M))
, then
M
has a minor
N
with
\varepsilon(N)>p(r(N))
and
r(N)\geq t
such that either

(1)

N
has a spanning-clique restriction, or

(2)

N
is vertically
s
-connected and has an
s
-element independent set
S
so that
\varepsilon(N)-\varepsilon(N/e)>p(r(N))-p(r(N)-1)
for each
e\in S
.

Theorem 9.2
.

There are functions
s_{\ref{spikes}}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
r_{\ref{spikes}}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{3}\to\mathbb{Z}
so that, for all integers
\ell\geq 2
,
k\geq 1
, and
m\geq 3
,
if
M
is a vertically
s_{\ref{spikes}}(k)
-connected matroid with no
U_{2,\ell+2}
-minor and no Reid geometry minor, and with an

-minor and

has a simple minor

of rank at least

-restriction and

special points.

In order to apply Theorem
9.2
, we need the following result of Geelen and Whittle

]

Theorem 9.3
.

There is a function
\alpha_{\ref{clique minor}}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}

-minor.

We now prove a restatement of Theorem
1.3
and then Theorem

.

Theorem 9.4
.

There is a constant

so that if

is a simple matroid of rank at least

with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}

|M|\leq{r(M)+2\choose 2}-2

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the class of matroids with no minor in
\{U_{2,5},F_{7},R_{9},U_{2,4}\oplus U_{2,4}\}
.
Note that
{x+2\choose 2}-2=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{3}{2}x-1

Let

Assume that there is a simple matroid

with

9.4.1
.

Each element in

Proof.

Then

\displaystyle\varepsilon(N)-\varepsilon(N/e)=1+\sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{N}(e)}\big(|L|-2\big).

∎

Since

Proof of Theorem
