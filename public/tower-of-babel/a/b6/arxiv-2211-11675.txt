Moment Propagation
by John T. Ormerod

Abstract
We introduce and develop moment propagation for approximate Bayesian inference. This method can be viewed as a variance correction for mean field variational Bayes which tends to underestimate posterior variances. Focusing on the case where the model is described by two sets of parameter vectors, we develop moment propagation algorithms for linear regression, multivariate normal, and probit regression models. We show for the probit regression model that moment propagation empirically performs reasonably well for several benchmark datasets. Finally, we discuss theoretical gaps and future extensions. In the supplementary material we show heuristically why moment propagation leads to appropriate posterior variance estimation, for the linear regression and multivariate normal models we show precisely why mean field variational Bayes underestimates certain moments, and prove that our moment propagation algorithm recovers the exact marginal posterior distributions for all parameters, and for probit regression we show that moment propagation provides asymptotically correct posterior means and covariance estimates.



Keywords:
Approximate Bayesian Inference, Variational Bayes, Moment Propagation.

Introduction

Variational Inference (VI) methods, also referred to as Variational Bayes (VB), are at the forefront in the analysis of models arising in many large complex problems, particularly where the sheer size of problem means a full Bayesian analysis is
infeasible.
Blei et al. 2017
summarises a large number of successful applications in
computational biology, computer vision and robotics, computational neuroscience, natural language processing, and speech recognition, and other applications where VI has made an impact.

VI methods perform the probability calculus behind many probabilistic machine learning methods in an approximate way in order to fit models quickly thereby avoiding Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) whose simulations are often much slower in practice. Broadly speaking, variational approximation is used to describe techniques where integration problems are transformed into optimization problems. Indeed several approximate Bayesian inference methods are based on approximating the posterior distribution by a chosen parametric form, and minimizing a discrepancy between the parametric form (sometimes called
q
-densities) and the exact posterior distribution. In particular MFVB, a popular VI approach, uses the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
(
Kullback and Leibler 1951
)
, and approximates the posterior distribution as a product of marginal distributions which are updated in an iterative fashion (discussed in greater detail in
Section
2.1
). Accessible expositions of these methods can be found in
Bishop 2006
,
Ormerod and Wand 2010
,
Murphy 2013
, and
Blei et al. 2017
. A relatively comprehensive broad overview of the more recent advances on VI can be found in
Zhang et al. 2019
.

The MFVB class of VI methods is often fast, deterministic, often have simple updates leading to uncomplicated implementations, and can perform well for certain models.
Despite the successes noted above, they are not without drawbacks. Without additional modification, MFVB is limited to conjugate models, and can be slow to converge. Most importantly, MFVB can be shown, either empirically or theoretically, to underestimate posterior variances
(
Humphreys and Titterington 2000
;
Hall et al. 2002
;
Wang and Titterington 2005
;
Consonni and Marin 2007
;
Turner et al. 2011
)
. For this reason these methods have been restricted to situations such as model exploration and prediction where inference is less important than other aspects of the analysis. In this paper we develop a new method,
Moment Propagation
(MP), makes progress towards solving the long-standing problem of MFVB underestimating posterior variances potentially making them suitable for Bayesian inferences for some models.

The limitation of MFVB to conjugate models
(where the full conditionals for every parameters is in the form of a known parametric family) lead to several modifications of the original approach.
Various approaches have been attempted to circumvent this limitation including local lower bounds for logistic regression
(
Jaakkola and Jordan 1997
)
and
(
Murphy 2013
, using the Bohning bound, Section 21.8.2)
,
structured variational inference
(
Saul and Jordan 1996
)

expanding the set of parameters using normal scale mixture representations
(
Consonni and Marin 2007
;
Neville et al. 2014
;
Luts and Ormerod 2014
)
,
approximate auxiliary variable representations via Gaussian mixtures

(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2009
)
, MFVB-Laplace hybrids
(
Friston et al. 2007
;
Wang and Blei 2013
)
,
using additional delta method approximations
(
Teh et al. 2006
;
Braun and McAuliffe 2010
;
Wang and Blei 2013
)
, and numerical quadrature
(
Faes et al. 2011
;
Pham et al. 2013
)
.

Wand et al. 2011
provide an overview of several of these approaches.
While these methods significantly increase the set of models possible to fit using MFVB-type
ideas, they are mostly applied to specific models, and they
usually come at additional cost both in terms speed, accuracy, and ease of implementation.

Fixed Form variational Bayes (FFVB) provide an alternative more flexible and applicable approach.
FFVB steps away from the product from of the density and chooses the
q
density to have a know parametric form. The most common choice of
q
-density is a multivariate Gaussian
(
Opper and Archambeau 2009
, e.g.,)
. The resulting ELBO
can be maximized directly
(
Opper and Archambeau 2009
;
Challis and Barber 2013
)
or through further approximation via the delta method
(
Braun and McAuliffe 2010
;
Wang and Blei 2013
)
.
More recently FFVB methods have stepped away from deterministic approaches to methods based on stochastic optimization
(
Hoffman and Gelman 2014
)
.
These allow for more flexibility in the choice of
q
-density than approaches directly optimize the ELBO because they avoid calculating expectations
arising in the ELBO numerically. However, this advantage is potentially at the cost of computational speed and may be susceptible to the lack of algorithmic convergence if the learning rate is not selected carefully. These methods are dominated by
stochastic gradient ascent approaches
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
;
Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla 2014
;
Ranganath et al. 2014
;
Kucukelbir et al. 2017
;
Tan 2021
)
. For an introduction
to this area see
Tran et al. 2020
. More recently there has been a push towards more flexible
q
-densities including skew-normal densities
including copulas based approaches

(
Han et al. 2016
;
Smith et al. 2020
)
, and highly flexible approaches such as variational inference with normalizing flows
(
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
)
,
variational hierarchical models
(
Ranganath et al. 2016
)
allowing for hierarchical representations of the
q
-densities, and boosting variational inference
(
Guo et al. 2016
;
Miller et al. 2017
;
Locatello et al. 2018
;
Dresdner et al. 2021
)
which uses normal mixtures.

There has been a growing push from researchers towards a rigorous understanding of VB procedures
mainly in asymptotic settings
(
Wang and Blei 2019
)
, and finite sample diagnostics.
Finite sample diagnostic approaches

(
Zhao and Marriott 2013
;
Huggins et al. 2020
;
Yao et al. 2018
)
.
However, several of these require Monte Carlo sampling and can add an overhead to the
original fit and detract from the original simplicity of MFVB methods. A recent fast alternative, Linear Response Variational Bayes (LRVB)
(
Giordano et al. 2015
)
allows a post-hoc correction of variational Bayes, but requires the VB approximated
posterior mean to be close to the posterior mode. Such approaches appear promising.

Similarly to MFVB the idea behind MP is to build parametric models (
q
-densities) for the marginal likelihoods. However,
MP does not rely on the assumption that the marginal posteriors are independent, nor does MP explicitly
minimize a discrepancy such as the KL-divergence. Instead MP accumulates information of the posterior distribution by approximating
marginal moments, and then using these moments to fit models to each marginal distribution. More specifically, for
a particular marginal posterior density, we use the remaining
q
-densities to approximate the
marginal posterior moments via, but not necessarily limited to, the law of total expectation and the
law of total variance. We have found such approximations quite accurate and could potentially be used
as a diagnostic for validating VI methods. Where possible we then use the method
of moments to pass (or propagate) moment information to the particular marginal posterior approximation.
Since the use of the the law of total expectation and the law of total variance for MP requires us to know the parametric forms of the
full conditional distributions, thus in this paper we are limited to conjugate
models. Further, MP requires the marginal posterior distributions corresponding to all unobserved parameters
in the Markov Blanket of a parameter need to be approximated. This leads to further complications. Hence,
we limit ourselves to two sets of parameter vectors. These complications will be addressed in future papers.

We illustrate our proposed method for linear regression, multivariate normal, and probit regression models.
We choose these models because (1) all of these models can be represented using two sets of parameters;
(2) because of their simple form almost all of the analysis for these models can be performed at a
reasonable depth; and (3) because they are archetypal models and if any new methodology fails in these
cases then it can be discarded into the dustbin of history. Successful application of MP for models
involving more than two sets of parameters will involve developing diagnostics for dependencies
and modelling such dependencies between all pairs of parameters. We leave this issue to future papers.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

We introduce the Moment Propagation method.

2.

We consider MFVB and MP methods for the linear regression, multivariate normal, and probit regression models.

3.

We show empirically that MP can provide better estimates than the posterior mean and variance of the regression coefficients compared to MFVB and Laplace approximations, and comparatively well to other methods.

In the supplementary material:

1.

We prove that MFVB underestimates the posterior variances and particular posterior expectations for these models.

2.

We prove are exact for linear regression and multivariate normal models, and an algorithm for probit regression with asymptotically correct posterior mean and covariance estimates.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section 2 we review Variational Bayesian inference. In Section 3 we introduce Moment Propagation. In Sections 4 to 6, we develop MP methods for the linear regression, multivariate normal, and probit regression models respectively. In Section 7 we discuss the limitations of MP and how these might be addressed, theoretical problems to solve, and compare MP with other methods. All derivations and proofs can be found in the
Appendices which appear as an online supplement.

2
Variational inference

Suppose that we have data
{\mathcal{D}}
and model this data via some
conditional likelihood
p({\mathcal{D}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
with parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In Bayesian inference, the parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is assigned a prior
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. The posterior distribution of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, denoted
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
, is
given by

p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})=\frac{p({\mathcal{D}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,p({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{p({\mathcal{D}})}\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad p({\mathcal{D}})=\int p({\mathcal{D}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,p({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,d{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

is the marginal distribution for
{\mathcal{D}}
.
In the above equation the integral is replaced by combinatorial sums for
the subset of parameters of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
that are discrete.
For most problems of interest there is no analytic expression
for
p({\mathcal{D}})
and approximation is required.

In VB, the posterior density
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
is approximated by some convenient
density
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
which is chosen to minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence between a chosen
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and the target posterior
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
.
This leads
to the following lower bound on the marginal log-likelihood

\begin{array}[]{rl}\log p({\mathcal{D}})&\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{q}\left[\log\left(\frac{p({\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)\right]+\mbox{KL}(q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,,\,p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}}))\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{q}\left[\log\left(\frac{p({\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)\right]\equiv\mbox{ELBO}\end{array}

(1)

where
\mbox{KL}(\,q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,,\,p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})\,)\equiv{\mathbb{E}}_{q}\left[\log\left\{p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})/q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right\}\right]

is the Kullback-Leibler divergence and
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}
denotes an expectation taken with respect to
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. The inequality in (
1
) follows from the fact that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is strictly positive and equal to zero if and only if
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})=q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

almost everywhere
(
Kullback and Leibler 1951
)
.
The second line in (
1
)
defines the ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound), a lower bound on the marginal log-likelihood,
\log p({\mathcal{D}})
.
Maximizing the ELBO with respect to
q
(over the set of all densities) or, if
q
is parameterized, over the parameters of
q
(called variational parameters) tightens the difference between
the ELBO and
\log p({\mathcal{D}})
, leading to an improved approximation
q
of
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
. The ELBO is often used to monitor convergence of VB methods.
There are two main strategies used to select
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

leading to Mean Field Variational Bayes (MFVB) and Fixed Form Variational
Bayes (FFVB) methods.

2.1
Mean Field Variational Bayes

For MFVB the parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is partitioned into

K
subvectors
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{k}}
.
We specify
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, a model for
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
, as
a product of marginal densities

q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}).

(2)

These

q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
’s act as approximations to the marginal posterior distributions of the
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
’s, i.e.,

p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})\approx q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
.

The form (
2
) assumes mutual posterior independence of the
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
’s,
a typically strong assumption, but may be reasonable in models with orthogonal parameters following some reparameterization.

Bishop 2006
(Section 10.1.2) provides
a heuristic argument that it is this independence assumption as the cause of the posterior variance
underestimation of MFVB methods. Furthermore, the KL-divergence is known to be “zero-avoiding”
(
Bishop 2006
, Section 10.1.2)
,
also potentially leading to posterior approximations with lighter tails than the true posterior
distribution. Despite these reasons suggesting VB methods always underestimate posterior variances,
examples exist in which this claim does not hold
Turner et al. 2008
.

Given the form (
2
), for fixed
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})\equiv\prod_{j\neq k}q_{j}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j})
,
it can be shown that the
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
which minimizes the KL divergence between
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
is of the form

q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\propto\exp\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{-q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})}\left\{\log p({\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right\}\right]

(3)

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{-q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})}(\,\cdot\,)
denotes expectation with respect to

q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})

(see
Bishop 2006
or
Ormerod and Wand 2010
for a derivation of this result).

For MFVB,
the ELBO is usually optimized
via coordinate ascent where
the densities (
3
) are calculated sequentially for
k=1,\ldots,K
and
all but the
k
th
q
-density, i.e.,
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})
, remains fixed
(
Blei et al. 2017
, leading to this approach
being named Coordinate Ascent Variational Inference in )
.
It can be shown that each update
results in a monotonic increase
in the value of the ELBO, which can then be used to monitor the convergence of the MFVB algorithm.
This usually means stopping when successive values of the ELBO differ by less than some threshold.
These iterations are guaranteed to converge under weak regularity conditions
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
. This process is summarised by Algorithm
1
.

If conjugate priors are used, then each

’s
will belong to a recognizable density family and the coordinate ascent
updates reduce to updating
parameters in the
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
family
(
Winn and Bishop 2005
)
.
In this case we let
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\equiv q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k};{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
where the
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}
’s are parameters of the density
q_{k}
.
In this paper we refer to the value of the parameters that maximizes equation (

) as

.
When the
k
th

-density has a known parametric form the updates for the
k
th partition
can be represented via

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mbox{update}_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1:k-1}^{(t+1)},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1:K}^{(t)})
,
for some function
\mbox{update}_{k}(\,\cdot\,)

where
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1:k-1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}\leftarrow\mbox{update}_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{-k})

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{-k}

th parameter removed and we drop all superscripts for brevity.
The consistency conditions then become the following system of
equations

\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}=\mbox{update}_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{-k})\}_{k=1}^{K}.

(5)

The above consistency conditions are used for analyzing the theoretical properties of the MFVB approximations for various models (see Appendix C.4 and Appendix D.4).

Fixed Form Variational Bayes and half-way houses

The MFVB approximation follows directly from the choice of partition of the parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Given the choice of factorization the distributional forms of
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
follow immediately from
(
3
). FFVB, on the other hand chooses the distributional form of
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv q({\boldsymbol{\theta}};{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
where
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
are parameters of
q
in advance. The optimal choice of

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is determined by minimizing
\mbox{KL}(q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,,\,p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}}))
. References for FFVB
can be found in the section

. In addition to MFVB and FFVB there are half-way houses between these methods. These include Semiparametric Mean Field Variational Bayes

3
Moment propagation

We will now describe our proposed Moment Propagation (MP) method.
In what follows it is helpful to work with directed acyclic graph (DAG) representations of Bayesian
statistical models. In this representation nodes of the DAG correspond to random variables or random
vectors in the Bayesian model, and the directed edges convey conditional independence. In this setting
the Markov blanket of a node is the set of children, parents, and co-parents of that node

(see
Bishop 2006
, Chapter 8 for an introduction)
.

Like MFVB, we often partition the parameter
vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
corresponding to the nodes in the DAG representation, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}\}
where it is often of interest to approximate the marginal
posterior distributions
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
,
k=1,\ldots,K
. Otherwise the user can provide a partition.
Unlike MFVB however, we will not assume that
the model for the joint posterior is not a product of marginal approximations, i.e.,

q({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\neq\prod_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
, nor
model the joint posterior distribution explicitly, and lastly, we will not use a discrepancy
(for example, the Kullback-Leibler divergence) to determine
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
.

We observe the following identity for the marginal posterior distribution for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})&\displaystyle=\int p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})\,p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})\,d{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k}\\
&\displaystyle=\int p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))\,p(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})\,d\,\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}),\par\end{array}

where
\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
is the set of unobserved
nodes in the Markov blanket
of the node
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
(integration is replaced with combinatorial sums where appropriate).
The conditional density
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})

is the full conditional density for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
and appears in methods such as Gibbs sampling
and MFVB. In this paper we only consider problems where
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})

takes the form of a known parametric density, i.e., conjugate models. Non-conjugacy leads to further complications that can be handled in multiple ways, but will not be considered in this paper.

Suppose that we have an approximation for
p(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
,
say
q(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))
. We could then approximate
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
by

p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})\approx q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})={\mathbb{E}}_{q(\text{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))}\left[p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))\right].

Generally, this involves an integral which we usually cannot evaluate analytically. If we can sample from
q(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))

this integral could be calculated using Monte Carlo, however, we have found that this approach is often slower than the approach we use later in this paper.

Instead, we choose a convenient parameterization of
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k};{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
, e.g.,
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k};{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})=\phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k};{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}=\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}\}
, and choose the settings of the hyperparameters
to approximate the exact posterior moments

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\left\{f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}}\right\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{p}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left\{f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\approx{\mathbb{E}}_{q}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left\{f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\right\}\right],

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}\left[f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\right],

for a set of functions
\{f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i})\}_{s=1}^{S_{k}}

where
f_{ks}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{ks}}
so that
\mbox{dim}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S_{k}}d_{ks}
and the inner expectation on the RHS of the first line is taken with respect to the full conditional
p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}|{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))
.
We call such moments the MP moments.
This can be thought of as gaining information about the approximate posterior for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}

through moments. We also calculate the moments with respect to the corresponding MP density

{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})]=F_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
.

Using moment matching, i.e., matching the moments of
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}[f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})]
with the corresponding MP moments

F_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
’s gives us a system of nonlinear equations

\begin{array}[]{c}\{{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}[f_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})]=F_{ks}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})\}_{s=1}^{S_{k}}.\end{array}

(6)

These
moment conditions
are analogous to the consistency conditions (
5
). The solution to these equations for become MP updates. We can think of this as passing the moments as approximated using

p({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))
and
q(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))
to the moments of
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
.

For example, suppose that we calculate the first two central MP moments via approximate forms of the law of total expectation and variance, we might use

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})&\displaystyle=&{\mathbb{E}}_{q(\mbox{\scriptsize MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))\right\}\right],\qquad\mbox{and}\\[8.61108pt]
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})&\displaystyle=&{\mathbb{E}}_{q(\mbox{\scriptsize MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))}\left[{\mathbb{V}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))\right\}\right]+{\mathbb{V}}_{q(\mbox{\scriptsize MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}))\right\}\right].\end{array}

(7)

If
q_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k};{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})=\phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k};\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{k},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k})
then moment matching leads
to the solution
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{k}={\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}={\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
.
Many densities have corresponding method of moments estimators. If
q
-densities are taken to be a density with a simple method of moments estimator, then this step can be performed very quickly.

The remaining detail is in how to approximate
p(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
. There are numerous ways to do this. However, at this point we narrow down to the case where
K=2
. When
K=2
we have

\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}\}
, and
\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}\}
, and we
approximate
p(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
by
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})
and

p(\mbox{MB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})\,|\,{\mathcal{D}})
by
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})
.
Then the MP algorithm for
K=2
then has two steps for each partition
k
, a moment estimation step, and a moment matching step. We then iterate between these two steps for each
k
. This process is summarised in Algorithm
2
. In the supplementary material in Appendix B we show that MP is exact for the example examined in
Bishop 2006
(Section 10.1.2) whereas
Bishop 2006
showed that MFVB generally underestimates posterior variances.

3.1
Convergence

One drawback of MP is the loss of the ELBO often used to monitor convergence where updates are guaranteed to result in a monotonic increase of the ELBO, and whose updates converge to at least a local maximizer of the ELBO.

Instead we will use the
q
-density parameters
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
to monitor convergence. We note that updates
can be written of the form

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(t+1)}=M({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(t)})

for some function
M
which maps the space of
q
-density parameters to itself.
If the sequence
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(t)}
converges to some point
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}
and
M(\,\cdot\,)
is continuous,
then
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}
must satisfy the fixed point equation
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}=M({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*})
, i.e., all moment
conditions are satisfied. The rate of convergence and other properties can then be analysed by considering the
Jacobian of
M(\,\cdot\,)
.
In practice we declare convergence when
\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(t+1)}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(t)}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
for some small
\epsilon>0
.
We have used
\epsilon=10^{-6}
in our numerical work. In order to make comparisons more comparable we also use this criteria for MFVB instead of the ELBO.

6
Probit regression

In the previous two models we were able to use the true parametric forms
of the marginal posterior distributions to inform the shapes of the

q
-densities. For the following example we do not compare the theoretical properties of the posterior approximation methods with exact posterior as it is intractable. Instead, we compare them with the
asymptotic form of the posterior distribution which is a multivariate Gaussian distribution by the Bernstein-von-Mises theorem.
Further, this example is interesting because we will not be specifying
the MP density for one of the sets of parameters.

Consider the probit model

p({\bf y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi({\bf x}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{y_{i}}[1-\Phi({\bf x}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]^{1-y_{i}},

where
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
are class labels and
{\bf x}_{i}
is a vector of
p
predictors with
i=1,\ldots,n
,

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is a vector of
p
coefficients to be estimated, and
\Phi(x)
is the normal cumulative distribution function.
It will be more convenient to work with the following representation

p({\bf y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi((2y_{i}-1){\bf x}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi({\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

where
{\bf z}_{i}=(2y_{i}-1){\bf x}_{i}
.
The advantage of this transformation is that
y_{i}
is absorbed into
the
{\bf z}_{i}
’s and we can fit the probit regression model for the case
where all the
y_{i}
’s are equal to one, with design matrix
{\bf Z}
where the

i
th row of
{\bf Z}
is
{\bf z}_{i}
, thus reducing algebra.
We will assume
a multivariate Gaussian prior of the form

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sim N_{p}({\bf 0},{\bf D}^{-1})
.
where
{\bf D}
is a positive definite matrix. For vague priors we might set
{\bf D}=\lambda\,{\bf I}_{p}
for some small
\lambda>0
.

To obtain tractable full conditionals we use an
auxiliary variable representation of the likelihood, equivalent to

Albert and Chib 1993
, where

p(y_{i}\,|\,a_{i},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\phi(a_{i}-{\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad p(a_{i})=I(a_{i}>0).

(30)

We can use this alternative representation since
\int p(y_{i},a_{i}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}})da_{i}=p(y_{i}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\Phi({\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. Note that, despite the prior
p(a_{i})
being
improper, the conditional likelihood
p(y_{i}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is proper.

q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}\leftarrow{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\bf a})
and

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}\leftarrow{\bf S}+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\bf a}){\bf Z}{\bf S}.

Note that the first term in the expression for
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})

is the VB approximation of
{\mathbb{V}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,|\,{\bf y})
. The second term was noted by
Consonni and Marin 2007
who used it to argue that the posterior variance was underestimated by MFVB. The second term measures the extent to which the posterior
variance is underestimated given approximations for
{\mathbb{E}}({\bf a}\,|\,{\bf y})

and
{\mathbb{V}}({\bf a}\,|\,{\bf y})
.
Here we explicitly approximate this second term for our MP approach.

Matching
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\bf a})
with
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\bf a})
and

{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\bf a})
with
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\bf a})

requires calculation of
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\bf a})

and
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\bf a})
given by

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\bf a})&\displaystyle={\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}+{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]\qquad\mbox{and}\\[4.30554pt]
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\bf a})&\displaystyle={\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}]+{\mathbb{V}}_{q}\left[{\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right]\end{array}

where

\zeta_{k}(t)=d^{k}\log\Phi(t)/dt^{k}
. The function
\zeta_{1}(t)
is sometimes referred to as the inverse Mills ratio
(
Grimmett and Stirzaker 2001
)

and requires special care to evaluate numerically when
t
is large and negative
(
Monahan 2011
;
Wand and Ormerod 2012
)
.
We use the approach of
Wand and Ormerod 2012
to evaluate
\zeta_{1}(t)
which involves an evaluation of
continued fractions implemented in
C
for speed. Alternatively, one could use
the
R
package
sn
to evaluate
\zeta_{1}(t)

(
Azzalini 2021
)
via the

zeta(k,t)
function. However, we have found this approach to be slower than our
C
implementation.
There is no closed form expression for

{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]
,
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]
, or
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}\left[{\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right]
.

Using the fact that if
q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\sim N(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta})

then
{\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q}}{{\sim}}N({\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\bf z}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf z}_{i})

the expectations
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\zeta_{1}({\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]\equiv\xi_{1}({\bf z}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},{\bf z}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf z}_{i})
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[\zeta_{2}({\bf z}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]\equiv\xi_{2}({\bf z}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},{\bf z}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf z}_{i})

where

\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\zeta_{d}(x)\phi(x;\mu,\sigma^{2})dx.

(31)

We considered delta method and quadrature methods to approximating (
31
).

1.

DM:
For this approximation we used the simple approximation we used the 2nd order delta method with
\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\approx\zeta_{d}(\mu)+\tfrac{1}{2}\zeta_{d+2}(\mu)\sigma^{2}
.

2.

QUAD:
For values of
\sigma^{2}<1/2
we employed a 10th order Taylor series around
x=\mu

on the integrand and integrated analytically, and for
\sigma^{2}>1/2
we used composite
trapezoidal integration on a grid of
N=50
samples on the effective domain of integration.
The former approach was fast and accurate, but gave poor accuracy for large values of
\sigma^{2}
.
The trapezoidal approach was much slower. We implemented our approach in
C
for speed.

The DM method is faster but less accurate then QUAD in its approximation of (
31
). More details on the two strategies are provided in Appendix E.3.
Further work is needed to approximate
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}\left[{\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right]
.
A first order delta method approximation of
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}\left[{\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right]
leads to

{\mathbb{V}}_{q}\left[{\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right]\approx[{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta})\}]\,{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}\,[{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta})\}].

Using the above, the approximate updates for
q({\bf a})
are

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\bf a})&\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}+\xi_{1}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}))\qquad\mbox{and}\\[8.61108pt]
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\bf a})&\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}[\xi_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}))]\\[4.30554pt]
&\displaystyle\qquad+[{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta})\}]\,{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}\,[{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta})\}].\end{array}

If we were to calculate
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\bf a})
,
a dense
n\times n
matrix,
then MP would not scale computationally for large
n
.
We can avoid this by combining the update for
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}

and
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\bf a})
.
The resulting algorithm is summarised in Algorithm
6
.

Algorithm 6

MP for probit regression

0:

{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
{\bf X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times p}

1:

Set
{\bf Z}=\mbox{diag}(2{\bf y}-{\bf 1}_{n}){\bf X}
,
{\bf S}=({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D})^{-1}
,
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}={\bf S}
, and initialize
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}
.

2:

repeat

3:

Calculate
{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}
and
\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T})

4:

Approximate
\xi_{1}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}))
and

\xi_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}))
(via DM or QUAD)

5:

Update
q({\bf a})
via

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{a}\leftarrow{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}+\xi_{1}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}))
.

6:

Update
q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
via

\begin{array}[]{rl}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}&\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{a}\\[4.30554pt]
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}&\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bf S}+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\,[\,{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\xi_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}))\}\,]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}\\[4.30554pt]
&\displaystyle\qquad+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\,[\,{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta})\}\,]\,{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T}\,[\,{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta})\}\,]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}\end{array}

7:

until
convergence criteria is met

The run-time complexity of Algorithm
6
(MP) is calculated as follows (assuming that
n>p
and the matrix
{\bf S}^{-1}
is dense and does not have a special structure that we can exploit). The cost of calculating
{\bf S}
is
O(np^{2}+p^{3})
. Line 3 costs
O(np)
for
{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}
and
O(np^{2})
for
\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}{\bf Z}^{T})
.
Line 4 and Line 5 then costs
O(n)
each.
The update for
q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
costs
O(np)
for
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}

(if
{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}
is calculated outside the main loop),
and
O(np^{2}+p^{3})
for
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}
.
Hence, the asymptotic cost per iteration is
O(np^{2}+p^{3})
(which is the same asymptotic time complexity per iteration as Newton-Raphson’s method for GLMs). However, the updates for
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{a}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}
are similar to those of MFVB.
Like MFVB, MP may be subjected to slow convergence
for some datasets (refer to Section
6.2
).

In the next result we show that the MP marginal posteriors for the probit regression parameters exhibit good asymptotic properties.

Result 1

Assume
{\bf x}_{i}
’s,
i=1,\ldots,n
are i.i.d. random
p
-vectors (with
p
fixed,
n
diverging) such that

{\mathbb{E}}({\bf x}_{i})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{x}
and
{\mathbb{V}}({\bf x}_{i})={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{x}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{x}
is full rank. Let
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=\mbox{arg}\max_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\{\,p({\bf y},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\,\}
denote the MAP estimator for
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. Then the converged values of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}

and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}
, denoted
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}^{*}

and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}^{*}
respectively of Algorithm
6
, satisfy

\lVert\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta}^{*}-\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\rVert=O_{p}(n^{-1})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lVert\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}^{*}-{\bf V}\rVert_{F}=O_{p}(n^{-2}),

where
{\bf V}=[{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}\{-\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})\}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}]^{-1}
,
\lVert\cdot\rVert
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{F}
denote the Euclidean norm and Froebenius norm respectively.

Result
1
shows that the MP posterior converges to the same limit as the Laplace approximate posterior under typical assumptions. Hence, the MP posterior is consistent when the Laplace approximate posterior is consistent under the assumptions of Result
1
. We speculate that, more generally that Result
1
holds more generally
for different initialization values. In particular we believe Algorithm
6
converges provided
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}
is not too large.

7
Conclusion

We have developed the the moment propagation method and shown that it can be used to develop algorithms that recover the true posterior distributions for all parameters of linear regression
and multivariate normal models. We have developed a MP based algorithm for probit regression,
shown it gives asymptotically correct posterior mean and covariance estimates, and shown that it
can be effective on real data compared to a variety of other techniques. For linear and multivariate normal models
we have shown that MP can use higher order posterior moments leading to more accurate posterior approximations. Lastly, it is clear that MFVB and MP updates can be interwoven without further difficulty.

Despite these contributions MP still has a number of limitations. We have only presented
work here where there are two sets of components, and have only considered conjugate models. These are severe limitations, but our current work suggests that these are not insurmountable ones.
We have left several theoretical avenues to explore. Convergence issues have been
only dealt with at a superficial level. When does MP converge?, When are the fixed points unique? What
is the rate of convergence MP methods? Can the rate of convergence be accelerated? Using
Gaussian
q
-densities when are estimates asymptotically correct? When does parameterization matter
and choice of approximated posterior moments matter? Can these methods be automated in a similar
way to MFVB
(
Winn and Bishop 2005
, e.g.)
? All such questions remain unanswered and are subject
to future work.

A.1 Moments for the Multivariate Gaussian Distribution

s

{\bf x}^{T}{\bf A}{\bf x}

{\bf A}

{\mathbb{E}}[({\bf x}^{T}{\bf A}{\bf x})^{h}]=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\frac{(h-1)!}{(h-1-i)!i!}\kappa(h-i){\mathbb{E}}[({\bf x}^{T}{\bf A}{\bf x})^{i}]

MFVB:
Letting

and applying

leads to normally distributed
q
-densities

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}
with mean

and covariance

(38)

The consistency conditions require (
38
) to hold for
i\in\{1,2\}

(with “
\leftarrow
” replaced with “
=
”). Solving these consistency equations leads to
leads to a unique solution given by

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}

Remark:

Extrapolating to situations where the true posterior is approximately normal, e.g., where the Bernstein–von Mises theorem holds
(see for example
Vaart 1998
, Section 10.2)
, we see that factorizing
q
-densities will often lead to underestimating posterior variances.
Further, in cases where there is approximately parameter orthogonality the off diagonal block of the posterior variance, analogous to
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{i,-i}
above, will be approximately
{\bf 0}
, leading to better MFVB approximations.

MP:
Suppose we model the marginal posterior distributions as
q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i})=\phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i};\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{i},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{i})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then the MP posterior mean and covariance of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})&\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{q}\left[{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}\,|\,{\mathcal{D}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})\right]={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1},\qquad\mbox{and}\\[8.61108pt]

&\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}.\par\end{array}

Similarly for
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})
and
{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})

q
-density and MP means and variances leads to the updates

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{i}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{i}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{ii}+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{i,-i}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{-i,-i}^{-1}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{-i}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{-i,-i}^{-1}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{-i,-i}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{-i,i}

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{i}

\begin{array}[]{rl}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{1}&\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21},\qquad\mbox{and}\\[4.30554pt]
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{2}&\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}.\par\end{array}

Substituting the second above equation into the first and rearranging leads to

(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11})-({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21})={\bf 0}

[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}]{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}^{-1}[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{12}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{21}]={\bf 0}.

The left-hand side square bracketed term is the Schur’s complement of a positive definite matrix, and hence convertible

(see
Horn and Johnson 2012
, Theorem 7.7.7)
.
It then follows, using basic properties of positive definite matrices that

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{11}

and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{2}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{22}
.
Hence, the MP method leads to the exact marginal posterior distributions.

Remark:

Note that the

does not need to be calculated implicitly or stored unlike FFVB where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}
are modelled jointly.
Note that for this problem MFVB iterations update the means while the variances
remain fixed, where as for the MP approximations, the means remain fixed across iterations
and the covariances are updated.

D.1 Exact posterior distributions for the MVN model

We will next state the full conditional distributions for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. These are useful for both the derivation of the MFVB and MP approximations.
The full conditional distributions for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
are given by

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,|\,{\bf X},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\par\sim N_{p}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}/\lambda_{n}\right)\par\par\par\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\par{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\sim\mbox{IW}_{p}\left({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}),\nu_{n}+1\right)\par\end{array}

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})={\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{0}+{\bf S}+n(\overline{{\bf x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})(\overline{{\bf x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{T}+\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\overline{{\bf x}}={\bf X}^{T}{\bf 1}_{n}/n,\quad\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{0}+n;\quad\nu_{n}=\nu_{0}+n,\\[8.61108pt]

(65)

For this example, we are able to calculate the exact posterior distribution and its corresponding moments. This can be used as a gold standard for comparing the quality of MFVB and MP algorithms.

{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X}\sim\mbox{IW}_{p}\left({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n},\nu_{n}\right)
.

The marginal posterior distribution for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,|\,{\bf X},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\sim N_{p}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}/\lambda_{n}\right)

{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X}\sim\mbox{IW}_{p}\left({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n},\nu_{n}\right)

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,|\,{\bf X}\par&\displaystyle\sim t_{p}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n},\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\lambda_{n}(\nu_{n}-p+1)},\nu_{n}-p+1\right),\end{array}

t
distribution

D.4 Result

Result 5

Based on the prior choice in (
24
), the MFVB approximation leads to the exact posterior expectation for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
,
element-wise underestimation of the posterior variance for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
,
element-wise underestimation of the posterior expectation for
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
, and provided
\nu_{n}>p+1
, the element-wise
posterior variances for
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
are underestimated.

Throughout Algorithm
8
the values

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
and
\widetilde{d}
are fixed. Upon termination of Algorithm
8
the following two equations will hold (with high precision) for the variational parameters:

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}=\frac{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}}{\lambda_{n}\widetilde{d}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}={\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}+\lambda_{n}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}.

These constitute two matrix equations and two matrix unknowns.
Solving for
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}

we get

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\lambda_{n}\nu_{n}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}^{*}=\frac{\nu_{n}+1}{\nu_{n}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}.

Note that the exact posterior variance of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
can be written as

{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\lambda_{n}\nu_{n}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\mathbb{V}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,|\,{\bf X})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\lambda_{n}(\nu_{n}-p-1)}

We can now see that

\lambda_{n}\nu_{n}>\lambda_{n}(\nu_{n}-p-1)
and hence,

\,|\,[{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})]_{ij}\,|\,<\,|\,[{\mathbb{V}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,|\,{\bf X})]_{ij}\,|\,
for all
(i,j)
.

Comparing true and approximate posterior expectations of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
we have

{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\nu_{n}-p-1}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=\frac{\nu_{n}+1}{\nu_{n}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\nu_{n}-p}

The VB estimate is underestimated when

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu_{n}-p-1}>\frac{\nu_{n}+1}{\nu_{n}}\frac{1}{\nu_{n}-p}&\displaystyle\implies\nu_{n}(\nu_{n}-p)>(\nu_{n}+1)(\nu_{n}-p-1)\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\implies\nu_{n}^{2}-p\nu_{n}>\nu_{n}^{2}-p\nu_{n}-\nu_{n}+\nu_{n}-p-1\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\implies 0>-p-1\end{array}

which is always true. Hence,
\,|\,[{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X})]_{ij}\,|\,>\,|\,[{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})]\,|\,
for all
(i,j)
.

Comparing true and approximate posterior element-wise variances of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{W}}{\mathbb{V}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X})&\displaystyle=\frac{(\nu_{n}-p+1)({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}\circ{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n})+(\nu_{n}-p-1)\mbox{dg}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n})\mbox{dg}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n})^{T}}{(\nu_{n}-p)(\nu_{n}-p-1)^{2}(\nu_{n}-p-3)}\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=\frac{(\nu_{n}-p+1)[{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X})\circ{\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X})]}{(\nu_{n}-p)(\nu_{n}-p-3)}\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(\nu_{n}-p-1)\mbox{dg}({\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X}))\mbox{dg}({\mathbb{E}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X}))^{T}}{(\nu_{n}-p)(\nu_{n}-p-3)}\par\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{W}}{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})&\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\nu_{n}+1}{\nu_{n}}\right)^{2}\frac{(\nu_{n}+1-p+1)({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}\circ{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n})}{(\nu_{n}+1-p)(\nu_{n}+1-p-1)^{2}(\nu_{n}+1-p-3)}\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\frac{\nu_{n}+1}{\nu_{n}}\right)^{2}\frac{(\nu_{n}+1-p-1)\mbox{dg}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n})\mbox{dg}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n})^{T}}{(\nu_{n}+1-p)(\nu_{n}+1-p-1)^{2}(\nu_{n}+1-p-3)}\par\par\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=\par\frac{(\nu_{n}-p+2)({\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\circ{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))+(\nu_{n}-p)\mbox{dg}({\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))\mbox{dg}({\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))^{T}}{(\nu_{n}+1-p)(\nu_{n}-p-2)}\\[8.61108pt]
\end{array}

Hence,
\,|\,[{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{W}}{\mathbb{V}}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,|\,{\bf X})]_{ij}\,|\,>\,|\,[{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{W}}{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})]_{ij}\,|\,
when

\frac{(\nu_{n}-p+1)}{(\nu_{n}-p)(\nu_{n}-p-3)}>\frac{(\nu_{n}-p+2)}{(\nu_{n}-p+1)(\nu_{n}-p-2)}

and

\frac{(\nu_{n}-p-1)}{(\nu_{n}-p)(\nu_{n}-p-3)}>\frac{(\nu_{n}-p)}{(\nu_{n}-p+1)(\nu_{n}-p-2)}

The first inequality implies

\begin{array}[]{rl}(x+1)^{2}(x-2)>x(x+2)(x-3)&\displaystyle\implies x^{3}-3x-2>x^{3}-x^{2}-6x\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\implies x^{2}+3x-2>0\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\implies x^{2}+3x-2>0\par\end{array}

where
x=\nu_{n}-p
(assuming

x>1
, i.e,
\nu_{n}>p+1)
.

The second inequality implies

\begin{array}[]{rl}(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)>x^{2}(x-3)&\displaystyle\implies x^{3}-2x^{2}-x+2>x^{3}-3x^{2}\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\implies x^{2}-x+2>0\par\end{array}

which is true provided
x>1
. Hence,
\nu_{n}>p+1
is sufficient for posterior variances to be element-wise greater than those for MFVB in absolute magnitude.

Result 6

)-(

) for the MP method for
the MVN model described by Algorithm

.

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}

•

The second solution corresponds to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n}
,
\widetilde{d}=p+3

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}=\frac{2{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{\nu_{n}-p-1},\quad\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}}{2\lambda_{n}(\nu_{n}-p-1)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widetilde{\nu}=4.

leading to an inexact estimation of the posterior distribution.

Consider the set of consistency conditions as the set of equations (

Matching
{\mathbb{V}}^{MP}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})

{\mathbb{V}}_{q}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}

we obtain

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}=\frac{\widetilde{d}-p-1}{\nu_{n}-p-1}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}.

Hence, we can write the expressions for
{\bf A}
and
\mbox{dg}({\bf B})
as

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\bf A}={\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{n}+\frac{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}}{(\widetilde{d}-p-1)}=\frac{\nu_{n}-p}{\widetilde{d}-p-1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{dg}({\bf B})&\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}\widetilde{\nu}^{2}(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}{(\widetilde{\nu}-2)^{2}(\widetilde{\nu}-4)}2\mbox{dg}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}})^{2}\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\widetilde{d}-p-1)^{2}}\frac{(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}{(\widetilde{\nu}-4)}2\mbox{dg}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}})^{2}\par\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=\frac{(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}{(\nu_{n}-p)^{2}(\widetilde{\nu}-4)}2\mbox{dg}({\bf A})^{2}.\par\end{array}

Hence, the moment matching equation for
\widetilde{d}
can be written as

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\widetilde{d}&\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle 2{\bf 1}^{T}[\mbox{dg}({\mathbb{E}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))^{2}]}{\mbox{tr}[{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbb{W}}{\mathbb{V}}_{q}^{MP}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})]}+p+3\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{2}{(\nu_{n}-p)^{2}}{\bf 1}^{T}[\mbox{dg}({\bf A})^{2}]}{\displaystyle\frac{2{\bf 1}^{T}[\mbox{dg}({\bf A})^{2}]+(\nu_{n}-p)\mbox{tr}({\bf B})}{(\nu_{n}-p)^{2}(\nu_{n}-p-2)}}+p+3\par\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=(\nu_{n}-p-2)\frac{\displaystyle 2{\bf 1}^{T}[\mbox{dg}({\bf A})^{2}]}{\displaystyle 2{\bf 1}^{T}[\mbox{dg}({\bf A})^{2}]+(\nu_{n}-p)\mbox{tr}({\bf B})}+p+3\end{array}

Substituting the above expressions for
{\bf A}
in terms of
\nu_{n}
,
\widetilde{d}
, p and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\widetilde{d}&\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle(\nu_{n}-p-2)}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}{(\nu_{n}-p)(\widetilde{\nu}-4)}\par}+p+3\par\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=(\nu_{n}-p-2)\frac{\displaystyle(\nu_{n}-p)(\widetilde{\nu}-4)}{\displaystyle(\nu_{n}-p)(\widetilde{\nu}-4)+(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}+p+3\par\par\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle=(\nu_{n}-p-2)\left[1-\frac{\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}{\displaystyle(\nu_{n}-p)(\widetilde{\nu}-4)+(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}\right]+p+3\par\par\\[17.22217pt]
&\displaystyle=\nu_{n}+1-\frac{\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nu}-1)(\nu_{n}-p-2)}{\displaystyle(\nu_{n}-p)(\widetilde{\nu}-4)+(\widetilde{\nu}-1)}\par\par\\[8.61108pt]

where the last line follows from using

\widetilde{\nu}=\widetilde{d}-p+1
. Letting

\begin{array}[]{rl}x&\displaystyle=y+1-\frac{\displaystyle x(y-2)}{y(x-3)+x}\\

Multiplying by

The solutions are:

If

and so

\begin{array}[]{lcl}q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\displaystyle=N(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta})&\quad\mbox{and}&q(a_{i})\displaystyle=\mbox{TN}_{+}(\widetilde{\mu}_{a,i},1),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,\end{array}

and

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta}\leftarrow{\bf S}

E.2 Linear Response Variational Bayes

At this point we could apply the LRVB approach of
Giordano et al. 2015
to correct the variances of MFVB.
This approach can be viewed as a post-hoc correction to MFVB.
Direct application of LRVB is not possible since
p(a_{i})
is not differentiable. Instead we absorb the MFVB update for
q({\bf a})

into
q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. This is equivalent for using the SEM method
of
Meng and Rubin 1991
. Indeed
Giordano et al. 2015
state
that for two parameter sets LRVB and SEM are equivalent.

We now argue that, for this model, LRVB leads to an approximation equivalent to the Laplace approximation. To see this first note that Algorithm
9
is identical to the Bayesian Expectation Maximization approach applied to the probit model. Applying the SEM method to
the probit regression model we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}M({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})&={\bf S}{\bf Z}[{\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta}+\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})],\qquad\mbox{and}\\[8.61108pt]
DM({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})&=\displaystyle\frac{\partial M({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})}{d{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta}}={\bf S}{\bf Z}[{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})\}]{\bf Z}.\end{array}

Then

{\mathbb{V}}^{SEM}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})={\bf S}[{\bf I}-DM({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})]^{-1}=[{\bf Z}\mbox{diag}\{-\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta})\}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}]^{-1}

which is the posterior covariance estimate corresponding to the Laplace approximation
when
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\beta}
is equal to the posterior mode.

E.3 Calculating
\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})

Fast, stable and accurate estimation of
\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is pivotal for the MP-QUAD method, as well as direct optimization approaches of the ELBO for and. We only focus on the case when
d\in\{0,1,2\}
since
these are the only cases needed for MP-QUAD and GVB.
We employ two different strategies for evaluating
\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
:
a Taylor series approximation, and composite trapezoidal integration.

A Taylor series expansion of the integrand around

x=\mu
in (
31
) leads to

\displaystyle\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta_{d+2k}(\mu)\,\sigma^{2k}}{2^{k}k!}

(72)

where
\xi_{d}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is the Gaussian smoothed version of
\zeta_{d}(x)
. The derivatives of

\zeta_{1}(t)
and its derivative are given by

\zeta_{1}(t)=\phi(t)/\Phi(t)
,
\zeta_{2}(t)=-t\,\zeta_{1}(t)-\zeta_{1}(t)^{2}
and
for
k=3,4,\ldots
the function

\zeta_{k}(t)
can be calculated recursively via
the formula

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(t)=-\left[\,t\,\zeta_{k-1}(t)+(k-2)\zeta_{k-2}(t)\,\right]-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2}{j}\,\zeta_{1+j}(t)\,\zeta_{k-1-j}(t).

Empirically we have found that the series (
72
) does not converges for all
\mu
,
\sigma^{2}
and
d
in particular when
\sigma^{2}>1
.
Note that for the probit regression model high leverage points lead to large
\sigma^{2}
.
Nevertheless,
the terms in the series
(
72
) converge very rapidly to 0, especially
when
\sigma^{2}<\tau
for some chosen
\tau<1
. Later, in Section
E.4 Proof of Result
, we will argue under mild
regularity conditions that
\sigma^{2}
values will be
O_{p}(n^{-1})

so that each additional term on the right hand side of
(
72
) gives an order of magnitude more
accuracy. Truncating (
72
) at just 5 terms is much faster than numerical quadrature
with accuracies up to 6-8 significant figures of accuracy. Adaptive quadrature methods
use in general many more function evaluations.

For
\sigma^{2}>\tau
we use composite trapezoidal quadrature. This involves A. finding good starting points, B. finding the mode of the integrand, C. finding the effective domain of the integrand, and D. applying composite trapezoidal quadrature. Note that because the effective domains will be the same for
d\in\{0,1,2\}

we focus on the case when
d=1
since this is the easiest to deal with.

Finding good starting points:

We work on the log-scale to avoid underflow issues.
Let
f(x)
be the log of the integrand for
\xi_{1}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, i.e.,
up to additive constants

f(x)=-\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\log\Phi(x)-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}

with
f^{\prime}(x)=-x-\zeta_{1}(x)-(x-\mu)/\sigma^{2}
and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=-1-\zeta_{2}(x)=1/\sigma^{2}
.

We wish to find the maximizer of
f(x)
with respect to
x
, corresponding to the mode of the integrand of
\xi_{1}
. We consider three different starting points

1.

For positive large
x
we have
\log\Phi(x)\approx 0

leading to the approximate mode
x_{1}=\mu/(1+\sigma^{2})
.

2.

For
x
close to zero we perform a Talyor series for
\log\Phi(x)
around
x=0
leading to
\log\Phi(x)\approx-\log(2)-\sqrt{2/\pi}x-x^{2}/\pi

leading to the approximate mode
x_{2}=(\mu-\sigma^{2}\sqrt{2/\pi})/(\sigma^{2}(1-\pi/2)+1)
.

3.

For negative large
x
we have
\log\Phi(x)\sim\log\phi(x)-\log(-x)
leading to the approximate mode
x_{3}=-\sqrt{\mu+\sigma^{2}}

(provided
\mu+\sigma^{2}>0
).

We evaluate
f(x)
at
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
(providing it exists), and choose the point with the largest value as the starting point,
x^{(0)}
.

Finding the mode of the integrand:

We then find the mode using Newton’s method
x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-f^{\prime}(x^{(t)})/f^{\prime\prime}(x^{(t)})
. When
|f^{\prime}(x^{(t)})/f^{\prime\prime}(x^{(t)})|<\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize mode}}
we stop. We have used
\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize mode}}=10^{-3}
in our implementation.
Let
x^{*}
denote the mode.

Finding the effective domain:

Let
s=-1/\sqrt{f^{\prime\prime}(x^{*})}
. To find the effective domain we take steps to the left and to the right of the mode
x^{*}
until
f(x^{*}-sL)
and
f(x^{*}+sR)
are both less than
\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize ED}}\times f(x^{*})
for some integers
L
and
R
.
We have used
\tau_{\mbox{\scriptsize ED}}=10^{-3}
in our implementation.

Composite trapezoid quadrature:
We then perform composite trapezoidal quadrature between
a=x^{*}-sL
and
b=x^{*}+sR

with
N+1
equally spaced quadrature points
{\displaystyle a=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N-1}<x_{N}=b}
and approximate the integral by

\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,dx\approx\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{F(x_{k-1})+F(x_{k})}{2}\Delta x_{k}\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad F(x)=\zeta_{d}(x)\phi(x;\mu;\sigma^{2})

and
\Delta x_{k}=x_{k}-x_{k-1}
. Note that this approach is extremely effective, particularly for integrals when all derivatives of
F
are close to
0
on the boundary of the
integral. In such cases the trapezoid rule can have exponential rates of convergence

(see Section 4.5.1 of
Press et al. 2007
, for details)
. We have found
N=50
to be sufficiently accurate and have used this value in our implementation.

\quad\displaystyle\qquad+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[\,{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\left\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)})\right\}\right]\,{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[\,{\bf I}_{n}+\mbox{diag}\left\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)})\right\}\right]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}\par\\[8.61108pt]
\quad\displaystyle={\bf S}+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[\,{\bf I}_{n}+{\bf W}^{(t)}\right]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}\\[8.61108pt]
\quad\displaystyle\qquad+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[\,{\bf I}_{n}+{\bf W}^{(t)}\right]\,{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[\,{\bf I}_{n}+{\bf W}^{(t)}\right]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}+O_{p}^{m}(n^{-2})\par\\[8.61108pt]
\quad\displaystyle=O_{p}^{m}(n^{-1})\par\end{array}

where
{\bf W}^{(t)}=\mbox{diag}\{\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,VB}^{(t)})\}
.
Hence, if

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)}-\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,VB}^{(t)}=O_{p}^{v}(n^{-1})

and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)}=O_{p}^{m}(n^{-1})
then

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t+1)}-\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,VB}^{(t+1)}=O_{p}^{v}(n^{-1})

and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t+1)}=O_{p}^{m}(n^{-1})
.

Now since
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,VB}^{(t)}\to\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
as
t\to\infty
upon
convergence we have

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{\beta,MP}=\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}+O_{p}^{v}(n^{-1})

Upon convergence of the algorithm we have
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t)}=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{(t+1)}\equiv\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}
and

\begin{array}[]{rl}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}\par&\displaystyle={\bf S}+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[\,{\bf I}_{n}+{\bf W}^{*}\right]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}\\[8.61108pt]
&\displaystyle\qquad+{\bf S}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[{\bf I}_{n}+{\bf W}^{*}\right]\,{\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}{\bf Z}^{T}\left[{\bf I}_{n}+{\bf W}^{*}\right]\,{\bf Z}{\bf S}+O_{p}^{m}(n^{-2})\par\par\end{array}

where
{\bf W}^{*}=\mbox{diag}[\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})]
. Multiplying both sides by
{\bf S}^{-1}
and rearranging we have

\begin{array}[]{l}({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D})\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D})-({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z})\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z})\\[8.61108pt]
\qquad\displaystyle={\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z}+O_{p}^{m}(n^{-2})\par\end{array}

which we can write as

\begin{array}[]{l}({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z})\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}(-{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z}+{\bf D})\\[8.61108pt]
\qquad\displaystyle={\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z}+O_{p}^{m}(n^{-2})\par\end{array}

left multiplying by
({\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf Z}+{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z})^{-1}

and right multiplying by
(-{\bf Z}^{T}{\bf W}^{*}{\bf Z}+{\bf D})^{-1}

we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\beta,MP}^{*}=\left[{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}\{-\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})\}{\bf Z}+{\bf D}\right]^{-1}+O_{p}^{m}(n^{-2})\par\end{array}

and the result is proved.

E.3 Methods compared

In what is to follow let

f({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\log p({\bf y},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})={\bf 1}_{n}^{T}\log\Phi({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\tfrac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}{\bf D}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\mbox{constants}

so that the gradient vector and Hessian matrix are given by

{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})={\bf Z}^{T}\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-{\bf D}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})={\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})){\bf Z}-{\bf D}

respectively.

Laplace’s method:
Here we maximize the log-likelihood via Newton-Raphson iterations, i.e., starting at
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
(
t=0
),

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}={\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-[{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})]^{-1}{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})

Upon convergence
q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\phi({\boldsymbol{\beta}};\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}})
where
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}={\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}

and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}=[-{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})]^{-1}

and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
is the value of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}
at convergence.

Gaussian Variational Bayes (GVB):
Let
q({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\phi({\boldsymbol{\beta}};\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}})
. Then, using (
1
) and similar ideas to the derivation of (
31
), the corresponding
ELBO is given by

\mbox{ELBO}={\bf 1}_{n}^{T}\xi_{0}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\bf Z}^{T}))-\tfrac{1}{2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}{\bf D}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mbox{tr}({\bf D}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}})+\tfrac{1}{2}\log|\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}|+\mbox{constants}.

Then using results from
Opper and Archambeau 2009
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla_{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}\mbox{ELBO}&={\mathbb{E}}_{q}[{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})]&={\bf Z}^{T}\xi_{1}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\bf Z}^{T}))-{\bf D}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\\[8.61108pt]
\nabla_{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}^{2}\mbox{ELBO}&={\mathbb{E}}_{q}[{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})]&={\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(\xi_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\bf Z}^{T}))){\bf Z}-{\bf D},\par\end{array}

and

\frac{\partial\mbox{ELBO}}{\partial\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij}}=\frac{1}{2}\mbox{tr}\left[\left\{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{-1}+{\mathbb{E}}_{q}({\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}))\right\}\frac{\partial\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}}{\partial\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij}}\right].

(74)

We compared two optimization approaches:

•

Direct Optimization:
Maximization the
ELBO
with respect to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
, and the parameters of a (dense, full rank, positive definite)
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
requires optimization over
O(d^{2})
parameters. We used the BFGS algorithm in the function
optim()

in
R

a first order
method that uses gradient information to construct an approximation of the inverse Hessian.
However, this approach uses
O(d^{4})
memory, and so we recommend a
limited memory Quasi-Newton method, see for example
Byrd et al. 1995
, when
d
is large. The objective function is given by the
ELBO
,
the gradient is given by the expression for
{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})]
above, and the gradient with respect to the parameterization of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
we give now. We use a Cholesky factorization parameterization of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
of the form

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}={\bf C}^{T}{\bf C}
where
{\bf C}
is an upper triangular matrix of the form

{\bf C}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}e^{c_{11}}&c_{12}&c_{13}&\ldots&c_{1d}\\
0&e^{c_{22}}&c_{23}&\ldots&c_{2d}\\
0&0&e^{c_{33}}&\ldots&c_{3d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&e^{c_{dd}}\\
\end{array}\right]

This parameterization been used in many places.
Exponentiation of the diagonal to ensure positiveness of
{\bf C}
. Note that
Tan and Nott 2018
use a similar
parameterization of the inverse of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
which can be useful when
{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is either sparse or has some other structure that can be exploited. The gradient vector can then be calculated using

\frac{\partial\mbox{ELBO}}{\partial c_{ij}}=\left[{\bf C}\left\{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{-1}+{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(\xi_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{\bf Z}^{T}))){\bf Z}-{\bf D}\right\}\right]_{ij}\exp[c_{ij}I(i=j)]

for the diagonal and upper diagonal elements of
{\bf C}
.

•

Double Stochastic Variational Bayes (DSVB):
As stated in the main paper, a stochastic gradient descent approach (based on code from
Tran et al. 2020
(is used with the default settings stated there) modified to use the reparameterization trick of
Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla 2014
for increased numeric stability. This method only requires
f({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and
{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and not their expectations with respect to
q
, which can sometimes be difficult to evaluate quickly, stably, and accurately.

The main difficulty with direct optimization or DSVB is the maximizing the lower bound
with respect to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
since for a dense, positive definite covariance matrix
(with no additional structure), the number of parameters required is
O(p^{2})
which can slow
down computations for even moderately high dimensions. The next method we
consider partially avoids this problem.

Delta Method Variational Bayes (DMVB):

Following
Wang and Blei 2013
,
a first order delta method approximation of the ELBO leads to (ignoring additive constants)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{AELBO}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}})&\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{n}^{T}\zeta_{0}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})-\tfrac{1}{2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}{\bf D}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mbox{tr}\left[\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}\left\{{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})){\bf Z}-{\bf D}\right\}\right]+\tfrac{1}{2}\log|\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}|.\end{array}

Fixing
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
, the derivatives of the approximate ELBO (AELBO) with respect to the parameters of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
are

\frac{\partial\mbox{AELBO}}{\partial\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij}}=\frac{1}{2}\mbox{tr}\left[\left\{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{-1}+{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})){\bf Z}-{\bf D}\right\}\frac{\partial\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}}{\partial\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij}}\right]

Setting the right hand side to zero for all
(i,j)
and solving for
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
leads to the unique solution (for all
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})=\left[{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(-\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})){\bf Z}+{\bf D}\right]^{-1}.

Substituting
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})
into the AELBO leads to (ignoring additive constants)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{AELBO}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}))&\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{n}^{T}\zeta_{0}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})-\tfrac{1}{2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}{\bf D}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log|{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(-\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})){\bf Z}+{\bf D}|,\end{array}

which is a function of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
only. We can then maximize

\mbox{AELBO}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*})
directly rather with respect to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}

which only involves
O(p)
parameters, rather than
O(p^{2})
parameters. The gradient vector with respect
to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
is then

\frac{\mbox{AELBO}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}))}{\partial\mu_{j}}=[{\bf Z}^{T}\zeta_{1}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})-{\bf D}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}]_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mbox{dg}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}){\bf Z}^{T})^{T}[{\bf Z}_{j}\circ\zeta_{3}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}})],

where
{\bf Z}_{j}
is the
j
th column of
{\bf Z}
.
We can then use any first order optimization method, e.g., BFGS. to maximize

\mbox{AELBO}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}))
with respect to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}
.
Let
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{*}
be this maximizer, then

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{*}=\left[{\bf Z}^{T}\mbox{diag}(-\zeta_{2}({\bf Z}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{*})){\bf Z}+{\bf D}\right]^{-1}
. This approach has the advantages that
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
does not need to be optimized over (and so scales well with
p
), and does not require expectations with respect to
q
of
f({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
,
{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})

or
{\bf H}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
