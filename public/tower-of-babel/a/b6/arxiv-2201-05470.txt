Vector calculus in two-dimensional space
Marián Fecko

Abstract




Vector calculus in three-dimensional space is ubiquitous in
applications of mathematics in physics and engineering.
Its two-dimensional version is, however, quite rare.
Here we try to provide a pedagogical account of the subject.
It is based on the logic of theory of differential forms.
For readers not familiar with the latter, the results are presented in detail
in the standard language and notations.

In vector calculus one often hears phrases like “integrate vector field over a surface”

So if something is integrated

- over a volume, it is necessarily a 3-form, respectively.

How should one understand the fact, then, that under surface integrals, where 2-form is to be standing,

It is, however,

In total, parametrizations of all types of forms in

(see e.g. section 8.5 in

There are 0-forms, 1-forms, 2-forms and 3-forms (this is just because of three dimensions of the space)
and we have, at each of these degrees, operation of

raising the degree in one unit and such that its square vanishes (this is so in any dimension).
One can display the situation in terms of the diagram

\Omega^{0}\overset{d}{\to}\Omega^{1}\overset{d}{\to}\Omega^{2}\overset{d}{\to}\Omega^{3}\hskip 56.9055ptdd=0

(1)

(it is known as the

Since there is also (usual Euclidean)

(denoted by star) of the linear space of

(It is just plus on each degree on

So in

\Omega^{0}\overset{*}{\leftrightarrow}\Omega^{3}\hskip 42.67912pt\Omega^{1}\overset{*}{\leftrightarrow}\Omega^{2}\hskip 42.67912pt*^{-1}=*

\alpha=*\beta

So “actually” we need just 0-forms (i.e.

and 1-forms (the rest may be parametrized in terms of the two).

The metric tensor provides another two important canonical isomorphisms, namely the (mutually inverse)

3
How it works in two dimensions

In two-dimensional case, the de Rham complex, i.e. the analogue of (
1
), just simplifies to

\Omega^{0}\overset{d}{\to}\Omega^{1}\overset{d}{\to}\Omega^{2}\hskip 56.9055ptdd=0

(15)

Key difference is in action of the Hodge star:

\Omega^{0}\overset{*}{\leftrightarrow}\Omega^{2}\hskip 42.67912pt\Omega^{1}\overset{*}{\leftrightarrow}\Omega^{1}

(16)

The left expression is a natural analogue of the left expression in (
2
),
it identifies the edge degrees of forms; explicitly

*f=fdS\hskip 28.45274pt*(fdS)=f\hskip 42.67912ptdS\equiv dx\wedge dy=\ \text{area 2-form}

(17)

This enables us to forget about 2-forms and express them in terms of 0-forms,
i.e. in terms of functions (scalar fields).

The right expression is, however, completely different:
While in (
2
) it is an isomorphism of
two different
spaces,
in (
16
) it is an isomorphism of a
single
space (the space of 1-forms)
on itself

(differing from the identity).
Explicitly, in usual Cartesian coordinates
(x,y)
in the plane,

*dx=dy\hskip 42.67912pt*dy=-dx

(18)

This does not mean that we cannot forget about 1-forms. Rather, it means that in 2D
there are as many as
two canonical
ways how 1-forms may be replaced by vector fields!
One of them is the same like it was in 3D, i.e. via the operation
\sharp

(raising of index). It reads

\begin{CD}\Omega^{0}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{1}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{2}\\
@V{\text{i}d}V{}V@V{}V{\sharp}V@V{}V{*}V\\
\mathcal{F}@>{}>{a_{0}}>\mathfrak{X}@>{}>{a_{1}}>\mathcal{F}\\
\end{CD}

(19)

When the arrows (directed upwards) are applied on scalar and vector fields (on
f
and
\mathbf{A}
)
we again get standard expressions we used to see under integral sign:

f\in\Omega^{0}\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\equiv A_{x}dx+A_{y}dy\in\Omega^{1}\hskip 28.45274ptfdS\in\Omega^{2}

(20)

Another one is, however, available, now - we first apply the star operator
(after which we still remain within 1-forms)
and
only then
we raise the index.
So we have
two different
analogues of the diagram (
4
):

\begin{CD}\Omega^{0}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{1}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{2}\\
@V{\text{i}d}V{}V@V{}V{\sharp}V@V{}V{*}V\\
\mathcal{F}@>{}>{a_{0}}>\mathfrak{X}@>{}>{a_{1}}>\mathcal{F}\\
\end{CD}\hskip 42.67912pt\begin{CD}\Omega^{0}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{1}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{2}\\
@V{\text{i}d}V{}V@V{}V{\sharp*}V@V{}V{*}V\\
\mathcal{F}@>{}>{b_{0}}>\mathfrak{X}@>{}>{b_{1}}>\mathcal{F}\\
\end{CD}

(21)

We see that also in 2D-vector calculus we make do with
vector and scalar
fields (bottom lines).
From the diagrams one can express explicitly the bottom (effective) arrows and get

a_{0}=\sharp d\hskip 28.45274pta_{1}=*d\flat\hskip 28.45274ptb_{0}=\sharp*d\hskip 28.45274ptb_{1}=*d*^{-1}\flat

(22)

When the (differential) operators are computed in (Cartesian) coordinates
(x,y)
,
we learn that two of them are natural analogues of the situation in 3D (gradient and divergence),
the remaining two are “new”, specific for 2D:

\begin{CD}\Omega^{0}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{1}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{2}\\
@V{\text{i}d}V{}V@V{}V{\sharp}V@V{}V{*}V\\
\mathcal{F}@>{}>{\text{\rm grad\,}}>\mathfrak{X}@>{}>{\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}}>\mathcal{F}\\
\end{CD}\hskip 42.67912pt\begin{CD}\Omega^{0}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{1}@>{d}>{}>\Omega^{2}\\
@V{\text{i}d}V{}V@V{}V{\sharp*}V@V{}V{*}V\\
\mathcal{F}@>{}>{\text{\rm Ham\,}}>\mathfrak{X}@>{}>{-{\text{\rm div\,}}}>\mathcal{F}\\
\end{CD}

(23)

(Notation
b_{0}=\text{\rm Ham\,}
is justified in section
3.1
and for
a_{1}=\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}
in section
3.2
.)
So, abstractly we have

\text{\rm grad\,}=\sharp d\hskip 28.45274pt\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}=*d\flat\hskip 28.45274pt\text{\rm Ham\,}=\sharp*d\hskip 28.45274pt{\text{\rm div\,}}=-*d*^{-1}\flat

(24)

and their actions, in Cartesian coordinates
(x,y)
, read:

\displaystyle\text{\rm grad\,}:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)

(25)

\displaystyle\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}:

\displaystyle(A_{x},A_{y})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial_{x}A_{y}-\partial_{y}A_{x})

(26)

\displaystyle\text{\rm Ham\,}:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(-\partial_{y}f,\partial_{x}f)

(27)

\displaystyle{\text{\rm div\,}}:

\displaystyle(A_{x},A_{y})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial_{x}A_{x}+\partial_{y}A_{y})

(28)

One can easily check vanishing of composition of the two arrows in bottom lines (in both diagrams):

\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\circ\text{\rm grad\,}=0\hskip 28.45274pt{\text{\rm div\,}}\circ\text{\rm Ham\,}=0

(29)

Indeed,

\displaystyle\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\circ\text{\rm grad\,}:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial_{y}\partial_{x}f-\partial_{y}\partial_{x}f)

\displaystyle=0

(30)

\displaystyle{\text{\rm div\,}}\circ\text{\rm Ham\,}:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(-\partial_{y}f,\partial_{x}f)

\displaystyle\mapsto(-\partial_{x}\partial_{y}f,\partial_{y}\partial_{x}f)

\displaystyle=0

(31)

This is the counterpart of (
9

However, not because we put to use

but rather on a single degree in two different diagrams.

It is also worth noticing that when two arrows from different diagrams are composed,
we get (for both possible cases) another well-known operator, the

{\text{\rm div\,}}\circ\text{\rm grad\,}=\triangle\hskip 28.45274pt\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\circ\text{\rm Ham\,}=\triangle

(32)

Indeed,

\displaystyle{\text{\rm div\,}}\circ\text{\rm grad\,}:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f)

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial^{2}_{x}+\partial^{2}_{y})f

\displaystyle\equiv\triangle f

(33)

\displaystyle\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\circ\text{\rm Ham\,}:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(-\partial_{y}f,\partial_{x}f)

\displaystyle\mapsto(\partial^{2}_{x}+\partial^{2}_{y})f

\displaystyle\equiv\triangle f

3.1
Hamiltonian fields

Notation
\text{\rm Ham\,}f
in (
23
) and (
27
) is abbreviation for
Hamiltonian field
generated by function
f
.
It is key concept in theory of Hamiltonian systems (for more details see, e.g.,

[
2
]
,

[
11
]
or Chapter 14 in
[
3
]
).
How could it occur here?

Well, it turns out that our
surface
form
\omega\equiv dS
mentioned in (
17
)
happens to be, at the same time,
symplectic
form
(it satisfies corresponding definition; in general it is to be “closed and non-degenerate 2-form”).
And whenever symplectic form is available,
a rule for construction of Hamiltonian (vector) fields is available as well:

f\mapsto\text{\rm Ham\,}f\hskip 28.45274pti_{\text{\rm Ham\,}f}\omega:=-df

(35)

Now, when we compute how (
35
) works here, what we get is exactly (
27
).

This also sheds a new light onto the second result in (
29
).
The fact, that divergence of Hamiltonian field (generated by any function) vanishes
is equivalent to well-known
Liouville theorem
from classical mechanics (see 14.3.7 in
[
3
]
).
It says that, for Hamiltonian systems,
volume
(here area)
in
phase
space (here our 2D space) is conserved w.r.t. time evolution.

3.2
Auxiliary third dimension

One can easily check that:

1. If, from a function
f(x,y)
in 2D plane, one constructs the following auxiliary vector field in 3D space

\mathbf{u}=(0,0,-f(x,y))

(36)

(try to think how it looks like), then its curl reads

\text{\rm curl\,}\mathbf{u}=(-\partial_{y}f,\partial_{x}f,0)\equiv(\text{\rm Ham\,}f,0)

(37)

2. If, from a vector field
\mathbf{A}
in 2D plane, one constructs the following auxiliary vector field in 3D space

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=(\mathbf{A},0)\equiv(A_{x}(x,y),A_{y}(x,y),0)

(38)

(try to think how it looks like), then its curl reads

\text{\rm curl\,}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=(0,0,\partial_{x}A_{y}-\partial_{y}A_{x})\equiv(0,0,\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{A})

(39)

What these two simple computations do reveal?

First, that our two “new” operations in 2D vector calculus,

a_{1}\equiv\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}
and
b_{0}\equiv\text{\rm Ham\,}
,
may also be discovered as hidden at special places of outputs of specially chosen inputs
of standard 3D vector calculus.
The approach treated in this paper finds their form and properties in “intrinsic” way,
with no reference to auxiliary additional dimensions.

And, second, we see motivation for notation of the operation
\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}
.
Well, mainly for the index 3
(in particular, that
\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{A}=(\text{\rm curl\,}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})_{3}
).
Motivation for the notation
curl
itself (which should be related to kind of
rotation
of something)
is discussed already in standard 3D vector calculus
(it stems for example from
vortex
-like motion of fluid flow;
see a few more words on this in Section
4.2
).

3.3
Integral identities and Green’s theorem

Integral theorems for 2D-vector calculus may be again derived from universal Stokes theorem
(
11
) for forms. Corresponding technical procedure just mimics the one used
in the 3D case at the end of Section
2
.

Since there are altogether as many as
four
basic squares (with upper arrow
d
) in two diagrams
in (
23
), four integral theorems are expected.

First, we assemble the corresponding four differential identities
(analogues of expressions (
10
)).
From the individual squares we get

\displaystyle df

\displaystyle=\text{\rm grad\,}f\cdot d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle df

\displaystyle=-*(\text{\rm Ham\,}f\cdot d\mathbf{r})

(40)

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=(\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{A})dS

\displaystyle d*(\mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=({\text{\rm div\,}}\mathbf{A})dS

(41)

Their integration and application of Stokes theorem (
11
) leads to

\displaystyle\int_{C}\text{\rm grad\,}f\cdot d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=f(B)-f(A)

\displaystyle\int_{C}*(\text{\rm Ham\,}f\cdot d\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=f(A)-f(B)

(42)

\displaystyle\int_{S}(\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{A})dS

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle\int_{S}({\text{\rm div\,}}\mathbf{A})dS

\displaystyle=\oint*(\mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{r})

(43)

Now, when we explicitly write down expressions under integral signs (with the help of (
18
) and (
25
) - (
28
)),
we discover that both identities in (
42
) say the same thing
and, similarly, the same is true for both identities in (
43
).
So we get
just two
mutually different integral identities.
When appropriate notations are used, they read as follows:

gradient theorem

\displaystyle\int_{c}(\partial_{x}f)dx+(\partial_{y}f)dy

\displaystyle:=f(B)-f(A)

(44)

Green’s theorem

\displaystyle\int_{S}(\partial_{x}g-\partial_{y}f)dxdy

\displaystyle:=\oint_{\partial S}fdx+gdy

(45)

The first one is a 2D version of the gradient theorem. This is well known and holds in any dimension.
It relates an integral along a curve
c
to an “integral” over its boundary
\partial c

(which reduces, here, to just two points,
A
, its beginning and
B
, its end).

The second one is Green’s theorem.
This is well-known, again, and is specific for two dimensional space.
It relates an integral over a 2D-domain
S
to an integral over its boundary
(being a closed circuit
\partial S
).

3.4
Perpendicular vector field

As it was already mentioned in Section
3
,
in two dimensions we encounter a specific situation that the Hodge star maps 1-forms
on 1-forms again (see (
16
)).
The latter are, however, in bijection with vector fields (via
\sharp
and
\flat
).
So, effectively, the Hodge star induces a canonical isomorphism of
vector fields

on themselves

\sharp*\flat:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{X}

(46)

A computation gives

\sharp*\flat:(A_{x},A_{y})\mapsto(-A_{y},A_{x})\hskip 28.45274pt\text{i.e.}\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbf{A}_{\pi/2}

(47)

We denote the result as
\mathbf{A}_{\pi/2}
, since it is evident from components
that the new vector is given, at each point, by
rotation
of the vector of the original vector field
by
\pi/2
in positive sense, i.e. in counter-clock-wise direction.
(This is compatible with the fact that, on 1-forms, the
square
of the star is the
minus
identity,
see (
18
)).

The mapping (
47
) is equivalent to a handy formula

*(\mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{r})=:\mathbf{A}_{\pi/2}\cdot d\mathbf{r}

(48)

Then, when combined with (
40
), we get the following useful relation
of the gradient field and the Hamiltonian one:

\text{\rm Ham\,}f=(\text{\rm grad\,}f)_{\pi/2}

(49)

This is also evident from their expressions in (
25

If

3.5
Poincaré lemma

Recall, first, what this useful lemma states.

The fact
dd=0
says that if a form
\alpha

\alpha=d\beta

then it is necessarily closed (

It turns out that the opposite implication does not, in general, hold.
Poincaré lemma, however, guarantees that on a domain which is
contractible to a point

(for example on a coordinate patch) the opposite implication still does hold.

In 3D vector calculus this leads frequently used and useful statements

\text{\rm curl\,}\mathbf{a}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{a}=\text{\rm grad\,}f\hskip 28.45274pt{\text{\rm div\,}}\mathbf{a}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{a}=\text{\rm curl\,}\mathbf{b}

When expressed in terms of the diagram (

And what exactly the deduction gives in 2D vector calculus, i.e. for diagrams (

This:

\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{a}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{a}=\text{\rm grad\,}f\hskip 28.45274pt{\text{\rm div\,}}\mathbf{a}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{a}=\text{\rm Ham\,}f

Such flows are described in terms of

that if the mass density

{\text{\rm div\,}}\mathbf{v}=0\hskip 56.9055pt\text{\emph{incompressible} fluid}

4.1
Incompressible fluid and stream function

Compare the second implications in (

{\text{\rm div\,}}\mathbf{v}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{v}=\text{\rm Ham\,}f\hskip 28.45274pt\text{i.e.}\hskip 28.45274pt(v_{x},v_{y})=(-\partial_{y}f,\partial_{x}f)

but rather other combinations of partial derivatives are needed.
In particular what we found is that one is to construct Hamiltonian field from

4.2
Vorticity-free flow and its potential

Now let us turn our attention to the first implication in (
51
).
We see that if a vector field
\mathbf{a}
in 2D has vanishing
\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{a}
,
it is necessarily a
gradient
of some function
g(x,y)
. So for the velocity field

\mathbf{v}
it holds

\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{v}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{v}=\text{\rm grad\,}g\hskip 28.45274pt\text{i.e.}\hskip 28.45274pt(v_{x},v_{y})=(\partial_{x}g,\partial_{y}g)

(57)

But what is the
physical
meaning of the fact
\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{v}=0
?

In 3D case,
vector
field
\text{\rm curl\,}\mathbf{v}
is known as the
vorticity
field.
It turns out that, in a given point, it represents twice the vector of the angular velocity,
by which the “droplet” centered in the point rotates.

The
scalar
function
\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{v}
is the 2D version of the vorticity of the flow.
(It is called vorticity as well.) If we placed a small body made of corc
somewhere on the surface of the 2D flow, it would rotate with angular velocity
2\ \text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{v}
.

So the condition
\text{\rm curl\,}_{3}\mathbf{v}=0
describes a
vorticity-free
flow.
As we see from (
57
), such flow is also “potential” one,
moreover in the standard sense, now
(i.e. that the vector field is computed as the gradient of the potential).

4.3
Where from complex analysis arises

Consider a 2D flow which happens to be “incompressible”
and

\mathbf{v}=\text{\rm Ham\,}f=\text{\rm grad\,}g\hskip 28.45274pt\text{i.e.}\hskip 28.45274pt(v_{x},v_{y})=(-\partial_{y}f,\partial_{x}f)=(\partial_{x}g,\partial_{y}g)

The last equality sign in (

5
Conclusion

In this paper the 2D analogue of the standard 3D vector calculus is discussed.
The approach is based on using of differential forms.
The standard logic, which helps so much for understanding 3D vector calculus, is repeated.
The approach is completely “intrinsic”, it does not use any embedding of the 2D space into
an auxiliary ambient 3D space.
We show what differential operators occur here (analogues of grad, curl and div from 3D),
how they are related and in which integral identities they may be found.
All results are also presented in standard language (i.e. with no forms whatsoever).
