Introduction

In many practical applications, there are massive amounts of unlabeled data but labeling is expensive. This distinction has driven the study of active learning
[
CAL94
,
BBZ07
,
Das11
,
Han14
,
ABL17
]
, where labels are initially hidden and the learner must pay for each label it wishes to be revealed. The goal is to design querying strategies to avoid less informative labeling requests, e.g. the labels that can be inferred from previously seen samples. Parallel to active learning, online learning concerns the scenario where the learner observes a stream of samples and makes real-time model updating in order to compete with the best model obtained by seeing all the history data in a batch
[
Ros58
,
LW89
,
CBLW96
,
Zin03
,
Sha12
,
Haz19
]
. Online learning algorithms have also been broadly investigated in machine learning, and have found various successful applications owing to its potential of savings in memory cost, low computational cost per sample, and its generalization ability
[
CCG04
,
KT08
]
.

In this paper, we study the important problem of active learning of homogeneous halfspaces in the online setting, where the learner observes a stream of unlabeled data and makes spot decision of whether or not to query the labels. The goal is to achieve the best of the two worlds: label efficiency from active learning and computational efficiency from online learning. In this spectrum, there are a number of early works that share the same merit with this paper. For example,
[
FSST97
]
proposed a query-by-committee learning algorithm and
[

]
developed a Perceptron-like algorithm, both of which are implemented in an online fashion and enjoy a near-optimal label complexity bound of

1.1
Main results

The main contribution of the paper is a novel online active learning algorithm that improves upon the state-of-the-art online algorithms. We introduce a few useful notations and informally describe our main results in this section; readers are referred to Section
4
for a precise statement.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the given concept class of halfspaces,
D
be the joint distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,1\}
, and for any
w\in\mathcal{C}
define its error rate as
\mathrm{err}_{D}(w)=\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(\operatorname{sign}(w\cdot x)\neq y)
. Our analysis hinges on the following distributional assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The unlabeled data distribution is isotropic log-concave, i.e. it has zero mean and unit covariance matrix, and the logarithm of its density function is concave.

Assumption 2
.

There exists an underlying halfspace
u\in\mathcal{C}
, such that
\mathrm{err}_{D}(u)\leq\nu
for some noise rate
\nu\geq 0
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
be the target error rate given to the learner. We have the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

If Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied and
\nu\leq O(\epsilon)
, then there is an efficient online active learner that outputs a halfspace
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{C}
with
\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{u}\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))\leq\epsilon
, with label complexity bound of
\tilde{O}\big(d\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})\big)
and sample complexity bound of
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{d}{\epsilon}\big)
.

We compare with state-of-the-art online algorithms of
[
KKMS05
,
YZ17
,
DKTZ20
]
that are tolerant to adversarial noise.
[
YZ17
]
presented an active learner and obtained analogous label and sample complexity to this work. However, their noise tolerance reads as
\nu=\tilde{\Omega}(\epsilon/\log d)
while our algorithm is able to tolerate
\nu=\Omega(\epsilon)
; in addition, our results apply to significantly broader marginal distributions. Since
[
KKMS05
,
DKTZ20
]
considered passive learning, our active learning algorithm naturally enjoys label complexity that has exponentially better dependence on
\epsilon
. Even for the sample complexity, we obtain improved dependence on
d
and
\epsilon
. On the other side, it is worth mentioning that all these three algorithms run faster than our algorithm. Also, the analysis of
[
DKTZ20
]
works under more general marginal distributions, in particular, distributions satisfying concentration, anti-concentration, anti-anti-concentration. Though our results can be generalized to their setting as well (see e.g.
[
ZL21
]
for the treatment), we do not pursue it in the paper for the sake of clean presentation.

In addition to the properties aforementioned, we show that our algorithm can essentially incorporate attribute efficiency
[
Lit87
]
. That is, when the concept class consists of
s
-sparse halfspaces, the obtained label and sample complexity scale as
\tilde{O}(s\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d))
. This characteristic is especially useful when there is limited availability of samples, a problem that has been studied for decades in machine learning and statistics; see, e.g.
[
CDS98
,
Tib96
,
KS04
,
CT05
,
Fel07
,
PVY17
]
.

We have the following result for learning sparse halfspaces.

Theorem 2

(Informal)
.

If Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied,
\nu\leq O(\epsilon)
, and
u
is
s
-sparse, then there is an efficient online active learner that outputs an
s
-sparse halfspace
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{C}
with
\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{u}\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))\leq\epsilon
, with label complexity bound of
\tilde{O}\big(s\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon})\big)
and sample complexity bound of
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{s}{\epsilon}\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d)\big)
.

Observe that Theorem
1
is a special case of the above by setting
s=d
. We note that the state-of-the-art online algorithms of
[
KKMS05
,
YZ17
,
DKTZ20
]
do not enjoy attribute efficiency – it is yet nontrivial for them to encompass this property. Hence we have exponentially better dependence on the dimension
d
in label and sample complexity. See Table
1
for a summary of the comparison.

Finally, through an interesting observation made in
[
ABL17
]
, it is possible to translate our main results for the adversarial noise model to the agnostic model of
[
Hau92
,
KSS92
]
where no assumption is made on the noise rate
\nu

\mathrm{OPT}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{w\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{err}_{D}(w)
.

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

If Assumption
1
is satisfied, then the algorithm tolerating the adversarial noise outputs a halfspace
\tilde{u}
with
\mathrm{err}_{D}(\tilde{u})\leq O(\mathrm{OPT})+\epsilon
, with same label and sample complexity as in Theorem
2

1.2
Overview of our techniques

Our algorithm is inspired in part by
[
ZSA20
]
. We present an overview of our techniques below, and highlight the algorithmic connection to them as well as the novelty.

1) Active learning via stagewise online mirror descent.
The first ingredient in our algorithm is a novel perspective of approaching active learning of halfspaces via stagewise online learning, recently utilized by
[
ZSA20
]
for learning halfspaces with benign noise. In each phase, given an initial halfspace
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, regardless of how the samples are generated, standard regret bound established for online mirror descent with linear loss
\left\langle w,\alpha g_{t}\right\rangle
and
\ell_{p}
-norm regularizer
\Phi(w)
implies that the produced sequence of iterates
\{w_{t-1}\}_{t=1}^{T}
must satisfy the following with certainty:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle u,-g_{t}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle w_{t-1},-g_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(u;w_{0})}{\alpha T}+\frac{\alpha}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g_{t}\right\rVert}_{q}^{2},

(1.1)

where
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the underlying halfspace we aim to approximate,
\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(\cdot;\cdot)
denotes Bregman divergence induced by
\Phi
, and
q\in(0,1)
is such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. Our goal is threefold: attribute efficiency, label efficiency, and small error rate. We will first specify
p\approx 1
to achieve attribute efficiency which is a well-known technique in online learning
[
GLS01
,
Gen03
]
. In order to reduce the error rate of the initial halfspace
w_{0}
with a few label queries, we need to design suitable gradients
g_{t}
and choose proper step size
\alpha
and iteration number
T
such that a) the right-hand side of (
1.1
) is as small as angle
O(\theta(w_{0},u))
; and b) the left-hand side is bounded from below by
\theta(\bar{w},u)
for certain halfspace
\bar{w}
that depends on the sequence
\{w_{t-1}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. It is then possible to show that
\theta(\bar{w},u)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\theta(w_{0},u)
, and we can use
\bar{w}
as the initial iterate for the next phase of online mirror descent to reduce the distance to
u
with geometric rate. Therefore, the crucial challenges lie in the design of
g_{t}
to accommodate specific noise model and an associated sampling scheme (since
g_{t}
depends on the sample). These are also the key technical differences between our work and
[
ZSA20
]
, which we elaborate on below.

2) A semi-random gradient update for tolerating adversarial noise.
In
[
ZSA20
]
, the gradient
g_{t}
is heavily tailored to the bounded noise condition
[
MN06
]
. We find it technically hard to reuse it for the adversarial noise since it is well known that the latter is a more involved noise model that will always violate the conditions assumed in the former.
4
4

4

In the bounded noise model, the adversary is constrained to flip the label of each given instance with probability at most
\eta\in[0,1/2)
, which dramatically limits its power. In the adversarial noise, nevertheless, the adversary has the freedom to choose
any
joint distribution over the instance and label space.
Therefore, we consider an alternative yet fairly natural candidate: we choose
g_{t}
as the original gradient used in the Perceptron algorithm. That is, given the currently learned halfspace
w_{t-1}
and a new labeled sample
(x_{t},y_{t})
, we set
g_{t}=-y_{t}x_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y_{t}\neq\operatorname{sign}(w_{t-1}\cdot x_{t})\}}
. It remains to develop a plausible sampling scheme so that a)
-g_{t}
will have nontrivial correlation with the underlying halfspace
u
; and b) only most informative instances are sampled for labeling. To this end, we propose a new sampling region
X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0<\hat{w}_{t-1}\cdot x\leq b\}
, where
\hat{w}_{t-1}=\frac{w_{t-1}}{\left\lVert w_{t-1}\right\rVert}
. Such time-varying region is different from active learning using empirical risk minimization
[
BBZ07
,
ABL17
,
Zha18
]
as in these works
x
is sampled from the full band
\left\lvert\hat{w}_{0}\cdot x\right\rvert\leq b
. On one hand, using our sampling region leads to a linear loss
\left\langle w_{t-1},g_{t}\right\rangle
at most
O(b)
, while the band used by ERM would result in a loss of
O(b\sqrt{s}\log d)
. Observe that a tighter control on the loss implies tighter upper bound in (
1.1
). On the other hand, we discover that by restricting on querying the label of instances in
X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}
, we are reducing the randomness of model updating because now
g_{t}=x_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y_{t}\neq 1\}}
, i.e. we update the model only when the returned label
y_{t}=-1
. It turns out that such
semi-randomness
facilitates our control of the correlation between each
-g_{t}
and the underlying halfspace
u
. We note that the semi-random updating rule is inspired by
[
YZ17
]
, where they used a much narrower sampling region
\big\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\frac{b}{2\sqrt{d}}\leq\hat{w}_{t-1}\cdot x\leq\frac{b}{\sqrt{d}}\big\}
and a carefully rescaled Perceptron gradient
g_{t}=(\hat{w}_{t-1}\cdot x_{t})\cdot x_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y_{t}\neq 1\}}
to accommodate their projection-free algorithm for learning under the uniform marginal distribution. In contrast, we incorporate different sampling strategy and gradients into (projected) mirror descent for PAC learning under isotropic log-concave marginal distributions.

3) A new characterization of the correlation between gradient and the underlying halfspace.
Our last ingredient is applying localization in the concept space
[
ABL17
]
. Roughly speaking, before running online mirror descent, it is possible to construct an
\ell_{2}
-ball where the underlying halfspace
u
resides in. Such trust region will be serving as the convex constraint set for online minimization. Using a novel analysis, we show that this interesting observation in allusion to the dedicated design of gradients implies that under the adversarial noise model,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{(x_{t},y_{t})\sim D_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}}[\left\langle u,-g_{t}\right\rangle]\geq f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})-\beta\cdot\theta(w_{0},u),

(1.2)

where we have the potential function

f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{(x_{t},y_{t})\sim D_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}}\big[\left\lvert u\cdot x_{t}\right\rvert\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x_{t}<0\}}\big],

(1.3)

and
\beta>0
is some quantity to be controlled. We argue that the function
f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})
serves almost as a measure of
\theta(w_{t-1},u)
; hence combining it with (
1.1
) we have that the average of
\theta(w_{t-1},u)
is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\theta(w_{0},u)
. This observation, in conjunction with a non-standard online-to-batch conversion, results in the desired halfspace
\bar{w}
. We note two different aspects compared to
[
ZSA20
]
. First, our potential function
f_{u,b}
is slightly distinct since we are considering a smaller sampling region. Second and more importantly, when deriving the lower bound for the correlation of
u
and
-g_{t}
, we carry out a more involved analysis but still incur a negative penalty
-\beta\cdot\theta(w_{0},u)
resulted from the adversarial noise model. In contrast, this term does not appear in
[
ZSA20
]
(since the bounded noise model they studied is more benign). Manipulating the penalty turns out to be subtle since if the factor
\beta
is large, there is no hope to upper bound the average of
f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})
by
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\theta(w_{0},u)
. We circumvent the technical issue by showing that
\beta
is dominated by
\nu/b
which is small as soon as
\nu\leq c_{0}\epsilon
for sufficiently small constant
c_{0}
and
b
is carefully chosen to be greater than
\epsilon
; see Lemma
13
in the appendix.

2
Preliminaries

We study PAC learning of sparse homogeneous halfspaces with adversarial noise, where the instance space is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the label space is
\{-1,1\}
, and the concept class is
\mathcal{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\mapsto\operatorname{sign}(w\cdot x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \left\lVert w\right\rVert=1,\ \left\lVert w\right\rVert_{0}\leq s\}
. Here,
\left\lVert w\right\rVert
denotes the
\ell_{2}
-norm and
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{0}
counts the number of non-zero elements in
w
. Observe that we say a halfspace is non-sparse if
s=d
. An adversary
\mathrm{EX}
with adversarial noise works as follows: it first chooses an arbitrary joint distribution
D
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,1\}
; the distribution
D
is then fixed throughout learning. Let
D_{X}
denote the marginal distribution over the instance space, which is promised to belong to a family of well-behaved distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
; in this paper it is assumed to be isotropic log-concave (Assumption
1
).

A learner is given the instance and label space, the concept class
\mathcal{C}
, the family of distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
(but not
D_{X}
), a target error rate
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and a failure confidence
\delta\in(0,1)
, and the goal is to output in polynomial time a halfspace
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{C}
such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{u}\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))\leq\epsilon
. In the passive learning setting, the learner is given access to a sample generation oracle
\mathrm{EX}
which returns a labeled sample
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,1\}
randomly drawn from the distribution
D
. Since we want to optimize the label complexity, we will consider a natural extension: when the learner makes a call to
\mathrm{EX}
, a labeled sample
(x,y)
is randomly drawn but only the instance
x
is returned. The learner must make a separate call to a label revealing oracle
\mathrm{EX}_{y}
to obtain the label
y
. We refer to the total number of calls to
\mathrm{EX}
as the sample complexity of the algorithm, and that of
\mathrm{EX}_{y}
as the label complexity.

In our active learning algorithm, we will often want to draw instances from
D_{X}
conditioned on a region
X_{\hat{w},b}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0<\hat{w}\cdot x\leq b\}
where
\hat{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b>0
are given; this can be done by rejection sampling, where we repeatedly call
\mathrm{EX}
until seeing an instance
x
that falls in
X_{\hat{w},b}
. We will refer to
X_{\hat{w},b}
as sampling region, and
b
is called band width. We denote by
D_{X|\hat{w},b}
(respectively
D_{\hat{w},b}
) the distribution
D_{X}
(respectively
D
) conditioned on the event that
x\in X_{\hat{w},b}
.

Let
w
be a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will frequently use
\hat{w}
to denote its
\ell_{2}
-normalization
\frac{w}{\left\lVert w\right\rVert}
. For a scalar
\gamma\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert w\right\rVert}_{\gamma}
the
\ell_{\gamma}
-norm of
w
. Let
s>0
be an integer less than
d
. The hard thresholding operation
\mathcal{H}_{s}(w)
zeros out all but the
s
largest (in magnitude) entries in
w
. For two vectors
w
and
w^{\prime}
, we write
\theta(w,w^{\prime})
for the angle between them.

We reserve
p
and
q
for specific values:
p=\frac{\ln(8d)}{\ln(8d)-1}
and
q=\ln(8d)
(note that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
). We will use the
\ell_{p}
-norm regularizer, that is,
\Phi(w)=\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert w-v\right\rVert}_{p}^{2}
for some given vector
v
. It is known that
\Phi(w)
is
1
-strongly convex with respect to the
\ell_{p}
-norm
[
SS07
, Lemma 17]
. We denote the Bregman divergence induced by
\Phi(w)
by
\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(w;w^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Phi(w)-\Phi(w^{\prime})-\left\langle\nabla\Phi(w^{\prime}),w-w^{\prime}\right\rangle
. Observe that
\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(w;v)=\Phi(w)
where
v
is the reference vector appearing in
\Phi(w)
.

We will sometimes phrase our theoretical guarantee in terms of angles between two halfspaces. The following lemma, due to
[
BL13
]
, is useful to convert the guarantee of angles to that of error rate.

Lemma 4
.

There exists an absolute constant
\bar{c}>0
such that the following holds. Let
D_{X}
be an isotropic log-concave distribution. For any two vectors
w
and
w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}(\operatorname{sign}(w\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(w^{\prime}\cdot x))\leq\bar{c}\cdot\theta(w,w^{\prime})
.

We remark that a closely related noise model is the agnostic model
[
Hau92
,
KSS92
,
KKMS05
]
, where only Assumption
1
is satisfied and the goal is to output a halfspace that approximates the best halfspace in
\mathcal{C}
. The results from our theorems under the adversarial noise model translate immediately into PAC guarantees for the agnostic model; see Section
4.1
for more details.

3
Main Algorithm

We present our online active learning algorithm in Algorithm
1
, which consists of two major stages: initialization and refinement. In the initialization stage, the goal is to find a halfspace
v_{0}
that has a constant acute angle with the underlying halfspace
u
. It will then be used as a warm start for the refinement stage, where the procedure
Refine
is repeatedly invoked to cut off the angle with
u
by half in each phase
k
. Therefore, after
K
phases of refinement, we will obtain a halfspace
v_{K}
satisfying
\theta(v_{K},u)=2^{-K}=O(\epsilon)
, which by Lemma
4
implies that
v_{K}
has small error rate with respect to the underlying halfspace
u
defined in Assumption
2
.

Since
Initialize
invokes
Refine
as well, we will introduce the latter first. Generally speaking, the
Refine
algorithm, i.e. Algorithm
3
, belongs to the family of online mirror descent algorithms with Perceptron gradient and
\ell_{p}
-norm regularization. The crucial ingredients that make it attribute and label efficient are a carefully crafted constraint set, a time-varying sampling region, and semi-random gradients, as we described in Section
1.2
. In particular, the constraint set
\mathcal{K}
is constructed in such a way that the underlying halfspace
u
is guaranteed to stay in it (with overwhelming probability). Thus, it serves as a trust region into which all the iterates
w_{t}
are projected back. It is worth mentioning that we did not put an
\ell_{1}
-norm constraint in
\mathcal{K}
; this is because we already have utilized the
\ell_{p}
-norm regularization to simultaneously guarantee attribute efficiency
[
GLS01
,
Gen03
]
and the stability of online minimization
[
Sha12
,
Ora19
]
. The second component in
Refine
is the time-varying sampling region
X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0<\hat{w}_{t-1}\cdot x\leq b\}
, which results in a linear loss as small as
O(b)
in each iteration. In contrast, a naive online approach to simulate the ERM algorithm of
[
Zha18
]
would lead to a loss as large as
O(b\sqrt{s}\log d)
. The idea of using time-varying sampling paradigm has appeared in a few online active learning algorithms
[
YZ17
,
ZSA20
]
. Ours is less restrictive than the one in
[
YZ17
]
, and is more dedicated to the much more challenging adversarial noise compared to the bounded noise model considered in
[
ZSA20
]
. Along with the new sampling region is a semi-random Perceptron gradient. Recall that the original gradient used in Perceptron is given by
g_{t}=-y_{t}x_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y_{t}\neq\operatorname{sign}(w_{t-1}\cdot x_{t})\}}
. Since
x_{t}\in X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}
, we update the model only when the label
y_{t}
returned by the adversary equals
-1
, thus inducing the gradient displayed in Algorithm
3
. Finally, after running mirror descent for
T
iterations, we perform an averaging scheme followed by hard thresholding, to ensure that the output
\tilde{w}
belongs the to concept class. We remark that the running time of
Refine
is polynomial in
d
, since in each iteration
t
, updating the model requires solving a convex program. Regarding the label complexity, it is exactly equal to the total iteration number
T
. We also remark that obtaining
x_{t}
can be done by calling
\mathrm{EX}
for
O(1/b)
times since the probability mass of
X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}
on
D_{X}
is
\Theta(b)
; see Lemma
29
.

Now we elaborate on the
Initialize
algorithm, namely Algorithm
2
. Technically speaking, one important condition for the success of our analysis is that an overwhelming portion of the iterates must have acute angles with the underlying halfspace
u
. Therefore, the hypothesis testing approach proposed in
[
ABL17
]
does not work out in our case since we will lose control of the intermediate iterates. To circumvent the technical challenge, we tailor the averaging-based initialization scheme of
[
ZSA20
]
to the adversarial noise model. It is possible to show that as far as the noise rate
\nu
is low,
w_{\mathrm{avg}}
has a positive correlation with
u
, and performing hard thresholding almost preserves it, i.e.
{u}\cdot{w^{\sharp}}=\Omega(1)
. Therefore, we obtain a good reference vector
w^{\sharp}
which is guaranteed to have an acute angle with
u
. Based on an enhanced constraint set that takes the correlation into consideration, we are able to show that most of the iterates are admissible, and hence our analysis of the
Refine
algorithm can be reused to show that the output
v_{0}
will have a small acute angle with
u
. Note that we are not making efforts to optimize the constants that appear in the
Initialize
algorithm; in practice, we believe that our algorithm works under reasonable constants. It is also worth mentioning that
[
ZL21
]
recently developed a simpler initialization scheme for the problem of learning halfspaces with Massart or Tsybakov noise; it will be interesting to adapt their approach to the adversarial noise model as a future work.

4
Performance Guarantee

We are now in the position to state our main theorem, which is a formal statement of Theorem
2
.

Theorem 5

(Main result)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied. If
\nu\leq c_{0}\epsilon
for some small absolute constant
c_{0}>0
, then with probability
1-\delta
, the output of Algorithm
1
,
\tilde{u}
, satisfies
\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}\big(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{u}\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)\big)\leq\epsilon
. Moreover, the running time is
\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon},\log\frac{1}{\delta})
, the label complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(s\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon},\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
, and the sample complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{s}{\epsilon}\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
.

Remark 6
.

A more concrete label and sample complexity reads as
\tilde{O}(s\log d\cdot\log^{3}\frac{d}{\epsilon\delta})
and
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{s}{\epsilon}\cdot\log^{4}\frac{d}{\delta}\big)
, respectively; see Theorem
18
and Theorem
24
in the appendix.

Remark 7

(Excess risk)
.

By the triangle inequality, we have
\mathrm{err}_{D}(\tilde{u})-\mathrm{err}_{D}(u)\leq\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{u}\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))\leq\epsilon
. Namely, the excess risk of
\tilde{u}
with respect to
u
is at most
\epsilon
over the underlying distribution.

Remark 8

(Implications to passive learning)
.

It is possible to convert our algorithm to an online passive learner, where there is only one oracle
\mathrm{EX}
that always returns a labeled instance upon request. To this end, observe that the active learner interacts with the oracle exclusively in Step 2 of
Refine
. Therefore, we only need to modify this step as follows in the passive setting: repeatedly call
\mathrm{EX}
to obtain a sequence of labeled instances
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
until seeing a pair
(x_{t},y_{t})
such that
x_{t}\in X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}
. Then we use
(x_{t},y_{t})
to update the classifier in Step 3 (rather than using all the labeled instances that are drawn from
\mathrm{EX}
). It is easy to see that the label and sample complexity of the passive learner are both
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{s}{\epsilon}\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
.

The following corollary, which is a formal statement of Theorem
1
, concerns learning of non-sparse halfspaces and is an immediate application of Theorem
5
by setting
s=d
.

Corollary 9
.

Assume same conditions as in Theorem
5
. With probability
1-\delta
,
\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}\big(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{u}\cdot x)\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)\big)\leq\epsilon
. Moreover, the running time is
\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon},\log\frac{1}{\delta})
, the label complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(d\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(\frac{1}{\epsilon},\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
, and the sample complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{d}{\epsilon}\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
.

4.1
Implications to agnostic learning

In the agnostic model
[
Hau92
,
KSS92
]
, the adversary chooses a joint distribution
D
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,1\}
and fixes it throughout the learning process. Let
\mathrm{OPT}=\min_{w\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{err}_{D}(w)
. The goal of the learner is to output a hypothesis
\tilde{u}
such that
\mathrm{err}_{D}(\tilde{u})\leq c\cdot\mathrm{OPT}+\epsilon
for some approximation factor
c\geq 1
. The crucial difference from the adversarial noise model is that now Assumption
2
may not be satisfied (in other words,
\mathrm{OPT}
can be very large compared to the target error rate
\epsilon
).

[
KKMS05
]
developed a polynomial regression algorithm that achieves approximation guarantee with
c=1
, where the computational and sample complexity are both
O(d^{2^{\operatorname{poly}(1/\epsilon)}})
for learning under isotropic log-concave distributions and are
O(d^{1/\epsilon^{4}})
for uniform distributions.

On the other side,
[
KKMS05
]
and a number of recent works also obtained weaker (yet still quite nontrivial) approximation guarantee of
O(\mathrm{OPT})+\epsilon
with running time and sample complexity polynomial in
d
and
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
; see, for example,
[
ABL17
,
Zha18
,
DKS18
,
DKTZ20
]
. We follow this line, and remark that our main result, Theorem
5
, can be immediately translated into the constant approximation guarantee under the agnostic model. In fact, Lemma C.1 of
[
ABL17
]
made an interesting observation that if an algorithm can tolerate adversarial noise of
\nu=\Omega(\epsilon)
, then it essentially achieves error rate of
O(\mathrm{OPT})+\epsilon
in the agnostic model, with the same running time, label complexity, and sample complexity (up to a constant multiplicative factor). We therefore have the following result, which is a formal statement of Theorem
3
, by combining Theorem
5
we established and Lemma C.1 of
[
ABL17
]
; we omit the proof since it is fairly straightforward.

Corollary 10
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
is satisfied. Then with probability
1-\delta
,
\mathrm{err}_{D}(\tilde{u})\leq c\cdot\mathrm{OPT}+\epsilon
for some absolute constant
c>1
. Moreover, the running time is
\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon})
, the label complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(s\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon},\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
, and the sample complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(\frac{s}{\epsilon}\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\delta})\big)
.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
5

Theorem
5
hinges on the following two important results, characterizing the performance of
Initialize
and
Refine
respectively. A more precise statement and a detailed proof can be found in Appendix
A
in the supplementary material.

Theorem 11
.

Consider the
Initialize
algorithm. If Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied and
\nu\leq c_{o}\epsilon
, then with probability
1-\delta^{\prime}
, the output of
Initialize
,
v_{0}
, is such that
\theta(v_{0},u)\leq\frac{\pi}{8}
. The running time is
\operatorname{poly}(d,\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})
, the label complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(s\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d,\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})\big)
, and the sample complexity is
\tilde{O}\big(s\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(d)\big)
.

Theorem 12
.

Consider the
Refine
algorithm. Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied and
\nu\leq c_{o}\epsilon
. Then with probability
1-\delta^{\prime}
, the output of the
Refine
algorithm,
\tilde{w}
, satisfies
\theta(\tilde{w},u)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\theta(w_{0},u)
. The running time of
Refine
is
T\cdot\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{b},\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})
, the label complexity is
T
, and the sample complexity is
O(T/b+T\log\frac{T}{\delta^{\prime}})
where
T
and
b
are the inputs to
Refine
.

We first explain the results in Theorem
12
. The label complexity of
Refine
is straightforward since we request one label per iteration, and the total number of iterations is
T
. We start with the analysis of the sample complexity of
Refine
, i.e. the number of calls to
\mathrm{EX}
in Step
2
therein. Since the marginal distribution is assumed to be isotropic log-concave, Lemma
29
shows that
\operatorname{Pr}_{x_{t}\sim D_{X}}(x_{t}\in X_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b})\geq c_{2}b
for some absolute constant
c_{2}>0
. Thus, by Chernoff bound, we need to call
\mathrm{EX}
for
O(\frac{1}{b}+\log\frac{T}{\delta^{\prime}})
times in order to obtain one
x_{t}
in the band with probability
1-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2T}
. Thus, by union bound over the
T
iterations in
Refine
, with probability
1-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2}
, the total number of calls to
\mathrm{EX}
is
O(\frac{T}{b}+T\log\frac{T}{\delta^{\prime}})
. Note that this is also the computational cost for sampling. On the other side, updating the iterates, i.e. Step
3
, involves solving a convex program in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which has a running time polynomial in
d
per iteration. Thus, the overall computational cost of
Refine
is
T\cdot\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{b},\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}})
.

Now we explain the results in Theorem
11
. Generally speaking, the
Initialize
algorithm consists of two major steps, one for constructing the constraint set
\mathcal{K}
and one for obtaining a good initial halfspace
v_{0}
based on
\mathcal{K}
. To obtain
\mathcal{K}
, it consumes
m
labeled instances and the computational cost for sampling them is
O(m)
since rejection sampling is not needed. Then it aims to find a point
w_{0}
in a convex set, which is polynomial-time solvable; in fact, we can set
w_{0}
to the zero vector and then project it onto
\mathcal{K}
, corresponding to solving a convex program. The second major step is to invoke
Refine
, for which we have just analyzed. Combining these observations and the parameters specified in the
Initialize
algorithm, we obtain the announced results.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

First, Theorem
11
implies
\theta(v_{0},u)\leq\frac{\pi}{8}
with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. In addition, for any phase
k
in Algorithm
1
, we specify in Theorem
12
that
w_{0}=v_{k-1}
and
\tilde{w}=v_{k}
, and obtain that
\theta(v_{k},u)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\theta(v_{k-1},u)
with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2k(k+1)}
. By telescoping, we get
\theta(v_{K},u)\leq 2^{-K}\cdot\frac{\pi}{8}\leq\epsilon/\bar{c}
in light of our setting of
K
; this inequality holds with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\delta}{2k(k+1)}\geq 1-\delta
by union bound. This in allusion to Lemma
4
gives the desired error rate of
\tilde{u}=v_{K}
with respect to
u
.

The running time of Algorithm
1
follow from those we analyzed for
Initialize
and
Refine
, and from the hyper-parameter settings on
b_{k}
,
T_{k}
, and
\delta_{k}
in each phase
k
. In particular, observe that
b_{k}\geq\epsilon
for all
k\leq K
. Therefore, the running time is given by
\operatorname{poly}(d,\log\frac{1}{\delta})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}T_{k}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{b_{k}},\log\frac{k^{2}}{\delta})=\operatorname{poly}(d,\frac{1}{\epsilon},\log\frac{1}{\delta})
.

Likewise, for label complexity and sample complexity, we can add up the cost in the initialization stage and that of the
K
phases of refinement to obtain the bounds as claimed.
∎

5
Conclusion and Future Works

This paper studies the fundamental problem of learning halfspaces with adversarial noise. We have presented the first attribute-efficient, label-efficient, and noise-tolerant algorithm in the online setting, under the general isotropic log-concave marginal distributions. Prior to this work, existing online learners are either subject to label inefficiency or suboptimal noise tolerance, or work under restrictive marginal distributions. We have shown that our label and sample complexity are near-optimal, and the learner achieves PAC guarantee in polynomial time. Prior to this work, such performance guarantee is only achieved by a very recent batch algorithm. Our results also have immediate implications to the agnostic model, and match the best known results obtained by polynomial-time batch algorithms.

We discuss a few important directions for future investigation. First, it is interesting to develop online PAC algorithms with
\mathrm{OPT}+\epsilon
approximation error under the agnostic model, by leveraging, for example, the polynomial regression technique
[
KKMS05
]
into the online mirror descent framework. Second, it is useful to extend the analysis to more general concept classes such as intersections of halfspaces
[
KOS02
,
DKS18
]
. It will also be important to design PAC algorithms that leverage additional types of queries such as pairwise comparison
[
KLMZ17
,
XZS+17
]
in the scenario where labels are extremely demanding (e.g. medical data), or to develop new projection-free algorithms for even faster computation.

Refine

Intuitively, since we are performing gradient descent, we would hope that the negative gradient has a nontrivial correlation with the underlying halfspace
u
. The following lemma formalizes the intuition.

Lemma 13
.

Given
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\left\lVert w-u\right\rVert\leq r
and
b>0
, let
g=x\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y=-1\}}
where
(x,y)\sim D_{\hat{w},b}
. If the noise rate
\nu\leq c_{0}b
for some absolute constant
c_{0}>0
, then

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[u\cdot(-g)\right]}\geq f_{u,b}(w)-\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\cdot(b+r),

where the expectation is taken over the random draw of
(x,y)
.

Proof.

By the definition of
g
, it follows that

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[u\cdot(-g)\right]}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[-(u\cdot x)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y=-1\}}\right]}.

(A.2)

As we can rewrite the indicator function
\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y=-1\}}
in an equivalent form as follows:

\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y=-1\}}=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x<0\}}+\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x>0,y=-1\}}-\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y=1,u\cdot x<0\}},

(A.3)

(
A.2
) can be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[u\cdot(-g)\right]}=\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[-(u\cdot x)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x<0\}}\right]}}_{E_{1}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[-(u\cdot x)\cdot\mathinner{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x>0,y=-1\}}-\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y=1,u\cdot x<0\}}\right)}\right]}}_{E_{2}}.

First, we argue that
E_{1}=f_{u,b}(w)
. In fact, when
u\cdot x\geq 0
,
E_{1}=0=f_{u,b}(w)
; when
u\cdot x<0
,
E_{1}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert]=f_{u,b}(w)
.

Let us now consider the term
E_{2}
, whose absolute value can be bounded by

\left\lvert E_{2}\right\rvert\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)\neq{y}\}}\right]}\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[(u\cdot x)^{2}\right]}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)\neq{y}\}}\right]}}.

By Lemma
28
,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[(u\cdot x)^{2}\right]}\leq c_{1}(b^{2}+r^{2})
. On the other side, from the definition of
\nu
-adversarial noise, we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathinner{\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)\neq{y}\}}\right]}=\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D_{\hat{w},b}}(y\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))\leq\frac{\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(y\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))}{\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}(x\in X_{\hat{w},b})}\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{2}\cdot b}.

In the first inequality of the above expression, we use the fact that for an event
A
,
\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D_{\hat{w},b}}(A)=\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(A\mid x\in X_{\hat{w},b})\leq\frac{\operatorname{Pr}_{(x,y)\sim D}(A)}{\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}(x\in X_{\hat{w},b})}
. In the second inequality, we use Lemma
29
to bound the denominator from below.

Therefore,

\left\lvert E_{2}\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{c_{1}(b^{2}+r^{2})\cdot\frac{\nu}{c_{2}\cdot b}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}(b^{2}+r^{2})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\cdot(b+r).

Now combining the above estimate and that of
E_{1}
, we prove the lemma.
∎

Lemma 14
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that the following holds.
Suppose the algorithm
Refine
is run with initialization
w_{0}
, step size
\alpha>0
, bandwidth
b>0
, convex constraint set
\mathcal{K}
, regularizer
\Phi(w)=\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert w-w_{0}\right\rVert}_{p}^{2}
, number of iterations
T
, where the following are satisfied:

1.

\left\lVert w_{0}-u\right\rVert_{1}\leq\rho
;

2.

w_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
and
u\in\mathcal{K}
;

3.

for all
w\in\mathcal{K}
,
\left\lVert w-u\right\rVert\leq r
.

Then, with probability
1-\delta
,

C\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq(b+r)\mathinner{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T}+C\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\right)}+\frac{\rho^{2}\log d}{\alpha T}+\alpha\cdot\log^{2}\frac{Td}{b\delta}.

Proof.

By standard analysis of online mirror descent (see, e.g. Theorem 6.8 of
[
Ora19
]
), we have

\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t-1}\cdot g_{t}-\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T}u\cdot g_{t}\leq\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(u,w_{0})+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\alpha g_{t}\right\rVert}_{q}^{2}.

Since
w_{t-1}\cdot g_{t}=w_{t-1}\cdot x_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{y_{t}=-1\}}
and
x_{t}
is such that
\hat{w}_{t-1}\cdot x_{t}>0
, we have
w_{t-1}\cdot g_{t}\geq 0
for all
t
. Using this observation and dividing both sides by
\alpha
, we obtain

\sum_{t=1}^{T}u\cdot(-g_{t})\leq\frac{\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(u,w_{0})}{\alpha}+\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g_{t}\right\rVert}_{q}^{2}.

(A.4)

We first present upper bounds for the right-hand side. In particular, note that

\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}(u,w_{0})=\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u-w_{0}\right\rVert}_{p}^{2}\leq\frac{\ln(8d)-1}{2}\rho^{2}\leq\frac{\rho^{2}\ln(8d)}{2},

(A.5)

where in the first inequality we use the fact that for any

,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u-w_{0}\right\rVert}_{p}\leq\left\lVert u-w_{0}\right\rVert_{1}
.

For the

-norm of
g_{t}
, denote

the

. We have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g_{t}\right\rVert}_{q}=\mathinner{\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\lvert g_{t}^{(j)}\right\rvert^{q}\Bigr)}^{1/q}\leq(d\left\lVert g_{t}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{q})^{1/q}\leq 2\left\lVert g_{t}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert_{\infty},

(A.6)

where the first inequality makes use of the definition of
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, the second inequality applies the setting

, and the last step follows from the setting of
g_{t}
. On the other side, it is known that for any
x_{t}\sim D_{X|\hat{w}_{t-1},b}
, with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2T}
, we have
\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq K_{1}\cdot\log\frac{Td}{b\delta}
for some absolute constant
K_{1}>0

30
. Hence, the union bound implies that with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2}

\max_{1\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq K\cdot\log\frac{Td}{b\delta}
, which further gives

\max_{1\leq t\leq T}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g_{t}\right\rVert}_{q}\leq 2K_{1}\cdot\log\frac{Td}{b\delta}.

(A.7)

Now we consider a lower bound of
\sum_{t=1}^{T}u\cdot(-g_{t})
. Define the filtration

\mathcal{F}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma(w_{0},x_{1},y_{1},w_{1},\dots,x_{t-1},y_{t-1},w_{t}),

and denote by
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{t-1}[\cdot]
the expectation over
(x_{t},y_{t})\sim D_{\hat{w}_{t-1},b}
conditioning on the past filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
; likewise for
\operatorname{Pr}_{t-1}(\cdot)

By existing tail bound of one-dimensional isotropic log-concave distributions in the band

(see, e.g. Lemma 3.3 of
[
ABL17
]
), and the fact that
\left\lVert u-\hat{w}_{t-1}\right\rVert\leq 2\left\lVert u-w_{t-1}\right\rVert\leq 2r

\operatorname{Pr}_{t-1}\big(\left\lvert u\cdot x_{t}\right\rvert\geq a\big)\leq K\exp\mathinner{\Bigl(-K^{\prime}\cdot\frac{a}{2r+b}\Bigr)},

for some constants
K,K^{\prime}>0
, implying that

\operatorname{Pr}_{t-1}\big(\left\lvert u\cdot(-g_{t})\right\rvert\geq a\big)\leq K\exp\mathinner{\Bigl(-K^{\prime}\cdot\frac{a}{2r+b}\Bigr)}.

Now applying Lemma

Z_{t}=u\cdot(-g_{t})
therein gives that with probability

\left\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}u\cdot(-g_{t})-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{t-1}[u\cdot(-g_{t})]\right\rvert\leq K_{2}(b+r)\mathinner{\left(\sqrt{T\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}.

(A.8)

The above concentration of martingales, in allusion to Lemma

\sum_{t=1}^{T}u\cdot(-g_{t})\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})-T\cdot\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\cdot(b+r)-K_{2}(b+r)\mathinner{\left(\sqrt{T\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}.

(A.9)

Combining (

), (
A.7

\displaystyle C\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq

\displaystyle\ (b+r)\mathinner{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta^{\prime})}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta^{\prime})}{T}+C\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\right)}

\displaystyle\ +\frac{\rho^{2}\log d}{\alpha T}+\alpha\cdot\log^{2}\frac{Td}{b\delta}

for some absolute constant
C>0
.
∎

Proposition 15
.

Suppose that the adversarial noise rate

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq\frac{\theta}{50\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 2^{33}}.

We will first verify that the premises of Lemma

\left\lVert w_{0}-u\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{\left\lVert w_{0}-u\right\rVert_{0}}\cdot\left\lVert w_{0}-u\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{2s}\cdot\theta

in Lemma

Now with

and

, Lemma

indicates that with probability

C\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq(b+2\theta)\mathinner{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T}+C\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\right)}+\frac{\theta^{2}\cdot 2s\log d}{\alpha T}+{\alpha}\cdot\log^{2}\frac{Td}{b\delta}.

and set

Lemma 17
.

Let
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{16}]
be a given scalar. Let

Define the index set

Now we translate these bounds on the angles to those of the cosine distance, and obtain

Lemma 19
.

Suppose that

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[y(u\cdot x)]\geq\frac{1}{9\cdot 2^{17}}

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[y(u\cdot x)]=

\displaystyle\ \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[y(u\cdot x)\mid y=\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)]\cdot\operatorname{Pr}(y=\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))

\displaystyle\ +\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[y(u\cdot x)\mid y\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x)]\cdot\operatorname{Pr}(y\neq\operatorname{sign}(u\cdot x))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert]\cdot(1-\nu)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert]\cdot\nu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (1-2\nu)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert].

Since
u\cdot x
is an isotropic log-concave random variable in
\mathbb{R}
, its density function is lower bounded by
2^{-16}
when
\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{9}
in view of Lemma
29
. Thus
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert]\geq\frac{1}{9\cdot 2^{16}}
. On the other side, we assumed
\nu\leq\frac{1}{4}
. Together, we obtain
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[y(u\cdot x)]\geq\frac{1}{9\cdot 2^{17}}
.
∎

Lemma 20
.

Let
m=O(\log\frac{1}{\delta})

be

First, Lemma

be a positive integer, and set

Proposition 22

Suppose that the adversarial noise rate

Initialize

\alpha=\tilde{\Theta}\mathinner{\left(\log^{-2}\frac{d}{\delta}\right)}

, regularizer
\Phi(w)=\frac{1}{2(p-1)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert w-w_{0}\right\rVert}_{p}^{2}

, where

is an arbitrary point in

that can be found in polynomial time. Then with probability

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq\frac{\zeta}{20\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 2^{33}}.

Proof.

We will first verify that the premises of Lemma
14
are satisfied. First of all, it is easy to see that
\left\lVert w_{0}-u\right\rVert_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{s}
. Hence we can choose

in Lemma

. Next, we have

with probability

. Hence we can choose

in Lemma

.

Now with
\rho=\sqrt{s}
and

, Lemma

indicates that with probability

C\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq(b+2)\mathinner{\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T}+C\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}c_{1}}{c_{2}}}\right)}+\frac{s\log d}{\alpha T}+{\alpha}\cdot\log^{2}\frac{Td}{b\delta}.

Lemma 23
.

Set
b=\frac{1}{81\cdot 2^{22}}
and let
\zeta=\frac{1}{9\cdot 2^{20}}
. Let
w_{0},\dots w_{T-1}
be a sequence of vectors such that for all
1\leq t\leq T
,
\left\lVert w_{t-1}\right\rVert\leq 1
. Further assume that
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq\frac{\zeta}{20\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 2^{33}}
. Let
\bar{w}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t-1}
and
v=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{s}(\bar{w})}{\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{s}(\bar{w})\right\rVert}
. Then
\theta(v,u)\leq\frac{\pi}{8}
.

Proof.

In light of Lemma
21
, we know that
u\in\mathcal{K}
. Also, our choices of
b
and
\zeta
implies that
b\leq\frac{\zeta}{36}
. Define
S=\{1\leq t\leq T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\geq\frac{\zeta}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}\}
. Then by the second part of Lemma
26
, for all
t\in\bar{S}
, we have
\theta(w_{t-1},u)<\zeta
. For all
t\in S
, we have a trivial estimate of
\theta(w_{t-1},u)\in[0,\pi]
.

Next we bound the size of
S
. Using the condition
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{u,b}(w_{t-1})\leq\frac{\zeta}{20\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 2^{33}}
, it is possible to show that
\frac{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}{T}\leq\frac{1}{20\cdot 2^{12}}
and thus
\frac{\left\lvert\bar{S}\right\rvert}{T}\geq 1-\frac{1}{20\cdot 2^{12}}

Now we translate these bounds on the angles to those of the cosine distance, and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\cos\theta(w_{t-1},u)

\displaystyle\geq\cos\zeta\cdot\mathinner{\left(1-\frac{1}{20\cdot 2^{12}}\right)}+(\cos\pi)\cdot\frac{1}{20\cdot 2^{12}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{5}\mathinner{\left(\frac{\pi}{128}\right)}^{2}\geq\cos\frac{\pi}{128}.

where in the last inequality we use the fact that

The above inequality, in combination with Lemma

\cos\theta(\bar{w},u)\geq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\cos\theta(w_{t-1},u)\geq\cos\frac{\pi}{128}.

Finally, we use Lemma

, which concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
The Structure of
f_{u,b}(w)

Our definition of the potential function
f_{u,b}(w)
slightly differs from that of
[
ZSA20
]
: in this work, the expectation is taken over
D
conditioned on
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0<w\cdot x\leq b\}
while in
[
ZSA20
]
it is conditioned on
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}-b\leq w\cdot x\leq b\}
. It can be seen that our function value is always less than that of
[
ZSA20
]
. However, we note that the difference in sampling region does not lead to significant difference in the structure of the potential function. In particular, we are still able to show that under certain conditions,
f_{u,b}(w)
serves as an upper bound of
\theta(w,u)
– a crucial observation made in
[
ZSA20
]
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
w
and
u
be two unit vectors. Suppose
b\in\big[0,\frac{\pi}{72}\big]
. We have

1.

If
\theta(w,u)\in[36b,\frac{\pi}{2}]
, then
f_{u,b}(w)\geq\frac{\theta(w,u)}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}
.

2.

If
\theta(w,u)\in[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi-36b]
, then
f_{u,b}(w)\geq\frac{\pi-\theta(w,u)}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}
.

Proof.

The proof of the first part follows closely from [Lemma 22, Part 1] of
[
ZSA20
]
. In particular, for the region

R_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}w\cdot x\in[0,b],\ u\cdot x\in\mathinner{\left[-\frac{\sin\theta(w,u)}{18},-\frac{\sin\theta(w,u)}{36}\right]}\right\}},

their analysis shows that

\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}(x\in R_{1})\geq\frac{b}{9\cdot 2^{18}}.

(B.1)

To see why it completes the proof of the first part, observe that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{{0\leq w\cdot x\leq b}\}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x<0\}}\right]}\geq

\displaystyle\ \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x\in R_{1}\}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \frac{\sin\theta(w,u)}{36}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x\in R_{1}\}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \frac{\theta(w,u)}{72}\cdot\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left(x\in R_{1}\right)}\geq\frac{\theta(w,u)\cdot b}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}},

where the first inequality uses the fact that
R_{1}
is a subset of both sets
\mathinner{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}w\cdot x\in[0,b]\right\}}
and
\mathinner{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}u\cdot x<0\right\}}
; the second inequality uses the fact that for all
x
in
R_{1}
,
\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert\geq\frac{\sin\theta(w,u)}{36}
; the third inequality uses the elementary fact that
\sin\phi\geq\frac{\phi}{2}
for any angle
\phi\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
.

As
\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left(w\cdot x\in[0,b]\right)}\leq b
by Lemma
29
, we have

f_{u,b}(w)=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left[\left\lvert u\cdot x\right\rvert\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{{0\leq w\cdot x\leq b}\}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\{u\cdot x<0\}}\right]}}{\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}\mathinner{\left(w\cdot x\in[0,b]\right)}}\geq\frac{\theta(w,u)\cdot b}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}\cdot\frac{1}{b}=\frac{\theta(w,u)}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}.

This completes the proof of the first part.

For the second part, we will define
\phi=\pi-\theta(w,u)
and consider

R_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}w\cdot x\in[0,b],\ u\cdot x\in\mathinner{\left[-\frac{\sin\phi}{18},-\frac{\sin\phi}{36}\right]}\right\}}.

Similar to the region
R_{1}
, we have
\operatorname{Pr}_{x\sim D_{X}}(x\in R_{2})\geq\frac{b}{9\cdot 2^{18}}
. Hence, using the same induction with the proof of first part, we have
f_{u,b}(w)\geq\frac{\phi}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}=\frac{\pi-\theta(w,u)}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}
.
∎

The following lemma connects the potential function
f_{u,b}(w)
to the angle
\theta(w,u)
.

Lemma 26
.

Let
w
and
u
be two unit vectors. We have the following:

1.

Suppose
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
is given. Set
b\leq\frac{\theta}{5\cdot 36}
. If
f_{u,b}(w)\leq\frac{\theta}{5\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}
, then
\theta(w,u)\leq\frac{\theta}{5}
.

2.

Let
w^{\sharp}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a unit vector with
w^{\sharp}\cdot u\geq\zeta
for some
\zeta\in(0,1)
. Set
b\leq\frac{\zeta}{36}
. If
w
is in
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\left\lVert w\right\rVert\leq 1,\ w\cdot w^{\sharp}\geq\zeta\}
and

, then

.

Proof.

The first part was already set out in Claim 10 of
[
ZSA20
]
. For the second part, first, we show that it is impossible for
\theta(w,u)\geq\frac{\pi}{2}
. Assume for contradiction that this holds.
By Lemma
27
, for all
w
in
\mathcal{K}
, we have that

\theta(w,u)\leq\pi-\zeta
. By the choice of

, we know that

, hence

. Now using the second part of Lemma

, we have

f_{u,b}(w)\geq\frac{\pi-\theta(w,u)}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}}\geq\frac{\zeta}{3^{4}\cdot 2^{21}},

which contradicts with the premise that

Therefore,

∎
