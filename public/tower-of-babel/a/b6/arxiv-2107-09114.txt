Abstract
A positive integer n n is said to be a Jordan-Pólya number if it can be written as a product of factorials. We obtain non-trivial lower and upper bounds for the number of Jordan-Pólya numbers not exceeding a given number x x .

1
Introduction

A positive integer
n
is said to be a
Jordan-Pólya number
if it can be written as a product of factorials.
Jordan-Pólya numbers arise naturally in a simple combinatorial problem. Given
k
groups of
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}
distinct objects, then the number of distinct permutations of these
n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{k}
objects which maintain objects of the same group adjacent is equal to
k!\cdot n_{1}!\cdot n_{2}!\cdots n_{k}!
, a Jordan-Pólya number.

The Jordan-Pólya numbers below 10,000 are

1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 36, 48, 64, 72, 96, 120, 128, 144,
192, 216, 240, 256, 288, 384, 432, 480, 512, 576, 720, 768, 864, 960,
1024, 1152, 1296, 1440, 1536, 1728, 1920, 2048, 2304, 2592, 2880,
3072, 3456, 3840, 4096, 4320, 4608, 5040, 5184, 5760, 6144, 6912,
7680, 7776, 8192, 8640, 9216.

For a longer list, see the
On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
, Sequence A001013.
Much study has been done on a particular subset of the Jordan-Pólya numbers, namely those which are themselves factorials. In particular, consider the equation

n!=a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{r}!\quad\mbox{ in integers }n>a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{r}\geq 2,\ r\geq 2.

(1.1)

This equation has infinitely many “trivial” solutions. Indeed, choose any integers
a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{r}\geq 2
and set
n=a_{2}!\cdots a_{r}!
. Then, choose
a_{1}=n-1
. One can easily see that
n!=n\cdot(n-1)!=a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{r}!
. Besides these trivial solutions of equation (
1.1
), we find the non-trivial solutions

9!=2!\cdot 3!^{2}\cdot 7!,\qquad 10!=6!\cdot 7!=3!\cdot 5!\cdot 7!,\qquad 16!=2!\cdot 5!\cdot 14!.

(1.2)

According to Hickerson’s conjecture, there are no other non-trivial solutions for equation (
1.1
).
In 2007, Luca
[
8
]
showed that if the
abc
conjecture holds, then equation (
1.1
) has only a finite number of non-trivial solutions.
In 2016, Nair and Shorey
[
9
]
showed that any other non-trivial solution
n
of (
1.1
), besides those in (
1.2
),
must satisfy
n>e^{80}
.

On the other hand, more than 40 years ago, Erdős and Graham
[
5
]
showed that the number of distinct integers of the form
a_{1}!a_{2}!\cdots a_{r}!
, where
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{r}\leq y
is
\exp\{(1+o(1))y(\log\log y)/\log y\}
as
y\to\infty
.

Here, letting
{\cal J}
stand for the set of Jordan-Pólya numbers and
{\cal J}(x)
for its counting function, we show that
{\cal J}(x)=o(x)
and in fact, given any small
\varepsilon>0
, we show the much stronger estimate

\mathcal{J}(x)<\exp\left\{(4+\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\log x}\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}\right\}\qquad(x\geq x_{1})

(1.3)

for some
x_{1}=x_{1}(\varepsilon)>0
.
We also show that, for any given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
x_{2}=x_{2}(\varepsilon)
such that

{\cal J}(x)>\exp\left\{(2-\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right\}\qquad(x\geq x_{2}).

(1.4)

2
Preliminary results

We first mention some known results in the form of lemmas and propositions that will prove useful in establishing the lower and upper bounds for
{\cal J}(x)
.

We start with a weak form of Stirling’s formula for the factorial function, a proof of which can be found on page 11 in the book of De Koninck and Luca
[
2
]
.

Lemma 1
.

For each integer
m\geq 1
, we have

m!>\left(\frac{m}{e}\right)^{m}.

We now state a more precise form of Stirling’s formula, which is a particular case of formula (4) in a 2009 paper of De Angelis
[
1
]
.

Lemma 2
.

For all integers
n\geq 2
,

n!=\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(1+\frac{1}{12n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right).

Lemma 3
.

Given any positive integers
a
and
b
,

{a+b\choose a}\leq\left(\frac{e(a+b)}{a}\right)^{a}.

Proof.

This follows from the following string of inequalities.

{a+b\choose a}=\frac{b+1}{1}\times\frac{b+2}{2}\times\cdots\times\frac{b+a}{a}\leq\frac{(b+a)^{a}}{a!}\leq\left(\frac{e(a+b)}{a}\right)^{a},

where we used Lemma
1
for this last inequality.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Given positive integers
k\leq R
, the number
S_{k}(R)
of solutions
(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k})
in non-negative integers
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}
of the inequality

r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots+r_{k}\leq R

satisfies
\displaystyle{S_{k}(R)={R+k\choose R}}
.

Proof.

It follows from formula (5.2) in the book of W. Feller
[
6
]
that the number of ways of writing a positive integer
m
as a sum of
k
non-negative integers is equal to
{m+k-1\choose k-1}
. Therefore, since
S_{k}(R)
is the sum of this last expression as
m
varies from 0 to
R
, we find, using induction, that

S_{k}(R)=\sum_{m=0}^{R}{m+k-1\choose k-1}={R+k\choose k}={R+k\choose R}.

∎

The next result, which is of independent interest, is a key element in the proof of the upper bound for
{\cal J}(x)
. Essentially, it says that the sequence of the exponents in the prime factorisation of
m!
decreases faster than the sequence of the primes to which they are attached increases.

Lemma 5
.

Let the prime factorisation of
m!
be written as

m!=2^{\alpha_{2}}\cdot 3^{\alpha_{3}}\cdot 5^{\alpha_{5}}\cdots p_{t}^{\alpha_{p_{t}}},

where
p_{t}
is the largest prime number not exceeding
m
. Then, given any primes
p,q
such that
p<q\leq p_{t}
, we have

\frac{\alpha_{p}}{\alpha_{q}}\geq\left\lfloor\frac{q}{p}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Let
p<q\leq p_{t}
be fixed. Then, there exist two positive integers
u\geq v
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p^{u}}\right\rfloor,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\alpha_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{m}{q}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{m}{q^{2}}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor\frac{m}{q^{v}}\right\rfloor.

(2.2)

Let
k
be the unique positive integer satisfying
kp<q<(k+1)p
. Clearly, our claim will be proved if we can show that

\alpha_{p}\geq k\,\alpha_{q}.

(2.3)

Now, if we can show that

\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p}\right\rfloor\geq k\left\lfloor\frac{m}{q}\right\rfloor,

(2.4)

then surely we will have

\displaystyle{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p^{i}}\right\rfloor\geq k\left\lfloor\frac{m}{q^{i}}\right\rfloor}
for each
i=2,3,\ldots,u
and therefore, in light of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), inequality (
2.3
) will follow.
This means that we only need to prove (
2.4
). Now, there exist two positive integers
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
such that

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{1}p+\theta_{1}\ \mbox{ for some non-negative integer }\theta_{1}\leq p-1,

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{2}q+\theta_{2}\ \mbox{ for some non-negative integer }\theta_{2}\leq q-1,

and therefore,

1=\frac{r_{1}p+\theta_{1}}{r_{2}q+\theta_{2}}\leq\frac{r_{1}p+p-1}{r_{2}q}<\frac{r_{1}p+p-1}{r_{2}\cdot kp},

thereby establishing that

r_{1}p+p-1>kr_{2}p,

so that

r_{1}+\frac{p-1}{p}>kr_{2}.

(2.5)

Since
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are two integers whereas
\displaystyle{\frac{p-1}{p}}
is a positive number smaller than 1, it follows from (
2.5
) that
r_{1}\geq kr_{2}
, which proves (
2.4
) since
\displaystyle{r_{1}=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p}\right\rfloor}

and
\displaystyle{r_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{q}\right\rfloor}
.
∎

The following result provides very useful explicit upper and lower bounds for the
k
-th prime number.

Lemma 6
.

If
p_{k}
stands for the
k
-th prime number, then

p_{k}<k\log k+k\log\log k\qquad(k\geq 6)

(2.6)

and

p_{k}>k\log k\qquad(k\geq 1).

(2.7)

Proof.

The first inequality is due to Rosser
[
10
]
, whereas the second is due to Rosser and Schoenfeld
[
11
]
.
∎

The prime number theorem can be written in various forms. We will be using the following, which is essentially Theorem 5.1 in the book of Ellison and Ellison
[
3
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Setting
\theta(x):=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p
, there exists an absolute constant
a>0
such that

\theta(x)=x\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{e^{a\sqrt{\log x}}}\right)\right).

Let
\Psi(x,y):=\#\{n\leq x:P(n)\leq y\}
, where
P(n)
stands for the largest prime factor of
n\geq 2
, with
P(1)=1
. Moreover, let
\pi(x)
stand for the number of prime numbers not exceeding
x
. The following estimate can be found in Granville
[
7
]
.

Proposition 2
.

If
y=o(\log x)
as
x\to\infty
, then

\Psi(x,y)=\left(\frac{\log x}{y}\right)^{(1+o(1))\pi(y)}.

The following is a 1969 result of Ennola
[
4
]
, a proof of which is given in the book of Tenenbaum
[
12
]
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of positive real numbers and set

N_{k}(z):=\#\left\{(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{k})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}:\nu_{1}\geq 0,\ldots,\nu_{k}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}a_{i}\leq z\right\}.

Then, for each positive integer
k
,

\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{a_{i}}<N_{k}(z)\leq\frac{(z+\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i})^{k}}{k!}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{a_{i}}.

(2.8)

3
The proof of the upper bound

Observe that for every integer
n
counted by
{\cal J}(x)
, each of its prime factors must be smaller than
\displaystyle{2\frac{\log x}{\log\log x}}
, provided
x
is sufficiently large. We now define the four integers
r,r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
each depending on
x
as follows.

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left(2\frac{\log x}{\log\log x}\right),\mbox{ so that }\ r\leq 3\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^{2}},

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right),\mbox{ which is asymptotic to }\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\mbox{ as }x\to\infty,

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left(\sqrt{\log x}\right),\mbox{ which is asymptotic to }\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\mbox{ as }x\to\infty,

\displaystyle r_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\left(\sqrt{\log x}\log\log x\right),\mbox{ which is asymptotic to }2\sqrt{\log x}\mbox{ as }x\to\infty.

Let
m
be a positive integer and
q_{1},q_{2}
be two prime numbers such that
q_{1}<q_{2}\leq m
. Assuming that
q_{1}^{\eta_{1}}\|m!
and that
q_{2}^{\eta_{2}}\|m!
, it follows from Lemma
5
that

\frac{\eta_{1}}{\eta_{2}}\geq\left\lfloor\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}}\right\rfloor.

Using these observations, we may write that
\mathcal{J}(x)\leq\#\mathcal{A}(x)
, where

\mathcal{A}(x):=\left\{a=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}:\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}\log p_{j}\leq\log x,\frac{a_{i}}{a_{j}}\geq\left\lfloor\frac{p_{j}}{p_{i}}\right\rfloor\right\}.

In order to derive an upper bound for
\#\mathcal{A}(x)
, we introduce the four sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r_{1}}):\exists a\in\mathcal{A}(x),a_{1}=b_{1},\ldots,a_{r_{1}}=b_{r_{1}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r_{2}-r_{1}}):\exists a\in\mathcal{A}(x),a_{r_{1}+1}=b_{1},\ldots,a_{r_{2}}=b_{r_{2}-r_{1}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r_{3}-r_{2}}):\exists a\in\mathcal{A}(x),a_{r_{2}+1}=b_{1},\ldots,a_{r_{3}}=b_{r_{3}-r_{2}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{4}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r-r_{3}}):\exists a\in\mathcal{A}(x),a_{r_{3}+1}=b_{1},\ldots,a_{r}=b_{r-r_{3}}\}.

It is then clear that

\#\mathcal{A}(x)\leq\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\times\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\times\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\times\#\mathcal{B}_{4}(x).

We will now provide upper bounds for each of the quantities
\#\mathcal{B}_{j}(x)
for
1\leq j\leq 4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrarily small number and let
x
be a large number.

First observe that

\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\leq\#\left\{(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{r_{1}}):\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}b_{j}\log p_{j}\leq\log x\right\}.

From this, it follows from Proposition
2
that

\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\leq\Psi\left(x,p_{r_{1}}\right)=\left(\frac{\log x}{p_{r_{1}}}\right)^{(1+o(1))r_{1}}\leq(\sqrt{\log x}\log\log x)^{3\sqrt{\log x}/(\log\log x)^{2}},

so that

\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\leq\exp\left(2\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right).

(3.1)

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\#\left\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r_{2}-r_{1}}):\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}-r_{1}}b_{j}\log p_{j+r_{1}}\right)+\left(b_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{1}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\right)\leq\log x\right\}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\#\left\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r_{2}-r_{1}}):b_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{1}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\leq\log x\right\},

where we used the fact guaranteed by Lemma
5
that
\displaystyle{\frac{a_{j}}{b_{1}}\geq\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{1}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor}
for
1\leq j\leq r_{1}
. We then perform a change of variable, namely the one given implicitly by

b_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{r_{2}-r_{1}}c_{j},\quad 1\leq k\leq r_{2}-r_{1}.

Given that the sequence
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{r_{2}-r_{1}}
is non-increasing, we have
c_{j}\geq 0,\,1\leq j\leq r_{2}-r_{1}
. From (
3.2
), it follows that

\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\leq\#\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r_{2}-r_{1}}):\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}-r_{1}}c_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{1}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\right)\leq\log x\right\}.

(3.3)

Now, it follows from the prime number theorem that

\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\log p}{p}>(1-\varepsilon)\log y,

(3.4)

provided
y
is sufficiently large.

Using inequality (
2.7
) of Lemma
6
, as well as inequality (
3.4
) and Proposition
1
, we may write that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{1}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p_{r_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\frac{\log p_{j}}{p_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\log p_{j}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\frac{\log p_{j}}{p_{j}}-\theta(p_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}(1-\varepsilon)\log p_{r_{1}}-(1+\varepsilon)p_{r_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\log x}.

Using this in (
3.3
), we get

\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\leq\#\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r_{2}-r_{1}}):\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}-r_{1}}c_{j}\leq 3\sqrt{\log x}\right\},

which, in light of Lemma
4
, yields

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{r_{2}-r_{1}+\lceil 3\sqrt{\log x}\rceil\choose\lceil 3\sqrt{\log x}\rceil}\leq\binom{\lceil\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil+\lceil 3\sqrt{\log x}\rceil}{\lceil 3\sqrt{\log x}\rceil}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{\lceil\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil+\lceil 3\sqrt{\log x}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil}\leq\binom{2\lceil 3\sqrt{\log x}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil},

where we used the fact that for any positive integers
a
and
b
, we have
\displaystyle{{a+b\choose b}={a+b\choose a}}
.
Using Lemma
3
, it then follows that

\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\leq\exp\left\{\frac{\sqrt{\log x}\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}\right\}.

(3.6)

An upper bound for
\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)
is obtained using a similar technique. We have

\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\leq\#\left\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r_{3}-r_{2}}):\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{3}-r_{2}}b_{j}\log p_{j+r_{2}}\right)+\left(b_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{2}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\right)\leq\log x\right\}.

(3.7)

Performing the change of variable

b_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{r_{3}-r_{2}}c_{j},\quad 1\leq k\leq r_{3}-r_{2},

we obtain from (
3.7
) that

\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\leq\#\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r_{3}-r_{2}}):\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{3}-r_{2}}c_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{2}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\right)\leq\log x\right\}.

(3.8)

Again using (
2.7
), (
3.4
) and Proposition
1
, while proceeding as we did to obtain (
3.5
), we find that

\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{2}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\geq p_{r_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}\frac{\log p_{j}}{p_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}\log p_{j}\geq\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\log x}\log\log x.

Using this in (
3.8
), we obtain

\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\leq\#\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r_{3}-r_{2}}):\sum_{j=1}^{r_{3}-r_{2}}c_{j}\leq 3\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right\},

from which we can deduce that

\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\leq\binom{\lceil\frac{3\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil+r_{3}}{\lceil 3\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil}\leq\binom{\lceil\frac{3\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil+\lceil 2\sqrt{\log x}\rceil}{\lceil 3\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\rceil}.

(3.9)

Again using Lemma
3
, we conclude from (
3.9
) that

\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\leq\exp\left\{(3+\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\log x}\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}\right\}.

(3.10)

We finally provide an upper bound for
\#\mathcal{B}_{4}(x)
again using the same approach. We have

\#\mathcal{B}_{4}(x)\leq\#\left\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r-r_{3}}):\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r-r_{3}}b_{j}\log p_{j+r_{3}}\right)+\left(b_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{3}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{3}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\right)\leq\log x\right\}.

Proceeding as before, we get

\sum_{j=1}^{r_{3}}\left\lfloor\frac{p_{r_{3}}}{p_{j}}\right\rfloor\log p_{j}\geq\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\log x}\,(\log\log x)^{2},

which yields

\#\mathcal{B}_{4}(x)\leq\#\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}):\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{j}\leq 3\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\right\},

from which we conclude

\#\mathcal{B}_{4}(x)\leq\binom{\lceil\frac{3\sqrt{\log x}}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\rceil+r}{\lceil 3\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\rceil}\leq\binom{\lceil\frac{3\sqrt{\log x}}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\rceil+\lceil 3\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\rceil}{\lceil 3\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{(\log\log x)^{2}}\rceil},

so that

\#\mathcal{B}_{4}\leq\exp\left\{(3+\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right\}.

(3.11)

Gathering estimates (
3.1
), (
3.6
), (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) completes the proof of the upper bound (
1.3
).

4
The proof of the lower bound

Many
elements of
{\cal J}
have two or more representations as a product of factorials. For instance, the number
24=4!=2!^{2}\cdot 3!
has two, whereas
576=4!^{2}=2!^{4}\cdot 3!^{2}=2!^{2}\cdot 3!\cdot 4!
has three. In fact, one can easily show that given an arbitrary integer
k\geq 2
, there exists a Jordan-Pólya number which has
k
representations as the product of factorials. For instance, take the numbers
n_{k}:=2^{3k+3}3^{k+1}
(
k=1,2,\ldots
). One can easily check that

\displaystyle n_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4!\cdot 3!^{k}\cdot 2!^{2k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4!^{2}\cdot 3!^{k-1}\cdot 2!^{2(k-1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4!^{k-1}\cdot 3!^{2}\cdot 2!^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4!^{k}\cdot 3!\cdot 2!^{2},

thereby revealing
k
distinct representations of
n_{k}
as a product of factorials.

This phenomenon must be taken into account when establishing a lower bound for
{\cal J}(x)
. This is why we will consider a subset of
{\cal J}
whose elements have a unique representation as a product of “prime factorials”. We choose
{\cal J}_{\wp}
as the set of those elements
n\in{\cal J}
which can be written as a product of prime factorials, that is, as
n=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}!^{\alpha_{i}}
for some non negative integers
\alpha_{i}
’s, where
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots
stands for the sequence of primes. The interesting feature of this set is that one can easily show that each of its elements has a unique representation as a product of prime factorials. Observe that
{\cal J}\setminus{\cal J}_{\wp}\neq\emptyset
since it contains the number
n=14!
and in fact many more.

We will establish a lower bound for
{\cal J}_{\wp}(x)
, which will ipso facto provide a lower bound for
{\cal J}(x)
.
Given a large number
x
, let
z=\log x
and set
a_{i}=\log(p_{i}!)
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Then, applying the first inequality in relation (
2.8
) of Proposition
3
, we get that, for each positive integer
k
,

{\cal J}_{\wp}(x)>\frac{\log^{k}x}{k!\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\log(p_{i}!)}.

(4.1)

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
k_{0}
be a large integer.
Using Lemma
2
,
we may write that for each large prime
p_{i}
, say with
i\geq k_{0}
,

\log(p_{i}!)=p_{i}\log p_{i}-p_{i}+O(\log p_{i})=p_{i}\log p_{i}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p}_{i}\right)\right),

so that, for each
k>k_{0}
, we have

\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\log(p_{i}!)=\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}p_{i}\cdot\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\log p_{i}\cdot\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)\right).

(4.2)

We will now overestimate each of the above three products.

Using Proposition
1
, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}p_{i}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\sum_{p\leq p_{k}}\log p\right\}=\exp\left\{\theta(p_{k})\right\}=\exp\left\{p_{k}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}k}\right)\right)\right\}

(4.3)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\exp\left\{(k\log k+k\log\log k)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}k}\right)\right)\right\},

where we used inequality (
2.6
) of Lemma
6
.

On the other hand, using once more the first inequality in Lemma
6
, we easily observe that

\log\log p_{i}<(1+\varepsilon)\log\log i
provided
i
is sufficiently large.
It follows that

\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\log p_{i}=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\log\log p_{i}\right\}<\exp\left\{\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}(1+\varepsilon)\log\log k\right\}<\exp\{(1+\varepsilon)k\log\log k\}.

(4.4)

Finally,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)\right)\right\}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}+O\left(\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{1}{\log^{2}p_{i}}\right)\right\}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{p_{k_{0}}}^{p_{k}}\frac{1}{\log t}d\,\pi(t)=\left.\frac{\pi(t)}{\log t}\right|_{p_{k_{0}}}^{p_{k}}+\int_{p_{k_{0}}}^{p_{k}}\frac{\pi(t)}{t\log^{2}t}\,dt

(4.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{\log p_{k}}+O\left(\frac{k}{\log^{2}p_{k}}\right)>\frac{k}{\log k}+O\left(\frac{k\log\log k}{\log^{2}k}\right),

using estimate (
4.6
) in (
4.5
), we find that

\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log p_{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)\right)<\exp\left\{-\frac{k}{\log k}+O\left(\frac{k\log\log k}{\log^{2}k}\right)\right\}.

(4.7)

Setting
\displaystyle{C_{0}=\prod_{i=1}^{k_{0}-1}\log(p_{i}!)}
and gathering inequalities (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and (
4.7
) in (
4.2
), we find that

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\log(p_{i}!)=\prod_{i=1}^{k_{0}-1}\log(p_{i}!)\cdot\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\log(p_{i}!)<C_{0}(k\log^{2}k)^{k}\ e^{-k/\log k}.

(4.8)

Finally, using Lemma
2
, we have that, provided
k_{0}
is large enough,

k!<(1+\varepsilon)k^{k}\,e^{-k}\,\sqrt{2\pi k}\qquad(k\geq k_{0}).

(4.9)

Combining (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) in (
4.1
), we obtain that

{\cal J}_{\wp}(x)>\frac{1}{C_{0}}\left(\frac{e^{1+1/\log k}\log x}{(1+\varepsilon)k^{2}\log^{2}k}\right)^{k}\qquad(k\geq k_{0}).

(4.10)

Our goal will be to search for an integer
k=k(x)
for which the function

f(k):=\left(\frac{e^{1+1/\log k}\log x}{(1+\varepsilon)k^{2}\log^{2}k}\right)^{k}

reaches its maximum value, or equivalently for which real number
s
the function
g(s):=\log f(s)
reaches its maximum value.
Since

g(s)=s\left(1+\frac{1}{\log s}+\log\log x-2\log s-2\log\log s\right),

we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{\log s}+\log\log x-2\log s-2\log\log s+s\left(-\frac{1}{s\log^{2}s}-\frac{2}{s}-\frac{2}{s\log s}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1+\log\log x-2\log s-2\log\log s-\frac{2}{\log s}.

For large
x
and large
s
, the right hand side of the above expression will be near 0 when

\log\log x-2\log s-2\log\log s=0,

or similarly,

\log(s^{2}\log^{2}s)=\log\log x
and therefore
s\log s=\sqrt{\log x}
, from which we find that

s=\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log s}=(1+o(1))\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\qquad(x\to\infty).

Substituting this value of
s
in (
4.10
), we find that

{\cal J}_{\wp}(x)>\exp\left\{(1+o(1))\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right\}\qquad(x\to\infty),

thus establishing the required lower bound (
1.4
).
