Abstract
. Let A = ( a 1 , a 2 , … , a n ) A=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}) be a sequence of relative prime integers, each greater than or equal to 2. The Frobenius number g ⁡ ( A ) g(A) represents the largest integer that cannot be expressed as a nonnegative linear combination of the elements in A A . Our recent work has introduced a combinatorial method for calculating g ⁡ ( A ) g(A) in the specific form A = ( a , a + B ) = ( a , a + b 1 , … , a + b k ) A=(a,a+B)=(a,a+b_{1},\ldots,a+b_{k}) . This method reduces the problem to a simpler optimization problem, denoted as O B ​ ( M ) O_{B}(M) . In this paper, we apply this method to solve the open problem of characterizing g ⁡ ( A ) g(A) for the square sequence A = ( a , a + 1 , a + 2 2 , … , a + k 2 ) A=(a,a+1,a+2^{2},\ldots,a+k^{2}) . We demonstrate that the Frobenius number g ⁡ ( a , a + 1 2 , a + 2 2 , … ) g(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},\ldots) for the infinite square sequence admits a simpler solution. This is because the corresponding optimization problem, O B ​ ( M ) O_{B}(M) , can be solved by applying number-theoretic principles, specifically Lagrange’s Four-Square Theorem and related results. Consequently, we are able to resolve the open problem by utilizing Lagrange’s Four-Square Theorem in conjunction with generating functions.

1.
Introduction

Let
A=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
be a sequence of relative prime integers, each greater than or equal to 2. The Frobenius number
g(A)
is the largest integer that cannot be expressed as a nonnegative linear combination of the elements in
A
. The determination of
g(A)
has been the subject of extensive research. For an exhaustive treatment of the subject, see
[
13
]
. When
n=2
, Sylvester
[
19
,
20
]
derived the formula
g(a_{1},a_{2})=a_{1}a_{2}-a_{1}-a_{2}
in 1882. For
n=3
, a formula involving rational functions was introduced by Denham
[
3
]
, and further investigated by Tripathi
[
22
]
. However, for
n\geq 4
, a general formula for
g(A)
remains elusive, although numerous special cases have been resolved (cf.
[
8
,
1
,
15
,
18
,
21
,
4
]
). Computational approaches to the Frobenius number have been explored by Kannan
[
9
]
and Ramrez Alfonsn
[
12
]
.

Our main objective is to address an open problem posed by Einstein, Lichtblau, Strzebonski, and Wagon
[
5
]
. This problem concerns the characterization of
g(A)
for the square sequence
A=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},\ldots,a+k^{2})
. In 2007, these authors analyzed the cases
k\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}
using geometric algorithms and conjectured that the Frobenius number for this sequence admits a formula of the form
\frac{1}{k^{2}}(a^{2}+ca)-d
, where
c
and
d
are integers dependent on
k
and the residue class of
a
modulo
k^{2}
.

We validate this conjecture by employing the combinatorial method introduced by Liu-Xin
[
10
]
, drawing upon Lagrange’s “Four-Square Theorem” from Number Theory.

To this end, we need to introduce some essential notation and results from
[
10
]
. Throughout this paper, we denote the sets of all integers, non-negative integers, and positive integers by
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathbb{P}
, respectively.

A key result due to Brauer and Shockley, which is widely used, is as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

Consider
A:=(a,B)=(a,b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})
with
\gcd(A)=1
,
d\in\mathbb{P}
, and
\gcd(a,d)=1
. Let
\mathcal{R}
represent the set
\left\{ax+\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}x_{i}\ \mid x,x_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
and
N_{r}:=N_{r}(a,B)=\min\{a_{0}\mid a_{0}\equiv r\mod a,\ a_{0}\in\mathcal{R}\}
. Then the Frobenius number is given by

g(A)=g(a,B)=\max_{r\in\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}}\{N_{r}\}-a=\max_{r\in\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}}\{N_{dr}\}-a.

Let
A=(a,ha+dB)=(a,ha+db_{1},\ldots,ha+db_{k})
. The computation of
N_{dr}
is reduced to a minimization problem defined by

O_{B}(M):=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}x_{i}=M,\ x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

Lemma 1.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
A=(a,ha+db_{1},\ldots,ha+db_{k})
,
k,h,d,b_{i}\in\mathbb{P}
and
\gcd(A)=1
. For a given
0\leq r\leq a-1
, we have

N_{dr}=\min\{N_{dr}(m)\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\},\qquad\text{where }N_{dr}(m):=\{O_{B}(ma+r)\cdot ha+(ma+r)d\}.

(1)

The lemma holds when
k
tends to infinity. See Lemma
2.7
for instance.

The Four-Square Theorem enters our analysis when we consider the infinite square sequence, i.e.,
g(A^{\prime})
, where

A^{\prime}=(a,a+B^{\prime})=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},\ldots)
.
The optimization problem
O_{B^{\prime}}(M)
is ideally solved by Number Theorists, in terms of the Four-Square Theorem and related results.
Consequently, we are able to determine
g(A^{\prime})
.
Similarly, we are able to determine
g(A^{\prime})
for the prime sequence
A^{\prime}=(a,a+1,a+p_{1},a+p_{2},\ldots)
, where
(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
is the prime sequence
(2,3,5,7,\ldots)
.

The finite case
A=(a,a+1,a+2^{2},\ldots,a+k^{2})
presents significant challenges. The corresponding optimization problem
O_{B}(M)

is difficult to solve. We establish several bounds using the Four-Square Theorem, establish certain stable properties by using generating functions, and finally prove the conjecture about
g(A)
.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Sections 2 and 3, we investigate the Frobenius number of two distinct infinite sequences, specifically
A^{\prime}=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},\ldots)
and
A^{\prime}=(a,a+1,a+p_{1},a+p_{2},\ldots)
. For these sequences, the corresponding optimization problems
O_{B^{\prime}}(M)
admit explicit solutions within the domain of Number Theory. This enables us to ascertain the values of
g(A^{\prime})
for both sequences.
In Section 4, we resolve the conjecture put forth by Einstein et al. regarding
g(a,a+1,a+2^{2},\ldots,a+k^{2})
.
Section 5 concludes the paper with a concluding remark.

2.
Infinite Square Sequence Formula

The Frobenius formula for square sequences remains an open problem.

Open problem 2.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
A=(a,a+1,a+4,\ldots,a+k^{2})
, where
k\in\mathbb{P}
and
a>2
. How can we characterize
g(A)
?

Einstein et al.
[
5
]
conjectured that the Frobenius number for this sequence is given by
\frac{1}{k^{2}}(a^{2}+ca)-d
for some integers
c,d
, which depend on
k
and the residue class of
a
modulo
k^{2}
. Using a geometric algorithm, they demonstrated the validity of this formula for
k=(1,2,3,4,5,6,7)
and corresponding
a\geq(1,1,16,24,41,67,136)
.

In this section, we begin by discussing a variation of this problem, which represents a relatively simple case, before addressing the open problem in Section
4
. We will primarily focus on the Frobenius number
g(A)
for the case when
k
approaches infinity, i.e., when
A=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},a+3^{2},\ldots)
. It is true that larger values of
k
that satisfy
a+k^{2}\geq g(a,a+1)
can be neglected, but including them simplifies the analysis.

To calculate
g(A)
, we need the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{P}
, we use the symbol
\iota(n)
to denote

\iota(n)=\min\{s\mid n=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+\cdots+a_{s}^{2},\ a_{i}\in\mathbb{P},1\leq i\leq s\}.

That is,
\iota(n)
is the minimum number of squares required to express
n
as a sum of squares.

The characterization of
\iota(n)
is known in Number Theory, as we shall soon introduce. Firstly, the Four-Square Theorem provides an upper bound for
\iota(n)
.

Lemma 2.3

(Lagrange theorem
[
6
]
)
.

Every positive integer is the sum of four squares.

To determine the precise value of
\iota(n)
, additional results and notations are required. For
n\in\mathbb{P}
, the
Standard Form
of
n
is the unique factorization of
n
into primes:
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, where
p_{i}>1
are distinct primes,
e_{i}>0
, for
1\leq i\leq k
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
6
]
)
.

A number
n\in\mathbb{P}
is the sum of two squares if and only if, in the Standard Form of
n
, all prime factors of the form
4m+3
(where
m\in\mathbb{N}
) have even exponents.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
6
]
)
.

A number
n\in\mathbb{P}
is the sum of three squares if and only if
n\neq 4^{r}(8t+7)
, where
r,t\in\mathbb{N}
.

With these lemmas in place, we can now determine
\iota(n)
as follows.

Theorem 2.6
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{P}
, if the Standard Form of
n
is
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, then
n
falls into one of the following four categories:

1) For every
1\leq i\leq k
,
e_{i}
is even, indicating that
n
is a perfect square.

2) There exists a
1\leq j\leq k
such that
e_{j}
is odd, and for all
1\leq i\leq k
with
p_{i}\equiv 3\mod 4
,
e_{i}
is even.

3)
n
is of the form
4^{r}(8t+7)
, where
r,t\in\mathbb{N}
.

4) Otherwise.

Accordingly, we have:

\begin{aligned} \iota(n)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1\ \ &\text{if}&n\in type1)\\
2\ \ &\text{if}&n\in type2)\\
4\ \ &\text{if}&n\in type3)\\
3\ \ &\text{if}&n\in type4)\\
\end{aligned}\right.\end{aligned}.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem is straightforward, as it follows directly from Lemmas
2.3
,
2.4
, and
2.5
.
∎

To characterize
g(A)
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},a+3^{2},\ldots)
. For
1\leq r\leq a-1
, we have

N_{r}=\min\{\iota(ma+r)\cdot a+ma+r\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Proof.

We appropriately generalize the definition of
N_{r}
. We have

\displaystyle N_{r}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}(a+i^{2})\mid\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}(a+i^{2})\equiv r\mod a,\ x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},i\geq 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\right)\cdot a+ma+r\mid\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\cdot i^{2}=ma+r,\ m,x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},i\geq 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\iota(ma+r)\cdot a+ma+r\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

(Note: there are only a finite number of
x_{i}
that are not 0.)
∎

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
A=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},a+3^{2},\ldots)
. For
1\leq r\leq a-1
, if there exists an
r
such that
\iota(r)=4,\ \iota(a+r)\geq 3,\ \iota(2a+r)\geq 2
, then

g(A)=3a+\max\{r\mid\iota(r)=4,\ \iota(a+r)\geq 3,\ \iota(2a+r)\geq 2\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.7
and the definition
g(A)=\max\{N_{r}\}-a
, this result follows trivially.
∎

If the above theorem does not apply, then we have the following result.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
A=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},a+3^{2},\ldots)
. For
1\leq r\leq a-1
, if the condition in Theorem
2.8
is not satisfied and at least one of the following three conditions is met:

1) There exists an
r
such that
\iota(r)=4
and
\iota(a+r)=2
;

2) There exists an
r
such that
\iota(r)=4
,
\iota(a+r)\geq 3
, and
\iota(2a+r)=1
;

3) There exists an
r
such that
\iota(r)=3
and
\iota(a+r)\geq 2
.

Then

g(A)=2a+\max\{r\mid\text{the r in conditions 1), 2), or 3)}\}.

In order to gain a clearer understanding of the above theorem, we provide Table
1
in the appendix.

Conjecture 2.10
.

Let
A=(a,a+1^{2},a+2^{2},a+3^{2},\ldots,)
. If
a>30
, then Theorem
2.8
always applies to give

g(A)=3a+\max\{r\mid\iota(r)=4,\ \iota(a+r)\geq 3,\ \iota(2a+r)\geq 2\}.

By examining Tables
1
in the appendix, we intuitively find that Conjecture
2.10
is likely to be correct. Theorem
2.9
appears to be valid only for
a=4,5,7,9,10,11,13,19,21,22,30
. On the other hand, the first numbers in the sequence
4^{r}(8t+7)
for
(r,t\in\mathbb{N})
are
7,15,23,28,31,39,47,\ldots
. To refute Conjecture
2.10
, we would need to find a number
a>30
such that
\iota(a+4^{r}(8t+7))\leq 2
or
\iota(2a+4^{r}(8t+7))=1
holds for any
4^{r}(8t+7)<a
. We guess that it is impossible.

3.
Infinite Prime Sequence Formula

In this section, we investigate the Frobenius formula for the infinite prime sequence. Let
p_{k}
denote the
k
-th prime number. It is a well-established fact that
p_{k}\geq k\log(k)
for
k=2,3,\dots
(
[
16
,
17
]
).
Consider the sequence
A=(a,a+p_{0},a+p_{1},\ldots,a+p_{m},\ldots)
, where
a\in\mathbb{P}
,
a>2
,
p_{0}=1
, and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},\ldots
represent the prime sequence, i.e.,
2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19,\ldots
. The question arises: How can we characterize the formula
g(A)
?

Conjecture 3.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

Every even-counting number greater than 2 is equal to the sum of two prime numbers.

This conjecture is also known as the
strong Goldbach conjecture
.
A similar result, known as the
weak Goldbach conjecture
, has been fully established by Helfgott.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
7
]
)
.

Every odd counting number greater than 7 is equal to the sum of three odd prime numbers.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{PN}
denote the set of all primes, i.e.,
\mathcal{PN}=\{2,3,5,7,11,\ldots\}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{P}
, we use the symbol
\tau(n)
to denote

\tau(n)=\min\{s\mid n=p_{1}+p_{2}+\cdots+p_{s},\ p_{i}\in\mathcal{PN}\cup\{1\},1\leq i\leq s\}.

It is evident that
\tau(n)\leq 3
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assuming the validity of the strong Goldbach conjecture (i.e., Conjecture
3.1
), then for any
n\in\mathbb{P}
, we have

\begin{aligned} \tau(n)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1\ \ \ \ &\text{if}\ \ n\in\mathcal{PN}\cup\{1\}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \\
3\ \ \ \ &\text{if}\ \ n\ \text{is odd, and }n,n-2\not\in\mathcal{PN}\cup\{1\}\\
2\ \ \ \ &\text{otherwise}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \\
\end{aligned}\right.\end{aligned}.

Proof.

Given the assumption that Conjecture
3.1
is true, the proof of this theorem follows directly from Proposition
3.2
and Definition
3.3
.
∎

Similarly, for the sequence
A=(a,a+p_{0},a+p_{1},\ldots,a+p_{m},\ldots)
, we have

\displaystyle N_{r}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}(a+p_{i})\mid\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}(a+p_{i})\equiv r\mod a,\ x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},i\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}\right)\cdot a+ma+r\mid\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}\cdot p_{i}=ma+r,\ m,x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},i\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\tau(ma+r)\cdot a+ma+r\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

It is noted that there are only a finite number of
x_{i}
that are not zero. Consequently,
g(A)=\max\{N_{r}\}-a
. As in the case of the square sequence, we can derive the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
A=(a,a+p_{0},a+p_{1},\ldots,a+p_{m},\ldots)
, where
a\in\mathbb{P}
,
a>2
,
p_{0}=1
, and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},\ldots
form the prime sequence. For
1\leq r\leq a-1
, if there exists an
r
such that
\tau(r)=3
and
\tau(a+r)\geq 2
, then

g(A)=2a+\max\{r\mid\tau(r)=3,\ \tau(a+r)\geq 2\}.

Similarly, it follows readily that

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
A=(a,a+p_{0},a+p_{1},\ldots,a+p_{m},\ldots)
, where
a\in\mathbb{P}
,
a>2
,
p_{0}=1
, and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},\ldots
constitute the prime sequence. For
1\leq r\leq a-1
, if the condition in Theorem
3.5
is not met and at least one of the following two conditions holds:

1) There exists an
r
such that
\tau(r)=3
and
\tau(a+r)=1
;

2) There exists an
r
such that
\tau(r)=2
.

Then

g(A)=a+\max\{r\mid\text{the r in conditions 1) or 2)}\}.

To better understand the above theorem, we provide Table
2
in the appendix, which suggests the following result.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
A=(a,a+p_{0},a+p_{1},\ldots,a+p_{m},\ldots)
, where
a\in\mathbb{P}
,
a>2
,
p_{0}=1
, and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},\ldots
form the prime sequence. If
a>44
, then the formula
g(A)
in Theorem
3.5
is the formula for the Frobenius sequence
A
, i.e., for any
1\leq r\leq a-1
,

g(A)=2a+\max\{r\mid\tau(r)=3,\ \tau(a+r)\geq 2\}.

Proof.

Let
\pi(x)
be the number of primes not exceeding
x
. When
a>44
, there exists
r<a
such that
\tau(r)=3
. For instance,
r=27=7+7+13
and
r=35=5+7+23
. We must demonstrate that there exists an
r<a
such that
\tau(r)=3
and
a+r\not\in\mathcal{PN}
.

If
a
is odd, then
a+r
is even, hence not a prime. If
a
is even and sufficiently large, then among the numbers less than
a
: there are
a/2
odd numbers (1 is odd, 2 is prime); the number of
r
satisfying
r\in\mathcal{PN}
and
r-2\in\mathcal{PN}
is at most
2\pi(a)
; the number of
r
satisfying
a+r\in\mathcal{PN}
does not exceed
\pi(2a)-\pi(a)
. Therefore, we only need to prove

\frac{a}{2}-2\pi(a)-(\pi(2a)-\pi(a))>0.

For a given
a
, there exists
k>2
such that
(k-1)\log(k-1)\leq a\leq k\log(k)
.
By
p_{k}\geq k\log(k)
, we only need to prove

\frac{(k-1)\log(k-1)}{2}-k-2k>0,

which is readily observed to be equivalent to
k>411
. Hence, the above inequality holds for
a\geq 2467
.
Finally, for even numbers
a
with
44<a<2467
, we employed a computer to verify the theorem. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.
Square Sequence Formula

In this section, we primarily address Open Problem
2.1
. We aim to affirm the conjecture proposed by Einstein et al.
[
5
]
with the aid of the well-known “Four-Square Theorem”.

Our investigation commences with Lemma
1.2
, which says that we must determine the value of

N_{r}=\min\left\{O_{B}(ma+r)\cdot a+(ma+r)\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}i^{2}=ma+r,\ \ m,x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq k\right\}

for every
r
,
where

O_{B}(M)=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}i^{2}=M,\ \ M,x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

We introduce the function
\iota_{k}(M)=O_{B}(M)
as defined below.

Definition 4.1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{P}
, we denote
\iota_{k}(n)
as

\iota_{k}(n)=\min\left\{s\mid n=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+\cdots+a_{s}^{2},\ a_{i}\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,k\},1\leq i\leq s\right\}.

In other words,
\iota_{k}(n)
represents the smallest number of squares required to express
n
as the sum of squares
1^{2},2^{2},3^{2},\ldots,k^{2}
. A representation of
n
utilizing exactly
\iota_{k}(n)
squares is deemed
optimal
.

For instance, if
k=6
and
n=79
, then
\iota_{k}(79)=4=\iota(79)
(as proven in Lemma
2.5
), with the optimal representation
n=6^{2}+5^{2}+3^{2}+3^{2}
.
It is noteworthy that the greedy algorithm yields
n=2\cdot 6^{2}+2^{2}+3\cdot 1^{2}
, which is not optimal.

Consequently, our objective is to explore efficient methods for calculating
\iota_{k}(M)
.
This is done in the next subsection by using generating functions.

4.1.
The Generating Function for
\iota_{k}(M)

Our main objective in this subsection is to establish the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm with respect to
k
for computing
\iota_{k}(r)
for all
r\in\mathbb{P}
.

By definition

\iota_{k}(r)=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}i^{2}=r,\ \ r,x_{i}\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

(2)

It is natural to consider the complete generating function:

CF(t;q)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{1-t_{i}q^{i^{2}}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{x_{1}+2^{2}x_{2}+\cdots+k^{2}x_{k}=n}t_{1}^{x_{1}}\cdots t_{k}^{x_{k}}\right)q^{n},

which encodes all information within nonnegative representations. By setting
t_{i}=t
for all
i
, we obtain

F(t,q)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{1-tq^{i^{2}}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{x_{1}+2^{2}x_{2}+\cdots+k^{2}x_{k}=n}t^{x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{k}}\right)q^{n}.

If we denote a solution
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
for (
2
) satisfying
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=\iota_{k}(r)
as
optimal
, then
the generating function
f(t,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{\iota_{k}(n)}q^{n}
extracts only one optimal representation, weighted by
t^{\iota_{k}(n)}
, for each
n
. It is easy to see that

f(t,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{\iota_{k}(n)}q^{n}:=\circledast F(t,q),

where
\circledast
is the operator defined as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

For a power series
G(t,q)
in
t,q
with nonnegative coefficients, we define

\circledast G(t,q)
as the power series obtained from
G(t,q)
by selecting the term of minimum degree (in
t
) within each coefficient (in
q
).

The basic fact is that
\circledast F(t,q)G(t,q)=\circledast(\circledast F(t,q))(\circledast G(t,q))
allows us to use Maple for the efficient computation of the first
M+1
terms of
f(t,q)
for any appreciably large value of
M
.
The procedure is as follows:

(1)

Initialize with
f_{1}:=\sum_{n=0}^{M}t^{n}q^{n}
.

(2)

Assuming
f_{i-1}
has been calculated, proceed to compute
f_{i}
as follows:
first compute
\circledast f_{i-1}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{h_{i}}t^{n}q^{i^{2}n}

where
h_{i}=\lfloor M/i^{2}\rfloor
will be optimized,
and then remove all terms with degrees in
q
larger than
M
.

(3)

Set
f(t,q)=f_{k}
.

Thus, we have proved the following result.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
k
be a fixed positive integer. For a given
M
, the first
M+1
term
f(t,q)|_{q^{\leq M}}
of
f(t,q)
can be computed in polynomial time in
M
. Consequently,
\iota_{k}(r),\ r\leq M
can be computed in polynomial time in
M
.

To completely determine
\iota_{k}(M)
for any natural number
M
, we rely on two key inequalities derived from the “Four-Square Theorem”.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
M\in\mathbb{N}
, the following bounds hold:

\left\lceil\frac{M}{k^{2}}\right\rceil\leq\iota_{k}(M)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k^{2}}\right\rfloor+4
.

Proof.

Consider
M
expressed as
M=sk^{2}+r_{1}
, where
s\geq 0
and
0\leq r_{1}\leq k^{2}-1
.
If
r_{1}=0
, then clearly
\iota_{k}(M)=s
.
If
1\leq r_{1}\leq k^{2}-1
, we have

\left\lceil\frac{M}{k^{2}}\right\rceil=s+1\leq\iota_{k}(M)\leq s+\iota_{k}(r_{1})\leq s+4=\left\lfloor\frac{M}{k^{2}}\right\rfloor+4.

The third inequality is a direct consequence of the “Four-Square Theorem”.
∎

The following lemma establishes that
\iota_{k}(r)
exhibits a certain stability and hence reduces the computation of
\iota_{k}(r),\ r\in\mathbb{N}
for only bounded
r
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For a given
k\in\mathbb{P}
, and for any
r\geq\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-2\right)k^{2}
, with
r\in\mathbb{P}
, it holds that
\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)=\iota_{k}(r)+1
.

Proof.

Trivially,
\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)\leq\iota_{k}(r)+1
always holds. If an optimal representation of
k^{2}+r
includes a term of
k^{2}
, then the removal of this term yields the inequality
\iota_{k}(r)\leq\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)-1
, and thus
\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)=\iota_{k}(r)+1
.

Thus, assume to the contrary that
\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)<\iota_{k}(r)+1
. This implies that
k^{2}+r
cannot have an optimal representation involving
k^{2}
. We will show that this is impossible.

Let
r=mk^{2}+j
, where
0\leq j\leq k^{2}-1
. By the Four-Square Theorem, we can express
j
as
j=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}
, where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{N}
and
a,b,c,d<k
.

By the assumption for
r
, we have
m\geq\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-2
. We choose
m
such that
mk^{2}\geq(m+3)(k-1)^{2}
. For such an
m
, the following inequality holds:

mk^{2}+j=mk^{2}+a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}\geq(m+3)(k-1)^{2}.

This implies that without using
k^{2}
,
\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)
must be at least
m+5
. Therefore, we have
m+5\leq\iota_{k}(k^{2}+r)<\iota_{k}(r)+1\leq m+5
, which is a contradiction.
∎

4.2.
The Frobenius Formula for Square Sequences

Firstly, we show that
N_{r}
behaves nicely when
a
is large. Specifically, we establish the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

If
a\geq 3k^{2}
, then
N_{r}=N_{r}(0)
for a given
r
, that is,
N_{r}
achieves the minimum when
m=0
.

Proof.

Consider a fixed
r
and any non-negative
m
. Let
ma+r=sk^{2}+r_{1}
, where
0\leq r_{1}<k^{2}
. By
a\geq 3k^{2}
, we have
(m+1)a+r=sk^{2}+a+r_{1}\geq(s+3)k^{2}+r_{1}
. Proposition
4.5
implies that
\iota_{k}(ma+r)=s+d
and
\iota_{k}((m+1)a+r)\geq(s+3)
for some
0\leq d\leq 4
. Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle N_{r}(m+1)

\displaystyle\geq(s+3)a+(m+1)a+r=(s+4)a+ma+r

\displaystyle\geq(s+d)a+ma+r=N_{r}(m).

Then
N_{r}(m)
is increasing and hence minimizes at
m=0
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
u=\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil+1\right)k^{2}
. For any
a\geq u
, it holds that

\mathop{\max}\limits_{0\leq r\leq a-1}\{N_{r}\}=\mathop{\max}\limits_{a-k^{2}\leq r\leq a-1}\{N_{r}\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.9
and
u\geq 3k^{2}
, we have
N_{r}=N_{r}(0)
. By Lemma
4.6
, for a given
r\geq\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-2\right)k^{2}
,
\iota_{k}(sk^{2}+r)
is increasing with respect to
s\geq 0
. There might be exceptions for small
r
. For example, when
k=5
, we observe that
\iota_{k}(7)=4,\iota_{k}(5^{2}+7)=2,\iota_{k}(2\times 5^{2}+7)=3,
etc.

Let
y=mk^{2}+j
with
1\leq j\leq k^{2}-1
. The Four-Square Theorem guarantees that
\iota_{k}(y)\leq m+4
.
Additionally, we can assert that

\iota_{k}(y+3k^{2})\geq\left\lceil\frac{y+3k^{2}}{k^{2}}\right\rceil=m+4
.
It follows that if
r\geq\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-2\right)k^{2}+2k^{2}
, then
\iota_{k}(r)\geq\iota_{k}(r-3k^{2})
. Combining with the stable property, we can conclude that
\iota_{k}(r)\geq\iota_{k}(r-sk^{2})
holds for all
s\geq 0
. Consequently, when
a\geq u=\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-2\right)k^{2}+3k^{2}
, we have
\mathop{\max}\limits_{0\leq r\leq a-1}\{N_{r}\}=\mathop{\max}\limits_{a-k^{2}\leq r\leq a-1}\{N_{r}\}
.
∎

The following theorem will show that the Frobenius formula for square sequences is a
“congruence class function” about
k^{2}
, i.e., divide it into
k^{2}
classes according to
\mod k^{2}
residue of
a
. Like the conjecture of Einstein et al.
[
5
]
, our function is also part of
k^{2}
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
k
be a fixed positive integer, and
A(a)=(a,a+1,a+2^{2},\ldots,a+k^{2})
. There exist two nonnegative integer sequences
t_{k}=(t_{k,j})_{0\leq j\leq k^{2}-1}
and
r_{k}=(r_{k,j})_{0\leq j\leq k^{2}-1}
such that for all
a\geq u=\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil+1\right)k^{2}
, the following Frobenius formula holds:

g(A(a))=(t_{k,j}\cdot a+r_{k,j})+(a+k^{2})\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a}{k^{2}}\right\rfloor-\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-1\right),\ \ \textrm{where }a\equiv j\mod k^{2}.

Furthermore, the sequences
t_{k}
and
r_{k}
are both increasing, and
t_{k,k^{2}-1}-t_{k,0}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let
a=sk^{2}+j\geq u=\left(\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil+1\right)k^{2}
, where
s\geq\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil+1
and
0\leq j\leq k^{2}-1
. By Lemma
4.10
, we have
\mathop{\max}\limits_{0\leq r\leq a-1}\{N_{r}\}=\mathop{\max}\limits_{a-k^{2}\leq r\leq a-1}\{N_{r}\}
.

Now
N_{r}=\iota_{k}(r)\cdot a+r
is dominated by the coefficient
\iota_{k}(r)
. This suggests that we shall first find

\iota=\mathop{\max}\limits_{a-k^{2}\leq r\leq a-1}\iota_{k}(r)

and then find the largest
a-k^{2}\leq\widehat{r}\leq a-1
satisfying
\iota_{k}(\widehat{r})=\iota
. Hence,
\max\{N_{r}\}=\iota_{k}(\widehat{r})\cdot a+\widehat{r}
, and the Frobenius number is
(\iota_{k}(\widehat{r})-1)\cdot a+\widehat{r}
.

Let
u\leq a\leq u+k^{2}-1
and
a\equiv j\mod k^{2}
. So
j
can take all the numbers in
\{0,1,2,\ldots,k^{2}-1\}
. For a certain
j
, according to the above discussion, there exists an
a-k^{2}\leq r_{k,j}\leq a-1
such that
g(A)=\max\{N_{r}\}-a=t_{k,j}\cdot a+r_{k,j}
, where
t_{k,j},r_{k,j}\in\mathbb{N}
and
t_{k,j}=\iota_{k}(r_{k,j})-1
.

For the same
j
, if
a=sk^{2}+j
and
s>\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil+1
, then
a=\left(s-\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-1\right)k^{2}+u+j
. By Lemma
4.6
, we find
\overline{r}=\left(s-\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-1\right)k^{2}+r_{k,j}
such that
\max\{N_{r}\}=N_{\overline{r}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle g(A(a))

\displaystyle=\left(t_{k,j}+s-\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-1\right)\cdot a+\left(s-\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-1\right)k^{2}+r_{k,j}

\displaystyle=(t_{k,j}\cdot a+r_{k,j})+(a+k^{2})\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a}{k^{2}}\right\rfloor-\left\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\right\rceil-1\right).

Thus, for each
0\leq j\leq k^{2}-1
, all
t_{k,j}
and
r_{k,j}
form the sequences
t_{k}
and
r_{k}
, respectively.

From the process of calculating
t_{k,j}
and
r_{k,j}
, it is evident that
u-k^{2}\leq r_{k,j}<u+k^{2}
. When
a=u
, let
\widehat{r}
be the largest number such that

u-k^{2}\leq\widehat{r}\leq u-1\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \iota_{k}(\widehat{r})=\mathop{\max}\limits_{u-k^{2}\leq r\leq u-1}\iota_{k}(r).

In the subsequent period from
u
to
u+k^{2}-1
, corresponding to
a=u+k^{2}
,
\widehat{r}^{\prime}=\widehat{r}+k^{2}
satisfies

u\leq\widehat{r}^{\prime}\leq u+k^{2}-1\ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \iota_{k}(\widehat{r}^{\prime})=\iota_{k}(\widehat{r})+1=\mathop{\max}\limits_{u\leq r\leq u+k^{2}-1}\iota_{k}(r).

Therefore, within any interval of length
k^{2}
starting with
a\equiv j\mod k^{2}
that lies between
u-k^{2}
and
u+k^{2}-1
, there are two cases: i) If the interval contains
\widehat{r}^{\prime}
, then

r_{k,j}=\widehat{r}^{\prime}
;
ii) If the interval contains
\widehat{r}
, then
r_{k,j}
lies between
\widehat{r}
and
\widehat{r}^{\prime}
, and
\iota_{k}(r_{k,j})
is either
\iota_{k}(\widehat{r})
or
\iota_{k}(\widehat{r})+1
.
Furthermore,

r_{k,j}
is increasing with respect to
j
. Consequently, it follows that the sequence
t_{k}
is increasing and
t_{k,k^{2}-1}-t_{k,0}\leq 1
.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Essentially, when
a\geq u(k)
(
u(k)
is a function related to
k\in\mathbb{P}
), the Frobenius formula for the sequence
A=(a,a+1,a+2^{2},\ldots,a+k^{2})
can be regarded as a “congruence class function” with
k^{2}
classes. Each segment of this function is a quadratic polynomial in
a
with a leading coefficient
\frac{1}{k^{2}}
.

Now we give an example to understand the above proof process intuitively.

Example 4.13
.

Let
k=3
. Our bound for
a
is
a\geq u=54
, and we know that
45\leq r_{k,j}<63
. By referring to Example
4.7

\displaystyle\circledast F(t,q)=\cdots

We have addressed Open Problem
2.1
on square sequence about Frobenius number. According to the above theorems and corollaries, our findings have the following similarities and differences with the conjecture proposed by Einstein et. al.

1. We combined the Four-Square Theorem and solved the optimization problem
\iota_{k}(M)
to obtain the Frobenius formula for the general
k
. In contrast, Einstein et al. derived the formula for
k\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}
through their geometric algorithm.

2. Our formulas align with the results of Einstein et al. The essence is that it is a “congruence class function” with
k^{2}
classes. Each class segment is represented by a quadratic polynomial in
a
, with the leading coefficient being
\frac{1}{k^{2}}
.

3. Our proof establishes the general range for
a
as
a\geq u=(\lceil\frac{3k}{2}\rceil+1)k^{2}
. In comparison, Einstein et al. provided the specific bounds of
(1,1,16,24,41,67,136)
for
k
being
(1,2,3,4,5,6,7)
, respectively. For any given
k
, the precise lower bound on
a
can be determined by calculating
\iota_{k}(r)
, with the aid of a computer. We have listed the lower bounds for
k\leq 13
in Corollary
4.14
. Note that when
k=6,7
, the result given by Einstein et al. is
a\geq 67,136
, but our result is
a\geq 68,137
. It’s just a typo in
[
5
]
.

4. The proof of Theorem
4.11
introduces a general method for computing the coefficients
t_{k,j}
and
r_{k,j}
through the calculation of
\iota_{k}(r)
. We have illustrated this with specific coefficients for
1\leq k\leq 5
in Example
4.13
.
For other cases, interested readers can try to calculate them.

5.
Concluding Remark

Our main contribution is Theorem
4.11
, which settles the conjecture proposed by Einstein et al. concerning the Frobenius number of a square sequence. A similar idea can be adapted to address the high power sequence case. For high power sequences, we can also discuss similarly using some results from
Waring’s Problem
, which can be stated as follows:
Given a positive integer
n
, find the smallest integer
m
such that any
a\in\mathbb{P}
can be written as the sum of
m

n
th powers of nonnegative integers.
The existence of such an
m
is known and is denoted as
\kappa(n)
. For a comprehensive treatment, see
[
6
, Chapters XX–XXI]
and references therein.

We present the following two results about
\kappa(n)
.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
6
]
)
.

It is known that
\kappa(2)=4,\kappa(3)=9,\kappa(4)=19,\kappa(5)=37.

Conjecture 5.2

(
[
6
]
)
.

For Waring’s Problem, the following formula for
\kappa(n)
is conjectured:

\kappa(n)=2^{n}+\left\lfloor\frac{3^{n}}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor-2.

This formula has been computationally verified for
6\leq n<471600000
.

Similar to previous discussions, when
a\geq u(k)
(
u(k)
is a function dependent on
k\in\mathbb{P}
), and given that
1\leq n<471600000
, the Frobenius formula for the sequence
A=(a,a+1,a+2^{n},\ldots,a+k^{n})
is a
“congruence class function” with
k^{n}
classes. Each class segment within this function is represented by a quadratic polynomial in
a
, with a leading coefficient
\frac{1}{k^{n}}
. When
n=1
,
k\leq a-1
, this is the result of Brauer as presented in
[
1
]
.

One of our future projects is to extend the Frobenius formula to a more general setting, where the sequence
A
is defined as
A=(a,ha+db_{1},ha+db_{2},\dots,ha+db_{k})=(a,ha+dB)
. Initial research progress can be found in
[
11
]
.
