Coupled charge and energy transfer dynamics in light harvesting complexes from a hybrid hierarchical equations of motion approach
Thomas P. Fay, David T. Limmer

Abstract
We describe a method for simulating exciton dynamics in protein-pigment complexes, including effects from charge transfer as well as fluorescence. The method combines the hierarchical equations of motion, which are used to describe quantum dynamics of excitons, and the Nakajima-Zwanzig quantum master equation, which is used to describe slower charge transfer processes. We study the charge transfer quenching in light harvesting complex II, a protein postulated to control non-photochemcial quenching in many plant species. Using our hybrid approach, we find good agreement between our calculation and experimental measurements of the excitation lifetime. Furthermore our calculations reveal that the exciton energy funnel plays an important role in determining quenching efficiency, a conclusion we expect to extend to other proteins that perform protective excitation quenching. This also highlights the need for simulation methods that properly account for the interplay of exciton dynamics and charge transfer processes.

II
Theory

We consider the coupled energy and charge transfer of a photoexcited chromophoric system, like that found in naturally occurring light harvesting complexes. In this section, we outline a general model hamiltonian, and review HEOM.

The LE state potential energy surfaces

We can often assume that the LE state energy shift operators
\Delta\hat{V}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}}
have Gaussian statistics in the ground state reference ensemble, meaning third-order cumulants and higher vanish. Further we assume correlation functions of
\Delta\hat{V}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}}
can be decomposed into a sum of contributions as follows

;
Ishizaki and Fleming 2009a

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\Delta{V}_{\LE_n}^{\bath_{\LE_n}}(t)\Delta\op{V}_{\LE_m}^{\bath_{\LE_m}}(0)}_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{b},n}}C_{n,r}(t)

(13)

where
\expectationvalue{\cdots}_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}=\Tr_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}[\cdots e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}}]/\Tr_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}}]
, and
\Delta{V}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}}(t)=e^{i\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}t/\hbar}\Delta{V}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}_{n}}}e^{-i\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}t/\hbar}
. Provided

is a smooth function it can be written in terms of a spectral density

as

\displaystyle C_{n,r}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}(t)=\frac{\hbar}{\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\differential{\omega}\mathcal{J}_{n,r}(\omega)\!\left[\coth(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2})\cos(\omega t)\!-\!i\sin(\omega t)\right].

(14)

With these assumptions,

can be written as a sum of independent harmonic bath Hamitlonians, and that the LE state energy shift terms

;

Overall this means we can write the

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{n,r}(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{n,r}}\frac{c_{nr\alpha}^{2}}{m_{nr\alpha}\omega_{nr\alpha}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{nr\alpha})

II.3
The hierarchical equations of motion

The hierarchical equations of motion provide a method for simulating the dynamics of a system linearly coupled to a harmonic bath. It was developed for Hamiltonians like those that we are interested in that can be decomposed as
Tanimura 2020

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\left(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{b},j}+\hat{V}_{j}\hat{B}_{j}\right).

(18)

where
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the sub-system Hamiltonian,
\hat{V}_{j}
are system operators,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{b},j}
is the Hamiltonian of harmonic bath
j
. The bath displacement operators,
\hat{B}_{j}
, are defined as,

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{b},j}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{j}}{\hat{p}_{j\alpha}^{2}/2m_{j\alpha}}+m_{j\alpha}\omega_{j\alpha}^{2}\hat{q}_{j\alpha}^{2}/2
and
\hat{B}_{j}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{j}}c_{j,\alpha}\hat{q}_{j,\alpha}.

as analogous to Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) where the index
n,r
is replaced with a single index
j
.
Assuming the bath displacement operator correlation functions can be decomposed as

\displaystyle C_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{jk}e^{-\nu_{jk}t}

(19)

and using the Gaussian property of the harmonic baths, we can obtain the system reduced density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)=\Tr_{\mathrm{b}}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)]
, from a hierarchy of auxiliary density operators (ADOs)

which obey the following equation of motion,

;
Ishizaki and Tanimura 2005
;
Tanimura 2020

\displaystyle\begin{split}\derivative{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}}(t)&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}},\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}}(t)\right]\!-\!\sum_{j,k}n_{jk}\nu_{jk}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}}(t)\!+\Xi_{\mathbf{n}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}}(t)\\
&-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{(n_{jk}+1)|a_{jk}|}[\hat{V}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{+}}]\\
&-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\frac{n_{jk}}{|a_{jk}|}}\left(a_{jk}\hat{V}_{j}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{-}}-{a}_{jk}^{*}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{-}}\hat{V}_{j}\right).\end{split}

(20)

where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1,0},n_{1,1},\dots,n_{j,k},\dots)
is a multi-index that specifies the excitation level of mode

for each bath

\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{\pm}=(n_{1,0},n_{1,1},\dots,n_{j,k}\pm 1,\dots)
, and

is a system superoperator that accounts for finite truncation of the hierarchy. The sub-system reduced density operator can be obtained as the zeroth element of this hierarchy

We can write down the truncated hierarchy as

\displaystyle|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}|\rho_{\mathbf{n}}(t)\rangle\!\rangle\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\!\rangle

(21)

where

is a basis vector corresponding to auxiliary density operator

is the Liouville space vector of this ADO. With this notation we can write down the equation of motion more compactly as

;
Ikeda and Nakayama 2022
;
Ke, Borrelli, and Thoss 2022

\displaystyle\derivative{t}|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\pazocal{L}|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle

(22)

\displaystyle=(\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{s}}\otimes\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{ado}}-\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{s}}\otimes\Gamma+\Xi+\pazocal{V})|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle

(23)

where
\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{s}}=-(i/\hbar)[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}},\ \cdot\ ]
is the system Liouvillian,

\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{ado}}

is a matrix of ADO decay rates,

is superoperator that accounts for finite truncation of the hierarchy

III
The hybrid HEOM/QME method

Using HEOM to study CT in these systems can be difficult due to the large reorganization energies, which give rise to large values of
a_{jk}
for baths associated with CT processes. The large coupling coefficients for certain modes means many ADOs are needed to converge the HEOM dynamics. This can make HEOM calculations involving CT states intractable when there are large numbers of system states and baths.
A simplification results as the reorganization energy of charge transfer processes is typically much larger than the electronic coupling between CT states and LE states, the use of perturbation theory, where the coupling to the CT states is treated as a small parameter, can be expected to give an accurate description of the coupled dynamics of the LE and CT states.
Blumberger 2015
;
Fay, Lindoy, and Manolopoulos 2018
;
Fay 2021

In this section, we describe how Zwanzig projection can be used to construct a hybrid HEOM/QME method in which radiative and electron transfer processes are treated with perturbation theory while preserving the high accuracy of the EET dynamics afforded by HEOM. Similar approaches have been used to derive hybrid HEOM methods to describe radiative processes in EET in protein-pigment complexes,
Kreisbeck et al. 2011
;
Chan et al. 2018
;
Ko, Cook, and Whaley 2022
but these have not included charge transfer processes. The approach taken here combines some of the ideas for describing radiative processes in Ref.
Kreisbeck et al. 2011
with Nakajima-Zwanzig theory based approaches that have been used to derive quantum master equations for spin density operators of different charge transfer states in photo-excited molecules.
Fay, Lindoy, and Manolopoulos 2018
;
Fay 2021
;
Fay and Limmer 2021

III.1
Zwanzig projection

Here we use the Zwanzig projection operator approach to construct a hybrid HEOM/QME method. We set up the problem by formally including the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}
and EM field degrees of freedom, as well as the full set of electronic states, in the system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
in the hierarchical equations of motion. This means the “system” Hamiltonian is taken to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}&=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{s}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{CT}}}\Pi_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}\left(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CT}_{n},\mathrm{s}}+\Delta\hat{V}_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}\right)+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}\\
&+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{CT}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CT},\mathrm{GS}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{EM}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{D}}.\end{split}

(24)

The response of the system to the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}
couplings is treated with the HEOM, with the set of
N_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}}=\sum_{n}N_{\mathrm{b},n}
baths indexed by
j=n,r
, and

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{n,r}=\outerproduct{\LE_n}{\LE_n}+\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{CT}}}\kappa_{n,r}^{\mathrm{CT}_{m}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{CT}_{m}}\,,

(25)

are the set of system-bath coupling operators. The coefficients
\kappa_{n,r}^{\mathrm{CT}_{m}}
here describe how the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}
degrees of freedom are coupled to the CT states.

The main variables of interest for this system are the reduced density operator for the LE states, and the CT and GS state populations. These populations are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathrm{s}}(t)=\hat{\Pi}_{A}\Tr_{\mathrm{b}}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{0}}(t)]\hat{\Pi}_{A}

(26)

where
\hat{\Pi}_{A}
is a projection operator onto the manifold of electronic states with
A=\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{CT}_{n}\text{ or }\mathrm{GS}
, and
\Tr_{\mathrm{b}}
denotes a trace over all
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}
and
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}
field degrees of freedom. Since we aim to treat the exciton dynamics with the HEOM, we construct the projection operator for the HEOM ADOs as

\displaystyle\pazocal{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A}\pazocal{P}_{A}\otimes\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{ado}}

(27)

\displaystyle\pazocal{P}_{A}

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}_{A}\hat{\rho}^{{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}}}\hat{\Pi}_{A}\Tr_{\mathrm{b}}[\ \cdot\ ]\hat{\Pi}_{A}\,

(28)

where local equilibrium density operator for the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}
degrees of freedom in state
A
is
\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}_{A}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{A}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}}/\Tr_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{A}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}}]
, and
\hat{H}_{A}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}
for
A=\mathrm{GS}
or
\mathrm{LE}
, and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{GS}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}+\Delta\hat{V}_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}
. The EM field density operator is approximated as the zero temperature equilibrium density operator of the bare EM field, where all field modes are in their ground-state,
\hat{\rho}^{{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}}}=\bigotimes_{{\bf\it k},p}\outerproduct{0_{\vb*{k}p}}{0_{\vb*{k}p}}
. This approximation is justified by the fact that we are only interested in spontaneous emission processes, since field modes at the LE state energies are not thermally excited at ambient temperatures, therefore
\beta\hbar\omega_{{\bf\it k}p}\approx 0
for field modes resonant with the LE-GS energy gaps. This type of projection operator is analogous to that used in Refs.
Fay, Lindoy, and Manolopoulos 2018
and
Fay 2021
for electronic state spin density operators. This projected density operator contains the reduced density operator hierarchy,
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
, for each electronic state
A
,

\displaystyle\pazocal{P}|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{A}|\rho_{A}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}\rangle\!\rangle\otimes|\rho^{{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}}}\rangle\!\rangle\otimes|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle

(29)

\displaystyle|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}|\sigma_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)\rangle\!\rangle\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\!\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{\Pi}_{A}\Tr_{\mathrm{b}}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{n}}(t)]\hat{\Pi}_{A}.

(31)

An example of the partitioning into separate hierarchies for different state manifolds,
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
, is illustrated in Fig.
2
for system with four LE states, two CT states and the ground state.

Figure 2:
An schematic of the states, interstate coupling, and partitioning into reduced hierarchies
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
for an example system with four LE states, two CT states and the GS.

Using this projection operator
\pazocal{P}
we can obtain a quantum master equation for the projected hierarchy of ADOs
\pazocal{P}|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle
using the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation
Nakajima 1958
;
Zwanzig 1960
;
Mori 1965

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\pazocal{P}|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\pazocal{P}\pazocal{L}\pazocal{P}|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle+\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\pazocal{K}(t-\tau)\pazocal{P}|\rho(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle\differential{\tau}\!\!,

(32)

where the memory kernel
\pazocal{K}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\pazocal{K}(t)=\pazocal{P}\pazocal{L}e^{(1-\pazocal{P})\pazocal{L}t}(1-\pazocal{P})\pazocal{L}\pazocal{P}.

(33)

The generalized master equations for
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
can be straightforwardly obtained from this by tracing out the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}
and
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}
degrees of freedom. This gives the following general hybrid HEOM/QME for
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle\!

\displaystyle=\!\pazocal{L}_{A}|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle+\!\sum_{B}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\pazocal{K}_{AB}(t-\tau)|\sigma_{B}(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle\!\differential{\tau}\!.

(34)

where
\pazocal{L}_{A}=\langle\!\langle 1_{\mathrm{b}}|\pazocal{P}_{A}\pazocal{L}\pazocal{P}_{A}|\rho^{\mathrm{b}}_{A}\rangle\!\rangle
is the component of
\pazocal{P}\pazocal{L}\pazocal{P}
that acts on
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
and similarly
\pazocal{K}_{AB}(t)
is the component of
\pazocal{K}_{AB}(t)=\langle\!\langle 1_{\mathrm{b}}|\pazocal{P}_{A}\pazocal{K}(t)\pazocal{P}_{B}|\rho^{\mathrm{b}}_{B}\rangle\!\rangle
that couples
|\sigma_{B}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
to
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
. Here
|1_{\mathrm{b}}\rangle\!\rangle
is the identity operator on the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}
and EM degrees of freedom and
|\rho_{A}^{\mathrm{b}}\rangle\!\rangle=|\rho_{A}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}\rangle\!\rangle\otimes|\rho^{{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}}}\rangle\!\rangle
, and the Liouville space inner product is
\langle\!\langle A|B\rangle\!\rangle=\Tr[\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{B}]
.

III.2
Approximating the exact master equation

While Eq. (
34
) is formally exact, evaluating the kernel requires evaluation of the exact dynamics generated by
\pazocal{L}
, which due to the large Hilbert space associated with
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}
and
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{EM}}
is difficult. However, in the absence of the excitonic dynamics, each would be able to be treated accurately with simplifying approximations. Firstly, we make the Markovian approximation, in which we assume the decay time-scale of
\pazocal{K}_{AB}(t)
is much faster than the dynamics of
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
, and therefore we approximate the time-convolution terms as
Sparpaglione and Mukamel 1988
;
Fay, Lindoy, and Manolopoulos 2018

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\pazocal{K}_{AB}(t-\tau)|\sigma_{B}(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle\differential{\tau}\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\pazocal{K}_{AB}(\tau)\differential{\tau}|\sigma_{B}(t)\rangle\!\rangle.

(35)

Secondly, we approximate the full kernel in Eq. (
33
) with its second order approximation in perturbation theory.
Sparpaglione and Mukamel 1988
;
Fay, Lindoy, and Manolopoulos 2018
Here the perturbation Liouvillian
\pazocal{L}_{V}
is taken to be

\displaystyle\pazocal{L}_{V}

\displaystyle=\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{ET}}+\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{R}}

(36)

\displaystyle\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{ET}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ET}},\ \cdot\ ]\otimes\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{ado}}

(37)

\displaystyle\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{R}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{D}},\ \cdot\ ]\otimes\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{ado}},

(38)

and with this the kernel
\pazocal{K}(t)
can be approximated as

\displaystyle\pazocal{K}(t)\approx\pazocal{K}^{(2)}(t)=\pazocal{P}\pazocal{L}_{V}e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V})t}\pazocal{L}_{V}\pazocal{P},

(39)

where we have defined
\pazocal{L}_{0}=\pazocal{L}-\pazocal{L}_{V}-\pazocal{V}
. Because the baths are uncorrelated, the second order kernel can be split into

\displaystyle\pazocal{K}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle=\pazocal{K}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ET}}(t)+\pazocal{K}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)

(40)

\displaystyle=\pazocal{P}\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{ET}}e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V})t}\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{ET}}\pazocal{P}+\pazocal{P}\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{R}}e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V})t}\pazocal{L}_{\mathrm{R}}\pazocal{P}

(41)

an electron transfer term,
\pazocal{K}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ET}}(t)
and a radiative decay term,
\pazocal{K}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)
.

The final simplification we make is to approximate the reference propagator
e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V})t}
appearing in the ET kernel as

\displaystyle e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V})t}\approx e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\tilde{\pazocal{V}})t}

(42)

where
\tilde{\pazocal{V}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n^{\prime}}\in\pazocal{N}_{\pazocal{K}}}|\mathbf{n}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathbf{n}|\pazocal{V}|\mathbf{n^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathbf{n^{\prime}}|
is
\pazocal{V}
projected onto a subset of the ADOs. We make this approximation to reduce the density of the coupling between elements of
|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle
in the equations of motion, and increase the computational efficiency of the method.
For the radiative decay term we simply approximate
e^{(\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V})t}\approx e^{\pazocal{L}_{0,\mathrm{s}}t}
, where
\pazocal{L}_{0,\mathrm{s}}
is just the coherent system dynamics term appearing in
\pazocal{L}_{0}
. In deriving explicit expressions for terms appearing in the hybrid HEOM/QME, it is important to note that
\pazocal{L}_{0}
and
{\pazocal{V}}
can be decomposed as
\pazocal{L}_{0}=\bigoplus_{A,B}\pazocal{L}_{0}^{AB}
and
{\pazocal{V}}=\bigoplus_{A,B}\pazocal{V}^{AB}
, where each term only acts on coherences
\outerproduct{A}{B}
between electronic states in manifolds
A
and
B
.

Applying the above set of approximations to Eq. (
34
), allows us to write the hybrid HEOM/QME as

\displaystyle\derivative{t}|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle\!

\displaystyle=\!\pazocal{L}_{A}|\sigma_{A}(t)\rangle\!\rangle+\sum_{B}\pazocal{R}_{AB}|\sigma_{B}(t)\rangle\!\rangle,

(43)

where
\pazocal{R}_{AB}
can be written as a sum of electron transfer and radiative decay terms,
\pazocal{R}_{AB}=\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}+\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{R}}
. We can write this system of equations more explicitly as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\derivative{t}\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{A,\mathrm{s}},\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)]-\gamma_{\mathbf{n}}\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)\\
&+\Xi_{A,\mathbf{n}}\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{(n_{jk}+1)|a_{jk}|}[\hat{V}_{A,j},\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{+}}(t)]\\
&-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\frac{n_{jk}}{|a_{jk}|}}\left(a_{jk}\hat{V}_{A,j}\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{-}}(t)-{a}_{jk}^{*}\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}_{jk}^{-}}(t)\hat{V}_{A,j}\right)\\
&+\sum_{B}\sum_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\pazocal{R}_{AB,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\hat{\sigma}_{B,\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(t),\end{split}

(44)

where
\hat{H}_{A,\mathrm{s}}
and
\hat{V}_{A}
are the components of the electronic state terms in
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\hat{V}
projected onto the manifold of electronic states
A
,
\Xi_{A,\mathbf{n}}
is superoperator that accounts for finite truncation, projected on
A
, and
\pazocal{R}_{AB,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
is the component of
\pazocal{R}_{AB}
that couples state
\hat{\sigma}_{A,\mathbf{n}}(t)
to
\hat{\sigma}_{B,\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(t)
. Now that we have the general form of the Markovian hybrid HEOM/QME, we just need to evaluate the transfer operators
\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}
and
\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{R}}
. This is detailed in the following sections.

III.3
The electron transfer term

We start by considering the electron transfer term,
\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}
. By noting that
\pazocal{L}_{0}+\tilde{\pazocal{V}}
does not mix populations and coherences between the
\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{CT}
and
\mathrm{GS}
manifolds, the elements of
\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}
where
A\neq B
can be evaluated straightforwardly as

\displaystyle\pazocal{R}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}=\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{L},AB}\pazocal{G}_{AB}^{B}\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{R},BA}+\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{R},BA}\pazocal{G}_{BA}^{B}\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{L},AB}.

(45)

where the
\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{L/R},AB}
terms are given by

\displaystyle\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{L},AB}\hat{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\hat{\Pi}_{A}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ET}}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{\sigma}

(46)

\displaystyle\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{R},BA}\hat{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\hat{\sigma}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ET}}\hat{\Pi}_{A}

(47)

and the
\pazocal{G}_{AB}^{C}
terms are given by

\displaystyle\pazocal{G}_{AB}^{B}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{t}G_{AB}^{B}(t)\pazocal{S}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}e^{\Lambda_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}t}{\pazocal{S}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}}^{-1}

(48)

where we have used the spectral decomposition of the projected reference Liouvillian

\displaystyle(\pazocal{L}_{0}^{AB}+\tilde{\pazocal{V}}_{\mathrm{ET}}^{AB})\pazocal{S}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}=\Lambda_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}\pazocal{S}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}

(49)

where
\pazocal{L}_{0}^{AB}
is defined as the block of
\pazocal{L}_{0}
that just acts on
AB
coherences, and likewise for
\tilde{\pazocal{V}}_{\mathrm{ET}}^{AB}
, and
\pazocal{S}_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}
is the matrix of eigenvectors and
\Lambda_{AB}^{\mathrm{ET}}
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Due to the block diagonal structure of
\pazocal{L}_{0}^{AB}+\tilde{\pazocal{V}}_{\mathrm{ET}}^{AB}
this can be straightforwardly evaluated. The correlation function
G_{AB}^{C}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle G_{AB}^{C}(t)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}[e^{-i\hat{H}_{A}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}t/\hbar}e^{+i\hat{H}_{B}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}t/\hbar}\hat{\rho}_{C}^{\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{CT}}}].

(50)

which is the moment generating function for the energy gap between subsystems
A
and
B
.
The remaining diagonal
\pazocal{R}_{AA}^{\mathrm{ET}}
terms are given by

\displaystyle\pazocal{R}_{AA}^{\mathrm{ET}}=-\sum_{B\neq A}\left(\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{L},BA}\pazocal{G}_{BA}^{A}\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{L},BA}+\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{R},AB}\pazocal{G}_{AB}^{A}\pazocal{L}_{H_{\mathrm{ET}}}^{\mathrm{R},AB}\right)

(51)

as required to conserve population.

For practical calculations we use the full version of these expressions, but insight into the effect of ET on the exciton dynamics can be gained by making some additional approximations. First we consider the limit where the system and LE bath time-scales are long compared to the decay time of
G_{\mathrm{CT},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{LE}}(t)
. In this limit the
\pazocal{R}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{ET}}
term becomes

\displaystyle\pazocal{R}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{ET}}=-\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{CT}}}\frac{k_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}}{2}\left\{\hat{P}_{n},\ \cdot\ \right\}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{CT}}}{\Delta_{n}}\left[\hat{P}_{n},\ \cdot\ \right].

(52)

a sum of dissipative and conservative terms. Here,
k_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}
is the Fermi’s Golden Rule rate constant for ET from the
\mathrm{LE}
manifold to the
\mathrm{CT}_{n}
state,
Sparpaglione and Mukamel 1988

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|V_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}|^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{\mathrm{CT},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{LE}}(t)e^{-i(E_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}-\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}})t/\hbar}\differential{t}

(53)

and
\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}
is an effective energy scale of the LE states such that
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{s}}\approx\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{LE}}
,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CT}_{n},\mathrm{s}}=E_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}
, with the effective coupling constant

\displaystyle|V_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}|^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{LE}}}|V_{\mathrm{LE}_{m},\mathrm{CT}_{n}}|^{2}

(54)

is given by a some over all of the
\mathrm{LE}
states. Finally,
\hat{P}_{n}=\outerproduct{\psi_{n}}{\psi_{n}}
is a projection operator onto the reactive state in the
\mathrm{LE}
manifold which is given by

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{n}}=\frac{1}{|V_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}|}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{LE}}}\ket{\LE_m}V_{\mathrm{LE}_{m},\mathrm{CT}_{n}},

(55)

and
\Delta_{n}
is an energy shift term,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=\frac{|V_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}|^{2}}{\hbar}\imaginary\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{\mathrm{CT},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{LE}}(t)e^{-i(E_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}}-\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}})t/\hbar}\differential{t}

(56)

which in the limit of a highly activated reaction is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\approx\frac{|V_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}|^{2}}{\Delta E_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}}

(57)

where
\Delta E_{\mathrm{CT}_{n}\leftarrow\mathrm{LE}}
is the vertical energy gap from the
\mathrm{LE}
state to the
\mathrm{CT}_{n}
state.
Fay, Lindoy, and Manolopoulos 2018
;
Fay and Limmer 2021

From this we deduce that electron transfer has three main effects on the exciton dynamics. Firstly the ET causes population loss from the LE manifold from the reactive state
\ket{\psi_{n}}
. Secondly it causes decoherence between the states
\ket{\psi_{n}}
state and the rest of the manifold of
\mathrm{LE}
states. Thirdly, the energy shift terms perturb the exciton dynamics, modifying the energy gaps and couplings between LE states. If the reactive state is just a specific localized LE state, i.e.
\ket{\psi_{n}}=\ket{\LE_{r_n}}
, then the energy shift term just alters the energy of this
\mathrm{LE}
state, which in the excitonic basis introduces coupling between delocalized excitonic states, as well as shifting exciton state energies. In the limit of Gaussian statistics for the bath and high temperature, this reduces to Marcus theory.
Marcus 1956
;
Hush 1958
;
Sparpaglione and Mukamel 1988

) ignores the effect of exciton formation, which shifts the free energy of states in the LE manifold, thereby changing the rate constants for electron transfer. The full hybrid HEOM/QME theory that we use in simulations however includes this important effect.

VI
Discussion

Using the hybrid HEOM/QME method developed in this paper we have been able to explore the charge transfer quenching dynamics in a LHCII monomer. Using previously parametrized models of excitation energy transfer and electron transfer in the LHCII pigment-protein complex, together with some physically motivated assumptions, we can obtain estimates of the excitation lifetime closer to experimental values than those obtained using simple kinetic models.
Cupellini et al. 2020
The lifetime estimate however depends strongly on the model of local excitation energies and couplings used to describe the excitation energy transfer preceding charge transfer quenching. This may have interesting implications for the understanding of non-photochemical quenching processes in light harvesting complexes. It is generally assumed that non-photochemical quenching is activated by conformational changes, induced by pH changes or chemical modifications of the protein or bound carotenoids, in protein-pigment complexes moving quenchers into positions where they can efficiently coupled to chromophores,
Park et al. 2019
;
Short et al. 2022
;
Chrysafoudi et al. 2021
;
Daskalakis, Papadatos, and Stergiannakos 2020
;
Ruban and Wilson 2021
;
Lapillo et al. 2020
;
Goss and Lepetit 2015
thereby increasing the rate of charge transfer quenching and/or excitation energy transfer quenching. The work here highlights the potential role of the excitation energy funnel in CT quenching, and we suggest that non-photochemical quenching could also be activated by changes in the site energies and couplings within the chromophore excited state manifold that funnel excitations towards quenching sites. These energy and coupling shifts could also be induced by conformational changes in the protein-pigment complex, and could occur simultaneously with the conformational changes that move quenchers closer to chromophores. In this way, the energy funnelling mechanism could work cooperatively with quenching activation by movement of quenchers. Interestingly it has recently been found in one computational study that pH changes, which are believed to play a role in activating non-photochemical quenching, can create modest shifts in the Chl excitation energies in LHCII.
Maity et al. 2021

Our exploration of the charge transfer spectral density has also highlighted the importance of understanding all details of charge transfer processes in light harvesting complexes, including the details of recombination processes and nuclear quantum effects. One particularly important factor for predicting charge transfer quenching rates is the rate of charge recombination from the CT state back to the electronic ground state of the system, which is strongly dependent on nuclear quantum effects as well as the energetics of charge recombination and the diabatic coupling strength. This is because the reverse electron transfer is often deep in the Marcus inverted regime, for example in LHCII the Lut-Chl
a
recombination processes have a free energy change of
-\Delta G\approx 3\lambda
, where nuclear tunnelling effects play a decisive role in determining electron transfer rates.
Lawrence and Manolopoulos 2020a
Although in our study of LHCII we have assumed that the electron transfers can be treated with a spin-boson mapping,
Blumberger 2015
the theory developed here does not rely on this assumption. The correlation functions needed to evaluate the ET kernels,
G_{AB}^{C}(t)
, can be evaluated using various approximations including anharmonicity in the potential energy surfaces.
Sparpaglione and Mukamel 1988
;
Rabani, Egorov, and Berne 1999
;
Egorov, Rabani, and Berne 1999
;
Tong et al. 2020
;
Lawrence and Manolopoulos 2018
;
Lawrence and Manolopoulos 2020b
For example
G_{AB}^{C}(t)
could be evaluated classically,
Sparpaglione and Mukamel 1988
or using analytic continuation together with path integral methods to incorporate anharmonic nuclear quantum effects.
Lawrence and Manolopoulos 2018

VII
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have outlined how to rigorously combine the hierarchical equations of motion method with quantum master equations in both the strong system-bath coupling and weak system-bath coupling limits, to model simultaneous excitation energy transfer and charge transfer in protein-pigment complexes. The hybrid HEOM/QME approach is based the application of Zwanzig projection, to derive a system of equation for a hierarchy of auxiliary density operators for the various electronic state manifolds. This method has been tested against numerically exact results for an excitonic dimer coupled to a charge transfer state, where it was found to yield accurate population and coherence dynamics across a range of excitation energy transfer regimes. We then applied the method to study charge transfer quenching in LHCII - a process suspected to play an important role in photoprotection in plants.

Using the hybrid HEOM/QME approach we have been able to study the interplay of excitation energy transfer and charge transfer quenching in a realistic model of LHCII. Our results highlight the importance of the excitation energy funnel in determining quenching efficiency in protein-pigment complexes, as well as the role of the CT state recombination rate when back electron transfer to reform LE states from CT states occurs at an appreciable rate, as is the case in LHCII. We expect that the energy funnel mechanism could play a role in activation of non-photochemical quenching in many systems, and that it could occur co-operatively with other NPQ activation mechanisms.

The parameters needed in the hybrid HEOM/QME model can all be obtained using well-established methods, for example exciton Hamiltonians can be fit based on spectroscopic data,
Müh, Madjet, and Renger 2010
;
Novoderezhkin, Marin, and van Grondelle 2011
or using QM/MM simulations of protein-pigment complexes.
Cignoni et al. 2022
Likewise electron transfer model parameters can be obtained using molecular dynamics simulations.
Blumberger 2015
;
Tong et al. 2020
By combining existing computational tools with the hybrid HEOM/QME method, currently implemented in the freely available Matlab code HEOM-lab,
Fay 2022b
it may be possible to shed light on the precise mechanisms that produce non-photochemical quenching in photosynthetic organisms,
Goss and Lepetit 2015
for example how chemical modifications of carotenoids in the xanthophyll cycle activates NPQ in LHCII and related proteins like LHCX1.
Park et al. 2019
;
Short et al. 2022
For this reason we anticipate the method will become a useful tool in studying non-photochemical quenching and reaction center processes in photosynthetic systems.

Appendix B
Simplification of the dimer model baths

In this appendix we describe how the three-bath model for the dimer-CT model can be reduced to a two bath model to speed up exact HEOM calculations. Suppose we have a system coupled to a set of baths,
j=1,...,N
, with identical frequency distributions, but different reorganization energies, i.e.
\pazocal{J}_{j}(\omega)=\eta_{j}\pazocal{J}_{0}(\omega)
with
\eta_{j}=\lambda_{j}/\lambda_{0}
, as in the dimer CT model considered here. We can write the coupling term for bath modes with frequency
\omega_{j\alpha}=\omega_{0\alpha}
as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{b},\alpha}=c_{\alpha}\hat{{\bf\it q}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\bf\it\eta}^{1/2}\hat{{\bf\it V}}

(73)

where
c_{\alpha}=c_{0\alpha}
,
[\hat{{\bf\it q}}_{\alpha}]_{j}=\hat{q}_{j\alpha}
,
[\hat{{\bf\it V}}]_{j}=\hat{V}_{j}
, and
{\bf\it\eta}
is a diagonal matrix of the values of
\eta_{j}
. We can insert an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{S}
, to re-write this as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{b},\alpha}=c_{\alpha}\hat{{\bf\it q}}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{S}{\bf\it\eta}^{1/2}\hat{{\bf\it V}}=c_{\alpha}\hat{\tilde{{\bf\it q}}}\cdot\hat{\tilde{{\bf\it V}}}.

(74)

We can then re-write the Hamiltonian in terms of a new set of uncorrelated baths with mode displacements
\hat{\tilde{{q}}}_{j\alpha}
, and coupling operators
\hat{\tilde{V}}_{j}
. If we choose
\mathbf{S}
such that one of the new system bath coupling operators is just proportional to the identity operator
\hat{\tilde{V}}_{1}\propto\hat{1}
, then we eliminate coupling between one of the baths and the system, and therefore reduce the complexity of the problem. For
\kappa=1
we can find such a transformation as follows. First we write
{\bf\it\eta}^{1/2}\hat{{\bf\it V}}
as

\displaystyle{\bf\it\eta}^{1/2}\hat{{\bf\it V}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\outerproduct{\LE_1}{\LE_1}\\
\outerproduct{\LE_2}{\LE_2}+\kappa\outerproduct{\CT}{\CT}\\
\sqrt{\eta}\outerproduct{\CT}{\CT}\end{matrix}\right)

(75)

where
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{\mathrm{LE}}
and
\eta=\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{CT}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{LE}}}
. We then set
\mathbf{S}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right)

(76)

which gives
\hat{\tilde{{\bf\it V}}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\bf\it V}}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{1}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\outerproduct{\LE_1}{\LE_1}-\outerproduct{\LE_2}{\LE_2}-\outerproduct{\CT}{\CT})\\
\sqrt{\eta}\outerproduct{\CT}{\CT}\end{matrix}\right).

(77)

We see that the system coupling operator for the new bath 1 is proportional to an identity operator, so coupling to this bath does not affect the system dynamics and it can eliminated.

For the
\kappa=0
case we set

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+\eta^{-1}}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+\eta^{-}1}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(2+\eta^{-1})}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+4\eta}}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+4\eta}}&\frac{2\sqrt{\eta}}{\sqrt{2+4\eta}}\end{matrix}\right)

(78)

which gives

\hat{\tilde{{\bf\it V}}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\bf\it V}}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+\eta^{-1}}}\hat{1}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\outerproduct{\LE_1}{\LE_1}-\outerproduct{\LE_2}{\LE_2})\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+4\eta}}\left(2\eta\outerproduct{\CT}{\CT}-\outerproduct{\LE_1}{\LE_1}-\outerproduct{\LE_2}{\LE_2}\right)\end{matrix}\right).

(79)

Again bath 1 can be eliminated because its coupling operator is proportional to an identity operator. We note that only the top row of
\mathbf{S}
is uniquely defined for any
\kappa
, by requiring that
\hat{\tilde{V}}_{1}\propto\hat{1}
, and the choice for the other rows, and other coupling operators, is not unique.

Finally we note that we can scale the reorganization energy of a given bath by
\alpha
if we also scale the corresponding coupling operator by
1/\sqrt{\alpha}
, because the coupling coefficients are proportional to
\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}
,
c_{j\alpha}\propto\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}
, without changing the Hamiltonian. The choice does not affect the exact dynamics, but it does affect how the the hierarchy is truncated with our reorganization energy weighted cut-off scheme. For the calculations we set
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{\mathrm{LE}}
and
\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{\mathrm{CT}}
, which means the coupling operator
\hat{\tilde{V}}_{3}
given above scaled down by

.

Appendix C
The internal conversion term

In this appendix we present a brief justification of the Lindblad form for the internal conversion term used to model direct non-radiative transitions from the
\text{Chl}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\smash{\raisebox{-3.18884pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{*}}}}}
states to the ground state in LHCII. The argument is essentially the same as that used to derive the radiative and ET coupling Hamiltonians. We start by assuming that the internal conversion coupling is described by a Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{GS}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{LE}}}\left(\hat{X}_{n}\outerproduct{\GS}{\LE_n}+\hat{X}_{n}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{\LE_n}{\GS}\right)

(80)

where we assume the
\hat{X}_{n}
operator acts on degrees of freedom other than the
\mathrm{b}_{\mathrm{LE}}
degrees of freedom, and we also assume that these operators commute at all times. We also assume that the thermal average of
\hat{X}_{n}
is zero. The non-adiabatic coupling between the LE states and the ground state depends primarily on local vibrational modes of the chromophore, so it is reasonable to assume that these operators commute.

We can now evaluate the second order Markovian Nakajima-Zwanzig relaxation operator for internal conversion with the projection operator given in main text. We will further approximate the reference Liouvillian as
\pazocal{L}_{0}+\pazocal{V}\approx-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{LE}}\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}},\ \cdot\ ]\otimes\pazocal{I}_{\mathrm{ado}}
, where
\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}
is the average LE state energy, an approximation that is justified because the mean energy difference between the LE states and the ground-state is much larger than energy differences within the LE manifold. From this it is straightforward to obtain the relaxation superoperator as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\pazocal{R}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{NR}}\hat{\sigma}&=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{LE}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\differential{t}\bigg(\expectationvalue{X_n^\dag(t)X_n(0)}_{\mathrm{b}}e^{i\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}t/\hbar}\outerproduct{\LE_n}{\LE_n}\hat{\sigma}\\
&+\expectationvalue{X_n^\dag(t)X_n(0)}_{\mathrm{b}}^{*}e^{-i\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}t/\hbar}\hat{\sigma}\outerproduct{\LE_n}{\LE_n}\bigg).\end{split}

(81)

Ignoring Lamb shift terms that originate from the imaginary parts of the
\expectationvalue{X_n^\dag(t)X_n(0)}_{\mathrm{b}}
correlation functions, this reduces to

\displaystyle\pazocal{R}_{\mathrm{LE},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{NR}}\hat{\sigma}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{LE}}}\frac{k_{\mathrm{NR},n}}{2}\left\{\outerproduct{\LE_n}{\LE_n},\hat{\sigma}\right\}

(82)

where
k_{\mathrm{NR},n}
is the non-radiative decay rate for internal conversion of state
\mathrm{LE}_{n}
. The corresponding term in the equation of motion for
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{GS},\mathbf{n}}(t)
is

\displaystyle\pazocal{R}_{\mathrm{GS},\mathrm{LE}}^{\mathrm{NR}}\hat{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{LE}}}{k_{\mathrm{NR},n}}\outerproduct{\GS}{\LE_n}\hat{\sigma}\outerproduct{\LE_n}{\GS}.

(83)

The reverse internal conversion rate is related to the forward rate by
k_{\mathrm{NR},n}^{\mathrm{back}}=e^{-\beta\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}}k_{\mathrm{NR},n}
, but because
\beta\bar{E}_{\mathrm{LE}}
is typically very large, we can safely ignore the back reaction terms.
