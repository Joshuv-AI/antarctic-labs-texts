Limit behaviour of random walks on ℤ m \mathbb{Z}^{m} with two-sided membrane
Victor Bogdanskii, Ilya Pavlyukevich, Andrey Pilipenko

Abstract
We study Markov chains on ℤ m \mathbb{Z}^{m} , m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 , that behave like a standard symmetric random walk outside of the hyperplane (membrane) H = { 0 } × ℤ m − 1 H=\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1} . The transition probabilities on the membrane H H are periodic and also depend on the incoming direction to H H , what makes the membrane H H two-sided. Moreover, sliding along the membrane is allowed. We show that the natural scaling limit of such Markov chains is a m m -dimensional diffusion whose first coordinate is a skew Brownian motion and the other m − 1 m-1 coordinates is a Brownian motion with a singular drift controlled by the local time of the first coordinate at 0 0 . In the proof we utilize a martingale characterization of the Walsh Brownian motion and determine the effective permeability and slide direction. Eventually, a similar convergence theorem is established for the one-sided membrane without slides and random iid transition probabilities.



Keywords:
Skew Brownian motion; Walsh’s Brownian motion; perturbed random walk;
two-sided membrane; weak convergence; martingale characterization.

Introduction

A multidimensional Brownian motion is a fundamental stochastic process that describes an idealized mathematical model of
a free physical diffusion in a homogeneous medium.
Having in mind the observations of pollen made my Brown or a later kinetic theory of gases, we can interpret a diffusion as a
collective motion of independent random walkers whose distribution density in space obeys the isotropic Gaussian distribution.

However in real physical or biological systems, the space is often separated into compartments by
membranes

that impede or facilitate the passage of the walker and create an anisotropy in the walkers’ collective motion.
From the physical point of view, a membrane is a thin slice of a material whose diffusivity
is different from the diffusivity of the environment.
Diffusions through membranes are observed in biological tissues where they
control the transport of ions, water molecules and gases, or in porous and composite materials.

A rigorous mathematical justification of the interpretation of a diffusion as a limit of scaled random walks is given by the
Functional Central Limit Theorem (FCLT, the Donsker–Prokhorov invariance principle).
For
m\geq 1
, let
\{\mathrm{e}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{e}_{m}\}
be a standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Consider a symmetric
random walk
Z=(Z(n))_{n\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
defined by identical one-step transition probabilities

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(Z(n+1)-Z(n)=\pm\mathrm{e}_{k}\Big|Z(0),\dots,Z(n)\Big)=\frac{1}{2m},\quad k=1,\dots m,\quad n\geq 0.

(1.1)

Then the FCLT yields the weak convergence in the uniform topology

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{Z([nt])}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)_{t\geq 0}\Rightarrow\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}W(t)\Big)_{t\geq 0},

(1.2)

where
W
is a standard
m
-dimensional Brownian motion.

In this paper we consider a novel class of scaling limits of random walks
in the presence of extended spatially non-homogeneous one- or two-sided barrier (membrane, interface). More precisely, in the
m
-dimensional
space we consider an semi-permeable hyperplane that separates the space into two half-space compartments.
The random walker will perform the symmetric random walk outside of the membrane, however the probability of the passage through the membrane
will depend on the hitting position of the membrane by the walker and on its incoming direction. Thus the probability to penetrate the
membrane will depend of the fact whether the walker has reached it from the “right” of from the “left”.
Furthermore we will assume that the membrane has a spatially regular structure according to one of the following setting. First,
we assume that the penetration probabilities are periodic in space, so that the membrane
reminds of a two-sided fabric. Alternatively, the membrane can imitate a “random” perforated surface so that the
penetration probabilities are iid random variables. We will show the scaled limit of such Markov chains
is a skew Brownian motion
in the direction perpendicular to the membrane.
The other coordinates converge to a standard Brownian motion, maybe, with a singular drift given by the local time of the first
coordinate at the origin.

The proofs of these results are purely probabilistic and employ methods of homogenization and dynamics of singular differential equations.
Our results give a path-wise picture of the diffusion through a two-side semi-permeable interface. In the physical language this corresponds to the
Langevin–Smoluchowsky approach to diffusions. It should be emphasized that physical papers (see, e.g., Novikov et al.
[

, Grebenkov et al.
[

]
, Moutal and Grebenkov
[

]
and references therein) devoted to similar problems use analytical
methods, mainly the analysis of the Fokker–Planck equation.

Two-sided periodic membrane: the model and the main result

Let
m\geq 2
and let
\{\mathrm{e}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{e}_{m}\}
be a standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Consider a Markov chain
Z=\{Z(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
that behaves as
a simple random walk outside of the hyperplane
H:=\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
, i.e., for all
k=1,\dots,m

\mathbf{P}\Big(Z({n+1})=z\pm\mathrm{e}_{k}\Big|Z(n)=z\Big)=\frac{1}{2m},\ z\notin H.

(2.1)

For each
n\geq 1
, we denote the first coordinate of
the process
Z
by
X
, and the other
(m-1)
coordinates by
Y
so that

Z=(X,Y)
.

We will interpret
H
as a semipermeable two-sided membrane that may let a particle pass from one half-space
to another with certain probabilities that can depend on the crossing direction. Moreover, the particle can
“slide”
along the membrane.

The membrane has to be homogeneous, i.e., the transition
probabilities are periodic in space.

Notice that if the membrane is two-sided, then
Z
is not a Markov chain, generally. Indeed,
its position upon leaving the membrane is determined by both current the location on the membrane and on the particle’s incoming direction.
Hence, in order to introduce a Markov structure
we have to enlarge the state space by splitting the membrane
H
into two parts
\mathcal{H}^{-}
and
\mathcal{H}^{+}
corresponding to its “right” and “left” sides.

To formalize the setting, we consider the Markov chain
\mathcal{Z}=(\mathcal{Z}(n))_{n\geq 0}

on the state space

\displaystyle\{\dots,-3,-2,-1,-0,+0,1,2,3,\dots\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}.

(2.2)

We also write
\mathcal{Z}=(\mathcal{X},Y)
, so that the process
Y
coincides with the process
Y
of the original random walk
Z
whereas the process

\mathcal{X}
is defined on the enlarged space
\{\dots,-3,-2,-1,-0,+0,1,2,3,\dots\}
.

Denote the “right” and “left” hand side of the membrane by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{-}:=\{-0\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1},\quad\mathcal{H}^{+}:=\{+0\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1},

(2.3)

and we set
\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{H}^{-}\cup\mathcal{H}^{+}
.

We assume that transition probabilities of
\mathcal{Z}

outside of
\mathcal{H}
satisfy
(
2.1
), namely

\mathbf{P}\Big(\mathcal{Z}({n+1})=z\pm\mathrm{e}_{k}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{Z}(n)=z\Big)=\frac{1}{2m},\ z\notin\mathcal{H},

(2.4)

where we agree that for each
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}

\displaystyle(1,y)-\mathrm{e}_{1}=(+0,y)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad(-1,y)+\mathrm{e}_{1}=(-0,y),

(2.5)

\displaystyle(+0,y)+\mathrm{e}_{1}=(1,y)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad(-0,y)+\mathrm{e}_{1}=(1,y),

\displaystyle(+0,y)-\mathrm{e}_{1}=(-1,y)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad(-0,y)-\mathrm{e}_{1}=(-1,y).

Suppose also that the perturbed random walk does not stay on the membrane, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(\mathcal{Z}({n+1})\in\{\pm 1\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{Z}(n)=z\Big)=1,\quad z\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.6)

However it is allowed that upon hitting the membrane (from the left or from the right) the particle can “slide”
along the membrane, so that its
y
-coordinate changes.
We assume that transition probabilities from
\mathcal{H}
have periodic structure.

Notation.

For
k_{2},\dots,k_{m}\geq 1
fixed, let
U
denote the box in
\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
defined by

\displaystyle U:=[0,k_{2}-1]\times\cdots\times[0,k_{m}-1].

(2.7)

For
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
,
y=(y_{2},\dots,y_{m})
, and
j\in U
,
j=(j_{2},\dots,j_{m})

\displaystyle y\equiv j\quad\text{if}\quad y_{i}\ (\textrm{mod}\ k_{i})=j_{i},\ i=2,\dots,m.

(2.8)

For each
j\in U
we set

\displaystyle H_{j}^{\pm}:=\{(\pm 0,y)\in H^{\pm}\colon y\equiv j\}.

(2.9)

Without loss of generality let us assume that the random walk
\mathcal{Z}
starts on the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}(0)=\pm 0
.

The following are our key assumptions concerning the transition probabilities of the random walk in the membrane.

A
{}_{\text{periodic}}
. Periodicity of the transition probabilities. We assume that there exist
k_{2},\dots,k_{m}\geq 1
such that for all

l_{2},\dots,l_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
, for all
z_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, for all
z_{1}\in\{-1,+1\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(\mathcal{Z}({n+1})=z_{1}

\displaystyle\Big|\,\mathcal{Z}(n)=z_{0}\Big)

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\Big(\mathcal{Z}(n+1)=z_{1}+k_{2}l_{2}\mathrm{e}_{2}+\cdots+k_{m}l_{m}\mathrm{e}_{m}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{Z}(n)=z_{0}+k_{2}l_{2}\mathrm{e}_{2}+\cdots+k_{m}l_{m}\mathrm{e}_{m}\Big).

A
γ
. To describe the transitions through the membrane, we denote by
0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\cdots

the successive arrivals of
\mathcal{Z}
to
\mathcal{H}
or, equivalently, of

\mathcal{X}
to
\{-0,+0\}
.

On the finite state space
\{-0,+0\}\times U

we consider an auxiliary embedded process
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}=(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
as follows.
We set

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Y}}(n)=\begin{cases}(-0,j),\ \text{ if }Y(\tau_{n})\equiv j\in U\text{ and }X(\tau_{n}+1)=-1,\\
(+0,j),\ \text{ if }Y(\tau_{n})\equiv j\in U\text{ and }X(\tau_{n}+1)=1.\end{cases}

(2.11)

It follows from the strong Markov property of
\mathcal{Z}
that
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
is a Markov chain.
It is easy to see that all the states of the set
\{-0,+0\}\times U
are connected for
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Hence there is a unique stationary distribution

\displaystyle\pi=\{\pi_{\{-0\}\times j},\pi_{\{+0\}\times j}\}_{j\in U}

(2.12)

of
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
on
\{-0,+0\}\times U

By the strong law of large numbers for Markov chains, for each

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}(k)=\{\pm 0\}\times j)=\pi_{\{\pm 0\}\times j}\ \ \text{a.s.}

(2.13)

With the help of the stationary distribution
\pi
we introduce the
effective permeability

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\in U}(\pi_{\{+0\}\times j}-\pi_{\{-0\}\times j})\in[-1,1].

(2.14)

A
c
. Finally, we describe the “slides” along the membrane. We denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm,j}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{E}\Big[Y(1)-Y(0)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{X}(0)=\pm 0,Y(0)=j\Big]\in\mathbb{R}^{m-1},\quad j\in U,

(2.15)

the mean slide sizes along the “right” of the “left” side of the membrane,
and assume that they are finite.
Introduce the

as

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{E}_{\pi}\Big[Y(1)-Y(0)\Big]=\sum_{j\in U}\Big(\pi_{\{+0\}\times j}\cdot\alpha_{+,j}+\pi_{\{-0\}\times j}\cdot\alpha_{-,j}\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{m-1}.

(2.16)

Now we are ready to formulate the main result of the paper.
Let
W_{X}
and
W_{Y}
be independent standard Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{R}^{m-1}

For

defined in (

\displaystyle X^{\gamma}(t)=\gamma L(t)+W_{X}(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.17)

at zero, see Harrison and Shepp
[
10

. Other characterizations

Furthermore, let

\displaystyle Y^{c}(t):=cL(t)+W_{Y}(t),\quad t\geq 0,

(2.18)

i.e., the process

is a

-dimensional Brownian motion that slides in the direction

Note that (

is already determined in (

For the original process
Z=(X,Y)
, denote the rescaled continuous time processes

\displaystyle X_{n}(t):=\frac{X([nt])}{\sqrt{n}},\quad Y_{n}(t):=\frac{Y([nt])}{\sqrt{n}},\quad t\geq 0.

(2.19)

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Z=(X,Y)
be a perturbed random walk satisfying the preceding assumptions. Then for any initial value

Z(0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
the
weak convergence
holds true:

\displaystyle(X_{n},Y_{n})\Rightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(X^{\gamma},Y^{c}),\quad n\to\infty,

(2.20)

where the processes
X^{\gamma}
and
Y^{c}
are defined in (
2.17
) and (
2.18
), and
\gamma
and
c
are defined in
(
2.14
) and (
2.15
) respectively.

The crux of the Theorem is transparent. Away from the membrane
H
, the limiting process
(X^{\gamma},Y^{c})
coincides with the
standard
m
-dimensional Brownian motion
(W_{X},W_{Y})/\sqrt{m}
. The perturbation of the transition probabilities on the two-sided membrane
results in the appearance of a singular drift in the direction
x
perpendicular to the membrane.
Hence the
x
-coordinate of the limiting process becomes a skew Brownian motion with the effective permeability parameter
\gamma\in[-1,1]
.
The
y
-coordinates also become a singular drift in the effective sliding direction
c\in\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
. This drift equals
zero as long as
the limiting process stays away from the membrane. However upon hitting the membrane, the limiting process performs a singular “sliding”
in the direction
c
controlled by the local time at zero of the
x
-coordinate.
Note that in the limit, the two-sided membrane structure disappears, and
the limiting process
(X^{\gamma},Y^{c})
is Markov in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Walsh’s Brownian motion and its martingale characterizations

Walsh’s Brownian motion (WBM) was introduced in the Epilogue of Walsh
[
36
]
as a diffusion on
d
rays on a two-dimensional plane
with the common origin.
On each ray WBM, is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion that however can change the ray upon hitting the origin, i.e., each ray is characterized
by a weight
p_{i}>0
,
i=1,\dots,d
,
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{d}=1,
that heuristically can be understood as a probability to go on the ray number
i
.
Hence, the conventional WBM is a process
X
on the plane expressed in polar coordinates as
X=(R_{t},\theta_{t})
where
R
is the reflecting
Brownian motion and
\theta
is a random process taking values on the set of

d
angles on
[0,2\pi)
and being constant during each excursion of
R
from
0
. This representation has been used in various
works on WBM including Barlow et al.
[
2
]
,
Freidlin and Sheu

[
5
]
,
Hajri

[
8
]
,
Hajri and Touhami

[
9
]
, and
Karatzas and Yan

[
15
]
.

In this paper prefer to embed the WBM into a
d
-dimensional Euclidean space as it was indicated in Walsh
[
36
]
.
To this purpose, let
E
be the union of non-negative coordinate half-axes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle E=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon x_{i}\geq 0\text{ and }x_{i}x_{j}=0,\ i\neq j,\ i,j=1,\dots,d\}.

(3.1)

We fix probabilities
p_{1},\dots,p_{d}>0
,
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{d}=1
, and also denote
q_{i}=1-p_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
.
Let also
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbf{P})
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual hypotheses. Then adopting
the Markovian characterization of the WBM from Barlow et al.
[
2
]
, we say that WBM is a continuous
Feller Markov process
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
on

(\max_{s\leq t}B_{s}-B_{t},\max_{s\leq t}B_{s})/\sqrt{m}
, which
has the same law as
(|B(\cdot)|,L^{0}(\cdot))/\sqrt{m}
by Lévy’s theorem, see, e.g.
[
14
, Corollary 19.3]
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

The sequence
\{(X^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),M^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),\nu^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot))_{j\in U}\}_{n\geq 1}

is weakly relatively
compact in
D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{6|U|})
and any limit point is a continuous processes.

Proof.

Notice that the modulus of continuity (in the uniform topology)
of any
X^{j,\pm}_{n}
is dominated by doubled modulus of continuity of

\{S(n\cdot)/\sqrt{n}\}
, and
the modulus of continuity
of any
M^{j,\pm}_{n}
is dominated by doubled modulus of continuity of

X^{j,\pm}_{n}
.
The third coordinate is the difference of the first two. Hence the proof of the Lemma follows from Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

To show convergence
\{(X_{n}^{j,\pm})_{j\in U}\}_{n\geq 0}

Due to Lemma

\Big\{\Big(X^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),M^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),\nu^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}\Big\}_{n\geq 1}

\Big(X^{j,\pm}(\cdot),M^{j,\pm}(\cdot),\nu^{j,\pm}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}
.

Let us check the conditions a)–d) of Theorem
3.2
for the process
\sqrt{m}\Big(X^{j,\pm}(\cdot),M^{j,\pm}(\cdot),\nu^{j,\pm}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}
.

a) It follows from the construction that
X^{j,\pm}(t)\geq 0
,

. Moreover,
only one of these processes may be positive at any fixed time.

b) and d) The processes
\nu^{j,\pm}(\cdot)
are non-decreasing a.s. and
\nu^{j,\pm}(0)=0
.
Lemma
4.2

\displaystyle\Big(|X(\cdot)|,\nu(\cdot)\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big(|B(\cdot)|,L^{0}(\cdot)\Big),

(4.17)

where

\displaystyle\nu(t)=\sum_{j\in U}(\nu^{j,-}(t)+\nu^{j,+}(t)).

(4.18)

Since
\int_{0}^{T}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(B(t)=0)\,\mathrm{d}t=0

\int_{0}^{T}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(|B(t)|>0)\,\mathrm{d}L^{0}(t)=0
a.s. for any

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(|X(t)|=0)\,\mathrm{d}t=0,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(|X(t)|>0)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(t)=0

almost surely.

c) It follows from the construction that

\displaystyle X^{j,\pm}(t)=M^{j,\pm}(t)+\nu^{j,\pm}(t)\ \text{a.s.}

(4.20)

for all
j\in U,t\geq 0

To show that

\displaystyle\nu^{j,\pm}(t)=\pi^{\pm}_{j}\nu(t)\ \text{a.s.}

(4.21)

we recall the strong law of large numbers (
2.13
) for Markov chains.
For any

defined in (

\displaystyle\frac{\nu_{n}^{j,\pm}(t)}{\sum_{k\in U}\nu_{n}^{k,\pm}(t)}=\frac{1}{L([nt])}\sum_{k=0}^{L([nt])}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(\hat{\mathcal{Y}}(k)=\{\pm 0\}\times j)\to\pi^{\pm}_{j}\ \text{a.s.}

(4.22)

where

is defined in (

The processes

.
Since the jumps of each

are uniformly bounded, the limits

are local martingales with respect to

due

[

. Moreover, the limit processes are continuous due to Lemma

.

It is left to show that

\displaystyle\langle M^{j,\pm}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{t}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X^{j,\pm}(s)>0)\,\mathrm{d}s\ \text{a.s.\ for }j\in U,\ t\geq 0.

(4.23)

By Skorokhod’s representation theorem there is a probability space and
the copies

\displaystyle\Big\{\Big(\tilde{X}^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),\tilde{M}^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),\tilde{\nu}^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}\Big\}_{n\geq 1}\text{ and }\Big\{\Big(\tilde{X}^{j,\pm}(\cdot),\tilde{M}^{j,\pm}(\cdot),\tilde{\nu}^{j,\pm}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}\Big\}

\displaystyle\Big\{\Big(X^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),M^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot),\nu^{j,\pm}_{n}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}\Big\}_{n\geq 1}\text{ and }\Big\{\Big(X^{j,\pm}(\cdot),M^{j,\pm}(\cdot),\nu^{j,\pm}(\cdot)\Big)_{j\in U}\Big\}

converges uniformly over

5
One-sided membrane with ergodic properties

The same method of decomposition of the perturbed Markov chain into a sum of excursions
combined with the strong law of large numbers
(
2.13
) can be applied for the analysis of a one-sided membrane
that has ergodic properties.

As in Section
2
, let
m\geq 2
and let
\{\mathrm{e}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{e}_{m}\}
be a
standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Consider a Markov chain
Z=(X,Y)
on
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
that behaves as
a simple random walk outside of the hyperplane
H:=\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
, i.e.,
(
2.1
) holds true.

Let
\{p_{y}\}_{y\in H}\subset[0,1]
.
Now we interpret
H
as a semipermeable non-homogeneous
membrane that may let a particle into one half-space with
probabilities
\{p_{y}\}_{y\in H}
. More precisely, we assume that
for each
z=(0,y)\in H
:

\displaystyle p_{y}=\mathbf{P}\Big(Z(n+1)=z+\mathrm{e}_{1}\Big|Z(n)=z\Big)=1-\mathbf{P}\Big(Z(n+1)=z-\mathrm{e}_{1}\,\Big|\,Z(n)=z\Big).

(5.1)

Note that the particle leaves the membrane in the direction orthogonal to
H
, i.e.,
Y(n+1)=Y(n)
for
Z(n)\in H
with probability 1, and hence there is
no slide along the membrane.

We assume that the membrane has the following ergodic property:

A
{}_{\text{SLLN}}(\beta)
: there is
\beta>0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{A\to\infty}\frac{1}{|V(A,o)|}\sum_{y\in V(A,o)}p_{y}=:\bar{p}\in[0,1],

(5.2)

where the limit is taken over all cubes
V(A,o)\subseteq H
of volume
|V(A,o)|

with side size larger than
A
and whose centre
o
is within distance
A^{\beta}
from the origin.

We give two clarifying examples for the assumption
A
{}_{\text{SLLN}}(\beta)
.

Example 5.1
.

Assume that the family
\{p_{y}\}_{y\in H}
has a periodic structure: there are
k_{2},\dots,k_{m}\geq 1
such that
for all
l_{2},\dots,l_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
and for all
y\in H

\displaystyle p_{y}=p_{y+k_{2}l_{2}\mathrm{e}_{2}+\cdots+k_{m}l_{m}\mathrm{e}_{m}}.

(5.3)

Then
\{p_{y}\}
clearly satisfy assumption
A
{}_{\text{SLLN}}(\beta)
for any
\beta>0
with

\displaystyle\bar{p}=\frac{1}{k_{2}\cdots k_{m}}\sum_{i_{2},\dots,i_{m}=1}^{k_{2},\dots,k_{m}}p_{(i_{2},\dots,i_{m})}.

(5.4)

Example 5.2
.

Let
\{p_{y}\}_{y\in H}
be i.i.d. random variables with values in
[0,1]
defined on a probability space
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathscr{F}^{\prime},\mathbf{P}^{\prime})
. Then for each fixed
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\prime}

the family
\{p_{y}(\omega^{\prime})\}
defines a random “environment”. Then
the assumption
A
{}_{\text{SLLN}}(\beta)
with
\beta>0
is satified with

\displaystyle\bar{p}=\mathbf{E}^{\prime}p_{y}.

(5.5)

To see this, let
\beta>0
, and let
V(A,o)
denote a cube with the size
A\in\mathbb{N}
and the centre at
o\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(A,o)|}\sum_{y\in V(A,o)}p_{y}\not\to\bar{p}\Big)=\mathbf{P}\Big(\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{A=k}^{\infty}\bigcup_{|o|\leq L^{\beta}}\Big\{\frac{1}{|V(A,o)|}\Big|\sum_{y\in V(A,o)}(p_{y}-\bar{p})\Big|>\frac{1}{m}\Big\}\Big).

(5.6)

For each
m\geq 1
and
A\geq 1
we apply Hoeffding’s inequality, see Chapter III, §5.8 in Petrov
[
26
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(\bigcup_{|o|\leq A^{\beta}}\Big\{\frac{1}{|V(A,o)|}

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{y\in V(A,o)}(p_{y}-\bar{p})\Big|>\frac{1}{m}\Big\}\Big)\leq(2A^{\beta})^{m-1}\mathbf{P}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(A,o)|}\Big|\sum_{y\in V(A,o)}(p_{y}-\bar{p})\Big|>\frac{1}{m}\Big)

(5.7)

\displaystyle\leq 2(2A^{\beta})^{m-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2|V(A,o)|/m^{2}}=2(2A^{\beta})^{m-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2A^{m-1}/m^{2}}.

Hence, the probability in (
5.6
) equals to 0.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let assumption
A
{}_{\text{SLLN}}(\beta)
holds true for
\beta>1
. Then for any initial value
Z(0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
the
weak convergence holds true:

\displaystyle(X_{n},Y_{n})\Rightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(X^{\gamma},Y^{0}),\quad n\to\infty,

(5.8)

where
X^{\gamma}
and
Y^{0}
are defined in (
2.17
) and (
2.18
),
with
\gamma=2\bar{p}-1
, and
c=0
.

Proof.

1. Without loss of generality assume that
Z(0)=0
. As in Section
4
we
decompose the Markov chain
Z=(X,Y)
into the “left” and the “right” excursions. Since the membrane is one-sided, and there is no need of
introducing the set
U
, the notation of Section
4
simplifies significantly. Similarly to (
4.2
) we define

\displaystyle X^{+}(n)

\displaystyle:=X(n)\cdot 1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(n)>0),

(5.9)

\displaystyle X^{-}(n)

\displaystyle:=|X(n)|\cdot 1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(n)<0),

so that

\displaystyle X(n)=X^{+}(n)-X^{-}(n),\quad n\geq 0.

(5.10)

Then we decompose these processes similarly to (
4.3
) as

\displaystyle X^{\pm}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(X^{\pm}(k)-X^{\pm}(k-1)\Big)\cdot 1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X^{\pm}(k-1)>0)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X^{\pm}(k-1)=0,X^{\pm}(k)=1)

(5.11)

\displaystyle=:M^{\pm}(n)+L^{\pm}(n),\qquad M^{\pm}(0)=L^{\pm}(0)=0.

The processes
M^{\pm}
are martingales, and
L^{\pm}
are non-decreasing processes.
Recall the processes
X_{n}(\cdot)
and
Y_{n}(\cdot)
defined in (
2.19
), and define additionally the scaled processes

X^{\pm}_{n}
,
M^{\pm}_{n}
,
\nu^{\pm}
similarly to (
4.7
) (omitting the index
j
), so that

\displaystyle X_{n}(t)=X^{+}(t)-X^{-}(t),\quad M_{n}(t)=M^{+}(t)-M^{-}(t),\quad\nu_{n}(t):=\nu^{+}_{n}(t)+\nu^{-}_{n}(t).

(5.12)

Analogously to reasoning of the previous section (Propositions
4.1
and
4.4
), we
have that
the sequence

\displaystyle\{(X^{+}_{n},X^{-}_{n},X_{n},M^{+}_{n},M^{-}_{n},M_{n},\nu^{+}_{n},\nu^{-}_{n},\nu_{n},Y_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}

(5.13)

is weakly relatively compact
in
D(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{10})

each its limit point
(X^{+},X^{-},X,M^{+},M^{-},M,\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\nu,Y)
is
continuous, and

\displaystyle X(t)=X^{+}(t)-X^{-}(t),\quad M(t)=M^{+}(t)-M^{-}(t),\quad\nu(t)=\nu^{+}(t)+\nu^{-}(t),

(5.14)

where
M^{+}
and
M^{-}
are local martingales with the brackets

\displaystyle\langle M^{\pm}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{t}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X^{\pm}(s)>0)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5.15)

Moreover, the process
\sqrt{m}|X|=\sqrt{m}(X^{+}+X^{-})
is a standard reflected Brownian motion,

\sqrt{m}\nu
is its local time at 0, and the process
\sqrt{m}Y
is a standard
(m-1)
-dimensional Brownian motion independent of
X
.

Hence to prove Theorem
5.3
it remains to show that

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{+}(t)}{\nu(t)}=\bar{p}\quad\text{a.s.\ for all $t>0$}.

(5.16)

To verify this, it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu^{+}_{n}(t)}{\nu_{n}(t)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{L^{+}([nt])}{L([nt])}=\bar{p}\quad\text{a.s.\ for all $t>0$}.

(5.17)

2. Let
\tau_{k}
be the moment of
k
th visit of
X
to 0,
k\geq 0
,
\tau_{0}=0
, so that
Z(\tau_{k})=(0,Y(\tau_{k}))
.
By the strong Markov property we have

\displaystyle\Law(\tau_{k}-(\tau_{k-1}+1))=\Law(\tau|X(0)=1),\quad k\geq 1,

(5.18)

and

\displaystyle\Law(Y(\tau_{k})-Y(\tau_{k-1}))=\Law(Y(\tau)|X(0)=1,Y(0)=0),\quad k\geq 1,

(5.19)

where
\tau
is the first return time to zero of
X
,

\displaystyle\tau=\inf\{k\geq 1\colon X(k)=0\}.

(5.20)

It is well known that
\tau_{k}<\infty
a.s. and
\tau_{k}\to+\infty
a.s. as
k\to\infty
.
Hence

\displaystyle Y(\tau_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(Y(\tau_{k})-Y(\tau_{k-1})\Big),\ \ {n\geq 0},

(5.21)

is a random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}

whose jumps have the probability distribution
\Law(Y(\tau)|X(0)=1,Y(0)=0)
.

We claim that

\displaystyle\frac{Y(\tau_{n})}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\to}}S,\quad n\to\infty,

(5.22)

where
S
is a
1
-stable random variable on
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
with the characteristic function
\mathbf{E}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle u,S\rangle}=\mathrm{e}^{-|u|/\sqrt{m}}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
.
Indeed, for each
n\geq 1
consider the symmetric random walk
\tilde{Z}=(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
on
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
starting at
\tilde{Z}(0)=(n,0,\dots,0)
. Denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(5.23)

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{1}

\displaystyle=\inf\{k\geq 1\colon\tilde{X}(k)=n-1\},

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{n}

\displaystyle=\inf\{k\geq 1\colon\tilde{X}(n)=0\}.

Then clearly

\displaystyle(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n},Y(\tau_{1}),\dots,Y(\tau_{n}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(\tilde{\tau}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\tau}_{n},\tilde{Y}(\tilde{\tau}_{1}),\dots,\tilde{Y}(\tilde{\tau}_{n}))

(5.24)

By the functional central limit theorem,

\displaystyle\Law\Big(\sqrt{m}\frac{\tilde{Z}([n^{2}\,\cdot])}{n}\Big|\tilde{Z}(0)=(n,0,\dots,0)\Big)\Rightarrow\Law\Big(W(\cdot)\Big|W(0)=(1,0\dots,0)\Big),

(5.25)

where
W
is a standard
m
-dimensional Brownian motion, and thus

\displaystyle\Big(n^{2}\tilde{\tau}_{n},n^{-1}\sqrt{m}\tilde{Y}(\tilde{\tau}_{n})\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\to}}\Big(\tau^{W},\big(W_{2}(\tau^{W}),\dots,W_{m}(\tau^{W})\big)\Big),

(5.26)

where
\tau^{W}=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon W_{1}(t)=0\}
. It is well known that
(W_{2}(\tau^{W}),\dots,W_{m}(\tau^{W}))
is a 1-stable random vector,
see, e.g., Theorem II.1.16 in Bass
[
3
]
.

3. With (
5.22
) in hand, we apply Theorem 1.3 from Dolgopyat et al.
[
4
]
, that states that
under the assumption
A
{}_{\text{SLLN}}(\beta)
with
\beta>1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{Y(\tau_{k})}\to\bar{p}\ \ \text{a.s.},\quad n\to\infty.

(5.27)

4. Now we are able to finish the proof of (
5.16
)
Then

\displaystyle L^{+}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(k-1)=0,X(k)=1)=\sum_{k\colon\tau_{k}\leq n-1}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1),

(5.28)

\displaystyle L(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(k-1)=0)=\max\{k\geq 1\colon\tau_{k}\leq n-1\}.

Since
\tau_{k}\to+\infty
a.s.,
L(n)\to\infty
a.s. as well as at least one of the processes
L^{+}(\cdot)
and
L^{-}(\cdot)
.

Consider the process

\displaystyle Q^{+}(n):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1),

(5.29)

and note that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{L^{+}(n)}{L(n)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q^{+}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big(1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1)-p_{Y(\tau_{k})}\Big)+\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{Y(\tau_{k})}

(5.30)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big(1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1)-p_{Y(\tau_{k})}\Big)+\bar{p}.

The process

\displaystyle V(n):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big(1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1)-p_{Y(\tau_{k})}\Big)

(5.31)

is a martingale difference with

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Big[1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1)-p_{Y(\tau_{k})}\Big]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{E}\Big[1\!\!\!\;{\rm I}(X(\tau_{k}+1)=1)-p_{Y(\tau_{k})}\Big]^{2}\leq 2.

(5.32)

Hence by the strong law of large numbers for martingales (Theorem 8b in Chapter II, §3 of Gikhman and Skorokhod
[
6
]
) we have convergence

\displaystyle\frac{V(n)}{n}\to 0\ \ \text{a.s.},\quad n\to\infty,

(5.33)

what finishes the proof of Theorem
5.3
.
∎
