Adaptive Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization
Nicholas R. Allgood, Ajinkya Borle, Charles K. Nicholas

Abstract
One of the major benefits of quantum computing is the potential to resolve complex computational problems faster than can be done by classical methods. There are many prototype-based clustering methods in use today, and selection of the starting nodes for the center points is often done randomly. For prototype-based clustering algorithms, this could lead to much slower convergence times. One of the causes of this may be prototype-based clustering accepting a local minima as a valid solution when there are possibly better solutions. Quantum computing, specifically quantum annealing, offers a solution to these problems by mapping the initial centroid problem to an Ising Hamiltonian where over time the lowest energy in the spectrum correlates to a valid, but better solution. A first approach to this problem utilizing quantum annealing was known as Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization (QOCI), but this approach has some limitations both in results and performance. We will present a modification of QOCI known as Adaptive Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization (AQOCI) which addresses many of the limitations in QOCI. The results presented are comparable to those obtained using classical techniques as well as being superior to those results found using QOCI.



1
Introduction

Cluster analysis is the study of grouping related data together for classification and other purposes. There are many types of algorithms to cluster data, but we will be specifically focusing on
prototype-based clustering
. Prototype-based clustering relies on creating a prototype of what a cluster could look like, and then proceeding to converge until a final cluster is created. All clustering algorithms must start from somewhere, and one of the key differences in prototype-based clustering is they start from a given center point or
centroid
and proceed to cluster and update the centroid and the surrounding data incrementally. Well-known clustering algorithms such as
k
-means and
k
-medoids are prototype-based clustering algorithms and have been used with great success across multiple domains. In a detailed literature review on prototype-based clustering we have found that the largest majority of these algorithms are initialized with a random centroid. Sometimes a random centroid can be ideal but other times it is detrimental to the performance and accuracy of the clustering algorithm. To get the best and most accurate groups of related data, we must have accurate center points to formulate a cluster, whether it be a computed mean or a sample from the data. Many clustering methods employ an iterative verification of the selected center point to confirm it is the best candidate to be the centroid.
[
11
]

We could perhaps save some computational overhead if we begin the clustering algorithm with the better possible centroids computed prior to forming the cluster. In classical cases, this would be computationally expensive, more so than using a random process to select the starting nodes. With a superposition of states in a quantum computer, we could offload this computationally expensive procedure to calculate a better set of centroids. One hidden benefit is that even if a performance increase is not gained, knowing that our computation is in the ground energy state we avoid being stuck in a local minima.

Cluster Analysis Metrics

Inertia:

Sum of squared errors that pertains to the cluster. One generally wants this value to be as low possible.

Silhouette Score:

Also known as the
Silhouette Coefficient
, a metric used to calculate the qualify of a clustering algorithm. Uses values ranged from
-1
to
+1
.

Homogeneity:

Used to measure how similar each of the samples are in a cluster. Uses values
-1
to
1
.

Completeness:

Measures how similar samples are placed together by the clustering algorithm. Uses values
-1
to
1
.

V-Measure:

Also known as the
Normalised Mutual Information Score
, is the average of homogeneity and completeness scores.

2
Related Work

Cluster analysis is a class of algorithms that group a set of related objects together where items are closer together the more strongly they are related. These groups are formally known as
clusters
and there are a wide variety of metrics that can be used to determine object similarity. Cluster analysis is widely used in many domains for a variety of uses such as classification and machine learning.

2.1
K-Means

k
-means clustering is a
prototype-based
clustering algorithm that is widely used and researched where
n
observations are partitioned into
k
clusters where each observation belongs the cluster with the closest mean. Prototype-based clustering all start with a center point known as the
centroid
and
k
-means this is the mean of the entire grouping of data. This centroid is used and updated as the
k
-means algorithm converges and the initial centroid of all clusters are random. The
n
partitions are partitioned in such a way that the variance inside of each cluster is minimized. By default,
k
-means uses squared Euclidean distances in lieu of non-squared Euclidean distances.

While there are many variants and formulations of
k
-means, we will be discussing only the standard version known as naive
k
-means. Mathematically,
k
-means is defined as follows: given a set of observations (
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, …,
x_{n}
), we partition the
n
observations into
k\leq n
sets
S=\{S_{1},S_{2},...,S_{k}\}
so to minimize the variance within each of the sets
S_{i}
. Formally:

\min_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{x\in S_{i}}||x-\mu_{i}||^{2}=\min_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\mathrm{Var}(S_{i})

(1)

where
\mu_{i}
is the mean of the points in
S_{i}
.

The full algorithm consists of three pieces, namely initialization, assignment, and update.

1.

Initialization

Let us randomly choose
k
observations from our data sets and use these as our initial centroids
m_{1}^{(1)},...,m_{k}^{(1)}

2.

Assignment

Given our initial centroids
m_{1}^{(1)},...,m_{k}^{(1)}
, assign each observation to the cluster with the nearest centroid in terms of least squared Euclidean distance.

S_{i}^{(t)}=\big\{x_{p}:||x_{p}-m_{i}^{(t)}||^{2}\leq||x_{p}-m_{j}^{(t)}||^{2}\ \forall j,1\leq j,\leq k\big\},

(2)

where every
x_{p}
is assigned to exactly one
S^{(t)}
, even when the possibility of being assigned to multiple
S^{(t)}
exists.

3.

Update

This step recalculates the centroids (means) for observations to each cluster:

m_{i}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{|S_{i}^{(t)}|}\sum_{x_{j}\in S_{i}^{(t)}x_{j}}

(3)

The algorithm has fully converged when the assignments no longer change. While there are variants that address this, one major shortcoming of naive
k
-means is there is no guarantee of finding a global optimum. There are a variety of heuristics that can quickly find a local-minima for
k
-means, however this is only a minima when compared to neighboring candidate solutions that are not over the entire problem space. As such
k
-means falls victim to a local-minima problem where the data plateaus which can give less than optimal clustering results.
[
16
]
.

2.2
Quantum Annealing

Quantum annealing is a method of quantum computing that relies on the adiabatic principle of the evolution of a quantum state over time. This is formally expressed as:

i\hslash\frac{d\ket{\psi}}{dt}=H\ket{\psi}

(4)

where
t
is time,
\psi
is the state of the system, and
\hslash
is Planck’s constant for which the exact value is not known, but for many practical purposes
\hslash=1
suffices.

A Hamiltonian is a mathematical model for expressing the total energy of a system and quantum annealing problems are expressed as a Hamiltonian with a minimization of energy as
H_{p}
. Once processed over time, our solution is an eigenvector with the lowest energy in
H_{p}
. The adiabatic principle states that as our time
T
tends towards
\inf
, we will be very close to the ground state of energy in
H_{p}
. Specifically we will be referencing an Ising Hamiltonian which (at the time of this writing) is the only Hamiltonian supported by D-Wave quantum annealers. Specifically the Ising Hamiltonian is defined as:

E=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}

(5)

where
E
is the sum over nearest neighbors
\langle ij\rangle
and J is an exchange constant that is positive.

2.3
Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization

Quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) is a combinatorial optimization problem that is functionally equivalent to an Ising Hamiltonian. The set of binary vectors of a fixed length
n>0
is denoted by
{B}^{n}
, where
B=\{0,1\}
is a set of binary values. Given a real upper triangular matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{nxn}
whose entries
Q_{ij}
define a weight for a pair of indices
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
within a binary vector. A function
f_{Q}:\mathbb{B}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
assigns a value to each binary vector:

f_{Q}(x)=x^{T}Qx=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}

(6)

With a QUBO problem, we wish to find a binary vector
x^{*}
that is minimal to
f_{Q}
such that:

x^{*}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}f_{Q}(x)

(7)

While the D-Wave quantum annealers support both Ising and QUBO formulations, for this work we chose to utilize a QUBO formulation.

2.4
Non-Negative Matrix Factorization

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) are a group of algorithms where a matrix
V
is factorized to two matrices
W
and
H
where all three matrices have no negative elements. Non-ngeative matrix factorization is often numerically approximated and utilized in a wide variety of domains. Matrix multiplication is implemented by computing the columns of vectors
V
as a linear combination of the column vectors in
W
with the coefficients supplied by the columns in matrix
H
. We are then able to state that each column of V can be computed as
v_{i}=Wh_{i}
where
v_{i}
is the
i
-th column vector of the product matrix V and
h_{i}
is the
i
-th column vector of the matrix
H
. The multiplication of the matrices will often result in dimensions of
W
and
H
being much lower than those of the product matrix. This property is foundational for how NMF works and the dimensions are much smaller than the original matrix
V
. Computing matrices
W
and
H
can be done in a variety of ways with a popular method being a multiplicative update method
[
14
]
.

2.5
Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization

Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization (QOCI) utilizes many ideas from non-negative matrix factorization but allows both positive and negative real numbers. Similar to non-negative matrix factorization, we utilize
V=WH
where
V
is the approximation of our data computed from the product of matrices
W
and
H
. Each column in matrix
W
represents cluster membership and matrix
H
is a binary matrix where a
1
in a single column is then multiplied with
W
resulting in
WH
where every column is a cluster and the entries in the matrix column are the centroids. It is through quadratic expansion we can derive our
linear
coefficients and our
quadratic
coefficients. Any constants that are created as part of the expansion are simply ignored in this formulation. The linear coefficients are those that correspond to a single variable and the quadratic coefficients are those that are paired along side multiple variables.

To get the correct centroids consistently a penalty function was introduced that made heavy use of
LaGrange Multipliers
in addition to relying on an implicit penalty applied when a quadratic equation was expanded. As a the annealing process happens, the penalty function puts extra weight on a solution that is determined to be less than optimal. Utilizing a QUBO formulation, we relied on a predictable style of quadratic expansion similar to
(a+b)^{2}=a^{2}+2ab+b^{2}
. This penalty is first applied to only the matrix
W
and known as a
linearization penalty
. A second penalty function is also introduced for the matrix
H
to ensure that a single
1
was in each column. For each penalty function, we have
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
where
\delta_{1}
is our linearization penalty function and
\delta_{2}
is our penalty function for matrix
H
.

The results are binary values read together are the result with the left most qubit being designated the
sign qubit
and the remaining qubits used to represent the value
[
4
]
. It will be assumed that the endianess for this part of the formulation will be from right to left, with the most significant qubit the left. The importance of this is that these will act as multipliers for the coefficients that are determined through quantum annealing.

An example using 4 qubits including the most significant as our sign bit, our expanded form would look similar to
-8q_{8}+4q_{4}+2q_{2}+q_{1}
where
n
in
q_{n}
refers to having
n
power of 2 as a label to our qubits. We can then express this more concisely:

\vartheta q_{j\theta}+\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\theta q_{j\theta}=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}2^{\theta}q_{j\theta}

(8)

where

\vartheta=\begin{cases}-2^{p+1},&two^{\prime}s\ complement\\
-2^{p+1}+2^{\Theta}&one^{\prime}s\ complement\\
\end{cases}

and
\Theta
is the maximum power of 2 based on the number of qubits necessary. As an example, if
[o,p]=[0,2]
and we need to represent
3
, then
q_{j4}=0,q_{j2}=1,q_{j1}=1
which gives us
w=2^{2}q_{j4}+2^{1}q_{j2}+2^{0}q_{j1}=3
.

3
Contribution

Adaptive Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization (AQOCI) is an attempt to address a few of the shortcomings of Quantum Optimized Centroid Initialization. AQOCI allows results to be interpreted as results in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in addition to allowing larger sample sizes to able to be accommodated. D-Wave limits how much time one gets on their quantum annealers using a free account which is still a major limitation, however, by utilizing an adaptive and iterative approach we were able to break the problem into smaller blocks of execution. Primary inspiration for this method came from reviewing the
Gauss-Seidel
and
Jacobi
methods for iteratively solving a system of linear equations. A similar iterative approach has been utilized to solve the heat equation
[
19
]
. The formulation we are proposing takes inspiration from Pollachini’s work, especially when they discuss scaling of the solution.

We start with our initial QUBO in a similar fashion to the QOCI formulation and then define a range of scaling values for our solution to be computed between. We manually define an
upper limit
and
lower limit
for this range and identify how many qubits will be used for represent these values. Scaling in itself is not sufficient as it does not address only having a result represented as an integer. To get a real value result a manual
offset
real value which is added to the polynomial expansion along to be utilized in conjunction with the scaling values. With the mentioned improvements, this changes our equation slightly as follows:

\lambda\big(v_{11}^{2}+v_{12}^{2}+v_{21}^{2}+v_{22}^{2}-2h_{11}v_{11}w_{11}...\big)+\textit{offset}

(9)

where
\lambda
is our scale value and
offset
is our offset value, both of which are applied to the polynomial expansion which is solved by a specified solver.

As utilized in the Gauss-Seidel and Jacobi methods we take an iterative approach to solving this linear system of equations. A completed QUBO is formulated with every iteration and subsequently submitted to the solver. Finding the number of iterations required is important to the overall optimality and we denote it by a tolerance value
t
. In much of the literature it is suggested that this value monitors the relative error of the problem at each
k
iterations shown as:

\epsilon^{k}\equiv\frac{||x^{(k)}-x||}{||x||}

(10)

where
x
being our substitution variables used in the polynomial expansions and
\epsilon\leq t
.
[
19
]
. For a small problem set with only a few equations this was ideal, however, in a much larger system of linear equations as such outlined by our QUBO formulation, we have found it has been more optimal to empirically determine a manual amount of iterations. There were several occurrences where depending on the data used, the
L2
norm would fluctuate in value and thus never satisfying our terminating condition of
\epsilon\leq t
. In addition, since this solution requires sending blocks of data to a specified solver, this could also quickly exceed the allowed time given if using a real quantum annealer. As mentioned previously, D-Wave has a limited set of time one can use on the free developer account on a monthly basis.

With each iteration, we obtain the results from the solver as a binary string and then interpret this as a real value. The real value at this stage is still equivalent to it’s integer value, so we need to do further post-processing on our pre-populated
scale
and
offset
lists. Our scale list is determined at first by our scale value
s
.

s=(upperLimit-lowerLimit)/(2^{bits}-1)

(11)

The upper limit and lower limit as previously mentioned, are predefined values that should fall within the minimum and maximum values we can represent with our working qubits. For example, if we have only 4 qubits to work with and assuming one of those qubits is our
sign
qubit, then we can only realistically use the remaining 3 qubits for our values. This limits us to a range of
-8
to
+7
.
Our scale list is a range from
s
to the number of
x
substitution variables. The offset list also contains a range of values the length of the number of
x
substitution variables, however, it initialize all of them with the value of our predefined lower limit.

Within an iteration we have to parse real values using our binary string, scale list, and offset list. In the event our binary string is 0 and our offset is equal to our current lower limit, we set a new scale value by taking a scale value from our scale list and divide by a scale factor. In our experiments our scale factor was set to
2
. If the value of the binary string was equal to our upper limit and our upper limit is equal to
2^{bits}-1*scale+lowerLimit
, we do a similar approach with adjusting our current value in our scale list divided by the provided scale factor. For the offset list, we make an adjustment by performing
(binstr*scale)+offset-(binstr)*newScale
. For all other cases, we continue with our division of our scale value by the scale factor, and for the offset we adjust it by performing
(binstr*scale)+offset-(binstr)*newScale
. More explicitly, we can express this as follows:

\begin{cases}\alpha=\alpha/\beta,o=o&\text{if binstr }=0,o=ll\\
\alpha=\alpha/\beta,o=b*\alpha+o-b*o&\text{if b}=2^{bits}-1,ul=2^{bits}-1*\alpha+ll\\
\alpha=\alpha/\beta,o=b*\alpha+o-3*\alpha&otherwise\\
\end{cases}

(12)

where
ll
is our lower limit,
ul
is our upper limit,
o
is our offset list,
\alpha
is our scale value,
\beta
is our scale factor, and
b
is our binary string returned by the solver.

4
Results

Our experiments utilized random Gaussian blobs and random samples from a malware research data set known as MOTIF
[
10
]
. The Gaussian blobs were generated in two dimensions and the MOTIF data was processed with Principal Component Analysis to reduce the dimensional to two-dimensional. ALl experiments were computed utilizing D-Wave software and hardware platforms
[
8
]
. The experiments were computed using three different methods of computation:

1.

TABU
A quantum inspired heuristic algorithm based on the Multistart TABU Search algorithm
[
18
]
. This is a local search method that attempts to avoid the local minima problem by keeping a list of candidate solutions that are temporarily not allowed. This allows the search space to be exhausted more thoroughly.

2.

Simulated Annealing
A quantum inspired algorithm inspired by metallurgy that starts with a random solution and then makes small changes to it. At each step, the algorithm decides whether to accept the new solution based on a probability that depends on the difference in the cost function and a temperature parameter. As the process continues, the temperature is gradually lowered, reducing the likelihood of accepting worse solutions, thus allowing the system to stabilize into a solution.

3.

Hybrid BQM
D-Wave’s Hybrid Binary Quadratic Model that utilizes a combination of a classical annealer and a quantum annealer. This solver has a proprietary process that takes submitted quantum programs and splits the computational blocks between the classical and quantum CPU.

To use each computational process, no change in the formulation was required. The TABU solver and simulated annealer were provided by D-Wave’s
Ocean
software. These processes were used to generate our centroids and then utilized as starting centroids for
k
-means to form clusters. As a control we computed the same number of clusters utilizing the same data letting
k
-means randomly select the starting centroids. In addition to the previously mentioned cluster analysis metrics, we also kept track of the number of iterations required for
k
-means to converge. It is worth noting again the way the maximum sample size was determined was through empirical means and limitations imposed by D-Wave’s free account.

5
Conclusion

In this work we have addressed many of the shortcomings by our previous algorithm QOCI and have provided a novel quantum algorithm for selecting better initial centroids for prototype-based clusters instead of relying on a random selection of centroids. Quantum computing has evolved by leaps and bounds within the past two decades and there are countless domains to where quantum computing is being explored. Despite the exciting new territory one must also remember to take a step back and re-evaluate existing methods. For the time being many are good enough, but there will always come a time when good enough is no longer acceptable.
