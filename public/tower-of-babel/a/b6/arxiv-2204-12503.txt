Cosmology from the kinetic polarized Sunyaev Zel’dovich effect
Selim C. Hotinli, Gilbert P. Holder, Matthew C. Johnson, Marc Kamionkowski

Abstract
The cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons that scatter off free electrons in the large-scale structure induce a linear polarization pattern proportional to the remote CMB temperature quadrupole observed in the electrons’ rest frame. The associated blackbody polarization anisotropies are known as the polarized Sunyaev Zel’dovich (pSZ) effect. Relativistic corrections to the remote quadrupole field give rise to a non-blackbody polarization anisotropy proportional to the square of the transverse peculiar velocity field; this is the kinetic polarized Sunyaev Zel’dovich (kpSZ) effect. In this paper, we forecast the ability of future CMB and galaxy surveys to detect the kpSZ effect, finding that a statistically significant detection is within the reach of planned experiments. We further introduce a quadratic estimator for the square of the peculiar velocity field based on a galaxy survey and CMB polarization. Finally, we outline how the kpSZ effect is a probe of cosmic birefringence and primordial non-Gaussianity, forecasting the reach of future experiments.

III.1
ILC-cleaned kpSZ maps

The frequency dependence of the kpSZ signal provides a window into removing some of the lensed primary CMB and the pSZ signals – both of which are black-body – that are dominant in comparison to the kpSZ. The multi-frequency information can be used to clean contributions to the temperature maps other than the kpSZ signal using a standard harmonic-space internal linear combination (ILC)
procedure. We write the covariance between the de-beamed CMB at different frequencies as a matrix satisfying

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{\ell}=\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{EE,\,\rm kpSZ}\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{\dagger}+\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}_{\rm kpSZ}{C}_{\ell}^{FG}+(\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}_{\rm kpSZ}\boldsymbol{N})_{\ell}

(49)

where
(\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}_{\rm kpSZ})_{ij}=1/[g(x_{i})g(x_{j})]
where
g(x_{i})
is the frequency-dependent coefficient of the kpSZ signal and
\tilde{C}^{EE,\,\rm kpSZ}_{\ell}
is the frequency-independent component of the kpSZ signal introduced in Eq. (
6
), i.e.
\tilde{C}^{EE,\,\rm kpSZ}_{\ell}={C}^{EE,\,\rm kpSZ}_{\ell}/[g(x)]^{2}
. Here,
{C}_{\ell}^{FG}
consists of the non-kpSZ contribution (foregrounds for kpSZ) to the CMB (the primary lensed blackbody CMB and pSZ signals) and
(\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{N})_{\ell}
is the de-beamed instrumental noise covariance (assumed diagonal).

For the contributions to the signal in the millimeter wavelength, we consider the black-body late-time kSZ, clustered CIB, Poisson CIB and tSZ foregrounds following Ref.
(
Madhavacheril et al. 2017
)
. We omit including the correlation between CIB and tSZ. We include the radio sources using the flux-limit-dependent radio-source power model from Ref.
(
Lagache et al. 2020
)
in the 39, 93 and 145 GHz channels. We assume flux limits of 10, 7 and 10 mJy (2, 1 and 1 mJy) for CMB-S4 (CMB-HD), respectively in those channels. Finally, we include the kSZ signal from reionization following Ref.
(
Park et al. 2013
)
and the lensed CMB black-body contribution using CAMB
(
Lewis et al. 2000
)
.

Following
the ILC method in harmonic space, we estimate the ILC-cleaned signal as

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{\ell m}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\dagger}_{\ell m}\boldsymbol{E}_{\ell m}

(50)

where
\boldsymbol{w}_{\ell m}
are weights that minimize the variance of the resulting signal satisfying

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{\ell m}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{C}_{\ell})^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}}{\boldsymbol{e}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{C})^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}}\,,

(51)

and the ensemble averaged
E
-mode power spectrum (same for the
B
-mode) of the cleaned map is

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{EE,\,\rm cleaned}=\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{EE,\,\rm kpSZ}+\boldsymbol{w}_{\ell}^{\dagger}[\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}_{\rm kpSZ}{C}_{\ell}^{FG}+(\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}_{\rm kpSZ}\boldsymbol{N})_{\ell}]\boldsymbol{w}_{\ell}\,.

(52)

We demonstrate the cleaned
E
-mode polarization spectra in Fig.
3

\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{2})
larger than the experimental white-noise. As the number of frequency bins plays an important role in reducing the dominant blackbody contribution, the proposed PIXIE experiment
(
Kogut et al. 2016
;
Kogut et al. 2014
)
with 15GHz wide frequency bins within the range 30GHz-3THz can potentially provide much more significant reduction. Using the experimental specifications of PIXIE, we find the blackbody signal can be removed almost perfectly from the CMB allowing PIXIE to provide detector-noise limited measurement of the kpSZ signal up to scales
\ell\sim 100

III.3
The kpSZ quadratic-estimator variance

Detecting the kpSZ from the polarization maps directly on large scales may be difficult due to atmospheric noise, unaccounted foregrounds and effects of masking, for example. An alternative method for measuring large-scale cosmological fluctuations is through using the non-Gaussian information on the small-scale CMB maps, together with maps of the galaxy distribution in tomographic redshift bins. This technique has been shown to be a very promising avenue to cosmological inference for the upcoming cosmology experiments. Here we go over the pSZ quadratic estimator formalism and evaluate the prospects to detect the large-scale kinematic quadrupole through pSZ tomography.

The minimum variance estimator for the remote quadrupole field can be reconstructed using observed maps of the CMB polarization and the fluctuations of the optical depth
\delta\tau(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=\sigma_{T}n_{e}a\delta_{e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
. The estimator is already calculated in Refs.
(
Alizadeh and Hirata 2012
;
Meyers et al. 2018
;
Deutsch et al. 2017
;
Deutsch et al. 2018
;
Deutsch et al. 2019
)
. Here, we shortly review the method presented in Ref.
(
Meyers et al. 2018
)
. Note we are using the flat-sky approximation, which we anticipate to agree well with a full-sky estimator that is calculated in Ref.
(
Alizadeh and Hirata 2012
)
, as this is also the case for lensing estimators
(
Okamoto and Hu 2003
)
. Parameters
\boldsymbol{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{L}
introduced in this section are two-dimensional Fourier wavevectors.

We wish to construct an estimator for the quadrupole field
\hat{p}
from observed maps of the CMB polarization
(Q(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})\pm iU(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}))^{\mathrm{obs}}
, and maps of the local optical depth
\delta\tau^{\mathrm{ext}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)
observed by some external survey, at small scales,

\displaystyle{\,{}_{\pm}}\hat{p}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)\sim\left[(Q(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})\pm iU(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}))^{\mathrm{obs}}\delta\tau(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)^{\mathrm{ext}}\right]_{f}\,,

(60)

filtered in order to minimize the variance of the estimator, similar to CMB lensing quadratic estimator
(
Hu and Okamoto 2002
)
. The observed CMB polarization will contain contribution that is primordial in its origin
(Q(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})\pm iU(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}))^{\mathrm{prim}}
, present in the absence of free electrons at late times; as well as contributions from Thomson scattering,
{\,{}_{\pm}}p^{\mathrm{pSZ}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)\delta\tau^{\mathrm{ext}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)
which include polarization sourced by both primordial and kinematic quadrupoles; as well as instrumental noise.

The remote quadrupole estimators can be found as

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{E}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)

(61)

\displaystyle=N^{EE}(\boldsymbol{L})\!\!\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\boldsymbol{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\delta\tau^{\rm ext}(\boldsymbol{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})f^{E}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{L})[E^{\rm obs}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\cos^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))+B^{\rm obs}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\sin^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))]\,,

and

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{B}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}},\eta)

(62)

\displaystyle=N^{BB}(\boldsymbol{L})\!\!\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\boldsymbol{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\delta\tau^{\rm ext}(\boldsymbol{L}-\boldsymbol{\ell})f^{B}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{L})[E^{\rm obs}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\sin^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))+B^{\rm obs}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\cos^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))]\,,

where we defined

\displaystyle{p}_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\eta)=(p^{E}\mp ip^{B})(\boldsymbol{\ell},\eta)e^{\mp 2i\phi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\,.

(63)

The filters that minimize the variance can be found as

\displaystyle f^{E}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{L})=[C_{\ell}^{EE,\rm obs}\cos^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))+C_{\ell}^{BB,\rm obs}\sin^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))]^{-1}

(64)

and

\displaystyle f^{B}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{L})=[C_{\ell}^{EE,\rm obs}\sin^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))+C_{\ell}^{BB,\rm obs}\cos^{2}(2(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}))]^{-1}\,.

(65)

The estimated variances of the reconstructed field of remote quadrupoles on large scales are then
(
Meyers et al. 2018
)
,

N^{EE}(\boldsymbol{L})\!=\!\!\Big[\!\int\!\!\frac{{\rm d}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(C_{|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}-{\boldsymbol{L}}|}^{\delta\tau\,g})^{2}}{C_{|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}-{\boldsymbol{L}}|}^{gg\,\rm obs}}\left(C^{EE,\mathrm{obs}}_{\ell}\cos^{2}\left[2\left(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\right)\right]+\!C^{BB,\mathrm{obs}}_{\ell}\sin^{2}\left[2\left(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\right)\right]\right)^{-1}\Big]^{-1},\\

N^{BB}(\boldsymbol{L})\!=\!\!\Big[\!\int\!\!\frac{{\rm d}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(C_{|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}-{\boldsymbol{L}}|}^{\delta\tau\,g})^{2}}{C_{|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}-{\boldsymbol{L}}|}^{gg\,\rm obs}}\left(C^{EE,\mathrm{obs}}_{\ell}\sin^{2}\left[2\left(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\right)\right]+\!C^{BB,\mathrm{obs}}_{\ell}\cos^{2}\left[2\left(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{L}}\!-\!\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\right)\right]\right)^{-1}\Big]^{-1},\\

and
N^{EB}(\boldsymbol{L})=0
due to symmetry. The
C^{\mathrm{obs}}
indicates the ILC-cleaned CMB spectrum including the instrumental noise, and
C_{|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}-{\boldsymbol{L}}|}^{\delta\tau g}
is cross-correlation between the optical depth fluctuations and and externally inferred map of galaxies. More details on derivation can be found in
(
Meyers et al. 2018
)
.

In order to evaluate the detection prospects of the kinetic pSZ effect from tomography, we cross-correlate the large-scale kinetic quadrupole reconstructed from maps of the CMB (and a density tracer) on small scales, with that inferred directly from a galaxy survey. We assume that the latter method provides a precise enough measurement of the large-scale density that we can infer the large-scale transverse velocity up to galaxy shot noise, which should be a reasonable approximation for the high number densities of galaxies expected in the surveys we are considering. We calculate the total signal-to-noise ratio similar to before as

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\ell^{\prime};p;XYWZ}C_{\ell}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{X}\hat{p}_{Y}}\textbf{cov}^{-1}\left(\tilde{C}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{X}\hat{p}_{Y}}_{\ell},\tilde{C}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{W}\hat{p}_{Z}}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{W}\hat{p}_{Z}}\,,

(66)

where
p\in\{p^{E},p^{B}\}
are the reconstructed
E
- and
B
-mode quadrupoles, the indices run over redshift bins, the fields with hats refer to the remote-quadrupole field reconstructed from the CMB, those without a hat refer to the quadrupole reconstructed from the galaxy distribution, the tilde refers to spectra including noise, and the covariance is given by

\displaystyle\textbf{cov}\left(\tilde{C}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{X}\hat{p}_{Y}}_{\ell},\tilde{C}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{W}\hat{p}_{Z}}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}{2\ell+1}f_{\mathrm{sky}}^{-1}\left(\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{X}\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{W}}\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\hat{p}_{Y}\hat{p}_{Z}}+\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{X}\hat{p}_{Z}}\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\hat{p}^{\rm gal}_{Y}\hat{p}_{W}}\right)\,.

(67)

To assess the detectability of the kpSZ effect, we take – similar to before – a null hypothesis scenario in which there is no signal in the CMB-reconstructed quadrupole, which we also take to have noise diagonal in the redshift bins (
\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}=\delta_{\hat{X}\hat{Y}}N_{\ell}^{\hat{X}}
), and no signal or noise in the cross with the galaxy-derived quadrupole (
\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\hat{X}^{\rm gal}\hat{Y}}=0

IV
Cosmology from the kpSZ effect

Measurement of the kpSZ effect may open new avenues for cosmological inference. Here, we give two possible applications and demonstrate the role of future kpSZ measurements in improving our understanding of the fundamental properties of the Universe.

IV.1
Detecting cosmic birefringence

If the parity symmetry predicted by the standard LCDM is broken due to new physics, the plane of linear polarizations sourced due to pSZ effect can rotate due to the photon’s coupling to the dark sector
(
Lue et al. 1999
;
Carroll and Field 1991
;
Harari and Sikivie 1992
)
. We model this cosmic-birefringence effect on the
E
- and
B
- mode polarization due to kpSZ effect as

\displaystyle(Q^{\prime}\pm iU^{\prime})(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=(Q\pm iU)^{\rm pkSZ}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})e^{\pm 2i\alpha}

(68)

where the kpSZ superscript denotes the kinetic polarization signal sourced at the electron locations. We omit showing this superscript in what follows. From the above equation, the modulation of the polarization modes due to birefringence sourced between their emission and their arrival to our detectors is

\displaystyle E_{\ell m}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=E_{\ell m}\cos 2\alpha-B_{\ell m}\sin 2\alpha

(69)

\displaystyle B_{\ell m}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=B_{\ell m}\cos 2\alpha+E_{\ell m}\sin 2\alpha\,.

(70)

The remote kinetic quadrupole fields measured from the cross of the CMB polarization and galaxies on small scales transforms similarly

\displaystyle{p^{E}_{\ell m}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p^{E}_{\ell m}\cos 2\alpha-p^{B}_{\ell m}\sin 2\alpha

(71)

\displaystyle{p^{B}_{\ell m}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p^{B}_{\ell m}\cos 2\alpha+p^{E}_{\ell m}\sin 2\alpha\,.

(72)

Like before, we assume templates of
E
- and
B
-mode fluctuations induced by the kpSZ effect can be reconstructed from a large-scale structure survey. The set of observables from cross-correlations of the templates and the reconstructed remote quadrupole satisfy

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{E}_{i}{p}^{E,\rm gal}_{j}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{E}_{i}{p}_{j}^{E,\rm gal}}\cos(2(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{\rm cal}))\,,

(73)

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{B}_{i}{p}^{B,\rm gal}_{j}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{B}_{i}{p}^{B,\rm gal}_{j}}\cos(2(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{\rm cal}))\,,

(74)

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{B}_{i}{p}^{E,\rm gal}_{j}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{E}_{i}{p}_{j}^{E,\rm gal}}\sin(2(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{\rm cal}))\,,

(75)

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{{p}^{E}_{i}{p}^{B,\rm gal}_{j}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-C_{\ell}^{{p}^{B}_{i}{p}_{j}^{B,\rm gal}}\sin(2(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{\rm cal}))\,,

(76)

where we parametrised the unknown CMB detector calibration with the parameter
\alpha_{\rm cal}
. Here, we only use the cross-correlation signal and define an ensemble information matrix as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{ab}=\sum_{\ell\ell^{\prime};ijkl}({\partial C_{\ell}^{{p}^{B}_{i}\hat{p}^{E,\rm gal}_{j}}}/{\partial\boldsymbol{p}_{a}})\textbf{cov}^{-1}\left(\tilde{C}^{{p}^{B}_{i}\hat{p}^{E,\rm gal}_{j}}_{\ell},\tilde{C}^{{p}^{B}_{k}\hat{p}^{E,\rm gal}_{l}}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)({\partial C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{{p}_{k}\hat{p}^{E,\rm gal}_{l}}}/{\partial\boldsymbol{p}_{b}})\,,

(77)

where
\boldsymbol{p}=\{\alpha_{\rm cal},\alpha\}
are the set of parameters we consider, where we define
\alpha
as the net rotation from a comoving range of
\alpha\simeq 220
Mpc, assuming constant differential birefringence at all redshifts, i.e. assuming constant
{\rm d}\alpha/{\rm d}\chi
.

We demonstrate the prospect of measuring the birefringence angle for two CMB experiments in Fig.
9
, assuming VRO experimental specifications for the galaxy survey. We find that the kpSZ tomography can potentially detect rotation angle per redshift bin of width
\Delta\chi\sim 220
Mpc to be
\sim 1
degree with future experiments. While these constraints may prove to be a marginal improvement compared to current and upcoming analyses e.g. Refs.
(

;
Abazajian et al. 2016b
)
, they may provide a complementary probe of birefringence by breaking the calibration angle degeneracy through tomography, and by being subject to different systematics than constraints based on the recombination and reionization contributions to the CMB. Cross-correlation with the reionization
E
-modes, as well as the pSZ effect from the primordial quadrupole may potentially improve these constraints. Finally, note that the pSZ tomography from the primordial quadrupole signal can also probe cosmic-birefringence due to a similar degeneracy breaking. We expand on the observations made here in Ref.
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
. Note that we have not considered the so-called ‘optical-depth degeneracy’ due to mis-modelling of the electron profiles in the absence of perfect knowledge of the electron distribution (see e.g. Ref.
(

)
for an in-depth discussion). This leads to a multiplicative bias on the reconstructed velocities. Here, we conjecture that a combination of the reconstructed remote quadruple auto-correlation along with cross-correlations with templates as discussed above, as well as the potential measurements of electron profiles (e.g. from fast-radio burst measurements
(
Madhavacheril et al. 2019

IV.2
Primordial non-Gaussianity

We now turn our attention to calculating the contribution to the remote quadrupole power-spectrum in the presence of a significant trispectrum, parameterized by an amplitude
\tau_{\rm NL}
, that can be sourced by models that deviate from the standard single-field slow roll inflation. Since the single-field slow roll inflation is just the simplest model, it is natural to wonder whether there may be other fields coupled to the inflaton. “Cosmological collider physics”
(
Baumann and Green 2012
;
Assassi et al. 2012
;
Chen and Wang 2012
;
Pi and Sasaki 2012
;
Noumi et al. 2013
;
Arkani-Hamed and Maldacena 2015
;
Gong et al. 2013
;
Lee et al. 2016
;
Kehagias and Riotto 2017
;
Kumar and Sundrum 2018
;
An et al. 2018a
;
An et al. 2018b
;
Baumann et al. 2018
;
Kumar and Sundrum 2019
;
Goon et al. 2019
;
Anninos et al. 2019
;
Kumar and Sundrum 2020
;
Hook et al. 2020
, e.g.)
refers to the effort to seek the signatures of these new fields in the distribution of primordial density perturbations. One simple starting point in this inquiry is the coupling of the inflaton
\phi(x)
to some other scalar
\psi(x)
through an interaction
\propto\psi\phi^{2}
(e.g., Ref.
(
Jeong and Kamionkowski 2012
)
). Such a coupling would enhance the inflaton (and thus density, or curvature) four-point function (or trispectrum) without any enhancement in the three-point function (or bispectrum)
Tseliakhovich et al. 2010
;
Chen and Wang 2010
;
Suyama and Yokoyama 2011
. A curvaton model could also enhance the trispectrum without much contribution to the bispectrum
(
Tseliakhovich et al. 2010
;
Smith and LoVerde 2011
)
. In many models, the trispectrum takes a “local-model” form, which amounts heuristically to a spatial modulation of the power-spectrum amplitude (equivalent to the scalar fossil field discussed in Ref.
Jeong and Kamionkowski 2012
). The primordial trispectrum amplitude
\tau_{\rm NL}
is currently constrained to be
\tau_{\rm NL}\lesssim 2\!\times\!10^{3}
at the 68% confidence level by
Planck

(
Ade et al. 2014
;
Akrami et al. 2019
)
. There is not much room to improve upon this significantly with CMB measurements due to Silk damping of the temperature fluctuations. There are possible ways to seek
\tau_{\rm NL}
in the trispectrum of the 21-cm brightness temperature
Cooray et al. 2008
, however, as well as the halo bias
Ferraro and Smith 2015
;
Yamauchi and Takahashi 2016
;
Sekiguchi et al. 2019
and the 3-point correlations between two CMB temperature and one
\mu
spectral distortion fluctuations
Bartolo et al. 2016
; where the expected sensitivities are
\tau_{\rm NL}\sim 50-100
.

Locally sourced density perturbations produce fluctuations that scale as dictated by the Poisson equation,
\delta(\boldsymbol{k},z)\!\propto\!k^{2}\Phi(\boldsymbol{k})
, or with higher factors of
k
, hence are much less significant at large scales compared to the primordial curvature fluctuations. Scale-dependence of cosmological fluctuations observed on the largest scales hence provide an opportunity to infer the initial conditions relatively unpolluted by the complicated local dynamics. Here, we are interested in a specific signal, the
\tau_{\rm NL}
-type non-Gaussianity, which boosts the signal from cross-correlations of fluctuations that are quadratic in density, such as the remote kinematic quadrupole field.

The (connected)
N
-point correlation function of the gravitational potential is given by

\displaystyle\langle\Phi(\boldsymbol{k}_{1})\!\ldots\!\Phi(\boldsymbol{k}_{N}\!)\rangle_{c}\!=\!(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1\dots N\!})F_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N}\!),\ \ \ \

(78)

where
\boldsymbol{k}_{1\ldots N}=\sum_{1}^{N}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
and
F_{\Phi}
is a function that depends on the specific model of inflation.
The collapsed 4-point function is equivalent to the large-scale correlation of small scale power, and therefore can be seen as directly related to the 2-point function of a quadratic perturbation, as the one in defined in Eq. (
22
), which can be generally expressed as

\displaystyle{\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},z)=B(z)[\delta(\boldsymbol{x},z)]^{2}\,,}

(79)

with 2-point correlations given by

\displaystyle{\langle\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},z)\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle\!=\!B(z)B^{\prime}(z^{\prime})\!\langle[\delta(\boldsymbol{x},z)]^{2}[\delta(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},z)]^{2}\rangle\,,}

(80)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L},z)\delta_{2}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle=\!B(z)B^{\prime}(z^{\prime})\!\!\!\int_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\!\!\int_{\boldsymbol{k}_{3}}\!\!\left\langle\delta(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},z)\delta(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\!-\!\boldsymbol{k}_{1},z)\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k}_{3},z^{\prime})\delta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{\prime}\!-\!\boldsymbol{k}_{3},z^{\prime})\right\rangle\,,\end{split}

(81)

which can be seen as equal to the collapsed limit of the 4-point when
\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\gg\boldsymbol{k}_{L}
and
\boldsymbol{k}_{3}\gg\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{\prime}
. Note also we defined
\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv\int[{\rm d}^{3}k/(2\pi)^{3}]
and
B(z)
as a redshift dependent coefficient. Eqs. (
80

81

C_{\ell}^{pp}
is a collapsed 4-point function.

In various multi-field inflation models

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{|\boldsymbol{k}_{12}|\rightarrow 0}F_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{k}_{4})=16\left({5}/{6}\right)^{2}\tau_{\rm NL}P_{12}P_{1}P_{3},

(82)

where
P_{i}=P_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{k}_{i})
and

\tau_{\rm NL}
as commonly done in literature, and can be directly related to the amplitude of the local bispectrum,
f_{\rm NL}
, in the absence of additional degrees of freedom (see e.g.,
(
Suyama and Yamaguchi 2008
;
Enqvist and Nurmi 2005

Byrnes et al. 2006

;

;

;

;
Smidt et al. 2010
)
).

The contribution from primordial non-Gaussianity to the power spectrum of
\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L},z)\delta_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}_{L},z)\right\rangle_{\rm NG}\!\!=16

\displaystyle\,\left({5}/{6}\right)^{2}\!\tau_{\rm NL}\sigma^{4}P_{\Phi}(k_{L})\times(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}+\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}_{L})\,,\ \

(83)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=B(z)\int{\rm d}[\ln k/(4\pi)]\Delta^{2}_{\delta}({k},z)

(84)

For the collapsed limit and the inflationary models described above,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle{\,{}_{\pm}}p(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}){\,{}_{\pm}}p(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{\prime})\rangle_{\rm NG}=f^{2}(x)(100/9)&\tau_{\rm NL}\sigma_{v_{\perp}}^{4}P_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L})\times(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}(\boldsymbol{k}_{L}+\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{\prime})\,.\ \ \end{split}

V
Discussion

The next generation of CMB experiments and galaxy surveys are poised to drive a paradigm shift, in which a wide variety of secondary effects in the CMB are detected at high enough significance to become powerful cosmological probes in their own right. In this paper, we discussed the prospects for these experiments to detect the kinetic polarized Sunyaev Zel’dovich (kpSZ) effect using two different techniques. First, we demonstrated that on large (roughly degree) angular scales it is possible to detect the kpSZ effect by using multifrequency measurements of the CMB from CMB-S4, CMB-HD, or PIXIE in cross-correlation with a transverse velocity template constructed using a Rubin LSST-like galaxy survey. Next, we constructed a quadratic estimator for the kinetic component of the quadrupole field based on the anisotropic small-angular scale cross-correlation between CMB and a galaxy survey. We demonstrated how the reconstructed remote kinetic quadrupole field can be used to constrain cosmic birefringence and primordial non-Gaussianity.

In future work, it will be important to compare the forecasts presented here to the analysis of simulations, where the full non-linear effects of gravity are accounted for. The potential biasing of the reconstruction due to mis-modelling of the electron density field, as well as the likely enhancement of noise due to this foreground when using different methods such as real-space matched filtering, also warrant further analysis. Despite the potential hurdles, the outlook for detecting kpSZ with near-term experiments is promising, and it may provide novel constraints on various cosmological scenarios. The kpSZ effect is just one of a number of CMB secondaries, and the detection of this signal will undoubtedly be an important milestone towards the development of new cosmological observables and new methods to constrain fundamental physics with future experiments.
