Introduction

Let
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
be the unit disk and
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
the unit circle. Consider the analytic automorphisms

This implies that the composition operators induced by these automorphisms are
reflections
(i.e., operators

such that

be the Hardy space of the disk, i.e.

H^{2}=\{f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}:f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\hbox{ with }\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}<\infty\}.

[

In particular, for

satisfies

The space

∗

C_{a}
indexed by

. Further efforts will be focused in understanding the interplay of the geometry of

and that of

The eigenspaces of

, the description of their eigenspaces, their relative position, and the induced symmetries.
In this task, it will be important the role of the unique fixed point

of

inside the disk. Namely,

\omega_{a}:=\frac{1}{\bar{a}}\{1-\sqrt{1-|a|^{2}}\}\ \hbox{ if }a\neq 0,\ \hbox{ and }\omega_{0}=0.

(1)

The contents of the paper are the following. In Section 2 we recall basic facts on the manifolds of reflections and symmetries, in particular the condition for existence of geodesics between points in the latter space. In Section 3 we state basic formulas concerning the operators

Preliminaries, on reflections and symmetries

Denote the set of reflections by

{\cal Q}=\{T\in{\cal B}(H^{2}):T^{2}=I\}.

The set
{\cal Q}
has rich geometric structure (see for instance
[
6
]
): is it a homogeneous C
∞
submanifold of
{\cal B}(H^{2})
, carrying the action of the group
Gl(H^{2})
of invertible operators in
H^{2}
:

G\cdot T=GTG^{-1},\ T\in{\cal Q},G\in Gl(H^{2}).

The set
{\cal P}
of
selfadjoint
reflections, or
symmetries
, is

{\cal P}=\{V\in{\cal Q}:V^{*}=V\}.

Note that a symmetry
V
is a selfadjoint unitary operator. Reflections and symmetries can be viewed alternatively as oblique and orthogonal projections, respectively. A reflection
T
gives rise to an idempotent (or oblique projection) with range equal to the eigenspace
\{f\in H^{2}:Tf=f\}
:
Q_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(I+T)
(and another with range equal to the other eigenspace
\{g\in H^{2}:Tg=-g\}
of
T
:
Q_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(I-T)
). If
S
is a symmetry, the corresponding idempotents
P_{+}
and
P_{-}
are orthogonal projections.

The set
{\cal P}
, in turn, can be regarded as the Grassmann manifold of
H^{2}
: to each reflection
V
corresponds a unique projection
P_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(I+V)
and a unique subspace
{\cal S}
such that
R(P_{+})={\cal S}
. The geometry of the Grassmann manifold in this operator theoretic context was developed in
[
6
]
,
[
14
]
:
{\cal P}
is presented as a homogeneous space of the unitary group (as in the classical finite dimensional setting), with a linear reductive connection and a Finsler metric. In
[
2
]
the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a geodesic of this connection between two subspaces
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
was stated: namely, that

\dim({\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp})=\dim({\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}).

(2)

Moreover, the geodesic is of the form
\delta(t)=e^{itX}{\cal S}
, for
X^{*}=X
co-diagonal with respect to both
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
:

X({\cal S})\subset{\cal S}^{\perp}\ \ \hbox{ and }\ \ X({\cal T})\subset{\cal T}^{\perp}.

The geodesic can be chosen of minimal length for the Finsler metric (see
[
14
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
2
]
). This latter condition amounts to finding
X
such that
\|X\|\leq\pi/2
.

The condition for the existence of a
unique
minimal geodesic (up to reparametrization) was given:

{\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp}=\{0\}={\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}.

(3)

In this case, the exponent
X=X_{{\cal S},{\cal T}}
is unique with the above mentioned conditions (
X_{{\cal S},{\cal T}}
selfadjoint, codiagonal with respect to
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
, with norm less or equal then
\pi/2
, satisfying
e^{iX_{{\cal S},{\cal T}}}{\cal S}={\cal T}
.).

In this paper we shall examine existence and uniqueness of geodesics of the Grassmann manifold of
H^{2}
, for the eigenspaces of
C_{a}
.

One of the remarkable features of the space
{\cal Q}
is the several natural projection maps that it has onto
{\cal P}
.
The natural projection maps

are the range, nullspace and unitary part in the polar decomposition:

1.

The
range map

, which maps

to the symmetry

, i.e. the symmetry which is the identity on

]

Then

and therefore

{\bf r}(T)=2(I+T)\{T+T^{*}\}^{-1}-I=(2I+T-T^{*})\{T+T^{*}\}^{-1}.

(4)

2.

The
null-space

{\bf n}
, which maps

to the symmetry which is the identity on

(5)

.

3.

The
unitary part

\rho

in the polar decomposition
, which maps
T
to

(6)

the unitary part in the polar decomposition

. We refer the reader to

Notice the following formulas:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let

then

{\bf r}(T)=2(I+T)(T^{*}T+I)^{-1}\ \hbox{ and }\ \ {\bf n}(T)=2(I-T)(T^{*}T+I)^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
T=\rho(T)|T|
be the polar decomposition. It is a straightforward computation (or see
[
6
]
) that
|T|\rho(T)=\rho(T)|T^{*}|
. Also it is eassy to see that since

T+T^{*}=\rho(T)|T|+|T|\rho(T)=\rho(T)(|T|+|T^{*}|)=\rho(T)(|T|+|T|^{-1}).

Using again that

(and therefore also that
\rho(T)|T|=|T|^{-1}\rho(T)
), we have that
\rho(T)
commutes with
|T|+|T|^{-1}
. Then

(T+T^{*})^{-1}=\rho(T)(|T|+|T|^{-1})^{-1}=(|T|+|T|^{-1})^{-1}\rho(T).

By an elementary functional calculus argument, we have that
(|T|+|T|^{-1})^{-1}=|T|(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1}
. Then

(T+T^{*})^{-1}=\rho(T)|T|(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1}=T(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1}.

Thus,

{\bf r}(T)=2(T+I)T(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1}=2(I+T)(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1},

and similarly

{\bf n}(T)=2(T-I)T(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1}=2(I-T)(|T|^{2}+I)^{-1}.

∎

We shall return to these formulas for the case

Theorem 4.7

Let

be a bounded analytic map,
\tilde{\varphi}
its boundary function, and

.

As a consequence, for

we have that (

\|C_{a}-C_{b}\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

(15)

On the other hand, it is a consequence of Theorem

In particular, contrary to what happens to

and

, then both

and
|C_{b}|\to|C_{a}|
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Fix

and

of

, we have that

\|\rho_{b}-\rho_{a}\|\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-r\right)\frac{1+|a|}{\sqrt{1-|a|^{2}}}.

By Berkson’s Theorem, if

Proof.

The proof follows by direct computation.
First, we claim that

(23)

Note that
