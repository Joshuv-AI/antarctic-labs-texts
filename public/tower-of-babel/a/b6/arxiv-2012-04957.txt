Optimal distributed composite testing in high-dimensional Gaussian models with 1-bit communication
Botond Szabó, Lasse Vuursteen, and Harry van Zanten

Abstract
In this paper we study the problem of signal detection in Gaussian noise in a distributed setting where the local machines in the star topology can communicate a single bit of information. We derive a lower bound on the Euclidian norm that the signal needs to have in order to be detectable. Moreover, we exhibit optimal distributed testing strategies that attain the lower bound.



Index terms—
Testing, distributed algorithms, hypothesis testing, federated learning, minimax lower bounds, Gaussian noise.

The rapidly increasing amount of available data in many fields of application has triggered the development of distributed methods
for data analysis.
Distributed methods, besides being able to speed up computations considerably,
can reduce local memory requirements and can also help in protecting privacy, by
refraining from storing a whole dataset in a single central location.
Moreover, distributed methods occur naturally when data is by construction observed and processed
at multiple locations, for instance in astronomy, meteorology, seismology, military radar or air traffic control systems.

In the context of decentralized detection, the task of distinguishing between different signals based on
information provided from a network of sensors has been investigated since the late seventies.
Motivated by applications such as surveillance systems and wireless communication, statistical hypothesis testing with distributed sensors has received considerable attention over the past few decades, e.g.
[
27
,
28
,
19
,
25
]
. Most of this literature has been concerned with distinguishing
between finitely many signals, by combining the decisions of sensors or machines receiving (noisy) data from the same underlying signal
[
30
,
10
]
. The statistical hypotheses considered at the time where either simple, or reduce to a simple hypothesis. Under the name of multiterminal data compression, hypothesis testing and estimation with observations in fixed finite sample spaces (alphabets) was investigated
[
3
,
16
,
15
,
4
,
22
,
31
]
. In this body of literature, each terminal observes a stream of iid observations in some alphabet, where the different terminals might receive observations from different distributions on different alphabets. Each of the terminals compresses the iid observations into a message that is sent to a central terminal, at which some inference goal is to be achieved based on the messages. For an overview, see
[
26
]
.

The information theoretic aspects of distributed statistical methods have only
been studied rigorously in terms of sample complexity relatively recently. The problem of distributed testing with a composite alternative hypothesis about a high-dimensional parameter with limited communication was first
considered in a few recent papers. In
[
2
]
, theoretical guarantees are derived for distributed uniformity testing of a discrete distribution in the case that a collection of machines each receive one observation. Here, sample complexity (in the sense of minimax convergence rate) is assessed in terms the cardinality of the sample space, the number of bits available for communication and the number of observations. In that paper, it is also shown that in the distributed setting with many local machines,
testing performance can strictly improve when all machines have access to a shared source of
randomness, a so-called
public coin
.
Distributed uniformity testing of a discrete distribution when multiple observations per machine are available is considered in
[
13
,
14
]
.

Most of the work, studying distributed inference in terms of sample complexity, up until now has focused on
distributed methods for estimating a signal in the normal-means model under bandwidth, or communication restrictions (see
for instance
[
33
,
8
,
32
,
6
,
9
]
). The canonical normal-means model postulates that we have an
observation
X
satisfying

\displaystyle X=\mu+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Z,

where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unknown signal,
Z\sim N_{d}(0,I_{d})
is an unobserved noise vector
with a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution, and
n
is the signal-to-noise ratio.
Note that this is equivalent to observing
n
independent copies of a
N_{d}(\mu,I_{d})
-vector. In the distributed setting considered in the aforementioned articles,
n
such observations are distributed across
m
machines, which then communicate a transcript to a central machine. The central machine then forms an estimate of
\mu
on the basis of these transcripts. The sample complexity of such a problem is expressed in
n
,
m
, the number of bits available for communication of the transcripts and the properties of
\mu
(e.g. Euclidian norm). Related to this normal-means model, on deriving minimax lower bounds and optimal distributed estimation strategies
in the context of nonparametric regression, density estimation and Gaussian signal-in-white-noise models (e.g.

[
23
,
34
,
6
,
17
,
24
]
).

In this paper, we investigate the information theoretic properties of distributed methods for

testing
for the presence of a signal in the normal-means model.
The theory on distributed testing in this setting is much less developed than that for estimation. Testing for the presence of a signal in the normal-means model translates to testing the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mu=0

that the sequence is identically equal to
0
.
Rejecting this hypothesis means declaring that there is a non-zero signal.
A fundamental question is how
large the signal should be in order to be detectable.
It is well known that in this non-distributed model, the size of the signal (by which we mean the Euclidean norm) has to be of order
d^{1/4}/\sqrt{n}
in order for the signal to be
detectable (see e.g.
[
5
]
).
An optimal test is for instance obtained by rejecting the hypothesis
H_{0}

if
\|X\|^{2}
is above a specific threshold, depending on
d
and
n
(see also Section
3.2
).

The question we address in this paper is how this changes in the distributed setting.
In our analysis we consider the distributed version of the normal-means model,
where the data (
n
independent draws from the
N_{d}(\mu,I_{d})
-distribution) is divided over
m\leq n

local machines, or cores. Equivalently, we assume that at
each local machine
j\in\{1,...,m\}
we observe a vector
X^{(j)}
which satisfies

X^{(j)}=\mu+\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}Z^{(j)},

(1)

where again
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the
Z^{(j)}
are independent
N_{d}(0,I_{d})
-distributed vectors.
Each machine carries out a test for the hypothesis
H_{0}:\mu=0
using its local data
X^{(j)}
,
where we allow that the machines use a public coin, i.e. a common random vector
U
that is available to all local machines.
Subsequently, the outcomes of the
m
local tests (which are single bits) are sent to a central machine, where
they are combined into a single, overall test.
We prove that in this distributed setting, the size of the signal
has to be of the order
(d(m\wedge d))^{1/4}/\sqrt{n}
in order for the signal to be
detectable. Moreover, we exhibit optimal distributed tests that achieve this detection bound.

The detection bound has a remarkable “regime change” or “elbow effect” at
m=d
.
As
m
grows from
1
to
d
the testing problem becomes more difficult, in the sense that
the signal needs to be ever larger to be detectable using an increasing number of machines.
This is intuitively understandable, since as
m
grows, the local signal-to-noise ratio
decreases, so it is reasonable to expect that the signal needs to be larger to be able to detect it.
The detection bound stops increasing if
m
grows above
d
however.
In that range, the decrease of the local signal-to-noise ratios is apparently balanced
by the increase of the number of bits that are transmitted from the local machines to the
central one.

The regime change is also reflected by the fact that we need different testing strategies
depending on how
m
and
d
are related. If
m
is below some threshold, and in particular
it does not increase with
n
, then, not surprisingly, we can simply use the
classical non-distributed test mentioned above at one of the local machines. If
m
is larger than this
threshold but
m\leq d
, then it is still possible to construct an optimal test
by combining local tests that are based on the test statistics
\|X^{(j)}\|^{2}
, but the
test needs to be constructed more carefully.
In the range
m\geq d
this strategy becomes sub-optimal and we have to
adopt a different approach, using the fact
that we have a public coin at our disposal. For this case
we construct an optimal distributed test by appropriately combining local tests that use
local test statistics of the form
U^{\top}X^{(j)}
, where
U
is a public random vector.

The approach to finding the lower bound can be sumarized as follows. As a first step, we lower bound the testing risk by a type of Bayes risk, where
\mu
is drawn from a prior such that it either belongs to the null hypothesis of the alternative, as in for example
[
18
]
. This Bayes risk can be related to the mutual information between the testing outcome and which hypothesis is selected, akin to techniques common in tackling (distributed) estimation problems through Fano-like inequalities in for example
[
33
,
11
]
. In particular, the tensorization property of the mutual information is used and combined with a so called strong data processing inequality to quantify the loss incurred in the distributed setup, similar to the approaches to distributed estimation in
[
32
,
8
,
9
]
.

Upon completion of this work, we came across the paper
[
1
]
, which considers a
setting similar to ours and claims some partly overlapping results.
There are also important differences between the papers, however. Most importantly perhaps, our proof strategy for the lower
bound is rather different. As a result our proof is arguably easier to verify and
at least provides an alternative to the approach proposed in
[
1
]
. Furthermore, the paper
[
1
]
does not allow the number
of machines (our
m
) to vary. Essentially, only the case that
m=n
is considered.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formally describe the model and the distributed testing problem, and introduce notations used throughout the paper. In Section
3
we present our main results. We state the detection lower bound in Section
3.1
, and we provide novel distributed tests achieving the theoretical limits in Section
3.2
. We provide a short simulation study demonstrating the regime change observed in the theoretical analysis in Section
4
.
The proofs for the distributed tests achieving the minimax rate is given in Section
5
, while the proofs of corresponding (technical) lemmas are deferred to Sections
6
-
8
.

2
Problem setting and notation

We assume we have
m
local machines. For
j=1,\ldots,m
, we have an observation
X^{(j)}

at machine
j
, which satisfies

X^{(j)}=\mu+\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}Z^{(j)}.

Here
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unknown signal of interest and
Z^{(1)},\ldots,Z^{(m)}

are independent,
N_{d}(0,I_{d})
-distributed vectors.
We allow both the dimension
d=d_{n}
and the number of machines
m=m_{n}
to depend on
the overal signal-to-noise ratio, or “sample size”

n
. In fact, the interesting cases (from an asymptotic perspective)
are the ones where both
d
and
m
are tending to infinity with
n
.
Nevertheless, we do not restrict ourselves to this asymptotic regime
and cover the finite
m
and
d
cases as well.

We are interested in distributed tests for the hypotheses

H_{0}:\mu=0,\hskip 18.49988pt\text{against}\hskip 18.49988ptH_{\rho}:\|\mu\|\geq\rho,

(2)

for
\rho>0
.
We consider public coin protocols, where each machine has access to a shared random vector
U
, which is independent of the observations
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}
.
Each local machine
j
carries out a local test. Using the local data
X^{(j)}
and the public coin
U

it produces a binary,
\{0,1\}
-valued outcome
T^{(j)}
.
The outcomes
T^{(1)},\ldots,T^{(m)}
of the local tests are transmitted to a
central machine where they are aggregated into a global test, described by a
\{0,1\}
-valued
variable
T_{dist}
. Schematically, a distributed test looks as follows:

\begin{matrix}(X^{(1)},U)&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 18.49988ptT^{(1)}\hskip 9.24994pt&\put(-10.0,4.0){\vector{3}{-1}{20.0}}\\
\vdots&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 18.49988pt\vdots\hskip 9.24994pt&\put(-10.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}\\
(X^{(m)},U)&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 18.49988ptT^{(m)}\hskip 9.24994pt&\put(-10.0,5.0){\vector{3}{1}{20.0}}\end{matrix}\hskip 18.49988ptT_{dist}.

(3)

We denote the collection of all distributed tests of this form by
\mathcal{T}_{dist}
.

The testing error, or risk of a distributed test
T_{dist}
, is defined as usual by

\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T_{dist})=P_{0}\left(T_{dist}=1\right)+\underset{\|\mu\|\geq\rho}{\sup}P_{\mu}\left(T_{dist}=0\right),

(4)

i.e. as the sum of the type one and type two errors of the test. (Here, and elsewhere, we denote
by
P_{\mu}
the underlying distribution assuming that
\mu
is the true signal.)
Uniform lower bounds for this risk express the impossibility of
detecting a signal of size
\rho
. Indeed,
fix a level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. If
\rho>0
is such that

\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T_{dist})>\alpha
for all
T_{dist}\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}
,
then it means that there exists no consistent level-
\alpha
test for testing
H_{0}

against
H_{\rho}
. In other words, no distributed test of level
\alpha
is able
to detect all signals that are larger than
\rho
in Euclidean norm.

Our aim is to find the detection threshold, i.e. the cut-off
\rho_{dist}

such that no consistent level-
\alpha
test exists if
\rho<\rho_{dist}

and at least one consistent level-
\alpha
test exists if
\rho\geq\rho_{dist}
.
We will show that, up to constants depending on the chosen level
\alpha
,
the detection threshold is given by

\rho^{2}_{dist}\asymp\min\Big\{\frac{\sqrt{dm}}{n},\frac{d}{{n}}\Big\}.

Moreover, we exhibit optimal tests for the case
\rho\geq\rho_{dist}
.

We write
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
and
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
.
For two positive sequences
a_{n},b_{n}
we use the notation
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}

C

a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}

a_{n}\asymp b_{n}

a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}

v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\|\cdot\|
.

, we denote by
D_{KL}(P\|Q)=\int\log\frac{dP}{dQ}dP

3
Main results

3.1
Lower bound for the detection threshold

The following theorem establishes the detection threshold.
Its proof is described in the remainder of the subsection.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and suppose that

\rho^{2}<c_{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{d(m\wedge d)}}{n}

(5)

for
{c_{\alpha}\leq({1-\alpha})^{2}/384}
. Then,

\underset{T\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}}{\inf}\;\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T)>\alpha,

where infimum is over all distributed tests
T\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}
given in (
3
).

The result tells us that if (
5
) holds, there does not exist a consistent test in
\mathcal{T}_{dist}
of level
\alpha\in(0,1)
for the hypotheses (
2
). In other words, no distributed test can detect all signals
of size
\rho
. It should be noted that we did not optimize for the value of the constant
c_{\alpha}
and the statement is likely to be still true for larger values of
c_{\alpha}
.

The proof of the theorem relies on three key lemmas, which we state below after introducing some necessary notations. As a first step, we use the basic fact that the supremum of the probability of a type two error of a test can be lower bounded by a Bayesian type two
error, i.e. for any prior distribution
\Pi
supported on
H_{\rho}

\underset{\mu\in H_{\rho}}{\sup}P_{\mu}\left(T=0\right)\geq\int_{H_{\rho}}P_{\mu}\left(T=0\right)\,d\Pi(\mu).

To further lower bound the risk we construct an appropriate Markov chain
and relate the testing problem to an information transfer problem through the chain.
Consider
V\sim\text{Ber}(1/2)
, i.e. a
V
is
0
or
1
, each with probability
1/2
, independent of the public coin random
vector
U
, such that the random vectors
X^{(j)}|(V=0)
,
j=1,\dots,m
follow (
1
) with
\mu=0
and
X^{(j)}|(V=1)
follows a Gaussian mixture
P_{\Pi}
defined as
P_{\Pi}(A)=\int P_{\mu}(A)\,d\Pi(\mu)
for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us denote by
{\mathbb{P}}
the joint probability measure describing the corresponding Markov dynamics

V\hskip 9.24994pt{{\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}\hskip 9.24994pt\mu}\hskip 18.49988pt\begin{matrix}\put(-10.0,-4.0){\vector{3}{1}{20.0}}&\hskip 9.24994pt(X^{(1)},U)&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 18.49988ptT^{(1)}\hskip 9.24994pt&\put(-10.0,4.0){\vector{3}{-1}{20.0}}\\
\put(-10.0,3.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 9.24994pt\vdots&\put(0.0,4.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 18.49988pt\vdots\hskip 9.24994pt&\put(-10.0,4.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}\\
\put(-10.0,12.0){\vector{3}{-1}{20.0}}&\hskip 9.24994pt(X^{(m)},U)&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\hskip 18.49988ptT^{(m)}\hskip 9.24994pt&\put(-10.0,5.0){\vector{3}{1}{20.0}}\end{matrix}\hskip 18.49988ptT.

(6)

We then have that for any distributed test
T
,

\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(T=1|V=0)+{\mathbb{P}}(T=0|V=1)=2{\mathbb{P}}(T\neq V).

(7)

The right hand side of (
7
) can be further bounded from below using the
mutual information
between
T
and
V
in the chain (
6
), defined by

I_{\Pi}(V,T)=D_{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}^{V\times T}\,\|\;{\mathbb{P}}^{V}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{T}\right),

where
{\mathbb{P}}^{V}
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{T}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{V\times T}
denote marginal- and joint distributions of
V
and
T
, and the subscript
\Pi
is used to indicate the dependence on the prior
\Pi
. Informally, the mutual information measures how much knowing
T
reduces uncertainty about
V
and vice versa. The lower bound based on the mutual information is given in the following lemma.
The proof of the lemma is deferred to Section
7.1
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Pi
be a prior on
H_{\rho}
and consider the dynamics (
6
). We have

\underset{T\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}}{\inf}\;\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T)\geq 1-\sqrt{2I_{\Pi}(V,T)}.

In view of the usual data processing inequality we have
I_{\Pi}(V,T)\leq I_{\Pi}(V,(T^{(1)},\dots,T^{(m)}))
.
The following lemma asserts that, up to an additional term, this further tensorizes conditional on the public coin randomness.

Lemma 2
.

Consider the dynamics (
6
). We have

I_{\Pi}(V,(T^{(1)},\dots,T^{(m)}))\leq\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I_{\Pi}(V,T^{(j)}|U)+{\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I_{\Pi}(\mu,T^{(j)}|U,V)}.

(8)

The proof of this lemma is given in Section
7.2
. This bound, combined with Lemma
1
, allows us to break down the difficulty of the ’global’ testing problem in terms of the difficult of the
m
’local’ testing problems, captured by the quantities
I_{\Pi}(V,T^{(j)}|U)
. These conditional local mutual informations quantify the capacity of the local tests to distinguish a signal drawn from the prior
\Pi
from
the zero signal. The second sum in the display of the lemma captures dependency between the transcripts and the prior draw
\mu\sim\Pi
. Essentially, it captures how well the signal can be
estimated
by the local tests.

We now discuss the choice of prior distribution
\Pi
. Let us set
\epsilon=\rho/\sqrt{d}
, let
R
be a
d
-dimensional vector of independent Rademacher random variables, and define the prior
\Pi
as the distribution of
\epsilon R
.
Note that
\Pi
has support contained in
H_{\rho}
. (Such choices are typically considered as least favorable priors supported on signals that are
difficult to detect, see for instance Section 3.2 of
[
18
]
.)

Since
V
,
\mu
and
X^{(j)}
are independent of
U
, conditioning on
U
does not distrupt the Markov chain property:
we have the chain
V|U\to\mu|U\to X^{(j)}|U\to T^{(j)}|U
. Consequently, the “estimation term”
I_{\Pi}(\mu,T^{(j)}|U,V)
can be handled using strong data processing techniques employed in distributed estimation, see for example Lemma 11 in
[
9
]
. For completeness, we adopted the aforementioned lemma for the above choice of prior distribution in the form of Lemma
9
in the appendix, which yields that
I_{\Pi}(\mu,T^{(j)}|U,V)\leq 128\frac{n\rho^{2}}{dm}I_{\Pi}(X^{(j)},T^{(j)}|U,V=1)
. Using that
T^{(j)}
is binary valued, we obtain that the second term in (
8
) is bounded above by
128\frac{n\rho^{2}}{d}
.

The loss of information about
V
resulting from the compression of
X^{(j)}|U
into
T^{(j)}|U
in this Markov chain is quantified by Lemma
3
below. The lemma comes in the form of a strong data processing inequality for the testing problem and forms the crux of the proof of the lower bound. It captures the difficulty of the local testing problem in terms of
n
,
m
,
d
and
\rho
.

Lemma 3

(Public Coin Strong Data Processing Inequality)
.

With
\Pi
as defined above we have

I_{\Pi}(V,T^{(j)}|U)\leq(48\beta\wedge 1)I_{\Pi}(X^{(j)},T^{(j)}|U),

where

\beta=\begin{cases}\frac{n^{2}\rho^{4}}{dm^{2}}\;\;\;\text{ if }\frac{m}{n\rho^{2}}<1/2,\\
\frac{2n\rho^{2}}{dm}\;\;\;\text{ if }\frac{m}{n\rho^{2}}\geq 1/2.\end{cases}

(9)

We give the proof of the lemma in Section
7.3
. By combining the information theoretic inequalities above
with the fact that
I(X^{(j)},T^{(j)}|U)\leq H(T^{(j)}|U)\leq 1
, which is true because
T^{(j)}
is a binary variable, we get that

I_{\Pi}(V,T)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}I_{\Pi}(V,T^{(j)}|U)+{\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I_{\Pi}(\mu,T^{(j)}|U,V)\leq 48\beta m+128\frac{n\rho^{2}}{d}}.

Therefore, in view of Lemma
1
,

\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T)\geq 1-4\sqrt{6\frac{n\rho^{2}}{d}{\left(\max\left\{\frac{n\rho^{2}}{m},2\right\}+8/3\right)}}.

For
\rho
satisfying (
5
), the right-hand side is bounded from below by
\alpha
for an
arbitrary distributed test
T\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}
.

3.2
Optimal tests attaining the lower bound

In this section, we exhibit a distributed testing procedure that is optimal in the sense
that it attains the lower bound of Theorem
1
. More precisely,
we show that if
\rho^{2}
is larger than a multiple of the right-hand side of (
5
),
there exists a distributed test for
H_{0}
against
H_{\rho}

with risk bounded by a chosen level
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Summarising, we have the following theorem, complementing the lower bound of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and suppose that

\rho^{2}\geq C_{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{d(m\wedge d)}}{n}

(10)

for
C_{\alpha}>0
a constant depending only on
\alpha
as given in (
15
).
Then there exists a test
T\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}
such that

\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T)\leq\alpha
.

We prove the theorem by constructing three concrete distributed tests, for three different ranges of
m
. We outline the construction of these tests in this section. The detailed verification that they are consistent at the level
\alpha
for testing
H_{0}
against
H_{\rho}
in their respective ranges is deferred to Section
5
.

First assume that the number of machines is large enough, but does not exceed the dimension
d
, i.e.
M_{\alpha}\leq m\leq d
, for a large enough constant
M_{\alpha}>0
given in (
15
).
In this case, Theorem
1
asserts that the detection lower bound for
\rho
is a multiple of
(dm)^{1/4}/n^{1/2}
.
We propose the following distributed test that is able to detect signals with Euclidian norm of that order
if
M_{\alpha}\leq m\leq d
. In this case no public coin is needed.
In this setting we first compute the local test statistic
S_{m\leq d}^{(j)}=(n/m)\|X^{(j)}\|^{2}

at every machine
j
. Under the null hypothesis,
S_{m\leq d}^{(j)}
follows a chi-square distribution with
d
degrees of freedom, i.e.
S_{m\leq d}^{(j)}\sim\chi^{2}_{d}
. Then for every
j
we consider the randomized test
T_{m\leq d}^{(j)}
using Bernoulli random variables

T_{m\leq d}^{(j)}|S_{m\leq d}^{(j)}\sim\text{Ber}\left(F_{\chi^{2}_{d}}\left(S_{m\leq d}^{(j)}\right)\right),

where
F_{\chi^{2}_{d}}
denotes the distribution function of
\chi^{2}_{d}
. Under the null hypothesis the
T_{m\leq d}^{(j)}

are independent and
\text{Ber}(1/2)
. At the central machines we combine the local test in
a global test
T_{m\leq d}\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}
by setting

T_{m\leq d}=\text{1}\left\{\left|\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}(T_{m\leq d}^{(j)}-1/2)\right|\geq\sqrt{m}\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}\right\},

(11)

with
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}=3\log\left(4/\alpha\right)
. By a standard Chernoff bound, the type
one error of this test is bounded by
\alpha/2

for
m
large enough. In Section
5.1
, we prove that the same is true for the type two error if

\|\rho\|^{2}\geq C_{\alpha}\sqrt{dm}/n
. as posed by Theorem

Next we assume that
m\geq d\vee M_{\alpha}
, in which case the detection lower bound for
\rho
is a multiple of

\sqrt{d/n}
. When
m/d\to\infty
, tests based on the statistics
S_{m\leq d}^{(j)}
cannot reach the detection lower bound anymore as can be observed by inspection of the variance of (
18
), or through Theorem 16 in
[
1
]
. We propose a novel distributed test using a public coin.
Specifically, we assume all machine have access to a vector

U=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
of independent standard normal random variables.
For
j=1,\ldots,m
we compute the local test statistics
S_{m\geq d}^{(j)}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{md}}U^{T}X^{(j)}
and the corresponding local tests

T_{m\geq d}^{(j)}=\text{1}\left\{S_{m\geq d}^{(j)}\geq 0\right\}.

Then we aggregate these local tests in the central machine to a distributed test
T_{m\geq d}\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}

by defining

T_{m\geq d}=\text{1}\left\{\left|\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}(T_{m\geq d}^{(j)}-1/2)\right|\geq\sqrt{m}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}\right\},

(12)

with
\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}=(1/3)\log\left(16/\alpha\right)
.
In Section
5.2
we prove that this test satisfies the required error bound if

\rho^{2}\geq C_{\alpha}d/n
.

Finally, we consider the case
m\leq M_{\alpha}
for completeness. We have to treat it separately for technical reasons,
although in practice we would probably simply use the first test above for all cases
m\leq d
.
To achieve optimality in this case we can simply choose a single machine
and conduct the hypothesis test we would use in the classical, non-distributed setting.
Specifically, we can for instance use as global test
T_{m\asymp 1}\in\mathcal{T}_{dist}

the test given by

T_{m\asymp 1}=\text{1}\left\{\frac{n}{\sqrt{d}m}\|X^{(1)}\|^{2}-\sqrt{d}\geq\kappa_{\alpha}\right\},

(13)

with
\kappa_{\alpha}=2/\sqrt{\alpha}
. See Section
5.3
for details.

5
Proof of Theorem
2

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given, recall that
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}=3\log\left(4/\alpha\right)
,
\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}=(1/3)\log\left(16/\alpha\right)
,
\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}=4/\alpha
, and set

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{(2^{5}5\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha})^{2},\bar{D},36\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2},4e^{2}\pi\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha^{1/2}\right\},

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{2^{4}M_{\alpha}^{2}\kappa_{\alpha}^{2},2(1+\sqrt{2})\kappa_{\alpha}M_{\alpha},80\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha},2^{12}e^{2}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha^{-5/2}\right\},

(15)

where
\bar{D}
is defined in Lemma
4
. Then we show below that for all
d,m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
at least one of the three distributed tests
T_{m\leq d},T_{m\geq d},T_{m\asymp 1},
given in (
13
)-(
12
), achieves uniform consistency at level
\alpha
, i.e.

\mathcal{R}(H_{\rho},T)\leq\alpha,

(16)

for some
T\in\{T_{m\asymp 1},T_{m\leq d},T_{m\geq d}\}
.

We distinguish three regimes in view of the interplay of
m
and
d
, and show in the following subsections that the corresponding tests reach uniform consistency:

•

Case 1:

M_{\alpha}<m\leq d
.

•

Case 2:

M_{\alpha}\vee d<m
.

•

Case 3:

m\leq M_{\alpha}
.

with corresponding tests
T_{m\leq d},T_{m\geq d},T_{m\asymp 1}
, respectively.

5.1
Case 1:
M_{\alpha}<m\leq d

In view of Chernoff’s bound, see Lemma
6
, for
m>4\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}
,

\displaystyle P_{0}\left(T_{m\leq d}=1\right)

\displaystyle=P_{0}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(T_{m\leq d}^{(j)}-1/2)\geq\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}/\sqrt{m}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-2\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}/3\right)<\alpha/2.

(17)

We now turn to bounding the type II error probability. For arbitrary
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d})\in H_{\rho}
,

S^{(j)}_{m\leq d}=\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\mu_{i}+Z_{i}^{(j)}\right)^{2}

(18)

with
Z_{i}^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
, follows a noncentral chi-square distribution with
d
degrees of freedom and noncentrality parameter
\delta:=(n/m)\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, i.e.
S^{(j)}_{m\leq d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind}}{{\sim}}\chi_{d}^{2}(\delta)
.

Let us take independent random variables
V_{d}^{\delta}\sim\chi_{d}^{2}(\delta)
and
U_{d}\sim\chi_{d}^{2}(0)
. Then in view of Lemma
4
, for
c>1/40
and for all
d\geq\bar{D}
,

\displaystyle E_{\mu}(F_{\chi^{2}_{d}}(S^{(j)}_{m\leq d}))=\text{Pr}\left(V^{\delta}_{d}\geq U_{d}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}+c\left(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\wedge\frac{1}{2}\right),

where
E_{\mu}
is the expectation corresponding to
P_{\mu}
. This further yields

\displaystyle P_{\mu}

\displaystyle\left(\left|\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\left[T^{(j)}_{m\leq d}-\frac{1}{2}\right]\right|\leq\sqrt{m}\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P_{\mu}\left(\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\left[T^{(j)}_{m\leq d}-E_{\mu}(F_{\chi^{2}_{d}}(S^{(j)}_{m\leq d}))\right]\leq\sqrt{m}\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{c\sqrt{m}}{\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\wedge\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)\right).

In view of Chernoff’s bound and the inequality
E_{\mu}(F_{\chi^{2}_{d}}(T^{(j)}_{m\leq d}))\leq 1
, this is further bounded by
2\exp(-\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}/3)=\alpha/2
, given that

\frac{c\sqrt{m}}{\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\wedge\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq 2.

(19)

This last inequality follows from the assumption (
10
)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}}\delta=\frac{n\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{dm}}\geq C_{\alpha}\geq 2\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}/c\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptm>16\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}/c^{2}.

5.2
Case 2:
M_{\alpha}\vee d<m

For the choice and
m>4\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}
the same bound as in (
17
) holds for
P_{0}\left(T_{m\geq d}=1\right)
.

For the type II error, consider
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d})\in H_{\rho}
. Define for
\eta>0
,
\nu>0
the events
D_{\eta}:=\{\|U\|_{2}/\sqrt{d}\leq\eta\}
and

A_{\nu,\eta}:=\left\{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}U_{i}>\nu\right\}\cap D_{\eta},\;B_{\nu,\eta}:=\left\{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}U_{i}<-\nu\right\}\cap D_{\eta},

where
U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{d})\sim N(0,I_{d})
is the public coin random vector, whose probability distribution we shall denote by
Q
. We set

\displaystyle\eta=4\alpha^{-1/2}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\nu=4e\sqrt{2\pi}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}\sqrt{\eta},

(20)

and note that
C_{\alpha}\geq 2^{7}\nu^{2}/(\pi\alpha^{2})
.

Then the type II error is bounded from above as

\displaystyle E_{Q}P_{\mu}\left(T_{m\geq d}=0|U=u\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A_{\nu,\eta}}P_{\mu}\left(\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}(T^{(j)}_{m\geq d}-1/2)\leq\sqrt{m}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}\bigg|U=u\right)dQ(u)

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{\nu,\eta}}P_{\mu}\left(\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}(T^{(j)}_{m\geq d}-1/2)\geq-\sqrt{m}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}\bigg|U=u\right)dQ(u)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+Q(A^{c}_{\nu,\eta}\cap B_{\nu,\eta}^{c}).

(21)

We show below that each of the first two terms on the right hand side are bounded by
\alpha/8
and the third term by
\alpha/4
, resulting our statement.

First we deal with the third term in (
21
). Since

\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}U_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{n}{d}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}),

the set
(A_{\nu,\eta}\cup B_{\nu,\eta})^{c}=A_{\nu,\eta}^{c}\cap B_{\nu,\eta}^{c}
, in view of Chebyshev’s inequality, assumption (
10
), and definitions (
20
), satisfies that

\displaystyle Q(A_{\nu,\eta}^{c}\cap B_{\nu,\eta}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Pr}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{d}}\|\mu\|_{2}|Z|\leq\nu\right)+Q(D_{\eta}^{c})\leq\frac{2\nu}{\sqrt{2\pi C_{\alpha}}}+\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\leq\alpha/4.

where
Z\sim N(0,1)
.

Next we deal with the first term on the right hand side of (
21
). For
u\in A_{\nu,\eta}
, we have
\|u\|_{2}/\sqrt{d}\leq\eta
, hence for
m\geq\nu^{2}/(2\eta^{2})
,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(-\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{m}}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}u_{i}Z_{i}\leq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\text{Pr}\left(-\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{m}\eta}<Z\leq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-\frac{\nu^{2}}{2m\eta^{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{m}\eta}\geq c^{\prime}\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{m}\eta},

where
Z,Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
and
c^{\prime}=(e\sqrt{2\pi})^{-1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{\mu}\left(S_{m\geq d}^{(j)}\geq 0\bigg|U=u\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{dm}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}u_{i}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}u_{i}Z_{i}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\text{Pr}\left(\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}u_{i}Z_{i}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\text{Pr}\left(-\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{m}}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}u_{i}Z_{i}\leq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}+c^{\prime}\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{\eta m}}.

Then in view of Chernoff’s bound

\displaystyle\int_{A_{\nu}}

\displaystyle P_{\mu}\left(\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}(T^{(j)}_{m\geq d}-1/2)\leq\sqrt{m}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}\bigg|U=u\right)dQ(u)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A_{\nu}}P_{\mu}\left(\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\left[T^{(j)}_{m\geq d}-E_{\mu}(T^{(j)}_{m\geq d}|U=u)\right]\leq\sqrt{m}\big(\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}-\frac{c^{\prime}\nu}{\sqrt{\eta}}\big)\bigg|U=u\right)dQ(u).

Since by definition (
20
)
\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}-c^{\prime}\nu/\sqrt{\eta}=-3\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}
, in view of Chernoff’s bound (Lemma
6
), for
36\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}<m
the preceding display is further bounded by
2e^{-3\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}^{2}}=\alpha/8
.

We can deal with the second term on the right hand side of (
21
) similarly. First, we obtain for
u\in B_{\nu,\eta}
that

P_{\mu}\left(S_{m\geq d}^{(j)}\geq 0\bigg|U=u\right)\leq 1/2-c^{\prime}\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{\eta m}}.

And then we can derive as above that

E_{Q}\text{1}_{B_{\nu}}P_{\mu}\left(\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\left[T^{(j)}_{m\geq d}-\frac{1}{2}\right]\geq-\sqrt{m}\tilde{\kappa}_{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha/8,

concluding the proof of case 2.

5.3
Case 3:
m\leq M_{\alpha}

Finally, for completeness, we deal with the case when the number of machines is limited and we are back bascially to the single server, centralized case from a minimax rate point of view.

Under the null hypothesis,
(n/m)\|X^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}
follows a chi-square distribution with
d
degrees of freedom, so by Chebyshev’s inequality

P_{0}\left(T_{m\asymp 1}=1\right)\leq 2/\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}=\alpha/2.

For any
\mu\in H_{\rho}
, it holds that

\displaystyle P_{\mu}\big(\frac{n}{\sqrt{d}m}

\displaystyle\|X^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{d}\leq\kappa_{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{n}{\sqrt{d}m}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2d^{-1/2}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}Z_{i}+d^{-1/2}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}(Z_{i}^{2}-1)\right|\leq\kappa_{\alpha}\right)

where
Z_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
,
i=1,...,d
. By the reverse triangle inequality, we can bound the previous probability with

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(\left|2d^{-1/2}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}Z_{i}\right|\geq\frac{n\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sqrt{d}m}\right)+\text{Pr}\left(\left|d^{-1/2}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}(Z_{i}^{2}-1)\right|\geq\frac{n\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sqrt{d}m}-\kappa_{\alpha}\right).

In view of Chebyshev’s inequality and
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\geq\rho^{2}\geq C_{\alpha}\sqrt{d}/n
,

\text{Pr}\left(\left|2d^{-1/2}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\mu_{i}Z_{i}\right|\geq\frac{n\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sqrt{d}m}\right)\leq\frac{16m^{2}}{n^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{16m^{2}}{C_{\alpha}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}

for
C_{\alpha}\geq 2^{6}M_{\alpha}^{2}/\alpha
. Likewise, for
C_{\alpha}\geq{2(1+\sqrt{2})\kappa_{\alpha}M_{\alpha}}

\frac{n\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sqrt{d}m}-\kappa_{\alpha}\geq\sqrt{2}\kappa_{\alpha}

and consequently, by applying again Chebyshev’s inequality, the second term is bounded by

\text{Pr}\left(\left|d^{-1/2}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}(Z_{i}^{2}-1)\right|\geq\sqrt{2}\kappa_{\alpha}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}.

Hence we can conclude that for
C_{\alpha}\geq\max\{2^{6}M_{\alpha}^{2}/\alpha,{2(1+\sqrt{2})\kappa_{\alpha}M_{\alpha}}\}
,

P_{\mu}\big(T_{m\asymp 1}=0\big)\leq\alpha/2,

concluding the proof of the theorem.

6
Lemmas for Theorem
2

In this section we collect the proofs of the lemmas used to derive the minimax testing upper bound.

Lemma 4
.

Let
U_{d}
and
V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}
be independent chi-square distributed random variables with
d
degree of freedom and non-centrality parameters zero and
\delta_{d}>0
, respectively. Then for a universal
\bar{D}\in\mathbb{N}
, not depending on
\delta_{d}
, we have for all
d\geq\bar{D}
that

\text{Pr}\left(V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}-U_{d}\geq 0\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{40}(\frac{{\delta_{d}}}{\sqrt{d}}\wedge\frac{1}{2}).

(22)

Proof.

First note that the function
\delta\mapsto\text{Pr}\left(V_{d}^{\delta}-U_{d}\geq 0\right)
is monotone increasing. Then

\text{Pr}\left(V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}-U_{d}\geq 0\right)\geq\text{Pr}\left(V_{d}^{\delta_{d}\wedge\sqrt{d}/2}-U_{d}\geq 0\right),

so without loss of generality we can assume that
\delta_{d}\leq\sqrt{d}/2
.

The density of
V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}
is

\underset{k=0}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}p_{d+2k},

where
p_{k}
denotes the
\chi^{2}_{k}
-density.
By the independence of
U_{d}
and
V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}
,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left(V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}-U_{d}\leq 0\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{k=0}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}\int_{\{v-u\geq 0\}}p_{d+2k}(v)p_{d}(u)d(v,u).

Let
U_{d}^{\prime}\sim\chi^{2}_{d}
and
U_{2k}^{\prime\prime}\sim\chi^{2}_{2k}
be independent from each other and
U_{d}
. For any given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{v-u\geq 0\}}p_{d+2k}(v)p_{d}(u)d(v,u)=\text{Pr}\left(U_{d}-U_{d}^{\prime}\leq U_{2k}^{\prime\prime}\right).

For convenience let us introduce the notation
W_{d}=(U_{d}-U_{d}^{\prime})/(2\sqrt{d})
. Conditioning and using independence once more, the latter equals

\int\text{Pr}\left(W_{d}\leq\frac{u}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)d{\mathbb{P}}_{U_{2k}^{\prime\prime}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}+\int\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{u}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)d{\mathbb{P}}_{U_{2k}^{\prime\prime}}(u).

Since
U_{2k}^{\prime\prime}
has a median larger than
2k/3
and the map
u\mapsto\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{u}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)
is increasing, we have that the second term in the last display satisfies

\displaystyle\int\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{u}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)p_{{2k}}(u)du

\displaystyle\geq\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{k}{3\sqrt{d}}\right)\int_{[\frac{2k}{3},\infty)}p_{{2k}}(u)du

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{k}{3\sqrt{d}}\right).

By combining the above inequalities we obtain that

\text{Pr}\left(V_{d}^{\delta_{d}}-U_{d}\leq 0\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\underset{k=0}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{k}{3\sqrt{d}}\right).

(23)

Assume now that
\delta_{d}\gtrsim 1
. Let
k_{d}
be the largest integer such that
k_{d}\leq 3\sqrt{d}
. We divide the sum on the right hand of the preceding display to two parts, i.e.
k<k_{d}
and
k\geq k_{d}
. By applying Lemma
5
with
\varepsilon_{d}=k
, it holds that for
c_{0}=e^{-9/8}/6
,

\displaystyle\underset{k=0}{\overset{k_{d}}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{k}{3\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{d}}\underset{k=1}{\overset{k_{d}}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{(k-1)!}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{0}{\delta_{d}}}{2\sqrt{d}}\underset{k=0}{\overset{k_{d}-1}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}.

We have
\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq 1\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\text{Pr}\left(0\leq Z\leq 1\right)>1/3
, hence there exists a

, such that for all
d\geq D_{1}
we have
\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq 1\right)>1/3
. For
k>k_{d}
we have
k>3\sqrt{d}
, hence for all
d\geq D_{1}
,

\displaystyle\underset{k>k_{d}}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{k}{3\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2}\underset{k>k_{d}}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}.

Since

, we have for

,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\underset{k=0}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k}}{k!}\text{Pr}\left(0\leq W_{d}\leq\frac{k}{3\sqrt{d}}\right)\geq\frac{c_{0}{\delta_{d}}}{2\sqrt{d}}(1-\frac{e^{-{\delta_{d}}/2}\left({\delta_{d}}/2\right)^{k_{d}}}{k_{d}!}).

The proof is finished by showing that for large enough

which is bounded from above by

for

, for some sufficiently large

.

∎

We prove a more general lemma, but before stating it we recall some information theoretic definitions and identities, see
[

,
20
]

For discrete random variables
X
and arbitrary random variable
Y
, define the
entropy of

X
as

H(X)=-\underset{x}{\overset{}{\sum}}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x)\log{\mathbb{P}}(X=x)

and the

X
given

H(X|Y)=E_{Y}H(X|Y=y)=-E_{Y}\underset{x}{\overset{}{\sum}}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x|Y=y)\log{\mathbb{P}}(X=x|Y=y).

We also recall that conditioning reduced entropy
H(X)\geq H(X|Y)
. Following from this conditioning, on an arbitrary random vector
Z
, also reduces conditional entropy

H(X|Y)=\int H(X|Y=y)dP_{Y}(y)\geq\int H(X|Y=y,Z)dP_{Y}(y)=H(X|Y,Z).

For random variables
X,Y,Z
we define the mutual information between
X
and
Y
and conditional mutual information between
X
and
Y
given
Z
as

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=D_{KL}(P_{(X,Y)}\|P_{X}\times P_{Y}),

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z=z)

\displaystyle=D_{KL}(P_{(X,Y)|Z=z}\|P_{X|Z=z}\times P_{Y|Z=z}),

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z)

\displaystyle=\int I(X;Y|Z=z)dP_{Z}(z).

Next we recall some conditions of the mutual information. First we note that
I(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
is independent from
Y
.
The chain rule for the mutual information between the random vector
Y=(Y^{(1)},...,Y^{(m)})
and
V
is

\displaystyle I(V;Y)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}I(V;Y^{(j)}|Y^{(1)},...,Y^{(j-1)}).

(25)

For discrete random variable
X
and arbitrary random variable
Y

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=E_{(X,Y)}\log\frac{dP_{(XY)}}{dP_{X}dP_{Y}}

(26)

\displaystyle=E_{(X,Y)}\log\frac{1}{dP_{X}}-E_{(X,Y)}\log\frac{1}{dP_{(X|Y=y)}}

(27)

\displaystyle=H(X)-H(X|Y).

(28)

In addition, by similar arguments, for arbitrary random variable

we have

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z)=H(X|Z)-H(X|Y,Z).

(29)

Lemma 7
.

Let us assume that the discrete random variable
V
and the discrete random vector
F
are such that the pair
(V,F)
is independent from the random variable
U
and the discrete random vector
Y=(Y_{1},...,Y_{m})
satisfies that
Y_{j}
is conditionally independent from
Y_{1:j-1}:=(Y_{1},...,Y_{j-1})
given
U
and
(V,F)
, then

I(V;Y)\leq\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I(V;Y_{j}|U){+\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I(F;Y_{j}|U,V)}.

Proof.

A non-public coin version of the lemma is given for instance in
[
21
]
.

First note that in view of (
28
) and since conditioning reduces entropy

\displaystyle I\big((Y,U);V\big)=H(V)-H(V|Y,U)\geq H(V)-H(V|Y)=I(Y;V).

Furthermore, by the chain rule (
25
) and the independence of
U
and
V
,

\displaystyle I\big((Y,U);V\big)=I\big(Y;V|U\big)+I(U;V)=I(Y;V|U).

Similarly, by the chain rule and nonnegativity of mutual information,

I\big(V;Y|U\big)=I\big((V,F);Y|U\big)-I\big(F;Y|U,V\big)\leq I\big((V,F);Y|U\big).

By the identity (
29
) and the chain rule (
25
),

\displaystyle I\big((V,F);Y|U\big)

\displaystyle=H(Y|U)-H(Y|V,F,U)

\displaystyle=\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}H(Y_{j}|Y_{1:j-1},U)-H(Y_{j}|V,F,Y_{1:j-1},U).

Since conditioning reduces entropy we have
H(Y_{j}|Y_{1:j-1},U)\leq H(Y_{j}|U)
. Furthermore, by the conditional independence of
Y_{1:j-1}
and
Y_{j}
given
(U,V,F)
results in
H(Y_{j}|V,F,Y_{1:j-1},U)=H(Y_{j}|V,F,U)
. Using these two facts, we obtain that

\displaystyle I\big((V,F);Y|U\big)

\displaystyle\leq\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}H(Y_{j}|,U)-H(Y_{j}|V,F,U)

\displaystyle=\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I\big((V,F);Y_{j}|U\big).

Combining the above displays and again applying the chain rule we now obtain that

I(Y;V)\leq\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}I\big((V,F);Y_{j}|U\big)=\underset{j=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}\left[I\big(V;Y_{j}|U\big)+I\big(F;Y_{j}|U,V\big)\right].

∎

7.4
Sub-Gaussianity lemma

First we recall some notations from Section
3.1
. Let us denote by
\Pi
the distribution of the random vector
\epsilon R
, where
R=(R_{1},\dots,R_{d})
has independent Rademacher marginals and
\epsilon>0
is small (it is taken to be
\epsilon=\rho/\sqrt{d}
). We take
V\sim Ber(1/2)
and set
X|(V=0)\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
and
X|(V=1)\sim P_{\Pi}
, where
P_{\Pi}=\int P_{\mu}d\Pi(\mu)
and
P_{\mu}
is a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
times the identity variance. Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{X}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{X|V}
denote the corresponding distributions of
X
and
X|V
.

Definition 1
.

A random variable
X
is called
\beta
-sub-Gaussian
if for all
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}e^{s(X-{\mathbb{E}}X)}\leq e^{\beta^{2}s^{2}/2}.

The lemma below shows that the likelihood ratios
\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X|V=0}}{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X}}(X)
and
\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X|V=1}}{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X}}(X)
are sub-Gaussian.

Lemma 8
.

The likelihood ratios

\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X|V=0}}{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X}}(X)\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X|V=1}}{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X}}(X)

are
\sqrt{24\beta}
-sub-Gaussian with

\beta=\begin{cases}d\epsilon^{4}/\sigma^{4},\;\;\;\text{ if }\sigma^{2}/\epsilon^{2}<d/2,\\
2\epsilon^{2}/\sigma^{2},\;\;\;\text{ if }\sigma^{2}/\epsilon^{2}\geq d/2.\end{cases}

(33)

Proof.

Using the notation

\mathscr{L}_{v}(X):=\frac{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X|V=v}}{d{\mathbb{P}}^{X}}(X),\hskip 9.24994ptv\in\{0,1\},

we show below that for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{X}e^{t(\mathscr{L}_{v}(X)-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\mathscr{L}_{v}(X))}\leq e^{24\beta t^{2}/2}.

This is implied by

{\mathbb{P}}^{X}\left(|\mathscr{L}_{v}-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\mathscr{L}_{v}|\geq s\right)\leq 12\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2\beta}\right)\,\,\,\text{ for all }s>0,

(34)

where the equivalence is well known, but a proof can be found in Lemma
11
. Since
|\mathscr{L}_{v}(X)-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\mathscr{L}_{v}(X)|=|\mathscr{L}_{v}(X)-1|\leq 1
, it is enough to consider
0<s<1
.

To prove (
34
), let us first introduce the notation
L:=\frac{dP_{\pi}}{dP_{0}}
, and note that

\mathscr{L}_{0}=\frac{2}{1+L}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathscr{L}_{1}=\frac{2}{1+L^{-1}}.

Then for
x\in\{\mathscr{L}_{0}-1\geq s\}
we have

\frac{2}{1+L}(x)=\mathscr{L}_{0}(x)\geq s+1\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ and }\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq\frac{2L}{1+L}(x)=1-\frac{1-L}{1+L}(x)\leq 1-s,

where the last inequality follows from
\mathscr{L}_{0}-1=\frac{1-L}{1+L}
.
Consequently,
L^{-1}(x)\geq\frac{s+1}{1-s}
. Similarly, for
x\in\{\mathscr{L}_{0}-1\leq-s\}
,

0\leq\frac{2}{1+L}(x)\leq 1-s\text{ and }\frac{2L}{1+L}(x)\geq 1+s

and thus

. Combining the above bounds results in for

that

Through the same computation, the above display is also true for

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{X}\left(|\mathscr{L}_{v}-{\mathbb{E}}\mathscr{L}_{v}|\geq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}^{X}\left(|\log L|\geq s\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}P_{0}\left(|\log(L)|\geq s\right)+\frac{1}{2}P_{\pi}\left(|\log(L)|\geq s\right).

Using Markov’s inequality the terms on the right hand side can be further bounded as

\displaystyle P_{0}\left(|\nu\log(L)|\geq\nu s\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\nu s}({\mathbb{E}}_{X|V=0}L^{\nu}+{\mathbb{E}}_{X|V=0}L^{-\nu})\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\,\text{ for }\nu>0,

(35)

\displaystyle P_{\pi}\left(|\lambda\log(L)|\geq\lambda s\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\lambda_{1}s}{\mathbb{E}}_{X|V=1}L^{\lambda_{1}}+e^{-\lambda_{2}s}{\mathbb{E}}_{X|V=1}L^{-\lambda_{2}}\;\text{ for }\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0.

(36)

Note that

\lambda_{1}=\nu-1
and
\lambda_{2}=\nu+1
in display (

\displaystyle e^{-s\nu}({\mathbb{E}}_{0}L^{\nu}+{\mathbb{E}}_{0}L^{-\nu})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\nu^{2}\frac{d\epsilon^{4}}{2\sigma^{4}}-s\nu\right)\left(1+e^{(3/2)\nu d\epsilon^{4}/\sigma^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\frac{\sigma^{4}}{d\epsilon^{4}}\right)\left(1+e^{(3/2)s}\right).

The remaining case is when
2/d>\epsilon^{2}/\sigma^{2}
. Choosing
\nu=s\sigma^{2}/(2\epsilon^{2})
results in
\nu\epsilon^{2}/\sigma^{2}<1/2
, hence again in view of Lemma
10

\displaystyle e^{-s\nu}({\mathbb{E}}_{0}L^{\nu}+{\mathbb{E}}_{0}L^{-\nu})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\nu^{2}\frac{d\epsilon^{4}}{2\sigma^{4}}-s\nu\right)\left(1+e^{(3/2)\nu d\epsilon^{4}/\sigma^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right)\left(1+e^{(3/2)s}\right).

Hence, by plugging in the last two displays into (
37
), and noting that for
0<s<1

\frac{1}{2}\left(1+e^{(3/2)s}\right)\left[1+e^{-s}+e^{s}\right]\leq 12,

we arrive at (
34
), for
\beta
given in (
33
), concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

[

The next lemma gives a well-known sufficient (and necessary) condition for the sub-Gaussian distribution. In the literature we did not find the present, required form of the lemma, hence for completeness we also provide its proof.

Lemma 11
.

Let
X
a mean-zero random variable satisfying

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X|\geq s\right)\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2\beta}\right)

for some
C\geq 2
,
\beta>0
and for all
s\in[0,\infty)
. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}e^{sX}\leq e^{2\beta Cs^{2}/2}.

Proof.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X|^{k}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(|X|^{k}>t\right)dt\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2/k}}{2\beta}\right)dt.

Changing coordinates to
u=t^{2/k}/(2\beta)
yields that the right hand side display equals

\displaystyle\frac{C}{2}(2\beta)^{k/2}k\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u}u^{k/2-1}du

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{2}(2\beta)^{k/2}k\Gamma(k/2).

By the dominated convergence theorem,
{\mathbb{E}}X=0
, and
C\geq 2
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}e^{sX}

\displaystyle=1+\underset{k=2}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{s^{k}{\mathbb{E}}X^{k}}{k!}\leq 1+\frac{C}{2}\underset{k=2}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{(2\beta s^{2})^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}{(k-1)!}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\underset{k=1}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\left[\frac{(C\beta s^{2})^{k}\Gamma(k)}{(2k-1)!}+\frac{(C\beta s^{2})^{k+1/2}\Gamma(k+1/2)}{(2k)!}\right].

Since
\Gamma(k+1/2)\leq\Gamma(k+1)=k\Gamma(k)=k!
and
(2k)!\geq 2^{k}(k!)^{2}
, the latter is further bounded by

\displaystyle 1+\left(1+\sqrt{{C}\beta s^{2}}\right)\underset{k=1}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{(C\beta s^{2}/2)^{k}}{k!}=e^{{C\beta s^{2}/2}}+{\sqrt{C\beta s^{2}}}(e^{{\beta Cs^{2}/2}}-1).

Since
(e^{x}-1)(e^{x}-\sqrt{x})\geq 0
, we obtain that

{\mathbb{E}}e^{sX}\leq e^{\frac{2C\beta s^{2}}{2}}.

∎

The following lemma is a well known result and follows from standard calculus, but we included it as we did not find a stand-alone proof.

Lemma 12
.

Let
Z
be
N(0,1)
,
0\leq\lambda\leq 1/4
. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda(Z^{2}-1)}\leq e^{2\lambda^{2}}.

Proof.

Using the change of variables
u=z\sqrt{1-2\lambda}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda(Z^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{\lambda(z^{2}-1)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-2\lambda)}}\int e^{-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}dz=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\sqrt{(1-2\lambda)}}.

The MacLaurin series of
-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-2\lambda)
reads

\frac{1}{2}\underset{k=1}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{(2\lambda)^{k}}{k},

which yields that the second last display equals

\exp\left(\frac{3}{2}\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\underset{k=3}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{(2\lambda)^{k}}{k}\right).

If
\lambda\leq 1/4
,

\underset{k=3}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\frac{(2\lambda)^{k}}{k}\leq\frac{(2\lambda)^{3}}{1-2\lambda}\leq\lambda^{2},

from which the result follows.
∎
