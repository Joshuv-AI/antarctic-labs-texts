Convergence analysis of Jacobi spectral collocation methods for weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equations with volume constraints
Jiashu Lu, Mengna Yang, Yufeng Nie

Abstract
This paper considers efficient spectral solutions for weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equations with Dirichlet-type volume constraints. The equation we consider contains an integral operator that typically has a singularity at the midpoint of the integral domain, and the approximation of the integral operator is one of the essential difficulties in solving nonlocal equations. To overcome this problem, two-sided Jacobi spectral quadrature rules are proposed to develop a Jacobi spectral collocation method for nonlocal diffusion equations. A rigorous convergence analysis of the proposed method with the L ∞ L^{\infty} norm is presented, and we further prove that the Jacobi collocation solution converges to its corresponding local limit as nonlocal interactions vanish. Numerical examples are given to verify the theoretical results.



1
Introduction

Nonlocal models with volume constraints have received much attention in the last two decades due to their capacity to represent physical phenomena that cannot be effectively characterized by partial differential equation (PDE) models. Unlike local PDE models, nonlocal models replace the usual spatial differential operators with integral operators. Thus, the usual continuity and smoothness requirements for displacements are eliminated, which makes it possible to describe discontinuities, such as crack propagation
[
1
]
and the general Markov jump processes in bounded domains
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we consider weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equations with volume constraints
[
3
]
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, open domain in
\mathbb{R}
. By defining
\delta
as the scope of the nonlocal interaction, the corresponding interaction domain is then defined as

\Omega_{c}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\Omega:\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\leq\delta\}.

Then, nonlocal diffusion equations with Dirichlet-type volume constraints can be formulated as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(x)=f(x),&x\in\Omega,\;\\
&u(x)=g(x),&x\in\Omega_{c},\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
f
and
g
are the prescribed functions and
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
denotes the weakly singular nonlocal diffusion operator, which is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(x)=\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}\gamma_{\delta}(x,y)\frac{1}{|y-x|^{\mu}}\left(u(y)-u(x)\right)dy,

(2)

where
0<\mu<1
and
\gamma_{\delta}
denote nonnegative and symmetric radial-type kernel functions.

Although nonlocal models can provide better modeling capabilities than traditional PDE models, they often lead to more computational difficulties when usual discretization methods are used. Such difficulties mainly come from the following two parts: the approximation of a singular nonlocal integral, and the numerical solution that yielded from denser discrete equations compared with the solutions to discrete PDE models. To date, many works have been performed to overcome one of the above difficulties. For example, a fast convolution-based method
[
4
]
reduces the computational cost of a singular nonlocal integral, a fast Fourier transform (FFT)-type method
[
5
,
6
]
achieves high computing efficiency in the process of solving discrete equations, a localized radial bias function (RBF) collocation method significantly reduces the condition number of a nonlocal stiffness matrix
[
7
]
, and a proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)-based fast algorithm
[
8
]
accelerates the computation of dense discrete equations in time iterations. However, limited work has been done to overcome both of the above two difficulties, and we fill this gap by developing a Jacobi spectral collocation method in this paper.

Spectral methods have now become one of the most popularly used methods for the discretization of spatial variables in solving PDEs
[
9
,
10
]
since they can provide very accurate approximations for sufficiently smooth solutions, and they have been successfully used to solve integral equations, e.g., Volterra integral equations
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
, Fredholm integral equations
[
14
,
15
]
and fractional differential equations
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. In
[
19
]
, Tian et al. developed a Legendre spectral method for nonlocal diffusion equations that can achieve a fixed accuracy with fewer unknowns; therefore, it alleviates the latter part of the above computational difficulties by significantly reducing the degrees of freedom of the discrete equations. In light of the above work, we develop a Jacobi spectral collocation method for weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equations with rigorous convergence analyses. Inspired by
[
20
]
, two-sided Jacobi spectral quadrature rules are proposed to accurately approximate singular nonlocal integrals. Combined with the above ideas, we completely overcome the two computational difficulties brought by the nonlocality of a nonlocal operator. Furthermore, we provide relevant theoretical analyses, which show that the numerical solution of a nonlocal diffusion equation converges to the correct local limit as
\delta\rightarrow 0
and
N\rightarrow\infty
. To verify the theoretical result, numerical experiments are also presented for nonlocal diffusion equations with volume constraints.

The layout of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, the Jacobi spectral collocation methods are presented for weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equations. Some useful lemmas are provided in Section 3 for convergence and asymptotic compatibility analysis. An error analysis using the
L^{\infty}
norm is given in Section 4, and we present an asymptotic compatibility analysis using the
L^{2}
norm in Section 5. Numerical experiments are carried out in Section 6 to justify the theoretical results and demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed methods. In Section 7, we close the paper with some conclusions.

2
Jacobi-collocation methods

Without loss of generality, we choose
\Omega=I:=(-1,1)
for convenience, and we note that one can treat more general domains through a simple linear transformation. Here,
\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
is the Jacobi weight function with
\alpha,\beta>-1
and
x\in I
.

Let
L^{2}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}(I)
be a weighted Hilbert space

L^{2}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}(I)=\left\{v:v\text{ is measurable and }||v||_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}<\infty\right\},

equipped with the norm

||v||_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}=\left(\int_{-1}^{1}|v(x)|^{2}\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the weighted inner product

(u,v)_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}=\int_{-1}^{1}u(x)v(x)\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx.

(3)

Before presenting the Jacobi collocation scheme for the nonlocal diffusion models, we rewrite the model equation in (
1
) as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &C_{\delta}u(x)-\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}\gamma_{\delta}(x,y)\frac{1}{|y-x|^{\mu}}u(y)dy=f(x),&x\in I,\;\\
&u(x)=g(x),&x\in I_{c}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

where

C_{\delta}=\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}\gamma_{\delta}(x,y)\frac{1}{|y-x|^{\mu}}dy=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\gamma_{\delta}(0,y)\frac{1}{|y|^{\mu}}dy

(5)

To approximate the nonlocal integral in (
4
), we introduce two-sided Jacobi spectral quadrature rules. First, by denoting

S_{1}u(x)=\int_{x-\delta}^{x}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{1})\frac{1}{(x-s_{1})^{\mu}}u(s_{1})ds_{1},\quad S_{2}u(x)=\int_{x}^{x+\delta}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{2})\frac{1}{(s_{2}-x)^{\mu}}u(s_{2})ds_{2},\quad x\in I,

(6)

and employing the following change of variables:

s_{1}(x,\theta)=\frac{2x-\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta,\quad s_{2}(x,\theta)=\frac{2x+\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta,\quad\theta\in[-1,1],

(7)

one can rewrite (
4
) as

\displaystyle C_{\delta}u(x)-S_{1}u(x)-S_{2}u(x)

(8)

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}u(x)-\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}(1-\theta)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{1}(x,\theta))u(s_{1}(x,\theta))d\theta+\int_{-1}^{1}(1+\theta)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{2}(x,\theta))u(s_{2}(x,\theta))d\theta\right)

\displaystyle=f(x).

For a given positive integer
N
, we denote the collocation points by
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
, which is the set of
N+1
Jacobi-Gauss-Lobatto points
[
9
]
corresponding to the weight function
\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(x)
, and we let
\mathbb{P}_{N}
be the space of all polynomials with degrees not exceeding
N
. Then, the two-sided Jacobi spectral quadrature rules are given to approximate
S_{1}(x_{i})
and
S_{2}(x_{i})
using Jacobi spectral quadratures with different weight functions.

Let
\{\theta^{1}_{j},\omega^{1}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
and
\{\theta^{2}_{j},\omega^{2}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
be the sets of Jacobi-Gauss points and weights with weight functions
\omega^{-\mu,0}(x)
and
\omega^{0,-\mu}
, respectively. For
\xi=1,2
, we set

\displaystyle H^{\xi}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})=\{j=1,...,M:s_{\xi}(x_{i},\theta^{\xi}_{j})\in I\},

\displaystyle H^{\xi}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})=\{j=1,...,M:s_{\xi}(x_{i},\theta^{\xi}_{j})\in I_{c}\},

Then, the Jacobi collocation scheme for (
8
) becomes a process of finding the approximate solution
u_{N}\in\mathbb{P}_{N}
such that

\displaystyle C_{\delta}u_{N}(x_{i})-\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left(\sum_{j\in H^{1}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{1}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))u_{N}(s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))+\sum_{j\in H^{2}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))u_{N}(s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=f(x_{i})+\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left(\sum_{j\in H^{1}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{1}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))g(s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))+\sum_{j\in H^{2}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))g(s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))\right).

In this paper, the case
M=N
is selected for convenience of analysis, and one can treat the other cases similarly. Then, the Lagrange interpolation polynomial
I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}u\in\mathbb{P}_{N}
corresponding to function
u
is defined as

I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}u(x_{i})=u(x_{i}),\quad 0\leq i\leq N.

We seek an approximate solution
u_{N}(x)
of the form

u_{N}(x)=I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}u(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}u_{k}h_{k}(x),

(10)

where
\{h_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{N+1}
is the Lagrange interpolation basis function.

Substituting (
10
) into (
9
) leads to

\displaystyle C_{\delta}u_{i}-\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}u_{k}\left(\sum_{j\in H^{1}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{1}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))h_{k}(s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))+\sum_{j\in H^{2}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))h_{k}(s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=f(x_{i})+\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left(\sum_{j\in H^{1}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{1}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))g(s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))+\sum_{j\in H^{2}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))g(s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))\right).

By denoting
\mathbf{u}_{N}=(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{N+1})
, we can rewrite (
11
) in the following matrix form:

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{N}=\mathbf{F},

(12)

where the entries of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
can be computed as

(\mathbf{A})_{i,k}=C_{\delta}\delta_{ki}-\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left(\sum_{j\in H^{1}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{1}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))h_{k}(s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))+\sum_{j\in H^{2}_{\text{in}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))h_{k}(s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))\right),

where
\delta_{ki}
is the Kronecker-Delta symbol, and the entries of the source vector
\mathbf{F}
are

\small(\mathbf{F})_{i}=f(x_{i})+\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left(\sum_{j\in H^{1}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{1}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))g(s_{1}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{1}))+\sum_{j\in H^{2}_{\text{out}}(x_{i})}\omega_{j}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))g(s_{2}(x_{i},\theta_{j}^{2}))\right).

Hence, one can obtain the coefficient vector
\mathbf{u}_{N}
by solving (
12
). The solvability theorem of the linear system in (
11
) is given in Section
4
.

3
Some useful lemmas

To obtain the convergence analysis, we introduce some useful lemmas in this section. First, for a nonnegative integer
m
, we define

H^{m}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}(I):=\{v:\partial^{k}_{x}v\in L^{2}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}(I),0\leq k\leq m\},

which is equipped with the norm

||v||_{m,\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}||\partial^{k}_{x}v||^{2}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Then, it is convenient to introduce the following seminorm:

|v|_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}^{m;N}:=\left(\sum_{k=\min(m,N+1)}^{m}||\partial^{k}_{x}v||_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We further denote
H^{r}(I):=H^{r}_{\omega^{0,0}}(I)
as a regular Hilbert space equipped with the norm
||v||_{m}
and the seminorm
|v|^{m;N}
. Moreover, for any
u,v\in C[-1,1]
, we define a discrete inner product
\left<u,v\right>_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta},N}
as

\left<u,v\right>_{N,\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}u(\theta_{i})v(\theta_{i})\omega_{i},

where
\{\theta_{i},\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
represents the Gauss points and Gauss weights corresponding to the weight functions
\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(x)
.

.

[

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
x_{1}<x_{2}
and
0<\mu<1
; the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}-\delta}^{x_{1}}\left[(x_{1}-\tau)^{-\mu}-(x_{2}-\tau)^{-\mu}\right]d\tau\leq c|x_{2}-x_{1}|^{1-\mu},

(13a)

\displaystyle\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{2}+\delta}\left[(\tau-x_{2})^{-\mu}-(\tau-x_{1})^{-\mu}\right]d\tau\leq c|x_{2}-x_{1}|^{1-\mu},

(13b)

where

is a constant that depends on
\mu
.

Proof.

Since
x_{1}<x_{2}
, we have

\left[(x_{1}-\tau)^{-\mu}-(x_{2}-\tau)^{-\mu}\right]>0,\quad\tau\in[x_{1}-\delta,x_{1}],

which leads to

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}-\delta}^{x_{1}}\left[(x_{1}-\tau)^{-\mu}-(x_{2}-\tau)^{-\mu}\right]d\tau

(14)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-1-\delta}^{x_{1}}\left[(x_{1}-\tau)^{-\mu}-(x_{2}-\tau)^{-\mu}\right]d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{-1-\delta}^{x_{1}}(x_{1}-\tau)^{-\mu}d\tau-\int_{-1-\delta}^{x_{2}}(x_{2}-\tau)^{-\mu}d\tau\right|+\left|\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}(x_{2}-\tau)^{-\mu}d\tau\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left[\left(\frac{x_{2}+1+\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}-\left(\frac{x_{1}+1+\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\right]\int_{-1}^{1}(1-\theta)^{-\mu}d\theta+\frac{|x_{2}-x_{1}|^{1-\mu}}{1-\mu},

where the last inequality follows from the linear transformation

\tau=\frac{x_{\xi}+1+\delta}{2}\theta+\frac{x_{\xi}-1-\delta}{2},\quad\xi=1,2.

One can easily observe that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x_{2}+1+\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}-\left(\frac{x_{1}+1+\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}=\frac{1-\mu}{2^{1-\mu}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}(y+1+\delta)^{-\mu}dy

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\mu}{2^{1-\mu}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}(y-x_{1})^{-\mu}dy=2^{\mu-1}|x_{2}-x_{1}|^{1-\mu},

where we use the fact that

. Combining (

). One can prove the estimate in (

4
Convergence analysis

First, we present a solvability theorem for the proposed numerical method (
11
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
0<\mu<1
and
-1<\alpha,\beta\leq 1
. Then, the collocation scheme in (
11
) leads to a unique numerical solution
u_{N}\in\mathbb{P}_{N}
, where
N
is sufficiently large.

Proof.

We prove that the assumptions
f(x)=0,x\in I
and
g(x)=0,x\in I_{c}
lead to a unique solution
u_{N}=0
. First, we define

\bar{u}_{N}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}u_{i}h_{i}(x),&\forall x\in I,\;\\
&g(x),&\forall x\in I_{c}.\end{aligned}\right.

Thus, the discrete equation in (
11
) can be written as

C_{\delta}\bar{u}_{N}(x_{i})-\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left[\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{-\mu,0}}+\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right]=0,

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\bar{u}_{N}(x)-I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}\left\{\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left[\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{1}(x,\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x,\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{-\mu,0}}+\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{2}(x,\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x,\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}\bar{u}_{N}(x)-S\bar{u}_{N}(x)-I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G(x)=0,

where

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left\{\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{1}(x,\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x,\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{-\mu,0}}-\left(\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{1}(x,\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x,\cdot))\right)_{\omega^{-\mu,0}}\right\}

(16)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left\{\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{2}(x,\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x,\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{0,-\mu}}-\left(\gamma_{\delta}(x,s_{2}(x,\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x,\cdot))\right)_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right\}.

Then, it is clear that

C_{\delta}\bar{u}_{N}(x)-S\bar{u}_{N}(x)=I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G(x).

The use of Lemma
3.6
implies that

||\bar{u}_{N}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq c||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G||_{L^{\infty}(I)}.

(17)

Directly from Lemma
3.1
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}G||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq N+1}|G(x_{i})|\max_{x\in I}\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}|h_{i}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq c||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}||_{\infty}N^{-m}\times

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N+1}\left(|\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))|_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}^{m;N}||\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))||_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}+|\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))|_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}^{m;N}||\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))||_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right).

A direct computation leads to

||\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))||_{\omega^{-\mu,0}}=\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1-\mu}{2}}\left(\int_{x_{i}-\delta}^{x_{i}}|\bar{u}_{N}(s)|^{2}(x_{i}-s)^{-\mu}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c||\bar{u}_{N}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}.

Similarly,

||\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))||_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}\leq c||\bar{u}_{N}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}.

Hence, we have

||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq c||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}||_{\infty}K^{*}_{m}N^{-m}||u_{N}||_{L^{\infty}(I)},

(18)

where

K^{*}_{m}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N+1}\left\{|\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))|_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}^{m;N}+|\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))|_{\omega^{-\mu,0}}^{m;N}\right\}.

The combination of (
17
), (
18
) and Lemma
3.2
implies that the assumptions
f(x)=0,x\in I
and
g(x)=0,x\in I_{c}
lead to
u_{N}=0
when
N
is sufficiently large. Hence, the spectral collocation solution
u_{N}
exists and is unique, as
\mathbb{P}_{N}
is finite dimensional.
∎

Then, we present a convergence analysis for the proposed numerical method in (
11
) with the
L^{\infty}
norm.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume
0<\mu<1
and
-1<\alpha,\beta\leq 1
. Let
u
and
u_{N}
be the solutions of the nonlocal diffusion equation in (
1
) and the collocation scheme in (
11
), respectively. If
u\in H^{r}(I)
and
r\geq 1
is an integer, then for sufficiently large
N
, we have the following error estimate:

\displaystyle||u-u_{N}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &c\ln N\left[K^{*}_{m}N^{-m}||u||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+N^{\frac{1}{2}-r}|u|^{r;N}\right],&-1<\alpha,\beta\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
&c\left[K^{*}_{m}N^{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}-m}||u||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+N^{1+\gamma-r}|u|^{r;N}\right],&\textup{otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

(19)

where
\gamma=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}
and
c
is a positive constant independent of
N,r,u
.

Proof.

First, we define

\bar{u}_{N}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}u_{i}h_{i}(x),&\forall x\in I,\;\\
&g(x),&\forall x\in I_{c}.\end{aligned}\right.

Inserting the collocation points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
into the nonlocal diffusion equation in (
8
) leads to

\displaystyle C_{\delta}u(x_{i})-f(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left[\left(\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot)),u(s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))\right)_{\omega^{-\mu,0}}+\left(\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot)),u(s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))\right)_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right]

for
1\leq i\leq N+1
. Then, (
9
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\bar{u}_{N}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left[\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{-\mu,0}}+\left<\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot)),\bar{u}_{N}(s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))\right>_{N,\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right],

for
1\leq i\leq N+1
.

Denoting
e=u-u_{N}
and
\bar{e}=u-\bar{u}_{N}
, we have the following error equation:

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\bar{e}(x_{i})=\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)^{1-\mu}\left[\left(\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot)),\bar{e}(s_{1}(x_{i},\cdot))\right)_{\omega^{-\mu,0}}+\left(\gamma_{\delta}(x_{i},s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot)),\bar{e}(s_{2}(x_{i},\cdot))\right)_{\omega^{0,-\mu}}\right]-G(x_{i}),

(20)

where
G(x)
is defined in (
16
). Then, we rewrite (
20
) as

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\left(I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}u(x)-\bar{u}_{N}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}\left(\int_{x-\delta}^{x}(x-s)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s)\bar{e}(s)ds+\int_{x}^{x+\delta}(s-x)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s)\bar{e}(s)ds\right)-I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G(x).

Consequently,

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\bar{e}

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}(u-\bar{u}_{N})

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}\left(u-I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}u+I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}u-\bar{u}_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}|s-x|^{-\mu}\left(\bar{e}(s)-\bar{e}(x)\right)ds

\displaystyle+G_{1}+G_{2}-I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}G,

where

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}(u-I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}u)

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}\left(\int_{x-\delta}^{x}(x-s)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s)\bar{e}(s)ds\right)-\int_{x-\delta}^{x}(x-s)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s)\bar{e}(s)ds

\displaystyle+I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}\left(\int_{x}^{x+\delta}(s-x)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s)\bar{e}(s)ds\right)-\int_{x}^{x+\delta}(s-x)^{-\mu}\gamma_{\delta}(x,s)\bar{e}(s)ds.

It is worthwhile to note that
\bar{u}_{N}(x)=u(x)
for
x\in I_{c}
, which leads to
\bar{e}(x)=0
for
x\in I_{c}
. Thus, by Lemma
3.6
,

||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}=||e||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq c\left(||G_{1}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||G_{2}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}G||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\right).

(21)

From (
18
), we have

||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq c||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}||_{\infty}K^{*}_{m}N^{-m}(||u||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}).

(22)

Combining estimate (
22
) and Lemma
3.2
, for sufficiently large
N
, we can obtain that

||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}G||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{3}||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+cK^{*}_{m}N^{-m}\ln N||u||_{L^{\infty}(I)},&-1<\alpha,\beta\leq\-\frac{1}{2},\\
&\frac{1}{3}||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+cK^{*}_{m}N^{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}-m}||u||_{L^{\infty}(I)},&\textup{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

Furthermore, using Lemma
3.1
and the Sobolev inequality
[
9
]
gives

\displaystyle||G_{1}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}||u-I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}u||_{L^{\infty}(I)}=C_{\delta}||u-I_{N}u+I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}(I_{N}u-u)||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}(1+||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}||_{\infty})||u-I_{N}u||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}(1+||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}||_{\infty})||u-I_{N}u||_{\omega^{0,0}}^{\frac{1}{2}}||u-I_{N}u||_{1,\omega^{0,0}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq c(1+||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}||_{\infty})N^{\frac{1}{2}-r}|u|_{\omega^{0,0}}^{r;N},

where
I_{N}
denotes the Legendre polynomial interpolation operator. Therefore, the combination of the above estimate and Lemma
3.2
implies

||G_{1}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &cN^{\frac{1}{2}-r}\ln N|u|_{\omega^{0,0}}^{r;N},&-1<\alpha,\beta\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
&cN^{1+\gamma-r}|u|_{\omega^{0,0}}^{r;N},&\textup{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(24)

Finally, from Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle||G_{2}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}\left(S_{1}\bar{e}\right)-S_{1}\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}\left(S_{2}\bar{e}\right)-S_{2}\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}\left(S_{1}\bar{e}\right)-T_{N}\left(S_{1}\bar{e}\right)||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||T_{N}\left(S_{1}\bar{e}\right)-S_{1}\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle+||I^{\alpha,\beta}_{N}\left(S_{2}\bar{e}\right)-T_{N}\left(S_{2}\bar{e}\right)||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||T_{N}\left(S_{2}\bar{e}\right)-S_{2}\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq(1+||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}||_{\infty})\left(||T_{N}\left(S_{1}\bar{e}\right)-S_{1}\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}+||T_{N}\left(S_{2}\bar{e}\right)-S_{2}\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c(1+||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}||_{\infty})N^{-\kappa}\left(||S_{1}\bar{e}||_{C^{0,\kappa}}+||S_{2}\bar{e}||_{C^{0,\kappa}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c(1+||I_{N}^{\alpha,\beta}||_{\infty})N^{-\kappa}||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}.

Thus, according to Lemma
3.2
and the above estimate, we have

||G_{2}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &cN^{-\kappa}\ln N||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)},&-1<\alpha,\beta\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
&cN^{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}|\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)},&\textup{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(25)

Then, under the assumption that
\kappa\geq 0
and
\gamma+\frac{1}{2}-\kappa<0
, for sufficiently large
N
, we have

||G_{2}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\frac{1}{3}||\bar{e}||_{L^{\infty}(I)}.

Combining (
21
), (
23
), (
24
) and (
25
) gives the desired estimate in (
19
).
∎

5
Asymptotic compatibility

We now prove the asymptotic compatibility of the spectral collocation methods, which means that the spectral collocation solution converges to its correct local limit as
1/N
and
\delta
both go to zero. This provides natural links between nonlocal models and traditional PDE models. Here,
u_{0}
is denoted as the solution of the following traditional diffusion equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-Cu_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)=f_{0}(x),&x\in I\;\\
&u_{0}(x)=g_{0}(x),&x\in\partial I.\end{aligned}\right.

(26)

Then, the asymptotic relation between
u_{0}
and
u_{N}
is established in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that all the conditions in Theorem
4.2
hold. Let
u,u_{N}
be the solutions of the nonlocal diffusion equation in (
1
) and the collocation scheme in (
9
), respectively. If
u\in H^{m}(I)\cap C^{4}(I)
and
m\geq 1
is an integer,
g(x)=g_{0}(x)
for
x\in\partial I
, and

||f(x)-f_{0}(x)||_{\omega^{0,0}}\leq c\delta^{\lambda}.

We further assume that the kernel function
\gamma_{\delta}
satisfies

\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}\gamma_{\delta}(x,y)\frac{1}{|y-x|^{\mu}}(y-x)^{2}dy=2C.

(27)

Then, it holds that

||u_{N}-u_{0}||_{\omega^{0,0}}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &O(N^{-m}\ln N)+O(N^{\frac{1}{2}-r}\ln N)+O(\delta^{\min\{2,\lambda\}}),&-1<\alpha,\beta\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
&O(N^{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}-m})+O(N^{1+\gamma-r})++O(\delta^{\min\{2,\lambda\}}),&\textup{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

For
x\in I
and
y\in[x-\delta,x+\delta]
, by Taylor’s theorem,

u(y)-u(x)=u^{\prime}(x)(y-x)+\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)(y-x)^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}u^{(3)}(x)(y-x)^{3}+u^{(4)}(\xi)(y-x)^{4},

(28)

where
\xi\in[\min\{x,y\},\max\{x,y\}]
. Substituting (
28
) into (
1
) and using (
27
) yields

-\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}\gamma_{\delta}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\mu}}\left(u(y)-u(x)\right)dy=-Cu^{\prime\prime}(x)+O(\delta^{2}).

(29)

Then, combining (
26
) and (
29
) leads to

-C\left(u-u_{0}\right)^{\prime\prime}=f(x)-f_{0}(x)+O(\delta^{2}).

Based on the regularity of the solutions of Poisson’s equation, we have

||u-u_{0}||_{2,\omega^{0,0}}\leq||f(x)-f_{0}(x)+O(\delta^{2})||_{\omega^{0,0}}=O(\delta^{\min\{2,\lambda\}}),

(30)

where we use the assumption that
(u-u_{0})|_{\partial I}=0
. The combination of the triangle inequality, Theorem
4.2
and (
30
) implies

\displaystyle||u_{N}-u_{0}||_{\omega^{0,0}}

\displaystyle\leq||u_{N}-u||_{\omega^{0,0}}+||u-u_{0}||_{\omega^{0,0}}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &O(N^{-m}\ln N)+O(N^{\frac{1}{2}-r}\ln N)+O(\delta^{\min\{2,\lambda\}}),&-1<\alpha,\beta\leq-\frac{1}{2},\\
&O(N^{\gamma+\frac{1}{2}-m})+O(N^{1+\gamma-r})+O(\delta^{\min\{2,\lambda\}}),&\textup{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we develop a Jacobi spectral collocation method for weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equations with volume constraints. To reach high-order accuracy for the approximation of a weak singular nonlocal integral, two-sided Jacobi spectral quadrature rules are established, and the spectral rate of convergence for the proposed method is established with the
L^{\infty}
norm. Moreover, we theoretically show that the discrete solution obtained by applying the Jacobi collocation method to a weakly singular nonlocal diffusion equation converges to the correct local limit as nonlocal interactions vanish. The numerical results are presented to verify the effectiveness and asymptotic compatibility of the proposed method. The possibility of extending such spectral collocation methods to nonlocal diffusion models in high dimensions will be investigated in future works.
