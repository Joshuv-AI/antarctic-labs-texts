Abstract
. We show that any finite coloring of an amenable group contains ‘many’ monochromatic sets of the form { x , y , x ​ y , y ​ x } , \{x,y,xy,yx\}, and natural extensions with more variables. This gives the first combinatorial proof of Bergelson and McCutcheon’s non-commutative Schur theorem, together with extensions. Our main new tool is the introduction of what we call ‘quasirandom colorings,’ a condition that is automatically satisfied by colorings of quasirandom groups, and a reduction to this case.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we will be concerned with the following conjectures of Bergelson and McCutcheon. Given a group
G
, let
A=\{g\in G:[G:C(g)]<\infty\},
where
C(g)
is the centralizer of
g
in
G
. In the following and throughout, a group is called
sufficiently non-commutative
if
[G:A]=\infty
. In amenable groups, this implies that the probability that two elements commute is zero and Tointon
[
Toi20
]
has shown that any finitely generated amenable group which is not virtually abelian is sufficiently non-commutative.

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
BR09
]
, Conjecture 2.3)
.

Suppose that a sufficiently non-commutative group is finitely colored. There is a monochromatic set of the form
\{x,y,xy,yx\}
with
xy\neq yx.

Question 1.2

(
[
BM98
]
, the paragraph before Theorem 3.4)
.

Suppose that a sufficiently non-commutative group is finitely colored. Is there a sequence
x_{1},...,x_{k}
with all possible products (in any order) monochromatic and distinct?

While the above conjectures are still quite open for general groups (and the free group on two generators in particular), a number of special cases have been closely analyzed and resolved. Namely, Bergelson and McCutcheon
[
BM98
,
BM07
]
were able to prove Conjecture
1.1
for colorings of infinite amenable groups. For finite groups (which are always amenable), Sanders
[
San19
]
later proved an analogous result, extending work of Bergelson and Tao
[
BT14
]
, who had proven a stronger density result in the more restrictive setting of finite quasirandom groups. Results similar to those of Bergelson and Tao, with good quantitative bounds, were also obtained by Austin
[
Aus15
]
and Tserunyan
[
Tse23
]
. For Question
1.2
, there appear to be no special cases where even the
k=3
case is fully known, with the main progress so far due to Bergelson, Christopherson, Robertson, and Zorin-Kranich, who proved a density version of a weaker form of the conjecture for infinite amenable quasirandom groups
[
BCRZK16
]
.

A peculiar aspect shared by all of the previously mentioned papers is that they deduce their coloring results from a ‘non-commutative Roth theorem,’ i.e., a density result which controls the pattern
\{x,xy,yx\}.
Moreover, it appears that there was no previously known direct ‘coloring’ proof of any special case of Conjecture
1.1
. This is in contrast with most classical results in Ramsey theory, where coloring results are typically much easier than their density counterparts and are often known well in advance of them. It has also served as a major bottleneck in extending progress on Conjecture
1.1
and Question
1.2
beyond the
k=2
case (where non-commutative measure recurrence results seem to quickly become elusive) and the setting of amenable groups (where density stops being a viable tool altogether).

In this paper we give a direct coloring proof of the amenable case of Conjecture
1.1
which extends to handle some of the previously unknown subcases of Question
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
G
is an infinite amenable group with
G
-invariant finitely additive probability measure
\mu.
In any finite coloring of
G
we have

\mu(\{y:\mu(\{x:\{x,y,xy,yx\}\textnormal{ is monochromatic}\})>0\})>0.

More generally, for any
N
we can find many

2.5
. Also, for a subset
I\subseteq\{m,...,n\},
the notation
\overrightarrow{\prod}_{a\in I}

tuples
(x_{N},...,x_{0})
for which the set

\{x_{i}\cdot x_{i+1}\cdot...\cdot x_{j},\overrightarrow{\prod_{a\in I}}x_{a}\cdot x_{0}\cdot x_{1}\cdot...\cdot x_{j}:0\leq i\leq j\leq N,I\subseteq\{j+1,...,N\}\}

is monochromatic.

Note that one wishes to find a monochromatic set of size
15
in the
k=3
case of Question

, corresponding to all non-repeating products in any order of terms

In this case, the second part of Theorem

controls

Up to keeping track of extra parameters, one can also obtain lower bounds on the measure of the sets in Theorem

and similarly deduce a version of the theorem for finite groups. The bounds this would give are fairly poor and most likely far from optimal, so we will omit the details for ease of exposition.

Using a more basic form of the same idea, we also make the following progress on the non-amenable case of Conjecture
1.1
.

Proposition 1.4
.

In any finite coloring of a group we have

\{y:\{x:\{x,xy,yx\}\textnormal{ is monochromatic}\}\textnormal{ is piecewise syndetic}\}

is piecewise syndetic.

A similar result was proven by Bergelson and Hindman in
[
BH92
]
using idempotent ultrafilters. The argument we give here appears to be the first combinatorial proof of this fact and primarily relies on a ‘piecewise syndetic pigeonhole principle’ (see Proposition

\{x,xy\}.
Unfortunately the notion of ‘many’ that appears here is less robust than the one appearing in the ‘density pigeonhole principle’ for amenable groups (see Lemma

1.1.
Techniques

The basic idea behind the proof of Theorem
1.3
was inspired by the author’s recent work with Sabok
[
Bow25
,
BS24
]
on finding monochromatic sets of the form
\{x,y,xy,x+y\}
in colorings of
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Q},
although the execution of the idea is quite different in the present paper. The starting point of these results is that it is fairly easy, using standard dynamical color focusing arguments, to prove a weaker result controlling the color of all terms excluding
y.
From here one then argues that if the coloring is ‘sufficiently random’ then this color focusing argument can be upgraded to also control the color of
y.
Otherwise, the coloring must be fairly structured and the desired conclusion can be deduced by exploiting this structure.

The primary difference between the arguments in the present paper and those in
[
Bow25
,
BS24
]
comes from which definition of ‘sufficiently random’ is useful. In
[
Bow25
,
BS24
]
the useful structure-versus-randomness dichotomy came from the dichotomy between thick and syndetic sets. While the thick case of the arguments from those papers is applicable to the present problem, it seems that syndeticity is a much less useful condition in the non-commutative setting.

Instead, the notion of randomness that we develop in the present paper is related to the properties of colorings of what are known as
quasirandom
groups in the finite setting
[
Gow08
]
and
weak mixing

[
BF09
]
or
minimally almost periodic

[
Neu34
]
groups in the infinite setting. Gowers in the finite setting
[
Gow08
]
and Bergelson and Furstenberg in the infinite setting
[
BF09
]
showed that, when amenable, such groups behave pseudorandomly in the sense that all of their large subsets contain roughly the expected number of finite product sets of a given length, and moreover, that it is easy to ‘switch’ between any large sets via multiplication in the sense that if
A,B\subset G
then there are roughly the expected number of
a\in A
and
b\in B
with
ab\in A.

Inspired by this, we will call a coloring of an arbitrary amenable group
quasirandom
if every color class has roughly the expected number of finite product sets of each length (see Section
4
for precise definitions) and deduce that in such colorings it is very easy to switch between color classes via multiplication in a sense similar to before. It turns out this latter property is precisely what is needed to upgrade the color focusing proof that deals with the configuration
\{x,xy,yx\}
to also control the color of the
y
term. This is explained in Section
5.1
, where we prove Theorem
1.3
for quasirandom colorings of amenable groups (and, consequently, arbitrary colorings of amenable quasirandom groups).

From here we then need to reduce the proof of Theorem
1.3
to the case that the coloring is quasirandom. This is done in Section
5.2
, where we show that in an arbitrary coloring
c
of an amenable group
G
there is a
D\subset G
such that
c
is a quasirandom coloring of
G\setminus D
and
G\setminus D
is invariant enough under multiplication to run the proof from Section
5.1
.

Notation and preliminaries

We use standard notation such as
[r]=\{1,...,r\}.

Throughout the paper, by
FP(x_{0},...,x_{k})
we mean the set of all non-repeating finite products of
x_{0},...,x_{k},
with products written from left to right in the order of the given indices. For example,
FP(x_{0},x_{1})=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{0}x_{1}\},
while
FP(x_{1},x_{0})=\{x_{1},x_{0},x_{1}x_{0}\}.

If
S=(s_{1},...,s_{n}),
by
FP(S)
we mean
FP(s_{1},...,s_{n}).
We will only use this notation in cases where the identity of the elements
s_{1},...,s_{n}
is not important, and typically not specify anything about
S
besides its size.

By an
IP_{n}
set we mean a set of the form
FP(S)
for some ordered tuple
S\subset G
with
|S|=n
. Recall that the finite unions theorem
[
GRS91
]
or the existence of idempotent ultrafilters
[
HS12
]
imply the following well-known fact:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be an infinite group. For any
r,n\in\mathbb{N}
there is an
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that any
r
-coloring of an
IP_{n^{\prime}}
set contains a monochromatic
IP_{n}
subset.

Remark 2.2
.

Throughout our constructions we can always ensure that all of the terms in
IP_{n}
sets that appear are distinct. In particular, the ultrafilter proof of Hindman’s theorem gives us infinitely many choices for the next term in any
{IP}_{n}
set that we construct, and only finitely many of these may conflict with our previous choices. This is even more explicit in the definition of large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
sets (see Definition
4.1
), where we have not only an infinite set but a positive measure set of such choices.

We will also use left and right versions of the
\prod
notation. Namely, if
F\subset\{1,...,n\}
then by
\overrightarrow{\prod}_{i\in F}x_{i}
we mean
x_{i_{1}}\cdot...\cdot x_{i_{|F|}},
where
i_{1}<i_{2}<...<i_{|F|},
and the reverse for
\overleftarrow{\prod}.
We take the convention that products taken over the empty set are the group identity.

2.1.
Amenability and recurrence

Throughout this paper, we say a group is
amenable
if it admits a finitely additive probability measure
\mu
that is
G
-invariant, i.e.,
\mu(A)=\mu(gA)=\mu(Ag)
for all
A\subset G
and
g\in G.
Our arguments will in fact work for amenable semigroups, although we do not emphasize this. If one prefers to avoid the use of such measures (whose existence in infinite groups can depend on the axiom of choice), then up to passing to appropriate Følner subsequences at various points of the argument one could replace the use of the measures
\mu
with densities along two-sided Følner sequences.

The following ‘density pigeonhole principle’ will be the primary tool that allows our proof to work for amenable groups and is just an elementary variant of the Poincaré recurrence theorem. A weaker analogue holds when replacing the positive measure set with a piecewise syndetic set in a non-amenable group, but that weaker variant is only enough to find monochromatic sets of the form
\{x,xy,yx\},
see Proposition
6.3
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\mu(A)>\varepsilon
and
(y_{0},...,y_{k})
is a sequence with
k
large enough depending on
\varepsilon
. There are
i\leq j
such that
y=y_{i}\cdot y_{i+1}\cdot...\cdot y_{j}
satisfies
\mu(A\cap Ay^{-1})>\varepsilon^{2}/2.
Similarly, there are
i^{\prime}\leq j^{\prime}
such that
y^{\prime}=y_{i^{\prime}}\cdot...\cdot y_{j^{\prime}}
satisfies
\mu((y^{\prime})^{-1}A\cap A)>\varepsilon^{2}/2.

This lemma is well-known, and its proof can be found in Bergelson’s survey article
[
Ber00
]
as Proposition 2.2.

We will also need the following iterated version of the above fact, which follows immediately from repeated applications of Lemma
2.3
. The
r=1
case is also recorded in
[
Ber00
]
as Proposition 2.5.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
\mu(A)>\varepsilon
and that
r,k\in\mathbb{N}.
There is a
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
depending only on
r,k
and
\varepsilon
such that if
S_{1}^{\prime},...,S_{r}^{\prime}\subset G
with
|S_{i}^{\prime}|\geq k^{\prime},
there are sets
S_{1},...,S_{r}
with
|S_{i}|=k
and
FP(S_{i})\subset FP(S_{i}^{\prime})
, and a set
B\subset A
with

\mu(B)>\frac{\varepsilon^{2^{rk}}}{2^{2^{rk}-1}},

such that

s_{1}\cdot...\cdot s_{r}B\subset A

whenever
s_{i}\in FP(S_{i})\cup\{1\}
for each
i\in[r].
Similarly, one can instead ensure that
Bs_{1}\cdot...\cdot s_{r}\subset A
with the same parameters.

2.2.
‘Many’ tuples

In order for our inductive arguments to go through we will often need to find ‘many’ tuples with a given property. Since finitely additive probability measures do not satisfy Fubini’s theorem, we must introduce the following notation to make this precise.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
G
be an amenable group with invariant finitely additive probability measure
\mu
.

•

Given a subset
X\subseteq G^{k+1},
we say that
X
is
large
if

\mu(\{x_{0}\in G:\mu(\{x_{1}\in G:...:\mu(\{x_{k}\in G:(x_{0},...,x_{k})\in X\})>0\}....\})>0\})>0.

•

Given a property
P:G^{k+1}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
we say that there are
many
tuples
(x_{0},...,x_{k})\in G^{k+1}
satisfying
P
if
\{\mathbf{v}\in G^{k+1}:P(\mathbf{v})=1\}
is large.

•

If there are many tuples
(x_{0},...,x_{k})
with property
P,
we say that
(x_{0},...,x_{i})
is a
valid choice
for
P
if there are many
(x_{i+1},...,x_{k})
with
P(x_{0},...,x_{k})=1.

•

If there are many tuples
(x_{0},...,x_{k})
with property
P
and
(x_{0},...,x_{i})
is a valid choice for
P
, we write

X_{i+1}(x_{0},...,x_{i})=\{x_{i+1}\in G:(x_{0},...,x_{i+1})\textnormal{ is a valid choice for }P\}.

For example, we will say that a set
A
contains many sets of the form
\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{0}x_{1}\}
if
\mu(\{x_{0}\in G:\mu(\{x_{1}\in G:x_{0},x_{1},x_{0}x_{1}\in A\})>0\})>0.
In this case,
x_{0}
is a valid choice with this property if
\mu(\{x_{1}:x_{0},x_{1},x_{0}x_{1}\in A\})>0,
and given a valid choice of
x_{0}
we have
X_{1}(x_{0})=\{x_{1}:x_{0},x_{1},x_{0}x_{1}\in A\}.

This notion of being large is partition regular in the following sense.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
X\subseteq G^{k}
is large and that

X=X_{1}\cup...\cup X_{r}.

Then
X_{i}
is large for some
i\in[r].

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k.
When
k=1,
this follows from finite additivity, since a finite union of measure zero sets has measure zero. Now suppose that
k>1
and that the result has been proven for subsets of
G^{k-1}.
For
x\in G,
let

X(x)=\{(x_{2},...,x_{k}):(x,x_{2},...,x_{k})\in X\},

and similarly for
i\in[r]
define

X_{i}(x)=\{(x_{2},...,x_{k}):(x,x_{2},...,x_{k})\in X_{i}\}.

The set of
x
for which
X(x)
is large has positive measure. For every such
x,
the inductive hypothesis applied to

X(x)=X_{1}(x)\cup...\cup X_{r}(x)

shows that
X_{i}(x)
is large for some
i\in[r].
Hence the sets

A_{i}=\{x\in G:X_{i}(x)\textnormal{ is large}\}

satisfy
\mu(\bigcup_{i\in[r]}A_{i})=\mu(\{x:X(x)\textnormal{ is large}\})
, and therefore
\mu(A_{i})>0
for some
i.
This is exactly the assertion that
X_{i}
is large.
∎

We will also need the following lemma, which follows from the fact that
\mu
is both left and right invariant.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
V\subseteq G^{M}
is large, and let
B_{1},...,B_{k}\subseteq[M]
be nonempty pairwise disjoint sets with
\max B_{i}<\max B_{j}
for
i<j
. For each
i,
let
p_{i}:B_{i}\rightarrow B_{i}
be a permutation. For each
i\in[k]
and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{M})\in V
let
B_{i}(\mathbf{v})=\overrightarrow{\prod}_{b\in B_{i}}v_{p_{i}(b)}.
The set of tuples

\{(B_{1}(\mathbf{v}),...,B_{k}(\mathbf{v})):\mathbf{v}\in V\}

is large.

Proof.

For
i\in[k],
suppose that
b^{\prime}=\max B_{i}
and we have already chosen a valid choice of
(v_{1},...,v_{b^{\prime}-1}).
Define

V_{b^{\prime}}(v_{1},...,v_{b^{\prime}-1})=\{v\in G:\textnormal{there are many }(v_{b^{\prime}+1},...,v_{M})\textnormal{ with }(v_{1},...,v_{b^{\prime}-1},v,...,v_{M})\in V\}

This has positive measure, so by the left and right invariance of
\mu
so does the set

\{\overrightarrow{\prod}_{b\in B_{i}}v_{p_{i}(b)}:v_{b^{\prime}}\in V_{b^{\prime}}(v_{1},...,v_{b^{\prime}-1})\}.

Since
\max B_{i}<\max B_{j},
this implies that
\{(B_{1}(\mathbf{v}),...,B_{k}(\mathbf{v})):\mathbf{v}\in V\}

∎

2.3.
Finite product trees

In this subsection we introduce finite product trees, which will be the main objects along which we induct throughout the paper.

Given an ordinal
\alpha
, we denote by
G^{<\alpha}=\bigcup_{n<\alpha}G^{n}
the set of all sequences of elements of
G
of length less than
\alpha.
When working with trees, we use boldface letters for elements of
G^{<\omega}.
We will identify
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{n})\in G^{<\omega}
with the product
L(\mathbf{v})=v_{1}\cdot...\cdot v_{n}
via the function
L:G^{<\omega}\rightarrow G
. Given
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{m})\in G^{m}
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},...,w_{n})\in G^{n},
by
\mathbf{v}\smallfrown\mathbf{w}
we mean the sequence
(v_{1},...,v_{m},w_{1},...,w_{n})\in G^{m+n}.

Definition 2.8
.

A
finite product tree
is a downward closed subset
T\subseteq G^{<\omega},
i.e., for all
n\geq 1
if
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{n+1})\in T
then
(v_{1},...,v_{n})\in T.

Remark 2.9
.

The above definition, and our notation concerning trees used throughout the paper, align with what are sometimes called
set-theoretic trees
, see, e.g.,
[
Tse16
]
Definition 2.1. One can naturally identify a set-theoretic tree
T
with a graph
H=(V,E),
where
V=T
and
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\in E
if
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}\smallfrown g
for some
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in T
and
g\in G.
Here, vertices of
H
can be thought of as being labeled by tuples from
G^{<\omega}
while edges are labeled by elements of
G.
Notice that if
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}\smallfrown{g}
for some
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in T
and
g\in G
, then
L(\mathbf{w})=L(\mathbf{v})\cdot g,
hence the name ‘finite product tree.’

.

•

has

•

3.
Warm-up: the two-color case

In this section we prove the
N=1
,
r=2
case of Theorem
1.3
. The argument here does not contain all of the ideas necessary to extend to more colors, but it was the starting point from which the proof of the more general result was discovered and captures the gist of the full argument while requiring significantly less notation and specialized arguments. In particular, we will be able to entirely avoid the notations involving trees that are introduced in Sections
2.3
and
4
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be an infinite amenable group and suppose that
G=C_{0}\cup C_{1}
is a two-coloring. Then there is a
y\in G
and an
i\in\{0,1\}
such that
\mu(\{x\in G:\{x,y,xy,yx\}\subset C_{i}\})>0
.

The argument we give here can be easily adapted to guarantee that there are many

satisfying the above and to obtain quantitative versions that also apply to finite groups, but we will not concern ourselves with this for ease of exposition.

Besides the variants of Poincaré’s theorem discussed in Section
2.1
, our only tool in this section will be the following corollary of Hindman’s finite products theorem:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be an infinite group, and suppose that
G=C_{0}\cup C_{1}.
There is an
i\in\{0,1\}
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a set
S\subset G
with
|S|=k
and
FP(S)\subset C_{i}.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By Lemma
3.2

4.
Quasirandom colorings

In this section we introduce quasirandom colorings and describe some of their basic properties. In the next definition and throughout one should refer to Section
2.2
for our definition of the term ‘many.’

Definition 4.1
.

Given an amenable group
(G,\mu)
we say that
A\subset G
is a
large

\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
set if for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
there are many sequences
(x_{0},...,x_{k})
with
FP(x_{k},...,x_{0})\subset A.

We say that a coloring of
G
is
quasirandom
if every positive measure color class is a large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
set.

Remark 4.2
.

It is a consequence of
[
BF09
]
Theorem 1 that any finite coloring of a weak mixing group is quasirandom. Up to introducing appropriate bounds, it follows from
[
Gow08
]
Theorem 5.3 that any finite coloring of a
D
-quasirandom group is approximately quasirandom.

An easy consequence of the existence of idempotent ultrafilters is that any finite coloring of an amenable group contains a large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
color class. Namely, if we consider the semigroup
(G,*),
where
a*b=ba\in G
and
p
is an idempotent ultrafilter in the left ideal of
(\beta G,*)
consisting of the ultrafilters
q
with
\mu(A)>0
for all
A\in q,
then every element of
p
is a large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
set. In particular, every finite coloring of
G
contains a color which is a large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
set. See
[
HS12
]
Section 6.7 for more detail.

We will not use the aforementioned ultrafilter facts. Instead, the following lemma will suffice for our purposes, which is an elementary variant of what we just described. Up to some setup, it follows from Hindman’s finite products theorem, Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
sets are partition regular, i.e., if
A=A_{1}\cup...\cup A_{r}
and
A
is a large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
set, then some
A_{i}
is a large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
set.

Proof.

Fix
k^{\prime}
and let

be large enough such that any
r
-coloring of an

set contains an
IP_{k^{\prime}+1}
set. By assumption,

contains many tuples
(x_{0},...,x_{k})
with

For a fixed valid choice of

consider

This satisfies
\mu(X_{k})>0
by assumption. Consider the auxiliary coloring
c:X_{k}\rightarrow[r^{2^{k+1}-1}]
given by the string listing the colors of elements of
FP(x_{k},...,x_{0})
. By the pigeonhole principle there is an

with
\mu(X_{k}^{\prime})>\mu(X_{k})/r^{2^{k+1}-1}
that is monochromatic with respect to

Replacing

gives many tuples

with

and so that for each

and valid choice of

depends only on

and not on the choice of

Now consider a fixed valid choice of

and consider the auxiliary

based on the value of

for some (and by the previous paragraph, any)

4.1.
Mixing in quasirandom colorings

In this subsection we show that it is easy to ‘switch’ between any desired color classes in a quasirandom coloring, in the sense that if
C_{1},C_{2}
are two positive measure color classes, then there are many
c_{1}\in C_{1}
and
c_{2}\in C_{2}
with

. This is the primary tool that will allow our color focusing arguments to control the color of the step size

in Theorem

, rather than just the

1.4
).

This ‘switching’ property can be seen as a qualitative analogue of the mixing property for quasirandom groups (see
[
Gow08
]
Theorem 5.3 and
[
BF09
]
Theorem 2.3), which implies that one can switch between any finite collection of large sets via multiplication in that setting.

To be more precise, we will need the following definition. Refer to Subsection
2.3
for our notation involving trees.

Definition 4.4
.

Given sets
A,R_{1},...,R_{r}\subset G,
a

of height

(2^{n}-1)r
is a finite product tree

(1)

for each

|S_{i}(\mathbf{v})|=n
and

The children of
\mathbf{v}
are precisely the elements of
FP(S_{i}(\mathbf{v}))

(2)

For any path

then
L(\mathbf{P})\in R_{i}
.

(3)

L(\mathbf{v})\in A
for all
\mathbf{v}\in T.

A
rootless color switching tree
is the same as above, but with the set
A=\{1\}
and without condition (3).

Remark 4.5
.

Typically in our applications, the sets

will be large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
subsets of distinct color classes. In this case, condition (2) produces for any

and
g_{j}\in R_{j}
with

and similarly for more terms. In other words, we can switch between large
\overleftarrow{IP_{0}}
color classes via multiplication, hence the name ‘color switching tree.’

For example, a color switching tree of height one and branching
(2^{n}-1)r
consists of an element
a\in A
and sets
FP(S_{1}),...,FP(S_{r})\subset G
with
|S_{i}|=n
and such that

and
a\cdot FP(S_{i})\subset A
for all

A rootless color switching tree is just the sets

as before, with no set

and element

specified. If

is a large

are large

we choose the descendant trees below every child which becomes determined by that generator. For a rooted tree with root

5.1.

In this section we prove Proposition

Before beginning, we recall a couple of needed definitions.

Definition 6.1
.

In a group

•

A set
T\subset G
is (right)
thick
if for any finite
F\subset G
there is a
t\in G
with
Ft\subset T.

•

A set

is (left)
syndetic
if there is a finite set
F\subset G
such that
F^{-1}S=G.

•

A set
A\subset G
is
piecewise syndetic
if it is the intersection of a thick set and a syndetic set.

We will also use the following basic facts about these notions of size. Their proofs all follow quickly from the definitions and can be found in
[
BHM98
]
, Section 2.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
G
be a group and
A\subset G
a piecewise syndetic set.

(1)

For every
g\in G,
the sets

and
Ag
are piecewise syndetic.

(2)

If
A=C_{0}\cup...\cup C_{r},
then some
C_{i}
is piecewise syndetic.

(3)

If
A
is piecewise syndetic and

then
B
is piecewise syndetic.

We will also need the following variant of the pigeonhole principle.

Proposition 6.3
.

If
A\subset G
is piecewise syndetic, then
\{g:g^{-1}A\cap A\textnormal{ is piecewise syndetic}\}
is syndetic.

This fact has a standard ultrafilter proof (which follows from combining
[

]

Proof of Proposition

.

This will follow from the following auxiliary claim about thick sets.

Claim 6.4

If

is thick, then for all

Proof.

Consider a finite subset

This implies that

Now to prove the Proposition, consider a piecewise syndetic set

where

is syndetic and

The set

We end with the following related questions, the first of which is a coloring variant of

Question 7.1

Suppose that a sufficiently non-commutative group is finitely colored. Are there infinite sets

such that

is monochromatic and

for all

A weaker form of the preceding question, in which

monochromatic.

Our second question concerns left vs right recurrence in amenable groups and requires the following definition.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
G
be an amenable semigroup with invariant measure
\mu.
We say that
S\subset G:

•

is a
set of weak left recurrence
if
\{s\in S:s^{-1}A\cap A\neq\emptyset\}\neq\emptyset
for all

with
\mu(A)>0.

•

is a

if
\{s\in S:\mu(s^{-1}A\cap A)>0\}\neq\emptyset
for all
A
with
\mu(A)>0.

•

is a
set of nice left recurrence
if
\{s\in S:\mu(s^{-1}A\cap A)>\mu(A)^{2}-\delta\}\neq\emptyset
for all
A
with
\mu(A)>0
and all
\delta>0.

In all of the above items we say that
S
is a
large set of (weak/nice) recurrence
if the set of such

has positive measure rather than just being non-empty. We also define right versions of these notions analogously.

A crucial fact used during the proof of Theorem
1.3
was that left
IP_{0}
sets and right

sets coincide, and, in particular via Lemma
2.3
, are both left and right sets of nice recurrence.

Question 7.3
.

When do the left and right notions from Definition
7.2
coincide?
