Atiyah-Hirzebruch spectral sequence for topological insulators and superconductors: E 2 E_{2} pages for 1651 magnetic space groups Preprint: YITP-23-17 Preprint: RIKEN-iTHEMS-Report-23
Ken Shiozaki, Seishiro Ono

Abstract
We compute the E 2 E_{2} pages of the momentum-space and real-space Atiyah-Hirzebruch spectral sequence (AHSS) for topological crystalline insulators and superconductors up to three spatial dimensions, considering the cell decomposition in which if a group action fixes a cell setwise then its group action fixes the same cell pointwise. We provide a detailed description of the implementation for computing the E 2 E_{2} pages of AHSS. Under a physically reasonable assumption, we enumerate all possible K K -groups that are compatible with the E 2 E_{2} pages for both momentum and real-space AHSS. As a result, we determine the K K -groups for approximately 59% of symmetry settings in three spatial dimensions. All the results can be found at this http URL .

In the

Although the weakening of the identity condition by stable equivalence gives a slightly coarser classification,

The

II
Symmetry and factor system

In this paper, we compute AHSSs for symmetry groups that are either the MSGs or the combination of MSGs and Particle-Hole Symmetry (PHS).
In this section, we summarize these symmetry groups and the factor system in momentum space.

We introduce an abbreviation to specify electronic insulators or superconductors for the physical system under consideration.
The electronic insulating system is abbreviated to TI (topological insulator), and the electric superconducting system to SC (superconductor).

II.1
MSG

Let
{\cal G}
be an MSG.
We denote the lattice translation group and magnetic point group by
\Pi
and
G={\cal G}/\Pi
, respectively.
An element of
{\cal G}
is specified by the Seitz symbol and the homomorphism
\phi:{\cal G}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{\pm 1\}
, which are introduced below.
The group
{\cal G}
acts on the three-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
as
g(\bm{x})=p_{g}\bm{x}+\bm{t}_{g}
for
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
, where
p_{g}\in O(3)
is a three-dimensional rotation matrix and
\bm{t}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a vector of (fractional) translation.
We employ the Seitz symbol
g=\{p_{g}|\bm{t}_{g}\}
to specify an element
g\in{\cal G}
.
(It should be noted that
\{p_{g}|\bm{t}_{g}\}
is, in fact, a representation of the group
{\cal G}
.)
They satisfy
p_{g}p_{h}=p_{gh}
and
\bm{t}_{gh}=p_{g}\bm{t}_{h}+\bm{t}_{g}
due to the group structure of
{\cal G}
.
The homomorphism
\phi
specifies whether
g\in{\cal G}
acts unitarily or antiunitarily on the one-particle Hilbert space.
The symmetry group
{\cal G}
acts as a projective representation on the one-particle Hilbert space:
Let
u_{g}
be representation matrices for
g\in{\cal G}
.
These matrices satisfy the group law, except for a
U(1)
phase, as in

\displaystyle u_{g}u_{h}^{\phi_{g}}=z_{g,h}u_{gh},\quad g,h\in{\cal G}.

(1)

Here, we introduced a short-hand notation for matrices
A
and the sign
\phi_{g}
so that
A^{\phi_{g}}=A
for
\phi_{g}=1
and
A^{\phi_{g}}=A^{*}
(complex conjugate of
A
) for
\phi_{g}=-1
.
The
U(1)
phases
z_{g,h}
are called the factor system.
We simply assume that the factor system is independent of lattice translations.
In other words,
z_{g,h}=z_{g\tau,h\tau^{\prime}}
holds for any lattice translations
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Pi
.
(This is not the case for the so-called magnetic translation symmetry.)

We introduce the Bravais lattice

\displaystyle L:=\{\tau(\bm{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|\tau\in\Pi\}

(2)

as the orbit of the lattice translation group for a fixed center
\bm{0}
of a unit cell, and we specify a unit cell by
\bm{R}\in L
.
To specify an MSG, it is useful to employ a complete set of left coset representatives of
\Pi
in
{\cal G}
, which can be written using the Seitz notation as
\{p_{g}|\bm{a}_{g}\}
for elements
g\in G
.
From the group structure of
{\cal G}
, the vector

\displaystyle p_{g}\bm{a}_{h}+\bm{a}_{g}-\bm{a}_{gh}\in L

(3)

is in the Bravais lattice.
All the data for
\phi,p_{g}
, and
\bm{a}_{g}
of the MSGs are available at
Stokes et al. 2013
, and we utilized them.

II.2
Factor system

Although the set of inequivalent factor systems
z_{g,h}
is classified by the group cohomology
H^{2}(G,U(1)_{\phi})
, where
U(1)_{\phi}
is the group
U(1)
with the left
G
-action defined as
g.z=z^{\phi_{g}}
for
z\in U(1)
, in this paper, we restrict our scope to factor systems that are realized in realistic electronic systems.
For electrons with integer spin,
z_{g,h}=z^{\rm sp}_{g,h}\equiv 1
.
For electrons with half-integer spin, the factor system is constructed as follows. It is enough to derive the factor system for spin 1/2.
The rotation matrix
p_{g}\in O(3)
for
g\in\mathcal{G}
is written as
p_{g}=R_{\hat{n}_{g},\theta_{g}}I^{\frac{1-\det p_{g}}{2}}
, where
R_{\hat{n}_{g},\theta_{g}}\in SO(3)
is a rotation matrix along the
\hat{n}_{g}
-axis by the
\theta_{g}
angle and
I
is the space inversion.
The space inversion trivially acts on the electrons, meaning that the space inversion
I
does not produce a nontrivial factor system. While the space rotation and the time-reversal symmetry (TRS) act on the electron wave function by elements of
Spin(3)=SU(2)
group as

\displaystyle u_{g}=\begin{cases}e^{-i\theta_{g}\hat{n}_{g}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2}&\phi_{g}=1,\\
e^{-i\theta_{g}\hat{n}_{g}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2}(i\sigma_{y})&\phi_{g}=-1.\\
\end{cases}

(4)

Here,
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
are the Pauli matrices. Using explicit representation matrices above, we have the factor system
z^{\rm sp}_{g,h}
by the defining relation
u_{g}u_{h}^{\phi_{g}}=z^{\rm sp}_{g,h}u_{gh}
. As a result, the factor systems
z^{\rm sp}_{g,h}
take values in the sign
\pm 1
.

II.3
Symmetry action in momentum space

Let
\hat{c}_{\sigma}(\bm{R}+\delta\bm{r})
and
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\bm{R}+\delta\bm{r})
be annihilation and creation operators of a complex fermion localized at the position
\bm{R}+\delta\bm{r}
.
Here,
\bm{R}\in L
represents the center position of unit cells,
\delta{\bm{r}}
is the displacement vector from the unit cell center for the fermions, and the subscript
\sigma
is the index for the internal degrees of freedom like spin and orbital at
{\bm{R}}+\delta{\bm{r}}
.
Let
{\cal G}
be an MSG.
An element
g\in{\cal G}
acts on the fermions as

\displaystyle\hat{g}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\bm{R}+\delta\bm{r})\hat{g}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(p_{g}(\bm{R}+\delta\bm{r})+\bm{t}_{g})[D_{g}]_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma},

(5)

Here,
\hat{g}
is unitary when
\phi_{g}=1
, and antiunitary when
\phi_{g}=-1
, and
D_{g}
matrices are a set of unitary matrices satisfying

\displaystyle D_{g}D_{h}^{\phi_{g}}=z_{g,h}D_{gh}

(6)

with the factor system
z_{g,h}
introduced before and does not depend on lattice translations.

In this paper, we introduce the fermion annihilation and creation operators in momentum space so that they are periodic by reciprocal lattice vectors.
Namely,

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\delta{\bm{r}},\sigma}({\bm{k}})=\sum_{{\bm{R}}\in\Pi}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\bm{R}+\delta{\bm{r}})e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}}.

(7)

(A reciprocal lattice vector is a vector in the dual lattice
\hat{L}:=\{{\bf G}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|e^{i\bm{{\bf G}}\cdot\bm{R}}=1{\rm\ for\ all\ }\bm{R}\in L\}
.)
This definition does not reflect the spatial position of the degrees of freedom, which requires caution when calculating physical quantities, etc. However, since it does not affect the classification of topological phases, we adopt this definition in this paper.
Let us introduce a permutation matrix

\displaystyle[P_{g}]_{\delta{\bm{r}}^{\prime},\delta{\bm{r}}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\delta{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\equiv p_{g}\delta{\bm{r}}+\bm{t}_{g}{\rm\ mod\ }L,\\
0&{\rm else},\end{array}\right.

for
g\in{\cal G}
, we have for
g\in{\cal G}
,

\displaystyle\hat{g}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}({\bm{k}})\hat{g}^{-1}=\sum_{i^{\prime}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}}(\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}})[u_{g}({\bm{k}})]_{i^{\prime}i}

(10)

with

\displaystyle[u_{g}({\bm{k}})]_{i^{\prime}i}

\displaystyle=[P_{g}]_{\delta{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\delta{\bm{r}}}[D_{g}]_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}e^{-i\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}}\cdot(p_{g}\delta{\bm{r}}+\bm{t}_{g}-\delta{\bm{r}}^{\prime})},

(11)

where the indices
\delta{\bm{r}}
and
\sigma
were merged as a single index
i=(\delta{\bm{r}},\sigma)
.
In particular,
[u_{\tau}({\bm{k}})]_{i^{\prime}i}=\delta_{i^{\prime}i}e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{t}}
for lattice translations
\tau=\{1|\bm{t}\}\in\Pi
.
Note that
u_{g}({\bm{k}})
is periodic
u_{g}({\bm{k}}+{\bf G})=u_{g}({\bm{k}})
for reciprocal lattice vectors
{\bf G}
.
It is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle u_{g}(\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}})u_{h}({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}=z_{g,h}u_{gh}({\bm{k}})

(12)

for
g,h\in{\cal G}
.

A free fermion Hamiltonian expressed in the momentum space is

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{{\bm{k}},ij}c^{\dagger}_{i}({\bm{k}})[h({\bm{k}})]_{ij}c_{j}({\bm{k}}).

(13)

The matrix
h({\bm{k}})
is also called a Hamiltonian.
With the definition (
7
),
h({\bm{k}})
is also periodic
h({\bm{k}}+\bm{G})=h({\bm{k}})
.
For free fermions, the MSG symmetry of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is that the matrix
h({\bm{k}})
satisfies

\displaystyle u_{g}({\bm{k}})h({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}u_{g}({\bm{k}})^{-1}=h(\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}})

(14)

for
g\in{\cal G}
.
Note that the lattice translation symmetry is fulfilled as it is written in momentum space.
Only the constraint conditions coming from the magnetic point group
G
are meaningful.

II.4
Symmetry of gap function

In superconductors, the mean-field Hamiltonian in momentum space is written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm MF}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{k}},ij}c^{\dagger}_{i}({\bm{k}})[h({\bm{k}})]_{ij}c_{j}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}},ij}(c^{\dagger}_{i}({\bm{k}})[\Delta({\bm{k}})]_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{j}(-{\bm{k}})+{\rm h.c.}).

(15)

The matrix
\Delta({\bm{k}})
is the gap function and satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta({\bm{k}})^{T}=-\Delta(-{\bm{k}})

(16)

due to the anticommutation relation of fermion operators.
The gap function
\Delta({\bm{k}})
is supposed to be a vector
\Delta({\bm{k}})=\sum_{a=1}^{{\rm dim\,}\rho}\eta_{a}\Delta_{a}({\bm{k}}),\eta_{a}\in\mathbb{C},
of some basis functions
\{\Delta_{a}({\bm{k}})\}_{a=1}^{{\rm dim\,}\rho}
satisfying

\displaystyle u_{g}({\bm{k}})\Delta_{a}({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}u_{g}(-{\bm{k}})^{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{{\rm dim\,}\rho}\Delta_{b}(\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}})[D^{\rho}_{g}]_{ba}

(17)

for
g\in{\cal G}
.
We assume
D^{\rho}_{g}
is independent of
{\bm{k}}
and lattice translations, meaning that
\rho
is a representation of the magnetic point group
G
.
From (
17
), the factor system for
D^{\rho}_{g}
is
(z_{g,h})^{2}
and thus trivial for spinless and spinful electrons.
Since different irreps do not coexist as a solution of the gap equation in general,
\rho
is an irrep of the magnetic point group
G
.
When
\rho
is not the trivial irrep of
G
, meaning that
D^{\rho}_{g}\neq 1
for some
g\in{\cal G}
, the gap function
\Delta({\bm{k}})
breaks the original MSG symmetry defined by (
5
).
However, when
\rho
is a one-dimensional irrep of
G
, one can recover the MSG symmetry using
U(1)
phase rotation
\hat{\sf u}(e^{i\theta})c^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\sf u}(e^{i\theta})^{-1}=c^{\dagger}_{i}e^{i\theta}
of complex fermions.
Let us write
D^{\rho}_{g}=\xi_{g}\in U(1)
for a one-dimensional irrep.
For each
\xi_{g}
, we pick a sign of the square root of
\xi_{g}
and denote it by
\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Then, the combined transformation
\hat{g}\hat{\sf u}((\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*})
becomes symmetry of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm MF}
.

The mean-field Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm MF}
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm MF}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}},ij}(c^{\dagger}_{i}({\bm{k}}),c_{i}(-{\bm{k}}))

\displaystyle[H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})]_{ij}\begin{pmatrix}c_{j}({\bm{k}})\\
c^{\dagger}_{j}(-{\bm{k}})\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}h({\bm{k}})&\Delta({\bm{k}})\\
\Delta({\bm{k}})^{\dagger}&-h(-{\bm{k}})^{T}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

The two-component spinor
\Psi_{i}({\bm{k}})=(c_{i}({\bm{k}}),c^{\dagger}_{i}(-{\bm{k}}))^{T}
and the matrix
H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})
are called the Nambu spinor and the Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian, respectively.
The relation (
16
) implies that the BdG Hamiltonian
H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})
satisfies the following particle-hole “symmetry”

\displaystyle U_{c}H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})^{*}U_{c}^{-1}=-H_{\rm BdG}(-{\bm{k}}),

\displaystyle U_{c}=\begin{pmatrix}&1\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

Therefore, the total symmetry group for the BdG Hamiltonian becomes
{\cal G}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}
generated by PHS.
Because
U_{C}U_{C}^{*}=1
we call the PHS in the form (
19
) the class D PHS in the Altland-Zirnbauer (AZ symmetry class
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
.
The one-dimensional irrep
\xi_{g}
is encoded in the factor system of
{\cal G}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}
.
On the Nambu spinor, the combined symmetry is

\displaystyle\hat{g}\hat{\sf u}((\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*})\Psi^{\dagger}({\bm{k}})(\hat{g}\hat{\sf u}((\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Psi^{\dagger}(\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}})U_{g}({\bm{k}})

(20)

with

\displaystyle U_{g}({\bm{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}u_{g}({\bm{k}})(\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*}\\
&u_{g}(-{\bm{k}})^{*}\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(21)

for
g\in{\cal G}
.
We find that

\displaystyle U_{g}(\phi_{h}p_{h}{\bm{k}})U_{h}({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}=z_{g,h}z_{g,h}^{\xi}U_{gh}({\bm{k}}),

(22)

\displaystyle U_{c}U_{g}({\bm{k}})^{*}=U_{g}(-{\bm{k}})U_{C}^{\phi_{g}},

(23)

with

\displaystyle z^{\xi}_{g,h}:=\xi_{g}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\xi_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\phi_{g}}(\xi_{gh}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}\in\{\pm 1\}.

(24)

In (
22
) we have used that
z_{g,h}
is a sign so that
z_{g,h}^{*}=z_{g,h}
.

Alternatively, the following phase choice, which is meaningful only for BdG Hamiltonian, is useful.

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{g}({\bm{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}u_{g}({\bm{k}})\\
&u_{g}(-{\bm{k}})^{*}\xi_{g}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(25)

With this,

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{g}(\phi_{h}p_{h}{\bm{k}})U^{\prime}_{h}({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}=z_{g,h}U^{\prime}_{gh}({\bm{k}}),

(26)

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{g}(-{\bm{k}})U_{C}^{\phi_{g}}=\xi_{g}U_{C}U^{\prime}_{g}({\bm{k}})^{*}.

(27)

II.5
SU(2)
symmetry and class C

Consider the cases in the presence of full
SU(2)
internal symmetry of normal state
h({\bm{k}})
for either the spin-1/2 or a pseudo-spin-1/2 internal degree of freedom.
We denote the Pauli matrices for the (pseudo) spin-1/2 degrees of freedom by
\bm{\sigma}
.
The normal part is written as
h({\bm{k}})=\tilde{h}({\bm{k}})\otimes\sigma_{0}
.
When the gap function also preservers
SU(2)
symmetry, the gap function is in the form

\displaystyle\Delta({\bm{k}})=\tilde{\Delta}({\bm{k}})\otimes(i\sigma_{y}).

(28)

The relation (
16
) means that

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}({\bm{k}})^{T}=\tilde{\Delta}(-{\bm{k}}).

(29)

On the basis of Nambu spinor
\Psi^{\prime}_{i}({\bm{k}})=(c_{i}({\bm{k}}),(i\sigma_{y})c_{i}^{\dagger}({\bm{k}}))^{T}
, the BdG hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})=\tilde{H}_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})\otimes\sigma_{0},

(30)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{h}({\bm{k}})&\tilde{\Delta}({\bm{k}})\\
\tilde{\Delta}({\bm{k}})^{\dagger}&-\tilde{h}(-{\bm{k}})^{T}.\end{pmatrix}

(31)

The relation (
29
) implies that the BdG Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}({\bm{k}})
satisfies the following PHS

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{C}\tilde{H}_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})^{*}\tilde{U}_{C}^{-1}=-\tilde{H}_{\rm BdG}(-{\bm{k}}),

(32)

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}&1\\
-1\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{U}_{C}\tilde{U}_{C}^{*}=-1.

(33)

This is the class C PHS
Schnyder et al. 2008
.

If the
SU(2)
symmetry in the electron system comes from the electron spin, the BdG Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})
satisfies the factor system for spinless electron systems.
On the other hand, if the
SU(2)
symmetry in the electron system comes from the pseudo-spin originating from an orbital degree of freedom, the BdG Hamiltonian
H_{\rm BdG}({\bm{k}})
satisfies the factor system for spinful electron systems.
We call the former cases the class C spinful SC and the latter class C spinless SC, respectively.

III
Preliminary for AHSS in general

We describe common preliminaries in momentum-space and real-space AHSS, including a class of cell decomposition used in this paper and the definition of the symmetry-resolved winding number.

III.1
Symmetry in one-particle Hilbert space

Although this paper focuses only on symmetries and factor systems in electron systems, we summarize here the more general symmetry classes
Freed and Moore 2013
.

Let
{\cal G}
be a discrete group that fits into the short exact sequence

\displaystyle\Pi\to{\cal G}\to G

(34)

with
\Pi\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
being the translational group in
d
-space dimensions.
A symmetry class is characterized by
{\cal G}
with the quintet
(p_{g},\bm{t}_{g},\phi_{g},c_{g},z^{\rm int}_{g,h})
for
g,h\in{\cal G}
, explained below.
The matrix
p_{g}
is an
O(d)
matrix, and
\bm{t}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a translation vector, meaning that
g\in{\cal G}
acts on the real space as
\bm{x}\mapsto g(\bm{x})=p_{g}\bm{x}+\bm{t}_{g}
.
We denote the one-particle Hilbert space on a
d
-dimensional lattice by
{\cal H}
and the symmetry action on
{\cal H}
by
\hat{g}
.
The homomorphism
\phi:{\cal G}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
specifies whether
\hat{g}
is unitary or antiunitary on
{\cal H}
.
We assume that
\phi(\Pi)=\{1\}
, i.e., the translation group
\Pi
is composed only of unitary elements.
The factor system
z^{\rm int}_{g,h}
specifies how
{\cal G}
is represented projectively on the Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, such that
\hat{g}\hat{h}=z^{\rm int}_{g,h}\widehat{gh}
.
We assume that
z^{\rm int}_{g,h}
does not depend on translations in the sense that
z^{\rm int}_{gt,gt^{\prime}}=z^{\rm int}_{g,h}
for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Pi
, meaning that
z
is a two-cocycle
z^{\rm int}\in Z^{2}(G,U(1)_{\phi})
, where
U(1)_{\phi}
means that
g\in G
acts on
U(1)
as
g.z=z^{\phi_{g}}
for
z\in U(1)
.
Let
\hat{H}
be a Hamiltonian on
{\cal H}
.
The homomorphism
c:{\cal G}\to\{\pm 1\}
specifies whether
\hat{g}
commutes or anticommutes with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{g}\hat{H}\hat{g}^{-1}=c_{g}\hat{H},\quad g\in{\cal G}.

(35)

We also assume that
c(\Pi)=\{1\}
, i.e., lattice translations commute with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.

Now we summarize the symmetry classes discussed in this paper.

III.1.1
TIs

For TIs, a symmetry class is specified by an MSG
{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
and whether it is spinless or spinful.
The group
{\cal G}
is an MSG
{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
equipped with the data
p_{g},\bm{t}_{g},\phi_{g}
.
For spinless TIs, the factor system
z^{\rm int}_{g,h}
is a trivial one,
z^{\rm int}_{g,h}\equiv 1
.
For spinful TIs, the factor system is that for spin-1/2 electrons,
z^{\rm int}_{g,h}=z^{\rm sp}_{g,h}\in\{\pm 1\}
, defined in Sec.
II.2
.

III.1.2
SCs

For SCs, a symmetry class is specified by an MSG
{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
, a one-dimensional irrep
\xi
of the magnetic point group
G_{\rm MPG}
of
{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
, whether it is spinless or spinful, and the type of PHS, either class D or class C.
The total symmetry group
{\cal G}
is the product
{\cal G}={\cal G}_{\rm MSG}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}=\{e,C\}
being the group of PHS.
PHS is an internal symmetry, meaning that the PHS
C
does not change the spatial position
C(\bm{x})=\bm{x}
, i.e.,
p_{C}={\bf 1}_{d}
and
\bm{t}_{C}=\bm{0}
.
As discussed in Sec.
II.4
, PHS behaves as an antiunitary symmetry and anticommutes with the BdG Hamiltonian, meaning that
\phi_{C}=-1
and
c_{C}=-1
.
For generic elements
g\in{\cal G}
,
p_{g},\bm{t}_{g},\phi_{g},
and
c_{g}
are extended with the group structure.

For a given irrep
\xi
, let
z_{g,h}^{\xi}
for
g,h\in{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
be the factor system introduced in (
24
).
Let
\pi_{1}:{\cal G}\to{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
and
\pi_{2}:{\cal G}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}
be the projections onto
{\cal G}_{\rm MSG}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{C}
, respectively.
The factor system can be summarized in the form

\displaystyle z^{\rm int}_{g,h}=z^{\rm sp}_{\pi_{1}(g),\pi_{1}(h)}z^{\xi}_{\pi_{1}(g),\pi_{1}(h)}z^{\rm PHS}_{\pi_{2}(g),\pi_{2}(h)},

\displaystyle g,h\in{\cal G}.

(36)

Here,
z^{\rm PHS}_{g,h}\equiv 1
for class D, and

\displaystyle z^{\rm PHS}_{g,h}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-1&g=h=C,\\
1&{\rm else},\end{array}\right.

for class C.

III.2
Cell decomposition

In this section, we introduce a class of cell decompositions used for the AHSS in this paper.
Let
X
be a space over which we want to compute the
K
-group.
The space
X
is either the Brillouin zone (BZ) torus
T^{d}
or the infinite real space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
is the space dimension.
Let
G
be a symmetry group acting on
X
.
For
d=3
, the group
G
is the MSG for the real space, whereas
G
is the magnetic point group for the momentum space.
We introduce a sequence of spaces

\displaystyle\emptyset=X_{-1}\subset X_{0}\subset X_{1}\subset\cdots\subset X_{d}=X

(39)

such that each
X_{p}
is obtained from
X_{p-1}
by gluing
p
-cells
D^{p}_{i}
, which are each homeomorphic to a
p
-dimensional disk
D^{p}
, to
X_{p-1}
along their boundary
(p-1)
-dimensional spheres
\partial D^{p}_{i}\cong S^{p-1}
. Additionally, there is a symmetry constraint on the
p
-cells:
For each
g\in G
,
p
-cells
D^{p}_{i}
are mapped to other
p
-cells by
g
.
In other words, for each
g\in G
,
g(D^{p}_{i})=D^{p}_{j}
holds with some
j
.
Each
X_{p}
is called the
p
-skeleton.
We refer to such a decomposition of the space
X
as a cell decomposition.

While the AHSS is defined for the above cell decomposition, we impose the following additional condition on the cell decomposition in this paper:

– If
g\in G
fixes the
p
-cell
D^{p}_{i}
setwise, then
g
fixes
D^{p}_{i}
pointwise.
Namely, if
g(D^{p}_{i})=D^{p}_{i}
, then
g(x)=x
for all
x\in D^{p}_{i}
.

Note that even with the additional constraint, the cell decomposition is not unique.
Nevertheless, the
E_{2}
-page is known to be unique.

In addition, we assign an orientation to each
p
-cell in such a way as to satisfy the symmetry.
All orientations of 0-cells are fixed to be positive.
Orientation is not necessary for the AHSS in general, but it is required to construct the first differential
d_{1}
, which will be developed later.

In Appendix
A
, we present an algorithm for computing cell decomposition such that the condition above is satisfied and the cell of maximum dimension (
d
-cell) is a convex fundamental domain.
For instance, FIGs.
1
and
2
show fundamental domains in the momentum and real spaces for MSG
P2_{1}1^{\prime}
.

III.3
Chiral symmetry and winding number

In odd spatial dimensions, one can define the winding number in the presence of chiral symmetry.
Let
G=G_{0}\coprod\gamma G_{0}
be an internal symmetry group composed of unitary and chiral type symmetry such that

\displaystyle u_{g}H({\bm{k}})u_{g}^{-1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H({\bm{k}})&g\in G_{0},\\
-H({\bm{k}})&g\in\gamma G_{0},\end{array}\right.

where
\gamma
is a representative element of
\{g\in\mathcal{G}|\phi_{g}=-c_{g}=+1\}
.
Let
\alpha
be an irrep of
G_{0}
.
If the mapped irrep
\gamma[\alpha]
is unitarily equivalent to
\alpha
, one can define the winding number
W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}
for the irrep
\alpha
as follows.
The construction of the winding number in this section is based on
Shiozaki 2022
.

Let
z_{g,h}\in Z^{2}(G,U(1))
be a factor system of projective representation appearing as
u_{g}u_{h}=z_{g,h}u_{gh}
for
g,h\in G
.
Let
\chi^{\alpha}_{g\in G_{0}}
be the character of the irrep
\alpha
of
G_{0}
.
The character of the mapped irrep
\gamma[\alpha]
is given by
\chi^{\gamma[\alpha]}_{g\in G_{0}}=\frac{z_{g,\gamma}}{z_{\gamma,\gamma^{-1}g\gamma}}\chi^{\alpha}_{\gamma^{-1}g\gamma}
.
If
\sum_{g\in G_{0}}(\chi^{\alpha}_{g})^{*}\chi^{\gamma[\alpha]}_{g}=1
, the two irreps
\alpha
and
\gamma[\alpha]
are unitarily equivalent to each other.
If this is the case, there are exactly two irreps
\alpha+
and
\alpha-
of
G_{0}\coprod\gamma G_{0}
such that the character
\chi^{\alpha\pm}_{g}
of the irrep
\alpha\pm
satisfies

\displaystyle\chi^{\alpha\pm}_{g\in G_{0}}=\chi^{\alpha}_{g},\quad\chi^{\alpha-}_{g\in\gamma G_{0}}=-\chi^{\alpha+}_{g}.

(42)

The orthogonality
\frac{1}{|G_{0}\coprod\gamma G_{0}|}\sum_{g\in G_{0}\coprod\gamma G_{0}}(\chi^{\alpha+}_{g})^{*}\chi^{\alpha-}_{g}=0
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G_{0}|}\sum_{g\in\gamma G_{0}}(\chi^{\alpha+}_{g})\chi^{\alpha\pm}_{g}=\pm 1.

(43)

With these characters, we introduce the projection
P_{\alpha\pm}
onto the
\alpha\pm
irrep as

\displaystyle P_{\alpha\pm}=\frac{{\rm dim}(\alpha\pm)}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}(\chi^{\alpha\pm}_{g})^{*}u_{g}.

(44)

here,
{\rm dim}(\alpha\pm)
is the dimension of the representation
\alpha\pm
.
The chiral matrix of the irrep
\alpha
is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}=P_{\alpha+}-P_{\alpha-},

(45)

and the winding number
W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}
of the irrep
\alpha
is given by

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}=\frac{n!}{(2\pi i)^{n}(2n)!}\int{\rm tr\,}[(H^{-1}dH)^{2n-1}\Gamma_{\alpha}].

(46)

For convenience, here, we have written
\alpha+
and
\alpha-
, but there is no way to choose one or the other as
\alpha+
or
\alpha-
.
Interchanging
\alpha+
and
\alpha-
flips the sign of the chiral matrix
\Gamma_{\alpha}
and the winding number
W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}
.
Therefore, the sign of the winding number
W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}
depends on the choice of the sign of the chiral matrix
\Gamma_{\alpha}
, which must be properly incorporated in the AHSS formulated in later sections.
The following expression of
W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}
is also useful.

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}_{2n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{(2\pi i)^{n}(2n)!}\frac{1}{|G_{0}|}\sum_{g\in\gamma G_{0}}(\chi^{\alpha+}_{g})^{*}

\displaystyle\times\int{\rm tr\,}[(H^{-1}dH)^{2n-1}u_{g}].

(47)

IV
Momentum-space AHSS

We provide a method for calculating the first differential
d_{1}
of the momentum-space AHSS
Shiozaki et al. 2022
.
In this section, we refer to
g,h,\dots
as elements of the group
G=\mathcal{G}/\Pi
.

In momentum space, we can consider the symmetry group of the Hamiltonian
H({\bm{k}})
as the group
G
.
Let
\{\{p_{g}|\bm{a}_{g}\}\}_{g\in G}
be a set of left coset representatives of
\Pi
in
\mathcal{G}
.
The symmetry constraints on the Hamiltonian
H({\bm{k}})
are written as

\displaystyle u_{g}({\bm{k}})H({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}u_{g}({\bm{k}})^{-1}=c_{g}H(\phi_{g}p_{g}{\bm{k}}),\quad g\in G,

(48)

together with the
{\bm{k}}
-dependent factor system

\displaystyle u_{g}(\phi_{h}p_{h}{\bm{k}})u_{h}({\bm{k}})^{\phi_{g}}=z_{g,h}({\bm{k}})u_{gh}({\bm{k}}),

(49)

\displaystyle z_{g,h}({\bm{k}}):=z^{\rm int}_{g,h}e^{-i\phi_{gh}p_{gh}{\bm{k}}\cdot(p_{g}\bm{a}_{h}+\bm{a}_{g}-\bm{a}_{gh})}

(50)

for
g,h\in G
.

What we aim to compute is the twisted equivariant
K
-group
{}^{\phi}K_{G}^{(z,c)-n}(X)
over the BZ torus
X
by Freed and Moore
Freed and Moore 2013
.
Here,

takes values in integers, and the
K
-group enjoys the Bott periodicity
{}^{\phi}K_{G}^{(z,c)-(n+8)}(X)\cong{}^{\phi}K_{G}^{(z,c)-n}(X)
.
Roughly speaking, the integers

have the following physical meanings:

n=1,n=0
, and
n=-1
correspond to gauge transformations in momentum space, gapped Hamiltonians, and gapless Hamiltonians realized only as a boundary of gapped Hamiltonians, respectively.
In particular, the
0

represents the classification of gapped Hamiltonians.

Hereafter, we set the spatial dimension to three.

IV.1
Preliminary

For a cell decomposition

introduced in Sec.

\displaystyle E_{1}^{p,-n}:={}^{\phi}K^{(z,c)+p-n}_{G}(X_{p},X_{p-1}).

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt\coprod_{{\sf a}\in I^{p}_{\rm orb}}G/G_{D^{p}_{\sf a}}\times\partial D^{p}_{\sf a})

Here,

Let us consider how to list all possible homomorphisms

\mathbb{Z}

To simplify the problem and maintain physical relevance, we adopt the following working assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

The rank of the
K
-group
{}^{\phi}K_{G}^{(z,c)-n}(X)
is the same as the rank of the
E_{2}
-page
\bigoplus_{p=0}^{3}E_{2}^{p,-n-p}

This property is known to hold for equivariant

G

Lück and Oliver 2001

K

The higher differentials

can be understood as the band inversion at a high-symmetry
p

(p+r)
-cells
Shiozaki et al. 2022

For example, consider a

-symmetric
0

0
-cell followed by the creation of four gapless points in adjacent
2

for

Let us consider the same band inversion twice as shown in Fig.

For the real-space AHSS, we assume the same one:

Assumption 2
.

The rank of the
K
-group
{}^{\phi}K^{\cal G}_{(z^{\rm int},c)-n}(\mathbb{E}^{3})

-page

We leave a discussion of the validity of this assumption for SCs.
As discussed in

, the origin of the second differential

is a vortex zero mode of topological SC with a unit Chern number.
The two-layered SC with Chern number 1 for each is equivalent to a single SC with Chern number 2 under the stable equivalence, and there are no vortex zeros.
This means that the image of

should be a 2-torsion.

These assumptions imply that higher-order differentials do not reduce the rank, i.e., there is no
\mathbb{Z}
in the image of

and

for

.
Thus, the homomorphisms to be considered have the following form

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus q}\oplus A\to B

(170)

with

torsion
\mathbb{Z}

The rank of

Using the third-isomorphism theorem,

\displaystyle\cong\frac{B/{\rm Im\,}f_{2}}{{\rm Im\,}[f_{1}^{\prime}:\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus q}\to B/{\rm Im\,}f_{2}]}

\displaystyle={\rm Coker\,}f_{1}^{\prime}.

VI.2
Extension

The
E_{4}
-page approximates the momentum space
K
-group in the following sense.
We have a filtration

\displaystyle 0=F_{4}K^{-n}\subset F_{3}K^{-n}\subset F_{2}K^{-n}

\displaystyle\subset F_{1}K^{-n}\subset F_{0}K^{-n}={}^{\phi}K^{(z,c)-n}_{G}(X),

(179)

where each quotient group is given as

\displaystyle F_{p}K^{-n}/F_{p+1}K^{-n}\cong E_{4}^{p,-p-n}.

(180)

The
K
-group is obtained by solving the extension problems as
\mathbb{Z}
-module sequentially.

F_{3}K^{-n}=E_{4}^{3,-3-n}
.
Then,
F_{p}K^{-n}
is given by an extension of
E_{4}^{p,-p-n}
by
F_{p+1}K^{-n}
for
p=2,1,0
in order.

Similarly, the
E^{4}
-page approximates the real space
K
-group as

\displaystyle 0=F_{-1}K_{-n}\subset F_{0}K_{-n}\subset F_{1}K_{-n}

\displaystyle\subset F_{2}K_{-n}\subset F_{3}K_{-n}={}^{\phi}K_{(z^{\rm int},c)-n}^{{\cal G}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(181)

with

\displaystyle F_{p}K_{-n}/F_{p-1}K_{-n}\cong E^{4}_{p,-p-n}.

(182)

We have
F_{0}K_{-n}=E^{4}_{0,-n}
, and
F_{p}K_{-n}
is an extension of
E^{4}_{p,-p-n}
by
F_{p-1}K_{-n}
for
p=1,2,3
in order.

Given a pair of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules
A
and
B
, equivalence classes of
\mathbb{Z}
-module extensions of
A
by
B
are classified by
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)

Weibel 1995
.
The group
{\rm Ext}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A,B)
has the following properties

\displaystyle{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}\Big(\bigoplus_{i}A_{i},\bigoplus_{j}B_{j}\Big)=\bigoplus_{i,j}{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A_{i},B_{j}),

(183)

and

\displaystyle{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}_{n},

(184)

\displaystyle{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\mathbb{Z}_{m})=\mathbb{Z}_{{\rm gcd}(n,m)},

(185)

\displaystyle{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}_{n})={\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z})=0.

(186)

The free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathbb{Z}
is not extended.
Hereafter we assume
A
is a torsion
\mathbb{Z}
-module.
The abelian group structure of
{\rm Ext}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A,B)
is represented by the Baer sum of two extensions
0\to B\to E_{i}\to A\to 0
for
i=0,1
.
(See Appendix
D
for the construction of the Baer sum.)
Thus, all extensions can be constructed from the extensions corresponding to the generators of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)
.

However, it is not feasible to compute extensions for all values of
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)
.
For example,
|{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\oplus n},\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m})|=2^{nm}
.
Changing the basis of
A
and
B
induces an automorphism of
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)
, but the
\mathbb{Z}
-module obtained as an extension via this basis transformation does not change.
For example,
{\rm Ext}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}_{4},\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}_{4}
, and each value of
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
gives
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{4},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2},\mathbb{Z}
respectively.
Here, the second and fourth extensions are related via the basis transformation of
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
(or
\mathbb{Z}
) multiplying the basis by
-1
.
Thus, if we are only interested in the
\mathbb{Z}
-module obtained as extensions, we can contract the elements of
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)
via basis transformations.
We will now formalize this contraction procedure.

We express an element of the group
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)
as a matrix
M
, which we call an extension matrix, using the following procedure.
First, we decompose
A
into its invariant factors as

\displaystyle A=\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}^{\oplus n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p_{k}}^{\oplus n_{k}},\quad p_{k}|p_{k-1}|\cdots|p_{1}.

(187)

Here,
p|q
denotes that
p
divides
q
.
We denote the basis of
A
as

\displaystyle a^{(p_{1})}_{1},\dots,a^{(p_{1})}_{n_{1}},\dots,a^{(p_{k})}_{1},\dots,a^{(p_{k})}_{n_{k}}.

(188)

Similarly, we decompose
B
into its invariant factors as

\displaystyle B=\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus m_{0}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{q_{1}}^{\oplus m_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{q_{l}}^{\oplus q_{l}},

\displaystyle q_{l}|q_{l-1}|\cdots|q_{1}.

(189)

We denote the basis of
B
as

\displaystyle{\cal B}

\displaystyle=(b^{(q_{0})}_{1},\dots,b^{(q_{0})}_{m_{0}},

\displaystyle\quad\quad b^{(q_{1})}_{1},\dots,b^{(q_{1})}_{m_{1}},\dots,b^{(q_{l})}_{1},\dots,b^{(q_{l})}_{m_{l}}),

(190)

where we formally wrote
q_{0}=\infty
.
An element of
{\rm Ext}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A,B)\supset{\rm Ext}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}_{p},B)
represents how
``p"\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is “carried-up” by an element of
B
.
We denote this correspondence as an extension matrix
M
by

\displaystyle f_{M}({\cal A})={\cal B}M,

(191)

with the basis

\displaystyle{\cal A}

\displaystyle=(p_{1}a^{(p_{1})}_{1},\dots,p_{1}a^{(p_{1})}_{n_{1}},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\dots,p_{k}a^{(p_{k})}_{1},\dots,p_{k}a^{(p_{k})}_{n_{k}}).

(192)

Next, let’s consider possible basis transformations for
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
.
For
{\cal B}
, any basis transformations that do not change the structure of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
B
are allowed.
Since it is allowed to add linear combinations of basis elements
b^{(q_{j})}_{t}
with
i\leq j
to a basis
b^{(q_{i})}_{s}
, we can use the following block lower triangular unimodular matrix in the form

\displaystyle V=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}v_{00}&O&O&\cdots\\
v_{10}&v_{11}&O\\
v_{20}&v_{21}&v_{33}\\
\vdots\\
\end{array}\right]

as a general basis transformation for
{\cal B}
.
On the other hand, the basis transformation of
{\cal A}
to be considered is not a basis transformation that does not change the structure as
\mathbb{Z}
-module
A
, but a basis transformation that keeps the integer lattice spanned by
{\cal A}
invariant.
For example, if we add
r
times
p_{j}a^{(p_{j})}_{t}
to
p_{i}a^{(p_{i})}_{s}
, we have

\displaystyle p_{i}a^{(p_{i})}_{s}+rp_{j}a^{(p_{j})}_{t}=p_{i}(a^{(p_{i})}_{s}+r\frac{p_{j}}{p_{i}}a^{(p_{j})}_{t})

(197)

which is well-defined only if
p_{i}|p_{j}
, i.e., in the case where
i\geq j
.
Therefore, the basis transformations for
{\cal A}
are given by block upper triangular unimodular matrices of the form

\displaystyle U=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}u_{11}&u_{12}&u_{13}&\cdots\\
O&u_{22}&u_{23}\\
O&O&u_{33}\\
\vdots\\
\end{array}\right].

Through these basis transformations, the entension matrix
M
changes to

\displaystyle M\mapsto V^{-1}MU.

(202)

Since the inverse of a lower-triangular block unimodular matrix is also a lower-triangular block unimodular, the above transformation is nothing but a “directional” Gauss-Jordan elimination from the upper left to the lower right.
We write the extension matrix
M
as a block matrix

\displaystyle M=(M^{(q_{i},p_{j})})_{0\leq i\leq l,1\leq j\leq k},

(203)

\displaystyle M^{(q_{i},p_{j})}=(M^{(q_{i},p_{j})}_{st})_{1\leq s\leq m_{i},1\leq t\leq n_{j}},

(204)

where
M^{(q_{i},p_{j})}_{st}
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{{\rm gcd}(p_{j},q_{i})}
.
(Here, we set
{\rm gcd}(p_{j},\infty)=\mathbb{Z}_{p_{j}}
.)
The operations allowed in the “directional” Gauss-Jordan elimination are as follows:

- Multiply a row vector by
(-1)
.

- Multiply a column vector by
(-1)
.

- (Row reduction)
Add an integer multiple
rv^{(q_{i})}_{s},r\in\mathbb{Z},
of a
(i,s)
th row vector
v^{(q_{i})}_{s}=(M^{(q_{i},p_{j})}_{st})_{1\leq j\leq k,1\leq t\leq n_{j}}
to other row vector
v^{(q_{i^{\prime}})}_{s^{\prime}}
with
(q_{i},s)\neq(q_{i^{\prime}},s^{\prime})
and
i\geq i^{\prime}
.
Here, when adding the row vector
rv^{(q_{i})}_{s}
to the row vector
v^{(q{i^{\prime}})}_{s^{\prime}}
with
i>i^{\prime}
, take the modulo
{\rm gcd}(q_{i^{\prime}},p_{j})
of the entries
rM^{(q_{i},p_{j})}_{st}
.

- (Column reduction) Add an integer multiple
rv^{(p_{j})}_{t},r\in\mathbb{Z},
of a
(j,t)
th column vector
v^{(p_{j})}_{t}=(M^{(q_{i},p_{j})}_{st})_{1\leq i\leq l,1\leq s\leq m_{i}}
to other column vector
v^{(p_{j^{\prime}})}_{t^{\prime}}
with
(p_{j},t)\neq(p_{j^{\prime}},t^{\prime})
and
j\geq j^{\prime}
.
Here, when adding the column vector
rv^{(p_{j})}_{t}
to the column vector
v^{(p_{j^{\prime}})}_{t^{\prime}}
with
j>j^{\prime}
, take the modulo
{\rm gcd}(q_{i},p_{j^{\prime}})
of the entries
rM^{(q_{i},p_{j})}_{st}
.

The extension matrix

\displaystyle M=\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}M^{(q_{0},p_{1})}&M^{(q_{0},p_{2})}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr M^{(q_{1},p_{1})}&M^{(q_{1},p_{2})}\\
\hline\cr\vdots&&\\

\displaystyle\to\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Lambda^{(q_{0},p_{1})}&O\\
\hline\cr O&O\\
\end{array}&M^{(q_{0},p_{2})}&\cdots\\

\hline\cr\vdots&&\\
\end{array},

So, the lower half of

\displaystyle\to\begin{tabular}[]{c|c|c|c|c}$\Lambda^{(q_{0},p_{1})}$&$O$&\lx@intercol\hfil$*$\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol&\hbox{\multirowsetup$M^{(q_{0},p_{3})}$}\\

Similarly,

The matrix

is a

in the above matrix) can be replaced by their remainders when divided by

In particular, if

, the entry can be replaced by 0.

The standard form of the extension matrix
M
in the sense described above can be enumerated by the following procedure.
First, determine the non-zero entries of the Smith normal form
\Lambda^{(q_{i},p_{j})}
.
This procedure simply constructs a set of pairs between the direct summands of
A
and
B
:
A=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}}^{\oplus n_{i}},B=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{l}\mathbb{Z}_{q_{j}}^{\oplus m_{j}}

\displaystyle\Big\{\big\{(\tilde{p}_{r},\tilde{q}_{r})\in\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}\times\{q_{0},\dots,q_{l}\}\big\}_{r=1}^{d}\Big|\sum_{r=1}^{d}\delta_{p_{i},\tilde{p}_{r}}\leq n_{i}{\rm\ for\ }i=1,\dots,k,

\displaystyle\quad{\rm\ and\ }\sum_{r=1}^{d}\delta_{q_{j},\tilde{q}_{r}}\leq m_{j}{\rm\ for\ }j=1,\dots,l,{\rm\ and\ }d=0,\dots,{\rm min}(\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i},\sum_{j=0}^{l}m_{j})\Big\}.

(221)

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed the calculation methods for the
E_{2}
-page and
E^{2}
-page of AHSS, and presented the results for the classification problem of topological phases in free fermion systems, namely TIs and SCs.
For a given symmetry class, the
K
-groups in momentum space and real space are defined independently, and both give the same
K
-group.
The physical interpretation of AHSS has already been detailed in momentum space in
Shiozaki et al. 2022
and in real space in
Shiozaki et al.
.
In this paper, we have focused on the technical aspects of the calculations.
The
E_{2}
and
E^{2}
pages serve as a first approximation in the classification problem, and in general, it is necessary to solve higher differentials and extension problems as
\mathbb{Z}
-modules.
Although the physical interpretations of higher differentials and extension problems are known, systematic calculation methods are still unknown except for a few cases.
In this paper, assuming that the rank of the
K
-group does not change by higher differentials, we have enumerated possible combinations of higher differentials and extensions for the obtained
E_{2}
and
E^{2}
pages, and calculated to what extent the classification results can be restricted by the isomorphism of the
K
-groups between momentum space and real space.
As a result, about 59% of the classifications were determined for TIs and SCs protected by MSGs.
The calculation results can be obtained from Supplemental Material
Sup
and
url
.

Finally, we summarize the calculation methods for
K
-groups that were not mentioned in this paper.

– For simple MSG symmetries, the
K
-group may be determined by the Mayer-Vietoris sequence.
For example, in the case of Table
5
, the
n=0
degree is known to be
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{4}^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\oplus 2}
in Ref.
Shiozaki et al. 2016
.

– The choice of filtration for introducing AHSS is not unique.
In Sec.
III.2
, we set the condition that if the action of the group element
g\in G
on the
p
-cell is closed in
D^{p}
, then the action of
g
preserves
D^{p}
pointwise, but this condition can be relaxed.
For example, one can take the dual cell decomposition of the cell decomposition introduced in Sec.
III.2
.

– In the momentum-space AHSS, higher differentials
d_{2}^{0,0}
and
d_{3}^{0,0}
correspond to symmetry indicators that detect gapless points at generic momenta on 2-cells and 3-cells, respectively
Shiozaki et al. 2022
.
In normal states, the correspondence between symmetry indicators and (higher) topological insulators
Song et al. 2018a
;
Khalaf et al. 2018
;
Peng et al. 2022b
, or gapless semimetals
Song et al. 2018b
;
Peng et al. 2022b
, is well-established.
As a result, some calculations in Sec.
VI.1
can be replaced with known higher differentials to get more correct
E_{\infty}
page.
Although symmetry indicators for superconductors have been proposed and classified
Geier et al. 2020
;
Ono et al. 2020
;
Ono et al. 2021
, a comprehensive correspondence between (higher) topological superconductors and gapless superconductors has yet to be achieved.

– In the real-space AHSS, some higher differentials have been systematically calculated for some symmetry classes.
In Ref.
Ono et al. 2022
, for time-reversal symmetric SCs with trivial pairing symmetry, a part of
d^{2}
is calculated based on the equivalence between the second differential
d^{2}_{2,-2}
of the real space AHSS and the superconducting vortex zero modes.

–
K
-groups are not just
\mathbb{Z}
-modules but are modules over some
K
-group.
For example, in the problem setting of this paper, the
K
-group is a module over the representation ring
R(G_{0})
of the point group
G_{0}
.
The differentials are homomorphisms as an
R(G_{0})
-module, and the extension is that as an
R(G_{0})
-module.
Although we did not discuss the implementation of the module structure in this paper, it is expected to impose strong constraints on the construction of higher differentials and the solution of extension problems.

Appendix A
An algorithm computing cell decomposition

This section presents an example of how to construct the cell decomposition introduced in Sec.
III.2
.
We describe the cell decomposition of real space in three dimensions.
The cell decomposition in momentum spaces in lower dimensions can also be obtained in the same way.
The input is an MSG
{\cal G}
, which is equipped with the set of symmetry actions
x\to g(x)
for
g\in{\cal G}
on points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We first describe how a stereohedron, a convex polyhedron that fills space isohedrally, is given.
Such a stereohedron is also called a fundamental domain or an asymmetric unit.
Next, we explain a method to compute 2-, 1-, and 0-cells in order, starting from the boundary of the stereohedron.

A.1
Fundamental domain

Let
{\cal G}
be an MSG.
Pick a reference point
a\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that the little group
{\cal G}_{a}=\{g\in{\cal G}|g(a)=a\}
is the same as that for generic points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
From such a reference point, a stereohedron is given by (
Schmitt 2016
, Theorem 1.2.1.)

\displaystyle DV(a)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|

\displaystyle|x-a|<|x-g(a)|\mbox{ for all }g\in{\cal G}\backslash{\cal G}_{a}\}.

(284)

(
DV
means the Dirichlet-Voronoi stereohedron in
Schmitt 2016
.)
Note that
DV(a)
is convex.

The set of vertices of the stereohedron
DV(a)
is computed as follows.
Only points in the orbit
{\cal G}(a)=\{g(a)|g\in{\cal G}\}
near the reference point
a
are needed.
We represent the lattice translation group elements
\tau\in\Pi
by
\tau=\{1|t\}
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the lattice translation vector.
For
\tau=\{1|t\}\in\Pi
, we introduce the open slab

\displaystyle S_{a}(t)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|(a-t,t)<(x,t)<(a+t,t)\}.

(285)

Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
be a basis of the group of lattice translations.
The following holds true (
Schmitt 2016
, Lemma B.2.).
The subset
{\cal O}
of the orbit
{\cal G}(a)
defined by

\displaystyle{\cal O}

\displaystyle=\left(({\cal G}(a)\backslash\{a\})\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}S_{a}(t_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\cup\{\pm t_{1}(a),\pm t_{2}(a),\pm t_{3}(a)\}

(286)

suffices for computing
DV(a)
.
For a set of points
{\cal S}
, let us introduce the Dirichlet-Voronoi stereohedron

\displaystyle DV(a;{\cal S})

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|

\displaystyle|x-a|<|x-p|\mbox{ for all }p\in{\cal S}\}.

(287)

The above implies that
DV(a)=DV(a;{\cal O})
.
We call
{\cal O}
the set of relevant points of
a
.

To efficiently compute
DV(a)
, we can do the following.
Introduce two subsets of
{\cal O}
by
{\cal O}_{0}:=\{\pm t_{1}(a),\pm t_{2}(a),\pm t_{3}(a)\}
and
{\cal O}^{\prime}:=({\cal G}(a)\backslash\{a\})\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}S_{a}(t_{i})
such that
{\cal O}_{0}\cup{\cal O}^{\prime}={\cal O}
.
As a starting big open polyhedron, define
DV(a)_{0}=DV(a;{\cal O}_{0})
.
We sort the set of points in order of distance from
a
and do the following for all the points
p_{1},\dots,p_{|{\cal O}^{\prime}|}\in{\cal O}^{\prime}
step by step.
For
p_{i}\in{\cal O}^{\prime}
, if there is a vertex of
DV(a)_{i-1}
in the region
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}||x-a|>|x-p_{i}|\}
, set
{\cal O}_{i}={\cal O}_{i-1}\cup\{p_{i}\}
and update
DV(a)_{i-1}
by
DV(a;{\cal O}_{i})
, otherwise

Appendix B
Computing irreducible characters

In this section we give a method to derive all the irreducible characters for a given finite group
G
with a factor system
z_{g,h}

Watanabe 2019
.
For the basis
\{\ket{g}\}_{g\in G}
labeled by the group elements, the (left) regular representation is defined by
\hat{g}\ket{k}=z_{g,k}\ket{gk}
.
The representation matrix
u^{R}_{g}
, which is defined by
\hat{g}\ket{k}=\sum_{h\in G}[u^{R}_{g}]_{hk}
, is

\displaystyle[u^{R}_{g}]_{hk}=z_{g,k}\delta_{h,gk}.

(288)

As a matter of fact, the regular representation decomposes as a direct sum of the irreps, and each irrep appears with the number of its dimension of the irrep.
Namely,

\displaystyle R=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\{{\rm irreps}\}}\alpha^{\oplus{\rm dim\,}\alpha}.

(289)

The key point here is that the regular representation includes all the irreps.

Given a Hermitian random matrix
H
, symmetrizing it by

\displaystyle H^{G}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}u_{g}Hu_{g}^{-1}

(290)

and diagonalizing
H^{G}
, we get the eigenvector
\ket{\phi_{\alpha}}
for irreps
\alpha
.
If the matrix
H
is taken at random, there is no accidental degeneracy of
{\rm dim\,}\alpha
numerically.
The irreducible character is given by
\chi^{\alpha}_{g}={\rm tr\,}[\braket{\phi_{\alpha}|\hat{g}|\phi_{\alpha}}]

Appendix C
Computation in
\mathbb{Z}
-module

Let
A
and
B

\mathbb{Z}
-modules.
For a homomorphism

\displaystyle f:A\to B,

(291)

we summarize how to compute
{\rm Im\,}f
and
{\rm Ker\,}f
.
Firstly, we express the group
A
as the quotient groups between torsion free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules as
A=\tilde{A}/P_{A}
and
B=\tilde{B}/P_{B}
.
For example, if
A=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/k_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/k_{u}\mathbb{Z}
be the invariant factor decomposition of
A
, then
\tilde{A}
and
P_{A}
can be
\tilde{A}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\oplus\underbrace{\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}}_{u}
and
P_{A}=k_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus k_{u}\mathbb{Z}
.
Set a lifted homomorphism
\tilde{f}:\tilde{A}\to\tilde{B}
such that the following diagram commutes.

\displaystyle\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>P_{A}@>{}>{}>\tilde{A}@>{}>{}>A@>{}>{}>0\\
@V{\tilde{f}}V{}V@V{\tilde{f}}V{}V@V{f}V{}V\\
0@>{}>{}>P_{B}@>{}>{}>\tilde{B}@>{}>{}>B@>{}>{}>0\\
\end{CD}

(292)

Assuming commutativity on the right side of the diagram,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}){\rm\ mod\ }P_{B}=f(\tilde{a}{\rm\ mod\ }P_{A}),

(293)

commutativity on the left side,
\tilde{f}(P_{A})\subset P_{B}
, follows from
\tilde{f}(P_{A}){\rm\ mod\ }P_{B}=f(P_{A}{\rm\ mod\ }P_{A})=f(0)=0
.
Given a matrix expression
M_{f}
of
f
for bases of
A
and
B
, a matrix expression
M_{\tilde{f}}
of such
\tilde{f}
is obtained by considering the matrix
M_{f}
as
\mathbb{Z}
-valued.

C.1
{\rm Im\,}f

From (
293
), we have

\displaystyle{\rm Im\,}f=\tilde{f}(\tilde{A}){\rm\ mod\ }P_{B}=\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})/(\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})\cap P_{B}).

(294)

Using the second isomorphism theorem, we get

\displaystyle{\rm Im\,}f=(\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})+P_{B})/P_{B}.

(295)

Here,
\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})+P_{B}
is the union

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})+P_{B}=\{x+y\in\tilde{B}|x\in\tilde{f}(\tilde{A}),y\in P_{B}\}.

(296)

C.2
{\rm Ker\,}f

Note that

\displaystyle{\rm Ker\,}f

\displaystyle=\{a\in A|f(a)=0\}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}|\tilde{f}(\tilde{a})\in P_{B}\}{\rm\ mod\ }P_{A}.

(297)

Using
\tilde{f}(P_{A})\subset P_{B}
,

\displaystyle{\rm Ker\,}f=\{\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}|\tilde{f}(\tilde{a})\in P_{B}\}/P_{A}.

(298)

Here, the numerator can be expressed as

\displaystyle\{\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}|\tilde{f}(\tilde{a})\in P_{B}\}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}|{}^{\exists}\tilde{b}\in P_{B},{\rm\ s.t.\ }\tilde{f}(\tilde{a})+\tilde{b}=0\}.

(299)

Thus, introducing the homomorphism

\displaystyle\tilde{f}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{B}}

\displaystyle:\tilde{A}\oplus P_{B}\to\tilde{B},

\displaystyle(\tilde{a},\tilde{b})\mapsto\tilde{f}(\tilde{a})+\tilde{b},

(300)

we have

\displaystyle\{\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}|\tilde{f}(\tilde{a})\subset P_{B}\}={\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{f}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{B}})|_{\tilde{A}}.

(301)

Here,
{\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{f}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{B}})|_{\tilde{A}}
is the projection of
{\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{f}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{B}})
onto
\tilde{A}
.
We get

\displaystyle{\rm Ker\,}f={\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{f}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{B}})|_{\tilde{A}}/P_{A}.

(302)

C.3
{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f

Now let us consider a sequence of homomorphisms

\displaystyle\begin{CD}A@>{f}>{}>B@>{g}>{}>C\end{CD}

(303)

satisfying
{\rm Im\,}f\subset{\rm Ker\,}g
.
By use of the formulas above, the quotient group
{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f=\frac{{\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{g}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{C}})|_{\tilde{B}}/P_{B}}{(\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})+P_{B})/P_{B}}.

(304)

Applying the third isomorphism theorem to it, this is a quotient group between torsion-free abelian subgroups of
\tilde{B}
,

\displaystyle{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f=\frac{{\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{g}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{C}})|_{\tilde{B}}}{\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})+P_{B}}.

(305)

Practically, this quotient group can be computed by first expanding the basis of
\tilde{f}(\tilde{A})+P_{B}
in the basis of
{\rm Ker\,}(\tilde{g}\oplus{\rm Id}_{P_{C}})|_{\tilde{B}}
(the pseudo-inverse matrix can be used) and then computing the Smith normal form of the matrix consisting of the expansion coefficients.

Appendix D
Baer Sum

Consider two extensions of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules as follows:

\displaystyle 0\to B\xrightarrow{f_{0}}E_{0}\xrightarrow{g_{0}}A\to 0,

(306)

\displaystyle 0\to B\xrightarrow{f_{1}}E_{1}\xrightarrow{g_{1}}A\to 0

(307)

We want to construct an extension corresponding to the sum in
{\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}(A,B)

\displaystyle f:B\to E_{0}\oplus E_{1},\quad b\mapsto(f_{0}(b),-f_{1}(b)),

\displaystyle g:E_{0}\oplus E_{1}\to A,\quad(e_{0},e_{1})\mapsto g_{0}(e_{0})-g_{1}(e_{1}),

(309)

Define the maps as above.
The inclusion

b\mapsto(f_{0}(b),-f_{1}(b))\mapsto g_{0}\circ f_{0}(b)+g_{1}\circ f_{1}(b)=0+0

The Baer sum is given by

\displaystyle 0\to B\xrightarrow{f^{\prime}}{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f\xrightarrow{g^{\prime}}A\to 0.

(310)

Here,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}:B\to{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f,

\displaystyle b\mapsto[(f_{0}(b),0)]=[(0,f_{1}(b))],

(311)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}:{\rm Ker\,}g/{\rm Im\,}f\to A,

\displaystyle(e_{0},e_{1})\mapsto g_{0}(e_{0})=g_{1}(e_{1}).

(312)

Suppose
(f_{0}(b),0)=(f_{0}(b^{\prime}),-f_{1}(b^{\prime}))

(f_{0}(b-b^{\prime}),f_{1}(b^{\prime}))=(0,0)

f_{0}
and
f_{1}

Notice that there is no intersection between

{\rm Im\,}f

The well-definedness of

follows from
(f_{0}(b),-f_{1}(b))\mapsto g_{0}(f_{0}(b))=0=g_{1}(f_{1}(b))

The exactness is as follows.
For the injectivity of
f^{\prime}
, suppose
[(f_{0}(b),0)]=0
.
Then, there exists
b^{\prime}\in B

b=0
.
The surjectivity of

follows from that
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
are surjective.
The inclusion
{\rm Im\,}f^{\prime}\subset{\rm Ker\,}g^{\prime}

[f_{0}(b),0]\mapsto g_{0}(f_{0}(b))=0
.
We will show
{\rm Ker\,}g^{\prime}\subset{\rm Im\,}f^{\prime}

Suppose
[(e_{0},e_{1})]\mapsto g_{0}(e)=g_{1}(e^{\prime})=0

Due to the surjectivity of

and
