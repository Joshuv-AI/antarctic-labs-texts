A novel locking-free virtual element method for linear elasticity problems
Jianguo Huang, Sen Lin, Yue Yu

Abstract
This paper devises a novel lowest-order conforming virtual element method (VEM) for planar linear elasticity with the pure displacement/traction boundary condition. The main trick is to view a generic polygon K K as a new one K ~ \widetilde{K} with additional vertices consisting of interior points on edges of K K , so that the discrete admissible space is taken as the V 1 V_{1} type virtual element space related to the partition { K ~ } \{\widetilde{K}\} instead of { K } \{K\} . The method is shown to be uniformly convergent with the optimal rates both in H 1 H^{1} and L 2 L^{2} norms with respect to the Lamé constant λ \lambda . Numerical tests are presented to illustrate the good performance of the proposed VEM and confirm the theoretical results.

Introduction

Robust numerical solution of parameter dependent partial differential equations (PDEs) is a very important topic in scientific computing.
A typical example is the linear elasticity problem which involves the Lamé constants
\lambda
and
\mu
.
If
\lambda
is very large, the material becomes nearly incompressible, which will lead to the so-called volume locking phenomenon
when the Courant element is used for discretization (cf.
[
9
]
).
Because of this, a huge work has been developed to overcome this difficulty.
We refer the reader to
[

41

and the references therein for details along this line.

On the other hand, the virtual element method (VEM) is a newly proposed numerical method for solving PDEs in recent years,
which can be viewed as a generalized finite element method allowing for the use of polytopal meshes (polygons or polyhedra) of very general shape.
It was first proposed and analyzed in
[
3
,
10
,
12
]

and immediately attracted much attention from the research community due to its advantages in handling problems with complex geometries,
high-regularity solutions (cf.
[
22
,
31
]
).
As far as we know, there have also developed some locking free VEMs for the linear elasticity problem.
Let
k
denote the order of the virtual element used. The conforming VEMs for this problem were first studied in
[
11
]
.
However, the condition
k\geq 2
is required so as to produce locking free optimal error estimates in the
H^{1}
norm.
More recently, a lowest-order locking-free VEM was devised in
[
37
]
to remedy this disadvantage
by modifying the
W_{1}

the extension of the well-known Bernardi-Raugel finite element method (cf.
[
15

) in general polygonal meshes.
In
[
40

, some nonconforming VEMs were proposed for attacking the previous problem in two and three dimensions.
It was shown in this paper that if
k\geq 1
(resp.
k\geq 2
), the method is locking-free for the pure displacement (resp. traction) problem.
We mention in passing that there are some other VEMs discussing numerical solution of elasticity problems in different aspects; see, e.g.,

In this paper, we are concerned with proposing and analyzing a novel lowest-order conforming locking-free VEM
for the pure dispalcement/traction problem, by means of a significant feature of VEMs. The main ideas behind the method include:

1.

For a generic polygonal element
K
with
N_{K}
edges, denoted by
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{K}}
its vertices which are numbered in a counter-clockwise order
and by
e_{i}=\overline{z_{i}z_{i+1}}
the edge connecting
z_{i}
to
z_{i+1}
, where
z_{N_{K}+1}:=z_{1}
.
Then we construct an auxiliary polygon
\widetilde{K}
(see Fig.
1
),
which has the same geometric shape as
K
but has more vertices. Precisely speaking,
besides the vertices of
K
,
\widetilde{K}
has additional vertices formed by the internal points
\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{K}}
of edges of
K
,
which are chosen such that
|\overline{z_{i}m_{i}}|=\alpha|\overline{z_{i}z_{i+1}}|
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is any fixed constant.
It is a significant advantage of VEMs that one can view an interior point of an edge as an additional vertex,
so that the difficulty of hanging nodes during adaptivity can be overcome very conveniently (cf.
[
30
]
).
But here we use the technique to construct locking free VEMs.

2.

Based on the
V_{1}
type virtual element space with respect the partition
\{\widetilde{K}\}
instead of the usual one
\{K\}
,
a locking free VEM is naturally devised using the ideas in
[
11
]
for
k\geq 2
.

Under the standard mesh assumption
C0
(see Subsection
3.1
), we can show the proposed method is locking free
and has the optimal convergence order by invoking a novel interpolation operator which satisfies the required commutative property
and admits the optimal error estimates. We offer several numerical tests to illustrate the performance of the proposed method
and confirm its theoretical predictions.

2
The linear elasticity problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex polygonal domain with the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Given an applied force
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, the linear elasticity problem is
to find the displacement vector
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\,u_{2})^{\intercal}
such that

\begin{cases}-\boldsymbol{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{f}&\text{in}~~\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}&\text{on}~~\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=[\varepsilon_{ij}]_{2\times 2}
,
\varepsilon_{ij}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{i})
,
and

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda({\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{I}.

Here,
\mu
and
\lambda
are the Lamé constants satisfying
\mu\in[\mu_{1},\mu_{2}]

with
0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, and
\boldsymbol{I}
is the identity matrix.

The variational formulation of (
2.1
) is
to find
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

a(\boldsymbol{u},\,\boldsymbol{v})=(\boldsymbol{f},\,\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V},

(2.2)

where

a(\boldsymbol{w},\,\boldsymbol{v})=2\mu(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}))+\lambda({\rm div}~\boldsymbol{w},\,{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V},

(2.3)

and

(\boldsymbol{f},\,\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}.

By the well-known Korn inequality (cf.
[
17
]
):

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(2.4)

it is easy to obtain the boundedness and coercivity of
a(\cdot,\,\cdot)
:

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{w},\,\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\lambda)|\boldsymbol{w}|_{1}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V},

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{v}|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim a(\boldsymbol{v},\,\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V},

which imply that the problem (
2.1
) has a unique solution by the Lax-Milgram lemma.

Since the first term of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
(cf. (
2.3
)) is related to
\mu
, we write

a_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})=(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}))

for later uses.

It is shown in
[
18
]
that the following regularity estimate holds for the solution
\boldsymbol{u}
of (
2.1
):

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}+\lambda\|{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0}.

(2.5)

3
Virtual element methods for the pure displacement problem

In this section, by means of a mesh refinement trick in VEMs,
we propose a novel lowest order locking-free conforming virtual element method on the fine mesh.

3.1
Mesh assumption and the virtual element space

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
be a family of decompositions of
\Omega
into polygonal elements.
The generic element is denoted by
K
with diameter
h_{K}={\rm diam}(K)
.
For clarity, we work on meshes satisfying the following condition (cf.
[
10
]
):

C0
.

For each element
K
, there exists positive constants
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
independent of
h_{K}
such that

(a)

K
is star-shaped with respect to a disc in
K
with radius
\geq\gamma_{1}{h_{K}}
;

(b)

the distance between any two vertices of
K
is
\geq\gamma_{2}h_{K}
.

Fig.
1:

The auxiliary polygon
\widetilde{K}
satisfying the mesh assumption
C0
.
The polygonal elements of the fine mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}
and the original mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
are denoted by
\widetilde{K}
and
K
, respectively.
The red circle points denote the original vertices, the blue square points denote the added vertices.

Due to the large flexibility of the meshes in VEMs, under the above assumption,
one easily finds that the mesh can be refined by taking the internal point
m_{i}
of each edge
e_{i}
of every element
K

as a new vertex (see Fig.
1
).
For the new edge
|\overline{z_{i}m_{i}}|
, we assume
|\overline{z_{i}m_{i}}|=\alpha|e_{i}|
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{K}
for a given constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
The resulting fine mesh will be denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}
with the generic element given by
\widetilde{K}
.
It’s obvious that
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}
still satisfies the assumption
C0
thought the generic constants may change.
Here,
h=\max_{\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}}h_{\widetilde{K}}
with
h_{\widetilde{K}}
being the diameter of
\widetilde{K}
.
It is evident that
h_{K}\eqsim h_{\widetilde{K}}
.

In this paper, we consider the lowest-order conforming virtual element space (VES) defined on the fine mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}
.
To this end, we briefly review the construction method mentioned in
[
10
]
.
The local scalar virtual element space on
\widetilde{K}
is defined as

V_{1}(\widetilde{K})=\big\{v\in H^{1}(\widetilde{K})~|~\Delta v=0~~\mbox{in}~~\widetilde{K},~v|_{e}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(e),\quad e\subset\partial{\widetilde{K}}\big\}.

(3.1)

The local virtual element space for linear elasticity is then given by
the tensor product space
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K})=(V_{1}(\widetilde{K}))^{2}
.
The corresponding local degrees of freedoms (DOFs) are:

1.

\chi_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\chi}
: the values at vertices of
\widetilde{K}
,

\chi_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{v}(a_{i}),\quad\mbox{where~$a_{i}=z_{i}$~or~$m_{i}$~is the vertex of ~$\widetilde{K}$}.

In what follows, denote by
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
the global VES, which is defined elementwise and required that
the degrees of freedom are continuous through the interior vertices while zero for the boundary DOFs,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{h}=\big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)~|~\boldsymbol{v}|_{\widetilde{K}}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K}),\quad\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}\big\}.

3.2
The virtual element method

As in
[
11
]
,
we first introduce the elliptic projection
{\Pi}_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\colon\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\widetilde{K})\to(\mathbb{P}_{1}(\widetilde{K}))^{2}

for dealing with the first term of (
2.3
), which is given by

\begin{cases}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\Pi}_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}),\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{p}))_{\widetilde{K}}=(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}),\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{p}))_{\widetilde{K}},\quad\boldsymbol{p}\in(\mathbb{P}_{1}(\widetilde{K}))^{2},\\
\int_{\partial{\widetilde{K}}}{\Pi}_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\partial{\widetilde{K}}}\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}s,\\
\int_{\widetilde{K}}{\rm{rot}}~{\Pi}_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\widetilde{K}}{\rm{rot}}~\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{cases}

(3.2)

One can check that the elliptic projection
{\Pi}_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}
can be computed by the given DOFs.
For simplicity, we write
\Pi_{1}
for the related elementwise defined global operator.

Next, Let
\Pi_{0}^{K}
be the usual
L^{2}
orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(K)
into the constant space over
K
. In other words,
for all
v\in L^{2}(K)
,
\Pi_{0}^{K}v=\int_{K}v\mathrm{d}x/|K|
. This definition naturally applies to a
L^{2}
vector valued function.
For all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(K)
, by integration by parts,

\int_{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial{K}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{K}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial{K}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{K}=(n_{1},n_{2})^{\intercal}
and
\boldsymbol{t}_{K}=(-n_{2},n_{1})^{\intercal}
denote the unit normal and tangent vector
to
\partial{K}
determined by the right hand side, respectively. Hence, for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(K)
,
we can compute
\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{v}
and
\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{v}
only using the function values at the vertices of
K
.
Therefore, we treat the second term in (
2.3
) using the exact method for the case
k\geq 2
given in
[
11
]
.

With the help of the above projections, we construct the local computable bilinear form by

\displaystyle a_{h}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=2\mu a_{\mu,h}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\lambda(\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\,\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{K},\quad\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K}),

(3.3)

where

\displaystyle a_{\mu,h}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}),\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))_{\widetilde{K}}+S^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

and

S^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\cdot\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N_{\widetilde{K}}}\chi_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\cdot\chi_{i}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

with
N_{\widetilde{K}}=2N_{K}
.

The locking-free conforming VEM of the variational problem (
2.2
) is
to find
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
such that

a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle,\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},

(3.4)

where

a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\sum\limits_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}a_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),

and the approximation of the right hand side is given by

\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle=\sum_{\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})_{\widetilde{K}},

(3.5)

where
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}|_{\widetilde{K}}=\Pi_{0}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{f}
and

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}=\frac{1}{|\partial{\widetilde{K}}|}\int_{\partial\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Remark 3.1
.

It is worth mentioning that the proposed lowest-order conforming virtual element on triangular meshes is not the well-known Courant element.

3.3
Stability of the virtual element method

We first recall a classic Korn’s inequality with respect to a star-shaped domain.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
18
,
23
]
)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain of diameter
h
, which is star-shaped with respect to a disc of radius
\rho
.
Then for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(D)
satisfying
\int_{D}{\rm{rot}}~\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=0
,
there holds

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{v}|_{1,D}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{0,D},

where the hidden constant
C
only depends on the aspect ratio
h/\rho
.

With the help of the above lemmas, we are able to derive the norm equivalence and the stability condition
for the proposed virtual element method (
3.4
) described as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the mesh assumption
C0
, for all
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
there hold

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}\eqsim\|\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{l^{2}},\quad\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*},

(3.6)

and

\displaystyle a_{\mu,h}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\eqsim a_{\mu}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\quad\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*},

(3.7)

where
a_{\mu}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
is the local conterpart of
a_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}
and Lemma
3.1
, one has

\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}\eqsim|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{1,\widetilde{K}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}.

(3.8)

According to the norm equivalence in
[
21
]
,

h_{\widetilde{K}}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}\eqsim\|\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{l^{2}}

for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K})
, and hence

h_{\widetilde{K}}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}\eqsim\|\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{l^{2}}.

(3.9)

In view of the inverse inequality in
[
21
]
and the standard Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality,
we further obtain

h_{\widetilde{K}}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}\eqsim|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{1,\widetilde{K}},

which together with (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) yields the first estimate.

The second estimate follows readily from the first estimate by noting that

\displaystyle a_{\mu}^{\widetilde{K}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}^{2}.

∎

4
Error analysis

Following the similar arguments in
[
11
]
, we can derive an abstract lemma for the error analysis.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{V}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
be the solution of the problem (
2.2
) and
(
3.4
), respectively.
Then under the mesh assumption
C0
, for any
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}

and for any piecewise polynomial
\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}\in(\mathbb{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}))^{2}
, there holds

|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|_{1}\lesssim\big(|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}|_{1}+|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}|_{1,h}+\lambda\|{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi_{0}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{0}+\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{{}^{\prime}}}\big),

(4.1)

where
\Pi_{0}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}=\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{v}
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
, and

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}_{h}^{{}^{\prime}}}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{f},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle}{|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{1}}.

(4.2)

Proof.

By setting
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}=\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}
and using the triangle’s inequality, it suffices to bound
|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}|_{1}
.
According to the equivalence (
3.7
) and Korn’s inequality (
2.4
), we have the coercivity

|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}|_{1}^{2}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h})\|_{0}^{2}=a_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h})\eqsim a_{\mu,h}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}).

The remaining arguments are similar to the ones given in the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
[
11
]
.
∎

We now prove the locking-free property of the proposed VEM.
To this end, we shall introduce a special interpolation operator as presented in the proof of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\boldsymbol{H}^{2}(\Omega)
be the solution of problem (
2.2
).
Then there exists a VEM function
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}=\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u},\quad\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}=\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u},\quad K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}|_{1,K}\lesssim h|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2,K},\quad K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

(4.4)

Proof.

By the definition of
\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}
and by integration by parts, one has

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{\partial{K}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{e\subset\partial{K}}\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then the first relation of (
4.3
) is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{e\subset\partial{K}}\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{e\subset\partial{K}}\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Similarly, the second relation of (
4.3
) is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{e\subset\partial{K}}\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{e\subset\partial{K}}\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, it suffices to construct a VEM function
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
such that

\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{e}\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad e\subset\partial{K}.

We construct the function
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}
by determining its DOF values.
For any edge
e_{i}
of
K
with two end points
z_{i}
and
z_{i+1}
with
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{K}
,
there exists one internal point
m_{i}
such that the edge
e_{i}
is divided into two subedges

e_{i1}=\overline{z_{i}m_{i}}
and
e_{i2}=\overline{m_{i}z_{i+1}}
, and

|e_{i1}|=\alpha|e_{i}|
. For the vertices of
K
, we define

\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(z_{i})=\boldsymbol{u}(z_{i}),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N_{K}.

Since
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}
is piecewise linear along
\partial K
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{e_{i}}\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{e_{i}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{e_{i1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{e_{i2}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{|e_{i1}|}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(z_{i})+\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(m_{i})\big)+\frac{|e_{i2}|}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(m_{i})+\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(z_{i+1})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{|e_{i}|}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(m_{i})+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(z_{i})+(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(z_{i+1})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{|e_{i}|}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(m_{i})+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}(z_{i})+(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{u}(z_{i+1})\big),

which forces us to define

\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}(m_{i})=\frac{2}{|e_{i}|}\int_{e_{i}}\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}s-\alpha\boldsymbol{u}(z_{i})-(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{u}(z_{i+1}),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N_{K}.

Denote
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}|_{K}=\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Since
\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K})
, we have by the inverse inequality and norm equivalence in
[
21
]
that

|\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}|_{1,K}^{2}\lesssim h_{K}^{-2}\|\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,K}^{2}\eqsim\sum_{p\in K}(\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}(p))^{2}\lesssim\|u\|_{0,\infty,K}^{2},

(4.5)

where
p\in K
means
p
is a vertex of
\widetilde{K}
.

Under the mesh assumption
C0
, by connecting the center of the disc used in the star-shaped condition with the vertices of
K
,
we can obtain a virtual triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{K}
of
K
, which is quasi-uniform and shape-regular with the mesh size
h_{K}
.
Moreover, the number of the triangles in
\mathcal{T}_{K}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
For all
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{K}
, it follows from the Sobolev inequality and the scaling argument that

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\infty,\tau}^{2}\lesssim h_{\tau}^{-2}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\tau}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1,\tau}^{2}+h_{\tau}^{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2,\tau}^{2},

where
h_{\tau}
denotes the diameter of
\tau
, which is proportional to
h_{K}
. This immediately implies

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\infty,K}^{2}\lesssim h_{K}^{-2}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,K}^{2}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1,K}^{2}+h_{K}^{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2,K}^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{2}(K).

(4.6)

On the other hand, by the Dupont-Scott theory (cf.
[
17
]
), for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{2}(K)

there exists an
\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}\in(\mathbb{P}_{1}(K))^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}\|_{0,K}+h_{K}|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}|_{1,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2,K}.

(4.7)

Observing that
\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}
, we have by (
4.5
)–(
4.7
) that

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}|_{1,K}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{I}_{K}\boldsymbol{u}|_{1,K}\leq|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}|_{1,K}+|\mathcal{I}_{K}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi})|_{1,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{K}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2,K}+h_{K}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}\|_{0,K}+|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}|_{1,K}+h_{K}|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi}|_{2,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{K}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2,K}.

So the required estimate (
4.4
) holds.
∎

Thanks to Lemmas
4.1
-
4.2
,
the projection error estimates (cf.
[
17
]
) and
the estimate for (
4.2
) (cf.
[
11
]
),
we are able to derive the following error estimate which is uniform with the incompressible parameter
\lambda
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
be given as in Lemma
4.1
.
Under the mesh assumption
C0
and the condition of regularity estimate (
2.5
),
there holds

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|_{1}\lesssim h\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0},

(4.8)

with the hidden positive constant
C
independent of
h
and
\lambda
.

Using the duality argument and following the similar proof of Lemma 2.4 in
[
11
]
,
we can establish the error estimate in
L^{2}
norm.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that the domain
\Omega
is convex. Under the same assumptions of Theorem
4.1
,
there holds

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0}\lesssim h^{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0}.

(4.9)

5
The VEM for the pure traction problem

In this section, we extend our approach to the pure traction problem described as follows:

\begin{cases}-{\mathbf{div}}~\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{f}&\text{in}~~\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}&\text{on}~~\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(5.1)

where
\boldsymbol{n}
denotes the unit vector outward normal to
\partial\Omega
.
The problem (
5.1
) is uniquely solvable in
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}
defined later
if and only if
\boldsymbol{f}
satisfies the following compatibility condition (cf.
[
17
,
18
]
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in{\bf{\rm RM}},

where

{\bf{\rm RM}}=\big\{\boldsymbol{v}~|~\boldsymbol{v}=(a+bx_{2},\,c-bx_{1})^{\intercal},~a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

We define the admissible space as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)~\big|~\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}s=\boldsymbol{0},~\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=0\Big\},

which is slightly different from the space
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
defined by (cf.
[
17
]
)

\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}^{1}(\Omega)=\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)~\big|~\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\boldsymbol{0},~\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x=0\Big\}.

Here, the first constraint is replaced by an integrand on the boundary
\partial\Omega
not the whole domain
\Omega
.
The choice here is considered for two reasons, one is to ensure the interpolation of the VEM function lies in the underlying VES,
and the other is to make the terms associated with Lagrange multipliers computable in the implementation.
One can refer to Subsection
6.1
for details.
The continuous variational problem (
5.1
) is the same as the one
given in (
2.2
), with the function space replaced by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}
.
The local VES on
\widetilde{K}
is given by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{1}(\widetilde{K})=(\widehat{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K}))^{2}
,
where
\widehat{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K})
is the same as the one given in (
3.1
), i.e.

\widehat{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K})=\big\{v\in H^{1}(\widetilde{K})~|~\Delta{v}=0~~\mbox{in}~~\widetilde{K},~{v}|_{e}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(e),\quad e\subset\partial{\widetilde{K}}\big\},

and the global VES given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)~|~\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{\widetilde{K}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{1}(\widetilde{K}),\quad\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h},~\int\nolimits_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s=\boldsymbol{0},~\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x=0\Big\}.

The locking-free conforming VEM of problem (
5.1
) is
to find
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
such that

a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle,\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}.

(5.2)

Here, the construction of the computable bilinear form and the approximation of the right hand side
are the same with that in (
3.4
).

We now establish a same result as in Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}\cap\boldsymbol{H}^{2}(\Omega)
be the weak solution of
problem (
5.1
).
Then there exists a VEM function
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}=\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u},\quad K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

and

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}|_{1}\lesssim h|\boldsymbol{u}|_{2}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
be the function given in Lemma
4.2
.
Then it immediately gives

\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}=\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{u},\quad\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}=\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u},\quad\,K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

Define

\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}-\frac{1}{|\partial\Omega|}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

We then have

\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}{\rm d}s=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{I}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

which indicates
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{J}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
. The estimates obviously follows from (
4.4
).
∎

With the help of the above lemma and following the same argument in Section
4
,
we are able to derive the parameter-free error estimates described as follows, whose proof is omitted for simplicity.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}
be the weak solution of problem (
5.1
) and

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
be the discrete solution of problem (
5.2
), respectively.
Then there holds

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|_{1}\lesssim h\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0}.

Moreover, if
\Omega
is convex, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0}\lesssim h^{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0}.

Here, the hidden positive constant
C
is independent of
h
and
\lambda
.

6
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present some numerical tests for the locking-free conforming VEMs (
3.4
)
and (
5.2
) to confirm the theoretical results. All the tests are implemented in Matlab R2016a.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}
be the exact solution of (
2.1
) (resp. (
5.1
)) and

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
the discrete solution of the proposed VEM (
3.4
) (resp. (
5.2
)).
For the ease of programming, we always choose
\alpha=1/2
in our numerical method.
That means, the midpoint of each edge of the polygonal element
K
is taken as an additional vertex of
\widetilde{K}
.
Since the VEM solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
is not explicitly known inside the polygonal elements,
as in
[
13
]
, we will evaluate the computable errors
by comparing the exact solution
\boldsymbol{u}
with the elliptic projection
\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}
of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
.
In this way, the discrete
L^{2}
error ErrL2 and
H^{1}
error ErrH1 are respectively quantified by

\displaystyle{\rm ErrL2}=\|\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0}=\Big(\sum\limits_{\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\widetilde{K}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm ErrH1}=|\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|_{1}=\Big(\sum\limits_{\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*}}|\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi_{1}^{\widetilde{K}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|_{1,\widetilde{K}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

6.1
Implementation of the proposed VEM

In this subsection, to help the reader use the numerical method,
we present the computer implementation of the
L^{2}
projection
\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}
.
One can refer to
[
25
]
for the numerical realization of other standard terms.
Let
\phi^{\intercal}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{N})
be the basis functions of
V_{1}(\widetilde{K})
and denote

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}=({\overline{\phi}_{1}},\cdots,{\overline{\phi}_{N}},{\underline{\phi}_{1}},\cdots,{\underline{\phi}_{N}}),\quad N=2N_{K}

to be the basis functions of the vector space
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\widetilde{K})
, where

{\overline{\phi}_{i}}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{i}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad~~{\underline{\phi}_{i}}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\phi_{i}\end{pmatrix},\quad~i=1,2,\cdots,{N}.

Then the definition of
L^{2}
projection
\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}
can be written in vector form as

\int_{K}\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{K}{\rm div}~{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}}\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad\boldsymbol{H}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{0*}=\boldsymbol{C}_{0},

where

\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{0*},\quad\boldsymbol{H}_{0}=|K|,\quad\boldsymbol{C}_{0}=\int_{K}{\rm div}~{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

By integration by parts,

\boldsymbol{C}_{0}=\int_{K}{\rm div}~{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial{K}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\partial{K}}[\phi^{\intercal}\cdot{n_{1}},\phi^{\intercal}\cdot{n_{2}}]\,\mathrm{d}s.

Owing to the Kronecker property
\chi_{i}(\phi_{j})=\delta_{ij}
, the two integrals of the right-hand side can be easily computed
by using the trapezoidal rule and the associated local stiffness matrix

\boldsymbol{B}_{K}=\int_{K}\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{\phi}\Pi_{0}^{K}{\rm div}~\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\intercal}\,\mathrm{d}x=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{0*}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{H}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{0*}

will be elementwise assembled.

On the other hand, for the pure traction problem, the discrete admissible function must satisfy the constraints

\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

To impose these constraints, we introduce Lagrange multipliers
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the augmented variational formulation:
Find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})\in\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{cases}a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\beta_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h,1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\beta_{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h,2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\beta_{3}\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x&=\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rangle,\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h},\\
\mu_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{h,1}\,\mathrm{d}s&=0,\qquad\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mu_{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}_{h,2}\,\mathrm{d}s&=0,\qquad\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mu_{3}\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x&=0,\qquad\mu_{3}\in\mathbb{R},\\
\end{cases}

(6.1)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{h,1},\boldsymbol{u}_{h,2})^{\intercal}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{h,1},\boldsymbol{v}_{h,2})^{\intercal}

and

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)~|~\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{\widetilde{K}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}_{1}(\widetilde{K}),\quad\widetilde{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\Big\}.

Let
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,N
be the nodal basis function of
\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
,
where
N
is the dimension of
\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h}
.
Then we can write

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}=:\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}).

Plug the above expression into (
6.1
), and take
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j}
for
j=1,\cdots,N
.
We have

\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}a_{h}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j})\,\chi_{i}+\beta_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\beta_{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\beta_{3}\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x&=0,\quad j=1,2,\cdots,N,\\
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,1}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\chi_{i}&=0,\\
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\chi_{i}&=0,\\
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\chi_{i}&=0.\end{cases}

Let

\boldsymbol{A}=\Big(a_{h}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i})\Big)_{N\times N},\quad\boldsymbol{d}_{1}=\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,1}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)_{N\times 1},\quad\boldsymbol{d}_{2}=\Big(\int_{\partial\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)_{N\times 1},\quad\boldsymbol{d}_{3}=\Big(\int_{\Omega}{\rm rot}~\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)_{N\times 1}

and

\boldsymbol{f}=\Big(\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\rangle\Big)_{N\times 1}.

The linear system can be written in matrix form:

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}&\boldsymbol{d}_{1}&\boldsymbol{d}_{2}&\boldsymbol{d}_{3}\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}^{\intercal}&0&&\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{2}^{\intercal}&&0&\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{3}^{\intercal}&&&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\chi}\\
\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\\
\beta_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{f}\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

6.3
The mixed boundary conditions

Test 2:
We solve the following linear elasticity problem on
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
with general boundary conditions:

\begin{cases}-{\mathbf{div}}~\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{f}&\text{in}~~\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}&\text{on}~~\Gamma_{1}.\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{2}&\text{on}~~\Gamma_{2},\end{cases}

(6.2)

where,
\Gamma_{2}=\big\{(x_{1},\,x_{2})~|~0\leq{x}_{1}\leq 1,x_{2}=0\big\}
and
\Gamma_{1}=\partial\Omega\backslash\Gamma_{2}
.
We select the load term
\boldsymbol{f}
and the boundary conditions such that the analytical solution of problem
(
6.2
) (cf.
[
38
]
) is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(x_{1},\,x_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\sin{(x_{1})}\sin{(x_{2})}\\
\cos{(x_{1})}\cos{(x_{2})}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Here the first term is divergence-free, but the second term provides a constant divergence
(2/\lambda)
.
It is clear that
{\rm div~}\boldsymbol{u}=0
as
\lambda
tends to infinity.

We take
\mu=0.5
and
\lambda\in\{1,\,10^{4},\,10^{7},10^{10}\}
on Voronoi polygonal meshes
\mathcal{M}_{3}
.

\Pi_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
for
\lambda=10^{10}
are shown in Fig.
6
.

h
for different choice of
\lambda
are illustrated
in Tabs.
3
-

and Fig.
7
, respectively.

7
Conclusions

We propose a novel locking-free conforming VEM in the lowest order case for solving linear elasticity problems.
The key is to view the mesh
K
as
\widetilde{K}
which is formed by including an interior point of each edge as an additional vertex.
Then the method in
[
11
]
can be extended to the case of
k=1
, if
V_{1}(K)
is replaced by
V_{1}(\widetilde{K})
.
The parameter-free property and the optimal error estimate are established. Numerical results are consistent with theoretical findings.
