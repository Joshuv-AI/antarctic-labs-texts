Rigidly-rotating scalar fields: Between real divergence and imaginary fractalization
Victor E. Ambru s ,, Maxim N. Chernodub

Abstract
The thermodynamics of rigidly rotating systems experience divergences when the system dimensions transverse to the rotation axis exceed the critical size imposed by the causality constraint. The rotation with imaginary angular frequency, suitable for numerical lattice simulations in Euclidean imaginary-time formalism, experiences fractalization of thermodynamics in the thermodynamic limit, when the system’s pressure becomes a fractal function of the rotation frequency. Our work connects these two phenomena by studying how thermodynamics fractalizes as the system size grows. We examine an analytically-accessible system of rotating massless scalar matter on a one-dimensional ring and the numerically-treatable case of rotation in the cylindrical geometry and show how the ninionic deformation of statistics emerges in these systems. We discuss a no-go theorem on analytical continuation between real- and imaginary-rotating theories. Finally, we compute the moment of inertia and shape deformation coefficients caused by the rotation of the relativistic bosonic gas. In all cases, we show that finite-mass effects are quantitative, leaving our conclusions qualitatively unchanged.



I
Introduction

Effects of rotation on the state of physical bodies have been a subject of passionate interest throughout the decades. In metals, the uniform rotation acts on electrons via a centrifugal force that produces a slight but experimentally perceptible gradient of electric potential measured at
\sim 10^{2-3}\,\mathrm{Hz}

Beams 1968

Barnett 1915

Einstein and De Haas 1915

\sim 10^{4}\,\mathrm{Hz}

Arabgol and Sleator 2019

However, the fastest rotation of matter has been produced in noncentral collisions of relativistic heavy ions that create quark-gluon plasma in which the vorticity reaches the values
\sim 10^{22}\,\mathrm{Hz}

;
Deng and Huang 2016
;
Jiang et al. 2016
. The fast rotation affects the local properties of quark-gluon plasma, leading to various spin polarization phenomena, allowing us to probe experimentally the interior of rapidly rotating plasma in terms of its local vortical structure
Becattini and Lisa 2020
;
Huang et al. 2020
.

There are various theoretical shreds of evidence that fast rotation also affects the chiral
Chen et al. 2016

Chernodub and Gongyo 2017a
;
Chernodub and Gongyo 2017b
;

Ambrus and Winstanley 2016

Moreover, the latest first-principle simulation reveals the instability of the rigidly rotating gluon plasma below the “supervortical” critical temperature

First-principle information about the quark-gluon plasma comes from lattice simulation in the Euclidean imaginary time formalism where the real angular momentum
\Omega

The imaginary rotation differs, however, from the imaginary baryonic chemical potential: the analytical continuation to real rotation used in numerical lattice simulations has some unusual features including the emergence of (stable) ghost-like excitations

In our work, we discuss the effect of rotation on the thermodynamics of the simplest possible system represented by the massless scalar fields. This limit is convenient as it allows many of the calculations to be performed analytically. We explore the effect of finite mass in all considered setups and confirm that all features remain qualitatively identical to those in the massless case.

First, we briefly introduce the real and imaginary rotation in Sec.

We show the advantages of both discussed approaches in Sec.

Furthermore, we reveal the analytical fractalization of the thermodynamics numerically in the three-dimensional rotating gas and explicitly show strong parallels with the fractalization of thermodynamics in the analytically-accessible one-dimensional ring. For simplicity, we implement the boundary using Dirichlet boundary conditions

II
Imaginary rotation and statistics

II.1
Real rotation and imaginary rotation

Let us consider a quantum-mechanical system of bosonic particles rotating uniformly (rigidly, as a solid body) with the constant angular frequency
\Omega
about the
z
axis. For simplicity, we can assume that the system of particles is rotating inside a cylinder possessing reflective boundary conditions, such as Dirichlet boundary conditions
Duffy and Ottewill 2003
. In the co-rotating frame, the free energy of the system takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\frac{V}{\beta}\sum\hskip-14.22636pt\int\limits_{\alpha,m}\hskip 5.69054pt\ln\left(1{-}e^{-\beta(\omega_{\alpha,m}+m\Omega)}\right),

(1)

where
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature,
V
is the volume of the system,
\omega_{\alpha,m}
is the energy spectrum of the particles in the laboratory reference frame, and
\alpha
is a collective notation of quantum numbers other than the projection of angular momentum,
m\equiv m_{z}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We work with zero-charge systems so that the chemical potential does not enter the free energy of the system (
1
). We also ignore the zero-point contribution associated with the vacuum Casimir energy since it does not affect the thermodynamics of the system.

In order to determine the thermodynamic characteristics (for example, energy, pressure, entropy, angular momentum, moment of inertia, etc), it is sufficient to evaluate the statistical integral (
1
). For bosonic particles, the contribution of each quantum level to the thermodynamic quantities is given by the Bose-Einstein distribution

\displaystyle n^{({\mathrm{bos}})}_{\omega}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}\,,\qquad\mbox{[bosonic statistics]},

(2)

where
\omega
is the energy of the quantum level. In a rigidly rotating system, the statistical weight is determined by the energy in the co-rotating reference frame:

\displaystyle\omega={\tilde{\omega}}_{\alpha,m}\equiv\omega_{\alpha,m}-m\Omega\,,

(3)

thus demonstrating explicitly how rotation with
\Omega\neq 0
affects the statistical particle distribution.

It is convenient to calculate the thermodynamic properties of rotating particles using the imaginary-time formalism in which the time coordinate is turned to a complex variable via the Wick transformation,
t\to\tau=it
. The imaginary time
\tau
is compactified to a circle of length
\beta=1/T
related to thermal equilibrium temperature
T
. The compactification imposes the matching conditions on the fields: all scalar fields
\phi
are periodic functions along the thermal direction,

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x},\tau)=\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\tau+\beta\right)\,,

(4)

while all fermionic fields (not considered in this article) obey anti-periodic boundary conditions.
The Bose-Einstein statistical distribution (
2
) for bosonic fields can be recovered automatically from the periodic boundary conditions (
4
)
Kapusta and Gale 2006
.

The imaginary-time approach is intensively used in numerical lattice simulations of quantum field theories where the partition function is formulated in terms of a statistical integral in Euclidean spacetime
Rothe 2012
. The lattice simulations are especially useful for obtaining information about non-perturbative effects that cannot be treated with standard perturbative methods
Rothe 2012
.

However, the imaginary-time techniques cannot be directly applied to rotating systems because the action of the Euclidean theory becomes a complex quantity at a nonzero angular frequency,
\Omega\neq 0
, thus exhibiting the so-called “sign problem”
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
. The latter property does not allow us to treat the partition function of a rotating system as a statistical integral, bringing us to an inconvenient similarity with finite-density systems, where the (baryonic) chemical potential also makes the Euclidean action a complex quantity
Rothe 2012
. The only practical way to avoid the sign problem for rotation is to consider the angular frequency as a purely imaginary variable:

\displaystyle\Omega=i\Omega_{I}\,.

(5)

The shift of the angular frequency to the complex plane (
5
) restores the real-valuedness of the Euclidean action
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
;
Braguta et al. 2020
. Having calculated the desired quantities at a set of imaginary
\Omega_{I}
, one can then apply an analytical continuation to map the results back to the realistic case of real rotation
Braguta et al. 2020
;
Braguta et al. 2021
. This prescription, applied to the angular frequency
\Omega
, follows a standard set of practices invoked to avoid the sign problem in simulations of finite-density systems
de Forcrand 2009
.

In the context of the imaginary-time formalism, there are two methods how one can implement the imaginary rotation (
5
). The first approach, originally proposed in Ref.
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
and adopted in various numerical Monte Carlo simulations of (quark-) gluon plasmas
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
;
Braguta et al. 2021
;
Chernodub et al. 2022
;
Braguta et al. 2023
, consists in (i) considering the system in a non-inertial co-rotating reference frame in Minkowski spacetime; (ii) turning the system, via a Wick transformation, to the curved Euclidean spacetime with a complex metric tensor; (iii) implementing the substitution (
5
) which makes the metric tensor real-valued again; (iv) simulating the thermodynamics at a set of non-zero
\Omega_{I}
with the standard periodic boundary conditions (
4
); (v) fitting the obtained numerical results by a reasonable analytical function and, finally, (vi) making an analytical continuation of the lattice results to the real-valued frequency by setting

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}_{I}\to-\Omega^{2}\,.

(6)

The second approach implements the imaginary rotation in the imaginary-time formalism in a more straightforward way using the property that the imaginary frequency
\Omega_{I}
corresponds, after all, to a uniform rotation of a subspace of a timeslice of the Euclidean spacetime about a certain fixed axis
Chernodub 2021a
;
Chen et al. 2022
. As the imaginary time variable
\tau
advances for a full period from
\tau=0
to
\tau=\beta
, the system experiences a spatial rotation by the angle:

\displaystyle\chi=\beta\Omega_{I}\equiv 2\pi\nu\,,\qquad\nu=\frac{\beta\Omega_{I}}{2\pi}\,.

(7)

The turn of the space necessitates a modification of the standard bosonic boundary conditions (
4
) which should now incorporate a translation in imaginary time with the uniform rotation of the Euclidean spacetime.

Under the imaginary rotation, the bosonic wavefunction appears to satisfy the rotwisted boundary condition:

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x},\tau)=\phi\left({\hat{R}}_{{\bm{\chi}}}\mathbf{x},\tau+\beta\right)\,,

(8)

where the
3\times 3
matrix

\displaystyle{\hat{R}}_{{\bm{\chi}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\cos\chi&\sin\chi&0\\
-\sin\chi&\cos\chi&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(9)

written in Cartesian coordinates, corresponds to the global rotation of the whole spatial Euclidean subspace,
\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}^{\prime}={\hat{R}}_{{\bm{\chi}}}\mathbf{x}
, by the angle (
7
) around the
z
axis. In the absence of rotation, the transformation (
9
) becomes a unit matrix and the boundary condition (
8
) reduces to the standard periodic condition for bosons (
4
). The rotwisted boundary conditions, visualized in Fig.
1
, have already been discussed in the context of the Euclidean lattice simulations of field theories
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chernodub et al. 2022
.

Figure 1:
The rotwisted boundary conditions (
8
) characterized by the statistical angle (
7
) produced by the imaginary angular velocity
\Omega_{I}
.

The boundary conditions (
8
) are obviously invariant under
2\pi
shifts of
\chi
, or equivalently, shifts by one unit in
\nu
:

\displaystyle\chi\to\chi+2\pi\,,\qquad\nu\to\nu+1\,,

(10)

and, in the parity-unbroken systems, under the reversal of the rotation angle:

\displaystyle\chi\to-\chi\,,\qquad\nu\to-\nu\,.

(11)

The latter condition holds for a system of neutral particles that we consider.
The symmetry under clockwise and counterclockwise rotations (
11
) can be broken, for example, for charged particles subjected to a background magnetic field which leads, in particular, to a rotation diode effect in semiconductors
Chernodub 2021b
.

II.2
Imaginary rotation and ninionic statistics

The differences between the two implementations of the imaginary rotation are two-fold: one can either consider the curved Euclidean spacetime with corotating coordinates and ordinary boundary conditions (
4
) implemented along the compactified time (the first approach) or use Cartesian coordinates with the rotwisted boundary conditions (
8
) following the second approach. In our article, we consider field theories subjected to imaginary rotation introduced via the rotwisted boundary condition.

The boundary conditions imposed on the fields in the imaginary time direction have one-to-one correspondence with the statistical distribution of the particles. For example, the equal-time commutation relations for bosonic fields imply the periodic boundary conditions (
4
), which lead to the Bose-Einstein distribution for bosons (
2
). Analogously, anti-commuting fermionic variables possess anti-periodic conditions in the compactified time direction which correspond to the Fermi-Dirac statistics
Kapusta and Gale 2006
. Therefore, it is appropriate to ask which statistical distribution corresponds to the rotwisted boundary conditions (
8
)?

It turns out that the imaginary rotation deforms the statistical distribution of fermions and bosons, leading to a “ninionic” deformation which matches neither the bosonic nor the fermionic statistical distributions
Chernodub 2022b
. For example, for bosons, the ninionic deformation takes the following form:

\displaystyle n^{({\mathrm{nin}})}_{\omega}(\xi)=\frac{e^{\beta\omega}\cos\xi-1}{1-2e^{\beta\omega}\cos\xi+e^{2\beta\omega}}\,,

(12)

where
\omega\equiv\omega_{\alpha,m}
is associated with the energy of the quantum state in the laboratory reference frame and
\xi=m\chi=2\pi m\nu
is the deformation parameter associated with the “statistical angle”
\chi
, Eq. (
7
). The latter depends on the angular velocity
\Omega_{I}
of the imaginary rotation in Euclidean spacetime. Notice that at the zero (modulo
2\pi
) statistical angle, the ninionic deformation (
12
) of the bosonic distribution (
2
) disappears:
n^{(\mathrm{nin})}_{\omega}(\xi=2\pi k)=n^{(b)}_{\omega}
with an integer
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The ninionic deformation (
12
) can be understood as the real part of the bosonic occupation number (
2
),

\displaystyle n^{({\mathrm{nin}})}_{\omega}(\xi)={\mathrm{Re}}\,n^{({\mathrm{bos}})}_{\omega+i\xi/\beta}\,,

(13)

at an imaginary chemical potential
\mu=\xi/\beta
.

Given the unusual form of the ninionic deformation of the bosonic statistical distribution (
12
), it is appropriate to ask how this deformation modifies the statistical properties of the thermal state? What are the consequences which are brought to the theory by the introduction of the new dimensionless parameter, the statistical angle (
7
)? The answer to this question, which depends on the volume of the rotating system, is one of the aims of our article.

In respect of the causality, the rigid rotation with real-valued angular velocity is a well-defined notion only for transversely-bounded systems. On the contrary, the imaginary rotation does not impose any bounds on the size of the system due to the absence of the notion of the light cone in the Euclidean space (in other words, there is no causality constraint in the imaginary time formalism because it has no notion of real time). Therefore, the imaginary rotation does not lead to causality problems
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
and can be formulated in the thermodynamic limit in the whole Euclidean space
Chen et al. 2022
. The relation between imaginary and real rotation in terms of the analytical continuation is another aim of our paper.

II.3
Ninionic statistics and fractal thermodynamics

Sticking to an infinite-volume system, one can show, both in the scope of a classical interacting field theory
Chernodub 2022a
as well as in a free bosonic quantum field theory
Chernodub 2022b
, that the imaginary rotation characterized by the nonvanishing value of the statistical angle
\chi
modifies the relation between the physical temperature
T\rightarrow T_{I}(\beta,\chi)\equiv T(\beta,i\beta\Omega_{I})
and the length of the compactified direction
\beta
:
1
1

1

Throughout this paper, we shall use the subscript (or superscript)
I
to indicate when an observable is computed under imaginary rotation.

\displaystyle T_{I}(\beta,\chi)=\frac{1}{\beta}f_{\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{\chi}{2\pi}\right)\,,

(14)

where

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{T}}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{1}{q}&\mathrm{if}\ x=\frac{p}{q}\in{\mathbb{Q}},\ \mathrm{with}\ p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \mathrm{coprimes},\\[5.69054pt]
0&\mathrm{if}\ x\notin{\mathbb{Q}}\,,\end{array}\right.

is the Thomae function. In other words, function (
II.3
) gives zero for all irrational numbers and equals to a nonzero number
1/q
determined by the denominator
q
of the rational argument
x=p/q\in{\mathbb{Q}}
with two natural coprime numbers
p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Figure 2:
Thomae function (
II.3
).

The Thomae function (
II.3
), shown in Fig.
2
, is known also under other names, such as the raindrop function, the modified Dirichlet function, the popcorn function, etc. This function has the amazing counter-intuitive property stating that the function is discontinuous if its argument
x
is rational, and it is continuous provided
x
is irrational. The Thomae function emerges naturally in various physical systems such as one-dimensional disordered crystals, Hubbard model of particles on a ring, phyllotaxis problem, and fractional quantum Hall effect (we refer to Ref.
Flack et al. 2023
for a comprehensive discussion). The Thomae function (
II.3
) possesses a nontrivial fractal structure
Flack et al. 2023
;
Devaney 1999
;
Nechaev and Polovnikov 2017
which equips the thermodynamics of imaginary rotation with fractal properties. The fractalization (and “defractalization”) of thermodynamics of imaginary rotating systems will also be addressed in this paper.

Notice that the behavior of the physical temperature (
14
) as a function of the statistical angle (
7
) is determined solely by the denominator
q
of the rational number
\chi/(2\pi)
and not by its numerator. Irrational (in units of
2\pi/\beta
) frequencies correspond to zero temperature (
14
).

In the absence of the imaginary rotation,
\chi=0
, one gets the standard relation between temperature
T
and the length of the imaginary time direction
\beta
, as expected:

\displaystyle T(\beta,0)=\frac{1}{\beta}\,.

(17)

The ninionic deformation of bosonic statistics can be readily understood in the imaginary time formalism for free massless bosons in the thermodynamic limit (on an infinite spatial line) where the particles possess the linear energy dispersion,
\omega_{k}=|k|
. In this conformal system, the thermal pressure of bosons
P
is equal to their energy density,
E\equiv P
, taking a well-known expression in the absence of imaginary rotation:

\displaystyle P_{0}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi}n^{(\mathrm{bos})}_{\omega_{k}}\,\omega_{k}=\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}}\,,\qquad[\Omega_{I}=0]\,.

(18)

The temperature of the system is given by the inverse length
\beta
of the compactified imaginary-time direction (
17
).

Looking ahead a little, one can also discuss the thermodynamics of the same system compactified into a ring of an infinitely large radius
R
which is subjected to the imaginary rotation with the angular velocity
\Omega_{I}
. The pressure can be derived via the ninionic statistics (
13
):

\displaystyle P_{I}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}n^{(\mathrm{nin})}_{\omega_{m}}(\xi_{m})\,\omega_{m}=\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}}f^{2}_{\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{\beta\Omega_{I}}{2\pi}\right)\,,

(19)

where the ninionic parameter
\xi_{m}=\chi m\equiv\beta\Omega_{I}m
is expressed via the angular momentum
m
and the statistical angle (
7
). The energy spectrum in the statistical sum (
19
),

\displaystyle\omega_{m}=\frac{1}{R}|m|\,,

(20)

corresponds to the laboratory frame. In the thermodynamic limit,
R\to\infty
, the energy gaps of the discrete spectrum (
20
) shrink, the variable
m/R
becomes the continuum momentum
k
, and the sum in Eq. (
19
) reduces to an integral thus bridging the gap between the exotic (
19
) and standard (
18
) statistical sums in the thermodynamic limit.

However, the presence of the imaginary rotation
\Omega_{I}
makes the system nontrivial even in the thermodynamic limit. Indeed, the pressure of the imaginary rotating system (
19
) has the same expression as the pressure of the non-rotating one (
18
) with only one important difference that the temperature of the former (
14
) becomes a fractal function (
II.3
) of the imaginary angular frequency
\Omega_{I}
. In the next section, we discuss the particularities of fractalization of thermodynamics by imaginary rotation working with an analytically-solvable example of a free massless particle confined to a one-dimensional ring.

III
Real and imaginary rotations

on the ring

III.1
Classical (non-quantum) system

Let us consider an ensemble of bosonic massless particles, constrained to move on a ring of fixed radius
R
with angle coordinates
\varphi
. Imposing that the system rotates with angular velocity
\Omega
, its free energy at inverse temperature
\beta
reads

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{\varphi}}{2\pi}\ln[1-e^{-\beta(k^{0}-\Omega J^{z})}],

(21)

where
J^{z}=-k_{\varphi}
represents the angular momentum of a particle spinning with azimuthal momentum
k^{\varphi}=-k_{\varphi}/R^{2}
, satisfying the dispersion relation
k^{0}=|k_{\varphi}/R|=|Rk^{\varphi}|>0
. The above integral can be readily evaluated,

\mathcal{F}=-\frac{\pi^{2}R}{3\beta^{2}}\Gamma^{2}(R),\quad\Gamma(R)=(1-\Omega^{2}R^{2})^{-1/2}.

(22)

Other thermodynamic quantities such as total entropy
\mathcal{S}
, thermodynamic pressure
\mathcal{P}
, and total angular momentum
\mathcal{M}
, can be obtained starting from the relation
Landau and Lifshitz 1996

d\mathcal{F}=\beta^{-2}\mathcal{S}d\beta-\mathcal{P}dV-\mathcal{M}d\Omega,

(23)

where
V=2\pi R
is the “volume” of the ring.
Denoting by an overhead bar volume-averaged quantities,
{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\equiv\mathcal{A}/V
, we have

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}=\beta^{2}\frac{\partial{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}}{\partial\beta},\quad{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}=-\frac{\partial{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}}{\partial\Omega},\quad\mathcal{P}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial R},

(24)

while the average energy
{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}
can be found from the Euler relation,

{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}={\overline{\mathcal{F}}}+\beta^{-1}{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}+\Omega{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}.

(25)

Taking into account the thermodynamic relations in Eq. (
24
), the above relation can be recast as

{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}=\left(\frac{\partial(\beta{\overline{\mathcal{F}}})}{\partial\beta}\right)_{\beta\Omega}.

(26)

Starting from Eq. (
22
), it is easy to derive

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi\Gamma^{2}(R)}{6\beta^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\Gamma^{2}(R)}{6\beta^{2}}[2\Gamma^{2}(R)-1],

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\Gamma^{2}(R)}{3\beta},

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\Omega R^{2}}{3\beta^{2}}\Gamma^{4}(R),

(27)

while
{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}=\mathcal{P}
for this one-dimensional system. It can be checked that
{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}
agrees with the energy density
E
computed using the well-known formula

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{Rdk^{\varphi}}{2\pi}\frac{k^{0}}{e^{\beta(k^{0}-R^{2}\Omega k^{\varphi})}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\Gamma^{2}(R)}{6\beta^{2}}[2\Gamma^{2}(R)-1]\equiv{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}.

(28)

Considering now the free energy at small values of the rotation parameter
\Omega
, we expand the free energy density in a power series in the velocity of a corotating particle

v_{R}=\Omega R\,,

(29)

at the system boundary
\rho=R
, following Ref.

{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}={\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(0)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{v_{R}^{2n}}{(2n)!}K_{2n},\quad K_{2n}=\left.\frac{1}{{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(0)}\frac{\partial^{2n}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}}{\partial v_{R}^{2n}}\right|_{\Omega=0},

where

are

Clearly,

and the

The results for the coefficients

of a one-component bosonic gas evaluates to

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\Omega\rightarrow 0}I(\Omega)=-\lim_{\Omega\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\partial{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}}{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=-{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(0)R^{2}K_{2}=\frac{\pi R^{2}}{3\beta^{2}},

(32)

which follows from the definition
{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}(\Omega)=I(\Omega)\Omega
of the moment of inertia
I(\Omega)
in terms of the average angular momentum
{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}
, obtained via the thermodynamic relation (

It is interesting to notice that recent first-principle simulations

consistent with our estimation (

\displaystyle K_{2}=2\,,\qquad\ \text{[per one bosonic d.o.f.]}\,.

(33)

This result is not unexpected since at sufficiently high temperatures, the gluon plasma becomes a weakly-interacting gas of gluons. It is worth mentioning that the value of
K_{2}
receives corrections only if the quanta are massive or if interactions are taken into account.

The next non-zero coefficient in the series (

\displaystyle K_{4}=24\,,\qquad\ \text{[per one bosonic d.o.f.]}\,.

(34)

corresponds to the correction to the free energy caused by the deformation of the rotating gas due to rotation. This correction also affects the moment of inertia,
I(\Omega)=I_{0}+I_{2}v_{R}^{2}/2+\dots
, with the universal non-interacting coefficient
I_{2}/I_{0}=4
.

We now consider the effect of the particle mass on the

coefficients. To this end, we evaluate
\mathcal{F}
from Eq. (

k^{0}=\sqrt{k_{\varphi}^{2}/R^{2}+M^{2}}
, with

\mathcal{F}=\frac{2R\gamma}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\ln(1-e^{-\beta k^{0}/\gamma}).

(35)

Changing the integration variable to
z=k^{0}/m
and expanding the logarithm using
\ln(1-x)=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x^{j}/j
, we arrive at

\mathcal{F}(\Omega,M)=-\frac{2R\gamma M}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}K_{1}\left(\frac{j\beta M}{\gamma}\right).

(36)

We can therefore evaluate the coefficients as

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(0,M)

\displaystyle=-\frac{M}{\pi\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}K_{1}(jM\beta),

\displaystyle\overline{K}_{2}(M)

\displaystyle=-\frac{M^{2}}{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}K_{2}(jM\beta),

\displaystyle\overline{K}_{4}(M)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3M^{2}}{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}[jM\beta K_{1}(jM\beta)+4K_{2}(jM\beta)],

(37)

where we introduced the notation
\overline{K}_{2n}(M)\equiv{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(0,M)K_{2n}(M)

As shown in Fig.

, while the shape coefficients

and

III.2
Relativistic rotation, particle spectrum

In this section, we consider a free massless particle on a ring of a fixed radius
R
with the angle coordinate
\varphi
as shown in Fig.
4
. For a static ring, the particle wavefunction is described by the Klein-Gordon equation:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}\right)\Phi(t,\varphi)=0\,,

(38)

which is formulated in the inertial, laboratory frame.

Figure 4:
Illustration of a particle on a ring of the radius
R
and the angular coordinate
\varphi
. The ring rotates with the angular velocity
\Omega
counterclockwise.

Let us consider the ring rotating with the constant angular velocity
\Omega
. The coordinates associated with the co-rotating reference frame (denoted by a tilde) are related to the laboratory coordinates as follows:

\displaystyle t={\tilde{t}}\,,\qquad{\tilde{\varphi}}=\varphi-\Omega t\ \ \mbox{mod}\ 2\pi\,.

(39)

In the co-rotating frame, the Klein-Gordon equation (
38
) transforms into the following equation:

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{t}}-\Omega\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\varphi}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\tilde{\varphi}^{2}}\right]\Phi(\tilde{t},\tilde{\varphi})=0\,,

(40)

which possesses the energy spectrum in the rotating reference frame:

\displaystyle{\tilde{\omega}}_{m}=\frac{1}{R}|m|-\Omega m\,,\qquad m\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

(41)

corresponding to the following eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\Phi(\tilde{t},\tilde{\varphi})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\omega\pi R}}e^{-i{\tilde{\omega}}\tilde{t}+im\tilde{\varphi}}\,.

(42)

The energy spectrum (
41
) is bounded from below provided the causality condition is satisfied:

\displaystyle R|\Omega|<1\,.

(43)

The thermodynamics of the system is determined in the rotating reference frame where all statistical distributions are set by the energy in the co-rotating frame
{\tilde{\omega}}_{m}
rather than by its laboratory-frame counterpart (
20
).

III.3
Free energy of rotating scalar field

We consider statistical mechanics of scalar particles in the rotating environment. The corresponding statistical sum,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}\equiv e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{n_{m}=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta(\omega_{m}-\Omega m)n_{m}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left[1-e^{-\beta(\omega_{m}-\Omega m)}\right]^{-1}\,,

(44)

is formulated via the sum over states labeled by the angular momentum
m
with the occupation number
n_{m}
of system’s levels that possess the total energy
{\tilde{E}}_{m,n_{m}}={\tilde{\omega}}_{m}n_{m}
in the rotating reference frame and the total angular momentum
L_{n,m_{n}}=mn_{m}
. In Eq. (
44
),
\mathcal{F}
stands for the free energy in the co-rotating reference frame. In the statistical sum, we do not take into account a
m=0
contribution which corresponds to the zero-energy mode and contributes to the zero-point (Casimir) vacuum energy
Milton 2001
. We concentrate on the thermal part of the free energy which possesses interesting fractal properties in the thermodynamic limit.

The thermodynamic free energy (
44
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\ln

\displaystyle\Bigl[\Bigr(1-e^{-\beta(1/R-\Omega)m}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\cdot\Bigl(1-e^{-\beta(1/R+\Omega)m}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,,

(45)

can be evaluated explicitly:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{12R}+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln

\displaystyle\biggl\{\eta\left[\frac{i\beta}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{R}-\Omega\right)\right]

\displaystyle\cdot\eta\left[\frac{i\beta}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{R}+\Omega\right)\right]\biggr\},

(46)

via the Dedekind
\eta
function:

\displaystyle\eta(z)=e^{\frac{i\pi z}{12}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{2\pi inz}\right)\,.

(47)

Notice that the Dedekind function (
47
) is defined only in the upper complex plane
{\mathrm{Im}}\,z>0
, which implies that the free energy (
46
) is well-defined if and only if the causality condition (
43
) is satisfied. The causality condition is absent for the case of the imaginary rotation (
5
), when the angular frequency
\Omega

becomes a purely imaginary quantity. Indeed, according to the analytical properties of the Dedekind function (
47
), the free energy (
46
) is a well-defined analytical function for any real value of the imaginary angular frequency
\Omega_{I}
at any radius of the ring
R
. Consequently, the rigid imaginary rotation, contrary to rotation in Minkowski spacetime, can be formulated in the thermodynamic limit.

For convenience of our subsequent analysis, we consider all physical quantities in units of the inverse length
1/\beta
of the imaginary time direction. We introduce the dimensionless length of the ring (the one-dimensional volume)
L
and the frequency of rotation
\nu
, respectively:

\displaystyle L=\frac{2\pi R}{\beta}\,,\qquad\nu=\frac{\beta\Omega_{I}}{2\pi}\equiv\frac{\chi}{2\pi}\,.

(48)

The normalized frequency
\nu
corresponds to the normalized statistical angle (
7
).

The free energy density
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}=\mathcal{F}_{I}/2\pi R
is given by

{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}=\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}L}\ln\left[\eta\left(\frac{i}{L}-\nu\right)\eta\left(\frac{i}{L}+\nu\right)\right].

(49)

After taking the thermodynamic limit
R\rightarrow\infty
and in the absence of rotation,
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}\rightarrow{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{0}=-P_{0}=-\pi/6\beta^{2}
, as established by Eq. (
18
). Note that
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{0}
agrees with the classical expression
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(0)
in Eq. (
31
).

The first term in Eq. (
49
) could be erroneously mistaken for the regularized zero-point (Casimir) energy contribution to the free energy. To show that this identification is not correct, let us consider the trivial case
\nu=0
which corresponds to a vanishing statistical angle,
\chi=0
(nontrivial angles will be considered shortly after). The low-temperature limit,
\beta\to\infty
, for a ring with a fixed radius
R
corresponds to a vanishing parameter
L
. Using the following relation, valid for vanishingly-small positive
L
,

\displaystyle\ln\eta\left(\frac{i}{L}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{12L}+\dots,

(50)

(where the ellipsis denote subleading terms in the limit
L\to 0
),
one gets from Eq. (
49
) that the normalized free energy vanishes in the low-temperature limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}=0.

(51)

For the sake of convenience, we present here the expressions for the normalized Casimir free energy and the Casimir pressure,
respectively:

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{\mathrm{Cas}}=-P_{\rm Cas}=\frac{1}{24\pi R^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}L^{2}},

(52)

which coincides with the first term in Eq. (
49
). The thermodynamic contribution (
49
) does not contain the zero-point energy since the latter should diverge in the
L\to 0
limit (
52
), which is not the case (
51
). Therefore, Eq. (
49
) represents a purely thermodynamic contribution.

III.4
Fractalization of thermodynamics

Using the property
\eta(-z^{*})=[\eta(z)]^{*}
valid for any complex number
z
from the upper complex semi-plane,
{\mathrm{Im}}\,z>0
, one gets for the thermodynamic part of the free energy density (
49

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}=\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}L^{2}}+\frac{2}{\beta^{2}L}\ln\left|\eta\left(\nu+\frac{i}{L}\right)\right|\,,

(53)

where we used the notations in (
48

The thermodynamic limit,
L\to\infty

53

, which relates the Dedekind
\eta
function (

function (
II.3
) as the following limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to+0}\epsilon\,|\eta(x+i\epsilon)|=-\frac{\pi}{12}f_{\mathsf{T}}^{2}(x)\,.

(54)

Applying (
54
) to the thermal part of the free energy density (
53
), we get that the (normalized) thermodynamic energy density “fractalizes” in the thermodynamic limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}=-P_{I}=-\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}}f_{\mathsf{T}}^{2}(\nu).

(55)

The non-analyticity of the Thomae function
f_{\mathsf{T}}
has a fractal nature
Nechaev and Polovnikov 2017
implying the fractalization of thermodynamics under imaginary rotation
Chernodub 2022b
. The result in Eq. (
55
) implies that close to the thermodynamic limit,
L\gg 1
, the non-analytical fractal part of the free energy density dominates over the analytical term in the thermodynamic free energy (
53
).
Interpreting the expression for
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}
in light of Eq. (
18
), we are led to define a rotation-dependent temperature

via

\displaystyle T_{\mathsf{T}}(\nu)=\beta^{-1}f_{\mathsf{T}}(\nu),

(56)

which depends on the statistical parameter
\nu\equiv\beta\Omega_{I}/2\pi=p/q
via the discontinuous Thomae function

as given in Eq. (
14
). Notice that in the thermodynamic limit, the thermodynamics of the system is determined by the ninionic statistics (
12
). This fact can be seen from the expression for pressure, given in Eq. (
55

19

The fractalization (
55

R
of the ring becomes infinitely large. At any finite

, all thermodynamic characteristics of the systems are analytical. Thus, it is instructive to see how thermodynamics acquires its fractal properties under imaginary rotation as the radius of the ring increases.

To see how the other thermodynamic quantities fractalize in the imaginary rotation case, it is convenient to rewrite the free energy in Eq. (

Employing a series expansion of the Bose-Einstein factor,

(58)

Switching now to imaginary rotation,

\mathcal{F}_{I}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\frac{e^{-\pi j/L}\sinh(\pi j/L)-\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{\sinh^{2}(\pi j/L)+\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}.

(59)

The pressure and average angular momentum can be derived from Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\beta^{2}L^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sinh^{2}(\pi j/L)-\cosh(2\pi j/L)\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{[\sinh^{2}(\pi j/L)+\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)]^{2}},

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\beta L}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sinh(2\pi j/L)\sin(\pi j\nu)\cos(\pi j\nu)}{[\sinh^{2}(\pi j/L)+\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)]^{2}},

where we introduced
{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}_{I}=-i{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}=\partial{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I}/\partial\Omega_{I}
, while
{\overline{\mathcal{S}}}_{I}=\beta(\mathcal{P}_{I}-{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{I})+2\pi\nu{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}_{I}

25

{\overline{\mathcal{E}}}_{I}=\mathcal{P}_{I}
.

To reveal the fractal features of the expressions in Eqs. (

Out of the new range for

In what follows, we split our observables

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{I}_{q}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}Lq}\sum_{Q=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{Q}\frac{\exp(-\pi qQ/L)}{\sinh(\pi qQ/L)}\rightarrow-\frac{\pi}{6\beta^{2}q^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{I}_{q}

Finally, we evaluate the

III.5
Analytical continuation: the disk of analyticity

Finally, let us discuss how the fractalization of thermodynamics in the thermodynamic limit leads to the absence of the analytical continuation from the imaginary angular frequencies to the real ones. In other words, we would like to see that the thermodynamic quantities obtained in an infinite volume limit at imaginary rotation cannot be directly connected to the thermodynamics of real rotation. Qualitatively, the validity of this statement can be deduced from both mathematical and physical arguments.

Mathematically, it is clear that a non-analytical function cannot be analytically continued to an analytical domain because the result will depend on the prescription used for the continuation procedure. Moreover, at the imaginary-rotating side in the thermodynamic limit, the pressure
P
cannot be expressed as a function of the imaginary velocity squared,
\Omega_{I}^{2}
, Eq. (
19
), which renders inapplicable the continuation prescription to the real rotation summarized in Eq. (
6
).

Physically, the causality condition (
43
) is incompatible with the continuation prescription (
6
) in the thermodynamic limit,
R\to\infty
, for any finite
\Omega_{I}
. However, outside of the thermodynamic limit at any finite
R
, the analytical continuation does exist. Let us briefly discuss this point for the example of the ring.

Since the length of the ring is always a positive number,
L>0
, the argument of the Dedekind
\eta
function in the free energy density (
53
) always belongs to the upper part of the complex plane, where the Dedekind function is an analytical and well-defined function. Therefore, the imaginary rotation is well-defined at any imaginary angular frequency
\nu
, contrary to its real counterpart (
46
).

It is convenient to write explicitly the normalized free energies
\beta^{2}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}
for real (
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{\,\mathrm{Re}}
) and imaginary (
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{\,\mathrm{Im}}
) angular frequencies, respectively:

\displaystyle\beta^{2}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{\,\mathrm{Re}}(x_{R},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi y^{2}}{6}+y\,\ln\Bigl[\eta\left(ix_{R}+iy\right)\eta\left(-ix_{R}+iy\right)\bigr],

(83)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{\,\mathrm{Im}}(x_{I},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi y^{2}}{6}+y\,\ln\bigl[\eta\left(x_{I}+iy\right)\eta\left(-x_{I}+iy\right)\bigr].

(84)

Here we defined the following real-valued quantities:

\displaystyle x_{R}=\frac{\beta\Omega}{2\pi},\qquad x_{I}=\frac{\beta\Omega_{I}}{2\pi},\qquad y=\frac{\beta}{2\pi R}\,,

(85)

which represent the normalized angular frequencies for real and imaginary rotation (
x_{R}
and
x_{I}
, respectively), and the inverse size of the ring
y>0
. The analytical continuation can be formulated in terms of the relation between (
83
) and (
84

Despite similarity of Eqs. (
83
) and (
84
), these quantities have different properties. The free energy for real rotation (
83
) is defined only in the strip
-y<x_{R}<y
because the Dedekind eta function is defined only in the upper part of the complex plane (excluding the real axis). Physically, the same condition coincides with the causality requirement (
43

84

x_{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Equations (
83

84

\displaystyle\beta^{2}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(z,z_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi z_{0}^{2}}{6}+y\,\ln\Bigl[\eta\left(iz+z_{0}\right)\eta\left(-iz+z_{0}\right)\bigr]\,,

(86)

with

\displaystyle z=x_{R}+ix_{I}\equiv\frac{\beta\left(\Omega+i\Omega_{I}\right)}{2\pi},\quad z_{0}=iy\equiv\frac{i}{L}.

(87)

For any

\displaystyle\left(\Omega^{2}+\Omega_{I}^{2}\right)R^{2}<1\,.

(88)

The free energy
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}
can be written as the following series

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}(z,z_{0})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{\beta}^{(2n)}z^{2n}\,,\quad|z|<|z_{0}|\,,

(89)

with the first two coefficients in the explicit form:

\displaystyle\beta^{2}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{(0)}_{\beta}(iy)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi y^{2}}{6}+y\log\bigl(\eta^{2}(iy)\bigr)\,.

(90)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}^{(2)}_{\beta}(iy)

\displaystyle=-y\frac{\bigl(\eta^{\prime\prime}(iy)\bigr)^{2}\eta(iy)-\bigl(\eta^{\prime}(iy)\bigr)^{2}}{\eta^{2}(iy)}\,.

(91)

One can check explicitly that the coefficients of the series (
89

88

One can also rewrite the free energy (

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}({\mathit{\Omega}},R)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}_{2n}(\beta,R){\mathit{\Omega}}^{2n},\qquad|{\mathrm{Re}}\,{\mathit{\Omega}}|R<1\,,

IV
Rigidly-rotating Bose-Einstein distribution

We now move on and consider a (3+1)d rigidly-rotating system comprised of uncharged, massless boson particles. In this section, we consider such a system from the perspective of relativistic kinetic theory, which is introduced briefly in Subsect.
IV.1
. In Subsect.
IV.2
, we discuss the thermodynamic properties of a rigidly-rotating system with real rotation parameter
\Omega
, whose properties for slow rotation are discussed in Sec.
IV.3
. Finally, Subsec.
IV.4
is dedicated to the case of imaginary rotation.

IV.1
Relativistic kinetic theory

Although throughout this article we consider non-interacting bosonic systems, it is instructive to discuss, for a brief moment, an interacting model. This approach will allow us to
elucidate thermal distributions in thermodynamic equilibrium and shed some light on the physical nature of imaginary rotating systems.

In relativistic kinetic theory, the system dynamics are described using the relativistic Boltzmann equation
de Groot et al. 1980
;
Cercignani and Kremer 2002
;
Rezzolla and Zanotti 2013
:

k^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}f_{\mathbf{k}}=C[f],

(99)

where
f_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv f_{\mathbf{k}}(x)
is the one-particle distribution function and
k^{\mu}=(k^{0},\mathbf{k})
is the on-shell momentum satisfying
k^{2}=0
. The macroscopic properties of the system can be described using the energy-momentum tensor,

T^{\mu\nu}=\int dK\,k^{\mu}k^{\nu}f_{\mathbf{k}},

(100)

where
dK=gd^{3}k/[(2\pi)^{3}k^{0}]
is the Lorentz-invariant integration measure and the degeneracy factor of a single neutral scalar field considered in this paper is
g=1
.
The conservation law
\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
demands that
k^{\mu}
be a collision invariant, i.e.

\int dKC[f]\,k^{\mu}=0.

(101)

The prototypical collision term is that corresponding to
2
-to-
2
scattering processes
de Groot et al. 1980
;
Denicol et al. 2012
,

C_{2\rightarrow 2}[f]=\frac{1}{2}\int dK^{\prime}dPdP^{\prime}W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}\\
\times(f_{\mathbf{p}}f_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}\tilde{f}_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{f}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-f_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\tilde{f}_{\mathbf{p}}\tilde{f}_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}),

(102)

where
\tilde{f}_{\mathbf{k}}=1+f_{\mathbf{k}}
is the Bose enhancement factor and the Lorentz-invariant transition rate
W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}
can be written in terms of the quantum-mechanical differential cross section
d\sigma/d\Omega
as

W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}=s\frac{d\sigma(s,\Theta_{s})}{d\Omega_{s}}(2\pi)^{6}\delta^{4}(k+k^{\prime}-p-p^{\prime}),

(103)

with
s=(k+k^{\prime})^{2}
and
\Theta_{s}
being the center of mass squared energy and emission angle, respectively, with

\cos\Theta_{s}=\frac{(k-k^{\prime})\cdot(p-p^{\prime})}{(k-k^{\prime})^{2}}.

(104)

According to the H theorem, the collision term
C[f]
drives the system towards local thermal equilibrium, described for the case of a free bosonic gas by the Bose-Einstein distribution:

f_{\mathbf{k}}^{\rm BE}=\frac{1}{\exp[u_{\mu}(x)k^{\mu}/T(x)]-1}\,,

(105)

where
T(x)
is the local temperature and
u^{\mu}(x)
is the local four-velocity. It is easy to check that
C[f]=0
when the gas is in thermal equilibrium, i.e.
f_{*}=f_{*}^{\rm BE}
for
*\in\{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\}
. In global thermal equilibrium,
f_{\mathbf{k}}=f_{\mathbf{k}}^{\rm BE}
at each space-time point and Eq. (
99
) becomes:

k^{\mu}k^{\nu}\partial_{\mu}\beta_{\nu}(x)=0,

(106)

where
\beta^{\mu}(x)=u^{\mu}(x)/T(x)
is the temperature four-vector. Thus, in global equilibrium,
\beta^{\mu}
satisfies the Killing equation,
\partial_{\mu}\beta_{\nu}+\partial_{\nu}\beta_{\mu}=0
. In this paper, we seek the solution that corresponds to rigid rotation:

\beta^{\mu}(\Omega)\partial_{\mu}=\beta(\partial_{t}-y\Omega\partial_{x}+x\Omega\partial_{y})=\beta(\partial_{t}+\Omega\partial_{\varphi}),

(107)

where
\Omega
is the angular velocity and
\beta=1/T_{0}
is a constant corresponding to the inverse temperature on the rotation axis (where
x=y=0
). The equilibrium distribution (
105
) thus reads:

f_{\mathbf{k}}^{\rm BE}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{\exp[\beta(k^{0}+\Omega k_{\varphi})]-1}\,,

(108)

where

\displaystyle k_{\varphi}=-\rho^{2}k^{\varphi}\,,\qquad k^{\varphi}=\rho^{-2}(-yk^{x}+xk^{y})\,,

(109)

with
\rho=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
being the distance from the point at
(x,y,z)
to the rotation axis.

IV.2
Thermodynamics of rigid rotation

We now discuss the properties of the rigidly-rotating global equilibrium state. From the relation
\beta^{\mu}\beta_{\mu}=1/T^{2}(x)
, we can identify the local temperature as
Cercignani and Kremer 2002

T(\rho)=\beta^{-1}\gamma(\rho),\quad\gamma(\rho)=(1-\rho^{2}\Omega^{2})^{-1/2},

(110)

where
\gamma(\rho)
is the Lorentz factor of a co-rotating particle at a distance
\rho
from the rotation axis. Relation (
110
) corresponds to the Tolman-Ehrenfest law
Tolman and Ehrenfest 1930
;
Tolman 1930
, which relates local temperature to the metric in a static gravitational field, in the curvilinear background of co-rotating reference frame.
Similarly, the local four-velocity reads

u^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=\gamma(\partial_{t}+\Omega\partial_{\varphi}).

(111)

Both the Lorentz factor and the local temperature
T(\rho)=\gamma(\rho)/\beta
diverge on the light cylinder, as
\rho\Omega\rightarrow 1
.

The energy-momentum tensor
T^{\mu\nu}(x)
can be obtained via

T^{\mu\nu}=\int dK\,k^{\mu}k^{\nu}f_{\mathbf{k}}=(E+P)u^{\mu}u^{\nu}-Pg^{\mu\nu}.

(112)

In the case of massless particles, the energy
E=3P
is expressed via the local pressure
P
, which reads

P(\rho)=\frac{1}{3}\int dK\,(k\cdot u)^{2}f^{\rm BE}_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{\pi^{2}\gamma^{4}(\rho)}{90\beta^{4}}.

(113)

We now consider the thermodynamic limit of our rigidly-rotating system. Identifying
F(\rho)=-P
as the local free-energy density, we consider the average free energy
{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}=\mathcal{F}/V

The positiveness of

V.1
Mode solutions

We consider a real, massless scalar field
\hat{\phi}

\hat{\phi}(x)=\sum_{j}[\hat{a}_{j}f_{j}(x)+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}f^{*}_{j}(x)],

\displaystyle(\Box+m^{2})f_{j}=0\,.

(148)

when we will discuss the Klein-Gordon field enclosed inside a cylindrical boundary.

V.3
Evaluation for imaginary rotation

We now seek to construct states which undergo imaginary rotation,
\Omega=i\Omega_{I}
, where
\Omega_{I}\in\mathbb{R}
. As also noted in Sec.
IV.4
,
a state under imaginary rotation leads to complex expectation values of physical observables. This problem can be alleviated by considering the hermiticized version of
\hat{\rho}
, namely

\hat{\rho}(\beta,\Omega)\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}[\hat{\rho}(\beta,\Omega)+\hat{\rho}^{\dagger}(\beta,\Omega)],

(162)

which is equivalent to averaging over the results obtained for positive and negative values of
\Omega_{I}
.
Under the above hermitization, the t.e.v. in Eq. (
156
) becomes

\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{e^{\beta\omega_{j}}\cos(\beta\Omega_{I}m_{j})-1}{e^{2\beta\omega_{j}}-2e^{\beta\omega_{j}}\cos(\beta\Omega_{I}m_{j})+1}\delta(j,j^{\prime}),

(163)

which is similar to the relativistic kinetic theory distribution
f_{\mathbf{k}}^{\rm im}
in Eq. (
125
) under the substitution
k_{\varphi}\rightarrow-m_{j}
. The thermodynamic state corresponding to the t.e.v. (
163
) is characterized by ninionic statistics (
12
). In what follows, we perform the calculations considering averages using the statistical operator
\hat{\rho}(\beta,i\Omega_{I})
, keeping in mind that the final result is obtained by taking the real part.

In order to analyse the functions
G_{abc}^{I}
, obtained by replacing
\Omega\rightarrow i\Omega_{I}
in Eq. (
157
), the Bose-Einstein factor can be expanded in a power series, as follows:

\frac{1}{e^{\beta\tilde{\omega}}-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{-j\beta\tilde{\omega}},

(164)

where
\tilde{\omega}=\omega-i\Omega_{I}m
has a positive real part,
\omega>0
.
Writing

G_{abc}^{I}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}G^{j;I}_{abc},

(165)

it can be seen that the power of
m
can be accounted for by taking derivatives with respect to the rotation parameter:

G^{j;I}_{abc}=\left(-\frac{i}{j\beta}\right)^{c}\frac{d^{c}G^{j;I}_{ab0}}{d\Omega^{c}_{I}}.

(166)

On the other hand, the sum over
m
can be performed in
G_{ab0}^{j;I}
using the summation theorem for Bessel functions [see Eq. (8.531.1) in Ref.
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2015
; and Eq. (10.23.7) in Ref.
Olver et al. 2010
]:

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-imx}J_{m}^{2}(z)=J_{0}\left(2z\sin\frac{x}{2}\right),

(167)

leading to

G_{ab0}^{j;I}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,e^{-j\beta\omega}\omega^{a}\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{dk}{2\pi^{2}}q^{b}J_{0}\left(2q\rho\sin\frac{j\beta\Omega_{I}}{2}\right)\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dxe^{-x}x^{a+b+1}}{2\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{a+b+2}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta(\cos\theta)^{b+1}J_{0}(\alpha_{j}x\cos\theta),

(168)

where
x=j\beta\omega
and
\theta
is defined by
(k,q)=\omega(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)
, while
\alpha_{j}
is given by

\alpha_{j}=\frac{2\rho}{j\beta}\sin\frac{j\beta\Omega_{I}}{2}=\frac{l}{\pi j}\sin(\pi j\nu),

(169)

with

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\rho}{\beta},\qquad\qquad\ L=\frac{2\pi R}{\beta},

(170a)

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\equiv\nu_{I}=\frac{\beta\Omega_{I}}{2\pi},\qquad\nu_{R}=\frac{\beta\Omega}{2\pi},

(170b)

where, for convenience, we reproduced Eq. (
48
) and introduced other notations to be used later (notice that
0\leqslant l\leqslant L
).

In order to perform the integral with respect to
\theta
in Eq. (
168
), we replace the Bessel function
J_{0}(x)
by its series expansion,

J_{0}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}x^{2k}}{4^{k}(k!)^{2}}\,.

(171)

The integral with respect to
\theta
can be performed now term by term using the relation (valid for
{\mathrm{Re}}\,\gamma>-1
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{\gamma}\theta d\theta=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+\gamma}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)}\,,

(172)

Using the following identities for the gamma functions,

\displaystyle\Gamma(n+1){\biggl|}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\displaystyle=n!\,,

(173)

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+n\right){\biggl|}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}n!}\,,

(174)

we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\cos\theta J_{0}(\alpha_{j}x\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\alpha_{j}^{2}x^{2})^{k}}{(2k+1)!}\,,

(175a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\cos^{3}\theta J_{0}(\alpha_{j}x\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2k)^{2}(-\alpha_{j}^{2}x^{2})^{k-1}}{(2k+1)!}\,,

(175b)

corresponding to the cases
b=0
and
2
in Eq. (
168
). The summation can be trivially performed,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\cos\theta J_{0}(\alpha_{j}x\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\alpha_{j}x)}{\alpha_{j}x},

(176a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\cos^{3}\theta J_{0}(\alpha_{j}x\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(\alpha_{j}x)}{\alpha_{j}^{2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip-35.0pt+(\alpha_{j}^{2}x^{2}-1)\frac{\sin(\alpha_{j}x)}{\alpha_{j}^{3}x^{3}},

(176b)

finally arriving at

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{n00}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\alpha_{j}(j\beta)^{n+2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dxe^{-x}x^{n}\sin(x\alpha_{j}),

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{n20}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\alpha_{j}^{3}(j\beta)^{n+4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dxe^{-x}x^{n}[x\alpha_{j}\cos(x\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle\hskip 80.0pt+(x^{2}\alpha_{j}^{2}-1)\sin(x\alpha_{j})].

(177)

Employing the identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-x+i\alpha_{j}x}x^{n}=\frac{n!}{(1-i\alpha)^{n+1}}\,,

(178)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-x}x^{n}\sin(\alpha_{j}x)

\displaystyle=n!\,{\rm Im}\left(\frac{1+i\alpha_{j}}{1+\alpha_{j}^{2}}\right)^{n+1},

(179)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-x}x^{n}\cos(\alpha_{j}x)

\displaystyle=n!\,{\rm Re}\,\left(\frac{1+i\alpha_{j}}{1+\alpha^{2}_{j}}\right)^{n+1},

(180)

with
{\rm Re}(z)=(z+z^{*})/2
and
{\rm Im}(z)=(z-z^{*})/2i
being the real and imaginary parts of a complex number
z
, we obtain

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{n00}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{2\pi^{2}\alpha_{j}(j\beta)^{n+2}}{\rm Im}\left(\frac{1+i\alpha_{j}}{1+\alpha^{2}_{j}}\right)^{n+1},

(181a)

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{n20}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\alpha_{j}^{3}(j\beta)^{n+4}}\Bigg[\alpha_{j}^{2}(n+2)!{\rm Im}\left(\frac{1+i\alpha_{j}}{1+\alpha_{j}^{2}}\right)^{n+3}

\displaystyle\hskip-15.0pt+\alpha_{j}(n+1)!\,{\rm Re}\left(\frac{1+i\alpha_{j}}{1+\alpha_{j}^{2}}\right)^{n+2}\hskip-15.0pt-n!\,{\rm Im}\left(\frac{1+i\alpha_{j}}{1+\alpha_{j}^{2}}\right)^{n+1}\Bigg].

(181b)

Specifically, for
\phi^{2}
and
T^{\mu\nu}
, we require:

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{000}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{2}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})},

(181c)

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{100}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{3}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{2}},

(181d)

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{200}

\displaystyle=\frac{3-\alpha_{j}^{2}}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

(181e)

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{020}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1-\alpha_{j}^{2})}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}}.

(181f)

Furthermore,
G^{j;I}_{001}
,
G^{j;I}_{101}
, and
G^{j;I}_{002}
can be obtained by means of Eq. (
166
):

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{001}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\rho^{2}\sin(j\beta\Omega_{I})}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{2}},

(181g)

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{101}

\displaystyle=\frac{4i\rho^{2}\sin(j\beta\Omega_{I})}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{5}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

(181h)

\displaystyle G^{j;I}_{002}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{2}\cos(j\beta\Omega_{I})}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{2}}-\frac{4\rho^{4}\sin^{2}(j\beta\Omega_{I})}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{6}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}}.

(181i)

Finally, the functions
G^{(1);j;I}_{000}
and
G^{(2);j;I}_{000}
corresponding to the expressions in Eq. (
161
) are

\displaystyle G^{(1);j;I}_{000}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{j}^{2}\frac{dG^{j}_{000}}{d\alpha_{j}^{2}}=-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{\pi^{2}j^{2}\beta^{2}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{2}},

(181j)

\displaystyle G^{(2);j;I}_{000}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{j}^{2}\frac{dG^{(1);j}_{000}}{d\alpha_{j}^{2}}=\frac{2\alpha_{j}^{2}(\alpha_{j}^{2}-1)}{\pi^{2}j^{2}\beta^{2}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}}.

(181k)

Substituting Eq. (
181c
) into Eq. (
158
) allows
\phi^{2}
to be expressed as

\displaystyle\phi^{2}_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\beta^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{j}^{2}},

(182a)

while the non-vanishing components of
T^{\mu\nu}
, given in Eq. (
160
), reduce to

\displaystyle T^{tt}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{3-\alpha_{j}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}(\alpha_{j}^{2}-1)\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{\pi^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

(182b)

\displaystyle T^{\rho\rho}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{3-2\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{3\pi^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{2}},

(182c)

\displaystyle T^{\varphi\varphi}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{[3-2\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)](1-3\alpha_{j}^{2})}{3\pi^{2}\rho^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

(182d)

\displaystyle T^{zz}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{3+3\alpha_{j}^{2}+2(1-\alpha_{j}^{2})\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{3\pi^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

(182e)

\displaystyle T^{t\varphi}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{4i\sin(2\pi j\nu)}{\pi^{2}(j\beta)^{5}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}}.

(182f)

The above results show that the diagonal components of
T^{\mu\nu}
are real-valued and even with respect to
\nu=\beta\Omega_{I}/2\pi
, while
T^{t\varphi}_{I}
is imaginary and odd with respect to
\nu\rightarrow-\nu
. Under the hermitization (
162
), it is clear that
T^{t\varphi}_{I}
vanishes and
T^{\mu\nu}_{I}
remains diagonal. As in the case of the classical relativistic kinetic theory (RKT) analysis in Sec.
IV.4
, the resulting state is not isotropic. Identifying as in the classical case
E_{I}=T_{I}^{tt}
and the perfect-fluid contribution
T^{\mu\nu}_{{\rm pf};I}={\rm diag}(E_{I},P_{I},\rho^{-2}P_{I},P_{I})
with
P_{I}=E_{I}/3
, the quantum shear-stress tensor
\pi_{I}^{\mu\nu}=T_{I}^{\mu\nu}-T^{\mu\nu}_{{\rm pf};I}={\rm diag}(0,\pi_{I}^{\rho\rho},\pi_{I}^{\varphi\varphi},\pi_{I}^{zz})
can be obtained as

\displaystyle\pi_{I}^{\rho\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{3\alpha_{j}^{2}-(2\alpha_{j}^{2}+1)\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{\pi^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

\displaystyle\pi_{I}^{\varphi\varphi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4}{9}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{6\alpha_{j}^{2}-(4\alpha_{j}^{2}-1)\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{\pi^{2}\rho^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}},

\displaystyle\pi_{I}^{zz}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{3\alpha_{j}^{2}-2(\alpha_{j}^{2}-1)\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{\pi^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{3}}.

(183)

At large distances from the rotation axis,
\alpha_{j}\rightarrow\infty
, implying that

T_{I}^{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(-1,1,-3\rho^{-2},1)\\
\times\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{3-2\sin^{2}(\pi j\nu)}{3\pi^{2}\beta^{4}j^{4}(1+\alpha_{j}^{2})^{2}}.

(184)

The structure of the above result is similar to that obtained in Eq. (
133
), with the important difference that the quantum field-theoretical (QFT)
T_{I}^{\mu\nu}
depends on
\nu
through the harmonic function
\sin(\pi j\nu)
. This property implies that
T_{I}^{\mu\nu}
obtained in QFT is periodic with respect to
\nu
, with period
\Delta\nu=1
, in agreement with the symmetry (
10
) expected on general grounds. This periodic behaviour is fundamentally different from that observed in RKT (Subsect.
IV.4
), where no periodicity in
\nu
can be seen.

V.4
Values on the rotation axis: no analytical connection between real and imaginary rotations

\phi^{2}
and the components of the energy-momentum tensor

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-2}=\pi^{2}/6
and
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-4}=\pi^{4}/90

(185c)

(185d)

(186)

represent the expectation values in the absence of rotation (

). The notation

represents the fractional part of

represents its integer part. The

(a) confirms that

is periodic with respect to the imaginary rotation parameter

\{\nu\}

and the radial pressure

are increased.

An alternative way of characterizing
T_{I}^{\mu\nu}
on the rotation axis is by using the Bernoulli polynomial

\displaystyle B_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}B_{n-k}x^{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n!}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\left[{\rm Li}_{n}(e^{2\pi ix})+(-1)^{n}{\rm Li}_{n}(e^{-2\pi ix})\right],

with

Before ending this subsection, we remark that the presence of odd powers of

with respect to the rotation parameter

V.5
High temperature expansion

Let us now consider the large temperature expansion, when
\beta\rightarrow 0
. Since
\beta
comes multiplied by
j
under the summation sign in Eqs. (
182
), higher-order terms with respect to
\beta
come with higher powers of
j
. Since the summation over
j
and the power series with respect to
j\beta
in general do not commute, this procedure allows only the coefficients of the
\beta^{-4}
and
\beta^{-2}
terms to be extracted. The results are

\displaystyle\phi_{I}^{2}=\frac{\gamma_{I}^{2}}{12\beta^{2}},\quad T_{I}^{tt}=\frac{\pi^{2}\gamma_{I}^{4}}{90\beta^{4}}(4\gamma_{I}^{2}-1)-\frac{\Omega_{I}^{2}\gamma_{I}^{6}}{36\beta^{2}}(6\gamma_{I}^{2}-5),

\displaystyle T_{I}^{\rho\rho}=\frac{\pi^{2}\gamma_{I}^{4}}{90\beta^{4}}-\frac{\Omega_{I}^{2}\gamma_{I}^{6}}{36\beta^{2}},\qquad T_{I}^{zz}=\frac{\pi^{2}\gamma_{I}^{4}}{90\beta^{4}}+\frac{\Omega_{I}^{2}\gamma_{I}^{6}}{36\beta^{2}},

\displaystyle\rho^{2}T_{I}^{\varphi\varphi}=\frac{\pi^{2}\gamma_{I}^{4}}{90\beta^{4}}(4\gamma_{I}^{2}-3)-\frac{\Omega_{I}^{2}\gamma_{I}^{6}}{36\beta^{2}}(6\gamma_{I}^{2}-5),

\displaystyle T_{I}^{t\varphi}=i\Omega_{I}\left[\frac{2\pi^{2}\gamma_{I}^{6}}{45\beta^{4}}-\frac{\Omega_{I}^{2}\gamma_{I}^{6}}{18\beta^{2}}(3\gamma_{I}^{2}-1)\right].

(193)

As discussed in the previous subsection, the
\nu^{3}
terms revealed in Eq. (
185
) are not captured by the perturbative series expansion approach. Nevertheless, the results reported in Eq. (
193
) are fully consistent with previously derived results, see Eq. (4.2.51) of Ref.
Ambrus 2014
; Eqs. (A.21,A.22) of Ref.
Ambrus 2017
; and Eqs. (7.19,7.22,7.23) of Ref.
Becattini et al. 2021
.

VII
Conclusions

In the present work, we studied the thermodynamic properties of a massless scalar field subjected to rigid rotation and an inter-relation of the real rotation with its imaginary analogue. The latter concept – a rigid rotation with an imaginary frequency
Chernodub 2021a
;
Chen et al. 2022
– has a practical interest since rotating systems cannot be implemented in Euclidean path-integral formalism, suitable, for example, for numerical first-principle calculations on the lattice
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
;
Braguta et al. 2020
;
Braguta et al. 2021
;
Braguta et al. 2023
;
Chernodub et al. 2022
. In this sense, rotation shares the deficiency suffered by finite-density systems, namely the sign problem
Yamamoto and Hirono 2013
, and needs to be implemented in Euclidean spacetime via its imaginary version supplemented with the subsequent analytical continuation to real angular frequencies
Braguta et al. 2020
;
Braguta et al. 2021
;
Braguta et al. 2023
.

Using the
1+1
-dimensional toy model of a scalar field under rigid rotation on a ring, we explicitly demonstrated that the analytical no-go theorem
Chernodub 2022b
, which describes the impossibility of continuation of the imaginary-angular-frequency thermodynamics to the real angular frequencies is related to the development of the fractality of thermodynamics for the former. The result applies in the thermodynamic limit. Within this model, thermodynamic functions such as pressure and energy density can be expressed analytically via the Dedekind
\eta
function (
47
). The latter function tends to the fractal, non-analytical Thomae function (
II.3
) as its argument approaches the real axis
Nechaev and Polovnikov 2017
, which corresponds to the infinite-volume (thermodynamic) limit.

In the case of the (3+1)-dimensional Minkowski space, we first considered a classical description of scalar particles under rotation using relativistic kinetic theory. In the absence of boundaries, rigid rotation with a real rotation parameter leads to a violation of causality and subsequent divergence of all observables on the light cylinder. Imaginary rotation can be described by a real distribution function only as an average of clockwise and counterclockwise rotations, leading to a seemingly non-equilibrium state. As expected, observables such as the energy-momentum tensor
T^{\mu\nu}
decrease as the distance to the rotation axis is increased, at a faster rate for faster rotation.

Under the quantum field theoretical treatment, rigid rotation with a real rotation parameter leads to the divergence of
T^{\mu\nu}
at each point of the space-time, also inside the light cylinder. Under imaginary rotation,
T^{\mu\nu}
evaluated on the rotation axis can be expressed via Bernoulli polynomials (
182
). Away from the rotation axis, we were able to demonstrate the fractalization of both the field fluctuations
\phi^{2}
and of
T^{\mu\nu}
in the case of imaginary rotation, in complete analogy to the
1+1
-dimensional toy model discussed above. In all cases, our results exhibit a periodicity with respect to the imaginary rotation parameter which is not present in the classical, kinetic analysis. For this reason, we found agreement with kinetic theory only in the limit of slow rotation and only in the vicinity of the rotation axis (before fractalization sets in).

For comparison with the case of real rotation, we took results obtained using a perturbative calculation for slow rotation (with respect to a stationary background) and found on the rotation axis an analytical result (
190
) which can be related to the one obtained for imaginary rotation in an essentially non-analytical way (
191
).
We conclude that even on the axis of rotation – which appears to be static in the rotating system – the non-analyticity is strong and unavoidable.

We demonstrated the same analytic–non-analytical transition using numerical calculations for the thermal scalar field system enclosed in a cylinder, undergoing rotation with imaginary angular frequencies. As the radius of the cylinder grows, the pressure becomes a non-analytical function of temperature expressed, again, via the Thomae function (demonstrated in Figs.
13
–
20
). In this limit, the boundary effects become less important and the results obtained in the unbounded case provide a good approximation for our observables inside the cylindrical boundary. For values of the rotation parameter respecting the causality constraints (i.e., when the light cylinder is outside the boundary), the fractalization features do not appear in the case of imaginary rotation. For sufficiently slow rotation and high temperature, our numerically-obtained results are compatible with the flip
\Omega_{I}^{2}\rightarrow-\Omega^{2}
from imaginary to real rotation, signaling the restoration of analytical continuation in this limit.

The exotic fractalization properties discussed above are related to a ninionic deformation (
12
) of statistical distributions at imaginary angular frequencies
Chernodub 2022b
. For this reason, we conclude that the results obtained in the infinite-volume system subjected to imaginary rotation cannot be analytically related to the properties of the physically rotating system (with a real angular frequency).
However, we explicitly demonstrated both for the analytically-treatable case on the ring and numerically-accessible case of rotating cylinder that the imaginary rotation in a spatially bounded system in Euclidean space can be continued to the real-frequency domain in Minkowski spacetime provided that the causality is respected for the latter spacetime.

We have also shown that for real-frequency rotation, the dimensionless moment of inertia
K_{2}
, normalized per one degree of freedom, is equal to two,
K_{2}=2
, in the thermodynamic limit of large radius
R
of the cylinder (
33
). The quantity
K_{2}
determines the correction to the free energy (
30
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\Omega)=\mathcal{F}(0)\biggl(1+\frac{1}{2}K_{2}R^{2}\Omega^{2}+\dots\biggr)\,,

(246)

due to small nonzero angular frequency,
\Omega\to 0
. This result matches well the first-principle result of Ref.
Braguta et al. 2023
on the behavior of gluons in high-temperature limit of Yang-Mills theory. We have also shown that in the thermodynamic limit, the generic expansion of the pressure (free energy) in higher orders in angular frequency (
30
) is characterized by exactly-calculable coefficients
K_{2n}
with
n=1,2,\dots
, given by identical expressions (
31
) for the ring and (
121
) for the cylinder, which also includes the shape coefficient
K_{4}=4!
responsible for an
\Omega^{4}
correction to the pressure.

Below we summarize our main findings:

1.

We demonstrated the no-go theorem
Chernodub 2022b
regarding the impossibility of continuation of the imaginary-angular-frequency thermodynamics to the real angular frequencies using an analytical
1+1
-dimensional toy model, revealing the development of the fractality of thermodynamics for the former.

2.

Since fractalization does not show up in the classical, kinetic theory treatment of imaginary rotation, we conclude that this is a purely quantum effect.

3.

We found similar fractalization on the unbounded Minkowski space for imaginary rotation, as well as in the thermodynamic (infinite volume) limit of the bounded system.

4.

For the case of a causal boundary that excludes the light cylinder, we were able to restore the analytical continuation from imaginary to real rotation in the limit of slow rotation and large temperatures.

5.

We attributed the exotic fractalization properties to a ninionic deformation (
12
) of statistical distributions at imaginary angular frequencies
Chernodub 2022b
.

6.

These statements are applicable both for massless and massive fields, with the fractal features become pronounced in the thermodynamic limit for massive particles. While an increasing mass of bosons enhances the moment of inertia
K_{2}
and the shape coefficient
K_{4}
of the rotating gas, this effect vanishes in the high-temperature limit.

The results obtained in this paper shed light on the implications of the effects of imaginary rotation obtained in the context of first-principle lattice simulations and constitute the basis for the analysis of more complicated systems, e.g., free fermions or the chiral phase transition in the effective QCD models such as the Nambu–Jona-Lasinio model or nonlinear sigma models. Extending the present analysis to the case of Dirac fermions is a logical avenue of future research
Palermo et al. 2021
.
