Multibranch Generative Models for Multichannel Imaging with an Application to PET/CT Synergistic Reconstruction
Noel Jeffrey Pinton, Alexandre Bousse, Catherine Cheze-Le-Rest, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract
This paper presents a novel approach for learned synergistic reconstruction of medical images using multibranch generative models. Leveraging variational autoencoders, our model learns from pairs of images simultaneously, enabling effective denoising and reconstruction. Synergistic image reconstruction is achieved by incorporating the trained models in a regularizer that evaluates the distance between the images and the model. We demonstrate the efficacy of our approach on both Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology and positron emission tomography/computed tomography datasets, showcasing improved image quality for low-dose imaging. Despite challenges such as patch decomposition and model limitations, our results underscore the potential of generative models for enhancing medical imaging reconstruction.



I
Introduction

Multimodal imaging refers to acquiring data from different sources or from different techniques to capture complementary information about the object or scene being observed. Multimodal imaging is used in various fields such as remote sensing
[
mm_rs_gomez2015
,
Dalla_Mura_Prasad_Pacifici_Gamba_Chanussot_Benediktsson_2015
]
robotics
[
xue_mm_hri_2020
,
Su_Qi_Chen_Yang_Sandoval_Laribi_2023
]
and medical imaging
[
Pichler_Judenhofer_Pfannenberg_2008
,
Decazes_Hinault_Veresezan_Thureau_Gouel_Vera_2021
]
.
Some of the modalities used in the latter field include positron emission tomography, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, ultrasound, and various optical imaging techniques. Positron emission tomography is a powerful medical imaging technique that uses a small amount of radioactive material to visualize and track various processes in the body. It provides valuable insights into cancer detection and other areas of medicine such as cardiology and neurology
[
bailey2005positron
]
. Positron emission tomography is often completed with computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging which provide anatomical information.

Images are reconstructed by solving modality-specific inverse problems. In medical imaging, early methods were based on inversion formulas such as filtered backprojection
[
natterer2001mathematics
]
for computed tomography and positron emission tomography as well as inverse fast Fourier transform for magnetic resonance imaging. These methods were then followed by model-based iterative reconstruction algorithms which consist of iteratively minimizing a cost function comprising a data fidelity term that encompasses the physics and statistics of the measurement and a regularizer to control the noise. Such methods include expectation-maximization for positron emission tomography and single-photon emission CT
[
mlem_shepp
]
and its regularized versions
[
green1990bayesian
,
depierro1995modified
]
, as well as penalized weighted least squares for computed tomography
[
elbakri2002statistical
]
.

Multimodal imaging systems produce multiple images of the same underlying object. In general, each modality is reconstructed individually. However, they correspond to images of the same object. Therefore, it is natural to take advantage of the intermodality information to reconstruct the images together, or
synergistically
, in order to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. In medical imaging, this approach can give room for dose reduction and/or scan time reduction.

Early synergistic techniques use handcrafted multichannel regularizers embedded within a model-based iterative reconstruction framework, such as joint total variation for color images
[
blomgren1998color
]
and positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
mehranian2017synergistic
]
, the parallel level set prior for positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
ehrhardt2014joint
]
and total nuclear variation for multienergy (or spectral) computed tomography
[
rigie2015joint
]
(see
[
arridge2021overview
]
for a review). These handcrafted regularizers promote structural similarities between the images, and therefore may not be suitable for modalities with different intrinsic resolution. For example, in positron emission tomography/computed tomography or positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging, the resolution of the positron emission tomography can be artificially enhanced, and while this enhancement may improve aesthetics and quantification in applications such as brain positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
thomas2016petpvc
]
, it may not accurately represent the actual distribution of radiotracers the rest of the body where higher sensitivity is required at the expense of spatial resolution (e.g., metastases detection).

Alternatively, the intermodality information can be learned with machine learning techniques. Dictionary learning techniques have been used in image reconstruction for single-energy computed tomography
[
xu2012low
]
but also for spectral computed tomography through tensor dictionary learning to sparsely represent the images in a joint multidimensional dictionary
[
zhang2016tensor
,
zhang2016spectral
,
wu2018low
,
li2022tensor
]
(see
[
bousse2023systematic
]
for a review). A similar multichannel dictionary learning approach was proposed for positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
sudarshan2020joint
]
. All these works reported better performances using multichannel dictionary learning as compared with single-channel dictionary learning. Dictionary learning relies on patch decomposition, which is not efficient for joint sparse representation across channels. To remedy this, multichannel convolutional DiL was proposed (see
[
perelli2022multi
]
for dual-energy computed tomography).

Multichannel dictionary learning is limited to encoding structural information only. In that sense, synergistic multichannel image reconstruction could benefit from the deeper architectures used in deep learning. However, few researchers addressed synergistic reconstruction using deep learning. For example in a recent work,
[
corda2023single
]

[
corda2023single
]
proposed an unrolling framework for synergistic positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging reconstruction. The training of unrolling models is supervised and computationally expensive as it integrates the imaging system forward model at each layer.

In this work, which is an extension of our previous work
[
pinton2023synergistic
,
pinton2023joint
]
, we investigate the feasibility of deep learning for synergistic multichannel image reconstruction through the utilization of a deep generative model (i.e., a variational autoencoder) incorporated within a model-based iterative reconstruction framework through a regularizer in a similar fashion as proposed by
[
duff2021regularising
]

[
duff2021regularising
]
(and to some extend
[
kelkar2021prior
]

[
kelkar2021prior
]
). For our approach, we used multiple-branch generators to map a single latent variable to multiple images. The training is unsupervised and performed on an image pair basis and does not involve the forward model, thus enabling the possibility to use the same model to any imaging system using the same modalities.

In Section
II
we present our multibranch VAE architecture and the corresponding reconstruction algorithm. Section
III
demonstrates the capability of our architectures to generate multiple images and to convey information across channels, and shows the results of their utilization in a denoising framework with data generated from the Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology database
[
deng2012mnist
]
and in for synergistic positron emission tomography/computed tomography reconstruction with a comparison with the parallel level set technique
[
ehrhardt2014joint
]
. Section
discusses the limitations of our method and experiments. Finally, Section
concludes this paper.

II
Method

II-A

Background on Medical Image Reconstruction

Image reconstruction corresponds to the task of estimating an image
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\triangleq\mathbb{R}^{m}
from a random measurement
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\triangleq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
m
and
n
are respectively the dimension of the image (number of pixels or voxels) and the dimension of the measurement (number of detectors). The image
\bm{x}
is a visual representation of the interior of an object (e.g., the patient).

The measurement is modeled with a forward model which takes the form of a mapping
\bar{\bm{y}}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
that incorporates the physics of the measurement such that given a ground truth image
\bm{x}^{\star}
the expected measurement matches the forward model, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{y}]=\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}^{\star})
. When the measurement consists of photon counting (e.g., positron emission tomography, single-photon emission CT and computed tomography),

\bm{y}
is a random vector that follows a Poisson distribution with independent entries, i.e.,

\bm{y}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}^{\star}))\,.

(1)

The forward model
\bar{\bm{y}}
depends on the imaging system. In positron emission tomography, it is traditionally written as

\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\tau(\bm{P}\bm{x}+\bm{r})

(2)

where
\bm{x}
is the radiotracer distribution image,
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the positron emission tomography system matrix such that each entry
[\bm{P}]_{i,j}
is the probability that a positron-electron annihilation in voxel
j
is detected by the
i
th detector pair (with incorporation of the attenuation factors, resolution and sensitivity),
\tau
is the acquisition time and
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a “background term” representing the expected scatter and randoms per unit of time. In computed tomography, the standard (monochromatic) model is

\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=I\cdot\exp(-\bm{A}\bm{x})

(3)

where
\bm{x}
is the attenuation image,
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the computed tomography system matrix,
I
is the X-ray intensity and the
\exp
function applied to a vector should be understood as operating on each element individually.

Image reconstruction is achieved by matching
\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
to
\bm{y}
, i.e.,

\text{find $\bm{x}$ s.t.}\quad\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})\approx\bm{y}\,,

(4)

which is an (ill-posed) inverse problem. As solving (
4
) cannot be achieved with an inversion formula without amplifying the noise, model-based iterative reconstruction techniques have been prevalent over the last decades. Model-based iterative reconstruction consists in solving an optimization problem of the form

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\,L(\bm{y},\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))+\beta R(\bm{x})

(5)

where
L
is a loss function that evaluates the goodness of fit between the measurement
\bm{y}
and the expectation
\bar{\bm{y}}
,
R\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a regularizer, and
\beta>0
is a weight, with an iterative algorithm. The loss function
L
is usually defined a negative Poisson log-likelihood, although it can be approximated by a weighted least squares loss (see for example
[
elbakri2002statistical
]
in computed tomography). The regularizer
R
promotes images that have desired properties, such as piecewise smoothness or sparsity of the gradient.

The choice of the algorithm to solve (
5
) largely depends on
\bar{\bm{y}}
,
L
, and
R
. Examples from the literature include separable paraboloidal surrogates for computed tomography
[
elbakri2002statistical
]
, maximum-likelihood expectation-maximization, ordered subsets expectation maximization
[
mlem_shepp
,
Hudson1994
]
and modified maximum-likelihood expectation-maximization
[
depierro1995modified
]
for positron emission tomography with smooth regularizers. Non-smooth regularizers can be addressed for example with a primal-dual algorithm
[
sidky2012convex
]
.

II-B

Synergistic Reconstruction in Multimodal Imaging

Multimodal hybrid imaging systems such as positron emission tomography/computed tomography, positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging, single-photon emission CT/computed tomography (and to some extent, spectral computed tomography) can acquire multiple measurement
\{\bm{y}_{k}\}=\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{K}\}
,
\bm{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}\triangleq\mathcal{Y}_{k}
, to reconstruct several images
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}=\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{K}\}
. For simplicity, we assume that the images
\bm{x}_{k}
are all
m
-dimensional. In general, each channel
k
is individually reconstructed by solving (
5
) using its corresponding forward model
\bar{\bm{y}}_{k}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}_{k}
, loss function
L_{k}\colon\mathcal{Y}_{k}\times\mathcal{Y}_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
and regularizer
R_{k}
. Another approach consists of reconstructing the images simultaneously by solving

\min_{\{\bm{x}_{k}\}\in\mathcal{X}^{K}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{k}L_{k}(\bm{y}_{k},\bar{\bm{y}}_{k}(\bm{x}_{k}))+\beta R_{\mathrm{syn}}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{K})

(6)

where
R_{\mathrm{syn}}\colon\mathcal{X}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
synergistic
regularizer that promotes structural and/or functional dependencies between the multiple images and the
\eta_{k}
s are positive normalized weights (
\eta_{k}>0
,
\sum_{k}\eta_{k}=1
) that tune the strength of the regularizer for each channel
k
independently
1
1

1

In (
6
) the weight
\beta
may also be incorporated in the
\eta_{k}
s. However, in this work, it is more convenient to keep them separated, cf. footnote in Section
II-D
.
.

A classical regularizer is joint total variation
[
blomgren1998color
]
which encourages joint sparsity of the image gradients. Similarly, total nuclear variation, which encourages common edge locations and a shared gradient direction among image channels, was used in spectral computed tomography
[
rigie2015joint
]
. Another example is the parallel level set prior which was used in positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
ehrhardt2014joint
]
. By promoting common features between the images, synergistic regularizers can convey information across channels in a way that each image
\bm{x}_{k}
leverages the entire raw data
\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{K}
, thus improving the signal-to-noise ratio. However, enforcing structural similarities may not be appropriate for modalities that do not have the same intrinsic resolutions, such as in positron emission tomography/computed tomography and positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging.

II-C

Learned Regularizers with Generative Models

Machine learning and deep learning techniques have been used in inverse problem-solving and image reconstruction
[
arridge2019solving
]
. These approaches have changed the paradigm of image reconstruction in the sense that they are trained to deliver the reconstructed images. For example, unrolling methods extend conventional iterative algorithms into a deep architecture for end-to-end reconstructions
[
monga2021algorithm
]
, while other techniques directly map the raw data into the image space
[
Kandarpa_Bousse_Benoit_Visvikis_2021
,
Ma2022_direct
,
Cao_Xu_2022
]
. Another category of technique aims at training a penalty
R_{\bm{\theta}}\colon(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to some parameter
\bm{\theta}
such that it promotes plausible multichannel image
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}
, that is to say, images that are plausible not only individually but also together.

II-C
1
Proposed Regularizer

This section focuses on generative model-based regularizers, i.e., based on a multichannel image patch generator
\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathrm{mult}}
with trainable parameter
\bm{\theta}
, of the form

\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\mathrm{mult}}\triangleq\left\{\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}^{1},\dots,\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}^{K}\right\}\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{U}^{K}

(7)

where for each
k=1,\dots,K
,
\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}^{k}\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{U}
is a generative model that maps a latent variable
\bm{z}
in the latent space
\mathcal{Z}\triangleq\mathbb{R}^{s}
to a
d
-dimensional image,
d<m
, corresponding to a patch (a portion of an image) in channel
k
, and
\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the patch space. The
s
-dimensional latent variable
\bm{z}
,
s<d
, encodes the information of the image patch. Note that
\bm{G}^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\bm{\theta}}
takes a single
\bm{z}
as input such that the
K
images correspond to the same
\bm{z}
.

We used a patch decomposition with overlaps to reduce training complexity and minimize hallucinations. Hallucinations arise when the generative model produces outputs that are not constrained by the range-space of the training data, leading to artifacts or erroneous features in the generated images. By using overlapping patches, the effect of local hallucinations in a patch are averaged out by neighboring patches.

In the following,
\bm{P}_{p}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
is the
p
th patch extractor,
p=1,\dots,P
, such that for each channel
k
,
\bm{u}_{k}=\bm{P}_{p}\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
is a “portion” of
\bm{x}_{k}
. The patches cover the entire image, with possible overlaps. The corresponding synergistic regularizer
R_{\bm{\theta}}
is then defined as

\begin{split}&R_{\bm{\theta}}(\{\bm{x}_{k}\})\triangleq\\
&\min_{\{\bm{z}_{p}\}\in\mathcal{Z}^{P}}\,\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\left\|\bm{G}^{k}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{p})-\bm{P}_{p}\bm{x}_{k}\right\|^{2}+\alpha H(\bm{z}_{p})\end{split}

(8)

where
H
is a regularizer on the latent variable with weight
\alpha>0
and the
\eta_{k}
are the same as in (
6
). The regularizer
R_{\bm{\theta}}
is minimized when for all patch
p
, each
\bm{P}_{p}\bm{x}_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,L
, is approximately generated from a same
\bm{z}_{p}
that is ‘regularized’ in the sense of
H
. Solving the penalized maximum likelihood problem (
6
) with
R_{\mathrm{syn}}=R_{\bm{\theta}}
requires to alternate minimization in
\{\bm{z}_{p}\}
and
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}
. The penalty
R_{\bm{\theta}}
allows some flexibility for the solution in the sense that the reconstructed image patches (i.e., obtained by solving (
6
)) are not necessarily in the range of
\bm{G}^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\bm{\theta}}
. Another approach discussed in
[
duff2021regularising
]

[
duff2021regularising
]
consists in imposing the images to be in the range of
\bm{G}^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\bm{\theta}}
. However, minimizing this regularizer is unpractical when using overlapping patches.

2
Proposed Generative Model

In this work we propose to utilize a multichannel image generator
\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{U}^{K}
trained as a multibranch VAE with a multibranch architecture inspired from
[
duff2021regularising
]

[
duff2021regularising
]
and
[
wang2024uconnect
]

[
wang2024uconnect
]
. While standard variational autoencoders are trained with a single encoder and a single generator (or decoder), our multibranch VAE uses a multibranch encoder mapping the
K
channels to a single latent variable
\bm{z}
which is then mapped to
K
images with
\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}=\left\{\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}^{k},\,k=1,\dots,K\right\}
with a multibranch decoder. Details on the training are given in Appendix
. In contrast with multichannel single-branch models, multibranch generative models introduce parallel pathways, each specializing in generating specific components of the data. By implementing this segregation, the original attributes unique to each modality can be better preserved in both the encoding and decoding components with minimal cross-talk, with interactions only taking place within the latent space.

The multibranch VAE architecture for
K=2
, 32
\times
32 patches, and
s=32
is represented in Figure
1
. In this work, we employed a four-layer convolutional NN network for the encoder and decoder branches with rectified linear unit
[
agarap2018deep
]
activation. All encoder branches have identical architecture and the same principle is also applied to all decoder branches. The variational autoencoder network was trained on bimodal (
K=2
) image pairs with little to no processing on the original image. The model is designed to be trained on matching images, such that it can learn to convey information from one image to another. In the positron emission tomography/computed tomography case, the image pairs need to be co-registered as patient motion and respiratory motion may cause misalignment between the positron emission tomography and the computed tomography which may affect the training.

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{p}^{(q)}={}

\displaystyle\quad\forall p=1,\dots,P

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k}^{(q+1)}={}

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\,L_{k}(\bm{y}_{k},\bar{\bm{y}}_{k}(\bm{x}))

Both sub-minimizations can be achieved with iterative algorithms initialized from the previous estimates

We define the “model-fitting” function

, which evaluates the goodness of the fit between a pair of target images
(\bm{x}^{\star}_{1},\bm{x}^{\star}_{2})
and the generated pair from a trained two-channel model

f_{\eta}(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{1}^{\star},\bm{x}_{2}^{\star})\triangleq\eta\left\|\bm{G}^{1}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})-\bm{x}^{\star}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+(1-\eta)\left\|\bm{G}^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})-\bm{x}^{\star}_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(13)

The optimal latent variable, denoted

\check{\bm{z}}_{\eta}\triangleq\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}f_{\eta}(\bm{z},\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})\,,

(14)

where we dropped the
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})
-dependency on the left-hand side to lighten the notation. Finally, the “predicted images” are given by

\check{\bm{x}}_{k}^{\eta}=\bm{G}^{k}_{\bm{\theta}}(\check{\bm{z}}_{\eta})\,,\quad k=1,2\,.

(15)

Thus,

IV \par
Discussion

This work follows up on our previous studies presented in
[
pinton2023synergistic
,\par
pinton2023joint\par\par
]
, where we initially trained our models on full images. However, we encountered potential overfitting issues due to the lack of data for training, resulting in overly optimistic results. To address this challenge, we adopted a patch decomposition approach. It has been previously reported that training on repetitive and consistent patches yields better results than training on the entire image
[
gupta2020patchvae\par\par
]
. To mitigate artifacts in the reconstructed images, our regularization strategy necessitates numerous overlapping patches. However, this comes at the expense of increased computational cost, as each patch requires its own latent variable. Besides, it is essential to remain vigilant about the potential for hallucinations. Future work may involve integrating more robust constraints and validation techniques to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the reconstructed images.

We demonstrated that our models successfully learn from two images simultaneously, suggesting their applicability for synergistic image reconstruction. Results obtained with Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology-trained models distinctly showcase how our generative model-based regularizer effectively utilize information from both images for denoising.

Our high-count PET/low-count CT and low-count PET/high-count CT experiments showed that multibranch VAE outperforms the parallel level set technique
[
ehrhardt2014joint\par\par
]
on low-count positron emission tomography (although it is outperformed by parallel level set with high-count data), thus demonstrating that multibranch VAE is suitable for low-dose imaging. However it is outperformed by U-Net denoising methods that are specifically trained to process this noise level.

The main drawback of variational autoencoders is their tendency to generate blurry images. While this phenomenon has limited impact on positron emission tomography due to its lower intrinsic resolution, it is more problematic for computed tomography. In a previous version of this work, we implemented a multibranch generative adversarial network which produced sharper images than those produced with multibranch VAE, with the Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology dataset. However the optimization with respect to the latent variable could not be achieved by limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno and therefore we deployed a computationally expensive particle swarm optimization
[
pso1995\par\par
]
(global optimizer). While this is reasonable with small images, is becomes unpractical if applied on a high number of patches.

Although we have demonstrated that multibranch generative models offer a viable approach for learned synergistic reconstruction, it is important to explore alternative options beyond variational autoencoders. Diffusion models have shown significant promise in generating high-quality images from training datasets
[
dhariwal2021diffusion\par\par
]
. These models can be seamlessly integrated into a penalized maximum likelihood reconstruction framework, as demonstrated by diffusion posterior sampling
[
chung2023diffusion\par\par
]
. Recent advancements in spectral computed tomography reconstruction have further highlighted the potential of diffusion models. More specifically, diffusion models are capable of capturing multichannel information and employing multichannel diffusion posterior sampling leads to superior spectral computed tomography images compared to conventional techniques
[
vazia2024diffusion
,\par
vazia2024spectral\par\par
]
. Therefore, the future of learned synergistic reconstruction may shift towards leveraging diffusion models.

V \par
Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the utility of generative models for learned synergistic reconstruction in medical imaging. By training on pairs of images, our approach harnesses the power of variational autoencoders to improve denoising and reconstruction outcomes. While challenges such as patch decomposition and inherent model limitations persist, our results demonstrate promising advancements in leveraging generative models for enhancing image quality and information exchange between modalities. Moving forward, further exploration of alternative models, such as diffusion models, may offer additional avenues for enhancing imaging outcomes in medical diagnostics and research. Overall, our findings contribute to the growing body of literature on learned synergistic reconstruction methods and pave the way for future developments in medical multimodal imaging technology.
