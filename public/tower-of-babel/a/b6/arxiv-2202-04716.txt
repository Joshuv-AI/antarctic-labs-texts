Ramsey and Hypersmoothness
Zoltán Vidnyánszky

Abstract
. Combining canonization results of Prömel-Voigt, Mathias, and Soare, we provide a new, natural example of an F σ F_{\sigma} equivalence relation that is not hypersmooth.



Assume that
E
is a countable Borel equivalence relation on the space
[\mathbb{N}]{N}
. It follows from the work of Mathias
[
4
]
and Soare
[
7
]
that one can find an
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
such that
E\upharpoonright[x]{N}
is hyperfinite (in fact, contained in
\mathbb{E}_{0}
, see
[
1
]
). In contrast, equivalence relations with uncountable classes can exhibit complicated behavior on every set of the form
[x]{N}
.

In this note, we give an example of an
F_{\sigma}
equivalence relation on
[\mathbb{N}]{N}
that is not hypersmooth (see
[
2
]
for more information on such equivalence relations) and the reason for this is completely Ramsey-theoretic. The equivalence relation comes from a natural generalization of the shift-graph (see
[
3
,
8
]
) and it is generated by two continuous functions.

1.
The construction

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
E
is an equivalence relation on
[\mathbb{N}]{N}
such that for every
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}

•

there exist almost disjoint
y,z\in[x]{N}
such that
yEzEx
and

•

E\upharpoonright[x]{N}
has uncountably many equivalence classes.

Then
E
is not hypersmooth.

In order to establish this theorem, we will need the following statement.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:[\mathbb{N}]^{\mathbb{N}}\to(2{N}){N}
are Borel. There exist an
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
and a countable set
C
such that for every
y,z\in[x]{N}
almost disjoint and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
f_{n}(z)=f_{n}(y)
implies
f_{n}(y)\in C
.

The proof of this theorem is based on the results of Prömel-Voigt
[
5
]
, Mathias
[
4
]
and Soare
[
7
]
and postponed to the next section.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Assume the contrary, and let
\varphi
be a Borel reduction from
E
to
E_{1}
. Let
f_{n}=p^{n}\circ\varphi
where
p((x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=(x_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Clearly, if
yEz
then for some
n
we have
f_{n}(x)=f_{n}(y)
.

Let
x
and
C
be as in Theorem
1.2
for the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We claim that
\varphi([x]{N})
is contained in countably many
E_{1}
equivalence classes, contradicting our second assumption. Indeed, take the countable set
C^{\prime}=\{(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:\exists m\ (\forall n<m\ x_{n}=0\land(x_{m},x_{m+1},\dots)\in C)\}
. If
x^{\prime}\in[x]{N}
then by the first assumption, there are
y,z\in[x^{\prime}]{N}
almost disjoint such that
yEzEx^{\prime}
. Then, for some
n
we have
f_{n}(y)=f_{n}(z)\in C
, so
\varphi(x^{\prime})\in[C^{\prime}]_{E_{1}}
.
∎

We identify elements of
[\mathbb{N}]{N}
with their increasing enumeration.
Let
S_{0},S_{1}
be maps on
[\mathbb{N}]{N}
defined by

S_{0}(\{n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},\dots\})=\{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\dots\},

S_{1}(\{n_{0},n_{1},\dots\})=\{n_{1},n_{3},n_{5},\dots\}.

Denote by
G
the graph determined by
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, and let
E
be the connected component equivalence relation of
G
.

For
x,y\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
define
x\ll y
if for each
m
there is a
k
such that
|x\cap(y_{k},y_{k+1})|>m
. It is easy to check the following:

Lemma 1.3
.

Assume that
x\ll y
and
\{x^{\prime},x\}\in G
. Then
x^{\prime}\ll y
.

Now we show that
E
is an example of the phenomenon described in the main theorem.

Proposition 1.4
.

E
is not hypersmooth.

Proof.

We check the conditions of Theorem
1.1
.
First, for any
x^{\prime}
we have
S_{1}\circ S_{0}(x^{\prime})\cap S_{1}(x^{\prime})=\emptyset
and both
S_{1}\circ S_{0}(x^{\prime})
and
S_{1}(x^{\prime})
are contained in
x^{\prime}
.

Second, assume that
x
is given and
\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset[x]{N}
is arbitrary. It is sufficient to produce a
y\in[x]{N}
such that
y
is not
E
equivalent to either of the
x_{n}
’s. Let
y\subset x
be so that
x_{n}\ll y
for all
n
. Then by Lemma
1.3
and by
y\not\ll y
we are done.
∎

The following proposition concludes our construction.

Proposition 1.5
.

E
is
F_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

For
x,y\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
let
x\leq y
stand for
\forall n\ (x_{n}\leq y_{n})
. Note that for each
i<2
we have
x\leq S_{i}(x)
. For
s\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}
let

D_{s}=\{(x,y):x\leq S_{s(n-1)}\circ\dots\circ S_{s(1)}\circ S_{s(0)}(y)\},

and let
D(y)=\bigcup_{s\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}}\{x:(x,y)\in D_{s}\}
, that is,
D(y)
is the collection of the points that are pointwise below some forward iterate of
y
.

Claim 1.6
.

If
xGx^{\prime}
and
x\in D(y)
then
x^{\prime}\in D(y)
. Thus,
[y]_{E}\subseteq D(y)
for all
y
.

Proof.

If
x^{\prime}=S_{i}(x)
for some
i<2
and
(x,y)\in D_{s}
, then
(x^{\prime},y)\in D_{s\mathbin{\raisebox{3.01389pt}{\scalebox{.7}{$\frown$}}}(i)}
. Otherwise, if
x=S_{i}(x^{\prime})
, then
x^{\prime}\leq S_{i}(x^{\prime})=x
, so
x^{\prime}\in D(y)
as well.
∎

Let
(x,y)\in R_{n}
iff
\exists k\leq n\ \exists x_{0},\dots,x_{k-1}\ \exists s_{0},\dots,s_{k-1}\in 2^{\leq n}

\forall i<k\ (x_{i}Gx_{i+1}\land(x_{i},y)\in D_{s_{i}})\land x_{0}=x\land x_{k-1}=y.

By Claim
1.6
,
E=\bigcup_{n}R_{n}
. It follows from the continuity of the functions
(S_{i})_{i<2}
that each
D_{s}
is closed and that for each
y
the set
\{x:(x,y)\in D_{s}\}
is compact. From this, it is straightforward to show that
R_{n}
is closed for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which finishes our proof.
∎

2.
A version of Prömel-Voigt

Let us recall the canonization results of Prömel and Voigt
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Prömel-Voigt
[
5
]
)
.

Assume that
f:[\mathbb{N}]{N}\to 2{N}
is a Borel function. There exist an
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
and a function
\Gamma:[x]{N}\to[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq\mathbb{N}}
with the following properties:

(1)

for every
y\in[x]{N}
we have
\Gamma(y)\subseteq y
,

(2)

for every
y,z\in[x]{N}
we have

f(y)=f(z)\iff\Gamma(y)=\Gamma(z)

(3)

either for every
y\in[x]{N}
we have that
\Gamma(y)
is finite or for every
y\in[x]{N}
it is infinite,

(4)

for every
y,z\in[x]{N}
we have that
\Gamma(y)
is not a proper initial segment of
\Gamma(z)
,

(5)

\Gamma
is continuous (where the topology on
[\mathbb{N}]^{\leq\mathbb{N}}
comes from its ideintification with
2{N}
).

For
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
and
f:[\mathbb{N}]{N}\to X
define

C_{f,x}=\{f(x^{\prime}):x^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{0}x\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
x
,
\Gamma
and
f
be as above. If for some
y\in[x]{N}
the set
\Gamma(y)
is finite, then for all
x^{\prime}\in[x]{N}
we have
f(y)\in C_{f,x^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let
x^{\prime}\in[x]{N}
be arbitrary. By the continuity of
\Gamma
we can find some
x^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{E}_{0}x^{\prime}
with
x^{\prime\prime}\in[x]{N}
such that
\Gamma(y)
is an initial segment of
\Gamma(x^{\prime\prime})
. By
4
this implies that
\Gamma(x^{\prime\prime})=\Gamma(y)
, so
f(y)=f(x^{\prime\prime})\in C_{f,x^{\prime}}
.
∎

Let us point out that the preceeding argument is the only thing what one needs to prove Theorem
1.2
, in the case of a single function. However, to treat countably many functions, we have to prove an analogous statement for sets of the form
[s,x]
, where
s\in\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
. This requires some work and the utilization of the result of Mathias and Soare mentioned in the introduction.

If
\Gamma
is a function as above, define a partial function
\Gamma^{*}:2{N}\to[x]{N}
by

\Gamma^{*}(w)=z\iff\exists y\in[x]{N}\ f(y)=w\text{ and }\Gamma(y)=z.

The following is obvious from the definition.

Lemma 2.3
.

•

\Gamma^{*}
is relatively Borel on the (possibly proper) analytic set
f([x]{N})
; in particular, it is Souslin-measurable.

•

For every
y\in[x]{N}
we have

\Gamma^{*}(f(y))=\Gamma(y).

We will need the following theorem (see also
[
1
]
).

Theorem 2.4

(Mathias
[
4
]
, Soare
[
7
]
)
.

Let
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:[\mathbb{N}]{N}\to[\mathbb{N}]{N}
be a collection of Souslin-measurable functions. There exists an
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
such that for every
y\in[x]{N}
and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
g_{n}(y)\in[x]{N}
implies that
g_{n}(y)\setminus y
is finite.

Note that Soare only establishes the above theorem for Borel functions (and the proof of Kanovei-Sabok-Zapletal is also only for such), however, as by Silver’s theorem
[
6
]
every Souslin-measurable set has the Ramsey property, the proof extends to the class of Souslin-measurable maps without further modification.

The key ingredient to prove Theorem
1.1
is the following. For a function
f
and an
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
define
C_{f,x}=\{f(x^{\prime}):x^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{0}x\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in[\mathbb{N}]{N}
with
\max s<\min x
be arbitrary and
f:[\mathbb{N}]{N}\to 2{N}
be a Borel function. There exists an
x^{\prime}\in[x]{N}
such that whenever
y,z\in[s\cup x^{\prime}]{N}
are almost disjoint and
f(y)=f(z)
then
f(y)\in C_{f,x^{\prime\prime}}
for each
x^{\prime\prime}\in[x^{\prime}]{N}
.

Proof.

For
t\subseteq s
and
y\in[x]{N}
define a function
f^{t}
by

f^{t}=f(t\cup y).

By applying Theorem
2.1

2^{|s|}
many times, we can find an
x^{*}\in[x]{N}
and functions
\Gamma_{t},\Gamma^{*}_{t}
with the properties as in the theorem with respect to the function
f^{t}
on
[x^{*}]{N}
. For
r,t\subseteq s
let
g^{r,t}
be the function
\Gamma^{*}_{t}\circ f^{r}
. Note that
g^{r,t}
is Souslin-measurable. By Theorem
2.4
we can find an
x^{\prime}\in[x^{*}]{N}
such that whenever
y\in[x^{\prime}]{N}
and
g^{r,t}(y)\in[x^{\prime}]{N}
for some
r,t\subseteq s
then
g^{r,t}(y)\setminus y
is finite.

We claim that
x^{\prime}
has the desired property. Let
y,z\in[s,x^{\prime}]{N}
be almost disjoint, and assume that
f(y)=f(z)
, let
r=y\cap s
and
t=z\cap s
. Using Lemma
2.2
it suffices to show that
\Gamma_{r}(y\cap x^{\prime})
is finite: indeed, in this case
f(y)=f^{r}(y\cap x^{\prime})\in C_{f^{r},x^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq C_{f,x^{\prime\prime}}
for each
x^{\prime\prime}\in[x^{*}]{N}
, in particular
f(y)\in C_{f,x^{\prime}}
. Now,

g^{t,r}(z\cap x^{\prime})=\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f^{t}(z\cap x^{\prime}))=\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f(t\cup z))=\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f(z))=

\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f(y))=\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f(r\cup y))=\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f^{r}(y))=\Gamma^{*}_{r}(f^{r}(y\cap x^{\prime}))=

\Gamma_{r}(y\cap x^{\prime})\subseteq y\cap x^{\prime}.

So, as
z\cap x^{\prime},g^{t,r}(z\cap x^{\prime})\in[x^{\prime}]{N}
, we must have that

g^{t,r}(z\cap x^{\prime})\setminus(z\cap x^{\prime})=\Gamma_{r}(y\cap x^{\prime})\setminus(z\cap x^{\prime})

is finite. Therefore,
\Gamma_{r}(y\cap x^{\prime})
is almost contained in both
y\cap x^{\prime}
and
z\cap x^{\prime}
, which show that this set is finite.
∎

Finally, we prove Theorem
1.2
using a standard fusion argument.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Define sequences
s_{0}\subset s_{1}\subset\dots
and
x_{0}\supseteq x_{1}\supseteq\dots
of elements in
[\mathbb{N}]^{<\mathbb{N}}
and
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mathbb{N}}
respectively, such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

•

\max s_{n}<\min x_{n}
,

•

s_{n+1}=s_{n}\cup\{\min x_{n}\}
,

•

for every
y,z\in[s_{n}\cup x_{n}]{N}
almost disjoint, if
f_{n}(y)=f_{n}(z)
then
f_{n}(y)\in C_{f_{n},x_{n}}

by induction on
n
. Indeed, let
s_{-1}=\emptyset
and
x_{-1}=\mathbb{N}
, given
s_{n-1},x_{n-1}
, let
s_{n}=s_{n-1}\cup\{\min x_{n-1}\}
. Using Lemma
2.5
for every
s\subseteq s_{n}
, we can find an
x_{n}\subseteq x_{n-1}
that satisfies all the above properties.

Now let
x=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}s_{n}
,
C=\bigcup_{n}C_{f_{n},x_{n}}
, and assume that
y,z\in[x]{N}
are almost disjoint and
f_{n}(y)=f_{n}(z)
. Then
y,z\in[s_{n}\cup x_{n}]{N}
, and hence
f_{n}(y)\in C_{f_{n},x_{n}}\subseteq C
, by the third property of the induction.
∎
