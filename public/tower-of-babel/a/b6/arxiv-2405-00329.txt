Abstract
. Synthetic data are an attractive concept to enable privacy in data sharing. A fundamental question is how similar the privacy-preserving synthetic data are compared to the true data. Using metric privacy, an effective generalization of differential privacy beyond the discrete setting, we raise the problem of characterizing the optimal privacy-accuracy tradeoff by the metric geometry of the underlying space. We provide a partial solution to this problem in terms of the “entropic scale”, a quantity that captures the multiscale geometry of a metric space via the behavior of its packing numbers. We illustrate the applicability of our privacy-accuracy tradeoff framework via a diverse set of examples of metric spaces.



1.
Introduction

A compelling approach to enable privacy in data sharing is based on the concept
of synthetic data
[
2
]
. The goal of synthetic data is to create a dataset that maintains the statistical properties of the original data while not exposing sensitive information. A fundamental challenge is to derive optimal bounds on the achievable utility
of synthetic data while maintaining privacy. Analyzing this privacy-utility tradeoff is the goal of this paper.

There are numerous versions of this problem, depending on the specific notion of privacy and the choice of the utility metric.
A popular and rigorous framework to define and quantify privacy is differential privacy. While differential privacy is a concept of the discrete world (where datasets can differ in a single element), it is often necessary to have more flexibility in the type of input data. Metric privacy, introduced in
[
6
]
, provides this flexibility, as it generalizes differential privacy beyond the discrete setting.
For that reason we will adopt metric privacy as our notion of privacy in this paper.
Metric privacy, tailored to the setting of synthetic data, is defined as follows:

Definition 1.1

(Metric privacy)
.

Let
(Z,\rho_{1})
be a metric space and
\alpha>0
.
A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\to Z

is called
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private
if, for any inputs
x,x^{\prime}\in Z
we have

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)\in S\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}}{\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime})\in S\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}}\leq\exp\left(\alpha\,\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime})\right).

(1.1)

Metric privacy has been utilized in various applications, such as in location privacy
[

and privacy-preserving machine learning see e.g.
[

[
4
]
. Metric privacy includes classical differential privacy as special case, see e.g. Lemma 4.1 in
[

With our notion of privacy in place, how shall we quantify utility, i.e., measure accuracy?
Naturally, we want to measure how much the privacy-preserving synthetic data
\mathcal{M}(x)
resemble the true data
x
. To that end we choose a (possibly different) metric
\rho_{2}

Definition 1.2

(Optimal accuracy)

Let
(Z,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
be a bimetric space

Problem 1.3

Express the accuracy

1.1.
State of the art

The study of the privacy-accuracy tradeoff for classical differential privacy
has a long history, a detailed review of which is beyond the scope of this paper.
The standard book on DP
[
9
]
contains detailed discussions on achievable rates of accuracy for a given privacy budget.
The paper
[
14
]
proves that for each fixed count query and a given differential
privacy budget, there exists a mechanism that is expected loss-minimizing.
Hardt and Talwar
[
16
]
use methods from convex geometry to determine a nearly optimal tradeoff between privacy and accuracy, where the error is measured by the Euclidean distance between the correct answer and the actual answer of a linear query.
Nikolov, Talwar and Zhang
[
19
]
extend these
results to the case of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy.
In
[
15
]
Gupte and Sundararaja adopt a framework of risk-averse agents to derive a universal
optimality result using a
minimax formulation.
Geng and Viswanath
[
13
]
give an optimal differentially private mechanism for certain query functions under
a general utility-maximization framework.
In
[
18
]
Nikolov derives differentially private mechanisms with optimal worst case sample complexity under mean square average error for statistical queries.

Regarding the privacy-utilty tradeoff for synthetic data, Ullman and Vadhan
[
20
]
prove that (under standard cryptographic assumptions) there is no polynomial-time differentially private algorithm that can generate synthetic Boolean data such that all two-dimensional marginals are close to those of the original Boolean dataset. In
[
3
]
the authors derive an computationally efficient algorithm to construct DP
synthetic data with approximately optimal average error.
The paper
[
4
]
establishes
asymptotically sharp privacy-accuracy bounds for synthetic data for general compact metric spaces.

The privacy-utility tradeoff for metric privacy is less well-understood than
for classical DP, since the achievable accuracy is strongly influenced by the geometry of the space.
In
[
5
]
the tradeoff between location privacy and utility is analyzed by constructing for a given privacy budget a mechanism that minimizes utility loss using linear programming techniques.
In
[
17
]
, the authors consider linear programming-based metric privacy mechanisms that balance the tradeoff between utility and computational complexity. The paper
[
11
]
investigates the privacy-accuracy tradeoff for metric privacy from an information theoretic perspective.

In this paper, we study the optimal privacy-accuracy tradeoff in terms of the geometry of the metric spaces, but we do not account for computational feasibility.

Basic properties of the entropic scale: same metric

Throughout this section, we assume that the two metrics
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\rho

2.1.
Background on packing and covering

Fact 2.1

(Packing implies covering)
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a metric space and
B\subset Z
be any subset. Then any maximal
\varepsilon
-separated subset
\mathcal{N}\subset B
is an
\varepsilon
-cover of
B
, i.e.

\forall x\in B\;\exists y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;\rho(x,y)\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

For contradiction, assume that there exists
x\in B
such that
\rho(x,y)>\varepsilon
for all
y\in\mathcal{N}
. Thus
x\not\in\mathcal{N}
, and
\mathcal{N}\cup\{x\}
is an
\varepsilon
-separated subset of
B
. This contradicts the maximality of
\mathcal{N}
.
∎

Fact 2.2

(Chain rule)
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a metric space and
B\subset Z
be any subset. Then for any
r,s>0
we have

N(B,s)\leq N(B,r)\cdot\sup_{a\in B}N\left(B(a,r),s\right).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{s}\subset B
be any
s
-separated subset. Let
\mathcal{N}_{r}\subset B
be a maximal
r
-separated subset.
Then
B
can be covered by
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{N}_{r}\right\rvert}\leq N(B,r)
balls of radius
r
centered at points in
B
. Each such ball
B(a,r)
contains at most
N\left(B(a,r),s\right)
points from
\mathcal{N}_{s}
. Therefore

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{N}_{s}\right\rvert}\leq N(B,r)\cdot\sup_{a\in B}N\left(B(a,r),s\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

The chain rule yields the following for any
s>0
:

\sup_{x\in Z}N\left(B(x,4s),s\right)\leq\sup_{x\in Z}N\left(B(x,4s),2s\right)\cdot\sup_{y\in Z}N\left(B(y,2s),s\right).

Iterating this bound, we obtain:

Fact 2.3

(Chain rule with many terms)
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a metric space. Then for any
s>0
and
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sup_{x\in Z}N\left(B(x,s2^{k_{0}}),s\right)\leq\prod_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\sup_{x_{k}\in Z}N\left(B(x_{k},s2^{k}),s2^{k-1}\right).

2.2.
Regularity of entropic scale

Proposition 2.4

(Regularity)
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a norm-convex metric space and
\alpha>0
. Then

s(Z,\alpha)\leq s(Z,t\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{t}s(Z,\alpha)\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

The proof is based on the following monotonicity property:

Lemma 2.5

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a norm-convex metric space. Then for all
x\in Z
,
t\in[0,1]
and
r,s>0
, we have

N\left(B(x,r),s\right)\leq N\left(B(x,tr),ts\right).

Proof.

Norm-convexity implies that

B(x,tr)\supset(1-t)x+tB(x,r)
.
Therefore

N\left(B(x,tr),ts\right)\geq N\left((1-t)x+tB(x,r),ts\right)=N\left(B(x,r),s\right).\qed

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

The lower bound is trivial by the definition of entropic scale. To check the upper bound, set
s\coloneqq s(Z,\alpha)
and fix any
x\in Z
. The definition of entropic scale guarantees that

N\left(B(x,r),s\right)\leq e^{\alpha r}\quad\text{for all }r>0.

Using Lemma
2.5
for
s/t
instead of
s
, and then the bound above for
tr
instead of
r
, we obtain

N\left(B(x,r),s/t\right)\leq N\left(B(x,tr),s\right)\leq e^{\alpha\cdot tr}\quad\text{for all }r>0.

By the definition of entropic scale, this yields
s(Z,t\alpha)\leq s/t
. The proof is complete.
∎

2.3.
Elementary lower bounds for entropic scale

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a metric space and
\alpha>0
. Then

\frac{1}{\alpha}\geq 2\diam(Z)\quad\text{implies}\quad s(Z,\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{2}\diam(Z).

Proof.

Assume that the conclusion is false. By the definition of entropic scale, this means that

N\left(B(x,r),\frac{1}{2}\diam(Z)\right)\leq e^{\alpha r}\quad\text{for any $x\in Z$ and $r>0$}.

Let us use this bound for
r=\diam(Z)
. We trivially have
B(x,r)=Z
, and
\alpha r\leq 1/2
holds by assumption. It follows that

N\left(Z,r/2\right)\leq e^{1/2}<2.

This means that the distance between any pair of points in
Z
must be bounded by
r/2
. (Otherwise a pair of
r/2
-separated points
x,y
would make the packing number at least
2
.) But this shows that

\diam(Z)\leq r/2=\diam(Z)/2
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a connected metric space and
\alpha>0
. Then

\frac{1}{\alpha}<2\diam(Z)\quad\text{implies}\quad s(Z,\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{2\alpha}.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists a pair of points
x,y\in Z
satisfying

\rho(x,y)>\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2\alpha}\quad\text{for some }\varepsilon>0.

(2.1)

Assume that the conclusion of the lemma is false. By definition of entropic scale, this means that

N\left(B(x,r),\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)\leq e^{\alpha r}\quad\text{for any }r>0.

Plug
r=2/(3\alpha)
to get

N\left(B\Big(x,\frac{2}{3\alpha}\Big),\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)\leq e^{2/3}<2.

This means that every point
y
in the ball
B(x,2/(3\alpha))
must satisfy
\rho(x,y)\leq 1/(2\alpha)
. (Otherwise a pair of
1/(2\alpha)
-separated points
x,y
would make the packing number at least
2
.) Thus we showed that

B\Big(x,\frac{2}{3\alpha}\Big)=B\Big(x,\frac{1}{2\alpha}\Big).

This means that any ball of the intermediate radius, and in particular the ball
B\coloneqq B(x,\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2\alpha})
, must be closed and open. Moreover, (
2.1
) shows that
y\not\in B
, so
B
is a proper subset of
Z
. This contradicts the connectedness of
Z
.
∎

2.4.
Doubling scale

Fixing
r=2s
in the definition of entropic scale (Definition
1.4
) leads to:

Definition 2.8

(Doubling scale)
.

The
doubling scale
of a metric space
(Z,\rho)
is defined as follows:

\underaccent{\bar}{s}(Z,\alpha)=\inf\left\{s>0\;\Big|\;N\left(B(x,2s),s\right)\leq e^{2\alpha s}\;\forall x\in Z\right\}.

Proposition 2.9

(Entopic vs doubling scale)
.

For any norm-convex metric space
(Z,\rho)
and any
\alpha>0
, we have

\frac{1}{4}s(Z,\alpha)\leq\underaccent{\bar}{s}(Z,\alpha)\leq s(Z,\alpha).

Proof.

The upper bound is trivial.
To prove the lower bound, let
s=\underaccent{\bar}{s}(Z,\alpha)
. Fix any
x\in Z
, and let us bound the packing number

N(r)\coloneqq N\left(B(x,r),s\right)

for each
r>0
. Consider three cases.

1. If
r\in(0,s/2)
, then
N(r)=1
.

2. If
r\in[s/2,2s)
, then the definition of
s
gives

N(r)\leq N(B(x,2s),s)\leq e^{2\alpha s}\leq e^{4\alpha r}.

3. If
r\geq 2s
, then
r\in[s2^{k_{0}-1},s2^{k_{0}})
for some
k_{0}\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
. Then by the chain rule (Fact
2.3
) we have

N(r)\leq\prod_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\sup_{x_{k}\in Z}N\left(B(x_{k},s2^{k}),s2^{k-1}\right).

Using Lemma
2.5
and the definition of
s
, we see that each factor in this product is bounded by
N(B(x,2s),s)\leq e^{2\alpha s}
. Thus

N(r)\leq e^{2\alpha sk_{0}}\leq e^{2\alpha r},

where in the last step we used that
k_{0}\leq 2^{k_{0}-1}
.

Combining all three cases, we conclude that

N(r)\leq e^{4\alpha r}
for any
r>0
. By the definition of entropic scale, this implies
s(Z,4\alpha)\leq s
. By Proposition
2.4
, we get

s(Z,\alpha)\leq 4s(Z,4\alpha)\leq 4s.

In view of the definition of
s
, this completes the proof.
∎

2.5.
Outer scale

Definition 2.10

(Outer scale)
.

The
outer scale
of a metric space
(Z,\rho)
is defined as follows:

\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)=\inf_{\gamma>0}\left\{\gamma+\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,\gamma)\right\}.

Proposition 2.11
.

For any metric space
(Z,\rho)
and any
\alpha>0
, we have

s(Z,\alpha)\leq 2\bar{s}(Z,\alpha).

Proof.

Fix any
\gamma>0
and let
s\coloneqq\gamma+\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,\gamma)
. Fix any
x\in Z
, and let us bound the packing number

N(r)\coloneqq N(B(x,r),2s)

for each
r>0
. Consider two cases.

1. If
r<s
, then
N(r)=1
.

2. If
r\geq s
, then, using that
2s\geq\gamma
and by definition of
s
we have

N(r)\leq N(Z,\gamma)\leq e^{\alpha s}\leq e^{\alpha r}.

Thus
N(r)\leq e^{\alpha r}
for any
r>0
. By the definition of entropic scale, this implies
s(Z,\alpha)\leq 2s
. In view of the definition of
s
, this completes the proof.
∎

For norm-convex spaces, the bound in Proposition
2.11
can be reversed up to a logarithmic factor:

Proposition 2.12
.

For any norm-convex metric space
(Z,\rho)
and any
\alpha>0
, we have

\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)\leq 3s(Z,\alpha)\left[1+\ln\frac{\diam(Z)}{s(Z,\alpha)}\right].

Proof.

Let
s\coloneqq s(Z,\alpha)
and

k_{0}\coloneqq\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{\diam(Z)}{s}\right\rceil.

Then
\diam(Z)\leq s2^{k_{0}}
and thus
Z\subset B(x,s2^{k_{0}})
for any
x\in Z
. Then by chain rule (Fact
2.3
) we have

N(Z,s)\leq\prod_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\sup_{x_{k}\in Z}N\left(B(x_{k},s2^{k}),s2^{k-1}\right).

Using Lemma
2.5
and the definition of
s
, we see that each factor in this product is bounded by
N(B(x,2s),s)\leq e^{2\alpha s}
. Thus

N(Z,s)\leq e^{2\alpha sk_{0}}.

It follows that

s+\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,s)\leq s+2sk_{0}\leq 3s\left[1+\ln\frac{\diam(Z)}{s}\right].

Recall the definitions of
s
and
\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)
to complete the proof.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

The logarithmic factor in Proposition
2.12
cannot be removed in general. For example, let
Z
be the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Euclidean metric, and let
\alpha>2d
. Then, as we will see in Theorem
6.1
,
s(Z,\alpha)\asymp d/\alpha
while it is not hard to check that
\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)\asymp(d/\alpha)\log(\alpha/d)
.

Nevertheless, the logarithmic factor can be removed if the packing numbers satisfy a
doubling condition
:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a norm-convex metric space and let
\alpha>0
. Set
s=s(Z,\alpha)
. Assume that

N(Z,s)\geq N(Z,\kappa s)^{2}\quad\text{for some }\kappa\geq 1.

Then

\frac{s}{2}\leq\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)\leq 2\kappa s.

For the proof, we need:

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a norm-convex metric space and let
\alpha>0
. Set
s=s(Z,\alpha)
. Then

\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,s)\leq\bar{s}(Z,\alpha).

Proof.

Fix any
\gamma>0
and use the chain rule (Fact
2.2
) and the definition of
s
to get

N(Z,s)\leq N(Z,\gamma)\cdot\sup_{z\in Z}N\left(B(z,\gamma),s\right)\leq N(Z,\gamma)\cdot e^{\alpha\gamma}.

Rearranging the terms yields

\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,s)\leq\gamma+\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,\gamma).

Take the infimum over
\gamma>0
to complete the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.14
.

The lower bound was proved in Proposition
2.11
, so it is enough to prove the upper bound. Using first the doubling condition, then Lemma
2.15
, and finally the definition of the outer scale, we get

\frac{2}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,\kappa s)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,s)\leq\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)\leq\kappa s+\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,\kappa s).

Rearranging the terms, we get

\kappa s+\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln N(Z,\kappa s)\leq 2\kappa s.

By the definition of the outer scale, this yields
\bar{s}(Z,\alpha)\leq 2\kappa s
.
The proposition is proved.
∎

3.
Lower bounds on accuracy

Theorem 3.1
.

For any bimetric space
(Z,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and any
\alpha>0
, we have

A(Z,\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{8}s(Z,2\alpha).

Proof.

Fix a point
x\in Z
and numbers
r,s>0
.
By the definition of packing numbers, there exists a subset
S(x,r)\subset B_{1}(x,r)
that is
(8s)
-separated in the metric
\rho_{2}
and has cardinality

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(x,r)\right\rvert}=N_{2}\left(B_{1}(x,r),8s\right).

The separation condition implies by triangle inequality that all balls
B_{2}(y,4s)
centered at points
y\in S(x,r)
are disjoint. Let
\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\to Z
be a
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private randomized algorithm. We have

1=\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)\in Z\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq\sum_{y\in S(x,r)}\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)\in B_{2}(y,4s)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

Since the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private, we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)\in B_{2}(y,4s)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq e^{-\alpha\rho_{1}(x,y)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(y)\in B_{2}(y,4s)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

Now, for
y\in S(x,r)\subset B_{1}(x,r)
, we have

e^{-\alpha\rho_{1}(x,y)}\geq e^{-\alpha r}
. Furthermore, Markov’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(y)\in B_{2}(y,4s)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),y)\leq 4s\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq\frac{3}{4}

whenever
s\geq\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),y)
.
Combining these bounds, we conclude the following. If

s=\sup_{y\in Z}\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),y),

then

1\geq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(x,r)\right\rvert}\cdot e^{-\alpha r}\cdot\frac{3}{4}.

Rearranging the terms, we get

N_{2}\left(B_{1}(x,r),8s\right)=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S(x,r)\right\rvert}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{4}{3}e^{\alpha r}\right\rfloor\leq e^{2\alpha r}.

By the definition of entropic scale, this yields

s(Z,2\alpha)\leq 8s=8\sup_{y\in Z}\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),y).

Take the infimum over all
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private mechanisms
\mathcal{M}
on both sides to complete the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For any bimetric space
(Z,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and any
\alpha>0
, we have

A(Z,\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{5}s_{\circ}(Z,2\alpha).

Proof.

Fix any
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private mechanism
\mathcal{M}
and any
r>0
. Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left\{\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(x),x)>2e^{\alpha r}s\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2e^{\alpha r}}\quad\text{whenever }s\geq\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(x),x).

Since
\mathcal{M}
is
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private, the probability bound above yields

\mathbb{P}\left\{\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),x)>2e^{\alpha r}s\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{where }r=\rho_{1}(x,y).

On the other hand, Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left\{\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),y)>3s\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{3}\quad\text{whenever }s\geq\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(y),y).

Combining the last two probability bounds by triangle inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left\{\rho_{2}(x,y)>2e^{\alpha r}s+3s\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}<1.

But for fixed
x,y
, the event in the left hand side is deterministic. Thus, the last bound implies that

\rho_{2}(x,y)\leq(2e^{\alpha r}+3)s\leq 5e^{\alpha r}s.

Summarizing, we have shown that

\rho_{2}(x,y)\leq 5e^{\alpha\rho_{1}(x,y)}s\quad\text{ where }s=\sup_{x\in Z}\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(x),x).

Now consider a ball
B_{1}(x,r)
with an arbitrary center
x\in Z
and arbitrary radius
r>0
. Since the
\rho_{1}
-distance between any pair of points in this ball is at most
2r
, it follows that

\diam_{2}(B_{1}(x,r))\leq 5e^{2\alpha r}s.

Since this holds for all
x\in Z
and
r>0
, we proved that

s_{\circ}(Z,2\alpha)\leq 5s=5\sup_{x\in Z}\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(\mathcal{M}(x),x).

Since this holds for any
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private mechanism
\mathcal{M}
, the proof is complete.
∎

4.
Upper bounds on accuracy: same metric

Throughout this section, we assume that the two metrics
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\rho
are the same.

Lemma 4.1

(Exponential mechanism)
.

For any metric space
(Z,\rho)
and
\alpha>0
, we have

A(Z,\alpha)\lesssim s(Z,\alpha/3)+\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Proof.

Let

s\coloneqq s(Z,\alpha/3).

(4.1)

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a maximal
s
-separated subset of
Z
. Define a randomized map
\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\to\mathcal{N}
as follows. Given an input
x\in Z
, output
y\in\mathcal{N}
with probability proportional to
e^{-\alpha\rho(x,y)/2}
. In other words, for each
x\in Z
, we let

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)=y\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Sigma(x)}e^{-\alpha\rho(x,y)/2}\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal{N},

where

\Sigma(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}}e^{-\alpha\rho(x,y)/2}.

(4.2)

It is clear that this mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
(\alpha,\rho)
-metrically private (this follows analogously to establishing differential privacy of the usual exponential mechanism, see
[
9
, Chapter 3.4]
).

To estimate the accuracy of
\mathcal{M}
, we need to fix any input
x\in Z
and bound the expected value of the random variable

A\coloneqq\rho(x,\mathcal{M}(x)).

We start by the trivial bound

\mathbb{E}A\leq 6s+\mathbb{E}A\mathbbm{1}_{\{A>6s\}}

(4.3)

By definition of
\mathcal{M}(x)
, we have

\mathbb{E}A\mathbbm{1}_{\{A>6s\}}=\frac{1}{\Sigma(x)}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;\rho(x,y)>6s}\rho(x,y)\,e^{-\alpha\rho(x,y)/2}.

Let us decompose the set of vectors
y
in this sum according to their distance from
x
as follows:

\left\{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;\rho(x,y)>6s\right\}=\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}_{k}

where

\mathcal{N}_{k}=\left\{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;6s+\frac{k-1}{\alpha}<\rho(x,y)\leq 6s+\frac{k}{\alpha}\right\}.

Thus

\mathbb{E}A\mathbbm{1}_{\{A>6s\}}=\frac{1}{\Sigma(x)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\left(6s+\frac{k}{\alpha}\right)e^{-\alpha\left(6s+(k-1)/\alpha\right)/2}.

Recall that by construction,
\mathcal{N}_{k}
is an
s
-separated subset of the ball
B(x,6s+k/\alpha)
. Thus, due to our choice of
s
in (
4.1
) and by the definition of entropic scale, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{N}_{k}\right\rvert}\leq e^{(\alpha/3)(6s+k/\alpha)}=e^{2\alpha s+k/3}.

(4.4)

Next, let us find a lower bound on
\Sigma
, a quantity we defined in (
4.2
). By definition of
\mathcal{N}
and Fact
2.1
, there exists
y\in\mathcal{N}
that satisfies
\rho(x,y)\leq s
. It follows that

\Sigma(x)\geq e^{-\alpha\rho(x,y)/2}\geq e^{-\alpha s/2}.

(4.5)

Then using (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}A\mathbbm{1}_{\{A>6s\}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\alpha s/2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{2\alpha s+k/3}\left(6s+\frac{k}{\alpha}\right)e^{-3\alpha s-(k-1)/2}

\displaystyle=e^{(1-\alpha s)/2}\left(6s\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-k/6}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}ke^{-k/6}\right)\lesssim e^{-\alpha s/2}\left(s+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right).

Plugging this bound into (
4.3
), we arrive at

\mathbb{E}A\lesssim s+e^{-\alpha s/2}\left(s+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right).

Summarizing, we proved that

A(Z,\alpha)\lesssim s+e^{-\alpha s/2}\left(s+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\quad\text{where }s=s(Z,\alpha/3).

Since
\alpha s\geq 0
, this bound is stronger that than the one announced in the statement of the lemma.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(Z,\rho)
be a connected metric space and
\alpha>0
. Then

A(Z,\alpha)\lesssim s(Z,\alpha/3).

Proof.

Assume first that
3/\alpha\geq 2\diam(Z)
. In this case, we can use a trivial mechanism
\mathcal{M}
that always outputs the same, arbitrarily chosen point in
Z
. This gives accuracy

A(Z,\alpha)\leq\diam(Z,\rho)
, while
Lemma
2.6
yields
s(Z,\alpha/3)\geq\frac{1}{2}\diam(Z)
. The desired bound follows.

Next, assume that
3/\alpha<2\diam(Z)
. In this case,
we can use the exponential mechanism, for which Lemma
4.1
gives accuracy
A(Z,\alpha)\lesssim s(Z,\alpha/3)+1/\alpha
. On the other hand, Lemma
2.7
yields
s(Z,\alpha/3)\geq 3/(2\alpha)
.
Combining these two bounds gives the conclusion.
∎

5.
Upper bound on accuracy: ultrametric

Theorem 5.1

(Ultrametric)
.

let
(Z,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
be a bimetric space such that
\rho_{2}
is an ultrametric. Then for any
\alpha>0
we have

A(Z,\alpha)\lesssim s(Z,\alpha/3)+s_{\circ}(Z,\alpha/7).

The proof of this result will generally follow the proof of Theorem
4.1
, but the exponential mechanism will be “relaxed”.

As before, let

s\coloneqq s(Z,\alpha/3).

(5.1)

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a maximal
s
-separated subset of
Z
in the
\rho_{2}
ultrametric.

For any two points
x,y\in Z
, let
\sigma(x,y)
denote the
\rho_{1}
-distance from
x
to the
\rho_{2}
-ball centered at
y
and with radius
s
, that is

\sigma(x,y)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\rho_{1}(x,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;v\in B_{2}(y,s)\right\}.

Define a randomized map
\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\to\mathcal{N}
as follows. Given an input
x\in Z
, output
y\in\mathcal{N}
with probability proportional to
e^{-\alpha\sigma(x,y)/2}
. In other words, for each
x\in Z
, we let

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)=y\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Sigma(x)}e^{-\alpha\sigma(x,y)/2}\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal{N},

(5.2)

where

\Sigma(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}}e^{-\alpha\sigma(x,y)/2}.

Since
\sigma(x^{\prime},y)\leq\sigma(x,y)+\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime},y\in Z
, the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
(\alpha,\rho_{1})
-metrically private (this follows analogously to proving differential privacy of the usual exponential mechanism, see
[
9
, Chapter 3.4]
).

By the definition of
\mathcal{N}
and Fact
2.1
, for each
x\in Z
there exists
y\in\mathcal{N}
that satisfies
\rho_{2}(x,y)\leq s
. It follows that
\sigma(x,y)=0
and thus

\Sigma(x)\geq 1.

(5.3)

We will first estimate the accuracy of
\mathcal{M}
in the “relaxed distance”
\sigma
, and then remove relaxation and transfer the result to the original ultrametric
\rho_{2}
. The following lemma is crucial; it is the only step of the argument where the ultrametric is used.

Lemma 5.2

(Relaxed ball)
.

For any
x\in Z
and
r>0
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\left\{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\sigma(x,y)<r\right\}\right\rvert}\leq N_{2}\left(B_{1}(x,r),s\right)\leq e^{\alpha r/3}.

Proof.

Consider the set

\mathcal{N}_{r}\coloneqq\left\{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\sigma(x,y)<r\right\}.

By the definition of
\sigma
, for each
y\in\mathcal{N}_{r}
there exists
v=v(y)\in Z
that satisfies

\rho_{1}\left(x,v(y)\right)<r\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{2}\left(v(y),y\right)\leq s.

(5.4)

Moreover, the definition of
\mathcal{N}
implies that
\rho_{2}(y,y^{\prime})>s
for any pair of distinct points
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{r}\subset\mathcal{N}
. We can combine this lower bound with the two upper bounds
\rho_{2}\left(v(y),y\right)\leq s
and
\rho_{2}\left(v(y^{\prime}),y^{\prime}\right)\leq s
using the max-triangle inequality for the ultrametic
\rho_{2}
. This gives

\rho_{2}(\left(v(y),v(y^{\prime})\right)>s.

(5.5)

One consequence of this bound is that the map
y\mapsto v(y)
is injective, so

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{N}_{r}\right\rvert}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert v(\mathcal{N}_{r})\right\rvert}.

(5.6)

Moreover, the set
v(\mathcal{N}_{r})
is
s
-separated in ultrametric
\rho_{2}
by (
5.5
) and is contained in the ball
B_{1}(x,r)
by the first inequality in (
5.4
). Thus,

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert v(\mathcal{N}_{r})\right\rvert}\leq N_{2}\left(B_{1}(x,r),s\right).

Combining this with (
5.6
), we obtain the first bound in the statement of the lemma.
The second bound follows from the definition of
s
in (
5.1
).
∎

Next we analyze the accuracy of the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
in the relaxed distance
\sigma
. We shall prove that

\mathbb{E}\,\sigma(x,\mathcal{M}(x))\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha}\quad\text{for any }x\in Z.

In fact we can prove a stronger tail bound, from which the bound on expectation follows immediately:

Lemma 5.3

(Relaxed accuracy)
.

For any
x\in Z
and
r>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma(x,\mathcal{M}(x))\geq r\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\lesssim e^{-\alpha r/6}.

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma(x,\mathcal{M}(x))\in[r,r+1/\alpha)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\sigma(x,y)\in[r,r+1/\alpha)}\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathcal{M}(x)=y\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

By the definition of mechanism
\mathcal{M}
in (
5.2
) and since
\Sigma(x)\geq 1
by (
5.3
), each term of the sum above is bounded by
e^{-\alpha\sigma(x,y)/2}\leq e^{-\alpha r/2}
. By Lemma
5.2
, the number of terms in the sum is bounded by

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\left\{y\in\mathcal{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\sigma(x,y)<r+1/a\right\}\right\rvert}\leq e^{\alpha(r+1/\alpha)/3}\leq 2e^{\alpha r/3}.

Thus, we have proved that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma(x,\mathcal{M}(x))\in[r,r+1/\alpha)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq e^{-\alpha r/2}\cdot 2e^{\alpha r/3}=2e^{-\alpha r/6}

for any
r>0
.
Using the above bound for
r+k/\alpha
instead of
r
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma(x,\mathcal{M}(x))\geq r\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma(x,\mathcal{M}(x))\in\left[r+\frac{k}{\alpha},r+\frac{k+1}{\alpha}\right)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2e^{-\alpha(r+k/\alpha)/6}=2e^{-\alpha r/6}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-k/6}\lesssim e^{-\alpha r/6}.

∎

The next lemma allows one to transfer accuracy bounds from the relaxed distance
\sigma
to the original ultrametric
\rho_{2}
.

Lemma 5.4

(Unrelaxation)
.

Let
x,y\in Z
and
r>0
. Then

\sigma(x,y)<r\quad\text{implies}\quad\rho_{2}(x,y)\leq s+e^{\alpha r/7}s_{\circ}

where
s=s(Z,\alpha/3)
and
s_{\circ}=s_{\circ}(Z,\alpha/7)
.

Proof.

If
\sigma(x,y)<r
, then by the definition of
\sigma
there exists
v\in Z
such that

\rho_{1}(x,v)<r\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{2}(v,y)\leq s.

(5.7)

Using the first inequality in (
5.7
) and then the definition of
s_{\circ}
, we get

\rho_{2}(x,v)\leq\diam_{2}\left(B_{1}(x,r)\right)\leq e^{\alpha r/7}s_{\circ}.

To complete the proof, combine this with the second inequality in (
5.7
) by triangle inequality.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Combining Lemmas
5.4
and
5.3
, we get for any
x\in Z
and
r>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left\{\rho_{2}(x,M(x))>s+e^{\alpha r/7}s_{\circ}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma(x,M(x))\geq r\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\lesssim e^{-\alpha r/6}.

From this one can easily conclude that

\mathbb{E}\rho_{2}(x,M(x))\lesssim s+s_{\circ}.

The theorem is proved.
∎

The result below is quite standard. It can be derived from the

]
.

Theorem 6.1

(Unit ball of a normed space)
.

Let
Z
be the closed unit ball of a
d
-dimensional normed space
X
with the induced metric. Then for any
\alpha>0
we have

A(Z,\alpha)\asymp s(Z,\alpha)\asymp\min\left(\frac{d}{\alpha},1\right).

Proof.

According to Theorem
1.5
, it is enough to prove the bound on the entropic scale
s(Z,\alpha)
.
Furthermore, by Proposition
2.9
, it is enough to prove the same bound for the doubling scale
\underaccent{\bar}{s}(Z,\alpha)
.

If
B_{X}(x,r)
and
B_{Z}(x,r)
denote the balls of
X
and
Z
centered at
x
and with radius

, then by the definition of
Z
we have

B_{Z}(x,r)=B_{X}(x,r)\cap B_{X}(0,1).

The rest of the argument is based on standard entropy bounds (e.g. see
[
22
, Chapter 5]
), namely

2^{d}\leq N\left(B_{X}(x,2),1\right)\leq 5^{d}\quad\text{for any }x\in X

To prove the upper bound on doubling scale, note that

N\left(B_{Z}(x,2s),s\right)\leq N\left(B_{X}(x,2s),s\right)=N\left(B_{X}(0,2),1\right)\leq 5^{d}\leq e^{2\alpha s}

for any

, and

N\left(B_{Z}(x,2s),s\right)\leq N\left(B_{X}(0,1),s\right)=1\leq e^{2\alpha s}

for any

. Combining the two bounds yields

\underaccent{\bar}{s}(Z,\alpha)\leq\min\left(\frac{d}{\alpha},2\right).

To prove the lower bound on the doubling scale, note that for any

we have

N\left(B_{Z}(0,2s),s\right)=N\left(B_{X}(0,2s),s\right)=N\left(B_{X}(0,2),1\right)\geq 2^{d}>e^{2\alpha s}.

This yields

\underaccent{\bar}{s}(Z,\alpha)>\frac{1}{10}\min\left(\frac{d}{\alpha},1\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 6.2

(Probability measures with Wasserstein metric)
.

Consider the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
equipped with the
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{\infty}
metric.
Let
Z
be the set of all probability measures on this cube, equipped with the
1
-Wasserstein metric. Then for any
\alpha>0
we have

A(Z,\alpha)\asymp s(Z,\alpha)\asymp\min\left(\alpha^{-\frac{1}{d+1}},1\right).

Proof.

Observe that
Z
is a norm-convex space, since
Z
is a convex subset of the normed space of all signed measures

Appendix A
The metric entropy of the set of all probability measures

Lemma A.1
.

Let
0<\gamma<1
. The
\gamma
-covering number of the set
Z
of all probability measures on
[0,1]^{d}
with respect to the
1
-Wasserstein distance is at most
\exp((\frac{5}{\gamma})^{d})
.

Proof.

Let
n=\lceil\frac{2}{\gamma}\rceil
and
S=\{\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{n}{n}\}^{d}
. We denote

\Lambda=\left\{\sum_{x\in S}a_{x}\delta_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\sum_{x\in S}a_{x}=1,\;a_{x}\in\left\{\frac{0}{|S|},\frac{1}{|S|},\ldots,\frac{|S|}{|S|}\right\}\;\forall x\in S\right\}.

Then
|\Lambda|
coincides with the number of ways to distribute
|S|
unlabeled balls into
|S|
bins. So

|\Lambda|=\binom{2|S|-1}{|S|-1}\leq 4^{|S|}=\exp(n^{d}\ln 4)\leq\exp\left(\left(\frac{5}{\gamma}\right)^{d}\right).

It remains to show that every probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]^{d}
has 1-Wasserstein distance at most
\frac{2}{n}
from a measure in
\Lambda
. First in view of the set
S
, there is a probability measure
\nu
supported on
S
such that
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq\frac{1}{n}
. We now show that
\nu
has 1-Wasserstein distance at most
\frac{1}{n}
from a measure in
\Lambda
.

By induction on
d
, one can construct an enumeration
x_{1},\ldots,x_{|S|}
of
S
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{|S|-1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq n^{d-1}-\frac{1}{n}\leq n^{d-1}.

In other words, a “travelling salesman” can visit every point in
S
by travelling a total distance of at most
n^{d-1}
.

We now construct a measure in
\Lambda
based on the measure
\nu
as follows. Write
\nu(\{x_{1}\})=\frac{m_{1}+\omega_{1}}{|S|}
where
m_{1}\in\{0,\ldots,|S|-1\}
and
0\leq\omega_{1}\leq 1
. The salesman moves the weight
\frac{\omega_{1}}{|S|}
from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
.

Next write
\nu(\{x_{2}\})+\frac{\omega_{1}}{|S|}=\frac{m_{2}+\omega_{2}}{|S|}
where
m_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,|S|-1\}
and
0\leq\omega_{2}\leq 1
. The salesman moves the weight
\frac{\omega_{2}}{|S|}
from
x_{2}
to
x_{3}
. Continue until the last step: the salesman moves the weight
\frac{\omega_{|S|-1}}{|S|}
from
x_{|S|-1}
to
x_{|S|}
. Because all the probabilities sum up to
1
, the new weight of
x_{|S|}
must be an integer multiple of
\frac{1}{|S|}
. So after moving all the weights, the new probability measure is in
\Lambda
.

Finally let us sum up the weights times the distances moved. We have

\sum_{k=1}^{|S|-1}\frac{\omega_{k}}{|S|}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{|S|-1}\frac{1}{|S|}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{n^{d-1}}{|S|}=\frac{1}{n}.\qed
