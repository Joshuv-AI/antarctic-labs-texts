Wenhao Tang, Daniel Hillerström, James McKinna, Michel Steuwer, Ornela Dardha, Rongxiao Fu, Sam Lindley

Abstract
. Structural subtyping and parametric polymorphism provide similar flexibility and reusability to programmers. For example, both features enable the programmer to provide a wider record as an argument to a function that expects a narrower one. However, the means by which they do so differs substantially, and the precise details of the relationship between them exists, at best, as folklore in literature. In this paper, we systematically study the relative expressive power of structural subtyping and parametric polymorphism. We focus our investigation on establishing the extent to which parametric polymorphism, in the form of row and presence polymorphism, can encode structural subtyping for variant and record types. We base our study on various Church-style λ \lambda -calculi extended with records and variants, different forms of structural subtyping, and row and presence polymorphism. We characterise expressiveness by exhibiting compositional translations between calculi. For each translation we prove a type preservation and operational correspondence result. We also prove a number of non-existence results. By imposing restrictions on both source and target types, we reveal further subtleties in the expressiveness landscape, the restrictions enabling otherwise impossible translations to be defined. More specifically, we prove that full subtyping cannot be encoded via polymorphism, but we show that several restricted forms of subtyping can be encoded via particular forms of polymorphism.

Introduction

Subtyping and parametric polymorphism offer two distinct means for
writing modular and reusable code. Subtyping
allows one value to be substituted for another provided that the type
of the former is a subtype of that of the
latter
(
Reynolds 1980
;
Cardelli 1988
)
. Parametric polymorphism allows
functions to be defined generically over arbitrary
types
(
Girard 1972
;
Reynolds 1974
)
.

There are two main approaches to
syntactic
subtyping: nominal
subtyping
(
Birtwistle et al. 1979
)
and structural
subtyping
(
Cardelli and Wegner 1985
;
Cardelli 1988
;
Cardelli 1984
)
. The former
defines a subtyping relation as a collection of explicit constraints
between named types. The latter defines a subtyping relation
inductively over the structure of types.
This paper is concerned with the latter.
For programming languages with variant types (constructor-labelled
sums) and record types (field-labelled products) it is natural
to define a notion of structural subtyping.
We may always treat a variant with a collection of constructors as a
variant with an
extended
collection of constructors (i.e.,
variant subtyping is covariant).
Dually, we may treat a record with a collection of fields as a record
with a
restricted
collection of those fields (i.e., record
subtyping is contravariant).

We can implement similar functionality to record and variant subtyping
using
row polymorphism

(
Wand 1987
;
Rémy 1994
)
.
A
row
is a mapping from labels to types and is thus a common
ingredient for defining both variants and records.
Row polymorphism is a form of parametric polymorphism that allows us
to abstract over the extension of a row.
Intuitively, by abstracting over the possible extension of a variant
or record we can simulate the act of substitution realised by
structural subtyping.
Such intuitions are folklore, but pinning them down turns out to be
surprisingly subtle.
In this paper we make them precise by way of translations between a
series of different core calculi enjoying type preservation and
operational correspondence results as well as non-existence results.
We show that though folklore intuitions are to some extent correct,
exactly how they manifest in practice is remarkably dependent on what
assumptions we make, and much more nuanced than we anticipated.
We believe that our results are not just of theoretical interest.
It is important to carefully analyse and characterise the relative
expressive power of different but related features to understand the
extent to which they overlap.

To be clear, there is plenty of other work that hinges on inducing a
subtyping relation based on generalisation (i.e., polymorphism) — and
indeed this is the basis for principal types in Hindley-Milner type
inference — but this paper is about something quite different,
namely encoding prior notions of structural subtyping using
polymorphism. In short, principal types concern polymorphism as
subtyping, whereas this paper concerns subtyping as polymorphism.

In order to distil the features we are interested in down to their
essence and eliminate the interference on the expressive power of
other language features (such as higher-order store), we take plain

Church 1940
-style call-by-name simply-typed

\lambda
-calculus (
\lambda
) as our starting point and consider the
relative expressive power of minimal extensions in turn.
We begin by insisting on writing explicit upcasts, type abstractions,
and type applications in order to expose structural subtyping and
parametric polymorphism at the term level.
Later we also consider ML-style calculi, enabling new expressiveness
results by exploiting the type inference for rank-1 polymorphism.
For the dynamic semantics, we focus on the reduction theory generated
from the
\beta
-rules, adding further
\beta
-rules for each term
constructor and upcast rules for witnessing subtyping.

First we extend the simply-typed
\lambda
-calculus with variants
(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}
), which we then further augment with
simple subtyping

(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
) that only considers the subtyping relation shallowly on
variant and record constructors (width subtyping), and (higher-rank)
row polymorphism (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho}
), respectively.
Dually, we extend the simply-typed
\lambda
-calculus with records
(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}
), which we then further augment with simple subtyping
(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}
) and (higher-rank)
presence polymorphism

(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\theta}
), respectively.
Presence polymorphism
(
Rémy 1994
)
is a kind of dual to row
polymorphism that allows us to abstract over which fields are present
or absent from a record independently of their potential types,
supporting a restriction of a collection of record fields, similarly
to record subtyping.
We then consider richer extensions with strictly covariant subtyping
(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
,
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
), which propagates the subtyping relation
through strictly covariant positions, and full subtyping
(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
,
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
), which propagates the subtyping
relation through any positions.
We also consider target languages with both row and presence
polymorphism (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho\theta}
,
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho\theta}
).
Our initial investigations make essential use of higher-rank
polymorphism.
Subsequently, we consider ML-like calculi with rank-1 row or presence
polymorphism (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
,
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\theta 1}
,
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho 1}
,

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\theta 1}
), which admit Hindley-Milner type inference

(
Damas and Milner 1982
)
without requirements of type annotations or
explicit type abstractions and applications.
The focus on rank-1 polymorphism demands a similar restriction to the
calculi with subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
,

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
,
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
,

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
), which constrains the positions where records
and variants can appear in types.

In this paper, we will consider only correspondences expressed as

compositional translations
inductively defined on language
constructs following
Felleisen 1991
.
In order to give a refined characterisation of expressiveness and
usability of the type systems of different calculi, we make use of two
orthogonal notions of
local
and
type-only
translations.

•

A
local
translation restricts which features are
translated in a non-trivial way. It provides non-trivial
translations only of constructs of interest (e.g., record types,
record construction and destruction, when considering record
subtyping), and is homomorphic on other constructs; a

global
translation may allow any construct to have a
non-trivial translation.

•

A
type-only
translation restricts which features a
translation can use in the target language. Every term must
translate to itself modulo constructs that serve only to
manipulate types (e.g., type abstraction and application); a

term-involved
translation has no such restriction.

Local translations capture the intuition that a feature can be
expressed locally as a macro rather than having to be implemented by
globally changing an entire program
(
Felleisen 1991
)
.
Type-only translations capture the intuition that a feature can be
expressed solely by adding or removing type manipulation operations
(such as upcasts, type abstraction, and type application) in terms,
thereby enabling a more precise comparison between the expressiveness
of different type system features.

This paper gives a
precise account of the relationship between
subtyping and polymorphism for records and variants
.
We present relative expressiveness results by way of a series of
translations between calculi, type preservation proofs,
operational correspondence proofs, and non-existence proofs.

Examples

To illustrate the relative expressive power of subtyping and
polymorphism for variants and records with a range of extensions, we
give a collection of examples. These cover the intuition behind the

2.1.
Simple Variant Subtyping as Row Polymorphism

We begin with variant types.
Consider the following function.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getAge}=\lambda x^{[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]}.~\mathbf{case}\ x\ \{\mathsf{Age}\ y\mapsto y;\mathsf{Year}\ y\mapsto 2023-y\}\\
\end{array}\end{array}

The variant type
[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]
denotes the type of
variants with two constructors
\mathsf{Age}
and
\mathsf{Year}
each containing an

\mathsf{Int}
.
We cannot directly apply
\mathtt{getAge}
to the following variant

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{year}=(\mathsf{Year}\ 1984)^{[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]}\\
\end{array}\end{array}

as
\mathtt{year}
and
x
have different types.
With simple variant subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
) which considers subtyping
shallowly on variants, we can upcast
\mathtt{year}:[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]
to the supertype
[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]
which has more labels.
This makes intuitive sense, as it is always safe to treat a variant
with fewer constructors (
\mathsf{Year}
in this case) as one with more
constructors (
\mathsf{Age}
and
\mathsf{Year}
in this case).

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getAge}\ (\mathtt{year}\vartriangleright[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}])\\
\end{array}\end{array}

One advantage of subtyping is reusability: by upcasting we can apply
the same
\mathtt{getAge}
function to any value whose type is a subtype
of
[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{age}=(\mathsf{Age}\ 9)^{[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}]}\\
&\mathtt{getAge}\ (\mathtt{age}\vartriangleright[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}])\\
\end{array}\end{array}

In a language without subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}
), we can simulate applying

\mathtt{getAge}
to
\mathtt{year}
by first deconstructing the variant
using
\mathbf{case}
and then reconstructing it at the appropriate type — a
kind of generalised
\eta
-expansion on variants.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getAge}\ (\mathbf{case}\ \mathtt{year}\ \{\mathsf{Year}\ y\mapsto(\mathsf{Year}\ y)^{[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]}\})\end{array}\end{array}

This is the essence of the translation

2.2.
Simple Record Subtyping as Presence Polymorphism

Now, we consider record types, through the following function.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}=\lambda x^{\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle}.~(x.\mathsf{Name})\end{array}\end{array}

The record type
\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle
denotes the type of records
with a single field
\mathsf{Name}
containing a string. We cannot directly
apply
\mathtt{getName}
to the following record

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{alice}=\langle\mathsf{Name}=\text{\textquotedbl Alice\textquotedbl};\mathsf{Age}=9\rangle\end{array}\end{array}

as the types of
\mathtt{alice}
and
x
do not match. With simple record
subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}
), we can upcast
\mathtt{alice}:\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle
to the supertype

\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle
.
It is intuitive to treat a record with more fields (
\mathsf{Name}
and

\mathsf{Age}
) as a record with fewer fields (only
\mathsf{Name}
in this case).

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}\ (\mathtt{alice}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle)\end{array}\end{array}

Similarly to variant subtyping, we can reuse
\mathtt{getName}
on
records of different subtypes.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{bob}=\langle\mathsf{Name}=\text{\textquotedbl Bob\textquotedbl};\mathsf{Year}=1984\rangle\\
&\mathtt{getName}\ (\mathtt{bob}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle)\end{array}\end{array}

In a language without subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}
), we can first deconstruct
the record by projection and then reconstruct it with only the
required fields, similarly to the generalised
\eta
-expansion of
records.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}\ \langle\mathsf{Name}=\mathtt{alice}.\mathsf{Name}\rangle\end{array}\end{array}

This is the essence of the local term-involved translation

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}=\Lambda\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.~\langle\mathsf{Name}=\text{\textquotedbl Alice\textquotedbl};\mathsf{Age}=9\rangle^{\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{1}}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}^{\theta_{2}}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle}\\

The presence variables

and

can be substituted

For a general translation, we must make the parameter type of

\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}=\lambda x^{\forall\theta.\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta}:\mathsf{String}\rangle}.~((x\ \bullet).\mathsf{Name})\\
&\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}\ (\Lambda\theta.~\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}\ \theta\ \circ)\end{array}\end{array}

This is the essence of the local type-only translation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\theta}}

in

Section

4.4
.
The duality between variants and records is reflected by the need for
dual kinds of polymorphism, namely row and presence
polymorphism, which can extend or shrink rows, respectively.

2.3.
Exploiting Contravariance

We have now seen how to encode simple variant subtyping as row
polymorphism and simple record subtyping as presence
polymorphism. These encodings embody the intuition that row
polymorphism supports extending rows and presence polymorphism
supports shrinking rows.
However, presence polymorphism is typically treated as an optional
extra for row typing. For instance,
Rémy 1994
uses row
polymorphism for both record and variant types, and introduces
presence polymorphism only to support record extension and default
cases (which fall outside the scope of our current investigation).

This naturally raises the question of whether we can encode simple
record subtyping using row polymorphism alone.
More generally, given the duality between records and variants, can we
swap the forms of polymorphism used by the above translations?

Though row polymorphism enables extending rows and what upcasting does
on record types is to remove labels, we can simulate the same
behaviour by extending record types that appear in contravariant
positions in a type.
The duality between row and presence polymorphism can be reconciled by
way of the duality between covariant and contravariant positions.

Let us revisit our
\mathtt{getName}\ \mathtt{alice}
example, which we
previously encoded using polymorphism.
With row polymorphism (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho}
), we can give the function a row
polymorphic type where the row variable appears in the record type of
the function parameter.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}_{\text{✗}}=\Lambda\rho.\lambda x^{\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\rho\rangle}.~(x.\mathsf{Name})\\
\end{array}\end{array}

Now in order to apply
\mathtt{getName}_{\text{✗}}
to
\mathtt{alice}
, we
simply instantiate
\rho
with
(\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int})
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}_{\text{✗}}\ (\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int})\ \mathtt{alice}\end{array}\end{array}

Though the above example suggests a translation which only introduces
type abstractions and type applications, the idea does not extend to a
general composable translation.
Intuitively, the main problem is that in general we cannot know which
type should be used for instantiation (
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
in this case) in
a compositional type-only translation, which is only allowed to use
the type of
\mathtt{getName}
and
\mathtt{alice}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle
. These tell us nothing about
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
.

In fact, a much stronger result holds. In

Section

4.5
, we prove that there exists no
type-only encoding of simple record subtyping into row polymorphism
(

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho}}

), and dually for variant types
with presence polymorphism (

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\theta}}

).

2.4.
Full Subtyping as Rank-1 Polymorphism

The kind of translation sought in
Section

2.3
cannot
be type-only, as it would require us to know the type used for
instantiation.
A natural question is whether type inference can provide the type.

In order to support decidable, sound, and complete type inference, we
consider a target calculus with rank-1 polymorphism (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
)
and Hindley-Milner type inference.
Now the
\mathtt{getName}\ \mathtt{alice}
example type checks without an
explicit upcast or type application.

2
2

2

Actually, the principal type of
\mathtt{getName}
should be
\forall\alpha\,\rho.\langle\mathsf{Name}:\alpha;\rho\rangle\to\alpha
. We ignore value type
variables for simplicity.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}=\lambda x.~(x.\mathsf{Name})&:\forall\rho.\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\rho\rangle\to\mathsf{String}\\
&\mathtt{alice}\phantom{xx}=\langle\mathsf{Name}=\text{\textquotedbl Alice\textquotedbl};\mathsf{Age}=9\rangle&:\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\\
&\mathtt{getName}\ \mathtt{alice}&:\mathsf{String}\end{array}\end{array}

Type inference automatically infers a polymorphic type for

\mathtt{getName}
, and instantiates the variable
\rho
with

\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
.
This observation hints to us that we might encode terms with explicit
record upcasts in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
by simply erasing all upcasts (and
type annotations, given that we have type inference).
The global nature of erasure implies that it also works for full
subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
) which lifts the width subtyping of rows to
any type by propagating the subtyping relation to the components of
type constructors. For instance, the following function upcast using
full subtyping is also translated into
\mathtt{getName}\ \mathtt{alice}
,
simply by erasing the upcast.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&(\mathtt{getName}\vartriangleright(\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\to\mathsf{String}))\ \mathtt{alice}\\
\end{array}\end{array}

Thus far, the erasure translation appears to work well even for full
subtyping. Does it have any limitations? Yes, we must restrict the
target language to rank-1 polymorphism, which can only generalise
let-bound terms.
The type check would fail if we were to bind
\mathtt{getName}
via

\lambda
-abstraction and then use it at different record types. For
instance, consider the following function which concatenates two names
using the
+\!\!+
operator and is applied to
\mathtt{getName}
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&(\lambda f^{\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\to\mathsf{String}}.~f\ (\mathtt{alice}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle)+\!\!+\ f\ (\mathtt{bob}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle))\ \mathtt{getName}\end{array}\end{array}

The erasure of it is

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&(\lambda f.~f\ \mathtt{alice}+\!\!+\ f\ \mathtt{bob})\ \mathtt{getName}\end{array}\end{array}

which is not well-typed as
f
can only have a monomorphic function
type, whose parameter type cannot unify with both

\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle
and

\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle
.

In order to avoid such problems, we will define an erasure translation
on a restricted subcalculus of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
.
The key idea is to give row-polymorphic types for record manipulation
functions such as
\mathtt{getName}
.
However, the above function
takes a record manipulation function of type

\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\to\mathsf{String}
as a parameter, which cannot be
polymorphic as we only have rank-1 polymorphism.
Inspired by the notion of rank-
n
polymorphism, we say that a type
has
rank-
n
records
, if
no path from the root of the type (seen as an abstract syntax tree)
to a record type passes to the left of
n
or more arrows.
We define the translation only on the subcalculus

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in which all types have
rank-2 records.

Such an erasure translation underlies the local type-only translation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}}

.

We obtain a similar result for presence polymorphism.
With presence polymorphism, we can make all records
presence-polymorphic (similar to the translation in

Section

2.2
), instead of making all
record manipulation functions row-polymorphic.
For instance, we can infer the following types for the

\mathtt{getName}\ \mathtt{alice}
example.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName}=\lambda x.~(x.\mathsf{Name})&:\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\to\mathsf{String}\\
&\mathtt{alice}\phantom{xx}=\langle\mathsf{Name}=\text{\textquotedbl Alice\textquotedbl};\mathsf{Age}=9\rangle&:\forall\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{1}}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}^{\theta_{2}}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\\
&\mathtt{getName}\ \mathtt{alice}&:\mathsf{String}\end{array}\end{array}

Consequently, records should appear only in positions that can be
generalised with rank-1 polymorphism, which can be ensured by
restricting
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
to the subcalculus
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in
which all types have rank-1 records.
We give a local type-only translation:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\theta 1}}

.

For variants, we can also define the notion of
rank-
n

variants
similarly.
Dually to records, we can either make all variants be row-polymorphic
(similar to the translation in

Section

2.1
) and require types to have
rank-1 variants (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
), or make all variant
manipulation functions be presence-polymorphic and require types to
have rank-2 variants (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
).
For instance, we can make the
\mathtt{getAge}
function
presence-polymorphic.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getAge}=\lambda x.~\mathbf{case}\ x\ \{\mathsf{Age}\ y\mapsto y;\mathsf{Year}\ y\mapsto 2023-y\}:\forall\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.{[\mathsf{Age}^{\theta_{1}}\!:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}^{\theta_{2}}\!:\mathsf{Int}]}\to\mathsf{Int}\\
&\mathtt{year}\phantom{xx}=\mathsf{Year}\ 1984:[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}]\\
&\mathtt{getAge}\ \mathtt{year}\end{array}\end{array}

We give two type-only encodings of full variant subtyping:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho 1}}

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\theta 1}}

.

Section

6
discusses in detail the four erasure
translations from full subtyping to rank-1 polymorphism with type
inference.

2.5.
Strictly Covariant Record Subtyping as Presence Polymorphism

The encodings of full subtyping discussed in
Section

2.4

impose restrictions on types in the source language and rely heavily
on type-inference.
We now consider to what extent we can support a richer form of
subtyping than simple subtyping, if we turn our attention to target
calculi with higher-rank polymorphism and no type inference.

One complication of extending simple subtyping to full subtyping is
that if we permit propagation through contravariant positions, then
the subtyping order is reversed.
To avoid this scenario, we first consider
strictly covariant
subtyping
relation derived by only propagating simple subtyping
through strictly covariant positions (i.e. never to the left of any
arrow).
For example, the upcast
\mathtt{getName}\vartriangleright(\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\to\mathsf{String})
in

Section

2.4
is ruled out.
We write
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
for our calculus with strictly covariant record
subtyping.

Consider the function
\mathtt{getChildName}
returning the name of the
child of a person.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getChildName}=\lambda x^{\langle\mathsf{Child}:\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\rangle}.~\mathtt{getName}\ (x.\mathsf{Child})\end{array}\end{array}

We can apply
\mathtt{getChildName}
to
\mathtt{carol}
who has a daughter

\mathtt{alice}
with the strictly covariant subtyping relation

\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Child}:\langle\mathsf{Name}\!:\!\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}\!:\!\mathsf{Int}\rangle\rangle\leqslant\langle\mathsf{Child}:\langle\mathsf{Name}\!:\!\mathsf{String}\rangle\rangle
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{carol}=\langle\mathsf{Name}=\text{\textquotedbl Carol\textquotedbl};\mathsf{Child}=\mathtt{alice}\rangle\\
&\mathtt{getChildName}\ (\mathtt{carol}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Child}:\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\rangle)\end{array}\end{array}

If we work in a language without subtyping (
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}
), we can still
use
\eta
-expansions instead, by nested deconstruction and
reconstruction.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getChildName}\ \langle\mathsf{Child}=\langle\mathsf{Name}=\mathtt{carol}.\mathsf{Child}.\mathsf{Name}\rangle\rangle\end{array}\end{array}

In general, we can simulate the full subtyping
(not only strictly covariant subtyping)
of both records and
variants using this technique. The nested de- and
re-construction can be reformulated into coercion functions to be more
compositional
(
Breazu-Tannen et al. 1991
)
. In

Section

5.1
, we show the standard local term-involved
translation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}}

formalising
this idea.

However, for type-only encodings, the idea of making every record
presence-polymorphic in
Section

2.2

does not work directly. Following that idea, we would translate

\mathtt{carol}
to

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{carol}_{\text{✗}}=\Lambda\theta_{1}^{\prime}\theta_{2}^{\prime}.~\langle\dots;\mathsf{Child}=\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}\rangle^{\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{1}^{\prime}}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Child}^{\theta_{2}^{\prime}}:\forall\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{1}}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}^{\theta_{2}}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\rangle}\\
\end{array}\end{array}

Then, as
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are abstracted inside a record, we
cannot directly instantiate
\theta_{2}
with
\circ
to remove the

\mathsf{Age}
label without deconstructing the outer record.
However, we can tweak the translation by moving the quantifiers

\forall\theta_{1}\theta_{2}
to the top-level through introducing new
type abstraction and type application, which gives rise to a
translation that is type-only but global.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{carol^{\prime}}=\Lambda\theta_{1}\theta_{2}\theta_{3}\theta_{4}.\langle\dots;\mathsf{Child}=\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}\ \theta_{3}\ \theta_{4}\rangle^{\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{1}}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Child}^{\theta_{2}}:\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{3}}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}^{\theta_{4}}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\rangle}\\
\end{array}\end{array}

Now we can remove the
\mathsf{Name}
of
\mathtt{carol^{\prime}}
and
\mathsf{Age}
of
\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}

by instantiating
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{4}
with
\circ
. As for simple subtyping,
we make the parameter type of
\mathtt{getChildName}
polymorphic, and re-abstract
over
\mathtt{carol^{\prime}}
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getChildName^{\prime}}=\lambda x^{\forall\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.\langle\mathsf{Child}^{\theta_{1}}:\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta_{2}}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\rangle}.~\mathtt{getName}\ ((x\ \bullet\ \bullet).\mathsf{Child})\\
&\mathtt{getChildName^{\prime}}\ (\Lambda\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.~\mathtt{carol^{\prime}}\ \circ\ \theta_{1}\ \theta_{2}\ \circ)\end{array}\end{array}

This is the essence of the global type-only translation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\theta}}

in

Section

5.2
.

2.6.
No Type-Only Encoding of Strictly Covariant Variant Subtyping as Polymorphism

We now consider whether we could exploit hoisting of quantifiers in
order to encode strictly covariant subtyping for variants
(
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
) using row polymorphism.
Interestingly, we will see that this cannot work, thus breaking the
symmetry between the results for records and variants we have seen so
far.
To understand why, consider the following example involving nested
variants.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{data}=(\mathsf{Raw}\ \mathtt{year})^{[\mathsf{Raw}:[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]]}\\
&\mathtt{data}\vartriangleright{[\mathsf{Raw}:[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}]]}\end{array}\end{array}

Following the idea of moving quantifiers, we can translate

\mathtt{data}
to use a polymorphic variant, and the upcast can then be
simulated by instantiation and re-abstraction.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{data}_{\text{✗}}=\Lambda\rho_{1}\rho_{2}.~(\mathsf{Raw}\ (\mathtt{year^{\prime}}\ \rho_{2}))^{[\mathsf{Raw}:[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int};\rho_{2}];\rho_{1}]}\\
&\Lambda\rho_{1}\rho_{2}.~\mathtt{data}_{\text{✗}}\ \rho_{1}\ (\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\rho_{2})\end{array}\end{array}

So far, the translation appears to have worked.
However, it breaks down when we consider the case split on a nested
variant.
For instance, consider the following function.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{parseAge}=\lambda x^{[\mathsf{Raw}:[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]]}.~\mathbf{case}\ x\ \{\mathsf{Raw}\ y\mapsto\mathtt{getAge}\ (y\vartriangleright[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}])\}\\
&\mathtt{parseAge}\ \mathtt{data}\end{array}\end{array}

Using an upcast and
\mathtt{getAge}
from

Section

2.1
in the case clause,
it accepts the nested variant
\mathtt{data}
.

The difficulty with encoding
\mathtt{parseAge}
with row polymorphism is
that the abstraction of the row variable for the inner record of

\mathtt{data_{\text{✗}}}
is hoisted up to the top-level, but case split
requires a monomorphic value. Thus, we must instantiate
\rho_{2}
with

\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}

before
performing the case split.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{parseAge}_{\text{✗}}=\lambda x^{\forall\rho_{1}\rho_{2}.[\mathsf{Raw}:[\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int};\rho_{2}];\rho_{1}]}.~\mathbf{case}\ (x\ \cdot\ (\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}))\ \{\mathsf{Raw}\ y\mapsto\mathtt{getAge}\ y\}\\
&\mathtt{parseAge}_{\text{✗}}\ \mathtt{data}_{\text{✗}}\end{array}\end{array}

However, this would not yield a compositional type-only translation,
as the translation of the
\mathbf{case}
construct only has access to the
types of
x
and the whole case clause, which provide no information
about
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
.
Moreover, even if the translation could somehow access this type
information, the translation would still fail if there were multiple
incompatible upcasts of
y
in the case clause.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathbf{case}\ x\ \{\mathsf{Raw}\ y\mapsto\dots y\vartriangleright[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]\dots y\vartriangleright[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int}]\}\end{array}\end{array}

The first upcast requires
\rho_{2}
to be instantiated with
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}

but the second requires it to be instantiated with the incompatible

\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{String}
.
The situation is no better if we add presence polymorphism. In

Section

5.3
, we prove that there exists
no type-only encoding of strictly covariant variant subtyping into row and
presence polymorphism (

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho\theta}}

).

2.7.
No Type-Only Encoding of Full Record Subtyping as Polymorphism

For variants, we have just seen that a type-only encoding of full
subtyping does not exist, even if we restrict propagation of simple
subtyping to strictly covariant positions.
For records, we have seen how to encode strictly covariant subtyping
with presence polymorphism by hoisting quantifiers to the top-level.
We now consider whether we could somehow lift the strictly covariance
restriction and encode full record subtyping with polymorphism.

The idea of hoisting quantifiers does not work arbitrarily, exactly
because we cannot hoist quantifiers through contravariant positions.
Moreover, presence polymorphism alone cannot extend rows. Consider the
full subtyping example
\mathtt{getName}\vartriangleright(\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\to\mathsf{String})
from

Section

2.4
. The
\mathtt{getName}
function is translated
to the
\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}
function in

Section

2.2
, which provides no way to
extend the parameter record type with
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}=\lambda x^{\forall\theta.\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta}:\mathsf{String}\rangle}.~((x\ \bullet).\mathsf{Name})\\
\end{array}\end{array}

A tempting idea is to add row polymorphism:

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}_{\text{✗}}=\Lambda\rho.\lambda x^{\forall\theta.\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta}:\mathsf{String};\rho\rangle}.~((x\ \bullet).\mathsf{Name})\\
\end{array}\end{array}

Now we can instantiate
\rho
with
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
to simulate the upcast.
However, this still does not work.
One issue is that we have no way to remove the labels introduced by
the row variable
\rho
in the function body, as
x
is only
polymorphic in
\theta
.
For instance, consider the following upcast of the function
\mathtt{getUnit}

which replaces the function body of
\mathtt{getName}
with an upcast of
x
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getUnit}=\lambda x^{\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle}.(x\vartriangleright\langle\rangle)\\
&\mathtt{getUnit}\vartriangleright(\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String};\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\to\langle\rangle)\end{array}\end{array}

Following the above idea,
\mathtt{getUnit}
is translated to

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getUnit}_{\text{✗}}=\Lambda\rho.\lambda x^{\forall\theta.\langle\mathsf{Name}^{\theta}:\mathsf{String};\rho\rangle}.x\ \circ\\
\end{array}\end{array}

Then, in the translation of the upcast of
\mathtt{getUnit}
, the row
variable
\rho
is expected to be instantiated with a row containing

\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
.
However, we cannot remove
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
again in the translation of the
function body, meaning that the upcast inside
\mathtt{getUnit}
cannot
yield an empty record.

Section

5.4
expands on the discussion here and
proves that there exists no type-only translation of unrestricted full
record subtyping into row and presence polymorphism
(

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho\theta}}

).

3.
Calculi

The foundation for our exploration of relative expressive power of
subtyping and parametric polymorphism is
Church 1940
’s simply-typed

\lambda
-calculus
(
Church 1940
)
.
We extend
it with variants and records, respectively. We further extend the
variant calculus twice: first with simple structural subtyping and
then with row polymorphism. Similarly, we also extend the record
calculus twice: first with structural subtyping and then with presence
polymorphism. In Section
5
and

6
, we explore further extensions with
strictly covariant subtyping, full subtyping and rank-1 polymorphism.

3.1.
A Simply-Typed Base Calculus

4.
Simple Subtyping as Polymorphism

In this section, we consider encodings of simple subtyping.
We present four encodings and two non-existence results as depicted in

Figure

1
.
Specifically, in addition to the standard term-involved encodings of simple
variant and record subtyping in
Section

4.1
and

Section

4.3
, we give type-only encodings of simple
variant subtyping as row polymorphism in

Section

4.2
, and simple record subtyping as
presence polymorphism in
Section

4.4

For each translation, we establish its correctness by demonstrating the
preservation of typing derivations and the correspondence between the
operational semantics.
In
Section

4.5
, we show the non-existence of type-only
encodings if we swap the row and presence polymorphism of the target languages.

Compositional Translations

We restrict our attention to compositional translations defined
inductively over the structure of derivations.
For convenience we will often write these as if they are defined on
plain terms, but formally the domain is derivations rather than terms,
whilst the codomain is terms.
In this section translations on derivations will always be defined on
top of corresponding compositional translations on types, kind
environments, and type environments, in such a way that we obtain a
type preservation property for each translation.

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}

We give a local term-involved compositional translation from
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
to

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\llbracket-\rrbracket&:&\mathsf{Derivation}\to\mathsf{Term}\\

\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\qquad}@{}r@{~~}c@{~~}l@{}}\mathsf{\eta\textrm{-}Case}&M^{[\ell_{i}:A_{i}]_{i}}&\leadsto_{\eta}&\mathbf{case}~M~\{\ell_{i}~x_{i}\mapsto(\ell_{i}\,x_{i})^{[\ell_{i}:A_{i}]_{i}}\}_{i}\end{array}

For type environments, we have
\llbracket\Gamma,x:A\rrbracket=\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket,x:\llbracket A\rrbracket

For kind environments, we have the identity function
\llbracket\Delta\rrbracket=\Delta

Every well-typed
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
term
\Delta;\Gamma\vdash M:A

to a well-typed
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}
term
\llbracket\Delta\rrbracket;\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash\llbracket M\rrbracket:\llbracket A\rrbracket
.

In order to state an operational correspondence result, we first
define
\leadsto_{\beta\vartriangleright}
as the union of
\leadsto_{\beta}
and
\leadsto_{\vartriangleright}
,
and
\rightsquigarrow_{\beta\vartriangleright}

correspondence between reduction in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
and reduction in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}
.

Theorem 4.2 (Operational Correspondence).

For the translation
\llbracket-\rrbracket
from
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
to
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}

Simulation

\displaystyle R\backslash R^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\ell:A)_{(\ell:A)\in R\text{ and }(\ell:A)\notin R^{\prime}}

Theorem 4.3 (Type Preservation).

Every well-typed
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}

to a well-typed

\llbracket\Delta\rrbracket;\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash\llbracket M\rrbracket:\llbracket A\rrbracket

For the translation
\llbracket-\rrbracket
from
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho}

4.4.

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}

Before presenting the translation, let us focus on order of labels in types.
Though generally we treat row types as unordered collections, in this
section we assume, without loss of generality, that there is a
canonical order on labels, and the labels of any rows (including
records) conform to this order.
This assumption is crucial in preserving the correspondence between
labels and presence variables bound by abstraction. For example,
consider the type
A=\langle\ell_{1}:A_{1};\dots;\ell_{n}:A_{n}\rangle
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}

Following the idea of making records presence polymorphic as
exemplified by
\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}
and
\mathtt{alice^{\prime}}
in

Section

2.2

as
\llbracket A\rrbracket=\forall\theta_{1}\dots\theta_{n}.\langle\ell_{1}^{\theta_{1}}:\llbracket A_{1}\rrbracket;\dots;\ell_{n}^{\theta_{n}}:\llbracket A_{n}\rrbracket\rangle
.
With the canonical order, we can guarantee that
\ell_{i}
always appears at the

i
-th position in the record and possesses the presence variable bound at the

i
-th position.
The full translation is as follows.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\llbracket-\rrbracket&:&\mathsf{Type}\to\mathsf{Type}\\
\llbracket\langle\ell_{i}:A_{i}\rangle_{i}\rrbracket&=&(\forall\theta_{i})_{i}.\langle\ell_{i}^{\theta_{i}}:\llbracket A_{i}\rrbracket\rangle_{i}\\
\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\llbracket-\rrbracket&:&\mathsf{Derivation}\to\mathsf{Term}\\
\llbracket\langle\ell_{i}=M_{i}\rangle_{i}^{\langle\ell_{i}:A_{i}\rangle_{i}}\rrbracket&=&(\Lambda\theta_{i})_{i}.\langle\ell_{i}=\llbracket M_{i}\rrbracket\rangle_{i}^{\langle\ell_{i}^{\theta_{i}}:\llbracket A_{i}\rrbracket\rangle_{i}}\\

\lx@intercol\hfil\text{where }\begin{array}[t]{@{~}l@{~}l@{\quad}l}P_{i}=\circ&,i\neq j&P_{j}=\bullet\end{array}\lx@intercol\\
\llbracket M^{\langle\ell_{i}:A_{i}\rangle_{i}}\vartriangleright\langle\ell^{\prime}_{j}:A^{\prime}_{j}\rangle_{j}\rrbracket&=&(\Lambda\theta_{j})_{j}.\llbracket M\rrbracket\,(@\,P_{i})_{i}\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\text{where }\begin{array}[t]{@{~}l@{~}l@{\quad}l@{~}l}P_{i}=\circ&,\ell_{i}\notin(\ell^{\prime}_{j})_{j}&P_{i}=\theta_{j}&,\ell_{i}=\ell^{\prime}_{j}\end{array}\lx@intercol\\

4.5.
Swapping Row and Presence Polymorphism

In
Section

4.2
and

Section

4.4
, we encode simple subtyping for variants
using row polymorphism, and simple subtyping for records using presence
polymorphism. These encodings enjoy the property that they only introduce new
type abstractions and applications.
A natural question is whether we can swap the polymorphism used by the encodings
meanwhile preserve the type-only property.
As we have seen in
Section

2.3
, an intuitive attempt to encode
simple record subtyping with row polymorphism failed. Specifically, we have the
problematic translation

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\phantom{=}\ \llbracket\mathtt{getName}\ (\mathtt{alice}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle)\rrbracket\\
&=\llbracket\mathtt{getName}\rrbracket\ (\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int})\ \llbracket\mathtt{alice}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\rrbracket\\
&=\mathtt{getName}_{\text{✗}}\ (\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int})\ \mathtt{alice}\end{array}\end{array}

First, the type information
\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int}
is not accessible to a compositional
type-only translation of the function application here.
Moreover, the type preservation property is also broken:

\llbracket\mathtt{alice}\vartriangleright\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\rrbracket
should have type

\llbracket\langle\mathsf{Name}:\mathsf{String}\rangle\rrbracket
, but here it is just translated to

\mathtt{alice}
itself, which has an extra label
\mathsf{Age}
in its record type.
We give a general non-existence theorem.

Theorem 4.9.

There exists no global type-only encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant}
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho}
,
and no global type-only encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant}
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\theta}
.

The extensions for
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho}
and
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\theta}
are straightforward
and can be found in
Appendix

A
.
The proofs of this theorem can be found in

Section

E.1
.
We will give further non-existence results in

Section

5
.
The core idea underlying the proofs of this kind of non-existence
result is to construct counterexamples and use proof by contradiction.
One important observation is that in our case a type-only translation
ensures that terms are invariant under the translation modulo type
abstraction and type application.
As a consequence, we may characterise the general form of any such
translation by accounting for the possibility of adding type
abstractions and type applications in every possible position.
Then we can obtain a contradiction by considering the general form of
type-only translations of carefully selected terms.

To give an example, let us consider the proof of
Theorem

4.9
.
Consider
\langle\rangle
and
\langle\ell=y\rangle\vartriangleright\langle\rangle
which have
the same type under environments
\Delta=\alpha_{0}
and
\Gamma=y:\alpha_{0}
.
Any type-only translation must yield
\llbracket\langle\rangle\rrbracket=\Lambda\overline{\alpha}.\langle\rangle
and

\llbracket\langle\ell=y\rangle\vartriangleright\langle\rangle\rrbracket=\Lambda\overline{\beta}.\llbracket\langle\ell=y\rangle\rrbracket\ \overline{B}=\Lambda\overline{\beta}.(\Lambda\overline{\alpha}^{\prime}.\langle\ell=\llbracket y\rrbracket\ \overline{A}^{\prime}\rangle)\ \overline{B}=\Lambda\overline{\beta}.(\Lambda\overline{\alpha}^{\prime}.\langle\ell=(\Lambda\overline{\beta}^{\prime}.y)\ \overline{A}^{\prime}\rangle)\ \overline{B}

which can be simplified to

\Lambda\overline{\gamma}.\langle\ell=\Lambda\overline{\delta}.y\rangle
.
Thus,
\llbracket\langle\rangle\rrbracket
has type
\forall\overline{\alpha}.\langle\rangle
,
and
\llbracket\langle\ell=y\rangle\vartriangleright\langle\rangle\rrbracket
has type
\forall\overline{\gamma}.\langle\ell:\forall\overline{\delta}.\alpha_{0}\rangle
.
By type preservation, they should still have the same type, which
implies
\forall\overline{\alpha}.\langle\rangle=\forall\overline{\gamma}.\langle\ell:\forall\overline{\delta}.\alpha_{0}\rangle
.
However, this equation obviously does not hold, showing a
contradiction.

The above proof relies on the assumption that translations should
always satisfy the type preservation theorem.
Sometimes this assumption can be too strong.
In order to show the robustness of our theorem, we provide three
proofs of
Theorem

4.9
in
Section

E.1
,
where only one of them relies on type preservation.
The second proof uses the compositionality and a similar argument to
the
\mathtt{getName}_{\text{✗}}
example in
Section

2.3
,
while the third proof does not rely on either of them.

In
Section

6
, we will show that it is possible to
simulate record subtyping with rank-1 row polymorphism and type
inference, at the cost of a weaker type preservation property and some
extra conditions on the source language.

5.
Full Subtyping as Polymorphism

So far we have only considered simple subtyping, which means the subtyping
judgement applies shallowly to a single variant or record constructor
(width subtyping).
Any notion of simple subtyping can be mechanically lifted to
full subtyping by inductively propagating the subtyping relation to the
components of each type.
The direction of the subtyping relation remains the same for covariant
positions, and is reversed for contravariant positions.

In this section, we consider encodings of full subtyping.
We first formalise the calculus
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
with full subtyping for
records and variants, and give its standard term-involved translation to

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}
(
Section

5.1
).
Next we give a type-only encoding of strictly covariant record subtyping
(
Section

5.2
) and a non-existence result for
variants (
Section

5.3
).
Finally, we give a non-existence result for type-only encodings of
full record subtyping as polymorphism (
Section

5.4
).

5.1.
Local Term-Involved Encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in

\mathsf{erase}(-)
transforms typed terms in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}

upcasts and type annotations.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\mathsf{erase}(M\vartriangleright A)=\mathsf{erase}(M)\quad\mathsf{erase}(\lambda x^{A}.M)=\lambda x.\mathsf{erase}(M)\quad\mathsf{erase}((\ell\,M)^{A})=\ell\,\mathsf{erase}(M)\end{array}

We show a correspondence between upcasting and erasure

Section

C.2
.
In the following, we always use the erasure semantics for calculi with
full subtyping or strictly covariant subtyping.

The idea of the local term-involved translation from

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}

5.3.
Non-Existence of Type-Only Encodings of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho\theta}

As illustrated by the example

\mathtt{parseAge}_{\text{✗}}\ \mathtt{data}_{\text{✗}}
in

Section

2.6
, the approach of hoisting
quantifiers to the top-level does not work for variants, because of
case splits.
Formally, we have the following general non-existence theorem showing
that no other approaches exist.

Theorem 5.3.

There exists no global type-only encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho\theta}
.

The idea of the proof is the same as that of
Theorem

4.9
which
we have shown in
Section

4.5
: construct the
schemes of type-only translations for certain terms and derive a
contradiction.
The terms we choose here are the nested variant
M=(\ell\,(\ell\,y)^{[\ell]})^{[\ell:[\ell]]}

for some free term variable
y
in the environment together with its
upcast
M_{1}=M\vartriangleright[\ell:[\ell;\ell^{\prime}]]
and its
case split
M_{2}=\mathbf{case}\ M\ \{\ell\ x\mapsto x\vartriangleright[\ell;\ell^{\prime}]\}
, similar to the counterexamples we give in

Section

2.6
.
To obtain a contradiction, we show that we cannot give a uniform
type-only translation of
M
such that both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
can be
translated compositionally.
The details of the proof can be found in

Section

E.2
.

As a corollary, there can be no global type-only encoding of

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho\theta}
.

One might worry that
Theorem

5.3
contradicts the duality
between records and variants, especially in light of

Blume et al. 2006
’s translation from variants with default cases to
records with record extensions. In their translation, a variant is
translated to a function which takes a record of functions. For
instance, the translation of variant types is:

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\llbracket[\ell_{i}:A_{i}]_{i}\rrbracket=\forall\alpha.\langle\ell_{i}:A_{i}\to\alpha\rangle_{i}\to\alpha\end{array}

In fact, there is no contradiction because a variant in a covariant
position corresponds to a record in a contravariant position, which
means that the encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{co}}
in

Section

5.2
cannot be used. Moreover, the
translation from variants to records is not type-only as it introduces

\lambda
-abstractions.

5.4.
Non-Existence of Type-Only Encodings of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho\theta}

As illustrated by the examples
\mathtt{getName^{\prime}}_{\text{✗}}
and

\mathtt{getUnit}_{\text{✗}}
in
Section

2.7
,
one attempt to simulate full record subtyping by both making record
types presence-polymorphic and adding row variables for records in
contravariant positions fails. In fact no such encoding exists.

Theorem 5.4.

There exists no global type-only encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho\theta}
.

Again, the proof idea is to give general forms of type-only
translations for certain terms and proof by contradiction.
Our choice of terms here are different from the counterexamples in

Section

2.7
this time.
Instead, we first consider two functions
f_{1}=\lambda x^{\langle\rangle}.x
and
f_{2}=\lambda x^{\langle\rangle}.\langle\rangle
of the same type

\langle\rangle\to\langle\rangle
.
Any type-only translations of these functions must yield terms of the following forms:

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l@{}c@{}l}\llbracket f_{1}\rrbracket&=\Lambda\overline{\alpha}_{1}.\lambda x^{A_{1}}.\Lambda\overline{\beta}_{1}.&x&\ \overline{B}_{1}\\
\llbracket f_{2}\rrbracket&=\Lambda\overline{\alpha}_{2}.\lambda x^{A_{2}}.\Lambda\overline{\beta}_{2}.&\llbracket\langle\rangle\rrbracket&\ \overline{B}_{2}=\Lambda\overline{\alpha}_{2}.\lambda x^{A_{2}}.\Lambda\overline{\beta}_{2}.(\Lambda\overline{\gamma}.\langle\rangle)\ \overline{B}_{2}\end{array}

and
(\Lambda\overline{\gamma}.\langle\rangle)\ \overline{B}_{2}
should also
have the same type.
As a result, the type
A_{1}
of

cannot contain any type variables
bound in
\overline{\alpha}_{1}
unless they are inside the type of some labels
which are instantiated to absent by the type application

.
Then, it is problematic when we want to upcast the parameter of

to be a wider record, e.g.,
f_{1}\vartriangleright(\langle\ell:\langle\rangle\rangle\to\langle\rangle)
.
Intuitively, because
A_{1}
cannot be an open record type with the row
variable bound in
\overline{\alpha}_{1}
, we actually have no way to expand

A_{1}
, which leads to a contradiction.
The full proof can be found in
Section

6.
Full Subtyping as Rank-1 Polymorphism

In
Section

4.5
, we showed that no type-only
encoding of record subtyping as row polymorphism exists.
The main obstacle is a lack of type information for instantiation.
By focusing on rank-1 polymorphism in the target language, we need no
longer concern ourselves with type abstraction and application
explicitly anymore.
Instead we defer to Hindley-Milner type inference
(
Damas and Milner 1982
)

as demonstrated by the examples in
Section

2.4
.
In this section, we formalise the encodings of full subtyping as
rank-1 polymorphism.

Here we focus on the encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
, a
ML-style calculus with records and rank-1 row polymorphism (the same
idea applies to each combination of encoding records or variants as
rank-1 row polymorphism or rank-1 presence polymorphism).
The specification of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
is given in

Section

A.3
, which uses a standard declarative
Hindley-Milner style type system and extends the term syntax with
let-binding
\mathbf{let}\,x=M\,\mathbf{in}\,N
for polymorphism.
We also extend
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
with let-binding syntax and its
standard typing and operational semantics rules.

As demonstrated in
Section

2.4
, we can use the following
(local and type-only) erasure translation to encode

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
, the fragment of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
where types
are restricted to have rank-2 records, in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\llbracket-\rrbracket&:&\mathsf{Derivation}\to\mathsf{Term}\\
\llbracket M\vartriangleright A\rrbracket&=&M\end{array}

Since the types of translated terms in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
are given by
type inference, we do not need to use a translation on types in the
translation on terms.
Moreover, we implicitly allow type annotations on

\lambda
-abstractions to be erased as they no longer exist in the
target language.

To formalise the definition of rank-
n

\mho^{n}(A)

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{split}\mho^{n}(\alpha)&=\mathsf{true}\\

\mho^{0}(A\to B)&=\mho^{0}(A)\land\mho^{0}(B)\\
\mho^{0}(\langle\ell_{i}:A_{i}\rangle_{i})&=\mathsf{false}\end{split}\end{array}

We define a type

to have rank-

\mho^{n}(A)

holds.
The predicate
\mho^{n}(A)
basically means no record types can
appear in the left subtrees of
n
or more arrows.

After giving the type and environment translation, we aim for a weak

Theorem 6.2 (Weak Type Preservation).

Every well-typed
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
term
\Delta;\Gamma\vdash M:A
is
translated to a well-typed
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
term

\llbracket\Delta;\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash\llbracket M\rrbracket:\tau
for some
A^{\prime}\leqslant A
and

\tau\preccurlyeq\llbracket A^{\prime}\rrbracket
.

The proof makes use of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{afull}}
, an algorithmic variant
of the type system of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
which combines

\mathsf{T\textrm{-}App}
and
\mathsf{T\textrm{-}Upcast}
into one rule
\mathsf{T\textrm{-}AppSub}
, and
removes all explicit upcasts in terms.

\frac{\Delta;\Gamma\vdash M:A\to B\quad\Delta;\Gamma\vdash N:A^{\prime}\quad A^{\prime}\leqslant A}{\Delta;\Gamma\vdash M\,N:B}\quad\textsc{Lab=$\mathsf{T\textrm{-}AppSub}$}

It is standard that
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{afull}}
is sound and complete with
respect to
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}

(
Pierce 2002
)
.
Immediately, we have that
\Delta;\Gamma\vdash M:A
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
implies
\Delta;\Gamma\vdash\widehat{M}:A^{\prime}
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{afull}}
for some
A^{\prime}\leqslant A
, where
\widehat{M}

is defined as
M
with all upcasts erased.
Thus, we only need to prove that
\Delta;\Gamma\vdash M:A
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{afull}}
implies

\llbracket\Delta;\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash\llbracket M\rrbracket:\tau
for some
\tau\preccurlyeq\llbracket A\rrbracket
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
.
The remaining proof can be done by induction on the typing derivations
in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{afull}}
, where the most non-trivial case is the

\mathsf{T\textrm{-}AppSub}
rule.
The core idea is to use instantiation in
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
to simulate
the subtyping relation
A^{\prime}\leqslant A
in the
\mathsf{T\textrm{-}AppSub}
rule.
This is possible because the source language
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{afull}}

is restricted to have rank-
2
records, which implies that
A\to B
is
translated to a polymorphic type where the record types in parameters
are open and can be extended to simulate the subtyping relation.
The full proof can be found in

Section

D.1
.

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
to
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
.
As shown in
Section

2.4
, we have three other results.
For records, we have another erasure translation from
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
,
the fragment of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
where types are restricted to have rank-1
records, to
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\theta 1}
with rank-1 presence polymorphism.
Similarly, for variants, we formally define a type
A
to have
rank-
n
variants, if the predicate
\Omega^{n}(A)
defined as
follows holds.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{split}\Omega^{n}(\alpha)&=\mathsf{true}\\
\Omega^{n}(A\to B)&=\Omega^{n-1}(A)\land\Omega^{n}(B)\\
\Omega^{n}([\ell_{i}:A_{i}]_{i})&=\land_{i}\Omega^{n}(A_{i})\end{split}\qquad\begin{split}\Omega^{0}(\alpha)&=\mathsf{true}\\
\Omega^{0}(A\to B)&=\Omega^{0}(A)\land\Omega^{0}(B)\\
\Omega^{0}([\ell_{i}:A_{i}]_{i})&=\mathsf{false}\end{split}\end{array}

We also have two erasure translations from
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}1}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}

to

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\rho 1}
and from
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}

to
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}}^{\theta 1}
.
They all use the same idea to let type inference infer
row/presence-polymorphic types for terms involving records/variants,
and use instantiation to automatically simulate subtyping.
We omit the metatheory of these three results as they are similar to
what we have seen for the encoding of
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
.

The requirement of rank-1 polymorphism and Hindley-Milner type
inference for target languages is not mandatory; target languages can
support more advanced type inference for higher-rank polymorphism like
FreezeML
(
Emrich et al. 2020
)
, as long as no type annotation is needed to
infer rank-1 polymorphic types.
One might hope to also relax the
\mho^{2}(-)
restriction in

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}2}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
via enabling higher-rank polymorphism.
However, at least the erasure translation do not work anymore.
For instance, consider the functions
\mathtt{id}=\lambda x^{\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle}.x
and
\mathtt{const}=\lambda x^{\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle}.\langle\ell=1\rangle
with the
same type
\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\to\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle
.
Type inference would give
\llbracket\mathtt{id}\rrbracket
the type
\forall\rho^{\mathsf{Row}_{\{\ell\}}}.\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int};\rho\rangle\to\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int};\rho\rangle
, and

\llbracket\mathtt{const}\rrbracket
the type
\forall\rho^{\mathsf{Row}_{\{\ell\}}}.\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int};\rho\rangle\to\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle
.
For a second-order function of type
(\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle\to\langle\ell:\mathsf{Int}\rangle)\to A
, we cannot give a type to the parameter of
the function after translation which can be unified with the types of
both
\llbracket\mathtt{id}\rrbracket
and
\llbracket\mathtt{const}\rrbracket
.
We leave it to future work to explore whether there exist other
translations making use of type inference for higher-rank
polymorphism.

7.
Discussion

We have now explored a range of encodings of structural subtyping for
variants and records as parametric polymorphism under different
conditions.
These encodings and non-existence results capture the extent to which
row and presence polymorphism can simulate structural subtyping and
crystallise longstanding folklore and informal intuitions.
In the remainder of this section we briefly discuss record extensions
and default cases (
Section

7.1
), combining subtyping
and polymorphism (
Section

7.2
),
related work (
Section

7.3
) and conclusions and future work
(
Section

7.4
).

7.1.
Record Extensions and Default Cases

Two important extensions to row and presence polymorphism are record
extensions
(
Rémy 1994
)
, and its dual, default cases

(
Blume et al. 2006
)
.
These operations provide extra expressiveness beyond structural
subtyping.
For example, with default cases, we can give a default age
42
to the
function
\mathtt{getAge}
in

Section

2.1
, and then apply it to
variants with arbitrary constructors.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mathtt{getAgeD}:\>\,\forall\rho^{\mathsf{Row}_{\{\mathsf{Age},\mathsf{Year}\}}}.{[\mathsf{Age}:\mathsf{Int};\mathsf{Year}:\mathsf{Int};\rho]}\to\mathsf{Int}\\
&\mathtt{getAgeD}=\lambda x.~\mathbf{case}\,x\,\{\mathsf{Age}\,y\mapsto y;\mathsf{Year}\,y\mapsto 2023-y;z\mapsto 42\}\\
&\mathtt{getAgeD}\ (\mathsf{Name}\,\text{\textquotedbl Carol\textquotedbl})\rightsquigarrow_{\beta}^{\ast}42\end{array}\end{array}

7.2.
Combining Subtyping and Polymorphism

Though row and presence polymorphism can simulate subtyping well and
support expressive extensions like record extension and default cases,
it can still be beneficial to allow both subtyping and polymorphism
together in the same language.
For example, the OCaml programming language combines row and presence
polymorphism with subtyping. Row and presence variables are hidden in
its core language.
It supports both polymorphic variants and polymorphic objects (a
variation on polymorphic records) as well as explicit upcast for
closed variants and records.
Our results give a rationalisation for why OCaml supports subtyping in
addition to row polymorphism. Row polymorphism simply is not expressive
enough to give a local encoding of unrestricted structural subtyping,
even though OCaml indirectly supports full first-class polymorphism.

Bounded quantification
(
Cardelli and Wegner 1985
;
Cardelli et al. 1994
)
extends
system F with subtyping by introducing subtyping bounds to type
variables.
There is also much work on the type inference for both polymorphism
and subtyping based on collecting, solving, and simplifying
constraints
(
Trifonov and Smith 1996
;
Pottier 1998
;
Pottier 2001
)
.
Algebraic subtyping
(
Dolan and Mycroft 2017
;
Dolan 2016
)
combines subtyping and
parametric polymorphism, offering compact principal types and
decidable subsumption checking.
MLstruct
(
Parreaux and Chau 2022
)
extends algebraic subtyping with
intersection and union types, giving rise to another alternative to
row polymorphism.

7.3.
Related Work

Row types.

Wand 1987
first introduced rows and row polymorphism.
There are many further papers on row types, which take a variety of
approaches, particularly focusing on extensible records.

Harper and Pierce 1990
extended System F with constrained quantification,
where predicates
\rho\,\mathsf{lacks}\,L
and
\rho\,\mathsf{has}\,L

are used to indicate the presence and absence of labels in row
variables.

Gaster and Jones 1996
and
Gaster 1998
explored a calculus with a
similar
\mathsf{lacks}
predicate based on qualified types.

Rémy 1989
introduced the concept of presence types and
polymorphism, and
Rémy 1994
combines row and presence
polymorphism.

Leijen 2005
proposed a variation on row polymorphism with support
for scoped labels.

Pottier and Rémy 2004
considered type inference for row and presence
polymorphism in HM(X).

Morris and McKinna 2019
introduced
Rose
, an algebraic foundation for
row typing via a rather general language with two predicates
representing the containment and combination of rows. It is parametric
over a row theory which enables it to express different styles of row
types (including
Wand 1987
and
Rémy 1994
’s style
and
Leijen 2005
’s style).

Row polymorphism vs structural subtyping.

Wand 1987
compared his calculus with row polymorphism (similar to

\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\rho 1}
) with
Cardelli 1984
’s calculus
with structural subtyping (similar to
\lambda_{\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$[]$}}\text{\scalebox{1}[.8]{$\langle\rangle$}}}^{\leqslant\mathrm{full}}
) and showed
that they cannot be encoded in each other by examples.

Pottier 1998
conveyed the intuition that row
polymorphism can lessen the need for subtyping to some extent, but
there are still situations where subtyping are necessary, e.g., the
reuse of
\lambda
-bound variables which cannot be polymorphic given
only rank-1 polymorphism.

Disjoint polymorphism.

Disjoint intersection types
(
d. S. Oliveira et al. 2016
)
generalise record
types.
Record concatenation and restriction
(
Cardelli and Mitchell 1991
)
are replaced
by a merge operator
(
Dunfield 2014
)
and a type difference
operator
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
, respectively.
Parametric polymorphism of disjoint intersection types is supported
via disjoint polymorphism
(
Alpuim et al. 2017
)
where type variables are
associated with disjointness constraints.
Similarly to our work,
Xie et al. 2020
formally prove that both row
polymorphism and bounded quantification of record types can be encoded
in terms of disjoint polymorphism.

7.4.
Conclusion and Future Work

We carried out a formal and systematic study of the encoding of
structural subtyping as parametric polymorphism.
To better reveal the relative expressive power of these two type
system features, we introduced the notion of type-only translations to
avoid the influence of non-trivial term reconstruction.
We gave type-only translations from various calculi with subtyping to
calculi with different kinds of polymorphism and proved their
correctness; we also proved a series of non-existence results.
Our results provide a precise characterisation of the long-standing
folklore intuition that row polymorphism can often replace subtyping.
Additionally, they offer insight into the trade-offs between subtyping
and polymorphism in the design of programming languages.

In future, we would like to explore whether it might be possible to
extend our encodings relying on type inference to systems supporting
higher-rank polymorphism, such as FreezeML
(
Emrich et al. 2020
)
.
We would also like to consider other styles of row typing such as
those based on scoped labels
(
Leijen 2005
)
and
Rose

(
Morris and McKinna 2019
)
.
In addition to variant and record types, row types are also the
foundation for various effect type systems, e.g. for effect
handlers
(
Hillerström and Lindley 2016
;
Leijen 2017
)
.
It would be interesting to investigate to what extent our approach can
be applied to effect typing.
Aside from studying the relationship between subtyping and row and
presence polymorphism we would also like to study the ergonomics of
these programming language features in practice, especially their
compatibility with others such as algebraic data types.
