Abstract
. There exists a high variability in mobility data volumes across different regions, which deteriorates the performance of spatial recommender systems that rely on region-specific data. In this paper, we propose a novel transfer learning framework called Reformd , for continuous-time location prediction for regions with sparse checkin data. Specifically, we model user-specific checkin-sequences in a region using a marked temporal point process (MTPP) with normalizing flows to learn the inter-checkin time and geo-distributions. Later, we transfer the model parameters of spatial and temporal flows trained on a data-rich origin region for the next check-in and time prediction in a target region with scarce checkin data. We capture the evolving region-specific checkin dynamics for MTPP and spatial-temporal flows by maximizing the joint likelihood of next checkin with three channels (1) checkin-category prediction, (2) checkin-time prediction, and (3) travel distance prediction. Extensive experiments on different user mobility datasets across the U.S. and Japan show that our model significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods for modeling continuous-time sequences. Moreover, we also show that Reformd can be easily adapted for product recommendations i.e. , sequences without any spatial component.



1.
Introduction

Recent research has shown that accurate advertisements on Points-of-Interest (POI) networks, such as Foursquare and Instagram, can achieve up to 25 times the return-on-investment
(
Metev 2021
)
. Consequently, predicting the time-evolving mobility of users,
i.e.
where
and
when
, is of utmost importance to power systems relying on spatial data.
Current approaches
(
Likhyani
et al. 2017
;
Cho
et al. 2011
;
Yuan et al. 2013
)
overlook the temporal aspect of a recommender system as it involves modeling continuous-time checkin sequences – which is non-trivial with standard neural architectures
(
Du et al. 2016
;
Zuo
et al. 2020
;
Mei and Eisner 2017
)
. The problem is further aggravated by the variation in volumes of mobility data across regions due to the growing awareness for personal data privacy
(
Barkhuus and Dey 2003
;
Wei
et al. 2016
)
. Therefore, there exists an underlying region-based data-scarcity, which further exacerbates the training of large neural models.

In recent years, Marked Temporal Point Processes(MTPP) have outperformed other neural architectures for characterizing asynchronous events localized in continuous time and are even used in a wide range of applications, including healthcare
(
Rizoiu
et al. 2018
)
, finance
(
Zhang
et al. 2020
;
Bacry
et al. 2015
)
, and social networks
(
Gupta
et al. 2021
;
Mei and Eisner 2017
;
Zuo
et al. 2020
)
. Recent works that deploy MTPP for predicting user mobility patterns are either: (i) limited to predicting the time of user-location interactions rather than actual locations
(
Yang
et al. 2018
)
, (ii) restricted to
one
dataset without a foreseeable way to easily utilize external information
(
Likhyani et al. 2020
)
, or (iii) disregard the opportunity to reuse trained parameters from external datasets by jointly embedding the checkin and time distributions
(
Chen
et al. 2020
)
. Thus, none of these approaches can be used for designing mobility prediction models for limited data regions.

In this paper, we present
Reformd
(
Re
usable
F
lows f
or

M
obility
D
ata), a novel transfer learning framework that learns spatial and temporal distribution of checkins using normalizing flows(NFs) on a checkin-rich
origin
region and transfers them for efficient prediction in a checkin-scarce
target
region. Specifically, we consider the series of checkins made by a user as her checkin sequence and model these sequences for all users from a region using a neural MTPP and learn the inter-checkin time interval and spatial-distance distributions as two independent NFs
(
McLachlan and
Peel 2004
;
Rezende and
Mohamed 2015
)
. To make the learned spatial and temporal NFs
invariant
of the underlying region, we restrict our model to learn the distribution of inter-checkin time intervals and spatial distance. These features are unaffected by the network characteristics that vary across regions – POI categories and user affinities towards these POIs. Therefore, these NFs can be easily extended for prediction in other mobility regions. The ability of NFs to provide faster sampling and closed-form training for continuous-time event sequences
(
Shchur
et al. 2020
)
make them a perfect medium to transfer mobility information. Moreover, for transferring across regions, we cluster the checkin sequences of each region, with each cluster containing checkin-sequences with
similar
spatial and temporal checkin patterns and only transfer the parameters across these clusters.

In summary, the key contributions we make in this paper via
Reformd
are three-fold:

(i)

We propose
Reformd
, a transfer-learning model for predicting mobility dynamics in checkin-scarce datasets by incorporating mobility parameters trained on a checkin-rich region.

(ii)

We present a novel NF-based transfer over the MTPP that not only enables a faster sampling of time and distance features of next checkin, but also achieves high performance even with limited fine-tuning on the target region.

(iii)

Finally, we empirically show that
Reformd
outperforms the state-of-the-art models by up to 20% and 23% for checkin-category and time prediction and can easily be extended to product recommendation datasets.

2.
Related Work

The key related works fall into the following categories:

Mobility Prediction:

Recent sequential POI prediction models consider the checkin trajectory for each user as a sequence of events and utilize an RNN based learning
(
Cheng
et al. 2013
;
Manotumruksa et al. 2018
;
Yuan et al. 2013
)
with some variants that incorporate the spatial features as well
(
Liu
et al. 2016
;
Feng
et al. 2018
)
. Another approach
(
Figueiredo et al. 2016
)
is a generic model for predicting user trajectories as well as next product recommendation. Recent approaches for checkin time prediction are limited to a single dataset
(
Chen
et al. 2020
;
Yang
et al. 2018
;
Likhyani et al. 2020
)
. They also model event-times as random variables rather than sequential flows and thus cannot be used for transfer across regions.

Temporal Point Process:

In recent years TPPs have emerged as a powerful tool to model asynchronous events localized in continuous time
(
Daley and
Vere-Jones 2007
;
Hawkes 1971
)
, which have a wide variety of applications, e.g information diffusion, disease modeling, finance, etc. Driven by these motivations, in recent years, there has been a surge of works on TPPs
(
Rizoiu
et al. 2018
;
Gupta
et al. 2021
;
Farajtabar et al. 2017
)
. Modeling the event sequences via a neural network led to further developments including neural Hawkes process
(
Mei and Eisner 2017
)
and several other neural models of TPPs
(
Xiao
et al. 2017b
;
Xiao
et al. 2017a
;
Omi
et al. 2019
)
, but cannot incorporate heterogeneous features as in a spatial networks. The approach most similar to our model is
(
Shchur
et al. 2020
)
that learns the inter-event time intervals using NFs, but ignores the spatial dynamics and is limited to a single data source.

3.
Problem Setup

We consider the mobility records for two regions with non-overlapping locations and users,
origin
and
target
as
\mathcal{R}^{\text{org}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\text{tgt}}
respectively. For any region, we represent a user trajectory as a sequence of checkins represented by
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{e_{i}=(c_{i},t_{i},d_{i})|i\in[k],t_{i}<t_{i+1},d_{i}<d_{i+1}\}
, where
t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the checkin time,
d_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the total distance traveled, and
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
is a discrete category of the
i
-th checkin with
\mathcal{C}
as the set of all categories, and
\mathcal{S}_{k}
denotes the first
k
checkins. We represent the inter-checkin times and distances as,
\Delta_{t,k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}
and
\Delta_{d,k}=d_{k}-d_{k-1}
respectively and model their distribution using NFs. Our goal is to capture these region invariant dynamics in origin region for mobility prediction in target region,
i.e.
given the checkin sequence for target region,
\mathcal{S}^{\text{tgt}}_{K}
and the MTPP trained on origin, we aim to predict the time and category of the
next
checkin,
e^{\text{tgt}}_{K+1}
.

4.
Model Description

We divide the working of
Reformd
into two parts: (i) the neural MTPP to capture mobility dynamics specific to a region, and (ii) transfer of NFs trained on the origin region to the target region. The overall schematic of
Reformd
is given in Figure
1
.

4.1.
Region-Specific MTPP

We model the checkin sequences using an MTPP that we build on a recurrent neural network (RNN). The RNN is used to obtain time-conditioned vector representation of sequences as in
(
Du et al. 2016
;
Mei and Eisner 2017
;
Omi
et al. 2019
)
. Later, via these embeddings we estimate the mark distribution and inter-event time and space densities sing a three-stage architecture:

Input stage:

In this stage we represent the incoming checkin at index
k
,
e_{k}
using a suitable vector embedding,
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
as:

(1)

\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=\boldsymbol{w}_{c}c_{k}+\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\Delta_{t,k}+\boldsymbol{w}_{d}\Delta_{d,k}+\boldsymbol{b}_{v},

where
\boldsymbol{w}_{\bullet},\boldsymbol{b}_{\bullet}
are trainable parameters and
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
denotes the vector embedding for checkin
e_{k}
respectively.

Update stage:
In this stage, we update the hidden state representation of the RNN to include the current checkin
e_{k}
as:

(2)

\boldsymbol{s}_{k}=\tanh(\boldsymbol{G}_{s}\boldsymbol{s}_{k-1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{v}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\Delta_{t,k}+\boldsymbol{g}_{d}\Delta_{d,k}+\boldsymbol{b}_{s}),

where
\boldsymbol{G}_{\bullet},\boldsymbol{g}_{\bullet},\boldsymbol{b}_{\bullet}
are trainable parameters and
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
denotes the RNN hidden state, i.e. a
cumulative
embedding for all previous checkins till the current time
t_{k}
.

.
Architecture of
Reformd
with flow-based transfer between origin region (red) and target region (blue).

Output stage:

Given the trajectory embedding
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
, we predict the
next
checkin time and the checkin category. Unlike
(
Du et al. 2016
;
Mei and Eisner 2017
)
that learn the time distribution using the RNN hidden state, we model the
density
of arrival times using a
LogNormal

(
Shchur
et al. 2020
)
flow denoted as
p_{t}(\Delta_{t,k+1})
conditioned on
s_{k}
as:

(3)

p_{t}(\Delta_{t,k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k})=\texttt{LogNormal}\big(\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}),\sigma^{2}_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k})\big),

with
[\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}),\sigma^{2}_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k})]=[\boldsymbol{W}_{\mu}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{W}_{\sigma^{2}}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\sigma^{2}}_{t}]
denote the
mean
and
variance
of the time distribution. Such a formulation reduces model complexity, facilitates faster training and sampling in a closed-form
(
Shchur
et al. 2020
)
.

To predict the time of the next checkin, we sample the probable time difference between the current and the next checkin as
\Delta_{t,k+1}\thicksim\mathcal{L}\mathcal{N}_{t}\big(\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}),\sigma^{2}_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k})\big)
, where
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{N}_{t}
denotes the learned log-normal parameters. The actual time of the next checkin is the sum of the sampled time difference and the
current
checkin time,
\widehat{t_{k+1}}=t_{k}+\Delta_{t,k+1}
. Similar to the temporal flow, we also model the inter-checkin density of spatial distances using a log-normal denoted as
p_{d}(\Delta_{d,k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k})
. We interpret this distribution as the
spatial
flow for a region.

The inter-location spatial distance plays a crucial role in determining the next POI
(
Cho
et al. 2011
;
Manotumruksa et al. 2018
)
. Unlike time, the distances between two checkin locations are
unchanged
throughout the data. Previous approaches
(
Du et al. 2016
;
Mei and Eisner 2017
)
ignore these spatial features and rely solely on the past checkin-categories.
Moreover, in a sequential setting the distance that the user will travel for her next checkin is not known. Our MTPPs, being generative models, and spatial flows overcome this drawback as we can sample the probable travel distance for the next checkin from the spatial flow as
\Delta_{d,k+1}\thicksim\mathcal{L}\mathcal{N}_{d}\big(\mu_{d}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}),\sigma^{2}_{d}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k})\big)
. Then, for predicting the next checkin, we use the sampled distance
\Delta_{d,k+1}
and RNN hidden state
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
via an attention weighted embedding
(
Bahdanau
et al. 2015
)
.

(4)

\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k}=\boldsymbol{s}_{k}+\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\Delta_{d,k+1},

where
\alpha,\boldsymbol{w}_{f}
denote the attention weight, a trainable parameter and
\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k}
denotes the
updated
hidden state. We then predict the next checkin category as:

(5)

\mathbb{P}(c_{k+1}=c|\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k})=\frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{V}_{s,c}\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k}+\boldsymbol{b}_{s,c})}{\sum_{\forall c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\exp(\boldsymbol{V}_{s,c^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k}+\boldsymbol{b}_{s,c^{\prime}})},

where
\boldsymbol{V}_{s,\bullet},\boldsymbol{b}_{s,\bullet}
are trainable parameters and
\bullet
denotes the entry corresponding to a category.
\mathbb{P}(c_{k+1}=c|\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k})
denotes the probability of next checkin being of category
c
with
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

Optimization:

Given the set of all sequences
\mathcal{S}
for a region
\mathcal{R}
, we maximize the joint likelihood for the next checkin, the log-normal density distribution of spatial, and temporal normalizing flows.

(6)

\mathscr{L}=\sum_{\forall\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\log\big(\mathbb{P}(c_{k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}^{*}_{k})\cdot p_{t}(\Delta_{t,k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k})\cdot p_{d}(\Delta_{d,k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k})\big).

where
\mathscr{L}
denotes the joint likelihood, which we represent as the sum of the likelihoods for all user sequences. We learn the parameters of
Reformd
using Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
optimizer.

4.2.
Flow-based Transfer

For transferring the mobility parameters across the regions, we follow the standard transfer learning procedure
(
Pan and Yang 2010
;
Szegedy et al. 2015
)
of training exclusively on the
origin
region and then fine-tuning for the target region. However, the affinity of a user towards a POI evolves with time
(
Cho
et al. 2011
;
Likhyani
et al. 2017
)
. For example, a POI with frequent user-checkins during the summer season might not be an attractive option in winters. We include these insights by training multiple independent normalizing flows each for spatial and temporal densities. Specifically, we cluster the checkin sequences in the origin region into
\mathcal{M}
equal clusters based on the
median
of the occurrence times for all the checkins. Later, for each cluster of sequences, we train spatial and temporal flows independently. In this setting our net likelihood changes as to include the sum of all
\mathcal{M}
likelihoods,
\mathscr{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{M}}\mathscr{L}_{i}
, where
\mathscr{L}_{i}
is the joint likelihood for trajectories in clusters
\mathcal{M}
.

As in origin region, we divide the user trajectories in the target region as well into
\mathcal{M}
clusters and for trajectories in target-cluster
m^{\text{tgt}}_{i}
we attentively factor the spatial and temporal flows corresponding to origin-cluster
m^{\text{org}}_{i}
. Mathematically, for the temporal flows in the target region our density of arrival times changes to:

(7)

[\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}),\sigma^{2}_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k})]^{\text{tgt}}=[\boldsymbol{W}_{\mu}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\mu}+\phi_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\text{org}}_{t},\boldsymbol{W}_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\sigma}+\phi_{t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\text{org}}_{t}],

where
\boldsymbol{s}_{k},\phi_{t},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\text{org}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\text{org}}_{t}
denotes the hidden state representation for the target region, attention parameter for temporal flow and the learned flow parameters of
mean
and
variance
for cluster
m_{i}
in origin region. Similarly, our spatial flows for target region include the origin flow parameters with an attention parameter
\phi_{d}
. For faster convergence, we share
\phi_{t}
and
\phi_{d}
across all
\mathcal{M}
. Other model components are same as in origin region and we maximize the joint likelihood for target region as in Equation
6
.

We highlight that our choice to divide the sequences based on
median
of user trajectories rather than the individual checkin locations is driven by the following technical point: in the latter case, the
net flow
–be it spatial or temporal– would be the sum of lognormal flows for each set. Such a formulation is undesirable since the result is neither
closed
nor does it remain a lognormal
(
Daniel 2004
)
, thus requiring involved techniques to approximate them
(
Barouch
et al. 1986
)
, which we would like to explore in future work. However with the current formulation, we can learn the parameters of different flows independently.

Experimental Settings

Dataset Description:

We use POI data from Foursquare
(
Yang
et al. 2019

in United States(US) and Japan(JP) and for each country we construct 4 datasets: one with large check-in data and three with limited data. The statistics of all datasets is given in Table

For each region, we consider the time of checkin and category as event time and mark and normalize the times based on the minimum and maximum event times. We set the embedding and RNN hidden dimension to
64
and
\mathcal{M}=3
for all our experiments. Other values for the model parameter had negligible differences.

Evaluation Protocol:

We split each stream of say
N
checkins
\mathcal{S}_{N}
into training and test set, where the training set (test set) consists of first 80% (last 20%) checkin. We evaluate models using standard metrics
(
Du et al. 2016
)
of (i) mean absolute error (MAE) of predicted and actual checkin times,
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{e_{i}\in\mathcal{S}}[|t_{i}-\widehat{t}_{i}|]
and (ii) mark (checkin category) prediction accuracy (MPA), i.e,
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{e_{i}\in\mathcal{S}}\#(c_{i}=\widehat{c}_{i})
. Here
\widehat{t_{i}}
and
\widehat{c_{i}}
are the predicted time and category of the
i
-th checkin. Moreover, the clustering of sequences into different sets is done based solely on the training data and using these thresholds we assign clusters to sequences in the test data.

5.3.
Prediction Performance (RQ1)

We report the prediction performance of different methods across our target datasets in Table
2
and make the following observations:

\bullet

Reformd
consistently yields the best performance on all the datasets. In particular, it improves over the strongest baselines by 10% and 19% for category and time prediction respectively. These results indicate the importance of spatial and temporal flow-based transfer from external data for prediction in limited-data regions.

\bullet

RMTPP
(
Du et al. 2016
)
is the second-best performer in terms of MAE of time prediction almost for all the datasets. For some datasets THP
(
Zuo
et al. 2020
)
outperforms RMTPP for mark category prediction. However
Reformd
significantly outperforms it across all metrics.

Qualitative Analysis:
We also perform a qualitative analysis to demonstrate how
Reformd
is able to model the checkin time distribution. For this, we plot the actual inter-checkin time differences and the difference time predicted by
Reformd
in Figure
2
for Virginia and Aichi datasets. From the results we note that the predicted inter-arrival times closely match with the true inter-arrival times and
Reformd
