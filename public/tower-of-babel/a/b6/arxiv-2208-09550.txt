Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP, and a new proof of the local convexity of the TAP free energy
Michael Celentano

Abstract
In many problems in modern statistics and machine learning, it is often of interest to establish that a first order method on a non-convex risk function eventually enters a region of parameter space in which the risk is locally convex. We derive an asymptotic comparison inequality, which we call the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality , which, in a certain class of problems involving a GOE {\rm GOE} matrix, is able to probe properties of an optimization landscape locally around the iterates of an approximate message passing (AMP) algorithm. As an example of its use, we provide a new, and arguably simpler, proof of some of the results of [ CFM21 ] , which establishes that the so-called TAP free energy in the ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -synchronization problem is locally convex in the region to which AMP converges. We further prove a conjecture of [ AMS22 ] involving the local convexity of a related but distinct TAP free energy, which, as a consequence, confirms that their algorithm efficiently samples from the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Gibbs measure throughout the “easy” regime.



1
Introduction

Many problems in modern statistics and machine-learning involve optimizing a non-convex loss function.
In many instances,
first-order methods like gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent,
approximate message passing, and related algorithms have been observed, both empirically and theoretically, to perform well even when the objective is highly non-convex.
Examples include phase retrieval
[
CLS15
,
CC15
,
SWW17
,
CCFM19
,
CLM16
]
,
matrix completion,
blind deconvolution
[
MWCC20
]
,
neural networks
[
VSL+22
,
GAS+19
,
SMBC+20a
]
, spiked matrix and tensor estimation
[
MZ20
,
MKUZ19
,
SMBC+19
,
SMBC+20b
]
, Gaussian mixture classification
[
MKUZ20
]
, among others.
In all of these examples, there are regimes in which first-order methods successfully find points with near-minimal objective value or learn parameters with good statistical performance.
Substantial research effort has been dedicated— and continues to be dedicated —to understanding what drives the success or failure of these methods in non-convex settings.

In some instances, the success of first-order methods can be explained by the following two-stage phenomenon
[
CCFM19
,
CFM21
]
.
Upon initialization,
the first-order method is in a region of parameter space where the landscape is non-convex.
In the first stage,
the algorithm makes progress towards the global optimum or builds correlation with the unknown signal,
despite the non-convexity of the surrounding landscape.
Often,
the analysis in this stage is primarily statistical:
in each step, the randomness in the data generating distribution is used to argue that, with high-probability, the algorithm continues to make progress towards the global optimum or the hidden signal.
This statistical analysis typically fails after sufficiently many iterations because— without resampling —the iterates become correlated with the randomness in the data in a complex way which is difficult to control.
It is important, then, that the algorithm eventually enters a second stage, in which it arrives in a region of parameter space which is locally convex.
Now,
the analysis becomes geometric:
the algorithm makes progress towards a local optimum based on standard results from convex optimization theory.
Because the statistical analysis in the first stage fails after sufficiently many iterations,
it is important that the argument in the second stage is geometric rather than statistical.
Once the local geometry of the landscape is established,
the analysis becomes worst-case and deterministic.

In this paper,
we present a comparison inequality—
the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality
—which in a certain class of problems is able to probe properties of an optimization landscape locally around the iterates of an approximate message passing algorithm.
Thus, it is well suited to establishing the two-stage phenomenon described in the previous paragraph when AMP is used in the first stage.
Recent papers
[
CMW20
,
MW22
,
CCM21
]
have shown that a large class of first-order methods,
including gradient descent, accelerated gradient descent, and related algorithms on appropriate optimization objectives,
are equivalent to AMP algorithms after a change of variables.
Thus, the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality can also probe the local landscape surrounding the iterates of such methods.

As an example,
we consider in detail the problem of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization and the corresponding optimization problem based on the TAP free energy.
This model and optimization problem are defined in Section
1.1
.
We demonstrate how to use the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality to probe the landscape of the TAP free energy locally around iterates of an AMP algorithm tailored to this model.
This provides a new— and arguably simpler —proof of one of the main results in the paper
[
CFM21
]
,
namely, that AMP eventually enters a region of strong convexity around a stationary point of the objective.
More recently,
the paper
[
AMS22
]
considered the problem of efficiently sampling from the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Hamiltonian,
which we also define in Section
1.1
.
One part of their proof requires considering a different but closely related version of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization problem and TAP free energy.
In order to establish the success of their algorithm,
they require establishing the convergence of AMP to a region of this TAP free energy which is strongly convex.
Our proof of the local convexity of the TAP free energy considered by
[
CFM21
]
is easily modified to arrive at the same result for the TAP free energy considered by
[
AMS22
]
.
This confirms that the algorithm of
[
AMS22
]

successfully samples from the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Hamiltonian throughout the computationally easy regime,
resolving the conjecture of computational tractability.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
1.1
,
we provide the necessary background on the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization problem and its corresponding approximate message passing algorithm and TAP free energy.
In Section
1.2
,
we provide an overview of our results.
In Section
1.3
,

The Sudakov-Fernique inequality is presented in Section

,

3
.

4
, which includes the application of the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality.

1.1
Background on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization and the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model

In the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization problem,
we observe the matrix

{\bm{Y}}=\frac{\lambda}{n}{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}+{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

(1)

where
{\bm{W}}\sim{\rm GOE}(n)
, and

x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}\{-1,+1\}
are unobserved and
\lambda
is known.
The goal is to estimate the unobserved vector
{\bm{x}}
with respect to
\ell_{2}
-loss.
The observations Eq. (
1
) only identify
{\bm{x}}
up to sign:
the posterior
\mathbb{P}({\bm{x}}\in\cdot\mid{\bm{Y}})
is
equal to the the posterior
\mathbb{P}(-{\bm{x}}\in\cdot\mid{\bm{Y}})
.
Thus, we seek an estimator
\widehat{\bm{m}}{}=\widehat{\bm{m}}{}({\bm{Y}})
for which
\mathbb{E}[\min\{\|\widehat{\bm{m}}{}\pm{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\}]/n
is small.

Several aspects of this problem are now well-understood from both an information-theoretic and computational point of view.
The paper
[
DAM16
]
studied the asymptotic Bayes risk in this model,
and established that for
\lambda<1
,
it is impossible to do better asymptotically (as
n\rightarrow\infty
) than the trivial estimator
\widehat{\bm{m}}{}({\bm{Y}})={\bm{0}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}]
.
For
\lambda>1
,
weak recovery becomes information-theoretically possible:
the Bayes estimator
\widehat{\bm{m}}{}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
achieves a non-trivial correlation with the signal,
and
\mathbb{E}[\min\{\|\widehat{\bm{m}}{}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\pm{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\}]/n
converges to a constant in
(0,1)
which
[
DAM16
]
characterize via the solution to a non-linear fixed-point equation.
Moreover, for all
\lambda>1
,
[
DAM16
]
proposed a practical algorithm— a so-called Approximate Message Passing (AMP) algorithm —which they proved achieves the Bayes risk up to asymptotically negligible terms provided it was initialized appropriately.
Although they conjectured that their results would hold when using the leading eigenvector of
{\bm{Y}}
as an initialization,
for technical reasons they only considered initializations based on independent side information.
For example,
their results handle initializations of the form

{\bm{y}}=\gamma_{0}{\bm{x}}+\sqrt{\gamma_{0}}{\bm{g}},

(2)

for some
\gamma_{0}>0
arbitrarily small but non-vanishing asymptotically,
where
{\bm{g}}\sim{\sf N}(0,{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
.
Explicitly, their algorithm takes the form

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{0}

\displaystyle={\bm{y}},\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle{\bm{m}}^{s}

\displaystyle=\tanh({\bm{z}}^{s}),

(3)

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{s+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\bm{Y}}{\bm{m}}^{s}-\lambda\widehat{\mathsf{b}}{}_{s}{\bm{m}}^{s-1},\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{b}}{}_{s}

\displaystyle=\lambda(1-Q({\bm{m}}^{s})),

where
{\bm{m}}\in[-1,1]^{n}
,
Q({\bm{m}}):=\|{\bm{m}}\|_{2}^{2}/n
,
and
\tanh(\,\cdot\,)
is applied coordinate-wise.
They show that

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{\bm{m}}{}^{s}-{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\min\{\|\widehat{\bm{m}}{}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\pm{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\}],

(4)

where the limits in
n
are taken almost-surely.
Note that the initialization in Eq. (
2
) breaks the symmetry, so that no minimum is needed inside the expectation on the left-hand side.
Nevertheless, it is unsatisfying for the result to rely on small but non-negligible side information,
which may not be available.
The later paper
[
MV21a
]
establishes the same result as
[
DAM16
]
when a spectral initialization is used;
that is,
{\bm{z}}^{0}
is taken to be the leading eigenvector of
{\bm{Y}}
, appropriately scaled.
For simplicity,
in this paper we will study the algorithm of
[
DAM16
]
with initialization (
2
),
although our results are easily extended to the case in which a spectral initialization is used (see Remark
1
).

An alternative but related approach is based on optimization.
This approach,
studied by
[
FMM21
,
CFM21
]
,
is based on minimizing— or attempting to minimize —the so-called TAP free energy

{\mathcal{F}}({\bm{m}};{\bm{Y}}):=-\frac{\lambda}{2n}{\bm{m}}^{\top}{\bm{Y}}{\bm{m}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathsf{h}}(m_{i})-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}(1-Q({\bm{m}}))^{2},

(5)

over the domain
{\bm{m}}\in[-1,1]^{n}
,
where
{\mathsf{h}}(m):=-\frac{1+m}{2}\log\frac{1+m}{2}-\frac{1-m}{2}\log\frac{1-m}{2}
is the binary entropy function.
The TAP free energy can be seen as a correction to a ‘naïve mean field’ free energy which was first derived in the context of spin-glasses in statistical physics
[
TAP77
]
.
The TAP free energy is related to the AMP algorithm Eq. (
3
):
the fixed-points of Eq. (
3
)
exactly correspond to the stationary points of
{\mathcal{F}}(\,\cdot\,;{\bm{Y}})
.
In fact, approximate message passing algorithms were first derived as an iterative construction of solution to the TAP equation
\nabla{\mathcal{F}}({\bm{m}};{\bm{y}})=0

[
Bol14
]
.
It is widely believed that the global minimizer of the TAP free energy asymptotically achieves Bayes optimal performance,
and the papers
[
FMM21
,
CFM21
]
have made progress towards making this conjecture rigorous.

[
FMM21
]
establishes that for sufficiently large
\lambda
,
all stationary points of the TAP free energy with sufficiently small objective value are clustered in a small region around the Bayes estimate, and so asymptotically achieve Bayes optimal performance.
The paper
[
CFM21
]
showed that for all
\lambda>1
,
the TAP free energy has a unique local minimizer close to the Bayes estimate,
and established its global minimality subject to a conjecture that was checked numerically.
Thus,
there is strong evidence that minimizing the TAP free energy achieves asymptotically optimal performance throughout the weak-recovery regime
\lambda>1
.
One potential advantage of the TAP formulation, however, is that it opens the possibility to use methods like gradient descent or other descent algorithms to construct estimates of
{\bm{x}}
.
Our purpose here is not to give a complete account of the origin and merits of the TAP approach.
We refer the interested reader to the discussions in
[
TAP77
,
MPV87
,
FMM21
,
CFM21
]
and references therein.
Rather, for our purposes,
the TAP free energy serves as an example of the use of the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality to probe the local structure of a non-convex objective around the iterates of an AMP algorithm.
Indeed, our approach will reproduce some of the results of
[
CFM21
]
, but arguably in a simpler way.

More recently,
the paper
[
AMS22
]
considered the problem of sampling from the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Hamiltonian.
In particular, for a matrix
{\bm{W}}\sim{\rm GOE}(n)
,
one would like to sample from the measure on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
with density relative to he uniform measure

\mu_{{\bm{W}}}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{Z(\lambda,{\bm{W}})}e^{(\lambda/2)\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}\rangle},

(6)

where
Z(\lambda,{\bm{W}})
is a normalizing constant.
The authors propose a randomized algorithm which runs in polynomial time, takes as input the matrix
{\bm{W}}
, and outputs a random vector
{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{alg}}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
with distribution, conditional on
{\bm{W}}
, denoted by
\mu_{{\bm{W}}}^{\mathrm{alg}}
.
For an appropriately chosen distance
\mathsf{dist}
on probability measures (in this case, the
\ell_{2}
-Wasserstein distance, which we will define in Section
1.4
),
the authors show that
\mathsf{dist}(\mu_{\bm{W}},\mu_{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{alg}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{p}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
when
\lambda<1/2
,
where the probabalistic convergence is over randomness in
{\bm{W}}
and auxiliary randomness in the algorithm.
Further, for
\lambda>1
,
they present an impossibility result:
no algorithm which satisfies a certain stability property is able to sample from
\mu_{{\bm{W}}}
in the sense
\mathsf{dist}(\mu_{\bm{W}},\mu_{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{alg}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{p}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.
A phase transition occurs at
\lambda=1
in the structure of the measure
\mu_{{\bm{W}}}
which forms the basis of this impossibility result, and which has led to the conjecture that sampling from the SK measure for
\lambda>1
is impossible for any polynomial time algorithm, not just those satisfying the given stability property.
Conversely, it is conjectured that for
\lambda<1
,
sampling is possible in polynomial time, and in fact, the authors of
[
AMS22
]
conjecture their algorithm achieves this.
Indeed, for
\lambda\in[1/2,1)
,
their proof breaks down in only one step,
in which the local strong convexity of a certain TAP free energy, similar but distinct from the one in Eq. (
5
), must be but has not been established.

We will not in this paper describe the details of the SK-sampling algorithm, and refer the interested reader to
[
AMS22
]
for details.
The step in which their argument breaks down occurs in a sub-routine which estimates the posterior mean of
{\bm{x}}
in the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization model (
1
) with side information (
2
).
Note that above, although we use
{\bm{y}}
as an initialization,
our interest was in computing the Bayes estimator given
\mathbb{P}({\bm{x}}\in\cdot|{\bm{Y}})
;
now we will be interested in computing the mean of
\mathbb{P}({\bm{x}}\in\,\cdot\,|{\bm{Y}},{\bm{y}})
.
This can be computed,
as above, using an AMP algorithm or by minimizing a certain TAP free energy whose stationary points agree with the fixed points of the AMP algorithm.
Because we have a different target,
the AMP algorithm used by
[
AMS22
]
is modified to

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{0}

\displaystyle={\bm{y}},\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle{\bm{m}}^{s}

\displaystyle=\tanh({\bm{z}}^{s}),

(7)

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{s+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\bm{Y}}{\bm{m}}^{s}+{\bm{y}}-\lambda\widehat{\mathsf{b}}{}_{s}{\bm{m}}^{s-1},\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{b}}{}_{s}

\displaystyle=\lambda(1-Q({\bm{m}}^{s})),

and the TAP free energy they use is modified to

{\mathcal{F}}({\bm{m}};{\bm{Y}}):=-\frac{\lambda}{2n}{\bm{m}}^{\top}{\bm{Y}}{\bm{m}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathsf{h}}(m_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\langle{\bm{y}},{\bm{m}}\rangle-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})(1+q_{\infty}-2Q({\bm{m}}))}{4},

(8)

where
q_{\infty}
is a deterministic constant which we will specify below in Proposition
3
.
We will show that with high-probability as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
the AMP algorithm (
7
) in finitely many iterations arrives in a region of space in which the TAP free energy (
8
) is locally strongly convex and contains a unique stationary point.
We establish this for any
\lambda>0
.
For
\lambda\in[1/2,1)
,
this closes the only gap in the proof of
[
AMS22
]
and confirms that in this regime,
their algorithm samples from the SK Gibbs measure in polynomial time in the sense of

\mathsf{dist}(\mu_{\bm{W}},\mu_{\bm{W}}^{\mathrm{alg}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{p}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.

1.2
Overview of results

We now provide a brief overview of our results.

1.

We prove a comparison inequality—
the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality
—which allows one to study properties of an optimization landscape defined in terms of the iterates of an approximate message passing algorithm.
Strictly speaking, this is not a new comparison inequality:
in fact, it is nothing but the original Sudakov-Fernique inequality applied to a matrix whose distribution conditional on the AMP iterates is drawn from a Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble.
Moreover, the idea of applying the Sudakov-Fernique inequality conditionally on linear constraints is not new; for example, it was used extensively in
[
CFM21
]
.
Our main insight is to identify certain structure in the conditional inequality which facilitates its analysis,
and to write the inequality in a way which better elucidates this structure.
The structure we identify is as follows:
whereas the original Sudakov-Fernique inequality involves an optimization involving a single high-dimensional Gaussian vector,
the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality involves an optimization involving a finite number of high-dimensional approximately-Gaussian vectors.
This structure greatly simplifies the analysis of the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality.

2.

We provide a novel proof that the TAP free energy of
[
FMM21
]
is strongly convex in a local region around the AMP fixed point.
Moreover, we show that for sufficiently large (but fixed)
k
,
the
k^{\text{th}}
iterate of the AMP algorithm arrives in this local region of strong convexity with asymptotically high-probability.
This result already appeared in
[
CFM21
]
,
but our analysis simplifies several key steps,
and in particular,
avoids using the Kac-Rice formula.
In fact, the structure described above allows us to reduce the analysis of the Sudakov-Fernique inequality conditioned on a large number of AMP iterates to a certain low-dimensional optimization problem which was already analyzed in
[
CFM21
]
.

3.

We apply our proof technique to the modified TAP free energy and AMP iteration considered in
[
AMS22
]
,
establishing for the first time the local strong convexity of their TAP free energy around sufficiently late iterates of the AMP algorithm.
This confirms their conjecture that the algorithm they present successfully samples from the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Gibbs measure for all
\lambda<1
.

We are able to analyze the TAP free energy of
[
CFM21
]
and of
[
AMS22
]
in almost exactly the same way,
and, in fact, our presentation of the two arguments is unified.
We feel that the ability to seamlessly analyze both problems at once is emblematic of the simplicity of the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP approach we develop.

1.3
Related literature

The Sudakov-Fernique inequality was developed in
[
Sud71
,
Sud79
,
Fer75
]
,
which built on the Slepian inequality of
[
Sle62
]
.
The Sudakov-Fernique inequality was used extensively in the analysis of the TAP free energy in
[
CFM21
]
,
and the analysis in this paper is inspired by the analysis which occurs there.

[
CFM21
]
apply the Sudakov-Fernique inequality after conditioning on stationarity of the TAP free energy at a point
{\bm{m}}
chosen
a priori
.
The TAP free energy in general has exponentially many stationary points,
and the authors are interested in local convexity around stationary points
{\bm{m}}
which have been chosen
a posteriori
using the AMP algorithm.
Thus, sophisticated arguments involving the Kac-Rice formula are required to show that the TAP free energy landscape around stationary points chosen a priori behave likes the landscape around points chosen a posteriori in this way.
We avoid this problem by conditioning on the full sequence of AMP iterates, and thus avoid the Kac-Rice formula.
Nevertheless, some keys steps in our argument, which we will point out in our proofs, are borrowed directly from
[
CFM21
]
.

The use of the Sudakov-Fernique inequality to study optimization problems in spiked
{\rm GOE}
models is related to the use of Gordon’s comparison inequality to study optimization problems in regression models.
Gordon’s inequality was first developed in
[
Gor85
,
Gor88
]
,
and has recently been applied to study regression estimates in
[
Sto13
,
TAH15
,
TOH15
,
MM21
,
CM21
]
.
The paper
[
CM21
]
used Gordon conditionally on the stationary point of an optimization problem, and arrived at a conditional comparison inequality with similar structure to that of the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality.

The sharp characterization of Approximate Message Passing algorithms using state evolution was developed in
[
Bol14
,
BM10
,
JM13
,
BMN19
]
.
The Bayes risk in the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization problem and the Bayes optimality of the corresponding AMP algorithm was first studied by
[
DAM16
]
,
and the paper
[
MV21b
]
extended the analysis to AMP with spectral initialization.
The TAP free energy was first introduced by physicists as a variational formula for the free energy in the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
[
TAP77
]
.
Substantial literature has studied the relationship between the TAP free energy and the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Gibbs measure and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization posterior.
We refer the reader to the discussions in
[
FMM21
,
CFM21
]
for pointers to the relevant literature.
As we have already described, this paper builds on the paper
[
CFM21
,
AMS22
]
,
which study the local convexity of the TAP free energy in the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization model and sampling from the Sherrington-Kirkipatrick Hamiltonian, respectively.

(a_{i},\breve{\bm{a}}_{i})
.
We will denote by
\mathbb{S}_{>0}^{m}
the space of
m\times m

We denote by
\mathbb{S}_{>0}^{\infty}
the space of bi-infinite symmetric matrices whose finite diagonal sub-matrices are all positive definite.
For a matrix
{\bm{A}}
,
we denote by
\|{\bm{A}}\|_{{\rm op}}
its operator norm.
For a vector

,
we denote by
\|{\bm{x}}\|
its Euclidean norm.
For any
n
, we denote by
\mathsf{B}_{2}(0,R)
the Euclidean ball in

with radius

centered at the origin:
\mathsf{B}_{2}(0,R)=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq n\}

The dimension will always be clear from context.

2
The Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality

First, we recall the following form of classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality

[
Sud71
,
Sud79
,
Fer75
]
.

Proposition 1

(Sudakov-Fernique inequality)
.

Let
{\bm{W}}\sim{\rm GOE}(n)
,

{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\sf N}({\bm{0}},{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
,
and for
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
let
{\bm{g}}({\bm{v}}):=\|{\bm{v}}\|{\bm{\xi}}
.
Then for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
closed set
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
,
and continuous function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}})\in K}{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}+f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})\geq t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}})\in K}\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\langle{\bm{g}}({\bm{v}}),{\bm{v}}\rangle+f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})\geq t\right).

(12)

(Here
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for some non-negative integer
m
).

Typically we would like to understand the optimization problem on the left-hand side of Eq. (
12
),
but this is difficult because it involves a
{\rm GOE}(n)
matrix.
The utility of the Sudakov-Fernique inequality is that it allows us to instead study the optimization problem on the right-hand side of Eq. (
12
), which is substantially easier because it involves a random vector.
The Sudakov-Fernique inequality states that this simpler optimization problem is stochastically larger than the complicated one involving a
{\rm GOE}(n)
matrix.

An important insight of this paper is that the comparison inequality we arrive at by applying Sudakov-Fernique after a finite number of iterations of an approximate message passing algorithm has a structure remarkably similar to that in Eq. (
12
).
Although we will apply these results to the particular AMP algorithms in Eqs. (
3
) and (
7
),
we will state the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality for a more general AMP algorithm,
as appears in, for example,
[
JM13
]
.
We will then specialize it to AMP for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization problem.

2.1
Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP: the general case

An AMP algorithm is defined by a sequence of
L_{s}
-Lipschitz functions
f_{s}:{\mathbb{R}}^{s+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
for some sequence of Lipschitz constants
L_{s}
.
The algorithm takes the form

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{0}

\displaystyle\sim{\sf N}(0,{\mathbf{I}}_{n}),\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle{\bm{m}}^{s}

\displaystyle=f_{s}({\bm{g}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{s}),

(13)

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{s+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}{\bm{m}}^{s}-\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathsf{b}}_{sj}{\bm{m}}^{j-1},

where
{\mathsf{b}}_{sj}
are carefully chosen deterministic constants which we will define below.
The functions
f_{s}
are applied row-wise to
{\bm{g}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{s}
;
that is,
f_{s}({\bm{g}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{s})_{i}=f_{s}(g^{0}_{i},\ldots,g^{s}_{i})
.
The iterations in Eqs. (
3
) and (
7
) are not in the form given by Eq. (
13
),
but are well approximated by an iteration in this form,
so that, using an argument that is standard in the AMP literature, our main result will extend to these iterations.

The behavior of the iterates of the AMP algorithm are well described by a certain scalar iteration called
state evolution
.
State evolution is given by a bi-infinite covariance matrix
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{\geq 0}

defined inductively by

K_{s+1,t+1}=\mathbb{E}[f_{s}(G_{0},\ldots,G_{s})f_{t}(G_{0},\ldots,G_{t})],

(14)

where
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})\sim{\sf N}(0,\bm{K}_{\leq t})
independent of
G_{0}\sim{\sf N}(0,1)
.
Note that the base case of this definition is handled automatically:
K_{1,1}=\mathbb{E}[f_{0}(G_{0})^{2}]
, where
G_{0}\sim{\sf N}(0,1)
.
Then, for
1\leq j\leq s
,

{\mathsf{b}}_{sj}:=\mathbb{E}[\partial_{G_{j}}f_{s}(G_{0},\ldots,G_{s})],

(15)

where
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{t})
and
G_{0}
have the distribution given by the state evolution.
Here, the derivative exists almost surely by Rademacher’s theorem.

The state evolution for AMP is given by the following theorem, proved, for example, in
[
JM13
]
.

Proposition 2

(State evolution)
.

For
{\bm{W}}\sim{\rm GOE}(n)
and any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
the empirical distribution of the coordinates of the AMP iteration converges almost surely
in
W_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{k+1})
as follows:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(g_{i}^{0},\ldots,g_{i}^{k+1})}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{W_{2}}{\sf N}(0,1)\times{\sf N}(0,\bm{K}_{\leq k}).

(16)

In particular, if
G_{0}\sim{\sf N}(0,1)
independent of
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})\sim{\sf N}(0,\bm{K}_{\leq k})
and, for
0\leq s\leq k
,
M_{s}:=f_{s}(G_{0},\ldots,G_{s})
,
we get almost surely

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(m_{i}^{0},\ldots,m_{i}^{k},g_{i}^{0},\ldots,g_{i}^{k+1})}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{W_{2}}(M_{0},\ldots,M_{k},G_{0},\ldots,G_{k}).

(17)

Proposition
2
states that, in a certain sense,
the iterates
{\bm{g}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{k}
behave like high-dimensional jointly Gaussian vectors with a covariance described by the state evolution matrix
\bm{K}_{\leq k}
.
A consequence of Proposition
2
and the definition of
\bm{K}
in Eq. (
14
) is that the geometry of the iterates
{\bm{m}}^{s}
agrees with the geometry of the iterates
{\bm{g}}^{s+1}
.
In particular,
if we let
{\bm{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k}
be the matrix whose
s^{\text{th}}
column is
{\bm{m}}^{s-1}
,
and
{\bm{G}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k}
be the matrix whose
s^{\text{th}}
column is
{\bm{g}}^{s}
,
we have almost surely

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\bm{G}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}=\bm{K}_{\leq k}.

(18)

Provided
\bm{K}_{\leq k}
is positive definite,
both
\{{\bm{m}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{k-1}\}
and
\{{\bm{g}}^{1},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{k}\}
span
k
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
and there is a canonical linear mapping between them which maps
{\bm{m}}^{s-1}
to
{\bm{g}}^{s}
for all
s
which, for
k
fixed with
n\rightarrow\infty
, is an approximate isometry.
This linear mapping is given by

\bm{T}:={\bm{G}}({\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}})^{-1}{\bm{M}}^{\top}.

(19)

The mapping
\bm{T}
first projects onto the space spanned by
\{{\bm{m}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{k-1}\}
,
and then maps this space onto
\{{\bm{g}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{k}\}
through an approximate isometry.

We are now ready to state the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality.

Theorem 1

(Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP)
.

Consider
{\bm{W}}\sim{\rm GOE}(n)
, any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
and the AMP iteration in Eq. (
13
).
Let
{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\sf N}(0,{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
independent of everything else.
Define
\bm{T}
as in Eq. (
19
),
and, for
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, define

{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{AMP}}({\bm{v}}):=\sqrt{n}\bm{T}{\bm{v}}+\|{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{M}}}^{\perp}{\bm{v}}\|{\bm{\xi}}.

(20)

For some
R>0
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
let
K_{n}=K_{n}({\bm{M}},{\bm{G}})
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
contained in the ball
\mathsf{B}_{2}(0,R)
which may depend on the AMP iterates
{\bm{M}},{\bm{G}}
,
and let
f_{n}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k})^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a continuous function.
Then,

\plimsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}})\in K_{n}}\big\{{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}+f_{n}({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}};{\bm{M}},{\bm{G}},{\bm{g}}^{0})\big\}\leq\plimsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}})\in K_{n}}\Big\{\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\langle{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{AMP}}({\bm{v}}),{\bm{v}}\rangle+f_{n}({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}};{\bm{M}},{\bm{G}},{\bm{g}}^{0})\Big\}.

(21)

It is important that both the optimization function
f_{n}
and the constraint set
K_{n}
in Theorem
1
can depend on the AMP iterates
{\bm{M}},{\bm{G}}
.
As we will see in the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-synchronization example,
this will be essential to applying Theorem
1
to probe local properties of an optimization around the AMP iterates.
The probabilistic suprema are taken with respect to all sources of randomness, including in the choice of constraint set
K
.
We prove Theorem
1
in Appendix
A
.

It is worth stopping here to interpret Theorem
1
.
As we have described,
we can view the matrix
\bm{T}
as first projecting onto the space spanned by
\{{\bm{m}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{k-1}\}
,
and then mapping this space onto
\{{\bm{g}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{k}\}
through an approximate isometry.
Moreover, the AMP state evolution (Proposition
2
) implies that every vector in the span of
\{{\bm{g}}^{1},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{k}\}
looks Gaussian in the sense of having coordinates with approximately Gaussian empirical distribution.
Thus,
\sqrt{n}\bm{T}{\bm{v}}
behaves like a vector drawn from
{\sf N}(0,\|{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{M}}}{\bm{v}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
.
1
1

1

We are not being precise about what we mean by “behaves like,” though a precise statement would involve the convergence of empirical coordinate distributions.

Because
{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\sf N}(0,{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
is independent of everything else,

{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{AMP}}({\bm{v}})
behaves like a vector drawn from
{\sf N}(0,\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
.
Note that the vector
{\bm{g}}({\bm{v}})
appearing in the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality (
12
) also exhibits this behavior:
{\bm{g}}({\bm{v}})=\|{\bm{v}}\|{\bm{\xi}}
is, for fixed
{\bm{v}}
, distributed
{\sf N}(0,\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
.
In this sense, the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality and the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality have a remarkably similar structure.

Nevertheless, they also have important differences.
In the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality,
{\bm{g}}({\bm{v}})
has a direction chosen uniformly at random and independent of everything else, but has a norm determined implicitly by the optimization variable
{\bm{v}}
.
In contrast,
in the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality,
both the direction and the norm of
{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{AMP}}({\bm{v}})
are determined implicitly by the optimization variable
{\bm{v}}
.
Whereas in the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality,
{\bm{g}}({\bm{v}})
lies in the 1-dimensional space spanned by the Gaussian vector
{\bm{\xi}}
,
in the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality,
{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{AMP}}({\bm{v}})
lies in the
(k+1)
-dimensional space spanned by the approximately Gaussian vectors
{\bm{g}}^{1},\ldots,{\bm{g}}^{k}
and the Gaussian vector
{\bm{\xi}}
.
Its decomposition into these components exactly corresponds to the decomposition of
{\bm{v}}
into
{\bm{m}}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{k-1}
and its component orthogonal to their span.
In this way, the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality is able to capture relationships between the AMP iterates, the optimization function
f
, the random matrix
{\bm{W}}
, and the constraints set
K
.

Finally, whereas the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality makes a comparison which is valid for finite
n
,
the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality makes a comparison which is valid asymptotically.
Nevertheless, the proof of Theorem
1
uses a finite sample comparison inequality similar to the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality, and which provides the more fundamental comparison.
Because it is more difficult to state and harder to motivate this inequality, we defer details to Appendix
A
.

Remark 1

\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{m}}^{k-1}\|/\sqrt{n}\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\mathsf{F}^{(1)}_{{\mathsf{x}},k}({\bm{v}},{\bm{u}};{\bm{M}},{\bm{G}},{\bm{\xi}})\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}^{(2)}_{{\mathsf{x}},\infty}(\epsilon)}\mathsf{F}_{{\mathsf{x}}}^{(2)}(\mu).

(61)

It now remains to bound the right-hand side of Eq. (

4.4
Analysis of

For

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{{\mathsf{x}}}^{(2)}(\mu^{\infty})

\displaystyle=2\lambda\Big\langle\frac{\langle M_{\infty},V\rangle_{L_{2}}}{\|M_{\infty}\|_{L_{2}}}\frac{G_{\infty}}{\|G_{\infty}\|_{L_{2}}}+\|{\mathsf{P}}_{M_{\infty}}^{\perp}V\|_{L_{2}}\Xi,\,V\Big\rangle

(62)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}[V]^{2}-\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{V^{2}}{1-U^{2}}\Big]-\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})+(1-\chi_{{\mathsf{x}}})2\lambda^{2}\langle V,M_{\infty}\rangle_{L_{2}}^{2}.

This representation allows to analyze the cases

{\mathsf{x}}=\mathsf{AMS}

For
\mu^{(\infty)}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathsf{x}},\infty}^{(2)}(\epsilon)

(63)

Introducing dual variables

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{{\mathsf{x}}}^{(2)}(\mu^{(\infty)})

\displaystyle=2\lambda\Big\langle\rho\frac{G_{\infty}}{\|G_{\infty}\|_{L_{2}}}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\Xi,\,V\Big\rangle_{L_{2}}+\lambda^{2}u^{2}-\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{V^{2}}{1-U^{2}}\Big]-\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})+(1-\chi_{{\mathsf{x}}})2\lambda^{2}q_{\infty}\rho^{2}

(65)

We have used here that
\|G_{\infty}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=\lambda^{2}q_{\infty}

\|M_{\infty}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=q_{\infty}

Note that
\Theta(g,m,\xi,u)
also depends on
\rho,\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}

but we have left this dependence implicit to lighten notation.

\alpha_{v}<1

\alpha_{v}

\big|\Theta(G_{\infty},M_{\infty},\Xi,U)-\Theta(G_{\infty},M_{\infty},\Xi,M_{\infty})\big|\leq L_{\alpha_{v}}\Big(2\lambda\Big(\frac{\rho}{\lambda q_{\infty}^{1/2}}G_{\infty}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\Xi\Big)+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}M_{\infty}}{q_{\infty}^{1/2}}+\alpha_{u}\Big)^{2}|U-M_{\infty}|,

for some

we combine the previous display with Eq. (
64

for any compact set

and any fixed
\alpha_{v}

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{(2)}(\mu^{(\infty)})\leq\min_{(\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u})\in K}\Big\{\lambda^{2}u^{2}

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})+(1-\chi_{{\mathsf{x}}})2\lambda^{2}q_{\infty}\rho^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle-\alpha_{\rho}\rho-\alpha_{u}u-\alpha_{v}+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Theta(G_{\infty},M_{\infty},\Xi,M_{\infty})\Big]\Big\}+C\epsilon,

where

\alpha_{v}
(but not on
\rho,u,\epsilon
),
and we remind the reader that
\mu^{(\infty)}
satisfies Eq.(

In particular,
for any compact set

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{S}}^{(2)}_{{\mathsf{x}},\infty}(\epsilon)}\mathsf{F}_{{\mathsf{x}}}^{(2)}(\mu)\leq\sup_{\rho,u\in[-1,1]}\min_{(\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u})\in K}\mathsf{L}_{{\mathsf{x}}}(\rho,u;\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}),

(70)

where

\mathsf{L}_{{\mathsf{x}}}(\rho,u;\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}):=\lambda^{2}u^{2}-\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})+(1-\chi_{{\mathsf{x}}})2\lambda^{2}q_{\infty}\rho^{2}-\alpha_{\rho}\rho-\alpha_{u}u-\alpha_{v}+\mathbb{E}\Big[\Theta(G_{\infty},M_{\infty},\Xi,M_{\infty})\Big],

(71)

and we remind the reader that
\Theta
depends implicitly on
\rho,\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}
.

We have now reduced the problem to upper-bounding a certain max-min problem of five variables similar to that derived by Lemma 4.9(a) of
[
CFM21
]
.
The remainder of our argument is nearly equivalent to theirs,
and in particular, their proof of
[
CFM21
, Lemma 4.9(b)]
.
The primary difference between our argument and theirs has occurred in the proof up to this point,
and in particular, in how this objective was derived:

[
CFM21
]
conditions on stationarity of the TAP free energy at a point (i.e.,
\nabla{\mathcal{F}}({\bm{m}})=0

Because the TAP free energy has many stationary points,
there is some subtlety in arguing that the conditional distribution which results from conditioning on
\nabla{\mathcal{F}}({\bm{m}})=0
for a
{\bm{m}}
chosen
a priori
reflects the behavior of the landscape around one of the stationary points of
{\mathcal{F}}
chosen
a posteriori

To make this connection,

[
CFM21

must invoke sophisticated arguments involving the Kac-Rice formula to control the number of such points.
We condition on arguably a more complicated event— a full sequence of AMP iterates —but because there is only one sequence of AMP iterates for each realization of
{\bm{W}}

we are able to avoid the difficulties that demanded the use of the Kac-Rice formula.

To analyze this max-min problem,
we write

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Theta(G_{\infty},M_{\infty},\Xi,M_{\infty})\Big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{4\lambda^{2}(1-\rho^{2})+\Big(\frac{2\rho}{q_{\infty}^{1/2}}G_{\infty}+\frac{\alpha_{\rho}M_{\infty}}{q_{\infty}^{1/2}}+\alpha_{u}\Big)^{2}}{4\Big(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\Big)}\right],

so that

\mathsf{L}_{{\mathsf{x}}}(\rho,u;\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v})=(\rho,u,\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u})^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}(\rho,u,\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u})^{\top}-\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})-\alpha_{v}+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\Big)^{-1}\right],

(73)

where

{\bm{A}}_{{\mathsf{x}}}^{(\alpha_{v})}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{{\mathsf{x}},11}&{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{12}\\[5.0pt]
{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{21}&{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(74)

where

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{{\mathsf{x}},11}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\chi_{{\mathsf{x}}})2\lambda^{2}q_{\infty}+\mathbb{E}\Big[(G_{\infty}^{2}/q_{\infty}-\lambda^{2})\left(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\right)^{-1}\Big]&0\\[5.0pt]
0&\lambda^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(75)

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{12}={\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})\top}_{21}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2q_{\infty}}\mathbb{E}\Big[G_{\infty}M_{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\Big)^{-1}\Big]&\frac{1}{2q_{\infty}^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[G_{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\Big)^{-1}\Big]\\[5.0pt]
0&-\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(\alpha_{v})}_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{q_{\infty}}\mathbb{E}\Big[M_{\infty}^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\Big)^{-1}\Big]&\frac{1}{q_{\infty}^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[M_{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{1-M_{\infty}^{2}}-\alpha_{v}\Big)^{-1}\Big]\\[5.0pt]

Letting
b_{\infty}=\mathbb{E}[M_{\infty}^{3}]=\mathbb{E}[M_{\infty}^{4}]

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(0)}_{{\mathsf{x}},11}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\chi_{{\mathsf{x}}})2\lambda^{2}q_{\infty}-\lambda^{2}(1-q_{\infty})+\frac{1}{q_{\infty}}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{\infty}^{2}\left(1-M_{\infty}^{2}\right)\right]&0\\[5.0pt]

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(0)}_{12}={\bm{A}}^{(0)\top}_{21}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2q_{\infty}}\mathbb{E}[G_{\infty}M_{\infty}(1-M_{\infty}^{2})]&-\frac{1}{2q_{\infty}^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}[G_{\infty}M_{\infty}^{2}]\\[5.0pt]

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(0)}_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1-b_{\infty}/q_{\infty}&q_{\infty}^{1/2}(1-b_{\infty}/q_{\infty})\\[5.0pt]

.
Using that

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(0)}_{{\mathsf{x}},11}

(82)

we conclude that
{\bm{A}}_{{\mathsf{x}},11}^{(0)}-{\bm{A}}_{12}^{(0)}({\bm{A}}_{22}^{(0)})^{-1}{\bm{A}}_{21}^{(0)}\prec 0

at which point the minimization over
(\alpha_{\rho},\alpha_{u})

.

Note that

Appendix A
Proof of the Sudakov-Fernique post-AMP inequality

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1
and Corollary
1

We start by providing a finite sample comparison inequality, which we call the

in Appendix

A.1
The conditional Sudakov-Fernique inequality

The conditional Sudakov-Fernique inequality is nothing but the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality applied conditional on a set of linear constraints.
Thus, it is not technically a new comparison inequality.
Our insight is to write the conditional inequality in a way which better reveals its structure and facilitates its application in the context of AMP.

We apply the classical Sudakov-Fernique inequality conditional on a set of linear-constraints on
{\bm{W}}
of the form

{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S},

(85)

for matrices
{\bm{R}},\bm{S}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k}
,
where
{\bm{R}}
is full rank.
Given any such matrices,
let
{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}={\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}({\bm{R}},\bm{S})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
be any solution to the equations

{\bm{R}}^{\top}\bm{S}=({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}){\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}+{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}^{\top}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}).

(86)

Lemma 2

(Existence of solutions to Eq. (
86
))
.

If
{\bm{R}}^{\top}\bm{S}
is symmetric (i.e.,
{\bm{R}}^{\top}\bm{S}=\bm{S}^{\top}{\bm{R}}
),
then Eq. (
86
) has solutions.
This occurs, in particular,
if there exists a symmetric matrix
{\bm{W}}
with
{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We may take
{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}\bm{S}
,
where the inverse exists because
{\bm{R}}
is full-rank.
Note that if
{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}
for symmetric
{\bm{W}}
, then
{\bm{R}}^{\top}\bm{S}={\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}^{\top}{\bm{R}}
.
∎

The purpose of defining
{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}
is to provide a simple representation of the distribution of
{\bm{W}}
conditional on the event
{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}
,
which we do in the next lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
{\bm{G}}_{\mathsf{SF}}=\bm{S}-{\bm{R}}{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}
for any
{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}
satisfying Eq. (
86
).
Further, let
\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}={\bm{G}}_{\mathsf{SF}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}
is the unique matrix satisfying
\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}{\bm{R}}={\bm{G}}_{\mathsf{SF}}
and
\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}=0
.
Then

{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}\quad\text{implies}\quad{\bm{W}}-{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}=\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}+\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}^{\top}.

(87)

Thus,

\mathsf{Law}\big({\bm{W}}\mid{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}\big)=\mathsf{Law}\big(\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}+\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}^{\top}+{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}\widetilde{\bm{W}}{}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}\big),

(88)

where
\widetilde{\bm{W}}{}\sim{\rm GOE}(n)
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

If
{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}
,
then

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}-{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}+{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}{\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}

(89)

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}+{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}\big({\mathbf{I}}_{n}-{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}\big)

\displaystyle=\bm{S}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}+{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}\bm{S}^{\top}-{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}\bm{S}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}+{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}\bm{S}^{\top}-{\bm{R}}{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{R}}^{\top}-{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}^{\top}{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}+\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}^{\top},

as desired.

Next, note that
{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}
is independent of
{\bm{W}}-{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}
,
and the occurrence of the event
{\bm{W}}{\bm{R}}=\bm{S}
is a function of
{\bm{W}}-{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{W}}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}
.
The final distributional claim of the lemma follows.
∎

We can now state the conditional Sudakov-Fernique inequality.

Theorem 3

(Conditional Sudakov-Fernique inequality)
.

Consider
{\bm{R}},\bm{S}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k}
,
and assume
{\bm{R}}
is full-rank.
Let
{\bm{B}}_{\mathsf{SF}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
,
{\bm{G}}_{\mathsf{SF}},\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k}
be random matrices which satisfy Eq. (
86

3

Define the (random) function
{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{SF}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by

{\bm{g}}_{\mathsf{SF}}({\bm{v}}):=\sqrt{n}\,\bm{T}_{\mathsf{SF}}{\bm{v}}+\|{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{v}}\|{\mathsf{P}}_{{\bm{R}}}^{\perp}{\bm{\xi}},

(90)

where
{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\sf N}(0,{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
independent of everything else.
Then, for any compact set
K=K({\bm{R}},\bm{S})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
(which may depend on
{\bm{R}},\bm{S}
) and bounded and continuous
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{m+n}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k})^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
