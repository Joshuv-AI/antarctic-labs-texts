Nonparametric quantile regression for spatio-temporal processes
Soudeep Deb ID, Cláudia Neves ID, Subhrajyoty Roy ID

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a new and effective approach to nonparametric quantile regression that accommodates ultrahigh-dimensional data arising from spatio-temporal processes. This approach proves advantageous in staving off computational challenges that constitute known hindrances to existing nonparametric quantile regression methods when the number of predictors is much larger than the available sample size. We investigate conditions under which estimation is feasible and of good overall quality and obtain sharp approximations that we employ to devising statistical inference methodology. These include simultaneous confidence intervals and tests of hypotheses, whose asymptotics is borne by a non-trivial functional central limit theorem tailored to martingale differences. Additionally, we provide finite-sample results through various simulations which, accompanied by an illustrative application to real-worldesque data (on electricity demand), offer guarantees on the performance of the proposed methodology.



KEY WORDS AND PHRASES:
Asymptotic theory, electricity demand forecasting, gaussian processes, multivariate quantiles.

Primary 62M20, 62A99; secondary 60G15, 60G70.

Introduction

Within the context of various economic, environmental and societal research problems, lies the common aim of wanting to estimate the effect of a covariate on the behavior of the quantiles of a continuous response variable whilst it is also well known that a simple regression model explaining the conditional expectation of the response variable may turn out short in providing a satisfactory description of the full spread of the targeted conditional distribution, particularly at the level of tail-related data
(
Reich, 2012
, cf.)
.

Originally proposed by
Koenker and Bassett Jr, 1978
, even in its most simplified setting, quantile regression serves as a valuable alternative to the familiar regression on conditional expectation, for it is not as heavily reliant on the typical assumption about normally distributed errors for assessing significance of the postulated model. Indeed, working off the inherent nonparametric nature of conditional quantiles, quantile regression proves as a reliable inference methodology to the extent of readily accommodating heavy-tailed distributions for the errors, also being robust to the presence of outliers
(
Chaudhuri et al., 1997
;
Ezzahrioui and Ould-Saïd, 2008
, see)
. In more complex situations, especially those concerning time series modeling or involving space-time changing attributes, quantile regression recognizably offers a plethora of opportunities to statistically meaningful analyses of empirical data from real-life applications.

The leading example in this paper has dedicated focus on conditional quantiles of electricity load profiles obtained from smart-meter records installed in many UK households. The key motivation behind this illustrative application is to gain improved understanding of peak-load events so as to help distribution network providers maintain a stable supply chain at particular times of the day or week and ward off rare but sometimes inevitable power outages
(
Jacob et al., 2020
)
. Like with many other fields in applied science, an important difficulty arises from the dimension of the space-time covariate (thus infinite-dimensional in theory) becoming increasingly large as the sample size increases. We tackle this problem through a multivariate time series approach under minimally restrictive assumptions on the data generating process, hence making it amenable to translate to other applied settings given the added flexibility for adjusting to different factors and confounding effects. Before delving into both rationale and technical aspects of our proposed suite of statistical methods concerning quantile regression on a spatio-temporal dimension, a succinct account of extant literature in this context is pertinent at this point.

There has been primarily two distinct nonparametric approaches towards modeling conditional quantiles when dealing with a response variable at a time. One such approach hinges on a pinball type loss function conditional on the value of the covariate and views the resulting estimation problem from the optimization modeling paradigm. In order to translate this into a viable and tractable problem, however, one often needs to make a set of assumptions which have typically ranged from a continuous functional of the covariate of interest, to functionals belonging to a reproducible kernel Hilbert space
(
Takeuchi et al., 2006
)
, and to polynomial functions of the covariate
(
Zhou and Wu, 2009
)
, all of which resulting in smooth quantile curves. A common caveat to this type of approaches is that they can still suffer from the well-known quantile crossing effect, which will lead to extra constraints yet to be factored into the optimization setup. To combat this issue, a nonparametric kernel estimation of the conditional distribution function was put forward
(cf.
Ferraty et al., 2005
;
Ezzahrioui and Ould-Said, 2008
, and references therein)
which, upon a relatively straightforward inversion procedure, has had proven efficacy in yielding conditional quantile curves that satisfy the non-crossing criterion. However, because there is a necessary inversion step involved in the latter, the former challenge now shifts to the smoothness property of the estimated quantile curve which is not always attainable in practice, thus posing a critical hurdle to the modeling of real-worldesque data.

When looking at multivariate time series or spatio-temporal data, a case which this paper seeks to address, we note that despite the vastly accumulating literature on conditional quantile function estimation in the independent setting, the development of flexible statistical methodology for the underpinning stochastic processes is still relatively in its infancy. Progress in these domains has been limited, possibly by the challenges faced when accounting for the core dependency structure in a way that does not break down at the laws of physics governing the process generating the data and therefore is capable of producing realistic inference. To the best of our knowledge, only a few successful attempts in this direction have been made thus far. A couple of them
(
Reich et al., 2011
;
Das and Ghosal, 2017
, e.g.)
are Bayesian approaches that tend to anchor on basis polynomials, with ensuing considerations in eliciting prior distributions to the relevant parameters alongside discussion on which allowances must be brought into practice to make the method feasible. Because any misspecification involving the latter is detrimental to the overall quality and feasibility of inference, the possibility of undermining end-users’ confidence in the statistical methods being employed cannot be wholly discarded. The approach taken by
Reich, 2012
;
Duan et al., 2021
, among others, considers each quantile curve at different locations separately, with the levels of the quantile curves not affecting each other except for the non-crossing condition. However, this implies that such approaches may fail in the situations where observations at most locations follow a general trend (thus remaining close to the median) with a few remainder locations exhibiting disparate behavior from the bulk.

In this paper, we introduce a nonparametric estimator of the conditional quantile of a continuous response variable. Our statistical procedure has the advantage of being able to deliver smooth estimates whilst demonstrating to stave off the above-mentioned quantile crossing effect. The extremity issue is overcome by modeling the multivariate quantiles of the relevant distribution through an appropriate random element of the unit euclidean ball. The asymptotic properties are developed as part of the theoretical study of the proposed statistical methodology, both aimed at estimation and testing. We consider the asymptotic framework where both the number of time-points and the number of locations grow to infinity, which required significant adaptation of the conventional delta-method used for deriving consistency and asymptotic normality of estimators, since our approach leverages the sub-Gaussian properties of the data to maintain higher-order moments under control for ensuring tightness. Moreover, ensuring consistency has dictated that weak convergence of the process must hold in a uniform sense in order to account for the changing vector spaces with varying dimension, hence being the source of additional complexity. This was handled by employing concentration inequalities based on a sequence of compact sets for guaranteeing boundedness in the differences between estimated and true quantiles. Lastly, to prove asymptotic normality, we make use of Portmanteau’s theorem and show that, for a rich class of smooth functions with bounded derivatives, the expectation of the desired functional of the normalized estimators converges to the expectation of the same functional value applied on the appropriate slice of Gaussian process, which we also identify.

The structure of this paper is as follows. The statistical methodological details and the key results developed are described in Sections
2
,
3
and
4
. Following the exposition of asymptotic theory, we go on to developing simultaneous confidence bands for the spatio-temporal quantile curves across all locations and time-points in Section
5
. Associated statistical tests of hypotheses are also described therein. In order to maintain the flow of the paper, we defer the proofs of the theoretical results until Section

(

, details in)
. This section focuses on inference, encompassing estimation, prediction and hypothesis testing, with a particular emphasis on two testing procedures for homogeneity. Numerical experiments that provide empirical evidence supporting the validity of our statistical methods are conducted as a supplement to this study. Notably, the fundamental large sample properties elucidated in the paper are mirrored in the finite sample simulation study detailed in the supplementary material accompanying this paper.

2
Spatio-temporal framework for quantile regression

This section presents the framework for the proposed quantile regression for spatio-temporal processes, also furthering related works by
De Gooijer et al., 2006
;
Zhou, 2010
;
Deb and Jana, 2021
.

Let
\left\{Y(t,s):\,t\in\mathcal{T},s\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
be a spatio-temporal process indexed by temporal domain consisting of a (dense) temporal horizon
\mathcal{T}=[0,1]
and by a countable spatial horizon set
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\bm{X}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\chi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional temporal covariate process such that for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\bm{X}(t)=\left(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{d}(t)\right)^{\prime}
is a predictor variable in
\chi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the sliding scale of generality, this covariate process can be allowed to be made fairly generic so as to encapsulate external factors that might affect the temporal dependence to the main process
Y(t,s)
, to being set out as specialized into a given periodic component, for example, aiming to capture inherent seasonality. Henceforth , our interest lies in the conditional space-time process
Y(t,s)\mid\{\bm{X}(t):t\in\mathcal{T}\}
with expectation and covariance structure respectively denoted by

\mathbb{E}(Y(t,s))=\mu(\bm{X}(t),s)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathrm{Cov}(Y(t,s_{1}),Y(t,s_{2}))=\Sigma(\bm{X}(t),s_{1},s_{2}),

(2.1)

for every
s,s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
. Note that the temporal dependence in the process
Y(t,s)
is essentially driven by that of the associated covariate process
\bm{X}(t)
. Of course, it would be beguiling to assume that
Y(t,s)
can actually be observed in the way of covering the whole set
\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{S}
, so we must settle with
Y(t,s)
indexed by the discrete subset of time points
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots t_{n}\}
and locations
\mathcal{S}_{p}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots s_{p}\}
, where both
n
and
p
are assumed sufficiently large, i.e. both tending to
\infty
albeit in a controlled manner for the latter which we impose from the outset as an intermediate sequence
p=p_{n}
such that
p/n\rightarrow 0
. This renders a more realistic framework for inference with a practical resonance: for each
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, suppose that the data is generated according to the nonparametric spatial regression model:

\bm{Y}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}Y(t,s_{1})\\
Y(t,s_{2})\\
\vdots\\
Y(t,s_{p})\end{pmatrix}=\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t))+\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\bm{X}(t))\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{t1}\\
\xi_{t2}\\
\vdots\\
\xi_{tp}\end{pmatrix},

(2.2)

where
\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t))
is a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
of the form

\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t)):=\begin{pmatrix}\mu(\bm{X}(t),s_{1})\\
\mu(\bm{X}(t),s_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\mu(\bm{X}(t),s_{p})\end{pmatrix},

the covariance matrix is given by
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{X}(t))
, whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
\Sigma(\bm{X}(t),s_{i},s_{j})
for
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant p
, and the (unobserved) errors
\left(\xi_{tj}\right)_{j=1,\dots p}
are independent and identically distributed with mean zero, positive density on an interval containing
0
(albeit not necessarily that of a normal distribution) and unit variance. Hereafter,
\bm{\xi}(t)
denotes the vector of
\xi_{tj}
’s.

The framework (
2.2
) lays bare that the key problem of estimating a spatial quantile at any given time
t\in\mathcal{T}
fits well into the logic of estimating a
p
-variate quantile for the distribution of
\bm{Y}(t)
centered at time-points
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
. It remains quite general on the whole, and especially in two ways outlined as follows. Firstly, it accommodates multivariate time series data even when there is no inherent spatial structure in the coordinates but rather a rank correlation can be inferred from domain knowledge, such as daily closing prices of multiple stock indices. If relevant information about the spatial dependency structure is available
a priori
, it can be encapsulated in
\bm{\Sigma}(\cdot)
through a set of suitable constraints. These constraints range from being precisely defined, fundamentally reliant on a certain postulated parametric model, to being defined by a given class of (unknown) distributions in a manner that echoes a familiar balancing act: the robustness-efficiency trade-off. Secondly, such a formulation enables inference using multivariate quantiles that may describe different degrees of extremity on different coordinates as it allows for possible different tail behavior at nearby locations. This justifies the focus on nonparametric estimation of the conditional multivariate quantiles of
\bm{Y}(t)
, given the temporal covariate process
\bm{X}(t)
, assumed predictable.

To this date, various definitions for a quantile of a multivariate distribution have been put forward in the literature, notably those drawing on convex hulls
(
Eddy, 1985
)
, minimum volume sets
(
Einmahl and Mason, 1992
)
, geometric generalization of spatial median
(
Chaudhuri, 1996
)
and center outward quantile functions
(
Figalli, 2018
)
. Among these, the geometric concept of a quantile in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 2
, originating from
Chaudhuri, 1996
and endorsed in
Konen and Paindaveine, 2022
, stands out as the most closely related to the present paper.

Indeed, central to this paper is the loss function
\Phi(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\left\|\bm{v}\right\|+\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle
(
\left\|\bm{v}\right\|
denotes the usual Euclidean norm of
\bm{v}
), for all
\bm{u}\in B^{d-1}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|\bm{x}\right\|<1\}
and the estimated quantile
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in which setting a recognizable form of the multivariate
\bm{u}
-quantile of a
d
-variate random vector
\bm{X}
beckons:

Q(\bm{u}):=\arg\min_{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(\bm{u},\bm{X}-\bm{q})\right],

(2.3)

(
Chowdhury and Chaudhuri, 2019
, cf.)
. As desired, a choice of
\bm{u}\in B^{d-1}
in (
2.3
) towards the boundary, i.e.
\left\|\bm{u}\right\|\to 1
, leads to a (high or low) extreme quantile, whereas fixing
\bm{u}
near the origin yields spatially centered measurable functionals, including expectiles such as the mean vector ascribed to the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-objective function in (
2.3
) or the spatial median in connection with
\mathcal{L}_{1}
-norm. Since the vector
\bm{u}
depends on the dimension
p
of the covariate, and
p=p_{n}
is such that
p\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
(in a controlled manner stipulated before), we shall consider a spatial process
u
(measurable with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
) satisfying

\int_{\mathcal{S}}|u(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant 1,

which, for every finite restriction
\mathcal{S}_{p}
of
\mathcal{S}
, gives rise to
\bm{u}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}=(u(s_{1}),\dots u(s_{p}))\in B^{p-1}
. In a similar vein, we now define for every
t\in\mathcal{T},\bm{x}\in\chi
, the
\bm{u}
-conditional spatial quantile of
\bm{Y}(t)
given
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}
as

Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})|_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}=\arg\min_{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}\|+\langle\bm{u}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}},\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}\rangle\mid\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}\right],

(2.4)

where
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})|_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
highlights that the conditional quantile process
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})
is indexed by a
p
-dimenstional spatial domain
\mathcal{S}_{p}
. The set
\mathcal{S}_{p}
has to be asymptotically dense in
\mathcal{S}
as
p\rightarrow\infty
, i.e., for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and however small
\epsilon>0
, we can find a point
s^{\ast}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
within the ball of radius
\epsilon
centered at
s
for sufficiently large
p
. It is important to stress that the inclusion of the temporal domain adds to a body of contemporary works in a substantive way since, to the extent of our knowledge, research thus far refrained to the study of nonparametric spatial quantile regression. Our proposal is distinctive in that the criterion (loss) function presiding the M-estimation contemplated in (
2.4
) is no longer a fixed function, but rather it is allowed to vary with
p
whilst preserving many of the fundamental properties for quantile regression investigated in
Chowdhury and Chaudhuri, 2019
. This is the key insight to expanding the notion of a spatial (multivariate) quantile across a direction, furthering the aim of tackling the spatio-temporal regression estimation problem stated in (
2.4
). The precise meaning of this will become more apparent in Section
3
.

In this instance, a natural question that comes to the fore is whether such a definition of conditional quantile process (as in (
2.4
)) entails identifiability across the set
\mathcal{S}_{p}
. For example, if
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is a superset of
\mathcal{S}_{p}
for some fixed
p
, then the restriction of
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}^{\ast}}
to the set of points
\mathcal{S}_{p}
should be same as
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})|_{\mathbb{R}_{p}}
. To see this, we consider
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}=\mathcal{S}_{p}\cup(\mathcal{S}^{\ast}-\mathcal{S}_{p})=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
and correspondingly define
\bm{Y}_{1}(t),\bm{Y}_{2}(t)
and
\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}
as the restrictions of
\bm{Y}(t)
and
\bm{q}
respectively. Finally, we note that

Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}^{\ast}}=\argmin_{\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\|\bm{Y}_{1}(t)-\bm{q}_{1}\|^{2}+\|\bm{Y}_{2}(t)-\bm{q}_{2}\|^{2}}\right.\\
\left.+\langle\bm{u}|_{S_{1}},\bm{Y}_{1}(t)-\bm{q}_{1}\rangle+\langle\bm{u}|_{S_{2}},\bm{Y}_{2}(t)-\bm{q}_{2}\rangle\mid\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}\right].

It now clearly follows that optimizing with respect to
\bm{q}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
yields a minimizer of (
2.4
) for any fixed value of
\bm{q}_{2}
, thus ensuring identifiability.

3
Nonparametric spatio-temporal quantile estimation

For ease of introduction, we first address the case where we look at one location at a time. This corresponds to setting
p=1
in the basic model (
2.2
), whereby the canonical nonparametric quantile estimator gives rise to the kernel-weighted version of the quantile
q
of order
\tau\in(0,1)
of the corresponding empirical probability distribution function of
Y(t)
given
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}
on
\chi=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Formally, for
u\equiv u(\tau)=(2\tau-1)
,
0<\tau<1
, the quantile
q
of order
\tau
is the minimizer of

\mathcal{M}_{u,n}^{(1)}(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t_{i}))\left(|Y(t_{i})-q|+u\cdot(Y(t_{i})-q)\right),

(3.1)

where
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is a suitably chosen kernel function. In this respect, we note that
\mathcal{M}_{u,n}^{(1)}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}
. With
\tau=1/2
standing for the median as the minimizer of
\mathcal{M}_{u,n}^{(1)}
,
u
can be viewed as a gauge for central displacement or tail-relatedness, since the greater the
|u|
the more tail-relatable the quantile. Clearly, finding minimizers of (
3.1
) configures an
M
-estimation problem, and with the stated sample criterion function incorporating a kernel function of bandwidth
b_{n}
, this is one of a nonparametric nature. By this token, a generalization to the
p
-multivariate case can be readily achieved by taking suitably normalized matrix-valued kernel functions
\bm{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
into the inner product that constitutes (
3.1
), thus giving way to the following definition of a nonparametric spatio-temporal
\bm{u}
-quantile that mirrors (
2.4
) in the associated sequence of statistical experiments: for
\bm{u}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
comprised of the set
\{u(s):s\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\}
, define the estimator

\hat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x}):=\arg\min_{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}),

(3.2)

where

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\left\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\right\|_{\bm{K}(t_{i})}+\left\langle\bm{u}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}},\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\right\rangle_{\bm{K}(t_{i})}\right\},

(3.3)

for which we write

\displaystyle\bm{K}(t)

\displaystyle:=\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}\right\|_{\bm{K}(t)}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\|\bm{K}(t)(\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q})\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{u},\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}\right\rangle_{\bm{K}(t)}

\displaystyle:=\bm{u}^{\intercal}\bm{K}(t)(\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}).

As the objective function
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}
in (
3.3
) has been highlighted to depend on both the parameter dimension
p
and sample size
n
, in view of (
2.4
) its counterpart population version (for criterion function) is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}^{(p)}(\bm{q}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}\|+\langle\bm{u}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}},\bm{Y}(t)-\bm{q}\rangle\mid\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}\right].

In Section
4
, this setting will be expanded with
p
taken as a sequence of positive integers indexed in
n
.

Before going any further, we spell out the key assumptions underpinning the basic asymptotic results established in this paper; each of which will be called upon as deemed appropriate and/or necessary throughout.

(A1)

The covariate process
\bm{X}(t)
is a predictable process, i.e., there exists a sequence of random variables
\{\epsilon_{t}:t=t_{1},t_{2},\dots\}
, with its natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
independent of the random variables
\xi_{tj}
, such that
\bm{X}(t_{i})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{(i-1)}}
-measurable for any
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

(A2)

There exists a sequence of convex, open sets
\mathcal{U}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R},\mathcal{U}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
where the sequence of criterion functions
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}
, defined as in (
3.3
) takes on the vector-valued
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{U}_{p}
, for all
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and for any fixed
p\in\mathbb{N}
not depending on
n
. Moreover, we assume that
\inf_{\bm{q}\in\mathcal{U}_{p}}\inf_{t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\|>\delta
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
, independent of
n
and
p
.

(A3)

The kernel matrix
\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
is a positive definite (
p\times p
) matrix for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
\bm{x}\in\chi
, and the support of the conditional distribution of each
\bm{Y}(t_{1}),\dots\bm{Y}(t_{n})
, provided
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}
is not enclosed in a straight line in
\mathcal{S}

(
Chowdhury and Chaudhuri, 2019
, cf.)
.

Assumption
(A1)
on the predictable process
\bm{X}(t)
makes it possible to write
\bm{X}(t_{i})=h(\dots,\epsilon_{t_{(i-2)}},\epsilon_{t_{(i-1)}})
for some measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\rightarrow\chi
. It is a common assumption in related literature
(
Zhao and Wu, 2008
, see, e.g.,)
. Assumption
(A2)
is an operational assumption, which in conjunction with
(A3)
, ensures that
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}
consists of a sequence of random convex functions on
\mathcal{U}_{p}
, for every
p\geqslant 1
; thereby forming a sequence of continuous functions. This, in turn, ascertains the existence of M-estimators
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}
in the quality of a uniquely defined sequence of minimizers of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}
. We further note that the objective function
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q})
is strictly convex in its argument
\bm{q}
, and the same conclusion is true for
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}^{(p)}(\bm{q})
regardless of the square-integrable spatial process
u
, and for every fixed
p\in\mathbb{N}
. This strict convexity ensures that the true quantile
\bm{q}_{0}
is a well-separated minimizer of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}^{(p)}
, that is, there exists a
\bm{q}_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{p}
for which
\inf_{\bm{q}:\,\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}_{0}\|\geqslant\delta}\,(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}^{(p)}(\bm{q})-\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}^{(p)}(\bm{q}_{0}))>0
, for arbitrarily small
\delta>0
. Assumption
(A2)
also ensures that the observed points
\bm{Y}(t_{i})
are excluded from the feasible set of
\bm{q}
, to ensure well-definedness of the estimating equation introduced later in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Estimation algorithm

The idea behind the Iteratively Re-weighted Least Squares (IRLS) type estimation as an effective means to achieve minimization in (
3.2
) is to replace the objective function with an equivalent version whose iterative minimization leads to the closest answer to the original problem of obtaining a quantile estimator
\hat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
. Firstly, we expand the sample criterion function
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}
as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\right)^{\intercal}\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})\left(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\right)}{\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\|_{\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})}}+\langle\bm{u},(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q})\rangle_{\bm{K}(t_{i})},

where we use the convention that
\bm{y}/\|\bm{y}\|=0
if
\bm{y}=\bm{0}
. In addition, we highlight that
\bm{q}
does not change with
i
. Henceforth, we shall use
w_{i}(\bm{q}):=\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\|^{-1}_{\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})}
so as to avoid notational burden. Minimization of the above with respect to
\bm{q}
yields the sequence of estimators

\hat{\bm{q}}_{n}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\bm{q})\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})\right]^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\bm{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\bm{q})\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})\bm{Y}(t_{i})\right\}.

(3.4)

The above expression suggests that, given an initial estimate
\hat{\bm{q}}^{(0)}
, it is possible to use (
3.4
) iteratively in order to improve estimation at the next iteration. Specifically,
\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k+1)}
is obtained by calculating
w_{i}(\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k)})
for all
i=1,2,\dots n
, using the current estimate and then using the above equation to go to the next step of the iteration. Despite the matrix
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\bm{q})\bm{K}(t_{i})
guaranteed to be positive definite with probability tending to one, in practice it may become numerically singular at some stage of the iterative algorithm, particularly when the number of locations
p
is large. By adding a small positive number
\rho
to the diagonal elements of this matrix, such a numerical complication can be easily averted. The hyperparameter
\rho
subsequently becomes the Lagrangian coefficient associated with a new constraint for the objective function (
3.2
):
\|\bm{q}\|\leqslant C
, for some fixed positive number
C
. This iterative procedure can also be viewed as a Newton-Raphson type procedure, ensuring its convergence. In particular, it is of straightforward verification that this iteration scheme reduces the value of the objective function
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\widehat{\bm{q}})
at the next iteration, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k+1)})\leqslant\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k)})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Combining this fact with the strict convexity of the objective function, it follows that the solution of the algorithm converges to the global minimizer of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q})
since it is inherently assumed that the set of global minimizers is non-empty. This result is formulated in the next Lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\bm{K}(t)=\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
be a matrix-valued kernel function such that the maximum eigenvalue of
\bm{K}(t)
is uniformly bounded for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then, with an initial estimate
\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(0)}
, the sequence of improved estimators obtained by the iteration equation

\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k+1)}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k)})\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})\right]^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\bm{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\widehat{\bm{q}}^{(k)})\bm{K}^{2}(t_{i})\bm{Y}(t_{i})\right\},\ k\geqslant 0,

converges as
k\rightarrow\infty
and the ultimate estimator
\widehat{\bm{q}}^{\ast}
is the solution
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
of (
3.2
).

3.2
Choice of the kernel and associated bandwidth

The matrix-valued kernel function
\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
involving the bandwidth
b_{n}>0
requires suitable normalizing constants
c_{n}>0
. Specifically, we consider the matrix

\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))=c_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{K}\left(\dfrac{\bm{x}-\bm{X}(t)}{b_{n}}\right),

given in terms of a symmetric and continuous kernel
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, with compact support on
B^{d-1}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant 1\}
, and in keeping with the formulation we typically encounter for such nonparametric adjacency matrices of weights
(
Deb et al., 2017
, e.g.)
. Specific choices for constants
b_{n},c_{n}>0
must be based not only on theoretical considerations, but also informed by empirical evidence on the behavior of competing estimators in situations of repeated sampling, all considered against the backdrop of assumptions threaded in the relevant subject literature. For example, univariate nonparametric kernel estimators for a trend in time series, which have been proposed in
Zhao and Wu, 2008
, appear suitably normalized via
c_{n}=\sqrt{n\,b_{n}}
. We also assume that
\mathcal{K}(0)=c_{n}\bm{I}_{p}
, similar to the univariate case where the kernel function at origin is taken to be equal to
1
. Given the choice of a particular form of kernel
\mathcal{K}
, one may choose
c_{n}
to be a function of the bandwidth
b_{n}
such that the eigenvalues of the normalized kernel matrix
\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
remain bounded away from
0
and infinity.

Since the problem of estimating the conditional kernel tackled in here is closely related to that of conditional density estimation, existing methods with proven efficacy can be invoked
(
Einbeck and Tutz, 2006
, see e.g.)
, alongside the use of graphical approaches such as an empirical scatterplot of the maximum eigenvalue of kernel matrix over different choices of
b_{n}
to obtain the rate of growth of the eigenvalues. It is also possible to couple our results with the theoretical findings on the spectrum of kernel matrices for popular kernels (e.g. Gaussian kernel, Matérn kernel) presented in
Barthelmé and Usevich, 2021
, albeit the choice of the bandwidth
b_{n}
should be moderated by a cross-validation approach that verifies the theoretical conditions set out, which in this particular research are outlined in Section
4
. A complete treatment of these and analogous conditions requires technical apparatus of which a detailed study can be found in
Fan et al., 1996
.

3.3
Key properties of the nonparametric quantile estimator

The results encompassing this section, summarized in Lemma
3.2
and ensuing considerations, are viewed as fundamental steps to establishing performance guarantees of the proposed nonparametric methodology for quantile estimation. Indeed, a desirable property of a quantile estimator is affine equivariance: if the samples
\bm{Y}(t)
are changed to
\bm{A}\bm{Y}(t)+\bm{b}
for some non-singular matrix
\bm{A}
, the estimator should also exhibit the same affine transformation. We note that the proposed quantile estimator
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
resulting from (
3.2
) is equivariant under location transformation, i.e., employing a shift to observations through
\bm{Y}(t_{i})+\bm{a}
changes the sequence of estimators accordingly to
\hat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})+\bm{a}
. Although the equivariance also holds in general with respect to a scale transformation
c\bm{Y}(t_{i})
, for some scalar
c\in(0,\infty)
, verification of the orthogonal equivariance property (or more general affine equivariance property) is dependent on the incorporating kernel matrix
\bm{K}(t_{i})

(
Chaudhuri, 1996
;
Chen, 1996
;
Konen and Paindaveine, 2022
, see)
.

As highlighted in Section
1
, quantile estimators should generally exhibit two key properties: non-crossing of the quantiles and smoothness of the estimator as a function of the data. In the following discussion, we demonstrate how upon a very minimal set of assumptions, both these properties follow in relation to
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
.

For each
n\geqslant 1
, the quantile estimator
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
is continuous with respect to observables
\bm{Y}=\left(\bm{Y}(t_{1}),\dots\bm{Y}(t_{n})\right)
. In view of assumption
(A2)
, we can without loss of generality restrict the optimization to a compact set
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
containing the true targeted quantile. Consequently,
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})=\bm{q}(\bm{Y})=\argmin_{\bm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{M}^{(p)}_{\bm{u},n}(\bm{q},\bm{Y})
. Now, let
\bm{Y}_{r}\rightarrow\bm{Y}_{0}
be a converging sequence with
r=1,2,\dots
, and let
\bm{q}_{r}=\bm{q}(\bm{Y}_{r})
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We proceed with a subsequencing argument: for any
\{\bm{q}_{r_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the compactness of
\mathcal{Q}
ensures that
\{\bm{q}_{r_{k}}\}
has an accumulation point
\bm{q}^{\prime}
, say. Also, by definition of
\bm{q}_{r_{k}}
, we have that
\mathcal{M}^{(p)}_{\bm{u},n}(\bm{q}_{r_{k}},\bm{Y}_{r_{k}})\leqslant\mathcal{M}^{(p)}_{\bm{u},n}(\bm{q}_{0},\bm{Y}_{r_{k}}),\ r_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\mathcal{M}^{(p)}_{\bm{u},n}
is a continuous function of
\bm{q}
and
r_{k}\rightarrow\infty
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}(\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{Y}_{0})\leqslant\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}(\bm{q}_{0},\bm{Y}_{0})
, thus entailing
\bm{q}^{\prime}=\bm{q}_{0}
for any subsequence, from which the continuity of the estimator ensues.

The next lemma outlines the desirable non-crossing property in any quantile estimation methodology
(
Chaudhuri, 1996
, e.g. in line with Theorem 2.1.2 of)
. It is mainly a technical lemma aiming to justify that the M-estimator
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
, viewed as the approximate (asymptotic) solution of the convex optimization problem (
3.2
), gains the configuration of a
Z
-estimation problem for every fixed
(\bm{u},\bm{x})
. This is envisaged to simplify substantially the proofs for asymptotic normality. Nevertheless, because we will need to take into account the spatial dependence structure on the basis of an infinitely growing number of locations, the rate of convergence will differ from the familiar
\sqrt{n}
for tightness mustered by predominant asymptotic theory for
Z
-estimators
(
van der Vaart, 1998
;
Kosorok, 2008
, see)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under assumptions
(A1)
to
(A3)
, for every
n,p\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{u}\in B^{p-1}
, the sequence of minimizers
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
of the objective function
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}
(as in (
3.3
)) satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\left[\dfrac{\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\hat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x}))}{\|\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\hat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x}))\|}+\bm{u}\right]=\bm{0},

(3.5)

for
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})\notin\{\bm{Y}(t_{1}),\dots,\bm{Y}(t_{n})\}
. The true quantile
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})
in the quality of minimizer of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}^{(p)}
verifies the population version of (
3.5
), specifically

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}(t)\mid\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}}\left[\dfrac{\bm{Y}(t)-Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})}{\|\bm{Y}(t)-Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})\|}+\bm{u}\right]=\bm{0}.

(3.6)

The actionable idea stemming from Lemma
3.2
– that we may regard (
3.5
) as an estimating equation for obtaining
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
from a finite sample data with
n
time-points and
p
spatial points – gives rise to an alternative Newton-Raphson type algorithm for obtaining the estimator. Notice that, for
\bm{u}=0
, the estimating equation simply corresponds to a weighted spatial median estimate of
\bm{Y}(t_{i})
values, weighted by the covariate process
\{\bm{X}(t):t\in\mathcal{T}\}
through the kernel matrix
\bm{K}(t)=\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
. However, for
\bm{u}\neq\bm{0}
, there is an additional term creeping up in the way of an approximation bias, thereby increasing or decreasing the coordinates of
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
as appropriate in a data-driven manner. This is found pivotal to ensuring that the estimated quantile curves returned by our methodology enjoy the non-crossing property. In view of (
3.6
), it is clear that if a single coordinate
u_{i}
of
\bm{u}
is positive and increases in value, then the corresponding coordinate of
\bm{Y}(t)-Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})
must be negative and decrease further in expectation, resulting in
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})
turning away from the center
\mathbb{E}(\bm{Y}(t))
. This justifies the non-crossing property.

4
Asymptotic results

The nonparametric quantile estimator introduced in Section
3
enjoys a number of desirable large-sample properties. This section is aimed at addressing these by allowing the number of spatial locations
p
tend to infinity, albeit in a controlled manner relatively to
n\rightarrow\infty
, as mentioned earlier. The obtained asymptotic properties provide the theoretical justification for the distinctive features of the quantile estimation and ensuing testing methodology proposed in this paper.

We now fix some more notations in order to progress. A bounded sequence of numbers
\{x_{n}\}
is throughout written as
x_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, springing out to the notation
x_{n}=\mathcal{O}(y_{n})
if
x_{n}/y_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. In general, if the random sequence
X_{n}
is tight, we write
\|X_{n}\|=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
, and if
\|X_{n}\|/\|Y_{n}\|\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{P}}\,0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we denote this fact by
X_{n}=o_{p}(Y_{n})
. In case of deterministic sequence,
x_{n}=o(y_{n})
denotes the convergence
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}|x_{n}/y_{n}|=0
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
denote the set of observable time-points that satisfy the smoothing condition
\max_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We also require that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is asymptotically dense in the temporal horizon
\mathcal{T}
. In other words, for any
\tilde{t}\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists a sequence of points
\tilde{t}_{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
such that
|\tilde{t}-\tilde{t}_{n}|\rightarrow 0
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume conditions
(A1)
-
(A3)
hold. Suppose that the number of spatial locations
p
is such that
p=p_{n}=o(n^{1/2})
and
p_{n}^{-1}\log n=\mathcal{O}(1)
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, for all
\bm{x}
lying in the compact set
\chi
, the sequence of estimators
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})
of the population (theoretical) quantile
Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})
satisfies

\|\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})-Q(\bm{u},\bm{x})\|=\mathcal{O}_{p}(p/\sqrt{n}),

(4.1)

provided a square-integrable spatial process
\bm{u}
, i.e.
\int_{\mathcal{S}}|u(s)|^{2}ds<1
.

In Theorem
4.1
, the number of spatial locations
p
is allowed to tend to infinity but at a vanishing rate relatively to
n^{1/2}
. It turns out that such a mild restriction on the growth rate of
p_{n}
is just what it takes for ensuring the consistency of the quantile estimator paving the way for attaining a non-degenarate weak limit.

For ascertaining the asymptotic normality of the proposed quantile estimator, however, the number of spatial locations must align with the initial requirement that
\mathcal{S}_{p}
becomes asymptotically dense in
\mathcal{S}
. Analogously to the temporal domain, we fix the setting where the observable spatial points
\mathcal{S}_{p}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{p}\}
satisfy the condition
\max_{1\leqslant j\neq k\leqslant n}d(s_{j},s_{k})\rightarrow 0
as
p\rightarrow\infty
. Here,
d(s_{j},s_{k})
represents the geodesic distance between the spatial coordinates
s_{j}
and
s_{k}
. This ensures that
\mathcal{S}_{p}
becomes dense in
\mathcal{S}
with a growing number of spatial locations
p_{n}
based on the geodesic metric. Also, the bandwidth
b_{n}
of the kernel matrix, architected and discussed in Section
3.2
as
b_{n}\rightarrow 0
, must be such that
nb_{n}\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
so that the maximum eigenvalue of
\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
is uniformly tight.

4.1
Assumptions

While assumptions
(A1)
to
(A3)
in Section
3
lay the groundwork for this paper, the next set of assumptions
(B1)
to
(B4)
are deemed sufficient for attaining a Gaussian limiting distribution for the proposed quantile estimator.

Let
f_{t}
denote the joint probability density function of
\bm{X}(t)
and
\mathcal{G}_{k}
and
\mathcal{G}_{k-}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{\bm{Y}(t,s),\bm{X}(t):t\leqslant t_{k},s\in\mathcal{S}\}
and
\{\bm{Y}(t,s):t<t_{k},s\in\mathcal{S}\}\cup\{\bm{X}(t):t\leqslant t_{k},s\in\mathcal{S}\}
respectively. Additionally, let
\mathcal{P}_{k}
be the projection operator defined as
\mathcal{P}_{k}(Z):=\mathbb{E}(Z\mid\mathcal{G}_{k})-\mathbb{E}(Z\mid\mathcal{G}_{k-1})
, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(B1)

Suppose that the joint probability density function
f_{t}
is differentiable with respect to each coordinate of
\bm{X}(t)
, for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
. This takes care of finite-dimensional distributions of the observable process.

(B2)

The bandwidth
b_{n}>0
satisfies the following boundary condition as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

\max\left(b_{n}^{4/3}\log n,\,\frac{(\log n)^{3}}{nb_{n}^{3}},\frac{(\log n)^{2}}{b_{n}^{4/3}}\frac{\Xi_{n}}{n^{2}}\right)=o(1),

with the cumulative effect of the first error
\bm{\xi}(0)
in predicting the covariate process
\bm{X}(n)
defined as

\Xi_{n}:=n\Theta_{2n}^{2}+\alpha_{n}

(
Zhao and Wu, 2008
, see)
where

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{x\in\chi}\|\mathcal{P}_{0}(f_{t}(\bm{x}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}))\|+\sup_{x\in\chi}\|\mathcal{P}_{0}(f_{t}^{\prime}(\bm{x}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}))\|,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=n}^{m}\left(\Theta_{n+k}-\Theta_{k}\right)^{2}.

(B3)

There exists a constant
c>1
such that

\dfrac{1}{c}<\sup_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\dfrac{\lambda_{1}(\bm{K}(t))}{\lambda_{p}(\bm{K}(t))}<c,

where
\bm{K}(t)=\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t))
is the
p\times p
kernel matrix as before and
\lambda_{1}(\bm{A})
and
\lambda_{p}(\bm{A})
respectively denote the largest and the smallest eigenvalues of the matrix
\bm{A}
.

(B4)

The number of spatial points
p_{n}
is such that
p_{n}b_{n}\rightarrow 0
,
p_{n}^{9/8}b_{n}\rightarrow\infty
and
p_{n}^{17/8}/n\rightarrow 0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption
(B1)
is natural and indeed a customary assumption that ensures smoothness of the probability density functions, allowing to employ the standard techniques in asymptotic analysis. Assumption
(B2)
is a further restriction to the growth of the bandwidth
b_{n}
of the kernel matrix, which exerts influence on the strength of dependence of the process. From
(B2)
, it results that
b_{n}^{-1}(n^{-1/3}\log n)=o(1)
, thus imposing a lower bound on
b_{n}
, and
b_{n}(\log n)^{3/4}=o(1)
, which provides an upper bound for
b_{n}
. A popular choice for
b_{n}>0
is that of the type
c_{0}n^{-\beta}
for some (fixed) constants
c_{0}>0
and
\beta>0
. If it is used in tandem with the kernel proposed in Section
3.2
, we find that
b_{n}=n^{-1/5}
, a sequence that has been singled out in
Zhao and Wu, 2008
for producing satisfactory results.

Next, we note that the third term embedded in the triplet in
(B2)
implies
\Xi_{n}/n^{2}=o(1)
due to the earlier condition
b_{n}^{-1}(n^{-1/3}\log n)=o(1)
in conjunction with
nb_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. Because each summand of the partial
\Theta_{n}
gives the contribution of
\epsilon_{0}
(as in
(A1)
) to the associated predictor
\bm{X}(t_{i+1})
, this term plays a relevant role in moderating the dependence of
\bm{X}(t)
. In particular, the random vectors
\bm{X}(t_{i})
must satisfy a summability property designed to maintain the integrity of the predictor process
\bm{X}(t)
by way of ensuring that the covariance structure of the spatio-temporal process is well-defined and can always be estimated. On the whole, this assumption has a number of parallels with published works on inference for spatio-temporal processes
(
Zhao and Wu, 2008
;
Zhou and Wu, 2009
;
Deb and Jana, 2021
, see e.g.)
). According to
Zhao and Wu, 2008
, a condition such as this corrals virtually the full spectrum for the strength of dependence, from the boundary case of the predictor process
\bm{X}(t)
exhibiting only short-range dependence (SRD, determined by
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Xi_{n}<\infty
) to a weakening long-range dependence (LRD).

Assumption
(B3)
on the other hand is required in order to control random oscillations of the kernel matrix. In keeping with the random matrix theory, Wigner’s law asserts that the eigenvalues of the
p\times p
covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{X}(t))
are of order
\sqrt{p}

(
Vershynin, 2010
)
for sufficiently large
p
, hence monotonically increasing with
n
and
p
. This means that for any fixed
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the ratio of the largest and the smallest eigenvalue of
\bm{K}(t)
remains bounded for sufficiently large
p
. For the first finitely many terms, boundedness of this ratio is ensured by tightness and positive definiteness of the kernel matrix
\bm{K}(t)
. Assumption
(B3)
is a stronger version of this statement that requires uniform boundedness over all choices of
t\in\mathcal{T}
. This assumption guarantees that there exists a suitable kernel matrix such that the eigenvalues of the covariance matrices of the kernel-weighted observables
\bm{Y}(t)
remain bounded. Finally, assumption
(B4)
in conjunction with the bounds on bandwidth
b_{n}
that ensue from
(B2)
gives that
(\log n)^{-2/3}\ll p_{n}\ll\left(n/\log n\right)^{1/3}
, for large enough
n
. This speaks to the ultra-dimension element in our methods.

4.2
Asymptotic distribution

In this section, we establish the Gaussian limiting distribution attained by the proposed quantile estimator. The consistency of the proposed quantile estimator expounded in Theorem
4.1
and accompanying text herald the rate at which the number of locations
p
can grow in relation to the number of time-points
n
so as to make this nonparametric quantile estimation feasible.

Continuing along the lines of (
3.3
) and adding to the notation introduced as part of Lemma
3.2
, we define

\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}):=-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\left[\dfrac{\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q})}{\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\|_{\bm{K}(t_{i})}}+\bm{u}\right].

Let
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}_{0})
be the matrix whose
i
-th row is comprised of the gradient of
\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q})
with respect to the
i
-th coordinate of
\bm{q}
. Also, let us denote by
\nabla^{3}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q})
the third order tensor whose
(i,j,k)
-th entry is the third order derivative of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q})
with respect to the
i,j
and
k
-th coordinate of
\bm{q}
. We have the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Under assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
and
(B1)
-
(B4)
, the following hold.

(P1)

There exists a
p
-variate Gaussian random variable
\bm{Z}(\bm{q}_{0})
with mean parameter
\eta_{t}(\bm{q}_{0})
and dispersion matrix
\Omega_{t}(\bm{q}_{0})
, such that

\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}_{p}}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[h\left((pb_{n})^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}_{0})\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[h(\bm{Z}(\bm{q}_{0})\right]\right\|=\mathcal{O}_{p}(p^{2}/n),

where
\mathcal{B}_{p}
is a sequence of compact subsets of
B^{p-1}
,
\mathcal{H}
is the set of bounded thrice differentiable functions from
\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}
with a bounded derivative up to the third order.

(P2)

There exists a collection of positive definite matrices
\{\Psi_{t}(\bm{q}_{0})\}
for all possible
\bm{q}_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{p}
as a minimizer of (
2.4
) with respect to the spatial points
\mathcal{S}_{p}
, such that

\sup_{\bm{q}_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{p}}\left\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}_{0})-\Psi_{t}(\bm{q}_{0})\right\|\xrightarrow{P}0.

(P3)

There exists
M>0
(not depending on
n
) such that

\sup_{\bm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{p}}\left\|\nabla^{3}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)})(\bm{q})\right\|\leqslant M.

The three parts
(P1)
-
(P3)
embodying Proposition
1
, whose proof is deferred to the supplementary material, mirror each of the usual necessary steps to demonstrating the asymptotic normality of
M
-estimators
(
Huber, 2011
)
. The asymptotic development for the proposed estimator of the conditional quantile of
\bm{Y}(t)
, given
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}

(

In order to lessen the notational burden throughout the remainder of this paper, we collect the relevant building blocks in the following:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}_{0})\mid\mathcal{G}_{t_{i}-}\right]=\bm{K}(t_{i})\left(\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t_{i}))-\bm{q}_{0}\right),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{1}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}_{0})\mid\mathcal{G}_{t_{i}-}\right]=\bm{K}(t_{i})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{X}(t_{i}))\bm{K}(t_{i}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{2}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{1}{\|\bm{\eta}(t_{i})\|}\bm{I}_{p}-\dfrac{\bm{\eta}(t_{i})\bm{\eta}(t_{i})^{\intercal}}{\|\bm{\eta}(t_{i})\|^{3}}\right),

(4.4)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{t}(\bm{q}_{0}):=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{npb_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\left[\dfrac{\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t))-\bm{q}_{0}}{\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t))-\bm{q}_{0}\|}-\dfrac{\bm{\eta}(t_{i})}{\|\bm{\eta}(t_{i})\|}\right],

(4.5)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{t}(\bm{q}_{0}):=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n^{2}p^{2}b_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\bm{V}_{2}(t_{i})\bm{V}_{1}(t_{i})\bm{V}_{2}(t_{i})^{\intercal}\bm{K}(t_{i}),

(4.6)

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{t}(\bm{q}_{0}):=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\bm{V}_{2}(t_{i})\bm{K}(t_{i}).

(4.7)

We arrive at the main result in this paper, stated in the following theorem.

.

(B1)
-
(B4)
and in the setup of Theorem

\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{B}_{p}}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\|\mathbb{E}\Bigl[h\left((pb_{n})^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{x})-\bm{q}_{0}\right)\Bigr]-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[h\left(-\bm{\Psi}_{t}(\bm{q}_{0})^{-1}\bm{Z}(\bm{q}_{0})\right)\Bigr]\right\|\xrightarrow{P}0,

where

At this point, we tactically turn focus to sketching a proof for Proposition
1

\bigl(\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}_{0})\bigr)/\|\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}_{0})\|

with respect to the random variables
\bm{Y}(t_{i})

\bm{Y}(t_{i})
or
\bm{\xi}(t_{i})
. Indeed, the expressions (
4.2
)-(
4.4

i.e.,

\nabla\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q}_{0})=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\left(\dfrac{\bm{\eta}(t_{i})}{\|\bm{\eta}(t_{i})\|}+\bm{u}\right)+\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\bm{V}_{2}(t_{i})\bm{V}_{1}^{1/2}(t_{i})\bm{\xi}(t_{i})\\
+\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{K}(t_{i})\|\|\bm{\eta}(t_{i})\|^{-3}\|\bm{V}_{1}^{1/2}(t_{i})\|\|\bm{\xi}(t_{i})\|^{2}).

The proof now unfolds in three steps. Owing to Lemma

are expounded in Appendix A1 as part of the supplementary material accompanying this paper.

As for the proof of Proposition

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u},n}^{(p)}(\bm{q})=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(t_{i})\biggl[\dfrac{1}{\left\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\right\|_{\bm{K}(t_{i})}}\bm{I}_{p}-\dfrac{\bm{K}(t_{i})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q})(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q})^{\intercal}\bm{K}(t_{i})}{\left\|\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\bm{q}\right\|^{3}_{\bm{K}(t_{i})}}\biggr]\bm{K}(t_{i}).

After splitting the above expression into a sum of martingale difference terms and a remainder term, the former sum, suitably normalized, can be shown to converge to zero via application of a martingale central limit theorem
(
Belloni and Oliveira, 2018
, see)
. The dominant component in the remainder term boils down to
\bm{\Psi}_{t}(\bm{q}_{0})
with everything else being asymptotically negligible. This is achieved through a dedicated Taylor’s expansion.

Finally, regarding Proposition
(P3)

independent of

and

for all

is a Lipschitz function of

with a Lipschitz constant
\bigl(\delta^{-1}\sup_{t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{p}}\lambda_{1}(\bm{K}(t_{i}))/\lambda_{p}(\bm{K}(t_{i}))\bigr)^{-3}

5
Statistical inference: interval estimation and hypotheses testing

While the estimation of quantile curves yielding from (
3.2
) possesses several desirable qualities, including the consistency and asymptotic normality, the underpinning convergence is pointwise in the temporal domain, that is, it is obtained for each value of the covariate process
\{\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}\}
. From the practical stance, an interesting approach resides in obtaining simultaneous confidence band. Inevitably, this will require extending the asymptotic properties of the estimator to the entire temporal domain, uniformly on the index range of
\bm{X}(\cdot)
. The means to achieve this has a number of parallels with the techniques exploited in
Zhao and Wu, 2008
.

To this end, we shall consider a set of values for the covariates, sufficiently apart in order that at every time-point
t
, the kernel estimator is affected by at most one of the values in that set. This is to ensure that no correlation between estimates are at play for sufficiently distant time-points. Upon the already obtained asymptotic normality results, this consideration opens way to making uniform statistical inference possible: asymptotic behavior of the proposed nonparametric estimator’s sequence across multiple time-points, uniformly, is now within reach. The ultimate aim is to devise meaningful asymptotic results pertaining to partial maxima of this sequence of estimators by drawing on extreme value theory.

We define
\chi_{n}=\{\bm{X}(t):t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\}\subseteq\chi
as the range of the covariate process
\{\bm{X}(t)\}
. We assume that, for any
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\chi_{n}
,
\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|>2b_{n}\rightarrow 0
thus implying that
\chi_{n}
becomes dense within
\chi
as
n
tends to infinity. The cardinality of
\chi_{n}
is encapsulated in
m_{n}
.

5.1
Simultaneous confidence bounds

For the sake of simplicity, the results comprising in this section refrain to
\bm{u}
-quantiles with
\bm{u}=(2\tau-1)\bm{1}_{p}/\sqrt{p}
for some
\tau\in(0,1)
. Henceforth, we denote this as
\bm{u}_{\tau}
. This framework essentially renders quantiles that spread evenly among the considered spatial locations. We shall denote the corresponding quantile estimator by
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})
(or sometimes simply by
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau,\bm{x})
) whereas the true targeted quantile is denoted by
Q(\tau,\bm{x})
. We emphasize that this slight restriction to the framework is imposed for convenience, but all results derived in this section can be adapted to incorporate the more general multivariate case as before, in a relatively straightforward manner.

By construction, it follows from the definition of
\chi_{n}
that, for any two points
\bm{x}_{i_{1}},\bm{x}_{i_{2}}\in\chi_{n}
,
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
, and every
\delta>0
, the exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|\bm{x}_{i_{1}}-\bm{x}_{i_{2}}\right\|<2\delta
,
i_{1},\,i_{2}>n_{0}
. Hence, for every
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
,
\|\bm{X}(t)-\bm{x}_{j}\|<\delta
,
j=i_{1},i_{2}
, which results from the triangle inequality. Since
\mathcal{K}
has bounded support in the unit hypersphere
B^{d-1}
, it thus follows that the cross products of the weights intervening in the objective function resolve to zero, i.e.

\bm{K}(\bm{x}_{i_{1}},\bm{X}(t))\bm{K}(\bm{x}_{i_{2}},\bm{X}(t))=c_{n}^{-2}\mathcal{K}\left(\dfrac{\bm{X}(t)-\bm{x}_{i_{1}}}{b_{n}}\right)\mathcal{K}\left(\dfrac{\bm{X}(t)-\bm{x}_{i_{2}}}{b_{n}}\right)=0

(5.1)

(the reader may wish to recall the discussion on
c_{n}
as part of Section
3.2
). Equation (
5.1
) lays bare that fixing two quantile levels
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
(
0<\tau,\tau^{\prime}<1
), so that objective function if optimized for
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau,\bm{x}_{i_{1}})
contains the terms with weights
\bm{K}(\bm{x}_{i_{1}},\bm{X}(t))
for some
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, whereas for the sequence of estimators
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau^{\prime},\bm{x}_{i_{2}})
these weights amount to
\bm{K}(\bm{x}_{i_{2}},\bm{X}(t))
, results in the two objective functions (both for
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau,\bm{x}_{i_{1}})
and
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau^{\prime},\bm{x}_{i_{2}})
) being uncorrelated, thus implying that the estimators
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau,\bm{x}_{i_{1}})
and
\widehat{\bm{q}}(\tau^{\prime},\bm{x}_{i_{2}})
are also uncorrelated.

Therefore, by taking a multitude of points on the set
\chi_{n}=\{\bm{x}_{i_{1}},\bm{x}_{i_{2}},\dots\bm{x}_{i_{m_{n}}}\}
, we obtain from Theorem
4.2
that the estimated process
\{\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})(s):s\in\mathcal{S}\}
becomes asymptotically a Gaussian process on both temporal and spatial domains, for every
\bm{x}\in\chi_{n}
. More concretely, on the finite set of spatial points
S_{d}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots s_{d}\}
and with unit vector
\bm{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have for every
\bm{x}\in\chi_{n}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, that

R_{n}(\bm{x},\bm{l},S_{d})=(pb_{n})^{-1}\dfrac{\bm{l}^{\intercal}(\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}-Q(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}-\bm{\mu}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}})}{\sqrt{\bm{l}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}\bm{l}}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(5.2)

We highlight that
\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}
and
Q(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}
respectively denote spatial process
\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})
and
Q(\tau,\bm{x})
restricted to the spatial locations in
S_{d}
. Letting
q_{0}=Q(\tau,\bm{x})
, then
\bm{\mu}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}
respectively denote mean and covariance structure, evaluated at the spatial locations in the set
S_{d}
, of the Gaussian process

characterized by

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[G(\bm{x},s)\right]&=\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}}^{-1}({q}_{0})\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}}({q}_{0})\\
\text{Cov}\left(G(\bm{x},s),G(\bm{x}^{\prime},s)\right)&=\begin{cases}\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}}^{-1}({q}_{0})\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}}({q}_{0})\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}}^{-1}({q}_{0}),&\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{\prime};\\
0,&\bm{x}\neq\bm{x}^{\prime}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(5.3)

where the terms
\bm{\eta}({q}_{0}),\bm{\Omega}({q}_{0})
and
\bm{\Psi}({q}_{0})
are evaluated at
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}
as defined in (
4.5
)-(
4.7

We now proceed to obtaining the asymptotic behavior of the supremum of

over the temporal index through
\sup_{\bm{x}\in\chi_{n}}\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})(s)
for any

. From the case in point that
\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x}_{i_{1}})
and
\hat{q}_{n}(\tau^{\prime},\bm{x}_{i_{2}})

,

. Owing to the rich theory of extreme values, there exists normalizing functions
C_{t}
specific to the folded normal distribution,

C_{t}(z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\log t}}\left(z-\frac{\log\log t+\log(4\pi)}{2}\right)

(5.4)

(see

, p.72)
, such that the weak convergence to the Gumbel distribution function is attained in the following sense: for all

\ \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\bm{x}\in\chi_{m_{n}}}\sup_{\left\|\bm{l}\right\|=1}|R_{n}(\bm{x},\bm{l},S_{d})|\leqslant C_{m_{n}}(z+2\log m_{n})\right)=\exp\{-e^{-z}\}.

(5.5)

Next, we consider the identification

\sup_{\left\|\bm{l}\right\|=1}\dfrac{\bm{l}^{\intercal}\bm{A}\bm{l}}{\bm{l}^{\intercal}\bm{B}\bm{l}}=\lambda_{1}\left(\bm{B}^{-1/2}\bm{A}\bm{B}^{-1/2}\right),

where
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
are positive definite matrices. In conjunction with the limiting distribution statement in (

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sup_{\bm{x}\in\chi_{m_{n}}}\dfrac{1}{pb_{n}}\lambda_{1}\left[\bm{\Sigma}_{q}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}|_{S_{d}}\bm{e}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}\,\bm{e}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{q}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}|_{S_{d}}\right]\\
<C_{m_{n}}\left(z+2\log m_{n}\right)\biggr)=\exp\{-e^{-z}\},

(5.6)

by taking supremum over all possible unit vectors
\bm{l}
and whereby
\bm{e}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}=(\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}-Q(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}-\bm{\mu}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}})
. Hence, under the assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
and
(B1)
-
(B4)
, with a judicious choice of
\chi_{m_{n}}
, i.e., so long as

can be guaranteed to fulfill (

from the “larger” set

. This result, which resonates with that in Theorem 2 of

\tau
-quantile curves

ascribed to multiple values
\bm{x}\in\chi_{n}
. Because

becomes increasingly dense in
\chi

tends to

\{Q(\tau,\bm{x}):\bm{x}\in\chi\}
arises from (

A contrast-like linear combination of the quantile curves across different locations stems from (
5.5
) as follows: with a proper specification of the set
S_{d}
and vector
\bm{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
\bm{l}^{\intercal}Q(\tau,\bm{x})
becomes the desired contrast-like quantile curve, we find that the asymptotic simultaneous
100(1-\alpha^{*})\%
-confidence bounds for
\bm{l}^{\intercal}Q(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}
consist of

\bm{l}^{\intercal}\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}-\bm{l}^{\intercal}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}\pm\left[C_{m_{n}}\left(-\log\left(-\log(1-\alpha^{*}\right))+2\log m_{n}\right)\right](pb_{n})\left(\bm{l}^{\intercal}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{S_{d}}\bm{l}\right)^{1/2},

with embedded consistent estimators
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}(\bm{x})
and
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(\bm{x})

\bm{\mu}_{q}(\bm{x})
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{q}(\bm{x})

\inf_{\bm{x}}R_{n}(\bm{x},\bm{l},S_{d})
and

5.2
Two tests

The statement in (
5.6
) can be adapted to the purpose of hypothesis testing aimed at statistically validating structural assumptions on the quantile curves across all locations simultaneously. Under the null hypothesis about the structural restriction of the quantile curve
Q(\tau,\bm{x})
at some specific quantile
\tau\in[0,1]
, we can calculate the observed value of the test statistic

\bm{e}_{0}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}=(\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}-\hat{q}_{0}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}})

(5.7)

where
\hat{q}_{0}(\tau,\bm{x})
stands as the estimate of
Q(\tau,\bm{x})
under the postulated null hypothesis
H_{0}
regarding a stable regime, and
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}(\bm{x})
is the estimate of the mean of the Gaussian process introduced in (
5.3
) under the null hypothesis. This can be done by replacing
\bm{\mu}(\bm{X}(t))
and
\bm{q}_{0}
by the estimated median
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{0},\bm{X}(t))
and the estimated quantile
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{n}(\bm{u},\bm{X}(t))
under
H_{0}
in the corresponding terms. Then, the null hypothesis is rejected if (
5.7
) exhibits large oscillation, i.e., at the nominal level of the test
\alpha^{*}
,
H_{0}
is rejected if

\dfrac{1}{pb_{n}}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\chi_{n}}\lambda_{1}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\bm{e}_{0}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\bm{e}_{0}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}^{\intercal}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\right)

exceeds the critical value
c_{\alpha^{*}}=C_{m_{n}}\left(-\log\left(-\log(1-\alpha^{*})\right)+2\log m_{n}\right)
.

A structural assumption that is often put to testing is that of homogeneity of the covariate effect across the spatial domain, which, if true, will automatically narrow down the spatio-temporal structure of the data to a temporal one. This is statistically parlayed in the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{(1)}:\,Q(\tau,\bm{x})=\beta(\bm{x})\bm{u}_{\tau}
, for all
\tau\in[0,1]
. Under
H_{0}^{(1)}
, the estimator
\widehat{\beta}(\bm{x})
arises from (
3.5
) as a solution of:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t_{i}))\left[\dfrac{\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t_{i}))(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\widehat{\beta}(\bm{x})\bm{u}_{\tau})}{\|\bm{K}(\bm{x},\bm{X}(t_{i}))(\bm{Y}(t_{i})-\widehat{\beta}(x)\bm{u}_{\tau})\|}+\bm{u}_{\tau}\right]=0,

and the null hypothesis is rejected for large values of the test statistic, i.e., if

\dfrac{1}{pb_{n}}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\chi_{n}}\lambda_{1}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\bm{e}_{1}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\bm{e}_{1}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}^{\intercal}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\right)>c_{\alpha^{*}}

on the relevant set
\mathcal{S}_{p}
of spatial locations, where

\bm{e}_{1}(\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}=(\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,\bm{x})|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}-\widehat{\beta}(x)\bm{u}_{\tau}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}(x)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}})
can be defined.

The second testing procedure with practical importance under the realm of (
2.2
) ascribes time to a covariate through
\bm{X}(t)=t
. The hypothesis of homogeneity over time then translates into
H_{0}^{(2)}:\,Q(\bm{u}_{\tau},t)/Q(\bm{u}_{\tau},1)=t
, for all
t\in(0,1]
and
\tau\in[0,1]
. At the significance level
\alpha^{*}
,
H_{0}^{(2)}
is rejected if

\dfrac{1}{pb_{n}}\sup_{t\in(0,1]}\lambda_{1}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(t)^{-1/2}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\bm{e}_{2}(t)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\bm{e}_{2}(t)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}^{\intercal}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}(t)^{-1/2}|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\right)>c_{\alpha^{*}}\,,

with associated
\bm{e}_{2}(t)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}=(\hat{q}_{n}(\tau,t)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}-t\hat{q}_{n}(\bm{u}_{\tau},1)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{q}(x)|_{\mathcal{S}_{p}})
.

Proofs

This section is encompassed of the proofs of the two main theorems in this paper as well as of their ancillary results. We begin by addressing Lemma

Proof of Lemma

7
Forecasting energy demand

In order to experimentally verify the good overall performance of the proposed quantile estimator, we conduct a simulation study that encompasses two distinct setups: one without any explicit spatial information and another setup with proper spatial information included. In both cases, we find that the proposed estimator achieves reasonable prediction accuracy. The simulation results are compiled in Appendix B featuring in the supplementary material.

In this section, the proposed quantile regression methodology is applied to produce inferences on the Thames Valley Vision (TVV) data, collected by Scottish and Southern Electricity Network (SSEN), the primary industrial partner of the TVV project funded by UK gas and electricity regulator Ofgem through the Low Carbon Networks Fund and Network Innovation Competition. The dataset handled here has been partly studied in
Jacob et al., 2020
. The project’s overall aim was to evaluate a typical low voltage network using monitoring in households and substations in order to simulate future demand scenarios.

The dataset under study consists of the energy profiles for
226
households with half-hourly resolution, between 19th July 2015 and 20th September 2015, thus spanning
9
weeks of data. In our analysis, the first
8
weeks of data are used as a training set whereas our proposed estimator is employed to predict different quantiles for last week’s electricity demand. Given the temporal resolution of the data, the number of observations for every location in the training set is
n=2688
. In line with the theory underpinning the quantile estimation introduced in the present paper (see Theorem
4.1
), the number of spatial points
p=226
far exceeds the benchmark of
\sqrt{n}\approx 52
. This provides opportunity to split the households into clusters of size not larger than
52
based on their average daily demand (ADD) profiles. To determine these clusters, we calculated the pairwise dynamic time warping (DTW) distance between every two household’s ADD profiles using the FastDTW algorithm from
Gold and Sharir, 2018
with Hierarchical clustering on the households.
Figure

1

j=1,2,\ldots,12
of sizes 21, 52, 42, 17, 10, 42, 28, 4, 2, 4, 2 and 2, respectively.

We find that the pairwise within-cluster distances tend to be smaller than the between-cluster distances, which is reflected in the lighter shaded blocks containing the reverse diagonal of the heatmap. Hence, it is reasonable to assume that electricity demand profiles of two households residing in two different clusters are independent or weakly dependent (the latter is not a deterrent to our methods). Consequently, we applied our proposed quantile estimation to each cluster separately. In a nutshell, our statistical analysis revolves broadly around two goals:

(i)

For tackling the estimation problem described in (i), we estimated quantile functions from the training data relating the electricity demand profiles per cluster. The one-week ahead predicted quantiles are displayed in
