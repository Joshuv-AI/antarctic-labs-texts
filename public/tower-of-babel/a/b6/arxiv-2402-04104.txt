A Godunov–type scheme for a scalar conservation law with space-time flux discontinuity
Kwame Atta Gyamfi

Abstract
We present and analyze a new finite volume scheme of Gudonov-type for a nonlinear scalar conservation law whose flux function has a discontinuous coefficient due to time-dependent changes in its sign along a Lipschitz continuous curve.

Introduction

In this article, we propose a numerical scheme for solving a scalar conservation law with a flux function that is discontinuous in space and time. The equation writes

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}F(t,x,\rho)=0,\\
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\;\rho(t,\pm 1)=0,\end{cases}

(1.1)

in which

•

t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}=[0,\infty)
is the time variable and
x\in\Omega=]-1,1[
is the space variable;

•

(t,x)\mapsto\rho(t,x)
is the unknown function, and
\rho\in\mathbf{L^{\infty}}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega;[0,1])
;

•

F
representing the flux function is given by

F(t,x,\rho)\,\dot{=}\,\sign(x-\xi(t))f(\rho)

(1.2)

where

f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\mbox{ is
concave}\,,\quad f(0)=0=f(1)\,,\quad\max_{\rho\in[0,1]}{f(\rho)}>0\,,\quad\lim_{\rho\to 0+}\frac{f(\rho)}{\rho}\in\mathbb{R};

(1.3)

\xi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\Omega\mbox{ is Lipschitz continuous with}\quad\left[\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\xi,\,\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\xi\right]\subset(-1,1)\,.

(1.4)

As equation (
1.1
) is a fundamental law, most partial differential equations (PDEs) problems arising in the physical and engineering sciences can be formulated in its form. Examples can be found in (1) porous media: modeling the two-phase flow in porous media
Gimse & Risebro 1992
;
Jaffré 1995
and continuous sedimentation in ideal clarifier-thickener units
Bürger et al. 2004
; (2) traffic flow: modeling a toll gate along a highway
Colombo & Goatin 2007
and traffic flow with changing road surface conditions
Bürger & Karlsen 2003
;
Mochon 1987
; (3) ion etching in semiconductor device fabrication
Ross 1988
; (4) pedestrian flow models
Hughes 2002
;
Amadori et al. 2014
;
El-Khatib et al. 2013
.

More precisely, equation (
1.1
) derives from the one-dimensional Hughes model
Hughes 2002
of pedestrian flow in a narrow corridor with fixed exits at
x=\pm 1
. However, in contrast to the Hughes model, the time-dependent flux interface
\xi(t)
considered in this work is not required to satisfy the implicit relation involving a monotone cost function
c(\rho)

\int_{-1}^{\xi(t)}c(\rho(t,x))dx=\int_{\xi(t)}^{1}c(\rho(t,x))dx,\quad\forall~t\geq 0.

(1.5)

Therefore, the equation considered in this work assumes no nonlocal conditions on the flux via the turning curve
x=\xi(t)
even though the numerical scheme proposed here can easily be adapted to solve Hughes’ model. It is worth noting that in the context of this model, the function
\xi(t)
is commonly known as the turning curve and represents the points where pedestrians change direction to optimize the distance or time required to reach exits. See
Amadori et al. 2014
. In this paper, we will use the term ”flux interface” or simply ”the interface” to refer to
\xi
. Moreover, we will refer to a scalar conservation law with discontinuous flux as the discontinuous flux problem.

Research works dedicated to solutions to discontinuous flux problems have been extensively documented in the literature. See articles
Jaffré 1995
;
Adimurthi et al. 2004
;
Dutta et al. 2014
;
Towers 2001
;
Bachmann 2006
;
Karlsen 2003
;
Andreianov et al. 2011
;
Diehl 1995
;
Adimurthi et al. 2005
;
Andreianov & Cancès 2012
;
Wen & Jin 2008
;
Bachmann 2006
. It is a well-known fact that even if the flux function is continuous and the initial data do not contain jump discontinuities, the global Cauchy problem for a scalar conservation law does not admit classical solutions that are valid for all times
t>0
. Thus, there exists a finite time beyond which classical solutions develop discontinuities, such as shock waves. The occurrence of these irregularities in the solution, which is severe for equations with discontinuous coefficients such as (
1.1
), is largely attributed to the nonlinearity of the flux function. For example, equation (
1.1
) generalizes to the system

\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(k(t,x)f(\rho))=0,\quad k_{t}=0

(1.6)

whose speed matrix (or Jacobian matrix) after writing (
1.6
) in nonconservative form has a repeated zero eigenvalue for some values of
(\rho,k)
.
For this reason, the appropriate setting for an admissible solution is the space of discontinuous functions, such as the space of
\mathbf{L^{\infty}}
functions, in which a shock wave is well-defined. Such solutions are sought in the weak (or distributional) sense and hence are referred to as weak solutions
Evans 1998
. Popular methods to construct solutions include numerical approximation methods based on finite volume/difference schemes
Andreianov & Sylla 2023
;
Bürger et al. 2004
;
Karlsen & Towers 2004
;
Karlsen & Towers 2017
, wavefront tracking algorithm
Goatin & Mimault 2013
;
Gimse & Risebro 1992
;
Coclite & Risebro 2005
, and others. In this paper, we discuss the existence of entropy weak solution to (
1.1
) by constructing the approximate solution via a finite volume method exclusive to this class of equations.

In general, deriving an existence theory for a class of hyperbolic conservation law relies on
a priori
estimates with which a compactness argument can be used ’to pass to the limit’ in the solution through an appropriate compactness theory such as Helly’s theory. However, for selected time-dependent discontinuous flux problems, such as (
1.1
), such estimates are difficult to obtain or nonexistent, even after transforming approximate solutions via an appropriate singular mapping, as was done in
Coclite & Risebro 2005
. In such situations, alternative approaches that are free from these estimates, including compensated compactness (see
Karlsen & Towers 2004
), measure-valued solutions (see
Andreianov et al. 2010
), and kinetic formulation (see
Dotti & Vovelle 2019
), provide handy tools to establish rigorous existence results. In the latter two cases, it is enough to use only standard estimates, such as the so-called ”weak BV estimates,” to prove the convergence of the approximate solution to the entropy process solution under appropriate CFL conditions
Eymard et al. 2000
;
Eymard et al. 2007
.

The notion of entropy solutions for discontinuous flux problems does not follow the standard notions suitable for a generic conservation law with continuous coefficients. The main ideas of entropy solutions for discontinuous flux problems rely on applying Kruzkhov’s admissibility conditions away from the flux interface but enforcing additional ”entropy conditions” at the interface to aid the selection of unique and admissible solutions. It has been argued in
Adimurthi et al. 2005
that as a result of this occurrence, there exists an infinite number of
L^{1}
contractive semigroups, each associated with an interface connection, for a variety of discontinuous flux problems. The principal features of entropy solutions are the shape of the flux and the traces in the solution at the interface (i.e., the so-called
A-B
entropy connection)
Andreianov & Cancès 2015
;
Dutta et al. 2014
;
Andreianov & Cancès 2012
. In the work of
Andreianov et al. 2011
, these concepts were unified by the introduction of a new framework called admissibility germs
\mathcal{G}
(or ”germs” for short) within which the entropy solutions described above reduced to elementary solutions of piecewise constant nature provided their traces satisfy a form of a Rankine-Hugoniot condition at the interface. In our context, this family of piecewise constant constant solutions, typically described by ordered pairs
(p_{l},p_{r})
of the form
p_{l}\chi_{x<\xi(t)}+p_{r}\chi_{x>\xi(t)}
, with
p_{l}
and
p_{r}
in
[0,R]
are maximal and complete.

In this context, we present and analyze a finite volume scheme to solve a scalar conservation law with a single time-dependent flux discontinuity that switches the sign of the flux coefficient between
+1
and
-1
at each
t>0
. We deal with the discontinuity by introducing a moving mesh near
\xi
in the discrete space-time domain and then adapt the value of the flux there based on the slope
\dot{\xi}
. Standard finite volume schemes for continuous flux problems involve discretizing the space domain into numerical cells bounded by grid points. Then the solution for each discrete time is approximated by solving a ’local’ Riemann problem at each grid point whose results are unionized to obtain the solution at the next time step. Here, the moving mesh consists in removing grid points nearest to
\xi
so that the CFL condition is not violated. This approach introduced by
Zhong et al. 1996
, has been used to study traffic flow models on a highway with a mobile bottleneck
Delle & Goatin 2014
;
Chalons et al. 2018
;
Delle & Goatin 2016
. Indeed, the scheme proposed in these works was only analyzed in a mathematically rigorous in
Sylla, Abraham 2022
where existence and uniqueness results were obtained in a fashion similar to ours. However, the scheme of
Sylla, Abraham 2022
uses the Engquist-Osher flux on standard mesh away from the moving interface and the Godunov numerical fluxes at the interfaces with the moving mesh and a flux crossing condition. This scheme was later extended in
Andreianov & Sylla 2023
, where the existence of solutions for conservation laws with multiple space-time flux interface discontinuities. Indeed, any standard ’entropy’ numerical flux such as the Godunovs, Lax Friederich’s, etc, works well for treating solutions away from the interfaces as long as it is monotone and consistent. We would like to highlight that the analysis conducted in
Andreianov & Sylla 2023
bears similarity to our own, and it may be considered more comprehensive, as it accounts for multiple moving interfaces. Nevertheless, considering the scarcity of contributions addressing space-time discontinuous flux problems, our work serves as an additional and valuable contribution to this field.

The remainder of the paper is organized into four main sections. Section
2
focuses on the theoretical aspect of the problem and discusses the Riemann problem for (
1.1
), including the Riemann solver at
\xi
. Here, we present the framework of admissibility germs and prove the relevant properties that could later be extended to obtain a comprehensive theory of the existence of weak entropy solutions in the next article. The next section, Section
3
, presents the numerical scheme and the mesh adaptations (the moving mesh), detailing all possible cases due to changes in slope of
\xi
. Furthermore, approximate entropy inequalities and a uniform
\mathbf{L^{\infty}}
bound on the numerical approximations are also presented. Finally, in Section
5
, we furnish details about the chosen examples, present their numerical simulations, and use them to explore the numerical convergence of the scheme achieved by calculating the error
\mathbf{L^{1}}
, which is provided for each example.

Problem setting

We start this section by defining a weak entropy solution of (
1.1

Definition 2.1
.

Assume that
f
,
\xi
satisfy (
1.3
), (
1.4
), respectively.
We say that map
(t,x)\mapsto\rho(t,x)
is a weak entropy solution to the initial-boundary value problem (
1.1
),
if
\rho
is in
\mathbf{C^{0}}\left([0,+\infty[;~\mathbf{L^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\infty}}}\left(\Omega;~[0,1]\right)\right)
and for any
c\in[0,1]
,
and any test function
\varphi\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}_{\mathbf{c}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2};~[0,+\infty[\right)

the following Kružkhov-type entropy inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!\!\int_{-1}^{1}\left[|\rho-c|\partial_{t}\varphi+\mathcal{F}(t,x,\rho,c)\partial_{x}\varphi\right]\text{d}x\text{d}t+\int_{-1}^{1}|\rho_{0}(x)-c|\,\varphi(0,x)\text{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[f(\rho(t,1-))-f(c)\right]\varphi(t,1)\text{d}t+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[f(\rho(t,-1+))-f(c)\right]\varphi(t,-1)\text{d}t

(2.7)

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{+\infty}f(c)\,\varphi(t,\xi(t))\text{d}t\geq 0,

where, recalling (
1.2
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,x,\rho,c)\dot{=}

\displaystyle\sign(\rho-c)\left[F(t,x,\rho)-F(t,x,c)\right]

(2.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sign(\rho-c)\sign(x-\xi(t))\left[f(\rho)-f(c)\right].

The first line in (
2.1
) originates from the Kružkov definition of entropy weak solution as would be in the case of a Cauchy problem,
Kružkov 1970
. The two terms in the second line come from the boundary condition introduced by Bardos et al. in
Bardos et al. 1979
whereas the term in the last line accounts for the traces in the solution along the discontinuity in the flux.

The solution of the Riemann problem serves as building block for the selection of a numerical scheme appropriate for the equation under consideration. Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f
satisfy (
1.3
).
Then for any given
\rho_{R}\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>v(\rho_{R})
there exists a unique
\rho_{M}\in(0,\rho_{R})
which satisfies (

Proof.

Define the map

\Phi(\rho)=\dfrac{f(\rho_{R})+f(\rho)}{\rho_{R}-\rho}\,,\qquad\rho\in(0,\rho_{R})\,.

It is immediate to see that

\lim_{\rho\to 0+}\Phi(\rho)=f(\rho_{R})/\rho_{R}=v(\rho_{R}),\qquad\lim_{\rho\to\rho_{R}-}\Phi(\rho)=+\infty,

(2.17)

and, by convexity of

, we have that

\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)=\frac{f^{\prime}(\rho)(\rho_{R}-\rho)+f(\rho_{R})+f(\rho)}{(\rho_{R}-\rho)^{2}}\geq\frac{2f(\rho_{R})}{(\rho_{R}-\rho)^{2}}>0.

(2.18)

Therefore
\Phi
is strictly increasing, and there exists a unique solution to (
2.14

∎

.

The following bounds hold for all

Remark 1
.

Since the flux switches sign, the solution to the Riemann problem satisfies the equation with convex flux
-f(\rho)
on the domain
\Omega_{L}=\{(x,t):x<\alpha t\}
, and with concave flux
+f(\rho)

on
\Omega_{R}=\{(x,t):x>\alpha t\}
.

Suppose the pair
(\gamma_{l}\rho(t),\gamma_{r}\rho(t))
are the left and right traces at
\xi(t)
at each
t>0
, then definition
2.1
yields the Rankine-Hugoniot condition

f(\gamma_{r}\rho(t))+f(\gamma_{l}\rho(t))=\xi^{\prime}(t)(\gamma_{r}\rho(t)-\gamma_{l}\rho(t)),

(2.20)

at
\xi(t)
.

As a consequence of Definition
2.1
, if the solution is discontinuous at

the jump is undercompressive and the characteristic lines impinge on
\xi(t)
from the side where the density is higher. More precisely,
if
\rho
is an entropy solution and
(\gamma_{l}\rho(t),\gamma_{r}\rho(t))
are its left and right traces at
\xi(t)
, at each
t>0
we have

\min\{0,-\xi^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(\gamma_{l}\rho(t))\}\max\{0,-\xi^{\prime}(t)+f^{\prime}(\gamma_{r}\rho(t))\}=0.

(2.21)

This necessary condition has been pointed out in
Karlsen 2003
, Prop. 5.1
in a more general framework and in
El-Khatib et al. 2013
, Prop. 2.4
for the Hughes model. The numerical examples used in Section
5
well illustrates this effect.

2.2
The germ of admissible solutions

The time-dependent discontinuity of
f
in
\rho
along the Lipschitz continuous curve
\xi
makes it more appropriate to consider entropy solutions given in Definition (
2.1
) within the admissibility germ framework. In this framework, the inequality (
2.1
) reduces to a pair of traces at
\xi
if the condition (
2.20
) is satisfied. It is important to note that since
\pm f
are assumed to be continuous over
[0,R]^{2}
and the measure of the set
\{s\in[0,1]\text{ s.t. }f^{\prime}(s)=0\}
is zero, the traces
\gamma^{l,r}\rho(t)
are actually one-sided strong traces, as proven in
Andreianov et al. 2011
, Theorem 2.1
. This notion of admissibility germs relies on the idea that Kruzhkov’s entropy is satisfied away from
\xi
, while the ”non-standard” Rankine-Hugoniot condition is adapted at
\xi
. Thanks to the germs, it should be possible to define the notion of an entropy process solution. We proceed to define the germ below.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\xi(t)=\alpha t
, with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
fixed. The germ of admissible solutions
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}
for the conservation law (
1.1
) is

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}:=\left\{(0,\rho_{\alpha})\in[0,1]^{2}:f(\rho_{\alpha})=\alpha\rho_{\alpha}\right\}.

(2.22)

Definition 2.4
.

A germ
\mathcal{G}
is
L^{1}
-dissipative (
L^{1}D
for short), if for any
(p_{l},p_{r})
,
(q_{l},q_{r})\in\mathcal{G}(\xi)
the following dissipative property holds

\mathcal{F}(t,x,p_{l},q_{l})-\mathcal{F}(t,x,p_{r},q_{r})\geq\alpha(\eta(p_{l},q_{l})-\eta(p_{r},q_{r})),

(2.23)

where
\mathcal{F}(t,x,\rho,c)
is defined in Definition (
2.1
) and
\eta(\rho,c):=|\rho-c|
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The unique maximal
L^{1}D
extension of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}
is the subset of
[0,1]^{2}
defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}:=\left\{(p_{l},p_{r})\in[0,1]^{2}:f(p_{r})+f(p_{l})=\alpha(p_{r}-p_{l})\right\}.

(2.24)

Proof.

The fact that
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
contains
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}
is obvious as

f(\rho_{\alpha})+f(0)=\alpha\rho_{\alpha}-0.

To show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
is
L^{1}D
we distinguish three cases :
\alpha=0
,
\alpha>0
, and
\alpha<0
.

If
\alpha=0

the germ only contains four elements
(0,0)
,
(0,1)
,
(1,0)
,
(1,1)
and we can check that (
2.23
) holds as an equality by direct computations.

If
\alpha>0

we consider two sub-cases

I.

If
q_{l}=q_{r}=0
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,x,p_{l},0)-\mathcal{F}(t,x,p_{r},0)

\displaystyle=(-f(p_{l})+f(0))-(f(p_{r})-f(0))

\displaystyle=-(f(p_{r})+f(p_{l}))=\alpha(p_{l}-q_{r})

\displaystyle=\alpha(\eta(p_{l},0)-\eta(p_{r},0))\,,

for all
p_{l},p_{r}\in[0,1]^{2}
. The case in which
q_{l}=q_{r}=1
is similar.

II.

If
q_{l}<q_{r}
(the reverse inequality being impossible due to the sign of
\alpha
and the geometry of the problem) we can observe that

\sign(p_{l}-q_{l})=\sign(p_{r}-q_{r}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,x,p_{l},q_{l})-\mathcal{F}(t,x,p_{r},q_{r})

\displaystyle=\sign(p_{l}-q_{l})\left(-f(p_{l})+f(q_{l})-f(p_{r})+f(q_{r})\right)

\displaystyle=\sign(p_{l}-q_{l})\alpha\left(q_{r}-q_{l}-p_{r}+p_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\eta(p_{l},q_{l})-\eta(p_{r},q_{r}))\,.

If
\alpha<0

the analysis is exactly the same as in the previous case.

It is also clear that
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
is maximal: given any
L^{1}D
germ
\mathcal{G}
one can look for its possible
L^{1}D
extensions, the largest of which is its dual
\mathcal{G}^{*}
, i.e. the set containing all couples

3
The Finite Volume Scheme

In this section, we present a finite volume scheme with a moving mesh adapted near
\xi
to approximate the weak solution of equation (
1.1
).
Our strategy to define the moving mesh consists in discretizing the space-time domain into a reference mesh, but at each time only cells closer to the flux interface
\xi
are adapted such that it is included in the computational mesh. The main feature of the computational (or moving) mesh is that the two cells adjacent to
\xi
have variable
lengths, whereas the cells on the reference mesh have a fixed cell length,
\mathsf{h}
. Modified marching formulas, which incorporate a modified numerical flux function based on the non-classical Riemann solver
(\mathcal{R}^{\alpha})
, are applied to the two cells adjacent to the turning curve. The standard Godunov marching formulas are used for cells away from
\xi
, where the reference mesh is established.

This is a slight variation from the mesh adaptation technique presented in
Zhong et al. 1996
and used in
Delle & Goatin 2014
for a traffic flow model with a moving constraint. This technique involves replacing the cell interface closest to
\xi
and shifting the adjacent interfaces, followed by a Lagrange interpolation formula to calculate a new temporal density average for two adjacent cells. Although their scheme is conservative, the recomputation of the new density averages makes it difficult to analyze the well-balanced property of the resulting scheme. The scheme proposed here does not suffer from this severity. Another variation of the discretization procedure also adapted for the traffic flow model with moving constraints can be found in
Sylla, Abraham 2022
. In the next subsections, we detail our space discretization procedure and present the numerical scheme.

3.3
The numerical fluxes

In this subsection, we introduce the numerical fluxes employed in the scheme.

Define

h^{\pm}(a,b):=\begin{cases}\min_{[a,b]}(\pm f),&\mbox{ if }a\leq b,\\
\max_{[b,a]}(\pm f),&\mbox{ if }a\geq b\end{cases}

(3.41)

and, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

h_{0}(a,b,\alpha)=\begin{cases}f(b)-\alpha b=-b\left(\alpha-v(b)\right),&\mbox{ if }\alpha\geq v(b),\\
-f(a)-\alpha a=a\left(-\alpha-v(a)\right),&\mbox{ if }\alpha\leq-v(a)\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(3.42)

or equivalently

\displaystyle h_{0}(a,b,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-b\,[v(b)-\alpha]_{-}+a\,[v(a)+\alpha]_{-},

(3.43)

where
[x]_{-}=\max\{-x,0\}
.

The maps
h^{\pm}
represent the Godunov fluxes at a vertical boundary of a cell, corresponding to the fluxes
\pm f
respectively, while
the map
h_{0}:[0,1]\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
represents the flux along the possibly non-vertical boundary cell with slope
\alpha
.

Notice that

h^{-}(a,b)=-\begin{cases}\max_{[a,b]}f,&\mbox{ if }a\leq b,\\
\min_{[b,a]}f,&\mbox{ if }a\geq b,\end{cases}

and hence
h^{-}(a,b)=-h^{+}(b,a)
.

Moreover, the maps
h^{\pm}
,
h_{0}
are Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant

Lip(h^{\pm})\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,,\qquad Lip(h_{0})\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+|\alpha|\,.

(3.44)

We stress that the numerical fluxes
h^{\pm}
,
h_{0}
are based on the exact Riemann solver at the interface. See Section
2.1
. In the following lemma, we provide some monotonicity properties of the numerical fluxes and the value of
h_{0}
when its arguments
(a,b,\alpha)

satisfy the Rankine-Hugoniot condition (
2.20
) at the flux interface.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any given
a
and
b
in
[0,1]
the following hold

(i)

h^{\pm}(a,a)=\pm f(a)
,

(ii)

h^{\pm}(a,b)
and
h_{0}(a,b,\alpha)
are nondecreasing with respect to
a
and nonincreasing with respect to
b
, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

(iii)

h_{0}(a,b,0)=0
,

(iv)

for all
\alpha
,

h_{0}(a,a,\alpha)=\begin{cases}-\alpha a+f(a),\quad&\mbox{ if }\alpha\geq v(a)\,,\\
-\alpha a-f(a),\quad&\mbox{ if }\alpha\leq-v(a)\,,\\
0,\quad&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3.45)

(v)

If

f(b)+f(a)=\alpha(b-a),

(3.46)

then

h_{0}(a,b,\alpha)=f(b)-\alpha b=-\alpha a-f(a)\,.

(3.47)

Proof.

All of the properties
(i)
–
(iv)
are direct consequences of the definitions in (
3.41
)–(
3.43
) and the fact that the map

\rho\mapsto\rho[v(\rho)+const.]_{-}
is nondecreasing, being the composition of two nonincreasing maps.

Concerning
(v)
,
by (
3.43
) and (
3.46
)

\displaystyle h_{0}(a,b,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-[\alpha b-f(b)]_{+}+[\alpha a+f(a)]_{-}

\displaystyle=-[\alpha b-f(b)]_{+}+[\alpha b-f(b)]_{-}

\displaystyle=-\alpha b+f(b),

where we used the identity
[x]_{+}-[x]_{-}=x
. Hence we get the first equality in (
3.47
);
the second one follows immediately from (
3.46
).
∎

Lemma 4.1
.

Given that the CFL condition (
3.37
) is satisfied, the functions
H^{\pm}
,
H^{AB}_{L}
and
H^{AC}_{R}
are nondecreasing with respect to their first three arguments
a,b,c
.

The functions
H^{C}_{L}
and
H^{B}_{R}

are nondecreasing with respect of their first four arguments
a,b,c,d
.

Proof.

The functions
H^{\pm}
,
H^{AB}_{L}
,
H^{AC}_{R}
,
H^{B}_{R}
, and
H^{C}_{L}
are Lipschitz continuous; therefore, we can check their monotonicity properties by computing a.e. the partial derivatives. To keep our presentation as light as possible, we provide detailed estimates only for
H^{B}_{R}
, see (
4.71
), as the other cases are very similar.

Thanks to Lemma
3.2
, the CFL condition (
3.37
) and the fact that
\mathsf{h}\leq\Delta_{R}\leq 2\mathsf{h}
, we have that

\displaystyle\partial_{a}H^{B}_{R}(a,b,c,d;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{R},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}\partial_{a}h_{0}\left(a,b,\alpha\right)\geq 0\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{b}H^{B}_{R}(a,b,c,d;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{R},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{R}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}-\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}|\partial_{b}h_{0}\left(a,b,\alpha\right)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\Delta_{R}-\mathsf{h}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}>0\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{c}H^{B}_{R}(a,b,c,d;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{R},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{h}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}-\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}\partial_{c}h^{+}\left(c,d\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathsf{h}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}>0\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{d}H^{B}_{R}(a,b,c,d;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{R},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}|\partial_{d}h^{+}\left(c,d\right)|\geq 0\,.

∎

For future use, we define the remainder terms

\mathsf{R}_{L}(b,\alpha)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\textrm{ if }\alpha\leq-v(b)\,,\\
f(b)+\alpha b,\quad&\textrm{ if }\alpha\in(-v(b),v(b))\,,\\
2f(b),\quad&\textrm{ if }\alpha\geq v(b)\,,\end{cases}

(4.78)

and
\mathsf{R}_{R}(b,\alpha):=2f(b)-\mathsf{R}_{L}(b,\alpha)
. Observe that the remainder terms are always non negative.

Let us now collect other useful properties of the maps defined at (
4.67
)–(
4.71
).

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
b\in[0,1]
we have

H^{\pm}(b,b,b;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h})=b\,,

(4.79)

\displaystyle H^{AB}_{L}(b,b,b;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\alpha)=b-\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{L}+\alpha\mathsf{k}}\mathsf{R}_{L}(b,\alpha){\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\leq b},

(4.80)

\displaystyle H^{C}_{L}(b,b,b,b;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{L},\alpha)=b-\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{L}+\mathsf{h}+\alpha\mathsf{k}}\mathsf{R}_{L}(b,\alpha),

\displaystyle H^{AC}_{R}(b,b,b;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\alpha)=b-\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{R}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}\mathsf{R}_{R}(b,\alpha),

(4.81)

\displaystyle H^{B}_{R}(b,b,b,b;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{R},\alpha)=b-\frac{\mathsf{k}}{\Delta_{R}+\mathsf{h}-\alpha\mathsf{k}}\mathsf{R}_{R}(b,\alpha).

(4.82)

Moreover, if

and

satisfy the Rankine-Hugoniot condition, namely

\displaystyle H^{AB}_{L}(b,b,c;\mathsf{k},\Delta_{L},\alpha)=H^{C}_{L}(b,b,b,c;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{L},\alpha)=b\,,

\displaystyle H^{AC}_{R}(b,c,c;\mathsf{k},\Delta_{R},\alpha)=H^{B}_{R}(b,c,c,c;\mathsf{k},\mathsf{h},\Delta_{R},\alpha)=c\,.

Proof.

These properties are direct consequences of Lemma
3.2
and of the identities

h_{0}(b,b,\alpha)-h^{-}(b,b)=-\alpha b+\mathsf{R}_{L}(b,\alpha)\,,\quad h^{+}(b,b)-h_{0}(b,b,\alpha)=-\alpha b+\mathsf{R}_{R}(b,\alpha)\,.

∎

.

(Uniform

\displaystyle Q^{\pm,n}_{j}=h^{\pm}(\rho^{n}_{j-1}\vee k,\rho^{n}_{j}\vee k)-h^{\pm}(\rho^{n}_{j-1}\wedge k,\rho^{n}_{j}\wedge k),

\displaystyle Q_{0}^{n}=h_{0}(\rho^{n}_{L}\vee k,\rho^{n}_{R}\vee k,\alpha_{n,\mathsf{k}})-h_{0}(\rho^{n}_{L}\wedge k,\rho^{n}_{R}\wedge k,\alpha_{n,\mathsf{k}})\,.

In the next lemma, we deduce entropy inequalities valid for each cell.

Lemma 4.3
.

(Discrete entropy inequalities).

Fix

,
