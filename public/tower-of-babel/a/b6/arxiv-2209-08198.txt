Grid-Free MIMO Beam Alignment through Site-Specific Deep Learning
Yuqiang Heng, and Jeffrey G. Andrews

Abstract
Beam alignment is a critical bottleneck in millimeter wave communication. An ideal beam alignment technique should achieve high beamforming gain with low latency, scale well to systems with higher carrier frequencies, larger antenna arrays and multiple user equipments, and not require hard-to-obtain context information. These qualities are collectively lacking in existing methods. We depart from the conventional codebook-based (CB) approach where the optimal beam is chosen from quantized codebooks and instead propose a grid-free beam alignment method that directly synthesizes the transmit and receive beams from the continuous search space using measurements from a few site-specific probing beams found via a deep learning pipeline. In realistic settings, the proposed method achieves a far superior signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)-latency trade-off compared to the CB baselines: it aligns near-optimal beams 100x faster or equivalently finds beams with 10-15 dB higher average SNR in the same number of searches, relative to an exhaustive search over a conventional codebook.



I
Introduction

Millimeter wave devices require highly directional beamforming to compensate for the severe isotropic path loss and to achieve viable signal strength. To reduce the cost and power consumption of a fully digital system, practical millimeter wave systems often adopt analog beams that concentrate energy in particular directions. On the other hand, the optimal choice of these narrow beams are susceptible to changes in the propagation environment such as blockage and reflection. It is critical for millimeter wave base stations and user equipments to find good beamforming directions during initial connection and then track these analog beams as the propagation conditions change, such as when a user equipment moves and rotates. As future cellular systems move to unlock larger bandwidths at higher carrier frequencies extending to the so-called “sub-terahertz” bands of up to 300 GHz, devices will adopt ever denser antenna arrays and narrower beams. As a result, beam management – the process of discovering and maintaining good analog beamforming directions – is a severe challenge that will only worsen as we move towards 6G and beyond.

I-A

Background and Related Work

Existing beam management approaches typically assume a “grid-of-beams” or codebook-based framework, where base stations and user equipments adopt codebooks of quantized beamforming directions. In order to ensure coverage in any site, the quantized beams usually distribute energy uniformly in the angular space, such as by using discrete Fourier transform codebooks. In this context, the problem of beam alignment becomes selecting the optimal beams from the finite codebooks at the base station and potentially at the user equipment. The most widely adopted method of beam selection is through a search. For instance, 5G NR adopts a beam management framework based on beam sweeping, measurement and reporting
[
2
,
3
]
. In the downlink, the base station exhaustively searches its codebook by periodically sending beamformed reference signals called Synchronization Signal Blocks. The user equipment measures and reports the received signal strength, and the base station then selects the best beam based on the user equipment’s feedback. The obvious drawback of an exhaustive search is its beam sweeping latency, which grows linearly with the total number of beam pairs. As systems move higher in frequency and adopt narrower beams, the size of the codebook increases accordingly. A terahertz system may easily adopt tens of thousands of beam pairs, rendering the the exhaustive search infeasible due to the prohibitive beam sweeping latency. A hierarchical search can reduce the latency for a single user equipment. By sweeping wider beams first and progress to narrower child beams, it iteratively reduces the search space. In the 5G NR framework, the base station can use Synchronization Signal Blocks to sweep the wide beams and use the more flexible Channel State Information Reference Signals for narrow-beam refinement. On the other hand, the radiation patterns of the wide beams need to be carefully designed since the hierarchical search is more prone to search errors caused by noisy measurements and imperfect wide-beam shapes
[
4
,
5
]
.

Another way to reduce the beam sweeping latency is to leverage context information (CI). The optimal beam pair of a link is intuitively a function of the topology of the propagation environment and the locations of the base station and the user equipment. Such spatial properties can be captured through context information such as sub-6 GHz out-of-band measurements
[
6
]
, global navigation satellite system coordinates of the user equipment
[
7
,
8
]
, radar measurements
[
9
,
10
]
, images captured by cameras and 3D point clouds of the environment
[
11
,
12
]
. By constructing a mapping from the context information to the index of the optimal beam through a lookup table or machine learning models, the search space can be substantially reduced. However, these context information-based methods are difficult to standardize and are not widely-adopted in practice due to the requirement of additional sensors and the varying hardware capabilities of millimeter wave devices. They are also not suitable for standalone systems due to the need of a robust feedback link usually occupying a lower frequency band.

Inspired by the success of deep learning in computer vision and natural language processing, recent works have explored deep learning in beam management. Neural network models can extract features from side information such as context information
[
12
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
and history measurements
[

]
to predict the optimal beam. Reinforcement learning approaches have been proposed to learn beam management policies through iterative interactions with the environment
[
14
,
15
]
. Reviews of beam management solutions based on machine learning and deep learning can be found in
[

,

]
.

I-B

Site-specific Adaptation

A promising way to reduce the beam sweeping latency is through site-specific adaptation. In scenarios where the angle-of-arrival and angle-of-departure distributions of the channel paths are highly non-uniform, such as in particular environment topologies and when there are several spatial clusters of user equipments, some beams in a uniform codebook may never get used. Leveraging this fact, the base station can compute the statistics of the entire codebook after an exhaustive training phase and prioritize the most frequently used beams
[
18
,
19
]
. By training using explicit Channel State Information
[
20
]
or through implicit rewards in a reinforcement learning framework
[
21
]
, neural networks can generate smaller codebooks from scratch that adapt to the environment and strategically direct energy towards the user equipments. The data-driven approach can also help select analog beams from standard codebooks using sensing measurements by optimizing the mapping function
[
22
]
or jointly learning the sensing beams and the mapping function
[
23
]
.

Compared to codebook-based beam management methods whose beamforming gain is limited by the resolution of the codebook, a grid-free approach extends the search space for the optimal beam to the high-dimensional continuous space and can in theory achieve better gain. For instance, maximum ratio transmission is known to be optimal under the unit power constraint and equal gain transmission under the unit modulus constraint. However, they require full Channel State Information to compute the beamforming weights, which is generally unavailable before beam alignment. Furthermore, even with Channel State Information, finding the optimal equal gain transmission and equal gain combining beams requires a grid search over possible weights in the multiple-input multiple-output setting and is computationally prohibitive
[
24
]
. Recent works have explored directly predicting the hybrid beamforming weights for the base station without full Channel State Information by learning sensing matrices
[
25
]
or a sequence of interactive sensing vectors based on feedback of previous measurements
[
26
]
.

In our previous work
[
27
]
, we proposed a codebook-based beam alignment method that uses the measurements of a few site-specific probing beams to predict the index of the optimal transmit narrow beam. However, it benefits significantly from trying a few candidate beams for each user equipment. As a result, the gain in the beam sweeping overhead diminishes with increasing number of user equipments. This is a common and important limitation of many existing methods: the hierarchical search requires searching all child beams, many context information-based models require trying the most likely candidates, and active learning-based methods require sweeping a different sequence of sensing beams for each user equipment. A beam sweeping procedure needs to be repeated for all user equipments in the cell, each of which may require a different set of beams. Hence the total beam sweeping latency increases linearly with the number of user equipments, which can be large and unknown in cellular systems. In codebook-based approaches, eventually the entire codebook will need to be searched, a major shortcoming of many of the aforementioned methods.

I-C

Contributions

In this work, we propose DL-GF, a one-shot grid-free (GF) beam alignment method based on unsupervised deep learning (DL). The proposed method learns a small number of probing beams through site-specific training and directly predicts the optimal analog beamforming vectors without using a quantized codebook. It possesses several qualities of an ideal beam alignment method, many of which are lacking in existing approaches:

•

High beamforming gain beyond standard codebooks.
The proposed method synthesizes continuous-valued analog beamforming weights. With the additional degree of freedom, the synthesized beams can achieve better beamforming gain compared to choosing optimally from large quantized codebooks.

•

Low beam sweeping latency with optimal scaling.
With site-specific training, the proposed method requires sweeping just a few probing beam pairs to capture sufficient channel information. Unlike many existing approaches that require sweeping a few candidate beams or child beams for each user equipment, the proposed method synthesizes the analog beams in one-shot. As a result, the beam sweeping latency does not increase with the number of user equipments. In our setting, DL-GF reduces the beam sweeping overhead by over 500
\times
vs. the exhaustive codebook-based method regardless of the number of user equipments.

•

Easy to adopt in cellular standards.
The proposed method does not require hard-to-obtain context information such as the location of user equipments and out-of-band measurements. Instead, it purely relies on measuring and reporting a few probing beams. The probing beams are also optimized for coverage, allowing the base station to discover new user equipments.

•

Joint transmit-receive beam alignment.
Unlike many existing methods that only consider beam alignment for the base station, the proposed method jointly predicts the analog beam for both the base station and the user equipment without any additional beam sweeping for the user equipment. The synthesized transmit and receive beam pairs are jointly optimized to achieve high beamforming gain, even when the user equipments have random orientations.

A preliminary version of this work considers transmit-only beam alignment
[
1
]
. This work extends the conference version to consider transmit and receive joint beam alignment in the multiple-input multiple-output setting, proposes a more sophisticated utility function, and presents much more comprehensive results.

System Model

A multiple-input multiple-output system is considered where the base station has an array of
N_{\textnormal{T}}
antennas, the user equipment has an array of
N_{\textnormal{R}}
antennas and both perform beam alignment. A geometric channel model with
L
paths is adopted:

\mathbf{H}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\alpha_{l}e^{j(\theta_{l}-2\pi\tau_{l}B)}\mathbf{a}_{\textnormal{R}}(\omega_{l}^{az},\omega_{l}^{el})\mathbf{a}_{\textnormal{T}}(\phi_{l}^{az},\phi_{l}^{el})^{H},

(1)

where
\mathbf{a}_{\textnormal{T}},\mathbf{a}_{\textnormal{R}}
are the transmit and receive array response vectors,
\alpha_{l},\theta_{l},\tau_{l}
are the gain, Doppler shift and delay,
\omega_{l}^{az},\omega_{l}^{el},\phi_{l}^{az},\phi_{l}^{el}
are the azimuth and elevation angle-of-arrival and angle-of-departure of path
l
.
For a uniform planar array with
N_{y}
and
N_{z}
antennas in the
y-z
plane, its array response vector is given by:

\mathbf{a}_{y}(\phi^{az},\phi^{el})=\\
\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\phi^{az}\sin\phi^{el}}&\cdots&e^{j(N_{y}-1)\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\phi^{az}\sin\phi^{el}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(2)

\mathbf{a}_{z}(\phi^{el})=\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\cos\phi^{el}}&\cdots&e^{j(N_{z}-1)\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\cos\phi^{el}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(3)

\lambda
is the carrier wavelength and
d
is the antenna spacing.

During data transmission in the downlink, if the base station adopts a transmit beam
\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{T}}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\textnormal{T}}\times 1}
and transmits a symbol
s
and the user equipment adopts a receive beam
\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{R}}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\textnormal{R}}\times 1}
, the received signal can be written as

y=\sqrt{P_{\textnormal{T}}}\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{R}}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{T}}s+\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{R}}^{H}\mathbf{n},

(4)

where
P_{\textnormal{T}}
is the transmit power and
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I})
is a complex additive white Gaussian noise. Assuming unit-power transmitted symbols, the signal-to-noise ratio achieved by the beam pair is

\textnormal{SNR}=\frac{P_{\textnormal{T}}|\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{R}}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{T}}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(5)

The base station and the user equipments are assumed to perform analog beamforming only. Each device has a single radio frequency chain connected to an array of phase shifters. Hence the beamforming vectors satisfy the unit-power, constant modulus constraint:

|[\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{T}}]_{i}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\textnormal{T}}}},i=1,\dots,N_{\textnormal{T}}\

(6)

|[\mathbf{v}_{\textnormal{R}}]_{j}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\textnormal{R}}}},j=1,\dots,N_{\textnormal{R}}.

(7)

The orientation of a user equipment is modeled with random rotations with respect to the local coordinate system which is attached to and rotates with the user equipment
[
28
]
. Each user equipment first rotates by the
z
axis, then by the new
y
axis, and finally by the new
x
axis. The angles of the three elemental rotations are modeled as random variables. The angle-of-arrival of the channel is then adjusted according to the orientation of the user equipment. A more sophisticated model may consider the shape of the user equipment and self–blockage of the device, which is left for future work.

III
The Proposed Method, Metrics and Baselines

Our objective is to directly predict optimal transmit and receive beams without searching large codebooks or candidate beams. In our previous work
[
27
]
, we demonstrated that a neural network classifier can accurately select the optimal beam index from a large codebook using measurements of a few learned site-specific probing beams, i.e., each base station learns unique probing beams that are well-suited to its propagation environment. Inspired by this idea, we propose to learn a small number of probing beam pairs at the base station and the user equipment then use their measurements to synthesize the optimal narrow beam pair from the continuous search space. The base station first sweeps
N_{\mathbf{F}}
transmit probing beams while the user equipment measures the received signal power using
N_{\mathbf{W}}
receive sensing beams. After the probing-beam sweeping phase, the user equipment uses the collected measurements as inputs to its beam synthesizer function
f_{\textnormal{R}}
to generate its receive beam. The measurements are also fed back to the base station, which uses them as inputs to its own beam synthesizer function
f_{\textnormal{T}}
to generate the transmit beam.

The proposed beam alignment procedure is illustrated in Fig.

.
During the first phase of the proposed method, the base station and the user equipments sweep a small number of probing and sensing beams to capture information about the channel.
Let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\textnormal{T}}\times N_{\mathbf{F}}},\mathbf{W}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\textnormal{R}}\times N_{\mathbf{W}}}
be matrices representing the base station probing and user equipment sensing beams, whose entries satisfy the constant modulus constraint for analog beamforming.
The base station periodically broadcasts probing symbols
\mathbf{s}_{\textnormal{probe}}
using beams in
\mathbf{F}
while each user equipment measures using the sensing beams in
\mathbf{W}
. Note that all user equipments in the cell share the same sensing beams. The periodic sweeping of the probing beams is similar to Synchronization Signal Block transmission in 5G, which also allows new user equipments to be discovered and synchronize to the base station. The total number of beams swept does not increase with the number of user equipments since multiple user equipments can measure the probing beams using shared time and frequency resources.

After beam sweeping, the composite received signal
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\mathbf{W}}\times N_{\mathbf{F}}}
consisting of the received signal of all combinations of probing and sensing beams can be written as

\mathbf{Y}=\sqrt{P_{\textnormal{T}}}\mathbf{W}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{F}\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{\textnormal{probe}})+\mathbf{W}^{H}\mathbf{N},

(8)

where
\mathbf{s}_{\textnormal{probe}}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\textnormal{F}}\times 1}
is the vector of transmitted probing symbols and
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\textnormal{R}}\times N_{\mathbf{F}}}
is the additive white Gaussian noise measurement noise matrix whose each element has noise power
\sigma_{\textnormal{probe}}^{2}
.

The probing beam pairs will likely have lower gain since there are many fewer of them to cover the angular space. To combat the low signal-to-noise ratio during the probing measurement phase, the base station can introduce redundancy in the probing symbols over time or frequency, such as by using a spreading sequence or repeating over subcarriers
[
2
,
29
]
. This is not too wasteful in the context of analog beamforming and 5G beam alignment, since a single analog beam can be transmitted at a time (such as for each Synchronization Signal Block). A spreading gain of
\beta\geq 1
is introduced to model the effective signal-to-noise ratio of the probing measurements:

\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{probe}}=\beta\frac{P_{\textnormal{T}}|\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{f}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(9)

We focus on the special case where
N_{\mathbf{F}}=N_{\mathbf{W}}=N_{\mathrm{probe}}
: the base station sweeps
N_{\mathrm{probe}}
probing beams while the user equipment uses a different receive sensing beam for each transmit probing beam so that the total number of probing beam pairs is
N_{\mathrm{probe}}
. The user equipment then reports the
N_{\mathrm{probe}}
received signal power measurements. As a result, the input feature to the beam synthesizer functions is

\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}|[\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{Y})]_{1}|^{2}&\cdots&|[\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{Y})]_{N_{\mathrm{probe}}}|^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(10)

The beam synthesizer functions
f_{\textnormal{T}},f_{\textnormal{R}}
at the base station and the user equipment then predict the transmit and receive beams for data transmission.

In principle, both the measurement and the feedback procedures are flexible and can be design choices. For example, the number of probing and sensing beams can be arbitrary. The user equipment can measure and feedback all combinations of probing and sensing beams, and the beam synthesizer functions can utilize the full measurement matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbbm{C}^{N_{\mathbf{W}}\times N_{\mathbf{F}}}
to generate the beamforming vectors. The user equipment may also feedback just
N_{\mathbf{F}}
measurements corresponding to the strongest sensing beam for each transmit probing beam. The specific design considered in this work is motivated by our wish to reduce the measurement and feedback overhead, i.e.,
N_{\mathrm{probe}}\ll N_{\mathbf{F}}N_{\mathbf{W}}
. Furthermore, it is also more compatible with the reference signal feedback procedure in 5G NR, where the user equipment beam is transparent to the base station. A comparison of different probing strategies is discussed in Sect.
V-C1
. However, there is extensive scope for future work exploring other approaches.

III-A

Problem Formulation

The parameters of the proposed method include the probing and sensing beams
\mathbf{F},\mathbf{W}
as well as the beam synthesizers
f_{\textnormal{T}},f_{\textnormal{R}}
, which need to be optimized with respect to a utility function. With the unit modulus constraint of the probing and predicted beam pairs in mind, the optimization problem can be written as:

\begin{array}[]{rrcll}\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{F},\mathbf{W},f_{\textnormal{T}},f_{\textnormal{R}}}&&\mathcal{U}&&\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{T}}&=&f_{\textnormal{T}}(\mathbf{z})&\\
&\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}&=&f_{\textnormal{R}}(\mathbf{z})&\\
&|[\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{T}}]_{i}|&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\textnormal{T}}}},&\forall i=1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{T}}\\
&|[\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}]_{i}|&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\textnormal{R}}}},&\forall i=1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{R}}\\
&|[\mathbf{F}]_{i,j}|&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\textnormal{T}}}},&\forall i=1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{T}},\\
&&&&\forall j=1,\cdots,N_{\mathbf{F}}\\
&|[\mathbf{W}]_{i,j}|&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\textnormal{R}}}},&\forall i=1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{R}},\\
&&&&\forall j=1,\cdots,N_{\mathbf{W}}.\\
\end{array}

(11)

There are several considerations when designing the utility function. The beam synthesizer functions should generate beams that tend to maximize the beamforming gain for each channel realization. The probing and sensing beams in
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{W}
serve two important purposes. First, they provide helpful information to the beam synthesizers and thus should capture characteristics of the channel. Second, they should allow the base station to discover new user equipments during the initial access process and thus should satisfy a minimum signal-to-noise ratio requirement. Since the optimal beamforming vectors can be easily computed in closed-form in the simpler multiple-input single-output or single-input multiple-output setting, the synthesized beams can be optimized to resemble the optimal equal gain transmission or equal gain combining beams by minimizing a supervised loss function such as the mean square error in
[
20
]
. However, finding the equal gain transmission and equal gain combining beam pairs in the multiple-input multiple-output setting requires solving its own non-convex optimization problem and may require a grid-search over possible weights, making a supervised utility function undesirable. To this end, we propose a two-component unsupervised utility function that does not require explicit labels for the probing or synthesized beams:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{BF}}+(1-\gamma)\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{IA}}

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{BF}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{H}}\left[\frac{|\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{T}}|^{2}}{||\mathbf{H}||^{2}_{\text{F}}}\right]

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{IA}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\textnormal{IA}}}\left[\max\limits_{i\in{1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{probe}}}}\frac{|\mathbf{w}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{f}_{i}|^{2}}{||\mathbf{H}||^{2}_{\text{F}}}\right]

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textnormal{IA}}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{H}:\max\limits_{i\in{1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{probe}}}}\frac{P_{\textnormal{T}}|\mathbf{w}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{f}_{i}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\geq\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{TH}}\}

(15)

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the set of channel realizations corresponding to possible user equipment locations in the cell. The first term
\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{BF}}
focuses on the beamforming gain of the synthesized beams
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{T}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}
and is the end-to-end objective. An obvious choice for the utility function is the average signal-to-noise ratio or achievable rate of the predicted beam pairs, such as adopted in
[
25
]
. However, it tends to emphasize user equipments with good channels more and neglect cell edge user equipments. In order to provide better coverage to all user equipments, we maximize the average beamforming gain normalized by the channel norm to give equal emphasis even for user equipments with worse channels. It also performs better than simply maximizing the average rate.

The second term
\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{IA}}
ensures the coverage of the probing beams, which are transmitted using Synchronization Signal Blocks in 5G so that a new user equipment may synchronize and connect with the base station. The strongest probing beam pair for a user equipment needs to exceed an signal-to-noise ratio threshold
\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{TH}}
for that user equipment to be discovered.
In the context of 5G, a new user equipment needs to decode the synchronization signals, Master Information Block and System Information Block in an Synchronization Signal Block, which may not enjoy the proposed spreading gain of the probing symbols.
Let
\mathbf{f}_{i}
and
\mathbf{w}_{i}
denote the
i
th probing and sensing beams, the signal-to-noise ratio for initial access is that of the strongest probing beam pair:

\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{IA}}=\max\limits_{i\in{1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{probe}}}}\frac{P_{\textnormal{T}}|\mathbf{w}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{f}_{i}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(16)

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\textnormal{IA}}
denote the set of channel realizations corresponding to user equipments that can achieve the signal-to-noise ratio threshold
\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{TH}}
for initial access with their strongest probing beam pairs. The second term
\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{IA}}
seeks to maximize the average normalized beamforming gain of the strongest probing beam pairs for those user equipments that cannot meet the minimum signal-to-noise ratio threshold
\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{TH}}
with any of the probing beam pairs. The coefficient
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a design parameter to balance the trade-off between the gain of the synthesized beams and of the probing beam pairs.

We focus on the beam alignment problem that finds analog beams for each user equipment individually. Systems that adopt hybrid beamforming can first perform beam alignment and select good analog beams, then design digital beams that optimize for inter-user interference and sum-rate based on the effective channel. Joint optimization of analog and digital beams in a multi-user equipment setting is a promising direction and is left for future work.

III-B

The Proposed NN Architecture

The optimization problem in (
11
) is difficult to solve since the unit-modulus constraints are non-convex while the functions
f_{\textnormal{T}}
and
f_{\textnormal{R}}
are generally unknown. We propose to parameterize the probing and sensing beams
\mathbf{F},\mathbf{W}
as well as the beam synthesizers
f_{\textnormal{T}},f_{\textnormal{R}}
using neural networks and optimize them in a data-driven fashion. The overall neural network architecture is illustrated in Fig.
2
. During the offline training phase, the input to the entire neural network model are the channel matrices. The transmit probing and receive sensing beams are implemented in the complex neural network module as two complex matrices. The entire complex neural network module essential computes complex-valued matrix multiplication and addition on the channel matrix. To enforce the unit-modulus constraint, the complex matrices are normalized element-wise by the magnitude. We find this implementation to perform better empirically compared to the implementation used in our previous work
[
27
]
, where the complex matrices are computed using the phase-shift values. The complex neural network module computes the composite matrix of received signals of all combinations of probing and sensing beams in (
8
) and can be considered to perform virtual sweeping of the probing beam pairs while being differentiable.
The power of the
N_{\textnormal{probe}}
probing beam pairs corresponding to the diagonal elements are extracted and fed into the transmit and receive beam synthesizer functions, each parameterized using an multilayer perceptron. The multilayer perceptron consists of 2 hidden layers with rectified linear unit activation and a final linear layer outputting the real and imaginary parts of the synthesized beamforming vector. The final predicted beams are normalized element-wise to enforce the unit-modulus constraint. With each batch of training channel realizations, the utility can be computed using the beamforming gain of the synthesized beams and that of the best probing beam pairs. The probing and sensing beams as well as the beam synthesizer functions are trained through stochastic gradient descent and backpropagation.

III-C

Practicality of the Proposed Method

The neural network model is trained offline prior to deployment. The training data can be obtained through ray-tracing simulations or from measurements. Due to the difficulty of obtaining Channel State Information in real time, online training and adaptation of the neural network remain an open research problem. On the other hand, the model proves to be fairly robust against imperfect training data, as we will show in Section
V-D2
. After the offline training phase, the probing beam pairs are extracted from the complex neural network module and implemented in radio frequency. The beam synthesizers
f_{\mathrm{T}},f_{\mathrm{R}}
are implemented at the base station and the user equipment respectively. During deployment, the base station and the user equipment periodically sweep the probing beam pairs while the user equipment measures the received signal power. The probing measurements are then reported to the base station. The base station and the user equipment use the probing measurements as inputs to
f_{\mathrm{T}}
and
f_{\mathrm{R}}
to synthesize the transmit and receive beams. Since the probing beams and the beam synthesizers are site-specific,
\mathbf{W}
and
f_{\mathrm{R}}
need to be transmitted to the user equipments. In a non-standalone system, this can be done through a lower-frequency side link. A new user equipment can then complete initial access using the strongest probing beam pair. We have previously shown that the transmit probing beams can be used for initial access in the multiple-input single-output setting
[
1
]
. Therefore in a standalone system, the base station may sweep the transmit probing beams while a new user equipment receives using a quasi-omnidirectional beam. The user equipment can complete initial access with the strongest transmit probing beam, download the receive components
\mathbf{W}
and
f_{\mathrm{R}}
, then perform the full joint transmit-receive beam alignment.

III-D

Baselines and Metrics

The proposed method optimizes the grid-free beam synthesizers and the probing beam pairs through deep learning, hence is referred to as the DL-GF method. It will be compared with five baselines: the deep learning-codebook-based method, the exhaustive search, the genie with DFT codebooks, the DFT+equal gain combining method, and maximum ratio transmission+maximum ratio combining. The same probing measurement spreading gain of
\beta
is adopted in DL-GF, DL-CB and the exhaustive search for a fair comparison.

•

DL-CB.
The DL-CB method replaces the transmit and receive beam synthesizers with two classifiers that predict the optimal beam indices in the base station and user equipment codebooks. It is an extension of our previous work
[
27
]
to the multiple-input multiple-output scenario. To improve its accuracy, the DL-CB method can sweep the top few candidate beam pairs predicted by the classifiers. In our experiment, the best beam pair is selected after trying all combinations of the top-3 predicted transmit and receive beams.

•

Exhaustive search.
The exhaustive search sweeps and measures all combinations of beam pairs in the base station and user equipment codebooks and selects the beam pair with the highest received signal power. Since the measurements are corrupted by noise, the beam pair selected by the exhaustive search may not be the one maximizing signal-to-noise ratio.

•

Genie DFT.
A genie always selects the optimal beam pair in the base station and user equipment codebooks. It is the same as the exhaustive search when the measurement noise power is zero.

•

DFT+EGC.
Only the base station has a codebook in the DFT+EGC baseline. The base station exhaustively tries all beams in its codebook while the user equipment uses the corresponding equal gain combining vector for each transmit beam. The best beam pair is selected assuming no measurement noise. It is expected to perform better than the genie DFT baseline due to the additional degree of freedom at the user equipment.

•

MRT+MRC.
Neither the base station nor the user equipment uses a codebook. The base station uses the optimal maximum ratio transmission beam while the user equipment uses the maximum ratio combining beam. The beamforming gain can be computed through an eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{H}^{H}\mathbf{H}
. The MRT+MRC baseline is the theoretical upper bound under the unit-power constraint and cannot be achieved under the stricter unit-modulus constraint.

One important performance metric for beam alignment is the signal-to-noise ratio achieved by the selected beams. We will compare the average signal-to-noise ratio as well as the signal-to-noise ratio distribution across different baselines. Secondly, new user equipments need to satisfy a minimum signal-to-noise ratio requirement so that they can be discovered during the initial access process and connect to the base station.
This is generally not a concern for codebook-based approaches that adopt uniform codebooks, but may be problematic when the probing beams are site-specific and have severe coverage holes. Therefore, we will also investigate the misdetection probability of user equipments, which is the probability that a user equipment achieves below-threshold signal-to-noise ratio with the strongest probing beam pair, formally defined as

P\Bigl(\max\limits_{i\in{1,\cdots,N_{\textnormal{probe}}}}\frac{P_{\textnormal{T}}|\mathbf{w}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{f}_{i}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}<\textnormal{SNR}_{\textnormal{TH}}\Bigr).

(17)

The signal-to-noise ratio threshold is chosen to be -5 dB
[
30
]
.

The signal-to-noise ratio vs. Beam Alignment Speed Trade-off

Conventional codebook-based beam alignment approaches can always find better beams more frequently by trying more candidates and increasing the resolution of the codebook. Similarly, the proposed deep learning-grid-free method can achieve better signal-to-noise ratio by sweeping more probing beams. However, such signal-to-noise ratio gain usually occurs at the cost of higher latency. In practical cellular systems with multiple user equipments, the beam alignment procedure may need to be performed for each user equipment. It is therefore important for operators to consider the trade-off between the signal-to-noise ratio and the overall beam alignment speed, which we define as the reciprocal of the total number of beams swept for all user equipments.
The average signal-to-noise ratio achieved at each beam alignment speed for deep learning-GF, deep learning-codebook-based and the exhaustive search is shown in Fig.
7
. Compared to the exhaustive search, the proposed deep learning-grid-free method is better by 5 to 10 dB in terms of the average signal-to-noise ratio at a given speed or faster by around two orders of magnitude when the achieved average signal-to-noise ratio is from 10 to 15 dB. The proposed grid-free approach is also strictly better than the codebook-based method, achieving 1-2.5 dB higher signal-to-noise ratio at each beam alignment speed. With multiple user equipments, other beam alignment methods that require an additional beam sweeping phase for each user equipment are expected to achieve a similar signal-to-noise ratio-speed trade-off as the deep learning-codebook-based with top-
k
search does. Since deep learning-grid-free is faster than deep learning-codebook-based with top-3 search by an order of magnitude with 10 or 20 user equipments, similar gains can be expected over methods such as the hierarchical search and active learning approaches. Interestingly, increasing the number of probing beams is often more efficient than searching more candidates for the deep learning-codebook-based method, particularly when a high beam alignment speed is required.

V-C

Validating the Proposed Design

V-C
1
Benefits of Site-specific Probing

The learned probing beam pairs provide important information about the channel for the downstream neural network beam synthesizers. To verify the benefits of site-specific probing, the learned probing and sensing beams are replaced with evenly spaced pencil beams from sub-sampled discrete Fourier transform codebooks. Compared to learning site-specific probing beam pairs, the average signal-to-noise ratio drops by over 10 dB when both the base station and the user equipment adopt discrete Fourier transform beams as shown in Fig.
10
. Note that in this setting, the user equipment measures and reports all combinations of the probing and sensing beams so that the total number of probing beam pairs is
N_{\textnormal{probe}}^{2}
. If the base station learns site-specific probing beams while the user equipment adopts discrete Fourier transform sensing beams, there is still a 2 dB loss in the average signal-to-noise ratio. Therefore, learning site-specific probing and sensing beams for the base station and the user equipment can provide significant gain.

The proposed method learns a user equipment beam for each base station probing beam. An alternative strategy is to measure all combinations of probing and sensing beams and feed back all the measurements, which requires a total number of
N_{\textnormal{probe}}^{2}
probing beam pairs instead of
N_{\textnormal{probe}}
in the proposed design. As shown in Fig.
10
, this approach is consistently worse than the proposed design, particularly when the number of probing beam pairs is small. Feeding back only the maximum receive measurement for each base station probing beam results in little further performance degradation when using more than 25 probing beam pairs. This indicates that most of the sensing measurements for each probing beam provide little additional information about the channel. Overall, the proposed probing strategy can capture information about the channel more efficiently with a limited number of probing measurements.

Verifying the Beam Synthesizer Architecture

The neural network beam synthesizers use a fully connected multilayer perceptron architecture due to the relatively small feature dimension. For comparison, an alternative beam synthesizer is designed with 3 one-dimensional convolutional layers followed by max pooling with a stride of 2 and a kernel size of 2, flattening, and a final fully connected output layer. Each convolutional layer has 64 channels, a kernel size of 3 and rectified linear unit activation. As shown in Fig.
10
, the proposed design consistently achieves better average signal-to-noise ratio than the convolutional neural network-based design does, indicating that the multilayer perceptron is an appropriate architecture in this setting.

V-C
3
Reducing the Misdetection Probability

The ideal beam alignment solution should not only achieve high beamforming gain for connected user equipments, but also be able to discover new user equipments and allow them to complete initial access. With the proposed DL-GF method, a new user equipment can do so with one of the probing beam pairs if it satisfies a minimum signal-to-noise ratio requirement. Naturally, adopting more probing beams will allow each to cover a smaller angular space, thus increasing the gain of the probing beams and reducing the misdetection probability. By explicitly incorporating the initial access performance in the utility function, we provide another tuning nob to reduce the misdetection probability.
The proposed utility function can indeed further reduce the misdetection probability when the number of probing beam pairs is fixed, as shown in Fig.
11
. Compared to simply optimizing the beamforming gain of the synthesized beams (
\gamma=1.0
), incorporating the initial access performance (
\gamma=0.3
) reduces the misdetection probability by 6.3 percentage points with 8 probing beam pairs and by 3.2 percentage points with 12 probing beam pairs while suffering from little signal-to-noise ratio loss. Although the gain diminishes with more probing beams, the two-component utility function still provides a powerful tool to improve the initial access coverage when the number of probing beams is limited. Since the
\mathcal{U}_{\textnormal{IA}}
term only covers user equipments below a specified signal-to-noise ratio threshold, the proposed utility function also allows the neural network model to adapt to different signal-to-noise ratio requirements for initial access. As shown in Fig.
12

V-D

Robustness of DL-GF

V-D
1
Robustness to Noisy Measurements

The beam synthesizers rely on the probing measurements to generate the beamforming weights. While measurement noise can cause search errors in exhaustive and hierarchical searches, it can also impact the prediction of the neural network models. A comparison of the average signal-to-noise ratio achieved by the predicted beams with increasing probing measurement spreading gain is shown in Fig.
13
. Both deep learning-based methods are trained at each probing measurement spreading gain level. As expected, the performance of the DL-GF, DL-CB and exhaustive search improves with increasing measurement spreading gain. The exhaustive search benefits less: its performance plateaus with almost no measurement spreading gain. On the other hand, the proposed DL-GF and the DL-CB methods are less robust to measurement noise due to their probing beams having lower gain. They both require a spreading gain of around 12 dB in the probing measurements to achieve their best performance. To further investigate the impact of noise, negative spreading gain is considered, which implies a setting where the measurements are noisier during probing than in data transmission. Interestingly, the average signal-to-noise ratio of the exhaustive search drops sharply and becomes the worst when the measurements are extremely noisy. At this point, the exhaustive search essentially produces random guesses since the beam measurements are dominated by noise. On the other hand, the deep learning-based methods converge to always predict the same beam pair. In the case of DL-CB, the predicted beam pair happens to be the most frequent optimal beam pair.

Robustness to Imperfect Training Data

Training deep learning models typically requires high-quality data. While ray-tracing is one of the most accurate ways to simulate millimeter wave channels, it is not easily scalable to a city-wide deployment and the generated data may still differ from actual channels due to mismatched environments and imperfect propagation models. If the training data is acquired through measurement campaigns, noisy measurement and channel estimation errors would also affect the quality of the data. It is therefore important to investigate the performance of the proposed method when there is a mismatch between the channels used for training and the actual channels during deployment. To simulate imperfect training data, the training channel matrices are corrupted with additive white Gaussian noise while the model is tested on clean channel data. The average signal-to-noise ratio of the proposed DL-GF method, the DL-CB baseline and the exhaustive search with increasing normalized mean square error in the training data is shown in Fig.
14
. Both the DL-GF and the DL-CB methods are relatively robust to noisy training data, experiencing little performance degradation when the channel normalized mean square error is smaller than -3 dB. A small amount of noisy in the training data actually benefits the proposed DL-GF method, which is a common phenomenon in machine learning where noisy training data can sometimes improve the robustness and generalizability of machine learning models
[
34
]
. Even with a channel normalized mean square error of 1 dB, the proposed DL-GF method can still outperform the exhaustive search and the DL-CB baseline trained on clean data. The added noise likely does not fundamentally shift the distribution of channels but instead makes it more “fuzzy”. During the unsupervised training procedure, the proposed neural networks still learn to beamform on these noisy channels and generalize well to the clean actual channels.

VI
Conclusion

The proposed grid-free approach provides a promising new paradigm for millimeter wave beam alignment by achieving better signal-to-noise ratio while being faster than existing methods by orders of magnitude. Its gain can be attributed to the combination of site-specific adaptation and the joint optimization of channel probing and beam synthesis.
Next-generation cellular systems can reap the benefits without overhauling the existing beam sweeping-based framework: the probing beams can be transmitted using periodic reference signals while traditional beamforming codebooks are replaced with neural network beam synthesizers. Operators can select the number of probing beam pairs based on the signal-to-noise ratio and initial access coverage requirement for each site.

There are many possible directions that this line of research could be extended, many of which have been identified throughout the paper. For example, scaling the site-specific training to city-wide networks and online adaptation to dynamic environments remain open research problems.
Multi-beam and multi-user equipment communication including the joint optimization of analog and digital beamforming is another promising direction.
The beam alignment problem has mostly been investigated to date from the BS point of view. It is important to also consider the unique beam alignment challenges on the user equipment side arising from multiple antenna panels, precarious dynamic rotations and more demanding power management.
