Self-Evaluation as a Defense Against Adversarial Attacks on LLMs
Hannah Brown *, Leon Lin *, Kenji Kawaguchi, Michael Shieh

Abstract
We introduce a defense against adversarial attacks on LLMs utilizing self-evaluation. Our method requires no model fine-tuning, instead using pre-trained models to evaluate the inputs and outputs of a generator model, significantly reducing the cost of implementation in comparison to other, finetuning-based methods. Our method can significantly reduce the attack success rate of attacks on both open and closed-source LLMs, beyond the reductions demonstrated by Llama-Guard2 and commonly used content moderation APIs. We present an analysis of the effectiveness of our method, including attempts to attack the evaluator in various settings, demonstrating that it is also more resilient to attacks than existing methods.

Introduction

Ensuring the safety of LLM outputs is becoming an increasing concern. Though models are trained to output helpful, harmless, and honest outputs through methods such as RLHF, and guardrails have been proposed for inference time
Team (2024)
;
Rebedea et al. (2023)

;

, in addition to existing methods for detecting harmful outputs

;
Hu et al. (2024)

Threat Model

We consider a setting in which an LLM
G
receives a potentially adversarial query
X
and is tasked with generating a safe response
Y
.
Y
should satisfactorily, truthfully, and safely respond to
X
. This means that if
X
is unsafe (e.g., how to commit a crime),
Y
should be a refusal to answer.

We assume that an adversary has query-only access to
G
, with an unlimited number of queries allowed. This allows them to observe model responses and perturb their input accordingly, but not access the weights of
G
. In this setting, a desirable defense achieves both high utility and high safety. A trivial defense, simply refusing to reply to all
X
, satisfies safety perfectly, but results in a useless
G
. An ideal defense responds well to all safe inputs and refuses all unsafe ones.

2.1
Attack

As demonstrated by
Zou et al.
, it is possible to find a suffix
S
that, when appended to
X
, results in
G
generating unsafe outputs. These suffixes have shown to be transferable across different model architectures and work across model inputs. We assume that
X
may contain an adversarial suffix
S_{G}
. We refer to this attack as the
generator-only attack
to differentiate it from later attacks.

2.2
Evaluation

We evaluate samples under the assumption that the evaluator is part of a pipeline. Here, it is reasonable to consider the ASR for the combination of the generator and the evaluator rather than only one component. For the input-only setting, we consider all samples from AdvBench harmful and all GPT-4 generated benign inputs to be safe, while for the output-only and input-output settings, we consider a sample harmful if the model input component is harmful. We evaluate this using the same evaluation criteria as the GCG paper. If the model output contains no refusal words, it’s considered harmful. A high ASR in this setting means that both the generator and evaluator are performing poorly at guarding against unsafe outputs. The reduction between the ASR against only the generator and the combined ASR shows the contribution of the evaluator. Attacking the Evaluator

Figure 6:
Overview of the direct attack. The adversary appends a suffix trained to attack both the evaluator and generator to their input, causing the generator to output a harmful response and the evaluator to misclassify it.

As both defenses presented depend on another LLM, a natural question is how susceptible the defenses themselves are to adversarial attacks. Is it possible to attack both
E
and
G
at the same time?

Input-only seems particularly susceptible to attack, as
E
and
G
receive the same input, allowing a suffix to be appended to an ideal location to attack both
Zou et al. (2023)
. We propose an attack that we will call the
direct attack
, in which we train a suffix to attack both the evaluator and generator as shown in
Fig.

6
. We demonstrate that, while it is possible to successfully attack input-only and input-output defenses using direct attack, the ASR of a model defended with
E_{I}
is still lower than that of an undefended model.

4.1
Attack Formulation

As
Y
is not directly controlled by the adversary, we are only able to consider the input-only and input-output settings for this attack. Rather than inserting suffix
S_{G}
which attacks only the generator, we assume that the adversary inserts
S_{GE}
which can attack both the generator and evaluator. Attacking the evaluator is slightly different from attacking the generator, in that the evaluator may fail in two ways: either by classifying unsafe inputs as safe (reducing the safety) or safe inputs as unsafe (reducing the utility). We consider both objectives, but primarily focus on the first, as it poses a higher risk. We assume that the adversary has query-only access to the combination of
G
and
E
; that is, it can observe
G
’s outputs when
E
marks them as safe, and can always infer
E
’s classifications.

4.2
Finding an Attack Suffix

We test a variety of methods to obtain
S_{GE}
. First, concatenation, in which we let
S_{GE}=S_{G}+S_{E}
, where
S_{G}
and
S_{E}
are fine-tuned as separate suffixes to attack the generator and evaluator and concatenated. Second, optimization in turn, where we use one suffix as
S_{GE}
and optimize it for the generator and evaluator objectives in turn, until the optimal balance between both is achieved. Finally, simultaneous optimization, where we use one suffix as
S_{GE}
and optimize it for
G
and
E
simultaneously by using the loss
L=\alpha L_{G}+\beta L_{E}
, where
L_{G}
and
L_{E}
are the losses for generator and evaluator respectively and

and
\beta
the weights on each.

6
Discussion

Existing defenses such as Llama-Guard2 exhibit more variation in their classifications and are sensitive to changes in suffixes that are not trained to target the evaluator, even in the simplest setting of input-only. In contrast, all evaluators we test can consistently classify attacked inputs as harmful regardless of the suffix appended.

Our results also provide more information about how adversarial suffixes bypass model alignment.
While adversarial suffixes can bypass generator alignment, they do not compromise the same model’s ability to classify input as harmful, which would represent a far stronger attack.

Our attacks demonstrate that, while it is possible to attack our defense, it does not make models more susceptible to attack; even when attacked, self-evaluation provides a higher level of defense than an undefended generator receives. Additionally, when compared to other defense methods, our ASR increases less when attacked through these methods, indicating that self-evaluation is a more robust defense method than existing methods.

LLM Defenses

Llama-Guard

and subsequently Llama-Guard 2

, and reasoning

Concurrent work

8
Conclusion

We demonstrate that pre-trained LLMs can identify attacked inputs and outputs with high accuracy, allowing the use of self-evaluation as a powerful and easy-to-implement defense against adversarial attacks. Though attacks against this defense exist, and stronger attacks may be found, we demonstrate that self-evaluation remains the current strongest defense against unsafe inputs, even when attacked, does not reduce model performance, and does not increase model susceptibility to attack.

Whereas existing defenses such as Llama-Guard and defense APIs fail when tasked with classifying samples with adversarial suffixes added, self-evaluation remains robust. The ease of implementation, potential for using small, low-cost models, and strength of defense offered make self-evaluation defense a valuable contribution to LLM safety, robustness, and alignment.

9
Limitations

We acknowledge that the attacks we propose in this paper are not the strongest possible attacks against this defense. They are not meant to be. Rather, they are meant to act as baseline demonstrations that our defense does not weaken model defenses further.

In this paper, we focus on adversarial suffixes as attacks. Future work considering other attacks would represent valuable contributions to the area. In addition, while we show the efficacy of self-evaluation as a defense, this exposes the fact that adversarial suffixes do not confuse models’ ability to classify harmful text, but simply cause them to generate it. This encourages future research into the mechanisms that allow adversarial suffixes to work, which could lead to a better understanding of LLMs in general and to the general area of LLM safety and robustness. However, both are out of the scope of this paper.

Following the Bender Rule
Bender (2011)
, we acknowledge that in this paper, we examine only English language inputs and outputs. As such, we cannot guarantee the effectiveness of our method for other languages. However, the results we demonstrate are promising, and we encourage future research verifying self-evaluation as a defense for other languages as well.

10
Ethical Considerations

This paper presents examples of harmful outputs which may be harmful to users. We have minimized the amount of harmful text shown in the main paper to what we believe is strictly necessary to understand our defense. However, for the sake of reproducibility, and a better understanding of our results, we do include examples in our appendix (
Appendices

F
and
E
) and in our supplemental material.

In addition, we demonstrate new methods of attacking models, potentially introducing ways for adversaries to attack models. However, we use these attacks as a method to demonstrate the efficacy of our defense and do not believe that they represent attacks a real-world adversary would not attempt given our defense.

For both issues, we believe the benefits of presenting this defense outweigh the possible harms.

Appendix B
Direct Attack Training and Evaluation

Here we provide full details on the training for the direct attack along with observations from training and evaluation.

B.1
Training the Direct Attack

To train suffixes for the direct attack, we select 100 harmful instructions from AdvBench to train universal suffixes to attack
E
using the GCG algorithm. We utilize a single A100-40G GPU, and initialize the suffix with ’!!!’. The batch size is 128, and the top-k parameter is 256. For the simultaneous attack, we applied a 3:1 weight ratio between generation and evaluation objectives. We evaluate on the same set of 100 samples (distinct from the training data) used to evaluate the generator-only attack.

B.2
Training Dynamics for Direct Attack

When optimizing objectives in turn, we observe that, while the ASR against both the generator and evaluator increase steadily throughout training, the evaluator is significantly easier to attack, with its ASR quickly reaching its maximum value of roughly
25
% and staying relatively steady over training. As shown in
Fig.

8
, neither the generator nor evaluator ASR is significantly impacted by switching objectives.
