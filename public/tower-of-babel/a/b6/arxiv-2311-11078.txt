Abstract
. The Monster Lie algebra 𝔪 {\mathfrak{m}} , which admits an action of the Monster finite simple group 𝕄 \mathbb{M} , was introduced by Borcherds as part of his work on the Conway–Norton Monstrous Moonshine conjecture. Here we construct an analog G ⁡ ( 𝔪 ) G(\mathfrak{m}) of a Lie group or Kac–Moody group, associated to 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} . The group G ⁡ ( 𝔪 ) G(\mathfrak{m}) is given by generators and relations, analogous to a construction of a Kac–Moody group given by Tits. In the absence of local nilpotence of the adjoint representation of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} , we introduce the notion of pro-summability of an infinite sum of operators. We use this to construct a complete pro-unipotent group U ^ + {\widehat{U}^{+}} of automorphisms of a completion 𝔪 ^ = 𝔫 − ⊕ 𝔥 ⊕ 𝔫 ^ + \widehat{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\ \oplus\ \mathfrak{h}\ \oplus\ \widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+} of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} , where 𝔫 ^ + \widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+} is the formal product of the positive root spaces of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} . The elements of U ^ + {\widehat{U}^{+}} are pro-summable infinite series with constant term 1. The group U ^ + {\widehat{U}^{+}} has a subgroup U ^ {im} + {\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}} , which is an analog of a complete unipotent group corresponding to the positive imaginary roots of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} .We construct analogs Exp : 𝔫 ^ + → U ^ + \Exp:\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}\to{\widehat{U}^{+}} and Ad : U ^ + → Aut ⁡ ( 𝔫 ^ + ) {\Ad}:{\widehat{U}^{+}}\to\Aut(\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}) of the classical exponential map and adjoint representation. We show that the action of 𝕄 {\mathbb{M}} on 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} induces an action of 𝕄 {\mathbb{M}} on 𝔪 ^ \widehat{\mathfrak{m}} , and that this in turn induces an action of 𝕄 {\mathbb{M}} on U ^ + {\widehat{U}^{+}} . We also show that the action of 𝕄 {\mathbb{M}} on 𝔫 ^ + \widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+} is compatible with the action of U ^ + \widehat{U}^{+} on 𝔫 ^ + \widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+} . Although the group G ⁡ ( 𝔪 ) G(\mathfrak{m}) is not a group of automorphisms, it contains the analog of a unipotent subgroup U + U^{+} , which acts as automorphisms of 𝔪 ^ \widehat{\mathfrak{m}} . Although G ⁡ ( 𝔪 ) G({\mathfrak{m}}) is not an automorphism group, we connect subgroups of G ⁡ ( 𝔪 ) G({\mathfrak{m}}) with groups of automorphisms of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} , of certain 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 2 \gl_{2} subalgebras of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} , of the completion 𝔪 ^ \widehat{\mathfrak{m}} , and of similar completions of 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} .



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{M}
be the Monster finite simple group and let
V^{\natural}
be the Moonshine module of Frenkel, Lepowsky and Meurman
[
FLM88
]
, a graded vertex operator algebra with
\Aut(V^{\natural})=\mathbb{M}
. With the construction of
V^{\natural}
, Frenkel, Lepowsky and Meurman proved the McKay–Thompson conjecture
[
Tho79
]
, that there exists an infinite dimensional
\mathbb{M}
-module whose graded dimension is the normalized elliptic modular invariant
J(q)
. In their seminal work
[
FLM88
]
, they also partially settled the Conway–Norton Monstrous Moonshine conjecture
[
CN79
]
by proving it for a subgroup
C
of
\mathbb{M}
which is an extension of the Conway group Co
1
by a certain extraspecial 2-group.

The
Monster Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{m}}
, which also admits an action of the Monster finite simple group
\mathbb{M}
, was constructed by Borcherds
[
Bor86
]
as a quotient of the physical space of the vertex algebra
V=V^{\natural}\otimes V_{{1,1}}
, where
V_{{1,1}}
is the vertex algebra for the even unimodular 2-dimensional Lorentzian lattice
\text{II}_{1,1}
. Borcherds showed that the Monster Lie algebra is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}(A)/\mathfrak{z},
where
\mathfrak{g}(A)
is the infinite dimensional Lie algebra associated with the Borcherds Cartan matrix
A
, and
\mathfrak{z}
is the center of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
(Section
2
). Using
\mathfrak{m}
and the No-ghost Theorem from string theory, Borcherds proved the remaining cases of the Conway–Norton conjecture. The Monster Lie algebra was one of the first examples of a new type of Lie algebra introduced by Borcherds, now known as a Borcherds algebra or generalized Kac–Moody algebra.

In addition to the profound mathematical importance of
\mathfrak{m}
, the appearance of
\mathfrak{m}
and other Borcherds algebras as symmetries in Heterotic string theory has been noted. In particular,
Harvey and Moore
[
HM96
]
showed that Borcherds algebras appear as gauge symmetry algebras of a compactification of the Heterotic string to two dimensions.
More recently, Paquette, Persson and Volpato
[
PPV16
]
showed that the Monster Lie algebra is an algebra of spontaneously broken gauge symmetries in their model of the compactified Heterotic string.

However, before this work, there was no construction of an analog of a reductive Lie group for infinite dimensional Borcherds algebras (see
[
Bor99
]
for the construction of an integral form of the universal enveloping algebra and formal group for the fake Monster Lie algebra).
Here we address this question of associating an analog of a Lie group to the Monster Lie algebra.

There are certain difficulties with constructing groups for Borcherds algebras in general. A fundamental difference between Kac–Moody algebras and Borcherds algebras is that in the Kac–Moody case, the simple roots are all real roots (have positive norm squared).
Hence the entire algebra is generated by real root vectors (those associated to the simple root vectors and their negatives).
For Borcherds algebras this is no longer the case, as there are simple roots which are imaginary (have non-positive squared norm).

For a Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, one way to construct an associated Kac–Moody group is to consider the group generated by certain automorphisms of an integrable representation of
\mathfrak{g}
, such as an integrable highest weight or adjoint representation. The root vectors associated with real roots of a Kac–Moody or Borcherds algebra are locally nilpotent with respect to their action on an integrable module. Thus, when considering the adjoint representation, there are automorphisms of the form
\exp(u\,\ad(e_{\alpha}))
for
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
e_{\alpha}
a root vector corresponding to a real root
\alpha
. This provides an infinite dimensional Kac–Moody analog of Chevalley groups for finite dimensional semisimple Lie algebras and is a straightforward way to construct a Kac–Moody group. However, this method breaks down for Borcherds algebras, since root vectors corresponding to imaginary simple roots do not act locally nilpotently on the adjoint representation, or on faithful highest weight modules of interest. In fact, the adjoint representation of the Monster Lie algebra is not integrable
[
Jur96
]
. Hence elements of the form
\exp(u\,\ad(e_{\alpha}))
no longer represent well-defined automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{m}}
.

Our approach to overcoming these obstacles is two-fold. Our first group construction is associated with a completion
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
of
\mathfrak{m}
. We start with the triangular decomposition

\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n^{+}}
,
where
\Delta_{\pm}
denotes the set of positive (respectively negative) roots,
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
is the root space associated to
\alpha
,
and
\mathfrak{n}^{\pm}=\oplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{\pm}}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
.
We obtain the formal completion of
\mathfrak{n}^{+}
by replacing this direct sum with a direct product

{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
, that is, by allowing elements with infinitely many nonzero components.
We define the completion of
\mathfrak{m}
as

\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{n}^{-}}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}.

In order to construct a complete pro-unipotent group

{\widehat{U}^{+}}\leq\Aut(\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+})\leq\Aut(\widehat{\mathfrak{m}})
(Section
5
, see also
[
CJM22
]
),
we introduce the notion of
pro-summability
, which allows us to define exponentials without the local nilpotence condition (Subsection
3.1
and
[
CJM22
]
).

We also construct analogs of the exponential map

\Exp:\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}\to{\widehat{U}^{+}}

and the adjoint representation

{\Ad}:{\widehat{U}^{+}}\to\Aut(\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+})
and show that they satisfy the familiar relationship
\Exp\left(\Ad(g)\right)(x)=g\Exp(x)g^{-1}
(Section
5.2
).

We show that the action of
{\mathbb{M}}
on
\mathfrak{m}
induces an action of
{\mathbb{M}}
on
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
, and that this in turn induces an action of
{\mathbb{M}}
on
{\widehat{U}^{+}}
(Theorem
6.1
). We show that the action of
{\mathbb{M}}
on
\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
is compatible with the action of
\widehat{U}^{+}
on
\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
. In fact, these actions also commute when extended to the parabolic subalgebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}^{+}
and parabolic group
\widehat{P}^{+}
containing
\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
and
\widehat{U}^{+}
respectively (Corollary
6.2
).

Our second approach is to construct a group
G(\mathfrak{m})
given by generators and relations. This is an analog for
\mathfrak{m}
of the group presentation for a symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra given by Tits (as in
[
Tit87
]
). The Monster Lie algebra
\mathfrak{m}
is symmetrizable since the Borcherds generalized Cartan matrix for
\mathfrak{m}
is symmetric. However, our approach is fundamentally different to that of
[
Tit87
]
, as we include explicit generators for imaginary simple roots.

We utilize the fact that
\mathfrak{m}
is generated by
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
subalgebras corresponding to both real and imaginary simple root vectors.
The group
{G}(\mathfrak{m})
is generated by a subgroup
\GL_{2}(-1)
corresponding to the unique real positive simple root
(-1,1)
, and an infinite family of subgroups
\GL_{2}(\ell,j,k)
corresponding to imaginary simple roots
(\ell+1,j-\ell)\in\text{II}_{1,1}
for
j\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq c(j)
, where
c(j)
is the coefficient of
q^{j}
in the normalized elliptic modular function
J(q)
. We determine the relations of
G(\mathfrak{m})
by analyzing the corresponding
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
subalgebras of
\mathfrak{m}
(see Section
2.7
).

Since the adjoint representation of the Monster Lie algebra is not integrable, it is not possible to construct all the group elements of
G(\mathfrak{m})
as automorphisms of
\mathfrak{m}
.
However, we show that the
\GL_{2}
subgroups of
G(\mathfrak{m})
corresponding to both real and imaginary roots of

\mathfrak{m}
are automorphism groups of the corresponding
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
subalgebras of
\mathfrak{m}
(Subsections
4.2
and
4.3
).

In the Kac–Moody case, it suffices to consider the group generated by root groups corresponding to real roots. We must consider roots that are not real in order to get a group
G(\mathfrak{m})
that reflects the full structure of
\mathfrak{m}
. For example, if we consider the group generated only by locally
\ad
-nilpotent elements of
\mathfrak{m}
, this would give
\GL_{2}
as an analog of a Kac–Moody group associated to
\mathfrak{m}
(see also
[
Kum22
]
).
In Section
7
, we show that the relations in the definition of
G(\mathfrak{m})
hold in automorphism groups of Lie algebras related to
\mathfrak{m}
, such as
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
subalgebras of
\mathfrak{m}
and completions of
\mathfrak{m}
relative to positive root systems.

Our two main group constructions are interconnected. The structure of
{\widehat{U}^{+}}
gives rise to relations in the group
G(\mathfrak{m})
. Conversely, the subgroup
U^{+}
of
G(\mathfrak{m})
whose generators correspond to all positive imaginary roots embeds as a dense subgroup of
{\widehat{U}^{+}}

[
ACJM23b
]
. This shows that, although the group
G(\mathfrak{m})
is not a group of automorphisms, the subgroup
U^{+}
of
G(\mathfrak{m})
acts as automorphisms of
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
.

The group
G(\mathfrak{m})
is a non-trivial amalgam of automorphism groups of Lie algebras related to
\mathfrak{m}
, as we prove in
[
ACJM23b
]
.
For a more general Borcherds algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, whose non-trivial highest weight and adjoint representations are not integrable, we expect that it is not possible in general to construct a Lie group analog for the whole Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
that acts as automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
. However, as we show in
[
ACJM23a
]
, the construction of a pro-summable complete pro-unipotent group
{\widehat{U}^{+}}
holds for all Borcherds algebras
\mathfrak{g}
and
{\widehat{U}^{+}}
acts on a completion
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
of
\mathfrak{g}
as automorphisms.
It should also be possible to construct an analog
G(\mathfrak{g})
of our group
G(\mathfrak{m})
for any Borcherds algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, relative to a Borcherds Cartan matrix
A
. We hope to consider this in future work.

Our method for constructing the group
G(\mathfrak{m})
is similar to an early construction of a Kac–Moody group by Tits
[
Tit81
]
, where he defined a Kac--Moody group as a subgroup of the multiplicative group of a completion of the universal enveloping algebra
1
1

1

This construction of Tits was later superceded by his definition of Kac–Moody groups in
[
Tit87
]
.
. However the notion of a root group associated to an imaginary root does not appear in
[
Tit81
]
or
[
Tit87
]
, where Kac–Moody groups are generated by automorphisms of the images of real root vectors under an adjoint or highest weight representation. Our approach is analogous to the construction of Kac–Moody groups in
[
Rou12
]
and
[
Kum12
]
, but is more explicit.

Our results in this work use the construction of
\mathfrak{m}
as
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{g}(A)/\mathfrak{z},
where
\mathfrak{g}(A)
is the infinite dimensional Lie algebra associated with the Borcherds Cartan matrix
A
(Section
2
) and
\mathfrak{z}
is the center of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
. In a future work
[
ACJ+23
]
we will give a construction of a Lie group analog
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{m})
using the equivalent construction of

\mathfrak{m}
as
\mathfrak{m}=P_{1}/R
, where
P_{1}
is the physical space of
V=V^{\natural}\otimes V_{{1,1}}
and
R
is the radical of a natural bilinear form on
P_{1}
. Our construction of
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{m})

encodes the action of
{\mathbb{M}}
on primary vectors in
V
induced from the
[
FLM88
]
-action of
{\mathbb{M}}
on
V^{\natural}
and
\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{m})
is conjecturally a proper subgroup of the group
G(\mathfrak{m})
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1-j}{2}\,h_{-1\,1}+\frac{1+j}{2}\,h_{1\,1}+\mathfrak{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(h_{-1\,1}+h_{1\,1}\right)+\frac{j}{2}\left(-h_{-1\,1}+h_{1\,1}\right)+{\mathfrak{z}}=-jh_{1}-h_{2}

when
j\neq 1
. Hence
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
span
{\mathfrak{h}}
. The relations (
M:1
)–(
M:5
) follow from the corresponding relations (
R:1
)–(
R:5
) for
{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
. Finally
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are linearly independent by (
M:2a
) and (
M:2b
).
∎

Before defining the roots of
\mathfrak{m}
, we extend the Lie algebra in order to ensure that the simple roots form a linearly independent set (see also
[
JLW95
]
).
The row space of
A
is spanned by the two rows indexed by
\{(\pm 1,1)\}
.
Thus, for
(j,k)\in I-\left\{(\pm 1,1)\right\}
, let
D_{jk}
be the derivation of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
defined by

D_{jk}(e_{pq})=\delta_{jp}\delta_{kq}e_{jk},\quad D_{jk}(f_{pq})=-\delta_{jp}\delta_{kq}f_{j},\quad D_{jk}(h_{pq})=0,

for all
p,q\in I
.
The span of the

is an abelian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{d}_{0}
of derivations of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
.
Now
\mathfrak{d}_{0}+\mathfrak{h}_{A}=\mathfrak{d}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{A}
is an abelian subalgebra of

\mathfrak{d}_{0}\ltimes\mathfrak{g}(A)
.
We define the
extended Monster Lie algebra
to be
\mathfrak{g}^{e}(A)=\mathfrak{g}^{e}:=\mathfrak{d}_{0}\ltimes\mathfrak{g}(A)
,
with Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}^{e}=\mathfrak{d}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{A}
.

2.2.
Decompositions of
\mathfrak{m}

We use
\mathfrak{h}^{e}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{e}
to define the roots of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
. In Subsection
2.4
, we show
how to obtain the root system of
\mathfrak{m}
.
For
\alpha\in(\mathfrak{h}^{e})^{*}
, define

\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{g}(A)\mid[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\mbox{ for all }h\in\mathfrak{h}^{e}\right\}.

Nonzero elements
\alpha\in(\mathfrak{h}^{e})^{*}
such that
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}\neq 0
are called
roots
of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
; the set of all roots is denoted
\Delta\subseteq(\mathfrak{h}^{e})^{*}
.
The root space
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
is contained in
\mathfrak{g}(A)
, but can be considered a subset of
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
or
\mathfrak{m}
,
since
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}\cap\mathfrak{h}^{e}=0
.

The
simple root

\alpha_{jk}\in(\mathfrak{h}^{e})^{*}
, for
(j,k)\in I
, is defined by the condition

\left[h,e_{jk}\right]=\alpha_{jk}(h)e_{jk}
for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}^{e}
.
Now
\alpha_{jk}(h_{1})=1
,
\alpha_{jk}(h_{2})=j
,
and
\alpha_{jk}(D_{pq})=\delta_{jp}\delta_{kq}
for
(p,q)\in I-\left\{(\pm 1,1)\right\}
, so the simple roots are linearly independent in
(\mathfrak{h}^{e})^{*}
.

Every root
\alpha\in\Delta
is an integral sum
\alpha=\sum_{(j,k)\in I}a_{jk}\alpha_{jk}
, so is contained in the

root lattice

Q:=\bigoplus_{(j,k)\in I}{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{jk}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{I}
.
We call
\alpha
a
positive root
if every
a_{jk}\geq 0
and a
negative root
if every
a_{jk}\leq 0
.
The set of all positive (respectively negative) roots is denoted
\Delta_{+}
(respectively
\Delta_{-}
).
We have subalgebras

{\mathfrak{n}^{\pm}}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{\pm}}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}.

Some elementary properties of
\mathfrak{m}
are summarized
in the following, which follows from Proposition 1.5 of
[
Jur96
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The Borcherds algebra

has the following properties:

(1)

(2)

[

]

we define a symmetric bilinear form on

This induces a form on
\mathfrak{h}^{e}

\mathfrak{g}^{e}
satisfying, for all

,
(p,q)\in{I}

\displaystyle\left(h_{jk},h_{pq}\right)_{\mathfrak{g}^{e}}

\displaystyle=-(j+p),

(B:3)

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{m}_{\beta}\right)_{\mathfrak{g}^{e}}

(B:4)

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{h}^{e},\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}\right)_{\mathfrak{g}^{e}}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{if $\alpha\in\Delta$,}

(B:5)

(B:6)

\displaystyle\left(D_{jk},e_{pq}\right)_{\mathfrak{g}^{e}}=\left(D_{jk},f_{pq}\right)_{\mathfrak{g}^{e}}

This also induces symmetric invariant bilinear forms on
\mathfrak{g}(A)
and on
\mathfrak{m}
, denoted
\left(\circ,\circ\right)_{\mathfrak{g}(A)}
and
\left(\circ,\circ\right)_{\mathfrak{m}}
.

We call
\alpha\in\Delta
a
real root
if
(\alpha,\alpha)_{Q}>0
and an
imaginary root
if
(\alpha,\alpha)_{Q}\leq 0
.
The set of real (respectively imaginary) roots is denoted
\Delta^{\re}
(respectively
\Delta^{\im}
);
we also denote the set of positive real roots (respectively negative real roots) by
\Delta^{\re}_{\pm}
and the set of positive imaginary roots (respectively negative imaginary roots) by

.
Hence
\alpha_{-1}:=\alpha_{-11}
is a real root with squared norm
2
, while
\alpha_{jk}
is an imaginary root
with squared norm
-2j
for

with

{\mathbb{N}}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
we define

\displaystyle I^{\re}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(j,k)\in I\mid a_{jk,jk}>0\right\}=\left\{(-1,1)\right\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle I^{\im}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(j,k)\in I\mid a_{jk,jk}\leq 0\right\}=\left\{(j,k)\mid j\in{\mathbb{N}},\;1\leq k\leq c(j)\right\}.

\mathfrak{m}
as a direct sum of a
\gl_{2}
subalgebra and two free subalgebras as in
[
Jur98
]
.
Define an
extended index set

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\{(\ell,j,k)\mid(j,k)\in I^{\im},\,0\leq\ell<j\}

\displaystyle=\{(\ell,j,k)\mid j\in{\mathbb{N}},\,1\leq k\leq c(j),\,0\leq\ell<j\}.

and set

e_{\ell,jk}:=\frac{\left(\ad e_{-1}\right)^{\ell}e_{jk}}{\ell!}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\ell,jk}:=\frac{\left(\ad f_{-1}\right)^{\ell}f_{jk}}{\ell!},

for

.

.

(

]
)

Let

2.3.
Roots and reflections for
{\mathfrak{g}}(A)

In this subsection, we explicitly describe the root system
\Delta
of
{\mathfrak{g}}(A)
with respect to
\mathfrak{h}^{e}
.
Recall that the extended Monster Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{g}^{e}=\mathfrak{d}_{0}\ltimes\mathfrak{g}(A)

with Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}^{e}=\mathfrak{d}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{A}
, and that
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{e}=\mathfrak{d}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{A}
were constructed so that the simple roots are linearly independent in
\mathfrak{h}^{e}
. In Subsection
2.4
, we show
how to obtain the more familiar root system of
\mathfrak{m}
in the lattice
\text{II}_{1,1}
.

For
\alpha\in\left(\mathfrak{h}^{e}\right)^{*}
, recall that the root space
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
can be considered a subset of either
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
,
\mathfrak{g}(A)
, or
\mathfrak{m}
.
The simple roots
{\alpha}_{jk}\in(\mathfrak{h}^{e})^{*}
are then defined for
(j,k)\in I
by the condition

[h,e_{jk}]=\alpha_{jk}(h)e_{jk}
, for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}^{e}
.
The root lattice of
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
is

Q={\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{-1}\oplus\bigoplus_{(j,k)\in I^{\im}}{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{jk}\subseteq\left(\mathfrak{h}^{e}\right)^{*}
.

We have one dimensional simple root spaces:

{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha_{-1}}={\mathbb{C}}{e}_{-1},\quad{\mathfrak{m}}_{-\alpha_{-1}}={\mathbb{C}}{f}_{-1},\quad{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha_{jk}}={\mathbb{C}}{e}_{jk},\quad{\mathfrak{m}}_{-\alpha_{jk}}={\mathbb{C}}{f}_{jk}.

If bases for the root spaces of
\mathfrak{g}(A)
are needed, they may be obtained using the Shirshov basis
[
Shi09
]
for a free Lie algebra and Theorem
2.3
. However, for our purposes,
a description of the set of all the roots suffices.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

\alpha=\ell\alpha_{-1}+\sum_{(j,k)\in I^{\im}}c_{jk}\alpha_{jk}

be an element of the root lattice
Q
.
Then
\alpha\in{\Delta}^{\im}_{+}
if and only if

(1)

\ell\geq 0
and
c_{jk}\geq 0
for all
(j,k)\in I^{\im}
with at least one
c_{jk}>0
;

(2)

\displaystyle\ell\leq\sum_{(j,k)\in I^{\im}}(j-1)c_{jk}
; and

(3)

if only one of the coefficients
c_{jk}
appearing in
\alpha
is nonzero, then either
c_{jk}=1
or
0<\ell<(j-1)c_{jk}
.

Proof.

Elements in the Shirshov basis
[
Shi09
]
are Lie products of terms

e_{\ell_{1},j_{1}k_{1}},e_{\ell_{2},j_{2}k_{2}},\dots,e_{\ell_{n},j_{n}k_{n}},

for any choice of indices
\left(\ell_{1},j_{1},k_{1}\right),\dots,\left(\ell_{n},j_{n},k_{n}\right)
in
E
, allowing repeats but such that the indices are not
all
equal.
Such a basis element is in
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
, where

\alpha=\left(\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{n}\right)\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{j_{1}k_{1}}+\cdots+\alpha_{j_{n}k_{n}}.

The result now follows, with (
2
) following from the fact that each
\ell_{i}\leq j_{i}-1
and (
3
) coming from the condition that the extended indices are not all equal.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

If
\alpha\in\Delta^{\im}
, then
k\alpha\in\Delta^{\im}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}-\{0\}
.

It follows that

{\mathfrak{m}}_{\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}}={\mathbb{C}}e_{\ell,jk}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathfrak{m}}_{-(\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk})}={\mathbb{C}}f_{\ell,jk},

for
\ell=0,1,\dots,j-1
.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}Q
be the
{\mathbb{R}}
-span of the roots and note that the bilinear form on
Q
extends to a form
(\circ,\circ)_{{\mathbb{R}}Q}
.
For a root
\alpha
of nonzero norm, we define the
reflection in
\alpha
by

w_{\alpha}:\quad{\mathbb{R}}Q\to{\mathbb{R}}Q,\quad x\mapsto x-\frac{2(\alpha,x)_{{\mathbb{R}}Q}}{(\alpha,x)_{{\mathbb{R}}Q}}\alpha.

The reflections
w_{{\alpha}_{-1}}
preserves the root lattice
Q
with

{w}_{\alpha_{-1}}\;:\;\alpha_{-1}\mapsto-\alpha_{-1},\qquad\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}\mapsto(j-\ell-1)\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}.

Note that
w_{\alpha_{-1}}
also preserves
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\im}_{\pm}
.
For
q\in\{1,\dots,c(1)\}
, the reflection
w_{\alpha_{1q}}
also preserves
Q
with

{w}_{\alpha_{1q}}\;:\;\alpha_{-1}\mapsto\alpha_{-1},\qquad\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}\mapsto\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}-(j+1)\alpha_{1q}.

However
{w}_{\alpha_{1q}}
does not preserve
\Delta
, since
\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}-(j+1)\alpha_{1q}
is never a root, as it has both positive and negative coefficients with respect to the simple roots.
Every other reflection of the form
w_{m\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{pq}}
fails to preserve
Q
.
Thus the
Weyl group

\langle w_{\alpha_{-1}}\rangle
for
\mathfrak{g}(A)
has order
2
.

2.4.
Roots and reflections for
\mathfrak{m}

The root system
\Delta
gives a decomposition

\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{h}_{A}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}.

In this subsection, we compare this with the coarser decomposition given by the roots with respect to
\mathfrak{h}:={\mathfrak{h}}_{A}/{\mathfrak{z}}={\mathbb{C}}h_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}h_{2}
.

Recall from
[
CS13
]
that the unique even 2-dimensional unimodular Lorentzian lattice
\text{II}_{1,1}
is the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}

equipped with the bilinear form given by matrix

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}~0&-1\\
-1&~0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
We have a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear surjective
specialization
map

\overline{\alpha}_{-1}=(1,-1)\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\alpha}_{jk}=(1,j),

Only finitely many roots in
\Delta
specialize to a given element of

by Lemma
2.4
.
The specialized root space decomposition for
\mathfrak{m}
can be written

{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{h}}\oplus\bigoplus_{\beta\in\overline{\Delta}}{\mathfrak{m}}_{\beta},

where

\mathfrak{m}_{\beta}=\left\{x\in{\mathfrak{m}}\mid[h,x]=\beta(h)x\text{ for all $h\in{\mathfrak{h}}$}\right\}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\Delta\text{ s.t.}\\
\overline{\alpha}=\beta\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}.

For
(\ell,j,k)\in E
, the root
\alpha_{\ell,jk}:=\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}\in\Delta

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}_{+}

\displaystyle=\left\{(1,-1)\right\}\cup\left\{(x,y)\mid x,y\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\},

and further

,
\overline{\Delta}=\overline{\Delta}_{+}\cup\overline{\Delta}_{-}
.

\mathfrak{m}

2.5.
Root strings and root spans

In this subsection, we describe some of the properties of root strings in the root system
\Delta
for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}(A)
and the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-spans of certain pairs of roots, which will be needed in future sections.

Proposition 2.6
.

Every root string of
\Delta
in the direction of
\alpha_{-1}
is finite.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta-\left\{\pm\alpha_{-1}\right\}
. Then the root string through
\alpha
in the direction of
\alpha_{-1}
consists of elements in the set

\left\{\alpha+i\alpha_{-1}\mid i\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}\cap\Delta.

When we specialize to
\overline{\Delta}
, this becomes
\left\{\overline{\alpha}+i\overline{\alpha}_{-1}\mid i\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}\cap\overline{\Delta}
which is clearly finite (see Figure
1
). So the original root string in
\Delta
is also finite.
∎

As before, we set
\alpha_{\ell,jk}=\ell\alpha_{-1}+\alpha_{jk}
.
We have root strings in
\Delta
in the direction of
\alpha_{-1}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{jk}=\alpha_{0,jk},\;\alpha_{1,jk},\;\dots,\;\alpha_{j-1,jk},

and

\displaystyle-\alpha_{j-1,jk},\;\dots,\;-\alpha_{1,jk},\;-\alpha_{jk}=-\alpha_{0,jk},

for
(j,k)\in I^{\im}
.
After specializing to
\mathfrak{m}
, these can be considered as the weights of the
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1)
-representations

V_{jk}^{\pm}
spanned by the corresponding weight vectors

\displaystyle e_{jk}=e_{0,jk},\;e_{1,jk},\;\dots,\;e_{j-1,jk},

and

\displaystyle f_{j-1,jk},\;\dots,\;f_{1,jk},\;f_{jk}=f_{0,jk},

respectively. These representations have highest weights
\overline{\alpha}_{j-1,jk}
and
-\overline{\alpha}_{0,jk}
, respectively.
We have
W(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1))=\left\langle w_{-1}\right\rangle
and the reflection
w_{-1}
reverses these root strings.

Given any root system
\Delta
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
, the
root span
is
\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{n}
, where we inductively define

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha+\beta\}\cap\Delta;

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\gamma+\alpha,\gamma+\beta\mid\gamma\in\Sigma_{n-1}\right\}\cap\Delta.

Note that
\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)=\emptyset
if and only if
\alpha+\beta\notin\Delta
.
In general,
\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)\subseteq\left\{a\alpha+b\beta\mid a,b\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}\cap\Delta
.
Also note that
\Sigma(\beta,\alpha)=\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)
and
\Sigma(-\alpha,-\beta)=-\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)
.
The following classification can be proved inductively by repeated application of Lemma
2.4
:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\Delta
be the root system for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}(A)
. Then we have the following.

(1)

\displaystyle\Sigma\left(\alpha_{-1},\alpha_{-1}\right)=\Sigma\left(\alpha_{-1},-\alpha_{-1}\right)=\emptyset
.

(2)

\displaystyle\Sigma\left(\alpha_{\ell,jk},\alpha_{-1}\right)=\begin{cases}\{(a\ell+b)\alpha_{-1}+a\alpha_{jk}\mid&\!\!a,b\in{\mathbb{N}},b<a(j-\ell)\},\\
&\text{\;\;if $0\leq\ell<j-1$,}\\
\emptyset&\text{\;\;if $\ell=j-1$.}\end{cases}

(3)

\displaystyle\Sigma\left(\alpha_{\ell,jk},-\alpha_{-1}\right)=\begin{cases}\{(a\ell-b)\alpha_{-1}+a\alpha_{jk}\mid&\!\!a,b\in{\mathbb{N}},b\leq a\ell\},\\
&\text{\;\;if $0<\ell<j$,}\\
\emptyset&\text{\;\;if $\ell=0$.}\end{cases}

(4)

\displaystyle\Sigma\left(\alpha_{\ell,jk},\alpha_{m,pq}\right)=\left\{(a\ell+bm)\alpha_{-1}+a\alpha_{jk}+b\alpha_{pq}\mid a,b\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
.

(5)

\displaystyle\Sigma\left(\alpha_{\ell,jk},-\alpha_{m,jk}\right)=

\begin{cases}\{\alpha_{-1}\}\cup\{(x\ell+y)\alpha_{-1}+x\alpha_{jk}\mid&\!\!\!\!x\in{\mathbb{Z}},y\in{\mathbb{N}},y<x(j-\ell)\},\\
&\text{\;\;if $\ell-m=1$},\\
\{-\alpha_{-1}\}\cup\{(x\ell-y)\alpha_{-1}+x\alpha_{jk}&\!\!\!\mid x\in{\mathbb{Z}},y\in{\mathbb{N}},y\leq x\ell\},\\
&\text{\;\;if $\ell-m=-1$},\\
\emptyset&\text{\;\;otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6)

\displaystyle\Sigma\left(\alpha_{\ell,jk},-\alpha_{m,pq}\right)=\emptyset\;
if
(j,k)\neq(p,q)
.

Note that these root spans are either empty or infinite.
As we see in Theorem
3.12
,
\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)
is the set of roots needed to compute the group commutator of unipotent generators corresponding to
\alpha
and
\beta
.

We contrast this situation with real roots
\alpha,\beta
for a Kac–Moody group, where the set

S(\alpha,\beta):=\left\{a\alpha+b\beta\mid a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\right\}\cap\Delta

is finite if and only if
\alpha
and
\beta
are a prenilpotent pair,
and furthermore
S(\alpha,\beta)=\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)
.
But
\Sigma(\alpha,\beta)
can be strictly smaller
than
S(\alpha,\beta)
in the Monster root system
\Delta

(or, indeed, in the Kac–Moody case if we consider imaginary roots
\alpha
and
\beta
).
For example,
\Sigma\left(\alpha_{-1},\alpha_{j-1,jk}\right)
is empty while
S(\alpha_{-1},\alpha_{j-1,jk})
is infinite.

2.6.
Positive root systems

We now define positive and simple root systems for
\mathfrak{m}
, following
[
Mor80
]
and
[
LN11
]
:

Definition 2.8
.

We say
\Pi\subseteq\Delta
is a
positive root system
if
\Pi\cap(-\Pi)=\emptyset
;
\Pi\cup(-\Pi)=\Delta
; and

\alpha+\beta\in\Pi
whenever
\alpha,\beta\in\Pi
and
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta
.
The corresponding
simple root system
is the set of all
\gamma\in\Pi
that cannot be written as a sum
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
for
\alpha,\beta\in\Pi
.

Note that
\Delta_{+}
is a positive root system and
\left\{\alpha_{jk}\mid(j,k)\in I\right\}
is its simple root system.
If
\Pi
is a positive root system, then
-\Pi
is also a positive root system and
\Delta
is a disjoint union of
\Pi
and
-\Pi
.
For every positive root system, we have a corresponding nilpotent subalgebra

\mathfrak{n}^{\Pi}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha},

and decomposition

{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathfrak{n}^{-\Pi}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{\Pi}.

We need the following straighforward results for constructing positive root systems:

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\pi:\text{\rm II}_{1,1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a functional with
\pi^{-1}(0)\cap\overline{\Delta}=\emptyset
, then
\left\{\alpha\in\Delta\mid\pi(\overline{\alpha})>0\right\}

is a positive root system.

Given
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define
\pi:\text{\rm II}_{1,1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
\pi(x,y)=ax+by
.
This functional will satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma
2.9
if either
b=0
, or
a/b<-1
, or
-1<a/b\leq 0
, or
a/b
is positive and irrational.
Note that
\pi(x,y)=\pm x
gives positive root systems
\Delta_{\pm}
, while

\pi(x,y)=\pm y
gives positive root systems
\left\{-\alpha_{-1}\right\}\cup\Delta^{\im}_{+}
and
\left\{\alpha_{-1}\right\}\cup\Delta^{\im}_{-}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
,
then there is a positive root system containing
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Proof.

It is easy to construct a functional
\pi:\text{\rm II}_{1,1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
\pi\left(\overline{\alpha}\right),\pi\left(\overline{\beta}\right)>0
. If
\pi^{-1}(0)\cap\overline{\Delta}
is nonempty, then
write
\pi(x,y)=ax+by
for
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and vary the values of
a,b
by small amounts to ensure that
a/b
is irrational while still having
\pi\left(\overline{\alpha}\right),\pi\left(\overline{\beta}\right)>0

∎

3.1.
Lie algebra gradings and pro-summability

Fix a lattice
\Lambda
(ie, a free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module).
Recall that a
{\mathbb{C}}
-Lie algebra
\mathfrak{L}
is
\Lambda
-graded
if

\mathfrak{L}=\bigoplus_{a\in\Lambda}\mathfrak{L}_{a},

with
\left[\mathfrak{L}_{a},\mathfrak{L}_{b}\right]\subseteq\mathfrak{L}_{a+b}
and
\dim\mathfrak{L}_{a}
finite for all
a,b\in\Lambda
.
Each component
\mathfrak{L}_{a}
has the topology of a finite dimensional
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space, which induces the product topology on

\mathfrak{L}
. This in turn induces topologies on
\End(\mathfrak{L})
and
\Aut(\mathfrak{L})
.
Given
a\in\Lambda
, the map
\phi\in\End(\mathfrak{L})
is called
graded of degree
a
if

\phi\left(\mathfrak{L}_{b}\right)\subseteq\mathfrak{L}_{b+a}

for all
b\in\Lambda
.
Suppose
\Pi
is another
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module and
\lambda:\Lambda\to\Pi
is a homomorphism with the property that, for each
p\in\Pi
,

\mathfrak{L}_{a}=0
for all but finitely many
a\in\lambda^{-1}(p)
.
Then we get a
\Pi
-grading

\mathfrak{L}=\bigoplus_{p\in\Pi}\mathfrak{L}_{p}
by setting

\mathfrak{L}_{p}=\bigoplus_{a\in\lambda^{-1}(p)}\mathfrak{L}_{a},

for all
p\in\Pi
.

The Monster Lie algebra
\mathfrak{m}
is
Q
-graded as shown in Proposition
2.2
.
Let
\lambda
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear map
\lambda:Q\to{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

\lambda(\alpha)=a+b
where
\overline{\alpha}=(a,b)
is the specialization of
\alpha
.
So
\lambda
induces a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading of
\mathfrak{m}
with components

\mathfrak{m}_{k}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\lambda^{-1}(k)\cap\Delta}{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}

for
k\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{m}_{0}:=\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1)
.
The fact that each
\Delta_{k}:=\lambda^{-1}(k)\cap\Delta
is finite ensures that each
\mathfrak{m}_{k}
is finite dimensional.
This
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading is compatible with the decomposition

\mathfrak{m}={\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{-}}\oplus\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1)\oplus{\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{+}},

since

{\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{\pm}}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta^{\im}_{\pm}}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{k\in\pm{\mathbb{N}}}\mathfrak{m}_{k}.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
x\in\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
, then
\ad(x)
is a graded endomorphism of degree
\alpha
.
If
x\in\mathfrak{m}_{k}
, then
\ad(x)
is a graded endomorphism of degree
k
.

We define the
(positive) formal completion
of
\mathfrak{m}
to be

\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}:={\mathfrak{n}^{-}}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}},

where

\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}:=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}.

Equivalently

\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{-}}\oplus\gl_{2}(-1)\oplus{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}},

where

{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}=\prod_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathfrak{m}_{k}
, since
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}={\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha_{-1}}\oplus{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
.

We note that
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
has an induced topology as a subset of

\mathfrak{X}:=\prod_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathfrak{m}_{k}
with the product topology.
The Lie product on
\mathfrak{m}
extends to
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
but does not extend to
\mathfrak{X}
.
Since
\mathfrak{X}
is a countable product of finite dimensional vector spaces, it is second countable,
and hence sequential
[
Mun99
]
.
We can also construct the space of linear maps
\End(\mathfrak{X})
with the pointwise topology.
Since
\mathfrak{X}
has a countable basis, this function space is also second countable and hence sequential.
This in turn induces a topology on
\Aut\left(\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}\right)\subseteq\End\left(\mathfrak{X}\right)
.
Recall that a sequential topology is entirely captured by limits of sequences – we will prove our topological results using limits without further comment.

Recall that a (formal) series
\sum_{i}\vartheta_{i}
of operators on a space
\mathfrak{Y}
is called summable
[
LL04
]
if, for all
y\in\mathfrak{Y}
,
\vartheta_{i}(y)
is nonzero for only finitely many values of
i
.
We now generalize this concept.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
I
and
K
be countable index sets.
Let
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
be a finite dimensional vector space for all
k\in K
, let
\mathfrak{Y}
be a subspace of
\prod_{k}\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, and write
(y)_{k}
for the projection of
y\in\mathfrak{Y}
onto
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, so that
y=\sum_{k\in K}(y)_{k}
.
Let
\vartheta_{i}
be an operator on
\mathfrak{Y}
for all
i\in I
.
We say that
\sum_{i}\vartheta_{i}
is
pro-summable
if
I_{k}(y):=\{i\in I\mid\left(\vartheta_{i}(y)\right)_{k}\neq 0\}

is finite for all
k\in K
and
y\in\mathfrak{Y}
.
If
\sum_{i}\vartheta_{i}
is pro-summable, then it is a well defined operator on
\mathfrak{Y}
with

\sum_{i}\vartheta_{i}(y):=\sum_{k\in K}\sum_{i\in I_{k}(y)}\left(\vartheta_{i}(y)\right)_{k}.

This definition depends on the choice of direct product decomposition
\prod_{k}\mathfrak{X}_{k}
.
Every space we will consider is a subspace of
\prod_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathfrak{m}_{k}
.
A pro-summable series is well defined without the use of limits, but note that not every well defined series is pro-summable (for example,
\exp\left(\ad\left(h_{1}\right)\right)
considered in Section
4
).
The product of two pro-summable series is well defined, but may not be pro-summable.
Given pro-summable series
\varsigma:=\sum_{j\in J}\varsigma_{j}
and
\vartheta:=\sum_{i\in I}\vartheta
, the product
\varsigma\vartheta
is pro-summable if and only if

\left\{(i,j)\in I\times J\mid(\varsigma_{j}\vartheta_{i}(y))_{k}\neq 0\right\}

is finite for all
k\in K
and
y\in\mathfrak{Y}
.

Given
x\in\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
, there are only
finitely many
k<0
with
(x)_{k}\neq 0
.
In other words, our choice of completion ensures that the formal sum
x=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(x)_{k}
is finite in the negative direction but potentially infinite in the positive direction.
For
x\in\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}-\{0\}
, the
order

N(x)
is the smallest integer with
(x)_{N(x)}\neq 0
, so that
x
can be written uniquely as a formal sum

x=\sum_{k=N(x)}^{\infty}(x)_{k}.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
, then

\exp\left(u\ad\left(e_{-1}\right)\right)
and
\exp\left(u\ad\left(f_{-1}\right)\right)
are summable on
\mathfrak{m}
and pro-summable on

\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
.
If
x\in\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
, then

\exp(\ad(x))
is pro-summable on
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Proposition
4.3
(see Section
4
).
A summable series on
\mathfrak{m}

clearly extends to a pro-summable series on
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
.
If
x\in\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
,
y\in\mathfrak{m}_{k}
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(x)_{n}
and

\left(\exp\left(\ad(x)\right)(y)\right)_{k}=(y)_{k}+\sum_{n=0}^{k-N(y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ s.t.}\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{\ad\left(x_{n_{i}}\right)\cdots\ad\left(x_{n_{m}}\right)}{n_{1}!\cdots n_{m}!}\left(y\right)_{k-n}

is a finite sum. So
\exp(\ad(x))
is pro-summable.
∎

3.2.
The complete group
{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{\im}

We now use the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading from the previous subsection to construct a complete group
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}
, which is an analog of a complete unipotent group, corresponding to the imaginary roots of the Monster Lie algebra.
Note however that the choice of grading is mostly for convenience:
Similar methods could be used to construct groups with respect to the original
Q
-grading, or the
{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading from Subsection
2.4
.
We could also use alternative maps
\lambda
, such as the

{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading on
\mathfrak{m}
given in
[
Bor92
]
.
These different choices of grading all give the same topological group up to isomorphism.

The completion has two decompositions

\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{-}}\oplus\mathfrak{gl}_{2}({-1})\oplus{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}

related by

\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}={\mathbb{C}}e_{-1}\oplus\widehat{\mathfrak{u}}^{+}
and

{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}={\mathbb{C}}f_{-1}\oplus{\mathfrak{u}}^{-}
.
Define subspaces

{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{k}:=\prod_{j\geq k}\mathfrak{m}_{j},

for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Now
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{k}
is a
\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
-module for all integers
k
;

{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{k}
is a pro-Lie algebra for
k\geq 0
; and

{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{k}
is pro-nilpotent Lie algebra for
k\geq 0
(Section
3.4
).
In particular
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}_{0}=\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1)\oplus{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}={\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define

{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}:=\left\{\varphi\in\Aut(\widehat{\mathfrak{m}})\mid\varphi(y)\in y+{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{k+n}\text{ whenever }y\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}\;\text{for some}\;k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

and
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}:={\widehat{U}^{+}}_{1}
.
Equivalently,
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}
consists of the Lie algebra automorphisms of
{\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}}
that can be written as a series with leading term
1
with respect to the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading of
{\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}}
.
In other words, the action of
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}
on
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
smears
x_{\ell}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}
across the infinite product
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{k+1}=\prod_{j\geq k+1}\mathfrak{m}_{j}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, both
{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{\im}
and
{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
are closed subgroups of
\Aut(\widehat{\mathfrak{m}})
.

Proof.

Since
\prod_{j\geq k+n}{\mathfrak{m}}_{j}
is closed in
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
, it is straightforward to show that if
\phi_{i}\to\phi
and each

\phi_{i}\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
then
\phi\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
.
∎

For convenience, we read all products from the right to the left. For example,

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle:=\cdots\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{3}\right)\right)\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{2}\right)\right)\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{n}\right)\right)\cdots\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{2}\right)\right)\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{1}\right)\right).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x_{k}\in\mathfrak{m}_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. Then

\prod_{k=n}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{k}\right)\right)

expands to a formal sum which is pro-summable. Hence this infinite product converges to
a well-defined automorphism in
{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\ell}
for some
\ell>0
. Now

\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)=1+\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)+\frac{1}{2!}\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)^{2}+\cdots

is a well-defined map
\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}\to\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}
since

\left(\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\left(y\right)\right)_{k}=0
is zero for
k<N(y)
, and for
k\geq N(y)
it is the finite sum

\left(\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\left(y\right)\right)_{k}=\left(y_{k}+\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\left(\left(y\right)_{k-\ell}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{2!}\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\left(y\right)_{k-2\ell}\right)+\cdots+\frac{1}{M!}\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)^{M}\left(\left(y\right)_{k-M\ell}\right),

where
M=\left\lfloor\frac{k-N(y)}{\ell}\right\rfloor
.
Furthermore, if
\ell>k-N(y)
then
\left(\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\left(y\right)\right)_{k}=(y)_{k}
. So,

\varphi:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)

is also well-defined, since

\left(\phi\left(y\right)\right)_{k}=\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k-N(y)}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\left(y\right)\right)_{k}.

Now
\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)
is an automorphism and it is easy to see that

\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{\ell}
.
Hence
\varphi
is an automorphism and
\prod_{\ell=n}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
.
The remaining results are similar.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
x_{i},x\in{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{i}\right)\right)\to\exp(\ad(x))
as
i\to\infty
if and only if
x_{i}\to x
as
i\to\infty
.
Let
x_{i,\ell},x_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\ell}
for
i,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{i,\ell}\right)\right)\to\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\qquad\text{as $i\to\infty$}

if and only if
x_{i,\ell}\to x_{\ell}
as
i\to\infty
for every
\ell
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the formulas for
\left(\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\left(y\right)\right)_{k}
in the proof of Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

\displaystyle{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}=\left\{\prod_{\ell=n}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)\;\middle|\;x_{\ell}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{\ell}\,\text{ for all $\ell\geq n$}\right\}.

So a generic element of
{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
can be considered as a power series with constant term
1
if we view the
\ad(x_{\ell})
as variables.

Proof.

Suppose
\varphi\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
. For
x_{k}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}
, we write

\varphi\left(x_{k}\right)=x_{k}+\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\varphi_{k,i}\left(x_{k}\right),

where
\varphi_{k,i}
is a linear map
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}\to{\mathfrak{m}}_{k+i}
.

For
m=1,2
, we can write

\varphi_{0,n}\left(h_{m}\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}p_{m,\alpha},
with
p_{m,\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}
.
Now

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\varphi(0)\right)_{n}=\left(\varphi\left(\left[h_{1},h_{2}\right]\right)\right)_{n}=\left(\left[\varphi\left(h_{1}\right),\phi\left(h_{2}\right)\right]\right)_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[h_{1}+\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\varphi_{0,k}\left(h_{1}\right),\;h_{2}+\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\varphi_{0,k}\left(h_{2}\right)\right]\right)_{n}

\displaystyle=\left[h_{1},\varphi_{0,n}\left(h_{2}\right)\right]+\left[\varphi_{0,n}\left(h_{1}\right),h_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\left[h_{1},p_{2,\alpha}\right]-\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\left[h_{2},p_{1,\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\left(\alpha\left(h_{1}\right)p_{2,\alpha}-\alpha\left(h_{2}\right)p_{1,\alpha}\right).

Hence

\alpha(h_{1})p_{2,\alpha}=\alpha(h_{2})p_{1,\alpha},

and so we have
p_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
such that

p_{1,\alpha}=\alpha\left(h_{1}\right)p_{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{2,\alpha}=\alpha\left(h_{2}\right)p_{\alpha}.

Now define

x_{n}:=-\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}p_{\alpha},

so that

\varphi_{0,n}\left(h_{m}\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}p_{m,\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\alpha\left(h_{m}\right)p_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\left[h_{m},p_{\alpha}\right]=-\left[h_{m},x_{n}\right]=\left[x_{n},h_{m}\right].

Hence
\varphi_{0,n}=\ad\left(x_{n}\right)|_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{0}}
.

Fix
\beta\in\Delta_{k}
and
y_{\beta}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\beta}
.
We can write

\varphi_{k,n}\left(y_{\beta}\right)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}q_{\gamma},

with
q_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\gamma}
.
Now, for
h\in\mathfrak{h}
,

\displaystyle\beta\left(h\right)\varphi_{k,n}\left(y_{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{k,n}\left(\beta(h)y_{\beta}\right)=\left(\varphi\left(\left[h,y_{\beta}\right]\right)\right)_{k+n}=\left(\left[\varphi\left(h\right),\varphi\left(y_{\beta}\right)\right]\right)_{k+n}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[h+\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\varphi_{0,j}\left(h\right),\;y_{\beta}+\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\varphi_{k,j}\left(y_{\beta}\right)\right]\right)_{k+n}

\displaystyle=\left[h,\varphi_{k,n}\left(y_{\beta}\right)\right]+\left[\varphi_{0,k}\left(h\right),y_{\beta}\right].

Hence

\displaystyle\beta\left(h\right)\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}q_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}\gamma\left(h\right)q_{\gamma}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\alpha\left(h\right)\left[p_{\alpha},y_{\beta}\right],

so

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}\left(\beta(h)-\gamma(h)\right)q_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\alpha(h)\left[p_{\alpha},y_{\beta}\right]=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}\left(\gamma(h)-\beta(h)\right)\left[p_{\gamma-\beta},y_{\beta}\right],

where we take
p_{\gamma-\beta}=0
if
\gamma-\beta
is not a root.
Since this holds for arbitrary
h\in\mathfrak{h}
, we get
q_{\gamma}=-\left[p_{\gamma-\beta},y_{\beta}\right]
and so

\displaystyle\varphi_{k,n}\left(y_{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}q_{\gamma}=-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta_{n+k}}\left[p_{\gamma-\beta},y_{\beta}\right]=-\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{n}}\left[p_{\alpha},y_{\beta}\right]=\left[x_{n},y_{\beta}\right].

That is,
\varphi_{k,n}=\ad\left(x_{n}\right)|_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}}
.

Hence
\varphi=\varphi^{(n+1)}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{n}\right)\right)
where
\varphi^{(n+1)}\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n+1}
.
By induction, we can write

\varphi=\varphi^{(N+1)}\prod_{\ell=n}^{N}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)

with
\varphi^{(N+1)}\in{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{N+1}
.
But
\varphi^{(N+1)}\to 1
as
N\to\infty
, and hence

\varphi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\varphi^{(N+1)}\prod_{\ell=n}^{N}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{\ell=n}^{N}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)=\prod_{\ell=n}^{\infty}\exp\left(\ad\left(x_{\ell}\right)\right)

as required.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
is the closure of

\left\langle\exp(\ad(x))\mid x\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{k},\;k\geq n\right\rangle.

Proof.

Since

\prod_{\ell=n}^{\infty}\exp(\ad(x_{\ell}))=\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{\ell=n}^{N}\exp(\ad(x_{\ell})).

∎

3.3.
The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula and commutators in
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}

In the next sections,
(X,Y)
denotes the group commutator
XYX^{-1}Y^{-1}

Recall the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula in the completion of the free Lie algebra generated by

and

[
Ste04

\exp(X)\exp(Y)=\exp(Z)

Lemma 3.9
.

If
x,y\in{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}

\exp(\ad(x))\exp(\ad(y))=\exp(\ad(z))

Proof.

The proof is immediate since we have

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle:=x+y+\frac{1}{2}[x,y]+\frac{1}{12}\left([x,[x,y]]-[y,[x,y]]\right)+\cdots\in{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}.\qed

Lemma 3.10
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have:

(1)

{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}=\exp\left(\ad\left(\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n}\right)\right)

\exp\left(\ad(x)\right){\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n+1}=\exp\left(\ad(x)+\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n+1}\right)

.

(3)

\exp\left(\ad(x+y)\right){\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n+1}=\exp\left(\ad(x))\exp(\ad(y)\right){\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n+1}
for
x,y\in\mathfrak{m}_{n}
.

(4)

\exp\left(\ad\left([x,y]\right)\right)\in\left(\exp\left(\ad(x)\right),\exp\left(\ad(y)\right)\right){\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n+m+1}
for
x\in\mathfrak{m}_{n}
and
y\in\mathfrak{m}_{m}
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.6
shows that
\exp\left(\ad\left(\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n}\right)\right)
is a closed set and Lemma
3.9
implies that
{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}\subseteq\exp\left(\ad\left(\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n}\right)\right)

Suppose
x\in\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n}

x_{n}\in\mathfrak{m}_{n}
with
x-x_{n}\in\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n+1}
.
So Lemma
3.9
gives us

x^{(n+1)}=x-x_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\left[x,-x_{n}\right]+\cdots\in\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}_{n+1}

Lemma 3.11

3.4.
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
as a pro-Lie algebra and
\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}
as a pro-Lie group

In the previous subsections, we have considered
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
as a direct product, in order to keep our approach as straightforward as possible.
In this section we relate our approach to the concepts of pro-Lie algebras and pro-Lie groups used by most other authors in the field (see for example
[
Kum12
]
).

We first generalize the concept of summability
[
LL04
]
to this setting.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\vartheta_{k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
be operators on a pro-Lie algebra

\mathfrak{L}:=\lim_{i}\mathfrak{L/L}_{i}
.
We say that
\vartheta:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\vartheta_{k}
is pro-summable if, for all
x\in\mathfrak{L}
and
i\in\Lambda
,

\vartheta_{k}(x)
is in
\mathfrak{L/L}_{i}
for all but finitely many values of
k
.

It follows that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\vartheta_{k}(x)
is a well-defined finite sum.

Let
\Lambda
be a directed index set (that is, a poset where, for all
i,j\in\Lambda
, there exists
k\in\Lambda
with
i\leq k
,
j\leq k
).
Recall from
[
Kum12
]
, that the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{L}
is a
pro-Lie algebra
if there is a system
(\mathfrak{L}_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}
of ideals with
\mathfrak{L}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{L}_{j}
whenever
i<j
and each quotient
\mathfrak{L}/\mathfrak{L}_{i}
is finite dimensional.
Further a pro-Lie algebra
\mathfrak{L}
is
pro-nilpotent
if each quotient
\mathfrak{L}/\mathfrak{L}_{i}
is nilpotent.
Equivalently
\mathfrak{L}
is a pro-Lie algebra if and only if it is an inverse limit of finite dimensional algebras; and is pro-nilpotent if and only if it is an inverse limit of finite dimensional nilpotent algebras.
We define pro-solvable algebras similarly.

Each quotient
\mathfrak{L}/\mathfrak{L}_{i}
has the topology of a finite dimensional
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space, which induces a topology on

\mathfrak{L}
.
This topology will serve a similar role as the pro-topology defined in
[
Kum12
]
, although Kumar starts with the discrete topology on vector spaces
in that book (see also

[
Rou12
]
).

Recall that

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{+}}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{k},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}^{+}_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{k},

\displaystyle{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{k},

\displaystyle{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{k}.

Lemma 3.14
.

For all
k\geq 0
,
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}/\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}={\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{+}}/{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}

is nilpotent. Hence

{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}=\varprojlim_{k}{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}/\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{+}}=\varprojlim_{k}{\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{+}}/{\mathfrak{n}}_{k}

are pro-nilpotent pro-Lie algebras, and
{\widehat{\mathfrak{n}}_{\im}^{+}}
is the completion of
{\mathfrak{n}_{\im}^{+}}
.

Similarly a group
G
is
pro-nilpotent
if there is a system
({G}_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}
of subgroups satisfying
{G}_{i}\unlhd{G}_{j}
whenever
i<j
and each quotient
{G}/{G}_{i}
is nilpotent.
A group
G
is
pro-unipotent
if there is a system
({G}_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}
, with each
G_{i}

acting unipotently on a
finite-dimensional vector space
V_{i},
so that there is an inclusion
G_{i}\hookrightarrow G_{j}
induced by an inclusion

V_{i}\hookrightarrow V_{j}
for all
i<j
.
It is easily seen that a pro-unipotent group is pro-nilpotent.

Lemma 3.15
.

The group

{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}=\varprojlim_{\,i}{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}/{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{i}\

is complete, pro-unipotent, and hence pro-nilpotent.

Proof.

Each group
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}/{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}
is unipotent with central series

{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}/{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}\geq{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{2}/{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}\geq\cdots\geq{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n-1}/{\widehat{U}^{+}}_{n}\geq 1,

by Theorem 16.2.6 of
[
Spr09
]
.
Hence
{\widehat{U}_{\im}^{+}}
is complete, pro-unipotent and hence pro-nilpotent.
∎

4.
Rank 2 linear subgroups

We have already noted that
\left\{e_{-1},f_{-1},h_{1},h_{2}\right\}
is a basis for a subalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1)\cong\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
of
\mathfrak{m}
and, for each

In this section, we construct groups

Note that we use a fixed branch of the complex logarithm in this section.

The particular choice is not critical, since we eventually show that all our actions and presentations are independent of the branch chosen.

4.1.
Standard rank 2 linear groups

Here we review the presentations of
\gl_{2}
and
\GL_{2}({\mathbb{C}})

We define a

for a
\gl_{2}
Lie algebra to be elements

\left\{\overline{e},\overline{f},\overline{h_{1}},\overline{h_{2}}\right\}
satisfying the defining relations

(gl:1e)

\displaystyle\left[\overline{h}_{1},\overline{h}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(gl:4a)

\displaystyle\left[\overline{h}_{1},\overline{e}\right]

\displaystyle=\overline{e},

(gl:4b)

\displaystyle\left[\overline{h}_{2},\overline{e}\right]

\displaystyle=-\overline{e},

(gl:4c)

\displaystyle\left[\overline{h}_{1},\overline{f}\right]

\displaystyle=-\overline{f},

(gl:4d)

\displaystyle\left[\overline{h}_{2},\overline{f}\right]

\displaystyle=\overline{f},

(gl:5)

\displaystyle\left[\overline{e},\overline{f}\right]

\displaystyle=\overline{h}_{1}-\overline{h}_{2}.

In the usual matrix representation of
\gl_{2}

\overline{e}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\overline{f}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,

\overline{h}_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\

,

\overline{h}_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\

.

Similarly,
for a group in the isomorphism class of

,
we define

to be elements

\overline{X}(u),\overline{Y}(u),\overline{H}_{1}(s),\overline{H}_{2}(s),\overline{w}(s),\overline{w}
for

and

satisfying the defining relations

(GLH:0)

\displaystyle\overline{w}(s)

\displaystyle:=\overline{X}(s)\overline{Y}\left(-s^{-1}\right)\overline{X}(s),\quad\overline{w}:=\overline{w}(1),

(GLH:1a)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{1}(s)\overline{H}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{H}_{1}(st),

(GLH:1b)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{2}(s)\overline{H}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{H}_{2}(st),

(GLH:2)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{1}(s)\overline{H}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{H}_{2}(t)\overline{H}_{1}(s),

(GL:1a)

\displaystyle\overline{X}(u)\overline{X}(v)

\displaystyle=\overline{X}(u+v),

(GL:1b)

\displaystyle\overline{Y}(u)\overline{Y}(v)

(GL:2)

\displaystyle\overline{Y}(-t)\overline{X}(s)\overline{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{X}\left(-t^{-1}\right)\overline{Y}\left(-t^{2}s\right)\overline{X}\left(t^{-1}\right),

(GL:3)

\displaystyle\overline{w}(s)\overline{w}

\displaystyle=\overline{H}_{1}\left(-s\right)\overline{H}_{2}\left(-s^{-1}\right).

(GL:4a)

(GL:4b)

\displaystyle=\overline{X}(-u),

(GL:5a)

\displaystyle\overline{w}\overline{H}_{1}(s)\overline{w}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\overline{H}_{2}(s),

(GL:5b)

\displaystyle\overline{w}\overline{H}_{2}(s)\overline{w}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\overline{H}_{1}(s),

(GL:6a)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{1}(s)\overline{X}(u)\overline{H}_{1}(s)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\overline{X}(su),

(GL:6b)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{2}(s)\overline{X}(u)\overline{H}_{2}(s)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\overline{X}\left(s^{-1}u\right),

(GL:6c)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{1}(s)\overline{Y}(u)\overline{H}_{1}(s)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\overline{Y}\left(s^{-1}u\right),

(GL:6d)

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{2}(s)\overline{Y}(u)\overline{H}_{2}(s)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\overline{Y}(su),

(GL:7)

\displaystyle\overline{Y}(s)

\displaystyle=\overline{X}\left(s^{-1}\right)\overline{H}_{1}\left(-s^{-1}\right)\overline{H}_{2}\left(-s\right)\overline{w}\overline{X}\left(s^{-1}\right).

\GL_{2}({\mathbb{C}})

\overline{X}(u)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&u\\

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{L}
be a Lie algebra and suppose
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{L}

is a

subalgebra with standard basis
\left\{\overline{e},\overline{f},\overline{h_{1}},\overline{h_{2}}\right\}
.
Let

.
If the series

\displaystyle\overline{X}(u)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left({u}\ad\left(\overline{e}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\overline{Y}(u)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left({u}\ad\left(\overline{f}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\log(s)\ad\left(\overline{h}_{1})\right)\right),

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{2}(s)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\log(s)\ad\left(\overline{h}_{2}\right)\right),

converge, then they extend to a standard generating set for a subgroup of

Proof.

Extend the generating set by taking

. The result now follows by the usual properties of exponentials.

Proposition 4.3

\displaystyle Y_{-1}(u):

\displaystyle H_{1}(s):

\displaystyle e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle\mapsto s^{(\ell+1)}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle\mapsto s^{-(\ell+1)}f_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle H_{2}(s):

\displaystyle e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle\mapsto s^{(j-\ell)}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle\mapsto s^{-(j-\ell)}f_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{-1}:

\displaystyle e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1)^{j-1-\ell}e_{j-1-\ell,jk},

\displaystyle f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1)^{j-1-\ell}f_{j-1-\ell,jk}.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.11
, the action of
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(-1)
on
\bigoplus_{(j,k)\in I^{\im}}V^{+}_{jk}
is equivalent to the action of
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
on
\left({\mathbb{C}}[X,Y]\right)^{*}
with

e_{\ell,jk}\mapsto\left(X^{\ell}Y^{j-1-\ell}\right)^{*}
. Here
\left(X^{a}Y^{b}\right)^{*}
denotes the map
X^{c}Y^{d}\mapsto\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}X^{a}Y^{b}
in
\left({\mathbb{C}}[X,Y]\right)^{*}
. Similarly the action on
\bigoplus_{(j,k)\in I^{\im}}V^{-}_{jk}
is equivalent to the action on
\left({\mathbb{C}}[X,Y]\right)^{*}
with

f_{\ell,jk}\mapsto\left(X^{j-1-\ell}Y^{\ell}\right)^{*}
.
The formulas given are now the standard action of
\GL_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
on
\left({\mathbb{C}}[X,Y]\right)^{*}
.
Explicitly

\displaystyle\ad(e_{-1})^{i}e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(\ell+i)!}{\ell!}e_{\ell+i,jk}&\text{if $0\leq i<j-\ell$,}\\
0&\text{if $i\geq j-\ell$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ad(f_{-1})^{i}e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(j-\ell-1+i)!}{(j-\ell-1)!}e_{\ell-i,jk}&\text{if $0\leq i\leq\ell$,}\\
0&\text{if $i>\ell$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ad(h_{1})^{i}e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=(\ell+1)^{i}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\ad(h_{2})^{i}e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=(j-\ell)^{i}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\ad(e_{-1})^{i}f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(j-\ell-1+i)!}{(j-\ell-1)!}f_{\ell-i,jk}&\text{if $0\leq i\leq\ell$,}\\
0&\text{if $i>\ell$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ad(f_{-1})^{i}f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(\ell+i)!}{\ell!}e_{\ell+i,jk}&\text{if $0\leq i<j-\ell$,}\\
0&\text{if $i\geq j-\ell$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ad(h_{1})^{i}f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}(\ell+1)^{i}f_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\ad(h_{2})^{i}f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}(j-\ell)^{i}e_{\ell,jk}.

Hence

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(e_{-1}))e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j-\ell-1}\binom{\ell+i}{i}a^{i}e_{\ell+i,jk}=\sum_{m=\ell}^{j-1}\binom{m}{\ell}a^{m-\ell}e_{m,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(f_{-1}))e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\binom{j-\ell-1+i}{i}a^{i}e_{\ell-i,jk}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{j-1-m}{j-1-\ell}a^{\ell-m}e_{m,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(h_{1}))e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=e^{(\ell+1)a}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(h_{2}))e_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=e^{(j-\ell)a}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(e_{-1}))f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\binom{j-\ell-1+i}{i}a^{i}f_{\ell-i,jk}=\sum_{m=\ell}^{j-1}\binom{m}{\ell}a^{m-\ell}f_{m,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(f_{-1}))f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j-\ell-1}\binom{\ell+i}{i}a^{i}f_{\ell+i,jk}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{j-1-m}{j-1-\ell}a^{\ell-m}f_{m,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(h_{1}))f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=e^{-(\ell+1)a}e_{\ell,jk},

\displaystyle\exp(a\ad(h_{2}))f_{\ell,jk}

\displaystyle=e^{-(j-\ell)a}e_{\ell,jk}.
