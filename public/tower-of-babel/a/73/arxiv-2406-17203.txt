On the exponential algebraic geometry
Boris Kazarnovskii

Abstract
. The set of roots of any finite system of exponential sums in the space ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} is called an exponential variety. We define the intersection index of varieties of complementary dimensions, and the ring of classes of numerical equivalence of exponential varieties with operations "addition-union" and "multiplication-intersection". This ring is analogous to the ring of conditions of the torus ( ℂ ∖ 0 ) n (\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n} and is called the ring of conditions of ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} . We provide its description in terms of convex geometry. Namely we associate an exponential variety with an element of a certain ring generated by convex polyhedra in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} . We call this element the Newtonization of the exponential variety. For example, the Newtonization of an exponential hypersurface is its Newton polyhedron. The Newtonization map defines an isomorphism of the ring of conditions to the ring generated by convex polyhedra in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} . It follows, in particular, that the intersection index of n n exponential hypersurfaces is equal to the mixed pseudo-volume of their Newton polyhedra.



1.
Introduction.

1.1.
Exponential Sums

Let
\Lambda\subset{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
be a finite set of linear functionals in the complex vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Recall that a function on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the form:

f(z)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda,\>0\neq c_{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0}c_{\lambda}\>{\rm e}^{\lambda(z)},

is called an exponential sum.
The finite set
{\rm supp\>}(f)=\Lambda
and it’s convex hull
\Gamma(f)=\text{conv}(\Lambda)
are respectively called the support and the Newton polytope of the exponential sum
f
.
Exponential sums form a ring.

By external signs, the ring of exponential sums resembles the ring of Laurent polynomials.
For example, these rings are generated respectively by characters of the additive group
\mathbb{C}_{+}^{n}
of the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and by characters of the complex torus
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}
.
However, finding algebraic-geometric properties of the ring of exponential sums is a difficult task.
The first result in this direction was obtained in
[
1
]
:
if the quotient of two exponential sums in one variable is an entire function, then this function is also an exponential sum.

Note that there is no division with a remainder
in the ring of exponential sums of one variable.
Accordingly, Ritt’s theorem is not obvious.
For many variables, the statement is proven in
[
2
]
.
An important distinction between exponential sums and polynomials is the infinity of the set of zeros of any non-invertible exponential sum of one variable.
For example, the set of zeros of the exponential sum
\mathrm{e}^{2\pi iz}-1
is
\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore, the existence of a common zero of two exponential sums does not imply the existence of a common divisor.
Indeed, exponential sums
\>{\rm e}^{z}-1
and
\>{\rm e}^{2\pi iz}-1
, which share the common zero at
z=0
,
are coprime because otherwise,
the set of common zeros would be infinite.
The сonjecture about the finiteness of the set
of common zeros of two coprime exponential sums, possibly coming from J. Ritt, is currently far from being proven.

If one of the exponential sums is
\>{\rm e}^{2\pi iz}-1
,
then the Ritt’s assumption
is a statement about the set of integer solutions of an exponential equation.
In this case, the assumption follows from the statement known as the ‘‘Mordell-Lang conjecture’’ for the complex torus
[
3
]
;
see also
[
4
]
.

1.2.
Exponential Varieties

The zero set of a
finitely generated ideal
of the ring of exponential sums is called an
exponential variety
(abbreviated as E-variety).
The first small step in algebraic geometry of E-varieties was made in
[
5
]
,
where the definition of the
algebraic dimension

{\rm dim_{a}}X
of an E-variety
X
was introduced.
Accordingly,
{\rm codim_{a}}X=n-{\rm dim_{a}}X
is called the algebraic codimension of
X
.
The algebraic dimension may turn out to be negative.
For example, the algebraic dimension of an E-variety in
\mathbb{C}^{1}

defined by the equations
\mathrm{e}^{iz}-1=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi iz}-1=0
is
-1
.
The algebraic dimension
{\rm dim_{a}}X
does not exceed the dimension of
X
as a closed analytic subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
An irreducible component
(X,z)
of the germ of the E-variety
X
at the point
z

is called
atypical
if its dimension as an analytic set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is greater than
{\rm dim_{a}}X
.

Example 1.1
.

Let a non-empty E-variety
X
be defined by the equations
f=g=0
.
Then, if the exponential sums
f
and
g
have no common divisor in the ring of exponential sums,
then
{\rm codim_{a}}X=2
; otherwise
{\rm codim_{a}}X=1
.
In particular, the point
z=0
is an atypical component of the E-variety
defined by the equations
\mathrm{e}^{iz}-1=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi iz}-1=0
in
\mathbb{C}^{1}
.
In
[
5
]
, it is proven that any atypical component of an E-variety of algebraic codimension two is an affine subspace.
In
[
5
]
the assumption was also made about the geometry of atypical components of E-varieties of arbitrary codimension.
The assumption was proven by B. Zilber in
[
6
]
;
see Theorem
2.1
in §
2
.

The concept of irreducibility for E-varieties is not defined.
A partial replacement is the concept of
equidimensionality
.
Recall that an algebraic variety is sometimes called equidimensional if the dimensions of its irreducible components are the same.
The concept of equidimensionality for E-varieties is defined in §
2
.
Every E-variety is a union of equidimensional E-varieties of different algebraic dimensions.
The set of equidimensional components of an E-variety is uniquely determined.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\leq n
and

\bullet

\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{k}
are finite subsets of the exponent space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}

\bullet

S(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{k})
is the space of systems of
k
exponential sums
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
such that
{\rm supp\>}(f_{i})\subset\Lambda_{i}

\bullet

Y(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k})
is an
E
-variety defined by the equations
f_{1}=\ldots=f_{k}=0
.

Then there exists the
E
-hypersurface
\mathfrak{D}

in
S(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{k})
(that is the
E
-variety defined by a single equation)
such that

\forall(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k})\notin\mathfrak{D}\colon\>{\rm codim_{a}}(Y(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}))=k

The equation of the E-hypersurface
\mathfrak{D}
can be seen as the exponential analog of the resultant of a system of
k
polynomial equations.
The proof of the theorem is provided at the end of §
2
.

Below, we 1) define the intersection index of exponential varieties, and 2) construct the ring of classes of numerical equivalence of exponential cycles in the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

with operations of "addition-union" and "multiplication-intersection".
This ring is analogous to the ring of conditions of the complex torus
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}
or, more generally,
to the ring of conditions of a spherical homogeneous space.
It is called the ring of conditions of the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We establish the ring isomorphism
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\mathfrak{S}{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
where
\mathfrak{S}{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the ring defined in §
3
,
generated by convex polytopes located in the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

The similar ring isomorphism for algebraic varieties in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}
is constructed in
[
7
]
:
the ring of conditions of the torus
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}
is isomorphic to the subring in the ring of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
generated by the Newton polytopes of algebraic hypersurfaces.
The image of a variety in the ring of convex bodies is called the Newtonization of the algebraic variety; see §
4.1
.
Similarly, the image of an E-variety under the isomorphism
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})

is called the Newtonization of the E-variety; see Definition
4.3
.
The Newtonization of an exponential hypersurface is its Newton polytope; see Definitions
4.2
and
4.3
.

A functional
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{n}{}^{*}
is called real if its values on the space
{\rm Re}\>{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
are real.
An exponential sum with real exponents is called quasi-algebraic.
Quasi-algebraic exponential sums also form a ring.
If the ideal of equations of an E-variety is generated by quasi-algebraic exponential sums,
then the E-variety is also called quasi-algebraic.
Classes of equivalence containing linear combinations of quasi-algebraic E-varieties form a subring
of the ring
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
called the
quasi-algebraic ring of conditions
of the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
In the quasi-algebraic case, the geometry of E-varieties is simpler than in the general case.
In particular, in this case, the structure of the ring of conditions is close to the structure of the ring of conditions of the complex torus:
the quasi-algebraic ring of conditions is isomorphic to the ring
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

generated by convex polytopes in the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; see §
3
.
(Recall that the ring of conditions of the torus
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}
is generated by polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with vertices at lattice points, see
[
7
]
.)
The quasi-algebraic ring of conditions is constructed in
[
8
]
.
A detailed announcement of the construction was previously published in
[
7
, Chapter 6]
.

1.3.
The Intersection Index of Exponential Varieties

In algebraic geometry, the intersection index typically arises as the number of points of a zero-dimensional algebraic variety.
However,
the set of points of a zero-dimensional E-variety is infinite.
We define
the weak density
d_{w}(X)
of zero-dimensional
E
-variety
X
as an analog of the number of points of zero-dimensional algebraic variety;
see Definition
5.1
.

Using the concept of the weak density,
will define
the real-valued intersection index

I(X,Y)

for E-varieties
X
and
Y
such that
{\rm dim_{a}}X+{\rm dim_{a}}Y=n
;
see Definition
5.2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For equidimensional
E
-varieties
X
and
Y
such that
{\rm dim_{a}}X+{\rm dim_{a}}Y=n
,
there exists an exponential hypersurface
Z\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that,
for all
z\not\in Z
, the following is true:
1)
{\rm dim_{a}}\left((z+X)\cap Y\right)=0
, and 2)
d_{w}((z+X)\cap Y)=I(X,Y)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be exponential hypersurfaces defined by the equations
f_{1}=\ldots=f_{n}=0
,
where
f_{i}
is an exponential sum with Newton polytope
\Delta_{i}
.
Then the intersection index
I(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
of the exponential hypersurfaces
X_{i}
is equal
to the mixed pseudovolume of the Newton polytopes

\Delta_{i}
of exponential sums
f_{i}
, multiplied by
n!
;
see Definition
1.3
in §
1.4
.

The proof of the theorem is given at the end of §
5
.
Previous versions of Theorem
1.3
can be found in
[
19
,
21
,
20
,
22
]
.

In the first part of the article, consisting of §
2
-
5
,
all the concepts listed above are defined,
and a construction of the ring of conditions of the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
based on the concept of intersection index of E-varieties is provided.
However, the concept of intersection index from Definition
5.2
weakly corresponds to the familiar notions of intersection index of algebraic varieties.
Properties of the intersection index, restoring the traditional context,
are presented in the second part of the article, consisting of §
6
-
8
.
In this part, the theory of toric varieties is essential for the exposition.

1.4.
Ring of Conditions of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

Let
\mathcal{C}(M)
be the set of classes of numerical equivalence of varieties in a homogeneous algebraic variety
M
.
Sometimes on the set
\mathcal{C}(M)
, a ring structure can be correctly defined with the operations of "addition-union" and "multiplication-intersection".
Then the ring
\mathcal{C}(M)
is called the
ring of conditions for a homogeneous space

M
.
This ring is an analog of the Chow ring of an algebraic variety; see
[
14
]
.
If the variety
M
is not compact,
then compared to the Chow ring, the ring of conditions typically contains significantly more information about the intersections of algebraic cycles.
The ring of conditions was first constructed in the works
[
24
,
23
]
for symmetric spaces.
Subsequently, this construction was extended to arbitrary spherical varieties of linear reductive groups.
If the homogeneous space
M
of a linear reductive group is not spherical,
then the algorithm for constructing the ring of conditions
\mathcal{C}(M)
from
[
24
,
23
]
is not applicable; see
[
25
]
.
Below, we consider
exponential cycles
, i.e., linear combinations of E-varieties in the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with real coefficients,
and construct the corresponding ring of conditions
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
It is worth noting that the same ring
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}^{n})

can be constructed using only integer coefficients in the mentioned linear combinations;
see property
(
\bf R_{2}
)
below.

Definition 1.1
.

We will call equidimensional E-varieties
X,Y
of algebraic codimension
k

numerically equivalent
if
I(X,Z)=I(Y,Z)
for any E-variety
Z
of algebraic dimension
k
.
All E-varieties of algebraic codimension
>n
are also considered numerically equivalent.

The definition of the ring of conditions is based on the following statement.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any equidimensional
E
-varieties
X,Y
, there exist classes of numerical equivalence
\Pi(X,Y)
,
\Sigma(X,Y)
such
that for some
E
-hypersurface
\mathfrak{D}(X,Y)
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
depending on
X,Y
, the following is true.
If
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus\mathfrak{D}(X,Y)
, then

(i)
all
E
-varieties
X\cap(z+Y)
are equidimensional and belong to the class
\Pi(X,Y)
;

(ii)
if
{\rm codim_{a}}X={\rm codim_{a}}Y
, then all
E
-varieties
X\cup(z+Y)
are equidimensional and belong to the class
\Sigma(X,Y)
;

(iii)
the classes
\Pi(X,Y)
,
\Sigma(X,Y)
depend only on the classes of numerical equivalence containing
X,Y
.

The proof of the theorem is provided in §
5
.
For equidimensional E-variety
X
, we denote it’s equivalence class as
\kappa(X)
.
We define
\kappa(X)+\kappa(Y)=\Sigma(X,Y)
and
\kappa(X)\cdot\kappa(Y)=\Pi(X,Y)
.
It follows from Theorem
1.4
that the classes of numerical equivalence form a commutative associative semiring,
graded by algebraic codimensions of E-varieties.

Definition 1.2
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{k}
the real vector space consisting of linear combinations of classes of numerical equivalence of equidimensional E-varieties with algebraic codimension
k
.
The commutative graded algebra
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})=\mathcal{C}_{0}+\ldots+\mathcal{C}_{n}
is called the ring of conditions for the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

The equivalence classes containing linear combinations of quasialgebraic E-varieties form a subring of the ring
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
called the
quasialgebraic ring of conditions for the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
The quasi-algebraic ring of conditions is isomorphic to the ring
\mathfrak{S}_{\text{vol}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
generated by convex polytopes in the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; see §
3
.
The construction of the quasi-algebraic ring of conditions was published in
[
8
]
.
A detailed overview of it can be found in
[
7
, Part 6]
.

Here are some of the main properties of the ring of conditions
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
.

(R1)

If the Newton polytopes of exponential sums
f
and
g
differ by a parallel shift in the space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
,
then the E-hypersurfaces
X
and
Y
defined by the equations
f=0
and
g=0
are numerically equivalent.
The converse is not true; see Example
1.2
(1).

(R2)

Any element of the ring
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
belongs to a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-algebra generated by the classes of numerical equivalence of exponential hypersurfaces.

(R3)

The spaces
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
are one-dimensional.
If
0
-dimensional E-varieties
X
and
Y
are numerically equivalent, then
d_{w}(X)=d_{w}(Y)
.
The weak density
d_{w}
is a coordinate in the one-dimensional space
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
If
k\neq 0
and
k\neq n
, then the vector space
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is infinite-dimensional.

(R4)

For
p+q=n
, the multiplication operation defines a non-degenerate pairing of real vector spaces
\mathcal{C}_{p}\times\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{C}_{n}\xrightarrow{d_{w}}{\mathbb{R}}
.

(R5)

Let the sum of algebraic codimensions of E-varieties
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
equals
n
,
and let
\bar{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
be the class of numerical equivalence of the E-variety
X_{i}
.
We call the intersection index of E-vartieties
X_{i}

I(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})=d_{w}(\bar{X}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\bar{X}_{k}),

where
\bar{X}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\bar{X}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
is a product of equivalence classes
\bar{X}_{i}
of
X_{i}

in the ring
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
If
X_{i}
is a zero hypersurface of the exponential sum
f_{i}
with a Newton polytope
\Delta_{i}
, then

I(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=n!\>\mathfrak{p}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}),

where
\mathfrak{p}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})
is the mixed pseudovolume of convex polytopes defined below (see definitions
1.3
and
1.4
).

Definition 1.3
.

The pseudovolume
\mathfrak{p}(\Delta)
of a polytope
\Delta\subset{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
is defined as

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\sum_{\Lambda\subset\Delta,\>\dim(\Lambda)=n}c(\Lambda)\>A(\Lambda)\>{\rm vol}_{n}(\Lambda),

where the summation is over all
n
-dimensional facets
\Lambda
of
\Delta
.
Here,

(i)
{\rm vol}_{n}(\Lambda)
is the
n
-dimensional volume of the face
\Lambda

(ii)
A(\Lambda)
is the exterior angle of the facet
\Lambda
, which is the angle of the dual cone
K_{\Lambda}

of the facet
\Lambda

(a full
n
-dimensional angle is considered equal to
1
).
Recall that
K_{\Lambda}
consists of points
w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
such that the linear functional
f(z)={\rm Re}\>z(w)
as a function on the polyhedron
\Delta
reaches a maximum simultaneously at all points of the face
\Lambda
.

(iii)
c(\Lambda)=\cos(T_{\Lambda}^{\bot},\sqrt{-1}T_{\Lambda})
is the cosine of the angle between the subspaces
T_{\Lambda}^{\bot}
and
\sqrt{-1}T_{\Lambda}
,
where
T_{\Lambda}\subset{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
is the tangent space of the facet
\Lambda
, and
T_{\Lambda}^{\bot}\subset{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
is the orthogonal complement of the space
T_{\Lambda}
.
Recall that the cosine of the angle between
n
-dimensional subspaces is, by definition, the distortion coefficient of the volume
{\rm vol}_{n}
when projected from one to the other.

Example 1.2
.

(1) The pseudovolume of a polygon in
{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
is equal to half of its perimeter, divided by
2\pi
.
Here, the length of a line segment is considered to be twice its actual length.

(2) The pseudovolume of a polytope
\Delta\subset{\rm Re}\>({{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*})
is
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}{\rm vol}_{n}(\Delta)
.

Definition 1.4
.

The mixed pseudovolume of convex polytopes
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}
in the space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
is defined as

\mathfrak{p}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\sum_{\Lambda\subset\Delta,\,\dim(\Lambda)=n}c(\Lambda)\>A(\Lambda)\>{\rm vol}_{n}(\Lambda_{1},\cdots,\Lambda_{n}),

where the summation is over
n
-dimensional faces
\Lambda
of the polyhedron-sum
\Delta=\Delta_{1}+\ldots+\Delta_{n}

(i.e.
\Delta
is the Minkowski sum of the polyhedra
\Delta_{i}
),

\Lambda_{i}\subset\Delta_{i}
– faces-summands of the
n
-dimensional face
\Lambda
(i.e.
\Lambda=\Lambda_{1}+\ldots+\Lambda_{n}
), and

{\rm vol}_{n}(\Lambda_{1},\cdots,\Lambda_{n})
is the
n
-dimensional mixed volume of the polytopes
\Lambda_{i}
.

Corollary 1.1
.

The function
\mathfrak{p}(\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\Delta_{n})
of non-negative variables
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
.
The mixed pseudovolume
\mathfrak{p}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})
is equal to the coefficient of the monomial
\lambda_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{n}
, divided by
n!
.

Corollary 1.2
.

The mixed pseudovolume is a symmetric multilinear
n
-form on the space of virtual convex polytopes in the space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
.

Let us remind that a virtual polytope is defined as the formal difference
\Delta-\Gamma
of two defined up to a translation convex polytopes
\Delta,\Gamma
.
Virtual polytopes form a real vector space with operations of ‘‘Minkowski addition’’ and ‘‘multiplication by number’’,
where
\lambda\cdot(\Delta-\Gamma)=\text{sign}(\lambda)(|\lambda|\Delta-|\lambda|\Gamma)
.

For completeness of the presentation, we will provide the criteria for the vanishing of the mixed volume and mixed pseudovolume of polytopes.

Definition 1.5
.

For a polytope
\Delta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
\dim(\Delta)
denote the dimension of the minimal affine subspace,
containing
\Delta
.
The
rank of a set of convex polytopes

\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{m}
in the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is defined as the minimum value
\dim(\Delta_{i_{1}}+\ldots+\Delta_{i_{k}})-k

over all
k\leqslant m
and all subsets
1\leqslant i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leqslant m
.

Proposition 1.1
.

[
7
, Assertion 2.1.6]

The mixed volume of

\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}
is zero if and only if the rank of the set of polytopes
\Delta_{i}
is negative.

Definition 1.6
.

For a polytope
\Delta\subset{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
, let
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\Delta)
denote the dimension of the minimal complex affine subspace, containing
\Delta
.
The
complex rank of a set of convex polytopes

\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{m}
is defined as the minimum value
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\Delta_{i_{1}}+\ldots+\Delta_{i_{k}})-k

over all
k\leqslant m
and all subsets
1\leqslant i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leqslant m
.

Proposition 1.2
.

[
28
, Corollary 3.3]

The mixed pseudovolume of polytopes
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}
in
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
is zero if and only if the complex rank of the set of polytopes
\Delta_{i}
is negative.

Corollary 1.3
.

The product of the classes of numerical equivalence of
k

E
-hypersurfaces in the ring
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
is zero if and only if the complex rank of the set of their Newton polytopes is negative.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{i}
be the Newton polytopes of the
k
E-hypersurfaces
X_{i}
. According to
(
\bf R_{3}
)
and
(
\bf R_{5}
)
,
we need to prove that the condition of negative complex rank for the set
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{k}
is equivalent to the condition:

\forall\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n-k}\subset{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}:\mathfrak{p}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{k},\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n-k})=0.

This follows from Definition
1.6
and Proposition

Remark 1.2
.

Let

Let us remind that

If

The integral of the measure

1.5.
Exponential Sums as Laurent Polynomials

We consider exponential sums as Laurent polynomials on the infinite-dimensional character torus
{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}
of the additive group
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}_{+}
in the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
This approach can be traced back to the famous publication by Hermann Weyl
[
31
]
.

The torus
{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}
is the projective limit of the character tori
{\mathbb{T}}_{G}
of finitely generated subgroups
G
in the additive group of the space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
.
The projective limit of the standard windings of the tori
{\mathbb{T}}_{G}

(see definition
2.1
)
is the standard winding
\omega_{\infty}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}

of the infinite-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}
.
The ring of exponential sums consists of the preimages of Laurent polynomials on
{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}
under the map
\omega_{\infty}
.

The rings of conditions
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{T}}_{G})
of the tori
{\mathbb{T}}_{G}
form an inductive system of rings.
We denote the direct limit of the rings
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{T}}_{G})
by
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty})
.
Using the representation of the ring
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{T}}_{G})
as the ring
generated by convex polyhedra in the character space of the torus,
we get a description of the ring
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty})
as a ring,
generated by finite-dimensional convex polyhedra in the infinite-dimensional character space of the torus
{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}
.

Let
\mathfrak{R}
denote the discrete additive group of the space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
.
Then the character space of the torus
{\mathbb{T}}_{n}^{\infty}
is
\mathfrak{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}
.
Consider the extension of the identity map
\mathfrak{R}\to{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}

to a linear map of real vector spaces
\Pi\colon\mathfrak{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
.
The action of the map
\Pi
on convex polytopes in the character space extends to a homomorphism
of the ring of convex polytopes in
\mathfrak{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}
to
the ring of convex polytopes
\mathfrak{S}_{\rm vol}({{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*})

in the space
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
defined in §
3.1
.
We construct
the ideal
J_{\mathfrak{p}}
in the ring
\mathfrak{S}_{\rm vol}({{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*})

(see §
3.1
),
such that the ring of conditions of the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is isomorphic to the quotient ring

\mathfrak{S}_{\rm vol}({{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*})/J_{\mathfrak{p}}
;
see §
3.1
and §
5
, respectively.

The presentation of results is done at the finite-dimensional level.
We use the computation of the ring of conditions of the torus,
given in
[
7
]
; see also
[
18
]
.
One of the main concepts in the algebraic geometry of subvarieties of the torus is the Newton polytope of an algebraic hypersurface.
The computation of the ring of condition of the torus from
[
7
]

can be regarded as the construction of an analog of the Newton polytope for an arbitrary algebraic variety:
a variety is associated with an element of a certain ring,
generated by convex polytopes with vertices in the character lattice of the torus.
We call this element the
Newtonization of an algebraic variety
.

In §
2
, any E-variety
X
is associated with an algebraic variety in a certain multidimensional torus,
called the
model of the
E
-variety

X
.
In §
4
, we define the
Newtonization of the
E
-variety

X
as the image
of the Newtonization of the model of
X
under a certain ring homomorphism,
generated by convex polytopes;
see Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.1
.
The Newtonization of an E-variety is an element of a certain ring generated by convex polytopes in
{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}^{*}
,
defined in §
3
.

By analogy with
[
7
]
,
we interpret the Newtonization map as associating an E-variety with its numerical equivalence class,
i.e., the corresponding element in the ring of conditions of the space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
;
see §
4.2
.
Using the Newtonizations of E-varieties,
we define in §
5
the concepts of weak density and intersection index.

The definition of the intersection index of E-varieties given in §
5
is not formally related in the usual way to the notions of the set of intersection points of E-varieties of complementary dimension.
The properties of the intersection index,
restoring the traditional context, are provided in §
6
.
Their proofs in §
8
heavily rely on the theory of toric varieties.

Definition 2.1

For

to the torus

Corollary 2.1
.

The pullback map

Proof.

The image of the standard winding map is dense in the Zariski topology. This implies the desired statement.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
X
be a
G
-variety defined by equations

, where

.
The equations

-

of the E-variety

.

The map

G
-varieties and the set of algebraic varieties in

.

Definition 2.3
.

The codimension of the

-model

of the

-variety

and is called the algebraic codimension of

Accordingly,

Proposition 3.1

[

Denote the graded symmetric algebra

Remark 3.1

By definition, the weight
w(K)
belongs to the component of degree
m-k
of the graded ring
\mathfrak{S}_{\rm vol}(V^{\bot}_{K})
. Since
\dim(V^{\bot}_{K})=m-k
, the dimension of this component is one. Therefore, any chosen method of measuring volume in the space
V^{\bot}_{K}
allows assigning a numerical value
{\rm vol}_{m-k}(w(K))
to the weight
w(K)
.

Now we define the addition of weighted
k
-dimensional fans
\mathcal{K,L}
.
The set
{\rm supp\>}(\mathcal{K})\cup{\rm supp\>}(\mathcal{L})
is the union of subsets of three types:

\{K\cap L\colon\,K\in{\mathcal{K}},L\in{\mathcal{L}}\},\,\,\{K\setminus{\rm supp\>}(\mathcal{L})\colon\,K\in\mathcal{K}\},\,\,\{L\setminus{\rm supp\>}(\mathcal{K})\colon\,L\in{\mathcal{L}}\}.

The subsets of the first type are cones, and those of the second and third types are open subsets of cones.
We assign weights
w(K)+w(L)
to
k
-dimensional subsets of the specified types,
where
w(K)
and
w(L)
are the weights of the cones
K
and
L
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a fan of cones with the support
{\rm supp\>}(\mathcal{K})\cup{\rm supp\>}(\mathcal{L})
,
such that each of the specified subsets is composed entirely of cones in the fan
{\mathcal{P}}
(it is obvious that such fans exist).
We assign the weights to
k
-dimensional cones
{\mathcal{P}}
containing them.
The sums of equivalent weighted fans are equivalent.
Thus, sums of equivalence classes of weighted fans are defined.

6.1.1.

Proposition 6.1
.

[cf. Definition
1.3
]

Let

-dimensional subspace in

Set

7.1.
Tropicalizations of Algebraic Varieties

Recall that the Newton polyhedra of Laurent polynomials on the torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
are
convex polyhedra in the character space
{\rm Re}\>{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}^{*}
.
The vertices of the Newton polyhedra are points of the character lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}\subset{\rm Re}\>{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}^{*}
of the torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
.
The Newtonization
{\rm nwt}(Y)
of an algebraic variety
Y\subset({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
is an element
of the subring
\mathfrak{S}_{\rm vol}({\mathbb{Z}}^{N})
generated by the Newton polyhedra
in the ring
\mathfrak{S}_{\rm vol}({\rm Re}\>{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}^{*})
; see §
4.1
.

Definition 7.1
.

The tropicalization of the element
{\rm nwt}(Y)
(see definition
3.8
) is called the
tropicalization of the algebraic variety

Y
.

The tropicalization of the variety
Y
is, up to subdivision,
a weighted fan of cones of dimension
\dim Y
in the space
{\rm Re}\>({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
; see definition
3.2
.
Any of these fans is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{\rm trop}(Y)
and called the tropical fan of the variety
Y
.
For any pair of tropical fans of the variety,
there exists a tropical fan that is their common subdivision.
The weights of the cones in these refinements are inherited.

In the space
{\rm Re}\>({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, there is a lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
associated with the one-parameter subgroups of the torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
. All cones of the fan
\mathcal{K}_{\rm trop}(Y)
can be chosen so that for all
K\in\mathcal{K}_{\rm trop}(Y)
, the subspace
V_{K}
generated by the points of the cone
K
is rational, i.e., generated by vectors from the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
. Denote by
V_{K}^{\bot}
the character space of the quotient torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}/{\mathbb{T}}_{K}
, where
{\mathbb{T}}_{K}
is the subtorus in
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
generated by the exponents of the points of the cone
K
. We further identify the space
V_{K}^{\bot}
with the orthogonal complement of
V_{K}
in the space
{\rm Re}\>({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. Consider in
V_{K}^{\bot}
a volume form such that the volume of the fundamental cube of the character lattice is equal to one. Using this volume form, we consider the weight
w(K)
as the number
{\rm vol}_{N-k}(w(K))
; see remark
3.1
. From the Kushnirenko-Bernstein-Khovanskii theorem, it follows that the number
(N-k)!w(K)
is an integer and positive. Such tropical varieties are called algebraic. Algebraic tropical varieties form a ring with operations defined in §
3.3
.

Applying the definition of the ring of conditions of the torus and theorems
4.1
and
3.3
, we obtain the following statement.

Corollary 7.1
.

(1)
The assignment of an algebraic variety
Y
to its tropicalization extends to an isomorphism of the ring of conditions of the torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
onto the ring of algebraic tropical varieties in the space
{\rm Re}\>({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

(2)
For algebraic varieties
X,Y
in the torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
, there exists an algebraic hypersurface
D(X,Y)
in
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
such that for
g\notin D(X,Y)
, the following holds: the tropicalization of the variety
X\cap(gY)
is equal to the product of the tropicalizations of
X
and
Y
in the ring of algebraic tropical varieties.

Let

be a completion of
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
associated with the fan
\mathcal{K}_{\rm trop}(Y)
. On the algebraic variety
Y_{\rm trop}
, the action of the torus
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
is defined. The orbits of this action are in one-to-one correspondence with the cones of the fan
\mathcal{K}_{\rm trop}(Y)

coincides with the union of the quotient tori
({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{N}
over the subtori

generated by the exponents of the elements of the cones

. The orbit corresponding to the cone

can be identified with the quotient torus

. Further, we use references to statements about the properties of toric varieties from the following list.

(
tr

1

) The closure of the variety

in

is compact.

(
tr

2

in some toric completion of the torus

.

(
tr

3

of the variety

-factorization of the fan

;

.

(

4

(

Definition 7.2
.

Let

\bullet

{\mathbb{V}}
be a subtorus of
{\mathbb{T}}
of dimension

\bullet

{\mathbb{V}}_{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the identity in the torus

\bullet

{\mathbb{L}}
be a subtorus of
{\mathbb{T}}
of dimension
k

\bullet

be an open domain in the shifted subtorus
\tau{\mathbb{L}}

\bullet

U_{\varepsilon}=\{l\cdot v\in{\mathbb{T}}\>|\>l\in U,v\in{\mathbb{V}}_{\varepsilon}\}

\bullet

\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon U_{\varepsilon}\to U
be the mapping defined as
\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon l\cdot v\mapsto l

\bullet

Y
be a
k
-dimensional algebraic variety in the
N
-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}
.

We call

an
\varepsilon
-
perturbation of the domain

if the restriction

\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon Y\cap U_{\varepsilon}\to U

is a finite-sheeted covering of the domain

.
It is assumed that locally at any point of
Y\cap U_{\varepsilon}

the sheet of the covering
\pi_{\varepsilon}
may have multiplicity not equal to one.

Definition 7.3
.

Let

\bullet

K
be a
k
-dimensional convex polyhedral rational cone in the space of one-parameter subgroups
{\rm Re}\>\mathfrak{T}=i{\rm Im\>}\mathfrak{T}
.

\bullet

V_{K}
be a
k
-dimensional subspace in

.

\bullet

{\mathbb{T}}_{K}
be the subtorus of
{\mathbb{T}}
generated by the exponents of the subspace
V_{K}
.

The domain
t_{K,\tau}=\tau\exp(\sqrt{-1}V_{K})\exp(K)
in the shifted subtorus
\tau{\mathbb{T}}_{K}
is called a

toric tongue with base
K
and shift

\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Hereafter, we use the following notation.

\bullet

\mathcal{K}
be a
k
-dimensional fan of convex rational polyhedral cones in the

.

\bullet

{\rm supp\>}\mathcal{K}
be the union of the cones of the fan
\mathcal{K}
.

\bullet

\leq k-m
.

\bullet

be the

Definition 7.4
.

A finite set of pairwise disjoint toric tongues

is called an
\varepsilon
-approximating set of tongues of the variety
Y
with an approximating tropical fan

\mathcal{K}
, if the following conditions hold.

(i) Each of the tongues

t_{K,\tau}\in T_{\varepsilon}(Y)
has an
\varepsilon
-perturbation
t_{K,\tau,\varepsilon}\subset Y
.
The degree of the covering
\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon t_{K,\tau,\varepsilon}\to t_{K,\tau}
is denoted by

.
We call

the

.
The sum of the weights of all tongues with base

equals
(N-k)!\>w(K)
,
where

is the weight of the cone

.

(ii) The set of bases of the tongues

is the set of

-dimensional fan of cones
\mathcal{K}
.

(iii) If

is sufficiently large, then

(iv) The coverings
\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon t_{K,\tau,\varepsilon}\to t_{K,\tau}
are unramified.

Theorem 7.1
.

For some tropical fan
{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
,
an
\varepsilon
-approximating set of tongues

with an approximating fan
{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
exists
for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

We provide a brief outline of the proof of the theorem.

Recall that there is a one-to-one correspondence
K\Leftrightarrow O_{K}

between the cones
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
and
the orbits
O_{K}
of the torus
{\mathbb{T}}
in the toric variety
Y_{\rm trop}
.
Namely, the orbit
O_{K}
can be identified with the quotient torus
{\mathbb{T}}/{\mathbb{T}}_{K}
;
see §
7.1
.

If
\dim K=k
, then the set
\bar{Y}_{K}=\{t\}
,
consisting of the limit points of the variety
Y
on the orbit
O_{K}
,
is a zero-dimensional algebraic variety,
i.e., it is finite;
see
(
\bf tr_{3}
)
,
(
\bf tr_{4}
)
.
For a limit point
t\in\bar{Y}_{K}
, choose its preimage
\tau
under the projection
map
{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{T}}/{\mathbb{T}}_{K}
.
The set
t_{K,\tau}
is a shifted toric tongue with base
K
and shift
\tau
.
Assign the tongue
t_{K,\tau}
a multiplicity
m(t_{K,\tau})
,
equal to the multiplicity of the point
t
in the zero-dimensional algebraic variety
\tilde{Y}_{K}
.

Let
R
be sufficiently large.
For all such tongues
t_{K,\tau}
for all
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
,
choose shifts of the tongues
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that for any
k
-dimensional cone
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
and any

, 1)
{\rm Re}\>\log\tau\in K_{R}
, and 2) the distance from

to
\partial K
is greater than

Then,
if

is sufficiently large,
the
\varepsilon
-perturbations

of the tongues

exist and satisfy conditions (i), (ii), and (iii) of Definition

.
In this case,
the degree of the covering
\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon t_{K,\tau,\varepsilon}\to t_{K,\tau}
equals the multiplicity of the corresponding point

in the variety
\tilde{Y}_{K}
.
However, condition (iv) may not hold,
i.e., the corresponding covering
\pi_{\varepsilon}
of the shifted tongue

may be ramified.
To obtain the property of unramified coverings,
it is sufficient to refine the construction of the tongues as follows.

Consider the subset in

,
consisting of all branching points of all coverings
\pi_{\varepsilon}\colon t_{K,\tau,\varepsilon}\to t_{K,\tau}
.
This set belongs to some proper subvariety

in

of dimension
<k
.
Therefore, the support of the fan

belongs
to the support of the fan
{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
.
Since

there exists a subdivision

of the fan
{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm trop}(Y)
such that

is a subfan of its

-dimensional skeleton; see
(
\bf tr_{6}
)
.
The fan
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
is also a support of the tropicalization

of the variety

see §

.
Repeating the construction of the set of tongues with
the replacement of the approximating fan

by the fan

we obtain the property of coverings

Remark 7.1
.

(1) If
\dim Y=0
, then

coincides with the set of points of the variety

.

(2) Let

.
Then, if
