Francesca Scarabel, Rossana Vermiglio

Abstract
We consider nonlinear delay differential and renewal equations with infinite delay. We extend the work of Gyllenberg et al , Appl. Math. Comput. (2018) by introducing a unifying abstract framework, and derive a finite-dimensional approximating system via pseudospectral discretization. For renewal equations, we consider a reformulation in the space of absolutely continuous functions via integration. We prove the one-to-one correspondence of equilibria between the original equation and its approximation, and that linearization and discretization commute. Our most important result is the proof of convergence of the characteristic roots of the pseudospectral approximation of the linear(ized) equations when the collocation nodes are chosen as the family of scaled zeros or extrema of Laguerre polynomials. This ensures that the finite-dimensional system correctly reproduces the stability properties of the original linear equation if the dimension of the approximation is large enough. The result is illustrated with several numerical tests, which also demonstrate the effectiveness of the approach for the bifurcation analysis of equilibria of nonlinear equations. The new approach used to prove convergence also provides the exact location of the spectrum of the differentiation matrices for the Laguerre zeros and extrema, adding new insights into properties that are important in the numerical solution of differential equations by pseudospectral methods.

Introduction

According to a standard definition, “a delay equation is a rule for extending a function of time towards the future on the basis of the (assumed to be) known past”
[
20
]
.
Delay equations (DEs) include renewal equations (REs), which specify the value of the unknown function in terms of its past values, delay differential equations (DDEs), which specify the derivative, and systems coupling both
[
13
,
19
,
20
]
.

In this paper we focus on equations with infinite delay (iDEs, including iDDEs and iREs), which are widely used in mathematical biology, and in particular in ecology and epidemiology, to describe physiologically structured population models, in which the individual rates are assumed to depend on a structuring variable (e.g., age, size, time since infection) that evolves in time
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
34
,
38
]
.
In behavioral epidemic models, the infinite delay can arise to incorporate the effect of information and waning memory
[
21
,
31
]
.
Other applications of iDDEs can be found in
[
30
]
and the references therein.

Our interest focuses on the long-term behavior of the systems, and in particular on the existence and stability of equilibria and their bifurcation analysis (i.e., how these properties change under parameter variation).
Differently from ordinary differential equations (ODEs), DEs generate infinite-dimensional dynamical systems where the state space is a suitable function space, hence substantially increasing the complexity of their theoretical and numerical analysis.
One way to overcome theoretically the infinite dimensionality of the problem is via reduction to finite-dimensional inertial or center manifolds
[
23
,
28
]
.
However in this paper we take a different approach, aiming at studying
numerically
the infinite-dimensional system by reducing it to a finite-dimensional system of ODEs, whose properties can be studied with well-established software, see e.g.
[
5
]
and references therein. While in some special cases this reduction can be done exactly, e.g., via the linear chain trick

[
9
,
15
,
16
]
, for more general kernels one usually relies on approximations.

Among the various techniques available for this reduction
[
3
,
25
,
39
]
, pseudospectral discretization (PSD) has been successfully applied in the last decade both to linear(ized) problems for the stability analysis of equilibria, and to nonlinear ones for the numerical bifurcation in various contexts, including DEs with bounded delay and PDEs with nonlocal boundary conditions
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
18
,
10
,
11
,
36
,
37
]
.
For DEs, the main advantage of using PSD compared to other approximation techniques is the fast (exponential) order of convergence for the stability of the equilibria, which follows from the fact that the eigenfunctions of the infinitesimal generator of the linear(ized) equation are exponential, hence analytic.

To treat the unbounded delay, several approaches are used in the literature, including domain truncation or suitable change of variables
[
3
,
25
,
39
]
, or even quadrature rules to approximate an iDDE with a DDE with a finite number of point delays to be studied via established methods (e.g.
[
5
]
) or software like DDE-BIFTOOL
[
22
]
.
However, these approaches do not exploit some of the fundamental features of iDEs, in particular the “exponentially fading memory”, which is the major advantage of the PSD proposed here.
In fact, the step from finite to infinite delay involves several complexities, both from a theoretical and numerical point of view.

From a theoretical point of view, for iDEs the principle of linearized stability (PLS) has been proved in a space of exponentially weighted functions defined on
\mathbb{R}_{-}:=(-\infty,0]
, with weight

w(\theta):=\mathrm{e}^{\rho\theta},\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{-},

(1)

for some
\rho>0
.
Using a modification of the sun-star calculus, Diekmann and Gyllenberg show that the stability of equilibria of a nonlinear equation is determined by the part of the spectrum of the infinitesimal generator of the linearized semigroup in the right-half plane

\mathbb{C}_{\rho}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \Re\lambda>-\rho\},

which contains eigenvalues only, that are roots of a characteristic equation
[
14
, Theorem 3.15 and Theorem 4.7]
.
Recently, the PLS and the Hopf bifurcation theorem have been proved for iDDEs by applying the integrated semigroup theory in
[
30
]
. We also refer to
[
40
]
for a Hopf result in a space of bounded uniformly continuous functions. However, in this paper we keep reference to
[
14
]
as it covers iDDEs, iREs and coupled iDDEs and iREs.
Finally, we note that other weight functions can be considered
[
29
]
, but here we restrict to the exponential weight (
1
) for which the PLS has been rigorously proved.

In this theoretical framework and motivated by the accuracy, effectiveness and flexibility shown by the PSD approach for bounded delay, a detailed experimental analysis has been carried out using collocation on the scaled Laguerre nodes in
[
27
]
for iDDEs.

Here, we introduce a unifying abstract differential equation (ADE) able to capture iDDEs and iREs within the same framework.
This pragmatic representation allows us to encompass all the fundamental concepts of iDEs and the general derivation of the finite-dimensional dynamical system through the weighted version of the PSD all at once.
For iREs, we also propose a new approach inspired by
[
20
,
37
]
: via integration, we map the original iRE, defined in a space of weighted integrable functions, to an equivalent equation defined in a subspace of absolutely continuous functions on any bounded subinterval of
\mathbb{R}_{-}
.
The main advantages are twofold: point evaluation, as well as polynomial interpolation and collocation, are well defined in the new space, and the domain of the infinitesimal generator is defined by a trivial condition, simplifying the implementation.

Most importantly, here we add the key element to the numerical investigations in
[
27
]
: the rigorous proof that the PSD accurately approximates the stability properties of the equilibria. To this aim, we first show that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the equilibria of the ADE and its PSD.
Then, we focus on the linear(ized) problems and prove the convergence of the stability indicators for the zero equilibrium, i.e., the eigenvalues of the infinitesimal generator in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
.
Due to the complexities introduced by the step from finite to infinite delay and in the absence of suitable interpolation results on the semi-unbounded interval, the proof follows a slightly different route compared to the standard ones for finite delay
[
7
,
37
]
.
The new approach guarantees the convergence of the approximated eigenvalues by using as collocation nodes the zeros or extrema of standard Laguerre polynomials suitably scaled.
Moreover, the general results for the Laguerre polynomials presented in the appendix provide an exact description of the location of the eigenvalues of the differentiation matrix with boundary condition built on the scaled Laguerre zeros and extrema.

The paper is organized as follows.
In
section

2
we recall the basic results of stability theory for iDEs and introduce their abstract formulation.
The unifying abstract framework is introduced in
section

3
, together with the PSD approach. In
section

4
, after deriving the characteristic equations, we show the one-to-one correspondence of equilibria of the original ADE and its finite-dimensional approximation, and prove the convergence of the characteristic roots. To improve readability, the results used in the proofs are collected in
appendix

A
for the zeros and extrema of the standard Laguerre polynomials in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Finally,
section

5
illustrates by numerical experiments the theoretical convergence of the eigenvalues for linear iDEs and the flexibility of the approach for numerical bifurcation analysis of equilibria of nonlinear iDEs.

2
Abstract formulation of nonlinear iDEs

In this section we recall from
[
14
]
some basic results for iDDEs and iREs, including the PLS for the stability of equilibria. In view of
section

3
, we also show how both iDDEs and iREs (and, by combining the two approaches, systems coupling both) can be formally written as semilinear ADEs.
Although we typically consider real-valued nonlinear equations and real-valued solutions, to apply spectral theory we assume that real Banach spaces are embedded into complex ones and all operators are extended to complex spaces. For further details on the complexification process we refer to
[
19
, Chapter III.7]
.

Let
\rho>0
and consider the weight function (
1
).
For
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d>0
, we denote with
|\cdot|
a norm in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Let
L^{\infty}_{\rho}:=L^{\infty}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the space of (the equivalence classes of) functions
\varphi
such that
|w\varphi|
is essentially bounded in
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, and
L^{p}_{\rho}:=L^{p}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
1\leq p<+\infty
, that of
\varphi
such that
w|\varphi|^{p}
is integrable, equipped with the norm

\|\varphi\|_{p,\rho}:=\|w\varphi\|_{p}=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}w(\theta)|\varphi(\theta)|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&1\leq p<+\infty,\\[5.0pt]
{\rm ess}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{-}}w(\theta)|\varphi(\theta)|,&p=\infty.\end{cases}

Note that
wL^{p}_{\rho}\cong L^{p}
, but we will often write
w
explicitly to keep reference to the original state space of the dynamical system.

To ease notation, we write
w\psi(\theta)
in place of
w(\theta)\psi(\theta)
.
We consider column vectors and use the convention of writing them as rows, but separating each column block-vector elements with semicolons.
We will also often abuse notation by using a symbol to denote both a constant function and the value it takes on.

2.1
Delay differential equations

A
d
-dimensional nonlinear iDDE is

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=F(y_{t}),\quad t\geq 0,

(2)

where
y_{t}(\theta):=y(t+\theta)
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
, is the “history function” at time
t\geq 0.

We assume that
F\colon\mathcal{C}_{\rho}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuously Fréchet differentiable map, where
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}:=\mathcal{C}_{0,\rho}(\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the Banach space of all functions
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}_{-}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
w\psi
is continuous and vanishes at
-\infty
, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\rho}
.

To represent (
2
) in the abstract framework, we consider the space

X:=w\mathbb{C}^{d}\times wL_{\rho}^{\infty}

equipped with the norm
\|(w\alpha;w\psi)\|_{X}=\max\{|\alpha|,\|w\psi\|_{\infty}\}.

By defining the embedding
j\colon\mathcal{C}_{\rho}\to X
as

j\psi:=(w\psi(0);w\psi)
, we identify
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
with the subspace
Y:=j(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})
of
X
.
Note that, by interpreting
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
as a constant function in
L_{\rho}^{\infty},
we have that
X\subset wL_{\rho}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}})
with
\bar{d}=2d
.

By defining
u(t):=jy_{t},
it is easy to verify that
u
satisfies the semilinear ADE

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}u(t)+\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}(u(t)),\quad t\geq 0,

(3)

where the linear unbounded operator
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}})\to X
is

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}(w\alpha;w\psi)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}(w\alpha;w\psi)-\rho(w\alpha;w\psi)=(0;w\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}),\\[8.0pt]
D(\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}})=\{(w\alpha;w\psi)\mid\psi\text{ is Lipschitz continuous, }\psi(0)=\alpha\},\end{array}

(4)

and the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}\colon Y\to X
is

\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}(w\alpha;w\psi)=(wF(j^{-1}(w\alpha,w\psi));0)=(wF(\psi);0).

(5)

Here,
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}
captures the “shift” of the solution,
\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}
captures the “extension” according to the rule defined by (
2
), while both account for “weight multiplication”. Note that the
\theta
-derivative in (
4
) should be interpreted as almost everywhere.

Now we consider the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}.
The canonical basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
determines
d
linearly independent vectors
\xi_{i}=(e_{i};0)
,
i=1,\dots,d,
and
\Xi_{d}:=\text{span}\{w\xi_{i}\mid i=1,...,d\}\subset X.
By defining
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\text{DDE}}(w\alpha;w\psi)=F_{i}(\psi)
, we get the finite-rank representation

\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}=w\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\text{DDE}}\xi_{i}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}
inherits the regularity properties of
F
: if
F
is continuously Fréchet differentiable, so is
\mathcal{F}^{\text{DDE}}
.

We are interested in the stability properties of an equilibrium
\bar{\psi}
of (
2
), which is a constant solution
\bar{\psi}
such that
F(\bar{\psi})=0
. The linearization of (
2
) at
\bar{\psi}
is

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=DF(\bar{\psi})y_{t},\quad t\geq 0,

(6)

where
DF(\bar{\psi})
is the Fréchet derivative of
F
at
\bar{\psi}
.

From (
4
)–(
5
) we get that
\bar{u}=(w\bar{\psi};w\bar{\psi})
is an equilibrium of (
3
) and vice versa. Moreover by introducing the linear operator
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DDE}}(w\alpha;w\psi)=(wDF(\bar{\psi})\psi;0)
, the abstract formulation of (
6
) is

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}u(t)+\mathcal{L}^{\text{DDE}}u(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(7)

By the PLS
[
14
, Theorem 4.7]
, the stability of
\bar{\psi}
is determined by the stability of the zero solution of (
6
) as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bar{\psi}
be an equilibrium of (
2
) and
\bar{u}=(w\bar{\psi};w\bar{\psi})
the corresponding equilibrium of (
3
). Then

•

if all the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DDE}}:=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{DDE}}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
have negative real part, then
\bar{u}
is exponentially stable;

•

if at least one eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DDE}}
has positive real part, then
\bar{u}
is unstable;

•

the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DDE}}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
correspond to roots of the characteristic equation

\det(\lambda I_{d}-DF(\bar{\psi})\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\cdot})=0

in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
, which are at most finitely many.

Given an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DDE}}
, its eigenfunction is of the form
(w\alpha;w\psi_{\lambda})
, where
\psi_{\lambda}(\theta):=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\theta}\,\alpha
, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

2.2
Renewal equations

Consider now the
d
-dimensional nonlinear iRE

y(t)=F(y_{t}),\quad t>0,

(8)

for
y_{t}\in L^{1}_{\rho}
, where
F\colon L^{1}_{\rho}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuously Fréchet differentiable map.
In particular, this assumption means that we only consider equations (
8
) such that their linearization is a linear Volterra integral equations, ruling out neutral REs with point delays.

To derive the abstract equation, we define the space
X:=wL^{1}_{\rho}
and the embedding
j:L^{1}_{\rho}\to X
given by

j\varphi(\theta):=w\int_{0}^{\theta}\varphi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
, for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
.
Note that

\|j\varphi\|_{X}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{e}^{\rho\theta}\int_{\theta}^{0}|\varphi(s)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{-\infty}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{\rho\theta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\ |\varphi(s)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{\|w\varphi\|_{1}}{\rho},

and
Y:=jL^{1}_{\rho}=\{w\varphi\in X\mid w\varphi=j\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}\text{ and }w\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}\in X\}
coincides with the subspace
AC_{0}:=AC_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{C}^{d})
of all absolutely continuous functions
w\varphi\in X
in any closed subset of
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, such that
w\varphi(0)=0.
Note that, for
w\varphi\in Y
,
j^{-1}(w\varphi)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}
.

By defining the state
u(t):=jy_{t}
, we can formally derive the ADE

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{RE}}u(t)+\mathcal{F}^{\text{RE}}(u(t)),\quad t\geq 0,

(9)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{RE}}(w\varphi)=\textstyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}}(w\varphi)-\rho w\varphi=w\textstyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}},

\displaystyle w\varphi\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{RE}})=Y,

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\text{RE}}(w\varphi)=-wF(j^{-1}w\varphi)=-wF(\textstyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}}),

\displaystyle w\varphi\in Y.

(11)

Similarly as before,
\mathcal{F}^{\text{RE}}
has finite rank with range
\Xi_{d}=w\mathbb{R}^{d}.

As expected, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the equilibria
\bar{\varphi}
of (
8
), which are constant functions such that
\bar{\varphi}=F(\bar{\varphi}),
and the equilibria
\bar{u}=j\bar{\varphi}
of (
9
). Moreover, by defining
\mathcal{L}^{\text{RE}}:=-wDF(\bar{\varphi})j^{-1},
the abstract formulation of the linearized RE

y(t)=DF(\bar{\varphi})y_{t}
,
t>0
, gives the linearized equation

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{RE}}u(t)+\mathcal{L}^{\text{RE}}u(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(12)

The following PLS holds
[
14
, Theorem 3.9]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\bar{\varphi}
be an equilibrium of (
8
) and
\bar{u}=j\bar{\varphi}
the corresponding equilibrium of (
11
). Then

•

if all the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{RE}}:=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{RE}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{RE}}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
have negative real part, then
\bar{u}
is exponentially stable;

•

if at least one eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{RE}}
has positive real part, then
\bar{u}
is unstable;

•

the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{RE}}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
correspond to zeros of characteristic equation

\det(I_{d}-DF(\bar{\varphi})\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\cdot})=0

in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho},
which are at most finitely many.

Given an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\text{RE}}
, the corresponding eigenfunction is
w\varphi_{\lambda}
, where
\varphi_{\lambda}(\theta):=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\cdot}-1}{\lambda}\alpha
, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Note also that
j^{-1}(w\varphi_{\lambda})=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\cdot}\alpha
.

3
A unifying abstract framework

We now propose a unifying abstract framework which is particularly useful to derive the approximating finite-dimensional system via PSD and to analyse the convergence in
section

4
.
We consider a general framework where
X
is a Banach space of
\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}
-valued functions defined on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
equipped with a non-weighted
p
-norm,
1\leq p\leq+\infty,
denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{X}.
In other words,
X
is a closed subspace of
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}).

As before, let
\rho>0
and
w
be the function (
1
). Consider the semilinear ADE

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}u(t)+\mathcal{F}(u(t)),\quad t\geq 0,

(13)

where
\mathcal{A}_{0}\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{0})\to X
is the linear unbounded operator

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}_{0}x=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}-\rho x=w\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}(\frac{x}{w}),\quad x\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0}),\\[8.0pt]
D(\mathcal{A}_{0})=\{x\in X\mid\mathcal{A}_{0}x\in X\text{ and }x(0)=\mathcal{B}x\},\end{array}

(14)

with
\mathcal{B}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
linear and bounded.

Remark 3
.

The operators
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{DDE}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\text{RE}}
defined in (
4
) and (
10
) are particular instances of (
14
) with
\mathcal{B}(w\alpha,w\psi)=(\alpha;\alpha)
and
\mathcal{B}(w\varphi)=0
, respectively.
In particular, the form of
\mathcal{B}
characterizes the type of iDE under investigation.

Note that
D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
is a subset of the Sobolev space
W^{p,1}(\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}),
hence it admits one (and only one) continuous representative
[
8
]
.
We assume that
\mathcal{F}\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{0})\to X
is continuously Fréchet differentiable and admits the finite-rank representation

\mathcal{F}(x)=w\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{F}_{i}(x)\xi_{i}\in\Xi_{d}

(15)

for some
\mathcal{F}_{i}\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{0})\to\mathbb{R}
sufficiently smooth,
\xi_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
, linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}
, and
\Xi_{d}:=w\,\text{span}\{\xi_{i}\mid i=1,...,d\}.

The continuity of the elements of
D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
, where we assume that
u(t)
lives for
t\geq 0
, justifies the choice of the PSD approach, which requires point evaluation to be well defined.

3.1
Reduction to finite dimension via PSD

Given a positive integer
N
, called
discretization index
, we consider a set of
N
distinct points in
\mathbb{R}_{-}
,

\Theta_{N}:=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N}\mid\theta_{N}<\dots<\theta_{1}<0\}.

We refer to the set
\Theta_{N}
as the
collocation nodes
.
Then, we represent every
x\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
by a set of vectors
X_{j}=x(\theta_{j})
,
j=1,\dots,N
via the
restriction operator

\mathcal{R}_{N}x=(X_{1};\dots;X_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\bar{d}}.

The operator
\mathcal{R}_{N}
is complemented with a
prolongation operator

\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
that maps
\mathbb{R}^{N\bar{d}}
to the space
D(\mathcal{A}_{0})\cap\Pi_{N}^{\bar{d}}
, where
\Pi_{N}^{\bar{d}}
is the space of
\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}
-valued polynomials of degree at most
N
.

\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
is defined such that
x_{N,0}=\mathcal{P}_{N,0}(X_{1};\dots;X_{N})
satisfies the implicit condition
x_{N,0}(0)=\mathcal{B}x_{N,0}
.
In practice, we first define the extended mesh

\Theta_{N,0}:=\Theta_{N}\cup\{\theta_{0}=0\}

and consider the
N+1
Lagrange polynomials
\ell_{i,0}
,
i=0,\dots,N
, associated with
\Theta_{N,0}
.
Then, we take

x_{N,0}=\beta\,\ell_{0,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\ell_{i,0},

with
\beta
implicitly defined by
x_{N,0}(0)=\mathcal{B}x_{N,0}
.
With these choices,

\mathcal{A}_{0}\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
maps into
w\Pi_{N-1}^{\bar{d}}
and the application of
\mathcal{R}_{N}
is well defined.

Using
\mathcal{R}_{N}
and
\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
we can derive the discrete versions of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}
, namely

\mathcal{A}_{0,N}:=\mathcal{R}_{N}\mathcal{A}_{0}\mathcal{P}_{N,0},\quad\mathcal{F}_{N}:=\mathcal{R}_{N}\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{P}_{N,0},

where
\circ
denotes composition of nonlinear operators.
Finally, the PSD of (
13
) is the semilinear ODE

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}U}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0,N}U(t)+\mathcal{F}_{N}(U(t)),\quad t\geq 0,

(16)

for
U(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{N\bar{d}}
.
In the next section, we study how the equilibria and stability indicators of (
13
) are approximated by the counterparts of (
16
).

4
Approximation of equilibria and their stability

An equilibrium
\bar{u}\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
of (
13
) is a solution of

\mathcal{A}_{0}\bar{u}+\mathcal{F}(\bar{u})=0
,
and, for
\mathcal{L}:=D\mathcal{F}(\bar{u})
, the linearization of (
13
) at
\bar{u}
is

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}u(t)+\mathcal{L}u(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(17)

The linearized operator
\mathcal{L}
inherits the finite-rank property and can be written as

\mathcal{L}x=w\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\mathcal{L}_{i}x)\xi_{i},\quad\text{for all }x\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0}),

for
\mathcal{L}_{i}\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{0})\to\mathbb{C}
,
i=1,\dots,d
. We define

\mathcal{L}^{\xi}\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{0})\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
by
\mathcal{L}^{\xi}:=(\mathcal{L}_{1};\dots;\mathcal{L}_{d}).

Inspired by the PLS for iDEs we assume the following throughout.

Hypothesis 4
.

The local stability of an equilibrium
\bar{u}
of the semilinear equation (
13
) can be inferred from the stability of the zero solution of the linearized equation (
17
), and
the position of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathcal{L}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
determines the stability of the zero solution of (
17
).

Proposition 5
.

Let
\rho=\rho_{1}+\epsilon
with
\rho_{1},\epsilon>0
. Then,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{1}}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathcal{L}
if and only if
\lambda
is a root of the characteristic equation

\det(I_{\bar{d}+d}-A(\lambda))=0,

(18)

where
A(\lambda):\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d}\to\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d}
is the linear operator

A(\lambda)(\beta;\gamma)=\left(\mathcal{B}x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma);\ \mathcal{L}^{\xi}x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)\right),

and
x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)
is the solution of the initial value problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}}=(\lambda+\rho)x+w\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\xi_{i},\\[5.0pt]
x(0)=\beta.\end{cases}

(19)

with
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In this case,
x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
is the eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
x=x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)
be the solution of (
19
).
By variation of constants,

x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)=e(\lambda+\rho)\beta+h(\lambda+\rho)\gamma,

(20)

where
e(\lambda+\rho)\colon\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}\to X
and
h(\lambda+\rho)\colon\mathbb{C}^{d}\to X
are defined by

\displaystyle e(\lambda+\rho)\beta(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{e}^{(\lambda+\rho)\theta}\beta=w(\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\theta}\beta,

\displaystyle h(\lambda+\rho)\gamma(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\textstyle{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\xi_{i}\right)\int_{0}^{\theta}\mathrm{e}^{(\lambda+\rho)(\theta-s)}w(s)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s},\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{-}.

Now, (
20
) is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathcal{L}
corresponding to
\lambda
if and only if there exists
(\beta;\gamma)\neq 0
such that
\beta=\mathcal{B}x
and
\gamma=\mathcal{L}^{\xi}x,
i.e., if and only if
\lambda
is a root of (
18
).

The computation of (part of) the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathcal{L}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
is therefore turned into the computation of the roots of (
18
).
From
4
, the position of the characteristic roots of (
18
) in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
determines the stability of the zero solution of (
17
).

Regarding the approximating system (
16
), the PLS holds from the classical theory of ODEs. To approximate the stability of an equilibrium
\bar{u}
of (
13
), we first need the following results to relate the linearized equations.

Theorem 6
(One-to-one correspondence of equilibria).

Let
N\geq 1
.
If
\overline{u}\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
is an equilibrium of (
13
), then
\overline{U}=\mathcal{R}_{N}\overline{u}
is an equilibrium of (
16
). Vice versa, if
\overline{U}
is an equilibrium of (
16
), then
\overline{u}=\mathcal{P}_{N,0}\overline{U}
is an equilibrium of (
13
).

Moreover, linearization at equilibrium and PSD commute. In other words, the linearization of (
16
) at
\overline{U}
coincides with the PSD of (
17
) and reads

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}U}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0,N}U(t)+\mathcal{L}_{N}U(t),\quad t\geq 0,

(21)

with
\mathcal{L}_{N}=\mathcal{R}_{N}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
.

Proof.

First, if
\overline{u}\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
is an equilibrium of (
16
), then
\overline{u}(0)=\mathcal{B}\overline{u}
. From (
15
) and the equality
\mathcal{A}_{0}\overline{u}+\mathcal{F}(\overline{u})=0
, integrating we have

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}\left(\frac{\overline{u}}{w}\right)=-\frac{\mathcal{F}(\overline{u})}{w}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{F}_{i}(\overline{u})\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\overline{u}(\theta)=w(\theta)(\mathcal{B}\overline{u}-\theta\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{F}_{i}(\overline{u})\xi_{i}).

Since
\overline{u}/w
is a polynomial of degree one and
\overline{u}(0)=\mathcal{B}\overline{u}
, defining
\overline{U}:=\mathcal{R}_{N}\overline{u}
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{N,0}\overline{U}=\overline{u}
. Therefore

\mathcal{A}_{0,N}\overline{U}+\mathcal{F}_{N}(\overline{U})=\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{0}\overline{u}+\mathcal{F}(\overline{u}))=0,

hence
\overline{U}
is an equilibrium of (
16
).
Vice versa, let
\overline{U}
be an equilibrium of (
16
) and let
\overline{u}:=\mathcal{P}_{N,0}\overline{U}\in D(\mathcal{A}_{0}).
Note that
(\mathcal{A}_{0}\overline{u})/w
is a polynomial of degree at most
N-1
, and the function

((\mathcal{A}_{0}\overline{u})/w+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{F}_{i}(\overline{u})\xi_{i})
is zero on
\Theta_{N}
. Therefore, it must be zero as a polynomial, and the polynomial
\overline{u}
is in fact an equilibrium of (
13
).

Let us now consider the linearization at
\overline{u}
. Trivially, (
21
) is the PSD of (
17
).
On the other hand, for the linearity of
\mathcal{R}_{N}
and
\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
we have

D(\mathcal{R}_{N}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{P}_{N,0})(\overline{U})=\mathcal{R}_{N}D(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{P}_{N,0})(\overline{U})=(\mathcal{R}_{N}D\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P}_{N,0}\overline{U})\mathcal{P}_{N,0})\overline{U}=\mathcal{R}_{N}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{N,0}\overline{U}.

For (
21
) we derive a discrete characteristic equation following
[
7
, Proposition 5.2]
.

Proposition 7
.

The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}_{0,N}+\mathcal{L}_{N}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
are the roots of the discrete characteristic equation

\det(I_{\bar{d}+d}-A_{N}(\lambda))=0,

(22)

where
A_{N}(\lambda):\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d}\to\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d}
is the linear operator

A_{N}(\lambda)(\beta;\gamma)=(\mathcal{B}x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma);\mathcal{L}x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)),

and
x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)\in w\Pi_{N}^{\bar{d}}
is the solution of the collocation equation

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{N,0}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}}(\theta_{i})=(\lambda+\rho)x_{N,0}(\theta_{i})+w(\theta_{i})\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\xi_{i}\,\quad i=1,\dots,N,\\[5.0pt]
x_{N,0}(0)=\beta.\end{cases}

(23)

4.1
Convergence of the approximation of characteristic roots

We now show that the roots of (
22
) converge to the roots of (
18
) as
N\to\infty
. We first assume that the operator
\mathcal{L}
can be computed exactly and so its PSD is given by
\mathcal{L}_{N}=\mathcal{R}_{N}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
. We later briefly discuss the impact of quadrature rules.

Here we take
\rho=\rho_{1}+\epsilon
for some
\rho_{1},\epsilon>0
, and we choose

\theta_{j}:=-\frac{t_{j,N}}{2\rho_{1}},\quad j=1,\dots,N,

(24)

where
t_{j,N}
,
j=1,\dots,N
are either the zeros or the extrema of the standard Laguerre polynomials in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(see
appendix

A
for details). We refer to (
24
) as the
scaled Laguerre zeros
or
extrema
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\rho=\rho_{1}+\epsilon
with
\rho_{1},\,\epsilon>0
, and
B
be an open ball in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{1}}
.
For
\Theta_{N}
chosen as the scaled Laguerre zeros or extrema (
24
), the collocation equation (
23
) admits a unique solution
x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)\in w\Pi_{N}^{\bar{d}}
for all
N\geq 1
,
\lambda\in B
,
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Moreover, let
x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)
be the solution of (
19
) and let

C(\lambda):=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\rho_{1}},\quad\text{and }\quad K^{p}_{\epsilon}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ for }p=\infty,\ \epsilon\geq 0,\\
(\frac{p\epsilon}{2\rho_{1}})^{-1/p}&\text{ for }1\leq p<\infty,\ \epsilon>0.\end{cases}

The error

e_{N}=x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)-x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)
satisfies

\|e_{N}\|_{X}\leq{C}_{0}(B,\epsilon)D_{N}(B)\,|(\beta;\gamma)|\\

where for the scaled Laguerre zeros

C_{0}(B,\epsilon):=kK^{p}_{\epsilon}\max_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}\left\{\frac{\max\{|\lambda|,1\}}{\Re\lambda+\rho_{1}}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad D_{N}(B):=\max_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}|C(\lambda)|^{N},

while for the scaled Laguerre extrema

C_{0}(B,\epsilon):=kK^{p}_{\epsilon}\,\max_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}\left\{2+\frac{|\lambda|}{2\rho_{1}(\Re\lambda+\rho_{1})}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad D_{N}(B):=\max_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}\Big|\frac{C(\lambda)^{N}}{1-C(\lambda)^{N+1}}\Big|,

where
k
is a positive constant.
Finally, since
|C(\lambda)|<1
,
\|e_{N}\|_{X}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

First we observe that, for the solution of (
19
), we can write
x=w\varphi\in L^{p}
with
\varphi\in L^{p}_{\rho}
solution of

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}}=\lambda\varphi+\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\xi_{i}}&\theta\leq 0,\\[5.0pt]
\varphi(0)=\beta.\end{cases}

Second, by defining

\theta\mapsto t:=-2\rho_{1}\theta,\quad\lambda\mapsto\mu:=-\frac{\lambda}{2\rho_{1}},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\xi_{i}\mapsto c:=-\frac{1}{2\rho_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}\xi_{i},

we transform the problem for
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
with
\Re\lambda>-\rho_{1}
into the problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta}}=\mu y+c,&t\geq 0,\\[5.0pt]
y(0)=\beta,\end{cases}

for
y
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\Re\mu<\frac{1}{2}.
Under this mapping,
\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{1}\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{-t/2},
while
w(\theta)=\mathrm{e}^{\rho\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{-(1/2+\delta)t}
with
\delta:=\frac{\epsilon}{2\rho_{1}}
, and the collocation nodes
\theta_{j}
are associated to the zeros or extrema
t_{j}
of the standard Laguerre polynomials in
\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Now the result follows by applying
proposition

10
and
proposition

11
in
appendix

A
on the positive real line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and mapping the bounds (
42
) and (
47
) back to
\mathbb{R}_{-}.

Finally, from the error bounds on the collocation equation we are ready to conclude the error bounds on the characteristic roots in the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\rho=\rho_{1}+\epsilon
, with
\rho_{1},\epsilon>0,
and
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{1}}
be a root of (
18
) with multiplicity
m
. Let
B
be an open ball in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{1}}
of center
\lambda^{*}
such that
\lambda^{*}
is the unique root of (
18
) in
B
.
For
\Theta_{N}
chosen as the scaled Laguerre zeros or extrema (
24
), there exists
N_{1}:=N_{1}(B)
such that, for
N\geq N_{1}
, equation (
22
) has
m
roots
\lambda_{i,N}
,
i=1,\dots,m
(taking into account multiplicities) and

\max_{i=1,\dots,m}|\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{i,N}|=O\left(D_{N}(B)^{1/m}\right).

(25)

Hence
\displaystyle{\max_{i=1,\dots,m}}|\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{i,N}|\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

The steps follow the lines of
[
7
, Section 5.3.2]
, to which we refer to further details.
From
theorem

8
, for all choices of the
p
-norm and for both choices of the nodes, for all
\lambda\in B
we can bound

\displaystyle\|(A(\lambda)-A_{N}(\lambda))(\beta;\gamma)\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{L}^{\xi})\|\cdot\|e_{N}\|_{X}\leq\|(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{L}^{\xi})\|\cdot C_{0}(B,\epsilon)D_{N}(B)|(\beta;\gamma)|,

with
C_{0}(B,\epsilon)
,
D_{N}(B)
obtained from
theorem

8
.
Therefore, considering the operator norm in
\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d}
, following the proof of
[
7
, Proposition 5.3]
we obtain the norm bound

\|(A(\lambda)-A_{N}(\lambda))\|\leq C_{1}(B)C_{0}(B,\epsilon)D_{N}(B)

for
C_{1}(B):=\max\left\{\left\|(\det)^{\prime}(I_{\bar{d}+d}-A(z)+\Delta)\right\|\colon z\in\overline{B},\ \Delta\in\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d},\ \|\Delta\|\leq 1\right\}.

This allows us to conclude the proof using Rouché’s Theorem from complex analysis
[
35
, Section 7.7]
.

The approximations (
16
) and (
21
) hold when
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{L}
can be computed exactly. However, such operators often involve integrals, hence need to be approximated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
via suitable quadrature rules. In this case, (
16
), (
21
) and (
22
) still hold with the operators defined by
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\mathcal{R}_{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
,
\mathcal{L}_{N}=\mathcal{R}_{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{P}_{N,0}
, and
A_{N}(\lambda)(\beta;\gamma)=(\mathcal{B}x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma);\ \widetilde{\mathcal{L}}x_{N,0}(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma))
.
As a consequence, the convergence analysis should also account for error

\widetilde{Q}(B):=\sup\left\{\frac{\|(\mathcal{L}-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})x(\cdot;\lambda,\beta,\gamma)\|_{X}}{|(\beta;\gamma)|}\quad\colon\quad\lambda\in\overline{B},\ (\beta;\gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{\bar{d}+d}\setminus\{0\}\right\}

and (
25
) should be replaced by

\max_{i=1,\dots,m}|\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{i,N}|=O\left(D_{N}(B)^{1/m}\right)+O\left(\widetilde{Q}(B)^{1/m}\right).

(26)

In practice, it is often convenient to use quadrature formulas associated with
\Theta_{N}
or
\Theta_{0,N}
, so that
\mathcal{L}
is approximated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}
by Gauss–Laguerre (on the scaled Laguerre zeros) or Gauss–Radau–Laguerre (on the scaled Laguerre extrema plus zero) quadrature formulas respectively, with corresponding error
\widetilde{Q}_{N}(B)
. In this case, the convergence
\lambda_{i,N}\to\lambda^{*}
follows from
\widetilde{Q}_{N}(B)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
and the convergence rate depends on the regularity of the integrands in suitable Sobolev spaces (see e.g.
[
32
, Section 5.1]
for Gaussian quadrature rules on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
).

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have introduced a general abstract framework for the pseudospectral reduction of nonlinear iDEs to a finite-dimensional system of ODEs, which can be studied with well-established software for ODEs (specifically we used MatCont for MATLAB).
For iREs we have proposed a novel approach compared to
[
27
]
, exploiting the equivalence via integration proposed for finite delay in
[
37
]
. In this way, the PSD returns directly an ODE system without involving an implicit algebraic condition.

For linear(ized) iDDEs and iREs we have proved the convergence of the PSD approximation of the characteristic roots to the true ones, answering an important question raised when the method was first proposed in
[
27
]
. The convergence holds for a general weighted
L^{p}
-norm, therefore also in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\rho}
. The numerical tests illustrate the theoretical result of convergence of characteristic roots and eigenfunctions in norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\rho}
for iDDEs and
\|\cdot\|_{1,\rho}
for iREs.
The theoretical error bound depends on the modulus, the real part, and the multiplicity of the target eigenvalue, as well as the discretization index
N
, the choice of the family of collocation nodes (chosen as the zeros or extrema of the scaled Laguerre polynomials, orthogonal with respect to a weight
\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{1}\cdot}
,
\rho_{1}<\rho
) and the quadrature formula used to approximate the integrals.
In particular, approximating eigenvalues with larger modulus with a given accuracy requires larger discretization indices.
Despite this, since the right half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
contains a finite number of eigenvalues, our theoretical result guarantees that all eigenvalues in
\mathbb{C}_{\rho}
can be approximated with desired accuracy by choosing
N
large enough.
However, determining such value of
N
is a difficult problem, unless one can estimate
a priori
the location of the spectrum. To investigate this problem experimentally, we performed extensive numerical tests, which confirm that the observed accuracy strongly depends on the specific eigenvalue of interest and the chosen quadrature formula. For optimal choices, discretization indices of the order of 20–30 usually guarantee machine precision for eigenvalues with multiplicity one.

Numerical tests on nonlinear equations demonstrate the flexibility of the method to study the bifurcation of equilibria.
To verify the correctness of the computed bifurcation diagrams, in this paper we have considered equations in which the bifurcation diagram can be obtained via an alternative route, hence allowing to compare the PSD output with reference results: the nonlinear iDDE (
31
) admits an equivalent ODE formulation, at least for integer shape parameters of the kernel, whose bifurcation properties can be studied independently with MatCont; and the iRE (
32
) admits an equivalent formulation as a DDE with finite delay that can be studied with bifurcation software for DDEs (here we used DDE-BIFTOOL for MATLAB).

Future work includes the proof of convergence of the resolvent operators, that may be useful in turn to prove the convergence of the solution operators of the initial value problems and of the periodic solutions.
From a computational point of view, we plan to investigate numerically the impact of different sets of collocation nodes and use truncated interpolation and quadrature schemes to improve computation times in the spirit of
[
33
]
.
While the abstract formulation encompasses other types of models, the convergence proof in this paper relies on several assumptions specific to iDEs, including the form of the boundary condition defined by
\mathcal{B}
and that the operator
\mathcal{F}
maps on a
d
-dimensional space of functions
w\Xi_{d}
, on which weighted interpolation is exact.
One could consider extending the techniques to more general boundary conditions in the form
\mathcal{B}x=0
.
For more general basis functions of
\Xi_{d}
or different (e.g., truncated) interpolation schemes, the analogous of
theorem

8
would require the assumption that the interpolation scheme is convergent on the functions in
\Xi_{d}
. Similarly, the one-to-one correspondence of equilibria and linearization (
theorem

6
) may hold only in an approximate sense, if interpolation is not exact on the equilibria.
Finally, the analysis conducted in the appendix also provides the exact location of the spectrum of the reduced differentiation matrix, i.e., the differentiation matrix with zero boundary condition, associated with the zeros and extrema of the standard Laguerre polynomials on the positive half-line.
Understanding the behavior of the spectrum of differentiation matrices is a notoriously difficult problem, and these results add a first significative contribution to the study of such matrices, which are commonly used in the numerical solution of differential equations. We aim to perform further explorations in future work.

Appendix A
General results for a collocation problem on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

In this section we assume
d=1
for simplicity and, in order to work with the family of the standard Laguerre polynomials
\{L_{N}\}_{N\geq 0}
, orthogonal with respect to the weight
\mathrm{e}^{-t}
on the positive half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)
, we consider the equivalent reformulation of the collocation problem on
\mathbb{R}_{+},
and we study the existence and uniqueness and the convergence of the collocation polynomials for two relevant choices of the collocation points: the zeros and the extrema of
L_{N}
, for which we refer for instance to
[
32
, Chapter 2.3.5]
. The same approach can be easily extended to other collocation points.

Given
\beta,c\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re\mu<1/2
, let
y(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mu t}\beta+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\mu(t-s)}c\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

be the solution of the initial value problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=\mu\,y+c,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\\[5.0pt]
y(0)=\beta.\end{cases}

(33)

Note that, since
\Re\mu<1/2
, for all
\delta\geq 0
and
w_{\delta}(t):=\mathrm{e}^{-(1/2+\delta)t},\ t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},
we have
w_{\delta}y\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C})
for
1\leq p\leq+\infty.

Let
\Pi_{N}
denote the set of algebraic polynomials of degree at most
N
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Given a set of distinct points
\{t_{i,N}\}_{i=1}^{N}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+},
the collocation polynomial
p_{N}\in\Pi_{N}
for (
33
) is defined by

\begin{cases}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{N}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=\mu\,p_{N}+c,\quad t\in\{t_{i,N}\}_{i=1}^{N},\\[5.0pt]
p_{N}(0)=\beta.\end{cases}

(34)

Since
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{N}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}-\mu\,p_{N}-c
is a polynomial of degree
N
which vanishes at the collocation points, the collocation equation (
34
) admits the following equivalent representation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{N}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}-\mu\,p_{N}=c+k_{N}q_{N},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\\[5.0pt]
p_{N}(0)=\beta,\end{array}\right.

(35)

where

q_{N}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(t-t_{i,N})
and
k_{N}
is a suitable constant. By expressing the polynomials in (
35
) as follows

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{N}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N-1}a_{j}t^{j},\quad p_{N}(t)=\beta+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{a_{j-1}}{j}\,t^{j},\quad\text{and }\quad q_{N}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{b_{j}}{j!}t^{j},

from (
35
) we derive the following recurrence relations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a_{0}=\mu\beta+c+k_{N}b_{0}\\
a_{j}-\frac{\mu}{j}a_{j-1}=k_{N}\frac{b_{j}}{j!},\quad j=1,\ldots,N,\\
a_{N}=0.\end{array}\right.

(36)

Now, by easy computations, we get from (
36
)

a_{j}=\frac{\mu^{j}}{j!}(\mu\beta+c)+k_{N}d_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,N,

with
d_{j}
given by

d_{j}j!=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\mu^{j-k}\,b_{k},\qquad j=0,1,\dots,N.

(37)

Finally, by requiring that
a_{N}=0,
we get the following equation for
k_{N}

\mu^{N}(\mu\beta+c)+k_{N}d_{N}N!=0.

(38)

Note that each
d_{j}
in (
37
) depends on
\mu
and, through the polynomial
q_{N},
on the collocation points. In the following we focus on two relevant choices: the zeros and the extrema of the standard Laguerre polynomial
L_{N}
. For both, we study the existence and uniqueness, and the convergence w.r.t. the weight function
w_{\delta}
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C})
, namely the behavior of
\|w_{\delta}(p_{N}-y)\|_{p}
as
N\to\infty.

Let
\hat{e}_{N}:=w_{\delta}(p_{N}-y).
From (
33
) and (
35
) we easily get

\hat{e}_{N}(t)=k_{N}w_{\delta}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\mu(t-s)}q_{N}(s)\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

(39)

Proposition 10
(Existence, uniqueness, convergence for Laguerre zeros).

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Re\mu<1/2,
and let
\{t_{i,N}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be the zeros of the
N
-degree standard Laguerre polynomial
L_{N}.
Then there exists a unique polynomial solution
p_{N}
of (
34
) for all
N\geq 1.
Moreover, defining

C(\mu):=\frac{\mu}{\mu-1},

(40)

we have

k_{N}q_{N}(t)=-C(\mu)^{N}L_{N}(t)\,(\mu\beta+c),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(41)

and the following error bound

\|\hat{e}_{N}\|_{p}\leq|C(\mu)|^{N}\frac{(|\mu||\beta|+|c|)K_{\delta}^{p}}{(1/2-\Re\mu)},

(42)

where

K_{\delta}^{p}=\begin{cases}1,&p=\infty,\,\delta\geq 0,\\
(p\delta)^{-1/p},&1\leq p<\infty,\,\delta>0.\end{cases}

(43)

The convergence
\|\hat{e}_{N}\|_{p}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
follows for
p=\infty
with
\delta\geq 0,
and for
\ 1\leq p<+\infty
with
\delta>0.

Proof.

For the standard Laguerre polynomial
L_{N}
it holds

q_{N}(t)=(-1)^{N}N!\,L_{N}(t)=(-1)^{N}N!\,\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}(-1)^{j}{N\choose j}\,\frac{t^{j}}{j!},

see
[
32
, Chapter 2.3.5]
.
Hence,
b_{j}=(-1)^{N}N!(-1)^{j}{N\choose j}
, and, from (
37
),

d_{N}=(-1)^{N}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}\mu^{N-j}(-1)^{j}{N\choose j}=(1-\mu)^{N}\neq 0

(44)

since
|1-\mu|\geq|1-\Re\mu|>\frac{1}{2}.
Then the existence and uniqueness of the collocation solution follows for all
N\geq 1.
From (
38
) and (
40
) we obtain
k_{N}=-\frac{C(\mu)^{N}}{(-1)^{N}N!}(\mu\beta+c)
and (
41
).

Let us study now the error
\hat{e}_{N}
. From (
39
) and (

\hat{e}_{N}(t)=C(\mu)^{N}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\delta t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{(\mu-1/2)(t-s)}\mathrm{e}^{-s/2}L_{N}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,(\mu\beta+c),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Since
\mathrm{e}^{-s/2}|L_{N}(s)|\leq L_{N}(0)=1

\displaystyle|\hat{e}_{N}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\,|C(\mu)|^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\delta t}\ \frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{(\Re\mu-1/2)t}}{1/2-\Re\mu}\,(|\mu||\beta|+|c|),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\mathrm{e}^{-\delta t}(1-\mathrm{e}^{(\Re\mu-1/2)t})\|_{\infty}\leq 1,&\text{ for all }\delta\geq 0,\\[5.0pt]

Proposition 11
(Existence, uniqueness, convergence for Laguerre extrema).

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Re\mu<1/2,
and let
\{t_{i,N}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be the extrema of the
(N+1)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}L_{N+1}
. Then there exists a unique polynomial solution
p_{N}
of (
34
) for all
N\geq 1.
Moreover, for
C(\mu)
defined as in (
40
) and

D_{N}(\mu):=\frac{C(\mu)^{N}}{1-C(\mu)^{N+1}},

k_{N}q_{N}(t)=D_{N}(\mu)\,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}L_{N+1}(t)\left(\frac{\mu\beta+c}{\mu-1}\right),\ t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(46)

and the following bound

\|\hat{e}_{N}\|_{p}\leq|D_{N}(\mu)|\frac{(|\mu||\beta|+|c|)K_{\delta}^{p}}{|\mu-1|}\left(2+\frac{|\mu|}{1/2-\Re\mu}\right)

(47)

with
K_{\delta}^{p}
defined in (

The convergence
\|\hat{e}_{N}\|_{p}\to 0
as

with

and for
\ 1\leq p<+\infty
with

Proof.

For the polynomial

q_{N}(t)=(-1)^{N}N!\,L_{N}^{(1)}(t)=(-1)^{N}N!\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}{N+1\choose N-j}\,t^{j}

b_{j}=(-1)^{N}N!(-1)^{j}{N+1\choose j+1}

\displaystyle d_{N}=(-1)^{N+1}\mu^{N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{N}(-\mu)^{-(j+1)}{N+1\choose j+1}=(-1)^{N+1}\left((\mu-1)^{N+1}-\mu^{N+1}\right)\neq 0
