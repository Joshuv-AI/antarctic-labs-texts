A least-squares Galerkin approach to gradient and Hessian recovery for nondivergence-form elliptic equations Thanks: This work was supported by the ModCompShock Marie Skłodowska–Curie International Training Network and was possible thanks to DISIM of the University of L’Aquila, Italy, where most of the reported research took place in 2017–18.
Omar Lakkis, Amireh Mousavi

Abstract
. We propose a least-squares method involving the recovery of the gradient and possibly the Hessian for elliptic equation in nondivergence form. As our approach is based on the Lax–Milgram theorem with the curl-free constraint built into the target (or cost) functional, the discrete spaces require no inf-sup stabilization. We show that standard conforming finite elements can be used yielding a priori and a posteriori convergence results. We illustrate our findings with numerical experiments with uniform or adaptive mesh refinement.



1.
Introduction

Elliptic equations in nondivergence form play an important role in
many domains of pure and applied mathematics ranging from nonlinear
PDEs

(
Caffarelli and Cabré 1995
;
Armstrong and Smart 2010
)

to Probability Theory

(
Evans 1985
;
Fabes and
Stroock 1983
)
,
continuum Game Theory,
homogenization
(
Capdeboscq et al. 2020
)
and
wave
propagation
(
Arjmand and
Kreiss 2017
)
. The
numerical approximation of such equations (references to be given
below) plays thus an important role. Here we propose a least-squares based gradient- or
Hessian-recovery Galerkin finite element method for the numerical
approximating of a function
u:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\varOmega
convex, solving
the following
linear elliptic Dirichlet boundary value problem
in nondivergence form

(1.1)

\mathcal{{L}}u:=\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!^{2}u+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\nabla u-cu=f\text{ and }\left.u\right|_{\partial\varOmega}=r

where
f\in\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)
,
r\in\operatorname{H}^{3/2}(\partial\varOmega)
, all
coefficients are measurable,
\boldsymbol{{A}}
is a uniformly elliptic
tensor-valued,

(1.2)

\lambda_{\flat}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{}\leqslant\boldsymbol{{A}}\leqslant\lambda_{\sharp}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{},\text{${\ }\text{almost everywhere }$ in }\varOmega,\text{ for some }\lambda_{\sharp}\geqslant\lambda_{\flat}>0,

c
is non-negative on
\varOmega
and
\boldsymbol{{A}}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
,
c
satisfy either
of the
Cordes condition
(
2.8
) or
(
2.9
) (to be discussed in
§
2.2
). Roughly speaking, the Cordes condition
allows us to reformulate the operator
\mathcal{{L}}
so that it is close
enough to an invertible operator in
divergence
form
thereby ensuring the
elliptic problem with discontinuous coefficients is well-posed
(see §
2.2
for more details).

A main difficulty in the study of elliptic PDEs in nondivergence
form is the lack of a natural variational structure which precludes
a straightforward use of weak solutions in
\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)
, say, and
their numerical approximation using the bilinear form given by the
exact problem. One is thus forced to find some suitable
approximation of the Hessian more or less directly. The appropriate
concept of generalized solution for nondivergence form equations is
that of viscosity solution, which relies on the maximum principle.
In this respect, finite difference methods have the advantage over
Galerkin methods, in that they replicate more easily the maximum
principle, which is very useful when aiming at the approximation of
viscosity solutions. On the other hand finite difference methods,
besides lacking the geometric flexibility and the higher order
approximation power of Galerkin methods, must be modified to take
into account coefficients that are more singular than Lipschitz

(
Froese and Oberman 2009
)
. Dealing with the
boundary is also not that straightforward as with Galerkin methods
Which we deal with in this article.

Galerkin methods for general elliptic PDEs in nondivergence form were
studied by
Böhmer 2010
, but
\operatorname{C}^{1}(\varOmega)
finite
elements are required for their practical
implementations
(
Davydov and Saeed 2013
)
. A

recovered Hessian
finite element method for approximating
the solution of nondivergence form elliptic equation was introduced by

Lakkis and Pryer 2011
; this method was later
generalized and fully analyzed by

Neilan 2017
.
Discontinuous
Galerkin
approaches have been proposed by

Smears and Süli 2013
,

Feng et al. 2017
and

Feng et al. 2018
. Further Galerkin
approaches for nondivergence form equation do exist such as the

two-scale Galerkin method
which is based on an
integro-differential scheme by

Nochetto and Zhang 2018
and the somewhat related
method of
Feng and Jensen 2017
, which draws on the

semi-Lagrangian methods
and the celebrated convergence
theorem of
Barles and Souganidis 1991
, the

primal-dual weak Galerkin
method

Wang and Wang 2018
and the variational
formulation of elliptic problems in nondivergence form of

Gallistl 2017
.

In this paper, we propose a least-squares approach combined with a
gradient and Hessian recovery. Our approach is related to the method
of
Smears and Süli 2013
in that the test
function is the elliptic operator (or an approximation thereof)
applied to the “variable function”, but, unlike them, we use
conforming finite elements. Our work is also connected to that of

Gallistl 2017
with the key departure that
our least-squares approach allows a cost-functional enforcement of the
curl-free requirement rather than imposing this on the function space
and having to enforce it discretely via inf-sup stable
discretizations. Indeed, a feature of the method we will propose is
that it is coercive and based on the idea of gradient- or
Hessian-recovery combined with Lax–Milgram theorem, which as noted by

Bramble et al. 1997
it is one of
the two main approaches of least squares Galerkin methods (the other
is a weighted-residual approach based on the Agmon–Douglis–Nirenberg
theory). An obviously non-exhaustive list of references to the
least-squares based Galerkin methods for linear and nonlinear we came
across is further complemented by

Aziz et al. 1985
(based on the ADN theory)

Bochev and
Gunzburger 2006
(which gives a
thorough survey at writing time)

Dean and Glowinski 2006
(which uses least-squares
to solve the Monge–Ampère equation, related to nondivergence PDEs)
and its further refinement

Caboussat et al. 2013
. We
deem it worth noting that an early attempt at least-squares FEMs for
elliptic equation in nondivergence form by

Bramble and
Schatz 1970
is quite
inspiring, despite the difficulties in practical implementations of
methods there proposed (they require the same
\operatorname{H}^{2}
-conformity as

Böhmer 2010
).

The rest of this article is structured as follows: in
§
2
we introduce the main
background material, the cost (or energy) functional
E_{\theta}
(where

\theta
is a parameter) to be minimized and the associated
bilinear forms; we give some technical remarks. In
§
3
we show that the
bilinear forms associated with

satisfy the Lax–Milgram
theorem’s assumptions thereby guaranteeing the least-squares problem
and the equivalent exact PDE are well-posed. In
§
4
we introduce the Galerkin
discretization, which, thanks to
§
3
, enjoys
quasi-optimality and convergence properties on general finite element
spaces without the need to enforce inf-sup; we also derive via a
residual–error a posteriori estimate, indicators and an adaptive
algorithm. Finally in §
5
we
illustrate the theoretical findings with numerical experiments in both
uniform and adaptive mesh refinement frameworks, before giving some
conclusions and outlook in §
6
.

2.2.
Cordes conditions

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
(typically
d=2,3
),

\varOmega
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of class
\operatorname{C}^{0,1}

\boldsymbol{{A}}\in\operatorname{L}_{\infty}(\varOmega;\operatorname{Sym}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})})

symmetric-matrix-valued function,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\operatorname{L}_{\infty}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{{A}}\right|^{2}+\nicefrac{{\left|\boldsymbol{b}\right|^{2}}}{{2\lambda}}+(\nicefrac{{c}}{{\lambda}})^{2}}{(\operatorname{tra}\boldsymbol{{A}}+\nicefrac{{c}}{{\lambda}})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{d+\varepsilon}\text{${\ }\text{almost everywhere }$ in }\varOmega

for some
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

In the special case
\boldsymbol{b}=0
and

, we may take

and the Cordes condition (
2.8
) is then replaced by

(2.9)

\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{{A}}\right|^{2}}{(\operatorname{tra}\boldsymbol{{A}})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{d-1+\varepsilon}\text{${\ }\text{almost everywhere }$ in }\varOmega

for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Since the right hand side of
(
2.8
) and
(
2.9
) are decreasing with respect

\varepsilon
, it suffices to find some
\bar{\varepsilon}>0

which satisfies them and then considering
\varepsilon\in(0,\bar{\varepsilon}]
small enough. By the same argument, as the
dimension increases, (
2.8
) and
(
2.9
) become more stringent.
It is easy to show that in two dimensions, all symmetric positive
definite matrices satisfy (
2.9
), whereas
this is not true in three (and higher) dimensions. For instance, taking

(2.10)

\boldsymbol{{A}}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&5\end{bmatrix}

in (
2.9
) violates it. Nonetheless the

) or
(

) cover a wide range of

equations

(

;

;

, e.g.)
.

If the boundary value of (

) be

) and the coefficients
satisfy

,

and (

),
existence, uniqueness and stability of the strong solution in

is proved by

while a more general version for convex domains based on the

, is proved by

to the case of a general nonlinear Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman

2.4.
A least-squares problem

We propose to formulate a least-squares alternative to
(
2.13
) which allows for weaker solutions. Consider

0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1
and start by introducing the linear operator

(2.14)

\begin{gathered}\begin{array}[]{rrcl}{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}}:&{\mathcal{{Y}}}&\to&{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}\\
&{(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}})}&\mapsto&{\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot(\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi)-c\varphi=:{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}})\text{\ .}}\end{array}\quad\end{gathered}

The role of
\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}
is to approach the operator
\mathcal{{L}}

from a mixed view point via

(2.15)

\mathcal{{L}}\varphi=\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}\left({\varphi,\nabla\varphi,\operatorname{D}\!^{2}\varphi}\right)\text{ for }\varphi\text{ twice differentiable}.

Although the aforementioned problem of finding a strong solution
u

of (
2.13
) in
\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
is
well-posed, working with such a high regularity assumption has
undesirable effects such as additional computational difficulties. As
we aim to a numerical scheme, to circumvent too stringent regularity
assumptions on
u
, we reformulate (
2.13
) to an
appropriate alternative in
\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)
. The idea behind the
reformulation and the theory that follows is, similar to mixed
formulation, considering
u\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
as
u\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
which also
\nabla u\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Motivated by
this reasoning, we introduce the following quadratic functional on

\mathcal{{W}}

(2.16)

\begin{split}E_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}):=&\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}-\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
&+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}})-f}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\end{split}

and consider the convex minimization problem of finding

(2.17)

(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}}\right)\in\mathcal{{W}}\end{subarray}}{\operatorname{argmin}}E_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}).

We recall that the

rotational
or
curl operator

(2.18)

\begin{array}[]{rrcl}{\nabla\!\times\!{}}:&{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}&\to&{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)^{\hat{d}}}\\
&{\boldsymbol{\psi}}&\mapsto&{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}\text{\ }}\end{array}\quad\text{ for }\hat{d}:=\binom{d}{2}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }d=2,\\
3&\text{ if }d=3\end{cases}

is such that in Cartesian coordinates one has

(2.19)

\nabla\!\times\!\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\partial_{1}{\psi_{2}}-\partial_{2}{\psi_{1}},\qquad\nabla\!\times\!\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{2}{\psi_{3}}-\partial_{3}{\psi_{2}}\\
\partial_{3}{\psi_{1}}-\partial_{1}{\psi_{3}}\\
\partial_{1}{\psi_{2}}-\partial_{2}{\psi_{1}}\end{bmatrix}.

More generally, a coordinate and dimension
d
-independent
definition of curl is the doubled
skew-symmetric part of the Jacobian,

(2.20)

\operatorname{D}\!\times\boldsymbol{\psi}:=\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}-\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}{}^{\intercal},\text{ for }\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

whereby when
d=2
or
3

the usual curl is characterized by

(2.21)

\left({\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)\times\boldsymbol{x}=\left({\operatorname{D}\!\times\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)\boldsymbol{x}\text{ for each }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where, for (columns)
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{y}

is the usual vector (external) product in
d=3
, and is

\det\left[{\begin{matrix}[r]\boldsymbol{x}&\boldsymbol{y}\end{matrix}}\right]
in
d=2
. In terms of
exterior algebra (and calculus) we are simply identifying
elements of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(the alternating
2
-forms) (or
skew-symmetric

matrices if preferred) with elements
of
\mathbb{R}^{\hat{d}}
, through the map
\boldsymbol{{J}}

(2.22)

a\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}[r]0&-a\\
a&0\end{bmatrix}=:\boldsymbol{{J}}a,\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}=\begin{bmatrix}[r]v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
v_{3}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}[r]0&-v_{3}&v_{2}\\
v_{3}&0&-v_{1}\\
-v_{2}&v_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}=:\boldsymbol{{J}}\boldsymbol{v},

for
d=2
and
d=3
respectively.
A useful consequence of this is that

(2.23)

\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{{J}}\boldsymbol{v}\!:\!\boldsymbol{{J}}\boldsymbol{w}.

2.5.
Remark (equivalence of (

) and (

))

If

2.6.
Euler–Lagrange equations

The Euler–Lagrange equation of the minimization problem

{(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})\in\mathcal{{W}}}

\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{{H}},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}-\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}}\right\rangle\\
+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}}),\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}})}\right\rangle\\
=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}})}\right\rangle\penalty\ \text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}})\in\mathcal{{W}}.

For numerical purposes it will be useful to rewrite
the Euler–Lagrange equation (

)
in the following equivalent system-form

\displaystyle\langle\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle+(1-\theta)\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})\boldsymbol{b},{\nabla\varphi}\rangle-\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})c,\varphi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)\left\langle{f\boldsymbol{b},\nabla\varphi}\right\rangle-\left\langle{fc,\varphi}\right\rangle\qquad\text{ for each }\phi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega),

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g},-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{{H}},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\theta\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\theta\left\langle{f\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle\qquad\text{ for each }\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathscr{{V}},

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})\boldsymbol{{A}}

\displaystyle-\left({\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{{H}}}\right),{\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{f\boldsymbol{{A}},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}}\right\rangle\qquad\text{ for each }\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}\in\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega;\operatorname{Sym}{(\mathbb{R}^{d})}).

2.7.
Remark (a Hessian-less approach)

We may consider the

(2.26)

(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\mapsto E_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}),

the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation be turned to finding

(u,\boldsymbol{g})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\mathscr{{V}}
such that

\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g}),\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle\\
=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle\text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\mathscr{{V}},

or in equivalent system-form

(2.28)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g}+(1-\theta)\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g})\boldsymbol{b},\nabla\varphi}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g})c,\varphi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)\left\langle{f\boldsymbol{b},\nabla\varphi}\right\rangle-\left\langle{fc,\varphi}\right\rangle\qquad\text{ for each }\varphi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega),

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g},-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\theta\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g})\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{g})\boldsymbol{{A}},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle

2.8.
Bilinear forms

In keeping with (

) and (

),
we define the symmetric bilinear forms

a_{\theta}:{\mathcal{{Y}}}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\hat{a}_{\theta}:{\left({\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}\left({\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)}\right)}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

by the expressions

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{\theta}\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}\,;\,\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}^{\prime}}\right)&:=\left\langle{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\varphi^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}-\boldsymbol{{\Xi}},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle\\
&\phantom{:=}+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}),\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}^{\prime})}\right\rangle\end{split}

respectively for all
\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\right)
and

Note that for any

we have

. In the analysis of the problem

Indeed we need to bound

3.1.
Key ideas of our least-squares approach

We develop the proof of
a_{\theta}
’s coercivity in two steps. First,
we prove that
\hat{a}_{\theta}
is coercive on
\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\mathscr{{V}}
; the key of the proof is considering an
appropriate operator on
\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}\left({\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)
say

\mathcal{{D}}
which for any
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\mathscr{{V}}
is close to
\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi})

and for some constant
C>0

(3.1)

\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{D}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\geqslant C\left({\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}}\right).

Then, by comparing
\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}
with
\boldsymbol{{\Xi}}
we get the coercivity
of
a_{\theta}
on
\mathcal{{W}}
.

Recalling the notation from (
2.8
) and
(
2.9
) introduce the
scaling
function

(3.2)

\gamma:=\begin{cases}\frac{\operatorname{tra}\boldsymbol{{A}}}{\left|\boldsymbol{{A}}\right|^{2}}&\text{ if }\lambda=0,\\
\frac{\operatorname{tra}\boldsymbol{{A}}+c/\lambda}{\left|\boldsymbol{{A}}\right|^{2}+\left|\boldsymbol{b}\right|^{2}/2\lambda+(c/\lambda)^{2}}&\text{ if }\lambda>0,\end{cases}

which was used in
Smears and Süli 2013
.
Uniform ellipticity (
1.2
), non-negativity of

and
uniform boundedness of the coefficients of
\mathcal{{L}}
imply that
\inf_{\varOmega}\gamma>0

and

(3.3)

\infty>\left\|{\gamma}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{\infty}(\varOmega)}=:C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{,$\mathcal{{L}}$}}}}.

3.2.
Lemma (a Miranda–Talenti estimate)

If
\boldsymbol{{A}}
satisfies the Cordes condition with
\lambda=0

(
2.9
), then for any

\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathscr{{V}}

(3.4)

\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\geqslant C_{\textup{\ref{const:special-Cordes}{{}}}}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}

where

(3.5)

C_{\textup{\ref{const:special-Cordes}{{,$\mathcal{{L}}$}}}}:=\frac{(1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon})^{2}}{{C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{}}}}^{2}\vee 1}}.

Proof.
The definition of
\gamma
in (
3.2
)
and the Cordes condition (
2.9
) imply
that

(3.6)

\left|\gamma\boldsymbol{{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{}\right|^{2}=d-\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{{A}}\right|^{2}}{(\operatorname{tra}\boldsymbol{{A}})^{2}}\leqslant 1-\varepsilon.

Hence we have

(3.7)

\left\|{(\gamma\boldsymbol{{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{})\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}\leqslant\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}.

Adding and subtracting
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}
and then using
(
2.33
) and (
3.7
) lead to

(3.8)

\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\gamma\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}=\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{(\gamma\boldsymbol{{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{})\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
\geqslant\left({\sqrt{\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-2.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}}-\left\|{(\gamma\boldsymbol{{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}{})\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}}\right)^{2}\\
\geqslant(1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon})^{2}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2},

from which we conclude.
∎

3.3.

Recalling the parameter

entering the Cordes

)
we define the

The name of this operator, which we need for our proof, rests on the

A similar idea

.

Let

3.6.
Theorem (a modified Miranda–Talenti estimate)

If
\varOmega
is a bounded open convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
0<\rho<2
and

0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1
then for any
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\mathscr{{V}}

we have

(3.13)

\left({1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}}}\right)\left\|{\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)}\right\|_{\lambda,\theta}^{2}\\
\leqslant\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{D}}_{\lambda}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left({\theta^{2}+(1-\theta)^{2}}\right)\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{\rho}}\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}.

Proof.
We start the proof by noting that thanks to

\varphi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)^{d}
we have

(3.14)

\left\langle{\nabla{}\kern-2.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi},\varphi}\right\rangle=-\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\varphi}\right\rangle.

Using the Maxwell estimate (
2.33
) and expanding

\left\|{\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}

imply

(3.15)

\left\|{\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)}\right\|_{\lambda,\theta}^{2}\leqslant\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{D}}_{\lambda}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}-2\lambda\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\varphi\rangle\\
+2\lambda\theta^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+2\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+4\lambda\theta(1-\theta)\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\varphi\rangle\\
=\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{D}}_{\lambda}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+2\lambda\theta^{2}\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla\varphi\rangle+2\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}\langle\nabla\varphi,\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle.

Applying a weighted Young’s inequality leads to

(3.16)

\left\|{\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)}\right\|_{\lambda,\theta}^{2}\leqslant\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{D}}_{\lambda}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\lambda\theta^{2}\rho\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
+\frac{\lambda\theta^{2}}{\rho}\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}\rho\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\frac{\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}}{\rho}\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}.

By subtracting
\lambda\theta^{2}\rho\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}\rho\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}
from
both sides and reversing the inequality we get

(3.17)

\begin{split}\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}&+\left\|{\mathcal{{D}}_{\lambda}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\frac{\lambda\theta^{2}}{\rho}\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
&+\frac{\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}}{\rho}\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
\geqslant&\left\|{\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)}\right\|_{\lambda,\theta}^{2}-\lambda\theta^{2}\rho\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}-\lambda(1-\theta)^{2}\rho\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
=&\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+2\lambda(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\left\|{\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
&+\lambda^{2}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}-2\lambda\theta(1-\theta)\rho\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\varphi}\right\rangle\\
=&\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+2\lambda(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\left\|{\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
&+\lambda^{2}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+2\theta(1-\theta)\rho\left\langle{\nabla{}\kern-2.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi},\lambda\varphi}\right\rangle\\
\geqslant&2\lambda(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\left\|{\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
&+\lambda^{2}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}-\theta(1-\theta)\rho\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-2.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}-\theta(1-\theta)\rho\lambda^{2}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
\geqslant&2\lambda(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\left\|{\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{4}})\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
&+(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{4}})\lambda^{2}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
\geqslant&(1-\nicefrac{{\rho}}{{2}})\left\|{(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\lambda,\theta}^{2},\end{split}

as claimed.
∎

3.7.
Theorem (coercivity of
\hat{a}_{\theta}
)

Let
\varOmega
be a bounded convex open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and the coefficients
\boldsymbol{{A}},\boldsymbol{b},c
satisfy the Cordes condition (either
(
2.8
) with
\lambda>0
or
(
2.9
) with
\boldsymbol{b}=0,c=0

and
\lambda=0
). Then the restricted bilinear form
\hat{a}_{\theta}
defined in
(
2.31
) satisfies

(3.18)

\begin{split}&\hat{a}_{\theta}\left({\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}\,;\,\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)\geqslant C_{\textup{\ref{const:coercivity-theta-ug}{{}}}}\left({\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}}\right)\end{split}

for all
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\mathscr{{V}}

where

(3.19)

C_{\textup{\ref{const:coercivity-theta-ug}{{,$\varOmega,\theta,\lambda,\varepsilon,{\mathcal{{L}}}$}}}}:=\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}}{2}\left({1\wedge\frac{\left({1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}}{\left({C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{}}}}^{2}\vee 1}\right)}}\right)&\text{ if }\lambda=0,\\[12.0pt]
\frac{\left({\sqrt[4]{1-\varepsilon}-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\left({{C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}}\wedge{4}\lambda^{2}}\right)}{{2\left({\sqrt[4]{1-\varepsilon}-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}}+\frac{2\lambda\left({\theta^{2}+\left({1-\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)}{1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\vee 1\vee C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{}}}}^{2}}&\text{ if }\lambda>0.\end{matrix}\right.

Proof.
We distinguish two cases according to whether
\lambda=0

or
\lambda>0
.

Case A.

Consider
\lambda=0
, then Lemma

3.2
leads to

(3.20)

\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
\geqslant\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\frac{\left({1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\left({C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{}}}}^{2}\vee 1}\right)}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}.

Putting

(3.21)

C_{\textup{\ref{const:hatC}{{}}}}:=\left({1\wedge\frac{\left({1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\left({C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{}}}}^{2}\vee 1}\right)}C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}}\right),

and using Young’s and Poincaré’s inequality we arrive at

(3.22)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{C_{\textup{\ref{const:hatC}{{}}}}}{2}\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}-C_{\textup{\ref{const:hatC}{{}}}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\left({1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\left({C_{\textup{\ref{const:sup-gamma}{{}}}}^{2}\vee 1}\right)}C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}\left({\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{C_{\textup{\ref{const:hatC}{{}}}}C_{\textup{\ref{eqn:Poincare}{{}}}}}{2}\left({\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}}\right),

which establishes the result for zero
\lambda
.

Case B.

Suppose
\lambda>0
,
let
\rho=2-2\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
and define

(3.23)

C_{\textup{\ref{const:mu}{{,$\lambda,\theta,\varepsilon$}}}}:=\frac{\lambda(\theta^{2}+(1-\theta)^{2})}{2-2\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}},

then from the Miranda–Talenti estimate, Theorem
3.6
,

Strictly speaking our approach here does not extend the classical

4.8.
Remark (curved domain)

In Theorem
4.7
, the domain is assumed polyhedral, so
that it can be triangulated exactly. If
\varOmega
has a curved boundary, isoparametric
finite elements may be used. In isoparametric method, a smooth or piecewise smooth
boundary,
\partial\varOmega
, guarantees that the elements with curved boundary
are not too distorted from triangles. Consequently, an error bound similar to
that of Lemma
4.6
can be established. The final result is that the
error using isoparametric finite element goes to zero at the same rate as if
ordinary Lagrange triangles were used on polyhedral domain.
This claim can be found in
Ciarlet 2002
.

5.1.
Numerical treatment of the zero tangential-trace

In proving the coercivity of
(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})\mapsto a_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{{H}})
, and thus the error estimates, we took
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathscr{{V}}
(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{g}
is a Sobolev fields with vanishing
tangential-trace) and consequently
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{{}_{\mathbb{V}{}}}\in\mathbb{G}{}
. However, enforcing a zero tangential-trace condition onto the
finite element space is not trivial. One way to effect such a
boundary condition is to consider the appropriate constraint on the
space and introduce a Lagrange multiplier variable; in this case, we
must determine subspaces that satisfy the corresponding inf-sup
condition and this may limit the choice of finite element spaces. To
circumvent this limitation, based on the discussion in
§
3.10
, we replace the zero-tangential-trace space

\mathbb{G}{}
, with the wider space
\tilde{\mathbb{G}{}}
in the
implementation and monitor the tangential-trace. Specifically, we
consider the discrete problem of finding

{(\mathsf{u}_{\mathbb{X}{}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{{}_{\mathbb{X}{}}},\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{{H}}}}{{}_{\mathbb{X}{}}})\in\mathbb{X}{}:=\mathbb{U}{}\times\tilde{\mathbb{G}{}}\times\mathbb{H}{}}

satisfying

(5.2)

a_{\theta}\left({\mathsf{u}_{\mathbb{X}{}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{{}_{\mathbb{X}{}}},\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{{H}}}}{{}_{\mathbb{X}{}}}\,;\,\mathsf{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\psi}}{},\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{{\Xi}}}}{}}\right)=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\mathsf{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\psi}}{},\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{{\Xi}}}}{})}\right\rangle\text{ for each }(\mathsf{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\psi}}{},\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{{\Xi}}}}{})\in\mathbb{X}{}

corresponding to a zero boundary problem and
(
4.5
) corresponding to a
nonzero boundary value.

6.
Conclusions and outlook

The least-squares based gradient or Hessian
recovery method presented is a practical and effective method for the
numerical approximation of solutions to linear elliptic equations in
nondivergence form. The advantages of the method herewith proposed
are:

(1)

Method (
4.4
) allows the use of a
wide choice of finite elements, including all standard conforming.
With the appropriate modifications one could envisage extending our
method to nonconforming elements as well, e.g.,

Smears and Süli 2013
.

(2)

Our least squares Lax–Milgram-based approach circumvents the need
for Lagrange multipliers or a curl-penalty stabilization
in inf-sup stable combinations
for
(\mathsf{u},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{})
(let alone

(\mathsf{u},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{},\boldsymbol{{\mathsf{{H}}}}{})
when the Hessian is needed)
as in
Gallistl 2017
,

Gallistl 2019

and
Gallistl and Süli 2019
.
We also can use a Céa quasi-optimality in the error analysis.

(3)

Through the least-squares approach, we are capable of considering
constraints that are assumed on the function spaces (to ensure well-posedness of the problem)
as square terms of the quadratic cost functional and then working in
general function spaces.

(4)

We are able to derive straightforward a posteriori error bounds,
with easily implemented estimators and indicators for which
the adaptive method shows convergence.

(5)

We can choose between a gradient-and-Hessian and gradient-only
recovery as observed in §
2.7
. The
Hessian is useful when our method is applied as the linear look
within a Newton or fixed-point method to a nonlinear elliptic
equation as in
Lakkis and Pryer 2013
,

Neilan 2014
,

Lakkis and Pryer 2015
and

Kawecki et al. 2018
.

(6)

An interesting issue, which we did not have room to address in this
paper, is the use of discontinuous Galerkin piecewise polynomial spaces
for the approximation of the gradient or the Hessian. Our method,
at least from the computational side can be easily adapted to
use such spaces, but the outcomes and gains are not clear, in that
the analysis would need serious reworking and the penalization
parameters required to get coercivity going might just give an unexpected
sting in the tail.

Our method is not without drawbacks of which we note the lack of
optimal convergence rate for the function value error

\left\|{u-\mathsf{u}_{\mathbb{V}{}}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}
and the slow convergence
for viscosity solutions (which we have not included in this work). We are aiming to
address thes issues in forthcoming work announced by

Lakkis and Mousavi 2020
.

Our FEniCS-based implementation is available on request for testing
and further research.
