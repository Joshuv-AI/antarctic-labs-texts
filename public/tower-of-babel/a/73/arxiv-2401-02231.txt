On the computation of coarse cohomology
Arka Banerjee

Abstract
. The purpose of this article is to relate coarse cohomology of metric spaces with a more computable cohomology. We introduce a notion of boundedly supported cohomology and prove that coarse cohomology of many spaces are isomorphic to the boundedly supported cohomology. Boundedly supported cohomology coincides with compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology if the space is proper and contractible. Our work generalizes an earlier result of Roe which says that the coarse cohomology is isomorphic to the compactly supported Alexander-Spanier cohomology if the space is uniformly contractible. As an application of our main theorem, we obtain that coarse cohomology of the complement can be computed in terms of Alexander-Spanier cohomology for many spaces.



1.
Introduction

John Roe
[
3
]
introduced the notion of coarse cohomology
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X)
of a metric space
X
to study large scale geometry of the space.
This cohomology roughly measures the way in which uniformly large bounded sets fit together.
Coarse cohomology is an invariant of the large scale geometry of the space: if two spaces are coarsely equivalent, then they have isomorphic coarse cohomology.
In general, coarse cohomology is hard to compute. For nice spaces Roe proved the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Roe
[
3
]
)
.

If
X
is uniformly contractible, then the coarse cohomology
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X)
is isomorphic to the compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology
\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X)
.

A space is called
uniformly contractible
if there exists a function
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that any set of diameter
r
is contractible inside a set of diameter
\rho(r)
.
Examples of uniformly contractible spaces include universal cover of compact aspherical complexes.
For example, Euclidean space of any dimension is uniformly contractible and proper. By Roe’s Theorem it follows that
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

In this article, our goal is to generalize the above theorem to facilitate computation of coarse cohomology for more spaces.
Our main motivation for doing that is to compute coarse cohomology of the complement of a subspace
A\overset{}{\subset}X
denoted by
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)
.
This notion was introduced and studied in
[
1
]
.
One way to describe this cohomology is as follows: consider complements of
expanding
neighborhoods
X-N_{f}(A)
, where
N_{f}(A)=\bigcup_{x\in A}B(x,f(x))
for proper functions
f:A\rightarrow(0,\infty)
.
These complements form a directed system of metric spaces under inclusion (in the direction of slower growth of
f
), and coarse (co)chain complexes of the complement
X-A
is defined to be the inverse limits of the usual coarse complexes of these spaces.

In
[
1
]
, it was shown that the coarse cohomology of the complement can be realized as the coarse cohomology of a single space.
Equip
X
with the following distance function

{d_{A}}(x,y)=\min\{d(x,A)+d(y,A),d(x,y)\}.

d_{A}
defines a pseudometric on
X
. It induces a metric on the space
X/\bar{A}
which is formed by identifying the closure of
A
to a point.

Proposition 1.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
A\overset{}{\subset}X
and
X/\bar{A}
is the metric space equipped with the metric induced from
d_{A}
. Then
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})
is isomorphic to
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)
.

So, one way to get the coarse cohomology of the complement of
A
is to understand the coarse cohomology of
(X/\bar{A},d_{A})
.
Unfortunately, Theorem
1.1
does not apply to the computation of
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})
because the space
(X/\bar{A},d_{A})
is rarely uniformly contractible even if
X
is.
On the other hand, if
X
is uniformly contractible, then
(X/\bar{A},d_{A})
retains those properties at infinity in some sense which we describe next.

We say
X
is
uniformly contractible at infinity
if there exist two non-decreasing control function
\rho,\mu:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a basepoint
b\in X
such that any set
B
of diameter
r
is contractible inside a set of diameter
\rho(r)
if
d(b,B)\geq\mu(r)
.

Our main theorem in this article is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
X
is uniformly contractible at infinity,
then its coarse cohomology
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X)
is isomorphic to its boundedly supported cohomology
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X)
.

We will define
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X)
later (Definition
2
) in the paper.
In particular, when
X
is proper and contractible,
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X)
coincides with the compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology (Example
2.2
).
Hence, Theorem
1.3
generalizes Theorem
1.1
.
In general,
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X)
is isomorphic to the reduced Alexander–Spanier cohomology of
X
at infinity with degree shifted down by one (Proposition
2.3
).
As a consequence of Theorem
1.3
, we prove the following.

Corollary 1.4
.

Suppose
X
is uniformly contractible at infinity.
Let
A\overset{}{\subset}X
so that
X\neq N_{r}(A)
for any
r
. Then
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)=\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*-1}$}(X-N_{r}(A))
for
*\geq 1
.

The hypothesis of Theorem
1.3
is much weaker than the hypothesis of Roe’s Theorem
1.1
.
In particular, the property of uniform contractibility at infinity is more robust compared to uniform contractibility.
For example, deleting a bounded set does not affect the property of being uniformly contractible at infinity.
This is not true for uniformly contractible spaces because such spaces are necessarily contractible and complement of bounded set in a contractible set might not be contractible anymore.
On the other hand, spaces satisfying uniform contractibility at infinity can be very far from being contractible (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
A subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that consists of countable union of circles
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and the ray
r:=[0,\infty)\times\{0\}
such that the
i^{th}
circle has radius
i
and distance between two consecutive circle grows to infinity. This is an example of a space that is uniformly contractible at infinity.

Our approach to prove Theorem
1.3
is different from the proof of Roe’s Theorem
1.1
in
[
3
]
.
The main part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
in
[
3
]
involve showing that coarse cohomology is isomorphic to the
\breve{\text{C}}
ech cohomology of certain open covers. In order to show that Roe relates this
\breve{\text{C}}
ech cohomology to certain presheaf cohomology using double complex.
Our approach to prove Theorem
1.3
follows an idea from
[
4
]
which is more geometric and direct in the sense that we explicitly construct (co)chain homotopy to establish the isomorphism between the concerned cohomology groups.

A space
X
is called
\emph{uniformlyacyclicatinfinity}
if there exist a functions
\rho:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and a basepoint
b\in X
such that any set
B
of diameter
r
, the inclusion
B\hookrightarrow N_{\rho(r)}(B)
induces trivial map between the singular homology. Another interesting aspect of the Theorem
1.3
is that it does not hold if we replace uniform contractibility at infinity by uniform acyclicity.
That means any proof of this theorem needed to be able to distinguish between uniform acyclicity and uniform contractibility. This makes the proof more subtle than one might initially expect it to be.

2.
Preliminaries

We introduce some notation for various cochain complexes and corresponding cohomology groups associated to
X
.
Let
R
be a ring.
The basic complex is the complex of all cochains with
d:\myc^{n-1}(X;R)\rightarrow\myc^{n}(X;R)
being the boundary maps as follows:

\displaystyle\myc^{n}(X;R)=\{\phi:X^{n+1}\to R\}

\displaystyle(d\phi)(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\phi(x_{0},\dots,\hat{x_{i}},\dots,x_{n})

It is an acyclic complex.

We will refer to points in
X^{n+1}
as
n
-simplices
.
We will refer to a continuous map
f:\Delta^{n}\rightarrow X
as a
singular
n
-simplex
.
We will often need to measure distances between simplices of different dimensions.
A convenient way to do this is to stabilize simplices by repeating the last coordinate, as follows.
Let
X^{\infty}
denotes the set of all eventually constant sequence in
X
equipped with the sup metric.
Let
i:X^{n+1}\to X^{\infty}
denote the map
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{0},\dots,x_{n},x_{n},x_{n},\dots)
.
For a cochain
\phi
define its stabilized support
\lvert\phi\rvert=\{i(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in X^{n+1}\text{ and }\phi(\sigma)\neq 0\}\overset{}{\subset}X^{\infty}
.

Let
\Delta=i(X)
denote the diagonal of
X^{\infty}
.
Let
||\phi||
be the intersection of the diagonal
\Delta=\{(x,x,\ldots,x)\mid x\in X\}\overset{}{\subset}X^{*+1}
and the closure of the support of the function
\phi:X^{*+1}\rightarrow R
.

Alexander–Spanier cohomology

Let
\myc^{*}_{0}(X;R)
be the complex of locally zero cochains:

\myc^{*}_{0}(X;R)=\{\phi\in\myc^{*}(X;R)\mid\lVert\phi\rVert=\emptyset\}.

Note that restriction of
d
gives a well defined map
\myc^{*}_{0}(X)\rightarrow\myc^{*+1}_{0}(X)
.
Consequently,
d
induces a well defined map
\myc^{*}_{as}(X;M)\rightarrow\myc^{*+1}_{as}(X;R)
, where

\myc^{*}_{as}(X;R)=\myc^{*}(X;R)/\myc^{*}_{0}(X;R).

Alexander–Spanier cohomology, denoted by
\myh^{*}(X;M)
, is the cohomology of the complex
(\myc^{*}_{as}(X;M),d)
.

Example 2.1
.

Alexander–Spanier cohomology groups coincide with the singular cohomology groups for locally finite complexes. On the other hand, they may be different for the spaces that are not locally connected. For instance, degree 1 Alexander–Spanier cohomology group of the Warsaw circle with coefficient in
\mathbb{Z}
is
\mathbb{Z}
whereas degree 1 singular cohomology group is trivial.

Compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology

Compactly supported cochains are the cochains from the following complex

\myc^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)=\{\phi\in\myc^{*}(X;R)\mid\lVert\phi\rVert\text{ is compact}\}.

Compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology
\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)
is the cohomology of the following cochain complex

\myc^{*}_{cas}(X;R)=\myc^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)/\myc^{*}_{0}(X;R).

with the usual boundary operator induced by
d
.

Boundedly supported cohomology

Now we define a natural generalization of compactly supported cochains, called boundedly supported cochains:

\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R):=\{\phi\in\myc^{*}(X;R)\mid||\phi||\text{ is bounded}\}

Boundedly supported cohomology
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)
is the cohomology of
\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)
with the usual boundary operator
d
.

Example 2.2
.

If
X
is proper and contractible, then

\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)=\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)
.
Indeed, if
X
is contractible, then
\myc^{*}_{0}(X;R)
is an acyclic complex. In this case, the sequence

0\to\myc^{*}_{0}\to\myc^{*}\to\myc^{*}_{cas}\to 0

gives
\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)=\myh^{*}(\myc^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R))
. Moreover, if
X
is proper, then
\myc^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)=\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)
.
Hence we get
\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;R)=\myh^{*}(\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R))=\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)
.
For example,

\displaystyle\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{Z})=\myh^{*}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{Z})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\text{if $*=n$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

In general, the boundedly supported cohomology is the same as the (reduced) Alexander–Spanier cohomology at infinity with degree shifted down by one.
More precisely we have the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

(1)

If
X
is bounded, then

\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)=\begin{cases}R&\text{if $*=0$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

If
X
is unbounded and
b\in X
, then

\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $*=0$}\\
\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*-1}$}(X-N_{r}(b);R)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof.

(
1
) If
X
is bounded then
\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)=\myc^{*}(X;R)
.
The cohomology of the latter complex is trivial except in degree 0.
Hence,
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)=0
for
*\geq 1
.

\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{0}(X;R)=\{\text{constant functions on $X$}\}\cong R
.

(
2
) Elements in
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{0}(X;R)
consists of constant functions defined on
X
with support contained in a neighborhood of
b
. This means, if
X
is not bounded, then
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{0}(X;R)
is trivial. Hence in this case
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{0}(X;R)=0
.

Consider the following maps between the cochain complexes where
j
is the inclusion map and
i
is induced by canonical restriction maps (followed by quotient maps)
i_{r}:\myc^{*}(X;R)\rightarrow\myc^{*}(X-N_{r}(b);R)\rightarrow\myc^{*}_{as}(X-N_{r}(b);R)
.

0\rightarrow\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)\xrightarrow{j}\myc^{*}(X;R)\xrightarrow{i}\varinjlim\myc^{*}_{as}(X-N_{r}(b);R)\rightarrow 0

The above is a short exact sequence because of the following

\displaystyle\ker(i)

\displaystyle=\{\phi\in\myc^{*}(X;R)\mid\phi\in\myc^{*}_{0}(X-N_{r}(A);R)\text{ for some r}\}

\displaystyle=\{\phi\in\myc^{*}(X;R)\mid||\phi||\text{ is bounded}\}

\displaystyle=\im(j)

The above short exact sequence of cochain complexes induces a long exact sequence of the corresponding reduced cohomology groups.
The reduced cohomology group of the middle complex is trivial in all degrees.
Hence, the long exact sequence implies that

\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)\cong\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*-1}$}(X-N_{r}(b);R)\text{ for }*\geq 1

∎

Example 2.4
.

Suppose
X
is the space appearing in figure
1
.
Since
X
is unbounded,
\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{0}(X)
is trivial.
Suppose
b\in X
. For
*\geq 1
, we have the following

\displaystyle\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X,\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*-1}$}(X-N_{r}(b);\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Pi_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{Z}/\oplus_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{Z}\quad&*=2\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We finish this section by briefly recalling the coarse cohomology.

Coarse cohomology

Coarse cohomology of a space
X
is the cohomology of the following cochain complex

\myc{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X;R):=\{\phi\in\myc^{*}(X;R)\mid|\phi|\cap N_{r}(\Delta)\text{ is bounded}\text{ for all }r\}

Example 2.5
.

According to Theorem
1.1
, coarse cohomology is isomorphic to compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology for uniformly contractible spaces. In particular, coarse cohomology of the universal cover of any compact aspherical space is isomorphic to its compactly supported Alexander–Spanier cohomology. For example,
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}
for
*=n
and is trivial otherwise.

3.
A warm-up theorem

A space is called
\emph{locallyacyclicatinfinity}
with coefficient in
R
if complement of some bounded subset in the space is locally acyclic with coefficients in
R
.
A space
X
is
\emph{uniformly}

\emph{acyclicatinfinity}
with coefficients in
R
if there exist two non-decreasing control functions
\mu,\rho:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and a basepoint
b\in X
such that any set
B
of diameter
r
and
d(b,B)\geq\mu(r)
, the inclusion
B\hookrightarrow N_{\rho(r)}(B)
induces trivial map between the singular homology with coefficients in
R
.
Sometimes, we will refer to such space as
(\mu,\rho)
-uniformly acyclic at infinity.

In this section we prove the following Theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
X
is uniformly acyclic at infinity and locally acyclic at infinity with coefficients in
R
, then the inclusion
\myc{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X;R)\hookrightarrow\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R)
induces an isomorphism on cohomology:

\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X;R)\cong\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X;R).

For a uniformly acyclic at infinity space, we can perform a version of the standard “connect the dots” construction and the proof of the above theorem relies on that.
Suppose
X
is
(\mu,\rho)
-uniformly contractible at infinity.
Every
1
-simplex
\sigma
of diameter
r
outside of the ball of radius
\mu(r)
is fillable, so we can pick a singular
1
-chain
f(\sigma)
, such that
\lvert f(\sigma)\rvert\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho(r)}(\sigma)
and
\partial f(\sigma)=\partial\sigma
.
Proceeding by induction on the dimension, if a simplex
\sigma
is sufficiently far from the base point, its boundary is already filled by a singular cycle that bounds a singular chain
f(\sigma)
contained in a controlled neighborhood of the simplex.
Moreover, if the space is locally acyclic at infinity, we can choose
f(\sigma)
to have small diameter whenever diameter of
\sigma
is small and is outside some bounded set.
In order to do that we can take the chain
f(\sigma)
to be of diameter
2k(\sigma)
where

k(\sigma):=\inf\{\diam(|c|)\mid\partial c=f(\partial\sigma)\}.

Here we are multiplying by
2
to ensure existence of such chain and that whenever
X
is locally acyclic at
x
, for any open neighborhood
U
of
x
, there exist another neighborhood
V\overset{}{\subset}U
of
x
, such that
|f(\sigma)|\overset{}{\subset}U
for all
\sigma\in V^{n+1}
.

Note that not every simplex is fillable, and that the diameter of fillings grow with dimension, as well as the size of the balls that we have to avoid.
To formalize the notion of sufficiently far, we make the following definition.
Given an increasing sequence of increasing control functions
\mu_{n}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, denote

\myc_{n}^{F}(X;R)=\langle\sigma^{n}\mid d(\sigma^{n},b)\geq\mu_{n}(\diam\sigma^{n})\rangle\overset{}{\subset}\myc_{n}(X;R)

Since
\mu_{n}
is increasing, this defines a subcomplex of the chain complex of finitely supported chains.
Let
V:\myc_{*}^{\mathrm{s}}(X;R)\to\myc_{*}(X;R)
denote the forgetful map, which maps a singular simplex to its vertices.
For the rest of the section we will suppress the coefficient
R
from the notation.
The previous discussion then gives us the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
X
is uniformly acyclic at infinity and is locally acyclic at infinity. Then there exist two non-decreasing sequence of control functions
\rho_{n},\mu_{n}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and
a map
M:\myc_{*}^{F}(X)\to\myc_{*}^{\mathrm{s}}(X)
where

\myc_{n}^{F}(X)=\langle\sigma^{n}\mid d(\sigma^{n},A)\geq\mu_{n}(\diam\sigma^{n})\rangle\overset{}{\subset}\myc_{n}(X)

such that

(1)

M
is a chain map.

(2)

\lvert M(\sigma^{n})\rvert\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{n}(\diam(\sigma^{n}))}(\sigma^{n})
.

(3)

There is bounded set
B\overset{}{\subset}X
such that for any
x\overset{}{\notin}B
, and a neighborhood
U
of
x
, there is a neighborhood
W
of
x
such that
\lvert M(\sigma^{n})\rvert\overset{}{\subset}U
for all
\sigma^{n}\in{W}^{n+1}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
X\overset{}{\subset}Y
. Let
f:\myc_{*}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{*}(Y)
be a chain map such that for any
n
-simplex
\sigma^{n}
,
|f(\sigma^{n})|\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{n}(\diam(\sigma^{n}))}(\sigma^{n})
for some non-decreasing sequence of function
\rho_{n}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
.
Then
f
and the inclusion map
i:\myc_{*}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{*}(Y)
are homotopic via a chain homotopy
D
such that
|D(\sigma)|\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{n}(\diam(\sigma^{n}))}(\sigma^{n})
.

Proof.

We can define
D
by induction on the dimension of
\sigma
.
Define
D_{0}(x):=(x,f(x))
. Note that
f(x)-x=\partial D(x)
and
D_{0}(x)\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{0}(\diam\{x\})}(x)
.
Suppose we have defined
D_{m}:\myc_{m}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{m+1}(X)
such that
D_{m}(\sigma)\overset{}{\subset}N_{\diam(\sigma)}(\sigma)
and
\partial D_{m}(\sigma)+D_{m-1}\partial(\sigma)=i(\sigma)-f(\sigma)
for any
m\leq n
.
To define
D_{n+1}(\sigma)
for an
(n+1)
-simplex
\sigma
, consider
c=i(\sigma)-f(\sigma)-D_{n}\partial(\sigma)
.
By induction hypothesis,
c
is a cycle and
|c|\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho(\diam(\sigma))}(\sigma)
.
Take a vertex
b
from the chain
c
, and consider the cone operator
T_{b}:\myc_{n+1}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{n+2}(X)
,
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+1})\mapsto(b,x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+1})
.
Define
D_{n+1}(\sigma):=T_{b}(c)
.
By construction,
\partial D_{n+1}(\sigma)=c=i(\sigma)-f(\sigma)-D_{n}\partial(\sigma)
and
|D_{n+1}(\sigma)|\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{n}(\diam(\sigma))}(\sigma)
.
∎

Let
\myc_{*}^{\mathcal{U}}(X)
be the complex of singular chains supported by
\mathcal{U}
.
Combining the previous two lemmas we can now prove the following which will be the main ingredient to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
X
is uniformly acyclic at infinity and locally acyclic at infinity. Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open cover of
X
.
For each
x\in X
, fix a set
U_{x}\in\mathcal{U}
that contains
x
.
Then there exist two increasing sequences of control functions
\mu_{n}
and
\rho_{n}
and
a chain map
S:\myc_{*}^{F}(X)\to\myc_{*}^{\mathcal{U}}(X)
where

\myc_{n}^{F}(X)=\langle\sigma^{n}\mid d(\sigma^{n},b)\geq\mu_{n}(\diam\sigma^{n})\rangle\overset{}{\subset}\myc_{n}(X)

and a map
G:\myc_{*}^{F}(X)\to\myc_{*+1}(X)
so that

(1)

G
is a chain homotopy between
VS
and the inclusion map
i:\myc_{*}^{F}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{*}(X)
:

\partial G(\sigma)=i(\sigma)-VS(\sigma)-G\partial(\sigma).

(2)

\lvert G(\sigma^{n})\rvert\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{n}(\diam(\sigma^{n}))}(\sigma^{n})
.

(3)

There exists a bounded set
B
such that for any point
x\overset{}{\notin}B
and a neighborhood
U
of
x
, there is a neighborhood
W
of
x
so that
|G(\sigma^{n})|\overset{}{\subset}U^{n+2}
for any
\sigma\in W^{n+1}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{*}
,
\rho_{*}
and
\myc_{*}^{F}(X)
be the ones provided by the lemma
3.2
.
We choose a barycentric subdivision map
p:\myc_{*}^{\mathrm{s}}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{*}^{\mathcal{U}}(X)
and compose it with the map
M
from lemma
3.2
to get the map
S:\myc_{*}^{F}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{*}^{\mathcal{U}}(X)
.
Since
|M(\sigma^{n})|\overset{}{\subset}N_{\rho_{n}(\diam(\sigma^{n}))}(\sigma^{n})
and
|p(M(\sigma^{n}))|\overset{}{\subset}|M(\sigma^{n})|
, it follows that

4.
Proof of the main theorem

In this section we prove our main theorem. We will continue to suppress the coefficient ring
R
from the notation.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
X
is uniformly contractible at infinity,
then the inclusion
\myc{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X)\hookrightarrow\myc{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X)
induces an isomorphism on cohomology:

\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X)\cong\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X).

The underlying idea of the proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
However, recall from Remark
3.7
that Theorem
4.1
does not hold if we replace uniform contractibility by uniform acyclicity.
This means any proof of the Theorem
4.1
needs to be able to distinguish between uniform
acyclicity and uniform contractibility.

Most of the work will go into proving Proposition
4.2
which is an analog of Proposition
3.4
when
X
is uniformly contractible at infinity.
The main difficulty of getting such analog is the absence of local acyclicity of
X
which was present in Proposition
3.4
.
To get around that, we exploit the Kuratowski’s embedding theorem
[
2
]
that says any metric space
X
can be isometrically embedded inside the locally acyclic space
\ell^{\infty}(X)
which is the space of all bounded sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
X
with the metric
d(\{x_{n}\},\{y_{n}\})=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{d(x_{n},y_{n})\}
.
We then take neighborhood of
X
inside
\ell^{\infty}(X)
.
This neighborhood will be locally acyclic but might not be uniformly acyclic which is another condition we absolutely need.
This tension make the proof of Proposition
4.2
more subtle than Proposition
3.4
.

For convenience, we will use
\ell^{\infty}
to mean
\ell^{\infty}(X)
for the rest of the paper.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose

in

of
\myc_{*}(X)
of the following form

and a map

with the following properties.

(1)

and the inclusion map

\partial G(\sigma)=VS(\sigma)-i(\sigma)-G\partial(\sigma)

Sketch of the proof:

As stated before, the main idea of the proof of Proposition
4.2
is similar to the Proposition
3.4
.
To get the map

by singular chains and then use barycentric subdivision on these chains until they fall inside
\myc_{*}^{\mathcal{U}}(X)
.
Using uniform contractibility of

at infinity, most of this filling process can be done inside

with the necessary control on the support as required by property (

).

But to have property (

), we need to fill small simplices by small singular chains. Unless

is locally acyclic this cannot be achieved by staying inside

.

This is where we use the ambient space
\ell^{\infty}
.
Since

is an open subset of

In summary, we fill ‘big’ simplices in

and ‘small’ simplices (small ones) in

The main difficulty is to choose these fillings in a compatible way so that it gives a chain map

If

which is already defined.
The problem is

To get around this problem, we first construct a chain map

that sends small simplices to singular chains which can be homotoped to the convex filling by staying inside

(Lemma

).
Convex filling of a simplex is the image of that simplex under the following chain map

\displaystyle c:

\displaystyle\myc_{*}(X)\rightarrow\myc_{*}^{\mathrm{s}}(\ell^{\infty})

\displaystyle(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto c(\sigma):(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n})\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}s_{i}x_{i}.

It is in the construction of this chain map

where we crucially use the fact that our space is uniformly contractible at infinity, not just uniformly acyclic at infinity.
Figure

illustrates that this construction cannot be performed when

is the Warsaw circle which is an acyclic space.

In the second step, we modify the chain map

5.
Computation of Coarse cohomology of the complement

We start by briefly reviewing the notion of coarse complement from
[
1
]
.
Roughly the idea is that coarse complement of a subset
A
in
X
is determined by the collection of subsets
\mathcal{S}
of
X
which are
coarsely disjoint
from
A
:

\mathcal{S}:=\{B\overset{}{\subset}X\mid N_{r}(B)\cap N_{r}(A)\text{ is bounded for any $r\geq 0$}\}

Coarse cohomology of the complement of
A
is defined to be the cohomology of the following complex

\myc{\mathrm{X}}^{n}(X-A)=\{\phi\in\myc^{n}(X)\mid\forall B\in\mathcal{S}\quad\phi|_{B}\in\myc{\mathrm{X}}^{n}(B)\}

with the usual coboundary operator
d
.
We denote the cohomology of the above complex by
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)
.
It was shown in
[
1
]
that
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)
can be computed as coarse cohomology of a single space which
we now recall from
[
1
]
.
Consider the following pseudometric
d_{A}
on
X
.

d_{A}(x,y):=\min\{d(x,A)+d(y,A),d(x,y)\}

Note that
d_{A}(x,y)=0
if and only if
(x,y)\in\bar{A}\times\bar{A}
where
\bar{A}
is the closure of
A
in
X
.
Hence the pseudometric
d_{A}
becomes a metric on the quotient space
X/\bar{A}
.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

The quotient map
q:X\rightarrow X/\bar{A}
induces isomorphism
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)\cong\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})
.

Proposition
5.1
allows us to use Theorem
4.1
to compute coarse cohomology of the complement.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
X/\bar{A}
is uniformly contractible at infinity
and unbounded, then

\displaystyle\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\text{if $*=0$}\\
\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*-1}$}(X-N_{r}(A))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof.

If
X/\bar{A}
is uniformly contractible at infinity, then Theorem
4.1
yields
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})=\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})
.
Combining this with Proposition
5.1
gives us
\myh{\mathrm{X}}^{*}(X-A)=\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})
.

Since
X/\bar{A}
is unbounded,
for any
b\in X/\bar{A}
,
Proposition
2.3
gives us the following

\displaystyle\myh{\mathrm{B}}^{*}(X/\bar{A})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\text{if $*=0$}\\
\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*-1}$}(X-N_{r}(A))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Finally we observe that
\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*}$}(X/\bar{A}-N_{r}(b))=\varinjlim\tilde{\operatorname{H}}\raisebox{0.5pt}{${}^{*}$}(X-N_{r}(A))
. That finishes the proof.
∎
