Few-photon transport in Fano-resonance waveguide geometries Preprint: APS/123-QED
Kristoffer B. Joanesarson, Jake Iles-Smith, Mikkel Heuck, Jesper Mørk

Abstract
We present a theoretical study of Fano interference effects in few-photon transport. Under appropriate conditions, a local defect in an optical waveguide induces a highly asymmetric transmission lineshape, characteristic of Fano interference. For a two-level emitter placed adjacent to such a defect, here modeled as a partially transmitting element, we find an analytical expression for the full time evolution of single-photon wavepackets and the emitter excitation probability. We show how the partially transmitting element affects the emitter lifetime and shifts the spectral position of the effective system resonances. Using input-output formalism, we determine the single and two-photon S S -matrices for both a two-level emitter and a cavity-emitter system coupled to a waveguide with a partially transmitting element. We show how the Fano interference effect can be exploited for the implementation of a Hong-Ou-Mandel switch in analogy with a tunable linear or nonlinear beam splitter.



I
Introduction

A long standing goal for quantum optics is the deterministic control of light at the single-photon level
Kimble 2008
. Optical waveguides coupled to (artificial) atoms are examples of promising candidates for guiding and manipulating the photonic qubits with the potential for scalable on-chip quantum information processing
Lodahl et al. 2015
. To efficiently manipulate the optical quantum states one can exploit that strong light-matter coupling can mediate photon-photon interactions and give rise to highly non-linear properties
Chang et al. 2014
;
Roy et al. 2017
. Examples of waveguide geometries with demonstrated strong light-matter interactions include nanofibres coupled to atoms
Mitsch et al. 2014
, superconducting waveguides coupled to tunable plasmon qubits
Mirhosseini et al. 2019
, plasmonic nanowires
Akimov et al. 2007
, and photonic crystal waveguides coupled to semiconductor quantum dots
Arcari et al. 2014
;
Javadi et al. 2015
.

Within few-photon scattering experiments and theoretical studies, the waveguide geometry is often modelled as an infinite or semi-infinite collection of harmonic oscillators coupled to one or more discrete-energy-level quantum systems such as a single two-level emitter (TLE)
Shen and Fan 2007
;
Nysteen et al. 2015a
, a Jaynes-Cummings (JC) system (i.e. a TLE coupled to a cavity)
Shi et al. 2011
;
Shen and Fan 2009a
;
Rephaeli and Fan 2012
, multi-level emitters
Witthaut and Sørensen 2010
;
Zheng et al. 2013
;
Das et al. 2018a
;
Das et al. 2018b
, multiple emitters
Rephaeli et al. 2011
;
Cheng et al. 2017
, whispering gallery mode cavities
Shen and Fan 2009b
, and a Kerr nonlinear cavity
Liao and Law 2010
;
Xu and Li 2014
. Common for these studies is that the transport properties for the waveguide geometries are almost always assumed to depend only on the specific type of discrete-energy-level quantum system and not on the waveguide properties. For the waveguide geometry in Fig.

The Fano interference effect for one and two-photon scattering in a waveguide coupled to a TLE was studied in the recent work by Xu and Fan

III
A Fano-resonance waveguide system with a two-level emitter – exact dynamics

In this section we consider a TLE symmetrically and frequency-independently coupled to a linearly dispersive waveguide with a PTE in the mirror-symmetry line. This particular geometry corresponds to the illustration in Fig.
1
(a) with the TLE in place of
\mathcal{S}
(System i. in Fig.
1
(d)). The Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\omega_{\mathrm{e}}\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}+\sum_{\mu=L,R}\int\differential{k}\,kb_{\mu}^{\dagger}(k)b_{\mu}(k)\\
&\quad+V\int\frac{\differential{k}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\differential{k'}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\quantity[ b_R^{\dagger}(k)b_L(k') + \mathrm{h.c.} ]\\

II

(

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}=\chi(t)\sigma_{+}\ket{\varnothing}+\sum_{\mu=L,R}\int\differential{k}\xi_{\mu}(t,k)b_{\mu}^{\dagger}(k)\ket{\varnothing}.

(8)

The coefficient

\displaystyle i\dot{\chi}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{e}\chi(t)+\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\differential{k}\,\quantity[\xi_R(t,k) + \xi_L(t,k)],

(9a)

\displaystyle i\dot{\xi}_{R}(t,k)

\displaystyle=k\xi_{R}(t,k)+\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\chi(t)+\frac{V}{2\pi}\int\differential{k'}\,\xi_{L}(t,k^{\prime}),

(9b)

\displaystyle i\dot{\xi}_{L}(t,k)

\displaystyle=k\xi_{L}(t,k)+\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\chi(t)+\frac{V}{2\pi}\int\differential{k'}\,\xi_{R}(t,k^{\prime}).

(9c)

Without a PTE, i.e.
V=0

;
Liao et al. 2015

\displaystyle\chi(t)

\displaystyle=\chi(t_{i})e^{-\quantity[\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}+i\omega_{\mathrm{e}}]\quantity(t-t_i)}-i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2\sqrt{\pi\Gamma}}\int\differential{k'}\,\xi(t_{i},k^{\prime})e^{-ik^{\prime}\quantity(t-t_i)}\frac{1-e^{-\quantity[\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}+i\qty(\omega_{\mathrm{e}}-k')]\quantity(t-t_i)}}{\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}+i\quantity(\omega_{\mathrm{e}}-k')},

with

III.1
Single-photon emission by an emitter relaxation process

Here we study the emitter relaxation process for an initially excited TLE in a waveguide with a PTE, corresponding to the geometry sketched in Fig.
1
(a) with system i. in place of
\mathcal{S}
. With
\chi(t_{i})=1
and
\xi_{R}(t_{i},k)=\xi_{L}(t_{i},k)=0
, Eq. (
10

\displaystyle\chi(t)=e^{-\quantity[\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}+i\omega_{\mathrm{e}}]\quantity(t-t_i)}.

(12)

The effect of the PTE is to increase the emitter lifetime by a factor of
\mathrm{Re}\{\quantity(1+iV/2)^{-1}\}^{-1}=1+(V/2)^{2}
. In the long-time limit,
t_{f}\longrightarrow\infty
, we find for the photon envelopes

\displaystyle\quantity|\xi_R(t_{f} , k)|^{2}=\quantity|\xi_L(t_f , k)|^{2}=\frac{\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi}}{1+\quantity(\frac{V}{2})^{2}}\quantity|\frac{1}{\qty(k-\omega_{\mathrm{e}})+i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}}|^{2}.

(13)

A photon generated by the relaxation process will thus have a Lorentzian spectrum even if we introduce a PTE. We see, however, that the center frequency is shifted by
\mathrm{Im}\{\tilde{\Gamma}/2\}=-\Gamma\frac{V}{2}(1+(V/2)^{2})^{-1}
. Both the frequency shift and altered emitter lifetime are characteristic of a Lamb shift and the Purcell effect induced by a change in the local density of states
Rybin et al. 2016
;
Lodahl et al. 2015

III.2
Single-photon scattering dynamics

We can use the analytical expressions for the state vector coefficients to calculate how a single photon scatters off the TLE as well as the probability of finding the emitter in the excited state as a function of time. In the following, we take the input state to be an initially right-propagating single photon, entering from the left side of the waveguide, with a Gaussian spectral envelope

\displaystyle\xi_{R}(k,t_{i})=\quantity(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2})^{1/4}e^{-\quantity(\frac{k-k_c}{2\sigma})^{2}}e^{-it_{i}\quantity(k-k_c)}.

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{e}}+\frac{\Gamma}{2}\times\mathrm{Im}\quantity{\frac{1}{1+\frac{iV}{2}}}\equiv\tilde{\omega}_{\mathrm{e}},

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}\times\mathrm{Re}\quantity{\frac{1}{1+\frac{iV}{2}}}.

IV
Few-photon scattering matrices

There are many ways of determining the scattering properties for waveguide systems considered in this work. One approach is by determining the full dynamics using a wavefunction approach, as we did in Sec.
III
, and calculating the asymptotic behavior. Such an approach can also be used to determine the evolution of a two-photon wavepacket in a waveguide coupled to a JC system
Stolyarov 2019
. If only the asymptotic wavepackets are of interest, however, determining the scattering matrix – or
S
-matrix – can offer a computational advantage. The
S
-matrix can be found in a number of ways by using e.g. the Lippmann-Schwinger formalism
Shen and Fan 2007
, path-integral techniques
Shi et al. 2015
, Dyson series
Hurst and Kok 2018
, or via the input-output relations using the method introduced by Fan et al. in
Fan et al. 2010
. In this section we will use the latter as this method is both common and transparent.

IV.1
Single-photon
S
-matrix

The single-photon
S
-matrix, which describes the scattering process of a single-photon input, is defined by

\displaystyle S_{p;k}^{\mu;\nu}={}_{\mu}\innerproduct{p^-}{k^+}_{\nu}=\matrixelement{\varnothing}{b_{\mathrm{out},\mu}(p)}{k^+}_{\nu},

(17)

where
\ket{p^{-}}
and
\ket{k^{+}}
are the scattering eigenstates defined in Sec.
II
. A single-photon state thus scatters according to

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\int\differential{k}\,\xi_{\mathrm{in},\nu}(k)b_{\nu}^{\dagger}(k)\ket{\varnothing}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\,\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}}=\sum_{\mu}\int\differential{p}\,\xi_{\mathrm{out},\mu}(p)b_{\mu}^{\dagger}(p)\ket{\varnothing},

(18)

where
\xi_{\mathrm{in},\nu}
(
\xi_{\mathrm{out},\mu}
) is the spectral single-photon input (output) envelope. From the definition of the
S
-matrix, the single-photon output envelopes are determined from

\displaystyle\xi_{\mathrm{out},\mu}(p)=\sum_{\nu}\int\differential{k}\,S_{p;k}^{\mu;\nu}\xi_{\mathrm{in},\nu}(k).

(19)

In order to determine
S_{p;k}^{\mu;\nu}
we first choose a specific waveguide geometry and determine a set of input-output relations.

IV.2
Input-output relations

The relation between incoming and outgoing fields can be described in terms of a set of input-output relations
Gardiner and Collett 1985
. In determining the input-output relations we assume a time-independent Hamiltonian similar to that of Eq. (
7
) but for an initially unspecified system and system-waveguide Hamiltonian term. Furthermore, we introduce coupling to additional, unspecified, waveguide channels, which account for dissipation into a reservoir
Rephaeli and Fan 2013
. The Hamiltonian then reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\sum_{\mu}\int\differential{k}\,kb_{\mu}^{\dagger}(k)b_{\mu}(k)+H_{\mathrm{s}}+H_{\mathrm{s-wg}}\\
&\quad+\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}V_{\mu,\nu}\int\frac{\differential{k}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\frac{\differential{k'}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}b_{\mu}^{\dagger}(k)b_{\nu}(k^{\prime}).\end{split}

(20)

The first and last terms are similar to the waveguide and PTE contributions in Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
2
), respectively. The second and third term correspond to the relevant system and its coupling to the waveguide, respectively. We use the Heisenberg equation of motion,
\dot{c}=-i\commutator{c}{H}
, where
c
is an arbitrary operator.
For the waveguide operators we use that
c=b_{\mu}(k)
and
[b_{\mu},H_{\mathrm{s}}]=0
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{b}_{\mu}(t,k)&=-i\commutator{b_{\mu}(t,k)}{H_{\mathrm{s-wg}}}\\
&\quad-ikb_{\mu}(t,k)-i\sum_{\nu/\mu}V_{\mu,\nu}\int\frac{\differential{k'}}{2\pi}b_{\nu}(t,k^{\prime}).\end{split}

(21)

This, as well as the input-output field operators as defined in Eq. (
4
), is sufficient to determine the full set of input-output relations and in turn the single-photon
S
-matrix. We illustrate this with two paradigmatic examples; first the case of a TLE and then a JC system coupled to a Fano-waveguide.

IV.3
Single-photon
S
-matrix – TLE

The single-photon
S
-matrix for a Fano-waveguide geometry coupled to a TLE in the mirror-symmetry line was first calculated in
Xu and Fan 2016
. We start by summarizing the results of
Xu and Fan 2016
before extending the analysis to include both a TLE and a cavity. We use the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\begin{split}H&=\omega_{\mathrm{e}}\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}+\sum_{\mu}\int\differential{k}\,kb_{\mu}^{\dagger}(k)b_{\mu}(k)\\
&\quad+\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}V_{\mu,\nu}\int\frac{\differential{k}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\frac{\differential{k'}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}b_{\mu}^{\dagger}(k)b_{\nu}(k^{\prime})\\
&\quad+\sum_{\mu}\int\differential{k}\quantity(\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{+} b_{\mu}(k) + \mathrm{h.c.}),\end{split}

(22)

which is similar to Eq. (
7
), except here we have accounted for an arbitrary number of waveguide modes. The total lifetime of the emitter is
1/\sum_{\mu}|\kappa_{\mu}|^{2}
. The relevant input-output equations are (see App.
B

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{-}(t)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{p;k}=\quantity[\mathbf{C} + i \frac{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{B}}{p-iA}]\delta(p-k).

\displaystyle=t_{B}-\frac{i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}}{2\Gamma}}{\quantity(p-\omega_{\mathrm{e}})+i\quantity(\tilde{\Gamma}+\gamma)/2},

(30a)

\displaystyle r(p)

\displaystyle=r_{B}-\frac{i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}}{2\Gamma}}{\quantity(p-\omega_{\mathrm{e}})+i\quantity(\tilde{\Gamma}+\gamma)/2},

The two-photon

\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
, and the input frequencies
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. The output directions are
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, and the output frequencies

and

\displaystyle S

{}_{p_{1}p_{2};k_{1}k_{2}}^{\mu_{1}\mu_{2};\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}={}_{\mu_{1}\nu_{2}}\innerproduct{p_1p_2^-}{k_1k_2^+}_{\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}

\displaystyle=S_{p_{1};k_{1}}^{\mu_{1};\nu_{1}}S_{p_{2};k_{2}}^{\mu_{2};\nu_{2}}+S_{p_{1};k_{2}}^{\mu_{1};\nu_{2}}S_{p_{2};k_{1}}^{\mu_{2};\nu_{1}}+iT_{p_{1}p_{2};k_{1}k_{2}}^{\mu_{1}\mu_{2};\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}.

(36)

The first two terms are the contributions from linear interactions. The third term is called the bound state term and accounts for the nonlinear interactions. For systems with a unique ground state and symmetrical waveguide coupling, the

;
Xu and Fan 2017

\displaystyle iT_{p_{1}p_{2};k_{1}k_{2}}^{\mu_{1}\mu_{2};\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}=\mathcal{M}_{p_{1},p_{2},k_{1},k_{2}}\delta(p_{1}+p_{2}-k_{1}-k_{2}),

B.3
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{p_{1},p_{2},k_{1},k_{2}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{\sqrt{2\Gamma}}\mathcal{G}(p_{1})\mathcal{G}(p_{2})\quantity[\mathcal{G}(k_1)+\mathcal{G}(k_2)],

(38)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(k)=\frac{\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{\sqrt{2\Gamma}}}{k-\omega_{\mathrm{e}}+i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}}.

(39)

Replacing the TLE for a JC system, we find (see App.
B.4
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{p_{1},p_{2},k_{1},k_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2ig}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{e}}(p_{1})\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{e}}(p_{2})\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{e-c}}(p_{1}+p_{2},k_{1},k_{2}),

(40)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{e-c}}(p_{1}+p_{2},k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}/2}{\sqrt{\Gamma/2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\quantity[p_1+p_2-2\omega_{\mathrm{c}} +i \tilde{\Gamma}]\quantity(\mathcal{G}_\mathrm{e}(k_1)+\mathcal{G}_\mathrm{e}(k_2))+2g\quantity(\mathcal{G}_\mathrm{c}(k_1)+\mathcal{G}_\mathrm{c}(k_2))}{\quantity[p_1+p_2 - \qty(\omega_{\mathrm{c}} + \omega_{\mathrm{e}} )+i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}]\quantity[p_1+p_2-2\omega_{\mathrm{c}} +i \tilde{\Gamma}]-2g^{2}},

(41)

and

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(k) \\ \mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{e}}(k)\endlx@physics@matrix)=\frac{1}{\quantity[k- \omega_{\mathrm{c}} +i \frac{\tilde{\Gamma} }{2}]\quantity[k -\omega_{\mathrm{e}} ]-g^{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \frac{\tilde{\Gamma}/2}{\sqrt{\Gamma/2}} \qty(k-\omega_{\mathrm{e}}) \\ \frac{\tilde{\Gamma}/2}{\sqrt{\Gamma/2}} g \endlx@physics@matrix).

V
Hong-Ou-Mandel switch

It has recently been experimentally demonstrated that the Fano effect can be used to make a tunable quantum optical filter in a waveguide
Foster et al. 2019
. Recent theoretical results have shown that the asymmetry of the Fano resonance can be used to suppress electron-phonon interactions in quantum dots
Denning et al. 2019
. In this section, we investigate how the Fano effect may be used to implement a tunable Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) switch. As was suggested by Roulet et al.
Roulet et al. 2016
, with circulators in optical waveguides and the ability to efficiently turn the HOM interference on and off, a configurable integrated on-chip circuit can be realized. As an example, they analyzed the scattering behavior of two initially counter-propagating photons in a waveguide coupled to a TLE. In this section we extend the analysis to include a PTE element in the waveguide, and investigate how the Fano interference effect may be exploited. We will assume that the two incident photons are identical, with Gaussian envelopes, i.e.

\displaystyle\ket{LR}=\int\differential{k_L}\xi(k_{L})b_{L}^{\dagger}(k_{L})\int\differential{k_R}\xi(k_{R})b_{R}^{\dagger}(k_{R})\ket{\varnothing},

(43)

where
\xi(k)
is the same spectral envelope as in Eq. (
14
). We define an ideal HOM switch as having the following properties

\displaystyle\ket{LR}\longrightarrow\begin{cases}\ket{LR}&\text{switch-OFF},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity[\ket{LL} + \ket{RR}]&\text{switch-ON},\end{cases}

(44)

where

\displaystyle\ket{LL(RR)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity[\int\dd{k} \xi(k)b_{L(R)}^{\dagger}(k)]^{2}\ket{\varnothing}.

(45)

In the switch-OFF configuration, the output state, just as the input, represents two photons propagating in different channels, whereas in the ON configuration, the output is a superposition of two photons propagating in the same channel. The latter is equivalent to a beam splitter transformation, except here the output arms are mapped onto the input arms.

V.1
Quasi monochromatic input photons

We first consider quasi monochromatic photons, i.e. the limit where
\sigma\rightarrow 0
(See Eq. (
14
)). Because we have assumed the photons to be quasi monochromatic, correlations arising from the nonlinear term
\mathcal{M}_{p_{1},p_{2},k_{1},k_{2}}
(Eq. (
38
)) in the scattering matrix effectively vanishes
Nysteen et al. 2015a
. In other words, the scattering event is linear, and the resulting output state is found by considering the two photons independently. This means, the switch is in the OFF configuration, i.e. the output state equals the input state
|LR\rangle
, when the transmission coefficient is either 0 or 1, as this corresponds to both photons being perfectly reflected or transmitted, respectively. Conversely, the ON-configuration is reached when the absolute squared transmission coefficient is
1/2
. In this case, each photon scatters into an equal superposition of being transmitted and reflected, but the terms corresponding to a counter-propagating output cancel. From the spectra in Fig.
3
(a) we can therefore easily identify the ON and OFF configurations indicated by the green and red regions, respectively. For
V=0
(row 1) the OFF configuration is reached when the two photons are either far detuned from the emitter, or when the two photons both are resonant with the emitter. In the former case, each photon will transmit without interaction and in the latter case, the two photons will both reflect as if there were a mirror. For the Lorentzian spectrum in row 1, the ON configuration is reached when the photon-emitter detuning is
\omega=\pm\Gamma/2
.

An unaddressed caveat for the protocol by Roulet et al.
Roulet et al. 2016
is that in order to realize the ON-configuration in the absence of a PTE (
V=0
), the emitter must be tuned to the energy where the transmission lineshape is the steepest, making the protocol highly susceptible to errors. In case of a balanced PTE, the ON-configuration is reached when the emitter-photon detuning is large compared to the waveguide coupling rate,
\Gamma
, or when
k_{c}-\omega_{\mathrm{e}}=\tilde{\omega}_{\mathrm{e}}
(see row 2 of Fig.
3
(a)). The OFF-configuration is reached for
k_{c}-\omega_{\mathrm{e}}=\tilde{\omega}_{\mathrm{e}}\pm(2+\sqrt{2})\Gamma/8
. Both the ON and OFF-configurations can thus be realized at frequencies where the derivative of the transmission coefficient is (arbitrarily close to) 0, thus suggesting that introducing a balanced PTE in the waveguide results in a HOM switch that is more robust with respect to small perturbations in both the emitter and photon energy. The price of achieving this, however, is that it requires an emitter detuning of several linewidths. For a PTE strength of
V=2
, the transmission spectrum is again Lorentzian, with ON-configurations at points where the slope is steep (row 3 of Fig.
3
(a)).

The analysis of the TLE can be carried over to the JC system: The OFF-configuration is reached for a transmission coefficient of zero or unity, whereas the ON-configuration is reached when the absolute squared transmission coefficient equals
1/2
. For the balanced PTE, this again means that both the ON-and OFF-configurations can be reached at points where the slope is zero (see Fig.
3
(b) and (c)).

V.2
Finite-width envelopes

As a final investigation, we consider the scattering of counter-propagating photons with finite spectral width in Fano-resonance waveguides. As has been shown for the case of a TLE in a waveguide with
V=0
, strong scattering-induced photon correlations are possible for envelope widths comparable to the waveguide coupling rate
Nysteen et al. 2015b
. In this connection, it was also suggested how the TLE can be used as a beam splitter for photons with envelopes of finite width. For Gaussian envelopes centered around
\omega_{\mathrm{e}}
, the scattering properties depend highly on the envelope width
\sigma
(see Fig.
4
(a)). For spectrally narrow or broad envelopes, two counter propagating photons will continue to be counter-propagating post scattering, i.e. the OFF-configuration is achieved. The reason for the former was explained in the previous subsection. The reason for the latter can most easily be explained by considering the wide distribution in frequencies; the photon is practically everywhere far-detuned from the emitter and thus does not interact with it. For
\sigma=0.43\,\Gamma
, the probability of a HOM-like interference is maximized. For this width the trade-off between having too narrow and too broad spectral envelopes is minimized. The ON-configuration is reached with an error probability (the probability of scattering into a counter-propagating output state,
|LR\rangle
)), of
0.11
.

VI
Summary

In this work we have analyzed the effect of Fano interference on few-photon scattering in waveguide geometries containing a partially transmitting element. In Sec.
I
we briefly motivated the study of such waveguide geometries, and in Sec.
II
we described the model Hamiltonian for a waveguide with a partially transmitting element as introduced in
Xu and Fan 2016
. The work presented in this paper contributes to the study of Fano waveguide geometries in a quantum optical context by analyzing dynamical single-photon scattering, scattering of photons with finite-width envelopes, and providing analytical expressions for single and two-photon
S
-matrices for more complicated waveguide geometries than studied previously. Below we give a brief summary of our results presented in Sec.
III
to Sec.
V
.

In Sec.
III
we found analytical expressions for the single-excitation dynamics for a two-level emitter in a waveguide with a partially transmitting element in the mirror-symmetry line. We showed that the emitter lifetime increases with the strength of the partially transmitting element, and that the emitted photons are redshifted. Furthermore, we determined the conditions for which a single-photon with Gaussian envelope maximizes the emitter excitation probability. In the limit of a vanishing partially transmitting element, we reproduce the results of e.g.
Chen et al. 2011
and
Liao et al. 2015
.

In Sec.
IV
we established a way of calculating the single-photon
S
-matrix for a general Fano-waveguide geometry including the effect of dissipation. We also determined the two-photon S-matrix for a two-level emitter and a Jaynes-Cummings system. In case of a TLE, we showed that the results agree with that of Xu and Fan
Xu and Fan 2016
. In case of a Jaynes-Cummings system, we obtain the same
S
-matrix coefficients as
Shen and Fan 2009a
in the limit of a vanishing partially transmitting element.

In Sec.
V
we discussed the switching properties of a Fano-waveguide geometry and compared the results to that of a waveguide without a partially transmitting element, as studied by Roulet et al. in
Roulet et al. 2016
. We showed that for quasi-monochromatic photons, the Fano interference effect can be exploited to generate tunable Hong-Ou-Mandel interference between counter-propagating photons in a way that is robust against small changes in the emitter energy. For finite width-envelopes, we investigated the Fano interference effect on the induced spectral correlations and found no advantage compared to the case without a Fano interference.
