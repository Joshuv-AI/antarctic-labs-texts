Generalization of five q q -series identities of Ramanujan and unexplored weighted partition identities Thanks: 2020 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 11P81, 11P84; Secondary 05A17. Keywords and phrases. q q -Series, Divisor functions, Bressoud-Subbarao’s identity, Weighted partition identities
Subhash Chand Bhoria, Pramod Eyyunni, Bibekananda Maji

Abstract
. Ramanujan recorded five interesting q q -series identities in a section that is not as systematically arranged as the other chapters of his second notebook. These five identities do not seem to have acquired enough attention. Recently, Dixit and the third author found a one-variable generalization of one of the aforementioned five identities. From their generalized identity, they were able to derive the last three of these q q -series identities, but didn’t establish the first two. In the present article, we derive a one-variable generalization of the main identity of Dixit and the third author from which we successfully deduce all the five q q -series identities of Ramanujan. In addition to this, we also establish a few interesting weighted partition identities from our generalized identity. In the mid 1980 1980 ’s, Bressoud and Subbarao found an interesting identity connecting the generalized divisor function with a weighted partition function, which they proved by means of a purely combinatorial argument. Quite surprisingly, we found an analytic proof for a generalization of the identity of Bressoud and Subbarao, starting from the fourth identity of the aforementioned five q q -series identities of Ramanujan.

Introduction

Many beautiful
q
-series identities can be found in Ramanujan’s notebooks. At the end of the second notebook
[
28
, pp. 354–355]
,
[
30

,
Ramanujan mentioned a list of five
q
-series identities. Before stating these identities,
we present some notations indispensable to the theory of
q
-series. If

q
is a complex number with
|q|<1
, then the
q
-Pochhammer symbol is defined as

\displaystyle(A)_{0}

\displaystyle:=(A;q)_{0}=1,\quad(A)_{n}:=(A;q)_{n}=(1-A)(1-Aq)\cdots(1-Aq^{n-1}),\quad n\geq 1,

\displaystyle(A)_{\infty}

\displaystyle:=(A;q)_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(A;q)_{n}.

We now state the list of five identities given by Ramanujan.

Entry 1:
For
a\neq 0,\ 1-bq^{n}\neq 0,\ n\geq 1
,

\frac{(-aq)_{\infty}}{(bq)_{\infty}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-b/a)_{n}a^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}}{(q)_{n}(bq)_{n}}.

(1.1)

It is also recorded in the Lost Notebook
[
29
, p. 370]
.

Entry 2:

Let
1-aq^{n}\neq 0
for
n\geq 1
. Then

(aq)_{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{na^{n}q^{n^{2}}}{(q)_{n}(aq)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}a^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.2)

Entry 3:

For
a\neq 0,
and
1-bq^{n}\neq 0,\ n\geq 0
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-q^{n})(b)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{n}-b^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.3)

Letting
a\rightarrow 0
, and replacing
b
by
aq
, one can obtain the next identity.

Entry 4:

For
1-aq^{n}\neq 0,\ n\geq 1
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}a^{n}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(aq)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.4)

The last identity is the following:

Entry 5:

For
1-aq^{n}\neq 0,\ n\geq 0
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{n}(q)_{n-1}}{(1-q^{n})(a)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{na^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.5)

These five identities were first proved by Berndt, (see

[
12
, pp. 262–265]
for more information). Infact, the ordering Entries

through

given above is due to Berndt.
As is the case with (

), one can deduce (
1.5
) also from (
1.3
).
Equation (

) was rediscovered independently by Uchimura
[

.
Around the same time as Ramanujan, the special case

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.6)

He proved that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nq^{n}(q^{n+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-q^{n})(q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

(1.7)

Bressoud and Subbarao

•

Let

be the number of positive divisors of
n
, then the identity of Bressoud and Subbarao reads

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}s(\pi)=d(n).

(1.8)

In the same paper
[
13
]
, using combinatorial arguments, they also derived a more general identity involving the generalized divisor function,

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}=\sum_{d|n}d^{m},

(1.9)

for any integer
m\geq 0
. But they didn’t mention a generating function identity corresponding to (
1.9
).
The identity (
1.8
) was rediscovered by Fokking, Fokking and Wang
[
20
]
and the importance of this identity is that
it connects the divisor function and the partition function.

In 2013, Andrews, Garvan and Liang
[
10
, Theorem 3.5]
established a one variable generalization of (
1.6
), namely, for
|cq|<1
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{FFW}(c,n)q^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-cq^{n})(q)_{n}}=\frac{1}{1-c}\left(1-\frac{(q)_{\infty}}{(cq)_{\infty}}\right),

(1.10)

where

\textup{FFW}(c,n):=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\left(1+c+\cdots+c^{s(\pi)-1}\right).

Recently, Dixit and Maji
[
16
]
obtained a one variable generalization of (
1.3
) and from this generalized identity,
they were able to connect many well-known
q
-series identities in the literature. Not only that, they also gave partition theoretic
interpretations of many of the
q
-series identities they found. But they were unable to relate (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)
to their generalized identity. Here we state the main identity of Dixit and Maji.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a,b,c
be three complex numbers such that
|a|<1
and
|cq|<1
. Then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-cq^{n})(b)_{n}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/c)_{m}c^{m}}{(b)_{m}}\left(\frac{aq^{m}}{1-aq^{m}}-\frac{bq^{m}}{1-bq^{m}}\right).

(1.11)

In addition, if
|b|<\min\{|c|,1\}
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}a^{n}}{(1-cq^{n})(b)_{n}}=\frac{(b/c)_{\infty}}{(b)_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(c)_{n}(b/c)^{n}}{(q)_{n}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{m}-b^{m}}{1-cq^{m+n}}.

(1.12)

In the present article, one of our main goals is to derive a one variable generalization of Theorem
1.1
.
Putting
c=1
in (
1.12
), one can easily derive (
1.3
). Again, letting
a\rightarrow 0
and replacing
b
by
zq
,
Dixit and Maji obtained the following one-variable generalization of Andrews, Garvan and Liang’s (
1.10
) result, namely,
for
|cq|<1
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}z^{n}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(1-cq^{n})(zq)_{n}}=\frac{z}{c}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(zq/c)_{n-1}}{(zq)_{n}}(cq)^{n}.

(1.13)

This identity turned out to be a rich source of weighted partition identities. (see
[
16
, p. 326]
)
In the next section, we state our one-variable generalization of Theorem
1.1
and its implications,
which includes a two variable generalization of the result of Andrews, Garvan and Liang
and a beautiful identity of Andrews.

2.
Main Results

We proceed to state a one-variable generalization of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
a,b,c,d
be four complex numbers such that
|ad|<1
and
|cq|<1
. Then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(b/a)_{n}(c/d)_{n}(ad)^{n}}{(b)_{n}(cq)_{n}}=\frac{(a-b)(d-c)}{(ad-b)}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{m}(bd/c)_{m}c^{m}}{(b)_{m}(ad)_{m}}\left(\frac{adq^{m}}{1-adq^{m}}-\frac{bq^{m}}{1-bq^{m}}\right).

(2.1)

Substituting
d=1
in (
2.1
), one can immediately obtain (
1.11
).
Letting
{a\to 0}
and
b=zq
in (
2.1
), we get a two-variable generalization of the result of
Andrews, Garvan and Liang, namely, (
1.10
).

Theorem 2.2
.

For
|cq|<1
, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{n}(c/d)_{n}d^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}}{(zq)_{n}(cq)_{n}}=\frac{z(c-d)}{c}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(zdq/c)_{n-1}(cq)^{n}}{(zq)_{n}}.

(2.2)

Putting

in (
2.2
) and employing the
q
-binomial theorem on the right side, one can obtain (

).
Now letting

in (
2.2
), we arrive at a beautiful
q
-series identity of Andrews
[
8
, Corollary 2.2, p. 24]

Theorem 2.3
.

For
|cq|<1
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}c^{n}q^{n^{2}}}{(zq)_{n}(cq)_{n}}=z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(cq)^{n}}{(zq)_{n}}.

(2.3)

At an initial glance, we see that the left side is symmetric in
z
and
c
, where as the expression on the right side doesn’t seem to be,
but it is indeed symmetric in
z
and
c
. Replacing
c
by
1/z
, one can see that both the sides represent the following
rank generating function:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{(zq)_{n}(z^{-1}q)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z(q/z)^{n}}{(zq)_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}N(m,n)z^{m}q^{n},

(2.4)

where
N(m,n)
denotes the number of partitions of
n
with rank
m
.
Theorem
2.3
is one of the crucial results which helped us to derive Entry
2
, namely, (
1.2
).

It would be unfair not to mention the following pleasant generalization of Bressoud-Subbarao’s identity (
1.9
)
in the main results. The said generalization is as follows.

Theorem 2.4
.

For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
we have,

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j}=\sum_{d|n}d^{m}a^{d}.

(2.5)

By letting
a=1
, we immediately derive the identity (
1.9
) of Bressoud and Subbarao.

Remark 1
.

We would like to remark that Bressoud and Subbarao gave a combinatorial proof of their result on the assumption that
m
is a positive integer. But,
as we have seen, their result actually holds for all integers
m
.

An analogous result to Bressoud-Subbarao’s partition identity may be obtained by plugging
a=-1
in Theorem
2.4
, yielding

Corollary 2.5
.

Given an integer
m
and a positive integer
n
, the following identity holds.

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\mathrm{rank}(\pi)+s(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}(-1)^{j}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}=\sum_{d|n}(-1)^{d}d^{m}.

(2.6)

4.
Proof of the main result and its applications

We shall start the proof by recalling a
{}_{3}\phi_{2}
transformation formula in
[
22
, p. 359, (III.9)]
:

\displaystyle{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}A,&B,&C\\
&D,&E\end{matrix}\,;q,\frac{DE}{ABC}\right]=\frac{\big(\frac{E}{A}\big)_{\infty}\big(\frac{DE}{BC}\big)_{\infty}}{\big(E\big)_{\infty}\big(\frac{DE}{ABC}\big)_{\infty}}{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}A,&\frac{D}{B},&\frac{D}{C}\\
&D,&\frac{DE}{BC}\end{matrix}\,;q,\frac{E}{A}\right].

Setting
A=q,\ B=\frac{bq}{a},\ C=\frac{cq}{d},\ D=bq,\ E=cq^{2}
above, we get,

\displaystyle{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}q,&\frac{bq}{a},&\frac{cq}{d}\\
&bq,&cq^{2}\end{matrix}\,;q,ad\right]=\frac{(cq)_{\infty}(adq)_{\infty}}{(cq^{2})_{\infty}(ad)_{\infty}}{}_{3}\phi_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}q,&a&\frac{bd}{c}\\
&bq,&adq\end{matrix}\,;q,cq\right].

In other words,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q)_{n}(bq/a)_{n}(cq/d)_{n}(ad)^{n}}{(bq)_{n}(cq^{2})_{n}(q)_{n}}

5.
Proof of Bressoud-Subbarao’s partition identity

The identities of Ramanujan continue to unearth many mathematical treasures hidden under them.
A glimpse of one such instance may be seen in the present section wherein we found that Bressoud-Subbarao’s identity (

is nothing more than an application of successive differentiation to the partition interpretation of Entry

(Equation (

Indeed, using a couple of simple operators, we prove a generalization of Bressoud-Subbarao’s partition identity, namely, Theorem
2.4
.

Definition 1
.

Let
f(a)
be a polynomial in ‘
a
’. We define the operators ‘
D
’ and ‘
I
’ respectively by

D[f(a)]:=a\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\{f(a)\}
, and

I[f(a)]:=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{f(t)}{t}dt
.

We begin with a few crucial lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Entry
4
has an appealing weighted partition implication, namely,

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+1}\left(\frac{a^{s(\pi)}-1}{a-1}\right)=\sum_{d|n}a^{d}.

(5.1)

We would like to remark here that (
5.1
) is Corollary
2.6
of Dixit and Maji, see
[
16
]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N},\ r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and let
F_{0}(a):=a^{r}(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{k})
. Then, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

D^{m}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=(r+1)^{m}a^{r+1}+(r+2)^{m}a^{r+2}+\cdots+(r+k)^{m}a^{r+k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(r+j)^{m}a^{r+j}.

(5.2)

We have

\displaystyle D^{1}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=D\left[F_{0}(a)\right]

\displaystyle=a\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left\{F_{0}(a)\right\}=a\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left\{a^{r}(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{k})\right\}

\displaystyle=a\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\{a^{r+j}\}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(r+j)a^{r+j},

so that (
5.2
) holds for
m=1
.
Suppose that Equation (
5.2
) holds for some integer
p\geq 1
, that is,

D^{p}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(r+j)^{p}a^{r+j}.

Now,

\displaystyle D^{p+1}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]

\displaystyle=D\left\{D^{p}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]\right\}=a\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}(r+j)^{p}a^{r+j}\right\}

\displaystyle=a\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left\{(r+j)^{p}a^{r+j}\right\}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(r+j)^{p+1}a^{r+j}.

This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Similarly, we have the following result for the operator
I
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
k,r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and let
F_{0}(a):=a^{r}(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{k})
. Given any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
we have

I^{m}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=\frac{a^{r+1}}{(r+1)^{m}}+\frac{a^{r+2}}{(r+2)^{m}}+\cdots+\frac{a^{r+k}}{(r+k)^{m}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{a^{r+j}}{(r+j)^{m}}.

(5.3)

Firstly, consider

\displaystyle I^{1}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=I\left[F_{0}(a)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}\frac{F_{0}(t)}{t}=\int_{0}^{a}t^{r}(1+t+\cdots+t^{k-1})dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{0}^{a}t^{r+j}dt=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{a^{r+j}}{r+j},

which means that (
5.3
) is true for
m=1
.
We now assume that Equation (
5.3
) holds for some integer
p\geq 1
,
thus,

I^{p}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{a^{r+j}}{(r+j)^{p}}.

Now,

\displaystyle I^{p+1}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]=I\left\{I^{p}\left[F_{0}(a)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}\frac{I^{p}\left[F_{0}(t)\right]}{t}dt=\int_{0}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t^{r+j-1}}{(r+j)^{p}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{a^{r+j}}{(r+j)^{p+1}},

and so we are done.
∎We are now all set to prove a generalization of Bressoud-Subbarao’s identity, namely, Equation
(
2.5
).

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

Applying the differential operator
D
successively
m
times on both sides of identity (
5.1
), we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}D^{m}\left(a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+1}\left(\frac{a^{s(\pi)}-1}{a-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|n}D^{m}\left(a^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}D^{m}\left(a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)}\left(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{s(\pi)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|n}D^{m}\left(a^{d}\right).

(5.4)

Note that
\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
s(\pi)\in\mathbb{N}
for
each
\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)
. So we are in a position to apply
Lemma
5.2

to each of the polynomials
F_{0}(a;\pi):=a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)}(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{s(\pi)})

as
\pi
ranges over the collection
\mathcal{D}(n)
.
Also, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle D^{m}\left[a^{d}\right]=d^{m}a^{d}.

Thus the partition identity (
5.4
) now becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}D^{m}\left[F_{0}(a;\pi)\right]=\sum_{d|n}d^{m}a^{d}.

Appealing to Lemma
5.2
, we get

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j}=\sum_{d|n}d^{m}a^{d},

(5.5)

which is nothing but identity (
2.5
) of Theorem
2.4

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Equation (
5.1
) is evidently the case
m=0
of
(
2.5
). We are left with the case when
m
is a negative integer. Suppose

m=-u
, where
u
is a positive integer. Here the
operator
I
plays an analogous role to that of
D
in the above discussion. We now
start by applying
I
to (
5.1
).

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}I^{u}\left(a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+1}\left(\frac{a^{s(\pi)}-1}{a-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|n}I^{u}\left(a^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}I^{u}\left(a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)}\left(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{s(\pi)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|n}I^{u}\left(a^{d}\right).

(5.6)

We now apply Lemma
5.3
to each of

F_{0}(a;\pi):=a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)}\left(a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{s(\pi)}\right),\ \pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)
. Equation (
5.6
) now takes the form

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}I^{u}\left[F_{0}(a;\pi)\right]=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}\frac{a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j}}{(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{u}}=\sum_{d|n}\frac{a^{d}}{d^{u}}.

In other words, for negative integers
m
, we have

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{s(\pi)}a^{\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j}(\ell(\pi)-s(\pi)+j)^{m}=\sum_{d|n}a^{d}d^{m}.

(5.7)

Combining (
5.5
) and (
5.7
), along with the case
m=0
,
we complete the proof of Theorem
2.4
.
∎

Remark 4
.

It may seem artificial to differentiate the partition implication of a
q
-series identity rather than the identity itself (namely Entry
4

in this case), which is the usual practice in the theory of
q
-series and partitions. But when we proceed to apply the operator
D

successively
k
times to Entry
4
, we arrive at

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{1-q^{n}}D^{k}\left(\frac{a^{n}}{(aq)_{n}}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{k}a^{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

In the above equation, we see that multiple differentiation on the left side
would become complicated owing to the presence of the
\frac{1}{(aq)_{n}}
factor.
This is mitigated by differentiating the partition implication instead, namely, Equation
(
5.1
). This has an added advantage that, we could ‘reverse’ the process and apply the integral operator
I
too, thereby extending
Bressoud-Subbarao’s identity to negative integers. To the best of our knowledge, this integral operator has not been applied to
q
-series identities directly.

On many occasions, it is seen that the inherent beauty of a

We let

(6.1)

We now study the special cases of (

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(q)_{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(-q)_{n}^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}q^{n}}{1+q^{n}}.

(6.2)

Start with the right side above, writing it as

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{-q^{n}}{1-(-q^{n})}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{m\cdot n}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d|k}(-1)^{d+\frac{k}{d}}\right)q^{k}.

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(q)_{n-1}q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}}{(-q)_{n}^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{q(1-q)}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{q^{2}(1-q^{2})}{(1+q^{2})^{2}}\cdots\frac{q^{n-1}(1-q^{n-1})}{(1+q^{n-1})^{2}}\cdot\frac{q^{n}}{(1+q^{n})^{2}}.

6.16
), we conjugate the set of partitions over which the sum runs,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\ast}(n)\\
s(\pi)=2,\ \ell(\pi)\geq 3\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)}\nu(\ell(\pi)-1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{D}_{1}(n)\\
\nu(\ell(\pi))=2,\ \#(\pi)\geq 3\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\#(\pi)}(s_{2}(\pi)-s(\pi)),

where
\mathcal{D}_{1}(n)
and
s_{2}(\pi)
are as defined in Theorem
2.8
.
Putting our observations together, the case
t\rightarrow 1
of the identity (

) thus gives us

d(n)=1+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}(n)}(-1)^{\#(\pi)-1}(s_{2}(\pi)-s(\pi)).

7.
Concluding Remarks

In this article, we have established a one-variable generalization of the main identity of Dixit and Maji
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
. From this,
all five entries of Ramanujan now follow as corollaries. We have seen that from one of these entries, namely Entry
4
, we were able to derive a
one-parameter generalization of the identity of Bressoud and Subbarao, namely, Theorem
2.4
,
by using the operators
D
and
I
. From the generalized identity, that is, Theorem
2.1
,
we found a few interesting partition-theoretic implications, including some in the spirit of
Bressoud and Subbarao, connecting certain divisor functions with weighted partition functions.

It would be desirable to find bijective proofs of these identities, for example, say (
2.10
) and (
2.6
), which is
the identity analogous to that of Bressoud and Subbarao obtained by putting
a=-1
in (
2.5
). Readers can further investigate the operators
D
and
I
,
especially in connection to newer weighted partition identities of Bressoud-Subbarao type.
It would also be fascinating to see if the one-parameter generalization of Bressoud-Subbarao’s identity, namely, (
2.5
)
holds for any complex number
m
, and not just for integers. Offcourse, in that case, we have to take recourse to some other technique.
