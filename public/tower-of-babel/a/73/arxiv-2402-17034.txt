Stellarator equilibrium axis-expansion to all orders in distance from the axis for arbitrary plasma beta
Wrick Sengupta \aff 1 \corresp, Eduardo Rodriguez \aff 3, Rogerio Jorge \aff 4,5, Matt Landreman \aff 6, Amitava Bhattacharjee \aff 1

Abstract
A systematic theory of the asymptotic expansion of the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equilibrium in the distance from the magnetic axis is developed to include arbitrary smooth currents near the magnetic axis. Compared to the vacuum and the force-free system, an additional magnetic differential equation must be solved to obtain the pressure-driven currents. It is shown that there exist variables in which the rest of the MHD system closely mimics the vacuum system. Thus, a unified treatment of MHD fields is possible. The mathematical structure of the near-axis expansions to arbitrary order is examined carefully to show that the double-periodicity of physical quantities in a toroidal domain can be satisfied order by order. The essential role played by the Birkhoff–Gustavson normal form in solving the magnetic differential equations is highlighted. Several explicit examples of vacuum, force-free, and MHD equilibrium in different geometries are presented.



1
Introduction

Understanding the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equilibrium in magnetically confined devices is paramount. The theory of axisymmetric MHD equilibrium, applicable to tokamaks, is well-developed at this point. The axisymmetric problem can be reduced to solving a nonlinear elliptic partial differential equation (PDE) called the Grad-Shafranov equation
(
Grad 1971
;
Freidberg 1982
)
. In contrast, the 3D theory cannot be reduced to a single elliptic PDE
(
Weitzner 2014
;
Helander 2014
;
Bauer et al. 2012b
)
. Analytical understanding is obstructed by the intrinsic nonlinearity of the MHD system, coupled with the three-dimensional nature of the 3D equilibrium, which is generic for stellarators. These obstacles hinder an intuitive understanding of important physical effects, such as the deformations of the vacuum plasma surfaces due to pressure-driven currents or the dependence of magnetic shear on the geometry. Large-scale numerical computation has taught us much about stellarator equilibrium, but simulations can be expensive. Furthermore, the computational error can be significant, particularly near the magnetic axis
(
Panici et al. 2022
)
. Therefore, obtaining an analytical understanding of 3D stellarator MHD equilibrium is beneficial for computational studies and their applications to experiments.

In this work, we shall focus on an asymptotic expansion called the near-axis expansion (NAE) developed by several authors
(
Mercier 1964
;
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
;
Garren & Boozer 1991
;
Bernardin & Tataronis 1985
;
Weitzner 2016
)
. The basic idea is to expand the MHD equilibrium equations in a power series in the distance from the magnetic axis. The small parameter is
\kappa a
, where
\kappa
is the curvature of the magnetic axis, and
a
is a typical length-scale of the order of the minor radius in the direction normal to the magnetic axis. Recently, there has been a surge of interest in applying the NAE to study quasisymmetry (QS)
(
Landreman & Sengupta 2018
;
Landreman & Sengupta 2019
;
Jorge et al. 2020a
;
Rodríguez et al. 2023
)
, quasi-isodynamic (QI) stellarators
(
Plunk et al. 2019
;
Mata et al. 2022
;
Rodriguez & Plunk 2023
;
Mata & Plunk 2023
)
, energetic particles
(
Figueiredo et al. 2023
)
, MHD stability
(
Landreman & Jorge 2020
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Rodríguez 2023
)
and turbulence
(
Jorge & Landreman 2020
;
Jorge & Landreman 2021
;
Rodríguez & Mackenbach 2023
)
.

The development of the NAE theory has proceeded along two approaches: direct and indirect. In the direct approach
(
Mercier 1964
;
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
;
Jorge et al. 2020a
;
Jorge et al. 2020b
;
Bernardin & Tataronis 1985
;
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
, one uses a coordinate system pioneered by Mercier, and expands in the distance from the magnetic axis. In the inverse approach, one expands in the toroidal flux-surface label
(
Garren & Boozer 1991
;
Weitzner 2016
;
Landreman & Sengupta 2018
;
Landreman et al. 2019
;
Landreman & Sengupta 2019
;
Rodriguez et al. 2021
;
Rodriguez & Bhattacharjee 2021
;
Rodriguez et al. 2022
)
.

Both the direct and the indirect approaches have distinct advantages and disadvantages. The inverse coordinate approach is best suited to address concepts such as quasisymmetry and omnigeneity, which impose constraints on the magnitude of the magnetic field. However, the direct approach offers better physical insight. This is mainly because the flux-surface shaping factors, such as ellipticity and triangularity, can be chosen independently in the direct approach but not in the indirect approach. Moreover, in the indirect approach, the lowest order involves the solution of a nonlinear Riccati equation. In the direct coordinate approach, we can avoid solving a nonlinear ODE by using Mercier’s formula
(
Mercier 1974
;
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
)
, which also follows from Floquet theory
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
. The expressions for the rotational transform
(
Mercier 1964
;
Helander 2014
)
and its derivatives are also physically more intuitive in the direct approach.

An outstanding issue in the NAE that needs to be addressed is the assumption of smooth solutions of the MHD equilibrium order by order. The mathematical analysis of the smoothness assumption is indeed quite technical
(
Burby et al. 2021
;
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
. The first problem we encounter is the classic problem of the occurrence of singular currents in stellarators on rational surfaces because of a lack of axisymmetry
(
Grad 1967
;
Mercier 1964
;
Weitzner 2016
;
Loizu et al. 2015
;
Helander 2014
)
. These singularities are fundamentally tied to the magnetic differential equations (MDE)
(
Newcomb 1959
)
, whose solution can be singular everywhere
\iota
, the rotational transform, is rational. Since rationals are dense, avoiding such singularities with non-constant
\iota
is challenging
(
Grad 1967
;
Boozer 1981b
;
Weitzner 2014
)
. In the NAE expansion, thankfully, the rational singularities are governed only by
\iota_{0}
, the on-axis rotational transform
(
Mercier 1964
;
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
;
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
.
Choosing it to be sufficiently irrational helps with the practical implementation of lower-order NAE
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
;
Landreman & Sengupta 2019
)
. However, since any irrational number can be approximated closely by rationals, the problem of singular divisors appearing in higher orders can only be partially resolved mathematically in this approach. An alternative is to assume that
\iota_{0}
is close to a given rational number and construct special non-singular solutions
(
Weitzner 2016
;
Jaquiery & Sengupta 2019
)
or use the near-resonant normal form theory
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
.

We remark that there could be other potential sources of non-smoothness as well. Even in the vacuum limit, the smoothness of the solution within the NAE framework is not obvious
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
. When currents are present, the situation is complicated by another factor: the possibility of occurrence of logarithmic terms of the form
\rho^{n}(\log\rho)^{m},n>m
that, if present, make the equilibrium weakly singular near the axis (
\rho=0
)
(
Weitzner 2016
)
. Perhaps the easiest way to understand the physical origin of these logarithmic terms is to think of the plasma in the large aspect ratio picture as a thin current-carrying loop
(
Jackson 1999
;
Freidberg 1982
)
. The logarithmic terms are then unavoidable in the poloidal flux function
(
Freidberg 1982
)
. However, this simplified description of plasma needs to be modified near the axis to avoid the singularities on the axis. As shown in
(
Greene et al. 1971
)
, removing such weakly singular terms is tricky, even at lower orders. These issues are expected to worsen in stellarators where the currents interact with the 3D geometry.
In this work, we develop a systematic way to avoid such singular terms within the NAE formalism, showing that the assumption of smoothness of the NAE can be satisfied order by order.

The current work generalizes the direct NAE approach of
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
by including force-free and plasma-beta-driven currents, and by discussing both the structure of the flux surface and the field line label to all orders. The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the basic formalisms developed by Mercier
(
Mercier 1964
;
Mercier 1974
)
and Weitzner
(
Weitzner 2014
;
Weitzner 2016
)
and show how they can be combined into one, which we call the Mercier-Weitzner formalism. We assume the rotational transform to be sufficiently irrational. With this assumption, we can solve the MDEs order by order without encountering resonances on rational surfaces. We discuss the NAE of the vacuum fields in Section
3
, force-free fields in Section
4
and finally, MHD fields with finite plasma beta in Section
5
.

Our strategy is to construct a new variable that mimics the vacuum scalar potential but fully incorporates the effects of currents. The NAE using this variable shows a structure very close to the vacuum problem. Under the assumption of smooth fields and currents near the axis, we can show that power-series solutions to ideal MHD can be constructed to all orders. We do not attempt to prove convergence of the series. We show that the NAE calculations are simplified to a great extent through the use of Birkhoff–Gustavson Hamiltonian normal form variables
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
;
Bernardin & Tataronis 1985
)
. Normal forms in the present work show up as a basis that diagonalizes the magnetic differential operator, thereby simplifying the calculations of the solutions of MDEs ubiquitous in MHD.

In Section
7
, we present a numerical example that compares the higher-order finite-beta near-axis asymptotic expansions with a finite aspect-ratio equilibrium generated using the VMEC code
(
Hirshman & Whitson 1983
)
.
We present our conclusions and open questions in Section
8
. Finally, a variety of special cases are discussed in the Appendix.

2
The Mercier-Weitzner near-axis formalism

Our goal in this work is to extend the direct approach to the near-axis formalism, as developed by Mercier
(
Mercier 1964
)
and Solov’ev-Shafranov
(
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
)
, to include finite plasma beta. Mercier’s original approach
(
Mercier 1974
)
indeed considers currents and finite beta. However, the methodology to calculate beyond the two lowest orders is not transparent.
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
extends Mercier’s work significantly but primarily for vacuum fields. Effects of plasma beta, such as on the flux surface shapes, are treated through expansion subsidiary to the NAE. Only recently, a complete description of NAE in direct coordinates that included both vacuum and force-free equilibria was obtained
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
.

Here we provide a unified framework to include vacuum, force-free as well as finite beta ideal MHD. We shall use Weitzner’s approach
(
Weitzner 2014
;
Weitzner 2016
)
to obtain finite-beta MHD equilibrium equations. In this approach, currents are included through the current potential obtained through the solution of an MDE. In the following, we describe the NAE in direct coordinates using the current potential approach due to Weitzner.

2.1
The Weitzner formulation of ideal MHD with scalar pressure

We begin with the discussion of the formalism developed in
(
Weitzner 2014
;
Weitzner 2016
)
to study three-dimensional (nonsymmetric) ideal MHD equilibria with scalar pressure. We assume throughout that the magnetic field
\bm{B}
satisfies the ideal MHD equations

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0,\quad\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B},\quad\bm{J}\times\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}p,

(2.1)

where
\bm{J}
represents the current, and
p
is the scalar pressure. We have used units such that
\mu_{0}
is set to unity. We shall assume that the magnetic field possess a set of nested toroidal flux surfaces, denoted by the flux label
\psi
, which are also iso-surfaces of the pressure. Furthermore, we assume that
\bm{\nabla}\psi
is nonzero everywhere except at the magnetic axis and possibly the separatrix.

To describe the magnetic field vector, we now use the following contravariant and covariant representation

\displaystyle\bm{B}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\bm{B}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\Phi+K\bm{\nabla}\psi.

(2.2b)

Equation (
2.2a
) is the standard Clebsch representation
(
D’haeseleer et al. 2012
;
Helander 2014
)
of the magnetic field with
\psi
and
\alpha
denoting the toroidal magnetic flux and field line label, respectively. The Clebsch form manifestly satisfies
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
and
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=0
. The second representation of the magnetic field, (
2.2b
), is the so-called Boozer-Grad representation (see Chapter 7.2 of
D’haeseleer et al. 2012
). We call
\Phi
the magnetic scalar potential and
K
the current potential, since, in the limit
K\to 0
, or in the case of
K=K(\psi)
, the curl of
\bm{B}
(
2.2b
) vanishes. The quantities
\Phi,K,\alpha
need not be single-valued in a torus. Note also that the separation of
\Phi,K
in the representation for
\bm{B}
in (
2.2b
) is not unique since a shift of
\Phi\to\Phi+\varphi(\psi)
results in a shift
K\to K-\varphi^{\prime}(\psi)
without changing
\bm{B}
.

For the sake of comparison, we first look at the standard representation given by

\displaystyle\bm{B}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\theta_{B}+\iota(\psi)\bm{\nabla}\phi_{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\bm{B}

\displaystyle=I_{B}(\psi)\bm{\nabla}\theta_{B}+G_{B}(\psi)\bm{\nabla}\phi_{B}+B_{\psi}(\psi,\theta_{B},\phi_{B})\bm{\nabla}\psi.

(2.3b)

Here,
(\psi,\theta_{B},\phi_{B})
represents the standard Boozer coordinates
(
Boozer 1980
;
Boozer 1981a
;
Boozer 1981b
)
with
2\pi\psi
equal to the toroidal flux,
\iota(\psi)
equal to the rotational transform and
(\theta_{B},\Phi_{B})
represent the poloidal and toroidal Boozer angles. The quantities
I_{B}(\psi),G_{B}(\psi),
and
B_{\psi}
are all single-valued functions. The Boozer representation is an important special case of (
2.2
) for the choice

\displaystyle\Phi=I_{B}(\psi)\theta_{B}+G_{B}(\psi)\phi_{B},\quad K=B_{\psi}-\left(I_{B}^{\prime}(\psi)\theta_{B}+G_{B}^{\prime}(\psi)\phi_{B}\right),\quad\alpha=\theta_{B}-\iota(\psi)\theta_{B}

(2.4)

In this work, we use the Boozer-Grad coordinates instead of the usual Boozer coordinates.

Returning to the covariant representation of the magnetic field as given in (
2.2b
), we find that the chief advantage of this form is that the current,
\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B}
, has a Clebsch form

\displaystyle\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}K\times\bm{\nabla}\psi,

(2.5)

which manifestly satisfies

\displaystyle\bm{J}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=0.

(2.6)

Furthermore, the ideal MHD force balance

\displaystyle\bm{J}\times\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}p,

(2.7)

takes a particularly simple form in terms of an MDE for
K

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}K=p^{\prime}(\psi).

(2.8)

The solution to (
2.8
) can be represented as

\displaystyle K=\overline{K}(\psi,\alpha)+G,\quad\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\overline{K}=0,\quad\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}G=p^{\prime}.

(2.9)

where
\overline{K}(\psi,\alpha)
is the homogeneous solution of the MDE and hence only a function of
\psi
and possibly
\alpha
.

Let us collect the basic equations in the Weitzner formalism. These are obtained by equating the contravariant and the covariant representations of
\bm{B}
as given in (
2.2
), together with (
2.8
),

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\Phi+K\bm{\nabla}\psi

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}K

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}(\psi).

(2.10b)

Note that (
2.10a
), which we will call the basic MHD equations, is a set of three coupled nonlinear PDEs, while (
2.10b
) determines
K
. Together, they form a complete set of equations for the variables
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha,K)
. We shall now discuss the boundary conditions that must be satisfied such that (
2.10
) has physically meaningful solutions in a torus.

In a torus, all physical quantities, such as magnetic fields and currents, must satisfy periodic boundary conditions in the two angles
(\theta,\phi)
, where
\theta
is a poloidal angle, and
\phi
is a toroidal angle. Unlike standard Boozer coordinates
(\theta_{B},\phi_{B})
, we do not require
(\theta,\phi)
to be straight field line coordinates. This provides greater flexibility and freedom since we do not have any additional constraints on the angles. As mentioned, the functions
(\alpha,\Phi,K)
in a toroidal domain are multi-valued. However, the multi-valued part of the
\{\Phi,\alpha,K\}
system is not arbitrary due to the physical requirement that the magnetic field and the current must be single-valued quantities.

Following
(
Weitzner 2016
;
Grad 1971
)
we observe that in order for
\bm{B},\bm{J}
(as given by (
2.2a
) and (
2.5
)) to be single-valued, only the
\bm{\nabla}\psi
covariant component of
\bm{\nabla}\alpha,\bm{\nabla}K
can be multi-valued. Furthermore, from (
2.2b
) we find that the secular terms of
\Phi
and
K
must be related to each other such that
\bm{B}
is single-valued.

As shown in
(
Weitzner 2014
)
, single-valuedness of
\bm{B},\bm{J}
in a torus implies that
\Phi,\alpha
and
K
must be of the form

\displaystyle\Phi(\psi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=I(\psi)\theta+G(\psi)\phi+\widetilde{\Phi}(\psi,\theta,\phi)

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\alpha(\psi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=f(\psi)(\theta-\iota(\psi)\phi)+\widetilde{\alpha}(\psi,\theta,\phi)

(2.11b)

\displaystyle K(\psi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=-I^{\prime}(\psi)\theta-G^{\prime}(\psi)\phi+\widetilde{K}(\psi,\theta,\phi),

(2.11c)

where,
(\widetilde{\Phi},\widetilde{\alpha},\widetilde{K})
denotes functions periodic in both
\theta
and
\phi
, and
\iota(\psi)
denotes the rotational transform of the magnetic field. The form (
2.11
) builds in the nestedness of flux-surfaces. The single-valued flux functions
I(\psi),G(\psi),\iota(\psi)
denote the same quantities as in standard Boozer coordinates (
2.4
). We briefly discuss the physical significance of these flux functions below.

Without loss of generality, we can choose
2\pi\psi
to be the toroidal flux, as is common in stellarator literature. As shown in Appendix
A
, with this choice, we can normalize
\alpha
such that
f(\psi)=1
. The physical interpretation of
I(\psi),G(\psi)
is obtained by considering the net current,
\oint\bm{J}\cdot\bm{dS}
, through a poloidal and a toroidal circuit. From (
2.5
) and (
2.11c
) it follows that
G^{\prime}(\psi),I^{\prime}(\psi)
are proportional to the net poloidal and toroidal current. In the vacuum limit (
K=0,p^{\prime}=0
), the net toroidal current is zero, while the net poloidal current is a constant due to external currents. Consequently, for vacuum fields, we have
I(\psi)=0
and
G(\psi)=G_{0}
is a constant.

Equations (
2.10
), subject to the conditions (
2.11
), constitute the Weitzner formalism. The MHD equations (
2.10a
), (
2.10b
) are highly nonlinear, and in a generic stellarator, analytical progress is severely hindered by lack of any apparent continuous symmetry. Furthermore, the periodicity constraint (
2.11
) imposes a nontrivial requirement. In order to gain valuable physical insight, one, therefore, resorts to asymptotic analysis in some small parameter.

2.2
Mercier’s near-axis expansion

Mercier developed one such asymptotic expansion scheme to study the behavior near the magnetic axis by utilizing the distance from the magnetic axis as the expansion parameter. In the following, we shall briefly describe Mercier’s near-axis formalism and Mercier’s coordinates
(
Mercier 1964
)
, which are also known as the direct coordinates
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
.

2.2.1
Mercier coordinates

The Mercier coordinates are defined with respect to the magnetic axis, which is a closed magnetic field line. Although the magnetic axis can be elliptic or hyperbolic
(
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
;
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
, we will only treat the elliptic case here. We will assume that the magnetic axis is a three-dimensional smooth closed curve of total length
L
, described by the Frenet-Serret frame (which we assume to exist)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\bm{r}_{0}}{d\ell}=\bm{t},\quad\dfrac{d\bm{t}}{d\ell}=\kappa\bm{n},\quad\dfrac{d\bm{n}}{d\ell}=\tau\bm{b}-\kappa\bm{t},\quad\dfrac{d\bm{b}}{d\ell}=-\tau\bm{n},

(2.12)

where
\bm{r}_{0}
is the position vector of the magnetic axis,
\ell
is the arclength along the axis, the functions
\kappa(\ell),\tau(\ell)
are the curvature and torsion of the axis, and the vectors
(\bm{t},\bm{n},\bm{b})
are the standard tangent, normal and binormal vectors in the Frenet-Serret frame. Now, let us consider a tube of radius
\rho
with the magnetic axis as its axis. We can define a poloidal angle
\theta
such that a point on the tube is described by

\displaystyle\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{0}+\bm{\rho},\quad\bm{\rho}=x\>\bm{n}(\ell)+y\>\bm{b}(\ell),\quad x=\rho\cos\theta\>,y=\rho\sin{\theta}.

(2.13)

The coordinate system
(\rho,\theta,\ell)
is not orthogonal when the torsion of the axis,
\tau(\ell)
, is nonzero. To obtain an orthogonal coordinate system,
Mercier 1964
replaced
\theta
by

\displaystyle\omega=\theta+\int\tau d\ell.

(2.14)

The line and volume elements,
(ds^{2},dV)
, of the Mercier coordinates are as follows:

\displaystyle ds^{2}\equiv|d\bm{r}|^{2}=(d\rho)^{2}+(\rho d\omega)^{2}+(h\>d\ell)^{2},\quad dV=\rho h\>d\rho\>d\omega\>d\ell,\quad h=1-\kappa\rho\cos{\theta}.

(2.15)

In other words, the
(\rho,\omega,\ell)
coordinates are orthogonal with
(1,\rho,h)
as the scale factors, and the metric tensor is given by

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\text{diag}\left(1,\rho^{2},h^{2}\right),\qquad\sqrt{g}\equiv\sqrt{\text{Det}(g_{ij})}=\rho h.

(2.16)

Associated with the Mercier coordinates are the following orthonormal vectors
(\bm{\hat{\rho}},\bm{\hat{\omega}},\bm{\hat{\ell}})
such that

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\rho}}\equiv\bm{\nabla}\rho=\cos{\theta}\,\bm{n}+\sin{\theta}\>\bm{b},\quad\bm{\hat{\omega}}\equiv\rho\bm{\nabla}\omega=-\sin{\theta}\,\bm{n}+\cos{\theta}\,\bm{b},\quad\bm{\hat{\ell}}\equiv h\bm{\nabla}\ell=\bm{t},

(2.17)

There is a certain freedom in choosing the poloidal and toroidal angles in the direct coordinates
(\rho,\omega,\ell)
. While the toroidal angle follows from the arclength
\ell
, one can generally add any arbitrary function
\delta(\ell)
, where
\delta^{\prime}(\ell)
is single-valued, to
\theta
to obtain another poloidal angle without changing the Jacobian of transformation. Besides
(\theta,\phi=2\pi\ell/L)
another possible choice is
(u,\phi)
, where

\displaystyle u=\theta+\delta(\ell)=\omega-\int\tau d\ell+\delta(\ell),\quad\phi=2\pi\frac{\ell}{L}.

(2.18)

Let us now describe the Weitzner system described in Section
2.1
using the Mercier coordinates. To connect to the contravariant form of
\bm{B}
(
2.2a
) in the Weitzner formalism, we shall make a choice
(\theta,\phi)
for defining angular coordinates. To connect to the covariant form of
\bm{B}
given by (
2.2b
) in the Weitzner formalism, we shall utilize the orthonormal vectors
(\bm{\hat{\rho}},\bm{\hat{\omega}},\bm{\hat{\ell}})
. Using (
2.17
), we can write the magnetic field in the following component form:

\displaystyle\bm{B}=B_{\rho}\bm{\hat{\rho}}+B_{\omega}\bm{\hat{\omega}}+B_{\ell}\bm{\hat{\ell}},

(2.19)

\displaystyle B_{\rho}=\Phi_{,\rho}+K\psi_{,\rho},\quad B_{\omega}=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\Phi_{,\omega}+K\psi_{,\omega}\right),\quad B_{\ell}=\frac{1}{h}\left(\Phi_{,\ell}+K\psi_{,\ell}\right).

(2.20)

Here and elsewhere, we shall use the notation
X_{,\ell}
to denote the partial derivative of
X
with respect to
\ell
holding the other two Mercier coordinates
(\rho,\omega)
fixed.

2.2.2
Near-axis expansions

The fundamental idea underlying the NAE is to solve the MHD equations using perturbation theory, where the expansion parameter scales like the distance from the magnetic axis,
\rho
. We can formally define the dimensionless expansion parameter
\epsilon
as

\displaystyle\epsilon=\kappa_{\text{max}}a,

(2.21)

where
\kappa_{\text{max}}
is the maximum axis curvature and
0\leq\rho<a
. With this definition, we have
\kappa\rho<1
throughout in the domain of validity of the NAE, which is necessary for the metric
\sqrt{g}=\rho h
to be positive everywhere. Expanding the quantities
(\psi,\alpha,\Phi,K)
in
\epsilon
and substituting them in the basic equilibrium equations (
2.10
), one can solve the MHD equations order by order in
\epsilon
.

It is usually assumed
(
Kuo-Petravic & Boozer 1987
;
Garren & Boozer 1991
)
that the physical quantities are sufficiently regular near the magnetic axis such that one can carry out a regular power series expansion in positive powers of
\rho
. Thus, any function of the form
\mathfrak{f}(\rho,\omega,\ell)
is expanded as follows

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(\rho,\omega,\ell)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\epsilon\rho)^{n}\>\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}(\omega,\ell)

(2.22)

The function
\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}(\omega,\ell)
can be determined order by order by substituting (
2.22
) into the MHD equations (
2.10
). Moreover, if
\mathfrak{f}
has a well-defined Taylor series near the magnetic axis, we would expect
\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}
to be a polynomial of order
n
in
(x,y)=\rho(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
. In
Jorge et al. 2020b
;
Duignan & Meiss 2021
, it has been demonstrated that for vacuum fields with nested surfaces, the functions
(\Phi-G_{0}\phi),\psi
are of such form. Therefore, for such regular functions, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(x,y,\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\>\sum_{m=0}^{n}\mathfrak{c}^{(n)}(\ell)x^{m}y^{n-m}

(2.23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\epsilon\rho)^{n}\>\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left(\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{mc}(\ell)\cos{\left(m\omega+\delta_{m}(\ell)\right)}+\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{ms}(\ell)\sin{\left(m\omega+\delta_{m}(\ell)\right)}\right),

where
\left(\mathfrak{c}^{(n)}(\ell),\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{mc}(\ell),\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{ms}(\ell),\delta_{m}(\ell)\right)
are functions of
\ell
that must be determined by substituting (
2.23
) into (
2.10
). We note that even though the series (
2.23
) looks like a regular Taylor series for
\mathfrak{f}(x,y,\ell)
near the axis, the series may be divergent. The NAE, like many other perturbative expansions, is written formally here, with no claims regarding the convergence of the series.

We shall substitute the formal series (
2.23
) into the basic MHD equations (
2.10
) and collect powers of
\rho
and the poloidal harmonics
(\cos{m\ell},\sin{m\ell})
. This then reduces the nonlinear PDE system (
2.10
) to a nonlinear ordinary differential equation (ODE) system for
(\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{mc}(\ell),\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{ms}(\ell))
, which is a significant step forward. Moreover, (
2.23
) already includes periodicity in
\omega
, and only the periodicity in
\ell
remains to be imposed on the ODEs.

Before proceeding further, we should point out some issues that can lead to a lack of regularity of the NAE. Firstly, although the periodicity in
\ell
can always be satisfied if the on-axis rotational transform is sufficiently irrational, the coefficients
(\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{mc}(\ell),\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{ms}(\ell))
can formally exhibit singular behavior when the on-axis rotational transform is close to a rational number. As shown in
(
Mercier 1964
;
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
)
, the form of the singularities on a
(m,n)
rational surface is
(\iota_{0}-n/m)^{-1}
, where
\iota_{0}
is the on-axis rotational transform. In this work, we shall assume that
\iota_{0}
is sufficiently irrational to formally avoid such resonances in the vicinity of the axis. Secondly,
we note that the regular power series expansion (
2.22
) is not always guaranteed to be correct. The possibility of having weak logarithmic singularities on the axis in the form of
\rho^{n}(\log\rho)^{m},n>m>0
has been pointed out in
Weitzner 2016
. Furthermore, functions such as
\alpha
can not be represented in the analytic power series given by (
2.23
). In this work, we demonstrate that for a large class of equilibria, one can still construct a power series in
\rho
valid to all orders in
\rho
without encountering logarithmically singular terms. We also pay special attention to the multi-valued function
\alpha,K
and discuss their structure.

In the following, we discuss various limits of ideal MHD: vacuum, force-free, and finally, finite beta MHD equilibrium. We start with the vacuum limit because its mathematical structure is fundamental and the easiest to analyze. Subsequently, we will show that the force-free and MHD fields can be formulated in a form very similar to the vacuum fields.

3
NAE of vacuum fields in Mercier-Weitzner formalism

NAE of vacuum fields in direct coordinates has been treated extensively in
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
. In the Mercier-Weitzner formalism,
K=0
corresponds to the vacuum limit, and the analysis is similar to
Jorge et al. 2020b
, which also uses direct coordinates. However, a key point of departure of our present work from all other previous developments of NAE is the treatment of the Clebsch variable, which represents the field line label,
\alpha
. The primary motivation for focusing on
\alpha
is that the rotational transform and its derivatives ( which includes the magnetic shear) are related directly to the secular part of
\alpha
( through (
2.11b
)). Thus, a systematic construction of
\alpha
is crucial.

Our goal in this Section is to study the structure of the Mercier-Weitzner equations in the vacuum limit. First, we show how the condition of nested flux surfaces leads to MDEs. We then explicitly employ the NAE and elucidate the properties of the solutions obtained, paying particular attention to the secular and periodic structure of the field line label,
\alpha
.

3.1
Basic equations and need for an alternative set of equations

In the vacuum limit, the basic equilibrium equations (
2.10
) reduces to

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha=\bm{\nabla}\Phi.

(3.1)

Projecting (
3.1
) onto the orthonormal vectors
(\bm{\hat{\rho}},\bm{\hat{\omega}},\bm{\hat{\ell}})
defined in (
2.17
), we get

\displaystyle\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho h\>\Phi_{,\rho}

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\left(\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\rho}-\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\rho}\Phi_{,\omega}

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{h}\Phi_{,\ell}

(3.2c)

Equation (
3.2
) is a closed set of equations that, in principle, can be solved for the three unknowns
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha)
. However, the coupling between the various quantities makes the system (
3.2
) difficult to tackle analytically in the direct coordinate approach to NAE. Therefore, instead of (
3.2
), we examine alternative equations that decouple
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha)
as much as possible.

To arrive at an alternative set of equations, we now look at the consistency conditions that directly follow from (
3.2
). To derive the first consistency condition,
we differentiate (
3.2b
) and (
3.2c
) with respect to
\omega
and
\ell
respectively and add them. Using the commutativity of the cross derivatives for the functions
\psi
and
\alpha
, we get a consistency condition

\displaystyle-\partial_{\rho}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)=\partial_{\omega}\left(\frac{h}{\rho}\Phi_{,\omega}\right)+\partial_{\ell}\left(\frac{\rho}{h}\Phi_{,\ell}\right)

(3.3)

It follows from (
3.2a
) that (
3.3
) is nothing but the Laplace equation for
\Phi
,

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi=\frac{1}{\rho h}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left(\rho h\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\rho}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}h}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\left(h\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\omega}\right)+\frac{1}{h}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\left(\frac{1}{h}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\ell}\right)=0,

(3.4)

which also follows from
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\Phi=0
.

Two other consistency conditions directly follow from (
3.1
) and are given by

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=\bm{\nabla}\Phi\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=0

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\alpha=\bm{\nabla}\Phi\cdot\bm{\nabla}\alpha=0,

(3.5b)

One might suppose that the complete set (
3.2
) could be replaced by the new set of equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), which are the MDEs for
\psi
and
\alpha
. The Laplace equation only involves
\Phi
and the scale factors and is ideally suited for determining
\Phi
. Once
\Phi
is known, we might determine the Clebsch variables
(\psi,\alpha)
through (
3.5a
) and (
3.5b
). The benefit of using the characteristic equations, (
3.5
), is that
\psi
and
\alpha
can be decoupled. However, several mathematical intricacies need to be addressed before replacing the complete set (
3.2
), which we now highlight.

Firstly, (
3.2
) is a third-order system because it has three first-order derivatives in
(\rho,\omega,\ell)
, while the Laplace equation is second order and so is (
3.5
) because of the two first derivatives. Therefore, the replacement of (
3.2
) by (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) raises the order by one since we have taken an additional derivative of the equations, which might lead to the inclusion of spurious solutions. Furthermore, the conditions (
3.4
),(
3.5a
) and (
3.5b
) are not completely independent when the equations are solved order by order. To see this, let us assume that the equations that govern the angular derivatives of
\Phi
, (
3.2b
) and (
3.2c
), have been solved. It is then straightforward to see that the radial derivative of
\Phi
given by (
3.2a
) and characteristic equations (
3.5a
) and (
3.5b
) are not independent but are related through

\displaystyle\Phi_{,\rho}-\frac{1}{\rho h}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)=\frac{1}{\psi_{,\rho}}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=\frac{1}{\alpha_{,\rho}}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\alpha.

(3.6)

As discussed in Appendix
B
, in order to retain the correct vacuum and MHD characteristics
(
Goedbloed 1983
;
Courant & Hilbert 2008
;
Friedrichs & Kranzer 1958
;
Weitzner 2014
)
we cannot simply replace the complete set (
3.2
) by the consistency conditions (
3.4
), (
3.5a
) and (
3.5b
). Fortunately, a workaround is possible that allows a partial decoupling of the variables
\Phi,\psi,\alpha
. In the following Sections, we will provide a recipe to carry out the finite-beta NAE efficiently and self-consistently to arbitrary orders by choosing the right set of fundamental equations. We will justify all the necessary mathematical steps below.

3.2
Alternative set of basic equations

We first observe that the consistency condition (
3.3
) implies that if the
\omega
and
\ell
components of the basic vacuum equations (
3.1
), i.e., (
3.2b
) and (
3.2c
), are satisfied, the solution of the Laplace equation satisfies the
\rho
component, (
3.2a
), up to an arbitrary function of
(\omega,\ell)
. Therefore, (
3.2a
) needs to be imposed to eliminate this arbitrariness. As can be seen from (
3.6
) (and further discussed later on), the equation for the radial derivative of
\Phi
(
3.2a
) is identical to the MDE for
\psi
(
3.5a
) order by order in
\rho
. Furthermore, it will be shown later that once (
3.4
) and (
3.5a
) (equivalently (
3.2a
)) are satisfied, the equations (
3.2b
) and (
3.2c
) are compatible, in the sense that either can be used to solve for
\alpha
. We will find it more straightforward to use (
3.2b
) to solve for
\alpha
up to a function of
\ell
by integrating with respect to
\omega
. The reason behind choosing (
3.2b
) over (
3.2c
) is that the
\Phi
and
\psi
can be shown to have a finite number of poloidal harmonics which depend only on the order of expansion, whereas no such restriction exists for the toroidal harmonics.

Finally, to determine
\alpha
fully, we need only the poloidal average of either (
3.2c
) or (
3.5b
). We use the latter because of the relevance of the MDE to calculating rotational transform and its higher derivatives. Note that the only MDE we solve is the MDE for
\psi
, (
3.5a
). We are not solving the MDE for
\alpha
; we are simply fixing the
\ell
dependent part of
\alpha
that was left undetermined because of the partial integration with respect to
\omega
. The procedure will be explained in detail in Sections
3.3
through
3.6
.

In summary, we have seen that equating the covariant and the contravariant representations of
\bm{B}
, (
2.2
), in the vacuum limit leads to a complete set of equations (
3.2
). However, the variables
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha)
are coupled nonlinearly, making
(
3.2
), not the best set of equations to start the NAE program in direct coordinates. The alternative is to solve the Laplace equation for
\Phi
(
3.4
) and the MDE for
\psi
(
3.5a
). We
use the
\Phi_{,\omega}
equation, (
3.2b
), to obtain
\alpha
up to a function of
\ell
, which is then determined from the poloidally averaged MDE for
\alpha
(
3.5b
). The Laplace equation does not introduce extraneous solutions because we are solving the MDE for
\psi
, which is equivalent to solving (
3.2a
), as can be seen from (
3.6
). Furthermore, the Laplace equation is also the necessary solvability condition for the function
\alpha
.

3.3
Derivation of the vacuum NAE equations: The lowest order

We now expand the quantities
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha)
in power series of
\rho
in the general form

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho,\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{(0)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho\;\Phi^{(1)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{2}\Phi^{(2)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{3}\Phi^{(3)}(\omega,\ell)+...

(3.7a)

\displaystyle\alpha(\rho,\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{(0)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho\;\alpha^{(1)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{2}\alpha^{(2)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{3}\alpha^{(3)}(\omega,\ell)+...

(3.7b)

\displaystyle\psi(\rho,\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\psi^{(2)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{3}\psi^{(3)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{4}\psi^{(4)}(\omega,\ell)...

(3.7c)

The analysis of the lowest-order quantities is important for the subsequent development of the analysis. Therefore, we now discuss in some detail the lowest-order structure. Firstly, note that the expansion of
\psi
starts at
\rho^{2}
since we need
\psi
to be zero on the axis. We also need
\psi_{,\rho}
to vanish on the axis to ensure that the poloidal field,
B_{\omega}=(1/\rho)\Phi_{\omega}\approx\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}
, is also zero on the magnetic axis. Furthermore, the vanishing of the poloidal field on the axis also leads to the
O(1/\rho^{2})
and
O(1/\rho)
of the vacuum system (
3.2c
) to be satisfied provided

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}(\omega,\ell)=\Phi_{0}(\ell),\quad\quad\Phi^{(1)}(\omega,\ell)=0.

(3.8)

We assume that the strength of the magnetic field on the axis is positive and single-valued, i.e.,

\displaystyle B_{0}(\ell)=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)>0,\quad B_{0}(\ell+L)=B_{0}(\ell).

(3.9)

To make
B_{0}(\ell)
single-valued
\Phi^{(0)}
must be of the form

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}(\ell)=\int B_{0}(\ell)d\ell=\overline{B}_{0}\ell+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}(\ell),\qquad\overline{B}_{0}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}B_{0}(\ell)d\ell,

(3.10)

where,
\widetilde{\Phi}_{0}(\ell)
is periodic in
\ell
.

Expanding the vacuum system (
3.2
) to
O(1)
, we obtain the following coupled equations for
(\Phi^{(2)},\psi^{(2)},\alpha^{(0)})

\displaystyle 2\Phi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}\psi^{(2)}_{,\omega}-\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\psi^{(2)}_{,\ell}

(3.11a)

\displaystyle 2\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)

(3.11b)

\displaystyle 2\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=-\Phi^{(2)}_{,\omega},

(3.11c)

Multiplying (
3.11a
) by
2\psi^{(2)}
and using (
3.11b
,
3.11c
) we get the following linear homogeneous equation for
\psi^{(2)}
:

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)\partial_{\ell}+\Phi^{(2)}_{,\omega}\partial_{\omega}+4\Phi^{(2)}\right)\psi^{(2)}=0

(3.12)

To solve (
3.12
) we need
\Phi^{(2)}
. The compatibility condition
\partial_{\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}=\partial_{\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}
with the use of (
3.11b
,
3.11c
) leads to the following Poisson equation for
\Phi^{(2)}
:

\displaystyle(\partial^{2}_{\omega}+2^{2})\Phi^{(2)}+\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{0}=0.

(3.13)

Since (
3.13
) is a simple harmonic oscillator, its solution can be easily seen to be of the form

\displaystyle\Phi^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{4}\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\ell)+\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)\left(f^{(2)}_{c2}(\ell)\cos{(2u(\omega,\ell))}+f^{(2)}_{s2}(\ell)\sin{(2u(\omega,\ell))}\right),

(3.14)

where,
f^{(2)}_{c2}(\ell),f^{(2)}_{s2}(\ell)
are the “integration constants” with respect to the
\partial_{\omega}
operator. We have also chosen
u
, defined by (
2.18
), as the helical angle. Note that from the form of
\Phi^{(0)}
as given in (
3.10
),
\Phi^{(2)}
can be made single-valued in
\ell
by ensuring that the integration constants are periodic.
The function
\Phi^{(2)}_{,\omega}
obtained from (
3.14
) is harmonic with zero net poloidal average.

The solution of the equation for
\psi^{(2)}
, (
3.12
), is of the form

\displaystyle\psi^{(2)}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)(a(\ell)+b(\ell)\cos{(2u)}),

(3.15)

which can be checked by substituting (
3.15
) in (
3.12
) and collecting the poloidal harmonics
1,\cos{2u},\sin{2u}
. Equating the coefficients of the various poloidal harmonics to zero, we get

\displaystyle a(\ell)=c_{0}\cosh{\eta(\ell)},\quad b(\ell)=c_{0}\sinh{\eta(\ell)},

(3.16)

together with

\displaystyle f^{(2)}_{c2}(\ell)=-\frac{b^{\prime}(\ell)}{4a(\ell)}=-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{\prime}(\ell),\quad f^{(2)}_{s2}(\ell)=\frac{b(\ell)}{2a(\ell)}\left(\delta^{\prime}(\ell)-\tau(\ell)\right).

(3.17)

Here
\eta(\ell)
is an arbitrary function of
\ell
. We require that

\left(f^{(2)}_{c2}(\ell),f^{(2)}_{s2}(\ell)\right)
are single-valued functions by requiring
\eta(\ell),\delta^{\prime}(\ell)
to be single-valued and periodic in
\ell
. With this choice, we make both
\Phi^{(2)},\psi^{(2)}
single-valued functions. Later, we determine the constant
c_{0}
by imposing the choice of
\psi
as the toroidal flux. To see that this is indeed the only solution, we can divide the equations for the
\omega,\ell
derivatives of
\alpha^{(0)}
, (
3.11b
) and (
3.11c
), by
2\psi^{(2)}
and eliminate
\alpha^{(0)}
through cross-differentiation. The resultant consistency condition

\displaystyle\partial_{\ell}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}{\psi^{(2)}}\right)+\partial_{\omega}\left(\frac{\Phi^{(2)}_{,\omega}}{\psi^{(2)}}\right)=0,

(3.18)

is a linear PDE for
(1/\psi^{(2)})
with periodic boundary conditions in
\omega,\ell
. Since the expression for
\psi^{(2)}
satisfies (
3.18
) and the periodic boundary conditions, it must be the unique solution.

For the physical interpretation of the various functions
\eta(\ell),\delta(\ell)
, we refer to the excellent review article
(
Helander 2014
)
. In short, the flux-surface shape obtained from the equation for
\psi^{(2)}
(
3.15
) is a rotating ellipse. The function
\eta(\ell)
controls the ellipticity, while
\delta(\ell)
measures its angle of rotation respect to the Frenet frame. We note that the choices of the free-functions in
f^{(2)}_{c2},f^{(2)}_{s2}
in (
3.17
) were made in terms of the shaping parameters of the lowest order flux-surface. Later, we make choices such that the interpretation of higher-order free-functions
\Phi^{(n+2)}_{c},\Phi^{(n+2)}_{s}
can be made in terms of higher-order flux surface shapes
\psi^{(n+2)}_{c},\psi^{(n+2)}_{s}
.

It is possible to find special coordinates such that
\psi^{(2)}
is a circle in these coordinates. Since
\Phi_{0}^{\prime}=B_{0}>0
and
a(\ell)>b(\ell)\geq 0
, it follows that
\psi^{(2)}
is non-vanishing. As a result, we can define “rotating coordinates”
(
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
)
or “normal form” coordinates

\displaystyle X_{\mathcal{N}}=e^{\eta/2}\rho\cos u,\quad Y_{\mathcal{N}}=e^{-\eta/2}\rho\sin u,

(3.19)

such that
\psi^{(2)}
, given by

\displaystyle\psi^{(2)}\rho^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(X^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}+Y_{\mathcal{N}}^{2}\right)\Phi_{0}^{\prime},

(3.20)

is a circle in the
(x_{\mathcal{N}}\equiv X_{\mathcal{N}}/\rho,y_{\mathcal{N}}\equiv Y_{\mathcal{N}}/\rho)
coordinates. The benefits of using the “normal form” coordinates have been noted in
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
. As shown in Appendix
F
, these coordinates are particularly useful in solving the coupled ODEs that result from the MDE for higher-order flux-surface shapes.

With
\Phi^{(2)}
and
\psi^{(2)}
in hand let us focus on obtaining
\alpha^{(0)}
. Substituting the expressions for
\psi^{(2)}
, (
3.15
), together with the expressions for
a,b
, (
3.16
), in the equation for
\alpha^{(0)}
, (
3.11b
), and integrating with respect to
\omega
we get

\displaystyle\alpha^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2c_{0}}\arctan{\left(e^{-\eta(\ell)}\tan{u}\right)}+\mathfrak{a}^{(0)}(\ell).

(3.21)

To determine the unknown function
\mathfrak{a}^{(0)}(\ell)
we only need the poloidally averaged (
3.11c
). Since
\Phi^{(2)}_{,\omega}
has zero average, the poloidally averaged
\alpha^{(0)}_{,\ell}
equation (
3.11c
) reads

\displaystyle\oint\frac{du}{2\pi}\left(2\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}\right)=0.

(3.22)

Using the expressions for
\alpha^{(0)},\psi^{(2)}
as given in (
3.21
) and (
3.15
), we can show that (
3.22
) yields

\displaystyle-2a(\ell)\>{\mathfrak{a}^{(0)}}^{\prime}(\ell)=\partial_{\ell}u=\delta^{\prime}(\ell)-\tau(\ell)

(3.23)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\alpha^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2c_{0}}\arctan{\left(e^{-\eta(\ell)}\tan{u}\right)}-\int d\ell\>\frac{\delta^{\prime}(\ell)-\tau(\ell)}{2c_{0}\cosh{\eta(\ell)}}.

(3.24)

Let us briefly summarize the findings of NAE up to
O(1)
. From the requirement that the poloidal magnetic field and the flux surface label vanish on the axis, we can determine
\Phi^{(0)},\Phi^{(1)}
to be as given in (
3.8
).
The vacuum system to
O(1)
gives three coupled equations for
\Phi^{(2)},\psi^{(2)},\alpha^{(0)}
. We have shown that these equations can be decoupled to yield a Poisson equation for
\phi^{(2)}
,(
3.13
) and an MDE for
\psi^{(2)}
, (
3.12
). The solutions
\Phi^{(2)},\psi^{(2)}
, given in (

) and (

), can then be used to determine

, which is given in (

) up to an overall constant. With the help of these lowest-order expressions, we now carry out the expansion to

in the next Section, where we demonstrate that at

, an analogous decoupling of the higher-order quantities

3.4
Derivation of the vacuum NAE equations:
n^{\text{th}}
order

The system (
3.2
) to
O(n)
leads to the following closed set of equations

\displaystyle\left({\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}}\partial_{\ell}-{\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}}\partial_{\omega}\right)\psi^{(n+2)}+\left({\alpha^{(n)}_{,\omega}}\partial_{\ell}-{\alpha^{(n)}_{,\ell}}\partial_{\omega}\right)\psi^{(2)}=-(n+2)\Phi^{(n+2)}+...

\displaystyle\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}\partial_{\omega}\left(\dfrac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)={\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}}\left(-(n+2)\>\psi^{(n+2)}\right)+...

(3.25b)

\displaystyle-\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}\partial_{\ell}\left(\dfrac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)={\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}}\left((n+2)\>\psi^{(n+2)}\right)+\Phi^{(n+2)}_{,\omega}+...

where

Although (

, an alternate approach to solving the system (

, the MDE for

, equation (

Expanding the equation relating the

), to

.

To solve the MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}
or equivalently (

) we need

as evident from the right-hand side of (

). We have demonstrated in Section

that the Laplace equation for

arises as an exact compatibility condition of (

) and (

), provided (

) holds. The same procedure works in higher orders as well. To

, elimination of
\alpha^{(n)}
from (

) and (

) through cross differentiation, i.e.,
\partial_{\ell}
(
3.25c
)
+\partial_{\omega}
(

\Phi
to

Now that the equation for its angular derivatives (

\displaystyle\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}\left({\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}}\partial_{\ell}-{\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}}\partial_{\omega}\right)\left(\dfrac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)=-{\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}}\left(\Phi^{(n+2)}_{,\omega}+\dots\right)+\dots

3.5
Derivation of the vacuum NAE equations: boundary conditions in toroidal geometry and periodicity constraints

To obtain solutions of the equations (I-IV) in a torus, we need to impose double-periodicity in the two angles and regularity in the radial coordinate near the axis. In Section
2
, we presented the secular and periodic pieces of
\Phi,\alpha
in equation (
2.11
) in the inverse coordinates. The structure of the functions of
\Phi,\psi,\alpha
follows from the physical requirement that
\bm{J},\bm{B}
be periodic in a torus. Since the Mercier direct coordinates do not use the flux-surface label as a coordinate, separating secular and periodic parts of the multi-valued functions
\Phi,\alpha
is challenging. In particular, the form of
\alpha
is crucial for understanding the MDEs. Hence, before discussing how to solve the equations, we need to understand the periodicity constraints in the direct coordinates.

With the choice of
2\pi\psi
to be the toroidal flux, we get the following expression for vacuum
(\alpha,\Phi)
from (
2.11
):

\displaystyle\alpha=\theta-\iota(\psi)\phi+\widetilde{\alpha}(\psi,\theta,\phi),\qquad\Phi=G_{0}\phi+\widetilde{\Phi}(\psi,\theta,\phi).

(3.28)

To work out the near-axis expansion in inverse Mercier coordinates
(\psi,\omega,\phi=\ell/L)
one needs to invert the function
\psi(\rho,\omega,\ell)
to obtain
\rho(\psi,\omega,\ell)
. Equivalently one needs to invert the NAE for
\psi
(
3.7c
) to obtain an expression for
\rho
as a power series in
\sqrt{2\psi}
. We can asymptotically invert the power series of
\psi
in
\rho
to obtain

\displaystyle\rho=\sqrt{\frac{\psi}{\psi^{(2)}}}-\frac{\psi^{(3)}}{2\psi^{(2)}}\left(\frac{\psi}{\psi^{(2)}}\right)-\frac{1}{8}\left(4\frac{\psi^{(4)}}{\psi^{(2)}}-5\left(\frac{\psi^{(3)}}{\psi^{(2)}}\right)\right)\left(\frac{\psi}{\psi^{(2)}}\right)^{3/2}+O(\psi^{2}).

(3.29)

Let us first focus on the field-line label
\alpha
. The near-axis expansion of
\alpha
in the inverse (denoted by subscripts) and the direct (denoted by superscripts) coordinates,

\displaystyle\alpha(\rho,\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{(0)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho\alpha^{(1)}(\omega,\ell)+\rho^{2}\alpha^{(2)}(\omega,\ell)+O(\rho^{3}),

(3.30a)

\displaystyle\alpha(\psi,\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}(\omega,\ell)+\sqrt{\psi}\alpha_{1}(\omega,\ell)+\psi\>\alpha_{2}(\omega,\ell)+O(\psi^{3/2}),

(3.30b)

are, therefore, connected through the relations

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{(0)},\quad\alpha_{1}=\frac{\alpha^{(1)}}{\sqrt{\psi^{(2)}}},\quad\alpha_{2}=\frac{\alpha^{(2)}}{\psi^{(2)}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\alpha^{(1)}}{\sqrt{\psi^{(2)}}}\frac{\psi^{(3)}}{\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{3/2}},

(3.31)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{(\psi^{(2)})^{n/2}}+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{(m)}}{\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{m/2}}\mathcal{P}_{m(n-1)},

where,
\mathcal{P}_{m(n-1)}
is a polynomial of order
(n-1)
whose arguments are
\left(\psi^{(j)}/\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{j/2}\right)
with
2\leq j\leq m+1
.
Expanding
\iota(\psi)
in a Taylor series in
\psi
near the axis,

\displaystyle\iota(\psi)=\iota_{0}+\iota_{2}\psi+\dots+\iota_{2k}\psi^{(2k)}+O(\psi^{(2k+1)}),

(3.32)

and using the form of
\alpha
in the inverse coordinates as given in (
2.11
), we find that

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=\theta-2\pi\iota_{0}\frac{\ell}{L}+\widetilde{\alpha}_{0},\quad\alpha_{1}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{1},\quad\alpha_{2}=-2\pi\iota_{2}\frac{\ell}{L}+\widetilde{\alpha}_{2},\quad\alpha_{2k}=-2\pi\iota_{2k}\frac{\ell}{L}+\widetilde{\alpha}_{2k},

(3.33)

where
\widetilde{\alpha}_{k},k=0,1,2
denotes functions periodic in the angles. Averaging the
\omega,\ell
derivatives of
\alpha_{2k}
from (
3.33
) over
\omega
and
\ell
, and exploiting the periodicity of
\widetilde{\alpha}_{k}
, we find that

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\partial_{\omega}\alpha_{2k}=\delta_{2k},\qquad 2\pi\iota_{2k}=-\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}d\ell\>\partial_{\ell}\left(\alpha_{2k}\right),

(3.34)

where,
\delta_{2k}=0
for
k>0
and
1
for
k=0
.

We are now in a position to determine the constant
c_{0}
in (
3.16
). To do so, we impose the choice of
2\pi\psi
as the toroidal flux (equivalently
f(\psi)=1
in (
2.11b
)), order by order, by insisting that the secular
\theta
piece only appears in
\alpha^{(0)}
as shown in (
3.33
). Here, we note that (
3.33
) implies that the average of
\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}
must be unity.

Using the form of
\alpha^{(0)}
obtained in (
3.24
) and averaging over
\omega
, we obtain the average
\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}
in the following form:

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2a(\ell)}\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(\ell)\cos{2u}}.

(3.35)

Here, we have used (
3.11b
) for
\alpha^{(0)}_{,\omega}
, (
3.15
) for the functional form of
\psi^{(2)}
, and (
3.16
) to define

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\ell)\equiv\frac{b(\ell)}{a(\ell)}=\tanh{\eta(\ell)}.

(3.36)

Replacing the
\omega
integral by a
u
integral, using the identity

\displaystyle\oint\frac{du}{2\pi}\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon\cos{2u}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{2}}}=\cosh{\eta(\ell)},

(3.37)

and finally imposing the condition that the average of
\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}
must be unity, we find that

\displaystyle c_{0}=\frac{1}{2},\quad a(\ell)=\cosh\eta(\ell).

(3.38)

Therefore, the expression for
\alpha^{(0)}
, (
3.24
), now reads

\displaystyle\alpha^{(0)}=\arctan{\left(e^{-\eta(\ell)}\tan{u}\right)}-\int d\ell\>\frac{\delta^{\prime}(\ell)-\tau(\ell)}{2a(\ell)}.

(3.39)

The on-axis rotational transform can be obtained from (
3.39
) by utilizing

\displaystyle\iota_{0}=\alpha(\ell,\theta)-\alpha(\ell+L,\theta)+N=\frac{v(L)-(\delta(L)-\delta(0))}{2\pi}+N,

(3.40)

where we have defined

\displaystyle v(\ell)=\int_{0}^{\ell}ds\frac{u^{\prime}(s)}{\cosh{\eta(s)}}=\int_{0}^{\ell}ds\frac{\delta^{\prime}(s)-\tau(s)}{\cosh{\eta(s)}}.

(3.41)

The extra integer
N
comes from the rotation of the Frenet frame
(
Helander 2014
)
.

We now explicitly derive the periodicity conditions in the direct coordinates. Firstly, every
\psi^{(m+2)},m=0,1,2,..
needs to be single-valued so that the flux-surface label
\psi
is single-valued. Secondly, by substituting the relation between the components of
\alpha
in the inverse representation
\alpha_{n}
and the direct representation
\alpha^{(n)}
as given in (
3.33
) into the identities (
3.34
), we get

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\partial_{\omega}\left(\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{(\psi^{(2)})^{n/2}}\right)=0,\qquad(n>0)

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\oint\frac{d\ell}{2\pi}\partial_{\ell}\left(\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{(\psi^{(2)})^{n/2}}+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{(m)}}{\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{m/2}}\mathcal{P}_{m(n-1)}\right)=-\iota_{2k}\quad(n=2k).

(3.42)

Returning to the discussion of the multi-valued structure of
\Phi
, we note that the analysis is almost identical to that of
\alpha
. The analogs of the relation between inverse and direct components (
3.30
), and the order-by-order form in inverse-coordinates (
3.5
) for
\Phi
are obtained by simply replacing
\alpha
by
\Phi
. An important difference between
\Phi
and
\alpha
occurs because in vacuum

in (
3.28
) is a constant. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}=G_{0}\frac{\ell}{L}+\widetilde{\Phi}_{0},\quad\Phi_{n}=\widetilde{\Phi}_{n}\quad\text{for all $n>0$},

(3.43)

which implies that except for
\Phi^{(0)}
, all other
\Phi^{(n)}
are periodic since
\psi^{(2)}
is periodic. Single-valuedness of
\Phi^{(n)},n>0
follows from the Laplace equation as will be shown in Section
3.6.1
.

To summarize, we have deduced the periodicity constraints on
(\Phi^{(n+2)},\psi^{(n+2)},\alpha^{(n)})
in this Section by connecting to the inverse coordinates. The flux label
\psi^{(n+2)}
must be periodic in both angles on a torus. In the inverse coordinates, the general periodicity requirement on
\Phi,\alpha
is given by (
3.28
) and order by order the requirement is (
3.33
) together with (
3.43
). In the direct coordinates, the periodicity constraints are given by (

) and single-valuedness of
\Phi^{(n+2)}
for all

.

3.6
Solutions of the vacuum NAE equations to
O(n)

Our goal now is to analyze the harmonic contents of
(\Phi^{(n+2)},\psi^{(n+2)},\alpha^{(n)})
. The structure of the vacuum potential
\Phi
was analyzed in detail in
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
. We briefly summarize some of the relevant results here. To analyze the harmonic content of
\psi^{(n+2)}
and
\alpha^{(n)}
, we study the structure and properties of MDEs in the next Sections.

3.6.1
Solution of Step I : The Poisson equation for
\Phi^{(n+2)}

The Laplace equation to
O(n)
is

\displaystyle\left(\partial^{2}_{\omega}+(n+2)^{2}\right)\Phi^{(n+2)}

(3.44)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\kappa^{m}\cos^{m}\theta\left[\kappa\left(\partial_{\omega}\Phi^{(n-m+1)}\sin\theta-(n-m+1)\Phi^{(n-m+1)}\cos\theta\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\left.(m+1)\partial^{2}_{\ell}\Phi^{(n-m)}+\frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2}\partial_{\ell}(\kappa\cos\theta)\partial_{\ell}\Phi^{(n-m-1)}\right].

As shown in
(
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
, the right side of (
3.44
) has at most harmonic terms of order
(n)
. If the
(n+2)
-th poloidal harmonics were present in the forcing term of (
3.44
), the inversion of (
3.44
) would lead to linear (hence secular)
\omega
terms in
\Phi^{(n+2)}
. Absence of
(n+2)
forcing poloidal harmonics implies that
\Phi^{(n+2)}
has poloidal up to
n+2
, with the coefficients of
(n+2)^{\text{th}}
harmonics being free.

Therefore, we can write down the solution of
\Phi^{(n+2)}
as

\displaystyle\Phi^{(n+2)}=\sum_{j}\left(\Phi^{(n+2)}_{cj}(\ell)\cos(ju)+\Phi^{(n+2)}_{sj}(\ell)\sin(ju)\right),

(3.45)

where,
u
is defined by (
2.18
). For even
n
,
j=2r
and
r
varies from
0
to
(n/2+1)
. For odd
n
,
j=2r+1
and
r
varies from
0
to
(n+1)/2
. The functions
\Phi^{(n+2)}_{c(n+2)}(\ell),\Phi^{(n+2)}_{s(n+2)}(\ell)
are the “free functions,” and the rest are completely determined by the lower order quantities.

We now ensure that derivatives of
\Phi^{(n+2)}
are single-valued since they are physically related directly to the components of the magnetic field. The right-hand-side is manifestly periodic in the poloidal angle. Therefore, periodicity in the poloidal angle is maintained order by order. Let us now check for periodicity in
\ell
. The presence of
\Phi^{(n-m+1)}
without derivatives in the forcing term on the right-hand side of (
3.45
) might suggest that
\Phi^{(n+2)}
is not periodic since
\Phi^{(0)}
is multi-valued in
\ell
after all. However,
\Phi^{(0)}
never appears without any derivative in the forcing term. To have
\Phi^{(0)}
, we must have
m=n+1
, which is outside the summation range. Furthermore,
\Phi^{(1)}=0
implies that for any
n
, the lowest nontrivial term of the form
\Phi^{(n-m+1)}
is
\Phi^{(2)}
for
m=n-1
, which is periodic. Therefore, the forcing term in (
3.44
) is a linear superposition of derivatives of
(\Phi^{(0)},\Phi^{(2)},...,\Phi^{(m)})
and
\Phi^{m}
terms with
m>0
. By induction
\Phi^{(n+2)}
must be single-valued for all
n>0
as required by (
3.43
).

This completes our solution of Step I. The solution of Step II is more involved and will be developed over the next few Sections.

3.6.2
Solution of Step II : Analysis of the general MDE

To solve the MDE for
\psi
in Step II, we need to discuss some properties of the MDE. In this Section, we shall derive these properties for a general vacuum MDE.

The MDE
(
Newcomb 1959
)
for a quantity
\chi
takes the general form

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\chi+F_{\chi}=0,\qquad\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\equiv B_{\rho}\partial_{\rho}+B_{\omega}\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{\omega}+B_{\ell}\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\ell}

(3.46)

where
F_{\chi}
is the forcing (or the inhomogeneous) term. Expressing the components of
\bm{B}
in (
3.46
) in terms of derivatives of the scalar potential
\Phi
, the magnetic differential operator (MDO) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}=\Phi_{,\rho}\partial_{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Phi_{,\omega}\partial_{\omega}+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\Phi_{,\ell}\partial_{\ell}.

(3.47)

Expanding in powers of
\rho
and using the expressions for
\Phi^{(0)},\Phi^{(1)}
from (
3.8
), we find that (
3.46
) takes the form

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\chi^{(n)}+\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{\chi}=0,\quad

(3.48)

where,

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\equiv\left(\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)\partial_{\ell}+\Phi^{(2)}_{,\omega}\partial_{\omega}+2n\Phi^{(2)}\right).

(3.49)

The term
\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{\chi}
consists of
\chi^{(n-1)}
and other lower order quantities obtained from both
F_{\chi}
and
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\chi
expanded to
O(n)
. Note also that the definition of the lowest order MDO depends on the order
n
due to the
\partial_{\rho}
operator in (
3.47
). Clearly, for
n=2
,
\psi^{(2)}
is a homogeneous solution of (
3.48
), as claimed earlier.

Unsurprisingly, we will find the MDO (
3.49
) repeatedly in this work. We shall therefore record a few essential properties of the MDO before we continue with the analysis of the MDE (
3.48
).

The first important property to note is that an alternate form of the MDO is given by

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\left(\cdot\right)=2\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}\left\{\alpha^{(0)},\frac{1}{\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\left(\cdot\right)\right\}_{(\omega,\ell)},

(3.50)

where,
\{f,g\}_{(\omega,\ell)}
denotes a standard Poisson of bracket of functions
f
and
g
with respect to
(\omega,\ell)
. The form (
3.50
) follows from the definition of MDO (
3.49
), the equation for
\psi^{(2)}
(
3.12
), and the relations (
3.11
) that are satisfied by
(\Phi^{(2)},\alpha^{(0)},\psi^{(2)})
.

The Poisson-bracket form of the MDO, (
3.50
), allows us to readily find the homogeneous solutions of the MDE (
3.48
). Thus, if
\chi^{(n)}_{h}
is a homogeneous solution of (
3.48
), it must be of the form

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}_{h}=\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}\mathcal{C}_{h}\left(\alpha^{(0)}\right)

(3.51)

When the rotational transform is irrational,
\mathcal{C}_{h}
must be a constant if
\chi^{(n)}_{h}
represents a physical quantity.

The next important property of the MDO (
3.50
) is that it possesses an annihilator (an operator to whose kernel
(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}
belongs). To obtain the annihilator, we use the following property of the Poisson bracket

\displaystyle\left\{\alpha^{(0)},\chi\right\}_{(\omega,\ell)}\equiv\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\chi_{,\ell}-\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}\chi_{,\omega}=\partial_{\ell}\left(\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\chi\right)-\partial_{\omega}\left(\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}\chi\right).

(3.52)

Here we have used the fact that
\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)},\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}
are single-valued in order to commute the partial derivatives
\partial_{\ell},\partial_{\omega}
acting on
\alpha^{(0)}
in (
3.52
). Averaging (
3.52
) over
(\omega,\ell)
we get

\displaystyle\oint d\ell\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left\{\alpha^{(0)},\chi\right\}_{(\omega,\ell)}=0,

(3.53)

provided
\chi
is single-valued. Therefore, the operator

\displaystyle\langle\left(\cdot\right)\rangle_{(n)}=\oint\frac{d\ell}{2\pi}\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}\left(\cdot\right)

(3.54)

is an annihilator for the MDO (
3.50
). Note that the factor of
2\pi
below
d\ell
is because (
3.50
) has a derivative with respect to
\ell
. When written in terms of
\phi=2\pi\ell/L
, the factor of
2\pi
turns out to be the usual measure.

Finally, we must discuss the poloidal harmonics generated when the MDO acts on the
n^{\text{th}}
harmonics. This property is essential in order to solve the MDE order by order. In the inverse coordinate approach, one can use analyticity to show that there is an upper bound on the poloidal harmonics at each order. In the direct coordinate approach, the upper bound on the poloidal harmonics is not evident since various powers of
\left(\psi^{(2)}\right)
appear in the MDO (
3.50
) and its annihilator (
3.54
).

To deduce the poloidal harmonic content of
\chi^{(n)}
that satisfies the MDE (
3.48
), we will need another important property of the MDO (
3.49
). Let us consider a generic periodic function
\chi^{(n)}_{j}
of the form

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}_{j}=\chi^{(n)}_{cj}(\ell)\cos{(ju(\omega,\ell))}+\chi^{(n)}_{sj}(\ell)\sin{(ju(\omega,\ell))},

(3.55)

with
u(\omega,\ell)
defined in (
2.18
). The integer
j
such that
2\leq j\leq n
, is even (odd) if n is even (odd). Using (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) we can show that

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\chi^{(n)}_{j}=C^{(n)}_{cj}(\ell)\cos{ju}+C^{(n)}_{sj}(\ell)\sin{ju}+(n-j)C^{(n)}_{(+)}(\omega,\ell)+(n+j)C^{(n)}_{(-)}(\omega,\ell)

(3.56a)

\displaystyle C^{(n)}_{cj}(\ell)=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\left(\frac{d\chi^{(n)}_{cj}}{d\ell}+n(\delta^{\prime}-\tau)\chi^{(n)}_{sj}-\frac{n}{2}\frac{\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{0}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}\chi^{(n)}_{cj}\right)

(3.56b)

\displaystyle C^{(n)}_{sj}(\ell)=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\left(\frac{d\chi^{(n)}_{sj}}{d\ell}-n(\delta^{\prime}-\tau)\chi^{(n)}_{cj}-\frac{n}{2}\frac{\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{0}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}\chi^{(n)}_{sj}\right)

(3.56c)

\displaystyle C^{(n)}_{(+)}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\left[\left(f^{(2)}_{c2}\chi^{(n)}_{cj}-f^{(2)}_{s2}\chi^{(n)}_{sj}\right)\cos{(j+2)u}+\left(f^{(2)}_{c2}\chi^{(n)}_{sj}+f^{(2)}_{s2}\chi^{(n)}_{cj}\right)\sin{(j+2)u}\right]

(3.56d)

\displaystyle C^{(n)}_{(-)}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\left[\left(f^{(2)}_{c2}\chi^{(n)}_{cj}+f^{(2)}_{s2}\chi^{(n)}_{sj}\right)\cos{(j-2)u}-\left(f^{(2)}_{s2}\chi^{(n)}_{cj}-f^{(2)}_{c2}\chi^{(n)}_{sj}\right)\sin{(j-2)u}\right].

(3.56e)

We note, in particular, that for
j=n
, there are no terms with
(n+2)
poloidal harmonics. Thus, for a function of the form (
3.55
), i.e. with
j^{\text{th}}
poloidal harmonics in

, where
j
has the same parity as
n
and
0\leq j\leq n
,
(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\chi^{(n)}_{j}
is a function with poloidal harmonics strictly between

and
\min{(j+2,n)}
.

Now let us define a function
\chi^{(n)}=\sum_{j}\chi^{(n)}_{j}
such that

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}=\begin{cases}\sum_{r=0}^{n/2}\chi^{(n)}_{2r},\quad\quad\quad\text{n is even}\\
\\
\sum_{r=0}^{(n-1)/2}\chi^{(n)}_{2r+1},\quad\text{n is odd},\end{cases}

(3.57)

where,
\chi^{(n)}_{j}
’s for all
j,n
are of the form (
3.55
),
(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\chi^{(n)}
will have even (odd) poloidal harmonics only up to
n
for even (odd) values of
n
.
If we are given a function
\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{\chi}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{\chi}=\begin{cases}\sum_{r=0}^{n/2}\left(F^{(n)}_{c(2r)}\cos{2ru}+F^{(n)}_{s(2r)}\sin{2ru}\right),\quad\quad\quad\text{n is even}\\
\\
\sum_{r=0}^{(n-1)/2}\left(F^{(n)}_{c(2r+1)}\cos{(2r+1)u}+F^{(n)}_{s(2r+1)}\sin{(2r+1)u}\right),\quad\text{n is odd},\end{cases}

the solution of

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n)}_{0}\chi^{(n)}+\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{\chi}=0

is obtained by solving the coupled ODEs obtained by equating to zero the sum of the

terms given in (

) and the
\mathfrak{f}^{(n)}_{\chi}
terms given in (

).

We want to emphasize that without the property that no

3.6.4
Solution of Step II : Decoupling the ODEs obtained from the MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}

In the last Section, we showed how the MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}
can be expanded in a finite number of poloidal Fourier harmonics, given in (
3.68
). Collecting the various harmonics, the MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}
, which is a PDE, can be reduced to a coupled set of ODEs for the amplitudes,
Y^{(n+2)}_{cm},Y^{(n+2)}_{sm}
, where
0\leq m\leq n+2
. The coupling between
m
and
m\pm 2
harmonics can be seen from (
3.56
) to involve the quantities
f^{(2)}_{c2},f^{(2)}_{s2}
. We now address how to decouple these ODEs. To do so, we need to discuss the normal form representation of
\psi^{(n+2)}
instead of the poloidal harmonic representation.

The poloidal harmonic structure of
\Phi^{(n+2)}
and
\psi^{(n+2)}
are the same order by order, both having a maximum poloidal frequency of
(n+2)
at order
(n+2)
. This observation is directly related to the “analyticity” (in the sense of regularity near the axis) of vacuum magnetic fields assumed by Mercier and others. Rigorous proof of the analyticity condition for
\Phi,\psi
can be found in
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
;
Jorge et al. 2020b
)
. More concretely, “analyticity” implies
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
;
Solov’ev & Shafranov 1970
)

\displaystyle\psi^{(n+2)}\rho^{n+2}=\mathcal{P}_{(n+2)}\left(X_{\mathcal{N}},Y_{\mathcal{N}}\right),

(3.72)

where,
\mathcal{P}_{(n+2)}
is a homogeneous polynomial of order
(n+2)
in
(X_{\mathcal{N}},Y_{\mathcal{N}})
with coefficients that are functions of
\ell
. Here, the normal form variables
(X_{\mathcal{N}},Y_{\mathcal{N}})
satisfy (
3.19
). At
O(n)
, the polynomial
\mathcal{P}_{(n+2)}
can have at most
(n+2)
poloidal harmonics since the normal form variables only have first harmonics.

In the following, we use the following variables obtained from the normal form variables
(X_{\mathcal{N}},Y_{\mathcal{N}})

\displaystyle x_{\mathcal{N}}\equiv\frac{X_{\mathcal{N}}}{\rho}=e^{\eta/2}\cos u,\qquad y_{\mathcal{N}}\equiv\frac{Y_{\mathcal{N}}}{\rho}=e^{-\eta/2}\sin u.

(3.73)

From the expression of
\psi^{(2)}
in normal form variables (
3.20
), we find that

\displaystyle x_{\mathcal{N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\psi^{(2)}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}}\cos\Theta,\quad y_{\mathcal{N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\psi^{(2)}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}}\sin\Theta,\quad\tan\Theta=e^{-\eta}\tan u.

(3.74)

Following
(
Duignan & Meiss 2021
)
,we now employ complex variables

\displaystyle z_{\mathcal{N}}=x_{\mathcal{N}}+iy_{\mathcal{N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\psi^{(2)}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}}e^{i\Theta},\qquad\overline{z}_{\mathcal{N}}=x_{\mathcal{N}}-iy_{\mathcal{N}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\psi^{(2)}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}}e^{-i\Theta},

(3.75)

to rewrite the expression of
\psi^{(n+2)}
in the complex normal form coordinates (
3.72
) as

\displaystyle\psi^{(n+2)}=\sum_{m=0}^{n+2}\Psi^{(n+2)}_{m(n+2-m)}(\ell)\>\overline{z}_{\mathcal{N}}^{m}{z_{\mathcal{N}}}^{(n+2-m)}.

(3.76)

The connection between the amplitudes
\Psi^{(n+2)}_{mr}(\ell)
and the amplitudes
\psi^{(n+2)}_{cm},\psi^{(n+2)}_{sm}
follows from the definitions (
3.73
) and (
3.75
). Note that the reality condition of
\psi^{(n+2)}
implies that
\Psi^{(n+2)}_{m(n+2-m)}={\overline{\Psi}}^{(n+2)}_{(n+2-m)m}
.

We now substitute the complex normal form expression for
\psi^{(n+2)}
(
3.76
) into the MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}
(
3.62
). For that we first need to evaluate
(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n+2)}_{0}\psi^{(n+2)}
.

In Appendix
C
, we prove the following identities

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n+2)}_{0}\zeta_{m\mathcal{N}}^{(n+2)}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\left(i\Omega_{0}(n+2-2m)-\frac{n+2}{2}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}\right)\zeta_{m\mathcal{N}}^{(n+2)},\quad\zeta_{m\mathcal{N}}^{(n+2)}=\overline{z}_{\mathcal{N}}^{m}\>{z}^{n+2-m}_{\mathcal{N}}

(3.77a)

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})^{(n+2)}_{0}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{m}(\ell)\zeta_{m\mathcal{N}}^{(n+2)}\right)=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{m}^{\prime}+\left(i\Omega_{0}(n+2-2m)-\frac{n+2}{2}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}\right)\mathcal{Z}_{m}\right)\zeta_{m\mathcal{N}}^{(n+2)},

(3.77b)

which show that the complex normal form basis diagonalizes the MDO. As before, we get rid of the
\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}
term by defining
Y^{(n+2)}={\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}^{-(n+2)/2}\psi^{(n+2)}
, equivalently by
\mathcal{Z}_{m}\to\mathcal{Z}_{m}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}^{(n+2)/2}
in (
3.77
).

Therefore, the substitution of the following form of
\psi^{(n+2)}
,

\displaystyle\psi^{(n+2)}=\left(\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{n+2}{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{n+2}\mathcal{Z}^{(n+2)}_{m(n+2-m)}(\ell)\>\overline{z}_{\mathcal{N}}^{m}{z_{\mathcal{N}}}^{(n+2-m)},\quad\mathcal{Z}^{(n+2)}_{m(n+2-m)}={\overline{\mathcal{Z}}}^{(n+2)}_{(n+2-m)m},

(3.78)

into the MDE (
3.62
) leads to a system of
(n+1)
decoupled complex ODEs of the form

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{d\ell}{\mathcal{Z}_{m(n+2-m)}^{(n+2)}}+i\Omega_{0}(n+2-2m)\mathcal{Z}^{(n+2)}_{m(n+2-m)}+\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{m}}=0,

(3.79)

where
\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}_{\mathcal{Z}_{m}}
is an appropriate forcing term. We refer to (
3.79
) as a reduced MDE. The above results also holds for

as shown in Appendix
C
.

The complex normal form (
3.72
) for
\psi^{(n+2)}
is, therefore, of great help in systematically solving the MDE for
\psi
by reducing it to a system of decoupled ODEs (
3.79
). In Appendix
F
we have shown explicitly how the MDE for
\psi^{(3)}
can be solved by leveraging the normal form structure.

3.6.5
Solutions of Steps III and IV : Determination of
\alpha

Our main goal here is to calculate
\alpha^{(n)}
given the solutions of
\Phi^{(n+2)},\psi^{(n+2)}
, combining Steps III and IV, outlined in Section
3.4
. Step III consists of integrating the equation for
\alpha_{,\omega}
with respect to
\omega
to determine
\alpha^{(n)}
up to a function
\mathfrak{a}(\ell)
. Step IV involves poloidally averaging the MDE for
\alpha^{(n)}
to get an equation for
\mathfrak{a}(\ell)
.

We start with Step III. Power series expansion of (
3.2c
), rewritten as

\displaystyle\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}-\frac{\rho}{h}\Phi_{,\ell}=0,

(
3.2c
)

at each order,
n
, leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left((n-m)\psi^{(m+2)}_{,\omega}\alpha^{(n-m)}-(m+2)\psi^{(m+2)}\alpha^{(n-m)}_{,\omega}+\left(\kappa\cos\theta\right)^{(n-m)}\Phi^{(m)}_{,\ell}\right)=0.

(3.80)

The function
1/h=(1-\kappa\cos\theta)^{-1}
has been expanded in powers of
\rho
as well.

We shall now split
\psi^{(n+2)}
into the homogeneous
\psi^{(n+2)}_{H}
solution and the particular solution
\psi^{(n+2)}_{P}
,

\displaystyle\psi^{(n+2)}=\psi^{(n+2)}_{P}+{\psi_{H}}^{(n+2)},\quad{\psi_{H}}^{(n+2)}=\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}\mathcal{Y}^{(n+2)}_{H}.

(3.81)

Here, we assume that the on-axis rotational transform is irrational, so
\mathcal{Y}^{(n+2)}_{H}
is a constant. Separating the
m=0
and
m=n
terms in (
3.80
) from the rest and using the lowest order relation (
3.11
) we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega}\left(\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)=\frac{\Phi^{(n)}_{,\ell}+\Sigma^{(n)}_{1}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}-(n+2)\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\psi^{(n+2)}_{P}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+2}}+\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\mathcal{Y}^{(n+2)}_{H}\right),

(3.82)

where,
\left(\Sigma^{(n)}_{1}-(\kappa\cos\theta)^{n}\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\right)
is the sum of terms in (
3.80
) from
m=1
to
(n-1)
.
Imposing the poloidal integral constraint on
\alpha^{(n)}
as given in (
3.42
) and equating the average of
\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}
to unity, as shown in (
3.35
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(n+2)}_{H}=\oint\frac{d\omega}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}\frac{\left(\Phi^{(n)}_{,\ell}+\Sigma^{(n)}_{1}\right)}{(n+2)}-\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\oint\frac{d\omega}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+2}}\psi^{(n+2)}_{P}.

(3.83)

We can now integrate (
3.82
) with respect to
\omega
to obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)=\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}(\ell)+\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}(\omega,\ell),

(3.84)

where,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}(\omega,\ell)=\int_{0}^{\omega}d\omega^{\prime}\left(\frac{\Phi^{(n)}_{,\ell}+\Sigma^{(n)}_{1}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}-(n+2)\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\frac{\psi^{(n+2)}_{P}+\psi^{(n+2)}_{H}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+2}}\right),\quad\oint\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\>\>\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}(\omega^{\prime},\ell)=0.

(3.85)

Note that the average of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}
is zero because of the condition (
3.83
) that determines the homogeneous solution
\psi^{(n+2)}_{H}
. This completes Step III.

We now proceed with Step IV. To determine
\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}(\ell)
, we use the Poisson bracket form of the MDO (
3.50
)

\displaystyle\left(\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\partial_{\ell}-\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}\partial_{\omega}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)+\frac{F^{(n+2)}_{\alpha}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}=0,

(3.86)

which was derived earlier in (
3.27
).

Writing (
3.86
) in the form (
3.52
) and integrating with respect to
\omega
between
(0,2\pi)
we obtain the poloidally averaged MDE

\displaystyle\partial_{\ell}\left(\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\left(\frac{\alpha^{(n)}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}}\right)\right)+\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left(\frac{F^{(n+2)}_{\alpha}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}\right)=0.

(3.87)

We note here that the derivative in
\omega
vanished because both
\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}
and
\left(\alpha^{(n)}/\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2}\right)
are single-valued in
\omega
.

From (
3.84
) and (
3.35
) we then obtain the following equation for
\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}(\ell)

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}}^{\prime}(\ell)+\partial_{\ell}\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\>\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}}{2\psi^{(2)}}\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}(\omega,\ell)+\oint\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left(\frac{F^{(n+2)}_{\alpha}}{\left(2\psi^{(2)}\right)^{n/2+1}}\right)=0

(3.88)

The solution of (
3.88
) can be obtained through direct integration with respect to
\ell
. Note that
{\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{(n)}}^{\prime}(\ell)
is not periodic in general since its average is related to the higher order magnetic-shear as given in (
3.42
).

We reiterate that the form of
\alpha
in inverse-coordinates, as given in (
3.28
), is well-known. In particular, the secular terms
\theta-\iota(\psi)\phi
remain separate from the periodic components of
\alpha
. However, in direct coordinates, the secular and the periodic terms are mixed because the
\iota(\psi)\phi
term is now expanded in a power series of
\rho
. This explains the need to take special care in order to obtain

in direct coordinates.

3.6.6
Summary of Steps I-IV

We now briefly summarize the main results obtained in Section
3.6
. We have individually analyzed the structure of
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha)
, proving that the necessary periodicity conditions (
3.28
) for obtaining physically meaningful solutions can be satisfied order by order. In Section (
3.6.1
) we have discussed the
O(n+2)
Laplace equation and the structure of its solution
\Phi^{(n+2)}
. We have also given a prescription, (
3.70
), with which the free functions (the homogeneous solution to the Poisson equation) can be expressed in terms of the flux-surface shaping given by (
3.71
).

In Section
3.6.2
, we study the general MDE and obtain some of its fundamental properties. These properties allow one to construct homogeneous solutions of the MDEs and annihilators for the MDEs. Later, we use the homogeneous solution for
\psi^{(n+2)}
and the annihilator to enforce the periodicity constraint (
3.42
). The last important property is that the poloidal harmonics of the solution of the MDE truncate if the forcing term has finite poloidal harmonics. Using these properties we found that at
O(n)
,
\Phi^{(n+2)}
will have at most
(n+2)
harmonics. The structure of
\alpha^{(n)}
is more complicated, and solving the MDE for
\alpha
(
3.27
) is far more involved than MDE for
\psi
given by (
3.61
). Fortunately, we do not have to solve the MDE for
\alpha
.

Finally, in Section
3.6.5
, we demonstrate how one can obtain
\alpha^{(n)}
once
\Phi^{(n+2)},\psi^{(n+2)}
has been constructed. This brings us to the end of the analysis of the structure of vacuum equations in the Mercier-Weitzner formalism. In Section
6.1
and Appendix
H
, we discuss analytically tractable and physically interesting limits of 3D vacuum fields that possess nested surfaces. We can obtain explicit solutions order by order using the NAE formalism we have developed thus far.

4
NAE of force-free fields

For force-free systems, the current
\bm{J}
and magnetic field
\bm{B}
are co-linear, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{J}=\lambda\bm{B},\quad\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B}.

(4.1)

Since the divergence of
\bm{J}
is zero, we have

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\lambda=0.

(4.2)

We further assume that the force-free fields possess nested flux surfaces. Therefore, (
4.2
) implies that
\lambda
must be a function of only
\psi
and
\alpha
. Physically, the
\alpha
dependence of
\lambda
is special. It is only possible if the rotational transform is a constant rational number. Any amount of magnetic shear will lead to

\displaystyle\lambda=\lambda(\psi),

(4.3)

which we assume from now on.

4.1
Basic formalism for the force-Free fields

In the Mercier-Weitzner formalism, force-free fields that possess nested flux surfaces can be seen to be of the form

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi+\overline{K}(\psi,\alpha)\bm{\nabla}\psi,\quad\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha,

(4.4)

since the current takes the form

\displaystyle\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}\overline{K}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi=-\overline{K}_{,\alpha}\bm{B}.

(4.5)

Comparing (
4.1
) with (
4.5
) we find that

\displaystyle\overline{K}=-\lambda(\psi)\>\alpha.

(4.6)

Note that a homogeneous solution
\overline{K}^{\prime}_{0}(\psi)
could be added to (
4.6
). However, from (
4.4
) we find that the
\overline{K}^{\prime}_{0}(\psi)
term can be absorbed by redefining
\Phi\to\Phi-\overline{K}_{0}(\psi)
. Hence, we can set
\overline{K}_{0}
to zero without loss of generality.

To carry out the NAE, we assume that
\lambda(\psi)
is sufficiently smooth that we can expand
\lambda
as

\displaystyle\lambda(\psi)=\lambda_{0}+\psi\lambda_{2}+\psi^{2}\lambda_{4}+...,

(4.7)

where,
\lambda_{i},i=0,2,4..
are constants. However, the usual near-axis expansion in direct coordinates (
3.7
) needs to be checked carefully. From the condition
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
we have

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\overline{K}\bm{\nabla}\psi)=0

(4.8)

We need to show that the solution of (
4.8
) can be expressed as a regular power series near the axis. In the vacuum limit, we obtained (
3.44
), where the right-hand side did not have any harmonics that would beat with the operator on the left-hand side. However, it is not guaranteed that
at each
O(\rho^{n})
, the term
\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\overline{K}\bm{\nabla}\psi)
will not generate such resonant harmonics.

To further illustrate this point, let us consider the case when
\lambda=
constant, i.e.,
K=-\lambda\alpha
. The
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
condition now reads

\Delta\Phi-\lambda\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\alpha\bm{\nabla}\psi)=0

Expanding as in (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\rho^{n}\left(\partial^{2}_{\omega}+(n+2)^{2}\right)\Phi^{(n+2)}+...

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\alpha\bm{\nabla}\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\rho^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\alpha^{(i)}\psi^{(n+2-i)}(n+2)(n+2-i)+\partial_{\omega}\left(\alpha^{(i)}\psi_{,\omega}^{(n+2-i)}\right)\right)+...

Using the Fourier representations:
\psi^{(n+2-i)}\sim\sum\psi^{(n+2-i)}_{m}e^{imu},\alpha^{(i)}\sim\sum\alpha^{(i)}_{p}e^{ipu}
,

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\alpha\bm{\nabla}\psi)\sim\sum_{n}\rho^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n+2}\sum_{m=0}^{n+2-i}\sum_{p=0}^{i}\left(\psi^{(n+2-i)}_{m}\alpha^{(i)}_{p}e^{i(m+p)u}c_{ni}+...\right)

where,
c_{ni}(\ell)
is some overall constant factor. Clearly, resonance can occur when
m+p=n+2
. If such a
(n+2)
harmonic is generated then we cannot invert

(\partial^{2}_{\omega}+(n+2)^{2})\Phi^{(n+2)}\sim e^{i(n+2)u},

to solve for a periodic
\Phi^{(n+2)}
. As shown in
(
Weitzner 2016
)
, if such a resonance occurs, then the regular power series will fail, and weakly singular terms like
\rho^{n}(\log{\rho})^{m}
will appear in the Mercier expansion.

In the following Section, we prove that such logarithmically singular terms can indeed be avoided under reasonable assumptions. However, the boundary cannot be arbitrarily specified and must be self-consistent with the NAE.

4.2
Definition of new variables

Equating
\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi-\lambda\alpha\bm{\nabla}\psi=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha
, we get the following system of equations for the force-free magnetic fields with flux surfaces in the Mercier-Weitzner formalism:

\displaystyle\Phi_{,\rho}-\lambda\alpha\psi_{,\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho h}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{,\omega}-\lambda\alpha\psi_{,\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{h}\left(\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\rho}-\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}\right)

(4.9b)

\displaystyle\Phi_{,\ell}-\lambda\alpha\psi_{,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\rho}\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}\right)

(4.9c)

As discussed in the last Section, unlike the vacuum limit, it is not immediately evident that resonances can be avoided in the system (
4.9
).

Our goal is, therefore, to bring the force-free system of equations (
4.9
) as close as possible to the vacuum system given by (
3.2
).
We now try to define a new variable
\xi
that will play the role analogous to
\Phi
in the vacuum limit. It is useful to define a variable
\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle\lambda(\psi)=\Lambda^{\prime}(\psi).

(4.10)

Now, we define
\xi
such that

\displaystyle\xi_{,\rho}\equiv\Phi_{,\rho}-\lambda\alpha\psi_{,\rho}=\Phi_{,\rho}-\alpha\Lambda_{,\rho}.

(4.11)

Integrating with respect to
\rho
we get

\displaystyle\xi=\Phi+\mathcal{I},\quad\mathcal{I}\equiv-\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\Lambda_{,\varrho}\alpha.

(4.12)

Note that
\xi,\Phi
, and
\alpha
have similar secular terms linear in the angles. Therefore, imposing the form (
2.11
) order by order on
\alpha
is enough to impose the right secular structure of
\xi
.

Since ultimately we hope to be able to expand in power series of
\rho
, the integral
\mathcal{I}
in (
4.12
), amounts to simply reshuffling the terms and multiplying the coefficients by fractions. With this definition in mind we now rewrite (
4.9
) in terms of
\xi
and its derivatives as

\displaystyle\xi_{,\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho h}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)

(4.13a)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\omega}-\Lambda_{,\omega}\alpha-\mathcal{I}_{,\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{h}\left(\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\rho}-\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}\right)

(4.13b)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\ell}-\Lambda_{,\ell}\alpha-\mathcal{I}_{,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\rho}\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}\right)

(4.13c)

The
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
condition in terms of
\xi
reads

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\Delta\xi-\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\omega}\left(h\left(\Lambda_{,\omega}\alpha+\mathcal{I}_{,\omega}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\ell}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{h}\left(\Lambda_{,\ell}\alpha+\mathcal{I}_{,\ell}\right)\right)=0.

(4.14)

Comparing (
4.13
), (
4.14
) to the vacuum limit given by (
3.2
) and (
3.4
), we find that the two differ because of the
(\Lambda_{,\omega}\alpha+\mathcal{I}_{,\omega})
term, which is an
O(1)
term in the
\rho
expansion. All other deviations from the vacuum limit are higher order in
\rho
.
Fortunately, a workaround is possible by noting that

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\left(\Lambda\alpha_{,\omega}\right)-\partial_{\omega}\left(\Lambda\alpha_{,\rho}\right)=\lambda\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\alpha_{,\rho}\psi_{,\omega}\right)

(4.15)

Integrating once again in
\rho
, using (
4.13c
) and simplifying, we get

\displaystyle-\Lambda_{,\omega}\alpha-\mathcal{I}_{,\omega}=\mathcal{W},\quad\mathcal{W}\equiv\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\frac{\varrho}{h}\lambda\left(\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi\right),\quad\Xi\equiv-\left(\Lambda_{,\ell}\alpha+\mathcal{I}_{,\ell}\right).

(4.16)

With these new definitions, we can write down the force-free equations as

\displaystyle\xi_{,\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho h}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)=B_{\rho}

(4.17a)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\omega}+\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{h}\left(\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\rho}-\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}\right)=\rho B_{\omega}

(4.17b)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\rho}\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}\right)=hB_{\ell}

(4.17c)

where,

\displaystyle\lambda=\Lambda^{\prime}(\psi),\;\;\mathcal{I}\equiv-\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\Lambda_{,\varrho}\alpha,\;\;\Xi\equiv-(\Lambda_{,\ell}\alpha+\mathcal{I}_{,\ell}),\;\;\mathcal{W}\equiv\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\frac{\varrho}{h}\lambda\left(\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi\right).

(4.18)

Finally, the Poisson equation for
\xi
reads

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\Delta\xi+\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\omega}\left(h\mathcal{W}\right)+\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\ell}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{h}\Xi\right)=0.

(4.19)

In Appendix
E
, we show in detail that just like in the vacuum limit, (
4.19
) can be solved order by order without encountering any resonances. In short, the basic idea of the proof is to note that while the first term in (
4.19
) is
O(1)
and of the form
(\partial^{2}_{\omega}+(n+2)^{2})\xi^{(n+2)}
, the second and the third term both are
O(\rho^{2})
smaller than the first term. Hence, the second and third term has at most
n^{\text{th}}
harmonics, thereby avoiding any resonance with the first term. Thus, the logarithmic terms are avoided. Once
\xi
has been obtained to
O(\rho^{n})
, we can use (
4.12
) to determine
\Phi
to the same order of accuracy.

4.3
NAE equations for Force-Free equilibrium

We shall now discuss the lowest-order NAE equations for the force-free equilibrium using the variables defined in the last Section. Finally, we shall discuss the lowest two orders before discussing the general
O(\rho^{n})
system of equations.

Substituting the near-axis power series expansion into the definitions of
\mathcal{I},\Xi
given in (
4.18
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=-\lambda_{0}\rho^{2}\alpha^{(0)}\psi^{(2)}+O(\rho^{3}),\;\;\Xi=\rho^{2}\lambda_{0}\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}+O(\rho^{3}),\;\;\mathcal{W}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\xi^{(0)}_{,\ell}\rho^{2}+O(\rho^{3}).

(4.20)

We are now in a position to investigate the Poisson equation for
\xi
, (
4.19
). First, we note from (
4.20
) that the contribution of
\Xi
to (
4.17b
) starts at
O(\rho^{4})
. Next, we find from (
4.20
) that the Poisson equation (
4.19
) matches the vacuum case exactly to
O(\rho^{-2}),O(\rho^{-1})
. Thus,
\xi
and
\Phi
differ only from the second order onward, i.e.,

\displaystyle\xi^{(0)}=\Phi_{0}(\ell),\quad\xi^{(1)}=0,\qquad B_{0}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell).

(4.21)

Consequently, we note that even though
\mathcal{W}\sim O(\rho^{2})
its contribution to (
4.19
) through the
\partial\omega(h\mathcal{W})
term is
O(\rho^{3})
since it is a function of
\ell
to lowest order. Therefore, even the
O(1)
Poisson equation assumes the same form as in the vacuum case, namely,

\displaystyle\left(\partial^{2}_{\omega}+4\right)\xi^{(2)}+\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\ell)=0,

(4.22)

whose solution is given by

\displaystyle\xi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{4}\Phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\ell)+\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(z)\left(f^{(2)}_{c2}(\ell)\cos{2u}+f^{(2)}_{s2}(\ell)\sin{2u}\right),

(4.23)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\theta+\delta(\ell)=\omega-\int\tau d\ell+\delta(\ell).

The components of the magnetic field are given by

\displaystyle B_{\rho}=2\rho\xi^{(2)}+O(\rho^{3}),\quad B_{\omega}=\rho\left(\xi^{(2)}_{,\omega}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\right),\quad B_{\ell}=(1+\kappa\rho\cos\theta)\Phi_{0}^{\prime}+O(\rho^{2}),

(4.24)

which implies that

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla})_{0}^{(n)}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\partial_{\ell}+\left(\xi^{(2)}_{,\omega}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\right)\partial_{\omega}+2n\xi^{(2)}.

(4.25)

Another implication of (
4.24
) is that the
O(\rho^{2})
NAE equations are

\displaystyle 2\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}=\Phi_{0}^{\prime},\quad 2\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}=-\left(\xi^{(2)}_{,\omega}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\right)

(4.26a)

\displaystyle 2\xi^{(2)}=\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}\psi^{(2)}_{,\omega}-\alpha_{,\omega}^{(0)}\psi^{(2)}_{,\ell}.

(4.26b)

The lowest order flux surface and field line label can be shown to be the one obtained by Mercier
(
Mercier 1964
)

\displaystyle\psi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)(a(\ell)+b(\ell)\cos{2u})

(4.27a)

\displaystyle a(\ell)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\cosh(\eta(\ell)),\quad b(\ell)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sinh(\eta(\ell))

(4.27b)

\displaystyle f^{(2)}_{s2}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{b}{2a}\left(\delta^{\prime}-\tau+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\right)\quad,\quad f^{(2)}_{c2}(\ell)=-\dfrac{a^{\prime}}{4b}=-\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{4}

(4.27c)

\displaystyle\alpha^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\left(e^{-\eta}\tan u\right)-\int d\ell\left(\dfrac{\left(\delta^{\prime}-\tau+\lambda_{0}/2\right)}{\cosh{\eta}}\right)

(4.27d)

As in the vacuum case, we choose
2\pi\psi
as the toroidal flux. A comparison with the vacuum solutions given in Section
3.3
shows minimal changes from vacuum solutions. The main difference arises because of the extra
\lambda_{0}/2
factor in the expression for
\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}
in (
4.26a
). While the structure of the equations stays the same, the forcing functions,
F_{\phi},F_{\psi},F_{\alpha}
, get modified to include the effects of force-free currents.

The on-axis rotational transform for the force-free case is given by

\displaystyle\iota_{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha(\ell,\theta)-\alpha(\ell+L,\theta)+N=\frac{v(L)-(\delta(L)-\delta(0))}{2\pi}+N,

\displaystyle v(\ell)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\ell}ds\frac{\delta^{\prime}(s)-\tau(s)+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}}{\cosh{\eta(s)}}.

(4.28)

As shown by
(
Mercier 1974
)
, pressure does not affect the on-axis rotational transform. Therefore, the on-axis rotational transform given in (
4.28
) also applies to the general MHD case.

We now proceed to describe the structure of the
O(\rho^{(n+2)})
equations. We note that just like in the vacuum case, equation (
4.17a
) gives us an MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}
. To obtain the MDE for
\alpha^{(n)}
we proceed exactly as in the vacuum limit by eliminating
\psi^{(n+2)}
from (
4.17b
) and (
4.17c
). Therefore, following the vacuum case, we can write down the steps involved in solving the Force-Free equations

(\xi^{(n+2)},\psi^{(n+2)},\alpha^{(n)})
. The steps are precisely the ones we discussed in the vacuum limit.

I.

Poisson equation (
4.19
) for
\xi^{(n+2)}

II.

The MDE for
\psi^{(n+2)}
from (
4.17a
)

III.

The
\alpha_{,\omega}
equation from (
4.17c
) to solve for
\alpha^{(n)}
up to a function
\mathfrak{\overline{a}}^{(n)}(\ell)

IV.

Poloidal
\omega
average of the MDE for
\alpha
to determine
\mathfrak{\overline{a}}^{(n)}(\ell)
.

We present a detailed demonstration of the above steps for
n=1
in Appendix
G
.

5
MHD equilibrium with finite
\beta\sim p^{\prime}/B^{2}

5.1
Basic formalism for the MHD fields: Current potentials

In the Mercier-Weitzner formalism, as mentioned in Section
2
, we have an inhomogeneous MDE of the form

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}K=p^{\prime}(\psi)

(
2.8
)

that must be solved to obtain the current potential
K
. In the force-free limit, we obtained the homogeneous solution
K=\overline{K}=-\lambda(\psi)\alpha
. In the presence of finite pressure gradients, in addition to
\overline{K}
, we also need to solve for the inhomogeneous solution,
G
, such that

\displaystyle K=\overline{K}(\psi,\alpha)+G(\rho,\omega,\ell),\quad\overline{K}(\psi,\alpha)=-\lambda\alpha,\quad\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}G=p^{\prime}(\psi).

(5.1)

Equation (
5.1
) implies

\displaystyle\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}K\times\bm{\nabla}\psi=\lambda(\psi)\bm{B}+\bm{\nabla}G\times\bm{\nabla}\psi.

(5.2)

Thus, the quantity
G
is the pressure-driven part of the current potential. Note that, in general, the function
\lambda
is not the parallel current (
j_{||}
) since

\displaystyle j_{||}\equiv\frac{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{B}}{B^{2}}=\lambda(\psi)-\frac{\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}G}{B^{2}}

(5.3)

Only in the force-free limit (
p^{\prime}=0,G=0
)
j_{||}
equal
\lambda
. Also, note that
G
is not single-valued. However, the secular parts of
G
need to be of the same form as
K
and
\alpha
in (
2.11
).

Let us first look at the axisymmetric limit to understand better the relation between
\lambda,G
, and the parallel current. The axisymmetric magnetic field and the current in a tokamak are given by
(
Freidberg 1982
)

\displaystyle\bm{B}=F(\Psi)\bm{\nabla}\zeta+\bm{\nabla}\zeta\times\bm{\nabla}\Psi,\quad\bm{J}=-F^{\prime}(\Psi)\bm{B}-R^{2}p^{\prime}(\Psi)\bm{\nabla}\zeta,

(5.4)

where,
(R,\zeta,z)
denotes the standard cylindrical coordinate system,
F(\Psi)
is the poloidal current, and
\Psi
is the poloidal flux function that satisfies
d\Psi/d\psi=\iota(\psi)
together the Grad-Shafranov equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{*}\Psi+FF^{\prime}+R^{2}p^{\prime}(\Psi)=0,\quad\Delta_{*}=R^{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{R^{2}}\bm{\nabla}\right).

(5.5)

Furthermore, in axisymmetry, the toroidal angle dependence can only be through secular terms. Thus,

\displaystyle G=\overline{G}(\Psi(R,z))\zeta+\widetilde{G}(R,z),\qquad\overline{G}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\zeta+\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\widetilde{G}=\iota p^{\prime}(\Psi).

(5.6)

Using the following axisymmetric relations
(
Freidberg 1982
;
Helander 2014
)

\displaystyle j_{||}=-F^{\prime}(\Psi)-\frac{p^{\prime}(\Psi)F}{B^{2}},\quad\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}=F\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}-B^{2}\partial_{\zeta},\quad

(5.7)

and (
5.6
), the pressure driven terms in the equation for
\lambda
, (
5.3
), cancel resulting in

\displaystyle-\lambda(\psi)=F^{\prime}(\Psi)+\frac{1}{\iota(\psi)}\overline{G}(\Psi).

(5.8)

Equation (
5.8
) shows that in the force-free limit
-\lambda(\psi)=F^{\prime}(\Psi)
, but for finite-beta, the pressure-driven current also contributes to
\lambda
through the secular term.

5.2
NAE of MHD fields

We now discuss the NAE of MHD fields. We postulate that
\overline{K},G
have the following expansions

\displaystyle\overline{K}(\psi,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\alpha(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{2}\psi+\lambda_{4}\psi^{2}+\dots),

(5.9a)

\displaystyle G(\rho,\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=G^{(0)}(\ell)+G^{(1)}(\omega,\ell)\rho+G^{(2)}(\omega,\ell)\rho^{2}+\dots

(5.9b)

where each quantity is finite and non-singular. By allowing power-series expansions of
\overline{K},G
, we are explicitly excluding current profiles that are singular near the axis. It is beyond the scope of this work to account for the effects of near-axis current singularities and will be left for future publications.

Carrying out the NAE assuming power series expansion in
\rho
, we get the following coupled PDE system at each order,
n
:

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}G)^{(n)}

\displaystyle=p^{(n+2)}+\dots,

(5.10a)

\displaystyle(\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi)^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\dots,

(5.10b)

\displaystyle(\Delta\Phi+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(K\bm{\nabla}\psi))^{(n)}

\displaystyle=0.

(5.10c)

We have precisely two MDE for
G
and
\psi
and one elliptic equation for
\Phi
. The function
\alpha
is obtained from
\Phi,\psi
without solving an MDE for
\alpha
. Therefore, the PDE is of mixed elliptic-hyperbolic type as is well-known
(
Weitzner 2014
;
Bauer et al. 2012a
)
. However, as discussed in Section
4
, it is not immediately obvious that (
5.10c
) can be solved for
\Phi
without encountering logarithmic singularities. In principle, the logarithmic singularities can still arise even if the current profiles are chosen to be smooth near the axis by imposing (
5.9
).

5.3
Definitions of new variables

The change of variables is the same as in the force-free case, with additional terms from
G
. We shall first the covariant and the contravariant forms of
\bm{B}

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\Phi+(-\lambda\alpha+G)\bm{\nabla}\psi=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha.

(5.11)

Then we define the new variable
\xi
, such that

\displaystyle\xi_{,\rho}=\Phi_{,\rho}+(-\lambda\alpha+G)\psi_{,\rho}.

(5.12)

Integrating (
5.13
) with respect to
\rho
we get

\displaystyle\xi=\Phi+\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{G},\quad\mathcal{I}\equiv-\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\Lambda_{,\varrho}\alpha,\quad\mathcal{G}=\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>G\psi_{,\varrho}.

(5.13)

The
(\rho,\omega,\ell)
components of (
5.11
) then yields

\displaystyle\xi_{,\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho h}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)=B_{\rho},

(5.14a)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\omega}+\mathcal{Y}-\left(\mathcal{I}_{,\omega}+\alpha\Lambda_{,\omega}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{h}\left(\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\rho}-\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}\right)=\rho B_{\omega},

(5.14b)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi+\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\rho}\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}\right)=hB_{\ell}.

(5.14c)

While the
B_{\rho}
and
B_{\ell}
equations above show minimal change when we go to the vacuum limit, the
B_{\omega}
equation needs modifications similar to the force-free case. Utilizing the same trick (
4.15
) that we used before in the force-free case, we find that

\displaystyle-\left(\mathcal{I}_{,\omega}+\alpha\Lambda_{,\omega}\right)=\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\frac{\varrho}{h}\lambda\left(\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi+\mathcal{X}\right).

(5.15)

Therefore, the final set of MHD equations has the form

\displaystyle\xi_{,\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho h}\left(\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\ell}-\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\omega}\right)=B_{\rho},

(5.16a)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\omega}+\mathcal{Y}+\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{h}\left(\psi_{,\ell}\alpha_{,\rho}-\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\ell}\right)=\rho B_{\omega},

(5.16b)

\displaystyle\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi+\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{\rho}\left(\psi_{,\rho}\alpha_{,\omega}-\psi_{,\omega}\alpha_{,\rho}\right)=hB_{\ell},

(5.16c)

\displaystyle\text{where,}\quad\lambda=\Lambda^{\prime}(\psi),\quad\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\equiv-\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\Lambda_{,\varrho}\alpha\>\>,\>\>\Xi\equiv-\left(\Lambda_{,\ell}\alpha+\mathcal{I}_{,\ell}\right),\>\>

(5.16d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>G\psi_{,\varrho},\>\>\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle\equiv G\psi_{,\omega}-\mathcal{G}_{,\omega},\>\>\mathcal{X}\equiv G\psi_{,\ell}-\mathcal{G}_{,\ell},\;\;\mathcal{W}=\int_{0}^{\rho}d\varrho\>\frac{\varrho}{h}\lambda\left(\xi_{,\ell}+\Xi+\mathcal{X}\right).

The Poisson equation for
\xi
is now given by

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\Delta\xi+\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\omega}\left(h\left(\mathcal{Y}+\mathcal{W}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{h}\partial_{\ell}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{h}(\Xi+\mathcal{X})\right)=0.

(5.17)

As in the force-free case,
\xi
plays the analogous role of
\Phi
in the vacuum limit, and it can be shown (see Appendix
E
) that the Poisson equation for
\xi
is free of any resonances.

5.4
NAE of MHD equations

The expansions of the quantities
\mathcal{I},\Xi
that appear in (
5.16
) have already been discussed in (
4.20
). From the NAE, (
5.9
) and (
2.22
) we find that the quantities
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{I},\Xi
, as defined in (
5.16d
), have the following forms

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}G^{(0)}(\ell)\psi^{(2)}+O(\rho^{3}),\quad\mathcal{Y}=O(\rho^{3}),\quad\mathcal{X}=-\rho^{2}{G^{(0)}}^{\prime}(\ell)\psi^{(2)}+O(\rho^{3}),

(5.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{0}\rho^{2}\alpha^{(0)}\psi^{(2)}+O(\rho^{3}),\;\;\Xi=\rho^{2}\lambda_{0}\psi^{(2)}\alpha_{,\ell}^{(0)}+O(\rho^{3}),\;\;\mathcal{W}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\xi^{(0)}_{,\ell}\rho^{2}+O(\rho^{3}).

Therefore, the deviation of the MHD system (
5.16
) from the vacuum system (
3.2
) is zero to
O(\rho^{-2}),O(\rho^{-1})
, just like the force-free equations (
4.17
). Moreover, (
5.18
) also implies that the expressions for the lowest order components of
\bm{B}
are the same as in the force-free case (
4.24
). As a result, MHD and force-free both have the same form of MDO given by (
4.24
). Consequently, the basic framework is analogous to the vacuum system and the effects of currents and pressure show up mostly through the forcing terms
F_{\phi},F_{\psi},F_{\alpha}
.

The only additional step in MHD is Step 0, which involves solving the MDE for
G
order by order. Step 0 follows the same procedure that we have discussed for the solution of the generic MDE in Section
3.6.2
. To
O(\rho^{0})
, the MDE for
G
is given by

\displaystyle G^{(0)}_{,\ell}\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)=p^{(2)},

(5.19)

which can be easily solved to obtain the following

\displaystyle G^{(0)}=p^{(2)}\int\dfrac{d\ell}{\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\ell)},\quad K^{(0)}=G^{(0)}-\lambda_{0}\alpha^{(0)}.

(5.20)

The details of the general first-order solutions are given in Appendix
G
. Due to the similarities between the force-free and the vacuum problem, we do not explicitly show Steps I-IV.

8
Discussion

In this work, we have presented an asymptotic theory of MHD equilibrium in a stellarator using the near-axis expansion scheme. Our approach generalizes previous work in this area by allowing MHD fields of arbitrary plasma beta. We have shown that the analyticity property of the vacuum fields, i.e., the existence of regular power-series expansions in the vicinity of the magnetic axis, can be retained if one allows for current profiles with regular power-series expansions near the axis. We have studied the vacuum limit carefully as a model for the force-free and MHD cases. We have shown that near the axis, we can transform to variables that allow us to describe the force-free and MHD systems analogous to the vacuum system.

We have demonstrated that one can carry out the NAE formally to all orders by following the same steps. The zeroth step consists of solving a magnetic differential equation for the pressure-driven part of the current. We have shown how using Birkhoff normal form considerably simplifies this calculation. The first step is to solve a Poisson equation for a scalar representing the magnetic scalar potential in the vacuum limit. The second step is to solve another MDE to obtain the flux function up to a homogeneous solution of the MDE. We fix this homogeneous solution by requiring the periodicity of physical quantities. Finally, with the help of the flux function and the solution to the Poisson equation, we can obtain the field line label through a simple integral over the poloidal angle up to a function of only the toroidal variable. We use the poloidally averaged MDE for the field line label to fix this unknown. The procedure described above is repeated in each order. We have given the complete details of the first two orders of the NAE for vacuum fields in Appendix
F
followed by force-free and MHD fields in Appendix
G
.

We have illustrated the finite beta near-axis procedure obtained through the Mercier-Weitzer formalism for various problems. We have treated the case of vacuum fields with a nearly circular cross-section in detail in Section
6.1
. The force-free and finite beta analogs of this example are given in Appendix
H.2
. Furthermore, the special cases of axisymmetry with Solov’ev profiles and magnetic axis with constant torsion are discussed in Section
6.2
. The analytical details of these examples are to be found in Appendices
H.3
and
H.4
.

The discussion of physical quantities, such as the magnetic well and the magnetic shear, which plays a crucial role in MHD equilibrium and stability, is out of the scope of the present work. Analytical expression of the near-axis magnetic shear has already been obtained in inverse coordinates
(
Rodriguez et al. 2022
)
, while the near-axis magnetic well has been studied in
(
Landreman & Jorge 2020
;
Kim et al. 2021
)
. In subsequent work, we shall calculate the magnetic well and the shear in direct coordinates using the formalism developed in this work.

The calculations presented here, while lengthy, move forward the NAE stellarator optimization program significantly by presenting reduced analytical models that can be automated in fast numerical optimization suites for stellarator design and for testing numerical codes.
