Near-Optimal Two-Pass Streaming Algorithm for Sampling Random Walks over Directed Graphs
Lijie Chen, Gillat Kol, Dmitry Paramonov, Raghuvansh R. Saxena, Zhao Song, Huacheng Yu

Abstract
For a directed graph G G with n n vertices and a start vertex u 𝗌𝗍𝖺𝗋𝗍 u_{\sf start} , we wish to (approximately) sample an L L -step random walk over G G starting from u 𝗌𝗍𝖺𝗋𝗍 u_{\sf start} with minimum space using an algorithm that only makes few passes over the edges of the graph. This problem found many applications, for instance, in approximating the PageRank of a webpage. If only a single pass is allowed, the space complexity of this problem was shown to be Θ ~ ​ ( n ⋅ L ) \widetilde{\Theta}(n\cdot L) . Prior to our work, a better space complexity was only known with O ~ ​ ( L ) \widetilde{O}(\sqrt{L}) passes. We settle the space complexity of this random walk simulation problem for two-pass streaming algorithms, showing that it is Θ ~ ​ ( n ⋅ L ) \widetilde{\Theta}(n\cdot\sqrt{L}) , by giving almost matching upper and lower bounds. Our lower bound argument extends to every constant number of passes p p , and shows that any p p -pass algorithm for this problem uses Ω ~ ​ ( n ⋅ L 1 / p ) \widetilde{\Omega}(n\cdot L^{1/p}) space. In addition, we show a similar Θ ~ ​ ( n ⋅ L ) \widetilde{\Theta}(n\cdot\sqrt{L}) bound on the space complexity of any algorithm (with any number of passes) for the related problem of sampling an L L -step random walk from every vertex in the graph.

1.3
Discussions and Open Problems

Better space complexity with more passes?

Our results leave open a couple of interesting directions for future work. The most significant open question is to understand the streaming space complexity of sampling random walks with more than two passes. In particular,
Theorem 1.2
implies that a three-pass streaming algorithm has space complexity at least
\widetilde{\Omega}(n\cdot L^{1/3})
. Can one get
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot L^{1/3})
space with three passes, or at least
O(n\cdot L^{1/2-\varepsilon})
space, for some constant
\varepsilon>0
? Note that, as explained in
Section 1.2.2
, such an algorithm must utilize its knowledge of the starting vertex when it reads the graph stream.

Theorem 1.2
does not rule out semi-streaming
\widetilde{O}(n)
space algorithms even when
p
is a moderately growing function of
n
and
L
. In
[
SGP11
]
, it is shown that such an
\widetilde{O}(n)
space algorithm exists with
p=\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{L})
passes. Does a semi-streaming algorithm with, say,
\mathrm{poly}\log(L)
passes exist?

Undirected graphs?

It would also be interesting to see what is the best two-pass streaming algorithm for simulating random walks on
undirected graphs
. Specifically, is it possible to combine our algorithm with the algorithm from
[
Jin19
]
to obtain an improvement over the optimal
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot\sqrt{L})
space complexity of a one-pass streaming algorithm for this problem?

Only outputting the end vertex?

Finally, our lower bounds only apply to the case where the algorithms need to output an entire random path
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{L})
. If instead only the last vertex
v_{L}
in the random walk is required, can one design better two-pass algorithms or prove a non-trivial lower bound?

2
Techniques

2.1
The Two-Pass Algorithm

We next overview our two-pass algorithm from
Theorem 1.1
, that simulates random walks with only
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot\sqrt{L})
space.

The folklore one-pass algorithm.

Before discussing our algorithm, it would be instructive to review the folklore
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot L)
-space one-pass algorithm for simulating
L
-step random walks in a directed graph
G=(V,E)
(for simplicity, we will always assume
L\leq n
in the discussions). The algorithm is quite simple:

1.

For every vertex
v\in V
, sample
L
of its outgoing neighbors
with replacement
and store them in a list
L^{\sf save}_{v}
of length
L
(that is, for each
j\in[L]
, the
j
-th element of
L^{\sf save}_{v}
is an
independent uniformly random
outgoing vertex of
v
). This can be done in a single pass over input graph stream using reservoir sampling
[
Vit85
]
.

2.

Given a starting vertex
u_{\sf start}\in V
, our random walk starts from
u_{\sf start}
and repeats the following for
L
steps: suppose we are currently at vertex
v
and it is the
k
-th time we visit this vertex, then we go from
v
to the
k
-th vertex in the list
L^{\sf save}_{v}
.

It is not hard to see that the above algorithm works: whenever we visit a vertex
v\in V
, the next element in the list
L^{\sf save}_{v}
will always be a uniformly random outgoing neighbor of
v
,
conditioned on
the walk we have produced so far; and we will never run out of the available neighbors of
v
as
|L^{\sf save}_{v}|=L
.

A naive attempt and the obstacles.

Since we are aiming at only using
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot\sqrt{L})
space, a naive attempt to improve the above algorithm is to just sample and store
\tau=O(\sqrt{L})
outgoing neighbors instead of
L
neighbors, and simulate the walk starting from
u_{\sf start}
in the same way.
The issue here is that, during the simulation of an
L
-step walk, whenever one visits a vertex
v
more than
\tau
times, one would run out of available vertices in the list
L^{\sf save}_{v}
, and the algorithm can no longer produce a legit random walk. For a simple example, imagine we have a star-like graph where
n-1
vertices are connected to a center vertex via two-way edges. An
L
-step random walk starting at the center would require at least
\Omega(L)
samples from the center’s neighbors, and our naive algorithm completely breaks.

Our approach: heavy and light vertices.

Observe, however, that in the above example of a star-like graph, we are only at risk of not storing enough random neighbors of the center node, as an
L
-step
random
walk would only visit the other non-center vertices a very small number of times. Thus, the algorithm may simply record all edges from the center with only
O(n)
space.
This observation inspires the following approach for a two-pass algorithm:

1.

In the first pass, we identify all the vertices that are likely to be visited many times by a random walk (starting from
some
vertex). We call such vertices
heavy
, while all other vertices are called
light
.

2.

In the second pass, we record
all
outgoing neighbors of all heavy vertices, as well as
O(\tau)
random outgoing neighbors with replacement of each of the light vertices.

Observe that the obtained algorithm is indeed
oblivious to the starting vertex
: the two passes described above do not use the starting vertex. Still, given the set of outgoing neighbors stored by the second pass, we are able to sample a random walk from any start vertex.

First pass: how do we detect heavy vertices?

The above approach requires that we detect, in a single pass, all vertices
v
that with a decent probability (say,
1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
), are visited more than
O(\tau)
times by an
L
-step random walk.
To this end, we observe that if a random walk visits a vertex
v
more than
\tau
times, this random walk must follow more than
\tau-1
self-circles around
v
in
L
steps. This, in turn, implies that a random walk that starts from
v
is likely to return to
v
in roughly
L/\tau=O(\sqrt{L})
steps.

The above discussion suggests the following definition of heavy vertices: a vertex
v
is
heavy
, if a random walk starting from
v
is likely (say, with probability at least
1/3
) to revisit
v
in
O(\sqrt{L})
steps. Indeed, this property is much easier to detect: we can run
O(\log n)
independent copies of the folklore one-pass streaming algorithms to sample
O(\log n)

O(\sqrt{L})
-step random walks starting from
v
, and count how many of them return to
v
at some step.

Second pass: can we afford to store the neighbors?

In
Section 4.3
, we show that for a light vertex
v
, an
L
-step random walk starting at any vertex visits
v

O(\sqrt{L})
times with high probability. Therefore, in the second pass, we can safely record only
O(\sqrt{L})
outgoing neighbors for all light vertices. Still, we have to record all the outgoing neighbors for heavy vertices.

The crux of our analysis is a
structural result
about directed graphs, showing that the total outgoing degree of all heavy vertices is bounded by
O(n\cdot\sqrt{L})
, and therefore we can simply store all of their outgoing neighbors. This is proved in
Section 4.1
, which may also be of independent interest.

Intuition behind the structure lemma.

Finally, we discuss the insights behind the above structure lemma for directed graphs. We will use
d_{\sf out}(v)
to denote the number of outgoing neighbors of
v
. For concreteness, we now say a vertex
v
is heavy if a random walk starting from
v
revisits
v
in
\sqrt{L}
steps with probability at least
1/3
.

Let
V_{\sf heavy}\subseteq V
be the set of heavy vertices and let
v\in V_{\sf heavy}
. By a simple calculation, one can see that for at least a
1/6
fraction of outgoing neighbors
u
of
v
, a random walk starting from
u
visits
v
in
\sqrt{L}
steps with probability at least
1/6
. The key insight is to consider the number of pairs
(u,v)\in V^{2}
such that a random walk starting from
u
visits
v
in
\sqrt{L}
steps with probability at least
1/6
. We will use
\mathcal{S}
to denote this set.

•

By the previous discussions, we can see that for each heavy vertex
v
, it adds at least
1/6

d_{\sf out}(v)
pairs to the set
\mathcal{S}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|\geq\frac{1}{6}\cdot\sum_{v\in V_{\sf heavy}}d_{\sf out}(v).

(1)

•

On the other hand, it is not hard to see that for each vertex
v
, there are at most
O(\sqrt{L})
many pairs of the form
(v,u)\in\mathcal{S}
, since a
\sqrt{L}
-step walk can visit only
\sqrt{L}
vertices. So we also have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|\leq O(n\sqrt{L}).

(2)

Putting the above (
Equation 1
and
Equation 2
) together, we get the desired bound

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{\sf heavy}}d_{\sf out}(v)\leq O(n\sqrt{L}).

2.2
Lower Bound for
p
-Pass Algorithms

We now describe the ideas behind the proof of
Theorem 1.2
, our
\widetilde{\Omega}(n\cdot L^{1/p})
space lower bound for
p
-pass randomized streaming algorithms for sampling random walks. We mention that many of the tools developed for proving space lower bounds are not directly applicable when one wishes to lower bound the space complexity of a
sampling
task and are more suitable for proving lower bounds on the space required to compute a function or a search problem
6
6

6

One such tool that cannot be used directly for our purpose is the very useful
Yao’s minimax principle

[
Yao77
]
that allows proving randomized communication lower bounds by proving the corresponding distributional (deterministic) communication lower bounds.
.

From sampling to function computation.

Our way around this is to first prove a reduction from streaming algorithms that sample a random walk from
u_{\sf start}
to streaming algorithms that compute the
(p+1)
-neighborhood of the vertex
u_{\sf start}
. This is done by considering a graph where a random walk returns to the vertex
u_{\sf start}
every
p+2
steps. If
p
is a constant, then a random walk of length
L
on such a graph can be seen as
L/(p+2)=O(L)
copies of a random walk of length
p+2
. Observe that if the
(p+1)
-neighborhood of the vertex
u_{\sf start}
has (almost)
L
vertices (and the probability of visiting each vertex is more or less uniform), then a random walk of length
L
is likely to visit all the vertices in the neighborhood and an algorithm that samples a random walk also outputs the entire neighborhood with high probability.

A lower bound for computing the
(p+1)
-neighborhood via pointer-chasing.

Having reduced sampling a random walk to outputting the
(p+1)
-neighborhood, we now need to prove that a space efficient
p
-pass streaming algorithms cannot output the
(p+1)
-neighborhood of
u_{\sf start}
, if this neighborhood has roughly
L
vertices. This is reminiscent of the “
pointer-chasing
” lower bounds found in the literature.

Pointer-chasing results are typically concerned with a graph with
p+1
layers of vertices (
p
layers of edges) and show that given a vertex in the first layer, finding a vertex that is reachable from it in the last layer cannot be done with less than
p
passes, unless the memory is huge. Classical pointer-chasing lower bounds (
e.g.
,
[
NW91
]
), consider graphs where the out-degree of each vertex is
1
, thus the start vertex reaches a unique vertex in the last layer. Unfortunately, this type of pointer-chasing instances are very
sparse
and a streaming algorithm can simply remember the entire graph in one pass using
\widetilde{O}(n)
memory.

Since we wish to have roughly
L
vertices in a
(p+1)
-neighborhood of
u_{\sf start}
, the out-degree of each vertex should be roughly
\Omega(L^{\frac{1}{p+1}})
(assuming uniform degrees). Pointer-chasing lower bounds for this type of
dense
graphs were also proved (
e.g.
,
[
GO16
]
and
[
FKM+09
]
), showing that
p
-pass algorithms essentially need to store an entire layer of edges, which is
\Omega(n\cdot L^{\frac{1}{p+1}})
in our case. However, this still does not give us the
\Omega(n\cdot L^{\frac{1}{p}})
lower bound we aspire for (and which is tight, at least for two passes).

Towards a tight lower bound: combining dense and sparse.

To get a better lower bound, we construct a hard instance that is a combination of the two above mentioned types of pointer-chasing instances, the dense and the sparse. Specifically, for a
p
-pass lower bound, we construct a layered graph with
p+2
layers of vertices
V_{1},\ldots,V_{p+2}
, where the first layer has only one vertex
u_{\sf start}
and all the other layers are of equal size (see
Figure 1
). To ensure that vertex
u_{\sf start}
is reached every
p+2
steps, we connect all vertices in the last layer to
u_{\sf start}
. Every vertex in layers
V_{2},\ldots,V_{p+1}
connects to a random set of roughly
L^{\frac{1}{p}}

2.3
Lower bounds for Oblivious Algorithms

Finally, we discuss the intuitions behind the proof of
Theorem 1.3
, showing that any algorithm that is oblivious to the starting vertex must use
\widetilde{\Omega}(n\cdot\sqrt{L})
space space. Our proof is based on a reduction from a multi-output generalization of the well-studied
\mathsf{INDEX}
problem for one-way communication protocols, denoted by
\mathsf{INDEX}_{m,\ell}
. In
\mathsf{INDEX}_{m,\ell}
, Alice gets
\ell
strings
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and Bob gets an index
i\in[\ell]
. Alice sends a message to Bob and then Bob is required to output the string
X_{i}
. (Note that when
m=1
it becomes the original
\mathsf{INDEX}
problem).

It is not hard to show that any one-way communication protocol solving
\mathsf{INDEX}_{m,\ell}
with non-trivial probability (say,
1/\mathrm{poly}\log(m)
) requires Alice to send at least
\widetilde{\Omega}(m\ell)
bits to Bob (see
Appendix C
).

Our key observation here is that if there is a starting vertex oblivious algorithm
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{S})
with
S
space for approximate simulation of an
L=\widetilde{O}(m)
-step random walk on a graph with
n=O(\sqrt{m}\cdot\ell)
vertices, then it implies a one-way communication protocol for
\mathsf{INDEX}_{m,\ell}
with communication complexity
S
and a decent success probability. Recall the lower bound for
\mathsf{INDEX}_{m,\ell}
, we immediately have
S=\widetilde{\Omega}(m\ell)=\widetilde{\Omega}(n\sqrt{L})
.

In more detail, given an
m
-bit string
X
, we will build an
O(\sqrt{m})
-vertex graph
H(X)
by encoding all bits of
X
as
existence/non-existence
of edges in
H
(this is possible since there are more than
m
potential edges in
H
). We also add some artificial edges to
H
to make sure it is strongly connected. Our construction will make sure that an
L=\widetilde{O}(m)
steps random walk in
H
will reveal all edges in
H
with high probability, which in turn reveals all bits of
X
(see the proof of
Theorem 1.3
for more details).

Now the reduction can be implemented as follows: given
\ell
strings
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, Alice constructs a graph
G=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\ell}H(X_{i})
, as the joint union of
\ell
graphs. Note that
G
has
n=O(\sqrt{m}\cdot\ell)
vertices. Alice then runs the preprocessing algorithm
\mathbb{P}
on
G
to obtain a string
M
, and sends it to Bob. Given an index
i\in[\ell]
, Bob simply runs
\mathbb{S}
with
M
together with a suitable starting vertex inside the
H(X_{i})
component of
G
. By previous discussions, this reveals the string
X_{i}
with high probability and proves the correctness of this reduction. Hence, the space complexity of
\mathbb{A}
must be
\widetilde{\Omega}(m\ell)=\widetilde{\Omega}(n\sqrt{L})
.

3.3
Useful Concentration Bounds on Random Variables

The following standard concentration bounds will be useful for us.

Lemma 3.2

(Multiplicative Chernoff bound,
[
Che52
]
)
.

Suppose
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
are independent random variables taking values in
[0,1]
. Let
X
denote their sum and let
\mu=\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[X]
denote the sum’s expected value. Then,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\geq(1+\delta)\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-{\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2+\delta}}},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\forall 0\leq\delta,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\leq(1-\delta)\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-{\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2}}},

\displaystyle\forall 0\leq\delta\leq 1.

In particular, we have that:

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\geq(1+\delta)\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\delta\mu}{3}\cdot\min(\delta,1)},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\forall 0\leq\delta,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\lvert{X-\mu}\rvert\geq\delta\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-{\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{3}}},

\displaystyle\forall 0\leq\delta\leq 1.

We also need the following Azuma-Hoeffding inequality.

Lemma 3.3

(Azuma-Hoeffding inequality,
[
Azu67
,
Hoe94
]
)
.

Let
Z_{0},\dotsc,Z_{n}
be random variables satisfying (1)
\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[|Z_{i}|]<\infty
for every
i\in\{0,\dotsc,n\}
and
\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[Z_{i}|Z_{0},\dotsc,Z_{i-1}]\leq Z_{i-1}
for every
i\in[n]
(i.e.,
\{Z_{i}\}
forms a supermartingale) and (2) for every
i\in[n]
,
|Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}|\leq 1
, then for all
\lambda>0
, we have

\Pr[Z_{n}-Z_{0}\geq\lambda]\leq\exp(-\lambda^{2}/2n).

In particular, the following corollary will be useful for us.

Corollary 3.4

(Azuma-Hoeffding inequality for Boolean random variables,
[
Azu67
,
Hoe94
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
be random variables satisfying
X_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for each
i\in[n]
. Suppose that
\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[X_{i}|X_{1},\dotsc,X_{i-1}]\leq p_{i}
for all

. Then for any
\lambda>0
,

\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq\lambda+\sum_{i=1}p_{i}\right]\leq\exp(-\lambda^{2}/2n).

Proof.

For

, let

. From the assumption one can see that all the

form a supermartingale and

.
∎

Standard Lemmas

We will need (a weak form of) Stirling’s approximation. We include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
n>0
, we have

\mathrm{e}\cdot\left\lparen\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}\right\rparen^{n}\leq n!\leq\mathrm{e}n\cdot\left\lparen\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}\right\rparen^{n}.

Proof.

We have:

\frac{n^{n}}{n!}=\frac{n^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\lparen\frac{i+1}{i}\right\rparen^{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\lparen 1+\frac{1}{i}\right\rparen^{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{e}=\mathrm{e}^{n-1}.

We also have:

\frac{n!}{n^{n+1}}=\frac{(n-1)!}{n^{n}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\lparen\frac{i}{i+1}\right\rparen^{i+1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{i+1}\right\rparen^{i+1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{-1}=\mathrm{e}^{1-n}.

Rearranging gives the result.
∎

(

(i.e., if a random walk starting from

We also let

Remark 4.2
.

, then the vertex is considered to be both heavy and light.

The following lemma is crucial for the analysis of our algorithm.

Lemma 4.3

(Upper bounds on the total out-degrees of heavy vertices)
.

Given a directed graph
G
with
n
vertices and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

\sum_{u\in V_{\sf heavy}^{\ell}(G)}d_{\sf out}(u)\leq O(n\cdot\ell).

Proof.

We define a set

of pairs of vertices as follows:

\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{(u,v)\in V^{2}:\textsf{visit}_{[0,\ell]}(u,v)\geq 1/6\}.

That is, a pair of vertices
u
and
v
belongs to
\mathcal{S}
if and only if an

-step random walk starting from
u
visits

with probability at least

.

For each fixed vertex
u
, we further define

\mathcal{S}_{u}\coloneqq\left\{v\in N_{\sf out}(u)\mid\textsf{visit}_{[0,\ell]}(v,u)\geq 1/6\right\},

and

\mathcal{H}_{u}\coloneqq\left\{v\in V\mid\textsf{visit}_{[0,\ell]}(u,v)\geq 1/6\right\}.

The following claim will be useful for the proof.

Claim 4.4
.

The following two statements hold:

1.

For every
u\in V
, it holds that
|\mathcal{H}_{u}|\leq O(\ell)
.

2.

For every
u\in V_{\sf heavy}
, it holds that
|\mathcal{S}_{u}|\geq 1/6\cdot d_{\sf out}(u)
.

Proof.

Fixing
u\in V
, the first item follows from the simple fact that

\sum_{v\in V}\textsf{visit}_{[0,\ell]}(u,v)\leq\ell+1.

Now we move to the second item, and fix
u\in V_{\sf heavy}
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
\left|\mathcal{S}_{u}\right|<1/6\cdot d_{\sf out}(u)
. We have

\displaystyle\textsf{visit}_{[1,\ell]}(u,u)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{v\in N_{\sf out}(u)}[\textsf{visit}_{[0,\ell-1]}(v,u)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{v\in N_{\sf out}(u)}[\textsf{visit}_{[0,\ell]}(v,u)]

\displaystyle<\Pr_{v\in N_{\sf out}(u)}[v\in\mathcal{S}_{u}]\cdot 1+\Pr_{v\in N_{\sf out}(u)}[v\notin\mathcal{S}_{u}]\cdot 1/6<1/6+1/6<1/3,

a contradiction to the assumption that

is heavy.
∎

Finally, note that by definition of
\mathcal{H}_{u}
and
\mathcal{S}_{u}
we immediately have

|\mathcal{S}|=\sum_{u\in V}|\mathcal{H}_{u}|\geq\sum_{u\in V}|\mathcal{S}_{u}|.

\sum_{u\in V_{\sf heavy}}d_{\sf out}(u)\leq 6\cdot\sum_{u\in V}|\mathcal{S}_{u}|\leq 6\cdot\sum_{u\in V}|\mathcal{H}_{u}|\leq O\left(n\cdot\ell\right),

which completes the proof.

∎

\beta

\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})
, where
\beta
is the probability that
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,V_{\sf full},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
outputs failure.

Proof.

Conclude from
\tau\cdot(n-|V_{\sf full}|)+\sum_{v\in V_{\sf full}}d_{\sf out}(v)\leq c_{2}\cdot\tau\cdot n
that
\mathbb{P}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(G,\tau,V_{\sf full})
always operates correctly.

To bound the statistical distance between the distribution of
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,V_{\sf full},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
and
\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})
. We construct another random variable
(\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})^{\prime}
, in which for every vertex
u
, we sample another
L
out-neighbors of
u
uniformly at random with replacement, and add them to the end of the list
L^{\sf save}_{v}
in
\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save}
.

Note that
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,V_{\sf full},(\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})^{\prime})
never outputs failure, and distributes exactly the same as
\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})
. On the other hand,
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,V_{\sf full},(\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})^{\prime})
and
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,V_{\sf full},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
are the same as long as
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,V_{\sf full},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
does not output failure, which completes the proof.
∎

The following corollary follows immediately from the lemma above. (Note that this special case exactly corresponds to the folklore one-pass streaming algorithm for simulating random walks.)

Corollary 4.9
.

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices and an integer
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save}
be random variable of the output of
\mathbb{P}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(G,L,\emptyset)
. For every
u_{\sf start}\in V
, the output distribution of
\mathbb{S}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,\emptyset,\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
distributes identically as
\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})
.

Description of the two-pass algorithm.

Now we are ready to describe our two pass algorithm

Algorithm 3

\mathbb{A}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}

\mathbb{P}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(G,L,\delta)

A directed graph

with

, and

Let

4:

The set
V_{\sf full}
and the collection of lists

Analysis of the algorithm.

We first show that with high probability,
\widetilde{V}_{\sf light}
and
\widetilde{V}_{\sf heavy}
are subsets of
V_{\sf light}
and
V_{\sf heavy}
respectively.

Lemma 4.10
.

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices,
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1/n)
, letting
\ell=\sqrt{L}
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
over the internal randomness of
\mathbb{P}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(G,L,\delta)
, it holds that
\widetilde{V}_{\sf light}\subseteq V_{\sf light}
and
\widetilde{V}_{\sf heavy}\subseteq V_{\sf heavy}
.

Proof.

Setting
c_{1}
in
Algorithm 3
to be a large enough constant and applying
Section 4.2
and the Chernoff bound, with probability at least
1-n\cdot\delta^{3}\geq 1-\delta/2
,
|w_{u}-\textsf{visit}_{[1,\ell]}(u,u)|\leq 0.1
for every
u\in V
. The lemma then follows from the definition of heavy and light vertices.
∎

Next, we show that with high probability, a random walk does not visit a light vertex too many times.

Lemma 4.11
.

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices,
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1/n)
, letting
\ell=\sqrt{L}
and
\gamma=c_{1}\cdot\log\delta^{-1}
, where
c_{1}>1
is the sufficiently large constant, for every vertex
u_{\sf start}\in V
and vertex
v\in V_{\sf light}^{\ell}(G)
, an
L
-step random walk starting from
u_{\sf start}
visits
v
more than
\gamma\cdot\ell
times with probability at most
\delta/2n
.

Proof.

Suppose we have an infinite random walk
\mathsf{W}
starting from
u_{\sf start}
in
G
. Letting
\tau=\gamma\ell
, the goal here is to bound the probability that during the first
L
steps,
\mathsf{W}
visits
v
more than
\tau
times. We denote this as the bad event
\mathcal{E}_{\sf bad}
.

Let
\mathsf{Z}_{i}
be the random variable representing the step at which
\mathsf{W}
visits
v
for the
i
-th time (if
\mathsf{W}
visits
v
less than
i
times in total, we let
\mathsf{Z}_{i}=\infty
).
\mathcal{E}_{\sf bad}
is equivalent to that
\mathsf{Z}_{\tau+1}\leq L
.

\mathsf{Z}_{\tau+1}\leq L
further implies that for at least
(\tau-\ell)

i\in[\tau]
,
\mathsf{Z}_{i+1}-\mathsf{Z}_{i}\leq\ell
and
\mathsf{Z}_{i}<\infty
. In the following we denote this event as
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and bounds its probability instead.

For each
i\in[\tau]
, let
\mathsf{Y}_{i}
be the random variable which takes value
1
if both
Z_{i}<\infty
and
\mathsf{Z}_{i+1}-\mathsf{Z}_{i}\leq\ell
hold, and
0
otherwise. Letting
\mathsf{Y}_{<i}=(\mathsf{Y}_{1},\dotsc,\mathsf{Y}_{i-1})
, the following claim is crucial for us.

Claim 4.12
.

For every
i\in[\tau]
and every possible assignments
Y_{<i}\in\{0,1\}^{i-1}
, we have

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}_{i}|\mathsf{Y}_{<i}=Y_{<i}]\leq 2/3.

Proof.

By the Markov property of the random walk, and noting that
\mathsf{Y}_{i}
is always
0
when
\mathsf{Z}_{i}=\infty
, we have.

\displaystyle\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}_{i}|\mathsf{Y}_{<i}=Y_{<i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Pr[\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j|\mathsf{Y}_{<i}=Y_{<i}]\cdot\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}_{i}|\mathsf{Y}_{<i}=Y_{<i},\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Pr[\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j|\mathsf{Y}_{<i}=Y_{<i}]\cdot\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}_{i}|\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j].

To further bound the quantity above, recall that the event
\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j
means that the random walk
\mathsf{W}
starting from
u_{\sf start}
visits the light vertex
v
for the
i
-th time at
\mathsf{W}
’s
j
-th step, and we have

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{Y}_{i}|\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j]=\Pr[\mathsf{Y}_{i}=1|\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j]=\Pr[\mathsf{Z}_{i+1}\leq j+\ell|\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j].

By the Markov property of the random walk
\mathsf{W}
,
\Pr[\mathsf{Z}_{i+1}\leq j+\ell|\mathsf{Z}_{i}=j]
equals the probability that a random walk starting from
v
revisits
v
in at most
\ell
steps. By the definition of light vertices, we can bound that by
2/3
, which completes the proof.

∎

Then by the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (
Section 3.3
),

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathsf{W}}[\mathcal{E}_{\sf bad}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{\mathsf{W}}[\mathcal{E}_{1}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\mathsf{W}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathsf{Y}_{i}\geq(\tau-\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\Omega(\tau-\ell-2/3\cdot\tau))\leq\delta/2n,

the last inequality follows from the fact that
\gamma=c_{1}\cdot\log\delta^{-1}
for a sufficiently large constant
c_{1}
.

∎

The correctness of the algorithm is finally completed by the following theorem.

Theorem 4.13

(Formal version of
Theorem 1.1
)
.

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices,
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1/n)
. Let
\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save}
and
\mathsf{V}_{\sf full}
be the two random variables of the output of
\mathbb{P}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(G,L,\delta)
. For every
u_{\sf start}\in V
, the following hold:

•

The output distribution of
\mathbb{S}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,\mathsf{V}_{\sf full},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
has statistical distance at most
\delta
from
\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})
.

•

Both of
\mathbb{P}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(G,L,\delta)
and
\mathbb{S}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,\mathsf{V}_{\sf full},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
use at most
O(n\cdot\sqrt{L}\cdot\log\delta^{-1})
words of space.

Proof.

Note that we can safely assume
L\leq n^{2}
, since otherwise one can always use
O(n^{2})
words to store all the edges in the graph. In this case, we have that
L\leq n\cdot\sqrt{L}
and the space for restoring the
L
-step output walk can be ignored.

Let
\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf heavy}=\mathsf{V}_{\sf full}
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf light}=V\setminus\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf heavy}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{\sf good}
be the event that
\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf light}\subseteq V_{\sf light}
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf heavy}\subseteq V_{\sf heavy}
. By
Section 4.3
, we have that
\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\sf good}]\geq 1-\delta/2
.

Now we condition on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\sf good}
. In this case, it follows from
Section 4.1
that
\mathbb{P}_{\sf one\text{-}pass}(G,\gamma\cdot\ell,\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf heavy})
operates correctly (by setting the constant
c_{2}
in
Algorithm 1
to be sufficiently large).

By
Section 4.3
and a union bound, the probability of
\mathbb{S}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf heavy},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
outputs failure is at most
\delta/2
. By
Section 4.2
, it follows that the statistical distance between the output distribution of
\mathbb{S}_{\sf two\text{-}pass}(V,u_{\sf start},L,\widetilde{\mathsf{V}}_{\sf heavy},\vec{\mathsf{L}}^{\sf save})
and
\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})
is at most
\delta/2
.

The theorem follows by combing the above with the fact that
\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\sf good}]\geq 1-\delta/2
.

∎

Similar to the algorithm in

Uniformly sampling via
\ell_{1}

For light vertices, uniformly sampling some out-neighbors from each vertex without replacement can be implemented via the following

Lemma 4.14

(

[

]
)
.

Let

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose there exists a constant
\beta\in(0,1]
, an integer
p\geq 1

that are sufficiently large and satisfy

such that there exists a (randomized)

\mathcal{A}
that takes space
\frac{n\cdot L^{1/p}}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{10^{20p}}}

n

G=(V,E)

, can sample from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that
\lVert\mathcal{D}-\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(u_{\sf start})\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{10}}

Then, there exists another randomized
p

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
that takes space
\frac{n\cdot L^{1/p}}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{10^{15p}}}

Let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be the algorithm that first runs
\mathcal{A}
on its input and
s
to get as output a walk
W=(v_{0},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{L})

E(W)=\left\{(v_{i-1},v_{i})\mid i\in[L]\right\}
to be the set of edges witnessed by
W
. The algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
then outputs all paths
P^{p+1}(s,W)
of length
p+1
starting for
s
using only the edges

.

Let

W

E(W)\not\subseteq E
or
P(N^{\leq p}(s))\not\subseteq E(W)
. Thus, we have, for all
G\in\supp(\mathcal{D}_{n,p})
that:

\displaystyle\Pr\left\lparen\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\left\lparen G\right\rparen\neq P^{p+1}(s)\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\Pr_{W\sim\mathcal{A}(G)}\left\lparen P^{p+1}(s,W)\neq P^{p+1}(s)\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(s)}\left\lparen P^{p+1}(s,W)\neq P^{p+1}(s)\right\rparen+1-\frac{1}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{10}}

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(s)}\left\lparen E(W)\not\subseteq E\vee P(N^{\leq p}(s))\not\subseteq E(W)\right\rparen+1-\frac{1}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{10}}.

(Union bound)

As

\Pr\left\lparen\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\left\lparen G\right\rparen\neq P^{p+1}(s)\right\rparen\leq\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(s)}\left\lparen P(N^{\leq p}(s))\not\subseteq E(W)\right\rparen+1-\frac{1}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{10}}.

Thus, to finish the proof, it suffices to show that
\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(s)}\left\lparen P(N^{\leq p}(s))\not\subseteq E(W)\right\rparen\leq\frac{1}{\left\lparen\log n\right\rparen^{20}}
. This is done in the rest of the proof. First, observe from the definition of

that

is a collection of at most

edges. We get by a union bound:

\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(s)}\left\lparen P(N^{\leq p}(s))\not\subseteq E(W)\right\rparen\leq L\cdot\max_{e\in P(N^{\leq p}(s))}\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}^{G}_{L}(s)}\left\lparen e\notin E(W)\right\rparen.

(3)

Fix

and observe that

Appendix A
Preliminaries in Information Theory

Throughout this subsection, we use sans-serif letters to denote random variables and reserve
E
to denote an arbitrary event. All random variables will be assumed to be discrete and we shall adopt the convention
0\log\frac{1}{0}=0
. All logarithms are taken with base
2
.

A.1
Entropy

Definition A.1

(Entropy)
.

The (binary) entropy of
\mathsf{X}
is defined as:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})=\sum_{x\in\supp(\mathsf{X})}\Pr(x)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x)}.

The entropy of
\mathsf{X}
conditioned on
E
is defined as:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E)=\sum_{x\in\supp(\mathsf{X})}\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x\mid E)}.

Definition A.2

(Conditional Entropy)
.

We define the conditional entropy of
\mathsf{X}
given
\mathsf{Y}
and
E
as:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y},E)=\sum_{y\in\supp(\mathsf{Y})}\Pr(y\mid E)\cdot\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y}=y,E).

Henceforth, we shall omit writing the
\supp(\cdot)
when it is clear from context.

Lemma A.3

(Chain Rule for Entropy)
.

It holds for all
\mathsf{X}
,
\mathsf{Y}
and
E
that:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\mid E)=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E)+\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X},E).

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\mid E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\Pr(x,y\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x,y\mid E)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\Pr(x,y\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\Pr(y\mid x,E)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\Pr(x,y\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x\mid E)}+\sum_{x,y}\Pr(x,y\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(y\mid x,E)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E)+\sum_{x}\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\sum_{y}\Pr(y\mid x,E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(y\mid x,E)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E)+\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X},E).\qed

Lemma A.4

(Conditioning reduces Entropy)
.

It holds for all
\mathsf{X}
,
\mathsf{Y}
and
E
that:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y},E)\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E).

Equality holds if and only if
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
are independent conditioned on
E
.

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y},E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\Pr(y\mid E)\cdot\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y}=y,E)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\Pr(y\mid E)\cdot\Pr(x\mid y,E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x\mid y,E)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\Pr(y\mid x,E)\cdot\log\frac{\Pr(y\mid E)}{\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\Pr(y\mid x,E)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x\mid E)}

(Concavity of
\log(\cdot)
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E).\qed

Lemma A.5
.

It holds for all
\mathsf{X}
and
E
that:

0\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E)\leq\log\left\lparen\left\lvert\supp(\mathsf{X})\right\rvert\right\rparen.

The second inequality is tight if and only if
\mathsf{X}
conditioned on
E
is the uniform distribution over
\supp(\mathsf{X})
.

Proof.

The first inequality is direct. For the second, we have by the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
that:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid E)=\sum_{x}\Pr(x\mid E)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x\mid E)}\leq\log\left\lparen\left\lvert\supp(\mathsf{X})\right\rvert\right\rparen.\qed

A.2
Min-Entropy

Definition A.6

(Min-Entropy)
.

The min-entropy of a discrete random variable
\mathsf{X}
is

\mathbb{H}_{\infty}(\mathsf{X})=\min_{x:\Pr(x)>0}\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x)}.

Fact A.7
.

If the random variable
\mathsf{X}
takes values in the set
\Omega
, it holds that

0\leq\mathbb{H}_{\infty}(\mathsf{X})\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})\leq\log\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert

Recall that
\mathsf{h}(x)=-x\log(x)-(1-x)\log(1-x)
for
x\in[0,1]
is the binary entropy function.

Lemma A.8
.

If the random variable
\mathsf{X}
takes values in the set
\Omega
and
\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert>1
, it holds that

2^{-\mathbb{H}_{\infty}(\mathsf{X})}\leq 1-\frac{\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})-1}{\log\left\lparen\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert\right\rparen}.

Proof.

If
\mathsf{X}
is a point mass, there is nothing to show. Otherwise, let
x^{*}
be such that
\Pr(x^{*})
is the largest possible (breaking ties arbitrarily). We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})

\displaystyle=\Pr(x^{*})\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x^{*})}+\sum_{x\neq x^{*}}\Pr(x)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{h}(\Pr(x^{*}))-\left\lparen 1-\Pr(x^{*})\right\rparen\cdot\log\frac{1}{1-\Pr(x^{*})}+\sum_{x\neq x^{*}}\Pr(x)\cdot\log\frac{1}{\Pr(x)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{h}(\Pr(x^{*}))+\left\lparen 1-\Pr(x^{*})\right\rparen\cdot\sum_{x\neq x^{*}}\frac{\Pr(x)}{1-\Pr(x^{*})}\cdot\log\frac{1-\Pr(x^{*})}{\Pr(x)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{h}(\Pr(x^{*}))+\left\lparen 1-\Pr(x^{*})\right\rparen\cdot\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{X}\neq x^{*}).

Using the fact that
\mathsf{h}(\cdot)\leq 1
and
Section A.2
, we have:

\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})\leq 1+\left\lparen 1-\Pr(x^{*})\right\rparen\cdot\log\left\lparen\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert\right\rparen.

Rearranging gives:

2^{-\mathbb{H}_{\infty}(\mathsf{X})}=\Pr(x^{*})\leq 1-\frac{\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})-1}{\log\left\lparen\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert\right\rparen}.

∎

A.3
Mutual Information

Definition A.9

(Mutual Information)
.

The mutual information between
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
is defined as:

\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{X}:\mathsf{Y})=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y})=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{Y})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}).

The mutual information between
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
conditioned on
\mathsf{Z}
is defined as:

\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{X}:\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{Z})=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Z})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X}\mid\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{Z})=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{Z})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Z}).

Fact A.10
.

We have
0\leq\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{X}:\mathsf{Y}\mid\mathsf{Z})\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{X})
.

Fact A.11

(Chain Rule for Mutual Information)
.

If
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{B}
,
\mathsf{C}
,
\mathsf{D}
are random variables, then

\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}:\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{D})+\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{B}:\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{A}\mathsf{D}).

The following lemmas are standard.

Lemma A.12
.

For random variables
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}
, if
\mathsf{A}
is independent of
\mathsf{D}
given
\mathsf{C}
, then,

\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C}\right\rparen\leq\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}\right\rparen.

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
are independent conditioned on
\mathsf{C}
, by

Section A.1
,
\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C})=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})
and
\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C})
. We have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})=\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}\right\rparen.

∎

Lemma A.13
.

For random variables
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}
, if
\mathsf{A}
is independent of
\mathsf{D}
given
\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C}
, then,

\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}\right\rparen\leq\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C}\right\rparen.

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{D}
are independent conditioned on
\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C}
, by
Section A.1
,
\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C})=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})
. Moreover, since conditioning can only reduce the entropy (again by
Section A.1
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{C})-\mathbb{H}(\mathsf{A}\mid\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C})=\mathbb{I}\left\lparen\mathsf{A}:\mathsf{B}\mid\mathsf{C}\right\rparen.

∎

Lemma C.1

passes an edge from
V_{1,i}
to
V_{2,j}
.

5.

Bob outputs
X_{\sf rec}(W)
.

We first show that with high probability, an
L
-step random walk starting from the starting vertex in
H_{\tau}(X_{i})
determines the string
X_{i}
. Formally, The following claim captures what we need.

Claim C.5
.

For every
X\in\{0,1\}^{\tau^{2}}
, letting
u_{\sf start}
be the starting vertex of
H_{\tau}(X)
, it holds that

\Pr_{W\sim\mathsf{RW}_{L}^{H_{\tau}(X)}(u_{\sf start})}[X_{\sf rec}(W)=X]\geq 1-\exp(-\log^{20}n).

Proof.

We will first bound the probability that
X_{\sf rec}(W)_{i,j}\neq X_{i,j}
for each
(i,j)\in[\tau]^{2}
and then apply a union bound. Fix
(i,j)\in[\tau]^{2}
, if
X_{i,j}=0
, since there is no edge from
V_{1,i}
to
V_{2,j}
, clearly
X_{\sf rec}(W)_{i,j}
is always
0
as well. Hence we only need to consider the case that
X_{i,j}=1
.

In this case, one can observe that for every two steps, the random walk visits the edge between
V_{1,i}
to
V_{2,j}
with probability at least
1/(\tau+1)^{2}
. And moreover, all these events are independent.

Hence, a random walk with
L=\tau^{2}\cdot\log^{40}n
steps visits the edge between
V_{1,i}
to
V_{2,j}
with probability

1-(1-1/(\tau+1)^{2})^{L/2}\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(1/\tau^{2}\cdot L))\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{40}n)).

The claim then follows from a union bound.
∎

\Pi
also has communication complexity less than
m\ell/\log^{20}m
, and success probability at least
1/\log^{10}n-\exp(-\log^{20}n)
, which contradicts
