Performance Bounds for PDE-Constrained Optimization under Uncertainty Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Peng Chen, Johannes O. Royset

Abstract
Computational approaches to PDE-constrained optimization under uncertainty may involve finite-dimensional approximations of control and state spaces, sample average approximations of measures of risk and reliability, smooth approximations of nonsmooth functions, penalty approximations of constraints as well as many other kinds of inaccuracies. In this paper, we analyze the performance of controls obtained by an approximation-based algorithm and in the process develop estimates of optimality gaps for general optimization problems defined on metric spaces. Under mild assumptions, we establish that limiting controls have arbitrarily small optimality gaps provided that the inaccuracies in the various approximations vanish. We carry out the analysis for a broad class of problems with multiple expectation, risk, and reliability functions involving PDE solutions and appearing in objective as well as constraint expressions. In particular, we address problems with buffered failure probability constraints approximated via an augmented Lagrangian. We demonstrate the framework on an elliptic PDE with a random coefficient field and a distributed control function.



keywords

PDE-constrained optimization, stochastic optimization, uncertainty quantification, performance bounds, sample average approximation, smooth approximation, buffered probability

Problems of this kind usually come with significant uncertainty in the form of unknown external loadings, material coefficients, boundary or initial conditions, geometries, and other factors, which leads to PDE-constrained optimization problems under uncertainty. Formulations of these problems leverage scalarizations of the random quantities of interest via expectations, variances

, superquantiles (a.k.a. conditional/average value-at-risk)

These formulations tend to involve integration with respect to a probability measure, which needs to be approximated by numerical integration, such as Monte Carlo and quasi Monte Carlo techniques
[

,

,
15
]
, sparse grid quadrature methods
[
10
]
, or variance-reduction techniques based on Taylor expansions
[
11
]
. These approximations compound the already significant challenges associated with discretization and solution of the underlying PDE. Techniques to mitigate the overall computational cost include those based on model reduction
[
6
,
24
]
, multilevel
[

,
2

[

[

[

[

[

In this paper, we analyze a multitude of approximations in PDE-constrained optimization under uncertainty. We focus on a broad class of problems involving several quantities of interest and, thus, several expectation, risk, and reliability functions. These quantities may arise in an objective function, as constraint functions, or both.

In addition to discretization of state and control spaces and numerical evaluation of integrals, the broad class of problems gives rise to several other approximations. Smooth functions approximate nonsmooth ones to facilitate the use of gradient-type algorithms. Penalty and Lagrangian terms model constraints. Weights aggregate multiple objective functions. The combined effect of this multitude of approximations on the obtained solutions is nontrivial and increasingly complex as we move towards more sophisticated optimization problems involving novel performance criteria and numerous quantities of interest. We lay out conditions under which small errors in the various approximations indeed lead to a guaranteed good performance of a control computed using these approximations. This does not hold in general. It is well known that approximating functions may converge pointwise to an actual function but have minimizers and minima far from those of the actual problem
[
38
, Figure 4.5]
. More concretely, a convergent Runga-Kutta method employed to solve an ordinary differential equation might still induce errors that prevent minimizers of the approximating optimization problems from converging to a minimizer of the actual problem
[
40
,
16
]
. We present a unified framework that addresses a wide array of approximations and applications. It stretches beyond PDE-constrained optimization, but also specializes to an example with a buffered failure probability constraint and to problems defined in terms of an elliptic PDE with a random coefficient field and a distributed control function.

The various inaccuracies lead to approximating optimization problems that subsequently need to be solved by optimization algorithms. Since these problems are rarely convex, one cannot expect their minimizers to be within reach. We furnish performance bounds and optimality gaps for
any
solution—local, global, or neither—obtained from the approximating problems. Still, better solutions of the approximating problems naturally translate into tighter bounds.

The reasoning towards performance bounds and optimality gaps follows a novel breakdown of errors into those caused by discretization of the control space on one side and those produced by all other approximations on the other side. This division is motivated by the fact that the former imposes a restriction on the problem while the latter can swing either way. As a result, we often analyze the “other approximations” in a setting of finite dimensions and this reduces the required assumptions. We omit an analysis of optimality conditions and the assumptions required to ensure convergence of stationary points of approximating problems to those of the actual problem; see for example
[
29
,
21
]
for efforts in this direction.
Our results provide the foundation for numerous algorithms, including those involving adaptive refinements. However, we defer detailed algorithms as well as numerical demonstrations to future publications.

Our main technical tool is epi-convergence; see for example
[
36
, Chapter 7]
,
[
38
, Section 4.C]
, and references therein. It is well known that epi-convergence of approximating functions ensures that the corresponding minimizers can only converge to a minimizer of the limiting function. However, epi-convergence can be difficulty to verify or might simply not hold for realistic problems involving a multitude of approximations. Many studies concentrate on a single source of approximation;
[
22
]
deals with nonsmoothness and
[
12
,
4
]
with sampling. The challenge of combining discretization of an underlying space with sample averages is illustrated by the less-than-ideal convergence results of Theorem 3.14 in
[
37
]
, which anyhow fails to address other approximations and expectation constraints. The difficulties compound for PDE-constrained optimization problems because the approximating problems solved by numerical algorithms are defined on finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces while the actual problem resides in infinite dimensions.

Problem Formulations

We consider the stochastic optimization problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{z\in Z}~\varphi(z)=\iota_{A}(z)+f(z)+h\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[G(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z)\big]\Big),

(2.1)

referred to as the
actual problem
, where the
control

z
resides in a separable Banach space
(Z,\|\cdot\|_{Z})
and is restricted to an
admissible set

A\subset Z
as expressed by the
indicator function
; for any set
C
,
\iota_{C}(c)=0
if
c\in C
and
\iota_{C}(c)=\infty
otherwise. The
objective function

\varphi:Z\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}=[-\infty,\infty]
is further given by a
cost function

f:Z\to\mathbb{R}
. Main complications stem from the last term in the objective function, which is defined by a
random field

\xi
, an
m

quantities of interest

G(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z)
, and a
monitoring function

h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. The monitoring function assesses the various quantities of interest, which in turn depend implicity on the solution of a
PDE
defined on a domain

and parametrized by
z\in Z
and realizations of
\xi

Formally, let

be a probability space and

\sigma

P(B)=\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega~|~\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}(\omega)\in B\})
for
B\in{\cal B}
. Without imposing any practical limitation, we let this probability space be complete.

We assume that the solutions of the PDE lie in a separable Banach space
(U,\|\cdot\|_{U})
and, for given
\xi\in\Xi
and
z\in Z
, the PDE has a unique solution in
U
denoted by
s(\xi,z)
. Section
5
furnishes details about the existence of such solutions. For the majority of the development, it suffices to recognize that the solutions of the PDE for various
\xi
and
z
are given by a
solution mapping

s:\Xi\times Z\to U
.

The quantities of interest are defined by the
performance functions

g_{i}:U\times Z\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, and the solution mapping
s
:

G:\Xi\times Z\to\mathbb{R}^{m},~~\mbox{ with }G(\xi,z)=\Big(g_{1}\big(s(\xi,z),z\big),\dots,g_{m}\big(s(\xi,z),z\big)\Big).

(2.2)

For any
z\in Z
, we write
\mathbb{E}\big[G(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z)\big]=\int G(\xi,z)dP(\xi)
, which is assumed to be well-defined and finite as discussed below.

We adopt the usual rules for extended arithmetic. In particular,
\infty-\infty=\infty
; see
[
38
, Section 1.D]
. Thus,
z\not\in A
and/or
h(\mathbb{E}[G(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z)])=\infty
produce

, which implies that

\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits\varphi=\{z\in Z~|~\varphi(z)=\inf\varphi<\infty\}

\inf\varphi=\inf\{\varphi(z)~|~z\in Z\}

.

2.1
Illustrative Examples

The actual problem (
2.1
) addresses a vast array of applications. We highlight some possibilities.

Example 1

(expectation minimization with regularization).
Suppose that we seek to determine a control
z\in A\subset Z=L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{q})
, the space of Lebesgue square-integrable functions from
D
to
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, that minimizes the expected system performance as quantified by
g_{1}:U\times Z\to\mathbb{R}
. This leads to the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{z\in A}~\mathbb{E}\Big[g_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z),z\big)\Big]+\theta\|z\|^{2}_{Z},

where a regularization term is included with
\theta\in[0,\infty)
. The problem is of the form (
2.1
): set
m=1
,
h(w)=w
and
f(z)=\theta\|z\|_{Z}^{2}
.

Example 2

(tracking objective under expectation constraint).
While using the same admissible controls as in Example
1
, suppose that we seek to bring the random state
s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z)
as close as possible to a target state
\bar{u}\in U
on average and force a performance function
g_{1}:U\times L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{q})\to\mathbb{R}
to lie between
\alpha
and
\beta
on average. This leads to the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{z\in A}~\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z)-\bar{u}\big\|_{U}\Big]~\mbox{ subject to }~\alpha\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[g_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z),z\big)\Big]\leq\beta,

which is of the form (
2.1
) with
m=2
,
h(w)=\iota_{[\alpha,\beta]}(w_{1})+w_{2}
,
f(z)=0
, and
g_{2}(u,z)=\|u-\bar{u}\|_{U}
. The bounds
\alpha\leq\beta
could coincide to produce an equality constraint or they might have
\alpha=-\infty
and
\beta=0
to produce the inequality
\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},z),z)]\leq 0
.

Example 3

(risk modeling).
Modeling focused on “worst-case” performance instead of “average” performance is accomplished using a regular measure of risk that maps random variables with finite second moments into
\overline{\mathbb{R}}

[
31
,
38
, Chapter 8]
. Let
{\cal R}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, be a collection of regular measures of risk. Suppose that we seek a control
\hat{z}\in\hat{A}\subset\hat{Z}
that minimizes the risk associated with a performance function
\hat{g}_{1}:U\times\hat{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
and satisfies constraints of nonnegative risk related to
\hat{g}_{i}:U\times\hat{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=2,3,\dots,m
. This leads to the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{\hat{z}\in\hat{A}}~{\cal R}_{1}\Big(\hat{g}_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)\Big)~\mbox{ subject to }~{\cal R}_{i}\Big(\hat{g}_{i}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)\Big)\leq 0,~~i=2,3,\dots,m,

which turns out to be expressible in the form (
2.1
) for common measures of risk.

Detail.
By
[
38
, Thm. 8.9]
, every regular measure of risk
{\cal R}
can be expressed as
{\cal R}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})=\min_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\gamma+{\cal V}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-\gamma)
for some regular measure of regret
{\cal V}
, which also maps random variables with finite second moments into
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus, the problem is equivalently stated as a minimization problem over
\hat{z}
and auxiliary variables
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}
using regular measures of regret
{\cal V}_{1},\dots,{\cal V}_{m}
corresponding to
{\cal R}_{1},\dots,{\cal R}_{m}
, respectively. Many common measures of regret
{\cal V}
are of the expectation kind, which means that for some
convex function
v:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
one has
{\cal V}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})=\mathbb{E}[v(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})]
for random variables
\eta
; see
[
33
]
and
[
38
, Chapter 8]
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the
penalty regret

{\cal V}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})=\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\}]/(1-\alpha)
is a prominent example producing the superquantile risk (a.k.a. CVaR and AVaR)
[
32
]
. If
{\cal V}_{1},\dots,{\cal V}_{m}
are of the expectation kind and expressible using the convex functions

, then the problem takes the equivalent form

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm minimize}_{\hat{z}\in\hat{A},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}}~\mathbb{E}\Big[\gamma_{1}+v_{1}\Big(\hat{g}_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)-\gamma_{1}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle~~\mbox{ subject to }~~\mathbb{E}\Big[\gamma_{i}+v_{i}\Big(\hat{g}_{i}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)-\gamma_{i}\Big)\Big]\leq 0,~~~i=2,3,\dots,m,

which indeed is of the form (
2.1
) with control space
Z=\hat{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, set of admissible controls
A=\hat{A}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, performance functions

g_{i}(u,z)=\gamma_{i}+v_{i}\big(\hat{g}_{i}(u,\hat{z})-\gamma_{i}\big),~i=1,\dots,m,~~\mbox{ for }~~z=(\hat{z},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m})

and monitoring function
h(w)=w_{1}+\iota_{(-\infty,0]}(w_{2})+\cdots+\iota_{(-\infty,0]}(w_{m})
.

Example 4

(buffered failure probability constraint).
A reliability constraint can beneficially be expressed in terms of a buffered failure probability
[
34
]
, which is better behaved mathematically than a failure probability
[
25
,
5
]
. For a random variable
\eta
with finite mean, the buffered failure probability is defined as

\bprob\{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}>0\}=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }~{\mathop{\rm prob}\nolimits}\{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}>0\}=0\\
1-\alpha&\mbox{ if }~{\mathop{\rm prob}\nolimits}\{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}>0\}>0~\mbox{ and }~\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}]<0\\
1&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the probability that makes the
\alpha
-superquantile
\bar{Q}_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})
of
\eta
equal to zero. As expressed by the
\alpha
-quantile
Q_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})
of
\eta
, the
\alpha
-superquantile is defined as
[
38
, Section 3.C]
:

\bar{Q}_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})=Q_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\Big[\max\big\{0,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-Q_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})\big\}\Big].

The problem of determining a control
\hat{z}\in\hat{A}\subset\hat{Z}
that minimizes a cost function
\hat{f}:\hat{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
plus the average value of a quantity of interest represented by
\hat{g}_{1}:U\times\hat{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
and produces sufficient reliability relative to another quantity of interest expressed by
\hat{g}_{2}:U\times\hat{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is formulated as

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm minimize}_{\hat{z}\in\hat{A}}\hat{f}(\hat{z})+\mathbb{E}\Big[\hat{g}_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)\Big]\\
&\mbox{ subject to }\bprob\Big\{\hat{g}_{2}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)>0\Big\}\leq 1-\alpha.\end{split}

(2.3)

The problem can be stated in the form (
2.1
).

Detail.
For
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\bar{Q}_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})\leq 0
if and only if
\bprob\{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}>0\}\leq 1-\alpha

[
38
, Section 3.E]
. This fact together with the discussion in Example
3
about the penalty regret imply that (
2.3
) is equivalent to

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm minimize}_{\hat{z}\in\hat{A},\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(\hat{z})+\mathbb{E}\Big[\hat{g}_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)\Big]\\
&\mbox{ subject to }~~\mathbb{E}\bigg[\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\max\Big\{0,\hat{g}_{2}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)-\gamma\Big\}\bigg]\leq 0.\end{split}

(2.4)

This problem is of the form (
2.1
) with
Z=\hat{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
,
A=\hat{A}\times\mathbb{R}
,
h(w_{1},w_{2})=w_{1}+\iota_{(-\infty,0]}(w_{2})
and, for
z=(\hat{z},\gamma)
,
f(z)=\hat{f}(\hat{z})
,
g_{1}(u,z)=\hat{g}_{1}(u,\hat{z})
, and
g_{2}(u,z)=\gamma+\max\{0,\hat{g}_{2}(u,\hat{z})-\gamma\}/(1-\alpha)
.

2.2
Approximations

The examples illustrate the breadth of situations addressed by (
2.1
), but also the significant challenges associated with the development and justification of computational procedures. In addition to the need for discretization of the control space
Z
and numerical solution of the underlying PDE,
m
expectations must be estimated and potentially nonsmooth performance functions might emerge from reformulations of risk measures (see Examples
3
and
4
) causing the need for smoothing. Nonlinear constraints commonly require approximations via augmented Lagrangian and penalty formulations. These considerations produce approximations of (
2.1
), where
Z
is replaced by a finite-dimensional subspace, the monitoring function
h
is replaced by smooth alternatives, expectations are replaced by sample average approximations and the quantities of interest, as summarized by
G
, are replaced by approximations capturing numerical solutions of the underlying PDE, and also smooth approximations necessitated by nonsmooth performance functions.

In the setting of Example
4
, one might face five types of approximations: A finite-dimensional subspace
\hat{Z}^{n}
restricts
\hat{Z}
, a sample
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{\nu}\}
furnishes an estimate of expectations, an approximating solution mapping
s^{\nu}
replaces
s
, an augmented Lagrangian term with parameter
\theta^{\nu}\in[0,\infty)
and multiplier
y^{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}
substitute for the constraint, and
\mathop{\rm smax}\nolimits(\gamma;\beta^{\nu})=\beta^{\nu}\ln(1+\exp(\gamma/\beta^{\nu}))
approximates the max-function
\gamma\mapsto\max\{0,\gamma\}
with an error of at most
2\beta^{\nu}
using a tunable parameter
\beta^{\nu}\in(0,\infty)
; see
[
38
, Ex. 4.16]
.

Before implementing these approximations, we reformulate (
2.4
) using a nonnegative slack variable
\sigma
:

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm minimize}_{\hat{z}\in\hat{A},\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\sigma\geq 0}\hat{f}(\hat{z})+\mathbb{E}\Big[\hat{g}_{1}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)\Big]\\
&\mbox{ subject to }~~\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sigma+\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\max\Big\{0,\hat{g}_{2}\big(s(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)-\gamma\Big\}\bigg]=0.\end{split}

(2.5)

The problem is of the form (
2.1
) with
Z=\hat{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
A=\hat{A}\times\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
,
h(w_{1},w_{2})=w_{1}+\iota_{\{0\}}(w_{2})
and, for
z=(\hat{z},\gamma,\sigma)
, one has
f(z)=\hat{f}(\hat{z})
,
g_{1}(u,z)=\hat{g}_{1}(u,\hat{z})
and

g_{2}(u,z)=\sigma+\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\max\big\{0,\hat{g}_{2}(u,\hat{z})-\gamma\big\}.

(2.6)

An approximating problem for (
2.5
) is then

\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm minimize}_{\hat{z}\in\hat{A}\cap\hat{Z}^{n},\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\sigma\geq 0}\hat{f}(\hat{z})+\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\hat{g}_{1}\big(s^{\nu}(\xi_{j},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)\\
&~~~~~~~~~+\frac{y^{\nu}}{\nu}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\sigma+\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathop{\rm smax}\nolimits\Big(\hat{g}_{2}\big(s^{\nu}(\xi_{j},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)-\gamma;~\beta^{\nu}\Big)\\
&~~~~~~~~~+\theta^{\nu}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\sigma+\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathop{\rm smax}\nolimits\Big(\hat{g}_{2}\big(s^{\nu}(\xi_{j},\hat{z}),\hat{z}\big)-\gamma;~\beta^{\nu}\Big)\Bigg)^{2},\end{split}

(2.7)

where the objective function being minimized is an augmented Lagrangian
[
38
, Ex. 6.10]
. Presumably, the approximating problem does not involve any difficult constraints, has a smooth objective function in many practical settings and can be addressed using standard nonlinear programming solvers.

One could hope that solutions of the approximating problem produce reasonable solutions for (
2.3
) when the subspace
\hat{Z}^{n}
is sufficiently close to
\hat{Z}
, the sample size
\nu
is large, the numerical solution mapping
s^{\nu}
approximates

\theta^{\nu}
is high, the multiplier
y^{\nu}
is suitably selected, and the smoothing parameter
\beta^{\nu}
is near zero. Of course, this cannot be expected in general. The next sections examine performance bounds for such approximations in the concrete setting of (
2.5
) and also more broadly.

3
General Approximation Theory

To prepare a foundation for a unified treatment of the actual problem (
2.1
), we approach an abstract optimization problem and its approximations. Suppose that
(X,d_{X})
is a metric space
1
1

1

The metric space setting here hints to the possibility of extending the reach of later results beyond Banach spaces.
and let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
. For
f:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, consider the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in X}f(x)

(3.1)

and the discretized problems

\Big\{\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in X}f(x)+\iota_{X^{n}}(x),~~n\in\mathbb{N}\Big\},

(3.2)

where
X^{n}\subset X
corresponds to a finite-dimensional space. Specifically, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume there is a surjective mapping
T_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X^{n}
, i.e., for every
x\in X^{n}
, one can identify a vector
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
x=T_{n}(x_{n})
. Through this correspondence, we identify the finite-dimensional problems

\Big\{\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{n}(x_{n}),~~n\in\mathbb{N}\Big\},

(3.3)

where
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

f_{n}(x_{n})=f\big(T_{n}(x_{n})\big).

(3.4)

We see that (
3.2
) and (
3.3

\inf\big\{f_{n}(x_{n})~\big|~x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\big\}=\inf\big\{f(x)+\iota_{X^{n}}(x)~\big|~x\in X\big\}.

(3.5)

Although finite dimensional, (
3.3
) might require approximations for computational and other reasons. We consider the collection of approximating problems

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}:~~~\Big\{\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{n}^{\nu}(x_{n}),~~\nu\in\mathbb{N}\Big\},

(3.6)

where
f_{n}^{\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is an approximation of

that presumably becomes more accurate as

. We seek to justify the following approach to solving (
3.1

3.6

3.1
). As a preview of our treatment of the actual problem (
2.1
), the finite-dimensional problems (
3.3
) correspond to a discretization of the control space and the approximating problems (
3.6
) implement all other approximations as well, including discretization adopted to solve the underlying PDE. Since discretization of the control space can be viewed as just another approximation, it appears somewhat arbitrary to single out that discretization. However, this separation is technically convenient and allows us to utilize the fact that discretization of the control space imposes a restriction on the problem while the other approximations may not have that characteristic.

We adopt the following notation and concepts. For
\delta\in[0,\infty)
and a function
g:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, the set of near-minimizers is given by

\delta\mbox{-}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits g=\big\{x\in\dom g~\big|~g(x)\leq\inf g+\delta\big\},

where
\inf g=\inf\{g(x)~|~x\in X\}
and
\dom g=\{x\in X~|~g(x)<\infty\}
. Convergence
x^{\nu}
to
x
along a subsequence indexed by
N\subset\mathbb{N}
is specified by
x^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-12.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0ptx
. The index set
N
is then taken from the collection of subsequences of
\mathbb{N}
denoted by
{\cal N}_{\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}
.

Definition 5

(epi-convergence).
For
g^{\nu},g:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we say that
g^{\nu}
epi-converges to
g
as
\nu\to\infty
, written
g^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptg
, when the following hold at every
x\in X
:

(a)

For all
x^{\nu}\to x
, one has
\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits g^{\nu}(x^{\nu})\geq g(x)
.

(b)

There exists
x^{\nu}\to x
such that
\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits g^{\nu}(x^{\nu})\leq g(x)
.

The main result of this section furnishes an optimality gap for a control obtained by solving the approximating problems (
3.6
).

Theorem 6
.

(optimality gap).
For a metric space
(X,d_{X})
, suppose that
f:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
,
X^{n}\subset X
, and
T_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X^{n}
is a surjective mapping. Let
f_{n},f_{n}^{\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, with
f_{n}
defined by (
3.4
). Suppose that the following hold for some
\bar{n}\in\mathbb{N}
:

(a)

f+\iota_{X^{n}}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptf
as
n\to\infty
.

(b)

\forall n\geq\bar{n}
,
f_{n}^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptf_{n}
as
\nu\to\infty
.

(c)

\inf f>-\infty
and,
\forall n\geq\bar{n}
,
\inf f_{n}<\infty
.

Then, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
, there exists
n_{\varepsilon}\geq\bar{n}
such that when
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
,
\delta^{\nu}\to\delta\in[0,\infty)
and
\{\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu}\in\delta^{\nu}\mbox{-}\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits f_{n}^{\nu},\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
has a cluster point
\bar{x}_{n}
, one obtains

f\big(T_{n}(\bar{x}_{n})\big)\leq\inf f+\varepsilon+\delta.

Proof.
Suppose that
\inf f
is finite and let
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
. Then, there is
x\in X
such that
f(x)\leq\inf f+\gamma
. In view of (a) and Definition
5
(b), there is
x^{n}\to x
such that
\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{n}(f(x^{n})+\iota_{X^{n}}(x^{n}))\leq f(x)
. Stringing these two inequalities together, we obtain that

\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{n}\big(\inf(f+\iota_{X^{n}})\big)\leq\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{n}\big(f(x^{n})+\iota_{X^{n}}(x^{n})\big)\leq f(x)\leq\inf f+\gamma.

Since
\gamma
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{n}\big(\inf(f+\iota_{X^{n}})\big)\leq\inf f

(3.7)

when
\inf f
is finite. The same holds trivially when
\inf f=\infty
. The possibility
\inf f=-\infty
is ruled out by (c).

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
. By (c) and (
3.7
),
\inf f>-\infty
and there is
n_{\varepsilon}\geq\bar{n}
such that

\inf(f+\iota_{X^{n}})\leq\inf f+\varepsilon~~~\forall n\geq n_{\varepsilon}.

(3.8)

Fix
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
. First, we establish that

\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu}(\inf f_{n}^{\nu})\leq\inf f_{n}.

(3.9)

An argument parallel to the one carried out to reach (
3.7
) confirms the claim when
\inf f_{n}
is finite. Since
\inf f_{n}=\infty
is ruled out by (c), it only remains to examine the case with
\inf f_{n}=-\infty
. Let
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
. Then, there is
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f_{n}(x_{n})\leq-\gamma
and also, by Definition
5
(b), there are
x_{n}^{\nu}\to x_{n}
such that
\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu}f_{n}^{\nu}(x_{n}^{\nu})\leq f_{n}(x_{n})
. Thus,

\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu}(\inf f_{n}^{\nu})\leq\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu}f_{n}^{\nu}(x_{n}^{\nu})\leq f_{n}(x_{n})\leq-\gamma.

Since
\gamma
is arbitrary, (
3.9
) holds in this case as well.

Second, let
N\in{\cal N}_{\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}
be the subsequence corresponding to the cluster point
\bar{x}_{n}
, i.e.,
\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-12.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{x}_{n}
. Then, (
3.9
) and the construction of
\{\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu},\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
ensure that

\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu\in N}f_{n}^{\nu}(\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu})\leq\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu\in N}(\inf f_{n}^{\nu}+\delta^{\nu})\leq\inf f_{n}+\delta.

By Definition
5
(a), this implies that

f_{n}(\bar{x}_{n})\leq\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits_{\nu\in N}f_{n}^{\nu}(\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu})\leq\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits_{\nu\in N}f_{n}^{\nu}(\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu})\leq\inf f_{n}+\delta.

Since
\inf f_{n}<\infty
by (c),
\bar{x}_{n}\in\dom f_{n}
. Thus,
\bar{x}_{n}\in\delta\mbox{-}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits f_{n}
. We then obtain

f\big(T_{n}(\bar{x}_{n})\big)=f_{n}(\bar{x}_{n})\leq\inf f_{n}+\delta=\inf(f+\iota_{X^{n}})+\delta\leq\inf f+\varepsilon+\delta,

where the second equality follows by (
3.5
) and the second inequality by (
3.8
).

Theorem
6
provides an optimality gap, relative to the infinite-dimensional problem (
3.1
), for any cluster point constructed by solving a sequence of approximating finite-dimensional problems. The gap is bounded by the sum of two terms:
\varepsilon
and
\delta
. While
\varepsilon
can be selected arbitrarily near zero, this typically results in a large
n_{\varepsilon}
and thus the need for minimizing a high-dimensional function
f_{n}^{\nu}
with a tolerance eventually near
\delta
. The theorem applies for any
\delta
so the approximating problems do not need to be solved to a high tolerance. The tolerance might not even be fully known. Still, the theorem provides a guarantee that better solutions of the approximating problems (i.e., lower
\delta^{\nu}
) translate into better solutions of the infinite-dimensional problem (
3.1
), at least in the sense of an upper bound.

The theorem does not require convexity, smoothness or continuity of the functions
f,f_{n}
, and
f_{n}^{\nu}
. However, the functions
f
and
f_{n}
are lower semicontinuous (lsc) by virtue of being epi-limits. We note that
\{\bar{x}_{n}^{\nu},\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and thus has a cluster point as long as the sequence is bounded.

In general,
\varepsilon
can be thought of as a
discretization error
due to the restriction from
X
to
X^{n}
and
\delta^{\nu}
(and its limiting value
\delta
) is an
optimization error
caused by our incomplete minimization of
f_{n}^{\nu}
. There is no
approximation error
in the theorem, something one might expect from replacing
f_{n}
in (
3.3
) by
f_{n}^{\nu}
in (
3.6
). This is avoided because, as
\nu\to\infty
, such errors vanish by assumption (b) in Theorem
6
.

Assumption 7

(continuity).

For a.e.

5
Application

This section examines the assumptions of Section
4
in the context of a distributed optimal control problems. Specifically, for
d=1,2
, or
3
, let

be a bounded open set representing a physical domain and let

be the corresponding boundary. Set

. We denote by

and

the spaces of Lebesgue square-integrable functions from

and

, respectively. As before,

is a probability space. We consider thermal conduction modeled by the following PDE with random conductivity coefficient: For a.e.

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}(\omega)\nabla u)&=c_{1}z\quad\text{in }D\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}(\omega)\nabla u\cdot\vec{n}&=c_{2}(s_{e}-u)\quad\text{on }\partial D,\end{split}

(5.1)

where the state variable

represents the unknown temperature field,

is a given exterior temperature along the boundary,

is the control in a nonempty admissible set

,
c_{1}:D\to[0,\infty)
and

are known coefficients that are uniformly bounded from above,
\vec{n}
is a unit normal vector pointing outside
\bar{D}
, and

is a thermal conductivity field. Specifically,

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\omega)(x)=\exp\bigg(b_{0}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{J}b_{j}(x)\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{j}(\omega)\bigg),

where
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{J}
are iid random variables defined on the probability space
(\Omega,{\cal F},{\mathbb{P}})
and
b_{0},\dots,b_{J}:\bar{D}\to\mathbb{R}
are given functions. This induces a probability space
(\Xi,{\cal B},P)
as discussed in Section
2
, where elements
\xi\in\Xi
are functions from
\bar{D}
to
(0,\infty)
. One example of such a random field has
\log({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
given by a truncated Kahunen–Loève expansion of a Gaussian random field

with mean

and covariance
{\cal C}

[
39
]
, where

,
(\lambda_{j},\varphi_{j})
is an eigenpair of
{\cal C}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{J}
follow the standard normal distribution. In another example,
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
represents a piecewise random thermal conductivity coefficient with
b_{j}
denoting a rescaled characteristic function supported on a subdomain
D_{j}\subset\bar{D}
.

Assumption 20

There exist
\underline{c},\overline{c}:\Xi\to(0,\infty)
such that

0<\underline{c}(\xi)=\essinf_{x\in\bar{D}}\xi(x)\leq\esssup_{x\in\bar{D}}\xi(x)=\overline{c}(\xi)<\infty,\;\text{for a.e. }\xi\in\Xi

Following the notation and terminology from (

and two performance functions: the discrepancy between the solution and a desired temperature field

and the shortfall of the solution average over

relative to a threshold temperature

We adopt the cost function

for some

in Theorem
17
and Proposition

Let

\displaystyle a(u,v;\xi)=\int_{D}\xi(x)\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)dx+\int_{\partial D}c_{2}(x)u(x)v(x)dx\quad\forall u,v\in V,

\displaystyle~b(z,v)=\int_{D}c_{1}(x)z(x)v(x)dx,~\text{ and }\ell(v)=\int_{\partial D}c_{2}(x)s_{e}(x)v(x)dx\quad\forall v\in V.

c_{\xi}^{s}(\xi,z)=\frac{\sqrt{1+c_{P}}}{\underline{c}(\xi)}\big(c^{s}_{2}(\xi)+c^{s}_{1}(\xi)\|z\|_{Z}\big).

(5.23)

The same holds for the approximation solution

by the same argument.

The error estimate (
5.14
) follows the proof of
[

, §4, Thm. 4.7]
, which satisfies

\big\|s(\xi,z)-s^{\nu}(\xi,z)\big\|_{U}\leq h\hat{c}\frac{\overline{c}(\omega)}{\underline{c}(\xi)}\big\|s(\xi,z)\big\|_{V},

(5.24)

where

is the finite element mesh size for the discretization of the domain

and
\hat{c}\in(0,\infty)
is a constant, independent of
\xi
and
z
.
Note that the mesh size

is related to the number of degrees of freedom
\nu
as
h=O((r^{\nu})^{-1/d})
in

, i.e.,
h\leq\breve{c}(r^{\nu})^{-1/d}
for some

. The mesh size is thus tending to zero. Then, we obtain (

) with

given by

c_{\nu}^{s}(\xi)=\hat{c}\breve{c}\overline{c}(\xi)/\underline{c}(\xi),

(5.25)

which concludes all the estimates.

To address Assumption

Proposition 23
.

The performance functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
defined in (
5.4
) satisfy the continuity estimates: for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U,z,z^{\prime}\in Z
,

\begin{split}\big|g_{1}(u,z)-g_{1}(u^{\prime},z^{\prime})\big|&\leq\big(\|u\|_{U}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{U}+2\|s_{d}\|_{U}\big)\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{U}\\
\big|g_{2}(u,z)-g_{2}(u^{\prime},z^{\prime})\big|&\leq|D_{t}|\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{U},\end{split}

(5.26)

where
|D_{t}|
is the size of the subdomain
D_{t}\subset D
. This implies Assumption
7
and Assumption
11
for
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. Moreover, for all
u\in U,z\in Z
, the performance functions are bounded from above by

\big|g_{1}(u,z)\big|\leq\big(\|u\|_{U}+\|s_{d}\|_{U}\big)^{2},~~~~~~~\big|g_{2}(u,z)\big|\leq|s_{t}|+|D_{t}|\|u\|_{U}.

Proof.
By definition of
g_{1}
in (
5.4
), we have

\begin{split}&\big|g_{1}(u,z)-g_{1}(u^{\prime},z^{\prime})\big|=\left|\int_{D}\big(u(x)-s_{d}(x)\big)^{2}-\big(u^{\prime}(x)-s_{d}(x)\big)^{2}dx\right|\\
&\leq\big(\|u\|_{U}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{U}+2\|s_{d}\|_{U}\big)\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{U},\end{split}

which concludes the continuity estimate. The upper bound for
g_{1}
is satisfied by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. For
g_{2}
, we have

\big|g_{2}(u,z)-g_{2}(u^{\prime},z^{\prime})\big|=\left|\int_{D_{t}}\big(u(x)-u^{\prime}(x)\big)dx\right|\leq|D_{t}|\;\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{U},

which concludes the continuity estimate. The upper bound for
g_{2}
is also satisfied by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Note that the continuity of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
implies the measurability of
g_{1}(s(\xi,z),z)
and
g_{2}(s(\xi,z),z)
w.r.t.
\xi

s(\xi,z)
w.r.t.
\xi
from Proposition
22
and the Doob–Dynkin lemma
[
28

, which verifies Assumption
7

\xi
