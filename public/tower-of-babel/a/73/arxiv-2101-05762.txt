Gromov–Hausdorff Distance Between Segment and Circle
Yibo Ji, Alexey A. Tuzhilin

Abstract
We calculate the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between a line segment and a circle in the Euclidean plane. To do that, we introduced a few new notions like round spaces and nonlinearity degree of a metric space.



1
Introduction

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance measures the difference between any two metric spaces. There are a few possibilities to do that. One of them, to embed isometrically the both spaces into all possible other metric spaces, then the least possible Hausdorff distance between the images will be just that characteristic. Another way is to establish correspondences between these spaces and to measure the least possible distortion of the spaces metrics produced by these correspondences.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance has many applications, for example, it helps to investigate the growth of groups, or it can be used in image recognition. However, to get concrete values of the distance, even in the cases of “simple spaces” like line segment and the standard circle, is a very non-trivial task. In this paper we discuss our original technique enabled to obtain the exact values in the latter case.

Preliminaries

In what follows, we work with various non-empty metric spaces, and the distance between points
p
and
q
of a metric space we denote by
|pq|
, independently on the choice of the space. For a metric space
X
,
x\in X
, non-empty
A,B\subset X
,
r>0
, and
s\geq 0
, we use the following notions and notations:

•

U_{r}(x)=\bigl\{y\in X:|xy|<r\bigr\}
(
open ball with center
x
and radius
r
);

•

B_{s}(x)=\bigl\{y\in X:|xy|\leq s\bigr\}
(
closed ball with center
x
and radius
s
);

•

|xA|=\inf_{a\in A}|xa|
(
distance between
x
and
A
);

•

U_{r}(A)=\bigl\{y\in X:|yA|<r\bigr\}
(
open
r
-neighborhood of
A
);

•

B_{s}(A)=\bigl\{y\in X:|yA|\leq s\bigr\}
(
closed
s
-neighborhood of
A
);

•

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\bigl\{t:A\subset U_{t}(B)\text{ and }U_{t}(A)\supset B\bigr\}
(
Hausdorff distance between
A
and
B
).

For any non-empty set
X
, we denote by
{\cal P}_{0}(X)
the set of all non-empty subsets of
X
. If
X
is a metric space, then the Hausdorff distance on
{\cal P}_{0}(X)
satisfies the triangle inequality, but can be equal
\infty
, and can vanish for different subsets. However, if we restrict
d_{H}
to the set
{\cal H}(X)
of all non-empty closed bounded subsets of
X
, then
d_{H}
becomes a metric, see
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

Given a metric space
X
, the following properties are simultaneously presented or not in the both
X
and
{\cal H}(X)
:
completeness, total boundness, compactness.

Given
r>0
, a subset of a metric space is called
r
-separated
if the distance between any its different points is at least
r
.

Given two sets
X
and
Y
,
a correspondence between
X
and
Y
is each subset
R\subset X\times Y
such that for any
x\in X
there exists
y\in Y
with
(x,y)\in R
and, vise versa, for any
y\in Y
there exists
x\in X
with
(x,y)\in R
. Let
{\cal R}(X,Y)
denote the set of all correspondences between
X
and
Y
. If
X
and
Y
are metric spaces, and
R\in{\cal R}(X,Y)
, then we define
the distortion
\operatorname{dis}R
of
R
as follows:

\operatorname{dis}R=\sup\Bigl\{\bigl||x_{1}x_{2}|-|y_{1}y_{2}|\bigr|:(x_{1},y_{1}),\,(x_{2},y_{2})\in R\Bigr\}.

Further,
the Gromov–Hausdorff distance
d_{GH}(X,Y)
between metric spaces
X
and
Y
, or for short
GH-distance
, is the value

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in{\cal R}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

An equivalent definition: it is the infimum of Hausdorff distances
d_{H}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
between all possible subsets
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
of the metric spaces
Z
, provided
X^{\prime}
is isometric to
X
, and
Y^{\prime}
is isometric to
Y
. It is well-known
[
1
]
that
d_{GH}
is a metric on the set of isometry classes of compact metric spaces, in particular, two compact metric spaces are isometric if and only if the GH-distance between them vanishes. In general situation,
d_{GH}
satisfies the triangle inequality, is bounded for bounded spaces (for non-bounded spaces it can be infinite), and can vanish for non-isometric spaces. Also, for any
A,B\in{\cal P}_{0}(X)
, it holds
d_{GH}(A,B)\leq d_{H}(A,B)
. In particular, if
{\bar{A}}
is the closure of
A
, then
d_{GH}(A,{\bar{A}})=d_{H}(A,{\bar{A}})=0
.

If
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
and
X
are some metric spaces such that
d_{GH}(X_{i},X)\to 0
, then we say that the sequence
X_{i}
is
Gromov–Hausdorff convergent
to
X
and write
X_{i}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{GH}}X
.

It is easy to see that for any correspondence
R\in{\cal R}(X,Y)
, its closure in
X\times Y
has the same distortion, thus, to achieve
d_{GH}(X,Y)
, it suffices to consider only closed correspondences. In other words, if
{\cal R}_{c}(X,Y)
is the set of all closed correspondences between
X
and
Y
, then we have

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in{\cal R}_{c}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

For any metric space
X
and a real number
\lambda>0
, we denote by
\lambda X
the metric space which differs from
X
by multiplication of all its distances by
\lambda
. For
\lambda=0
, we define
\lambda X
as a single point space.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be metric spaces. Then

(1)

if
X
is a single-point metric space, then
d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diam}Y
;

(2)

if
\operatorname{diam}X<\infty
, then

d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\frac{1}{2}|\operatorname{diam}X-\operatorname{diam}Y|;

(3)

d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\}
, in particular,
d_{GH}(X,Y)<\infty
for bounded
X
and
Y
;

(4)

for any metric spaces
X
,
Y
and any
\lambda>0
, we have
d_{GH}(\lambda X,\lambda Y)=\lambda d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

3
Homogeneous and round metric spaces

Fix a real
0<b\leq\operatorname{diam}X
and an integer
n\geq 2
. A metric space
X
is called
(b,n)
-homogeneous
if for any point
x\in X
there exists a
b
-separated
n
-point subset
S\subset X
such that
x\in S
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be a
(b,n)
-homogeneous metric space. Suppose that
Y
is not
(a,n)
-homogeneous for some
0<a<b
. Then
2d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq b-a
.

Proof.

Put
c=b-a
and suppose to the contrary that
2d_{GH}(X,Y)<c
, then there exists
R\in{\cal R}(X,Y)
such that
\operatorname{dis}R<c
, therefore, for any
(x_{1},y_{1}),\,(x_{2},y_{2})\in R
with
|x_{1}x_{2}|\geq b
, it holds
|y_{1}y_{2}|>|x_{1}x_{2}|-c\geq b-c=a
. Since
Y
is not
a
-homogeneous, there exists
y\in Y
such that there is no
(a,n)
-separated
n
-point subset
T\subset Y
with
y\in T
. Since
R
is a correspondence, there exists
x\in X
such that
(x,y)\in R
. Since
X
is
(b,n)
-homogeneous, there exists
b
-separated
S=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset X
such that
x_{1}=x
. Let
T=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
with
y_{1}=y
and
(x_{i},y_{i})\in R
for any
i
. Then
T
is an
a
-separated
n
-point subset
Y
containing
y
, a contradiction.
∎

A metric space
X
is called
round
if it is
(b,2)
-homogeneous for any
0<b<\operatorname{diam}X
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sphere of radius
r>0
w.r.t. some norm. Then
X
is round, but not
(a,2)
-homogeneous for each
a>2r
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
X
be a round metric space,
0<a<\operatorname{diam}X
, and suppose that a metric space
Y
is not
(a,2)
-homogeneous. Then
2d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\operatorname{diam}X-a
.

Proof.

Theorem
3.1
implies that
2d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq b-a
for each
b
,
0<a<b<\operatorname{diam}X
, and the result follows from the arbitrariness of
b
.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be spheres of radii
b
and
a
w.r.t. some norms, then

2d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq 2b-2a.

4
Nonlinearity degree of a metric space

In this section, we introduce the notion of nonlinearity degree and investigate several its properties. To start with, we denote by
\operatorname{Lip}_{a}(X)
the set of all real-valued
a
-Lipschitz functions defined on a metric space
X
.

Definition 4.1
.

The nonlinearity degree of a metric space
X
is defined as follows:

c(X):=\inf_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)}\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f(x)-f(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}.

Remark 4.2
.

Clearly,
c(X)\geq 0
, and for any
X\subset\mathbb{R}
we have
c(X)=0
(indeed, to achieve the value
0
, we can take the inclusion mapping as
f
). Also, since
c(X)\leq\operatorname{diam}X
, then for each bounded space
X
, the value
c(X)
is finite.

If
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is
a
-Lipschitz, then for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
the function
f+b
is
a
-Lipschitz as well, and for any
x,y\in X
the value
|xy|-|f(x)-f(y)|
remains the same. In addition, for a bounded metric space, each
a
-Lipschitz function is bounded, thus, to calculate the value
c(X)
in this case, we can restrict ourselves by those
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)
that satisfy
\inf f(X)=0
. The set of all such functions we denote by
\operatorname{Lip}_{1}^{0}(X)
. Hence, we got the following

Theorem 4.3
.

For any bounded metric space
X
, we have

c(X):=\inf_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}^{0}(X)}\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f(x)-f(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}.

The nonlinearity degree can be used to estimate the minimal Gromov–Hausdorff distance between metric space
X
and non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

For any metric space
X
, we have

\bigl|X\,{\cal P}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\bigr|=\inf_{Z\in{\cal P}_{0}(\mathbb{R})}d_{GH}(X,Z)\leq c(X)/2.

If
X
is bounded, then there exists
Z\in{\cal H}(\mathbb{R})
such that
d_{GH}(X,Z)\leq c(X)/2
.

Proof.

If
c(X)=\infty
then the result holds.

Now, suppose that
c(X)<\infty
. Take
f_{k}\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)
such that

\sup\Bigl\{|xy|-\bigl|f_{k}(x)-f_{k}(y)\bigr|:x,y\in X\Bigr\}\leq c(X)+\frac{1}{k},

and put
X_{k}=f_{k}(X)
. Let
R_{k}\in{\cal R}(X,X_{k})
be the graph of
f_{k}
, namely,

R_{k}=\Bigl\{\bigl(x,f_{k}(x)\bigr):x\in X\Bigr\}.

Since
f_{k}
is
1
-Lipschitz, then for any
\bigl(x,f_{k}(x)\bigr),\,\bigl(y,f_{k}(y)\bigr)\in R_{k}
we have
|f_{k}(x)-f_{k}(y)|\leq|xy|
, therefore,

\operatorname{dis}R_{k}=\sup_{x,y\in X}\Bigl\{|xy|-\bigl|f_{k}(x)-f_{k}(y)\bigr|\Bigr\}\leq c(X)+\frac{1}{k}.

Due to the arbitrariness of
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we get

\inf_{Z\in{\cal P}_{0}(\mathbb{R})}d_{GH}(X,Z)\leq\inf_{k}d_{GH}(X,X_{k})\leq c(X)/2.

Now we prove the existence of
Z
for bounded
X
. By Theorem
4.3
, we can take
f_{k}
from
\operatorname{Lip}_{1}^{0}(X)
instead of from
\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)
. Let
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k}
be the closure of
X_{k}=f_{k}(X)
. Since
d_{GH}(X_{k},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k})=0
, then we have
\inf_{k}d_{GH}(X,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k})\leq c(X)/2
by the triangle inequality. The boundedness of
X
implies
d=\operatorname{diam}X<\infty
, and by Theorem
2.2
, we have

\operatorname{diam}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k}=\operatorname{diam}X_{k}\leq\operatorname{diam}X+2d_{GH}(X,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k})\leq d+c(X)<\infty.

Since
X_{k}\geq 0
and
0\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k}
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
X_{k}\subset\bigl[0,d+c(X)\bigr]=:I
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and, by compactness of
{\cal H}(I)
due to Theorem
2.1
, there exists a convergent subsequence
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k_{i}}\in{\cal H}(I)
. Put
Z=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k_{i}}
. Since
d_{GH}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k_{i}},Z)\leq d_{H}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k_{i}},Z)
, we obtain
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{k_{i}}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{GH}}Z
, therefore
d_{GH}(X,Z)\leq c(X)/2
.
∎

Example 4.5
.

Now we show that the inequality in Theorem
4.4
cannot be changed to equality, even in the case of compact
X
. Let
X
be the standard unit circle in the Euclidean plane, endowed with the intrinsic metric. We shall find a compact

Z\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
d_{GH}(X,Z)<c(X)/2
. Let us take as
Z
the segment
[0,\frac{2}{3}\pi]
. Below, in Lemma
6.1
, we shall prove that
d_{GH}(X,Z)=\frac{1}{3}\pi
. On the other hand, given any
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(S^{1},\mathbb{R})
, the function
g(x)=f(x)-f(-x)
has at least one zero point
x_{0}
. Then

\sup\Bigl\{|xy|-\bigl|f(x)-f(y)\bigr|:x,y\in X\Bigr\}\geq|x_{0}(-x_{0})|-\bigl|f(x_{0})-f(-x_{0})\bigr|=\pi.

Since the choice of
f
is arbitrary, we know that
c(X)\geq\pi>2d_{GH}(X,Z)
.

The next result characterizes all compact metric spaces with
c(X)=0
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Each compact metric space
X
with
c(X)=0
is isometric to a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
, there exists a compact subset
Z\subset\mathbb{R}
with
d_{GH}(X,Z)=0
, thus
X
and
Z
are isometric.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

For a compact metric space
X
, there exists a function
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)
such that

\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f(x)-f(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}=c(X).

Proof.

By Theorem
4.3
, we can choose a sequence
f_{n}\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}^{0}(X)
such that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
it holds

\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}<c(X)+\frac{1}{n}.

The next lemma is a direct consequence of Arzelà-Ascoli theorem
[
1
]
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Each uniformly bounded sequence of
L
-Lipschitz functions on a compact metric space contains a subsequence uniformly convergent to an
L
-Lipschitz function.

Since any collection of functions from
\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)
is uniformly bounded, we can apply Lemma
4.8
, thus, w.l.o.g. we can suppose that the sequence
f_{n}
itself uniformly converges to some
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(X)
. Take arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and choose
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
n\geq N
and any
x\in X
we have

\bigl|f(x)-f_{n}(x)\bigr|<\varepsilon/3\ \ \text{and}\ \ 1/n<\varepsilon/3,

hence for any
x,y\in X
and any
n\geq N
it holds

\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f(x)-f(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}\leq\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}+2\varepsilon/3<c(X)+\varepsilon.

Due to the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, we have
\sup\bigl\{|xy|-|f(x)-f(y)|:x,y\in X\bigr\}\leq c(X)
, that concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Proposition
4.7
gives another proof of Theorem
4.4
when
X
is compact.

Theorem 4.10
.

Suppose we have a connected compact metric space
X
equipped with a homeomorphism
\alpha
of order
2
such that
\bigl|\alpha(x)\,x\bigr|=\operatorname{diam}X
for each
x\in X
, and a compact metric space
Y
such that
c(Y)<\operatorname{diam}X
. Then we have
2d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq m
, where
m=\frac{2}{3}\bigl(\operatorname{diam}X-c(Y)\bigr)
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
2d_{GH}(X,Y)<m
, thus there is a closed correspondence
R\in{\cal R}_{c}(X,Y)
such that
\operatorname{dis}R<m
.

By Proposition
4.7
, there exists
v\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(Y)
such that

\sup\bigl\{|y_{1}y_{2}|-|v(y_{1})-v(y_{2})|:y_{1},y_{2}\in Y\bigr\}=c(Y).

In the following text, we will abbreviate
c(Y)
as
c
and
\alpha(x)
as
\alpha_{x}
.

Since
X\times Y
is compact and
R
is its closed subset, then
R
is compact, together with
R(x)=R\cap\bigl(\{x\}\times Y\bigr)
and
R(\alpha_{x})=R\cap\bigl(\{\alpha_{x}\}\times Y\bigr)
. Since the function
v
is continuous, then the restrictions of
v
to
R(x)
and
R(\alpha_{x})
are bounded and attain their maximal and minimal values, thus the following function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is correctly defined:

f(x)=\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)-\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr).

Lemma 4.11
.

For any sequence
x_{i}\in X
such that
x_{i}\to{\tilde{x}}\in X
it holds

\limsup_{i\to+\infty}f(x_{i})\leq f({\tilde{x}}).

Proof.

Let us put
R^{v}:=\bigl\{\bigl(x,v(y)\bigr):(x,y)\in R\bigr\}\subset X\times\mathbb{R}
, then we can rewrite
f
as follows:

f(x)=\max R^{v}(x)-\min R^{v}(\alpha_{x}).

Since
R
is compact, then
R^{v}
is compact as the image of
R
under the continuous mapping
\operatorname{id}\times v
. Thus
R^{v}
is closed and bounded.

We put
z_{i}=\max R^{v}(x_{i})
,
w_{i}=\min R^{v}(\alpha_{x_{i}})
, then
f(x_{i})=z_{i}-w_{i}
, both the sequences
z_{i}
,
w_{i}
are bounded, and both the
z:=\limsup_{i\to+\infty}z_{i}
,
w:=\liminf_{i\to+\infty}w_{i}
are finite. Now we choose subsequences
z_{k_{i}}
and
w_{l_{i}}
such that

z=\lim_{i\to+\infty}z_{k_{i}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ w=\lim_{i\to+\infty}w_{l_{i}}.

Since
R^{v}
is closed,
(x_{k_{i}},z_{k_{i}}),(\alpha_{x_{l_{i}}},w_{l_{i}})\in R^{v}
, and
z
,
w
are finite, then

({\tilde{x}},z)=\lim_{i\to+\infty}(x_{k_{i}},z_{k_{i}})\in R^{v}\ \ \text{and}\ \ (\alpha_{\tilde{x}},w)=\lim_{i\to+\infty}(\alpha_{x_{l_{i}}},w_{l_{i}})\in R^{v},

therefore,
z\in R^{v}({\tilde{x}})
and
w\in R^{v}(\alpha_{\tilde{x}})
. Thus, we get

\max R^{v}({\tilde{x}})\geq z=\limsup_{i\to+\infty}z_{i}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \min R^{v}(\alpha_{\tilde{x}})\leq w=\liminf_{i\to+\infty}w_{i}.

In account,

f({\tilde{x}})\geq z-w=\limsup_{i\to+\infty}z_{i}-\liminf_{i\to+\infty}w_{i}=\\
=\limsup_{i\to+\infty}z_{i}+\limsup_{i\to+\infty}(-w_{i})\geq\limsup_{i\to+\infty}(z_{i}-w_{i})=\limsup_{i\to+\infty}f(x_{i}),

and the proof is completed.
∎

Since
\operatorname{dis}R<m
and
|x\,\alpha_{x}|=\operatorname{diam}X
, then for any
y_{1}\in R(x)
and
y_{2}\in R(\alpha_{x})
we have
|y_{1}y_{2}|>\operatorname{diam}X-m
, thus

\bigl|v(y_{1})-v(y_{2})\bigr|\geq|y_{1}y_{2}|-c>\operatorname{diam}X-m-c=:d.

So, either
v(y_{1})-v(y_{2})>d
or
v(y_{2})-v(y_{1})>d
. Since the values
\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)
and
\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)
are attained, then for each
x\in X
it holds
either
f(x)>d
or
-f(x)>d
, and the latter is equivalent to
f(x)<-d
.

We put
A:=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)>d\bigr\}
and
B:=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)<-d\bigr\}
. Since
c\leq\operatorname{diam}X
and
m=\frac{2}{3}(\operatorname{diam}X-c)
, we get

d=\operatorname{diam}X-m-c=\frac{1}{3}(\operatorname{diam}X-c)\geq 0\geq-\frac{1}{3}(\operatorname{diam}X-c)=c+m-\operatorname{diam}X=-d,

hence
A
is the complement of
B
. Moreover, since
f(x)\neq\pm d
, we have

A=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)\geq d\bigr\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ B=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)\leq-d\bigr\}.

Now we show that
A
is closed and, thus,
B
is open. Indeed, consider an arbitrary sequence
x_{i}\in A
converging to some
x\in X
. Then, by Lemma
4.11
, we have
f(x)\geq\limsup\limits_{x_{i}\to x}f(x_{i})\geq d
, thus
x\in A
and, therefore,
A
is closed.

Lemma 4.12
.

We have
\alpha(B)\subset A
.

Proof.

Take arbitrary
x\in B
, then, by definition,
\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)-\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)\leq-d
, thus

-f(x)=\min v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)-\max v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)\leq\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)-\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)\leq-d,

so
f(x)\geq d
and, hence,
\alpha_{x}\in A
.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

It holds that
\alpha(A)\subset B
.

Proof.

If not, there exists
x\in A
such that
\alpha_{x}\in A
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\max v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)\geq\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)
. Since
x\in A
, we have, by definition,
\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)\geq d+\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)
.

Recall that for any
y\in R(x)
and
y^{\prime}\in R(\alpha_{x})
it holds
\bigl|v(y)-v(y^{\prime})\bigr|>d
, thus we get

\max v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)\geq d+\max v\bigl(R(x)\bigr)\geq 2d+\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr).

As we mentioned above,
R(\alpha_{x})
is compact and
v
is continuous, hence there exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in R(\alpha_{x})
such that
v(y_{1})=\max v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)
and
v(y_{2})=\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)
. Since
v
is
1
-Lipschitz, we have
|y_{1}y_{2}|\geq v(y_{1})-v(y_{2})
, therefore,

m>\operatorname{dis}R\geq\operatorname{diam}R(\alpha_{x})\geq|y_{1}y_{2}|\geq v(y_{1})-v(y_{2})=\\
=\max v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)-\min v\bigl(R(\alpha_{x})\bigr)\geq 2d=2\bigl(\operatorname{diam}X-m-c\bigr).

Thus
m>\frac{2}{3}(\operatorname{diam}X-c)
which achieves contradiction and, hence, we have
\alpha(A)\subset B
.
∎

Since
X=A\sqcup B
and
\alpha\colon X\to X
is a homeomorphism, Lemmas
4.12
and
4.13
imply that the restriction of
\alpha
to
A
is a homeomorphism onto
B
, hence both
A
and
B
are proper closed-open, therefore,
X
is not connected, a contradiction which completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5
Geometric calculation of distortion

Consider the special case we are interesting in, namely, let
X=I_{\lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a segment of the length
\lambda
, and
Y=S^{1}=\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}=1\bigr\}
be the standard circle endowed with the intrinsic metric. Since we are interesting in the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between
I_{\lambda}
and
S^{1}
, it does not matter where we place
I_{\lambda}
on the line
\mathbb{R}
. We will use two possible placements:
[0,\lambda]
and
[-\lambda/2,\,\lambda/2]
. In this section we will use the latter one.

Take a correspondence
R\in{\cal R}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})
. Our goal is to calculate geometrically the distortion of
R
. To do that, we parameterize the circle
S^{1}
by the standard angular coordinate
-\pi\leq\varphi\leq\pi
, thus the interior distance between points
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in S^{1}
equals
\min\bigl\{|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}|,2\pi-|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}|\bigr\}
. Let
-\lambda/2\leq t\leq\lambda/2
be the standard coordinate on the segment
I_{\lambda}
. Now we represent the correspondence
R
as a subset of rectangle
Q:=[-\lambda/2,\,\lambda/2]\times[-\pi,\pi]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

To calculate the distortion, we need to maximize the function

f\bigl((t_{0},\varphi_{0}),(t,\varphi)\bigr)=\begin{cases}\bigl|\,|t-t_{0}|-|\varphi-\varphi_{0}|\,\bigr|&\text{if $|\varphi-\varphi_{0}|\leq\pi$},\\
\bigl|\,|t-t_{0}|-2\pi+|\varphi-\varphi_{0}|\,\bigr|&\text{if $|\varphi-\varphi_{0}|\geq\pi$}\\
\end{cases}

over all
(t_{0},\varphi_{0}),\,(t,\varphi)\in R
. In other words, we need to find the least possible
a
such that
f\bigl((t_{0},\varphi_{0}),(t,\varphi)\bigr)\leq a
for all
(t_{0},\varphi_{0}),\,(t,\varphi)\in R
.

Take arbitrary
a>0
, and denote by
D_{a}(t_{0},\varphi_{0})
the subset of the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
consisting of all
(t,\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
f\bigl((t_{0},\varphi_{0}),(t,\varphi)\bigr)\leq a
. Clearly that
D_{a}(t_{0},\varphi_{0})
can be obtained from
D_{a}:=D_{a}(0,0)
by shifting by
(t_{0},\varphi_{0})
, i.e.,
D_{a}(t_{0},\varphi_{0})=(t_{0},\varphi_{0})+D_{a}
. Evident observation shows that
D_{a}
looks like the blue domain in Figure
1
, left-hand side.

Figure 1:
Geometric calculation of distortion.

It is bounded by the segments parallel to bisectors of quadrants, and its sizes are given by the labels in the figure. Thus, we get the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Under the notations introduced above,
\operatorname{dis}R\leq a
if and only if for any
(t_{0},\varphi_{0})\in R
it holds
R\subset D_{a}(t_{0},\varphi_{0})
. In particular, the distortion of
R\subset Q
equals the least possible
a
satisfying the previous condition.

Remark 5.2
.

If a graph
P
satisfies the above property with respect to
a
, then any subgraph of it also satisfies the above property.

Let us note that the domain
Q\cap D_{a}(t_{0},\varphi_{0})
is depicted on the right-hand side of Figure
1
in green, as the intersection of blue and yellow domains.

6
Calculating GH-distance between circle and segment

Now we apply the technique described above to calculate the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the standard circle
S^{1}=\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}=1\bigr\}
with intrinsic metric and the segment
I_{\lambda}=[0,\lambda]\subset\mathbb{R}
. We start from the following

Lemma 6.1
.

For each
0\leq\lambda<\pi
we have

d_{GH}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})\geq\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4}.

Proof.

Notice that the metric space
S^{1}
is round,
\operatorname{diam}S^{1}=\pi
, and for any
\lambda^{\prime}>\lambda
the segment
I_{\lambda}
is not
(\lambda^{\prime}/2,2)
-homogeneous. Thus, for any
\pi>\lambda^{\prime}>\lambda
we can use Corollary
3.3
and get

d_{GH}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})\geq\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{4}.

The result follows from arbitrariness of
\lambda^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

For each
0\leq\lambda\leq 2\pi/3
we have

d_{GH}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})=\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, we get the necessary lower bound for the
d_{GH}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})
. It remains to get the same upper bound. To do that, we will construct a special correspondence
R_{\lambda}\in{\cal R}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})
with
\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=\pi-\lambda/2
, which completes the proof.

For
\lambda=0
we put
R_{\lambda}=I_{\lambda}\times S^{1}
. Since the space
I_{\lambda}
consists of a single point, we get

\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=\operatorname{diam}S^{1}=\pi=\pi-\lambda/2.

Now, suppose that
\lambda>0
. Identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
R_{\lambda}
be the graph of the mapping
\gamma_{\lambda}\colon I_{\lambda}\to S^{1}
defined as
\gamma\colon t\mapsto e^{2\pi i\,t/\lambda}
. Notice that the image of the mapping
t\mapsto 2\pi t/\lambda
,
t\in I_{\lambda}
, equals
\{e^{it}:t\in[0,2\pi]\}
, thus
R_{\lambda}
is a correspondence, and for any
t,t^{\prime}\in I_{\lambda}
, we have

\bigl|\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\bigr|=\min\Bigl\{\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}|t-t^{\prime}|,\,2\pi-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}|t-t^{\prime}|\Bigr\}.

Since the value
\bigl|\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\bigr|
depends only on
s:=|t-t^{\prime}|
, we put
f(s)=\bigl|\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\bigr|-|t-t^{\prime}|
, then

\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=\max_{0\leq s\leq\lambda}\bigl|f(s)\bigr|.

Since the function

f(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}s-s&\text{for $0\leq\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}s\leq\pi$},\\
2\pi-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}s-s&\text{for $\pi\leq\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}s\leq 2\pi$},\end{cases}

is linear on
[0,\lambda/2]
and on
[\lambda/2,\lambda]
, we get

\max_{0\leq s\leq\lambda}\bigl|f(s)\bigr|=\max\Bigl\{\bigl|f(0)\bigr|,\,\bigl|f(\lambda/2)\bigr|,\,\bigl|f(\lambda)\bigr|\Bigr\}=\max\bigl\{0,\,|\pi-\lambda/2|,\,\lambda\bigr\}.

Since
0\leq\lambda\leq 2\pi/3
, then
|\pi-\lambda/2|=\pi-\lambda/2\geq\lambda>0
, therefore
\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=\pi-\lambda/2
.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

If
\frac{2}{3}\pi\leq\lambda\leq\frac{7}{6}\pi
, then we have
d_{GH}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})=\frac{\pi}{3}
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
6.5
, it suffices to construct a correspondence
R_{\lambda}\in{\cal R}(I_{\lambda},S^{1})
with
\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=2\pi/3
, which completes the proof.

Again, we identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
R_{\lambda}
be the graph of the mapping
\gamma_{\lambda}\colon I_{\lambda}\to S^{1}
defined as
\gamma\colon t\mapsto e^{3\pi i\,t}
. Notice that the image of the mapping
t\mapsto 3\pi t
,
t\in I_{\lambda}
, contains
\{e^{it}:t\in[0,2\pi]\}
, thus
R_{\lambda}
is a correspondence, and for any
t,t^{\prime}\in I_{\lambda}
, we have

\bigl|\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\bigr|=\min\Bigl\{3|t-t^{\prime}|,\,2\pi-3|t-t^{\prime}|,\,3|t-t^{\prime}|-2\pi,\,4\pi-3|t-t^{\prime}|\Bigr\}.

Since the value
\bigl|\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\bigr|
depends only on
s:=|t-t^{\prime}|
, we put
f(s)=\bigl|\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\bigr|-|t-t^{\prime}|
, then

\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=\max_{0\leq s\leq\lambda}\bigl|f(s)\bigr|.

Since the function

f(s)=\begin{cases}2s&\text{for $0\leq s\leq\frac{\pi}{3}$},\\
2\pi-4s&\text{for $\frac{\pi}{3}\leq s\leq\frac{2}{3}\pi$},\\
2s-2\pi&\text{for $\frac{2}{3}\pi\leq s\leq\pi$},\\
4\pi-4s&\text{for $\pi\leq s\leq\frac{7}{6}\pi$},\\
\end{cases}

is linear on
[0,\pi/3]
,
[\pi/3,2\pi/3]
,
[2\pi/3,\pi]
,
[\pi,7\pi/6]
, we get

\max_{0\leq s\leq 7\pi/6}\bigl|f(s)\bigr|=\max\Bigl\{\bigl|f(0)\bigr|,\,\bigl|f(\pi/3)\bigr|,\,\bigl|f(2\pi/3)\bigr|,\,\bigl|f(\pi)\bigr|,\,\bigl|f(7\pi/6)\bigr|\Bigr\}=\max\{0,\,2\pi/3\}=2\pi/3,

therefore,
\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=2\pi/3
.
∎

If

Thus, it suffices to construct a correspondence

with

. We will use notations from Section

and apply Theorem
5.1
.

Consider the correspondence depicted in Figure

.

Lemma 6.5

For each
