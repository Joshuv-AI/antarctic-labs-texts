Structure-Preserving Numerical Methods for Two Nonlinear Systems of Dispersive Wave Equations
Joshua Lampert, Hendrik Ranocha

Abstract
We use the general framework of summation by parts operators to construct conservative, entropy-stable and well-balanced semidiscretizations of two different nonlinear systems of dispersive shallow water equations with varying bathymetry: (i) a variant of the coupled Benjamin-Bona-Mahony (BBM) equations and (ii) a recently proposed model by Svärd and Kalisch (2023) with enhanced dispersive behavior. Both models share the property of being conservative in terms of a nonlinear invariant, often interpreted as entropy function. This property is preserved exactly in our novel semidiscretizations. To obtain fully-discrete entropy-stable schemes, we employ the relaxation method. We present improved numerical properties of our schemes in some test cases.

The shallow water equations are a widely used model to describe phenomena with large wavelengths and comparably small waterheights, such as tsunamis generated by (bigger) earthquakes or inundations. However, if we consider waves with shorter wavelengths in the deep sea, the effect of dispersion becomes pertinent
[
23
]
. This concerns, e.g., waves generated by submarine landslides. However, dispersion is not modeled by the shallow water equations. Therefore, alternative models that are able to describe dispersive behavior need to be constructed. On the other hand, we would like to keep at least two properties of the shallow water equations. First, the special nonlinearity of the shallow water equations has proven to describe the evolution of oceanic water waves well.
Second, the dispersive model should preserve the physically interpretable energy/entropy (in)equality. Thus, we focus on models that build on the shallow water equations, but add additional terms that are capable to describe dispersion, while satisfying an entropy condition. Specifically, we look at two different models: A system of two coupled Benjamin-Bona-Mahony (BBM) equations with variable bottom topography
[
28
]
called BBM-BBM equations and a dispersive system recently proposed by Svärd and Kalisch
[
65
]
.

There are several other dispersive shallow water models, see
[
32
, Chapter 6]
for a brief overview. Widely used dispersive equations are the models by Peregrine
[
48
]
, by Madsen and Sørensen
[
39
]
, and the related model by Schäffer and Madsen
[
61
]
, see also
[
33
,
3
]
. Another important model that is closely related to the BBM-BBM equations is the Nwogu system
[
47
]
. All of these models can be seen as optimization of a previous one, and share a similar structure: The dispersive terms are modeled as higher-order derivative terms.

For all of the models mentioned above, the mathematical entropy function conserved by the shallow water equations is not conserved
[
28
]
.
However, both the BBM-BBM model and the equations by Svärd and Kalisch mimic the entropy structure of the classical shallow water equations.
Thus, we concentrate on these two models to develop structure-preserving numerical methods.
Using such structure-preserving methods can not only lead to physically more accurate numerical solutions, but can also improve stability properties and reduce the error of the scheme, e.g.
[
55
,
56
,
50
]
.

A different approach to the above-mentioned models is taken in
[
16
]
, where a dispersive shallow water model is formulated as a hyperbolic system of equations containing only first derivatives. Nonetheless, the authors of
[
16
]
show that the models by Peregrine and Madsen-Sørensen can be formulated with their model.
There are also other dispersive shallow water models with an entropy structure,
e.g., the Serre-Green-Naghdi equations and hyperbolic approximations thereof

[
17
,
8
,
24
]
.
However, we concentrate on the BBM-BBM equations and the model of Svärd and Kalisch
in this article and leave the other models for future work.

To construct numerical methods that are entropy-conservative on a semidiscrete level (discrete in space, continuous in time), we utilize the framework of summation by parts (SBP) operators and split forms, cf. e.g.
[
19
]
, that allows a systematic approach to obtain entropy-conservative and entropy-dissipative semidiscretizations. The idea is to mimic the mathematical properties used on the continuous level (SBP operators mimic integration by parts, split forms mimic the product and chain rule) on the semidiscrete level.

To integrate the resulting ordinary differential equations in time, we employ the Relaxation Runge-Kutta (RRK) method
[
31
,
59
]
.
This is a small modification of classical Runge-Kutta methods to preserve the entropy condition of the semidiscretization also for the fully-discrete solution.

We focus on models that are able to describe a possibly non-constant bottom topography (bathymetry). In this situation, the shallow water equations and their dispersive forms have non-trivial steady solutions that are constant in time. By using consistent SBP operators, the resulting numerical scheme will automatically preserve the most important set of stationary solutions, i.e., the lake-at-rest solution. Thus, our numerical method is well-balanced.

equations in

Next, we develop structure-preserving semidiscretizations of these dispersive
shallow water equations in

to obtain a fully-discrete entropy-conservative scheme. We present several

4

Finally, we summarize our work in
Section

5
and give an outlook
on future work.

2
Dispersive shallow water models

This section provides a review of results about two different systems of nonlinear dispersive wave equations. Both base on the shallow water equations. Therefore, we first collect some classical results about the shallow water equations.

The shallow water equations are often used to model water waves with a long wavelength compared to the water depth and serve as a baseline model for this work. For simplicity, we only look at one space dimension
x\in\mathbb{R}
and periodic boundary conditions. The shallow water equations can be written in conservative form as (
1.1a
), (
1.1b
).

The water height and the momentum (or discharge)
P=hv
form the vector of conserved variables
\bm{u}=\begin{pmatrix}h\\
P\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{U}=\left\{(u_{1},u_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,u_{1}>0\right\}
. Written as a balance law

\bm{u}_{t}+\bm{f}(\bm{u})_{x}=-\bm{g}(\bm{u},z)z_{x}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bm{s}(\bm{u},z),

(2.1)

the flux function
\bm{f}
and the source term
\bm{s}
for the shallow water equations are given by

\bm{f}(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}hv\\
\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}+hv^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}P\\
\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}+\frac{P^{2}}{h}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{g}(\bm{u},z)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
h\end{pmatrix},\quad z(x)=gb(x).

For smooth solutions
\bm{u}
, the equations can also equivalently be written in primitive variables
\bm{q}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
v\end{pmatrix}
consisting of the total water height
\eta=h+b
and the velocity
v
:

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+((\eta+D-\eta_{0})v)_{x}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.2a)

\displaystyle v_{t}+g\eta_{x}+vv_{x}

\displaystyle=0.

(2.2b)

In the primitive formulation, we usually work with the
still water depth

D=\eta_{0}-b
for a reference water height
\eta_{0}
. From (
1.1a
), we can see that for some spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
with periodic boundary conditions, the total mass

\mathcal{M}(t;\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}h\textrm{d}x

is conserved over time, i.e.
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\mathcal{M}(t;\bm{u})=0
. In addition, the total discharge

\mathcal{I}(t;\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}P\textrm{d}x

is a conserved quantity if the bathymetry is constant. Often, balance laws have an additional conserved, or at least dissipated, nonlinear invariant that, in many cases, is closely related to a physical entropy. Following
[
7
, Chapter 3.1]
, a twice differentiable function
U(\bm{u},z)
is a
(partial) entropy
for a balance law (
2.1
) if there is a
(partial) entropy flux

F(\bm{u},z)
, such that

U_{u}(\bm{u},z)\cdot\bm{f}^{\prime}(\bm{u})=F_{u}(\bm{u},z)\quad\forall\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}.

The entropy pair
(U,F)
then satisfies for any smooth solution
\bm{u}
the scalar balance law

U(\bm{u},z)_{t}+F(\bm{u},z)_{x}=-mz_{x}

(2.3)

for
m(\bm{u},z)=\bm{w}(\bm{u},z)\cdot\bm{g}(\bm{u},z)-F_{z}(\bm{u},z)
. Here,
\bm{w}(\bm{u},z)=\nabla_{u}U(\bm{u},z)
are the
entropy variables
. For general solutions we postulate an entropy inequality

U(\bm{u},z)_{t}+F(\bm{u},z)_{x}\leq-mz_{x}.

For the shallow water equations with bathymetry as source term we have the partial entropy

U(\bm{u},z)=\frac{1}{2}(hv^{2}+gh^{2})+ghb=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{P^{2}}{h}+gh^{2}\right)+hz

(2.4)

and the partial entropy flux

F(\bm{u},z)=\frac{1}{2}hv^{3}+gh^{2}v+gbhv=\frac{1}{2}\frac{P^{3}}{h^{2}}+ghP+Pz.

(2.5)

In this case
m
simply vanishes, which, by (
2.3
), yields the conservation of the total entropy

\mathcal{E}(t;\bm{u},z)=\int_{\Omega}U(\bm{u},z)\textrm{d}x.

For the shallow water equations, the entropy function
U
also corresponds to the physical total (i.e., potential plus kinetic) energy. In primitive variables, the entropy function is usually written as

U(\bm{q},z)=\frac{1}{2}(g\eta^{2}+(\eta+D-\eta_{0})v^{2}),

(2.6)

which differs from the entropy (
2.4
) in the constant term
\frac{1}{2}gb^{2}
. This term is neglected since we are usually only interested in the change of energy. In the following, we will drop the explicit dependency of the entropy function on the bathymetry and write, e.g.,
U(\bm{u})
instead of
U(\bm{u},z)
.

Another typical property of balance laws in general, and of the shallow water equations in particular, is the existence of non-trivial steady-state solutions, i.e., solutions that are constant in time, see e.g.
[
7
, p. 67]
. This is the case if the flux derivative and the source term exactly balance each other. The most physically relevant steady solution is the special
lake-at-rest
solution, where
v=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle(h+b)_{x}

\displaystyle=0.

These special solutions of the shallow water equations, in which the flow is steady, are
often of particular interest since most of the domain is governed by small perturbations
in the water height in many practical cases. Moreover, very often a realistic simulation
converges to a steady-state when
t\to\infty
, e.g.
[
38
]
.

2.1
BBM-BBM equations with variable topography

The BBM-BBM equations are a coupled variant of the BBM (Benjamin–Bona–Mahony) equation and have been studied, e.g. in
[
5
]
. As common in the literature about the BBM-BBM equations
[
30
,
43
,
29
]

\eta_{0}=0

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+((\eta+D)v)_{x}-\frac{1}{6}D^{2}\eta_{xxt}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.7a)

\displaystyle v_{t}+g\eta_{x}+vv_{x}-\frac{1}{6}D^{2}v_{xxt}

\displaystyle=0.

(2.7b)

Compared to the shallow water equations written in primitive variables (
2.2a
), (
2.2b
), the BBM-BBM equations differ only in the additional dispersive terms containing the mixed third derivatives. Well-posedness results for the BBM-BBM equations are obtained in
[
5
]
and later more generally in
[
6
]
. An analytical soliton solution with constant speed
c=\frac{5}{2}\sqrt{gD}

\rho=\frac{18}{5}

has been derived in
[
12

for the dimensionless case and reads for

as

\displaystyle\eta(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{15}{4}D\left(2\sech^{2}(\theta)-3\sech^{4}(\theta)\right),

(2.8a)

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{15}{2}\sqrt{gD}\sech^{2}(\theta).

(2.8b)

Some works extend the classical BBM-BBM equations to a varying bathymetry, see e.g.
[
44
,
30
,
28
,
29
]
. Here, we focus on the model

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+((\eta+D)v)_{x}-\frac{1}{6}(D^{2}\eta_{xt})_{x}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle v_{t}+g\eta_{x}+vv_{x}-\frac{1}{6}(D^{2}v_{t})_{xx}

\displaystyle=0

(2.9b)

analyzed in two-dimensional form in
[

,
29
]
. The equations can also be seen as a simplification of the model from
[

2.7a

2.7b

[
28

Defining the operators
F_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\eta+D)v-\frac{1}{6}D^{2}\eta_{xt}
and
F_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=g\eta+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-\frac{1}{6}(D^{2}v_{t})_{x}
, the BBM-BBM equations can be written in conservation form:

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+(F_{1})_{x}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle v_{t}+(F_{2})_{x}

\displaystyle=0.

Written in this form, one can immediately see that the total mass

\mathcal{M}(t;\eta)=\int_{\Omega}\eta\textrm{d}x

and the total velocity

\mathcal{V}(t;v)=\int_{\Omega}v\textrm{d}x

are invariants of the BBM-BBM equations with periodic boundary conditions.
In particular, since the equations are written in primitive variables, it is not the total momentum (for constant bathymetry) that is conserved, but the total velocity.
Contrary to many other dispersive shallow water models, the BBM-BBM equations preserve the same energy

\mathcal{E}(t;\bm{q})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}g\eta^{2}+(\eta+D)v^{2}\textrm{d}x

2.2
Dispersive model by Svärd and Kalisch

Recently, a new dispersive shallow water model was presented in

[
65
]
. In a first step, they presented a new energy-bounded
model for a constant bathymetry using general dispersive terms including

2.11

Finally, the authors of
[
65
]
generalized the model to a variable topography by choosing the coefficients such that the influence of the dispersive terms decreases in shallower water.

For constant

[
65

propose the model

\displaystyle h_{t}+(hv)_{x}

\displaystyle=\alpha(h+b)_{xxx},

(2.12a)

2.2

2.2
Svärd and Kalisch (2023).

Any smooth solution

[

(2.16)

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=g((h+b)\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}g(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)^{2}_{x})_{x}+\frac{1}{2}(v^{2}\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x},

\displaystyle M_{\beta}

\displaystyle=(\hat{\beta}vv_{xt})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\beta}v_{x}^{2})_{t},

\displaystyle M_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((v(\hat{\gamma}v_{x})_{x})_{x}+(v\hat{\gamma}v_{xx})_{x}-(\hat{\gamma}v_{x}^{2})_{x}\right).

Proof.

We present the proof based on
[
65
]
for completeness
since it also guides the construction of entropy-preserving numerical methods.
Because
U
is an entropy of the shallow water equations with entropy flux
F
, from (

z(x)=x
, we are only left to show
m(\bm{u})=\bm{w}(\bm{u})\cdot\bm{s}(\bm{u})=M_{\alpha}+M_{\beta}+M_{\gamma}
for the entropy variables

\bm{w}(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{v^{2}}{2}+g(h+b)\\
v\end{pmatrix}

and the dispersive terms

\bm{s}(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}\\
(\hat{\alpha}v(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}+(\hat{\beta}v_{x})_{xt}+\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\gamma}v_{x})_{xx}+\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\gamma}v_{xx})_{x}\end{pmatrix}.

We start by collecting all terms that contain the coefficient
\hat{\alpha}

g(h+b)(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}+v(\hat{\alpha}v(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}.

(2.17)

By applying the product rule in the first step and the chain rule in the second step, the first term becomes

\displaystyle g(h+b)(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}

\displaystyle=g((h+b)\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}-g(h+b)_{x}\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x}

\displaystyle=g((h+b)\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}g(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x}^{2})_{x},

i.e. the first two terms in
M_{\alpha}
above. The remaining two terms in (

M_{\alpha}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}+v(\hat{\alpha}v(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(v^{2}\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}+vv_{x}\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x}\,+

\displaystyle(v\hat{\alpha}v(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}-v_{x}v\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}v^{2}(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x})_{x}.

Continuing with the

Finally, the terms containing

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}v(\hat{\gamma}v_{x})_{xx}+\frac{1}{2}v(\hat{\gamma}v_{xx})_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}((v(\hat{\gamma}v_{x})_{x})_{x}-v_{x}(\hat{\gamma}v_{x})_{x}+(v\hat{\gamma}v_{xx})_{x}-v_{x}\hat{\gamma}v_{xx})

In total, it therefore yields

The relation (

(2.18)

which we call

in the following. The corresponding

\hat{F}(\bm{u})=F(\bm{u})-F_{\alpha}(\bm{u})-F_{\beta}(\bm{u})-F_{\gamma}(\bm{u})

consists of the entropy flux for the shallow water equations
F
and additional terms that stem from the dispersive terms. The dispersive contributions to the modified entropy flux take the form

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=g(h+b)(\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b))_{x})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}g\hat{\alpha}(h+b)^{2}_{x}+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\alpha}(h+b)_{x})_{x},

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\hat{\beta}vv_{xt},

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(v(\hat{\gamma}v_{x})_{x}+v\hat{\gamma}v_{xx}-\hat{\gamma}v_{x}^{2}\right).

We note that the modified entropy and entropy flux include derivatives and therefore take the form of operators. Due to
theorem

2.2

\hat{U}(\bm{u})_{t}+\hat{F}(\bm{u})_{x}=0.

As an immediate consequence, the

\hat{\mathcal{E}}(t;\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}\hat{U}(\bm{u})\textrm{d}x

(2.19)

is conserved for smooth solutions
\bm{u}
and periodic boundary conditions. Let us assume
\tilde{\beta}\geq 0
and therefore

This entropy bound can be seen in analogy to an entropy inequality and can be used as a stability criterion, especially for numerical methods.

Although a linear stability analysis has been performed in

]

), (
2.14b
).

3
Structure-preserving discretizations

In this section, we first review the concept of summation by parts operators.
Afterwards, we develop new structure-preserving methods for the BBM-BBM
equations and the model by Svärd and Kalisch.

3.1
Review of summation by parts operators

In recent years, summation by parts operators have gained particular interest as they allow to transfer analytical results on the continuous level to numerical methods. This is done by mimicking integration by parts discretely, which is one of the key ingredients at the continuous level. In this way, many analytical properties can be obtained in a straightforward manner.

SBP operators were first developed for finite difference methods

[
34
,
64
,
42
]

to mimic stability proofs based on integration by parts as used in
finite element methods. However, exact integration can be impossible or
expensive in finite element methods. In this case, SBP formulations can be
advantageous since they include a quadrature rule.
In particular, split forms
[
19
]
can be used with
SBP operators to avoid the need for exact integration and the lack of
discrete chain and product rules
[
51
]
.
Several classes of numerical methods can be formulated via SBP oeprators,
including finite volume methods
[
45
,
46
]
,
continuous Galerkin methods
[
26
,
25
,
1
]
,
discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods
[
22
,
9
,
11
]
,
and flux reconstruction methods
[
27
,
58
]
.
Further references are given in the review articles

[
66
,
18
,
13
]
.

The definitions and results presented in this chapter are mainly based on the formulations used in
[
50
]
.
In the following, multiplication of discrete functions
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
is performed component-wise
(\bm{u}\bm{v})_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
and similarly
(\bm{u}^{k})_{i}=u_{i}^{k}
. We also define
\bm{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}
,
\bm{0}=(0,\ldots,0)^{T}
,
\bm{e}_{L}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
, and
\bm{e}_{R}=(0,\ldots,0,1)^{T}
.

Summation by parts operators describe
i
-th derivatives
\frac{\textrm{d}^{i}}{\textrm{d}x^{i}}
by a
derivative matrix

D_{i}
and integration by a
mass matrix
(or
norm matrix
)
M
.

3.1
.

Given a grid
\bm{x}
, a
p
-th order accurate
i
-th derivative matrix

D_{i}

[
50
, Definition 2.1]
is a matrix that satisfies for all
k=0,\ldots,p

D_{i}\bm{x}^{k}=k(k-1)\ldots(k-i+1)\bm{x}^{k-i}.

The derivative matrix
D_{i}
is called
consistent
if
p\geq 0
.

A consistent
i
-th derivative matrix satisfies
D_{i}\bm{1}=\bm{0}
.

3.2
.

A
first-derivative summation by parts (SBP) operator

[
50
, Definition 2.2]
consists of a grid
\bm{x}
, a consistent first-derivative matrix
D_{1}
and a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
, such that

MD_{1}+D_{1}^{T}M=\bm{e}_{R}\bm{e}_{R}^{T}-\bm{e}_{L}\bm{e}_{L}^{T}.

(3.1)

A
periodic first-derivative SBP operator

[
50
, Definition 2.3]
consists of a grid
\bm{x}
, a consistent first-derivative matrix
D_{1}
and a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
, such that

MD_{1}+D_{1}^{T}M=0.

(3.2)

Sometimes we also refer to the derivative matrix
D_{i}
as an SBP operator.

3.3
.

A first-derivative SBP operator mimics integration by parts, which can be seen by multiplying (
3.1
) by
\bm{u}^{T}
from the left and by
\bm{v}
from the right:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}MD_{1}\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\approx$}}}&+&\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}D_{1}^{T}M\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\approx$}}}&=&\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}\bm{e}_{R}\bm{e}_{R}^{T}\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$=$}}}&-&\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}\bm{e}_{L}\bm{e}_{L}^{T}\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$=$}}}\\
\overbrace{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}uv_{x}\textrm{d}x}&+&\overbrace{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{x}v\textrm{d}x}&=&\overbrace{u(x_{\text{max}})v(x_{\text{max}})}&-&\overbrace{u(x_{\text{min}})v(x_{\text{min}})}.\end{array}

Introducing the short-hand notation
\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{M}=\bm{u}^{T}M\bm{v}
for the inner product that is induced by
M
, we can see that a periodic first-derivative SBP operator satisfies

\langle D_{1}\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{M}=-\langle\bm{u},D_{1}\bm{v}\rangle_{M}.

The symmetry of
M
yields
\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{M}=\langle\bm{v},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}
and from the positive-definiteness it follows
\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\geq 0
and
\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}=0\Leftrightarrow\bm{u}=\bm{0}
.
In
[
41
]
so-called upwind operators are introduced that are commonly used to allow entropy-dissipative numerical methods as we will see later.

3.4
.

A
first-derivative upwind SBP operator

[
50
, Definition 2.4]
consists of a grid
\bm{x}
, consistent first-derivative matrices
D_{1,\pm}
and a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
, such that

MD_{1,+}+D_{1,-}^{T}M=\bm{e}_{R}^{T}\bm{e}_{R}-\bm{e}_{L}^{T}\bm{e}_{L}

and
\frac{1}{2}M(D_{1,+}-D_{1,-})
being negative semidefinite.

A
periodic first-derivative upwind SBP operator

[
50
, Definition 2.5]
consists of a grid
\bm{x}
, consistent first-derivative matrices
D_{1,\pm}
and a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
, such that

MD_{1,+}+D_{1,-}^{T}M=0

and
\frac{1}{2}M(D_{1,+}-D_{1,-})
being negative semidefinite.

In terms of inner products, periodic upwind operators satisfy

\langle D_{1,+}\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{M}=-\langle\bm{u},D_{1,-}\bm{v}\rangle_{M}.

A (central) first-derivative SBP operator can be constructed from first-derivative upwind SBP operators by defining
D_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(D_{1,+}+D_{1,-})
.

3.5
.

A
second-derivative SBP operator

[
50
, Definition 2.6]
consists of a grid
\bm{x}
, a consistent second-derivative matrix
D_{2}
, a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
and derivative vectors
\bm{d}_{L}
,
\bm{d}_{R}
approximating the evaluation of the first derivative at the left/right endpoint as
\bm{d}_{L/R}^{T}\bm{u}\approx u^{\prime}(x_{\text{min}/\text{max}})
, such that

MD_{2}=D_{2}^{T}M+\bm{e}_{R}\bm{d}_{R}^{T}-\bm{e}_{L}\bm{d}_{L}^{T}-\bm{d}_{R}\bm{e}_{R}^{T}+\bm{d}_{L}\bm{e}_{L}^{T}.

A
periodic second-derivative SBP operator

[
50
, Definition 2.7]
consists of a grid
\bm{x}
, a consistent second-derivative matrix
D_{2}
and a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
, such that

MD_{2}=D_{2}^{T}M.

(3.3)

For periodic second-derivative SBP operators we can write
\langle D_{2}\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{M}=\langle\bm{u},D_{2}\bm{v}\rangle_{M}
.

3.6
.

As in the case of first-derivative SBP operators second-derivative SBP operators mimic integration by parts via

\footnotesize\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}MD_{2}\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\approx$}}}&=&\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}D_{2}^{T}M\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\approx$}}}&+&\underbrace{\bm{u}^{T}\bm{e}_{R}\bm{d}_{R}^{T}\bm{v}-\bm{u}^{T}\bm{e}_{L}\bm{d}_{L}^{T}\bm{v}-\bm{u}^{T}\bm{d}_{R}\bm{e}_{R}^{T}\bm{v}+\bm{u}^{T}\bm{d}_{L}\bm{e}_{L}^{T}\bm{v}}_{\mathrel{\rotatebox{90.0}{$=$}}}\\
\overbrace{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}uv_{xx}\textrm{d}x}&=&\overbrace{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{xx}v\textrm{d}x}&+&\overbrace{u(x_{\text{max}})v^{\prime}(x_{\text{max}})-u(x_{\text{min}})v^{\prime}(x_{\text{min}})-u^{\prime}(x_{\text{max}})v(x_{\text{max}})+u^{\prime}(x_{\text{min}})v(x_{\text{min}})}.\end{array}

SBP operators of higher derivative order can be defined similarly, see e.g.
[
40
]
.

3.7
.

SBP operators can be interpreted as classical finite difference methods, where the mass matrix of the periodic operators on a regular grid is given by
M=\Delta xI
. Spectral finite difference methods
[
10
]
can be obtained by using a Lobatto-Legendre basis for polynomials of degree
p
. Here, the grid is non-uniform and given by the Lobatto-Legendre quadrature nodes and the mass matrix is given as the diagonal matrix
M=\diag(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{p})
, where
\omega_{i}
are the Lobatto-Legendre quadrature weights.

Moreover, instead of globally applying the SBP operator, we can also obtain finite element schemes, where the operators act locally. For the finite element method we divide the domain into elements. On each element, we consider a grid and a corresponding SBP operator. Across the elements, the local SBP operators can be coupled in a discontinuous or a continuous way yielding the nodal discontinuous and the nodal continuous Galerkin method, respectively. Using SBP operators we can, e.g., formulate the discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method (DGSEM)
[
20
]
. For more details on how the coupling works, see e.g.
[
50
]
and references therein.

Many methods involving SBP operators use a diagonal norm matrix
M
, which we often assume implicitly. In this case, we have
\langle\bm{u}\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{M}=\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\bm{w}\rangle_{M}
.

Let us collect some useful standard properties of SBP operators in the following lemma.

3.8
.

Periodic first- and second-derivative SBP operators
D_{i}
,
i=1,2
and periodic upwind SBP operators
D_{1,+}
,
D_{1,-}
satisfy

1.

\bm{1}^{T}MD_{i}=\bm{0}^{T}
and
\bm{1}^{T}MD_{1,\pm}=\bm{0}^{T}
,
[
50
, Lemma 2.1]

2.

\langle D_{1}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}=0
,

3.

\langle D_{1,-}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\geq 0
and
\langle D_{1,+}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\leq 0
.

Proof.

1.

Using (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and the consistency of the derivative matrices, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{1}^{T}MD_{1}

\displaystyle=-\bm{1}^{T}D_{1}^{T}M=-(D_{1}\bm{1})^{T}M=\bm{0}^{T},

\displaystyle\bm{1}^{T}MD_{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}^{T}D_{2}^{T}M=(D_{2}\bm{1})^{T}M=\bm{0}^{T},

\displaystyle\bm{1}^{T}MD_{1,\pm}

\displaystyle=-\bm{1}^{T}D_{1,\mp}^{T}M=-(D_{1,\mp}\bm{1})^{T}M=\bm{0}^{T}.

2.

Symmetry of
M
yields

\langle D_{1}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}=-\langle\bm{u},D_{1}\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\Rightarrow 2\langle D_{1}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}=0.

3.

From the negative-definiteness of
\frac{1}{2}M(D_{1,+}-D_{1,-})
we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{u},(D_{1,+}-D_{1,-})\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\leq 0\Rightarrow\langle\bm{u},D_{1,+}\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\leq\langle\bm{u},D_{1,-}\bm{u}\rangle_{M}

and therefore

\langle D_{1,-}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}=-\langle\bm{u},D_{1,+}\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\geq-\langle\bm{u},D_{1,-}\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\Rightarrow 2\langle D_{1,-}\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{M}\geq 0.

The other inequality follows in the same way.

∎

3.2
Semidiscretization of the BBM-BBM equations with variable topography

An entropy-conservative discretization of the BBM-BBM equations with constant bathymetry based on SBP operators has already been performed in
[

]
, and also analyzed in
[

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\left(I-\frac{1}{6}D^{2}D_{2}\right)^{-1}D_{1}(D\bm{v}+\bm{\eta}\bm{v}),

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\left(I-\frac{1}{6}D^{2}D_{2}\right)^{-1}D_{1}\left(g\bm{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{v}^{2}\right).

(3.4b)

For further numerical approximations of the BBM-BBM equations with constant bottom topography see, e.g.,
[

,

]
.
For simplicity, we only consider periodic boundary conditions. In this contribution, we generalize the semidiscretization to the case of a variable topography, while ensuring entropy-conservation.

In
[
28
]
, the authors use a finite element discretization of (

), (
2.9b
) that provably converges to the exact solution, but the proposed method does not conserve the energy exactly. In the following, we put forward an energy-conserving method based on SBP operators. To do so, we define the diagonal matrix

K=\diag(\bm{D}^{2})=\begin{pmatrix}D^{2}(x_{1})&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&D^{2}(x_{N})\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})^{T}
is the grid of the involved SBP operators. Then, the semidiscretization is formulated as

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{t}+\left(I-\frac{1}{6}D_{1}KD_{1}\right)^{-1}D_{1}(\bm{D}\bm{v}+\bm{\eta}\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{t}+\left(I-\frac{1}{6}D_{2}K\right)^{-1}D_{1}\left(g\bm{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{v}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

(3.5b)

Particularly, for the case of

being constant (resulting in

), the semidiscretization coincides with (
3.4a
), (
3.4b
) taking

in the first equation. Due to the more complex structure of the elliptic operators compared to the case of a constant bathymetry, an analogous version of the proof in
[

(specifically Lemma 2.2 and Lemma 2.3) does not hold anymore. Thus, we turn back to mimicking the continuous form of the energy-conservation proof on a discrete level. This requires to take
D_{2}=D_{1}^{2}
. The semidiscretization (
3.5a
), (
3.5b
) under the condition

can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{t}+D_{1}\underbrace{\left(\bm{D}\bm{v}+\bm{\eta}\bm{v}-\frac{1}{6}KD_{1}\bm{\eta}_{t}\right)}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bm{F_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{t}+D_{1}\underbrace{\left(g\bm{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{v}^{2}-\frac{1}{6}D_{1}K\bm{v}_{t}\right)}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bm{F_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

(3.6b)

This version of the semidiscretization mimics the conservation form of the BBM-BBM equations and can be utilized to prove the energy-conservation of the numerical scheme. In the context of SBP operators, the discrete total entropy is given by

\mathcal{E}(t;\bm{\eta},\bm{v})=\bm{1}^{T}M\bm{U}

(3.7)

for the discrete entropy (cf. (

\bm{U}(\bm{\eta},\bm{v})=\frac{1}{2}g\bm{\eta}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\eta}+\bm{D})\bm{v}^{2}.

Analogously, the discrete total mass and velocity are

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t;\bm{\eta})=\bm{1}^{T}M\bm{\eta},

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t;\bm{v})=\bm{1}^{T}M\bm{v}.

(3.8b)

3.9
.

If
D_{1}
is a periodic first-derivative SBP operator and
D_{2}
is a periodic second-derivative SBP operator, then the semidiscretization (
3.5a
), (

\mathcal{M}
(

(

\mathcal{E}
(

Proof.

The conservation of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{V}
follows immediately by
lemma

3.8
item 1:

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\mathcal{M}(t;\bm{\eta})=\bm{1}^{T}M\bm{\eta}_{t}=-\bm{1}^{T}MD_{1}\bm{F}_{1}=0

and similarly for
\mathcal{V}
.

Because
K
and
M
are diagonal, they commute and therefore we have:

\langle\bm{\eta}_{t},D_{1}K\bm{v}_{t}\rangle_{M}=-\langle KD_{1}\bm{\eta}_{t},\bm{v}_{t}\rangle_{M}.

Using this together with (
3.6a

3.6b

3.4
Relaxation method

For the structure-preserving time integration, we focus on the relaxation method based on explicit Runge-Kutta methods
[
31
,
59
]
. A general
s
-step Runge-Kutta method for an ordinary differential equation
u^{\prime}(t)=f(t,u(t))
with a right-hand side
f
, that potentially explicitly depends on time, can be written as

\displaystyle u^{n+1}

\displaystyle=u^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}k_{i},

(3.13a)

\displaystyle k_{i}

\displaystyle=f\left(t_{n}+c_{i}\Delta t,u^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}k_{j}\right),\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,s\},

(3.13b)

where
k_{i}
are the slopes,
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1\ldots s}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
a matrix and
\bm{b}=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots s}\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\bm{c}=(c_{i})_{i=1\ldots s}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
are vectors. All weights are assumed to be non-negative. We also define the
stage values

y_{i}
as

y_{i}=u^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}k_{j}.

As we have seen in the previous sections, we obtain semidiscretizations of the form
u^{\prime}=f(u)
that conserve some nonlinear, convex and differentiable invariant
J(u)
for all solutions
u
:

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}J(u)=0\leq 0\Leftrightarrow J^{\prime}(u)\cdot f(u)=0,

which we, usually, identify by the total entropy
\mathcal{E}
. A
conservative
time-stepping scheme satisfies the discrete analog

J(u^{n+1})=J(u^{n})=J(u^{0}).

Similarly, one can look at dissipative semidiscretizations, where a nonlinear quantity
J(u)
is dissipated by the ODE, i.e.

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}J(u)\leq 0\Leftrightarrow J^{\prime}(u)\cdot f(u)\leq 0.

(3.14)

On a discrete level, this means a
dissipative
(or
monotone
) method satisfies

J(u^{n+1})\leq J(u^{n}).

A dissipative method is also called
strongly stable

[
52
, Definition 2.2]
.

To achieve the energy conservation or dissipation for the fully-discrete scheme, relaxation methods have been introduced first in
[
31
]
for
J(\bm{u})=\|\bm{u}\|^{2}
and later in
[
59
,
56
]
for general
J
. The idea is to start with a Runge-Kutta method but replace the update step (
3.13a
) by

u^{n+1}_{\gamma}=u^{n}+\gamma_{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}k_{i},

where the new parameter
\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
is determined by solving the equation

J(u^{n+1}_{\gamma})-J(u^{n})=\gamma_{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}J^{\prime}(y_{i})\cdot k_{i}.

(3.15)

Here,
u^{n+1}_{\gamma}
can be understood as an approximation of
u(t+\Delta t)
by scaling the weights
b_{i}
with
\gamma_{n}
(known as
incremental direction technique
(IDT)) or as an approximation of
u(t+\gamma_{n}\Delta t)
by scaling the time step
\Delta t
by
\gamma_{n}
(
relaxation Runge-Kutta
(RRK) method), cf.
[
31
, Section 2.2]
. We mainly focus on the RRK method. Solving (
3.15
) corresponds to finding a root of the scalar function

r(\gamma)=J\left(u^{n}+\gamma\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}k_{i}\right)-J(u^{n})-\gamma\Delta te,\quad e=\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}J^{\prime}(y_{i})\cdot k_{i},

(3.16)

which can be done by some standard root finding algorithm, or in some cases analytically. By choosing
\gamma_{n}
like this, it is guaranteed
by construction
that
J(u^{n+1}_{\gamma})\leq J(u^{n})
for a dissipative ODE because
k_{i}=f(y_{i})
, equation (
3.14
), and the assumption
b_{i}\geq 0
gives
e\leq 0
. For a conservative ODE,
e
vanishes, (
3.15
) reduces to
J(u^{n+1}_{\gamma})=J(u^{n})
, and discrete conservation over time is established. All linear invariants, i.e.
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{V}
or
\mathcal{I}
from the previous chapters, are also conserved by the relaxation approach
[
59
]
.

From (
3.15
), we can easily see that
u^{n+1}_{\gamma}
can be seen as a linear combination with parameter
\gamma_{n}
between the old value
u^{\text{old}}=u^{n}
and the new value
u^{\text{new}}=u^{n+1}
computed by the original Runge-Kutta method (
3.13a
), i.e.

u^{n+1}_{\gamma}=u^{\text{old}}+\gamma_{n}(u^{\text{new}}-u^{\text{old}}).

(3.17)

Similarly, the time
t^{n+1}_{\gamma}=t+\gamma_{n}\Delta t
after performing the RRK step is given by a linear combination between the old time
t^{\text{old}}=t
and the new time
t^{\text{new}}=t+\Delta t
:

t^{n+1}_{\gamma}=t^{\text{old}}+\gamma_{n}(t^{\text{new}}-t^{\text{old}}).

(3.18)

Lastly, solving (
3.15
) can be seen as solving

J(u^{n+1}_{\gamma})=J^{\text{old}}+\gamma_{n}(J^{\text{new}}-J^{\text{old}})

(3.19)

for
\gamma_{n}
, where
J^{\text{old}}=J(u^{\text{old}})
and

J^{\text{new}}=J^{\text{old}}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}J^{\prime}(y_{i})\cdot k_{i}.

Thus, an implementation of the RRK method can conveniently build on an existing implementation of a Runge-Kutta method by finding a root of
r
after the usual Runge-Kutta step computed
u^{n+1}
by (
3.13a
) and finally setting the new time and the new value according to (
3.18
) and (
3.17
).

3.13

Using the formulation given by (

5
Conclusions

In order to develop structure-preserving numerical methods, this article follows the approach to
mimic the mathematical tools, used on the continuous level, discretely. This is reached
by using summation by parts operators that mimic differentiation and integration in a
way such that a discrete analog of the integration by parts formula is satisfied. Split
forms are used to replicate the product and chain rules. By these means, the
partial differential equations have been reduced to semidiscretizations satisfying the same
physical structures. The relaxation method is used when solving the resulting ordinary
differential equations so that the structures are not destroyed by the time integration.

Particularly, a more detailed look has been taken into two models, the BBM-BBM equations with varying bottom topography
[
28
,
29
]
and the equations introduced by Svärd and Kalisch in
[
65
]
. Both models are founded on the shallow water equations and extend them by adding additional dispersive terms. These dispersive terms include higher-order derivatives and have to be taken into account carefully. Although both models share some similarities with the shallow water equations, the dispersive behavior of solutions to the equations can lead to different physical properties. While the BBM-BBM equations conserve the same total entropy function as the shallow water equations, the model by Svärd and Kalisch only conserves a modified entropy that also includes dispersive contributions. In
section

3
, new semidiscretizations for both equations have been presented that base on SBP operators and provably conserve the entropy for the BBM-BBM equations and the modified entropy for the Svärd-Kalisch equations, respectively (cf.
theorem

3.9
and
theorem

3.11
). For the equations of Svärd and Kalisch an entropy-stable modification has been introduced. In addition to fulfilling an entropy condition, the presented schemes conserve all linear invariants and preserve the lake-at-rest steady solution, i.e., they are well-balanced.

Using semidiscretizations based on SBP operators has several advantages. Firstly, it closely follows the mathematical analysis on the continuous level and, therefore, structure-preserving schemes can be obtained easily. Secondly, SBP operators allow a quite general framework that can be used in different contexts and leave some choices for customization. They can be used as a classical finite difference method, but by coupling SBP operators across elements they can also be formulated as either continuous or discontinuous Galerkin method. Lastly, numerical methods of arbitrary order in space can easily be obtained by choosing SBP operators of according order.

The results obtained in
section

3
have been verified in
section

4
. Test cases that compare the different presented models and solvers have been simulated. It has been demonstrated that for high wavenumbers the dispersive effects are captured by the model of Svärd and Kalisch more accurately than by the BBM-BBM equations, which is in alignment with the results on the dispersion relations of the two models presented in
section

2
. The solution to the Svärd-Kalisch equations for a standard benchmark problem
[
14
]
proved to be remarkably accurate, particularly for the upwind scheme. Numerical results also underline that using fully-discrete entropy-conserving discretizations can be essential to obtain accurate numerical solutions, especially in long-term simulations.

For practical applications, it would be necessary to analyze the equations formulated in two spatial dimensions, including the development of entropy-stable numerical discretizations for two dimensions. The two-dimensional case has already been analyzed for the BBM-BBM equations in
[
28
,
29
]
and first results for the equations by Svärd and Kalisch are given in
[
65
]
. Future research should focus on the development of structure-preserving generalizations of the presented schemes to the two-dimensional equations. Finally, different boundary conditions than periodic ones (e.g., reflecting (wall) boundary conditions) should be considered, especially for the two-dimensional case. In that case, boundary terms need to be considered explicitly in the semidiscretization. This can, e.g, be done similarly to
[
50
]
, for reflecting boundary conditions.
