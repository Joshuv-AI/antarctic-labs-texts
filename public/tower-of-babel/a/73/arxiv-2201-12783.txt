On a problem of Romanoff type
Yuchen Ding

Abstract
. Let 𝒫 \mathcal{P} and ℕ \mathbb{N} be the sets of all primes and natural numbers, respectively. In this article, it is proved that there is a positive lower density of the natural numbers which can be represented by the form p + 2 m 1 2 + 2 m 2 2 ​ ( p ∈ 𝒫 , m 1 , m 2 ∈ ℕ ) . p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}~~(p\in\mathcal{P},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}). This solves a problem of Chen and Yang in 2014.

1.
Introduction

In 1849, de Polignac
[
28
]
conjectured that every odd number greater than 1 is the sum of a prime and a power of 2. However, de Polignac
[
29
]
himself soon recognized that 127 and 959 are two counterexamples. Actually, he pointed out that Euler had already found the same counterexamples many years ago. Later, de Polignac’s report aroused great interest to Romanoff. A remarkable theorem of Romanoff
[
30
]
states that the odd numbers which can be represented by the form

p+2^{m}~(p\in\mathcal{P},m\in\mathbb{N})

have a positive lower density. In the opposite direction, van der Corput
[
9
]
proved that the odd numbers greater than 1 which can not be represented by the form
p+2^{m}
with
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
still possess a positive lower density of all the natural numbers. In 1950, answering a question of Romanoff, Erdős
[
15
]
constructed a specific arithmetic progression, none of which can be written as the sum of a prime and a power of 2. This is surprising since it is easy to see that van der Corput’s result follows immediately from the one of Erdős.

The quantitative version of Romanoff’s theorem was first considered by Chen and Sun
[
8
]
, who showed that the lower density is larger than 0.0868. There are a few improvements of this lower density, see
[
13
,
21
,
27
]
. In the opposite direction as pointed by Chen and Sun, Erdős’ construction
[
15
]
illustrated that the lower density is no larger than 0.49999991. Suggested by Bombieri, Pintz
[
27
]
had conjectured that there is a constant
c_{0}
(=0.434…) such that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\{n:n\leqslant x,~n=p+2^{m},~m\in\mathbb{N},~p\in\mathcal{P}\}}{x}=c_{0}.

Recently, Del Corso et al.
[
12
]
thought that
0.437...
is another probable candidate of the constant
c_{0}
.
There are a huge number of variants of Romanoff’s theorem and the readers may refer to
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
14
,
17
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
.

Let
x
be a large number. Define

V=\left\{n:n=p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}},p\in\mathcal{P},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and
V(x)=|V\cap[1,x]|
.
In 2014, Chen and Yang
[
7
]
proved that

V(x)\gg\frac{x}{\log\log x}

and posed the following problem for further research.

Problem 1

(Chen–Yang)
.

Does there exist a positive integer
k
such that the set of positive integers which can be represented as
p+\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{m_{i}^{2}}
with
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
has a positive lower density? If such
k
exists, what is the minimal value of such
k
.

Certainly, the problem of Chen and Yang is still a new variant of the Romanoff theorem. In this article, we shall prove that
k=2
is admissible, i.e.,
V(x)\gg x
. It is clear that
k=1
does not satisfy the requirement of the problem since

\#\{p+2^{m^{2}}:p\in\mathcal{P},m\in\mathbb{N}\}\ll\frac{x}{\log x}\cdot\sqrt{\log x}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}}.

Thereby the minimal value of
k
is
2
. Thus, we give a complete answer to the above problem. Now, we restate it as the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
V
be defined as above. Then we have
V(x)\gg x.

Remark 1.1
.

Actually, one can prove a slightly stronger result using the same arguments.

V_{g}(x)=\#\left\{n:n\leqslant x,~n=p+g^{m_{1}^{2}}+g^{m_{2}^{2}},p\in\mathcal{P},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\gg_{g}x,

where
g
is a fixed positive integer larger than 1. We do not proceed this general case as the consideration of powers of
2
in Romanoff type problems is a common practice.

From the Theorem
1.1
, it follows clearly that we have the following corollary by the Shnirel’man density (see for example
[
23
]
).

Corollary 1.1
.

There exists an integer
s
such that every sufficiently large integer can be represented by the form

p_{1}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+p_{s}+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+2^{m_{2s}^{2}},

where
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
and
m_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant 2s
.

2.
Some Auxiliary Results

Let
a,m,r,k,d,\ell
and
n
be integers and
y
be a positive number. The order of
a
modulo
d
is the smallest positive integer
\ell
such that

a^{\ell}\equiv 1\ ({\rm{mod}}\ d).

The aim of this section is to establish two propositions which shall be used in the proof of our theorem.

Proposition 2.1
.

The number of the solutions to the congruence equation

z^{2}\equiv a\ ({\rm{mod}}\ m)

is at most
4\sqrt{m}
.

The proof of Proposition
2.1
follows directly from the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
19
, Corollary 0.3.3]
Let
f(z)
be a quadratic polynomial with integer coefficients. If
m
is a positive integer, let
n_{m}(f)
be the number of solutions of
f(z)\equiv 0\ ({\rm{mod}}\ m)
in
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
. Then

n_{m}(f)=\prod_{p}n_{p^{e}}(f),

where the product is taken over all primes, and where
e=e_{p}(m)
is the exponent of
p
in
m
, that is, the largest integer so that
p^{e}
divides
m
.

Let
\Delta
be an integer congruent to
0
or
1
modulo
4
, define

\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if~}\Delta\equiv 1\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 8),\\
-1,&\text{if~}\Delta\equiv 5\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 8),\\
0,&\text{if~}\Delta\equiv 0\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 4).\\
\end{cases}

(2.1)

Lemma 2.2
.

[
19
, Theorem 0.3.4]
Let
f(z)=az^{2}+bz+c
, with discriminant
\Delta=b^{2}-4ac
, and let
p
be a prime number not dividing
a
. If
\Delta\neq 0
, let
\ell\geqslant 0
be the largest integer for which
\Delta=p^{2\ell}\Delta_{0}
with
\Delta_{0}\equiv 0
or
1\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 4)
. Let
e
be a nonnegative integer. If
e>2\ell
, then the following statements are true.

(1) If
\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{p}\right)=1
, then
n_{p^{e}}(f)=2p^{\ell}
.

(2) If
\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{p}\right)=-1
, then
n_{p^{e}}(f)=0
.

(3) If
\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{p}\right)=0
, then

n_{p^{e}}(f)=\begin{cases}p^{\ell},&\text{if~}e=2\ell+1,\\
0,&\text{if~}e>2\ell+1.\end{cases}

On the other hand, if
e<2\ell
is written as
e=2k+r
with
r=0
or
1
, then
n_{p^{e}}(f)=p^{k}
. This equation also holds for all
e\geqslant 0
if
\Delta=0
.

Now we turn back to the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let

m=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot p_{t}^{e_{t}}

be the standard factorization of
m
. Note that
2<\sqrt{p}
for all
p>3
, which means that

n_{p^{e}}(f)<\sqrt{p^{e}}
for
p>3
and
n_{p^{e}}(f)<2\sqrt{p^{e}}
for
p=2,3
from Lemma
2.2
.
Taking
f(z)=z^{2}+a
in Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
gives

n_{m}(f)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}n_{p_{i}^{e_{i}}}(f)<2^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}^{e_{i}}}=4\sqrt{m}.

∎

The second proposition to be established is the following one.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
m
and
a
be two integers and
2\leqslant y\leqslant m
be a positive number. Denoting by
N(y,m;a)
the number of the solutions to the congruence equation

z^{2}\equiv a\ ({\rm{mod}}\ m)

with
1\leqslant z\leqslant y
, then we have

N(y,m;a)\ll y^{2/3}.

Proof.

The proof of the proposition shall be divided into two cases. Suppose first that
m<y^{4/3}
, then we have

N(y,m;a)\leqslant 4\sqrt{m}\ll\sqrt{m}<y^{2/3}

by the Proposition
2.1
.
We now assume
m\geqslant y^{4/3}
. Let
t
be the least integer such that
y<\sqrt{tm}
, which implies that

t=\left\lfloor\frac{y}{\sqrt{m}}\right\rfloor+1\leqslant\frac{y}{\sqrt{m}}+1.

Consider the following
t
short intervals

[1,\sqrt{m}),~[\sqrt{m},\sqrt{2m}),~\cdot\cdot\cdot,~[\sqrt{(t-1)m},\sqrt{tm}).

It can be seen that there exists at most one integer
z
between each interval satisfying the congruence equation

z^{2}\equiv a\ ({\rm{mod}}\ m).

Thus we have

N(y,m;a)\leqslant t\leqslant\frac{y^{2}}{m}+1\ll y^{2/3}.

∎

3.
Proof of The Theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let

r(n)=\#\left\{(p,m_{1},m_{2}):n=p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}},p\in\mathcal{P},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

be the corresponding representation function. In view of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\left(\sum_{n\leqslant x}r(n)\right)^{2}\leqslant V(x)\left(\sum_{n\leqslant x}r^{2}(n)\right).

(3.1)

It is trivial that

\sum_{n\leqslant x}r(n)=\sum_{p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}\leqslant x}1\geqslant\pi\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)\cdot\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{\log(x/3)}{\log 2}}\right\rfloor\cdot\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{\log(x/3)}{\log 2}}\right\rfloor\gg x

(3.2)

from the prime number theorem, where
\pi(x)
is the number of primes below
x
. Hence, it remains to prove
\sum_{n\leqslant x}r^{2}(n)\ll x
. By the definition of
r(n)
, it can be seen that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}r^{2}(n)=\sum_{n\leqslant x}\left(\sum_{n=p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}}1\right)^{2}=\sum_{p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}=q+2^{k_{1}^{2}}+2^{k_{2}^{2}}\leqslant x}1,

(3.3)

where
p,q
are primes and
m,k
are natural numbers to the rest of the proof.
That is to say,
\sum_{n\leqslant x}r^{2}(n)
does not exceed the number of the solutions to the following constraints

p+2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}=q+2^{k_{1}^{2}}+2^{k_{2}^{2}},~p,q\leqslant x,~m_{i},k_{i}\leqslant\sqrt{\log x/\log 2},~i=1,2.

(3.4)

From now on, let

h=2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}.

(3.5)

First we consider the case
h=0
. Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
m_{1}\geqslant k_{1}
. In this case we have

2^{k_{1}^{2}}\left(2^{m_{1}^{2}-k_{1}^{2}}-1\right)=2^{m_{2}^{2}}\left(2^{k_{2}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}}-1\right).

(3.6)

Note that both
2^{m_{1}^{2}-k_{1}^{2}}-1
and
2^{k_{2}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}}-1
are odd numbers if
m_{1}\neq k_{1}
, which means
k_{1}=m_{2}
and
k_{2}=m_{1}
in this situation. Thus we conclude that
h=0
if and only if
\{m_{1},m_{2}\}=\{k_{1},k_{2}\}
.
For
h\neq 0
, let
\pi_{2}(x,h)
be the number of the prime pairs
p
and
q
with
q-p=h
below
x
. It is well known (see for example
[
23
, Theorem 7.3]
) that

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(x,h)\ll\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\prod_{p|h}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).

(3.7)

Therefore from the analysis above and equation (
3.7
), it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}r^{2}(n)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
k_{1}=m_{1},k_{2}=m_{2}\end{subarray}}\pi(x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i},k_{j}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
i,j=1,2\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\prod_{p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).

(3.8)

Still by the prime number theorem, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
k_{1}=m_{1},k_{2}=m_{2}\end{subarray}}\pi(x)\ll x.

(3.9)

So it remains to prove

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i},k_{j}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
i,j=1,2\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\ll\log^{2}x.

(3.10)

Since the factor
1+\frac{1}{p}
for
p=2
is bounded by
\frac{3}{2}
which can be neglected, we need only to consider odd primes
p
in the equation (
3.10
). Following an idea of Elsholtz et al.
[
14
]
, we split the product into the following two parts:

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\\
2<p<\log x\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)~~~~\text{and}~~~~\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\\
p\geqslant\log x\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).

(3.11)

Note that
2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}<2x
, we know that the number of the primes satisfying the constraints
p\geqslant\log x
and
p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}
is no larger than
\frac{\log 2x}{\log 2}
. Hence the second product can be bounded as

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\\
p\geqslant\log x\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\ll\left(1+{\frac{1}{\log x}}\right)^{\frac{\log 2x}{\log 2}}\ll 1.

(3.12)

Thus to prove the equation (
3.10
), it suffices to prove

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i},k_{j}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
i,j=1,2\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\\
2<p<\log x\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\ll\log^{2}x.

(3.13)

It is not difficult to see that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i},k_{j}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
i,j=1,2\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\\
2<p<\log x\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i},k_{i}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
i,j=1,2,~h\neq 0\\
d|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\end{subarray}}1,

(3.14)

where
\mu(d)
is the Möbius function and
P^{+}(d)
is the largest prime factor of
d
.
For any fixed integers
d
,
m_{1},~m_{2}
and
k_{1}
with
2\nmid d
and
d|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}
, there is some
l_{m_{1},m_{2},k_{1}}
such that

\displaystyle 2^{k_{2}^{2}}\equiv 2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}\equiv l_{m_{1},m_{2},k_{1}}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ d).

(3.15)

Let
e_{2}(d)
stand for the order of
2
modulo
d
. Then from the congruent condition of the equation (
3.15
), we know that there exists some
\widetilde{l}_{m_{1},m_{2},k_{1}}
such that

k_{2}^{2}\equiv\widetilde{l}_{m_{1},m_{2},k_{1}}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ e_{2}(d)),

(3.16)

providing that there do exist positive integers
k_{2}
satisfying the equation (
3.15
).
For
e_{2}(d)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}
, the number of the solutions to the congruence equation (
3.16
) is at most
4\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}
from Proposition
2.1
. Thus there are at most

\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}}{e_{2}(d)}\right\rfloor+1\right)4\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}

(3.17)

choices of
k_{2}
below
\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}
if
d,m_{1},~m_{2}
and
k_{1}
are fixed for
e_{2}(d)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}
. For
e_{2}(d)>\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}
, the number of the solutions to the congruence equation (
3.16
) below
\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}
is at most
O\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right)
from the Proposition
2.2
.
Taking these bounds of the solutions to the congruent condition into the equation (
3.14
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i},k_{j}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\\
i,j=1,2,~h\neq 0\\
d|2^{m_{1}^{2}}+2^{m_{2}^{2}}-2^{k_{1}^{2}}-2^{k_{2}^{2}}\end{subarray}}1\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\\
e_{2}(d)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\right)^{3}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}}{e_{2}(d)}\right\rfloor+1\right)4\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}

\displaystyle~~~+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\\
e_{2}(d)>\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\right)^{3}\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle(\log x)^{2}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\\
e_{2}(d)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}}+\frac{(\log x)^{2}}{\log\log x}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\\
e_{2}(d)>\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d},

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle(\log x)^{2}S_{1}(x)+\frac{(\log x)^{2}}{\log\log x}S_{2}(x),~\text{say}.

(3.18)

By a result of Erdős and Turán
[
16
]
, we have

\sum_{2\nmid d}\frac{1}{d\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}}<\infty.

(3.19)

Therefore the sum
S_{1}(x)
can be bounded as

S_{1}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\\
e_{2}(d)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\nmid d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d\sqrt{e_{2}(d)}}<\infty.

(3.20)

To deal with the sum
S_{2}(x)
, we exchange the summations back to the product.

\displaystyle S_{2}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\\
e_{2}(d)>\sqrt{\frac{\log x}{\log 2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d}<\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<2x\\
2\nmid d\\
P^{+}(d)<\log x\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(d)}{d}\leqslant\prod_{2<p<\log x}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).

(3.21)

By the logarithmic inequality

\log\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)<\frac{1}{p}

(3.22)

and the Mertens formula

\sum_{p<\log x}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log\log x+B+O\left(\frac{1}{\log\log x}\right),

(3.23)

we obtain that

\displaystyle\prod_{2<p<\log x}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(\log\log\log x+B+O\left(\frac{1}{\log\log x}\right)\right)\ll\log\log x.

(3.24)

The proof of the theorem is complete by combining the equations (
3.10
), (
3.13
), (
3.14
), (
3
), (
3.20
), (
3.21
) and (
3.24
).
∎
