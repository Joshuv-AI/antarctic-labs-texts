Nonautonomous k k -contact field theories
Xavier Rivas

Abstract
This paper provides a new geometric framework to describe non-conservative field theories with explicit dependence on the space-time coordinates by combining the k k -cosymplectic and k k -contact formulations. This geometric framework, the k k -cocontact geometry, permits to develop a Hamiltonian and Lagrangian formalisms for these field theories. We also compare this new formulation in the autonomous case with the previous k k -contact formalism. To illustrate the theory, we study the nonlinear damped wave equation with external time-dependent forcing.

Introduction

During the second half of the 20th century, geometric methods have been widely applied to mechanics and field theory with the aim of providing geometric descriptions of a large variety of systems in applied mathematics, physics, engineering, etc. Some of the most frequent geometric structures involved in geometric mechanics and field theory are symplectic, multisymplectic or

In recent years, there has been a growing interest for non-conservative systems. In particular, contact geometry
[
4
,
27
,

]
has been used to study mechanical systems with dissipation

5
,
7
,
10
,
14
,
25
,
38
]
. This has many applications in thermodynamics
[

,
43
]
, quantum mechanics
[

]
, circuit theory
[

]
and control theory
[

]
among others
[

,

,
29
,
30

. Recently, contact mechanics have been generalized in order to deal with time-dependent contact systems
[

,
41
]
. It is worth pointing out that contact geometry allows to study more systems than just dissipative ones
[
23
]
. In the last years, a generalization of both contact and
k
-symplectic structures was devised to describe autonomous field theories with damping
[
24
,
26
,
32
]
both in the Hamiltonian and Lagrangian formulations.

The main goal of this paper is to extend the
k
-contact formulation to non-autonomous field theories by combining it with
k
-cosymplectic geometry
[
19
,
20
]
. This leads to the definition of a
k
-cocontact structure
as a couple of families of
k
differential one-forms: the first family accounting for the space-time coordinates, and the other one encoding the dampings or dissipations, inspired in the contact formulation. It is worth noting that the number of independent variables of the system coincides with the number of “dissipation coordinates”. This new geometry enables us to introduce the notion of
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian system as a
k
-cocontact manifold together with a Hamiltonian function. With these elements we can state the
k
-cocontact Hamilton equations, which indeed add dissipation terms to the well-known Hamiltonian field equations
[
22
]
.

In addition we also generalize the Lagrangian formulation of field theories to consider non-autonomous non-conservative ones. In this new formalism, the phase bundle is
M=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where the direct sum has to be understood as a fibered sum of vector bundles, with adapted coordinates
(t^{\alpha},q^{i},v_{\alpha}^{i},z^{\alpha})
. Then, given a Lagrangian function
L:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we define a family
\eta^{\alpha}_{L}
of one-forms which, when

-cocontact structure on
M

k
-cocontact Lagrangian field equations are the
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian field equations for the Lagrangian energy. When written in coordinates, they are the Euler–Lagrange equations with some additional damping terms.

We also compare the
k
-cocontact formalism introduced in this work in the autonomous case with the previous
k
-contact formalism and see that they are partially equivalent, in the same way as autonomous
k
-cosymplectic systems are closely related to
k
-symplectic systems
[
22
]
. Finally, we apply this formalism to the nonlinear damped wave equation with a time-dependent external force, both in the Hamiltonian and Lagrangian formulations.

In Section

5

k

2.1
k
-contact manifolds

Consider an
m
-dimensional manifold
M
. A
generalized distribution
on
M
is a subset
D\subset\mathrm{T}M
such that
D_{x}\subset\mathrm{T}_{x}M
is a vector subspace for every
x\in M
. A distribution
D
is said to be
smooth
if it can be locally spanned by a family of vector fields, and
regular
if it is smooth and of locally constant rank. A
codistribution
on
M
is a subset
C\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
such that
C_{x}\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{x}M
is a vector subspace for every
x\in M
.

Given a distribution
D
, the anihilator
D^{\circ}
of
D
is a codistribution. If
D
is not regular,
D^{\circ}
may not be smooth. Using the usual identification
E^{\ast\ast}=E
of finite-dimensional linear algebra, it is clear that
(D^{\circ})^{\circ}=D
.

A differential one-form
\eta\in\Omega^{1}(M)
generates a smooth codistribution, denoted by
\langle\eta\rangle\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
. This codistribution has rank 1 at every point where
\eta
does not vanish. Its anihilator is a distribution
\langle\eta\rangle^{\circ}\subset\mathrm{T}M
that can be described as the kernel of the linear morphism
\widehat{\eta}\colon\mathrm{T}M\to M\times\mathbb{R}
defined by
\eta
. This codistribution has corank 1 at every point where
\eta
does not vanish.

In the same way, every two-form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(M)
induces a linear morphism
\widehat{\omega}\colon\mathrm{T}M\to\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
defined as
\widehat{\omega}(v)=i(v)\omega
. The kernel of this morphism
\widehat{\omega}
is a distribution
\ker\widehat{\omega}\subset\mathrm{T}M
. Notice that the rank of
\widehat{\omega}
is even.

Given a family of
k
differential one-forms
\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
, we will denote

•

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}=\langle\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\rangle\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
,

•

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}=\left(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}\right)^{\circ}=\ker\widehat{\eta^{1}}\cap\dotsb\cap\ker\widehat{\eta^{k}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
,

•

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta^{1}}\cap\dotsb\cap\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta^{k}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
,

•

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\right)^{\circ}\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
.

With the preceding notations, a
k
-contact structure
on a manifold
M
is a family of
k
differential one-forms
\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
such that
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
is a regular distribution of corank
k
,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
is a regular distribution of rank
k
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\{0\}
. We call
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}
the
contact codistribution
,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}
the
contact distribution
,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
the
Reeb distribution
and
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}
the
Reeb codistribution
. A manifold
M
endowed with a
k
-contact structure
\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
is a
k
-contact manifold
.

Remark 2.1
.

In the particular case
k=1
, a 1-contact structure is given by a one-form
\eta
. In this case, we recover the notion of contact manifold
[
14
,
25
]
.

Given a
k
-contact manifold
(M,\eta^{\alpha})
, the Reeb distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
is involutive, and therefore integrable, and there exists a unique family of
k
vector fields
R_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, called
Reeb vector fields
of
M
, such that
i(R_{\alpha})\eta^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}
and
i(R_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0
. The Reeb vector fields commute and span the Reeb distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\langle R_{1},\dotsc,R_{k}\rangle
.

Example 2.2

(Canonical
k
-contact structure)
.

Consider
k\geq 1
and let
Q
be a smooth manifold. The manifold product
M=\oplus^{k}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
has a canonical contact structure given by the one-forms
\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
defined as

\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-\theta^{\alpha}\,,

where
(z^{1},\dotsc,z^{k})
are the canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\theta^{\alpha}
is the pull-back of the Liouville one-form
\theta
of the cotangent bundle
\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q
with respect to the projection
\mathrm{pr}^{\alpha}:M\to\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q
to the
\alpha
-th component. Take coordinates
(q^{i})
on
Q
. Then,
M
has natural coordinates
(q^{i},p_{i}^{\alpha},z^{\alpha})
. Using these coordinates, we have

\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-p_{i}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}q^{i}\,,\qquad\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{1}},\dotsc,\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{k}}\right\rangle\,,\qquad R_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\,.

Example 2.3

(Contactification of a
k
-symplectic manifold)
.

Consider a
k
-symplectic manifold
(P,\omega^{\alpha})
such that
\omega^{\alpha}=-\mathrm{d}\theta^{\alpha}
and the product manifold
M=P\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Let
(z^{\alpha})
be the cartesian coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and denote also by
\theta^{\alpha}
the pull-back of
\theta^{\alpha}
to the product manifold
M
. Consider the one-forms
\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-\theta^{\alpha}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
.

Then,
(M,\eta^{\alpha})
is a
k
-contact manifold because
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}=\langle\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\rangle
has rank
k
,
\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}=-\mathrm{d}\theta^{\alpha}
, and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\bigcap_{\alpha}\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\theta^{\alpha}}=\langle\partial/\partial z^{1},\dotsc,\partial/\partial z^{k}\rangle
has rank
k
since
(P,\omega^{\alpha})
is
k
-symplectic, and the last condition is immediate.

Notice that the so-called canonical
k
-contact structure described in Example
2.2
is just the contactification of the
k
-symplectic manifold
P=\oplus^{k}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q
.

Theorem 2.4

(
k
-contact Darboux Theorem)
.

Consider a
k
-contact manifold
(M,\eta^{\alpha})
of dimension
\dim M=n+kn+k
such that there exists an integrable subdistribution
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}
with
\rk\mathcal{V}=nk
. Then, around every point of
M
there exists a local chart
(U,q^{i},p_{i}^{\alpha},z^{\alpha})
,
1\leq\alpha\leq k
, where
1\leq i\leq n
, such that

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\eta^{\alpha}\right|_{U}}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-p_{i}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}q^{i}\,,\quad{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\right|_{U}}=\left\langle R_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\right\rangle\,,\quad{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{V}\right|_{U}}=\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}\right\rangle\,.

These coordinates are called
Darboux coordinates
of the
k
-contact manifold
(M,\eta^{\alpha})
.

2.2
Hamiltonian formalism for
k
-contact systems

The geometric setting introduced in the previous section allows us to introduce the notion of
k
-contact Hamiltonian system
[
24
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
k
-contact Hamiltonian system
is a family
(M,\eta^{\alpha},h)
, where
(M,\eta^{\alpha})
is a
k
-contact manifold and
h\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M)
is called a
Hamiltonian function
. Consider a map
\psi\colon D\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to M
. The
k
-contact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations for the map
\psi
are

\begin{cases}i(\psi_{\alpha}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}=\left(\mathrm{d}h-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}}h)\eta^{\alpha}\right)\circ\psi\,,\\
i(\psi_{\alpha}^{\prime})\eta^{\alpha}=-h\circ\psi\,.\end{cases}

(1)

In Darboux coordinates, if the map
\psi
has local expression
\psi(t)=(q^{i}(t),p_{i}^{\alpha}(t),z^{\alpha}(t))
, equations (
1
) read

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial q^{i}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial p^{\alpha}_{i}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}=-\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\right)\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial z^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}=\left(p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}-h\right)\circ\psi\,.\end{cases}

(2)

Definition 2.6
.

Consider a
k
-contact Hamiltonian system
(M,\eta^{\alpha},h)
and a
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}=(X_{\alpha})\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)
. The
k
-contact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations for the
k
-vector field

\mathbf{X}
are

\begin{cases}i(X_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}h-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}}h)\eta^{\alpha}\,,\\
i(X_{\alpha})\eta^{\alpha}=-h\,.\end{cases}

(3)

A
k
-vector field solution to these equations is a
k
-contact Hamiltonian
k
-vector field
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The
k
-contact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
3
) admit solutions. They are not unique if
k>1
.

Consider a
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)
with local expression in Darboux coordinates

X_{\alpha}=(X_{\alpha})^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}+(X_{\alpha})^{\beta}_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\beta}}+(X_{\alpha})^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\beta}}\,.

Then, equation (
3

\begin{cases}(X_{\alpha})^{i}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}\,,\\
(X_{\alpha})^{\alpha}_{i}=-\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\right)\,,\\
(X_{\alpha})^{\alpha}=p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}-h\,.\end{cases}

(4)

.

Consider an integrable

\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)

\psi\colon D\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to M

\mathbf{X}
satisfies the
k
-contact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
1

\mathbf{X}
is a solution to (

2.3
Lagrangian formalism for
k
-contact systems

The Hamiltonian formalism presented in the previous section has a Lagrangian counterpart. Consider the phase bundle
\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
endowed with adapted coordinates
(q^{i},v^{i}_{\alpha},z^{\alpha})
with the usual canonical structures: the Liouville vector field
\Delta=v_{\alpha}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\alpha}^{i}}
and the canonical
k
-tangent structure
J^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\alpha}^{i}}\otimes\mathrm{d}q^{i}
(see
[
26
]
for details). A
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}=(X_{\alpha})\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a
second-order partial differential equation
(or
sopde
) is
J^{\alpha}(X_{\alpha})=\Delta
.

Given a Lagrangian function
L\colon\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
Lagrangian energy
associated to the Lagrangian
L
is the function
E_{L}=\Delta(L)-L
, and the
contact one-forms

\eta^{\alpha}_{L}\in\Omega^{1}(\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
associated to
L
are given by
\eta_{L}^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-\theta_{L}^{\alpha}
, where
\theta^{\alpha}_{L}=\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{t}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut J^{\alpha}}\circ\mathrm{d}L
.

The Lagrangian
L
is
regular
, namely
\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial v_{\alpha}^{i}\partial v_{\beta}^{j}}
is non-degenerate, if and only if the contact one-forms
\eta^{\alpha}_{L}
define a
k
-contact structure on
\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Thus, we can consider the
k
-contact Hamiltonian system
(\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k},\eta_{L}^{\alpha},E_{L})
, whose corresponding field equations read

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^{i}_{\alpha}}\circ\psi\right)=\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^{i}}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^{i}_{\alpha}}\right)\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial(s^{\alpha}\circ\psi)}{\partial t^{\alpha}}=L\circ\psi\,,\end{cases}

(6)

and are called
k
-contact Euler–Lagrange equations
(for more details on the
k
-contact Lagrangian formulation, see
[
26
,
40
]
).

3
k
-cocontact geometry

Let
\tau^{1},\dotsc,\tau^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
be a family of closed one-forms on
M
and let
\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
be a family of one-forms on
M
. We will use the following notations:

•

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}=\langle\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\rangle\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
,

•

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}=\left(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}\right)^{\circ}=\ker\widehat{\eta^{1}}\cap\dotsb\cap\ker\widehat{\eta^{k}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
,

•

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta^{1}}\cap\dotsb\cap\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta^{k}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
,

•

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\right)^{\circ}\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
,

•

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}=\langle\tau^{1},\dotsc,\tau^{k}\rangle\subset\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
,

•

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}=\left(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}\right)^{\circ}=\ker\widehat{\tau^{1}}\cap\dotsb\cap\ker\widehat{\tau^{k}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
.

With these notations, we can define the notion of
k
-cocontact structure:

Definition 3.1
.

A
k
-cocontact structure
on a manifold
M
is a family of
k
closed differential one-forms
\tau^{1},\dotsc,\tau^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
and a family of
k
differential one-forms
\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
such that, with the preceding notations,

(1)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
is a regular distribution of corank
k
,

(2)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
is a regular distribution of corank
k
,

(3)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\subset\mathrm{T}M
is a regular distribution of rank
2k
,

(4)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}
is a regular distribution of corank
2k
,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
is a regular distribution of rank
k
, and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
is a regular distribution of rank
k
,

(5)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}=\{0\}
.

We call
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}
the
contact codistribution
,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}
the
contact distribution
,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
the
Reeb distribution
,
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}
the
Reeb codistribution
,
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}
the
space-time codistribution
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}
the
space-time distribution
.

A manifold
M
endowed with a
k
-cocontact structure
\tau^{1},\dotsc,\tau^{k},\eta^{1},\dotsc,\eta^{k}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
is a
k
-cocontact manifold
.

Notice that the condition
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}=\{0\}
implies that

\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}\oplus\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}\oplus\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}\,.

Remark 3.2
.

In the particular case
k=1
, a 1-cocontact structure is given by two one-forms
\tau,\eta
, with
\mathrm{d}\tau=0
. The conditions in Definition
3.1
mean the following: (1)
\eta\neq 0
everywhere, (2)
\tau\neq 0
everywhere, (4)
\tau\wedge\eta\neq 0
, (5)
\ker\widehat{\tau}\cap\ker\widehat{\eta}\cap\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta}=\{0\}
, which implies that
\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta}
has rank 0, 1 or 2, and (3) implies that
\ker\widehat{\mathrm{d}\eta}
has rank 2. Thus, a 1-cocontact structure coincides with the cocontact structure introduced in
[
12
]
to describe time-dependent contact mechanics.

Lemma 3.3
.

The Reeb distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
and the space-time distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}
are involutive, and therefore integrable.

Proof.

Given
X,Y
two sections of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
and applying the relation

i_{[X,Y]}=\mathscr{L}_{X}i_{Y}-i_{Y}\mathscr{L}_{X}=\mathrm{d}i_{X}i_{Y}+i_{X}\mathrm{d}i_{Y}-i_{Y}\mathrm{d}i_{X}-i_{Y}i_{X}\mathrm{d}

to the closed two-form
\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}
, the result is zero. In the same way, one can check that
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}
is also involutive.
∎

As a consequence, the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{S}}
is also involutive, and therefore integrable. Moreover, the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}
is also involutive and integrable. The following theorem characterizes a family of vector fields spanning the Reeb distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(M,\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha})
be a
k
-cocontact manifold. Then, there exist a unique family
R^{t}_{1},\dotsc,R^{t}_{k},R^{z}_{1},\dotsc,R^{z}_{k}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
such that

\displaystyle i(R^{t}_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0\,,\qquad i(R^{t}_{\alpha})\eta^{\beta}=0\,,\qquad i(R^{t}_{\alpha})\tau^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle i(R^{z}_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0\,,\qquad i(R^{z}_{\alpha})\eta^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,,\qquad i(R^{z}_{\alpha})\tau^{\beta}=0\,.

(8)

The vector fields
R^{t}_{\alpha}
are called
space-time Reeb vector fields
. The vector fields
R^{z}_{\alpha}
are called
contact Reeb vector fields
.

In addition, the Reeb vector fields commute and span the Reeb distribution introduced in Definition
3.1
:

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\langle R^{t}_{1},\dotsc,R^{t}_{k},R^{z}_{1},\dotsc,R^{z}_{k}\rangle\,,

thus motivating its name.

Proof.

Consider
\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}\oplus\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}\oplus\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}
. The family of one-forms
\{\eta^{\beta}\}
is a global frame of the contact codistribution
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{C}}
and the family of one-forms
\{\tau^{\beta}\}
is a global frame of the space-time codistribution
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}
. We can find a global frame
\bar{\eta}^{\mu}
of the Reeb codistribution
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}}
so that
(\eta^{\beta},\bar{\eta}^{\mu},\tau^{\beta})
is a global frame of
\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
. Let
(R^{z}_{\alpha},R_{\nu},R_{\alpha}^{t})
be the corresponding dual frame of
\mathrm{T}M
, where the vector fields
R_{\alpha}^{z}
and
R_{\alpha}^{t}
are uniquely determined by the conditions

\displaystyle\langle\eta^{\beta},R_{\alpha}^{z}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,,\qquad\langle\bar{\eta}^{\mu},R_{\alpha}^{z}\rangle=0\,,\qquad\langle\tau^{\beta},R_{\alpha}^{z}\rangle=0\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\langle\eta^{\beta},R_{\alpha}^{t}\rangle=0\,,\qquad\langle\bar{\eta}^{\mu},R_{\alpha}^{t}\rangle=0\,,\qquad\langle\tau^{\beta},R_{\alpha}^{t}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,.

(10)

Notice that the relations involving the
\bar{\eta}^{\mu}
do not depend on the choice of the one-forms
\bar{\eta}^{\mu}
, this means that the vector fields
R_{\alpha}^{z}
and
R_{\alpha}^{t}
are sections of the Reeb distribution
(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{R}})^{\circ}=\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
. This amounts to
i(R_{\alpha}^{z})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0
and
i(R_{\alpha}^{t})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0
for every
\alpha=1,\dotsc,k
. Since the one-forms
\eta^{\beta}
and
\tau^{\beta}
are globally defined, so are the vector fields
R_{\alpha}^{z}
and
R_{\alpha}^{t}
.

To prove that the Reeb vector fields
R_{\alpha}^{z},R_{\beta}^{t}
commute, notice that

i_{[X,Y]}\eta^{\gamma}=0\,,\qquad i_{[X,Y]}\mathrm{d}\eta^{\gamma}=0\,,\qquad i_{[X,Y]}\tau^{\gamma}=0\,,

for every
X,Y\in\langle R_{\alpha}^{z},R_{\beta}^{t}\rangle
, which is a consequence of their definition.
∎

The following proposition proves the existence of a special set of coordinates, the so-called adapted coordinates.

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider a
k
-cocontact manifold
(M,\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha})
. Then, around every point in
M
, there exist local coordinates
(t^{\alpha},x^{I},z^{\alpha})
such that

R_{\alpha}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\tau^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}\,,\qquad R_{\alpha}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-f_{I}^{\alpha}(x^{J})\mathrm{d}x^{I}\,,

where the functions
f_{I}^{\alpha}
only depend on the coordinates
x^{I}
. These coordinates are called
adapted coordinates
.

Proof.

Since the Reeb vector fields commute, there exists a set of local coordinates
(t^{\alpha},x^{I},z^{\alpha})
simultaneously straightening out the Reeb vector fields (see
[
36
, p.234]
for details):

R_{\alpha}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad R_{\alpha}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\,.

Let us write the forms
\tau^{\beta}
and
\eta^{\beta}
using these coordinates. The conditions
i(R_{\alpha}^{t})\eta^{\beta}=0
and
i(R_{\alpha}^{z})\eta^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}
imply that
\eta^{\beta}=\mathrm{d}z^{\beta}-f_{I}^{\beta}(t^{\alpha},x^{J},z^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}x^{I}
. On the other hand, we have that
\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=\mathrm{d}x^{I}\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{I}^{\beta}
. In this case, the conditions
i(R_{\alpha}^{t})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0
and
i(R_{\alpha}^{z})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\beta}=0
imply that
\partial f_{I}^{\beta}/\partial t^{\alpha}=0
and that
\partial f_{I}^{\beta}/\partial z^{\alpha}=0
, and thus

\eta^{\beta}=\mathrm{d}z^{\beta}-f_{I}^{\beta}(x^{J})\mathrm{d}x^{I}\,.

Repeating this process for the forms
\tau^{\beta}
, taking into account that
\mathrm{d}\tau^{\beta}=0
and redefining the coordinates
t^{\alpha}
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Example 3.6

(Canonical

.

Let

and let

. Consider the product manifold

endowed with natural coordinates
(t^{\alpha};q^{i},p_{i}^{\alpha};z^{\alpha})
. We have the canonical projections
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}^{\alpha}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{3}^{\alpha}}

Theorem 3.7

(Darboux theorem for
k
-cocontact manifolds)
.

Let
(M,\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha})
be a
k
-cocontact manifold with dimension
\dim M=k+n+kn+k
such that there exists an integrable subdistribution
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{D}^{C}
with
\rk\mathcal{V}=nk
. Then, around every point of
M
there exist local coordinates
(t^{\alpha},q^{i},p_{i}^{\alpha},z^{\alpha})
, where
1\leq\alpha\leq k
and
1\leq i\leq n
, such that, locally,

\tau^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}\,,\qquad\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-p_{i}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}q^{i}\,.

Using these coordinates,

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\left\langle R_{\alpha}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\,,\ R_{\alpha}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\right\rangle\,,\qquad\mathcal{V}=\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}\right\rangle\,.

These coordinates are called
Darboux coordinates
of the
k
-cocontact manifold
(M,\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.5
, there exist local coordinates
(t^{\alpha},x^{I},z^{\alpha})
such that

R_{\alpha}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\tau^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}\,,\qquad R_{\alpha}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-f_{I}^{\alpha}(x^{J})\mathrm{d}x^{I}\,.

Since the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\left\langle R_{\alpha}^{t}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\right\rangle
is involutive, and therefore integrable, we can consider (at least locally) the quotient manifold
\widetilde{M}=M/(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}})
, with the projection
\rho\colon M\to\widetilde{M}
and local coordinates
(x^{I},z^{\alpha})
.

The one-forms
\eta^{\alpha}
, the vector fields
R_{\alpha}^{z}
and the distribution
\mathcal{V}
can be projected to
\widetilde{M}
and the distribution
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}}
induced by
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}
is
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{C}}}=\left\langle R_{\alpha}^{z}\right\rangle
.

It is easy to check that the manifold
(\widetilde{M},\widetilde{\eta}^{\alpha})
, where
\widetilde{\eta}^{\alpha}
are the projections of
\eta^{\alpha}
to
\widetilde{M}
, is a
k
-contact manifold. Since the projected distribution
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
has rank

, by Theorem

, around every point there exists a local chart

in

such that

\widetilde{\eta}^{\,\alpha}=\mathrm{d}\widetilde{z}^{\,\alpha}-\widetilde{p}_{i}^{\,\alpha}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{q}^{\;i}\,,\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}=\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widetilde{p}_{i}^{\,\alpha}}\right\rangle\,.

With all this in mind, in

, we can take coordinates
(t^{\alpha},x^{I},z^{\alpha})=(t^{\alpha},q^{i},p_{i}^{\alpha},z^{\alpha})
, with

,

and

∎

Taking into account the previous theorem, we can consider the manifold introduced in Example

-cocontact structures.

4
Hamiltonian formalism

This section introduces the notion of
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian system and its Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations. The existence of solutions to these equations is proved. We provide local expressions of the Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations for maps and
k
-vector fields in both adapted and Darboux coordinates.

Definition 4.1
.

A
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian system
is a tuple
(M,\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha},h)
, where
(\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha})
is a
k
-cocontact structure on the manifold
M
and
h\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is a
Hamiltonian function
. Given a map
\psi\colon D\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to M
, the
k
-cocontact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations for the map
\psi
are

\begin{cases}i(\psi_{\alpha}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}=\left(\mathrm{d}h-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}^{t}}h)\tau^{\alpha}-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}^{z}}h)\eta^{\alpha}\right)\circ\psi\,,\\
i(\psi_{\alpha}^{\prime})\eta^{\alpha}=-h\circ\psi\,,\\
i(\psi_{\alpha}^{\prime})\tau^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,.\end{cases}

(11)

Now we are going to look at the expression in coordinates of the Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
11
).

Consider first the adapted coordinates
(t^{\alpha},x^{I},z^{\alpha})
. In these coordinates,

R_{\alpha}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\,,\quad\tau^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}\,,\quad R_{\alpha}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\,,\quad\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-f_{I}^{\alpha}(x^{J})\mathrm{d}x^{I}\,,\quad\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\omega^{\alpha}_{IJ}\mathrm{d}x^{I}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{J}\,,

where
\omega^{\alpha}_{IJ}=\dfrac{\partial f_{I}^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{J}}-\dfrac{\partial f_{J}^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{I}}
. Consider a map
\psi\colon D\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to M
with local expression
\psi(s)=(t^{\alpha}(s),x^{I}(s),z^{\alpha}(s))
. Then,

\psi^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\left(t^{\beta},x^{I},z^{\beta};\frac{\partial t^{\beta}}{\partial s^{\alpha}},\frac{\partial x^{I}}{\partial s^{\alpha}},\frac{\partial z^{\beta}}{\partial s^{\alpha}}\right)\,.

Then, the Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations in adapted coordinates read

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial x^{J}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\omega_{JI}^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{I}}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial s^{\alpha}}f_{I}^{\alpha}\right)\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial s^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}-f_{I}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial x^{I}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}=-h\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial t^{\alpha}}{\partial s^{\beta}}=\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\,.\end{cases}

(12)

On the other hand, if the local expression in Darboux coordinates of a map
\psi\colon D\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\to M
is
\psi(r)=(t^{\alpha}(r),q^{i}(r),p_{i}^{\alpha}(r),z^{\alpha}(r))
, where
r=(r^{1},\dotsc,r^{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then, the Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations in Darboux coordinates read

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial t^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\alpha}}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,,\\
\frac{\partial q^{i}}{\partial r^{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}{\partial r^{\alpha}}=-\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\right)\circ\psi\,,\\
\frac{\partial z^{\alpha}}{\partial r^{\alpha}}=\left(p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}-h\right)\circ\psi\,.\end{cases}

(13)

Definition 4.2
.

Consider a
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian system
(M,\tau^{\alpha},\eta^{\alpha},h)
. The
k
-cocontact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations for a
k
-vector field

\mathbf{X}=(X_{\alpha})\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)
are

\begin{cases}i(X_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}h-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}^{t}}h)\tau^{\alpha}-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}^{z}}h)\eta^{\alpha}\,,\\
i(X_{\alpha})\eta^{\alpha}=-h\,,\\
i(X_{\alpha})\tau^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,.\end{cases}

(14)

A
k
-vector field solution to these equations is a
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian
k
-vector field
. We will denote this set of
k
-vector fields by
\mathfrak{X}_{\rm ham}^{k}(M)
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The
k
-cocontact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
14
) admit solutions. They are not unique if
k>1
.

Proof.

Consider the bundle maps

\rho:\mathrm{T}M\to\textstyle\bigoplus\nolimits^{k}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M\,,\qquad\sigma:\bigoplus\nolimits^{k}\mathrm{T}M\to\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M\,,

given by

\rho(X)=(i_{X}\mathrm{d}\eta^{1},\dotsc,i_{X}\mathrm{d}\eta^{k})\,,\qquad\sigma(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})=i_{X_{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}\,.

These morphisms can be extended to
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M)
-modules. Notice that
\ker\rho=\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
is the Reeb distribution. Using the natural identification
(E\oplus F)^{*}=E^{*}\oplus F^{*}
, the transposed morphism of
\tau
is
\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{t}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut\tau}=-\rho
, taking into account that
\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{t}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}}=-\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}
.

The first Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equation for a
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}
can be written as

\tau\circ\mathbf{X}=\mathrm{d}h-R_{\alpha}^{z}(h)\eta^{\alpha}-R_{\alpha}^{t}(h)\tau^{\alpha}\,.

A sufficient condition for this linear equation to have solutions
\mathbf{X}
is that the right-hand-side must be in the image of
\tau
, that is, anihilated by any section of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}=\ker\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{t}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut\tau}
. But since

i_{R}(\mathrm{d}h-R_{\alpha}^{z}(h)\eta^{\alpha}-R_{\alpha}^{t}(h)\tau^{\alpha})=0\,,\quad\text{for every }R\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}\,,

we can conclude that the first Hamilton-De Donder–Weyl has solutions. Notice that if
\mathbf{X}
is a solution to the first equation,
\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{R}
, where
\mathbf{R}
is a
k
-vector field whose components are in
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{R}}
, is also a solution. On the other hand, the second and third equations have common solutions
\mathbf{R}
whose components belong to the Reeb distribution, for instance
\mathbf{R}=(-hR_{1}^{z}+R_{1}^{t},R_{2}^{t},\dotsc,R_{k}^{t})
.

The non-uniqueness for
k>1
is obvious.
∎

Consider a
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)
with local expression in adapted coordinates

X_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\beta}}+B_{\alpha}^{I}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{I}}+D_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\beta}}\,.

Thus, equations (
14
) in adapted coordinates read

\begin{cases}A_{\alpha}^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,,\\
B_{\alpha}^{J}\omega_{JI}^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{I}}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial z^{\alpha}}f_{I}^{\alpha}\,,\\
D_{\alpha}^{\alpha}-f_{I}^{\alpha}B_{\alpha}^{I}=-h\,.\end{cases}

(15)

On the other hand, consider a
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)
with local expression in Darboux coordinates

X_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\beta}}+B_{\alpha}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}+C_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}^{\beta}}+D_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\beta}}\,.

Imposing equations (
14
), we get the conditions

\begin{cases}A_{\alpha}^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,,\\
B_{\alpha}^{i}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}\,,\\
C_{\alpha i}^{\alpha}=-\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\right)\,,\\
D_{\alpha}^{\alpha}=p_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{i}^{\alpha}}-h\,.\end{cases}

(16)

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{X}^{k}(M)
be an integrable
k
-vector field. Then
\mathbf{X}
is a solution to (
14
) if and only if every integral section of
\mathbf{X}
satisfies the
k
-cocontact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
11
).

Proof.

Recall that since
\mathbf{X}
is integrable, every point of
M
is in the image of an integral section of
\mathbf{X}
. The proposition is a direct consequence of this fact and of equations (
11
) and (
14
).
∎

It is worth noting that, as in the
k
-symplectic and
k
-contact cases, equations (
11
) and (
14
) are not completely equivalent since a solution to (
11
) may not be an integral section of an integrable
k
-vector field
\mathbf{X}
solution to equations (
14
).

The following proposition provides an alternative way of writing the
k
-cocontact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations for
k
-vector fields.

Proposition 4.5
.

The
k
-cocontact Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
14
) are equivalent to

\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}_{X_{\alpha}}\eta^{\alpha}=-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}^{t}}h)\tau^{\alpha}-(\mathscr{L}_{R_{\alpha}^{z}}h)\eta^{\alpha}\,,\\
i(X_{\alpha})\eta^{\alpha}=-h\,,\\
i(X_{\alpha})\tau^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,.\end{cases}

(17)

Before introducing the Legendre map associated to a Lagrangian function, let us recall the notion of fibre derivative. Given two vector bundles
E,F
over the same base manifold

of

obtained by restricting the map

of

is a symmetric bilinear form on

5.3

In this section, a particular type of Lagrangian functions is studied in full detail: the so-called Lagrangians with holonomic damping term

Definition 5.15
.

A
Lagrangian function with holonomic damping term
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a function
\mathcal{L}=L+\phi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
, where
L=\bar{\tau}_{2}^{\ast}L_{\circ}
, where
\bar{\tau}_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q
for some Lagrangian function
L_{\circ}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q)
and
\phi=\tau_{0}^{\ast}\phi_{\circ}
, for
\phi_{\circ}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Taking natural coordinates
(t^{\alpha},q^{i},v_{\alpha}^{i},z^{\alpha})
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, a Lagrangian with holonomic damping term has the expression

\mathcal{L}(t^{\alpha},q^{i},v_{\alpha}^{i},z^{\alpha})=L(t^{\alpha},q^{i},v^{i}_{\alpha})+\phi(t^{\alpha},q^{i},z^{\alpha})\,.

(39)

It is clear that the momenta
p_{i}^{\alpha}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial v^{i}_{\alpha}
defined by the Legendre map are independent of the coordinates
z^{\alpha}
, namely one has that
\dfrac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{\alpha}\partial v^{i}_{\beta}}=0
for Lagrangian functions with holonomic damping term.

Proposition 5.16
.

Consider the Lagrangian function with holonomic damping term
\mathcal{L}=L+\phi
. Then, its Cartan forms, contact forms, Lagrangian energy and Reeb vector fields read

\theta_{\mathcal{L}}^{\alpha}=\theta_{L}^{\alpha}\,,\quad\eta_{\mathcal{L}}^{\alpha}=\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}-\theta_{L}^{\alpha}\,,\quad E_{\mathcal{L}}=E_{L}-\phi\,,\quad(R_{\mathcal{L}}^{t})_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha}}\,,\quad(R_{\mathcal{L}}^{z})_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\alpha}}\,.

where
\theta_{L}^{\alpha}
are the Cartan one-forms of
L
considered (via pull-back) as one-forms on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
E_{L}
is the energy of
L
as a function on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

The Legendre map of
\mathcal{L}
, namely
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, can be expressed as
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{F}L\times\mathrm{Id}_{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, where
\mathcal{F}L
is the Legendre map of
L
. The fibred Hessians are related by
\mathcal{F}^{2}\mathcal{L}(t^{\alpha},{v_{q}}_{\alpha},z^{\alpha})=\mathcal{F}^{2}L(t^{\alpha},{v_{q}}_{\alpha})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{L}
is regular if, and only if,
L
is regular.

The proof of this proposition is straightforward by taking local coordinates. It is also clear that
\mathcal{L}
is hyperregular if and only if
L
is hyperregular. In this case, the Legendre map
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{L}
is a diffeomorphism and one can state the canonical Hamiltonian formulation for the Lagrangian with holonomic damping term
\mathcal{L}=L+\phi
via the Legendre map.

Consider the
k
-cocontact Lagrangian system
(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\bigoplus^{k}\mathrm{T}Q\times\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathcal{L})

Definition 6.1
.

For an autonomous

i(X_{\alpha})\tau^{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\beta}\,.\end{cases}

(46)

.

Every autonomous

-cocontact Hamiltonian system

-contact Hamiltonian system

Theorem 6.3
.

Consider an autonomous
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian system

7
An example: one-dimensional nonlinear wave equation with damping

A one-dimensional nonlinear wave with an external time-depending forcing can be modeled by the equation

u_{tt}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{x}}(t,u_{x})\right)-\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}(t,u)\,,

(52)

where
u\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and
u(t,x)
,
f(t,u_{x})
and
g(t,u)
are smooth functions. Notice that if
g(t,u)=0
and
f(t,u_{x})=c^{2}u_{x}^{2}/2
with
c\in\mathbb{R}
, we recover the usual wave equation
u_{tt}=c^{2}u_{xx}
. This equation can be obtained from the Lagrangian function
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\bigoplus^{2}\mathrm{T}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

[
18
]
given by

L(t,x;u,u_{t},u_{x})=\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-f(t,u_{x})-g(t,u)\,,

where we will assume the regularity condition
\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}\neq 0
. We are going to modify this Lagrangian function in order to add a damping term proportional to
u_{t}
to equation (
52
).

Lagrangian formalism

Consider the Lagrangian function with holonomic damping term
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\oplus^{2}\mathrm{T}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by
\mathcal{L}(t,x;u,u_{t},u_{x};z^{t},z^{x})=L(t,x;u,u_{t},u_{x})+\phi(x,z^{t})
, where
\phi(x,z^{t})=-\gamma(x)z^{t}
. Then, we have

\mathcal{L}(t,x;u,u_{t},u_{x};z^{t},z^{x})=\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-f(t,u_{x})-g(t,u)-\gamma(x)z^{t}\,.

(53)

For this Lagrangian, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}=-\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}\right)\mathrm{d}t-z^{t}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\mathrm{d}u+u_{t}\mathrm{d}u_{t}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{x}}\mathrm{d}u_{x}-\gamma(x)\mathrm{d}z^{t}\,,

(54)

\displaystyle E_{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-u_{x}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{x}}+f(t,u_{x})+g(t,u)+\gamma(x)z^{t}\,,

(55)

(56)

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathcal{L}}^{1}=\mathrm{d}z^{t}-u_{t}\mathrm{d}u\,,\qquad\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathcal{L}}^{1}=\mathrm{d}u\wedge\mathrm{d}u_{t}\,,

(57)

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}=\mathrm{d}z^{x}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{x}}\mathrm{d}u\,,\qquad\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t\partial u_{x}}\mathrm{d}t\wedge\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u_{x}\wedge\mathrm{d}u\,,

(58)

\displaystyle(R^{t}_{\mathcal{L}})_{1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t\partial u_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{x}}\,,\qquad(R^{t}_{\mathcal{L}})_{2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,,\qquad(R^{z}_{\mathcal{L}})_{1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{t}}\,,\qquad(R^{z}_{\mathcal{L}})_{2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{x}}\,.

(59)

Now, consider a 2-vector field
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\bigoplus^{2}\mathrm{T}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
with local expression

X_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+A_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+B_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+C_{\alpha t}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{t}}+C_{\alpha x}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{x}}+D_{\alpha}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{t}}+D_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{x}}\,.

For this 2-vector field, the third equation in (
28
) gives the conditions
A_{1}^{t}=1
,
A_{1}^{x}=0
,
A_{2}^{t}=0
and
A_{2}^{x}=1
. We have

i(X_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\eta^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{L}}=-B_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t\partial u_{x}}\mathrm{d}t+\left(-C_{1t}+A_{2}^{t}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t\partial u_{x}}+C_{2x}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}u+B_{1}\mathrm{d}u_{t}-\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}B_{2}\mathrm{d}u_{x}\,,

and

\mathrm{d}E_{\mathcal{L}}-(\mathscr{L}_{(R^{t}_{\mathcal{L}})_{\alpha}}E_{\mathcal{L}})\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}-(\mathscr{L}_{(R^{z}_{\mathcal{L}})_{\alpha}}E_{\mathcal{L}})\eta_{\mathcal{L}}^{\alpha}=-u_{x}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t\partial u_{x}}+\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}+\gamma(x)u_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}u+u_{t}\mathrm{d}u_{t}-u_{x}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u_{x}\,,

and then the first equation in (
28

\displaystyle(B_{2}-u_{x})\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t\partial u_{x}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(60)

\displaystyle C_{1t}-C_{2x}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}+\gamma(x)u_{t}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(61)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{t}\,,

(62)

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=u_{x}\,.

(63)

Finally, the second equation in (

Hamiltonian formalism

In order to give a Hamiltonian description of the system introduced above, let us consider the Legendre map associated to the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}
given in (
53
). The Legendre map associated to
\mathcal{L}
is the map
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\bigoplus^{2}\mathrm{T}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\bigoplus^{2}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

\mathcal{F}\mathcal{L}(t,x;u,u_{t},u_{x};z^{t},z^{x})=\left(t,x;u,p^{t}\equiv u_{t},p^{x}\equiv-\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{x}};z^{t},z^{x}\right)\,.

Notice that the regularity condition
\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u_{x}^{2}}
assumed implies that the Legendre map is a local diffeomorphism and thus the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
is regular. In order to simplify the computations, from now on we will consider the particular case
f(t,u_{x})=u_{x}^{2}/2
.

Consider then the product manifold
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\bigoplus^{2}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
equipped with local coordinates
(t,x;u,p^{t},p^{x};z^{t},z^{x})
. This manifold has a canonical 2-cocontact structure given by

\tau^{1}=\mathrm{d}t\,,\qquad\tau^{2}=\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad\eta^{1}=\mathrm{d}z^{t}-p^{t}\mathrm{d}u\,,\qquad\eta^{2}=\mathrm{d}z^{x}-p^{x}\mathrm{d}u\,.

It is clear that
\mathrm{d}\eta^{1}=\mathrm{d}u\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{t}
and
\mathrm{d}\eta^{2}=\mathrm{d}u\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{x}
. In this case, the Reeb vector fields are

R_{1}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,,\qquad R_{2}^{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,,\qquad R_{1}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{t}}\,,\qquad R_{2}^{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{x}}\,.

The Hamiltonian function
h
such that
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{L}^{\ast}h=E_{\mathcal{L}}
is

h(t,x;u,p^{t},p^{x};z^{t},z^{x})=\frac{1}{2}(p^{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(p^{x})^{2}+g(t,u)+\gamma(x)z^{t}\,.

Consider a 2-vector field
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2})\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\bigoplus^{2}\mathrm{T}^{\ast}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
with local expression

Y_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+A_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+B_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+C_{\alpha}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{t}}+C_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{x}}+D_{\alpha}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{t}}+D_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{x}}\,.

The Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations (
14
) for the 2-vector field
\mathbf{Y}
yield the conditions

\begin{cases}A_{1}^{t}=1\,,\qquad A_{1}^{x}=0\,,\qquad A_{2}^{t}=0\,,\qquad A_{2}^{x}=1\,,\\
B_{1}=p^{t}\,,\qquad B_{2}=-p^{x}\,,\\
C_{1}^{t}+C_{2}^{x}=-\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}-\gamma(x)p^{t}\,,\\
D_{1}^{t}+D_{2}^{x}=\frac{1}{2}(p^{t})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(p^{x})^{2}-g(t,u)-\gamma(x)z^{t}\,.\end{cases}

Consider now an integral section
\psi(r)=(t(r),x(r);u(r),p^{t}(r),p^{x}(r);z^{t}(r),z^{x}(r))
of the 2-vector field
\mathbf{Y}
. As in the Lagrangian case, it is clear that
\psi
satisfies the equation

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}(t,u)+\gamma(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=0\,,

which corresponds to the equation of a damped vibrating string with external forcing.

8
Conclusions and further research

In this paper we have introduced a new geometric framework to describe non-autonomous non-conservative field theories:
k
-cocontact structures. This geometric structure combines the notions of
k
-contact and
k
-cosymplectic manifolds and permits to develop Hamiltonian and Lagrangian formulations of non-autonomous non-conservative field theories.

In more detail, in Definition
3.1
we have introduced the notion of
k
-cocontact structure as a couple of families of
k
differential one-forms satisfying certain properties. We have studied the geometry of these manifolds and, in particular, we have proved the existence of Darboux-type coordinates.

Using this geometric framework, the notion of
k
-cocontact Hamiltonian system is presented, along with its corresponding field equations, generalizing the Hamilton–De Donder–Weyl equations of Hamiltonian field theory. We have also compared this formulation with the
k
-contact formalism introduced in
[
24
]
and shown that they are partially equivalent for autonomous field theories.

Moreover, we have developed a Lagrangian formulation for non-autonomous non-conservative field theories. In particular, we have given the conditions determining if a Lagrangian function yields a
k
-cocontact structure and we have introduced the corresponding field equations generalizing the well-known Euler–Lagrange equations.

In order to illustrate the formalisms introduced in this paper, we have studied with full detail the example of a nonlinear damped wave equation with an external time-dependent forcing, both in the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations.

The formalisms introduced in this work open some lines of future research. The first would be to compare the
k
-cocontact formulation introduced in this paper and the
k
-contact formalism
[
24
,
26
]
with the so-called multicontact formalism
[
13
]
recently introduced. In this work we have only considered regular Lagrangian functions. The singular case would require the weakening of the notion of
k
-cocontact structure, defining the notion of
k
-precocontact structure
. Another very interesting line of research would be to study the symmetries of
k
-cocontact systems, obtaining conservation and dissipation laws.
