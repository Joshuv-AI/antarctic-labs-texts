Increasingly global convergence of Hermite series
Ron Kerman 1, Vít Musil 2

Abstract
. We study the convergence of the Hermite series of measurable functions on the real line. We characterize the norm convergence of truncated partial Hermite sums in rearrangement invariant spaces provided that the truncations vanish sufficiently slowly. Moreover, we provide the necessary and sufficient conditions for convergence in the Orlicz modular.



1.
Introduction and main result

Hermite series

The
k
-tk Hermite function,
h_{k}
, is given at
x\in\mathbb{R}
by

h_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}\gamma_{k}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{k}e^{-x^{2}}}{{\mathrm{d}}x^{k}}=H_{k}(x)e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2}}\quad\text{for $k=0,1,\dots$},

in which
\gamma_{k}=\pi^{-1/4}2^{-k/2}(k!)^{-1/2}
and
H_{k}
is the
Hermite polynomial of degree
k
. Given a suitable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
,
its Hermite series is

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}h_{k},\quad\text{where}\quad c_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}fh_{k}\quad\text{for $k=0,1,\dots$}

We denote the
n
-th partial sum of the Hermite series of
f
by
S_{n}f=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}h_{k}
.

Hermite series, often under the name Gram-Charlier series of type A or Gauss-Hermite series, were initially applied to approximate probability density functions
[
15
]
and later to problems in Astrophysics
[
3
]
.
The series raises many questions of physical interest, for example, in the study of the harmonic oscillator in Quantum Mechanics and as equatorial waves in Dynamic Meteorology and Oceanography
[
4
]
.

Norm convergence of Hermite series

In 1965, Askey and Wainger
[
1
]
showed that for
4/3<p<4
, one has

(1.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|S_{n}f-f\|_{p}=0\quad\text{whenever}\quad\|f\|_{p}<\infty,

whereas the same does not hold for
1\leq p\leq 4/3
or
p\geq 4
.

A special case of our general result asserts that for all
1<p<\infty

one has

(1.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\|_{p}=0\quad\text{if}\quad\|f\|_{p}<\infty,

where
\chi_{n}
are characteristic functions of sufficiently slowly increasing subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, namely

(1.3)

\chi_{n}=\chi_{(-T_{n},T_{n})}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{n}=o(n^{1/34})\text{ as $n\to\infty$}.

We work with an expression for the truncated partial sum

(1.4)

\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n}),\quad n=0,1,\dots,

due to G. Sansone, which is a refinement of the one employed
by J.V. Uspensky
[
16
]
to prove his classical pointwise
convergence theorems for the Hermite series.
The heart of truncated partial sum operator (
1.4
)
is the Dirichlet operator

F_{N}(f\chi_{n})(x)=\int_{-T_{n}}^{T_{n}}\frac{\sin\bigl(N(x-y)\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}

with
N\in\mathbb{R}
being tied to
n
.
It is through this fact that we are
able to connect the truncated partial sum (
1.4
) to
another well-known operator of a certain function related to
f
,
namely to the Stieltjes transform
S

defined for measurable function
g
on
(0,\infty)
by

Sg(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g(s)}{t+s}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\quad\text{for $t\geq 0$}.

General result

The general result involves so-called rearrangement-invariant (r.i.
) norms,
a general framework at the intersection of measure theory and functional analysis
which abstracts Lebesgue, Lorentz, Orlicz and many more customary function spaces,
see e.g.

[
2
]
.
The definitions are summarised at the beginning of Section
3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\rho_{X}
and
\rho_{Y}
be r.i.
norms
such that
\varphi_{X}(0+)=0
and
X_{b}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq Y(\mathbb{R})
.
Suppose that
\chi_{n}
is taken as in (
1.3
).
Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

The norm
\rho_{Y}
obeys (
4.3
) and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\bigr\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}=0\quad\text{for every $f\in X_{b}(\mathbb{R})$};

(ii)

The Stieltjes transform is bounded between
X(0,\infty)
and
Y(0,\infty)
, i.e.

\bigl\|Sg\bigr\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\lesssim\|g\|_{X(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for every $g\in X(0,\infty)$}.

Recall that
\varphi_{X}
denotes the fundamental function, i.e.

\varphi_{X}(t)
is the norm of
\chi_{E}

for any set of measure
t
and
X_{b}(\mathbb{R})
is a closure of simple functions in
X(\mathbb{R})
.
The assumption can be equivalently formulated as that closure in
X
of smooth and compactly supported
functions is contained in
Y
, see Lemma
4.1
.

The convergence of the truncated partial sum (
1.4
) therefore reduces to the study of the
boundedness of Stieltjes transform
S
.
In the scope of r.i.
spaces, an exhaustive treatment of optimal spaces for
S
is available in
[
7
, Chapter 5]
.
Examples of optimal pairs of Lorentz-Zygmund spaces are contained in
[
7
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Orlicz modular convergence

One can also apply Theorem
1.1
to Orlicz norm by employing a well-known analysis of Stieltjes transform in Orlicz spaces, see e.g.

[
5
]
.
Our next result characterizes the convergence when the norm in
(i)
of Theorem
1.1

is replaced by Orlicz modular.

Recall that a function
A\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
is called a Young function if

A
is left-continuous, convex, satisfying
A(0)=0
, and not constant in
(0,\infty)
.
Next
\widetilde{A}(t)=\sup\{\tau t-A(\tau):\tau\geq 0\}
denotes the conjugate Young function to
A
.
For a Young function
A
, denote

(1.5)

E^{A}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\text{measurable}:\textstyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}A\bigl(c|f|\bigr)<\infty\text{ for all $c>0$}\right\}.

If
A
is finite-valued then
E^{A}(\mathbb{R})
coincides with the closure of smooth,
compactly supported functions on
\mathbb{R}
in the norm of the corresponding Orlicz
space, see Section
6
for details.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be Young functions with

B(t)\leq A(ct)
for some
c>0
independent of
t>0
.
Assume that
A
is finite-valued and
B
obeys

(1.6)

\int_{0}^{\infty}B\Bigl(\kappa\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{t}\right)\Bigr)\,{\mathrm{d}}t<\infty\quad\text{for some $\kappa>0$}.

Then, given
\chi_{n}
as in (
1.3
),
the following are equivalent:

(i)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}B\bigl(|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f|\bigr)=0\quad\text{for every $f\in E^{A}(\mathbb{R})$};

(ii)

There exists
K>0
such that

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{B(s)}{s^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\frac{A(Kt)}{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\widetilde{A}(s)}{s^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\frac{\widetilde{B}(Kt)}{t}\quad\text{for $t>0$}.

Remark 1.3
.

If
(ii)
holds, then the theorem’s assumptions are satisfied.
Indeed, by monotonicity of
B
,

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{B(s)}{s^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\geq\int_{t/2}^{t}\frac{B(s)}{s^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\geq B\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\int_{t/2}^{t}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}s}{s^{2}}=\tfrac{2}{t}B\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right),

and the first inequality in
(ii)
, in fact, implies
that
B(t)\leq A(2Kt)
for
t>0
.
Furthermore,
(ii)
also yields (
1.6
)
as shown in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let us turn our attention to some special cases and examples.
A Young function
A
is said to satisfy the
\Delta_{2}

condition if there is
C>0
such that
A(2t)\leq CA(t)

for
t>0
. We write
A\in\Delta_{2}
. It is well known that

A\in\Delta_{2}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\widetilde{A}(s)}{s^{2}}\leq\frac{\widetilde{A}(Kt)}{t}\quad\text{for $t>0$}

for some
K>0
. Next,
A
obeys the
\nabla_{2}
condition if there
is
c>0
such that
A(t)\leq\frac{1}{2c}A(ct)
for
t>0
. This we denote
by
A\in\nabla_{2}
.
It is immediate that
A\in\nabla_{2}
if and only if
\widetilde{A}\in\Delta_{2}
.
Another importance of the
\Delta_{2}
condition arises in connection
with the
E^{A}(\mathbb{R})
space. Namely,

E^{A}(\mathbb{R})=L^{A}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{if and only if}\quad A\in\Delta_{2},

where
L^{A}(\mathcal{R})
is an Orlicz space defined by

L^{A}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\text{measurable}:\textstyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}A\bigl(c|f|\bigr)<\infty\text{ for some $c>0$}\right\}.

This yields an immediate consequence.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
A
be a Young function obeying
A\in\Delta_{2}
and
A\in\nabla_{2}
.
Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}A\bigl(|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f|\bigr)=0\quad\text{for every $f\in L^{A}(\mathbb{R})$}.

Example 1.5
.

A typical example of a Young function satisfying the hypothesis of
Corollary
1.4
is the function
A(t)=t^{p}
for
t>0
with

p\in(1,\infty)
. Here,
L^{A}(\mathbb{R})=L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
, a customary Lebesgue space, and
Corollary
1.4
reads as the special case stated in (
1.2
).

Perturbations
L^{A}(\mathbb{R})
of the
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
spaces in which

(1.7)

A(t)=\begin{cases}t^{p}(\log\frac{1}{t})^{\alpha_{0}}&\text{near zero}\\
t^{p}(\log t)^{\alpha_{\infty}}&\text{near infinity}\end{cases}

with
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
also obey

A\in\Delta_{2}
and
A\in\nabla_{2}
and can serve as examples of Young functions
that Corollary
1.4
applies to as well.

Limiting cases of (
1.7
) as
p\to 1^{+}
and
p\to\infty
are captured in the next example.

Example 1.6
.

Let
A
and
B
be Young functions satisfying

A(t)=\begin{cases}t(\log\tfrac{1}{t})^{\alpha_{0}+1}&\text{near zero}\\
t(\log t)^{\alpha_{\infty}+1}&\text{near infinity}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad B(t)=\begin{cases}t(\log\tfrac{1}{t})^{\alpha_{0}}&\text{near zero}\\
t(\log t)^{\alpha_{\infty}}&\text{near infinity},\end{cases}

in which
\alpha_{0}<-1
and
\alpha_{\infty}\geq 0
.
Then
A\in\Delta_{2}
and
A
with
B
obey assumption
(ii)

of Theorem
1.2
.

Another admissible example of Young functions
A
and
B

is

A(t)=\begin{cases}\exp(-t^{\beta_{0}})&\text{near zero}\\
\exp t^{\beta_{\infty}}&\text{near infinity}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad B(t)=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-t^{\frac{\beta_{0}}{1-\beta_{0}}}\right)&\text{near zero}\\
\exp\left(t^{\frac{\beta_{\infty}}{1+\beta_{\infty}}}\right)&\text{near infinity}\end{cases}

with
\beta_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{\infty}>0
.

Theorem
1.2
is a more explicit form of an assertion involving
so-called rearrangement-invariant function norms. The general result,
Theorem
1.1
, is stated and proved in Sec.
5
and applied to
Orlicz context in Sec.
6
. Before that, though, we obtain estimates
of certain terms in an expression for truncated partial sum
(
1.4
) due to Sansone in Sec.
2
. These estimates
are then used in certain pointwise and norm inequalities in Sec.
3

to get technical results in Sec.
4
used in the proofs of all the
statements presented.

2.
The Sansone estimates

By
A\lesssim B
and
A\gtrsim B
we mean that
A\leq C\,B
and
A\geq C\,B
,
respectively, where
C
is a positive constant independent of the appropriate
quantities involved in
A
and
B
.
For brevity, we write
\chi_{T}=\chi_{(-T,T)}
for
T\in(0,\infty]
.
We will make use of another well-known classical operator, namely the Hilbert transform
given for a measurable
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

Hf(x)=\text{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(y)}{x-y}\,{\mathrm{d}}y\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}

whenever the integral exists.

Now, we recover the pointwise estimates of partial sums of the Hermite series,
based on the monograph by G. Sansone
[
14
]
. We keep most of the Sansone’s
notation, but we also made several adjustments.
According to
[
14
, p. 372, Eq. (5)]
, we have

(2.1)

S_{n}(f\chi_{T})(x)=\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}\int_{-T}^{T}k_{n}(x,y)f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y,

where

(2.2)

k_{n}(x,y)=\frac{h_{n}(x)h_{n+1}(y)-h_{n}(y)h_{n+1}(x)}{x-y}.

By
[
14
, p. 325, Eq. (14
1
) and (14
2
)]
,
we can express the Hermite functions for odd and even
n
by different formula
as follows.

h_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle h_{n}(0)\biggl[\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)+\frac{x^{3}}{6}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}+R(n,x)\biggr]&\text{if $n$ is even}\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{h^{\prime}_{n}(0)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\biggl[\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)-\frac{x^{3}}{6}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}+R(n,x)\biggr]&\text{if $n$ is odd},\end{cases}

where the remainder
R
enjoys the estimate

|R(n,x)|\lesssim\omega(n,x),

in which

(2.3)

\omega(x,n)=x^{2}(x^{4}+1)n^{-1}+x^{17/2}n^{-5/4}

as follows by
[
14
, p. 327, Eq. (15
1
), (15
2
) and p. 374]
.

Now, we let us compute the kernel
k_{n}
if
n
is even. We obtain
(compare with
[
14
, p. 373, Eq. (7)]
)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}(x-y)k_{n}(x,y)=\\
-c_{n}&\biggl[\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)+\frac{x^{3}}{6}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}+R(n,x)\biggr]\\
\times&\biggl[\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)-\frac{y^{3}}{6}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+3}}+R(n+1,y)\biggr]\\
+c_{n}&\biggl[\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)+\frac{y^{3}}{6}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}+R(n,y)\biggr]\\
\times&\biggl[\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)-\frac{x^{3}}{6}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+3}}+R(n+1,x)\biggr].\end{split}

Here, by

[
14
, p. 373, Eq. (8)]
,

(2.5)

c_{n}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}\frac{h_{n}(0)\,h^{\prime}_{n+1}(0)}{\sqrt{2n+3}}=1+\frac{\varepsilon}{6n}\quad\text{and}\quad|\varepsilon|<3.

Multiplying all the terms in (
2.4
) and plugging them into
(
2.1
), we get

(2.6)

S_{n}(f\chi_{T})(x)=c_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{7}\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{k}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y

in which

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{1}^{(n)}(x,y)&=\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)\\
&\quad-\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr),\end{split}

\displaystyle K_{2}^{(n)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2n+1}}\Bigl[-x^{3}\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+y^{3}\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)\Bigr],

\displaystyle K_{3}^{(n)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2n+3}}\Bigl[y^{3}\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-x^{3}\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)\Bigr],

\displaystyle K_{4}^{(n)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{3}y^{3}}{36\sqrt{(2n+1)(2n+3)}}\Bigl[\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)\Bigr],

\displaystyle K_{5}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=R(n+1,x)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)-R(n+1,y)\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+R(n,x)\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)-R(n,y)\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr),

\displaystyle K_{6}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=R(n+1,y)\frac{x^{3}}{6}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,x\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}-R(n+1,x)\frac{y^{3}}{6}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+1}}

\displaystyle\quad+R(n,y)\frac{x^{3}}{6}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,x\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+3}}-R(n,x)\frac{y^{3}}{6}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{\sqrt{2n+3}}

and

K_{7}^{(n)}=R(n+1,x)R(n,y)-R(n+1,y)R(n,x).

If
n
is odd, the kernel
k_{n}
and also the partial sum
S_{n}(f\chi_{T})

can be represented in an analogous way. We omit the details.

Let us estimate all the terms from (
2.6
).

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{1}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|+\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&=\bigl|H(m_{1}f_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m_{2}f_{T})(x)\bigr|,\quad x\in(-T,T),\end{split}

where
\|m_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(-T,T)}\leq 1
,
j=1,2
.
Next

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{2}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\frac{T^{3}}{6\sqrt{2n+1}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2n+1}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{y^{3}\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\lesssim\frac{T^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\bigl|H(m_{1}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigl|H(m_{3}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|,\quad x\in(-T,T),\end{split}

where
\|m_{3}\|_{L^{\infty}(-T,T)}\leq T^{3}
,
and similarly

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{3}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2n+3}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{y^{3}\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\frac{T^{3}}{6\sqrt{2n+3}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigl|H(m_{4}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\frac{T^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\bigl|H(m_{1}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|,\quad x\in(-T,T),\end{split}

where
\|m_{4}\|_{L^{\infty}(-T,T)}\leq T^{3}
.
For
K_{4}
, we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{4}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\frac{T^{3}}{36\sqrt{(2n+1)(2n+3)}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{y^{3}\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\frac{T^{3}}{36\sqrt{(2n+1)(2n+3)}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{y^{3}\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\lesssim\frac{T^{3}}{n}\Bigl[\bigl|H(m_{3}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m_{4}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|\Bigr],\quad x\in(-T,T),\end{split}

and for
K_{5}
, we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{5}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\bigl|R(n+1,x)\bigr|\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\bigl|R(n,x)\bigr|\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{R(n+1,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|+\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{R(n,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\lesssim\omega(T,n)\Bigl[\bigl|H(m_{1}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m_{2}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|\Bigr]\\
&\quad+\Bigl[\bigl|H(m^{(n)}_{5}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m^{(n)}_{6}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|\Bigr],\quad x\in(-T,T),\end{split}

where
\|m^{(n)}_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(-T,T)}\leq\omega(T,n)
,
j=5,6
.
Next

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{6}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\frac{\bigl|R(n+1,x)\bigr|}{6\sqrt{2n+1}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{y^{3}\sin\bigl(\sqrt{2n+1}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\frac{\bigl|R(n,x)\bigr|}{6\sqrt{2n+3}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{y^{3}\cos\bigl(\sqrt{2n+3}\,y\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\frac{T^{3}}{6\sqrt{2n+1}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{R(n+1,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\frac{T^{3}}{6\sqrt{2n+3}}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{R(n,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\lesssim\frac{\omega(T,n)}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigl[\bigl|H(m_{3}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m_{4}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|\Bigr]\\
&\quad+\frac{T^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigl[\bigl|H(m^{(n)}_{5}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m^{(n)}_{6}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|\Bigr],\quad x\in(-T,T).\end{split}

And finally

(2.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{K_{7}^{(n)}(x,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|&\leq\bigl|R(n+1,x)\bigr|\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{R(n,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\quad+\bigl|R(n,x)\bigr|\biggl|\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{R(n+1,y)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\biggr|\\
&\lesssim\omega(T,n)\Bigl[\bigl|H(m^{(n)}_{5}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|+\bigl|H(m^{(n)}_{6}f\chi_{T})(x)\bigr|\Bigr],\quad x\in(-T,T).\end{split}

Note that all of the above integrals are to be taken in the same Cauchy principal
value sense as the one for Hilbert transform.

3.
Rearrangement invariant spaces and ensuing inequalities

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote either
\mathbb{R}
or
(0,\infty)
. Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R})
be the class
of real-valued measurable functions on
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R})
the set of
nonnegative functions in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R})
. For
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R})
,

f^{*}\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
denotes the non-increasing rearrangement of

f
defined by

f^{*}(t):=\inf\bigl\{\lambda>0:|\{x\in\mathcal{R}:|f(x)|>\lambda\}|\leq t\bigr\}\quad\text{for $t>0$},

where
|E|
is the Lebesgue measure of a measurable set
E\subset\mathcal{R}
.

A mapping
\rho\colon\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R})\to[0,\infty]
is called a
rearrangement-invariant (r.i.
for short) Banach function norm on

\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R})
, if for all
f
,
g
,
f_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, in

\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R})
, for all constants
a\geq 0
and for every measurable
subset
E
of
\mathcal{R}
, the following properties hold:

(P1)

\rho(f)=0
iff
f=0
a.e.
;

\rho(af)=a\rho(f)
;

\rho(f+g)\leq\rho(f)+\rho(g)

(P2)

0\leq f\leq g
a.e.
implies
\rho(f)\leq\rho(g)

(P3)

0\leq f_{n}\uparrow f
a.e.
implies
\rho(f_{n})\uparrow\rho(f)

(P4)

|E|<\infty
implies
\rho(\chi_{E})<\infty

(P5)

|E|<\infty
implies
\int_{E}f\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq c_{E}\rho(f)

(P6)

\rho(f)=\rho(g)
whenever
f^{*}=g^{*}

for some constant
0<c_{E}<\infty
depending on
E
but independent of
f
.
We also call
\rho
just
r.i.\hbox{}
norm for brevity.

Given r.i.
norm
\rho_{X}
,
the collection
X(\mathcal{R})
of all functions
f
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R})

for which
\|f\|_{X(\mathcal{R})}=\rho_{X}(|f|)
is finite is called an rearrangement-invariant
Banach function space (or just r.i.
space).
For
E\subset\mathcal{R}
measurable, we write

\|f\|_{X(E)}=\|f\chi_{E}\|_{X(\mathcal{R})}.

A fundamental result of Luxemburg
[
2
, Chapter 2, Theorem 4.10]

asserts that to every r.i.
space
X(\mathcal{R})
there corresponds
an r.i.
space
X(0,\infty)
such that

\|f\|_{X(\mathcal{R})}=\|f^{*}\|_{X(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for $f\in X(\mathcal{R})$}.

This space is called the representation
space of
X(\mathcal{R})
. Clearly, if
\mathcal{R}=(0,\infty)
, the space
X(\mathcal{R})

and its representation space coincide.

The following estimate of Hilbert transform is well known.
It asserts that
there is a constant
C>0
such that
for any
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
.

(3.1)

(Hf)^{*}(t)\leq C\,Sf^{*}(t)\quad\text{for $t\in(0,\infty)$.}

The proof can be found in

[
2
, Chapter 3, Theorem 4.7]
. This inequality yields an immediate consequence.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\rho_{Z}
be an r.i.
norm and let
T\in(0,\infty]
.
Assume that
f,m\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

\|H(mf\chi_{T})\|_{Z(-T,T)}\lesssim\|m\|_{L^{\infty}(-T,T)}\|Sf^{*}\|_{Z(0,\infty)}.

The next result shows an inequality between
Hermite partial sums and the Stieltjes transform.
Its proof is a consequence of the Sansone estimates
introduced in Sec.

4.
Density results

We recall a few notions related to r.i.
spaces first.
Let
\rho_{X}
be an r.i.
norm. Its fundamental function

\varphi_{X}
is given for
t\geq 0
by

\varphi_{X}(t)=\rho_{X}(\chi_{E_{t}}),

in which
E_{t}\subset\mathcal{R}
is any measurable set obeying
|E_{t}|=t
.
Fundamental function is nondecreasing and absolutely continuous except
perhaps at the origin.

A function
f
in r.i.
space
X(\mathcal{R})
is said to have absolutely continuous norm
if
\|f\|_{E_{n}}\to 0
for every sequence
\{E_{n}\}
of measurable sets in
\mathcal{R}

such that
\chi_{E_{n}}\to 0
a.e. The set of all functions in
X(\mathcal{R})
of
absolutely continuous norm is denoted by
X_{a}(\mathcal{R})
.

Any function of the form of a finite sum
\sum a_{n}\chi_{E_{n}}
, where

\{a_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\{E_{n}\}
are measurable sets in
\mathcal{R}
, is called a
simple function. Simple functions belong to any r.i.
space. The closure of the
set of simple functions in r.i.
space
X(\mathcal{R})
is denoted by
X_{b}(\mathcal{R})
.

A connection between the sets
X_{a}(\mathbb{R})
,
X_{b}(\mathbb{R})

and the closure of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
is the subject of the next result.
Recall that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the set of infinitely-differentiable and
compactly-supported functions in
\mathbb{R}
.

|y|\geq 1
. Therefore
we may finish (
4.6
) by

|F_{N}f(x)|\leq\frac{C}{\pi N}\int_{x-T}^{x+T}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}y}{|y|}=\frac{C}{\pi N}\log\biggl(1+\frac{2T}{|x-T|}\biggr).

Finally,

\displaystyle\|F_{N}f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R])}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\pi N}\left\|\log\left(1+\tfrac{2T}{|x-T|}\right)\right\|_{Y(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R])}=\frac{2C}{\pi N}\left\|\log\left(1+\tfrac{2T}{x-T}\right)\right\|_{Y(2T+1,\infty)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2C}{\pi N}\left\|D_{2T}\left(\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{x}\right)\right)\right\|_{Y(T+1,\infty)}\leq\frac{4TC}{\pi N}\left\|\log\left(1+\frac{1}{x}\right)\right\|_{Y(0,\infty)},

where the last inequality is due to the boundedness of dilatation operator
(
4.1
) on
Y
.
Since the last norm is finite by assumption (
4.3
),
we conclude that

\|F_{N}f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R])}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\rho_{Y}
be an r.i.
norm satisfying (
4.3
)
and let
\chi_{n}
be as in (
1.3
).
Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\chi_{n}S_{n}f-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}=0\quad\text{for every $f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})$}.

[
Proof]Let
f
be given. Since it is compactly supported,
we may, without loss of generality, assume that
the support is contained in
(-T_{n_{1}},T_{n_{1}})
.
We have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\chi_{n}S_{n}f-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|S_{n}f-c_{n}F_{N}f\|_{Y(-T_{n},T_{n})}+|c_{n}-1|\|F_{N}f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}+\|F_{N}f-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})},\end{split}

where
N
and
c_{n}
are defined by (
3.4
) and (
2.5
),
respectively.

An application of Lemmas
3.3
and
3.1
shows that

(4.8)

\|S_{n}f-c_{n}F_{N}f\|_{Y(-T_{n},T_{n})}\lesssim\beta(T_{n},n)\|Sf^{*}\|_{Y(0,\infty)}.

Next, since
f^{*}
is bounded and supported in
(0,T_{n_{1}})

(4.9)

Sf^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{T_{n_{1}}}\frac{f^{*}(s)}{s+t}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq C\log\biggl(1+\frac{T_{n_{1}}}{t}\biggr)

with
C=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
.
Therefore, as the dilatation operator (
4.1
) is bounded,

\|Sf^{*}\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\leq C\left\|D_{T_{n_{1}}}\!\left(\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{t}\right)\right)\right\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\leq CT_{n_{1}}\left\|\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{t}\right)\right\|_{Y(0,\infty)},

and consequently, thanks to assumption (
4.3
),

(4.10)

\|Sf^{*}\|_{Y(0,\infty)}<\infty.

Since
\beta(T_{n},n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
due to the choice of
T_{n}
in
(
1.3
), inequality (
4.8
) ensures that the first term of
(
4.7
) vanishes as
n\to\infty
.

As for the second term of (
4.7
), we claim that

(4.11)

\|F_{N}f\|_{Z(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|Sf^{*}\|_{Z(0,\infty)}.

Indeed, by definition, we have

\displaystyle F_{N}f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin\bigl(N(x-y)\bigr)}{x-y}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle=\sin(Nx)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cos(Ny)\frac{f(y)}{x-y}\,{\mathrm{d}}y-\cos(Nx)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sin(Ny)\frac{f(y)}{x-y}\,{\mathrm{d}}y\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$},

and thus

\|F_{N}f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|H(m_{1}f)\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}+\|H(m_{2}f)\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})},

in which
\|m_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=1
,

and (

) follows by
Lemma
3.1
. Inequality (

\lim_{n\to\infty}|c_{n}-1|\|F_{N}f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}=0,

since

as

.

Finally, the third term of (

Lemma
4.2
.

5.
General result and proof of Theorem
1.1

Before we present the proof, we need an auxiliary result
asserting that the convergence problem reduces to a uniform
boundedness of all the partial sum operators.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\rho_{X}
and
\rho_{Y}
be r.i.
norms.
Suppose that
\varphi_{X}(0+)=0
,
X_{b}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq Y(\mathbb{R})

and
\rho_{Y}
obeys (
4.3
).
The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}=0\quad\text{for every $X_{b}(\mathbb{R})$};

(ii)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\;\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|f\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}\quad\text{for every $f\in X_{b}(\mathbb{R})$}.

[
Proof]To show the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i), denote

M=\bigl\{f\in X_{b}(\mathbb{R}):\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\to 0\bigr\}.

Our aim is to show that
X_{b}\subseteq M
.
Lemma
4.1
asserts that under the assumption
\varphi_{X}(0+)=0

the space
X_{b}(\mathbb{R})
coincides with the closure of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
in
X(\mathbb{R})
,
proving that

\overline{\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})}^{X}\subseteq M
.
By Lemma
4.3
, we have that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})\subset M
. Therefore, it remains
to show that
M
is closed in
X(\mathbb{R})
.
To this end, suppose that
\{f_{k}\}
is a sequence of
functions from
M
converging to
f
in the norm of
X(\mathbb{R})
.
Thanks to the embedding
X_{b}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq Y(\mathbb{R})
, we have

(5.1)

\|f-f_{k}\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|f-f_{k}\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}

for all
k
.
Then, using (
5.1
) and
(ii)
,

\displaystyle\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\|S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-S_{n}(f_{k}\chi_{n})\|_{Y(-T_{n},T_{n})}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f_{k}\chi_{n})-f_{k}\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}+\|f_{k}-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f-f_{k}\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}+\|S_{n}f_{k}-f_{k}\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}.

Now for a given
\varepsilon>0
take
k_{0}
sufficiently large so that

\|f_{k_{0}}-f\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}<\varepsilon
. To this
k_{0}
, we can select
a
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f_{k}\chi_{n})-f_{k}\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}<\varepsilon
for every

n\geq n_{0}
. Therefore
\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\varepsilon

for
n\geq n_{0}
, proving that
f\in M
.

The converse follows immediately by the uniform boundedness principle.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
also requires the notion
of associate space.
With r.i.
norm
\rho
, the functional
\rho^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathbb{R}

given by

\rho^{\prime}(g)=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathcal{R}}fg\,{\mathrm{d}}x:f\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R}),\,\rho(f)\leq 1\right\}.

is also an r.i.
norm, called the associate norm of
\rho
. If

X(\mathcal{R})
is the r.i.
space determined by an r.i.
norm
\rho_{X}
, then the r.i.
space
X^{\prime}(\mathcal{R})
determined by
\rho_{X}^{\prime}
is called the associate space of

X(\mathcal{R})
. It holds that
(X^{\prime})^{\prime}(\mathcal{R})=X(\mathcal{R})
, see
[
2
, Chapter 1,
Theorem 2.7]
. Especially,

(5.2)

\rho(f)=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathcal{R}}fg\,{\mathrm{d}}x:f\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathcal{R}),\,\rho^{\prime}(g)\leq 1\right\}.

[
Proof of Theorem
1.1
]
Lemma
5.1
asserts that
(i)
is equivalent to
the following statement.

(i)’

The norm
\rho_{Y}
obeys (
4.3
) and

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\;\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|f\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}\quad\text{for every $f\in X_{b}(\mathbb{R})$}.

Next, we prove that
(ii)
is in fact equivalent to a boundedness
on a slightly restricted domain.

(ii)’

The Stieltjes transform is bounded between
X_{b}(0,\infty)
and
Y(0,\infty)
, i.e.

\bigl\|Sg\bigr\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\lesssim\|g\|_{X(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for every $g\in X_{b}(0,\infty)$}.

Clearly,
(ii)
implies
(ii)’
. Conversely,
(ii)’
asserts
that there is
C>0
such that

(5.3)

\sup\left\{\|Sg\|_{Y}:g\in X_{b},\,\|g\|_{X}\leq 1\right\}\leq C.

Note that all the spaces in this paragraph are taken over
(0,\infty)
.
Next, by (
5.2
),

(5.4)

\|Sg\|_{Y(0,\infty)}=\sup\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}Sg(x)f(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x:f\in Y^{\prime},\,\|f\|_{Y^{\prime}}\leq 1\right\},

where
Y^{\prime}
denotes the associate space of
Y
.
Clearly,

(5.5)

\int_{0}^{\infty}Sg(x)f(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)Sf(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

due to Fubini’s theorem.
Using (
5.5
) and (
5.4
), (
5.3
) is equivalent
to

(5.6)

\sup\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)Sf(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x:g\in X_{b},\,f\in Y^{\prime},\,\|g\|_{X}\leq 1,\,\|f\|_{Y^{\prime}}\leq 1\right\}\leq C.

The result of
[
2
, Chapter 1, Theorem 3.12]

asserts that the supremum over
X_{b}
coincides with the one over
X
, that is

\sup\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x:g\in X_{b},\,\|g\|_{X}\leq 1,\,\right\}=\sup\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x:g\in X,\,\|g\|_{X}\leq 1,\,\right\}

for any eligible
h
. In conclusion, we may replace
X_{b}
by
X
in inequality
(
5.6
). We use (
5.5
) and (
5.4
) again, to arrive back
to (
5.3
) with
X_{b}
replaced by
X
which is nothing but
(ii)
.

Now, it is in turn enough to proove the equivalence of
(i)’
and
(ii)’
.
Let us prove that
(i)’
implies
(ii)’
first.
Let
g\in X_{b}(0,\infty)
. We may assume that
g
is nonnegative. Using Lemma
3.4

and lower semi-continuity of r.i.
norm, we obtain

(5.7)

\|Sg\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\lesssim\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\sum_{m=0}^{3}\bigl\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f_{k,m}\chi_{n})\bigr\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})},

where the functions
f_{k,m}
are given by (
3.8
). By their definition,

(5.8)

\|f_{k,m}\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|g\|_{X(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for all eligible $k$, $m$ and $n$.}

Also
f_{k,m}\in X_{b}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, we may apply the uniform bound from

(i)’
, and (
5.7
) with (
5.8
) yield

\|Sg\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\lesssim\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\sum_{m=0}^{3}\|f_{k,m}\|_{X(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|g\|_{X(0,\infty)},

proving
(ii)’
.

Conversely, assume that
(ii)’
holds and let
f\in X_{b}(\mathbb{R})
.
Lemma
3.2
implies that

\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})\|_{Y(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\alpha(T_{n},n)\|Sf^{*}\|_{Y(0,\infty)}\lesssim\|f^{*}\|_{X(0,\infty)}=\|f\|_{X(\mathbb{R})},

where we used the assumption with
g=f^{*}\in X_{b}(0,\infty)

and the fact that
\alpha(Tn,n)
obeying (
3.3
)
is bounded, by the choice of the sequence
T_{n}
in (
1.3
).
This proves the inequality in
(i)’
.
It remains to show that
\rho_{Y}
satisfies (
4.3
).
Let us set
g=\chi_{(0,1)}
. Then
g\in X_{b}(0,\infty)

and

Sg(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}s}{s+t}=\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{t}\right)\quad\text{for $t\in(0,\infty)$}

and (
4.3
) follows by
(ii)’
, since

.

6.
Orlicz spaces and proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we restrict the general result to the Orlicz setting and prove the
main result from the introduction, Theorem
1.2
.
We start by recalling definitions and basic properties of Orlicz spaces.
See
[
2
, Chapter 4, Section 8]
for details and
as well
[
13
]
for the interesting history of these spaces.
Let
A
be a Young function and let
\mathcal{R}
denote
\mathbb{R}
or
(0,\infty)
.
The Orlicz space
L^{A}(\mathcal{R})
is defined by

L^{A}(\mathcal{R})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R}):\int_{\mathcal{R}}A(c|f|)<\infty\text{ for some $c>0$}\right\}

and equipped with Luxemburg norm

\|f\|_{L^{A}(\mathcal{R})}=\rho_{A}(|f|)=\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\int_{\mathcal{R}}A(|f|/\lambda)<\infty\right\}.

The functional
\rho_{A}
is an r.i.
Banach function norm.
Its fundamental function, denoted by
\varphi_{A}
, obeys

(6.1)

\varphi_{A}(t)=\frac{1}{A^{-1}(1/t)}\quad\text{for $t>0$},

where
A^{-1}
stands for the generalised right-continuous inverse of
A
.
For Young functions
A
and
B
, one has that
L^{A}(\mathcal{R})\subseteq L^{B}(\mathcal{R})

if and only if there is
c>0
such that
B(t)\leq A(ct)
for
t\geq 0
.
The space
E^{A}(\mathcal{R})
, defined by

E^{A}(\mathcal{R})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R}):\int_{\mathcal{R}}A(c|f|)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu<\infty\text{ for all $c>0$}\right\},

coincides with the subspace of functions in
L^{A}(\mathcal{R})
having an
absolutely continuous norm that is

(6.2)

L^{A}_{a}(\mathcal{R})=E^{A}(\mathcal{R}),

see
[
12
, Theorem 4.12.13]
.
With
f_{n},f\in L^{A}(\mathcal{R})
,

n\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

(6.3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{A}(\mathcal{R})}=0

if and only if

(6.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{R}}A\bigl(\lambda|f-f_{n}|\bigr)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu=0\quad\text{for all $\lambda>0$.}

The proof that (
6.3
) implies (
6.4
)
is given in
[
13
, Section 3.4, Theorem 12]
.
Moreover, as remarked in
[
13
, Section 3.4, Remark, p. 87]
,
Morse and Transue have shown the converse in their
paper
[
11
]
, where it appears as an
observation necessary to a rather involved argument.

[
Proof of Theorem
1.2
]
Set
\rho_{X}=\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{Y}=\rho_{B}
. Let us verify the
hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
. By (
6.1
),

\varphi_{A}(s)=1/A^{-1}(1/s)
for
s>0
. Therefore

\varphi(0+)=0
if and only if
A^{-1}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
This happens if and only if
A
is finite-valued.
Next,
L^{A}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq L^{B}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
B(t)\leq A(ct)

for
t\geq 0
with global constant
c>0
. Finally,
\rho_{B}

obeys (
4.3
) if and only if (
1.6
) holds, by the
very definition of the Luxemburg norm.

Theorem
1.1
now asserts that (
1.6
) holds and

(6.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f\|_{L^{B}(\mathbb{R})}=0\quad\text{for every $f\in L^{A}_{b}(\mathbb{R})$}

if and only if

(6.6)

\|Sg\|_{L^{B}(0,\infty)}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{A}(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for every $f\in L^{A}(0,\infty)$}.

Let us analyze (
6.5
) first.
Lemma
4.1
together with (
6.2
) ensures that

(6.7)

L^{A}_{b}(\mathbb{R})=\overline{\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})}^{L^{A}}=L^{A}_{a}(\mathbb{R})=E^{A}(\mathbb{R}),

in particular,
L^{A}_{b}(\mathbb{R})=E^{A}(\mathbb{R})
.
For
h\in E^{A}(\mathbb{R})
, the limit holds if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}B\bigl(\lambda|\chi_{n}S_{n}(h\chi_{n})-h|\bigr)=0

for every
\lambda>0
, due to the equivalence of (
6.3
)
and (
6.4
).
Setting
f=\lambda h
, we still have
f\in E^{A}(\mathbb{R})

and thus, (
6.5
) is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}B\bigl(|\chi_{n}S_{n}(f\chi_{n})-f|\bigr)=0\quad\text{for every $f\in E^{A}(\mathbb{R})$},

which is
(i)
.

Let us focus on (
6.6
). For
g\in\mathcal{M}(0,\infty)
, define

Pg(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s\quad\text{and}\quad Qg(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{g(s)}{s}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\quad\text{for $t>0$}.

Then

Pg(t)+Qg(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\left\{\tfrac{1}{s},\tfrac{1}{t}\right\}g(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s

and, since

\frac{1}{s+t}\leq\min\left\{\tfrac{1}{s},\tfrac{1}{t}\right\}\leq\frac{2}{s+t}\quad\text{for $s,t>0$},

we infer that
Sg\leq Pg+Qg\leq 2Sg
. Consequently, as
P
and
Q
are positive
operators, (
6.6
) holds if and only if

(6.8)

\|Pg\|_{L^{B}(0,\infty)}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{A}(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for every $f\in L^{A}(0,\infty)$}

and

(6.9)

\|Qg\|_{L^{B}(0,\infty)}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{A}(0,\infty)}\quad\text{for every $f\in L^{A}(0,\infty)$}

hold simultaneously.
Finally, the fact that (
6.8
)
and (
6.9
) are characterised by the first and
the second inequality of
(ii)
, respectively,
is well know, see
[
6
,
5
,
8
,
10
,
9
]
for instance.
