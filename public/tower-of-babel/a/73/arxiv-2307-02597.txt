Euler-Bernoulli beams with contact forces: existence, uniqueness, and numerical solutions Thanks: This work was funded by NSERC DG, CRC, and ERA programs.
Mohamed A. Serry, Sean D. Peterson, Jun Liu

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the Euler-Bernoulli fourth-order boundary value problem (BVP) w ( 4 ) = f ⁡ ( x , w ) w^{(4)}=f(x,w) , x ∈ [ a , b ] x\in[a,b] , with specified values of w w and w ′′ w^{\prime\prime} at the end points, where the behaviour of the right-hand side f f is motivated by biomechanical, electromechanical, and structural applications incorporating contact forces. In particular, we consider the case when f f is bounded above and monotonically decreasing with respect to its second argument. First, we prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the BVP. We then study numerical solutions to the BVP, where we resort to spatial discretization by means of finite difference. Similar to the original continuous-space problem, the discrete problem always possesses a unique solution. In the case of a piecewise linear instance of f f , the discrete problem is an example of the absolute value equation. We show that solutions to this absolute value equation can be obtained by means of fixed-point iterations, and that solutions to the absolute value equation converge to solutions of the continuous BVP. We also illustrate the performance of the fixed-point iterations through a numerical example.

Introduction

In biomechanical applications, especially those related to voice production, contact forces arise naturally. For example, prior to and during voice production, the vocal folds (VFs) typically experience contact forces as they touch each other and/or collide. In general, and due to the emergence of contact forces and the complexity of the air flow interacting with the VFs during phonation, voice-related models are typically non-smooth. Despite the non-smoothness, numerical solvers that are designed primarily for smooth systems are often implemented when solving phonation equations. Moreover, the nature of solutions (e.g., existence, uniqueness, smoothness, etc.) to these non-smooth equations is usually dismissed as they tend to be analytically intractable, and the effectiveness of numerical solvers (i.e., convergence) are tested only empirically. Consequently, a major portion of the numerical studies of voice-related models lack mathematical rigor, with no guarantees regarding the correctness and accuracy of the reported numerical solutions. Therefore, it is important to investigate voice-related models mathematically, when feasible, and elucidate the nature of their analytical and numerical solutions.

The VFs are often modelled as elastic structures, with incorporated repulsive contact forces that take place when the folds touch each other, with forces being proportional to the degree of compression (see, e.g.,

2
Problem formulation

As a generalization of the Euler-Bernoulli model with contact discussed in the introduction, we consider the fourth-order BVP over the compact interval
[a,b]
with respect to the function
w:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle w^{(4)}=f(x,w),

\displaystyle~x\in[a,b],

(1a)

\displaystyle w(a)=\alpha_{1},~w(b)=\alpha_{2},

\displaystyle~w^{\prime\prime}(a)=\beta_{1},~w^{\prime\prime}(b)=\beta_{2},

(1b)

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are specified boundary values, and
f:[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following set of properties:

\displaystyle f~\text{is continuous on }[a,b]\times\mathbb{R},

(2a)

\displaystyle f(x,y)\leq M~\forall(x,y)\in[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}~\text{for some}~M\in\mathbb{R},

(2b)

\displaystyle y_{1}\geq y_{2}\Rightarrow f(x,y_{1})\leq f(x,y_{2})~\forall x\in[a,b].

(2c)

These properties resemble different contact models with linear and nonlinear springs. A particular instance of
f
that will be investigated herein, which also satisfies assumptions (
2
) (with
M=0
), is

f(x,y)=-K(y-g(x))\mathbf{H}(y-g(x)),

(3)

where
g:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function defining a contact surface,
K\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a stiffness coefficient, and
\mathbf{H}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the Heaviside function.

Theorem 1
.

The BVP (
1
) with

satisfying

Proof.

The BVP (
1
) can be written in the equivalent integral form (see, e.g.,
[
28
]

w(x)=\bar{w}_{M}(x)+\int_{a}^{b}\tilde{G}(x,s)\left(\int_{a}^{b}\tilde{G}(s,t)f_{M}(t,w(t))\mathrm{d}t\right)\mathrm{d}s,~x\in[a,b],

(4)

where
\bar{w}_{M}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the fourth-order polynomial satisfying
\bar{w}^{(4)}=M

1b

\tilde{G}:[a,b]\times[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

f_{M}(\cdot,\cdot)\mathrel{:=}f(\cdot,\cdot)-M.

(5)

Note that
f_{M}
is continuous, nonpositive, and monotonically decreasing in its second argument due to assumptions (
2

is given by:

\begin{split}\bar{w}_{M}(x)=&\alpha_{1}+\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}}{b-a}-(\beta_{2}+2\beta_{1})\frac{b-a}{6}+\frac{M(b-a)^{3}}{24}\right)(x-a)+\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}(x-a)^{2}\\

(6)

and the Green’s function

\tilde{G}(x,s)=\frac{-1}{b-a}\begin{cases}(b-x)(s-a),&a\leq s\leq x\leq b,\\
(b-s)(x-a),&a\leq x\leq s\leq b.\end{cases}

(7)

Note that
\tilde{G}(x,s)\leq 0
and

Theorem 7
.

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{true}}

is absolutely continuous and equal to

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied a fourth-order boundary value problem with a bounded above and monotonically decreasing right-hand side, showing solution existence and uniqueness for both the original problem and its discrete counterpart obtained through finite difference. We then focused on a particular piecewise linear instance of the right-hand side, showing that the solution to the discrete equation can be obtained through fixed-point iterations and that the solution to the discrete equation converges to the solution of the continuous problem. We illustrated the effectiveness of the fixed-point iterations through a numerical example.

Typical voice production incorporates dynamic VF vibrations and time-varying contact forces (microswitches and beams on elastic foundations also often incorporate dynamic vibrations with contact forces). It would be of interest to extend the techniques covered herein to explore dynamic Euler-Bernoulli beam models with contract forces. Moreover, extensions of the analysis presented herein can be considered in future works to cover more sophisticated beam (e.g., Timoshenko model
[
45
]
) and plate models.
