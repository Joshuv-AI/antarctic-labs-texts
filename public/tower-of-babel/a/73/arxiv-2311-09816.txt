Performance Trade-offs of Watermarking Large Language Models
Anirudh Ajith, Sameer Singh, Danish Pruthi

Abstract
Amidst growing concerns of large language models (LLMs) being misused for generating misinformation or completing homework assignments, watermarking has emerged as an effective solution for distinguishing human-written and LLM-generated text. A prominent watermarking strategy is to embed a signal into generated text by upsampling a (pseudorandomly-chosen) subset of tokens at every generation step. Although this signal is imperceptible to a human reader, it is detectable through statistical testing. However, implanting such signals alters the model’s output distribution and can have unintended effects when watermarked LLMs are used for downstream applications. In this work, we evaluate the performance of watermarked LLMs on a diverse suite of tasks, including text classification, textual entailment, reasoning, question answering, translation, summarization, and language modeling. We find that watermarking has negligible impact on the performance of tasks posed as k k -class classification problems in the average case. However, the accuracy can plummet to that of a random classifier for some scenarios (that occur with non-negligible probability). Tasks that are cast as multiple-choice questions and short-form generation are surprisingly unaffected by watermarking. For long-form generation tasks, including summarization and translation, we see a drop of 15 15 - 20 20 % in the performance due to watermarking. Our findings highlight the trade-offs that users should be cognizant of when using watermarked models, and point to cases where future research could improve existing trade-offs.



1
Introduction

Capable of generating realistic human-like responses, large language models (LLMs) and derived chatbot applications (such as ChatGPT) can serve a wide variety of requests: from writing emails and articles
to translating, summarizing, and rewriting content
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Chowdhery et al. 2022
)
.
As these systems gain popularity, there are concerns they may be abused
to spread targeted misinformation and influence public opinion
(
Panditharatne and Giansiracusa 2023
)
or conduct social engineering attacks
(
Grbic and Dujlovic 2023
)
.
Recent months have witnessed an uptick in
students misusing these tools to complete their homework
(
Gecker 2023
)
.
Such concerns have spurred interest in methods for distinguishing human-written text from LLM-generated content.

One approach to differentiate human-authored and LLM-generated content is to train post-hoc classifiers that exploit the distributional differences between LLM generations and human writing. However, these classifiers often suffer from large false-positive rates and have been the cause for false accusations of plagiarism
(
O’Neill 2023
)
. OpenAI, citing low detection accuracy, has terminated their detection service
(
OpenAI 2023
)
. Additionally, as LLMs continue to improve and these distributional differences narrow, the effectiveness of such approaches may wane. The performance of such post-hoc classifiers is also likely to deteriorate as model developers fine-tune their models.

A promising alternative is to intentionally embed a ‘watermark’ signal into LLM-generated text that is imperceptible to an unsuspecting reader but can be algorithmically detected
(
Atallah et al. 2001
;
Chiang et al. 2004
;
Topkara et al. 2006
;
Jalil and Mirza 2009
)
.
Given the pressing need to detect generated content,
seven major corporations including OpenAI, Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon, Anthropic, and Inflection have recently committed to watermark their models
(
Press 2023
)
.
One prominent watermarking strategy for LLMs works by pseudo-randomly partitioning the model’s vocabulary tokens into ‘green’ and ‘red’ lists
at every generation step
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
.
To implant a watermark, the probabilities associated with tokens in the green list are artificially boosted before decoding a token. The watermark can later be verified by assessing the proportion of green tokens in a given piece of text (as content with a large fraction of green tokens is likely to be generated).
While the original paper studies the impact of watermarking on the language modeling ability (as measured by perplexity), it is unclear how watermarked models perform on downstream applications. This is particularly important to evaluate as many future LLMs will be watermarked.

In our work, we watermark LLaMA models
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
with
varying signal strengths covering light, medium and heavy watermarks,
and evaluate their performance on a
diverse selection of tasks spanning text classification, textual entailment, common-sense reasoning, question-answering, machine-translation and summarization.
To better understand our observations,
we group these tasks under four broad categories: (i) tasks that can be cast as
k
-class classification problems, (ii) tasks that are often posed as multiple choice questions (with question-specific options), (iii) short-form conditional generation, and (iv) long-form generation.
Specifically, we find that

•

For tasks that can be cast as
k
-class classification problems, e.g., sentiment analysis, textual entailment and boolean question answering, the impact of watermarking on performance is negligible in the average case. However, we find that performance can be severely impacted if the class tokens get segregated into different partitions by the watermarking scheme. The resulting systematic boosting of the logits of a subset of classes can reduce an LLMs accuracy to that of a random classifier.
We find that this can occur with prohibitively large probabilities (
18
-
25
%) for many tasks.

•

Tasks posed as multiple-choice question-answering problems such as some common-sense reasoning and several question-answering benchmarks only see negligible performance drops. We find that the examined watermarking scheme leaves model’s highest-likelihood alternative unchanged, thus resulting in the same prediction (as that from a unwatermarked model).

•

In short-form generation tasks such as question-answering benchmarks, we surprisingly notice little to no drop in performance. This is because the output distribution of a model on these tasks tends to be low entropy, assigning the majority of the probability mass to top few tokens. Boosting green list tokens therefore has little effect on the generation.

•

Long-form generation tasks such as summarizaiton and translation see noticeable performance drops of up to 20% as measured by typical translation and summarization metrics (eg. BLEU, ROUGE-L). Qualitatively, we observe that watermarking can result in responses that are factually inaccurate, or cause the model to degenerate into repetitions of short token-sequences (especially for higher watermarking strengths).

Our findings about the downstream implications of watermarking LLMs (and the key factors underlying them) will allow model developers and users to make informed choices when watermarking their models and adapting them. We hope that our results will also spur the development of novel watermarking schemes and decoding strategies for LLMs that exhibit better trade-offs between detection accuracy and downstream performance.

Background

The watermarking scheme proposed by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
is a deterministic algorithm parameterized by 3 hyperparameters
\gamma,\delta
and
k
, and a keyed psuedorandom function
F_{\cdot}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{g,r\}^{m}
.
It works by modifying the logits obtained from the language model at each generation step. Formally, given a language model
\mathcal{M}
with vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
and a prefix comprising tokens
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{n}
the scheme involves first computing the logits
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{w}_{1}\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{n})
of the language model that would ordinarily be used to predict the
(n+1)
th token. Next, the terminal token of the prefix
\mathbf{w}_{n}
is fed to
F
under the key
k
to obtain a partition of
\mathcal{V}
into a green list
\mathcal{G}
and a red list
\mathcal{R}
such that
|\mathcal{G}|=\lfloor\gamma|\mathcal{V}|\rfloor
.

F_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{n})\in\{g,r\}^{|V|}

such that
\sum_{x\in F_{k}(\mathbf{w_{n}})}\mathbbm{1}[x=g]=\left\lfloor\gamma|V|\right\rfloor.

That is, a fraction of the vocabulary,
\gamma
, is assigned to the green list
\mathcal{G}
by the partitioning function
F
.

Finally, the logits of each of the tokens in
\mathcal{G}
are boosted by
\delta>0
. This procedure is carried out at every generation step to obtain modified logits. These resultant logits can then be used for sampling tokens (for generation) or even computing the likelihood or perplexity of a given sequence.

When this scheme is used, it is likely that the fraction of tokens occurring from their green lists in the generated text will exceed
\gamma
. On the other hand, in any text written without knowledge of the watermarking scheme (such as typical human-written text), the expected fraction of tokens belonging to their green lists would be
\gamma
due to the psuedorandom nature of the partitioning operation. The watermark detection scheme proposed by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
works by assessing the plausibility of the null hypothesis that the given text was written with no knowledge of the watermarking scheme (and the hash key
k
). Specifically, given a token sequence
x
of length
T
that was written without knowledge of the scheme, the number of green list tokens in
x
, denoted by
|x|_{G}
, can be assumed to be normally distributed with a mean of
\gamma T
and a standard deviation of
\sqrt{T\gamma(1-\gamma)}
.
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
’s watermark detection scheme works by computing a z-score

z=(|x|_{G}-\gamma T)/\sqrt{T\gamma(1-\gamma)}

(1)

and rejecting the null hypothesis if this z-score exceeds a chosen threshold.

3
Evaluation Approach

Since it is likely that many commercially available LLMs will be watermarked in the future, it becomes important to understand the effect of watermarking on downstream tasks. We evaluate the effect of the watermarking scheme by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
on a diverse suite of tasks.
We categorize the considered tasks into four buckets for our study: (a)
tasks that are cast as classification
problems, (b) tasks posed as multiple-choice questions, and both (c) short-form conditional generation and (d) long-form conditional generation tasks.
In our study, we use the standard metrics to evaluate LLMs for each of these categories:

1.

Classification
tasks such as sentiment analysis and textual entailment form the bulk of NLP benchmarks used to evaluate language models today. In these tasks, the input text
\mathbf{x}
is formatted using a suitable prompt template
T
and the class
\mathbf{y}
which maximizes
p(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))
is chosen as the model’s prediction.

\hat{y}=\argmax_{\mathbf{y}\in L}p(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))

Prompt templates used with such tasks are typically such that
T(\mathbf{x})
always ends with the same token (e.g.,
\n
or
:
). Modifying the logits using a deterministic hash of the prompt’s terminal token could therefore introduce a systematic bias in the probabilities that the model assigns to the classes.
This could adversely affect classification accuracy if the logits of incorrect labels are boosted in test inputs.

2.

Several open-book question-answering, reading-comprehension and common-sense reasoning tasks are posed as
multiple-choice question-answering
tasks to language models. In these tasks, every test input
\mathbf{x}
is associated with a set of possible answer choices
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})
. When a test input
\mathbf{x}
is formatted using a suitable template
T
, the answer choice that the language model assigns the highest average log likelihood to,

\argmax_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})}\quad\text{avg-log-likelihood}(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))

is chosen as the model’s prediction.
It is conceivable that watermarking may increase the log-likelihoods of incorrect answer choices, thereby hurting model performance.

3.

Some open-domain question-answering and reading-comprehension tasks are posed to language models as
short-form conditional generation
tasks and require models to output concise free-form responses. Given a test input
\mathbf{x}
formatted using a prompt template
T
, the model produces a sequence
\mathbf{y^{*}}
which maximizes the conditional likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))
.

\mathbf{y}^{*}=\argmax_{\mathbf{y}}p(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))

Typically, the generated sequence is bounded by a certain length or concludes when the model outputs an end-of-sequence token. If watermarking biases the model towards certain tokens or sequences, it may lead to responses that are either incoherent or divergent from the expected answer, affecting the model’s performance, which is typically estimated by proportion of responses that exactly match the reference, or overlap with them.

4.

Long-form generation
includes tasks like machine translation and summarization. For an input text
\mathbf{x}
, formatted using an appropriate prompt template
T
, the model is tasked with producing an extended sequence
\mathbf{y^{*}}
that maximizes the conditional likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))
.

\mathbf{y}^{*}=\argmax_{\mathbf{y}}p(\mathbf{y}|T(\mathbf{x}))

The generated sequences are often evaluated against multiple ground truth references using metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU, allowing for some flexibility for paraphrases. Watermarking modifies the logits of all tokens, including salient tokens like proper nouns, and may adversely affect the overall performance.

3.1
Setup

Datasets.

We study the effect that the watermarking scheme by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
has on a collection of classification, multiple-choice QA and generation tasks. We select 2 classification tasks: MNLI and SST-2 from the GLUE benchmark
(
Wang et al. 2019b
)
, and 3 tasks: BoolQ, CB and RTE from the SuperGLUE benchmark
(
Wang et al. 2019a
)
.
These tasks span yes/no question answering, textual entailment and sentiment analysis. We select the commonsense NLI dataset HellaSwag
(
Zellers et al. 2019
)
, the commonsense QA dataset PIQA
(
Bisk et al. 2020
)
and the reading-comprehension dataset RACE
(
Lai et al. 2017
)
as our multiple-choice question-answering datasets. We use the reading comprehension datasets CoQA
(
Reddy et al. 2019
)
, DROP
(
Dua et al. 2019
)
and SQuAD
(
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
)
to evaluate the short-form conditional generation abilities of watermarked models and the WMT translation tasks En-Fr, En-De and En-Ro
(
Bojar et al. 2014
;
Bojar et al. 2016
;
Barrault et al. 2020
)
and summarization tasks XSUM
(
Narayan et al. 2018
)
and CNN/DM
(
See et al. 2017
;
Hermann et al. 2015
)
as our long-form generation tasks. We evaluate models’ performance on these tasks using the metrics that are usually associated with each of them. For instance, most classification and multiple-choice QA tasks are evaluated using accuracy while short-form generation tasks are evaluated using F1 scores, translation tasks are evaluated using BLEU scores
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
and summarization tasks are evaluated using ROUGE-L scores
(
Lin 2004
)

Methodology.

To understand the trade-off between the strength of the embedded watermark signal and the drop in downstream performance, we assess the performance of LLaMA 7B
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
on a variety of tasks for a range of watermark settings
(\gamma,\delta)\in\{0.,10,0.25,0.50,0.90\}\times\{1,2,5,10\}
to simulate a broad spectrum of signal strengths.
We work under the assumption that the metrics (e.g., accuracy, BLEU, etc.) typically associated with individual tasks are reflective of human perception of quality of the model for that task. Unless otherwise stated, we report results based on average performance evaluated over 3 random choices of watermark hash keys.

Selecting representative pareto-optimal settings.

Each watermark setting
(\gamma,\delta)
represents a different trade-off between the strength of the embedded watermark signal and the quality of the resultant language model. We assess language model quality by evaluating the mean perplexity of 1000 text snippets extracted from the OpenWebText corpus. We characterize language model quality under each of these watermark settings using “relative perplexity": the ratio of the mean perplexity obtained under the watermark setting to that obtained using an unwatermarked model. Orthogonally, we assess the strength of the embedded watermark signal in each setting as the mean z-score reported by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
’s detector on 100-token long generations by the watermarked language model when prompted using prefixes of length 1000 tokens sampled from the OpenWebText corpus.

4.2
Multiple-choice Question-answering tasks

Performance on multiple-choice QA tasks is relatively unaffected even by heavy watermarks.

We observe that accuracy on datasets framed as multiple-choice question-answering tasks is almost unaffected by light and moderate watermarks and we notice only a
~\sim 5\%
drop for heavy watermarking. We see similar qualitative behavior across all
3
tasks despite salient differences among them. The objective in HellaSwag is to find the best continuation among 4 alternatives for a given passage, while RACE is a reading-comprehension task, and PIQA tests open-book common-sense reasoning. The options are also distributionally different in these datasets, with median lengths of 8, 34 & 17 tokens for RACE, HellaSwag and PIQA respectively.

The option with the maximum average-log-likelihood does not typically change due to watermarking.

We see that accuracy on these tasks remains surprisingly high and does not fall to random classifier levels under any of the
(\gamma,\delta)
settings that we evaluate (Table
2
). This is due to the length of the options in these tasks. As these options are sufficiently long, the fraction of green tokens that occur in each of them is close to
\gamma
. As a consequence, each of the alternatives see an almost uniform rise in perplexity (and hence average log-likelihood), often leaving the model’s prediction unchanged.
We notice that for a large proportion of examples, the ordering among the candidate options remains unchanged due to watermarking (Figure
4
right).
Watermarked model predictions match those of the original model in 93% of examples under moderate and 84% of examples under heavy watermarking, thus leaving accuracies largely intact.

4.3
Generation tasks

Short-form generation tasks are only mildly affected by moderate watermarks.

We find that short-form generation tasks see F1 score drops of only
0.02
-
0.04
under moderate watermarks in all tasks (Figure
6(a)
). These scores are practically unaffected by light watermarks and dramatically affected only by heavy watermarks. This is due to fact that the logits of the model’s predictions
far exceed
the logits of the vast majority of remaining tokens in magnitude (Figure
5
).
The model’s prediction only changes under watermark application when
\delta
is large enough to boost the logits of arbitrary green list tokens to values larger than those of the original model’s prediction. This appears to happen only under heavy watermarks.

Performance on long-form generation tasks is considerably affected by moderate watermarks.

We notice that BLEU and ROUGE-L scores drop by
5

6(b)
). Heavy watermarks can cause large drops in these scores, severely impacting the generation quality. We observe qualitatively that while moderate watermarks can lead to occasional factual errors in the generations, heavy watermarks can cause them to degenerate into repeats of a short sequence of tokens (examples are in Table

). This degradation is commensurate with the impact of watermarking on language-modeling performance. Figure

5
Related Work

Watermarking.

Watermarking has been widely studied for continuous-valued data but has classically been considered difficult for discrete data such as text
(
Petitcolas et al. 1999
;
Katzenbeisser and Petitcolas 1999
)
. In early 2000s, some rule-based methods were proposed to watermark textual data, involving
the use of synonyms and targeted parse-tree modifications
(
Chiang et al. 2003
;
Topkara et al. 2006
;
Venugopal et al. 2011
)
. However, implanting strong watermarks without severely degrading text quality was challenging for these approaches. Later work saw the use of LSTMs
(
Fang et al. 2017
)
and masked language-models
(
Ueoka et al. 2021
)
for generating watermarked text.

A recent work proposes a simple
method for watermarking the outputs of an arbitrary off-the-shelf autoregressive LLM by only intervening at the decoding stage
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
. Given its simplicity and ease of detection, it has received widespread
attention.
Since this approach embeds the watermark signal effectively only for high-entropy tokens, it retains the quality of generated text.
There have been follow-up analyses studying the feasibility of “scrubbing" this type of watermark from watermarked text using paraphrasing-based attacks
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023b
)
. This has inspired a range of novel watermarking strategies and extensions of the original scheme
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
, that aim to be robust to these types of attacks
(
Yoo et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Kuditipudi et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Hou et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Takezawa et al. 2023
;
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023b
)
.

There have been some attempts to use deep-learning based approaches to implant and detect watermarks in LLM-generated text
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023a
)
. A recent proposal extends the original scheme
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
)
to recover the performance drop on conditional generation tasks due to watermarking, by forcibly inserting semantically related words into the green list at every generation step
(
Fu et al. 2023
)
.
Another line of work
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
proposes a method for injecting watermarks into text generated from black box LLMs (without access to their logits) drawing on ideas of both rule-based and LM-based watermarking in prior work.
Recently, some cryptographically-inspired methods for watermarking language model outputs were proposed that render the resulting text distribution computationally indistinguishable from that of the original model by any polynomial-time adversary
(
Christ et al. 2023
;
Aaronson and Kirchner 2023
)
.

Downstream effects of watermarking.

Most prior work on watermarking has evaluated their resulting models using perplexity measurements for text generated by watermarked models.
There has been little work assessing the effect of watermarking on downstream tasks. The original paper evaluates the performance of watermarked models on TriviaQA, a closed-book question answering task
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
, whereas a follow-up study evaluates two different watermarking schemes
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
;
Aaronson and Kirchner 2023
)
on
6
generation tasks
(
Fernandez et al. 2023
)
, but it does not attempt to uncover the causes for the observed performance drops.
On the other hand, our study
covers a broader spectrum of tasks and also sheds light on the underlying reasons that explain the observed trade-offs.

6
Limitations

There are several important limitations of our study.
First, we restrict
ourselves to studying the downstream effects of the watermarking scheme proposed by
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023a
. While this scheme is slowly becoming the go-to option for most watermarking purposes, it is plausible that model developers may use different watermarking schemes which may lead to
different trade-offs.

Second,
we analyze the effect of watermarking only on LLaMA 7B, and evaluate performance under the typical decoding strategies used for each of the tasks we study.
While we expect results to be qualitatively similar under different choices of models, it is plausible that not all decoding strategies would be similarly affected by watermarks.
There may also exist watermark-aware decoding strategies designed to mitigate performance drops. For example, it may be possible to activate the watermark only while generating function words and not content words in a manner that restores performance on long-form generation tasks, without sacrificing much of the watermark strength. This is an exciting avenue for future work.

7
Conclusion

As an increasing number of future language models
would be watermarked,
we analyzed
if and to what extent
does watermarking hurt.
We evaluated
the downstream performance
of watermarked language models for a
diverse suite of NLP tasks, including
sentiment analysis, textual entailment,
question answering,
common-sense reasoning,
machine translation and summarization.
Through our evaluation,
we found that
for tasks that can be posed as classification tasks
with fixed labels, there are negligible
drops in the performance in the average case, but the fall could
be drastic for non-trivial number of cases.
For tasks that are evaluated as multiple choice questions
(with different options for each question), we notice almost
no performance degradation.
Same for short-form conditional generation. We do
notice quality drops for long-form conditional generation tasks (e.g., machine translation and summarization), comparable
to drops in the language modeling quality of watermarked
language models.
We believe that our characterization of the performance trade-offs—and identification of cases where the trade-offs are noteworthy—will allow developers and users to make an informed choice about applying and using watermarked LLMs.
