A new approach to the theory of optimal income tax
Vassili N. Kolokoltsov, Egor M. Dranov, Denis E. Piskun

Abstract
The Nobel-price winning Mirrlees’ theory of optimal taxation inspired a long sequence of research on its refinement and enhancement. However, an issue of concern has been always the fact that, as was shown in many publications, the optimal schedule in Mirrlees’ paradigm of maximising the total utility (constructed from individually optimised individual ones) usually did not lead to progressive taxation (contradicting the ethically supported practice in developed economies), and often even assigned minimal tax rates to the higher paid strata of society. The first objective of this paper is to support this conclusion by proving a theorem on optimal tax schedule in (practically most exploited) piecewise-linear environment under a simplest natural utility function. The second objective is to suggest a new paradigm for optimal taxation, where instead of just total average utility maximization one introduces a standard deviation of utility as a second parameter (in some analogy with Marcowitz portfolio optimization). We show that this approach leads to transparent and easy interpreted optimality criteria for income tax.



Key words:
optimal income tax schedule, piecewise linear taxation, two-parameter optimization.

Piecewise-linear tax in the standard setting

Recall that in the standard approach to optimal income tax (see
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
),
it is assumed that individuals are characterised by a parameter
n\geq 0
, yielding their production
per unit of efforts (e.g. time), with a (exogenously given) distribution
f(n)
. Therefore, if an
individual chooses a level of efforts
l
, then his income before tax is
y=nl
. Mechanism design
of a government is specified by a tax function
t(y)
, which is assumed to be continuous and nondecreasing,
so that the income of an individual after tax is
c(nl)=y-t(y)=nl-t(nl)
. Individuals are supposed to choose
l

that maximises certain (exogenously given) utility function
u(c,l)
that is assumed to be increasing in
c
and
decreasing in
l
. We denote by
l_{max}(n)
a point of maximum of
u(c(nl),l)
for an individual parametrized
by
n
. For a utility discussed below, a finite point
l_{max}(n)
always exists, though may be not unique for
some discrete set of
n
.

Of course, the final quantitative result depends essentially on
u
, even if one specifies its additional
natural properties, as proposed e.g. in
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
. The simplest reasonable
u
can be
chosen as
u(c,l)=c-D(l)
with an increasing convex function
D(l)
, which we adopt here. Moreover, as can be seen
from our exposition below, the choice of the function
D
is not very essential, and thus we shall use the utility function

u(c,l)=c-l^{2}/2.

When
l_{\max}(n)
are chosen the total expected utility becomes

U=\int_{0}^{\infty}u(c(nl_{\max}(n)),l_{\max}(n))f(n)dn=\int_{0}^{\infty}[nl_{\max}(n)-t(nl_{\max}(n))-\frac{1}{2}l_{\max}^{2}(n)]f(n)dn.

(1)

Since
l_{\max}
are not unique only on a discrete set of
n
,
this non-uniqueness does not affect the value of
U
.

We also assume, as in
[
9
]
, that the tax is fully distributed, so that

\int_{0}^{\infty}t(y)f(n)dn=\int_{0}^{\infty}t(nl_{\max}(n))f(n)dn=0.

(2)

In the standard approach, the objective of the government is to find a function
t(y)
that maximises (
1
)
under constraint (
2
).

We shall work with the piecewise-linear tax environment suggested in
[

]

[

[

In this paper we shall reduce our attention to the case of one bracket (linear case)
and two brackets (one kink). An extension to arbitrary number of brackets will be considered elsewhere.

In case of two brackets the tax on income

t(y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\alpha+(1-\beta_{1})y,\quad y<y_{1}\\

\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in[0,1]

\beta_{1},\beta_{2},y_{1},\alpha
.

When
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta

3

t(y)=-\alpha+(1-\beta)y

(4)

Theorem 2.1
.

The maximum of (
1

\alpha\geq 0,\beta_{1}\in[0,1],\beta_{2}\in[0,1],y_{1}\geq 0

of under constraint (

\alpha=0,\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=1

Proof.

For
t(y)
of form (

u(c(ln),l)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{1}(l,n)=\alpha+\beta_{1}nl-\frac{1}{2}l^{2},\quad l<y_{1}/n\\
&u_{2}(l,n))=\alpha+(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})y_{1}+\beta_{2}nl-\frac{1}{2}l^{2},\quad l>y_{1}/n\end{aligned}\right.

Hence the points of maximum for
u_{1}(l,n)
and
u_{2}(l,n)
are

l_{1m}=\min(\beta_{1}n,y_{1}/n),\quad l_{2m}=\max(\beta_{2}n,y_{1}/n).

The key points that distinguish different maxima are

n_{1}=\sqrt{y_{1}/\beta_{1}},\quad n_{2}=\sqrt{y_{1}/\beta_{2}},\quad n_{3}=\sqrt{\frac{2y_{1}}{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}}.

The following result specifies the optimal choice of individuals
under
t(y)
of type (
3
). We omit a proof,
as it is obtained by a straightforward analysis
of two quadratic function. It can be seen also as a
particular performance of a more general result from
[

]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(i) Let
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
(or

\left\{\begin{aligned} &l_{\max}(n)=\beta_{1}n\\

when

(ii) Let
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}
(or
n_{1}\leq n_{2}

l_{\max}(n)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &l_{\max}(n)=\beta_{1}n\\

when
n<n_{1}
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &l_{\max}(n)=\beta_{2}n\\
&u_{\max}(n)=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}^{2}n^{2}-y_{1}(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})\\
&t_{\max}(n)=-\alpha+(1-\beta_{2})\beta_{2}n^{2}+(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})y_{1}\end{aligned}\right.

when
n\geq n_{2}
, and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &l_{\max}(n)=y_{1}/n\\
&u_{\max}(n)=\alpha+\beta_{1}y_{1}-\frac{y_{1}^{2}}{2n^{2}}\\
&t_{\max}(n)=-\alpha+(1-\beta_{1})y_{1}\end{aligned}\right.

when
n\in[n_{1},n_{2}]
.

Recall that we are analysing the optimization problem of finding
\alpha\geq 0,\beta_{1}\in[0,1]
,

\beta_{2}\in[0,2],y_{1}\geq 0
that maximise the total expected utility

U=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{\max}(n)f(n)dn

under the constraint

\int_{0}^{\infty}t(nl_{\max}(n))f(n)dn=0.

If
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
, the constraint takes the form

\alpha=\int_{0}^{n_{3}}(1-\beta_{1})\beta_{1}n^{2}f(n)dn

+\int_{n_{3}}^{\infty}[(1-\beta_{2})\beta_{2}n^{2}+(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})y_{1}]f(n)dn

(5)

If

\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}
, the constraint takes the form

\alpha=\int_{0}^{n_{1}}(1-\beta_{1})\beta_{1}n^{2}f(n)dn+(1-\beta_{1})y_{1}\int_{\beta_{1}}^{\beta_{2}}f(n)dn

+\int_{n_{2}}^{\infty}[(1-\beta_{2})\beta_{2}n^{2}+(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})y_{1}]f(n)dn.

(6)

Substituting we get, in the first case
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
, that

U=\int u_{\max}(n)f(n)dn=\int_{0}^{n_{3}}(\beta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2})n^{2}f(n)dn+\int_{n_{3}}^{\infty}(\beta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}^{2})n^{2}f(n)dn.

And then

\frac{\partial U}{\partial\beta_{2}}=\frac{\partial n_{3}}{\partial\beta_{2}}(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})(1-\frac{1}{2}(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}))n_{3}^{2}f(n_{3})

+\int_{n_{3}}^{\infty}(1-\beta_{2})n^{2}f(n)dn,

\frac{\partial U}{\partial\beta_{1}}=\frac{\partial n_{3}}{\partial\beta_{1}}(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})(1-\frac{1}{2}(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}))n_{3}^{2}f(n_{3})

+\int_{0}^{n_{3}}(1-\beta_{1})n^{2}f(n)dn,

which both are positive.

In case
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}
,

U=\int_{0}^{n_{1}}(\beta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2})n^{2}f(n)dn

+\int_{n_{2}}^{\infty}(\beta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}^{2})n^{2}f(n)dn+\int_{n_{1}}^{n_{2}}(y_{1}-\frac{y_{1}^{2}}{2n^{2}})f(n)dn.

And then

\frac{\partial U}{\partial\beta_{2}}=\frac{\partial n_{2}}{\partial\beta_{2}}[(y_{1}-\frac{y_{1}^{2}}{2n_{2}^{2}})-(\beta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{2}^{2})n_{2}^{2}]f(n_{2})

+\int_{n_{2}}^{\infty}(1-\beta_{2})n^{2}f(n)dn,

\frac{\partial U}{\partial\beta_{1}}=-\frac{\partial n_{1}}{\partial\beta_{1}}[(y_{1}-\frac{y_{1}^{2}}{2n_{1}^{2}})-(\beta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2})n_{1}^{2}]f(n_{1})

+\int_{0}^{n_{1}}(1-\beta_{1})n^{2}f(n)dn,

which again both are positive, because the first terms vanish.
Therefore, the maximum is realised on
\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}=1
.

3
New paradigm: linear tax

Taking partial inspiration from the Marcowitz portfolio theory, we suggest here a new paradigm
for the optimal taxation, where instead of the usual total utility optimization, we suggest a
two-criteria optimization problem, choosing as a second criteria the variance or the standard deviation
of the utility function. While in Marcowitz theory the variance is taken as a measure of risk
(with the usual criticism that only deviations in the undesired direction are related to risk),
in our setting the variance expresses the level of inequality in a society that can be also interpreted as
the level of social tension. In the same way as the optimal portfolio theory can be (and was) recast
alternatively using different measures of risk, our theory can be also modified by using other measures
of social inequality, for a modern review of such measures we refer to
[
3
]
.

In order to see how it works, let us start with the simple case of linear tax (
4
)
and the simplest utility function
u=c-l^{2}/2
, where all calculations can be performed explicitly.

Then
c=\alpha+\beta nl
and the point, where
u
achieves maximum, is
l_{\max}(n)=\beta n
.
The corresponding values of utility and tax are

u_{\max}(n)=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(\beta n)^{2},\quad t_{\max}(n)=-\alpha+(1-\beta)\beta n^{2}.

Equalising total tax to zero yields

\alpha=(1-\beta)\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}n^{2}f(n)dn=(1-\beta)\beta\mathbf{E}N^{2},

where we denoted by
N
the random variable of skills with the density
f(n)

and by
\mathbf{E}
the corresponding expectation. Thus

U=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{\max}(n)f(n)\,dn=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\mathbf{E}N^{2}=\beta(1-\frac{1}{2}\beta)\mathbf{E}N^{2}.

(7)

Confirming again the result of the previous section we see that maximum of
U

equals
\mathbf{E}N^{2}/2
and occurs at
\beta=1
.

Next we calculate the variance. Since the variance
\sigma^{2}_{u}
of the utility
is not changed by an additive constant, we can calculate it with

\alpha=0
yielding directly

\sigma_{u}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\beta^{4}[\mathbf{E}N^{4}-(\mathbf{E}N^{2})^{2}]=\frac{1}{4}\beta^{4}\sigma^{2}(N^{2}),

(8)

where
\sigma^{2}(N^{2})
is the variance of the random variable
N^{2}
. Thus
\sigma_{u}
is also increasing with
\beta

and takes its maximum value
\sigma(N^{2})/2
at
\beta=1
.

As is the standard approach in a two-criteria optimisation, we can state the two dual problems,
one of minimising
\sigma_{u}
under a given level of
U
, and another of maximising
U

under a given level of
\sigma_{u}
. These two problems usually lead to one and the same
equation linking optimal values of criteria, the corresponding set of solutions being
referred to as the efficient frontier.

In the present simple case with only one parameter
\beta
, the total utility and its variance
are seen to lie on the curve given in parametric form by
(
8
) and (
7
). Excluding
\beta
yields the equation connecting expected utility

U
and its standard deviation
\sigma_{u}
:

\left(\frac{2\sigma_{u}}{\sigma(N^{2})}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\sigma_{u}}{\sigma(N^{2})}-\frac{U}{\mathbf{E}N^{2}}=0.

(9)

In terms of normalised variables

\tilde{\sigma}_{u}=\sigma_{u}/\sigma(N^{2}),\quad\tilde{U}=U/\mathbf{E}N^{2}

it rewrites as

2\tilde{\sigma}_{u}=(\tilde{\sigma}_{u}+\tilde{U})^{2},

(10)

which is seen to be a parabola. We are interested in its part with
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}\geq 0
,
which is a concave curve lying in the first quadrant of the
(\tilde{U},\tilde{\sigma}_{u})
-plane and
crossing
\{\tilde{U}=0\}
-axis at the points
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}=0
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}=2
.
The maximum of
\tilde{U}
is
1/2

that occurs at
\tilde{\sigma}=1/2
. Since we are interested in minimal
\sigma
,
the part of the parabola with
\tilde{\sigma}>1/2

is of no interest. Moreover, this part corresponds to
\beta>1
, which are not allowed.
So only the part with
\tilde{\sigma}\in[0,1/2]
represents the real
efficient frontier
,
where optimal solutions lie.

In order to choose a point on the efficient frontier of a two-criteria optimization, the usual approach
is to specify a new utility function of the form
V=U-c\sigma_{u}
, where
c
is the key parameter for the decision making.
In the financial context this parameter measures the risk tolerance (or risk aversion). In the present social choice setting,

1/c
designates the growth of social inequality, which is acceptable for a unit growth of the total welfare.
This is of course an exogenously given parameter that must be fed to the mathematical model by public authorities.

For an example, assume that our distributions are normalised so that
\tilde{U}=U
,
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}=\sigma_{u}=\sigma
.
Thus, given
c
, we have to maximise
V=U-c\sigma
on the efficient frontier given by (
10
).
Geometrically, this means to find the point on this frontier, which is tangent to a line parallel to
\{U=c\sigma\}
.
Analytically, this means to find
\beta\in[0,1]
yielding

\max\{U-c\sigma:2\sigma=(\sigma+U)^{2},\,\,\sigma\in[0,1/2]\}.

It is straightforward to see that maximum in this problem is achieved on

\sigma=\frac{1}{2(c+1)^{2}},\quad U=\frac{2c+1}{2(c+1)^{2}},

which, by (
8
) (recall that we assumed
\sigma(N^{2})=1
), corresponds to

\beta=\beta(c)=(1+c)^{-1}
. Thus we see that the optimal
\beta(c)
can take any value
from
[0,1]
depending on the choice of
c
.
For
c=0
we return to the maximization of
U
yielding
\beta(c)=1
;
and while
c
increases to infinity,
\beta(c)
tends to zero.

Remark 1
.

Of course the same result can be obtained by searching
\max\{U-c\sigma\}
directly over all
\beta\in[0,1]
.

Logarithmic utility function

In this section we will consider the model with the linear taxation (
4
) and the following utility function:

u(c,l)=\ln(c)+A\,\ln(1-l),

(11)

where
c=c(y)=y-t(y),\,y=nl

By differentiating, we get:

l_{\max}(n)=\frac{A\beta n-\alpha}{(A+1)\beta n}\mathds{1}_{[\frac{\alpha}{A\beta},+\infty)}(n),

where
\mathds{1}_{X}
