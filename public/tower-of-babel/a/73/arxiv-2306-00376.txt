Abstract
. We extend the detailed study of the linearized dynamics obtained for cnoidal waves of the Korteweg–de Vries equation in [ Rod18 ] to small-amplitude periodic traveling waves of the generalized Korteweg–de Vries equations that are not subject to Benjamin–Feir instability. With the adapted notion of stability, this provides for such waves, global-in-time bounded stability in any Sobolev space, and asymptotic stability of dispersive type. When doing so, we actually prove that such results also hold for waves of arbitrary amplitude satisfying a form of spectral stability designated here as dispersive spectral stability. Keywords : periodic traveling waves; asymptotic stability; generalized Korteweg–de Vries equations; dispersive estimates. 2010 MSC : 35B10, 35B35, 35Q53, 35P05, 37K45.



1.
Introduction

Outline

In this note, we are concerned with the linear stability of periodic traveling waves of the generalized Korteweg-de Vries (gKdV) equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u))=0\,.

(1.1)

where
f
is a smooth function. This encompasses the power-type case when
f
is a nonlinear monomial, that is,
f(u)=a\,u^{p}
, for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\geq 2
, and some
a\neq 0
. The latter class includes the classical Korteweg–de Vries equation, obtained with
p=2
, and the modified Korteweg–de Vries equations, obtained with
p=3
, which are known to be integrable.

A traveling wave of (
1.1
), of speed
c
, is a solution taking the form
(t,x)\mapsto\underline{u}(x-ct)
for some profile
\underline{u}
. Such a wave is called periodic if its profile is a periodic function. A key point is that even if our background solutions are spatially periodic the dynamics we are interested in is set on the real line, that is,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, in accordance with most of related concrete applications, we aim at understanding how a periodic traveling wave behaves under smooth localized perturbations.

The study of the generalized Korteweg–de Vries equations and, even of the more specialized question of the stability of their traveling waves, have grown too considerably to be properly reported in the present note. We simply refer the reader to
[
Zhi01
,
Tao06
,
LP15
,
AP09
,
KP13
]
as possible entering gates to various aspects of the field. On the more specific question of periodic waves of such equations we refer to
[
Rod13
,
Rod15
]
and the introductions of
[
BGMR21
,
NRS22
]
for a general picture and a comprehensive list of references.

We only point out that, with the exception of the linear analysis in
[
Rod18
]
and some case-studies of integrable equations
[
ML09
,
MLT12
]
, the stability analysis of periodic waves of dispersive equations is for the moment essentially restricted to spectral studies. Note moreover that though most of the analysis of
[
Rod18
]
relies on robust arguments, it does use at some key steps detailed spectral information obtained in
[
BD09
]
from the integrability through a Lax pair representation of the classical Korteweg–de Vries equation.

An inspection of
[
Rod18
]
shows that integrability is used there in two fundamentally different ways: on one hand to check a strong form of spectral stability that we designate as dispersive spectral stability, on the other hand to obtain a suitable form of high-frequency expansions of Bloch eigenfunctions.

In the present note, motivated by this observation, we achieve two tasks. On one hand we replace the high-frequency analysis of
[
Rod18
]
with a robust one, hence proving that for any periodic wave of (gKdV) dispersive spectral stability implies linear asymptotic stability and comes with the detailed asymptotic behavior provided in
[
Rod18
]
. On the other hand we prove dispersive spectral stability for small-amplitude periodic waves that are not subject to Benjamin–Feir instability.

We regard the precise identification of the right notion of dispersive spectral stability and the high-frequency analysis as the main contributions of the present note. Indeed, though not written down elsewhere in the literature, a large part of the spectral stability analysis for small-amplitude waves could be considered, to some extent, as common knowledge of the community, or, in other words, as part of the folklore. In particular, the Benjamin–Feir instability criterion has been derived in a considerably more general framework in
[
BGMR21
,
AR22
]
(by combining the small-amplitude expansions of
[
BGMR20
]
with the general spectral criterion of
[
BGNR14
,
AR22
]
). Likewise, the fact that when the Benjamin-Feir instability is precluded spectral stability — in the sense that the spectrum lies on the imaginary axis — holds for small-amplitude waves has been proved for power-type nonlinearities in
[
HK08
]
(with arguments close to those used in the related
[
GH07
]
). In the stability direction our spectral analysis merely extends the standard argument to more general nonlinearities and, more importantly, strengthens the spectral conclusions so as to reach a stage when the linear analysis of
[
Rod18
]
completed with the present high-frequency analysis could be applied.

Main results

To provide precise statements, let us fix a periodic wave of (gKdV) of profile
\underline{u}
and speed
c
. In a frame moving at speed
c
this corresponds to a stationary solution and the dynamics linearized about it obeys
\partial_{t}u={\mathcal{L}}u
where
{\mathcal{L}}:=-\partial_{x}((\partial_{x}^{2}+f^{\prime}(\underline{u})-c)\,\cdot\,)
. When discussing spectral properties we regard
{\mathcal{L}}
as acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{3}(\mathbb{R})
but we shall consider various extensions when turning to dynamical considerations.

It is convenient to use Floquet-Bloch theory to describe the spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}
. We recall some rudiments of it in the next section but for the moment it is sufficient to know that the spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}
is given by a discrete set of curves locally parameterized by a real number called Floquet exponent
\xi
and that multiplicity refers to multiplicity at a fixed Floquet exponent.

We say that the wave associated with
(\underline{u},c)
is dispersively spectrally stable if the following list of properties holds.

(1)

The spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}
lies on the imaginary axis.

(2)

Each nonzero element of the spectrum is simple and the second-order derivative with respect to the Floquet exponent of the spectral curve passing through it is nonzero.

(3)

The spectral element
0
corresponds to the Floquet exponent
0
and has multiplicity three. Moreover the derivatives of the three curves passing through it are distinct and their third-order derivatives are nonzero.

The first property alone is referred to as spectral stability and, correspondingly, failure of it is known as spectral instability. Concerning the third one, we point out that it is a non trivial but classical fact of the periodic wave stability theory that the corresponding curves are always at least
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
and that they are analytic when their first-order derivatives are distinct.

It follows from elementary considerations that
f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c_{0}\geq 0
is necessary for the existence of a family of periodic waves with profiles
\underline{u}
converging to a constant value
\underline{u}_{0}
and speed
c
converging to
c_{0}
, and that
f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c_{0}>0
is sufficient to guarantee such an existence. With this in mind we may state our first main result.

Theorem 1.1

(Spectral stability)
.

Let
(\underline{u}_{0},c_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfy
f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c_{0}>0
and set

\Delta_{BF}:=(f^{\prime\prime}(\underline{u}_{0}))^{2}-3\,f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})(f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c_{0})\,.

(1.2)

(1)

If
\Delta_{BF}<0
, periodic waves associated with
(\underline{u},c)
sufficiently close to
(\underline{u}_{0},c_{0})
are spectrally unstable.

(2)

If
\Delta_{BF}>0
, periodic waves associated with
(\underline{u},c)
sufficiently close to
(\underline{u}_{0},c_{0})
are dispersively spectrally stable.

In order to analyze linear stability, we need to use the right way to think about proximity. With this goal in mind, a linear analogous to the notion of space-modulated stability introduced in
[
JNRZ14
]
was designed in
[
Rod18
]
. It consists in measuring the size of a function
u
with

N_{X}(u):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(w,\psi)\\
(u,\partial_{x}\psi)\in X^{2}\\
u=w+\psi\underline{u}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(\|w\|_{X}+\|\partial_{x}\psi\|_{X}),

(1.3)

for some choice of a functional space
X
endowed with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. We refer to
[
Rod15
,
Rod18
]
for a detailed discussion of this choice. We only mention that if we were, instead, studying solitary waves or kinks, we would measure the size of
u
with

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(w,\psi_{0})\in X\times\mathbb{R}\\
u=w+\psi_{0}\underline{u}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\|w\|_{X}

so as to obtain a linear analogous of the classical notion of orbital stability.

With this in hands, let us denote
(S_{{\mathcal{L}}}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
the one-parameter group generated by
{\mathcal{L}}
and state our second main result.

Theorem 1.2

(Linear stability)
.

Assume that the wave associated with
(\underline{u},c)
is dispersively spectrally stable.

(1)

For any
s\in\mathbb{R},
there exists
C_{s}>0
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}

N_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}(S_{{\mathcal{L}}}(t)(u_{0}))\leq C_{s}N_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}(u_{0}).

(2)

There exits
C>0
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}

N_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}(S_{{\mathcal{L}}}(t)(u_{0}))\leq C\,(1+|t|)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\,N_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}(u_{0})\,.

With the same assumption as in the foregoing theorem we may also provide a detailed description of the dynamics. Roughly speaking Theorem
1.2
proves that up to an error of order
|t|^{-1/3}
the linearized dynamics is described by the arising of a space-time modulation of phase shift,
\psi(t,x)\,\underline{u}^{\prime}
being thought as a linear part of
\underline{u}(x+\psi(t,x))
. By adding a space-time modulation of the other parameters describing the wave one captures the dynamics up to a
|t|^{-1/2}
-error. Moreover the dynamics of these parameters is itself well-captured by a constant-coefficient system, an averaged modulation system. To some extent, the latter system may be derived formally through an adapted form of geometrical optics. For more heuristics on the latter we refer to
[
Rod13
,
Rod15
]
whereas the corresponding detailed analysis may be found in
[
Rod18
]
.

Actually, in order to prove Theorem
1.2
a weaker form of dispersive spectral stability would be sufficient and we have chosen to stick to the stronger form only because it is useful to derive the modulation behavior discussed above and does hold in the cases that we have analyzed. We refer to
[
Rod18
]
for more optimal assumptions.

In the next section, we provide some background and elementary preliminaries to the proof of our main results. In Section
3
we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
by carrying out a small-amplitude/slow/side-band analysis. In Section
4
we complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
by providing a detailed high-frequency analysis, replacing the one of
[
Rod18
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

Wave parameterization and small-amplitude limit

Up to a phase shift, periodic wave profiles may be identified with connected components that are reduced to closed loops of level sets defined by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\underline{u})^{2}+\mathcal{W}(\underline{u};c,\lambda)=\mu

with potential

{\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u};c,\lambda)=F(\underline{u})-\frac{c}{2}\underline{u}^{2}-\lambda\underline{u},

where
F
is the anti-derivative of
f
such that
F(0)=0
and
(\lambda,\mu)
are wave parameters complementing the phase speed
c
. Throughout, without losing generality, we quotient the phase shift freedom by imposing that
\underline{u}
is even. For a full discussion of the sense in which the identification is carried out and justifications of other claims of the present subsection, we refer the reader to any of the devoted sections in
[
BGNR14
,
BGMR16
,
BGMR20
,
BGMR21
,
AR22
,
NRS22
]
.

Constant states are then characterized as critical points of
{\mathcal{W}}(\cdot;c_{0},\lambda_{0})
for some
(c_{0},\lambda_{0})
. Those generating small-amplitude periodic waves are necessarily local minimizers of
{\mathcal{W}}(\cdot;c_{0},\lambda_{0})
and we are only interested in non degenerate ones, that is on
\underline{u}_{0}
such that
\partial_{u}{\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u}_{0};c_{0},\lambda_{0})=0
and
\partial_{u}^{2}{\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u}_{0};c_{0},\lambda_{0})>0
.

When considering small-amplitude waves, it is sufficient to hold
(c,\lambda)
fix to the value
(c_{0},\lambda_{0})
of the limiting endstate
\underline{u}_{0}
and send
\mu
to
{\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u}_{0};c_{0},\lambda_{0})
from above. We shall do so and consistently we drop the index
0
on
(c,\lambda)
and introduce
\delta
through

\mu^{\delta}:={\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u}_{0};c,\lambda)+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\partial_{u}^{2}{\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u}_{0};c,\lambda)

so as to quantify the limit as
\delta\to 0
. Near
\underline{u}_{0}
the wave profiles
\underline{u}_{\delta}
of parameter
(c,\lambda,\mu^{\delta})
have scaled profiles
\underline{v}_{\delta}(\cdot):=\underline{u}_{\delta}(k_{\delta}^{-1}\,\cdot)
and (fundamental) wave numbers
k_{\delta}
expands as

\displaystyle\underline{v}_{\delta}(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta\to 0^{+}}}{{=}}\underline{u}_{0}+\delta\cos(2\pi x)+\delta^{2}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})}{12(f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c)}\big(\cos(4\pi x)-3\big)+{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{3})\,,

\displaystyle k_{\delta}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta\to 0^{+}}}{{=}}\frac{\sqrt{f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c}}{2\pi}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{32\pi\sqrt{f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c}}\,\left({f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})}-\frac{5\,(f^{\prime\prime}(\underline{u}_{0}))^{2}}{3\,\left(f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c\right)}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{4})\,.

At some point, we shall need to parameterize small-amplitude waves. We shall use
(\delta,\lambda,c)
and the implicit phase shift as parameters. For later use, we already note here that

\partial_{c}k_{0}\,\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}-\partial_{\lambda}k_{0}\,\partial_{c}\underline{u}_{0}\,=\,-\frac{1}{4\pi\,(f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\neq 0\,.

Note that in the foregoing computation we have taken into account that
\underline{u}_{0}
depends on
(\lambda,c)
through
\partial_{u}{\mathcal{W}}(\underline{u}_{0};c,\lambda)=0
.

Linearization and Bloch symbols

When discussing stability it is also convenient to scale period to a fixed value. To do so, we introduce
k
the wave number of the background wave and
\underline{v}(\cdot):=\underline{u}(k^{-1}\,\cdot)
its scaled profile. We also replace
u
solving
\partial_{t}u={\mathcal{L}}u
where
{\mathcal{L}}=-\partial_{x}((\partial_{x}^{2}+f^{\prime}(\underline{u})-c)\,\cdot\,)
with
v
such that
v(t,\cdot)=u(t,k^{-1}\,\cdot)
. In terms of
v
, the linearized dynamics obeys

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v

\displaystyle=Lv\,,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle:=-k\,\partial_{x}((k\,^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+f^{\prime}(\underline{v})-c)\,\cdot\,)\,.

The operator
L
is a differential operator with coefficients of period
1
and we consider it as acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{3}(\mathbb{R})
.

In order to analyze the structure of the spectrum of
L
, we rely on the Bloch-wave decomposition

\displaystyle g(x)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i\xi\,x}\check{g}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(2.1)

provided by the Floquet-Bloch transform

\displaystyle\check{g}(\xi,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\,2\pi j\,x}\hat{g}(\xi+2j\pi)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\,e^{-i\xi\,(x+\ell)}g(x+\ell)\,\,,

(2.2)

which, up to a
\sqrt{2\pi}
factor, is an isometry from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to
L^{2}((-\pi,\pi);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}))
. Here and throughout we use the following convention for the Fourier transform

\hat{g}(\xi):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\xi\,x}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Note that
(e^{i\xi\,\cdot}\check{g}(\xi,\cdot))(x+1)=e^{i\xi}\,e^{i\xi\,x}\check{g}(\xi,x)
and, consistently,
\xi
is called a Floquet exponent and measure a deviation from
1
-periodicity.

The action of
L
on a function
g
defined on
\mathbb{R}
may be analyzed through

(Lg)(x)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i\xi\,x}L_{\xi}(\check{g}(\xi,\cdot))(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

where the Bloch symbols
L_{\xi}:=-k\,(\partial_{x}+i\xi)((k^{2}\,(\partial_{x}+i\xi)^{2}+f^{\prime}(\underline{v})-c)\,\cdot\,)
are operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
with domain
H^{3}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
. Each
L_{\xi}
has compact resolvent thus its spectrum is reduced to a discrete set of eigenvalues with finite multiplicity. A key related fact is the spectral decomposition

\sigma(L)=\bigcup_{\xi\in(-\pi,\pi]}\sigma_{per}(L_{\xi})\,,

where we have marked with the index
per
the fact that
L_{\xi}
acts on functions defined over
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

The Hamiltonian structure implies the following relation between
L_{\xi}^{*}
and its adjoint
L_{\xi}

(\partial_{x}+i\xi)\,(L_{\xi})^{*}\,=\,-L_{\xi}\,(\partial_{x}+i\xi)\,.

Since
(\partial_{x}+i\xi)
is boundedly invertible on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
when
\xi\neq 0
, we deduce that the spectrum is symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis, with respect to both location and multiplicity. A continuity argument extends the latter to
L_{0}
.

Elementary spectral considerations

To discuss the small-amplitude we denote as
L^{\delta}_{\xi}
the Bloch symbol corresponding to
\underline{v}_{\delta}
.

Simple Fourier series computations provide a full description of the spectrum at
\delta=0
. The eigenvalues of
L_{\xi}^{0}
are given by
\lambda_{j}(\xi)
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, where

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=\Lambda(2\pi\,j+\xi)\,,

\displaystyle\Lambda(\zeta)

\displaystyle:=-k_{0}\,i\zeta\,(k_{0}^{2}\,(i\zeta)^{2}+f^{\prime}(\underline{u}_{0})-c\,)=-i\,k_{0}^{3}\,\zeta\,(4\pi^{2}-\zeta^{2})\,.

Moreover eigenvalues of
L_{\xi}^{0}
except for the eigenvalue
0
of
L_{0}^{0}
that have multiplicity three. Furthermore, the third-order derivative of the eigenvalues of
L_{\xi}^{0}
with respect to
\xi
never vanishes and the second-order derivative vanishes only for the eigenvalue
0
of
L_{\xi}^{0}
.

Since simple eigenvalues perturb smoothly, this already provides a large part of Theorem
1.1
. There remains only to discuss three things.

(1)

What happens to the spectrum near the origin ?

(2)

Why does the spectrum far from the origin remains on the imaginary axis ?

(3)

Can the perturbation argument be made uniform with respect to the eigenvalue ?

The first point is the object of the next section.

Concerning the second point, we observe that a direct perturbative argument shows that provided one excludes a neighborhood of the origin in the spectral plane and

L_{\xi}^{\delta}

As for the third point, it is sufficient to focus on the high frequency regime and to prove that there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for any
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
and any
(j,\xi)

\frac{1}{2i\pi}\oint_{\partial(B(\lambda_{j}(\xi),r_{\delta}(j,\xi)))}(\lambda I-L_{\xi}^{\delta})^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,.

Incidentally we recall that the rank of a spectral projector is equal to the sum of the algebraic multiplicities of the associated eigenvalues and that the rank map is continuous on projectors. Since the distance between consecutive eigenvalues scales like

\displaystyle\|(\lambda I-L_{\xi}^{0})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\to H^{1}_{per}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})}

Remark 2.1
.

We point out that the arguments sketched here may also be applied to analyze the high-frequency regime of a wave of arbitrary amplitude. Indeed for a fixed wave (possibly of large amplitude), by taking

3.
Spectral stability – proof of Theorem
1.1

To conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we only need to consider the spectrum of
L_{\xi}^{\delta}
near the origin when
(\delta,\xi)
is small. Our strategy follows very closely similar computations carried out in
[
NRS22
]
.

To begin with, we introduce the spectral projector

\displaystyle\Pi_{\xi}^{\delta}:=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\oint_{S}(\lambda I-L_{\xi}^{\delta})^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda

(3.1)

where
S
is a circle centered at
0
with small radius (independent of
(\delta,\xi)
), which is well-defined when
(\delta,\xi)
is small. Once a smooth basis
(q_{1}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot),q_{2}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot),q_{3}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot))
of
\Ran(\Pi_{\xi}^{\delta})
and a smooth dual basis
(\tilde{q}_{1}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot),\tilde{q}_{2}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot),\tilde{q}_{3}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot))
of
\Ran((\Pi_{\xi}^{\delta})^{*})
have been chosen, the question is reduced to the study of the spectrum of

D_{\xi}^{\delta}:=\left(\langle\tilde{q}_{j}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot),L_{\xi}^{\delta}q_{\ell}^{\delta}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle_{L_{per}^{2}}\right)_{1\leq j,\,\ell\leq 3}

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{L_{per}^{2}}
denotes the canonical scalar product of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
skew-linear in its first argument.

Simple computations

We begin with co-periodic computations, that is, by computing with
\xi=0

\displaystyle q_{1}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{\partial_{x}\underline{v}_{\delta}}{2\pi\delta}\,,

\displaystyle q_{2}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\delta}\underline{v}_{\delta}-\partial_{\delta}k_{\delta}\frac{\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{c}\underline{v}_{\delta}-\partial_{c}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{\lambda}\underline{v}_{\delta}}{\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{c}k_{\delta}-\partial_{c}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{\lambda}k_{\delta}}\,,

(3.2)

\displaystyle q_{3}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial_{c}k_{\delta}\,\partial_{\lambda}\underline{v}_{\delta}-\partial_{\lambda}k_{\delta}\partial_{c}\underline{v}_{\delta}}{\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{c}k_{\delta}-\partial_{c}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{\lambda}k_{\delta}}\,,

which corresponds to

\displaystyle L_{0}^{\delta}q_{1}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\,,

\displaystyle L_{0}^{\delta}q_{2}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=E_{12}^{\delta}\delta^{2}\ q_{1}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)\,,

\displaystyle L_{0}^{\delta}q_{3}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=E_{13}^{\delta}\delta\ q_{1}^{\delta}(0,\cdot)\,,

where

\displaystyle E_{12}^{\delta}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{2\pi k_{\delta}\,\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}}{\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{c}k_{\delta}-\partial_{c}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{\lambda}k_{\delta}}\frac{\partial_{\delta}k_{\delta}}{\delta}\,,

\displaystyle E_{13}^{\delta}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{2\pi k_{\delta}\partial_{\lambda}k_{\delta}}{\partial_{\lambda}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{c}k_{\delta}-\partial_{c}\underline{u}_{0}\partial_{\lambda}k_{\delta}}\,.

Without even making explicit the dual basis, this choice yields

\displaystyle D_{0}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&E_{12}^{\delta}\delta^{2}&E_{13}^{\delta}\delta\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)\,.

From this choice, we build the basis of the general case through Kato’s extension process detailed in
[
Kat66
