Abstract
. This paper aims to undertake an exploration of the behavior of the moduli space of line arrangements while establishing its combinatorial interplay with the incidence structure of the arrangement. In the first part, we investigate combinatorial classes of arrangements whose moduli space is connected. We unify the classes of simple and inductively connected arrangements appearing in the literature. Then, we introduce the notion of arrangements with a rigid pencil form. It ensures the connectedness of the moduli space and is less restrictive that the class of C 3 C_{3} arrangements of simple type. In the last part, we obtain a combinatorial upper bound on the number of connected components of the moduli space. Then, we exhibit examples with an arbitrarily large number of connected components for which this upper bound is sharp.

1.
Introduction

Complex line arrangements are fundamental objects in algebraic geometry, algebraic topology, and also combinatorics. The moduli space of a line arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is a geometrical object which describes isomorphism classes, under the natural action of
\PGL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
, of arrangements that are lattice-isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
. The topological study of the moduli space is extremely important in this context, e.g. arrangements in the same connected component of the moduli space have diffeomorphic complements
[
Ran89
]
. In this paper, we focus on a combinatorial study of the moduli space of line arrangements and we explore how such a study can provide fundamental information about its geometry and topology.

As proved by Mnëv in
[
Mnë88
]
, the moduli space of a line arrangement can behave as badly as one can imagine. Vakil qualifies such behavior as
Murphy’s law

[
Vak06
]
. More precisely, Mnëv Universality Theorem states that every singularity of finite type over
\mathbb{Z}
appears in at least one moduli space. Recently, it was shown in
[
CL23
]
that the realization space of line arrangements is smooth for
|\mathcal{A}|\leq 11
and then nodal singularities appear for
|\mathcal{A}|=12
. In addition, there are several basic geometric or topological aspects of the moduli space that can not be fully predicted by a combinatorial study, as it is shown by the classical Pappus’ hexagon theorem. Thus, a natural question follows: which property of the moduli space can be combinatorially deduced?

We recall, in Section
2
, the definitions of the realization space and moduli space of a line arrangement. Also, we develop a systematic study of the inductive construction of the moduli space of ordered arrangements by introducing the notion of the
type
(Definition
2.6
). Roughly speaking, it encodes the combinatorial complexity of the line-by-line construction of an ordered arrangement by counting the multiple points contained in the new line at each step.

Section
3
is dedicated to the investigation of several known combinatorial classes of arrangements with connected moduli spaces: e.g. the class of
nice
arrangements
[
JY94
]
and their generalization to
simple
arrangements
[
WY05
]
, or the one of
inductively connected
arrangements and the
C_{3}
arrangements of simple type
both introduced in
[
NY12
]
. We prove in Theorem
3.9
that any generalized simple arrangement is inductively connected, answering a question from Nazir and Yoshinaga (see the introduction of
[
NY12
]
). In the last part of this section, we first introduce the combinatorial class of
inductively rigid
arrangements whose moduli space is a unique point. Using this as a substructure, we define the class of arrangements with a
rigid pencil form
(Definition
3.17
), which contains all the
C_{3}
arrangements of simple type. Likewise, any arrangement in this class has a connected moduli space (Theorem
3.19
).

In the latter part of this paper (Section
4
), we introduce an upper bound on the number of connected components of the moduli space of an arrangement. We first define the notion of
m
-perturbation
which transforms any combinatorics into an inductively connected one (Definition
4.4
). By the work of Nazir and Yoshinaga
[
NY12
]
, this allows to embed the moduli space of our original arrangement in an affine space of a relatively small dimension (Theorem
4.5
). From this embedding, we derive an upper bound of the number of connected components of the moduli space (Theorem
4.10
) by inductively bounding the degree of the
m
polynomial conditions arising from the
m
-perturbation. Finally, by providing explicit examples, we prove that the upper bound obtained is sharp even for non-trivial cases for which the number of connected components is arbitrarily large (Theorem
4.12
).

2.
Moduli spaces of line arrangements

Throughout all the paper, a line arrangement
\mathcal{A}
refers to a finite set of lines
\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}\}
in the complex projective plane
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
. In this section, we will recall some basic definitions about moduli space of line arrangements. We will also fix some notations and define the type of an ordered arrangement and the naive dimension of its moduli space.

2.1.
Line combinatorics and line arrangements

Definition 2.1
.

An
abstract line combinatorics
is a pair
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{P})
where:

•

\mathcal{L}
is the data of an ordered set of
n

lines
, codified by the list
\mathcal{L}=\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
,

•

\mathcal{P}
is a finite subset of the power set of
\mathcal{L}
codifying the
singular points
, satisfying:

(1)

for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, one has
|P|\geq 2
,

(2)

for all
i,j\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists a unique
P\in\mathcal{P}
such that
i,j\in P
.

Two abstract line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{P})
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
are called
equivalent
, denoted
\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, if there exists a (non-necessarily order-preserving) bijection

\Phi:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
such that
\Phi(\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Whenever there is no confusion, we will summarize an abstract line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}
by describing only the set
\mathcal{P}
.

Let
\Arr_{n}
be the set of all the line arrangements of
n
lines. Given
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
, its abstract line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
(or shortly its

) is given by
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
, with

\mathcal{P}=\left\{P\subset\mathcal{A}\,\left|\,\bigcap_{L\in P}L\neq\emptyset\;\text{ and }\;\forall L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\setminus P:\;L^{\prime}\cap\bigcap_{L\in P}L=\emptyset\right.\right\}.

A
multiple point
of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
is an element
P\in\mathcal{P}
such that
|P|\geq 3
. Singular points which are not multiple points are called
double points
. An abstract line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}
is just called
line combinatorics
if there exists an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
. By Pappus’ hexagon theorem, we know there exist abstract line combinatorics which are not line combinatorics.

2.2.
Realization and moduli spaces

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{P})
be an abstract line combinatorics, and let
n
be the cardinality of
\mathcal{L}
.
The
realization space
of
\mathcal{C}
is the set:

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{C})=\left\{\mathcal{B}\in\Arr_{n}\mid\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{B})\simeq\mathcal{C}\right\}.

The
moduli space

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})
of the abstract line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}
is the quotient of the realization space
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{C})
by the action of
\PGL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
. For brevity sake, we set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}):=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}))
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}):=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}))
for any
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
with combinatorics
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
.

A line
\ell:ax+by+cz=0
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
can be consider as a point
(a:b:c)
in the dual
\mathbb{C}{\mathbb{P}^{\vee}}^{2}
, then an element of
\Arr_{n}
is naturally identified with a point in
\big(\mathbb{C}{\mathbb{P}^{\vee}}^{2}\big)^{n}
.
Three different lines
\ell_{i}=(a_{i}:b_{i}:c_{i})
,
\ell_{j}=(a_{j}:b_{j}:c_{j})
and
\ell_{k}=(a_{k}:b_{k}:c_{k})
are
concurrent
if and only if

\Delta_{i,j,k}:=\det(\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k})=\begin{vmatrix}a_{i}&a_{j}&a_{k}\\
b_{i}&b_{j}&b_{k}\\
c_{i}&c_{j}&c_{k}\end{vmatrix}=0.

By definition, the realization space of a line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})

\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}

\big(\mathbb{C}{\mathbb{P}^{\vee}}^{2}\big)^{n}
as follows:

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c|ll}&\ell_{i}\neq\ell_{j},&\forall i\neq j\\
(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n})\in\big(\mathbb{C}{\mathbb{P}^{\vee}}^{2}\big)^{n}&\Delta_{i,j,k}=0,&\text{if $\{i,j,k\}\subset P$, for a $P\in\mathcal{P}$}\\
&\Delta_{i,j,k}\neq 0,&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right\}.

If
\mathcal{A}
is not in
\mathfrak{X}(n)
or
\overline{\mathfrak{X}}(n)
, then it contains four lines in general position. Up to a relabelling, one can assume that these lines are
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{4}
. Any isomorphism class in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
admits a unique representative

2.3.
Type of an arrangement and naive dimension of the moduli space

An
ordered arrangement
is a pair
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
, where
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
and
\omega
is a bijective map

\omega:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\},

called the
order
on
\mathcal{A}
. For the sack of brevity, we will sometime express an order arrangement as an ordered list
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\right\}
where the indices of the lines are chosen in a compatible way with the order, i.e. such that
\omega(\ell_{i})=i
, for any
\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
be an ordered arrangement and
\mathcal{B}
be a subarrangement of
\mathcal{A}
. There exists a unique order
\omega_{\mathcal{B}}
on
\mathcal{B}
such that for any
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, one has that

\omega_{\mathcal{B}}(\ell)<\omega_{\mathcal{B}}(\ell^{\prime})\iff\omega(\ell)<\omega(\ell^{\prime}).

This order will be called the
order induced
by
\omega
on
\mathcal{B}
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\omega^{-1}(\left\{1,\dots,i\right\})
, for
i\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
. When
\omega
is the order given by the indices, then
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\left\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{i}\right\}
. Consider the following ascending chain of (ordered) arrangements:

(2)

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\left\{\ell_{1}\right\}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{i}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{A},

We denote by
\tau_{i}(\mathcal{A},\omega)
(alternatively,
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega,\ell_{i})
or
\tau_{i}
depending on the context) the cardinality of the intersection
|\omega^{-1}(i)\cap\Sing(\mathcal{A}_{i-1})|
. Roughly speaking,
\tau_{i}
counts the number of multiple points of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
contained in the
i
th line. Note that we also have:

(3)

\tau_{i}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}_{i})}(|P|-2)\,-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}_{i-1})}(|Q|-2).

Definition 2.6
.

The
type
of an ordered arrangement
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
is the
n
-tuple defined as

\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega)=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

It is clear that the type depends on the choice of an order. In fact, both the ascending chain (
2
) and the type are of combinatorial nature. So they can be defined directly over an abstract line combinatorics
\mathcal{C}
. By an extension of notation, we will also denote
(\mathcal{C})_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq(\mathcal{C})_{n}=\mathcal{C}
and
\tau(\mathcal{C},\omega)
.

Remark 2.8
.

If
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
be an ordered arrangement. The following equality holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2-\tau_{i})=2|\mathcal{A}|-\sum_{P\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})}(|P|-2).

Proof.

First, if
|\mathcal{A}|=1
, then both sides of the equality are obviously
0
.

Assume that the equality holds for any ordered arrangement of
n
lines. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an ordered arrangement of
n+1
lines and take
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\ell_{n+1}\}
.
By the induction hypothesis and equation (
3
), one has:

\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(2-\tau_{i})=(2-\tau_{n+1})+2|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})}(|Q|-2)=2|\mathcal{A}|-\sum_{P\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})}(|P|-2).\qed

The right-hand side of the equality in the preceding proposition can be seen as a preliminary attempt to establish a combinatorial formula for the dimension of the realization space
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
, despite its naive nature. The part
2|\mathcal{A}|
gives the number of required variables in the construction, while the sum over
P\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
corresponds to the restriction imposed by the singular points. Since the action of
\PGL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
can fix four points in generic position, this induces a reduction of
8
to pass from the dimension of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
to the dimension of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a line arrangement. The
naive dimension
of the moduli space is:

\naive\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=2|\mathcal{A}|-8-\sum_{P\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})}(|P|-2).

Remark 2.11
.

The naive dimension is fully determined by the combinatorics of
\mathcal{A}
, so we can define it on any abstract line combinatorics. Recall that in general, the naive dimension is not equal to the dimension of the moduli space, by Pappus’ hexagon theorem.

From
[
DIM20

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is an inductively connected arrangement such that
\mathcal{A}\notin\mathfrak{X}(n)\cup\overline{\mathfrak{X}}(n)
, then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is isomorphic to a Zariski open subset of a complex space of dimension
\naive\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is connected.

Proof.

In the proof of
[
NY12

,
the authors show that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{i+1})
is isomorphic to either a
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}

-fibration over

\tau_{i}
.

When
\tau_{i}=1
, the
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
-fibration describes the choice of the new line
\ell_{i}
in a pencil, which is supported by a singular point of
\mathcal{A}_{i-1}
. Given that there is already a line in
\mathcal{A}_{i-1}
which belongs to this pencil and since
\ell_{i}
should be different from this one, we can thus describe the choice of
\ell_{i}
as a
\mathbb{C}
-fibration over
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{i-1})
.

Similarly, when
\tau_{i}=0
the
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-fibration describes the choice of a generic line
\ell_{i}
. Fix

. Since
\ell_{i}
should not pass through
P
and thus it does not belong to the pencil supported on

, one can describe the choice of
\ell_{i}
as a
\mathbb{C}^{2}
-fibration.

According to Proposition
3.3
, we can assume that
\mathcal{A}_{4}
is generic, and it can be completely fixed by the action of
\PGL_{3}
, i.e.
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{4})=\left\{\mathcal{A}_{4}\right\}
. By induction, we obtain that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is a Zariski open subset of the complex space of dimension
\sum_{i=5}^{n}(2-\tau_{i})
and by Proposition
2.9
the result holds.
∎

In fact, an explicit parametrization of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
as an open Zariski subset of an affine complex space is provided in Section
4.2
for any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

If
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
is an inductively connected arrangement such that
\mathcal{A}\notin\mathfrak{X}(n)\cup\overline{\mathfrak{X}}(n)
, then

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{i=5}^{n}(2-\tau_{i})=\naive\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}).

Remark 3.6
.

In the same spirit as

Note that the reciprocal is not true in general, as it is shown in the following example.

3.2.
Nice, simple and generalized simple arrangements

In
[
JY94
]
, Jiang and Yau defined the combinatorial class of
nice
arrangements, which was generalized by Wang and Yau in
[
WY05
]
as the classes of
simple
and
generalized simple
arrangements. In the introduction of
[
NY12
]
, Nazir and Yoshinaga ask about the relation between these combinatorial classes and the class of inductively connected arrangements. The purpose of this section is to prove that any generalized simple arrangement is actually inductively connected. First, let us recall the definition of generalized simple arrangements.

For any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we define
G_{\mathcal{A}}
as the graph whose vertices
v_{P}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the multiple points
P
of
\mathcal{A}
(i.e. the singular points whose multiplicity is at least
3
), and two vertices
v_{P}
and
v_{Q}
are joined by an edge if and only if the two corresponding points
P
and
Q
lie on the same line of
\mathcal{A}
. Associated with this graph, one define the following notions:

•

A
star
centered in
v_{P}
, and denoted
\St(v_{P})
, is the subgraph of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
generated by
v_{P}
and any neighbor vertex of
v_{P}
. The open star
\overset{\circ}{\St}(v_{P})
is the complex composed of
v_{P}
and any adjacent edge (without the neighbor vertices of
v_{P}
).

•

A
reduced circle
of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
is a tuple of
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
of vertices of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
, such that for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, one has that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})
is an edge of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
and
v_{i-1}\neq v_{i+1}
(the indices are considered modulo
k
).

•

A
generalized free net
based on a tuple
(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
of reduced circles of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
(where
B_{0}
can also be a single vertex), is the maximal subgraph
\Net(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
whose vertices are at a distance of at most
1
from at least one of the circles
B_{i}
, and such that

(N1)

for all
i\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, two vertices
v
and
w
of
B_{i}
are connected by an edge if and only if they are adjacent in
B_{i}
,

(N2)

for all
i,j\in\{0,\dots,m-2\}
and two vertices
v\in B_{i}
and
w\in B_{j}
, it does not exist
z\in B_{k}
with
k>\max(i,j)
which verifies that
(v,z)
and
(w,z)
are both edges in
\Net(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
,

(N3)

for all pair of vertices
v,w\in\Net(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
, it does not exist
z\in G_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\Net(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
which verifies that
(v,z)
and
(w,z)
are both edges in
G_{\mathcal{A}}
,

(N4)

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there exists a vertex
v\in B_{i}
which is not connected by an edge to any reduced circle
B_{j}
with
j<i
. The vertex
v
is named the
free vertex of the circle
B_{i}
.

The open net
\overset{\circ}{\Net}(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
is the complex formed by the vertices of the
B_{i}
and all the edges of
\Net(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
(i.e. all the end vertices of the net are removed).

Definition 3.8
.

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
generalized simple
if there are stars
\St(v_{1}),\dots,\St(v_{k})
and generalized free nets
\Net_{1},\dots,\Net_{l}
which are pairwise disjoint in
G_{\mathcal{A}}
and such that the graph

G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}=G_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\left\{\overset{\circ}{\St}(v_{1}),\dots,\overset{\circ}{\St}(v_{k}),\overset{\circ}{\Net}_{1},\dots,\overset{\circ}{\Net}_{l}\right\}

is a forest.
When
l=0
, the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
nice
; and it is
simple
when for all nets have
m=2
.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a generalized simple arrangement, then
\mathcal{A}
is inductively connected.

In order to prove our result, the two following technical lemmas are required. They allow to remove all the lines which pass through a multiple point with a corresponding vertex of valence at most
2
in the graph
G_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
be an arrangement and
v_{P}
a vertex of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
of valence
m\leq 2
, and let
\mathcal{A}_{P}=\{L\in\mathcal{A}\mid P\in L\}
. There exists an order
\omega_{P}
on
\mathcal{A}_{P}
which verifies that for any order
\omega
on
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
, and for all
\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}
, we have that
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega\oplus\omega_{P},\ell)\leq 2
.

Proof.

Let
\omega
be a fixed order on
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
. Note that if
\omega_{P}
is an order of
\mathcal{A}_{P}
then
\omega\oplus\omega_{P}
is an order on
\mathcal{A}
. We study each case by the possible values of the valence:

If
m=0
, then
v_{P}
is an isolated vertex of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
. That is equivalent to say that any line of
\mathcal{A}_{P}
has only
P
as multiple point. So for any order
\omega_{P}
on
\mathcal{A}_{P}
, one has
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega\oplus\omega_{P},\ell)\leq 1
, for all
\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}
.

If
m=1
, we denote by
\ell_{P}
the line corresponding to the unique edge of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
which ends at
v_{P}
. Remark that
\ell_{P}
contains exactly two multiple points, since otherwise
m
should be at least
2
. As in the previous case, any line of
\mathcal{A}_{P}\setminus\{\ell_{P}\}
contains only
P
as multiple point. Consider any order
\omega_{P}
on
\mathcal{A}_{P}
such that
\omega_{P}(\ell_{P})=1
. For any line
\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}\setminus\{\ell_{P}\}
, we have that
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega\oplus\omega_{P},\ell)\leq 1
. In
\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}\mid\ell\neq\ell_{P}\}
, the point
P
is no longer a multiple point (neither a singular point). So, in the previous arrangement,
\ell_{P}
contains a unique multiple point. Then we have
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega\oplus\omega_{P},\ell_{P})=1
.

If
m=2
and the two edges which join
v_{P}
correspond to the same line
\ell_{P}
, then
\ell_{P}
contains at most
3
multiple points. We consider any order
\omega_{P}
such that
\omega_{P}(\ell_{P})=1
, then end of the proof is similar to the case
m=1
. The only difference is that in
\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}\mid\ell\neq\ell_{P}\}
, the line
\ell_{P}
contains at most
2
multiple points and thus
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega\oplus\omega_{P},\ell_{P})\leq 2
.

If
m=2
and the two edges which join
v_{P}
correspond to two different lines
\ell_{P}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{P}
, then each line contains exactly two multiple points. So any line in
\mathcal{A}_{P}
contains at most
2
multiple points. This implies that for any order
\omega_{P}
on
\mathcal{A}_{P}
, one has
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega\oplus\omega_{P},\ell)\leq 2
, for all
\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a line arrangement, let
v_{P}
be a vertex of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
with valence at most
2
and let
\mathcal{A}_{P}=\{\ell\in\mathcal{A}\mid P\in\ell\}
. If
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected then so is
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
inductively connected, then there exists an order
\omega_{0}
on
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
such that
\tau(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P},\omega_{0},\ell)\leq 2
, for all
\ell\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
. By Lemma
3.10
, there exists an order
\omega_{P}
on
\mathcal{A}_{P}
such that
\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega_{0}\oplus\omega_{P},\ell)\leq 2
, for all
\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{P}
. By Remark
2.8
, we deduce that
\max(\tau(\mathcal{A},\omega_{0}\oplus\omega_{P}))\leq 2
, and so that
\mathcal{A}
is inductively connected.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.9
.

For any multiple point
P
of
\mathcal{A}
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{P}
the arrangement
\{\ell\in\mathcal{A}\mid P\in\mathcal{A}\}
. Note that
G_{\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}}
is a (non necessarily strict) subgraph of
G_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\overset{\circ}{\St}(v_{P})
. The strategy of the proof is to remove one-by-one all the vertices of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
in such a way that, at each step, the removed vertex has valence at most
2
.

Step 1
. The vertices of
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

By hypothesis,
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
is a forest. Let
v_{P}
be an end-point of
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
, that is there is at most one edge in
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
which meets
v_{P}
. Then, we know that the stars and the generalized net are disjoint. Furthermore, condition (N3) implies that there are no two edges in the same generalized net that meet
v_{P}
. This implies that there is at most
1
edge in the union of the stars and of the generalized nets which meet
v_{P}
. So the valence of
v_{P}
is at most
2
. So by Lemma
3.11
, if
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected then so is
\mathcal{A}
.

As noted at the beginning of the proof,
G_{\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}}
is a subgraph of
G_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\overset{\circ}{\St}(v_{P})
. The remaining part of
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
in
G_{\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}}
is also a forest (as a subgraph of a forest) and then we can apply the same argument on a new end-point. By successive applications of this argument, we obtain that if
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\bigcup_{v_{P}\in G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}}\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected then so is
\mathcal{A}
.

Step 2
. The vertices of
\St(v_{P})
.

The graph
G_{\mathcal{B}}
is contained in the disjoint union of the stars and of the generalized nets. Remark that the union of the stars is also a forest. So we can apply exactly the same argument as in
step 1
to remove all the vertices included in any star. Thus, we obtain that if
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\bigcup_{v_{P}\in\cup\St(v_{i})}\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected then so is
\mathcal{B}
.

Step 3
. The vertices of
\Net_{i}
.

The graph
G_{\mathcal{D}}
is contained in the disjoint union of the generalized nets. By definition, we have that
\Net_{l}=\Net(B_{0},\dots,B_{m})
for a fixed
m
. Remark that all the vertices outside the reduced circles
B_{i}
are not in
G_{\mathcal{D}}
since they have been removed at Step 1. Let
v_{P}
be the free vertex of
B_{m}
, and let
v_{Q}
be one of its two neighbors in
B_{m}
. According to conditions (N1) and (N4), the valence of
v_{f}
is exactly
2
. By Lemma
3.11
, if
\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected then so is
\mathcal{D}
. It follows from conditions (N1) and (N2), that in
G_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{P}}
the vertex
v_{Q}
as valence at most
2
. So we can inductively apply this argument on all the vertices of
B_{m}
, and then on each reduced circle
B_{i}
of
\Net_{l}
. At the end, we obtain that if
\mathcal{D}\setminus\bigcup_{i=0}^{m}\bigcup_{v_{P}\in B_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected then so is
\mathcal{D}
.

Applying the latter on each generalized net
\Net(B^{j}_{0},\dots,B^{j}_{m_{j}})
, we obtain that if the arrangement
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\bigcup_{j=0}^{l}\bigcup_{i=0}^{m_{j}}\bigcup_{v_{P}\in B^{j}_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{P}
is inductively connected, then so is
\mathcal{D}
.

As a consequence of the previous steps, the obtained graph
G_{\mathcal{E}}
is empty. Thus, the arrangement
\mathcal{E}
is either empty or generic. In both cases, we have that
\mathcal{E}
is inductively connected. According to the previous steps, this implies that
\mathcal{A}
is also inductively connected.
∎

The fact that the diffeomorphism type of the complement of both nice and generalized simple arrangements is combinatorially determined is well-known and forms the main theorems of
[
JY94
]
and
[
WY05
]
, respectively. This can also be deduced from Theorem
3.9
, Proposition
3.4
and Randell’s Lattice–Isotopy Theorem
[

]
.

3.3.
Inductively rigid arrangements

Following a similar approach as for inductively connected arrangements, we introduce another combinatorial class of arrangements with connected moduli space.

Definition 3.12
.

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
is
inductively rigid
if there exists an order
\omega
such that for any arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{i}
in the ascending chain (
2
), we have
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{i})=\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}
.

Note that if an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is inductively rigid, then any
\mathcal{A}_{i}
in the induced ordered chain (
2
) is also inductively rigid. As a consequence, any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
with
|\mathcal{A}|\leq 3
is inductively rigid. In addition, no arrangement in the classes
\mathfrak{X}(n)
and
\overline{\mathfrak{X}}(n+1)
is inductively rigid for
n\geq 4
, by Remark
2.5
.

Proposition 3.13
.

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}\in\Arr_{n}
is inductively rigid if and only if either
n\leq 4
and
\mathcal{A}\notin\mathfrak{X}(4)
, or
n\geq 5
and there exists an order
\omega
on
\mathcal{A}
such that:

(A1)

\mathcal{A}_{4}
is generic, i.e. for all
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, one has
\tau_{i}(\mathcal{A},\omega)=0
,

(A2)

for all
i\in\{5,\dots,n\}
, one has
\tau_{i}(\mathcal{A},\omega)\geq 2
.

Proof.

Any arrangement of at most three lines is inductively rigid. Whenever
n=4
, either
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{X}(4)
or
\mathcal{A}
is fixed by the
\PGL_{3}
-action and thus
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=\left\{\mathcal{A}\right\}
. The first case is not inductively rigid while the second one is. Assume that
n\geq 5
, one has that:

•

If for any order
\omega
the condition
(A1)
does not hold, then
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{X}(n)\cup\overline{\mathfrak{X}}(n)
with
n\geq 5
and thus, due to Remark
2.5
,
\mathcal{A}
is not inductively rigid.

•

If for any order
\omega
, the condition
(A2)
does not hold, then there exists
i\geq 5
such that
\tau_{i}(\mathcal{A},\omega)<2
. This implies that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{i})>0
and thus
\mathcal{A}
is not inductively rigid.

•

If there exists an order
\omega
over
\mathcal{A}
such that
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold, then by a recursive application of
[
NY12
, Lemma 3.2]
, we deduce that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{i})=\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}
for all
i\in\{5,\dots,n\}
. Then,
\mathcal{A}
is inductively rigid.

Since we have covered all possible cases, we obtain the stated equivalence.
∎

As an important consequence of the previous result, we conclude that the class of inductively rigid arrangements is combinatorial. The former class is not contained in the class of inductively connected arrangements, as it is shown in the following example.

Example 3.14
.

For
n\geq 1
, consider the family of arrangements
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
defined by the
4(n+1)
lines:
z=0
,
x-kz=0
,
y-kz=0
, for
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and
x+y-kz=0
for
k\in\{0,\dots,2n\}
, see Figure
4
. It is left to the reader to verify that the arrangements
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
are inductively rigid but not inductively connected for

.

3.4.
Arrangements with a rigid pencil form

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
C_{k}
if
k
is the minimal integer such that there exists a subarrangement
\mathcal{D}_{k}\subset\mathcal{A}
of

lines such that
\Sing_{\geq 3}(\mathcal{A})
is contained in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
. Nazir and Yoshinaga proved that if an arrangement is
C_{0}
,
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
then it is inductively connected, and so that its moduli space is connected, see
[
NY12
, Theorem. 3.11]
. They also introduced the combinatorial class
C_{3}
of simple
2
2

2

The term simple used here is not related to the one of Section
3.2
.
type.

Definition 3.15
.

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}
of class
C_{3}
is of
simple type
if one of following conditions holds:

(i)

the three lines of
\mathcal{D}_{3}
are in generic position, and one of them contains a unique multiple point,

(ii)

the three lines of
\mathcal{D}_{3}
are concurrent.

Theorem 3.16

(
[
NY12
, Thm. 3.15]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a
C_{3}
arrangement of simple type then its moduli space
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is connected.

The main argument to prove that
C_{3}
arrangement of simple type with concurrent lines is irreducible use the fact that one can fix the three lines using the action of
\PGL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
. Using the combinatorial notion of inductively rigid arrangements, one can define a larger class of arrangements with a connected moduli space.

Definition 3.17
.

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}
has a
rigid pencil form
if it contains an inductively rigid subarrangement
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with a singular point
P_{0}
such that for any multiple point
P\in\Sing_{\geq 3}(\mathcal{A})
one of the following conditions holds:

(RP1)

P
is a singular point of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,

(RP2)

the line
(P,P_{0})
is in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.18
.

Any
C_{3}
arrangement of simple type has a rigid pencil form.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
C_{3}
arrangement of simple type. Assume that
\mathcal{A}
verifies condition (i) of Definition
3.15
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{3}=\{\ell,\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}\}
be a generic subarrangement that contains all the multiple points of
\mathcal{A}
, and such that
\ell^{\prime\prime}
contains a unique multiple point
Q_{0}
. There exist two lines
\ell_{Q_{0}},\ell^{\prime}_{Q_{0}}\in\mathcal{A}
passing through
Q_{0}
which do not contained
\ell\cap\ell^{\prime}
. The arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{\ell,\ell^{\prime},\ell_{Q_{0}},\ell^{\prime}_{Q_{0}}\}
is inductively rigid. The multiple point
Q_{0}
is contained in
\ell_{Q_{0}}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{Q_{0}}
, and all the other multiple points of
\mathcal{A}
are contained in
\ell\cup\ell^{\prime}
. So
\mathcal{A}
has a rigid pencil form.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}
verifies condition (ii) of Definition
3.15
. Then any multiple point is in the
3
-pencil
\mathcal{D}_{3}
. Since
\mathcal{D}_{3}
is in
\mathfrak{X}(3)
, then it is inductively rigid. So
\mathcal{A}
has a rigid pencil form.
∎

Theorem 3.19
.

If
\mathcal{A}
has a rigid pencil form then its moduli space
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is connected.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is similar to the one of
[
NY12
, Proof of Theorem 3.15, case (ii)]
. One can assume first that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is maximal, in the sense that for all
\ell\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
one has
|\ell\cap\Sing(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})|<2
. Up to a projective transformation, one can also assume that
P_{0}
has coordinates
(0:1:0)
and that any line
\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
which pass through
P_{0}
is defined by the equation
x-\lambda_{i}z=0
, for a fixed
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. Denote by
P_{i}
the multiple points of
\mathcal{A}
which are not singular points of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. By Condition
(RP2)
, each
P_{i}
has coordinates
(\lambda_{i}:\alpha_{i}:1)
, for some
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. The moduli space is then defined by some collinearity relations between the points
P_{i}
and the singular points of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. More precisely, there are two types:

•

The collinearity between three points
(P_{i},P_{j},P_{k})
given by the linear equation on
\alpha_{i}
,
\alpha_{j}
and
\alpha_{k}
:

\alpha_{i}(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})+\alpha_{j}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})+\alpha_{k}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})=0.

Lemma 4.3

If

is an elementary perturbation of

then
\sum_{P\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}(|P|-2)=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}(|P|-2)-1

Proof.

Assume that

at

-(1), all the terms in the right-sided sum, except for that depending on

Note that, according to Definition

Definition 4.4
.

A

For brevity sake, a

will be denoted by

4.2.

\Theta(\left\langle P\right\rangle)=\Lambda(\ell_{i})+\Lambda(\ell_{j}).

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement and let
\mathcal{C}_{0}\prec\mkern-5.0mu\prec\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
be a
m
-perturbation. There exists a parametrization
\Psi
of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
as in (
5
) such that for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:

\max(\deg a_{j},\deg b_{j},\deg c_{j})\leq\Lambda(\ell_{j}).

Moreover, consider the description of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
induced by
\Psi
as in (
4
). If
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}\prec\mathcal{C}_{i}
is the elementary perturbation at
(\ell_{j},P)
, and if
\ell_{j_{1}}
and
\ell_{j_{2}}
are two lines passing through the parent of
P
in
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}
, then:

\deg\Delta_{i}\leq\Lambda(\ell_{j_{1}})+\Lambda(\ell_{j_{2}})+\Lambda(\ell_{j}).

Proof.

The first inequality holds straightforward by construction of
\Lambda
and
\Theta
. More precisely, the rules
(R1)
,
(R2)
and
(R4)
control the behaviour of the degrees of the polynomials in equations (
7
), (
8
) and (
6
), respectively, meanwhile
(R3)
controls the one of (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
). Furthermore, by Equation (
12
),
\Delta_{i}=\det(\Psi(\bv)_{j_{1}},\Psi(\bv)_{j_{2}},\Psi(\bv)_{j})
, then the first inequality induces the second part of the lemma.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a line arrangement and assume that
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
admits a
m
-perturbation
\mathcal{C}_{0}\prec\mkern-5.0mu\prec\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
. The following inequality holds:

\#\compo(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(\Lambda(\ell_{i_{1}})+\Lambda(\ell_{i_{2}})+\Lambda(\ell_{j})\big).

where
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}\prec\mathcal{C}_{i}
is the elementary perturbation at
(\ell_{j},P)
, and
\ell_{j_{1}}
,
\ell_{j_{2}}
are two lines contained in the parent of
P
in
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}
.

Proof.

In Theorem
4.5
, we have obtain a description of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
as the intersection of at most
m
proper algebraic hypersurfaces
\left\{V_{i}:\Delta_{i}=0\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
, in a non-empty Zariski open subset of an affine linear space. By
[
Ful98
, Ex. 8.3.6]
, this implies that
\#\Irr(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\deg\Delta_{i}
. By Lemmas
4.8
and
4.9
, the result holds.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

There are three types of choices in the construction of the upper bound in the previous theorem: the first one is obviously the choice of the
m
-perturbation
\mathcal{C}_{0}\prec\mkern-5.0mu\prec\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
, the second one is in rule
(R3)
(the choice of the lines
\ell_{i_{1}}
and
\ell_{i_{2}}
when
\tau_{i}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\omega_{0})=1
), and the third one is the choice of the lines
\ell_{j_{1}}
and
\ell_{j_{2}}
in the elementary perturbation
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}\prec\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Note that the value of the upper bound depends on the previous choices.

Heuristically, we can optimize the upper bound by taking the lines
\ell_{i_{1}},\ell_{i_{2}}
and
\ell_{j_{1}},\ell_{j_{2}}
which minimize the value of their images by the map
\Lambda
.

4.4.
Sharpness of the upper bound

By construction, the inequality in Theorem
4.10
becomes an equality for any inductively connected or inductively rigid arrangement. The question is then, is this inequality still sharp in non-trivial cases?

Theorem 4.12
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, there exists an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
such that
\#\compo(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))\geq N
, and

\#\compo(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(\Lambda(\ell_{i_{1}})+\Lambda(\ell_{i_{2}})+\Lambda(\ell_{j})\big),

with the notation of Theorem
4.10
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime number such that
p\geq N
. Fix a primitive
p
-root of unity
\zeta
. Consider the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{p}=\left\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{2p+2}\right\}
with lines:

\ell_{1}\colon x-y=0,\quad\ell_{2}\colon x-\zeta y=0,\quad\ell_{2i+1}\colon x+\zeta^{i}z=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\ell_{2i+2}:\zeta^{-i}y+z=0,

for
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
. The multiple points of
\mathcal{A}_{p}
are the two points of multiplicity
p
:

\{\ell_{3},\ell_{5},\dots,\ell_{2p+1}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\ell_{4},\ell_{6},\dots,\ell_{2p+2}\},

and the
2p
triple points given by:

\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2i+1},\ell_{2i+2}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\ell_{2},\ell_{2i+2},\ell_{[2i]+3}\}

for
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, where
[a]
is the value of
a
modulo
2p
such that
0\leq[a]<2p
.

Consider the elementary perturbation
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{A}_{p})
on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}_{p})
at
(\ell_{2p+2},\{\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\ell_{2p+2}\})
. Let
\omega_{0}
be the following order on
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{A}_{p})
:

(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{2p+2})\longmapsto(5,6,1,2,3,4,7,8,\dots,(2p+2)).

We have that
\tau(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{A}_{p}),\omega_{0})=(0,0,0,0,2,1,2,2\dots,2,2)
. So
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{A}_{p})
is inductively connected, and thus it is a
1
-perturbation of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}_{p})
. Using the rules
(R1)
-
(R2)
-
(R3)
-
(R4)
, we can compute that:

\Lambda_{p}:(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{2p+2})\longmapsto(0,1,0,0,0,0,1,1,2,2,\dots,p-2,p-2).

By Theorem
4.10
, we obtain that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{p})
has at most
\Lambda_{p}(\ell_{2})+\Lambda_{p}(\ell_{3})+\Lambda_{p}(\ell_{2p+2})=1+0+(p-2)=p-1
connected components. On the other hand, the computation of the moduli space using Theorem
4.5
shows that the dimension is zero. Furthermore, it contains
\mathcal{A}_{p}
for any choice of primitive
p
-root of unity
\zeta
. We conclude that
\#\compo(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}_{p}))=p-1
.
∎

Note that the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{2}
corresponds to the Ceva arrangement, while the arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{3}
are the MacLane arrangements
[
Mac36
]
. This family of arrangements appears in
[
CS18
]
in the context of Zariski pairs as subarrangements of the reflection arrangements associated with
G(N,N,3)
.

4.5.
Class of
\kappa_{m}
arrangements

In the spirit of the class of
C_{k}
arrangements, let us introduce the following class.

Definition 4.13
.

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
\kappa_{m}
if
m
is the smaller integer such that
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
admits a
m
-perturbation.

Example 4.14
.

The class of
\kappa_{0}
arrangements corresponds to the class of inductively connected arrangements, and the arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{p}
defined in Section
4.4
are all
\kappa_{1}
.

For fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathfrak{C}_{m}(n)
as the maximal number of connected components of the moduli space among all
\kappa_{m}
arrangements of
n
lines.
It is clear that
\mathfrak{C}_{0}(n)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. Note that if
\mathcal{A}
is a
\kappa_{m}
arrangement, then the arrangement
\mathcal{A}\cup\{\ell\}
is also
\kappa_{m}
for any line
\ell
which is generic with
\mathcal{A}
. It follows that
\mathfrak{C}_{m}(n+1)\geq\mathfrak{C}_{m}(n)
.

Question.
For fixed
m
, how the number
\mathfrak{C}_{m}(n)
behaves when
n
grows?

As it is shown in the proof of Theorem
4.12
, we know that
\mathfrak{C}_{1}(n)
grows at least as
n/2
.
