Finite element analysis of the nearly incompressible linear elasticity eigenvalue problem with variable coefficients
Arbaz Khan, Felipe Lepe, David Mora, Jesus Vellojin

Abstract
In this paper we present a mathematical and numerical analysis of an eigenvalue problem associated to the elasticity-Stokes equations stated in two and three dimensions. Both problems are related through the Herrmann pressure. Employing the Babuška–Brezzi theory, it is proved that the resulting continuous and discrete variational formulations are well-posed. In particular, the finite element method is based on general inf-sup stables pairs for the Stokes system, such that, Taylor–Hood finite elements. By using a general approximation theory for compact operators, we obtain optimal order error estimates for the eigenfunctions and a double order for the eigenvalues. Under mild assumptions, we have that these estimates hold with constants independent of the Lamé coefficient λ \lambda . In addition, we carry out the reliability and efficiency analysis of a residual-based a posteriori error estimator for the spectral problem. We report a series of numerical tests in order to assess the performance of the method and its behavior when the nearly incompressible case of elasticity is considered.

Introduction

The accurate solution of eigenvalue problems coming from continuum mechanics is crucial because the stability
of systems relying on fluids or structures, or their coupling, hinges on understanding vibration modes associated with spectral problems.
Notable monographs currently addressing this subject are cited here for reference
MR2652780
.

One important aspect that is still under investigation is understanding the eigenvalue properties in parameter limits between Stokes and elasticity equations. It is well-established that in elasticity equations where the Poisson ratio is close to
1/2
, classical numerical methods exhibit the locking phenomenon,
which can be mitigated, for example, with mixed formulations, least-squares, among others
(see, e.g.
MR4570534
;
meddahi2013finite
;
MR4542511
;
MR4396855
;
MR4271564
;
MR4445536
;
MR3973678
).
In this incompressible regime, the equations align with Stokes equations. A study in
MR3962898
demonstrates that the spectrum of the elasticity eigenvalue problem converges to that of the Stokes eigenvalue problem as the Poisson ratio tends to
1/2
. Recent research, as seen in
zhong2023spectral
, proposes a mixed method for the linear elasticity where the stress, rotations, and displacement are considered as unknowns. Here the convergence of the method also works correctly for the case when Poisson ratio tends to
1/2
. Also, novel strategies like least squares formulations have been applied to elasticity eigenvalue problems
MR4271564
.

In the realm of a posteriori analysis for elasticity eigenvalue problems, we have, for example,
MR3973678
, where a locking-free mixed formulation based on the finite element method has been proposed for the load problem, accompanied by a priori and a posteriori error estimates. The authors provide estimation strategies that prove to be robust with respect to the material parameters. They ensure robustness in elasticity, which involves multiple parameters compared to the single viscosity parameter in the Stokes problem. We have also the work of
bertrand2021adaptive
. Here, the authors aim to expand the results obtained in a previous study of a conforming mixed finite elements for the elasticity problem to address the related eigenvalue problem. Their study shows that using a residual-type error estimator can effectively steer an adaptive scheme Additionally, they explore how implementing a postprocessing technique can improve the convergence rate of the scheme. Postprocessing techniques have also been explored recently in
MR4471016
, where a reliable and efficient a posteriori error estimators for mixed formulations for the elasticity eigenvalue problem is studied, including an analysis for the perfectly incompressible case.

The concepts presented in
MR3973678
can be extended to the eigenvalue problem under the same norm. It is well-known that such a problem lacks a unique solution due to eigenvalues/eigenfunctions exhibiting a multiplicity greater than one. Adding to this, we have a parameter dependent problem that connects the elasticity eigenvalue problem with the Stokes one, where the Poisson ratio serves as the involved parameter. This is an important issue, since according this parameter changes, the spectrum of the elasticity operator changes and hence, the multiplicity of the eigenvalues starts to behave different according to this. Since we have a fixed eigenvalue problem (Stokes) as a limit, the elasticity eigenvalue problem change its spectrum according to the change of the Poisson ratio when we are approximating to the spectrum of Stokes. Then, the question that emerge is how does the spectrum of the parameter-dependent elasticity problem converge towards the Stokes spectrum. This issue is difficult to handle and must be studied.

Our contribution focuses on analyzing both the Stokes and elasticity eigenproblems within a unified locking-free system. The key lies in considering the Herrmann pressure that connects the fluid and elastic representations. The introduction of variable coefficients, reflecting the physical attributes of the structure, adds another layer of complexity to our analysis. Specifically, these variable coefficients demands the use of a weighted norm with spatial-dependent parameters in order to ensure robustness of the estimates. It is worth emphasizing that, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this work represents a pioneering effort in examining the elasticity/Stokes eigenvalue problem with variable coefficients. We also propose a scheme that expand the numerical analysis to encompass two families of finite elements known for their inf-sup stability: the Taylor-Hood elements and the mini element.
Moreover, others inf-sup stable finite elements could be also considered.
However, for an accurate analysis of the eigenvalue problem, certain adjustments become essential. More precisely, a suitable scaling of the problem with respect to physical parameters is required in order to achieve robustness for the spectral problem. The approach is to leverage simple tools for the analysis, and a key argument is the problem’s regularity, which we will use to conduct both a priori and a posteriori error estimation with the aid of the compact operators theory
BO
.
Finally, we stress that our locking-free method is very competitive in terms of computational cost.

The outline of our manuscript is the following: In Section
2
we present the model problem, describing the parameters involved on the elasticity eigensystem and their relation with the Stokes eigenvalue problem through the Herrmann’s pressure. We outline the scaling of the problem and the respective variational formulation which we analyze under a suitable weighted norm. Regularity results are presented together with the parameter dependent solution operator. Also, we report an analysis of the relation between the spectrums of the elasticity and the Stokes eigenvalue problems when the Poisson ratio tends to
1/2
. Section
3
is dedicated to the discretization of the eigenvalue problem, where we introduce the finite element spaces for our study, the corresponding discrete version of the nearly incompressible eigenvalue problem, and we derive convergence of the method, and error estimates for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. In Section
4
we design an a posteriori error estimator which we prove under standard techniques it reliability and efficiency. Finally in Section
5
we report a series of numerical tests in order to study the performance of the method in two and three dimensions. Here the aims are two: first, to confirm the results related to the a priori analysis, where we compute the spectrum and order of convergence for the approximation and secondly, to analyze the performance of the a posteriori estimator where the aim is to recover the optimal order of convergence when the configuration of the problem does not allow sufficiently smooth eigenfunctions, deteriorating the a priori convergence rates.

2
The model problem

In this section we present the eigenvalue problem of our interest.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
an open and bounded
domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let us assume that
the boundary is divided into two parts
\partial\Omega:=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
,
where
|\Gamma_{D}|>0
. In particular, we assume that the structure is fixed on
\Gamma_{D}
and free of stress on
\Gamma_{N}
.

The eigenvalue problem of interest is given by: Find
(\widehat{\kappa},\boldsymbol{u})
such that

\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\left[2\widehat{\mu}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\widehat{\lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\mathbb{I}\right]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\kappa}\rho\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N},

where
\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}}
is the natural frequency,
\boldsymbol{u}
is the displacement,
\rho
is the material density,
the Lamé parameters
\widehat{\lambda}
and
\widehat{\mu}
are defined by

\widehat{\lambda}:=\frac{E(x)\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}:=\frac{E(x)}{2(1+\nu)},

and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
represents the strain tensor given by
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{v})^{\texttt{t}})
where
t
represents the transpose operator. On the other hand, regarding to the physical parameters,
E(x)
represents the space-variable Young modulus and
\nu
represents the Poisson ratio which we assume as a constant. Let us assume the existence of two positive constants

E_{\min}
and
E_{\max}
such that the Young’s modulus is bounded in the sense

E_{\min}\leq E(x)\leq E_{\max}.

Scaling the first equation of (
2.1
) by
(1+\nu)
we obtain the following eigenvalue problem: Find
(\kappa,\boldsymbol{u})
such that

\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\left[2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\mathbb{I}\right]

\displaystyle=\kappa\rho\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N},

where the scaled eigenvalue and the Lamé coefficients are given by

\kappa:=(1+\nu)\widehat{\kappa},\qquad\lambda:=\frac{E(x)\nu}{1-2\nu},\qquad\mu:=\frac{E(x)}{2}.

(2.3)

Note that for a given
E(x)
, the eigenvalue problem (
2.2
) has the advantage of having
\mu
independent of
\nu
. The incompressibility of the material is explicitly given only by the behavior of the constant
\nu
through the Lamé parameter
\lambda
.

With the scaled parameters specified in (
2.3
) we rewrite system (
2.1
) as the following system:
We seek the eigenvalue
\kappa
, a vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
, and a scalar field
p
such that

\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\kappa\rho\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{\lambda}p

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N}.

We note from (
2.3
) that if the Poisson ratio approaches
1/2
,
then
\lambda\uparrow\infty
.
In such case, we recover the Stokes system.
This is an important matter in view of deriving robust stability and error bounds. From now and on, and for simplicity,
in the analysis below we consider
\rho=1
.

2.1
Variational formulation and preliminary results

The aim of this section is to introduce a variational formulation
for system (
2
). From now and on the relation
\texttt{a}\lesssim\texttt{b}
indicates that
\texttt{a}\leq C\texttt{b}
, with a positive constant
C
which is independent of
a
,
b
.

Since we are focused in the pressure and displacement, the resulting formulation is a saddle-point problem that can be studied according to the theory of Babuška–Brezzi
MR3097958
.

Let us begin by defining the spaces
\mathrm{H}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\;:\;\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\right\}
and
\mathrm{Q}:=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
where we will seek the displacement and the pressure, respectively. Now, by testing
system (
2
) with adequate
functions and imposing the boundary conditions,
we end up with the following saddle point variational formulation:
Find
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
with
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\neq(\boldsymbol{0},0)
such that

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,dx-\int_{\Omega}p\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,dx

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\lambda(x)}pq\,dx

\displaystyle=\,0

\displaystyle\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.

This variational problem can be rewritten as follows:

Find
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)&=&\kappa d(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u},q)-c(p,q)&=&\;0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q},\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

where the bilinear forms

a:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}
,

b:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
,

c:\mathrm{Q}\times\mathrm{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
,
and

d:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}

are defined by

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=2\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,dx,\quad b(\boldsymbol{v},q):=-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,dx,

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,dx,\quad c(p,q):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\lambda(x)}p\,q\,dx,

for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}
, and
p,q\in\mathrm{Q}
.

To perform the analysis, we need a suitable
norm which in particular depends on the parameters of the problem.
With this in mind, and for all
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times Q
,
we define the following weighted norm,

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}\times Q}^{2}:=\|\mu(x)^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mu(x)^{-1/2}q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\lambda(x)^{-1/2}q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Moreover, in what follows, we assume that
\mu(x),1/\lambda(x)\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Let us introduce and define the kernel of
b(\cdot,\cdot)
by

\mathrm{X}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}\,:\,b(\boldsymbol{v},q)=0,\quad\forall\,q\in Q\}\,=\,\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}\,:\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,=\,0\,\,\,\,{\rm in}\,\,\Omega\},

and let us recall that the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the inf-sup condition:

\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}}}{{\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0}}}\frac{|b(\boldsymbol{v},q)|}{\|\mu(x)^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}}\geq\beta_{2}\|\mu(x)^{-1/2}q\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{Q},

(2.6)

with an inf-sup constant
\beta_{2}>0
only depending on
\Omega
; see
MR3973678
, for instance.

Now, we are in a position to introduce the solution operator which we denote by

\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
and is such that
\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
, where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
is the solution of the following well posed source problem: Given
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
find
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{p})&=&d(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},q)-c(\widehat{p},q)&=&\;0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

Thanks to the Babuška-Brezzi theory
we have that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
is well defined and the following estimate holds

\|\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{f},\widehat{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

with a constant depending on the inverse of
\mu
and the Poincaré constant.
It is easy to check that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
is a selfadjoint
operator with respect to the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product. Also, let
\chi
be a real number such that
\chi\neq 0
. Notice that
(\chi,\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}
is an eigenpair of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
if and only if there exists
p\in\mathrm{Q}
such that,
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))
solves problem (
2.5
) with
\chi:=1/\kappa
.

The following result states an additional regularity
result for problem (
2.7
).

Lemma 2.1

Let
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})
be the unique solution
of (
2.7
), then there exists

s\in(0,1]
such that
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)

and the following estimate holds (see for instance
grisvard1986problemes
or
MR4430561
)

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\widehat{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where the hidden constant depends on
\Omega
and the Lamé coefficients.

Let us remark that Lemma
2.1
holds true
even for
\lambda\uparrow\infty
(Further comments on this fact can be seen in
MR3962898
).

As a consequence of this result, we conclude compactness of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
.
In fact, since
\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}

we have that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
is a compact operator. Hence, we are in position to establish the spectral characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
.

Theorem 2.1

The spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu})=\{0\}\cup\{\chi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\{\chi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive eigenvalues.

On the other hand, from
MR0975121
we have the following regularity result for the eigenfunctions of the eigenproblem (
2.5
).

Theorem 2.2

If
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
solves (
2.5
), then, there exists
r>0
such that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{H}^{1+r}(\Omega)^{d}
and
p\in\mathrm{H}^{r}(\Omega)
. Moreover, the following estimate holds

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1+r,\Omega}+\|p\|_{r,\Omega}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Now,
Let us end this section introducing the bilinear form

\mathcal{A}:(\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q})\times(\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)):=a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)+b(\boldsymbol{u},q)+c(p,q),

(2.8)

which allows us to rewrite the eigenvalue problem as follows. Find
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q))=\kappa d(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times Q.

Let us remark that
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bounded bilinear form, in the sense that there exists a positive constant
\widehat{C}:=\max\{2,\mu_{min}^{-1/2}\mu_{max}^{1/2}\}

such that

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q))\leq\widehat{C}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}\times Q}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}\times Q}\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}.

Next we state the stability result for the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 2.2

For any
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
, there exists
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
with
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
such that

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)).

Recalling the definition of the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{u},-p))=\|(2\mu)^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\lambda^{-1/2}p\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Thanks to inf-sup condition (
2.6
), for any

, we find

with

This allows us to conclude that

\geq(C_{2}-1/2\epsilon)\|(2\mu)^{-1/2}p||_{0,\Omega}^{2}-(C_{1}^{2}\epsilon/2)\|(2\mu)^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})||_{0,\Omega}.

Making the specific choices

and

, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{u},-p))+\delta\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},0))

\displaystyle\geq\|(2\mu)^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\lambda^{-1/2}p\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\delta((C_{2}-1/2\epsilon)\|(2\mu)^{-1/2}p\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-(C_{1}^{2}\epsilon/2)\|(2\mu)^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\|_{0,\Omega})

Additionally, we have

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}+\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},-p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

The proof is complete.

2.2
The limit problem

The solution operator
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
depends strongly on the values of the Poisson ratio. This must be understood in the sense that there exists a family of problems depending on
\nu
that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
must solve. As we observe on the definition of the bilinear form
c(\cdot,\cdot)
, when
\lambda
blows up, i.e.
\lambda\uparrow\infty
, then
c(\cdot,\cdot)\downarrow 0
and hence, we are in presence of a Stokes-type of problem. So the natural question is what happens with the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
when
\lambda\uparrow\infty
. Let us begin by introducing the following eigenvalue problem: Find
(\kappa_{0},(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p_{0}))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v})+\;b(\boldsymbol{v},p_{0})&=&\;\kappa_{0}d(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},q)&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.\end{array}\right.

(2.9)

For this eigenvalue problem, let us introduce the following solution operator
\boldsymbol{T}_{0}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
defined by
\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{0}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}
, where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\widehat{p}_{0})
solves the following source problem: Find
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\widehat{p}_{0})\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{p}_{0})&=&d(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},q)&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.\end{array}\right.

(2.10)

This operator
\boldsymbol{T}_{0}
is well defined, self-adjoint, and compact
and its spectral characterization is given as follows.

Lemma 2.3

The spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}_{0}
satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{0})=\{0\}\cup\{\chi_{k}^{0}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
where
\{\chi_{k}^{0}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of
positive eigenvalues such that
\chi_{k}^{0}\downarrow 0
as
k\uparrow+\infty
.

For the operators
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{0}
, we have the following result.

Lemma 2.4

The following estimate holds

\|(\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}-\boldsymbol{T}_{0})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}-\boldsymbol{T}_{0})\boldsymbol{f}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim{\|\lambda^{-1/2}\widehat{p}_{0}\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim(2(1-2\nu)/\nu)^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d},

. Subtracting (

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,dx-\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}_{0})\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,dx

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}_{0})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim\|\lambda^{-1/2}\widehat{p}_{0}\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim(2(1-2\nu)/\nu)^{1/2}\|\mu(x)^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Numerical discretization

In this section our aim is to describe a finite element discretization of problem (

Given a mesh

, we denote by

and

the finite element spaces that approximate the velocity field and the pressure, respectively. In particular, our study is focused in the following two elections:

(a)

The mini element
(

, Section 4.2.4)
: Here,

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\oplus\mathbb{B}(T)]^{d}\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{H},

(b)

The lowest order Taylor–Hood element

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{2}(T)]^{d}\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{H},

The discrete analysis will be performed in a general manner, where the both families of finite elements, namely Taylor-Hood and mini element, are considered with no difference. If some difference must be claimed, we will point it out when is necessary.

3.1

Now we are in position to introduce the discrete counterpart of the eigenvalue problem (
2.5
): Find

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})&=&\kappa_{h}d(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h},\\

(3.1)

Since the choice of mini element and Taylor-Hood are inf-sup stable, we are in position to introduce the discrete solution operator

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\widehat{p}_{h})&=&d(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h},\\

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widehat{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

is an eigenvalue of problem (

)

is an
eigenvalue of

with the

Moreover, if

represents the Lagrange interpolator operator and

represents the

-projection, the solutions of the continuous and discrete problems satisfy

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\lesssim\inf_{(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{p}-q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathcal{L}_{h}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p}-\mathcal{P}_{h}\widehat{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\

Hence, from Lemma

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim h^{s}\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\widehat{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\right)\lesssim h^{s}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(3.2)

Similar as the continuous case, with the aid of the bilinear form

4
A posteriori error analysis

The aim of this section is to introduce a suitable
residual-based error estimator for the Stokes/elasticity
eigenvalue problem. The main aim is to prove that the proposed estimator is equivalent with the error.
Moreover, on the forthcoming analysis we will focus only on eigenvalues with multiplicity

and notations. For any element

We decompose

where

For each inner edge

and for any sufficiently smooth function

, we define the jump of its normal derivative on

by

\left[\!\!\left[\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{n}}}\right]\!\!\right]_{\ell}:=\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}|_{K})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K}+\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}|_{K^{\prime}})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K^{\prime}},

where
K
and

are the two elements in
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
sharing the

\ell
and

and
\boldsymbol{n}_{K^{\prime}}
are the respective outer unit normal vectors.

4.1

The local element residual
(\eta_{K})
and the edge jump-residual estimator
(\eta_{J})
are defined as follows:

For the jump terms, we define

\displaystyle J_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\![{(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\textbf{n}}]\!],&E\in\Omega\cap\mathcal{E}_{h}\\
{(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\textbf{n}},&E\in\Gamma_{N}\cap\mathcal{E}_{h}\\

Here, the parameters

,

and

Finally, we discuss the definition of the global a posteriori estimatior

4.2

The task here is to prove that the error is upper bounded by the estimator, together with
the high order terms. This is stated in the following result.

Theorem 4.1

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim\eta+\Theta+|\kappa-\kappa_{h}|+\kappa\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

where the hidden constant is independent of the mesh size,
\nu
and the discrete solutions. Additionally, the following reliability of the eigenvalue also holds:

\displaystyle|\kappa-\kappa_{h}|\lesssim\eta^{2}+\Theta^{2}.

Given

2.5

3.1
)) in order to obtain the error equation

(4.1)

Thanks to Lemma
2.2
, we have that for
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-\widehat{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-\widehat{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{A}((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{w},r)).

(4.2)

Let us define
\boldsymbol{w}_{I}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}
as the Clément interpolant (see
(

, Chapter 2)
) and
r_{I}\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}
as the standard
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-projection of
r
. Then, from (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) we obtain

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w})_{0,\Omega}-\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{w},r))\\
=(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w})_{0,\Omega}+\kappa_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I})_{0,\Omega}-\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I},r-r_{I}))\\
=\underbrace{-\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I},r-r_{I}))+(\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I})_{0,\Omega}}_{\Lambda_{1}}+\underbrace{(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w})_{0,\Omega}}_{\Lambda_{2}}.

An application of integration by parts together with Cauchy–Schwarz and the approximation results of the Clément interpolant gives

|\Lambda_{1}|\lesssim\eta{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},\quad|\Lambda_{2}|\lesssim\|\mu_{h}^{1/2}(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

This completes the proof. Using

(\kappa-\kappa_{h})d(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h});(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}))-\kappa d(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})

with the above result leads to the second stated result.

4.3
Efficiency

The analysis for the efficiency follows the classic arguments based on bubble functions. An interior bubble function
\psi_{K}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(K)
for a polytope
K

can be constructed piecewise as the sum of the cubic
bubble functions for each tetrahedron of the
sub-triangulation
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{K}
that attain the value 1 at the barycenter of the tetrahedron. On the other hand, a facet bubble function
\psi_{\ell}
for

\ell\in\partial K
is a piecewise quadratic function
attaining the value 1 at the barycenter of
\ell
and vanishing
on the polytope
K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
that do not contain
\ell

on its boundary.

The following results which establish standard estimates
for bubble functions will be useful in what follows (see
MR1885308
;
MR3059294
).

Lemma 4.1
(Interior bubble functions)

For any
K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, let
\psi_{K}
be the corresponding interior bubble function.
Then, there holds

\displaystyle\|q\|_{0,K}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{K}\psi_{K}q^{2}\leq\|q\|_{0,K}^{2}\qquad\forall q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K),

\displaystyle\|q\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi_{K}q\|_{0,K}+h_{K}\|\nabla(\psi_{K}q)\|_{0,K}\lesssim\|q\|_{0,K}\qquad\forall q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K),

where the hidden constants are
independent of
h_{K}
.

Lemma 4.2
(Facet bubble functions)

For any
K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
and
\ell\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
, let
\psi_{\ell}

be the corresponding facet bubble function. Then, there holds

\|q\|_{0,\ell}^{2}\lesssim\int_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}q^{2}\leq\|q\|_{0,\ell}^{2}\qquad\forall q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(\ell).

Moreover, for all
q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(\ell)
, there exists an extension of
q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)
(again denoted by
q
) such that

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\psi_{\ell}q\|_{0,K}+h_{K}^{1/2}\|\nabla(\psi_{\ell}q)\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\ell},

where the hidden constants are independent of
h_{K}
.

Now, we are in a position to establish the efficiency
\eta
, which is stated in the following result.

Lemma 4.3

(Efficiency) The following estimate holds

\eta\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+\Theta+h.o.t,

here the hidden constant is independent of the meshsize,
\nu
, and the discrete solution.

Proof

Also, we define
\boldsymbol{v}_{T}:=\psi_{T}h_{T}^{2}\rho_{1}^{2}\textbf{R}_{1,T}
, where
\psi_{T}
is the bubble that satisfies the properties of Lemma
4.1
. Now we compute a bound for the term
h_{T}^{2}\|\rho_{1}\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}
. To do this task, let us recall that
the continuous problem satisfies
-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))+\nabla p-\rho\kappa\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Hence

h_{T}^{2}\|\rho_{1}\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}^{2}\leq\int_{T}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\mu_{h}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))-\nabla p_{h}+\rho\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\\
=\int_{T}(-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\mu_{h}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}))+\nabla\texttt{e}_{p}+\rho\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\rho\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\texttt{e}_{\mu}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{u}})))\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\\
=\underbrace{\int_{T}(-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\mu_{h}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}))+\nabla\texttt{e}_{p}-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\texttt{e}_{\mu}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{u}})))\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}}_{\textrm{T}_{1}}+\underbrace{\int_{T}(\rho\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\rho\kappa\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}}_{\textrm{T}_{2}}.

To establish the bound of
\textrm{T}_{1}
, we apply integration by parts which gives

T_{1}=\int_{T}(-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\mu_{h}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}))+\nabla\texttt{e}_{p}-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(2\texttt{e}_{\mu}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{u}})))\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\\
=\int_{T}((2\mu_{h}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}))-\texttt{e}_{p}I+\texttt{e}_{\mu}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{u}}))\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\leq({\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{T}+\Theta_{K})h_{T}\|\rho_{1}\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}.

For the term
\textrm{T}_{2}
is enough to follow the proof of
(
MR2473688
, Theorem 3.2)
in order to obtain

\textrm{T}_{2}\lesssim h_{T}^{2}(|\kappa-\kappa_{h}|+\kappa\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T})\|\rho_{1}\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}.

Recalling the definition of
R_{2,T}
and the continuous problem satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}+1/\lambda\;p=0
, we find

\|\rho_{2}^{1/2}R_{2,T}\|_{0,T}=\|\rho_{2}^{1/2}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+1/\lambda(p-p_{h}))\|_{0,T}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{T}.

Next, we define
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}:=\psi_{\ell}h_{E}\rho_{E}^{2}[\![{(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\textbf{n}}]\!]
, where
\psi_{\ell}
is the bubble that satisfies the properties of Lemma
4.2
. Now we compute a bound for the term
h_{E}\|\rho_{E}J_{\ell}\|_{0,\ell}^{2}
. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle h_{E}\|\rho_{E}J_{\ell}\|_{0,\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim([\![{(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\textbf{n}}]\!],\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{\ell}=([\![{(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-(p_{h}-p)\mathbb{I}})\textbf{n}]\!],\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{\ell}

(4.3)

Using Integration by parts on
\omega_{T}
gives

\displaystyle([\![{(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-(p_{h}-p)\mathbb{I})\textbf{n}}]\!],\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{\ell}=\sum_{T\in\omega_{T}}

\displaystyle\Big((2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-2\mu_{h}(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}))_{T}+(\texttt{e}_{p},\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{T}

\displaystyle\quad+(\textbf{R}_{1,T},\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{T}+(\rho\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\rho\kappa\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{T}\Big).

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz with Lemma
4.2
and combining with (

\displaystyle h_{E}\|\rho_{E}J_{\ell}\|_{0,\ell}^{2}

Combining the above estimates leads to the the stated efficiency result.

5
Numerical results

This section is devoted to analyze the performance of the proposed finite element method through different experiments, considering different values of
\mu
and
\lambda
. The aim is to compare the computationally the spectrums of the Stokes limit and elasticity eigenvalue problems. To do this task, we have implemented a FEniCS script
AlnaesBlechta2015a
for the codes. The meshes have been constructed using the GMSH software
geuzaine2009gmsh
.

The convergence rates of the eigenvalues have been obtained with a standard least square fitting and highly refined meshes. The Taylor-Hood and mini-element families can be used indistinctively as observed in the theory provided in Section
2
. Taylor-Hood elements will be preferred for the 2D and 3D representations, whereas the adaptive test are performed with the mini-element family.

We also denote by
N
the mesh refinement level, whereas
dof
denotes the number of degrees of freedom. We denote by
\kappa_{h_{i}}
the i-th discrete eigenfrequency, and by
\widehat{\kappa}_{h_{i}}:=\kappa_{h_{i}}/(1+\nu)
the unscaled version of
\kappa_{h_{i}}
.

Hence, we denote the error on the
i
-th eigenvalue by
\texttt{err}(\widehat{\kappa}_{i})
with

\texttt{err}(\widehat{\kappa}_{i}):=|\widehat{\kappa}_{h_{i}}-\widehat{\kappa}_{i}|.

We remark that computing
\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}_{h_{i}}}
or
\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}}
gives the corresponding discrete and exact eigenfrequency.

With the aim of assessing the performance of our estimator, we consider domains with singularities in two and three dimensions in order to observe the improvement of the convergence rate. On each adaptive iteration, we use the blue-green marking strategy to refine each
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
whose indicator
\eta_{T^{\prime}}
satisfies

\eta_{T^{\prime}}\geq 0.5\max\{\eta_{T}\,:\,T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\}.

The effectivity indexes with respect to
\zeta
and the eigenvalue
\widehat{\kappa_{i}}
are defined by

\texttt{eff}(\widehat{\kappa_{i}}):=\frac{\texttt{err}(\widehat{\kappa_{i}})}{\eta^{2}}.

E=\rho=1

r\geq 1.2
for the limit Stokes scenario. We also note that both methods deals with the singularity in a slightly different manner.
