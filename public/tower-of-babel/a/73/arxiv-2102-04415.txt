Self-duality of the lattice of transfer systems via weak factorization systems
Evan E. Franchere, Kyle Ormsby, Angélica M. Osorno, Weihang Qin, Riley Waugh

Abstract
. For a finite group G G , G G -transfer systems are combinatorial objects which encode the homotopy category of G G - N ∞ N_{\infty} operads, whose algebras in G G -spectra are E ∞ E_{\infty} G G -spectra with a specified collection of multiplicative norms. For G G finite Abelian, we demonstrate a correspondence between G G -transfer systems and weak factorization systems on the poset category of subgroups of G G . This induces a self-duality on the lattice of G G -transfer systems.

4.
A categorical approach to transfer systems

In this section we define transfer systems for arbitrary finite posets, generalizing the definition for Dedekind groups. We show that for a finite lattice
\mathcal{P}
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between transfer systems on
\mathcal{P}
and weak factorization systems on
\mathcal{P}
. Using this, we prove that
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{P})
is self-dual whenever
\mathcal{P}
is a finite self-dual lattice. We compare this result with the involution defined for
G=C_{p_{1}\dots p_{n}}
in
[
BBPR
]
.

4.1.
Transfer systems on posets

We now generalize the definition of a transfer system to an arbitrary poset and characterize them in categorical terms.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P},\leq)
be a poset. A

on
\mathcal{P}
consists of a partial order
\;\mathcal{R}\;
on
\mathcal{P}
that refines

, if

,

, and

(x\wedge z)\;\mathcal{R}\;z
.

As noted above, for a Dedekind group

\mathrm{Sub}(G)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a poset considered as a category. A collection of morphisms
\mathcal{R}
is a transfer system if and only if
\mathcal{R}
is a subcategory that contains all objects and is closed under pullbacks.

Proof.

Reflexivity translates to containing the identity morphism for all objects, while transitivity translates to being closed under composition. Note that for a diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}

in
\mathcal{P}
, the pullback, if it exists, is given by

. Thus, being closed under restriction translates precisely to being closed under pullbacks.
∎

Remark 4.6

Model categories are closely related to weak factorization systems. Indeed, as proved in

•

Remark 4.9

In a poset
\mathcal{P}
, the only isomorphisms are the identity maps, and the only retract of a morphism is itself. Thus,

and

imply that if

is a weak factorization system in a lattice

, then

is a transfer system on

.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
be a pair of classes of morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
. Then
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
is a weak factorization system on

if and only if

(1)

every morphism

can be factored as

with

and

,

(2)

, and

(3)

are closed under retracts.

We use this result to construct weak factorization systems from transfer systems on a poset.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite lattice and let

be a transfer system on
\mathcal{P}
. Then there is a unique weak factorization system
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})
on
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

For a weak factorization system we need
\mathcal{L}={}^{\boxslash}\mathcal{R},
so
\mathcal{L}
is uniquely determined by
\mathcal{R}
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is a poset, the only retract of a morphism

is itself. Thus, by

, it suffices to show that every morphism can be factored, since

implies
\mathcal{L}\boxslash\mathcal{R}
.

Let

be a morphism in
\mathcal{P}
. If
x\to y
is in
\mathcal{L}
we are done because we can factor with the identity. Suppose then that

is not in
\mathcal{L}
. This means that there exists a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w}

with

that does not admit a lift, meaning that

, and hence

.

Then, since

While it is already known that

Recall that
\mathrm{Sub}(C_{p^{n}})

Definition 5.1

by restriction. Since edges of this form are covering relations in
[n]
, we know these edges cannot be obtained by closing under transitivity. Thus the only edges that could generate the edge
i\to(i+1)
are precisely edges of the form
i\to j
where
j\geq i+1
. From this it is then clear that in order to generate
\mathcal{R}
, a generating set must have a size of at least
\lvert S\rvert
. Note that a vertex
i
is in
S
if and only if it is the source of an edge in
M(\mathcal{R})
. Thus
\lvert M(\mathcal{R})\rvert=\lvert S\rvert
, and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\pi
be a noncrossing partition of
[n]
. Then the transfer system
\langle J(\pi)\rangle
consists of the closure under restriction of
J(\pi)
.

Proof.

As noted in
[
Rub20
, Construction A.1]
,
\langle J(\pi)\rangle
is constructed by first closing under restriction and then under transitivity. Thus, it is enough to prove that the closure under restriction of
J(\pi)
is already closed under transitivity. Suppose
i\to j
and
j\to k
are obtained by restriction from
J(\pi)
. This means that there exist
j^{\prime}\geq j
and
k^{\prime}\geq k
such that
i
and
j^{\prime}
are in the same class and
j^{\prime}
is the largest element therein, and similarly,
j
and
k^{\prime}
are in the same class and
k^{\prime}
is the largest element therein. Since
\pi

k^{\prime}\leq j^{\prime}
. Then
i\to k
is the restriction of
i\to k^{\prime}
, which is in
J(\pi)
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{R}\in\operatorname{Tr}([n])
. Let
\psi(\mathcal{R})
be the partition of
[n]
associated to the equivalence relation generated by
M(\mathcal{R})
. Then

(1)

\psi(\mathcal{R})
is a noncrossing partition, and

(2)

the map
\psi\colon\operatorname{Tr}([n])\to\operatorname{NC}_{n+1}
is a bijection with inverse
\chi(\pi)=\langle J(\pi)\rangle
.

Proof.

We denote by
\sim_{\mathcal{R}}
the equivalence relation generated by
M(\mathcal{R})
,
i.e.
,
\sim_{\mathcal{R}}
is the intersection of all equivalence relations containing
M(\mathcal{R})
where directed edges in
M(\mathcal{R})
are interpreted as relations. Using the transitivity of a transfer system, one can check that for
i<j\in[n]
,
i\sim_{\mathcal{R}}j
if and only if either
i\to j\in M(\mathcal{R})
or there exists
k>j
such that
i\to k\in M(\mathcal{R})
and
j\to k\in M(\mathcal{R})
. Note that in either case, there exists
k\geq j
such that
i\to k\in M(\mathcal{R})
and
j\to k\in\mathcal{R}
.

To prove that
\psi(\mathcal{R})
is a noncrossing partition, let
a<b<c<d
in
[n]
such that
a\sim_{\mathcal{R}}c
and
b\sim_{\mathcal{R}}d
. Thus, there exist
e\geq c
and
f\geq d
such that
a\to e
and
b\to f
are in
M(\mathcal{R})
, and
c\to e
and
d\to f
are in
\mathcal{R}
. By restriction, we get that
a\to b
and
b\to c
are in
\mathcal{R}
, and thus
a\to f
and
b\to e
are also in
\mathcal{R}
by transitivity. Since
a\to e
and
b\to f
are maximal, that implies that
e=f
, and hence
a
,
b
,
c
, and
d
are all in the same equivalence class, as required.

We now prove that
\psi
is a bijection. Note that
Lemma

5.6
implies that for a noncrossing partition
\pi
,

J(\pi)=M(\chi(\pi)).

Since the partition generated by
J(\pi)
is precisely
\pi
, it follows that
\psi(\chi(\pi))=\pi
.

Similarly, the description above of the equivalence relation generated by
M(\mathcal{R})
implies that

M(\mathcal{R})=J(\psi(\mathcal{R})).

That combined with
Lemma

5.4
proves that
\chi(\psi(\mathcal{R}))=\mathcal{R}
, thus showing that
\psi
and
\chi
are indeed inverses of each other.

∎

Remark 5.8
.

The above theorem recovers
[
BBR
, Theorem 1]
via a direct bijection with noncrossing partitions. The original proof in
[
BBR
]
proceeds by establishing the Catalan recurrence relation among the numbers
|\operatorname{Tr}([n])|
.

With
\psi
established as a bijection, we can now use
\psi
to directly enumerate transfer systems minimally generated by edge sets of a certain size. Note that refinement of partitions gives an order relation on the collection of noncrossing partitions.

Proposition 5.9

(
[
Kre72
]
, see also
[
Sim00
]
)
.

The poset of
\operatorname{NC}_{n}
under refinement forms a lattice called the
Kreweras lattice
. It is graded by the function

\operatorname{rank}(\pi):=n-\operatorname{bk}(\pi),

where
\operatorname{bk}(\pi)
is the number of blocks of
\pi
. ∎

Remark 5.10
.

Beware that the Tamari lattice is a strict extension of the Kreweras lattice.

Definition 5.11
.

For
n\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n
, the
(n,k)
-th
Narayana number
is

\mathrm{Nar}(n,k):=\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}.

Proposition 5.12

(
[
Kre72
]
, see also
[
Sim00
]
)
.

Let
\operatorname{NC}_{n}(k)
denote the number of partitions in
\operatorname{NC}_{n}
with rank
k
. Then
\operatorname{NC}_{n}(k)
is given by the
(n,k)
-th Narayana number,
i.e.
,

\operatorname{NC}_{n}(k)=\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}.

∎

With this rank property established in the context of noncrossing partitions, we can then use
\chi
to see what this rank looks like for transfer systems.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\pi\in\operatorname{NC}_{n+1}
. Then the rank of
\pi
in the Kreweras lattice is equal to the minimal generating number for
\chi(\pi)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.4
, the minimal generating number for
\chi(\pi)
is equal to the cardinality of
M(\chi(\pi))
. As noted in the proof of
5.7
,
M(\chi(\pi))=J(\pi)
. Note that
J(\pi)
consists precisely of the elements of
[n]
that are not maximal within their block in
\pi
, thus, the cardinality of
J(\pi)
is
n+1-\operatorname{bk}(\pi)
, as desired.
∎

Let
\operatorname{Tr}_{k}([n])
denote the set of transfer systems on
[n]
minimally generated by
k
edges.

Corollary 5.14
.

For all
1\leq k\leq n+1
,

\lvert\operatorname{Tr}_{k}([n])\rvert=\operatorname{NC}_{n+1}(k)=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}\binom{n+1}{k-1}.

∎

Proof.

The bijection
\chi
takes rank
k
noncrossing partitions of
[n]
to transfer systems on
[n]
minimally generated by
k
edges. Since the former are counted by the indicated Narayana numbers, so are the latter.
∎
