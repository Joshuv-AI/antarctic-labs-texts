Elastoplasticity of gradient-polyconvex materials
Martin Kružík, Jiří Zeman

Abstract
. We propose a model for rate-independent evolution in elastoplastic materials under external loading, which allows large strains. In the setting of strain-gradient plasticity with multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient, we prove the existence of the so-called energetic solution. The stored energy density function is assumed to depend on gradients of minors of the deformation gradient which makes our results applicable to shape-memory materials, for instance.



1.
Introduction and notation

Elastoplasticity at large strains is an area of ongoing research, bringing together contributions from modelling, analysis and numerical simulations. For the mathematical analysis of elastoplastic models, it is often convenient to use powerful tools from the calculus of variations, which are now able to treat quasistatic evolutionary problems as well (see e.g.
[
49
]
,
[
41
]
as pioneering works). In principle, the existence of solutions could be ensured by assuming
some generalized convexity of the strain energy, but general material behavior may contradict this assumption. For example, this is manifested in shape-memory alloys (SMA)
[
4
]
, magnetostrictive
[
14
]
and ferroelectric materials
[
54
]
, even if convexity is understood in a generalized sense, such as
polyconvexity
(first defined in
[
1
]
).

As a remedy, one can then recourse to higher-gradient regularizations, where the stored energy density
W
also depends e.g. on the second gradient of the deformation. From a mathematical point of view, this adds compactness to the model, which is instrumental in proving the existence of solutions by the direct method.
[
2
]
Materials with such constitutive equations are referred to as non-simple and were introduced by Toupin
[
57
,
58
]
. Since then, the concept has been elaborated by many authors so that its thermodynamical side is also better understood
[
25
,
7
,
50
,
13
,
18
,
45
,
19
]
.

Gradient-polyconvex
(GPC) materials form a special class of non-simple solids and appeared in
[
5
]
for the first time. Their stored energy density is not a function of the full second gradient
\nabla^{2}y
of the deformation
y\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which maps the reference configuration
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but it only depends, in a convex way, on the weak gradients of
{\rm cof}\nabla y
(and that of
{\rm det}\nabla y
, if desirable). (See Section
3
for the definition of a cofactor matrix.) To interpret the condition physically, note that since
{\rm det}\nabla y
measures the local change in volume between the reference and current
configuration of the material and
{\rm cof}\nabla y
describes the transformation of area
[
27
, p. 78]
, the stored energy
W
depending on their gradients offers a control of how abruptly these
changes vary in space.
Gradient polyconvexity has since been applied to the evolution of SMA
[
35
]
and a numerical implementation of GPC material models is also available
[
32
]
. GPC allows us to consider stored energy densities without assuming any notion of convexity in the deformation gradient variable. This makes it suitable for modelling of shape-memory alloys, for instance, because the resulting functional is lower semicontinuous in the underlying weak topology. An alternative approach to energy functionals that are not lower semicontinuous is relaxation
[
10
]
, however, available results prevent us from considering energies tending to infinity for extreme compression. In this article, we study an elastoplastic model using gradient polyconvexity.

The idea of strain-gradient plasticity is similar to that of nonsimple materials, as it also uses higher-order terms to prevent physical quantities from unrealistic fine-scale oscillations. Incorporating gradients of plastic variables in the constitutive equations is common in the engineering literature
[
44
, p. 250]
and we refer an interested reader to
[
15
,
26
,
28
,
36
,
3
,
48
,
59
]
or
[
16
]
and the references therein. Gradient terms account for non-local interactions of dislocations and we include them as well, as they offer a suitable regularization to our model.

For our problem, we formulate the so-called
energetic solution
(let us name
[
46
]
as an early reference; other related sources are cited in
[
44
]
). One advantage of the energetic formulation is that it avoids derivatives of constitutive equations and time derivatives of the solution itself.
[
41
]
The variational nature of this solution concept also combines well with homogenization and relaxation. Two conditions lie at the core of the energetic formulation: a
stability inequality
, which couples minimization of the elastic energy with a principle of maximum dissipation, and an
energy balance
. The two requirements together imply that a usual
plastic flow rule
is satisfied for sufficiently smooth solutions.

The plan of the paper is as follows: in Section 2 we review some basic facts from the modelling of
materials with internal variables
, loosely following
[
21
]
and
[
30
]
. For the sake of completeness, we also motivate the definition of an energetic solution, although this has been done more thoroughly in previous works of Mielke
et al.
The main part of this paper is Section 3, where we study the rate-independent behavior of elastoplastic GPC materials under external loads and prove the existence result. The section is concluded with an example from crystal plasticity, which illustrates the usability of our findings.

Our approach draws inspiration from
[
39
]
, where the existence of energetic solutions in large-strain elastoplasticity is proved in the presence of plastic strain gradients and a polyconvex
W
. However, as our stored energy is gradient polyconvex, our findings apply to the setting of multiwell energies, as encountered e.g. in shape-memory alloys, cf.
[
6
]
,
[
43
]
or
[
37
]
for instance. We remark that energetic formulations in elastoplasticity have also been propounded: in the linear framework
[
22
]
, without strain gradients
[
8
]
, using a finite quasiconvex energy
[
33
]
, involving a plastic Cauchy-Green tensor
[
23
,
24
]
, for numerical computations
[
45
]
, and elsewhere. The paper
[

]
discusses different assumptions in quasistatic large-strain elastoplastic evolutions. Lastly, in
[
34
]
, rate-independent dislocation-free plasticity is treated.

In what follows,

,

\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose modulus is integrable with the power

\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

Further,

and their gradients belong to

. Finally,

W^{1,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

.
The weak convergence in

(weakly star for

for all

where
\beta^{\prime}=\beta/(\beta-1)
if

,
\beta^{\prime}=1

if

and
\beta^{\prime}=+\infty
for

. Weak convergence of mappings and their gradients in

. We also write

or

to denote weak convergence. Finally,

.

2.
Motivation: modelling inelastic processes with internal variables

Consider
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a bounded Lipschitz domain representing the so-called reference configuration of a solid body, and a mapping
y\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the deformation which the body is subjected to. We explain the idea of elastoplasticity and the concept of energetic solutions following freely the exposition in
[
21
]
and restricting ourselves to simple materials.

According to Han and Reddy
[
30
, p. 34]
, plastic deformation ‘is most conveniently described in the framework of materials with internal variables’. Those material models are not only governed by external (controllable) variables, such as temperature or strain, but also incorporate a vector
z\in Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
of internal variables, which describe e.g. an ongoing chemical reaction, elastoplastic
behavior or material damage. (Details can be found in
[
29
]
or in more recent works on the subject listed in
[
30
, p. 39]
.) The
hyperelastic
stored energy density
\mathcal{W}
then has the form
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}(F,z)
, if we consider a
simple material
, i.e. a material with constitutive equations involving only the first gradient
F=\nabla y
of the deformation
y
.

The thermodynamic conception is usually that differentiating
\mathcal{W}
with respect to
F
, we get the mechanical stress, whereas the derivative
-\partial_{z}\mathcal{W}
gives another stress-like variable – the so-called thermodynamic force

\displaystyle Q:=-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{W}(F,z)

(2.1)

associated with the internal variable
z
. We can imagine that
Q
tries to restructure the material irreversibly, which would lead to changing the value of
z
. The development of convex analysis
[
47
]
allowed quite a general formulation of an evolution rule for the internal variable
z
. Assuming the existence of a nonnegative convex potential of dissipative forces
\delta=\delta(\dot{z})
, where
\dot{z}
denotes the time derivative of
z
, we write the
flow law
as

\displaystyle Q(t)\in{\partial^{\rm sub}}\delta(\dot{z}(t))

(2.2)

everywhere in
\Omega
. A common simplifying assumption is
rate-independent
behavior. It is suitable for some particular materials or for the modelling of processes with low rates of external loading. In simple terms, rate-independence means that rescaling the loading in time only results in a corresponding time-rescaling of the deformation and no additional viscous, inertial or thermal effects arise. Rate-independence translates into positive one-homogeneity of
\delta
, i.e.
\delta(\alpha\dot{z})=\alpha\delta(\dot{z})
for all
\alpha>0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Since the subdifferential is
monotone
(see
[
51
]
), by (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle(Q(t)-\theta)\cdot(\dot{z}(t)-\xi)\geq 0.

(2.3)

for all
\theta\in{\partial^{\rm sub}}\delta(\xi)
. Choosing
\xi=0
and observing that the one-homogeneity of
\delta
implies
\delta(\dot{z})=\omega\cdot\dot{z}
for all
\omega\in{\partial^{\rm sub}}\delta(\dot{z})
we get

\displaystyle\delta(\dot{z}(t))=Q(t)\cdot\dot{z}(t)\geq\theta\cdot\dot{z}(t)

(2.4)

for all
\theta\in{\partial^{\rm sub}}\delta(0)
.
Hence we derived the so-called
maximum dissipation principle
(see e.g.
[
31
]
or
[
52
,
53
]
) which states that the plastic dissipation that takes place in reality is not less than any possible dissipation due to thermodynamic forces “available” in the so-called
elastic domain

{\partial^{\rm sub}}\delta(0)
.

Hereafter,
\nu
is the outer unit normal to
\partial\Omega
, and
\partial\Omega\supset\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}
which are disjoint. Let
f(t)\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the (volume) density of external
body
forces
and
g(t)\colon\Gamma_{1}\subset\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the (surface) density of surface forces. The conservation of momentum yields the
equilibrium equations

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\mathcal{W}(\nabla y(t),z(t))\right)=f(t)\mbox{ in $\Omega$},

(2.5)

\displaystyle y(t,x)=y_{0}(x)\mbox{ on $\Gamma_{0}$},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\mathcal{W}(\nabla y(t),z(t))\nu(x)=g(t,x)\mbox{ on $\Gamma_{1}$}.

(2.7)

If
z_{0}\in Z
is an initial condition for the internal variable, the system of equations (
2.5
)-(
2.7
) together with (
2.8
):

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{W}(\nabla y(t),z(t))\in\partial\delta(\dot{z}(t)),\ z(0)=z_{0},

(2.8)

governs the mechanical behavior characterized by the unknowns
y(t)
,
z(t)
.

Unfortunately, the system (
2.5
)-(
2.7
) is ill-posed in many situations. See the works of Suquet
[
55
]
and Temam
[
56
]
for early analysis of this problem.

To get existence results, it seems necessary to include the gradient of the internal variable, i.e. use the strain energy density

\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\nabla y,z,\nabla z):=\mathcal{W}(\nabla y,z)+\epsilon|\nabla z|^{\alpha}

for
\alpha\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let us briefly recall how an energy balance, which appears in the
energetic
formulation of this regularized problem, is obtained, under sufficient smoothness and integrability assumptions on all the present mappings. For details, see
[
21
,
41
]
.

The functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(t,y(t),z(t)):=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{W}(\nabla y(t),z(t))\,\mathrm{d}x+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla z(t)|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x-L(t,y(t)),

(2.9)

expresses the potential energy in our system, where the work done by external forces is

\displaystyle L(t,y(t)):=\int_{\Omega}f(t)\cdot y(t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{1}}g(t)\cdot y(t)\,\mathrm{d}S.\

(2.10)

We also introduce the total dissipation along
z
,

{\rm Diss}(z;[0,t]):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\delta(\dot{z}(s))\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s.

Calculating the thermodynamic force
\partial_{z}\tilde{\mathcal{W}}
, using the relation
\delta(\dot{z})=\omega\cdot\dot{z}
,
\omega\in{\partial^{\rm sub}}\delta(\dot{z})
, mentioned above (
2.4
), we can deduce from (
2.5
)-(
2.7
) the following energy balance:

\mathcal{I}(t,y(t),z(t))+{\rm Diss}(z;[0,t])=\mathcal{I}(0,y(0),z(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{L}(s,y(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

Due to low regularity in time, a more general expression for
{\rm Diss}(z;[0,t])
must be used in practice, though – see (
2.14
) below.

To this end, we define a dissipation distance between two values
z_{0},z_{1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
of the internal variable:

\displaystyle D(x,z_{0},z_{1}):=\inf_{z}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\delta(x,z(s),\dot{z}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s;\ z(0)=z_{0},\ z(1)=z_{1}\right\},

(2.11)

where
z\in C^{1}([0,1];{\mathbb{Z}})
, and set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{\Omega}D(x,z_{1}(x),z_{2}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.12)

for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}:=\{z\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m};\ z(x)\in Z\mbox{ a.e. in $\Omega$}\}
, as in
[
41
]
.

In order to find a quasistatic evolution of the system Mielke, Theil, and Levitas
[
46
]
came up with the following definition of the energetic solution which conveniently overcomes nonsmoothness mentioned above. Moreover, it fully exploits the possible variational structure of the problem and allows for very general
energy and dissipation functionals. This concept has versatile applications to many problems in continuum mechanics of solids. Additionally, working with
{\mathcal{I}}
and
\mathcal{D}
directly allows us to include also higher derivatives of
y
into the model or to require integrability of some functions of
\nabla y
as also done in this contribution.

2.1.
Energetic solution

Suppose that the evolution of
y(t)\in{\mathbb{Y}}
and
z(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is studied during a time interval
[0,T]
.
The following two properties characterize the energetic solution due to Mielke
et al.

[
46
]
.

(i) Stability inequality:

\forall t\in[0,T],\,\tilde{z}\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,\tilde{y}\in{\mathbb{Y}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(t,y(t),z(t))\leq\mathcal{I}(t,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})+\mathcal{D}(z(t),\tilde{z})

(2.13)

(ii) Energy balance:

\forall\ 0\leq t\leq T

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(t,y(t),z(t))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},z;[0,t])=\mathcal{I}(0,y(0),z(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{L}(\xi,y(\xi))\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(2.14)

\text{where }{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},z;[s,t]):=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}(z(t_{i}),z(t_{i-1}));\ \{t_{i}\}\mbox{ partition of }[s,t]\right\}.

Definition 2.2
.

The mapping
t\mapsto(y(t),z(t))\in{\mathbb{Y}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
is an energetic solution to the problem

(\mathcal{I},\delta,L)
if the stability inequality and energy balance
are satisfied for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Remark 2.3
.

The mechanical idea behind stability inequality (i) is the following: imagine first that
\tilde{z}:=z(t)
,
then
\mathcal{D}(z(t),\tilde{z})
vanishes, since no change in the internal variables implies no dissipation.
Consequently, (i) simplifies to
\mathcal{I}(t,y(t),z(t))\leq\mathcal{I}(t,\tilde{y},\tilde{z})
for all
\tilde{y}\in{\mathbb{Y}}
and
y(t)
is a
global minimizer of
\mathcal{I}(t,\cdot,z(t))
over
{\mathbb{Y}}
. So we see that in this case, (i) has the meaning of an elastic equilibrium. If
\tilde{z}\neq z(t)
, then the amount of dissipated
energy between the states
\tilde{z}
and
z(t)
must, by (i), at least compensate for, if not outweigh the associated loss in the total energy, which is a version of the principle of maximum dissipation.
[
41
]

We will write
\mathbb{Q}:=\mathbb{Y}\times\mathbb{Z}
and set
q:=(y,z)
.
Next let us define the set of
stable states
at time
t
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(t):=\{q\in\mathbb{Q};\ \forall\tilde{q}\in\mathbb{Q}:\ \mathcal{I}(t,q)\leq\mathcal{I}(t,\tilde{q})+\mathcal{D}(q,\tilde{q})\}

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{[0,T]}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times\mathcal{S}(t).

(2.16)

Moreover, a sequence
\{(t_{k},q_{k})\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is called
stable
if
q_{k}\in\mathcal{S}(t_{k})
.

3.
Applications to elastoplasticity

This section shows how the energetic approach can be applied to an elastoplastic problem of gradient-polyconvex materials.

3.1.
Gradient polyconvexity

Gradient polyconvexity was first defined in
[
5
]
in analogy with classical polyconvexity
[
1
]
. The difference is that here we assume that the stored energy density is convex in gradients of minors of the deformation gradient but not in minors alone. More precisely, the following definition is taken from
[
5
]
. We recall that for an invertible
F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
we define the cofactor of
F
,
{\rm cof}F=({\rm det}F)F^{-\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. As is shown in
[
5
]
there are maps
y\in W^{1,1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
{\rm det}\nabla y
and
{\rm cof}\nabla y
are Lipschitz continuous but
y\not\in W^{2,1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The same conclusion can be reached for every
n\geq 3
. On the other hand, if
n=2
then
\nabla{\rm cof}\nabla y
has the same entries (up to the minus sign) as
\nabla^{2}y
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open domain. Let
\mathcal{W}_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lower semicontinuous function. The functional

\displaystyle J(y)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\nabla y(x),\nabla[{\rm cof}\nabla y(x)])\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3.1)

defined for any measurable function
y\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which the weak derivatives
\nabla y
,
\nabla[{\rm cof}\nabla y]
exist and are integrable is called
gradient polyconvex
if the function
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,\cdot)
is convex for every
F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

We assume that for some
c>0
, and numbers
\alpha>n-1
,
s>0
it holds that for every
F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and every
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,H)\geq\begin{cases}c\big(|F|^{\alpha}+({\rm det}F)^{-s}+|H|^{\alpha/(n-1)}\big)&\text{ if }{\rm det}F>0,\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(3.2)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm. These growth assumptions can be surely weakened but we stick to them in order to simplify our presentation.

The idea behind condition (
3.2
) is that the energy blows up if the deformation does not preserve orientation or if the measures of strain on the right-hand side grow to extreme values. Coercivity conditions involving
|F|
and
{\rm det}F
are commonly used in nonlinear elasticity (see e.g.
[
9
]
) and reflect variations in volume or changes of the distances of points caused by the deformation. The term
H
which is a placeholder for
\nabla{\rm cof}\nabla y
penalizes spatial changes of
{\rm cof}\nabla y
and, consequently, it aims at suppressing abrupt areal changes in the deformed configuration.

An important feature of gradient polyconvexity is that
no
convexity assumptions on
\mathcal{W}_{1}
are needed in the
F
-variable, so that very general material laws can be considered including multiwell energy functions
[
6
]
or the St. Venant–Kirchhoff energy density
[
9
]
. We call materials whose stored
J
energy obeys (
3.1
) also gradient-polyconvex.

3.2.
Assumptions on problem data

As in
[
26
]
we will consider so-called
separable materials
, i.e. materials where the elastoplastic energy density
has the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(F_{\rm e},H,F_{p},\nabla F_{\rm p},p,\nabla p):=\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{\rm e},H)+\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{p},\nabla F_{\rm p},p,\nabla p).

(3.3)

We will assume that
\mathcal{W}_{1}
is continuous and satisfies (
3.2
) while for
\mathcal{W}_{2}
we require:

(i) The plastic part
\mathcal{W}_{2}
is continuous in its all arguments.

(ii) Suppose that there are two constants
C,c>0
so that the following assumption holds for constants
c_{1}>0
,

\beta>n
, and
\omega>n
:

\displaystyle C(1+|F_{\rm p}|^{\beta}+|G|^{\beta}+|p|^{\omega}+|\pi|^{\omega})

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{\rm p},G,p,\pi)

\displaystyle\geq c(|F_{\rm p}|^{\beta}+|G|^{\beta}+|p|^{\omega}+|\pi|^{\omega})-c_{1}.

(3.4)

(iii) There is
c_{2}>0
,
v^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a modulus of continuity
\hat{\omega}
such that for all
\hat{\alpha}>0
,
F_{\rm p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{\rm p},G,p+\hat{\alpha}v^{*},\pi)-\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{\rm p},G,p,\pi)|\leq\hat{\omega}(\hat{\alpha})(\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{\rm p},G,p,\pi)+c_{2}).

(3.5)

Furthermore, let us suppose that for every
F_{\rm e}
,
F_{\rm p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the functions
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{\rm e},\cdot)
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}(F_{\rm p},\cdot,p,\cdot)
are convex.

The dissipation distance
\mathcal{D}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\to[0,+\infty]
takes the form (
2.12
) for a function
D\colon\Omega\times({\rm SL}(n)\times\mathbb{R}^{m})^{2}
and we only change the definition of
{\mathbb{Z}}
to (
3.11
) below. We make the following assumptions on
\mathcal{D}
:

(i) Lower semicontinuity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z,\tilde{z})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{D}(z_{k},\tilde{z}_{k}),

(3.6)

whenever
z_{k}{\rightharpoonup}z
and
\tilde{z}_{k}{\rightharpoonup}\tilde{z}
.

(ii) Positivity:

\displaystyle\text{If }\{z_{k}\}\subset Z\text{ is bounded and }\min\{\mathcal{D}(z_{k},z),\mathcal{D}(z,z_{k})\}\to 0\text{ then }z_{k}{\rightharpoonup}z.

(3.7)

(iii) For all

,
z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
:
\mathcal{D}(z_{1},z_{2})=0
if and only if
z_{1}=z_{2}
.

(iv) Triangle inequality:
\mathcal{D}(z_{1},z_{3})\leq\mathcal{D}(z_{1},z_{2})+\mathcal{D}(z_{2},z_{3})
for all
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
,
z_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We refer the reader to
[

]
to see that (ii) follows from (i) and (iii). After stating Proposition

((

In order to prove the existence of a solution to (

(3.9)

where

3.3.
Formulation of the problem

From now on,
y\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
will represent the deformation of a material body, whose reference configuration is a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
y
models both
elastic
and
plastic
behaviour, we split the deformation gradient
F=\nabla y
as
F=F_{\rm e}F_{\rm p}
, where
F_{\rm e}
stands for an elastic part and
F_{\rm p}\in{\rm SL}(n):=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n};\ {\rm det}\ A=1\}
is a plastic part, which irreversibly transforms the material. To capture e.g.
back stresses
, we use the vector
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of hardening internal variables. Written together,
z(x)=(F_{\rm p}(x),p(x))
is a plastic variable, lying in
{\rm SL}(n)\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
for almost all
x\in\Omega
.

The energy functional
{\mathcal{I}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,y(t),z(t)):=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{W}(\nabla yF_{\rm p}^{-1},\nabla[({\rm cof}\nabla y)F_{\rm p}^{\top}],F_{\rm p},\nabla F_{\rm p},p,\nabla p)\,\mathrm{d}x-L(t,y(t)),

(3.10)

with
L
defined in (
2.10
).

Our stored energy density
\mathcal{W}
does not explicitly depend on the spatial variable
x
, but treating the inhomogeneous case would not need many modifications.

Let us remark that
({\rm cof}\nabla y)F_{\rm p}^{\top}
is the cofactor of the elastic part
F_{\rm e}
, since by the product rule for cofactor matrices
[
9
, p. 4]
and by
F_{\rm p}\in{\rm SL}(n)
, we have

{\rm cof}F_{\rm e}={\rm cof}(FF_{\rm p}^{-1})=({\rm cof}F){\rm cof}(F_{\rm p}^{-1})=({\rm cof}F){\rm det}(F_{\rm p}^{-1})(F_{\rm p}^{-1})^{-\top}=({\rm cof}F)F_{\rm p}^{\top}.

The admissible deformations
y
lie in

\mathbb{Y}:=\{y\in W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n});\ y=y_{0}\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{0}\},

where
\Gamma_{0}\subset\partial\Omega
with a positive surface measure and
y_{0}\in W^{1-1/d,d}(\Gamma_{0};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is given. Assuming that
\Gamma_{1}\subset\partial\Omega
as in Section 2, we suppose
\Gamma_{0}\cap\Gamma_{1}=\mathchar 31\relax
.
For the internal states
z
let us define the set

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}:=\{(F_{\rm p},p)\in W^{1,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times W^{1,\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}):\ F_{\rm p}(x)\in{\rm SL}(n)\mbox{ for a.e.~$x\in\Omega$}\}.

(3.11)

For ease of notation, we write
q=(y,z)\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\in\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{Y}\times\mathbb{Z}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
and understand
\mathcal{I}(t,\cdot)
,
L(t,\cdot)
and
\mathcal{D}
as functions of
q
, that is:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{W}(\nabla yF_{\rm p}^{-1},\nabla[({\rm cof}\nabla y)F_{\rm p}^{\top}],F_{\rm p},\nabla F_{\rm p},p,\nabla p)\,\mathrm{d}x-L(t,q(t)),

\displaystyle L(t,q(t):=L(t,y(t)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(q_{1},q_{2}):=\mathcal{D}(z_{1},z_{2})

if
q_{1}=(y_{1},z_{1})
and
q_{2}=(y_{2},z_{2})
.

In order to prove the existence of an energetic solution to our problem we will need the following results of technical nature.

3.4.
Auxiliary results

We start this section by the following reverse Young inequality.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
a>0
,
b>0
,
\delta>0
,
r>1
. Then

\frac{a}{b}\geq r\delta^{\frac{r}{r-1}}a^{\frac{1}{r}}-(r-1)\delta^{\frac{r^{2}}{(r-1)^{2}}}b^{\frac{1}{r-1}}.

Proof.

Young’s inequality states that given a pair of positive numbers
\alpha,\beta
and
1<p,q<+\infty
,
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, then

\alpha\beta\leq\frac{\alpha^{p}}{p}+\frac{\beta^{q}}{q}.

(3.12)

Set
p=r
,
\alpha=a^{\frac{1}{r}}
,
\beta=\delta^{\frac{r}{r-1}}b
in (
3.12
). Then
q=\frac{r}{r-1}
and Young’s inequality yields

\frac{r-1}{r}\delta^{\frac{r^{2}}{(r-1)^{2}}}b^{\frac{r}{r-1}}+\frac{1}{r}a\geq\delta^{\frac{r}{r-1}}a^{\frac{1}{r}}b,

which after multiplying by
\frac{r}{b}
implies the desired result.
∎

It will also be useful to give a name to a kind of convergence which makes
{\mathcal{I}}(t,\cdot)
lower semicontinuous.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that the sequence
\{q_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathbb{Q}
,
q_{k}=(y_{k},z_{k})=(y_{k},F_{{\rm p}k},p_{k})

gpc-converges
to
q_{*}=(y_{*},z_{*})=(y_{*},F_{{\rm p}*},p_{*})
if
z_{k}{\rightharpoonup}z_{*}
in
\mathbb{Z}
,
\nabla{\rm cof}(\nabla y_{k}F_{{\rm p}k}^{-1}){\rightharpoonup}\nabla{\rm cof}(\nabla y_{*}F_{{\rm p}*}^{-1})
in
L^{\alpha/(n-1)}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n})
and
\nabla y_{k}\to\nabla y_{*}
in measure. We write
q_{k}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny gpc}}}{\to}}q_{*}
for short.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
t_{k}\to t_{*}
with
t_{k}
,
t_{*}\in[0,T]
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
q_{k}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny gpc}}}{\to}}q_{*}
,
\{q_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathbb{Q}
. Then
{\mathcal{I}}(t_{*},q_{*})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}{\mathcal{I}}(t_{k},q_{k})
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
[
17
, Corollary 7.9]
. Note that we can construct a subsequence such that
\nabla y_{k_{j}}F_{{\rm p}k_{j}}^{-1}\to\nabla y_{*}F_{{\rm p}*}^{-1}
almost everywhere.∎

Even though we do not have the weak lower semicontinuity of
{\mathcal{I}}
in general, we can get it for a subsequence provided the respective values of
{\mathcal{I}}
are bounded.

Lemma 3.5
.

Provided that
\alpha^{-1}+\beta^{-1}\leq d^{-1}<(n-1)^{-1}
and
d>\frac{\beta(n-1)}{\beta-1}
, let
t_{k}\in[0,T]
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
q_{k}{\rightharpoonup}q_{*}
in
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose there is
C_{I}>0
such that for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
the bound

holds true. Then there exists a subsequence
\{q_{k_{j}}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
\{q_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that gpc-converges to the same limit
q_{*}
.

Proof.

The proof goes the same way as in Proposition
3.8
. To keep the flow of ideas uninterrupted there, we postpone the presentation to that section.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be lower semicontinuous with respect to gpc-convergence and let (

) and (

Let it for all

Then for any stable sequence

The assumptions (

The reasoning follows the lines of

by the compact embedding. Then

3.5.
Incremental problems

Next, we define the following sequence of incremental problems. We consider a
stable
initial condition
q^{0}_{\tau}:=q^{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Let us take
\tau>0
, a time step, chosen in the way that
N=T/\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
1\leq k\leq N
,
t_{k}:=k\tau
, find
q_{\tau}^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

q^{k}_{\tau}
solves

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize }&{\mathcal{I}}(t_{k},q)+\mathcal{D}(q_{\tau}^{k-1},q)\\
\mbox{subject to }&q^{k}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{Q}.\end{array}\right\}\

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}+\beta^{-1}\leq d^{-1}<\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(n-1)^{-1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d>\frac{\beta(n-1)}{\beta-1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
. Let the assumptions on
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{D}
be satisfied.
Let further (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) be satisfied. Then the problem (
3.5
) has a solution for all
k=1,\ldots,T/\tau
. In addition, for the solution
q_{\tau}^{k}=(y_{\tau}^{k},z_{\tau}^{k})
we get
{\rm det}\nabla y_{\tau}^{k}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Given
q_{\tau}^{k-1}\in\mathbb{Q}
from the previous time step, suppose that
\{q_{j}\}\subset\mathbb{Q}
is a minimizing sequence for
q\mapsto{\mathcal{I}}(t_{k},q)+\mathcal{D}(q_{\tau}^{k-1},q)
.
The assumption (
3.2
) implies that
\{z_{j}\}
is uniformly bounded in

W^{1,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times W^{1,\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Hence, as
\beta,\omega>1
we can extract a weakly converging subsequence (not relabeled)
z_{j}{\rightharpoonup}z
in
W^{1,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times W^{1,\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. The strong convergence of
z_{j}\to z:=(F_{\rm p},p)
in
L^{\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times L^{\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
ensures that
F_{\rm p}(x)\in{\rm SL}(n)
almost everywhere. Write
z_{j}=(F_{\rm p}^{j},p^{j})
,
q_{j}=(y^{j},z_{j})
. Exploiting the submultiplicativity of the Euclidean norm, we estimate using Lemma
3.2

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla y^{j}(x)(F_{\rm p}^{j})^{-1}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\nabla y^{j}\|_{L^{d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}^{\alpha}}{\|F^{j}_{\rm p}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\geq\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\alpha}{d}\delta^{\alpha/(\alpha-d)}\|\nabla y^{j}\|_{L^{d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}^{d}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-\frac{\alpha-d}{d}\delta^{\alpha^{2}/(\alpha-d)^{2}}\|F^{j}_{\rm p}\|_{L^{\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}^{\beta}.

(3.21)

The
L^{d}
-term on the right hand side is bounded due to (
3.2
) and the boundedness of
\{y^{j}\}
in
W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
follows by the Poincaré inequality if
\delta>0
is taken small. Hence
y^{j}{\rightharpoonup}y
in
W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(up to a subsequence). This then also implies that
{\rm cof}\nabla y^{j}{\rightharpoonup}{\rm cof}\nabla y
in
L^{d/(n-1)}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
; cf.
[
9
]
.
Due to reflexivity of
W^{1,\alpha/(n-1)}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
we get for a non-relabelled subsequence that

({\rm cof}\nabla y^{j})F^{j\top}_{\rm p}{\rightharpoonup}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\,\Xi\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
in
W^{1,\alpha/(n-1)}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
for some
\Xi
. However, the weak convergence of
\{{\rm cof}\nabla y^{j}\}_{j}
and the strong convergence of

\{F_{\rm p}^{j}\}
allow us to identify
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Xi\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=({\rm cof}\nabla y)F^{\top}_{\rm p}
. Moreover, the growth condition (
3.2
) implies that
{\rm det}\nabla y>0
a.e. in
\Omega
(see
[
5
]
).
Cramer’s rule together with
{\rm det}F_{\rm p}^{j}=1
and
{\rm det}^{n-1}(\nabla y^{j}(F^{j}_{\rm p})^{-1})={\rm det}({\rm cof}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[\nabla y^{j}(F^{j}_{\rm p})^{-1}]\color[rgb]{0,0,0})={\rm det}({\rm cof}\nabla y^{j})
give

\frac{{\rm cof}[\nabla y^{j}(F_{\rm p}^{j})^{-1}]}{{\rm det}\nabla y^{j}}=(\nabla y^{j}(F_{\rm p}^{j})^{-1})^{-\top}

and the transpose of the left-hand side converges pointwise (for a subsequence again) to
(\nabla y(F_{\rm p})^{-1})^{-1}
a.e. in
\Omega
. This, together with the fact that
{\rm det}\nabla y>0
a.e., implies
that
\nabla y^{j}(F^{j}_{\rm p})^{-1}\to\nabla y(F_{\rm p})^{-1}
a.e. in
\Omega
. Then by
[
17
, Corollary 7.9]
we see that
\mathcal{I}
is weakly lower semicontinuous.

The assumptions on
\mathcal{D}
ensure that it is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathbb{Q}
, too. By the direct method of the calculus of variations, we conclude that a minimizer
q_{\tau}^{k}
exists.

\Box

Remark 3.9
.

Note that in fact, we proved that a minimizing sequence has a gpc-convergent subsequence.

Proof.

To show the stability estimate (

q_{\tau}^{k}
and a triangle inequality for
\mathcal{D}
. Indeed, since
q_{\tau}^{k}
is a minimizer,

\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right)\leq\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q\right),

(3.24)

from which we infer that

\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)\leq\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q\right)-\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right).

However, the structure of the metric
implies that

\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q\right)-\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k}_{\tau},q\right),

from which (
3.22
) follows.

The next step is to verify energy inequality (
3.23
), following the thoughts of
[
46
]
. Testing the stability of
q^{k-1}_{\tau}
with
q:=q^{k}_{\tau}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right)

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)+L\left(k\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)-L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right).

For nonnegative integers
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
with
k_{1}\leq k_{2}\leq N
, let
t_{I}=k_{1}\tau
and
t_{I\!I}=k_{2}\tau
. Summing (
3.25
) over
k=k_{1}+1,\dots,k_{2}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\left[L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)-L\left(k\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left(k_{2}\tau,q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(k_{1}\tau,q^{k_{1}}_{\tau}\right)

(3.26)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right).

Replacing
q_{\tau}^{k_{1}}
,
q_{\tau}^{k_{2}}
with
q_{\tau}
evaluated at suitable time points, we discover the first inequality in (
3.23
),

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{I\!I}}\dot{L}\left(t,q_{\tau}(t-\tau)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}\left(k_{2}\tau,q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(k_{1}\tau,q^{k_{1}}_{\tau}\right)+\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}\left(t_{I\!I},q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(t_{I},q^{k_{1}}_{\tau}\right)+{\rm Var}\left(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};\left[t_{I},t_{I\!I}\right]\right)

(for a step function, calculating
{\rm Var}\left(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};\left[t_{I},t_{I\!I}\right]\right)
is easy). The proof of the second inequality in (
3.23
) is similar. Starting from the minimality of
q^{k}_{\tau}
, when compared with
q^{k-1}_{\tau}
in (
3.24
), yields

\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q^{k}_{\tau}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right)\leq\mathcal{I}\left(k\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)\\
=\mathcal{I}\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)+L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)-L\left(k\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right).

We sum again over
k=k_{1}+1,\dots,k_{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left(k_{2}\tau,q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left(k_{1}\tau,q^{k_{1}}_{\tau}\right)+\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\mathcal{D}\left(q^{k-1}_{\tau},q^{k}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\left[L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)-L\left(k\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)\right],

so we find

\mathcal{I}\left(k_{2}\tau,q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(k_{1}\tau,q^{k_{1}}_{\tau}\right)+{\rm Var}\left(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};\left[t_{I},t_{I\!I}\right]\right)\leq-\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{I\!I}}\dot{L}\left(t,q_{\tau}(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

and the second inequality in (
3.23
) is shown.

\Box

We would like to pass with the step size
\tau
to zero and for this we need certain
a priori
bounds.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) be satisfied.
Then there is
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\tau>0
:

\left\lVert y_{\tau}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}\left(0,T;W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)}<\kappa,

(3.27)

{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};[0,T]))<\kappa,

(3.28)

\left\lVert z_{\tau}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}\left(0,T;W^{1,\alpha}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times W^{1,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\right)}<\kappa.

(3.29)

Proof.
For
q=(y,F_{\rm p},p)\in\mathbb{Q}
, set

V(q)=\int_{\Omega}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{W}(\nabla y(x)F_{\rm p}^{-1},\nabla[({\rm cof}\nabla y(x))F_{\rm p}^{\top}(x)],F_{\rm p}(x),\nabla F_{\rm p},p(x),\nabla p(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\color[rgb]{0,0,0}.

Like in the proof of Proposition
3.8
, we can show by the growth conditions on
\mathcal{W}
that for any
q
with
V(q)<+\infty
and some constants
C_{0}
,
C>0
,

\displaystyle-C_{0}+C\left(\|y\|^{d}_{W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|F_{\rm p}\|^{\beta}_{W^{1,\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}+\|p\|^{\omega}_{W^{1,\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\right)\leq V(q).

(3.30)

Using a lower energy estimate from the proof of (
3.23
) for
k_{1}=0

we get

V(q^{k_{2}}_{\tau})-L(k_{2}\tau,q^{k_{2}}_{\tau})-V(q^{0}_{\tau})+L(0,q^{0}_{\tau})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{k_{2}}\left[L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)-L\left(k\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)\right].

Hence for a constant
C
which only depends on the data and the initial condition,

\displaystyle V(q^{k_{2}}_{\tau})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{k_{2}}\left[L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)-L\left(k\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)\right]+L(k_{2}\tau,q^{k_{2}}_{\tau})+C.

(3.31)

So, denoting
Y_{\tau}^{d}:=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq T/\tau}\|y^{\ell}_{\tau}\|^{d}_{W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and taking into account (
3.30
) with
q:=q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}
we have

\displaystyle Y_{\tau}^{d}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{k_{2}}\left[L\left((k-1)\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)-L\left(k\tau,q^{k-1}_{\tau}\right)\right]+C(q^{k_{2}}_{\tau}).

(3.32)

This gives the bound (
3.27
), because
Y_{\tau}
is raised to the power
d>1
on the left hand side, whereas the right hand side is merely linear in
y_{\tau}^{\ell}
. We can proceed similarly for (
3.29
). The upper bound on the total dissipation can be derived by algebraic manipulations as in
[
21
]
.

\Box

3.7.
Limit passage

The following lemma is proved in
[
38
]
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\colon\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to[0,+\infty]
satisfy (
3.6
) and (
3.7
). Let
\mathcal{K}
be a weakly sequentially compact subset of
\mathbb{Z}
. Then for every sequence
\{z_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
z_{k}\colon[0,T]\to\mathcal{K}
for which

\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\,{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},z_{k};[0,T])<C
,
C>0
, there exists a subsequence (not relabelled), a function
z\colon[0,T]\to\mathcal{K}
, and a function
\Delta\colon[0,T]\to[0,C]
such that:

(i)
{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},z_{k};[0,t])\to\Delta(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(ii)
z_{k}{\rightharpoonup}z
in
\mathbb{Z}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and

(iii)
{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},z;[t_{0},t_{1}])\leq\Delta(t_{1})-\Delta(t_{0})
for all
0\leq t_{0}<t_{1}\leq T
.

Let us denote
\mathbb{X}:=L^{\beta}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\times L^{\omega}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Finally, we proved the existence of an energetic solution.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}+\beta^{-1}\leq d^{-1}<\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(n-1)^{-1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d>\frac{\beta(n-1)}{\beta-1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
. Let
q^{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
be a stable initial condition. Let the assumptions on
\mathcal{W}
,
\mathcal{D}
,
f
and
g
from Section
3.2
hold. Let further (
3.16
) or (3.A), (3.B), (3.C) hold.
Then there is a process
q\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{Q}

with
q(t)=(y(t),z(t))
such that
q
is an energetic solution according to Definition
2.2
.
The following convergence statements also hold:

(i)
for a
t
-dependent (not relabelled) subsequence w-
\lim_{\tau\to 0}y_{\tau}(t)=y(t)
in
W^{1,d}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(ii) for a (not relabelled) subsequence
\lim_{\tau\to 0}z_{\tau}(t)=z(t)
in
\mathbb{X}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(iii) for a (not relabelled) subsequence

\lim_{\tau\to 0}{\mathcal{I}}_{\tau}(t,q_{\tau}(t))={\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and

(iv) for a (not relabelled) subsequence
\lim_{\tau\to 0}{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};[0,t])={\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[0,t])
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

We have adapted the proof from
[
21
,
42
]
and divided it into three steps.

Step 1
:
Assertion (i) follows from the
a priori
estimate in Proposition
3.11
and (ii) results from Lemma
3.12
.
Hence we have a limit
q(t)=(y(t),z(t))
. We easily get that
q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Put
S(t,\tau):=\max_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}}\{k\tau;\ k\tau\leq t\}
. Then
\lim_{\tau\to 0}S(t,\tau)=t
and by definition
q_{\tau}(t):=q_{\tau}(S(t,\tau))\in\mathcal{S}(S(t,\tau))
. As we have seen,
\mathcal{D}
can be assumed such that (
3.13
) holds. Hence by Proposition
3.6
, the limit is stable, i.e.
q(t)\in\mathcal{S}(t)
(thanks to our
a priori
estimates, we can pass to a gpc-convergent subsequence to get lower semicontinuity).

Step 2
:
Notice that
\theta_{\tau}(t):=\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}(t,q_{\tau}(t))
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T)
by (
3.8
), (
3.9
) and (
3.27
), so we deduce a weak*-convergent subsequence, which we do not relabel, with the limit
\theta
. By Fatou’s lemma
\theta_{\rm i}\leq\theta
.

Our interpolation satisfies
q_{\tau}(t)=q_{\tau}(k\tau)
for
0\leq t-k\tau<\tau
. Successively using (
3.23
), (
3.8
), (
3.9
) and (
3.27
), we get for some
C
,
C_{1}>0

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,q_{\tau}(t))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};[0,t])

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(k\tau,q_{\tau}(k\tau))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q_{\tau};[0,k\tau])+C\tau

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q_{\tau}(0))-\int_{0}^{k\tau}\dot{L}(s,q_{\tau}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+C\tau

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q_{\tau}(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\dot{L}(s,q_{\tau}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+C_{1}\tau.

On account of Lemma
3.12
(i) and Proposition
3.6
we find that for
\tau\to 0

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))+\Delta(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\theta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Following
[
42
]
we set
\theta_{\rm i}(t):=\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\theta_{\tau}(t)
. Since
\Delta(t)\geq{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[0,t])
and by Fatou’s lemma
\int_{0}^{t}\theta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\geq\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{\rm i}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
, for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[0,t])

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{\rm i}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

The convergence
\theta_{\tau}(s)=\dot{L}(s,q_{\tau}(s))\to\dot{L}(s,q(s))
and extracting an
s
-dependent subsequence of
\{\theta_{\tau}(s)\}
converging to
\theta_{\rm i}(s)
show that
\theta_{\rm i}(s)=\dot{L}(s,q(s))
.
We finally get the upper energy estimate

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[0,t])

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\dot{L}(s,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3.33)

Step 3:
The fact that
q(t)
is stable for all
t\in[0,T]
will be useful in proving the lower estimate. Consider a partition of a time interval
[t_{I},t_{I\!I}]\subset[0,T]
such that
t_{I}=\vartheta_{0}^{M}<\vartheta_{1}^{M}<\vartheta_{2}^{M}<\cdots<\vartheta_{K_{M}}^{M}=t_{I\!I}
and
\max_{i}(\vartheta_{i}^{M}-\vartheta_{i-1}^{M})=:\vartheta^{M}\to 0
as
M\to\infty
. Let us test the stability of
q(\vartheta_{k-1}^{M})
with
q(\vartheta_{k}^{M})
,
k=1,\ldots,K_{M}
.
Similarly to (
3.26
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{M}}\left[L\left((\vartheta_{k-1}^{M},q(\vartheta_{k}^{M})\right)-L\left(\vartheta_{k}^{M},q(\vartheta_{k}^{M})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left(t_{I\!I},q(t_{I\!I})\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(t_{I},q(t_{I})\right)

(3.34)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{M}}\mathcal{D}\left(q(\vartheta_{k-1}^{M}),q(\vartheta_{k}^{M})\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{M}}-\int_{\vartheta_{k-1}^{M}}^{\vartheta_{k}^{M}}\dot{L}(s,q(\vartheta_{k}^{M}))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\left(t_{I\!I},q(t_{I\!I}\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(t_{I},q(t_{I})\right)

(3.35)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[t_{I},t_{I\!I}]).

We finally rearrange the terms as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{M}}\int_{\vartheta_{k-1}^{M}}^{\vartheta_{k}^{M}}\dot{L}(s,q(\vartheta_{k}^{M}))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{M}}\dot{L}(\vartheta_{k}^{M},q(\vartheta_{k}^{M}))(\vartheta_{k}^{M}-\vartheta_{k-1}^{M})

(3.36)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{M}}\int_{\vartheta_{k-1}^{M}}^{\vartheta_{k}^{M}}(\dot{L}(s,q(\vartheta_{k}^{M}))-\dot{L}(\vartheta_{k}^{M},q(\vartheta_{k}^{M})))\,\mathrm{d}s.

The last sum on the right-hand side of (
3.36
) goes to zero with
\vartheta^{M}\to 0
because the time derivative of the external loading is uniformly continuous in time by (
3.8
) and (
3.9
).
The first sum on the right-hand side converges to
\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{I\!I}}\dot{L}(s,q(s))\,\mathrm{d}s
by
[
11
, Lemma 4.12]
.

Altogether, the upper and lower estimates give us the energy balance

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[0,t])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\dot{L}(s,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3.37)

Moreover,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{\rm i}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))+{\rm Var}(\mathcal{D},q;[0,t]))\leq{\mathcal{I}}(t,q(t))+\Delta(t)

(3.38)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\theta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq{\mathcal{I}}(0,q(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{\rm i}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Thus in fact, all inequalities in (
3.38
) are equalities and we get (iv). Proposition
3.6
also implies (iii). ∎

Finally, we include a simple example from single-crystal plasticity, covered by our approach, in the spirit of
[
26
]
and
[
40
]
.

Example 3.1

(single slip without hardening)
.

Consider two orthonormal vectors
a
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, describing the motion of edge dislocations in such a way that
a
is the glide direction and
b
is the slip-plane normal.
Further we focus on a particular case of the so-called separable material
where

\mathcal{W}(F_{\rm e},H,z)=\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{\rm e})+c|H|^{2}+c{\rm det}(F_{\rm e})^{-s}+\varepsilon|F_{\rm p}|^{6}+\varepsilon|\nabla F_{\rm p}|^{6},\;c,s,\varepsilon>0,

with
F_{\rm p}(x,t)=\mathbb{I}+\gamma(x,t)a\otimes b
where
\gamma
is the plastic slip and we can choose
\mathcal{W}_{1}
to be e.g. the stored energy density of the Saint Venant–Kirchhoff material, i.e.
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{\rm e})=\frac{1}{8}(\textstyle{F_{\rm e}}F_{\rm e}-\mathbb{I}):\mathbb{C}:(\textstyle{F_{\rm e}}F_{\rm e}-\mathbb{I})
so that
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{\rm e})\geq c(|F_{e}|^{4}-1)
.
The vectors
a
,
b
are not fixed in the reference configuration in general (more precisely, they lie in an intermediate lattice space, see
[
27
]
). The slip-plane normal
\tilde{b}
in the reference configuration is given by
\tilde{b}=(F_{\rm p})^{\top}b
. However, in the special case of a single slip we obtain
\tilde{b}=b
and the slip-plane normal remains unchanged during plastic transformations.

As
F_{\rm p}
is completely described by
\gamma
, we identify
z:=\gamma
and use the dissipation potential

\delta(\dot{\gamma})=\kappa|\dot{\gamma}|,

where
\kappa>0
represents the resistance to the slip.

This corresponds to the dissipation distance

\mathcal{D}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\int_{\Omega}\kappa|\gamma_{1}(x)-\gamma_{2}(x)|\mathrm{d}x.

An extension to multi-slip, described by several glide directions
\{a_{i}\}
and slip-plane normals
\{b_{i}\}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, would be feasible, cf.
[
40
]
.

As a last remark, note that our stored energy
\mathcal{W}
could also depend on
F_{\rm e}^{-1}
, cf.
[
5
]
.
