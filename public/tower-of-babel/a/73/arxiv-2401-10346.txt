Solution uniqueness of convex optimization problems via the radial cone
Jalal Fadili, Tran T. A. Nghia, Duy Nhat Phan

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we mainly study solution uniqueness of some convex optimization problems. Our characterizations of solution uniqueness are in terms of the radial cone. This approach allows us to know when a unique solution is a strong solution or even a tilt-stable one without checking second-order information. Consequently, we apply our theory to low-rank optimization problems. The radial cone is fully calculated in this case and numerical experiments show that our characterizations are sharp.

Introduction

In many disciplines of science and engineering, solving the following linear inverse problem is essential:

\Phi x=y_{0},

(1.1)

where
\Phi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
is a linear operator between two finite dimensional vector spaces and
y_{0}=\Phi x_{0}
is an element of
\mathbb{Y}
. Particularly, in signal/image processing,
y_{0}
is a known observation of an unknown signal
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
through the forward linear operator
\Phi
. In regression in statistical learning,
y_{0}
is the response vector,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
is the unknown parameter vector, and
\Phi
is the design matrix. Recovering exactly the signal
x_{0}
by solving the linear system (
1.1
) through least-squares raises non-uniqueness issues, as usually the dimension of
\mathbb{Y}
is much smaller than the dimension of
\mathbb{X}
, which makes (
1.1
) underdetermined and thus has infinitely many solutions. On the other hand, the vector
x_{0}
usually possesses some special features (in the form of some low complexity) that cannot be captured by naive least-squares. Thus, a form complexity regularization is necessary in which the recovery of
x_{0}
is cast as the regularized optimization problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\quad g(x)\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\Phi x=y_{0},

(1.2)

where
g:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is some particular function/regularizer, which is chosen to promote the low complexity, i.e. the expected features, of the original vector
x_{0}
. Solving this optimization problems narrows down the possible candidate solutions for
x_{0}
. And if we want the solution of (
1.2
) to be exactly
x_{0}
, it should hopefully be the unique solution. That is one of the main reasons why solution uniqueness of problem (
1.2
) has drawn much attention from different authors and research groups; see, e.g.,
[

,

,

,

,

,

can be nonconvex in many problems
[

,

]
, in this paper we focus on the case when it is a continuous convex function. Unlike system (
1.1
), problem (
1.2
) likely has a unique solution provided that the number of observations, i.e., the dimension of
\mathbb{Y}
is large enough compared to some intrinsic dimension of
x_{0}
(but still much smaller than that of
\mathbb{X}
)
[
9
,

,

.

Another optimization problem relative to (

(1.3)

is a proper lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) convex function that is twice differentiable with positive definite Hessian in the interior of its domain. This problem is often used when there is noise in the observation

and the loss function

Contributions

In Theorem
3.1
, we show that
\bar{x}
is the unique optimal solution of problem (
1.3
) if and only if
\bar{y}=-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})\in\partial g(\bar{x})
and

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{(\partial g)^{-1}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})=\{0\},

(1.5)

where
\mathcal{R}_{(\partial g)^{-1}(\bar{y})}
is the radial cone at
\bar{x}
to the set
(\partial g)^{-1}(\bar{y})
defined in Section
2
. For problem (
1.2
), we also have a similar result that
x_{0}
is the unique solution if and only if there exists a
dual certificate

y\in\partial g(x_{0})\cap\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}
such that

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{(\partial g)^{-1}(y)}(x_{0})=\{0\}.

(1.6)

This characterization was constructed in
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
for the case of nuclear norm minimization (
1.4
) and recently in
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
for convex gauge functions with some additional assumptions. Our results work for any continuous convex functions
g
. Most importantly, we advance this approach in the following ways:

•

We establish the connection between solution uniqueness and strong minima
[
6
,
44
]
for problems (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Obviously, a strong solution is a unique solution for convex optimization problems. The opposite implication is not true in general. But when
g
is the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm,
[
20
]
recently shows that unique solutions of problems (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are also strong ones via second-order analysis. It is not well-understood the reason why the structure of
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm can give such an equivalence. In this paper, we have a clearer answer for this phenomenon. Closedness of the radial cone plays an important role there. Indeed, we prove this equivalence in Corollary
3.2
in the case when the function
g
satisfies the
quadratic growth condition

[
6
,
21
]
and the radial cone in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) is closed; see also Remark
4.7
for similar analysis on problem (
1.2
). This is quite a surprise because usually one has to check second-order information for strong minima, while our conditions (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) only use first-order information.

•

We unify the two approaches of using radial cones and descent cones for solution uniqueness. As discussed above, solution uniqueness is also characterized by descent cones introduced in
[
9
]
. In the recent paper
[
20
, Theorem 5.1]
, the descent cone of
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm (and thus
\ell_{1}
norm) has an exact computation via its first-order information. We generalize that result in this paper for any continuous convex function
g
. Exact formulae of the descent cone via the radial cone and the
critical cone
are provided in Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.4
. These calculations allow us to link up the two aforementioned approaches for solution uniqueness in Theorem
4.5
. Consequently, the descent cone of the nuclear norm is also computed exactly in this paper.

•

We are able to apply our general theory to the nuclear norm minimization problem (
1.4
). Although solution uniqueness of problem (
1.2
) was already characterized in
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
via the radial cone, it was not fully calculated there. In Lemma
3.5
, we provide a complete computation of this radial cone via initial data. This leads us to a full characterization of solution uniqueness for problem (
1.4
) in Corollary
4.8
. Other interesting connections of solution uniqueness with sharp minima
[
14
,
38
,
12
,
20
]
and tilt stability
[
40
,
37
,
31
,
16
]
for nuclear norm minimization problems are also obtained in Sections
3
and
4
. One of our most exciting results is to show the equivalence between solution uniqueness and tilt stability for problem (
1.3
) in Corollary
3.10
under
Nondegenerate Condition
(a.k.a.
Strict Complementary Condition

[
6
]
) when
g
is the nuclear norm. This implicitly indicates that solution uniqueness makes problem (
1.3
) stable to be solved. Numerical experiments on the nuclear norm minimization problem (
4.12
) in Section
5
confirm that our characterizations of solution uniqueness are sharp and computationally friendly.

Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, let
\mathbb{X}
be an Euclidean space and
\mathbb{X}^{*}
be its dual space endowed with the inner product
\langle v,u\rangle
for
v\in\mathbb{X}^{*}
and
u\in\mathbb{X}
. Suppose that
\|\cdot\|
is the norm in
\mathbb{X}
induced by the latter inner product. We denote
\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bar{x})
by the open ball with center
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{X}
and radius
r>0
. We first recall some notions and classical results from convex analysis that will be important to our exposition; see e.g.
[
43
]
for a comprehensive account.

Let
\Omega
be a closed convex set in
\mathbb{X}
and
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. We write
{\rm cone}\,\Omega
for the
conic hull
of
\Omega
and
{\rm aff}\,\Omega
for the
affine hull
of
\Omega
.
The
relative interior
of
\Omega
is denoted by

{\rm ri}\,\Omega=\{x\in\Omega|\;\exists\varepsilon>0,\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap{\rm aff}\,\Omega\neq\emptyset\};

The
radial cone
(known also the
cone of feasible directions
) and the
tangent cone
to
\Omega
at
\bar{x}
are defined, respectively, by

\mathcal{R}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})={\rm cone}\,(\Omega-\bar{x})=\mathbb{R}_{+}(\Omega-\bar{x})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})=\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})},

(2.1)

which means that
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
is the topological closure of the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})
; see, e.g.,
[
6
, Definition 2.54 and Proposition 2.55]
. When
\Omega
is a
polyhedral
set, i.e., it can be represented as the intersection of finitely many closed half spaces, the radial cone is closed and thus is exactly the tangent cone at the same point
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. But in general, the radial cone is not closed and strictly smaller than the tangent cone.

Now let
\varphi:\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a proper lower semi-continuous (l.s.c.) convex function with nonempty domain
\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{x\in\mathbb{X}|\;\varphi(x)<\infty\}.
For
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
, the
subdifferential
of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}
is defined by

\partial\varphi(\bar{x})\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{v\in\mathbb{X}^{*}|\;\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})\geq\langle v,x-\bar{x}\rangle,\forall x\in\mathbb{X}\}.

The
Legendre-Fenchel conjugate
of
\varphi
is the l.s.c. convex function
\varphi^{*}:\mathbb{X}^{*}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\varphi^{*}(v)\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\sup\{\langle v,x\rangle-\varphi(x)|\;x\in\mathbb{X}\}\quad\mbox{for}\quad v\in\mathbb{X}^{*}.

Note that
v\in\partial\varphi(x)
if and only if
x\in\partial\varphi^{*}(v)
, i.e.,
\partial\varphi^{*}(v)=(\partial\varphi)^{-1}(v)=\{x\in X|\;v\in\partial\varphi(x)\}
, which plays an important role in our study later in the paper. The directional derivative of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}
in some direction
w\in\mathbb{X}
is

d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{\varphi(\bar{x}+tw)-\varphi(\bar{x})}{t}.

If
\varphi
is continuous around
\bar{x}
, it is well-known that

d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)=\sup_{v\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})}\langle v,w\rangle.

(2.2)

The point
\bar{x}
is called a minimizer or an optimal solution of the following problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\quad\varphi(x),

(2.3)

if
\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(\bar{x})
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, i.e.,
0\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
or
\bar{x}\in\partial\varphi^{*}(0)
. We say
\bar{x}
is a
unique
(or
strict
) solution problem (
2.3
) if
\varphi(x)>\varphi(\bar{x})
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\{\bar{x}\}
, which means that
\{\bar{x}\}=\partial\varphi^{*}(0)
. Moreover,
\bar{x}
is said to be a
sharp solution
if there exists some modulus
c>0
such that

\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})\geq c\|x-\bar{x}\|\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{X}.

The notion of sharp minima was introduced independently by Polyak
[
38
]
and Crome
[
14
]
under the name
strong uniqueness
. On the other hand,
\bar{x}
is a
strong solution
if there exist
\varepsilon>0
and modulus
\kappa>0
such that

\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x}).

The notion of strong minima is weaker than that of sharp minima. For instance, the former is only a local property. We refer the readers to
[
6
,
39
,
44
]
for different ways to characterize sharp/strong minima and their roles in optimization.

Finally in this section, we recall the
quadratic growth condition
for functions.

Definition 2.1

(Quadratic growth condition)
.

The convex function
\varphi
is said to satisfy the quadratic growth condition at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
if there exist
\varepsilon>0
and modulus
\kappa>0
such that

\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})-\langle\bar{v},x-\bar{x}\rangle\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}{\rm dist}\,(x;\partial\varphi^{*}(\bar{v}))^{2}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x}).

(2.4)

When the function
\varphi
is convex, the quadratic growth condition is equivalent to the
Łojasiewicz inequality with exponent
\frac{1}{2}

[
5
]
and the
metric subregularity of the subdifferential

[
2
,
16
,
49
]
. There are broad classes of convex functions satisfying the quadratic growth condition such as
piece-wise linear quadratic convex
functions
[
44
, Definition 10.20]
and many
convex spectral
functions
[
8
]
; see also
[
48
]
for some other ones.

3
Solution uniqueness of convex composite optimization problems

3.1
General regularizer

In this section we study the solution uniqueness of the following convex composite optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\quad\varphi(x)\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}f(\Phi x)+g(x),

(3.1)

where
\Phi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
is a linear operator between two Euclidean spaces,
f:\mathbb{Y}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a proper l.s.c. convex function that is differentiable in the interior of its domain, and
g:\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a proper l.s.c. convex function with

\Phi^{-1}\left({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)\right)\cap\mbox{\rm dom}\,g\neq\emptyset.

(3.2)

Throughout this section, we have two additional standing assumptions on the loss function
f
:

(a)

f
is twice continuously differentiable in
{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
.

(b)

The Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(y)
is positive definite for all
y\in{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
, which implies that
f
is strictly convex in the interior of its domain.

The class of loss functions satisfying the above standing assumptions includes strongly convex and twice continuously differentiable functions
[
48
]
together with the discrete
Kullback-Leiber divergence
(known also as
relative entropy
)
[
15
]
that have been used widely in statistical/machine learning and signal processing.

Our first result establishes the characterization for solution uniqueness of problem (
3.1
) via the radial cone.

Theorem 3.1

(Solution uniqueness via the radial cone)
.

Let
\bar{x}\in\Phi^{-1}\left({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)\right)\cap\mbox{\rm dom}\,g
be an optimal solution of problem (
3.1
) and
\bar{y}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})\in\partial g(\bar{x})
. Then
\bar{x}
is the unique solution if and only if

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})=\{0\}.

(3.3)

Proof.

Suppose that
\bar{x}
is a unique solution of problem (
3.1
). Pick any
w\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
, we find some
t>0
such that
\bar{x}+tw\in\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
. As
\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
is a convex set and
\bar{x}\in\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
, we have
\bar{x}+\alpha tw\in\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Since
\Phi^{-1}\left({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)\right)
is an open set containing
\bar{x}
,
\bar{x}+\alpha tw\in\Phi^{-1}({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f))
for sufficiently small
\alpha>0
. It follows that

\partial\varphi(\bar{x}+\alpha tw)=\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi(\bar{x}+\alpha tw))+\partial g(\bar{x}+\alpha tw)=\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})+\partial g(\bar{x}+\alpha tw)\ni 0.

Hence
\bar{x}+\alpha tw
is an optimal solution of problem (
3.1
). Since
\bar{x}
is the unique solution of (
3.1
), we have
\bar{x}=\bar{x}+\alpha tw
, i.e.,
w=0
. This implies condition (
3.3
).

Conversely, suppose that condition (
3.3
) holds. Let
\tilde{x}
be any optimal solution of problem (
3.1
). It follows that the whole segment
[\bar{x},\tilde{x}]
belong to the solution set of (
3.1
). Setting
w=\tilde{x}-\bar{x}
, there exists
t_{0}>0
sufficiently small such that
\bar{x}+t_{0}w\in\Phi^{-1}\left({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)\right)
. Moreover,

-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})\in\partial g(\bar{x})\quad{\rm and}\quad-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t_{0}w))\in\partial g(\bar{x}+t_{0}w).

(3.4)

Due to the monotonicity of
\partial g
, we have

0\leq\langle-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t_{0}w))+\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),t_{0}w\rangle=t_{0}\langle-\nabla f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t_{0}w))+\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\Phi w\rangle.

(3.5)

As
f
is twice continuously differentiable in
{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
, we obtain from the mean-value theorem that

\langle\nabla f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t_{0}w))-\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\Phi w\rangle=t_{0}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi(\bar{x}+tt_{0}w))\Phi w,\Phi w\rangle dt.

(3.6)

Since the Hessian mapping
\nabla^{2}f
is positive definite and continuous on
{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
, there exists some
\mu>0
such that

\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi(\bar{x}+tt_{0}w))\Phi w,\Phi w\rangle\geq\mu\|\Phi w\|^{2}

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Combining this with (
3.6
) and (
3.5
) gives us that
\Phi w=0
, i.e.,
w\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi.
Observe from the inclusion (
3.4
) that

\bar{y}=-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})=-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t_{0}w))\in\partial g(\bar{x}+t_{0}w),

which implies that
\bar{x}+t_{0}w\in\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
, i.e.,
w\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
. As condition (
3.3
) is satisfied, we have
w=0
. Thus
\tilde{x}=\bar{x}
which entails uniqueness of
\bar{x}
.

\hfill\Box

When the function
g
additionally satisfies the quadratic growth condition in Definition
2.1
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{y}=-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})
, the recent paper
[
21
, Corollary 3.4]
shows that the following condition

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})=\{0\}

(3.7)

characterizes strong minima at the optimal solution
\bar{x}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
is the tangent cone (
2.1
) to
\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
at
\bar{x}
. As the tangent cone is the closure of the radical cone. Condition (
3.7
) coincides with condition (
3.3
) when the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
is closed. This observation allows us to establish the equivalence between solution uniqueness and strong minima for problem (
3.1
) in the following result.

Corollary 3.2

(Strong minima and solution uniqueness)
.

Suppose that
\bar{x}\in\Phi^{-1}\left({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)\right)\cap\mbox{\rm dom}\,g
is an optimal solution of problem (
3.1
) and the function
g
satisfies the quadratic growth condition at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{y}=-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})
. Suppose further that the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
is closed. Then
\bar{x}
is a strong solution of problem (
3.1
) if and only if it is a unique solution.

Remark 3.3

(Closedness of the radial cone)
.

The radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
is closed if and only if

\mbox{\rm cone}\,\left(\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})-\bar{x}\right)\mbox{ is closed}.

(3.8)

This condition is automatic when
\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
is a polyhedral set. A particular example of functions
satisfying both (
3.8
) and the quadratic growth condition in Corollary
3.2
is the class of
convex piecewise linear-quadratic functions

[
44
, Definition 10.20]
, i.e.,
\mbox{\rm dom}\,g
is a union of finitely many polyhedral sets, relative to each of which the function
g(x)
has a convex quadratic expression; see also
[
44
, Proposition 10.21 and Theorem 11.24]
. This class of functions covers some important regularizers such as
\ell_{1}
(sparsity) norm, the
anisotropic total variation
, and the
elastic net regularizer
widely used in optimization, statistical/machine learning, and signal processing. The equivalence between solution uniqueness and strong minima for this class was observed in
[
4
]
. That allows
[
4
]
to obtain some new characterizations of solution uniqueness for
\ell_{1}
optimization problems (Lasso in particular) by using second-order analysis for strong minima, different from those in
[
19
,
45
,
50
,
51
,
22
]
.

Another important regularizer that is not convex piecewise linear-quadratic is the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm (a.k.a. group Lasso regularizer) defined by

g(x)=\|x\|_{1,2}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\|x_{J}\|\quad\mbox{for}\quad x\in\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.9)

where
\mathcal{J}
is a partition of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
with
p
distinct groups. It is shown in
[
48
]
that
\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
is a polyhedral set. Thus the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(\bar{x})
is also closed. It is also known that the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm satisfies the quadratic growth condition at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{y}
; see also
[
48
]
. By Corollary
3.2
again,
\bar{x}
is a strong solution of group Lasso problem (
3.1
) if and only if it is a unique solution. This observation was obtained recently in
[
20
, Theorem 5.3]
by a different approach via second-order analysis and the computation of the descent cone of the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm.

Another situation where condition (
3.8
) holds is when the
dual nondegeneracy condition
is satisfied in the sense that

\bar{x}\in{\rm ri}\,\partial g^{*}(\bar{y}).

(3.10)

Indeed, this condition means that
\mbox{\rm cone}\,\left(\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})-\bar{x}\right)
is the subspace in
\mathbb{X}
parallel to
\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})
, which is closed in finite dimension.
\triangle

3.2
Nuclear norm case

We now study a major class of problem (
3.1
), the nuclear norm optimization problem for low-rank matrix recovery:

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}}\quad f(\Phi X)+\|X\|_{*},

(3.11)

where
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear operator (
n_{2}\geq n_{1}
), the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a proper l.s.c. convex function satisfying the standing assumptions (
a
)-(
b
), and
g(X)=\|X\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm of
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, which is the sum of all singular values of
X
.
The dual norm of nuclear norm is known as the
spectral norm

\|X\|
, the largest singular value of
X
.

We work under the Forbenius inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

\langle X,Y\rangle={\rm Tr}\,(X^{T}Y)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}},

where
{\rm Tr}\,(\cdot)
is the trace operator in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.
This induces the Frobenius norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
as follows

\|X\|_{F}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\sqrt{\langle X,X\rangle}=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\,(X^{T}X)}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}.

We recall that for
\overline{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, the singular valued decomposition (SVD, in brief) of
\overline{X}
reads

\overline{X}=U\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Sigma}_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}_{n_{1}\times n_{2}}V^{T}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\overline{\Sigma}_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(\overline{X})&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&\sigma_{r}(\overline{X})\end{pmatrix},

(3.12)

where
r={\rm rank}\,(\overline{X})
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}
are orthogonal matrices, and
\sigma_{i}(\overline{X})
are the ordered positive singular values of
\overline{X}
, i.e.
\sigma_{1}(\overline{X})\geq\sigma_{2}(\overline{X})\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{r}(\overline{X})>0
. Denote
\mathcal{O}(\overline{X})
by the set of all such pairs
(U,V)
satisfying (
3.12
). We write
U=\begin{pmatrix}U_{I}&U_{J}\end{pmatrix}
and
V=\begin{pmatrix}V_{I}&V_{K}\end{pmatrix}
, where
U_{I}
and
V_{I}
are the submatrices of the first
r
columns of
U
and
V
, respectively. It follows from (
3.12
) that
\overline{X}=U_{I}\overline{\Sigma}_{r}V_{I}^{T}
, which is known as a
compact or reduced SVD
of
\overline{X}
.

The subdifferential of the nuclear norm was computed in
[
47
, Example 2]
. The subdifferential of its Legendre-Fenchel conjugate (i.e. the normal cone to the spectral ball) was formalized in
[
48
, Proposition 10]
, which can be obtained from
[
47
, Example 1]
via classical convex analysis calculus, i.e. the formula of the normal cone to a level set
[
43
, Corollary 23.7.1]
. We summarize these results in the following lemma which plays an important role in our paper.

Lemma 3.4

(Subdifferentials of the nuclear norm and its conjugate)
.

The subdifferential of the nuclear norm at
\overline{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
is computed by

\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}=\left\{U\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&W\end{pmatrix}V^{T}|\;\|W\|\leq 1\right\}\quad\mbox{for any}\quad(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{X}).

(3.13)

Moreover,
\overline{Y}\in\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}
if and only if
\|\overline{Y}\|\leq 1
and

\|\overline{X}\|_{*}=\langle\overline{Y},\overline{X}\rangle.

(3.14)

Furthermore, for any
\overline{Y}\in\mathbb{B}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}|\;\|Z\|\leq 1\}
, we have

\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})=U\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p(\overline{Y})}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\quad\mbox{for any}\quad(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{Y}),

(3.15)

where
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p}
is the set of all
p\times p
symmetric positive semidefinite matrices and
p(\overline{Y})
is defined by

p(\overline{Y})\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\#\{i|\;\sigma_{i}(\overline{Y})=1\}.

(3.16)

Let
\overline{Y}\in\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}
and
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{X})
. Note from (
3.13
) that
\overline{Y}
can be written as

\overline{Y}=U\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&\overline{W}\end{pmatrix}V^{T}

(3.17)

with some
\overline{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)}
satisfying
\|\overline{W}\|\leq 1
.
Let
(\widehat{U},\widehat{V})\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{W})
and
\widehat{U}\Sigma\widehat{V}^{T}
be a full SVD of
\overline{W}
. We get from (
3.17
) that

\overline{Y}=\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&\Sigma\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\overline{U}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}(U_{I}\;U_{J}\widehat{U})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{V}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}(V_{I}\;V_{K}\widehat{V}).

(3.18)

Observe that
\overline{U}^{T}\overline{U}=\mathbb{I}_{{n_{1}}}
and
\overline{V}^{T}\overline{V}=\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}
. It follows that
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{X})\cap\mathcal{O}(\overline{Y})
, which means
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}
have
simultaneous ordered SVD

[
29
,
30
]
with orthogonal pair
(\overline{U},\overline{V})
in the sense that

\overline{X}=\overline{U}({\rm Diag}\,\sigma(\overline{X}))\overline{V}^{T}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\overline{Y}=\overline{U}({\rm Diag}\,\sigma(\overline{Y}))\overline{V}^{T},

(3.19)

where
\sigma(\overline{X})\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\left(\sigma_{1}(\overline{X}),\ldots,\sigma_{n_{1}}(\overline{X})\right)^{T}
containing all the singular values of
\overline{X}
in decreasing order and

{\rm Diag}\,\sigma(\overline{X})\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(\overline{X})&\ldots&0&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\ddots&0&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\ldots&\sigma_{n_{1}}(\overline{X})&0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix}_{n_{1}\times n_{2}}.

In the spirit of Theorem
3.1
, to study the solution uniqueness of problem (
3.11
) we have to compute the radial cone of the nuclear norm. Its explicit formula below is helpful for further studies in this paper and also for the numerical experiments when checking solution uniqueness in Section
5
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\overline{Y}\in\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}
. Suppose that
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}
have the simultaneous ordered SVD with an orthogonal matrix pair
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{X})\cap\mathcal{O}(\overline{Y})
. Then we have

\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})=\left\{U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}|\;A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}\right\},

(3.20)

where
\mathbb{S}^{r}
is the set of
r\times r
symmetric matrices,
r=\mbox{\rm rank}\,(\overline{X})
and
p=p(\overline{Y})
is defined in (
3.16
).

Proof.
To verify the inclusion “
\subset
” in (
3.20
), pick any
W\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})\setminus\{0\}
. Thus, there exists
\nu>0
such that
\overline{X}+\nu W\in\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})
. By formulae (
3.12
) and (
3.15
), there exist
A\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
,
D\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
, and
C\in\mathbb{S}^{p-r}_{+}
such that
W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&D&0\\
D^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}
. As
\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})
is a convex set and
\overline{X}\in\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})
, we have
\overline{X}+tW\in\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})
for any
t\in(0,\nu)
. If follows from (
3.15
) that

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA&tD\\
tD^{T}&tC\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{p}_{+}.

(3.21)

Let
\lambda_{\min}:\mathbb{S}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
be the smallest eigenvalue function. It is well-known that this function is
1
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Frobenius norm. As
\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r})=\sigma_{r}(\overline{X})>0
, we have
\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)>0
for all
t\in[0,\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r})/\|A\|_{F})
, i.e., the matrix
\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA
is positive definite or
\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA\succ 0
for all such
t
. We then get by the Schur complement that

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA&tD\\
tD^{T}&tC\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0\quad\mbox{iff}\quad C-tD^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}D\succeq 0;

(3.22)

see also
[
25
, Theorem 7.7.6 and its proof]
.
For any
x\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,C
, we derive from (
3.21
) and (
3.22
) that

0\leq x^{T}\left(C-tD^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}D\right)x=-tx^{T}D^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}Dx,

which implies
Dx=0
as
\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA\succ 0
; i.e.
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,C\subset\mbox{\rm Ker}\,D
. Define
\mathcal{X}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{GC|\;G\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}\}
, a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
. We show next that
D\in\mathcal{X}
. Indeed, by contradiction suppose that
D\notin\mathcal{X}
, it follows from the separation theorem that there exists a matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
such that

\mbox{\rm Tr}\,(DH^{T})=\langle D,H\rangle<0=\min_{G\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}}\langle GC,H\rangle=\min_{G\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\,(C^{T}G^{T}H)=\min_{G\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}}\mbox{\rm Tr}\,(G^{T}HC^{T}),

(3.23)

which implies that
HC^{T}=0
, i.e.,
CH^{T}=0
. Thus each row of
H
is a vector in
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,C
, which implies that
DH^{T}=0
since we have shown just before that
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,C\subset\mbox{\rm Ker}\,D
. But this contradicts (
3.23
). Thus
D\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e., there exists
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
such that
D=BC
. This verifies the inclusion “
\subset
” in (
3.20
).

To justify the converse inclusion “
\supset
” in (
3.20
), pick any
W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with
A\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
, and
C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}
. Again, arguing as above, we have
\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)>0
for all
t>0
small enough, meaning that
\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA
is nonsingular. According to the Schur complement (
3.22
) and formula (
3.15
) again,
\overline{X}+tW\in\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})
if and only if

C-tC^{T}B^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}BC\succeq 0.

As
C\in\mathbb{S}^{p}_{+}
, it is diagonalizable. Hence we may write
C=Z\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}Z^{T}
with
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
being a diagonal matrix of positive eigenvalues of
C
for some
s\leq p-r
and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-r)\times(p-r)}
being an orthogonal matrix. It follows that
C-tC^{T}B^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}BC\succeq 0
if and only if

0\preceq\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}-t\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}Z^{T}B^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}BZ\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda-t\Lambda M_{t}\Lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

(3.24)

where
M_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
is the submatrix of the positive semidefinite matrix
Z^{T}B^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}BZ
with the same position as
\Lambda
in
\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
. As
\overline{\Sigma}_{r}\succ 0
,
Z^{T}B^{T}(\Sigma_{0}+tA)^{-1}BZ
and
M_{t}
are continuous mappings with respect to
t
around
0
. Since
M_{t}
is symmetric, we can choose
t>0
small enough such that
\Lambda-t\Lambda M_{t}\Lambda\succeq 0
. Thus condition (
3.24
) is valid, which implies
C-tC^{T}B^{T}(\overline{\Sigma}_{r}+tA)^{-1}BC\succeq 0
. This together with the Schur complement (
3.22
) and formula (
3.15
) tells us that
X_{0}+tW\in\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})
, which implies
W\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
. The proof is complete.
\hfill\Box

The following result is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
and formula (
3.20
) of the radial cone.

Corollary 3.6

(Solution uniqueness of low-rank optimization problems)
.

Suppose that
\overline{X}
is an optimal solution of problem (
3.11
) with
\overline{X}\in\Phi^{-1}({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f))
and
\overline{Y}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\overline{X})\in\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}
. Then
\overline{X}
is the unique solution of problem (
3.11
) if and only if

\Phi\left(\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}\right)=0~\mbox{with}~A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A=C=0,

(3.25)

where the pair
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{X})\cap\mathcal{O}(\overline{Y})
is from (
3.19
),
r={\rm rank}\,(\overline{X})
, and
p=p(\overline{Y})
from (
3.16
).

Remark 3.7

(Closedness of the radial cone for the nuclear norm)
.

As the nuclear norm satisfies the quadratic growth condition
[
8
,
48
]
, Corollary
3.2
tells us that
\overline{X}
is a unique solution of problem (
3.11
) if and only if it is a strong solution provided that the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is closed. Note that its closure is the tangent cone (
2.1
) computed in
[
21
, Corollary 4.2]

\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})=\left\{\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}A&B&0\\
B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}|\;A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}\right\};

(3.26)

see also
[
32
, Lemma 2.3 and Proposition 2.1]
for a different computation and approach.
If
p=r
, it is obvious that

\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})=\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})=\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{S}^{r}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T},

which implies that the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is closed in this case.

If
p>r
, we show next that the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is not closed. Indeed, pick

W_{k}=\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{E}&0\\
\mathbb{E}^{T}&\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{I}_{p-r}&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}\to W=\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{E}&0\\
\mathbb{E}^{T}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}

as
k\to\infty
, where
\mathbb{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
is the matrix of all entries
1
and
\mathbb{I}_{p-r}
is the
(p-r)\times(p-r)
identity matrix. Note that
W_{k}\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
, but
W\notin\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
. Hence the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is not closed. This situation is very different from the case when the regularizer
g
is
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm when the corresponding radial cone is always closed; see Remark
3.3
.

Thus the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is closed if and only if
p=r
. This is the case of dual nondegeneracy condition (
3.10
) discussed in Remark
3.3
. Indeed, note from (
3.15
) that

{\rm ri}\,(\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y}))=\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{S}_{++}^{p}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T},

(3.27)

where
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{p}
is the set of all symmetric and positive definite
p\times p
matrices. As
\overline{X}=\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\Sigma}_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}
,
p=r
if and only if
\overline{X}\in{\rm ri}\,(\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y}))
. Note further from (
3.13
) that

{\rm ri}\,(\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*})=\left\{U\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&W\end{pmatrix}V^{T}|\;\|W\|<1\right\}.

Thus, the case
p=r
is also equivalent to the
nondegeneracy condition

\overline{Y}\in{\rm ri}\,(\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}).

(3.28)

Consequently, the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is closed if and only if the nondegeneracy condition (
3.28
) is satisfied.
\triangle

Remark 3.8

(Checking condition (
3.25
) via optimization)
.

Condition (
3.25
) means that the following optimization problem

\max\;\frac{1}{2}\|A\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{1}{2}\|C\|_{F}^{2}\;\;\mbox{s.t.}\;\;\Phi\left(\overline{U}\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\overline{V}^{T}\right)=0,A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}

has global maximum value
0
and global maximizers
(0,\overline{B},0)\in\mathbb{S}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}
for any
\overline{B}
.
This is a nonlinear concave optimization problem. Unfortunately, most of available solvers will provide local maximizers. However, if
(0,\overline{B},0)\in\mathbb{S}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}
are local maximizers for any
\overline{B}
, then
0
is the global maximum value. Indeed, suppose that
(A,B,C)\in\mathbb{S}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}
satisfies the first equality in (
3.25
), hence is a feasible point of the above optimization problem. We observe that
(tA,B,tC)
is also feasible for any
t\neq 0
. By choosing sufficiently small
|t|>0
,
(tA,B,tC)
is in a small neighborhood of
(0,B,0)
. If
(0,B,0)
is a local maximizer of the above optimization problem, we have

t^{2}\|A\|^{2}_{F}+t^{2}\|C\|^{2}_{F}\leq 0,

which implies
A=0
and
C=0
. Hence
0
is the global maximum value.
\triangle

Corollary 3.9

(Strong minima vs solution uniqueness)
.

Let
\overline{X}\in\Phi^{-1}({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f))
be an optimal solution of problem (
3.11
). Suppose that Nondegeneracy Condition (
3.28
) is satisfied. Then
\overline{X}
is a unique solution of problem (
3.11
) if and only if it is a strong solution.

Proof.

Suppose that Nondegeneracy Condition (
3.28
) is satisfied. As discussed at the end of Remark
3.7
, the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\overline{Y})}(\overline{X})
is closed. Note also that the nuclear norm satisfies the quadratic growth condition (
2.4
) by
[
48
,
8
]
. By Corollary
3.2
,
\overline{X}
is a unique solution of problem (
3.11
) if and only if it is a strong solution.

\hfill\Box

When Nondegeneracy Condition (
3.28
) is satisfied, it follows from
[
31
, Theorem 6.3]
that
\overline{X}
is a strong solution of problem (
3.11
) if and only if it is a
tilt stable
solution of in the sense that the optimal solution set

S(V)\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}{\rm argmin}\quad\{f(\Phi X)+\|X\|_{*}-\langle V,X\rangle|\;X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\}\quad\mbox{for}\quad V\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous around
0
with
S(0)=\{\overline{X}\}
; see also the recent result
[
28
, Corollary 3.10]
for similar equivalence. Tilt stability was introduced by Poliquin and Rockafellar
[
40
]
for general optimization frameworks. This important stability is characterized in
[
40
]
via the
generalized Hessian
/
second-order limiting subdifferential
introduced by Mordukhovich
[
34
]
; see also
[
2
,
16
]
for different characterizations for tilt stability. Although the second-order limiting subdifferential has rich calculus
[
35
,
37
]
, its computation is nontrivial in many situations including the nuclear norm function in (
3.11
). By combining Corollary
3.9
with the aforementioned
[
31
, Theorem 6.3]
we obtain below the equivalence between solution uniqueness and tilt stability for problem (
3.11
) under Nondegeneracy Condition (
3.28
). This equivalence is interesting, as we see that the assumption of solution uniqueness is already included in the definition of tilt stability (
S(0)=\{\overline{X}\})
. Somewhat Nondegeracy Condition (
3.28
) makes the problem stable.

Corollary 3.10

(Solution uniqueness and tilt stability)
.

Suppose that
\overline{X}\in\Phi^{-1}({\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f))
is an optimal solution of problem (
3.11
) and that Nondegeneracy Condition (
3.28
) holds. Then
\overline{X}
is a tilt-stable solution if and only if it is the unique solution.

4
Solution uniqueness of convex optimization problems with linear constraints

4.1
General regularizer

In this section, we study solution uniqueness of the optimization problem (
1.2
)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\quad g(x)\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\Phi x=y_{0},

(4.1)

where
g:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
continuous
convex function,
\Phi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
is still a linear operator between two Euclidean spaces, and
y_{0}
is a vector in
\mathbb{Y}
. Suppose that
x_{0}
is a
feasible solution
of problem (
4.1
), i.e.,
\Phi x_{0}=y_{0}
. Solution uniqueness of this problem can be characterized via the so-called
descent cone

[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
recalled below.

Definition 4.1

(Descent cone
[
9
]
)
.

The descent cone at
x_{0}
is defined by

\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}{\rm cone}\,\{x-x_{0}|\;g(x)\leq g(x_{0})\}.

(4.2)

When
g
is a polyhedral convex function, the set
\{x-x_{0}|\;g(x)\leq g(x_{0})\}
is polyhedral and thus
\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})
is a closed convex cone. But it is not closed in general; see, e.g.,
[
20
, Example 4.3]
for the case of
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm. Note that the closure of the descent cone lies in the
critical cone
defined by

\mathcal{C}(x_{0})=\{w\in\mathbb{X}|\;dg(x_{0})(w)\leq 0\}

(4.3)

and the interior of the critical cone is a subset of the descent cone;
see, e.g.,
[
20
, Lemma 3.6]
. Hence the differences between them are on the boundary of
\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
denoted by
{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
. In the recent paper
[
20
, Theorem 5.1]
, the descent cone of the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm is computed exactly via the critical cone and first-order information. Here, we extend that result to the case of general continuous convex function by involving the radical cone.

Proposition 4.2

(Descent cone via radial cone)
.

Suppose that
g:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous convex function. The descent cone
D_{g}(x_{0})
in (
4.2
) is computed by

D_{g}(x_{0})=\bigcup_{y\in\partial g(x_{0})}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})\cap{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})\right]\cup({\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})).

(4.4)

Proof.

Let us start to prove the inclusion “
\subset
” in (
4.4
). Pick any
w\in D_{g}(x_{0})
, there exists some
\tau>0
such that
g(x_{0}+\tau w)\leq g(x_{0})
. As the function
g
is convex and continuous, we derive from (
2.2
) that

0\geq g(x_{0}+\tau w)-g(x_{0})\geq\tau\max_{y\in\partial g(x_{0})}\langle y,w\rangle=\tau dg(x_{0})(w),

(4.5)

which implies that
dg(x_{0})(w)\leq 0
.
Note further that
dg(x_{0})(\cdot)
is also a proper convex and continuous function. It follows that

{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})=\{u\in\mathbb{X}|\;dg(x_{0})(u)<0\}.

If
dg(x_{0})(w)<0
, we have
w\in{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
. If
dg(x_{0})(w)=0
, i.e.,
w\in{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
(the boundary of
\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
), note from (
4.5
) that
g(x_{0}+\tau w)=g(x_{0})
and there exists some
y\in\partial g(x_{0})
such that
\langle y,w\rangle=0
. Hence we have

g(x_{0}+\tau w)=g(x_{0})=\langle y,x_{0}\rangle-g^{*}(y)=\langle y,x_{0}+\tau w\rangle-g^{*}(y),

which implies that
y\in\partial g(x_{0}+\tau w)
, i.e.,
x_{0}+\tau w\in\partial g^{*}(y)
. It follows that
w\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})
. The inclusion “
\subset
” in (
4.4
) is verified.

To justify the converse inclusion “
\supset
” in (
4.4
), we pick any
w
in the right-hand side. If
w\in{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
, i.e.,
dg(x_{0})(w)<0
, there exists some
\tau>0
sufficiently small so that

g(x_{0}+\tau w)-g(x_{0})\leq 0,

which means
w\in\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})
. If
w\in{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(x_{0})
, i.e.,
dg(x_{0})(w)=0
, there exists some
y\in\partial g(x_{0})
such that
w\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})
. We find some
t>0
such that
x_{0}+tw\in\partial g^{*}(y)
. It follows that

g(x_{0}+tw)=\langle y,x_{0}+tw\rangle-g^{*}(y)=g(x_{0})+t\langle y,w\rangle\leq g(x_{0})+tdg(x_{0})(w)=g(x_{0}),

which also implies that
w\in\mathcal{D}_{g}(\bar{x})
. This verifies the inclusion “
\supset
” in (
4.4
).

\hfill\Box

The following result taken from
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
is the well-known characterization of solution uniqueness via the descent cone.

Proposition 4.3

(Descent cone for solution uniqueness
[
9
]
)
.

A feasible point
x_{0}
of problem (
4.1
) is the unique solution if and only if

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})=\{0\}.

The exact computation of the descent cone (
4.4
) gives the impression that it is a big and complicated set. But its intersection with
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi
seems to be small and simple as in the above result. We enhance this observation by providing next the calculation of this intersection when
x_{0}
is an optimal solution, which means
0\in\partial g(x_{0})+\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}
, i.e.,

\Delta(x_{0})\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\partial g(x_{0})\cap\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}\neq\emptyset.

(4.6)

This is known as the Source Condition
[
24
]
. An element of
\Delta(x_{0})
is called a
dual certificate
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
x_{0}
be an optimal solution of problem (
4.1
). Then we have

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})\quad\mbox{for any}\quad y\in\Delta(x_{0}).

(4.7)

Proof.

As
x_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.1
),
\Delta(x_{0})\neq\emptyset
. Pick any dual certificate
y\in\Delta(x_{0})
. To justify the “
\subset
” inclusion in (
4.7
), take any
w\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})
, we find some
\tau>0
such that
g(x_{0}+\tau w)\leq g(x_{0})
. Since
\Phi(x_{0}+\tau w)=\Phi x_{0}=y_{0}
, we have
g(x_{0}+\tau w)=g(x_{0})
. Note also that
\langle y,w\rangle=0
as
y\in\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}
and
w\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi
. It follows that

g(x_{0}+\tau w)=g(x_{0})=\langle y,x_{0}\rangle-g^{*}(y)=\langle y,x_{0}+\tau w\rangle-g^{*}(y),

which implies that
x_{0}+\tau w\in\partial g^{*}(y)
, i.e.,
w\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})
. This verifies inclusion “
\subset
” in (
4.7
).

Conversely, pick any
w\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})
. We find
t>0
such that
x_{0}+tw\in\partial g^{*}(y)
. It follows that

g(x_{0}+tw)=\langle y,x_{0}+tw\rangle-g^{*}(y)=\langle y,x_{0}\rangle-g^{*}(y)=g(x_{0}),

which implies
w\in\mathcal{D}_{g}(x_{0})
and verifies the converse inclusion “
\supset
” in (
4.7
).

\hfill\Box

Combining this result with Proposition
4.3
, we obtain a characterization of solution uniqueness for problem (
4.1
) via the radical cone, which is similar to Theorem
3.1
in Section 3.

Theorem 4.5

(Characterizations for solution uniqueness via radial cones)
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

x_{0}
is a unique optimal solution of problem (
4.1
).

(ii)

Source Condition (
4.6
) holds and for any dual certificate
y\in\Delta(x_{0})
, one has

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})=\{0\}.

(4.8)

(iii)

Source Condition (
4.6
) holds and there exists a dual certificate
\bar{y}\in\Delta(x_{0})
such that

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{y})}(x_{0})=\{0\}.

(4.9)

Proof.
Let us start by proving the implication [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)] by supposing that
x_{0}
is a unique optimal solution of problem (
4.1
). Hence Source Condition (
4.6
) holds. Moreover, (
4.8
) comes from Proposition
4.3
and Proposition
4.4
. This verifies (ii).

The implication [(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)] is trivial. Let us justify [(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(i)] by supposing that there exists some
\bar{y}\in\Delta(x_{0})\neq\emptyset
such that condition (
4.9
) holds. As Source Condition holds,
x_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.1
). By Proposition
4.3
and Proposition
4.4
again,
x_{0}
is the unique solution.
\hfill\Box

Remark 4.6
.

The equivalence between (i) and (iii) in Theorem
4.5
was established recently in
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
when
g
is the nuclear norm; see our Corollary
4.8
for further details about this case. This equivalence is also obtained in
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
when
g
is a gauge function. The appearance of (ii) in the above result is crucial. It tells us that when condition (
4.9
) holds at some
\bar{y}\in\Delta(x_{0})
, it is also satisfied at any other dual certificate. Hence, the choice of
\bar{y}
in condition (
4.9
) is not important. If we know one dual certificate of problem (
4.1
), we only need to check condition (
4.9
) at this dual certificate for solution uniqueness. Such a dual certificate can be found via solving a “gauge” convex optimization problem; see

[
20
, Section 4]
for more details. It is possible to prove Theorem
4.5
directly without using Proposition
4.3
, but our approach here highlights the connection between the descent cone and the radial cone for solution uniquessness.
\triangle

Remark 4.7

(Strong Minima vs Solution Uniqueness)
.

When the function
g
satisfies the quadratic growth condition at
x_{0}
for any
y\in\partial g(x_{0})
and it is
second-order regular
at
x_{0}
in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 3.85]
, it is shown recently in
[
21
, Theorem 3.5]
that
x_{0}
is a strong solution if and only if

\left[\bigcap_{y\in\Delta(x_{0})}\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})\right]\cap\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{C}(x_{0})=\{0\}.

(4.10)

If
x_{0}
is a strong solution of problem (
4.1
), it is certainly a unique solution. Conversely, if
x_{0}
is a unique solution of problem (
4.1
) and the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})
is closed at some dual certificate
y\in\Delta(x_{0})
, Theorem
4.5
tells us that

0=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0}),

which clearly verifies (
4.10
). Thus
x_{0}
is a strong solution of problem (
4.1
) if and only if it is the unique solution provided that the function
g
satisfies the quadratic growth condition at
x_{0}
for any
y\in\partial g(x_{0})
and the second-order regularity at
x_{0}
, and that the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(y)}(x_{0})
is closed at some dual certificate
y\in\Delta(x_{0})
.

The
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm satisfies all the three latter conditions; see also Remark
3.3
. Hence,
x_{0}
is a strong solution of problem (
4.1
) if and only if it is the unique solution in this case. This fact was established recently in
[
20
, Thereom 5.1]
by a different approach.

It is also known that the nuclear norm
g(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{*}
satisfies the quadratic growth condition at
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
for any
Y\in\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}
and second-order regularity at
X_{0}
; see
[
8
,
48
]
, but the radical cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})
is not always closed. As discussed in Remark
3.7
, this radial cone
is closed if and only if

Y\in{\rm ri}\,\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}
.
The existence of such dual certificate
Y
means

\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}\cap{\rm ri}\,\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}\neq\emptyset,

(4.11)

which is known as the
Nondegeneracy Source Condition
introduced in
[
11
,
12
]
for nuclear norm minimization problems. We will add some details about this case in Corollary
4.10
.
\triangle

4.2
Nuclear norm case

Next let us consider the nuclear norm optimization problem:

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}}\quad\|X\|_{*}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\Phi X=M_{0},

(4.12)

where
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear operator (
n_{2}\geq n_{1}
) and
M_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is some known vector. The following characterizations of solution uniqueness for nuclear norm minimization problem (
4.12
) come directly from Theorem
4.5
and the calculation of the radial cone in (
3.20
).

Corollary 4.8

(Solution uniqueness of nuclear norm minimization problem)
.

Let
X_{0}
be an optimal solution of problem (
4.12
). The following are equivalent

(i)

X_{0}
is a unique solution of problem (
4.12
).

(ii)

For any dual certificate
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
, the system

\Phi\left(U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\right)=0\quad\mbox{with}\quad A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}

(4.13)

gives
A=0
and
C=0
, where the pair
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{O}(Y)
comes from the simultaneous ordered singular value decomposition of
X_{0}
and
Y
,
r={\rm rank}\,(X_{0})
, and
p=p(Y)
from (
3.16
).

(iii)

Condition
(ii)
holds at some dual certificate
\overline{Y}\in\Delta(X_{0})
.

Remark 4.9
.

As discussed in Remark
4.6
, solution uniqueness for nuclear norm minimization problem (
4.12
) is characterized via the radical cone in
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
. The above result advances
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
in some ways. The most important thing is that the radical cone is fully calculated, i.e., the general condition (
4.9
) is explicitly expressed by condition (
4.13
) at some dual certificate. This condition can be written and solved as a nonlinear optimization problem; see our previous Remark
3.8
. Moreover, we can pick any dual certificate
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
and check (
4.13
) at
Y
for solution uniqueness at
X_{0}
. A particular dual certificate of problem (
4.12
) can be found by solving a
spectral norm minimization problem

[
20
, Remark 5.5]
. Specifically, suppose that the compact SVD of
X_{0}
is

X_{0}=U_{0}\Sigma_{0}V_{0}^{T},

(4.14)

where
\Sigma_{0}
is the diagonal matrix including all the positive singular values of
X_{0}
with decreasing order and
{\rm rank}\,(\Sigma_{0})=r
, and
U_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r}
and
V_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}
are orthogonal matrices.
Recall the
model tangent space

[
12
]
at
X_{0}
:

\mathbb{T}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{U_{0}Y^{T}+XV_{0}^{T}|\;X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}\}.

(4.15)

Consider the following spectral norm minimization problem

\min_{Z\in\mathbb{T}^{\perp}}\quad\|Z\|\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\mathcal{N}Z=-\mathcal{N}E_{0}\quad\mbox{with}\quad E_{0}=U_{0}V_{0}^{T},

(4.16)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a linear operator satisfying
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\mathcal{N}=\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}
. When
X_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.12
),
[
20
, Remark 5.5]
shows that the optimal value of problem (
4.16
) denoted by
\rho(X_{0})
is smaller than or equal to
1
. Moreover, if
Z_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.16
),
Z_{0}+E_{0}
is a dual certificate of problem (
4.12
); see our Section
5
for further details on Numerical Experiment.
\triangle

The following result is similar to Corollary
3.9
, at which we provide the equivalence between solution uniqueness and strong minima under Nondegenerate Source Condition (
4.11
). It is already discussed in Remark
4.7
.

Corollary 4.10
.

Suppose that
X_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.12
) and Nondegenerate Source Condition (
4.11
) is satisfied. Then
X_{0}
is a strong solution of problem (
4.12
) if and only if it is the unique solution.

Proof.

As discussed in Remark
4.7
, Nondegenerate Source Condition (
4.11
) guarantees closedness of the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})
for some dual certificate
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
. It follows that

\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})=\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0}).

Hence we have

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0}).

By Theorem
4.5
and
[
21
, Theorem 5.2]
,
X_{0}
is a strong solution of problem (
4.12
) if and only if it is the unique solution.

\hfill\Box

The following result gives a necessary condition for solution uniqueness.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
X_{0}
be an optimal solution of problem (
4.12
). If
X_{0}
is a unique solution, then the following Strict Restricted Injectivity holds

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap U_{0}\mathbb{S}^{r}V_{0}^{T}=\{0\}.

(4.17)

If additionally Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) holds, then Strict Restricted Injectivity is also sufficient for solution uniqueness at
X_{0}
.

Proof.

Note from the formula of radial cone (
3.20
) that

U_{0}\mathbb{S}^{r}V_{0}^{T}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})\quad\mbox{for any}\quad Y\in\Delta(X_{0}).

If
X_{0}
a unique solution of problem (
4.12
), we get from Theorem
4.5
that Strict Restricted Injectivity (
4.17
) is satisfied.

Conversely, if Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) holds, there exists
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
such that
Y\in{\rm ri}\,\partial\|X_{0}\|
, which means that
p(Y)=r
. By the formula of radial cone (
3.20
) again, we have

\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})=U_{0}\mathbb{S}^{r}V_{0}^{T}.

It follows from Theorem
4.5
that Strict Restricted Injectivity (
4.17
) is also sufficient for solution uniqueness at
X_{0}
.

\hfill\Box

Strict Restricted Injectivity is introduced recently in
[
21
, Corollary 5.7]
for nuclear norm minimization problem (
4.12
), as a necessary condition for strong minima at
X_{0}
. The above result is quite similar, but slightly stronger as a unique solution of problem (
4.12
) may be not a strong one. The following example taken from
[
21
, Example 5.11]
shows such difference.

Example 4.12

(Difference between unique solution and strong solution in problem (
4.12
))
.

Consider the following optimization problem

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\|X\|_{*}\quad\text{ subject to }\quad\Phi X\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\begin{pmatrix}X_{11}+X_{22}\\
X_{12}-X_{21}+X_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(4.18)

Note that
X_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.18
). Indeed, it is easy to see that

\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}={\rm span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\right\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&[-1,1]\end{pmatrix}.

It follows that

\Delta(X_{0})=\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}\cap\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}\cap{\rm ri}\,\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}=\emptyset.

Thus
X_{0}
is a solution, but Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) is not satisfied. In the view of Remark
4.7
, the latter is well-expected, as we want
X_{0}
to be a unique solution, but not a strong solution in this example.

For
Y_{0}=\mathbb{I}_{2}\in\Delta(X_{0})
, we obtain the radial cone from (
3.20
) and the tangent cone from (
3.26
) respectively

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y_{0})}(X_{0})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&bc\\
bc&c\end{pmatrix}|\;a,b\in\mathbb{R},c\geq 0\right\}\mbox{and}\;\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(Y_{0})}(X_{0})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}|\;a,b\in\mathbb{R},c\geq 0\right\}.

(4.19)

To check condition (
4.13
) or (
4.8
), due to (
4.19
) we solve the system
\Phi\begin{pmatrix}a&bc\\
bc&c\end{pmatrix}=0
with
c\geq 0
, which means

a+c=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad bc-bc+c=0.

This clearly gives us
a=c=0
, i.e., condition (
4.13
) is satisfied. Thus,
X_{0}
is a unique solution of problem (
4.18
).

To check whether
X_{0}
is a strong solution, we have to verify the condition

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{T}_{\partial g^{*}(Y_{0})}(X_{0})=\{0\}

according to
[
21
, Theorem 5.2]
. By (
4.19
), we solve the system
\Phi\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}=0
with
c\geq 0
:

a+c=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad b-b+c=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R},c\geq 0,

which gives us
a=c=0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence the above condition for strong minima at
X_{0}
fails, i.e.,
X_{0}
is not a strong solution of problem (
4.18
).
\triangle

Remark 4.13

(Comparisons with
[
28
]
)
.

The main advantages of using the descent cone in Proposition
4.3
over the radial cone in Theorem
4.5
for solution uniqueness are that we do not need to know if
X_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem
4.12
initially and do not need to find a dual certificate. But the descent cone seems to be bigger and more complicate than the radial cone due to(
4.4
). In the recent paper
[
26
]
, Hoheisel and Paquette introduced the following set

W(X_{0})=\left\{UM\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\left|\;\begin{array}[]{ll}&M\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{1}}_{+}-\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},r},\,{\rm Tr}\,(M)=0,\\
&R\in\mathcal{V}_{n_{1}-r,n_{2}-r},\,M\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&RR^{T}\end{pmatrix}=M\end{array}\right.\right\},

(4.20)

where
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})
, the set
\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},r}
is defined by

\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},r}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{1}}_{+}|\;A\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{++}\right\},

and
\mathcal{V}_{n_{1}-r,n_{2}-r}
is the
Stiefel manifold
:

\mathcal{V}_{n_{1}-r,n_{2}-r}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)}|\;R^{T}R=\mathbb{I}\}.

In particular
[
26
, Corollary 4.1]
shows that
X_{0}
is a unique solution of problem (
4.12
) if and only if
X_{0}
is an optimal solution and

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap W(X_{0})=\{0\}.

(4.21)

The (possibly nonconvex) set
W(X_{0})
seems to be more complicated than our radial set (
3.20
). Computing
W(X_{0})
seems to be complicated in high dimensions, but its span
[
26
, Proposition 3.2]
is rather simple

\displaystyle{\rm span}\,W(X_{0})=\left\{U\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}V^{T}|\;A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{2}-r)},C\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times r},D\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)}\right\}.

(4.22)

Thus a sufficient condition for solution uniqueness at
X_{0}
is

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap{\rm span}\,W(X_{0})=\{0\};

(4.23)

see
[
26
, Corollary 4.2]
. This fact can be obtained directly from Theorem
4.5
as

\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})\subset{\rm span}\,W(X_{0})

for any
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
. The condition (
4.23
) also shows that
X_{0}
is a strong solution of problem (
4.12
) according to
[
21
, Theorem 5.2]
. In our Example
4.12
later, condition (
4.23
) fails, but
X_{0}
is still a unique solution.

When
X_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.12
) as assumed in
[
26
, Corollary 4.1]
, observe from the proof of
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
that

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap W(X_{0})=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap{\rm cone}\,\{X-X_{0}|\;\|X\|_{*}=\|X_{0}\|_{*}\}=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{D}(X_{0}),

(4.24)

where
\mathcal{D}(X_{0})
is the descent cone of nuclear norm at
X_{0}
.
This together Proposition
4.3
explains why condition (
4.21
) ensures the solution uniqueness at
X_{0}
.

Although our condition (
4.13
) depends on the existence of a dual certificate when
X_{0}
is an optimal solution, it is more computable and verifiable than (
4.21
); see also our Section
5
. In the framework of Example
4.12
, the set
W(X_{0})
in (
4.20
) is computed by

\begin{array}[]{ll}W(X_{0})=\left\{M\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&r\end{pmatrix}|\;M\in\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}-\mathcal{F}^{2,1},{\rm Tr}\,(M)=0,r\in\{-1,1\}\right\}.\end{array}

For any
M\in\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}-\mathcal{F}^{2,1}
with
{\rm Tr}\,(M)=0
, we write

M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a-d&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad d>0.

As
{\rm Tr}\,(M)=0
, we have
d=a+c>0
. Thus

M=\begin{pmatrix}-c&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}

Since
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
a+c>0
, if
c=0
then
a>0
and
b=0
. If
c>0
,
b
is arbitrary, as
a
can be chosen sufficiently large. Hence the set of all matrices
M
is

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-c&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}|\;c>0,b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

It follows that

W(X_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-c&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}|\;c>0,b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-c&-b\\
b&-c\end{pmatrix}|\;c>0,b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

This set is nonconvex and completely different from the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y_{0})}(X_{0})
obtained in (
4.19
). Moreover,
\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y_{0})}(X_{0})
is not a subset of
W(X_{0})
and vice versa. This observation highlights our distinct approach for solution uniqueness from
[
26
]
. Nonconvexity of the set
W(X_{0})
may be a challenge when checking condition (
4.21
) in problems with higher dimension.
\triangle

Remark 4.14

(Solution Uniqueness and Sharp Minima)
.

In
[
12
]
Candès and Retch shown that
X_{0}
is a unique solution of problem (
4.12
) if the following
Restricted Injectivity

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathbb{T}=\{0\}

(4.25)

together with Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
)
holds. In the spirit of Theorem
4.5
, this fact is explained as follows. When Restricted Injectivity (
4.25
) and Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) are satisfied, Strict Restricted Injectivity (
4.17
) holds as
U_{0}\mathbb{S}^{r}V_{0}^{T}\subset\mathbb{T}
.
By Corollary
4.11
,
X_{0}
is the unique solution of problem (
4.12
). In
[
26
]
, Hoheisel and Paquette also use the dual version of Restricted Injectivity:

\mbox{\rm Im}\,\Phi^{*}+\mathbb{T}^{\perp}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

together with Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) as sufficient conditions of solution uniqueness.

It is shown in
[
20
, Theorem 4.6]
that the combination of Restricted Injectivity (
4.25
) and Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) indeed characterizes the stronger property,
sharp minima
at
X_{0}

[
14
,
38
]
, which means that there exists some
c>0
such that

\|X\|_{*}-\|X_{0}\|_{*}\geq c\|X-X_{0}\|_{F}\quad\mbox{for any \; $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}$\; satisfying}\quad\Phi X=M_{0}.

In Example
4.12
, we show that a unique solution is not a strong solution, thus not a sharp solution too. Even if Nondegeneracy Source Condition (
4.11
) holds, solution uniqueness and sharp minima of nuclear norm minimization are different; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Example 5.10]
.
\triangle

The next corollary gives the minimum bound for exact recovery. It slightly enhances the recent
[
21
, Corollary 5.8]
by replacing the requirement of strong minima there by solution uniqueness. Its proof based on Strict Restricted Injectivity (
4.17
) is completely the same; so we omit the detail.

Corollary 4.15

(Minimum bound for exact recovery)
.

Suppose that
X_{0}
is an
n_{1}\times n_{2}
matrix with rank
r
. Then one needs at least
\frac{1}{2}r(r+1)
measurements
m
of
M_{0}
so that solving the nuclear norm minimization problem (
4.12
) recovers exactly the unique solution
X_{0}
.

Moreover, there exist infinitely many linear operators
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\frac{1}{2}r(r+1)}
such that
X_{0}
is the unique solution of problem (
4.12
).

The rest of the section is devoted to the computation of the descent cone of the nuclear norm via its first-order information, as it is an important notion used in
[
9
,
1
]
to find statistical dimensions in the theory of exact recovery. According to formula (
4.4
), we also need to know the critical cone (
4.3
) of the nuclear norm:

\mathcal{C}(X_{0})=\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}|\;dg(X_{0})(W)\leq 0\}.

(4.26)

For any
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, we write

W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}V^{T}

with some
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{2}-r)}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r}
, and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)}
. By formula (
3.13
) and (
2.2
), we have

dg(X_{0})(W)=\max_{Y\in\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}}\langle Y,W\rangle={\rm Tr}\,A+\|D\|_{*}.

Combining this with (
4.26
) gives us that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})=\Big\{U\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}V^{T}|&A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{2}-r)},C\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},D\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)},\\
&{\rm Tr}\,A+\|D\|_{*}=0\Big\}.\end{array}

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})=\Big\{U\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}V^{T}|&A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{2}-r)},C\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},D\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)},\\
&{\rm Tr}\,A+\|D\|_{*}<0\Big\}.\end{array}

The following result gives an exact computation of the descent cone for the nuclear norm, which is similar with its formula for
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm established in
[
20
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Proposition 4.16

(Descent cone of nuclear norm)
.

Let
U_{I}\Sigma V_{I}^{T}
be the SVD of
X_{0}
. The descent cone at
X_{0}
is calculated by

\mathcal{D}(X_{0})=\mathcal{E}\cup({\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})),

(4.31)

where

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{E}=\Big\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}|&W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T},A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{1}-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n_{1}-r},\\
&{\rm Tr}\,(A+C)=0,(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})\Big\}.\end{array}

Moreover, if
X_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
4.12
), we have

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{D}(X_{0})=\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\left\{U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}|\;A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r},{\rm Tr}\,(A+C)=0\right\}

for any dual certificate
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
with
p=p(Y)
from (
3.16
) and any pair
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{O}(Y_{0})
.

Proof.

Let us verify the inclusion “
\subset
” in (
4.31
). Pick any
W\in\mathcal{D}(X_{0})\setminus{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
, by formulae (
4.3
) and (
3.20
) there exist
Y\in\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}
and a pair
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{O}(Y_{0})
such that

W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\in\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})

with some
A\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)}
,
C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}
,
p=p(Y)
defined in (
3.16
). Define

\overline{C}=\begin{pmatrix}C&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{1}-r}_{+}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{B}=\begin{pmatrix}B&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{1}-r)}.

We obtain that
W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&\overline{B}\,\overline{C}&0\\
\overline{C}^{T}\overline{B}^{T}&\overline{C}&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\in\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. Note from (
4.2
) that

0={\rm Tr}\,A+\|\overline{C}\|_{*}={\rm Tr}\,A+{\rm Tr}\,\overline{C}.

This tells us that
W\in\mathcal{E}
defined in (
4.16
) and verifies the inclusion “
\subset
” in (
4.31
).

To justifies the converse inclusion in (
4.31
). Pick any
W\in\mathcal{E}
with representation

W=U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}

with some
A\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n_{1}-r)}
, and
C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n_{1}-r}
such that
{\rm Tr}\,(A+C)=0
. By (
4.2
),
W\in\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. Moreover, define
Y=U\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\in\partial\|X_{0}\|_{*}
by (
3.13
). Note from formula (
3.20
),
W\in\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})
. It follows that
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\partial g^{*}(Y)}(X_{0})\cap\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. By (
4.4
), we have the inclusion “
\supset
” in (
4.31
). The equality in (
4.31
) is verified.

The second part of this proposition follows directly from Proposition
4.4
and the formula (
4.2
) for the boundary of the critical cone.

5
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments to illustrate the solution uniqueness for the nuclear norm minimization problem (
4.12
). These experiments are performed for different matrix ranks
r
and numbers of measurements
m
.

In the experiment, we generate
X_{0}
, an
n\times n
matrix of rank
r
, by sampling two factors
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random entries and setting
X_{0}=WH^{*}
. We vectorize problem (
4.12
) in the following form:

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\quad\|X\|_{*}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\Phi\ \text{vec}(X)=\Phi\ \text{vec}(X_{0}),

(5.1)

where
\Phi
is an
{m\times n^{2}}
matrix drawn from the standard Gaussian ensemble. The point
X_{0}
is said to be
recovered
if solving problem (
5.1
) gives us an optimal solution
\overline{X}
that is relatively close to
X_{0}
, in particularly,
\|\overline{X}-X_{0}\|_{F}/\|X_{0}\|_{F}<10^{-3}
as proposed in
[
11
]
. We use cvxpy package to solve this problem to find
\overline{X}
.

In order to check whether
X_{0}
is a unique solution of problem (
5.1
), by Corollary
4.8
and Remark
3.8
, we have to verify that the following nonconvex optimization problem has global maximum value
0
:

\displaystyle\max\;\frac{1}{2}\|A\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{1}{2}\|C\|_{F}^{2}\;\;

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mbox{subject to }\;\;\Phi\left(U\begin{pmatrix}A&BC&0\\
C^{T}B^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\right)=0,A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r},

where
(U,V)\in\mathcal{O}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{O}(Y)
is from (
3.19
) with
Y\in\Delta(X_{0})
being a particular dual certificate computed as in Remark
4.9
,
r={\rm rank}\,(X_{0})
, and
p=p(Y)
from (
3.16
). Indeed, let
\widehat{U}\Sigma\widehat{V}^{T}
be an SVD of
\Phi
,
\widehat{V}_{G}
be the matrix whose columns are the last
n^{2}-r
columns of
V
, and
U_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{0}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V_{0}^{T}
be a full SVD of
X_{0}
. To find a particular certificate of (
5.1
), we solve the following problem by using the
cvxpy
package:

\min_{Z\in\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\perp}}\|Z\|\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad N\text{vec}(Z)=-N\text{vec}(E_{0}),

where
N=V_{G}^{T}
,
E_{0}=U_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V_{0}^{T}
, and
\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\perp}
is known by

\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\perp}=\left\{U_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix}V_{0}^{T}|\;D\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-r)\times(n-r)}\right\}.

Let
\rho(X_{0})
be the optimal value of this problem and
Z_{0}
be an optimal solution. Note also from
[
20
, Proposition 4.4 and Remark 4.5]
,
X_{0}
is an optimal solution of problem (
5.1
) if and only if
\rho(X_{0})\leq 1
. Due to possible errors in numerical computation, we increase this bound to
\rho(X_{0})<1.001
for optimal solutions. It is also known from
[
20
, Section 4]
that
Y=Z_{0}+E_{0}
is a particular dual certificate of (
5.1
).

Back to the maximum problem above, we rewrite it as follows

\displaystyle\max\;\frac{1}{2}\|A\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{1}{2}\|C\|_{F}^{2}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\;\;\Phi\left(U\begin{pmatrix}A&D&0\\
D^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\right)=0,D=E,E=BC,A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(p-r)},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r}.

It is worth noting that this problem takes the format of the one considered in
[
7
]
, for which a multiblock version of ALBUM (Adaptive Lagrangian-Based mUltiplier Method)
[
7
]
, named mALBUM, is investigated. The algorithm mALBUM for solving (
5.3
) iteratively generates a sequence
\{A^{k},C^{k},B^{k},D^{k},E^{k},F^{k},G^{k})\}
by

\displaystyle(A^{k+1},C^{k+1},D^{k+1})\in

\displaystyle\arg\min-\langle A^{k},A\rangle-\langle C^{k},C\rangle-\langle F^{k},D\rangle-\langle G^{k},B^{k}C\rangle+\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\|E^{k}-D\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\|E^{k}-B^{k}C\|^{2}

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\;\;\Phi\left(U\begin{pmatrix}A&D&0\\
D^{T}&C&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{T}\right)=0,A\in\mathbb{S}^{r},C\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p-r},

\displaystyle B^{k+1}\in

\displaystyle\arg\min-\langle G^{k},BC^{k+1}\rangle+\frac{\beta_{k}}{2}\|E^{k}-BC^{k+1}\|^{2},

\displaystyle E^{k+1}=

\displaystyle\left(-F^{k}-G^{k}\right)/(2\beta_{k})+(D^{k+1}+B^{k+1}C^{k+1})/2,

\displaystyle F^{k+1}=

\displaystyle F^{k}+\beta_{k}(E^{k+1}-D^{k+1}),

\displaystyle G^{k+1}=

\displaystyle G^{k}+\beta_{k}(E^{k+1}-B^{k+1}C^{k+1}).

Here, the parameter
\beta
is updated adaptively by using the following rule:
if

\|E^{k+1}-D^{k+1}\|+\|E^{k+1}-B^{k+1}C^{k+1}\|>M\quad\mbox{ for some large constant $M$},

then
\beta_{k+1}\leftarrow\nu\beta_{k}
with some constant
\nu>0
; otherwise,
\beta_{k+1}\leftarrow\beta_{k}
. In our experiment, we set
\nu=1.2
,
M=10^{5}
, and
\beta_{0}=1
. We use the
cvxpy
package to solve sub-problems.

To start the algorithm, the initial values of
(A^{0},C^{0},B^{0},D^{0},E^{0},F^{0},G^{0})
are chosen as follows:
A^{0}=(V_{1}+V_{1}^{T})
, where
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is drawn from the standard Gaussian ensemble,
B^{0}
is drawn from the standard Gaussian ensemble,
C^{0}=V_{2}V_{2}^{T}
, where
V_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-r)\times(p-r)}
is drawn from the standard Gaussian ensemble,
D^{0}=E^{0}=B^{0}C^{0},F^{0}=0
, and
G^{0}=0
.

We classify solution uniqueness at
X_{0}
if
\|\overline{A}\|+\|\overline{C}\|<10^{-5}
and
\rho(X_{0})<1.001
, where
\overline{A}
and
\overline{C}
are outputs of mALBUM.
To demonstrate the occurrence of solution uniqueness for problem (
5.1
), we create graphs that show the proportion of solution uniqueness with respect to the number of measurements in Figures
1
. For each fixed value of
n=40
and
2\leq r\leq 7
, we conduct a simulation study on 100 random instances at each number of measurements
m
. At each
m
, the proportion of cases (among
100
) of
X_{0}
that are recovered by solving problem (
5.1
) are depicted as the red curve
[
11
]
. The percentage of cases of
X_{0}
that are unique solutions of problem (
5.1
) based on the above classification is displayed as the blue curve. It is natural to see that all solutions

that can be recovered by solving problem (
