Fractional Topological Charges in 2D Magnets
Nina del Ser, Imane El Achchi, Achim Rosch

Abstract
Magnetic skyrmions and antiskyrmions are characterised by an integer topological charge 𝒬 = ∓ 1 \mathcal{Q}=\mp 1 , describing the winding of the magnetic orientation. Half-integer winding numbers, 𝒬 = ± 1 2 \mathcal{Q}=\pm\frac{1}{2} , can be obtained for magnetic vortices (merons). Here, we discuss the physics of magnets with fractional topological charge which is neither integer nor half-integer. We argue that in ferromagnetic films with cubic anisotropy, textures with 𝒬 = ± 1 6 \mathcal{Q}=\pm\frac{1}{6} or ± 1 8 \pm\frac{1}{8} arise naturally when three or more magnetic domains meet. We also show that a single magnetic skyrmion with 𝒬 = − 1 \mathcal{Q}=-1 can explode into four fractional defects, each carrying charge 𝒬 = − 1 4 \mathcal{Q}=-\frac{1}{4} . Additionally, we investigate a point defect with a non-quantised fractional charge ( 𝒬 ≠ n m , n , m ∈ ℤ \mathcal{Q}\neq\frac{n}{m},n,m\in\mathbb{Z} ) which can move parallel to a magnetic domain wall. Only defects with fractional charge lead to an Aharonov-Bohm effect for magnons. We investigate the resulting forces on a fractional defect due to magnon currents.

Magnetic skyrmions

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})/|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})|

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})

\mathcal{Q}

\rho_{\text{top}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\differential^{2}{r}\rho_{\text{top}}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\rho_{\text{top}}(\mathbf{r})=\hat{\mathbf{M}}\cdot(\nabla_{x}\hat{\mathbf{M}}\times\nabla_{y}\hat{\mathbf{M}}),

(1)

with

Here,

measures the solid angle covered by the vectors
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{r})

Any system which has periodic boundary conditions or is smoothly embedded into a ferromagnetic background will have an integer winding number,
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}

4\pi

Non-integer winding numbers of point-like defects can be obtained by considering magnetic textures with non-trivial (non-ferromagnetic) orientations far away from the origin. A prime example are so-called merons

;
Ezawa 2011
;
Yu et al. 2018
;
Göbel et al. 2021

These are magnetic vortices, realised, for example, in magnets with an easy-plane anisotropy. Far from the centre of the meron, the magnetisation winds around the equator of the unit sphere. Close to the centre, the magnetisation points either up or down.
Thus,
\hat{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{r})
covers half of the unit sphere, giving rise to a topological charge of either
\mathcal{Q}=+\frac{1}{2}
or
\mathcal{Q}=-\frac{1}{2}
, respectively.
Merons play an important role in the stability of magnetic skyrmions.
When the stabilising magnetic field is lowered, a skyrmion embedded in a ferromagnetic background becomes unstable and may decay into a pair of merons, each of which carries
\mathcal{Q}=-\frac{1}{2}
, or exactly half of the skyrmion’s original integer winding number
\mathcal{Q}=-1

Ezawa 2011
;
Schütte and Garst 2014
.

Recently, another class of defects with fractional charge has also been considered: fractional charges which occur close to the boundary of the sample
Hirosawa et al. 2020
;
Parkin et al. 2021
. In this case, the absence of quantisation arises from the restricted area of integration in Eq. (
1
). Another possibility is to form a lattice of fractional charges, as we predicted should occur in the quantum Hall phase of twisted bilayer graphene
Bömerich et al. 2020
. In this case, we found that gate voltages stabilise a so-called double tetarton lattice, consisting of topological defects covering one quarter of the unit sphere twice, but the charge per magnetic unit cell remains quantised to an integer. We will not consider either of these cases in the following, instead focusing solely on isolated fractional charges which form in the bulk of magnetic films.

The presence of a topological winding number has profound effects on the stability of magnetic textures, the possibilities to manipulate them and on their interaction with electrons and magnons. Skyrmions, for example, can change their winding number only via singular spin configurations
Heil et al. 2019
. Perhaps more importantly, magnons and electrons pick up Berry phases when following a magnetic texture. The physical effect of the Berry phase is best described as an emergent electromagnetic field
Volovik 1987
;
Zang et al. 2011
;
Schulz et al. 2012
, given by

\displaystyle B_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\hbar}{e}\rho_{\text{top}}(\mathbf{r}).

(2)

Here, we use a convention where majority or minority electrons carry an emergent charge
q=\mp\frac{1}{2}e
, respectively, while
q=e
for magnons, which can be obtained by flipping a majority to a minority electron. The electron charge
e
in these formulas has been introduced such that
B_{\text{eff}}
has units of Tesla, but it has no physical meaning as it drops out of the product
qB_{\text{eff}}
. In units of the flux quantum
\Phi_{0}=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{q}
, the total flux is given by
\mp\mathcal{Q}
for majority(minority) electrons and
2\mathcal{Q}
for magnons. Incoming magnons or electrons will generally be scattered by this emergent magnetic flux. However, a fundamental distinction between integer and fractional
\Phi
occurs if the incoming particles have low energy and momentum
k
. In this case, Aharonov and Bohm
Aharonov and Bohm 1959
showed that the scattering cross-sections drop to zero for integer
\Phi
, because such a flux can be removed by a gauge transformation. For fractional
\mathcal{Q}
, however, the cross section grows
singularly
with
1/k
.

In the following, we will first point out that fractional topological textures occur frequently in standard ferromagnetic systems and give several examples. Then, we will show how a skyrmion may be triggered to explode into smaller fractionally charged topological defects. Finally, we will explore the scattering of magnons from fractional textures and the Aharonov-Bohm effect for magnons.

Textures with fractional topological charge —
Arguably the simplest way to generate a fractional topological charge
\mathcal{Q}
is to consider a magnetic vortex in a two-dimensional easy-plane magnet in the presence of an out-of-plane magnetic field. The energy density of such a system is given by

\mathcal{F}=\frac{A}{2}(\nabla\hat{\mathbf{M}})^{2}+\kappa_{u}\hat{M}_{z}^{2}-b\hat{M}_{z},

(3)

where
A
is the spin stiffness of the ferromagnet,
\kappa_{u}>0
is the anisotropy energy density and
b=B|\mathbf{M}|
parametrizes the strength of the external magnetic field. Note that as we are in 2D,
\nabla=(\nabla_{x},\nabla_{y},0)^{T}
throughout the paper.
Due to the interplay of anisotropy and magnetic field, the magnetisation in the ferromagnetic phase is tilted, and can lie anywhere on the cone defined by the angle

\theta_{\text{FM}}=\arccos[b/(2\kappa_{u})]
, relative to the
z
-axis. In this case, magnetic vortices and antivortices have topological charge

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\pm\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\phi}\int_{0}^{\theta_{\text{FM}}}\differential{\theta}\sin\theta=\pm\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{|b|}{4\kappa_{u}}\right).

(4)

For
b=0
, one obtains merons with
\mathcal{Q}=\pm 1/2
, whereas for finite
|b|<2\kappa_{u}
, the charge takes smaller values. We will argue below that
the properties of such a state change qualitatively when
|\mathcal{Q}|
is not a multiple of
1/2
. Recently, fractional vortices have also been investigated as so-called “screw dislocations” in helical magnets, see
Azhar et al. 2022
for some of their more exotic manifestations.

Cubic magnets —

For
b=0
, fractional charges arise naturally in two-dimensional ferromagnets with cubic anisotropy
described by the energy density

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\frac{A}{2}(\nabla\hat{\mathbf{M}})^{2}-\kappa_{c}(\hat{M}_{x}^{4}+\hat{M}_{y}^{4}+\hat{M}_{z}^{4}).

(5)

For
\kappa_{c}>0
, there are six domains (easy axes) with orientation
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{FM}}\in\{(\pm 1,0,0)^{T},(0,\pm 1,0)^{T},(0,0,\pm 1)^{T}\}

At a
90^{\circ}
domain wall, e.g. from
(1,0,0)^{T}
to
(0,1,0)^{T}

(1,1,0)^{T}/\sqrt{2}

\hat{M}_{z}=0
throughout. This is guaranteed by the product of time-reversal symmetry and a
180^{\circ}
rotation symmetry about the
z

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(x)=(\cos(\phi(x)),\sin(\phi(x)),0)^{T}
, with
\phi(x)=\arctan(e^{\sqrt{\frac{4 \kappa_c}{A}} (x-x_0)})

;
Wheeler 2015

∘

(1,0,0)^{T}
and
(-1,0,0)^{T}

∘

∘

\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{FM}1}=(0,0,1)^{T}

\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{FM}2}=(0,1,0)^{T}

\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{FM}3}=(1,0,0)^{T}

The three domains smoothly transform into each other as we move anticlockwise around their common meeting point. Far away from the defect, the magnetisation interpolates exactly along the great arc connecting the domain orientations, as discussed above.
This implies that the magnetisation covers exactly
1/8
of the unit-sphere and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\pm\frac{1}{8}.

Exploding skyrmion —
Another way to generate quantised fractionally charged defects is via skyrmion explosion. To show this, we consider the model

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=

\displaystyle\frac{A}{2}(\nabla\hat{\mathbf{M}})^{2}+D(\hat{M}_{z}(\nabla\cdot\hat{\mathbf{M}})-(\hat{\mathbf{M}}\cdot\nabla)\hat{M}_{z})

\displaystyle-\kappa_{c}(\hat{M}_{x}^{4}+\hat{M}_{y}^{4}+\hat{M}_{z}^{4})-b(t)\hat{M}_{z}

(7)

\kappa_{c}=3.2

Mobile fractional defects within symmetry-broken domain walls —

A different class of defects can be obtained by investigating topological textures occurring as a “domain wall within a domain wall”. Here, we consider a 2D system with easy-plane anisotropy terms, an external magnetic field and DMI interactions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2}(\nabla\hat{\mathbf{M}})^{2}+\frac{A_{\text{ani}}}{2}(\nabla_{y}\hat{\mathbf{M}})^{2}+\kappa_{p}(\hat{M}_{x}^{4}+\hat{M}_{y}^{4})

\displaystyle+\kappa_{u}\hat{M}_{z}^{2}-b\hat{M}_{z}+D(\hat{M}_{z}(\nabla\cdot\hat{\mathbf{M}})-(\hat{\mathbf{M}}\cdot\nabla)\hat{M}_{z}),

(8)

where, to help stabilise the system once magnon currents are added, we also added some anisotropy to the Heisenberg coupling by introducing
A_{\text{ani}}
(see App.

In the case
\kappa_{p},\kappa_{u}>0

\phi_{\text{FM}}=\frac{\pi}{4}+n\frac{\pi}{2},n\in\{0,1,2,3\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\theta_{\text{FM}}(b,\kappa_{p},\kappa_{u}),&0\leq|b|\leq 2\kappa_{u},\\

(1,-1)^{T}

This fractional defect has a property which makes it different from all the others discussed before: it can move at zero energy cost parallel to the domain wall using only

Aharonov-Bohm scattering —
What distinguishes defects with a fractional charge from skyrmions (and merons) with an integer (half-integer) winding number? A key difference is the Aharonov-Bohm effect. The standard Aharonov-Bohm effect describes how a charged particle picks up a quantum-mechanical phase when encircling
a magnetic flux, despite never coming in contact with the magnetic field.
As discussed in the introductory section, a topological defect with charge
\mathcal{Q}
acts like an effective magnetic field with flux
2\mathcal{Q}\Phi_{0}
, where
\Phi_{0}
is the flux quantum. If
2\mathcal{Q}
is integer, there is no Aharonov-Bohm effect: an integer flux can be gauged away. In contrast, for fractional
\mathcal{Q}
, one obtains, for
k\to 0
, a highly singular differential cross section, as first calculated by Aharonov and Bohm
Aharonov and Bohm 1959
.

One way to observe this effect is to track the force on the defect in the presence of a magnon current
j_{m}
. The force perpendicular to
j_{m}
is computed (in the limit of weak damping) from the change of magnon momentum due to scattering
Lekner 2005
,

\displaystyle F_{\perp}=\hbar k\,j_{m}\,\sigma_{\perp},\qquad\sigma_{\perp}={\int}\mathrm{d}\chi(-\sin\chi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\chi},

(10)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\chi}
is the differential cross section and
\hbar k
is the magnon momentum. If the wavelength of magnons is short compared to the size
R_{d}
of the defect,
kR_{d}\gg 1
, the differential cross section can be calculated from the classical Lorentz force
q(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})
, while for
kR_{d}\ll 1
we use the Aharonov-Bohm result
Aharonov and Bohm 1959
, see App.
C
. Therefore, we approximate

\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}

\displaystyle\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\sin( 4 \pi\mathcal Q)}{k}T_{k}&\quad{\text{for}}\quad kR_{d}\ll 1,\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{4\pi\mathcal{Q}}{k}&\quad{\text{for}}\quad kR_{d}\gg 1,\end{array}\right.

where the transmission rate
T_{k}
,
0\leq T_{k}\leq 1
, is a correction factor which we add to take into account that only those magnons which are transmitted through the domain walls contribute to Aharonov-Bohm scattering, see App.
C
for details. While the Aharonov-Bohm effect is periodic in the flux, the Lorentz force is simply linear in
\mathcal{Q}
.
The smooth cross-over between the two limits has been discussed in the PhD thesis of Vivek Lohani
Lohani 2022
. Importantly, the sign of
\sigma_{\perp}
changes as a function of
k
for
1/2<2\mathcal{|}Q|<1
. The Aharonov-Bohm effect and Lorentz force have opposite signs in this range of
|\mathcal{Q}|
, and thus low- and high-energy magnons scatter in opposite directions. This effect will occur even if
T_{k}
becomes small for small
k
.
Therefore, we can use a sign change in the force as a smoking gun signature of Aharonov-Bohm scattering, which is only activated for fractionally charged defects.

4
(c) shows the result of numerical simulations for the “domain wall in a domain wall” defect shown in Fig.

(a), where a magnon current is generated by inducing an oscillating magnetisation
\delta\mathbf{M}(t)

v_{\perp}\propto F_{\perp}
. The velocity shows the expected sign change when one crosses over from the Aharonov-Bohm regime (driving frequency
\omega
close to the gap of the ferromagnet) to the Lorentz-force regime (larger
\omega
).

Conclusions —
In our paper, we have argued that magnetic textures with fractional topological charge are not rare: they naturally occur, for example, in magnetic vortices in a transverse magnetic field or when three ferromagnetic domain walls meet in systems with cubic anisotropy. Additionally, if a magnetic skyrmions splits into four pieces upon lowering the magnetic field, each piece obtains
1/4
of the initial topological charge. Finally, we discussed a mobile fractionally charged defect arising from a domain wall within a domain wall. In some of the cases above, the fractional topological charge took rational values like
1/6
or
1/8
, which was guaranteed by additional symmetries.

Two related features distinguish fractional topological textures from their integer counterparts. First, the magnetisation far away from a fractional defect is necessarily non-collinear. For example, several domain walls meet at the location of the defect. Second, magnons pick up a Berry phase
4\pi\mathcal{Q}
when moving around the defect, giving rise to an Aharonov-Bohm effect when
\mathcal{Q}
is neither integer nor half-integer. This phase is picked up when the magnon traverses the domain walls. The interference of magnons can then lead to an effective
Aharonov-Bohm force
on the defect.
Here, it is most interesting to consider defects with
\frac{1}{4}<\mathcal{|}Q|<\frac{1}{2}
, as in this case Lorentz and Aharonov-Bohm forces have opposite signs.

For the future, it will be interesting to investigate the coupled dynamics of magnons and fractionally charged textures. How can one obtain bound states or lattices of fractionally charged defects and how do they interact with magnon currents? We expect that the fractional emergent flux and the resulting Aharonov-Bohm effect will play an important role in such settings.

Appendix C
Transport cross sections

To compute quantities such as spin and thermal conductivities or forces on magnetic textures arising from magnon scattering, one has to keep track of the change in momentum of magnons when an incoming magnon with
\mathbf{k}_{\text{in}}=k(1,0)
is scattered to
\mathbf{k}_{\text{out}}=k(\cos\chi,\sin\chi)
. The rate of change of momentum is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\Delta\mathbf{P}}{dt}=-j_{m}\,\hbar k\,\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{\|}\\
\sigma_{\perp}\end{pmatrix},

(15)

where
j_{m}
is the magnon current. The transport cross sections
\sigma_{\parallel}
and
\sigma_{\perp}
are computed from the differential cross section using

\displaystyle\sigma_{\parallel}

\displaystyle={\int}\differential{\chi}(1-\cos\chi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\chi}\thinspace,

(16)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}

\displaystyle={\int}\differential{\chi}(-\sin\chi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\chi}.

Eq. (
15
) is valid in a limit where magnon damping can be neglected, see App.
E
for a calculation in the presence of a finite Gilbert damping
\alpha
.

In the limit of large momentum, one can simply calculate the cross section from classical physics
Schroeter and Garst 2015
.
Incoming charged particles with a high velocity are deflected very little, thus their trajectory is well approximated by
\mathbf{r}(t)=(vt,y_{0})
. The total momentum transfer per particle is then given by

\Delta\mathbf{P}=\int\mathop{dt}q(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})
. Multiplying this with the current of incoming particles, one obtains the following rate of change of momentum for
\mathbf{B}=(0,0,B(\mathbf{r}))
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\Delta P_{\perp}}{dt}=-j_{m}\int\differential{y_0}\int\differential{t}\,q\,v\,B(\mathbf{r}(t))=j_{m}\int\differential^{2}{r}\,q\,B(\mathbf{r}),

(17)

and therefore, in this large-
k
limit,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}\approx\frac{q\Phi}{\hbar k}=\frac{2\pi(\Phi/\Phi_{0})}{k},

(18)

where
\Phi
is the total flux and
\Phi_{0}=2\pi\hbar/q
is the flux quantum. In contrast,
\sigma_{\|}
is quadratic in the external field and vanishes within the approximation used above.

In the opposite limit of
k\to 0
, the wavelength is much larger than the size of the defect. Thus, one has to study the Aharonov-Bohm setup, where
B(\mathbf{r})=\Phi\delta^{2}(\mathbf{r})
. In this case, one obtains

\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sin(2 \pi\Phi/\Phi_0)}{k}.

(19)

The derivation of Eq. (
19
) is remarkably subtle and has been the source of major controversies in the literature

Wexler and Thouless 1998
;
Nielsen and Hedegård 1995
;
Berry 1999
;
Keating and Robbins 2001
;
Shelankov 1998
. Technically, the problem arises from the treatment of the (highly singular) forward scattering. This problem can, e.g., be resolved by studying
the scattering of finite-size wave packets or of a beam of incoming particles of finite width
Shelankov 1998
. Alternatively, one can analyse the scattering problem of an incoming plane wave at
finite
distance from the scattering centre
Nielsen and Hedegård 1995
.
In all these cases, it is essential to correctly include the interference of incoming and scattered waves for
\chi=0
to obtain the correct formula for
\sigma_{\perp}
(there is no such problem from
\sigma_{\|}
). Only when the AB scattering problem is correctly regularised for
\chi\to 0
, does one find that the classical result, Eq. (
18
), and the quantum result, Eq. (
19
), coincide for
\Phi\to 0
.

The discussion given above assumed a charged particle coupled to a magnetic field
(0,0,B({\bm{r}}))
. We have, however, also considered other contributions. Magnon number is not a conserved quantity; therefore, magnons can be absorbed, or a high-energy magnon can decay into a pair of low-energy magnons. In the limit of vanishing Gilbert damping,
\alpha\to 0
, magnon absorption is forbidden by energy conservation. Inelastic decay is also not possible by energy conservation if the magnon energy
E_{m}
is in the energy window
\hbar\Omega_{g}<E_{m}<2\hbar\Omega_{g}
. We can thus ignore magnon absorption in the small-
\alpha
limit, when the magnon mean-free path is large compared to the size of the domain, see App
E
for an analytical calculation of magnon currents for finite
\alpha
.

Besides the effects from
B({\bm{r}})
, one has to take into account that the presence of the magnetic texture will lead to an effective potential,
V({\bm{r}})
, for magnons
Schütte and Garst 2014
. The classical result Eq. (
18
) is not affected by
V({\bm{r}})
in the large-
k
(classical) limit, as in this case the kinetic energy is large compared to
V({\bm{r}})
. The small-
k
limit, Eq. (
19
), is more subtle. Here, the cross section arises from the interference of magnons passing the defect far away from the defect centre. However, far from the defect centre the magnons have to cross a domain wall, see Fig. 4(a).
Only magnons which are transmitted through the domain wall can contribute to the Aharonov-Bohm effect and thus to the transverse force on the defect. Therefore, we multiply the formula for the cross section in Eq. (
Fractional Topological Charges in 2D Magnets
) by the transmission rate
T_{k}
through the domain wall, which may vanish for
k\to 0
.

Appendix E
Fractional flux driven by magnon currents: analytics

In this section, we derive analytically for a strongly simplified toy model how the force on a magnetic texture can be obtained via perturbation theory directly from the LLG equation. As described below, we study a simplified rotationally invariant magnon scattering problem, where an effective magnetic flux is added “by hand”, see below for details.
The analytical calculation can be used to check some of the assumptions underlying Eq. (
10
), including the effects of a
small Gilbert damping.

For the sake of our calculation, it is mathematically more convenient to consider the dynamics in our system as being induced by an oscillating external magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{1}(\mathbf{r},t)
, where
B_{1}\sim\delta M/M_{0}
. In the limit of weak driving,
\epsilon=\delta M/M_{0}\ll 1
, the following perturbative ansatz accurately describes
del Ser and Lohani 2023
the time-dependent magnetisation,

\hat{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}-\epsilon^{2}\mathbf{V}\,t)+\epsilon\mathbf{M}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(21)

where
\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(0)}
is the equilibrium (normalised) magnetisation,
\mathbf{M}^{(1)}
is the linear response and
\mathbf{V}
is the constant linear velocity of the magnetic texture. As has been discussed in Ref.
del Ser and Lohani 2023
, it is sufficient to compute oscillations of the magnetisation linearly in
\epsilon
to determine the velocity to order
\epsilon^{2}
. Substituting Eq. (
21
) into the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation, crossing with
\mathbf{M}
, projecting onto
\nabla\mathbf{M}
and integrating over space, see
del Ser and Lohani 2023
App.B for technical details, we arrive at an effective Thiele

\mathbf{G}\crossproduct\mathbf{V}+\alpha\mathcal{D}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{F},

(22)

where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{e}_{z}\frac{M_{0}}{|\gamma|}4\pi\mathcal{Q}
is the gyrocoupling vector,
\mathcal{D}_{ij}=\frac{M_{0}}{|\gamma|}\int\differential^{2}{r}\,\nabla_{i}\hat{\mathbf{M}}\cdot\nabla_{i}\hat{\mathbf{M}}
is the dissipation matrix,
\gamma=qg/(2m)
is the gyromagnetic ratio and
\mathbf{F}
is the force divided by the sample thickness, defined by

\displaystyle F_{i}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta M}{M_{0}}\right)^{2}\frac{M_{0}}{|\gamma|}\Big\langle\int\differential^{2}{r}[\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(0)}\cdot(\dot{\mathbf{M}}^{(1)}\crossproduct\nabla_{i}\mathbf{M}^{(1)})

(23)

\displaystyle+\alpha\dot{\mathbf{M}}^{(1)}\cdot\nabla_{i}\mathbf{M}^{(1)}-|\gamma|\mathbf{B}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{i}\mathbf{M}^{(1)}]\Big\rangle_{T}.

The time averaging over one period of the drive,
T=2\pi/\Omega

is constant in time.

Let us consider Eq. (
22

4

scales proportional to the length of the system in the

If we now wanted to calculate
