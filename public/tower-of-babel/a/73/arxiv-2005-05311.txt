Completeness and injectivity
Soichiro Fujii

Abstract
We show that for any quantale 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} , a 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} -category is skeletal and complete if and only if it is injective with respect to fully faithful 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} -functors. This is a special case of known theorems due to Hofmann and Stubbe, but we provide a different proof, using the characterisation of the MacNeille completion of a 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} -category as its injective envelope. For Lawvere metric spaces, our results yield those of Kemajou, Künzi and Otafudu. We point out that their notion of Isbell convexity can be seen as a geometric formulation of categorical completeness for Lawvere metric spaces.

Introduction

The main purpose of this paper is to present a theorem claiming the equivalence between
completeness
and
injectivity
in the context of
quantale-enriched categories
.
In order to convey the idea of the theorem, we start with describing two classical theorems in order theory and metric space theory, and then a variant of the latter for directed metric spaces; the first and third of them are instances of our theorem (modulo minor modifications).

Theorem 1.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

A poset is a complete lattice if and only if it is injective.

Recall that a poset
{\mathcal{E}}
is a

if it has all suprema (or equivalently all infima) of subsets of
{\mathcal{E}}
. On the other hand, a poset
{\mathcal{E}}
(whose partial order relation we denote by
\preceq_{\mathcal{E}}
) is said to be
injective
if, whenever we have posets
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
, a monotone map
f\colon{\mathcal{C}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}
(i.e., a function such that for all
c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}

c\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}c^{\prime}
implies
f(c)\preceq_{\mathcal{E}}f(c^{\prime})
), and an order embedding
i\colon{\mathcal{C}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
(i.e., a function such that
c\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}c^{\prime}
if and only if
i(c)\preceq_{\mathcal{D}}i(c^{\prime})

g\colon{\mathcal{D}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}
making the diagram

{\mathcal{C}}
{\mathcal{D}}
{\mathcal{E}}
f
i

Notice that the property of being a complete lattice is described totally in terms of the

structure of a poset, whereas that of being injective is formulated solely in terms of its

The fascination of this theorem due to Banaschewski and Bruns lies in the fact that it connects an internal property with an external one.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
2
]

A metric space is hyperconvex if and only if it is injective.

A metric space

Let us elaborate the definition. One can view each pair

The existence of a point

For example,

The definition of injectivity for metric spaces parallels that for posets. A metric space

and
{\mathcal{D}}

Again, this theorem of Aronszajn and Panitchpakdi is interesting in that it relates the internal property of hyperconvexity with the external one of injectivity.

1.2

2
Quantales

We first introduce
quantales

[
22
]
, also known as
complete
idempotent semirings

[
6
,
20
]
.
They are the enriching (or base) categories
in the portion of enriched category theory we shall be concerned with.

Definition 2.1
.

A (unital)
quantale

{\mathcal{Q}}

is a complete lattice
(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}})

equipped with a monoid structure
(Q,I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{\mathcal{Q}})

such that the multiplication
\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}
preserves arbitrary suprema in each variable:

(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i})\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x=\bigvee_{i\in I}(y_{i}\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x)
and

y\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i})=\bigvee_{i\in I}(y\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x_{i})
.
We often omit the subscript
{\mathcal{Q}}
from the data of a quantale,
writing it simply as
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq,I,\circ)
.

Notice that in the definition of quantale,
we do not assume commutativity of the multiplication
\circ
by default;
quantales with commutative multiplication are said to be

commutative
.

The notion of
adjunction
is central to category theory. In this paper we shall only need the particularly simple case of adjunctions between posets, also known as
Galois connections
.
Recall that given posets
(L,\preceq)
and
(L^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
,
two functions
f\colon L\longrightarrow L^{\prime}

and
u\colon L^{\prime}\longrightarrow L
are said to form an
adjunction

if, for any
l\in L
and
l^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}
,

f(l)\preceq^{\prime}l^{\prime}\iff l\preceq u(l^{\prime})

(1)

holds.
We call
f
the
left adjoint
of
u
and
u
the
right
adjoint
of
f
,
and write them as
f\dashv u
.
The adjointness relation (
1
) is powerful enough to
determine each of the functions
f
and
u
from the other, and force both of
them to be
monotone
functions
[
27
]
.

We record the following well-known fact.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(L,\preceq)
be a complete lattice and
(L^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
be a poset.
A function
f\colon L\longrightarrow L^{\prime}
preserves arbitrary suprema
if and only if there exists a function
u\colon L^{\prime}\longrightarrow L

such that
f\dashv u
.

As the first application of Proposition
2.2
,
observe that in any quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq,

I,\circ)

there are two
residuation
operations:
for any
x\in Q
, the function
(-)\circ x\colon Q\longrightarrow Q

preserves arbitrary suprema, and hence has a right adjoint

(-)\mathbin{\swarrow}x\colon Q\longrightarrow Q

called the
right extension along
x
;
similarly, for any
y\in Q
the function

y\circ(-)
has a right adjoint
y\mathbin{\searrow}(-)
,
called the
right lifting along
y
.
Of course, in a commutative quantale the right extensions and right liftings
coincide.
The defining adjointness relations are:

y\preceq z\mathbin{\swarrow}x\iff y\circ x\preceq z\iff x\preceq y\mathbin{\searrow}z.

(2)

Focusing on the leftmost and rightmost formulas of
(
2
), we obtain

z\mathbin{\swarrow}x\succeq y\iff x\preceq y\mathbin{\searrow}z.

That is,
z\mathbin{\swarrow}(-)\colon Q\longrightarrow Q
(regarded as a
function from the poset
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq)
to its
dual
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}=(Q,\succeq)
) is the left
adjoint of
(-)\mathbin{\searrow}z\colon Q\longrightarrow{Q}
(from
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}
to
{\mathcal{Q}}
)
for any
z\in Q
.
The three types of adjunctions

{\mathcal{Q}}
{\mathcal{Q}}
(-)\circ x
(-)\mathbin{\swarrow}x
\dashv

{\mathcal{Q}}
{\mathcal{Q}}
y\circ(-)
y\mathbin{\searrow}(-)
\dashv

{\mathcal{Q}}
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}
z\mathbin{\swarrow}(-)
(-)\mathbin{\searrow}z
\dashv

are fundamental in the theory of quantales.

We conclude this section with some examples of quantales.

Example 2.3

(
[
19
]
)
.

The two-element quantale
{\mathbf{2}}=(\{\bot,\top\},\vdash,\top,\wedge)
.
The underlying poset of this quantale consists of
\top
for “truth” and
\bot
for “falsity”, ordered by the entailment relation
\vdash
, so that
\bot\vdash\top
.
The monoid structure is given by conjunction

.

Example 2.4

(
[
19
]
)
.

The
Lawvere quantale

{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}=([0,\infty],\geq,0,+)
.
Here,
([0,\infty],\geq)
is the poset
([0,\infty),\geq)
of all nonnegative real numbers ordered by the
opposite

\geq
of the usual order
\leq
, extended with the
least
element
\infty
.
The

operation is the extension of addition for nonnegative real numbers to
[0,\infty]
so that
x+\infty=\infty+x=\infty
for all
x\in[0,\infty]
. (This extension is forced by the axioms of quantale.)
The right extension/right lifting is given by an extension of the

truncated subtraction
\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}
, defined by

u\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}t=\max\{u-t,0\}
for nonnegative real numbers

and
u
.
Precisely, for
y,z\in[0,\infty]
,

z\mathbin{\swarrow}y=y\mathbin{\searrow}z=\begin{cases}z\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}y&\text{ if }y,z\in[0,\infty)\\
0&\text{ if }y=\infty\\
\infty&\text{ if }z=\infty\text{ and }y\in[0,\infty).\end{cases}

This quantale is introduced in
[
19
]
for a categorical approach to the theory of metric spaces.

Example 2.5

(
[
19
]
)
.

{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{max}}}=([0,\infty],\geq,0,\max)
.
Its underlying poset
([0,\infty],\geq)
is the same as that of
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}
. We take the binary max operation with respect to the usual ordering
\leq
, namely the binary meet operation with respect to
\geq
, as the multiplication. The right extension/right lifting is given by

z\mathbin{\swarrow}y=y\mathbin{\searrow}z=\begin{cases}0\ \text{ if }y\geq z\\
z\ \text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This quantale is related to (a generalisation of) ultrametric spaces.

We remark that more generally, any
locale
, i.e., a complete lattice in which the binary meet operation
\wedge
satisfies the infinitary distributive law
(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i})\wedge x=\bigvee_{i\in I}(y_{i}\wedge x)
, acquires a quantale structure with

as the multiplication;
indeed quantales were first introduced as a quantum theoretic generalisation of locales
[
22
]
.
The poset
([0,\infty],\geq)
, or more generally any totally ordered complete lattice, is a locale.

Example 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,e,\cdot)
be a monoid. The

free quantale generated by
{\mathcal{M}}

is
\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{M}}=(\mathcal{P}M,{\subseteq},\{e\},\cdot)
,
where
\mathcal{P}M
is the power set of
M
and the multiplication

on

is the unique supremum-preserving extension of the original multiplication on

, which is given by

A\cdot B=\{\,a\cdot b\mid a\in A,b\in B\,\}

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}M
.
Unlike the previous examples, this quantale is not commutative unless

is.

Example 2.7
.

Let
A
be a set. The poset
(\mathcal{P}(A\times A),\subseteq)

of all binary relations on

admits a quantale structure

(\mathcal{P}(A\times A),\subseteq,I_{A},\circ)
, where

denotes the diagonal relation on

This quantale is not commutative in general.

3
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories

In this section, we introduce
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories for a quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}
. They are instances of the well-established notion of enriched category
[
15
]
.

Throughout the rest of this paper,
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}},I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{\mathcal{Q}})
denotes an arbitrary quantale, unless otherwise specified.

Definition 3.1
.

A
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category

{\mathcal{C}}
consists of:

(CD1)

a set
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
of
objects
;

(CD2)

for each
c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
, an element
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\in{Q}

satisfying the following axioms:

(CA1)

for each
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
I_{\mathcal{Q}}\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c)
;

(CA2)

for each
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime\prime})
.

We also write
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
for
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
.

Example 3.2
.

In the case
{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathbf{2}}
, we may identify the data of a
{\mathbf{2}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}=({\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})},({\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime}))_{c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}})
with a set
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
equipped with a binary relation
\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}
on it (defined as the set of all pairs
(c,c^{\prime})\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}\times{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
with
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})=\top
).
Axioms (CA1) and (CA2) for a
{\mathbf{2}}
-category then translate to reflexivity and transitivity of
\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}
respectively, hence a
{\mathbf{2}}
-category is nothing but a

.

Example 3.3
.

In the case
{\mathcal{Q}}={\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}
, we may regard
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}
-categories as generalised metric spaces
[
19
]
.
Objects of an
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}
-category

are thought of as

and the element
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\in[0,\infty]
as the
distance from
c
to
c^{\prime}
.
Notice that the axioms for
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}
-category indeed translate to some of the axioms for metric spaces:

(CA1)

for each
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
0\geq{\mathcal{C}}(c,c)
(that is,
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c)=0
); and

(CA2)

for each
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})+{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\geq{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime\prime})
(the triangle inequality).

We call
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{+}}
-categories
Lawvere metric spaces
.
Every metric space is a Lawvere metric space, but not conversely. Lawvere metric spaces are more general than metric spaces in the following three aspects:

1.

distance may take
\infty
;

2.

distance is non-symmetric (or
directed
), i.e.,
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})
may be different from
{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c)
; and

3.

{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})={\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c)=0
does not necessarily imply
c=c^{\prime}
.

Example 3.4
.

Similarly,
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{max}}}
-categories may be regarded as generalised ultrametric spaces; note that axiom (CA2) now reads:

(CA2)

for each
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
\max\{\,{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}),{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\,\}\geq{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime\prime})
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,e,\cdot)
be a monoid. A
\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{M}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}
has, for each pair
c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
, a subset
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\subseteq M
. These subsets must satisfy:

(CA2)

for each
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,

; and

(CA2)

for each

It follows that a

In fact, this example can be vastly generalised. For any (ordinary)
