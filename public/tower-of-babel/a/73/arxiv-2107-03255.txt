A Formula for Designing Zero-Determinant Strategies
Daizhan Cheng

Abstract
A formula is presented for designing zero-determinant(ZD) strategies of general finite games, which have n n players and players can have different numbers of strategies. To this end, using semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, the profile evolutionary equation for repeated finite games is obtained. Starting from it, the ZD strategies are developed for general finite games, based on the same technique proposed by Press and Dyson [ 14 ] . A formula is obtain to design ZD strategies for any player directly, ignoring the original ZD design process. Necessary and sufficient condition is obtained to ensure the effectiveness of the designed ZD strategies. As a consequence, it is also clear that player i i is able to unilaterally design | S i | − 1 |S_{i}|-1 dominating linear relations about the expected payoffs of all players. Finally, the fictitious opponent player is proposed for networked evolutionary networks (NEGs). Using it, the ZD-strategies are applied to NEGs. It is surprising that an individual in a network may use ZD strategies to conflict the whole rest network.

Though there are some efforts for extending ZD strategy technique, proposed by the pioneer work of Press and Dyson for prisoner’s dilemma, to general cases
[

, to the author’s best knowledge, a systematic description for most general games has not been obtained. The purpose of this paper is to present a general framework for general finite games.

Recently, a new matrix product, called the semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, was proposed

Using STP, this paper presents a profile evolutionary equation (PEE) for general finite games, which is essentially the same as the Markov matrix for the memory-one game in

The main contribution of this paper is the formula, which provides an algebraic equation for designing ZD strategies, which does not involve any original determinant.

Finally, the ZD strategies for Networked evolutionary games (NEGs) are investigated. By proposing and using fictitious opponent player (FOP) the networked evolutionary games can be formulated as a two player game, where a player, say, player

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: A brief survey is given in Section 2. Then It is used to develop profile evolutionary equation (PEE) of finite evolutionary games. Finally, some properties of transition matrix of PPE are investigated, which are important for designing ZD strategies. Section 3 deduces a general formula for designing ZD strategies. Necessary and sufficient conditions for the designed ZD strategies to be effective is presented. Some numerical examples are followed to describe the design procedure. The fictitious opponent player (FOP) is proposed in Section 4 for networked evolutionary games (NEGs). Using FOP, the technique of ZD strategies has been applied to NEGs.
Section 5 is a brief conclusion.

Remark II.2

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

STP is a fundamental tool in this approach, and all the matrix products used in this paper without product symbol are assumed to be STP. Hence, in this paper the product of two arbitrary size matrices is well proposed.

II-B

PEE of Finite Games

Definition II.6

Consider a finite game
G=(N,S,C)
, where

(i)

N=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
is the set of players.

(ii)

S=\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}

(iii)

A matrix formulation of the evolution game

[
5
]

(i)

Identifying

S_{i}\sim\Delta_{k_{i}}
.

(ii)

Setting
\kappa=\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}

S\sim\Delta_{\kappa}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{k_{i}}
.

(iii)

Let

be the vector form of a strategy for player

. Then

is a profile.

(iv)

For each player’s payoff function

, there exists a unique row vector
V^{c}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
such that

\displaystyle c_{i}(x)=V^{c}_{i}x,\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

(7)

Now consider an evolutionary game
G^{e}
of

, which stands for (infinitely) repeated

. Then each player can determine his action at

using historic knowledge. It was proved in

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+1)=L_{i}x(t),\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

Two types of strategies are commonly used:

•

Pure strategy:

L_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k_{i}\times\kappa},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

•

III-A

A Universal Formula for ZD-strategies

Consider the transition matrix
L
of PEE (
14

\displaystyle\kappa_{(i)}:=\begin{cases}1,\quad i=1,\\
\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k_{j},\quad i=2,3,\cdots,n.\end{cases}

(26)

\displaystyle\kappa^{(i)}:=\begin{cases}0,\quad i=0,\\
\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}k_{j},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n-1,\\

Using them, for each pair

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}\Phi_{i,j}&:=&\left\{s=(\alpha-1)\kappa^{(i-1)}+(j-1)\kappa^{(i)}+\beta\right.\;|\\
&&\left.\alpha=1,2,\cdots,\kappa_{(i)};~\beta=1,2,\cdots,\kappa^{(i)}\right\}.\end{array}

Then we have the following result:

Lemma III.1

Let
M=L-I_{\kappa}
. Define

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}\Xi_{i,j}&:=&\mathop{\sum}\limits_{s\in\Phi_{i,j}}\Row_{s}(M),\\
&&~~~~~~~~~~~i=1,2,\cdots,n;~j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}.\end{array}

Then

\displaystyle\Xi_{i,j}=p_{i,j}-\xi_{i,j},\quad,i=1,2,\cdots,n;\;j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i},

where

p_{i,j}=(p^{1}_{i,j},p^{2}_{i,j},\cdots,p^{\kappa}_{i,j}),

and

is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\xi_{i,j}(s)=\begin{cases}1,\quad s\in\Phi_{i,j},\\
0,\quad s\not\in\Phi_{i,j}.\end{cases}

(33)

It is obvious from Lemma

can be used to design

for player
i

Algorithm III.2

Consider an evolutionary game
G^{e}
, where

. Assume player
i

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\ell_{i,j}(Ec_{1},Ec_{2},\cdots,Ec_{n},1),\\
\quad 1\leq i\leq n;j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1,\end{array}

where

is a linear equation. Then his ZD strategies can be designed as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}p_{i,j}&=&(p^{1}_{i,j},p^{2}_{i,j},\cdots,p^{\kappa}_{i,j})\\
&=&\mu_{i,j}\ell_{i,j}\left(V^{c}_{1},V^{c}_{2},\cdots,V^{c}_{n},{\bf 1}_{\kappa}^{T}\right)+\xi_{i,j},\\
&&\quad j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1,\end{array}

where
\mu_{i,j}\neq 0
are adjustable parameters.

Equation (
III.2
) is a fundamental formula, which provides a convenient way to design ZD strategies for our preassigned purposes.

Definition III.3

A set of ZD strategies is rational, if the following rationality conditions are satisfied.

(i)

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{i,j}\leq 1,\quad j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1.

(39)

(ii)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k_{i}-1}p_{i,j}\leq 1.

(40)

Remark III.4

(i)

It is obvious that rationality is a fundamental requirement. Non-rational strategies are meaningless.

(ii)

It is clear that player
i
can unilaterally design at most
|S_{i}|-1
linear relations. Because when

p_{i,j}
,
j<|S_{i}|
are all determined,
p_{i,|S_{i}|}
is uniquely determined by

p_{i,|S_{i}|}={\bf 1}^{T}_{\kappa}-\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{|S_{i}|-1}p_{i,j}.

(iii)

Of course, player
i
need not to design
|S_{i}|-1
relations. Is he intends to design
r<|S_{i}|-1
relations, equation (
40
) has to be modified by reducing the summation to
r
items.

(iv)

The ZD design formula (
III.2
) can be used simultaneously by multi-players, or even all
n
players.

Even though a set of ZD strategies is rational, it may not be effective. That is, the goal (
III.2
) may not be reached. We need the following result:

Theorem III.5

Consider an evolutionary game,
G^{e}
, where
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n}]}
. A set of ZD strategies designed by formula (
III.2
) is effective, if and only if,

(i)

There exists an
u\in\Upsilon^{\kappa}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}L^{t}=u{\bf 1}^{T}_{\kappa}.

(41)

(ii)

\displaystyle\rank(L-I_{\kappa})=\kappa-1.

(42)

Note that verifying the two conditions in Theorem
III.5
is not an easy job. Hence we may replace them by the following one.

Corollary III.6

Consider an evolutionary game,
G^{e}
, where
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n}]}
. If
L
is primitive, then the set of ZD strategies designed by formula (
III.2
) is effective.

Remark III.7

(i)

Even though primitivity of
L
is only a sufficient condition, it is almost necessity because only a zero-measure set of
L
may not be primitive. That is, all non-primitive
L
have certain boundary values as
p^{r}_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
for some
(r,i,j)
. So the designer, who intends to use ZD strategies, is better to avoid using such values.

(ii)

Any player can not unilaterally make the conditions in Theorem
III.5
satisfied. It depends on other players’ strategies. What the player
i
can do is to do his best, that is, to ensure his designed rows,
\Xi_{i,j}
,
j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}
are linearly independent. (A Chinese idiom says that “Mou Shi Zai Ren, Cheng Shi Zai Tian”, (Man proposes, God disposes. That is the situation for ZD strategy Designer.)).

IV
Application to Networked Evolutionary Games

This section considers how to design ZD strategies for a player,
i
, in a networked evolutionary game (NEG). We propose a method, called a fictitious opponent player (FOP) conversion.

IV-A

Fictitious Opponent Player

Definition IV.1

[
5
]
An NEG is a triple
((N,E),G,\Pi)
, where
(N,E)
is a network graph with
N
as the set of players;
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[2;k,k]}
is a symmetric game with two players, called the fundamental network game;
\Pi
is the strategy updating rule, which describes how each player to update his strategies using his neighborhood information.

Remark IV.2

(i)

G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[2;k,k]}
is symmetric, if
S_{1}=S_{2}:=S_{0}
and for any
x,y\in S_{0}

c_{1}(x,y)=c_{2}(y,x).

(ii)

If
(i,j)\in E
, then players
i
and
j
will play game
G
at each time. (In this paper only the fixed graph is considered.)
Since
G
is symmetric, the the order of two players does not affect the result.

(iii)

Such an NEG is denoted by
G^{ne}=((N,E),G,\Pi)
.

Let player
i\in N
, and
\deg(i)=d
. Then he may consider
N\backslash\{i\}
as one player, called the FOP of
i
, denoted by
p_{-i}
. Assume
|S_{0}|=k
, the neighbors’ strategies can be considered as the strategies of
p_{-i}
. That is,
p_{-i}
has totally
k^{d}
strategies.

In fact, we need not to distinct different neighbors, hence if
S_{0}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{k}\}
, then the set of strategies of
p_{-i}
, denoted by
S_{-i}
, is

\displaystyle S_{-i}=\{\underbrace{s_{1}s_{1}\cdots s_{1}}_{d},\underbrace{s_{1}s_{1}\cdots s_{2}}_{d},\cdots,\underbrace{s_{k}s_{k}\cdots s_{k}}_{d}\}.

(81)

Each
s_{*}\in S_{-i}
can be expressed as

s_{*}=(\underbrace{s_{1}s_{1}\cdots s_{1}}_{d_{1}}\underbrace{s_{2}s_{2}\cdots s_{2}}_{d_{2}}\cdots\underbrace{s_{k}s_{k}\cdots s_{k}}_{d_{k}}),

where
d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{k}=d
.
Hence, we can also express
s_{*}
by
(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})
, which means
s_{j}
has been used by
d_{j}
neighbors,
1\leq j\leq k
. Using this notation, we have

\displaystyle S_{-i}=\{(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})\;|\;d_{j}\geq 0,\;\forall j;\;\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}=d\}.

(82)

It is easy to verify that define the strategies of
p_{-i}
in this way, by ignoring the order of neighbors, the total number of strategies is reduced from
k^{d}
to

\frac{(k+d-1)!}{(k-1)!d!}.

This treatment reduces the computational complexity.

From the point of view of player
i
, the NEG is equivalent to a game between him and
p_{-i}
, who has the set of strategies
S_{-i}
defined by (
82
).
Let
s_{*}=(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})\in S_{-i}
. Then the payoff functions for
c_{i}
and player
p_{-i}
’, denoted by
c_{-i}
, are

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}c_{i}(x_{i},s_{*})=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}(c_{i}(x_{i},s_{j}),\\
c_{-i}(x_{i},s_{*})=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}(c_{j}(x_{i},s_{j}).\\
\end{array}

Note that the FOP formulation is particularly suitable for using ZD strategies, because it is not effected by the structure and size of network graph, even though the size might be
\infty
. As long as the stationary distribution of the overall network exists, ZD strategies are still applicable. Moreover, it is easily designable.

V
Conclusion

This paper extends the technique of ZD strategies proposed by Press and Duson to general finite games. Using STP, a fundamental formula is presented to design ZD strategies for general finite games. In addition to the generality, it causes the design procedure very easy. Condition for the rationality of ZD strategies is presented. Then, necessary and sufficient condition for the effectiveness of the designed ZD strategies is also obtained, which put the ZD technique on a solid foundation. Some numerical examples are presented to demonstrate the efficiency of the method proposed in this paper.

Finally, the NEGs are considered. A new concept, called FOP, is proposed as the opponent player for a preassigned player
i
. Using it, the ZD strategies for player
i
is designed for the game between himself and his FOP. It is a surprising fact that one single player may be able to challenge whole network by using ZD strategies, no matter how large the network is.

Some important issues remained for further study, such as:

(i)

How to design rational ZD strategies for a complicated game?

(ii)

How to predict the efficiency of ZD strategies for a player?

(iii)

What will happen when ZD strategies are used to against ZD strategies?
