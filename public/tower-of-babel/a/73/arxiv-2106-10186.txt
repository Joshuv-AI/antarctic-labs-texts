An analytical model for tidal evolution in co-orbital systems
Jérémy Couturier, Philippe Robutel, Alexandre C. M. Correia

Abstract
Close-in co-orbital planets (in a 1:1 mean motion resonance) can experience strong tidal interactions with the central star. Here, we develop an analytical model adapted to the study of the tidal evolution of those systems. We use a Hamiltonian version of the constant time-lag tidal model, which extends the Hamiltonian formalism developed for the point-mass case. We show that co-orbital systems undergoing tidal dissipation either favour the Lagrange or the anti-Lagrange configurations, depending on the system parameters. However, for all range of parameters and initial conditions, both configurations become unstable, although the timescale for the destruction of the system can be larger than the lifetime of the star. We provide an easy-to-use criterion to determine if an already known close-in exoplanet may have an undetected co-orbital companion.



∎

I. Application to exoplanets

1
Introduction

In the framework of the three-body problem, planets in the co-orbital resonance correspond to a system in a 1:1 mean motion resonance. In other words, the planets have the same mean orbital period, which means that the difference of their mean longitudes librates, generally around a value close to
\pm 60^{\circ}
or equal to
180^{\circ}
. Beyond the famous collinear and equilateral configurations described respectively by
Euler, 1764
and
Lagrange, 1772
, other types of orbits are possible in this resonance, making the co-orbital dynamics very rich. While the Euler configurations, denoted by
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
and
L_{3}
in the restricted three-body problem, are unstable for all mass ranges, the Lagrange equilibria are linearly stable, provided that the three masses satisfy the relation
27(m_{0}m_{1}+m_{0}m_{2}+m_{1}m_{2})<(m_{0}+m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}
established by
Gascheau, 1843
for circular orbits. More general criteria exist for equilateral eccentric configurations
(
Danby, 1964
;
Roberts, 2002
;
Nauenberg, 2002
, see)
.
For the previous inequality to be fulfilled, it is necessary that one of the masses is dominating. Thus, we denote by
m_{0}
the mass of the star, which is much larger than that of the planets
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. With these notations, the Gascheau condition is satisfied when
(m_{1}+m_{2})/m_{0}\lesssim 1/27
.
Therefore, in the planar case and for small eccentricities, when the sum of the planetary masses is smaller than about
\displaystyle{1/27}
of the stellar mass, tadpole orbits arise, allowing the difference in the mean longitude to librate around
\pm 60^{\circ}
, with a maximum amplitude that increases as the sum of the planetary masses decreases.
When
(m_{1}+m_{2})/m_{0}\lesssim 3\times 10^{-4}
, horseshoe-shaped orbits can arise
(
Laughlin and Chambers, 2002
, see)
. They librate around
180^{\circ}
with a very large amplitude of at least
312^{\circ}
.
For moderate to large eccentricities, quasi-satellite orbits are also possible, for which the planets appear to revolve around each other
(
Giuppone et al., 2010
;
Pousse et al., 2017
, see)
, while many other exotic trajectories exist at high eccentricity
(
Leleu et al., 2018
)
.
The dynamics of the inclined problem is even more complex by allowing, among other things, transitions between the different types of orbits mentioned above
(
Namouni, 1999
)
.

The solar system contains several examples of co-orbital subsystems, for which there exists a very strong hierarchy between the masses of their components, as for the Jovian Trojans (or more generally of the giant planets), which correspond to a triplet Sun/Jupiter/asteroid, or the systems of the Trojan satellites of Saturn, like the subsystem Saturn/Helene/Dione.
The only exception is the triplet Saturn/Janus/Epimetheus, where the masses of the two satellites differ only by a factor of three.

The detection of co-orbital exoplanets with current instrumentation is very challenging. Moreover, as co-orbital planets are expected to have neighbouring companions, their detection from observational data is a highly degenerate problem.
In order to observe these systems, several techniques have been explored or developed, namely, radial velocity
(
Leleu et al., 2015
)
, transits
(
Janson, 2013
;
Hippke and Angerhausen, 2015
)
, combinations of transit and radial velocity
(
Ford and Gaudi, 2006
;
Leleu et al., 2017
)
, or transit timing variations of the planet
(
Ford and Holman, 2007
;
Madhusudhan and Winn, 2009
;
Vokrouhlický and Nesvorný, 2014
)
.
Nonetheless, despite several studies dedicated to this quest
(
Madhusudhan and Winn, 2009
;
Janson, 2013
;
Lillo-Box et al., 2018a
;
Lillo-Box et al., 2018b
)
, no co-orbital planets have been detected so far.

The theories of planetary formation do not prohibit the existence of co-orbital planets. Indeed,
Laughlin and Chambers, 2002
introduced two possible processes that can form such systems: planet-planet gravitational scattering, and accretion in situ at the stable Lagrange points of a primary.
Depending on the physical characteristics of the gas disc, the first process can lead to systems with a high diversity of mass ratios
(
Cresswell and Nelson, 2008
)
, but also to equal mass co-orbitals
(
Giuppone et al., 2012
)
.
In the in-situ scenario, different models lead to various upper limits to the mass that can form at Lagrange’s equilibrium points of a giant planet: a maximum mass of about 0.6 Earth mass for
Beaugé et al., 2007
, while
Lyra et al., 2009
obtained 5 to 15 Earth mass planets at the same locations.
Once formed in the disc, the stability of the co-orbital system is not necessarily guaranteed.
Beaugé et al., 2007
found that inward migration tends to slightly increase the libration amplitude of the co-orbital system, and instability during the late migrating stages with low gas friction may lead to the destruction of the system. Another study from
Pierens and Raymond, 2014
shows that equal mass co-orbitals (from super-Earths to Saturns) are heavily disturbed during the gap-opening phase of their evolution.
Recently,
Leleu et al., 2019
studied the dynamics of a pair of migrating co-orbital planets and showed that, depending on the mass-ratio, the eccentricities of the planets and the type of dissipative forces, the two planets may evolve towards the stable Lagrangian points, or may scatter out of the system.

For systems with orbital periods
P_{\rm orb}\lesssim 10
days, the planets undergo strong tidal interactions with the parent star
(
Correia et al., 2020
, e.g.,)
, which arise from differential and inelastic deformation of the planet.
In the two-body problem, the ultimate stage for tidal evolution is the synchronization of the rotation and orbital periods, alignment of the planet spin axis with the normal to the orbit, and the circularization of the orbit
(
Hut, 1980
;
Adams and Bloch, 2015
, e.g.,)
.
In the full
\displaystyle{N}
-body problem,
Moeckel, 2017
proved that a relative equilibrium (solid rotation of the whole configuration) is never an energy minimiser of the space phase at a given total angular momentum. Thus, applied to the three-body problem with tidal dissipation, this result implies that the Lagrangian equilibria are made unstable by tides. However, we know neither the timescale of such instability nor if the phenomenon expands to the whole space phase, and we even less know what are the consequences to the dynamics of the co-orbital configuration.
Indeed, although the spin of close-in planets quickly evolves into an equilibrium configuration, the orbital evolution is much slower
(
Correia and Laskar, 2010
, e.g.,)
, and so the co-orbital configuration may survive the whole age of the system.

Rodríguez et al., 2013
performed some numerical simulations in the case of two identical co-orbital planets, but so far no analytical results have been provided to the consequences of tidal dissipation in the co-orbital resonance.

The present work attempts to study the influence of tidal dissipation in the dynamics of co-orbital planets.
We develop an analytical model to account for tidal effects on the rotation, eccentricities and libration amplitude of these bodies.
We consider only the tidal effects raised by the star on the planets, which dominate the tidal evolution.
We thus obtain the non-conservative effects that shape the long-term dynamics. In section
2
, we summarize the main known results on the co-orbital unrestricted three-body problem in the planar case and we introduce the elements useful to the following sections. In section
3
, we establish the tidal formalism and we give analytic answers to the aforementioned questions in the vicinity of the Lagrangian points. We also present a tool to help with the detection of co-orbital exoplanets in section
3.3
. The results from section
3
are expanded to the whole space phase in section
4
, where the validity of section
3
is also tested numerically.
In table
4
of Appendix
A
, we list all the notations used throughout this paper.

2
The co-orbital resonance

2.1
The averaged Hamiltonian

In order to construct the Hamiltonian associated with the coorbital resonance, we only consider in this section the point mass planar planetary three-body problem. The case of extended bodies is studied in Section
3
.
We start with two planets of masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
small with respect to the mass
m_{0}
of the star around which they orbit.
For both planets we define the quantities
\beta_{j}=m_{0}m_{j}/(m_{0}+m_{j})
,
\mu_{j}=\mathcal{G}(m_{0}+m_{j}),
where
\mathcal{G}
is the gravitational constant. We also introduce the small parameter
\varepsilon=(m_{1}+m_{2})/m_{0}
.
In order to define a canonical coordinate system related to the elliptic elements
(a_{j},e_{j},\lambda_{j},\varpi_{j})
(respectively the semi-major axis, the eccentricity, the mean longitude and longitude of the pericenter of the planet
j
), we start from Poincaré heliocentric coordinates
(\lambda_{j},\tilde{x}_{j},\Lambda_{j},x_{j})
where

\Lambda_{j}=\beta_{j}\sqrt{\mu_{j}a_{j}},\quad x_{j}=\sqrt{\Lambda_{j}}\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-e_{j}^{2}}}\exp(i\varpi_{j}),\quad\tilde{x}_{j}=-i\bar{x}_{j}.

(1)

In these coordinates, the Hamiltonian system derives from the sympletic form

\Omega=\sum_{j\in\{1,2\}}\left(d\lambda_{j}\wedge d\Lambda_{j}+d\tilde{x}_{j}\wedge dx_{j}\right).

(2)

Following
Robutel and Pousse, 2013
, the planetary Hamiltonian can be written as

H=H_{K}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})+H_{P}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2}),

(3)

where the Keplerian part
H_{K}
, of order
\varepsilon
, takes the form

H_{K}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})=-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{\beta_{j}^{3}\mu_{j}^{2}}{2\Lambda_{j}^{2}}.

(4)

The perturbative part
H_{P}
, due to planet-planet interactions, takes into account both direct and indirect effects. It is a quantity of order
\varepsilon^{2}
, and can be expanded, at least for small to moderate eccentricities, in power series of
(x_{j},\tilde{x}_{j})
with coefficients that are trigonometric polynomials in
\lambda_{j}
depending on
\Lambda_{j}
.

We assume that the system is in, or close to, the co-orbital resonance. In this case, the angle
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
varies slowly with respect to the mean longitudes. We can therefore study the dynamics of the averaged problem over a fast angle. Moreover, in the 1:1 mean-motion resonance, the values of the semi-major axes are always close to the same constant quantity denoted by
\bar{a}
. As a consequence, the action variables
\Lambda_{j}
will remain close to
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\!\star}
defined as

\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\!\star}=(\Lambda_{1}^{\star},\Lambda_{2}^{\star})\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\Lambda_{j}^{\star}=m_{j}\sqrt{\mu_{0}\bar{a}}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\mu_{0}=\mathcal{G}m_{0}.

(5)

It follows that the mean-motion of the planet
j
at
\Lambda_{j}^{\star}
satisfies

\frac{d\lambda_{j}}{dt}=\frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial\Lambda_{j}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\!\star})=\frac{\beta_{j}^{3}}{m_{j}^{3}}\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{0}^{3/2}}\bar{a}^{-3/2}=\eta\left(\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}\right),

(6)

where the Kepler law reads

\eta=\sqrt{\mu_{0}\bar{a}^{-3}}.

(7)

Since we are only interested in a study of the problem in the vicinity of the resonance, we expand the Hamiltonian in a neighborhood of
(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})=(\Lambda_{1}^{\star},\Lambda_{2}^{\star})
defined above. Then, in order to average the Hamiltonian, we perform the following variable transformation

(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\longmapsto(Z,Z_{2},\phi,\phi_{2})=(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{1}^{\star},\,\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}-\Lambda_{1}^{\star}-\Lambda_{2}^{\star},\,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},\,\lambda_{2}).

(8)

As described by
Niederman et al., 2020
, if we constrain the
\Lambda_{j}
to belong to a neighborhood of
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\!\star}
of order
\varepsilon^{1+\iota}
with
1/2\geq\iota>1/3
, the Keplerian part reads

\displaystyle H_{K}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=\eta Z_{2}-\frac{3}{2}\eta\left(\frac{Z^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{\star}}+\frac{\left(Z_{2}-Z\right)^{2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{\star}}\right)+\,\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\iota+2}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{K}(Z,Z_{2})+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\iota+2}\right)\,,

where the constant terms have been neglected.

Since the perturbation is of order
\varepsilon^{2}
, a zero-order expansion in
\Lambda_{j}-\Lambda_{j}^{\star}
generates a remainder that has the same size as in (
9
). Thus, we will limit ourselves to

\begin{split}&H_{P}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})=H_{P}(\Lambda_{1}^{\star},\Lambda_{2}^{\star},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})+\,\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\iota+2}\right)\\
&\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;=\hat{H}_{P}(\phi,\phi_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\iota+2}\right).\end{split}

(10)

In order to uncouple the variables associated with the different timescales, we perform the following linear transformation

(Z,Z_{2},\phi,\phi_{2})\longmapsto(I,I_{2},\xi,\xi_{2})=(Z-\delta Z_{2},\,Z_{2},\,\phi,\,\delta\phi+\phi_{2}),\quad\text{with}\quad\delta=\frac{m_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},

(11)

where
\displaystyle{x_{j}}
and
\displaystyle{\tilde{x}_{j}}
are unchanged.
Thus, in the
(I,I_{2})
variables, the Keplerian part
\hat{H}
takes the following form

\hat{H}_{K}(Z,Z_{2})=\check{H}_{K}(I,I_{2})=\eta I_{2}-\frac{3}{2}\eta\frac{I_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda_{1}^{\star}+\Lambda_{2}^{\star}}-\frac{3}{2}\eta\frac{\Lambda_{1}^{\star}+\Lambda_{2}^{\star}}{\Lambda_{1}^{\star}\Lambda_{2}^{\star}}I^{2}.

(12)

This decoupling stresses the different dynamical timescales involved in the problem. Indeed, as

\dot{\xi}_{2}=\frac{\partial\check{H}_{K}}{\partial I_{2}}=\eta+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\iota})\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{\xi}=\frac{\partial\check{H}_{K}}{\partial I}=-3\eta\frac{\Lambda_{1}^{\star}+\Lambda_{2}^{\star}}{\Lambda_{1}^{\star}\Lambda_{2}^{\star}}I=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\iota}),

(13)

a fast motion related to
\eta
is associated with the orbital angle
\xi_{2}
, a semi-fast motion is associated to the resonant angle
\xi
, while a slow (or secular) evolution corresponds to the evolution of the variables
x_{j}
(see below).

It is therefore legitimate to average the Hamiltonian over the fast angle
\xi_{2}
. For details of the averaging process, we refer to
Robutel and Pousse, 2013
and
Niederman et al., 2020
. Simply remember that replacing the perturbation

\check{H}_{P}(\phi,\phi_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})=\hat{H}_{P}(\xi,\xi_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})

(14)

by its average over the fast angle
\xi_{2}
amounts to neglecting a remainder of order
\varepsilon^{\iota+2}
. This last statement remains valid as long as the distance between the two planets does not go towards zero with
\varepsilon

(see
Robutel et al., 2016
, for more details)
. Finally, we are left with the averaged Hamiltonian

\check{H}(I,I_{2},\xi,x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})=\check{H}_{K}(I,I_{2})+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\check{H}_{P}(\xi,\xi_{2},x_{1},x_{2},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2})d\xi_{2}.

(15)

Since
\xi_{2}
no longer appears in the Hamiltonian, its conjugated action,
I_{2}=\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}-\Lambda_{1}^{\star}-\Lambda_{2}^{\star}
, is an integral of the averaged Hamiltonian
\check{H}
.

Our last transformation consists in introducing dimensionless variables that are no longer proportional to the planetary masses (or to their square root).
We thus define the new coordinates
(J,J_{2},X_{1},X_{2},\bar{X}_{1},\bar{X}_{2})
as

J=\frac{I}{m\bar{a}^{2}\eta},\quad J_{2}=\frac{I_{2}}{m\bar{a}^{2}\eta},\quad X_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m_{j}\bar{a}^{2}\eta}}\,x_{j},\quad\bar{X}_{j}=i\sqrt{\frac{2}{m_{j}\bar{a}^{2}\eta}}\,\tilde{x}_{j},

(16)

where
m=\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}
.

J
and
J_{2}
are at most of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
for tadpole orbits and
\varepsilon^{1/3}
for horseshoe one
(
Robutel and Pousse, 2013
, see)
, while
X_{j}
is close to the eccentricity vector of the planet
J
, that is

X_{j}=e_{j}\exp(i\varpi_{j})\left(\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}1+\mathcal{O}\left(J\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(J_{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)+\mathcal{O}\left(e_{j}^{2}\right)\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}\right).

(17)

The semi-major axes
\displaystyle{a_{j}}
are linked to the variables
\displaystyle{J}
and
\displaystyle{J_{2}}
by the relations

a_{j}=\bar{a}\mathcal{R}_{j}^{2},

(18)

with

\mathcal{R}_{j}=1+f_{j},\;\;\;\;\;f_{1}=\frac{m}{m_{1}+m_{2}}J_{2}+\frac{m}{m_{1}}J,\;\;\;\;\;f_{2}=\frac{m}{m_{1}+m_{2}}J_{2}-\frac{m}{m_{2}}J.

(19)

In order to make the Hamiltonian equations as close as possible to their standard form, we rescale both time and energy as

\mathcal{H}=\frac{\check{H}}{m\bar{a}^{2}\eta^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau=t\eta.

(20)

The equations of the motion become

\displaystyle\dot{J}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\xi},\quad\dot{J_{2}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\xi_{2}},

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial J},\quad\dot{\xi_{2}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial J_{2}},

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{j}=-2i\frac{m}{m_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\bar{X}_{j}},

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{X}}_{j}=2i\frac{m}{m_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial X_{j}}.

where the dot denotes the derivation with respect to
\tau
.
The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
now reads

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{K}+\mathcal{H}_{P},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{H}_{K}(J,J_{2})=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{m}J^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{m}{m_{1}+m_{2}}J_{2}^{2}+J_{2}.

(22)

As mentioned above, the perturbation
\mathcal{H}_{P}
can be expanded in powers of the
(X_{j},\bar{X}_{j})
as

\mathcal{H}_{P}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{2n}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{H}_{2n}=\sum_{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{p}}|=2n}\Psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(\Delta^{-1},e^{i\xi},e^{-i\xi}\right)X_{1}^{p_{1}}X_{2}^{p_{2}}\bar{X}_{1}^{\bar{p}_{1}}\bar{X}_{2}^{\bar{p}_{2}}\,.

(23)

In the previous expression,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{p}}
is the multi-index
(p_{1},p_{2},\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
,
\Psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{p}}}
is a polynomial in

\left(\Delta^{-1},e^{i\xi},e^{-i\xi}\right)
and
\Delta=\sqrt{2-2\cos\xi}
.
The invariance by rotation of the system, which is equivalent to the conservation of its total angular momentum, yields an additional constraint on the multi-indexes
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{p}}
known as the D’Alembert rule

p_{1}+p_{2}=\bar{p}_{1}+\bar{p}_{2}\,.

(24)

The expression of
\mathcal{H}_{P}
expanded up to degree
4
reads

\begin{split}&\mathcal{H}_{0}=\frac{m}{m_{0}}\left(\cos\xi-\left(2-2\cos\xi\right)^{-1/2}\right),\\
&\mathcal{H}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{m}{m_{0}}\left\{\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}A_{h}\left(X_{1}\bar{X}_{1}+X_{2}\bar{X}_{2}\right)+B_{h}X_{1}\bar{X}_{2}+\bar{B}_{h}\bar{X}_{1}X_{2}\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}\right\},\\
&\mathcal{H}_{4}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{m}{m_{0}}\left\{\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}D_{h}\left(X_{1}^{2}\bar{X}_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}\bar{X}_{2}^{2}\right)+E_{h}X_{1}^{2}\bar{X}_{2}^{2}+\bar{E}_{h}X_{2}^{2}\bar{X}_{1}^{2}\right.\\
&\left.+F_{h}\left(X_{1}X_{2}\bar{X}_{1}^{2}+\bar{X}_{1}\bar{X}_{2}X_{2}^{2}\right)+\bar{F}_{h}\left(\bar{X}_{1}\bar{X}_{2}X_{1}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}\bar{X}_{2}^{2}\right)+G_{h}X_{1}X_{2}\bar{X}_{1}\bar{X}_{2}\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}\right\},\end{split}

(25)

with

A_{h}=\frac{5\cos 2\xi-13+8\cos\xi}{4\Delta^{5}}-\cos\xi\,,\quad B_{h}=e^{-2i\xi}-\frac{e^{-3i\xi}+16e^{-2i\xi}-26e^{-i\xi}+9e^{i\xi}}{8\Delta^{5}}.

(26)

The coefficients
\displaystyle{D_{h},\,E_{h},\,F_{h}}
and
\displaystyle{G_{h}}
of the fourth degree are given in Appendix
B
.

2.2
The semi-fast dynamics

Since the Hamiltonian is even in the variables
X_{j}
and
\bar{X}_{j}
, the manifold
X_{1}=X_{2}=0
is invariant by the flow of the averaged Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. In other words, the average problem has solutions for which both orbits are circular, but not necessarily Keplerian. Indeed, along such motions, the eccentricities remain equal to zero while the semi-major axes evolve. The dynamics of these particular trajectories derives from the one degree of freedom Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}+\mathcal{H}_{0}
whose phase portrait is plotted in Figure
1
.
This Hamiltonian possesses three fixed points located in
\displaystyle{\left(J=0,\xi=\pi/3\right)}
,
\displaystyle{\left(J=0,\xi=5\pi/3\right)}
and
\displaystyle{\left(J=0,\xi=\pi\right)}
corresponding to the famous Lagrangian equilibria. The two stable points that correspond to configurations where the three bodies occupy the vertices of an equilateral triangle are labeled by
L_{4}
and
L_{5}
, while aligned Euler configuration is denoted by
L_{3}
.
The separatrices emanating from the unstable equilibrium point
L_{3}
split the phase space in three distinct regions. The first two areas contain the tadpole orbits inside the two lobs including respectively
L_{4}
and
L_{5}
. These trajectories, that surround the stable equilibria, are periodic with a frequency of order
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
(and equal to
\sqrt{27\varepsilon/4}
at
L_{4}
or
L_{5}
). The third domain corresponds to the outer region where the orbits called horseshoe surround the three fixed points.
The
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
points are absent from this phase portrait because of the zero order truncation performed to the perturbation. They are replaced, in this model, by the singular line
\xi=0
where the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}+\mathcal{H}_{0}
tends to minus infinity, regardless of the values of the action
J
. As a result, the model that we consider here is not valid for very large amplitude horseshoe orbits
(
Robutel and Pousse, 2013
, see)
. It is also worth mentioning that the libration frequency (semi-fast frequency) tends towards infinity when approaching the singularity and the averaging process no longer makes sense in the neighbourhood of the singularity.

Phase portrait of the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}_{K}+\mathcal{H}_{0}}
with
\displaystyle{m_{1}=0.001\,m_{0}}
and
\displaystyle{m_{2}=0.0004\,m_{0}}
. The two elliptic fixed points
\displaystyle{L_{4}}
and
\displaystyle{L_{5}}
are energy maximizers while the hyperbolic fixed point
\displaystyle{L_{3}}
is on a saddle point. Around
\displaystyle{L_{4,5}}
are the tadpoles orbits and outside of the separatrix emanating from
\displaystyle{L_{3}}
are the horseshoe orbits. The Hamiltonian tends to minus infinity as the angular separation between the planets
\xi
moves towards zero.

2.3
The secular dynamics

As we will show in Section
3
, tidal dissipations tend to circularize the orbits. Thus, in this section, we will limit the study to the orbits with small eccentricities. For that purpose, it is sufficient to truncate the Hamiltonian equations to the first order in the
X_{j}
along the circular orbits described above (Sect.
2.2
), which is equivalent to considering the trajectories associated to the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}+\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

According to (
25
), the variational equations are given by

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{X}_{1}\\
\dot{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{i}{m_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{2}A_{h}(\xi(t))&m_{2}\bar{B}_{h}(\xi(t))\vskip 2.84526pt\\
m_{1}B_{h}(\xi(t))&m_{1}A_{h}(\xi(t))\ \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
X_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(27)

where
(J(t),\xi(t))
is a solution of the semi-fast Hamiltonian system derived from
\mathcal{H}_{K}+\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Despite being linear, the differential equation (
27
) is, a priori, not integrable, except in some special cases.
Indeed, if one chooses a stationary solution corresponding to one of the fixed points of the semi-fast system, the linear equation (
27
) is no longer time-dependent and therefore becomes trivially integrable.
This case is fully studied in
Robutel and Pousse, 2013
. Here, we just remind the main results that play an important role in the next sections.
We are not interested in the unstable configuration
L_{3}
and we restrict the study to the stable equilibria

3
Tidal effects

3.1
Tidal model

Tides arise from differential and inelastic deformations of a body
\displaystyle{j}
(e.g., the planet) due to the gravitational effect of a perturbing body
\displaystyle{i}
(e.g., the star or the companion planet).
Tidal contributions to the orbital and spin evolution are based on a very general formulation of the tidal potential, initiated by
Darwin, 1880
.
Since celestial bodies are not perfectly rigid, there is a distortion that gives rise to a tidal bulge.
This redistribution of mass modifies the gravitational potential generated by the body
\displaystyle{j}
in any point of the space,
\displaystyle{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}}
.
The additional amount of potential is known by the tidal potential
(
Kaula, 1964
, e.g.)

V(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}})=-\kappa_{2,j}\frac{\mathcal{G}m_{i}}{R_{j}}\left(\frac{R_{j}}{r}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{R_{j}}{r_{i}^{\bigstar}}\right)^{3}P_{2}\left(\cos S\right),

(32)

where the indice
\displaystyle{i}
refers to the body responsible for the tidal bulge (perturbing body), while the indice
\displaystyle{j}
stands for the body where the bulge is raised.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}}
is the position of the perturbing body with respect to the barycenter of body
\displaystyle{j}
,
\displaystyle{R_{j}}
is the radius of the body
\displaystyle{j}
,
\kappa_{2,j}
is the second Love number,
\displaystyle{P_{2}}
is the second Legendre polynomial, and
\displaystyle{S}
is the angle between
\displaystyle{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}^{\bigstar}}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}}
.
For an interacting body with mass
m_{k}
at the position
\displaystyle{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}_{k}}
the amount of tidal potential energy is then given by

U_{ijk}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}_{k})=m_{k}V(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}_{k})=-\kappa_{2,j}\frac{\mathcal{G}m_{k}m_{i}}{R_{j}}\left(\frac{R_{j}}{r_{k}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{R_{j}}{r_{i}^{\bigstar}}\right)^{3}P_{2}\left(\cos S\right).

(33)

A system of
\displaystyle{N}
bodies undergoing tidal forces is described by
\displaystyle{N\left(N-1\right)^{2}}
tidal potentials. For
\displaystyle{N=3}
, this gives
\displaystyle{12}
contributions, but tides raised on a planet by another planet, as well as tides raised by the central star on a planet and interacted with by the other planet can be neglected due to their very small contribution.
Only
\displaystyle{6}
contributions remain:
\displaystyle{4}
of these are of order
\kappa_{2,0}(\varepsilon m_{0})^{2}R_{0}^{5}
, and correspond to tides raised on the star and interacted with by the planets, while the last
\displaystyle{2}
are of order
\kappa_{2,j}m_{0}^{2}R_{j}^{5}
, and correspond to tides raised on a planet by the star and interacted with by the star.
For planets in the solar system we usually have
\kappa_{2,j}\approx 0.5

(
Yoder, 1995
)
, while for Sun-like stars we have
\kappa_{2,0}\approx 0.02

(
Claret and Cunha, 1997
)
.
Assuming a constant density for all bodies we have
R_{j}\propto m_{j}^{1/3}
and thus

\frac{\kappa_{2,0}(\varepsilon m_{0})^{2}R_{0}^{5}}{\kappa_{2,j}m_{0}^{2}R_{j}^{5}}=\frac{\kappa_{2,0}}{\kappa_{2,j}}\varepsilon^{1/3}\ll 1,

(34)

that is, tides raised on the star can also be neglected.
Therefore, in this work we consider only the 2 terms corresponding to tides raised on each planet by the star and interacted with by the star.

The dissipation of the mechanical energy inside the planet introduces a delay
\Delta t_{j}
, and hence a phase shift between the initial perturbation and the maximal tidal deformation.
As a consequence, the star exerts a torque on the tidal bulge which modifies the spin and the orbit of the planet.
In all the following, we use the notation

z_{j}^{\bigstar}\left(t\right)=z_{j}\left(t-\Delta t_{j}\right),

(35)

\displaystyle{z_{j}}
being any quantity.

Tidal dissipation is usually modeled through the quality factor
Q_{j}
, which measures the amount of energy dissipated in a tidal cycle
(
Munk and MacDonald, 1960
, e.g.,)
.
For a given tidal frequency
\sigma
, the tidal dissipation can be related to this delay through
(
Efroimsky, 2012
, e.g.,)

Q_{j}^{-1}(\sigma)=\sin(\sigma\Delta t_{j}(\sigma))\approx\sigma\Delta t_{j}(\sigma).

(36)

The exact dependence of
\Delta t_{j}(\sigma)
on the tidal frequency is unknown.
In order to take into account tidal dissipation, we need to adopt a tidal model.
A large variety of models exists, but the most commonly used are the constant-
Q

(
Munk and MacDonald, 1960
, e.g.,)
, the linear model
(
Mignard, 1979
, e.g.,)
,
the Maxwell model
(
Correia et al., 2014
, e.g.,)
,
and the Andrade model
(
Efroimsky, 2012
, e.g.,)
.
Some models appear to be better suited to certain situations, but no model is globally accepted.
Nevertheless, regardless of the tidal model adopted, the qualitative conclusions are more or less unaffected, and the system always loses mechanical energy.

In this work we adopt a viscous linear model for tides
(
Singer, 1968
;
Mignard, 1979
)
.
In this model it is assumed that the time delay
\Delta t_{j}
is constant and independent of the frequency.
This tidal model is widely used and provides very simple expressions for the tidal interactions.

, respectively, have non-zero negative real parts. Therefore, both eccentric eigen-modes Lagrange and anti-Lagrange are damped to zero. Similarly, the rotation rates of both planets are also damped. On the contrary, the real part of
ϡ
and
\bar{\text{\LGR\textsampi{} }\!\!}
, perturbations of
i\nu
and
-i\nu
, are strictly positive, which leads to an exponential increase of the libration amplitude when the system is around
\displaystyle{L_{4,5}}
.

3.2.3
Characteristic timescales

We define here the characteristic timescale of a given proper mode of the system (
56
) as the time needed for its amplitude to be multiplied (or divided, if the corresponding real part is negative) by a factor
\displaystyle{\exp\left(1\right)}
. According to the eigenvalues (
60
), these times are

\begin{split}&\tau_{\text{rot}}^{j}=\frac{1}{6\pi}\alpha_{j}\text{\LGR\textqoppa{} }\!\!_{j}^{2}\frac{m_{j}}{m_{0}}\frac{Q_{j}}{q_{j}}\left[1+\frac{3m_{j}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}\text{\LGR\textqoppa{} }\!\!_{j}^{2}\alpha_{j}\right]T,\\
&\tau_{\text{L}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{21\pi}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{Q_{1}}+\frac{q_{2}}{Q_{2}}\right)^{-1}T,\\
&\tau_{\text{AL}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{21\pi}\left(\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}\frac{q_{1}}{Q_{1}}+\frac{m_{1}}{m_{2}}\frac{q_{2}}{Q_{2}}\right)^{-1}T,\\
&\tau_{\text{lib}}=\frac{7}{3}\tau_{\text{AL}},\end{split}

(61)

where
\displaystyle{T=2\pi/\eta}
is the orbital period. We note that the times
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{rot}}^{j}}
are much smaller than the three other characteristic times, due to the presence of the factor
\displaystyle{\text{\LGR\textqoppa{} }\!\!_{j}^{2}\ll 1}
. That is, regardless of the parameters and initial conditions, the rotations of the planets are damped to their equilibrium (
54
) in a timescale such that the eccentricities and the libration angle do not undergo significant damping or excitation.

We know from the eigenvalues (
60
) that the two eccentric eigen-modes Lagrange and anti-Lagrange are damped to zero, while the libration amplitude of the resonant angle
\displaystyle{\xi}
exponentially increases. We now compare the timescales
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{AL}}}
,
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{L}}}
and
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}}
to determine which proper mode amplitudes evolve faster. Even though both eccentric modes are damped, the damping times may be different. By comparing them, we can find if the system favours the Lagrange or the anti-Lagrange configuration. Indeed if

\begin{split}&\frac{\tau_{\text{AL}}}{\tau_{\text{L}}}<1\;\rightarrow\;\text{ then the system settles in Lagrange whereas if},\\
&\frac{\tau_{\text{AL}}}{\tau_{\text{L}}}>1\;\rightarrow\;\text{ then the system settles in anti-Lagrange}.\end{split}

(62)

Moreover, comparing the time
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}}
with the eccentric times
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{AL}}}
and
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{L}}}
allows us to know if the system is still eccentric or already circular when the libration amplitude has significantly increased. We have

\tau_{\text{lib}}=\frac{1}{9\pi}\frac{\varepsilon}{q_{1}/Q_{1}+q_{2}/Q_{2}}\frac{\tau_{\text{AL}}}{\tau_{\text{L}}}T,

(63)

and so, for a given sum of the planetary masses,
\displaystyle{\varepsilon}
, and sum of the dissipation rates,
\displaystyle{q_{1}/Q_{1}+q_{2}/Q_{2}}
, the system moves away from
\displaystyle{L_{4,5}}
faster if it favours Lagrange, and slower if it favours anti-Lagrange.

These results are a priori valid only in a small neighboorhoud of
\displaystyle{L_{4,5}}
, but in fact, the simulations from section
4
show that the behaviour of the system near
\displaystyle{L_{4,5}}
is valid even at high libration amplitudes. This means that the increase in the libration amplitude is unbounded, leading to a systematic destruction of the system due to close encounters between the planets. The disruption time depends on
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}}
, and so Lagrange-like systems have a short life expectancy, while anti-Lagrange-like systems have a long life expectancy. Moreover, a Lagrange-like system is eccentric when old
4
4

4

Old means that its age is significant with respect to its expectancy of life.
(as long as it was eccentric when young), while an anti-Lagrange-like system is always circular when old. Indeed, for an anti-Lagrange-like system, the characteristic time
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{e}}}
of eccentricity damping is given by
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{AL}}}
and then,
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}=7\tau_{\text{e}}/3}
ensures that the eccentricity is damped when the libration amplitude has significantly increased. On the other hand, for a Lagrange-like system,
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{e}}=\tau_{\text{L}}}
and then,
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}\ll\tau_{\text{e}}}
ensures that the system is still eccentric when the libration amplitude has significantly increased.

Although it is clear that
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}}
depends on the semi-major axis
\displaystyle{\bar{a}}
, equation (
63
) does not explicitly show it, since
\displaystyle{\bar{a}}
is hidden in the variables
\displaystyle{q_{j}}
,
\displaystyle{T}
and
\displaystyle{Q_{j}}
. We have

\tau_{\text{lib}}\propto\frac{\tau_{\text{AL}}}{\tau_{\text{L}}}\bar{a}^{\beta},

(64)

where
\displaystyle{\beta}
is
\displaystyle{6.5}
for the constant-
\displaystyle{Q}
model and
\displaystyle{8}
for the linear model (constant
\displaystyle{\Delta t_{j}}
). Thus, for a large
\displaystyle{\bar{a}}
or a strong anti-Lagrange tendency, co-orbital planetary systems may survive for the entire life-time of the star in the main sequence.

Interestingly, the ratio between the two eccentric damping timescales depends only on the ratio between the planetary masses and the ratio between the dissipation rates inside the planets. That is, denoting

x=\frac{m_{1}}{m_{2}}\;\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\;y=\frac{q_{2}Q_{1}}{q_{1}Q_{2}}=\frac{q_{2}\eta\Delta t_{2}}{q_{1}\eta\Delta t_{1}}=\frac{\kappa_{2,2}\text{\LGR\textqoppa{} }\!\!_{2}^{5}\Delta t_{2}}{\kappa_{2,1}\text{\LGR\textqoppa{} }\!\!_{1}^{5}\Delta t_{1}},

(65)

we have

\frac{\tau_{\text{AL}}}{\tau_{\text{L}}}=\frac{x\left(1+y\right)}{1+yx^{2}}.

(66)

The equality between the eccentric damping timescales (
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{AL}}=\tau_{\text{L}}}
) occurs at
\displaystyle{x=1}
and
\displaystyle{xy=1}
, plotted by black lines in figure

, where we also show the ratio
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{AL}}/\tau_{\text{L}}}
as a function of
\displaystyle{x}
and
\displaystyle{y}
. We clearly observe two regions, corresponding to Lagrange-like and anti-Lagrange-like systems.

Let us assume that the quality factor
\displaystyle{Q_{j}}
is mass independent. We then deduce from
\displaystyle{\text{\LGR\textqoppa{} }\!\!_{j}\propto m_{j}^{1/3}}
and equation (
65
) that

y\propto x^{-5/3}.

(67)

The blue line in figure

plots
\displaystyle{y=x^{-5/3}}
. It shows the path followed by a system with two initially identical planets (white spot) when we change the mass repartition between them. We conclude that for a given sum of the planetary masses
\displaystyle{\varepsilon}
and sum of the dissipation rates
\displaystyle{q_{1}/Q_{1}+q_{2}/Q_{2}}

\displaystyle{m_{1}=m_{2}=\varepsilon m_{0}/2}

\displaystyle{m_{1}}
or
\displaystyle{m_{2}}
tends towards
\displaystyle{\varepsilon m_{0}}

3.3
Application to the detection of co-orbital exoplanets

We can use the results from section
3.2
to ascertain if an already discovered exoplanet may have an undetected co-orbital companion. For any co-orbital system, we denote
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{hs}}}
and
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}}
the time needed to reach the horseshoe-shaped orbits, and the time needed for close encounters to disrupt the co-orbital resonance, respectively. In the rest of this section, we use the observational parameters to estimate
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}}
and then discard cases such that
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}}
is too small.

The libration amplitude
\Phi=\max\left(\xi-60^{\circ}\right)
is defined as the angular distance to
\displaystyle{L_{4}}
. If
\displaystyle{\Phi_{0}}
is the initial libration amplitude of the system and if
\displaystyle{\Phi}
is a small libration amplitude greater than
\displaystyle{\Phi_{0}}
, then, by definition of
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}}
, the time
\displaystyle{\tau_{\Phi}}
needed to reach the libration amplitude
\displaystyle{\Phi}
is given by

\frac{\tau_{\Phi}}{\tau_{\text{lib}}}=\ln\left(\frac{\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}\right).

(68)

Equation (
68
) a priori cannot be used to predict the time
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{hs}}}
, since the separatrix
\displaystyle{L_{3}}
is far from the fixed point
\displaystyle{L_{4}}
. Nevertheless, by performing numerical simulations of equations (
51
) with arbitrary parameters and initial conditions, we verify that the expression

\frac{\tau_{\text{hs}}}{\tau_{\text{lib}}}\approx\ln\left(\frac{60}{\Phi_{0}}\right)\approx 4.1-\ln\left(\Phi_{0}\right),

(69)

is always a good approximation as long
\displaystyle{\Phi_{0}\leq 15^{\circ}}
, where
\displaystyle{\Phi_{0}}
is in degrees.
Although
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{hs}}/\tau_{\text{lib}}}
only depends on
\displaystyle{\Phi_{0}}
, we expect that
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}/\tau_{\text{lib}}}
also depends on
\displaystyle{\varepsilon}
, since this parameter controls the maximum libration amplitude before the system becomes unstable. The smaller
\displaystyle{\varepsilon}
is, the larger the maximum libration amplitude is. Since
\displaystyle{\varepsilon=3\times 10^{-4}}
is the highest value allowing horseshoe-shaped orbits
(
Leleu et al., 2015
)
, the equality
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}=\tau_{\text{hs}}}
occurs at this value. If we fix
\displaystyle{\Phi_{0}=10^{\circ}}
, we have

1/2\,\tau_{\text{hs}}\leq\tau_{\text{dest}}\leq 2\,\tau_{\text{hs}}\;\Leftrightarrow\;10^{-9}\lesssim\varepsilon\lesssim 0.005,

(70)

and so,
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{hs}}}
and
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}}
do not differ by more than a factor 2 for a wide range of
\displaystyle{\varepsilon}
. We thus consider that
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}\approx\tau_{\text{hs}}}
in this range and equations (
63
) and (
69
) can be used to predict
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{dest}}}
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the tidal evolution of co-orbital planetary systems. We have shown that these systems are always unstable, regardless of the parameters and initial configuration. However, for well chosen parameters, the disruption timescale can be arbitrarily high if the planets orbit far enough from the central star and/or if the mass repartition between both planets is far enough from equal masses. Since the current detection methods of exoplanets (radial velocity and transit) favour the detection of close-in systems with roughly equal masses for the planets, this work gives a satisfactory explanation to why no co-orbital planets have been discovered so far, although planetary formation models predict their formation. In figure
3
, we give a useful plot to predict if an already detected exoplanet may have a co-orbital companion.

We provide analytic criteria to determine the evolution timescales of a co-orbital system, which allow us to predict if a system is eccentric or circular when it is on the verge of being destroyed and the eccentric proper-mode it has settled into. We also show the difference between the two proper-mode according to whether the system is in the tadpole or horseshoe region. We show that the tidal evolution is essentially dominated by three characteristics timescales.
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{L}}}
and
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{AL}}}
are responsible for damping the proper-modes of the eccentricity and
\displaystyle{\tau_{\text{lib}}}
for pumping the libration amplitude of the resonant angle. The latter is also responsible for the destruction of the co-orbital configuration, and it provides us with an estimate of its life time.

We obtain a complete system of averaged equations describing the evolution of the system which is consistent with the conservation of the total angular momentum at any order in eccentricity and yields no significant difference with respect to the
\displaystyle{n}
-body direct equations when compared by numerical integrations. This shows that the averaging process over the orbital period, as well as all approximations made are correct. Therefore, this system of equations allows us to perform fast numerical integrations with a larger time-step.

In the present work we did not consider tides raised on the star (see Eq. (
34
)). We did not consider the general relativity and the rotational oblateness of the bodies either, since these conservative effects only slightly modify the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues of the linearized system (Eq. (
60
)), which only results in small changes in the fundamental frequencies
\displaystyle{\nu,\,g_{1}\,\text{and}\,g_{2}}
, and does not impact the timescales of the co-orbital system.
The quasi-satellite configuration
(
Pousse et al., 2017
)
, which is a peculiar type of 1:1 mean motion resonance that does not exist at zero eccentricity, was also not studied by this work, because our model is singular when the planets have the same mean longitude. However, since tidal effects damp the eccentricities of the planets, we believe that this type of orbit is even more unstable than the Lagrange and anti-Lagrange configurations. This was numerically confirmed by
Rodríguez et al., 2013
in the case of two identical planets.

Appendix E
Diagonalization of a perturbed matrix

We show here the method that we use to obtain the eigenvalues of a perturbed matrix once a diagonal basis of the principal matrix is known. Indeed, the computation of the eigenvalues (
60
) is equivalent to finding the roots of the characteristic polynomial of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}_{0}+\mathcal{Z}_{1}}
, given in equations (
94
) and (
95
). Even when the degree of this polynomial is reduced to four using the
\displaystyle{0}
eigenvalue, it is hard to obtain its roots in a convenient form. The method we use here, briefly presented by
(
Laskar et al., 2012
)
, gives the eigenvalues and eigenvectors very easily.

Let
\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{M}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)}
be a
\displaystyle{n\times n}
complex matrix where
\displaystyle{\zeta}
is a small quantity with respect to
\displaystyle{1}
. Assume that we know a diagonal basis for
\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}_{0}}

\mathcal{D}_{0}=P_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{0}P_{0}=\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{i}\right),

(81)

where the columns of
\displaystyle{P_{0}}
are the eigenvectors of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
and the
\displaystyle{\lambda_{i}}
its eigenvalues, which are not assumed to be of multiplicity one but which are assumed to be sorted by value, that is, equal eigenvalues are consecutive. This does not restrict the generality, as any permutation can be applied on the columns of
\displaystyle{P_{0}}
to achieve that. We now define

\mathcal{Q}_{1}=P_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{1}P_{0}.

(82)

If
\displaystyle{P}
is the matrix of the eigenvectors of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{Q}_{1}}
, and since
\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{Q}_{1}}
is near diagonal, we write

(83)

We have

P^{-1}\left(\mathcal{D}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{Q}_{1}\right)P=\mathcal{D}_{0}+\zeta\left(\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}\mathcal{Q}_{1}+\left[\mathcal{D}_{0},P_{1}\right]\phantom{\frac{1}{1}\!\!\!\!\!}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\zeta^{2}\right),

(84)

where
\displaystyle{\left[\mathcal{D}_{0},P_{1}\right]=\mathcal{D}_{0}P_{1}-P_{1}\mathcal{D}_{0}}

\mathcal{Q}_{1}+\left[\mathcal{D}_{0},P_{1}\right]=\mathcal{D}_{1},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}
. The solution of the cohomological equation is

p_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{q_{i,j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}&\text{ if }\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j},\vskip 2.84526pt\\
0&\text{ else},\end{matrix}\right.

(86)

The matrix
\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is now block diagonal :

P^{-1}P_{0}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{M}_{1}\right)P_{0}P=\text{diag}\left(\mathcal{B}_{0}^{1}+\zeta\mathcal{B}_{1}^{1},\,...\,,\mathcal{B}_{0}^{r}+\zeta\mathcal{B}_{1}^{r}\right),\;\;\;\;\;r\leq n

(88)

where
\displaystyle{\forall i\leq r\;\;\exists k\leq n}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{0}^{i}=\lambda_{k}I_{m(k)},

(89)

and
\displaystyle{m(k)}
denotes the multiplicity of
\displaystyle{\lambda_{k}}
and thus the size of the block. The computation of the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}_{0}+\zeta\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is reduced to the computation of the eigenvalues of the blocks
\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}_{0}^{i}+\zeta\mathcal{B}_{1}^{i}}
