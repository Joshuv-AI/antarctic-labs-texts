Nonlinear static isogeometric analysis of arbitrarily curved Kirchhoff-Love shells
G. Radenković, A. Borković, B. Marussig

Abstract




Abstract

The geometrically rigorous nonlinear analysis of elastic shells is considered in the context of finite, but small, strain theory. The research is focused on the introduction of the full shell metric and examination of its influence on the nonlinear structural response.
The exact relation between the reference and equidistant strains is employed and the complete analytic elastic constitutive relation between energetically conjugated forces and strains is derived.
Utilizing these strict relations, the geometric stiffness matrix is derived explicitly by the variation of the unknown metric. Moreover, a compact form of this matrix is presented. Despite the linear displacement distribution due to the Kirchhoff-Love hypothesis, a nonlinear strain distribution arises along the shell thickness. This fact is sometimes disregarded for the nonlinear analysis of thin shells based on the initial geometry, thereby ignoring the strong curviness of a shell at some subsequent configuration. We show that the curviness of a shell at each configuration determines the appropriate shell formulation. For shells that become strongly curved at some configurations during deformation, the nonlinear distribution of strain throughout the thickness must be considered in order to obtain accurate results. We investigate four computational models: one based on the full analytical constitutive relation, and three simplified ones.
Robustness, accuracy and relative efficiency of the presented formulation are examined via selected numerical experiments.
Our main finding is that the employment of the full metric is often required when the complete response of the shells is sought, even for the initially thin shells. Finally, the simplified model that provided the best balance between efficiency and accuracy is suggested for the nonlinear analysis of strongly curved shells.

Keywords
: strongly curved Kirchhoff-Love shell; nonlinear analysis; reciprocal shift tensor; analytical constitutive relation

Finally, the validity of the present formulation is confirmed via several numerical examples. By considering the efficiency analysis and accuracy of results, the most involved reduced model is recommended as the favorable one.

The paper is structured as follows. The next section deals with the metric of a shell with the emphasis on the reciprocal shift tensor. Subsequently, the strict definition of the shell’s kinematics is introduced and the rigorous relation between equidistant and reference strains is highlighted. The virtual power of an arbitrarily curved shell is postulated and discretized in the fourth section. It is shown that through-the-thickness integration can be performed analytically. Thorough numerical experiments are given in the fifth section and these are followed by the conclusions.

2
Metric of the shell continuum

A detailed and rigorous definition of the shell metric is presented in this section. The classical KL assumption states that a straight line element is rigid and remains perpendicular to the shell’s midsurface in the deformed configuration. This assumption leads to the degeneration of a 3D continuum model into an arbitrarily shaped surface. Note that another approach also exists in which the surface is primarily observed and the so-called directors are introduced at each point on the surface,
[
1
,
2
]
. The material law that follows from this approach, however, neglects the influence of curvature of the shell, restricting its applicability solely to small-curvature shells,
[
2
]
. The present analysis is performed with respect to the convective frame of reference while the complete shell kinematics is defined by the translation of shell midsurface.

Remark
. It is interesting to note that the convective coordinate frame is not an arbitrary choice among the spatial coordinate frames. We must find coordinates and base vectors in order to fully describe the metric of the spatial configuration. This is a significant issue since we are essentially dealing with one equation with two unknowns. The solution is to keep one quantity constant, either the coordinates or the base vectors. It is a standard practice in computational mechanics to keep the coordinates constant, which gives rise to the convective description, where every particle at each configuration has the same coordinates.

Regarding the notation, boldface lowercase letters are used for vectors, while uppercase is used for tensors and matrices. An overbar designates the quantity at the equidistant surface of the shell while the asterisk sign is used to designate deformed configuration. Finally, the hat symbol designates the local component of a vector, with respect to the curvilinear coordinates. The direct and index notations are applied simultaneously, depending on the context, and the standard summation convention is adopted. The Greek index letters take values of 1 and 2 while the Latin indices take values of 1, 2, and 3. Partial and covariant derivatives with respect to the
m
th
coordinate are designated with
()_{,m}
and
()_{|m}
, respectively.

The elaboration on the NURBS-based IGA surface modeling is excluded for brevity since it is readily available in the literature. For a detailed insight into the IGA, references
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
are recommended.

2.1
Metric of the midsurface

The midsurface of a shell with respect to the Cartesian coordinates is determined by its position vector
\textbf{r}=\{x=x^{1},y=x^{2},z=x^{3}\}
which will be described as:

\textbf{r}=x^{m}\textbf{i}_{m}=\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{J=1}^{M}R_{IJ}(\xi,\eta)\textbf{r}_{IJ},\quad x^{m}=\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{J=1}^{M}R_{IJ}(\theta^{\alpha})x^{m}_{IJ},

(1)

where
R_{IJ}
are bivariate NURBS basis functions,
\textbf{r}_{IJ}=\{x_{IJ},y_{IJ},z_{IJ}\}
are the position vectors of the control points
IJ
, while
N
and
M
are the total numbers of control points along the
\xi
and
\eta
directions, respectively,
[
10
]
. Furthermore,
\textbf{i}^{m}=\textbf{i}_{m}
are the base vectors of the Cartesian coordinate system while
\theta^{\alpha}
are coordinates
\xi
and
\eta
such that
\theta^{1}=\xi
and
\theta^{2}=\eta
, see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
The mapping between (center) the parameter domain
\mathscr{P}
, (left) reference and (right) deformed configurations of a Kirchhoff-Love shell. The shell boundaries are colored with solid green lines, the equidistant surface is indicated by dashed red lines, and the midsurface is colored in gray with black boundaries. Quantities at the deformed configuration are denoted with an asterisk while those at the equidistant surfaces have an overbar.

Tangent vectors of the midsurface are given by

\textbf{g}_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial\textbf{r}}{\partial\theta^{\alpha}}=x^{m}_{,\alpha}\textbf{i}_{m},\quad x^{m}_{,\alpha}=\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{J=1}^{M}R_{IJ,\alpha}x^{m}_{IJ},

(2)

where
x^{m}_{,\alpha}
are the partial derivatives of the Cartesian components of the midsurface with respect to the
\theta^{\alpha}
coordinates. In order to describe the complete geometry of a shell, the third coordinate
\theta^{3}=\zeta
is required. Due to the KL hypothesis, this coordinate line is rigid, straight and perpendicular to the
\theta^{\alpha}
coordinate lines. It follows that the base vector
\textbf{g}_{3}
and its reciprocal counterpart
\textbf{g}^{3}
are the same. They have the unit length and represent the normal at each point of the shell midsurface:

\textbf{g}^{3}=\textbf{g}_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}(\textbf{g}_{1}\times\textbf{g}_{2})=x^{m}_{,3}\textbf{i}_{m},\quad x^{m}_{,3}=x_{m,3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}x^{k}_{,1}x^{l}_{,2}e_{klm},

(3)

where
e_{klm}
is permutation symbol while
g
is the determinant of the metric tensor of shell midsurface:

g_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}x^{m}_{,1}x_{m,1}&x^{m}_{,1}x_{m,2}&0\\
x^{m}_{,2}x_{m,1}&x^{m}_{,2}x_{m,2}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}&0\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\;g_{12}=g_{21},\;g=\det g_{ij}=g_{11}g_{22}-{g_{12}}^{2}.

(4)

The reciprocal metric tensor of midsurface is:

g^{ij}={g_{ij}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{g}\begin{bmatrix}g_{22}&-g_{12}&0\\
-g_{12}&g_{11}&0\\
0&0&g\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g^{11}&g^{12}&0\\
g^{21}&g^{22}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\;\det g^{ij}=1/g,

(5)

while the reciprocal tangent vectors are:

\displaystyle\textbf{g}^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}(\textbf{g}_{2}\times\textbf{g}_{3})=x^{,1}_{n}\textbf{i}^{n},\quad x^{,1}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}x^{m}_{,2}x^{k}_{,3}e_{mkn},

(6)

\displaystyle\textbf{g}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}(\textbf{g}_{3}\times\textbf{g}_{1})=x^{,2}_{n}\textbf{i}^{n},\quad x^{,2}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}x^{m}_{,3}x^{k}_{,1}e_{mkn}.

In order to completely define the metric of the midsurface, the Christoffel symbols are required. They stem from the differentiation of the base vectors with respect to the curvilinear coordinates,
[
1
]
:

\textbf{g}_{\alpha,\beta}=x^{m}_{,\alpha\beta}\textbf{i}_{m}=\Gamma_{\alpha\beta k}\textbf{g}^{k}=\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}\textbf{g}_{n}\Rightarrow\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{\alpha\beta k}&=x^{m}_{,\alpha\beta}x_{m,k}\\
\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}&=x^{m}_{,\alpha\beta}x^{,n}_{m}\end{aligned}\end{cases}\Rightarrow\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}=g^{nk}\Gamma_{\alpha\beta k}.

(7)

Here,
\Gamma_{\alpha\beta k}
and
\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}
are the Christoffel symbols of the first and the second kind, respectively. The components of the differentiated base vectors with respect to the Cartesian coordinates are:

x^{m}_{,\alpha\beta}=\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}x^{m}_{,n}=\Gamma_{\alpha\beta n}x^{m,n}.

(8)

The Christoffel symbols of the second kind related to the third coordinate line
\Gamma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}
are of special importance since they represent curvatures of the surface with respect to the coordinate system
\theta^{\alpha}
. They determine the curvature tensor and it is a common practice to designate a particular symbol for them, i.e.,
\Gamma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}=b_{\alpha\beta}
. The components of the curvature tensor of a surface can be determined by differentiating the normal with respect to the curvilinear coordinates:

\textbf{g}_{3,\alpha}=-\Gamma^{\mu}_{3\alpha}\textbf{g}_{\mu}=-b^{\mu}_{\alpha}\textbf{g}_{\mu},\;(\Gamma^{3}_{3\alpha}=0),

(9)

where
b^{\mu}_{\alpha}
are mixed components of the curvature tensor which relate to the covariant ones by
b^{\mu}_{\alpha}=g^{\mu\nu}b_{\nu\alpha}
,
[
1
]
.

2.2
Metric of an equidistant surface

The position vector of an equidistant surface and its appropriate tangent vectors, Fig.
1
, are:

\displaystyle\bar{\textbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\textbf{r}+\zeta\textbf{g}_{3},

(10)

\displaystyle\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bar{\textbf{r}}}{\partial\theta^{\alpha}}=\textbf{g}_{\alpha}-\zeta b^{\nu}_{\alpha}\textbf{g}_{\nu}=(\delta^{\nu}_{\alpha}-\zeta b^{\nu}_{\alpha})\textbf{g}_{\nu}=\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}\textbf{g}_{\nu},\;\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}=\delta^{\nu}_{\alpha}-\zeta b^{\nu}_{\alpha},

where
\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}
are components of the shift tensor,
[
46
]
, while
\delta^{\nu}_{\alpha}
are components of the Kronecker tensor. This expression reveals the fact that the normal
\textbf{g}_{3}=\bar{\textbf{g}}_{3}
and the tangent vectors of an equidistant surface are mutually orthogonal:

\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\alpha}=\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}g_{3\nu}=0.

(11)

Furthermore, the metric tensor and its determinant at an equidistant surface are:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\alpha}\cdotp\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\beta}=\bar{C}^{\mu}_{\alpha}\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\beta}g_{\mu\nu},

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{g}

\displaystyle=\det\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\alpha\beta}=\left(\det\bar{C}^{\mu}_{\alpha}\right)\left(\det\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\beta}\right)g.

Using the relations

\det\bar{C}^{\mu}_{\alpha}=\det( \delta^\mu_\alpha- \zeta b^\mu_\alpha)=1-\zeta b^{\nu}_{\nu}+\zeta^{2}b,\quad b=\det b^{\mu}_{\alpha},\quad b^{\nu}_{\nu}=\Tr b^{\mu}_{\alpha},

(13)

the determinant of the metric tensor at an equidistant surface reduces to:

\bar{g}=\left(1-\zeta b^{\nu}_{\nu}+\zeta^{2}b\right)^{2}g={g_{0}}^{2}g,\quad g_{0}=\det\bar{C}^{\mu}_{\alpha},

(14)

where
g_{0}
is the determinant of the shift tensor, also known as
the shifter
,
[
19
]
, while
b^{\nu}_{\nu}
and
b
are the trace and determinant of the curvature tensor, respectively. Note that the trace of the curvature tensor is equal to the sum of the principal curvatures,
b^{\nu}_{\nu}=K_{1}+K_{2}=2H
, where
H
is
the mean curvature
,
[
46
]
.

For the explicit derivation of the constitutive relation which follows, it is crucial to establish the analytical relation between the tangent vectors of the equidistant and middle surfaces. From Eq. (
10
) it follows that the reciprocal shift tensor
C^{\alpha}_{\nu}=\left(\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}\right)^{-1}
could be introduced as well:

\bar{\textbf{g}}^{\alpha}=C^{\alpha}_{\nu}\textbf{g}^{\nu},

(15)

and it can be easily numerically calculated. However, we are interested in its analytical form. Let us utilize the fact that the base vector
\textbf{g}_{3}
is independent of
\zeta
coordinate and observe the vector product:

\bar{\textbf{g}}_{3}\times\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\mu}=\textbf{g}_{3}\times\bar{C}^{\gamma}_{\mu}\textbf{g}_{\gamma}=\sqrt{g}\bar{C}^{\gamma}_{\mu}e_{3\gamma\nu}\textbf{g}^{\nu}.

(16)

If we multiply this expression with
e^{3\mu\alpha}
:

\sqrt{\bar{g}}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\bar{\textbf{g}}^{\beta}=\sqrt{g}\bar{C}^{\gamma}_{\mu}\delta^{\mu\alpha}_{\gamma\nu}\textbf{g}^{\nu},

(17)

the analytical form of the reciprocal shift tensor follows as:

C^{\alpha}_{\nu}=\frac{1}{g_{0}}\delta^{\alpha\mu}_{\nu\gamma}\bar{C}^{\gamma}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{g_{0}}\left[\left(1-\zeta b^{\gamma}_{\gamma}\right)\delta^{\alpha}_{\nu}+\zeta b^{\alpha}_{\nu}\right].

(18)

The same result can be found in
[
1
]
and it allows us to represent the reciprocal metric tensor at an arbitrary point as an analytic function of the metric and curvature of the midsurface:

\bar{g}^{\alpha\beta}=\bar{\textbf{g}}^{\alpha}\bar{\textbf{g}}^{\beta}=C^{\alpha}_{\mu}C^{\beta}_{\nu}g^{\mu\nu}.

(19)

The tensor
C^{\alpha}_{\mu}C^{\beta}_{\nu}
is a rational function of the
\zeta
coordinate where the numerator and denominator are polynomials of the
2^{nd}
and
4^{th}
degree, respectively. The relation between the metrics of the equidistant and reference surfaces directly influences the equivalent strain relations,
[
2
]
. In order to simplify the computational model, many types of approximations are readily introduced by researchers, mostly based on first- or second-order Taylor approximations of the shift tensor,
[
47
]
. The exact analytical form of the reciprocal shift tensor, on the other hand, is often disregarded. An interesting discussion on the influence of the exact shift tensor is given in
[
2
]
.

Finally, let us define the relations between a differential area at the equidistant and middle surfaces which follow from (
14
). If we notice that these areas are:

\displaystyle\differential{\veq{a}_\alpha}

\displaystyle=\differential{\seq{a}_\alpha\veq{g}^\alpha}=\differential{\zeta}\textbf{g}_{3}\times\bar{\textbf{g}}_{\beta}=\differential{\zeta}\sqrt{\bar{g}}e_{3\beta\alpha}\bar{\textbf{g}}^{\alpha},\quad\differential{\seq{a}_\alpha}=\sqrt{\bar{g}}\differential{\zeta}e_{3\beta\alpha},

(20)

\displaystyle\differential{\ve{a}_\alpha}

\displaystyle=\differential{a_\alpha\ve{g}^\alpha}=\differential{\zeta}\textbf{g}_{3}\times\textbf{g}_{\beta}=\differential{\zeta}\sqrt{g}e_{3\beta\alpha}\textbf{g}^{\alpha},\quad\differential{a_\alpha}=\sqrt{g}\differential{\zeta}e_{3\beta\alpha},

the required relation follows as:

\differential{\seq{a}_\alpha}=\sqrt{\frac{\bar{g}}{g}}\differential{a_\alpha}=g_{0}\differential{a}_{\alpha}.

(21)

Additionally, the differential volume of a shell is:

\differential{v}=\left(\differential{\xi}\textbf{g}_{1}\times\differential{\eta}\textbf{g}_{2}\right)\cdotp\differential{\zeta}\textbf{g}_{3}=\sqrt{\bar{g}}\differential{\xi}\differential{\eta}\differential{\zeta}=g_{0}\differential{a}\differential{\zeta},

(22)

where
\differential{a}=\sqrt{g}\differential{\xi}\differential{\eta}
is the differential area of the midsurface.

3
Kirchhoff-Love shell theory

After the metric of the shell continuum is defined, the next step is to introduce some strain measure. For the convective coordinate frame, the Lagrange strain equals the difference between the current and reference metrics:

{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left({g}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}-{g}_{\alpha\beta}\right).

(23)

Due to the KL hypothesis, the components of the shear strain rate along the normal direction vanish, i.e.
d_{\alpha 3}=0
. Consequently, the position of the line element is completely determined by the translation of the midsurface, which is the only generalized coordinate of the KL shell. This fact gives rise to the so-called
rotation-free
shell theories,
[
11
]
. These formulations are especially efficient for the finite rotation analysis since the parametrization of the finite rotation variables is not required.

\displaystyle\textbf{q}^{\mathsf{T}}

(29)

and it is represented analogously:

\textbf{v}=\dot{\textbf{u}}=\textbf{N}\dot{\textbf{q}},\;\;\textbf{$\dot{\textbf{q}}$}^{\mathsf{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{$\dot{\textbf{u}}_{11}$}^{\mathsf{T}}&\textbf{$\dot{\textbf{u}}_{12}$}^{\mathsf{T}}&...&\textbf{$\dot{\textbf{u}}_{IJ}$}^{\mathsf{T}}&...&\textbf{$\dot{\textbf{u}}_{NM}$}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\dot{\textbf{u}}_{IJ}=\begin{bmatrix}v^{1}_{IJ}&v^{2}_{IJ}&v^{3}_{IJ}\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

The strain rate tensor is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient and it equals the material derivative of (
23
):

{d}_{\alpha\beta}=\dot{\epsilon}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{g}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{v}_{\alpha|\beta}+\hat{v}_{\beta|\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{v}_{\alpha,\beta}+\hat{v}_{\beta,\alpha}-2\hat{v}_{m}{\Gamma}^{*m}_{\alpha\beta}\right),

(31)

where
\hat{v}_{m}
are the components of the velocity vector with respect to the local curvilinear coordinates, which are related to the global ones as:

\textbf{v}=\hat{v}_{m}\textbf{g}^{*m}=\hat{v}^{m}\textbf{g}^{*}_{m}={v}_{k}\textbf{i}^{k}={v}^{k}\textbf{i}_{k}\Rightarrow\begin{cases}\hat{v}_{m}&={v}_{k}\textbf{i}^{k}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{*}_{m}={x}^{*k}_{,m}{v}_{k}\\
\hat{v}^{m}&={v}^{k}\textbf{i}_{k}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{*m}={x}^{*,m}_{k}{v}^{k}\end{cases}.

(32)

The covariant components of the strain rate with respect to the Cartesian coordinates can be written as:

\displaystyle{d}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({x}^{*k}_{,\alpha}{v}_{k,\beta}+{x}^{*k}_{,\beta}{v}_{k,\alpha}+2{x}^{*k}_{,\alpha\beta}{v}_{k}-2{\Gamma}^{*m}_{\alpha\beta}{x}^{*n}_{,m}{v}_{n}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({x}^{*k}_{,\alpha}{v}_{k,\beta}+{x}^{*k}_{,\beta}{v}_{k,\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\beta}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{\beta}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right),

while the trace of the strain rate tensor is:

{d}^{\alpha}_{\alpha}={g}^{*\alpha\beta}{d}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left({x}^{*,\beta}_{n}{v}^{n}_{,\beta}+{x}^{*,\alpha}_{n}{v}^{n}_{,\alpha}\right)={x}^{*,\alpha}_{n}{v}^{n}_{,\alpha}=\textbf{g}^{*\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}.

(34)

The material derivative of (
24
) gives the velocity vector of an equidistant surface:

\bar{\textbf{v}}=\textbf{v}(\zeta)=\textbf{v}+\zeta\textbf{v}_{3},

(35)

where the following notation is introduced:

\bar{\textbf{v}}=\dot{\bar{\textbf{r}}}^{*}=\bar{v}^{n}\textbf{i}_{n}=\dot{\bar{x}}^{*n}\textbf{i}_{n},\;\textbf{v}_{m}=\dot{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{m}={v}^{n}_{,m}\textbf{i}_{n}=\dot{x}^{*n}_{,m}\textbf{i}_{n}.

(36)

Thus, the vector
\textbf{v}_{3}
is the material derivative of the deformed normal:

\displaystyle\textbf{v}_{3}

\displaystyle=\dot{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{3}={v}_{k,3}\textbf{i}^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}^{*}}}\left(\textbf{v}_{1}\times\textbf{g}^{*}_{2}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{1}\times\textbf{v}_{2}-\frac{\dot{g}^{*}}{2\sqrt{{g}^{*}}}\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle{v}_{k,3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}^{*}}}\left[\left({v}^{m}_{,1}{x}^{*n}_{,2}+{x}^{*m}_{,1}{v}^{n}_{,2}\right){e}_{mnk}-\frac{\dot{g}^{*}}{2\sqrt{{g}^{*}}}{x}^{*}_{k,3}\right],

and its derivation requires special attention. Firstly, the material derivative of the determinant of the metric tensor, utilizing Eqs. (
4
), (
31
), and (
33
), is computed:

\displaystyle\dot{g}^{*}

\displaystyle=2\left({d}_{11}{g}^{*}_{22}+{d}_{22}{g}^{*}_{11}-2{d}_{12}{g}^{*}_{12}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=2\left[\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{1}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{1}\right){g}^{*}_{22}+\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{2}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{2}\right){g}^{*}_{11}-2\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{1}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{2}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{2}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{1}\right){g}^{*}_{12}\right],

which leads to:

\frac{\dot{g}^{*}}{2{g}^{*}}={d}_{11}{g}^{*11}+{d}_{12}{g}^{*12}+{d}_{21}{g}^{*21}+{d}_{22}{g}^{*22}={d}^{1}_{1}+{d}^{2}_{2}={d}^{\alpha}_{\alpha}.

(39)

If we represent the tangent vectors of the deformed configuration as:

\displaystyle\textbf{g}^{*}_{1}

\displaystyle={x}^{*n}_{,1}\textbf{i}_{n}=\sqrt{{g}^{*}}\left(\textbf{g}^{*2}\times\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\right),\quad{x}^{*n}_{,1}=\sqrt{{g}^{*}}{x}^{*,2}_{k}{x}^{*}_{l,3}{e}^{kln},

(40)

\displaystyle\textbf{g}^{*}_{2}

\displaystyle={x}^{*n}_{,2}\textbf{i}_{n}=\sqrt{{g}^{*}}\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\times\textbf{g}^{*1}\right),\quad{x}^{*n}_{,2}=\sqrt{{g}^{*}}{x}^{*}_{k,3}{x}^{*,1}_{l}{e}^{kln},

the first part of Eq. (
37
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}^{*}}}\left(\textbf{v}_{1}\times\textbf{g}^{*}_{2}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{1}\times\textbf{v}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\textbf{v}_{1}\times\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\times\textbf{g}^{*1}\right)-\textbf{v}_{2}\times\left(\textbf{g}^{*2}\times\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=\left(\textbf{g}^{*\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}-\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*\alpha}.

By the insertion of Eqs. (
39
), (
34

41

37

\displaystyle\textbf{v}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\textbf{g}^{*\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}-\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*\alpha}-\left(\textbf{g}^{*\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}=-\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*\alpha},

(42)

\displaystyle{v}_{k,3}

\displaystyle={x}^{*,\alpha}_{m}{v}^{m}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{k,3}-{x}^{*,\alpha}_{k}{v}^{m}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{m,3}-{x}^{*,\alpha}_{m}{v}^{m}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{k,3}=-{x}^{*,\alpha}_{k}{v}^{m}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{m,3},

which is the same result as calculated in
[
1
]
. Finally, the components of velocity at an equidistant surface (

\bar{\textbf{v}}=\bar{v}_{n}\textbf{i}^{n}=\textbf{v}-\zeta\left(\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\textbf{g}^{*\alpha},\;\bar{v}_{n}={v}_{n}-\zeta{x}^{*,\alpha}_{n}{v}^{m}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{m,3}.

(43)

Remark.
Proving that the line element remains perpendicular to the midsurface at each configuration is now straightforward. That is, multiplying Eq. (

{x}^{*k}_{,\alpha}
gives:

{x}^{*k}_{,\alpha}{v}_{k,3}=-{x}^{*k}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*,\beta}_{k}{v}^{m}_{,\beta}{x}^{*}_{m,3}=-{\delta}^{\beta}_{\alpha}{v}^{m}_{,\beta}{x}^{*}_{m,3}=-{v}^{m}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{m,3},

(44)

and the shear strain rates in the planes perpendicular to the tangent planes of the midsurface vanish:

2{d}_{\alpha 3}={x}^{*n}_{,\alpha}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}{v}_{n,\alpha}=0.

(45)

Additionally, the strain rate along the normal also vanishes at each configuration:

{d}_{33}={x}^{*n}_{,3}{v}_{n,3}=-{x}^{*n}_{,3}{x}^{*,\alpha}_{n}{v}^{k}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{k,3}=-{\delta}^{\alpha}_{3}{v}^{k}_{,\alpha}{x}^{*}_{k,3}=0.

(46)

3.2
Strain rate at an equidistant surface

The crucial step for the derivation of structural theories is the definition of the strain at each point of a structure via chosen reference quantities. It allows a dimensional reduction from 3D to 2D. The strain rate at an equidistant surface is, analogously to Eq. (
33
):

\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}={d}_{\alpha\beta}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{\alpha}\cdotp\bar{\textbf{v}}_{\beta}+\bar{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{\beta}\cdotp\bar{\textbf{v}}_{\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{x}^{*n}_{,\alpha}\bar{v}_{n,\beta}+\bar{x}^{*n}_{,\beta}\bar{v}_{n,\alpha}\right),

(47)

where the components of the velocity gradient at an arbitrary point,
\bar{v}_{n,\beta}
, follow from Eq. (
43
):

\bar{v}_{n,\alpha}={v}_{n,\alpha}+\zeta{v}_{n,3\alpha},

(48)

while the derivative of the velocity of normal stems from Eq. (
42
):

\displaystyle{v}_{n,3\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\left({x}^{*,\mu}_{n,\alpha}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3}+{x}^{*,\mu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu\alpha}{x}^{*k}_{,3}+{x}^{*,\mu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3\alpha}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle={\Gamma}^{*\mu}_{\alpha\nu}{x}^{*,\nu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3}-{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}{x}^{*,3}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3}-{x}^{*,\mu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu\alpha}{x}^{*k}_{,3}+{x}^{*,\mu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{b}^{*\nu}_{\alpha}{x}^{*k}_{,\nu}.

Remark.
Utilizing the last two expressions, we can show that the shear strain rate
{d}_{\alpha 3}
is zero at an equidistant surface, as well as at the midsurface:

\displaystyle 2\bar{d}_{\alpha 3}

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{3}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)=\bar{x}^{*n}_{,\alpha}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}\bar{v}_{n,\alpha}

(50)

\displaystyle=\left({\delta}^{\mu}_{\alpha}-\zeta{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}\right){x}^{*n}_{,\mu}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}\left({v}_{n,\alpha}+\zeta{v}_{n,3\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle={x}^{*n}_{,\alpha}{v}_{n,3}-\zeta{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}{x}^{*n}_{,\mu}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}{v}_{n,\alpha}

\displaystyle+\zeta{x}^{*n}_{,3}\left({\Gamma}^{*\mu}_{\alpha\nu}{x}^{*,\nu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3}-{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}{x}^{*,3}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3}-{x}^{*,\mu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu\alpha}{x}^{*k}_{,3}-{x}^{*,\mu}_{n}{v}_{k,\mu}{b}^{*\nu}_{\alpha}{x}^{*k}_{,\nu}\right)

\displaystyle={x}^{*n}_{,\alpha}{v}_{n,3}-\zeta{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}{x}^{*n}_{,\mu}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}{v}_{n,\alpha}-\zeta{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}{v}_{k,\mu}{x}^{*k}_{,3}

\displaystyle={x}^{*n}_{,\alpha}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}{v}_{n,\alpha}-\zeta{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}\left({x}^{*n}_{,\mu}{v}_{n,3}+{x}^{*n}_{,3}{v}_{n,\mu}\right)=0,

which proves that the line segment is perpendicular to the equidistant surfaces as well as to the midsurface.

Now we have all the ingredients required for the exact relation between equidistant and reference strain rates. Inserting Eqs. (

) and (
49
) into Eq. (

), returns the components of the equidistant strain rate:

\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}={A}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}{d}_{\mu\nu}-\zeta{B}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\dot{\kappa}_{\mu\nu},

(51)

where:

\displaystyle{A}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\bar{C}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}\left({\delta}^{\nu}_{\beta}+\zeta{b}^{*\nu}_{\beta}\right)+\bar{C}^{*\nu}_{\beta}\left({\delta}^{\mu}_{\alpha}+\zeta{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}\right)\right]={\delta}^{\mu}_{\alpha}{\delta}^{\nu}_{\beta}-\zeta^{2}{b}^{*\mu}_{\alpha}{b}^{*\nu}_{\beta},

(52)

\displaystyle{B}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({\delta}^{\nu}_{\alpha}\bar{C}^{*\mu}_{\beta}+{\delta}^{\mu}_{\beta}\bar{C}^{*\nu}_{\alpha}\right),

while the tensor of the rate of curvature change of the midsurface,
\dot{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}
, is introduced as:

\displaystyle\dot{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\dot{b}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}=\dot{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\dot{\textbf{g}}^{*}_{3}=\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{*}_{3}+\textbf{g}^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{3}={v}_{k,\alpha\beta}{x}^{*k}_{,3}+{\Gamma}^{*n}_{\alpha\beta}{x}^{*k}_{,n}{v}_{k,3}

(53)

\displaystyle={x}^{*k}_{,3}{v}_{k,\alpha\beta}-{\Gamma}^{*\nu}_{\alpha\beta}{x}^{*k}_{,\nu}{x}^{*,\mu}_{k}{v}^{m}_{,\mu}{x}^{*}_{m,3}-{b}^{*}_{\alpha\beta}{x}^{*k}_{,3}{x}^{*,\mu}_{k}{v}^{m}_{,\mu}{x}^{*}_{m,3}={x}^{*k}_{,3}\left({v}_{k,\alpha\beta}-{\Gamma}^{*\mu}_{\alpha\beta}{v}_{k,\mu}\right).

The detailed derivation of Eqs. (

) and (

) is given in Appendix A. Equations (

. This assumption corresponds to the following approximation:

{A}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}={B}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\approx{\delta}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta},

(54)

which reduces Eq. (

\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}={d}_{\alpha\beta}-\zeta\dot{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}.

(55)

A notable exception is the reference
[

]
where one type of simplified, but nonlinear, strain distribution is utilized. However, the comparison of different models is not considered.

It is interesting to note that the physical components of strain are distributed nonlinearly due to the metric, although the previous equation imposes a linear strain distribution:

\bar{d}_{(\alpha\beta)}=\frac{\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}}{\sqrt{\bar{g}_{\alpha\alpha}\bar{g}_{\beta\beta}}}.

(56)

The linear analysis performed in
[

]
shows that for thick shells, the utilization of a nonlinear strain distribution and an exact shifter is recommended, along with exact integration. It is also noted that for

, the error introduced by the assumption of a linear strain distribution is not significant.

4
Finite element formulation

In line with the previous derivation, we will formulate isogeometric finite elements of the KL shell using the principle of virtual power. The present formulation follows the approach given in
[
45
]
.
The generalized coordinates are the components of the velocities of the control points.

Let us start from the generalized Hooke law for the linear elastic material, also known as the Saint Venant-Kirchhoff material model. This is well-suited for the small strain and large rotation analysis,
[
2
]
. The components of the stress and strain rates are related as:

\dot{\sigma}^{i}_{j}=2\mu{d}^{i}_{j}+\lambda{\delta}^{i}_{j}{d}^{k}_{k},

(57)

where
\mu
and
\lambda
are the Lamé material parameters. The plane stress assumption states that the normal component of the stress rate in the direction of the shell thickness is negligible compared to the other components:

\dot{\sigma}^{3}_{3}=2\mu{d}^{3}_{3}+\lambda\left({d}^{\alpha}_{\alpha}+{d}^{3}_{3}\right)=0,

(58)

and the corresponding strain rate becomes:

{d}^{3}_{3}=-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}{d}^{\alpha}_{\alpha}.

(59)

The existence of the
{d}^{3}_{3}
component violates the KL hypothesis and it is neglected here, together with the
\dot{\sigma}^{3}_{3}
. This is a known inconsistency of the KL theory caused by its approximate nature. Nevertheless, a great quantity of literature accounts for this component of strain rate since it increases the accuracy of the mechanical model and is easily introduced via the static condensation of the material tensor,
[
13
]
.

Here, the plane stress and the plane strain conditions are enforced and the stress-strain relation takes its final form:

\dot{\sigma}^{\alpha\beta}=2\mu\left({g}^{*\alpha\nu}{g}^{*\beta\gamma}+\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}{g}^{*\alpha\beta}{g}^{*\nu\gamma}\right){d}_{\nu\gamma}={D}^{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}{d}_{\nu\gamma},

(60)

where
{D}^{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}
are the components of the constitutive, material, tensor.

4.1
Principle of virtual power

The principle of virtual power states that at any time, the total power of the external, internal and inertial forces is zero for any admissible virtual state of motion. If the inertial effects are neglected and only the surface loads are considered, it can be written as:

\delta P=\int_{v}{\sigma}^{ij}\delta{d}_{ij}\differential{v}-\int_{a}\hat{p}^{i}\delta\hat{v}_{i}\differential{a}=\int_{v}\bm{\sigma}:\delta\textbf{d}\differential{v}-\int_{a}\textbf{p}\delta\textbf{v}\differential{a}=0,

(61)

where
\bm{\sigma}
is the Cauchy stress tensor,
d
is the strain rate tensor, and
p
is the vector of external surface loads. All these quantities are measured with respect to the current configuration. This configuration is unknown and its determination is the main subject of the nonlinear analysis. For problems with deformation-independent loads, it is only required to linearize the Cauchy stress. At the current
(n+1)
configuration this stress is approximated by:

\displaystyle{}^{(n+1)}{\bm{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=^{(n)}_{(n+1)}{\bm{\sigma}}+\frac{{\operatorname{d}}^{t}\>{}^{(n)}{\bm{\sigma}}}{\operatorname{d}t}\Delta{t}_{(n+1)},

(62)

\displaystyle{}^{(n)}_{(n+1)}{\bar{\sigma}}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{{}^{(n)}{\bar{g}}}{{}^{(n+1)}{\bar{g}}}}\>^{(n)}{\bar{\sigma}}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{{}^{(n)}{g}_{0}}{{}^{(n+1)}{g}_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{{}^{(n)}{g}}{{}^{(n+1)}{g}}}\>^{(n)}{\bar{\sigma}}_{\alpha\beta},

where
{}^{(n)}_{(n+1)}{\bar{\sigma}}_{\alpha\beta}
are the components of the equidistant stress from the previous
(n)
configuration expressed with respect to the metric of the current
(n+1)
configuration. Similarly,
{}^{(n)}{\bar{\sigma}}_{\alpha\beta}
is the same quantity but expressed with respect to the previous
(n)
configuration. The additive decomposition of stress, as in Eq. (
62
), is only valid if all of the terms are expressed with respect to the same metric. However, the rigorous integration of this expression is far from trivial due to the ratio of shifters in two different configurations. If the incremental change of curvature is small, this ratio can be approximated as
{}^{(n)}{g}_{0}/^{(n+1)}{g}_{0}\approx 1
. An appropriate time derivative is designated with
\operatorname{d}^{t}()/\operatorname{d}t
while
\Delta{t}_{(n+1)}={t}_{(n+1)}-{t}_{(n)}
is the time increment.

The rate form of stress-strain relations requires an objective time derivative. Note that the material derivative is not objective, while the corotational (Jaumann) and convective time derivatives fulfill this property,
[
48
,
49
]
. The relation (
62
) is rewritten as:

^{(n+1)}{\bm{\sigma}}=^{(n)}{\bm{\sigma}}+\left({L}_{v}\>^{(n)}{\bm{\sigma}}\right)\Delta{t}_{(n+1)}

(63)

where
{L}_{v}
designates Lie time derivative which matches the convective derivative, for convective coordinates. With respect to the covariant metric base it is:

{L}_{v}\bm{\sigma}=\dot{\bm{\sigma}}-\textbf{L}\bm{\sigma}-\bm{\sigma}\textbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\textbf{L}=\hat{v}_{i|j}\textbf{g}^{i}\otimes\textbf{g}^{j},

(64)

where
L
is the spatial velocity gradient. On the other hand, since the material time derivative of the stress tensor reads:

\dot{\bm{\sigma}}=\dot{\sigma}^{ij}\textbf{g}_{i}\otimes\textbf{g}_{j}+\textbf{L}\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\sigma}\textbf{L}^{\mathsf{T}},

relation (
64
) returns:

{L}_{v}\bm{\sigma}=\dot{\sigma}^{ij}\textbf{g}_{i}\otimes\textbf{g}_{j}\Rightarrow\left({L}_{v}\bm{\sigma}\right)^{ij}=\dot{\sigma}^{ij}.

(65)

Therefore, the components of the stress rate tensor equal the material derivative of the components of the stress tensor. This is an important fact which will be utilized further.

After the insertion of Eqs. (
63
) and (
65
) into Eq. (
61
), the linearized form of the principle of virtual power at the current configuration is obtained:

\displaystyle\int_{v}\dot{\bm{\sigma}}:\delta\textbf{d}\differential{v}\Delta t+\int_{v}\bm{\sigma}:\delta\textbf{d}\differential{v}=\int_{a}\textbf{p}\delta\textbf{v}\differential{a},

(66)

\displaystyle\int_{v}\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}\delta\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}\differential{v}\Delta t+\int_{v}\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha\beta}\delta\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}\differential{v}=\int_{a}\hat{p}^{i}\delta\hat{v}_{i}\differential{a}.

From this equation and further in the text, the notation is slightly simplified for the sake of readability by removing the time indices and asterisks. This can be done without introducing any notational ambiguity since (i) the stress and strain rates are instantaneous quantities, while the known stress
\bm{\sigma}
is calculated at the previous configuration and transformed to the metric of the current configuration, see Eqs. (
62
) and (
66
), and (ii) all integrations are performed with respect to the metric of the current configuration, in accordance with the updated Lagrangian procedure,
[
50
]
.

In order to reduce the dimension from 3D to 2D, it is necessary to integrate the left-hand side of Eq. (
66
) along the thickness. Thus, integrals over the area of midsurface are obtained:

\displaystyle\int_{v}\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}\delta\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}\differential{v}\Delta t+\int_{v}\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha\beta}\delta\bar{d}_{\alpha\beta}\differential{v}=

\displaystyle\int_{a}\left(\dot{N}^{\mu\nu}\delta{d}_{\mu\nu}+\dot{M}^{\mu\nu}\delta\dot{\kappa}_{\mu\nu}\right)\differential{a}\Delta t

(67)

\displaystyle+\int_{a}\left({N}^{\mu\nu}\delta{d}_{\mu\nu}+{M}^{\mu\nu}\delta\dot{\kappa}_{\mu\nu}\right)\differential{a},

where
{N}^{\mu\nu}
and
{M}^{\mu\nu}
are stress resultants and stress couples, which are energetically conjugated with the reference strain rates of the midsurface,
{d}_{\mu\nu}
and
\dot{\kappa}_{\mu\nu}
, while
\dot{N}^{\mu\nu}
and
\dot{M}^{\mu\nu}
are their respective rates:

\displaystyle\dot{N}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}{A}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}{g}_{0}\differential{\zeta}=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}\left({\delta}^{\mu}_{\alpha}{\delta}^{\nu}_{\beta}-\zeta^{2}{b}^{\mu}_{\alpha}{b}^{\nu}_{\beta}\right){g}_{0}\differential{\zeta},

(68)

\displaystyle\dot{M}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\zeta\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}{B}^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\beta}{g}_{0}\differential{\zeta}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\zeta\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}\left({\delta}^{\nu}_{\alpha}\bar{C}^{\mu}_{\beta}+{\delta}^{\mu}_{\beta}\bar{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}\right){g}_{0}\differential{\zeta}.

These expressions are the same as those obtained in
[
51
]
but they are rarely applied for the nonlinear analysis due to the complexity. If we introduce the vectors of generalized section forces and strain rates of the shell midsurface:

\displaystyle\textbf{f}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{N}^{11}&{N}^{22}&{N}^{12}&{M}^{11}&{M}^{22}&{M}^{12}\end{bmatrix},

(69)

\displaystyle\textbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{d}_{11}&{d}_{22}&2{d}_{12}&\dot{\kappa}_{11}&\dot{\kappa}_{22}&2\dot{\kappa}_{12}\end{bmatrix},

Eq. (
66
) can be written in compact matrix form as:

\int_{a}\dot{\textbf{f}}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\textbf{e}\differential{a}\Delta t+\int_{a}\textbf{f}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\textbf{e}\differential{a}=\int_{a}\textbf{p}\delta\textbf{v}\differential{a}.

(70)

The geometrically exact relations (
67
) and (
68
) are crucial for the accurate formulation of structural shell theories. In particular, energetically conjugated pairs are defined rigorously and the appropriate constitutive matrices are guaranteed to be symmetric, as will be shown in the next section.

4.2
Analytical relation between energetically conjugated pairs

The integration represented with Eqs. (
67
) and (
68
) is an essential step for the development of the structural theory of shells, since it allows the calculation of virtual power by the reference quantities of the midsurface. This requires the constitutive relation between the stress and strain rates at an arbitrary point of the shell continuum. Analogously to Eq. (
60
), this relation is:

\dot{\bar{\sigma}}^{\alpha\beta}=2\mu\left(\bar{g}^{\alpha\nu}\bar{g}^{\beta\gamma}+\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\bar{g}^{\alpha\beta}\bar{g}^{\nu\gamma}\right)\bar{d}_{\nu\gamma}=\bar{D}^{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}\bar{d}_{\nu\gamma}.

(71)

Using Eq. (
19
), it is evident that the following transformation is valid:

\displaystyle\bar{D}^{\mu\nu\epsilon\varphi}

\displaystyle=2\mu\left({C}^{\mu}_{\rho}{C}^{\epsilon}_{\delta}{g}^{\rho\delta}{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}{C}^{\varphi}_{\beta}{g}^{\alpha\beta}+\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}{C}^{\mu}_{\rho}{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}{g}^{\rho\alpha}{C}^{\epsilon}_{\delta}{C}^{\varphi}_{\beta}{g}^{\delta\beta}\right)

(72)

\displaystyle={C}^{\mu}_{\rho}{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}{C}^{\epsilon}_{\delta}{C}^{\varphi}_{\beta}2\mu\left({g}^{\rho\delta}{g}^{\alpha\beta}+\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}{g}^{\rho\alpha}{g}^{\delta\beta}\right)={C}^{\mu}_{\rho}{C}^{\nu}_{\alpha}{C}^{\epsilon}_{\delta}{C}^{\varphi}_{\beta}{D}^{\rho\alpha\delta\beta}.

This relation explicitly relates the components of constitutive tensor at an arbitrary point with the ones at the midsurface via the reciprocal shift tensor. In this way, the constitutive relations between the energetically conjugated rates of generalized section forces and reference strain rates of the midsurface are:

\displaystyle\dot{N}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle={D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{M}{d}_{\gamma\lambda}+{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{MB}\dot{\kappa}_{\gamma\lambda},

(73)

\displaystyle\dot{M}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle={D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{BM}{d}_{\gamma\lambda}+{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{B}\dot{\kappa}_{\gamma\lambda},

where:

\displaystyle{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{M}

\displaystyle=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}{A}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}\bar{D}^{\mu\nu\delta\varphi}{A}^{\gamma\lambda}_{\delta\varphi}{g}_{0}\differential{\zeta},

(74)

\displaystyle{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{MB}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\zeta{A}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}\bar{D}^{\mu\nu\delta\varphi}{B}^{\gamma\lambda}_{\delta\varphi}{g}_{0}\differential{\zeta},

\displaystyle{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{BM}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\zeta{B}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}\bar{D}^{\mu\nu\delta\varphi}{A}^{\gamma\lambda}_{\delta\varphi}{g}_{0}\differential{\zeta}={D}^{\gamma\lambda\alpha\beta}_{MB},

\displaystyle{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{B}

\displaystyle=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\zeta^{2}{B}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}\bar{D}^{\mu\nu\delta\varphi}{B}^{\gamma\lambda}_{\delta\varphi}{g}_{0}\differential{\zeta}.

Now, we can represent Eq. (

\dot{\textbf{f}}=\textbf{D}\textbf{e},

(75)

where

is the symmetric constitutive tensor at the current configuration:

\textbf{D}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{D}_{M}&\textbf{D}_{MB}\\
\textbf{$\;\textbf{D}_{MB}$}^{\mathsf{T}}&\textbf{D}_{B}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{M}&{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{MB}\\
{D}^{\gamma\lambda\alpha\beta}_{MB}&{D}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\textbf{g}_{\alpha}\otimes\textbf{g}_{\beta}\otimes\textbf{g}_{\gamma}\otimes\textbf{g}_{\lambda}.

(76)

Complete expressions for the components of the material tensor

are given in Appendix B along with the closed-form solutions of the appropriate integrals. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first complete analytical representation of the constitutive relation between the energetically conjugated pairs for the elastic KL shell.

4.3
Variation of strains

Since the strain rate is a function of the generalized coordinates as well as the metric, we must vary it with respect to both arguments. By noting that the variation of the tangent vectors can be expressed as:

\delta\textbf{g}_{\alpha}=\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\Delta t,

(77)

the variations of the reference strains are given by:

\displaystyle\delta{d}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\delta\frac{1}{2}\left(\textbf{g}_{\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\beta}+\textbf{g}_{\beta}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\beta}+\delta\textbf{v}_{\beta}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right)\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\left(\textbf{g}_{\alpha}\cdotp\delta\textbf{v}_{\beta}+\textbf{g}_{\beta}\cdotp\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha}\right),

\displaystyle\delta\dot{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\delta\left[\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\left(\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\textbf{v}_{\mu}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\delta\textbf{v}_{3}\cdotp\left(\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\alpha\beta}\textbf{v}_{\lambda}\right)-\delta{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\lambda}\right)\right]\Delta t+\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha.\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\delta\textbf{v}_{\mu}\right).

Most of the terms in the previous expression can be easily computed since the variation is explicitly performed with respect to the unknown variables. However, the first addend of

The variation of the normal can be performed analogously as the material differentiation given in Eq. (

For the variation of the Christoffel symbols we need the variations of reciprocal base vectors (

\displaystyle\textbf{g}^{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\textbf{g}_{2}\times\textbf{g}_{3}\right)/\sqrt{g}\Rightarrow\delta\textbf{g}^{1}=-\left[\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{2}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{1}\right)\textbf{g}^{2}+\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{1}\right)\textbf{g}^{3}+\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{1}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{1}\right)\textbf{g}^{1}\right]\Delta t,

\displaystyle\textbf{g}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\textbf{g}_{3}\times\textbf{g}_{1}\right)/\sqrt{g}\Rightarrow\delta\textbf{g}^{2}=-\left[\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{2}\right)\textbf{g}^{3}+\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{1}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{2}\right)\textbf{g}^{1}+\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{2}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{2}\right)\textbf{g}^{2}\right]\Delta t,

\delta{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\alpha\beta}=\delta\left(\textbf{g}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{\lambda}\right)=\delta\textbf{g}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\textbf{g}^{\lambda}+\textbf{g}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdotp\delta\textbf{g}^{\lambda}=\left[\textbf{g}^{\lambda}\cdotp\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\textbf{g}^{\lambda}\cdot\delta\textbf{v}_{\nu}\right)\right]\Delta t.

(81)

By inserting Eqs. (

\displaystyle\delta\dot{\kappa}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\left(\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\delta\textbf{v}_{\mu}\right)

(82)

\displaystyle-\left\{\left(\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\delta\textbf{v}_{\mu}\right)\textbf{g}^{\mu}\cdotp\left(\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\textbf{v}_{\nu}\right)+\left[\textbf{g}^{\nu}\cdotp\delta\textbf{v}_{\alpha,\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\textbf{g}^{\nu}\cdotp\delta\textbf{v}_{\mu}\right)\right]\left(\textbf{g}_{3}\cdotp\textbf{v}_{\nu}\right)\right\}\Delta t.

4.4.1
Numerical solution procedure

The incremental form of Eq. (
95
) emphasizes that the equilibrium path must be considered as a set of discrete equilibrium points
(\textbf{q}^{i},\textbf{Q}^{i})
which correspond to a set of fictitious moments in time. In order to find these points, some iterative procedure must be employed. Here, the arc-length method is primarily used for all examples. It is worth noting that the Newton-Raphson method is applicable for the examples that do not exhibit snap-through behavior. We compared the performance of the linearized arc-length method, the cylindrical arc-length method, and the modified Riks method,
[
52
,
53
]
. For the numerical tests which follow in the next section, all of these variants return similar results. The exception is the last example where only the modified Riks method, as implemented in Abaqus, found the complete structural response,
[
54
]
. Hence, this procedure is briefly presented.

The main idea of the arc-length method is to introduce a load proportionality factor
\lambda^{i}
which scales the value of total external load,
\textbf{Q}^{f}
, and allows it to increase or decrease during the deformation process:

\textbf{Q}^{i}={\lambda}^{i}\textbf{Q}^{f}.

(98)

This approach results in an indeterminate system of
3NM
equations with
3NM+1
unknowns where the additional unknown is the load proportionality factor itself,
[
52
]
. We start from some known, converged, configuration defined in a point
(\textbf{q}^{i},{\lambda}^{i})
and look for the next equilibrium point
(\textbf{q}^{i+1},{\lambda}^{i+1})
. The first step is to find a predictor solution. Therefore, the predictor tangential displacement vector,
{}^{0}{\textbf{q}}_{T}
, is calculated from the known tangent stiffness and the total external load:

{\textbf{K}}^{i}_{T}\>{}^{0}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}_{T}=\textbf{Q}^{f}.

(99)

The predictor load increment
\Delta\>^{0}{\lambda}^{i+1}
follows from the appropriate constraint equation,
[
52
,
54
]
:

\Delta\>^{0}{\lambda}^{i+1}=\pm\frac{\Delta\>{l}^{i+1}}{\sqrt{{}^{0}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}_{T}\>\cdotp\>^{0}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}_{T}+1}}.

(100)

Here,
\Delta{l}^{i+1}
is the arc-length value which is estimated for the first increment, and calculated for all the other increments as a function of desired number of iterations. For convenience, the displacement vectors are scaled by the maximum displacement component of the corresponding linear solution and designated with tilde,
[
54
]
. The sign of the predictor solution is calculated from the condition that the projection of the predictor tangential displacement onto the previously converged displacement increment must be positive, ensuring continuation along the equilibrium path. This condition can be written as:

\Delta\>^{0}{\lambda}^{i+1}\left({}^{0}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}_{T}\Delta\>{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i}+\Delta\>{\lambda}^{i}\right)>0.

(101)

In general, the predictor solution does not satisfy the equilibrium and the arc-length iterations act as correctors. For the first iteration, the increments of displacement and load are initialized from the predictor solutions:

\Delta\>^{1}{\lambda}^{i+1}=\Delta\>^{0}{\lambda}^{i+1},\;\Delta\>^{1}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}=\Delta\>^{0}{\lambda}^{i+1}\>{}^{0}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}_{T}.

(102)

Now, the current tangent stiffness,
{}^{j}{\textbf{K}}^{i+1}_{T}
, and internal forces,
{}^{j}{\textbf{F}}^{i+1}
, are calculated and convergence is checked for each iteration
j=1,2,3...
. The convergence criteria can be defined with respect to the current unbalanced forces,

{}^{j}{\bm{\Psi}}^{i+1}=\textbf{Q}^{i}-\>^{j}{\textbf{F}}^{i+1}
, or/and with respect to the previously calculated iterative displacement. If the defined criteria is not met, new tangential,
{}^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}_{T}
, and residual,
\delta\>^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}
, displacements are calculated from:

^{j}{\textbf{K}}^{i+1}_{T}\>{}^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}_{T}={\textbf{Q}}^{f},\;\;^{j}{\textbf{K}}^{i+1}_{T}\>\delta\>^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}=\>^{j}{\bm{\Psi}}^{i+1}.

(103)

The iterative load factor,
\delta\lambda
, follows from the condition that a new potential equilibrium point is orthogonal to the tangential displacement in the arc-length solution space:

\delta\lambda=-\frac{\delta\>^{j}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}\>\cdotp\>^{0}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}_{T}}{{}^{j}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}_{T}\>\cdotp\>^{0}{\tilde{\textbf{q}}}^{i+1}_{T}+1}.

(104)

Finally, the new solution is:

\displaystyle{}^{j+1}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}

\displaystyle={\textbf{q}}^{i}+\Delta\>^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}+\delta\>^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}+\delta\lambda\>^{j}{\textbf{q}}^{i+1}_{T},

(105)

\displaystyle{}^{j+1}{\lambda}^{i+1}

\displaystyle={\lambda}^{i}+\Delta\>^{j}{\lambda}^{i+1}+\delta\lambda,

and the procedure is repeated until the convergence criteria is fulfilled,
[
54
]
.

Numerical examples

To examine the influence of the different constitutive relations, we introduce four models

•

6
Conclusions

A rigorous geometric nonlinear IGA shell formulation is presented and validated by thorough numerical experiments. A key finding is that the initial configuration is not enough to determine the slenderness of the structure; all subsequent deformed configurations must be considered. As a result, some standard approximations of applied shell theories lead to erroneous results for strongly curved shells.

We strictly derive the nonlinear equation of equilibrium by considering the geometrically exact relations between the equidistant and reference strains of a KL shell. The isogeometric approach is applied for the spatial discretization and the arc-length method is employed as a solution procedure. The geometric stiffness matrix is derived by the variation of strains with respect to the metric. The symmetry of this matrix is maintained which proves that the selected force and strain quantities are energetically conjugated.

The main contribution of the paper is the full analytical integration of the virtual power along the thickness direction which is performed using the reciprocal shift tensor. The resulting
D^{a}
model is complex and thus, computationally expensive. However, this constitutive model is exact, and allows us to derive different reduced constitutive models
D^{0}
,
D^{1}
, and
D^{2}
. Based on the present numerical experiments, it follows that the approximative constitutive model
D^{2}
is well-suited for the nonlinear modeling of strongly curved shells. It is as efficient as the simple
D^{0}
model and returns results which are virtually indistinguishable from the exact ones. On the other hand, the displacement field of thin, small-curvature shells, is practically indifferent to the more rigorous constitutive relations, no matter how large the displacements and rotations are and the simple decoupled equations of the
D^{0}
model can return reasonably accurate results. Regardless of the constitutive model used, nonlinear distribution of strain through the thickness should be considered during the post-processing phase in order to obtain accurate strain field of strongly curved shells.

A detailed inspection of the observed differences between IGA and Abaqus was beyond the scope of the present research and it should be a subject of further research. The development of an adaptive refinement scheme that applies higher regularity on smooth parts of the deformed structure, and lower regularity at the kinks and similar irregularities would be an interesting improvement. Additionally, the different constitutive relations could be applied, depending on the curviness of the observed area, like the blended shell approach suggested in
[
58
]
. More accurate shell models should also include the change of thickness as well, since this directly influences the curviness.
