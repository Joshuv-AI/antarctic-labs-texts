We give a short generating function proof of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem: for integers h h and k ≠ 0 k\neq 0 , the numbers M n ​ ( h , k ) M_{n}(h,k) defined by k ​ x ​ e h ​ x − 1 e k ​ x − 1 = ∑ n = 0 ∞ M n ​ ( h , k ) ​ x n n ! kx\frac{e^{hx}-1}{e^{kx}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{n}(h,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!} are integers. 1.
Introduction

The Bernoulli polynomials
B_{n}(u)
may be defined by

(1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}(u)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{xe^{ux}}{e^{x}-1},

and the
n
th Bernoulli number
B_{n}
is
B_{n}(0)
.

For
k\neq 0
, let

(2)

M_{n}(h,k)=k^{n}\bigl(B_{n}(h/k)-B_{n}\bigr),

so

(3)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{n}(h,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=kx\frac{e^{hx}-1}{e^{kx}-1}

Almkvist and Meurman
[
1
]
showed that if
h
and
k
are integers then
M_{n}(h,k)
is an integer. Other proofs were given by Sury
[
11
]
,
Bartz and Rutkowski
[
2
]
, Clarke and Slavutskii
[
3
]
, and the author
[
7
]
. The Almkvist–Meurman theorem also follows immediately from a much earlier result of Vandiver
[
12
]
, as explained in
[
7
]
. Additional historical remarks can also be found in
[
7
]
. The case
h=1
of the theorem (from which the general case is easily derived, as explained below) was recently rediscovered by Farhi
[
6
]
, who gave a new proof.

We give here another new proof, using generating functions, of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem. The proof in
[
7
]
also uses generating functions, but in a different way.

A
Hurwitz series
is a (formal) power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}x^{n}/n!
for which each
f_{n}
is an integer; i.e., it is the exponential generating function for a sequence of integers. It is well known that Hurwitz series are closed under addition and multiplication.
Moreover, if
f
and
g=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}x^{n}/n!
are Hurwitz series then the composition
f(g(x))
is a Hurwitz series, and if
g_{1}=1
then
g(x)
has a compositional inverse that is a Hurwitz series.

We will define a
generalized Hurwitz series
to be a power series whose coefficients, as an exponential generating function, are polynomials in some set of variables (in our application
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
,

\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
) with integer coefficients.

Since all of the generating functions we will be concerned with are exponential, by the “coefficients” of a generating function we mean its coefficients as an exponential generating function.

2.
Proof of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem

We first note that the general case of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem can be derived from the case in which
h=1
and
k
is positive, since for any integers
h
and
k
(with
k\neq 0
),
kx(e^{hx}-1)/(e^{kx}-1)
can be expressed as a Hurwitz series times
|k|x(e^{x}-1)/(e^{|k|x}-1)
.
This follows easily from

e^{hx}-1=-e^{hx}(e^{-hx}-1)
,
e^{kx}-1=-e^{kx}(e^{-kx}-1)
, and for
h
a positive integer,

e^{hx}-1=(1+e^{x}+\cdots+e^{(h-1)x})(e^{x}-1)
.
When
h=1
and
k
is a positive integer, the generating function (
3
) may be written

(4)

\frac{kx}{1+e^{x}+\cdots+e^{(k-1)x}}.

To motivate our proof, we first prove the case
h=1,k=2
of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem, for which the generating function (
4
) is
2x/(e^{x}+1)
,
the generating function for the Genocchi numbers, which is sequence
A001469
in the OEIS (On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
[
9
]
).

We start by looking at what seems to be a completely unrelated sequence of integers, sequence
A007889
, which has several combinatorial interpretations. These numbers count alternating trees, local binary search trees, and regions of the Linial arrangement.
Postnikov
[
10
]
showed that the exponential generating function
A=A(x)
for these numbers (where we take the constant term to be 0), satisfies

(5)

1+A=e^{(x/2)(2+A)}.

In this form it is not clear that
A
is a Hurwitz series, though it is clear that
2
is the only prime that can occur in the denominators of the coefficients of
A
. However we can rearrange (
5
) in a way that does show that
A
is a Hurwitz series.
Squaring both sides of (
5
) gives

(6)

(1+A)^{2}=1+A(2+A)=e^{x(2+A)}.

Subtracting 1 from each side and dividing by
2+A
gives

(7)

A=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(2+A)^{n-1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

from which it is clear that
A
is a Hurwitz series.

Solving (
5
) for
x
shows that the compositional inverse of
A(x)
is

(8)

\frac{2\log(1+x)}{2+x},

which is therefore a Hurwitz series. Although we will not need this fact, the coefficient of
x^{n}/n!
in (
8
) is
(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i!\,(n-i-1)!
.
1
1

1

This can be proved easily from the beta integral:
since

\int_{0}^{1}z^{i}(1-z)^{j}\,dz=\frac{i!\,j!}{(i+j+1)!},

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\frac{i!\,j!}{(i+j+1)!}u^{i}v^{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}(uz)^{i}(v(1-z))^{j}\,dz

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dz}{(1-uz)(1-v(1-z))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1-u)+\log(1-v)}{uv-u-v}.

Setting
u=v=-x
and multiplying by
x
gives the formula for the coefficients of (
8
).

These numbers are
A003149
in the OEIS.

Replacing
x
with
e^{x}-1
in (
8
) gives another Hurwitz series,

\frac{2x}{e^{x}+1},

the exponential generating for the Genocchi numbers, thus proving the case
k=2
of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem.

A related proof of the integrality of the Genocchi numbers was given by Farhi
[
5
]
, who used the fact that
(
8
) is a Hurwitz series, but did not use (
7
) or the connection with the alternating tree numbers.

To extend this proof to the case
h=1
,
k
a positive integer of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem, which, as we have seen, implies the general theorem, we replace the left side of (
6
) with

(1+A)^{k}=1+A\left[\binom{k}{1}+\binom{k}{2}A+\cdots+\binom{k}{k}A^{k-1}\right]=1+Ap_{k}(A),

where
p_{k}(u)
is the polynomial

\binom{k}{1}+\binom{k}{2}u+\cdots+\binom{k}{k}u^{k-1}=\bigl((1+u)^{k}-1\bigr)/u.

We then replace the right side of (
6
) with
e^{xp_{k}(A)}
, so we consider the functional equation

(9)

(1+A)^{k}=e^{xp_{k}(A)}.

Subtracting
1
from each side of (
9
) and dividing by
p_{k}(A)
gives
the functional equation

(10)

A=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(A)^{n-1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

which shows that
A
is a Hurwitz series.

Solving (
9
) for
x
shows that the compositional inverse of
A
is

(11)

\frac{k\log(1+x)}{p_{k}(x)}=\frac{kx\log(1+x)}{(1+x)^{k}-1}

so (
11
) is a Hurwitz series. Replacing
x
with
e^{x}-1
in (
11
) gives

kx\frac{e^{x}-1}{e^{kx}-1},

which must therefore be a Hurwitz series. This completes our proof of the Almkvist–Meurman theorem.

3.
Additional comments

I do not know of a formula for the coefficients of (
11
) analogous to the formula for the coefficients of (
8
), nor do I know of a combinatorial interpretation to (
9
) or (
12
) that generalizes the combinatorial interpretations for the case
k=2
. If we set
B=1+A
then (
9
) may be written in the somewhat more suggestive form

(12)

B=e^{x(1+B+\cdots+B^{k-1})/k}.

There is an interesting generalization of the fact that
2\log(1+x)/(2+x)
is a Hurwitz series whose compositional inverse counts certain trees, although in this generalization there is no analogue of the replacement of
x
with
e^{x}-1
in (
8
) that gave us the the Genocchi numbers.

Consider the functional equation for
F
,

(13)

\frac{(1+\alpha_{1}F)(1+\beta_{2}F)}{(1+\alpha_{2}F)(1+\beta_{1}F)}=e^{((\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}\alpha_{2})F+\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2})x}.

If we set
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{2}=1
and
\alpha_{2}=\beta_{1}=0
then (
13
) reduces to (
6
), with
A
replacing
F
.
Solving (
13
) for
x
shows that

F
is the compositional inverse of

(14)

\frac{1}{(\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}\alpha_{2})x+\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}}\log\left(\frac{(1+\alpha_{1}x)(1+\beta_{2}x)}{(1+\alpha_{2}x)(1+\beta_{1}x)}\right).

It is not obvious from (
13
) or (
14
) that
F
is a generalized Hurwitz series
but this follows from a result of
Drake
[
4
, Proposition 1.8.1 and Theorem 1.8.4]
who showed that the coefficient of
x^{n}/n!
in (
14
) is a polynomial in
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
homogeneous of degree
n-1
, and the coefficient of

\alpha_{1}^{a_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{a_{2}}\beta_{1}^{d_{1}}\beta_{2}^{d_{2}}x^{n}/n!
in (
14
), where
n=a_{1}+a_{2}+d_{1}+d_{2}+1
, is

(-1)^{n-1}(a_{1}+a_{2})!\,(d_{1}+d_{2})!\binom{a_{1}+d_{1}}{a_{1}}\binom{a_{2}+d_{2}}{a_{2}}
. This generalizes the formula given earlier for the coefficients of (
8
). That
F
is a generalized Hurwitz series also follows from the combinatorial interpretation for its coefficients (see Drake
[
4
]
and Gessel, Griffin, and Tewari
[
8
]
, and the references cited there).

We can also rewrite (
13
) in a form analogous to (
7
).
We first subtract 1, from (
13
), getting

\quad\frac{F}{(1+\beta_{1}F)(1+\alpha_{2}F)}\cdot\bigl((\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1})F+\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}-\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\bigr)\\
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl((\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1})F+\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}-\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\bigr)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},\quad

from which we obtain

(15)

F=(1+\beta_{1}F)(1+\alpha_{2}F)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl((\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1})F+\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}-\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\bigr)^{n-1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

which makes it clear that
F
is a generalized Hurwitz series.

Unfortunately it does not seem possible to get a generalization of the Genocchi numbers from (
14
).
