Distribution Testing with a Confused Collector Some results in this paper appeared together with a number of others in an earlier preprint [ FH23 ] . We have expanded upon our earlier results and included them here, separate from the remainder of [ FH23 ] , after receiving feedback on the preprint.
Renato Ferreira Pinto Jr, Nathaniel Harms

Abstract
We are interested in testing properties of distributions with systematically mislabeled samples. Our goal is to make decisions about unknown probability distributions, using a sample that has been collected by a confused collector , such as a machine-learning classifier that has not learned to distinguish all elements of the domain. The confused collector holds an unknown clustering of the domain and an input distribution μ \mu , and provides two oracles: a sample oracle which produces a sample from μ \mu that has been labeled according to the clustering; and a label-query oracle which returns the label of a query point x x according to the clustering. Our first set of results shows that identity, uniformity, and equivalence of distributions can be tested efficiently, under the earth-mover distance, with remarkably weak conditions on the confused collector, even when the unknown clustering is adversarial . This requires defining a variant of the distribution testing task (inspired by the recent testable learning framework of Rubinfeld & Vasilyan), where the algorithm should test a joint property of the distribution and its clustering. As an example, we get efficient testers when the distribution tester is allowed to reject if it detects that the confused collector clustering is “far” from being a decision tree. The second set of results shows that we can sometimes do significantly better when the clustering is random instead of adversarial. For certain one-dimensional random clusterings, we show that uniformity can be tested under the TV distance using O ~ ​ ( n ρ 3 / 2 ​ ϵ 2 ) \widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho^{3/2}\epsilon^{2}}\right) samples and zero queries, where ρ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \rho\in(0,1] controls the “resolution” of the clustering. We improve this to O ⁡ ( n ρ ​ ϵ 2 ) O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho\epsilon^{2}}\right) when queries are allowed.

The idea is that the confused collector holds a

Not much can be done if we allow the confused collector to hold

Results and Organization.

This paper is clustered into two parts. Part I shows how to sidestep the impossibility we just
described, even when the clustering is
adversarial
, by defining a natural relaxation of the
distribution testing task, where the algorithm tests a joint property of the distribution and its
clustering. We present general baseline upper bounds for the
identity
and
equivalence
testing
tasks, under adversarial clusterings. One example application, motivated by the
example where the confused collector is a machine learning classifier, is that the relaxed testing
tasks can be done efficiently when the tester expects the clustering to be realized by a

decision tree
and can test this assumption.

These results provide a foundation for Part II, where we present more involved technical results
showing how to significantly improve upon the above baseline results in certain special cases where
we assume
randomized
clusterings. Motivated by the “environmental randomness” example and
applications to property testing, we show that certain random clusters of the domain
[n]
allow the
standard (non-relaxed)
uniformity testing
task to be accomplished efficiently with

zero
label queries, and even more efficiently with queries.

1.1
Part I: Adversarial Clustering

We begin our exploration by establishing that natural testing tasks can be performed efficiently
even with an

confused collector. It is not obvious

We give general upper bounds for the basic

\epsilon

1.1.1
The Testing Task

Let us explain how to arrive at a suitable definition of distribution testing when faced with an
adversarially-chosen clustering. Consider the simple problem of distinguishing distributions
\mu

supported on a single element, from the distributions
\nu
which have probability mass
1/2
on
each of two elements. This is trivial in the standard distribution testing model, but impossible if
the confused collector holds any clustering with a cell of size at least 2, because the two supports
of
\mu
may lie in the same cell. One may consider three ways to fix this problem:

Change the distance metric.
We should not use TV distance to define our testing task, because
the adversary can choose the clustering to “hide” arbitrarily large TV distances. Assuming the
domain
\mathcal{X}
is equipped with an ambient metric denoted by
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we instead
use the
earth-mover
(or
Wasserstein
) distance on distributions, defined as

\mathsf{EMD}_{\mathsf{dist}}(\mu,\nu)\vcentcolon=\inf_{\pi}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x,y)\cdot\mathsf{dist}(x,y)\,,

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
\pi
of
\mu
and
\nu
. This means that transporting
mass within a cell of the clustering cannot transform a distribution
\mu
into another one,
\nu
,
that is
far
in
\mathsf{EMD}_{\mathsf{dist}}
, as long as the cell is small with respect to the ambient
metric
\mathsf{dist}
.

Reject bad clusterings.
By itself, using
\mathsf{EMD}
does not solve our problems, since
there is no guarantee that the cells are small. One solution is to demand that the confused
collector use a low-diameter clustering, but this is too strong: we may
not trust that the collector has acquiesced to our demands; low-diameter clusterings
of the entire (large) domain may be costly for the collector to compute; and low-diameter
clusterings do not depend on the input probability distribution – the collector may itself have
learned the clustering from the input distribution, and we may be satisfied with a collector who
makes poor distinctions on low-probability elements. Instead, we allow the tester to reject
clusterings not belonging to a defined set of “good” clusterings, while requiring it to succeed on
any clustering that passes the test.

Restrict the clusterings.
Some types of clusterings make the task of rejecting bad
clusterings infeasible. Using label queries, the tester can learn the entire
clustering by querying every point in the domain, but we want sublinear query complexity. If, for
example, the clusters are not even required to be
connected
, then it might become infeasible
to efficiently detect bad clusterings. But, by making reasonable assumptions on the clusterings
(including, say, that the cells of the clustering are connected or convex), it becomes feasible to
check whether the given clustering is suitable.

This leads to our definition of distribution testing. For convenience, we assume that the
metric
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
is normalized, meaning it has diameter 1.

(Testing with an Adversarial Confused Collector)

target
\nu

using an EMD tester similar to that of

[

by making use of the algorithm’s ability to output

1.2
Part II: Random Clustering

If the confused collector has a
random
clustering instead of an adversarial clustering, one
hopes to improve upon the results in Part I, and we show that this is true for certain random
clusterings. We now wish for the tester to be correct with high probability, over both
the samples
and
the clustering. Specifically, for a property
\mathcal{P}
, input
\mu
, parameter

\delta
, and distribution
\mathcal{D}
over the class
\mathcal{U}
of clusterings, we require that the testing
algorithm
A
satisfies:

•

If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, then
\underset{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})\sim\mathcal{D};\;A}{\mathbb{P}}\left[A\text{ accepts}\right]\geq 1-\delta
; and

•

If
\mu
is
\epsilon
-far from
\mathcal{P}
, then
\underset{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})\sim\mathcal{D};\;A}{\mathbb{P}}\left[A\text{ rejects}\right]\geq 1-\delta
.

It may sometimes be natural to allow the algorithm to
CLUSTER-REJECT
, but this will not be
necessary at present. When the algorithm is not allowed to
CLUSTER-REJECT
, standard probability
boosting techniques
do not work
: the algorithm has no control over the clustering, which is
fixed, and therefore the error probability depends on the distribution over clusterings.

We will focus on random clusterings defined as follows.
The domain is
[n]
, which we think of as vertices of a path (or cycle)
G=([n],E)
.
For parameter
\rho\in(0,1]
, which we call the
resolution
, the distribution
\mathcal{U}_{\rho}

over clusterings is defined by taking a random subgraph
\bm{H}
of
G
where each edge is deleted
independently with probability
\rho
, and the vertices are clustered by their connected components
in
\bm{H}
. In other words, each consecutive pair of the domain is “separated” into different
cells with probability
\rho
, so that the resolution
\rho
controls the granularity of the
clustering.

Our motivations for this choice of random clustering are, first, that it captures a type of
“environmental randomness” in the collection of samples, like the example where fossils from
different years can be distinguished only if a random geological event occurred between those years.
We think of “environmental randomness” as being uncorrelated with the input probability
distribution
\mu
, whereas other natural random clusterings (e. g. a machine-learning classifier) may
depend on
\mu
. The one-dimensional clusterings we study are a simple and natural starting point for
understanding how environmental randomness affects distribution testing tasks.

The second motivation is that these random clusterings occur in the study of certain
distribution-free property testing problems. These problems are outside the scope of the current
paper and will be explained formally in future work
5
5

5

A partial treatment of this occurrence
is given in a preprint
[
FH23
]
which contains some results from Part II of this paper, and
will be elaborated upon in future work.
, but we give a simplified description in

Section

1.2.1
Results

We give results for testing
uniformity
of distributions under these random clusters, where
the algorithm should accept the uniform distribution over
[n]
and reject any distribution over

[n]
that is
\epsilon
-far from uniform in TV distance. From
Theorem

3.18
in Part I, we
obtain a tester using
\widetilde{O}(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
samples and
\widetilde{O}(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
queries under the
\mathsf{EMD}
distance, but we now hope to use the TV distance
(which corresponds roughly to testing under
\mathsf{EMD}
with parameter
\approx\epsilon/n
). The optimal
sample complexity for testing uniformity in the standard distribution testing model is
\Theta(\sqrt{n}/\epsilon^{2})

[
Pan08
,
VV17
]
.

Naïve benchmark algorithm.

A reasonable algorithm that one might first propose is as follows. Suppose that the
tester uses queries to exactly learn the clustering
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})
of the domain
[n]
. It may
then define
\nu^{*}
as the distribution over the representatives of the clusters
r_{i}\vcentcolon=\mathsf{rep}(\Gamma_{i})
obtained by sampling
x
according to the uniform distribution over
[n]
and then
taking the representative of its cluster,
\mathsf{rep}(\Gamma_{\gamma(x)})
. Define
\mu^{*}
as the
distribution received from the confused collector, so that
\mu^{*}
is the distribution over
representatives
\mathsf{rep}(\Gamma_{\gamma(x)})
when
x\sim\mu
is sampled from the unknown input
distribution. Then the algorithm runs an identity test on
\mu^{*}
and
\nu^{*}
.

A back-of-the-envelope calculation of the complexity of this algorithm is as follows. The expected
number of clusters is
\Theta(\rho n)
, so we require at most
O(\rho n\log n)
queries to learn
them with binary search, and the identity test will be performed on a domain of size
O(\rho n)
.
When
\mu
goes from the original distribution to the clustered distribution
\mu^{*}
, it begins with
TV distance
\epsilon
from uniform, and might shrink to TV distance
\rho\epsilon
from the target

\nu^{*}
, forcing us to set the distance parameter for the identity test no larger than
\rho\epsilon
. (An example of this shrinkage is obtained by adding
\epsilon/n
mass to each odd element
of the uniform distribution, and subtracting
\epsilon/n
from each even element, so that the
\pm\epsilon/n
perturbations in each cluster balance out, except for an expected surplus of
\approx\pm\epsilon/2n
for each of the
\rho n
clusters.) Plugging in the identity testing bound, we get
sample complexity
O\left(\frac{\sqrt{\rho n}}{\rho^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho^{3/2}\epsilon^{2}}\right)
and query complexity
O(\rho n\log n)
.

Our first result, which is the main technical challenge of this paper, shows that we can in fact get
the same bound on the sample complexity while using
zero
queries. This is important because,
for some of the “environmental randomness” and property testing motivations we are interested in,
it may not be possible to pose queries to the confused collector.

Theorem 1.4

(Main theorem; see
Theorem

4.35
for specific requirements on
\rho,\epsilon
.)
.

Let
G
be a path or cycle on
n
vertices and let
\rho,\epsilon\in(0,1]
satisfy
\rho\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-1/5}\epsilon^{-4/5})
. Then
\epsilon
-testing uniformity under TV
distance with the confused collector can be done using
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho^{3/2}\epsilon^{2}}\right)
samples and zero queries.

Since the algorithm has no control over the clustering, improvements to the error probability can be
achieved by improving the resolution parameter. The proof is summarized in

Section

1.2.2
below. It does not use a reduction to identity testing, and is
instead a direct analysis of a generalization of the standard uniformity tester, which requires a
new technical lemma on the concentration of random quadratic forms. The cycle has a cleaner
analysis, but the path and cycle cases do not appear to directly reduce to one another, which is why
both are included in the theorem.

With the sample complexity of the natural benchmark algorithm matched by a zero-query algorithm, one
may wonder if queries can still be helpful, but it is not clear how to improve the algorithm or
analysis of the benchmark: using the instance-optimal tester of
[
VV17
]
does not immediately
improve the analysis. However, we show it is indeed possible to improve the sample complexity when
queries are allowed. The algorithm is simple but, to us, much less natural than the benchmark. It
is the same as the benchmark algorithm except that it tests identity
only on the singleton
clusters
(i. e. clusters of size 1), crucially using the instance-optimal algorithm of
[
VV17
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a path or cycle on
n
vertices, and let
\rho,\epsilon
satisfy
\rho\geq\Omega((\epsilon n)^{-1/4})
. Then testing uniformity under TV distance with the
confused collector requires
O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho\epsilon^{2}}\right)
samples and

O(\rho n\log n)
queries.

The natural parameter regime is where
\rho=o(1)
; for the allowed constants
\delta
in the
theorem, setting
\rho=n^{-\delta}
produces a sublinear
O(n^{1-\delta}\log n)
query
complexity.

1.2.2
Proof Overview.

We now give an overview of the proof of our main technical
Theorem

1.4
,
which shows that there is a zero-query tester that matches the sample complexity of the
benchmark algorithm. Let us review the standard uniformity tester
[
GR11
,
DGPP19
]
(see also

[
Can22
]
). Let
\mu
be the input distribution over
[n]
. For a sample
S
of size
m
, let

X_{i}
be the multiplicity of element
i
in
S
. The tester counts the number of “collisions” in
the sample: it computes
Y\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(X_{i}-1)
, and rejects if this
is too large. This works because
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]=\mu^{\top}\mu=\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, which is large when
\mu
is
far from uniform. Now we describe the zero-query tester for the confused collector. For input
distribution
\mu
on domain
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(which are the vertices of the path or cycle), we use the
standard Poissonization technique, so that element
j
occurs in the sample with multiplicity
\bm{T}_{j}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(m\mu_{j})
independently of the other elements. Recall that
\bm{H}
is the random
subgraph of the path or cycle that determines the clusters of the domain, and redefine
\bm{X}_{i}
as
the number of sample points contained in the
i^{th}
connected component of
\bm{H}
, which the
tester cannot distinguish: the
\bm{X}_{i}
variables remain Poisson, but they are not independent.
The tester computes a “collision count”, as in the standard algorithm:

\bm{Y}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i}\bm{X}_{i}(\bm{X}_{i}-1)=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\bm{T}-\|\bm{T}\|_{1}\right)\,,

where
\bm{\Phi}
is the random Boolean matrix with
\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}=1
iff
i,j
belong to the same
connected component of
\bm{H}
. The expected value is
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]=\mu^{\top}\phi\mu
where

\phi=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}\right]
, and we show that this is again large when
\mu
is far from uniform, using
spectral analysis of the matrix
\phi
which is either Toeplitz (for paths) or circulant (for
cycles).

To complete the analysis, we require concentration of measure for the random quadratic form
\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\bm{T}-\|\bm{T}\|_{1}
. This is similar to Hanson-Wright inequalities, except that
Hanson-Wright inequalities apply to fixed matrices
\Phi
whereas our matrix
\bm{\Phi}
is random.
We prove the following concentration inequality in terms of a concentration measure

\chi(\mu)
, which roughly satisfies
\chi(\mu)\lesssim\|\mu\|_{\infty}/\rho
(but is less stringent in general) and which the algorithm can separately
test:

Lemma 1.6

(Informal; see
Lemma

4.27
)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a constant.
Let
\mu
be a
probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and
suppose
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
and
m\leq\poly(n)
. Then for all
\tau>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]\right\rvert\geq\tau\right]\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho\tau^{2}}\cdot\max\left\{\chi(\mu),1/m\right\}^{2}\cdot O(\log^{2}n)\,.

1.3
Discussion and Open Problems

Related work.

[
FKKT21
]
studied statistical learning (e. g. Gaussian mean
estimation) in a model with similar motivation to ours. The difference is that
[
FKKT21
]
chooses
a random clustering for each sample point independently and provides an explicit representation of
the cell containing each point, whereas in our model the sample points are all labeled by the

same
clustering, the algorithm is not given the cell explicitly, and the algorithm can make
label queries. If we suppose that the input distribution
\mu
is held by
multiple
confused
collectors, and each sample point is labeled by a
random
collector, then we nearly recover
the model of
[
FKKT21
]
, except that we allow label queries and do not receive explicit
representations of the cells. In this interpretation,
[
FKKT21
]
require that the collectors
jointly hold an “information-preserving” clustering (which approximately preserves TV distance
between any pair of distributions), which is unnecessary in our model.

Our model also shares some conceptual similarities with the “huge object” model

[
GR22
]
, where the algorithm must test properties of a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
by taking
samples and, for each sampled element
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, querying a subset of its bits. In both models,
the algorithm has incomplete information about the sample, and the goal is to test with respect to
the earth-mover distance. The difference is that, in the huge object model, the algorithm has incomplete
information about the sample because the objects are too large to observe entirely,
but perfect knowledge could be obtained if enough queries are used. In the confused collector
model, the incomplete information is due to imprecise classification of the sample and may not be
possible to acquire.

We remark also that several distribution testing algorithms in the literature use random clustering
as a step in the tester, including algorithms for testing in the huge objects model
[
CFG+22
]

(which assign each
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to a cluster determined by a random subset of bits); and algorithms
for testing with communication constraints, which compress the domain to save space (see e. g.
[
ACH+20
]
and the survey
[
Can20
]
). For example,
[
ACH+20
]
gives results for testing
identity on unstructured domains, using random clusterings produced with few bits of randomness
shared between parties. In these settings, the algorithm has control over the clustering, whereas we
are interested in the case where the clustering is not controlled.

Questions.
We would like to know more about how to manage
multiple
confused
collectors. One may consider a setting where the input
\mu
is held by multiple collectors (as
above), or where the collectors hold separate inputs
\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{t}
(as studied in

[
LRR13
,
LRR14
,
AS20
]
for the standard model). Each sample point could be drawn from a random
collector, or a chosen collector; or one might pay cost
k
to get a single sample point labeled by

k
of the collectors. How efficiently can one detect and select collectors with complementary
expertise, and combine their expertise?

In Part I, we reduce identity or equivalence testing in the clustered domain, to the identity or
equivalence testing in the standard model, but this is not as easy for properties like monotonicity
(e. g.
[
BKR04
,
AGP+19
]
) or
k
-histograms (e. g.
[
ILR12
,
Can16
,
CDGR18
,
CDKL22
]
), which are not
preserved by clustering; it would be interesting to study these.

An open problem of
[
DNNR11
]
is to find tight bounds on the sample complexity for estimating

\mathsf{EMD}
between distributions on
[0,1]^{d}
. This appears to be open still, and it would be helpful in
the confused collector model to have optimal bounds on tolerant testing under
\mathsf{EMD}
.

The random clustering model in Part II is tailored to the path and cycle, and
may not be sensible for more general graphs. It would be interesting to know which natural random
clusterings of graphs allow for efficient zero-query algorithms, as in

Theorem

1.4
. We also wonder what properties of distributions,
beyond uniformity, admit testers (with and without queries) under TV distance; e. g. is identity
testing possible for some non-trivial class of target distributions
\nu
?

Finally, it would be interesting to investigate instance-optimal identity testing
[
VV17
]

(see also
[
DK16
,
BCG19
]
) in the confused collector model, since cell discovery etc. can
be tailored to the known distribution.

Distribution-free property testing.

One of our original motivations for studying the confused collector model is its relation to some
distribution-free property testing problems. This will be the subject of future work and is outside
the scope of this paper, but the connection boils down to this:

In distribution-free testing of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, it becomes necessary to test a joint
property of the input distribution and the set
f^{-1}(1)
, which we can think of as a union of
connected components; if
f:\mathbb{R}\to\{0,1\}
, then
f^{-1}(1)
is a union of intervals. But, given
two samples
x,y\in f^{-1}(1)
, it is not possible to know whether they came from the same or
different intervals, unless another sample
z
occurs in
f^{-1}(0)
between
x,y
. Testing a joint
property of the input distribution with
f^{-1}(1)
therefore must be done with the tester seeing
only a “coarsening” of
f^{-1}(1)
. The confused collector model allows us to study this
phenomenon in a simpler setting from first principles, and there is in fact a formal connection
between the problem just described and the random clusterings in Part II of this paper, which will
be elaborated in future work.

.

3.1
Cell Discovery and Cell Rejection with Queries

Our testers will require subroutines for two tasks,

and

.
A cell-discovery algorithm will, given a membership oracle to an unknown cell
H
and its
representative
h\in H
, output an approximation to the cell
H
. A cell-rejection algorithm will,
given the same inputs and diameter thresholds
t_{1}<t_{2}
, reject the cell
H
if
\mathsf{diam}(H)>t_{2}

and accept if
\mathsf{diam}(H)<t_{1}
.

Definition 3.1

(Cell Discovery)
.

Fix domain
\mathcal{X}
, let
\mathcal{U}
be a class of clusterings and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.
Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\rho\in[0,1)
, and let
\nu
be a distribution over
\mathcal{X}
. An

-cell-discovery algorithm

for

is a randomized
algorithm defined as follows. The inputs to the
algorithm
D
are
(H,h)
where
H\in\Gamma

is its representative

, where access to
H

is only given implicitly via the label queries
\mathsf{LABEL}(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})
while

is given

over
the randomness of

1.

If
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{G}

, and moreover

. When

.

If the output is a container

.

3.2
General Upper Bound

We give a general result for identity and equivalence testing in an arbitrary (finite) metric space,
with the sample and query complexity expressed in terms of abstract bounds on the query complexity
of cell discovery and rejection, as well as the optimal size of a low-diameter partitioning of
space. We will apply the general result to specific cases in the next section.

The result requires a lemma about EMD. A similar but not equivalent statement appears in

[
IT03
,
DNNR11
]
, so for the sake of completeness we sketch a proof in

Section

.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{dist})
be a finite metric space, let
\mu,\nu
be probability distributions over

\mathcal{X}
, and let
\Gamma
be a clustering of
\mathcal{X}
. Then

\mathsf{EMD}_{\mathsf{dist}}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{X})\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor},{\nu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor})+\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})\right]\,.

Our general result will require a (meta-)algorithm for testing the diameter, described in the next
lemma. Let us first describe the parameters in the statement of this lemma.

•

\epsilon
is the EMD distance parameter that determines which distributions should be
rejected by the tester.

•

\Delta
is the diameter threshold we wish to use for diameter-guarded testing. We wish to
detect whether there are too many clusters with diameter larger than
\Delta
. We need
\Delta<\epsilon/2
because larger
\Delta
incurs larger inaccuracy in EMD through clustering.

•

\beta
is a parameter which determines the “gap” between the acceptance and rejection
conditions for the clustering itself. For
\beta\in(0,1)
, write
c_{\beta}\vcentcolon=\frac{\beta}{96\ln(24)}
, which is a constant that is convenient for the proof, and note that
c_{1/4}
is the
constant
c
from
Definition

1.3
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{dist})
be a finite unit-diameter metric space, let
\mathcal{U}
be a class of clusterings,
let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
be a subclass, and let
0<2\Delta<\epsilon<1/2
,
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
satisfy
\Delta\leq\beta\epsilon/2
.
Then there exists an algorithm
TestClustering
with
sample complexity
m(\epsilon,\delta)=O(\log(1/\delta)/\beta\epsilon)
and query complexity

q(\epsilon,\delta)=O\left(m(\epsilon,\delta)\cdot\mathsf{qreject}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{G},\Delta,\beta\epsilon/2,\rho)\right)
, where
\rho=\frac{1}{24m(\epsilon,\delta)}
, satisfying the following: for every clustering
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{U}

and probability distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{X}
,

1.

If
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{G}
and
\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})>\Delta\right]\leq c_{\beta}\cdot\epsilon
,
TestClustering
accepts with
probability at least
1-\delta
.

2.

If
\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})\right]>\beta\epsilon
,

TestClustering
rejects with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

We prove the claim for
\delta=1/12
; the general case follows by standard boosting.
Let
k\vcentcolon=\frac{2}{\beta}\ln(24)
. The algorithm makes
s=\lceil k/\epsilon\rceil

requests to
\mathsf{SAMP}(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep},\mu)
to obtain the representatives
r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s}
where
for each
i\in[s]
,
r_{i}\vcentcolon=\mathsf{rep}(\gamma(x_{i}))
is the representative of cell

\Gamma_{\gamma(x_{i})}
for a randomly drawn
x_{i}\sim\mu
.

For each
r_{i}
, algorithm runs the
(\Delta,\beta\epsilon/2,\rho)
-cell rejection algorithm on
input
(\Gamma_{i},r_{i})
. The algorithm outputs
reject
if the cell rejection algorithm
rejects or outputs
\bot
on any of the
r_{i}
.

The sample and query complexity claims are immediate, so now we show correctness. First, suppose

(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{G}
and
\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})>\Delta\right]\leq c_{\beta}\cdot\epsilon
. Then the probability that some
x_{i}
satisfies

\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(x_{i})})>\Delta
is, by the union bound, at most

\left\lceil\frac{k}{\epsilon}\right\rceil\cdot c_{\beta}\cdot\epsilon\leq 2k\cdot c_{\beta}=\frac{4\ln(24)}{\beta}\cdot\frac{\beta}{96\ln(24)}=\frac{1}{24}\,.

When this event does not occur, each
x_{i}
satisfies
\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(x_{i})})\leq\Delta
,
and since
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{G}

1-\rho

.

On the other hand, suppose
\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})\right]>\beta\epsilon
.
We claim that

\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})>\beta\epsilon/2\right]>\beta\epsilon/2
.
Suppose for a contradiction that this is not the case. Then

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})>\beta\epsilon/2\right]\cdot\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{X})+\underset{\bm{x}\sim\mu}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathsf{diam}(\Gamma_{\gamma(\bm{x})})\leq\beta\epsilon/2\right]\cdot\beta\epsilon/2

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta\epsilon}{2}\cdot 1+1\cdot\frac{\beta\epsilon}{2}=\beta\epsilon\,,

a contradiction, so the claim holds. Then, the probability that

for every

When this event does not occur, we have

for some

except with
probability at most

. Thus the algorithm rejects except with probability of
failure at most
1/12
.
∎

and there is an
\epsilon

, under

\mathsf{EMD}_{\mathsf{dist}}
, with error probability

and sample complexity

Proof.

Write
\Gamma^{*}\vcentcolon=\Gamma^{*}_{\epsilon/4}

\nu^{*}\vcentcolon={\nu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma^{*}}\rfloor}
and

We may now conclude the proof by applying the known upper bounds on identity and equivalence

3.3.3
Connected Cells

Our first result is a very general result which shows that sublinear sample and query complexity
suffice when the only promise on the clusterings is that they are connected, and the algorithm is
required to succeed on any connected clustering with high probability of low diameter.

Theorem 3.13
.

Fix domain
[n]^{d}
, let
\epsilon>0
, and let
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the normalized
\ell_{p}

metric on
[n]^{d}
for any
p\geq 1
. Let
\Delta\leq\frac{1}{8d^{1/p}}\epsilon
. Then

(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C},\Delta)
-diameter-guarded
\epsilon
-testing equivalence under
\mathsf{EMD}_{\mathsf{dist}}
requires
at most
m(\epsilon)=2^{O(d)}\cdot\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon^{-\max\{2,\frac{2d}{3}\}}\right)
samples and
q(\epsilon)=O\left(d\epsilon^{d-2}n^{d-1}\right)
queries, and

\epsilon
-testing identity requires at most
O(m(\epsilon))
samples and at most
O(m(\epsilon)+q(\epsilon))
queries.

Note that the domain size is
N=n^{d}
, so
n^{d-1}=N^{1-1/d}
,
which is sublinear in the domain size.
The result on identity testing follows from reducing identity testing to equivalence testing, using
label queries to simulate samples from
\mathsf{SAMP}(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep},\nu)
where
\nu
is the known
distribution. We use a reduction to equivalence instead of a direct result for identity testing,
because it may require as many as
O(N)
queries to perform cell discovery when the only promise on
the cells is that they are connected. To prove the theorem it is sufficient to prove bounds on the
cell rejection task; then the result follows from
Lemma

3.7
and

Lemma

3.12
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Fix domain
[n]^{d}
and let
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the normalized
\ell_{p}
metric for any
p\geq 1
. Then for any
0<\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}<1
that satisfy
\epsilon_{2}>2d^{1/p}\cdot\epsilon_{1}
,

\mathsf{qreject}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=O(d(\epsilon_{2}\cdot n)^{d-1})\,.

Proof.

Let
H\subseteq[n]^{d}
be any connected set, and let
h\in H
. Let
k=\lceil\epsilon_{2}\cdot n/2\rceil
and let
B\subseteq[n]^{d}
be the set of points
B=\{x\in[n]^{d}\;|\;\|x-h\|_{\infty}=k\}
. The cell rejection algorithm will simply query every element of
B
,
using
|B|\leq d(1+2k)^{d-1}=O(d(\epsilon_{2}\cdot n)^{d-1})
queries, and accept if and only
if
B\cap H=\emptyset
.

Suppose that
H
has diameter at most
\epsilon_{1}
, so that
\|x-y\|_{p}\leq\epsilon_{1}\cdot d^{1/p}\cdot n<\epsilon_{2}n/2
for all
x,y\in H
. Specifically, for all
x\in H
,

\|x-h\|_{\infty}\leq\|x-h\|_{p}<k\,,

so
H\cap B=\emptyset
, and the algorithm will correctly accept.

Now suppose that
H
has diameter at least
\epsilon_{2}
, so there exist two points
x,y\in H

with
\mathsf{dist}(x,y)\geq\epsilon_{2}
. By the triangle inequality, one of
\mathsf{dist}(x,h)\geq\epsilon_{2}/2

or
\mathsf{dist}(y,h)\geq\epsilon_{2}/2
must hold; assume
\mathsf{dist}(x,h)\geq\epsilon_{2}/2
. Then

\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{2}\leq\mathsf{dist}(x,h)=\frac{\|x-h\|_{p}}{d^{1/p}\cdot n}\leq\frac{d^{1/p}\cdot\|x-h\|_{\infty}}{d^{1/p}\cdot n}\,,

so
\|x-h\|_{\infty}\geq k
. Therefore, since
H
is connected, there exists
x^{\prime}
on any path from

to

such that
x^{\prime}\in H\cap B
, so the algorithm will correctly reject.
∎

To prove the theorem, fix domain

Fix domain

We may now prove the required bounds on cell discovery with

Lemma 3.17
.

Fix domain
[n]^{d}
. Then, for any

Proof sketch.

It suffices to consider cell discovery with

some flexibility is required at the boundary points. This technical point will be irrelevant for
absolutely continuous distributions with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
1)
\Gamma_{i}
is Lebesgue
measurable; 2) there is an open convex set
S
such that
S\subseteq\Gamma_{i}\subseteq\mathsf{cl}({S})
; and 3)
\mathbb{B}(r_{i},\delta)\subseteq\Gamma_{i}
.

A set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
closed axis-aligned box
if it can be written as
B=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\forall i\in[d],\,a_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq b_{i}\}
for some setting of
a_{i}
’s and
b_{i}
’s, and
we write
\mathsf{side}_{i}(B)\vcentcolon=b_{i}-a_{i}
for the side length of
B
along the
i
-th coordinate. We
say Lebesgue measurable set
B
is an
axis-aligned box
if there exists a closed axis-aligned
box
B^{\prime}
such that
\interior B^{\prime}\subseteq B\subseteq B^{\prime}
, and we write
\mathsf{side}_{i}(B)\vcentcolon=\mathsf{side}_{i}(B^{\prime})
. We then define
\mathcal{B}
as the class of clusterings of
[0,1]^{d}
whose cells are
axis-aligned boxes, and
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{CV}_{\delta}
.

For any bounded convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
B^{*}(K)
denote the its minimum closed
axis-aligned bounding box, namely

B^{*}(K)\vcentcolon=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\forall i\in[d],\,\inf_{y\in K}y_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq\sup_{y\in K}y_{i}\right\}\,.

For this application, we will assume for simplicity that the input distributions
\mu,\nu
over

[0,1]^{d}
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Then the Radon-Nikodym
theorem implies that any Lebesgue measurable set in
[0,1]^{d}
is
\mu
- and
\nu
-measurable; in
particular, this applies to the cells in clusterings from
\mathcal{CV}_{\delta}
.

The main ingredient behind cell discovery and rejection in this setting is a “bounding box”
procedure, which relies on the following result of
[
LSV20
]
on convex optimization using
membership and evaluation oracles.

Theorem 3.22

(
[
LSV20
]
)
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
0<r<R
, and
\epsilon>0
. There exists a randomized algorithm
which, given membership oracle access to a convex set
K
satisfying
\mathbb{B}(x_{0},r)\subseteq K\subseteq\mathbb{B}(x_{0},R)
and evaluation oracle access to a convex function
f
, uses
O\left(d^{2}\log^{2}\left(\frac{dR}{\epsilon r}\right)\right)
queries and outputs, with probability at
least
2/3
, a point
z\in\mathbb{B}(K,\epsilon)
satisfying

f(z)\leq\min_{x\in K}f(x)+\epsilon\left(\max_{x\in K}f(x)-\min_{x\in K}f(x)\right)\,.

We remark that, since we are only concerned with approximations,
\min
and
\max
are exchangeable
with
\inf
and
\sup
as necessary.

Lemma 3.23

(Bounding box by convex optimization)
.

Fix domain
[0,1]^{d}
, and let
\delta,\rho\in(0,1)
. There exists a randomized algorithm

BoundingBox
which, on input
(H,h)
from some unknown clustering
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{CV}_{\delta}
, where
h
is the representative of
H
, and access to
H
is given implicitly via
label queries while
h
is given explicitly, makes
O(d^{3}\log^{2}(d/\delta)\log(d/\rho))
label
queries and outputs a pair of closed axis-aligned boxes
B\subseteq B^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

satisfying, with probability at least
1-\rho
,

1.

B\subseteq\interior B^{*}(H)
;

2.

B^{*}(H)\subseteq\interior B^{\prime}
; and

3.

\mathsf{side}_{i}(B^{\prime})\leq 2\cdot\mathsf{side}_{i}(B)
for each
i\in[d]
.

Proof.

Let
\delta^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\delta/8
. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

For each
i\in[d]
,

(a)

Using standard boosting arguments, the algorithm uses label queries to
simulate membership queries to
H
, and takes the median of

O(\log(d/\rho))
executions of the convex optimization algorithm of
Theorem

3.22
with
convex set
H
, objective
f(x)=x_{i}-h_{i}
, and error parameter
\epsilon=\delta^{\prime}
, to obtain an
estimate
\bm{\ell}_{i,-}
that satisfies
\bm{\ell}_{i,-}=\ell^{*}_{i,-}\pm\delta
with
probability at least
1-\frac{\rho}{2d}
where
\ell^{*}_{i,-}\vcentcolon=|\inf_{x\in H}x_{i}-h_{i}|
.

(b)

Similarly, using another
O(\log(d/\rho))
executions of the convex
optimization algorithm with objective
f(x)=h_{i}-x_{i}
, obtain an estimate
\bm{\ell}_{i,+}
that
satisfies
\bm{\ell}_{i,+}=\ell^{*}_{i,+}\pm\delta
, where
\ell^{*}_{i,+}\vcentcolon=|\inf_{x\in H}h_{i}-x_{i}|
.

2.

Output the boxes
B
and
B^{\prime}
given by

B=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\forall i\in[d],\,h_{i}-\bm{\ell}_{i,-}+2\delta^{\prime}\leq x_{i}\leq h_{i}+\bm{\ell}_{i,+}-2\delta^{\prime}\right\}

and

B^{\prime}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\forall i\in[d],\,h_{i}-\bm{\ell}_{i,-}-2\delta^{\prime}\leq x_{i}\leq h_{i}+\bm{\ell}_{i,+}+2\delta^{\prime}\right\}

Each execution of the convex optimization algorithm uses
O\left(d^{2}\log^{2}\left(\frac{d\cdot\sqrt{d}}{\delta^{\prime}\cdot\delta}\right)\right)
label queries by
Theorem

3.22
, where
we have plugged in
R=O(\sqrt{d})
for the radius of the ball containing
[0,1]^{d}
. Each of
the
d
iterations uses
O(\log(d/\rho))
executions, so the query complexity claim follows. We
now show correctness.

We first show that
B\subseteq\interior B^{*}(H)
. For any
y\in B
and for each
i\in[d]
,
we have

y_{i}\leq h_{i}+\bm{\ell}_{i,+}-2\delta^{\prime}\leq h_{i}+\ell^{*}_{i,+}+\delta^{\prime}-2\delta^{\prime}<h_{i}+\ell^{*}_{i,+}=h_{i}+\sup_{x\in H}x_{i}-h_{i}=\sup_{x\in H}x_{i}\,,

and similarly
y_{i}>\inf_{x\in H}x_{i}
. Since this holds for every
i\in[d]
, we conclude
that
y\in\interior B^{*}(H)
, as desired.

Next, we show that
B^{*}(H)\subseteq\interior B^{\prime}
. For any
y\in B^{*}(H)
and for each
i\in[d]
, we have

y_{i}\geq\inf_{x\in H}x_{i}=h_{i}-\left\lvert\inf_{x\in H}x_{i}-h_{i}\right\rvert=h_{i}-\ell^{*}_{i,-}\geq h_{i}-\bm{\ell}_{i,-}-\delta^{\prime}>h_{i}-\bm{\ell}_{i,-}-2\delta^{\prime}\,,

and similarly
y_{i}<h_{i}+\bm{\ell}_{i,+}+2\delta^{\prime}
. Thus
y\in\interior B^{\prime}
, as desired.

To verify the third requirement, let
e_{i}
denote the
i
-th basis vector and note that, for
each
i\in[d]
, we have that
h-\delta e_{i},h+\delta e_{i}\in H
by definition of

\mathcal{CV}_{\delta}
, and hence
\ell^{*}_{i,+},\ell^{*}_{i,-}\geq\delta=8\delta^{\prime}
. Therefore

\frac{\mathsf{side}_{i}(B^{\prime})}{\mathsf{side}_{i}(B)}=\frac{\bm{\ell}_{i,+}+\bm{\ell}_{i,-}+4\delta^{\prime}}{\bm{\ell}_{i,+}+\bm{\ell}_{i,-}-4\delta^{\prime}}\leq\frac{\ell^{*}_{i,+}+\ell^{*}_{i,-}-2\delta^{\prime}+4\delta^{\prime}}{\ell^{*}_{i,+}+\ell^{*}_{i,-}-2\delta^{\prime}-4\delta^{\prime}}=\frac{\ell^{*}_{i,+}+\ell^{*}_{i,-}+2\delta^{\prime}}{\ell^{*}_{i,+}+\ell^{*}_{i,-}-6\delta^{\prime}}\leq\frac{18\delta^{\prime}}{10\delta^{\prime}}<2\,.

where in the first inequality we used the lower bounds on the random variables along with the
fact that
\frac{a+b}{a-b}\leq\frac{a^{\prime}+b}{a^{\prime}-b}
when
b<a^{\prime}\leq a
, and similarly for the
second inequality.
∎

We now use this subroutine to obtain cell rejection and discovery algorithms.

Lemma 3.24
.

Let
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the normalized
\ell_{p}
metric on
[0,1]^{d}
with
p\geq 1
, and let

\epsilon,\delta,\rho\in(0,1)
. Then

\mathsf{qreject}(\mathcal{CV}_{\delta},\mathcal{CV}_{\delta},\epsilon/d^{1/p},2\epsilon,\rho)=O(d^{3}\log^{2}(d/\delta)\log(d/\rho))\,.

Proof.

The algorithm is the following: simulate
BoundingBox
on input
(H,h)
to obtain output

B\subseteq B^{\prime}
; accept if
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)\leq\epsilon
and reject otherwise.

Suppose
BoundingBox
succeeds as per
Lemma

3.23
, which occurs with
probability at least
1-\rho
. We verify the requirements of
Definition

3.2
.

The first requirement is trivially satisfied, since we never output
\bot
. Now, suppose

\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)\leq\epsilon/d^{1/p}
, so that for any
x,y\in H
and
i\in[d]
, we have

|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\|x-y\|_{p}=d^{1/p}\mathsf{dist}(x,y)\leq d^{1/p}\cdot\epsilon/d^{1/p}=\epsilon
, and thus
\mathsf{side}_{i}(B^{*}(H))\leq\epsilon
. Then
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)\leq\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B^{*}(H))\leq\epsilon
, the first inequality since
B\subseteq\interior B^{*}(H)
and the second since

\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B^{*}(H))=\frac{1}{d^{1/p}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathsf{side}_{i}(B^{*}(H))^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq\frac{1}{d^{1/p}}\cdot\left(d\cdot\epsilon^{p}\right)^{1/p}=\epsilon\,.

Thus the algorithm accepts.

On the other hand, suppose
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)>2\epsilon
. Then
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B^{*}(H))\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)>\epsilon
,
the first inequality since
\mathsf{side}_{i}(B^{\prime})\leq 2\cdot\mathsf{side}_{i}(B)
and the second since
B^{*}(H)\subseteq\interior B^{\prime}
. Thus the algorithm rejects.
∎

If we are only required to accept clusterings made of axis-aligned boxes, then we may obtain a
tighter gap between the diameters we guarantee to accept and reject, as well as a cell discovery
procedure (which enables the uniformity testing application).

Lemma 3.25
.

Let
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the normalized
\ell_{p}
metric on
[0,1]^{d}
with
p\geq 1
, and let

\epsilon,\delta,\rho\in(0,1)
. Then

\mathsf{qreject}(\mathcal{CV}_{\delta},\mathcal{B}_{\delta},\epsilon,2\epsilon,\rho)=O(d^{3}\log^{2}(d/\delta)\log(d/\rho))\,.

Proof.

The algorithm is the following: simulate
BoundingBox
on input
(H,h)
to obtain output

B\subseteq B^{\prime}
. Query
\mathsf{LABEL}(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})
on two opposite corner points of
B
(namely
corner points that differ on every coordinate, chosen arbitrarily), say
x,y
, and output

\bot
if
\gamma(x)\neq\gamma(h)
or
\gamma(y)\neq\gamma(h)
. Otherwise, accept if

\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)\leq\epsilon
and reject if
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)>\epsilon
.

Suppose
BoundingBox
succeeds as per
Lemma

3.23
, which occurs with
probability at least
1-\rho
. We verify the requirements of
Definition

3.2
.

First, suppose
(\Gamma,\mathsf{rep})\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}

\bot

Now, suppose
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)\leq\epsilon
and suppose the algorithm does not output
\bot
.
Then
H
contains two opposite corner points of
B
, so
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)\leq\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)\leq\epsilon
, and the algorithm accepts.

On the other hand, suppose
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)>2\epsilon
and suppose the algorithm does not
output
\bot
. Then as in

3.24
, we have
\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(B^{*}(H))\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathsf{diam}_{\mathsf{dist}}(H)>\epsilon

∎

Lemma 3.26
.

Let
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the normalized

on input

. We verify the requirements of

.

Part II: Random Clustering

Having established bounds for worst-case adversarial clusterings in Part I, we now consider random
clusterings. We will prove two results:

1.

An upper bound on testing uniformity of distributions over the vertices of paths and cycles
with
zero
label queries (
Theorem

4.35

in
Section

4.4
). The proof of this theorem spans

Sections

4.2
,
4.3
and
4.4
.

2.

An improved bound in the same setting when queries are allowed
(
Theorem

4.38

.

4.1
Preliminaries

In Part II, we will consider the domain to be
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the integers mod
n
, which we interpret as
the vertices of either a path or a cycle. We will always write
\mu
for an arbitrary probability
distribution (which will be the input distribution to the tester), and write
\nu
for the uniform
distribution.

Let us recall the random clustering model. Let
G=(V,E)
be a path or cycle on
n
vertices

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Let
\rho\in(0,1]
be the “resolution” parameter. It will be convenient to also define

\eta\vcentcolon=1-\rho
. Then we will define a class
\mathcal{U}_{\rho}
of random clusterings. A random
clustering
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})\sim\mathcal{U}_{\rho}
is constructed as follows:

1.

Each edge
e\in E
is deleted independently with probability
\rho
to obtain a random
subgraph
\bm{H}
of
G
.

2.

The clustering
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})
is defined as
\bm{\Gamma}=\{\bm{\Gamma_{1}},\dotsc,\bm{\Gamma}_{\bm{k}}\}
where
\bm{k}
is the number of connected components of
\bm{H}

and each
\bm{\Gamma_{i}}
is a (maximal) connected component. Then
\bm{\mathsf{rep}}(\bm{\Gamma}_{i})
is
selected uniformly at random from
\bm{\Gamma}_{i}
(the specific choice of
\bm{\mathsf{rep}}
is not
important).

Observe that a resolution
\rho=1
means that
\bm{H}
has no edges (with probability 1) and
therefore each
\bm{\Gamma}_{i}
is a singleton – every element of the domain is distinguished
perfectly. We define the conditions that must hold for a uniformity tester in the confused collector
model with these random clusterings:

Definition 4.1

(Distribution Testing with Random Clustering)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be either a cycle or a path and let
\epsilon,\rho,\delta\in(0,1)
. We say an
algorithm
A
is an
(\epsilon,\rho)
-uniformity tester for
G
, with error probability
\delta
,
if it is given clustered-sample and label oracle access to inputs
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})
and

\mu
, with
\mu
being an arbitrary distribution over
V
and
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})\sim\mathcal{U}_{\rho}
defined above, and satisfies

1.

If
\mu=\nu
then
\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{A}\text{ accepts}\right]\geq 1-\delta
; and

2.

If
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
then
\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{A}\text{ rejects}\right]\geq 1-\delta
,

where the probabilities are over the randomness of
A
, the responses to the oracle calls, and the
random clustering
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\mathsf{rep}})\sim\mathcal{U}_{\rho}
.

4.1.1
Notation for Random Subgraphs

It is convenient to introduce a shared vocabulary for analyzing the cycle and the path. We will
label the
n
vertices of the cycle with the set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of integers mod
n
, and we will also label
the
edges
of the cycle with the set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, so that edge
i
connects vertices
i
and
i+1

(with arithmetic mod
n
). We will treat the path on
n
vertices as the subgraph of the cycle that
excludes edge
n-1
connecting vertices labeled
0
and
n-1
. When the subgraph
H
contains edge

e
, we will sometimes abuse notation and write
e\in H
.

A
circular interval
is a tuple
\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
where
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
d\geq 0
, we define the elements
\mathcal{E}\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
as the multiset of elements
starting at vertex
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and containing the
d-1
elements “clockwise” from
i
, i. e. the
multiset
\{i,i+1,i+2,\dotsc,i+d-1\}
, where addition is mod
n
. Note that for
d=1
this
contains only
i
, while for
d>n
this contains some elements with multiplicity greater than
1. If
d<0
, we define the elements
\mathcal{E}\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
as the multiset of elements
starting at vertex
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and containing the
|d|-1
elements “counter-clockwise”, i. e. the
multiset
\{i,i-1,i-2,\dotsc,i-|d|+1\}
.

The
endpoints
of
\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
are the integers
i
and
i+d-1
if
d\geq 0
, or
i+d+1
and
i
if
d<0
. We will often drop the
\mathcal{E}
from the notation, and equivocate
between the tuple
\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
and its multiset of elements, so that we write
x\in\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
instead of
\mathcal{E}\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
. However, a circular interval is

not
identified with its multiset of elements; for example, the circular intervals
\langle\!\langle i,n\rangle\!\rangle
and
\langle\!\langle i+1,n\rangle\!\rangle
both contain the same elements
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, but they have
different endpoints.

For a circular interval
I
and a vector
u:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

u[I]\vcentcolon=\sum_{s\in I}u_{s}\,,

where we note that
s
may occur multiple times in
I
and
u_{s}
is counted each time.

For a circular interval
\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
, we will define the circular interval
\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle^{*}
to be the integers corresponding to the
edges
induced by the vertices

\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
; specifically

\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle^{*}=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\text{ if }d=0\\
\langle\!\langle i,d-1\rangle\!\rangle&\text{ if }d\geq 1\\
\langle\!\langle i-1,1-|d|\rangle\!\rangle&\text{ if }d\leq-1\,.\end{cases}

For any
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we say that a circular interval
I

crosses

s
if
s\in I^{*}
; i. e.
s
is an edge between two vertices in
I
.

Fix any subgraph
H
of the cycle (or path), and suppose that
H
has
b
connected components; note
that each connected component is a circular interval. We define the
cells
induced by
H

as
\Gamma_{1},\dotsc,\Gamma_{n}
such that
\Gamma_{1},\dotsc,\Gamma_{b}
are the connected components
of
H
, while
\Gamma_{b+1},\dotsc,\Gamma_{n}=\emptyset
. For each vertex
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we define

\gamma(i)\vcentcolon=t\text{ such that }i\in\Gamma_{t}\,.

We say that two vertices
i,j
are
joined
if
\gamma(i)=\gamma(j)
, and we define the

join matrix

\Phi=\Phi(H)
as

\Phi_{i,j}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\gamma(i)=\gamma(j)\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We define a
join function

J
such that for any circular interval

I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
,

J(I)\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}\left[\forall e\in\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle^{*}:e\in H\right]\,.

Thus if
J(I)=1
, then for every
i,j\in\mathcal{E}(I)
we have
\Phi_{i,j}=1
.

For the path, the circular intervals that cross the edge between vertices
0
and
n-1

are irrelevant, so it is convenient to define
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}}
as the set of all circular intervals, and

\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}
as the set of all circular intervals that do not cross edge
n-1
.

We will use
\mathcal{I}\in\{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}},\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}\}
to denote the set of circular intervals relevant to
the analysis. In the case
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}}
, each pair of vertices
i\leq j
has two disjoint paths
connecting them and therefore may be joined together in two ways. We define
\smallinterval(i,j)

and
\largeinterval(i,j)
as the two circular intervals defined as follows. Let

I_{1}\vcentcolon=\langle\!\langle i,j-i+1\rangle\!\rangle\qquad\text{ and }\qquad I_{2}\vcentcolon=\langle\!\langle j,n-(j-i)+1\rangle\!\rangle

as the circular intervals corresponding to the two separate paths between
i
and
j
. Then we
define

\smallinterval(i,j)\vcentcolon=\arg\max_{I\in\{I_{1},I_{2}\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I]\right]\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\largeinterval(i,j)\vcentcolon=\arg\min_{I\in\{I_{1},I_{2}\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I]\right]\,,

breaking ties arbitrarily. Note that, in the case of the path, we will have only one way of joining

i
and
j
, so that
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]\right]=0
in this case. Symmetrically, when

i>j
we define
\smallinterval(i,j)\vcentcolon=\smallinterval(j,i)
and

\largeinterval(i,j)\vcentcolon=\largeinterval(j,i)
.

For
\mathcal{I}\in\{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}},\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}\}
, we define

\zeta(\mathcal{I})\vcentcolon=\max_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]\right]\,.

Proposition 4.2
.

\zeta(\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}})=0
and
\zeta(\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}})\leq(1-\rho)^{n/2}
.

Proof.

For distinct
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, define
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
as above, and note that for
a\in\{1,2\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I_{a}]\right]=(1-\rho)^{|I_{a}^{*}|}\,.

Since
I_{1},I_{2}
partition
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, so we have either
|I_{1}|\geq n/2
or
|I_{2}|\geq n/2
, so the
minimum is at most
(1-\rho)^{n/2}
.
∎

4.1.2
Notation for Clustered Samples

For a fixed sample (i. e. multiset)
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, write
T_{i}
for the
multiplicity of element
i
in
S
. We then define for each
i\in[n]
the variable

X_{i}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}:\gamma(j)=i}T_{j}\,,

which is the total multiplicity of elements from cell
\Gamma_{i}
that occur in
S
.

Observe that the above variables depend on the subgraph
H
and the sample
S
. For a random
subgraph
\bm{H}
chosen according to the confused collector sampling procedure, and a random sample

\bm{S}
of vertices, we write the above variables in bold to denote the random variables depending
on
\bm{H}
and
\bm{S}
. We will then write

\phi\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}\right]\,,

and observe that

\phi_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{\gamma}(i)=\bm{\gamma}(j)\right]\,.

We will write
\bm{\Phi}
for the random join matrix and
\phi
for its expectation when
statements hold for both the path and the cycle. Otherwise, we will specify
\bm{\Phi}^{\mathsf{path}}
or

\bm{\Phi}^{\mathsf{cycle}}
.

We will use the standard Poissonization technique to simplify the analysis. We write
m
for the
sample-size parameter. For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, let
\bm{T}_{j}
be the random variable counting the
number of sample points obtained from element
j
. Using the Poissonization technique, we have

\bm{T}_{j}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(m\mu_{j})

for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and therefore

\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(m\cdot\mu[\bm{\Gamma}_{i}])

for each
i\in[n]
.

4.2
Expectation of the Test Statistic

The main step in our algorithm for testing uniformity will be to accept or reject depending on
whether the
test statistic
surpasses some threshold. The test statistic is:

Definition 4.3

(Test Statistic)
.

For a fixed parameter
m
and random variables defined as above, we define

\bm{Y}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}(\bm{X}_{i}-1)\,.

By expanding the variables
\bm{X}_{i}
, the test statistic may be written as the quadratic form

\bm{Y}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\bm{T}-\|\bm{T}\|_{1}\right)\,.

We start by giving an expression for the expectation of the statistic
\bm{Y}
. Recall that we write

\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to[0,1]
for the distribution over the vertices (of either the path or the
cycle),
m
is the sample-size parameter, and
\phi=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}\right]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

The statistic
\bm{Y}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\bm{Y}}\right]=\mu^{\top}\phi\mu
.

Proof.

We use the facts that
\bm{T}
and
\bm{\Phi}
are independent and that, for
i\neq j
,

\bm{T}_{i}
and
\bm{T}_{j}
are independent. We will also use the property that, for

\bm{Z}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(\lambda)
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Z}\right]=\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{Z}\right]=\lambda
and, therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Z}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Z}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Z}\right]^{2}
. We obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\bm{T}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|_{1}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\bm{T}_{j}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\right]-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{j}\right]\phi_{i,j}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\right]-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\right]=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(m\mu_{i})(m\mu_{j})\phi_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\mu^{\top}\phi\mu\,.\qed

We would like to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]
is small when
\mu=\nu
is uniform, and large when
\mu

is far from uniform. Letting
\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}
denote the test statistic when
\mu
is the uniform
distribution, we have:

Proposition 4.5

(Expectation of
\bm{Y}
in the uniform case)
.

When
\mu=\nu
,
\bm{Y}=\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]=\nu^{\top}\phi\nu=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}\,.

Proof.

The claim follows from
Proposition

4.4
and the
assumption that
\mu=\nu=\vec{1}/n
.
∎

We must now show that
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]
is larger than this threshold when
\mu
is far from uniform.

Proposition 4.6
.

Write
\mu=\nu+z
. Then
\bm{Y}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]=\nu^{\top}\phi\nu+2\nu^{\top}\phi z+z^{\top}\phi z\,.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
Proposition

4.4
by expanding the
quadratic form and recalling that
\bm{\Phi}
is always a symmetric matrix, and hence so is

\phi=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}\right]
.
∎

Therefore, we must find a lower bound on
2\nu^{\top}\phi z+z^{\top}\phi z
. Our strategy will
be:

1.

Show that the minimum eigenvalue of
\phi
is large, and hence so is
z^{\top}\phi z
when

\|z\|_{2}^{2}
is large; we will do this separately for the path and the cycle.

2.

Show that the term
\nu^{\top}\phi z
is small in absolute value, so it does not affect the sum
too much.

Both
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}
and
\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}
enjoy nice properties (they are a Toeplitz and a circulant matrix,
respectively), and we bound the minimum eigenvalue of each in turn. Let
{\lambda_{\min}}(\cdot),{\lambda_{\max}}(\cdot)
denote the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of a (real symmetric) matrix,
respectively.

4.2.1
Expectation for the Path

When
G
is the path, the expected join matrix
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{\mathsf{path}}\right]
has a simple
formulation in terms of
\rho
. Recall the notation
\eta\vcentcolon=1-\rho
.

Proposition 4.7
.

The matrix
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}
is given by

\phi^{\mathsf{path}}_{i,j}=\eta^{\lvert i-j\rvert}

for each
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Proof.

Here, the relevant intervals are
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}
. Hence,
for any
i<j
, we have that
i
and
j
are in the same cell if and only if every edge
between them is in
\bm{H}
:

\phi^{\mathsf{path}}_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{\mathsf{path}}_{i,j}=1\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\forall e\in\langle\!\langle i,j-i+1\rangle\!\rangle^{*}:e\in\bm{H}\right]=\eta^{j-i}\,.\qed

Lemma 4.8

(Minimum eigenvalue of
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}
)
.

Let
\rho\in(0,1]
. Then
\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{path}})>\rho/2
.

Proof.

The matrix
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}
is a symmetric Toeplitz matrix,
and its inverse may be found as in
[
Sra
]
.
Recall that
\eta=1-\rho
, so that
0\leq\eta<1
and
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}_{i,j}=\eta^{\lvert i-j\rvert}
by
Proposition

4.7
.
Then the inverse of
\phi^{\mathsf{path}}
(written
\phi^{-1}
for short)
is the following tridiagonal matrix:

\phi^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\eta^{2}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}1&-\eta&0&0&\dotsm&0\\
-\eta&1+\eta^{2}&-\eta&0&\dotsm&0\\
0&-\eta&1+\eta^{2}&-\eta&\dotsm&0\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
0&\dotsc&\dotsc&-\eta&1+\eta^{2}&-\eta\\
0&\dotsc&\dotsc&0&-\eta&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

Now, we may upper bound the maximum eigenvalue of
\phi^{-1}

using the Gershgorin circle theorem:

\lambda_{\max}\left(\phi^{-1}\right)\leq\max_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\left\{\phi^{-1}_{i,i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left\lvert\phi^{-1}_{i,j}\right\rvert\right\}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-\eta^{2}}\right)\cdot\left(1+\eta^{2}+2\eta\right)=\frac{1+\eta}{1-\eta}\,.

Hence we obtain

\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{path}})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}\left(\phi^{-1}\right)}\geq\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}>\frac{1-\eta}{2}=\rho/2\,.\qed

4.2.2
Expectation for the Cycle

When
G
is the cycle, so that the expected join matrix is
\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{\mathsf{cycle}}\right]
,
we need to account for the small and large intervals (in the notation of

Section

4.1.1
) connecting
i
and
j
, as follows.

Proposition 4.9
.

The matrix
\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}
is given by

\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}_{i,j}=\eta^{\lvert i-j\rvert}+\eta^{n-\lvert i-j\rvert}-\eta^{n}\,.

Proof.

The sets
\smallinterval(i,j)^{*}
and
\largeinterval(i,j)^{*}
have sizes

\min\{\lvert i-j\rvert,n-\lvert i-j\rvert\}
and
\max\{\lvert i-j\rvert,n-\lvert i-j\rvert\}
, respectively
(recall they partition the edges of the cycle). By the principle of inclusion-exclusion,

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{\mathsf{cycle}}_{i,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{J}(\smallinterval(i,j))=1\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{J}(\largeinterval(i,j))=1\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[\forall e\in E:e\in\bm{H}\right]

\displaystyle=\eta^{\lvert i-j\rvert}+\eta^{n-\lvert i-j\rvert}-\eta^{n}\,.\qed

It is convenient to work with a simplified close approximation for
\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}
. Essentially, we wish
to ignore the large intervals and instead work with the matrix
\phi^{\smallinterval}
given by

\phi^{\smallinterval}_{i,j}\vcentcolon=\eta^{\left\lvert\smallinterval(i,j)\right\rvert-1}\,.

We will need the observation that
\zeta(\mathcal{I})
is negligibly small in our range of parameter
\rho
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a constant. Suppose
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
, and let
K>0

be any constant. Then for all sufficiently large
n
,

\zeta(\mathcal{I})\leq\eta^{n/2}=o(n^{-K})\,.

Proof.

The first inequality is
Proposition

4.2
. The second one is easy to check:

\eta^{n/2}=(1-\rho)^{n/2}\leq e^{-\rho\cdot n/2}\leq e^{-\Omega(n^{-\delta}\cdot n)}=e^{-\Omega(n^{1-\delta})}=o(n^{-K})\,.\qed

We are now ready to lower bound the eigenvalues of
\phi^{\smallinterval}
and

\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}
. We first lower bound
\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\smallinterval})
, and then show that the
approximation error is negligible.

Fact 4.11

(Eigenvalues of circulant matrices; see
[
Gra06
]
)
.

Let
c_{0},c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-1}\\
c_{n-1}&c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{2}&c_{3}&c_{4}&\cdots&c_{1}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&\cdots&c_{0}\end{bmatrix}

given by
M_{j,k}=c_{(k-j)\mod n}
has eigenvalues

\lambda_{\ell}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}\omega^{\ell k}\qquad\ell=0,1,\dotsc,n-1\,,

where
\omega=e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{n}}
is a primitive
n
-th root of unity.

Lemma 4.12

(Minimum eigenvalue of
\phi^{\smallinterval}
)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a constant and suppose
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
. Then for all
sufficiently large
n
,
\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\smallinterval})>\rho/3
.

Proof.

First assume
n
is odd. For each
k=0,1,\dotsc,n-1
, let

c_{k}\vcentcolon=\phi^{\smallinterval}_{0,k}
, so that
\phi^{\smallinterval}
is a symmetric
circulant matrix of the form stated in
4.11
. In particular,
letting
h\vcentcolon=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
for convenience, we have

c_{k}=\begin{cases}\eta^{k}&\text{if }k\leq h\\
\eta^{n-k}&\text{if }k>h\,.\end{cases}

Therefore for each
\ell=0,1,\dotsc,n-1
, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ell}
is

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}\omega^{\ell k}=c_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{h}\eta^{k}\omega^{\ell k}+\sum_{k=h+1}^{n-1}\eta^{n-k}\omega^{\ell k}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{h}\eta^{k}\omega^{\ell k}+\sum_{k=1}^{h}\eta^{n-(n-k)}\omega^{\ell(n-k)}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{h}\eta^{k}\omega^{\ell k}+\sum_{k=1}^{h}\eta^{k}\omega^{-\ell k}=1+\frac{\eta\omega^{\ell}-\eta^{h+1}\omega^{\ell(h+1)}}{1-\eta\omega^{\ell}}+\frac{\eta\omega^{-\ell}-\eta^{h+1}\omega^{-\ell(h+1)}}{1-\eta\omega^{-\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\eta(\omega^{\ell}+\omega^{-\ell})+\eta^{2}+\eta\omega^{\ell}-\eta^{2}-\eta^{h+1}\omega^{\ell(h+1)}+\eta^{h+2}\omega^{\ell h}+\eta\omega^{-\ell}-\eta^{2}-\eta^{h+1}\omega^{-\ell(h+1)}+\eta^{h+2}\omega^{-\ell h}}{1-\eta(\omega^{\ell}+\omega^{-\ell})+\eta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\eta^{2}-\eta^{h+1}(\omega^{\ell(h+1)}+\omega^{-\ell(h+1)})+\eta^{h+2}(\omega^{\ell h}+\omega^{-\ell h})}{1-\eta(\omega^{\ell}+\omega^{-\ell})+\eta^{2}}=\frac{(1-\eta)(1+\eta)\pm O(\eta^{n/2})}{1-2\eta\cos(2\pi\ell/n)+\eta^{2}}\,,

where we used the identity
e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}=2\cos(\theta)
in the last step.
Thus, recalling that
\eta=1-\rho\in[0,1)
, we conclude that
\lambda_{\ell}
is lower bounded
by

\frac{(1-\eta)(1+\eta)\pm O(\eta^{n/2})}{1-2\eta\cos(2\pi\ell/n)+\eta^{2}}\geq\frac{(1-\eta)(1+\eta)}{(1+\eta)^{2}}-\frac{O(\eta^{n/2})}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\geq\frac{\rho}{2}-\frac{O(\eta^{n/2})}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\,.

Then, using
Proposition

4.10
with
K=3\delta
,

\frac{O(\eta^{n/2})}{(1-\eta)^{2}}=\frac{o(n^{-3\delta})}{\rho^{2}}=o(n^{-3\delta}\cdot n^{2\delta})=o(n^{-\delta})=o(\rho)\,,

and thus
\lambda_{\ell}>\rho/3
. When
n
is even, the same argument applies with an extra term
of order
O(\eta^{n/2})
, which leaves the asymptotic analysis unaffected.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a constant and suppose
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
. Then for all
sufficiently large
n
,
\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}})>\rho/4
.

Proof.

Let
\phi^{\mathsf{err}}\vcentcolon=\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}-\phi^{\smallinterval}
. It is standard to check that

\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\smallinterval})+\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{err}})
.
Since
\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\smallinterval})>\rho/3
by

Lemma

4.12
, it suffices to show that

\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{err}})>-o(\rho)
.
Since
\phi^{\mathsf{cycle}}_{i,j}=\eta^{\lvert\smallinterval(i,j)\rvert-1}+\eta^{\lvert\largeinterval(i,j)\rvert-1}-\eta^{n}
by

Proposition

4.9
, we obtain

\phi^{\mathsf{err}}_{i,j}=\eta^{\lvert\largeinterval(i,j)\rvert-1}-\eta^{n}

for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. By definition of
\zeta(\mathcal{I})
and recalling

Proposition

4.2
, we conclude that

\|\phi^{\mathsf{err}}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta(\mathcal{I})\leq\eta^{n/2}\,.

By the Gershgorin circle theorem and
Proposition

4.10
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\phi^{\mathsf{err}})

\displaystyle\geq\min_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\Big\{\phi^{\mathsf{err}}_{i,i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\left\lvert\phi^{\mathsf{err}}_{i,j}\right\rvert\Big\}>-n\cdot\eta^{n/2}\geq-n\cdot o(n^{-1-\delta})=-o(n^{-\delta})\geq-o(\rho)\,.\qed

4.2.3
Bounds on the Cross Term

Now we show that the cross term
\nu^{\top}\phi z
is small. When
G
is the cycle, this term will
in fact be zero; when
G
is the path, the cross term is relevant due to the asymmetry between the
vertices closer to the endpoints or to the middle. However, this will not be a problem as long as

\|z\|_{\infty}
is not too large (which indeed the tester will be able to check).

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\rho\in(0,1]
and let
\phi=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}\right]
be the corresponding expected join matrix.
Let
\delta>0
be any positive real number. Then for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

1.

\sum_{i}z_{i}=0
; and

2.

\|z\|_{\infty}\leq\delta
,

it is the case that

\left\lvert\nu^{\top}\phi z\right\rvert\leq\frac{2\delta}{n\rho^{2}}\,.

Proposition

4.14
is proved by constructing a worst-case vector
z
whose negative
(respectively positive) entries are as concentrated as possible toward the ends (respectively the
middle) of the vector, subject to the
\ell_{\infty}
constraint, and evaluating geometric series to
upper bound the cross term. The proof is elementary, and we defer it to

Appendix

B
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
C>0
, and let
\mu
be a distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
satisfying
\|\mu\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C\log n}{m}
. Then as long as
\frac{8C\log n}{\rho^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\leq m\leq Cn\log n
, the
following holds:

\left\lvert 2\nu^{\top}\phi z\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2n}\epsilon^{2}\rho\,.

Proof.

Write
\mu=\nu+z
. Since
\|\mu\|_{1}=\|\nu\|_{1}=1
, it follows that
\sum_{i}z_{i}=0
,
satisfying the first condition of
Proposition

4.14
. We will show that
z
also
satisfies the second condition with
\delta\vcentcolon=\frac{C\log n}{m}
. Indeed, that
z_{i}\leq\frac{C\log n}{m}
for every
i
follows immediately from the assumption on
\mu
. On the other
hand, using the assumption that
m\leq Cn\log n
we conclude that
z_{i}=\mu(i)-\frac{1}{n}\geq-\frac{1}{n}\geq-\frac{C\log n}{m}
and thus
\|z\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C\log n}{m}
as
desired.
Proposition

4.14
implies that
\left\lvert\nu^{\top}\phi z\right\rvert\leq\frac{2C\log n}{mn\rho^{2}}
, and combining this inequality with our assumed lower bound on
m
we obtain

\left\lvert 2\nu^{\top}\phi z\right\rvert\leq 2\cdot\frac{2C\log n}{mn\rho^{2}}=\frac{8C\log n}{\rho^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{2}\rho}{2n}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\epsilon^{2}\rho\,.\qed

4.2.4
Gap in Expected Values

We combine the previous results to show the desired separation in the expected value of
\bm{Y}
.
The first step is to incorporate the eigenvalue bounds:

Proposition 4.16
.

For all sufficiently large
n
, the following holds.
Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
, and write

\mu=\nu+z
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n}\epsilon^{2}\rho+2\nu^{\top}\phi z\,.

Proof.

Combine
Propositions

4.6
and
4.5
,
along with the fact that
x^{\top}Mx\geq\lambda_{\min}(M)\|x\|_{2}^{2}
for any symmetric matrix

M
and vector
x
and the eigenvalue bounds

Lemmas

4.8
and
4.13
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=\nu^{\top}\phi\nu+2\nu^{\top}\phi z+z^{\top}\phi z\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\lambda_{\min}(\phi)\|z\|_{2}^{2}+2\nu^{\top}\phi z

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\frac{\rho}{4}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+2\nu^{\top}\phi z=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+2\nu^{\top}\phi z\,.

Then, since
\|z\|_{1}=2\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>2\epsilon
, we have

\|z\|_{2}^{2}>\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{n}\right)^{2}\cdot n=4\epsilon^{2}/n
, concluding the
proof.
∎

Lemma 4.17

(Separation in the expected value of
\bm{Y}
)
.

Let
C,c>0
be constants, and let
m=c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n
. Then for all sufficiently large
n
and all
\epsilon,\rho\in(0,1]
satisfying
\rho\geq(c/C)^{2/3}\frac{\log^{2/3}n}{n^{1/3}\epsilon^{4/3}}
, the following holds. Suppose
\mu
is
a distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
satisfying
\|\mu\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C\log n}{m}
and

\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
. Write
\mu=\nu+z
. Then the test statistic
\bm{Y}

satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.16
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n}\epsilon^{2}\rho+2\nu^{\top}\phi z\,.

Hence, we will be done if we can show that

2\nu^{\top}\phi z\geq-\frac{1}{2n}\epsilon^{2}\rho
. This will follow immediately from

Lemma

4.15
as long as we can verify the preconditions on
m
. We first
check the lower bound:

m\geq\frac{8C\log n}{\rho^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\iff c\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log^{2}n}{\rho^{3/2}}\geq\frac{8C\log n}{\rho^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\iff\rho\geq(8C/c)^{2/3}\frac{1}{n^{1/3}\log^{2/3}n}\,,

which holds for all sufficiently large
n
by our assumption on
\rho
. As for the upper bound,

m\leq Cn\log n\iff c\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log^{2}n}{\rho^{3/2}}\leq Cn\log n\iff\rho\geq(c/C)^{2/3}\frac{\log^{2/3}n}{n^{1/3}\epsilon^{4/3}}\,,

which holds by assumption. Hence
Lemma

4.15
applies and we are done.
∎

4.3
Concentration of the Test Statistic

Our goal in this section is to establish the concentration of the test statistic
\bm{Y}
, by giving
the formal version of
Lemma

1.6
. Recall that the (random) partition
of vertices into cells
\bm{\Gamma}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{\Gamma}_{n}
depends on the random subgraph
\bm{H}
.
We start by noting that we can break down the variance of
\bm{Y}
into two components by the law of
total variance:

\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{Y}\right]=\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]+\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]\,.

4.3.1
Relative Concentration

One of the main tools in our analysis will be “relative concentration”​​, which compares the
probability mass of
\mu
inside the circular intervals
I
to the size of that interval, weighted
by the resolution
\rho
– with the crucial addition that intervals with weight less than some
threshold
t
essentially have no effect, so this notion differs from the standard
\ell_{\infty}

norm in that it “smooths out” small perturbations.

Definition 4.18

(Relative Concentration)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\in\{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}},\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}\}
and let
\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a probability
distribution. Let
t>0
. Then we define

\chi_{t}(\mu)\vcentcolon=\max_{I\in\mathcal{I}:|I|\leq n}\frac{\mu[I]}{\max\left\{\rho|I^{*}|,t\right\}}\,.

We will instantiate this measure with
t=\frac{1}{\log n}
and use it in the analysis of the
tester in
Section

4.4
. We will require the following lemma, which allows us to
find an interval
I
exhibiting a large ratio between
\mu[I]
and
\rho|I^{*}|
if we assume
high relative concentration
\chi_{t}(\mu)
.

Proposition 4.19
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\in\{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}},\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}\}
, and let
\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a probability
distribution. Then there exists
I\in\mathcal{I}
satisfying:

1.

\rho|I^{*}|\leq t
; and

2.

\mu[I]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot t\cdot\chi_{t}(\mu)
.

Proof.

By definition of relative concentration, there exists an interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
of size at most

n
such that either

1.

\rho|I^{*}|\leq t
and
\mu[I]=t\cdot\chi_{t}(\mu)
; or

2.

\rho|I^{*}|\geq t
and
\mu[I]=\rho|I^{*}|\cdot\chi_{t}(\mu)
.

In the former case,
I
satisfies the required conditions and we are done.
Therefore, we may assume that the second condition holds.

Let
I
be an minimal interval satisfying

\rho|I^{*}|\geq t
and
\mu[I]\geq\rho|I^{*}|\chi_{t}(\mu)
(in particular,
equality will hold). Note that we must have
|I|\geq 2
, since otherwise
I^{*}
would be empty,
contradicting the assumption that
\rho|I^{*}|\geq t>0
.

Consider any partition
partition
I=L\cup R
into two intervals
L,R
such that
L
is on the left and
R
is on
the right. Then the following inequality holds:

\chi_{t}(\mu)=\frac{\mu[I]}{\rho|I^{*}|}=\frac{\mu[L]+\mu[R]}{\rho|L^{*}|+\rho|I^{*}\setminus L^{*}|}\leq\max\left\{\frac{\mu[L]}{\rho|L^{*}|},\frac{\mu[R]}{\rho|I^{*}\setminus L^{*}|}\right\}\leq\max\left\{\frac{\mu[L]}{\rho|L^{*}|},\frac{\mu[R]}{\rho|R^{*}|}\right\}\,.

Consider separately the cases where
|I|
is even or odd. First suppose
|I|
is even. Then
partition
I=L\cup R
such that
|L|=|R|=|I|/2
and observe that
|L^{*}|=|R^{*}|
,
so either
\rho|L^{*}|,\rho|R^{*}|<t
or
\rho|L^{*}|,\rho|R^{*}|\geq t
. If
\rho|L^{*}|<t

then either
\mu[L]\geq\mu[I]/2\geq\frac{t}{2}\chi_{t}(\mu)
and
L
satisfies
the desired conditions (due to the inequality above), or
\mu[R]\geq\mu[I]/2
and
R
satisfies
the desired conditions. On the other hand, if
\mu[L]\geq t
then either
L
or
R
contradicts
the minimality of
I
.

Now suppose
|I|
is odd. Consider two ways to partition
I
into intervals. Let
I=L_{-}\cup R_{-}
be the partition such that
|L_{-}|=\lfloor|I|/2\rfloor
and
|R_{-}|=\lceil|I|/2\rceil
, and let
I=L_{+}\cup R_{+}
be the partition such that
|L_{+}|=\lceil|I|/2\rceil
and
|R_{+}|=\lfloor|I|/2\rfloor
.
Note that
|L_{-}^{*}|=|R_{+}^{*}|
and
|L_{+}^{*}|=|R_{-}^{*}|=|I^{*}|/2
.

Suppose that
\mu[L_{-}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu[I]
. If
\rho|L_{-}^{*}|\leq t
then
L_{-}^{*}
satisfies the
required conditions and we are done, so assume
\rho|L_{-}^{*}|>t
. Since
|I^{*}|>2|L_{-}^{*}|
, we have

\mu[L_{-}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu[I]=\frac{1}{2}\rho|I^{*}|\chi_{t}(\mu)>\rho|L_{-}^{*}|\chi_{t}(\mu)\,,

which contradicts the minimality of
I
. A similar argument shows that
R_{+}
satisfies the
required conditions when
\mu[R_{+}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu[I]
. So we may assume
\mu[L_{-}],\mu[R_{+}]<\frac{1}{2}\mu[I]
in the sequel, which implies
\mu[L_{+}],\mu[R_{-}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu[I]
.

Now if
\rho|L_{+}^{*}|\leq t
, then
L_{+}^{*}
satisfies the required conditions and we are done.
Suppose
\rho|L_{+}^{*}|>t
and recall
|L_{+}^{*}|=|R_{-}^{*}|=|I^{*}|/2
. Then

\rho|I^{*}|=2\rho|L_{+}^{*}|
, so

\mu[L_{+}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu[I]\geq\frac{1}{2}\rho|I^{*}|\chi_{t}(\mu)=\rho|L_{+}^{*}|\chi_{t}(\mu)\,,

which contradicts the minimality of
I
and concludes the proof.
∎

4.3.2
Variance of the Test Statistic: First Component

We bound the first component of the variance,
\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]
.

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\in\{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}},\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}\}
.
For every
i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the following hold:

1.

\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(\bm{J})_{i,j}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\right]
;

2.

\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(\bm{J})_{i,j}\cdot\Phi(\bm{J})_{k,\ell}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\cdot\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]+4\cdot\zeta(\mathcal{I})
.

Proof.

Recall that
\Phi(\bm{J})_{i,j}=1
if and only if
\bm{\gamma}(i)=\bm{\gamma}(j)
. This will occur
if
\smallinterval(i,j)\subseteq\bm{\Gamma}_{\gamma(i)}
, which happens when

\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]=1
, yielding the first conclusion. Next, observe

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}=\max\{\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)],\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]\}\leq\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]+\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]\,.

To prove the second statement, expand the product and use the fact that

\bm{J}
is Boolean-valued:

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Phi}_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]+\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]\right)\left(\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]+\bm{J}[\largeinterval(k,\ell)]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]+2\left(\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]+\bm{J}[\largeinterval(k,\ell)]\right)\,.

We have
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\largeinterval(i,j)]\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\largeinterval(k,\ell)]\right]\leq\zeta(\mathcal{I})

by definition, so the conclusion follows from taking the expectation.
∎

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\in\{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{cycle}},\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}\}
. There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the first
component of the variance of
\bm{Y}
satisfies

\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]\leq 5\zeta(\mathcal{I})+c\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{I}\\
1\leq d\leq n\end{subarray}}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\mu[I]^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}(I)\right]\,.

Proof.

Fix any subgraph
H
. Conditional on
\bm{H}=H
,

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}^{\top}\Phi\bm{T}\right]-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|_{1}\right]=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\bm{T}_{j}\right]\Phi_{i,j}-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Poi}(m)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\right]+\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{i}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{T}_{j}\right]\Phi_{i,j}\right)-\frac{1}{m}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(m+m^{2}\mu^{\top}\Phi\mu\right)-\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle=\mu^{\top}\Phi\mu\,,

and therefore the desired variance is

\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]=\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\mu^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\mu\right]\,.

Then, recalling that
\phi=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}\right]
, we expand

\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\mu^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\mu\right]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\mu^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\mu\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\mu\right)^{2}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mu^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\mu\right]\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu^{\top}\bm{\Phi}\mu\right)^{2}\right]-\left(\mu^{\top}\phi\mu\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]-\left(\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\phi_{i,j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Phi}_{k,\ell}\right]-\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\phi_{i,j}\phi_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Phi}_{k,\ell}\right]-\phi_{i,j}\phi_{k,\ell}\right)\,.

We now use
Proposition

4.20
to simplify the quantity

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Phi}_{k,\ell}\right]-\phi_{i,j}\phi_{k,\ell}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Phi}_{k,\ell}\right]-\phi_{i,j}\phi_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]+4\cdot\zeta(\mathcal{I})\,.

If the intervals
\smallinterval(i,j)
and
\smallinterval(k,\ell)
are disjoint, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]=0\,.

On the other hand, if these intervals are not disjoint, we will employ the simple upper bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]\,.

We then consider two cases.

First, suppose that for every edge
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
\smallinterval(i,j)
crosses
s
or

\smallinterval(k,\ell)
crosses
s
. Then
\mathbb{Z}_{n}\subseteq\smallinterval(i,j)^{*}\cup\smallinterval(k,\ell)^{*}
, so
\bm{J}(\smallinterval(i,j))\cdot\bm{J}(\smallinterval(k,\ell))=1

only when every edge appears in
\bm{H}
, which happens with probability at most
\zeta(\mathcal{I})

(since this event implies that every large interval is joined).
In this case,
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]\leq\zeta(\mathcal{I})
.

As for the second case, let
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
be such that neither
\smallinterval(i,j)
nor

\smallinterval(k,\ell)
crosses
s
. Since
\smallinterval(i,j)
and
\smallinterval(k,\ell)
are not
disjoint, it follows that there exists an interval
I=I_{i,j,k,\ell}\in\mathcal{I}
satisfying the
following:

1.

The set
\smallinterval(i,j)\cup\smallinterval(k,\ell)
is equal to
the set of elements of
I_{i,j,k,\ell}
, where we are here taking the union as sets (not as
multisets);

2.

The endpoints of
I_{i,j,k,\ell}
are two of the indices
i,j,k,\ell
; and,

3.

|I_{i,j,k,\ell}|\leq n
(because, in particular,
I_{i,j,k,\ell}
does not cross
s
).

It follows that
\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]=1
if and only if

\bm{J}[I]=1
, and hence we have the upper bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I]\right]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Phi}_{k,\ell}\right]-\phi_{i,j}\phi_{k,\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(i,j)]\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[\smallinterval(k,\ell)]\right]+4\cdot\zeta\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\zeta+\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\left(\mathds{1}\left[\mathbb{Z}_{n}\subseteq\smallinterval(i,j)^{*}\cup\smallinterval(k,\ell)^{*}\right]\cdot\zeta(\mathcal{I})+\mathds{1}\left[I_{i,j,k,\ell}\text{ exists}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I_{i,j,k,\ell}]\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq 5\zeta(\mathcal{I})\|\mu\|_{1}^{4}+\sum_{i,j,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\mathds{1}\left[I_{i,j,k,\ell}\text{ exists}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I_{i,j,k,\ell}]\right]\,.

The latter is bounded by summing over all intervals
I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{I}

with
d\leq n
and for each one taking the expression
c\cdot\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\sum_{j,k\in I}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I]\right]
, where
i
and
i+d-1
are the endpoints of
I
, and
c
is a constant
counting the number of ways to get intersecting intervals with endpoints in
i,(i+d-1),k,\ell
.
Now, using
\sum_{j,k\in I}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}=\mu[I]^{2}
, we obtain

\mathrm{Var}\left[\mu^{\top}\Phi(\bm{J})\mu\right]\leq 5\zeta(\mathcal{I})+c\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{I}\\
1\leq d\leq n\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}[I]\right]\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\mu[I]^{2}\,.\qed

Lemma 4.22

(First component of the variance)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a constant, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large and let
\mu
be a
probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Suppose that
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
and
0<t<1
. Then

\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]\leq O\left(\frac{\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}}{\rho}\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.21
, for some constant
c>0
we have

\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]\leq 5\zeta(\mathcal{I})+c\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{I}\\
1\leq d\leq n\end{subarray}}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\mu[I]^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}(I)\right]\,.

We start with the second component of the RHS.
Recall that for any
I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}(I)\right]\leq\eta^{d-1}
(this value
may be zero if
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{path}}
and
I
crosses the edge between vertices
0
and
n-1
).
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{I}\\
1\leq d\leq n\end{subarray}}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\mu[I]^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{J}(I)\right]\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\mu[\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle]^{2}\eta^{d-1}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\eta^{d-1}\left[\chi_{t}(\mu)\cdot\max\left\{\rho(d-1),t\right\}\right]^{2}

(Relative concentration)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{d=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\eta^{d-1}\left[\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}\cdot\left(\rho^{2}d^{2}+1\right)\right]

(Since
t<1
)

\displaystyle\quad=\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}\left(\rho^{2}\sum_{d=1}^{n}d^{2}\eta^{d-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}\eta^{d-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu_{i}\mu_{i+d-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\left(\rho^{2}\sum_{d=1}^{n}d^{2}\eta^{d-1}+\sum_{d=1}^{n}\eta^{d-1}\right)

(By Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\left(\rho^{2}\cdot\frac{1+\eta}{\rho^{3}}+\frac{1}{\rho}\right)

(Since
\rho=1-\eta
)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 3\cdot\frac{\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}}{\rho}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(Since $\eta<1$)}\,,

as desired. Now, we show that the term
5\zeta(\mathcal{I})
satisfies the claimed bound.
First,
Proposition

4.10
implies that
\zeta(\mathcal{I})\leq 1/n^{3}
.
Note that, by taking intervals
I=\langle\!\langle i,1\rangle\!\rangle
in

Definition

4.18
, we have that

\chi_{t}(\mu)\geq\mu(i)/t
for every
i
, and hence

\chi_{t}(\mu)\geq 1/nt
. Along with the inequalities
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\geq 1/n
,

\rho\leq 1
and
t<1
, we obtain

\zeta(\mathcal{I})\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}t^{2}}\leq\frac{\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}}{n}\leq\frac{\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}}{\rho}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\,.\qed

4.3.3
Variance of the Test Statistic: Second Component

We now bound the second component of the variance,
\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]
.
The first step is to relate it to the 2- and 3-norms of the clustered
vector
\mu
. Recall that
\bm{T}_{i}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(m\mu_{i})
is the number of occurrences of vertex
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
in the sample. We first compute the variance of the terms
\bm{X}_{i}(\bm{X}_{i}-1)

that make up the test statistic:

Proposition 4.23
.

If
\bm{X}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(\lambda)
, then
\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{X}(\bm{X}-1)\right]=4\lambda^{3}+2\lambda^{2}
.

Proof.

The Poisson random variable
\bm{X}
has the following raw moments (see e. g.
[
Rio37
]
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda+\lambda^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda+3\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{3}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda+7\lambda^{2}+6\lambda^{3}+\lambda^{4}\,.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[X(X-1)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(X(X-1))^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X(X-1)\right]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[(X^{2}-X)^{2}\right]-(\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X\right])^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[X^{4}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[X^{3}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=(\lambda+7\lambda^{2}+6\lambda^{3}+\lambda^{4})-2(\lambda+3\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{3})+(\lambda+\lambda^{2})-(\lambda+\lambda^{2})^{2}+2(\lambda+\lambda^{2})\lambda-\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=4\lambda^{3}+2\lambda^{2}\,.\qed

Recall the notation
{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}
for the distribution on the cluster representatives
induced by
\mu
(
Definition

2.3
).

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
H
be a subgraph with induced cells
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{1},\dotsc,\Gamma_{b})
, and let
\mu
be a
distribution on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
Then the conditional variance of
\bm{Y}
given
\bm{H}=H
satisfies

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]=\frac{2}{m^{2}}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{m}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{3}^{3}\,.

Proof.

Using
Proposition

4.23
, the desired variance is

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\bm{X}_{i}(\bm{X}_{i}-1)\right]=\frac{1}{m^{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\left[4\left(m\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\right)^{3}+2\left(m\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{m}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{3}^{3}+\frac{2}{m^{2}}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}\,.\qed

We will need the following auxiliary result.

Proposition 4.25

(Cells are almost always small)
.

Let
K\geq 2
be a constant, and
suppose
n\geq 3
. Then the cells
\bm{\Gamma}=(\bm{\Gamma}_{i},\dotsc,\bm{\Gamma}_{\bm{b}})

induced by
\bm{H}
satisfy

\left\lvert\bm{\Gamma}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}\qquad\forall i\in[\bm{b}]

except with probability at most
1/n^{K}
.

Proof.

Let
d\vcentcolon=\left\lceil\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}\right\rceil
, and fix any interval

I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
. The probability that
I
is joined is

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{J}(I)=1\right]=(1-\rho)^{d-1}\leq(1-\rho)^{\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}-1}\leq(1-\rho)^{\frac{(2K-1)\log n}{\rho}}\leq e^{-(2K-1)\log n}=1/n^{2K-1}\leq 1/n^{K+1}\,,

where we used the facts that
n\geq 3\implies\frac{\log n}{\rho}\geq 1
and that

K\geq 2\implies 2K-1\geq K+1
. Now, if any cell has size at least
d
, then some interval

I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle
satisfies
J(I)=1
. Since there are at most

n
such intervals, the probability of this event is at most
1/n^{K}
by the union bound.
∎

Lemma 4.26

(Second component of the variance)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large and let
\mu
be a probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Suppose
m\leq\poly(n)
and
0<t<1
. Then

\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]\leq O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\cdot\left(1+m\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Proof.

Let
K\geq 2
be some constant such that
m\leq O(n^{K-1})
.
By
Proposition

4.25
, the cells

\bm{\Gamma}=(\bm{\Gamma}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{\Gamma}_{n})
induced by
\bm{H}
are such that

\left\lvert\bm{\Gamma}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}
for all
i
,
except with probability at most
1/n^{K}
. We show that the variance is small when this condition
holds, and that the low-probability case where the condition fails does not contribute too much
to the expectation.

Case 1.
Suppose
H
is such that its induced cells

\Gamma=(\Gamma_{1},\dotsc,\Gamma_{b})
satisfy

\left\lvert\Gamma_{i}\right\rvert\leq\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}

for every
i\in[b]
. We wish to show that
\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]

satisfies the upper bound from the statement.
We start with the result from

Lemma

4.24
:

\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]=\frac{2}{m^{2}}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{m}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{3}^{3}\,.

We start by bounding the first term in the RHS. For each cell
\Gamma_{i}
, we have

\left({\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\right)_{i}^{2}=\left(\sum_{j\in\Gamma_{i}}\mu_{j}\right)^{2}=\sum_{j,k\in\Gamma_{i}}\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\leq\sum_{j,k\in\Gamma_{i}}\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{2}=\left\lvert\Gamma_{i}\right\rvert\sum_{j\in\Gamma_{i}}\mu_{j}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\log n}{\rho}\right)\sum_{j\in\Gamma_{i}}\mu_{j}^{2}\,.

Hence, we obtain

\frac{1}{m^{2}}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{b}{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}(i)^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\right)\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\,,

as desired. Moving on to the second term, first note that

\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{3}^{3}=\sum_{i}\mu[\Gamma_{i}]^{3}\leq\sum_{i}(\max_{j}\mu[\Gamma_{j}])\mu[\Gamma_{i}]^{2}=\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{\infty}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}
. We claim that

\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{\infty}\leq O\left(\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)
.
Fix any
i\in[b]
and consider entry

\left({\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\right)_{i}=\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]
. The anticoncentration of

\mu
yields

\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\leq\chi_{t}(\mu)\cdot\max\left\{\rho\left(\left\lvert\Gamma_{i}\right\rvert-1\right),t\right\}\,.

Combining with the assumption that
\left\lvert\Gamma_{i}\right\rvert\leq O\left(\frac{\log n}{\rho}\right)

and recalling that
t<1
, we obtain that

\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\leq O\left(\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)
, as
claimed. We have already shown that

\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\cdot O(\log n)
, and thus

\frac{1}{m}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{3}^{3}\leq\frac{1}{m}\cdot O\left(\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)\cdot\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\cdot O(\log n)=O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\cdot m\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\,,

which concludes Case 1.

Case 2.
In the rare event that
\bm{H}=H
is a subgraph that
fails the small-cells condition of Case 1, we will fall back to a looser upper bound for
the conditional variance that holds for every
H
. We once again start with the result
from
Lemma

4.24
:

\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]=\frac{2}{m^{2}}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{m}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{3}^{3}\,.

Using
\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{1}=1
along with the monotonicity of
\ell_{p}
norms gives

\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]\leq\frac{2}{m^{2}}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{4}{m}\|{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma}\rfloor}\|_{1}^{3}\leq\frac{6}{m^{2}}\cdot m=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot O(n^{K-1})\,.

Concluding the argument.
We now combine both cases to upper bound the expected
variance. Using
Proposition

4.25
, we have

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{H}\text{ satisfies }\|\bm{\Gamma}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}\right]\underset{\bm{H}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\right.\right]\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}\text{ satisfies }\|\bm{\Gamma}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}\right.\right]\\
+\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{H}\text{ does not satisfy }\|\bm{\Gamma}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2K\log n}{\rho}\right]\max_{H}\left\{\underset{\bm{T}}{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\bm{Y}\;\;\left|\;\;\bm{H}=H\right.\right]\right\}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\cdot\left(1+m\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot O(n^{K-1})\,.

Since
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|\nu\|_{2}^{2}=1/n
, the first term dominates the second, concluding the
proof.
∎

We obtain the general form of our concentration bound; the result below gives

Lemma

1.6
with
\chi(\mu)=\chi_{t}(\mu)
for any
0<t<1
. While we use a specialized version in the
analysis of our tester (
Lemma

4.33
) that is better by logarithmic
factors, we prove the following for the sake of generality.

Lemma 4.27

(Formal version of
Lemma

1.6
)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a constant, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large and let
\mu
be a
probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Suppose
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
,
m\leq\poly(n)

and
0<t<1
. Then for all
\tau>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]\right\rvert\geq\tau\right]\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho\tau^{2}}\cdot\max\left\{\chi_{t}(\mu),\frac{1}{m}\right\}^{2}\cdot O(\log^{2}n)\,.

Proof.

By

Lemmas

4.22
and
4.26
,
along with the law of total variance, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{Y}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}}{\rho}\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\cdot\left(1+m\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\cdot O\left(\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}+2\cdot\chi_{t}(\mu)\cdot\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right)\cdot O(\log^{2}n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\cdot\max\left\{\chi_{t}(\mu),\frac{1}{m}\right\}^{2}\cdot O(\log^{2}n)\,.

The claim follows by Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎

4.4
Testing Uniformity Without Queries

We now give the upper bound for testing uniformity on either the path or the cycle with zero
queries. The tester is
Algorithm

1
, and consists of two steps:

1.

Concentration test: checks whether any count in the sample is too large;
this case corresponds to highly concentrated distributions, which can be rejected.

2.

Collision-based test: accept or reject depending on whether the test statistic
\bm{Y}
is
below a certain threshold.

Algorithm 1

Uniformity tester in the confused collector model.

Set
m\leftarrow c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log^{2}n}{\rho^{3/2}}
.

Constants:

\alpha,\beta,L,c>0
to be defined later.

Requires:

\rho\geq\frac{L\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}
.

1:

procedure

UniformityTester-ConfusedCollector
(
\mu,n,\epsilon,\rho
)

2:

Let
X=(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})
be the variables defined in

Section

4.1.2

for a sample of size
\mathsf{Poi}(m)
from
\mu
.

3:

If

\max_{i}X_{i}\geq\alpha\log n
then
reject
.

4:

Y\leftarrow\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i}X_{i}(X_{i}-1)
.

5:

If

Y\geq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}+\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho

then
reject
.

6:

Accept
.

Remark on the optimality of the collision-based tester.

Considering that we give a
Poissonized tester whose main statistic
\bm{Y}
is equivalent to the collision-based statistic of

[
GR11
]
when
\rho=1
, it may seem surprising that we obtain a sample complexity of
\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{n}/\epsilon^{2}\right)
– as opposed to
O\left(\sqrt{n}/\epsilon^{4}\right)
– when it
is known that, for an analysis based on bounding the variance of
\bm{Y}
and applying Chebyshev’s
inequality, establishing the optimal sample complexity is only possible with a different test
statistic (e. g. the modified chi-squared statistic
[
CDVV14
,
DKN15
,
VV17
]
) or a careful analysis
of the non-Poissonized tester
[
DGPP19
]
(see also the Remark in Section 2 therein). Our analysis
implicitly avoids this issue via the relative concentration test, which upper bounds

\|\mu\|_{\infty}
, and indeed specializing our proof to the case
\rho=1
would yield a sample
complexity dependence on
1/\epsilon^{4}
rather than
\sqrt{n}/\epsilon^{4}
; and since our analysis
only handles the case
\epsilon\geq\widetilde{\Omega}\left(n^{-1/4}\right)
(see

Remark

4.36
), the term
\sqrt{n}/\epsilon^{2}
dominates

1/\epsilon^{4}
.

4.4.1
Easy Case: Highly Concentrated Distributions

Definition 4.28

(Highly concentrated distributions)
.

Given a constant
C>0
, positive integer
m
, resolution parameter
\rho
, and probability
distribution
\mu
over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we say that
\mu
is
C
-highly concentrated (under resolution

\rho
with respect to
m
)
if
\chi_{t}(\mu)\geq\frac{C\log^{2}n}{m}
, where

t=\frac{1}{\log n}
.

To understand this definition (in particular, the choice of
t
), observe that

Algorithm

1
tries to reject highly concentrated distributions by checking
whether any entry
\bm{X}_{i}
in its clustered sample is large, namely
\bm{X}_{i}\geq\alpha\log n

(which is unlikely to occur under the uniform distribution). When
\mu
is highly concentrated,

Proposition

4.19
ensures an interval
I
exists, satisfying
\rho|I^{*}|\leq 1/\log n
(so
I
is likely to be joined in the clustering) and
\mu[I]\geq\frac{C\log n}{2m}
; so, for sufficiently large
C
, the
\mathsf{Poi}(m\mu[I])
will be larger than

\alpha\log n
, causing the algorithm to reject.

Furthermore, when
\mu
is not highly concentrated, the condition

\chi_{t}(\mu)<\frac{C\log^{2}n}{m}
means that the term
\max\left\{\chi_{t}(\mu),\frac{1}{m}\right\}
in the general concentration inequality
(
Lemma

4.27
) cannot be improved by more than logarithmic factors.
Therefore this choice of definition is both sensible for the first step of the tester, and nearly
optimal for our analysis of the second step of the tester.

Observation 4.29
.

Let
t\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\log n}
. Then by inspecting the definition of relative concentration
and breaking down into the cases where
|I|=1
or
|I|\geq 2
, we have

\chi_{t}(\mu)\leq\max\left\{\frac{2}{\rho},\log n\right\}\cdot\|\mu\|_{\infty}
.

Remark 4.30
.

If
\mu
is not
C
-highly concentrated, then in particular
\|\mu\|_{\infty}<\frac{C\log n}{m}
,
as can be seen by taking intervals
I=\langle\!\langle i,1\rangle\!\rangle
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

We now show that the first step of the tester correctly accepts the uniform distribution and
rejects highly concentrated distributions with good probability. Therefore, we will be able
to assume that
\mu
is not highly concentrated when analyzing the second step of the tester.

We will need the following tail bounds for the Poisson distribution, as stated in
[
Can17
]
.

Fact 4.31
.

Let
\bm{X}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(\lambda)
for some
\lambda>0
. Then for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}\leq\lambda-t\right],\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}\geq\lambda+t\right]\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2(\lambda+t)}}\,.

The result below uses the hypothesis
m\leq n\rho
, which intuitively corresponds to the sublinear
sample complexity regime in the standard uniformity testing model. This hypothesis holds in the
range of parameters we consider, but not necessarily in more extreme regimes (see

Remark

4.36
).

Lemma 4.32
.

For sufficiently large constant
\alpha>0
and all sufficiently large
n
, the following holds.
Suppose
m\leq n\rho
. Then for any distribution
\mu
over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we have:

1.

If
\mu
is uniform, the first step of the tester only rejects with probability
at most
1/100
; and

2.

If
\mu
is
4\alpha
-highly concentrated, the first step of the tester rejects
with probability at least
99/100
.

Proof.

Completeness.
Suppose
\mu
is the uniform distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
From
Proposition

4.25
, we obtain that every cell has size at most

\frac{4\log n}{\rho}
except with probability
o(1)
. Assume that this is the case, and fix some
particular cell
\Gamma_{i}
. The number of elements sampled from this cell is distributed as

\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathsf{Poi}\left(m\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\right)=\mathsf{Poi}\left(m|\Gamma_{i}|/n\right)
.
Then, using
4.31
and for
\alpha>16
, the probability that

\bm{X}_{i}
is so large that the tester rejects is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}_{i}\geq\alpha\log n\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Poi}\left(m\cdot\frac{4\log n}{\rho}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\right)\geq\alpha\log n\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Poi}(4\log n)\geq\alpha\log n\right]\qquad

(Since
m\leq n\rho
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Poi}(4\log n)-4\log n\geq(\alpha-4)\log n\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{(\alpha-4)^{2}\log^{2}n}{2(4\log n+(\alpha-4)\log n)}}\leq e^{-\frac{(\alpha/2)^{2}\log n}{2\alpha}}\leq e^{-2\log n}=1/n^{2}\,.

Hence, the probability that this happens for any
\bm{X}_{i}
is at most
1/n=o(1)
.

Soundness.
Suppose
\mu
is
4\alpha
-highly concentrated.
Using
Proposition

4.19
and the definition of high concentration,
we may choose some interval
I=\langle\!\langle i,d\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{I}
satisfying

1.

(d-1)\rho\leq\frac{1}{\log n}
,
and thus
|I|=d\leq 1+\frac{1}{\rho\log n}
; and

2.

\mu[I]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\log n}\cdot\frac{4\alpha\log^{2}n}{m}=\frac{2\alpha\log n}{m}
.

We first claim that all the elements in
I
will be joined with high probability, i. e. every
edge in
I^{*}
will be sampled into
\bm{H}
. Indeed, by the union bound, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[J(I)=0\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\exists e\in I^{*}:e\not\in\bm{H}\right]\leq(|I|-1)\cdot\rho\leq\frac{1}{\rho\log n}\cdot\rho=\frac{1}{\log n}=o(1)\,.

Now, suppose every element in
I
belongs to the same cell, say
\Gamma_{i}
. Recall that the
random variable
\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathsf{Poi}\left(m\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\right)
counts the number
of elements drawn from this cell, and by our assumption on
I
, we have

m\mu\left[\Gamma_{i}\right]\geq m\cdot\frac{2\alpha\log n}{m}=2\alpha\log n\,.

We now claim that, with high probability,
\bm{X}_{i}>\alpha\log n
and hence the tester will
reject. Indeed, using
4.31
, the probability that this does not
occur is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}_{i}\leq\alpha\log n\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Poi}\left(2\alpha\log n\right)\leq\alpha\log n\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\mathsf{Poi}\left(2\alpha\log n\right)\leq 2\alpha\log n-\alpha\log n\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}\log^{2}n}{2(2\alpha\log n+\alpha\log n)}}=e^{-\frac{\alpha\log n}{6}}=o(1)\,.\qed

4.4.2
Correctness of the Tester

Combining our separation and concentration results, we can show that
\bm{Y}
is concentrated on the
correct side of the tester’s threshold. First, we specialize our general concentration result to the
case of non-highly-concentrated distributions.

Lemma 4.33
.

Let
C>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
be constants, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large and
suppose
\mu
is a probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
that is not
C
-highly concentrated
with respect to
m
. Suppose
\rho\geq\Omega(n^{-\delta})
and
m\leq\poly(n)
. Then for all

\tau>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]\right\rvert\geq\tau\right]\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho m^{2}\tau^{2}}\cdot O\left(\log^{4}n\right)\,.

Proof.

We again combine

Lemmas

4.22
and
4.26
,
which apply since here
0<t=\frac{1}{\log n}<1
, via the law of total variance, and
conclude with Chebyshev’s inequality (directly applying the general

Lemma

4.27
would also work, but lead to a small loss in logarithmic
factors). We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{Y}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\chi_{t}(\mu)^{2}}{\rho}\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\cdot\left(1+m\chi_{t}(\mu)\log n\right)\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{4}n}{\rho m^{2}}\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+O\left(\frac{\log n}{m^{2}\rho}\cdot\left(1+m\cdot\frac{\log^{2}n}{m}\cdot\log n\right)\right)\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho m^{2}}\cdot O\left(\log^{4}n\right)\,.\qed

Lemma 4.34
.

Let
\alpha,L>0
be constants. Then there exist constants
\beta>0
, and

c=c_{\alpha,\beta}>0
such that
the following holds for all sufficiently large
n
.
Let
\epsilon,\rho\in(0,1]
satisfy
\rho\geq\frac{L\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}
.
Suppose
\mu
is a probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
that is not
4\alpha
-highly concentrated
with respect to
m=c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n
.
Let
T\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}+\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho
be the
threshold used by the second step of
Algorithm

1
.
Then the test statistic
\bm{Y}
satisfies the following:

1.

(Completeness) If
\mu=\nu
, then
\bm{Y}<T
with probability at least
99/100
;

2.

(Soundness) If
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
, then
\bm{Y}>T
with probability at
least
99/100
.

Proof.

Completeness.
Suppose
\mu=\nu
.
By
Proposition

4.5
,
\bm{Y}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}
.
Hence for any fixed
\beta
(to be chosen below),
it suffices to show that
\bm{Y}<\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]+\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho
with good
probability. By
Lemma

4.33
and using the fact that

\|\nu\|_{2}^{2}=1/n
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]+\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]\right\rvert\geq\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho\right]\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho m^{2}\left(\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho\right)^{2}}\cdot O(\log^{4}n)=\frac{O(n\log^{4}n)}{\beta^{2}m^{2}\epsilon^{4}\rho^{3}}\,,

and we have

\frac{O(n\log^{4}n)}{\beta^{2}m^{2}\epsilon^{4}\rho^{3}}\leq 1/100\iff m\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n\right)\,,

(1)

as desired. Thus, there exists constant

c^{(1)}=c^{(1)}_{\alpha,\beta}
such that if

m\geq c^{(1)}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n

then, for all sufficiently large
n
,
\bm{Y}<T
with probability at least
99/100
.

Soundness.
We proceed similarly.
We first note that the conditions of
Lemma

4.17

are met: the condition
\|\mu\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C\log n}{m}
holds with
C=4\alpha
by

Remark

4.30
, and the lower bound on
\rho
holds
for the following reason. First, since
\rho\leq 1
always, its lower bound implies that

\epsilon\geq\Omega(n^{-1/4})
:

\frac{L\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}\leq\rho\leq 1\implies\epsilon\geq\frac{L^{5/4}\log n}{n^{1/4}}\,.

Then, we conclude that
\rho=\Omega\left(\frac{\log^{2/3}n}{n^{1/3}\epsilon^{4/3}}\right)
,
since
\rho\geq\frac{L\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}
and

\frac{\left(\frac{\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}\right)}{\left(\frac{\log^{2/3}n}{n^{1/3}\epsilon^{4/3}}\right)}=(\log n)^{2/15}n^{2/15}\epsilon^{8/15}\geq(\log n)^{2/15}n^{2/15}\Omega(n^{-2/15})=\omega(1)\,.

Thus, writing
\mu=\nu+z
, by

Lemmas

4.17
and
4.5

we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}^{(\nu)}\right]+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}+\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Therefore it suffices to show that, for appropriately chosen
\beta
, we have

\bm{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny?}}}}{{>}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho\,.

Recall that, when
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
, we have
\|z\|_{1}>2\epsilon
, which implies

\|z\|_{2}^{2}>4\epsilon^{2}/n
and hence

\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}>\frac{1}{2n}\epsilon^{2}\rho
, so that

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}\rho<\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}+2\beta\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-(1-2\beta)\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Thus, for
\beta\leq 1/3
, we have
1-2\beta\geq\beta
and it suffices to show that the
following holds with probability at least
99/100
:

\bm{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny?}}}}{{>}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-\beta\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\,.

We apply
Lemma

4.33
again, along with

\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\|\nu\|_{2}^{2}+\|z\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}+\|z\|_{2}^{2}
and

\|z\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{n}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-\beta\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]\right\rvert\geq\beta\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}}{\rho m^{2}\left(\beta\frac{\rho}{8}\|z\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}\cdot O(\log^{4}n)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\frac{1}{n}+\|z\|_{2}^{2})O(\log^{4}n)}{\beta^{2}m^{2}\rho^{3}\|z\|_{2}^{4}}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{n}O(\log^{4}n)}{\beta^{2}m^{2}\rho^{3}(\epsilon^{2}/n)^{2}}+\frac{O(\log^{4}n)}{\beta^{2}m^{2}\rho^{3}(\epsilon^{2}/n)}=\frac{O(n\log^{4}n)}{\beta^{2}m^{2}\rho^{3}\epsilon^{4}}\,.

This failure probability is asymptotically the same as that obtained in the completeness case.
Thus there exists a constant
c^{(2)}=c^{(2)}_{\alpha,\beta}>0
such that, for

m\geq c^{(2)}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n

and all sufficiently large
n
,
\bm{Y}>T
with probability at least
99/100
.
Setting
c=\max\left\{c^{(1)}_{\alpha,\beta},c^{(2)}_{\alpha,\beta}\right\}

concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we establish correctness by combining our results for the two steps of the tester:

Theorem 4.35

(Formal version of
Theorem

1.4
)
.

There exist constants
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
,
c=c_{\alpha,\beta}>0
,
and
L=L_{c}>0
such that
the following holds for all sufficiently large
n
. Suppose
\epsilon,\rho\in(0,1]
satisfy

\rho\geq\frac{L\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}
and let
G
be either the cycle or the
path on
n
vertices.
Then
Algorithm

1
(instantiated with constants

\alpha,\beta,L\text{ and }c
) is an
(\epsilon,\rho)
-tester for uniformity with sample
complexity
\Theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n\right)
and query
complexity 0.

Proof.

We start by instantiating
\alpha>0
large enough as per

Lemma

4.32
. That lemma also requires that
m\leq n\rho
, which we
now verify. Fix any constant
c>0
and suppose

m=c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho^{3/2}}\log^{2}n
. Then

m\leq n\rho\iff c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log^{2}n}{\rho^{3/2}}\leq n\rho\iff\rho^{5/2}\geq c\cdot\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{1/2}\epsilon^{2}}\iff\rho\geq\frac{c^{2/5}\log^{4/5}n}{n^{1/5}\epsilon^{4/5}}\,.

Therefore, for any choice of
c
, setting
L=L_{c}\geq c^{2/5}
ensures that
m\leq n\rho
.

Thus, instantiate
\beta,c>0
as provided by

Lemma

4.34
, and the corresponding
L_{c}
as above.
Now, we can use
Lemmas

4.32
and
4.34

to establish overall correctness of the tester. (Note that the sample complexity claim follows
from the specification of the algorithm.)

Completeness.
By
Lemma

4.32
, the first step of the tester
rejects only with probability at most
1/100
. Likewise, by

Lemma

4.34
, the second step of the tester rejects
only with probability at most
1/100.
Hence the total rejection probability is at most

2/100<1/10
.

Soundness.
There are two cases depending on the concentration of
\mu
. First, suppose

\mu
is
4\alpha
-highly concentrated. Then the first step of the tester rejects with
probability at least
99/100
by
Lemma

4.32
. On the other hand, if

\mu
is not
4\alpha
-highly concentrated, then the second step of the tester rejects with
probability at least
99/100
by
Lemma

4.34
. Either
way, the tester rejects with probability at least
99/100>9/10
.
∎

Remark 4.36
.

Our tester and analysis cover the range of resolution parameter
\rho\geq\widetilde{\Theta}\left(n^{-1/5}\epsilon^{-4/5}\right)
, which, as shown in the proof of

Lemma

4.34
, also implies an
\epsilon\geq\widetilde{\Theta}\left(n^{-1/4}\right)
bound. As shown above, together with our sample complexity these
bounds ensure that
m\leq n\rho
, which was required by our

Lemma

4.32
. An interesting question is whether there exists a
zero-query tester for more extreme parameter ranges, in particular those requiring sample
complexity
m\gg n
. For the situation where queries are allowed, see the next subsection.

4.5
Testing Uniformity With Queries

We show that the uniformity testing result from
Theorem

4.35
can be improved
if queries are allowed. The tester will use the instance-optimal identity tester of
[
VV17
]
to
test identity on the
singletons
of the random clustering.

Definition 4.37
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a set and let
\Gamma
be a clustering of
\mathcal{X}
. We say that
x\in\mathcal{X}
is a

\Gamma
-singleton
if
\Gamma_{\gamma(x)}=\{x\}
.

Theorem 4.38
.

There exists a constant
L>0
such that the following holds for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
G
be the path or cycle on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and let
\rho\in\left[L(n\epsilon)^{-1/4},1\right]
. Then there is an
(\epsilon,\rho)
-tester for uniformity on
G

using
O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho\epsilon^{2}}\right)
clustered samples and
O(\rho n\log n)

label queries.

Proof.

Let
\nu
be the uniform distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Let
c_{1},c_{2}>0
be universal constants to
be specified later. The algorithm is the following:

1.

Using binary search, attempt to learn every cell of the clustering using
O(\log n)

label queries per cell. Stop and reject if more than
c_{1}\cdot\rho n
cells are
discovered, so that
O(\rho n\log n)
queries are used at most.

2.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
be the set of
\Gamma
-singletons of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and let

\overline{S}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus S
. If
|S|>3\rho^{2}n
, halt and reject.
Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the clustering

\{\overline{S}\}\cup\{\{x\}\;|\;x\in S\}
,
i. e.
\Gamma^{\prime}
has one cluster for each
\Gamma
-singleton plus one
cluster for any remaining elements. Run the instance-optimal identity tester of

[
VV17
]
on input distribution
{\mu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma^{\prime}}\rfloor}
against target distribution

{\nu}_{\lfloor{\Gamma^{\prime}}\rfloor}
with proximity parameter
c_{2}\rho^{2}\epsilon
and success
probability
99/100
, and accept or reject according to the output of the tester.

First step.

We first claim that the first step rejects only with probability at most
1/100
. Note that the
number of clusters satisfies
|\bm{\Gamma}|\leq 1+\sum_{e\in G}\mathds{1}\left[e\not\in\bm{H}\right]
, and
this sum ranges over edges
e=(x,x+1)
for
0\leq x\leq n-1
when
G
is the cycle, and
0\leq x\leq n-2
when
G
is the path. Since each edge of
G
appears in
\bm{H}
with probability

1-\rho
, the expected number of clusters is
\mathbb{E}\left[|\bm{\Gamma}|\right]\leq 1+\rho n\leq 2\rho n
,
the last inequality since
\rho n\geq Ln^{3/4}\epsilon^{-1/4}\geq 1
. Choosing
c_{1}=200
,
Markov’s inequality yields that
|\bm{\Gamma}|\geq c_{1}\cdot\rho n=200\rho n
only with
probability at most
1/100
.

Number of singletons.

Let us also upper bound the number of singletons. Let
A_{1}\vcentcolon=\{2k:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}\cap\{1,\dotsc,n-2\}
, and let
A_{2}\vcentcolon=\{2k+1:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}\cap\{1,\dotsc,n-2\}
. Note that for each
i
, the vertices of
G
indexed by elements of
A_{i}
form an
independent set, so the random variables
\{\mathds{1}\left[x\in\bm{S}\right]\;|\;x\in A_{i}\}
are mutually
independent. Moreover, for each
x\in A_{i}
we have that
(x-1,x)
and
(x,x+1)
are both
edges of
G
, so
\mathbb{P}\left[x\in\bm{S}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[(x-1,x)\not\in\bm{H}\text{ and }(x,x+1)\not\in\bm{H}\right]=\rho^{2}
. For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, define
\bm{X}_{i}\vcentcolon=|\bm{S}\cap A_{i}|=\sum_{x\in A_{i}}\mathds{1}\left[x\in\bm{S}\right]
. Let
a_{i}\vcentcolon=|A_{i}|
, and note that
n/3\leq a_{i}\leq n/2
for all sufficiently large
n
. Then
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}_{i}\right]=\rho^{2}a_{i}
and a Chernoff bound
yields, for any constant
0<\delta<1
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}_{i}\geq(1+\delta)\rho^{2}a_{i}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}\rho^{2}a_{i}}{3}}\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}\rho^{2}n}{9}}\leq 1/100\,,

the third inequality since
\rho^{2}n\geq L^{2}n^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/2}=\omega_{n}(1)
. Then,
observing that
|\bm{S}|\leq\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{X}_{2}+2
since at most two elements of
\bm{S}
do
not appear in
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
, the Chernoff and union bounds yield

\mathbb{P}\left[|\bm{S}|\geq 3\rho^{2}n\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[|\bm{S}|-2\geq 2\rho^{2}n\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}_{1}\geq\rho^{2}n\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{X}_{2}\geq\rho^{2}n\right]\leq 2/100\,,

the first inequality since
\rho^{2}n\geq 2
. Thus
|\bm{S}|\leq 3\rho^{2}n
except with
probability at most
2/100
.

Second step (correctness).

To show correctness of the second step, we need to show that the identity tester will accept
when
\mu=\nu
and reject when
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
, both with probability at
least (say)
98/100
. When
\mu=\nu
, we have
{\mu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}={\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}
for any value of
\bm{\Gamma}
, so the identity tester will indeed
accept.

Now, suppose
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)>\epsilon
. We need to show that

\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor},{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor})\geq c_{2}\rho^{2}\epsilon

with good probability. We again break the analysis into even- and odd-indexed elements to make
use of independence. For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, let
b_{x}\vcentcolon=\left(\frac{1}{n}-\mu(x)\right)^{+}
. For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let

w_{i}\vcentcolon=\sum_{x\in A_{i}}\left(\nu(x)-\mu(x)\right)^{+}=\sum_{x\in A_{i}}b_{x}\,.

Let
\epsilon_{0}\vcentcolon=\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)
. Recalling that
\mu
and
\nu
have total mass
1

each, we have

\epsilon_{0}=\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}b_{x}\leq\left(\sum_{x\in A_{1}}b_{x}\right)+\left(\sum_{x\in A_{2}}b_{x}\right)+b_{0}+b_{n-1}\leq w_{1}+w_{2}+\frac{2}{n}\,.

Since
\epsilon_{0}\geq\epsilon\geq L^{4}\rho^{-4}n^{-1}\geq 4/n
, we conclude that either
w_{1}\geq\epsilon_{0}/4
or
w_{2}\geq\epsilon_{0}/4
. Suppose without loss of generality that
w_{1}\geq\epsilon_{0}/4
.

Define random variable
\bm{y}_{x}\vcentcolon=\mathds{1}\left[x\in\bm{S}\right]\cdot b_{x}
for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Let
\bm{Y}\vcentcolon=\sum_{x\in A_{1}}\bm{y}_{x}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor},{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\left\lvert{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}(z)-{\mu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}(z)\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}:z\neq\overline{\bm{S}}}\left({\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}(z)-{\mu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}(z)\right)^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\bm{S}}\left(\frac{1}{n}-\mu(x)\right)^{+}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in A_{1}}\mathds{1}\left[x\in\bm{S}\right]\cdot b_{x}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{Y}\,.

Thus our task is to show that
\bm{Y}\geq 2c_{2}\rho^{2}\epsilon
with good probability. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]=\sum_{x\in A_{1}}\mathbb{P}\left[x\in\bm{S}\right]\cdot b_{x}=\rho^{2}w_{1}\geq\rho^{2}\epsilon_{0}/4\,.

The random variables
\bm{y}_{x}
for
x\in A_{1}
are mutually independent and bounded in
[0,b_{x}]
, so Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}\leq\rho^{2}\epsilon_{0}/8\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{Y}\right]-\rho^{2}\epsilon_{0}/8\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\rho^{4}\epsilon_{0}^{2}/32}{\sum_{x\in A_{1}}b_{x}^{2}}}\,.

Since
\sum_{x\in A_{1}}b_{x}=w_{1}
and each
b_{x}
lies within
[0,1/n]
, it holds that

\sum_{x\in A_{1}}b_{x}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{w_{1}}{1/n}=w_{1}/n
(which would be
attained if there were
n\cdot w_{1}
values
x
with
b_{x}=1/n
). Also noticing that
w_{1}\leq\epsilon_{0}
and recalling that
\epsilon_{0}\geq\epsilon
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{Y}\leq\rho^{2}\epsilon_{0}/8\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\rho^{4}\epsilon_{0}^{2}/32}{w_{1}/n}}\leq e^{-\rho^{4}\epsilon_{0}n/32}\leq e^{-L^{4}(n\epsilon)^{-1}\cdot\epsilon n/32}=e^{-L^{4}/32}\leq 1/100

for sufficiently large
L
. Thus
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor},{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor})\geq\rho^{2}\epsilon/16
except with probability at most
1/100
.
Hence the second step, with constant
c_{2}=1/16
, rejects with probability at least
98/100
.

Second step (sample complexity).

Finally, we show that the second step satisfies the claimed sample complexity. The
instance-optimal identity tester of
[
VV17
]
requires at most
O\left(\max\left\{\frac{1}{\delta},\frac{\|p^{-\max}\|_{2/3}}{\delta^{2}}\right\}\right)
samples, where
p

is the known target distribution,
p^{-\max}
is the vector obtained by removing a largest entry
from
p
, and
\delta
is the distance parameter. Here we have
p={\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}

and
\delta=c_{2}\rho^{2}\epsilon
. Since removing a non-largest entry as opposed to a largest
entry from
{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}
can only overestimate the sample complexity, we may
upper bound
\|{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}^{-\max}\|_{2/3}
by removing the entry corresponding
to
\overline{\bm{S}}
, so that only the singletons remain:

\|{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}^{-\max}\|_{2/3}\leq\left(|\bm{S}|\cdot(1/n)^{2/3}\right)^{3/2}\,.

We have shown that
\bm{S}\leq 3\rho^{2}n
except with probability at most
2/100
, in which
case the algorithm rejects, so
all we need to show is that the sample complexity is as claimed when
\bm{S}\leq 3\rho^{2}n
. In
this case,

\|{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}^{-\max}\|_{2/3}\leq\left(3\rho^{2}\cdot n^{1/3}\right)^{3/2}=O\left(\rho^{3}\sqrt{n}\right)

and thus

\frac{\|{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}^{-\max}\|_{2/3}}{\delta^{2}}\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho\epsilon^{2}}\right)\,.

This term dominates the expression
\max\left\{\frac{1}{\delta},\frac{\|{\nu}_{\lfloor{\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}}\rfloor}^{-\max}\|_{2/3}}{\delta^{2}}\right\}
as long as

\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\epsilon}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\rho\epsilon^{2}}\iff\rho\geq\frac{\epsilon}{n^{1/2}}\impliedby\frac{L}{n^{1/4}\epsilon^{1/4}}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{n^{1/2}}\iff Ln^{1/4}\geq\epsilon^{5/4}\,,

which holds since the RHS is at most
1
, concluding the proof.
∎
