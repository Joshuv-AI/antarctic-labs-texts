Moderate deviations for functionals over infinitely many Rademacher random variables
Marius Butzek, Peter Eichelsbacher, Benedikt Rednoß

Abstract
. In this paper, moderate deviations for normal approximation of functionals over infinitely many Rademacher random variables are derived. They are based on a bound for the Kolmogorov distance between a general Rademacher functional and a Gaussian random variable, continued by an intensive study of the behavior of operators from the Malliavin–Stein method along with the moment generating function of the mentioned functional. As applications, subgraph counting in the Erdős–Rényi random graph and infinite weighted 2-runs are studied.



1.
Introduction and applications

The theory of moderate deviations goes back to H. Cramér in 1938: Knowing that for an independently identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequence
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=0,\mathbb{E}(X_{k}^{2})=1,W_{n}:=n^{-1/2}(X_{1}+...+X_{n})
and
\Phi
the standard normal distribution function, the statement

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(W_{n}>x)}{1-\Phi(x)}\longrightarrow 1\quad\text{for}\quad x=O(1)

is valid, he was asking what happens if
x
depends on
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x\rightarrow\infty
for
n\rightarrow\infty
? Can we find an interval such that (
1.1
) holds for
0\leq x\leq I(n),I(n)\rightarrow\infty
? The answer was given by himself: Under the assumption
\mathbb{E}[e^{t_{0}\left|X_{1}\right|}]\leq c<\infty
for some
t_{0}>0
and a constant
c
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(W_{n}>x)}{1-\Phi(x)}\leq 1+A\,n^{-1/2}(1+x^{3})\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq x\leq a\,n^{1/6},

and
A
and
a
are positive constants depending only on
t_{0}
and
c
. The result is optimal (see e.g.
[
6
]
and
[
20
]
). Reminiscent of (
1.2
) for a sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables, such that
Y_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}Y
, the moderate deviation of Cramér-type is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}>x)}{\mathbb{P}(Y>x)}=1+\text{error term}\rightarrow 1

with range
0\leq x\leq a_{n}
, where
a_{n}\rightarrow\infty
for
n\rightarrow\infty
.

In
[
29
]
Zhang was able to prove Cramér-type moderate deviations for unbounded exchangeable pairs. He developed them by stopping the proof of the corresponding Berry–Esseen-type inequalities, he had obtained before by Stein’s method, at a certain point and continuing differently. Stein’s method
is a powerful tool by itself to derive upper bounds for differences of probability distributions, originally for the normal distribution and later extended to other distributions. Zhang rearranged the fragments of the so called Stein-equation and the bound of its solution, a technique that was already seen in
[
4
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
23
]
and
[
27
]
.

Our ambition is to prove a similar result for functionals over infinitely many independent Rademacher random variables taking values +1 and
-1
only. This type of result intersects with
[
11
]
, where the authors obtain Cramér-type moderate deviations via
p
-Wasserstein bounds, and we will refer to that. For Rademacher functionals a Kolmogorov bound in the context of normal approximation was shown recently in
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
such that the bounding terms can be expressed in terms of operators of the so called Malliavin–Stein method. Normal approximation of functionals over infinitely many Rademacher random variables was derived
already in
[
18
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
7
]
. Theorem 3.1 in
[
9
]
will be our starting point.

1.1.
Application to infinite weighted 2-runs

To begin with, we introduce some of the possible applications for our theorem. In what follows, we use the usual big-O notation
O(.)
with the meaning that the implicit constant does not depend on the parameters in brackets. For a sequence
a=(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
p>0
we write
\left\|a\right\|_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}:=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|a_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
.

Due to their simple dependence structure, runs, and more generally weighted or incomplete
U
-statistics, lend themselves to normal approximations,
see
[
25
]
, where an exchangeable pair coupling is employed for a normal approximation. In
[
25
]
the authors studied even degenerate weighted

U
-statistics, where either weights are considered which ensures a weak dependence or kernel functions are considered which depend on the sample size
n
in a specific way. See also
[
19
]
, where subgraph counts in random graphs are considered, see Subsection 1.2. Here we consider infinite weighted 2-runs, where random variables are possibly depending on the whole infinite sequence of i.i.d. Rademacher random variables.

Let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a double-sided sequence of i.i.d. Rademacher random variables such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)=\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
and let for each
n\in\mathbb{N},(a^{(n)}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a double-sided summable sequence of real numbers. Usually 2-runs are definded with a square-summable sequence but this will be not enough.

The sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of standardized infinite weighted 2-runs is then defined as

\displaystyle F_{n}:=\frac{G_{n}-\mathbb{E}[G_{n}]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(G_{n})}},\quad G_{n}:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{(n)}_{i}\xi_{i}\xi_{i+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\xi_{i}:=(X_{i}+1)/2
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. More generally one can consider an infinite weighted
d
-run defined by

\displaystyle G_{n}(d):=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{(n)}_{i}\xi_{i}\cdots\xi_{i+d-1},

which is a weighted degenerate
U
-statistic of degree
d
.
However, since the analysis for any
d
is of the cost of a quite cumbersome notation, we will focus on the case where
d=2
(2-runs).

For recent results on 2-runs combined with Malliavin–Stein method see
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
18
]
. Our moderate deviation is given as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Recall the definition of
F_{n}
from above. Then

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(F_{n}>z)}{1-\Phi(z)}=1+O(1)(1+z^{2})\gamma_{n}(z),

for
0\leq z\leq\min\{C_{n}^{-1/3},C_{n}^{-2},\operatorname{Var}(G_{n})^{1/2}\}
such that
(1+z^{2})\gamma_{n}(z)\leq 1
, where O(1) is bounded by a constant only depending on the coefficient sequence
(a^{(n)}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(z)

\displaystyle:=e^{O(1)z(\operatorname{Var}(G_{n}))^{-1/2}}\!\left((1+z^{1/2}+z)C_{n}\right),\quad C_{n}:=\frac{\left\|a^{(n)}\right\|_{l^{4}(\mathbb{Z})}^{2}}{\operatorname{Var}(G_{n})}.

Remark 1.2
.

The constant
C_{n}
has an important meaning. It is the order of the corresponding Berry-Esseen bound of the Kolmogorov distance
\sup_{z\in\mathbb R}|\mathbb{P}(F_{n}\leq z)-\Phi(z)|
between the distribution function of
F_{n}
and the standard normal distribution function in
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
. Depending on the coefficient sequence,
C_{n}
can behave differently: By (
4.1
) and (
4.20
)
C_{n}
is in general bounded by a constant, but it can be a constant itself (see e.g.
a^{(n)}_{i}=\frac{1}{i^{2}}
). So, to make (
1.3
) tend to 0 and the range increase in
n
, the condition
C_{n}\rightarrow 0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
is sufficient. We give now examples, where this is the case and where the resulting rate is optimal.

Example 1.3
.

We consider
a^{(n)}_{i}=\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{\left|i\right|\leq n\right\}}\,\forall\,i\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is obviously a summable sequence. Then
\left\|a^{(n)}\right\|_{l^{4}(\mathbb{Z})}^{2}=O\!\left(n^{1/2}\right),\operatorname{Var}(G_{n})=O\!\left(n\right)
and
C_{n}=O\!\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
. The moderate deviation we get is

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(F_{n}>z)}{1-\Phi(z)}=1+O(1)(1+z^{2})\gamma_{n}(z),

for
0\leq z\leq n^{1/6}
such that
(1+z^{2})\gamma_{n}(z)\leq 1
, where

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(z)

\displaystyle:=e^{O(1)zn^{-1/2}}\!\left((1+z^{1/2}+z)n^{-1/2}\right).

In order to discuss the quality of this result, we use a lower bound of the Kolmogorov distance known from
[
10
, Theorem 1(c)]
, which got later refined by
[
24
, Corollary 3.12]
.
Since
G_{n}
is almost surely an integer between
-n
and
n
, said results imply that the Kolmogorov distance for normal approximation of
F_{n}
is bounded from below by
c_{0}\cdot(\operatorname{Var}(G_{n}))^{-\frac{1}{2}}

for some constant
c_{0}>0
.
As
\operatorname{Var}(G_{n})
is of order
n
, we conclude that
C_{n}
being of order
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is optimal.

Example 1.4
.

We generalize the previous example to
a^{(n)}_{i}=n^{-\beta}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{\left|i\right|\leq n^{\alpha}\right\}}\,\forall\,i\in\mathbb{Z},\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\beta>0
. Then
\left\|a^{(n)}\right\|_{l^{4}(\mathbb{Z})}^{2}=O\!\left(n^{(\alpha-4\beta)/2}\right),\operatorname{Var}(G_{n})=O\!\left(n^{\alpha-2\beta}\right)
and
C_{n}=O\!\left(n^{-\alpha/2}\right)
. If we choose
\beta=0
and
\alpha\geq 1
the moderate deviation we get is of the same form as (
1.4
) with range
0\leq z\leq n^{\alpha/6}
respectively
0\leq z\leq(\operatorname{Var}(G_{n}))^{\alpha/6}
.
Using the same argumentation as in the previous example, we see that the rate of
C_{n}
is again optimal.

1.2.
Application to subgraph counts in the Erdős–Rényi random graph

As a further application we derive a Cramér-type moderate deviation result for the subgraph counting statistic
in the Erdős–Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
.

Consider a random graph on
n\in\mathbb{N}
vertices.
Each possible edge between two vertices is included with probability
p\in(0,1)

independently of all other edges,
where
p
may depend on
n
even though we will not make this visible in our notation.

Let
{G_{0}}
be a fixed graph with at least one edge.
A subgraph
H\subset G(n,p)
is called a
copy
of
{G_{0}}
in
G(n,p)
if it is isomorphic to
{G_{0}}
.
Note that we are calling two graphs
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})

isomorphic

if there is an edge-preserving bijection
f:V_{1}\rightarrow V_{2}
between their sets of vertices,
such that two vertices
v,w\in V_{1}
are joined by an edge
\{v,w\}\in E_{1}
in
G_{1}
if and only if the vertices
f(v),f(w)\in V_{2}
are joined by an edge
\{f(v),f(w)\}\in E_{2}
in
G_{2}
.

We are interested in the standardized number
W
of copies of
{G_{0}}
in
G(n,p)
.

It is well known under which necessary and sufficient assumption
W
is asymptotically normal.
To state this condition and further results, we will use the notation
q:=1-p
as well as

\displaystyle\Psi_{\min}:=\Psi_{\min}({G_{0}}):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\subset{G_{0}}\\
e_{H}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\{n^{v_{H}}\cdot p^{e_{H}}\},

with
H\subset{G_{0}}
a subgraph of
{G_{0}}
,

v_{H}
the number of vertices of
H
,
and
e_{H}
the number of edges of
H
.
Further,
a_{H}
denotes the number of automorphisms of
H
.
Some very important yet easy to prove bounds for
\Psi_{\min}
are

\displaystyle n^{2}p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}\leq\Psi_{\min}\leq n^{2}p.

Theorem 2 in
[
26
]
states that
W
is asymptotically normal if and only if

\displaystyle q\cdot\Psi_{\min}\xlongrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty.

The best known convergence rate regarding the bounded Wasserstein distance
d_{\mathrm{bWass}}
is presented in Theorem 2 in
[
3
]
:
There is a constant
C_{G_{0}}
only depending on
{G_{0}}
so that

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{bWass}}(W,N)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{G_{0}}}{\sqrt{q\Psi_{\min}}},

where
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is standard normal distributed, and

d_{\mathrm{bWass}}(W,N)=\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\big|\mathbb{E}h(W)-\mathbb{E}h(N)\big|

with
{\mathcal{H}}
being the set of all bounded Lipschitz-functions with
\|h\|_{\infty}+\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
The same bound holds true for the Wasserstein distance, where
{\mathcal{H}}
is the collection of Lipschitz-functions with Lipschitz-constant 1. The result can be shown by only a slight modification of the original proof in
[
3
]
.
For the Kolmogorov distance, the same order of the bound can be obtained. This was a long standing problem that has been lately solved using different approaches by
[
22
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
.

A refined Cramér-type moderate deviation result for the subgraph counting statistic in the Erdős–Rényi random graph is shown in Proposition 2.3 in
[
12
]
:
For any fixed constant
c_{0}>0
there is a constant
C_{c_{0},{G_{0}}}
only depending on
c_{0}
and
{G_{0}}
, so that

\displaystyle\max\bigg\{\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W<-t)}{\Phi(-t)\exp(-\frac{\gamma t^{3}}{6})}-1\bigg|,\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W>t)}{(1-\Phi(t))\exp(\frac{\gamma t^{3}}{6})}-1\bigg|\bigg\}\leq C_{c_{0},{G_{0}}}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\Psi_{\min}}{q\cdot p^{2e_{{G_{0}}}}}\bigg)^{\frac{5}{2}}\cdot\frac{1+t^{2}}{n^{6}}

for

\displaystyle 0\leq t\leq c_{0}\cdot\bigg(\frac{q\cdot p^{2e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}}\bigg)^{\frac{5}{4}}\cdot n^{3},

where
\Phi
is the distribution function of
the standard normal distribution,
and
\gamma=\mathbb{E}[W^{3}]
.

Another Cramér-type moderate deviation result is known from Theorem 3.1 in
[
28
]
:
There is a constant
C_{G_{0}}
only depending on
{G_{0}}
, so that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W>t)}{1-\Phi(t)}-1\bigg|\leq C_{G_{0}}\cdot(1+t^{2})\cdot b_{n}(p,t)

with

\displaystyle b_{n}(p,t):={}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1+t}{\sqrt{\Psi_{\min}}},&0<p<\frac{1}{2},\\[15.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1+\frac{t}{\sqrt{q}}}{n\sqrt{p}},&\frac{1}{2}<p<1,\end{cases}

for all

\displaystyle 0\leq t\leq\frac{q\cdot p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\sqrt{\Psi_{\min}}}\cdot n^{2}

that satisfy

\displaystyle(1+t^{2})b_{n}(p,t)\leq 1.

For any subgraph
H\subset{G_{0}}
with at least one edge but more than two vertices, there is

n^{v_{H}}p^{e_{H}}\geq n^{2}p\cdot\frac{n^{v_{H}-2}}{2^{e_{H}-1}}\geq n^{2}p\cdot\frac{n}{2^{e_{{G_{0}}}-1}}

in case of
\frac{1}{2}<p<1
.
Hence, we know that
\Psi_{\min}=n^{2}p

for
\frac{1}{2}<p<1
and
n\geq 2^{e_{{G_{0}}}-1}
.
We further know that

\frac{1+t}{\sqrt{\Psi_{\min}}}\leq\frac{1+\frac{t}{\sqrt{q}}}{\sqrt{q\Psi_{\min}}}\leq 2\cdot\frac{1+t}{\sqrt{\Psi_{\min}}}

in case of
0<p<\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore, as long as
n\geq 2^{e_{{G_{0}}}-1}
,
(
1.5
) can be rewritten
without loss of sharpness in the resulting rate:

(1.6)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W>t)}{1-\Phi(t)}-1\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot C_{G_{0}}\cdot\frac{(1+t^{2})\big(1+\frac{t}{\sqrt{q}}\big)}{\sqrt{q\Psi_{\min}}}.

Further, Theorem 5.2 in
[
11
]
presents a moderate deviation result for bounded and locally dependent random variables.
Application to the context of subgraph counting yields
that there exist constants
c_{G_{0}}
and
C_{G_{0}}
so that

(1.7)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W>t)}{1-\Phi(t)}-1\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C_{G_{0}}\cdot(1+t)\cdot(1+|\log(\Delta)|+t^{2})\cdot\Delta

for
0\leq t\leq\Delta^{-\frac{1}{3}}

with
\Delta=\frac{\Psi_{\min}}{n^{3}p^{2e_{{G_{0}}}}q}+\left(\frac{\Psi_{\min}}{n^{3}p^{2e_{{G_{0}}}}q}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{n}\log(n)\leq c_{G_{0}}
.
As one can show that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q\Psi_{\min}}}=o\left(\Delta\right)
, result (
1.6
) from
[
28
]
is stronger than (
1.7
) from
[
11
]
.

With our approach we are able to improve on the result of
[
28
]
.
We will prove the following result.

Theorem 1.5

(Subgraph counts in the Erdős–Rényi random graph — general result)
.

Let
{G_{0}}
be a graph with at least one edge.
Let
W
be the standardized number of copies of
{G_{0}}

in the Erdős–Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
.
And assume that
n\geq 4v_{{G_{0}}}^{2}
.
Then for all
t\geq 0
there is

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W>t)}{1-\Phi(t)}-1\bigg|\leq 50c_{G_{0}}\cdot\exp\Big(c_{G_{0}}\cdot t^{2}\cdot s(t)\Big)\cdot(1+t^{2})\cdot s(t),

with

(1.8)

\displaystyle s(t)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\frac{t}{\min\{\sqrt{\Psi_{\min}},1\}}\bigg)\frac{1}{\sqrt{q\Psi_{\min}}}\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{5Dt}{\sigma}\bigg)

and

(1.9)

\displaystyle c_{G_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{9}{2}+\frac{15}{2}e_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot(v_{{G_{0}}}!)^{4}\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}{a_{{G_{0}}}^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

The result holds for all
t\geq 0
.
Similar to the approach by
[
28
]
,
the result can be simplified
by restricting
t
to be smaller than a suitable bound:

Corollary 1.6

(Subgraph counts in the Erdős–Rényi random graph — bounded domain)
.

Let
{G_{0}}
be a graph with at least one edge.
Let
W
be the standardized number of copies of
{G_{0}}

in the Erdős–Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
.
Let
c_{1},c_{2}>0
be positive numbers.
Assume that

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq 4v_{{G_{0}}}^{2},

\displaystyle 0\leq t

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\cdot\frac{n^{2}\cdot p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{\Psi_{\min}}},

\displaystyle t^{2}\cdot s(t)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2},

with
s(t)
as given in (
1.8
).
Then

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{P}(W>t)}{1-\Phi(t)}-1\bigg|\leq C_{c_{1},c_{2},{G_{0}}}\cdot\frac{1+t^{3}}{\sqrt{q\Psi_{\min}}},

where

\displaystyle C_{c_{1},c_{2},{G_{0}}}

\displaystyle=100(1+c_{1})\cdot c_{G_{0}}\cdot e^{5c_{1}\hat{c}_{G_{0}}+c_{2}c_{G_{0}}},

with
\hat{c}_{G_{0}}=\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{v_{{G_{0}}}!}\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}^{2}\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}{\sqrt{a_{{G_{0}}}}}

and
c_{G_{0}}
as given in (
1.9
).

In case of
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}q>0
,
our result is of the same order as the result of
[
28
]
presented in (
1.6
).
However, in case of
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}q=0
, our result yields the better rate.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we list all the definitions and notions we deal with, in particular the operators from Malliavin calculus. Since this is just a summary, we refer to
[
17
]
for details and to
[
1
]
for further results related to the topic. For the setting of Bernoulli processes see also
[
21
]
.

Throughout the upcoming definitions we will need the following notations: Let
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})
be the space of real square-summable sequences. Moreover, by
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\otimes p}
we mean the
p
th tensor product of
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Relevant subsets are
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\circ p}
, the symmetric functions in
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\otimes p}
, and
l_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\circ p}
, the symmetric functions in
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\otimes p}
which vanish on diagonals.

We start with
X=(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, a sequence of Rademacher random variables, e.g.
\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=1)

\displaystyle=p_{k}\in(0,1),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=-1)

\displaystyle=q_{k}=1-p_{k},

and, if needed, the standardized random variable

\displaystyle Y_{k}=\frac{X_{k}-p_{k}+q_{k}}{2\sqrt{q_{k}p_{k}}}.

We are interested in random variables
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma(X),\mathbb{P})
and we will use that in our setting
F
can be written as

\displaystyle F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_{n}(f_{n}),

where

\displaystyle J_{n}(f)=\sum_{(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\Delta^{n}}f(i_{1},...,i_{n})Y_{i_{1}}Y_{i_{2}}...Y_{i_{n}}

with
f\in l_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\circ n}
and
\Delta_{n}:=\{(i_{1},...,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:i_{j}\neq i_{k}\,\text{for}\,j\neq k\}
.
J_{n}(f)
is called the
n
th discrete multiple integral.
For
F=f(X)=f(X_{1},X_{2},...)\in L^{1}(\Omega,\sigma(X),\mathbb{P})
we define the discrete gradient
D_{k}F
of
F
at
k
th coordinate:

\displaystyle D_{k}F

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}(F_{k}^{+}-F_{k}^{-}),

\displaystyle DF

\displaystyle:=(D_{1}F,D_{2}F,...),

where
F_{k}^{+}:=f(X_{1},...,X_{k-1},+1,X_{k+1},...)
and
F_{k}^{-}:=f(X_{1},...,X_{k-1},-1,X_{k+1},...),\,k\in\mathbb{N}
. For the main result we will consider

\displaystyle F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}:=\{F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma(X),\mathbb{P})|\,\mathbb{E}[\left\|DF\right\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{N})}^{2}]<\infty\},

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\|DF\right\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{N})}^{2}]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(D_{k}F)^{2}\right].

Next we define the divergence operator
\delta
, also known as Skorokhod operator, and its domain
Dom(\delta)
. For
u:=(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in(L^{2}(\Omega)){N}
with

\displaystyle u_{k}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_{n-1}(f_{n}(\cdot,k)),

where
f_{n}\in l_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\circ n-1}\otimes l^{2}(\mathbb{N})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that
u\in Dom(\delta)
, if

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n!\left\|\widetilde{f_{n}}\mathbbl{1}_{\Delta_{n}}\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\otimes n}}<\infty.

By
\tilde{f}(k_{1},...,k_{n}):=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}f(k_{\sigma(1)},...,k_{\sigma(n)})
we mean the canonical symmetrization of a function
f
in n variables such that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is the symmetric group on
\{1,...,n\}
. Then, for
u\in Dom(\delta)
, the operator
\delta
is given by

\displaystyle\delta(u):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_{n}\!\left(\widetilde{f_{n}}\mathbbl{1}_{\Delta_{n}}\right).

Another way to characterize
\delta
is by the duality

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle DF,u\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[F\delta(u)],\quad F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2},u\in Dom(\delta),

such that we can identify
\delta
as the adjoint operator of
D
. Furthermore we can rewrite its domain to

\displaystyle Dom(\delta)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega,l^{2}(\mathbb{N}))\bigg|\exists\,C_{u}>0\,\forall\,F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}:\left|\mathbb{E}[\langle DF,u\rangle]\right|\leq C_{u}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[F^{2}]}\right\}.

For

\displaystyle F\in Dom(L)=\left\{F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_{n}(f_{n})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma(X),\mathbb{P})\bigg|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2}n!\left\|f_{n}\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}(\mathbb{N})^{\otimes n}}<\infty\right\}

we define by

\displaystyle LF:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}-nJ_{n}(f_{n}),\quad L^{-1}F:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{n}J_{n}(f_{n})

the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator
L
and the pseudo-inverse Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator
L^{-1}
. You can show that
F\in Dom(L)
is equivalent to
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
DF\in Dom(\delta)
; in this case, it holds that

(2.2)

\displaystyle L=-\delta D.

At last we recall the main ideas of Stein’s method for normal approximation, starting with the important characterisation

(2.3)

\displaystyle Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(Z)-Zf(Z)]=0

for all continuous differentiable
f
such that the appearing expectations exist. So if a random variable is in some sense close to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, it is likely that the expectation in (
2.3
) is close to 0. This motivates the Stein-equation, written in the case of Kolmogorov distance, namely

\displaystyle f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-Ff_{z}(F)=\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{F\leq z\right\}}-\Phi(z),

respectively

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{z}^{\prime}(F)-Ff_{z}(F)]=\mathbb{P}(F\leq z)-\Phi(z).

The solution to this equation is given, see Lemma 2.1 in
[
2
]
, by

(2.4)

\displaystyle f_{z}(w)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Phi(w)(1-\Phi(z))}{p(w)}&w\leq z,\\
\frac{\Phi(z)(1-\Phi(w))}{p(w)}&w>z,\end{cases}

where
p(w)=e^{-w^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}
is the density of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Later on we will use the following bounds, see Lemma 2.3 in
[
5
]
:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1-\Phi(w)}{p(w)}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{w},\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\right\},\quad w>0,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left|wf_{z}(w)\right|\leq 1,\quad w\in\mathbb{R},

and

(2.7)

\displaystyle\left|wf_{z}(w)\right|\leq 1-\Phi(z),\quad w<0\leq z.

Note that (
2.7
) follows with (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) by writing

\displaystyle\left|wf_{z}(w)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|w\right|\sqrt{2\pi}e^{w^{2}/2}\Phi(w)(1-\Phi(z))

\displaystyle=(1-\Phi(z))(1-\Phi(\left|w\right|))\sqrt{2\pi}\left|w\right|e^{\left|w\right|^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\Phi(z)),

where we also used the symmetry of
\Phi
. We will need this more precise bound of
\left|wf_{z}(w)\right|
for the main result in section 3, where we distinguish different cases for
w\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.
Main Results

Now we present the main theorem of our paper.

Theorem 3.1

(Moderate deviations for Rademacher functionals)
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
with
\mathbb{E}[F]=0,\operatorname{Var}(F)=1
, and

\displaystyle Ff_{z}(F)+\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{F>z\right\}}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\in Dom(\delta).

Assume that there exists a constant
A>0
and increasing functions
\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(t)
such that
e^{tF}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and

(A1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|e^{tF}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}(t)\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right],

(A2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|e^{tF}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2}(t)\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right],

for all
0\leq t\leq A
.
For
d_{0}\geq 0
, let

\displaystyle A_{0}(d_{0}):=\max\left\{0\leq t\leq A:\frac{t^{2}}{2}\!\left(\gamma_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}(t)\right)\leq d_{0}\right\}.

Then, for any
d_{0}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}(F>z)}{1-\Phi(z)}-1\right|\leq 25e^{d_{0}}(1+z^{2})\!\left(\gamma_{1}(z)+\gamma_{2}(z)\right)

provided that
0\leq z\leq A_{0}(d_{0})
.

In consequence, the following result is achieved.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under the assumptions from Theorem
3.1
, there is

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}(F>z)}{1-\Phi(z)}-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq 25e^{\frac{z^{2}}{2}\!\left(\gamma_{1}(z)+\gamma_{2}(z)\right)}(1+z^{2})\!\left(\gamma_{1}(z)+\gamma_{2}(z)\right)

for all
0\leq z\leq A
.

As a first application we treat the i.i.d.-case: For our sequence
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of Rademacher random variables we consider the standardized
n
th partial sum

\displaystyle F:=F_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}-(2p-1)}{2\sqrt{pq}}.

The classical result can be received:

Corollary 3.3
.

Recall the definition of
F_{n}
from above. Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(F_{n}>z)}{1-\Phi(z)}=1+O(1)(1+z^{2})\gamma_{n}(z),

for
0\leq z\leq n^{1/6}
such that
(1+z^{2})\gamma_{n}(z)\leq 1
, where O(1) is bounded by a constant and

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(z)

\displaystyle:=e^{O(1)zn^{-1/2}}\frac{(1+z)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Remark 3.4
.

We obtain the optimal range
0\leq z\leq n^{1/6}
from (
1.2
), but there is no
\log(n)
in our error term compared to
[
11
, Corollary 2.2]
. The proof of Corollary
3.3
is basically a shorter version of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For the proof of Theorem
3.1
we will need two auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 3.5

(Bound for the moment generating function)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, for
0\leq t\leq A
, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right]\leq\exp\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{2}(1+\gamma_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}(t))\right\}.

Then, for
0\leq t\leq A_{0}(d_{0})
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right]\leq e^{d_{0}}e^{t^{2}/2}.

Proof.

Let
h(t):=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right]
. We recall that
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right]<\infty
is implied by
e^{tF}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
for
0\leq t\leq A
, and so, by the continuity of the exponential funtion, we have
h^{\prime}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[Fe^{tF}\right]
. It follows with (
2.2
) and (
2.1
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Fe^{tF}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(LL^{-1}F)e^{tF}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(-\delta DL^{-1}F)e^{tF}\right]

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle De^{tF},-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right].

Now we consider the
k
-th component of
De^{tF}
, which gives us

\displaystyle D_{k}e^{tF}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\left[e^{tF_{k}^{+}}-e^{tF_{k}^{-}}\right]

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{F_{k}^{-}}^{F_{k}^{+}}e^{tu}du

\displaystyle=t\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{F_{k}^{-}}^{F_{k}^{+}}\left[e^{tu}-e^{tF}\right]du+te^{tF}D_{k}F

\displaystyle=:tR_{k}+te^{tF}D_{k}F.

If we define
R:=(R_{1},R_{2},\ldots)
, we can go on from (
3
) by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Fe^{tF}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle tR,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle te^{tF}DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right]

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq t\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right]+t\mathbb{E}\left|\langle R,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|+t\mathbb{E}\left[\left|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|e^{tF}\right].

Without loss of generality
F_{k}^{-}\leq F\leq F_{k}^{+}
since for the other case we just have to change the sign. Then we can bound
R_{k}
as follows.

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{F_{k}^{-}}^{F_{k}^{+}}\left[e^{tu}-e^{tF}\right]du

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\int_{F_{k}^{-}}^{F_{k}^{+}}\left[e^{tF_{k}^{+}}-e^{tF_{k}^{-}}\right]du

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}\left[e^{tF_{k}^{+}}-e^{tF_{k}^{-}}\right]\int_{F_{k}^{-}}^{F_{k}^{+}}du

\displaystyle=D_{k}e^{tF}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}D_{k}F

and by combining both cases

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left|R_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{k}q_{k}}}D_{k}e^{tF}\cdot D_{k}F.

By condition (
A2
) and (
3.6
) we get

\displaystyle t\mathbb{E}\left|\langle R,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq t\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\left|R\right|,\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq t\mathbb{E}\left[\langle De^{tF},\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq t\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|e^{tF}\right]

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq t\gamma_{2}(t)\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right].

By condition (
A1
), for
0\leq t\leq A
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle t\mathbb{E}\left[\left|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|e^{tF}\right]\leq t\gamma_{1}(t)\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tF}\right].

Combining (
3
), (
3
) and (
3.8
), we have for
0\leq t\leq A
,

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Fe^{tF}\right]

\displaystyle\leq th(t)+\{t(\gamma_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}(t))\}h(t)

\displaystyle=\{1+\gamma_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}(t)\}\,t\,h(t).

Having in mind that
h(0)=1
, and
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are increasing, we complete the proof of (
3.2
) by solving the foregoing differential inequality:

\displaystyle\log(h(t))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{h^{\prime}(s)}{h(s)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}(1+\gamma_{1}(s)+\gamma_{2}(s))sds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}(1+\gamma_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}(t))sds

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}}{2}\!\left(1+\gamma_{1}(t)+\gamma_{2}(t)\right),

now we apply
\exp(.)
on both sides. At last, (
3.3
) follows immediately from (
3.2
) by definition of
A_{0}(d_{0})
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, we have for
0\leq z\leq A_{0}(d_{0})
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{0\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]\leq 6e^{d_{0}}(1+z^{2})\gamma_{1}(z)

and

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{0\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]\leq 6e^{d_{0}}(1+z^{2})\gamma_{2}(z).

Proof.

Same as
[
29
]
we apply the idea in
[
4
, Lemma 5.2]
for this proof. For
a\in\mathbb{R}
, denote
[a]=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\leq a\}
. Next, we define
H:=1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{0\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{[z]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{j-1\leq F\leq j\right\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{[z]\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right].

For the first term we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{[z]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{j-1\leq F\leq j\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{[z]}je^{(j-1)^{2}/2-j(j-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|e^{jF}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{j-1\leq F\leq j\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{j=1}^{[z]}je^{-j^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|e^{jF}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{j-1\leq F\leq j\right\}}\right]

and similarly, for the second

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{[z]\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq ze^{[z]^{2}/2-[z]z}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|e^{zF}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{[z]\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3ze^{-z^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|e^{zF}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{[z]\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right].

For both terms, we used similar manipulations, namely for
j-1\leq F\leq j
:

•

e^{(j-1)^{2}/2-j(j-1)}=e^{j^{2}/2-j+1/2-j^{2}+j}=e^{-j^{2}/2}e^{1/2}\leq 3e^{-j^{2}/2}
.

•

e^{(j-F)^{2}/2}\leq e^{1/2}\Leftrightarrow e^{F^{2}/2}\leq e^{-j^{2}/2+jF+1/2}\Leftrightarrow e^{F^{2}/2}\leq e^{(j-1)^{2}/2-j(j-1)}e^{jF}
.

And for
[z]\leq F\leq z
:

•

e^{[z]^{2}/2-[z]z}=e^{[z]^{2}/2-[z]z+z^{2}/2-z^{2}/2}=e^{(z-[z])^{2}/2}e^{-z^{2}/2}\leq 3e^{-z^{2}/2}
.

•

e^{(z-F)^{2}/2}\leq e^{(z-[z])^{2}/2}\Leftrightarrow e^{F^{2}/2}\leq e^{(z-[z])^{2}/2+zF-z^{2}/2}\Leftrightarrow e^{F^{2}/2}\leq e^{[z]^{2}/2-[z]z}e^{zF}
.

By condition (
A1
) and (
3.3
), and recalling that
\gamma_{1}
is increasing, for any
0\leq x\leq z\leq A_{0}(d_{0})

\displaystyle e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right|e^{xF}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}(x)\mathbb{E}[e^{xF-x^{2}/2}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{d_{0}}\gamma_{1}(x)\leq e^{d_{0}}\gamma_{1}(z).

By the foregoing inequalities,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{0\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{d_{0}}\gamma_{1}(z)\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{[z]}j+z\right)\leq 6e^{d_{0}}(1+z^{2})\gamma_{1}(z).

The other statement of the lemma can be shown analogously.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We note at first that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(F>z)-(1-\Phi(z))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|1-\mathbb{P}(F\leq z)-(1-\Phi(z))\right|=\left|\Phi(z)-\mathbb{P}(F\leq z)\right|.

By Stein’s method and the proof of

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left|f_{z}^{\prime}(F)\!\left(1-\langle DF,-DL^{-1}F\rangle\right)\right|,

For the upcoming estimation we can split
J_{2}
into two terms, namely

\displaystyle\left|J_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|\left|Ff_{z}(F)+\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{F>z\right\}}\right|\right]\leq J_{21}+J_{22}

\displaystyle J_{21}

\displaystyle J_{22}

Using the same arguments as in the proof of
[

, in particular (
2.4
), (

) and (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle J_{21}\leq J_{23}+J_{24}+J_{25}

\displaystyle J_{24}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot(1-\Phi(z))\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{0\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right],

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{F>z\right\}}\right]=J_{22}.

Thus,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\left|J_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq J_{23}+J_{24}+2\cdot J_{25}.

For

, by condition (
A2
) with

and noting that
\gamma_{2}
is increasing,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{F<0\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2}(0)\leq e^{d_{0}}\gamma_{2}(z).

For
J_{24}
, by Lemma
3.6
, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|Fe^{F^{2}/2}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{0\leq F\leq z\right\}}\right]\leq 6e^{d_{0}}(1+z^{2})\gamma_{2}(z).

For

, by condition (
A2
) and (
3.3
), for

,

\displaystyle J_{25}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\delta\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}DF\left|DL^{-1}F\right|\right)\right|\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{F>z\right\}}e^{zF}e^{-zF}\right]

.

5.
Proofs II: Subgraph counts in the Erdős–Rényi random graph

There are
\binom{n}{2}
possible edges in the Erdős-Rényi random graph.
Hence, we can describe it by using as many Rademacher random variables:
Let
E
be the set of all possible edges of
G(n,p)

and let
(X_{k})_{k\in E}
be a set of independent Rademacher random variables,
in which
X_{k}=1
indicates the presence of edge
k
in
G(n,p)
.
Thus,
\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=1)=p
for all
k\in E
.
In our calculations, we will make use of the following rescaled versions of
(X_{k})_{k\in E}
:
For
k\in E
we define

B_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{k}+1)
, which is Bernoulli(
p
)-distributed,
and
Y_{k}=(pq)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(B_{k}-p)
, which is standardized.
Further, for any subset
A\subset E
,
we shorten the notation for the product of
(B_{k})_{k\in A}

or
(Y_{k})_{k\in A}

by defining
B_{A}:=\prod_{k\in A}B_{k}

and
Y_{A}:=\prod_{k\in A}Y_{k}
,
where
B_{\emptyset}=Y_{\emptyset}=1
by convention.
For any integrable random variable
Z
, let
Z^{c}:=Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]
denote the centered version of
Z
.

Let
{G_{0}}
be a graph with at least one edge.
Neither the standardized number of copies of
{G_{0}}
in
G(n,p)

nor
\Psi_{\min}
do depend on the number of isolated vertices of
{G_{0}}
,
see Lemma 4.3 in
[
8
]
.
Hence, without loss of generality, we may assume that
{G_{0}}
does not have isolated vertices.
This way, every copy of
{G_{0}}
in
G(n,p)
can be identified by its set of edges.
Therefore, it will be useful to simplify our notation:

For any set of edges
\Gamma\subset E

let
\operatorname{graph}(\Gamma)
denote the graph consisting of all edges given by
\Gamma
and all necessary vertices.
Let
v_{\Gamma}:=v_{\operatorname{graph}(\Gamma)}
,
e_{\Gamma}:=e_{\operatorname{graph}(\Gamma)}
,
and
a_{\Gamma}:=a_{\operatorname{graph}(\Gamma)}
.
We will call
\Gamma
to be a
copy
of
{G_{0}}
if
\operatorname{graph}(\Gamma)
is a copy of
{G_{0}}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}:=\{\Gamma\subset E\,|\,\Gamma
is a copy of
{G_{0}}\}
be the set of all possible copies of
{G_{0}}
in
G(n,p)
,
and let
\mathcal{M}_{k}:=\{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\,|\,k\in\Gamma\}
be the set of all possible copies that contain a specific edge
k\in E
.
Further, for any non-empty
A\subset E
let

\mathcal{M}_{A}:=\bigcup_{k\in A}\mathcal{M}_{k}

be the set of all possible copies that contain at least one of the edges given by
A
.
This last definition will be mainly used in case of
A=\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}
being a copy of
{G_{0}}
.
In this case,
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
contains all possible copies of
{G_{0}}
that have at least one edge in common with
\Gamma
.
We will call
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
to be the
neighborhood
of
\Gamma
, and the elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
to be the
neighbors
of
\Gamma
.
Due to symmetry, the cardinality of the neighborhood
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
does not depend on the choice of
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}
.
We will denote this cardinality by
D
.

The number of copies of
{G_{0}}
in
G(n,p)
is given by

\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}}B_{\Gamma}
.
Its standardization is

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}}(B_{\Gamma}-\mathbb{E}[B_{\Gamma}])=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}}B_{\Gamma}^{c},

where
\sigma^{2}:=\operatorname{Var}(\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}}B_{\Gamma})
.
From Lemma 4.2 in
[
8
]
we know that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q}{2\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}!\cdot a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{n^{2v_{{G_{0}}}}p^{2e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}}

for
n\geq 4v_{{G_{0}}}^{2}
.

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
and Corollary
1.6
,
which have been presented in our introductory section.
These proofs are postponed to the end of this section.
Before, we present several lemmas that deal with some basic yet very important properties of our random variables.
These results will be useful repeatedly in the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We start by exploring the behavior of our most important operators,
the discrete gradient
D_{k}
with
k\in E

and the pseudo-inverse Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator
L^{-1}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any subset
A\subset E
and any edge
k\in E

there is

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle D_{k}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{pq}\cdot\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{k\in A\right\}}\cdot B_{A\backslash\{k\}},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle-D_{k}L^{-1}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{pq}\cdot\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{k\in A\right\}}\cdot\sum_{\{k\}\subset\alpha\subset A}\frac{p^{|A|-|\alpha|}}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\alpha|-1}}B_{\alpha\backslash\{k\}}.

In particular, all expressions above are non-negative.

Remark 5.2
.

In the application of Lemma
5.1

(ii)
it will be useful to keep in mind that

\displaystyle\sum_{\{k\}\subset\alpha\subset A}\frac{1}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\alpha|-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\subset A\backslash\{k\}}\frac{1}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\alpha|}}=\sum_{i=0}^{|A|-1}\bigg(\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\subset A\backslash\{k\}\\
|\alpha|=i\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\binom{|A|-1}{i}}\bigg)=\sum_{i=0}^{|A|-1}\frac{1}{|A|}=1

for any non-empty subset
A\subset E
and any edge
k\in A
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

First, we note that
D_{k}B_{A}^{c}=D_{k}(B_{A}-\mathbb{E}[B_{A}])=D_{k}B_{A}
.
If
k\not\in A
,
B_{A}
is independent of
X_{k}
so that
D_{k}B_{A}=0
.
If
k\in A
, there is
B_{A}=B_{A\backslash\{k\}}\cdot B_{k}=B_{A\backslash\{k\}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}(X_{k}+1)

so that

D_{k}B_{A}=\sqrt{pq}\cdot(B_{A\backslash\{k\}}\cdot 1-B_{A\backslash\{k\}}\cdot 0)
.
This proves
(i)
.

To prove the second part of our statement,
we need to represent
B_{A}
with regard to the standardized random variables
Y_{\ell}=(pq)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(B_{\ell}-p)
,
\ell\in E
.
By expansion of the product, we see that

\displaystyle B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=B_{A}-\mathbb{E}[B_{A}]=\prod_{\ell\in A}\big((pq)^{\frac{1}{2}}Y_{\ell}+p\big)-p^{|A|}=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subset A}p^{|A|-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}q^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}Y_{\alpha}.

Now we are able to describe how the pseudo-inverse Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator works on this expression.
We get

\displaystyle-L^{-1}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subset A}\frac{p^{|A|-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}q^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}}{|\alpha|}Y_{\alpha}.

Having applied the operator,
we now want to transform the result back to a representation using the Bernoulli variables
(B_{\ell})_{\ell\in E}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle-L^{-1}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subset A}\bigg(\frac{p^{|A|-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}q^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}}{|\alpha|}\cdot\prod_{\ell\in\alpha}\Big((pq)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(B_{\ell}-p)\Big)\bigg)=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\alpha\subset A}\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\subset\alpha}\frac{p^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}}{|\alpha|}(-1)^{|\alpha|-|\hat{\alpha}|}B_{\hat{\alpha}}.

The double sum can be rearranged
so that we can first choose
\hat{\alpha}
as an arbitrary subset of
A
,
and then fill up the rest
\tilde{\alpha}:=\alpha\backslash\hat{\alpha}\subset A\backslash\hat{\alpha}
.
However, using this rearrangement,
we have to explicitly exclude the case in which
\hat{\alpha}=\tilde{\alpha}=\emptyset
.
This leads to

\displaystyle-L^{-1}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\subset A}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\alpha}\subset A\backslash\hat{\alpha}\\
\hat{\alpha}\cup\tilde{\alpha}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{p^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}}{|\hat{\alpha}|+|\tilde{\alpha}|}(-1)^{|\tilde{\alpha}|}B_{\hat{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\tilde{\alpha}\subset A}\frac{p^{|A|}}{|\tilde{\alpha}|}(-1)^{|\tilde{\alpha}|}B_{\emptyset}+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\hat{\alpha}\subset A}\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}\subset A\backslash\hat{\alpha}}\frac{p^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}}{|\hat{\alpha}|+|\tilde{\alpha}|}(-1)^{|\tilde{\alpha}|}B_{\hat{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=p^{|A|}\sum_{i=1}^{|A|}\binom{|A|}{i}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i}+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\hat{\alpha}\subset A}\Bigg(p^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}B_{\hat{\alpha}}\sum_{i=0}^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}\binom{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}{i}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{|\hat{\alpha}|+i}\Bigg).

The first term above is not random but deterministic
and hence not of interest,
as it will disappear after the application of the discrete gradient.
To handle the second term, we use the Beta-function,
which is usually defined via
\operatorname{Beta}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}dt

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
that have a positive real part.
It is well known that in case of
x,y\in\mathbb{N}

there is

\sum_{i=0}^{x-1}\binom{x-1}{i}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{y+i}=\operatorname{Beta}(x,y)=(x+y-1)^{-1}\cdot\binom{x+y-2}{y-1}^{-1}
.
Application of these results on

x=|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|+1
and
y=|\hat{\alpha}|
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}\binom{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}{i}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{|\hat{\alpha}|+i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\hat{\alpha}|-1}},

so that

\displaystyle-L^{-1}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=p^{|A|}\sum_{i=1}^{|A|}\binom{|A|}{i}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i}+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\hat{\alpha}\subset A}\frac{p^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\hat{\alpha}|-1}}B_{\hat{\alpha}}.

When applying the discrete gradient,
the deterministic term cancels itself out,
while the second term can be handled the same way as shown in the first part of this proof.
We arrive at

\displaystyle-D_{k}L^{-1}B_{A}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{pq}\cdot\sum_{\emptyset\neq\hat{\alpha}\subset A}\frac{p^{|A|-|\hat{\alpha}|}}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\hat{\alpha}|-1}}B_{\hat{\alpha}\backslash\{k\}}\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{k\in\hat{\alpha}\right\}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{pq}\cdot\mathbbl{1}_{\left\{k\in A\right\}}\cdot\sum_{\{k\}\subset\alpha\subset A}\frac{p^{|A|-|\alpha|}}{|A|\cdot\binom{|A|-1}{|\alpha|-1}}B_{\alpha\backslash\{k\}}.

This proves the second part of our statement.
∎

Next, we want to improve our understanding of the correlation of our Bernoulli random variables
\{B_{A}\}_{A\subset E}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
let
A_{i}\subset E
be a set of edges.
And let
I\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite index set.
Then the following statements hold:

(
i
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}]\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{2}}]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}\cup A_{2}}],

(
ii
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}^{c}B_{A_{2}}^{c}]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}\cup A_{2}}],

(
iii
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\prod_{i\in I}B_{A_{i}}^{c}\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2^{|I|}\mathbb{E}[B_{\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}}].

Proof.

Obviously,
\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{i}}]=p^{|A_{i}|}
is non-negative for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Further,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}]\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{2}}]

\displaystyle=p^{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}\leq p^{|A_{1}\cup A_{2}|}=\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}\cup A_{2}}].

This proves
(i)
.
To varify
(ii)
, see

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}^{c}B_{A_{2}}^{c}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\big(B_{A_{1}}-\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}]\big)\cdot\big(B_{A_{2}}-\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{2}}]\big)\big]=\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}\cup A_{2}}]-\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}]\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{1}}].

Hence,
(ii)
follows from
(i)
.
Finally, by expansion of the product, we see

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\prod_{i\in I}B_{A_{i}}^{c}\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\hat{I}\subset I}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{i\in\hat{I}}B_{A_{i}}\Big]\cdot\prod_{i\in I\backslash\hat{I}}\mathbb{E}[B_{A_{i}}]\bigg)\leq\sum_{\hat{I}\subset I}\mathbb{E}[B_{\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}}]=2^{|I|}\mathbb{E}[B_{\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}}],

where we again used our result from
(i)
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

We will later have to deal with sums of powers of
p
,
in which the structure of the sum is determined via the neighborhood structure of the copies of
{G_{0}}
.
These sums together with the variance
\sigma^{2}
are the main reason for the appearance of
\Psi_{\min}
in our results,
and will now be examined in detail.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be a natural number,
and let
M=(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{M}
be an ordered set of copies,
in which each copy is a neighbor of at least one of its predecessors,
i.e.
\Gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\Gamma_{i}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{j}}
for all
2\leq i\leq m
.
We then call
M
to be a
set of connected copies
of size
m
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
m
and
\hat{m}
be natural numbers,
and let
\bar{m}=m+\hat{m}
.
Then

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{M:|M|=m\text{\,con.\,cop.}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}

\displaystyle\leq c_{m}\cdot\frac{n^{m\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot p^{m\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}^{m-1}},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\text{\,con.\,cop.}\\
\hat{M}:|\hat{M}|=\hat{m}\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M\cup\hat{M}}\Gamma|}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\bar{m}}\cdot\frac{n^{\bar{m}\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot p^{\bar{m}\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}^{\bar{m}-2}\cdot\min\{\Psi_{\min},1\}},

where the sums run over all sets of connected copies of size
m
or
\hat{m}
, respectively,
and where the constants are given by

c_{k}:=(v_{{G_{0}}}!)^{k-1}\cdot 2^{\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot a_{{G_{0}}}^{-k}

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

This lemma summarizes a counting technique that is commonly used in the field of random subgraph counting,
see e.g. (3) in
[
26
]
, (3.10) in
[
3
]
, and (3.10) in
[
13
]
.
However, in contrast to these references, we formulate the result of this counting strategy as an independent lemma
for an arbitrary number of connected copies of
{G_{0}}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

To prove
(i)
, let us first assume that
m=1
.
In this case, every set of connected copies
M
consists of only one copy
\Gamma_{1}

that can be arbitrarily chosen among all copies in
\mathcal{M}
.
Therefore, we are interested in the size of
\mathcal{M}
:
There are
\binom{n}{v_{{G_{0}}}}
possibilities to choose
v_{{G_{0}}}
vertices.
On every set of
v_{{G_{0}}}
vertices, we can find
\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}
copies of
{G_{0}}
.
So there are

\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\binom{n}{v_{{G_{0}}}}\leq\frac{n^{v_{{G_{0}}}}}{a_{{G_{0}}}}

possible choices for
\Gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=1\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}}}p^{|\Gamma|}\leq\frac{n^{v_{{G_{0}}}}}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot p^{e_{G_{0}}}=c_{1}\cdot\frac{n^{1\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot p^{1\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}^{0}}.

Next, we assume that
(i)
holds true for some fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}

and we want to prove that
(i)
still holds if
m
is increased by
1
.
If
\hat{M}
is a set of connected copies of size
m+1
,
then the first
m
elements of
\hat{M}
form a set of connected copies of size
m
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{M}:|\hat{M}|=m+1\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in\hat{M}}\Gamma|}

\displaystyle=\;\;\;\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}\;\sum_{\mathclap{\Gamma_{m+1}\in\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}}}p^{|\Gamma_{m+1}\backslash\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}.

Given
M
a set of connected copies of size
m
,

\Gamma_{m+1}
has to be a neighbor of at least one
\Gamma\in M
.
In particular, the intersection
\Gamma_{m+1}\cap\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma
may not be empty.
To understand the resulting structure, we fix a non-empty subset

H\subset\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma

and we count the possible choices for
\Gamma_{m+1}
,
whose intersection with
\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma
equals
H
.
Assuming that
H
is small enough that it can be completed to a copy of
{G_{0}}
,
there are
\binom{n-v_{H}}{v_{{G_{0}}}-v_{H}}
possibilities
to complete the
v_{H}
vertices of
H
to a set of
v_{{G_{0}}}
vertices.
On such a set there are
\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}
copies of
{G_{0}}
.
Hence, we can find at most

\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\binom{n-v_{H}}{v_{{G_{0}}}-v_{H}}\leq\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot n^{v_{{G_{0}}}-v_{H}}

possible copies
\Gamma_{m+1}\in\mathcal{M}
with
H=\Gamma_{m+1}\cap\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma
.
Further note that

|\Gamma_{m+1}\backslash\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|=|\Gamma_{m+1}\backslash H|=e_{{G_{0}}}-e_{H}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}\;\sum_{\mathclap{\Gamma_{m+1}\in\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}}}p^{|\Gamma_{m+1}\backslash\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}

\displaystyle\leq\;\;\;\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}\;\sum_{\mathclap{H\underset{{G_{0}}}{\subset}\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}}}v_{{G_{0}}}!\cdot a_{{G_{0}}}^{-1}\cdot n^{v_{{G_{0}}}-v_{H}}\cdot p^{e_{{G_{0}}}-e_{H}},

where
H\underset{{G_{0}}}{\subset}\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
denotes
that
H
is a non-empty subset of
\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}

while additionally being isomorphic to a subset of
{G_{0}}
.
There are less than
2^{m\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}
sets with these properties.
For each of them there is
n^{v_{H}}p^{e_{H}}\geq\Psi_{\min}
,
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}\;\sum_{\mathclap{H\underset{{G_{0}}}{\subset}\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}}}v_{{G_{0}}}!\cdot a_{{G_{0}}}^{-1}\cdot n^{v_{{G_{0}}}-v_{H}}\cdot p^{e_{{G_{0}}}-e_{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\;\;\;\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}\cdot 2^{m\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{n^{v_{{G_{0}}}}p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}}.

As we assumed that
(i)
holds true for
m
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}M:|M|=m\\
\text{\,con.\,cop.}\end{subarray}}}p^{|\bigcup_{\Gamma\in M}\Gamma|}\cdot 2^{m\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{n^{v_{{G_{0}}}}p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{m}\cdot\frac{n^{m\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot p^{m\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}^{m-1}}\cdot 2^{m\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{v_{{G_{0}}}!}{a_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot\frac{n^{v_{{G_{0}}}}p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}}

\displaystyle=c_{m+1}\cdot\frac{n^{(m+1)\cdot v_{{G_{0}}}}\cdot p^{(m+1)\cdot e_{{G_{0}}}}}{\Psi_{\min}^{(m+1)-1}}.

This finishes the proof of
(i)
.
To prove the second part, we want to adapt our strategy from above.
Let
M=(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{M}
be a set of connected copies of size
m
,
and
\hat{M}=(\hat{\Gamma}_{i})_{i=1}^{\hat{m}}\subset\mathcal{M}
be a set of connected copies of size
\hat{m}
.
Further, let

\bar{M}:=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{m},\hat{\Gamma}_{1},\dots,\hat{\Gamma}_{\hat{m}})

be the set created by joining
M
and
\hat{M}
.
If we could be sure that
\bar{M}
is a set of connected copies, we could directly apply
(i)
.
However, we can not generally assume that
\bar{M}
is a set of connected copies.
In fact,
\bar{M}
is a set of connected copies of size
m+\hat{m}

if and only if
\hat{\Gamma}_{1}\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{i}}
.
Hence, we have to modify our strategy from
(i)
only with respect to
\hat{H}=\hat{\Gamma}_{1}\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma_{i}}
,
which can be empty in this case.
Therefore, the lower bound

n^{v_{\hat{H}}}\cdot p^{e_{\hat{H}}}\geq\Psi_{\min}

does not necessarily hold.
We have to take the case
\hat{H}=\emptyset
into account, which results in

n^{v_{\hat{H}}}\cdot p^{e_{\hat{H}}}\geq\min\{\Psi_{\min},1\}
.
This proves the second part of the statement. ∎

Before we turn to the main proofs, we want to exemplify two bounding strategies in connection with the moment generating function.
We will repeatedly use these strategies, later.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\subset\mathcal{M}
be sets of copies of
{G_{0}}
,
and let
F
be a non-negative functional of
\{B_{\Gamma}\}_{\Gamma\in A_{2}}
,
so that
F
is a random variable independent from
\{B_{\Gamma}\}_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\hat{A}_{2}}
,
where
\hat{A}_{2}:=\bigcup_{\Gamma\in A_{2}}\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
is the set of all copies of
{G_{0}}
that depend on at least one element from
A_{2}
,
in particular
A_{2}\subset\hat{A}_{2}
.
Then for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[F\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash A_{1}}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[F]\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}|A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2}|}\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{tW}].

In particular, there is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash A_{1}}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}|A_{1}|}\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{tW}].

Proof.

There is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[F\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash A_{1}}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[F\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\hat{A}_{2}\backslash A_{1}}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash(A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2})}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[F\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\hat{A}_{2}\backslash A_{1}}(1-p^{e_{{G_{0}}}})}\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash(A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2})}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[F]\cdot e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}|\hat{A}_{2}\backslash A_{1}|(1-p^{e_{{G_{0}}}})}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash(A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2})}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big].

Further,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}\backslash(A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2})}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\frac{t}{\sigma}\sum_{\Gamma\in A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2}}B_{\Gamma}^{c}}\cdot e^{tW}\Big]\leq e^{\frac{t}{\sigma}|A_{1}\cup\hat{A}_{2}|p^{e_{{G_{0}}}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{tW}].

This proves the first inequality.
The second inequality follows from the special case of
F=1
with
A_{2}=\emptyset
.
∎

.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

This proof is based on our theoretical results presented in Theorem
3.2
.
Since
W
depends on only finitely many Rademacher random variables
we may apply this theorem as long as we find suitable functions

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.
In the application of this theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}\cdot D_{k}W\cdot|D_{k}L^{-1}W|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}\cdot D_{k}W\cdot(-D_{k}L^{-1}W),

,

.

.

in case of

These considerations allow us to rephrase Theorem
3.1

with the notation introduced in (
5.2
):
To apply this theorem, we need to construct functions
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
so that

(A1’)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\sqrt{pq}\sum_{k\in E}U_{k}^{c}\Big|\cdot e^{tW}\Big]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\sum_{k\in E}Y_{k}U_{k}\Big|\cdot e^{tW}\Big]

For the construction of suitable
\gamma_{1}

\gamma_{2}

.

)
