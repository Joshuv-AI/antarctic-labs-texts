A Solvable Deformation of Quantum Mechanics
Alba GRASSI † and Marcos MARIÑO ‡

Abstract
The conventional Hamiltonian H = p 2 + V N ​ ( x ) H=p^{2}+V_{N}(x) , where the potential V N ​ ( x ) V_{N}(x) is a polynomial of degree N N , has been studied intensively since the birth of quantum mechanics. In some cases, its spectrum can be determined by combining the WKB method with resummation techniques. In this paper we point out that the deformed Hamiltonian H = 2 ​ cosh ⁡ ( p ) + V N ​ ( x ) H=2\cosh(p)+V_{N}(x) is exactly solvable for any potential: a conjectural exact quantization condition, involving well-defined functions, can be written down in closed form, and determines the spectrum of bound states and resonances. In particular, no resummation techniques are needed. This Hamiltonian is obtained by quantizing the Seiberg–Witten curve of 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 Yang–Mills theory, and the exact quantization condition follows from the correspondence between spectral theory and topological strings, after taking a suitable four-dimensional limit. In this formulation, conventional quantum mechanics emerges in a scaling limit near the Argyres–Douglas superconformal point in moduli space. Although our deformed version of quantum mechanics is in many respects similar to the conventional version, it also displays new phenomena, like spontaneous parity symmetry breaking.

Introduction

The search for solvable models in quantum mechanics is as old as quantum mechanics itself. A particularly important family of models which has attracted much attention in the last century is based on Hamiltonians of the form

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{p}^{2}+V_{N}(\mathsf{x}),

(1.1)

where
V_{N}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
N
. When
N=2
(the harmonic oscillator), the spectral problem was solved in the 1920s, but for potentials of higher degree there is no obvious analytic solution. On the contrary, the Hamiltonian (
1.1
) with
N\geq 3
has become the textbook testing ground for approximation techniques, like stationary perturbation theory or the WKB method. Starting in the 1970s, it was realized that these approximation methods can be sometimes upgraded by using resummation techniques, providing in this way a close analogue of exact solutions. In particular, exact or uniform versions of the WKB method lead to quantization conditions for anharmonic oscillators
[
3
,
4
,
10
,

,
61
,
96
,
102
,
103
,
109
,
110
,
111
]
. These conditions require Borel–Écalle resummations of the WKB periods and include non-perturbative corrections to the perturbative quantization condition of Dunham
[
33
]
, which in turn is a generalization to all orders in
\hbar
of the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition.

In some cases, (
1.1
) can be solved with more powerful techniques. For example, when the potential is monic (i.e.,
V_{N}(x)=x^{N}
), the ODE/IM correspondence
[
31
,
32
]
provides exact quantization conditions in
terms of non-linear integral equations of the TBA type. Voros has pointed out that the solution of (
1.1
) arises as the fixed point of a simple iteration mechanism
[
104
,
105
]
, although its convergence is only guaranteed when
V_{N}(x)
is monic
[

]
. More recently, it has been pointed out in
[

]
that WKB periods for arbitrary polynomial potentials can be computed exactly through a TBA system, extending in this way some of the results of
[

,
32
]
. The TBA system of
[

]
was obtained as a limiting case of the one appearing in
[
43
]
, in the context of
{\cal N}=2
supersymmetric gauge theory.

These remarkable developments lead to many questions. For example, one would like to obtain explicit quantization conditions for arbitrary polynomial potentials, and to clarify the relationship between the exact WKB method and methods based on integral equations of the TBA type. Another important question is whether there exist a solvable deformation of the Hamiltonian (
1.1
). Such a deformation is likely to provide new perspectives on the original quantum-mechanical problem. In this paper, we make a step in this direction: we show that the following deformation of (
1.1
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}=2\cosh\mathsf{p}+V_{N}(\mathsf{x}),

(1.2)

is solvable. More precisely, exact quantization conditions determining the spectrum of (
1.2
) can be written down
in closed form
for
any
potential
V_{N}(x)
, in terms of
actual
functions. In particular, no resummation techniques are needed.

Although the physics of this deformed version of quantum mechanics is in many respects similar to the conventional version, there are also some notable differences. We find for example that the deformed version of the symmetric double-well potential displays spontaneous parity symmetry breaking for special values of the parameters. This is of course forbidden in conventional quantum mechanics

How do we recover in our formulation the standard Hamiltonian (

Unfortunately, this is not straightforward, since the AD points are natural boundaries for the convergence of the series obtained with gauge theory instanton calculus. In addition, straightforward iteration procedures to solve the TBA system seem to break down in the vicinity of these points. Our work sheds however a new light on the long-standing problem of finding exact quantization conditions for anharmonic oscillators: this problem can be solved by understanding the scaling regime of the NS free energy near the AD points. In particular, we expect that such a scaling regime of the TBA equations of
[

[

2
The spectral problem

2.1
General aspects

As we mentioned in the Introduction, the spectral problem we will consider arises in the quantization of an algebraic curve. This curve is of the form

\displaystyle\Lambda^{N}\big({\rm e}^{p}+{\rm e}^{-p}\big)+W_{N}(x)=0,

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle W_{N}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}(-1)^{k}x^{N-k}h_{k}.

(2.2)

We will set
h_{1}=0
without loss of generality. The curve (
2.1
) has various incarnations in mathematical physics. It is the spectral curve of the Toda lattice
[
9
]
, and it is the SW curve for
{\cal N}=2
Yang–Mills theory with gauge group
{\rm SU}(N)

[
7
,
68
,
69
,
95
]
. In the context of SW theory, this curve is often written as

\displaystyle y^{2}=W_{N}^{2}(x)-4\Lambda^{2N},

(2.3)

after the change of variables

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{p}={\Lambda^{-N}\over 2}(y-W_{N}(x)).

The possible values of the coefficients
h_{2},\dots,h_{N-1}
form an
(N-1)
-dimensional moduli space
{\cal M}
, which is of paramount importance in SW theory. This moduli space is often parametrized by the variables
\zeta_{I}
,
I=1,\dots,N
, which are defined by

\displaystyle W_{N}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x-\zeta_{I}),

(2.4)

and satisfy the constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1}^{N}\zeta_{I}=0.

(2.5)

The coefficients
h_{k}
are given by the elementary symmetric functions of the moduli
\zeta_{I}
. In terms of Schur polynomials
s_{R}(\zeta_{I})
, labelled by Young tableaux
R
, one has
[
74
]

\displaystyle h_{k}=s_{(1^{k})}(\zeta_{I}),

where
(1^{k})
is the Young tableau with
k
boxes and
k
rows. We note that the permutation group
S_{N}
acts on the moduli
\zeta_{I}
by permutation of their labels. This action leaves the
h_{k}
invariant, therefore the correspondence between the
h_{k}
and the
\zeta_{I}
is multivalued: two points
\zeta_{I}
,
\zeta^{\prime}_{I}
related by a permutation correspond to the same point in moduli space. We will usually denote

\displaystyle h=-h_{2}.

We will also use sometimes the terminology of integrable systems and call the
h_{k}
Hamiltonians, since they correspond to the Hamiltonians of the Toda lattice.

The quantization of (
2.1
) is done by promoting
\mathsf{x}
,
\mathsf{p}
to Heisenberg operators satisfying the canonical commutation relation

\displaystyle[\mathsf{x},\mathsf{p}]={\rm i}\hbar.

(2.6)

We will consider the following spectral problem, associated to the quantization of the spectral curve (
2.1
):

\displaystyle\big\{\Lambda^{N}\big({\rm e}^{\mathsf{p}}+{\rm e}^{-\mathsf{p}}\big)+W_{N}(\mathsf{x})\big\}\big|\psi\rangle=0,

(2.7)

which can be written, in the position representation, as a difference equation for the wavefunction
\psi(x)
:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{N}\big(\psi(x+{\rm i}\hbar)+\psi(x-{\rm i}\hbar)\big)+W_{N}(x)\psi(x)=0.

(2.8)

Equivalently, we can write (
2.7
) as

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{N}|\psi\rangle=(-1)^{N-1}h_{N}|\psi\rangle,

(2.9)

where the Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{N}=\Lambda^{N}\big({\rm e}^{\mathsf{p}}+{\rm e}^{-\mathsf{p}}\big)+V_{N}(\mathsf{x}),

(2.10)

and

\displaystyle V_{N}(\mathsf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{k}\mathsf{x}^{N-k}h_{k}

is a degree
N
potential. The coefficient
(-1)^{N-1}h_{N}
appearing in (
2.2
) is then interpreted as the eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian (this is the point of view used in
[
21
,
49
]
in the quantization of mirror curves). We note that, when
N=2
, the Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{2}=2\Lambda^{2}\cosh(\mathsf{p})+\mathsf{x}^{2},

and it is equivalent, after a canonical transformation, to the Schrödinger operator appearing in the modified Mathieu equation. For
N\geq 3
,
\mathsf{H}_{N}
is a deformation of the standard Hamiltonian for a non-relativistic particle in an arbitrary potential, in one dimension:

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}^{\rm QM}_{N}=\Lambda^{N}\mathsf{p}^{2}+V_{N}(\mathsf{x}).

The relation to the standard quantum mechanical spectral problem will be made more precise in Section
6
.

Let us now examine in detail the spectral problem (
2.9
). We will assume for simplicity that the coefficients in the Hamiltonian
\Lambda,h_{2},\dots,h_{N-2}
are real
4
4

4

This condition can be easily relaxed and the generalizations will be studied elsewhere.
. Not surprisingly, the spectrum depends crucially on the parity of
N
. When
N
is even, the potential
V_{N}(x)
is confining, and (
2.9
) leads to an infinite, discrete spectrum as shown in
[
72
, Proposition 2.1]
. A heuristic argument supporting this conclusion is that the region in phase space

\displaystyle{\cal R}(E)=\big\{(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\colon 2\cosh(p)+V_{N}(x)\leq E\big\}

(2.11)

is compact and has finite volume, as measured by the canonical symplectic form
\omega={\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}p
. In fact, the spectrum should be captured at large quantum numbers by the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition

\displaystyle{\rm vol}_{0}(E)\approx 2\pi\hbar\left(n+{1\over 2}\right),

where
{\rm vol}_{0}(E)
is the volume of the region (
2.11
).

When
N
is odd, the situation is very different, since the potential
V_{N}(x)
is unbounded from below and there are no bound states. However, as in the case of anharmonic oscillators of odd degree in standard quantum mechanics
[
19
,
70
]
, we expect to have an infinite tower of
resonances
, associated to the so-called Gamow states. We recall that Gamow states are described by wavefunctions
\psi(x)
which satisfy appropriate boundary conditions. In the case of odd potentials, one requires decay at infinity in the direction in which the potential grows, and Gamow–Siegert boundary conditions (i.e., purely outgoing states) in the direction in which the potential decreases. In this paper we will follow a down-to-earth approach to the spectral problem (
2.9
) when
N
is odd, and we will define and compute the resonant eigenvalues by using the complex dilatation method
[
108
]
. We recall that, in this method, one considers the group of complex dilatations, acting on the Heisenberg operators
\mathsf{x}
,
\mathsf{p}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{x}\mathsf{U}_{{\rm i}\theta}^{-1}={\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{x},\qquad\mathsf{U}_{{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{p}\mathsf{U}^{-1}_{{\rm i}\theta}={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{p}.

We usually take
\theta
to be real, and we consider the rotated Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{N}(\theta)=\mathsf{U}_{{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{H}_{N}\mathsf{U}^{-1}_{{\rm i}\theta}=\exp\big({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{p}\big)+\exp\big({-}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{p}\big)+V_{N}\big({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}\mathsf{x}\big).

(2.12)

When
\theta\not=0
, his Hamiltonian has square-integrable eigenfunctions with complex eigenvalues, which are independent of the precise value of
\theta
beyond a given threshold, as explained in
[
108
]
. These eigenfunctions are obtained by acting with
\mathsf{U}_{{\rm i}\theta}
on Gamow states.

It is illuminating to consider the
classical
dynamics associated to the classical counterpart of the Hamiltonian (
2.10
), where
(-1)^{N-1}h_{N}=E
can be now regarded as the conserved energy of the system. Since
\cosh(p)\geq 1
for real
p
, the classical motion is confined to the intervals where

\displaystyle W_{N}(x)\geq-2\Lambda^{N}.

In these intervals, the EOM is given by

\displaystyle\dot{x}={\sqrt{W^{2}_{N}(x)-4\Lambda^{2N}}}.

(2.13)

The function inside the square root (
2.13
) is precisely the SW curve (
2.3
). If we write
W_{N}(x)
as in (
2.4
), where
\zeta_{I}=\zeta_{I}(h_{2},\dots,h_{N})
, the zeroes of this function (which are the branch points of the hyperelliptic curve (
2.3
)) are given by
[
68
]

\displaystyle e^{\pm}_{I}(h_{2},\dots,h_{N},\Lambda)=\zeta_{I}\big(h_{2},\dots,h_{N}\pm 2\Lambda^{N}\big).

(2.14)

In Fig.
1
we show the potential
V_{3}(x)
and the points
e_{I}^{\pm}
,
I=1,2,3
. There is one compact interval where motion is allowed, namely
[e_{1}^{+},e_{3}^{+}]
. This is one of the intervals of instability of the Toda lattice (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
). The other interval of instability is
[e_{2}^{-},e_{1}^{-}]
, but in our problem motion is not allowed in this interval, since
p
is complex there and of the form
{\rm Re}(p)+{\rm i}\pi
. As we will see, however, this interval, as well as the intervals between the points
e_{I}^{+}
and
e^{-}_{I}
, play a rôle in the quantum theory.

2.2
Relation to the Toda lattice

The difference equation (
2.8
) is very similar to the Baxter equation of the quantum-mechanical Toda lattice
[
52
,
53
,
66
,
90
,
97
]
. In solving this equation, one imposes very stringent boundary conditions on
\psi(x)
, which are only satisfied for discrete values of the
h_{k}
,
k=2,\dots,N-1
. These values lead to an infinite set of points in moduli space which give the spectrum of the commuting Hamiltonians of the underlying integrable system. We will call these points, by a slight abuse of language, the
Toda lattice points
.

In contrast, in the spectral problem we are considering, we impose conventional boundary conditions for
\psi(x)
: square integrability, when
N
is even, and Gamow–Siegert boundary conditions
when
N
is odd. With these conventional boundary condition, (
2.9
) leads to an infinite tower of discrete eigenvalues
h^{(n)}_{N}(h_{2},\dots,h_{N-1})
,
n=0,1,2,\dots
, as a function of the parameters
h_{2},\dots,h_{N-1}
. Geometrically, the allowed values of the moduli form a discrete family of submanifolds
{\cal S}_{n}
of codimension one inside the moduli space, labelled by the non-negative integer
n

[
21
]
.

It is easy to show that the Toda lattice points belong to the union of submanifolds
\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\cal S}_{n}
. To see this, we recall that the Baxter equation for the Toda lattice is
[
90
]

\displaystyle({\rm i}\Lambda)^{N}Q(x+{\rm i}\hbar)+(-{\rm i}\Lambda)^{N}Q(x-{\rm i}\hbar)=W_{N}(x)Q(x).

The solutions to this equation considered in
[
90
]
satisfy two conditions: they are entire in the complex
x
-plane, and they have the following decay behavior at infinity along the real axis,

\displaystyle Q(x)\approx{\rm e}^{-{N\pi\over 2\hbar}|x|},\qquad x\rightarrow\pm\infty.

These conditions restrict the values of
h_{k}
,
k=2,\dots,N
to a discrete set of values. Let us now consider the following function,

\displaystyle\varphi(x)={\rm e}^{{\pi(N-2)x\over 2\hbar}}Q(x).

It is immediate to check that
\varphi(x)
is entire, satisfies the difference equation (
2.8
), and, for
N>1
, it decreases exponentially at infinity on the real line. In particular,
\varphi(x)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Therefore, when
N
is even, it is an admissible eigenfunction of (
2.9
). When
N
is odd, it is an admissible Gamow state, but a very special one, since its resonant energy is real and it is described by a square integrable wavefunction. We conclude that, if
h_{2},\dots,h_{N}
lead to a solution of the Baxter equation,
h_{N}
is an eigenvalue or resonance of (
2.9
) for the values of the parameters given by
h_{2},\dots,h_{N-1}
.

2.3
Examples and new phenomena

Although the deformation of quantum mechanics we are studying is similar to standard one-dimensional quantum mechanics in many respects, it also displays some striking differences. For example, in the deformed version of the symmetric double-well potential, there are degenerate states and parity symmetry breaking at Toda lattice points. These are forbidden in conventional quantum mechanics, yet they occur here. In the deformed version of the cubic oscillator, we find special resonances with purely real eigenvalues. We note that restoration of parity symmetry and complex eigenvalues are the signal of non-perturbative tunneling effects in the double-well and the cubic oscillator, respectively. It turns out that, in our deformed quantum mechanics, tunneling effects are suppressed at the Toda lattice points, leading to this unconventional phenomena. Let us now illustrate these general considerations with a closer look at the spectral problem (
2.9
) for
N=3
,
N=4
.

The quantum periods

As in the conventional exact WKB method, the basic ingredients in our exact quantization conditions are the quantum periods associated to the curve (

\Delta_{\pm}
is the set of positive (respectively, negative) roots. We define the total NS free energy (including perturbative and instanton corrections) through the equation

\displaystyle{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}={\partial F^{\rm inst}_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}+2\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{+}}\gamma_{\rm 4d}\left(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha},\hbar\right)\boldsymbol{\alpha}.

We note that

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{-}}\gamma_{\rm 4d}(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha},\hbar)\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\pi\hbar\boldsymbol{\rho}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{+}}\gamma_{\rm 4d}(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha},\hbar)\boldsymbol{\alpha},

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}={1\over 2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{+}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(N-i)\boldsymbol{e}_{i}

is the Weyl vector, so that we can also write

\displaystyle{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}={\partial F^{\rm inst}_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta}\gamma_{\rm 4d}(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha},\hbar)\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\pi\hbar\boldsymbol{\rho}.

The relation (
3.6
) in 5d induces a corresponding one in 4d, of the form

\displaystyle h_{k}=h_{k}(\boldsymbol{a};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}),\qquad k=2,\dots,N.

(3.15)

In the NS limit, these relations become the 4d quantum mirror map, which we will write as

\displaystyle h_{k}=h_{k}(\boldsymbol{a},\hbar).

(3.16)

This is a resummed or exact version of the inverse function to the quantum
A
-periods appearing in the first line of (
3.1
). The explicit form of the relations (
3.15
) can be obtained by expanding the 5d functions (
3.6
) in powers of
R
around
R=0
. Explicitly, this goes as follows. Let us introduce

\displaystyle c_{2}(Y)={1\over 2}\sum_{i\geq 1}y_{i}(y_{i}-1).

Then, the character (
3.7
) has the expansion,

\displaystyle{\rm Ch}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}({\cal E})=N+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\cal C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{Y})R^{k},

(3.17)

where the very first
{\cal C}_{k}
are

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{Y})={1\over 2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{2}-\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{Y}),

(3.18)

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{3}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{Y})=\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\left({\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}\over 2}\ell(\boldsymbol{Y})+\epsilon_{1}\sum_{I=1}^{N}c_{2}(Y^{t}_{I})+\epsilon_{2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}c_{2}(Y_{I})-\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}\ell(Y_{I})\right)+{1\over 6}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{3}.

There are similar expansions for the characters in higher representations, which can be derived from (
3.17
). Through (
3.8
) one derives an expansion for
H_{1}
in powers of
R
. On the other hand, this expansion has the form

\displaystyle H_{1}=N+\sum_{j\geq 2}{R^{j}\over j!}p_{j},

where the
p_{j}
are the quantum analogues of Newton polynomials, and they can be related to the
h_{k}
by using standard results in the theory of symmetric functions. The general Newton polynomial
p_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is labelled by a vector
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)
, and is given by

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\prod_{j\geq 1}p_{j}^{k_{j}}.

Let us denote

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{k}|=\sum_{j\geq 1}k_{j}.

Then
[
74
]
,

\displaystyle h_{n}=s_{(1^{n})}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{k})\over z_{\boldsymbol{k}}}p_{\boldsymbol{k}},

where
z_{\boldsymbol{k}}
has been defined in (
3.9
), and

\displaystyle\epsilon(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{|\boldsymbol{k}|-\ell(\boldsymbol{k})}.

Note that
p_{1}=0
, so that only vectors of the form
(0,k_{2},\dots)
contribute in the above sum. We find, for example,

\displaystyle h_{2}=-{1\over 2}p_{2},\qquad h_{3}={1\over 3}p_{3},\qquad h_{4}={1\over 8}p_{2}^{2}-{1\over 4}p_{4}.

One finds in this way, for example (see also
[
39
]
),

\displaystyle h_{2}(\boldsymbol{a};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}t)=-{1\over Z}\sum_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\big((-1)^{N}\Lambda^{2N}\big)^{\ell(\boldsymbol{Y})}{\cal C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{Y}){\cal Z}_{\boldsymbol{Y}},

\displaystyle h_{3}(\boldsymbol{a};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})={2\over Z}\sum_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\big((-1)^{N}\Lambda^{2N}\big)^{\ell(\boldsymbol{Y})}{\cal C}_{3}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{Y}){\cal Z}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}.

(3.19)

It follows from the first line in (
3.18
) that
h=-h_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle h(\boldsymbol{a};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})={1\over 2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{2}-\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\Lambda^{2N}{\partial\over\partial\Lambda^{2N}}\log Z(\boldsymbol{a};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}).

(3.20)

In particular, in the NS limit we have

\displaystyle h(\boldsymbol{a},\hbar)={1\over 2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{2}-\Lambda^{2N}{\partial\over\partial\Lambda^{2N}}F_{\rm NS}^{\rm inst}(\boldsymbol{a},\hbar).

(3.21)

The relations (
3.20
), (
3.21
) generalize Matone’s relation
[
81
]
to the Omega-background
[
37
]
.

An important observation is that instanton calculus gives the NS free energy and the quantum mirror maps as infinite sums in the instanton counting parameter
\Lambda^{2N}
. When
\hbar=0
, these infinite sums are convergent in the so-called large radius or semiclassical region, in which the
a_{i}
’s are large. As it is well-known in SW theory (and similar to what happens in mirror symmetry), there is a natural boundary for the region of convergence, which is the discriminant locus of the SW curve. Near this locus, convergence breaks down, and one needs to perform an analytic continuation to have access to other regions in moduli space. This should remain qualitatively true when
\hbar\not=0
, namely, the expansions (
3.12
) and (
3.19
) should have a finite radius of convergence around the region at infinity in moduli space. Although the precise analytic structure of the resulting functions is not known, numerical calculations seem to validate this picture (some analyticity results for the instanton partition function have been derived in
[
35
]
, but they do not apply to the case
\hbar>0
in which we are interested). This expected analyticity is of paramount importance, since it means that the quantum periods, defined as instanton sums in gauge theory, are well-defined functions,
at least in some region of moduli space, and Borel-type resummations are no longer necessary.

It was pointed out in
[
89
]
, and further elaborated in
[
14
,
62
,
83
]
, that the NS free energy and the quantum mirror maps can be also computed in terms of a set of integral equations reminiscent of TBA equations. In this approach, one first introduces a function
\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{\alpha})
as a solution of the following integral equation,

\displaystyle\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi}K(x-y)\log\big(1+\Lambda^{2N}Q(y;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(y;\boldsymbol{a})}\big),

where

\displaystyle K(x)=\frac{2\hbar}{x^{2}+\hbar^{2}},\qquad Q(x;\boldsymbol{a})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(x-\alpha_{j}-{\rm i}\hbar/2)(x-\alpha_{j}+{\rm i}\hbar/2)}.

The NS free energy is then given by

\displaystyle F_{\text{NS}}^{\text{inst}}(\boldsymbol{a},\hbar)=-\frac{\hbar}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm d}x\biggl[{-}\frac{1}{2}\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})\log\big(1+\Lambda^{2N}Q(x;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{F_\text{NS}^\text{inst}(\boldsymbol{a},\hbar) =-\frac{\hbar}{2\pi} \int_{{\mathbb{R}}} {\rm d}x}{}+{\rm Li}_{2}\big({-}\Lambda^{2N}Q(x;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})}\big)\biggr].

From these expressions one can also compute the partial derivatives of the NS free energy as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\text{NS}}^{\text{inst}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{a}}=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\rm d}x{\partial\log Q(x;\boldsymbol{a})\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}\log\big(1+\Lambda^{2N}Q(x;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})}\big).

One can also use the TBA integral equations to compute the quantum mirror map. The result is more easily formulated in terms of the Newton polynomials
p_{k}
:

\displaystyle p_{k}=\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{k}+{k\over 2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}x\big[(x+{\rm i}\hbar/2)^{k-1}-(x-{\rm i}\hbar/2)^{k-1}\big]\log\big(1+\Lambda^{2N}Q(x;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})}\big),

for
k=2,\dots,N
. One finds in this way, for example,

\displaystyle h={1\over 2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{2}+\frac{\hbar}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\log\big(1+\Lambda^{2N}Q(x;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})}\big),

\displaystyle h_{3}={1\over 3}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\alpha_{I}^{3}+\frac{\hbar}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}xx\log\big(1+\Lambda^{2N}Q(x;\boldsymbol{a}){\rm e}^{-\varphi(x;\boldsymbol{a})}\big).

It is natural to ask whether and where the TBA equations provide well-defined functions for the NS free energy and the quantum mirror maps, complementing in this way the approach based on instanton calculus in gauge theory. The results of
[
62
]
guarantee that these quantities are well defined in a region of moduli space containing the Toda lattice points. Numerical studies support this conclusion, but it would be of course important to know if this region can be enlarged, maybe to the whole of moduli space.

We conclude this summary by emphasizing that supersymmetric gauge theory, in the NS limit, provides a very powerful resummation of the traditional quantum periods appearing in the all-orders WKB method, as applied to the SW curve. This will allow us to define exact quantization conditions for the spectral problem (
2.9
) involving actual functions, and not resurgent functions.

4
The exact quantization condition

4.1
Statement and properties

We are now ready to state the exact quantization condition for the spectral problem (
2.7
) (or, equivalently, (
2.9
)). Let us consider the following vector in the weight lattice
\Lambda_{w}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(-1)^{i-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}={1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(-1)^{i-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}.

(4.1)

The Weyl orbit of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is defined by

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\big\{w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\colon w\in{\cal W}_{N}\big\},

(4.2)

i.e., it consists of all the vectors in
\Lambda_{\rm w}
obtained by acting with an element of the Weyl group
{\cal W}_{N}
on
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Since the Weyl group acts as the permutation group
S_{N}
on the indices of the weights
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
, the number of different elements in the Weyl orbit will be

\displaystyle d^{\rm e}_{N}={N\choose N/2}

when
N
is even, and

\displaystyle d^{\rm o}_{N}={N\choose{N-1\over 2}}

when
N
is odd.

There are two different cases for the exact quantization conditions, depending on the parity of
N
. When
N
is even, the quantization condition determining the spectrum of bound states is

\displaystyle\boxed{\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in{\cal W}_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\exp\left({{\rm i}\over\hbar}{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right)\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-\left(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{2}}=0.}

(4.3)

When
N
is odd, the quantization condition determining the spectrum of resonances is

\displaystyle\boxed{\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in{\cal W}_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\exp\left({{\rm i}\over\hbar}{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-{\pi\over\hbar}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right)\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-\left(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{2}}=0.}

(4.4)

As we mentioned above, these quantization conditions provide one single constraint on the values of the moduli. To solve the spectral problem (
2.9
), we fix the
N-2
coefficients
h_{2},\dots,h_{N-1}
in the
potential
V_{N}(x)
. The quantization condition and the quantum mirror map (
3.16
) for these
N-2
coefficients give in total
N-1
conditions for
N-1
independent variables
a_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N-1
. The values of these variables, once plugged into the quantum mirror map for
h_{N}
, finally give the eigenvalue for the spectral problem.

The quantization conditions (
4.3
), (
4.4
) have two important formal properties: they are invariant under the action of the Weyl group on
\boldsymbol{a}
, and they are solved by the points in the moduli space corresponding to the spectrum of the periodic Toda lattice, as expected from our discussion in Section
2
. Let us first discuss invariance under the Weyl group. This is only expected, since two points
\boldsymbol{a}
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}=w(\boldsymbol{a})
related by an element of the Weyl group represent the same point in moduli space. To check the invariance, let us suppose that we act with an element
w\in{\cal W}_{N}
on
\boldsymbol{a}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta}\gamma_{\rm 4d}(w(\boldsymbol{a})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha},\hbar)\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta}\gamma_{\rm 4d}\big(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot w^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\hbar\big)\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta}\gamma_{\rm 4d}(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha},\hbar)w(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

where we used invariance of
\Delta
and of the Cartan–Killing form under Weyl permutations. We conclude that, under
w\in{\cal W}_{N}
, one has

\displaystyle{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}\rightarrow w\left({\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}+\pi\hbar\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)-\pi\hbar\boldsymbol{\rho}.

We recall that, under the action of an element
w
of the Weyl group, the positive roots are transformed into positive or negative roots (for this and other statements on root and weight lattices, see, e.g.,
[
58
]
or
[
28
, Chapter 13]
). Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle w(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\epsilon(w,\boldsymbol{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime},\qquad\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{+},\qquad\epsilon(w,\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\pm 1,

and

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi w(\boldsymbol{a})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot w^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_+} \left( 2 \sinh\left( {\pi w(\boldsymbol{a})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right) \right)^{-(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha})^2}}{}=\epsilon\big(w^{-1},\boldsymbol{n}\big)\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-\left(w^{-1}(\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{2}},

where

\displaystyle\epsilon(w,\boldsymbol{n})=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}(\epsilon(w,\boldsymbol{\alpha}))^{(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}}

is a sign depending on
w
and
\boldsymbol{n}
. The l.h.s. of the quantization condition becomes, for
N
even,

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in{\cal W}_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\epsilon\big(w^{-1},\boldsymbol{n}\big){\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot\left(w^{-1}(\boldsymbol{n})-\boldsymbol{n}\right)}\exp\left({{\rm i}\over\hbar}{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot w^{-1}(\boldsymbol{n})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\times\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-\left(w^{-1}(\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{2}}.

Since we are summing over a Weyl orbit, this is invariant provided

\displaystyle\epsilon(w,\boldsymbol{n}){\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot(w(\boldsymbol{n})-\boldsymbol{n})}=1,

(4.5)

for all
w\in{\cal W}_{N}
and all
\boldsymbol{n}
appearing in the sum. This can be verified by considering the generators of the Weyl group
w_{i}

i=1,\dots,N-1
, which are reflections associated to simple roots. These reflections leave invariant all the positive roots, except
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
, which changes sign. Then,

\displaystyle\epsilon(w_{i},\boldsymbol{n})=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}},

where we have used the fact, easily checked, that, for any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta
,
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}
can only take the values
\pm 1
,
0
. At the same time

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot(w_{i}(\boldsymbol{n})-\boldsymbol{n})=-(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},

so that

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot(w(\boldsymbol{n})-\boldsymbol{n})}=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}},

and (
4.5
) holds. A similar argument holds for
N
odd.

It can be explicitly checked that the points
\boldsymbol{a}
in the spectrum of the Toda lattice satisfy (
4.3
), (
4.4
). These points are characterized by the quantization conditions
[
62
,
89
]

\displaystyle{1\over\hbar}{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}=2\pi\boldsymbol{\ell}+\pi\boldsymbol{\rho},

(4.6)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\ell_{k}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\qquad\ell_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}.

To verify this property, we have to evaluate

\displaystyle{{\rm i}\over\hbar}{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot(w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\boldsymbol{\gamma})=2\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot(w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\boldsymbol{\gamma})+\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot(w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

The first term in the r.h.s. belongs to
2\pi{\rm i}{\mathbb{Z}}
. This is because, since
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is a weight,

\displaystyle w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\Lambda_{\rm r}

and its product with an element in
\Lambda_{\rm w}
like
\boldsymbol{\ell}
is an integer. The condition that points in the spectrum of the Toda lattice satisfy the quantization condition is

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in{\cal W}_{N}}{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\rho}\cdot w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-\left(w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{2}}=0,

for
N
even, and

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in{\cal W}_{N}}{\rm e}^{(\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\rho}-\pi\boldsymbol{a}/\hbar)\cdot w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\left(2\sinh\left({\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\over\hbar}\right)\right)^{-\left(w(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)^{2}}=0,

for
N
odd. Although we do not have a proof of this fact, we have verified up to high values of
N
that the above equalities are satisfied by any
\boldsymbol{a}
.

Our quantization conditions (
4.3
), (
4.4
) are very similar to what is obtained with the exact WKB method in ordinary quantum mechanics. In this case, the relevant curve is (in appropriate units)

\displaystyle p^{2}=E-V(x),

(4.7)

where
E
is the energy,
p
is the momentum, and
V(x)
is the potential. The all-orders WKB method applied to (
4.7
) makes it possible to define quantum periods, similar to (
3.1
). These periods are generically complex and they inherit the integral lattice structure of the space of one-cycles on the curve. One obtains in this way an integer lattice
\Lambda_{\rm WKB}
of rank
2g
, where
g
is the genus of the curve (
4.7
). In the seminal work of Balian, Parisi and Voros
[
10
]
it was argued that in principle all the periods in the lattice
\Lambda_{\rm WKB}
play a rôle in the quantum theory. In particular, the exact quantization condition will involve complex periods which are invisible in the classical theory. This was illustrated in
[
10
]
and further investigated in
[
102
,
103
]
in the example of the pure quartic oscillator with Hamiltonian
\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{p}^{2}+\mathsf{x}^{4}
. In the classical theory, the motion takes place in the interval
\big[{-}E^{1/4},E^{1/4}\big]
. The
perturbative
WKB condition involves the quantum period obtained by integrating around this interval. However, the quantum period obtained by integration around the interval
\big[{-}{\rm i}E^{1/4},{\rm i}E^{1/4}\big]
, although it is classically invisible, gives a
non-perturbative correction
which is crucial to reproduce the correct spectrum.

More generally, quantization conditions obtained with the exact WKB method in ordinary quantum mechanics involve a sum of terms which is then set to zero. Each term in this sum is given by a numerical coefficient, times a
Voros multiplier

[
25
,
26
,
29
]
. We recall that a Voros multiplier has the form

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\cal A}/\hbar},\qquad{\cal A}\in\Lambda_{\rm WKB}.

For example, the quantization condition for the resonances of the standard cubic oscillator can be written as
[
4
,
25
]
:

\displaystyle 1+{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\nu/\hbar}+{\rm e}^{-{\partial F\over\partial\nu}/\hbar}=0,

(4.8)

where we have used the notation in
[
23
]
to emphasize the similarities with the case studied in this paper. In this notation,
\nu
is the quantum period around the perturbative cycle of the cubic oscillator, while
{\rm i}\partial F/\partial\nu
is the quantum period around the tunneling cycle.

The quantization conditions (
4.3
), (
4.4
) can be also written as a sum of Voros multipliers. These multipliers involve integer linear combinations of the quantum periods of the SW curve:

\displaystyle{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial a_{j}},\qquad\pi{\rm i}a_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,N-1.

The quantum
B
-periods are the ones that appear in the quantization condition of the Toda lattice. They correspond to the cycles around the intervals of instability in the spectral curve. The contribution from the quantum
A
-periods is new, and as we will see in Section
5
, their origin is purely non-perturbative: they are the remnants in 4d of the non-perturbative correction due to the standard topological string in the TS/ST correspondence. In contrast to the solution of the spectral problem of the Toda lattice, which involves only the “perturbative”
B
-periods, the solution of the spectral problem associated to the SW curve requires these new, non-perturbative Voros multipliers.

Although our quantization conditions are very similar to the ones appearing in the exact WKB method, there is as well an important difference: in quantization conditions like (
4.8
), the quantum periods are
formal
power series, which have to be resummed with Borel–Écalle techniques in order to obtain the actual spectra. In particular, the form of the quantization conditions can change as we cross Stokes lines (this is in fact the case for the cubic oscillator, and the form (
4.8
) is only valid for a specific lateral resummation, see
[
4
]
for more details). In contrast, our quantization conditions (
4.3
), (
4.4
) involve resummed versions of these periods, which can be defined as convergent instanton sums in gauge theory or as well-defined solutions to TBA equations. Therefore, our quantization conditions are truly exact, in the sense that they involve actual functions.

As we already anticipated, the quantization conditions (
4.3
), (
4.4
) can be derived from the TS/ST duality
[
21
,
49
]
which was originally formulated for real values of
\hbar
. However it has been shown in
[
50
]
that this correspondence can be extended to complex values as well. We have tested that (
4.3
), (
4.4
) also holds for complex values of
\hbar
as far as
{\rm Re}(\hbar)\neq 0
.

4.2
Examples and evidence

4.2.1
The case
\boldsymbol{N=2}

Let us first consider the case
N=2
. The vector (
4.1
) is
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
, and the Weyl orbit is

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\},

so it contains two elements. Setting
a_{1}=a
, we find the quantization condition

\displaystyle\cos\left({1\over\hbar}{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial a}\right)=0.

This is the quantization condition found in
[
89
]
for
N=2
Toda lattice. In this case the integrable system has a single Hamiltonian, which equivalent to our
\mathsf{H}_{2}
. Therefore our exact quantization condition reproduces the expected result.

4.2.2
The case
\boldsymbol{N=3}

Let us then consider the next case,
N=3
. The Hamiltonian is given in (
2.15
). The vector (
4.1
) is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}={1\over 2}(\boldsymbol{e}_{1}-\boldsymbol{e}_{2}+\boldsymbol{e}_{3}),

and its Weyl orbit contains three elements:

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{3}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left\{{1\over 2}(\boldsymbol{e}_{1}-\boldsymbol{e}_{2}+\boldsymbol{e}_{3}),{1\over 2}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\boldsymbol{e}_{2}+\boldsymbol{e}_{3}),{1\over 2}(\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\boldsymbol{e}_{2}-\boldsymbol{e}_{3})\right\}.

Let us introduce the functions
\phi_{1,2}(a_{1},a_{2},\hbar)
, defined as

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(a_{1},a_{2};\hbar)={1\over\hbar}\left({\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial a_{2}}-2{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial a_{1}}\right),\qquad\phi_{2}(a_{1},a_{2};\hbar)={1\over\hbar}\left(2{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial a_{2}}-{\partial F_{\rm NS}\over\partial a_{1}}\right).

Then, elementary algebra shows that the quantization condition can be written as

5
Derivation from the TS/ST correspondence

5.1
The TS/ST correspondence

We now summarize the main ingredients of the TS/ST correspondence in the higher genus case. Our presentation is very synthetic and it mainly serves the goal of setting our notation. We refer the reader to
[
21
,
22
,
77
]
for more details and background.

Toric CY threefolds are specified by a matrix of charges
Q_{i}^{\alpha}
,
i=0,\dots,k+2
,
\alpha=1,\dots,k
, satisfying the condition,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k+2}Q_{i}^{\alpha}=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\ldots,k.

The Newton polygon
{\cal N}
associated to these charges is given by a set of points
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{i}=(\nu_{1}^{i},\nu_{2}^{i})
,
i=0,1,\dots,k+2
, satisfying the constraints

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k+2}Q_{i}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{i}=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,k.

The mirror curve to the toric CY is described by the curve,

\displaystyle W({\rm e}^{x},{\rm e}^{p})=0,

where

\displaystyle W({\rm e}^{x},{\rm e}^{p})=\sum_{i=0}^{k+2}a_{i}{\rm e}^{\nu_{1}^{i}x+\nu_{2}^{i}p}.

(5.1)

The coefficients
a_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,k+2
, are not independent, and we should use instead the Batyrev coordinates

\displaystyle z_{\alpha}=\prod_{i=0}^{k+2}a_{i}^{Q_{i}^{\alpha}},\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,k.

The curve (
5.1
) can be quantized by promoting
x
,
p
to Heisenberg operators
\mathsf{x}
,
\mathsf{p}
satisfying the canonical commutation relation

\displaystyle[\mathsf{x},\mathsf{p}]={\rm i}\hbar

and using Weyl’s ordering prescription. We want to solve the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle W\big({\rm e}^{\mathsf{x}},{\rm e}^{\mathsf{p}}\big)|\psi\rangle=0.

(5.2)

The quantization condition determining the spectrum is a single constraint among the values of the Batyrev coordinates, as emphasized in
[
21
]
. According to the conjecture put forward in
[
21
,
49
]
, this constraint can be obtained by using information from topological string theory on the CY threefold. The information we need is the following. First, we need the Gopakumar–Vafa free energy
[
45
]

\displaystyle F_{\rm GV}(\boldsymbol{t},g_{s})=\sum_{g\geq 0}\sum_{\boldsymbol{d}}\sum_{w=1}^{\infty}{1\over w}n_{g}^{\boldsymbol{d}}\left(2\sin{wg_{s}\over 2}\right)^{2g-2}{\rm e}^{-w\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}}.

In this equation,
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
is the vector of complexified Kähler parameters,
g_{s}
is the string coupling constant, and
n_{g}^{\bf d}
are the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants. The topological string free energy has also a perturbative piece, which we write as

\displaystyle F_{\rm p}(\boldsymbol{t},g_{s})=\frac{1}{6g_{s}^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{k}a_{ijk}t_{i}t_{j}t_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(b_{i}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{g_{s}^{2}}b_{i}^{\rm NS}\right)t_{i}.

(5.3)

Here,
a_{ijk}
,
b_{i}
,
b_{i}^{\rm NS}
are coefficients which can be explicitly computed for any toric CY model (see
[
56
]
for examples). In addition, we need the NS free energy of the toric CY,
F^{\rm BPS}_{\rm NS}(\boldsymbol{t},\hbar)
, which can be expressed in terms of the refined BPS invariants
N^{\boldsymbol{d}}_{j_{L},j_{R}}
of
[
59
]
as

\displaystyle F^{\rm BPS}_{\rm NS}(\boldsymbol{t},\hbar)=\sum_{j_{L},j_{R}}\sum_{w,\boldsymbol{d}}N^{\boldsymbol{d}}_{j_{L},j_{R}}\frac{\sin\frac{\hbar w}{2}(2j_{L}+1)\sin\frac{\hbar w}{2}(2j_{R}+1)}{2w^{2}\sin^{3}\frac{\hbar w}{2}}{\rm e}^{-w\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}}.

Another ingredient we need is the
\boldsymbol{B}
field first introduced in
[
55
]
. This is a
k
-component vector which satisfies the following requirement: for all
{\bf d}
,
j_{L}
and
j_{R}
such that
N^{{\bf d}}_{j_{L},j_{R}}
is non-vanishing, we must have

\displaystyle(-1)^{2j_{L}+2j_{R}+1}=(-1)^{{\boldsymbol{B}}\cdot{\bf d}}.

Finally, we need an additional fact about local CY manifolds. In these manifolds, the moduli are of two types: we have
g_{\Sigma}
moduli

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}={\rm e}^{\mu_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,g_{\Sigma},

and in addition we have “mass parameters”
\xi_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r_{\Sigma}
, where
r_{\Sigma}=k-g_{\Sigma}
. The Batyrev coordinates can be written as

\displaystyle-\log z_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{g_{\Sigma}}C_{ij}\mu_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{r_{\Sigma}}\alpha_{ij}\log{\xi_{j}},\qquad i=1,\dots,k.

We now define the so-called modified grand potential of the CY
X
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{X}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\xi},\hbar)=F_{\rm p}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\boldsymbol{t}(\hbar),\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\hbar}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{t_{i}(\hbar)}{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}F^{\rm BPS}_{\rm NS}(\boldsymbol{t}(\hbar),\hbar)+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hbar}\left(\frac{F^{\rm BPS}_{\rm NS}(\boldsymbol{t}(\hbar),\hbar)}{\hbar}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathsf{J}_X(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\xi},\hbar) =}{}+F_{\rm GV}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\boldsymbol{t}(\hbar)+\pi{\rm i}\boldsymbol{B},\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\hbar}\right).

In this equation,
\boldsymbol{t}(\hbar)
is the quantum mirror map
[
1
]
, which is a function of
\hbar
and the Batyrev coordinates (therefore of the moduli and the mass parameters). We recall that, in the large radius region of moduli space,

\displaystyle t_{i}(\hbar)=-\log z_{i}+{\cal O}(z_{k}).

We are now ready to define the quantum theta function of the model, as

\displaystyle\Theta_{X}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}};\hbar)=\sum_{{\bf m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{g_{\Sigma}}}\exp\big(\mathsf{J}_{X}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+2\pi{\rm i}{\bf m},\boldsymbol{\xi},\hbar)-\mathsf{J}_{X}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\xi},\hbar)\big).

It was conjectured in
[
21
,
49
]
that the vanishing of this quantum theta function gives the quantization condition for the spectral problem (
5.2
). This conjecture has been verified in many examples. In particular, in the higher genus case, it has been checked in
[
13
,
21
,
22
,
99
]
. As we will now show, our quantization conditions (
4.4
), (
4.3
) are limiting cases of this conjecture for a particular family of toric CY manifolds.

5.2
The 4d limit

The geometries we will consider are the toric CY known in the literature as resolved
Y^{N,0}
singularities (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
54
]
for a useful summary of their properties). These geometries engineer 4d SW theory with gauge group
{\rm SU}(N)
in an appropriate limit
[
65
]
. Their mirror curve is precisely the SW curve of 5d,
{\rm SU}(N)
supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory (
3.5
), which we will write as

\displaystyle W\big({\rm e}^{x},{\rm e}^{p}\big)=a_{1}{\rm e}^{p}+a_{2}{\rm e}^{-p}+\sum_{i=0}^{N}\kappa_{i}{\rm e}^{(N/2-i)x}=0.

(5.4)

The coefficients appearing here are related to the ones in (
3.5
) and (
3.11
) by

\displaystyle a_{1}=a_{2}=\left(R\Lambda\right)^{N},\qquad\kappa_{i}=(-1)^{i}H_{i},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,N,

and we recall that
H_{0}=H_{N}=1
. This CY has
N-1
moduli, which can be taken to be the coefficients
\kappa_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N-1
, and one mass parameter, which is
\Lambda R
. The Batyrev coordinates are

\displaystyle z_{i}={\kappa_{i-1}\kappa_{i+1}\over\kappa^{2}_{i}}={H_{i-1}H_{i+1}\over H_{i}^{2}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N-1,

\displaystyle z_{N}={a_{1}a_{2}\over\kappa_{0}\kappa_{N}}=(-1)^{N}(R\Lambda)^{2N}.

The model has
N
Kähler moduli
t_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
. The first
N-1
correspond to true moduli, and their quantum mirror map is given by

\displaystyle t_{i}(\hbar)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}C_{ij}\mu_{j}+{\cal O}\left({\rm e}^{\mu_{k}}\right),\qquad i=1,\dots,N-1,

where
C_{ij}
is the Cartan matrix of
{\rm SU}(N)
, while

\displaystyle t_{N}=-\log z_{N}

(5.5)

is a parameter. We note that the relation (
5.5
) has no corrections. As in the previous section, we will collect the “true” Kähler moduli
t_{1},\dots,t_{N-1}
in a vector of
\Lambda_{\rm w}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}t_{j}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}.

This should not be confused with the generic vector
\boldsymbol{t}
appearing the formulae of the previous subsection. The modified grand potential of the geometry can be computed by using standard tools in topological string theory. The perturbative free energy (
5.3

6
The limit of standard quantum mechanics

The Hamiltonian (
1.2
) is clearly a deformation of the standard Hamiltonian (
1.1
). We would like to make this statement more precise, since we would eventually like to use our exactly solvable deformation to shed light on conventional quantum mechanics. It turns out that the conventional Hamiltonian (
1.1
) emerges in a very non-trivial regime of the underlying supersymmetric gauge theory: we have to approach the AD superconformal point
[
6
]
inside the moduli space of the
{\rm SU}(N)
theory
[
34
]
.

Let us see this in detail. It is clear that, in order to recover (
1.1
), we should consider small values of the momentum. Let us introduce a scaling parameter
\alpha
, which we will eventually take to zero, and let us consider the following scaling

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}=\alpha^{\lambda_{1}}\widetilde{\mathsf{p}},\qquad\mathsf{x}=\alpha^{\lambda_{2}}\widetilde{\mathsf{x}},

so that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}>0,\qquad\lambda_{2}>0,\qquad\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1,

(6.1)

The values of the exponents
\lambda_{1,2}
will be fixed in a moment. We will also scale the Planck constant as

\displaystyle\hbar=\alpha\hbar_{\rm QM},

so that

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathsf{p}},\widetilde{\mathsf{x}}]=\hbar_{\rm QM}.

We now scale the coefficients in the potential

\displaystyle W_{N}(\mathsf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{k}\mathsf{x}^{N-k}h_{k}+(-1)^{N}h_{N}

as

\displaystyle h_{k}=\alpha^{\Delta_{k}}\widetilde{h}_{k},\qquad k=2,\dots,N-1,

and

\displaystyle h_{N}=2(-1)^{N-1}\Lambda^{N}+\alpha^{\Delta_{N}}\widetilde{h}_{N}.

We then find

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{N}+(-1)^{N}h_{N}=\Lambda^{N}\left({\rm e}^{\mathsf{p}}+{\rm e}^{-\mathsf{p}}\right)+W_{N}(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathsf{H}_N +(-1)^N h_N}{}=\alpha^{2\lambda_{1}}\Lambda^{N}\widetilde{\mathsf{p}}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}(-1)^{k}\alpha^{(N-k)\lambda_{2}+\Delta_{k}}\widetilde{\mathsf{x}}^{N-k}\widetilde{h}_{k}+\cdots,

where the dots indicate higher order corrections. We now choose
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and
\Delta_{k}
,

, in such a way that

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{N}+(-1)^{N}h_{N}=\alpha^{2\lambda_{1}}\big(\mathsf{H}^{\rm QM}_{N}+(-1)^{N}\widetilde{h}_{N}\big)+\cdots,

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}^{\rm QM}_{N}=\Lambda^{N}\widetilde{\mathsf{p}}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{k}\widetilde{\mathsf{x}}^{N-k}\widetilde{h}_{k}.

This requires that

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{1}=N\lambda_{2},\qquad(N-k)\lambda_{2}+\Delta_{k}=2\lambda_{1},\qquad k=2,\dots,N,

which, after imposing (
6.1

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}={N\over N+2},\qquad\lambda_{2}={2\over N+2},

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}={2k\over N+2},\qquad k=2,\dots,N.

For

{\cal N}=2

{\rm SU}(N)

Our quantization conditions, expanded around this point, should give exact quantization conditions for the conventional quantum-mechanical anharmonic oscillators.

However, in order to implement this concretely, we would need analytic expressions for the NS free energy and quantum mirror maps near this point, as a function of the rescaled moduli

7
Conclusions and outlook

The results presented in this paper open many avenues for further investigation.

Our conjectural quantization conditions have passed many tests, but it would be interesting to prove them, at least at the physical level of rigor. In order to do this, one could develop the analogue of the (exact) WKB method for our deformed theory, and use semiclassical intuition (maybe based on a path integral formulation) to understand the non-perturbative corrections appearing in (
4.3
), (
4.4
). More generally, the deformation of quantum mechanics presented in this paper can be regarded as a solvable testing ground for many structures that appear in conventional quantum mechanics. One could study the deformed versions of PT-symmetric Hamiltonians, of quasi-exactly solvable models and of supersymmetric quantum mechanics. One could also apply techniques from the theory of resurgence to understand the non-perturbative structure that we have uncovered. For example, the perturbative series for the energy levels of the Hamiltonian (
1.2
) can be studied in detail by using a generalization of the BenderWu package
[
51
,
98
]
due to Jie Gu, and the large order behavior
of these series should contain information about the non-perturbative corrections that we have found. Finally, since our exact quantization conditions involve explicit functions, it should be possible to study in detail the analytic continuation of the eigenvalue problem to complex values of the parameters, as in
[
11
]
.

As we have also emphasized, our deformed version of quantum mechanics displays phenomena which are forbidden in the conventional version. Most notably, we find that, for special values of the parameters, tunneling is suppressed. This leads, in the case of even potentials, to spontaneous parity-symmetry breaking, and in the case of unbounded potentials, to special resonances with real energies. It would be interesting to explore in more detail these qualitatively new phenomena and their implications.

In this paper we have focused on the spectrum of the Hamiltonian, but we would like to determine as well the eigenfunctions. The TS/ST correspondence also provides information on these
[
78
,
79
]
(the results on wavefunctions obtained in
[
64
,
93
]
are likely to be valid only at the Toda lattice points, and not in the more generic case we have considered here). Understanding the 4d limit of the proposal of
[
78
,
79
]
is therefore an obvious direction to pursue.

In this paper we have considered the simplest quantum problem arising in SW theory, corresponding to pure
{\rm SU}(N)
,
{\cal N}=2
Yang–Mills theory. The quantization of SW curves with other gauge groups and/or matter content is certainly possible, and leads to spectral problems which are likely to be solvable by combining the TS/ST correspondence with geometric engineering. This would provide an interesting testing ground for the conjectures put forward in
[
21
,
49
]
, and would enrich the world of solvable quantum-mechanical models with new acquisitions.

It would be also very interesting to understand in more detail the relation to integrable systems. It was pointed out in
[
38
,
47
,
57
,
99
]
that the spectrum of the integrable systems associated to quantum mirror curves can be obtained by considering the common vanishing locus of various quantum theta functions, differing among them by a rotation of the moduli. Presumably, the same thing is true in the 4d limit, and by rotating appropriately the Hamiltonians we can find different spectral problems with different quantization conditions, in such a way that the intersecting loci give precisely the Toda lattice points. However, it is not clear to us why this procedure should lead to an enhanced decay of the wavefunctions, as required by the analysis of
[
90
]
. This enhanced decay also takes place in the case of cluster integrable systems
[
79
]
. It would be very interesting to clarify this issue in the comparatively simpler case of quantized SW curves.

Perhaps the most pressing problem open by our results is how to make contact with ordinary quantum mechanics. As we have explained, our deformation reduces the problem to understanding the exact NS free energy near the AD points of moduli space. This is not straightforward. However, the fact that TBA formulations of quantization conditions exist for some quantum-mechanical Hamiltonians
[
31
,
32
,
42
]
suggests that an analytic determination of this scaling regime is under reach. Such a determination, combined with the limit of our quantization conditions, would provide an exact, resummation-free solution of the anharmonic
oscillator.
