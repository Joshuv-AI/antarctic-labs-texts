The nonlinear Schrödinger equation with sprinkled nonlinearity
Benjamin Harrop-Griffiths, Rowan Killip, Monica Vişan

Abstract
. We prove global well-posedness for the cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation with nonlinearity concentrated on a homogeneous Poisson process.



1.
Introduction

We consider the dynamics of a field
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
under the Hamiltonian

(1.1)

\displaystyle E[\psi]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi(x)|^{4}\,d\mu(x),

where
\mu
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}
. This leads to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

(NLS
μ
)

\displaystyle i\psi_{t}=-\psi_{xx}+2|\psi|^{2}\psi\,d\mu.

This model is relevant in physical scenarios where the wave self-interaction is mediated by the environment. The measure
\mu
captures the strength of these effects and how they vary in space due to variations in the ambient matter. In optics, for example, nonlinear effects originate from the interaction of light with the atoms of the matter through which it is traveling
[
4
]
.

As the atomic scale is significantly smaller than the wavelength of visible light, one may hope that homogenization effects allow one to treat the light-matter interaction as spatially homogeneous and so take
d\mu
as a multiple of Lebesgue measure. Indeed, one may interpret the evident successes in modeling optical experiments with this homogeneous NLS (see
[
8
]
) as compelling evidence that the presumption of homogenization was justified.

The quest to better understand such homogenization phenomena is one of our motivations for introducing the sprinkled nonlinearity model in this paper. In this model,
\mu
is random and distributed according to a Poisson process (we will elaborate on this later). We contend that this model is physically natural; moreover, it is also consciously adversarial to naive notions of homogenization. Nevertheless, the question of homogenization of the sprinkled nonlinearity has been successfully treated in the subsequent and very recent paper
[
9
]
.

The most obvious anathema to the traditional homogeneous NLS (in which
\mu
is Lebesgue measure) is to choose
\mu
as a single point mass. A substantial literature on this model has been developed in both mathematics and physics. For a thorough account of the mathematical literature on this point-concentrated nonlinearity, we recommend the recent review
[
32
]
. Such highly concentrated nonlinearities arise as effective models in a number of physical situations, as described in

[
1
,
20
,
19
,
21
,
5
,
22
,
24
]
. Indeed, advances in nanoscale fabrication have led researchers to consider double barriers
[
15
]
, as well as periodic arrays of such nonlinearities
[
10
,
12
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
.

Rather than placing the centers of nonlinear interaction in a regular array, we are interested in sprinkling them in a completely random manner through the ambient material. The simplest physical model for such a sprinkling of points is the Gibbs state for a gas of non-interacting particles. The resulting distribution of points is known as a (homogeneous) Poisson process. This is also the natural model for dilute impurities in a crystal (or raisins in a plum pudding). We will discuss these connections more fully in subsection
1.3
.

Given the locations for our point interactions, we build the measure
\mu
by placing a delta measure at each point. As there will only be finitely many points in each compact set, our measure
\mu
is locally finite, that is, it gives finite mass to any compact set. We write
\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{R})
for the set of locally finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}
. We make
\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{R})
a measurable space by endowing it with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}
induced by the vague topology, that is, the weakest topology under which
\mu\mapsto\int\phi\,d\mu
is continuous for every continuous function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
of compact support.

To describe a random configuration of point interactions, we must allow
\mu
to be random. This means choosing a probability space
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P})
and a (measurable) map
\mu:\Omega\to\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{R})
. In this setup, the (random) number of points in a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
is given by the random variable
\mu(K):\Omega\to[0,\infty)
defined by
\omega\mapsto\mu(K;\omega)
. Similarly, the expected (=average) number of points in the set
K
may be written variously as

\bbE\Bigl\{\mu(K)\Bigr\}=\int_{\Omega}\mu(K;\omega)\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\mathbbm{1}_{\mkern-2.0muK}(x)\,d\mu(x;\omega)\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\bbE\Bigl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\!\mathbbm{1}_{\mkern-2.0muK}(x)\,d\mu(x)\Bigr\}.

In general, we will suppress the dependence on
\omega
as in the first and last expressions.

The specific distribution of points we wish to consider is the homogeneous Poisson process:

Definition 1.1
.

A random measure
\mu:\Omega\to\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{R})
is (distributed as) the
homogeneous Poisson process of unit intensity
if it satisfies

(1.2)

\bbE\Bigl\{\exp\Bigl(-\textstyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\,d\mu(x)\Bigr)\Bigr\}=\exp\Bigl(-\textstyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}[1-e^{-\phi(x)}]\,dx\Bigr)\quad\text{for all}\quad\phi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}).

The functionals appearing on LHS(
1.2
) are known as Laplace functionals. They uniquely determine the distribution of the measure
\mu
; see
[
16
, Corollary 2.3]
.

This is a
Poisson
process because the number of points
\mu(K)
in any compact set
K
follows a Poisson distribution. The average number of points is
|K|
, the Lebesgue measure of
K
. More generally, for any collection of disjoint measurable sets
E_{j}
, the random variables
\mu(E_{j})
are
independent
Poisson random variables with means
|E_{j}|
.

The special role of Lebesgue measure in dictating the average number of points in any set makes our Poisson process
homogeneous
and of
unit intensity
. Homogeneity expresses the translation invariance of Lebesgue measure. One may alter the intensity by multiplying Lebesgue measure by a (positive) numerical factor. By employing scaling, however, we see that there is no loss of generality in selecting unit intensity. We have chosen the process to be homogeneous because we regard this as the most physical case. Nevertheless, our methods allow much flexibility in this regard, as will become evident in due course.

The main result of this paper is the demonstration of (almost-sure) well-posedness for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
NLSμ
) when the nonlinearity is concentrated on a Poisson process:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mu
be distributed according to the Poisson process (
1.2
). Then the equation (
NLSμ
) is almost surely globally well-posed in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Specifically,

(a)
Given any initial data
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}
, almost surely there is a unique
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
that satisfies

(1.3)

\psi(t)=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\psi_{0}-2i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)d\mu\Bigr]\,d\tau.

(b)
The energy (
1.1
) of the solution is both finite and conserved; so too is the mass

(1.4)

M[\psi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|^{2}\,dx.

(c)
For any sequence of initial data,
\psi_{n,0}\to\psi_{0}
in
H^{1}
, the corresponding solutions
\psi_{n}\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
, which exist almost surely, converge to
\psi
in
L^{p}\bigl(d\mathbb{P};C([-T,T];H^{1})\bigr)\mkern-5.0mu:

(1.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bbE\Bigl[\,\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{p}\Bigr]=0,

for any time
T>0
and any
1\leq p<\infty
.

We say that a
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
satisfying (
1.3
) is a
strong solution
to (
NLSμ
) with initial data
\psi_{0}
. Note that RHS(
1.3
) will, almost surely, constitute a tempered distribution for any
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
. The crux of this is the convergence of

(1.6)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\overline{e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi(x)}|\psi(\tau,x)|^{2}\psi(\tau,x)\,d\mu(x)\,d\tau

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
; indeed,
\psi(\tau,x)
is uniformly bounded on the region of integration and
e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi
belongs to Schwartz space, which ensures sufficient decay to integrate
d\mu
.

On the other hand, it is far from clear that RHS(
1.3
) belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, as claimed in Theorem
1.2
. There is, of course, the question of regularity; this appears already in the case of a point nonlinearity. The more poignant problem here is decay: For almost every
\mu
there is a choice of
\phi\in H^{1}
and
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
so that (
1.6
) diverges for every
t>0
!

The origins of such divergence can be seen more easily at the level of the energy (
1.1
). For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the random variables
\mu\bigl([k,k+1)\bigr)
are independent and Poisson-distributed with unit mean. In particular, the probability that there are
n
many points in any unit interval
[k,k+1)
is
e^{-1}/n!
. The Borel–Cantelli lemma then ensures that, almost surely, for all
n\geq 1
we have
\mu\bigl([k,k+1)\bigr)\geq n
for infinitely many choices of
k
. As a consequence, for almost every sample
\mu
of the Poisson process there exists a sequence of distinct integers
x_{n}
with the property that

(1.7)

\mu\bigl([x_{n},x_{n}+1)\bigr)\geq n\quad\text{for each}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

It is then elementary to choose a
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, comprised of bumps (centered on these intervals) with slowly decaying amplitudes, so that
\psi_{0}
has infinite energy. This divergence shows that the (zero probability) set of exceptional measures
\mu
that is excluded in Theorem
1.2
must depend on
\psi_{0}
.

Earlier, we described highly concentrated nonlinearities as representative of the case where the characteristic scale of the nonlinearity is much much narrower than that of the wave. This can be made rigorous by considering a family of models with mollified nonlinearities and showing that their solutions converge to those of (
NLSμ
). Just such a result was proved for finite combinations of point interactions in
[
6
,
18
]
. We wish to do the same for our model. To this end, we define the absolutely continuous measure
\mu^{\varepsilon}
with density (Radon–Nikodym derivative)

(1.8)

\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,d\mu(y),\quad\text{where}\quad\rho^{\varepsilon}(x)=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)

and
\rho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(-1,1)
is non-negative, even, and satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)\,dx=1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\mu
be distributed according to the Poisson process (
1.2
). Then, almost surely, the solutions
\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)
, associated to the mollified measures
\mu^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
1.8
), converge to the solution
\psi(t)
to (
NLSμ
); indeed,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bbE\Bigl[\,\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{p}\Bigr]=0,

for any time
T>0
and any
1\leq p<\infty
.

If
\mu
were a finite measure, then each
\mu^{\varepsilon}
would have a bounded density and correspondingly, the well-posedness of the regularized model would be covered by the standard arguments described in
[
7
]
. For the Poisson process, however, the mollification procedure does nothing to reduce the impact of the sequences of ‘rich’ intervals such as the intervals
[x_{n},x_{n}+1)
described in (
1.7
). In this way, the model with mollified coupling measure retains many of the original difficulties and this compels us to develop a unified well-posedness theory. This theory will be deterministic and can be applied to very general measures
\mu
; see Section
3
.

As we have explained, our archenemy in this work is the inevitable, but extremely erratic, appearance of rich clusters of points in samples of the Poisson process. It is crucial to control the wave’s interaction with these clusters. For this purpose, we introduce a carefully chosen weighted
L^{2}
norm that conforms to the highs and lows of the process.

Given a positive measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
and an integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}
we define

(1.10)

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}=4+\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl[\mu(I_{\ell})^{2}-|k-\ell|\Bigr],\quad\text{where}\quad I_{\ell}=[\ell-\tfrac{1}{2},\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}).

This function provides a slowly varying approximation to
\mu(I_{k})
in which rich intervals have a correspondingly wide range of influence. We then define an associated weight via linear interpolation:

(1.11)

w(x;\mu)=(1+k-x)\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}+(x-k)\mathcal{N}_{k+1}(\mu)^{2}\quad\text{for $x\in[k,k+1)$}.

Finally, we define
{L_{\mu}^{2}}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};w(x;\mu)\,dx)
, that is, the Hilbert space with norm

\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}\,w(x;\mu)\,dx.

Naturally, we will only consider measures
\mu
for which
w(x;\mu)
is finite. As we will show in Lemma
2.1
, this is guaranteed by the single condition
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
. Lemma
4.1
then ensures that
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)
is almost surely finite for samples from the Poisson process.

The engine of our paper is the following entirely deterministic well-posedness result:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mu
be a positive Borel measure for which
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
. Then (
NLSμ
) is globally well-posed in
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
.

Precisely, given any initial data
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
there exists a unique global strong solution
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
of (
NLSμ
) satisfying
\psi(0)=\psi_{0}
. This solution conserves both the mass and the energy. Moreover, for any time
T>0
the solution map
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}\ni\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi\in C([-T,T];H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
is continuous.

This result is used to prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
4
. Indeed, Lemma
4.1
shows that any fixed
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
belongs to
L^{2}_{\mu}
for almost every sample
\mu
from the Poisson process (
1.2
). The arguments used to prove Theorem
1.4
also yield a deterministic analogue of Theorem
1.3
; see Corollary
3.7
. This constitutes the basis for the proof of Theorem
1.3
in Section
4
.

1.1.
Outline of the proof

We first focus on the deterministic result Theorem
1.4
. As Lemma
4.1
demonstrates, the restriction
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
is satisfied by samples from the Poisson process; consequently, we must accept that there exist unit intervals with arbitrarily large
\mu
measure. On the other hand, the condition that
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}_{\mu}
ensures that initially, at least, the waveform is not strongly concentrated on such rich clusters. In particular, the energy is initially finite; see Lemma
2.3
.

To proceed, we must confront the natural (and inevitable) spreading of dispersive waves into such rich regions. Indeed, a concentrated nonlinearity naturally creates high frequency waves and these, we fear, may carry the mass to regions where
\mu
is large. While we believe such transportation does take place, we will ultimately be able to control it in a satisfactory manner by proving
L^{2}_{\mu}
bounds.

For nonlinear Schrödinger equations, the transportation of
L^{2}
norm is governed by the mass current (= momentum density), which is given by
2\operatorname{Im}[\;\!\overline{\psi}\psi^{\prime}\;\!](x)
. This we will control using the conservation of energy.

Energy conservation is, of course, a property of true solutions, which is precisely what we are trying to construct! It is not a property of Picard iterates, for example. In fact, construction of solutions via Picard iteration is problematic: one needs
H^{1}
bounds on the iterates to control transport and one needs transport bounds to control
H^{1}
growth. Moreover, there is no easy way to exhibit the smallness needed to close a bootstrap argument. One may make the time interval small, but how short it needs to be is delicately entwined with the spreading of the Picard iterates into rich intervals.

Instead, we will construct solutions in the style of an energy method. The first step is to regularize the model, not solely in the sense of (
1.8
), but also by limiting the existence of rich clusters. This we do with a spatial truncation:

(1.12)

V^{\varepsilon}(x)=\varphi^{\varepsilon}(x)\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,(x)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{where}\quad\varphi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\varphi(\varepsilon x)

and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(-2,2)
is a non-negative bump function that is identically
1
on
[-1,1]
.
The resulting model is easily solved by contraction mapping; see Proposition
3.2
.

We then show that the sequence of solutions to this regularized model converge as
\varepsilon\to 0
, at least on short time intervals, and moreover, the limit
\psi(t,x)
is an
L^{\infty}_{t}H^{1}_{x}
solution to (
NLSμ
); see Proposition
3.4
. At this moment in the argument, we know that
t\mapsto\psi(t)
is weakly
H^{1}
-continuous, but we do not know that it is norm continuous. Nevertheless, Proposition
3.1
guarantees uniqueness of such a solution.

To prove well-posedness of (
NLSμ
), we must show that the limiting solution
\psi
belongs to
C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
and demonstrate continuity of the data-to-solution map. We start by proving that
\psi
conserves both mass and energy. This is then used to demonstrate that
\psi
is a global in time solution and that it belongs to
C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
; see Proposition
3.5
. Finally, the proof of Theorem
1.4
is completed with Proposition
3.6
, which shows that the data-to-solution map
\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi(t)
is
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
-continuous. In this way, the deterministic analysis is completed in Section
3
.

Section
4
is devoted to proving our main results about the stochastic model, namely, Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. Lemma
4.1
shows that our deterministic Theorem
1.4
is almost surely applicable for each initial data
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}
. This leaves us to verify the convergence claims (
1.5
) and (
1.9
). Our earlier results already yield almost sure convergence. We deduce
L^{p}(d\mathbb{P})
convergence by exhibiting a suitable dominating function and applying the dominated convergence theorem.

1.2.
Summary of existing literature

To the best of our knowledge, the model discussed in this paper has not been considered previously. It synthesizes two facets of the theory that have been considered before: the coupling constant is given by a stationary process and is concentrated on a zero-dimensional set.

Nonlinear Schrödinger equations with an inhomogeneous coupling constant in front of the nonlinearity arise very naturally in many physical contexts. This has led to a great many mathematical investigations of this case, too numerous to recount here. However, the literature on this subject (that we know of) has centered on the case of a single localized perturbation of the coupling constant. In an experimental setting, this corresponds to a carefully engineered apparatus.
By comparison, the paper
[
25
]
stands out to us for treating coupling constants given by certain stationary stochastic processes, including periodic, quasiperiodic, and alloy-type models. Concretely,
[
25
]
proves homogenization to the case of constant coupling constant in the mass-critical two-dimensional setting.

As noted earlier, nonlinear Schrödinger equations with nonlinearity concentrated at a single point (or at finitely many points) have generated considerable interest in both mathematics and physics; see the recent review
[
32
]
. The quantity of literature on these models is so great that we will confine our discussion to the cubic model in one dimension and focus only on well-posedness questions.

A cubic nonlinearity with a single delta measure at
x=0
as coupling constant

(NLS
δ
)

\displaystyle i\psi_{t}=-\psi_{xx}\pm 2\delta|\psi|^{2}\psi

is a mass-critical model. This is the observation that the scaling

\psi(t,x)\mapsto\sqrt{\lambda}\psi(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)

that preserves the class of solutions also preserves the (conserved) mass (
1.4
). As we will describe, the known phenomenology of (
NLSδ
) mimics that of the mass-critical homogenous equation.

Local existence for (
NLSδ
) with initial data in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is known and such solutions were shown to be global in the case of small initial data; see
[
11
]
. As observed in
[
13
]
, the model (
NLSδ
) is pseudoconformally invariant. This transformation yields explicit blow-up solutions in the focusing case.

Most researchers have discussed this problem for initial data
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}
. For such data, the defocusing (
NLSδ
) was shown to be globally well-posed in
[
3
,
18
]
. In the focusing case, there exists an explicit mass threshold so that (
NLSδ
) is globally well-posed in
H^{1}
below this threshold and ill-posed above it; see
[
13
]
. For either sign of the nonlinearity, small solutions scatter; see
[
2
, §2]
.

As noted earlier in the context of Theorem
1.3
, the papers
[
6
,
18
]
have already demonstrated the validity of the
\delta
interaction as describing the limit of less singular nonlinearities.

1.3.
Point processes

The purpose of this section is to provide additional background on possible choices for the distribution of the concentrated nonlinearities. In particular, we will describe two physical origins of the Poisson process, as well as its connection to other models.

The statistics of a gas of a fixed number of particles in finite volume is described by the canonical ensemble
[
27
,
14
]
. Let us consider
n
non-interacting particles in the finite interval
\Lambda=[-L,L]
. In this case, the Laplace functionals of the particle positions
X_{j}
can be easily evaluated: for any
\phi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
,

(1.13)

\displaystyle\bbE_{n,\Lambda}\Bigl\{\exp\Bigl(-{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\phi(X_{j})\Bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{n}}\int_{\Lambda^{n}}\exp\Bigl(-{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\phi(x_{j})\Bigr)\,dx_{1}\ldots\,dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\biggl(1+\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\int_{\Lambda}\bigl[e^{-\phi(x)}-1\bigr]\,dx\biggr)^{n}.

This shows that the
X_{j}
are just
n
statistically independent samples from the uniform distribution on
\Lambda
. (There is no temperature dependence because there is no interaction.)

The Poisson process (
1.2
) arises naturally in the thermodynamic limit of this model. To take the thermodynamic limit, we must send both
n
and
L
to infinity in such a way that the density
n/|\Lambda|
converges. Specifically, taking this limit with
n/|\Lambda|\to 1
, we see that the Laplace functionals (
1.13
) converge to those of the Poisson process (
1.2
). This in turn guarantees convergence to the Poisson process (
1.2
); see
[
16
, §4.2]
.

As noted earlier, the investigation of electron and photon dynamics in periodic structures have led a number of researchers to consider the model (
NLSμ
) with

(1.14)

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta(x-k)\,dx;

see
[
10
,
12
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. This has come to be known as the nonlinear Kronig–Penney model. We are not aware of any prior discussion of the well-posedness problem for this model; however, it fits easily within the scope of Theorem
1.4
. Indeed, the absence of randomness and of rich intervals (in the sense of (
1.7
)) make this model rather easier to treat than the general case we consider in this paper.

Earlier in the introduction we mentioned that the Poisson process also arises as the natural model of dilute impurities in a crystal. This can be understood as a variation on (
1.14
). Specifically, consider

(1.15)

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}Z_{k}\delta(x-kh)\,dx

where
h>0
is the atomic spacing and
Z_{k}
are independent Bernoulli random variables. We choose
Z_{k}=1
with probability
p
, which represents the presence of the impurity; with probability
1-p
there is no impurity and
Z_{k}=0
. The Laplace functionals for this model are easily evaluated: for any
\phi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
,

(1.16)

\displaystyle\bbE_{p,h}\Bigl\{\exp\Bigl(-{\textstyle\int}\phi(x)\,d\mu(x)\Bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\biggl(1+p[e^{-\phi(hk)}-1]\biggr).

For any fixed
h>0
and
p\in[0,1]
, the model described by (
1.15
) lies within the scope of Theorem
1.4
. However, the fundamental rationale for the concentrated nonlinearity is that the atomic scale is much smaller than the characteristic scale of the wave
\psi
; correspondingly, we must send
h\to 0
. As
h\to 0
, we send
p\to 0
in such a way that
p/h\to 1
; this ensures a limiting unit density of impurities. In this regime, it is easy to verify that RHS(
1.16
) converges to RHS(
1.2
). In this way, we see that the Poisson process also provides the natural description of low-density impurities in a crystal in the regime relevant to us.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper,
\lesssim
and
\gtrsim
indicate bounds that hold up to a multiplicative constant whose exact value is of no consequence. We abbreviate
A\lesssim B\lesssim A
as
A\simeq B
. We write
C(\mathbb{R})
for the space of bounded continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
.

For all
s>\frac{1}{2}
we have the Sobolev embedding
H^{s}\hookrightarrow C(\mathbb{R})
, as demonstrated by the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality

(2.1)

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{1-\frac{1}{2s}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{1}{2s}}.

As we consider finite-energy solutions, we will typically use the case
s=1
. The principal properties of the space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
that we employ are the following:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{H^{1}}+\|f\|_{H^{1}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}}\|g\|_{H^{1}}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{-1}}\|g\|_{H^{1}}.

The first estimate is a direct consequence of the product rule. The bound (
2.3
) expresses the algebra property of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and follows from (
2.2
) and (
2.1
). The final estimate (
2.4
) is implied by (
2.3
) and the realization of
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
as the dual of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
through the usual
L^{2}
-paring.

We fix a non-negative function
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(-1,1)
so that
\chi_{k}(x)=\chi(x-k)
satisfies
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\chi_{k}(x)=1
. One may readily verify that

\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\simeq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\|f\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\simeq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Using complex interpolation, it follows that

(2.5)

\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\simeq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

for all
0\leq s\leq 1
.

We now turn to the properties of the space
{L_{\mu}^{2}}
. Recalling the definitions (
1.10
) and (
1.11
), we have the following:

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\mu
is a positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}
for which
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
, then
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)<\infty
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and we have the estimates

(2.6)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}-\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(\mu)^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq|k-\ell|

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all $k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}$},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mu(I_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(\mu)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)

\displaystyle\quad\text{whenever $|k-\ell|\leq 3$}.

The corresponding weight
w
is a
1
-Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying the estimate

(2.8)

|w(x;\mu)|\leq\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)^{2}+|x|,

and we have the equivalent norm

(2.9)

\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}\simeq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

From the estimate

\Bigl|\bigl[\mu(I_{\ell})^{2}-|k-\ell|\bigr]-\bigl[\mu(I_{\ell})^{2}-|k+1-\ell|\bigr]\Bigr|\leq 1,

we see that if one of
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)
,
\mathcal{N}_{k+1}(\mu)
is finite, then the other is also finite and we may bound

|\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}-\mathcal{N}_{k+1}(\mu)^{2}|\leq 1.

By induction on
|k|
, if
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
then we have
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)<\infty
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and the estimate (
2.6
) holds.

The inequality in (
2.7
) follows directly from the definition (
1.10
). For the equivalence, we note that (
2.6
) and the fact that
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}\geq 4
ensures that if
|k-\ell|\leq 3
then

|\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}-\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(\mu)^{2}|\leq 3\leq\tfrac{3}{4}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}.

Next, by (
2.6
) and the definition (
1.11
), we see that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(2.10)

|w(x;\mu)-w(y;\mu)|\leq|x-y|,

and hence
w
is
1
-Lipschitz. The estimate (
2.8
) then follows from the fact that
w(0;\mu)=\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)^{2}
. Moreover, if
x\in\supp\chi_{k}\subseteq(k-1,k+1)
then (
2.10
) yields

|w(x;\mu)-\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}|=|w(x;\mu)-w(k;\mu)|\leq 1\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}.

Using this, (
2.9
) follows from (
2.5
).
∎

Next we discuss the relationship between
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)
and the mollified measure
\mu^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
1.8
):

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\mu
is a positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}
with
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
, then the function
\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and we have the estimate

(2.11)

\displaystyle\bigl\|\chi_{k}\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu),

uniformly for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, as well as the estimate

(2.12)

\|\chi_{k}(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon})\|_{H^{-1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\eta}\,\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu),

uniformly for
0\leq\varepsilon<\eta\leq 1
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
,
\mu
is a locally finite Borel measure and so
\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

To prove the estimates (
2.11
) and (
2.12
), we argue by duality. If
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
and
\phi\in L^{\infty}
then the function
\rho^{\varepsilon}*(\chi_{k}\phi)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(k-2,k+2)
satisfies

\|\rho^{\varepsilon}*(\chi_{k}\phi)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}.

We may then apply (
2.7
) to obtain

|\langle\chi_{k}d\mu^{\varepsilon},\phi\rangle|=\left|\int_{k-2}^{k+2}\rho^{\varepsilon}*(\chi_{k}\phi)\,d\mu\right|\lesssim\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\|\rho^{\varepsilon}*(\chi_{k}\phi)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}},

which gives us (
2.11
).

For (
2.12
) we take
\phi\in H^{1}
and apply Plancherel’s Theorem followed by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to obtain

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\chi_{k}\bigl(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon}\bigr),\phi\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{k-2}^{k+2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl[\rho^{\eta}(x-y)-\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-y)\bigr]\phi(x)\chi_{k}(x)\,dx\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{k-2}^{k+2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\eta}\overline{{\widehat{\rho}{\mkern 3.0mu}}^{\prime}(h\xi)}e^{iy\xi}\xi\,\widehat{(\phi\chi_{k})}(\xi)\,dh\,d\xi\,d\mu(y).

We then apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, (
2.7
), and then (
2.3
) to bound

\bigl|\bigl\langle\chi_{k}\bigl(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon}\bigr),\phi\bigr\rangle\bigr|\lesssim\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\int_{\varepsilon}^{\eta}\|{\widehat{\rho}{\mkern 3.0mu}}^{\prime}(h\xi)\|_{L_{\xi}^{2}}\|\xi\widehat{(\phi\chi_{k})}\|_{L_{\xi}^{2}}\,dh\lesssim\sqrt{\eta}{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\|\phi\|_{H^{1}}.\qed

We conclude this section with several nonlinear estimates involving the doubly mollified density
V^{\varepsilon}
that was introduced in (
1.12
):

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mu
be a locally finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

If
\frac{2}{3}\leq s\leq 1
and
f_{j}\in H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
, then we have the estimate

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|f_{j}|\,d\mu+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|f_{j}|\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|f_{j}|\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{4}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{3}{4}},

uniformly for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, as well as

(2.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\,d\mu=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}.

If
f_{j}\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
and
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
, then

(2.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{1}f_{2}\,g|\,d\mu+\bigl\|f_{1}f_{2}\,g\,\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|f_{1}f_{2}\,g\,d\mu\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}},

uniformly for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
.

Finally, if
f\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
and
0<\varepsilon\leq\eta\leq 1
, then

(2.17)

\||f|^{2}f(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon})\|_{H^{-1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\eta}\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|f\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Proof.

Given
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
f\in H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
, we apply the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality (
2.1
) followed by Hölder’s inequality and then the equivalent norms (
2.5
) and (
2.9
) to estimate

(2.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2-\frac{1}{s}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2-\frac{1}{s}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2-\frac{1}{s}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{1}{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2-\frac{1}{s}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{1}{s}}.

Arguing similarly, if
\frac{2}{3}\leq s\leq 1
we may bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{4-\frac{2}{s}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{2}{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{H^{s}}^{3}

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)^{2}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{H^{s}}^{6}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{3}.

Turning to the estimate (
2.13
), we first note that by Sobolev embedding we have
f_{j}\in C(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, for any
k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|\,d\mu
is well-defined. Using that
\supp\chi_{k_{j}}\subseteq(k_{j}-1,k_{j}+1)
, we may then apply (
2.7
) followed by Hölder’s inequality and (
2.19
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|\,d\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}}(\mu)^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{4}\left[\sum_{k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}}(\mu)\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}\!\!\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{4}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{3}{4}}.

Applying the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem, we may then bound

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|\,d\mu\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{4}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{3}{4}},

as claimed. For the second and third terms on
LHS
(
2.13
), we first use (
2.11
) and then (
2.7
) to estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|V^{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4},k_{5}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\bigl\|\chi_{k_{5}}\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr\|_{L^{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}}(\mu)^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}},

and the proof is then completed as for the first term.

Next we prove the first equality in (
2.14
); the second follows by the same argument. We take
K>1
to be an integer and estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}|\leq K,\ |k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}|>K,\ |k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}||V^{\varepsilon}|\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|\,d\mu\right].

As
\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, for any fixed
k_{1},\ldots,k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\,d\mu.

Moreover, from (
2.13
) we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}|>K,|k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}||V^{\varepsilon}|\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}|\,d\mu\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}|>K,|k_{i}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{3}{4}}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{4}\left[\sum_{|k_{j}|\geq K}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{3}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Taking
\varphi^{\frac{2}{K-1}}(x)=\varphi(\frac{2}{K-1}x)
to be the smooth cutoff function discussed in (
1.12
), we observe that
1-\varphi^{\frac{2}{K-1}}
is identically
1
for
|x|\geq K-1
. As a consequence, we may replace
f_{j}
by
(1-\varphi^{\frac{2}{K-1}})f_{j}
and then apply Hölder’s inequality with (
2.5
) and (
2.9
) to obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{4}\left[\sum_{|k_{j}|\geq K}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|\chi_{k_{j}}f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{3}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}\lesssim\prod_{j=1}^{4}\|(1-\varphi^{\frac{2}{K-1}})f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|(1-\varphi^{\frac{2}{K-1}})f_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{\frac{3}{4}}.

The estimate (
2.14
) now follows from first taking
\varepsilon\to 0
and then
K\to\infty
.

The estimate (
2.15
) is proved similarly to (
2.13
). For the first term on
LHS
(
2.15
) we apply (
2.7
) followed by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
2.18
) with
s=1
to bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\chi_{k_{1}}f_{1}||\chi_{k_{2}}f_{2}||g|\,d\mu

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}-k_{2}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{k_{1}}\mathcal{N}_{k_{2}}}\|\chi_{k_{1}}f_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\chi_{k_{2}}f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For the second term on
LHS
(
2.15
) we use (
2.11
) and (
2.7
) to estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\chi_{k_{1}}f_{1}||\chi_{k_{2}}f_{2}||g|\bigl|\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr|\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\\
|k_{j}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\bigl\|\chi_{k_{3}}\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr\|_{L^{1}}\|\chi_{k_{1}}f_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\chi_{k_{2}}f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}-k_{2}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{k_{1}}\mathcal{N}_{k_{2}}}\|\chi_{k_{1}}f_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\chi_{k_{2}}f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}},

and then proceed as for the first term.

For (
2.16
) we take
\phi\in H^{1}
and apply (
2.15
) followed by (
2.1
) to bound

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle\phi,f_{1}f_{2}g\,d\mu\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The estimate then follows by duality.

Finally, we apply the product estimates (
2.4
) and (
2.2
), followed by (
2.12
), (
2.7
), and finally (
2.18
) to estimate

\displaystyle\||f|^{2}f(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon})\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{j}-k_{\ell}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\|\chi_{k_{1}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\chi_{k_{2}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\chi_{k_{3}}f\|_{H^{1}}\|\chi_{k_{4}}(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon})\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|k_{1}-k_{2}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{k_{1}}\mathcal{N}_{k_{2}}}\|\chi_{k_{1}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\chi_{k_{2}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\eta}\,\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|f\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

which yields (
2.17
).
∎

3.
Deterministic well-posedness

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
. We assume throughout that
\mu
is a fixed Borel measure for which
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
. We denote the space
H^{1}
endowed with the weak topology by
H_{w}^{1}
.

We start by proving a Lipschitz estimate for the solution map of (
NLSμ
), which yields uniqueness as an immediate corollary:

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that for some
T>0
there are two solutions
\psi,\phi\in C([-T,T];H_{w}^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
of the Duhamel formulation (
1.3
) of (
NLSμ
). Then for all times
|t|\leq T
we have the estimate

(3.1)

\|\psi(t)-\phi(t)\|_{H^{-1}}\leq Ce^{CK^{2}(1+K^{2})|t|^{2}}\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}},

where
C>0
is some absolute constant and

(3.2)

K=\sup_{|t|\leq T}\Bigl[\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}\|\psi\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}+\|\phi\|_{H^{1}}\|\phi\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}\Bigr].

Proof.

Using the Duhamel formula (
1.3
) we may write the difference as

\psi(t)-\phi(t)=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\bigl[\psi(0)-\phi(0)\bigr]-2i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[\bigl(|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)-|\phi(\tau)|^{2}\phi(\tau)\bigr)d\mu\Bigr]\,d\tau.

To apply a Gronwall’s inequality-type argument, we must estimate the difference
\psi-\phi
in the same norm on both the left and right hand sides of this identity. As we will see, the
L^{\infty}
norm is very convenient for this; correspondingly, we will first prove the Lipschitz-type bound in this norm; see (
3.7
). The
H^{-1}
-bound (
3.1
) that we need later in our argument will be deduced as a consequence of (
3.7
).

For the first term, we apply (
2.1
) to bound

(3.3)

\bigl\|e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\bigl[\psi(0)-\phi(0)\bigr]\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\bigl\|e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\bigl[\psi(0)-\phi(0)\bigr]\bigr\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}.

For the second term, we take a test function
f\in L^{1}
so that for
\tau\neq t
we have
e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}f\in C(\mathbb{R})
with the dispersive estimate

(3.4)

\|e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{|t-\tau|}}\|f\|_{L^{1}}.

Applying (
2.15
), for
0\leq\tau<t\leq T
we may then bound

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle f,e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[\bigl(|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)-|\phi(\tau)|^{2}\phi(\tau)\bigr)d\mu\Bigr]\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}f}\bigl(|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)-|\phi(\tau)|^{2}\phi(\tau)\bigr)\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\Bigl[\|\psi(\tau)\|_{H^{1}}\|\psi(\tau)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}+\|\phi(\tau)\|_{H^{1}}\|\phi(\tau)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\Bigr]\Bigl\|\bigl(\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\bigr)\overline{e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}f}\Bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{t-\tau}}K\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{L^{1}},

where
K
is defined as in (
3.2
). By duality, we then have

(3.5)

\Bigl\|e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[\bigl(|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)-|\phi(\tau)|^{2}\phi(\tau)\bigr)d\mu\Bigr]\Bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{t-\tau}}K\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Combining (
3.3
), (
3.5
), and then applying Hölder’s inequality, for
0<t\leq T
we may bound

\displaystyle\|\psi(t)-\phi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}+K\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{t-\tau}}\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}+K\left[\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{2}{3}}\,d\tau\right]^{\frac{3}{4}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\,d\tau\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}+\left[K^{4}t\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\,d\tau\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Taking the forth power, we arrive at

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|\psi(t)-\phi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}^{4}+K^{4}t\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\,d\tau.

This is amenable to the classical form of Gronwall’s inequality, yielding

(3.7)

\|\psi(t)-\phi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim e^{CK^{4}t^{2}}\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Finally, we apply (
2.16
) to bound

\displaystyle\|\psi(t)-\phi(t)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{-1}}+\!\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\|\bigl(|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)-|\phi(\tau)|^{2}\phi(\tau)\bigr)d\mu\bigr\|_{H^{-1}}d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}}+K\!\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi(\tau)-\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{\infty}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl[1+Kte^{CK^{4}t^{2}}\Bigr]\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{H^{1}},

which yields (
3.1
) for times
0<t\leq T
. The corresponding estimate for times
-T\leq t<0
follows from a parallel argument.
∎

Next, we turn to the problem of existence. Taking
V^{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
to be defined as in (
1.12
), we consider solutions
\psi^{\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
of the regularized equation,

(NLS
ε
)

\displaystyle i\psi_{t}^{\varepsilon}=-\psi_{xx}^{\varepsilon}+2V^{\varepsilon}|\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\psi^{\varepsilon}.

The proof of the following proposition can be found in
[
7
]
, with the exception of the final statement that follows from the argument of
[
33
]
:

Proposition 3.2
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
the equation (
NLSε
) is globally well-posed in
H^{1}
.
More precisely, for all initial data
\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\in H^{1}
there exists a unique solution
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
of the Duhamel formulation

\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)-2i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[V^{\varepsilon}|\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\Bigr]\,d\tau

of (
NLSε
). This solution conserves both the mass and the corresponding energy,

E^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi_{x}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{4}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx.

Moreover, for all times
T>0
the solution map
H^{1}\ni\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\mapsto\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C([-T,T];H^{1})
is locally Lipschitz. Finally, if
\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\in\mathcal{S}
then
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathcal{S})
.

We wish to prove that
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)
converges as
\varepsilon\to 0
. A key first step is to obtain bounds that are uniform for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
:

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
and
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
is the solution of (
NLSε
) with initial data
\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)=\psi_{0}
, then
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R};{L_{\mu}^{2}})
and there exists a constant
C>0
so that for all
T>0
we have the estimates

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl[1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\Bigr]\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}+CT\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\lambda T\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+CT\langle\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}\lambda\rangle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\Bigl[\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\Bigr],

uniformly for
0<\varepsilon,\lambda\leq 1
.

Proof.

Combining an approximation argument with the well-posedness provided by Proposition
3.2
, we see that it suffices to prove these bounds for
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
. As noted in Proposition
3.2
, this ensures that that the solution
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathcal{S})
.

By conservation of mass and energy,

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\lesssim\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigl[M[\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)]+E^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=M[\psi_{0}]+\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}E^{\varepsilon}[\psi_{0}].

Thus by (
2.13
), we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl[1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\Bigr]\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

which proves (
3.8
).

As
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\in\mathcal{S}
, the estimate (
2.8
) ensures that
\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}<\infty
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We may then integrate by parts to obtain

(3.12)

\tfrac{d}{dt}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}=-2\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\overline{\psi^{\varepsilon}}\psi_{x}^{\varepsilon}\bigr)_{x}\,w\,dx=2\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{\psi^{\varepsilon}}\psi_{x}^{\varepsilon}\,w_{x}\,dx,

and so, by (
2.10
),

\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}+2T\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}},

which proves (
3.9
). The estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) follow from a parallel argument, with
w
replaced by
(1-\varphi^{\lambda})^{2}
and
(1-\varphi^{\lambda})^{2}w
, respectively. In the second instance, we also use that

\bigl|[(1-\varphi^{\lambda})^{2}w]_{x}(x)\bigr|\lesssim(1-\varphi^{\lambda}(x))\bigl[1+\lambda|\varphi_{x}(\lambda x)||w(x)|\bigr]\lesssim\langle\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}\lambda\rangle(1-\varphi^{\lambda}(x)),

which follows from (
2.8
) and (
2.10
).

Given initial data
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
, the estimate (
3.9
) ensures that the solution
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})\cap L^{\infty}([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
. To complete the proof we have to verify that
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
. To this end, we take
t,\tau\in[-T,T]
,
0<\lambda\leq 1
, and use (
2.8
) to bound

\displaystyle\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi^{\lambda}[\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)]\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}+2\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl(\mathcal{N}_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\bigr)\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

As the map
\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)
is
L^{2}
-continuous, we deduce that

\limsup_{t\to\tau}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\lesssim\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

Employing (
3.8
) and (
3.10
) in (
3.11
), we send
\lambda\to 0
and obtain

\lim_{t\to\tau}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}=0,

which proves that
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
.
∎

Using these uniform estimates for
\psi^{\varepsilon}
, we now prove existence of local-in-time solutions to (
NLSμ
):

Proposition 3.4
.

Given
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
, there exists
T>0
and a solution
\psi\in C([-T,T];H_{w}^{1}\cap H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
, for every
0\leq s<1
, to the Duhamel formulation (
1.3
) of (
NLSμ
).
The solution
\psi(t)
conserves mass and satisfies the energy inequality

(3.13)

E\bigl[\psi(t)\bigr]\leq E\bigl[\psi_{0}\bigr],

for all
|t|\leq T
. Moreover, if
\psi^{\varepsilon}
is a family of solutions to (
NLSε
) with
\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\to\psi_{0}
in
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
, then

(3.14)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\ \bigl\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi(t)\bigr\|_{H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}=0\quad\text{for each}\quad 0\leq s<1.

Proof.

We will construct
\psi(t)
as a limit of solutions
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)
to (
NLSε
). For this purpose, it would suffice to consider solutions
\psi^{\varepsilon}
to (
NLSε
) with
\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)=\psi_{0}
, rather than with a sequence of initial data
\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\to\psi_{0}
in
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
. However, the extra generality we consider here will be useful when proving (
3.14
). It is no loss of generality to demand that

(3.15)

\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{H^{1}}\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+1\quad\text{and }\quad\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}+1.

Given
T>0
, we define the control quantities

A=\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

Recalling (
3.8
) and (
3.15
), we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle A^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl[1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\bigr]\bigl[1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\bigr]^{3},

and using (
3.9
) as well, we have

(3.17)

B^{2}\leq\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}+CTA^{2}\lesssim[1+T]\bigl[1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\bigr]^{2}\bigl[1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\bigr]^{3}.

Taking
0<\varepsilon\leq\eta\leq 1
, we use the Duhamel formula (
1.3
) to write

\displaystyle\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}[\psi^{\eta}(0)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)]+I+I\!I+I\!I\!I,

where

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=-2i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[\bigl(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon}\bigr)|\psi^{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\eta}(\tau)d\mu^{\eta}\Bigr]\,d\tau,

\displaystyle I\!I

\displaystyle:=-2i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[\varphi^{\varepsilon}|\psi^{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\eta}(\tau)\bigl(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon}\bigr)\Bigr]\,d\tau,

\displaystyle I\!I\!I

\displaystyle:=-2i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[\varphi^{\varepsilon}\bigl[|\psi^{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\eta}(\tau)-|\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\bigr]d\mu^{\varepsilon}\Bigr]\,d\tau.

Given dyadic
N\geq 1
we define the Littlewood–Paley projection

\widehat{P_{\leq N}f}(\xi)=\varphi\bigl(\tfrac{\xi}{N}\bigr)\widehat{f}(\xi),

where
\varphi
is the same bump function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(-2,2)
employed in (
1.12
). We also use the notation
P_{>N}=1-P_{\leq N}
. We begin by bounding the low frequency part of each of the terms
I,I\!I,I\!I\!I
in both
L^{\infty}
and
H^{s}
for
0\leq s<1
.

Using the dispersive estimate (
3.4
) and then (
2.15
), we bound the first term by

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{|t|\leq T}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{|t-\tau|}}\bigl\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon}\bigr)|\psi^{\eta}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\eta}(\tau)\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\eta}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr\|_{L^{1}}\,d\tau\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{T}AB\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Replacing the dispersive estimate by Bernstein’s inequality, we similarly obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{s+\frac{1}{2}}T\sup_{|t|\leq T}\bigl\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon}\bigr)|\psi^{\eta}(t)|^{2}\psi^{\eta}(t)\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\eta}\!\!}{dx}\,\bigr\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{s+\frac{1}{2}}TAB\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}},

for any
0\leq s<1
.

The estimates for the third term are essentially identical. Applying the dispersive estimate (
3.4
) followed by (
2.15
) we get

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\!I\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\sqrt{T}AB\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}},

while replacing the dispersive estimate by Bernstein’s inequality yields

\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\!I\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim N^{s+\frac{1}{2}}TAB\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

For the remaining term, we first apply (
2.17
) to bound

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim T\sup_{|t|\leq T}\bigl\||\psi^{\eta}(t)|^{2}\psi^{\eta}(t)\bigl(d\mu^{\eta}-d\mu^{\varepsilon}\bigr)\bigr\|_{H^{-1}}\lesssim TA^{2}B\sqrt{\eta}.

We may then use Bernstein’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{3}{2}}TA^{2}B\sqrt{\eta},

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{s+1}TA^{2}B\sqrt{\eta}.

We now combine our estimates at low frequencies with Bernstein’s inequality at high frequencies and (
2.1
) to bound

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\|\psi^{\eta}(0)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{H^{1}}+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{>N}\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{>N}\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}I\!I\!I\|_{L^{\infty}}

(3.18)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\|\psi^{\eta}(0)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{H^{1}}+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}A+\sqrt{T}AB\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+N^{\frac{3}{2}}TA^{2}B\sqrt{\eta}+\sqrt{T}AB\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

We now choose
T>0
sufficiently small (depending only on
\psi_{0}
) to ensure that

\sqrt{T}AB\ll 1,

and hence the final term on
RHS
(
3.18
) can be absorbed into the left-hand side. From (
2.1
) and (
3.10
), we may bound the third term on
RHS
(
3.18
) as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sup_{|t|\leq T}\biggl\{\Bigl[\|(1-\varphi^{\eta})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|(1-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Bigr]\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\biggr\}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\Bigl[\|(1-\varphi^{\eta})\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|(1-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\eta TA^{2}\Bigr]A^{2}.

This converges to zero as
\eta,\varepsilon\to 0
. As a consequence, we may pass to the limit in (
3.18
) to obtain

\limsup_{\eta,\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim N^{-\frac{1}{2}}A.

Taking the limit as
N\to\infty
, we see that
\psi^{\varepsilon}
converges in
C([-T,T];L^{\infty})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Let
\psi
denote this limit.

Arguing as in (
3.18
) and choosing
T
so that
\sqrt{T}AB\ll 1
, but using our
H^{s}
low frequency bounds in place of those in
L^{\infty}
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\psi^{\eta}(0)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{H^{s}}+N^{s-1}A

\displaystyle\quad+N^{s+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{T}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|(\varphi^{\eta}-\varphi^{\varepsilon})\psi^{\eta}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad+N^{s+1}\sqrt{T}A\sqrt{\eta}+N^{s+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{T}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Passing to the limit as
\eta,\varepsilon\to 0
and then
N\to\infty
now shows that
\psi^{\varepsilon}\to\psi
in
C([-T,T];H^{s})
for all
0\leq s<1
.

As the solutions
\psi^{\varepsilon}
conserve mass, we may pass to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
to obtain

\|\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}=\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\quad\text{for all $|t|\leq T$}.

In view of (
2.8
) and the
C([-T,T];L^{2})
convergence already proved,

\limsup_{\eta,\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\bigl\|\varphi^{\lambda}(\psi^{\eta}(t)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t))\bigr\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{2}\lesssim\Bigl(\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\Bigr)\limsup_{\eta,\varepsilon\to 0}\bigl\|\psi^{\eta}-\psi^{\varepsilon}\bigr\|_{C_{t}L^{2}}^{2}=0

for any fixed
\lambda>0
. Thus, in order to demonstrate that
\psi^{\varepsilon}\to\psi
in
C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
, it suffices to show that

(3.19)

\limsup_{\lambda\to 0}\ \sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\bigl\|[1-\varphi^{\lambda}]\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{2}=0.

To this end, we first employ (
3.10
) to obtain

\limsup_{\lambda\to 0}\ \sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\bigl\|[1-\varphi^{\lambda}]\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\limsup_{\lambda\to 0}\ \sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\bigl\|[1-\varphi^{\lambda}]\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0.

Using this in (
3.11
), we may then conclude that

\text{LHS\eqref{777}}\leq\limsup_{\lambda\to 0}\ \sup_{0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1}\bigl\|[1-\varphi^{\lambda}]\psi^{\varepsilon}(0)\bigr\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{2}=0.

Weak compactness ensures that for each time
t\in[-T,T]
we have weak convergence
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)\rightharpoonup\psi(t)
in
H^{1}
. As
\psi^{\varepsilon}\in C([-T,T];H^{1})
, it follows that
\psi\in C([-T,T];H_{w}^{1})
.

Fixing
\frac{2}{3}\leq s<1
, we use (
2.13
) to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{4}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|^{4}\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl[|\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{4}-|\psi|^{4}\Bigr]\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|^{4}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|^{4}\,d\mu\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\Bigl[\|\psi^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{3}+\|\psi\|_{H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{3}\Bigr]\|\psi^{\varepsilon}-\psi\|_{H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|^{4}\,V^{\varepsilon}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|^{4}\,d\mu\right|,

which then converges to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
by (
2.14
). As a consequence, for all
|t|\leq T
we have

(3.20)

\displaystyle E\bigl[\psi(t)\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)]=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E^{\varepsilon}\bigl[\psi_{0}\bigr]=E\bigl[\psi_{0}\bigr],

giving us the energy inequality (
3.13
).

To complete the proof, we have one final task: verifying that
\psi
is a solution to (
1.3
). For any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\|e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi\|_{H^{s}}=\|\phi\|_{H^{s}}\quad\text{and}\quad\||x|e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\||x|\phi\|_{L^{2}}+|t-\tau|\|\phi\|_{H^{1}};

the second estimate here is a consequence of the identity

xe^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi=e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}[x\phi+2i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}\phi].

In this way, (
2.8
) yields

\|e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi\|_{H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}\lesssim_{\phi}\mathcal{N}_{0}+\sqrt{|t-\tau|}.

We may then argue as in the proof of (
3.13
), using (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) to show that for all
|t|\leq T
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\langle\phi,\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[V^{\varepsilon}|\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\Bigr]\,d\tau\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi}\,V^{\varepsilon}|\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)|^{2}\psi^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\,dx\,d\tau

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{e^{-i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi}\,|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)\,d\mu\,d\tau

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\langle\phi,\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-\tau)\partial_{x}^{2}}\Bigl[|\psi(\tau)|^{2}\psi(\tau)d\mu\Bigr]\,d\tau\right\rangle,

so
\psi
satisfies (
1.3
) in the sense of (tempered) distributions.
∎

To complete the proof of the existence of solutions to (
NLSμ
) as stated in Theorem
1.4
, we must extend the local-in-time solution constructed in Proposition
3.4
to a global-in-time solution. We then combine the uniqueness result from Proposition
3.1
with the energy inequality (
3.13
) to improve the time continuity properties of our solution. Following this strategy, we will prove the following:

Proposition 3.5
.

Given
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
there exists a unique strong solution
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
of (
NLSμ
). This solution conserves both the mass and the energy. Moreover, if
\psi^{\varepsilon}
denote the solutions to (
NLSε
) with initial data
\psi_{0}
, then

(3.21)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\ \bigl\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi(t)\bigr\|_{H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}=0\quad\text{for each}\quad 0<T<\infty.

Proof.

We first note that uniqueness follows from Proposition
3.1
.

Turning to the problem of global existence, let
I\subseteq(0,\infty)
be the set of times
T>0
for which there exists a solution
\psi\in C([-T,T];H_{w}^{1}\cap H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
for all
0\leq s<1
of (
NLSμ
) with
\psi(0)=\psi_{0}
, which conserves mass and satisfies the energy inequality (
3.13
).

By definition,
I
is (relatively) closed. Proposition
3.4
shows that
I
is non-empty. Moreover, if
T\in I
then we may apply Proposition
3.4
with initial data
\psi(\pm T)\in H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
to show that
I
is open. As a consequence,
I=(0,\infty)
, yielding global existence.

Let us now suppose for a contradiction that there exists some time
T\neq 0
for which

E\bigl[\psi(T)\bigr]<E\bigl[\psi_{0}\bigr].

We may then define

\phi(t,x)=\psi(T+t,x)

to obtain a solution
\phi\in C(\mathbb{R};H_{w}^{1}\cap H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
for all
0\leq s<1
of (
NLSμ
) for which

E\bigl[\phi(-T)\bigr]>E\bigl[\phi(0)\bigr],

contradicting the fact that the unique solution of (
NLSμ
) with initial data
\phi(0)
satisfies the energy inequality (
3.13
). As a consequence, we must have

E\bigl[\psi(t)\bigr]=E\bigl[\psi_{0}\bigr]

for all times
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

From (
2.13
) and the fact that
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
for
\frac{2}{3}\leq s<1
, the map

t\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(t)|^{4}\,d\mu

is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
. Combining this with the conservation of energy we see that for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\left[M[\psi(t)]+2E[\psi(t)]-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(t)|^{4}\,d\mu\right]=\|\psi(t_{0})\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Consequently, the map
\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}
is continuous. As
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H_{w}^{1})
, by the Radon–Riesz theorem we then have
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
.

It remains only to prove (
3.21
). From Proposition
3.4
, we obtain convergence in a short time interval
[-T,T]
, but only in the weaker
C([-T,T];H^{s}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
norm. Nevertheless, this allows us to deduce weak convergence
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})\rightharpoonup\psi(t)
in
H^{1}
, whenever
t_{\varepsilon}\to t\in[-T,T]
. We will demonstrate how to use conservation of energy to deduce convergence in
C([-T,T];H^{1})
. This combination of Proposition
3.4
and upgraded convergence can then be iterated to cover any compact time interval.

If convergence of
\psi^{\varepsilon}
to
\psi
in
C([-T,T];H^{1})
were to fail, then (passing to a subsequence) we could find
t_{\varepsilon}\to t\in[-T,T]
such that

(3.22)

\displaystyle\inf_{\varepsilon}\bigl\|\psi^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})-\psi(t)\bigr\|_{H^{1}}>0.

It is important here that we have already showed that
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
.

By energy conservation and (
2.14
), we have

E[\psi(t)]=E[\psi_{0}]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}E^{\varepsilon}[\psi_{0}]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}E^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})].

Combining this with the convergence of the potential energies (see the proof of (
3.20
)), we deduce that the
H^{1}
norms of
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})
converge to that of
\psi(t)
. By the Radon–Riesz theorem and the weak convergence noted above, this implies norm convergence, contradicting (
3.22
).
∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.4
, it remains to prove that the solution map is continuous.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
\psi_{n,0}\to\psi_{0}
is a convergent sequence of
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
initial data with corresponding solutions
\psi_{n},\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
, then for any time
T>0
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}=0.

Proof.

Our starting point is Proposition
3.1
, which yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{-1}}=0.

Next, we upgrade this to convergence in
C([-T,T];H^{\frac{2}{3}})
. From the conservation of mass and energy followed by (
2.13
), we may bound

M[\psi_{n}(t)]+E\bigl[\psi_{n}(t)\bigr]=M[\psi_{n,0}]+E\bigl[\psi_{n,0}\bigr]\lesssim\Bigl[1+\|\psi_{n,0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\|\psi_{n,0}\|_{L^{2}}\Bigr]\|\psi_{n,0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

from which we obtain the uniform bound

(3.23)

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\Bigl[M[\psi_{n}(t)]+2E[\psi_{n}(t)]\Bigr]<\infty.

As a consequence, for dyadic
N\geq 1
we may bound

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{\frac{2}{3}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{\leq N}(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\|_{H^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{>N}\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{\frac{2}{3}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|P_{>N}\psi(t)\|_{H^{\frac{2}{3}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{5}{3}}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{-1}}+N^{-\frac{1}{3}}\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\quad+N^{-\frac{1}{3}}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}.

By first taking
n\to\infty
and then
N\to\infty
, we see that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
C([-T,T];H^{\frac{2}{3}})
.

We recall that a compact subset
Q\subseteq H^{-1}
is bounded and equicontinuous, and consequently

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\phi\in Q}\|P_{>N}\phi\|_{H^{-1}}=0;

see, e.g.,
[
17
,
26
]
. Mimicking our argument for convergence in
C([-T,T];H^{\frac{2}{3}})
, we see that for any compact subset
Q\subseteq H^{-1}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\sup_{\phi\in Q}|\langle\phi,\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{|t|\leq T}\sup_{\phi\in Q}|\langle\phi,P_{\leq N}(\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t))\rangle|+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\sup_{\phi\in Q}|\langle\phi,P_{>N}\psi_{n}(t)\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\sup_{\phi\in Q}|\langle\phi,P_{>N}\psi(t)\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim N^{2}\sup_{\phi\in Q}\|\phi\|_{H^{-1}}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sup_{\phi\in Q}\|P_{>N}\phi\|_{H^{-1}}\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}}+\sup_{\phi\in Q}\|P_{>N}\phi\|_{H^{-1}}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}},

so once again taking
n\to\infty
and then
N\to\infty
gives us

(3.24)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\sup_{|t|\leq T}\;\sup_{\phi\in Q}\;|\langle\phi,\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\rangle|=0.

We will return to this result later when proving
C([-T,T];H^{1})
convergence.

Turning to convergence in
C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
, we first note that as
\psi_{n,0}\to\psi_{0}
in
{L_{\mu}^{2}}
we have

(3.25)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\;\sup_{n\geq 1}\;\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{n,0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\;\sup_{n\geq 1}\;\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{n,0}\|_{L^{2}}=0.

In view of the convergence (
3.21
), using (
3.10
), (
3.11
), (
3.23
), and (
3.25
), we find that

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\ \Bigl[\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sup_{n}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Bigr]=0

and

(3.26)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\ \Bigl[\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}+\sup_{n}\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{n}(t)\|_{{L_{\mu}^{2}}}^{2}\Bigr]=0.

We are now ready to address convergence in
C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
. Clearly

\displaystyle\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\leq\|\varphi^{\lambda}[\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)]\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}+\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi_{n}(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}+\|(1-\varphi^{\lambda})\psi(t)\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}

holds for all
\lambda>0
and
t\in[-T,T]
. Using (
2.8
) we may bound

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\varphi^{\lambda}[\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)]\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl(\mathcal{N}_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\bigr)\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

Recalling (
3.26
) and that we have already proved convergence in
C([-T,T];H^{\frac{2}{3}})
, we may send
n\to\infty
and then
\lambda\to 0
, we deduce that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}})
.

As we have now shown that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
C([-T,T];H^{\frac{2}{3}}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
, (
2.13
) gives us

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi_{n}(t)|^{4}\,d\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(t)|^{4}\,d\mu\right|=0.

Conservation of mass and energy then yields

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\Bigl|\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}-\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl|M\bigl[\psi_{n,0}\bigr]-M[\psi_{0}]\Bigr]+2\Bigl|E\bigl[\psi_{n,0}\bigr]-E\bigl[\psi_{0}\bigr]\Bigr|

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{|t|\leq T}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi_{n}(t)|^{4}\,d\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(t)|^{4}\,d\mu\right|,

which converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Finally, as
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1})
, the set

Q=\bigl\{(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\psi(t):|t|\leq T\bigr\}\subseteq H^{-1}

is compact. We may then use (
3.24
) with the convergence of norms to see that

\displaystyle\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{|t|\leq T}\Bigl|\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}-\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\quad+2\sup_{|t|\leq T}\sup_{\phi\in Q}\Bigl|\langle\phi,\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\rangle\Bigr|

converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.
∎

We now turn our attention to a deterministic version of Theorem
1.3
. This theorem concerns solutions
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to
(\text{NLS}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}})
with
\mu^{\varepsilon}
defined by (
1.8
). Specifically, we prove an analogue of (
3.21
) for the solutions
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to

(3.27)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{\varepsilon}=-\partial_{x}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}+2|\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{with}\quad\psi_{\varepsilon}(0)=\psi_{0}\in{L_{\mu}^{2}}\cap H^{1}.

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\mu)<\infty
and
\psi_{0}\in{L_{\mu}^{2}}\cap H^{1}
. For each
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, there is a unique global solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to (
3.27
). Moreover,

(3.28)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ \sup_{|t|\leq T}\ \bigl\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)-\psi(t)\bigr\|_{H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}=0\quad\text{for each}\quad 0<T<\infty.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, the definition (
1.8
) of
\mu^{\varepsilon}
guarantees that

\mu^{\varepsilon}(I_{k})\leq\mu(I_{k-1})+\mu(I_{k})+\mu(I_{k+1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(I_{k})\leq\mu^{\varepsilon}(I_{k-1})+\mu^{\varepsilon}(I_{k})+\mu^{\varepsilon}(I_{k+1})

and consequently,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu)\simeq\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mu^{\varepsilon})\quad\text{and}\quad w(x;\mu)\simeq w(x;\mu^{\varepsilon})\quad\text{uniformly for $\varepsilon\in(0,1]$.}

This demonstrates that the norms on
{L_{\mu}^{2}}
and
{L_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}^{2}}
are equivalent, uniformly for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.
In particular,
\psi_{0}\in{L_{\mu}^{2}}
implies that
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}
. Thus, Theorem
1.4
guarantees that there is a unique global solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
to (
3.28
); moreover, this solution is
H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}
-continuous and conserves both the mass and the energy functional

E_{\varepsilon}[\psi]:=\int\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx+\int\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi(x)|^{4}\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}(x).

Using mass and energy conservation, as well as (
3.21
) and (
3.9
), we also see that

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}}}<\infty\quad\text{for any}\quad 0<T<\infty.

The existence of the solutions
\psi_{\varepsilon}
and the fact that they obey such bounds allow us to repeat the proof of Proposition
3.4
replacing
\varphi^{\eta}\equiv\varphi^{\varepsilon}\equiv 1
(but retaining
\varphi^{\lambda}
). In this way, we find that
\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)
are Cauchy in
C([-T,T];{L_{\mu}^{2}}\cap H^{2/3})
and that the limit is the unique solution
\psi(t)
to (
NLSμ
). Although this convergence is local in time, once we demonstrate
C([-T,T];H^{1})
convergence, this argument can be iterated as in the proof of Proposition
3.5
.

The proof of Proposition
3.5
also contains the key idea for upgrading convergence, namely, energy conservation and the Radon–Riesz argument.
Writing

\displaystyle\biggl|\int|\psi|^{4}\,d\mu-\int|\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{4}\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\biggr|\int|\psi|^{4}\,[d\mu-d\mu^{\varepsilon}]\biggr|+\int\bigl||\psi|^{4}-|\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{4}\bigr|\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}

and then applying (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) (where we note that
d\mu^{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{d\mu^{\mkern-1.0mu\varepsilon}\!\!}{dx}\,\,dx
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)|^{4}\,d\mu^{\varepsilon}=\int|\psi(t)|^{4}\,d\mu\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in[-T,T].

In this way, energy conservation shows that
\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\to\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}
and the Radon–Riesz theorem allows us to upgrade convergence in the weak topology to norm convergence.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

To apply Theorem
1.4
to prove Theorem
1.2
, we require one additional lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mu
be distributed according to the Poisson process (
1.2
). Then

(4.1)

\bbE\,\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}\lesssim 1,

and
\mathcal{N}_{k}<\infty
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
almost surely. Moreover, if
f\in L^{2}
then

(4.2)

\bbE\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2p}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2p}

for any
1\leq p<\infty
; in particular,
f\in{L_{\mu}^{2}}
almost surely.

Proof.

Fixing a choice of
r>4
, we apply Young’s inequality to bound

\mu(I_{\ell})^{2}|k-\ell|^{\frac{r}{2}-1}\leq\tfrac{2}{r}\bigl(\tfrac{r-2}{r}\bigr)^{\frac{r-2}{2}}\mu(I_{\ell})^{r}+|k-\ell|^{\frac{r}{2}};

in particular, if
k\neq\ell
then

\mu(I_{\ell})^{2}-|k-\ell|\lesssim\frac{\mu(I_{\ell})^{r}}{\langle k-\ell\rangle^{\frac{r}{2}-1}}

and so, for any
1\leq p<\infty
,

\mathcal{N}_{k}^{2p}\lesssim 1+\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\mu(I_{\ell})^{pr}}{\langle k-\ell\rangle^{p(\frac{r}{2}-1)}}.

We recall that the
\mu(I_{\ell})
are independent Poisson random variables with mean
\bbE\mu(I_{\ell})=1
. As a consequence,

\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\bbE\mu(I_{\ell})^{pr}\lesssim 1,

and hence

(4.3)

\bbE\bigl(\mathcal{N}_{k}^{2p}\bigr)\lesssim 1+\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\bbE\mu(I_{\ell})^{pr}}{\langle k-\ell\rangle^{p(\frac{r}{2}-1)}}\lesssim 1,

which gives us (
4.1
). Consequently,
\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}<\infty
almost surely and the estimate (
2.6
) then ensures that almost surely we have
\mathcal{N}_{k}^{2}\leq\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}+|k|<\infty
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Similarly, if
f\in L^{2}
then we may apply (
2.9
), Minkowski’s inequality, (
4.3
), and then (
2.5
) to bound

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\bbE\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2p}\Bigr\}^{\frac{1}{p}}\simeq\Bigl\{\bbE\Bigl[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}_{k}^{2}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Bigr]^{p}\Bigr\}^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl\{\bbE\Bigl[\mathcal{N}_{k}^{2p}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2p}\Bigr]\Bigr\}^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\chi_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\simeq\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

from which we obtain (
4.2
).
∎

We are now in a position to complete the proofs of our main theorems:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Given
\psi_{0}\in H^{1}
, Lemma
4.1
ensures that we almost surely have
\mathcal{N}_{0}<\infty
and
\psi_{0}\in{L_{\mu}^{2}}
. We may then apply Proposition
3.5
to deduce that almost surely there exists a unique strong solution
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{1}\cap{L_{\mu}^{2}})
of (
NLSμ
) that conserves mass and energy.

It remains to prove our continuity statement (
1.5
). We begin by fixing
2\leq p<\infty
,
T>0
, and a sequence of initial data
\psi_{n,0}
that converges to
\psi_{0}
in
H^{1}
. It suffices to show that every subsequence of
\psi_{n,0}
admits a further subsequence that satisfies (
1.5
). To keep the notation unburdened by further subscripts, all subsequences of
\psi_{n,0}
will still be denoted
\psi_{n,0}
. We start by observing that any subsequence of
\psi_{n,0}
admits a further subsequence that satisfies two additional properties:

(4.4)

\sup_{n}\|\psi_{n,0}\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim 1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n}\|\psi_{n,0}-\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim 1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}.

To prove that (
1.5
) holds for sequences satisfying (
4.4
), we will combine Proposition
3.6
with the dominated convergence theorem. Our main task is verifying the existence of a suitable dominating function. We choose

(4.5)

F_{T}(\mu):=\sup_{n}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\Bigl[\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\Bigr]^{\frac{p}{2}},

where
\psi(t)
and
\psi_{n}(t)
denote the solutions to (
NLSμ
) with initial data
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{n,0}
, respectively. In light of the conservation of mass and energy,

\displaystyle F_{T}(\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n}\biggl[\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\psi_{n,0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\int|\psi_{0}|^{4}+|\psi_{n,0}|^{4}\,d\mu\biggr]^{\frac{p}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{p}+\biggl[\int|\psi_{0}|^{4}\,d\mu\biggr]^{\frac{p}{2}}\!+\sum_{n}\biggl\{\|\psi_{n,0}-\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{p}+\biggl[\int|\psi_{n,0}-\psi_{0}|^{4}\,d\mu\biggr]^{\frac{p}{2}}\biggr\}.

Employing (
2.13
), then (
4.2
), and finally (
4.4
), we find that

(4.6)

\bbE\{F_{T}(\mu)\}\lesssim_{p}1+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{p}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2p}.

This shows that our dominating function has finite expectation.

By Jensen’s inequality, followed by (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), we also have

\bbE\Bigl\{\sum_{n}\|\psi_{n,0}-\psi_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}\Bigr\}\leq\sum_{n}\sqrt{\bbE\|\psi_{n,0}-\psi_{0}\smash[b]{\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}^{2}}}<\infty,

which shows that
\psi_{n,0}\to\psi_{0}
in
{L_{\mu}^{2}}
almost surely. By hypothesis, we know that
\psi_{n,0}\to\psi_{0}
in
H^{1}
. These two observations allow us to apply Proposition
3.6
and so deduce that

(4.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{|t|\leq T}\|\psi_{n}(t)-\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}=0\quad\text{almost surely.}

Thus, we see that (
1.5
) follows from the dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma
4.1
shows that both
\mathcal{N}_{0}<\infty
and
\psi_{0}\in{L_{\mu}^{2}}
almost surely. Thus, Corollary
3.7
shows that (
3.28
) holds almost surely. To deduce
L^{p}(d\mathbb{P})
convergence, we employ the dominated convergence theorem as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. As a dominating function, we choose

F_{T}(\mu):=\sup_{|t|\leq T}\;\Bigl[\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\,\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\Bigr]^{\frac{p}{2}}.

Using the conservation of mass and energy, as well as (
2.13
) and (
3.29
), we have
