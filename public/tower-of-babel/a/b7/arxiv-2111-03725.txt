Algorithms and data structures for first-order logic with connectivity under vertex failures
Michał Pilipczuk, Nicole Schirrmacher, Sebastian Siebertz, Szymon Toruńczyk, Alexandre Vigny

Abstract
We introduce a new data structure for answering connectivity queries in undirected graphs subject to batched vertex failures. Precisely, given any graph G G and integer parameter k k , we can in fixed-parameter time construct a data structure that can later be used to answer queries of the form: “are vertices s s and t t connected via a path that avoids vertices u 1 , … , u k u_{1},\ldots,u_{k} ?” in time 2 2 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) \smash{2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}} . In the terminology of the literature on data structures, this gives the first deterministic data structure for connectivity under vertex failures where for every fixed number of failures, all operations can be performed in constant time. With the aim to understand the power and the limitations of our new techniques, we prove an algorithmic meta theorem for the recently introduced separator logic , which extends first-order logic with atoms for connectivity under vertex failures. We prove that the model-checking problem for separator logic is fixed-parameter tractable on every class of graphs that exclude a fixed topological minor. We also show a weak converse. This implies that from the point of view of parameterized complexity, under standard complexity theoretical assumptions, the frontier of tractability of separator logic is almost exactly delimited by classes excluding a fixed topological minor. The backbone of our proof relies on a decomposition theorem of Cygan, Lokshtanov, Pilipczuk, Pilipczuk, and Saurabh [SICOMP ’19], which provides a tree decomposition of a given graph into bags that are unbreakable. Crucially, unbreakability allows to reduce separator logic to plain first-order logic within each bag individually. Guided by this observation, we design our model-checking algorithm using dynamic programming over the tree decomposition, where the transition at each bag amounts to running a suitable model-checking subprocedure for plain first-order logic. This approach is robust enough to provide also an extension to efficient enumeration of answers to a query expressed in separator logic.

Introduction

1.1
Connectivity under vertex failures

In many applications, we do not need to answer a query once but rather
need to repeatedly answer queries over dynamically changing data. A
prime example are databases, where a database is repeatedly modified
and queried by the users. In most cases, the modifications to the
database can be expected to be small compared to its size, which
suggests that in a preprocessing step, we can set up a data structure
that is efficiently updated after each modification and that allows
for efficient querying. In 1997, Frigioni and Italiano introduced the

dynamic subgraph model

[
FI97
]
, which
allows to conveniently model such dynamic situations in the particular
case of connectivity in network infrastructures that are subject to
node or link failures. This model, which has been studied intensively
in the data structures community under the name of

connectivity oracles under vertex failures
, is as
follows. We are given a
graph
G
and an integer
k
. We imagine that
G
is a network in
which at every moment, at most
k
vertices (or edges) are inactive
(are subject to
failures
). The goal is to construct a data
structure that supports the following two operations. First, one can

update

G
by resetting the set of failed vertices to a given
set of size at most
k
. Second, one can
query

G
by asking,
for a given pair of vertices
s
and
t
, whether
s
and
t
can be
connected by a path that avoids the failed vertices.

Following the introduction of the problem by Frigioni and
Italiano
[
FI97
]
and the more general problem
of batched vertex or edge failures by Pătraşcu and
Thorup
[
PT07
]

Strictly speaking,
in
[
PT07
]
Pătraşcu and Thorup considered only
edge failures. To the best of our knowledge, the first to
systematically investigate (batched) vertex failures on general
graphs were Duan and Pettie in
[
DP10
]
.
, there has been a long line of work on data
structures for connectivity oracles under vertex and edge failures.
We refer to a remarkably comprehensive literature overview in the work
of Duan and Pettie
[
DP20
]
, which also provides the currently
best bounds for the problem as far as combinatorial and deterministic
data structures are concerned. Their data structure can be initialized
in time
\mathcal{O}(|G|\|G\|\cdot\log|G|)
, takes
\mathcal{O}(k\|G\|\log|G|)

space, and supports updates in
\mathcal{O}(k^{3}\log^{3}|G|)
time and queries
in
\mathcal{O}(k)
time. Here
\|G\|
is the vertex count of
G
and
\|G\|
is the joint number of edges and
vertices in
G
. These bounds can be slightly improved at the cost of
allowing randomization, however, the polylogarithmic dependency on the
size of the graph in the update time persists. As noted
in
[
DP20
]
, from known results it is possible to derive data
structures with constant time complexity of operations for
k\leqslant 3

(or
k\leqslant 4
for edge failures), but, citing their words, “scaling
these solutions up, even to an arbitrarily large constant
k
, becomes
prohibitively complex, even in the simpler case of edge failures.”

Recently, van den Brand and Saranurak
[
vdBS19
]
proposed a very
different approach to the problem, using which they obtained a
randomized data structure that can be initialized in time

\mathcal{O}(|G|^{\omega})
, takes
\mathcal{O}(|G|^{2}\log|G|)
space, and supports
updates in
\mathcal{O}(k^{\omega})
time and queries in
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
time. In particular, the update and the query time are constant for constant
k
;
to the best of our knowledge, this is the only data structure that has
this property known so far. The approach is algebraic and, simplifying
it substantially, boils down to storing a matrix of counts of walks
between pairs of vertices and extracting answers to queries using
algebraic operations on those counts. To manipulate the counts
efficiently, one needs to work on their short hashes, for instance in
the form of elements from a fixed finite field. This introduces
randomization to the picture and avoiding it within this methodology
seems difficult. We remark that the data structure of van den Brand
and Saranurak
[
vdBS19
]
works even for the more general problem
of reachability in directed graphs under vertex failures, and can
handle arc insertions.

As the first main contribution of this paper, we prove the following
theorem. Note that querying whether two vertices are connected by a
path avoiding failed vertices is equivalent to an update followed by a
connectivity query in the terminology of previous
works
[
vdBS19
,
DP10
,
DP20
,
PT07
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given a graph
G
and an integer
k
, one can in time

2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}\cdot|G|^{2}\|G\|
construct a data structure that
may answer the following queries in time
2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}
: given

s,t\in V(G)
and
k
vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
of
G
, are
s
and

t
connected in
G
by a path that avoids the vertices

u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
? The space usage of the data structure is

2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}\cdot\|G\|
.

Note that in
theorem

1.1
, for every fixed
k
, all
operations are supported in constant time (which is doubly-exponential
in
k
). Also, the data structure is entirely deterministic and purely
combinatorial. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
deterministic data structure with constant time queries. Note
also that for a fixed
k
, the space usage of our data structure is
linear in the size of the graph, as opposed to the quadratic
dependency in the result of van den Brand and
Saranurak
[
vdBS19
]
.

Our approach to proving
theorem

1.1
is completely new
compared to the previous approaches

[
vdBS19
,
DP10
,
DP20
,
PT07
]
. Our key
combinatorial observation is that connectivity queries over a constant
number
k
of vertex failures can be evaluated in constant time
provided the underlying graph
G
is sufficiently well-connected. The
requirement on the well-connectedness of
G
depends on the number
k
. Obviously, there is no guarantee that the input graph
G

satisfies this property. We therefore use a decomposition theorem of
Cygan, Lokshtanov, Pilipczuk, Pilipczuk, and Saurabh
[
CLP+19
]
that (roughly) states the following
(see
theorem

3.2
for a precise statement). For
every graph
G
and fixed number
k
, there is a tree decomposition
where every intersection of adjacent bags has bounded size and every bag
is well-connected for parameter
k
. Moreover, such a tree
decomposition can be computed in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}|G|^{2}\|G\|
. The
data structure of
theorem

1.1
is constructed around the tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
provided by the theorem of Cygan et
al.
[
CLP+19
]
. Intuitively speaking, whether
s
and
t
are connected via a path that
avoids the vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
can be decided using dynamic programming over
\mathcal{T}
. We employ known
techniques developed for answering queries on trees in constant time, for instance a deterministic variant of Simon’s
factorization
[
Col07
]
, to be able to compute the outcome of this dynamic programming efficiently for any query given on input.
We provide a more detailed overview of the proof of

theorem

1.1
in
section

2
. The full proof is
provided in
section

4
.

The time complexity guarantees provided by
theorem

1.1

can be improved by allowing larger space usage. We give a variant of

theorem

1.1
(
theorem

6.1
) that offers queries
in time
k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
after initialization in time

2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot|G|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, but assumes space usage

k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|G|^{2}
. The main idea here is to use a later variant
of the decomposition of Cygan et al.
[
CLP+19
]
, proved
in
[
CKL+21
]
, that provides quantitatively better bounds on
the adhesion and unbreakability of the decomposition, at the cost of
relaxing a technical condition on the unbreakability (see the
difference between strong and weak unbreakability, explained in

section

3
). The need of dealing with this relaxation turns
out to be a nuisance that, nonetheless, can be overcome in our
approach.

1.2
Algorithmic meta theorems

Once an algorithmic technique has been applied to solve a specific
problem, it is very desirable to understand the power and the
limitations of that technique, that is, to understand which other
problems can be solved with that technique and which problems
cannot. An approach to this very general goal is to prove an

algorithmic meta theorem
: a theorem that establishes
tractability for a whole class of problems,
possibly on certain restricted input instances. As logic allows to
conveniently define classes of algorithmic problems, namely, the class
of all problems that can be expressed in the logic under
consideration, algorithmic meta theorems are often formulated as
model-checking problems for a logic. In the
model-checking
problem
for a logic
{\cal L}
on a class of graphs
\mathscr{C}
we are
given a graph
G\in\mathscr{C}
and a sentence
\varphi\in{\cal L}
, and the task
is to decide whether
\varphi
holds in
G
. The archetypal result of
this form is a result of Courcelle stating that every formula of
monadic-second order logic (
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
) can be evaluated in linear time
on all graphs whose treewidth is bounded by some fixed constant
[
Cou90
]
.
This
result has been extended to various other logics
{\cal L}
and graph
classes
\mathscr{C}
.

This approach to algorithmic meta theorems can be made precise in the
language of parameterized complexity, as follows. Say that
{\cal L}
is

tractable
on
\mathscr{C}
if the model-checking problem for
\mathscr{C}

and
{\cal L}
is
fixed-parameter tractable (fpt)
: it can be solved
in time
f(\varphi)\cdot\|G\|^{c}
, for some computable function
f

and a constant
c
, both depending only on
\mathscr{C}
. Results about
tractability of a logic
{\cal L}
on a class
\mathscr{C}
imply the tractability
of a vast array of problems — namely all problems that can be
expressed in the logic
{\cal L}
— on a given class of graphs. For
instance, if model-checking
first-order logic
(
\mathsf{FO}
) is fpt on
a class of graphs
\mathscr{C}
, then in particular the dominating set problem
is fpt on
\mathscr{C}
, since the existence of a dominating set of size
k

can be expressed by a first-order sentence with
k+1
quantifiers that
range over the vertices of the graph. Similarly, the independent set
problem is then also fpt on
\mathscr{C}
. On the other hand, if the more
powerful logic
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
is tractable on a class of graphs
\mathscr{C}
, then
the
3
-colorability problem, or the hamiltonicity problem are
polynomial-time solvable on
\mathscr{C}
, since both those problems can be
expressed using an
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
sentence, whose quantifiers that range
over
sets
of vertices and edges of the graph.

There has been a long line of work on fixed-parameter tractability of
model-checking
\mathsf{FO}
, as it is arguably the most fundamental logic.
This culminated in the work of Grohe, Kreutzer and
Siebertz
[
GKS17
]
, who proved that this problem is
fixed-parameter tractable on every class that is
nowhere
dense
. Those classes include for example the class of planar
graphs, or every class with bounded maximum degree, or of bounded
genus. Without going into details, nowhere denseness is a general
notion of uniform sparseness in graphs, and it is broader than most
well-studied concepts of sparseness considered in structural graph
theory, such as excluding a fixed (topological) minor. As observed by
Dvořák et al.
[
DKT13
]
, the result of Grohe et al. is
tight in the following sense: whenever
\mathscr{C}
is not nowhere dense and
is closed under taking subgraphs, then
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking on
\mathscr{C}

is as hard as on general graphs, that is,
\mathsf{AW}[\star]
-hard. This means
that as far as subgraph-closed classes are concerned, sparsity —
described formally through the notion of nowhere denseness — exactly
delimits the area of tractability of
\mathsf{FO}
and the limits of the
locality method for
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking.

The aforementioned classic theorem of Courcelle
[
Cou90
]
states
that the model-checking problem for
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
is fixed-parameter
tractable on a class
\mathscr{C}
if
\mathscr{C}
has bounded treewidth. The proof
translates the given sentence into a suitable tree automaton working
on a tree decomposition of the input graph; this approach brings a
wide range of automata-based tools to the study of
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
on
graphs. Again, as shown by Kreutzer and
Tazari
[
KT10
]
and Ganian et
al.
[
GHL+14
]
, under certain complexity assumptions this
is (almost) the most one could hope for: on subgraph-closed classes
whose treewidth is poly-logarithmically unbounded, model-checking of

\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
becomes intractable from the parameterized perspective. So
for
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
, bounded treewidth (almost) delimits the frontier of
tractability, at least for subgraph-closed classes, and automata-based techniques exactly explain what can be done algorithmically.

With the aim to provide a meta theorem that captures the essence of
the techniques developed to answer connectivity under vertex failures,
we search for a logic that can express these queries. It is easy to
see that
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
is strong enough, but in fact,
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
is much
stronger and as explained above, it is intractable beyond graphs of
bounded treewidth. On the other hand,
\mathsf{FO}
can only express local
properties and in particular, it cannot even express the very simple
query of whether two vertices are in the same connected component. This
naturally leads to an extension of
\mathsf{FO}
that may use connectivity
under vertex failures as atomic formulas. Interestingly, such a logic
was only very recently introduced independently by
Bojańczyk
[
Boj21
]
and by Schirrmacher et
al.
[
SSV21
]
under the name
separator logic
, and
denoted
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
. This logic extends
\mathsf{FO}
by predicates

\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
(one for each
k\geqslant 0
) with the
following semantics: if
s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
are vertices of a graph

G
, then
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
holds if and only if there is
a path that connects
s
with
t
and does not pass through any of the
vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
. Thus, separator logic involves very basic
connectivity queries that have a global character, unlike plain
\mathsf{FO}
,
which is local. On the other hand, while
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})

predicates are expressible in
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
, it is no surprise that the
expressive power of
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
is strictly weaker than that of

\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
. For example,
\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
can express bipartiteness, while

\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
cannot
[
SSV21
, Theorem 3.11]
. Separator logic can
still express many interesting algorithmic properties, such as the
feedback vertex set problem and many recently studied elimination
distance problems
[
BD16
]
.

As the second main result of the paper, we prove an algorithmic meta
theorem for separator logic. We show that the frontier of tractability
of
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
is almost exactly delimited by classes of graphs that
exclude a fixed topological minor. More precisely, we prove the
following complementary results.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of graphs that exclude a fixed graph as a
topological minor. Then given
G\in\mathscr{C}
and an
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
sentence

\varphi
, one can decide whether
\varphi
holds in
G
in time

f(\varphi)\cdot\|G\|^{3}
, where
f
is a computable function
depending on
\mathscr{C}
.

For the lower bound, we will need a technical assumption. We say that
a class
\mathscr{C}
admits
efficient encoding of topological minors

if for every graph
H
there exists
G\in\mathscr{C}
such that
H
is a
topological minor of
G
, and, given
H
, such
G
together with a
suitable topological minor model can be computed in time polynomial in

|H|
. Note that in particular such
G
can be only polynomially
larger than
H
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a subgraph-closed class of graphs that admits efficient
encoding of topological minors. Then the model-checking of

\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
on
\mathscr{C}
is
\mathsf{AW}[\star]
-hard, even for the fragment that uses
only
\mathsf{conn}_{k}
predicates with
k\leqslant 2
.

Let us fix
an
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
sentence
\varphi
, and let
k+2
be the maximum arity of
connectivity predicates appearing in
\varphi

that
\mathsf{conn}_{k}
predicates can be rewritten to

\mathsf{FO}

Coming back to our dynamic programming on the tree decomposition,
we choose to present it using
a new framework based on automata, as this brings us closer
to the classic understanding of logic on tree-decomposable
graphs. Intuitively, the automaton processes a given tree in a
bottom-up manner, but we assume that on the children of every node
there is an additional structure of a graph. We say that such trees are
augmented
with graphs.
When the automaton processes a node
x
,
it chooses a transition based on an
\mathsf{FO}
query executed on the
graph on the children of
x
, where each child is labeled with the
state computed for it before in the run. Then testing whether a
formula
\varphi\in\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
holds on a graph
G
is reduced to deciding
whether an automaton constructed from
\varphi
accepts such an

augmented tree
, constructed from the tree decomposition
provided by the theorem of Cygan et al.
[
CLP+19
]
.

The benefit of this approach is that other questions related to the
logic
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
can be reduced in the same way to questions about tree
automata over augmented trees. We showcase this by proving that given
an
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
query
\varphi(\bar{x})
with free variables and a graph

G
from a fixed topological-minor-free class
\mathscr{C}
, the answers to

\varphi(\bar{x})
on
G
can be enumerated with constant delay after
fpt preprocessing (see
theorem

8.2
for a precise
statement). Similarly, the formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
can be queried in
constant time after fpt preprocessing (see

theorem

8.1
). In general, we believe that the
framework of automata over augmented trees may be of independent
interest, as it seems to be a convenient model for understanding
dynamic programming procedures over tree-decomposable structures.

2.1
Data structure for
connectivity under vertex failures: proof of

theorem

1.1

Unbreakability.

As mentioned in
section

1
, the central idea of this work
is to tackle problems involving connectivity predicates using a decomposition into well-connected — or, more formally,
unbreakable
— parts. We
first need to recall a few definitions.

A
separation
in a graph
G
is a pair
(A,B)
of vertex subsets
such that
A\cup B=V(G)
and there are no edges in
G
between

A-B
and
B-A
. The
order
of the separation
is the cardinality of the
separator

A\cap B
. A vertex subset

X
is
(q,k)
-unbreakable
in a graph
G
if for every
separation
(A,B)
in
G
of order at most
k
in
G
, either

|A\cap X|\leqslant q
or
|B\cap X|\leqslant q
. So intuitively speaking, a
separation of order
k
cannot break
X
in a balanced way: one of the
sides must contain at most
q
vertices of
X
.

The notion of unbreakability has been implicitly introduced in the
context of parameterized algorithms by Kawarabayashi and Thorup in
their work on the
k
-Way Cut
problem
[
KT11
]
.
This work has brought about the method of
recursive
understanding
, which was then made explicit by Chitnis, Cygan,
Hajiaghayi, Pilipczuk, and Pilipczuk in the technique of

randomized contractions

[
CCH+16
]
. Intuitively,
recursive understanding is a Divide&Conquer scheme using which one
can reduce problems on general graphs to problems on suitably
unbreakable graphs, provided certain technical conditions are
satisfied. The technique was also applied in the context of
model-checking: Lokshtanov, Ramanujan, Saurabh, and
Zehavi
[
LRSZ18
]
proved that the problem of deciding a
property expressed in
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
in fpt time on general graphs can be
reduced to the same problem on suitably unbreakable graphs. This
result was used to provide algorithms for computing elimination
distance to certain graph
classes
[
AKP+21a
,
FGT21
,
Jd21
]
,
which is very much related to separator logic
(see
[
SSV21
, Example 3.5]
).

In this work, we will not use recursive understanding or randomized
contractions
per se
, but their conceptual successor: the
decomposition into unbreakable parts. Precisely, the following theorem
was proved by Cygan, Lokshtanov, Pilipczuk, Pilipczuk, and
Saurabh
[
CLP+19
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
CLP+19
]
)
.

There is a function
q(k)\in 2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
such that given a graph
G

and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one can in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}\cdot|G|^{2}\|G\|

construct a (rooted) tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
of
G

satisfying the following.

•

All adhesions in
\mathcal{T}
are of size at most
q(k)
; and

•

every bag of
\mathcal{T}
, say at node
x
, is

(q(k),k)
-unbreakable in the subgraph of
G
induced by the
union of bags at all descendants
2
2

2

We follow the
convention that every node is its own ancestor and descendant.

of
x
.

We remark that a different variant of

theorem

2.1
was later proved by Cygan, Komosa,
Lokshtanov, Pilipczuk, Pilipczuk, and
Wahlström
[
CKL+21
]
. This variant provides much stronger
unbreakability guarantees —
q(k)=k
— at the cost of relaxing the
unbreakability to hold only in the whole graph
G
. See

section

3
for a discussion. The variant
of
[
CKL+21
]
can be also used in our context and this leads
to improving some quantitative bounds, however for simplicity, we focus
on
theorem

2.1
in this overview.

A typical usage of
theorem

2.1
is to
apply bottom-up dynamic programming on the obtained tree
decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
. The subproblem for a node
x
of
T

corresponds to finding partial solutions in the subgraph of
G

induced by the union of bags at descendants of
x
, for every possible
behavior of such a partial solution on the adhesion connecting
x

with its parent. That this adhesion has size at most
q(k)
gives an
upper bound on the number of different behaviors. To solve such a
subproblem one needs to aggregate the solutions computed for the
children of
x
by solving an auxiliary problem on the subgraph
induced by the bag of
x
. In various problems of interest, the
unbreakability of the bag becomes helpful in solving the auxiliary
problem. This general methodology has been successfully used to give
multiple fpt algorithms for cut
problems
[
CLP+19
,
CKL+21
,
SZ18
]
, most prominently
for
Minimum Bisection

[
CLP+19
]
. More recent uses
include a parameterized approximation scheme for the
Min

k
-Cut
problem
[
LSS20
]
and a fixed-parameter
algorithm for
Graph Isomorphism
parameterized by the size of
the excluded minor
[
LPPS21
]
. On a high level, we apply
the same methodology here.

The case of totally unbreakable graphs.

Let us come back
to the problem of evaluating connectivity queries: we are given a
graph
G
and after some preprocessing of
G
, we would like to be
able to quickly evaluate for given vertices
s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}

whether
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
holds. Consider first the
following special case: the whole
G
is
(q,k)
-unbreakable for some
parameter
q
(or more formally,
V(G)
is
(q,k)
-unbreakable in

G
). The key observation is that then, whether

\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
holds can be easily decided in time
polynomial in
q
and
k
as follows.

Run a breadth-first search from
s
in
G-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
, but
terminate the search once
q+1
different vertices have been reached.
If the search reached
t
, then for sure
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})

holds. If the search finished before reaching
q+1
different vertices
and it did not reach
t
, then for sure
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})

does not hold. In the remaining case, perform a symmetric procedure
starting from
t
. The key observation is that if both breadth-first
searches — from
s
and from
t
— got terminated after reaching

q+1
different vertices, then the
(q,k)
-unbreakability of
G

implies that
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
do not separate
s
from
t
. So
then
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
holds. It is straightforward to
implement this procedure in time polynomial in
q
and
k
given the
standard encoding of
G
through adjacency lists.

In the general case, we cannot expect the given graph
G
to be

(q,k)
-unbreakable for any parameter
q
bounded in terms of
k
.
However, we can use
theorem

2.1
to compute a
tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
of
G
into parts that are

(q,k)
-unbreakable for
q\in 2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
. The idea is that then, a
query
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
can be evaluated by bottom-up
dynamic programming on
\mathcal{T}
. By suitably precomputing enough
auxiliary information about
\mathcal{T}
, this evaluation can be performed in
time depending only on
q
and
k
.

Evaluating a query on the decomposition.

Consider then
the following setting: we are given the decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})

provided by
theorem

2.1
, and for given

s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
we would like to decide whether

\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
holds. So far let us not optimize the
complexity: the goal is only to design a general algorithmic
mechanism which will be later implemented efficiently using
appropriate data structures.

For every node
x
of
T
, let
\mathsf{adh}(x)
be the adhesion between
x

and its parent (or
\emptyset
if
x
is the root), let
\mathsf{cone}(x)
be
the union of the bags at the descendants of
x
, and let

\mathsf{comp}(x)=\mathsf{cone}(x)-\mathsf{adh}(x)
. Call a node
x

affected

if
\mathsf{comp}(x)
contains a vertex of
\{s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
, and

unaffected
otherwise. Further, let

D(x)=\mathsf{adh}(x)\cup(\mathsf{comp}(x)\cap\{s,t\})
; note that
|D(x)|\leqslant q+2
.
Our goal is to compute the following
(connectivity) profile
for
every node
x
of
T
:

\mathsf{profile}(x)=\left\{\{a,b\}\in\binom{D(x)}{2}\quad\Big|\quad a\textrm{ and }b\textrm{ are connected in }G[\mathsf{cone}(x)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}]\right\}.

We remark that the tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
can be chosen so that it
satisfies the following basic connectivity property: for every node

x
and vertices
a,b\in\mathsf{adh}(x)
, there is a path connecting
a

and
b
whose all internal vertices belong to
\mathsf{comp}(x)
. Thus, for
every unaffected node
x
, we have that

\mathsf{profile}(x)=\binom{D(x)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}}{2}
.

It is easy to see that the profile of
x
is uniquely determined by
the profiles of the children of
x
and the subgraph induced by the
bag of
x
. The following lemma shows that this can be done
efficiently.

Lemma 2.2

(
lemma

4.4
, informal statement)
.

Let
x
be a node of
T
. Suppose that for each affected child
z

of
x
we are given
\mathsf{profile}(z)
. Then, subject to suitable
preprocessing of
G
, one can compute
\mathsf{profile}(x)
in time
polynomial in
q
.

Note that every node has at most
k+2
affected children, hence the
input to the algorithm of
lemma

2.2
is of size
polynomial in
q
.

Let us sketch the proof of
lemma

2.2
. In essence, we
apply the same strategy as the one from the case when the whole
graph
G
is unbreakable. Let
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
be the
graph obtained from
G[\mathsf{bag}(x)]
by turning the adhesion
\mathsf{adh}(z)

into a clique, for every child
z
of
x
. Note that
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
does
not depend on the query, and thus can be precomputed upon
initialization. As
\mathsf{bag}(x)
is

(q,k)
-unbreakable in
G[\mathsf{cone}(x)]
, to test whether two vertices

a,b\in\mathsf{bag}(x)
are connected in

G[\mathsf{cone}(x)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}]
it suffices to apply
breadth-first searches from
a
and
b
in
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
that are
terminated after reaching
q+1
different vertices, except that these
searches are forbidden to use vertices from

\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}\cap\mathsf{bag}(x)
as well as those edges originating
from turning adhesions of affected children into cliques that are not
in respective profiles. Provided
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
is precomputed and a
list of affected children together with their profiles is given on
input, it is easy to implement this algorithm so that it runs in time
polynomial in
q
. Thus we can decide for every pair

\{a,b\}\in\binom{\mathsf{adh}(x)}{2}
whether it should be included in

\mathsf{profile}(x)
. A similar reasoning can be applied to pairs in

\binom{D(x)}{2}
that include
s
or
t
.

By applying
lemma

2.2
bottom-up, we can compute all the
profiles in time
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|\leqslant q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|G|
. Then
we can read whether
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
holds by checking
whether
\{s,t\}
belongs to the profile of the root of
T
. Hence,
the query
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
can be evaluated in time

q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|G|
.

Data structure.

Our final goal is to enrich the
decomposition
\mathcal{T}
with some additional information so that the
mechanism presented above can be executed in time depending only on

q
.

For a vertex
v
of
G
, let
\mathsf{top}(v)
be the unique top-most node
of
T
whose bag contains
v
. Let

\widehat{S}\coloneqq\{s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
and let

X=\{\mathsf{top}(v)\colon v\in\widehat{S}\}
.

Note that
|X|\leqslant|\widehat{S}|\leqslant k+2

. Let now

be the

and all lowest common

to

For every node

in

, or
y
if no such ancestor exists

Note that every affected node lies on a path joining

Let

.
Then

is the set of all nodes that are affected children of

The idea is that when evaluating query
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
,
we can compute the profiles only for the nodes of
Y\cup Y^{\prime}
, instead of all
affected nodes of

. Note that the root of
T
was explicitly added to

from the profile of the root.

We process the nodes of
Y\cup Y^{\prime}
in a bottom-up manner. Consider then any element

x\in Y\cup Y^{\prime}
; our task is to compute its profile based on the profiles
of its strict descendants belonging to
Y\cup Y^{\prime}
.
We distinguish two cases.
In the first case,
x\in Y
, so we may compute
\mathsf{profile}(x)
using the algorithm of

lemma

2.2
.
The second case is of an element
x\in Y^{\prime}
; then
x=f(y)
where
y=f^{-1}(x)
.
Observe that the path from
y
to

consists of affected nodes,
whose siblings are unaffected nodes.
We now show how
\mathsf{profile}(x)
can be
computed from
\mathsf{profile}(y)
.

For two nodes

of
T
, where
c
is an ancestor of
d
, let
\mathsf{torso}(c,d)
be
the set of all pairs
\{a,b\}\in\binom{\mathsf{adh}(c)\cup\mathsf{adh}(d)}{2}
such
that in

there is a path from
a
to

whose all internal
vertices belong to
\mathsf{comp}(c)-\mathsf{cone}(d)
. Then to compute

\mathsf{profile}(x)
from
\mathsf{profile}(y)
, construct an

(\mathsf{adh}(x)\cup\mathsf{adh}(y)
and edges
\mathsf{profile}(y)\cup\mathsf{torso}(x,y)
.
Now
observe that
\mathsf{profile}(x)
is the reachability relation in this graph
restricted to
\mathsf{adh}(x)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
. The key point
implying the correctness of this observation is that there are no
vertices from

in
\mathsf{comp}(x)-\mathsf{comp}(y)
.

Observe now that the values of
\mathsf{torso}(c,d)
for

ranging over
ancestor/descendants pairs in

are independent of the query.
Therefore, upon initialization we can compute a data structure that
can be queried for those valued efficiently. The statement below
describes two possible implementations.

Lemma 2.3

(
lemma

6.2
and
lemma

4.2
,
combined and simplified)
.

Given
\mathcal{T}
, one can
set up data structures that can answer

the following specifications:

1.

initialization time
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|G|^{2}\|G\|
, memory usage

q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|G|^{2}
, query time
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
; or

2.

initialization time
2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}\cdot|G|
, memory usage

2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}\cdot|G|
, query time
2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}
.

The first point of
lemma

2.3
is actually straightforward:
just compute and store all the
\mathcal{O}(|T|^{2})=\mathcal{O}(|G|^{2})
answers to the
torso queries, each in time
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot\|G\|
using
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

applications of breadth-first search. The second point, which trades
exponential dependency on
q
for obtaining linear memory usage and

observe that we may now complete the proof of

theorem

1.1
. Indeed, using the data structure from the
second point of

2.3
, we can compute
\mathsf{profile}(x)

from
\mathsf{profile}(y)
for each

and

. By
performing this procedure for all the at most

nodes of

in a

, from which the answer to the query can be read. The running time
is dominated by

calls to the data structure of the second
point of
lemma

2.2
, each taking

2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}=2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}
time.

Finally, let us discuss the proof of the second point of

lemma

2.2
. We reduce this problem to the problem of
evaluating
product queries
in a semigroup-labeled tree,
defined as follows. Suppose
T
is a rooted tree whose edges are

. The task is to set

, where

is an

, outputs the product of the elements of

on the
path in

from

to

. The problem of evaluating torso queries
can be reduced to this abstract setting by considering a suitable
semigroup of
bi-interface graphs
, which represent connectivity
between corresponding adhesions; see
[
BP16
]
for the
origin of this concept. This semigroup has size
2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}
. So it
then suffices to use the following result.

Theorem 2.4

(
theorem

5.1
, simplified)
.

There is a data structure for the problem of evaluating product
queries in a tree
T
labeled with elements of a finite semigroup

S
that achieves query time
|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, memory usage

|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|
, and initialization time

|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|
.

can be easily derived from the
deterministic variant of Simon’s factorization, due to

[

]
. However, in

we

This finishes the proof of
theorem

1.1
. Note that in
the argumentation there are two modules that can be replaced with
other solutions:

•

The decomposition of
theorem

2.1
can be
replaced with the more recent variant from
[

]
. This allows us to have

, implying that

in the memory usage.

.

2.2
Model-checking
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
:

Theorems

1.2
and
1.3

We now turn to the proofs of
theorem

1.2
and
theorem

1.3
.
In those theorems, we fix a class
\mathscr{C}

and consider the model-checking problem for the logic
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
,
that is, the problem of deciding whether a given
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
sentence holds in a given graph
G\in\mathscr{C}
.

Lower bound.

First let us explain why we require
\mathscr{C}
to exclude a fixed topological minor
in
theorem

1.2
.
Clearly, the model-checking problem for
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
generalizes the model-checking problem for
\mathsf{FO}
, so
\mathscr{C}
should be in particular a class for which it is known that

\mathsf{FO}
model-checking is
\mathsf{FPT}
.
This is the case not only for classes that exclude a topological minor but also for all nowhere dense classes
[
GKS17
]
. So let us see why it is not enough to merely assume that
\mathscr{C}
is nowhere dense.

For a graph
G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-subdivision

G^{(k)}
of
G
is obtained from
G
by replacing each edge by a path of length
k+1
.
It follows from the definition of nowhere denseness that the class
\mathscr{C}
of all graphs of the form
G^{(n)}
, where
G
is an arbitrary graph on
n
vertices, is nowhere dense.
However, a formula of
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
can easily recover
G
from
G^{(n)}
, at least when
G
has no vertices of degree
2
. Indeed, the original vertices of
G
are precisely the vertices of
G^{(n)}
that have degree at least
3
, which can be expressed by an
\mathsf{FO}
formula
\varphi_{V}(x)
. Furthermore, two such vertices
u,v
are adjacent in
G
if and only if there is some vertex
z
of degree
2
in
G^{(n)}
such that
for every vertex
w
of
G^{(n)}
,
\mathsf{conn}(z,w,u,v)
holds only if
w
has degree
2
in
G^{(n)}
. This condition can be written using an
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
formula
\varphi_{E}(u,v)
.
Using this observation, any
\mathsf{FO}
sentence
\varphi
can be replaced by an
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
sentence
\varphi^{\prime}
, so that for every graph
G
as above,
\varphi
holds in
G
if and only if
\varphi^{\prime}
holds in
G^{(n)}
. Essentially,
\varphi^{\prime}
is obtained from
\varphi
by replacing every atomic formula
E(x,y)
(denoting adjacency) by
\varphi_{E}(x,y)
, and guarding all quantifiers by
\varphi_{V}(x)
.
Therefore, if we could efficiently model-check
\varphi^{\prime}
on the nowhere dense class
\mathscr{C}
, we could as well model-check
\varphi
on the class of all graphs (of size at least
3
and minimum degree at least
3
), as follows:
given an arbitrary graph
G
, first construct the graph
G^{(n)}\in\mathscr{C}
(in time polynomial in
n=|V(G)|
)
and then test
\varphi^{\prime}
on
G^{(n)}
. Hence, if
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
model-checking was fpt on
\mathscr{C}
, then
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking would be fpt on the class of all graphs, implying
\mathsf{FPT}=\mathsf{AW}[\star]
. So nowhere dense graph classes are too general for the statement of
theorem

1.2
to hold. Intuitively, the notion of nowhere denseness is not preserved under contracting long paths, which can be simulated in the logic
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
.

The same simple argument proves
theorem

1.3
.
Indeed, the argument works not only for the graph
G^{(n)}
obtained as the
n
-subdivision of
G
but also for any subdivision
G^{\prime}
(obtained by replacing each edge independently by any number of vertices) of
G
,
as long as
G^{\prime}
can be computed from
G
in polynomial time. The condition that
\mathscr{C}
is subgraph-closed and admits efficient encoding of topological minors guarantees precisely that for any given graph
G
we can construct, in polynomial time, a graph
G^{\prime}
which is a subdivision of
G
and belongs to
\mathscr{C}
. This yields
theorem

1.3

(see
section

9
for details).

Upper bound.

We now give some details concerning the proof of
theorem

1.2
, which is the second main contribution of the paper.

The starting observation is that for a

(q,k)
-unbreakable graph
G
, the query
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(u,v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
can be expressed in plain
\mathsf{FO}
. Indeed, this query fails if and only if there is a set
A
of at most
q
vertices which contains exactly one of the vertices
u
and
v
, and such that all neighbors of vertices of
A
outside of
A
are contained in
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
. The existence of such a set of
q
vertices can be expressed using
q
existential quantifiers followed by a universal quantifier.
So
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
with connectivity queries of arity at most
k+2

is equally expressive as plain
\mathsf{FO}
on
(q,k)
-unbreakable graphs, as long as
q
is a constant.

Now, if the graph
G
is not
(q,k)
-unbreakable, we work with the
tree decomposition into
(q,k)
-unbreakable parts given by
theorem

3.2
, where
q=q(k)
is a constant depending on
k
.
The next observation is that the constant-time querying algorithm used in the proof of
theorem

1.1
and explained above, can be seen as a formula on a suitably defined logical structure. Indeed,
if we look into the data structure answering the queries
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(u,v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, we notice that each such query triggers a constant number
of operations asking about the least common ancestor of two nodes in the tree decomposition, or asking about the membership of a vertex to a bag.
Additionally, the algorithm may ask, for two vertices
u,v
belonging to a bag
x
,
whether
u
and
v
are adjacent in the original graph, or whether they are adjacent in the bag graph
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
. Finally, the algorithm also performs torso queries.
We may therefore construct a logical structure, corresponding to the data structure, such that this basic functionality
is expressible by a first-order formula in this structure. Then the query

\mathsf{conn}_{k}(u,v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
becomes expressible using a plain
\mathsf{FO}
formula in the logical structure. It will then remain to show that we can model check
\mathsf{FO}
formulas in fpt on the resulting logical structure.

It is convenient to abstract away the details of our logical structure, and represent it in the following form:
It is a tree
T
(possibly vertex-labeled), together with additional edges (possibly labeled) between some nodes which are siblings in the tree; see
fig.

5
. We call such a structure an
augmented tree
.
In such a tree, the set of children of any node
v
carries a structure of a graph.
In the particular case of our construction, those graphs will precisely correspond to bag graphs.
Recall that a bag graph is obtained from the subgraph induced by a bag by turning all adhesions towards children into cliques. It is not difficult to show (see
lemma

3.4
) that if the original graph
G
is
H
-topological-minor free, then the resulting bag graphs
are
H^{\prime}
-topological-minor-free for some
H^{\prime}
depending on
H
and on
k
.
Hence, we obtain a tree augmented with
H^{\prime}
-topological-minor-free graphs.
And the original query
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(u,v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
can be now represented as an

\mathsf{FO}
formula in the augmented tree, where the
\mathsf{FO}
formula may use the ancestor relation
\leqslant
on the tree nodes, as well as the adjacency relation between the siblings.
Showing this basically amounts to revisiting the proof of
lemma

2.2
and arguing that the atomic algorithmic operations can now be simulated by
\mathsf{FO}
formulas in the augmented tree.

Let us note here that the remainder of the argument would go through even if we assumed that the resulting tree is augmented with graphs from a fixed nowhere dense class
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}

which is not necessarily topological-minor-free.
However, if the original graph
G
merely belongs to a nowhere dense class
\mathscr{C}
,
then the bag graphs, and hence the graphs in the augmented tree, no longer need to belong to any fixed nowhere dense class. In fact, they may contain arbitrarily large cliques.

Continuing with the proof, now it remains to show that we can solve
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking on trees augmented with
H^{\prime}
-topological-minor-free graphs in fixed-parameter time,
where the
\mathsf{FO}
formulas may use the ancestor relation in the tree as well as the edge relation among the siblings.
For this, we lift the usual, automata-based techniques for model-checking on trees, to the case of augmented trees. We define automata that process augmented trees in a bottom-up fashion, labeling the nodes of the tree with states from a finite set of states, starting from the leaves and proceeding towards the root.
At any node
x
, to determine the state of the automaton at
x
, we consider the graph induced on the children of
x
in the augmented tree, vertex-labeled by the states computed earlier. Then the state at
x
is determined by evaluating a fixed collection of first-order sentences on this labeled graph. In this way, all nodes of the tree are labeled by states, and the automaton accepts the augmented tree depending on the state at the root.

By construction, it can be decided in fpt time whether or not
a given such automaton accepts a given tree that is augmented with graphs
that come from, say, a nowhere dense class. Additionally, using standard techniques,
we show that for every first-order sentence
\varphi
on augmented trees there is such an automaton that determines whether
\varphi
holds in a given augmented tree.
Therefore,
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking is fpt on trees augmented with graphs from a nowhere dense class; this in particular applies to classes with excluded topological minors.

To summarize, to model-check a sentence
\varphi
of
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
on a
H
-topological-minor-free graph
G
, we perform the following reasoning:

1.

Using
theorem

2.1
, compute a tree decomposition
\cal T
of
G
with
(q,k)
-unbreakable bags, where
k+2
is the maximal arity of the connectivity predicates in
\varphi
, and
q=q(k)
.

2.

Turn
\cal T
into a tree
\cal T^{\prime}
augmented with
H^{\prime}
-topological-minor free graphs, where
H^{\prime}
depends on
H
and
k
.

3.

In the augmented tree
\cal T^{\prime}
, the connectivity predicate
\mathsf{conn}_{k}(u,v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
can be expressed using a plain
\mathsf{FO}
formula. This follows by analyzing how connectivity predicates are evaluated in constant time, and observing that the atomic operations can be performed using
\mathsf{FO}
formulas in the augmented trees.

4.

Therefore, also
\varphi
can be expressed as a plain
\mathsf{FO}
formula
\varphi^{\prime}
, which is evaluated in
\cal T^{\prime}
.

5.

Convert
\varphi^{\prime}
into an automaton
\cal A
processing augmented trees,
using standard automata-based techniques.

6.

Run
\cal A
on
\cal T^{\prime}
in a bottom-up fashion. This involves performing
\mathsf{FO}
queries on the graphs induced on the children of any given node, and uses one of the known algorithms for
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking on
H^{\prime}
-topological-minor-free graphs.

section

7
describes the basic toolbox of automata on augmented trees,
explaining in detail the last two steps above, abstracting away from the specific application, and providing further results on augmented trees, e.g. concerning query enumeration.
In fact, with the same methods we can show that not only
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking is fpt on trees augmented with graphs from a nowhere dense class,
but also a more general logic
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
combining the power of
\mathsf{FO}
on the augmenting graphs with
\mathsf{MSO}
on trees can be solved in fpt on augmented trees.
We then show in
section

8
how to express the predicates
\mathsf{conn}_{k}
on augmented trees using this more general logic
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
.

Preliminaries

Throughout this paper we assume the standard word RAM model with

, where

the input. Recall that in this model we allow using arrays indexed by

for the existence of data structures for least common ancestor queries

[

]

3.1
Graphs

Basic notation.

We consider finite, undirected graphs
without loops. For a graph

, we write
|G|
for
|V(G)|
and

\|G\|
for
|V(G)|+|E(G)|
. For a pair of vertices

, a

-

is a path with endpoints
u
and
v
. We say that

and

are
connected
in

if there is a

-
v
path in

; otherwise

and

are

. The graph

is
connected if all pairs of its vertices are connected. The

of a path consists of all its vertices apart from the

if each
of them is disjoint with the interior of the other. For a vertex
subset

we write

for the subgraph of
G

induced by
X
.

Trees and ancestors.

A (rooted) tree is an acyclic and connected graph
T
with a
distinguished root vertex
r
. We write
\mathsf{parent}(x)
for the parent of
a node
x
of
T
, and
\mathsf{children}(x)
is the set of children of
x

in
T
. By convention we set
\mathsf{parent}(r)=\bot
. We call a vertex

x\in V(T)
an ancestor of a vertex
y\in V(T)
, written

x\preccurlyeq_{T}y
, or simply
x\preccurlyeq y
if

is clear from the
context, if
x
lies on the unique path between
y
and the root
r
.
Note that we consider every node to be an ancestor of itself. For
nodes
x,y\in V(T)
, we write
\mathsf{lca}(x,y)
for the least common
ancestor of
x
and
y
in
T
. Note that
x
is an ancestor of
y
if
and only if
\mathsf{lca}(x,y)=x
. If
x
is a strict ancestor of
y
(that
is,
x\preccurlyeq y
and
x\neq y
), then we define the
y
-directed
child of
x
to be the unique child
y^{\prime}
of
x
that is an
ancestor of
y
; we denote it by
\mathsf{dir}(x,y)
.

Topological minors.

A
topological minor model
of a
graph
H
in a graph

is an injective mapping
\eta
that maps
vertices of
H
to vertices of
G
and edges of
H
to pairwise
internally vertex-disjoint paths in
G
so that for every

uv\in E(H)
, the endpoints of
\eta(uv)
are
\eta(u)
and

\eta(v)
. We say that

is

-topological-minor-free
if
there is no topological minor model of

in

. A class of graphs

\mathscr{C}
is
topological-minor-free
if there exists a graph

such that every member of
\mathscr{C}
is
H
-topological-minor-free.

Tree decompositions.

A
tree decomposition
of a
graph
G
is a pair
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
, where
T
is a rooted tree and

\mathsf{bag}\colon V(T)\to 2^{V(G)}
is a mapping that assigns to each node

x
of
T
its
bag

\mathsf{bag}(x)
, which is a subset of vertices of

G
. The following conditions have to be satisfied:

1.

For every
u\in V(G)
, the set of nodes
x\in V(T)
satisfying

u\in\mathsf{bag}(x)
induces a connected and nonempty subtree of
T
.

2.

For every edge
uv\in E(G)
, there exists a node
x\in V(T)

such that
\{u,v\}\subseteq\mathsf{bag}(x)
.

Recall that if
r
is the root of
T
, then by convention

\mathsf{parent}(r)=\bot
and
\mathsf{bag}(\bot)=\emptyset
.

With a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
we associate a few auxiliary
mappings. For a node
x\in V(T)
, we define the following:

•

The
adhesion
of
x
is

\mathsf{adh}(x)\coloneqq\mathsf{bag}(\mathsf{parent}(x))\cap\mathsf{bag}(x).

•

The
margin
of
x
is

\mathsf{mrg}(x)\coloneqq\mathsf{bag}(x)-\mathsf{adh}(x).

•

The
cone at
x
is

\mathsf{cone}(x)\coloneqq\bigcup_{y\succcurlyeq_{T}x}\mathsf{bag}(y).

•

The
component at
x
is

\mathsf{comp}(x)\coloneqq\mathsf{cone}(x)-\mathsf{adh}(x)=\bigcup_{y\succcurlyeq_{T}x}\mathsf{mrg}(y).

It is easy to see that the margins
\{\mathsf{mrg}(x)\colon x\in V(T)\}
are
pairwise disjoint and cover the whole vertex set of
G
. Whenever we
use the notation introduced above, the tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
will
be always clear from the context.

The
adhesion
of a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
is defined
as the largest size of an adhesion, that is,

\max_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{adh}(x)|
.

We will commonly assume (without loss of generality) that the tree
decompositions that we work with satisfy some basic connectivity
conditions, subsumed in the following definition. A tree decomposition

\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
is
regular
if for every non-root node

x\in V(T)
we have that

1.

the margin
\mathsf{mrg}(x)
is nonempty;

2.

the graph
G[\mathsf{comp}(x)]
is connected; and

3.

every vertex of
\mathsf{adh}(x)
has a neighbor in
\mathsf{comp}(x)
.

Note that the last two conditions imply that for every node
x
and
every pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in\mathsf{adh}(x)
there is a
u
-
v

path in
G[\mathsf{cone}(x)]
whose interior is contained in
\mathsf{comp}(x)
.

Unbreakability.

A
separation
in a graph
G
is a
pair of vertex subsets
A,B\subseteq V(G)
such that
A\cup B=V(G)

and there is no edge with one endpoint in
A-B
and the other
in
B-A
. The
order
of the separation
(A,B)
is the
size of its
separator

A\cap B
.

For
q,k\in\mathbb{N}
, a vertex subset
X
in a graph
G
is

(q,k)
-unbreakable
if for every separation
(A,B)
of
G
of
order at most
k
, we have

|A\cap X|\leqslant q\qquad\textrm{or}\qquad|B\cap X|\leqslant q.

The notion of unbreakability can be lifted to tree decompositions by
requiring it from every individual bag. There are, however, two
different ways of understanding this.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix
q,k\in\mathbb{N}
. A tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
of a graph
G

is

•

weakly
(q,k)
-unbreakable if for every
x\in V(T)
,

\mathsf{bag}(x)
is
(q,k)
-unbreakable in
G
; and

•

strongly
(q,k)
-unbreakable if for every
x\in V(T)
,

\mathsf{bag}(x)
is
(q,k)
-unbreakable in
G[\mathsf{cone}(x)]
.

It is straightforward to see that strong
(q,k)
-unbreakability
implies weak
(q,k)
-unbreakability, but the converse is not true.

We recall known results about the existence and computability of
unbreakable tree decompositions (
theorem

3.2
was already stated as

Theorem 3.1

(
[
CKL+21
]
)
.

For every graph
G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a weakly

(k,k)
-unbreakable tree decomposition of

of adhesion at most

. Moreover, given
G
and

, such a tree decomposition can be
computed in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot|G|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[

]
)
.

There is a function

and

In this paper we will mostly use

Given any weakly or strongly
(q,k)
-unbreakable decomposition we can,
if necessary, refine it so that it becomes regular. Hence, we may
assume that the tree decompositions constructed by the algorithms of

and

are regular. More precisely, this refinement can be achieved by

]
allows tree decompositions to
be rooted forests rather than rooted trees. However, a rooted forest
can be turned into a rooted tree by adding a fresh root with an empty

from this new root.

Bag graphs.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
be a regular tree decomposition of a graph
G
.
With a node
x
of
G
we associate the
bag graph

\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
,
which is the graph obtained from
G[\mathsf{cone}(x)]
by contracting, for
each
y\in\mathsf{children}(x)
, the set
\mathsf{comp}(y)
into a single vertex,
identified with
y
. More precisely,
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
is a graph defined
as follows:

•

The vertex set of
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
is the disjoint union of

\mathsf{bag}(x)
and the set of children of

in

.

•

For

,

and

are adjacent in
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)

if and only if they are adjacent in

.

•

For

and

,

and

are
adjacent in
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
if and only if there exists
w\in\mathsf{comp}(y)

that is adjacent to

in

.

•

No two distinct

are adjacent in
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
.

Since
\mathcal{T}
is regular, for every

, the

in

is equal to

.

We observe that the sizes of all bag graphs roughly sum up to the size
of the graph

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
be a regular tree decomposition of a graph
G
,
say of adhesion
a
. Then

\sum_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)|\leqslant(a+2)|G|\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sum_{x\in V(T)}\|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)\|\leqslant\|G\|+(a+2)^{2}|G|.

Proof.

For the bound on
\sum_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)|
, observe the vertex
set of
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
is the disjoint union of
\mathsf{adh}(x)
,
\mathsf{mrg}(x)
,
and
\mathsf{children}(x)
. Every adhesion has size at most
a
, every node
of
T
has at most one parent, and every vertex of
G
is in exactly
one margin. Finally,
|T|\leqslant|G|
, hence

\sum_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)|\leqslant a|T|+|G|+|T|\leqslant(a+2)|G|.

For the bound on
\sum_{x\in V(T)}\|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)\|
, call an edge

uv\in E(\mathsf{bgraph}(x))

vital
in the graph
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
if

u,v\in\mathsf{mrg}(x)
. Note that every edge of
G
is vital in at most
one bag graph
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
, hence the sum over all
x\in V(T)
of the
number of vital edges in
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
is at most
|E(G)|
. On the
other hand, the non-vital edges of
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
are those incident to
the vertices of
\mathsf{adh}(x)
and those incident to the children of

x
. The total number of non-vital edges of the first kind is at most

a\cdot|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)|
, as
|\mathsf{adh}(x)|\leqslant a
, and the total number of
edges of the second kind is at most
a\cdot|\mathsf{children}(x)|
, as every
child of
x
has degree at most
a
in
\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V(T)}\|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)|+|E(G)|+a\cdot\sum_{x\in V(T)}\left(|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)|+|\mathsf{children}(x)|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(a+2)|G|+|E(G)|+a(a+2)|G|+a|T|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|G\|+(a+2)^{2}|G|.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We observe that topological-minor-freeness of
G
carries over to bag

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
be a regular tree decomposition of a graph

say of adhesion

is

-topological-minor-free. Then for each

, the graph

is

-topological
-minor-free, where

Proof.

Suppose there is a topological minor model
\eta

in

\mathsf{bgraph}(x)
. Observe that no child of

can be in the image of

Finally, we need an observation about how strong unbreakability of a

.

Let

Proof.

Logic

Signatures.

We will only consider finite relational signatures
\Sigma
consisting
of unary (arity
1
) and binary (arity
2
) relation symbols. A
signature consisting only of unary relation symbols will be called an
alphabet. A
\Sigma

consists of a finite universe

and an interpretation
R(\mathbf{A})\subseteq V(\mathbf{A})^{m}
of
each
m
-ary relation symbol

. When there is no ambiguity,
we will not distinguish between relation symbols and their
interpretations.

Graphs are represented as
\Sigma
-structures where the universe is
the vertex set and
\Sigma
consists of one binary relation symbol

, interpreted as the edge relation.

First-order logic (
\mathsf{FO}
).

We assume an infinite supply
\textsc{Var}_{1}
of first-order variables
and an infinite supply
\textsc{Var}_{2}
of second-order set
variables. First-order variables will always be denoted by small
letters
x,y,z,\ldots
, while second-order variables will be denoted
by capital letters
X,Y,Z,\ldots
.

For a fixed signature
\Sigma
, formulas of first-order logic (
\mathsf{FO}
)
are constructed from atomic formulas of the form
x=y
, where

x,y\in\textsc{Var}_{1}
, and
R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
, where

is an
m
-ary relation symbol and

,
by applying the Boolean operators
\neg
,
\wedge
and
\vee
, and
existential and universal quantification
\exists x
and
\forall x
.

A variable
x
not in the scope of a quantifier is a free variable (we
will not consider formulas with free set variables). A formula without
free variables is a sentence.
We write
\varphi(\bar{x})
to indicate that the free variables of a
formula
\varphi
are contained in the set of variables
\bar{x}
. A
valuation of
\bar{x}
in a set

is a function

\bar{a}\colon\bar{x}\to A
. Let
A^{\bar{x}}
denote the set of all
valuations of
\bar{x}
in
A
.

The satisfaction relation between
\Sigma
-structures and formulas is
defined as usual by structural induction on the formula. When
\mathbf{A}

is a
\Sigma
-structure,
\varphi(\bar{x})
is a formula with free
variables contained in
\bar{x}
, and
\bar{a}\in V(\mathbf{A})^{\bar{x}}
is
a valuation of
\bar{x}
, we write

or
\mathbf{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a})

to denote that
\varphi
holds in
\mathbf{A}
when the variables are
evaluated by
\bar{a}
. We let

\varphi(\mathbf{A}):=\{\bar{a}\in A^{\bar{x}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathbf{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a})\}
.

Separator logic (
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
).

Assume that
\Sigma
contains a distinguished binary relation symbol

E(\cdot,\cdot)
that will always be interpreted as the edge relation
of a graph, that is, as an irreflexive and symmetric relation. For a

\Sigma
-structure
\mathbf{A}
, write

for the graph

(V(\mathbf{A}),E(\mathbf{A}))
.
Separator logic
(
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
) is

\mathsf{conn}_{k}(x,y,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
, for all

, where

{x,y,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in\textsc{Var}_{1}}
. The satisfaction relation
between
\Sigma
-structures and
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
formulas is as for
first-order logic, where the atomic formula

\mathsf{conn}_{k}(x,y,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
holds in a
\Sigma
-structure

with a valuation
\bar{a}
of the variables
x,y,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
to
elements

, if:

{G(\mathbf{A})\models\mathsf{conn}_{k}(s,t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})}
.

Monadic second-order logic (
\mathsf{MSO}
).

Monadic second-order logic (
\mathsf{MSO}
) extends
\mathsf{FO}
by existential and
universal quantification
\exists X
and
\forall Y
over second-order
set variables. We will consider
\mathsf{MSO}
formulas over the signature

A\cup\{\preccurlyeq\}
, where

is a finite alphabet and
\preccurlyeq
is a
binary relation symbol that will always be interpreted as the ancestor

formulas over such signatures.
Hence, formulas of
\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)
will only be evaluated over
rooted trees (represented by the ancestor relation

)
decorated with predicates from

, and the semantics are defined as
usual by structural induction on the formula.

First-order logic with
\mathsf{MSO}
atoms.

We will also consider

\mathsf{FO}
formulas with restricted
\mathsf{MSO}
atoms. Fix a signature
\Sigma

and an alphabet
A
. The logic
\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A),\Sigma)

denotes first-order logic over signature
\Sigma
where in addition to
the standard syntax, one can use formulas of

as

For every node

Data structure

With the basic ingredients in place, we may proceed with the proof of

•

As for initialization, the time needed to compute

is

. It is straightforward to see
that for each particular

is

, as promised.

We now estimate the space usage of the data structure. The data
structures
\mathbb{L}
and
\mathbb{D}
take space
\mathcal{O}(|T|)\leqslant\mathcal{O}(|G|)
and

2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}\cdot|T|\leqslant 2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}\cdot|G|
, respectively, by

lemma

and
lemma

4.2
. By

3.3
, we have

\sum_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{bag}(x)|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(q|G|)\leqslant 2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot|G|
,
hence the total size of the description of the tree decomposition

is

as well. Finally, by

we have

\sum_{x\in V(T)}\|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)\|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(q^{2}|G|)
. Recall that

is obtained from

by removing all
the children of

and turning their neighborhoods into cliques. As
each of these neighborhoods has size at most

and

\sum_{x\in V(T)}|\mathsf{children}(x)|\leqslant|T|\leqslant|G|
, we conclude that
these operations add at most
\binom{q}{2}\cdot|G|

\sum_{x\in V(T)}\|\mathsf{bgraph}(x)\|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(q^{2}|G|)

Before continuing, we need to clarify how exactly each graph

, is represented in the data

The edges of

can be partitioned into

—

— and

the list of all children of

that this representation of

can be computed and
stored within the time and space complexity claimed above.

Lemma 4.4
.

Recall that the data structure contains the

Claim 4.3

Given

The next claim is the key observation: connectivity in

Claim 4.4

Given

Proof of the claim.

By

, the following algorithm

•

Run a breadth-first search from

–

–

–

If the search terminated due to the queue being empty before any

and

•

Do the same with the roles of

•

If neither of the steps above reached the conclusion that

Now it is straightforward to see that the algorithm presented above

Now observe that for every pair

5
Torso queries

In this section we give a proof of
lemma

4.2
. As mentioned,
the main idea is to reinterpret torso queries as queries about
products along paths in a tree labeled with elements of a fixed finite
semigroup, and then design a data structure for this more general kind
of queries. We start by introducing the setting of semigroup-labeled
trees and showing how torso queries fit into this setting. Then we
design a data structure for path product queries in semigroup-labeled
trees. We believe that this data structure is a general tool of
independent interest, hence we actually give two different proofs. The
first one is based on a classic approach using deterministic Simon’s
factorization of Colcombet
[
Col07
]
, and can be viewed as a
reinterpretation of ideas from
[
Col07
]
. The second is a
self-contained, simpler argument that seems new.

Semigroup-labeled trees and path product queries.

Recall that a semigroup
S
consists of a set of elements, called the

support
and also denoted by
S
, and a binary associative
operation on the support called the
product
and denoted

\cdot
. A semigroup is finite if it has finite support.

For a semigroup
S
, an
S
-labeled tree
is a rooted tree
T
together with a map that with each edge
e
of
T
associates an element
\sigma(e)\in S
. Suppose
x,y
are nodes of
T
, where
x
is a strict ancestor of
y
. Then we define

\sigma(x,y)\coloneqq\sigma(e_{1})\cdot\sigma(e_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\sigma(e_{\ell}),

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}
are the consecutive edges of the path from

x
to
y
in
T
(that is,
e_{1}
is incident with
x
and
e_{\ell}
is
incident with
y
).

The following theorem is the main result of this section: it provides
an efficient data structure for path product queries, parameterized by
the size of the semigroup.

Theorem 5.1
.

Fix a finite semigroup
S
and suppose we are given an
S
-labeled
tree
T
. Then one can in time
|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|
construct a
data structure that can answer the following query in time

|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
and using
|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
operations in
S
: given
nodes
x,y\in V(T)
, where
x
is a strict ancestor of
y
, output

\sigma(x,y)
. The data structure uses
|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|

memory and stores
|S|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|
elements of
S
.

Later we give two proofs of
theorem

5.1
, but let us see
now how torso queries can be understood through path product queries
in a semigroup-labeled tree. That is, we are going to prove

lemma

4.2
assuming
theorem

5.1
.

Torso queries as path product queries.

Assume the setting
from the statement of
lemma

4.2
. We interpret torso queries
as path product queries over trees labeled with a finite semigroup of
size
2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}
. For this, we shall use the semigroup of

bi-interface graphs
, introduced for a similar purpose
in
[
BP16
]
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. A
bi-interface graph
of arity
r
consists of a
graph
G
and two partial mappings
\mathsf{left},\mathsf{right}\colon V(G)\rightharpoonup[r]

that are injective and satisfy the following property: if

u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cap\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})
, then
\mathsf{left}(u)=\mathsf{right}(u)
. The domains
of the mappings
\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})
and
\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})
are called the
left
interface
and the
right interface
, respectively.

A bi-interface graph is
basic
if its vertex set is a subset of

[r]\times\{\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S}\}
,

\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})-\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})\subseteq[r]\times\{\mathsf{L}\}
,

\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})-\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\subseteq[r]\times\{\mathsf{R}\}
,

\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cap\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})\subseteq[r]\times\{\mathsf{S}\}
, and

V(G)=\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cup\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})
. By
B_{r}
we denote the set of
all basic bi-interface graphs of arity
r
. Note that

|B_{r}|\leqslant 2^{\mathcal{O}(r^{2})},

as this is an upper bound on the total number of different graphs on
vertex set
[r]\times\{\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R},\mathsf{S}\}
.

Next, we define the
abstraction operation
, which takes a
bi-interface graph
\mathbb{G}=(G,\mathsf{left},\mathsf{right})
and outputs the basic
bi-interface graph
\llbracket\mathbb{G}\rrbracket
, of same arity, defined as follows:

•

First, for a vertex
u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cup\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})
, define

\iota(u)
as

\iota(u):=\begin{cases}(\mathsf{left}(u),\mathsf{L})&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})-\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}}),\\
(\mathsf{right}(u),\mathsf{R})&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})-\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}}),\text{and}\\
(\mathsf{left}(u),\mathsf{S})=(\mathsf{right}(u),\mathsf{S})&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cap\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}}).\end{cases}

•

The vertex set of
\llbracket\mathbb{G}\rrbracket
is

\{\iota(u)\colon u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cup\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})\}
.

•

In
\llbracket\mathbb{G}\rrbracket
there is an edge between
\iota(u)
and

\iota(v)
if and only if in
G
there is a path from
u
to
v

that does not pass through any other vertex of

\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})\cup\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})
.

•

The interface mappings
\mathsf{left}^{\prime},\mathsf{right}^{\prime}
of
\llbracket\mathbb{G}\rrbracket
are
naturally inherited from
\mathbb{G}
:
\mathsf{left}^{\prime}(\iota(u))=\mathsf{left}(u)
for

u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}})
and
\mathsf{right}^{\prime}(\iota(u))=\mathsf{right}(u)
for

u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}})
.

Thus, intuitively speaking,
\llbracket\mathbb{G}\rrbracket
is obtained by applying the
torso operation in
\mathbb{G}
with respect to the union of the interfaces,
and reindexing the vertices according to the labels assigned to the
interfaces.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(G,\mathsf{left}_{G},\mathsf{right}_{G})
and
\mathbb{H}=(H,\mathsf{left}_{H},\mathsf{right}_{H})
be two
bi-interface graphs of arity
r
. The
composition
of
\mathbb{G}
and

\mathbb{H}
is the bi-interface graph
\mathbb{G}\cdot\mathbb{H}=(J,\mathsf{left}_{G},\mathsf{right}_{H})
,
where
J
is obtained from the disjoint union of
G
and
H
by fusing
a vertex
u\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{right}_{G}})
with a vertex
v\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{left}_{H}})

whenever
\mathsf{right}_{G}(u)=\mathsf{left}_{H}(v)
. Thus, bi-interface graphs of arity
r

with the composition operation form an (infinite) semigroup; call it

S_{r}
. We can now endow the basic bi-interface graphs of arity
r

with composition operation
\odot
defined as follows: for basic
bi-interface graphs
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
, we set

\mathbb{A}\odot\mathbb{B}\coloneqq\llbracket\mathbb{A}\cdot\mathbb{B}\rrbracket.

Thus, basic bi-interface graphs of arity
r
endowed with
\odot
form
a semigroup of size
2^{\mathcal{O}(r^{2})}
; call this semigroup
B_{r}
. It is
straightforward to check that the abstraction operation

\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
is a semigroup homomorphism from
S_{r}
to
B_{r}
, for
every
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

With the definitional layer introduced, we can state a lemma that
encapsulates the translation from torso queries to queries over

B_{q}
-labeled trees. This lemma will be used not only in this section,
but also later on for the purpose of viewing computation of torsos in
algebraic terms.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose we are given a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathsf{bag})
of a graph

G
of adhesion
q
. Then one can in time
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|\|G\|

compute injective colorings

\{\lambda_{x}\colon\mathsf{adh}(x)\to[2q]\colon x\in V(T)\}
and a mapping

\sigma\colon E(T)\to B_{2q}
such that the following properties are
satisfied:

•

If
x
is the parent of
y
in
T
,
u\in\mathsf{adh}(x)
, and

v\in\mathsf{adh}(y)
, then

\lambda_{x}(u)=\lambda_{y}(v)\qquad\textrm{if and only if}\qquad u=v.

•

If
x
is a strict ancestor of
y
in
T
, and we denote

\mathbb{G}(x,y)=(G[\mathsf{cone}(x)-\mathsf{comp}(y)],\lambda_{x},\lambda_{y}),

then
\mathbb{G}(x,y)
is a correctly defined bi-interface graph and

\llbracket\mathbb{G}(x,y)\rrbracket=\sigma(x,y).

Proof.

Compute the colorings
\lambda_{x}
in a top-down manner as follows:

•

If
r
is the root of
T
, then
\mathsf{adh}(r)
is empty, hence set

\lambda_{r}
to be the empty coloring.

•

If
y
is a child of
x
and
\lambda_{x}
has already been
defined, then construct
\lambda_{y}
as follows: for each

\lambda_{y}(u)\coloneqq\lambda_{x}(u)
, and then color the vertices
of
\mathsf{adh}(y)-\mathsf{adh}(x)
using colors from

[2q]-\lambda_{x}(\mathsf{adh}(x))
in an arbitrary injective
manner. Note that since
|\mathsf{adh}(x)|\leqslant q
, we have

|[2q]-\lambda_{x}(\mathsf{adh}(x))|\geqslant q

Proof of
theorem

5.1
using deterministic Simon’s factorization.

We now give a proof that follows closely the combinatorial idea
presented by Colcombet in
[
Col07
, Lemma 3]
. See also the
work of Kazana and Segoufin
[
KS13
]
that applies this idea in
the context of enumeration of
\mathsf{MSO}
queries on trees. We need a few
definitions, which are taken directly from
[
Col07
]
.

Fix a finite semigroup
S
. Let
P
be an oriented path whose edges
are labeled by elements of
S

\sigma\colon E(P)\to S
be this
labeling. As
P
is oriented, there is a natural order

on
V(P)

signifying which vertex is earlier. For two nodes
x<y
of
P
, we
denote
\sigma(x,y)=\sigma(e_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot\sigma(e_{\ell})
,
where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}
are the consecutive edges of the subpath of

from
x
to

.

For

, a

of

is a labeling

\lambda\colon V(P)\to[h]

of

are called

\lambda(x)=\lambda(y)=i

\lambda(z)\geqslant i
for all
x<z<y
.
Clearly, being

\lambda^{-1}(i)
. Then the split

is called

if for all vertices

that are pairwise

-neighbors and
satisfy

and
x^{\prime}<y^{\prime}
, we have

\sigma(x,y)\cdot\sigma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\sigma(x,y).

Now let
T
be a rooted tree with edges labeled by elements of
S

through a labeling
\sigma\colon E(T)\to S
. Again, a split of height

h
of

is a labeling
\lambda\colon V(T)\to[h]
. We say that such

restricting it to any root-to-leaf path in

oriented from the root to the leaf.

Colcombet’s theorem states that in every tree there is always a

. The following

[

]
.

Theorem 5.5

(

]
)

at most
|S|
. Moreover, such a split can be computed in time

.

To be more precise, Colcombet states his result in terms of the

As in
[

]
and multiple other applications, the tree
variant stated above can be obtained by running the automaton on every

and run it on the root-to-leaf paths in

Now observe that we can compute the consecutive factors on

Therefore, using (

Automata on augmented trees

We consider
augmented trees
, that are, rooted trees in which the set of children of any given
node carries the structure of a graph, or more generally, a relational
structure (see
fig.

5
).

Figure 5
:
A tree augmented with graphs. The root is at the top, the edges of the tree
are marked black, while the edges of the graphs are marked red,
and they may only connect nodes that are siblings in the tree.
As a first-order relational structure, the tree is equipped with
the ancestor relation induced by the tree, as well as the red edge relation.

Unranked, unordered trees are a special case of this, by
assuming the children form a set with no relations, while ordered
trees are a special case where the children form a total order. Here,
we allow the children to carry an arbitrary structure, but in our
application, this structure will be an edge- and vertex-colored
graph that excludes some fixed topological minor. We also consider
automata that process such trees from the leaves towards the root in a deterministic fashion, by labeling each node by a state from a finite state space. To determine whether a node
v
should be labeled with a state
q
,
a first-order sentence
\delta_{q}
is evaluated in the structure induced by the children of
v
.

Augmented trees can be viewed as relational structures equipped with
the ancestor relation
\preccurlyeq
of the tree, as well as the relations
\Sigma
relating the children of any given node, with which the tree is augmented.
Therefore, the logic
\mathsf{FO}(\preccurlyeq,\Sigma)
is able to mix the two
structures, and express for example that every node whose children satisfy
a certain
\mathsf{FO}(\Sigma)
-definable property has an ancestor that satisfies another
\mathsf{FO}(\Sigma)
-definable property.

We use those automata as a tool for solving various computation problems related to the logic
\mathsf{FO}(\preccurlyeq,\Sigma)
.
Namely, the main results of this section are:

1.

every formula of
\mathsf{FO}(\preccurlyeq,\Sigma)
can be converted into an equivalent automaton on augmented trees
(
theorem

7.1
),

2.

automata on augmented trees, and hence
\mathsf{FO}(\preccurlyeq,\Sigma)
sentences, can be efficiently evaluated on trees augmented with structures from a class
\mathscr{C}
, assuming that the model-checking of first-order logic can efficiently be solved on
\mathscr{C}

(
theorems

7.4
and
7.3
),

3.

automata on augmented trees, and hence
\mathsf{FO}(\preccurlyeq,\Sigma)
formulas, can be efficiently queried on trees augmented with structures from a class
\mathscr{C}
, assuming first-order queries can be efficiently answered on
\mathscr{C}

(
theorems

7.5
and
7.6
),

4.

satisfying valuations to automata on augmented trees, and hence
\mathsf{FO}(\preccurlyeq,\Sigma)
formulas, can be efficiently enumerated on trees augmented with structures from a class
\mathscr{C}
, assuming first-order queries can be efficiently enumerated on
\mathscr{C}

(
theorems

7.11
and
7.12
).

All the results proved in this section are generic and can be applied to trees augmented with various kinds of structures.
More generally, they are proved for the more
powerful logic
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
that
allows to access the tree structure using not only the relation
\preccurlyeq
, but indeed any
\mathsf{MSO}
-definable relation on trees labeled with labels from a finite alphabet
A
.
Our results generalize known results concerning the evaluation and enumeration of
\mathsf{MSO}
queries on usual trees, e.g.
[
KS13
]
.

In the next section, we use the results from this section
in the context of topological minor-free graph classes. Namely, we reduce the problem of evaluating/enumerating
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
queries on such classes to the problem of evaluating/enumerating
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
queries on trees augmented with topological minor-free graph classes.
However, the results obtained in this section may be of independent interest,
and potentially of broader applicability.

In the algorithmic statements proved in this section, we will assume that the class
\mathscr{C}
with which the trees are augmented
is a class for which
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking,
query answering or query enumeration can be solved efficiently.
We remark here that all these assumptions are satisfied whenever
\mathscr{C}
is a topological minor-free graph class,
as will happen in our applications.
More generally, they are satisfied
for any class with bounded expansion, by results of
[
KS20
]
or
[
Tor20
]

of
{\mathcal{A}}
on an augmented tree
T
is a labeling

\rho\colon V(T)\to Q
such that for every node
v
with letter
a
and
whorl
\mathbf{A}_{v}
, the state
q=\rho(v)
is the unique state such that

\delta_{q,a}
holds in the
Q
-labeled
structure
\mathbf{A}_{v}

with labeling
\rho
restricted to
\mathbf{A}_{v}
. Formally,
\mathbf{A}_{v}

is viewed as a structure over the signature
\Sigma\cup Q
,
where a predicate
q\in Q
holds on a vertex
v
if and only if

\rho(v)=q
. The run is
accepting
if it labels the root with
an accepting state. The automaton
{\mathcal{A}}

accepts
an augmented
tree
T
if it has an accepting run on it.

Note that there is exactly one run of
{\mathcal{A}}
on any given augmented
tree
T
, by the assumption that the sentences

(\delta_{q,a})_{q\in Q}
are mutually inconsistent and their
disjunction is equivalent to true. It is undecidable if a given
collection of transition sentences satisfies the
requirements. However, this will not be a concern for us, as all the
automata constructed in the proofs below will satisfy the required
conditions by construction. Moreover, it is not difficult to come up
with an equivalent model with decidable syntax, by syntactically
enforcing that the transition sentences satisfy the required
conditions.

7.2
From
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
to automata

As we will see, automata will have the power to simulate first-order sentences over augmented trees,
that is, for every first-order sentence
\varphi
in the signature of augmented trees there is a automaton
{\mathcal{A}}_{\varphi}
on augmented trees that accepts an augmented tree
T
if and only if
T
satisfies
\varphi
.
We also consider automata that process trees together
with valuations, in order to capture formulas
\varphi(\bar{x})
with free variables. A tree
T
over an alphabet
A
together with a valuation
\bar{a}\colon\bar{x}\to V(T)
is modeled as a tree
T\otimes\bar{a}
over an extended alphabet, defined as follows.

Let
\bar{x}
be a set of variables. Given an
alphabet
A
, construct a new alphabet

A\otimes\bar{x}:=A\times 2^{\bar{x}}
.
That is, an element of this alphabet is a pair
(a,\bar{y})
consisting of a letter
a\in A
and a set of variables
\bar{y}\subseteq\bar{x}
.
Given a tree
T
with labels
from
A
and a partial valuation (partial function)

\bar{a}\colon\bar{x}\rightharpoonup V(T)
, define a new tree
T\otimes\bar{a}

over the alphabet
A\otimes\bar{x}
, in which a node
v
has label

(a,\bar{y})
if and only if
v
has label
a
in
T
and

\bar{y}=\{x\in\bar{x}\mid\bar{a}(x)=v\}
. In particular, taking the
empty partial function
\emptyset\colon\bar{x}\rightharpoonup V(T)
, we obtain
the tree
T\otimes\emptyset
, in which every node
v
that has label

a
in
T
has label
(a,\emptyset)
in
T\otimes\emptyset
.

Fix a finite alphabet
A
and a set of variables
\bar{x}
.
An automaton
with variables
\bar{x}
on augmented trees over the alphabet
A
, denoted
{\mathcal{A}}(\bar{x})
,
is an automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
on augmented trees over the alphabet

A\otimes\bar{x}
. Given an augmented tree
T
we may view
{\mathcal{A}}(\bar{x})
as a query that given a tuple
\bar{a}\in V(T)^{\bar{x}}

determines whether or not
{\mathcal{A}}
accepts the tree
T\otimes\bar{a}
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}(T)\subseteq V(T)^{\bar{x}}
denote the set of valuations

\bar{a}\colon\bar{x}\to V(T)
such that
{\mathcal{A}}
accepts

T\otimes\bar{a}
.

The following theorem states that this automata model is at least as
powerful as
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For every formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
of
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
there is an automaton

{\mathcal{A}}
on augmented trees over the alphabet
A\otimes\bar{x}
such
that for every augmented tree
T
over the alphabet
A
we have

{\mathcal{A}}(T)=\varphi(T)
.
Moreover,

{\mathcal{A}}
is computable from
\varphi
.

In the case when
\Sigma
is empty,
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
boils down to

\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)
. It is well known (see
e.g.
[
Löd12
]
) that
\mathsf{MSO}
on trees is captured by
tree automata, where the automata may count the number of
occurrences of any fixed state in the children, up to a certain
threshold. This can be easily expressed using first-order sentences
(in fact, boolean combinations of existential sentences) over the
signature consisting only of unary predicates denoting the
states. Hence, this yields a tree automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
on augmented
trees for
\Sigma=\emptyset
. Therefore, the case when

\Sigma=\emptyset
follows from known results.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

We proceed by induction on the structure of the formula

\varphi(\bar{x})
. Formally, in the induction we are considering not
only the formula
\varphi
itself, but also a specified set
\bar{x}
of
variables containing the free variables of
\varphi
. In particular, if

\bar{x}\subsetneq\bar{z}
, then
\varphi(\bar{x})
and
\varphi(\bar{z})

are separate cases that we need to consider. However, note that if
we have an automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
over the alphabet

for

as in the statement and

,
then the automaton

can be also viewed as an automaton over
the alphabet

, and this automaton then satisfies
the statement for

.

In the inductive base,

is an atomic formula: either

for some formula
\mu(\bar{x})\in\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)
or

R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
for some

. The first case is
well-known, see the remark preceding the proof, and this yields an
automaton on augmented trees in which the transition sentences do
not use the relations from
\Sigma
. We consider the second case.

The automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
for

, where

,
has states

and

, where

is an accepting

is a rejecting state. Observe that

can only be true if all

are elements of a
fixed
\mathbf{A}_{v}
, as the relation

does not contain tuples with
elements of different whorls. Hence, the automaton can proceed from
the leaves, which we initiate with

, to the root as
follows. For every node

it checks if all the variables

are
interpreted in
\mathbf{A}_{v}
(recall that the alphabet is

) and satisfy

. If this is
the case, then the automaton changes state to
q_{acc}
and
propagates this state. Otherwise, it propagates the
state

. This intuition is formalized by the following
formulas

\displaystyle\delta_{q_{acc},(a,U)}

\displaystyle=\exists y.\,q_{acc}(y)\vee\exists y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}.\,\big(R(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{i\leqslant t}\hskip 5.69054pt\bigvee\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W\subseteq 2^{\bar{x}}}}{{x_{i}\in W}}}\hskip 5.69054pt\bigvee_{a\in A}(a,W)\hskip 2.84526pt(y_{i})\big).

\displaystyle\delta_{q_{rej},(a,U)}

\displaystyle=\neg\delta_{q_{acc},(a,U)}.

The case when
\varphi(\bar{x})
is a disjunction of formulas

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be

\alpha(\bar{x})
and
\beta(\bar{x})

{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{B}}
whose states are pairs
(p,q)
, where

is a

{\mathcal{A}}
and

is a state of
{\mathcal{B}}
, and transition

, where
\delta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{-}
and

are
the transition sentences of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, respectively. The

are pairs
(p,q)
such

{\mathcal{A}}
or

is accepting in
{\mathcal{B}}
. Then the automaton

satisfies the condition
in the statement, for the formula

.

The case when
\varphi
is a negation of a formula is handled by the
usual complementation of automata, since the automata are
deterministic and have exactly one run on every given tree. Hence,
replacing the set

of accepting states with its complement

that accepts a tree

It remains to consider the case when

may be viewed as a formula with one free variable

.

For an augmented tree

and a node

, where

is the valuation mapping

that accepts an augmented tree

holds in

. Let

.

Given a tree

.

Given an augmented tree

over the alphabet

, such that for

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\mathrm{state}({\mathcal{A}},T_{v}\mathop{\otimes}\emptyset),

\displaystyle q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{state}({\mathcal{A}},T_{v}\mathop{\otimes}v),

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{state}({\mathcal{A}},T_{v}\mathop{\otimes}w)\mid w\text{ is a child of $v$ in $T$}\}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{state}({\mathcal{A}},T_{v}\mathop{\otimes}w)\mid w\in V(T),v\prec w\}.

Lemma 7.2
.

There is an automaton
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
with states

Then the automaton

In the following sections, we show how to efficiently evaluate and enumerate
{\mathsf{FO}(\mathsf{MSO}(\preccurlyeq,A)\cup\Sigma)}
-queries
on

-augmented trees.

7.3
Evaluation

First, we show how to evaluate automata on
augmented trees. By
evaluating
an automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
on an
augmented tree
T
we mean the problem of computing the (unique) run

\rho\colon V(T)\to Q
of
{\mathcal{A}}
on
T
, where
Q
is the set of
states of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a logic. We say that labeled model-checking for
\mathcal{L}
on a class
\mathscr{C}
of
\Sigma
-structures is

efficient
if there is a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and
an algorithm that, given a
\Sigma\cup A
-sentence
\delta

(where the elements of
A
are viewed as unary predicates)
and a structure

\mathbf{A}\in\mathscr{C}
labeled with elements of
A
, determines whether it satisfies
\delta
in time

f(|\delta|)\cdot|\mathbf{A}|
. We say that evaluation of automata on

\mathscr{C}
-augmented trees is
efficient
if there is a computable
function
g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and an algorithm that, given an automaton on
augmented trees and a
\mathscr{C}
-augmented tree
T
over an alphabet
A
,
evaluates
{\mathcal{A}}
on
T
in time
f(|{\mathcal{A}}|)\cdot|T|
. Note that
for a
\mathscr{C}
-augmented tree

we have

|T|=\sum_{v\in V(T)}|\mathbf{A}_{v}|+1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of
\Sigma
-structures such that labeled model-checking for first-order logic is
efficient on
\mathscr{C}
.
Then automata evaluation on
\mathscr{C}
-augmented
trees is efficient.

Proof.

Let
Q
be the set of states of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be as in the definition of efficiency of labeled model-checking on
\mathscr{C}
.

Given a
\mathscr{C}
-augmented tree

,
compute the run
\rho\colon V(T)\to Q
of

{\mathcal{A}}
on
T
by processing the nodes
v
of
T
from the leaves
towards the root. Namely, if
v
has label

, evaluate each of the
transition sentences
\delta_{q,a}
(for

) in the whorl

at

restricted to

. Then exactly one of the sentences

holds,
and set

to

.

The total time spent on evaluating the sentences

is
bounded by

\sum_{q\in Q,a\in A,v\in V(T)}f(|\delta_{q,a}|)\cdot|\mathbf{A}_{v}|

for a computable

.
∎

As a corollary, by

Theorem 7.4

.

7.4
Query answering

In the query-answering problem, a query
\varphi(\bar{x})
and an augmented tree
T
are given, and the task is to compute

|T|
a data structure that allows to answer in constant time queries of the form: Does a given tuple
\bar{a}\in V(T)^{\bar{x}}
satisfy
\varphi(\bar{x})
?
This boils down to the query evaluation problem in case when

has no free variables. In this section, we show how to efficiently solve the query-answering problem on
\mathscr{C}
-augmented trees.

Say that labeled query-answering is
efficient

for a logic
\mathcal{L}

on a class of
\Sigma
-structures
\mathscr{C}

if there is a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and an algorithm
that, given a structure
\mathbf{A}\in\mathscr{C}
vertex-labeled
by elements of a finite alphabet

and a

\bar{a}\in V(\mathbf{A})^{\bar{x}}

?

Our goal now is to prove the following.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of
\Sigma
-structures for which labeled query-answering of first-order queries is efficient.
Then labeled query-answering of

-augmented trees vertex-labeled with

.

As previously, we start with reformulating our task

of
\Sigma

\bar{x}

with variables
\bar{x}
and a

-augmented tree

as a query.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
\mathscr{C}

without repetitions. More information on enumeration
algorithms, together with constant delay specifically can be found
in
[
Str19

.

Say that first-order enumeration is

in the signature

(where elements of

\varphi(\mathbf{A})
with delay
f(|\varphi|)
.

9
Lower bounds

We proved that the model-checking problem for separator logic is
fixed-parameter tractable on classes of graphs that exclude a fixed
topological minor. The next natural question is whether this result
can be improved in terms of generality. In this section we prove

theorem

1.3
that shows that, under technical assumptions, this
is not the case.

The reduction is based on the following lemma.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of graphs that is closed under taking subgraphs
and is not topological-minor-free. Then for every sentence
\varphi
of

\mathsf{FO}
over graphs, there is an
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
sentence
\psi
such that
for every graph
G
, there is a graph
H\in\mathscr{C}
such that

G\models\varphi
iff
H\models\psi
.

Proof.

The sentence
\psi
is obtained from
\varphi
by the following syntactic
modifications. First, each quantifier is restricted to the set of
vertices of degree at least
3
. Second, we replace each atom of
the form
E(x,y)
by the following
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
formula, which states
that
x
and
y
are connected by a path of vertices of degree
2
.

\varphi_{E}(x,y)\coloneqq E(x,y)\vee\exists z.\Big(d^{=2}(z)\wedge\mathsf{conn}(x,z)\wedge\mathsf{conn}(y,z)\wedge\forall w.\big(\mathsf{conn}(z,w,x,y)\rightarrow d^{=2}(w)\big)\Big),

Here
d^{=2}(x)
is a formula checking that
x
has degree two. Note
that
\psi
is derived from
\varphi
only, it does not depend on

\mathscr{C}
.

Now let
G
be any graph, we define
H\in\mathscr{C}
as follows. We first
add two private neighbors to each vertex of
G
. This yields a graph

G^{\prime}
. Since
\mathscr{C}
is not topological-minor-free,
G^{\prime}
is a
topological minor of some graph
H\in\mathscr{C}
. As
\mathscr{C}
is closed under
taking subgraphs, we can assume that
H
is a subdivision of
G^{\prime}
.

We finally have
G\models\varphi
iff
H\models\psi
. To see this,
note that all original vertices of
G
have degree at least
3
is

G^{\prime}
; and that, in
H
, two vertices of degree at least three
satisfy
\varphi_{E}
if and only if they are adjacent or connected by a
path of nodes of degree two.
∎

Unfortunately,
lemma

9.1
in general does not
provide an fpt reduction from
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking on general graphs
to
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
model-checking on classes
\mathscr{C}
as in the lemma
statement. This is because the graph
H
whose existence is guaranteed
by the assumptions might not be of size polynomial in the size of
G
,
making the reduction non-fpt.

We get around this obstacle by adding an appropriate
assumption. Recall that a class
\mathscr{C}
admits
efficient encoding
of topological minors
if for every graph
G
there exists

H\in\mathscr{C}
such that
G
is a topological minor of
H
, and, given

G
, such
H
together with a suitable topological minor model can be
computed in time polynomial in
|G|
. Note that in particular such
H

can be only polynomially larger than
G
. We may now
prove
theorem

1.3
.

See
1.3

Proof.

Recalling that
\mathsf{FO}
model-checking on general graphs is

\mathsf{AW}[\star]
-complete
[
FG06
]
, it suffices to apply the
reduction presented in the proof of
lemma

9.1

and note that it can be performed in polynomial time due to the
assumption of polynomial embedding of topological minors.
∎

It is well-known that the subgraph closure of the class of

n
-subdivisions of
n
-vertex complete graphs has bounded
expansion. As this class admits efficient encoding of topological
minors, we obtain the following.

Corollary 9.1
.

There is a class
\mathscr{C}
with bounded expansion such that

\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
model-checking on
\mathscr{C}
is
\mathsf{AW}[\star]
-hard.

Corollary

9.1
provides a separation between the parameterized
complexity of
\mathsf{FO}
and
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
model-checking, as the former problem
is fixed-parameter tractable on every class of bounded
expansion
[
DKT13
]
.

10
Discussion

In this work we have added computational problems related to
\mathsf{conn}

queries in graphs and the logic
\mathsf{FO}\hskip 0.6pt\texttt{{+}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathsf{conn}
to the growing list of
applications of the decomposition theorem of Cygan et
al.
[
CLP+19
]
. Several questions can be asked about possible
improvements of the obtained complexity bounds, especially regarding
the data structure of
theorem

1.1
.

First, in both the model-checking algorithm of
theorem

1.2
and
the construction algorithm of the data structure of

theorem

1.1
, the main bottleneck for the polynomial
factor is the running time of the algorithm to compute the tree
decomposition of Cygan et al. (see
theorem

3.2
).
This running time is cubic in the size of the graph, while in both
applications presented in this work, the remainder of the construction
takes fpt time with at most quadratic dependence on the graph size.
Therefore, it seems imperative to revisit

theorem

3.2
with the purpose of improving the
time complexity of the construction algorithm. We note that polynomial
factor of the running time of the algorithm of

theorem

3.1
is not specified
in
[
CKL+21
]
, but it is also at least cubic.

Second, in
section

6
we showed how to improve the time
complexity of queries to polynomial in
k
by using the weakly
unbreakable tree decomposition provided by

theorem

3.1
. The drawback is that the space usage
of the data structure becomes
k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|G|^{2}
. However, the
quadratic dependence on
|G|
is caused only by the brute-force
implementation of the data structure for torso queries provided by

lemma

6.2
. So now we have two implementations of
this data structure:

•

lemma

4.2
offers query time
2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}
and space
usage
2^{\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}\cdot|T|
.

•

lemma

6.2
offers query time
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
and space usage
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|^{2}
.

Is it possible to design a data structure that would offer query time

q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
while keeping the space usage at
q^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot|T|
?
In our proof of
lemma

4.2
, we apply the more general

theorem

5.1
to the semigroup of bi-interface graphs of
arity
2q
. It is possible that a better understanding of the
structure of this semigroup might lead to an improvement proposed
above.
