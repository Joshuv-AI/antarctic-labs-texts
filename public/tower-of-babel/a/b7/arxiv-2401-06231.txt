Localized Gradual Photomediated Brightness and Lifetime Increase of Superacid Treated Monolayer MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
Kurt H. Tyson, James R. Godfrey, James M. Fraser, Robert G. Knobel

Abstract
Monolayer semiconducting transition metal dichalcogenides (S-TMDs) have been extensively studied as materials for next-generation optoelectronic devices due to their direct band gap and high exciton binding energy at room temperature. Under a superacid treatment of bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (TFSI), sulfur-based TMDs such as MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} can emit strong photoluminescence (PL) with photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) approaching unity. However, the magnitude of PL enhancement varies by more than two orders of magnitude in published reports. A major culprit behind the discrepancy is sulfur-based TMD’s sensitivity to above band-gap photostimulation. Here, we present a detailed study of how TFSI-treated MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} reacts to photostimulation with increasing PL occurring hours after constant or pulsed laser exposure. The PL of TFSI-treated MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} is enhanced up to 74 times its initial intensity after 5 hours of continuous exposure to 532 n \mathrm{n} laser light. Photostimulation also enhances the PL of untreated MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} but with a much smaller enhancement. Caution should be taken when probing MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} PL spectra as above-bandgap light can alter the resulting intensity and peak wavelength of the emission over time. The presence of air is verified to play a key role in the photostimulated enhancement effect. Additionally, the rise of PL intensity is mirrored by an increase in measured carrier lifetime of up to ∼ \sim 400 p \mathrm{p} consistent with the suppression of non-radiative pathways. This work demonstrates why variations in PL intensity are observed across samples and provides an understanding of the changes in carrier lifetimes to better engineer next-generation optoelectronic devices.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) semiconducting materials such as transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) hold promise for ultrathin and flexible optoelectronic devices due to their single molecular layer thinness and lack of out-of-plane dangling bonds
Mak et al. 2010
;
Siao et al. 2018
;
Laturia et al. 2018
. More importantly, at the monolayer limit, semiconducting TMDs (S-TMDs) such as
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
exhibit a direct band gap at the K-K’ location of the Brillouin zone
Mak et al. 2010
with large exciton binding energy (several hundred meV) stable at room temperature
Hill et al. 2015
. In combination with their ability to form heterostructures without the need of lattice matching
Li et al. 2018
;
Lin et al. 2014
, many atomically thin optoelectronic devices such as photodetectors
Lopez-Sanchez et al. 2013
;
Liu et al. 2021
, light emitters
Kwon et al. 2019
;
Hötger et al. 2021
;
Lien et al. 2018
, lasers
Salehzadeh et al. 2015
, and photovoltaics
Singh et al. 2017
;
Tsai et al. 2014
;
Wi et al. 2014
have been demonstrated.

Two key challenges hindering S-TMD based devices are their inherently high defect density and susceptibility to their environment
Aryeetey et al. 2020
;
Mirabelli et al. 2016
;
Park et al. 2018
. This causes the untreated photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) to be low (0.01-6%)
Amani et al. 2015
;
Mak et al. 2010
depending on the quality of the prepared material. Reduction of PLQY greatly limits efficient light emission and absorption. In
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, sulfur vacancies are the dominant defect observed in both chemical vapor deposition and mechanically exfoliated crystals
Roy et al. 2018
;
Hong et al. 2015
;
Pain et al. 2022
. These defects create midgap states which lead to non-radiative recombination of neutral and charged excitons
Bretscher et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2018
;
Goodman et al. 2017
, reducing the PLQY. Attempts to repair the effects of these defect sites have been reported including passivating agents
Bretscher et al. 2021
, chemical dopants
Tanoh et al. 2019
;
Mouri et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2021
, thermal annealing
Su et al. 2017
, and electrostatic gating
Lien et al. 2019
. One of the most successful methods involves a superacid, bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide (TFSI) treatment which demonstrated increased PLQY to near unity at low fluences
Amani et al. 2015
;
Amani et al. 2016
. Although the PL enhancement is thought to involve passivation and p-doping, the exact mechanism is heavily debated
Bretscher et al. 2021
;
Amani et al. 2015
;
Goodman et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Tanoh et al. 2021
. Unfortunately, the PL enhancement using TFSI acid is highly variable between studies and samples, ranging from 10
\times
to over 200
\times
PL
Amani et al. 2016
;
Yamada et al. 2020
;
Tanoh et al. 2019
;
Cadiz et al. 2016
;
Alharbi et al. 2017
;
Roy et al. 2018
. In some cases, the difference is as stark as two orders of magnitude despite using a similar methodology. This raises questions of what factors can cause such discrepancy. Recently, interest has been drawn towards
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
’s photosensitivity where ultraviolet light and hydrogen annealing with light-based "treatments" have been shown to greatly increase PL in ambient conditions
Yamada et al. 2020
;
Sivaram et al. 2019
;
Ardekani et al. 2019
;
Yamada et al. 2021
. In this report, we show that the light used for PL metrology can in fact lead to the photo-enhancement over a period of hours, a possible reason for the large discrepancies in PL intensity in previous work. In addition, we observe not only greater PL enhancement with increasing excitation power but also faster rates of growth, but the enhancement requires air to be present, as verified by our PL measurements in vacuum.

To further probe the origin of the photostimulation, we perform ultrafast single-photon counting lifetime (SPCL) measurements. Increased PL could be due to a reduction in non-radiative pathways, an increase in the number of injected excitons, or a combination of both. In TFSI-treated samples, we observe the PL lifetime increases with photostimulaton, consistent with reduced non-radiative recombination. We observe this improving PL lifetime in a high exciton density regime, which is of particular interest for high-current device applications.

2.4
TFSI-Treated MoS2 Shows Improved PL Lifetime In High Exciton Density Regime

550\,$\mathrm{n}$

13.4\,$\mathrm{n}$

\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

3
Conclusion

We have shown enhancement of PL intensity and increased lifetime in TFSI-treated
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
over hours of above-bandgap laser exposure. This process is observed in both superacid-treated and untreated
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, though modest in untreated samples, indicating the mechanisms at play are not limited to acid treatments alone. The considerably stronger photo-enhancement effect in TFSI-treated
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
may be due to a combination of mechanisms to repair sulfur defects and p-dope the monolayer. We have also observed real-time changes to the lifetime dynamics of treated
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as PL is enhanced. Photo-induced increases to measured PL lifetimes suggest suppression of fast non-radiative pathways rather than purely addition to the total carrier population. Studies involving the quantification of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
’s PL emission spectra are cautioned to control for light exposure to more accurately compare chemical and electrical treatments on S-TMD optics.
TFSI treatment has a synergistic effect with photo-enhancement on PL intensity observed in air while both TFSI-treated and untreated samples display no change in vacuum with laser exposure. The results of this work hope to provide insight and support toward understanding inherent mechanisms at play when engineering efficient and effective 2D TMD-based optoelectronics.

Experimental Methods

4.1
Preparation of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
Monolayers

Bulk
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
crystals are purchased from 2D Semiconductors. Exfoliated
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayers are fabricated using the ‘gold-assisted exfoliation’ method. PL and electrical conductivity of monolayer
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
using this method are equivalent to the ‘scotch-tape’ exfoliation, implying minimal impact on crystal quality
Desai et al. 2016
. 100-orientation silicon wafers with 286 nm of
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
were cleaned with acetone, isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and deionized (DI) water followed by oxygen plasma for
60\,$\mathrm{s}$
in order to create a clean interface. The oxide thickness allows optimal contrast on optical microscopy between single- and multi-layer TMD flakes. Bulk
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
exfoliated by tape had
70\,$\mathrm{n}$
of gold evaporated onto the bulk surface via physical vapour deposition. Thermal release tape was used to lift the gold film and attach monolayer
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
onto a clean silicon wafer. The wafer was heated until the tape was released. Iodine-based (KI) gold etchant (Sigma Aldrich) was used to remove the gold film leaving monolayer
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
on clean silicon. Residual adhesive from the thermal release tape is removed with an additional acetone bath for
1$\mathrm{h}\mathrm{r}$
and thermal annealing at
350${}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$
in argon gas with 5% hydrogen gas. To remove bulk
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
debris, a polypropylene carbonate (PPC) and polymethylsiloxane (PDMS) film is used in conjunction with a micromanipulator system to pick up bulk material and left-over debris while leaving the thin monolayer
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
behind. The PPC/PDMS film is gentle enough to not detach any monolayer
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
which is strongly adhered to the Si
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
substrate via thermal annealing. AFM measurements to verify monolayer thickness are measured on a Bruker Icon AFM in non-contact tapping mode.

4.2
Chemical Treatments

All TFSI treatments are performed in a positive-pressure, nitrogen-filled glove box.
50\,$\mathrm{m}$
of TFSI was diluted in
10\,$\mathrm{m}$
of dichloroethane (DCE) to create a
5.0\,$\mathrm{m}$
solution. A further 1:10 dilution of dichlorobenzene (DCB) was used to create a
0.5\,$\mathrm{m}$
solution.
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
samples are placed in TFSI solution and sealed in a vial for
1\,$\mathrm{h}\mathrm{r}$
. Samples are nitrogen blow-dried followed by a
5\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}$
annealing step at
80${}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$
on a hotplate in ambient conditions to dry the remaining wet acid. Optical characterization and measurements are carried out immediately after TFSI treatment in order to limit the degradation of the TFSI coating and its exposure to light.

4.3
Photoluminescence Characterization

CW PL measurements are performed on a home-built microPL system, equipped with a Thorlabs CPS532 diode laser (
532\,$\mathrm{n}$
center wavelength) as an excitation light source. Excitation spot size is
\sim
2.0
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}
diameter FWHM unless otherwise stated. The objective lens used in all measurements is a Thorlabs C240TMD-B (NA 0.5, f =
8.0\,$\mathrm{m}$
). Fluorescence is analyzed using a Jobin Yvon iHR320 Imaging Spectrometer equipped with an Andor iXon electrically-cooled charge coupled device (CCD) camera. The system can be switched between a white-light imaging mode for locating samples with sub-
2\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
accuracy, and the CW PL measurement mode.

SPCL measurements are performed using an ultrafast light source coupled into the same microPL system used for CW PL measurements. We use
1100\,$\mathrm{n}$
,
300\,$\mathrm{f}$
duration laser pulses generated by a Light Conversion Pharos/Orpheus Laser and optical parametric amplifier (OPA) system at 1 MHz rep rate. The
1100\,$\mathrm{n}$
OPA output is doubled to
550\,$\mathrm{n}$
via second harmonic generation (SHG) in an external
1.0\,$\mathrm{m}$
-thick anti-reflective (AR)-coated beta barium borate (BBO) crystal, with a high-resolution motorized rotation stage and manual rotation mount for angle tuning in two degrees of freedom. Excitation spot size is
\sim
2.0\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
diameter FWHM. Fluorescence from the pulsed excitation is bandpass filtered from
630\,$\mathrm{n}$
to
670\,$\mathrm{n}$
and delivered to a Perkin-Elmer single-photon avalanche photodiode connected to a qutag time-to-digital converter. The IRF is measured by moving to a region of the sample with only bare substrate, removing the longpass filter to permit transmission of the
550\,$\mathrm{n}$
laser, and reduction of a variable attenuator until a signal is detected with similar count rates to the histograms obtained from
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
PL.
