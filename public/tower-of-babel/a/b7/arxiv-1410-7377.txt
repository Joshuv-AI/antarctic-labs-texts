On the entropic structure of reaction-cross diffusion systems


Abstract
This paper is devoted to the study of systems of reaction-cross diffusion equations arising in population dynamics. New results of existence of weak solutions are presented, allowing to treat systems of two equations in which one of the cross diffusions is convex, while the other one is concave. The treatment of such cases involves a general study of the structure of Lyapunov functionals for cross diffusion systems, and the introduction of a new scheme of approximation, which provides simplified proofs of existence.

Introduction

1.1
The system

All the systems that we are going to tackle in this study take the following form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{i}-\Delta[a_{i}(u_{1},\dots,u_{I})\,u_{i}]

\displaystyle=r_{i}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{I})\,u_{i},\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega,\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,I,

(1)

where
a_{i},r_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the unknown being here the family of
I
nonnegative functions
u_{1},\dots,u_{I}
. Here and hereafter,
\Omega
is a bounded open set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose unit normal outward vector at point
x\in\partial\Omega
is denoted by
n=n(x)
. The system is completed with initial conditions given by a family of functions
u_{1}^{in},\dots,u_{I}^{in}
, and homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, that is
\partial_{n}u_{i}:=\nabla u_{i}\cdot n=0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\partial\Omega
for
i=1,\dots,I
.

Introducing the vectorial notations
U:=(u_{1},\dots,u_{I})
and
U^{in}:=(u_{1}^{in},\dots,u_{I}^{in})
, and denoting by
A
and
R
the maps
A:U\mapsto(a_{i}(U)\,u_{i})_{i}
and
R:U\mapsto(r_{i}(U)\,u_{i})_{i}
, the previous system (
1
) can be summarized in the vectorial equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U-\Delta[A(U)]

\displaystyle=R(U),\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega,

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}U

\displaystyle=0,\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\partial\Omega,

(3)

\displaystyle U(0)

\displaystyle=U^{in},\text{ on }\Omega.

(4)

Such systems have received a lot of attention lately (cf.
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
, for example). Their origin is to be found in the seminal paper
[
16
]
, where one typical example
(now known in the literature as the SKT system) is introduced: namely,
I=2
, and
A,R
are given by affine functions:

a_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})=d_{1}+d_{11}\,u_{1}+d_{12}\,u_{2},\qquad a_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=d_{2}+d_{21}\,u_{1}+d_{22}\,u_{2},

r_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})=R_{1}-R_{11}\,u_{1}-R_{12}\,u_{2},\qquad r_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=R_{2}-R_{21}\,u_{1}-R_{22}\,u_{2}.

The corresponding equations model the evolution of individuals belonging to two
species in competition, which increase their diffusion rate in order to
avoid the individuals of the other (or the same) species. This evolution can lead
to the formation of patterns when
t\to\infty
(cf.
[
16
]
).
There is an important literature on the question of existence of global classical solutions to the SKT systems (but only for particular cases). To summarize the approach, one can prove local existence of classical solutions
using Amman’s theorem
[
1
]
and the difficulty relies then in the proof of bounds on the solutions in suitable Sobolev spaces to prevent blow up. The works in this direction always have restrictions on the coefficients (typically no cross diffusion is introduced for one of the species, cf. for example
[
6
]
) and/or on dimension. In particular, it is worth noticing that existence of global classical solutions to the full SKT system (that is, when all coefficients are positive) remains a challenging open problem except in dimension 1.

Our work however deals with the existence of global weak solutions for which an important step forward was made by Chen and Jüngel in

[
4
]
. They indeed showed that a hidden Lyapunov-style
functional (that is, a Lyapunov functional if the terms
r_{1},r_{2}

are neglected) exists for this system
without restriction on coefficients such as strong self diffusion for instance.
In
[
5
]
, this structure was shown to be robust enough to treat
functions
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
such as

a_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})=d_{1}+d_{11}\,u_{1}^{\delta_{11}}+d_{12}\,u_{2}^{\delta_{12}},\qquad a_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=d_{2}+d_{21}\,u_{1}^{\delta_{21}}+d_{22}\,u_{2}^{\delta_{22}},

(5)

when
\delta_{12}\in]0,1[
and
\delta_{21}\in]0,1[
.
A tool coming out of the reaction-diffusion theory (namely, duality lemmas, cf.
[
15
]
)
was also introduced in the context of cross diffusion type systems in
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
. It was then used associated with the entropy structure in
[
5
]

in order to extend the range of entropies that could be treated.

In this paper, we investigate more deeply the structure of systems like
(
1
), in order to exhibit as much as possible
the conditions which enable the existence of Lyapunov-style
functionals. Subsection
3.2
is directly devoted to this study.
As an application, we show that cross-diffusion terms like (
5
)
can be treated as soon as the product
\delta_{12}\,\delta_{21}
belongs to
]0,1[
, thus significantly enlarging the conditions described in
[
5
]
.

Another difficulty appearing in many works on equations involving
cross diffusion is the difficulty, once
a priori
estimates
have been established, to write down an approximation scheme which
implies existence.
An important issue comes from the fact that the entropy structure and the duality estimates
are of very different nature. Therefore it is difficult to build an approximation that preserves both properties. In
[
4
]
and related works, the entropy induces a better integrability, or even boundedness in
[
11
,
12
]
, making the use of duality estimates unnecessary. In
[
5
]
, the approximation procedure is inspired from
[
4
]
and duality is proved to be satisfied
a posteriori
(in fact at some intermediate step).
This is related to a lack of robustness of the Lyapunov-style
functionals (it is indeed difficult to extend them to the approximate system). We therefore present in this paper a new approximation scheme
which is definitely easier to grasp than those presented in

[
4
]
or
[
5
]
. Indeed, we use a time-discretized version of the equation for which existence can be obtained thanks to a standard (Schauder-type) fixed-point theorem, and which conserves the structure of the time-continuous equation from the viewpoint of
a priori
estimates. As a consequence, the passage to the limit when the discretization step goes to
0
is not much more difficult than the passage to the limit in a sequence of solutions to the equation (that is, the weak stability of the equation). Note that a related (yet different) time-discrete approximation was recently used (together with a regularization) in the context of cross-diffusion systems in
[
11
]
.

1.2
Assumptions

Throughout this study, we will state and prove results necessitating one or several of the following assumptions on the parameters of (
1
) :

H1

The functions
a_{i}
and
r_{i}
are continuous from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

H2

For all
i
,
a_{i}
is lower bounded by some positive constant
\alpha>0
, and
r_{i}
is upper bounded by a positive constant
\rho>0
. That is
a_{i}(U)\geq\alpha>0
,

r_{i}(U)\leq\rho
for all
U\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}
.

H3

A
is a homeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}
to itself.

The set of all these assumptions will be invoked at the beginning of each statement (if needed) by writing
(\textbf{H})
. Assumption
H3
will be of utmost importance during the establishment of our approximation scheme. Assumptions
H1
and
H2
are usually
easily checked and appear quite natural. It is however less clear to understand what type of systems satisfies
H3
. We therefore describe in Section
5.1
some examples of systems satisfying
H3
.

1.3
Notations

Since we will always work on
Q_{T}:=[0,T]\times\Omega
, we will simply denote by
\textnormal{L}^{p}_{t}(\textnormal{L}^{q}_{x})
the corresponding evolution spaces and we will use the same convention for Sobolev spaces
\textnormal{H}^{\ell}
and
\textnormal{W}^{\ell,p}
(for
\ell\in\mathbb{N},p\in[1,\infty]
). Cones of nonnegative functions will be noted specified with a
+
subscript, for instance
\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}
is the cone of all essentially bounded nonnegative functions. For
p\in[1,\infty[
,
we will sometimes use the notation
\textnormal{L}^{p^{+}}
to speak of the set of all
\textnormal{L}^{q}
functions with
q>p
. We define also the space
\textnormal{H}^{1}_{m}(\Omega)
of
\textnormal{H}^{1}(\Omega)
functions having zero mean. Its dual is denoted by
\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}(\Omega)
. For any space of functions defined on
\Omega
whose gradient has a well-defined trace on
\partial\Omega
(such as
\textnormal{H}^{2}(\Omega)
or
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
for instance), we add the subscript
\nu
(the former spaces becoming then
\textnormal{H}^{2}_{\nu}(\Omega)
and
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{\nu}(\overline{\Omega})

Given a normed space
X
, we will always denote by
\|\cdot\|_{X}
its norm, except for
\textnormal{L}^{p}
spaces for which we often write
\|\cdot\|_{p}
. If
(x_{n})_{n}
is a sequence of
X
,
(x_{n})_{n}\dot{\,\in\,}X
, and
(x_{n})_{n}\ddot{\,\in\,}X
respectively mean that
(x_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
X
, and
(x_{n})_{n}
is relatively compact in

.

The symbol
|\cdot|
will always represent the Euclidian norm (but possibly in different dimensions depending of the context), whereas
|\cdot|_{1}
will be the Manhattan norm. Finally, given two vectors
X=(x_{i})_{i}
and
Y=(y_{i})_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{I}
, we write

whenever
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all

. We extend this order relation to
\mathbb{R}^{I}
valued functions

and write, for a real number

. The same convention is used
for
<
.

1.4
Main application

The main application of the methods developed in this work is
a new theorem of existence of (very) weak solutions for systems with the same structure as in
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta\Big[u_{1}\,(d_{1}+u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}})\Big]=u_{1}\,(\rho_{1}-u_{1}^{s_{11}}-u_{2}^{s_{12}}),

\displaystyle\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{2}-\Delta\Big[u_{2}\,(d_{2}+u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}})\Big]=u_{2}\,(\rho_{2}-u_{2}^{s_{22}}-u_{1}^{s_{21}}),

\displaystyle\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u=\partial_{n}v=0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\partial\Omega.

(8)

((Very) Weak solution)

\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0

two nonnegative functions in

\displaystyle u_{1}\,[u_{1}^{s_{11}}+u_{2}^{s_{12}}+u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}]\,+\,u_{2}\,[u_{1}^{s_{21}}+u_{2}^{s_{22}}+u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}]\,\in\,\textnormal{L}^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\textnormal{L}^{1}(\Omega)),

(9)

Remark 1.3
.

We consider in this theorem
the case
\gamma_{2}>1
,
\gamma_{1}<1
, which falls outside of the scope of the systems studied in
[
5
]
.

Note that Theorem
1.2
and its proof still hold when one adds some positive constants in front of the non-linearities.

A more technical aspect concerns the self-diffusion, that we have chosen to disregard here.
As it was the case in
[
5
]
, adding self-diffusion terms
tends in fact
here
to facilitate the study of the system, and it
gives rise to extra estimates on the gradients of the densities.

Note also that Theorem
1.2
may be generalized to the case when power rate diffusion coefficients are replaced by mere functions
a_{ij}(u_{i})
, with
ad hoc
assumptions of regularity/increasingness/concavity on the functions
a_{ij}
. An extension to reaction coefficients
R
different from power laws is also certainly possible.

The condition
s_{12}<\gamma_{2}+s_{22}/2
is in fact not optimal. It can be improved using different interpolations. As it will be seen, we have
(A)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{\max(2,s_{21})}u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}<\infty
,
(B)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}+s_{22}}<\infty
and
(C)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}u_{2}^{2}<\infty
. Interpolating between
(A)
and
(B)
leads to the condition
s_{12}<\gamma_{2}+s_{22}(1-1/\max(2,s_{21}))
while interpolating between
(A)
and
(C)
leads to the condition
s_{12}<2-(2-\gamma_{2})(1-\gamma_{1})/(\max(2,s_{21})-\gamma_{1})
. All in all, we get the sufficient condition

s_{12}<\max\bigg(\gamma_{2}+s_{22}/2,\gamma_{2}+s_{22}(1-1/s_{21}),2-(2-\gamma_{2})(1-\gamma_{1})/2-\gamma_{1}),

2-(2-\gamma_{2})(1-\gamma_{1})/(s_{21}-\gamma_{1})\,\bigg).

In this formula, the four different expressions can lead to the best condition on
s_{12}
, depending on the coefficients
s_{21},s_{22},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
. For instance : if
s_{21}=1,s_{22}=2,\gamma_{2}=2
, the best condition is given by the first expression; if
s_{21}=4,s_{22}=2,\gamma_{2}=2
, the best condition is given by the second expression, if
s_{21}=1,s_{22}=1/5,\gamma_{2}=3/2
, the best condition is given by the third expression, if
s_{21}=4,s_{22}=1/5,\gamma_{2}=3/2
, the best condition is given by the fourth expression.

Thanks to estimates (
12
) and (
14
), we can show that the quantity
\nabla\left\{[d_{1}+u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}]u_{1}\right\}
lies in the space
\textnormal{L}^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\textnormal{L}^{1}(\Omega))
, so that
u_{1}
is actually a weak solution of (
6
) (in the sense that in the weak formulation (
10
) we can replace
-\iint\Delta\psi_{1}[d_{1}+u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}]u_{1}
by
\iint\nabla\psi_{1}\cdot\nabla\left\{[d_{1}+u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}]u_{1}\right\}
for all
\psi_{1}\in\mathscr{C}^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{C}^{2}_{\nu}(\overline{\Omega}))
, and therefore by a density argument enlarge the set of test functions
\psi_{1}
to
\mathscr{C}^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathscr{C}^{1}_{\nu}(\overline{\Omega}))
). If
\gamma_{2}\leq 2
, we can use estimates (
12
) and (
14
) to show that
\nabla\left\{[d_{2}+u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}]u_{2}\right\}
also lies in
\textnormal{L}^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\textnormal{L}^{1}(\Omega))
, so that
u_{2}
is actually a weak solution of (
7
) (in an analogous sense).
We use for that the computation
\nabla u_{2}=2\gamma_{2}^{-1}u_{2}^{1-\gamma_{2}/2}\nabla u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}/2}\in\textnormal{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\textnormal{L}^{2}(\Omega))\times\textnormal{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\textnormal{L}^{2}(\Omega))
. However, in the case when

, this computation does not hold true anymore, and in this case our theorem only gives very weak solutions.

2
Semi-discrete scheme

We begin here the presentation of general statements which will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. More precisely, we intend in this section to introduce a semi-discrete implicit scheme to approximate multi-dimensional systems of the form (
1

5

and the references therein), the approximation procedure of these systems frequently leads to intricate or technical methods. The main reason is that the hidden entropy structure often relies on functionals defined on nonlinear subspaces of

(15)

Definition 2.1

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\left(\rho U^{k}-R(U^{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2\rho\tau N}\int_{\Omega}U^{0},

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{I}u_{i}^{k}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{I}a_{i}(U^{k})u_{i}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega,U^{0},A,\rho,N\tau),

(21)

where
C(\Omega,U^{0},A,\rho,N\tau)
is a positive constant depending only on
\Omega
,
A
,
\rho
,
N\tau
and
\|U^{0}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{1}\cap\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}(\Omega)}
.

Remark 2.3

The main feature of the scheme (

(

2.1.1
Fixed point

Proof of Theorem
2.4
: Our aim is to apply the Leray-Schauder fixed point Theorem, which can be stated in the following way (see for example Theorem 11.6 p.286 in
[
8
]
):

Theorem 2.5
.

[Leray-Schauder]
Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a Banach space and let
\Lambda
be a continuous and compact mapping of
[0,1]\times\mathfrak{B}
into
\mathfrak{B}
such that
\Lambda(0,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{B}
. Suppose that there exists a constant
L>0
such that
for all
\sigma\in[0,1]
,

\Lambda(\sigma,x)=x\implies\|x\|_{\mathfrak{B}}<L.

Then the mapping
\Lambda(1,\cdot)
of
\mathfrak{B}
into itself has at least one fixed point.

In order to apply Theorem
2.5
to our problem, we start by defining, for
U\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}
,

\displaystyle\overline{M}(U):=\max\left\{\frac{M_{p,\Omega}}{2\rho},\frac{2+\tau\operatorname*{\max}_{i=1\cdots n}\|r_{i}(U)\|_{\infty}}{\alpha}\right\},

where
p>d/2
is fixed and
M_{p,\Omega}
is the constant defined in Lemma
5.4
. The quantity
\overline{M}(U)
is well defined because
r_{i}
is assumed to be continuous (so that
r_{i}(U)\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
). The definition of
\overline{M}(U)
and the fact that
\rho\tau<1/2
imply the two following inequalities (almost everywhere on
\Omega
)

\displaystyle\overline{M}(U)

\displaystyle>\tau M_{p,\Omega},

\displaystyle\overline{M}(U)A(U)-U+\tau R(U)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

where the second (vectorial) inequality has to be understood coordinates by coordinates.

Consider now the following maps (here both
A
and
R
are extended
to continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{I}
by parity):

\displaystyle\Psi:\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}_{+}\times(\tau M_{p,\Omega},+\infty)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\longmapsto(S+\overline{M}(U)A(U)-U+\tau R(U),\overline{M}(U)),

\displaystyle\Theta:\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}^{I}\times(\tau M_{p,\Omega},+\infty)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}_{+}

\displaystyle(U,M)

\displaystyle\longmapsto(M\textnormal{Id}-\tau\Delta)^{-1}U,

\displaystyle\Phi:\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}^{I}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}^{I}

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\longmapsto A^{-1}(U),

where the inverse operator in the definition of
\Theta
has to be understood with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
and in the strong sense (that is,
\Theta(U,M)\in\textnormal{H}^{2}_{\nu}(\Omega)^{I}
and
(M\textnormal{Id}-\tau\Delta)(\Theta(U,M))

=U
a.e. on
\Omega
).

Notice that for all
M
,
\Theta(\cdot,M)
indeed sends
\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}
to
\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}
thanks to Lemma
5.4
of the Appendix, since
p>d/2
, and
remembering Sobolev embedding
\textnormal{W}^{2,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Using the maximum principle, we also can see that
\Theta(\cdot,M)
preserves the nonnegativeness of the components, so that
\Theta(\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}^{I}\times(\tau M_{p,\Omega},+\infty))\subset\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}^{I}
.

We can therefore consider the mapping
\Phi\circ\Theta\circ\Psi
,
and it is clear that any fixed point of this mapping will give us a solution of the discretized system (
22
). We hence plan to apply the Leray-Schauder Theorem to prove the existence of such a fixed point. We consider for this purpose the map
\Lambda(\sigma,\cdot):=\Phi\circ\sigma\Theta\circ\Psi
. We obviously have
\Lambda(0,\cdot)=0
. Let us first check the continuity and compactness of
\Lambda
, and then look for a uniform estimate for the fixed points of the applications
\Lambda(\sigma,\cdot)
, to prove that
\Lambda(1,\cdot)=\Phi\circ\Theta\circ\Psi
indeed has a fixed point.

2.1.2
Compactness and continuity of
\Lambda
.

Lemma 2.6
.

(
H
)

The map
\Lambda:[0,1]\times\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}\rightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}
is compact and continuous.

Proof.

Thanks to the Heine-Cantor Theorem and the continuity of
A
,
R
and
A^{-1}
(see assumptions
H
), we see that both
\Phi
and
\Psi
are continuous. It is hence sufficient to prove the continuity and compactness of
\sigma\Theta
from
[0,1]\times\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}_{+}\times(\tau M_{p,\Omega},+\infty)
to
\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)_{+}^{I}
. The compactness of this mapping is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
5.4
of the Appendix
together with the corresponding Sobolev embeddings.

For the continuity,
let us define
\tilde{U}:=\Theta(U,M)
. By maximum principle, we see that

\|\tilde{U}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\|U\|_{\infty}}{M}\leq\frac{\|U\|_{\infty}}{\tau M_{p,\Omega}}.

Furthermore, for given
(U,M),(U^{\prime},M^{\prime})
, defining similarly
\tilde{U}^{\prime}:=\Theta(U^{\prime},M^{\prime})
, we can write

M(\tilde{U}-\tilde{U}^{\prime})-\tau\Delta(\tilde{U}-\tilde{U}^{\prime})=(U-U^{\prime})+(M^{\prime}-M)\tilde{U}^{\prime},\quad\partial_{n}(\tilde{U}-\tilde{U}^{\prime})=0.

Still by maximum principle, we immediately get

\displaystyle\|\tilde{U}-\tilde{U}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\left(\|U-U^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+|M^{\prime}-M|\|\tilde{U}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau M_{p,\Omega}}\left(\|U-U^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+|M^{\prime}-M|\frac{\|U^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\tau M_{p,\Omega}}\right),

which yields the continuity of the application
\Theta
, and thereby of the application
\Lambda
.
∎

2.1.3
Estimates on fixed points of
\Lambda(\sigma,\cdot)
.

In order to apply the Leray-Schauder fixed-point Theorem, we need an
a priori
estimate (uniform in
\sigma
) on the fixed points of
\Lambda(\sigma,\cdot)
. The fixed point equation is rewritten as

\displaystyle\overline{M}(U)A(U)-\tau\Delta[A(U)]=\sigma(S+\overline{M}(U)A(U)-U+\tau R(U)).

We prove the

Lemma 2.7
.

(
H
)

For any
\sigma\in[0,1]
, any fixed point
U\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}
of
\Lambda(\sigma,.)
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}\leq C(\Omega,I\|S\|_{\infty}).

Proof.

Notice first that such fixed points are necessarily nonnegative, so that
R(U)\leq\rho U
(assumption
H2
) and

\displaystyle|A(U)|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{I}a_{i}(U)u_{i}\in\textnormal{H}^{2}_{\nu}(\Omega).

The equations associated to the fixed point imply the following vectorial inequality:

\displaystyle\overline{M}(U)(1-\sigma)A(U)+\sigma(1-\tau\rho)U-\tau\Delta[A(U)]\leq\sigma S,\quad\text{ a.e. on }\Omega.

Summing up each coordinate of this inequality, we get

\displaystyle\overline{M}(U)(1-\sigma)|A(U)|_{1}+\sigma(1-\tau\rho)|U|_{1}-\tau\Delta|A(U)|_{1}\leq\sigma|S|_{1},\quad\text{ a.e. on }\Omega,

(23)

which, after multiplication by
|A(U)|_{1}
and integration on
\Omega
, leads to

\displaystyle(1-\sigma)\int_{\Omega}\bar{M}(U)|A(U)|_{1}^{2}+\sigma(1-\rho\tau)\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}

\displaystyle+\tau\int_{\Omega}|\nabla|A(U)|_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\int_{\Omega}|S|_{1}|A(U)|_{1}\,.

For
\sigma=0
, we have
U=0=A(U)
, otherwise we get

\displaystyle(1-\rho\tau)\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|S|_{1}|A(U)|_{1}\leq I\|S\|_{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1},

whence, since
\rho\tau<1/2
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}\leq 2I\|S\|_{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}.

(24)

Thanks to the continuity of
A
, we see that for all
R>0
, there exists
C_{1}(R)>0

such that

\displaystyle|U|_{1}\leq R\Longrightarrow|A(U)|_{1}\leq C_{1}(R),

so that small values of
U
will be handled. On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\int|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|U|_{1}\geq R}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}\geq R\int_{|U|_{1}\geq R}|A(U)|_{1}.

Now cutting the r.h.s. of (
24
) in two terms, corresponding to small and large values of
U
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2I\|S\|_{\infty}\left(\int_{|U|_{1}\geq R}|A(U)|_{1}+\int_{|U|_{1}<R}|A(U)|_{1}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2I\|S\|_{\infty}}{R}\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}+2I\|S\|_{\infty}|\Omega|C_{1}(R).

(26)

Taking
R=4I\|S\|_{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}\leq 4I\|S\|_{\infty}|\Omega|C_{1}(R).

Reusing computation (
25
), we eventually get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|U|_{1}\geq R}|A(U)|_{1}+\int_{|U|_{1}<R}|A(U)|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{R}\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}|U|_{1}+|\Omega|C_{1}(R)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{4I\|S\|_{\infty}}{R}+1\right)|\Omega|C_{1}(R),

which gives the conclusion with
C(\Omega,I\|\textnormal{S}\|_{\infty})=2\,|\Omega|\,C_{1}(4\,I\,\|\textnormal{S}\|_{\infty})
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

(
H
)

There exists a constant
C(\Omega,I\|S\|_{\infty})
depending only on

and

such that for any

Thanks to the nonnegativeness of

and the condition

, inequality (

\displaystyle-\Delta|A(U)|_{1}\leq\frac{\sigma}{\tau}|S|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}|S|_{1},\quad\text{ a.e. on }\Omega.

Applying Lemma

to
w=|A(U)|_{1}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}a_{i}(U)u_{i}\geq 0

\displaystyle 0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{I}a_{i}(U)u_{i}\leq C(\Omega)\left(\frac{1}{\tau}I\|S\|_{\infty}+\int_{\Omega}|A(U)|_{1}\right),\quad\text{ a.e. on }\Omega,

2.2.1

Let us first give a few properties of regularity for the family

Proposition 2.9

(
H
)
.

For all
k\geq 1
and
p\in[1,\infty[
,

\displaystyle U^{k}

\displaystyle\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})^{I},

\displaystyle U^{k}

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{A,R,\tau}\text{ on }\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle A(U^{k})

\displaystyle\in\textnormal{W}^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)^{I},

where
\eta_{A,R,\tau}>0
is a strictly positive constant depending on the maps
A
and
R
and
\tau
.

Proof.

Since
R
is continuous, for each
k\geq 0
,
R(U^{k})\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I}
. We hence can write, for any positive constant
M>0
and any

\displaystyle MA(U^{k})-\Delta[A(U^{k})]=\frac{U^{k-1}-U^{k}}{\tau}+R(U^{k})+MA(U^{k})\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{I},

so that we can directly apply Lemma

of the Appendix

for all finite values of

, whence, by Sobolev embedding,
A(U^{k})\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})^{I}
, and

thanks to Assumption
H3
.
For the lower bound on

, we will proceed by induction and prove that if
U^{k-1}\geq\varepsilon
for some constant
\varepsilon>0
, then

for another constant

2.2.2
(Uniform)
\textnormal{L}^{1}
estimate

We write down the standard
\textnormal{L}^{1}
estimate obtained by a direct integration of the equations:

Proposition 2.10

(
H
)
.

Assuming that
\rho\tau<1/2
, the family
(U^{k})_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq k\leq N-1}\int_{\Omega}U^{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2\rho\tau N}\int_{\Omega}U^{0},

(30)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\left(\rho U^{k}-R(U^{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2\rho\tau N}\int_{\Omega}U^{0}.

(31)

Proof.

For (
30

:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}U^{k}\leq(1-\rho\tau)^{-k}\int_{\Omega}U^{0}.

(32)

Indeed, thanks to Assumption
H2
, (

) implies (almost everywhere on
\overline{\Omega}
)

\displaystyle\frac{U^{k}-U^{k-1}}{\tau}-\Delta A(U^{k})\leq\rho U^{k}.

(33)

Integrating then (
33
) on
\Omega
, we get

\displaystyle(1-\rho\tau)\int_{\Omega}U^{k}\leq\int_{\Omega}U^{k-1},

so that (
32
) follows by a straightforward induction. Since
\tau N=T
and
\rho\tau<1/2
, we have
(1-\rho\tau)^{-N}\leq 2^{2\rho\tau N}
, whence (

). Integrating the first equation in (

, and summing for

, we get

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}R(U^{k})\leq\int_{\Omega}U^{0},

so that using (

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\left(\rho U^{k}-R(U^{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tau\rho\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-\rho\tau)^{-k}\int_{\Omega}U^{0}+\int_{\Omega}U^{0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\tau\rho}{1-\rho\tau}\frac{(1-\rho\tau)^{-(N-1)}-1}{(1-\rho\tau)^{-1}-1}+1\right]\int_{\Omega}U^{0}

2.2.3
(Uniform) Duality estimate

We now focus on another uniform (in
\tau
) estimate for this scheme, which is reminiscent of a duality lemma first introduced in
[
13
]
, which writes:

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\rho>0
. Let
\mu:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a continuous function lower bounded by a positive constant. Smooth nonnegative solutions of the differential inequality

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Delta(\mu u)\leq\rho u\text{ on }[0,T]\times\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}(\mu u)=0\text{ on }[0,T]\times\partial\Omega,

satisfy the bound

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{T}}\mu u^{2}\leq\exp(2\rho T)\times\left(\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}^{2}\,\|u^{0}\|_{\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}(\Omega)}^{2}+\langle u^{0}\rangle^{2}\int_{Q_{T}}\mu\right),

where
u^{0}:=x\mapsto u(0,x)
,
\langle u^{0}\rangle
denotes its mean value on
\Omega
and
\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}
is the Poincaré-Wirtinger constant.

The proof of the previous lemma can be easily adapted from the proof of lemma A.1 in Appendix A of
[
2
]
. We are here concerned with the following discretized version:

Lemma 2.12
.

Fix
\rho>0
and
\tau>0
such that
\rho\tau<1
. Let
(\mu^{k})_{0\leq k\leq N-1}
be a family of nonnegative functions
which are integrable on
\Omega
. Consider a family
(u^{k})_{0\leq k\leq N-1}
of nonnegative bounded functions, such that for all
k
,
\mu^{k}u^{k}\in\textnormal{H}^{2}(\Omega)
, and which satisfies in the strong sense

\displaystyle\frac{u^{k}-u^{k-1}}{\tau}-\Delta(\mu^{k}u^{k})

\displaystyle\leq\rho u^{k},\text{ on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}(\mu^{k}u^{k})

\displaystyle=0,\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Then, this family satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{k}|u^{k}|^{2}\leq(1-\rho\tau)^{-2N}\left(\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}^{2}\,\|u^{0}\|_{\textnormal{H}_{m}^{-1}}^{2}+\langle u^{0}\rangle^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{k}\right).

Proof.

Since
\rho\tau<1
, the sequence of nonnegative functions
v^{k}:=(1-\rho\tau)^{k}u^{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle v^{k}-\frac{\tau}{1-\rho\tau}\Delta\mu^{k}v^{k}

\displaystyle\leq v^{k-1},\text{ a.e. in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}v^{k}

\displaystyle=0,\text{ a.e. on }\partial\Omega.

Summing from
k=1
to
k=n
we get for all
1\leq n\leq N-1
,

\displaystyle v^{n}-\frac{1}{1-\rho\tau}\Delta S^{n}

\displaystyle\leq v^{0}\text{ a.e. in }\Omega,

where

\displaystyle S^{n}:=\tau\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu^{k}v^{k}\,\in\,\textnormal{H}^{2}(\Omega).

We multiply this last inequality by
\tau\mu^{n}v^{n}=S^{n}-S^{n-1}
(with the convention
S^{0}=0
) and integrate over
\Omega
. We get

\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}|v^{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{1-\rho\tau}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla S^{n}-\nabla S^{n-1})\cdot\nabla S^{n}\leq\int_{\Omega}v^{0}(S^{n}-S^{n-1}),

and therefore,

\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}|v^{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2(1-\rho\tau)}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla S^{n}|^{2}-|\nabla S^{n-1}|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}v^{0}(S^{n}-S^{n-1}).

We sum up over
n
again to obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}|v^{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2(1-\rho\tau)}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla S^{N-1}|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}v^{0}S^{N-1}.

Using Poincaré-Wirtinger’s and Young’s inequalities, the right-hand side can be dominated by

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v^{0}S^{N-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}v^{0}(S^{N-1}-\langle S^{N-1}\rangle)+\langle v^{0}\rangle\int_{\Omega}S^{N-1}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}\|v^{0}\|_{\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}(\Omega)}\|\nabla S^{N-1}\|_{2}+\langle v^{0}\rangle\int_{\Omega}S^{N-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}^{2}\|v^{0}\|_{\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\|\nabla S^{N-1}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}+\langle v^{0}\rangle\int_{\Omega}S^{N-1},

from which we get, since

,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}|v^{n}|^{2}\leq\frac{\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}^{2}\|v^{0}\|_{\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}}^{2}}{2}+\langle v^{0}\rangle\int_{\Omega}S^{N-1}.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality one easily gets from the definition of
S^{N-1}

\displaystyle\langle v^{0}\rangle\int_{\Omega}S^{N-1}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}|v^{n}|^{2}}\sqrt{\langle v^{0}\rangle^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}},

so that using another time Young’s inequality, one obtains

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}|v^{n}|^{2}\leq\textnormal{C}_{\Omega}^{2}\|v^{0}\|_{\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}}^{2}+\langle v^{0}\rangle^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n},

from which we may conclude using
v^{n}=(1-\rho\tau)^{n}u^{n}
.
∎

We eventually apply the previous lemma to get the following estimate:

Corollary 2.13

(
H
)
.

Suppose
\rho\tau<1/2
. Then there exists a constant
C(\Omega,U^{0},A,\rho,N\tau)
depending only on
\Omega
,

,
\rho
,
N\tau
and
\|U^{0}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{1}\cap\textnormal{H}^{-1}_{m}(\Omega)}
such that the family

satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{I}u_{i}^{k}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{I}a_{i}(U^{k})u_{i}^{k}\right)\leq C(\Omega,U^{0},A,\rho,N\tau).

(34)

Proof.

In order to apply Lemma
2.12
to inequality (
33
), we introduce two families of real-valued functions :

\displaystyle u^{k}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{I}u_{i}^{k},

\displaystyle\mu^{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{I}a_{i}(U^{k})u_{i}^{k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{I}u_{i}^{k}}.

Notice that
\mu^{k}
is a well-defined nonnegative function on

thanks to Proposition
2.9

Summing all coordinates of the vectorial inequality (

\displaystyle\frac{u^{k}-u^{k-1}}{\tau}-\Delta(\mu^{k}u^{k})\leq\rho u^{k}.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{k}|u^{k}|^{2}\leq 2^{4\rho N\tau}C(\Omega,u^{0})\left(1+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}\mu^{k}\right).

From the definition of

The entropy estimate for two species

This section is devoted to the elucidation of the hidden entropy
structure for equations of the form (

section are not really needed in the proof of Theorem

, since the computations which are presented here as

a priori

Let us first recall that in

was investigated a system that
took the following general form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta\big[(d_{11}(u_{1})+a_{12}(u_{2}))\,u_{1}\big]

\displaystyle=u_{1}\big(\rho_{1}-s_{11}(u_{1})-s_{12}(u_{2})\big),

(35)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{2}-\Delta\big[(d_{22}(u_{2})+a_{21}(u_{1}))\,u_{2}\big]

\displaystyle=u_{2}\big(\rho_{2}-s_{22}(u_{2})-s_{21}(u_{1})\big),

(36)

under various conditions on the functions
d_{ij}
,
a_{ij}
,
s_{ij}
, for
i,j\in\{1,2\}
, One of these conditions was that both
a_{12}
and
a_{21}
had to be increasing and concave. We intend here to relax this assumption in order to include several convex/concave cases. Due to the large number of possibilities, the treatment of this system in its full generality (that is, all possible functions

,

and
s_{ij}

(subsection

).

3.1
A simple specific example

Since self-diffusion (
d_{ii}
functions) usually tends to improve the
estimates, we consider the case of constant self-diffusion rates. Similarly, since reaction terms have no real influence on the entropy structure,
we will not consider them here.
Consider hence the following system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta\Big[u_{1}(d_{1}+u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}})\Big]

\displaystyle=0,

(37)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{2}-\Delta\Big[u_{2}(d_{2}+u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}})\Big]

\displaystyle=0,

(38)

where
d_{1},d_{2},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
. The entropy exhibited in
[
5
]
, corresponds to the limitation :
\gamma_{1}\in]0,1[
,
\gamma_{2}\in]0,1[
(we speak only about the control of the entropy here). Let us explain how to control an entropy in cases when
\gamma_{2}>1
under the condition
\gamma_{1}<1/\gamma_{2}
.

We present the following result:

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider
d_{1}>0
,
d_{2}>0
and
\gamma_{2}>1
,
0<\gamma_{1}<1/\gamma_{2}
. Let
(u_{1},u_{2})
be a classical (that is, belonging to
\mathscr{C}^{2}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})
) positive (that is, both
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are positive on
Q_{T}
) solution to (
37
) – (
38
) with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. Then the following
a priori
estimates hold for
i=1,2
and all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{i}(t)=\int_{\Omega}u_{i}(0),

(39)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}(t)^{\gamma_{2}}+C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big\{|\nabla u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}/2}|^{2}+|\nabla u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}/2}|^{2}+\left|\nabla(u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}/2}u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}/2})\right|^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T,u_{1}(0),u_{2}(0)}.

(40)

Proof.

Integrating on
[0,t[\times\Omega
eq. (
37
) – (
38
), we obtain estimate (
39
).

Define

\displaystyle h_{1}(t):=t-\frac{t^{\gamma_{1}}}{\gamma_{1}},\quad h_{2}(t):=\frac{t^{\gamma_{2}}}{\gamma_{2}}-t,

so that both functions reach their minimum for
t=1
. Define the corresponding entropy

\displaystyle\textnormal{E}(u_{1},u_{2}):=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{1-\gamma_{1}}\int_{\Omega}\left[h_{1}(u_{1})-h_{1}(1)\right]\,+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{2}-1}\int_{\Omega}\left[h_{2}(u_{2})-h_{2}(1)\right]\,.

Differentiating this functional and performing the adequate integrations by parts, we get the following identity:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\textnormal{E}(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1}d_{1}\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-2}|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}+\gamma_{2}d_{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-2}|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\,u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}\left[\gamma_{1}\left|\frac{\nabla u_{1}}{u_{1}}\right|^{2}+\gamma_{2}\left|\frac{\nabla u_{2}}{u_{2}}\right|^{2}+2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\frac{\nabla u_{1}}{u_{1}}\cdot\frac{\nabla u_{2}}{u_{2}}\right]=0.

Since
0<\gamma_{1}\,\gamma_{2}<1
, the quadratic form
Q
associated to the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{2}&\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\\
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}&\gamma_{1}\end{pmatrix}

is positive definite, whence the existence of a constant
C>0
such as

\displaystyle Q(x,y)\geq C\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right).

Using the previous inequality in the entropy identity, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\textnormal{E}(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1}d_{1}\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-2}|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\gamma_{2}d_{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-2}|\nabla u_{2}|^{2}+C\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\,u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}\left[\,\left|\frac{\nabla u_{1}}{u_{1}}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\nabla u_{2}}{u_{2}}\right|^{2}\,\right]\leq 0,

from which we obtain, changing the constant
C

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\textnormal{E}(u_{1}(t),u_{2}(t))+C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}/2}|^{2}+C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}/2}|^{2}+C\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\sqrt{u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\,u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}}\right|^{2}\leq 0.

(41)

Since
h_{1}
reaches its minimum at point
1
, we have
h_{1}(u_{1})-h(1)\geq 0
, and integrating
the previous inequality on
[0,t]
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}-1}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}(t)^{\gamma_{2}}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}/2}|^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}/2}|^{2}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\sqrt{u_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\,u_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{2}-1}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}(t)+\textnormal{E}(u_{1},u_{2})(0),

and the conclusion follows using (
39
).
∎

3.2
The general entropy structure

Let us consider the general form (
2
) in the case of two species. As before, we assume that
R=0

in order to simplify the presentation.

We will now perform rather formal (unjustified) computations, in order to bring out the entropy structure. In Section
4
, all these computations will be rigorously
justified (under the corresponding set of assumptions), at the (semi-)discrete level, in order to prove the existence of global weak solutions to eq. (
6
), (
7
) [Theorem
1.2
].

We consider a function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
If
U
is solution of (
2
) with
R=0
, introducing
W:=\nabla\Phi(U)
, a straightforward computation (yet completely formal) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U)-\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U),\Delta[A(U)]\rangle=0,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner-product on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whence after integration by parts, using the repeated index convention,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U),\Delta[A(U)]\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{i}\Phi)(U)\partial_{jj}[A_{i}(U)]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}[(\partial_{i}\Phi)(U)]\partial_{j}[A_{i}(U)]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}\Phi(U)\partial_{j}u_{k}\partial_{\ell}A_{i}(U)\partial_{j}u_{\ell}.

Summing first in the
i
index, we recognize a matrix product and we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U),\Delta[A(U)]\rangle=-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\partial_{j}U,\textnormal{D}^{2}\Phi(U)\textnormal{D}(A)(U)\partial_{j}U\rangle,

where
\textnormal{D}(A)
is the Jacobian matrix of
A
,
and
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)
is the Hessian matrix of
\Phi
. We eventually get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U)+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\partial_{j}U,\textnormal{D}^{2}\Phi(U)\textnormal{D}(A)(U)\partial_{j}U\rangle=0.

(42)

This identity implies that
\Phi(U)
becomes a Lyapunov functional of the system, as soon as
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)\textnormal{D}(A)
is positive-semidefinite (in the sense of symmetric matrices, that is, its symmetric part is positive-semidefinite). The previous computation motivates the following definition

Definition 3.2

(Entropy)
.

Consider
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
an open set. A real valued function
\Phi\in\mathscr{C}^{2}(D)
is called an
entropy
on
D
for the system (
2
) if it is nonnegative, and satisfies, for all
X\in D
,
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)\textnormal{D}(A)(X)
is positive-semidefinite (in the sense that its symmetric part is positive-semidefinite).

Remark 3.3
.

Because of the nonnegativeness of
\Phi
, one easily gets the estimate
\Phi(U)\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}_{t}(\textnormal{L}^{1}_{x})
(depending on the initial data), but in fact (
42
) often implies much more than this simple estimate for the solutions of the system. Indeed, in many situations, the second term in the l.h.s. of (
42
) yields
estimates on the gradients of the unknowns (as in Subsection
3.1
). This will be of crucial importance in
Section
4
, since no other estimate on the gradients is known for the system
considered in this application.

Remark 3.4
.

The set
D
depends on the type of system that we consider. It is the set of expected values for the vector solution
U
. In our framework, we expect positive solutions, that is
D=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, but it is sometimes useful to consider bounded sets for
D
, this is for instance the case in
[
11
]
.

We end this section with a simple generic example, for two species, for which we do have an entropy on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, and which will cover the specific example treated in Subsection
3.1
. As explained before, self-diffusion generally eases the study of the system. To simplify the presentation, we hence assume here again that the diffusion terms are purely of cross-diffusion type, that is
A(U)=(u_{i}a_{i}(u_{j}))_{i=1,2}
with
j\neq i
. The idea is then simple, if
\det\textnormal{D}(A)
and
\textnormal{Tr}\,\textnormal{D}(A)
are both nonnegative, then
\textnormal{D}(A)
has two nonnegative eigenvalues and is hence not far from being positive-semidefinite, in the sense that it would indeed be positive-semidefinite if it were symmetric ; this last property may be satisfied after multiplication by a diagonal matrix, keeping the positiveness of the trace and the determinant: this will be done thanks to
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)
. More precisely, we present the following elementary proposition:

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider
a_{1},a_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
two
\mathscr{C}^{1}
functions and for
X=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
define

\displaystyle A(X)

\displaystyle:=(x_{i}a_{i}(x_{j}))_{i}\quad(i\neq j),

\displaystyle\Phi(X)

\displaystyle:=\phi_{1}(x_{1})+\phi_{2}(x_{2}),

where
\phi_{i}
is a nonnegative second primitive of
z\mapsto a_{j}^{\prime}(z)/z
(
i\neq j
). If
a_{1},a_{2}
are increasing and
\det\textnormal{D}(A)\geq 0
, then
\Phi
is an entropy on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
for the system (
2
), in the sense of Definition
3.2
.

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}(A)(X)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(x_{2})&x_{1}a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2})\\
x_{2}a_{2}^{\prime}(x_{1})&a_{2}(x_{1})\end{pmatrix},\quad\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{a_{2}^{\prime}(x_{1})}{x_{1}}&0\\
0&\frac{a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2})}{x_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

so that

\displaystyle M(X):=\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)\textnormal{D}(A)(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\star&a_{2}^{\prime}(x_{1})a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2})\\
a_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2})a_{2}^{\prime}(x_{1})&\star\end{pmatrix}

is obviously symmetric. If the functions
a_{i}
are increasing, all the coefficients of
M(X)
are nonnegative, so that
\textnormal{Tr}\,M(X)\geq 0
; we also see that

\displaystyle\det M(X)=\det\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(X)\det\textnormal{D}(A)(X)\geq 0,

which allows to conclude.
∎

. Since each term of the previous inequality is (at least) integrable, we can integrate it on
\Omega
, and sum over
1\leq k\leq\ell
in order to get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U^{\ell})-\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),\Delta[A(U^{k})]\rangle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),R(U^{k})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U^{0}).

(55)

Now since
\nabla\Phi(U^{k})\in\textnormal{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)^{2}
for all
p<\infty
(see above), the following integration by parts rigorously holds
(because
A(U^{k})
satisfies homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),\Delta A(U^{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\langle\partial_{j}U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(U^{k})\textnormal{D}(A)(U^{k})\partial_{j}U^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(U^{k})\textnormal{D}(A)(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle,

(56)

where
\textnormal{D}(A)
is the Jacobian matrix of
A
, and
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)
is the Hessian matrix of
\Phi
(and the last line is a small abuse of notation). At this stage, we know, thanks to Proposition
3.5
and the very definition of
\Phi
, that the r.h.s. of (
56
) is nonnegative. However, as noticed in Remark
3.3
, the mere lower bound by
0
is most probably a bad estimate. Indeed, we will see that the r.h.s. of (
56
) will allow us to obtain estimates for the gradient of
U^{k}
(as it was the case in Subsection
3.1
). We can first compute
\textnormal{D}(A)
(recall the definition of
A
in (
44
) ) and
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{D}(A)(X)=\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}+x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}&\gamma_{2}x_{1}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-1}\\
\gamma_{1}x_{2}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-1}&d_{2}+x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-2}&0\\
0&\gamma_{2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-2}\end{pmatrix}.

Writing
\textnormal{D}(A)(X)=\text{diag}(d_{1},d_{2})+M_{A}(X)
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(U^{k})\textnormal{D}(A)(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)\,\text{diag}(d_{1},d_{2})(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(U^{k})M_{A}(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle,

and since
\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)
is diagonal,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(U^{k})\textnormal{D}(A)(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=d_{1}\gamma_{1}\int_{\Omega}(u_{1}^{k})^{\gamma_{1}-2}|\nabla u_{1}^{k}|^{2}+d_{2}\gamma_{2}\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}-2}|\nabla u_{2}^{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},S(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle,

(57)

where
S(X)=\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(X)M_{A}(X)
. Let us now write

\displaystyle S(X)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-2}&0\\
0&\gamma_{2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}&\gamma_{2}x_{1}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-1}\\
\gamma_{1}x_{2}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-1}&x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}&\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-1}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-1}\\
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-1}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}-1}&\gamma_{2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}-2}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}x_{1}^{-2}&\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}^{-1}\\
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}^{-1}&\gamma_{2}x_{2}^{-2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

so that eventually

\displaystyle S(X)

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{-1}&0\\
0&x_{2}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle:=L}}{{\overbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\\
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}&\gamma_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}}}}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{-1}&0\\
0&x_{2}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

which means that (the quadratic form associated to) the matrix
S(X)
acts on
W:=(w_{1},w_{2})
through

\displaystyle\langle W,S(X)W\rangle=x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}\langle Z,LZ\rangle,

where
Z:=(w_{1}/x_{1},w_{2}/x_{2})
. But since
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}<1
, the quadratic form associated to
L
is positive definite, whence the existence of a constant
C:=C(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})>0
such as

\displaystyle\langle W,S(X)W\rangle\geq C\left(\frac{w_{1}^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}}+\frac{w_{2}^{2}}{x_{2}^{2}}\right)x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}.

Going back to (
57
) and using the previous inequality, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla U^{k},\textnormal{D}^{2}(\Phi)(U^{k})\textnormal{D}(A)(U^{k})\nabla U^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq d_{1}\gamma_{1}\int_{\Omega}(u_{1}^{k})^{\gamma_{1}-2}|\nabla u_{1}^{k}|^{2}+d_{2}\gamma_{2}\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}-2}|\nabla u_{2}^{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle+C\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{|\nabla u_{1}^{k}|^{2}}{(u_{1}^{k})^{2}}+\frac{|\nabla u_{2}^{k}|^{2}}{(u_{2}^{k})^{2}}\right)(u_{1}^{k})^{\gamma_{1}}(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq C\int_{\Omega}\Big\{|\nabla(u_{1}^{k})^{\gamma_{1}/2}|^{2}+|\nabla(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}/2}|^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle+C\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\sqrt{(u_{1}^{k})^{\gamma_{1}}\,(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}}}\right|^{2},

where we changed the constant
C
in the last line. If we call
\Gamma_{k}
the r.h.s. of the previous equality, we sum up the previous estimates (
55
) and write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U^{\ell})+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\Gamma_{k}\leq C\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),R(U^{k})\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U^{0})\right).

Since
\textnormal{U}^{0}
(=
U^{0}_{N}
) approaches
U^{in}
in
\textnormal{L}^{1}(\Omega)\times\textnormal{L}^{\gamma_{2}}(\Omega)
and
|\Phi(x_{1},x_{2})|\lesssim 1+|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|^{\gamma_{2}}
, it is easy to check that
\|\Phi(U^{0}_{N})\|_{1}\lesssim 1+\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{\gamma_{2}}^{\gamma_{2}}
up to some irrelevant constant (independent of
N
of course). On the other hand,
\phi_{1}\geq 0
and
z^{\gamma_{2}}\lesssim\phi_{2}(z)+z
. Using the
\textnormal{L}^{1}
estimate (
19
) given by Theorem
2.2
, we infer eventually

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{\ell})^{\gamma_{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\Gamma_{k}\leq C\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),R(U^{k})\rangle+1+\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{\gamma_{2}}^{\gamma_{2}}\right).

Obtaining the desired estimate now
reduces to handling the reaction terms. Notice that from the definition of
R
,
R(X)=(\rho_{1}x_{1},\rho_{2}x_{2})-R^{-}(X)
,
with
R^{-}(X)\geq 0
given by

\displaystyle R^{-}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(x_{1}^{s_{11}}+x_{2}^{s_{12}})\\
x_{2}(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}})\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\rho=\max(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
, estimate (
20
) of Theorem
2.2
implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}R^{-}(U^{k})\leq C(\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{1}).

(58)

Using the definition of
\phi_{i}
, one easily checks that for all
x_{i}>0
,
x_{i}\phi^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\lesssim\phi_{i}(x_{i})+1
, up to some irrelevant constant.
We hence get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{\ell})^{\gamma_{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\Phi(U^{k})+1+\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{\gamma_{2}}^{\gamma_{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle-C\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),R^{-}(U^{k})\rangle,

which, using
\phi_{1}(z)\lesssim 1+z
and
\phi_{2}(z)\lesssim 1+z^{\gamma_{2}}
together with estimate (
19
), may be written

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{\ell})^{\gamma_{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}}+1+\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{\gamma_{2}}^{\gamma_{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle-C\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\Phi(U^{k}),R^{-}(U^{k})\rangle.

Expanding
\langle\nabla\Phi(X),R^{-}(X)\rangle
, we get

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\Phi(X),R^{-}(X)\rangle=\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})x_{1}(x_{1}^{s_{11}}+x_{2}^{s_{12}})+\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2})x_{2}(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}}).

Since
\gamma_{2}>1
, we can write
\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2})x_{2}=c_{2}(x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}-x_{2})
with
c_{2}>0
. Furthermore, if
x_{1}\geq 1
, one easily checks that
\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})\geq 0
, and on the other hand,
x_{1}\mapsto x_{1}\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})
continuously extends to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and is hence lower bounded for
x_{1}\leq 1
by some negative constant
m_{1}
. All in all, we get for
X\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
the estimate

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\Phi(X),R^{-}(X)\rangle\geq m_{1}(x_{1}^{s_{11}}+x_{2}^{s_{12}})+c_{2}(x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}-x_{2})(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}}),

whence

\displaystyle c_{2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}})-\langle\nabla\Phi(X),R^{-}(X)\rangle\leq|m_{1}|(x_{1}^{s_{11}}+x_{2}^{s_{12}})+c_{2}x_{2}(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}}).

Remember now that
s_{11}<1
and
s_{12}<\gamma_{2}+s_{22}/2
. The last inequality implies in particular the existence of a constant
C>0
such as
|m_{1}|x_{2}^{s_{12}}\leq C+c_{2}x^{\gamma_{2}+s_{22}}/2
, so that we have using
x_{1}^{s_{11}}\leq 1+x_{1}

\displaystyle\frac{c_{2}}{2}x_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}})-\langle\nabla\Phi(X),R^{-}(X)\rangle\leq|m_{1}|(1+x_{1})+c_{2}x_{2}(x_{2}^{s_{22}}+x_{1}^{s_{21}}),

and we may eventually write (changing the constant
C
again)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{\ell})^{\gamma_{2}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}}\left\{(u_{1}^{k})^{s_{21}}+(u_{2}^{k})^{s_{22}}\right\}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\Gamma_{k}

(59)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}^{k})^{\gamma_{2}}+1+\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{\gamma_{2}}^{\gamma_{2}}\right),

(60)

where we used estimates (
19
) and (
20
) (with its consequence (
58
)) to get the estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\tau\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{k}\{(u_{2}^{k})^{s_{22}}+(u_{1}^{k})^{s_{21}}\}\leq C(\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{1}).

We may now conclude using a discrete Gronwall Lemma. Indeed, if we call
w_{\ell}
the first integral in the l.h.s. of (
59
) and define
w_{0}:=C(1+\|u_{1}^{in}\|_{1}+\|u_{2}^{in}\|_{\gamma_{2}}^{\gamma_{2}})
, we have (since
\Gamma_{k}\geq 0
)

\displaystyle(1-C\tau)w_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}w_{k}+w_{0},

whence, as soon as
C\tau<1
,

\displaystyle(1-C\tau)^{\ell}\,w_{\ell}\leq C\tau\,\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}(1-C\tau)^{\ell-1}\,w_{k}+(1-C\tau)^{\ell-1}\,w_{0},

from which we get by a straightforward induction

\displaystyle w_{\ell}\leq\frac{w_{0}}{(1-C\tau)^{\ell}}\,,

and the conclusion of the proof of Proposition
4.1
easily follows
from this last estimate.
∎

4.3
Passage to the limit

We come back to the proof of Theorem
1.2
. We introduce at this level the

Definition 4.2
.

For a given family
h:=(h^{k})_{0\leq k\leq N-1}
of functions defined on
\Omega
, we denote by
\underline{h}^{N}
the step (in time) function defined on
\mathbb{R}\times\Omega
by

\displaystyle\underline{h}^{N}(t,x):=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h^{k}(x)\mathbf{1}_{]k\tau,(k+1)\tau]}(t).

We then have by definition, for all

,

\displaystyle\|\underline{h}^{N}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{q}\big([0,T];\textnormal{L}^{p}(\Omega)\big)}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau\|h^{k}\|^{q}_{\textnormal{L}^{p}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/q},

and in particular

\displaystyle\|\underline{h}^{N}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{p}(Q_{T})}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tau\int_{\Omega}|h^{k}(x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}.

Using an analogous notation for the family of vectors
(U^{k}_{N})_{0\leq k\leq N-1}

that equation (

on
\mathbb{R}_{-}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\underline{U}^{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(U^{k}_{N}-U^{k-1}_{N})\otimes\delta_{t^{k}}+U^{0}_{N}\otimes\delta_{0}\text{ in }\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(]-\infty,T[\times\Omega)^{2}

(61)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\tau((\Delta[A(U^{k})]+R(U^{k}))\otimes\delta_{t^{k}}+U^{0}_{N}\otimes\delta_{0}\text{ in }\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(]-\infty,T[\times\Omega)^{2},

(62)

where

\displaystyle\underline{u_{1}}^{N}+\underline{u_{2}}^{N}

\displaystyle\nabla(\underline{u}_{1}^{N})^{\gamma_{1}/2}

\displaystyle\dot{\,\in\,}\textnormal{L}^{2}_{t,x},

where (

5.1
Examples of systems satisfying

In this section, we provide sufficient conditions on the functions

\displaystyle A:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}

\displaystyle X:=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{I}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(X)\,x_{1}\\
\vdots\\

is a homeomorphism from

to itself. More precisely, in our framework assumptions
H1
and
H2
are satisfied for the functions

(that is, continuity and positive lower bound) and we assume the existence of a convex entropy. This last property implies in particular that

5.1.1
Two species with increasing diffusions

We start here with the case when
I=2
(two species).

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
a_{1},a_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
are continuous functions, lower bounded by
\alpha>0
. Assume that on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
A
is strictly increasing (that is, each component is strictly increasing w.r.t. each of its variables) and that on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
,
A
is
\mathscr{C}^{1}
and
\det\textnormal{D}(A)
remains strictly positive. Then
A
is a homeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
to itself and a
\mathscr{C}^{1}
-diffeomorphism from
(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{2}
to itself.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
A
is a bijection from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
to itself. Then, the inverse function theorem ensures that
A
is a diffeomorphism on
(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ast})^{2}
. Thanks to the positive lower bound for
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
and the continuity of
A
on
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
, it is easy to check that
A^{-1}
is continuous on
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
.

Let us fix
(f,g)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
and find
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
such that
A(u,v)=(f,g)
, that is

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}(u,v)\\
A_{2}(u,v)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(u,v)\,u\\
a_{2}(u,v)\,v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}.

We first solve the first equation, considering
u
as the unknown: for any
v\geq 0
, the function
A_{1}(\cdot,v):u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto a_{1}(u,v)\,u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is strictly increasing (by assumption) and onto (due to the continuity and positivity of
a_{1}(\cdot,v)
). Therefore it is a bijection: we write
u=u_{f}(v)
the only solution in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of
a_{1}(u,v)\,u=f
.

The monotonicity of
A_{1}
(in
u
and
v
) implies that
u_{f}
is strictly decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. This together with the continuity of
A_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
implies that
u_{f}
belongs to the class
\mathscr{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
: indeed, for any
v\geq 0
, if
v_{n}
is a decreasing (resp. increasing) sequence converging to
v
, then
u_{f}(v_{n})
is increasing (resp. decreasing) and upper (resp. lower) bounded by
u_{f}(v)
, therefore it converges to some limit
l
satisfying
A(l,v)=\lim A(u_{f}(v_{n}),v_{n})=f
, that is,
l=u_{f}(v)
. Furthermore, we have on
(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ast})^{2}
,
\det\textnormal{D}(A)=[\partial_{u}A_{1}][\partial_{v}A_{2}]-[\partial_{1}A_{2}][\partial_{2}A_{1}]>0
. By the assumption of monotonicity of
A
, the four derivatives appearing here are nonnegative, hence
\partial_{u}A_{1}>0
. Therefore by the implicit function theorem,
u_{f}
belongs to the class
\mathscr{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ast})
, and for all
v>0
,
u_{f}^{\prime}(v)=-\{\partial_{v}A_{1}/\partial_{u}A_{1}\}(u,u_{f}(v))
.

We then inject
u=u_{f}(v)
in the second equation: we want to solve
a_{2}(u_{f}(v),v)v=g
. For
v>0
, we compute the derivative

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\{A_{2}(u_{f}(v),v)\}=[u_{f}^{\prime}(v)\partial_{u}A_{2}+\partial_{v}A_{2}](u_{f}(v),v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\partial_{u}A_{1}}\,\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{u}A_{1}&\partial_{v}A_{1}\\
\partial_{u}A_{2}&\partial_{v}A_{2}\end{pmatrix}(u_{f}(v),v)>0.

The function
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto a_{2}(u_{f}(v),v)v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous, strictly increasing by the previous computation, and it is onto (by continuity and thanks to the lower bound for
a_{2}
). We write
v_{f,g}
the only solution
v\geq 0
of
a_{2}(u_{f}(v),v)\,v=g
. Then
(u,v)=(u_{f}(v_{f,g}),v_{f,g})
is the only solution of
A(u,v)=(f,g)
.
∎

5.2
Elliptic estimates

We start by recalling the following standard elliptic estimate (see for instance Theorem 2.3.3.6 in
[
10
]
)

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
p\in(1,\infty)
and any regular open set
\Omega
, there exists positive constants
M_{p,\Omega}
and
C_{p,\Omega}
such that for all
M>M_{p,\Omega}
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}&Mw-\Delta w=f\in\textnormal{L}^{p}(\Omega),\\
&w\in\textnormal{W}^{2,1}_{\nu}(\Omega).\end{array}\right\}\Longrightarrow\|w\|_{\textnormal{W}^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p,\Omega}\|f\|_{p}\,.

Using this result we get the following useful Lemma:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f\in\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and let
w
satisfy

w\in\textnormal{H}^{2}_{\nu}(\Omega),\;w\geq 0,\;\ -\Delta w\leq f\text{ in }\Omega.

Then there exists
C:=C(\Omega)
such that

\|w\|_{\infty}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{\infty}+\|w\|_{1}\right).

(79)

Proof.

First, we fix
p\in(d/2,\infty)
and
M>M_{p,\Omega}
(see Lemma
5.4
) and rewrite the equation as
Mw-\Delta w\leq f+Mw
. Using
w\geq 0
, the comparison principle, the elliptic estimate of Lemma
5.4
and the Sobolev embedding
\textnormal{W}^{2,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we get

\displaystyle\|w\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|f+Mw\|_{p}\right)\leq C\left(\|f\|_{p}+M\|w\|_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|f\|_{p}+M\|w\|_{\infty}^{(p-1)/p}\|w\|_{1}^{1/p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|f\|_{p}+\varepsilon\|w\|_{\infty}+c(\varepsilon)\|w\|_{1}\right)\mbox{ (Young's inequality)},

and we conclude by choosing
\varepsilon
small enough.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Obviously, the conclusion of Lemma
5.5
would be the same when one only assumes that

, for some

.
