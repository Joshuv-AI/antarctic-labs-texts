A randomized algorithm for nonconvex minimization with inexact evaluations and complexity guarantees
Shuyao Li, Stephen J. Wright

Abstract
We consider minimization of a smooth nonconvex function with inexact oracle access to gradient and Hessian (without assuming access to the function value) to achieve approximate second-order optimality. A novel feature of our method is that if an approximate direction of negative curvature is chosen as the step, we choose its sense to be positive or negative with equal probability. We allow gradients to be inexact in a relative sense and relax the coupling between inexactness thresholds for the first- and second-order optimality conditions. Our convergence analysis includes both an expectation bound based on martingale analysis and a high-probability bound based on concentration inequalities. We apply our algorithm to empirical risk minimization problems and obtain improved gradient sample complexity over existing works.

Introduction

Given a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we seek a point
x
that approximately satisfies optimality conditions to be a local minimizer of
f
.
Specifically, for small positive tolerances
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}
, we say that
x
is an
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order point
when

\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\epsilon_{g},\quad\lambda_{\min}\left(\nabla^{2}f(x)\right)\geq-\epsilon_{H}.

(1)

We define a simple algorithm for finding such a point and analyze its complexity, expressed in terms of
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}
, Lipschitz constants, failure probabilities, and algorithmic constants.
Our approach is based on a method using exact derivative information in which steps of only two types are taken: gradient descent steps and negative curvature steps for the Hessian.
It allows gradient and Hessian evaluations to be
inexact
, where the gradient inexactness is bounded in a relative sense (that is, as a fraction of the norm of the quantity being approximated).
Moreover, it does not require evaluation of the
value
of the objective function
f
1
1

1

Approaches of this type are called “objective-function-free optimization (OFFO)” in
[
GJT23
]
, which is an essential element of line-search and trust-region methods.
In such methods, the function value evaluation may need to be quite precise (and thus expensive and intolerant of noise
[
CS18a
,
CMS18
,
Bla+19
,
CSS19
,
BCS21
]
) when the amount of decrease in
f
over a step is small.
Instead, it makes use of conservative short steps to ensure descent.
Our strategy does not allow the algorithm to exploit local properties of the function (as opposed to its worst-case behavior).

A distinctive feature of our method is that it “flips a coin” to decide whether to move in a positive or negative sense along a direction of negative curvature for an approximate Hessian.
(A more technical term for this strategy would be “Rademacher randomness.”)
Because of this feature, our step may not yield descent in
f
on every step; this fact must be accounted for in our analysis.
We can still obtain reasonable complexity results despite possible non-monotonicity because steps that increase
f
are taken only when the gradient is small, so that the increase in
f
at any such step is limited.

Our complexity results include both expected and high-probability stopping times for the algorithm; see Sections
2.2
and
2.3
, respectively.
Section
3
gives a more detailed comparison with several prior works, chiefly
[
Yao+22
]
, focusing on the requirements for gradient inexactness and on the usefulness of decoupling the tolerances
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}
.

In Section
4
), we focus on the case in which
f
has “finite-sum” form

f(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x),

(2)

for a (typically large) value of
N
.
We derive bounds on the number of samples (evaluations of
\nabla f_{i}(x)
or
\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)
for some

) required to meet the required level of inexactness for
\nabla f(x)
and
\nabla^{2}f(x)
at each iterate.
Finally, we give a “total sample-operation complexity” result, which is a bound on the total number of samples needed to find the point satisfying (
1
), to high probability.

Finally, our approach to the analysis does not require a strong coupling between the tolerances
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}

We state several corollaries to our main result to show how various choices of these quantities, where both are expressed in terms of some underlying tolerance

In prior works, a coupling of
\epsilon_{H}=O(\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}})

\epsilon_{H}=o(\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}})

1.1
Prior Work

Approximate Second-Order Points.

Finding global minima of smooth nonconvex functions is intractable in general.
A common surrogate problem is to seek approximate second-order stationary points.
This approach is advantageous in many practical settings where local minima are in fact global minima, as in the case (under certain assumptions) in tensor decomposition
[
Ge+15
]
, dictionary learning
[
SQW17
]
, phase retrieval
[
SQW16
]
, and low-rank matrix factorization problems
[
GJZ17
]
such as sensing, completion, and robust PCA.
See a discussion of this issue in
[
Jin+17
, Section 1]
.
A comprehensive technical review
[
ZQW20
]
discusses the loss landscape of problems in which there are no spurious local minima.

There are first-order methods that make perturbations to gradient descent
[
Jin+17
,
Jin+21
]
or stochastic gradient descent
[
Ge+15
,
Jin+21
]
) directions to guarantee convergence to second-order stationary points. In some cases these methods require careful hyperparameter tuning and their iteration complexity can be high.

Second-order methods can make use of negative curvature directions of the Hessian (instead of random noise) to escape saddle points more efficiently. We focus on two types of computational complexity for this class of algorithms.

Iteration complexity
describes the number of iterations needed to achieve the target accuracy, where each iteration may involve solving a difficult trust region problem or minimizing a cubic function.

Operation complexity
describes the total number of gradient evaluations and Hessian-vector products needed to attain target accuracy.

For trust-region methods,
[
CGT12
]
obtained iteration complexity
O(\max\{\epsilon_{H}^{-1}\epsilon_{g}^{-2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\})
and
[
GYY16
]
obtained
O(\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\})
iteration complexity. For operation complexity,
[
Cur+21
]
designed a Newton-CG trust region method that achieves
\tilde{O}\big({\epsilon_{g}^{-7/4}}\big)
, assuming
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
.

Cubic regularization
[
NP06
]
and its adaptive variants
[
CGT11
]
find approximate second-order points with iteration complexity
O(\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\})
and
O(\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\})
, respectively.
With further assumptions on the cubic subproblems,
[
CGT12
]
also obtained
O(\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\})
iteration complexity.
A recent algorithm described in
[
GJT23
]
matches this complexity while also eliminating the need for function value evaluations, a property that our algorithm also enjoys.
However, it lacks second-order guarantees when derivatives are inexact.
Unique to this algorithm is its adaptability: Despite not requiring function evaluations, it does not require prior knowledge of Lipschitz parameters.
It achieves adaptivity by choosing steps based on a model function that becomes steadily more conservative as the algorithm progresses, possibly even more conservative than the usual Taylor-series-based upper bound.
For the best operation complexity to our knowledge,
[
Aga+17
]
achieved
\tilde{O}\big({\epsilon_{g}^{-7/4}}\big)
, assuming
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
.

Fixed-step and line-search methods are most relevant to our current paper.

[
RW18
,
ROW20
]
use a line search method that takes a Newton-CG step when the gradients are large and computes a negative curvature step when the gradients are small.
They obtain iteration complexity of
O\big({\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\}}\big)
and
\tilde{O}\big({\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3}\epsilon_{H}^{3},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\}}\big)
, respectively, and their operation complexity is a factor of
\epsilon_{H}^{-1/2}
greater than their iteration complexity.

[
Car+18
]
similarly combined accelerated gradient descent and minimum eigenvector to achieve operation complexity
O\big({\epsilon_{g}^{-7/4}}\big)
, assuming
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
.

Inexact derivatives.

There is increasing interest in finding approximate second-order stationary points when exact derivatives are not available.
Some papers directly model inexactness in the gradient and Hessian oracles.
Others pay special attention to the sampling setting, where the objective function has a finite-sum structure
f=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}
or stochastic structure
f(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}f(\cdot,\omega)
and the inexactness comes from (batch) subsampling of
f_{i}
or
f(\cdot,w)
. Since the algorithms and their guarantees are quite different in the inexact and stochastic settings, we review these alternatives separately.

Stochastic settings (including finite-sum problems (
2
)).

Specific to stochastic settings, we use the term “total sample-operation complexity” to describe the total number of gradient or Hessian-vector product evaluations of a single sample
f_{i}
.
Perturbed stochastic gradient descent
[
Ge+15
,
Jin+21
]
has total sample-operation complexity
\tilde{O}\big({\epsilon_{g}^{-4}}\big)
when
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
.

[
Arj+20
]
obtained approximate second-order stationary points with total sample-operation complexity

O\big({\epsilon_{g}^{-3}+\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\epsilon_{H}^{-2}+\epsilon_{H}^{-5}}\big)
.
With access to stochastic gradients and stochastic Hessian-vector products, they improve on Natasha 2
[
All18
]
and use stochastic gradient descent with a variance reduction scheme when gradients are large, and Oja’s method to compute a negative curvature direction when gradients are small.
They also utilize a step of coin-flipping similar to ours to decide the sense of the negative curvature direction in their complex methods, but their inexactness is assumed to come only from sampling and they obtain only a constant-probability bound.
If access to full stochastic Hessians is assumed, they consider cubic regularization methods to remove the dependence on the gradient Lipschitz constant in their complexity bounds.
(We do not focus on reducing the dependence on gradient or Hessian Lipschitz constants.)

[
Ber+22
]
considered batch subsampling
settings, combining a Newton-type line search method with a routine
that uses minimum eigenvectors to escape saddle points.
They obtained iteration complexity
O\big({\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2}}\big)
(assuming

\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
), while each iteration might require
O\big({\epsilon_{H}^{-1/2}}\big)

Hessian-vector products, resulting in
O\big({\epsilon_{H}^{-7/4}}\big)
operation complexity. Only an expectation bound on the iteration complexity was proved.
Application of this approach to batch subsampling setting is challenging because it requires more accurate access to function value oracle: for each iteration, they require
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-2})
samples to approximate the gradient,
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-1})
samples to approximate the Hessian — and
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-3})
samples to approximate function values.

Inexact settings beyond (sub)sampling.

Some works have examined a setting in which the inexactness in gradient, Hessian, and sometimes function value oracles is not due to sampling.
We use the term “general inexact” to refer to this setting.
The stochastic settings discussed above can be viewed as a special case of the general inexact setting, because one can use
O(\delta_{g}^{-2})
samples to construct an inexact gradient oracle and
O(\delta_{H}^{-2})
samples to construct an inexact Hessian oracle with high probability, where
\delta_{g}
and
\delta_{H}
are the gradient and Hessian inexactness requirements.
(Details are given in
[
XRM20
,
Tri+18
,
JSX23
]
and Section
4
.)
In addition, for all works reviewed below (but not our paper),
\delta_{g}=O(\epsilon_{g})
and
\delta_{H}=O(\epsilon_{H})
.
Since algorithms for general inexact settings do not consider the finite-sum or stochastic structure of the problem, the sample complexity and total sample-operation complexity derived in this way are generally not competitive with algorithms that are customized to stochastic settings.
However, the concept of general inexactness is useful in the context of outliers and adversarial corruptions
[
Li+23
]
, making it an important setup to consider.

[
XRM20
]
analyzed trust-region methods and cubic regularization algorithms; they considered the settings where Hessians are inexact but gradients and function values are exact.

[
Tri+18
]
introduced a stochastic cubic regularization method that tolerates inexactness in both gradients and Hessians, assuming
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
.
Although their results are stated only for stochastic settings, their analysis can be extended to the general inexact case.
They obtained iteration complexity
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2})
, which translates to
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-7/4})
operation complexity. In batch subsampling settings, the total sample-operation complexity is
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-7/2})
.

[
Yao+22
]
considers a fixed-step approach that has guarantees for finding approximate second-order stationary points assuming
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}}
, where the gradients and Hessians are inexact.
They obtained the same iteration complexity
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2})
as in
[
Tri+18
]
, and the operation complexity and the total sample-operation complexity in batch subsampling settings are therefore the same.
The settings are quite similar to ours, but we can tolerate more inexactness in the gradient, as discussed below in Section
3
.

Concurrent with our work,
[
Liu+23
]
have proposed a trust region method that tolerates both gradient and Hessian inexactness and achieves iteration complexity
O(\max\{\epsilon_{H}^{-1}\epsilon_{g}^{-2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\})
.
However, this method requires either exact function value evaluation or else batch subsampling in a finite-sum setting to approximate the function value.

[
PS20
]
considers the iteration complexity of finding an approximate first-order stationary point for convex and nonconvex objectives. This line search algorithm requires access to the approximate function value.
[
JSX23
]
analyzed the oracle complexity and also considered trust region methods, and similarly obtained only first-order guarantees. We mention those papers because they allow for
relative
error in the gradient, as does our algorithm.

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

We first construct a supermartingale
M_{k}
based on function values.
We need to select a filtration to define this supermartingale formally.
We define the filtration
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
to be the filtration generated by
\sigma_{i},\;i\leq k-1
, the randomness used to compute the minimum eigenvalue and eigenvector of
H_{i},\;i\leq k
, the randomness (if any) used by the inexact gradient and Hessian oracles to compute
g_{i}
and
H_{i},\;i\leq k
, and the randomness (if any) used to compute the stepsize
\alpha_{i},\;i\leq k

It follows that

and
\sigma_{k}\notin\mathcal{G}_{k}
.

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{k}\right]=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[2\frac{\alpha_{k}}{M}\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}\sigma_{k}\hat{p}_{k}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]=0
.
We therefore have from (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{k+1})\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{2\epsilon_{H}^{3}}{9M^{2}}.

By combining with the minimum decrease (
5
) obtained when the gradient step is taken, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{k+1})\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\leq f(x_{k})-\min\left(\frac{\epsilon_{g}^{2}}{6L},\frac{2\epsilon_{H}^{3}}{9M^{2}}\right)=f(x_{k})-R_{\epsilon}.

Consider the stochastic process
M_{k}:=f(x_{k})+kR_{\epsilon}
. Since
x_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
, we also have
M_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k+1}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{k+1})+(k+1)R_{\epsilon}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{k})-R_{\epsilon}+(k+1)R_{\epsilon}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(x_{k})+kR_{\epsilon}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]=M_{k}.

Hence,
\{M_{k}\}
is a supermartingale with respect to filtration
\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
.

Let
T
denote the iteration at which our algorithm stops.
Since the decision to stop at iteration

depends on

and
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
, we have
\{T=k\}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
, which implies

We will use the supermartingale convergence theorem (Fact
2.8
) to show that

almost surely, since the function value cannot decrease indefinitely as it is bounded from below by
\bar{f}
. To apply Fact
2.8
, we must be able to allow

, so we need to transform
\{M_{k}\}
into a supermartingale
\{Y_{k}\}
that is well defined even after the algorithm terminates.

It follows from Fact
2.7
that
Y_{k}:=M_{\min(k,T)}
is also a supermartingale. Since
Y_{k}\geq\bar{f}
, it follows from the supermartingale convergence theorem (Fact
2.8
) applied to

that
Y_{k}\rightarrow Y_{\infty}
almost surely for some random variable

with
\mathbb{E}Y_{\infty}\leq\mathbb{E}Y_{0}=\mathbb{E}M_{0}=f(x_{0})<\infty
. Hence
\mathbb{P}[Y_{\infty}=+\infty]=0
. On the other hand, as

, so

We can then apply the optional stopping theorem (Fact

where the first equality uses

By reorganizing this bound, we obtain
\mathbb{E}T\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-\bar{f}}{R_{\epsilon}}

∎

Remark 2.10
.

The algorithm in
[

, Section 3.6]
also finds an
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order stationary points without allowing for gradient and Hessian inexactness.
Consequently, the algorithm there is deterministic; it does not need to introduce Rademacher randomness in the negative curvature step.

In summary, the expected iteration complexity of Algorithm

Thus Azuma’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{s}\tau_{i}>\frac{s\epsilon_{H}^{3}}{18M\epsilon_{g}}\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2\left(\frac{s\epsilon_{H}^{3}}{18M\epsilon_{g}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\frac{8}{3}\alpha\right)^{2}s}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{s\,\epsilon_{H}^{6}}{1152M^{2}\alpha^{2}\epsilon_{g}^{2}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{K\epsilon_{H}^{6}}{1152M^{2}\alpha^{2}\epsilon_{g}^{2}}\right)

Recall that
C:=2304\frac{M^{2}\alpha^{2}\epsilon_{g}^{2}}{\epsilon_{H}^{6}}
. To make the above probability less than
\frac{\delta}{n}
, it suffices that

K\geq\frac{C}{2}\log(n/\delta)=\frac{C}{2}(\log(A+BK)+\log(1/\delta)),

(16)

where
A=2\left(\frac{f(x_{0})-\bar{f}}{R_{\epsilon}}\right)
.

We achieve (
16
) by choosing
K
large enough that simultaneously
\frac{K}{2}\geq\frac{C}{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
and
\frac{K}{2}\geq\frac{C}{2}(\log(A+BK)
.

For the latter lower bound
K\geq C\log(A+BK)
, it suffices that simultaneously
K\geq C\log(2A)
and
K\geq C\log(2BK)
, where the latter is equivalent to

K/C\geq\log(K/C)+\log(2BC).

(17)

To derive an explicit sufficient condition for the latter, we use the following fact.

Fact 2.18
.

Given any
V
with
\log V\geq 1
, we have that
T-\log T\geq\log V
provided that
T\geq 2\log V
.

Proof of Fact:
Note that
T-\log T
is an increasing function of
T
for
T\geq 1
, so it suffices to prove that
T-\log T\geq\log V
for
T=2\log V
. We know that
S\geq\log(2S)
for all
S\geq 1
. By setting
T=2\log V
and
S=\log V
, we have

T-\log T=2\log V-\log(2\log V)\geq 2\log V-\log V=\log V,

as required.

Resuming the proof, we set
T=K/C
and
V=2BC
to obtain the following sufficient condition for (
17
), provided that
\log(2BC)\geq 1
:

K/C\geq 2\log(2BC)\iff K\geq 2C\log(2BC).

On the other hand, if
\log(2BC)<1
, then
K/C\geq 1\implies K/C\geq\log(K/C)+1>\log(K/C)+\log(2BC)
, satisfying (
17
).

The result follows from these requirements on
K
together with
K\geq C\log(2A)
and
K\geq C\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
.

∎

2.4
Computation of Inexact Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

We include a lemma demonstrating that Assumption
2.4

can be satisfied using matrix-vector products. This result bridges the gap between
iteration complexity and operation complexity, with the latter being defined as
the cumulative count of gradient evaluations and Hessian-vector products.
The algorithm on which the result is based is a variant of the Lanczos algorithm, tailored to
computing the minimum eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix
H
without
knowledge of an upper bound on
\left\|{H}\right\|
.

Lemma 2.19

(
[
ROW20
, Lemma 10]
)
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a real symmetric matrix. Let

0<\epsilon_{H}\leq 9\left\|{H}\right\|
and
\delta\in(0,1)
be given. With probability at least

1-\delta
over uniformly random initialization on the unit sphere, there exists an algorithm that on input
H
(but not
\left\|{H}\right\|
)
outputs a unit vector
v
such that

v^{\top}Hv\leq\lambda_{\min}(H)+\epsilon_{H}/9

(18)

in at most

\min\left\{d,1+2\ln\left(25d/\delta^{2}\right)\sqrt{{\|H\|}/{\epsilon_{H}}}\right\}

(19)

matrix-vector multiplications by
H
.

In other words, the total number of gradient evaluations and Hessian-vector products required by our algorithm is
O\big({n\epsilon_{H}^{-1/2}}\big)
, with
n
as specified in (
11
):

Corollary 2.20
.

Suppose the function
f
satisfies Assumption
2.1
and its inexact gradient and Hessian computations satisfy Assumption
2.3
. Then, Algorithm
1
terminates after
O(n\epsilon_{H}^{-1/2})
gradient evaluations or Hessian-vector products with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
n
is defined in (
11
). When
\epsilon_{g}=\epsilon
,
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon M}
, and the algorithm takes a short step

\tilde{O}\big({\epsilon^{-9/4}}\big)

3
Comparison with previous results

Section
1.1
reviewed prior works of finding approximate second-order stationary points with inexact derivatives under varied settings and assumptions.
Here, we revisit the paper
[
Yao+22
]
, which is especially relevant,
That paper addresses a setting similar to ours, with inexact gradients and Hessians and, in its latter part, no need for function evaluations.
It selects the sense of the negative curvature step
\hat{p}_{k}
so that
g_{k}^{T}\hat{p}_{k}\leq 0
and establishes conditions on gradient and Hessian inexactness and target error
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}
to ensure sufficient decrease.
The conditions on

[

]

We focus in this section on the

inexactness conditions, showing that our conditions are less stringent than those of

We denote the bound on

[

From Assumption

\Delta_{g,k}=(1/3)\max\{\epsilon_{g},\|g_{k}\|\}

By simplifying
[

\delta_{g,k}\leq\frac{1}{8}\min\left\{\frac{3\epsilon_{H}^{2}}{65M},\max\{\epsilon_{g},\|g_{k}\|\}\right\}.

(20)

It is immediately clear that
\delta_{g,k}<\Delta_{g,k}

In the most useful situations, we may actually have
\delta_{g,k}\ll\Delta_{g,k}
.
Consider first the setting
\epsilon_{H}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}M}
(which is assumed immediately following
[
Yao+22
, Condition 2.4]
for the remainder of that paper). By substitution into (
20

\delta_{g,k}\leq\frac{3}{520}\epsilon_{g}
, so we lose the advantage of
relative
inexactness allowed by
\Delta_{g,k}
, as well as having a smaller constant factor.
(Section
4
gives an example in which our relaxed gradient inexactness requirement improves the sample complexity for the finite sum problem (

).)
Next, when the strong coupling between
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}
is broken, and we have
\epsilon_{H}<\sqrt{\epsilon_{g}M}

\delta_{g,k}
becomes even smaller.
When

and

Next we discuss a low-rank matrix recovery problem in which
\epsilon_{H}^{3}
and
\epsilon_{g}^{2}
are on the same order.

Let
M^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a real symmetric and positive semidefinite matrix. Suppose the rank of
M^{*}
is
r
, where
r<d
, and let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
. Defining the nonconvex objective function
f(U)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{UU^{\top}-M^{*}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
, we seek a global minimum
\min_{U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}}f(U)
, which then solves the symmetric low-rank matrix factorization problem.
Let
\sigma^{\star}_{1}
and
{\sigma^{\star}_{r}}
denote the largest singular value and smallest nonzero singular value of
M^{*}
, respectively, and define
\kappa=\sigma^{\star}_{1}/{\sigma^{\star}_{r}}
to be the condition number.
(We abuse the notation in the following discussion and let
f(\vect(U))=f(U)
so that the gradient
\nabla f(U)\in\mathbb{R}^{dr}
remains a vector and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(U)\in\mathbb{R}^{dr\times dr}
is a matrix.)

The following strict saddle property gives a context in which approximate second-order
stationary points are in the neighborhood of local minima.

Definition 3.1
.

Function
f(\cdot)
is
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H},\zeta)
-
strict saddle
if, for any
U
, at least one of following holds:

•

\left\|{\nabla f(U)}\right\|\geq\epsilon_{g}
.

•

\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(U))\leq-\epsilon_{H}
.

•

U
is
\zeta
-close to
\mathcal{X}^{\star}
— the set of local minima.

This property provides a guarantee that a point is

\zeta
-close to the region of local optima if it is an

(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order stationary point.

The matrix recovery literature, for instance
[
Jin+17
]
, provides
the constants for the strict saddle property of
f(\cdot)
and its
gradient Lipschitz and Hessian Lipschitz constants.

Fact 3.2
.

All local minima of the function
f(\cdot)
are global minima. Moreover,
f(\cdot)
satisfies

(\frac{1}{24}({\sigma^{\star}_{r}})^{3/2},\frac{1}{3}{\sigma^{\star}_{r}},\frac{1}{3}({\sigma^{\star}_{r}})^{1/2})
-strict
saddle property. For any
\Gamma>\sigma^{\star}_{1}
, inside the region
\{U:\left\|{U}\right\|^{2}<\Gamma\}
,
f(\cdot)
has gradient Lipschitz constant

and Hessian Lipschitz constant

.

An approximate second-order stationary point is useful for this problem because the function

satisfies a regularity condition similar to strong convexity inside
\frac{1}{3}{\sigma^{\star}_{r}}^{1/2}
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{X}^{\star}
, so gradient descent initialized at an

-approximate second-order point, where

\epsilon_{g}=\frac{1}{24}{\sigma^{\star}_{r}}^{3/2},\quad\epsilon_{H}=\frac{1}{3}{\sigma^{\star}_{r}},

(21)

will achieve local linear convergence
[

, Section 3.2]
to a local minimum.
That is, once we find such an approximate second-order point, the additional cost of finding a solution to high accuracy is relatively low.
Note also that (

required in Corollary

Let us compare the gradient inexactness conditions between our work in

4
Sampling

We consider the finite sum problem (
2
) and investigate the number of samples that are necessary to construct inexact gradient and Hessian oracles that satisfy Assumption
2.3
.
We focus on sample complexity for the gradient inexactness condition of this paper which, as noted above, is less stringent than for previous methods.
(As noted at the start of Section
3
, the Hessian inexactness condition resembles those of previous works, so such results as
[
XRM20
, Lemma 16]
and
[
Tri+18
, Lemma 4]
capture the sample complexity for Hessian inexactness in this paper too.)

In this section, we give sample complexity results for estimating the gradient and Hessian to the required accuracy at each point.
We then give a result for total sample-operation complexity, which is a high-probability bound for the total number of stochastic gradient or Hessian-vector product evaluations on a single sample required by the algorithm to find the
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order point.
We conclude with a brief comparison with other algorithms.

We have the following sample complexity result for gradient subsampling.
The proof closely tracks that of
[
Yao+22
, Lemma 2.13]
, replacing
\epsilon_{g}
by
\max(\epsilon_{g},\|\nabla f(x)\|)
throughout.

Lemma 4.1

(Gradient Subsampling)
.

For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, suppose there is an upper bound
G(x)
such that
\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|\leq G(x)<\infty
for all
i\in[N]
. For any given
\xi\in(0,1)
, let
S_{g}
be a set of indices that is sampled uniformly with replacement from
\{1,2,\dotsc,N\}
, with

|S_{g}|\geq 256\left(\frac{G(x)}{\max\{\epsilon_{g},\left\|{\nabla f(x)}\right\|\}}\right)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\xi}.

(22)

Then for
g(x):=\frac{1}{|S_{g}|}\sum_{i\in S_{g}}\nabla f_{i}(x)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\nabla f(x)-g(x)\|\leq\frac{1}{3}\max\{\epsilon_{g},\left\|{g(x)}\right\|\}\right)\geq 1-\xi.

Proof.

If we can prove that

\|\nabla f(x)-g(x)\|\leq\frac{1}{4}\max\{\epsilon_{g},\left\|{\nabla f(x)}\right\|\}=:\Delta(\epsilon_{g},x),

(23)

then it follows from the triangle inequality that
\|\nabla f(x)-g(x)\|\leq\frac{1}{3}\max\{\epsilon_{g},\left\|{g(x)}\right\|\}
.
We can therefore prove the result by showing that (
23
) holds with probability at least
1-\xi
. The required sample size is shows by
[
Yao+22
, Lemma 2.12]
to be

\left|{S_{g}}\right|\geq 16\frac{G(x)^{2}}{\Delta(\epsilon_{g},x)^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\xi},

which yields the result when we substitute the definition of
\Delta(\epsilon_{g},x)
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In the context of Remark
2.14
, we may need to choose
\xi
such that
\xi=\delta/n=O(\delta^{2}\epsilon_{g}^{2})
to achieve a failure probability of at most

This choice causes the modest factor of
(\log\delta+\log\epsilon_{g})
to appear in the lower bound on

. We formally establish this observation in Theorem

with a tighter bound.

Remark 4.3

(Alternative assumptions)
.

If we assume in addition to the assumption of Lemma

(namely,
\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|\leq G(x)<\infty
for all
i\in[N]
) that there is an upper bound
V_{g}(x)
on the variance of the sample gradients, that is,

\mathbb{E}_{i}\left\|{\nabla f_{i}(x)-\nabla f(x)}\right\|^{2}\leq V_{g}(x),

we can use the matrix Berstein inequality to refine the lower bound on

to

\tilde{O}\left(\max\left\{\frac{V_{g}(x)}{\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{2},\left\|{\nabla f(x)}\right\|^{2}\}},\frac{G(x)}{\max\{\epsilon_{g},\left\|{\nabla f(x)}\right\|\}}\right\}\right),

by following the approach of
[
Tri+18

).
If we only assume the second-moment upper bound

, without a bound

on sample gradient norms, then we need to employ

O\left(\frac{V_{g}(x)}{\xi^{2}\max\{\epsilon_{g},\left\|{\nabla f(x)}\right\|\}^{2}}\right)

whose polynomial dependence on

makes it much less appealing.

Remark 4.4
.

With the same analysis method,
[

|S_{g}|\geq 480000\left(\frac{G(x)}{\epsilon_{g}}\right)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\xi}.

Lemma

improves on this bound via a better constant factor and relative gradient inexactness.

Remark 4.5

(Adaptive sample size)
.

The lower bound on
|S_{g}|
cannot quite be evaluated in practice because we do not know
\|\nabla f(x)\|
(only the estimate
\|g(x)\|
). A similar issue was tackled by
[

[

]
to develop an adaptive

We can also utilize subsampling to estimate Hessians.
As mentioned in the beginning of this section, our requirements on Hessian accuracy are similar to those of previous works, and so is the following sample complexity result, which is stated for completeness.

Lemma 4.6

(Hessian Subsampling
[
XRM20

.

For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Theorem 4.7

(Total Sample-Operation Complexity)
.

Consider the finite-sum problem (
2

1
with the settings of Corollary
2.12
.
Choose
\delta\in(0,.5]

Let the inexact gradient and Hessian oracles be obtained by independent with-replacement batch subsampling, that is,

and

H(x):=\frac{1}{|S_{H}|}\sum_{i\in S_{H}}\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)
, where

\left|{S_{g}}\right|
and
\left|{S_{H}}\right|
are given by
(
22
) and (
24

\xi=\delta/n
and
n
as defined in Theorem

2.12

1-2\delta

1
will output an

(\frac{4}{3}\epsilon_{g},\frac{4}{3}\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order stationary points
within

\tilde{O}\left({\max\{\epsilon_{g}^{-4},\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\epsilon_{H}^{-3},\epsilon_{H}^{-11/2},\epsilon_{g}^{2}\epsilon_{H}^{-13/2}\}}\right)

f_{i}

Proof.

By Lemmas
4.1
and
4.6

Recall that Theorem

Remark 4.8
.

We match the
\tilde{O}\left({\epsilon_{g}^{-4}}\right)
total sample-operation complexity of perturbed (stochastic) gradient descent methods
[

,

]
when

As mentioned in Section

, our algorithm considers general inexact settings and does not take advantage of the stochastic or finite-sum structure of the problem, which (partly) explains why our total sample-operation complexity is worse than the optimal rate

However, we note that our gradient sample complexity is an overestimate because of our relative tolerances on gradient accuracy, and we leave it to future work on how the total sample-operation complexity can be reduced by adaptively adjusting the sample size as discussed in Remark

Among algorithms that tackle general inexact settings, we also obtain worse iteration complexity than the

[

However, apart from the advantage that we tolerate more inexactness, we also highlight that our method is much simpler than capped conjugate gradient method and stochastic cubic regularization.

5
Conclusion

We have presented a simple randomized algorithm that finds an
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order stationary point of a smooth nonconvex function with inexact oracle access to gradient and Hessian.
Compared to previous studies, our method’s main advantage is its enhanced tolerance for inexactness. Specifically, the gradient inexactness measure can be relative, and no coupling between the parameters
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{H}
that define accuracy in first- and second-order conditions is required. Both features make our inexactness criteria easier to satisfy, as evidenced in the low-rank matrix factorization example of Section
3
and the subsampling complexity result for finite-sum problems in Section
4
. Moreover, our approach eliminates the need for approximate evaluations of the function value, which typically come with stringent requirements in terms of precision and therefore high sample complexity in finite-sum settings.
