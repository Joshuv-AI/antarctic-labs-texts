Supervised heterogeneous network estimation via survival-based Bayesian graphical models
Xing Qin, Xu Liu, Shuangge Ma, Mengyun Wu

Abstract
Heterogeneity is a fundamental characteristic of cancer. To accommodate heterogeneity, subgroup identification has been extensively studied and broadly categorized into unsupervised and supervised analysis. Compared to unsupervised analysis, supervised approaches potentially hold greater clinical implications. Under the unsupervised analysis framework, several methods focusing on network-based subgroup identification have been developed, offering more comprehensive insights than those restricted to mean, variance, and other simplistic distributions by incorporating the interconnections among variables. However, research on supervised network-based subgroup identification remains limited. In this study, we develop a novel supervised Bayesian graphical model for jointly identifying multiple heterogeneous networks and subgroups. In the proposed model, heterogeneity is not only reflected in molecular data but also associated with a clinical outcome, and a novel similarity prior is introduced to effectively accommodate similarities among the networks of different subgroups, significantly facilitating clinically meaningful biological network construction and subgroup identification. The consistency properties of the estimates are rigorously established, and an efficient algorithm is developed. Extensive simulation studies and a real-world application to TCGA data are conducted, which demonstrate the advantages of the proposed approach in terms of both subgroup and network identification.



Keywords:

Bayesian analysis; Gaussian graphical models; Finite mixture of regression models; Heterogeneous network estimation.

For cancer, heterogeneity is a fundamental characteristic and is essential for unraveling the inherent complexities of biological systems. To describe and accommodate heterogeneity, researchers have conducted extensive studies on subgroup identification with high-dimensional molecular data, which can be roughly classified into unsupervised and supervised. Unsupervised approaches typically emphasize differences in the means of molecular factors (Figure

Despite significant achievements, existing techniques remain inadequate as they focus on the distributional properties or the influences of individual variables but neglect the interconnections among variables. There is a strong need for more informative approaches for subgroup identification.

A comparative overview of unsupervised and supervised approaches for subgroup identification.

The importance of network-based subgroup identification has been emphasized in recent literature
[
20
]

[
2

[

25

Among the diverse statistical methods for network analysis, Gaussian graphical models (GGMs) have been prominent. Here, the nodes of a network are represented by random variables with a joint Gaussian distribution, while the edges reflect conditional dependencies between nodes that are determined by non-zero entries of the inverse covariance matrix (or precision matrix)
[
16
,
9
]
. To detect unknown heterogeneity, several network-based methods have been proposed under the framework of the Gaussian mixture model (GMM), with particular attention to identifying both structural differences and potential commonalities among multiple networks. For example,
[
10
]
incorporated a joint fused graphical lasso penalty into GMM for learning the related structures across different unknown subgroups. Similarly, a SCAN method proposed by
[
11
]
used a joint group graphical lasso penalty for simultaneous subgroup identification and joint graphical model estimation.
[
18
]
decomposed the GGM into a set of sparse regression problems and introduced an approach for jointly estimating multiple networks in the presence of unknown sample heterogeneity. Under the Bayesian framework,
[
5
]
proposed a BCGS method that imposes a joint spike-and-slab graphical lasso prior on the precision matrices when the subgroup information is unknown.

However, these GMM-based approaches belong to the unsupervised paradigm, focusing solely on the discovery of molecularly separable subgroups, do not consider any clinical outcomes, and may lead to subgroups lacking sufficient clinical implications. Published literature has demonstrated that supervised heterogeneity analysis that incorporates clinical information is promising in identifying clinically relevant subgroups and provides a more comprehensive understanding of disease
[
22
,
28
]
.
However, the existing supervised heterogeneity methods predominantly focus on detecting heterogeneous predictor-outcome associations without appropriately accommodating the network structures among predictors. Overall, there is a notable gap in jointly exploring molecular network structures and conducting subgroup identification in a supervised manner.

In this paper, we develop a novel supervised network-based heterogeneity analysis approach to simultaneously conduct network estimation and subgroup identification. This approach is among the first to conduct joint estimation for multiple networks in a supervised manner with unknown subgroup structures. Significantly advancing from the existing unsupervised network-based subgroup identification approaches
[
11
,
14
,
5
]
, we develop a novel Bayesian framework integrating a finite mixture of Gaussian graphical models with supervised regression models to accommodate heterogeneity involving not only the distributions of predictors but also the predictor-outcome associations, leading to clinically more meaningful biological network construction and subgroup identification. Additionally, advancing from the existing joint network analysis based on the group penalties
[
6
,
11
,
14
,
5
]
, a novel similarity prior is introduced to capture the similarity information between the values of connection effects of distinct networks, facilitating more effective information borrowing and more appropriate modeling of the underlying biological mechanisms.
Furthermore, unlike the existing supervised subgroup identification approaches
[
1
,
28
]
, we fully take the specific genetic networks into account while simultaneously accommodating the clinical information. The consistency properties of the proposed estimators are rigorously established, achieved through an efficient algorithm. Comprehensive simulation studies are conducted, demonstrating the advantages of the proposed approach in terms of subgroup and network identification. Additionally, a real-world application to TCGA data highlights the practical utilization of our approach.

2.1
Bayesian mixture modeling

Denote
g_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
as the subgroup membership of the
i
th subject. Consider the accelerated failure time (AFT) model:

z_{i}=\ddot{\beta}_{0k}+\ddot{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\text{~when~}g_{i}=k,

where
\ddot{\beta}_{0k}
and
\ddot{\bm{\beta}}_{k}=\left(\ddot{\beta}_{k,1},\ldots,\ddot{\beta}_{k,p}\right)^{\top}
are the subgroup-specific intercept and regression parameters, and
\epsilon_{i}
is a normally distributed random variable with mean
0
and precision parameter
{\tau}_{k}^{2}
.
We propose the following Bayesian mixture model with a reparameterization technique:

\displaystyle t_{i}\mid\left(\delta_{i},z_{i},c_{i}\right)=z_{i}^{\delta_{i}}c_{i}^{1-\delta_{i}},\delta_{i}\mid\left(z_{i},c_{i}\right)={I}\left\{z_{i}\leq c_{i}\right\},c_{i}\sim f_{C}(c_{i}),

(1)

\displaystyle z_{i}\mid\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{x}_{i},g_{i}=k;\bm{\tau}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(z_{i}\mid\beta_{0k}/\tau_{k}+\bm{\beta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}/\tau_{k},\tau_{k}^{-2}\right),\bm{x}_{i}\mid\left(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega},g_{i}=k\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{x}_{i}\mid\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Omega}_{k}^{-1}\right).

Here,
f_{C}
is the density function of the logarithm of censoring time
C
,
\bm{\mu}_{k}=\left(\mu_{k,1},\cdots,\mu_{k,p}\right)^{\top}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{k}=\left(\omega_{k,jl}\right)_{p\times p}
are the mean and precision matrix for the predictors in the
k
th subgroup, and
\bm{\beta}
,
\bm{\tau}
,
\bm{\mu}
, and
\bm{\Omega}
are the vectors of
\left({\beta}_{0k},\bm{\beta}_{k}\right)
’s,
{\tau}_{k}
’s,
\bm{\mu}_{k}
’s, and
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
’s, respectively, with
{\bm{\beta}}_{k}=\ddot{\bm{\beta}}_{k}\tau_{k}
and
\beta_{0k}=\ddot{\beta}_{0k}\tau_{k}
. For the
k
th subgroup, we estimate the network based on
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
, where
\omega_{k,jl}\neq 0
corresponds to a
connection between predictors
l
and
j
.

After integrating out
\bm{z}
and
\bm{c}
, we have the joint distribution of
\left(\bm{t},\bm{\delta},\textbf{X}\right)
as:

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{t},\bm{\delta},\textbf{X}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega},\bm{\tau},\bm{g}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left[f_{k}\left(t_{i},\bm{x}_{i},\delta_{i}\mid{\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\Omega}}_{k},{\bm{\beta}}_{k},{\beta}_{0k},{{\tau}}_{k}\right)\right]^{I\left\{g_{i}=k\right\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left\{f_{\bm{X}}\left(\bm{x}_{i}\mid\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Omega}_{k}\right)\left[f_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{k},\beta_{0k},{\tau}_{k}\right)S_{C}(t_{i})\right]^{\delta_{i}}\left[f_{C}(t_{i})S_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{k},\beta_{0k},{\tau}_{k}\right)\right]^{1-\delta_{i}}\right\}^{{I}\left\{g_{i}=k\right\}},

where
f_{\bm{X}}
and
f_{Z}
are the density functions of
\bm{X}
and
Z
given in (
1
), respectively, and
S_{C}
and
S_{Z}
are the survival functions of
C
and
Z
, respectively.

In (
1
), to accommodate censoring, we adopt prior
z_{i}^{\delta_{i}}c_{i}^{1-\delta_{i}}
for
t_{i}
. Here, we assume random censoring with
c_{i}
being independent of
z_{i}
and
\bm{x}_{i}
, as in the literature
[
22
,
8
]
. We propose combining the AFT model with the mixture Gaussian graphical model to accommodate the multi-layer heterogeneity. Specifically, in the AFT model, we introduce a subgroup-specific coefficient
\bm{\beta}_{k}
to explicitly capture the heterogeneous associations of the molecular predictors with survival time. Here, a reparameterization technique based on the noise level is adopted for
\bm{\beta}_{k}
to ensure that the proposed estimator remains scale-invariant under the mixture model
[
23
,
28
]
. This technique facilitates an “adaptive adjustment” of the coefficients, aligning them with data characteristics and leading to more accurate estimations. On the other hand, within the Gaussian graphical model, we utilize
\bm{\mu}_{k}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
to characterize heterogeneity attributed to both differential mean expressions and differential network structures. Consequently, the network’s heterogeneity is not only intricately linked to the interested measurements but also closely connected to the distribution of survival time, making the resulting networks clinically more meaningful. For example, researchers can better understand the differences between patients with good survival and those with bad survival on specific biological networks, thereby more effectively guiding subsequent treatments.

2.2
Prior specification

We propose the following priors for the parameters in (
1
):

\displaystyle\omega_{k,jl}\mid\gamma_{k,jl}\sim\gamma_{k,jl}\operatorname{LP}\left(\omega_{k,jl};v_{1}\right)+\left(1-\gamma_{k,jl}\right)\operatorname{LP}\left(\omega_{k,jl};v_{0}\right),\gamma_{k,jl}\sim\operatorname{Bern}\left(p_{1}\right),\text{ for }j\neq l,

\displaystyle\beta_{k,j}\sim\operatorname{LP}\left(\beta_{k,j};1/\lambda_{1}\right),\mu_{k,j}\sim\operatorname{LP}\left(\mu_{k,j};1/\lambda_{2}\right),g_{i}\mid\bm{\pi_{g}}\sim\operatorname{Multi}\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{K}\right).

Here,
\gamma_{k,jl}
is a binary indicator corresponding to
\omega_{k,jl}
,
\operatorname{LP}(y;v)=\exp(-|y|/v)/(2v)
is the density function of the Laplace distribution with a scale parameter
v
,
p_{1}\in(0,1)
is the prior probability for the Bernoulli distribution, and
\bm{\pi}_{g}=\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{K}\right)^{\top}
is the vector composed of the Multinomial distribution’s parameters, which specifies the prior probabilities that each sample is drawn from different subgroups.
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
, and
v_{1}>v_{0}
are the positive tuning parameters.

In the proposed Bayesian framework, we first introduce a spike-and-slab Laplace prior
[
21
,
26
]
for
\omega_{k,jl}
based on the indicator
\gamma_{k,jl}
, for
identifying individual sparse patterns of the
K
networks. With this prior, when
\gamma_{k,jl}=1
, the corresponding entry
\omega_{k,jl}
comes from the slab component with a relative large variance
2v_{1}^{2}
and is likely to take a value away from zero; when
\gamma_{k,jl}=0
,
\omega_{k,jl}
comes from the spike component with a small variance
2v_{0}^{2}
that can induce values towards zero. Compared to the Laplace prior, the spike-and-slab Laplace prior involves two different scale parameters
v_{1}
and
v_{0}
to distinguish active from ignorable coefficients and can nonlinearly adapt to coefficients with
\gamma_{k,jl}
, achieving “selective shrinkage” with a smaller bias. This Laplace distribution-based prior can automatically achieve sparsity via the
L_{1}
norm on
\omega_{k,jl}
. This advances from the commonly adopted spike-and-slab Gaussian prior which yields nonsparse posterior modes for
\omega_{k,jl}
’s and relies on a post-data thresholding-based selection strategy utilizing
\gamma_{k,jl}
. The strategy that is based on the estimation and sparsity of the continuous
\omega_{k,jl}
can lead to more effective identification of sparse posterior patterns
[
21
]
and can also facilitate a simpler theoretical study and a more efficient computation.

Additionally, to accommodate the sparse structure of the truly associated predictors and predictor means, sparse priors are assumed for
{\beta}_{k,j}
’s and
\mu_{k,j}
’s for regularized estimation. As the estimation of
{\beta}_{k,j}
’s and
\mu_{k,j}
’s is less challenging than
\omega_{k,jl}
, we choose the relatively simple Laplace prior for both the regression coefficients and predictor means, which involves fewer parameters and less computational complexity.

2.3
Additional priors for accommodating common structures

To borrow information from cross-subgroup similarity, we further introduce a latent vector
\bm{\theta}_{jl}
for
\bm{\omega}_{jl}
and assume a Gaussian distribution with the precision matrix based on a similarity-based matrix
\bm{L}^{(jl)}
,

\bm{\theta}_{jl}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},u\left(\bm{L}^{(jl)}\right)^{-1}\right),\bm{\theta}_{jl}\mid\bm{\omega}_{jl}\sim{I}\left\{\bm{\theta}_{jl}=\bm{\omega}_{jl}\right\},

with
u
being the positive tuning parameter.
Here, we define
L^{(jl)}_{k,k}=(K-1)/\left(\theta_{k,jl}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\right)
and
L^{(jl)}_{k,k^{\prime}}=-1/\sqrt{\left(\theta_{k,jl}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\right)\left(\theta_{k^{\prime},jl}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\right)}
for
1\leq k\neq k^{\prime}\leq K
and
1\leq j<l\leq p
, with
\epsilon
being a small constant. Thus, for each
\bm{\theta}_{jl}
, the prior involves
\bm{\theta}_{jl}^{\top}\bm{L}^{(jl)}\bm{\theta}_{jl}=0.5\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\left(\theta_{k,jl}/\sqrt{\theta_{k,jl}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}-\theta_{k^{\prime},jl}/\sqrt{\theta_{k^{\prime},jl}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}
, which is an approximation to the Bayesian counterpart of the sign-based constraint penalty
0.5\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\left[\operatorname{sgn}(\theta_{k,jl})-\operatorname{sgn}(\theta_{k^{\prime},jl})\right]^{2}
. Combined with the indicator function
{I}\left\{\bm{\theta}_{jl}=\bm{\omega}_{jl}\right\}
, the proposed approach encourages the pairwise relationships of predictors across subgroups to have similar signs, leading to networks with a shared pattern of sparsity as well as edges with shared positive or negative relationships. This significantly advances from the previous research based on the group penalty
[
6
,
11
,
14
,
5
]
, which utilizes group selection without appropriately accounting for the similarity of the estimated values and fails to sufficiently address the relationships between different networks
[
15
]
. This is also more flexible than the strategy based on fused lasso
[
6
,
25
,
10
,
27
]
, which promotes both shared directions and magnitudes of edges. Similar prior assumptions can also be applied to
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\mu}
when considering the common structures within regression coefficients and predictors’ means.

2.4
Posterior Inference

The complete posterior distribution of the parameters conditionally on the available data is:

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega},\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma},\bm{g}\mid\bm{t},\bm{\delta},\textbf{X};\bm{\tau},\bm{\pi_{g}}\right)\propto

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{t},\bm{\delta},\textbf{X}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega},\bm{\tau},\bm{g}\right)f\left(\bm{\beta}\right)f\left(\bm{\mu}\right)f\left(\bm{\Omega}\mid\bm{\gamma}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle\times f\left(\bm{\gamma}\right)f\left(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\Omega}\right)f\left(\bm{0}\mid\bm{\theta}\right)f\left(\bm{g}\mid\bm{\pi_{g}}\right).

Here, for a better illustration of the proposed framework, we rewrite the prior for
\bm{\theta}_{jl}
as the generative model with observation vector
\bm{0}
. We note that since the distribution
f\left(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\Omega}\right)
is the indicator function, there is no need to estimate
\bm{\theta}
separately. Figure
2
gives a graphical representation of the Bayesian model.

Figure 2:
A graphical representation of the proposed approach.

Based on (
2
), we develop an effective Expectation Maximization (EM) algorithm for posterior inference of the parameter set
\bm{\Psi}=\operatorname{vec}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Omega}}\right)
. First, we introduce an indicator
\rho_{k,i}=I\left\{g_{i}=k\right\}
and take
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\rho}=\left(\rho_{1,1},\cdots,\rho_{K,n}\right)^{\top}
as latent variables. Then, for initialization, we set
\bm{\beta}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{k}=\bm{I}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, where
\bm{I}
is an identity matrix, randomly partition the subjects into
K
subgroups, and initialize
{\tau}_{k}
and
\bm{\mu}_{k}
as the precision parameter of residuals and the vector of predictor centers in the
k
th subgroup, respectively. To obtain
\bm{L}^{(jl)}
for each
j<l
, we adopt the initial values of the precision matrices, choose
\epsilon=0.001
, and update
\bm{L}^{(jl)}
accordingly based on
\omega_{k,jl}
’s in the following iterations.

In the E step, given the estimations
\left({\bm{\Psi}}^{(m-1)},{\bm{\pi}}_{\bm{g}}^{(m-1)}\right)
in the
(m-1)
th step, compute the conditional expectations of
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\rho}
as
\gamma_{k,jl}^{(m)}=\left(\frac{p_{1}}{2v_{1}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left|\omega_{k,jl}^{(m-1)}\right|}{v_{1}}\right\}+\frac{1-p_{1}}{2v_{0}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left|\omega_{k,jl}^{(m-1)}\right|}{v_{0}}\right\}\right)^{-1}

\frac{p_{1}}{2v_{1}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left|\omega_{k,jl}^{(m-1)}\right|}{v_{1}}\right\}
and
{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}=\frac{{\pi}_{k}^{(m-1)}f_{k}\left(t_{i},\bm{x}_{i},\delta_{i}\mid\bm{\Psi}_{k}^{(m-1)}\right)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}{\pi}_{k^{\prime}}^{(m-1)}f_{k}\left(t_{i},\bm{x}_{i},\delta_{i}\mid\bm{\Psi}_{k^{\prime}}^{(m-1)}\right)}
, where
\bm{\Psi}_{k}=\operatorname{vec}\left({\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\Omega}}_{k},{\bm{\beta}}_{k},{\beta}_{0k},{{\tau}}_{k}\right)
.

Then the conditional expectation of the log-posterior can be calculated as:

\displaystyle L_{n}\left({\bm{\Psi}},\bm{\pi}_{\bm{g}}\mid{\bm{\Psi}}^{(m-1)},\bm{\pi}_{\bm{g}}^{(m-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\gamma},\bm{\rho}\mid{\bm{\Psi}}^{(m-1)},\bm{\pi}_{\bm{g}}^{(m-1)}}\log f\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega},\bm{\gamma},\bm{g}\mid\bm{t},\bm{\delta},\textbf{X},\bm{c};\bm{\tau},\bm{\pi_{g}}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle L_{1,n}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\tau}\right)+L_{2,n}\left(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega}\right)+L_{3,n}\left(\bm{\pi}_{g}\right)+\text{Const},

with
L_{1,n}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\tau}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}}\left[(1-\delta_{i})\log S_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{k};\beta_{0k},{\tau}_{k}\right)+\delta_{i}\log f_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{k};\beta_{0k},{\tau}_{k}\right)\right]-\lambda_{1}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_{k,j}|
,

L_{2,n}\left(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Omega}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\frac{{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}}{2}\left[\log\left|\bm{\Omega}_{k}\right|-\left(\bm{x}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}_{k}\right)^{\top}\bm{\Omega}_{k}\left(\bm{x}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}_{k}\right)\right]-\lambda_{2}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}|\mu_{k,j}|-\sum_{j<l}\left\{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\left[\frac{\gamma^{(m)}_{k,jl}}{v_{1}}+\frac{1-\gamma^{(m)}_{k,jl}}{v_{0}}\right]\left|\omega_{k,jl}\right|+\frac{u\bm{\omega}_{jl}^{\top}\bm{L}^{(jl)}\bm{\omega}_{jl}}{2}\right\}
, and
L_{3,n}\left(\bm{\pi}_{\bm{g}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\log\pi_{k}
.

In the M step, we update the parameters
\left(\bm{\Psi},\bm{\pi}_{\bm{g}}\right)
by maximizing
L_{1,n},L_{2,n}
, and
L_{3,n}
, separately.

Denote
u_{k,i}^{(m-1)}={f_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{(m-1)};{\beta}_{0k}^{(m-1)},{\tau}_{k}^{(m-1)}\right)}/{S_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{(m-1)};{\beta}_{0k}^{(m-1)},{\tau}_{k}^{(m-1)}\right)}
and
\tilde{z}_{i,k}^{(m-1)}=\delta_{i}t_{i}+(1-\delta_{i})\left[\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{(m-1)}/{\tau}_{k}^{(m-1)}+{\beta}_{0k}^{(m-1)}/{\tau}_{k}^{(m-1)}+{u_{k,i}^{(m-1)}}/{\left({\tau}_{k}^{(m-1)}\right)^{2}}\right]
. The optimization for
\bm{\tau}
and
\bm{\beta}
proceeds as follows (and we refer to Supplementary Materials for more details).

(1)

Compute

\tau_{k}^{(m)}=\frac{\widetilde{b}_{k}^{(m)}+\sqrt{\left(\widetilde{b}_{k}^{(m)}\right)^{2}+4\widetilde{a}_{k}^{(m)}\widetilde{c}_{k}^{(m)}}}{2\widetilde{a}_{k}^{(m)}}
,
where
\widetilde{b}_{k}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\tilde{z}_{i,k}^{(m-1)}\left[(1-\delta_{i})(u_{k,i}^{(m-1)}-\right.

\left.\tau_{k}^{(m-1)}t_{i}+r_{ik}^{(m-1)})+r_{ik}^{(m-1)}\right]
,
\widetilde{c}_{k}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\left[\delta_{i}+(1-\delta_{i})\left(u_{k,i}^{(m-1)}-\tau_{k}^{(m-1)}t_{i}+r_{ik}^{(m-1)}\right)\right]
with

r_{ik}^{(m-1)}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{k}^{(m-1)}+\beta_{0k}^{(m-1)}
, and
\widetilde{a}_{k}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\left(\tilde{z}_{i,k}^{(m-1)}\right)^{2}
.

(2)

Conduct a coordinate descent (CD) algorithm to optimize
L_{1,n}
with respect to
\bm{\beta}_{k}
. Specifically,
for
j=1,\cdots,p
, carry out the following steps sequentially. Compute
h_{kj}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}x_{i,j}^{2}
and
res_{-kj}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\left(\tau_{k}^{(m)}\tilde{z}_{i,k}^{(m-1)}-r_{ik}^{(m)}\right)x_{i,j}+h_{kj}^{(m)}\beta_{k,j}^{(m-1)}
, where
r_{ik}^{(m)}=r_{ik}^{(m-1)}+x_{i,j}\beta_{k,j}^{(m)}-x_{i,j}\beta_{k,j}^{(m-1)}
. Then update
\beta_{k,j}^{(m)}=\frac{res_{-kj}^{(m)}-\lambda_{1}\operatorname{sgn}\left(res_{-kj}^{(m)}\right)}{h_{kj}^{(m)}}
if
\left|res_{-kj}^{(m)}\right|>\lambda_{1}
and
\beta_{k,j}^{(m)}=0
otherwise.

(3)

Compute
{\beta}^{(m)}_{0k}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\left({\tau}_{k}^{(m)}z_{i,k}^{(m-1)}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{(m)}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}}.

Then, we maximize
L_{2,n}
with respect to
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\Omega}
as follows.

(1)

For each
k=1,\ldots,K
, compute
{\bm{\mu}}_{k}^{(m)}=\begin{cases}\frac{grad_{k,j}^{(m)}-\lambda_{2}\operatorname{sgn}\left(grad_{k,j}^{(m)}\right)}{\omega_{k,jj}^{(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}},&\text{if }~\left|grad_{k,j}^{(m)}\right|>\lambda_{2},\\
0,&\text{otherwise, }\end{cases}
where
grad_{k,j}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\left[\sum_{l\neq j}\left(x_{i,l}-\mu_{k,l}^{(m-1)}\right)\omega_{k,lj}^{(m-1)}+x_{i,j}\omega_{k,jj}^{(m-1)}\right].

(2)

For
\bm{\Omega}
, we have
\left({\bm{\Omega}}_{1}^{(m)},\ldots,{\bm{\Omega}}_{K}^{(m)}\right)=\arg\max_{\bm{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\Omega}_{K}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}{n_{k}}\left[\log\left|\bm{\Omega}_{k}\right|-\operatorname{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{S}}_{k}\bm{\Omega}_{k}\right)\right]\right.

\left.-\sum_{j<l}\left[2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\gamma^{(m)}_{k,jl}}{v_{1}}+\frac{1-\gamma^{(m)}_{k,jl}}{v_{0}}\right)\left|\omega_{k,jl}\right|+u{\bm{\omega}_{jl}^{\top}\bm{L}^{(jl)}\bm{\omega}_{jl}}\right]\right\},

where
n_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}_{k}={\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(m)}\right)\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{k}^{(m)}\right)^{\top}}/n_{k}
is a pseudo sample covariance matrix.
This optimization problem can be efficiently solved using the ADMM algorithm. We refer to Supplementary Materials for details.

Final, maximizing
L_{3,n}
with respect to
\bm{\pi}_{g}
subject to
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1
yields

\pi_{k}^{(m)}=\frac{\sum_{i}{\rho}_{k,i}^{{(m)}}}{n}.

Through the iterative E and M steps, convergence is typically attained within a reasonable number of iterations. To maintain consistency in model selection, with the estimate
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}
obtained by the EM algorithm, we further adopt a threshold strategy and have the final estimate
\widetilde{\omega}_{k,jl}=\hat{\omega}_{k,jl}{I}\left\{\left|\hat{\omega}_{k,jl}\right|>0.001\right\}
, following the literature
[
13
,
11
]
. We refer to Theorem
1
for more details. To improve stability, we explore multiple random initializations of subjects’ subgroup memberships, and the final estimates are selected based on the smallest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), as defined below.

For the proposed algorithm, to reduce computational cost, we fix
\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)=(0.5\sqrt{n\log p},

0.5\sqrt{n\log p})
,
p_{1}=0.85
, and
v_{1}=1
. For the choice of
v_{0}
and
u
, we adopt the AIC criterion defined as

-2\log f\left(\bm{t},\bm{\delta},\textbf{X}\mid\hat{\bm{\beta}},\hat{\bm{\mu}},\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}};\hat{\bm{\tau}},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{g}\right)+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{s}_{k},

where
\hat{\bm{\beta}},\hat{\bm{\mu}},\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}},\hat{\bm{\tau}}
, and
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{g}
are the final updates from the proposed algorithm and
\hat{s}_{k}=\#\left\{(j,l):\widetilde{\omega}_{k,jl}\neq 0,1\leq l<j\leq p\right\}
.

Theoretical properties

Denote
\bm{\Psi}^{*}=\operatorname{vec}\left(\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\tau}^{*},\bm{\mu}^{*},\bm{\Omega}^{*}\right)

\mathcal{S}

\left|\mathcal{S}\right|
as its cardinality. Define the sparsity parameters as
d_{\bm{\mu}}=\left|\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{U}_{k}^{*}\right|,d_{\bm{\beta}}=\left|\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{V}_{k}^{*}\right|
, and
s=\left|\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{W}_{k}^{*}\right|
. Define
\Xi
as some non-empty convex set of parameters
\bm{\Psi}
in
\mathbb{R}^{Kp^{2}+2Kp+2K}
. Denote
{\rho}_{k,i}^{\bm{\Psi}}
as the probability that the
i
th subject belongs to the
k
th subgroup given
\bm{{\Psi}}
.

Furthermore, for a
q
-dimensional vector
\bm{\nu}
, denote
\left\|\bm{\nu}\right\|_{\max}=\max_{1\leq j\leq q}\left|\nu_{j}\right|
, and for a matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times q_{2}}
, define its min, max, and max induced norms as
\|\bm{M}\|_{\min}=\min_{i,j}\left|M_{ij}\right|
,
\|\bm{M}\|_{\max}=\max_{i,j}\left|M_{ij}\right|
, and
\|\bm{M}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,q_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{q_{2}}\left|M_{ij}\right|
, respectively.
For a square matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, denote
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{A})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A})
as its smallest and largest eigenvalues. The following conditions are assumed.

Assumption 1

\underline{C}_{1}\leq\min_{k}\lambda_{\min}\left(\bm{\Omega}_{k}^{*}\right)\leq\max_{k}\lambda_{\max}\left(\bm{\Omega}_{k}^{*}\right)\leq\bar{C}_{1}
, where
\underline{C}_{1}
and
\bar{C}_{1}
are positive constants. Moreover, let
\ddot{\ell}_{1}\left(\bm{\Psi},\bar{\bm{\Psi}}\right)
and
\ddot{\ell}_{2}\left(\bm{\Psi},\bar{\bm{\Psi}}\right)
be the second derivatives of
\sum_{i}\sum_{k}{\rho}_{k,i}^{\bm{\Psi}}\delta_{i}\log f_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{k};\bar{\beta}_{0k},\bar{\tau}_{k}\right)

and
\sum_{i}\sum_{k}{\rho}_{k,i}^{\bm{\Psi}}(1-\delta_{i})\log S_{Z}\left(t_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i},\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{k};\bar{\beta}_{0k},\bar{\tau}_{k}\right)
with respect to
\operatorname{vec}\left({\bm{\beta}},\bm{\tau}\right)
, respectively. Then for any
\underline{\bm{\Psi}}\in\Xi
,
\bm{\Psi}\in\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}\left(\bm{\Psi}^{*}\right)=\left\{\bm{\Psi}\in\Xi:\left\|\bm{\Psi}-\bm{\Psi}^{*}\right\|_{2}\right.

\left.\leq\alpha\right\}
and
\bar{\bm{\Psi}}\in\left\{\bar{\bm{\Psi}}\mid\left\|\bar{\bm{\Psi}}-\bm{\Psi}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq 2\alpha\right\}
, there exists
\widetilde{\eta}>0
satisfying
\lambda_{\max}\left(\ddot{\ell}_{1}\left(\bm{\Psi},\bar{\bm{\Psi}}\right)\right)\leq-\widetilde{\eta}
and
\lambda_{\max}\left(\ddot{\ell}_{2}\left(\bm{\Psi},\underline{\bm{\Psi}}\right)\right)\leq 0.

Assumption 2

For a small constant
\eta>0
and
W
given in Supplementary Materials,
K
subgroups are sufficiently separable such that for any pair
\{(j,k),1\leq j\neq k\leq K\}
and any
\bm{\Psi}\in\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}\left(\bm{\Psi}^{*}\right)

\rho_{k}^{\bm{\Psi}}\cdot\rho_{j}^{\bm{\Psi}}\leq\frac{\eta}{24\sqrt{3}(K-1)\sqrt{KW}},\text{~a.s.}
.

Assumption 3

\left\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\right\|_{\max}
,
\left\|\bm{\tau}^{\ast}\right\|_{\max}
,

\left\|\bm{\mu}^{*}\right\|_{\max}
, and
\max_{k}\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
are bounded from above, and
\left\|\bm{\tau}^{\ast}\right\|_{\min}
is bounded from below. Furthermore,
\max\limits_{i=1,\cdots,n}{c}_{i}\leq\bar{C}_{2}
and
\max\limits_{i=1,\cdots,n}\left\|\bm{x}_{i}\right\|_{\max}\leq\bar{C}_{3}
for some constants
\bar{C}_{2}
and
\bar{C}_{3}
.

Assumption 4

The number of subgroups
K
satisfies
K\sqrt{\log n/p}=o\left(1\right)
.

Assumption 5

The tuning parameters satisfy
u=O\left(\sqrt{{n(Ks+p)\log p}/{(Kp^{2}s^{2})}}\right)

1/v_{0}=O\left(\sqrt{nK(Ks+p)\log p/s}\right)
,
1/v_{1}=O\left(\sqrt{nK(Ks+p)\log p/s}\right)
, and
v_{0}<v_{1}
.

Assumption
1
is a restricted strong concave condition for the log-likelihood function.
Assumption
2
is the sufficient separability condition, which requires that the probability of each subject belonging to a subgroup is either close to 1 or 0. Such an assumption is often assumed in the literature
[
11
,
20
]
. Assumption
3
puts constraints on the bounds of the true parameters and the predictors, which are very common in the relevant literature. Assumption
4
allows
K
to grow at a rate slower than
\sqrt{p/\log n}
, which is also considered in the related literature
[
11
,
20
]
. Assumption
5
restricts the rate of the tuning parameters, which are commonly assumed in high-dimensional studies
[
26
,
20
,
28
]
.

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{k}=\mathcal{W}_{k}^{*}
,

\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{k}=\mathcal{V}_{k}^{*}
for any

3
Simulation

Simulation studies are conducted to evaluate the performance of the proposed approach under the following settings. (a)

K=2
and
3
and
p=100
. (b) Consider two settings for the sample size. One is a balanced design where there are 150 subjects in each subgroup, while the other is an imbalanced design with sample sizes being
100
and
200
for
K=2
and
100,150
, and
200
for
K=3
. Thus, there are
n=300
subjects for
K=2
, and
n=450
for
K=3
in total. (c)
The predictor observations
\left\{\bm{x}_{i}\right\}
in the
k
th subgroup are simulated from
\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\bm{\Omega}_{k}^{-1}\right)
, where
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
is generated based on the corresponding network structure. Specifically, following
[
6
]
, we simulate each network with ten unconnected subnetworks. To comprehensively examine the performance of the proposed approach under different degrees of similarity across subgroup-specific precision matrices, we consider three specific settings S1-S3, with the number of subnetworks sharing the same sparsity structure in all subgroups being 3, 5, and 7, respectively.
For each subnetwork, we consider three types of structure, including the
Power-law network with the degrees following a power-law distribution, the Nearest-neighbor network with
p/10
points being first generated on a unit square and one nearest neighbor then being identified for each point by calculating the pairwise distances of the
p/10\times(p/10-1)/2
points, and the Erdös-Rényi network with edges randomly added with a probability of 0.1. For the
k
th subgroup, set
{\omega}_{k,jj}=0
and
{\omega}_{k,jl}=0
if
j
and
l(\neq j)
are not connected in the
k
th simulated network; otherwise, the signs of
{\omega}_{k,jl}
’s are randomly set to positive or negative with an equal probability, and their absolute values are independently generated from uniform distributions with support on
[0.5,1]
for
K=2
, and
[0.8,1]\times I\left\{k=1\right\}+[1.8,2]\times I\left\{k=2\right\}+[2.8,3]\times I\left\{k=3\right\}
for
K=3
. We further add
0.01+\left|\lambda_{\min}\left(\bm{\Omega}_{k}\right)\right|
to the diagonal elements of the
k
th precision matrix to ensure that
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
is positive definite. (d) Each logarithmic survival observation
t_{i}
is generated as follows. First, the logarithmic survival times of the
k
th subgroup are generated from
\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\beta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i},0.01^{2}\right)
, where five elements of
\bm{\beta}_{k}
are non-zero, and the rest
p-5
elements are all zero with
\bm{\beta}_{1}=\left(2,2,2,2,2,0,\ldots,0\right)^{\top}
,
\bm{\beta}_{2}=-\left(2,2,2,2,2,0,\ldots,0\right)^{\top}
, and
\bm{\beta}_{3}=\left(0,0,1,1,1,1,1,0,\ldots,0\right)^{\top}
, respectively. Then, the censoring time is simulated from a Gamma distribution with the shape and scale parameters controlling the censoring rate to be
\sim 20\%
. There are a total of 36 scenarios.

In addition to the proposed approach, six alternative approaches are also considered. BCGS is a Bayesian approach for simultaneously clustering and learning multiple sparse graphical models using a joint spike-and-slab graphical lasso prior
[
5
]
.
SCAN is a mixture Gaussian graphical model-based approach that applies the group lasso penalty to accommodate common structures across unknown heterogeneous subgroups
[
11
]
. TGemBag and TJGL are two “ideal approaches” which assume that the true subgroup memberships are known and adopt GemBag and JGL for jointly estimating multiple networks for the true subgroups, respectively. Here, GemBag is a Bayesian graphical model for accommodating similarity as well as difference of multiple networks
[
26
]
, and JGL employs a likelihood-based joint graphical Lasso for multiple network estimations
[
6
]
. FMGemBag and FMJGL are two two-stage approaches, where in the first stage, a finite mixture (FM) of AFT models developed in
[
22
]
is used to obtain the subgroup memberships of all subjects, which can account for right censoring and intangible heterogeneous predictor-survival associations, and in the second stage, GemBag and JGL are adopted for network estimation based on the estimated subgroup memberships, respectively. Among these methods, BCGS, SCAN, TJGL, and FMJGL can be realized using
R
package
JGL
, and TGemBag and FMGemBag can be realized using
R
codes at https://github.com/xinming104/GemBag. BCGS and SCAN achieve identification of unknown heterogeneity and network structures simultaneously, however, are unsupervised and only account for predictors.
GemBag and JGL are developed for estimating precision matrices with known subgroup memberships. We consider two types of subgroup memberships for GemBag and JGL, including the one based on the FM approach, which utilizes the information of survival for heterogeneous analysis, and the “ideal” type based on the true subgroup memberships.

To evaluate the performance of different approaches, the following measures are calculated:
(1) Clustering error (CE) for evaluating the heterogeneity identification performance, defined as
\operatorname{CE}(\hat{\varphi},\varphi^{*})\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{l}n\\
2\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left|\left\{(i,j):{I}\left\{\hat{\varphi}_{i}=\hat{\varphi}_{j}\right\}\neq{I}\left\{\varphi_{i}^{*}=\varphi_{j}^{*}\right\};i<j\right\}\right|
with
\hat{\varphi}
and
{\varphi}^{*}
being the estimated and true subgroup memberships.
(2) Precision matrix squared error (PME) for evaluating the estimation performance, defined as
\operatorname{PME}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}-\bm{\Omega}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}}{K}.

(3) True and false positive rates (TPR and FPR) for evaluating the network identification performance, defined as
\operatorname{TPR}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\sum_{j<l}{I}\left\{\omega_{k,jl}^{*}\neq 0,\widetilde{\omega}_{k,jl}\neq 0\right\}}{K\sum_{j<l}{I}{\left\{\omega_{k,jl}^{*}\neq 0\right\}}}
and
\operatorname{FPR}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\sum_{j<l}{I}{\left\{{\omega}_{k,jl}^{*}=0,\widetilde{\omega}_{k,jl}\neq 0\right\}}}{K\sum_{j<l}{I}\left\{{\omega}_{k,jl}^{*}=0\right\}}.
Here, similar to other network-based heterogeneity analysis studies
[
11
,
5
]
, we focus on the performance of estimation and identification on
\omega_{k,jl}
’s, which can be more challenging than on
\mu_{k,j}
’s and
\beta_{k,j}
’s.

100 replicates are conducted for each scenario, where the true value of
K
is used for all approaches. The summary results for
K=2
are shown in Tables
1
-
3
, and the rest of the results are provided in Supplementary Materials. Under all simulated scenarios, the proposed approach consistently demonstrates superior or competitive performance compared to the alternative approaches. In terms of subgroup identification, the proposed approach performs significantly better with much smaller CEs. Among the alternatives, the FM methods tend to exhibit the poorest subgroup identification performance, while SCAN and BCGS have better subgroup identification accuracy but are still much inferior compared to the proposed approach.

As the similarity between the networks increases (from S1 to S3), it is more challenging to identify subgroup memberships. However, the proposed approach still shows more prominent advantages compared to SCAN, BCGS, FMGemBag, and FMJGL in terms of both heterogeneity and network identification performance. It is interesting that, under S3, although with a certain level of clustering errors, the proposed approach has better network identification performance than TGemBag and TJGL with “ideal” true subgroup memberships, suggesting the effectiveness of the proposed similarity-based prior. Under the scenarios with an imbalanced design or a larger number of subgroups, performance of all approaches declines. Under these scenarios, the proposed approach, when compared to TGemBag, exhibits a slightly worse performance in network identification and estimation due to the more complex unknown heterogeneity and the relatively limited sample size. However, it still consistently outperforms the other alternatives. It maintains its superiority under the scenarios with various types of network structure, including the more challenging power-law networks with denser structures and hub structures. With relatively simpler nearest neighbor networks, when all methods generally perform well with higher TPR values, the proposed approach retains significantly fewer FPs compared to most alternatives.

Data Analysis

We illustrate the proposed approach in constructing gene networks of different subgroups of cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data. Date is downloaded from the TCGA Provisional using
R
package
cgdsr
. We consider the metastatic subjects and use the mRNA gene expression data and overall survival time for the predictors and response, respectively.
360
subjects are available with
19,039
gene expression measurements. Among them,
53.6\%
are non-censored, with the observed survival times ranging from 2.6 to 357.1 months (median 44.05 months). As the sample size is relatively small, we follow the existing studies
[
1
]
and conduct gene filtering. Specifically, we first identify 2,260 genes with significance levels below
0.01
from univariate Cox-regression models and then focus on those present in six pathways (pyrimidine metabolism, P53 signaling, cysteine and methionine metabolism, chemokine signaling, cell cycle, and hedgehog signaling pathways) that have demonstrated important implications for SKCM in the literature. With this preprocessing, 91 genes are obtained for downstream analysis.

As the number of subgroups
K
is unknown, we consider the candidate set
\left\{2,3,4,5,6,7\right\}
and adopt the Gap statistic for selecting the optimal one. With the proposed approach, the Gap statistic leads to two subgroups with sizes of 128 (subgroup 1) and 232 (subgroup 2). The two networks consist of 181 and 345 edges among 80 and 83 genes, respectively. Among them, 52 edges are common and all have the same signs. A graphical representation is provided in Figure
3
, and more detailed estimation results are provided in Supplementary Materials. To gain deeper insights into the identified networks, we conduct Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using DAVID 2021 to examine the functional and biological connections of the related genes. Some significantly enriched GO terms are obtained for the common edges, and genes that exhibit unique edges in the two subgroups are found to be linked to different GO terms. The sensible biological findings provide support to the validity of the proposed network estimation. We refer to Supplementary Materials for the detailed GO enrichment analysis results.

Comparative analysis using the alternative approaches is also carried out with
K=2
. The summary results are reported in Table S4 of Supplementary Materials, which provide the number of subjects and network edges in the two subgroups identified by different approaches and their overlaps. The subgroups identified using different approaches are matched by correlation. In Table S4, different approaches are observed to identify subgroups with different subjects. To get a deeper understanding of the heterogeneity analysis performance, we examine whether these subgroups are associated with survival time. In Figure
4
, we present the Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves of survival time of different subgroups identified by the proposed and alternative approaches. It is observed that the KM curves of the two subgroups are significantly distinguished from each other for the proposed approach (P value: 0.005). Comparatively, the FM approach shows less significant differences. BCGS and SCAN approaches fail to identify subgroups with distinct survival. These biologically reasonable findings indicate that the proposed approach can satisfactorily identify survival-related subgroups and also provide support for its effectiveness in clinical subgroup identification.

In Table S4, different approaches demonstrate a moderate number of overlapping edges. To provide indirect support for the network estimation results, we use the resampling strategy and randomly divide the data into a training and a testing set. As in the literature
[
7
]
, the values of the negative log-likelihood statistic (NLS) for the 91 genes are calculated to evaluate prediction accuracy. The average NLS values of 100 resamplings are 7102.118 for the proposed approach, 7658.492 for BCGS, 7674.739 for SCAN, 8044.482 for FMGemBag, and 8221.576 for FMJGL, which suggest a satisfactory network estimation accuracy of the proposed approach.

Discussion

In this article, we have introduced a novel Bayesian approach for jointly learning multiple sparse networks in a supervised paradigm when the subgroup memberships are unknown. Benefiting from the Bayesian framework, the proposed approach can effectively accommodate both the heterogeneous distributions of predictors and the heterogeneous associations between predictors and survival time, leading to results with stronger clinical implications. In addition to the sparse priors for network estimation, we have introduced a similarity prior to encourage similar network sparsity as well as similar positive or negative effects across different subgroups, which can more effectively address the similarity of subgroups. We have thoroughly examined the theoretical properties of the proposed approach. A series of numerical experiments have been conducted to demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed approach in both heterogeneity and network identification. By applying the approach to TCGA data, we have discovered various gene networks for SKCM patients and identified biologically relevant relationships associated with clinically sensible subgroups.

In this study, we have employed the commonly used Gaussian mixture models for heterogeneity analysis, which show satisfactory performance. It can be of interest to explore alternative robust techniques, such as the
t
-distribution mixture model or other nonparametric mixture graphical models, for studying heavy-tailed data. Our focus in this data analysis has been mainly on the identification of gene networks using expression data. There is vast potential for a more comprehensive understanding of cancer mechanisms by incorporating other omics measurements, such as mutation and DNA methylation.
