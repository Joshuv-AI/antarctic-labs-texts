Optimal Ramsey interferometry with echo protocols based on one-axis twisting
M. S. Scharnagl, T. Kielinski, K. Hammerer

Abstract
We study a variational class of generalised Ramsey protocols that include two one-axis twisting (OAT) operations, one performed before the phase imprint and the other after. In this framework, we optimise the axes of the signal imprint, the OAT interactions, and the direction of the final projective measurement. We distinguish between protocols that exhibit symmetric or antisymmetric dependencies of the spin projection signal on the measured phase. Our results show that the quantum Fisher information, which sets the limits on the sensitivity achievable with a given uniaxially twisted input state, can be saturated within our class of variational protocols for almost all initial twist strengths. By incorporating numerous protocols previously documented in the literature, our approach creates a unified framework for Ramsey echo protocols with OAT states and measurements.



I
Introduction

Quantum metrology employs quantum strategies, as e.g. entanglement and squeezing, to enhance the precision of measurements beyond classical bounds
Pezzè et al. 2018
and has a wide range of applications, e.g. in gravitational wave detection, quantum phase estimation, quantum magnetometer, quantum spectroscopy and atomic clock synchronization
Liu et al. 2021
. Here we consider Ramsey interferometry as the most common method in quantum metrology with a variety of applications such as atom interferometers and optical atomic clocks. These in turn pave the way for the search for new physics, such as experiments on Lorentz violation
Dreissen et al. 2022
, the search for dark matter
Derevianko and Pospelov 2014
and for variation of the fundamental constants
Safronova 2019
, geodesy
Grotti et al. 2018
and tests of general relativity
Takamoto et al. 2020
.

The precision in phase estimation achievable in a Ramsey protocol is restricted by quantum projection noise, i.e. unavoidable quantum fluctuations in a measurement. The standard Ramsey protocol using classical states is limited by the standard quantum limit (SQL). Nevertheless, it is possible to overcome this limitation up to the Heisenberg limit (HL) by using entangled or spin squeezed states, as pointed out by
Wineland et al. 1994
. One promising method creating spin squeezed states is one-axis twisting (OAT)
Kitagawa and Ueda 1993
which can be realized experimentally through collisions in Bose-Einstein condensates
Estève et al. 2008
;
Gross et al. 2010
, via cavity feedback squeezing of cold atoms
Colombo et al. 2022a
;
Li et al. 2022
or by implementing Mølmer-Sørensen gates on trapped ions
Blatt and Wineland 2008
. Besides the simple squeezing protocols which already allow to reduce the phase estimation error by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(N^{1/3})

Kitagawa and Ueda 1993
, there have been several previous investigations on so-called echo protocols, where OAT
Leibfried et al. 2004
;
Leibfried et al. 2005
;
Davis et al. 2016
;
Fröwis et al. 2016
;
Macrì et al. 2016
;
Nolan et al. 2017
;
Schulte et al. 2020a
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Volkoff and Martin 2022
;
Colombo et al. 2022b
or other squeezing methods
Burd et al. 2019
;
Anders et al. 2018
applied before and after the phase imprint help improving the sensitivity of the Ramsey protocol even further.

In previous work
Schulte et al. 2020a
we considered a variational class of echo protocols which was defined such as to allow for an analytical optimization of geometric control parameters corresponding to rotation axes and angles. Within this variational class, many of the protocols known in the literature, as well as some new protocols, could be identified as local maxima of the achievable sensitivity. This allowed a systematization of echo protocols, which, however, remained partial due to certain constraints of the variational class adopted in
Schulte et al. 2020a
. This concerns, on the one hand, restrictions on geometric control, which excludes, for example, some of the protocols of
Fröwis et al. 2016
;
Nolan et al. 2017
. On the other hand, the variational class was constrained to protocols whose signal
S(\phi)
is anti-symmetric with respect to the inversion of the metrological phase, i.e.
S(-\phi)=-S(\phi)
. This constraint excluded e.g. the schemes of
Leibfried et al. 2004
;
Li et al. 2023
generating a symmetric signals
S(-\phi)=S(\phi)
.

Building on the protocols considered in
Schulte et al. 2020a
, in this paper we aim at a much more general systematization of echo protocols, which is broader in terms of both their geometric degrees of freedom and the (anti)symmetry of the signal. To this purpose, we define an enlarged variational class, still based on one OAT operation each before and after signal imprint, covering all protocols studied in
Leibfried et al. 2004
;
Leibfried et al. 2005
;
Davis et al. 2016
;
Fröwis et al. 2016
;
Macrì et al. 2016
;
Nolan et al. 2017
;
Schulte et al. 2020a
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Volkoff and Martin 2022
;
Colombo et al. 2022b
. This generality comes at the cost of a largely numerical optimization over the variational class considered here. Our main findings are: (i) In this generalized class of Ramsey protocols, the quantum Fisher information (QFI), which bounds the maximum possible sensitivity, can be saturated for almost all initial twisting strengths
\mu\in[0,\pi]
. Here, OAT operations are described by unitaries
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\mu)=\exp(-i\mu S_z^2/2)
with collective spin operator
S_{z}
. Saturation of the QFI is achieved by means of suitably one-axis twisted projective spin measurements. (ii) Protocols with anti-symmetric signal saturate the QFI for all twisting strengths, except in a neighborhood around
\mu\simeq\pi
. (iii) For an initial OAT around
\mu\simeq\pi
, generating GHZ-like states, the QFI is saturated by schemes with symmetric signals, with the protocols of
Leibfried et al. 2004
included as a special case.

The article is organized as follows: In Sec.
II

1

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}=\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\ket{\downarrow}^{\otimes N}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\ket{\downarrow}_{j}+\ket{\uparrow}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}

The phase estimation error can be classified by the mean squared error

\epsilon_{\textrm{M}}(\phi)=\sum_{m}\left[\hat{\phi}(m)-\phi\right]^{2}p(m|\phi),

(1)

III
Optimal protocols

III.1
Figures of merit

In order to optimize the variational class of protocols with anti-symmetric or symmetric signals that have been identified, we need to suitably adapt the figure of merit based on the phase measurement error of the anti-symmetric standard Ramsey protocol in Eq. (
2
). For anti-symmetric Ramsey protocols this is straight forwardly achieved for a working point
\phi=0
by

\displaystyle\Delta\phi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{k})=\left.\frac{\Delta{S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}}{|\partial_{\phi}\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|}\right|_{\phi=0}.

(4)

Since we want to reduce
\Delta\phi
as far as possible, we maximize the inverse of the phase deviation
\Delta\phi
, the sensitivity

\displaystyle(\Delta\phi)^{-1}_{\rm opt}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\max_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{1}{\Delta\phi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{k})},

(5)

with respect to the directions
\boldsymbol{n}
,
\boldsymbol{m}
, and
\boldsymbol{k}
. Analogous to
Schulte et al. 2020a
, we performed the optimization over
\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m}
via a singular value decomposition (cf. Appendix
A
), while we executed the optimization of
\boldsymbol{k}
using differential evolution (DE), a numerical routine for global optimization of constrained parameters.

For symmetric Ramsey protocols the signal
\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\phi)\rangle
has an extremum at
\phi=0
, such that the slope vanishes there. Therefore, the phase variance at
\phi=0
, as defined in Eq. (
4
), is no longer a meaningful measure to characterize the quality of symmetric protocols. Instead, symmetric protocols are operated with a two-point-sampling method
Leibfried et al. 2004
. This means that, in contrast to the one-point-sampling used for anti-symmetric protocols, for symmetric protocols the system is no longer probed at only one phase value
\phi=0
, but at two points with additional phase shift
\pm\varphi
. The combined error signal after these two measurement cycles is then used to estimate the imprinted phase
\phi
. We therefore optimize the phase deviation at
\phi=\varphi\neq 0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Delta\phi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{k})=\left.\frac{\Delta{S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}}{|\partial_{\phi}\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|}\right|_{\phi=\varphi},

(6)

whereby the operating point
\varphi
, as proposed by
Leibfried et al. 2004
, is optimally chosen as the inflection point of the signal curve
\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\phi)\rangle
, since the slope
|\partial_{\phi}\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|
becomes maximum there.

IV
Noise and imperfections

The above optimizations only consider an ideal case and disregard any noise. In this section, we will consider three important types of imperfections or limitations, namely particle number fluctuations, finite dynamic range and dephasing during twisting operations.

Another significant source of noise is the dephasing that occurs during the twisting. V
Summary and Outlook

In conclusion, we have provided a comprehensive systematization of one-axis twisting echo protocols, building upon the results of our earlier work
Schulte et al. 2020a
. We accounted for and optimized a larger set of geometrical control parameters, also considering protocols with symmetric signals. Although this approach limited us to primarily numerical optimization, we identified a larger number of local sensitivity maxima compared to
Schulte et al. 2020a
. A significant finding is that the class of entangled measurements considered in this study enables the saturation of the QFI for nearly all initial squeezing strengths
\mu_{1}
. This was not achievable in the protocols discussed in
Schulte et al. 2020a
, emphasizing the importance of optimizing the direction,
\boldsymbol{k}
, of the second twisting. Most of the QFI is saturated by protocols with anti-symmetric signals, except near
\mu_{1}=\pi
, where only protocols with symmetric signals can saturate the QFI.

Furthermore, we analyzed the impact of various noise sources, including particle number fluctuations, prior phase noise, and dephasing noise during the twisting process, on the sensitivity of these optimal protocols. We discovered that the sensitivity for the majority of the identified protocols is not stable under particle number fluctuations; hence, these protocols are most suitable for experiments where the particle number,
N
, is well-controlled. For experiments with substantial particle number fluctuations, only the OUT protocols discussed in
Schulte et al. 2020a
(A3), protocols at the maximum of region A4, and protocols with minor twisting and untwisting (A2) are appropriate. Regarding dephasing noise during the twisting operation, we demonstrated that protocols in the maxima of regions A2, A3, A4, and S3 are especially stable. Interestingly, even with significant dephasing noise, the sensitivity of the optimal A2 region protocols can surpass that of the simple squeezing protocols (A1, S1). Our examination of the trade-off between sensitivity gain and dynamic range loss revealed that the protocols at the maximum of regions A2 and A4, along with the optimal protocols of region S3, exhibit the greatest resilience to prior phase noise. However, with extensive prior phase noise, only the simple squeezing protocols (A1, S1) maintain non-vanishing sensitivity. An optimization using Bayesian phase estimation in the variational class of echo-protocols discussed here, along with its application in optical atomic clocks, will be addressed in subsequent work.

Appendix C
Derivation of our figure of merit from the mean squared error

For anti-symmetric protocols, the mean squared error is given by

\epsilon(\phi)=\sum_{m}\left[\hat{\phi}(m)-\phi\right]^{2}p(m|\phi).

Evaluating this at
\phi=0
and using a linear phase estimator gives

\displaystyle\epsilon(\phi)

\displaystyle\overset{\phi=0}{=}(\Delta\phi)^{2}=\sum_{m}\left[\hat{\phi}(m)-0\right]^{2}p(m|\phi)=\sum_{m}\hat{\phi}^{2}(m)p(m|\phi)

\displaystyle\,\,=\sum_{m}\left(\frac{m}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}|_{\phi=0}\right)^{2}p(m|\phi)=\frac{1}{(\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}|_{\phi=0}}\sum_{m}m^{2}\,p(m|\phi)

\displaystyle\,\,=\frac{\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2}\rangle|_{\phi=0}}{(\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}|_{\phi=0}}=\frac{(\Delta S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}}{(\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}}\Big|_{\phi=0}

For symmetric protocols, we know that

S_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\phi)=S_{\boldsymbol{m}}(-\phi),\quad\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\phi)=-\,\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}(-\phi).

Since symmetric protocols have an extremum at
\phi=0
, we measure the signal at
\phi=\pm\varphi
:

1.

(\hat{\phi}+\varphi)=\frac{m_{+}}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\big|_{\phi=\varphi}

2.

(\hat{\phi}-\varphi)=\frac{m_{-}}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\big|_{\phi=-\varphi}

A suitable estimator for the phase uncertainty is therefore

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{m_{+}}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\big|_{\phi=\varphi}+\frac{m_{-}}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\big|_{\phi=-\varphi}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\Big|_{\phi=\varphi}\left(m_{+}-m_{-}\right).

We assume that the conditional probabilities
p(m_{+}|\phi+\varphi)
and
p(m_{-}|\phi-\varphi)
are independent, i.e.

\displaystyle p(m_{+},m_{-}|\phi)=p(m_{+}|\phi+\varphi)\cdot p(m_{-}|\phi-\varphi).

With this, we derive

\displaystyle\epsilon(\phi)

\displaystyle\,\,=\sum_{m_{+},m_{-}}p(m_{+}|\phi+\varphi)\,p(m_{-}|\phi-\varphi)\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}|_{\phi=\varphi}}(m_{+}-m_{-})-\phi\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\phi\,=\,0}{=}\sum_{m_{+},m_{-}}p(m_{+}|\phi+\varphi)\,p(m_{-}|\phi-\varphi)\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}}|_{\phi=\varphi}}\right]^{2}\,(m_{+}-m_{-})^{2}

\displaystyle\,\,=\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{(\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}|_{\phi=\varphi}}\,\Big(\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2}\rangle|_{\phi=\varphi}+\underbrace{\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2}\rangle|_{\phi=-\varphi}}_{=\,\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2}\rangle|_{\phi=\varphi}}-2\,\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|_{\phi=\varphi}\,\underbrace{\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|_{\phi=-\varphi}}_{=\,\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|_{\phi=\varphi}}\Big)

\displaystyle\,\,=\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{(\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}|_{\phi=\varphi}}\,2\,\Big(\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2}\rangle-\langle S_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle^{2}\Big)\Big|_{\phi=\varphi}

\displaystyle\,\,=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\Delta S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}}{(\partial_{\phi}S_{\boldsymbol{m}})^{2}}\Big|_{\phi=\varphi}.
