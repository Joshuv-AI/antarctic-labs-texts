Synthesis of Hierarchical Controllers Based on Deep Reinforcement Learning Policies
Florent Delgrange, Guy Avni, Anna Lukina, Christian Schilling, Ann Nowé, Guillermo A. Pérez

Abstract
We propose a novel approach to the problem of controller design for environments modeled as Markov decision processes (MDPs). Specifically, we consider a hierarchical MDP a graph with each vertex populated by an MDP called a “room.” We first apply deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to obtain low-level policies for each room, scaling to large rooms of unknown structure. We then apply reactive synthesis to obtain a high-level planner that chooses which low-level policy to execute in each room. The central challenge in synthesizing the planner is the need for modeling rooms. We address this challenge by developing a DRL procedure to train concise “latent” policies together with PAC guarantees on their performance. Unlike previous approaches, ours circumvents a model distillation step. Our approach combats sparse rewards in DRL and enables reusability of low-level policies. We demonstrate feasibility in a case study involving agent navigation amid moving obstacles.



1
Introduction

We consider the fundamental problem of constructing control
policies
for environments modeled as
Markov decision processes
(MDPs). We are inspired by two techniques
with complementary benefits and drawbacks. The first
is reinforcement learning (RL)
[
52
]
, where the designer chooses how rewards are issued for actions, and control policies are trained to optimize rewards. Particularly, since the introduction of deep RL (DRL)
[
41
]
, we are witnessing successful training of controllers in domains of increasing size, often surpassing human capabilities in their quality. However, a notorious challenge of RL is training for tasks with sparse rewards.
For example, consider a robot obtaining a reward for exiting a building. Since it is improbable that a random exploration reaches the exit, training is time-consuming and expensive, and often fails even in small environments. Training is even harder when the building contains obstacles.

The second technique
is
reactive synthesis

[
46
]
, in which an optimal policy is constructed based on a given model of the environment and an objective specified as a logical formula.
Two key advantages of synthesis, in contrast to DRL, are guarantees and simplicity of describing objectives in logic, especially the qualitative high-level ones.
For example, exiting the building in linear temporal logic (LTL) is
\Diamond\text{Exit}
.
Automating logical specifications for RL is an ongoing effort
[
7
]
.

In this work, we propose a novel approach to designing policies in environments with a hierarchical structure. We model such environments as an undirected graph, called a
map
, in which the vertices are populated by MDPs called
rooms
. We develop a controller as follows. In each room, we train DRL policies to complete short-horizon room-specific tasks; e.g., safely exiting a room via one of the doors.
Based on the DRL policies, we synthesize a high-level
planner
to solve a long-horizon task given on the map; e.g., reach a target room.
We call the combination of low-level policies and high-level planner a
hierarchical controller
.

A key challenge in synthesizing a planner is that in order to decide which low-level policy the planner chooses in a room, we require an estimate on the probability that the policy succeeds. This is challenging for two reasons.
First, the policy that DRL outputs is a neural network (NN), which is too large to incorporate in a synthesis procedure.
Second, the transition probabilities in the rooms are unknown and rooms can only be accessed via a simulator.

Taking a step back, these challenges arise since we aim to formally reason about components represented as NNs. This is a fundamental problem (e.g.,
[
34
]
). A common two-step approach first trains an NN,
distills

[
29
]
a concise
latent
model, and applies formal reasoning on the latent model
(see
[
20
,
2
,
17
,
18
,
13
,
54
]
).
Note that formal reasoning provides guarantees only if distillation provides error bounds on the approximation of the latent model.

In this work, we devise a novel DRL procedure that trains a latent policy directly, thereby circumventing the need for model distillation. The procedure outputs a latent MDP together with a mapping from concrete environment states to latent states. We provide probably approximately correct (PAC) guarantees on the value of the latent policy. We believe that our approach can be of independent interest. Our procedure alternates between optimization for DRL (specifically, we apply
deep Q-networks
, (DQN)) and optimizing the abstraction,
based on a technique developed in
[
17
,
18
]
for model distillation.

We synthesize a planner by constructing and solving an MDP based on the high-level map and success probabilities obtained from the latent models. In addition, we develop a technique to reuse the guarantees of the rooms in which we estimate the entrance probabilities of the rooms under a random planner.

Finally, we demonstrate the feasibility of our approach in a case study for navigating an agent in a grid world with the objective to reach a distant location while avoiding adversarial moving obstacles. DQN is not capable of finding a policy that reaches the target, even when using well established techniques like reward shaping to guide training. We then develop a hierarchical controller as follows. We partition the grid into smaller grid maps (“rooms”). On these rooms, we demonstrate the applicability of our procedure to train latent policies. We synthesize a planner for the latent policies.
We report positive results. First, the hierarchical controller achieves high probability of success, thereby demonstrating how the approach addresses the problem of sparse rewards in training. Second, the observed values are close to the ones predicted by the offline model, demonstrating the quality of our automatically-constructed model.

In summary, we propose a novel approach to developing controllers in hierarchical environments based on low-level DRL and high-level planner synthesis. Our unified DRL procedure learns verifiable latent space models to abstract each room, and show that we can bound the gap between the values of the hierarchical objective using the average error computed during this abstraction. We experimentally demonstrate applicability in a challenging case study.

Preliminaries

Notation.

The support of a distribution
P
is
\text{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P}}\right)=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid P(x)>0\right\}

Markov decision processes.

An

A stationary

A policy gives rise to a probability distribution over paths of

and

\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\mathbf{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)

The probability of a path
\rho=s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
is

\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}}}\right)\cdot\text{{E}}_{a\sim\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s_{n-1}}}\right)}\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{n}\mid s_{n-1},a}}\right)
.

We let

\xi_{\pi}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}\mid s}}\right)=\text{{P}r}_{\rho\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\pi}}\left[\rho\in\left\{s_{0},\dots,s_{n}\mid s_{n}=s^{\prime}\right\}\mid s_{0}=s\right]

denote the probability distribution
of
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}
after exactly

steps, starting from state

.
Under policy

,

is a
bottom strongly connected component
(BSCC) of

if (i)

is a maximal subset satisfying
\xi_{\pi}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime}\mid s}}\right)>0

and some

, and (ii)

\text{{E}}_{a\sim\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)}\,\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C\mid s,a}}\right)=1

s\in\mathcal{S}
.
An MDP

is

\pi

\left\{s\in\mathcal{S}\mid\exists n\geq 0,\text{{E}}_{s_{0}\sim\mathbf{I}}\,\xi_{\pi}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\mid s_{0}}}\right)>0\right\}
consists of a unique aperiodic BSCC.
In that case, for

,
\xi_{\pi}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{\pi}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s}}\right)
is the

of
\mathcal{M}
under

.
We write

as shorthand for a distribution over
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
that is obtained by sampling

and then sampling

Objectives and values.

A qualitative
objective
is a set of infinite paths
\mathbb{O}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\omega}
.
For

with

, we consider the

We also write

Formally,
\mathbb{O}(T,B)=\left\{s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},\dots\mid\exists i\in\mathbb{N},\,s_{i}\in T\text{ and }\forall i\ s_{i}\notin B\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\omega}

The

val\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\mathbb{O}}}\right)=\Pr_{\rho\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\pi}}\left[\rho\in\mathbb{O}\right]
.
Given a policy
\pi
, the value of a state

is the value obtained by running
\pi
from that particular state:
val_{s}=\Pr_{\rho\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\pi}}\left[\rho\in\mathbb{O}\mid s_{0}=s\right]
.
We consider a

variant of the value function (see
[
4

5
,
16
,
8
]
), which satisfies
\lim_{\gamma\to 1}val\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\mathbb{O},\gamma}}\right)=val\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\mathbb{O}}}\right)
, where
\gamma\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
is the discount factor.
In particular, for any reach-avoid objective
\mathbb{O}(T,B)

and

,

val\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi,\mathbb{O},\gamma}}\right)=\text{{E}}_{\rho\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\pi}}\left[\sup_{i\geq 0}\gamma^{i}\mathds{1}\left\{{s_{i}\in T}\right\}\mathds{1}\left\{{\rho\in\mathbb{O}}\right\}\right]
, where
s_{i}
denotes the
{i^{\text{th}}}
state of

.
We omit

when it is clear from the context.

Reinforcement learning

(RL) is an approach to learn a policy over an MDP in a model-free manner (i.e., the explicit MDP is possibly unknown).
We focus on
Q-learning

[
57
]
, which is an established RL algorithm.
The idea is to learn the
Q-values
of states as a function
Q:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
, such that
Q(s,a)
represents the expected return of taking action
a
in state
s
and proceeding with an optimal policy from the next turn.
Executing action
a_{i}
in state
s_{i}
and transitioning to

incurs a

computed via a

\mathit{rew}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Given a discount factor
\gamma\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
, the
cumulative reward
along a path is
\mathcal{J}=\sum_{i>0}\gamma^{i}r_{i}
.
The goal is to find the optimal solution of the Bellman equation
[
48
]
:

Q^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)=\text{{E}}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)}\,\left[\mathit{rew}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a,s^{\prime}}}\right)+\gamma\cdot\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}}}\right)\right]
for any
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, with
\text{{E}}_{s_{0}\sim\mathbf{I}}\,\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{0},a}}\right)\right]=\max_{\pi}\text{{E}}_{\pi}\left[\mathcal{J}\right]
.
Q-values are learned iteratively:
at each step
i\geq 0
, a transition
\langle s,a,r,s^{\prime}\rangle
is drawn in
\mathcal{M}
, and

Q_{i+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)=Q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right)+\alpha(r+\gamma\allowbreak\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\allowbreak Q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}}}\right)-Q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a}}\right))

for a given learning rate
\alpha\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
. Under some assumptions,
Q_{i}
is guaranteed to converge to
Q^{*}

[
55
]
.
Training of an agent happens via interaction with the environment in episodic simulations.
Each episode ends in one of three ways: success (the objective was achieved), failure (an illegal state was reached), or the simulation eventually resets, indicating that the agent is stuck or that a time-out occurred.

Q-learning is implemented by maintaining a table of size
\left|\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\right|
of the Q-values. This is intractable for environments with large or continuous state spaces.

Deep Q-networks (DQN)
[
42

is an established technique to obtain scalability (even for continuous state spaces), at the cost of losing convergence guarantees, by approximating the Q-values in parameterized NNs.
The algorithm proceeds by fixing a network

56

The resulting network

DQN learns to optimize the

L_{\text{DQN}}(\theta)=\text{{E}}_{s,a,r,s^{\prime}\sim\xi_{\pi^{\epsilon}}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}\,;\,\widehat{\theta})-Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a\,;\,\theta}}\right)}}\right)^{2},

(1)

where
\pi^{\epsilon}
is
\epsilon
-greedy
, i.e.,
\pi^{\epsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mid s}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon}}\right)\mathds{1}\left\{{a=\arg\max_{a^{\prime}}Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,a^{\prime}}}\right)}\right\}+\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}}
for some
\epsilon\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
.
In practice,
\xi_{\pi^{\epsilon}}
is emulated by a
replay buffer
where encountered transitions are stored and then sampled later on to minimize
L_{\text{DQN}}
.

Problem Formulation

In this section, we formalize a model of environments with hierarchical structure, define hierarchical policies, and state the problem of finding such policies.
Intuitively, we consider an MDP given by a high-level
map
as an undirected graph, each of whose vertices is associated with a “low-level” MDP called a
room
. A hierarchical controller operates as follows. In each room, we assume access to a collection of
low-level policies
, each optimizing an objective that is local to the room. We will obtain these low-level policies using DRL, since the structure of the rooms is not necessarily known and their size can be large. When transitioning between two rooms, a high-level
planner
chooses which low-level policy to follow in the new room. We synthesize the planner to optimize a high-level reachability objective. The challenge we overcome in the next section is obtaining concise low-level policies with guarantees to enable a feasible synthesis procedure.

Hierarchical structure.

A
room
is
R=\langle\mathcal{S}_{R},\mathcal{A}_{R},\mathbf{P}_{R},D_{R},\mathcal{I}_{R},\mathcal{O}_{R}\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}_{R},\mathcal{A}_{R}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{R}
are as in definition of MDPs
\mathcal{M}
,
D_{R}

is a set of
directions
,
\mathcal{I}_{R}\colon D_{R}\rightarrow\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{S}_{R}}}\right)

is an
entrance function
that takes a direction from which the room is entered and produces an initial distribution over states, and
\mathcal{O}_{R}\colon D_{R}\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{S}_{R}}
is an
exit function
that returns a set of
exit states
from the room in a given direction
d\in D_{R}
.
We assume that states can only be assigned to a single exit, i.e.,
s\in\mathcal{O}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
implies that there is no other
d^{\prime}\neq d
so that
s\in\mathcal{O}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d^{\prime}}}\right)
.

proceeds according to the low-level policy

If

Suppose that

Problem statement.

For a hierarchical model
\mathcal{H}=\langle\mathcal{G},\mathcal{R},\ell,v_{0},d_{0}\rangle

,

4
Obtaining Latent Low-Level Policies using DRL

In this section, we describe an approach to obtain low-level policies for rooms.
There are fundamental challenges in reasoning about policies that are trained using DRL since such policies are typically represented using large NNs.
In this section, we develop a novel unified DRL procedure called
WAE-DQN
, which outputs a latent model together with a concise policy accompanied by PAC guarantees. We believe that WAE-DQN may be of independent interest.
Proofs of all our claims in this and subsequent sections can be found in Appendix.

4.1
Latent models and policies

We start by introducing a framework for defining concise policies. Throughout this section, fix an MDP environment
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathbf{P},\mathbf{I}\rangle
.
A
latent model
abstracts a concrete MDP and is itself an MDP

\mathcal{M}
via a

\phi\colon\mathcal{S}\to\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
. We focus on latent MDPs with a finite state space.

Let

be a policy in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
, called a
latent policy
.
The key feature is that
\phi
allows to control
\mathcal{M}
using
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. For each state

, let

in
\mathcal{M}
follow the distribution
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}}\right)

. Abusing notation, we refer to
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

.

Recall that

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

4.2
Quantifying the quality of the abstraction

In this section, given
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, we bound the difference between

and
\,\overline{\!{val}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\gamma)

\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
abstracts
\mathcal{M}
. Note that computing
val(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\gamma)
is intractable. To overcome this, we define a local measure of distance
L_{\mathbf{P}}
on the transitions of
\mathcal{M}
and
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
, and use it to bound the difference between the values. In the next section, we will show how to obtain a PAC estimation for
L_{\mathbf{P}}
and in turn a PAC estimation for the quality of the abstraction.

We define the
transition loss
, denoted
L_{\mathbf{P}}
, w.r.t. a distance metric
\mathcal{D}
on probability distributions over
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}

\mathcal{D}(P,P^{\prime})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left\|P-P^{\prime}\right\|_{1}

P,P^{\prime}\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}\right)

We describe the intuition behind the definition. Suppose that in
\mathcal{M}
, action
a
is taken in a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
This corresponds to taking action
a
in state
\phi(s)
of
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
, which gives rise to a distribution
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),{a}}}\right)\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}}}\right)

In
\mathcal{M}

in

\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)
over
\mathcal{S}

\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}

\phi\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,{a}}}\right)

\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)
into
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}}}
; formally

\phi\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}\mid s,{a}}}\right)=\Pr_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)}[\phi(s^{\prime})=\,\overline{\!{s}}^{\prime}]

The abstraction is accurate if the two distributions
are close. We define

by taking the expectation according to the stationary distribution
\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}

47

even for unknown probabilities (using a generative model).

L_{\mathbf{P}}=\text{{E}}_{s\sim\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},a\sim\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu({\cdotp}\mid s)}\,\,\mathcal{D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\mathbf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,{a}}}\right),\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right),{a}}}\right)}}\right).

(2)

Recall that training in RL proceeds in episodes, where an episode terminates either when the objective is certainly satisfied or violated, or when a reset occurs. We restrict
\mathcal{M}
accordingly to be an

Assumption 1
(Episodic process)

The environment
\mathcal{M}
contains a distinguished reset state

such that (i) under any policy,

is almost surely visited, and (ii)

Remark 1
(Ergodicity)

Assumption

In ergodic MDPs, each state is almost surely visited infinitely often

4.3
PAC estimates to the quality of the abstraction

The previous section establishes a bound on the quality of the abstraction based on

Every time the simulation proceeds from

Lemma 2

4.4
Obtaining latent policies during training

In this section, we describe WAE-DQN, a DRL procedure that trains a latent MDP and policy.
WAE-DQN alternates between latent policy optimization (via DQN, see Section
2
), and representation learning, which we describe next.

Wasserstein Auto-encoded MDP

(WAE-MDP)
[
18
]
is a distillation technique that provides PAC guarantees. Given an MDP
\mathcal{M}
, a policy
\pi
trained using DRL, and the number of states in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
, gradient descent is applied to learn the transition probabilities and the embedding function
\phi
by minimizing
L_{\mathbf{P}}
. In addition, a policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
is distilled
such that
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}

exhibits
bisimilarly close

[
37
,
24
,
17
]
behaviors to

\mathcal{M}
when
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is executed, thus providing PAC guarantees on the difference between the two values from Lemma
1
.

WAE-DQN.

Algorithm
1

unifies the training and distillation steps.
At each step
t=1,\ldots,{\cal T}
, we first explore the environment as in standard RL using an
\epsilon
-greedy policy to collect transitions into a replay buffer. We then perform two separate optimizations. The first one optimizes the abstraction parameters:
\theta_{\text{WAE}}
for the transitions in
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}
, and
\iota
for the embedding
\phi
.
Second, we optimize the parameters
\theta_{\text{DQN}}
to learn the policy as in DQN. We stress that the
Q
-function takes as input a latent state.
Notably, the introduction of another target assignment
\hat{\iota}
for the embedding
\phi
, which is used in conjunction with the target
Q
-network, is necessary to stabilize the training process (in the same spirit as
[
62
]
).

We point to the advantages of WAE-DQN.
WAE-MDPs enjoy theoretical
representation
guarantees ensuring that any states clustered to the same latent representation yield close values when
L_{\mathbf{P}}
in minimized:
for any latent policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
so that
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)
,

\left|val_{s_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\gamma}}\right)-val_{s_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\gamma}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{L_{\mathbf{P}}}{1-\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{1}}}\right)}+\frac{1}{\xi_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{2}}}\right)}}}\right)
.
Notably, learning a latent policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
for
\mathcal{M}
in parallel to learning a model of the latent space (corresponding to the input representation of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
) means that
(i) the embedding function
\phi
groups states with close values, which supports the learning of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
;
(ii)
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
prescribes the same actions for states exhibiting similar behaviors, enhancing its robustness to environmental perturbations.
Then, the direct learning of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
brings two key benefits:
(i) it removes the need for alternating between policy optimization and distillation phases, simplifying the learning process;
(ii) it enables the use of the same latent space for different rooms with similar structures.

Input:

steps
\mathcal{T}
, model updates
\mathcal{N}
, batch sizes

, and
\alpha,\epsilon\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}

5.2
Initial Distribution Shifts: Training vs. Synthesis

Our hierarchical controller construction occurs in two phases. First, we create a set of low-level policies
\mathrm{\Pi}
by running Algorithm
1
in each room (Section
4.4
). Notably, training in each room is independent and can be executed in parallel. However, independent training introduces a challenge: an
initial distribution shift
emerges when combining low-level policies using a planner. Our value bounds for a room
R
in direction
d
depend on a loss
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{R,d}
, computed based on the stationary distribution. This distribution may significantly change depending on a planner’s choices. In this section, we address this challenge by showing, under mild assumptions on the initial distribution of each room
R
, that their transition losses
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{R,d}
obtained under any latent policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{R,d}
for direction
d
still guarantee to bound the gap between the values of the original and latent hierarchical models.

Training rooms.

To construct
\mathrm{\Pi}
, we trained low-level policies via Algorithm
1
by simulating each room individually.
Precisely, for any room
R\in\mathcal{R}
and direction
d\in D_{R}
, we train a WAE-DQN agent by considering
R
as an episodic MDP with
some
initial distribution
\mathbf{I}_{R}
, which yields (i) a low-level latent policy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{R,d}

\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{R}
, and (iii) a state-embedding function
\phi_{R}
.
Since
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{R,d}
needs to learn to maximize the values of the objective
\mathbb{O}^{d}_{R}
which asks for reaching the exit state in direction
d
, we restart the simulation when the latter is visited.
Formally, the related training room is an episodic MDP
R_{d}=\langle\mathcal{S}_{R},\mathcal{A}_{R},\mathbf{P}_{R}^{d},\mathbf{I}_{R}\rangle
, where
s_{\text{reset}}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}
,
\mathbf{P}_{R}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)=\mathbf{P}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\cdotp}\mid s,a}}\right)
when
s\not\in\mathcal{O}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
, and
\mathbf{P}_{R}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s_{\text{reset}}\mid s,a}}\right)=1
otherwise.
We define
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{R}^{d}
similarly for
\,\overline{\!{\mathcal{M}}}_{R}
when the direction
d
is considered.

Distribution shift.

Crucially, by considering each room individually, a noticeable
initial distribution shift
occurs when switching between the training and synthesis phases. During training, there is no high-level controller, so the
initial distribution of the room
R
is merely
\mathbf{I}_{R}
.
In contrast, during synthesis, room entries and exits are determined by the distributions influenced by the choices made by the controller within the hierarchical MDP
\mathcal{H}
.
This implies that the induced initial distribution of each individual room depends on the likelihood of visiting other rooms and is further influenced by the other low-level policies.

We contend that this shift may induce significant consequences: denote by
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{R,d}
the transition loss of the room
R_{d}
operating under
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\pi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{R,d}

and by
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\tau,\mathrm{\Pi}}
the transition loss of the hierarchical model
\mathcal{H}
operating under
\langle\tau,\mathrm{\Pi}\rangle
.
Then, in the worst case,
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{\tau,\mathrm{\Pi}}
and
L_{\mathbf{P}}^{R,d}
might be completely unrelated whatever the room
R
and direction
d
.
To see why, recall that transition losses are defined over stationary distributions of the respective models (Eq.
2
). One can see this shift as a perturbation in the transition function of the rooms. Intuitively, by Assumption
1
, each room is almost surely entered infinitely often, meaning that such perturbations are also repeated infinitely often, possibly leading to completely divergent stationary distributions
[
45
]
, meaning that we loose the abstraction quality guarantees possibly obtained for each individual training room.

Fortunately, we claim that under some assumptions, when the initial distribution of each training room

is wisely chosen, we can still link the transition losses

minimized in the training rooms to

.

we define the entrance loss as

The high-level objective is to reach a global position within the grid while keeping the life points strictly positive.
Episodes start from an initial grid position and conclude when the agent reaches the target or runs out of life points.
The state space of the environment comprises two components:

a set of

3

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)
of the room
R

(ii)

step, power-up, and life-point counters
\langle a,b,c\rangle

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we demonstrated how to integrate DRL with reactive synthesis to obtain a hierarchical controller for reach-avoid specifications.
The algorithm involves training low-level policies and an abstract model using our WAE-DQN approach. Subsequently, we compute the value of the specification for the high-level MDP, optionally estimating PAC bounds. Finally, we synthesize a high-level controller for the reach-avoid property in the resulting high-level MDP, preserving theoretical bounds.
Our experiments demonstrate the feasibility of our approach for hierarchical scenarios which are hard to handle via pure DRL.

As future work, combining the learning of all latent policies into a single training process, e.g., via goal-conditioned RL
[
9
,
19
]
or ensemble modeling
[
32
,
40
,
3
]
, instead of running one WAE-DQN agent per direction (cf. Fig.
2
), would enable learning the latent entrance function into the same procedure, fostering end-to-end learning. On the theoretical front, refining the introduced PAC bounds and developing an efficient estimation algorithm would also be valuable.
