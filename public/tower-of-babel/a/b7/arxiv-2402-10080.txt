Navigational hierarchies of regular languages
Thomas Place, Marc Zeitoun

Abstract
We study a celebrated class of regular languages: star-free languages. A long-standing goal is to classify them by the complexity of their descriptions. The most influential research effort involves concatenation hierarchies , which measure alternations between “complement” and “union plus concatenation”. We explore alternative hierarchies that also stratify star-free languages (and extensions thereof). They are built with an operator 𝒞 ↦ TL ​ ( 𝒞 ) \mathcal{C}\hskip-1.72218pt\mapsto\hskip-1.72218pt\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}) . It takes a class of languages 𝒞 \mathcal{C} as input, and produces a larger one TL ​ ( 𝒞 ) \textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}) , consisting of all languages definable in a variant of unary temporal logic, where the future/past modalities depend on 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . Level n n in the navigational hierarchy of basis 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is then constructed by applying this operator n n times to 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . As bases 𝒢 \mathcal{G} , we focus on group languages and natural extensions thereof, denoted 𝒢 + \mathcal{G}^{+} . We prove that the navigational hierarchies of bases 𝒢 \mathcal{G} and 𝒢 + \mathcal{G}^{+} are strictly intertwined and we conduct a thorough investigation of their relationships with their concatenation hierarchy counterparts. We also look at two standard problems on classes of languages: membership (decide if a language is in the class) and separation (decide, for two languages L 1 , L 2 L_{1},L_{2} , if there is a language K K in the class with L 1 ⊆ K L_{1}\subseteq K and L 2 ∩ K = ∅ L_{2}\cap K=\emptyset ). We prove that if separation is decidable for 𝒢 \mathcal{G} , then so is membership for level two in the navigational hierarchies of bases 𝒢 \mathcal{G} and 𝒢 + \mathcal{G}^{+} . We take a closer look at the trivial class ST = { ∅ , A ∗ } \textup{ST}=\{\emptyset,A^{*}\} . For the bases ST and ST + \textup{ST}^{+} , the levels one are the standard variants of unary temporal logic. The levels two correspond to variants of two-variable logic, investigated recently by Krebs, Lodaya, Pandya and Straubing. We solve one of their conjectures. We also prove that for these two bases, level two has decidable separation . Combined with earlier results on the operator 𝒞 ↦ TL ​ ( 𝒞 ) \mathcal{C}\!\mapsto\!\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}) , this implies that level three has decidable membership.



I
Introduction

Context.

This paper addresses a fundamental question in automata theory: understanding specific formalisms used to describe regular languages. A prime example is the class of
star-free
languages, which are defined by regular expressions that exclude Kleene star but allow complement. This class is remarkable for its robustness. McNaughton and Papert
[
23
]
showed that star-free languages are precisely those expressible in first-order logic
\textup{FO}(<)
, and Kamp
[
13
]
proved that
\textup{FO}(<)
matches the expressiveness of linear time temporal logic
LTL
. Additionally, Schützenberger
[
40
]
showed that
membership
in this class is decidable: given a regular language, one can decide whether it is star-free. This led Brzozowski and Cohen
[
4
]
to classify star-free languages according to the difficulty of expressing them, resulting in the
dot-depth hierarchy
.

The dot-depth is a
concatenation hierarchy
. Such a hierarchy consists of an increasing sequence of classes of languages, called its
levels
. It is fully determined by its initial level, called its
basis
. Once the basis is fixed, the construction process is uniform, and made through the operator
BPol
. For a class
\mathcal{D}
, the class
\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{D})
consists of all languages
L_{0}a_{1}L_{1}\cdots a_{n}L_{n}
, where the
L_{i}
’s are in
\mathcal{D}
and the
a_{i}
’s are letters. Then, the class
\textup{BPol}(\mathcal{D})
is the least Boolean algebra containing
\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{D})
.

Level
zero
in the concatenation hierarchy of basis
\mathcal{C}
is
\textup{BPol}_{0}(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}
. Level
n+1
is
\textup{BPol}_{n+1}(\mathcal{C})=\textup{BPol}(\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathcal{C}))
,
i.e.
, it is obtained by applying the “
BPol
” operator to level
n
. Therefore, the union of all levels, called the
star-free closure

\mathit{\textup{SF}}(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
, is stratified by such a concatenation hierarchy. The dot-depth starts from the basis
\textup{DD}=\{\emptyset,\{\varepsilon\},A^{+},A^{*}\}
, where
A
is the alphabet. Brzozowski and Knast
[
5
]
showed that it is strict:
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\textup{DD})\subsetneq\textup{BPol}_{n+1}(\textup{DD})
. Other standard hierarchies include the one of Straubing and Thérien
[
43
,
45
]
of basis
\textup{ST}=\{\emptyset,A^{*}\}
, the group hierarchy
[
22
]
whose basis

consists of languages recognized by a permutation automaton
[
47
]
, and the modulo hierarchy
[
7
]
whose basis
\textup{MOD}\subseteq\textup{GR}
consists of languages for which membership of a word is determined by its length modulo an integer.

Individual levels of concatenation hierarchies are themselves robust: level
n
corresponds to the class
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{n}
of all languages expressible in first-order logic with
n
blocks of quantifiers
\exists^{*}/\forall^{*}
, under a tailored signature constructed from the basis
[
31
]
. This was first discovered by Thomas
[
48
]
for the dot-depth. In this context, group languages are relevant bases, as they lead to natural signatures. For example, the class
MOD
yields predicates testing the value of a position modulo a fixed integer. Interesting bases are also obtained by extending such classes
\mathcal{G}
into larger ones, denoted
\mathcal{G}^{+}
, obtained by adding or deleting the empty word to the languages of
\mathcal{G}
. On the logical side, this adds the successor relation
[
27
,
36
]
.

This motivates to study membership for
individual levels
of concatenation hierarchies. However, this is challenging. For instance, in the dot-depth and Straubing-Thérien hierarchies, decades of research have shown decidable membership only for levels
one

[
42
,
14
]
,

[
32
]
and

[

-separation

containing the first and disjoint from the second). Typically, solving such problems is harder than solving
\mathcal{C}
-membership. However, they lead to

. For instance, the decidability of
\mathcal{C}
-separation implies the decidability of
\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{C})
-membership
[
31
]
. While a similar result exists for
\textup{BPol}(\mathcal{C})

[
38
]
, it requires going beyond separation, and is, above all, significantly more complex.

Navigational hierarchies.
In this paper, we adopt a different point of view: to get insight on concatenation hierarchies, we study an alternate, yet closely related classification of the star-free closure

of a class

,

,

,
8
,
15

17

with future/past modalities built from

\mathcal{C}
gives rise to a future modality

and a past one

generalizing the classic ones

and

\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C})
-membership reduces to
\mathcal{C}
-separation
[

]

One denotes level

in the navigational hierarchy of

Level one in the navigational hierarchy of basis

is the standard class of languages definable in

is also standard: it is the class of languages definable in
unary temporal logic

,

,

(“next”) and

(“yesterday”) modalities. Finally, level two in the navigational hierarchy of basis ST corresponds to a class recently investigated in
[

,
16
,
15

Contributions.

We carry out a general study of navigational hierarchies. We first briefly compare them with concatenation hierarchies: we show that
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\textup{TL}_{n}(\mathcal{C})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
for any class
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, as announced above, we prove that both kinds of hierarchies classify the star-free closure of
\mathcal{C}
.

The remainder of the paper focuses on specific bases of the form
\mathcal{G}
or
\mathcal{G}^{+}
, where
\mathcal{G}
represents a class of group languages (using the basis
\mathcal{G}^{+}
instead of

boils down to adding the standard “next” and “yesterday” modalities). Group languages are those recognized by a complete, deterministic and co-deterministic automaton
[
47
]

\emptyset

A^{*}
, then each level of the concatenation or navigational hierarchy of basis

is a subclass of

We first prove that for

\mathcal{G}
, the navigational hierarchies of bases
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{+}
are both strict and that they are strictly intertwined (if the alphabet has at least two letters). As a byproduct, we reprove the same property for concatenation hierarchies. We next sharpen the comparison between both types of hierarchies: we show that if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{G}
or
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{G}^{+}
, the class
\textup{TL}_{2}(\mathcal{C})
is incomparable with any level above three of the concatenation hierarchy of basis

. The situation is depicted in Fig.

L
is not in level

of a hierarchy, it suffices to exhibit a disjoint language

cannot be separated by this level. This approach significantly simplifies the inductive construction of languages outside each level.

In the second part of the paper, we focus on the class

Connection with concatenation hierarchies

We now compare the two kinds of hierarchies. We start with a preliminary easy result.

Proposition 10
.

For every class
\mathcal{C}
,
\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C})=\textup{TL}(\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{C}))
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\textup{TL}(\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{C}))
as
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{C})
. For the converse inclusion, we show that the modalities
\textup{F}_{L}
and
\textup{P}_{L}
with

can be simulated using only modalities
\textup{F}_{K}
and
\textup{P}_{K}
with

. By symmetry, we only consider the former. For all
\varphi\in\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}]

such that for all

and

,

.

By definition of
\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{C})
,
L\in\textup{Pol}(\mathcal{C})
is built from the languages in
\mathcal{C}
using union and marked product. We perform an induction on this construction. If
L\in\mathcal{C}
, it suffices to define
\langle\varphi\rangle_{L}=\textup{F}_{L}~\varphi
, which is a
\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}]
formula. If
L=L_{0}\cup L_{1}
with

\langle\varphi\rangle_{L}=\langle\varphi\rangle_{L_{0}}\vee\langle\varphi\rangle_{L_{1}}
. Finally, if

with

and

, we let
\langle\varphi\rangle_{L}=\langle a\wedge\langle\psi\rangle_{L_{1}}\rangle_{L_{0}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

V
Group bases

In the literature, concatenation hierarchies use a few specific bases consisting solely of group languages, which we also adopt for navigational hierarchies. In this section, we introduce and motivate such “group bases”. We prove that for the resulting hierarchies, membership at level
two
reduces to separation for the basis, show that the navigational hierarchies are strict, and compare them to concatenation hierarchies.

V-A

Definitions

Group languages
are those that can be recognized by a morphism into a finite
group
. A
group prevariety
is a prevariety
\mathcal{G}
consisting of group languages only. We also look at a slight extension: for each class
\mathcal{G}
, we let
\mathcal{G}^{+}
be the class consisting of all languages
\{\varepsilon\}\cup L
and
A^{+}\cap L
for

. One can verify that if

is a prevariety, then so is

.

The simplest group prevariety is
\textup{ST}=\{\emptyset,A^{*}\}
. We let

[
43
,
45
]
and the dot-depth
[
4
]
. They both classify the original class

of star-free languages
[
40
]

[

VI
Rating maps

We use the framework of
[
30
]
to handle separation and covering. It relies on objects called
rating maps
. They “rate” covers. For each lattice
\mathcal{C}
, each rating map
\rho
and each language
L
, we define the optimal
\mathcal{C}
-covers of
L
for
\rho
. We reduce
\mathcal{C}
-covering to the computation of optimal
\mathcal{C}
-covers.

VI-A

Multiplicative rating maps

A
semiring
is a tuple
(R,+,\cdot)
where
R
is a set and “
+
” and “
\cdot
” are two binary operations called addition and multiplication, which satisfy the following axioms:

•

(R,{+})
is a commutative monoid. Its identity is denoted by
0_{R}
.

•

(R,{\cdot})
is a monoid. Its identity is denoted by
1_{R}
.

•

Distributivity: for
r,s,t\in R
,
r\cdot(s+t)=(r\cdot s)+(r\cdot t)
and
(r+s)\cdot t=(r\cdot t)+(s\cdot t)
.

•

0_{R}
is a zero for
(R,{\cdot})
:
0_{R}\cdot r=r\cdot 0_{R}=0_{R}
for all
r\in R
.

A semiring
R
is
idempotent
when
r+r=r
for all
r\in R
(there is no additional constraint on the multiplication). If
(R,+,\cdot)
is an idempotent semiring, we define a relation
\leq
over
R
: for all
r,s\in R
, we let
r\leq s
if and only if
r+s=s
. One can check that
\leq
is a partial order compatible with addition and multiplication and that every morphism between two idempotent semirings is increasing for this ordering.

1
.

A key example is the set of all languages
(2^{A^{*}},\cup,\cdot)
, with union as addition and language concatenation as multiplication (
\{\varepsilon\}
is neutral). The ordering is inclusion.

When dealing with subsets of an idempotent semiring
R
, we shall often apply a
downset operation
. Given
S\subseteq R
, we write
\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S=\{r\in R\mid r\leq s\text{ for some $s\in S$}\}
. We extend this notation to Cartesian products of arbitrary sets with
R
. Given some set
X
and
S\subseteq X\times R
, we write
\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S=\{(x,r)\in X\times R\mid\text{$\exists s\in R$ s.t. $r\leq s$ and $(x,s)\in S$}\}
.

Multiplicative rating maps.
We define a
multiplicative rating map
as a semiring morphism
\rho:(2^{A^{*}},\cup,\cdot)\to(R,+,\cdot)
where
(R,+,\cdot)
is a
finite
idempotent semiring. That is,

1.

\rho(\emptyset)=0_{R}
and for every
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\rho(K_{1}\cup K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})+\rho(K_{2})
.

2.

\rho(\{\varepsilon\})=1_{R}
and for every
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
, we have
\rho(K_{1}K_{2})=\rho(K_{1})\cdot\rho(K_{2})
.

For the sake of improved readability, for all words
w\in A^{*}
, we write
\rho(w)
for
\rho(\{w\})
. Also, we write
\rho_{*}:A^{*}\to R
for the restriction of
\rho
to
A^{*}
: for all
w\in A^{*}
, we have
\rho_{*}(w)=\rho(w)
(this notation is useful when referring to the language
\rho_{*}^{-1}(r)\subseteq A^{*}
consisting of all
words

w\in A^{*}
such that
\rho(w)=r
). It is a morphism into the finite monoid
(R,\cdot)
.

7
.

As the adjective “multiplicative” suggests, there exists a more general notion of “rating map”. We do not use this notion, see
[
30
]
for the general framework.

Now, a
full rating map
is a multiplicative rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
such that for every language
K\subseteq A^{*}
, there exist finitely many words
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in K
such that
\rho(K)=\rho(w_{1})+\cdots+\rho(w_{k})
. In this case,
\rho
is fully defined by the morphism
\rho_{*}:A^{*}\to R
. Indeed, for each
K
,
\rho(K)
is equal to the sum of all elements
\rho(w)
for
w\in K
(although this sum may be infinite, it simplifies to a finite one because addition is commutative and idempotent). Hence, full rating maps are finitely representable. In particular, we can discuss algorithms that take full rating maps as input.

Optimal imprints.
Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a multiplicative rating map. Given a finite set of languages
\mathbf{K}
, the
\rho
-imprint of
\mathbf{K}

is the subset of
R
defined by
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})=\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}\big\{\rho(K)\mid K\in\mathbf{K}\big\}
. Intuitively, when
\mathbf{K}
is a cover of some language
L
, this object measures the “quality” of
\mathbf{K}
(smaller is better).

We now define optimality. Consider a lattice
\mathcal{C}
. For a language
L
, an
optimal

\mathcal{C}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
is a
\mathcal{C}
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
with the least possible
\rho
-imprint,
i.e.
, such that
\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})\subseteq\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K}^{\prime})
for every
\mathcal{C}
-cover
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
of
L
. In general, there may be infinitely many optimal
\mathcal{C}
-covers for a given multiplicative rating map
\rho
. However, at least one always exists if
\mathcal{C}
is a lattice. We state this property in the following lemma (proved in
[
30
, Lemma 4.15]
).

Lemma 30
.

Given a lattice
\mathcal{C}
, a multiplicative rating map
\rho
and a language
L
, there exists an optimal
\mathcal{C}
-cover of
L
for
\rho
.

By definition, given a lattice
\mathcal{C}
, a multiplicative rating map
\rho
and a language
L
, all optimal
\mathcal{C}
-covers of
L
for
\rho
have the same
\rho
-imprint. Hence, this unique
\rho
-imprint is a
canonical
object for
\mathcal{C}
,
\rho
and
L
. We call it the
\mathcal{C}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint on
L
and we denote it by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[L,\rho\right]
. That is,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[L,\rho\right]=\mathcal{I}[\rho](\mathbf{K})
for every optimal
\mathcal{C}
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L
for
\rho
.

VI-B

Application to covering

Deciding
\mathcal{C}
-covering for a fixed class
\mathcal{C}
reduces to finding an algorithm that computes
\mathcal{C}
-optimal imprints. If
\mathcal{C}
is a
Boolean algebra
, we have the following statement of
[
30
]
.

Proposition 31
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Boolean algebra. There exists an effective reduction from
\mathcal{C}
-covering to the following problem: “given as input a full rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
and
F\subseteq R
, decide whether
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]\cap F=\emptyset
”.

Proof.

We sketch the reduction (see
[
30
]
for details). Let
(L_{0},\{L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\})
be an input of the
\mathcal{C}
-covering problem. One can compute a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
recognizing all
regular
languages
L_{i}
: for each
i\leq n
, there exists
F_{i}\subseteq M
such that
L_{i}=\alpha^{-1}(F_{i})
. We build a full rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to 2^{M}
. Its rating set is the idempotent semiring
(2^{M},\cup,\cdot)
whose multiplication is obtained by lifting the one of
M
. For all
K\subseteq A^{*}
, we define
\rho(K)=\alpha(K)
. Now, let
F\subseteq 2^{M}
be the set of all subsets
X\subseteq M
such
that
X\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all

. Using the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is a Boolean algebra, one can check that
(L_{0},\{L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\})
is
\mathcal{C}
-coverable if and only if
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]\cap F=\emptyset
.
∎

The converse is also true: if
\mathcal{C}
-covering is decidable, then one can compute the
\mathcal{C}
-optimal imprints (see Appendix
E
).

Proposition 32
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Boolean algebra with decidable covering. Given as input a regular language
L
and a full rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
, one can compute the set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[L,\rho\right]
.

Pointed optimal imprints.
By Proposition
31
, an algorithm computing
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]
from a full rating map
\rho
yields a procedure for
\mathcal{C}
-covering. In Section
VII
, we consider
\mathcal{C}=\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
. The procedure itself involves an object carrying
more information
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Boolean algebra,
\eta:A^{*}\to N
be a morphism into a finite monoid and
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a multiplicative rating map. The
\eta
-pointed
\mathcal{C}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}[\eta,\rho]=\bigl\{(t,r)\in N\times R\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\eta^{-1}(t),\rho\right]\bigl\}.

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}[\eta,\rho]\subseteq N\times R
. It will be convenient to use the a functional notation here: given a subset
S\subseteq N\times R
and

for the set
S(t)=\{r\in R\mid(t,r)\in S\}
.

The set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}[\eta,\rho]
encodes the
\mathcal{C}
-optimal
\rho
-imprint on
A^{*}
,
i.e.
, the subset
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]
of
R
. Indeed, the following easy lemma is an immediate corollary of
[
30
, Fact 4.7]
.

Lemma 33
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Boolean algebra,
\eta:A^{*}\to N
be a morphism and

.

Our goal is to compute
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})}[\eta_{\textup{AT}},\rho]\subseteq 2^{A}\times R
, in order to decide
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-covering. Recall that
\eta_{\textup{AT}}:A^{*}\to 2^{A}
is the canonical
AT
-morphism recognizing
all
languages of

.

VII
Optimal imprints for
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})

We turn to our main theorem. It characterizes the
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-optimal imprints. The characterization yields a least fixpoint algorithm for computing the set
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})}[\eta_{\textup{AT}},\rho]
from a full rating map
\rho
. By Proposition
31
and Lemma
33
, this yields the decidability of
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-covering. Note that the algorithm uses a subprocedure from
[
36
,
34
]
deciding
covering
for the simpler class
\textup{TLX}=\textup{TL}(\textup{DD})
, which is a nontrivial question.

8
.

Considering
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-covering is mandatory even if one is solely interested in separation, as mentioned in the introduction. There does not appear to be a simpler, specialized algorithm for
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-separation. This is unsurprising, as performing a fixpoint computation often requires extra information (see, for example the case of star-free languages
[
29
]
). Finally, the framework itself seems indispensable: there is no clear way to formulate
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-operation without rating maps.

Preliminaries.
We use a construction taking as input an idempotent semiring
R
and an arbitrary subset
Q\subseteq R
to build a full rating map
\pi_{R,Q}:2^{Q^{*}}\to R
on the alphabet
Q
.

9
.

Given
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\in Q
, the notation
q_{1}\cdots q_{n}
is ambiguous: it could either denote a word of length
n
in
Q^{*}
or the multiplication of
n
elements in
Q
. For the sake of avoiding confusion, given an element
q\in Q
, we shall write
(q)
when we use it as a letter: we denote by
(q_{1})(q_{2})\cdots(q_{n})\in Q^{n}
the word made of the
n

q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\in Q
.

Since we are defining a full rating map, it suffices to specify the image of single letters
(q)\in Q
. Since
Q\subseteq R
, we simply define
\pi_{R,Q}((q))=q
for all
q\in Q
. Our characterization uses
TLX
-optimal
\pi_{R,Q}
-imprints (see
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-operation below).

Characterization.
Consider a full rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. We define the
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-saturated subsets of
2^{A}\times R
for
\rho
. We shall prove that
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})}[\eta_{\textup{AT}},\rho]
is the least such set (with respect to inclusion). Let
S\subseteq 2^{A}\times R
. We say that
S
is
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-saturated
for
\rho
when the following conditions hold:

1.

Trivial elements.

(\eta_{\textup{AT}}(w),\rho(w))\in S
for all
w\in A^{*}
.

2.

Downset.
We have
\mathord{\downarrow_{R}}S=S
.

3.

Multiplication.
For every
(B_{1},r_{1}),(B_{2},r_{2})\in S
, we have
(B_{1}\cup B_{2},r_{1}r_{2})\in S
.

4.

\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-operation.
For all
B\in 2^{A}
and

, we have
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{TLX}}\left[Q^{+},\pi_{R,Q}\right]\subseteq S(B)
.

Theorem 34
.

Let
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
be a full rating map, Then,
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})}[\eta_{\textup{AT}},\rho]
is the least
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-saturated subset of

.

Theorem
34
provides an algorithm computing the set
\mathcal{P}_{\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})}[\eta_{\textup{AT}},\rho]
from an input full rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Indeed, the least
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-saturated subset of
2^{A}\times R
for
\rho
can be computed using a least fixpoint procedure. It starts from the set of trivial elements and saturates it with the three operations in the definition. Clearly, downset and multiplication can be implemented. For
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
-operation, one needs to compute the set
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{TLX}}\left[Q^{+},\pi_{R,Q}\right]
from the full rating map
\pi_{R,Q}
. In view of Proposition
32
, this boils down to deciding

-covering and it is shown in
[
36
,
34
]
that this problem is decidable.

In view of Lemma
33
, this yields a procedure that computes
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})}\left[A^{*},\rho\right]\!\subseteq\!R
from an input full rating map
\rho:2^{A^{*}}\to R
. Thus,
\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
has decidable covering by Proposition

.

Corollary 35
.

Separation and covering are decidable for the class
\textup{TL}_{2}(\textup{ST})=\textup{TL}(\textup{AT})
.

Corollary

also implies the decidability of separation and covering for the class
\textup{TL}(\textup{LT})

that the classes

with

.

10
.

Corollary 36

Separation and covering are decidable for the class

VIII
Conclusion

We defined navigational hierarchies using the “unary temporal logic” operator
\mathcal{C}\mapsto\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C})
. We focused on bases of the form
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{G}
or
\mathcal{G}^{+}
, where
\mathcal{G}
is a group prevariety. In this context, we proved that the navigational hierarchies of basis
\mathcal{C}
are
strict
and
strictly intertwined
, and we compared them with the associated concatenation hierarchies. Additionaly, we proved that if
\mathcal{G}
has decidable
separation
, then membership is decidable for levels
one
and
two
in both hierarchies. Our main theorem provides a stronger result for the particular bases
ST
and
\textup{DD}=\textup{ST}^{+}
. Specifically, it implies that level
two
in both hierarchies has decidable
covering
, which in turn ensures that level
three
has decidable membership.

Several follow-up questions arise. A natural one is whether our results can be extended to higher levels in navigational hierarchies. Addressing this would require studying the operator
\mathcal{C}\mapsto\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C})
in a broader context, as our main theorem heavily relies on the specific properties of the class
AT
. Another question is to generalize the link existing between the hierarchies of bases ST and DD. As seen in Section
VII
, we have
\textup{TL}_{2}(\textup{DD})=\textup{TL}_{2}(\textup{ST})\circ\textup{SU}
and we used this connection to get the decidability of covering for
\textup{TL}_{2}(\textup{DD})
. It turns out that this can be generalized: we have
\textup{TL}_{n}(\textup{DD})=\textup{TL}_{n}(\textup{ST})\circ\textup{SU}
for all
n\geq 1
. We leave the proof for further work.

. Finally, we have

for every

. Since

, this exactly says that
\pi_{R,Q}(y)\leq\rho([w]_{\alpha})
as desired.
∎

We now prove Lemma
45
. Recall that we defined a mapping
\mu:(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{*}\to\mathbb{B}^{*}
.

See
45

Proof.

We start with a preliminary construction. Consider a letter
(\ell,U)\in\mathbb{B}=\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\times\mathbf{U}_{D}
, we construct a formula
\psi_{\ell,U}\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
such that for every
w\in(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{*}
and every distinguished position
i\in\textup{{Pos}}(w)
, we have
w,i\models\psi_{\ell,U}\Leftrightarrow\mu(i)\text{ has label }(\ell,U)
. By hypothesis on
\mathbf{U}_{D}
, there exists a formula
\varphi\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
defining
U
. Using Fact
44
, we modify
\varphi
so that given
w\in(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{*}
, the evaluation of the resulting formula at a distinguished position
i
is constrained to the next maximal infix in
\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*}
. More precisely, we use structural induction to build two formulas
\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}
and
\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}
such that given
w\in(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{*}
and two distinguished positions
i,j\in\textup{{Pos}}(w)
such that
i<j
and there is no distinguished positions in-between
i
and
j
, the two following properties hold for every
k\in\textup{{Pos}}(w(i,j))
:

•

If
k<|w(i,j)|+1
, then,

w,i+k\models\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}\Leftrightarrow w(i,j),k\models\varphi.

•

If
1<k
, then,

w,i+k\models\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}\Leftrightarrow w(i,j),k\models\varphi.

It will then suffice to define
\psi_{\ell,U}=\ell\wedge\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}
. We only describe the construction. That it satisfies the above properties can be verified.

•

If
\varphi\in\mathbb{A}\cup\{\top,\bot\}
, we let
\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}=\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}=\varphi
.

•

If
\varphi=min
, we let
\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}=\bigvee_{\ell\in\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}}\ell
and
\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}=\bot
.

•

If
\varphi=max
, we let
\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}=\bot
and
\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}=\bigvee_{\ell\in\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}}\ell
.

Assume now that
\varphi=\textup{F}_{L}~\varphi^{\prime}
for some
L\in\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
. We let,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}&=&\textup{F}_{L\cap\beta^{-1}(\{D,\emptyset\})}~\langle\varphi^{\prime}\rangle_{max}\\
\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}&=&\left(\bigvee_{\ell\in\mathbb{A}_{D}}\ell\right)\wedge\textup{F}_{L\cap\{D,\emptyset\}}~\langle\varphi^{\prime}\rangle_{max}\end{array}

Finally, assume that
\varphi=\textup{P}_{L}~\varphi^{\prime}
for some
L\in\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
. We let,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\langle\varphi\rangle_{min}&=&\left(\bigvee_{\ell\in\mathbb{A}_{D}}\ell\right)\wedge\textup{F}_{L\cap\beta^{-1}(\{D,\emptyset\})}~\langle\varphi^{\prime}\rangle_{min}\\
\langle\varphi\rangle_{max}&=&\textup{F}_{L\cap\{D,\emptyset\}}~\langle\varphi^{\prime}\rangle_{min}\end{array}

This completes the construction.

We are ready to prove the lemma. Let
K\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\gamma})
. By hypothesis, there exists a formula
\Gamma\in\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}]
defining
K
. Using induction on
\Gamma
, we define
\lfloor\Gamma\rfloor\in\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}_{\beta}]
such that for every
w\in(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{*}
and distinguished position
i\in\textup{{Pos}}(w)
, we have
w,i\models\lfloor\Gamma\rfloor\Leftrightarrow\mu(w),\mu(i)\models\Gamma
. Since
(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{*}=\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}^{*}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
, it will follow that
\mu^{-1}(K)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{*}
is defined by the formula,

\textup{F}_{\{\varepsilon\}}~\Biggl(max\vee\bigvee_{\ell\in\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}}\ell\Biggr)\wedge\lfloor\Gamma\rfloor\in\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}_{\beta}]

This yields
\mu^{-1}(K)\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
which completes the proof (note that here
\{\varepsilon\}\in\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
since
\{\varepsilon\}=\beta^{-1}(\emptyset)
because
(\mathbb{A},\beta,\tau)
is a good triple).

If
\Gamma\in\{min,max,\top,\bot\}
, we define
\lfloor\Gamma\rfloor=\Gamma
. Assume now that
\Gamma=(\ell,U)\in\mathbb{B}
. We let
\lfloor\Gamma\rfloor=\psi_{\ell,U}
. It remains to handle the temporal modalities: there exists

such that
\Gamma=\textup{F}_{L}~\Gamma^{\prime}
or
\Gamma=\textup{P}_{L}~\Gamma^{\prime}

is recognized by

. We let

. Abusing terminology, we also write “
\mathbb{A}_{D}
” and “

” for the formulas “

\Gamma=\textup{F}_{L}~\Gamma^{\prime}

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\left((\textup{F}_{\{\varepsilon\}}~\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}})\wedge\textup{F}_{H}~\lfloor\Gamma^{\prime}\rfloor\right)\\

Finally, we prove Lemma
46
. Recall that an alphabet
\mathbb{A}
is fixed together with two subalphabets
\mathbb{A}_{D}
and
\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}
such that
\mathbb{A}_{D}\cap\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}=\emptyset

\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
is defined over the alphabet

.

See

Proof.

First, observe that we may assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon\not\in V
. Indeed, if we have
\varepsilon\in V
, then
UV=U\cup U(V\cap\mathbb{A}^{+})
. Thus, since we already know that
U\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
, it suffices to show that
U(V\cap\mathbb{A}^{+})\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
. Since it is clear that
(V\cap\mathbb{A}^{+})\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
, this boils down to the special case when
\varepsilon\not\in V
.

We assume from now on that
\varepsilon\not\in V
. Hence, we have
V\subseteq(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{+}
(in particular, every word in
V
begins by a letter in
\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}
). Since
U\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*}
,
V\subseteq(\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}\mathbb{A}_{D}^{*})^{+}
and
\mathbb{A}_{D}\cap\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}=\emptyset
, observe that a word
w\in\mathbb{A}^{*}
belongs to
UV
if and only if there exists
b\in\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}
such that it satisfies the following three properties:

1.

w
admits a decomposition
w=ubv
such that the prefix
u
belongs to
(\mathbb{A}\setminus\{b\})^{*}
(
i.e.
, this decomposition is unique as it showcases the leftmost occurrence of a letter in
\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}
inside
w
).

2.

The prefix
u
of
w
belongs to
U
.

3.

The suffix
v
of
w
belongs to
b^{-1}V
.

Thus, it suffices to prove that these three properties can be expressed with
\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}_{\beta}]
formulas. We start by presenting a preliminary formulas that we shall need. It pinpoints the leftmost occurrence of the letter
b\in\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}
inside a word:

\psi_{b}=b\wedge\neg\textup{P}~b.

The first property above is now expressed by the formula
\textup{F}~\psi_{b}
. It remains to prove that the other two properties can be defined as well. Let us start with the second one. By hypothesis,
U\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
is defined by a formula
\varphi_{U}\in\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}_{\beta}]
. We modify it to construct a formula
\varphi^{\prime}_{U}
. It is obtained from
\varphi_{U}
by restricting its evaluation to positions that are smaller than the leftmost one labeled by a “
b
”. More precisely, we build
\varphi^{\prime}_{U}
by applying the following modifications to
\varphi_{U}
:

1.

We recursively replace every subformula of the form “
\textup{F}_{L}~\zeta
” by “
\textup{F}_{L}~(\zeta\wedge(\psi_{b}\vee\textup{F}~\psi_{b}))
”.

2.

We replace every atomic subformula of the form “
max
” by
\psi_{b}
.

3.

We replace every atomic subformula of the form “
a
” for
a\in\mathbb{A}
by
a\wedge\neg\psi_{b}
.

We are ready to construct a formula for the third property. Since
V\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
, it follows from Proposition
9
that
b^{-1}V\in\textup{TL}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta})
. Hence, we get a formula
\varphi_{b,V}\in\textup{TL}[\mathcal{C}_{\beta}]
defining
b^{-1}V
. We now construct a formula
\varphi^{\prime}_{b,V}
from
\varphi_{b,V}
by restricting its evaluation to positions that are larger than the leftmost one labeled by a “
b
”. More precisely, we build
\varphi^{\prime}_{b,V}

\varphi_{b,V}

1.

We recursively replace every subformula “
\textup{P}_{L}~\zeta

\textup{P}_{L}~(\zeta\wedge(\psi_{b}\vee\textup{P}~\psi_{b}))

2.

We replace every atomic subformula of the form “
min

\psi_{b}
.

3.

We replace every atomic subformula of the form “

” for

The language

\bigvee_{b\in\overline{\mathbb{A}_{D}}}\left(\textup{F}~\left(\psi_{b}\wedge\varphi^{\prime}_{b,V}\right)\wedge\varphi^{\prime}_{U}\right).
