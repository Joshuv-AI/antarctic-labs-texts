Abstract
. We propose a new approach to deriving quantitative mean field approximations for any probability measure P P on ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} with density proportional to e f ⁡ ( x ) e^{f(x)} , for f f strongly concave. We bound the mean field approximation for the log partition function log ∫ e f ⁡ ( x ) d x \log\int e^{f(x)}dx in terms of ∑ i ≠ j 𝔼 Q ∗ ​ | ∂ i ​ j f | 2 \sum_{i\neq j}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}|\partial_{ij}f|^{2} , for a semi-explicit probability measure Q ∗ Q^{*} characterized as the unique mean field optimizer, or equivalently as the minimizer of the relative entropy H ( ⋅ | P ) H(\cdot\,|\,P) over product measures. This notably does not involve metric-entropy or gradient-complexity concepts which are common in prior work on nonlinear large deviations. Three implications are discussed, in the contexts of continuous Gibbs measures on large graphs, high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression, and the construction of decentralized near-optimizers in high-dimensional stochastic control problems. Our arguments are based primarily on functional inequalities and the notion of displacement convexity from optimal transport. Keywords: Mean field approximation, log-concavity, Gibbs measures, Bayesian regression, mean field control, log-Sobolev inequality, Poincaré inequality, displacement convexity AMS MSC 2020: 60F10; 39B62



1.
Introduction

At the center of the recent theory of
nonlinear large deviations
is the problem of justifying the
mean field approximation
for the partition function of a Gibbs measure. Given a (reference) probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, suppose a probability measure
P
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
takes the form

\displaystyle P(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{f(x)}\mu^{\otimes n}(dx),

for a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and normalizing constant
Z
, where
\mu^{\otimes n}
denotes the
n
-fold product measure. A recurring problem in diverse applications is the approximation of the often intractable
partition function

Z
. It obeys the well-known Gibbs variational principle

(1.1)

\displaystyle\log Z=\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}\,d\mu^{\otimes n}=\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q\,|\,\mu^{\otimes n})\right),

where
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is the set of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
H
denotes the relative entropy

\displaystyle H(Q\,|\,Q^{\prime})\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{dQ}{dQ^{\prime}}\log\frac{dQ}{dQ^{\prime}}\,dQ^{\prime}\ \text{ if }Q\ll Q^{\prime},\qquad H(Q\,|\,Q^{\prime})\coloneqq\infty\ \text{ if }Q\not\ll Q^{\prime}.

Note that
Q=P
is the unique optimizer in (
1.1
).
Letting
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
denote the set of product measures
Q=Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n}
in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the
mean field approximation
is

(1.2)

\displaystyle\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}\,d\mu^{\otimes n}\approx\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q\,|\,\mu^{\otimes n})\right).

In the cases studied in this paper, the left-hand side is expected to be of order
n
; a precise formulation of (
1.2
) is then to find conditions under which the difference is
o(n)
, so that the mean field approximation becomes asymptotically correct at the leading order. Note that the right-hand side of (
1.2
) is trivially a lower bound for the left, because of (
1.1
), and it is only the upper bound that incurs an error which must be estimated.

The groundbreaking work of
[
22
]
, motivated by applications to subgraph counts in sparse random graphs, showed how to justify the mean field approximation in the case that
\mu^{\otimes n}
is the uniform measure on the hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. Their key assumption is that the gradient of
f
has
low complexity
, as measured by the metric entropy of the range
\nabla f(\{-1,1\}^{n})
. A number of subsequent papers have since refined this approach and results on subgraph counts
[
23
,
24
,
44
]
, in addition to other noteworthy applications such as Ising models
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
26
,
33
,
39
]
.
Most applications thus far involve discrete
\mu
, but the theory has been extended to compactly supported measures
[
2
,
4
,
53
]
.
Alternative and often more convenient estimates have appeared, still based on “gradient complexity” but quantifying it in a different way, eschewing covering number estimates in favor of the simpler and weaker Gaussian-width
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
or Rademacher-width
[
3
]
.

In this paper, we propose an alternative approach to the mean field approximation, designed most notably for the case where
f
is concave and the reference measure
\mu
is strongly log-concave (see Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.4
).
In particular, we deal with continuous
\mu
of unbounded support, which covers a rather different host of applications, discussed in Section
2
, compared to the somewhat more discrete-oriented prior literature.
Our approach is based on a semi-explicit representation for the mean field optimizer
Q^{*}
in (
1.2
), which we show to be unique as soon as
P
is strictly log-concave, and which is in fact also the unique minimizer of
H(\cdot\,|\,P)
over product measures.
We control the error in the approximation (
1.2
) by a constant times
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\sum_{i\neq j}|\partial_{ij}f|^{2}
, which is typically much simpler to work with compared to the aforementioned notions of gradient complexity.
Eldan
[
33
,
34
]
and Austin
[
4
]
also analyze the mean field approximation by approximating
P
by product measures in entropy, but our methods and bounds are very different from theirs; notably, they approximate
P
not by a single product measure but by a mixture, which is natural when the mean field optimizer is not unique, as is explained well in
[
33
]
. The uniqueness of the mean field optimizer in our setting means that we expect
P
to concentrate around a single
pure state
, rather than a mixture of states.

In the rest of this section, we describe our general results on mean field approximations for log-concave measures,
along with some related ideas and generalizations,
with proofs deferred to Section
3
.
Section
2
develops three applications: Gibbs measures with heterogeneous interactions, high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression, and high-dimensional stochastic control problems.

1.1.
Main results

Recall for
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
that a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
is said to be
\kappa
-concave
if
x\mapsto f(x)+\frac{\kappa}{2}|x|^{2}
is concave.
If
f
is finite-valued and
C^{2}
, i.e., twice continuously differentiable, then
f
is
\kappa
-concave if and only if
\nabla^{2}f(x)\leq-\kappa I
in semidefinite order, for each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We say that a probability measure
P
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
\kappa
-log-concave if it takes the form
P(dx)=e^{f(x)}dx
for some
\kappa
-concave function
f
.
We will work with the (negative of the) differential entropy

H(Q):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}Q(x)\log Q(x)\,dx,

for an absolutely continuous probability measure
Q(dx)=Q(x)dx
on a Euclidean space, well-defined in
(-\infty,\infty]
whenever the negative part of
Q\log Q
is integrable; we adopt the convention that
H(Q)=\infty
if
Q
is not absolutely continuous, or if
(Q\log Q)^{-}
is not integrable.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
denote the set of product measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}):{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
denote the identity map, so that we may write
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}[g(X)]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g\,dQ
for the expectation under
Q
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider a
C^{2}
and
\kappa
-log-concave probability measure
P(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{f(x)}dx
, for some
\kappa>0
. Assume there exist
c_{1}\geq 0
and
0\leq c_{2}<\kappa/2
such that
|f(x)|\leq c_{1}e^{c_{2}|x|^{2}}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then the following conclusions hold:

(1)

There exists a unique product measure
Q^{*}=Q^{*}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{*}_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with strictly positive density a.e. satisfying
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
and the fixed point equation

(1.3)

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{i}(dx_{i})=Z_{i}^{-1}\exp\big({\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[f(X)\,|\,X_{i}=x_{i}]\big)\,dx_{i},\qquad Z_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n.

(2)

Q^{*}
is
\kappa
-log-concave.

(3)

Q^{*}
is the unique optimizer in

(1.4)

\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\right).

(4)

If we define

R_{f}:=\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f(x)}\,dx-\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\right),

then

(1.5)

0\leq R_{f}\leq\frac{1}{2\kappa}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathrm{Var}}_{Q^{*}}(\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[|\partial_{ij}f(X)|^{2}].

The supremum in (
1.4
) is finite, as we will see in Lemma
3.4
. Also, as will be seen in the proof of Proposition
3.9
, our assumptions ensure that
\partial_{i}f(x_{i},\cdot)\in L^{1}\big(\prod_{j\neq i}Q^{*}_{j}\big)
, so the conditional variance in (
1.5
) is well-defined in
[0,\infty]
. The final quantity in (
1.5
) controlling our mean field approximation error involves only the cross-derivatives
i\neq j
, which are insensitive to additively separable perturbations
f(x)\to f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{f}_{i}(x_{i})
. On the other hand, the measure
Q^{*}
is sensitive to these perturbations, but in the tractable sense that
Q^{*}_{i}(dx_{i})
must be multiplied by
\exp\tilde{f}_{i}(x_{i})
(and a new normalizing constant). In particular, both upper bounds in (
1.5
) vanish if
f
is already additively separable, i.e., if
P
is a product measure.

In Theorem
1.1
, the measure
Q^{*}
is defined implicitly, which can make bounding
R_{f}
difficult.
In the simplest case where
\nabla^{2}f
is bounded, we need no knowledge of
Q^{*}
to obtain

\displaystyle R_{f}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\partial_{ij}f(x)|^{2},

which is sharp enough for many applications.
But even when
\nabla^{2}f
is unbounded, we can take advantage of the fact that
Q^{*}
is
\kappa
-log-concave by Theorem
1.1
(2), which implies in particular that it has finite moments of all orders controlled in terms of
\kappa
.

A guiding example is the class of Gibbs measures with pairwise interactions of the form

(1.6)

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}J_{ij}K(x_{i}-x_{j}),

where
V
is
\kappa
-concave,
K
is even and concave, and
J
is a symmetric matrix with nonnegative entries. Then
\partial_{ij}f(x)=-J_{ij}K^{\prime\prime}(x_{i}-x_{j})
for
i\neq j
, and for
K^{\prime\prime}
bounded we immediately deduce
R_{f}\leq{\mathrm{Tr}}(J^{2})\|K^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}/2\kappa^{2}
from Theorem
1.1
.
Corollary
2.3
below proves a similar
O({\mathrm{Tr}}(J^{2}))
bound merely assuming that
K^{\prime\prime}
has at most exponential growth, plus a symmetry assumption.
Since
\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}dx
is order
n
in this case, we obtain a successful mean field approximation whenever
J
satisfies
{\mathrm{Tr}}(J^{2})=o(n)
, a well-established condition in the literature. We postpone to Section
2.1
further discussion of this class of examples.

As a first corollary of Theorem
1.1
, we deduce the following non-asymptotic law of large numbers for the empirical measure.

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, for any 1-Lipschitz function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

(1.7)

{\mathbb{E}}_{P}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(X_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[\varphi(X_{i})]\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\leq\frac{(1+\sqrt{2R_{f}})^{2}}{\kappa n}.

Remark 1.3
.

Corollary
1.2
can be interpreted as a form of concentration of the empirical measure
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
around the measure
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q^{*}_{i}
. Alternatively, the Poincaré inequality for
P
implies
{\mathrm{Var}}_{P}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(X_{i}))\leq 1/\kappa n
for 1-Lipschitz
\varphi
, which in turn implies a form of concentration of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
around its mean
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}
, where
P_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
marginal of
P
. However, the latter is normally not as useful, because the marginals of
P
are typically not as tractable as the various characterizations of
Q^{*}
provided by Theorem
1.1
.

It is often convenient to work with a probability measure as a reference measure, in place of Lebesgue measure, as is common in the literature on mean field approximations (see for example
[
2
,
4
,
22
,
33
,
53
]
). Theorem
1.1
implies a similar result in terms of reference probability measures.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
V_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
C^{2}
and
\kappa
-concave for some
\kappa>0
, such that
\rho_{i}(dx)=e^{V_{i}(x)}dx
is a probability measure, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Let
\rho=\rho_{1}\times\cdots\times\rho_{n}
.
Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
C^{2}
and concave. Assume there exist
c_{1}\geq 0
and
0\leq c_{2}<\kappa/2
such that
|g(x)|\leq c_{1}e^{c_{2}|x|^{2}}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then the following conclusions hold:

(1)

There exists a unique product measure
Q^{*}=Q^{*}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{*}_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with strictly positive density a.e. satisfying
g\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
and

(1.8)

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{i}(dx_{i})=Z_{i}^{-1}\exp\big({\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[g(X)\,|\,X_{i}=x_{i}]\big)\,\rho_{i}(dx_{i}),\qquad Z_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n.

(2)

Q^{*}
is
\kappa
-log-concave.

(3)

Q^{*}
is the unique optimizer in

(1.9)

\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g\,dQ-H(Q\,|\,\rho)\right).

(4)

If we define

R^{\rho}_{g}:=\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{g}\,\,d\rho-\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g\,dQ-H(Q\,|\,\rho)\right),

then

(1.10)

0\leq R^{\rho}_{g}\leq\frac{1}{2\kappa}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathrm{Var}}_{Q^{*}}(\partial_{i}g(X)\,|\,X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[|\partial_{ij}g(X)|^{2}].

For certain symmetric choices of
g
, the bound (
1.10
) is related to the theorems of Cramér and Sanov on large deviations, which are settings in which the Gibbs variational principle is well known to be nearly saturated by product measures.
For instance, if
g(x)=nG\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\big)
for some continuous concave
G
, we obtain
R^{\rho}_{g}\leq\|G^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}/2\kappa^{2}
, which is certainly
o(n)
when
G^{\prime\prime}
is bounded.

1.2.
Overview and proof ideas

We explain here some key ideas behind Theorem
1.1
and its corollaries.
The simple identity

(1.11)

\displaystyle\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f(x)}\,dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ+H(Q)=H(Q\,|\,P)

is valid for probability measures
Q
with finite entropy and implies (see Lemma
3.4
for details)

(1.12)

\displaystyle\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f(x)}\,dx-\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\right)=\inf_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}H(Q\,|\,P),

and also that optimizing (
1.4
) is equivalent to optimizing

(1.13)

\displaystyle\inf_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}H(Q\,|\,P).

That is,
Q^{*}
from Theorem
1.1
is the optimizer in (
1.13
).
This can be seen as an
entropic projection
, in the sense of Csiszar
[
25
]
, onto the set of product measures. A minimizer in (
1.13
) always exists, because the set of product measures is weakly closed and
H(\cdot\,|\,P)
has weakly compact sub-level sets. But uniqueness is not obvious and in fact fails in general, because the set of product measures is not convex.
We establish the uniqueness of the optimizer in Lemma
3.6
in the case where
P
is strictly log-concave, by exploiting the notion of displacement convexity from the theory of optimal transport, with similarities to the work of McCann
[
45
]
.

Once we know that the optimizer
Q^{*}
for (
1.4
) takes the form (
1.3
), the proof of the mean field approximation (
1.5
) is fairly quick, if we ignore certain technical points:
The right-hand side of the identity (
1.12
) is precisely
H(Q^{*}\,|\,P)
. We first use the log-Sobolev inequality for
P
, which is ensured by
\kappa
-log-concavity and the famous result of Bakry-Émery
[
5
]
, to get

\displaystyle H(Q^{*}\,|\,P)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\kappa}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|\nabla\log\frac{dQ^{*}}{dP}\right|^{2}\,dQ^{*}.

Since
Q^{*}=Q^{*}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{*}_{n}
is a product measure, the formula (
1.3
) implies

(1.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\log Q^{*}(x)=\partial_{i}\log Q^{*}_{i}(x_{i})=\partial_{i}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[f(X)\,|\,X_{i}=x_{i}]={\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i}=x_{i}].

Thus,

H(Q^{*}\,|\,P)\leq\frac{1}{2\kappa}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i}]-\partial_{i}f(X)\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2\kappa}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathrm{Var}}_{Q^{*}}(\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i}).

Differentiating (
1.14
) again shows easily that
Q^{*}
is
\kappa
-log-concave since
f
is concave. Hence,
Q^{*}
and its marginals obey a Poincaré inequality, and we deduce

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{Q^{*}}(\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\left[|\partial_{ij}f(X)|^{2}\,|\,X_{i}\right].

Combining the last two inequalities yields (
1.5
). See Section
1.3.3
below for a discussion of a generalization of this argument beyond the strongly log-concave case.

The proof of Corollary
1.2
begins with the observation that the
\kappa
-log-concavity of
P
in Theorem
1.1
implies the quadratic transport inequality
[
48
, Theorems 1 and 2]

(1.15)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(Q^{*},P)\leq\frac{2}{\kappa}H(Q^{*}\,|\,P),

where
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
denotes the quadratic Wasserstein distance defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(Q^{*},P)=\inf_{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|x-y|^{2}\,\pi(dx,dy),

where the infimum is over
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with marginals
Q^{*}
and
P
. Combining (
1.15
) with the inequality
H(Q^{*}\,|\,P)\leq R_{f}
discussed above, we arrive at
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(Q^{*},P)\leq 2R_{f}/\kappa
.
The quadratic Wasserstein distance enjoys a useful and fairly well known
subadditivity
inequality, which we prove in Section
3.4
for the sake of completeness: If

P_{S}
denotes the marginal law of
(X_{i})_{i\in S}
under
P
for a set
S\subset[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and similarly for
Q^{*}_{S}
, then we have

(1.16)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\sum_{S\subset[n],\,|S|=k}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(Q^{*}_{S},P_{S})\leq\frac{1}{\lfloor n/k\rfloor}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(Q^{*},P)\leq\frac{2}{\kappa\lfloor n/k\rfloor}R_{f}\leq\frac{4k}{n\kappa}R_{f}

for any
1\leq k\leq n
.
With (
1.16
) in hand, the proof of Corollary
1.2
is straightforward.
Moreover, in our cases of interest where
R_{f}=o(n)
, the bound (
1.16
) quantifies a form of
approximate independence
: Most
k
-particle marginals of
P
are
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-close to product measures, if
k=o(n/R_{f})
.

Remark 1.5
.

We work throughout the paper with state space
{\mathbb{R}}
, for simplicity. That is, we study approximations of measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by
n
-fold products of measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
, as opposed to, say, approximations of measures on
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
by
n
-fold products of measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Most of our arguments, based primarily on convexity and functional inequalities, extend to the case of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
or even Riemannian manifolds with lower curvature bounds in the spirit of Bakry-Émery
[
5
,
6
]
. The only difficulty is in the uniqueness claimed in Theorem
1.1
(proven in Proposition
3.9
), which would require a finer analysis involving regularity of certain optimal transport maps.

1.3.
Additional discussion and results

The remaining results presented in this section will not be used in the rest of the paper but serve to elaborate on the structure of the main theorem. The reader mainly interested in applications or proofs of the above results may skip to Sections
2
or
3
, respectively, with no loss of continuity.

1.3.1.
More on entropic projections

Reversing the order of arguments in the relative entropy in (
1.13
) leads to a very different optimization problem, but it is instructive to compare the two.
The infimum

(1.17)

\inf_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}H(P\,|\,Q)

is uniquely attained by taking
Q=P^{*}:=P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{n}
to be the product of the marginals of
P
.
Indeed, from the simple identity
H(P\,|\,Q)=H(P\,|\,P^{*})+H(P^{*}\,|\,Q)
, it follows that
H(P\,|\,Q)\geq H(P\,|\,P^{*})
for all
Q
, with equality if any only if
Q=P^{*}
.

The Gaussian case highlights the difference between (
1.17
) and (
1.13
). Suppose
P
is a centered Gaussian with nonsingular covariance matrix
\Sigma
. In this case it is easy to see that the (unique) minimizer of
H(Q\,|\,P)
among product measures
Q
is the centered Gaussian with covariance matrix
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, where
\widetilde{\Sigma}^{-1}
is the diagonal matrix obtained by deleting the off-diagonal entries of
\Sigma^{-1}
. On the other hand, the unique minimizer of
H(P\,|\,Q)
among product measures
Q
is the centered Gaussian with covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
obtained by deleting the off-diagonal entries of
\Sigma
.

1.3.2.
Tilts

A similar bound to Corollary
1.4
is available if one seeks a stronger mean field approximation, in which
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is replaced by the sub-class of product measures given by
tilts
of a given reference measure.
We focus on the case of Gaussian reference measure, as it is not obvious how to extend the argument to a general reference measure. For
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
\gamma_{y,t}
denote the Gaussian with mean
y
and covariance matrix
tI
, with
\gamma_{t}:=\gamma_{0,t}
, noting that
\gamma_{y,t}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
t>0
, and let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
C^{2}
and concave. Assume there exist
c_{1}\geq 0
and
0\leq c_{2}<1/2t
such that
|f(x)|\leq c_{1}e^{c_{2}|x|^{2}}
. Then there is a unique
y^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
satisfying

(1.18)

\displaystyle y^{*}=t\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\nabla f\,d\gamma_{y^{*},t},

and it holds that

(1.19)

\displaystyle\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}\,d\gamma_{t}\leq\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,d\gamma_{y,t}-H(\gamma_{y,t}\,|\,\gamma_{t})\right)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\partial_{ij}f|^{2}\,d\gamma_{y^{*},t}.

Noting that
H(\gamma_{y,t}\,|\,\gamma_{t})=|y|^{2}/2t
, a simple calculation shows that
y^{*}
uniquely attains the supremum in (
1.19
).
The difference between (
1.19
) and (
1.10
) is that the former includes the diagonal terms
i=j
in the sum. This is natural; an additively separable function
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i})
yields a product measure
P(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{f(x)}\gamma_{t}(dx)
, but it takes an
affine
function
f
for
P
to be a Gaussian. Small off-diagonal derivatives
\partial_{ij}f
can be naturally interpreted as meaning
f
is close to being additively separable, but the full Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f
must to be small in order for
f
to be close to affine.

The above proposition is worth comparing with prior results based on gradient complexity. It was shown in
[
3
, Proposition 3.4, arXiv version]
that if
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{1}
then

(1.20)

\displaystyle\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}\,d\gamma_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,d\gamma_{y,t}-H(\gamma_{y,t}\,|\,\gamma_{t})\right)+\sqrt{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\big(x\cdot\nabla f(y)\big)\,\gamma_{t}(dx).

The last integral is (
\sqrt{t}
times) the
Gaussian mean-width
of the set
\nabla f({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. This estimate (
1.20
) has the advantage of applying to non-concave functions
f
, but it is only meaningful if
\nabla f
is bounded. Proposition
1.6
, on the other hand, can accommodate non-Lipschitz but concave functions
f
.

1.3.3.
Generalization of the main theorem

We briefly discuss how Theorem
1.1
can generalize beyond the strongly log-concave setting.
Essentially, strong log-concavity is needed only for the uniqueness claims and to justify the log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities as explained in Section
1.2
.

Uniqueness
of
Q^{*}
is actually not essential if one is interested only in a bound like (
1.5
).
The
existence
of an optimizer
Q^{*}
is automatic, and it is not hard to show that it must satisfy the fixed point equation (
1.3
), modulo technical conditions.
If it can be shown that
Q^{*}
admits a strictly positive
C^{2}
density, and that
P
and
Q^{*}
obey a log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequality, respectively, with constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, then the following bound can be proven as in Section
1.2
:

0\leq R_{f}\leq C_{1}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathrm{Var}}_{Q^{*}}(\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i})\leq 2C_{1}C_{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[|\partial_{ij}f(X)|^{2}].

It is unclear if our assumed bound on
|f(x)|
is needed or merely an artifact of our proof technique. We use the assumed bound on
|f(x)|
in the proof of Theorem 1.1 only to show that
Q^{*}
is strictly positive a.e., but this can be shown directly in many particular cases, such as when
f
is symmetric.

2.1.
Gibbs measures with pairwise interactions

First, we study Gibbs measures with pairwise interaction potentials of the form (

where the following assumption holds:

.

\kappa

29

To work toward applying Theorem

with

Lemma 2.2
.

Define

), and suppose Assumption

\kappa

The following corollary will allow us to cover the case of unbounded

Define

With

Then

\displaystyle R_{f}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathrm{Tr}}(J^{2})a\kappa^{-2}e^{b^{2}/\kappa}.

Remark 2.4
.

Corollary

shows that

as long as

.
The assumption

for quadratic interaction models, first in
[

and then in
[

In contrast, in our setting we can allow measures of unbounded support, and we show both uniqueness of the optimizer and the convergence of the empirical measure in Theorems

below. On the other hand, our results require concavity assumptions which were not needed in

Using Corollary

. Under additional assumptions on the matrix

2.1.1.
Doubly stochastic matrices

In the following

and

on

Theorem 2.5
.

Define

by (
1.6
), and suppose Assumption
2.1
holds.
Assume there exist
a,b\geq 0
such that
|K^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{2}\leq ae^{b|x|}
for all

Assume further that the symmetric matrix

\sum_{j=1}^{n}J_{ij}=1
for all

(1)

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f(x)}dx=\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V\,dQ+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}K(x-y)Q(dx)Q(dy)-H(Q)\right).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}\to Q,\text{ weakly in law}.

, and we refer also to

2.1.2.
Graphons

Another case in which we can derive asymptotics of the log partition function is when the matrix
J
converges to a graphon
W
in cut metric. Below we introduce the relevant notions, deferring to
[
15
,
14
,
16
,
43
]
for additional background:

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
denote the space of all symmetric measurable functions from
[0,1]^{2}
to
[0,\infty)
which are integrable. For
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
, define the strong cut (pseudo-)metric by

d_{\square}(W_{1},W_{2}):=\sup_{S,T\subset[0,1]}\bigg|\int_{S\times T}\big(W_{1}(u,v)-W_{2}(u,v)\big)dudv\bigg|,

and their weak cut (pseudo-)metric by

\delta_{\square}(W_{1},W_{2}):=\inf_{\varphi}d_{\square}(W_{1},W_{2}^{\varphi}),

where the infimum is over all invertible measure-preserving maps
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, and
W_{2}^{\varphi}(u,v):=W_{2}(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))
.
Given a symmetric matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
with nonnegative entries, we define a function
W_{A}\in\mathcal{W}
by setting
W_{A}(u,v)\coloneqq A_{\lceil nu\rceil,\lceil nv\rceil}
.
We say that a sequence of symmetric matrices
\{A_{n}\}

converges in weak cut metric
to a function
W\in\mathcal{W}
if
\delta_{\square}(W_{A_{n}},W)\to 0
.

Remark 2.7
.

Suppose
G_{n}
is the adjacency matrix of an Erdős-Rényi random graph on
n
vertices with parameter
p_{n}
, such that
np_{n}\to\infty
. If
J_{n}=\frac{1}{np_{n}}G_{n}
, then
nJ_{n}
converges in strong cut metric to the constant function
1
(see
[
14
, Example 3.3.1]
). Similar convergences hold if
G_{n}
arises from a stochastic block model, where the edge probability matrix has a block structure, in which case the limiting
W
retains the same block structure. For more examples of convergent sequence of graphs in cut metric, we refer again to
[
15
,
14
,
16
,
43
]
and references therein.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
denote the space of all probability measures on
[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}
with uniform first marginal.
Note that any
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
admits the disintegration
\mu(du,dx)=du\mu_{u}(dx)
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Define
f
by (
1.6
), and suppose Assumption
2.1
holds.
Assume there exist
a,b\geq 0
such that
|K^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{2}\leq ae^{b|x|}
for all
x
.
Assume further that
V
is even,
K
is nonpositive,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{V(x)}dx=1
, and

is a sequence of matrices such that

converges in weak cut metric to a function

. Assume also that
{\mathrm{Tr}}(J_{n}^{2})=o(n)
.

(1)

Defining the probability measure

, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f(x)}\,dx\\
&\ \ =\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\int_{([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})^{2}}\!\!W(u,v)K(x-y)\mu(du,dx)\mu(dv,dy)-\int_{0}^{1}\!\!H(\mu_{u}\,|\,\rho)\,du\bigg).\end{split}

(2)

The supremum in (

) is attained by a unique

, and if

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}\to\int_{0}^{1}\mu^{*}_{u}\,du,\text{ weakly in law}.

Remark 2.9

It follows from

that the condition

holds automatically if

multiplied by

However, if

in Theorem

2.1.3.
On the symmetry of
Q^{*}

This short section elaborates on conditions under which one can check that
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[X_{i}-X_{j}]=0
, which was needed in Corollary
2.3
. The main two conditions we found are evenness and a weak form of permutation invariance.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
S
be a set of permutations of
[n]
. We say that
S
is
transitive
if for every
i,j\in[n]
there exists
\pi\in S
such that
\pi(i)=j
. We say also that a function
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
invariant under
S
if
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})
for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\pi\in S
.

Lemma 2.11
.

In the setting of Theorem
1.1
, the following implications hold:

(1)

If
f
is even, meaning
f(-x)=f(x)
for all
x
, then
Q^{*}_{i}
is even for each
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(2)

Suppose
f
is invariant under a transitive set of permutations. Then
Q^{*}_{1}=Q^{*}_{2}=\cdots=Q^{*}_{n}
.

In both cases, we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[X_{i}-X_{j}]=0
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

When
f
is of the form (
1.6
), it is clear that
f
is even if
K
and
V
are, and indeed
V
is assumed even in Theorem
2.5
to enable an application of Lemma
2.11
(1).
We will not apply Lemma
2.11
(2), but we find it interesting in its own right. For instance, (2) holds if
f
is symmetric, i.e., invariant under all permutations. Another natural case covered by (2) is where
f
is of the form (
1.6
) and
J
is a scalar multiple of the adjacency matrix of a
vertex transitive
graph.

2.2.
High dimensional Bayesian linear regression

Our next application is concerned with high dimensional Bayesian linear regression. Suppose we observe a set of data
\left\{(y_{i},X_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
y_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
X_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. Let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\top}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
. Consider the linear regression model

\displaystyle y=\mathbf{X}\beta+\varepsilon,\quad\varepsilon\sim\gamma_{\sigma^{2}},

where
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
denotes the Gaussian with mean
0
and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}I
. Here
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is the unknown parameter.

Following a Bayesian approach, assume that
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\pi
, where
\pi
is a prior distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}
with density proportional to
e^{V}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
for some
V:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. The posterior density
\pi_{y,\mathbf{X}}
of
\beta
given
y
and
\mathbf{X}
is then proportional to
e^{f_{y,\mathbf{X}}}
, where

\displaystyle f_{y,\mathbf{X}}(\beta):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}V(\beta_{i})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left|y-\mathbf{X}\beta\right|^{2}.

The posterior distribution is the central object of inference in Bayesian statistics. Note that even though
{\beta}
has independent coordinates under the prior, the coordinates of
{\beta}
are no longer independent under the posterior. Frequently, mean-field techniques are used to approximate such complex posterior distributions, including and beyond the set up of Bayesian linear regression (see
[
1
,
10
,
49
,
52
,
54
]
and references therein). In particular, it is useful to understand what conditions guarantee the validity of a mean field approximation, showing that the posterior is close to a product measure. Using Theorem
1.1
, the following corollary provides sufficient conditions under which the posterior is indeed mean-field. Leveraging this, it also derives a law of large numbers for the empirical measure under the true posterior distribution.

Corollary 2.12
.

Assume
V
is
\kappa_{1}
-concave for some
\kappa_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and that there exists
c_{1}\geq 0
and
0\leq c_{2}<\kappa/2
such that
|V(x)|\leq c_{1}e^{c_{2}x^{2}}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Set
J={\bf X}^{\top}{\bf X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
, and assume that
J\geq\kappa_{2}I
for some
\kappa_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}\sigma^{-2}>0
. Then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\bigg|\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}e^{f_{y,{\bf X}}(\beta)}d\beta-\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{p})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}f_{y,{\bf X}}\,dQ-H(Q)\right)\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{(\kappa_{1}\sigma^{2}+\kappa_{2})^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq p}J_{ij}^{2}.

Moreover, for every
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the inner supremum in (
2.5
) is attained by a unique
Q^{*}_{y}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{p})
, and for any 1-Lipschitz function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{y,\mathbf{X}}}\Bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varphi(\beta_{i})-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}_{y}}\big[\varphi(\beta_{i})\big]\bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}\Big(\kappa_{1}\sigma^{2}+\kappa_{2}+\sqrt{2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq p}J_{ij}^{2}}\Big)^{2}}{p(\kappa_{1}\sigma^{2}+\kappa_{2})^{3}}.

The proof of this corollary is by a direct application of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
, and is hence omitted. Indeed, the concavity assumption on
V
and the lower bound on
J
ensure that
\nabla^{2}f_{y,\mathbf{X}}(\beta)\leq-(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}\sigma^{-2})I
for all
\beta
.

Remark 2.13
.

The uniformity in
y
in (
2.5
) implies that the mean field approximation continues to hold with high probability, under any distributional assumption on
y
. Note that when
n,p\to\infty
in any arbitrary manner, the right-hand side of (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) are
o(p)
and
o(1)
respectively, as long as
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq p}J_{ij}^{2}=o(p)
when
n,p\to\infty
. We also point out that the same conclusion as in (
2.5
) above was derived in
[
46
, Theorem 1]
using very different techniques, under the assumption that the prior distribution
\pi
is compactly supported. In our setup, we allow the support to be non-compact, but instead assume that the prior distribution is strongly log-concave. One added advantage of our setup is that we also get the law of large numbers under no extra assumptions.

2.3.
Stochastic control

This section describes an application of Corollary
1.4
to a class of high-dimensional stochastic optimal control problems. Let
T>0
, and let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
C^{2}
and concave. Consider the stochastic control problem

(2.7)

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{orig}}\coloneqq\sup\,{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[g(X_{T})-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{i}(t,X_{t})|^{2}dt\Bigg],

where the supremum is over pairs
(\alpha,X)
, where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}):[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a measurable function and
X=(X^{1},\ldots,X^{n})
a weak solution of the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

(2.8)

\displaystyle dX^{i}_{t}=\alpha_{i}(t,X_{t})dt+dB^{i}_{t},\qquad X^{i}_{0}=0,\ \ i=1,\ldots,n,

defined on an arbitrary filtered probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
,
satisfying also
\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha(t,X_{t})|^{2}\,dt<\infty
a.s.
Here
B=(B^{1},\ldots,B^{n})
is an
n
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-Brownian motion, and
X
is required to be
{\mathbb{F}}
-adapted.
We call such a pair
(\alpha,X)

admissible
.
There is a well known semi-explicit solution to (
2.7
) which has come to be known as the
Föllmer drift
, which we will discuss in Remark
2.15
below.

We interpret
i=1,\ldots,n
as the indices of different “players,” each facing an independent source of randomness
B^{i}
, and each choosing a control
\alpha_{i}
which can depend on the full information of all
n
players. Players “cooperate” in the sense that
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
are chosen together to optimize (
2.7
).
When
g
is of the form

(2.9)

\displaystyle g(x)=G\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}\bigg),\ \ \text{for some }G:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{R}},

we recover a well-studied class of problems which goes under the name
mean field control
in the cooperative setting
[
19
]
, or
mean field games

Remark 2.15
.

In fact, the proof of Corollary

For a measure

3.
Proof of the main theorem

The proofs will make use of the well known log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities for strongly log-concave measures, recalled here for convenience as we will use them in several parts of the paper. The former is due to Bakry-Émery (see
[
5
]
or
[
6
, Corollary 5.7.2]
), and the latter is a consequence of the Brascamp-Lieb inequality
[
18
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Log-Sobolev inequality)
.

If
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{2}
and
\kappa
-concave, and
R(dx)=e^{h(x)}dx
is a probability measure, then
R
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality,

\displaystyle H(Q\,|\,R)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\kappa}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|\nabla\log\frac{dQ}{dR}\right|^{2}\,dQ,

for every
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
Q\ll R
and the weak gradient of
\log dQ/dR
exists in
L^{2}(Q)
.

Theorem 3.2

(Poincaré inequality)
.

If
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
\kappa
-concave, and
R(dx)=e^{h(x)}dx
is a probability measure, then
R
satisfies the Poincaré inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{R}(\varphi)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\varphi^{2}\,dR-\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\varphi\,dR\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,dR,

for every continuously differentiable function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in
L^{1}(R)
.

The above Poincaré inequality is normally stated with the additional assumptions that
h
is
C^{2}
, which is easily removed by mollification by a Gaussian, and that
\varphi\in L^{2}(R)
, which can be weakened to
L^{1}(R)
by monotone approximation, though both sides may be infinite.

We will also make use of the Gibbs variational principle, which is well known, but we give the proof as we need a non-standard form which is careful about edge cases. Recall our convention that
H(Q):=\infty
if
Q
is not absolutely continuous or if
Q\log Q\notin L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

Theorem 3.3

(Gibbs variational principle)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
be measurable, bounded from above and such that
Z\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}\,dx\in(0,\infty)
. Define
P\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by
P(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{f(x)}\,dx
. Then

(3.1)

\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\right)=\log Z\in(-\infty,\infty),

and the following are equivalent:

(1)

H(P)<\infty
.

(2)

The supremum in (
3.1
)
is attained uniquely by
P
.

(3)

There exists a maximizer in (
3.1
).

Proof.

We first prove (
3.1
). Since
f
is bounded from above,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ\in[-\infty,\infty)
is well-defined for all
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
We may thus restrict the supremum in (
3.1
) to those
Q
with
H(Q)<\infty
. For
H(Q)<\infty
, we have the simple identity

(3.2)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)=-H(Q\,|\,P)+\log Z.

Therefore,

\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\right)=-\inf\Big\{H(Q\,|\,P):Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\ H(Q)<\infty\Big\}+\log Z,

and it suffices to show that the infimum on the right-hand side is zero. We proceed by approximation. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
B_{k}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
denote the centered ball of radius
k
, and define the probability density
Q_{k}=P1_{B_{k}}/P(B_{k})
. Since
f
is bounded from above, the density
Q_{k}
is bounded and supported on the bounded set
B_{k}
. Thus
Q_{k}\log Q_{k}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, or
H(Q_{k})<\infty
, and we conclude that
H(Q\,|\,P)\leq\liminf_{k}H(Q_{k}\,|\,P)
. Finally, since
P(B_{k})\to 1
,

\displaystyle H(Q_{k}\,|\,P)=-\log P(B_{k})\to 0.

This proves the claim (
3.1
).

Turning to the equivalence of (1–3), the implication (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) follows by taking
Q=P
in (
3.2
). The implication (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) is trivial. Lastly, for the implication (3)
\Rightarrow
(1), suppose
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
attains the supremum in (
3.1
). We know from (
3.1
) that the supremum is not
-\infty
, so
H(Q)<\infty
. Then, for any
R\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with
H(R)<\infty
, the identity (
3.2
) implies

\displaystyle-H(R\,|\,P)+\log Z

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dR-H(R)\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)=-H(Q\,|\,P)+\log Z.

Rearrange and minimize over
R
to get

\displaystyle H(Q\,|\,P)\leq\inf\Big\{H(R\,|\,P):R\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\ H(R)<\infty\Big\}=0,

where the last equality was shown just above while proving (
3.1
). It follows that
H(Q\,|\,P)=0
, so
Q=P
, and
H(P)=H(Q)<\infty
. This completes the proof.

∎

Proof of Theorem

This section proves Theorem
1.1
in several parts, and we assume throughout that

Since

\kappa

\displaystyle f(x)\leq a-b|x|^{2},\quad\text{ for all }x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ \text{ where }a:=f(0)+\kappa^{-1}|\nabla f(0)|^{2},\ b:=\kappa/4.

This implies that

, so
P(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{f(x)}dx
is well defined.
Moreover,
f
is bounded from above, so
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ
is well defined in

for every
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
Note lastly that
fe^{f}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

H(P)<\infty
, which follows from the growth assumption on

and the fact that the
\kappa
-log-concave measure

satisfies
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{c|x|^{2}}P(dx)<\infty
for each

. (In fact, every absolutely continuous log-concave measure has finite entropy
[
11

Lemma 3.4
.

It holds that

(3.4)

\displaystyle-\infty<\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\right)<\infty,

and any
Q^{*}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
attaining the supremum satisfies
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
.
Also, equation (
1.12
) is valid.

Proof.

The Gibbs variational formula (Theorem
3.3
) implies that the supremum in (
3.4
) is no greater than
\log Z<\infty
. To see that it is not
-\infty
, note that
f
is locally bounded because it is concave and real-valued. Hence, if
Q
is any product measure with bounded support and finite entropy (such as the uniform measure on
[0,1]^{n}
), we can bound the supremum from below by
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)>-\infty
.
Now, if
Q^{*}
is an optimizer, then
H(Q^{*})<\infty
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}fdQ^{*}>-\infty
, the latter implying that
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
since
f
is bounded from above.

To prove (
1.12
), note that the simple calculation (
1.11
) is valid for any
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with
H(Q)<\infty
, though both sides are
+\infty
if and only if
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ=-\infty
. Since
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ
always exists in
[-\infty,\infty)
, the supremum in (
3.4
) remains the same when restricted to those
Q
with
H(Q)<\infty
. By infimizing (
1.11
) over
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with finite entropy, we deduce that the left-hand side of (
1.12
) is finite and equals
\inf\{H(Q\,|\,P):Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\,H(Q)<\infty\}
. To complete the proof, we claim that if
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfies
H(Q\,|\,P)<\infty
and
H(Q)=\infty
, then there exists
Q_{k}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
H(Q_{k})<\infty
for each
k
and
H(Q_{k}\,|\,P)\to H(Q\,|\,P)
. Indeed, define the probability density
Q_{k}=Q1_{B_{k}}/Q(B_{k})
, where
B_{k}=[-k,k]^{n}
, for

large enough that
Q(B_{k})>0
. Then

\displaystyle H(Q_{k}\,|\,P)=\frac{1}{Q(B_{k})}\int_{B_{k}}\log\frac{dQ}{dP}\,dQ-\log Q(B_{k})

is finite and converges to
H(Q\,|\,P)
as
k\to\infty
. In particular,
\log(dQ_{k}/dP)\in L^{1}(Q_{k})
. We also have
\log P=f-\log Z\in L^{1}(Q_{k})
because
f
is locally bounded and
Q_{k}
has compact support. We deduce that
\log Q_{k}\in L^{1}(Q_{k})

H(Q_{k})<\infty
, which completes the proof.

The following proposition shows essentially that the fixed point problem (
1.3
) is the first order condition for optimality in (

). This extends naturally to much more general settings, with
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},dx)
replaced by a general

Proposition 3.5

(Optimality to fixed point)
.

Suppose
Q^{*}=Q^{*}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{*}_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
attains the supremum in (
3.4
).
Then
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
and
Q^{*}
satisfies the fixed point equation

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{i}(dx_{i})&=Z_{i}^{-1}e^{\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})}\,dx_{i},\qquad\text{where }\hat{f}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}\text{ is defined by }\\
\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})&:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\prod_{j\neq i}Q^{*}_{j}(dx_{j}),\qquad i\in[n].\end{split}

Proof.

Note that
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
by Lemma
3.4
.
By assumption,
(Q^{*}_{1},\ldots,Q^{*}_{n})
attains the supremum

\displaystyle\sup_{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,d(Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n})-H(Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n})\right).

Clearly,
\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})={\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[f(X)\,|\,X_{i}=x_{i}]
for
Q^{*}_{i}
-a.e.
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Also, it is well known that entropy tensorizes for product measures:
H(Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(Q_{i})
.
From these and the tower property it follows for each
i\in[n]
that
Q^{*}_{i}
attains the supremum

(3.6)

\displaystyle S_{i}:=\sup_{Q_{i}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{f}_{i}\,dQ_{i}-H(Q_{i})\right).

We wish to invoke the Gibbs variational principle (Theorem
3.3
) to deduce that this supremum is uniquely attained by the probability measure with density proportional to
e^{\hat{f}_{i}}
, and thus
Q^{*}_{i}(dx_{i})=Z_{i}^{-1}e^{\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})}\,dx_{i}
, which yields (
3.5
).
It remains to carefully check the conditions of Theorem
3.3
.
We know that
Q^{*}_{i}
attains the supremum (
3.6
), so we must just check that
Z_{i}\in(0,\infty)
.
Note that (
3.3
) implies
f(x)\leq a-bx_{i}^{2}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and thus
\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})\leq a-bx_{i}^{2}
for all
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
which implies
Z_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})}\,dx_{i}<\infty
. Next, recall from Lemma
3.4
that
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
, so by Fubini’s theorem,
Q_{i}^{*}(|\hat{f}_{i}|<\infty)=1
. Note that
Q^{*}_{i}
is absolutely continuous since
H(Q_{i}^{*})<\infty
. Hence,
\{|\hat{f}_{i}|<\infty\}
has nonzero Lebesgue measure, and so
Z_{i}>0
.

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists a unique maximizer in (
3.4
).

Proof.

We first prove existence. Recalling the identity (
1.12
), the optimizers of (
3.4
) are in one-to-one correspondence with the optimizers of
\inf_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}H(Q\,|\,P)
. The latter exist because
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a weakly closed subset of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and because
H(\cdot\,|\,P)
has weakly compact sub-level sets.

We next prove uniqueness. Let
Q^{*}=Q^{*}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{*}_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
denote any optimizer of (
1.5
).
Define
G_{1},G_{2}:({\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}))^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle G_{1}(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n})\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(dx_{i}),\quad G_{2}(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n})\coloneqq H(Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n}).

That is,
Q^{*}
is a maximizer of
G=G_{1}-G_{2}
, and we will show it must be the only one.
Let
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be distinct from
Q^{*}
. We denote by
M(t)=(M_{1}(t),\dots,M_{n}(t))
the displacement interpolations between the marginals, i.e.,

\displaystyle M_{i}(t)=Q^{*}_{i}\circ((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+tT_{i})^{-1},

where
T_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the
Q_{i}^{*}
-a.s. unique nondecreasing function satisfying
Q^{*}_{i}\circ T_{i}^{-1}=Q_{i}
. Since
Q^{*}_{i}
and
Q_{i}
are distinct, there exists
i
such that
T_{i}
is different from the identity map on a set with strictly positive
Q^{\ast}_{i}
-measure.
Writing out the expression of
G_{1}
,

\displaystyle G_{1}(M(t))

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\Big((1-t)x_{1}+tT_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,(1-t)x_{n}+tT_{n}(x_{n})\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q^{*}_{i}(dx_{i}),

we see that
t\mapsto G_{1}(M(t))
is strictly concave because
f
is strictly concave and
Q\neq Q^{*}
.
Tensorization of entropy yields
G_{2}(M(t))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(M_{i}(t))
, and it is well known that differential entropy is displacement convex
[
51
, Theorem 5.15(i)]
. That is,
t\mapsto H(M_{i}(t))
is convex for each
i
. We deduce that
t\mapsto G(M(t))
is strictly concave. This proves uniqueness: if
Q
were also an optimizer, then
G(M(1))=G(Q)=G(Q^{*})=G(M(0))
would imply
G(M(t))>G(Q^{*})
for some
t\in(0,1)
.

Remark 3.7

(X_{j})_{j\neq i}

x_{-i}=(x_{j})_{j\neq i}
and, with some abuse of notation,
f(x)=f(x_{i},x_{-i})
.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfies the fixed point equation (
3.5
), then
Q
is
\kappa
-log-concave.

Proof.

By (
3.5
), the density of
Q
is proportional to
e^{\hat{F}}
, where
\hat{F}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})
and
\hat{f}_{i}
is given by (
3.5
). By the
\kappa
-concavity of
f
, for any
y,z\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
t\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}

\displaystyle(tz+(1-t)y)+\frac{\kappa}{2}(tz+(1-t)y)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}\Big[f(tz+(1-t)y,x_{-i})+\frac{\kappa}{2}(tz+(1-t)y)^{2}\Big]Q_{-i}(x_{-i})dx_{-i}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}\Big[tf(z,x_{-i})+t\frac{\kappa}{2}z^{2}+(1-t)f(y,x_{-i})+(1-t)\frac{\kappa}{2}y^{2}\Big]Q_{-i}(x_{-i})dx_{-i}

\displaystyle=t\hat{f}_{i}(z)+t\frac{\kappa}{2}z^{2}+(1-t)\hat{f}_{i}(y)+(1-t)\frac{\kappa}{2}y^{2}.

This shows that
\hat{f}_{i}
is
\kappa
-concave, and thus so is
\hat{F}
.
∎

The next proposition, in conjunction with Proposition
3.5
, shows that the optimizers of (
3.4
) and the solutions of the fixed point problem (
1.3
) are exactly the same.

Proposition 3.9

(Fixed point to optimality)
.

Let
Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfy
f\in L^{1}(Q)
and the fixed point problem (
3.5
).
Then
Q
has strictly positive density a.e. and is a maximizer of (
3.4
).

Proof.

We first show that
Q
has strictly positive density a.e. Since
Q=Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n}
satisfies the fixed point equation (
3.5
), each
Q_{i}
has a density with exponent

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\prod_{j\neq i}Q_{j}(dx_{j})\geq-c_{1}e^{c_{2}x_{i}^{2}}\prod_{j\neq i}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{c_{2}x_{j}^{2}}Q_{j}(x_{j})\,dx_{j}

for every
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. From Lemma
3.8
we know that
Q
is
\kappa
-log-concave.
Since
c_{2}<\kappa/2
, we deduce that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{c_{2}x_{j}^{2}}Q_{j}(x_{j})<\infty
.
Thus
\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})>-\infty
for all
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Define
G(R)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dR-H(R)
for
R\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Let
Q^{*}
be an optimizer of
\sup\{G(R):R\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\}
, which exists uniquely by Lemma
3.6
. By Proposition
3.5
, we have
f\in L^{1}(Q^{*})
, and
Q^{*}
satisfies the fixed point equation (
3.5
). The argument given in the previous paragraph implies that
Q^{*}
has a strictly positive density a.e.
To complete the proof, we must show that
G(Q)\geq G(Q^{*})
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
T_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
denote the unique nondecreasing function satisfying
Q_{i}\circ T_{i}^{-1}=Q^{*}_{i}
, and define
M_{i}(t)=Q_{i}\circ((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+tT_{i})^{-1}
. Let
M(t)=M_{1}(t)\times\cdots\times M_{n}(t)
, so that
G(M(t))=g_{1}(t)-g_{2}(t)
, where

\displaystyle g_{1}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\big((1-t)x_{1}+tT_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,(1-t)x_{n}+tT_{n}(x_{1})\big)\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(dx_{i}),

\displaystyle g_{2}(t)

\displaystyle:=H\big(M_{1}(t)\times\cdots\times M_{n}(t)\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(M_{i}(t)).

Let us write
g^{\prime+}
for the right-derivative of a real-valued function
g
, when it exists.
Note that
T_{i}
is a.e. differentiable, as it is monotone.
Using
[
51
, Theorem 5.30]
, we may compute the right-derivatives at zero as

\displaystyle g_{1}^{\prime+}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\partial_{i}f(x)\big(T_{i}(x_{i})-x_{i}\big)Q(x)dx,

\displaystyle g_{2}^{\prime+}(0)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(T_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})-1\right)Q_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}.

We wish to rewrite both terms in more useful forms.

We first claim that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\partial_{i}f(x)\big(T_{i}(x_{i})-x_{i}\big)Q(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{f}^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})\big(T_{i}(x_{i})-x_{i}\big)Q_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i},

where
\hat{f}_{i}
is defined as in (
3.5
).
To see this, note that
\hat{f}_{i}(x_{i})={\mathbb{E}}_{Q}[f(x_{i},X_{-i})]
for all
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, so

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\prime+}_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}[f(x_{i}+h,X_{-i})-f(x_{i},X_{-i})].

By the concavity of
f
, the difference quotient
[f(x_{i}+h,X_{-i})-f(x_{i},X_{-i})]/h
increases as
h\downarrow 0
, and it is bounded from below for
0<h\leq h_{0}
by
[f(x_{i}+h_{0},X_{-i})-f(x_{i},X_{-i})]/h_{0}
, which has finite
Q
-expectation for a.e. choice of
h_{0}>0
by Fubini’s theorem since
f\in L^{1}(Q)
.
Hence, by monotone convergence,

(3.8)

\hat{f}^{\prime+}_{i}(x_{i})={\mathbb{E}}_{Q}[\partial_{i}f(x_{i},X_{-i})].

Moreover, this quantity is finite and nonincreasing in
x_{i}
because
\hat{f}_{i}
is a concave real-valued function. In addition,
\hat{f}^{\prime}_{i}=\hat{f}^{\prime+}_{i}
a.e. since concave functions are a.e. differentiable.
Using (
3.8
), we see that the right-hand side of (
3.7
) equals
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}[{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}[\partial_{i}f(X)\,|\,X_{i}](T_{i}(X_{i})-X_{i})]
, which yields (
3.7
).

We next integrate by parts to get

(3.9)

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(T_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})-1\right)Q_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(T_{i}(x_{i})-x_{i}\right)Q_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\,dx_{i}.

To justify this carefully, we use Lebesgue-Stieltjes integration by parts: Note that the probability density function of
Q_{i}
is absolutely continuous because it is proportional to
e^{\hat{f}_{i}}
, and
\hat{f}_{i}
is absolutely continuous as a concave function. Let
F_{Q_{i}}
and
F_{Q^{*}_{i}}
denote the CDFs of
Q_{i}
and
Q^{*}_{i}
respectively. Recalling that
T_{i}=F_{Q^{*}_{i}}^{-1}\circ F_{Q_{i}}
is the monotone map pushing
Q_{i}
forward to
Q^{*}_{i}
, and that both
Q_{i}
and
Q^{*}_{i}
admit strictly positive densities, the function
T_{i}
is absolutely continuous. Hence, there is no jump term in the integration by parts, and we must only show that the boundary terms vanish. For this it suffices to show that there exist sequences
x^{\pm}_{n}\to\pm\infty
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(T_{i}(x^{\pm}_{n})-x^{\pm}_{n})Q_{i}(x^{\pm}_{n})=0.

If this were not the case, it would imply that
|T_{i}(x)-x|Q_{i}(x)\leq(|T_{i}(x)|+|x|)Q_{i}(x)
is bounded away from zero for
|x|
sufficiently large. This would in turn imply that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|T_{i}(x_{i})|+|x_{i}|)Q_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}=\infty
, contradicting
the fact that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|T_{i}(x_{i})|+|x_{i}|)Q_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x_{i}|Q^{*}_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x_{i}|Q_{i}(x_{i})dx_{i}<\infty.

Both integrals are finite because
Q_{i}
and
Q^{*}_{i}
are
\kappa
-log-concave by Lemma
3.8
and thus admit finite moments of every order.
With (
3.9
) and (
3.7
) now justified, we see that the right-derivative of
G(M(t))
at
t=0
is

\displaystyle g_{1}^{\prime+}(0)-g_{2}^{\prime+}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\hat{f}^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i})Q_{i}(x_{i})-Q_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})\big)\big(T_{i}(x_{i})-x_{i}\big)dx_{i}.

This is in fact zero, because
Q_{i}
is proportional to
e^{\hat{f}_{i}}
. We saw in the proof of Lemma
3.6
that
G(M(t))
is concave. Since we now know that it has vanishing right-derivative at
t=0
, it follows that
G(M(1))\leq G(M(0))
. That is,
G(Q^{*})\leq G(Q)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{i},Q_{i})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P,Q).

Indeed, to prove this, let
(X,Y)
be an optimal coupling of
(P,Q)
. Let
X_{i}
be the
{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{i}}
coordinate, for
i=1,\ldots,m
, and similarly define

. Then
(X_{i},Y_{i})
is a coupling of
(P_{i},Q_{i})

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P,Q)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[|X-Y|^{2}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}|X_{i}-Y_{i}|^{2}\right]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{i},Q_{i}).

Now, let
1\leq k\leq n
, and let
m=\lfloor n/k\rfloor
. Let
\Pi
be the set of vectors
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{m})
of disjoint
k

[n]
. Let
Q_{S_{i}}
and
P_{S_{i}}
denote the corresponding marginals, on those coordinates in
S_{i}\subset[n]
. Note that

. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{S_{i}},Q_{S_{i}})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{m}},Q_{S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{m}})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P,Q).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{S\subset[n],\,|S|=k}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{S},Q_{S})=\frac{1}{|\Pi|}\sum_{(S_{1},\ldots,S_{m})\in\Pi}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{S_{i}},Q_{S_{i}}).

be given as therein. Computing derivatives as above, we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle R_{f}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[|\partial_{ij}f(X)|^{2}]=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}J_{ij}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\big[|K^{\prime\prime}(X_{i}-X_{j})|^{2}\big].

Using the assumption on
K^{\prime\prime}
, we find

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\big[|K^{\prime\prime}(X_{i}-X_{j})|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq a{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\big[e^{b|X_{i}-X_{j}|}\big].

By assumption,
X_{i}-X_{j}
has mean zero under
Q^{*}
.
It follows from the
\kappa
-log-concavity of
Q^{*}
that the law of
X_{i}-X_{j}
is
(\kappa/2)
-log-concave (see, e.g.,
[
50
, Theorem 3.7(a) and Theorem 3.8]
).
This implies that it is subgaussian in the sense that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[e^{s(X_{i}-X_{j})}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{s^{2}/\kappa},\qquad\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Indeed, this can be deduced from the log-Sobolev inequality (Theorem
3.1
) via Herbst’s argument or
[
12
, Theorem 1.3]
.
Thus, using (
4.2
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\big[|K^{\prime\prime}(X_{i}-X_{j})|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq a{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}\big[e^{b(X_{i}-X_{j})}+e^{b(X_{j}-X_{i})}\big]\leq 2ae^{b^{2}/\kappa}.

Combine this with (
4.1
) to complete the proof.
∎

4.1.
Doubly stochastic matrices

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
2.5
. We first need a straightforward lemma about displacement convexity, which is likely known.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in[0,1]
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}=1
. Then there exists a random vector
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
such that
X_{i}\sim Q_{i}
for each
i
and

\displaystyle H\bigg(\mathrm{Law}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}X_{i}\bigg)\bigg)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}H(Q_{i}).

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
, with the case
n=1
holding trivially.
Assume that the statement of the lemma is true for some
n
. Let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n+1}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{n+1}\in[0,1]
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}t_{i}=1
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
t_{n+1}<1
and that
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n+1}
have finite entropy, as otherwise there is nothing to prove.
For
i=1,\dots,n
, define
\tilde{t}_{i}\coloneqq t_{i}/(1-t_{n+1})
, so that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{t}_{i}=1
. By assumption, we may find a random vector
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
such that
X_{i}\sim Q_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
and

(4.3)

\displaystyle H(\widetilde{Q})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{t}_{i}H(Q_{i}),

where
\widetilde{Q}
denotes the law of
\widetilde{X}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{t}_{i}X_{i}
. By absolute continuity, there is a unique nondecreasing function
T:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\widetilde{Q}\circ T^{-1}=Q_{n+1}
.
The entropy functional is displacement convex
[
51
, Theorem 5.15(i)]
, which means that the function

\displaystyle[0,1]\ni t\mapsto H\big(\widetilde{Q}\circ(tT+(1-t)\mathrm{Id})^{-1}\big)

is convex. In particular, letting
X_{n+1}=T(\widetilde{X})
, we find

\displaystyle H\big(\mathrm{Law}(t_{n+1}X_{n+1}+(1-t_{n+1})\widetilde{X})\big)

\displaystyle=H\big(\widetilde{Q}\circ(t_{n+1}T+(1-t_{n+1})\mathrm{Id})^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\leq t_{n+1}H(Q_{n+1})+(1-t_{n+1})H(\widetilde{Q}).

By (
4.3
) and the definition of
\tilde{t}_{i}
, we have
(1-t_{n+1})H(\widetilde{Q})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}H(Q_{i})
, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.5
(
1
).

Let us abbreviate

(4.4)

M_{n}:=\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}M_{n}(Q),

where we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{n}(Q)&:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f\,dQ-H(Q)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V(x)\,Q_{i}(dx)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{ij}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}K(x-y)\,Q_{i}(dx)Q_{j}(dy)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(Q_{i}),\end{split}

where the last equality used the symmetry of
J
and
K
, the fact that the diagonal entries of
J
are zero, and the tensorization of entropy. Recall that
\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{f}\,dx=M_{n}+R_{f}
, by definition of
R_{f}
.
We will complete the proof by showing that

(4.5)

\displaystyle M_{n}=n\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V\,dQ+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}K(x-y)Q(dx)Q(dy)-H(Q)\right),

and that the optimizer
Q^{*}=Q^{*}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{*}_{n}
in (
1.4
) must be i.i.d., or
Q^{*}_{1}=\cdots=Q^{*}_{n}
.
Indeed, the i.i.d. form of
Q^{*}
implies
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*}}[X_{i}-X_{j}]=0
for all
i,j
. Using this and the assumption
{\mathrm{Tr}}(J^{2})=o(n)
, we may apply Corollary
2.3
to deduce that
R_{f}/n\to 0
, and Theorem
2.5
(
1
) follows.

The proof of the inequality
(\geq)
in (
4.5
) is immediate upon restricting the supremum in (
4.4
) to i.i.d. measures and using
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{ij}=n
:

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})}\bigg(n\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V(x)\,Q(dx)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{ij}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}K(x-y)\,Q(dx)Q(dy)-nH(Q)\bigg)

\displaystyle=n\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}})}\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V\,dQ+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}K(x-y)Q(dx)Q(dy)-H(Q)\bigg).

To prove the inequality
(\leq)
in (
4.5
), fix
Q=Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
arbitrarily. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists a random vector
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
such that
X_{i}\sim Q_{i}
for all
i
and

(4.6)

H(\overline{Q})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(Q_{i}).

where
\overline{Q}
denotes the law of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Using the concavity
V
, we find

(4.7)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V(x)\,Q_{i}(dx)={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(X_{i})\bigg]\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}V\,d\overline{Q}.

Let
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
be an independent copy of
X
.
Using the concavity of
K
and the fact that
\sum_{i}J_{ij}=\sum_{j}J_{ij}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{ij}

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}K(x-y)\,Q_{i}(dx)Q_{j}(dy)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{ij}K(X_{i}-Y_{j})\bigg]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[K\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{ij}(X_{i}-Y_{j})\bigg)\bigg]

(4.8)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[K\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}\bigg)\bigg]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x-y)\,\overline{Q}(dx)\overline{Q}(dy).

Combining (
4.6
), (
4.7
), and (
4.8
), we see that

\displaystyle M_{n}(Q)/n

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}V\,d\overline{Q}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x-y)\,\overline{Q}(dx)\overline{Q}(dy)-H(\overline{Q})=M_{n}(\overline{Q}^{\otimes n})/n.

In other words, for an arbitrary choice of product measure
Q
, we may increase
M_{n}(Q)
by replacing
Q
with the i.i.d. measure
\overline{Q}^{\otimes n}
. This completes the proof. ∎

Proof of Theorem
2.5
(
2
).

We first justify the uniqueness claim. From part (
3
) of Theorem
1.1
, we know that the optimizer

, where

is the (necessarily unique) optimizer of (

), which does not depend on

Turning to the proof of (

), recall that

, and use Corollary

and the aforementioned i.i.d. form of the optimizer

4.2.
Graphons proofs

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.8
.
For
W\in\mathcal{W}
and any measurable function
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
bounded from above, define
T_{W,\psi}:{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

T_{W,\psi}(\mu):={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\otimes 2}}\left[\psi(X_{1},X_{2})W(U_{1},U_{2})\right].

where
(U_{1},X_{1})
and
(U_{2},X_{2})
are independent with law
\mu
.
Note that
W\geq 0
is integrable, so
T_{W,\psi}(\mu)
is well-defined in
[-\infty,\infty)
. Let
\overline{\mu}:=\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\times\rho
, and define
I:{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})\to[0,\infty]
by

I(\mu):=H(\mu\,|\,\overline{\mu})=\int_{0}^{1}H(\mu_{u}\,|\,\rho)\,du,

with the second identity coming from the chain rule for relative entropy
[
31
, Theorem B.2.1]
, and we recall that
\rho(dx)=e^{V(x)}dx
is a probability measure. We begin with two lemmas pertaining to the continuity of
T_{W,\psi}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a compact interval.
Let
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be supported on
\mathcal{K}^{2}
and continuous when restricted to
\mathcal{K}^{2}
.

(1)

If
\{W_{\ell}\}
converges to
W
in strong cut metric and
W_{\ell},W\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})}\Big|T_{W_{\ell},\psi}(\mu)-T_{W,\psi}(\mu)\Big|\to 0.

(2)

The map
\mu\to T_{W,\psi}(\mu)
is continuous on
\{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}):I(\mu)<\infty\}
, with respect to the topology of weak convergence.

Proof.

We begin with (1). Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the space of functions
\phi:\mathcal{K}^{2}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
of the form

(4.9)

\displaystyle\phi(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_{i}a_{i}(x)b_{i}(y),

for some
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and continuous functions
a_{i},b_{i}:\mathcal{K}\to[0,1]
. It is easy to check that
\mathcal{V}
is closed under multiplication, contains the constant functions, separates points in
\mathcal{K}^{2}
, and is a vector subspace of the space
C(\mathcal{K}^{2})
of continuous real-valued functions on
\mathcal{K}^{2}
.
By the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem, we deduce that
\mathcal{V}
is dense in
C(\mathcal{K}^{2})
with the supremum norm. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and find
\phi\in\mathcal{V}
such that
|\psi-\phi|<\varepsilon
uniformly on
\mathcal{K}^{2}
.
Extend the domain of
\phi
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by setting
\phi=0
on the complement of
\mathcal{K}^{2}
.
Then for all
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
,

\displaystyle\big|T_{W_{\ell},\psi}(\mu)-T_{W_{\ell},\phi}(\mu)\big|\leq\varepsilon\|W_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}},\quad\big|T_{W,\psi}(\mu)-T_{W,\phi}(\mu)\big|\leq\varepsilon\|W\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}.

Consequently, using the triangle inequality, we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle\big|T_{W_{\ell},\psi}(\mu)-T_{W,\psi}(\mu)\big|\leq\varepsilon\|W_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}+\varepsilon\|W\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}+\big|T_{W_{\ell},\phi}(\mu)-T_{W,\phi}(\mu)\big|.

Since
\phi
is of the form (
4.9
), we have

\displaystyle T_{W_{\ell},\phi}(\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_{i}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\bar{a}_{i}(u)\bar{b}_{i}(v)W_{\ell}(u,v)dudv,

where we define
\bar{a}_{i}(u):={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[a_{i}(X)\,|\,U=u]
, and
\bar{b}_{i}
similarly. This yields

(4.11)

\displaystyle\big|T_{W_{\ell},\phi}(\mu)-T_{W,\phi}(\mu)\big|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}|c_{i}|d_{\square}(W_{\ell},W).

Noting that
d_{\square}(W_{\ell},W)\to 0
implies
\|W_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}\to\|W\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}
, we may now combine (
4.10
) and (
4.11
), sending
\ell\to\infty
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
, to prove the claim (1).

To prove (2), let
\mu_{k}
be a sequence of measures in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
converging weakly to
\mu_{\infty}
, such that
I(\mu_{\infty})<\infty
. Let
W_{\ell}
be a sequence of continuous functions in
\mathcal{W}
converging in
L_{1}[0,1]^{2}
to
W
. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\big|T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{k})-T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{\infty})\big|\leq 2\sup_{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})}\big|T_{W,\psi}(\nu)-T_{W_{\ell},\psi}(\nu)\big|+\big|T_{W_{\ell},\psi}(\mu_{k})-T_{W_{\ell},\psi}(\mu_{\infty})\big|.

The first term converges to
0
as
\ell\to\infty
, by part (1) and the fact that convergence in
L_{1}[0,1]^{2}
implies convergence in strong cut metric. The second term converges to
0
for fixed
\ell
as

, using the fact that
\mu_{k}
converges weakly to
\mu_{\infty}
, and the set of discontinuity points of
W_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
is contained in

, which has measure

under
\mu_{\infty}^{\otimes 2}
(as
\mu_{\infty}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on

).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
\mu_{m}
is a sequence of measures in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
converging weakly to
\mu_{\infty}
. Let
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function, and let
W\in L_{1}[0,1]^{2}
. For

, let
(U^{m}_{1},X^{m}_{1}),(U^{m}_{2},X^{m}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu_{m}

W(U^{m}_{1},U^{m}_{2})\psi(X^{m}_{1},X^{m}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}W(U^{\infty}_{1},U^{\infty}_{2})\psi(X^{\infty}_{1},X^{\infty}_{2}).

Proof.

If
W
is continuous, then the claim is immediate.
For a general
W
, we proceed as follows: Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a compact set such that
{\mathbb{P}}(X^{m}_{1}\in\mathcal{K},X^{m}_{2}\in\mathcal{K})\geq 1-\varepsilon
, which is again possible by tightness of
\{\left(X^{m}_{1},X^{m}_{2}\right)\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Let

be a continuous function with

\|W-g\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{1\vee\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{K}}|\psi(x,y)|}.

Then on the event

Thus, for any continuous function

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\phi\big(W(U^{m}_{1},U^{m}_{2})\psi(X^{m}_{1},X^{m}_{2})\big)-{\mathbb{E}}\phi\big(g(U^{m}_{1},U^{m}_{2})\psi(X^{m}_{1},X^{m}_{2})\big)\Big|\leq 2\varepsilon.

∎

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(Q_{i}\,|\,\rho)=H(\hat{\rho}\,|\,\rho)=-\log\rho(B)<\infty,

and also

T_{W_{nJ},\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q))\geq-\|W_{nJ}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}\sup_{x,y\in B}|\psi(x,y)|.

Since
\psi
is continuous, it is bounded on the compact set
B
. Since
W_{nJ}
converges in strong cut metric to
W
, we have
\|W_{nJ}\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}\to\|W\|_{L_{1}[0,1]^{2}}
, and thus the right-hand side is bounded. This proves that
\inf_{n}M_{n}^{\psi}/n>-\infty
.
We now prove the upper and lower bounds in (
4.13
) separately.

Proof of the upper bound in (
4.13
)
:
Let
Q^{n}=Q^{n}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{n}_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be any near-optimizer of
M_{n}^{\psi}(\cdot)
, meaning

(4.15)

M_{n}^{\psi}(Q^{n})\geq M_{n}^{\psi}-o(n).

Note that
M_{n}^{\psi}(Q^{n})/n
is bounded from below by some constant
C
, as shown just above. Since
J
has nonnegative entries and
\psi\leq 0
, we have
T_{W_{nJ},\psi}\leq 0
which implies

\displaystyle C\leq M_{n}^{\psi}(Q^{n})/n=T_{W_{nJ},\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))-I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\leq-I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n})).

This implies
\sup_{n}I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))<\infty
. Since the sub-level sets of
I
are weakly compact, the sequence
(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))
has a limit point. Let
\mu_{\infty}
be any limit point. Lower semicontinuity of
I(\cdot)
gives
I(\mu_{\infty})<\infty
. For each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
\psi_{m}(x,y)\coloneqq\psi(x,y)1_{\{|x|,|y|\leq m\}}
. Note that
\psi\leq\psi_{m}\leq 0
, and thus
T_{W_{nJ},\psi}\leq T_{W_{nJ},\psi_{m}}
. By part (1) of Lemma
4.2
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})}\Big|T_{W_{nJ},\psi_{m}}(\mu)-T_{W,\psi_{m}}(\mu)\Big|\to 0,

for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore, for all
m
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{W_{nJ},\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{W_{nJ},\psi_{m}}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{W,\psi_{m}}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))=T_{W,\psi_{m}}(\mu_{\infty}),

where the last step uses part (2) of Lemma
4.2
. The left-hand side above does not depend on
m
, and thus

\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{W_{nJ},\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\leq\inf_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{W,\psi_{m}}(\mu_{\infty})=T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{\infty}),

where the last equality follows from the monotone convergence theorem and the fact that
\psi_{m}\downarrow\psi
pointwise.
Using the lower semicontinuity of
I
, we deduce

\displaystyle T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{\infty})-I(\mu_{\infty})

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(T_{W_{nJ},\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))-I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\big)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}M_{n}^{\psi}(Q^{n})/n.

Bound the left-hand side by a supremum to prove the upper bound in (
4.13
). Moreover, once we prove (
4.13
), then this argument shows the following: for any near-optimizing sequence
Q^{n}={\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
in the sense of (
4.15
), the sequence
\{\mu_{n}(Q^{n})\}
is tight, and for any limit point
\mu_{\infty}
of
\{\mu_{n}(Q^{n})\}
it holds that
\mu_{\infty}
is an optimizer for the right-hand side of (
4.13
).

Proof of the lower bound in (
4.13
)
:
To prove the lower bound in (
4.13
), we first claim that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})}\big(T_{W,\psi}(\mu)-I(\mu)\big)=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}),\,\text{compact support}}\big(T_{W,\psi}(\mu)-I(\mu)\big).

The inequality (
\geq
) is obvious.
To prove the reverse, let
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
such that
I(\mu)<\infty
, and define
\mu^{m}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
with compact support by setting
d\mu^{m}/d\mu=1_{[0,1]\times[-m,m]}/\mu([0,1]\times[-m,m])
, which is well defined for large enough
m
. Then

\displaystyle I(\mu_{m})

\displaystyle=H(\mu^{m}\,|\,\overline{\mu})=\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\log\frac{d\mu^{m}}{d\overline{\mu}}\,d\mu^{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\log\frac{d\mu^{m}}{d\mu}\,d\mu^{m}+\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\log\frac{d\mu}{d\overline{\mu}}\,d\mu^{m}.

The second term converges to
I(\mu)
by dominated convergence. The first term equals
-\log\mu([0,1]\times[-m,m])
and vanishes as
m\to\infty
. Finally, since
W\geq 0
and
\psi\leq 0
, it is straightforward to check by monotone convergence that
T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{m})\to T_{W,\psi}(\mu)
, and thus
T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{m})-I(\mu_{m})\to T_{W,\psi}(\mu)-I(\mu)
as
m\to\infty
. This proves (
4.16
).

Now, to prove the lower bound in (
4.13
), we let
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
with compact support and with
I(\mu)<\infty
, and note that necessarily
\mu\ll\overline{\mu}
.
By defining
h(\cdot,\cdot):=\frac{d\mu}{d\overline{\mu}}
, we have
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}h(u,\cdot)d\rho=1
for a.e.
u\in[0,1]
since both
\mu
and
\overline{\mu}
have uniform first marginal. For each
i\in[n]
, define
h^{n}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty)
by

h^{n}_{i}(x)\coloneqq n\int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}h(u,x)du.

By Fubini’s theorem,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}h^{n}_{i}\,d\rho=1
for all
i
. We may thus define
Q^{n}=Q^{n}_{1}\times\cdots\times Q^{n}_{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by setting

\frac{dQ^{n}_{i}}{d\rho}=h^{n}_{i}
, and define
\mu_{n}(Q^{n})
as before; note for later use the key identity
\frac{d\mu_{n}(Q^{n})}{d\overline{\mu}}(u,x)=h_{\lceil nu\rceil}^{n}(x)
. If
\mathcal{K}
denotes a compact interval such that
[0,1]\times\mathcal{K}
contains the support of
\mu
, then
[0,1]\times\mathcal{K}
also contains the support of
\mu_{n}(Q^{n})
, and we may replace
\psi
by
\psi 1_{\mathcal{K}^{2}}
in the following argument.
Recalling the formula (
4.14
) for
M_{n}^{\psi}(Q)
, we may use part (1) of Lemma
4.2
to get

(4.17)

\displaystyle T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))-\frac{1}{n}M_{n}^{\psi}(Q^{n})-I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\to 0.

To complete the proof of the lower bound, we will show that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{W,\psi}(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))=T_{W,\psi}(\mu),\quad\text{and}\quad I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\leq I(\mu),\ \forall n.

Once (
4.18
) is established, it will follow from the lower semicontinuity of
I
that
I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))\to I(\mu)
, and we use (
4.17
) to deduce

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}M_{n}^{\psi}/n\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}^{\psi}(Q^{n})/n=T_{W,\psi}(\mu)-I(\mu).

This holds for every
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
of compact support satisfying
I(\mu)<\infty
. Hence, taking the supremum and recalling (
4.16
) yields the desired lower bound in (
4.13
).

It remains to prove (
4.18
). Note that

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\Big|\frac{d\mu}{d\overline{\mu}}-\frac{d\mu_{n}(Q^{n})}{d\overline{\mu}}\Big|\,d\overline{\mu}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|h(u,x)-h^{n}_{\lceil nu\rceil}(x)|\,\rho(dx)du

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\mu}}\big|h(U,X)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\mu}}[h(U,X)|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}]\big|,

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by
(\lceil nU\rceil,X)
. The right-hand side converges to
0
by Levy’s upwards convergence theorem, since
{\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\mu}}|h(U,X)|=1<\infty
. Thus the probability measure
\mu_{n}(Q^{n})
converges in total variation to
\mu
, and the first claim in (
4.18
) follows from part (2) of Lemma
4.2
. To prove the second claim in (
4.18
), use convexity of
\varphi(x)\coloneqq x\log x
for
x\geq 0
, along with Jensen’s inequality, to get

\displaystyle I(\mu_{n}(Q^{n}))

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\mu}}\varphi({\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\mu}}[h(U,X)|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}])\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\mu}}\varphi(h(U,X))=I(\mu).

This proves (
4.18
), completing the proof of the lower bound, and thus Theorem
2.8
(1). ∎

Proof of Theorem
2.8
(
2
).

We first discuss the optimization problem. The functional to be optimized can be written as

\Phi(\mu):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}W(u,v)K(x-y)\mu(du,dx)\mu(dv,dy)-\int_{0}^{1}H(\mu_{u}\,|\,\rho)\,du

We will show the existence of an optimizer via the weak upper semicontinuity: Since
W\geq 0
and
K\leq 0
, monotone convergence yields

\displaystyle 2T_{W,\widetilde{K}}(\mu)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\otimes 2}}[W(U_{1},U_{2})K(X_{1}-X_{2})]=\inf_{m>0}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\otimes 2}}\left[\big(W(U_{1},U_{2})K(X_{1}-X_{2})\big)\vee(-m)\right].

For each
m
, the expectation appearing on the right-hand side is continuous as a function of
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
, by Lemma
4.3
. Hence, the left-hand side is upper semicontinuous. Since relative entropy is lower semicontinuous with compact sub-level sets, the existence of an optimizer follows.

We prove uniqueness of the optimizer via displacement convexity. Letting
\widetilde{K}(x,y)=(1/2)K(x-y)
, we may rewrite
\Phi(\mu)=\Phi_{1}(\mu)+T_{W,\widetilde{K}}(\mu)-\Phi_{2}(\mu)
, where we define

\Phi_{1}(\mu):=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}V(x)\,\mu_{u}(dx)du,\qquad\Phi_{2}(\mu):=\int_{0}^{1}H(\mu_{u})\,du,

where we used the simple identity
H(\nu\,|\,\rho)=H(\nu)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}V\,d\nu
.
Let
\mu^{0},\mu^{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
be two optimizers, written in disintegrated form as
du\mu^{i}_{u}(dx)
for
i=0,1
.
Let
F^{i}_{u}(x)=\mu^{u}_{i}(-\infty,x]
denote the CDF, with generalized inverse
\overline{F}^{i}_{u}(y):=\inf\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}:y\leq F^{i}_{u}(x)\}
. Then, for each
u\in[0,1]
,
T_{u}(x):=\overline{F}^{1}_{u}(F^{0}_{u}(x))
denotes the unique nondecreasing function with
\mu^{0}_{u}\circ T_{u}^{-1}=\mu^{1}_{u}
. Since
F^{i}_{u}(x)
is right-continuous in
x
and measurable in
u
, it is jointly measurable in
(u,x)
, and the same is easily seen to be true for
\overline{F}^{i}_{u}(x)
and thus
T_{u}(x)
. Consider the map
\overline{T}:[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}
given by
\overline{T}(u,x)=(u,T_{u}(x))
.
Define the interpolation
\mu^{t}:=\mu^{0}\circ((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+t\overline{T})^{-1}
for each
t\in[0,1]
. Then we have

\displaystyle T_{W,\widetilde{K}}(\mu^{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}W(u,v)K(x-y)\mu^{t}(du,dx)\mu^{t}(dv,dy)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}W(u,v)K\big((1-t)(x-y)+t(T_{u}(x)-T_{u}(y))\big)\mu^{0}(du,dx)\mu^{0}(dv,dy).

Since
K
is concave and
W\geq 0
,
t\mapsto T_{W,\widetilde{K}}(\mu^{t})
is concave. Note also that

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(\mu^{t})=\int_{0}^{1}H\big(\mu^{0}_{u}\circ((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+tT_{u})^{-1}\big)\,du

is a convex function of
t
, by the displacement convexity of entropy
[
51
, Theorem 5.15(i)]
. By the
\kappa
-concavity of
V
, the function
t\mapsto\Phi_{1}(\mu^{t})
is strictly concave, and we find that
t\mapsto\Phi(\mu^{t})
is strictly concave. Since
\mu^{0}
and
\mu^{1}
are both optimizers, we have
\Phi(\mu^{0})=\Phi(\mu^{1})
. Hence, we must have
\mu^{0}=\mu^{1}
, as otherwise the strict concavity would be contradicted.

With existence and uniqueness of the optimizer settled, we lastly prove the claim (
2.4
) in part (
2
) of Theorem
2.8
.
Note that Theorem
1.1
implies uniqueness of the optimizer
Q^{n}
in
\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}M_{n}(Q)
for each
n
.
Since
Q^{n}
is optimal and thus a fortiori near-optimal, we may use the following fact proven in the course of proving the upper bound in Theorem
2.8
(1): The sequence
\{\mu_{n}(Q^{n})\}
is tight (since
Q^{n}
is), and any limit point is an optimizer for the right-hand side of (
4.13
). We have just shown the latter optimizer to be unique, and let us denote it
\mu^{*}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}}([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})
. Thus,
\mu_{n}(Q^{n})\to\mu^{*}
weakly.
From part (1) and Corollary
1.2
, for any bounded 1-Lipschitz function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{P}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(X_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{n}}[\varphi(X_{i})]\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Note that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{n}}[\varphi(X_{i})]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi(x)\,Q^{n}_{i}(dx)=\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi(x)\,\mu_{n}(Q^{n})(du,dx).

Using the weak convergence
\mu_{n}(Q^{n})\to\mu^{*}
, the right-hand side converges to

\int_{[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi(x)\mu^{*}(du,dx)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\,dR^{*},\ \ \text{where }R^{*}:=\int_{0}^{1}\mu^{*}_{u}\,du.

We deduce that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(X_{i})\to\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi\,dR^{*}
in probability for each bounded Lipschitz
\varphi
. This is enough to deduce the convergence in distribution
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}\to R^{*}
. ∎
