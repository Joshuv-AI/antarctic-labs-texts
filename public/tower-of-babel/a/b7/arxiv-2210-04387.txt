Discrete breathers of nonlinear dimer lattices: bridging the anti-continuous and continuous limits
Andrew Hofstrand, Huaiyu Li, Michael I. Weinstein

Abstract
In this work, we study the dynamics of an infinite array of nonlinear dimer oscillators which are linearly coupled as in the classical model of Su, Schrieffer and Heeger (SSH). The ratio of in-cell and out-of-cell couplings of the SSH model defines distinct phases : topologically trivial and topologically non-trivial. We first consider the case of weak out-of-cell coupling, corresponding to the topologically trivial regime for linear SSH; for any prescribed isolated dimer frequency, ω b \omega_{b} , which satisfies non-resonance and non-degeneracy assumptions, we prove that there are discrete breather solutions for sufficiently small values of the out-of-cell coupling parameter. These states are 2 ​ π / ω b 2\pi/\omega_{b} - periodic in time and exponentially localized in space. We then study the global continuation with respect to this coupling parameter. We first consider the case where ω b \omega_{b} , the seeding discrete breather frequency, is in the (coupling dependent) phonon gap of the underlying linear infinite array. As the coupling is increased, the phonon gap decreases in width and tends to a point (at which the topological transition for linear SSH occurs). In this limit, the spatial scale of the discrete breather grows and its amplitude decreases, indicating the weakly nonlinear long wave regime. Asymptotic analysis shows that in this regime the discrete breather envelope is determined by a vector gap soliton of the limiting envelope equations. We use the envelope theory to describe discrete breathers for SSH- coupling parameters corresponding to topologically trivial and, by exploiting an emergent symmetry, topologically nontrivial regimes, when the spectral gap is small. Our asymptotic theory shows excellent agreement with extensive numerical simulations over a wide range of parameters. Analogous asymptotic and numerical results are obtained for the continuations from the anti-continuous regime for frequencies, ω b \omega_{b} , below the acoustic or above the optical phonon bands.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation and background

There is great current interest in the study of wave propagation through discrete and continuous periodic media, which exhibits nontrivial topological properties. While it is common for physical systems to support defect modes concentrated at points or interfaces, these modes are in general not stable against significant perturbations
of the structure. However, it has been recognized that topological characteristics in the bulk (Floquet-Bloch) band structure can give rise to modes which are robust against large (but localized) perturbations of the system. The role of band structure topology in wave physics was first recognized in the context of condensed matter physics, e.g. the integer quantum Hall effect
[
20
]
and topological insulators
[
14
]
.
The hallmark of topological materials is the presence of
topologically protected edge states. These states are localized at interfaces (line defects, facets), propagate unidirectionally and are robust against localized - even large - imperfections in the system.
Many of the topological wave phenomena observed in these contexts were subsequently realized in engineered metamaterial systems in photonics
[
13
,
28
]
, acoustics
[
26
]
, electronics
[
12
]
and elasticity
[
41
]

which, in the regime of linear phenomena, are characterized by a linear band structure. There is very wide interest in technologies based on topologically protected states due to the potential for extraordinarily robust energy and information transfer in communications and computing.

Such systems can naturally be probed in the nonlinear regime via strong excitation, and so it is of great interest to study whether topological properties
persist in the regime where nonlinear effects are present and whether perhaps different topological phenomena emerge
[
12
,
6
,
7
,
19
,
33
]
.

Among the simplest models exhibiting
topological phases
is the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) model
[
39
]
, a discrete (tight binding) model on one-dimensional lattice in which two ”atoms” per cell (dimers) are linearly and nearest-neighbor coupled.

Summary of the article and results

We study the existence and properties of
discrete breathers
, solutions of the infinite lattice nonlinear system (
6
), which are periodic in time and localized on the discrete lattice
\mathbb{Z}
. We outline the key points of this paper:

1.

Existence of discrete breathers; Theorem

Assume that the anharmonic potential in (

V(-x)=V(x)
. Let

denote a non-resonant and non-degenerate
T_{b}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{b}}-

\lambda=0

infinite dimer array, associated with (
4

for all
\lambda
sufficiently small and non-zero,
there is a unique

periodic solution

x_{n}^{B}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right]_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

with

of the globally coupled lattice equations (

This solution lies in the space

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{T_{b}}
, consisting of sequences

,
which satisfy

, and together with derivatives up to order

, are

x_{n}^{B}(t;\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\Big|^{2}dt<\infty.

Furthermore, the mapping
\lambda\mapsto\|X^{\lambda}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}_{T_{b}}}
is smooth.
For a discussion of the behavior when the non-resonance hypothesis is violated,
see in particular Remark
4
and Figure

Our proof is based on a Poincaré continuation strategy, used in the pioneering article
[
23
]
on discrete breathers. The richer structure of the building-block isolated dimer dynamical system (
4
) (anti-continuous limit) allows for richer behaviors in the global array. Finally recall that for general nonlinear autonomous dynamical systems the period of the solution varies along the continuation. Here, the symmetry condition on
V(x)
enables us to restrict our study to time-reversible solutions with fixed period. The analysis can be adapted to more general potentials,
V(x)
, by incorporating the determination of the discrete breather period as a function of
\lambda

2.

Applications of Theorem
3
.
In Section
3
we apply Theorem
3
to obtain discrete breather solutions which are continuations of two classes of solutions to the isolated dimer dynamical system (
4
): in-phase (Type I) and out-of-phase (Type II) solutions. We verify the non-resonance and non-degeneracy assumptions of Theorem

by a combination of rigorous analysis and numerical computation.

3.

Exponential spatial decay of discrete breathers, Theorem
7

The breather solutions obtained via Theorem

have the square-summable decay behavior of functions in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{T_{b}}
. Hence they are only guaranteed to decay at infinity in a mild sense. In Section

[

\lambda\to\lambda_{\star}

As

This multiple scale expansion describes discrete breathers as a bifurcation from

For Theorem
3
on the existence of discrete breathers we only require that

is bounded linear map on

. The exponential decay hypothesis on the coupling matrix

is used in Section

to prove spatial exponential decay of discrete breathers.

For
\lambda=0
there is no coupling among the
individual dimers in the array; this is the
anti-continuous limit
:

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n}^{A}=-V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{A})+\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{n}^{B}

(15)

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n}^{B}=-V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{B})+\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{n}^{A},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{0}^{A}=-V^{\prime}(x_{0}^{A})+\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{0}^{B}

(16)

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{0}^{B}=-V^{\prime}(x_{0}^{B})+\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{0}^{A}.

A general analysis of (

) requires a study of a four-dimensional phase space. In Section

we consider two classes of periodic orbits, in-phase and out-of-phase; each leads to a reduction of (

For now, we assume that
(x^{A}_{*}(t),x^{B}_{*}(t))
denotes a periodic solution of (

) of period

(frequency
\omega_{b}=2\pi/T_{b}

\lambda=0
, the infinite dimer array (

) has a breather solution

X_{*}(t)=\Big\{\cdots,0,0,\begin{pmatrix}x_{*}^{A}(t)\\
x_{*}^{B}(t)\end{pmatrix},0,0,\cdots\Big\}.

(17)

Equivalently, in terms of the mapping

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\ddot{x}_{n}^{A}+V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{A})-\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{n}^{B}\\
\ddot{x}_{n}^{B}+V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{B})-\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{n}^{A}\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\left[{\bf R}\begin{pmatrix}x^{A}\\
x^{B}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},

(18)

we have

F(X_{\star},0)=0.

Our first goal is to construct a mapping
\lambda\mapsto X^{\lambda}(t)

for all real
\lambda\neq 0

T_{b}-
periodic in time, spatially decaying sequences, such that

(19)

We now introduce a function space framework appropriate for an application of the implicit function theorem.
Let
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}
denote the Banach space of infinite sequences of

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

\displaystyle=\Big\{X(t)=\{x_{n}(t)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}:x_{n}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}T_{b}),\ X(t)=X(-t)\Big\}

(20)

endowed with the norm given by

\|X(t)\|_{{}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}}^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|x_{n}\|^{2}_{H^{2}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|\begin{pmatrix}x^{A}_{n}\\
x^{B}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\Big\|_{H^{2}}^{2},

and
\mathcal{H}^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

\displaystyle=\Big\{X(t)=\{x_{n}(t)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}:x_{n}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/T_{b}\mathbb{Z}),X(t)=X(-t)\ \textrm{a.e.}\ \Big\}

x^{B}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

we have that

F:\mathcal{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H}^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}\quad\textrm{is a $C^{1}$ mapping.}

Theorem 3
(Breathers near the anticontinuum limit).

Consider the nonlinear dimer array (

, satisfying (

(22)

(b)

Non-degeneracy: The nullspace of the operator

Remark 4
(On the non-resonance condition (
22
)).

Let
\lambda=0
. The linearization of the dimer system is given by the block-diagonal system:

\displaystyle\ddot{\eta}_{0}^{A}

\displaystyle=-V^{\prime\prime}(x_{*}^{A}(t))\eta_{0}^{B}+\gamma_{\text{in}}\eta_{0}^{B}

(24)

\displaystyle\ddot{\eta}_{0}^{B}

\displaystyle=-V^{\prime\prime}(x_{*}^{B}(t))\eta_{0}^{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}\eta_{0}^{A},\

(25)

in the
n=0
dimer and, for
n\neq 0

\displaystyle\ddot{\eta}_{n}^{A}=-V^{\prime\prime}(0)\eta_{n}^{B}+\gamma_{\text{in}}\eta_{n}^{B}

(26)

\displaystyle\ddot{\eta}_{n}^{B}=-V^{\prime\prime}(0)\eta_{n}^{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}\eta_{n}^{A}.

Each
n\neq 0
block has the four distinct frequencies,
\tilde{\omega}
, given by
the solutions of:
\tilde{\omega}^{2}=V^{\prime\prime}(0)+\gamma_{\rm in}
and

\tilde{\omega}^{2}=V^{\prime\prime}(0)-\gamma_{\rm in}
. Hence, the infinite system
has the identical four distinct frequencies, each now having infinite multiplicity,
with corresponding modes supported on distinct dimer cells. For
\lambda\neq 0
and small, these infinite multiplicity frequencies
perturb to phonon bands, intervals of spectra, corresponding to the time harmonic solutions,
\xi e^{-i\omega t},\ \xi\in l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, of

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\ddot{x}_{n}^{A}+V^{\prime\prime}(0)x_{n}^{A}-\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{n}^{B}\\
\ddot{x}_{n}^{B}+V^{\prime\prime}(0)x_{n}^{B}-\gamma_{\text{in}}x_{n}^{A}\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\left[{\bf R}\begin{pmatrix}x^{A}\\
x^{B}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

The non-resonance condition (
22
) ensures that nonlinearity-induced harmonics of the breather frequency
\omega_{b}
do not resonate
with the phonon spectrum. Such resonances are known to lead to the slow
resonant radiation damping of coherent structures; see Figure
3
, and also, for example,

[
35
,
36
,
37
,
43
]
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

The proof is based on the implicit function theorem; see, for example,
[
27
]
. Clearly we have
F(X_{*},0)=0
.
To apply the implicit function theorem we must check that differential of the mapping
F

at
(X,\lambda)=(X_{*},0)
,

F_{X}(X_{*},0):\mathcal{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}\to\mathcal{H}^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

is one to one and onto, and that the inverse is bounded.
The differential with respect to
\lambda
at any
(X,\lambda)
is

\left[\frac{\partial F(X;\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\right]_{n}=\left[{\bf R}\begin{pmatrix}x^{A}\\
x^{B}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{n}.

(28)

The differential with respect to
X
is given by the block diagonal operator

F_{X}(X_{*};0)Y=\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&&&&\\
&L_{0}&&&\\
&&L_{*}&&\\
&&&L_{0}&\\
&&&&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\
y_{-1}\\
y_{0}\\
y_{1}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},\quad y_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}T_{b};\mathbb{R}^{2}),\ j\in\mathbb{Z},

(29)

where the
2\times 2
block operators are
L_{*}
, displayed in (
23
), and

\displaystyle L_{0}=\left[\dfrac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+V^{\prime\prime}(0)\right]I_{2\times 2}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\ .

(30)

Here,
I_{2\times 2}=\sigma_{0}
denotes the
2\times 2
identity matrix.

We first claim that the operators
L_{0}
and
L_{*}
both map

H^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}\equiv\{x\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}T_{b};\mathbb{R}^{2}):\ x(-t)=x(t)\}

(31)

to

H^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}\equiv\{x\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}T_{b};\mathbb{R}^{2}):\ x(-t)=x(t)\ {\rm a.e.}\ \}.

We further claim that these maps are one to one, onto and have a bounded inverse:

\displaystyle\|L_{0}^{-1}f\|_{H^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\|f\|_{H^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}}

(32)

\displaystyle\|L_{*}^{-1}f\|_{H^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\|f\|_{H^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}},\

(33)

where
C_{0}
and
C_{*}
are constants.

The bound (
32
) on
L_{0}^{-1}
follows by an explicit Fourier series calculation using
the Non-resonance Hypothesis (
22
). Indeed, consider the equation

L_{0}y=f,\quad{\rm where}\quad f\in L^{2}

is given by a Fourier series:

\begin{pmatrix}f^{A}(t)\\
f^{B}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{f}^{n,A}\\
\hat{f}^{n,B}\end{pmatrix}e^{2n\pi it/T_{b}}.

(34)

We seek a solution

y(t)=\begin{pmatrix}y^{A}(t)\\
y^{B}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{y}^{n,A}\\
\hat{y}^{n,B}\end{pmatrix}e^{2n\pi it/T_{b}}

(35)

and find that a solution
y(t)\in H_{{}_{T_{b}}}^{2}
can be constructed for arbitrary
f\in L^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

if and only if

\text{det}\begin{pmatrix}-\left(n\omega_{b}\right)^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}(0)&-\gamma_{\text{in}}\\
-\gamma_{\text{in}}&-\left(n\omega_{b}\right)^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}(0)\end{pmatrix}\neq 0\quad\textrm{for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$}.

(36)

Equivalently,
\left((n\omega)^{2}-V^{\prime\prime}(0)\right)^{2}-\gamma_{\rm in}^{2}\neq 0
or

\left((n\omega)^{2}-V^{\prime\prime}(0)-\gamma_{\rm in}\right)\ \left((n\omega)^{2}-V^{\prime\prime}(0)+\gamma_{\rm in}\right)\neq 0,\quad\textrm{for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$}.

This is precisely the Nonresonance Hypothesis (
22
) so the proof of the bound (
32
) is complete.

The bound (
33
) on
L_{*}^{-1}
follows from
the Non-degeneracy Hypothesis; see (
23
). Indeed, the spectrum of
L_{*}
acting in
H^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}
is discrete, and non-degeneracy implies that
0\not\in{\rm spec}(L_{*})
. Hence,

\|L_{*}^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}_{T_{b}}}\leq[{\rm dist}\left(0,{\rm spec}(L_{*})\right)]^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{T_{b}}}
. Standard elliptic theory
implies the bound (
33
).

The inverse of
F_{X}(X_{*};0)
acting on sequences

Y=(\dots,y_{-1},y_{0},y_{1},\dots)\in\mathcal{H}^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

is then given by

F_{X}(X_{*};0)^{-1}Y=\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&&&&\\
&L_{0}^{-1}&&&\\
&&L_{*}^{-1}&&\\
&&&L_{0}^{-1}&\\
&&&&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\
y_{-1}\\
y_{0}\\
y_{1}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},\quad y_{j}\in H^{0}(\mathbb{Z}T_{b};\mathbb{R}^{2}),\ j\in\mathbb{Z},

(37)

which satisfies the bound

\|F_{X}(X_{*};0)^{-1}Y\|_{{}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}}}^{2}\leq C_{1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|y_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=C_{1}\|Y\|_{{}_{\mathcal{H}^{0}_{{}_{T_{b}}}}}^{2},

where
C_{1}=\max\{C_{0},C_{*}\}
. We may now apply the implicit function theorem
to obtain the existence of a curve
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{b})\mapsto X^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}
such that
X^{\lambda}=X_{*}
for
\lambda=0
and
F(X^{\lambda},\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{b})
. This completes the proof of
Theorem
3
.

The nullspaces of

{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}

The

To obtain a second, linearly independent, solution we proceed as follows. Denote

Remark 6
.

Condition (
47
) is equivalent to the condition of
[

]
on the non-degeneracy of the classical action.

{H}^{2}_{{}_{T_{b}}}
- nullspace of

Since
L_{*}
is a second-order ordinary differential operator its nullspace is at most two-dimensional. Further,

Type II states correspond to periodic solutions,
z_{*}^{(II)}(t)
, of the equation

\ddot{z}=-V^{\prime}(z)-\gamma_{\text{in}}z
;

+\gamma_{\text{in}}
in the ODE for Type I states is replaced by

-\gamma_{\text{in}}
.
The corresponding replacement of
+\gamma_{\text{in}}
by
-\gamma_{\text{in}}
in the linearized analysis shows that verifying the non-degeneracy hypothesis of Theorem
3
on the nullspace of

L_{*}
reduces to studying the nullspace of the diagonal operator

{\rm diag}(L_{*+},L_{*-})
; compare with (
44
).
Hence for Type II states, the nullspace of
L_{*}
reduces to the nullspace of the same two scalar linear operators as in the case of Type I states.
and hence the arguments presented in Section
3.2.1
can be applied to Type II states as well. We conclude that
Theorem
3

applies to all Type II states (out-of-phase periodic orbits), except at a possibly discrete set of energies,
E
. Of course, for any fixed
E
, it is easy to numerically verify the non-degeneracy hypothesis of Theorem
3
.

4
Exponential spatial localization of discrete breathers

The breather solutions constructed in Section
2

lie in a Banach with weak spatial decay.
Let
\lambda\mapsto X^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{H}
be
C^{1}
with respect to
\lambda
and solve
F(X^{\lambda},\lambda)=0,\quad\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\star})
.
Here,

X^{\lambda}=\{x_{n}(\cdot;\lambda)\},\quad x_{n}(t;\lambda)\in H^{2}_{T_{b}},\quad\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{2}_{T_{b}}}^{2}<\infty,

(48)

and
X^{0}=X_{*}
is the anticontinuum limit solution.
In this section we prove the following result on exponential spatial decay.

Theorem 7
.

Consider the setting of Theorem
3
and let
\lambda\mapsto X^{\lambda}
denote a curve of discrete breathers, defined for
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{b})
. Then, there exist constants
C_{1}>0
,
C_{2}>0
and
|\mu|<1
such that for all
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{b})

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}

\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\leq C_{1}\exp(C_{2}\lambda)\ \mu^{|n|}.

Our arguments are related to those
presented in
[
23
]
. In our proof, we reduce spatial decay to a Combes-Thomas type bound given in Proposition
8
below.

Differentiation of equation
F(X^{\lambda},\lambda)=0
(see (
18
)) yields

\frac{dX^{\lambda}}{d\lambda}=-F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial F(X^{\lambda};\lambda)}{\partial\lambda},\qquad X^{\lambda}\Big|_{\lambda=0}=X_{*}.

(49)

Written out componentwise, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\frac{dx_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\right]_{nm}\left(\frac{\partial F(X^{\lambda};\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\right)_{m}

(50)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\right]_{nm}\left({\bf R}x\right)_{m}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\right]_{nm}\sum_{l}R_{ml}x_{l}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l}\left[\sum_{m}\left[F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\right]_{nm}R_{ml}\right]\ x_{l}

\displaystyle\equiv-\sum_{l}Q_{nl}(X^{\lambda},\lambda)\ x_{l},

where we have used (
28
).
Our goal is to prove exponential decay of the sequence of norms

\Big\{\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{2}_{T_{b}}}\Big\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Since the mapping
\lambda\mapsto\{x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\}
is
C^{1}([0,\lambda_{\star});\mathcal{H}^{2})
we have

\frac{d\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}}{d\lambda}\leq\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|Q_{nl}(X^{\lambda},\lambda)\Big\|_{\mathcal{B}\left(H^{0}_{T_{b}}\right)}\cdot\big\|x_{l}(\cdot,\lambda)\big\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}

(51)

The key to estimating
\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}
from (
51
) is:

Proposition 8
.

There are constants
M>0
and
\mu_{b}\in(0,1)
such that for all
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\star})
, and all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}

\left\|\left(F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\right)_{n,m}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}\left(H^{0}_{T_{b}}\right)}<M\mu_{F}^{|n-m|}.

(52)

The proof of Proposition
8
is presented below in Section
4.1
.
Using (
52
) together with the assumed bound on
{\bf R}
in (
13
) we have:

\displaystyle\|Q_{nl}(X^{\lambda},\lambda)\|

\displaystyle=\Big\|\left(F_{X}\left(X^{\lambda};\lambda\right)^{-1}\right)_{n,m}R_{ml}\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq M\mu_{F}^{|n-m|}\times C\nu_{R}^{|m-l|}\leq MC\mu_{b}^{|n-l|},

where

0<\mu_{b}\equiv\max\{\mu_{b},\nu_{R}\}<1.

Applying
(
52
) in (
51
) we have

\frac{d\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}}{d\lambda}\leq\Big\|\frac{dx_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)}{d\lambda}\Big\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\leq M^{\prime}\ \sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{|n-m|}\|x_{m}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}},

(53)

where

J_{|n-m|}=\mu_{b}^{|m-n+1|}+\mu_{b}^{|m-n-1|}.

(54)

Recall that
0<\mu_{b}<1
.
We shall use (
53
) to show that for any
\mu
such that
\mu_{b}<\mu<1
, the sequence

\{w_{n}(\lambda)\}\equiv\Big\{\mu^{-|n|}\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\Big\}

is uniformly bounded for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Since,
a priori
, we only know that
\{\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\}
is square summable we work with a cut-off sequence. For each
N\geq 1
, define

\{w^{(N)}_{n}(\lambda)\}\equiv\Big\{[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\Big\}

where

\rho^{(N)}(n)=\begin{cases}\mu^{|n|}&|n|<N\\
\mu^{N}&|n|\geq N\end{cases}

(55)

Multiplying (
53
) by

[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}
we obtain

\frac{dw^{(N)}_{n}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}\leq\ M^{\prime}\ \sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}J_{|n-m|}\rho^{(N)}(m)w^{(N)}_{m}(\lambda),

(56)

where
w^{(N)}_{n}(0)=[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}\|x_{*,n}\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}
.
One checks easily, using the form of (
54

0<\mu_{b}<\mu<1
, that there is a constant,
C_{J}>0
, which is independent of
N
, such that:

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\quad\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}J_{|n-m|}\rho^{(N)}(m)\leq\ C_{J}.

Integrate (
56
) with respect to
\lambda
over the interval
[0,\lambda)
,
\lambda<\lambda_{\star}
, to obtain

w^{(N)}_{n}(\lambda)\leq[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}\|x_{*,n}\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}+M^{\prime}\ C_{J}\ \int_{0}^{\lambda}\sup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}w^{(N)}_{m}(\lambda^{\prime})d\lambda^{\prime}

Finally, let
[w^{(N)}](\lambda)\equiv\sup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}w^{(N)}_{m}(\lambda)

and note that for all

[w^{(N)}](0)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}[\rho^{(N)}(n)]^{-1}\ \|x_{*,n}\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu^{-|n|}\|x_{*,n}\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\equiv c_{*}<\infty.

Then,

It follows from Gronwall’s inequality that

[w^{(N)}](\lambda)\leq c_{*}\exp(M^{\prime}C_{J}\lambda),\quad\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\star}).

(57)

Since

is arbitrary and the right hand side of (

we have that

\|x_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{0}_{T_{b}}}\leq c_{*}\exp(M^{\prime}C_{J}\lambda)\ \mu^{|n|},\ n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\star}).

This completes the proof of exponential decay, modulo Proposition

in the following subsection.

4.1
Proof of Proposition

We prove the bound (

for the Combes-Thomas estimate in
[

.

From expression for

given in (

\displaystyle F_{X}(X(\lambda),\lambda)

\displaystyle={\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda{\bf R}

where

{\bf\Delta}(\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\ddots&&&&&&\\
&0&L^{(-1)}(\lambda)&0&&&\\
&&0&L^{(0)}(\lambda)&0&&\\
&&&0&L^{(1)}(\lambda)&0&\\
&&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\ ,

L^{(j)}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+V^{\prime\prime}(x_{j}^{A}(t,\lambda))&0\\

1&0\end{pmatrix},

(59)

and

\displaystyle{\bf R}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\ddots&&&&&&\\
&R^{\top}&0&R&&&\\
&&R^{\top}&0&R&&\\
&&&R^{\top}&0&R&\\
&&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\ ,

(60)

with

Note that
{\bf\Delta}(0)=F_{X}(X_{\star};0)
is invertible by non-resonance and non-degeneracy hypotheses of Theorem
3
:

\|{\bf\Delta}(0)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}_{T_{b}}\to\mathcal{H}^{0}_{T_{b}}}\leq\frac{1}{c_{gap}}.

By smoothness of

and
\lambda\mapsto X^{\lambda}
we have, for some

, that
{\bf\Delta}(\lambda)
has a bounded inverse

We shall study decay of the
(m,n)
matrix element of
F_{X}(X(\lambda),\lambda)^{-1}
by studying
the solution of the equation

\left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda{\bf R}\right)u=v

in an exponentially weighted space. Fix

and define
f(m)=\eta|m-n|
, for all

, where

is to be chosen.
We denote by

\mathcal{H}^{0}_{T_{b}}
given

\{\alpha_{m}\}\mapsto\{e^{f(m)}\alpha_{m}\}

Therefore,

e^{f}\left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda{\bf R}\right)^{-1}v\ =\ \left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda\ e^{f}{\bf R}e^{-f}\right)^{-1}\ e^{f}v.

Coordinatewise, we have

e^{f(m)}[\left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda{\bf R}\right)^{-1}]_{{}_{mj}}v_{j}\ =\ [\left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda\ e^{f}{\bf R}e^{-f}\right)^{-1}]_{{}_{mj}}\ e^{f(j)}v_{j},

where we sum over repeated indices.

Now take

, whose only non-zero entry is a one in the

slot. Then,

e^{f(m)}[\left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda{\bf R}\right)^{-1}]_{{}_{mn}}=[\left({\bf\Delta}(\lambda)-\lambda\ e^{f}{\bf R}e^{-f}\right)^{-1}]_{{}_{mn}}

\displaystyle\Big\|\lambda\ {\bf\Delta}(\lambda)^{-1}\ e^{f}{\bf R}e^{-f}\Big\|\

with norm in the space

5
Analysis of the weakly nonlinear long wave regime - continuum theory

From Figure
2
(b) and (c) we have that, as
\lambda
tends to
\lambda_{\star}
: the maximum amplitude of the discrete breather decreases toward zero
and its spatial width becomes large on the scale of the lattice-spacing.
To describe this behavior precisely, we use a multiple scale analysis
designed to capture the weakly nonlinear long wave regime. To carry this out it is useful making a suitable rescaling and recentering of (
5
).

Introducing the natural small parameter

Since we are interested in the regime where the band gap width tends toward zero, we introduce the parameter:

\epsilon\equiv\gamma_{\text{in}}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}

(66)

which tends to zero as the phonon gap width tends to zero at
\lambda_{\star}=|\gamma_{\rm in}/\gamma_{\rm out}|
,
and we rewrite (
5
) as

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n}^{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}x_{n}^{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}(x_{n}^{B}+x_{n-1}^{B})+(V^{\prime\prime}(0)x_{n}^{A}-V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{A}))-\epsilon x_{n-1}^{B}

(67)

\displaystyle\ddot{x}_{n}^{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}x_{n}^{B}+\gamma_{\text{in}}(x_{n}^{A}+x_{n+1}^{A})+(V^{\prime\prime}(0)x_{n}^{B}-V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{B}))-\epsilon x_{n+1}^{A}.

Here, we set
\omega_{0}^{2}\equiv V^{\prime\prime}(0)
. The frequency
\omega_{0}
is at the center of the phonon gap. We study breather solutions whose frequency
\omega_{b}
lies within the order
\epsilon
width gap and we express this as:

\omega_{b}=\omega_{0}-\frac{\epsilon\nu}{2\omega_{0}}.

(68)

The order one parameter,
\nu
, in (
68
) determines the offset from
\omega_{0}

within the
\epsilon-
width gap .

Balancing weak nonlinearity with linear (phonon) dispersion

Since
V(z)
is assumed to be smooth with
V^{\prime}(-z)=-V^{\prime}(z)
, and
V^{\prime}(0)=0
, the
leading order nonlinearity is cubic;

V^{\prime}(z)=V^{\prime\prime}(0)z+\frac{1}{6}V^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(0)z^{3}+\dots
.
To fix an example we choose the leading order behavior:
V(z)=3z+\Gamma z^{3}+\dots
; see (
3
).

Let us assume that
\epsilon=\gamma_{\rm in}-\lambda\gamma_{\rm out}
is strictly positive and small; we shall comment on the case where
\epsilon
is negative and small below. In order to balance the linear phonon dispersion with nonlinearity we rescale the amplitude:

\begin{pmatrix}x_{n}^{A}\\
x_{n}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\begin{pmatrix}y_{n}^{A}\\
y_{n}^{B}\end{pmatrix},

(69)

and we obtain:

\displaystyle\ddot{y}_{n}^{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}y_{n}^{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}(y_{n}^{B}+y_{n-1}^{B})-\epsilon\Gamma(y_{n}^{A})^{3}-\epsilon y_{n-1}^{B}

(70)

\displaystyle\ddot{y}_{n}^{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}y_{n}^{B}+\gamma_{\text{in}}(y_{n}^{A}+y_{n+1}^{A})-\epsilon\Gamma(y_{n}^{B})^{3}-\epsilon y_{n+1}^{A},

where we have dropped terms of order
\epsilon^{2}
and higher.

In terms of our parameter
\epsilon
, defined in (
66
), the two band functions (
7
) may be re-expressed as:

(\omega^{2})_{\pm}(k)-\omega_{0}^{2}=\pm\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+4\gamma_{\rm in}(\gamma_{\rm in}-\epsilon)\cos^{2}\left({k\over 2}\right)},\quad\omega_{0}^{2}\equiv V^{\prime\prime}(0).

(71)

As noted in the Introduction, for
\epsilon\neq 0
, there are two disconnected intervals of spectrum,.
The interval of spectrum associated with the
+
branch is called the
optical band
and that associated with the
-
branch is called the
acoustic band
. The open interval of energies lying between these bands is called the phonon gap; see Figure
1
b
with
E=\omega^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}
.
The maximum and minimum of
(\omega^{2})_{\pm}(k)-\omega_{0}^{2}
(top of the optical band) occur for
k=0
, and phonon gap width is at its smallest for
k=\pi
. We next carry out, for
\epsilon
small, an asymptotic study which yields
discrete breathers in three regimes

(A)

\omega_{b}^{2}
in the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
spectral gap,

(B)

\omega_{b}^{2}
below and near the minimum of the acoustic band, and

(C)

\omega_{b}^{2}
above and near the maximum of the optical band.

In all three regimes, the solution will be shown to have the structure of slow modulation of rapidly oscillatory plane wave states. Since the dispersion relation for regimes (B) and (C) is approximately quadratic, the governing envelope equations will be of (nonlinear) Schroedinger type. And since the dispersion relation for regime (A) is that of a gapped linear crossing (Dirac point),
the governing envelope equations will be of (nonlinear) massive Dirac type.

5.1
Asymptotic study of discrete breathers in Regime (A);

\omega^{2}
in the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
spectral gap

Centering the analysis near the asymptotic linear band crossing

Recall that as
\epsilon\sim 0
(
\lambda\sim\lambda_{\star}
) the spectral gap of the linear band structure is narrowest in a neighborhood of
k=\pi
and closes at quasimomentum
k=\pi
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
The asymptotic solution we seek is of the form of a wave-packet, spectrally localized at
k=\pi
and hence we set

\begin{pmatrix}y_{n}^{A}\\
y_{n}^{B}\end{pmatrix}\equiv e^{i\pi n}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{n}^{A}\\
Y_{n}^{B}\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{n}\begin{pmatrix}Y_{n}^{A}\\
Y_{n}^{B}\end{pmatrix},

(72)

and we obtain

\displaystyle\ddot{Y}_{n}^{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}Y_{n}^{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}(Y_{n}^{B}-Y_{n-1}^{B})-\epsilon\Gamma(Y_{n}^{A})^{3}+\epsilon Y_{n-1}^{B}

(73)

\displaystyle\ddot{Y}_{n}^{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}Y_{n}^{B}+\gamma_{\text{in}}(Y_{n}^{A}-Y_{n+1}^{A})-\epsilon\Gamma(Y_{n}^{B})^{3}+\epsilon Y_{n+1}^{A}.

In the next section, we embark on an asymptotic analysis construction of nonlinear standing wave states of the rescaled and recentered system (
73
).

The solutions we seek are to be spectrally concentrated on the set of momentum

q\equiv k-\pi\approx 0
over a band width of order
\epsilon
. We now deduce a continuum approximation flowing from this requirement. Using the discrete Fourier inversion formula we write, for
J=A,B
:

Y^{J}_{n\pm 1}-Y^{J}_{n}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{inq}(e^{\pm iq}-1)\chi^{J}\left({q\over\epsilon}\right)dq,

where
\chi^{J}(z)
is rapidly decaying away from
z=0
and smooth. The overall factor of
\epsilon^{-1}
ensures that
Y_{n}
is of order one for small
\epsilon

(consistent with the multiple scale expansion below). Changing variables (
q=\epsilon Q
) and using the approximation
e^{\pm i\epsilon Q}-1\approx\pm i\epsilon Q
, we have

\displaystyle Y^{J}_{n\pm 1}-Y^{J}_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\epsilon\int_{-\pi/\epsilon}^{\pi/\epsilon}e^{iQ(\epsilon n)}\left(\pm iQ\right)\chi^{J}(Q)dQ\approx\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iQ(\epsilon n)}\left(\pm iQ\right)\chi^{J}(Q)dQ

\displaystyle\equiv\pm\epsilon\partial_{Z}u_{J}(Z)|_{Z=\varepsilon n}=\pm\partial_{z}u_{J}(n),\quad Z=\epsilon z.

Here,
z
and
Z=\epsilon z
are fast and slow continuum spatial scales, which we systematically introduce below as independent variables in a multiple scale analysis.

This continuum approximation gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u_{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}u_{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}\partial_{z}u_{B}-\epsilon\Gamma u_{A}^{3}+\epsilon(u_{B}-\partial_{z}u_{B})

(74)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u_{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}u_{B}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\partial_{z}u_{A}-\epsilon\Gamma u_{B}^{3}+\epsilon(u_{A}+\partial_{z}u_{A}).

We now seek a solution to
(
74

z
and
t
, as well as newly introduced “slow” space and time variables

and
T
, all treated as independent variables:

u_{A,B}=u_{A,B}(t,z,T,Z),\quad{\rm where}\quad Z=\epsilon z,\quad T=\epsilon t

(75)

We rewrite (
74
)
in terms of the extended set of variables, by making the replacements

\partial_{t}\to\partial_{t}+\epsilon\partial_{T},\quad\partial_{z}\to\partial_{z}+\epsilon\partial_{Z}.

This gives

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\epsilon\partial_{T}\right)^{2}u_{A}=-\omega_{0}^{2}u_{A}+\gamma_{\text{in}}\left(\partial_{z}+\epsilon\partial_{Z}\right)u_{B}-\epsilon\Gamma u_{A}^{3}+\epsilon(u_{B}-\left(\partial_{z}+\epsilon\partial_{Z}\right)u_{B})

(76)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\epsilon\partial_{T}\right)^{2}u_{B}=-\omega_{0}^{2}u_{B}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\left(\partial_{z}+\epsilon\partial_{Z}\right)u_{A}-\epsilon\Gamma u_{B}^{3}+\epsilon(u_{A}+\left(\partial_{z}+\epsilon\partial_{Z}\right)u_{A}).

Further, we expand
u_{A,B}=u_{A,B}(t,z,T,Z)
in powers of
\epsilon

u_{A,B}=u_{A,B}^{(0)}(z,t,Z,T)+\epsilon u_{A,B}^{(1)}(z,t,Z,T)+\epsilon^{2}u_{A,B}^{(2)}(z,t,Z,T)+\cdots

(77)

Recall that we are in the regime of an order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)-
frequency gap around
\omega_{0}
. Since we seek breather-like (spatially localized) states
we impose the boundary condition at infinity:

u_{A,B}^{(j)}(z,t,Z,T)\to 0\quad{\rm as}\quad|Z|\to\infty,\quad j=0,1,\dots

(78)

We then substitute (
77
) into (
76

\epsilon^{j},\ j=1,2,\dots
. Each equation is of the form

\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{0}\right)U^{(j)}=F^{(j)},

(79)

where
U^{(j)}=(u_{A}^{(j)},u_{B}^{(j)})^{\top}
and

F^{(j)}=(F_{A}^{(j)},F_{B}^{(j)})^{\top}
. Here,

\mathcal{L}_{0}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\omega_{0}^{2}&\gamma_{\text{in}}\partial_{z}\\
-\gamma_{\text{in}}\partial_{z}&-\omega_{0}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(80)

We view each equation in the hierarchy (
79
) as a PDE with respect to the fast variables
z
and

6.3
Dynamical stability

In this section we discuss the linear dynamical stability of discrete breathers. In particular we study the time evolution for the linearization of the dynamical system (
5
) about representative numerically computed discrete breathers.
This dynamical system is an infinite system of coupled linear ordinary equations (ODEs) with
T_{b}=2\pi/\omega_{b}-
periodic coefficients.
Its stability/instability properties are characterized by the spectrum of the
monodromy operator
: the operator which maps an initial state to the state at time
T_{b}
. The monodromy operator is the Jacobian of the Poincaré mapping,
\partial_{u}G(u(0);\lambda)
, where the Poincaré map:
X(0)\mapsto G(u(0);\lambda)
maps an initial data vector
X(0)
for (
5
) into the solution at time
T_{b}
. The spectrum of

\partial_{u}G(u(0);\lambda)
is called the
Floquet spectrum
and points in the Floquet spectrum are called
Floquet multipliers
. A discrete breather,
X^{\lambda}
, is spectrally stable if its associated Floquet spectrum lies in the closed unit disc in
\mathbb{C}
.
Since our coupled dimer network is Hamiltonian, if

\mu
is a Floquet multiplier then so are
\bar{\mu}
,
1/\mu
and
1/\bar{\mu}
; complex Floquet multipliers come in quartets and real Floquet multipliers in reciprocal pairs. It follows that a discrete breather of the system is spectrally stable only if the Floquet spectrum is a subset of the unit circle.

If, for the purpose of numerical approximation, we truncate the linearized dynamics to a finite system with
N-
dimers, we have a system of
2N
coupled linear ODEs of second order with
T_{b}=2\pi/\omega_{b}-
periodic coefficients. Written as a system of first order ODEs, we have an equivalent system of
4N
ODEs. The Poincaré mapping is then a mapping
u(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{4N}\mapsto G^{(N)}(u(0);\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{4N}
,
and the monodromy operator is approximated by
\partial_{u}G^{(N)}(u(0);\lambda)
, a
4N\times 4N
matrix. We investigate the linear spectral stability of representive choices of numerically computed breathers by computing the eigenvalues of
\partial_{u}G^{(N)}(u(0);\lambda)
, for tractable and appropriately large values of
N
. These eigenvalues are taken as approximations to the exact Floquet spectrum associated with the
X^{\lambda}
. The choices of
N
are provided in figures.

7
Discussion and final remarks

We have studied - analytically and numerically - discrete breathers of a SSH-like network of nonlinear dimer oscillators. In particular, we have proved existence of discrete breathers (or DBs, solutions which are periodic in time and exponentially localized in space,) near the anti-continuous limit, in which our coupling parameter,
\lambda
is small and non-zero. We numerically continue branches of discrete breathers till near the critical coupling value,
\lambda_{\star}
, at which the phonon gap closes, corresponding to the topological transition in linear SSH.
In this latter limiting regime, discrete breathers have a multi-scale wave-packet structure, where the envelope of this wave-packet is characterized by long-wave envelope nonlinear envelope PDEs of nonlinear Dirac type (for DB frequencies in the phonon gap) and nonlinear Schroedinger type (for DB frequencies above or below the phonon spectrum). For discrete breather’s whose frequencies are “in-gap”, the continuum limit states are a type of gap-soliton. Our envelope theory shows excellent agreement with simulations of the discrete breathers for
\lambda
near
\lambda_{\star}
.
Moreover,
an emergent symmetry, present for the continuum (vanishing phonon gap) limit, but not
present in the discrete model enables us to construct — from DBs for
\lambda
in the topologically trivial linear regime of SSH (
\lambda-\lambda_{\star}<0
and small) — DBs in the
topologically nontrivial linear regime (
\lambda-\lambda_{\star}>0
and small).
A further consequence of our analysis is clarification of the chiral character of mid-gap discrete breathers.

This work is influenced by the agenda (for photonics, phononics, and mechanical systems) of exploring the interplay between nonlinearity and novel band structures for which, in the linear regime, there are topological phenomena; see references in Section
1.3
and those cited therein. While aspects of linear band structure topology arise in the bifurcation and continuation of nonlinear discrete breathers, a theory of topological states in nonlinear systems remains an open challenge.

Fourier Coefficient Method

A numerical method for computing discrete breathers was originally described in
[
25
]
based on solving for fixed points of a Poincaré mapping. Here we describe a Fourier-based numerical method for constructing time-reversible discrete breathers of system (
15
), in the setting of
theorem

3
.

Given that both the non-resonance and non-degeneracy conditions in
theorem

3
are satisfied, we know that there exists
\lambda_{b}>0
and a unique time-periodic and reversible, exponentially localized solution to (
15
) for
0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{b}
. To construct such solutions, we will truncate the infinite-dimensional system in space and time and iteratively solve a large system of nonlinear equations, transforming between the time- and frequency-domains.

We will employ the Newton-Raphson method to solve the system, which requires knowledge of the non-singular Jacobian of the mapping (
18
). To begin we define the Fourier series

x_{n}^{A,B}(t)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{A,B}e^{i\omega_{b}mt},\qquad\hat{x}_{n,m}^{A,B}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}x_{n}^{A,B}(t)e^{-i\omega_{b}mt}dt

where, recall,
\omega_{b}=2\pi/T_{b}
is the breather frequency. Next we define the transformed mapping for
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}

\mathcal{F}\{F(X(t),\lambda)\}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-m^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{A}+\mathcal{F}\{V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{A})\}_{m}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}\hat{x}_{n-1,m}^{B}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{B}\\
&-m^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{B}+\mathcal{F}\{{V^{\prime}(x_{n}^{B})}\}_{m}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}\hat{x}_{n+1,m}^{A}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\hat{x}_{n,m}^{A}.\end{aligned}\right.

(125)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the isometric Fourier mapping
\mathcal{F}:L^{2}_{\text{per}}[0,T_{b}]\to l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Linearizing the above system about
X(t)
in the time-domain and transforming leads to

\mathcal{F}\{F_{X}(X(t),\lambda)Y(t)\}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-m^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{A}+\mathcal{F}\{V^{\prime\prime}(x_{n}^{A})y_{n}^{A}\}_{m}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{B}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}\hat{y}_{n-1,m}^{B}\\
&-m^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{B}+\mathcal{F}\{V^{\prime\prime}(x_{n}^{B})y_{n}^{B}\}_{m}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{A}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}\hat{y}_{n+1,m}^{A}.\end{aligned}\right.

(126)

where
Y=\{y_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}T_{b};\mathbb{R}^{2})\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. The expression above can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{F_{X}(X(t),\lambda)\}*\hat{Y}(t)=\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(127)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &-m^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{A}+\left(\mathcal{F}\{V^{\prime\prime}(x_{n}^{A})\}*\hat{y}_{n}^{A}\right)_{m}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{B}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}\hat{y}_{n-1,m}^{B}\\
&-m^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{B}+\left(\mathcal{F}\{V^{\prime\prime}(x_{n}^{B})\}*\hat{y}_{n}^{B}\right)_{m}-\gamma_{\text{in}}\hat{y}_{n,m}^{A}-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}\hat{y}_{n+1,m}^{A},\end{aligned}\right.

where
*
denotes the convolution operation:
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\times l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

We now truncate the above exact expressions for
N
lattice sites with zero boundary conditions at the lattice edges, as an approximation for exponentially decaying lattice breathers. Taking
N_{t}
equally-spaced points in
[0,T_{b}]
, we use the following definition of the discrete Fourier transform

\hat{X}_{m}=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{t}-1}X_{p}e^{-i\omega_{b}p\Delta tm}=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{t}-1}X_{p}e^{-2\pi ipm/N_{t}}

(128)

and inverse

X_{p}=\dfrac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}-1}\hat{X}_{m}e^{2\pi ipm/N_{t}}.

(129)

We can write the
2NN_{t}\times 2NN_{t}
truncated block tri-diagonal Jacobian matrix of (

\hskip-5.69046pt\mathcal{F}\{F_{X}(X,\lambda)\}=\begin{pmatrix}M(x_{0}^{A})&-\gamma_{\text{in}}I_{N_{t},N_{t}}&0&\cdots&0\\
-\gamma_{\text{in}}I_{N_{t},N_{t}}&M(x_{0}^{B})&-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}I_{N_{t},N_{t}}&0&\\
0&-\lambda\gamma_{\text{out}}I_{N_{t},N_{t}}&M(x_{1}^{A})&-\gamma_{\text{in}}I_{N_{t},N_{t}}&\\

We can now utilize fast-Fourier-transform FFT libraries and evaluate the nonlinear terms in the systems above by evaluating them in the time-domain via an inverse FFT and then transforming back into the frequency-domain. Also recall that a necessary condition to invoke
