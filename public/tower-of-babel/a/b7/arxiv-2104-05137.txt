The nonlinear Quadratic Interactions of the Schrödinger type on the half-line
Isnaldo Isaac Barbosa, Márcio Cavalcante

Abstract
In this work we study the initial boundary value problem associated with the coupled Schrödinger equations with quadratic nonlinearities, that appears in nonlinear optics, on the half-line. We obtain local well-posedness for data in Sobolev spaces with low regularity, by using a forcing problem on the full line with a presence of a forcing term in order to apply the Fourier restriction method of Bourgain. The crucial point in this work is the new bilinear estimates on the classical Bourgain spaces X s , b \displaystyle X^{s,b} with b < 1 2 \displaystyle b<\frac{1}{2} , jointly with bilinear estimates in adapted Bourgain spaces that will used to treat the traces of nonlinear part of the solution. Here the understanding of the dispersion relation is the key point in these estimates, where the set of regularity depends strongly of the constant a \displaystyle a measures the scaling-diffraction magnitude indices.

In the last years the study of initial boundary value problems for nonlinear dispersive models on the half-lines has given attention of many researchers (see
[
7

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}

1.1
Presentation of the model

Consider the quadratic interactions

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{i\partial_{t}u(x,t)+p\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)+\overline{u}(x,t)v(x,t)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0};\\

{u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}\right.

Another application for the system (

In order to simulate numerically these experiments, we need some accurate models. The kinetic ones are the most relevant but very difficult to deal with practical computations. The fluids ones like bi-fluid Euler–Maxwell system seem more convenient but still inoperative in practice because of the high frequency motion and the small wavelength involved in the problem. 1.3
Setting of the problem

This work is dedicated to the study the initial boundary value problem associated to system (
1.1
) on the half-line, more precisely

(1.2)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)+\partial^{2}_{x}u(x,t)+\bar{u}(x,t)v(x,t)=0,&x\in(0,+\infty),\;t\in(0,T),\\
i\partial_{t}v(x,t)+a\partial^{2}_{x}v(x,t)+u^{2}(x,t)=0,&x\in(0,+\infty),\;t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),&x\in(0,+\infty),\\
u(0,t)=f(t),\ v(0,t)=g(t),&t\in(0,T),\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
are complex valued functions, where
\displaystyle a>0
. The model (
1.2
) is given by the nonlinear coupling of two dispersive equations of Schrödinger type through the quadratic terms

(1.3)

N_{1}(u,v)=\overline{u}\cdot v\ \mbox{ and }N_{2}(u,v)=u^{2}.

An important point in this model is the fact that the functional mass is not conserved, since some bad terms of boundary appear in the mass functional. More precisely, define the functional of mass for the system (
1.2
) by

\mathcal{M}(t)=\|u(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}+\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}.

Formally, by multiplying the first equation of the system (
1.2
) by
\displaystyle\overline{u}
and the second equation by
\displaystyle\overline{v}
, integrating by parts, taking the imaginary part and using Im
\displaystyle(\overline{u}^{2}v)=-\text{Im}(u^{2}\overline{v})
, we get

(1.4)

\mathcal{M}(t)=\mathcal{M}(0)+\text{Im}\int_{0}^{t}\overline{u}(0,s)\partial_{x}u(0,s)ds+a\text{Im}\int_{0}^{t}\overline{v}(0,s)\partial_{x}v(0,s)ds.

This identity suggesters on the case of homogeneous boundary conditions a global result on the space
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.

1.4
About the physics parameter
\displaystyle a

In this model the effect of the dispersion relation depends strongly of the values of
\displaystyle a
(where
\displaystyle 1/a
denotes the mass of particle), where this value interferes directly on the dynamic of the model. More precisely, let the following frequencies and resonances variables

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\tau=\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}&\xi=\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}&\\
\omega=\tau+\xi^{2},&\omega_{1}=\tau_{1}-\xi_{1}^{2},&\omega_{2}=\tau_{2}+a\xi_{2}^{2}.\end{array}\right.

Then we have the following dispersion relation

|\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|=|\xi^{2}+\xi_{1}^{2}-a\xi_{2}^{2}|.

•

On the case
\displaystyle 0<a<\frac{1}{2}
we have the relation
\displaystyle|\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|\geq(1-2a)(\xi^{2}+\xi_{1}^{2})
. While on the case
\displaystyle a>\frac{1}{2}
, we get
\displaystyle|\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|\geq 2|\xi-\mu_{a}\xi_{2}|\cdot|\xi-(1-\mu_{a})\xi_{2}|,\mbox{ where }\ \mu_{a}=\frac{1-\sqrt{2a-1}}{2}.
Note that in both cases we have a good relation dispersion, in the sense that it is possible to control the frequencies with the modulations. These situation on the physical context is known as the non resonant mass condition (
\displaystyle a\neq\frac{1}{2}
).

•

On the more critical case
\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{2}
we have not a good dispersion relation, with avoid a local result is a more larger region of regularity. This last situation, on the physical context is knows as the mass resonance condition.

In this context we call mass resonance condition the case
\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{2}
, while the mass nonresonance condition on the case
\displaystyle a\neq\frac{1}{2}
.

1.5
Functional spaces for the initial-boundary data

Now we discuss appropriate functional spaces for the initial and boundary data, examining again the behavior of solutions of the linear problem on the line
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
as motivation.

On the line
\displaystyle\mathbb{R},
we define the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-based inhomogeneous Sobolev spaces
\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
equipped with the norm
\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|\langle\xi\rangle^{s}\widehat{\phi}(\xi)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},
where
\displaystyle\langle\xi\rangle=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}
and
\displaystyle\hat{\phi}

denotes the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle\phi
. The operator
\displaystyle e^{iat\partial_{x}^{2}}

denotes the linear homogeneous solution group associated to the linear Schrödinger equations, respectively, posed on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}

i.e.,

\displaystyle e^{iat\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ix\xi}e^{-iat\xi^{2}}\widehat{\phi}(\xi)d\xi.

Some important time localized smoothing effects for the unitary groups
\displaystyle e^{iat\partial_{x}^{2}}
can be found in
[
19
]
. More specifically, we have the following estimate:

\left\|\psi(t)e^{iat\partial_{x}^{2}}\phi(x)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}\left(\mathbb{R}_{x};H^{(2s+1)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}_{t}\right)\right)}\leq c\|\phi\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}_{x})},

where
\displaystyle\psi(t)
is a localized smooth cutoff function. This smoothing effect suggests that for data
\displaystyle(u_{0},v_{0},f,g)
in the IBVP (
1.2
) is natural to consider the following hypothesis: we put

\mathcal{H}_{+}^{\kappa,s}:=H^{\kappa}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{x}\right)\times H^{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{x}\right)\times H^{(2\kappa+1)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{t}\right)\times H^{(2s+1)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{t}\right).

We fix a cutoff function
\displaystyle\psi
in
\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{0}
such that
\displaystyle 0\leq\psi(t)\leq 1,

(1.6)

\psi(t)=\begin{cases}1,\ \ \mbox{ if }\ \ |t|\leq 1,\\
0,\ \ \mbox{ if }\ \ |t|\geq 2\end{cases}

and
\displaystyle\psi_{T}(t)=\psi\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)
.

As far as we know, the local well-posedness for the system (
1.2
), on the half-line, was never considered previously.

Main Results

Our main local well-posedness result is the following statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the Sobolev index pair
\displaystyle(\kappa,s)

\displaystyle s\neq\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\kappa\neq\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle|\kappa|-1/2\leq s<\min\{\kappa+1/2,\ 2\kappa+1/2,1\}\;\text{and}\ \kappa<1\ \ \text{for}\;a>\frac{1}{2}

(ii)

\displaystyle 0\leq\kappa=s<1\;for\;a=\frac{1}{2}\ (\text{resonant\ case});

\displaystyle\max\{-\frac{1}{2},|\kappa|-1\}\leq s<\min\{\kappa+1,\ 2\kappa+1,1\}\;\text{and}\ \kappa<1\ \text{for}\;0<a<\frac{1}{2}

For any
\displaystyle a>0
and
\displaystyle(u_{0},v_{0})\in H^{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{+})

and

\widetilde{u}(0,t)=f(t)\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{v}(0,t)=g(t)

2
Preliminary results

A important point in the approach used here is the understanding of the how capture the dispersive effect smoothing caused by the nonlinear part of a dispersive equation. To illustrate this situation we consider the Cauchy problem of the form

(2.1)

i\partial_{t}u(x,t)+ia\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)=F(u(x,t)),

where
\displaystyle F
is a nonlinear function.

The Cauchy Problem for (
2.1
) is rewritten as the following integral equation

(2.2)

u(t)=U_{a}(t)u_{0}-i\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}U_{a}(t-t^{\prime})F(u(t^{\prime}))dt^{\prime},

where
\displaystyle U_{a}(t)=e^{-ita\partial_{x}^{2}}
is the group that solves the linear part of (
2.1
).

There are a number of ways to capture this dispersive smoothing effect, but one particularly convenient way is via the
\displaystyle X^{s,b}
spaces (also known as Fourier restriction spaces, Bourgain spaces, or dispersive Sobolev spaces), introduced by Bourgain in
[
3
]
. The full name of these
is
\displaystyle X_{\tau=a\xi^{2}}^{s,b}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\right),
thus these spaces take
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
as their domain and are adapted to a single characteristic hypersurface
\displaystyle\tau=a\xi^{2}.
Roughly speaking, these spaces are to dispersive equations as Sobolev spaces are to elliptic equations (fore more details see
[
21
]
and
[
26
]
).

Let
\displaystyle X_{a}^{s,b}
be the completion of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with respect to the norm

(2.3)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\|f\right\|_{X_{a}^{s,b}}&=\left\|\langle\xi\rangle^{s}\langle\tau+a\xi^{2}\rangle^{b}\widehat{f}(\tau,\xi)\right\|_{L^{2}_{\tau}L^{2}_{\xi}}.\end{array}

The following lemma was proved while establishing the local well-posedness of the Zakharov system by Ginibre, Tsutsumi and Velo in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}<b^{\prime}\leq 0\leq b\leq b^{\prime}+1
,
\displaystyle\psi
a cutoff function and
\displaystyle T\in[0,1]
. Then for
\displaystyle F\in X_{a}^{s,b^{\prime}}
we have

(2.4)

\left\|\psi_{1}(t)U_{a}(t)u_{0}\right\|_{X_{a}^{s,b}}\leq c\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{s}},

(2.5)

\left\|\psi_{T}(t)\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}U_{a}(t-t^{\prime})F(u(t^{\prime}))dt^{\prime}\right\|_{X_{a}^{s,b}}\leq cT^{1+b^{\prime}-b}\left\|F\right\|_{X_{a}^{s,b^{\prime}}}.

2.2
Riemann-Liouville fractional integral operator

For Re
\displaystyle\alpha>0,
the tempered distribution
\displaystyle\frac{t_{+}^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
is defined as a locally integrable function by

\left\langle\frac{t_{+}^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)},f\right\rangle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\alpha-1}f(t){d}t.

For Re
\displaystyle\alpha>0,
integration by parts implies that

\frac{t_{+}^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=\partial_{t}^{k}\left(\frac{t_{+}^{\alpha+k-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha+k)}\right)

for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}.
This expression allows to extend the definition, in the sense of distributions, of
\displaystyle\frac{t_{+}^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
to all
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}

If
\displaystyle f\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}\right),
we define

\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}f=\frac{t_{+}^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}*f

Thus, for Re
\displaystyle\alpha>0

\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}f(s)\mathrm{d}s

and notice that

\mathcal{I}_{0}f=f,\quad\mathcal{I}_{1}f(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\mathrm{d}s,\quad\mathcal{I}_{-1}f=f^{\prime}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{I}_{n}\mathcal{I}_{\beta}=\mathcal{I}_{\alpha+\beta}.

We now introduce the Duhamel boundary forcing operator similar to the introduced in

\displaystyle f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}f(x,t)

Thus, for

4
The Duhamel boundary forcing operator classes associated to linear Schrödinger equation

In order to get our results in a larger class of index regularity, we need to work with a class of boundary forcing operators in order to obtain the required estimates for the second-order derivative of traces. In this way, we define the generalization of operators
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}
.

For
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that Re
\displaystyle\lambda>-2
and
\displaystyle f\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}\right)
define

\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(x,t)=\left[\frac{x_{-}^{\lambda-1}}{\Gamma(\lambda)}*\mathcal{L}_{a}\left(\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f\right)(\cdot,t)\right](x)

with
\displaystyle\frac{x_{-}^{\lambda-1}}{\Gamma(\lambda)}=\frac{(-x)_{+}^{\lambda-1}}{\Gamma(\lambda)}.

These definition implies

(4.1)

\left(i\partial_{t}+a\partial_{x}^{2}\right)\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(x,t)=\frac{C}{\Gamma(\lambda)}x_{-}^{\lambda-1}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f(t).

If Re
\displaystyle\lambda>0,
then

(4.2)

\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(x,t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\int_{x}^{+\infty}(y-x)^{\lambda-1}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f\right)(y,t)dy.

For Re
\displaystyle\lambda>-2,
using (
4.1
) we obtain

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda+2)}\int_{x}^{+\infty}(y-x)^{\lambda+1}\partial_{y}^{2}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\Lambda}{2}}f\right)(y,t)dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{a}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\frac{(y-x)^{\lambda+1}}{\Gamma(\lambda+2)}\left(i\partial_{t}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f\right)(y,t)dy+\frac{C}{a}\frac{x_{-}^{\lambda+1}}{\Gamma(\lambda+2)}\mathcal{I}_{-1/2-\lambda/2}f(t)

Notice that
\displaystyle\left.\frac{x_{\pm}^{\lambda-1}}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\right|_{\lambda=0}=\delta_{0},
then
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{0}f(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{a}f(x,t)
.

From Lemma
4.3
it follows that
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(x,t)
is well defined for
\displaystyle\lambda>-2
for
\displaystyle t\in[0,1]
Moreover, the dominated convergence theorem and Lemma 3.2 imply that, for fixed
\displaystyle t\in[0,1]
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1,
the function
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}f(x,t)
is continuous in
\displaystyle x
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
. The following result establishes the values of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\pm}^{\lambda}f(x,t)
at
\displaystyle x=0

Lemma 4.1
.

If Re
\displaystyle\lambda>-1
and
\displaystyle f\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}\right),
then
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(0,t)=\sqrt{a}e^{i\frac{\lambda\pi}{4}}f(t)
.

Proof.

By using (
4.3
), we have that

\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(0,t)=-\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{y^{\lambda+1}}{\Gamma(\lambda+2)}i\partial_{t}\mathcal{L}_{a}\left(\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f\right)(y,t)dy.

By complex differentiation under the integral sign, we have that
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(0,t)
is analytic
in
\displaystyle\lambda,
for
\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1.
By analyticity, we shall only compute
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(0,t)
for
\displaystyle 0<\lambda<2
.
For the computation in the range
\displaystyle 0<\lambda<2,
we use the representation (
4.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\lambda}f(0,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{\lambda-1}\mathcal{L}_{a}\left(\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f\right)(y,t)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{\lambda-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{iy^{2}}{4a\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)}}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}f\left(t^{\prime}\right)dt^{\prime}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda)}\int_{0}^{t}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}}f\left(t^{\prime}\right)\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{\lambda-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{\frac{iy^{2}}{4a(t-t^{\prime})}}dydt^{\prime}

Set
\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{\lambda-1}e^{\frac{iy^{2}}{4a(t-t)}}dy.
Changing variables
\displaystyle r=\frac{y^{2}}{4a\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)},
then
\displaystyle y=r^{\frac{1}{2}}2\sqrt{a}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and

we get

I=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[r^{\frac{1}{2}}2\sqrt{a}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{\lambda-1}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{ir}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}dr=2^{\lambda-1}\sqrt{a}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{\frac{\lambda}{2}-1}e^{ir}dr

By a change of contour,

I=2^{\lambda-1}\sqrt{a}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}i^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{\frac{\lambda}{2}-1}e^{-r}dr=2^{\lambda-1}\sqrt{a}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}i^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right),\text{ for }\lambda\in(0,2)

Using the formula

\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(\lambda)}=\frac{2^{1-\lambda}\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}

for

we obtain

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}i^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}f\left(t^{\prime}\right)dt^{\prime}=i^{\lambda/2}\sqrt{a}\mathcal{I}_{\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{-\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}f(t)=\sqrt{a}e^{i\frac{3\lambda\pi}{4}}f(t).
