Effective Viscosity of a Suspension of Hot Particles
Osher Arbib, Naomi Oppenheimer

Abstract
Active particles with a temperature distribution, “hot particles”, have a distinct effect on the fluid that surrounds them. The temperature gradients they create deem the fluid’s viscosity spatially dependent, therefore violating the linearity of the problem, making a full description of velocity and pressure fields challenging. Using energy dissipation analysis and Lorentz Reciprocal Theorem, we show that it is still possible to study global properties of such hot suspensions. Namely, we calculate the effective stress tensor and effective viscosity of a dilute hot suspension, adding a correction that includes contributions from the bulk fluid and the particles themselves. As examples of this method, we derive the effective stress and viscosity of a suspension of spherical particles with different heat distributions. We show that when the particles are non-Brownian and are all oriented along the same direction, the viscosity is no longer isotropic and depends on the direction of shear relative to the orientation of the particles. In such cases, the fluid is also non-Newtonian. If the particles’ orientation is fixed due to an external field, the stress tensor is no longer symmetric, and the viscosity has odd components.

Introduction

The viscosity,
\eta

A common way to measure the viscosity is to place a fluid between two walls,
hold the bottom fixed and move the upper surface with a constant velocity.
The force density required to move the

When a bare fluid undergoes shear, it experiences a certain stress. If the fluid is not bare but includes a volume fraction

(1)

Specifically, for a rigid spherical particle with a radius

We extend the work presented in

{\bf A}{:}{\bf B}=A_{ij}B_{ji}

(5)

II
Background

Let us first provide the foundational terminology necessary for describing a suspension of particles under conditions where inertial effects are negligible. Specifically, we consider a fluid undergoing steady, laminar shear. We then go on to outline Lorentz Reciprocal theorem for a hot suspension and the temperature and viscosity distribution around hot particles. We denote the velocity and pressure fields of the fluid by
{\bf u}({\bf r})
and
p({\bf r})
respectively. The stress tensor is given by

{\bm{\sigma}}=-p{\bf I}+2\eta{\bf E},

(6)

where
\eta
is the fluid’s viscosity and
{\bf E}
is the symmetric-traceless shear-rate tensor defined as
{\bf E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\nabla}{\bf u}+\left(\bm{\nabla}{\bf u}\right)^{T}\right)
. The flow obeys Stokes equations
[
33
]
:

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bf u}=0,

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}=0.

(8)

Equation
7
comes from mass conservation for an incompressible fluid, and Eq.
8
comes from momentum conservation. The energy dissipation of a fluid in a volume
V
surrounded by an area
S
is defined as
[
42
]

\Phi=\int_{V}\bm{\sigma}:\bm{\nabla}{\bf u}dV=\int_{V}2\eta{\bf E}:{\bf E}dV,

(9)

where the second equality holds since
{\bf E}
is a symmetric-traceless tensor. Utilizing the Divergence Theorem, we can rewrite the energy dissipation as

\Phi=\int_{V}\bm{\sigma}:\bm{\nabla}{\bf u}\,dV=\int_{V}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\bm{\sigma}\cdot{\bf u}\right)dV=\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}\,dS,

(10)

where in the second equality, we have used Eq.
8
and
{\bf n}
is a unit vector normal to the surface.
Throughout this paper, we distinguish between the particulate fluid velocity
{\bf u}
, stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
, and shear-rate tensor
{\bf E}
and those of a bare fluid with no particles, for which we use the notation
{\bf u}^{\infty}
,
\bm{\sigma}^{\infty}
,
{\bf E}^{\infty}
.

II.1
Lorentz Reciprocal Theorem

Lorentz Reciprocal Theorem is a fundamental integral theorem for viscous fluids that connects two configurations — a fluid with velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathbf{1}}
and stress
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{1}}
and a fluid with velocity
\bm{u}^{\mathbf{2}}
and stress
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{2}}
,

\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{1}}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\mathbf{2}}dS=\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{2}}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\mathbf{1}}dS,

(11)

under the assumption that both fluids’ velocities tend to zero on the volume’s boundaries.
A possible interpretation of this theorem is that it connects the energy-dissipation of a stress field
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{1}}
acting on a fluid with velocity
{\bf u}^{\mathbf{2}}
with respect to that of stress
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{2}}
and flow
{\bf u}^{\mathbf{1}}
, where
S
and
V
are the surface and volume being compared.

For two fluids of different viscosities, which can, in general, be spatially varying, where the first (second) fluid viscosity is
\eta^{\mathrm{1}}({\bf r)}
[
\eta^{\mathrm{2}}({\bf r})
], there is an additional term that includes the viscosity difference and the shear-rate tensor
\bf E^{\mathbf{1}}
(
\bf E^{\mathbf{2}}
) of the first (second) fluid
[
31
]

\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{1}}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\mathbf{2}}dS=\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathbf{2}}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\mathbf{1}}dS+\int_{V}2(\eta^{\mathrm{1}}({\bf r})-\eta^{\mathrm{2}}({\bf r})){\bf E}^{\mathbf{1}}:{\bf E}^{\mathbf{2}}dV.

(12)

II.2
Temperature Distribution

We describe the temperature field

surrounding a spherical particle with radius

from the surrounding fluid, akin to
[

]

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{1}}=\frac{1}{\Delta T^{Q^{\mathrm{1}}}}\frac{1}{4\pi k_{0}a^{2}}\int{\bf r}\mathbf{\rho}^{q}({\bf r},\theta,\phi)dV

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{2}}=\frac{1}{\Delta T^{Q^{\mathrm{2}}}}\frac{1}{4\pi k_{0}a^{3}}\int\left({\bf r}{\bf r}-\frac{1}{3}{\bf I}\right)\mathbf{\rho}^{q}({\bf r},\theta,\phi)dV,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{1}{\Delta T^{Q^{\mathrm{m}}}}\frac{1}{4\pi k_{0}a^{m}}\int\overbracket{{\bf r}_{1}...{\bf r}_{m}}\mathbf{\rho}^{q}({\bf r},\theta,\phi)dV.

For higher orders we have used the notation

]

(14)

This equation can be solved using spherical harmonic moments of the heat flux (see

\displaystyle T({\bf r})

\displaystyle=T^{\infty}+\sum_{m=1}\frac{1}{m!}\frac{1}{r^{m-1}}\overbracket{{\bf n}_{1}...{\bf n}_{m}}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle=T^{\infty}+\Delta T^{Q^{\mathrm{0}}}\frac{a}{r}+\Delta T^{Q^{\mathrm{1}}}\frac{a^{2}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{1}}\cdot{\bf n}}{r^{2}}+\Delta T^{Q^{\mathrm{2}}}\frac{a^{3}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{2}}:\left({\bf n}{\bf n}-\frac{1}{3}{\bf I}\right)}{2r^{3}}+...

III
Effective Viscosity for a Suspension of Hot Particles

In this section, we calculate the corrections to Einstein’s relation (Eq.
1
) coming from the temperature distribution over the particles’ surface.
To compute this correction, we will use Eq.
3
, i.e.

\eta^{\mathrm{eff}}=\eta^{\infty}\frac{\Phi}{\Phi^{\infty}}
.
The energy dissipation of a bare viscous homogeneous fluid,
\Phi^{\infty}
,
in a container of volume
V
is given by

\Phi^{\infty}=\int_{V}2\eta^{\infty}{\bf E}^{\infty}:{\bf E}^{\infty}dV=2\eta^{\infty}{\bf E}^{\infty}:{\bf E}^{\infty}V.

(18)

The energy dissipation in the presence of hot particles,
\Phi
, is separated into two contributions — on the boundary of the volume (“bulk fluid contribution”) and around the particles themselves (“hot stresslet contribution”)

\Phi=\Phi^{\mathrm{b}}+\Phi^{\mathrm{p}}.

(19)

For both terms we use the extended Lorentz reciprocal theorem, Eq.
12
, which takes into account the local-perturbation to the viscosity as represented in Eq.
16
, where the normalized-temperature distribution
\mathcal{T}({\bf r)}
is given by the heat multipole expansion as in
Eq.

15
.

Assuming the viscosity difference,
\xi
, is small, we will expand the shear-rate tensor in powers of
\xi
, using a perturbation expansion. Terms of order
O(\xi^{2})
will be neglected. The shear-rate tensor can be represented as a summation of two terms — the free fluid shear-rate tensor plus the effect of the disturbance due to the presence of the particles, which includes the change to the viscosity. The disruption of the shear-rate tensor due to the particles can also be divided into two contributions — the first,
\widetilde{\bf E}^{(0)}
, represents the shear-rate tensor disruption due to the presence of a “cold” particle. The second,
\widetilde{\bf E}^{(1)}
, represents the correction to the shear-rate tensor that is related to local viscosity changes. This second term is of order
\xi

[
31
]

\displaystyle{\bf E}({\bf r})

\displaystyle={\bf E}^{\infty}+\widetilde{\bf E}({\bf r})

(20)

\displaystyle={\bf E}^{\infty}+\widetilde{\bf E}^{(0)}({\bf r})+\xi\widetilde{\bf E}^{(1)}({\bf r}).

The total shear rate tensor,
{\bf E}
, must be proportional to the shear rate tensor of the bare fluid,
{\bf E}^{\infty}
. We thus define the normalized 4-th rank
shear-rate tensor
\bm{\mathcal{E}}
as

{E}_{ij}({\bf r})={\mathcal{E}}_{ijkl}({\bf r}){E}^{\infty}_{kl}.

(21)

In particular, we can represent the different terms in
Eq.

20
using the normalized strain-rate tensor

\widetilde{\bf E}(\bm{r})=\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(\bm{r}):{\bf E}^{\infty}
.
In the next subsections, we derive separately each of the terms in
\widetilde{\bf E}
. First, the bulk contribution in Sec.
III.1
, then the contribution of the particles in Sec.
III.2
.

III.1
Bulk Fluid Contribution

In considering the bulk, we examine the energy dissipation, represented as

\Phi^{\mathrm{b}}=\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\infty}\,dS,

(22)

where
{\bf u}^{\infty}
denotes the velocity on the boundary,
S
, that has not changed due to viscosity variations.
We would like to relate
\Phi^{\mathrm{b}}
to the dissipation of a particulate-free fluid
\Phi^{\infty}
. To account for viscosity differences due to the presence of hot particles, we employ Lorentz’s reciprocal theorem (Eq.
12
). We express
\Phi^{\mathrm{b}}
as

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{b}}

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\bm{\sigma}^{\infty}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}dS+\int_{V}2\Delta\eta{\bf E}^{\infty}:{\bf E}dV

(23)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\infty}+\Phi^{\Delta\eta},

where
\Delta\eta({\bf r})=\eta({\bf r})-\eta^{\infty}
, and the second term is labeled
\Phi^{\Delta\eta}
.
We proceed to relate
\Phi^{\Delta\eta}
to
\Phi^{\infty}
. The local viscosity of the bulk fluid is changed due to the presence of hot particles. It can be written as a sum of all the temperature differences on all particles. From Eq.
16
we have

\displaystyle\eta({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\eta^{\infty}\left(1-\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\xi\mathcal{T}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}})\right).

(24)

For a dilute homogeneous suspension, neglecting mutual effects between particles and the correction to the shear rate coming from viscosity changes,
{\bf E}^{(1)}
, which gives a correction of
O(\xi^{2})
to the energy dissipation, the shear-rate contribution from particles is given by

\displaystyle{E}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\sum_{p}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{ijkl}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}})\right){E}_{kl}^{\infty},

(25)

where
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbf{p}}}({\bf r})
is the normalized strain-rate tensor, as defined in Eq.
21
, for each particle.
Using Eq.
25
we can express
\Phi^{\Delta\eta}
as

\displaystyle\Phi^{\Delta\eta}=

\displaystyle-\int_{V}2\eta^{\infty}{E}_{ij}^{\infty}{E}_{kl}^{\infty}\xi\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{T}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}})\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}^{p_{1}}_{ijkl}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}_{1}})\right)dV

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Phi^{\infty}}{V}\xi\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\int_{V}\mathcal{T}({{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}}})dV-\xi{E}_{ij}^{\infty}{E}_{kl}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{1}}\int_{V}\mathcal{T}({{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}}}){{\mathcal{E}_{ijkl}^{p_{1}}}}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}_{1}})dV.

Throughout this work, we will assume a continuous density such that summation over the number of particles will be replaced by integration over the volume
dV^{p}
with

dv^{p}=\varphi({\bf r^{p}})dV.

(27)

The
local
volume fraction

\varphi({\bf r}^{\mathbf{p}})
is assumed constant and is given by

\varphi=\frac{Nv^{\mathrm{p}}}{V},

(28)

where
N
is the number of particles in the volume
V
, normalized by the particle’s volume
v^{\mathrm{p}}.

We can now see that the second term is negligible as it is of order

. We are left with

\frac{{\Phi}^{\mathrm{b}}}{\Phi^{\infty}}=1-\xi{\varphi}\frac{V}{v^{\mathrm{p}}}\big\langle\mathcal{T}({\bf r})\big\rangle.

(29)

The hot particles heat the fluid causing the viscosity to decrease. Notice that this term gives an extensive correction. For a given concentration, the larger the system, the hotter it will be.

III.2
Hot Particle Contribution

We will now compute the contribution to the effective viscosity coming from the stresslet and the torque, under the assumption of a dilute suspension such that the energy dissipation from each particle is independent of its neighbors and can be calculated separately,

\Phi^{\mathrm{p}}={\sum}_{\rm p}\int_{S^{\mathbf{p}}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\mathbf{p}}dS.

(30)

For its calculation, we first model the motion of a particle in the fluid, assuming it translates with velocity
\bf U
and rotates with angular velocity
\bm{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{\omega}
, where
\bm{\varepsilon}
is the permutation symbol. We also assume linear-flow of the fluid in the absence of particles

{\bf u}^{\infty}={\bf U}^{\infty}+\bm{\Omega}^{\infty}\times{\bf r}+{\bf E}^{\infty}\cdot{\bf r}.

\left.{\bf u}^{\mathbf{p}}\right|_{S^{\mathbf{p}}}=({\bf U}^{\infty}-{\bf U})+({\bf\Omega}^{\infty}-\bm{\Omega})\times{\bf r}+{\bf E}^{\infty}\cdot{\bf r}.

\int_{S^{\mathbf{p}}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot{\bf n}\cdot{\bf u}^{\mathbf{p}}dS={\bf F}\cdot({\bf U}^{\infty}-{\bf U})+{\bf T}\cdot({\bf\Omega}^{\infty}-\bm{\Omega})+\bm{\mathcal{S}}:{\bf E}^{\infty},

III.3
Tensorial Form of the Effective Viscosity

Using energy dissipation allows us to calculate a scalar correction to the viscosity, but the full Rheological response of the fluid is missing. Batchelor
[
3
]
calculated the effective viscosity as the ratio of applied strain-rate to the bulk stress. This method gives similar results when the fluid is isotropic, but allows us to easily extract a tensorial viscosity in cases where the fluid is no longer isotropic, such as when the particles are all oriented in the same direction. In his work, Batchelor defined the tensorial effective viscosity as

\ \langle{\Sigma}\rangle_{ij}=-p^{\mathrm{eff}}\delta_{ij}+{\eta}^{\mathrm{eff}}_{ijkl}\frac{\partial u^{\infty}_{k}}{\partial r_{l}}.

(53)

He showed that the effective viscosity has two contributions — one coming from the stresslet and the other from the torque, which can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=-p^{\mathrm{eff}}{\bf I}+2\eta^{\infty}{\bf E}^{\infty}+\frac{\varphi}{v^{\mathrm{p}}}\left(\langle\bm{\mathcal{S}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\langle\mathbf{T}\rangle\right).

(54)

The ratio between average stress and strain rate reproduces Einstein’s formula (
Eq.

1
) for a cold colloidal suspension, in which case
\eta^{\mathrm{eff}}
is scalar.
For a suspension of hot particles, there are two corrections to Eq.
54
. We can write,

\langle\bm{\Sigma}\rangle=-p^{\mathrm{eff}}{\bf I}+\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{b}}+\varphi\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{p}}.

(55)

The first term,
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{b}}
, comes from the bulk fluid being heated

\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{b}}=\langle\eta{\bf E}^{\infty}\rangle=2\eta^{\infty}\left(1-\xi N\langle\mathcal{T}({\bf r})\rangle\right){\bf E}^{\infty}=2\eta^{\infty}\left(1-\xi\varphi\frac{V}{v^{p}}\langle\mathcal{T}({\bf r})\rangle\right){\bf E}^{\infty}.

(56)

The second term,
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{p}}
, includes the particle contribution to the bulk stress, which, using
Eqs.

49
and
47
, is given by

\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{1}{v^{\mathrm{p}}}\left[\left(\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{\bm{\mathcal{S}}\bf E}-\xi\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{\bm{\mathcal{S}}\bf E}\right):{\bf E}^{\infty}-\xi\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{\bm{\mathcal{S}}\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{\Omega}^{\infty}+\right.\left.\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\left({\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{{\bm{T}\bm{\Omega}}}-\xi\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{{\bm{T}\bm{\Omega}}}\right)\cdot\bm{\Omega}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{2}\xi\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{\bm{T{\bf E}}}:{\bf E}^{\infty}\right].

(57)

Then, following past works
[
3
,
5
,
48
,
14
]
, the effective viscosity comes from an induced simple shear flow,
{\bf u}^{\infty}=y\hat{x}
, which includes a shear-rate tensor and a vorticity vector

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{\infty}=\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle;\quad\bm{\Omega}^{\infty}=\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

(58)

The scalar effective viscosity is defined by the ratio

\eta^{\mathrm{p}}=\Sigma_{12}/\dot{\gamma}.

(59)

As expected,
Eq.

4
is also recovered by the Batchelor method.
We can also consider a tensorial effective viscosity as given by Eq.
53
. In what follows, we do not explicitly write the full tensorial viscosity tensor as it is a fourth-order tensor with 81 terms. However, we do express the anisotropic nature of the suspension by examining the different terms of the effective stress tensor. For an isotropic suspension, the stress is simply proportional to the shear-rate by a scalar multiplier, which is not the case for a hot, anisotropic suspension. In the next section, we will give results for a few particular examples. We will present the effective viscosity as historically defined by Eq.
59
, but we will also examine the anisotropic nature by showing different terms in the stress tensor that do not appear in the shear-rate tensor. We will also showcase how applying a different simple shear will result in a different effective scalar viscosity, again highlighting the anisotropic nature.

V
Conclusions

To conclude, in this paper, we calculated the effective stress tensor and effective viscosity of a suspension of hot particles. The particles are assumed to be heated (for example, by an external field), and the temperature distribution over the particles’ surface is given by
\mathcal{T}({\bf r})
. The temperature affects the local
viscosity. We assumed a small temperature difference defined by a small-parameter,
\xi
.
In order to calculate the correction to the viscosity, we used the extended Lorentz reciprocal theorem. First, we used it to calculate an extensive correction to the viscosity coming from the heated fluid. This correction depends on system size — for a given concentration of hot particles, a larger system is hotter than a smaller one, therefore its viscosity will be lower in comparison. The second correction comes from the particles themselves — each particle is force-free and, therefore, changes the stress distribution by the force dipole. In cases where there is no external torque, the particles only affect the flow by the symmetric part of the force dipole — the stresslet. The stresslet is modified by the temperature distribution on the particle. For spherical particles, and if the particles are free to rotate, the only correction comes from the heat monopole. Higher-order multipoles do not contribute. The correction to the stresslet is negative. If the particles are non-Brownian and are all oriented in the same direction, there may be additional contributions to the stresslet from the quadrupole and the hexadecapole. The fluid is no longer isotropic, and the viscosity, in principle, has a tensorial form as the relationship between external strain rate and the fluid’s stress. In such a case, the fluid is also non-Newtonian as there are normal stress differences. If the particles’ orientation is fixed due to an external field, there is a torque acting on them, prohibiting rotation. In such a case, angular momentum is no longer conserved, and the stress tensor is not symmetric as there are contributions coming from the vorticity field and the torque. This latter case can be thought of as a fluid with odd viscosity.
