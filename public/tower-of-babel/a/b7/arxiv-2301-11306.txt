1.
Introduction

Geometric Ramsey theory has its origins in series of papers by Erdős et al.
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, where they studied geometric configurations which cannot be destroyed by partitioning Euclidean space into finitely many classes. The fundamental problem is to classify those finite sets
X
which are
Ramsey
, in the sense that for every number of colors
r\in\mathbf{N}
there is a dimension
d=d(r,X)
for which every
r
-coloring of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
contains a monochromatic, congruent copy of
X
.

The simplest example of a Ramsey set is a regular
k
-simplex; that is,
k+1
equidistant points. Indeed, for any dimension
d\geq kr
, any
r
-coloring of a regular
d
-simplex contains
k+1
points of the same color, forming a monochromatic regular
k
-simplex. On the other hand, a simple construction using the geometry of the Euclidean metric shows that any set of three collinear points is not Ramsey. In fact, Erdős et al.
[
6
]
showed that every Ramsey set must be
spherical
; that is, contained in some sphere. This has led to the conjecture by Graham
[
13
]
that a finite set
X
is Ramsey if and only if it is spherical.

This conjecture is far from settled. Examples of sets known to be Ramsey include vertices of “bricks” (
k
-dimensional rectangles)
[
6
]
, non-degenerate simplices
[
9
]
, trapezoids
[
18
]
, regular polygons and regular polyhedra
[
17
]
. Common to many of these results was the exploitation of additional symmetries of the configuration. It was observed by Leader, Russell and Walters
[
20
]
that all known examples of Ramsey sets are
subtransitive
in the sense that they can be embedded in a higher dimensional set on which the rotation group acts transitively. They introduced a rival conjecture that a finite set
X
is Ramsey if and only if it is subtransitive, and further showed
[
19
]
that almost all 4-point subsets of a circle are not subtransitive. This was later extended by Eberhard
[
5
]
to show that almost all
(k+2)
-point sets on the
(k-1)
-sphere are not subtransitive. It remains an open question whether or not such configurations are Ramsey.

The aim of this article is to show that an analogue of Graham’s conjecture holds in finite field geometries for 4-point spherical configurations spanning two dimensions, and more generally for spherical
(k+2)
-point configurations spanning
k
dimensions. We in fact prove a stronger
density
version; namely that if
d\geq 2k+6
and
q
is taken to be odd and sufficiently large with respect to
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then every set
A\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
of size
|A|\geq\alpha q^{d}
contains an isometric copy of every such configuration
X
. To be clear, here we say that two sets
X
and
X^{\prime}
are
isometric
if there is a bijection
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})|^{2}=|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}
for all
x\in X
and
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
, where
|x|^{2}=x\cdot x
is the usual dot product of the vector
x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
with itself.

Our approach takes the point of view modern arithmetic combinatorics which has been very successful in the study of linear patterns in subsets of
\mathbf{Z}
of positive density
[
10
,
11
]
. In fact, one of the main purpose of this article is to extend these techniques to the setting of geometric Ramsey theory, where one counts configurations determined by both linear as well as certain non-linear relations, i.e. isometries.

The setting of vector spaces over finite fields provides a useful model to study many problems in arithmetic combinatorics; see especially the surveys
[
14
,
25
,
28
]
. In the context of geometric Ramsey theory over finite fields, notable results have been obtained by a number of authors
[
3
,
15
,
16
,
24
]
. However, those results concern patterns consisting of points in general position, with no linear relations between them, and hence are fundamentally different.

Analogous results for simplices in Euclidean spaces and the integer lattice have been given in
[
1
,
22
]
, and it reasonable to expect that our approach here may be successfully adapted to these settings. In the context of geometric (density) Ramsey theory in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, some results using this approach were recently
obtained in
[
4
,
21
]
. We hope to address further adaptation in the near future.

Outline of paper

The main results of the paper are stated is
Section

A key observation of the paper, see

which measures uniformity along combinatorial rectangles. If a set

contains many, in fact the statistically correct number of, isometric copies of

. The proof of

is presented in
Section

.

In order to handle arbitrary sets

we prove an

, see
10
in
Section

7.1
, to establish that functions with large uniformity norm correlate with structured sets. Given such an inverse theorem there are then various standard iterative procedures that one may hope to adapt to this setting to complete the argument. We follow an
energy increment
route which to leads a so-called
arithmetic regularity lemma
, namely
7
, that allows us to decompose the indicator function of
A
as
1_{A}=f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}
, where
f_{1}
is highly structured,
f_{2}
has small

norm and
f_{3}
has small uniformity norm. The proof of
7
is presented in
Section

7
.

In Sections
5.2
and
5.3
we demonstrate how

leads to a proof of our main results. This consists of counting the isometric copies of

along the main term

and showing the contribution of the functions
f_{2}
and

are negligible. The setback of this approach is that it leads to very weak bounds, in fact the dependence of

on

is tower-exponential. It seems quite possible that one could instead proceed via a density increment argument and obtain better, exponential type bounds, but we do not pursue this here.

We conclude the paper with an appendix in which we discuss the necessity of the spherical condition in the statement of our main result.

2.
Main results

We will always work with a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
of odd characteristic. For vectors
v,w\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
, we define their dot product
v\cdot w:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}w_{j}
as usual and we will work with the isotropic measurements of
length

|v|^{2}:=v\cdot v
and
distance

|v-w|^{2}
. For any
u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and
\lambda\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
, we define the
sphere

S_{\lambda}(u)=\{x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}:|x-u|^{2}=\lambda\}
, and we will simply write
S_{\lambda}
when
u=0
. For
k\in\mathbf{N}
, we will say that
X\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}

spans
k
dimensions
when
\dim(\operatorname{Span}(X-X))=k
, and we will call
X

spherical
provided exists a sphere
S_{\lambda}(u)\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
with
X\subseteq S_{\lambda}(u)
. Note the
(k+1)
-point spherical configurations spanning
k
dimensions are exactly the
k
-simplices, which were shown in
[
24
]
to appear as isometric copies in sufficiently dense subsets of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
provided
d>k
.

In Euclidean spaces it is easy to see that if the finite sets
X
and
X^{\prime}
are isometric, then
X^{\prime}=z+U(X)

for some vector
z
and orthogonal transformation
U\subseteq O(d)
, i.e.
X^{\prime}
can be obtained from
X
by a rigid motion.
The same may not hold in finite field geometries due to the presence of self-orthogonal vectors
x
for which
|x|^{2}=0
.
However, it follows from Witt’s extension theorem
[
27
]
that if the subspace
V:=\operatorname{Span}(X-X)
is non-degenerate in
the sense that
V\cap V^{\perp}=\{0\}
, then isometric copies of
X
are indeed obtained by rigid motions. We use this fact
in the appendix to construct dense subsets avoiding isometric copies of non-spherical sets, establishing the necessity of
restricting our attention to spherical configurations
X
.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbf{N}
with
d\geq 2k+6
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
q\geq q(\alpha,k)
. If
A\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
with
|A|\geq\alpha q^{d}
, then
A
contains at least
c(\alpha,k)q^{(k+1)d-k(k+1)/2}
isometric copies of every
(k+2)
-point spherical configuration spanning
k
dimensions.

Here we write
c(\alpha,k)
to stand in for some positive constant depending only on
\alpha
and
k
, and
q\geq q(\alpha,k)
to indicate
q
is taken sufficiently large with respect to
\alpha
and
k
. We will use similar notation to indicate the dependency of constants
c,C>0
that may change between occurrences. It is helpful to think of
\alpha
and
k
as fixed with
q
allowed to tend toward infinity, and implicit constants in our big-O notation may depend on
k
.

Note that if
A
would be a random subset of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
of density
\alpha
then it would contain
\alpha^{k+2}q^{(k+1)d-k(k+1)/2}
isometric copies of a
(k+2)
-point spherical configuration
X
up to an error. This is because there are
k(k+1)/2
quadratic relations, given by the length of the edges, between the points of the configuration
X
and each vertex is contained in
A
with probability
\alpha
.

It immediately follows from
1
that for any fixed number of colors
r
, if we take
q
sufficiently large with respect to
r
, then any
r
-coloring of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{10}
contains monochromatic, isometric copies of all 4-point spherical sets spanning 2 dimensions; this establishes a finite field version of Graham’s conjecture that all cyclic quadrilaterals are Ramsey. In fact, we prove a stronger statement relative to spheres; see
[
12
,
23
]
for some so-called “sphere Ramsey” results in the Euclidean setting.

Theorem 2
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbf{N}
with
d\geq 2k+6
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
q\geq q(\alpha,k)
. If
\lambda\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
and
A\subseteq S_{\lambda}
with
|A|\geq\alpha q^{d-1}
, then
A
contains at least
c(\alpha,k)q^{(k+1)d-(k+1)(k+2)/2}
isometric copies of every
(k+2)
-point spherical configuration spanning
k
dimensions.

A straightforward counting argument reveals that
2
quickly implies
1
, this argument is presented in
Section

4
below.

We remark here that the relationship between
d
and
k
in both theorems could be improved if one were only interested in “high rank” configurations; see the comments following
3
. Our methods are further able to prove a version of
2
when
\lambda=0
provided
d>2k+6
, but we do not pursue this since it does not impact our proof of
1
.

3.
Preliminaries

Here we record notation and ingredients that we will require for the proof of
2
. Given any function
f:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
and
B\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
, we write

\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}f(x):=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{x\in B}f(x)

for the average of
f
over
B
, and we will understand the average
\mathbf{E}_{x}f(x)
is taken over
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
. We will condense multiple averages
\mathbf{E}_{y_{1}}\mathbf{E}_{y_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{E}_{y_{k}}
as
\mathbf{E}_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}}
, and to indicate linear independence we will use the notation

\mathbf{E}_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}}^{*}:=\frac{1}{q^{kd}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\\
\text{linearly independent}\end{subarray}}

Letting
\chi
denote the canonical additive character of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, we define the Fourier transform
\widehat{f}:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
by

\widehat{f}(\xi):=\mathbf{E}_{x}f(x)\chi(-\xi\cdot x)

and we recall the Fourier inversion formula

f(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\chi(\xi\cdot x).

Given two functions
f,g:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
, we recall Plancherel’s identity

\mathbf{E}_{x}f(x)\overline{g(x)}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}

and, defining the convolution
f*g(x)=\mathbf{E}_{y}f(y)g(x-y)
, we also recall
\widehat{f*g}(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi)\widehat{g}(\xi)
.

We will write
\sigma_{\lambda}=q1_{S_{\lambda}}
for a normalized indicator function of
S_{\lambda}
, where we have the asymptotic

(1)

\widehat{\sigma}_{\lambda}(\xi)=\begin{cases}1+O(q^{-1/2})&\text{if }\xi=0\\
O(q^{-1/2})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

valid for
d\geq 2
and
\lambda\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
; see, for instance,
[
16
, Lemma 2.2]
exploiting Weil’s bounds on Kloosterman sums.

To vectors
y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{j}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
, we will associate the
spherical measure

(2)

\sigma_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}}^{c_{1},\ldots,c_{j}}(y_{j})=\begin{cases}q^{j}&\text{if }y_{i}\cdot y_{j}=c_{i}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq j\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

This is essentially an
L^{1}
-normalized indicator function for the intersection of the sphere
S_{c_{j}}
with
j-1
hyperplanes, so one should expect Fourier decay in appropriate directions. To import the corresponding Fourier asymptotics, we set

\delta_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}}(\xi):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\xi\in\operatorname{Span}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1})\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and record the simplest case of
[
24
, Lemma 7]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{j}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
with
c_{j}\neq 0
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
linearly independent. If
d\geq 2j
, then

|\widehat{\sigma}_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}}^{c_{1},\ldots,c_{j}}(\xi)|=\delta_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}}(\xi)+O(q^{-1/2}).

It is worth mentioning if one is willing to impose technical conditions on the defining vectors and scalars, then results within
[
24
]
include stronger asymptotics valid in the range
d>j
. These would allow us to improve the required relationship of
d\geq 2k+6
in
2
for configurations avoiding self-orthogonal subspaces, but we opt instead for a uniform result valid for all
(k+2)
-point spherical configurations spanning
k
dimensions. For clarity of presentation, we will often suppress the scalars
c_{1},\ldots,c_{j}
in the notation (
2
) since we will always restrict ourselves to
c_{j}\neq 0
and
3
does not depend on the other scalars.

We will also require the notion of Bohr sets, which provide a substitute for fine subgroup structure. We define the Bohr set of spectrum
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and radius
\rho\in(0,1]
by

(3)

B(\Gamma,\rho):=\{x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}:|\chi(\xi\cdot x)-1|\leq\rho\text{ for all }\xi\in\Gamma\}.

Setting
\beta=|B(\Gamma,\rho)|/q^{d}
, we record the standard bound

(4)

\beta\geq\Big(\frac{\rho}{2\pi}\Big)^{|\Gamma|}

which can be found in
[
26
]
. Setting
B=B(\Gamma,\rho)
, we will use the
L^{1}
normalized indicator function
\mu_{B}=\beta^{-1}1_{B}
and its repeated convolution
\nu_{B}=\mu_{B}*\mu_{B}*\mu_{B}*\mu_{B}
. The structure provided by repeated convolution will frequently be useful. For example, provided
d\geq 2
, we can apply Fourier inversion, (
1
) and Plancherel to see

(5)

\mu_{B}*\mu_{B}*\sigma(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)^{2}\widehat{\sigma}(\xi)\chi(\xi\cdot x)=1+O\Big(q^{-1/2}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)|^{2}\Big)=1+O(\beta^{-1}q^{-1/2}),

from which it immediately follows that

(6)

\nu_{B}*\sigma(x)=1+O(\beta^{-1}q^{-1/2}).

In other words, provided
q
sufficiently large with respect to
\beta
, repeated convolutions of
\mu_{B}
with
\sigma
can be considered essentially constant.

We call the Bohr set
B(\Gamma,\rho)
regular if for every
\epsilon>0
, we have both

\displaystyle|B(\Gamma,(1+\epsilon)\rho)|

\displaystyle\leq(1+100\epsilon|\Gamma|)|B(\Gamma,\rho)|

\displaystyle|B(\Gamma,(1-\epsilon)\rho)|

\displaystyle\geq(1-100\epsilon|\Gamma|)|B(\Gamma,\rho)|;

This definition, due to Bourgain
[
2
]
, ensures that all Bohr sets are only a small dilation away from being regular (see
[
26
, Lemma 4.25]
), and regular Bohr sets are essentially closed under addition by elements of their interior. Given a regular Bohr set
B=B(\Gamma,\rho)
, we will write
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
in the case that
B^{\prime}=B(\Gamma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
is another regular Bohr set with
\Gamma^{\prime}\supseteq\Gamma
and
\rho^{\prime}\leq\epsilon\rho/(200\lvert\Gamma\rvert)
. The following standard lemma provides the main consequence of regularity for our purposes; we include the proof for completeness.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
B=B(\Gamma,\rho)
regular with
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
, and
f:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
with
|f|\leq 1
. For any
y\in B^{\prime}
,

\lvert\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}f(x)-\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}f(x+y)\rvert\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

We have

\lvert\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}f(x)-\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}f(x+y)\rvert=\frac{1}{|B|}\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}f(x)(1_{B}(x)-1_{B}(x-y))\Big|\leq\frac{|B\triangle(y+B)|}{|B|}

Since
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
and
y\in B^{\prime}
we have the relationship

B\triangle(y+B)\subseteq B(\Gamma,\rho+\rho^{\prime})\setminus B(\Gamma,\rho-\rho^{\prime})

and our claim follows from the bound
|B(\Gamma,\rho+\rho^{\prime})\setminus B(\Gamma,\rho-\rho^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon|B|
resulting from regularity.
∎

4.
Reduction to Dense Spherical Sets

In this section we present the straightforward counting argument that estblishes
1
as a consequence of
2
. The main observation is that the collection of spheres of a fixed radius
\lambda\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
provides a uniform cover of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
. Hence, any subset
A\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
with density
\alpha
has density nearly
\alpha
on a large number, in fact a positive proportion, of these spheres.
2
then implies that within each of these spheres,
A
contains many of the sought after configurations. By counting the contribution of each of these spheres, it is easy to see that
A
contains a positive proportion of the count of all such configurations within
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
, as each fixed configuration is contained in approximately the same number of spheres.

We first record the characterization of spherical configurations that will be most useful as we proceed. This follows in a straightforward way from
16
in the Appendix.

Lemma 5
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
be any
(k+2)
-point spherical configuration spanning
k
dimensions. If any
(k+1)
-point subset of
X
that spans
k
dimensions is contained in a sphere
S_{\lambda}(u)
, then
X\subseteq S_{\lambda}(u)
as well.

Let us now see how
2
implies
1
.

Proof of
1
.

Fixing
\lambda\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
, we first establish that for many centers
u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
,
|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|
is large. For
d\geq 2
and
x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
, (
1
) implies

\mathbf{E}_{u}\sigma_{\lambda}(u-x)=1+O(q^{-1/2}),
in which case we can ensure
q
is sufficiently large for

\displaystyle(\alpha/2)q^{2d-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\\
|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|\geq(\alpha/4)q^{d-1}\end{subarray}}|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\\
|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|<(\alpha/4)q^{d-1}\end{subarray}}|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq|S_{\lambda}||\{u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}:|A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)|\geq(\alpha/4)q^{d-1}\}|+(\alpha/4)q^{2d}.

Again using (
1
) and ensuring
q
sufficiently large, we can conclude

|\{u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}:|(A-u)\cap S_{\lambda}|\geq(\alpha/4)q^{d-1}\}|\geq(\alpha/8)q^{d}.

Fixing now some
(k+2)
-point spherical configuration
X
spanning
k
dimensions, we see that for each of these good centers
u
,
2
guarantees that
A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)
contains at least
c(\alpha,k)q^{(k+1)d-\binom{k+2}{2}}
isometric copies of
X
. We need to account for how many spheres we count each isometric copy within. By translating
X
if necessary, we can parametrize it as

X=\{0,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},a_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}v_{k}\}

for linearly independent
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Then for a translation
x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and linearly independent vectors
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
with
y_{i}\cdot y_{j}=v_{i}\cdot v_{j}
for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq k
, we wish to count how many spheres
S_{\lambda}(u)
contain the configuration

X^{\prime}=\{x,x+y_{1},\ldots,x+y_{k},x+a_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}y_{k}\}.

Since
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
are linearly independent,
3
applies to the spherical measure

\sigma_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}}(u):=\sigma_{\lambda}(u)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{\lambda}(y_{j}-u)

so that, for
d\geq 2k+2
,

\mathbf{E}_{u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\sigma_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}}(x-u)=1+O(q^{-1/2}).

5
ensures that
\{x,x+y_{1},\ldots,x+y_{k}\}\subseteq S_{\lambda}(u)
implies
X^{\prime}\subseteq S_{\lambda}(u)
as well, so we have shown

|\{u\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}:X^{\prime}\subseteq S_{\lambda}(u)\}|=(1+O(q^{-1/2}))q^{d-k-1}.

Hence, within each of the
(\alpha/8)q^{d}
good spheres
S_{\lambda}(u)
,
A\cap S_{\lambda}(u)
contains
c(\alpha,k)q^{(k+1)d-\binom{k+2}{2}}
isometric copies of
X
, and each of these copies is contained in roughly
q^{d-k-1}
spheres. In total this yields that
A
contains
c(\alpha,k)q^{(k+1)d-\binom{k+1}{2}}
isometric copies of
X
as claimed.
∎

For the remainder, we fix

For brevity, we set

and a normalized counting operator on functions

The starting point for our argument is to show that the counting operator (
7

by

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}:=\Big(\mathbf{E}_{x,h,h^{\prime}}f\sigma(x)\overline{f}\sigma(x+h)\overline{f}\sigma(x+h^{\prime})f\sigma(x+h+h^{\prime})\Big)^{1/4},

which is the usual Gowers

. While the

. Note that if

then it is also the case that

.

\displaystyle 1_{A}

\displaystyle=f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=\nu_{B}*(1_{A}\sigma)

\displaystyle\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(|\Gamma|^{-1},\rho)

\displaystyle\lVert f_{3}\rVert_{L^{2}(S)}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.

We take

where
|y|^{2}=|v|^{2}
,
|z|^{2}=|w|^{2}
, and

. To detect these copies, we define the spherical measures

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}(y)

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{x}^{-|v|^{2}/2,|v|^{2}}(y),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\sigma_{x,y}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{x,y}^{-|w|^{2}/2,v\cdot w,|w|^{2}}(z)

Without trying to be too careful, notice that uniformly in

Arguing similarly to eliminate the average in

From the decomposition

For the second case, with one of

\mathcal{N}_{X}^{B}(f_{i_{0}},\ldots,f_{i_{k+1}})=\sum_{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widehat{\mu}_{B_{2}}(\xi_{j})^{2}\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{i_{0}}\chi_{\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{k}},f_{i_{1}}\chi_{-\xi_{1}},\ldots f_{i_{k}}\chi_{-\xi_{k}},f_{i_{k+1}}).

As before,
\lVert f\chi_{\xi}\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}=\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}
, so we apply the triangle inequality,
6
and Plancherel to conclude

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{X}^{B}(f_{i_{0}},\ldots,f_{i_{k+1}})|

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{-k}\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}+O(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C|\Gamma|}{\epsilon\rho}\right)^{C|\Gamma|}\varphi(|\Gamma|^{-1},\rho)+O(\epsilon).

We see that by taking

\varphi(|\Gamma|^{-1},\rho)=\epsilon\left(\frac{c\epsilon\rho}{|\Gamma|}\right)^{C|\Gamma|},

we can ensure case (ii) contributes at most
O(\epsilon)
.

For the third case, we assume
i_{0}=3
and apply the triangle inequality for

\mathcal{N}_{X}^{B}(f_{3},f_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k+1}})\leq 2^{k+1}\mathbf{E}_{x_{0}}|f_{3}(x_{0})|\sigma(x_{0})\mathbf{E}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{B_{2}}*\mu_{B_{2}}(x_{j})\sigma^{(j+1)}(x_{j})+O(\epsilon).

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz and the fact that
\lVert f_{3}\rVert_{L^{2}(S)}\leq\epsilon
, we conclude case (iii) again contributes at most an
O(\epsilon)
error term. In total we have shown

\mathcal{N}^{B}_{X}(1_{A},\ldots,1_{A})\geq\alpha^{k+2}+O(\epsilon)

which we can insist is at least
\alpha^{k+2}/2
by taking
\epsilon
sufficiently small with respect to
\alpha
and
k
. Then since
\beta\geq c(\alpha,k)
, we are done by recalling (

).
∎

6.
Proof of
6

We establish
6
through two careful applications of Cauchy-Schwarz. In a sense, each application of Cauchy-Schwarz replaces our configuration with a more regular configuration, and we are left considering averages over geometric rectangles rather than more complicated spherical configurations.

We fix a spherical
(k+2)
-point configuration
X
spanning
k
dimensions parameterized by

X=\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}\}

with
v_{1}-v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}-v_{0}
linearly independent and

v_{k+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}v_{j}

for coefficients
a_{j}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
. It is enough by symmetry to show

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|\leq\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}+O(q^{-1/8}).

For
x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
, we will understand that

x_{k+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{i}x_{i},

and we will detect if
\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k+1}\}\simeq X
with the spherical measures
\sigma(x_{0})
and, for
1\leq j\leq k
,

\sigma^{(j)}(x_{j}):=\sigma^{c_{0,j},\ldots,c_{j-1,j},\lambda}_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{j-1}}(x_{j})

where we define
c_{i,j}=|v_{i}-v_{j}|^{2}/2-\lambda
, since if
|x_{i}|^{2}=|x_{j}|^{2}=\lambda
, checking whether
|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}=|v_{i}-v_{j}|^{2}
amounts to checking whether
x_{i}\cdot x_{j}=|v_{i}-v_{j}|^{2}/2-\lambda
. Then we can express our counting operator

\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})=\mathbf{E}_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}}\sigma(x_{0})\prod_{j=0}^{k+1}f_{j}(x_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{k}\sigma^{(j)}(x_{j})+O(q^{-1}),

where we have included the negligible amount of linearly dependent vectors. Rearranging and applying the triangle inequality,

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|\leq\mathbf{E}_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma^{(j)}(x_{j})\left|\mathbf{E}_{x_{k}}f_{k}(x_{k})f_{k+1}(x_{k+1})\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\right|+O(q^{-1}).

Introducing the differencing notation

\Delta_{h}f(x):=f(x)\overline{f}(x+h),

we square both sides and apply Cauchy-Schwarz for

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma^{(j)}(x_{j})\mathbf{E}_{x_{k},h}\Delta_{h}f_{k}(x_{k})\Delta_{h}f_{k+1}(x_{k+1})\Delta_{h}\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})+O(q^{-1}).

We introduce new spherical measures with an additional condition involving
h
defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}(x_{0})

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{h}^{0}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{h,x_{0},\ldots,x_{j-1}}^{0,c_{0,j},\ldots,c_{j-1,j},\lambda}(x_{j}).

which allows us to rewrite

\sigma(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma^{(j)}(x_{j})\Delta_{h}\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})=\sigma_{h}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\sigma(x_{k}+h).

Our bound from above can then be rearranged as

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}_{h,x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma_{h}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})\mathbf{E}_{x_{k}}\Delta_{h}f_{k}(x_{k})\Delta_{h}f_{k+1}(x_{k+1})\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\sigma(x_{k}+h)+O(q^{-1}).

We claim that in the average above, the dependence of
x_{k+1}
on
x_{k}
is superficial. That is, while

x_{k+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}x_{j},

it must be the case that at least two coefficients
a_{j}
are nonzero since we are working with a
(k+2)
-point spherical configuration. In particular,
a_{j}\neq 0
for some
0\leq j<k
, and for this distinguished
j
, we can reindex in
x_{j}
in the average above to replace
x_{k+1}
with

x_{k+1}^{\prime}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{\prime}x_{j},

allowing us to rearrange our bound above as

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}_{h,x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\Delta_{h}f_{k+1}(x_{k+1}^{\prime})\sigma_{h}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})\mathbf{E}_{x_{k}}\Delta_{h}f_{k}(x_{k})\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\sigma(x_{k}+h)+O(q^{-1}),

where we may have needed to adjust the implicit scalars in our spherical measures. This allows us to proceed as before by applying the triangle inequality for

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}_{h,x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma_{h}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})\left|\mathbf{E}_{x_{k}}\Delta_{h}f_{k}(x_{k})\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\sigma(x_{k}+h)\right|+O(q^{-1}),

and Cauchy-Schwarz once more for

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{4}\leq\mathbf{E}_{h,x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma_{h}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})\mathbf{E}_{x_{k},h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h}f_{k}(x_{k})\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\sigma(x_{k}+h)+O(q^{-1}).

We can again reorganize our spherical measures, rewriting

\sigma_{h}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{h}^{(j)}(x_{j})\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\sigma^{(k)}(x_{k})\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\sigma(x_{k}+h)=\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h}\sigma(x_{k})\sigma_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime}}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime},x_{0},\ldots,x_{j-1}}(x_{j})

for appropriate implicit scalars. Using this to rearrange our bound above, we have

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{4}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h}f_{k}\sigma(x_{k})\mathbf{E}_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime}}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime},x_{0},\ldots,x_{j-1}}(x_{j})+O(q^{-1}).

Applying
3
, we have, uniformly in
x_{k},h,h^{\prime}
when
d\geq 2k+6
,

\mathbf{E}_{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}}\sigma_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime}}(x_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime},x_{0},\ldots,x_{j-1}}(x_{j})=1+O(q^{-1/2}),

establishing

|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|^{4}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x_{k},h,h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h^{\prime}}\Delta_{h}f_{k}\sigma(x_{k})+O(q^{-1/2})

from which we see
|\mathcal{N}_{X}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{k+1})|\leq\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}+O(q^{-1/8})
as required. ∎

This section is dedicated to establishing

. We begin in

by establishing

, an

7.1.
An Inverse Theorem

One way to see that functions with large
U^{2}_{\perp}(S)
norm exhibit Fourier bias is to relate the
U^{2}_{\perp}(S)
norm to the usual
U^{2}(\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d})
norm. In order to do so, define for
v,w\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
the normalized indicator function

\ell_{v=w}=\begin{cases}q^{d}&\text{if }v=w\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Then one can express

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{4}=\mathbf{E}_{x,y,z,w}\overline{f}\sigma(x)f\sigma(y)f\sigma(z)\overline{f}\sigma(w)\ell_{x+w=y+z}

Expanding
\ell_{x+w=y+z}
via orthogonality,

\ell_{x+w=y+z}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\chi(\xi(x+w-y-z)),

providing the identity

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{4}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{4}.

The right hand side is precisely
\lVert f\sigma\rVert_{U^{2}(\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d})}
. It is tempting to here use Plancherel to bound
\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{4}
above by
\sup_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{2}\lVert f\sigma\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
, but this is not generally helpful since the
L^{2}
term may grow with
q
. By being a bit more careful, we establish the following inverse theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Let
f:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
with
|f|\leq 1
. If
d\geq 8
, then

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)\rvert^{1/4}+O(q^{-1/32}).

Proof.

We apply absolute values and the triangle inequality for

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{4}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma(x)\lvert\mathbf{E}_{y,z,w}\overline{f}\sigma(y)\overline{f}\sigma(z)f\sigma(w)\ell_{x=y+z-w}\rvert

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz,

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{8}\leq\left(\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma(x)\right)\left(\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma(x)\lvert\mathbf{E}_{y,z,w}\overline{f}\sigma(y)\overline{f}\sigma(z)f\sigma(w)\ell_{x=y+z-w}\rvert^{2}\right).

For any fixed
x
and
y
, we have

\mathbf{E}_{z,w}\sigma(z)\sigma(w)\ell_{x=y+z-w}=\mathbf{E}_{z}\sigma_{x+y}(z)

for some measure
\sigma_{x+y}
. Then since
d\geq 4
, we can apply (
1
) and
3
for

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{8}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma(x)\mathbf{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},z_{1},w_{1}\\
y_{2},z_{2},w_{2}\end{subarray}}\overline{f}\sigma(y_{1})f\sigma(y_{2})\overline{f}\sigma(z_{1})f\sigma(z_{2})f\sigma(w_{1})\overline{f}\sigma(w_{2})\ell_{x=y_{1}+z_{1}-w_{1}}\ell_{x=y_{2}+z_{2}-w_{2}}+O(q^{-1/2})

Moving the average in
x
inside and rearranging a bit, this simplifies to

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{8}\leq\mathbf{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},z_{1},w_{1}\\
y_{2},z_{2},w_{2}\end{subarray}}\overline{f}\sigma(y_{1})f\sigma(y_{2})\overline{f}\sigma(z_{1})f\sigma(z_{2})f\sigma(w_{1})\overline{f}\sigma(w_{2})\sigma(y_{1}+z_{1}-w_{1})\ell_{w_{2}-w_{1}=y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}}+O(q^{-1/2})

Setting
f_{1}=f
and
f_{2}=\overline{f}
, rearranging a bit more and applying the triangle inequality,

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{8}\leq\mathbf{E}_{w_{1},w_{2}}\sigma(w_{1})\sigma(w_{2})\lvert\mathbf{E}_{y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}\sigma(y_{1}+z_{1}-w_{1})\ell_{w_{2}-w_{1}=y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}}\prod_{j=1,2}f_{j}\sigma(y_{j})f_{j}\sigma(z_{j})\rvert+O(q^{-1/2})

Setting
f_{3}=f
and
f_{4}=\overline{f}
and again requiring
d\geq 4
, we apply Cauchy-Schwarz as before and rearrange for

(14)

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{16}\leq\mathbf{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\sigma(y_{j})f_{j}\sigma(z_{j})\ell_{y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}=y_{4}-y_{3}+z_{4}-z_{3}}W_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\end{subarray}}+O(q^{-1/2})

where

W_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\end{subarray}}=\mathbf{E}_{w}\sigma(w)\sigma(y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}+w)\sigma(y_{1}+z_{1}-w)\sigma(y_{3}+z_{3}-w)

Since
d\geq 4
, we can restrict the sum in (
14
) to only consider the terms when the vectors
y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1},y_{1}+z_{1},y_{3}+z_{3}
are linearly independent at the cost of an error that can be absorbed in our current error of
O(q^{-1/2})
. For these vectors, the function

\sigma^{\prime}(w)=\sigma(w)\sigma(y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}+w)\sigma(y_{1}+z_{1}-w)\sigma(y_{3}+z_{3}-w)

is a measure for which
3
applies, allowing us to conclude

W_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\end{subarray}}=\mathbf{E}_{w}\sigma^{\prime}(w)=1+O(q^{-1/2}),

valid for
d\geq 8
. We have arrived at the estimate

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{16}\leq\mathbf{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\sigma(y_{j})f_{j}\sigma(z_{j})\ell_{y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}=y_{4}-y_{3}+z_{4}-z_{3}}+O(q^{-1/2})

Expanding
\ell_{y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}=y_{4}-y_{3}+z_{4}-z_{3}}
via orthogonality, we have

\ell_{y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}=y_{4}-y_{3}+z_{4}-z_{3}}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\chi(\xi\cdot(y_{4}-y_{3}-y_{2}+y_{1}+z_{4}-z_{3}-z_{2}+z_{1})),

from which we have the identity

\mathbf{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\\
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}\sigma(y_{j})f_{j}\sigma(z_{j})\ell_{y_{2}-y_{1}+z_{2}-z_{1}=y_{4}-y_{3}+z_{4}-z_{3}}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{8}

It follows that we can conclude

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{16}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{8}+O(q^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{4}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{4}+O(q^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{4}+O(q^{-1/2}),

where we have used both
|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|\leq 1+O(q^{-1/2})
and
\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{4}=\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}^{4}\leq 1+O(q^{-1/2})
.
∎

7.2.
Proof of
7

In order to use the Fourier bias from
10
to prove
7
, we will construct a sequence of Bohr sets, refining at each stage, until we arrive at
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
for which
\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)
and
\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)
are close in an
L^{2}
sense. This will require a number of technical lemmas, the first of which indicates that these two convolutions are somewhat orthogonal.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
B=B(\Gamma,\rho)
a regular Bohr set with
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
, and
f:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
with
|f|\leq 1
. If
d\geq 2
and
q\geq q(|\Gamma|,\rho,\epsilon)
, then

\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)-\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}+O(\epsilon).

Proof.

By expanding the square,
\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)-\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}
is equal to

\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}-2\mathbf{E}_{x}\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)(x)\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)(x)+\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2},

so it suffices to show that

\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)(x)\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)(x)+O(\epsilon)

Using the relationship
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
, we can apply
4
to see

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}\chi(x\cdot\xi)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}\mathbf{E}_{y\in B^{\prime}}\chi((x+y)\cdot\xi)\rvert+\epsilon

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathbf{E}_{x\in B}\chi(x\cdot\xi)\mathbf{E}_{y\in B^{\prime}}\chi(y\cdot\xi)\rvert+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\xi)\rvert+\epsilon.

in which case we apply Plancherel and this observation for

\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)\rvert^{8}\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\leq\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)\rvert^{4}\left(\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\xi)\rvert+\epsilon\right)^{4}\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)\rvert^{2}

Writing
\beta=|B|/q^{d}
, we can apply Plancherel and (
5
) for

\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu_{B}}(\xi)|^{4}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{2}=\lVert\mu_{B}*\mu_{B}*f\sigma(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 1+O(\beta^{-1}q^{-1/2}).

Using the bound (
4
), we see that we can take
q
sufficiently large and apply Plancherel again to conclude

\displaystyle\lVert\nu_{B}*f\sigma\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}|\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)|^{4}|\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\xi)|^{4}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|^{2}+O(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{x}\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)(x)\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)(x)+O(\epsilon).\qed

With
11
in hand, we are ready to translate

Proposition 12
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
,
\epsilon\in(0,c\eta^{8})
for
c>0
sufficiently small,
B=B(\Gamma,\rho)
a regular Bohr set, and
f:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
with
|f|\leq 1
. If
d\geq 2
,
q\geq q(|\Gamma|,\rho,\eta,\epsilon)
, and

\lVert f-\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}\geq\eta,

then there exists
B^{\prime}=B(\Gamma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
with
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
,
\lvert\Gamma^{\prime}\rvert\leq\lvert\Gamma\rvert+1
,
\rho^{\prime}\geq c(|\Gamma|,\epsilon)\rho
and

\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}\geq\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}+c\eta^{8}.

Proof.

From the inverse theorem,
\lVert f-\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{U^{2}_{\perp}(S)}\geq\eta
implies the existence of some
\gamma\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
with

|\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)-[(\nu_{B}*(f\sigma))\sigma]^{\wedge}(\gamma)|\geq\eta^{4}+O(q^{-1/32}).

Expanding
[(\nu_{B}*(f\sigma))\sigma]^{\wedge}(\gamma)
, we can apply (
1
) for

\displaystyle[(\nu_{B}*(f\sigma))\sigma]^{\wedge}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\widehat{\sigma}(\xi)\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma-\xi)^{4}\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma-\xi)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma)^{4}\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)+O\Big(q^{-1/2}\sum_{\gamma}|\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)|^{4}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)|\Big)

Applying the uniform bounds
|\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)|\leq 1
and
\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)\rvert\leq 1+O(q^{-1/2})
together with Plancherel, we have

(15)

[(\nu_{B}*(f\sigma))\sigma]^{\wedge}(\gamma)=\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma)^{4}\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)+O(\beta^{-1}q^{-1/2}).

in which case we can ensure
q
is sufficiently large and refine our inverse statement to

(16)

|\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)||1-\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma)^{4}|\geq\eta^{4}/2.

Set
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup\{\gamma\}
. It follows from
[
26
, Lemma 4.25]
that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
we can choose a radius
\rho^{\prime}\in[\epsilon\rho/(400|\Gamma|),\epsilon\rho/(200|\Gamma|)]
such that
B^{\prime}:=B(\Gamma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
is regular, in which case
B^{\prime}\prec_{\epsilon}B
. From
11
,

\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*(f\sigma)-\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}+O(\epsilon),

so we would like to provide a lower bound for

\lVert\nu_{B^{\prime}}*f\sigma-\nu_{B}*f\sigma\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\xi)^{4}-\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)^{4}\rvert^{2}\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\geq\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\gamma)^{4}-\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma)^{4}\rvert^{2}\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)\rvert^{2}

As

,
\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\gamma)-1\rvert\leq\rho^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
. Combining this with

\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{B^{\prime}}(\gamma)^{4}-\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma)^{4}\rvert^{2}\lvert\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)\rvert^{2}\geq|1-\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\gamma)^{4}|^{2}|\widehat{f\sigma}(\gamma)|^{2}+O(\epsilon)\geq\eta^{8}/4+O(\epsilon).

Provided

for a sufficiently small absolute constant

, we have managed to show

For the same conclusion with

norms, we can apply (
15
) for

\displaystyle\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}}[\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\sigma]^{\wedge}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\mu}_{B}(\xi)^{4}\widehat{f\sigma}(\xi)}

\displaystyle=\lVert\nu_{B}*(f\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}+O(\beta^{-2}q^{-1/2}).

and ensure
q
is sufficiently large.
∎

Appendix: Necessity of the Spherical Condition

By an isometry of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
we mean a linear map
U:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
so that
|U(x)|^{2}=|x|^{2}
for all
x
. It is easy to see that
U(x)\cdot U(y)=x\cdot y
for all
x
and
y
, hence
U^{T}U=UU^{T}=I
. We call a set
X=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\}

non-degenerate
if
V\cap V^{\bot}=\{0\}
for the subspace
V=\operatorname{Span}(X-X)
.

Lemma 14
.

Suppose
X
is a non-degenerate
k+1
-point configuration and
Y=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}\}
is isometric to
X
. Then there exists a vector
z
and an isometry
U
of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
so that
Y=z+U(X)
.

Proof.

By performing a translation we can assume
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
. Assume that the vertices are labeled so that
|y_{i}-y_{j}|^{2}=|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}
for all
0\leq i\leq j\leq k
. Let
V
and
W
be the subspace spanned by the sets
X
and
Y
respectively. Since
X
and hence
Y
are non-degenerate, we have that the map
\phi:x_{i}\to y_{i},\ 1\leq i\leq k
extends to an isometry
\Phi:V\to W
. By Witt’s extension theorem
[
27
]
there is an orthogonal transformation
U:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
extending the map
\Phi
. Clearly
U(X)=Y
.
∎

Let us reformulate the property that a set
X
is non-degenerate. Assume
X=\{0,x_{1},\dots x_{k}\}
and
X^{\prime}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
is a linearly independent set such that
\operatorname{Span}(X^{\prime})=\operatorname{Span}(X)
. Let
M
be the symmetric
l\times l
matrix with entries
m_{ij}=x_{i}\cdot x_{j}
for
1\leq i,\,j\leq l
.

Lemma 15
.

The set
X
is non-degenerate if and only if the associated inner product matrix
M
has maximal rank.

Proof.

Clearly
X
is non-degenerate if and only if
X^{\prime}
is linearly independent. Let
V=\operatorname{Span}(X^{\prime})
and assume that
0\neq v\in V
such that
v\bot V
. Then
v=\sum_{i=1}^{l}b_{i}x_{i}
and
v\cdot x_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}m_{ji}b_{i}=Mb_{j}=0
for all
1\leq j\leq l
. Thus
Mb=0
for a non-zero vector
b=(b_{j})
and
M
has rank less than
l
. Conversely if
\operatorname{rank}(M)<l
then
Mb=0
for some
b=(b_{j})\neq 0
and the vector
v=\sum_{i}b_{i}x_{i}
is orthogonal to
V=\operatorname{Span}(X^{\prime})=\operatorname{Span}(X)
.
∎

Note that the inner product matrix drops rank if some non-trivial algebraic relations between the distances
|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}
of the points
x_{i},\,x_{j}
of
X
, hence generic configurations are non-degenerate. Next, we show that the spherical condition is necessary over finite fields as well, at least for non-degenerate sets. We follow the proof in
[
6
]
with some minor modifications. First we give the following characterization of spherical sets.

Lemma 16
.

Let
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
. Then
X
is spherical if and only if the following holds. For every
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
if

(I)\quad\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}=0,\ \ \textit{ and}\ \ \ \ \sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}x_{i}=0,\ \ \textit{then also}

(II)\quad\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}=0.

Proof.

Suppose
X
is spherical, that is
|x_{i}-z|^{2}=r
for
0\leq i\leq k
for some
z\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and
r\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Then
|x_{i}|^{2}=r-|z|^{2}+2x_{i}\cdot z
for all
i
, hence

\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}(|z|^{2}+r)+2\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}x_{i}\cdot z=0.

Conversely, assume
X
is not spherical. We show that there exists
c_{0},\ldots,c_{k}
satisfying (I) but not (II). One may assume that
X
is minimal that is
X^{\prime}
is spherical for
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
,
X^{\prime}\neq X
. Since a simplex is spherical there is a non-trivial linear combination
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}(x_{i}-x_{0})=0
and by reindexing the vertices one may assume
a_{k}\neq 0
. Taking
X^{\prime}=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}\}
one has
|x_{i}-z|^{2}=r
for
1\leq i<k
but
|x_{k}-z|^{2}-r=b\neq 0
. Then
|x_{i}|^{2}-|x_{0}|^{2}=2(x_{i}-x_{0})\cdot z
for
1\leq i<k
and
|x_{k}|^{2}-|x_{0}|^{2}=2(x_{k}-x_{0})\cdot z\,+b
. Thus

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}(|x_{i}|^{2}-|x_{0}|^{2})=2\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{k}(x_{i}-x_{0})\cdot z+a_{k}b=a_{k}b\neq 0.

Taking
c_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}
and
c_{i}=a_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
be a non-spherical set of
k
elements. Then there exists a set
A\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
of size
|A|\geq c_{k}q^{d}
which does not contain any set
Y
of the form
Y=z+U(X)
, where
c_{k}>0
is a constant depending only on
k
.

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{\ast}
such that
|\chi(t)-1|\geq 1/2
,
\chi
being a non-trivial character. Such a
t
exists as
\sum_{t}\chi(t)=0
. If
X
is not spherical then, by scaling, there exists
c_{0},\ldots,c_{k}
so that (I) holds and
\sum_{i=0}c_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}=t
. If
Y=z+U(X)
then
\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}y_{i}=0
and moreover

\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}|y_{i}|^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}(|z|^{2}+2z\cdot U(x_{i})+|x_{i}|^{2})=t.

Let
B=B(\Gamma,\rho)
be the Bohr set with
\Gamma=\{c_{0},\ldots,c_{k}\}
and
\rho=\frac{1}{4(k+1)}
. By the estimate (
4
),
|B|\geq c_{k}q
, for some constant
c_{k}>0
depending only on
k
. Define
A=\{x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}:\ |x|^{2}\in B\}
. Since the number of solutions to
|x|^{2}=b
is at least
\frac{1}{2}q^{d-1}
uniformly for
b\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
for
q\geq q_{0}
, we have that
|A|\geq\frac{c_{k}}{2}q^{d}
. We show that
Y=z+U(x)
for some
z\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{d}
and an orthogonal transformation
U
then
Y\subsetneq A
. Indeed if
Y\subseteq A
then
|\chi(c_{i}|y_{i}|^{2})-1|\leq\frac{1}{4(k+1)}
for
0\leq i\leq k+1
. It follows

|\chi(\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}|y_{i}|^{2})-1|=|\prod_{i=0}^{k}\chi(c_{i}|y_{i}|^{2})-1|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}|\chi(c_{i}|y_{i}|^{2})-1|\leq 1/4.

This implies
|\chi(t)-1|\leq 1/4
contradicting our choice of
t
.
∎
