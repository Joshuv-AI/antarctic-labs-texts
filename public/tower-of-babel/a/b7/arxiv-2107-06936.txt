Performance of Bayesian linear regression in a model with mismatch
Jean Barbierabel=e1, mark]jbarbier@ictp.it, msaenz@ictp.it, Wei-Kuo Chenabel=e2, mark]wkchen@umn.edu, Dmitry Panchenkoabel=e3, mark]panchenk@math.toronto.edu, Manuel Sáenzabel=e1, mark]jbarbier@ictp.it, msaenz@ictp.it

Abstract
In this paper we analyze, for a model of linear regression with gaussian covariates, the performance of a Bayesian estimator given by the mean of a log-concave posterior distribution with gaussian prior, in the high-dimensional limit where the number of samples and the covariates’ dimension are large and proportional. Although the high-dimensional analysis of Bayesian estimators has been previously studied for Bayesian-optimal linear regression where the correct posterior is used for inference, much less is known when there is a mismatch. Here we consider a model in which the responses are corrupted by gaussian noise and are known to be generated as linear combinations of the covariates, but the distributions of the ground-truth regression coefficients and of the noise are unknown. This regression task can be rephrased as a statistical mechanics model known as the Gardner spin glass, an analogy which we exploit. Using a leave-one-out approach we characterize the mean-square error for the regression coefficients. We also derive the log-normalizing constant of the posterior. Similar models have been studied by Shcherbina and Tirozzi and by Talagrand, but our arguments are much more straightforward. An interesting consequence of our analysis is that in the quadratic loss case, the performance of the Bayesian estimator is independent of a global “temperature” hyperparameter and matches the ridge estimator: sampling and optimizing are equally good.

Introduction and set-up

Linear regression with random covariates in the high-dimensional regime, where both the number of data points and their dimension scale proportionally, is a paradigmatic problem for modern inference. It has been studied mostly from two complementary perspectives:
(i)
In Bayesian statistics where, due to important simplifications that occur in this setting, analyses have focused on the case of the estimator obtained as the expectation of the “true” posterior distribution of the regression coefficients

Our work focuses on Bayesian inference outside the optimal setting, for which much less is known. The lack of perfect knowledge of the model generating the data forces one to make certain inaccurate prior assumptions on the distribution of the regression coefficients as well as on the form of the model. This is, in a sense, more realistic and of practical interest. One natural setting for this, is to consider Bayesian linear regression with some sources of mismatch. Due to the lack of certain simplifying symmetries inherent to the optimal setting of inference, known as the “Nishimori identities” in physics

Our model

1.2
Main results and contributions

Our main contributions are establishing the asymptotics of the log-normalizing constant and the performance of estimator (
4
). Informally, we prove that, whenever the four-dimensional system (
17
)–(
20
) presented below admits a unique solution, the log-normalizing constant converges to some non-random quantity
F(q,\rho)
, that is

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl|\frac{1}{N}\ln{Z}(\mathcal{D})-(-\kappa\gamma+F(q,\rho))\Bigr|^{2}=0,

and the mean-square error converges to some non-random quantity
q
, that is

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{x}-x^{*}\|_{2}^{2}=q.

(5)

Here
(q,\rho)
is the unique critical point of the real-valued function
F
with domain over the set
\{(q,\rho):0\leqslant q\leqslant\rho<\infty\}
defined in (
21
) below.

There are two major findings that follow from our results. First of all, somewhat surprisingly (
5
) implies that the performance of the Bayesian estimator with gaussian prior is insensitive to the statistical properties of the coefficients
x^{*}
other than its empirical second moment. The second major finding regards our main running example for our results which will play a special role given its tractability in the analysis and its importance in statistics. This example will correspond to the model where the function
u(s)
is of the form
-s^{2}/(2\Delta)
for some
\Delta>0
. Notice that this model is still mismatched since
\Delta
is not necessarily equal to the
\Delta_{*}
appearing in the model generating the data. When we convert the posterior distribution into a
Gibbs measure
parametrized by the
inverse temperature

\beta
we discover that, for quadratic
u
, the mean-square error is independent of the inverse temperature
\beta
in the high-dimensional limit. This means that when the performance is evaluated using a square loss, Bayesian regression with gaussian prior and ridge regression have the same performance.
In other words, sampling and optimizing are equivalent in this setting. One may argue that the closedness between the mean and the mode of the posterior distribution with gaussian prior is mainly due to the isotropy of the covariates. This intuition makes perfect sense in the classical regime, where a lot of data is accessible
M\gg N
when maximum likelihood estimation is optimal
[
53
]
. But in the high-dimensional regime where both
M
and
N
are large and comparable, this feature becomes much less evident. To the best of our knowledge, this fact was not rigorously obtained prior to our work. Exploring the generality of this equivalence between optimization and sampling procedures is an interesting open direction for future research. See
[
2
,
5
]
for related connections between M-estimation and Bayesian inference from statistical mechanics heuristics.

The approach in this paper relies primarily on a connection between our regression task and a generalized version of the Shcherbina-Tirozzi (ST) mean-field spin glass model
[
44
]
. We study the limits of the corresponding log-partition function and some critical quantities called the overlaps. By utilizing the so-called smart path method, analogous results were intensively explored by Talagrand
[
48
]
assuming that
u
grows at most linearly. Whereas the machinery therein relies on quite heavy analysis, the advantage of our approach is methodological. In addition to being able to relax the growth condition for
u
from linear to quadratic, our analysis is also rather simple and self-contained. It is based on methods coming from the mathematical physics of mean-field spin glasses: mainly a rigorous version of the cavity method
[
48
,
38
]
also called the leave-one-out method in statistics (see Sections
5
and
6
for details). We expect for the simplicity of our approach to allow us to extend the analysis to generalized linear models in mismatched settings, for which results similar to the present ones exist only in the Bayesian-optimal setting of inference
[
8
]
. This is left for future work.

1.4
Organization

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we explain the link between the high-dimensional regression problem presented here and the ST spin glass model.
In Section
3
, we first state two results on the main quantities of interest in spin glass models, namely, the free energy (i.e., log-partition function) and overlaps (that will later be related to the mean-square error) in the ST model. We then state our main results on the regression task with a particular emphasis on the classical example of the Bayesian regression with quadratic loss and gaussian prior. Sections
4
–
6
are devoted to establishing results concerning the ST model. More explicitly, Section
4
establishes a number of concentration properties for the overlaps and free energy. Section
5
shows how to obtain certain fixed-point equations for the “order parameters” of the model based on the concentration results in Section
4
by utilizing the cavity method and simple gaussian integration by parts. Section
6
proves a closed-form expression for the limiting log-partition function of the posterior in a straightforward manner thanks to the convergence results for the order parameters. Finally, our results on the regression task are established in Section
7
.

2
Connection to the Shcherbina-Tirozzi model

In this section, we explain how our regression task can be related to a generalization of the ST model
[
44
]
, a connection mentioned (but not exploited) in
[
20
]
. For
h\in\mathbb{R},
consider the
Hamiltonian
(i.e., energy function)
H_{N}=(H_{N}(\sigma))_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}
defined by

\displaystyle H_{N}(\sigma):=\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\big(S_{k}(\sigma)+z_{k}\big)-h\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}-\kappa\|\sigma\|^{2}

(6)

and the corresponding log-partition function, also called
free energy
, by

\displaystyle F_{N}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp H_{N}(\sigma)\,d\sigma.

(7)

In the terminology of the large deviation theory
[
19
,
51
]
, it is the scaled cumulant log-moment generating function associated with the energy per variable
H_{N}(\sigma)/N
.
When
z_{k}=0
and
u
grows at most linearly, the Hamiltonian
H_{N}
is known as the original ST model extensively studied in
[
48
]
. In the setting of the present paper, we allow
u
to grow quadratically (see Assumption
1
below for details).

For a given realization of the randomness
(G,z)
defining an instance of the model, we will refer to each element of a sequence
(\sigma^{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
of independently drawn samples from the
Gibbs measure

\displaystyle G_{N}(d\sigma):=\frac{\exp H_{N}(\sigma)\,d\sigma}{\int\exp H_{N}(\sigma^{\prime})\,d\sigma^{\prime}}

as
replicas
.

Denote by
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
the Gibbs expectation of replicas
(\sigma^{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
:

\displaystyle\big\langle f((\sigma^{\ell})_{\ell\leqslant L})\big\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{NL}}f((\sigma^{\ell})_{\ell\leqslant L})\prod_{\ell\leqslant L}G_{N}(d\sigma^{\ell}).

(8)

Two key quantities for our analysis will be the
overlaps
between replicas defined according to

R_{1,1}:=\frac{\|\sigma^{1}\|^{2}}{N}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad R_{1,2}:=\frac{\sigma^{1}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}.

(9)

Additionally, we also define a pair of “conjugate overlaps” in terms of the following two auxiliary quantities

\displaystyle A(\sigma^{\ell}):=\Big(u^{\prime}\big(S_{k}(\sigma^{\ell})+z_{k}\big)\Big)_{k\leqslant M}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad B(\sigma^{\ell}):=\Big(u^{\prime\prime}\big(S_{k}(\sigma^{\ell})+z_{k}\big)\Big)_{k\leqslant M}.

(10)

For convenience, we will sometimes write
A^{\ell}=(A_{k}^{\ell})_{k\leqslant M}
,
B^{\ell}=(B_{k}^{\ell})_{k\leqslant M}
,
S_{k}^{\ell}
instead of
A(\sigma^{\ell})
,
B(\sigma^{\ell})
, or
S_{k}(\sigma^{\ell})
. Whenever the replica index
\ell
is omitted, it is assumed to be equal to
1
. The conjugate overlaps are then defined by

Q_{1,1}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\leqslant M}\Big((A_{k})^{2}+B_{k}\Big)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad Q_{1,2}:=\frac{1}{N}\langle A^{1},A^{2}\rangle.

(11)

As one shall see, the vector
(R_{1,1},R_{1,2},Q_{1,1},Q_{1,2})
of overlaps order parameters is the “correct” one, in the sense that all the statistical analysis can be asymptotically expressed solely in terms of the limits of these quantities and the model at the “macroscopic level” is completely determined by them.

In view of our regression task, the posterior measure (
3
) can be thought of as a Gibbs measure of the spin glass model with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{N}(x):=\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{ki}(x_{i}-x_{i}^{*})-z_{k}\Big)-\kappa\|x\|^{2},

that is,

\displaystyle P(dx\mid\mathcal{D})=\widetilde{G}_{N}(dx):=\frac{\exp{\widetilde{H}_{N}(x)}\,dx}{\int\exp{\widetilde{H}_{N}(x^{\prime})}\,dx^{\prime}}.

(12)

As in the ST model, we define the free energy by

\widetilde{F}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\widetilde{H}_{N}(x)\,dx.

Denote by
(x^{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
the replicas drawn i.i.d. from measure (
12
) and by
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle^{\sim}
the Gibbs expectation associated to the Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{N}
, defined similarly as (
8
) but instead with
\widetilde{G}_{N}
.

In particular, from (
4
), we readily see that the Bayesian estimator

\widehat{x}=\langle x\rangle^{\sim}.

The connection between the ST and our models relies on a number of change of variables. First of all, by letting
w:=x-x^{*}
, we may write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\widetilde{H}_{N}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{ki}w_{i}-z_{k}\Big)-\kappa\|w+x^{*}\|^{2}\Big)\,dw

\displaystyle=\exp(-\kappa\|x^{*}\|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{ki}w_{i}-z_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-2\kappa\sum_{i\leqslant N}x_{i}^{*}w_{i}-\kappa\|w\|^{2}\Big)\,dw.

To simplify this integral, take an orthogonal matrix
O\in\mathcal{O}(N)
such that
Ox^{*}=N^{-1/2}{\|x^{*}\|}\bm{1}_{N}
. Note that conditionally on
x^{*}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(GO^{\intercal})_{k\ell}(GO^{\intercal})_{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big(\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{ki}O_{\ell i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{k^{\prime}i}O_{\ell^{\prime}i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}O_{\ell i}O_{\ell^{\prime}i}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}(OO^{\intercal})_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},

which implies that
GO^{\intercal}\sim G
conditionally on
x^{*}
. Note also that
\langle x^{*},O^{\intercal}a\rangle=N^{-1/2}\|x^{*}\|\langle\bm{1}_{N},a\rangle
and
\|O^{\intercal}a\|=\|a\|.
From these and using the change of variable
\sigma:=Ow
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{ki}w_{i}-z_{k}\Big)-2\kappa\sum_{i\leqslant N}x_{i}^{*}w_{i}-\kappa\|w\|^{2}\Big)\,dw\\
&\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big((\bar{G}O^{\intercal}\sigma)_{k}-z_{k}\Big)-2\kappa\langle x^{*},O^{\intercal}\sigma\rangle-\kappa\|O^{\intercal}\sigma\|^{2}\Big)\,d\sigma\\
&\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big((\bar{G}O^{\intercal}\sigma)_{k}-z_{k}\Big)-\frac{2\kappa\|x^{*}\|}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}-\kappa\|\sigma\|^{2}\Big)\,d\sigma,\end{split}

(13)

which implies, from the rotational invariance of
G
mentioned above and the symmetry of
z_{k},
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}g_{ki}w_{i}-z_{k}\Big)-2\kappa\sum_{i\leqslant N}x_{i}^{*}w_{i}-\kappa\|w\|^{2}\Big)\,dw

\displaystyle\qquad={\mathbb{E}}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big((\bar{G}\sigma)_{k}+z_{k}\Big)-\frac{2\kappa\|x^{*}\|}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}-\kappa\|\sigma\|^{2}\Big)\,d\sigma.

To sum up,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\widetilde{F}_{N}=-\kappa{\mathbb{E}}\gamma_{N}+\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\big(S_{k}(\sigma)+z_{k}\big)-2\kappa\sqrt{\gamma_{N}}\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}-\kappa\|\sigma\|^{2}\Bigr)\,d\sigma

for
\gamma_{N}:=\|x^{*}\|^{2}/N.

If now we randomize
h=h_{N}=2\kappa\sqrt{\gamma_{N}}
in the Hamiltonian (
6
) of the ST model independently of all other randomness, then we readily get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\widetilde{F}_{N}=-\kappa{\mathbb{E}}\gamma_{N}+{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}

(14)

and similarly,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{x}-x^{*}\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\|\langle x\rangle^{\sim}-x^{*}\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\|\langle\sigma\rangle\|^{2}.

(15)

Consequently, to understand our regression problem, we may study the limiting free energy and overlaps corresponding to the generalized ST model first. This characterization is part of our main results and is stated in the next section.

3
Main results

The first part of our main results consists of an expression for the limiting free energy and the convergence of the overlaps in the generalized ST model defined in (
6
). Throughout the rest of this paper, the following assumption on the function
u
is in force.

Assumption 1
(Growth condition).

The function
u(s)
is concave, non-positive, and there exists a constant
d>0
such that

\displaystyle\max\bigl(|u(0)|,|u^{\prime}(0)|,\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty},\|u^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\infty},\|u^{(4)}\|_{\infty}\bigr)\leqslant d

and

\displaystyle|u^{\prime}(s)|\leqslant d(1+\sqrt{|u(s)|}).

Remark 3.1
.

The concavity of
u
is conceptually an important assumption, which ensures that our posterior distribution is a log-concave measure and, as a result, the overlaps are concentrated under the Gibbs measure
G_{N}
. While the other assumptions are purely technical, overall they weaken the settings considered in
[
48
]
. In particular, in this reference
u
is allowed to grow at most linearly and thus its results do not apply to our main example, namely, a quadratic
u
.

To formulate our limiting free energy, we introduce
\bar{F}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\bar{F}(q,\rho,r,\bar{r})

\displaystyle:=\alpha{\mathbb{E}}\ln{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\exp u\big(\widetilde{z}\sqrt{\Delta_{*}+q}+\xi\sqrt{\rho-q}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{r+h^{2}}{2\kappa+r-\bar{r}}-\ln(2\kappa+r-\bar{r})+rq-\bar{r}\rho+\ln 2\pi\Big)

(16)

for

and
0\leqslant\bar{r}\leqslant r
, where

are independent random variables with normal law

\theta=\widetilde{z}\sqrt{\Delta_{*}+q}+\xi\sqrt{\rho-q}

\widetilde{z}

must satisfy the following system of equations:

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\Psi(r,\bar{r}):=\frac{r+h^{2}}{(2\kappa+r-\bar{r})^{2}},

(17)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\bar{\Psi}(r,\bar{r}):=\frac{1}{2\kappa+r-\bar{r}}+\frac{r+h^{2}}{(2\kappa+r-\bar{r})^{2}},

(18)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\Phi(q,\rho):=\alpha{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}u^{\prime}(\theta)\exp{u(\theta)}}{{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\exp{u(\theta)}}\Bigr)^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\bar{r}

\displaystyle=\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.1111pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\Phi$}}(q,\rho):=\alpha{\mathbb{E}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}(u^{\prime\prime}(\theta)+u^{\prime}(\theta)^{2})\exp{u(\theta)}}{{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\exp{u(\theta)}}.

In addition, for any
0\leqslant q<\rho,
we consider the function

\displaystyle F(q,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\alpha{\mathbb{E}}\ln{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\exp u\big(\widetilde{z}\sqrt{\Delta_{*}+q}+\xi\sqrt{\rho-q}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\ln(\rho-q)+h^{2}(\rho-q)+\frac{\rho}{\rho-q}-2\kappa\rho+\ln 2\pi\Big).

(21)

Observe that if
(q,\rho,r,\bar{r})

17
)–(

\bar{F}(q,\rho,r,\bar{r})

Here, if we express

Lemma 4.5

Proof.

Concentration of the free energy

For clarity, here we will now denote

Lemma 4.9

5
Fixed point equations

This section establishes the proof of Theorem

5.1
M-cavity

For every measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{Nn}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, define the Gibbs expectation associated to the “cavity in
M
” by

\langle f\rangle_{c}:=\frac{1}{Z_{c}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{Nn}}\big(\prod_{\ell\leqslant n}d\sigma^{\ell}\big)f\exp{\sum_{\ell\leqslant n}H_{N,c}(\sigma^{\ell})},

where the M-cavity Hamiltonian is

H_{N,c}(\sigma^{\ell}):=\sum_{k=2}^{M}u\bigl(S_{k}(\sigma^{\ell})+z_{k}\bigr)+h\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}^{\ell}-\kappa\|\sigma^{\ell}\|^{2}

and
Z_{c}
is its corresponding partition function. In other words, the Hamiltonian here singles out the component
u(S_{M}(\sigma)+z_{M})
from
H_{N}(\sigma).
It is easy to check that for every measurable
f:\mathbb{R}^{Nn}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,

\langle f(\sigma^{1},\ldots,\sigma^{n})\rangle=\frac{\langle f(\sigma^{1},\ldots,\sigma^{n})\exp{\sum_{\ell\leqslant n}u(S_{1}(\sigma^{\ell})+z_{1})}\rangle_{c}}{\langle\exp{\sum_{\ell\leqslant n}u(S_{1}(\sigma^{\ell})+z_{1})}\rangle_{c}},

(55)

where
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
is the expectation with respect to the Gibbs measure proportional to
\exp\sum_{\ell\leqslant n}H_{N}(\sigma^{\ell})
of the “replicated system”.
The following two lemmas will play an essential role in establishing the fixed point equations in the next subsection.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
k\geqslant 1
, there exists some
C_{k}>0
such that, uniformly on
N
,

{\mathbb{E}}\big\langle|u(S_{1}(\sigma)+z_{1})|^{k}\big\rangle\leqslant C_{k}.

Proof.
Here we will denote
u(S_{1}(\sigma)+z_{1})
by
u_{1}\leqslant 0
. Because
|u_{1}|^{k}
is an increasing function of
|u_{1}|
and
\exp(-|u_{1}|)
is decreasing, the Harris inequality implies
\langle|u_{1}|^{k}\exp(-|u_{1}|)\rangle_{c}\leqslant\langle|u_{1}|^{k}\rangle_{c}\langle\exp(-|u_{1}|)\rangle_{c}
. As a consequence we have that

{\mathbb{E}}\langle|u_{1}|^{k}\rangle={\mathbb{E}}\frac{\langle|u_{1}|^{k}\exp(-|u_{1}|)\rangle_{c}}{\langle\exp(-|u_{1}|)\rangle_{c}}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\langle|u_{1}|^{k}\rangle_{c}.

Furthermore, because the standard gaussian vector
g_{1}:=(g_{11},\dots,g_{1N})
is independent of
\sigma
under the cavity measure, by the rotational invariance of
g_{1}
we have that
\langle g_{1},\sigma\rangle\sim g\|\sigma\|
where
g
is an independent standard gaussian variable, so under the cavity measure
S_{1}(\sigma)\sim g\|\sigma\|/\sqrt{N}=g\sqrt{R_{1,1}}
. Thus, by Assumption
1

{\mathbb{E}}\langle|u_{1}|^{k}\rangle_{c}\leqslant d^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\langle(1+2g^{2}R_{1,1}+2z_{1}^{2})^{k}\rangle_{c}.

Finally, by the independence of
R_{1,1}
from

and
z_{1}
and Proposition
4.1
, the right-hand side of this last equation is easily seen to be smaller than some constant that does not depend on
N
.

\square

Lemma 5.2

There exist constants

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,1}\rangle_{c}-{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,1}\rangle\Big|\leqslant\frac{K}{\sqrt{N}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{c}-{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle\Big|\leqslant\frac{K^{\prime}}{\sqrt{N}}.

M_{\ell}:=N^{-1}\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}^{\ell}.
A direct differentiation gives that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}F_{N,t}=(h-h_{N})\langle M_{1}\rangle_{t}

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=2N(h-h_{N})\langle R_{1,2}(M_{1}-M_{3})\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=2N(h-h_{N})\langle(R_{1,2}-\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{t})(M_{1}-\langle M_{1}\rangle_{t})\rangle_{t}.

From these, the mean value theorem and the Hölder inequality, there exists
t\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big|F_{N}(h_{N})-F_{N}(h)\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigl({\mathbb{E}}|h_{N}-h|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl({\mathbb{E}}\langle M_{1}^{2}\rangle_{t}\bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigl({\mathbb{E}}|h_{N}-h|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\langle\|\sigma^{1}\|^{2}\rangle_{t}}{N}\Bigr)^{1/2}

(65)

and some
t\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\bigl|{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{h_{N}}-{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{h}\bigr|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2N\bigl({\mathbb{E}}|h_{N}-h|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl({\mathbb{E}}\bigl\langle\bigl|R_{1,2}-\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{t}\bigr|^{4}\bigr\rangle_{t}\Bigr)^{1/4}\Bigl({\mathbb{E}}\bigl\langle\bigl|M_{1}-\langle M_{1}\rangle_{t}\bigr|^{4}\bigr\rangle_{t}\Bigr)^{1/4}.

(66)

The same proof as of Lemma
4.4
yields that for any
k\geqslant 1
there exists some
K>0
such that for all
t\in[0,1]
and
N\geqslant 1,

\displaystyle\langle\|\sigma\|^{2k}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant KN^{k}\bigl(\|\bar{z}\|^{2k}+\|\bar{G}\|_{op}^{2k}+h^{2k}+h_{N}^{2k}+1\bigr).

(67)

Since the expectation of every term in the bracket is bounded by a universal constant, we obtain (
62
) by using (
7
) and taking
k=1.

As for (
63
), note that for any fixed
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

f(\sigma):=N^{-1}w\cdot\sigma
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
\|w\|/N
and that the Hessian of the interpolated Hamiltonian
H_{N,t}(\sigma)
in
F_{N,t}
is bounded from above by
-\kappa{\rm Id}/2.
From the Brascamp-Lieb inequality
[
15
, Theorem 5.1]
,

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\Bigl|\frac{w\cdot\sigma^{1}}{N}-\frac{w\cdot\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}}{N}\Bigr|^{4}\Bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K\|w\|^{4}}{N^{4}},

(68)

where
K
is a constant independent of
w,N,t.
Therefore, letting
w=(1,\ldots,1)
implies

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\bigl|M_{1}-\langle M_{1}\rangle_{t}\bigr|^{4}\bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K\|w\|^{4}}{N^{4}}=\frac{K}{N^{2}}.

(69)

In addition, writing

\displaystyle R_{1,2}-\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{1}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}-\frac{\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}+\frac{\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}-\frac{\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\cdot\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle_{t}}{N},

we can apply (
68
) with the choices
w=\sigma^{2}
and
w=\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}
to get

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\Bigl|\frac{\sigma^{1}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}-\frac{\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}\Bigr|^{4}\Bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K\langle\|\sigma^{2}\|^{4}\rangle_{t}}{N^{4}}=\frac{K\langle\|\sigma^{1}\|^{4}\rangle_{t}}{N^{4}}

and

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\Bigl|\frac{\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\cdot\sigma^{2}}{N}-\frac{\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\cdot\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle_{t}}{N}\Bigr|^{4}\Bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K\|\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle_{t}\|^{4}}{N^{4}}\leqslant\frac{K\langle\|\sigma^{1}\|^{4}\rangle_{t}}{N^{4}}.

Putting these together and applying (
67
) with
k=2
, we obtain that for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
and
N\geqslant 1,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigl\langle\bigl|R_{1,2}-\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{t}\bigr|^{4}\bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{32K{\mathbb{E}}\langle\|\sigma^{1}\|^{4}\rangle_{t}}{N^{4}}\leqslant\frac{K^{\prime}}{N^{2}}

(70)

for some constant
K^{\prime}
independent of
t,N.

Plugging (
69
) and (
70
) into (
7
) validates (
63
).
∎

Now we proceed to establish (
22
) and (
23
). From Theorem
3.3
and (
15
),

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=q

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\|\langle x\rangle^{\sim}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\|\langle\sigma\rangle_{h_{N}}\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\langle R_{1,2}\rangle_{h_{N}}.

These and (
63
) together yields (
23
).

As for (
22
), from (
13
), we can write

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{N}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\gamma_{N}+F_{N}(x^{*}),

where

\displaystyle F_{N}(x^{*}):=\frac{1}{N}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k\leqslant M}u\Big((\bar{G}O^{\intercal}\sigma)_{k}-z_{k}\Big)-h_{N}\sum_{i\leqslant N}\sigma_{i}-\kappa\|\sigma\|^{2}\Big)\,d\sigma

and
O
is an orthonormal matrix satisfying
Ox^{*}=N^{-1/2}{\|x^{*}\|}\bm{1}_{N}.
Write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{F}_{N}-{\mathbb{E}}\widetilde{F}_{N}&=-\kappa(\gamma_{N}-{\mathbb{E}}\gamma_{N})\\
&\qquad+F_{N}(x^{*})-{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*}]\\
&\qquad+{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*}]-{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(x^{*}).\end{split}

(71)

Here the first term on the right-hand side vanishes in
L^{2}
norm by the given assumption. For the second term,
observe that conditionally on
x^{*}
,
F_{N}(x^{*})
is the same as
F_{N}(h_{N})
in distribution.
Lemma
4.9
(with
n=1
and
\lambda=\eta=0
) allows us to write that with probability one,

\displaystyle\mbox{Var}(F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*})=\mbox{Var}(F_{N}(h_{N})\mid h_{N})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K(1+h_{N}^{4})}{N},

where
K
is independent of
N.
Hence, we can bound the second term by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigl|F_{N}(x^{*})-{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*}]\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big({\mathbb{E}}\bigl|F_{N}(x^{*})-{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*}]\bigr|^{2}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\big({\mathbb{E}}\,\mbox{Var}(F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*})\big)^{1/2}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{K(1+{\mathbb{E}}h_{N}^{4})}{N}}.

Lastly, write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*}]-{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(x^{*})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(h_{N})\mid h_{N}]-{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(h_{N})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(h_{N})-F_{N}(h)\mid h_{N}]+\bigl({\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(h)-{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(h_{N})\bigr).

We bound the third term in (
71
) by using the Jensen inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigl|{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(x^{*})\mid x^{*}]-{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(x^{*})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\bigl|{\mathbb{E}}[F_{N}(h_{N})-F_{N}(h)\mid h_{N}]\bigr|+\bigl|{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(h)-{\mathbb{E}}F_{N}(h_{N})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\bigl|F_{N}(h_{N})-F_{N}(h)\bigr|\to 0,

where the last limit used (
62
). Combined with Theorem
3.2
and the fact that
{\mathbb{E}}h_{N}^{4}
is bounded by assumption (
1
), this completes our proof for (
22
).
