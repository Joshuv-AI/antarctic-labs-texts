Stochastic Contextual Bandits with Long Horizon Rewards
Yuzhen Qin, Yingcong Li, Fabio Pasqualetti, Maryam Fazel, Samet Oymak

Abstract
The growing interest in complex decision-making and language modeling problems highlights the importance of sample-efficient learning over very long horizons. This work takes a step in this direction by investigating contextual linear bandits where the current reward depends on at most s s prior actions and contexts (not necessarily consecutive), up to a time horizon of h h . In order to avoid polynomial dependence on h h , we propose new algorithms that leverage sparsity to discover the dependence pattern and arm parameters jointly. We consider both the data-poor ( T < h T<h ) and data-rich ( T ≥ h T\geq h ) regimes, and derive respective regret upper bounds O ~ ​ ( d ​ s ​ T + min ⁡ { q , T } ) \tilde{O}(d\sqrt{sT}+\min\{q,T\}) and O ~ ​ ( s ​ d ​ T ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{sdT}) , with sparsity s s , feature dimension d d , total time horizon T T , and q q that is adaptive to the reward dependence pattern. Complementing upper bounds, we also show that learning over a single trajectory brings inherent challenges: While the dependence pattern and arm parameters form a rank-1 matrix, circulant matrices are not isometric over rank-1 manifolds and sample complexity indeed benefits from the sparse reward dependence structure. Our results necessitate a new analysis to address long-range temporal dependencies across data and avoid polynomial dependence on the reward horizon h h . Specifically, we utilize connections to the restricted isometry property of circulant matrices formed by dependent sub-Gaussian vectors and establish new guarantees that are also of independent interest.

Introduction

Multi-armed bandits (MAB) serve as a prototypical model to study exploration-exploitation trade-off in sequential decision-making (e.g., see
Bubeck et al. 2012
). The agent needs to repeatedly make decisions by interacting with an unknown environment, aiming to maximize the cumulative reward. As a generalization of MAB, the contextual bandits allow the agent to take actions based on contextual information
Chu et al. 2007
. Extensive studies have been conducted on contextual bandits due to its wide applications such as clinical trials, recommendation, and advertising (e.g., see
Woodroofe 1979
;
Chu et al. 2011
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2010
;
Qin et al. 2022b
;
Qin et al. 2022a
).

Most existing work on contextual bandits assume that each reward only depends on a single action and the associated context. This action can be the one just taken (instantaneous reward) or the one taken a certain number of steps before (delayed rewards). However, in realistic decision-making scenarios, the reward generating process can have a more complex, non-Markovian nature. Multiple prior actions can jointly affect the current reward. For instance, whether a learner will take a course recommended by an online education platform depends not only on that course, but also on what combination of courses they have taken before.
Recommending courses in a complicated curriculum to users with diverse backgrounds and past experiences requires accounting for the combined effects of past contexts on the current recommendation.
Similarly, the attention mechanism
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
is finding increasing success in reinforcement learning and NLP applications
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
and it makes predictions by assessing the similarities between current and past contexts (e.g., that correspond to words in a sentence or frames in a video game) and creating a history-weighted adaptive context. In connection to this, the benefit of using a long context history has been well acknowledged in RL and control theory (e.g. frame/state stacking practice
Hessel et al. 2018
). These observations motivates the following central question:

Q:

Can we provably and efficiently learn from long-horizon rewards? What is the role of reward dependence structure in sample efficiency?

In this work, we thoroughly address these questions for a novel variation of stochastic linear contextual bandits problems, where the current reward depends on
a subset of prior contexts, up to a time horizon of
h
(see Fig.
1
for an illustration). Specifically, the reward is determined by a
filtered context
that is a linear combination of prior
h
selected contexts.
Moreover, inspired by practical decision making scenarios, we account for sparse interactions where only
s
(
s\ll h
) of
h
prior contexts actually contributing to the current reward. Here
s=1
corresponds to the special instance of delayed rewards. Crucially, we develop strategies that leverage this sparse dependence structure of the reward function and establish regret guarantees for long horizon rewards.

1.1
Related Work

1.1.1
Composite anonymous rewards.

Pike-Burke et al. 2018
considered bandits with composite anonymous rewards, where 1) the reward that the agent receives at each round is the sum of the delayed rewards of an unknown subset of past actions, and 2) individual contributions of past actions to the reward are not discernible.
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2018
generalized this setting to a case where the reward generated by an action is not simply revealed to the agent at a single instant in the future, but rather spreads over multiple rounds. Recent work along this line is also found in
Garg and Akash 2019
,
Zhang et al. 2022
, and
Wang et al. 2021
.
In this paper, we consider a contextual setting with, which is different from the above ones and poses new challenges since each arm no longer has a fixed reward distribution.

1.1.2
Delayed rewards.

Bandits with delayed rewards are also related to our work. Stochastic linear bandits with random delayed rewards was studied in
Vernade et al. 2020
.
Li et al. 2019
investigated the case where the delay is unknown. Generalized stochastic linear bandits with random delays were studied in
Zhou et al. 2019
and
Howson et al. 2022
.
Cella and Cesa-Bianchi 2020
and
Lancewicki et al. 2021
studied bandit problems with reward-dependent delays. In
Lancewicki et al. 2021
and
Thune et al. 2019
, delays are allowed to be unrestricted. Arm-dependent delays in stochastic bandits are studied in
Gael et al. 2020
. Delays are also considered in adversarial bandits
(
Bistritz et al. 2019
;
Gyorgy and Joulani 2021
;
Zimmert and Seldin 2020
)
.
Recently, non-stochastic cooperative linear bandits with delays have also been studied
(
Ito et al. 2020
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2019
)
. In fact, our setting captures unknown fixed delays and also aggregated and anonymous delayed rewards.

1.1.3
Sparse parameters.

In sparse bandits, feature vectors can have large dimension
d
, but only a small subset,
s\ll d
, of them affect rewards.
Early studies on sparse
linear bandits
are found in
Carpentier and Munos 2012
and
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2012
. Recent results studied both the data-poor and data-rich regimes, depending on whether the total horizon
T
is less or larger than
d
. In the data-rich regime,
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
proved a regret lower bound
\Omega(\sqrt{sdT})
. In the data-poor regime,
Hao et al. 2020
showed a regret lower bound
\Omega(s^{\frac{1}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}})
. A recent work used information-directed sampling techniques
Hao et al. 2021
. Sparse
contextual linear bandits
also receive increasing interests.
Kim and Paik 2019
proposed an algorithm that combines Lasso with doubly-robust techniques, and provided an upper bound
O(s\log(dT)\sqrt{T})
. An extended setting wherein each arm has its own parameter was studied in
Bastani and Bayati 2020
,
Wang et al. 2018
, where upper bounds
O(s^{2}\log^{2}(T))
and
O(s^{2}\log(T))
were shown, respectively.
Oh et al. 2021
proposed an exploration-free algorithm and obtained an upper bound
O(s^{2}\log(d)+s\sqrt{T\log(dT)})
.
In
Ariu et al. 2022
, a thresholded Lasso algorithm is presented, resulting in an upper bound
O(s^{2}\log(d)+\sqrt{sT})
. In
Ren and Zhou 2020
, the dynamic batch learning approach was used and a upper bound
O(s\cdot{\rm polylog}(d)+\log(T)\sqrt{sT\log(d)})
was obtained. In comparison, sparsity in our case results from the reward dependence structure. As we will discuss in Sec.
2.2
, learning the dependence pattern is challenging since the measurements have an inherent circulant structure.

1.1.4
Online Convex Optimization (OCO).

Another line of research related to ours is OCO with memory where the losses depend on the past decisions taken from a convex set
(
Anava et al. 2015
;
Shi et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2022
)
.

1.2
Contributions

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

1. We introduce a new contextual bandit model, motivated by realistic scenarios where rewards have a long-range and sparse dependence on prior actions and contexts. The problem of identifying the reward parameter and sparse delay pattern admits a special low-rank and sparse structure.

2. We propose two sample-efficient algorithms for the data-poor and data-rich regimes by leveraging sparsity prior. For the former, we prove a regret upper bound
O\big(d\sqrt{sT\log(dT)}+\min\{q,T\}\big)
that is adaptive to the reward dependence pattern described by
q
; for the latter, we obtain a regret upper bound
O(\sqrt{sdT\log(dT)})
. Note that neither of the bounds has polynominal dependence on the horizon
h
, enabling efficient learning across long horizons; and both are optimal in
T
(up to logarithmic factors).

3. We make technical contributions to address temporal dependencies within data that has a block-Toeplitz/circulant matrix form. First, the seminal work by
Krahmer et al. 2014
on Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) of circulant matrices assume context vectors have i.i.d. entries. We generalize their result to milder concentrability conditions that allow dependencies. Second, we establish results that highlight the challenges of low-rank estimation unique to circulant measurements. In line with theory, numerical experiments demonstrate that our sparsity-based approach indeed outperforms low-rank ones.

Problem Setting

Notation.
Given
{\bm{x}}=[{\bm{x}}_{1}^{\top},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{h}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{dh}
with each block
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denote
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2,1}^{(d)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{h}\|{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{2}
; for
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}:=\sup_{\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\leq 1}\|\bm{A}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}
denotes its operator norm. Let
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
for any integer
n
. For any
\mathcal{S}\subset[n]
,
{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the sub-vector of
{\bm{x}}
with entries indexed by
\mathcal{S}
. Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be the inner product; for
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\rangle={\rm Tr}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{B})
. Let
\otimes
be the Kronecker product. Given
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, it is said to satisfy RIP if there is
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
(1-\delta)\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\bm{A}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}
holds for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
; the smallest

2.1
Stochastic Linear Contextual Bandits

In this paper, we study a stochastic linear contextual bandit problem with rewards that depend on past actions and contexts (see Fig.
1
for an illustration).
Let
K
be the number of arms, and then the action set is
[K]
. At each round
t
, the agent observes
K
context vectors,
\{{\bm{x}}_{t,a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a\in[K]\}
, each associated with an arm and drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution
\nu
. It then selects an action
a_{t}\in[K]
and receives a reward generated by

r_{t}=\langle{\bm{y}}_{t,a_{t}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle+\varepsilon_{t},

(1)

where

{\bm{y}}_{t,a_{t}}=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}w_{i}{\bm{x}}_{t-i,a_{t-i}},\text{ and }t\in[T].

Here
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the coefficient vector,
\varepsilon_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is additive noise that is zero-mean 1-sub-Gaussian. Particularly, the vector
{\bm{y}}_{t,a_{t}}
is the
filtered context
, determined by the weight vector
{\bm{w}}:=[w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{h-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
that describes how rewards depend on the past and current selected contexts (where
w_{i}\geq 0
). The range of the dependence
h
can be very large, indicating that a reward can have a long-range contextual dependence. Assume
{\bm{x}}_{j,a_{j}}=0
for
j\leq 0
since
a_{j}
’s in this case correspond to nonexistent actions.

In this paper, we consider sparse contextual dependence, that is, the weight vector
{\bm{w}}
is
s
-sparse (i.e.,
\|{\bm{w}}\|_{0}\leq s
) with
s\ll h
. This is particularly relevant to many realistic situations since often only a small number of past “events” matter. As we mentioned before, this setting captures: a) bandits with unknown delays (
{\bm{w}}
has only one non-zero entry and it is
1
-valued), and b) bandits with aggregated and anonymous rewards (all the non-zero entries of
{\bm{w}}
are
1
-valued).

The coefficient vector
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the weight vector
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
are unknown. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}\leq 1
and
\|{\bm{w}}\|_{1}\leq 1
. We also made the mild boundedness assumption that
{\bm{x}}_{t,a}
satisfies
\|{\bm{x}}_{t,a}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for all
t\in[T]
and
a\in[K]
.

The agent’s objective is to maximize the cumulative reward over the course of
T
rounds, or equivalently, to minimize the pseudo-regret defined as

R_{T}=\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}{w_{i}}\left(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,a^{*}_{t}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle-\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,a_{t}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle\right)\right],

(2)

where
a^{*}_{t}=\argmax_{a\in[K]}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,a},\bm{\theta}\rangle
defines the optimal action at round
t
.

Remark 1
.

The definition of the regret here is slightly different from simply summing up
\langle{\bm{y}}_{t,a_{t}^{*}}-{\bm{y}}_{t,a_{t}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle
. In fact, the two regret definitions are essentially the same. The reason is that taking an action, say
a_{t}
, gives the agent a total reward
\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}w_{i}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,a_{t}},\bm{\theta}\rangle
that spreads over the next
h
rounds. Therefore, the agent can make decisions without knowing
{\bm{w}}
if
\bm{\theta}
is known as a prior: a greedy strategy seeking to maximize the instantaneous reward at each round maximizes the cumulative reward in the long run. Further discussion on this point can be found in Appendix
F.1
.
\triangle

Remark 2
.

Although the only knowledge of
\bm{\theta}
seems sufficient for our decision-making purpose, learning
\bm{\theta}
is actually challenging. This is because each reward can come in a composite manner, possibly consisting of the contributions from the latest
h
actions. Learning
\bm{\theta}
requires to sort out the reward dependence structure.
\triangle

2.2
Discussion of Challenges

Next, we discuss some technical challenges inherent in our problem. For this purpose, we denote
\bm{\xi}_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t,a_{t}}
as the chosen context for each
t
to make notation concise.

2.2.1
Circulant design matrices in low-rank matrix recovery.

Let
\bm{Z}_{t}=[\bm{\xi}_{t},\bm{\xi}_{t-1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{t-h+1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h}
, and then (
1
) can be rewritten as

r_{t}=\langle\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{\theta}{\bm{w}}^{\top}\rangle+\varepsilon_{t}.

(3)

At first glance, it seems that the problem reduces to reconstructing the rank-
1
and sparse matrix
\bm{\theta}{\bm{w}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h}
, and classic techniques for low-rank (and sparse) matrix recovery can be applied (e.g.,
Richard et al. 2012
,
Oymak et al. 2015

Davenport and Romberg 2016
, and
Wainwright 2019
). However, we find this is
not
true due to the
Toeplitz/circulant structure
of the design matrices
Z_{t}
. The following lemma shows that circulant matrices, even if its first row has i.i.d. entries, do not obey RIP for rank-1 matrices with exponentially high probability (see Appendix
C
for more details).

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\bf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be a subsampled circulant matrix whose first row has i.i.d. Gaussian entries (normalized properly) and
n\leq p
. For any
\delta\leq 1
, there exists a constant
c<1
such that with probability at least
1-c^{p}
,
{\bf{C}}
does not obey RIP over rank-
1
matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
with
p=p_{1}p_{2}
.

We further provide numerical experiments in Fig.
2
to show that circulant measurements are indeed problematic while dealing with low-rank matrix recovery.

{\bm{a}}_{t}=\{a_{t},a_{t-1},\dots,a_{1}\}
denote the sequence of past actions,
{\bm{z}}_{{\bm{a}}_{t}}=[\bm{\xi}_{t}^{\top},\bm{\xi}_{t-1}^{\top},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{t-h+1}^{\top}]^{\top}
, and
\bm{\phi}={\bm{w}}\otimes{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dh}
. Then, (
1
) can be rewritten as

r_{t}=\langle{\bm{z}}_{{\bm{a}}_{t}},\bm{\phi}\rangle+\varepsilon_{t}.

(4)

Since
{\bm{w}}
is
s
-sparse, reconstructing
\bm{\theta}
and
{\bm{w}}
becomes to estimate the
s
-block-sparse vector
\bm{\phi}
.

Denote
{\bm{r}}_{t}=[r_{1},\dots,r_{t}]^{\top}
and
{\bm{{\varepsilon}}}_{t}=[\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{t}]^{\top}

4

{\bm{r}}_{t}=\scalebox{0.75}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}_{1}^{\top}&0&\cdots&0\\
\bm{\xi}_{2}^{\top}&\bm{\xi}_{1}^{\top}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

(5)

One can observe that the design matrix

2.2.2
Circulant matrices with dependent entries.

Estimating
\bm{\phi}
is a sparse regression problem. RIP and related restricted eigenvalue condition (REC) are widely used for such problems
(
Candes and Tao 2007
;
Bickel et al. 2009
)
. Earlier studies show that sub-sampled circulant matrices whose first row is i.i.d. sub-Gaussian satisfy RIP for
s
-sparse vectors if there are at least
\tilde{\Omega}(s\log^{2}(s))
samples
(
Krahmer et al. 2014
)
. In our case, the circulant matrix is generated by random vectors with
dependent
entries (i.e., entries in each
\bm{\xi}_{t}
may be dependent). The new challenge is: how many samples are needed for such circulant measurements to satisfy RIP/REC?

We first present a technical result on RIP that paves the way for the analysis of our bandit problem, which is also of independent interest (see Appendix Part I for the proof).

Theorem 1

(6)

{\bm{C}}=\scalebox{0.9}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\top}&\bm{\xi}_{n-1}^{\top}&\cdots&\bm{\xi}_{1}^{\top}\\

Experiments.
We perform some experiments to compare our algorithm AD-Lasso with the following three:

1.

Sparse-Alternating Gradient Descent
(SA-GD). The core of SA-GD is rank-1 and then sparse matrix estimation. Based on (
3
), SA-GD alternatively reconstructs
\bm{\theta}
and
{\bm{w}}
by gradient descent, and projects
{\bm{w}}
to the
s
-sparse space.

2.

Single-Weight Matching Pursuit
(SW-MP). The core of SW-MP is to locate the
largest
weight in
{\bm{w}}
by testing the correlation between the reward vector and the columns of the context matrix. Then, with this location information,
\bm{\theta}
is estimated simply by the least-squares regression, ignoring other weights in
{\bm{w}}
.

3.

UCB with Matching Pursuit
(UCB-MP). This algorithm is similar to SW-MP; the difference is that in each epoch we use UCB to update
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
and make decisions.

To facilitate fair comparison, we use the same doubling scheme with identical epoch lengths for all the algorithms. The only difference is the method we use to estimate
{\bm{\theta}}
(see Appendix
G
for more details of these algorithms). Different sparsity and reward dependence structure are considered in the experiments (see the caption in Fig.
6
).

Our algorithm outperforms SA-GD significantly when
{\bm{w}}
is highly sparse (see (a1), (a2), (b1), and (b2)). Since SA-GD is primarily reliant on rank-1 factorization, this indicates that, relative to low-rankness, sparsity plays a more dominant role in the estimation quality in line with our theory. Surprisingly, as
{\bm{w}}
becomes less sparse, our algorithm can still outperform SA-GD, even in the regime
sd>d+h
. This supports the difficulty of low-rank matrix estimation with circulant measurements, which is consistent with our discussion in Sec.
2.2
. Yet, stronger theoretical analysis is desirable to formalize these findings beyond our Lemma
1
.

AD-Lasso performs as well as SW-MP and UCB-MP, even when the weights of
{\bm{w}}
are highly concentrated over few entries. When the weights are more spread out, AD-Lasso works much better, indicating that simply exploring and exploiting the largest weight becomes suboptimal.

4
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we introduce a novel variation of the stochastic contextual bandits problem, where the reward depends on
s
prior contexts, up to a time horizon of
h
. Leveraging the sparsity in the reward dependence pattern, we propose two algorithms that account for both the data-poor and data-rich regimes. We also derive horizon-independent (up to
\log(h)
terms) regret upper bounds for both algorithms, establishing that their sample efficiency is theoretically guaranteed.

Our work opens up many future potential directions. For instance, the reward can depend on the prior contexts in a nonlinear fashion or sparsity pattern can vary in a data-dependent fashion. In either scenarios learning the reward dependence pattern will be more challenging. Also, beyond bandit problems, it is of interest to explore RL and control scenarios with long-term non-markovian structures where new strategies will be required.

B.1
Bound of the off-block-diagonal term

To show the bound of the off-block-diagonal term, the analysis is essentially the same as to that in
Krahmer et al. 2014
. The main difference is that we derive a new decoupling inequality in Theorem

(see there for a discussion of our contribution). We provide a complete proof here to be self-contained.

Lemma B.1
.

The off-block-diagonal term

satisfies

\displaystyle\Pr\left[O_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})\geq c_{1}E+t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{2}\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{V^{2}},\frac{t}{U}\right\}\right),

(A13)

where

\displaystyle E=\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm op}})\Big(\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm op}})+d_{F}(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{p}d_{2\to 2}(\mathcal{A})\Big),

(A14)

\displaystyle V=d_{2\to 2}(\mathcal{A})\Big(d_{F}(\mathcal{A})+\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm op}})\Big),

(A15)

\displaystyle U=d_{2\to 2}^{2}(\mathcal{A}).

(A16)

Here, the quantities
d_{F}(\mathcal{A})
,

, and
\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm op}})
satisfy

\displaystyle d_{F}(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|\bm{A}\|_{F},

\displaystyle d_{2\to 2}(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\bm{A}\|_{{\rm op}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm op}})\leq c\int_{0}^{d_{2\to 2(\mathcal{A})}}\sqrt{\log N(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{\rm op},u)du},

where

is the covering number of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the metric
(\|\cdot\|_{\rm op},u)
.

To construct the proof of the Lemma

, we need the following two lemmas from

.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of matrices, let
\bm{\xi}
be a sub-Gaussian random vector, and let
\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
\bm{\xi}
. Then for any

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\bm{A}\bm{\xi},\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle\right\|_{L_{1}}\lesssim\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm op}})\cdot\|N_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})\|_{L_{1}}+\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\langle\bm{A}\bm{\xi},\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle\|_{L_{1}},

where

.

.

If

is an independent copy of

, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\langle\bm{A}\bm{\xi},\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle\|_{L_{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{p}d_{F}(\mathcal{A})d_{2\to 2}(\mathcal{A})+pd_{2\to 2}^{2}(\mathcal{A}).

Suppose

\displaystyle\Pr\big(|z|\geq e(\alpha+\beta\sqrt{u}+\gamma y)\big)\leq e^{-u}.

\displaystyle\|O_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})\|_{L_{1}}

Theorem B.1

(Decoupling Inequality)
.

Let
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be independent random vectors satisfying
\mathbb{E}{\bm{x}}_{i}=\bm{0}
. Let
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}_{1}^{\top},{\bm{x}}_{2}^{\top},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
. Define

\mathcal{B}_{o}:=\left\{\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{dn\times dn}\right\}

with

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{B}_{11}&\bm{B}_{12}&\dots&\bm{B}_{1n}\\
\bm{B}_{21}&\bm{B}_{22}&\dots&\bm{B}_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{B}_{n1}&\bm{B}_{n2}&\dots&\bm{B}_{nn}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{B}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\text{ for all }i,j.

Let
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
{\bm{x}}
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|4\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right|.

Remark 11
.

In
Rauhut 2010
, a similar decoupling inequality was presented, where
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
are
1
-dimensional independent random variables. Here, we show that it can be generalized to multi-variate independent random vectors, without requiring the entries in each vector to be also independent. The core of the proof of this theorem follows from
Rauhut 2010
.

Proof.

Consider a sequence of independent random variable
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{n}
with each
\delta_{i}
taking the values
0
and
1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. For
j\neq k
,
\mathbb{E}\delta_{j}(1-\delta_{k})=\frac{1}{4}
. Let
\delta=(\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{n})
.

Since for any
\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}
,
\bm{B}_{ii}=\mathbf{0}
for all
i
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|\leq

\displaystyle 4\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}(1-\delta_{j})]{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},\delta}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}\delta_{i}(1-\delta_{j}){\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|.

Next, we define the set
\mathcal{I}(\delta):=\{k\in[n]:\delta_{k}=1\}
. Then, it follows from the Fubini’s theorem that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|\leq 4\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\delta)}\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta)}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|.

For a fixed
\delta
, one can see that
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\delta)}
and
\{{\bm{x}}_{j}\}_{j\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta)}
are independent. Therefore, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|\leq 4\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\delta)}\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta)}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right|.

Then, there exists
\delta_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|\leq 4\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right|.

Since
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}]=\bm{0}
, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}\left(\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}\mathbb{E}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right)+\sum_{i\notin\mathcal{I}(\delta_{0})}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right|.

Applying the Fubini’s theorem, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right|\leq 4\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{o}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{B}_{ij}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{j}\right|,

which completes the proof.
∎

B.2
Bound of the block-diagonal term

Now, we turn to bound the block-diagonal term
D_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})
. Define
\mathcal{B}:=\{\bm{B}=\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}:\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times nd}\}
. Recall that
\mathcal{A}
is defined after (
A8
). Given any
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{B}_{11}&\bm{B}_{12}&\cdots&\bm{B}_{1n}\\
\bm{B}_{21}&\bm{B}_{22}&\cdots&\bm{B}_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{B}_{n1}&\bm{B}_{n2}&\cdots&\bm{B}_{nn}\end{bmatrix}.

One can see that
\bm{B}_{ij}=\bm{B}_{ji}
. Since
\bm{B}
is determined by
\bm{A}
, we denote
\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})={\rm Blkdiag}(\bm{B}_{11},\bm{B}_{22},\dots,\bm{B}_{nn})
be the block-diagonal matrix associated with
\bm{B}=\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}
. Then,
D_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})
can be rewritten into

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})=\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{A}^{i})^{\top}\bm{A}^{i}\bm{\xi}_{i}-\langle\bm{A}^{i},\bm{A}^{i}\rangle\right)\right|=\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\big|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\big|.

We have the following result for
D_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})
.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
\bm{\xi}_{1},\bm{\xi}_{2},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{n}
be independent random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\bm{\xi}=[\bm{\xi}_{1}^{\top},\bm{\xi}_{2}^{\top},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\top}]^{\top}
. Assume that
\bm{\xi}
satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality (
A4
). Then, there exists a constant
k
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}D_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\xi})\geq t\right]\leq 2N\Big(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{F},\frac{t}{6nd}\Big)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{ck^{2}}\min(mt^{2},mt)\right),

(A18)

where
N(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{F},u)
is the covering number of
u
-cover of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the metric
\|\cdot\|_{F}
.

Proof.

We construct the proof in two parts:

1.

In part I, we prove

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\big|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\big|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{ck_{1}^{2}}\min(mt^{2},mt)\right).

(A19)

Since it follows from the Hanson-Wright inequality (
A4
) that there is
k_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\big|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\big|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{ck_{1}^{2}}\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{F}^{2}\|},\frac{t}{\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{{\rm op}}}\right)\right),

(A20)

we will just show

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m},

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{{\rm op}}\leq\frac{1}{m}.

2.

In part II, we complete the proof following a similar covering argument as in the proof of
Foucart and Rauhut 2013
.

Part I-
Step 1
:
In this step, we bound
\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{F}^{2}
.
For every
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|B_{ii}\|_{F}^{2}
. Since
\bm{B}=\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\bm{R}_{\Omega}V
with
\bm{V}
given in (
A7
), we have

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{ii}=\frac{1}{m}(\bm{V}^{i})^{\top}\bm{R}_{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{R}_{\Omega}\bm{V}^{i},

(A21)

where
\bm{V}^{i}
is the
i
th block column of
\bm{V}
. Recall the definition of
\bm{R}_{\Omega}
, one can derive that

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{ii}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j\in\Omega}(\bm{V}^{i}_{j})^{\top}\bm{V}^{i}_{j},

(A22)

where
\bm{V}^{i}_{j}
is the
j
th row of
\bm{V}^{i}
.
Due to the block-diagonal structure of
\bm{B}
, it holds that
\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{B}_{ii}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{B}_{ii}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Big\|\sum_{j\in\Omega}(\bm{V}^{i}_{j})^{\top}\bm{V}^{i}_{j}\Big\|_{F}^{2}\right).

Using the expression of
\bm{V}
defined in (
A7
), one can derive that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Big\|\sum_{j\in\Omega}(\bm{V}^{i}_{j})^{\top}\bm{V}^{i}_{j}\Big\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}\right)+2m\sum_{i\neq j}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}\|{\bm{a}}_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}^{\top}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=m(\|{\bm{a}}_{1}\|^{2}+\dots+\|{\bm{a}}_{n}\|^{2})^{2},

where the last inequality has used the fact that
\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}\|_{F}=\|{\bm{a}}_{i}\|^{2}
.
Since
\|{\bm{a}}_{1}\|^{2}+\dots+\|{\bm{a}}_{n}\|^{2}\leq 1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{B}_{ii}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot m=\frac{1}{m},

which proves
\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m}
.

Part I-
Step 2
:
In this step, we bound
\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|D(\bm{A})\|_{{\rm op}}
. Since
\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})
is symmetric for the
\mathbb{D}(A)
associated with any
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, by the definition of the operator norm, we know
\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{{\rm op}}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))
.
Since
\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})
has the diagonal form
{\rm Blkdiag}(\bm{B}_{11},\bm{B}_{22},\dots,\bm{B}_{nn})
, one can derive that
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\bm{B}_{ii}):i=1,2,\dots,p\}
. Given the expressions of
\bm{B}_{ii}
shown in (
A22
), it follows from Lemma
B.5
that
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{B}_{ii})\leq\frac{1}{m}
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
, which implies that
\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\|_{{\rm op}}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\leq\frac{1}{m}
.

Part II:
Let
\mathcal{A}_{u}
be the smallest
u
-cover of the set
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the metric
\|\cdot\|_{F}
, and its cardinality is
N(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{F},u)
. It holds that
\min_{A_{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{u}}\|\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{u}\|_{F}\leq u
for all
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
. From the inequality (
A19
), one can derive that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\exists\bm{A}_{u}\in\mathcal{\bm{A}}_{u}:\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u}))\right|>\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\bm{A}_{u}\in\mathcal{\bm{A}}_{u}}\Pr\left[\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u}))\right|>\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2N(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{F},u)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{ck_{1}^{2}}\min(m\eta^{2},m\eta)\right),

where the second inequality follows from Lemma
B.6
.
Then, one can derive that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sup_{\bm{A}_{u}\in\mathcal{\bm{A}}_{u}}\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u}))\right|\leq\eta\right]\geq 1-2N(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{F},u)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{ck_{1}^{2}}\min(m\eta^{2},m\eta)\right).

Now, observe that for any
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u}))+\bm{\xi}^{\top}\big(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\big)\bm{\xi}+{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u}))-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u}))\right|+\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\big(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\big)\bm{\xi}\right|+\left|{\rm Tr}\big(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\big)\right|.

Assume that
\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\right|\leq\eta
, and we arrive at

\displaystyle\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\right|\leq\eta+\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\|_{2}\|\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}+nd\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\|_{2},

where
\bm{A}_{u}
is chosen such that
\|\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{u}\|_{F}\leq u
.
Now, observe that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{u}\|_{F},

and

\displaystyle\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{u}=(\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{u})^{\top}(\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{u})+\bm{A}_{u}^{\top}(\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{u})+(\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{u})^{\top}\bm{A}_{u}.

Using the fact that
\|\bm{A}_{u}\|_{F}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})-\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}_{u})\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{u}\|_{F}\leq u^{2}+2u

Since
\bm{\xi}
satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality (
A4
),
\|\bm{\xi}\|_{2}^{2}\leq nd
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-nd)
. Therefore,
\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\right|\leq\eta+2nd(u^{2}+2u)
. Choose
\eta=\frac{t}{2}
and
u=\frac{t}{6nd}
, we deduce, using the fact
u^{2}+2u<3u
for
u<1
, that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sup_{\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{A})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{A}))\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2N\Big(\mathcal{A},\|\cdot\|_{F},\frac{t}{6nd}\Big)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4ck_{1}^{2}}\min(mt^{2},mt)\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

B.3
Useful Lemmas and Theorems

Lemma B.5
.

Let
{\bm{a}}=[{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\top},{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\top},\dots,{\bm{a}}_{n}^{\top}]^{\top}
, where
{\bm{a}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, be a vector that satisfies
\|{\bm{a}}\|\leq 1
. Consider the matrix
\bm{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}
, where
k_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n
. Then, the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{P}
satisfies
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{P})\leq 1
for any possible combinations of
k_{i}
’s.

Proof.

It can be seen that the matrix
\bm{P}
satisfies
\bm{P}^{\top}=\bm{P}
. Therefore,
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{P})=\|\bm{P}\|
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\bm{P})=\|\bm{P}\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}.

Observe that
\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}=\|{\bm{a}}_{i}\|^{2}
. As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\bm{P})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{a}}_{i}\|^{2}=\|{\bm{a}}\|^{2}\leq 1,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma B.6
.

Assume
\bm{\xi}_{1},\bm{\xi}_{2},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are independent isotropic sub-Gaussian random vectors, and each
\bm{\xi}_{i}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{\xi}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{\xi}_{i})|\geq\eta\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{1}{c}\min\left\{\frac{\eta^{2}}{k^{4}\|\bm{P}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{\eta}{k^{2}\|\bm{P}\|_{\rm op}}\right\}\right)

for any positive semi-definite matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Consider positive semi-definite matrices
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2},\dots,\bm{P}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let
\mathbb{D}(P):={\rm Blkdiag}(\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2},\dots,\bm{P}_{n})
and
\bm{\xi}=[\bm{\xi}_{1}^{\top},\bm{\xi}_{2}^{\top},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\top}]^{\top}
. Then, there exists
k_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\bm{\xi}-\mathbb{E}(\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\bm{\xi})|\geq\eta\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{1}{c}\min\left\{\frac{\eta^{2}}{k^{4}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{\eta}{k^{2}\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\|_{\rm op}}\right\}\right).

(A23)

Proof.

Due to the special block diagonal structure of
\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})
, it can be seen that
\bm{\xi}^{\top}\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\bm{\xi}-{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{D}(\bm{P}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{\xi}_{i}-{\rm Tr}(\bm{P}_{i})\big)
. Each
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{\xi}_{i}-{\rm Tr}(\bm{P}_{i})
is a mean zero sub-exponential random variable.
Applying the Bernstein’s inequality
[
Vershynin 2018
, Chap. 2]
, one can derive that there exists
k_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{\xi}_{i}-{\rm Tr}(\bm{P}_{i}))\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{ck_{1}^{2}}\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{P}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\sup_{i}\|\bm{P}_{i}\|_{{\rm op}}}\right)\right).

Using the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{P}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\|_{F}^{2}
and
\sup_{i}\|\bm{P}_{i}\|_{{\rm op}}=\|\mathbb{D}(\bm{P})\|_{\rm op}
completes the proof.
∎

Theorem B.3
.

Let
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an isotropic and
1
-Sub-Gaussian random vector satisfying the Hanson-Wright inequality

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-\mathbb{E}(\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi})|\geq\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\big(-\frac{1}{c}\min\big\{\frac{\eta^{2}}{k^{4}\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{\eta}{k^{2}\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}\big\}\big),~~~~~\forall\eta>0,

(A24)

for any positive semi-definite matrix
A
.
Let
\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
\bm{\xi}
. Then,
\bm{\xi}-\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
also satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality.

Proof of
Theorem
B.3
:

To construct the proof, we will use the following lemma from
Krahmer et al. 2014
.

Lemma B.7
.

Let
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\mathbb{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. If
\bm{\xi}=[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d}]^{\top}
is an isotropic,
1
-Sub-Gaussian random vector and
{\bm{y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}
, then for every
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{T}}|\langle\bm{h},{\bm{y}}\rangle|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq c\left(\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{T}}|\langle\bm{h},\bm{g}\rangle|+\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{E}|\langle\bm{h},{\bm{y}}\rangle|^{p})^{1/p}\right),

(A25)

where
c
is a constant and
\bm{g}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{j}{\bm{x}}_{j}
for
g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{d}
independent standard normal random variables.
\triangle

Observe that

(\bm{\xi}-\bm{\xi}^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{A}(\bm{\xi}-\bm{\xi}^{\prime})=\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}+(\bm{\xi}^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}-(\bm{\xi}^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-\bm{\xi}^{\top}A\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
. Since the first two terms satisfy the Handson-Wright inequality, it suffices to show

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}|\geq\eta\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\big(-\frac{1}{c_{1}}\min\big\{\frac{\eta^{2}}{k_{1}^{4}\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{\eta}{k_{1}^{2}\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}\big\}\big),~~~~~\forall\eta>0.

(A26)

Define set
\mathbb{S}=\{\bm{A}{\bm{x}}:\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq 1,{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. Since
\bm{\xi}
is
1
-Sub-Gaussian, the random variable
\langle\bm{\xi},\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle
is also Sub-Gaussian conditionally on
\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}|\langle\bm{\xi},\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\left(\big(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}|\langle\bm{\xi},\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle|^{p})^{1/p}\big)^{p}\right)^{1/p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\lesssim}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}(\sqrt{p})^{p}\|\bm{A}\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\|^{p}_{2}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{\prime}}\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{S}}|\langle\bm{h},\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\lesssim}}c\sqrt{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{S}}|\langle\bm{h},\bm{g}\rangle|+\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{S}}(\mathbb{E}|\langle\bm{h},\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle|^{p})^{1/p}\right),

where the inequality (a) has used the inequality
\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}(\mathbb{E}|\langle X,\theta\rangle|^{p})^{1/p}\leq L\sqrt{p}
for an
L
-Sub-Gaussian random vector
X
with
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
Krahmer et al. 2014
), and the inequality (b) has used Lemma
B.7
.
Next, we bound the two terms on the right-hand side of the above inequality. For the first term, one can derive that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{S}}|\langle\bm{h},\bm{g}\rangle|=\mathbb{E}\|\bm{A}\bm{g}\|_{2}\leq(\mathbb{E}\|A\bm{g}\|_{2}^{2})^{1/2}=\|\bm{A}\|_{F}.

For the second term, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{h}\in\mathbb{S}}(\mathbb{E}|\langle\bm{h},\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle|^{p})^{1/p}=\sup_{\bm{z},\|\bm{z}\|\leq 1}(\mathbb{E}|\langle A\bm{z},\bm{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle|^{p})^{1/p}\lesssim\sqrt{p}\sup_{\bm{z},\|\bm{z}\|\leq 1}\|A\bm{z}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{p}\|A\|_{\rm op}.

Now, one can use Lemma
B.4
to show the inequality (
A26
).

\square

Appendix C
Circulant Matrices Do Not Obey Matrix RIP

Let
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a vector with i.i.d. complex normal distribution
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(0,1)
. Let
{\bm{C}}_{\bm{\xi}}
be the circulant matrix induced by
\bm{\xi}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}_{\bm{\xi}}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{p}&\xi_{p-1}&\cdots&\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{1}&\xi_{p}^{\top}&\cdots&\xi_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\xi_{p-1}&\xi_{p-2}&\cdots&\xi_{p}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}.

Note that
{\bm{C}}_{\bm{\xi}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}{\bm{F}}_{p}^{*}{\rm diag}({\bm{d}}){\bm{F}}_{p}
where
{\bm{F}}_{p}
is the Discrete Fourier Transform with
{\bm{F}}_{p}[i,j]=\omega^{ij}
for
0\leq i,j<p
and
\omega=e^{-\iota 2\pi/p}
. Here diagonal
{\bm{d}}
relates to the singular values of
{\bm{C}}_{\bm{\xi}}
and obeys
{\bm{d}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}{\bm{F}}_{p}\bm{\xi}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(0,1)
.

Suppose
p=p_{1}p_{2}
for some positive integers
p_{1},p_{2}
. Let
f_{k}
denote the
k
th row of
{\bm{F}}_{p}
. Observe that, for all
1\leq k\leq p
,
f_{k}
corresponds to a rank
1
matrix
\text{mtx}(f_{k})={\bm{a}}{\bm{b}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
since it can be written as the Kronecker product

\displaystyle f_{k}={\bm{a}}\otimes{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}p_{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad{\bm{a}}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{0}\\
\omega^{p_{2}k}\\
\vdots\\
\omega^{(p_{1}-1)p_{2}k}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{b}}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{0}\\
\omega^{k}\\
\vdots\\
\omega^{(p_{2}-1)k}\end{bmatrix}.

Lemma C.1
.

Consider the set of vectors
\mathcal{M}=(f^{\ast}_{k}/\sqrt{p})_{k=1}^{p}
that are equivalent to rank-1 matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
with unit Frobenius norm. Let
\bar{{\bm{C}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\text{Mask}_{n}({\bm{C}}_{\bm{\xi}})
be the subsampled normalized circulant matrix with
n
rows. Let
M_{p}=\max_{1\leq k\leq p}||d_{k}|^{2}-1|
. We have that

\displaystyle M_{p}=\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in\mathcal{M}}\big|\|\bar{{\bm{C}}}{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}-1\big|.

(A27)

Specifically, for all
\delta\leq 1
, there exists a constant
0<c<1
such that with probability at least
1-c^{p}
,
M_{p}>\delta
and
\bar{{\bm{C}}}
does not obey
\delta
-RIP over rank
1
matrices.

Proof of
Lemma
1
:

Let
{\bm{e}}_{k}
be the
k
th element of the standard basis. Let
\bm{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\text{Mask}_{n}({\bm{F}}_{p}^{\ast})
be the subsampled normalized DFT matrix. Observe that each column
{\bm{a}}_{k}
of
A
has unit Euclidean norm as it contains
n
entries with powers of
\omega
. Now, observe that

Appendix D
Deterministically-Chosen Context Vectors Satisfy the Hanson-Wright Inequality

In our algorithms in data-poor and data-rich regime, we select the following greedy action at each round:

a_{t}\in\argmax\nolimits_{a\in[K]}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,a},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{{i-1}}\rangle,~~~~~~i\geq 1.

The following theorem ensures that each selected context vector
{\bm{x}}_{t,a_{t}}
also satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality if each
x_{t,a},a\in[K]
, does. The property will play very important role in the analysis of our algorithms.

Theorem D.1
.

Suppose
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are i.i.d. random vectors and
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let
E_{\bm{x}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}{\bm{x}}_{i}]
. Assume that
x_{i}
satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality (stated below in equivalent forms)

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}{\bm{x}}_{i}-E_{\bm{x}}|\geq t\Big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-C\min\big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}\big\}\Big)

(A28)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\Pr\Big(|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}{\bm{x}}_{i}-E_{\bm{x}}|\geq\sqrt{\tau}\|\bm{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\tau\|\bm{A}\|/C|\Big)\geq 2e^{-\tau}.

(A29)

Let
\bm{\xi}
be a random vector deterministically chosen from the set
\{{\bm{x}}_{a},a\in[K]\}
. The identical Hanson-Wright bound holds up to
\mathcal{O}(\log(2K))
, that is,

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}]|\geq c\log(2K)t\Big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-C\min\big(\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}\big)\Big).

Proof.

Through union bound, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{x}}|\geq t\Big)\leq Q(t):=\min\left(1,2K\exp\Big(-C\min\big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}\big\}\Big)\right).

Denote
R=|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}A{\bm{x}}_{i}-E_{\bm{x}}|
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R]

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}tdQ(t)

\displaystyle=-[tQ(t)]_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}Q(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\left(1,2K\exp\Big(-C\min\big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|}\big\}\Big)\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\big(1,2Ke^{-C\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}}}\big)dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\big(1,2Ke^{-C\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}}\big)dt.

Let
T_{1}=\sqrt{\log(2K)/C}\|\bm{A}\|_{F}
. We can derive

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\big(1,2Ke^{-C\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}}}\big)dt

\displaystyle=T_{1}+\int_{T_{1}}^{\infty}2Ke^{-C\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle=T_{1}+\int_{0}^{\infty}2Ke^{-C\frac{(t+T_{1})^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-C\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle=T_{1}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\|\bm{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}\leq T_{1}+\|\bm{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}.

Likewise, let
T_{2}=\log(2K)\|\bm{A}\|/C
, and we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\big(1,2Ke^{-C\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|}}\big)dt

\displaystyle=T_{2}+\int_{T_{1}}^{\infty}2Ke^{-C\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|}}dt

\displaystyle=T_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}2Ke^{-C\frac{t+T_{1}}{\|\bm{A}\|}}dt

\displaystyle=T_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-C\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|}}dt

\displaystyle=T_{2}+\|\bm{A}\|/C.

Combing the bounds above and using
\log(2K)\geq 1
(
K>1
since it is the number of arms) we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R]=\mathbb{E}[|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}A{\bm{x}}_{i}-E_{\bm{x}}|]

\displaystyle\leq\big(\sqrt{\log(2K)}+1\big)\|\bm{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+(\log(2K)+1)\|\bm{A}\|/C

\displaystyle\leq 4\log(2K)\big(\|\bm{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\|\bm{A}\|/C\big).

Now, applying the equivalent bound (
A29
), we have

\displaystyle\Pr\big(|\bm{\xi}^{\top}A\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{x}}|\geq\sqrt{\tau}\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\tau\|A\|/C\big)\leq 2Ke^{-\tau}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Pr\big(|\bm{\xi}^{\top}A\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{x}}|\geq\sqrt{\tau}\sqrt{\log(K)}\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\tau\log(K)\|A\|/C\big)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\Pr\big(|\bm{\xi}^{\top}A\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{x}}|\geq\sqrt{\tau}\sqrt{\log(2K)}\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\tau\log(2K)\|A\|/C\big).

(A30)

Let
E_{\bm{\xi}}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}]
, and we can derive that

\displaystyle|E_{\bm{\xi}}-E_{\bm{x}}|\leq|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}A{\bm{x}}_{i}-E_{\bm{x}}|\leq 4\log(2K)\big(\|\bm{A}\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\|\bm{A}\|/C\big).

Combining this with (
A30
), we obtain that, with probability at
1-2e^{\tau}
,

\displaystyle|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{\xi}}|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sqrt{\tau}\log(2K)\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\tau\log(2K)\|A\|/C}_{\text{Probability}}+\underbrace{4\log(2K)\big(\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\|A\|/C\big)}_{\text{Expectation}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\log(2K)\Big(\sqrt{\tau+16}\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+(\tau+16)\|A\|/C\Big).

This implies that, with probability at least
1-2e^{\tau}
, for an absolute constant
c>0
, we have

\displaystyle|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{\xi}}|\leq c\log(2K)\Big(\sqrt{\tau}\|A\|_{F}/\sqrt{C}+\tau\|A\|/C\Big).

Now, using the equivalence between (
A28
) and (
A29
), we prove

\displaystyle\Pr\big(|\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\xi}-E_{\bm{\xi}}|\geq c\log(2K)t\big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-C\min\big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\bm{A}\|_{\rm op}}\big\}\Big).

∎

G.1
Details of Algorithms

In this section, we first provide some further details of the Sparse-Alternating Gradient Descent (SA-GD), Single-Weight Match Pursuit (SW-MP), and UCB with Match Pursuit (UCB-MP) presented in Section
3.2
.
As we mentioned in Section
3.2
, all algorithms use the same doubling trick as our adaptive-Lasso to enable fair comparison. The only difference is that they learn
\bm{\theta}
and make decisions in different ways.

SA-GD.
To elaborate how SA-GD works, we rewrite (
4
) into

r_{t}=\bm{\theta}^{\top}Z_{t}{\bm{w}}+\varepsilon_{t},

where
Z_{t}=[\bm{\xi}_{t},\bm{\xi}_{t-1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{t-h+1}]
. Recall that the
i
th epoch starts at
t=\tilde{T}_{i-1}+1
and ends at
\tilde{T}_{i}
. We then define the loss function in the
i
th epoch as

\displaystyle f_{i}(\bm{\theta},{\bm{w}})=\sum_{t=\tilde{T}_{i-1}+1}^{\tilde{T}_{j}}(r_{t}-\bm{\theta}^{\top}Z_{t}{\bm{w}})^{2}.

Then,
{\bm{\theta}}
and
\bm{\phi}
are estimated by

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k+1)}=\bm{\theta}^{(k)}-\beta\nabla f_{i,{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{w}}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{w}}^{(k+1)}={\bm{w}}^{(k)}-\beta\nabla f_{i,{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{w}}^{(k)}),

alternatively, where
f_{i,{\bm{\theta}}}
and
f_{i,{\bm{w}}}
are the gradients of
f_{i}
with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
and
{\bm{w}}
, respectively. We choose a threshold
\epsilon
. When the loss
f_{i}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k^{*})},\hat{\bm{w}}^{(k^{*})})\leq\epsilon
, the alternative gradient descent algorithm stops and we project
{\bm{w}}^{(k^{*})}
to the
s
-sparse subspace.

We wish to mention that, due to our intricate problem setting, most existing algorithms will perform worse than ours since they do not learn the long-horizon reward pattern. For instance, for the special case where
w_{0}=0
(where
{\bm{w}}=[w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{h}]^{\top}
), it is not hard to see that the classic UCB and OFUL
Chu et al. 2011
,
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
will fail since nothing can be learned. To enable more fair comparison, we equip the classic algorithms with the ability to estimate the location of the largest weight in
{\bm{w}}
, which we will elaborate below.

SW-MP.
The single-weight matching pursuit algorithm employs the same idea as the classic matching pursuit. Instead of estimating all non-zero values in a vector, we only look for the largest non-zero, and that is why we call this algorithm single-weight matching pursuit.

In each epoch, the rewards are generated by

The first step of SW-MP is to estimate the location of the largest
w_{i}
in the weight vector
{\bm{w}}
. To do that, it tests which block column (see the above shaded area for an example of a block column) are most correlated with the reward vector
{\bm{r}}
. Let
\Xi^{k}
be the
k
th block column. Then, we solve the optimization problem at the end of each epoch

\displaystyle k^{*}=\argmax_{k\in\{1,2,\dots,h\}}\frac{1}{\|\Xi^{k}\|}\|{\bm{r}}^{\top}\Xi^{k}\|

to find the index of the largest weight in
{\bm{w}}
. Then, we estimate
{\bm{\theta}}
by solving the following least squares problem

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|{\bm{r}}-\Xi^{k^{*}}{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}.

This estimated
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
will be used for decision-making in the next epoch. Note that, as expected, this algorithm can be inaccurate when there are many non-zeros in
{\bm{w}}
, which is also evidenced in (a3), (a4), (b3), and (b4) in Fig.
6
.

UCB-MP.
This algorithm shares some similarities as SW-MP. At the end of each epoch
i
, we use the same approach as SW-MP to estimate the location of the largest
w_{i}
in the weight vector
{\bm{w}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle k^{*}=\argmax_{k\in\{1,2,\dots,h\}}\frac{1}{\|\Xi^{k}\|}\|{\bm{r}}^{\top}\Xi^{k}\|,

Using this learned location, we use UCB
Chu et al. 2011
to learn
\bm{\theta}
and make decisions at the next epoch. Specifically, at each round, we update the confidence bound only using the chosen context vector and the corresponding reward
k^{*}
rounds ago.
