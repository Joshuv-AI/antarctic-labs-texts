q q -Pearson pair and moments in q q -deformed ensembles
Peter J. Forrester, Shi-Hao Li, Bo-Jian Shen, Guo-Fu Yu

Abstract
. The generalisation of continuous orthogonal polynomial ensembles from random matrix theory to the q q -lattice setting is considered. We take up the task of initiating a systematic study of the corresponding moments of the density from two complementary viewpoints. The first requires knowledge of the ensemble average with respect to a general Schur polynomial, from which the spectral moments follow as a corollary. In the case of little q q -Laguerre weight, a particular ϕ 2 3 {}_{3}\phi_{2} basic hypergeometric polynomial is used to express density moments. The second approach is to study the q q -Laplace transform of the un-normalised measure. Using integrability properties associated with the q q -Pearson equation for the q q -classical weights, a fourth order q q -difference equation is obtained, generalising a result of Ledoux in the continuous classical cases.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Special properties of moments for classical ensembles with unitary symmetry

An ensemble of
N\times N
complex Hermitian random matrices
\{H\}
is said to have
unitary symmetry if the probability density function (PDF) has the invariance
property
P(U^{-1}HU)=P(H)
for all unitary matrices
U\in\mathcal{U}(N)
. A structurally simple example
is when, up to proportionality,
P(H)
is given by
\prod_{l=1}^{N}w(x_{l})
, where

\{x_{l}\}_{l=1}^{N}
are the eigenvalues of
H
and
w(x)
is referred to as the weight function.
In this circumstance, up to normalisation, the corresponding eigenvalue PDF is given
by (see
[
19
, Prop. 1.3.4]
)

\prod_{l=1}^{N}w(x_{l})\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|x_{k}-x_{j}|^{2}.

(1.1)

The class of PDFs of the form (
1.1
) are closely related to orthogonal polynomials. Thus let

\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=0,1,\dots}
, where each
p_{k}(x)
is a monic polynomial of degree

k
, have the orthogonality

\int_{I}w(x)p_{j}(x)p_{k}(x)\,dx=h_{j}\delta_{j,k},

(1.2)

where
I
denotes the support of
w(x)
, and
h_{j}>0
is the normalisation.
It is a standard result in random matrix theory (see
[
19
, Prop. 5.1.2]
)
that

\rho_{(k),N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\det[K_{N}(x_{j},x_{l})]_{j,l=1}^{k},

(1.3)

where

K_{N}(x,y)=\Big(w(x)w(y)\Big)^{1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{p_{j}(x)p_{j}(y)\over h_{j}}.

(1.4)

Here
\rho_{(k),N}
denotes the
k
-point correlation function, obtained by integrating
a suitably normalised version of (
1.1
) over all variables except
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
.
In the case
k=1
this corresponds to the eigenvalue density, and we read off from
(
1.3
) and (
1.4
) that

\rho_{(1),N}(x)=w(x)\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{(p_{j}(x))^{2}\over h_{j}}.

(1.5)

A distinguished class of weight functions have the property that their logarithmic derivative
can be written in the particular rational function form

\frac{d}{dx}\log w(x)={a_{0}+a_{1}x\over b_{0}+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}},

(1.6)

for some polynomials of degree less than or equal to one in the numerator
and two in the denominator, as indicated. Requiring too that
w(x)
has all moments
finite leaves just three possibilities, up to linear scalings,

w^{(G)}(x)=e^{-x^{2}},\qquad w^{(L)}(x)=x^{a}e^{-x}\chi_{x>0},\qquad w^{(J)}(x)=x^{a}(1-x)^{b}\chi_{0<x<1},

(1.7)

referred to as the Gaussian, Laguerre and Jacobi weights respectively. The notation

\chi_{A}
used in (
1.7
) is the indicator function of the condition
A
, which takes
on the value one for
A
true, and zero otherwise. For the parameters in the Laguerre and
Jacobi case we require
a,b>-1
for the weights to be normalisable.
Note that the corresponding orthogonal
polynomials in (
1.2
) are, up to normalisation,
the classical Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials
[
55
]
. The corresponding
(classical) random matrix ensembles
with eigenvalue PDF (
1.1
) are given the names GUE, LUE and JUE respectively, with the
first letter corresponding to the weight, and UE denoting unitary ensemble. Here unitary is
used in the context of the symmetry noted in the first paragraph.

For a non-negative integer
k
, the moments associated with the spectral density are defined by

m_{k,N}=\int_{I}x^{k}\rho_{(1),N}(x)\,dx.

(1.8)

There are special properties associated with the moments
in the case of the classical ensembles.
Consider for example the GUE.
It is a long established result in the applications of random matrices, due to
Harer and Zagier
[
31
]
, that the moments (
1.8
) have combinatorial and topological
significance. This come about through the large
N
terminating expansion

{2^{k}\over N^{1+2k}}m_{2k,N}^{(G)}=\sum_{g=0}^{[k/2]}{c(g;k)\over N^{2g}}.

(1.9)

It was shown in
[
31
]
that the coefficients
c(g;k)
can be specified as the number of pairings of the
edges of a
2k
-gon giving a figure that can be embedded on a surface of genus
g
.
The leading coefficient in (
1.9
) is given by

c(0;k)={1\over k+1}\binom{2k}{k},

(1.10)

which is the
k
-th Catalan number.

An analogous expansion to (
1.9
) holds for the LUE
with Laguerre parameter
a=\alpha N

[
15
,
13
]
.
The latter of these references involves so-called double monotone Hurwitz
numbers, which have both combinatorial and topological significance.
The recent work
[
27
]
considers the coefficients in the
1/N^{2}

expansion of the moments for the ensemble (
1.1
) in the case of the JUE with
Jacobi parameters
a=\alpha_{1}N
,

b=\alpha_{2}N
. Unlike the Gaussian and Laguerre cases, this expansion no longer
terminates. It is shown that the coefficients can be expressed in terms of
triple monotone Hurwitz numbers.

Another significant feature of the moments of the GUE and LUE
is that they permit evaluations in terms of hypergeometric polynomials.
Thus for the GUE
[
58
, Eq. (4.33)]

{2^{2k}\over N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|^{2k}\rho_{(1),N}^{(G)}(x)\,dx={\Gamma(2k+1)\over\Gamma(k+1)}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big({-k,1-N\atop 2}\Big|2\Big).

(1.11)

This result in fact remains valid for complex moments Re
\,k>-1/2

[
14
]
.
In the case of the LUE, it is known
[
14
, Eq. 4.11]

m_{k,N}^{(L)}=N(N+a){(k+a)!\over(1+a)!}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\Big({1-k,2+k,1-N\atop 2,2+a}\Big|1\Big),

(1.12)

which also extends to continuous
k
.

1.2.
q
-classical ensembles with unitary symmetry and their moments

It is well known that the classical polynomials admit
q
-generalisations catalogued
according to the Askey scheme; see e.g.
[
33
,
38
]
.
Distinguishing the weight functions for classical
q
-orthogonal
polynomials is that they satisfy the
q
-Pearson equation

D_{q}(\sigma(x)w(x))=\tau(x)w(x),\qquad D_{q}f(x)={f(x)-f(qx)\over(1-q)x},

(1.13)

where it is required that
\sigma(x)
be a polynomial
of degree less than or equal to 2, and
\tau(x)
be a polynomial of degree less than or
equal to 1. Note that with
D_{q}
replaced by a derivative with respect
to
x
, (
1.13
) is equivalent to (
1.6
). Our interest in the present article is
in the moments of the density corresponding to the probability density function
(
1.1
) with
w(x)
being given by a classical
q
-weight, and the support of the measure
being appropriately chosen.

It would seem
that the first study of this type
[
48
,
5
]
was for the particular deformation of (
1.1
)
from the real line to the unit circle with eigenvalue PDF proportional to

\prod_{l=1}^{N}\theta_{3}(e^{i\theta_{l}};q)\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|e^{i\theta_{k}}-e^{i\theta_{j}}|^{2}.

(1.14)

Here

\theta_{3}(z;q)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n},\qquad|q|<1

(1.15)

is a Jacobi theta function. This came about through the study of a certain solvable
U(N)
lattice
gauge theory in two-dimensions.

Let the eigenvalue density associated with (
1.14
) be denoted
\rho_{(1),N}^{(RS)}(\theta)
.
Here the superscript
(RS)
indicates that (
1.14
) relates to the Rogers-Szegö polynomials
in the circular analogue of (
1.2
).
Define the moments

m_{k,N}^{(RS)}(q)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{ik\theta}\rho_{(1),N}^{(RS)}(\theta)\,d\theta,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(1.16)

Then we have from
[
48
,
5
]
that

m_{k,N}^{(RS)}(q)=-{(-q)^{k}\over 1-q^{2k}}\,{}_{2}\phi_{1}\Big({q^{2k},q^{-2k}\atop q^{2}}\Big|q^{2};q^{2N+2}\Big).

(1.17)

Here the special function on the RHS refers to Heine’s
q
-generalisation of the
Gauss hypergeometric function,

{}_{2}\phi_{1}\Big({a_{1},a_{2}\atop b_{1}}\Big|q;z\Big):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{(a_{1};q)_{n}(a_{2};q)_{n}\over(q;q)_{n}(b_{1};q)_{n}}z^{n},

(1.18)

with

(u;q)_{n}:=(1-u)(1-qu)\cdots(1-q^{n-1}u).

(1.19)

Moreover it is noted in
[
48
,
5
]
that with the scaling
q=e^{-\lambda/N}
, the moments
permit a
1/N^{2}
expansion (recall (
1.9
))

{1\over N}m_{k,N}^{(RS)}(q)\Big|_{q=e^{-\lambda/N}}=\mu_{k,0}^{(RS)}(\lambda)+{1\over N^{2}}\mu_{k,2}^{(RS)}(\lambda)+{1\over N^{4}}\mu_{k,4}^{(RS)}(\lambda)+\cdots

(1.20)

with

\mu_{k,0}^{(RS)}(\lambda)=-{(-1)^{k}\over 2\lambda k}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big({-k,k\atop 1}\Big|e^{-2\lambda}\Big).

(1.21)

Attracting attention in the recent literature
[
21
,
9
]
is the particular example
of (
1.1
) proportional to

\prod_{l=1}^{N}w^{(SW)}(u_{l};q)\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(u_{k}-u_{j})^{2},\qquad u_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

(1.22)

where

w^{(SW)}(u;q)={k\over\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-k^{2}(\log u)^{2}},\qquad q=e^{-1/(2k^{2})},

(1.23)

which is (one form of) the Stieltjes-Wigert weight from the theory of orthogonal
polynomials
[
55
]
. Note that for the weight (
1.23
),

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{n}w^{(SW)}(u;q)\,du=q^{-(n+1)^{2}/2}.

(1.24)

The ensemble (
1.22
) turns out to be closely related to
(
1.14
). In particular, it was shown in
[
21
, Prop. 1.1]
that the moments
m_{k,N}^{(SW)}(q)
of the spectral
density corresponding to (
1.22
) have, for
k\geq 1
, the evaluation

{1\over N}q^{Nk}m_{k,N}^{(SW)}(q)=-{1\over N}{(-q^{-1/2})^{k}\over 1-q^{-k}}\,{}_{2}\phi_{1}\Big({q^{k},q^{-k}\atop q^{-1}}\Big|q^{-1};q^{-N-1}\Big)

(1.25)

(cf. (
1.17
)). Furthermore, scaling
q=e^{-\lambda/N}
, the same
1/N^{2}
expansion of the RHS results as
in (
1.20
), but with
-2\lambda
replaced by
\lambda
throughout.

There is an equivalent formulation of the result (
1.25
) which replaces the continuous integral giving an average over
the probability density function by a Jackson
q
-integral. First recall that the Jackson integral with terminals 0 to
\infty
is
defined by

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,d_{q}x=(1-q)\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f(q^{k})q^{k}.

(1.26)

By introducing the quantity

c_{q}=(-q,-1,q;q)_{\infty},\qquad(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{l=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha q^{l})(1-\beta q^{l})(1-\gamma q^{l}),

(1.27)

we know from
[
10
]
that with

{w}^{(\widetilde{SW})}(x;q)={1\over(1-q)\sqrt{q}c_{q}}x^{-1/2}e^{((\log x)/(2\log q))^{2}},

(1.28)

we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}{w}^{(\widetilde{SW})}(x;q)(q^{-1/2}x)^{n}\,d_{q}x=q^{-(n+1)^{2}/2}.

(1.29)

This is precisely the same as for (
1.24
), which is permitted since the quadratic exponential
order of the rate of increase of the moments means they do not uniquely determine a weight function.
As a consequence, the ensemble specified by replacing each
w^{(SW)}(u_{l};q)
by

w^{(\widetilde{SW})}(u_{l};q)
, and with the
u_{l}
restricted to the
q
-lattice in the sense of
(
1.26
), we have the moments of this particular Jackson integral ensemble when multiplied by
q^{-k}

are also specified by (
1.25
).

1.3.
Moments via Schur averages in the
q
case

The method used to derive (
1.25
) in
[
21
]
made use of knowledge
of a more general result, namely the closed form evaluation of
[
18
]

\langle s_{\kappa}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\rangle^{(SW)},

(1.30)

where
s_{\kappa}
denotes the Schur polynomial indexed by the partition

s_{\kappa}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):={\det[x_{k}^{\kappa_{N-j+1}+j-1}]_{j,k=1}^{N}\over\det[x_{k}^{j-1}]_{j,k=1}^{N}}.

Its relevance is seen from the identity (see e.g.

\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{k}=\sum_{r=0}^{{\rm min}\,(k-1,N-1)}(-1)^{r}s_{(k-r,1^{r})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}),

(1.32)

where
(k-r,1^{r})
denotes the partition with largest part
\kappa_{1}=k-r
,

(r\leq N-1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}\rho_{(1),N}(x)\,d_{q}x=\sum_{r=0}^{{\rm min}\,(k-1,N-1)}(-1)^{r}\langle s_{(k-r,1^{r})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\rangle.

(1.33)

This is the same strategy as used in
[
5
]
to derive (
1.17
).

In Section

we will discuss in detail particular

-weights which give evaluations of

\langle s_{\kappa}\rangle
simple enough that

as already remarked the evaluation (

. In subsection

]
. In subsection

-Hermite weight, which in a certain scaling, limits to the Gaussian weight in

) for

. The corresponding probability density function (

on the

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,d_{q}x:=\int_{0}^{b}f(x)\,d_{q}x-\int_{0}^{a}f(x)\,d_{q}x

(1.34)

with

\int_{0}^{\alpha}f(x)\,d_{q}x:=(1-q)\alpha\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n}f(\alpha q^{n})

1.4.
Integrable structures associated with
q
-orthogonal polynomial weights

Combining (
1.8
) and (
1.5
) shows that the exponential generating function of the moments
can be expressed as a sum according to

\int_{I}e^{tx}\rho_{(1),N}(x)\,dx=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{1\over h_{j}}\int_{I}e^{tx}(p_{j}(x))^{2}\,d\mu,\quad d\mu=w(x)\,dx.

(1.36)

In the works
[
39
,
40
]
Ledoux undertook a study of integrability properties
associated with the integral in the summand on the RHS of (
1.36
),
in the classical continuous cases, and also classical discrete cases. This is equivalent to studying

\int_{I}e^{tx}\Big(\rho_{(1),N}(x)-\rho_{(1),N-1}(x)\Big)\,dx,

(1.37)

which is the exponential generating function for the difference
m_{k,N}-m_{k,N-1}
.
Making essential use of the Pearson equation, Ledoux derived a fourth order differential (difference) equation for

\int_{I}e^{tx}(p_{j}(x))^{2}\,d\mu
in the classical continuous (discrete) cases respectively.
This methodology will be revised in Section
3
. In Section
4
, with the meaning of
the integral, and also the exponential function
e^{tx}
,
appropriately adjusted to the
q
setting as in Section
2
, the classical
q
analogues of Ledoux’s integrability results are obtained.
Thus, the
q
case as described in Section
1.2
is missed from consideration in
[
39
,
40
]
.
Specifically, in Section
4
the approach of
[
39
]
is used to derive a fourth order
q
-differential equation
satisfied by the
q
-Laplace transform of the un-normalised measure
(p_{n}(x;q))^{2}\,d_{q}\mu
.
Here
d_{q}\mu
denotes the appropriately weighted
q
-lattice corresponding to classical

q
-orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}(x;q)\}
. Crucial to our considerations is the
q
-Pearson equation (
1.13
).

In
[
39
,
40
]
, knowledge that (
1.37
) satisfies a fourth order differential (difference) equation was not
used in obtaining explicit formulas for
\{m_{k,N}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in the classical cases. Instead, for the classical continuous

t
of the LHS of (
1.36
) is then simply
related to

\int_{I}e^{tx}p_{N}(x)p_{N-1}(x)\,d\mu.

(1.38)

The methods used in
[

]
to study (
1.37
) applied to (

[

]
), and a second order linear
recurrence for the moments in the case of the classical Laguerre weight (obtained first in
[

]
).

derivative of the LHS of (

). Instead in
[

]
special properties of the shifted

-th moment

With these points in mind,

2.
Averages over Schur polynomials

2.1.
The Stieltjes-Wigert weight (
1.28
)

Let
(\widetilde{SW})
refer to an average with respect to the weight (
1.28
) in (
1.1
) on the
q
-lattice in the sense of (
1.26
).
Let (SW) refer to an average with respect to the weight (
1.23
) in (
1.1
). Our interest is in the moments of the density,

m_{k,N}^{(\widetilde{SW})}
, which we calculate via the identity (
1.33
).

With
s_{\kappa}
the Schur polynomial (
1.31
),
according to (
1.24
) and (
1.29
) we have that
\langle s_{\kappa}\rangle^{(\widetilde{SW})}=\langle s_{\kappa}\rangle^{({SW})}
.
The exact form of the average on the RHS is known from
[
21
, Prop. 3.1]
,

[
53
, Appendix A.2]
as well as the earlier work
[
18
]
.
Reading off from these references then gives

\langle s_{\kappa}\rangle^{(\widetilde{SW})}=q^{-{1\over 2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\kappa_{l}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}{1-q^{-(\kappa_{j}-j-\kappa_{k}+k)}\over 1-q^{-(k-j)}}.

(2.1)

As shown in
[
21
, Coroll. 3.3 and Prop. 1.1]
, specialising to
\kappa=(k-r,1^{r})
and making use of
(
1.33
) then implies that
(q^{Nk}/N)m_{k,N}^{(\widetilde{SW})}
is given by (
1.25
), or equivalently

{1\over N}q^{(N-1/2)k}m_{k,N}^{(\widetilde{SW})}=-{1\over N}{(-q^{-1/2})^{k}\over 1-q^{-k}}p_{k}^{(lq\text{-}J)}(q^{-N};1,q|q^{-1}).

(2.2)

Here
p_{k}^{(lq\text{-}J)}
denotes the little
q
-Jacobi polynomial, specified in terms of
the
q
-hypergeometric function (
1.18
) by

p_{n}^{(lq\text{-}J)}(x;a,b|q)={}_{2}\phi_{1}\Big({q^{-n},abq^{n+1}\atop aq}\Big|q;qx\Big).

(2.3)

There is a further equivalent form of
m_{k,N}^{(\widetilde{SW})}
.
This involves the
q
-binomial coefficient

\bigg[\begin{array}[]{cc}n\\
l\end{array}\bigg]_{q}={(1-q^{n})(1-q^{n-1})\cdots(1-q^{n-l+1})\over(1-q^{l})(1-q^{l-1})\cdots(1-q)}

(2.4)

for
n,l
non-negative integers.
Thus from
[
21
, Eq. (3.15) combined with (3.14)]
we read off

\displaystyle q^{(N-1/2)k}m_{k,N}^{(\widetilde{SW})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{r}q^{-(k-r)^{2}/2-r/2}\bigg[{N+k-r-1\atop k}\bigg]_{q^{-1}}\bigg[{k-1\atop r}\bigg]_{q^{-1}}

\displaystyle=q^{-kN-(k^{2}-2k)/2}\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{r}q^{(r^{2}+r)/2+kr}\bigg[{N+k-r-1\atop k}\bigg]_{q}\bigg[{k-1\atop r}\bigg]_{q},

(2.5)

where the final equality follows from the general property of the
q
-binomial
coefficients

\bigg[\begin{array}[]{cc}n\\

3.1.
The Pearson equation and applications

The structured differential relation for the weight function

\displaystyle(\sigma(x)w(x))^{\prime}=\tau(x)w(x),

(3.1)

is to be referred to as the Pearson equation in the continuous case. Here it is assumed that
\sigma(x)w(x)
decays upon approaching
the boundary of the support of
w(x)
.
As is consistent with (
1.6
) — this is the original form of the Pearson differential equation
[
49
]
— the classical weights are when
\sigma
and
\tau
are polynomials
of degrees less than 2 and 1 respectively.
The Pearson equation has been generalised to discrete measures/nonuniform lattices; see for example
[
33
,
38
,
44
]
. It was proved that for the discrete classical orthogonal polynomials, the weight function
w(s)
should satisfy a discrete analogue of the Pearson equation

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{\Delta x(s-\frac{1}{2})}[\sigma(s)w(s)]=\tau(s)w(s),

where
\Delta
is the forward difference operator corresponding to the lattice, and
x(s)
denotes the position of the lattice. Interesting examples include the linear discrete lattice and exponential discrete lattice. The former one induces the so-called discrete orthogonal polynomials, whose weight functions satisfy a linear discrete equation

[
44
]

\displaystyle\frac{w(x+1)}{w(x)}=\frac{\sigma(x)+\tau(x)}{\sigma(x+1)}.

(3.2)

and the latter one corresponds to the
q
-orthogonal polynomials, and their weight functions satisfy a
q
-difference equation (
1.13
).

In addition to its applications in the classification of classical orthogonal polynomials, the Pearson equation
has seen a number of applications in the contexts of complex analysis and random matrix theory.
One class of application is to the evaluation of classical beta integrals
[
50
]
and Barnes and Ramanujan-type integrals on the
q
-linear lattice
[
51
]
. Applications in random matrix theory up to the year 2010 have been discussed in the monograph
[
19
]
. The most prominent is to connect classical orthogonal polynomials to particular
skew orthogonal polynomials. This result was used to evaluate the Christoffel-Darboux kernel for orthogonal and symplectic ensembles with classical weights, and to show in particular that
they are rank one perturbations of the kernel for the corresponding unitary ensemble
[
1
]
. Subsequently the
q
-Pearson equation has been used to
generalise this theory, for the symplectic ensembles, to the classical
q
-cases
[
24
,
54
]
.
Differential identities that follow form the Pearson equation in the classical cases
[
19
, Sec. 5.4]
have found application in the derivation
of formulas for the structure function — that is the covariance of the linear statistics
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(ik_{1}\lambda_{j})
,
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(-ik_{2}\lambda_{j})
—
in the Gaussian and Laguerre unitary ensembles of random matrix theory
[
22
,
23
]
.
Another recent development has been in relation to the analyse of the partition function of particular two-component log-gas systems on a line with charges
+1
and
+2
by making use of the Pearson pair in the classical cases
[
25
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\sigma(x)D_{q}f(x)g(x)d_{q}\mu=-\int_{a}^{b}\tau(x)f(qx)g(qx)d_{q}\mu-\int_{a}^{b}\sigma(x)f(qx)D_{q}g(x)d_{q}\mu,

(4.2)

where
d_{q}\mu=\rho(x;q)d_{q}x
and

are two arbitrary

Proof.

Let’s start with the general integration by parts formula given by
[

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\sigma(x)D_{q}f(x)g(x)d_{q}\mu=f(x)g(x)\sigma(x)\rho(x)|_{a}^{b}-\int_{a}^{b}f(qx)D_{q}(g(x)\sigma(x)\rho(x))d_{q}x.

Since for classical
q
-weight functions,
\sigma(x)\rho(x)
vanishes at the end points of support

, we know the first term on the right hand side vanishes.
In relation to the second term on the right hand side, we observe from the

\displaystyle D_{q}(g(x)\sigma(x)\rho(x))=D_{q}g(x)\sigma(x)\rho(x)+g(qx)\tau(x)\rho(x),

thus completing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2

With

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\sigma(x)D_{q^{-1}}f(x)g(x)d_{q}\mu=-q\int_{a}^{b}\tau(x)f(x)g(x)d_{q}\mu-\int_{a}^{b}\sigma(x)f(q^{-1}x)D_{q^{-1}}g(x)d_{q}\mu.

(4.3)

(4.5)

Appendix

Here we rederive known differential equations satisfied by the Laplace transform of the un-normalized measure
(P_{N}(x))^{2}d\mu

in the classical continuous cases using the Pearson pair formalism
of Section
3.2
. The classical continuous cases correspond to the weights (
1.7
), distinguished by having all moments
finite, and the Cauchy weight

\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{-a}
,
a>1/2
,
where the number of finite moments depends on
a
.
The corresponding Pearson pairs are

\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1,-2x)&\text{Hermite case,}\\
(x,a+1-x)&\text{Laguerre case,}\\
(x(1-x),a+1-(a+b+2)x)&\text{Jacobi case,}\\
(1+x^{2},2(1-a)x)&\text{Cauchy case}\end{array}\right.

According to the theory of Section
3.2
, these imply the differential
operators

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{H}=-2\frac{d}{ds},\quad\mathbb{A}^{(1)}_{H}=-2,\quad\mathbb{B}_{H}=1,\quad\mathbb{B}^{(1)}_{H}=\mathbb{B}^{(2)}_{H}=0,\quad\lambda_{N}^{(H)}=2N,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{L}=a+1-\frac{d}{ds},\quad\mathbb{A}^{(1)}_{L}=-1,\quad\mathbb{B}_{L}=\frac{d}{ds},\quad\mathbb{B}_{L}^{(1)}=1,\quad\mathbb{B}_{L}^{(2)}=0,\quad\lambda_{N}^{(L)}=N,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{J}=a+1-(a+b+2)\frac{d}{ds},\quad\mathbb{A}_{J}^{(1)}=-a-b-2,\quad\mathbb{B}_{J}=\frac{d}{ds}-\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}},\quad\mathbb{B}_{J}^{(1)}=1-2\frac{d}{ds},

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{B}_{J}^{(2)}=-2,\quad\lambda_{N}^{(J)}=N(N+a+b+1),

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{C}=2(1-a)\frac{d}{ds},\quad\mathbb{A}_{C}^{(1)}=2(1-a),\quad\mathbb{B}_{C}=1+\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}},\quad\mathbb{B}_{C}^{(1)}=2\frac{d}{ds},\quad\mathbb{B}_{C}^{(2)}=2,\quad

\displaystyle\qquad\lambda_{N}^{(C)}=-N(N-2a+1)

in the four cases respectively.
Application of Proposiiton
3.1
then gives the
differential equations of the Laplace transforms of the un-normalized measure.
These are

\displaystyle 4s\Phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+4\Phi^{{}^{\prime}}-(s^{3}+(8N+4)s)\Phi=0,

in Hermite case

\displaystyle(s^{3}-s)\Phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+(3s^{2}+2(2N+a+1)s-1)\Phi^{{}^{\prime}}+(s(1-a^{2})+a+1+2N)\Phi=0,

in the Laguerre case

\displaystyle s^{3}\Phi^{(4)}+(6s^{2}-2s^{3})\Phi^{{}^{\prime\prime\prime}}+\left(s^{3}-9s^{2}+[6-4N(N+a+b+1)-(a+b)(a+b+2)]s\right)\Phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle+\left(3s^{2}+[4N(N+a+b+1)+2(a+1)(a+b)-6]s-(a+b)(a+b+4)-4N(N+a+b+1)\right)\Phi^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\left((1-a^{2})s+(a+b)(a+1)+2N(N+a+b+1)\right)\Phi=0

in the Jacobi case, and

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}\Phi^{(4)}

\displaystyle+6\lambda^{2}\Phi^{{}^{\prime\prime\prime}}+\left(2\lambda^{3}+[4a(1-a)+6-4N(N-2a+1)]\lambda\right)\Phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle+\left(6\lambda^{2}+4[a(1-a)-N(N-2a+1)]\right)\Phi^{{}^{\prime}}+\left(\lambda^{3}+[2-4N(N-2a+1)+4a]\lambda\right)\Phi=0,

in the Cauchy case.
The first three cases was considered in
[
39
]
and the Cauchy one was recently shown in
[
6
]
; see also
[
26
]
in relation to the latter.
