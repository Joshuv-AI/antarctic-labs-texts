On Meshfree Collocation to Compute the Probability of Default under a Regime-Switching Synchronous-Jump Tempered Stable Lévy Model
Davood Damircheli, Mohsen Razzaghi, Seyed-Mohammad-Mahdi Kazemi, Ali Foroush Bastani

Abstract
In the paper [Hainaut, D. and Colwell, D.B., A structural model for credit risk with switching processes and synchronous jumps, The European Journal of Finance 22(11) (2016): 1040-1062], the authors exploit a synchronous-jump regime-switching model to compute the default probability of a publicly traded company. Here, we first generalize the proposed Lévy model to more general setting of tempered stable processes recently introduced into the finance literature. Based on the singularity of the resulting partial integro-differential operator, we propose a general framework based on strictly positive-definite functions to de-singularize the operator. We then analyze an efficient meshfree collocation method based on radial basis functions to approximate the solution of the corresponding system of partial integro-differential equations arising from the structural credit risk model. We show that under some regularity assumptions, our proposed method naturally de-sinularizes the problem in the tempered stable case. Numerical results of applying the method on some standard examples from the literature confirms the accuracy of our theoretical results and numerical algorithm.

Proposed Model for the Value of Firm’s Assets

Before presenting the details of this process, we first remind the reader of some general properties of continuous-time Markov processes. In this respect, we assume that the economic state at time

The Structural Model of Default Risk

Let us consider a firm whose capital structure consists of debt and equity where the debt component is issued as a single zero-coupon bond,
B
with principal value
L
. We also assume that the firm can only default at the maturity of debt,

. Assume also a risk-neutral probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q})
equipped with some filtration,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. On this filtration is
defined the firm’s value process,
V_{t}
, that represents the total value of the firm’s assets. The probability measure

is taken to be a risk neutral measure but the following results are

We define the default event to occur only if the total value of the firm’s assets at the maturity is not sufficient to cover the loan’s redemption:

\textmd{Default Event}=\{V_{T}<L\},

(2.10)

and the probability of default is denoted by

(2.11)

In case of bankruptcy, a fraction

, called the recovery rate, of asset value is repaid to debt-holders.

In this work, as in Guo et al.

]
, the debt recovers at a different magnitude

B(t,T)={\mathbb{E}}({\rm e}^{-r(T-t)}(R(\alpha_{T})V_{T}\mathbbm{1}_{V_{T}<L}+L\mathbbm{1}_{VT\geq L})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}).

(2.12)

We will present the approach used to compute the probabilities of default and the defaultable bond prices in the reminder.

3
Derivation of the PIDEs for Default Probabilities

In order to derive the PIDEs corresponding to the default probability of a given firm, there exists two main approaches which are usually exploited and give equal results:

1.

Using Itô’s formula for general semi-martingales (or infinitesimal generator)
[
29
]
;

2.

Using the Lévy–Khintchine formula and the tempered Fourier transform (see Proposition (1.9) of
[
26
]
).

The second approach is more useful in characterizing some intrinsic properties of the integro-differential operator of the problem which will be needed in later parts of the paper.

The probability of default of a firm in state
\alpha_{t}
and time
t
under the measure
\mathbb{P}
is defined as

\displaystyle D(t,T,X_{t},{\alpha_{t}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(V_{T}\leq L|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{V_{T}\leq L}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right),

(3.1)

in which
D\left(t,T,x,j\right)
is the probability of default at regime
j
when the state process
X_{t}
takes the value
x
. It could be shown (see e.g. Hainaut and Colwell
[
11
]
) that
D\left(t,T,x,j\right)
for
j=1,2,\cdots,H
satisfy the following system of partial integro-differential equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}D(t,T,x,j)+\mathcal{L}D(t,T,x,j)=0,\hskip 56.9055ptj=1\ldots H,\\
\\
D(t,T,x,j)=\mathbbm{1}_{x<\ln\left(L/V_{0}\right)},\hskip 95.3169ptj=1\ldots H,\\
\\
\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}D(t,T,x,j)=1,\hskip 93.89418ptj=1\ldots H,\\
\\
\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}D(t,T,x,j)=0,\hskip 93.89418ptj=1\ldots H,\end{array}\right.

(3.2)

in which
\mathcal{L}
is the infinitesimal generator of default probabilities of the form (see e.g.
[
17
,
29
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}D(t,T,x,j)=

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D(t,T,x,j)+\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{2}}D(t,T,x,j)

(3.3)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\neq j}q_{j,k}\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}D(t,T,x+z,k)-D(t,T,x,j)\mu(j,k,z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}\left(D(t,T,x+z,j)-D(t,T,x,j)-z\mathbbm{1}_{|z|\leq 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D(t,T,x,j)\right)v(j,\mathrm{d}z).

Due to the relation
\sum_{i=1}^{H}q_{j,i}=0
, the generator can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}D(t,T,x,j)=

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D(t,T,x,j)+\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{2}}D(t,T,x,j)

(3.4)

\displaystyle+q_{j,j}D(t,T,x,j)+\sum_{k\neq j}q_{j,k}\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}D(t,T,x+z,k)\mu(j,k,z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}}\left(D(t,T,x+z,j)-D(t,T,x,j)-z\mathbbm{1}_{|z|\leq 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D(t,T,x,j)\right)v(j,\mathrm{d}z).

In order to make the presentation and implementation of the numerical meshfree scheme in the next section simple, we transform the PIDEs (
3.2
) forward in time. To this end and using a common change of variables of the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\tau}u(\tau,x,j)=\mathcal{L}u(\tau,x,j),\hskip 62.59596ptj=1\ldots H,\\
\\

\\
\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}u(\tau,x,j)=1,\hskip 71.13188ptj=1\ldots H,\\
\\
\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}u(\tau,x,j)=0,\hskip 71.13188ptj=1\ldots H.\end{array}\right.

(3.5)

Developing a stable numerical scheme to solve Eq. (
3.2

In the sequel, we will propose our approach to approximate the solution of these systems of partial integro-differential equations.

4.1
Problem Discretization

In this section, we describe a general numerical scheme based on RBF collocation to numerically solve the transformed linear partial integro-differential equation formulation (
3.2
)
which could be represented as

U_{\tau}={\cal L}U,

(4.2)

in which

U(\tau,x):=[u(\tau,x,1),u(\tau,x,2),\cdots,u(\tau,x,H)]^{T}.

(4.3)

Many numerical schemes for time-dependent PDEs separate the discretization of time and space variables into distinct phases and develop the theory by assuming one discretization (outer discretization) to be carried out first leading to a so-called
semi-discrete problem
. After investigating the thus arising type of problem, one continues to perform the second discretization (inner discretization), ending up with a fully discrete scheme (see e.g.
[
49
]
).

We follow this tradition by first discretizing the equation (
3.5
) in time and then using Newton’s method for linearizing the time-discrete problem at the PDE level. Such a technique transforms the nonlinear stationary PDE at each time level into a sequence of linear PDEs which could now be solved using RBF collocation based on multiquadric (MQ) radial basis functions (see
[
50
]
and
[
51
]
for more details).

4.2
Semi-Discretization in Time

Given an equally spaced time grid
0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<...<\tau_{N}=T
which subdivides the interval
[0,T]
into
N
sub-intervals of the form
[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}],~n=0,1,2,\cdots,N-1
with
\tau_{n}=n\Delta\tau
and
\Delta\tau=T/N
, we first employ the temporal semi-discretization

U(\tau_{n},x)\approx U_{n}(x)

\frac{{U_{n+1}(x)-U_{n}(x)}}{{\Delta\tau}}=\theta{\cal L}[{U}]\big|_{\tau=\tau_{n}}+(1-\theta){\cal L}[{U}]\big|_{\tau=\tau_{n+1}},

(4.4)

to arrive at

[1-\Delta\tau(1-\theta){\cal L}]{U}_{n+1}(x)=[1+\Delta\tau\theta{\cal L}]{U}_{n}(x),\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

(4.5)

in which
\theta\in[0,1]
is the
implicitness level
of the scheme.

This last expression could be re-phrased as a linear elliptic partial differential equation of the form

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\Delta\tau}U_{n+1}(x)={F}_{n},\quad n=0,\cdots,N-1,

(4.6)

with

{\cal L}_{\Delta\tau}:=1-\Delta\tau(1-\theta){\cal L},\quad{F}_{n}:=[1+\Delta\tau\theta{\cal L}]U_{n}(x).

(4.7)

4.3
Meshfree RBF Collocation

In order to give a rough idea of the radial basis function collocation, we first need the following concepts:

Definition 4.4
.

A multivariate function
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is called radially symmetric or isotropic on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, if
\Phi({\bf x})=\Phi({\bf y})
whenever
\|{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf y}\|
. A radially symmetric function
\Phi(\cdot)
could be represented as
\Phi(\bf{x})=\phi(\|\bf{x}\|)
,
\bf{x}\in

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for some univariate function,
\phi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Note that the norms will be the usual Euclidean 2-norm, defined by
\|{\bf x}\|_{2}:=\sqrt{{\bf x}^{T}{\bf x}}
.

Before presenting the method, we first truncate the infinite spatial domain of the problem into a finite sub-domain of the form
I=[x_{min},x_{max}]
and then consider the projection of
U(\tau,x)
onto a finite dimensional space as a linear combination of radial basis function of the form

U_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\Upsilon_{j}^{(n)}\phi\left(\left\|x-x_{j}\right\|\right)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\Upsilon_{j}^{(n)}\Phi_{j}(x),

(4.8)

in which,
x_{j}
for
j=1,\cdots,N_{s}
is a set of scattered data points in the sub domain
I
and
\Upsilon_{j}^{(n)}:=[\upsilon_{j1}^{(n)},\upsilon_{j2}^{(n)},\cdots,\upsilon_{jH}^{(n)}]^{T}
. Now, if we use
\theta=0
and Substitute (
4.8
) into (
4.6
), we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\Upsilon_{j}^{(n+1)}\Phi_{j}(x)-\Delta\tau\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\Upsilon_{j}^{(n+1)}\mathcal{L}\Phi_{j}(x)=U_{n}(x).

(4.9)

So we have

(\Phi-\Delta\tau\Phi_{\mathcal{L}})\Upsilon^{(n+1)}={\bf F}^{(n)}.

(4.10)

where

\displaystyle\Phi=\left[\Phi_{j}(x_{i})\right]_{i,j=1,\cdots,N_{x}},

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{L}}=\left[\mathcal{L}\Phi_{j}(x_{i})\right]_{i,j=1,\cdots,N_{x}},

(4.12)

\displaystyle{\bf F}^{(n)}:=[U_{n}(x_{1}),U_{n}(x_{2}),\cdots,U_{n}(x_{N_{x}})],

(4.13)

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{(n+1)}:=\left({\Upsilon_{1}^{(n+1)}}^{T},{\Upsilon_{2}^{(n+1)}}^{T},\ldots,{\Upsilon_{N_{x}}^{(n+1)}}^{T}\right)^{T}.

(4.14)

It is worth mentioning that mega matrix
\Phi_{\mathcal{L}}
has a block structural where off-diagonal blocks entangles data from different regimes (see for more details
[
13
]
).

6
Conclusion

In this investigation, a radial basis function collocation method is proposed to evaluate the default probability of a firm where the value of the firm is satisfying the newly proposed synchronous-jump regime-switching model. We explained the detailed methodology of the proposed method including deriving a semi-discrete system from discretizing the time derivative with finite-differences and meshfree collocation on the spatial direction. Generalizing the proposed synchronous-jump regime-switching model to benefit from tempered stable processes, we theoretically proved that the proposed numerical method is stable and inherently de-singularizes the problem. The numerical experiments confirm the efficiency and accuracy of the method. This method can be utilized for other structural models based on the synchronous-jump regime-switching model both for pricing financial derivatives and also credit risk computations.
