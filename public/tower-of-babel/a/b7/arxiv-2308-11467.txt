The Bourbaki degree of plane projective curves
Marcos Jardim, Abbas Nasrollah Nejad, Aron Simis

Abstract
. Bourbaki sequences and Bourbaki ideals have been studied by several authors since its inception sixty years ago circa. Generic Bourbaki sequences have been thoroughly examined by the senior author with B. Ulrich and W. Vasconcelos, but due to their nature, no numerical invariant was immediately available. Recently, J. Herzog, S. Kumashiro, and D. Stamate introduced the Bourbaki number in the category of graded modules as the shifted degree of a Bourbaki ideal corresponding to submodules generated in degree at least the maximal degree of a minimal generator of the given module. The present work introduces the Bourbaki degree as the algebraic multiplicity of a Bourbaki ideal corresponding to choices of minimal generators of minimal degree. The main intent is a study of plane curve singularities via this new numerical invariant. Accordingly, quite naturally, the focus is on the case where the standing graded module is the first syzygy module of the gradient ideal of a reduced form f ∈ k ⁡ [ x , y , z ] f\in k[x,y,z] – i.e., the main component of the module of logarithmic derivations of the corresponding curve. The overall goal of this project is to allow for a facet of classification of projective plane curves based on the behavior of this new numerical invariant, with emphasis on results about its lower and upper bounds.

Introduction

Much of recent work on the homological facets of a plane curve
X=V(f)
has largely focused on the nature of the gradient ideal
J_{f}
of
f
. And yet, this approach is not so new (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
,
[
30
]
, and possibly earlier), coming from the need to characterize certain classes of singularities in purely algebraic terms, that is to say, by drawing upon the core of commutative algebra.
For example, a typical algebraic puzzle asks if there is a clear-cut watershed between the nature of an ideal
I\subset R
generated by three forms of the same degree and the gradient ideal of a reduced form. Of course, thanks to Dolgachev’s theorem, the Cremona issue makes them as apart as possible. But, what about the homological behavior? Does it disentangle the conundrum as to when the strong geometric background of the gradient comes from a loose choice of three forms?

The commutative algebra comes to help facing this conundrum by bringing out the related homology. This way, one trades out the hard geometric classification of singularity types for a “classification” of homological types referring to Betti numbers and degree shifts in minimal free resolutions. In terms of a classical algebraic language, one is after syzygies of graded modules and their degrees. In order to look closer at these syzygies one may resort to several methods, one of which is to reduce the problem to the case of homogeneous ideals.
The choice of trailing after the idea of the Bourbaki ideal goes along this pattern.

The notion of a Bourbaki sequence, that basically relates a module to an ideal via a pivotal submodule, was introduced in
[
2
]
.
It has soon been considered by several authors, for various purposes (
[
1
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
37
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
22
]
).
The present work is closer to
[
22
]
in that one searches to extract a numerical invariant out of a Bourbaki sequence in the graded module category.
However, in contrast to
loc.cit.
, where the Bourbaki number of a graded module
M
is the shift of the Bourbaki ideal in a graded Bourbaki sequence corresponding to the choice of an equigenerated submodule generated in degree at least the degree of a minimal generator of
M
of
maximal
degree, here we consider instead a Bourbaki ideal coming from a Bourbaki sequence corresponding to choices of minimal generators of
minimal
possible degree.
Moreover, the numerical invariant considered here is totally different, namely, it is the degree (multiplicity) of the Bourbaki ideal in the discussion.

The above is as much nearness as there exists between the two approaches.
We focus on a particular graded module over the polynomial ring
R:=k[x,y,z]
, having in mind the understanding of projective plane curve theory from this angle.
Namely, we consider the main direct summand of the module of logarithmic derivations associated with a reduced form
f\in R
. This module is canonically identified with the first syzygy module
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
of the gradient (Jacobian) ideal
J_{f}\subset R
of
f
.
Since
R/J_{f}
determines the singularities of the associated projective plane curve
X:=V(f)\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}^{2}
, the quest has both algebraic and geometric facets.

The novelty introduced here is a
Bourbaki degree

\operatorname{Bour}(X)
of
X
, defined as the degree of
R/I_{\epsilon}
, where
I_{\epsilon}
is the Bourbaki ideal associated to a choice of a minimal generator
\epsilon
of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
of minimal possible degree.
Bourbaki ideals defined this way have been considered before by Dimca–Sticlaru (
[
12
, Section 5]
,
[
13
, Section 3]
) and, in a non-explicit way, by du Plessis–Wall (
[
16
]
).
Both are closely related to this work but do not single out any new numerical invariant attached to a Bourbaki sequence, by rather focusing on other invariants, such as the total Tjurina number and the stability threshold related to the former.
Drawing upon the facilitation of a Bourbaki degree, our work entails further precision to the relationship among some of these invariants and the curve geometry.
We now briefly describe the sections of the paper.

The first section contains notation and preliminaries for the rest of the paper.
Here we introduce the module
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
of first syzygies of the gradient ideal
J_{f}
in terms of the module of logarithmic derivations of
f\subset R=k[x,y,z]
, thereafter elaborating on its basic properties. It will be our standing graded module throughout the paper.

The second section evolves around the details of a Bourbaki ideal
I_{\epsilon}\subset R
associated with the choice of a minimal generator
\epsilon
of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
. We give a formula for the degree (multiplicity) of
R/I_{\epsilon}
in terms of the degree of
R/J_{f}
, the standard degree
e
of
\epsilon
and the homogeneous degree of
f
.
This approach is pretty much the same as the one by Dimca and co-authors, but it has the advantage for the reader that it collects the basic facts and the respective proofs in one single theorem in purely algebraic terms, while the former is somewhat spread out in a few different papers and uses both algebraic and geometric arguments.

Likewise, we recover the definitions introduced in du Plessis–Wall, where they make use of a suitable colon ideal.

The tight relationship between the various terms in the formula above of the degree of
R/I_{\epsilon}
has encouraged us to call it the
Bourbaki degree

\operatorname{Bour}(X)

of
X=V(f)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
associated to
\epsilon
, in the case where the homogeneous degree of the latter is the initial degree of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
.
Focusing on the initial degree complies with Dimca’s approach, although a Bourbaki degree as such has not been a theme thereof.

As a follow-up to the basic structural theorem and the introduction of the Bourbaki degree
\operatorname{Bour}(X)
, we give some examples to illustrate the behavior of the latter. One of these has to do with the maximality behavior of nodal curves in this respect: besides attaining almost maximal bound for
\operatorname{Bour}(X)
, it also frequently attains the maximal possible degree of a generating syzygy. In this thread of line, we conjecture that the curve
f=(x^{2}-y^{2})z^{d-1}-(x^{d-1}-y^{d-1})x^{2}-y^{d+1}
(for any
d\geq 2
) does attain such maximum.

Next, is one main theorem proving the upper bound
\operatorname{Bour}(X)\leq e^{2}
for a singular curve
X
, where
e
is a minimal syzygy homogeneous degree.
Taking into account that the total Tjurina number coincides with the multiplicity
\deg(R/J_{f})
, this bound implies the main result in
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
.
The theorem also implies that if
e=1
then
f
is either free or nearly free, a result originally due to Dimca and co-authors.

A note of interest in the proof of this theorem is the inclusion of radical ideals
\sqrt{H}\subset\sqrt{I_{\epsilon}}
, where
H
denotes the ideal generated by the coordinates of a syzygy
\epsilon
of minimal homogeneous degree. This inclusion might be strict or an equality as the choice of
\epsilon
varies.

In the sequel, we establish a few scattered facts in the case where
f
is irreducible.
Using that the total Milnor number is bounded by
d(d-1)
in this case, we improve the structural lower bound to
\operatorname{Bour}(X)
all the way to
\operatorname{Bour}(X)\geq d+e(e-d)
.
As a corollary, there are no irreducible free divisors of degree less than
5
, a result that has been spread around as a clear statement, but no rigorous proof has been given so far. Finally, along this line, we show that if
f
is an irreducible free divisor of degree
5
then its minimal syzygy degree is
2
, a situation that repeats itself throughout many examples in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
,
[
26
]
).

The section ends with a characterization of curves such that
\operatorname{Bour}(X)\leq 2
, which takes care of the nearly free curves and some one-plus curves introduced by Dimca and co-authors.

The last section focuses on lower bounds to
\operatorname{Bour}(X)
.
We restrict ourselves to quasi-homogeneous singularities, with particular endeavor for the case of nodal singularities. In the spirit mentioned before in this Introduction, yet another paradigm of the maximality phenomenon of nodal singularities now takes place, this time around in regard to the minimal syzygy degree, which is now at least
d-1
.
By drawing upon the known upper bounds for the number of singular points, we state lower bounds for
\operatorname{Bour}(X)
in the reduced and irreducible cases, reflecting the cases where
e=d-1
or
e=d
.
Next is a final blow about the maximality of nodal curves, to the effect that a curve having a unique singular point, which is nodal, is characterized by the value
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=d^{2}-1
.

We end the section with some comments on the relation to Dolgachev’s theorem.
In particular, we give another proof of the case of Dolgachev’s theorem where one assumes that the curve is quasi-homogeneous.
This observation has been stated before in
[
27
]
and
[
34
]
.

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
Logarithmic derivations

In this short section, we recall the notion of the module (respectively, sheaf) of logarithmic derivations, frequently referred to as the
Derlog
module (respectively, sheaf). For an encompassing treatment, see
[
28
]
,
[
33
]
.

Fix a perfect infinite field
k
of high enough characteristic (as a function of certain data). For the geometric purpose, we may assume that it is algebraically closed of characteristic zero.
Let
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
denote a standard graded polynomial ring. The set of
k
-linear derivations of
R
into itself is an
R
-module, denoted
\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R,R)
, or more simply,
\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)
. The usual partial derivatives
\partial_{x_{i}}:=\partial/\partial x_{i}
, as maps from
R
to
R
, are
k
-linear derivations and form a free basis of the
R
-module
\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)
.
Since
R
is standard graded, then
\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)
admits a natural
{\mathbb{N}}
-grading where

\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}R_{n}\partial_{x_{i}},\;n\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
R_{n}
denotes the
k
-vector space spanned by the homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
.

The following notion can be stated more generally for ideals
I\subset R
, but in this work, we are only interested in the case of a homogeneous principal ideal
(f)
, where
f\in R_{d+1}
, with
d\geq 1
. Thus, we are looking at a hypersurface in projective space.

Definition 1.1
.

The
module of logarithmic derivations
of
f
is the submodule

\operatorname{Der}_{f}(R):=\{\delta\in\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)\ |\ \delta(f)\in(f)\}\subset\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R).

Since
f\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)\subset\operatorname{Der}_{f}(R)
, latter has same rank as
\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)

It has yet another notable submodule, namely,

\operatorname{Der}_{f}(R)^{0}:=\{\delta\in\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)\ |\ \delta(f)=0\}\subset\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R).

Writing a
k
-linear derivation as
\delta=\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
then, upon identification
\operatorname{Der}_{k}(R)\simeq R^{m}
via orderly mapping the partial derivatives to the canonical basis of
R^{m}
,
\operatorname{Der}_{f}(R)^{0}
becomes the
R
-module
\mathcal{Z}(\partial)
of (first) syzygies of the set
\{\partial f/\partial_{x_{i}}\,|\,1\leq i\leq m\}
.
Since the latter are canonical generators of the
gradient ideal

J_{f}
of
f
, we rename
{\rm Syz}(J_{f}):=\mathcal{Z}(\partial)
.

With this notation, there is a remarkable short exact sequence of
R
-modules (see, e.g.,
[
33
, Proposition 3.2]
)

0\rightarrow{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}_{f}(R)\longrightarrow J_{f}\colon\kern-2.0pt{{}_{R}}(f)\rightarrow 0.

Assuming, moreover, that char
(k)
does not divide
d+1
, the above sequence splits, and the complementary direct summand is identified with the
Euler derivation
\delta_{E}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
:

(1)

\operatorname{Der}_{f}(R)={\rm Syz}(J_{f})\bigoplus R\delta_{E},

1.2.
The role of syzygies

Note the graded presentation of the gradient ideal

(2)

0\rightarrow\sum_{e}R\epsilon_{d+e}(-(d+e))\longrightarrow R^{3}(-d)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Theta_{f}}}{{\longrightarrow}}J_{f}\rightarrow 0,

where
\Theta_{f}
maps the canonical
i
th basis element to
\partial_{x_{i}}
, while
\epsilon_{d+e}
is a syzygy of degree
d+e
in the grading of
R^{3}(-d)
, i.e., of standard degree
e
in the polynomial ring
R
. Shifting by
d
, we can write

(3)

0\rightarrow{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\longrightarrow R^{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Theta_{f}}}{{\longrightarrow}}J_{f}(d)\rightarrow 0.

Thus, throughout
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
is the module of syzygies of
J_{f}
in a graded resolution of the latter, with degrees of generators setback to standard degree.

From now on, assume that
m=3
and that
f
is reduced (i.e.,
(f)
is a radical ideal). For common reasons, we replace the variables by
x,y,z
.
Throughout,
k
is algebraically closed and char
(k)
does not divide
d+1
. In particular,
f\in J_{f}
.

It is natural to assume that the partial derivatives
f_{x},f_{y},f_{z}
of
f
are algebraically independent over
k
, as otherwise in this dimension the Hesse–Gordan–Noether classical result forces
f_{x},f_{y},f_{z}
to actually be
k
-linearly independent, i.e., dependent upon only two variables up to a linear change of variables (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Proposition 2.7]
). In other words, the algebraic dependence of
f_{x},f_{y},f_{z}
implies that
V(f)
is a cone. The hypothesis that
f_{x},f_{y},f_{z}
of
f
are algebraically independent over
k
can be rephrased, e.g., to the effect that
k
-subalgebra
k[f_{x},f_{y},f_{z}]\subset R=k[x,y,z]
has dimension three, or that the gradient ideal
J_{f}
has maximal analytic spread, or still that the fiber cone algebra of
J_{f}
is (isomorphic to)
k[x,y,z]
.

Since
f
is reduced,
J_{f}
has codimension at least two, hence it is locally Cohen–Macaulay in the punctured spectrum of
R=k[x,y,z]
(being primary to the maximal ideal or the ring itself in every localization).
Thus, locally in the punctured spectrum,
J_{f}
is either a codimension two perfect ideal or else the localized ring.
Then (
3
) implies that locally in the punctured spectrum,
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
is free in two generators.
In particular,
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
has rank two. In addition, being a submodule of a free module, it is reflexive.

Let
X:=V(f)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
denote the corresponding reduced projective plane curve of degree
d+1
, with singular scheme
{\rm Proj}(R/J_{f})
. Throughout we will be mainly focusing on a singular curve
X
, so
{\rm codim}(J_{f})=2
will be the case.

The coherent sheaf associated to the graded
R
-module
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
is denoted
\mathcal{T}_{f}
. With
{\mathcal{J}}_{f}
denoting the ideal sheaf associated to the gradient ideal
J_{f}
, (
3
) reads out as an exact sequence

(4)

0\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{f}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Theta_{f}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{J}}_{f}(d)\rightarrow 0.

This gives an identification

{\rm Syz}(J_{f})_{n}\simeq\mathrm{H}^{0}({\mathbb{P}}^{2},\mathcal{T}_{f}(n)),\;n\in{\mathbb{N}},

thus substantiating the terminology
vector fields sections
annihilating
f
used in
[
16
]
.

The following is the basic theorem that will guide our results.
It can be considered as an encore to
[
12
, Theorem 5.1]
, with a different emphasis as convenient to the sequel.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\epsilon\in{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
be a minimal homogeneous generator of degree
e\geq 1
. Then the following hold.

(a)

M_{\epsilon}
is free if and only if
f
is a free divisor.
In this case,
\deg(R/J_{f})=d^{2}+e(e-d)
.

(b)

If
f
is not a free divisor,
M_{\epsilon}
is isomorphic to a proper homogeneous ideal
I_{\epsilon}\subset R
of codimension two, such that the induced isomorphism
M_{\epsilon}\simeq I_{\epsilon}(e-d)
is homogeneous of degree zero.
In addition, for such an ideal
I_{\epsilon}
one has

(7)

\deg(R/I_{\epsilon})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{2}+e(e-d)-\deg(R/J_{f})&\mbox{if $f$ is singular}\\
d^{2}+e(e-d)&\mbox{if $f$ is smooth}.\end{array}\right.

(c)

If
f
is not a free divisor, pick a complete set of minimal homogeneous generators of the
R
-module
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
that include
\epsilon
, and let

(8)

0\rightarrow F_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\left[\lambda\atop\psi\right]}}{{\longrightarrow}}R(-e)\oplus F_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\epsilon,\varphi)}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\rightarrow 0

denote a minimal graded free resolution based on these generators, where the remaining shifts have not been specified.
Then a graded minimal free resolution of
I_{\epsilon}
is

(9)

0\rightarrow F_{1}(d-e))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\psi}}{{\longrightarrow}}F_{0}(d-e)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\circ\varphi}}{{\longrightarrow}}I_{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0.

In particular,
I_{\epsilon}
is a codimension
2
perfect ideal.

Proof.

First,
M_{\epsilon}
is a torsion-free module of rank one. Indeed, quite generally, if
F\subset E
is a free submodule of a torsionfree module
E
then
E/F
is torsionfree. This is because
F:_{E}(a)=F
for any
0\neq a\in R
.
Clearly,
\operatorname{rk}E/F=\operatorname{rk}E-\operatorname{rk}F
.

(a)
Suppose that
M_{\epsilon}
is a free
R
-module. Since
M_{\epsilon}
has rank one,
M_{\epsilon}\simeq R
. Then the exact sequence (
5
) splits
as
\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\simeq R(-e)\oplus R
, that is,
\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
is a free
R
-module generated by
\epsilon
and another minimal generator, necessarily, of (standard) degree
d-e
. In other words, one gets a graded isomorphism

\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\simeq R(-e)\oplus R(d-e)=R(-e)\oplus R(-(e-d)).

This means that
J_{f}
has a Hilbert–Burch free resolution, i.e., it is a codimension two perfect ideal. In other words,
f
is a free divisor (
[
33
, Proposition 3.7]
).

The converse is clear.

The second statement is a calculation with the Hilbert polynomial along the free resolution of
R/J_{f}
:

0\rightarrow R(-(d+e))\oplus R(-(2d-e))\longrightarrow R(-d)^{3}\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow R/J_{f}\rightarrow 0.

(b)
Again quite more generally, any finitely generated torsionfree module over a Noetherian ring
R
, having rank
r
, is isomorphic to a submodule of
R^{r}
(
[
35
, Proposition 3.5.3]
).

Thus, in the present case,
M_{\epsilon}
can be embedded as an ideal
I_{\epsilon}\subset R
.
Since
f
is not a free divisor by assumption, item (a) implies that
I_{\epsilon}
is not a free
R
-module, hence it is a proper ideal that is not principal.
Suppose that
I_{\epsilon}
has height one.
Since
R
is an UFD,
I_{\epsilon}=a\tilde{I}
, for some
0\neq a\in R
and an ideal
\tilde{I}
of height at least two. Then,
I_{\epsilon}
and
\tilde{I}
are isomorphic as
R
-modules by multiplication by
a^{-1}
, hence
M_{\epsilon}\simeq\tilde{I}
.
Since
M_{\epsilon}
is graded,
I_{\epsilon}
is homogeneous and so
a
and
\tilde{I}
can be taken to be homogeneous.
Finally, we rename
\tilde{I}
back to
I_{\epsilon}
.

Next, we claim that, under the assumption of the item,
I_{\epsilon}
has codimension exactly two.
Indeed, if
I_{\epsilon}))
has a regular sequence of length three, then by “décalage” (
[
35
, Proposition 6.2.77]
) ,
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{\epsilon},R(-e))=\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(R/I_{\epsilon},R(-e))=0
. Dualizing the exact sequence
0\rightarrow R(-e)\longrightarrow{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\longrightarrow I_{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0
into
R(-e)

yields the split exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mbox{\rm Hom}(I_{\epsilon},R(-e))\longrightarrow\mbox{\rm Hom}({\rm Syz}(J_{f}),R(-e))\longrightarrow\mbox{\rm Hom}(R(-e),R(-e))\simeq R\rightarrow 0.

Therefore,
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})^{*}\simeq\mbox{\rm Hom}({\rm Syz}(J_{f}),R(-e))\simeq R^{2},

hence
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
is free since it is reflexive. Then
f
is a free divisor, contradicting the assumption of this item.

It remains to determine a shift
s
such that the induced isomorphism
M_{\epsilon}\simeq I_{\epsilon}(s)
is graded homogeneous (i.e., of degree zero).
For this, we resort to a Hilbert function calculation.

We use the additive property of the Hilbert polynomial on the lowest horizontal graded exact sequence in (
6
). Then we draw on the respective tautological presentations of
R(d)/J_{f}(d)
and of
R(s)/I_{\epsilon}(s)
and use the fact that, since both
R(d)/J_{f}(d)
and
R(s)/I_{\epsilon}(s)
are one dimensional, their degrees (multiplicities) coincide with the corresponding Hilbert polynomials.

Thus, we get:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{HP}_{R^{3}}(t)-\operatorname{HP}_{R(-e)}(t)-\operatorname{HP}_{J_{f}(d)}(t)-\operatorname{HP}_{I_{\epsilon}(s)}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{HP}_{R^{3}}(t)-\operatorname{HP}_{R(-e)}(t)-\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)}(t)-\operatorname{HP}_{R(s)}(t)+\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)/J_{f}(d)}(t)+\operatorname{HP}_{R(s)/I_{\epsilon}(s)}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\binom{t+2}{2}-\binom{t-e+2}{2}-\binom{t+d+2}{2}-\binom{t+s+2}{2}+\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)/J_{f}(d)}(t)+\mathfrak{e}_{0}(R/I_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e-d-s)t+\frac{1}{2}(-e^{2}-d^{2}-s^{2}+3e-3d-3s)+\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)/J_{f}(d)}(t)+\mathfrak{e}_{0}(R/I_{\epsilon}),

because
\dim R/I_{\epsilon}=2
, where
\mathfrak{e}_{0}
denotes the algebraic multiplicity of a homogeneous quotient
R/I
.
Since we are thinking of
t
as a variable over
k
, and
[\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)/J_{f}(d)}(t)]_{t}=0
for
t\geq 1
as
\dim R/J_{f}\leq 1
, it follows that
s=e-d
and, additionally,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{e}_{0}(R/I_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(d^{2}+e^{2}+s^{2}-3(e-d-s)\right)-\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)/J_{f}(d)}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{2}+e(e-d)-\operatorname{HP}_{R(d)/J_{f}(d)}(t).

Now, replacing the multiplicity by the degree in case it is defined, we conclude:

\deg(R/I_{\epsilon})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{2}+e(e-d)-\deg(R/J_{f})&\mbox{if $f$ is singular}\\
d^{2}+e(e-d)&\mbox{if $f$ is smooth}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 2.5

(1) Theorem

2.2.
Propaedeutic examples

As a rule, when bringing up examples, we assume that the ground field characteristic is well-behaved with respect to the essential exponents.

Example 2.6

Let

2.3.
A distinguished upper bound

We give an upper bound for the Bourbaki degree solely in terms of the initial degree of the syzygies of the gradient ideal of a curve.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
X=V(f)
be a singular curve of degree
d+1
and let
e=\mathrm{indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f}))
. Then

(13)

\operatorname{Bour}(X)\leq e^{2}.

Proof.

Dualize into
R
the lowest horizontal exact sequence in (
6
), with
M_{\epsilon}=I_{\epsilon}(e-d)
. Observing that
\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)),\,R)=0
because
R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e))
has homological dimension one, entails the short exact sequence

(14)

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)),\,R)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{\epsilon}(e-d),R)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(J_{f},R)\rightarrow 0.

Since
J_{f}
is Cohen–Macaulay locally on the punctured spectrum,
\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(J_{f},R)
vanishes locally on the punctured spectrum.

Claim 1:

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)),\,R)\simeq(R/H)(e)
, where
H\subset R
is the ideal generated by the coordinates of
\epsilon
regarded as column vector.

To see this, dualize into
R
the mid vertical exact sequence in (
6
) to get the exact sequence

0\rightarrow(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)))^{*}\rightarrow R^{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tilde{\epsilon}^{t}}}{{\rightarrow}}R(e)\rightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)),\,R)\rightarrow 0,

where the image of the map
\tilde{\epsilon}^{t}
is
H(e)
, with
H
as explained. Thus, we obtain the following short exact sequence

0\rightarrow H(e)\rightarrow R(e)\rightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)),\,R)\rightarrow 0,

giving
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(R^{3}/\tilde{\epsilon}(R(-e)),\,R)\simeq(R/H)(e)
canonically.

So much for the claim.
We then have:

Claim 2:
Every minimal prime of
R/I_{\epsilon}
is a minimal prime of
R/H
, and moreover, for any such prime one has a surjective map

(15)

(R/H)(e)_{\wp}\twoheadrightarrow R/I_{\epsilon}(e-d)_{\wp}.

Indeed, substituting the result of Claim 1 in (
14
) and localizing in the punctured spectrum, we obtain surjective maps

(R/H)(e)_{\wp}\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{\epsilon}(e-d),R)_{\wp}

throughout all primes
\wp\neq{\mathfrak{m}}
.
In particular, this holds if
\wp
is a minimal prime of
R/I_{\epsilon}
. In addition, it forces any such prime to be a minimal prime of
R/H
; in fact, it lies on the support of
R/I_{\epsilon}
, while similarly, the support of
R/H
in codimension two is the set of minimal primes of
R/H
.

Next, recall that the module
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
is free of rank two locally on the punctured spectrum. Therefore, the Bourbaki sequence implies that the Bourbaki ideal
I_{\epsilon}(e-d)
is generically a complete intersection (of two generators).
Then, as is well-known,
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(I_{\epsilon}(e-d),R)_{\wp}\simeq R/I_{\epsilon}(e-d)_{\wp}
for every minimal prime of the latter.

This takes care of Claim 2.

Now, note the length inequality from (
15
)

\ell((R/H)(e)_{\wp})\geq\ell(R/I_{\epsilon}(e-d)_{\wp}).

As a result, we get
\deg(R/I_{\epsilon}(e-d))\leq\deg(R/H)
via
the associativity formula for degrees.
The stated bound now follows since
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=\deg(R/I_{\epsilon}(e-d))
and
\deg(R/H)\leq e^{2}
because
H
is generated by three forms of degree
e
.

[

e=1

Proof.

By Theorem

\operatorname{Bour}(X)=0

2.4.
Special results

In this part, we collect a few results for the Bourbaki degree of certain classes of curves.

The following result characterizes smooth curves in terms of the Bourbaki degree.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
X
be a reduced curve of degree
d+1\geq 2
. Then
\mathrm{Bour}(X)=d^{2}
if and only if
X
is smooth.

Proof.

If
X
is smooth, then
J_{f}
is generated by an
R
-sequence, hence
{\rm Syz}(f)
is generated by the Koszul relations. Therefore,
e=d
.
In this case, the result follows from (
7
).
Conversely, let
\mathrm{Bour}(X)=d^{2}
and assume that
X
is singular.
Then (
7
) yields
\deg(R/J_{f})=e(e-d)
. Since
e
is the initial degree of
{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
then
e\leq d
, hence
\deg(R/J_{f})\leq 0
, which is impossible with
\dim R/J_{f}=1
.

In the non-smooth case, if
f
irreducible, one can add:

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
f\in R=k[x,y,z]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d+1\geq 2
and let
e:={\rm indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f}))
. Supposing that
f
is irreducible, one has:

(i)

If
f
is not a free divisor then
\operatorname{Bour}(X)\geq e(e-d)+d
.

(ii)

If
f
is a free divisor then
e\geq 2
and
d\geq 4;
in particular, there are no irreducible free divisors of degree
2\leq d+1\leq 4
. Moreover, if
d+1=5
then
e=2
.

(iii)

If
d\geq 2
and
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=1
then
e<d
.

Proof.

(i) We have
\deg(R/J_{f})=\tau(X)\leq\mu(X)\leq d(d-1)
, where the last inequality is stated in
[
7
, Chapter 4, Section 4, (4.5)]
when
f
is irreducible.
Now apply (
7
).

(ii) If
f
is free then by Theorem
2.1
(a), one has
\deg(R/J_{f})=d^{2}+e(e-d)
and, on the other hand, as above,
\deg(R/J_{f})\leq d(d-1)
. Therefore,
e\geq 2
.

For the additional statement, one can verify that
d\leq 3
leads to a contradiction with the inequality
e(e-d)+d\leq 0
.

(iii) Again, by the assumption and the above inequality, we have
e(e-d)+d\leq 1
.
Another calculation with
e\geq d
(hence,
e=d
) and
d\geq 2
leads to a contradiction.

Remark 2.16
.

Note that (ii) may fail if
f
is not irreducible, e.g.,
f=xyz
.

Recall the following class of curves introduced in
[
11
]
.

Definition 2.17
.

A reduced curve
X=V(f)
of degree
d+1
is called
nearly free
if
R/J_{f}
has a graded minimal free resolution of the form

(16)

0\rightarrow R(-d-a_{2}-1)\rightarrow R(-d-a_{1})\oplus R^{2}(-d-a_{2})\rightarrow R^{3}(-d)\rightarrow R\rightarrow R/J_{f}\rightarrow 0,

for some integers
1\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}
.

In analogy to the case of free curves, these shifts are called the
exponents
of
X
, and satisfy the equality
a_{1}+a_{2}=d+1
– in particular, one has
a_{1}\leq(d+1)/2
.

The following result characterizes nearly free curves in terms of the Bourbaki degree.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
X=V(f)
be a reduced curve of degree
d+1
. Then
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=1
if and only if
X
is nearly free.

Proof.

Assume that
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=1
. Since a Bourbaki ideal is a codimension two perfect ideal, the Bourbaki ideal
I_{\epsilon}
of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
associated to a syzygy
\epsilon
of degree
e=\mathrm{indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f}))
is a complete intersection generated by two linear forms and has the graded minimal free resolution

0\rightarrow R(-2)\rightarrow R(-1)\oplus R(-1)\rightarrow I_{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0.

Now we apply part (c) of Theorem
2.1
. One has

0\rightarrow R((e-d)-2)\rightarrow R^{2}((e-d)-1)\oplus R(-e)\rightarrow\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})\rightarrow 0,

which forces that
d-e+1\geq e
. Therefore, we get

0\rightarrow R(-d-(d-e+1)-1)\rightarrow R(-d-e)\oplus R^{2}(-d-(d-e+1))\rightarrow R^{3}(-d)\rightarrow R\rightarrow R/J_{f}\rightarrow 0,

which proves the assertion.

Conversely, assume that
X
is nearly free with exponent
a_{1}\leq a_{2}
. Then by (
16
), the
R
-module
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
has the graded minimal resolution of the form

0\rightarrow R(-a_{2}-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\begin{bmatrix}h\\
\ell_{1}\\
\ell_{2}\end{bmatrix}}}{{\rightarrow}}R(-a_{1})\oplus R^{2}(-a_{2})\rightarrow\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})\rightarrow 0,

where
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
are linear forms.
By Theorem
2.1
(c), choosing the unique generator
\epsilon
of standard degree
a_{1}
, we get the minimal graded resolution of
I_{\epsilon}

0\rightarrow R(-a_{2}-(a_{1}-d)-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{1}\\
\ell_{2}\end{bmatrix}}}{{\rightarrow}}R^{2}(-a_{2}-(a_{1}-d))\rightarrow I_{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0,

which forces that
a_{2}+(a_{1}-d)=1
as
I_{\epsilon}
is a codimension
2
perfect ideal.
Thus,
I_{\epsilon}
is generated by two linear forms, hence
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=\deg(R/I_{\epsilon})=1
.

Since the definition of nearly free curves is given in terms of the shape of the corresponding free resolution, one might expect that larger values of
\operatorname{Bour}(X)
are responsible for certain shapes of the resolution as for
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=1
.
This sort of approach has been the main thread of recent work of Dimca–Sticlaru, by stressing numerical invariants other than the one here.
The following proposition is an example of how one may proceed one step further with a case embodied in the event of
3
-
syzygy curves
as in
[

, Proposition 3.1]
.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
X=V(f)
be a singular singular curve of degree
d+1
and let
e={\rm indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f}))
. Then
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=2
if and only if

R/J_{f}
has a graded minimal free resolution of the form

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow R(-(d+(d-e+3)))\kern-7.0pt

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\kern-7.0ptR(-(d+(d-e+2)))\oplus R(-(d+(d-e+1)))\oplus R(-(d+e))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\rightarrow}}

\displaystyle R^{3}(-d)\rightarrow R\rightarrow R/J_{f}\rightarrow 0,

where
e\leq d+1/2
.

Proof.

Suppose that

has the graded minimal resolution as stated. Then,
\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})={\rm im}(\varphi)
has the graded minimal free resolution

0\rightarrow R(-(d-e+3))\rightarrow R(-(d-e+2))\oplus R(-(d-e+1))\oplus R(-e)\rightarrow\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})\rightarrow 0.

By Theorem
2.1
(c) the graded minimal resolution of
I_{\epsilon}
is of the form

(17)

0\rightarrow R(-3)\rightarrow R(-2)\oplus R(-1)\rightarrow I_{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0.

Hence

\mathrm{HS}_{R/I_{{\epsilon}}}(t)=\dfrac{1-t-t^{2}+t^{3}}{(1-t)^{3}}=\dfrac{1+t}{(1-t)}.

Therefore,
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=\deg(R/I_{\epsilon})=2
.
Conversely, assume that the Bourbaki number of

is two. In this degree the codimension two perfect ideal

is necessarily a complete intersection generated by a linear form and a quadratic form, which implies the graded minimal free resolution

0\rightarrow R^{3}(-(d-e+3))\rightarrow R(-(d-e+2))\oplus R(-(d-e+1))\rightarrow I_{\epsilon}(e-d),

which forces that

(c), it gives a minimal free resolution of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})

by shifting by

.

We note that a reduced curve whose gradient ideal admits a free resolution as in the above proposition is a special case of both a
3
-syzygy curve and a

– the latter having been characterized in terms of the shape of the free resolution of the corresponding gradient ideal in
[

, partially drawing on
[

]
.

It is quite obvious that, in terms of the Bourbaki degree, the result of
[

reads to the effect that a

-syzygy curve is characterized by

being the product of two suitable integers

.
It remains the question as to when such integers validate the effective existence of a corresponding

Remark 2.20
.

The inverse question, as to whether or when a given homogeneous codimension two Cohen–Macaulay ideal
I\subset R
is the Bourbaki ideal of some torsionfree maximal Cohen–Macaulay module
M
, has been solved in
[
21
]
, under the additional requirement that the sought Bourbaki sequence be of the form
0\rightarrow F\rightarrow M\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0
, where
F
is free of rank equal to the
type
of
I
(i.e., the number of columns in an
(n+1)\times n
matrix defining
I
).
In addition, the solution is essentially unique.

To relate to our setup, we have to observe two points: first, in our typical Bourbaki sequence, the departing module
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f}
is torsionfree – hence, has maximal dimension – but it has depth
2
, so is not Cohen–Macaulay. Thus, an inverse question would have to change gear at this point, possibly renouncing to any uniqueness statement.
Second, the free kernel in our Bourbaki sequence has rank one. Thus, to engage in the above, even forgetting about the Cohen–Macaulay property, we would impose that
I
has type one, which means that
I
is a complete intersection of two forms.

Then, in this situation, pretty much like in the proof of Proposition
2.18
(first direction), we can try to reconstruct a certain free resolution and expect it to be the resolution of a torsionfree
R
-module isomorphic to
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
, for some
f\in R
.
Though it sounds quite loose, this is what is happening in the proof of the theorem when the two forms are linear.

Of course, in our setup, the natural question would be as to when a given homogeneous codimension two Cohen–Macaulay ideal
I\subset R
is the Bourbaki ideal of some reduced curve
X
, for a suitable generating syzygy degree.
At the moment, we even lack some natural necessary conditions.

3.
Lower bounds to the Bourbaki degree

For any two positive integers
d,e
, one has
d^{2}+e(e-d)>d^{2}-2ed+e^{2}=(d-e)^{2}\geq 0
. Therefore, (
7
) implies that
d^{2}+e(e-d)
is an upper bound for the degree of a Bourbaki ideal associated with any choice among the minimal generators of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f})
. In particular, the Bourbaki degree of a plane curve of degree
d+1
is bounded above by
d^{2}+e(e-d)
, where
e={\rm indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f}))
– note that this bound bounces between the two extremes
d^{2}
and
d(d-1)+1
.

In this section, we deal with finding a corresponding lower bound.

3.1.
Milnor and Tjurina numbers

We provide a brief retrospection of the Milnor and Tjurina numbers in the case of a reduced singular curve
X=V(f)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
of degree
d+1\geq 2
.

Let
p\in\mathrm{Sing}(X)
. Viewing
p
as a point of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, its ideal is a minimal prime
\wp
of the one-dimensional ring
R/J_{f}
.Thus, assuming that
k
is algebraically closed,
\wp
is generated by two linear forms. By a projective transformation, assume, say, that
p=[0:0:1]
. Considering the affine chart
U_{z}={\mathbb{A}}_{k}^{2}
with coordinate ring
A=k[T,U]=k[x/z,y/z]
,
p
corresponds to the point whose ideal is the maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}:=(T,U)
. Set accordingly,
F(T,U):=f(x/z,y/z,1)
, and let
J_{F}=(F_{X},F_{Y})
be the gradient ideal of

in
k[T,U]
and
\tilde{J}_{F}:=(F,F_{T},F_{U})
its “full” Jacobian ideal.

Definition 3.1
.

The
Milnor number
(respectively, the
Tjurina number
)
\mu_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(F)
(respectively,
\tau_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(F)
) of
F\in k[T,U]
at
{\mathfrak{p}}
is
\dim_{k}A_{{\mathfrak{p}}}/(J_{F})_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
(respectively,
\dim_{k}A_{{\mathfrak{p}}}/(\tilde{J}_{F})_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
.
We often write
\mu_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(X)
(respectively,
\tau_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(X)
) if no confusion arises.

We call
M_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(F):=A_{{\mathfrak{p}}}/(J_{F})_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
and
T_{{\mathfrak{p}}}(F):=A_{{\mathfrak{p}}}/(\tilde{J}_{F})_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
the Milnor local algebra and the Tjurina local algebra at
{\mathfrak{p}}
, respectively.
The
total Milnor number
(respectively,
total Tjurina number
) of
V(f)

is the summation of the local Milnor numbers (respectively, local Tjurina numbers) at all singular points of
X=V(f)
.
The latter will be denoted by
\mu(X)
and
\tau(X)
, respectively.

The following is well-known, but we include a proof:

Proposition 3.2
.

(
k
is algebraically closed and char
(k)
does not divide
d+1
)
. Let
f\in R
be a reduced form of degree
d+1
defining a singular curve. Then, with the above notation, one has

(18)

\deg(R/J_{f})=\tau(X).

Proof.

By a linear change of coordinates, we may assume that
z\notin\wp
, for every minimal prime
\wp
of
R/J_{f}
.
By the associativity formula, one has:

\displaystyle\deg(R/J_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\wp}\ell(R_{\wp}/\mathcal{P}_{\wp}),\;\text{\rm where $\mathcal{P}$ is the ${\wp}$-primary component}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\wp}\ell(R_{\wp}/(J_{f})_{\wp})=\sum_{\wp}\ell(R_{\wp}/(f_{x},f_{y},f_{z})_{\wp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\wp}\ell(R_{\wp}/(f,f_{x},f_{y})_{\wp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau(X)

where
z\notin{\wp}
and the Euler relation
(d+1)f=f=xf_{x}+yf_{y}+zf_{z}
imply that
f_{z}\in(f,f_{x},f_{y})
.

The following consequence gives another proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X=V(f)
be a reduced singular curve of degree
d+1
and let
e=\mathrm{indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(J_{f}))
. Then

d(d-e)\leq\tau(X)\leq d^{2}+e(e-d).

Proof.

Since
V(f)
is singular, by Proposition
3.2
one can replace
\tau(X)
by
\deg(R/J_{f})
.
Then, the upper bound follows form (
7
), and the lower bound follows form Theorem
2.10
.

A natural question is whether one can compute the Bourbaki degree of a curve in terms of the Bourbaki degrees of its components.

Next is the picture in the case of two smooth curves.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
X_{1}=V(f_{1})
and
X_{2}=V(f_{2})
are smooth curves intersecting transversally, then

\operatorname{Bour}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})=\operatorname{Bour}(X_{1})+\operatorname{Bour}(X_{2})+(\deg f_{1}-1)(\deg f_{2}-1).

Proof.

Because of transversality, the intersection multiplicity of the two curves at every intersection point is one.
Then, by Bézout theorem and (
18
), one has
\deg(R/J_{f_{1}f_{2}})=\deg f_{1}\deg f_{2}
.
On the other hand, the singular points of
X_{1}\cup X_{2}
are nodal.
Now, for a non-irreducible nodal curve
V(g)
, the initial degree of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{g})
is
\deg f-2
(
[
10
, Example 2.2 (i)]
).
Then the result follows from (
2.1
).

3.2.
Quasi-homogeneous singularities

We refer to
[
36
, Section 2.2]
for the details of this subsection.

Quite generally, a polynomial
f\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is
Eulerian
if
f\in J_{f}=(f_{x_{1}},\ldots,f_{x_{n}})
. The Euler relation says that a homogeneous polynomial whose degree does not divide char
(k)
is Eulerian.
The notion of a quasi-homogeneous or weighted homogeneous polynomial is as near as one can get to the Eulerian property of a homogeneous polynomial.

Definition 3.5
.

f
is said to be
quasi-homogeneous
if one has

\lambda f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}f_{x_{i}},

for some integer
\lambda>0
not dividing char
(k)
and integers
w_{i}>0
whose
\gcd
is one.

The integers
w_{i}
are called (integer) weights, while
w_{i}/\lambda
are called rational weights.

We will henceforth only consider quasi-homogeneous singularities in affine dimension two.
Let
X=V(f)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
as before, with
f
reduced.
A singular point
p\in\operatorname{Sing}{X}
is called
quasi-homogeneous
singularity if
\mu_{p}(X)=\tau_{p}(X)
. If
X
has only quasi-homogeneous singularities, then
\mu(X)=\tau(X)
. The
Arnold exponent
of a quasi-homogeneous singularity
p\in X
with rational weights
(w_{1}/\lambda,w_{2}/\lambda)
is defined to be
\alpha_{F}=(w_{1}+w_{2})/\lambda
.

Notable examples of quasi-homogeneous singularities are the
simple singularities
.

We first list a couple of results to be used next.

Theorem 3.6
.

(
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
)
Let
X
be a reduced curve of degree
d+1
having only quasi-homogeneous singularities. Then
\mathrm{indeg}(\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f}))\geq\alpha(d+1)-2
, where
\alpha
is the minimum of the Arnold exponents of the singular points of
X
.

The following estimate holds for a reduced plane curve of degree
d+1
:

(19)

|\operatorname{Sing}V(f)|\leq d(d+1)/2.

This follows from the inequalities

2\,|\operatorname{Sing}V(f)|\leq\sum_{p\in|\operatorname{Sing}V(f)|}m_{p}(f)(m_{p}(f)-1)=\sum_{p\in|\operatorname{Sing}V(f)|}m_{p}(f)m_{p}(f_{x})\leq d(d+1),

where
m_{p}(f)
(respectively,
m_{p}(f_{x})
) denotes the multiplicity of
p
on
V(f)
(respectively, on
V(f_{x})
).
For the last inequality see
[
18
, Section 5.3, Corollary 1]
.

Moreover, if
f
is irreducible then (
[
19
, Lemma 18.5]
shows:

(20)

|\operatorname{Sing}V(f)|\leq d(d-1)/2.

In the sequel, by a nodal curve we understand a reduced curve whose singular points are simple nodes, one of the well-known simple singularities.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
X=V(f)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
denote a reduced nodal curve of degree

(i)

.

(ii)

The following bounds hold:

\mathrm{Bour}(X)\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d(d-1)/2&\mbox{if $e=d$}\\

If, moreover,

is irreducible then

\mathrm{Bour}(X)\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d(d+1)/2&\mbox{if $e=d$}\\
1+d(d-1)/2&\mbox{if $e=d-1$}.\end{array}\right.

If

is not a free divisor

)
. If

Proof.

A nodal singularity is quasi-homogeneous with Milnor number one. Therefore,
\tau(X)=\mu(X)=|\operatorname{Sing}{X}|

18
.

(i) The Arnold exponent of a nodal singularity is one (
[
10

(ii) Drawing upon (

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bour}(X)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d^{2}+e(e-d)-d(d+1)/2=d(d-1)/2+e(e-d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d(d-1)/2&\mbox{if $e=d$}\\

Similarly, if

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
X
be an irreducible curve of degree

\operatorname{Bour}(X)=d^{2}-1
if and only if

has a unique singular point and this is a nodal point.

Proof.

Assume that
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=d^{2}-1

11
) and (

d^{2}-1=\operatorname{Bour}(X)=d^{2}+e(e-d)-\tau(X)<d^{2}-e(e-d)-1,

hence

, a contradiction since

.

It follows that
X
has a unique singular point because otherwise
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=d^{2}
by Proposition
2.14
.

Now, suppose the unique singular point

of

Then we claim that
\tau(X)>1
. Indeed, otherwise,
\tau(X)=1
. That is, locally at

is one. By
[

Since

3.3.
Relation to a theorem of Dolgachev

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be a curve of degree
d+1
defined by the reduced homogeneous polynomial
f\in R=k[x,y,z]
. The
polar degree
of
X
, denoted by
\deg(\nabla(f))
, is the degree of the rational
polar map

\nabla(f)\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{2},\quad p\mapsto[f_{x}(p):f_{y}(p):f_{z}(p)].

By
[
9
, section 3]
, the polar degree is given by the formula

(22)

\deg(\nabla(f))=d^{2}-\mu(X).

The curve
X
is called
homaloidal
if
\deg(\nabla(f))=1
, i.e. if the polar map is a Cremona map of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. Now, drawing upon (
11
), one has

(23)

e^{2}-ed+\deg(\nabla(f))\leq\operatorname{Bour}(X),

with equality when
X
has only quasi-homogeneous singularities.

Recall the theorem of Dolgachev
[
14
]
which tells us that a reduced homaloidal curve of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
has degree at most three.
Once this is known, it becomes an easy exercise to verify the types of such homaloidal quadrics and cubics. In particular, any such cubic is either conjugate to
f=xyz
or
f=x(y^{2}-xz)
.
In particular, these are free divisors of exponents
(1,1)
.

We isolate the following case of Dolgachev’s theorem, with an argument via the Bourbaki number:

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
X=V(f)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
stand for a reduced homaloidal curve of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.
If the singularities of
X
are quasi-homogeneous then
X
is either a free divisor of
\deg f=3
or a non-degenerate quadric.

Proof.

By the above preliminaries, under the present assumptions, one has
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=e(e-d)+1
.
Since lives in the initial degree of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
, then
1\leq e\leq d
.
Since
\operatorname{Bour}(X)\geq 0
, it follows that
e(e-d)\geq-1
.
Thus, either, say,
e(e-d)=-1
., forcing
e=1,d=2
Moreover,
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=0
, which means that
X
is a free divisor, necessarily of exponents
(1,1)
.

Else,
e(e-d)\geq 0
, hence forcefully,
e(e-d)=0
.
This means that
e=d
and
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=1
. By Proposition
2.18
, the latter means that
X
is a nearly-free divisor. But, the initial degree of
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
is the degree of a Koszul relation, and there are three of these which are independent. Since
\mbox{\rm Syz}(R/J_{f})
is minimally generated by three elements for a nearly-free curve
f
, we must conclude that
f
is smooth.
But, for the latter,
\operatorname{Bour}(X)=d^{2}
(Proposition
2.14
) hence
d^{2}=1
, which means that
f
is a smooth quadric.

is rational and cuspidal, then
\deg(\nabla(f))=d
, in particular,
e^{2}-ed+d\leq\operatorname{Bour}(X).

Can these data help advance a facet of that conjecture?
