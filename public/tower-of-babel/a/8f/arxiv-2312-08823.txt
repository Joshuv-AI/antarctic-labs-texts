Fast sampling from constrained spaces using the Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm


Abstract
We propose a new method called the Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm for approximate sampling from distributions whose support is a compact and convex set. This algorithm adds an accept-reject filter to the Markov chain induced by a single step of the Mirror Langevin algorithm ( Zhang et al. 2020 ) , which is a basic discretisation of the Mirror Langevin dynamics. Due to the inclusion of this filter, our method is unbiased relative to the target, while known discretisations of the Mirror Langevin dynamics including the Mirror Langevin algorithm have an asymptotic bias. For this algorithm, we also give upper bounds for the number of iterations taken to mix to a constrained distribution whose potential is relatively smooth, convex, and Lipschitz continuous with respect to a self-concordant mirror function. As a consequence of the reversibility of the Markov chain induced by the inclusion of the Metropolis-Hastings filter, we obtain an exponentially better dependence on the error tolerance for approximate constrained sampling. We also present numerical experiments that corroborate our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Continuous distributions supported on high-dimensional spaces are prevalent in various areas of science, more commonly so in machine learning and statistics.
Samples drawn from such distributions can be used to generate confidence intervals for a point estimate, or provide Monte Carlo estimates for functionals of distributions.
This motivates development of efficient algorithms to sample from such distributions.
However, not all distributions are supported on the entire space (for e.g.,
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
).
In one dimension, some common examples are the Gamma and Beta distributions (supported on
(0,\infty)
and
(0,1)
, respectively), and the latter’s generalisation to multiple dimensions is the Dirichlet distribution (supported on a simplex
\Delta_{d+1}
, a compact and convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
).
Such constrained distributions not only occur in theory, but also in practice: for instance, in several Bayesian models
(
Pakman and Paninski 2014
)
, latent Dirichlet allocation
(
Blei et al. 2003
)
for topic modelling, regularised regression
(
Celeux et al. 2012
)
, more recently in language models
(
Kumar et al. 2022
)
, and for modelling metabolic networks
(
Heirendt et al. 2019
;
Kook et al. 2022
)
.
Analogous to sampling, optimisation over constrained domains are also of interest, and constrained optimisation is generally harder than unconstrained optimisation.
Two popular families of approaches for solving constrained optimisation problems are interior-point methods
(
Nesterov and Nemirovski 1994
)
, and mirror descent
(
Nemirovski and Yudin 1983
)
.

The constrained sampling problem

More formally, in this paper, we are interested in generating (approximate) samples from a target distribution
\Pi
with support
\mathcal{K}
that is a compact, convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and whose density
\pi
is of the form

\pi(x)\propto e^{-f(x)}~.

Here,
f:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
is termed the
potential
of
\Pi
.

The constrained sampling problem has been long-studied and a variety of prior approaches exist.
The earliest inventions initially focused on obtaining uniform samples over
\mathcal{K}
(i.e., when
f=0
).
This task had applications in estimating the volume of
\mathcal{K}
, and these methods were subsequently generalised to sampling from log-concave distributions supported on
\mathcal{K}
(equivalently when
f
is convex in its domain).
These inventions include the
Hit-And-Run

(
Smith 1984
;
Lovász 1999
;
Lovász and Vempala 2003
)
, and
BallWalk
algorithms
(
Lovász and Simonovits 1993
;
Kannan et al. 1997
)
.
Amongst other later modifications and analyses of these algorithms, the
DikinWalk

(
Kannan and Narayanan 2009
;
Narayanan and Rakhlin 2017
)
is particularly notable due to the use of Dikin ellipsoids which are extensively used in the design and analysis of interior-point methods in optimisation.
A commonality of all the aforementioned methods is that they only require calls to the (unnormalised) density of the target distribution.

To see how the gradients of
f
could be useful, consider the unconstrained setting where
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
A popular algorithm for sampling in this setting is the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (
ULA
), which is the Euler-Maruyama discretisation of the continuous-time Langevin dynamics (
LD
).

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}~dB_{t}

(
LD
)

\displaystyle X_{k+1}-X_{k}

\displaystyle=-h\cdot\nabla f(X_{k})+\sqrt{2h}\cdot\xi_{k};\quad\xi_{k}\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})~.

(
ULA
)

Without the Brownian motion term
dB_{t}
,
LD
resembles the gradient flow of
f
, and analogously, setting
\xi_{k}=\bm{0}
in
ULA
recovers gradient descent with step size
h
.
In the seminal work of
Jordan et al. 1998
, the Langevin dynamics was shown to be the gradient flow of the KL divergence in the space of probability measures equipped with the Wasserstein metric via the Fokker-Planck equation – thus making the connection between sampling and optimisation more substantive; see also the paper by
Wibisono 2018
.
However, in the constrained sampling problem described earlier, the Langevin dynamics (or algorithm) is not applicable since the solutions (or iterates) are no longer guaranteed to remain in
\mathcal{K}
, which we recall is a compact and convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The Mirror Langevin dynamics (
MLD
)
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Chewi et al. 2020
)
was proposed to address this inapplicability, which draws from the idea of mirror descent in optimisation.
Specifically, an invertible
mirror map
is used, which is often defined as the gradient of a barrier function (also called the
mirror function
)
\phi
over
\mathcal{K}
.
A closely related dynamics was proposed in
Hsieh et al. 2018
which is also termed Mirror Langevin dynamics, but this is not the focus of this work – for a detailed comparison between the two dynamics, see
Zhang et al. 2020
, and our interest is the dynamics defined as follows.

Y_{t}=\nabla\phi(X_{t});\quad dY_{t}=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}~\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}~dB_{t}~.

(
MLD
)

As noted for the Langevin dynamics in
LD
, without the Brownian motion term
dB_{t}
,
MLD
resembles the original mirror dynamics as proposed by
Nemirovski and Yudin 1983
for optimisation.
Fundamentally, the mirror function serves two key purposes: (1) it changes the geometry of the primal space (
\mathcal{K}
) suitably; the underlying metric is given by the Hessian of the mirror function, and (2) it allows us to perform unconstrained
LD
-style diffusion in the dual space (the range of the mirror map) which is unconstrained when the domain is bounded.
Due to the occurrence of the time-varying diffusion matrix in
MLD
, the dynamics can be discretised in a variety of ways.
The most basic discretisation (or also called the Euler-Maruyama discretisation) of
MLD
is commonly referred to as the Mirror Langevin algorithm (
MLA
)
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022a
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} Y_{k}&=\nabla\phi(X_{k})~;\\
Y_{k+1}-Y_{k}&=-h\cdot\nabla f(X_{k})+\sqrt{2h}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{k})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}~\xi_{k}~,\qquad\xi_{k}\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})~;\\
X_{k+1}&=(\nabla\phi)^{-1}(Y_{k+1})~.\end{aligned}\right.

(
MLA
)

Other discretisations have since been proposed and analysed
(
Ahn and Chewi 2021
;
Jiang 2021
)
; however, the advantage of
MLA
over these other discretisations is that
MLA
can be implemented exactly, whereas the latter methods involve simulating a stochastic differential equation (SDE) which cannot be performed exactly, and has to be approximated for practical purposes.

Theoretically, for
MLA
, the distribution of
X_{k}
as
k\to\infty
is not guaranteed to coincide with
\Pi
, and such an algorithm is said to be
biased
, where the distance between this limit and
\Pi
is termed the
bias
.
This phenomenon is not specific to
MLA
; for instance, the more popular
ULA
is also biased, and this bias is shown to scale with the step size
h
under relatively weak assumptions made on the target distribution – see
Durmus and Moulines 2017
;
Dalalyan 2017b
;
Dalalyan 2017a
;
Dalalyan and Karagulyan 2019
;
Cheng and Bartlett 2018
;
Vempala and Wibisono 2019
;
Li et al. 2022b
for a variety of analyses of
ULA
.
Interestingly, due to the dependence on
h
, the bias of
ULA
tends to
0
as
h\to 0
(thus termed a “vanishing bias”).
In the case of
MLA
however, the first analysis by
Zhang et al. 2020
suggested that
MLA
had a non-vanishing bias, and conjectured that this was unavoidable.
Notably, their analysis assumed a
modified self-concordance condition
on
\phi
, which is sufficient to ensure the existence of strong solutions for the continuous-time dynamics (
MLD
).
In contrast, a more natural assumption placed on
\phi
is
self-concordance
, especially considered in optimisation.
A subsequent but different analysis of
MLA
by
Jiang 2021
assumed the more standard self-concordance condition over
\phi
, but their analysis was also unable to improve the non-vanishing bias of
MLA
.
They also presented an analysis of an alternate yet impractical discretisation of
MLD
developed and analysed by
Ahn and Chewi 2021
that we refer to as
MLA
FD
, and independent analyses in both of these works showed that
MLA
FD
has a vanishing bias when the more natural self-concordance condition over
\phi
is considered.
More recently,
Li et al. 2022a
improved the result due to
Zhang et al. 2020
, and showed that
MLA
has a vanishing bias when
\phi
satisfies the modified self-concordance condition.
This newer result is based on a mean-square analysis technique developed in
Li et al. 2019
, for which the modified self-concordance condition is more amenable.
However, this modified self-concordance condition does not imply some of the more desirable properties that self-concordance functions satisfy, for example
affine invariance
, which we discuss later.

A summary of our work

Despite these recent results, a question that has been left unanswered is if
MLA
, or any other exactly implementable algorithm based on discretising
MLD
has a vanishing bias when
\phi
is simply self-concordant.
In this paper, we give an algorithm that is
unbiased
, and with provable guarantees while assuming self-concordance of
\phi
.
Our proposed algorithm applies a Metropolis-Hastings filter to the proposal induced by a single step of
MLA
– we call this the
Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm
(or
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
in short).
Each iteration of the algorithm is composed of three key steps, and these are formalised in
Algorithm

1
in the sequel.

1.

Generate a proposal
Z_{k}
from iterate
X_{k}
using a single step of
MLA
.

2.

Compute the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability
p_{\mathrm{accept}}
.

3.

With probability
p_{\mathrm{accept}}
, set
X_{k+1}=Z_{k}
(accept); otherwise set
X_{k+1}=X_{k}
(reject).

In principle, this is similar to the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (
MALA
)
(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
)
, which applies a Metropolis-Hastings filter to the proposal induced by a single step of
ULA
.
Due to the form of
MLA
,
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
is exactly implementable much like
MALA
, which crucially relies on the ability to compute the proposal densities.
Moreover by construction, the inclusion of a Metropolis-Hastings filter results in a Markov chain that is reversible with respect to the target distribution.
Consequently, the algorithm converges to the target distribution (and is therefore unbiased) unlike
MLA
and other discretisations of
MLD
, and does so exponentially quickly.
In particular, let
f
be
\mu
-strongly convex,
\lambda
-smooth, and
\beta
-Lipschitz relative to a self-concordant mirror function
\phi
(see
Section

2.1
for formal definitions).
We show in
Theorem

3.1
that

1.

when
\mu>0
, the
\delta
-mixing time of
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
scales as
{\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\max\left\{d^{3},d\lambda,\beta^{2}\right\}\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
, and

2.

when
\mu=0
, the
\delta
-mixing time of
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
scales as
{\cal O}\left(\nu\cdot\max\{d^{3},d\lambda,\beta^{2}\}\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
, where
\nu
is a constant that depends on the structure of
\mathcal{K}
as induced by
\phi
.

We obtain these guarantees through the classical one-step overlap technique due to
Lovász and Simonovits 1993
, which was also used to give mixing time guarantees for
MALA
in
Dwivedi et al. 2018
;
Chewi et al. 2021
.
Our analysis is however not a direct consequence of these aforementioned results specifically due to the occurrence of the mirror map
\nabla\phi
and metric
\nabla^{2}\phi
in
MLA
, which poses certain difficulties.
We handle these newer quantities by strongly leveraging the self-concordant nature of the mirror function
\phi
and use recent isoperimetry results for distributions supported on a Hessian manifold induced by a self-concordant function
(
Gopi et al. 2023
)
to provide guarantees for
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
.
We compare these mixing time upper bounds to those obtained for other unadjusted discretisations of
MLD
in more detail later this work, but here briefly remark on the mixing time upper bound for
MLA
shown in
Li et al. 2022a
which scales as
{\cal O}(\nicefrac{{d\lambda^{2}}}{{\mu^{3}\delta^{2}}})
, while assuming that
\phi
satisfies a modified self-concordance condition.

A special case where
\phi=f

In this case, the Mirror Langevin dynamics (
MLD
) specialises to the
Newton Langevin dynamics
(
NLD
), which is the sampling analogue of the continuous time version of Newton’s method in optimisation.

Y_{t}=\nabla f(X_{t});\quad dY_{t}=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}~\nabla^{2}f(X_{t})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}~dB_{t}~.

(
NLD
)

Chewi et al. 2020
showed that when
f
is strictly convex over
\mathcal{K}
,
NLD
converges exponentially quickly to the target in
\chi^{2}
-divergence at a rate that is invariant to affine transformations of
\mathcal{K}
.
This result is due to the Brascamp-Lieb inequality.
Hence, it is reasonable to expect that the Newton Langevin algorithm, which is the specialisation of the Mirror Langevin algorithm (
MLA
) with
\phi=f
, also has mixing time guarantees that are invariant to affine transformations.
However, specialising the most recent analysis of the Mirror Langevin algorithm in
Li et al. 2022a
with
\phi=f
is unable to show this, owing to the modified self-concordance assumption, which we elaborate on later in
Section

1.1
.
Despite this, our analysis does indeed yield a guarantee for the
Metropolis-adjusted Newton Langevin algorithm
that is invariant to affine transformations of
\mathcal{K}
.

1.1
Related work

The original version of
DikinWalk

(
Kannan and Narayanan 2009
)
was developed with the goal of sampling uniformly from polytopes, and the iterative algorithm was based on generating either uniform samples from Dikin ellipsoids centered at the iterates, or samples from a Gaussian distribution centered at the iterates with a covariance given by the Dikin ellipsoid
(
Sachdeva and Vishnoi 2016
;
Narayanan 2016
)
.
Other related algorithms are the
JohnWalk

(
Gustafson and Narayanan 2018
)
,
VaidyaWalk

(
Chen et al. 2018
)
, and
WeightedDikinWalk

(
Laddha et al. 2020
)
.

Narayanan and Rakhlin 2017
modify the filter in
DikinWalk
to make it amenable for the more general constrained sampling problem, and was recently modified by
Mangoubi and Vishnoi 2023
in proposing a modified
Soft-Threshold DikinWalk
, which primarily focuses on the case where
\mathcal{K}
is a polytope.

Kook and Vempala 2023
develop a broader theory of interior point sampling methods while generalising the ideas from the analysis of
DikinWalk
in
Laddha et al. 2020
.
As remarked in
Narayanan and Rakhlin 2017
, while the existence of such a self-concordant barrier is a stronger assumption than a separation oracle for
\mathcal{K}
, the self-concordant barrier enables leveraging the geometry of
\mathcal{K}
better.

The aforementioned methods related to
DikinWalk
do not require access to gradients of
f
.
Drawing inspiration from projected gradient descent for optimising functions over constrained feasibility sets,
Bubeck et al. 2018
propose a projected Langevin algorithm, where each step of
ULA
is projected onto the domain.
Another class of approaches
(
Brosse et al. 2017
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al. 2022
)
propose obtaining a good approximation
\widetilde{\Pi}
of the target
\Pi
such that the support of
\widetilde{\Pi}
is
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, but this does not eliminate the likelihood of iterates lying outside
\mathcal{K}
.
This enables running the simpler unadjusted Langevin algorithm over
\widetilde{\Pi}
to obtain approximate samples from
\Pi
.

MALA

(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
;
Dwivedi et al. 2018
;
Chewi et al. 2021
)
, or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (
HMC
)
(
Neal 2011
;
Durmus et al. 2017
;
Bou-Rabee et al. 2020
)
is also a viable option for sampling from log-concave distributions over
\mathcal{K}
; however, guarantees for these methods assume Euclidean geometry of
\mathcal{K}
, which poses difficulties.
Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (
RHMC
)
(
Girolami and Calderhead 2011
;
Lee and Vempala 2018
)
extends
HMC
to allow for sampling in such settings and with provable guarantees, and this has recently been modified to handle constrained domains better in
Kook et al. 2022
;
Noble et al. 2023
, while assuming that there exists a self-concordant barrier for
\mathcal{K}
that is computable.

Girolami and Calderhead 2011
also proposed the Riemannian Langevin algorithm as a generalisation of
ULA
, and this was subsequently analysed in
Gatmiry and Vempala 2022
;
Li and Erdogdu 2023
.
Relatedly, drawing from recent developments in proximal methods for sampling,
Gopi et al. 2023
propose a novel proximal sampler for sampling over non-Euclidean spaces based on the log-Laplace transform.
While this proximal sampler has desirable and interesting theoretical properties, it is yet to be experimented with in practice.

Condition number independence

In this context, the
condition number
is a property of the domain
\mathcal{K}
and is defined as
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}=\frac{R}{r}
, where
R
is the radius of the smallest ball containing
\mathcal{K}
and
r
is the radius of the largest ball contained entirely in
\mathcal{K}

(
Kannan and Narayanan 2009
)
.
A Euclidean ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of arbitrary radius has condition number
1
, and non-unitary affine transformations of this ball results in sets with different condition numbers.
Constrained sampling methods over
\mathcal{K}
that have mixing time guarantees that are independent of
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
are valuable, and this is because the complexity of approximate sampling from distributions with affine invariant properties over a possibly ill-conditioned domain
\mathcal{K}
is the same as approximate sampling from similar distributions over a well-conditioned (or
isotropic
) domain like a ball.
It is important to note that
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
is not the same as the value
\kappa=\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{\mu}}
; the latter corresponds to the conditioning of the potential relative to a mirror function defined over
\mathcal{K}
.
The dependence on
\kappa
is expected; intuitively, it is harder to sample from sharper / peakier distributions which correspond to a higher value of
\kappa
, even when supported over a well-conditioned domain.

The classical Hit-and-Run and ball walk algorithms for approximate uniform sampling from
\mathcal{K}
are not affine invariant, and the mixing time of these algorithms scaled as
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}
.
The Dikin walk is an essential improvement over these methods because its mixing time guarantees are independent of
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
, and this is primarily due to the involvement of the Dikin ellipsoids.
There is continued interest in developing methods whose mixing time guarantees are independent of
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
: recent analyses of
RHMC
have shown to satisfy this
(
Lee and Vempala 2018
;
Kook et al. 2023
)
.
As far as discretisations of
MLD
are concerned, the analysis of
MLA
FD
in
Ahn and Chewi 2021
gives mixing time guarantees that depend on constants in the assumptions made.
Notably, these assumptions are affine invariant, which implies these constants are independent of
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
, and results in mixing time guarantees that are
independent
of
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
.
On the other hand, the recent analysis of
MLA
due to
Li et al. 2022a
assumes a
modified self-concordance
condition.
This is not affine invariant in the sense that the parameter in this condition can change when the domain is transformed by an affine map
(
Li et al. 2022a
, §D)
.
Put simply, this parameter could depend on
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
.
As this parameter appears in their mixing time guarantee, it suggests that the mixing time of
MLA
is also possibly dependent on
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
.
Our mixing time guarantees for
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
crucially does not depend on
{\cal C}_{\mathcal{K}}
much like the guarantees for
MLA
FD
, and this is due to the affine invariance of the assumptions we make for the analysis.

.

Preliminaries

We begin by introducing some general notation that will be used throughout this work.
We remind the reader that
\mathcal{K}
is a compact, convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Notation

The set
\{1,\ldots,m\}
is denoted by
[m]
.
We denote the set of positive reals by
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix.
For
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define
\langle x,y\rangle_{A}=\langle x,Ay\rangle
, and
\|x\|_{A}=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{A}}
.
When the subscript is omited as in
\|x\|
, then this corresponds to
A=I_{d\times d}
, or the Euclidean norm of
x
.
For a set
{\cal A}
,
\mathrm{int}({\cal A})
denotes its interior, and collection of all measurable subsets of
{\cal A}
is denoted by
{\cal F}({\cal A})
.
For a measurable map
T:{\cal A}\to{\cal B}
and a distribution
P
supported on
{\cal A}
, we denote
T_{\#}P
to be the pushforward measure of
P
using
T
; in other words,
T_{\#}P
is the law of
T(x)
where
x
is distributed according to
P
, and
T_{\#}P
has support
{\cal B}
.
Unless specified explicitly, the density of a distribution
P
(if it exists) at
x
is denoted by
dP(x)
.

2.1
Function classes

Legendre type

For both the mirror descent algorithm in optimisation and
MLA
, a method to compute the inverse of
\nabla\phi
is required, as respective updates are made in the set given by range of
\nabla\phi
(termed the
dual space
), and then mapped back to
\mathcal{K}
, which can appear difficult at first glance.
When the mirror function
\phi
is of
Legendre type
, this is less daunting, since
(\nabla\phi)^{-1}
can be computed in terms of the
convex conjugate
of
\phi
.
A function with domain
\mathcal{K}
is of Legendre type if it is differentiable and strictly convex in
\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
, and has gradients that become unbounded as we approach the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
; a precise definition of Legendre type functions can be found in
Rockafellar 1970
.
The convex conjugate
1
1

1

also referred to as the
Fenchel-Legendre dual
.
of
\phi
denoted by
\phi^{*}
is defined as

\phi^{*}(y)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{K}}~\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(x)~.

The domain of
\phi^{*}
is the range of
\nabla\phi
.
Crucially, when
\phi
is of Legendre type, then
\nabla\phi
is an invertible map between
\mathcal{K}
and the domain of
\phi^{*}
, and the inverse of this map is
\nabla\phi^{*}
.
For a given
y
, the maximiser
\hat{x}
which achieves
\phi^{*}(y)=\langle\hat{x},y\rangle-\phi(\hat{x})
by optimality satisfies
y=\nabla\phi(\hat{x})
, or
\hat{x}=\nabla\phi^{*}(y)
.
When it is possible to express
\phi^{*}
in closed form,
\nabla\phi^{*}
can be computed as well.
Otherwise,
\nabla\phi^{*}(y)
can be estimated by approximately solving the maximisation problem in the definition of
\phi^{*}
.
Additionally, Corollary 13.3.1 from
Rockafellar 1970
posits that when
\mathcal{K}
is a bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the domain of
\phi^{*}
is
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
This implies that for any
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exists a unique
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
such that
\nabla\phi^{*}(y)=(\nabla\phi)^{-1}(y)=x
.
This fact is essential for
MLA
as the Gaussian random vector
\xi_{k}
in
MLA
is unrestricted, and
X_{k+1}
could be undefined unless the domain of

Self-concordance

Self-concordant functions are ubiquitous in the study and design of interior-point methods in optimisation, and are defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(
Nesterov 2018
)
.

A thrice differentiable strictly convex function
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
is said to be self-concordant with parameter
\alpha\geq 0
if for all
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
and
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

|\nabla^{3}\psi(x)[u,u,u]|\leq 2\alpha\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}\psi(x)}^{3}~.

Relative convexity and smoothness

These classes of functions can be viewed as generalisations of convex and smooth functions with different geometrical properties, and were independently studied in
Bauschke et al. 2017
and
Lu et al. 2018
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
g,~\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be differentiable convex functions.
We say that
g
is
\mu
-relatively convex with respect to
\psi
if
g-\mu\cdot\psi
is convex, where
\mu\geq 0
.
Equivalently, when
g,~\psi
are twice differentiable, then
\mu\cdot\nabla^{2}\psi(x)\preceq\nabla^{2}g(x)
for all
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
g,~\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be differentiable convex functions.
We say that
g
is
\lambda
-relatively smooth with respect to
\psi
if
\lambda\cdot\psi-g
is convex, where
\lambda\geq 0
.
Equivalently, when
g,~\psi
are twice differentiable, then
\lambda\cdot\nabla^{2}\psi(x)\succeq\nabla^{2}g(x)
for all
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a differentiable convex function.
The Bregman divergence of
\psi
at
y
with respect to
x
is defined as
D_{\psi}(y;x)=\psi(y)-\psi(x)-\langle\nabla\psi(x),y-x\rangle
.
The
\mu
-relative convexity and
\lambda
-relative smoothness of a function
g
with respect to
\psi
imply respectively that for any
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
,

\displaystyle g(y)

\displaystyle\geq g(x)+\langle\nabla g(x),y-x\rangle+\mu\cdot D_{\psi}(y;x)~,

\displaystyle g(y)

\displaystyle\leq g(x)+\langle\nabla g(x),y-x\rangle+\lambda\cdot D_{\psi}(y;x)~.

When
\psi(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}
, and
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
D_{\psi}(y;x)=\frac{\|y-x\|^{2}}{2}
for any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and substituting the equation for
D_{\psi}
in the inequalities above recovers the standard first order definitions of convexity and smoothness
(
Nesterov 2018
, §2.1)
.

Relative Lipschitz continuity

This class of functions is a generalisation of Lipschitz continuity of a differentiable function, and has been useful in the analysis of
MLA
FD

(
Ahn and Chewi 2021
)
.

Definition 2.4

(
Jiang 2021
;
Ahn and Chewi 2021
)
.

Let
g:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a differentiable function.
We say that
g
is
\beta
-relatively Lipschitz continuous with respect to a twice differentiable strictly convex function
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
with parameter
\beta
if for all
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
, it holds that

\|\nabla g(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\psi(x)^{-1}}\leq\beta\enskip\Leftrightarrow\enskip\beta^{2}\cdot\nabla^{2}\psi(x)\succeq\nabla g(x)\nabla g(x)^{\top}~.

Similar to the relative convexity and smoothness definition, when
\psi(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}
with
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\beta
-relative Lipschitz continuity of
g
with respect to
\psi
reduces to
\|\nabla g(x)\|\leq\beta
, which is an equivalent characterisation of Lipschitz continuity of
g
.
A special case to make note of is when
\psi=g
, and a function
g
that satisfies
\|\nabla g(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}g(x)^{-1}}\leq\beta
for all
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
is termed a

(
Nesterov 2018
, §5.3.2)
.
This property is useful in the analysis of Newton’s method in optimisation.
To avoid clashing with this terminology, we will strictly refer to

as the

.

Remark

The above properties: self-concordance, relative convexity, relative smoothness and relative Lipschitz continuity are invariant to affine transformations of the domain.
More precisely, let

be an affine transformation of suitable dimensions.

•

If

is a self-concordant function with parameter

(

.

•

If

is a

-relatively convex (or

-relatively smooth, or

-relatively Lipschitz continuous) function with respect to
\psi
, then

is also a
\mu
-relatively convex (or
\lambda
-relatively smooth, or

-relatively Lipschitz continuous, resp.) function with respect to

(see

Symmetric Barrier

Symmetric barriers were introduced in
Laddha et al. 2020
where it was used to obtain mixing time bounds for the Dikin walk and weighted variant.
This property was originally introduced in
Gustafson and Narayanan 2018
in the development of the John walk.

Definition 2.5

(
Laddha et al. 2020
)
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a twice differentiable function, and let

be the Dikin ellipsoid of radius

centered at

We say that
\psi
is a symmetric barrier with parameter

if for all

{\cal E}_{x}^{\psi}(1)\subseteq\mathcal{K}\cap(2x-\mathcal{K})\subseteq{\cal E}_{x}^{\psi}(\sqrt{\nu})~.

Examples of such barriers are the log-barrier of a polytope formed by
m

\nu=m
(
Lemma

5.4
), and the log-barrier of an ellipsoid with unit radius is also a symmetric barrier with parameter
\nu=2
(
Lemma

5.5
).
Notably, the symmetric barrier parameters of these log-barriers is independent of the conditioning of these sets.

2.2
Markov chains, conductance and mixing time

Markov chains

Let the domain of interest be
\mathcal{K}
.
A (time-homogeneous) Markov chain over
\mathcal{K}
is characterised by a set of transition kernels
{\bf P}=\{{\cal P}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{K}\}
, where
{\cal P}_{x}
is the one-step distribution that maps measurable subsets of
\mathcal{K}
to non-negative values.
With this setup, we have a transition operator
{\mathbb{T}}_{{\bf P}}
on the space of probability measures defined by

({\mathbb{T}}_{{\bf P}}\mu)(S)=\int_{\mathcal{K}}{\cal P}_{y}(S)\cdot d\mu(y)\qquad\forall~S\in{\cal F}(\mathcal{K})~.

The distribution after
k
applications of the transition operator to
\mu
is denoted by
{\mathbb{T}}_{{\bf P}}^{k}\mu
.
A probability measure
\pi
is called a
stationary
measure of a Markov chain
{\bf P}
if
\pi={\mathbb{T}}_{{\bf P}}\pi
.
A Markov chain
{\bf P}
is said to be
reversible
with respect to a measure
\pi
if for any
A,B\in{\cal F}(\mathcal{K})
,

\int_{A}{\cal P}_{x}(B)\cdot d\pi(x)=\int_{B}{\cal P}_{y}(A)\cdot d\pi(y)~.

If
{\bf P}
is reversible with respect to
\pi
, then
\pi
is a stationary measure of
{\bf P}
; this can be checked by substituting the equality due to reversibility with
A={\cal K},~B=S
.

Conductance, total variation distance and mixing time

The
conductance
of a Markov chain quantifies how likely a Markov chain would visit low probability regions; thus the mixing of Markov chains depends on how low its conductance is.
Formally, the conductance of a Markov chain
{\bf P}=\{{\cal P}_{x}:x\in{\cal K}\}
with stationary measure
\pi
supported on
\mathcal{K}
is defined as

\Phi_{{\bf P}}=\inf_{A\in{\cal F}(\mathcal{K})}~\frac{1}{\min\{\pi(A),1-\pi(A)\}}\int_{x\in A}{\cal P}_{x}(\mathcal{K}\setminus A)\cdot d\pi(x)~.

To measure how quickly a Markov chain mixes to its stationary distribution, we use the
total variation (TV) distance
, which is a metric in the space of probability measures.
The TV distance between two distributions
\mu
and
\nu
with support
\mathcal{K}
is defined as

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{A\in{\cal F}(\mathcal{K})}\mu(A)-\nu(A)~.

Let
{\bf P}
be a Markov chain with reversible distribution
\pi
.
For
\delta\in(0,1)
, the
\delta
-mixing time
from an initial distribution
\mu_{0}
of
{\bf P}
, denoted by
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}(\delta;{\bf P},\mu_{0})
, is defined as the least number of applications of
{\mathbb{T}}_{{\bf P}}
to
\mu_{0}
to achieve a distributions that is at most
\delta
away from
\pi
.
Formally,

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}(\delta;{\bf P},\mu_{0})=\inf\{k\geq 0:\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\mathbb{T}}_{{\bf P}}^{k}\mu_{0},\pi)\leq\delta\}~.

Finally, we introduce the notion of a
warm distribution
, which is useful for obtaining mixing time guarantees.
A distribution
\mu
supported on
\mathcal{K}
is said to be a
M
-warm with respect to another distribution
\Pi
also supported on
\mathcal{K}
if

\sup_{A\in{\cal F}(\mathcal{K})}\frac{\mu(A)}{\Pi(A)}=M~.

3.2
Applications of
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
with provable guarantees

In this subsection, we discuss some applications of

for which we can infer mixing time guarantees from

We use

3.2.1
Uniform sampling over polytopes and intersection of ellipsoids

For uniform sampling, the target density
\pi
is a constant function, and consequently
\nabla f(x)=0
for any

In this setting,

can be viewed as a Gaussian

in the dual space

We are interested in approximate uniform sampling from the following sets.

•

\mathsf{Polytope}(A,b)

•

\mathsf{Ellipsoids}(\{(c_{i},M_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m})

Let
\Pi_{0}
be a

For polytopes,

4.3
Acceptance rate versus dimension

Recall from

that a step size that scales as

.
Therefore, the choices of step size we used for the uniform sampling and Dirichlet sampling tasks discussed earlier are larger than what is sufficient theoretically.

.
We define the average acceptance rate for a choice of step size to be the average number of accepted proposals over

iterations after a burn-in period of

For uniform sampling, we consider step sizes

for
\gamma\in\{0.5,0.75,1.0,1.5\}
.
For Dirichlet sampling, we consider step sizes
h\propto d^{-\gamma}
for
\gamma\in\{0.75,1.0,1.5,2.0\}
.

For the uniform sampling task, when

5.1
Results for conductance using one-step overlap

The following classical result by
Lovász and Simonovits 1993
is the basis of the mixing time guarantee of
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
, which is a common tool for studying reversible Markov chains.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
{\bf Q}
be a lazy, reversible Markov chain over
\mathcal{K}
with stationary distribution
\Pi
and conductance
\Phi_{{\bf Q}}
.
For any
\Pi_{0}
that is
M
-warm with respect to
\Pi
, we have for all
k\geq 1
that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\mathbb{T}}_{\bm{Q}}^{k}\Pi_{0},\Pi)\leq\sqrt{M}\left(1-\frac{\Phi_{{\bf Q}}^{2}}{2}\right)^{k}~.

The above result indicates that it suffices to show a non-vacuous lower bound on the conductance of a reversible Markov chain to derive mixing time upper bounds for it.
To do so, we use the
one-step overlap
technique developed in
Lovász 1999
.
The gist of this technique is to show that when given a reversible Markov chain
{\bf Q}=\{{\cal Q}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{K}\}
, the distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\mathbb{T}}_{\bm{Q}}(\delta_{x}),{\mathbb{T}}_{\bm{Q}}(\delta_{y}))
(also equal to
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\cal Q}_{x},{\cal Q}_{y})
) is uniformly bounded away from
1
for any
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
that are close.
For
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
, recall that
{\bf T}=\{{\cal T}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{K}\}
denotes the Markov chain induced by a single iteration of the algorithm, and
{\cal P}_{x}
is proposal distribution defined whose density is given in
Eq.

2
.

Lemmas

5.1
and
5.2
precisely state how a one-step overlap can yield lower bounds on the conductance of
{\bf T}
, whose stationary distribution has density
\pi\propto e^{-f}
.
These are two separate lemmas, one for when
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex relative to a self-concordant mirror function
\phi
with
\mu>0
, and the other for when the potential
f
is simply convex i.e., when
\mu=0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a self-concordant function with parameter
\alpha
, and let
f
be
\mu
-relatively convex with respect to
\phi

Assume that for any

\int_{A_{1}}{\cal T}_{x}(A_{2})\pi(x)dx\geq\frac{3}{16}\cdot\min\left\{1,\frac{\sqrt{\mu}\cdot\Delta}{2\cdot(8\alpha+4)}\right\}\cdot\min\{\Pi(A_{1}),\Pi(A_{2})\}~.

Lemma 5.2

Let

\nu

Assume that for any

.

These lemmas are based on two different isoperimetric inequalities.
Specifically,

Next, we establish the one-step overlap that we have assumed in the aforementioned lemmas.
The triangle inequality for the TV distance gives for any

Proofs of conductance results and one-step overlap in

Prior to presenting the the proofs of

5.1
and
5.2
, we first introduce and elaborate on two specific isoperimetric lemmas which we use in the proofs, which follow after this introduction.

The proof of
Lemma

5.1
uses an isoperimetric lemma for a distribution supported on a compact, convex subset
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with density
\pi\propto e^{-f}
where
f
is relatively strongly convex with respect to a self-concordant function
\psi
with domain
\mathcal{K}
.
This lemma is due to
Gopi et al. 2023
; their original lemma assumes the self-concordance parameter of
\psi
to be
1
while we generalise it for
\psi
with an arbitrary self-concordance parameter
\alpha
.
Additionally, we have the following metric
d_{\psi}:\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\cup\{0\}
.
For any two points
x,y\in\mathcal{K}
,
d_{\psi}(x,y)
is the Riemannian distance between
x,y
as measured with respect to the Hessian metric
\nabla^{2}\psi
over
\mathcal{K}
.
We overload this notation to take sets as arguments; for two disjoint sets
A,B
of
\mathcal{K}
,
d_{\psi}(A,B)
is defined as
d_{\psi}(A,B)=\inf_{x\in A,y\in B}d_{\psi}(x,y)
.
We state the lemma due to
Gopi et al. 2023
as stated in
Kook and Vempala 2023
, which addresses the subtlety of
f
only taking finite values in
\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
, and also highlight the dependence on the self-concordance parameter.

Proposition 5.2

(
Kook and Vempala 2023
)
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a self-concordant function with parameter
\alpha
, and let
f
be a
\mu
-relatively convex function with respect to
\psi
.
Then, for any given partition
\{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\}
of
\mathcal{K}
,

\Pi(A_{3})\geq C_{\alpha}\cdot\sqrt{\mu}\cdot d_{\psi}(A_{1},A_{2})\cdot\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1})~,\Pi(A_{2})\right\}

for positive constant
C_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{8\alpha+4}
.

To operationalise this in order to obtain lower bounds on the conductance using the one-step overlap which uses a closeness criterion in terms of
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}
, we require a relation between
d_{\phi}(x,y)
and
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}
.
The self-concordance property of
\phi
ensures that the metric
\nabla^{2}\phi
is relatively stable between two points that are close, and thus implying that the Riemannian distance between
x,y
is approximately the local norm
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}
.
The following lemma from
Nesterov and Todd 2002
formalises this intuition.
The lemma below is a slight modification of their original lemma that makes the dependence on the self-concordance parameter explicit.

Proposition 5.3

(
Nesterov and Todd 2002
)
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex and self-concordant with parameter
\alpha
.
For any
\Delta\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})
, if for any
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
it holds that
d_{\psi}(x,y)\leq\Delta-\alpha\cdot\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2}
, then
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\psi(x)}\leq\Delta
.

Next, for the proof of
Lemma

5.2
, we use an isoperimetric inequality for a log-concave distribution
\Pi
from
Lovász and Vempala 2003
.
Before we state this, we introduce the notion of the cross ratio between two points in
\mathcal{K}
, which is compact.
Let
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
, and consider a line segment between
x
and
y
.
Let the endpoints of this extension of the line segment to the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
be
p
and
q
respectively (thus forming a chord), with points in the order
p,x,y,q
.
The cross ratio between
x
and
y
(with respect to
\mathcal{K}
) is

\mathrm{CR}(x,y;\mathcal{K})=\frac{\|y-x\|\cdot\|q-p\|}{\|y-q\|\cdot\|p-x\|}~.

We overload this notation to define the cross ratio between sets as

\mathrm{CR}(A_{2},A_{1};\mathcal{K})=\inf\limits_{y\in A_{2},~x\in A_{1}}\mathrm{CR}(y,x;\mathcal{K})~.

Proposition 5.4

(
Lovász and Vempala 2003
)
.

Let
\Pi
be a log-concave distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with support
\mathcal{K}
.
For any measurable partition
\{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\}
of
\mathcal{K}
,

\Pi(A_{3})\geq\mathrm{CR}(A_{2},A_{1};\mathcal{K})\cdot\Pi(A_{2})\cdot\Pi(A_{1})~.

As seen previously, to operationalise this isoperimetric inequality, we have to relate the cross ratio between two points to the local norm
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}
.
When
\phi
is a symmetric barrier, this is possible due to the following lemma.

Proposition 5.5

(
Laddha et al. 2020
)
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric barrier with parameter
\nu
.
Then, for any
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
,
\mathrm{CR}(y,x;\mathcal{K})\geq\frac{\|y-x\|_{\nabla^{2}\psi(y)}}{\sqrt{\nu}}
.

With all of these ingredients, we give the proofs of
Lemmas

5.1
and
5.2
.
The proofs of both lemmas only differ in the isoperimetric inequalities used, and hence are presented together.

5.3.2
Proof of
Lemma

5.3

In this subsection, we give the proof of both statements of the lemma.
Specifically,

Part 1

provides the proof for the first statement about

, and

Part 2

provides the proof for the second statement about
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\cal T}_{x},{\cal P}_{x})
.

Before giving the proofs, we state some key properties of
\phi
due to its self-concordance.

Proposition 5.6

(
Nesterov 2018
)
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be a self-concordant function with parameter
\alpha
.

1.

For any
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
such that
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}<\frac{1}{\alpha}
,

(1-\alpha\cdot\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)})^{2}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)\preceq\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\preceq\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)})^{2}}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)~.

2.

For any
x,y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{K})
such that
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
, the matrix

G_{y}=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\phi(y+\tau(x-y))d\tau

satisfies

G_{y}\preceq\frac{1}{1-\alpha\cdot\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)~.

3.

\phi^{*}
is also a self-concordant function with parameter
\alpha
.

Part 1.
.

By definition of
{\cal P}_{x}
,
{\cal P}_{x}=(\nabla\phi^{*})_{\#}\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x}
, where
\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x}={\cal N}(\nabla\phi(x)-h\cdot\nabla f(x),2h\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(x))
.
Starting with Pinsker’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\cal P}_{x},{\cal P}_{y})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}({\cal P}_{x},{\cal P}_{y})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}((\nabla\phi^{*})_{\#}\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x},(\nabla\phi^{*})_{\#}\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{y})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x},\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{y})}~.

Since
\phi
is of Legendre type,
\nabla\phi^{*}
is a bijective and differentiable map.
This allows us to instantiate
Vempala and Wibisono 2019
to get the final equality.

To further work with
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x},\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{y})
, we use the identity

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}({\cal N}(m_{1},\Sigma_{1}),{\cal N}(m_{2},\Sigma_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{trace}(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}\Sigma_{1}-I)+\log\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}+\|m_{2}-m_{1}\|^{2}_{\Sigma_{2}^{-1}}\right)~.

In our case,

\Sigma_{1}=2h\nabla^{2}\phi(x)~,~\Sigma_{2}=2h\nabla^{2}\phi(y)~,~m_{1}=\nabla\phi(x)-h\nabla f(x)~,~m_{2}=\nabla\phi(y)-h\nabla f(y)~.

This yields a closed form expression
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x},\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{y})=\frac{1}{2}\left(T_{1}^{P}+T_{2}^{P}\right)

where

\displaystyle T_{1}^{P}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{trace}(\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)-I)-\log\det\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)~,

\displaystyle T_{2}^{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h}\|(\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla\phi(x))-h(\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x))\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}~.

The rest of the proof is dedicated to showing that

T_{1}^{P}\leq\frac{h\cdot d}{20}~;\qquad T_{2}^{P}\leq\frac{1}{80}+\frac{9}{2}\cdot h\cdot\beta~,

under assumptions made in the statement of the lemma.

For convenience, we denote
\|x-y\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)}
by
r_{y}
.
From the statement of the first part of the lemma,
r_{y}\leq\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10\cdot\bar{\alpha}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10\cdot\alpha}
, since
\bar{\alpha}=\max\{1,\alpha\}
.

5.3.2.1
Bounding
T_{1}^{P}

Owing to the cyclic property of trace and the product property of determinants, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{trace}(\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(x))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{trace}(\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})

\displaystyle\det\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\det\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Let
M=\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
for convenience, and let
\{\lambda_{i}(M)\}_{i=1}^{d}
be its eigenvalues.

T_{1}^{P}=\mathrm{trace}(M-I)-\log\det M=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\{\lambda_{i}(M)-1-\log\lambda_{i}(M)\}~.

Lemma

A.1
states that
\lambda_{i}(M)-1-\log\lambda_{i}(M)\leq\frac{(\lambda_{i}(M)-1)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}(M)}
.
Therefore, we have the upper bound

T_{1}^{P}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(\lambda_{i}(M)-1)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}(M)}~.

From
Proposition

5.6
(1), each
\lambda_{i}(M)
is bounded as

\lambda_{i}(M)\in\left[\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}~,~(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}\right]~.

The function
t\mapsto\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{t}
is strictly convex for
t>0
.
This is because its second derivative is
t\mapsto\frac{1}{t^{3}}
.
This implies that when
t
is restricted to a closed interval
[a,b]
, the maximum of
\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{t}
is attained at the end points.

\max_{t\in[a,b]}\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{t}=\max\left\{\frac{(a-1)^{2}}{a},\frac{(b-1)^{2}}{b}\right\}~.

When
b=\frac{1}{a}
, the maximum on the right hand side is
\frac{(a-1)^{2}}{a}
.
We use this fact with bounds on
t=\lambda_{i}(M)
, and thus have for all
i\in[d]
that

\frac{(\lambda_{i}(M)-1)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}(M)}\leq\frac{((1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}-1)^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}=\frac{\alpha^{2}\cdot r_{y}^{2}\cdot(2-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}\leq\frac{4\cdot\alpha^{2}\cdot r_{y}^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}~.

and consequently,

T_{1}^{P}\leq d\cdot\frac{4\cdot\alpha^{2}\cdot r_{y}^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}~.

The function
t\mapsto\frac{4t^{2}}{(1-t)^{2}}
is an increasing function since it is a product of two increasing functions
t\mapsto 4t^{2}
and
t\mapsto\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}
.
As stated earlier,
r_{y}\leq\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10\cdot\alpha}
, which implies

T_{1}^{P}\leq d\cdot\frac{4\cdot\frac{h}{100}}{(1-\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10})^{2}}\leq\frac{h\cdot d}{20}~.

The final inequality uses the fact that
h\leq 1
which implies
\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10}\right)^{-2}\leq\frac{100}{81}\leq\frac{5}{4}
.

5.3.2.2
Bounding
T_{2}^{P}

Using the fact that
\|a+b\|_{M}^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}_{M}+2\|b\|_{M}^{2}
successively,

\displaystyle T_{2}^{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h}\|(\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla\phi(x))-h(\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x))\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2h}\left(2\cdot\|\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla\phi(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}+2h^{2}\cdot\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h}\cdot\|\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla\phi(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}+2h\cdot\|\nabla f(y)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}+2h\cdot\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}~.

Note that

\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\tau}\nabla\phi(y+\tau(x-y))d\tau=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\phi(y+\tau(x-y))(x-y)d\tau=G_{y}(x-y).

From
Proposition

5.6
(2), and using the fact that
r_{y}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
, we have
G_{y}\preceq\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)
.
By virtue of
\phi
being of Legendre type,
G_{y}\succ 0
.
As a result,

G_{y}^{-1}\succeq(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}\Leftrightarrow G_{y}\preceq\frac{1}{1-\alpha\cdot r_{y}}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)~,

(11)

where the equivalence is due to the Loewner ordering.
Let
v=G_{y}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(x-y)
.

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|G_{y}(x-y)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\langle v,G_{y}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}G_{y}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\langle v,v\rangle}{1-\alpha\cdot r_{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle x-y,G_{y}(x-y)\rangle}{1-\alpha\cdot r_{y}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{y}^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}~.

The inequalities above are due to
Eq.

11
.
Next, from
Proposition

5.6
(1), we have
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\succeq\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(y)
, and using the fact that
\phi
is strictly convex yields

\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}\cdot\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}~.

This gives a bound for
T_{2}^{P}
in terms of
r_{y}
as

\displaystyle T_{2}^{P}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{y}^{2}}{h\cdot(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}+2h\cdot\|\nabla f(y)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(y)^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{2h\cdot\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{y}^{2}}{h\cdot(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}+2h\cdot\beta^{2}+\frac{2h\cdot\beta^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{2}}~,

where in the final inequality, we have used the fact that
f
is a
\beta
-relatively Lipschitz with respect to
\phi
.
Finally, since
\alpha\cdot r_{y}\leq\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10}\leq\frac{1}{10}
,
(1-\alpha\cdot r_{y})^{-2}\leq\frac{5}{4}
as noted earlier.
This gives the upper bound

T_{2}^{P}\leq\frac{5}{4}\cdot\frac{r_{y}^{2}}{h}+\frac{9}{2}\cdot h\cdot\beta^{2}~.

From the statement of the lemma,
r_{y}\leq\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10\cdot\max\{1,\alpha\}}
which also implies that
r_{y}\leq\frac{\sqrt{h}}{10}
.
Finally, substituting this bound over
r_{y}
, we get

T_{2}^{P}\leq\frac{5}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{h}{100}+\frac{9}{2}\cdot h\cdot\beta\leq\frac{1}{80}+\frac{9}{2}\cdot h\cdot\beta^{2}~.

Using the bounds derived for
T_{1}^{P}
and
T_{2}^{P}
, we can complete the proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x},\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{y})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{h\cdot d}{20}+\frac{1}{80}+\frac{9}{2}\cdot h\cdot\beta^{2}\right)~.

Finally, applying Pinsker’s inequality as stated in the beginning of the proof yields

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\cal P}_{x},{\cal P}_{y})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{h\cdot d}{20}+\frac{1}{80}+\frac{9}{2}\cdot h\cdot\beta^{2}}~.

∎

Part 2.
.

For

(12)

The explicit forms of the

Recall that the transition kernel has an atom i.e.,

The explicit form of this is given by

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}({\cal T}_{x},{\cal P}_{x})=1-{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\right\}\right]~.

Our goal is to bound this quantity from above, which equivalently implies bounding the expectation on the right hand side from below.
This can be achieved using Markov’s inequality; for any
t>0
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\right\}\right]\geq t~{\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\right\}\geq t\right]~.

If

{\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\right\}\geq t\right]={\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\geq t\right].

Due to
Eq.

2

\frac{\pi(z)\cdot p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)\cdot p_{x}(z)}=\exp\left(f(x)-f(z)+\frac{3}{2}\left\{\log\det\nabla^{2}\phi(x)-\log\det\nabla^{2}\phi(z)\right\}\right.\\
+\frac{1}{4h}\|\nabla\phi(z)-\nabla\phi(x)+h\cdot\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}\\
\left.-\frac{1}{4h}\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)+h\cdot\nabla f(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}}^{2}\right).

Concisely,
\frac{\pi(z)\cdot p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)\cdot p_{x}(z)}=\exp({\cal A}(x,z))
, where
{\cal A}(x,z)=T_{1}^{A}+T_{2}^{A}+T_{3}^{A}+T_{4}^{A}+T_{5}^{A}
and each of these terms are

\displaystyle T_{1}^{A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\nabla\phi(z)-\nabla\phi(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}}{4h}-\frac{\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}}^{2}}{4h}

(14a)

\displaystyle T_{2}^{A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{3}{2}\log\det\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)

(14b)

\displaystyle T_{3}^{A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(f(x)-f(z)-\langle\nabla f(z),\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\rangle_{\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}}\right)

(14c)

\displaystyle T_{4}^{A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(f(x)-f(z)-\langle\nabla f(x),\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\rangle_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\right)

(14d)

\displaystyle T_{5}^{A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{h^{2}}{4h}\left(\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}-\|\nabla f(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}}^{2}\right).

(14e)

The self-concordant nature of
\phi
enables using
Proposition

5.6
to control some of these quantities above, but only when
\nabla\phi(x)
and
\nabla\phi(z)
are close in the local norm.
Therefore, we condition on an event
{\mathfrak{E}}
which implies that
\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\leq\frac{3}{10\cdot\alpha}
.
To be specific,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\geq t\wedge{\mathfrak{E}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}\left[\left.\frac{\pi(z)p_{z}(x)}{\pi(x)p_{x}(z)}\geq t~\right|~{\mathfrak{E}}\right]\cdot{\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}({\mathfrak{E}})~.

The remainder of the proof consists of three parts.

•

In
5.3.2.3
, we identify an event
{\mathfrak{E}}
satisfying our requirements, and show that it occurs with high probability.
We show that for the choice of step size
h
assumed,
{\mathfrak{E}}
also implies that
\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\leq\frac{3}{10\cdot\alpha}
.

•

Next, in
5.3.2.4
, we condition on event
{\mathfrak{E}}
and give lower bounds for each of the
T_{i}^{A}
quantities by leveraging the implication in the previous part.

•

Finally, in
5.3.2.5
, we show that for the choice of
h
in the statement of the lemma there exists
t\leq 1
such that the conditional probability is
1
, thus reducing the lower bound to
t\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}[{\mathfrak{E}}]
.

5.3.2.3
Identifying an event
{\mathfrak{E}}

Since
{\cal P}_{x}=(\nabla\phi^{*})_{\#}\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x}
,

z\sim{\cal P}_{x}~\Leftrightarrow~\nabla\phi(z)\sim\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{x}={\cal N}(\nabla\phi(x)-h\cdot\nabla f(x),2h\nabla^{2}\phi(x))~.

We use this to show that
\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}
concentrates well, since given
\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
,
\nabla\phi(z)
is distributionally equivalent to
\nabla\phi(x)-h\cdot\nabla f(x)+\sqrt{2h}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\xi
when
z\sim{\cal P}_{x}
.

We begin by expanding the squared norm as

\|\nabla\phi(z)-\nabla\phi(x)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\\
=h^{2}\cdot\langle\nabla f(x),\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)\rangle+2h\cdot\langle\xi,\xi\rangle+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\langle\xi,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla f(x)\rangle.

Consider the following probability

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{z\sim{\cal P}_{x}}(\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}>h^{2}\cdot\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\quad={\mathbb{P}}_{\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})}(2h\cdot\|\xi\|^{2}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\langle\xi,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla f(x)\rangle>2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\quad\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})}(\|\xi\|^{2}>d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon})+{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})}(\langle\xi,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla f(x)\rangle>\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})

where the final inequality uses the fact that for random variables
{\sf a},{\sf b}
,

\displaystyle({\sf a}\leq a)\wedge({\sf b}\leq b)\Rightarrow{\sf a}+{\sf b}\leq a+b

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}({\sf a}+{\sf b}>a+b)\leq{\mathbb{P}}({\sf a}>a\vee{\sf b}>b)\leq{\mathbb{P}}({\sf a}>a)+{\mathbb{P}}({\sf b}>b)~.

Through
\chi^{2}
concentration guarantees
(
Laurent and Massart 2000
, Lem. 1)
, we get

{\mathbb{P}}(\|\xi\|^{2}>d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{8}~,

where
{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}
was previously defined in
Eq.

12
.
Also, since
\xi
is Gaussian,
\langle\xi,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla f(x)\rangle
is a mean zero, sub-Gaussian random variable with variance parameter
\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}
.
This implies that for any
x\in\mathcal{K}
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})}(\langle\xi,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla f(x)\rangle>t)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\|\nabla f(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\right)

where the final inequality is due to the fact that
f
is
\beta
-relatively Lipschitz with respect to
\phi
.
Therefore,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{d})}(\langle\xi,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla f(x)\rangle>\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{8}~,

where
{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}
was previously defined in
Eq.

12
.
Therefore, with probability at least
1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}\cdot\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}~.

We hence pick the event
{\mathfrak{E}}
to be

{\mathfrak{E}}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\left\{\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{h^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}}\right\}.

Using the simple algebraic fact that
\sqrt{a+b+c}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{c}
, and from the choice of
h
in the lemma it can be verified that

h\leq\frac{1}{10\cdot\beta\cdot\alpha};\quad h\leq\frac{1}{200\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\alpha^{2}};\quad h\leq\frac{1}{50\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\cdot\alpha^{\nicefrac{{4}}{{3}}}\cdot\beta^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}~,

and we are assured that
\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\leq\frac{3}{10\cdot\alpha}
as required.

5.3.2.4
Lower bound on the
T_{i}^{A}
quantities

In this part, we condition on the event
{\mathfrak{E}}
which implies that
\|\nabla\phi(z)-\nabla\phi(x)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}\leq\frac{3}{10\cdot\alpha}
to obtain lower bounds on
{\cal A}(x,z)
defined previously.
We do so by giving lower bounds for each of the
T_{i}^{A}
quantities individually defined in
Eq.

14
.

In this part of the proof, we will make use of the identity

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}

\displaystyle\forall~x\in\mathcal{K}~.

(15)

This can be derived by differentiating the identity
\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))=x
by the invertibility of
\nabla\phi(x)
.

T_{1}^{A}
:

We use the shorthand notation
v_{x,z}=\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)
for convenience.

\displaystyle T_{1}^{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4h}\left(\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}-\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{1}{4h}\left(\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}^{2}-\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(z))}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}\frac{1}{4h}\left(\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}^{2}-\frac{\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}^{2}}{(1-\alpha\cdot\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h\cdot\alpha^{2}}\cdot\frac{(\alpha\cdot\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))})^{2}}{4}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{\geq}-\frac{3\alpha}{2h}\cdot\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}^{3}~.

Step
(i)
uses
Eq.

15
.
Step
(ii)
uses the fact that
\phi^{*}
is self-concordant with parameter
\alpha
from
Proposition

5.6
(3), and the result of
Proposition

5.6
(1) to change the metric from
\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(z))
to
\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))
since
\|v_{x,z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
.
Step
(iii)
uses an algebraic lemma
Lemma

A.2
.

T_{2}^{A}
:

We can use
Eq.

15
and the product property of determinants to rewrite
T_{2}^{A}
as follows.

\displaystyle T_{2}^{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\log\det\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\log\det\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}~\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(z))~\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\lambda_{i}(M^{*})

where
M^{*}=\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(z))\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, and
\{\lambda_{i}(M^{*})\}
is the sequence of its eigenvalues.
These eigenvalues lie in an interval due to
\phi^{*}
being self-concordant, and the associated Hessian ordering as given in
Proposition

5.6
(1,3) courtesy of
\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
as guaranteed by
{\mathfrak{E}}
.
This yields

\lambda_{i}(M^{*})\geq(1-\alpha\cdot\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))})^{2}~,

and we use this to obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle T_{2}^{A}

\displaystyle\geq 3d\cdot\log(1-\alpha\cdot\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))})

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{9d}{2}\cdot\alpha\cdot\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}~.

The final inequality is due to
Lemma

A.3
.

T_{3}^{A}+T_{4}^{A}
:

Let
\psi=f\circ\nabla\phi^{*}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle D_{\psi}(\bar{x};\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\psi(\bar{x})-\psi(\bar{z})-\langle\nabla\psi(\bar{z}),\bar{x}-\bar{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\psi(\bar{x})-\psi(\bar{z})-\langle\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{z})\nabla f(\nabla\phi^{*}(\bar{z})),\bar{x}-\bar{z}\rangle~.

The Bregman commutator
\zeta_{\psi}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of
\psi

(
Wibisono et al. 2022
)
is defined as

\zeta_{\psi}(\bar{x},\bar{z})=\frac{1}{2}(D_{\psi}(\bar{x};\bar{z})-D_{\psi}(\bar{z};\bar{x}))~.

Substituting
\bar{x}=\nabla\phi(x)
and
\bar{z}=\nabla\phi(z)
gives

\displaystyle D_{\psi}(\nabla\phi(x);\nabla\phi(z))

\displaystyle=f(x)-f(z)-\langle\nabla f(z),\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\rangle_{\nabla^{2}\phi(z)^{-1}}=T_{3}^{A}

\displaystyle D_{\psi}(\nabla\phi(z);\nabla\phi(x))

\displaystyle=f(z)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),\nabla\phi(z)-\nabla\phi(x)\rangle_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}=-T_{4}^{A}~.

Consequently, the sum of
T_{3}^{A}
and
T_{4}^{A}
is

T_{3}^{A}+T_{4}^{A}=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\psi}(\nabla\phi(x);\nabla\phi(z))-D_{\psi}(\nabla\phi(z);\nabla\phi(x))\right)=\zeta_{\psi}(\nabla\phi(x),\nabla\phi(z))~.

For convenience, we use the shorthand notation
\bar{x}
and
\bar{z}
for
\nabla\phi(x)
and
\nabla\phi(z)
respectively.
Define
p_{t}=\bar{z}+t(\bar{x}-\bar{z})
for
t\in[0,1]
.
We work with the following identity from
Wibisono et al. 2022

\zeta_{\psi}(\bar{x},\bar{z})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-2t)\nabla^{2}\psi(p_{t})[\bar{x}-\bar{z},\bar{x}-\bar{z}]dt~.

The Hessian of
\psi
is

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi(p_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{3}\phi^{*}(p_{t})[\nabla f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))]+\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})\nabla^{2}f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{3}\phi^{*}(p_{t})[\nabla f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))]+\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-1}\nabla^{2}f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-1}

Consequently,

|\zeta_{\psi}(\bar{x},\bar{z})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\cdot|\nabla^{3}\phi^{*}(p_{t})[\bar{x}-\bar{z},\bar{x}-\bar{z},\nabla f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))]|dt}_{I_{1}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\cdot|\langle\bar{x}-\bar{z},\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-1}\nabla^{2}f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-1}[\bar{x}-\bar{z}]\rangle|dt}_{I_{2}}~.

We can bound
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
using properties of
\phi
,
f
and the fact that
\bar{x}=\nabla\phi(x),\bar{z}=\nabla\phi(z)
.
From
Proposition

5.6
(3), we know that
\phi^{*}
is self-concordant with parameter
\alpha
.
This implies

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\left(2\alpha\cdot\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})}^{2}\cdot\|\nabla f(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\left(2\alpha\cdot\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})}^{2}\cdot\beta\right)dt

The last inequality uses the fact that
f
is
\beta
-relatively Lipschitz with respect to
\phi
and by writing
\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})=\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-1}
from
Eq.

15
.

Since
f
is
\lambda
-relatively smooth with respect to
\phi
, we have for any
w\in\mathcal{K}
,

\nabla^{2}f(w)\preceq\lambda\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi(w)\Leftrightarrow\nabla^{2}\phi(w)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\nabla^{2}f(w)\nabla^{2}\phi(w)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\preceq\lambda\cdot I~.

With this, we can bound
I_{2}
as

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\cdot\lambda\cdot|\langle\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\bar{x}-\bar{z}),\nabla^{2}\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(p_{t}))^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\bar{x}-\bar{z})\rangle|dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\cdot\lambda\cdot\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})}^{2}dt~.

Collecting the bounds,

\displaystyle|\zeta_{\psi}(\bar{x},\bar{z})|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\alpha\cdot\beta+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\cdot\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\cdot\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})}dt

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\left(\alpha\cdot\beta+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\cdot\int_{0}^{1}|1-2t|\cdot\frac{\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})}}{(1-\alpha\cdot(1-t)\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})})^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\left(\alpha\cdot\beta+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\cdot\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\frac{100\cdot|1-2t|}{(7+3t)^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\alpha\cdot\beta+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\cdot\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))}~.

Recall that
\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
and
\|p_{t}-\bar{x}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})}=(1-t)\|\bar{z}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})}<\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
when
t\in[0,1]
.
In Step
(i)
, we use this fact to use
Proposition

5.6
(1) to get

\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(p_{t})\preceq\frac{1}{(1-\alpha\cdot(1-t)\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})})^{2}}\cdot\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})

Step
(ii)
uses the fact that
\|\bar{x}-\bar{z}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\bar{x})}\leq\nicefrac{{3}}{{10\cdot\alpha}}
and that
(1-x)^{-2}
is increasing for
x<1
.
The final inequality is an upper bound on the integral which can be computed as a closed form expression with value
\approx 0.73
.
This gives us the final bound

|\zeta_{\psi}(\nabla\phi(x),\nabla\phi(z))|\leq\gamma\cdot\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}~,

where
\gamma=\frac{\lambda}{2}+\alpha\cdot\beta
, which implies

T_{3}^{A}+T_{4}^{A}\geq-\gamma\cdot\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(z)\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}}^{2}~.

T_{5}^{A}
:

Since
f
is
\beta

T_{5}^{A}\geq-\frac{h\cdot\beta^{2}}{4}.

For convenience, we will use the shorthand notation

to denote

Collating these lower bounds, we get the net lower bound on

{\cal A}(x,z)\geq-\frac{3\bar{\alpha}}{2h}\cdot\ell_{x,z}^{3}-\frac{9d\cdot\bar{\alpha}}{2}\cdot\ell_{x,z}-\gamma\cdot\ell_{x,z}^{2}-\frac{h\cdot\beta^{2}}{4}~.

5.3.2.5
Choosing
t
in the conditional probability quantity

Under the event
{\mathfrak{E}}
, we can substitute the upper bound on
\ell_{x,z}
in the lower bound for
{\cal A}(x,z)
.

This gives us

\displaystyle{\cal A}(x,z)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{3\alpha}{2h}\cdot(h^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{9d\cdot\alpha}{2}\cdot(h^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma\cdot(h^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}+2h\cdot d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}h\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})-\frac{h\cdot\beta^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3\alpha\cdot\sqrt{h}}{2}(h\cdot\beta^{2}+2d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{9\sqrt{h}\cdot\alpha}{2}(h\cdot d^{2}\cdot\beta^{2}+2d^{3}\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma\cdot h\cdot(h\cdot\beta^{2}+2d\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}+2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{h}\cdot\beta\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon})-\frac{h\cdot\beta^{2}}{4}~.

Define the following constants.

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{24}\right)^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}};\quad C_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{72}\right)^{2};\quad C_{\varepsilon}^{(3)}=\frac{\varepsilon}{16}

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}^{(i,1)}=\frac{C_{\varepsilon}^{(i)}}{3}~;\quad C_{\varepsilon}^{(i,2)}=\frac{C_{\varepsilon}^{(i)}}{6\cdot{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}}~;\quad C_{\varepsilon}^{(i,3)}=\frac{C_{\varepsilon}^{(i)}}{6\sqrt{2}\cdot{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}}\qquad\forall~i\in[3]~.

When
\varepsilon<1
, note that all of these constants are strictly less than
1
, since
{\sf N}_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
and
{\sf I}_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
.
With this we define some limits on

.

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{\max}(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{\left(C_{\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}\right)^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}~,~\left(C_{\varepsilon}^{(1,2)}\right)^{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{d^{3}}~,~\left(C_{\varepsilon}^{(1,3)}\right)^{\nicefrac{{6}}{{5}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha^{\nicefrac{{4}}{{5}}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta^{\nicefrac{{6}}{{5}}}}\right\}~,

\displaystyle C^{(2)}_{\max}(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{\left(C_{\varepsilon}^{(2,1)}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}~,~\left(C_{\varepsilon}^{(2,2)}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{d^{3}}~,~\left(C_{\varepsilon}^{(2,3)}\right)^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha^{\nicefrac{{4}}{{3}}}}\right\}~,

\displaystyle C^{(3)}_{\max}(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{(C_{\varepsilon}^{(3,1)})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}~,~(C_{\varepsilon}^{(3,2)})\cdot\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma}~,~(C_{\varepsilon}^{(3,3)})\cdot\frac{1}{\beta^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}\right\}~,

\displaystyle C_{\max}^{(4)}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\cdot\beta^{2}}~.

6
Conclusion

To summarise, we introduce the Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
), and provide non-asymptotic mixing time guarantees for it.
This algorithm adds a Metropolis-Hastings accept-reject filter to the proposal Markov chain defined by a single step of the Mirror Langevin algorithm (
MLA
) which is the Euler-Maruyama discretisation of the Mirror Langevin dynamics (
MLD
), at each point in
\mathcal{K}
.
The resulting Markov chain is reversible with respect to the target distribution that we seek to sample from, unlike the Markov chain induced by
MLA
.

Our mixing time guarantees are strongly related to the maximum permissible stepsize that results in non-vanishing acceptance rates, and our current analysis shows that this scales as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{d^{3}}}
as
d
grows.
Motivated by our empirical studies, we believe that for structured domains like polytopes where
\phi
is chosen to be the log-barrier of the domain, a step size that scales as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{d^{\gamma}}}
for
\gamma<3
can lead to non-vanishing acceptance rates for
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
, and consequently yield better mixing time guarantees.
Another question that we leave for the future is to check if the relative Lipschitz continuity assumption over
f
can be relaxed or removed altogether for the mixing time analysis of
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
, especially when used in conjuction with relative convexity and smoothness of
f
.
This is motivated by the analysis in
Li et al. 2022a
, which does not employ such a condition to derive mixing time guarantees for
MLA
.

More conceptually,
1Metropolis-adjusted Mirror Langevin algorithm (MAMLA)
also strongly uses the dual form of
MLD
i.e., by using
MLA
which is a discretisation of the SDE in the dual space.
Using Itô’s lemma, the equivalent SDE in the primal space is given by
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022a
)

dX_{t}=(\nabla\cdot\{\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})^{-1}\}-\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})^{-1}\nabla f(X_{t}))dt+\sqrt{2\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})^{-1}}dB_{t}~.

While an algorithm based on the discretision of the primal SDE by itself will not guarantee feasible iterates, the Metropolis-Hastings filter could be used to ensure that (1) the iterates are feasible at all times, and (2) the resulting Markov chain is reversible with respect to the target distribution.
Notably, the primal space SDE introduces a third-order quantity, specifically
\nabla\cdot\{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)^{-1}\}
which might be hard to compute.
However, owing to the fact that the Metropolis-adjustment results in a Markov chain that is reversible with respect to the target, it would be instructive to analyse a proposal that does not contain the aforementioned third order expression in it.

Element-wise, this is equivalent to stating

y_{i}=-\frac{1}{x_{i}}+\frac{1}{1-\bm{1}^{\top}x}\qquad\forall~i\in[d]~.

The goal is to recover
x
given some real vector
y
.
Let
C=1-\bm{1}^{\top}x
.
Then,

y_{i}=-\frac{1}{x_{i}}+\frac{1}{C}\Leftrightarrow x_{i}=\frac{C}{1-Cy_{i}}~.

(19)

Consequently,
C
must satisfy

C=1-\bm{1}^{\top}x=1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{C}{1-Cy_{i}}\Leftrightarrow f(C)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}C+C\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{C}{1-Cy_{i}}-1=0~.

By the domain constraints,
C\in(0,1)
(equality is attained when
x
lies on the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
).

Note the following properties of
f
:

1.

the asymptotes of
f
are given by
y_{1}^{-1},\ldots,y_{d}^{-1}
with
\lim\limits_{C\to y_{i}^{+}}f(C)=-\infty
and
\lim\limits_{C\to y_{i}^{-}}f(C)=\infty
.

2.

f
is increasing between two consecutive asymptotes, and

3.

f(0)=-1
.

We identify ranges to perform binary search on, and this depends on the sign of
y_{(1)}=\max_{j\in[d]}y_{j}
.

y_{(1)}\leq 0
:

in this case, since there are no positive asymptotes,
f
must be increasing in the domain
(0,\infty)
.
Since
f(0)=-1
, the root
C^{\star}
could lie anywhere between
(0,1)
, and therefore the search bounds as
(0,1)
.

y_{(1)}>0
:

in this case, there is at least one positive asymptote.
For the sake of contradiction, let
C^{\star}>y_{(1)}^{-1}
.
Then for the
j
such that
y_{j}=y_{(1)}
, we have

y_{j}=-\frac{1}{x_{j}}+\frac{1}{C^{\star}}\Rightarrow\frac{1}{x_{j}}=\frac{1}{C^{\star}}-y_{j}<y_{j}-y_{j}<0~.

which is a contradiction as
x_{j}\geq 0
.
Therefore, the root
C^{\star}
must be at most
\min\{1,y_{(1)}^{-1}\}
.
This gives the search bounds
\left(0,\min\{1,y_{(1)}^{-1}\}\right)
.

Having identified the bounds, one can use a binary search method to identify the root.
With
C^{\star}
, the corresponding
x
can be obtained from
Eq.

19
.
