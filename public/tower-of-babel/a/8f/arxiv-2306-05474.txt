The Effective Field Theory of Large-Scale Structure and Multi-tracer II: redshift space and realistic tracers
Rodrigo Voivodic

Abstract
We extend the multi-tracer (MT) formalism of the effective field theory of large-scale structure to redshift space, comparing the results of MT to a single-tracer analysis when extracting cosmological parameters from simulations. We used a sub-halo abundance matching method to obtain more realistic multi-tracer galaxy catalogs constructed from N-body simulations. Considering different values for the sample shot noise and volume, we show that the MT error bars on A s A_{s} , ω cdm \omega_{\rm cdm} , and h h in a full-shape analysis are approximately 50 % 50\% smaller relative to ST. We find that cosmological and bias coefficients from MT are less degenerate, indicating that the MT parameter basis is more orthogonal. We conclude that using MT combined with perturbation theory is a robust and competitive way to accommodate the information present in the mildly non-linear scales.

2.3
Combining tracer bias coefficients

Since the bias and stochastic parameters encapsulate information from different fields in the cases of MT and ST, it does not make sense to compare them directly.
When comparing ST and MT, it is useful to define the
effective tracer

[
47
]
.
This
effective tracer
is a way to provide a direct (and fair) comparison between the parameters extracted in ST and MT. We define the effective tracer overdensity as

\delta^{\rm eff}=\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\sum_{i}\bar{n}^{i}\delta^{i}\;,

(2.26)

where
\bar{n}=\sum_{i}\bar{n}^{i}
. Therefore, the effective parameters when considering two tracers are a weighted mean of each tracer coefficient

\displaystyle b^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\left(\bar{n}^{A}b^{\mathrm{A}}_{\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\right]}+\bar{n}^{B}b^{\mathrm{B}}_{\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\right]}\right)\,,

(2.27)

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\left[\bar{n}^{A}c^{AA}+\bar{n}^{B}c^{BB}+2\sqrt{\bar{n}^{A}\bar{n}^{B}}c^{AB}\right]\,.

(2.28)

Unless stated otherwise, we refer to the free coefficients of the effective tracer when showing the MT bias and stochastic parameters in Sec.
4
.

3.2
Covariance

We calculate in this section the covariance matrix for multi-tracer in redshift space. This calculation is based on
[
66
]
and in
[
35
]
, which calculates the covariance for the angular spectrum.
4
4

4

We acknowledge and are very thankful to Enea di Dio for providing his derivation for the covariance matrix for multi-tracer in redshift space.

Defining the power spectrum of two (discrete) fields
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{B}
with its cross shot noise included

\langle\delta_{A}(\boldsymbol{k})\delta_{B}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})P^{AB}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,

(3.1)

we can calculate the covariance by trivially generalizing the real space covariance of
[
67
,
68
]
to multi-tracer
[
47
]

{\rm Cov}\left[P^{AB}(\boldsymbol{k}),P^{CD}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\right]=\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{V_{s}}\left[\delta_{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})P^{AD}(\boldsymbol{k})P^{BC}(\boldsymbol{k})+\delta_{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})P^{AC}(\boldsymbol{k})P^{BD}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]\,,

(3.2)

where
V_{s}
is the sample volume. Notice that we kept only the Gaussian contribution (
PP
), neglecting the trispectrum part
[
68
,
69
]
. Since we are interested in scales
k<0.2\,h/
Mpc (and more concretely focusing on
k\sim 0.14\,h/
Mpc), the non-Gaussian contribution to the covariance is very small
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
. Using the projection (
2.12
), we can now write down the covariance for the projected multipoles as

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left[P^{AB}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{k}),P^{CD}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(2\ell+1\right)\left(2\ell^{\prime}+1\right)}{4V_{s}}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(k-k^{\prime}\right)}{2\pi k^{2}}\int d\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{k}}}d\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\mu_{k}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\mu_{k^{\prime}}\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left[P^{AC}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)P^{BD}\left(-\mathbf{k}\right)\delta_{D}\left(\hat{\mathbf{k}}-\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\right)+P^{AD}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)P^{BC}\left(-\mathbf{k}\right)\delta_{D}\left(\hat{\mathbf{k}}+\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(2\ell+1\right)\left(2\ell^{\prime}+1\right)}{4V_{s}}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(k-k^{\prime}\right)}{k^{2}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\int d\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{1}}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{2}}\left(-\mu\right)

\displaystyle\left[P_{\ell_{1}}^{AC}\left(k\right)P^{BD}_{\ell_{2}}\left(k\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\mu\right)+P^{AD}_{\ell_{1}}\left(k\right)P^{BC}_{\ell_{2}}\left(k\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}\left(-\mu\right)\right]\,,

where
\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{k}}}
is the integral over the solid angle formed w.r.t the
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
direction. Again, we only kept the
PP
contribution. We can simplify the Legendre polynomial products by writing

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{1}}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{2}}\left(\mu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{3}}\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{3}}\left(\mu\right)\,,

where the
2\times 3
matrices are the Wigner 3-j symbols. Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\int d\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{1}}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{2}}\left(-\mu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(-1\right)^{\ell_{2}}\sum_{\ell_{3}}\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell&\ell^{\prime}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\int d\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}\left(-\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{1}}\left(\mu\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell_{2}}\left(-\mu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(-1\right)^{\ell^{\prime}+\ell_{2}}\sum_{\ell_{3}}\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell&\ell^{\prime}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}\,,

which we can use to write the final covariance for MT in redshift space

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left[P^{AB}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{k}),P^{CD}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(2\ell+1\right)\left(2\ell^{\prime}+1\right)}{2V_{s}}\frac{\delta_{D}\left(k-k^{\prime}\right)}{k^{2}}

(3.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(-1\right)^{\ell_{2}}\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell&\ell^{\prime}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\left[P_{\ell_{1}}^{AC}\left(k\right)P_{\ell_{2}}^{BD}\left(k\right)+\left(-1\right)^{\ell^{\prime}}P_{\ell_{1}}^{AD}\left(k\right)P_{\ell_{2}}^{BC}\left(k\right)\right]\,.

Notice that, since the simulations described in Sec.
3.1
were performed with the
paired & fixed
method
[
60
]
, we can (artifitially) reescale the volume
V_{s}
of the sample considered in the covariance
[

]

4
Results

In this section we explain the main results of the paper. We start by discussing the relevance of the higher-in-
\mu
coefficients in the counter-terms to parametrize the FoG for MT and the importance of the cross-stochastic coefficients. Later, we consider the behaviour of MT for different shot noises and box volumes. In the last part, we explain and quantify the benefits of MT analysis compared to ST. We show results for
z=0
, different box sizes and sample shot noise.

We now move to estimate and understand the improvements of MT compared to ST.
We start by comparing on the left panel of Fig.

In

). Throughout this section, we aim to provide more arguments to show that MT can be a way to extract more powerful constraints in redshift space.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we extended the results of
[
47
]
to redshift space, showing that the multi-tracer is still a relevant tool not only to beat the cosmic variance on linear scales but to break degeneracies between free parameters on the mildly non-linear regime. We also improved the previous analysis by considering more realistic galaxies generated by the state-of-the-art SHAMe method and a SFR tracer split.

In addition, we found that the FoG effects can be starker for different populations when doing the MT tracer splitting. We concluded that the higher-in-
\mu
parameters in the EFT counter-terms are necessary to absorb those effects and extract unbiased cosmological parameters in an MT analysis. In addition, the relevance of those higher-in-
\mu
terms indicate that it may be interesting to consider in the future the clustering wedges statistics from
[
77
,
16
]
for MT. Furthermore, we saw that the cross-stochastic terms did not deteriorate the MT improvement, in agreement with
[
47
]
.

We summarize in Fig.
8
the main results of this work, comparing MT error bars relative to ST for all bias and cosmological parameters for
\bar{n}=\bar{n}_{\rm high}
and different values of
k_{\rm max}
. We see that the error bars for MT are, on average,
50\%
smaller for
A_{s}
,
h
, and
\omega_{\rm cdm}
. For the bias parameters, the error bars for MT are between 1.5 to 3 times better, with this improvement being smaller for
b_{1}
and larger for
b_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}
and
b_{\Gamma_{3}}
. Those improvements are consistent with the conclusions of the first paper
[
47
]
, with the difference that we are now able to break the degeneracy between
A_{s}
and
b_{1}
in redshift space.

:
The multi-tracer (MT) improvements relative to the single-tracer (ST) scenario in a full-shape power-spectrum analysis. We display the size of the error bars of MT compared to ST for different values of

A common criticism to the EFTofLSS framework relies on a large number of free parameters present in the theory. This leads to a fundamental question:
when is it reasonable and justified to add new free parameters to an analysis?
The success of EFTofLSS when providing competitive cosmological constraints
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
shows that this criticism of EFT does
not
hold. The extra parameters added are
physical
and are exactly what guarantees the robustness of the theory by parametrizing the UV regime in a
self-controlled
manner. Those parameters are (relatively) well measured in data, which is one of the reasons why FS provides competitive constraints. In general, a
n
-tracer analysis will have about
n
-times more coefficients within the EFTofLSS framework. Again, we found that for the case considered in this work, those extra parameters do
not
jeopardize the constraints, but improve them. Despite sounding counter-intuitive to have a more constraining theory with more free parameters, the presence of the cross power spectrum between the tracers reduces the correlation between the EFT and the cosmological coefficients, which ultimately induce tighter constraints on both set of parameters. Those parameters are better determined for MT (Fig.
6
) and present fewer degeneracies (Fig.
7
). In contrast, part of the information encoded in intermediate scales for each sub-tracer would be smoothed out when doing a single-tracer analysis (see the left panel of Fig.
1
).

To conclude, MT provides an exciting perspective to maximize the information from galaxy surveys. There are several ways to split a sample of galaxies into sub-samples, and the MT gains might depend on how it is performed. In this work, we focused on a SFR split. It would be interesting in future work to explore other ways to split the tracers, such as considering the local environment density
[
78
]
. Furthermore, this work is a fundamental step before applying MT to data, using simulations to test for the first time the EFTofLSS pipeline for MT in redshift space.
