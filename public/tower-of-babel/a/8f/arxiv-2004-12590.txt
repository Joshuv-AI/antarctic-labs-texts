In-Place Bijective Burrows-Wheeler Transforms
Dominik Köppl, Daiki Hashimoto, Diptarama Hendrian, Ayumi Shinohara

Abstract
One of the most well-known variants of the Burrows-Wheeler transform (BWT) [Burrows and Wheeler, 1994] is the bijective BWT (BBWT) [Gil and Scott, arXiv 2012], which applies the extended BWT (EBWT) [Mantaci et al., TCS 2007] to the multiset of Lyndon factors of a given text. Since the EBWT is invertible, the BBWT is a bijective transform in the sense that the inverse image of the EBWT restores this multiset of Lyndon factors such that the original text can be obtained by sorting these factors in non-increasing order. In this paper, we present algorithms constructing or inverting the BBWT in-place using quadratic time. We also present conversions from the BBWT to the BWT, or vice versa, either (a) in-place using quadratic time, or (b) in the run-length compressed setting using 𝒪 ( n lg r / lg lg r ) \mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg r/\lg\lg r) time with 𝒪 ( r lg n ) \mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r\lg n) bits of words, where r r is the sum of character runs in the BWT and the BBWT.

Introduction

The
Burrows-Wheeler transform
(BWT)
[
6
]
is one of the most favored options
both for (a) compressing and (b) indexing data sets.
On the one hand, compression programs like
bzip2
apply the BWT to achieve high compression rates.
For that, they leverage the effect that the BWT built on repetitive data tends to have long character runs,
which can be compressed by run-length compression, i.e., representing a substring of
\ell

a
’s by the tuple
(\texttt{a},\ell)
.
On the other hand, self-indexing data structures like the FM-index
[
11
]
enhance the BWT to a full-text self-index.
A combined approach of both compression and indexing is the run-length compressed FM-index
[
21
]
,
representing a BWT with
r_{\mathsf{BWT}}
character runs, i.e., maximal repetitions of a character, run-length compressed in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r_{\mathsf{BWT}}\lg n)
bits.
This representation can be computed directly in run-length compressed space thanks to Policriti and Prezza
[
30
]
.
The BWT and its run-length compressed representation have been intensively studied during the past decades (e.g.,
[
12
,
1
,
14
]
and the references therein).
Contrary to that, a variant, called the
bijective
BWT (BBWT)
[
16
]
, is far from being well-studied despite its mathematically appealing characteristics
1
1

The BBWT is a bijection between strings without the need of an artificial delimiter needed, e.g., to invert the BWT.
.
As a matter of fact, we are only aware of one index data structure based on the BBWT
[
3
]
and of two non-trivial construction algorithms
[
5
,
2
]
of the (uncompressed) BBWT, both with the need of additional data structures.

In this article, we shed more light on the connection between the BWT and the BBWT by quadratic time in-place conversion algorithms in
\cref
secInPlaceConversions constructing the BWT from the BBWT, or vice versa.
We can also perform these conversions in the run-length compressed setting
in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg r/\lg\lg r)
time with space linear to the number of the character runs (cf.
\cref
secRunLengthCompressed and
\cref
thmRunLength),
where
r
is the sum of character runs in the BWT and the BBWT.

2
Related Work

Given a text
T
of length
n
,
the BWT of
T
is the string obtained by assigning
\mathsf{BWT}[i]
to the character preceding the
i
-th lexicographically smallest suffix of
T
(or the last character of
T
if this suffix is the text itself).
By this definition, we can construct the BWT with any suffix array
[
22
]
construction algorithm.
However, storing the suffix array inherently needs
n\lg n
bits of space.
Crochemore et al.
[
9
]
tackled this space problem with an in-place algorithm constructing the BWT in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n^{2})
online on the reversed text
by simulating queries on a dynamic wavelet tree
[
17
]
that would be built on the (growing) BWT.
They also gave an algorithm for restoring the text in-place in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n^{2+\epsilon})
time.

In the run-length compressed setting,
Policriti and Prezza
[
30
]
can compute the run-length compressed BWT having
r_{\mathsf{BWT}}
character runs
in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg r_{\mathsf{BWT}})
time while using
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r_{\mathsf{BWT}}\lg n)
bits of space.
They additionally presented an adaption of the wavelet tree on run-length compressed texts,
yielding a representation using
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r_{\mathsf{BWT}}\lg n)
bits of space with
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(\lg r_{\mathsf{BWT}})
query and update time.
Finally, practical improvements of the run-length compressed BWT construction were considered by Ohno et al.
[
29
]
.

The BBWT is the string obtained by assigning
\mathsf{BBWT}[i]
to the last character of the
i
-th smallest string
in the list of all conjugates of the factors of the Lyndon factorization sorted with respect to the
\prec_{\omega}
order
[
23
, Def. 4]
.
Bannai et al.
[
2
]
recently revealed a connection between the bijective BWT and suffix sorting by
presenting an
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n)
time BBWT construction algorithm based on SAIS
[
28
]
.
With dynamic data structures like a dynamic wavelet tree
[
27
]
,
Bonomo et al.
[
5
]
could devise an algorithm computing the BBWT in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg n/\lg\lg n)
time.
With nearly the same techniques, Mantaci et al.
[
24
]
presented an algorithm computing the BWT (and simultaneously the suffix array if needed)
from the Lyndon factorization.
All these construction algorithms need however data structures taking
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg n)
bits of space.
However, the latter two (i.e.,
[
5
]
and
[
24
]
) can work in-place by simulating the
\mathsf{LF}
mapping (cf.
\cref
secSearchSteps,secRewinding),
which we focus on in
\cref
secConstructBWTC.

3
Preliminaries

Our computational model is the word RAM model with word size
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathup{\Omega}\mathopen{}(\lg n)
.
Accessing a word costs
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(1)
time.
An algorithm is called
in-place
if it uses, besides a rewriteable input, only
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(\lg n)
bits of working space.
We write
[{\textrm{{b}}}(I)\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}{\textrm{{e}}}(I)]=I
for an interval
I
of natural numbers.

3.1
Strings

Let
\Sigma
denote an integer alphabet of size
\sigma
with
\sigma=n^{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(1)}
.
We call an element
T\in\Sigma^{*}
a
string
.
Its length is denoted by
|T|
.
Given an integer
j\in[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}|T|]
, we access the
j
-th character of
T
with
T[j]
.
Concatenating a string
T\in\Sigma^{*}

k
times is abbreviated by
T^{k}
.
A string
T
is called
primitive
if there is no string
S\in\Sigma^{+}
with
T=S^{k}
for an integer
k
with
k\geq 2
.

When
T
is represented by the concatenation of
X,Y,Z\in\Sigma^{*}
, i.e.,
T=\textit{XYZ}
,
then
X
,
Y
and
Z
are called a
prefix
,
substring
and
suffix
of
T
, respectively;
the prefix
X
, substring
Y
, or suffix
Z
is called
proper
if
X\neq T
,
Y\neq T
, or
Z\neq T
, respectively.
For two integers
i,j
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq|T|
, let
T[i\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}j]
denote
the substring of
T
that begins at position
i
and ends at position
j
in
T
.
If
i>j
, then
T[i\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}j]
is the empty string.
In particular, the suffix starting at position
j
of
T
is called the
j
-th suffix
of
T
, and denoted with
T[j\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}]
.
An occurrence of a substring
S
in
T
is treated as a sub-interval of
[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}|T|]
such that
S=T[{\textrm{{b}}}(S)\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}{\textrm{{e}}}(S)]
.
The
longest common prefix (LCP)
of two strings

and
T
is the longest string that is a prefix of both
S
and
T
.

Orders on Strings.

We denote the
lexicographic order
with
\prec_{\textup{lex}}{}
.
Given two strings
S
and
T
,
S\prec_{\textup{lex}}T
if
S
is a prefix of
T
or there exists an integer
\ell
with

1\leq\ell\leq\min(|S|,|T|)
such that
S[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}\ell-1]=T[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}\ell-1]
and
S[\ell]<T[\ell]
.
Next we define the
\prec_{\omega}{}
order
of strings, which is based on the lexicographic order of infinite strings:
We write
S\prec_{\omega}{}T
if the infinite concatenation
S^{\omega}:={SSS}\cdots
is lexicographically smaller than

T^{\omega}:={TTT}\cdots
.
For instance,
\texttt{ab}\prec_{\textup{lex}}\texttt{aba}
but
\texttt{aba}\prec_{\omega}\texttt{ab}
.

Rank and Select Queries.

Given a string
T\in\Sigma^{*}
,
a character
\texttt{c}\in\Sigma
, and an integer
j
,
the
rank
query
T.{\textrm{{rank}}}_{\texttt{c}}(j)
counts the occurrences of
c
in
T[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}j]
, and
the
select
query
T.{\textrm{{select}}}_{\texttt{c}}(j)
gives the position of the
j
-th
c
in
T
.
We stipulate that
{\textrm{{rank}}}_{\texttt{c}}(0)={\textrm{{select}}}_{\texttt{c}}(0)=0
.
A
wavelet tree
is a data structure supporting rank and select queries.

3.2
Lyndon Words

Given a string
T=T[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}n]
, its
i
-th
conjugate

{\textrm{{conj}}}_{i}(T)
is defined as
T[i+1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}n]T[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}i]
for an integer
i\in[0\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}n-1]
.
We say that
T
and all of its conjugates belong to the
conjugate class

{\textrm{{conj}}}(T):=\{{\textrm{{conj}}}_{0}(T),\ldots,{\textrm{{conj}}}_{n-1}(T)\}
.
If a conjugate class contains
exactly
one conjugate that is lexicographically smaller than all other conjugates,
then this conjugate is called a
Lyndon word

[
20
]
.
Equivalently, a string
T
is said to be a Lyndon word if and only if
T\prec_{\textup{lex}}S
for every proper suffix
S
of
T

[
10
, Prop. 1.2]
.

The
Lyndon factorization

[
8
]
of
T\in\Sigma^{+}

is the factorization of
T
into a sequence of lexicographically non-increasing Lyndon words
T_{1}\cdots T_{t}
,
where (a) each
T_{x}\in\Sigma^{+}
is a Lyndon word for
x\in[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}t]
, and (b)
T_{x}\succeq_{\textup{lex}}T_{x+1}
for each
x\in[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}t)
.
Each Lyndon word
T_{x}
is called a
Lyndon factor
.

Lemma 1

(
[
10
, Algo. 2.1]
)
.

Given a string
T
of length
n
,
there is an algorithm that outputs
the Lyndon factors
T_{1},\ldots,T_{t}
one by one in increasing order in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n)
total time
while keeping only a constant number of pointers to positions in
T
that (a) can move one position forward at one time or (b) can be set to the position of another pointer.

Proof.

The algorithm of Duval uses three variables
i
,
j
, and
k
(cf.
\cref
algoDuval in the appendix) pointing to text positions.

k
is the ending position of the previously computed Lyndon factor (or zero at the beginning).
On each step,
j\in[k+2\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}n]
is incremented by one, while
i
is either incremented by one or reset to
k+1
,
as long as
T[k+1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}i-1]=T[j-(i-k)\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}j-1]
is a prefix of a Lyndon word starting at
T[k+1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}]
.
If
T[k+1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}i]
is no longer such a prefix,
then
T[k+1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}i-1]
is either a Lyndon factor or a repetition of Lyndon factors, each of length
j-i
.
In total, we visit at most
2n
characters by incrementing the text positions
i
,
j
, and
k
.
∎

For what follows, we fix a string
T[1\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}n]
over an alphabet

.
We use the string

Its Lyndon factors are

3.3
Burrows-Wheeler Transforms

We denote the bijective BWT of
T
by
\mathsf{BBWT}
, where
\mathsf{BBWT}[i]
is the last character of the
i
-th string in the list

A property of

used in this paper as a starting point for an inversion algorithm is the following:

Lemma 2

(
[
5

)
.

\mathsf{BBWT}[1]=T[n]

Proof.

There is no conjugate of a Lyndon factor that is smaller than the smallest Lyndon factor

since

T_{t}\preceq_{\textup{lex}}T_{x}\prec_{\textup{lex}}T_{x}[j\mathbin{{.}\,{.}}{}]

and every

Therefore,
T_{t}
is the smallest string among all conjugates of all Lyndon factors.
Hence,
\mathsf{BBWT}[1]
is the last character of

, which is

∎

The BWT of

, called in the following

\texttt{\$}T

Originally, the BWT is defined by reading the last characters of all cyclic rotations of

We further write

Since

(and analogously

Backward and Forward Steps

Having the location of
T[i]
in
\mathsf{BWT}
,
we can compute

\mathsf{FL}[i]:=\mathsf{BWT}.{\textrm{{select}}}_{\mathsf{F}[i]}(\mathsf{F}.{\textrm{{rank}}}_{\mathsf{F}[i]}(i)),

(1)

where

3.5
Steps in the Bijective BWT

The major difference to the BWT is that the LF mapping of the BBWT can contain multiple cycles, meaning
that
\mathsf{LF}
(or
\mathsf{FL}
) recursively applied to a
\mathsf{BBWT}
position would result
in searching circular (more precisely, the search stays within the same Lyndon factor).
This is because
\mathsf{BBWT}
is the extended BWT
[
23
, Thm. 20 and Remark 12]
applied to the multiset of Lyndon factors
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{t}\}
.
This fact was exploited for circular pattern matching
[
19
]
, but is not of interest here.

Instead, we follow the analysis of the so-called
rewindings

[
3
, Sect. 3]
:
Remembering that we store the last character of all conjugates of all Lyndon factors in
\mathsf{BBWT}
,
we observe that the entries in
\mathsf{BBWT}
representing the Lyndon factors (i.e., the last characters of the Lyndon factors)
are in sorted order (starting with
T_{t}[|T_{t}|]
and ending with
T_{1}[|T_{1}|]
).
That is because the lexicographic order and the
\prec_{\omega}
order are the same for Lyndon words
[
5
, Thm. 8]
.
Applying the backward step at such an entry results in a rewinding, i.e.,
we can move from the beginning of a Lyndon factor
T_{x}
(represented by
T_{x}[|T_{x}|]
in
\mathsf{BBWT}
)
to the end of
T_{x}

Theorem 3

4.1
Run-length Compressed Wavelet Trees

Given a run-length compressed string

It consists of (1) a dynamic wavelet tree maintaining the starting characters of each character run and (2) a dynamic Fenwick tree maintaining the

It can be accelerated to

1.

2.

on
r

(\lg n)
-bit numbers uses
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r\lg n)

Corollary 4

[

)

We can construct the

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg r_{\mathsf{BWT}}/\lg\lg r_{\mathsf{BWT}})

Open Problems

Our algorithm of
\cref
secInvertBBWT converts
\mathsf{BBWT}
to
\mathsf{BWT}
, Lyndon factor by Lyndon factor.
It would be interesting to find another conversion that works character-wise.
Here, our inversion algorithm extracts a Lyndon factor in text order from
\mathsf{BBWT}
, while the used BWT construction algorithm parses the text in reverse text order.

Crochemore et al.
[
9
, Sect. 4]
proposed a space and time trade-off algorithm based on their in-place techniques computing or inverting
\mathsf{BWT}
.
We are positive that it should be possible to adapt their techniques for computing or inverting
\mathsf{BBWT}
or
\mathsf{BWT}^{\circ}
with a trade-off parameter.

From the combinatorial perspective, we question whether the number of distinct Lyndon words of
T
is bounded by the runs in
\mathsf{BBWT}
.
If we can affirm this question, it would be possible to adapt the
\mathsf{BBWT}
based index data structure
[
3
]
for
\mathsf{RLBBWT}
using
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r_{\mathsf{BBWT}}{}\lg n)
bits of space
because this solution needs a bit vector with rank and select support marking the positions in
\mathsf{BBWT}
corresponding to the distinct Lyndon factors.
If this number is at most the number of runs
r_{\mathsf{BBWT}}
,
then we can store this bit vector entropy-compressed in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(r\lg n)
bits when
r_{\mathsf{BBWT}}=\mathop{}\mathopen{}o\mathopen{}(n)
since

nH_{0}(r)=n\lg(n/(n-r))+r\lg((n-r)/r)\leq n\lg r\Leftrightarrow r\lg((n-r)/r)\leq n\lg(r(n-r)/n)
for
r=r_{\mathsf{BBWT}}
.

Speaking of
\mathsf{RLBBWT}
, we wonder whether we can construct
\mathsf{RLBBWT}
online in run-length compressed space similar to
\cref
corTextToRLBWT.
With the run-length compressed wavelet tree, the algorithm of Bonomo et al.
[
5
, Thm. 17]
works in
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n\lg r_{\mathsf{BBWT}}/\lg\lg r_{\mathsf{BBWT}})

Complementary to the study of

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(n^{2})

Lemma 7
.

Given that

is the number of character runs in the run-length compressed text

,
