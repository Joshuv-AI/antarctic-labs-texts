Continuous Wavelet Frames on the Sphere: The Group-Theoretic Approach Revisited
S. Dahlke, F. De Mari, E. De Vito, M. Hansen, M. Hasannasab, M. Quellmalz, G. Steidl, G. Teschke

Abstract
In [ 3 ] , Antoine and Vandergheynst propose a group-theoretic approach to continuous wavelet frames on the sphere. The frame is constructed from a single so-called admissible function by applying the unitary operators associated to a representation of the Lorentz group, which is square-integrable modulo the nilpotent factor of the Iwasawa decomposition. We prove necessary and sufficient conditions for functions on the sphere, which ensure that the corresponding system is a frame. We strengthen a similar result in [ 3 ] by providing a complete and detailed proof.



1
Introduction

In the recent twenty years, continuous and discrete wavelet frames on the sphere were examined
with different approaches and in particular with various techniques for imitating a dilation on the sphere.
The methods range from extending the discrete wavelet scheme based on multiresolution to spheres
[
6
,
14
]
, to
Fourier analytic ones with appropriately weighted sums of spherical harmonics

[
11
,
23
,
24
,
21
]
, lifted spherical wavelets
[
26
]
,
and constructions on tangent bundles of the sphere

[
7
]
.
Further, a spherical wavelet transform based on an integral transform
with a singular kernel was proposed
in
[
17
]
.
For more recent papers following the approximate identities idea, resp. using singular integrals,
we refer to
[
4
,
5
,
18
]
.
In connection with coorbit spaces spherical wavelets were considered in

[
8
]
.
Various applications of spherical wavelet frames can be found,
e.g., in
[
12
,
22
,
25
]
.

In
[
3
]
Antoine and Vandergheynst proposed a
group-theoretic approach that generalizes that of
Grossmann et al.
[
15
,
16
]
from locally compact groups to the homogeneous space
given by a quotient of the Lorentz group.
For the 2-sphere, the construction and analysis of continuous wavelet frames was outlined in
[
3
]

and for general
n
-spheres in
[
2
]
.
In this paper, we exclusively focus on this construction and
our aim consists in adding missing information
to the analysis in
[
3
]
, which in our opinion appears to be important and non-trivial.
We will clearly indicate the differences and additions we made by corresponding remarks.
In particular, it appears that finding an admissible function such that the
corresponding function system indeed has upper and lower frame bounds
is quite involved. We consider these results as the main contribution of our paper.

The outline of this paper is as follows.
In Section
2
,
we set the stage by giving the formal group-theoretic definition of wavelets on the sphere.
Our main result is stated in Section
3
.
Section
4

contains a proof of general necessary and sufficient conditions for a
function to be admissible.
These conditions rely on a sequence of numbers
that has to be bounded from above in order to get an upper frame bound and
uniformly bounded from below away from zero to have a lower frame bound.
While it is not hard to deduce these general sequence conditions, and indeed they were already provided in
[
3
]
,
the main work consists in finding functions which fulfill these conditions.
This is the content of the subsequent sections.
In Section
5
, we derive necessary and sufficient conditions on functions such that an
upper frame bound for the corresponding continuous frame can be ensured.
To this end, we explore the asymptotic behavior of zonal projections.
In Section
6
, we adopt the nice intuition of Antoine and Vandergheynst to
switch to stereographic projections.
We show that the derived conditions on the admissible function can be rewritten
as moment conditions on the isometrically transferred
admissible function to the plane.
Finally, the lower frame bound is treated in Section
7
.
As known from frame theory, conditions for lower frame bounds are typically
more complicated than the Bessel condition, which is also the case for our setting.

Preliminaries

In this section, we first provide the basic notation on square integrable group representations and
continuous wavelet frames in general and subsequently specify it to our spherical setting.
For more information, we refer to
[
1
,
13
]
.

2.1
Continuous wavelet frames on homogeneous spaces

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and fix
a locally compact space
X
equipped with a Radon-measure
\nu
.
A family
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in X}\subset H
is called a
continuous frame
,
if for every
\phi\in H
the map
x\mapsto\langle\phi,\eta_{x}\rangle
is measurable and
there exist constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
such that

c\|\phi\|^{2}\leq\int_{X}|\langle\phi,\eta_{x}\rangle|^{2}\,d\nu(x)\leq C\|\phi\|^{2}

(1)

for all
\phi\in H
.
For a continuous frame
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in X}
, the corresponding

frame operator

A
is given
in its weak formulation by

\langle A\phi,\psi\rangle=\int_{X}\langle\phi,\eta_{x}\rangle\langle\eta_{x},\psi\rangle\,d\nu(x),\quad\phi,\psi\in H\,,

and condition (
1
) is equivalent to the fact that the frame
operator
A
is bounded and boundedly invertible.

The prototype of a continuous frame is given by a square-integrable
representation of a locally compact group
G
with left Haar measure

\mu
. Let
U
be a
continuous unitary representation
of
G

in
H
,
i.e.

U
is a mapping from

G
into the space
\mathcal{U}(H)
of unitary operators on
H
fulfilling

U(gg^{\prime})=U(g)U(g^{\prime})
for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
, and
the function
g\mapsto\langle\phi,U(g)\psi\rangle
must be continuous for every
\phi,\psi\in H
.
In what follows, we will only consider continuous representations and
omit the word ’continuous’ in this context.
An irreducible representation is
called
square integrable
,
if there exists a vector
\eta\in H\setminus\{0\}
such that

\int_{G}\bigl|\langle\eta,U(g)\eta\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\mu(g)<\infty.

(2)

In this case, the vector
\eta
is called
admissible
.
With the choice
\nu=\mu
, general
results from representation theory

[
9
]
guarantee that
the family
\{U(g)\eta\}_{g\in G}
is a
continuous frame, and the
corresponding frame operator
A_{\eta}
with

\langle A_{\eta}\phi,\psi\rangle=\int_{G}\langle\phi,U(g)\eta\rangle\langle U(g)\eta,\psi\rangle\,d\mu(g),\quad\phi,\psi\in H,

(3)

is in fact a multiple of the identity.

In this paper, we are concerned with a homogeneous space
X=G/N
,
where
N
is a closed subgroup of
G

instead of the whole group. We fix
on
X
a strongly quasi-invariant
measure
\nu
, see (
7
) and

[
10
]
for the
general theory. Since in general a representation is not directly defined on
X
,
we need to introduce a (measurable) section
\sigma:X\rightarrow G
, i.e. a map which for each
x\in X
assigns an element
\sigma(x)\in G
such that
\sigma(x)
belongs to the coset
x
.
We then call a representation
U
of
G
in
H

square-integrable modulo

(N,\sigma)
, if there exists
a vector
\eta\in H\backslash\{0\}
such that
the family

\{U(\sigma(x))\eta\}_{x\in X}
is a
continuous frame,
i.e.
such that the operator
A_{\eta}
weakly defined via

\langle A_{\eta}\phi,\psi\rangle=\int_{X}\langle\phi,U\left(\sigma(x)\right)\eta\rangle\langle U\left(\sigma(x)\right)\eta,\psi\rangle\,d\nu(x)\,,\quad\phi,\psi\in H,

(4)

is bounded and boundedly
invertible. We call the
corresponding vector
\eta\in H\backslash\{0\}

admissible
.

Notice that the definition of
admissibility is often given in a
different way in the literature, as pointed out in the following remark.

Remark 2.1
.

We observe that in
[
3
]
and in

[
8
]
a vector
\eta\in H\backslash\{0\}
is called admissible if

\int_{X}\bigl|\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\nu(x)<\infty

(5)

for all
\phi\in H
.
If
X=G
and the
representation
U
is irreducible,
then the upper and lower bounds follow from (
2
).
However, if
X=G/N
with
N

non-trivial, (
2
) does not imply (
5
),
which in general is not sufficient to ensure the lower bound, see

[
13
]
for a
complete discussion.

2.2
Group theoretic aspects

We briefly recall the group theoretical
construction, introduced in
[
3
]
, of wavelets on the two dimensional sphere

\mathbb{S}^{2}:=\{\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:\omega^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\omega=1\}=\{(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}:\varphi\in[0,2\pi),\theta\in[0,\pi]\}.

There is no harm in regarding
\omega
and
(\theta,\varphi)
both as points on the sphere and as points
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and we shall do so. We equip
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the Riemannian
surface element
d\Sigma(\omega)=\sin\theta d\theta d\varphi
.
The Hilbert space
H
we are interested in is
L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\Sigma)
and we denote the inner product by

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the norm by

\|\cdot\|=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

The group
G={\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}
is the connected component of the identity of the
Lorentz group

{\mathrm{O}}(3,1)=\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{4,4}:g^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}I_{3,1}g=I_{3,1}\},

where
I_{3,1}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,-1)
. Its
Iwasawa
decomposition
reads as

{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}=KAN,

where
K
is the maximal compact subgroup of
rotations

K=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}:\gamma\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3)\right\}\sim{\mathrm{SO}}(3)=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{3,3}:\gamma^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\gamma=I_{3},\det\gamma=1\},

the factor
A
is the Abelian subgroup of
dilations
,

A=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&0&0\\
0&\frac{a+a^{-1}}{2}&\frac{a-a^{-1}}{2}\\
0&\frac{a-a^{-1}}{2}&\frac{a+a^{-1}}{2}\end{bmatrix}:a\in{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}\right\}\sim{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+},

and
N
is the nilpotent (in fact Abelian) subgroup of
translations

N=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&-b&b\\
b^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}&1-\frac{|b|^{2}}{2}&\frac{|b|^{2}}{2}\\
b^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}&-\frac{|b|^{2}}{2}&1+\frac{|b|^{2}}{2}\end{bmatrix}:b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right\}\sim{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

From now on, we identify
K
,
A
and
N
with
{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
,
{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}
and

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, respectively. The minimal parabolic subgroup of
{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}
is

P=MAN,

where
M
is the centralizer of
A
in
K
, namely

M=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}u&0\\
0&I_{2}\end{bmatrix}:u\in{\mathrm{SO}}(2)\right\}\sim{\mathrm{SO}}(2)=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2,2}:u^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}u=I_{2},\det u=1\}.

Since

{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}/P=KAN/MAN\sim K/M={\mathrm{SO}}(3)/{\mathrm{SO}}(2)\sim\mathbb{S}^{2},

the group
{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}
acts transitively on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and we denote
the corresponding action by

{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\ni(g,\omega)\mapsto g\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

In particular, for all rotations
\gamma\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma\omega

\displaystyle=\gamma.\omega,

(6)

where
\gamma.\omega
denotes the action of the matrix
\gamma
on
the vector
\omega
, and for all dilations
a\in{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}
,

a\omega=(\theta_{a},\varphi)=:\omega_{a},\qquad\tan(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{a})=a\tan(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta).

The measure
d\Sigma
is quasi-invariant with respect to the action of

{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}
. This means for all
g\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)
that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f(g\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\kappa(g,\omega)f(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega),

(7)

where
f:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is such that one of the two sides, hence both, is
finite, and
\kappa
is a function that enjoys the cocycle property

\kappa(g_{1}g_{2},\omega)=\kappa(g_{1},\omega)\kappa(g_{2},g_{1}^{-1}\omega),\quad g_{1},g_{2}\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1),\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

(8)

In particular, for all rotations
\gamma\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
,

\kappa(\gamma,\omega)=1,\quad\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

and for all dilations
a\in{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}
,

\kappa(a,\omega)=\frac{4a^{2}}{[(a^{2}-1)\cos\theta+(a^{2}+1)]^{2}},\quad\omega=(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

As a particular instance of the theory of parabolic induction

[
19
]
, there is a natural irreducible unitary
representation of
{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}
acting on
L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
as

\bigl[U(g)f\bigr](\omega)=\kappa(g,\omega)^{1/2}f(g^{-1}\omega),\quad g\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1),\,f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}).

(9)

The above representation is not square integrable. However, as
first stated in
[
3
]
, it is
square-integrable modulo
(N,\sigma)
, where the homogeneous space is

X:={\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}/N\sim{\mathrm{SO}}(3)\times{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+},

and the section
\sigma:X\to{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)_{0}
is

\sigma(\gamma,a)=\gamma a,\quad\gamma\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3),a\in A\,.

Note that
X
admits an
{\mathrm{SO}}(3,1)
-invariant measure
\nu
given by

d\nu(\gamma,a)=\frac{d\mu(\gamma)\,da}{a^{3}},

where
\mu
is the Haar measure of
{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
normalized in such a way
that
\mu({\mathrm{SO}}(3))=8\pi^{2}
and
da
is the Lebesgue measure on the real
line.

As shown in
[
3
, Prop. 3.2]
, the representation
(
9
) on the homogeneous space
X
factorizes as

U(\sigma(\gamma,a))=\lambda(\gamma)D_{a},

where
\lambda
is the quasi-regular representation of
{\mathrm{SO}}(3)

acting on
L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
given for
\gamma\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
by

\bigl[\lambda(\gamma)f\bigr](\omega)=f(\gamma^{-1}\omega),\quad\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\,f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}),

(10)

and
a\mapsto D_{a}
is the unitary representation of
{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}
acting on

L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
via dilation operators, namely, for any
a\in{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}
,

(D_{a}f)(\omega):=\kappa(a,\theta)^{1/2}f(\omega_{1/a}),\quad\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\,f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}).

In
[
3
]
the continuous wavelets on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
are defined as
the family

\left\{U(\sigma(x))\eta\right\}_{x\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3)\times{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}},

where
\eta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is a suitable function. Theorem
3.1

below provides necessary and sufficient conditions on the vector

\eta
such that

\left\{U(\sigma(x))\eta\right\}_{x\in{\mathrm{SO}}(3)\times{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}}
is a
continuous frame,
i.e.
it satisfies (
1
). We refer to

[
3
]
for a geometrical interpretation of
U(\sigma(x))
in
terms of stereographic projections.

The above result was first stated in
[
3
]
with a sketch of the
proof. In Section
4
, we provide a complete proof, filling in details that are missing
in
[
3
]
. To this end, we denote the spherical harmonics by

Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\varphi},\quad|m|\leq\ell,\,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
P_{\ell}^{m}
denote the associated Legendre polynomials. We
recall that

\{Y_{\ell}^{m}\}_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},|m|\leq\ell}

form an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and that for each
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

the orthogonal projection onto the spherical zone

\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}=\Span\{Y_{\ell}^{m}:|m|\leq\ell\}

is given by

\Pi_{\ell}f(\omega)=\sum_{|m|\leq\ell}\langle f,Y_{\ell}^{m}\rangle Y_{\ell}^{m}(\omega)=(2\ell+1)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}P_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})f(\omega^{\prime})d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime}),\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

(11)

where
P_{\ell}=P_{\ell}^{0}
is the Legendre polynomials of degree
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Note that each
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
is an invariant subspace of dimension
2\ell+1
for the representation
\lambda
of
{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
, and that the restriction of
\lambda

to
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
is irreducible.

3
Main result

The main results of this paper are the following necessary and sufficient conditions on a function

\eta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
to be admissible.

Theorem 3.1

(Main Result)
.

Fix
\eta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\eta(\theta,\varphi)|\frac{1}{1+\cos\theta}\,d\Sigma(\omega)<+\infty\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\eta(\theta,\varphi)|\frac{\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})}{1+\cos\theta}\,d\Sigma(\omega)<+\infty\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\esssup_{(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left(|\eta(\theta,\varphi)|\frac{1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})}{1-\cos\theta}\right)<+\infty.\,

(14)

Then there is a constant
B>0
such that

\int_{X}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\nu(x)\leq B\|\phi\|^{2}

for all
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\eta(\theta,\varphi)\frac{1}{1+\cos\theta}\,d\Sigma(\theta,\phi)=0\,.

(15)

Under this condition, the frame operator

A_{\eta}:L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
defined by

\langle A_{\eta}\phi,\psi\rangle=\int_{X}\bigl\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\bigr\rangle\langle U(\sigma(x))\eta,\psi\rangle\,d\nu(x)

(16)

is bounded, and it is boundedly invertible if and only if the function

\theta\mapsto\int_{0}^{2\pi}\eta(\theta,\varphi)\,d\varphi\neq 0\,.

(17)

The proof of the theorem is given in the rest of the paper, but
we first add a few comments and some notation. For
\eta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, set

\displaystyle\eta^{[1]}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\eta(\theta,\varphi)}{1+\cos\theta},\quad

\displaystyle\quad(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle\eta^{[2]}(\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\eta^{[1]}(\theta,\varphi)\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2}),\quad

\displaystyle\quad(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\eta(\theta,\varphi)\,d\varphi,\quad

\displaystyle\quad\theta\in[0,\pi]\,.

(20)

Conditions (
12
) and (
13
) state that
\eta^{[1]}
and

\eta^{[2]}
are integrable functions, respectively,
and (
17
) means that
\widetilde{\eta}\neq 0
. It is
clear that if
\eta
is an axisymmetric function,

i.e.
,
\eta
is independent of the longitude
\varphi
, then

\widetilde{\eta}=\eta
and (
17
) simply states that
\eta
is a
non-zero vector. Under these
assumptions, if
\eta
is a continuous function with support contained
in
(0,\pi)
, conditions (
12
), (
13
)
and (
14
) hold true. The following result shows that there is
a rather natural construction for functions
\eta
fulfilling the
vanishing mean condition (
15
).

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
\zeta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and
\alpha>0
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{\zeta(\theta,\varphi)}{1+\cos\theta}\,d\Sigma(\omega)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{D_{\alpha}\zeta(\theta,\varphi)}{1+\cos\theta}\,d\Sigma(\omega).

As a consequence, the function

\eta=\zeta-\alpha^{-1}D_{\alpha}\zeta
fulfills the
cancellation condition
(
15
).

The proof of the lemma is given at the end of Section
7
.

4
General necessary and sufficient admissibility condition

The following theorem gives a necessary and sufficient condition
for the frame operator associated to
\{U(\sigma(x))\eta\}_{x\in X}

to be bounded and boundedly invertible. The relation to
[
3
, Theorem 3.3]
is explained in a subsequent remark.
We set:

\eta_{a}:=D_{a}\eta\,.

Theorem 4.1
.

Take
\eta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and set

G_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a},\eta_{a}\rangle\frac{da}{a^{3}}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a}\|^{2}\frac{da}{a^{3}}\,,\qquad\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(21)

Then the following conditions are equivalent:

i)

There exists a constant
C_{\eta}>0
such that for every
\phi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
,

\int_{X}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\nu(x)\leq C_{\eta}\|\phi\|^{2}.

(22)

ii)

The sequence
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is bounded.

If one of the above two conditions holds true, then the best constant
C_{\eta}
is given by

C_{\eta}=8\pi^{2}\sup_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}G_{\ell},

and the operator
A_{\eta}:L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
defined by (
16
) fulfills

\displaystyle\langle A_{\eta}\phi,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}G_{\ell}\langle\Pi_{\ell}\phi,\psi\rangle,

(23)

for all
\phi,\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. Furthermore,
A_{\eta}
is boundedly invertible if and only if the
sequence
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is bounded from below by a
strictly positive constant.
In this case, we have

\|A_{\eta}^{-1}\|=\Bigl(\inf_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}G_{\ell}\Bigr)^{-1}\,.

As a consequence,
\eta
is an admissible vector if and only if there exist
constants
0<c_{\eta}\leq C_{\eta}
such that for all
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,

c_{\eta}\leq G_{\ell}\leq C_{\eta}\,.

Proof.

Standard arguments show that condition i) is equivalent to assuming the existence of a dense
subset
\mathcal{D}\subseteq L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
such that (
22
) holds
true for any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}

[
13
]
.
For the sake of completeness, we outline
the proof. Define the voice transform

V_{\eta}:\operatorname{dom}(V_{\eta})\subseteq L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\to L_{2}(X,\nu)
by

V_{\eta}\phi(x):=\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\rangle,\qquad\text{ for a.e. }x\in X\,,

where

\dom(V_{\eta}):=\{\phi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\mid\langle\phi,U(\sigma(\cdot))\eta\rangle\in L^{2}(X,\nu)\}.

Since
\dom(V_{\eta})
is a vector space containing
\mathcal{D}
, we see that
V_{\eta}
is a
densely defined operator. A direct computation shows that
V_{\eta}
is closed. Thus, the closed
graph theorem together with the fact that (
22
) holds true for all

\phi\in\mathcal{D}
implies that
\dom V_{\eta}=L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and
V_{\eta}
is a bounded operator,
so that (
22
) holds true for every
\phi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
Furthermore, it is straightforward to
check that
A_{\eta}=V_{\eta}^{*}V_{\eta}
is given by (
16
), so that
A_{\eta}
is
a positive bounded operator by construction.

Hence, it is sufficient to show that condition ii) is equivalent to the existence of a dense
subset
\mathcal{D}\subseteq L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
such that (
22
) holds true for every

\phi\in\mathcal{D}
.
To this purpose, we define

\mathcal{D}:=\Biggl\{\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\phi_{\ell}\ \Big|\ L\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\,\phi_{\ell}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\Biggr\},

which is a dense subspace of
L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. Fixing
\phi\in\mathcal{D}
, so that

\phi=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\Pi_{\ell}\phi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathrm{SO}}(3)}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,\lambda(\gamma)D_{a}\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\mu(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=0}^{L}\int_{{\mathrm{SO}}(3)}\bigl\langle\Pi_{\ell}\phi,\lambda(\gamma)D_{a}\eta\bigr\rangle\overline{\bigl\langle\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}\phi,\lambda(\gamma)D_{a}\eta\bigr\rangle}\,d\mu(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=0}^{L}\int_{{\mathrm{SO}}(3)}\bigl\langle\Pi_{\ell}\phi,\lambda_{\ell}(\gamma)\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a}\bigr\rangle\overline{\bigl\langle\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}\phi,\lambda_{\ell^{\prime}}(\gamma)\Pi_{\ell^{\prime}}\eta_{a}\bigr\rangle}\,d\mu(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\frac{8\pi^{2}}{2\ell+1}\bigl\lVert{\Pi_{\ell}\phi\bigr\rVert}^{2}\,\bigl\lVert{\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a}\bigr\rVert}^{2}\,,

where
\lambda_{\ell}
is the restriction of the representation
\lambda
to the

\lambda
-invariant subspace
\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}=\Pi_{\ell}L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, and that last equality
holds true since
(\lambda_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a family of
irreducible non equivalent representations of the compact group
{\mathrm{SO}}(3)

and the Schur orthogonality relations give

\int_{{\mathrm{SO}}(3)}\bigl\langle\phi,\lambda_{\ell}(\gamma)\psi\bigr\rangle\,\overline{\bigl\langle\phi^{\prime},\lambda_{\ell^{\prime}}(\gamma)\psi^{\prime}\bigr\rangle}\,d\mu(\gamma)=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu({\mathrm{SO}}(3))}{\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{Y}_{\ell})}\langle\phi,\phi^{\prime}\rangle\langle\psi^{\prime},\psi\rangle&\ell=\ell^{\prime},\\
0&\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\end{cases}

for all
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}
,
\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\ell^{\prime}}
.
Hence, by (
10

\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{X}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{{\mathrm{SO}}(3)}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,\lambda(\gamma)D_{a}\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\mu(\gamma)\right)\,\frac{da}{a^{3}}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\frac{8\pi^{2}}{2\ell+1}\bigl\lVert\Pi_{\ell}\phi\bigr\rVert^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl\lVert\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a}\bigr\rVert^{2}\,\frac{da}{a^{3}}

(25)

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}G_{\ell}\bigl\lVert\Pi_{\ell}\phi\bigr\rVert^{2},

(26)

where in view of Fubini’s theorem the left-hand side is finite if and only if each

G_{\ell}
in the sum is finite. Furthermore, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{D},\lVert\phi\rVert=1}\int_{X}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{L\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in\oplus_{\ell=0}^{L}\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}\\
\lVert\phi\rVert=1\end{subarray}}8\pi^{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}G_{\ell}\bigl\lVert\Pi_{\ell}\phi\bigr\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\sup_{L\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\sup_{\ell\leq L}G_{\ell}=8\pi^{2}\sup_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}G_{\ell}.

The above equality shows that (
22
) holds true for all
\phi\in\mathcal{D}

if and only if the sequence
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is bounded and,
in such a case, the best constant is given by
8\pi^{2}\sup_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}G_{\ell}
.

It remains to show the representation (
23
). Since
A_{\eta}
is a positive bounded
operator, it is enough to show (
23
) for
\phi=\psi\in\mathcal{D}
. With this choice it follows

\langle A_{\eta}\phi,\phi\rangle=\lVert V_{\eta}\phi\rVert^{2}=\int_{X}\bigl|\bigl\langle\phi,U(\sigma(x))\eta\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\,d\nu(x)=8\pi^{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}G_{\ell}\bigl\lVert\Pi_{\ell}\phi\bigr\rVert^{2}\,.

The above equation makes it clear that
A_{\eta}
is boundedly
invertible if and only if the sequence
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is
bounded from below by a positive constant. The last claim
characterizing the admissible vectors is now clear.
∎

The identity (
23
) states that
A_{\eta}
commutes with the left-regular representation

\lambda
of
{\mathrm{SO}}(3)
, which can be proven directly using (

immediately becomes clear that the boundedness of

is indeed
necessary and sufficient for the boundedness of

.

Remark 4.2

(Relation to
[
3
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Using our notation, Theorem 3.3 in
[
3
]
claims that there exists a function
\eta
such that

is bounded.
Indeed the authors prove essentially the relations given in

We first prove (

\Pi_{\ell}

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a},\eta_{a}\rangle}{a^{2}(2\ell+1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}P_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})\frac{\kappa(a,\theta)^{1/2}\kappa(a,\theta^{\prime})^{1/2}}{a^{2}}\eta(\omega_{1/a})\overline{\eta(\omega^{\prime}_{1/a})}\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}P_{\ell}(\omega_{a}\cdot\omega^{\prime}_{a})\frac{\kappa(a^{-1},\theta)^{1/2}\kappa(a^{-1},\theta^{\prime})^{1/2}}{a^{2}}\eta(\omega)\overline{\eta(\omega^{\prime})}\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})\,.

\lim_{a\downarrow 0}I_{1}=8P_{\ell}^{\prime}(1)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta^{\prime}}{2})-2\tan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})\tan(\tfrac{\theta^{\prime}}{2})\cos(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})}{(1+\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta^{\prime})}\eta(\omega)\overline{\eta(\omega^{\prime})}\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime}).

C_{2}:=\esssup_{\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{|\eta(\omega)|}{1-\cos\theta}\bigl(1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})\bigr)\,

which is finite by assumption (
14
).
After rewriting (
44
) as

\displaystyle 4a^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bigl|Q_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})\bigr|\bigl(1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{1/a})\bigr)\bigl(1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta^{\prime}_{1/a})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\kappa(a,\theta)^{1/2}\kappa(a,\theta^{\prime})^{1/2}}{a^{2}}|\eta(\omega_{1/a})|\,|\eta(\omega^{\prime}_{1/a})|\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})\,,

and applying
\frac{\kappa(a,\theta)^{1/2}}{a}\leq\frac{2}{1-\cos\theta}
, we can
estimate this by

\displaystyle 16a^{2}

\displaystyle\biggl(\esssup_{\omega=(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{|\eta(\omega_{1/a})|}{1-\cos\theta}\bigl(1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{1/a})\bigr)\biggr)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bigl|Q_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})\bigr|\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=16a^{2}\biggl(\esssup_{\omega=(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{|\eta(\omega)|}{1-\cos\theta_{a}}\bigl(1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})\bigr)\biggr)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bigl|Q_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})\bigr|\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 16a^{2}C_{2}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|Q_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})|\,d\Sigma(\omega)\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})\,,

where at the end we used
a\leq\varepsilon<1
, which implies
\theta_{a}\leq\theta
and thus

1-\cos\theta\geq 1-\cos\theta_{a}
.
To calculate the last integral, we observe for
Q_{\ell}
defined in (
37
) and for fixed
\omega=(\theta,\varphi)
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|Q_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})|\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|Q_{\ell}(R_{\omega}\omega\cdot R_{\omega}\omega^{\prime})|\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|Q_{\ell}(e_{z}\cdot\omega^{\prime\prime})|\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}|Q_{\ell}(\cos\theta^{\prime\prime})|\,\sin\theta^{\prime\prime}d\theta^{\prime\prime}\,d\varphi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}|Q_{\ell}(s)|\,ds=2\pi\|Q_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}([-1,1])}\,,

where the rotation
R_{\omega}
maps
\omega
onto the north pole
e_{z}
, and we used
the substitution
s=\cos\theta^{\prime\prime}
.
We conclude

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|Q_{\ell}(\omega\cdot\omega^{\prime})|\,d\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})\,d\Sigma(\omega)=8\pi^{2}\|Q_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}([-1,1])}.

Arranging everything together, we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a},\eta_{a}\rangle}{(2\ell+1)a^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{3}a^{2}\bigl(1+\|Q_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}([-1,1])}\bigr)\,,

where
C_{3}
is a suitable constant, which implies the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\langle\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a},\eta_{a}\rangle}{(2\ell+1)a^{2}}\,\frac{da}{a}\leq C_{3}\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(1+\|Q_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}([-1,1])}\bigr)\,.

(46)

Recalling
\varepsilon^{2}\sim\ell^{-1}
, it remains to show that

\|Q_{\ell}\|_{L_{1}([-1,1])}=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
.
Based
on the Mean Value Theorem applied to
P_{\ell}
, we have for every
x\in[0,1]
that

|Q_{\ell}(x)|=\frac{1-P_{\ell}(x)}{1-x}\leq\|P^{\prime}_{\ell}\|_{L_{\infty}([0,1])}=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left|Q_{\ell}(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}\left|\frac{1-P_{\ell}(x)}{1-x}\right|dx+\int_{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}^{1}|Q_{\ell}(x)|dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{1-\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}\Bigl(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}+1\Bigr)+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=2(2\ell-1)+\frac{\ell+1}{2}=\frac{9\ell-3}{2},

which shows that the right-hand side of (
46
) is uniformly bounded.
∎

6
Stereographic projection and moment conditions

In this section, we consider an isometric transform of the mother wavelet
\eta
based on the stereographic projection.
Recalling the definition of
\eta^{[1]}
and
\eta^{[2]}
given
by (
18
) and (
19
), we reinterpret the cancellation condition (
15
) on
\eta^{[1]}

as a vanishing mean condition of the transformed
\eta

and integrability condition (
13
) of
\eta^{[2]}
as existence condition of the second moment of the transformed
\eta
.
The interesting relation between the cancellation condition and the transformed admissible function was already
discovered by Antoine and Vandergheynst
[
3
]
.

The stereographic projection allows to map
\mathbb{S}^{2}

without the south pole onto the real plane.
In polar coordinates in the plane the stereographic projection and its inverse are given by

(\theta,\varphi)\mapsto(\tan(\tfrac{\theta}{2}),\varphi),\quad(r,\varphi)\mapsto(2\arctan r,\varphi),

respectively.
The stereographic projection allows to map functions on the sphere to functions on the real plane and vice versa.
The following lemma corresponds to

[
3
, Lemma 3.5]
under the correspondence
2\arctan r=\arccos\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}

Lemma 6.1
.

The mapping
\Theta:L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\rightarrow L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,2\pi),r^{-1}dr\,d\varphi)
, defined by

(\Theta f)(r,\varphi)=\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}f\bigl(2\arctan r,\varphi\bigr)

is an isometric isomorphism.
Moreover, the usual dilation
D_{a}^{+}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, which can be written in polar coordinates
as
(D_{a}^{+}f)(r,\varphi)=f(r/a,\varphi)
, fulfills the relation

\Theta D_{a}=D_{a}^{+}\Theta
.

Proof.

Since
\theta=2\arctan r
if and only if
r=\tan(\theta/2)
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta}{dr}=\frac{2}{1+r^{2}},

and furthermore

\sin\theta=\frac{2\tan(\theta/2)}{1+\tan^{2}(\theta/2)}=\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}.

Inserting this, we conclude

\displaystyle\|\Theta f\|^{2}_{L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,2\pi),r^{-1}dr\,d\varphi)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}|(\Theta f)(r,\varphi)|^{2}r^{-1}\,dr\,d\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\biggl(\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\biggr)^{2}|f(\theta,\varphi)|^{2}r^{-1}\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}\,d\theta\,d\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}|f(\theta,\varphi)|^{2}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi=\|f\|^{2}.

Thus,
\Theta
is an isometry, and obviously bijective. For the second part, we
have on the one hand

\displaystyle(\Theta D_{a}f)(r,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\Theta\bigl[\kappa(a,\theta)^{1/2}f\bigl(2\arctan(\tfrac{1}{a}\tan\tfrac{\theta}{2}),\cdot\bigr)\bigr](r,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\kappa(a,2\arctan r)^{1/2}\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}f\bigl(2\arctan(r/a),\varphi\bigr),

and since
\cos\theta=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}
we further get

\kappa(a,2\arctan r)^{1/2}=\frac{2a}{(a^{2}-1)\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}+(a^{2}+1)}=\frac{2a(1+r^{2})}{2a^{2}+2r^{2}}\,.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(D_{a}^{+}\Theta f)(r,\varphi)

\displaystyle=D_{a}^{+}\Bigl[\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}f\bigl(2\arctan r,\cdot\bigr)\Bigr](r,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{2ar}{a^{2}+r^{2}}f\bigl(2\arctan(r/a),\varphi\bigr)\,,

which finally shows
\Theta D_{a}=D_{a}^{+}\Theta
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The substitution
r=\tan(\theta/2)
also yields the new interpretation of the cancellation condition (
15
) on
\eta^{[1]}

and the integrability (
13
) of
\eta^{[2]}
. First, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\eta(\theta,\varphi)}{1+\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\eta(\theta,\varphi)\tan(\theta/2)\,d\theta\,d\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta(\theta,\varphi)\,\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\,dr\,d\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Theta\eta)(r,\varphi)\,dr\,d\varphi\,.

Thus,
\eta^{[1]}
is integrable if
\Theta\eta

is integrable w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure as a function on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times(0,2\pi)
.
The cancellation condition on
\eta^{[1]}

becomes a vanishing mean condition for
\Theta\eta
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure
dr\,d\varphi
on

(0,\infty)\times(0,2\pi)
. Note that this is neither the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
in polar coordinates, nor
the measure considered in Lemma
6.1
.

Similarly, we can rewrite

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{\eta(\theta,\varphi)}{1+\cos\theta}\tan^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\bigr)\,d\Sigma=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}(\Theta\eta)(r,\varphi)\,dr\,d\varphi\,.

Hence the integrability condition for
\eta^{[2]}
turns into the existence of the second moment of

\Theta\eta
when considered as a function in
L_{2}((0,\infty)\times(0,2\pi),dr\,d\varphi)
.

Finally, the boundedness of
\frac{\eta}{1-\cos\theta}(1+\tan^{2}(\frac{\theta}{2}))

transfers to the decay of
\Theta\eta
as
r\to 0
and
r\to\infty
, respectively, in view of

\Theta\Big(\frac{\eta}{1-\cos\theta}\bigl(1+\tan^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})\Big)=\frac{(1+r^{2})^{2}}{2r^{2}}\Theta\eta\,.

Remarkably, the conditions for
\eta
to be admissible resemble conditions
for the Fourier transform of
\Theta\eta
to be a wavelet in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
– weighted integrability and decay towards the origin (
\longleftrightarrow
smoothness and vanishing moments, respectively, for the wavelet).

7
Lower frame bound

In this section, we are concerned with sufficient conditions for the invertibility of
A_{\eta}
and boundedness of

A_{\eta}^{-1}
.
In Proposition
7.2
, we prove that the numbers
G_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, are positive
if
\tilde{\eta}
defined in (
14
) does not vanish,
and in Theorem
7.3
that these numbers are indeed uniformly bounded away from zero under Assumption (
12
).
Since
[
3
, Prop. 3.4]
claims the same, we start with a remark pointing out the differences.

Remark 7.1

(Relation to
[
3
, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

The first part of the proof of Proposition 3.4 in
[
3
]
is devoted to show that
G_{\ell}>0
for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
It ends with the claim that the fact that the convolution of some functions
vanishes for all parameters
a>0

implies that one of the functions is zero.
However, the convolution of two functions vanishes if their respective Fourier transforms have disjoint supports.
Therefore, we invest some work in proving
G_{\ell}>0
in the next proposition.

The second part of the proof in
[
3
]
deals with the uniform boundedness of
(G_{\ell})_{\ell}
away from zero.
At the end of the proof, it is stated that ’the only contribution of the integral over
\theta
comes from the region

a\sim 1/\ell
’ – that intuition is correct.
However, with increasing
\ell
these regions become smaller and smaller, so that we were unfortunately not able to follow the subsequent
argument. We will therefore address the issue again in the subsequent Theorem
7.3
.

Proposition 7.2
.

For every
\eta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
satisfying (
15
)
the numbers
G_{\ell}
,
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
are strictly positive.

Proof.

Step 1:

Let
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be fixed. By definition of the zonal projections, we have

\|\Pi_{\ell}\eta_{a}\|^{2}=\sum_{|m|\leq\ell}|\langle Y_{\ell}^{n},\eta_{a}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{|m|\leq\ell}|\widehat{\eta}_{a}(\ell,m)|^{2},

(47)

where
\widehat{\eta}_{a}(\ell,m):=\langle\eta_{a},Y_{\ell}^{m}\rangle
.
Thus it suffices to show that

|\widehat{\eta}_{a}(\ell,0)|^{2}>0
.
Note that in the
particular case of an axisymmetric function
\eta
, i.e.,

\eta
is independent of the longitude
\varphi
,
we have actually
|\widehat{\eta}_{a}(\ell,m)|=0
for all
m\neq 0
.
Using Lemma
6.1
we rewrite the Fourier coefficients as

\displaystyle\widehat{\eta_{a}}(\ell,0)

\displaystyle=\langle D_{a}\eta,Y_{\ell}^{0}\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}=\langle\Theta D_{a}\eta,\Theta Y_{\ell}^{0}\rangle_{L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{a}^{+}\Theta\eta,\Theta Y_{\ell}^{0}\rangle_{L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Theta Y_{\ell}^{0})(r,\varphi)\overline{(\Theta\eta)(r/a,\varphi)}\,\frac{dr}{r}\,d\varphi.

As
Y_{\ell}^{0}
does not depend on
\varphi
, we may assume w.l.o.g. that
\eta
is axisymmetric,
otherwise we simply replace
\eta
by
\widetilde{\eta}
as
defined by (
20
). Then the
\varphi
integration just
produces a constant factor
2\pi
.
Moreover, denoting

\check{g}(r):=\overline{g(r^{-1})}
,
we obtain, up to a normalizing factor depending on
\ell
,

\widehat{\eta_{a}}(\ell,0)\sim 2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(\Theta[P_{\ell}(\cos\cdot)]\bigr)(r)(\Theta\eta)^{\vee}(a/r)\,\frac{dr}{r}.

The last integral can be understood as the convolution of the functions

\Theta[P_{\ell}(\cos\cdot)]
and
(\Theta\eta)^{\vee}
, defined on the multiplicative group

{\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+}
equipped with the corresponding Haar measure
\frac{dr}{r}
.
Thus, for
G_{\ell}=0
to be true for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, this convolution needs to vanish for every

a>0
. However, the convolution of two functions can only vanish if their respective
Mellin transforms have disjoint support.

Step 2:
The Mellin transform of a function
g:(0,\infty)\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
which is locally integrable
w.r.t. the Haar measure
\frac{dr}{r}
, is defined by

(\mathcal{M}f)(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{s}g(r)\,\frac{dr}{r}.

It is then easy to check that
\mathcal{M}(f\ast g)(s)=\mathcal{M}f(s)\mathcal{M}g(s)
, where

f\ast g
is the convolution in
L_{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{*}^{+},\tfrac{dr}{r})
. We are now interested in the Mellin
transform of

\Theta[P_{\ell}(\cos\cdot)](r)=\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}P_{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}\Bigr).

Using the substitution
\rho=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}
, i.e.,
r^{2}=\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\bigl[\Theta[P_{\ell}(\cos\cdot)]\bigr](s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{s}\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}P_{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}\Bigr)\,\frac{dr}{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigl(\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}\Bigr)^{s/2}(1+\rho)P_{\ell}(\rho)\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^{1/2}(1+\rho)^{3/2}}d\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-\rho)^{\frac{s-1}{2}}(1+\rho)^{-\frac{s+1}{2}}P_{\ell}(\rho)\,d\rho\,.

From this we can conclude that the integral converges precisely for
-1<\Re s<1
, since the
Legendre-Polynomials are non-vanishing in the endpoints. Moreover, it is readily checked that it
actually defines an analytic function on this open strip. As a consequence, the set of roots of

\mathcal{M}[\Theta[P_{\ell}(\cos\cdot)]]
cannot contain a cluster point.

For functions
f\in L_{1}((0,\infty),\frac{dr}{r})\cap L_{2}((0,\infty),\frac{dr}{r})
, the
particular line
s=\frac{1}{2}+it
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, is always contained in the domain of convergence.
This gives rise to an isometry
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}:L_{2}((0,\infty),\tfrac{dr}{r})\rightarrow L_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}f(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{1}{2}+it}f(r)\,\frac{dr}{r}\,.

This version of the Mellin transform inherits the convolution property, i.e.,

\sqrt{2\pi}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(f\ast g)(t)=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}f(t)\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}g(t),

for almost all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and for all
f,g\in L_{2}((0,\infty),\frac{dr}{r})
.

Applying this general theory to our present situation we conclude that the function

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\bigl[\Theta[P_{\ell}(\cos\cdot)]\bigr]
has global support.
Therefore

\widehat{\eta_{a}}(\ell,0)
vanishes for all
a>0

exactly when

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}[(\Theta\eta)^{\vee}]=0
and hence only for
\eta\equiv 0
.
∎

For convenience, we provide an alternative proof of the proposition in the appendix.

To prove that
(G_{\ell})_{\ell}

7.2
to show that
\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}G_{\ell}>0
.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
\eta\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
satisfy (
12
).
Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\ell+1}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|\langle Y_{\ell}^{0},\eta_{a}\rangle|^{2}\,\frac{da}{a^{3}}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,J_{0}(2ct)\widetilde{\eta}(2\arctan t)\,dt\right)^{2}\,\frac{dc}{c}\,,

(48)

where
\widetilde{\eta}
is defined by (
20
).
Furthermore, if (
17
) holds true, the sequence
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}

is bounded below by a strictly positive number.

Proof.

Step 1:

For deriving a lower bound for the numbers
G_{\ell}
, it suffices
to consider in (
47
) the term
with
m=0

G_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{1}|\langle Y_{\ell}^{0},\eta_{a}\rangle|^{2}\frac{da}{a^{3}}\,.

For the scalar product we obtain

\langle Y_{\ell}^{0},\eta_{a}\rangle=\langle D_{a^{-1}}Y_{\ell}^{0},\eta\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\kappa(a^{-1},\theta)\sqrt{\frac{(2\ell+1)}{4\pi}}P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta_{a}))\eta(\theta,\varphi)\,d\Sigma\,.

\displaystyle G_{\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\langle Y_{\ell}^{0},\eta_{a}\rangle|^{2}\,\frac{da}{a^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\kappa(a^{-1},\theta)^{\frac{1}{2}}P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta_{a}))\eta(\theta)\,d\Sigma\right]^{2}\frac{da}{a^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{2\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\pi}a^{-1}\kappa(a^{-1},\theta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{a})\,\eta(\theta)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\right]^{2}\frac{da}{a}

As a next step, we deal with the inner integral.

Step 2:
For every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define
f_{\ell}:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
as

f_{\ell}(a)=\left[\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{(1-a^{2})\cos\theta+(1+a^{2})}P_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{a})\,\eta(\theta)\,d\theta\right]^{2}.

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f_{\ell}(a)}{a}\,da

\ell
. We have for all

\displaystyle\lim_{a\to 0}\kappa_{a}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0}(\theta),

(50)

\displaystyle 0\leq\kappa_{a}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{0}(\theta)=\tan\frac{\theta}{2}.

(51)

Using the Taylor expansion of
\theta_{a}=2\arctan\left(a\,\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\right)

(52)

(0,\pi)
by assumption, and hence, by
Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\to\infty}f_{\ell}\left(\frac{c}{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\kappa_{\frac{c}{\ell}}(\theta)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{\frac{1}{\ell}})\,\eta(\theta)\,d\theta\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\kappa_{0}(\theta)\,J_{0}\biggl(2c\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\biggr)\eta(\theta)\,d\theta\right)^{2}=:f(c).

Step 3:
We want to estimate the integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f_{\ell}(a)}{a}\,da
. For every fixed
k>0
and
b>1
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\ell}(a)\,\frac{da}{a}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{c}{\ell}\Bigr)\,\frac{dc}{c}\geq\sum_{j=-k}^{k}\int_{b^{j-1}}^{b^{j}}f_{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{c}{\ell}\Bigr)\,\frac{dc}{c}\geq\sum_{j=-k}^{k}b^{-j}\int_{b^{j-1}}^{b^{j}}f_{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{c}{\ell}\Bigr)\,dc\,.

Since
f_{\ell}(\frac{c}{\ell})
converges to
f(c)
for
\ell\to\infty
pointwise
with respect to
c
, and using (
54
) to estimate

\left|f_{\ell}\left(\frac{c}{\ell}\right)\right|\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}|\kappa_{0}(\theta)\,\eta(\theta)|\,d\theta\right)^{2},

independently of
\ell
, we verify for every
j
that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{b^{j-1}}^{b^{j}}f_{\ell}\left(\frac{c}{\ell}\right)\,dc=\int_{b^{j-1}}^{b^{j}}f(c)\,dc=\int_{b^{j-1}}^{b^{j}}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\kappa_{0}(\theta)\,J_{0}\biggl(2c\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\biggr)\eta(\theta)\,d\theta\right)^{2}\,dc.

This implies

\displaystyle\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f_{\ell}(a)}{a}\,da

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=-k}^{k}\int_{b^{j-1}}^{b^{j}}b^{-j}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}\,J_{0}\biggl(2c\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\biggr)\eta(\theta)\,d\theta\right)^{2}\,dc

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{b}\int_{b^{-k-1}}^{b^{k}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,J_{0}(2ct)\eta(2\arctan t)\,dt\right)^{2}\,\frac{dc}{c}\,,

(55)

where the last line uses the substitution
t=\tan\frac{\theta}{2}
leading to

\cos\theta=\frac{1-t^{2}}{1+t^{2}}
,
\sin\theta=\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}
, and

d\theta=\frac{2\,dt}{1+t^{2}}
. Noting that (
55
) is valid for all
k
,
we conclude

\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{f_{\ell}(a)}{a}\,da\geq\frac{1}{b}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,J_{0}(2ct)\eta(2\arctan t)\,dt\right)^{2}\,\frac{dc}{c}\,.

(56)

Further, the fact that this estimate holds for all
b>1

implies the lower bound (
48
).

Step 4:
The inner integral in (
56
) is the Hankel transform of the function

g(t):=\frac{2}{1+t^{2}}\eta(2\arctan t),
evaluated at
2c
.
Parseval’s theorem for the Hankel transform
[
20
]
states

\int_{0}^{\infty}t|g(t)|^{2}\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}c\,\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}tg(t)J_{0}(ct)\,dt\right|^{2}\,dc.

This implies that for any non-zero function
\eta
(which particularly entails
g\not\equiv 0
)
the right-hand-side integral is not zero. Thus, there exists
1<B<\infty
such that

\int_{0}^{B}\,c\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}tg(t)J_{0}(2ct)\,dt\right|^{2}dc>0\,,

which in turn yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{B}\,\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}tg(t)J_{0}(2ct)\,dt\right|^{2}\frac{dc}{c}\geq\frac{1}{B^{2}}\int_{0}^{B}\,c\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}tg(t)J_{0}(2ct)\,dt\right|^{2}dc>0\,.

Ultimately we conclude that (
56
) does not vanish so that

\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}G_{\ell}\geq 4\pi\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\ell}(a)\,\frac{da}{a}>0\,.

∎

Appendix A
Alternative proof of Proposition
7.2

In addition to the transform
\Theta
from Lemma
6.1
, we define a second transform

{\cal J}:L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,2\pi),r^{-1}dr\,d\varphi)\to L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times[0,2\pi),dr\,d\varphi)

via

({\cal J}f)(r,\varphi)=f(e^{r},\varphi),\quad f\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,2\pi))\,.

Then it is straightforward to check for arbitrary
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
that

{\cal J}D_{e^{b}}^{+}=T_{b}{\cal J},

where
T_{b}f(r,\varphi):=f(r-b,\varphi)
.
Setting
\widetilde{Y}_{\ell}^{n}:={\cal J}\Theta Y_{\ell}^{n}
, we find for every

(r,\varphi)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times[0,2\pi)
that

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{\ell}^{n}(r,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\,\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+n)!}}\frac{(-1)^{\ell n}}{\cosh r}P_{\ell}^{n}(\tanh r)e^{in\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(2\ell+1)\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+n)!}}(-1)^{\ell n}\phi_{\ell}^{n}(r)e^{in\varphi}\,,

(57)

where
\phi_{\ell}^{n}(r):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{4\pi}}\,\cosh r}P_{\ell}^{n}(\tanh r)
.
For simplicity let us once again assume that
\eta(\theta,\varphi)=\eta(\theta)
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle G_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Pi_{\ell}D_{a}\eta\|^{2}\,\frac{da}{a^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\langle D_{e^{b}}\eta,Y_{\ell}^{0}\rangle\right|^{2}e^{-2b}\,db

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\langle{\cal J}\Theta D_{e^{b}}\eta,{\cal J}\Theta Y_{\ell}^{0}\rangle\right|^{2}e^{-2b}\,db,

where the last equality holds since both
\Theta
and
\cal J
are isometries.
Let

\psi:={\cal J}\Theta\eta
.
Then for every
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
it holds

{\cal J}\Theta D_{e^{b}}\eta={\cal J}D_{e^{b}}^{+}\Theta\eta=T_{b}{\cal J}\Theta\eta=T_{b}\psi.

By (
A
), we also have

{\cal J}\Theta Y_{\ell}^{0}(r,\varphi)=\widetilde{Y}_{\ell}^{0}(r,\varphi)=\sqrt{(2\ell+1)}\phi_{\ell}^{0}(r).

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle G_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\langle T_{b}\psi,\phi_{\ell}^{0}\rangle\right|^{2}\,e^{-2b}\,db=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\langle{\cal F}T_{b}\psi,{\cal F}\phi_{\ell}^{0}\rangle\right|^{2}\,e^{-2b}\,db

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\langle M_{-b}{\cal F}\psi,{\cal F}\phi_{\ell}^{0}\rangle\right|^{2}e^{-2b}\,db=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left|{\cal F}^{-1}\left({\cal F}\phi_{\ell}^{0}\,\overline{{\cal F}\psi}\right)(b)\right|^{2}e^{-2b}\,db\,.

Hence, for
G_{\ell}=0
, we need that the function

{\cal F}^{-1}\left({\cal F}\phi_{\ell}^{0}\,\overline{{\cal F}\psi}\right)
, and thus

{\cal F}\phi_{\ell}^{0}\,\overline{{\cal F}\psi}
, vanishes almost everywhere. However, since

\phi_{\ell}^{0}(r)\leq e^{-r}
, the first factor
{\cal F}\phi_{\ell}^{0}
is an analytic function,
more precisely, it can be extended to an analytic function on the strip

\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|\Re z|<1\}
.
In particular, it has only isolated roots. This implies
that
G_{\ell}=0
is only possible if
{\mathcal{F}}\psi
vanishes almost everywhere, which in turn
contradicts the assumption
\widetilde{\eta}\not\equiv 0
.

\Box
