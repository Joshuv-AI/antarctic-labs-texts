Extractable work in quantum electromechanics
Oisín Culhane, Mark T. Mitchison, John Goold

Abstract
Recent experiments have demonstrated the generation of coherent mechanical oscillations in a suspended carbon nanotube, which are driven by an electric current through the device above a certain voltage threshold, in close analogy with a lasing transition. We investigate this phenomenon from the perspective of work extraction, by modelling a nano-electromechanical device as a quantum flywheel or battery that converts electrical power into stored mechanical energy. We introduce a microscopic model that qualitatively matches the experimental finding, and compute the Wigner function of the quantum vibrational mode in its non-equilibrium steady-state. We characterise the threshold for self-sustained oscillations using two approaches to quantifying work deposition in non-equilibrium quantum thermodynamics: the ergotropy and the non-equilibrium free energy. We find that ergotropy serves as an order parameter for the phonon lasing transition. The framework we employ to describe work extraction is general and widely transferable to other mesoscopic quantum devices.

Self-sustained oscillations.—
From here on, we focus on the steady state reached by the mechanical oscillator at long times. Figure

Extractable work.—
The electromechanical set-up described here is an ideal platform to study work storage and extraction, central concepts in the field of quantum thermodynamics. The non-equilibrium steady state of the device gives rise to a non-thermal state in the vibrational mode with a total energy that depends on the voltage applied. The vibrational degree of freedom thus serves as a battery or flywheel that stores energy deposited by the electronics. However, the division of this energy into useful work and waste — as per the first law of thermodynamics — is far from obvious, since some of the deposited energy merely contributes to increasing the entropy of the oscillator, i.e. heating.

We consider two different approaches to quantifying the deposited work. The first is the ergotropy, which gives the maximum work that can extracted from a quantum battery by a cyclic unitary transformation
Allahverdyan et al. 2004
. Let the state of the battery be given by
\hat{\rho}_{b}=\sum_{k}r_{k}|r_{k}\rangle\langle r_{k}|
, with
r_{k}\geq r_{k+1}
, while the Hamiltonian may be written in the energy eigenbasis as
\hat{H}_{b}=\sum_{k}\epsilon_{k}|\epsilon_{k}\rangle\langle\epsilon_{k}|
, with
\epsilon_{k}\leq\epsilon_{k+1}
the energies. The battery state
\hat{\rho}_{b}
is called passive with respect to
\hat{H}_{b}
if its energy cannot be decreased by a cyclical variation of the Hamiltonian parameters over a fixed time interval
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, such that
\hat{H}(t_{1})=\hat{H}(t_{2})=\hat{H}_{b}
. Mathematically, the conditions for passivity are that
[\hat{\rho}_{b},\hat{H}_{b}]=0
and
r_{n}\geq r_{m}
whenever
\epsilon_{n}<\epsilon_{m}
. If either of these conditions are violated then work may be extracted. By optimising over all possible protocols, one obtains an upper bound on the extractable work known as the ergotropy
Allahverdyan et al. 2004
:

\mathcal{W}_{E}=\sum_{j,k}r_{j}\epsilon_{k}\left(\lvert\braket{r_{j}|\epsilon_{k}}\rvert^{2}-\delta_{jk}\right).

(11)

Such unitary work extraction is rather idealised since the requisite protocol would often be challenging to implement. We thus consider a second approach to work extraction based on the non-equilibrium free energy
Donald 1987
;
Vedral 2002
;
Esposito and Van den
Broeck 2011
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
, which has its origin in the resource-theory formulation of thermodynamics. Here, one considers a more general class of transformations known as thermal operations, corresponding to evolution in contact with a heat bath at temperature
T
. For example, one could envisage discharging the battery by using the stored mechanical energy to drive a current against a voltage bias, in which case the heat bath would comprise thermal electrons in the leads. The non-equilibrium free energy of the battery state is given by
\mathcal{F}_{\rm neq}=U(\hat{\rho}_{b})-TS(\hat{\rho}_{b})
, where
S(\hat{\rho})=-\Tr[\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}]
is the von Neumann entropy. This is generally greater than the free energy of the state at thermal equilibrium
\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}=-k_{B}T\log{\mathcal{Z}}
with
\mathcal{Z}=\Tr{\exp(-\hat{H}_b/k_BT)}
. The maximum extractable work under a thermal operation is given by the difference

\mathcal{W}_{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm neq}-\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq},

(12)

so that any non-thermal state is in principle a resource from which work may be extracted in this framework. Since thermal operations may be non-unitary, we have
\mathcal{W}_{E}\leq\mathcal{W}_{F}
.

In our case, the battery is a vibrational mode with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{b}=\omega_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
and we consider the work extractable from its steady state
\hat{\rho}_{b}
. The results of this analysis are presented in Fig.
4
. The energy of the oscillator increases monotonically with voltage, as expected. This is reflected in the behaviour of the the von Neumann entropy, which grows rapidly below threshold and eventually saturates far above threshold. We now turn to the ergotropy (red, solid) and the result is striking. Below threshold the ergotropy is exactly zero, meaning the non-thermal state of the oscillator is passive, while above threshold the state immediately becomes non-passive and
\mathcal{W}_{E}
increases monotonically until saturation. Remarkably, therefore, we conclude that the ergotropy serves as an order parameter for the phonon lasing transition — unlike, for example, the mode quadratures themselves — so that the electromechanical battery only stores useful work above threshold. This is a key finding of our work. Meanwhile, the non-equilibrium free energy difference
\mathcal{W}_{F}
is shown as red, dashed. This follows a similar trend to the ergotropy but with one exception: even the passive oscillator steady states below threshold may yield some work output under thermal operations due to their distance from thermal equilibrium. However, the value of
\mathcal{W}_{F}
below threshold is relatively small, demonstrating the importance of self-sustained oscillations for effective battery charging.

Conclusions.—
We have quantified the conversion of electrical power into mechanical work stored in the quantum state of a NEMS device. Our results demonstrate that the self-sustained oscillations that were recently observed in Refs.
Wen et al. 2019
;
Urgell et al. 2019
can be interpreted as an electromechanical battery, thus bridging a gap between quantum thermodynamic theory and mesoscopic device physics. We found that typical battery states in the quasi-adiabatic regime are diagonal in the energy eigenbasis but it may be possible to engineer configurations that generate mechanical coherence — from which additional work can be extracted
Korzekwa et al. 2016
;
Francica et al. 2020
— for example, by working in the strong electromechanical coupling regime
Vigneau et al. 2021
. A pressing question for future work is to devise concrete protocols for transferring energy from the non-equilibrium battery states back into the electronic circuit
Lörch et al. 2018
, opening up the exciting prospect of on-demand energy storage and extraction using mechanical degrees of freedom. Such electromechanical energy conversion could also be performed using heat rather than charge currents
Vikström et al. 2016
. Finally, we note that the coherent oscillations above threshold could be harnessed to implement an autonomous quantum clock
Erker et al. 2017
;
Mitchison 2019
;
Milburn 2020
;
Schwarzhans et al. 2021
;
Pearson et al. 2021
, whose thermodynamics will be analysed in a forthcoming publication.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Fokker-Planck equation

In this Appendix we derive a Fokker-Planck equation for the Wigner function of the mechanical mode, which is equivalent to the Langevin equation (
6
) in the main text. Compressing all the electronic components of the Hamiltonian into a single term,
\hat{H}_{\text{el}}=\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{T}
, we have

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{m\omega_{0}^{2}\hat{x}^{2}}{2}-F\hat{x}\hat{n}+\hat{H}_{\text{el}}.

(13)

We introduce the Wigner transform of the density matrix,

\hat{\rho}(x,p)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\differential y\,\bra{x+y}\hat{\rho}\ket{x-y}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2yp},

(14)

which is an operator-valued function of
x
and
p
that acts on the electronic Hilbert space. The Wigner function of the oscillator follows from
W(x,p)=\Tr[\hat{\rho}(x,p)]
, while the electronic state is given by
\hat{\rho}_{\rm el}=\int\differential x\int\differential p\,\hat{\rho}(x,p)
.

The time evolution of the system follows the Liouville-von Neumann equation
\text{d}\hat{\rho}/\text{d}t=-\mathrm{i}[\hat{H},\rho]
. Using the operator correspondences for the Wigner function
Gardiner and Zoller
, we obtain the corresponding equation of motion

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}_{\rm el}-Fx\hat{n},\hat{\rho}(x,p)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\hat{\rho}(x,p)+m\omega_{0}^{2}x\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\hat{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{F}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left\{\hat{n},\hat{\rho}(x,p)\right\}.

(15)

Physically, the first line represents the evolution of the electronic degrees of freedom conditioned on a particular oscillator position
x
, the second line represents the free mechanical evolution, and the third line represents the force on the oscillator due to the electronic charge.

Now, our key assumption is that the electronic degrees of freedom, evolving under the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm el}-Fx\hat{n}
, relax to a stationary state much faster than the characteristic evolution timescale of the oscillator. This is ensured by the timescale separation of Ineq. (
5
),
which justifies the adiabatic elimination of the electronic degrees of freedom from the mechanical equations of motion. Following the standard procedure
Gardiner 2009
, the elimination is carried by defining the following operators:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}[\hat{H}_{\rm el}-Fx\hat{n},\bullet],

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\left(m\omega_{0}^{2}x-F\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial p}-\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=-\frac{F}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left\{\hat{n}-\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x},\bullet\right\},

(18)

such that
\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}(x,p)=(\mathcal{L}_{x}+\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V})\hat{\rho}(x,p)
. Here,
\langle\bullet\rangle_{x}=\Tr[\bullet\hat{\rho}_{x}]
denotes an average with respect to the electronic stationary state,
\hat{\rho}_{x}
, defined by

\hat{\rho}_{x}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\rho}_{B},

(19)

where
\hat{\rho}_{B}
denotes the initial state of the leads and
\hat{\rho}_{0}
is an arbitrary initial reference state for the electronic system
S
, i.e. the local charge degrees of freedom coupled to the oscillator. Here, we take the leads to be infinitely large and prepared in equilibrium at certain temperatures and chemical potentials, i.e.
\hat{\rho}_{B}\propto\prod_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{\alpha}(\hat{H}_{\alpha}-\mu_{\alpha}\hat{N}_{\alpha})}
, where
\hat{H}_{\alpha}
and
\hat{N}_{\alpha}
are respectively the Hamiltonian and number operator of lead
\alpha
, while
\beta_{\alpha}
and
\mu_{\alpha}
are the corresponding inverse temperatures and chemical potentials. Strictly speaking, the steady-state density operator
\hat{\rho}_{x}
describes the entire system-lead composite and exists only asymptotically (e.g. in the sense of the McLennan-Zubarev ensemble
McLennan 1959
;
Zubarev 1970
). However, we will shortly see that the only relevant properties of
\hat{\rho}_{x}
are those describing the vicinity of the localised charge (in particular, the noise spectrum associated with
\hat{n}
), which can be meaningfully assumed to relax quickly to the stationary state. Note that
\hat{\rho}_{x}
depends on
x
but not on
p
, and satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{x}\hat{\rho}_{x}=0
and
\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{x}]=1
.

Let us now define a projector on the space of operator-valued functions of
x
and
p
, as

\mathcal{P}\hat{A}(x,p)=\Tr\left[\hat{A}(x,p)\right]\hat{\rho}_{x},

(20)

whose orthogonal complement is denoted by
\mathcal{Q}=1-\mathcal{P}
. The projector obeys the relations

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P},

(21a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{x}

\displaystyle=0=\mathcal{L}_{x}\mathcal{P},

(21b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=0,

(21c)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}]

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}_{x}}{\partial x}\Tr[\bullet],

(21d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}]

\displaystyle=0.

(21e)

Eq. (
21a
) is the defining property of a projector, which follows here from the normalisation of
\hat{\rho}_{x}
. The first equality in Eq. (
21b
) follows from the definition (
19
) of
\hat{\rho}_{x}
, while the second equality holds since
\mathcal{L}_{x}
generates a trace-preserving evolution. Eq. (
21c
) follows from the fact that the operator
\hat{n}-\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}
has vanishing mean in the stationary state
\hat{\rho}_{x}
, which itself is independent of
p
. Finally, Eq. (
21d
) can be proved by a direct calculation, while Eq. (
21e
) is satisfied because
\partial\hat{\rho}_{x}/\partial x
is traceless.

Let
\hat{r}=\mathcal{P}\hat{\rho}(x,p)
denote the projected density matrix and
\hat{q}=\mathcal{Q}\hat{\rho}(x,p)
its orthogonal complement. Applying these projectors to the left-hand side of Eq. (
A
), inserting appropriate factors of
1=\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q}
, and using the properties (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{r}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}\hat{r}+\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{q},

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{q}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{x}\hat{q}+\mathcal{Q}\left(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V}\right)\hat{q}+\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V})\hat{r}.

(23)

Since the adiabatic condition implies that
\mathcal{L}_{x}\gg\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}
, we see that
\hat{q}
relaxes quickly to its steady state and can therefore be approximately replaced in Eq. (
22
) by its stationary value. To proceed, we formally integrate Eq. (
23
) to obtain

\displaystyle\hat{q}(t)=\mathcal{G}(t)\hat{q}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\mathcal{G}(t-t^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}\left(\mathcal{H+\mathcal{V}}\right)\hat{r}(t^{\prime}),

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t)=\exp\left\{\left[\mathcal{L}_{x}+\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V})\right]t\right\}.

(25)

We now make the crucial assumption that
\hat{q}(0)=0
, i.e. the electronic degrees of freedom begin in the stationary state
\hat{\rho}_{x}
(or reach it after a negligibly short time). Substituting the solution (
24
) into Eq. (
22
) and shifting the integration variable
t^{\prime}\to t-t^{\prime}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{r}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}\hat{r}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V}\right)\mathcal{G}(t^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}\left(\mathcal{H+\mathcal{V}}\right)\hat{r}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}\hat{r}(t)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime}\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}\left(\mathcal{H+\mathcal{V}}\right)\hat{r}(t).

(26)

On the second line, we have made the following approximations: (i) since
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}\ll\mathcal{L}_{x}
we write
\mathcal{G}(t)\approx\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t}
to lowest non-trivial order in small quantities (Born approximation), (ii) assuming that the integrand decays rapidly as a function of
t^{\prime}
, we approximate
\hat{r}(t-t^{\prime})\approx\hat{r}(t)
and extend the upper integration limit to infinity (Markov approximation).

To further simplify the integrand, we use Eqs. (
21
) and the obvious property
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}=0
to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{V})\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\left([\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}]+\mathcal{H}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{V}\right)\mathcal{Q}\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{H}-[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}]+\mathcal{V})\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{V}\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V}-[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}])\mathcal{P}.

(27)

Tracing over the electronic degrees of freedom finally yields a formal master equation for the Wigner function,
W(x,p)=\Tr[\hat{\rho}(x,p)]
, in the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}W=\mathcal{H}W-\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime}\,\Tr\left[\mathcal{V}\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{V}-[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}]\right)\hat{\rho}_{x}\right]W.

(28)

The first term describes the free evolution of the oscillator while the second term describes diffusion and damping due to the fluctuating charge.

It is straightforward to evaluate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,\Tr\left[\mathcal{V}\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t}\mathcal{V}\hat{\rho}_{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D(x)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}},

(29)

where the diffusion coefficient is given by

D(x)=F^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,\braket{\left\{\delta\hat{n}(t),\delta\hat{n}(0)\right\}}_{x},

(30)

and we defined
\delta\hat{n}(t)=\hat{n}(t)-\braket{\hat{n}}_{x}
as the charge fluctuation operator evaluated in the Heisenberg picture, with
\hat{n}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\dagger}t}\hat{n}
and
\mathcal{L}_{x}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{i}[\hat{H}_{\rm el}-Fx\hat{n},\bullet]
the adjoint Liouvillian. The remaining contribution to Eq. (
28
) is evaluated as

\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,\Tr\left[\mathcal{V}\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{x}t}[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{H}]\hat{\rho}_{x}\right]=\gamma(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial p}p,

(31)

where we defined the damping rate

\gamma(x)=\frac{F}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,\Tr\left[\hat{n}(t)\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}_{x}}{\partial x}\right].

(32)

We express
\gamma(x)
in terms of the charge noise spectrum as follows. First, note that consistency with the Markov approximation requires the integrand of Eq. (
32
) to decay to zero for large times. Any differentiable function
g(t)
that vanishes at infinity,
\lim_{t\to\infty}g(t)=0
, satisfies

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,g(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\int_{t}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime}\,\frac{\differential g}{\differential t^{\prime}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,t\frac{\differential g}{\differential t},

(33)

where the first equality follows from the definition of integration and the second equality follows after switching the order of integration,
\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,\int_{t}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime}\,\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\differential t
, then carrying out the trivial integral over
t
and relabelling dummy variables. For the particular case of
g(t)=\Tr[\hat{n}(t)\partial\hat{\rho}_{x}/\partial x]
, we have
\differential g/\differential t=\Tr[\hat{n}(t)\mathcal{L}_{x}\partial\hat{\rho}_{x}/\partial x]
. By differentiating
\mathcal{L}_{x}\hat{\rho}_{x}
with respect to
x
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}_{x}\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}_{x}}{\partial x}=-\mathrm{i}F[\hat{n},\hat{\rho}_{x}].

(34)

Putting everything together, we get

\gamma(x)=\frac{F^{2}}{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t\,\braket{\mathrm{i}t\left[\hat{n}(t),\hat{n}(0)\right]}_{x}.

(35)

Eqs. (
30
) and (
35
) are equivalent to Eq. (
7
) in the main text.

It is now straightforward to write down the equation of motion for the Wigner function

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left(m\omega_{0}^{2}x-F\braket{\hat{n}}_{x}+\gamma(x)p\right)W-\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{D(x)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial p^{2}}.

(36)

Eq. (
A
) is a genuine Fokker-Planck equation for the Wigner function of the oscillator. Its solution may take negative values if the initial condition for
W(x,p)
is non-classical. However, whenever
W(x,p)\geq 0
it may be interpreted as a probability distribution associated with the Langevin equation (
6
); see Ref.
Gardiner 2009
. The Wigner function is then reconstructed as

W(x^{\prime},p,t)=\mathbb{E}[\delta(x^{\prime}-x(t))\delta(p-m\dot{x}(t))],

(37)

where
\mathbb{E}[\bullet]
denotes an average over Langevin trajectories
x(t)
evolved up to a given time
t
. The Wigner function generates expectation values with symmetric
\hat{x}-\hat{p}
operator ordering
Gardiner and Zoller
, such as

\displaystyle\Tr[\hat{x}\hat{\rho}(t)]=\int\differential x\int\differential p\,xW(x,p,t),

(38)

\displaystyle\Tr[\hat{p}\hat{\rho}(t)]=\int\differential x\int\differential p\,pW(x,p,t).

(39)

The Langevin equation can also be used to reconstruct symmetrised two-time correlation functions, such as
C(t,t+\tau)=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{x}(t+\tau),\hat{x}(t)\}\rangle=\real\langle\hat{x}(t+\tau)\hat{x}(t)\rangle
. Using the Born-Markov assumption and the quantum regression formula
Gardiner and Zoller
, this can be written as

\langle\hat{x}(t+\tau)\hat{x}(t)\rangle=\Tr\left[\hat{x}\mathcal{E}(t+\tau,t)\left(\hat{x}\hat{\rho}(t)\right)\right],

(40)

where
\mathcal{E}(t+\tau,t)
is the time evolution superoperator. In phase space, the expression
\mathcal{E}(t+\tau,t)\left(\hat{x}\hat{\rho}(t)\right)
corresponds to the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation (
A
) with initial condition

\hat{x}\hat{\rho}(t)\to\bar{W}(x,p,t)=xW(x,p)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\frac{\partial W(x,p,t)}{\partial p},

(41)

where
W(x,p,t)
is the Wigner function associated with
\hat{\rho}(t)
. This evolves to

\int\differential x^{\prime}\differential p^{\prime}\Pi(x,p,t+\tau|x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)\bar{W}(x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t),

(42)

with
\Pi(x,p,t+\tau|x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)
the Green function of the Fokker-Planck equation, i.e. its formal solution for a point initial condition
\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(p-p^{\prime})
. Taking the real part of Eq. (
40
) and using Eq. (
38
), we thus obtain

\displaystyle\real\langle\hat{x}(t+\tau)\hat{x}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\differential x\int\differential x^{\prime}\int\differential p\int\differential p^{\prime}\,x\Pi(x,p,t+\tau|x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)x^{\prime}W(x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\int\differential x\int\differential x^{\prime}\,xx^{\prime}P(x,t+\tau;x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[x(t+\tau)x(t)],

(43)

where

P(x,t+\tau;x^{\prime},t)=\int\differential p\!\int\differential p^{\prime}\,\Pi(x,p,t+\tau|x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)W(x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)

(44)

is the joint probability distribution for the random variables
x(t+\tau)
and
x(t)
given that
x(t)
is distributed according to
P(x,t)=\int\differential p\,W(x,p,t)
.

Finally, let us briefly recap the assumptions underlying Eq. (
A
). We assume that the electronic degrees of freedom are initially in the stationary state,
\mathcal{Q}\hat{\rho}(0)=0
, and relax quickly back to this state when perturbed,
\mathcal{L}_{x}\gg\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}
. These assumptions are well satisfied in the quasi-adiabatic regime defined by Eq. (
5
). Eq. (
A
) then follows from a Born-Markov approximation for the projected density matrix (
20
). Note, however, that we make no assumptions about the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
of the electronic system or the operator
\hat{n}
, which could in principle describe an arbitrary interacting system so long as the quasi-adiabatic assumption is satisfied.

Appendix B
Solving for the electronic steady state

To solve the Langevin equation one needs to calculate the occupation
\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}
and the noise spectrum
S_{x}(\omega)
for the central system in its steady state. To achieve this we use an equation of motion approach.

The electronic Hamiltonian conditioned on the oscillator position
x
is defined by

\hat{H}_{x}=\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{T}-Fx\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c},

(45)

where
\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{T}
is given in Eqs. (
1
)–(
3
). We ignore the constant term proportional to
N_{0}
since this merely shifts the zero of energy. Eq. (
45
) describes a resonant-level model comprising a quantum dot coupled to two electrodes, whose analysis can be found in many texts, e.g. Refs.
Ryndyk 2016
;
Schaller 2014
. The Heisenberg equations of motion for this system read

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\hat{c}=-\mathrm{i}\left(\epsilon_{x}\hat{c}+\sum_{\alpha\in L,R}\sum_{k}g_{k\alpha}\hat{d}_{k\alpha}\right),

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\hat{d}_{k\alpha}=-\mathrm{i}\left(\Omega_{k\alpha}\hat{d}_{k\alpha}+g_{k\alpha}\hat{c}\right),

(47)

where
\epsilon_{x}=\epsilon-Fx
. Formally integrating Eq. (
47
) and inserting the result into Eq. (
46
), one finds

\frac{\text{d}\hat{c}}{\text{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\epsilon_{x}\hat{c}(t)+\sum_{\alpha}\left(\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}(t)-\int^{\infty}_{t_{0}}\differential t^{\prime}\chi_{\alpha}(t-t^{\prime})\hat{c}(t^{\prime})\right),

(48)

where we introduced the memory kernel
\chi_{\alpha}(t)
and the noise operator
\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}(t)
, defined by

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(t-t^{\prime})=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\sum_{k}g^{2}_{k\alpha}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{k\alpha}(t-t^{\prime})}

(49)

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}g_{k\alpha}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{k\alpha}(t-t_{0})}\hat{d}_{k\alpha}(t_{0}),

(50)

with
t_{0}
defining the initial time at which the leads are prepared in thermal equilibrium. Since we are interested in the steady state, we take the limit
t_{0}\to-\infty
. Eq. (
48
) is then easily solved in the Fourier domain to obtain

\tilde{c}(\omega)=G(\omega)\tilde{\zeta}(\omega),

(51)

where
G(\omega)
is the retarded Green function

G(\omega)=[\omega-\epsilon_{x}-\tilde{\chi}_{L}(\omega)-\tilde{\chi}_{R}(\omega)]^{-1},

(52)

with
\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}
the self energy of electrode
\alpha=L,R
,

\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\fint\frac{\text{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)}{2},

(53)

where
\fint
is a principal-value integral and
\kappa_{\alpha}
is the spectral density of the quantum dot coupled with the left or right electrode.

The non-equilibrium steady state depends on the statistical properties of the noise operator. The initial state of the reservoirs is assumed to be thermal and uncorrelated, i.e.
\langle\hat{d}_{\alpha,k}^{\dagger}(t_{0})\hat{d}_{\alpha^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(t_{0})\rangle=f_{\alpha}(\Omega_{k})\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
, where
f_{\alpha}(\omega)=(\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{\alpha}(\omega-\mu_{\alpha})}+1)^{-1}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution. The statistics of the noise operator can be found in the frequency domain, where

\tilde{\zeta}_{\alpha}(\omega)=-\sqrt{2\pi}i\sum_{k}g_{k\alpha}\delta(\omega-\Omega_{k\alpha})\hat{d}_{k\alpha}(t_{0}).

(54)

One can calculate the two-point noise spectrum to be

\langle\tilde{\zeta}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)\tilde{\zeta}_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega^{\prime})\rangle=\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})f_{\alpha}(\omega)\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}

(55)

Substituting this back into Eq. (
51
) of the dot

\langle\tilde{c}^{\dagger}(\omega)\tilde{c}(\omega^{\prime})\rangle=\sum_{\alpha\in L,R}A(\omega)\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)f(\omega),

(56)

where
A(\omega)=|G(\omega)|^{2}
is the spectral function. To find the steady state occupation we evaluate the Fourier transform at the time
t=t^{\prime}
to obtain

\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\rangle_{x}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\alpha\in L,R}\int A(\omega)\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)f_{\alpha}(\omega).

(57)

For the parameters we consider, with
\tfrac{1}{2}(\mu_{L}+\mu_{R})=\epsilon
so that the dot is half-filled on average, this yields
N_{0}=\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\rangle_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}
and thus
\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}=\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\rangle_{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}
.

To find the dissipation rate and diffusion coefficient one needs to calculate the charge noise spectrum

S_{x}(\omega)=F^{2}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\text{d}t\,e^{i\omega t}\left[\langle\hat{n}(t)\hat{n}(0)\rangle_{x}-\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}^{2}\right].

(58)

One thus needs to calculate the two-point correlation function of the operator
\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}
:

\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{c}(t)\hat{c}^{\dagger}(0)\hat{c}(0)\rangle

(59)

We proceed using a similar method to the occupation function using a Green’s Function approach

\displaystyle\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{c}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{c}^{\dagger}(\upsilon)\hat{c}(\upsilon^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle G^{*}(\omega)G(\omega^{\prime})G^{*}(\upsilon)G(\upsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\zeta}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{\zeta}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\zeta}^{\dagger}(\upsilon)\hat{\zeta}(\upsilon^{\prime})\rangle

(60)

To calculate
\langle\hat{\zeta}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{\zeta}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\zeta}^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime\prime})\hat{\zeta}(\omega^{\prime\prime\prime})\rangle
we make use of Wick’s theorem using the identity

\displaystyle\langle\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}\hat{d}_{k^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{j\beta}\hat{d}_{j^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\delta_{k^{\prime}k}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}f(\Omega_{k})\delta_{j^{\prime}j}\delta_{\beta^{\prime}\beta}f(\Omega_{j})+

\displaystyle\delta_{kj^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\beta^{\prime}}f(\Omega_{k})\delta_{k^{\prime}j}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta}\left[1-f(\Omega_{j})\right]

(61)

The first term in this expression is equivalent to the product of the means and will cancel with the contribution from
\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}^{2}
in Eq. (
58
). The second term in equation (
B
) is unique to the four-point correlation function, and its contribution to
\langle\hat{\zeta}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{\zeta}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\zeta}^{\dagger}(\upsilon)\hat{\zeta}(\upsilon^{\prime})\rangle
is

\displaystyle 4\pi^{2}\sum_{k}\sum_{j}

\displaystyle|g_{k\alpha}|^{2}|g_{j\beta}|^{2}f_{\alpha}(\Omega_{k})\left[1-f_{\beta}(\Omega_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\,\delta(\omega+\upsilon^{\prime})\delta(\omega^{\prime}+\upsilon)\delta(\omega-\Omega_{k\alpha})\delta(\omega^{\prime}-\Omega_{j\beta})

(62)

Using the same method as the two point correlation function, the contribution to
\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{c}(\omega^{\prime})\hat{c}^{\dagger}(\upsilon)\hat{c}(\upsilon^{\prime})\rangle
is therefore

\displaystyle A(\omega)A(\omega^{\prime})\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)\kappa_{\beta}(\upsilon)f_{\alpha}(\omega)[1-f_{\beta}(\upsilon)]\delta(\omega+\upsilon^{\prime})\delta(\omega^{\prime}+\upsilon).

(63)

Performing a Fourier transform, change of variables and letting
s=s^{\prime}=0
and
t=t^{\prime}
one arrives at the result

\displaystyle\langle\hat{n}(t)\hat{n}(0)\rangle_{x}-\langle\hat{n}\rangle_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\text{d}\omega\text{d}\omega^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}A(\omega+\omega^{\prime})A(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\kappa_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})f_{\alpha}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\left[1-f_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\right].

(64)

Inserting this into Eq. (
58
) one finds

\displaystyle S_{x}(\omega)=

\displaystyle F^{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\text{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}A(\omega+\omega^{\prime})A(\omega^{\prime})\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\kappa_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})[1-f_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})]

(65)

From Eq. (
7
) in the main text, the diffusion coefficient is therefore

D(x)=F^{2}\!\!\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in L,R}\int\frac{\text{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}A(\omega^{\prime})^{2}\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})\kappa_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})f_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})[1-f_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})],

(66)

while the dissipation rate for the system is

\displaystyle m\gamma(x)=

\displaystyle F^{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int\frac{\differential\omega}{2\pi}A(\omega)\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)f_{\alpha}(\omega)

\displaystyle\times\,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\omega^{\prime}}\left(\kappa_{\beta}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})A(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\left[1-f_{\beta}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\right]\right)\Bigg\rvert_{\omega^{\prime}=0}

(67)

One can thus perform these calculations for any system given the spectral density
\kappa_{\alpha}(\omega)
. For the Lorentzian spectral density (
9
) the electrode self energy is given by

\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha}(\omega-\omega_{0\alpha})}{2\left[(\omega-\omega_{0\alpha})^{2}+\delta_{\alpha}^{2}\right]}-i\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha}^{2}}{2\left[(\omega-\omega_{0\alpha})^{2}+\delta_{\alpha}^{2}\right]}.

(68)

This is used in (
52
) to calculate the spectral function of the system.

Appendix C
Numerical Simulation of the Langevin Equation

The Langevin equation, equation 7 in text, was solved by using the stochastic Euler method. The space for the occupation, fluctuation and dissipation graphs were calculated using the formulae derived in appendix B. From these graphs it is possible to solve the Langevin equation using a stochastic Euler method
Gardiner 2004
;
Jacobs 2010
. A time-step
\Delta t
was selected, the system was evolved to a time
t+\Delta t
using the values of the oscillator at the position at time t. The evolution step from time
t
to time
t+\Delta t
is given by

\displaystyle v(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=v(t)+\left(-\omega_{0}^{2}x(t)-\gamma[x(t)]v(t)+\langle n\rangle[x(t)]\right)\Delta t

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{D[x(t)]}\Delta W,

(69)

\displaystyle x(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=x(t)+v(t)\Delta t,

(70)

where
\Delta W
is a Gaussian variable with mean 0 and variance
\Delta t
. Once an initial state of the oscillator is selected, an array of velocity and position values can be generated. From this array we can construct the Wigner Functions seen in Figure
2
of the main text.

For each bias

Appendix D
The Wigner Function

The Wigner function used throughout the paper represents a quasi-probability distribution similar to phase space in classical mechanics. Phase space explores ensembles of trajectories, while the Wigner space explores quantum probabilities. However, Wigner quasi-probability distributions can take negative values setting them apart from their classical counterparts. The Wigner function is given as the Fourier Transform of the symmetric characteristic function
Barnett and Radmore 2016

W(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d^{2}\xi\chi(\xi)\text{exp}(\alpha\xi^{*}-\alpha^{*}\xi),

(71)

where
\alpha
is a complex number with real position and imaginary momentum of the oscillator. The symmetric characteristic function (
\chi
), given by

\chi(\xi)=\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}\hat{D}(\xi)\right]

(72)

where
\hat{D}(x)=\text{exp}(x\hat{a}^{\dagger}-x^{*}\hat{a})
is the Glauber displacement operator. The Wigner function acts as the closest parallel to the outcome of measurements of the
\hat{p}
and
\hat{q}
operator, thus one can set the initial state of the system and evolve the system to steady-state using the probability distribution as the Wigner Function of the system.

Once we have the steady state Wigner function, it is important to reconstruct the state back in density matrix formalism to evaluate the thermodynamics of the system. The calculations to convert the Wigner Function to the density matrix can be found in reference
Paternostro et al. 2009
. The formula for each element of the the density matrix
\rho=\gamma_{n,m}\ket{n}\bra{m}
is given by

\gamma_{n,m}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}\zeta\chi(\zeta)f_{nm}(\zeta),

(73)

where
\chi
is the characteristic function of the system and
f_{nm}(\zeta)=\bra{n}\hat{D}(-\zeta)\ket{m}
. When n>m this is given by

f_{nm}=\sqrt{\frac{m!}{n!}}(-\zeta)^{n-m}e^{-|\zeta|^{2}/2}\mathcal{L}^{n-m}_{m}(|\zeta|^{2}),

(74)

where
\mathcal{L}^{n-m}_{m}(|\zeta|^{2})
is the generalised Laguerre polynomial of degree m and argument
|\zeta|^{2}
. For the case when m=n this simplifies to

f_{nn}=e^{-|\zeta|^{2}/2}\mathcal{L}^{0}_{n}(|\zeta|^{2}).

(75)

To convert the Wigner Function to the Characteristic function one needs to perform an inverse Fourier transform

\chi(\zeta)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\text{d}\alpha W(\alpha)\text{exp}(-\alpha\zeta*+\alpha*\zeta).

(76)

For all the systems in consideration in this paper the Wigner function appears to be radially symmetric, this simplifies the calculations for the density matrix in two ways: The original integral for the Wigner Function can be calculated in polar coordinates with the angular component able to be evaluated analytically. The set of equations then simplifies down to

\chi(r)=2\pi\int duu\mathcal{J}_{0}(2ru)W(u),

(77)

where u is the radial value for the second integral,
\mathcal{J}_{0}
is the Bessel function of the first kind and r is the radial value of the generated Wigner function.

The second simplification is that the off diagonal components for the density matrix are all 0, ie there are no coherences in the energy eigenbasis. This is due to the rotational symmetry of the system inferring that the density matrix of the system is not evolving over time. Because of these simplifying factors the density matrix could be calculated very quickly for a large number of terms for the system. The full equation describing the transform is therefore given by

\rho_{nn}=8\pi\int\int dudru\mathcal{J_{0}}(2ru)W(u)re^{-|r|^{2}/2}\mathcal{L}^{0}_{n}(r^{2}).

(78)

Fluctuation, Dissipation and Occupation Functions

The fluctuation, dissipation and occupations functions were numerically calculated using the formulae outlined in appendix
B
. As shown in Fig.
6
at equilibrium the ratio between the fluctuation and dissipation is 4 for all positions, this implies the fluctuation dissipation theorem (FDT),
D(x)=2m\gamma(x)/\beta
, is satisfied. This highlights that at equilibrium
\gamma(x)>0
. The FDT arises due to the detailed balance condition,
S_{x}(-\omega)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\omega}S_{x}(+\omega)
. Out of equilibrium we require an energy dependent spectral density to achieve negative damping as outlined in
Bennett and Clerk 2006
. We achieve this using a Lorentzian spectral density giving rise to the occupation, fluctuation and dissipation curves seen in Fig.
5
. Once a threshold voltage is reached, a region of the dissipation curve becomes negative leading to the lasing state of the system.

The effect of temperature is shown in Fig.
7
. As the temperature of the leads are increased (
\beta
is decreased), the negative dissipation is mollified. As the temperature is increased the Fermi distributions of the leads become increasingly similar, the effect of this is to cause the excess charge on the oscillator to be closer to 0 for all values of the position as well as significantly weakening the strength of the negative dissipation as well as reducing the region of negative damping.
