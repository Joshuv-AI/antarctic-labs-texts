On Value Iteration Convergence in Connected MDPs
Arsenii Mustafin, Alex Olshevsky, Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis

Abstract
This paper establishes that an MDP with a unique optimal policy and ergodic associated transition matrix ensures the convergence of various versions of the Value Iteration algorithm at a geometric rate that exceeds the discount factor γ \gamma for both discounted and average-reward criteria.



1
Introduction

Markov decision process is a common mathematical tool to model multi-step iteration with an action-reward system, which draws increased attention due to recent popularity of Reinforcement learning. Value iteration and policy iteration are two basic algorithms which are used to solve MDP, or to find optimal optimal policy. Two algorithms are different in their nature: policy iteration finds optimal policy exact optimal policy, but requires more operations every iteration of the algorithm. Value iteration find approximate optimal policy up to desired accuracy
\epsilon
, every iteration of this algorithm requires less operations, but overall number of iterations depends on
\epsilon
. In general, value iteration converges geometrically at a rate determined by the discount factor
\gamma
. An example where this convergence rate is exact can be provided. Faster convergence can occur under additional assumptions, as shown in
[
3
]
, Section 6.6.6, or in
[
1
]
.

1.1
Motivation and contribution

In this paper, we aim to analyze the additional convergence of the Value Iteration algorithm beyond the theoretically guaranteed convergence with discount factor
\gamma
. An example of this convergence is shown in Figure
1
.

We show that under the fairly general assumptions of an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain implied by a unique optimal policy (Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
), value iteration exhibits geometric convergence at a rate faster than
\gamma
. This additional convergence factor reduces the computational complexity required to achieve an accuracy of
\epsilon
from:

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log{1/\epsilon}+\log(1/(1-\gamma))}{\log{(1/\gamma)}}\right)\quad\text{to}\quad\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)+\log(1/(1-\gamma))}{\log(1/\gamma)+\log(1/\tau)/N}\right)

in the discounted reward criteria case (
\gamma<1
).

Additionally, we establish geometric convergence in the average total reward case with a total complexity of

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log{(1/\epsilon)}}{\log{(1/\tau)}/N}\right).

Convergence of the value iteration algorithm on a random MDP with different discount rates. It can be seen that as
\gamma
approaches one, the convergence rate remains geometric with a rate less than
\gamma
.

We consider a discounted reward Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the finite set of states,
\mathcal{A}
is the finite set of actions,
\mathcal{P}={P(s^{\prime}|s,a)}_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}
represents the transition probabilities,
r
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount rate. We denote the reward received during the transition from
s
to
s^{\prime}
as
r(s,s^{\prime})
and the expected reward received after taking action
a
in state
s
as
r(s,a)
. A deterministic policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
maps states to actions. Given a policy, we can define a state value function, which is equal to

V^{\pi}(s):=\E\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},\pi(s_{t}))|s_{0}=s\right]

in discounted infinite horizon reward criteria and

V^{\pi}(s):=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\E\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}r(s_{t},\pi(s_{t}))|s_{0}=s\right]

in average total reward criteria.

A policy
\pi^{*}
is called optimal if it maximizes the values of all states. A policy
\pi^{\epsilon}
is called
\epsilon
-optimal if the state values implied by
\pi
differ from the optimal state values by less than a positive real number
\epsilon
:

V^{*}(s)-V^{\pi}(s)\leq\epsilon,\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

The Value Iteration Algorithm
1
is one of the algorithms used to find an
\epsilon
-optimal policy. The pseudocode presents a general version of the algorithm, and in the paper, we analyze three versions of it:

•

Synchronous without learning rate
(classical value iteration algorithm):
\alpha=1
and every action is updated at every step,
\mathcal{S}{t}=\mathcal{S};\forall t
.

•

Synchronous with learning rate
:
\alpha\in(0,1)
and every action is updated at every step,
\mathcal{S}{t}=\mathcal{S};\forall t
.

•

Asynchronous with learning rate
:
\alpha\in(0,1)
and the set of actions updated at each step is different.

The choice of stopping criteria
H
depends on the reward criteria. We aim to show that under the assumption that the Markov Reward process implied by the optimal policy is irreducible and aperiodic, all algorithms exhibit geometric convergence with a rate smaller than the discount factor
\gamma
.

In our analysis, we show convergence in terms of the span of an error vector
\text{span}(e)
, which is a vector of differences between current values
V
and optimal values
V^{*}
:

\textrm{sp}(e)=\max{(e)}-\min{(e)},\quad e=V-V^{*}.

Lemma 3.5

For the consecutive value vectors

\textrm{sp}(V_{t}-V_{t+1})\leq\textrm{sp}(e_{t})(1+\gamma)

(1)

Proof.

We denote two states where vector

\displaystyle\textrm{sp}(V_{t}-V_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\textrm{sp}(e_{t}-e_{t+1})=\max_{s}(e_{t}(s)-e_{t+1}(s))-\min_{s}(e_{t}(s)-e_{t+1}(s))\leq

\displaystyle\leq(e_{t}(\overline{s})-e_{t+1}(\underline{s}^{\prime}))-(e_{t}(\underline{s})-e_{t+1}(\overline{s}^{\prime}))=\textrm{sp}(e_{t})+\textrm{sp}(e_{t+1})\leq

\displaystyle\leq(1+\gamma)\textrm{sp}(e_{t})

3.2
Synchronous with learning rate

As we can see from Subsection
3.1
, there are two main sources of error vector span convergence: it is shrinking by discount factor
\gamma
and reverts to the mean due to mixing properties of the stochastic matrix
P_{t}^{\prime}
. In this section we show that by adding learning rate we introduce a trade off between these two sources of convergence: by increasing learning rate part of part of
\gamma
convergence is being "sacrificed" to provide faster information exchange between states and bolster the mean reversion.

One immediate result of introducing the learning rate is that now it is guaranteed, that under MRP produced by optimal policy a number of updates
N_{\alpha}
required to guarantee that every state affects every other state is at most
n-1
.

3.1
and
3.2
hold, span of the error vector obtained after
n

\alpha\in(0,1)
has the following property:

\textrm{sp}(e_{N_{\alpha}})\leq\gamma^{N_{\alpha}}\tau_{\alpha}\textrm{sp}(e_{0}),

\gamma^{N_{\alpha}}\tau_{\alpha}\in(0,1)
.

Proof of this theorem is similar to the proof of Theorem

3.3
Asynchronous with learning rate

The convergence analysis of asynchronous algorithm is harder than previous cases due to its dependence on choices of states
\mathcal{S}_{t}
every iteration. For the algorithm to converge we need to guarantee meaningful choice strategy. Otherwise, for example when only a few states being updated infinite number of times, while others are not being updated at all, convergence cannot be achieved. To guarantee proper state selection we make a following assumption:

Assumption 3.11
.

There exists a natural number
B
such that during every
B
updates every state is being updated at least
n
times.

Also, if in the synchronous update case contraction factor
\gamma
being applied to all states simultaneously and, thus, decreases a span of a error vector, in asynchronous case it might be increasing, if algorithm prioritize choice of some states compare to others. Thus, two factors,
\gamma
-contraction and mean reversion via information exchange, which previously were both contributing to faster convergence, now might work in opposite direction: due to unbalanced application of the updates,
\gamma
-contraction might increase the span of the error vector. In this case dynamics of error vector span might be described by the following theorem:

Theorem 3.12
.

Convergence of the
Asynchronous algorithm with a learning rate:
If Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.11
hold, span of the error vector obtained after
B
steps of asynchronous Value Iteration algorithm with learning rate
\alpha\in(0,1)
has the following property:

\textrm{sp}(e_{B})\leq\gamma^{N}\tau^{\prime}\textrm{sp}(e_{0})+(1-\gamma^{B})\min(e_{0}),

where
\gamma^{N}\tau^{\prime}\in(0,1)
.

This Theorem immediately gives a convergence result in average reward case stated in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.13
.

In average reward criteria case set the algorithm stopping criteria
H
equal to
\epsilon
. Then asynchronous Value iteration algorithm with learning rate will output
\epsilon
-optimal policy after at most:

\frac{\log{1/\epsilon}+\log 2-\log{\textrm{sp}(e_{0})}}{\log{(1/\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime})}/B_{\alpha}}

iterations, which results in total iteration complexity of

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log{(1/\epsilon)}}{\log{(1/\tau_{\alpha}^{\prime})}/B_{\alpha}}\right).

(6)

At the same time, the term
(1-\gamma^{B})\min(e_{0})
does not allow to establish the convergence guarantees in discounted case using the same proof strategy.

Proofs

4.1
Key idea of the proofs

Let’s analyze a single update on a single state value.

\displaystyle v_{t+1}(s)

\displaystyle=v^{*}(s)+e_{t+1}(s)=r(s,\pi_{t}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})v_{t}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r(s,\pi_{t}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})(v^{*}(s^{\prime})+e_{t}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=r(s,\pi_{t}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})v^{*}(s^{\prime})+r(s,\pi^{*}(s))-r(s,\pi^{*}(s))

\displaystyle+\gamma\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})v^{*}(s^{\prime})-\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})v^{*}(s^{\prime})\right)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=v^{*}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})+

\displaystyle\underbrace{r(s,\pi_{t}(s))-r(s,\pi^{*}(s))+\gamma\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})v^{*}(s^{\prime})-\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})v^{*}(s^{\prime})\right)}_{\Delta(a)}

\displaystyle\implies e_{t+1}(s)\leq\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})

(7)

with the equality achieved when action
a=\pi_{t}(s)=\pi^{*}(s)
. Note, that the term
\Delta(a)
corresponds to the loss caused by one time choice of action
a
instead of optimal action in state
s
, in the recent literature it is called an
advantage
of policy
\pi_{t}
to
\pi^{*}
. The inequality carries the ideas of convergence of error vector span. Firstly, because non-increasing and non-decreasing properties of stochastic matrix, span will be contracting by
\gamma
each iteration (if
\gamma<1
). Secondly, the convergence will follow from mixing properties of matrix
P^{*}
if the optimal action is chosen and from additional term
\Delta(a)\geq\delta
otherwise.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
3.4

First, we derive a lower bound for a new error vector:

\displaystyle v_{t+1}(s)

\displaystyle=v^{*}(s)+e_{t+1}(s)=r(s,\pi_{t}(a))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{t}(s,s^{\prime})v_{t}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq r(s,\pi^{*}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})v_{t}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r(s,\pi^{*}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})(v^{*}(s^{\prime})+e_{t}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=r(s,\pi^{*}(s))+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})v^{*}(s^{\prime})+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\implies e_{t+1}(s)\geq\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})

(8)

Combining Inequalities
8
and
7
and them rewriting in matrix form we yield:

\displaystyle\gamma P^{*}e_{t}\leq e_{t+1}\leq\gamma P_{t}e_{t}

(9)

Since all elements of
e_{t+1}
lie between two quantities, it might be written as a convex combination of those quantities, or:

\displaystyle e_{t+1}=\gamma[D_{t}P^{*}+(I-D_{t})P_{t}]e_{t}=\gamma P_{t}^{\prime}e_{t},

(10)

where
D_{t}
is a diagonal matrix. We now analyze diagonal entries of
D_{t}
. As it was shown in Subsection
4.1
, in the case when for state
s_{i}
vector
v_{i}
implies an optimal actions (thus,
P^{*}e_{t}=P_{t}e_{t}
), we choose
D_{t}(i,i)=1
. In the other case, recall that the second inequality is strict only when action choice is optimal, otherwise value of
e_{t+1}(s_{i})
will be smaller by at least
\delta
:

e_{t+1}(s_{i})\leq\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})-\gamma\delta,

which, in turn, implies that the value of
D_{t}(i,i)
cannot be zero. We can derive the lower bound on it as (for simplicity we denote
\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s,s^{\prime})e_{t}(s^{\prime})
as
P(s)e_{t}
for any transition matrix
P
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}e_{t+1}(s)=\gamma(D_{t}(i,i)P^{*}(s)e_{t}+(1-D_{t}(i,i))P_{t}(s)e_{t})\\
e_{t+1}(s_{i})\leq\gamma P_{t}(s)e_{t}-\gamma\delta\end{cases}\implies

\displaystyle D_{t}(i,i)\gamma(P_{t}(s)e_{t}-P^{*}(s)e_{t})\geq\gamma\delta\implies

\displaystyle\forall t,i:D_{t}(i,i)\geq\frac{\delta}{P_{t}(s)e_{t}-P^{*}(s)e_{t}}\geq\frac{\delta}{\textrm{sp}(e_{t})}\geq\frac{\delta}{\textrm{sp}(e_{0})}

Having the bound on
D_{t}(i,i)
we can establish that for any two states
s,s^{\prime}
if the transition under optimal policy has non-zero probability
P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})
, this transition will also have non-zero probability in matrix
P^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle P_{t}^{\prime}(s,s^{\prime})\geq\frac{\delta(1-\gamma)}{r_{\rm max}-r_{\rm min}}\min_{s,s^{\prime}}(P^{*}(s,s^{\prime}))=\delta^{\prime}.

(11)

Thus, the result of
N
steps of value iteration algorithm in terms of error vector might be expressed as:

e_{N}=\gamma^{N}P_{N}^{\prime}P_{N-1}^{\prime}\dots P_{1}^{\prime}e_{0}.

Every entry of
e_{N}(s)
might be expressed as convex combination of entries of
e_{0}
(we denote coefficients
\lambda
), where each coefficient
\lambda_{s^{\prime}}
represents a sum of probabilities of patches
u
from state
s^{\prime}
to
s
of length
N
, denoted as
U_{N}(s^{\prime},s)
. Since the matrix
P^{*N}
is positive, for any two states
s_{i},s_{j}
there exists at least one path
u_{ij}:(s_{i},s_{u_{1}},\dots,s_{u_{N-1}},s_{j})
of length
N
, such that of any of two consecutive entries
s_{p_{i}}
and
s_{p_{i+1}}
the transition from first to second will have non-zero probability,
i.e.

P^{*}(s_{p_{i}},s_{p_{i+1}})>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle e_{N}(s)=

\displaystyle\gamma^{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{s^{\prime}}e_{0}(s^{\prime})=\gamma^{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\sum_{u\in U_{N}(s^{\prime},s)}P(u)\right)e_{0}(s^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\gamma^{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\sum_{u\in U_{N}(s^{\prime},s)}\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}P_{i+1}^{\prime}(s_{u_{i}},s_{u_{i+1}})\right)e_{0}(s^{\prime})

Earlier we established two facts: that for every state
s^{\prime}
a paths of length
N
from
s^{\prime}
to
s
,
i.e.

\prod_{i=1}^{N}P^{*}(s_{u_{i}},s_{u_{i+1}})>0
. The second fact is that for any two states
s,s^{\prime}
and time step
t
,
P^{*}(s,s^{\prime})>0\implies P_{t}^{\prime}(s,s^{\prime})\geq\delta^{\prime}
. Combining these facts together we have that every coefficient
\lambda_{s^{\prime}}\geq\delta^{\prime N}
. Thus we can rewrite the error representation as:

\displaystyle e_{N}(s)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{s^{\prime}}e_{0}(s^{\prime})=\gamma^{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\delta^{\prime N}e_{0}(s^{\prime})+(\lambda_{s^{\prime}}-\delta^{\prime N})e_{0}(s^{\prime})=

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}n\delta^{\prime N}\overline{e}_{0}+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}(\lambda_{s^{\prime}}-\delta^{\prime N})e_{0}(s^{\prime}),

where
\overline{e}_{0}
denotes the mean of vector
e_{0}
. This implies

\displaystyle\gamma^{N}(n\delta^{\prime N}\overline{e}_{0}+(1-n\delta^{\prime N})\min(e_{0}))\leq e_{N}(s)\leq\gamma^{N}(n\delta^{\prime N}\overline{e}_{0}+(1-n\delta^{\prime N})\max(e_{0}))\implies

\displaystyle\textrm{sp}(e_{N})\leq\gamma^{N}(1-n\delta^{\prime N})\max(e_{0})-\gamma^{N}(1-n\delta^{\prime N})\min(e_{0})=\gamma^{N}(1-n\delta^{\prime N})\textrm{sp}(e_{0})

Defining
\tau=(1-n\delta^{\prime N})
concludes the proof.
Q.E.D.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyzed the convergence of the Value Iteration algorithm. We show that under the assumption of sufficient mixing provided by the optimal policy, the Value Iteration algorithm exhibits geometric convergence at a rate faster than the discount factor
\gamma
.
