Limit Laws for Critical Dispersion on Complete Graphs
Umberto De Ambroggio, Tamás Makai, Konstantinos Panagiotou, Annika Steibel

Abstract
We consider a synchronous process of particles moving on the vertices of a graph G G , introduced by Cooper, McDowell, Radzik, Rivera and Shiraga (2018). Initially, M M particles are placed on a vertex of G G . In subsequent time steps, all particles that are located on a vertex inhabited by at least two particles jump independently to a neighbour chosen uniformly at random. The process ends at the first step when no vertex is inhabited by more than one particle; we call this (random) time step the dispersion time . In this work we study the case where G G is the complete graph on n n vertices and the number of particles is M = n / 2 + α ​ n 1 / 2 + o ⁡ ( n 1 / 2 ) M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2}) , α ∈ ℝ \alpha\in\mathbb{R} . This choice of M M corresponds to the critical window of the process, with respect to the dispersion time. We show that the dispersion time, if rescaled by n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} , converges in p p -th mean, as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty and for any p ∈ ℝ p\in\mathbb{R} , to a continuous and almost surely positive random variable T α T_{\alpha} . We find that T α T_{\alpha} is the absorption time of a standard logistic branching process, thoroughly investigated by Lambert (2005), and we determine its expectation. In particular, in the middle of the critical window we show that 𝔼 ⁡ [ T 0 ] = π 3 / 2 / 7 \mathbb{E}[T_{0}]=\pi^{3/2}/\sqrt{7} , and furthermore we formulate explicit asymptotics when | α | |\alpha| gets large that quantify the transition into and out of the critical window. We also study the (random) total number of jumps that are performed by the particles until the dispersion time is reached. In particular, we prove that it centers around 2 7 ​ n ​ ln ⁡ n \frac{2}{7}n\ln n and that it has variations linear in n n , whose distribution we can describe explicitly. Introduction

The
dispersion process
introduced by Cooper, McDowell, Radzik, Rivera and Shiraga
[
4

G

happy

unhappy
otherwise.
Initially,
M\geq 2

G

Subsequently, at discrete time steps, all unhappy particles move
simultaneously
and
independently
to a neighbouring vertex selected uniformly at random, while the happy particles remain in place. The process terminates at the first time step at which all particles are happy; we call this (random) time step the
dispersion time
.

It is clear that if the number of particles is small – compared to the number of vertices in the graph – then the dispersion time should be small as well. Intuitively, increasing the number of particles makes it more and more difficult for the particles to disperse quickly.
This transition from ’fast’ to ’slow’ dispersion is quite well-understood and sharp when the underlying graph is the complete graph on
n
vertices with loops, in which case we write
T_{n,M}
for the dispersion time started with
M

The typical order of

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
M=M(n)=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\delta>0

T_{n,M,\delta}\coloneqq\inf\{t>0:U_{t}\leq n^{1/2}/\delta\}

\Big(n^{-1/2}\,U_{T_{n,M,\delta}+\lfloor sn^{1/2}\rfloor}\Big)_{s\geq 0}\to X,

dX_{s}=\left(2\alpha X_{s}-\frac{7}{4}X_{s}^{2}\right)ds+\sqrt{X_{s}}dB_{s},\penalty\ \penalty\ s>0,\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{0}=\delta^{-1}.

(1)

For more background on SDEs in general and the specific equation encountered here we refer to Section
2
. Let us mention only that stochastic processes satisfying (
1
) are well-studied and are also called in the literature

or
Feller diffusions with logistic growth

dX_{s}=(aX_{s}-cX_{s}^{2})ds+\sqrt{\gamma X_{s}}dB_{s},\;s>0,\text{ with initial condition }X_{0}=x\geq 0,

(2)

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
. They appear in the context of population dynamics and stochastically extend the deterministic logistic growth model that describes the evolution of a population under the influences of natural birth, mortality and inter-individual competition. A prime source on the topic is Lambert
[
13
]
, who provides a thorough and detailed discussion of the properties of solutions to (
2
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all sufficiently large

\mathbb{P}\big(T_{n,M}\leq n^{1/2}/Ac_{\alpha}\big)\leq e^{-A}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\big(T_{n,M}>Ac_{\alpha}n^{1/2}\big)\leq e^{-A},\qquad A\geq 1.

Together with Theorem
1.1
we readily obtain convergence in

n^{-p/2}\mathbb{E}\big[T_{n,M}^{p}\big]\sim\mathbb{E}\big[T_{\alpha}^{p}\big],\quad p\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

\mathbb{E}[T_{0}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[n^{-1/2}T_{n,n/2+o(n^{1/2})}\big]=\frac{\pi^{3/2}}{\sqrt{7}},

which is in the interval
2.104\pm 0.001

Our second main result addresses the total number of jumps
\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}
performed by the particles until the dispersion time is reached.

.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
M=M(n)=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}

n^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}-\frac{2}{7}n\ln n\bigg)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}A_{\alpha}.

, denotes the error function.

Regarding the definition and the actual value of

, see also Remark

.

Variations on the Theme.

Our work opens up opportunities for studying a variety of models that are related to the dispersion process or extensions of it. In a general setting,
happiness
can be defined as a property of individual vertices and particles. More specifically, each vertex may have a
capacity
, which, if exceeded, deems all particles on that vertex as unhappy. On the other side, each particle
p
may have a
stress level
, which dictates an upper bound on the particles that share a vertex with
p
so that
p
is still happy. We leave it as an open problem to study the precise behavior in a general setting, where for example the empirical distributions of the capacities and the stress levels fulfill appropriate convergence properties.

In a different line of research it would be challenging to provide detailed studies of dispersion processes on graphs different than the complete graph. We believe, for example, that our results also hold if the underlying graph is a sufficiently dense Erdős-Rényi random graph, which is obtained by retaining independently each edge of the complete graph on
n
vertices with probability
p
. In particular, if, say,
p=\omega(n^{-1/2})
, guaranteeing that the minimum degree is much larger than
n^{1/2}
, then similar results as in Theorem
1.1
should hold, as the process finishes after
O(n^{1/2})
rounds if the graph is complete. However, it might be the case that even on much sparser graphs the behavior does not change (since, for example, in most steps just an
O(n^{1/2})
number of particles move). We consider it as an important and eminent challenge to study the effect of the edge probability
p
on the distribution of the dispersion time.

Related work.

The aforementioned paper
[
10
]
studies, apart from complete graphs, the dispersion time on several other families, like paths, trees, grids, hypercubes and Cayley
graphs. The dispersion process was also studied by Frieze and Pegden
[
10
]
, who considered the
dispersion distance
on the infinite line
L_{\infty}
. They showed that the dispersion distance is
\Theta(n)
when there are
n
particles in the system, improving upon previous results in
[
4
]
. A similar setup was considered by Shang
[
17
]
, who studied the dispersion distance on
L_{\infty}
in a non-uniform dispersion process.

Processes where particles move on the vertices of a graph have been widely studied over the past decades; we refer to
[
4
]
for references. Concerning processes whose scope is to
disperse
particles on discrete structures, arguably the best known such model is Internal Diffusion Limited Aggregation, see
[
7
,
14
,
3
]
. There, particles sequentially start (one at a time) from a specific vertex designated as the origin.
Each particle moves randomly until it finds an unoccupied vertex; then it occupies it forever and it does not move at subsequent steps.
Another well-studied class of models are Activated Random Walks that evolve on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see
[
16
]
for an extensive review. Roughly speaking, we place particles on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and some of them are initially active while others are asleep. The rules of the process are then as follows. Whenever a particle is alone on a vertex, it falls asleep with a certain rate. On the other hand, active particles jump according to independent random choices, and whenever they encounter a particle that is asleep, they wake it up.

In the remainder of the section we provide a formal definition of the model that we study, then we fix some notation, and eventually we conclude with a brief outline of the paper.

Outline.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section we give some basic background on SDEs and present the main tool, diffusion approximation, that we use to study the process of the number of unhappy particles. Moreover, we take a closer look at the logistic branching processes and collect the properties that will be relevant here; our main source is the thorough study

In order to apply the diffusion approximation framework it is necessary to study the

2
Probabilistic Preliminaries

In this section we collect several probabilistic facts that we will use: diffusion approximation, properties of the logistic Feller diffusion and martingale inequalities.

2.1
Diffusion Approximation

A main tool that we will use in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the concept of
diffusion approximation
, which allows us to approximate a sequence
(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Markov chains with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
{d\in\mathbb{N}}
, by a continuous-time stochastic process. More specifically, we examine convergence properties of
(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to a process satisfying a stochastic differential equation (SDE)

d\mathbf{X}_{s}=b(\mathbf{X}_{s})ds+\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{s})d\mathbf{B}_{s},\quad s>0,

(6)

where
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are suitable functions and
\mathbf{B}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.
In this section we provide an overview of the necessary results from stochastic calculus.
Additionally, we collect some properties of the limit process that will emerge within the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
In what follows we denote discrete time by
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(so, for example,
\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}=(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
), whereas
s\geq 0
represents continuous time. Moreover, for all
i,j\in[d]
, the subscript
i
denotes the
i
-th component of a
d
-dimensional vector and the subscript
ij
refers to the entry in row
i
and column
j
of a
d\times d
-dimensional matrix.

Let us consider (
6
).
A
(weak) solution to (
6
) with initial value
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a triple
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{B},\mathscr{P})
, where
\mathscr{P}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
is a filtered probability space with the filtration satisfying the usual conditions, i.e.
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is right-continuous and complete. Further,
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
and
\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{B}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
are continuous stochastic processes that are adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
such that

•

\mathbf{B}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
, i.e.
\mathbf{B}
is a standard Brownian motion and
\mathbf{B}_{s}-\mathbf{B}_{r}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{r}
for any
0\leq r<s
;

•

\mathbf{X}_{s}
satisfies (
6
) and the initial condition, i.e.

\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}+\int_{0}^{s}b(\mathbf{X}_{r})dr+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{r})d\mathbf{B}_{r},\quad s\geq 0,

or, equivalently, if we write
\mathbf{X}_{s}=(X_{1,s},\dots,X_{d,s})
for
s\geq 0
, then

X_{i,s}=x_{i}+\int_{0}^{s}b_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{r})dr+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{ij}(\mathbf{X}_{r})dB_{j,r},\quad i\in[d],\penalty\ s\geq 0.

Moreover, we say that there is
(weak) uniqueness
if whenever
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{B},\mathscr{P})
and
(\mathbf{\tilde{X}},\mathbf{\tilde{B}},\tilde{\mathscr{P}})
solve (
6
) weakly and satisfy
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{\tilde{X}}_{0}
, then
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{X}}
have the same law.

In order to get the diffusion approximation to work, we construct a sequence of right-continuous and continuous-time stochastic processes from the given sequence
(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of discrete time Markov chains by using constant interpolation between the time points. Then, under appropriate conditions specified in the subsequent theorem,
(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly
to the solution of an SDE.
With the necessary concepts at hand we are now ready to present our main tool, and we refer for example to
[
8
, Ch. 8]
for an extensive treatment.

Theorem 2.1

(Diffusion Approximation)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be continuous functions and assume that for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the SDE (
6
) possesses a unique solution such that
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}
. Furthermore, let
h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a sequence with
\lim_{n\to\infty}h(n)=0
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}=(\mathbf{Y}_{t}^{(n)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a discrete-time Markov chain with values in
S^{(n)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\mathbf{x}\in S^{(n)}
and
i,j\in[d]

b_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i,t+1}^{(n)}-x_{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t}^{(n)}=\mathbf{x}\big]}{h(n)},\quad a_{ij}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(Y_{i,t+1}^{(n)}-x_{i})(Y_{j,t+1}^{(n)}-x_{j})\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t}^{(n)}=\mathbf{x}\big]}{h(n)},

and
\gamma^{(n)}_{p}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[|\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}|^{p}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t}^{(n)}=\mathbf{x}\big]/h(n)
for
p\geq 2
.
Let
a\coloneqq\sigma\sigma^{T}
and assume that for all
R<\infty
and
i,j\in[d]

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in S^{(n)},|\mathbf{x}|\leq R}|b_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})-b_{i}(\mathbf{x})|=0,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in S^{(n)},|\mathbf{x}|\leq R}|a_{ij}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})-a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})|=0,

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in S^{(n)},|\mathbf{x}|\leq R}\gamma^{(n)}_{p}(\mathbf{x})=0\penalty\ \text{ for some }\penalty\ p\geq 2.

Finally, assume that
\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}_{0}\to\mathbf{x}
as
n\to\infty
.
Then
(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}_{\lfloor s/h(n)\rfloor})_{s\geq 0}
converges weakly to a strong Markov process
\mathbf{X}
that satisfies the SDE (
6
) with
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}
.

2.2
The (Standard) Logistic Branching Process

We already discussed in the introduction that the processes that will be relevant here are the so-called logistic branching processes, given by the solution of

dX_{s}=(aX_{s}-cX_{s}^{2})ds+\sqrt{\gamma X_{s}}dB_{s},\;s>0,

with
X_{0}=x\geq 0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
, see also (
2
) (and (
1
) for the particular case that will appear here).
In the remainder of this section we collect some key properties that will be handy. The first one is about the existence and uniqueness of solutions, see for example
[
9
, Cor. 2.11]
,
[
13
, Sec. 3]
,
[
11
, Thm. 5.1]
and references therein.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all initial states
x\geq 0
and for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
, there exists a unique solution
(X_{a,c,\gamma,x},B_{a,c,\gamma,x},\mathscr{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x})
to (
2
). Moreover,
X_{a,c,\gamma,x}
is non-negative.

In what follows it will be convenient to consider a specific choice of the filtered probability space
\mathscr{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}=(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{s})_{s\geq 0},\mathbb{P}^{\prime})
(where all components depend on the parameters
a,c,\gamma,x
) from the previous lemma that we construct as follows.
Let
C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
be the space of all continuous maps
\xi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and let
X
be the coordinate process given by
X_{s}(\xi)=\xi(s)
for all
s\geq 0
and
{\xi\in C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})}
.
Additionally, consider the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}=\sigma\{X_{s}\mid s\geq 0\}
and equip the measurable space
(C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{F})
with the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
given by
{\mathcal{F}_{s}=\sigma\{X_{r}\mid 0\leq r\leq s\}}
for all
s\geq 0
, which we may complete and right-continuously extend in order to fulfil the usual conditions.
Since
X_{a,c,\gamma,x}
is a continuous process, it is possible to switch from
\mathscr{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
to the canonical probability space
(C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0},\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x})
via the map
{\Omega^{\prime}\ni\xi^{\prime}\mapsto X_{a,c,\gamma,x}(\xi^{\prime})\in C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
is the probability measure given by
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}(A)={\mathbb{P}^{\prime}}((X_{a,c,\gamma,x})^{-1}(A))
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
. By this particular choice of probability measure we obtain that the coordinate process
X
on the space
(C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0},\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x})
has the same law as
X_{a,c,\gamma,x}
under
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
, i.e. under
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
the process
X
satisfies (
2
). The following corollary is now an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.2
, and a similar construction was also performed in
[
13
]
.

Corollary 2.3
.

For all initial states
x\geq 0
and for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
, there is a unique solution
(X,B_{a,c,\gamma,x},\mathscr{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x})
to (
2
), where
X
is the coordinate process and thus independent of
a,c,\gamma,x
. Moreover,
X
is non-negative
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
-almost surely, where
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
denotes the probability measure of
\mathscr{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
.

For the rest of this paper we will adopt the above procedure and consider solutions to (
2
) only with respect to the canonical probability space
(C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0},\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x})
.
Our first main object of interest in Theorem
1.1
is the time at which the logistic branching process
X
hits zero. To this end, define

T(\xi)\coloneqq\inf\big\{s\geq 0:\xi(s)=0\big\},\quad\xi\in C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}).

The author of
[
13
]
establishes that
T
is finite
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
-almost surely. Moreover,
X_{s}=0
for all
{s\geq T}
under
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
, i.e. upon hitting zero the process becomes constant, which is why we also refer to
T
as
absorption time
.
Although it seems to be well-known, we could not find an explicit reference for the fact that
T
is continuous if the underlying process is a logistic Feller diffusion.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
x>0,a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
. Then
T
is continuous under
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
.

Proof.

Since
X
has continuous paths and
X_{0}=x>0
under
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
, we obtain
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}(T=0)=0
. The continuity of
T
then follows for example from
[
12
, Thm. 5.1]
, which states that
T
is an infinite convolution of elementary mixtures of exponential distributions.
∎

Within our context, it will be necessary to consider solutions to (
2
) with initial value
x\to\infty
. The required results are covered by the following statement, whose proof can be found in
[
13

\theta:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\lambda\mapsto\int_{0}^{\lambda}\exp\left(\frac{\gamma}{4c}v^{2}-\frac{a}{c}v\right)dv.

(7)

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
x\geq 0,a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
.
Then the expectation of
T
under

In addition, the measures

Under

Set

A_{s}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}\xi(u)du,\quad s\geq 0,\penalty\ \xi\in C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})\penalty\ .

In order to study the distribution of

Lemma 2.6
.

For any
\xi\in C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
let
A(\xi)\coloneqq(A_{s}(\xi))_{s\geq 0}
and let

A^{-1}(\xi)
be its right-inverse, i.e.
(A(\xi)\circ A^{-1}(\xi))_{s}=s
for all
s\in\{A_{u}(\xi)\mid u\geq 0\}
.
Let
X
be the coordinate process. Then, for all
x\geq 0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0

R\coloneqq X\circ A^{-1}
under
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
satisfies the SDE

dR_{s}=(a-cR_{s})ds+\sqrt{\gamma}dB_{s},\;s\in[0,A_{\infty}],\text{ with initial condition }R_{0}=x.

(8)

Processes satisfying (
8
) for all

are called
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (O-U) processes
and are very well-studied. With this lemma at hand we show the following handy relation between
A_{\infty}
(under
\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}
) and the first time that the corresponding O-U process hits zero.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
R
be defined as in Lemma
2.6
. For all
x\geq 0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
c,\gamma>0
,

A_{\infty}=T(R)=\inf\{s\geq 0:R_{s}=0\}\quad\mathbb{P}_{a,c,\gamma,x}\text{-almost surely.}

Proof.

Let
X,A^{-1}
be as in Lemma
2.6
.
Let
\xi\in C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
be non-negative such that
\xi(s)=0
for all
s\geq T(\xi)
, so that
\xi(s)>0
for all
s\in[0,T(\xi))
. In order to simplify notation we omit the dependence on
\xi
in the forthcoming calculations, that is, we write
R_{s}
for
R_{s}(\xi)
,
R=(R_{s})_{s\geq 0}
and similarly for
A,A^{-1},X
and
T
; in particular, since
X
is the coordinate process,
X=\xi
. Note that by our choice of
\xi
the integral
A
is strictly increasing on
[0,T)
and hence invertible on
[0,T)

A^{-1}
is also the left-inverse of

R_{A_{T}}=(X\circ A^{-1})_{A_{T}}=\xi(A^{-1}(A_{T}))=0,

All in all,

2.3
Estimates for Martingales (with Subgaussian Increments)

Here we collect some preliminary facts concerning subgaussian random variables and martingales. Following standard notation, see for example the extensive book
[
2
]
, we call a random variable
X

subgaussian with parameter

\sigma^{2}>0
if

\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{s(X-\mathbb{E}[X])}\Big]\leq e^{s^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}\text{ for every }s\in\mathbb{R}.

The following result provides a handy way of checking whether a random variable is subgaussian.

Theorem 2.9

(Thm. 2.1 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. If, for some
v>0
,

\mathbb{P}(X>\lambda),\mathbb{P}(X<-\lambda)\leq e^{-\lambda^{2}/2v}\quad\text{for all }\lambda>0,

then
X
is subgaussian with parameter
\sigma^{2}=16v
.

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a filtration on some probability space. Let
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a real-valued, discrete-time stochastic process adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and such that
\mathbb{E}[M_{t}]<\infty
for each
t
. We say that
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is called a
martingale
if
\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=M_{t}
for each
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We say that
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
submartingale
if
\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\geq M_{t}
, whereas it is called a
supermartingale
if
\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq M_{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We continue with a simple lemma that provides a concentration bound for subgaussian supermartingales. Even though such a result is probably known or implicit in other works, we could not find a proper reference and hence we include the (short) proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a supermartingale with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that
M_{0}=0
and
M_{t}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is a subgaussian random variable with parameter
\sigma_{t}^{2}
. Then

\mathbb{P}(M_{t}\geq\lambda)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right),\qquad\lambda>0,\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof.

Let
s\geq 0
.
Using the supermartingale property of
M_{t}
and the fact that
M_{t}
, conditional on
{\cal F}_{t-1}
, is subgaussian with parameter
\sigma_{t}^{2}
, we readily obtain that

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{s(M_{t}-M_{t-1})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[e^{s(M_{t}-\mathbb{E}[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}])}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\leq e^{s^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}/2}.

Using the tower property of conditional expectation and induction we see that

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sM_{t}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sM_{t-1}}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{s(M_{t}-M_{t-1})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\big]\leq e^{s^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sM_{t-1}}\big]\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}s^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right).

Then, by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(M_{t}\geq\lambda)=\mathbb{P}\big(e^{sM_{t}}\geq e^{s\lambda}\big)\leq e^{-s\lambda}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sM_{t}}\big]\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}s^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}-s\lambda\right).

Choosing
s=\lambda/\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}\sigma_{i}^{2}>0

completes the proof.
∎

Hoeffding’s lemma implies that every bounded random variable is subgaussian and one can deduce the well-known Azuma-Hoeffding inequality, see e.g.
[
15
, Theorem 3.2.1]
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a supermartingale and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
|M_{i}-M_{i-1}|\leq c_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq N
. Then

\mathbb{P}(M_{N}-M_{0}\geq b)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{N}c^{2}_{i}}\right),\quad b\geq 0\kern 5.0pt.

3
Drift and Variation of the Unhappy Particles

In this section we study the
drift
and the
variation
of the process of unhappy particles; more precisely, we determine exact and asymptotic expressions for the quantities

\mathbb{E}\big[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\mid U_{t}\big]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\big[(U_{t+1}-U_{t})^{2}\mid U_{t}\big].

We begin as in
[
5
]
by writing

U_{t+1}-U_{t}=X_{t+1}-Y_{t+1},\quad\text{where}\quad X_{t+1}\coloneqq\left|\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\right|,\penalty\ Y_{t+1}\coloneqq\left|\mathcal{U}_{t}\cap\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\right|.

(9)

That is,
X_{t+1}
stands for the number of particles that were happy at step

From here on we deviate from

X_{t+1,h}\coloneqq\mathbb{1}[h\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1}]\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{t+1,u}\coloneqq\mathbb{1}[u\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}]

X_{t+1}=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{t}}X_{t+1,h}\kern 5.0pt\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{t+1}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{t}}Y_{t+1,u}.

(10)

The following simple lemma, also established in
[
5
]
, determines the drift. We include a (short) proof here that uses our notation and because it is instructive for the computation of the second moment that will follow.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\big[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\mid U_{t}\big]=H_{t}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{U_{t}}\right)-U_{t}\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{U_{t}-1}.

Proof.

The probability that none of the
U_{t}
unhappy particles jump to a specific fixed vertex is
(1-1/n)^{U_{t}}
. So, an arbitrary happy particle
h\in{\cal H}_{t}
is not joined by any unhappy particle (and thus remains happy) with that probability, and we obtain

\mathbb{E}\big[X_{t+1,h}\mid U_{t}\big]=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{U_{t}},\quad h\in\mathcal{H}_{t}.

(11)

Similarly, any unhappy particle
u\in{\cal U}_{t}
has
n-H_{t}
choices for a position
p_{u,t+1}
that is not occupied by a happy particle, and all other
U_{t}-1
unhappy particles will
not
choose
p_{u,t+1}
to move to with probability
(1-1/n)^{U_{t}-1}
. Thus

\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{t+1,u}\mid U_{t}\big]=\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{U_{t}-1},\quad u\in\mathcal{U}_{t}.

(12)

The statement follows from (
9
), (
10
) and linearity of expectation.
∎

We will need later the following simple, non-asymptotic estimate that was shown in
[
5
]
and that follows rather easily from Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\varepsilon:\mathbb{N}\to[-1,1]
and
M=M(n)\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon)n/2\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1+\varepsilon)U_{t}-7U_{t}^{2}/2n\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}\big]\leq(1+\varepsilon)U_{t}-U_{t}^{2}/n.

We proceed by deriving an exact expression for the variation. To this end, we introduce the following quantities:

A\coloneqq H_{t}\left(1-(1-1/n)^{U_{t}}\right)+U_{t}\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}(1-1/n)^{U_{t}-1};

B\coloneqq H_{t}(H_{t}-1)\left(1-2\left(1-1/n\right)^{U_{t}}+\left(1-2/n\right)^{U_{t}}\right);

C\coloneqq U_{t}(U_{t}-1)\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}\frac{n-H_{t}-1}{n}(1-2/n)^{U_{t}-2};

D\coloneqq 2H_{t}U_{t}\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}(1-1/n)^{U_{t}-1}\left(1-\big(1-1/(n-1)\big)^{U_{t}-1}\right).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[(U_{t+1}-U_{t})^{2}\mid U_{t}\right]=A+B+C-D.

Proof.

From (
9
) we obtain that
(U_{t+1}-U_{t})^{2}=X^{2}_{t+1}+Y^{2}_{t+1}-2X_{t+1}Y_{t+1}
, and so

\mathbb{E}\left[(U_{t+1}-U_{t})^{2}\mid U_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t+1}Y_{t+1}\mid U_{t}\right].

(13)

Recalling (
10
) and that
X_{t+1,h}=\mathbb{1}[h\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1}]
, we obtain

X^{2}_{t+1}=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{t}}X_{t+1,h}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t},h\neq h^{\prime}\end{subarray}}X_{t+1,h}X_{t+1,h^{\prime}}.

Note that the distribution of
X_{t+1}
, conditional on
U_{t}
, is invariant under the choice of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
and
\mathcal{U}_{t}
. We will use this fact without further reference within this proof. Then, by linearity of expectation and (
11
) we see that

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}\right]=H_{t}\left(1-(1-1/n)\right)^{U_{t}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t},h\neq h^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}).

(14)

Similarly, we obtain

Y^{2}_{t+1}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{t}}Y_{t+1,u}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t},u\neq u^{\prime}\end{subarray}}Y_{t+1,u}Y_{t+1,u^{\prime}},

where
Y_{t+1,u}=\mathbb{1}[u\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}]
, and so, using (
12
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}\right]=U_{t}\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}(1-1/n)^{U_{t}-1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t},u\neq u^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}).

(15)

Note that from (
14
) and (
15
) we already spot the
A
term in the statement of the lemma. Next we compute the probabilities that appear in the sums on the right-hand side of (
14
) and (
15
). To this end, let
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
with
h\neq h^{\prime}
and note that

\mathbb{P}(h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})=1-\mathbb{P}(h\notin\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})-\mathbb{P}(h^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})+\mathbb{P}(h,h^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}).

We have already argued in Lemma
3.1
when deriving (
11
) that

\mathbb{P}(h\notin\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})=\mathbb{P}(h^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})=(1-1/n)^{U_{t}}.

Moreover,
h
and
h^{\prime}
both remain happy if all particles in
{\cal U}_{t}
jump to vertices different from
p_{h,t},p_{h^{\prime},t}
, which occurs with probability
(1-2/n)^{U_{t}}
.
So

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t},h\neq h^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})=H_{t}(H_{t}-1)\left(1-2(1-1/n)^{U_{t}}+(1-2/n)^{U_{t}}\right),

(16)

which gives the term
B
in the statement of the lemma. Next, let
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}
with
u\neq u^{\prime}
. Then we claim that

\mathbb{P}(u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})=\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}\frac{n-H_{t}-1}{n}(1-2/n)^{U_{t}-2}.

To see this, note that there are
n-H_{t}
vertices that are not occupied by happy particles and to which
u
could jump to in order to become happy. This leaves
n-H_{t}-1
spots where
u^{\prime}
could jump to become happy.
Moreover, in order to actually be happy, none of the remaining
U_{t}-2
particles in
U_{t}
may jump on the vertices where
u
and
u^{\prime}
moved to. Thus

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{t},u\neq u^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})=U_{t}(U_{t}-1)\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}\frac{n-H_{t}-1}{n}(1-2/n)^{U_{t}-2},

(17)

which corresponds to the term
C
in the statement of the lemma.
It remains to evaluate the term
2\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}Y_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]
in (
13
), which, by linearity of expectation equals

2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in\mathcal{H}_{t},u\in\mathcal{U}_{t}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(h\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1},u\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\mid U_{t}).

Observe that, conditional on
u\in{\cal U}_{t}
becoming happy at time
t+1
, there remain
U_{t}-1
unhappy particles that could make
h\in{\cal H}_{t}
unhappy, and they jump independently to uniformly random positions in
\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{p_{u,t+1}\}
. Therefore

\mathbb{P}(h\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\mid U_{t},u\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1})=1-(1-1/(n-1))^{U_{t}-1},\quad h\in{\cal H}_{t},u\in{\cal U}_{t}.

Hence, by recalling (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(h\in\mathcal{U}_{t+1},u\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(u\in\mathcal{H}_{t+1}\mid U_{t})\left(1-(1-1/(n-1))^{U_{t}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-H_{t}}{n}(1-1/n)^{U_{t}-1}\left(1-(1-1/(n-1))^{U_{t}-1}\right).

Summing over all
h\in{\cal H}_{t},u\in{\cal U}_{t}
yields the term
D
.
∎

We proceed with establishing sharp asymptotic bounds for the drift and the variation; later, when we apply Theorem
2.1
, we will exploit these estimates when
U_{t}=\Theta(n^{1/2})
. In what follows we will make use of the following elementary asymptotic estimate, which can readily be established by considering the Taylor series expansion with a remainder term of the function
x\mapsto(1-x)^{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, at
x=0
.

Fact 3.4
.

Uniformly for any
x\in[0,1]
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

(1-x)^{N}=1-xN+\binom{N}{2}x^{2}-\binom{N}{3}x^{3}+O(N^{4}x^{4}),

(18)

as
N\to\infty
.

We proceed with deriving an asymptotic expression for the drift. Note that in the next two lemmas we work under the general assumption that the number of particles
M=(1+\varepsilon)n/2
, with
\varepsilon=o(1)
; later we will set
\varepsilon=2\alpha/n^{1/2}+o(1/n^{1/2})
so that
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})
, which is the main focus of the present paper.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n)=o(1)
,
u:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and
M=M(n)\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon)n/2\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, uniformly,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\mid U_{t}=u\big]=\varepsilon u-\frac{u^{2}}{n}\left(\frac{7}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}\right)+O\left(\frac{u}{n}+\frac{u^{3}}{n^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Recall that
M-U_{t}=H_{t}
. Since
M=(1+\varepsilon){n}/{2}
, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that

\mathbb{E}\left[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}=u\right]\\
=(1+\varepsilon)\frac{n}{2}\left(1-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{u}\right)+u\left(\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{u}-\Big(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{u}{n}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{u-1}\right).

(19)

We use (
18
) to obtain the uniform bounds

(1-1/n)^{u}=1-\frac{u}{n}+\frac{u^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{u}{n^{2}}+\frac{u^{3}}{n^{3}}\right),\quad(1-1/n)^{u-1}=1-\frac{u-1}{n}+O\left(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}\right).

(20)

By approximating the terms in (
19
) with (
20
), where only a second order estimate is used for the second
(1-n^{-1})^{n}
term, and using that
\varepsilon
is bounded, we obtain, maintaining the order of the terms within (
19
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}=u]

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{u}{n}-\frac{u^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\Big(\frac{u}{n^{2}}+\frac{u^{3}}{n^{3}}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+u\Bigg(1-\frac{u}{n}+O\Big(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{u}{n}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{u-1}{n}+O\Big(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big)\Bigg).

Collecting and cancelling terms then yields the statement.
∎

The next lemma establishes an asymptotic expression for the variation when
U_{t}
is not too big.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n)=o(1)
and
u:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be such that
u=o(n^{2/3})
and
M=M(n)\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon)n/2\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, uniformly,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(U_{t+1}-U_{t})^{2}\mid U_{t}=u\big]=u+o(\varepsilon u^{2}+u).

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
3.3
that

\mathbb{E}\left[(U_{t+1}-U_{t})^{2}\mid U_{t}\right]=A+B+C-D,

where the terms
A,B,C,D
are defined just prior to Lemma
3.3
. We estimate these terms individually, under the assumption that
U_{t}=u=o(n^{2/3})
. We start with
A
which, using
H_{t}=M-U_{t}=M-u
, can be rewritten as

A=(M-u)\left(1-(1-1/n)^{u}\right)+u(n-(M-u))n^{-1}(1-1/n)^{u-1}.

Using (
20
) and recalling that
M=(1+\varepsilon)n/2
we obtain

1-(1-1/n)^{u}=\frac{u}{n}+O\Big(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big),\penalty\ (1-1/n)^{u-1}=1-\frac{u-1}{n}+O\Big(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)

and

u\frac{n-(M-u)}{n}=u\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{u^{2}}{n}.

Note that, since
u=o(n)
,

(M-u)\left(1-(1-1/n)^{u}\right)=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}u+o(u),\quad u(n-(M-u))n^{-1}(1-1/n)^{u-1}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}u+o(u);

whence we obtain

A=u+o(u).

(21)

We proceed by evaluating
B
which, as
U_{t}=u
, is given by

B=(M-u)((M-u)-1)\left(1-2\left(1-1/n\right)^{u}+\left(1-2/n\right)^{u}\right).

Let us start by making a useful observation. Note that, by applying (
18
) to the expressions
(1-x)^{N}
and
(1-2x)^{N}
for
x\in[0,1/2]
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we obtain uniformly

1-2(1-x)^{N}+(1-2x)^{N}=N(N-1)x^{2}-N(N-1)(N-2)x^{3}+O(N^{4}x^{4}).

(22)

Consequently, using (
22
) we obtain

1-2\left(1-1/n\right)^{u}+\left(1-2/n\right)^{u}=\frac{u(u-1)}{n^{2}}-\frac{u^{3}}{n^{3}}+O\left(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{3}}+\frac{u^{4}}{n^{4}}\right).

(23)

Also, using once more the fact that
M=(1+\varepsilon)n/2
we see that

(M-u)((M-u)-1)=\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}n-u\right)^{2}-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}n+u=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}n^{2}-un(1+\varepsilon)+O(u^{2}+n).

Then, recalling our assumption
u=o(n^{2/3})
, after multiplying the expression that we have just derived for
(M-u)((M-u)-1)
with the expression on the right-hand side of (
23
) and truncating the computations at terms that are
o(u)
we obtain

B=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}u(u-1)-\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right)+o(u).

(24)

Next we consider the terms
C
and
D
. Recall that, since
U_{t}=u
,

C=u(u-1)\frac{n-(M-u)}{n}\frac{n-(M-u)-1}{n}(1-2/n)^{u-2}.

Since
M=n(1+\varepsilon)/2
we can write

\frac{n-(M-u)}{n}\frac{n-(M-u)-1}{n}=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}+\frac{u}{n}(1-\varepsilon)+O\left(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\right).

Then, since
u=o(n^{2/3})
, truncating at
o(u)
terms we obtain

u(u-1)\frac{n-(M-u)}{n}\frac{n-(M-u)-1}{n}=u(u-1)\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}+\frac{u^{3}}{n}(1-\varepsilon)+o(u).

Moreover, using (
18
) we see that

(1-2/n)^{u-2}=1-\frac{2u}{n}+O\left(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\right).

By multiplying the last two expressions and using
u=o(n^{2/3})
we arrive at

C=u(u-1)\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}+\frac{u^{3}}{n}\frac{1-\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+o(u).

(25)

Finally we evaluate the term
D
which, as
U_{t}=u
, can be written as

D=\left(1-(1-1/(n-1))^{u-1}\right)2(M-u)u\frac{n-(M-u)}{n}(1-1/n)^{u-1}=D_{1}D_{2}(1-1/n)^{u-1},

where we set

D_{1}\coloneqq 1-(1-1/(n-1))^{u-1}\text{ and }D_{2}\coloneqq 2(M-u)u\frac{n-(M-u)}{n}.

Since
M=(1+\varepsilon)n/2
, we can write

D_{2}=\left(n(1+\varepsilon)-2u\right)u\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{u}{n}\right)=\frac{un}{2}(1-\varepsilon^{2})+2\varepsilon u^{2}-\frac{2u^{3}}{n}.

(26)

Moreover, again using (
18
), we claim that

D_{1}=\frac{u-1}{n}-\frac{u^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{u^{3}}{n^{3}}+\frac{u}{n^{2}}\right).

To see this, let us start by noticing that, thanks to (
18
), we can write

D_{1}=\frac{u-1}{n-1}-\frac{u^{2}}{2(n-1)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{u^{3}}{n^{3}}+\frac{u}{n^{2}}\right).

Observing that
n/(n-1)=1+1/n+O(1/n^{2})
then establishes the claim about
D_{1}
.
Analogous computations show that the remaining
(1-1/n)^{u-1}
term that appears in the expression for
D
satisfies

(1-1/n)^{u-1}=1-\frac{u-1}{n}+O\left(\frac{u^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)

and hence after a little algebra we arrive at

D_{1}(1-1/n)^{u-1}=\frac{u-1}{n}-\frac{3u^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{u}{n^{2}}+\frac{u^{3}}{n^{3}}\right).

Multiplying the last expression with (
26
) and using that
u=o(n^{2/3})
we obtain

D=u(u-1)\frac{1-\varepsilon^{2}}{2}-\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{3}{4}(1-\varepsilon^{2})-2\varepsilon\right)+o(u).

Consequently, by combining this bound with (
25
) we obtain

\displaystyle C-D

\displaystyle=u(u-1)\left(\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}-\frac{1-\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+\frac{3}{4}(1-\varepsilon^{2})-2\varepsilon\right)+o(u)

\displaystyle=-u(u-1)\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right)+\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{5}{4}(1-\varepsilon^{2})-2\varepsilon\right)+o(u).

Summing
A
and
B
as given in (
21
) and (
24
), respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle A+B+C-D

\displaystyle=u+\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}u(u-1)-\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle-u(u-1)\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right)+\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{5}{4}(1-\varepsilon^{2})-2\varepsilon\right)+o(u)

\displaystyle=u(1-\varepsilon^{2})+u^{2}\varepsilon^{2}-\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}-\frac{5}{4}(1-\varepsilon^{2})+2\varepsilon\right)+o(u)

\displaystyle=u-u\varepsilon^{2}+u^{2}\varepsilon^{2}-\frac{u^{3}}{n}\left(\frac{7\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\right)+o(u).

But

u^{3}\varepsilon/n,u^{2}\varepsilon^{2}=o(\varepsilon u^{2})

and so the proof is finished.
∎

4
The Early Steps

In this section we provide, roughly speaking, tight bounds for the typical trajectory of the process
(U_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of unhappy particles until the first step where
U_{t}
drops to a value below
n^{1/2}/\delta
, where
\delta>0
is (arbitrarily) small. The main result of this section is the following statement.

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
such that the following is true.
Let
\epsilon>0,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}
, and let for
\delta>0

T_{n,M,\delta}\coloneqq\inf\left\{t>0:U_{t}\leq n^{1/2}/\delta\right\}.

Then, for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
and all sufficiently large
n
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

U_{T_{n,M,\delta}}\sim n^{1/2}/\delta,\quad T_{n,M,\delta}=(1\pm\epsilon)\frac{4}{7}\delta n^{1/2},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{0\leq t\leq T_{n,M,\delta}}U_{t}=\frac{2}{7}n\ln n+\frac{4}{7}n\ln{\delta}+\chi n\pm\epsilon n.

In particular, (roughly)
\frac{4}{7}\delta n^{1/2}
steps are required to drop below
n^{1/2}/\delta
unhappy particles, and at this step the accumulated number of unhappy particles, which corresponds to the total number of jumps, is (roughly)
\frac{2}{7}n\ln n
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Lemma
4.1
. As a preparation we first establish that the (conditional) distribution of the number
U_{t+1}
of unhappy particles, given
U_{t}
, is subgaussian. This simple fact will be useful in several occasions, and it will allow us to apply the machinery from Section
2.3
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
M=M(n)\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, conditional on
U_{t}
, the random variable
U_{t+1}
is subgaussian with parameter
64U_{t}
. In particular,

\mathbb{P}\big[U_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]\geq\lambda\mid U_{t}\big],\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{P}\big[U_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]\leq-\lambda\mid U_{t}\big]\leq e^{-\lambda^{2}/8U_{t}},\quad\lambda>0.

Proof.

Let us assume without loss of generality that the set of unhappy particles at time
t
is
\mathcal{U}_{t}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{U_{t}}\}
.
Define the Doob martingale

M_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p_{1,t+1},\dots,p_{i,t+1}}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}],\quad 1\leq i\leq U_{t},

and set
M_{0}=\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]
. In words,
M_{i}
is the (conditional) expectation of
U_{t+1}
given
U_{t}
, knowing the values of
p_{1,t+1},\dots,p_{i,t+1}
, i.e. the movements of the first
i
particles that were unhappy at step
t
. Clearly,
M_{U_{t}}=U_{t+1}
and
|M_{i+1}-M_{i}|\leq 2
for
1\leq i\leq U_{t}
since, by changing the position to which any one of the
U_{t}
particles moves to, we alter
U_{t+1}
by at most
2
.
Therefore, we can apply the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality from Theorem
2.11
and obtain

\mathbb{P}\big[U_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]\geq\lambda\mid U_{t}\big]\leq\exp\left(-\lambda^{2}/8U_{t}\right);

the other bound is obtained analogously. The claim then follows from Theorem
2.9
.
∎

An important and useful consequence of the previous statement is the powerful property that the number of unhappy particles
U_{t+1}
is typically always ‘close’ to its conditional expected value
\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]
, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
{\mathbb{P}}({\cal E})=1-o(1/n)
, where

{\cal E}\coloneqq\bigcap_{t\geq 0}{\cal E}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}_{t}\coloneqq\left\{\big|U_{t+1}-{\mathbb{E}}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]\big|\leq 4\sqrt{U_{t}\ln n}\right\}.

Proof.

Theorem
1.3
asserts that
T_{n,M}\leq t_{n}\coloneqq n^{1/2}\ln^{2}n
with probability
1-o(1/n)
, so it suffices to consider
t\leq t_{n}
only in the following sense. Since
\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t}=\emptyset
for
t\geq T_{n,M}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{t\leq T_{n,M}}\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t}\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{t\leq t_{n}}\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t}\Big)+\mathbb{P}(T_{n,M}>t_{n})=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{t\leq t_{n}}\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t}\Big)+o(1/n),

and hence we can focus on the events
\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t}
for
t\leq t_{n}
only. It follows from Lemma
4.2
that

\mathbb{P}\left(|U_{t+1}-{\mathbb{E}}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]\big|\geq 4\sqrt{U_{t}\ln n}\mid U_{t}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{16U_{t}\ln n}{8U_{t}}\right)=2n^{-2}.

A union bound then yields
\mathbb{P}({\cal E})=1-o(1/n)-2t_{n}n^{-2}=1-o(1/n)
, as claimed.
∎

We proceed with the proof of Lemma
4.1
. For any
0<\delta,\eta<1
set

i^{*}=i^{*}(\delta,\eta)\coloneqq\left\lfloor\left(\frac{\ln(\delta n^{3/10})}{\eta}\right)^{3}\right\rfloor\text{ and }u_{i}\coloneqq u_{i}(\delta,\eta)=\delta^{-1}n^{1/2}\exp\left(\eta(i^{*}-i)^{1/3}\right),\penalty\ 0\leq i\leq i^{*}.

(27)

Note that
u_{i^{*}}=n^{1/2}/\delta
and
e^{-\eta}n^{4/5}\leq u_{0}\leq n^{4/5}
.
Moreover, let
T_{0}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geq 0:U_{t}<u_{0}\}
,
and define recursively the stopping times

T_{i}\coloneqq\inf\big\{t\geq 0:U_{T_{0}+T_{1}+\dots+T_{i-1}+t}<u_{i}\big\},\quad 1\leq i\leq i^{*}.

These times play the role of ‘checkpoints’ for the evolution of the number of unhappy particles. Indeed, at time
T_{0}
we have, for the first time, less than
u_{0}
unhappy particles.
Then, after
T_{1}
additional steps, the number of unhappy particles falls for the first time below
u_{1}
, …, and after
T_{i}
additional steps, the number of unhappy particles falls for the first time below
u_{i}
. Eventually, after
T_{0}+\dots+T_{i^{*}}
steps the number of unhappy particles will fall for the first time below
n^{1/2}/\delta
, so that
T_{n,M,\delta}=T_{0}+\dots+T_{i^{*}}
. The definition of the
u_{i}
’s may look a bit cumbersome, but, as we will see, it is chosen with great care so that it allows us to control the total deviation of all the
U_{t}
’s simultaneously from their typical trajectory.

The rest of the section is structured as follows. First, we study
T_{0}
by showing that
U_{t}
, as long as it is rather largish, stays ‘very close’ to a deterministic value that can be obtained by iterating a (deterministic) function
t
times. This statement is in a specific sense best possible, as after some point in time the number of unhappy particles starts fluctuating too much. Moreover, we obtain fine control of the sum of the iterates of this function. Then, in Subsection
4.2
we use martingale arguments to show a general statement that allows us to bound the number of steps that are needed to drop from a given number
u
of unhappy particles to a slightly smaller number
(1-\eta)u
. Then we apply this statement with (essentially) the ratios
1-\eta\approx u_{i+1}/u_{i}
to gain sufficient control of all
T_{i}
’s. Finally, in Subsection
4.3
we combine our findings to prove Lemma
4.1
.

4.1
Analysis of
T_{0}

In order to study the beginning of the process we show that the number of unhappy particles at step
t
, as long as
t
is not too large, stays close to it’s iterated expectation, or, more precisely, to an asymptotic version thereof, using Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}
. Define the functions

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto x+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)(1-e^{-x})-x\left(\frac{1}{2}+x\right)e^{-x}.

and
f^{(t)}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
inductively by
f^{(0)}(x)=x
and
f^{(t+1)}(x)=f(f^{(t)}(x))
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, with probability tending to one,

U_{t}=f^{(t)}(1/2)n\pm(t+1)n^{1/2}\ln n,\quad t\geq 0.

Proof.

We begin with two observations that we shall use later. Since
U_{t}\leq n
, by applying Lemma
4.3
we obtain that with probability
1-o(1)
and with room to spare

U_{t+1}=\mathbb{E}[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}]\pm 4n^{1/2}\ln^{1/2}n,\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(28)

Moreover, using Lemma
3.1
we obtain for all sufficiently large
n
and for all
0\leq x\leq 1
such that
xn\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\big[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\mid U_{t}=xn\big]=(1/2-x)\left(1-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{xn}\right)-x(1/2+x)\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{xn-1}\pm 3\alpha n^{-1/2}.

Note that
(1-n^{-1})^{n}=e^{-1}(1\pm n^{-1})
, since
(1-n^{-1})^{n}
is increasing and
(1-n^{-1})^{n-1}
is decreasing (and both approach
e^{-1})
.
Thus it follows from the definition of
f(x)
given in the statement of the lemma that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\big[U_{t+1}\mid U_{t}=xn\big]=f(x)\pm(3|\alpha|+1)n^{-1/2},\quad\text{where}\quad xn\in\mathbb{N},t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(29)

In the rest we will assume that
n
is so large that we can use (
29
) and
\ln n\geq 4|\alpha|
. Moreover, we assume that (
28
) holds. Under these assumptions we will show (by induction on
t
) that

U_{t}=f^{(t)}(1/2)n\pm(t+1)n^{1/2}\ln n\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

so that the lemma is established.
Since
U_{0}=M
and
f^{(0)}(1/2)=1/2
the claim is obviously true for
t=0
. Next, assume the claim holds for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
. From (
28
) and (
29
) together with the induction hypothesis we obtain

\frac{1}{n}U_{t+1}=f\big(f^{(t)}(1/2)\pm(t+1)n^{-1/2}\ln n\big)\pm(3|\alpha|+1)n^{-1/2}.

Note that
f^{\prime}(x)=e^{-x}(1-5x/2+x^{2})
, so that
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 1
for all, say,
x\in[0,1]
. Thus, by Taylor’s theorem,
f(x+y)=f(x)\pm y
for all
x,y
such that
x,x+y\in[0,1]
. We obtain

\frac{1}{n}U_{t+1}=f^{(t+1)}(1/2)\pm(t+1)n^{-1/2}\ln n\pm(3|\alpha|+1)n^{-1/2}.

Since
\ln n\geq(3|\alpha|+1)
the claim is established.
∎

Towards the proof of Theorem
1.4
the following lemma about the sum of the iterates of
f
will be very useful.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
f,f^{(t)}
be as in Lemma
4.4
. Then
f^{(t)}(1/2)
is decreasing in
t
and
there is a
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
such that, as
N\to\infty
,

f^{(N)}(1/2)=\frac{4}{7}N^{-1}+O\big(N^{-2}\ln N\big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{t\leq N}f^{(t)}(1/2)=\frac{4}{7}\ln N+\chi+O(N^{-1}\ln N).

Remark 4.6
.

Numerical investigations show that
\chi=-0.1236(\dots)
. However, the convergence is quite slow, as indicated by our error bounds.

Proof.

Let us collect some properties of
f
.
Note that
f^{\prime}(x)=e^{-x}(1-5x/2+x^{2})
and that
1/2
and
2
are the roots of
1-5x/2+x^{2}
. So,
f^{\prime}
is positive and decreases on
[0,1/2)
. Since
f(0)=0
we obtain
f(x)>0
for
x\in(0,1/2]
. Moreover,
f^{\prime}(0)=1
and thus
(f(x)-x)^{\prime}<0
on
(0,1/2]
; again using that
f(0)=0
we obtain
f(x)<x
in this interval. All in all we have established

0<f(x)<x,\quad x\in(0,1/2].

(30)

Let us consider the sequence
(f^{(t)}(1/2))_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
which, according to (
30
), is
decreasing
and
positive
. Since
0
is the only fixpoint of the (continuous)
f
in
[0,1/2]
we obtain

f^{(t)}(1/2)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to\infty.

(31)

A simple computation shows that

f(0)=0,f^{\prime}(0)=1,f^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{7}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=e^{-x}(8-13x/2+x^{2}).

and so there is a
C>0
such that

f(x)=x-\frac{7}{4}x^{2}\pm Cx^{3},\quad x\in[0,1/2].

(32)

Up to now we have collected several properties of
f
that are actually sufficient to establish the claim. We will proceed with a routine argument, and the interested reader may consult
[
6
, Ch. 8.4]
for prototypical applications. Let us abbreviate
x_{t}=f^{(t)}(1/2)
and define
y_{t}=x_{t}^{-1}
. We obtain from (
32
) and (
31
), as
t\to\infty
,

y_{t+1}=y_{t}\Big(1-\frac{7}{4}x_{t}+O(x_{t}^{2})\Big)^{-1}=y_{t}\Big(1+\frac{7}{4}x_{t}+O(x_{t}^{2})\Big)=y_{t}+\frac{7}{4}+O(x_{t}).

Since
x_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
we obtain that, say,
y_{t+1}-y_{t}\geq 1
for all sufficiently large
t
. Thus
y_{t}\geq t/2
and so
x_{t}\leq 2/t
for all sufficiently large
t
. This, in turn, implies
y_{t}=\frac{7t}{4}+O(\ln t)
, and thus

x_{t}=f^{(t)}(1/2)=\frac{4}{7}t^{-1}+O\big(t^{-2}\ln t\big),\quad t\to\infty.

In other words, if we set
g(t)\coloneqq f^{(t)}(1/2)-4/7t
, then
|g(t)|=O(t^{-2}\ln t)
. Thus
\sum_{t\geq 0}g(t)
is absolutely convergent, and since
\sum_{1\leq t\leq N}t^{-1}=\ln N+\gamma+O(N^{-1})
for a
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
the claim is established.
∎

By combining the previous facts we readily obtain the likely value of
T_{0}
and
U_{T_{0}}
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R},M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\delta,\eta<1
. Then, with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty
,

T_{0}=(1\pm 2\eta)\frac{4}{7}n^{1/5}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{T_{0}}=(1+o(\ln^{-4}n))u_{0}.

Proof.

Recall from (
27
) that
e^{-\eta}n^{4/5}\leq u_{0}\leq n^{4/5}
.
By Lemma
4.4
with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty

U_{t}=f^{(t)}(1/2)n\pm(t+1)n^{1/2}\ln n,\quad t\geq 0.

We will assume that this event holds for the remainder of the proof. Setting
N_{-}\coloneqq 4(1-\eta)n^{1/5}/7
by Lemma
4.5
, we have for every
t\leq N_{-}
that

U_{t}\geq(1+\eta)n^{4/5}+O\left(n^{3/5}\ln{n}\right)\pm 2n^{7/10}\ln n>n^{4/5}\geq u_{0}

for all large enough
n
. Moreover, setting
N_{+}\coloneqq 4(1+2\eta)n^{1/5}/7
,

U_{N_{+}}\leq\frac{n^{4/5}}{1+2\eta}+O\left(n^{3/5}\ln{n}\right)\pm 2n^{7/10}\ln n<e^{-\eta}n^{4/5}\leq u_{0}

for all large enough
n
. Therefore
4(1-\eta)n^{1/5}/7\leq T_{0}\leq 4(1+2\eta)n^{1/5}/7,

establishing the first claim. Concerning the second statement, it follows again from Lemma
4.5
that, whenever
t\in[N_{-},N_{+}]
,

|U_{t+1}-U_{t}|\leq O(nt^{-2}\ln t)+2(t+2)n^{1/2}\ln n=o(n/t\ln^{4}{n})

and so
U_{t+1}=(1+o(\ln^{-4}{n}))U_{t}
for all such
t
.
∎

4.2
Analysis of
T_{i},1\leq i\leq i^{*}

Our next lemma states that if we begin with a sufficiently large number
u
of unhappy particles, then typically the number of steps that are required to drop below
(1-\eta)u
unhappy particles is roughly
4\eta n/7u
and, during those steps, the number of unhappy particles never exceeds
(1+\eta)u
. In the sequel, this will allow us to study
T_{i}
, by selecting
u_{i}=(1-\eta_{i})u_{i-1}
, see (
27
). In the proof of Lemma
4.8
below we construct two processes, a supermartingale and a submartingale, which bound from below and above, respectively, the number of unhappy particles; then we analyse these processes by exploiting that
U_{t}
, conditional on
U_{t-1}
, is subgaussian, see Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
0<\eta<1/16
. Then there is an
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
the following is true.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, let
u:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be such that
3|\alpha|\eta^{-1}n^{1/2}\leq u\leq n/\ln^{1/2}n
and, for
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, let

T=T(u,t_{0})\coloneqq\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:U_{t_{0}+t}\not\in[(1-\eta)u,(1+\eta)u]\big\}.

Then, conditional on
U_{t_{0}}=u
, with probability at least
1-8\exp\left(-\eta^{3}u^{2}/800n\right)
,

T=(1\pm 5\eta)\frac{4\eta n}{7u}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{t_{0}+T}=(1-\eta)(1\pm 12\eta^{2})u.

Proof.

Since
(U_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a Markov chain, we assume without loss of generality within the proof that
t_{0}=0
; in particular, with slight abuse of notation,
U_{0}=u
. Set
\varepsilon\coloneqq{2M}/{n}-1\sim{2\alpha}n^{-1/2}
and define, for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the two discrete-time random processes

W_{t}^{+}\coloneqq U_{t\wedge T}+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1+\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}(t\wedge T)\quad\mbox{and}\quad W_{t}^{-}\coloneqq U_{t\wedge T}+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}(t\wedge T).

For
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the process of unhappy particles until step
t
, and set
{\cal F}\coloneqq({\cal F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. We will argue that
(W_{t}^{+})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a submartingale and that
(W_{t}^{-})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a supermartingale (both with respect to
\cal F
).
By applying Lemma
3.5
we see that there is a

n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have for every
3|\alpha|\eta^{-1}n^{1/2}\leq u\leq n/\ln^{1/2}n
that

{\mathbb{E}}\big[U_{t+1}-U_{t}\mid U_{t}=u\big]=\varepsilon u-\frac{u^{2}}{n}\left(\frac{7}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}\right)+O\left(\frac{u}{n}+\frac{u^{3}}{n^{2}}\right)=-\big(1\pm\eta)\frac{7}{4}\frac{u^{2}}{n}.

In what follows we will always assume that
n\geq n_{0}
.
Note that, by the Markov property of
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
\mathbb{E}[W_{t}^{-}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{t}^{-}\mid U_{t-1}]
. Since
(1-\eta)u\leq U_{t-1}\leq(1+\eta)u
if
t\leq T
, we obtain under this assumption that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{t}^{-}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[U_{t}\mid U_{t-1}]+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}t

\displaystyle\leq U_{t-1}-(1-\eta)\frac{7}{4}\frac{U_{t-1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}t

\displaystyle\leq U_{t-1}-(1-\eta)\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}u^{2}}{n}+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}t=W_{t-1}^{-}.

(33)

On the other hand,
W_{t}^{-}=W_{t-1}^{-}
when
t>T
, showing that
W_{t}^{-}
is a supermartingale. An analogous argument, using again that
(1-\eta)u\leq U_{t-1}\leq(1+\eta)u
when
t\leq T
, shows that
\mathbb{E}[W_{t}^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\geq W_{t-1}^{+}
and therefore
W_{t}^{+}
is a submartingale. Having established the super/sub-martingale nature of
W^{-}
and
W^{+}
we claim that

\mathbb{P}(T<t^{-})\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{3}u^{2}}{800n}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad t^{-}=t^{-}(u,n,\eta)\coloneqq\frac{4\eta n}{7u}\frac{1-\eta}{(1+\eta)^{3}}.

(34)

To see this, note that if
T<t^{-}
then either
U_{t^{-}\wedge T}>(1+\eta)u
or
U_{t^{-}\wedge T}<(1-\eta)u
and hence

\mathbb{P}(T<t^{-})\leq\mathbb{P}(U_{t^{-}\wedge T}>(1+\eta)u)+\mathbb{P}(U_{t^{-}\wedge T}<(1-\eta)u).

(35)

We start by estimating the probability that
U_{t^{-}\wedge T}>(1+\eta)u
. To this end we will utilize the supermartingale
W^{-}_{t^{-}}
since, roughly speaking, it is ‘hard’ for a supermartingale to become
large

and hence the probability of interest should be (very) small.
Similarly, we will estimate the probability that
U_{t^{-}\wedge T}
is smaller than
(1-\eta)u
by by utilizing
W^{+}_{t^{-}}
; this will give us a good bound on the probability of interest since it is ‘hard’ for a submartingale to become
small
.
Following this discussion and using that
W^{-}_{0}=U_{0}=u
we obtain

\mathbb{P}(U_{t^{-}\wedge T}>(1+\eta)u)=\mathbb{P}\Big(W^{-}_{t^{-}}-\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}(t^{-}\wedge T)>(1+\eta)u\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(W^{-}_{t^{-}}-W^{-}_{0}>\eta u\big).

(36)

Lemma
4.2
asserts that
U_{t}
, conditional on
U_{t-1}
, is a subgaussian random variable with parameter
64U_{t-1}
. This in turn implies that, for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the random variable
W_{t}^{-}
conditional on
U_{t-1}
is subgaussian with parameter
64(1+\eta)u\leq 100u
(with room to spare); the same is true for
W_{t}^{+}
conditional on
U_{t-1}
.
Thus, using Lemma
2.10
we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\big(W^{-}_{t^{-}}-W^{-}_{0}\geq\eta u\big)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}u^{2}}{200ut^{-}}\right),

and substituting the value of
t^{-}
we see that the last expression is at most half of the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(T<t^{-})
in (
34
). It remains to bound the second probability on the right-hand side of (
35
). Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(U_{t^{-}\wedge T}<(1-\eta)u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(W^{+}_{t^{-}}-W^{+}_{0}<-\eta u+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1+\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}(t^{-}\wedge T)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(W^{+}_{t^{-}}-W^{+}_{0}<-\eta u+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1+\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}t^{-}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(W^{+}_{t^{-}}-W^{+}_{0}<-\eta u+\eta u-\eta^{2}u\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\big(-(W^{+}_{t^{-}}-W^{+}_{0})>\eta^{2}u\big).

Using once more Lemmas
2.10
and
4.2
we see that the last expression is at most

\exp\big(-\frac{7\eta^{3}u^{2}}{800n}\big)
,
which is also at most half of the expression in (
34
), completing its proof.
Our next goal is to show

\mathbb{P}(T>t^{+})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{3}u^{2}}{800n}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad t^{+}=t^{+}(u,n,\eta)\coloneqq\frac{4\eta n}{7u}\frac{1+\eta}{(1-\eta)^{3}}>t^{-}.

(37)

To see this, observe first that if
T>t^{+}
, then
(1-\eta)u<U_{t^{+}}<(1+\eta)u
. So, obviously,

\mathbb{P}(T>t^{+})\leq\mathbb{P}\big(U_{t^{+}}>(1-\eta)u,T>t^{+}\big).

Note that it doesn’t look necessary to maintain the event
\{T>t^{+}\}
, but we will actually need it in a moment.
Expressing
U_{t}
in terms of
W^{-}_{t}
once more we obtain

\mathbb{P}\big(U_{t^{+}}>(1-\eta)u,T>t^{+}\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(W^{-}_{t^{+}}-W^{-}_{0}\geq-\eta u+\frac{7}{4}\frac{(1-\eta)^{3}u^{2}}{n}t^{+}\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(W^{-}_{t^{+}}-W^{-}_{0}\geq\eta^{2}u\Big).

Hence, using again Lemmas
2.10
and
4.2
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(T>t^{+})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{7u^{2}\eta^{3}(1-\eta)^{3}}{800n(1+\eta)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}\eta^{3}}{800n}\right),

So far we have established that

\mathbb{P}(t^{-}\leq T\leq t^{+})\geq 1-3\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{3}u^{2}}{800n}\right).

(38)

To complete the proof of the lemma, it remains to show that
U_{T}=(1-\eta)(1\pm 12\eta^{2})u
with the required probability bound indicated in the statement of the lemma. Note that either
U_{T}\geq(1+\eta)u
or
U_{T}\leq(1-\eta)u
. Thus, proceeding as in (
36
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(U_{T}\geq(1+\eta)u)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\big(W^{-}_{T}-W^{-}_{0}\geq\eta u\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(W^{-}_{T\wedge t^{+}}-W^{-}_{0}\geq\eta u\big)+\mathbb{P}(T>t^{+}).

Using Lemmas
2.10
and
4.2
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\big(W^{-}_{T\wedge t^{+}}-W^{-}_{0}\geq\eta u\big)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{7\eta u^{2}}{16\cdot 200n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{3}u^{2}}{800n}\right),

and so, together with (
37
), we arrive at

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(n^{-1/2}T_{n,M}\geq s\big)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(n^{-1/2}T_{n,M}\geq s,T_{n,M,\delta}\leq\delta n^{1/2}\big)+\delta

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\big(T\geq s-\delta\big)+\delta.

Note that for all
\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,x_{1}}(T\geq\tau)\geq\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,x_{2}}(T\geq\tau)
for all

x_{1}>x_{2}\geq 0
and
\tau\geq 0
, since
X
is almost surely continuous and needs a positive and finite amount of time to drop from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
. So, since according to Lemma
2.5
we have
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}=\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}
and
T
is continuous,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\big(T\geq s-\delta\big)\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}\big(T\geq s-\delta\big)=\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}\big(T\geq s\big),

which yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(n^{-1/2}T_{n,M}\leq s)=\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}(T\leq s),\quad s\geq 0.

Thus,
n^{-1/2}T_{n,M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}T_{\alpha}
, where
T_{\alpha}
satisfies (
48
).
Moreover,
\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}(T>0)=1
implies that
T_{\alpha}
is positive almost surely, and this completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

6
Absorption Time of the Diffusion Process

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. In this section we study properties of the absorption time of the diffusion process
T_{\alpha}
in Theorem
1.1
, which, as we showed in Section
5
, is distributed like the first time
T
the coordinate process hits zero under the measure
\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}
, see also (
48
).
We begin with considering the expected value

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[n^{-1/2}T_{n,M}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,\infty}\left[T\right],

where
M=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})
, and provide asymptotic bounds when
|\alpha|\to\infty
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\frac{\pi^{3/2}}{\sqrt{7}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{7}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{m+1}{2})}{m!}\left(\frac{8\alpha}{\sqrt{7}}\right)^{m}t_{m},

(55)

where

t_{m}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{2}{(\frac{m+1}{2}+2k)(\frac{m+3}{2}+2k)}=H_{(m-1)/4}-H_{(m-3)/4},\quad m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

and
H_{x}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\Big(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+x}\Big)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
x>-1
.

Proof.

Abbreviate
A=A(\alpha)\coloneqq{8\alpha}/{7}
.
From (
7
) and Lemma
2.5
, we obtain that

\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{7}\lambda^{2}+A\lambda\right)\int_{0}^{\lambda}\exp\left(\frac{1}{7}\mu^{2}-A\mu\right)d\mu\;d\lambda\kern 5.0pt.

Substituting
\mu=\lambda u
and applying Fubini’s theorem yields

\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{7}(1-u^{2})\lambda^{2}+A(1-u)\lambda\right)d\lambda\;du.

(56)

We shall use the well-known fact that for any
c>0
and
n>-1

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}\exp(-cx^{2})dx=\frac{1}{2}c^{-(n+1)/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right),

which follows directly by a simple change of variables in the definition of the Gamma function.
By expanding
\exp(A(1-u)\lambda)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(A(1-u))^{m}\lambda^{m}/m!
we obtain, again by Fubini’s theorem,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{7}(1-u^{2})\lambda^{2}+A(1-u)\lambda\right)&d\lambda=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{7}(1-u^{2})\lambda^{2}\right)\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{1}{m!}(A(1-u))^{m}\lambda^{m}d\lambda\\
&=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{1}{m!}(A(1-u))^{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{7}(1-u^{2})\lambda^{2}\right)d\lambda\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{1}{m!}(A(1-u))^{m}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1-u^{2}}{7}\right)^{-(m+1)/2}.\end{split}

Since
1-u^{2}=(1-u)(1+u)
we further obtain from (
56
) and Fubini that

\begin{split}\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]&=\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{m+1}{2})}{m!}\left(\sqrt{7}A\right)^{m}\frac{(1-u)^{(m-1)/2}}{(1+u)^{(m+1)/2}}du\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{m+1}{2})}{m!}\left(\frac{8\alpha}{\sqrt{7}}\right)^{m}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-u)^{(m-1)/2}}{(1+u)^{(m+1)/2}}du.\end{split}

(57)

To evaluate the last integral, we substitute
u=(1-\sqrt{v})/(1+\sqrt{v})
, i.e.
du=-dv/(\sqrt{v}(1+\sqrt{v})^{2})
. Moreover, let
x>-1
, and notice that by integration by parts, the fact that
(1-y^{x})\ln(1-y)\to 0
as
y\to 1
, and the series representation of the logarithm

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-y^{x}}{1-y}dy=-\int_{0}^{1}xy^{x-1}\ln(1-y)dy=\sum_{k\geq 1}x\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y^{x+k-1}}{k}dy=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{x}{k(x+k)}=H_{x}.

(58)

Thus

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-u)^{(m-1)/2}}{(1+u)^{(m+1)/2}}du=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{v^{(m-3)/4}}{2(1+\sqrt{v})}dv=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{v^{(m-3)/4}-v^{(m-1)/4}}{2(1-v)}dv=\frac{H_{(m-1)/4}-H_{(m-3)/4}}{2}.

By plugging this expression into (
57
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\frac{2}{\sqrt{7}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}du+\frac{1}{\sqrt{7}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{m+1}{2})}{m!}\left(\frac{8\alpha}{\sqrt{7}}\right)^{m}\left(H_{(m-1)/4}-H_{(m-3)/4}\right).

The statement now follows from
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
and
\int_{0}^{1}1/\sqrt{1-u^{2}}du=\pi/2.

∎

Next we establish the asymptotic behaviour for
|\alpha|\to\infty
of
\mathbb{E}[T_{\alpha}]
. In order to do so, we do not use the explicit sum from (
55
) – although we could do so – but rather exploit the integral representation that we have encountered previously. In particular, note that from Lemma
2.5
, see also (
56
), we obtain

\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{7}(1-u^{2})\lambda^{2}+\frac{8\alpha}{7}(1-u)\lambda\Big)d\lambda\;du.

By substituting
u
for
1-u
we obtain

\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-Q\lambda^{2}+L\lambda\big)d\lambda\;du,\quad\text{where}\quad Q=\frac{2u-u^{2}}{7},\penalty\ L=\frac{8\alpha u}{7},

(59)

which is the starting point in the following two proofs. We continue with the case
\alpha\to\infty
, where we show that
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]
grows very fast, namely quadratic exponential in
\alpha
.

Corollary 6.2
.

As
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=(1+o(1))\frac{\sqrt{7\pi}}{8}\cdot\frac{e^{16\alpha^{2}/7}}{\alpha^{2}}.

(60)

Proof.

In order to estimate (
59
) we first study in more abstract terms the general behavior of the integral of
\exp(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda)
where
A,B
are parameters; we will be particularly interested in the case where
A
is positive and bounded (later we will set
A=Q=\frac{1}{7}(2u-u^{2})
for
u\in[0,1]
) and
B\to\infty
(later we will set
B=L=8\alpha u/7
, and the main contribution will come from
u
close to 1). Note that
-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda=-(\sqrt{A}\lambda-{B}/{2\sqrt{A}})^{2}+{B^{2}}/{4A}
, so by changing the variable
x=\sqrt{A}\lambda-{B}/{2\sqrt{A}}
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}e^{B^{2}/4A}\int_{-B/2\sqrt{A}}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}}dx.

(61)

Since
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}}dx=\sqrt{\pi}
we readily obtain the general upper bound

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{A}}e^{B^{2}/4A},\quad A,B>0.

(62)

However, we will also require tighter bounds. Note that

\int_{-\infty}^{-B/2\sqrt{A}}e^{-x^{2}}dx=\int_{-\infty}^{-B/2\sqrt{A}}\frac{|x|}{|x|}e^{-x^{2}}dx\leq\frac{2\sqrt{A}}{B}\int_{-\infty}^{-B/2\sqrt{A}}|x|e^{-x^{2}}dx=\frac{\sqrt{A}}{B}e^{-B^{2}/4A}

so that by

\int_{-B/2\sqrt{A}}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}}dx=\sqrt{\pi}-\int_{-\infty}^{-B/2\sqrt{A}}e^{-x^{2}}dx

we obtain from (
61
) that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda=(\pi/A)^{1/2}e^{B^{2}/4A}\pm\frac{1}{B},\quad A,B>0.

(63)

With these preparations at hand we turn to the task of estimating (
59
). Let
0<\delta<1/2
be some (small) quantity to be specified later. We split the integration range
u\in(0,1]
(neglecting
u=0
) in three parts
(0,1/2],[1/2,1-\delta]
and
[1-\delta,1]
. Let us consider the interval
[1-\delta,1]
first, namely we start by estimating

\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du.

(64)

As we will see, the main contribution to (
59
) comes from (
64
). We will apply (
63
) with

A=Q=\frac{2u-u^{2}}{7},B=L=\frac{8\alpha u}{7},\quad\text{so that}\quad\frac{B^{2}}{4A}=\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}\frac{u}{2-u}.

Note that for this particular choice of
A
and
B
we obtain

\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du=\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7\pi}}{\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}}e^{\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}\frac{u}{2-u}}\pm\frac{7}{8\alpha u}\right)du.

(65)

With the purpose of simplifying the terms
u/(2-u)
and
1/\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}
observe that

\frac{u}{2-u}-(1-2(1-u))=\frac{2(1-u)^{2}}{2-u}

(66)

and, since
1-\delta\leq u\leq 1
, we readily see that the ratio on the right-hand side of (
66
) is at most
2(1-u)^{2}\leq 2\delta^{2}
. Whence we arrive at

0\leq\frac{u}{2-u}-(1-2(1-u))\leq 2\delta^{2}.

Moreover, for
1-\delta\leq u\leq 1
we also have that

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}}-1\right|\overset{}{\leq}1-u\leq\delta,

since, obviously,
\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}\leq 1
and moreover, by writing, say,
u=1-y
for
0\leq y\leq 1/2
and simplifying,
\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}(2-u)\geq 1
.
Going back to (
65
) we see that, uniformly in
\delta
as
\alpha\to\infty

\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du=(1+O(\delta))\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\sqrt{7\pi}e^{\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}(1-2(1-u)+O(\delta^{2}))}du.

Since

\int_{1-\delta}^{1}e^{-\frac{32\alpha^{2}}{7}(1-u)}du=\frac{7}{32\alpha^{2}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{32\alpha^{2}}{7}\delta}\right)

we arrive at the expression

\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du=(1+O(\delta))\sqrt{7\pi}e^{\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}(1+O(\delta^{2}))}\frac{7}{32\alpha^{2}}(1-e^{-32\alpha^{2}\delta/7}).

(67)

We now choose the value of
\delta
that will be most convenient to work with. Fix
\delta=9\alpha^{-2}\ln\alpha
, so that
\alpha^{2}\delta\to\infty
and
\alpha^{2}\delta^{2}\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
. Substituting this value of
\delta
into (
67
) we conclude that

\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du\sim\frac{(7^{3}\pi)^{1/2}}{32}\alpha^{-2}e^{\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}},\quad\alpha\to\infty.

(68)

Let us now turn our attention to the range
u\in(0,1/2]\cup[1/2,1-\delta]
in (
59
).
For the first interval we apply (
62
) with
A=Q=\frac{2u-u^{2}}{7},B=L=\frac{8\alpha u}{7}
, so that

\frac{B^{2}}{4A}=\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}\frac{u}{2-u}\leq\frac{1}{3}\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7},\quad u>0.

Thus, recalling the basic integral
\int 1/\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}du=\text{arcsin}(u-1)+C
and the values
\text{arcsin}(-1)=-\pi/2
and
\text{arcsin}(-1/2)=-\pi/6
,
we arrive (by (
62
)) at the (rough) bound

\int_{0}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du\leq e^{\frac{1}{3}\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}}\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{\sqrt{7\pi}}{\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}}du=\frac{\sqrt{7}\pi^{3/2}}{3}e^{\frac{1}{3}\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}},

(69)

which is much smaller than the right-hand side in (
68
). We conclude by considering the case
u\in[1/2,1-\delta]
. We apply again (
62
) with
A=Q=\frac{2u-u^{2}}{7},B=L=\frac{8\alpha u}{7}
, so that

\frac{B^{2}}{4A}\leq(1-\delta)\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}\quad\text{and, say,}\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\leq 4.

Thus, we obtain

\int_{1/2}^{1-\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-A\lambda^{2}+B\lambda\big)d\lambda du\leq 4\sqrt{\pi}\int_{1/2}^{1-\delta}e^{(1-\delta)\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}}du\leq 4\sqrt{\pi}e^{(1-\delta)\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{7}},

which, by our choice of
\delta=9\alpha^{-2}\ln\alpha
, is at most
4\sqrt{\pi}\alpha^{-9}e^{{16\alpha^{2}}/7}
. By combining this with (
69
) for the case
u\in(0,1/2]
and (
68
) for
u\in[1-\delta,1]
we finally obtain, as
\alpha\to\infty
, from (
59
) the claim.
∎

In contrast to the quadratic exponential behavior as
\alpha\to\infty
established in Corollary
6.2
, we show that the behavior is rather moderate (i.e., polynomial with logarithmic corrections) when
\alpha\to-\infty
.

Corollary 6.3
.

As
\alpha\rightarrow-\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=(1+o(1))\frac{\ln|\alpha|}{|\alpha|}.

Proof.

Let us start with two auxiliary estimates that we shall exploit. Using
1-e^{-y}\leq y
for
y\geq 0
we obtain
e^{-Qx^{2}}=1\pm Qx^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R},Q>0
. Also,
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-Lx}dx=1/L
for all
L>0
and we obtain (for
Q,L>0
)

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-Qx^{2}-Lx\Big)dx-\frac{1}{L}\right|=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-Lx}dx-\frac{1}{L}\pm Q\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-Lx}dx\leq 2QL^{-3},

(70)

where the inequality follows from integration by parts of the second integral.
This estimate will be useful when
L
is large. On the other hand, when
L
is small, we will use a different bound. Indeed, noting that
|e^{-Lx}-1|\leq Lx
for all
Lx>0
and that
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-Qx^{2}}dx=\sqrt{\pi}/2\sqrt{Q}
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}xe^{-Qx^{2}}dx=1/2Q
for
Q>0
, we obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-Qx^{2}-Lx\Big)dx-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{Q}}\right|\leq\frac{L}{2Q},\quad Q,L>0.

(71)

With those facts at hand we continue with the proof of the corollary.
From (
59
) we readily obtain

\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-Q\lambda^{2}-L\lambda\big)d\lambda\;du,\quad\text{where}\quad Q=\frac{2u-u^{2}}{7},\penalty\ L=\frac{8|\alpha|u}{7}.

We will use (
71
) to estimate the integral when
u
is close to zero and (
70
) otherwise. More precisely, let
0<\delta<1
. Then, by (
71
) together with the fact that for any
0\leq u\leq 1

2LQ^{-1}=\frac{16|\alpha|u}{2u-u^{2}}\leq 16|\alpha|

we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-Q\lambda^{2}-L\lambda\big)d\lambda\;du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{Q}}\pm 16|\alpha|\right)du=\frac{\sqrt{7\pi}}{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{du}{\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}}\pm 16|\alpha|\delta.

Since
u\leq 2u-u^{2}\leq 2u
for
0\leq u\leq\delta\leq 1
,
we obtain (uniformly in
\delta,\alpha
)

\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-Q\lambda^{2}-L\lambda\big)d\lambda\;du=O\big(\sqrt{\delta}+|\alpha|\delta\big).

(72)

Moreover, by (
70
) and
QL^{-3}\leq|\alpha|^{-3}u^{-2}
we obtain (again uniformly in
\delta,\alpha
)

\begin{split}\int_{\delta}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-Q\lambda^{2}-L\lambda\big)d\lambda\;du&=\int_{\delta}^{1}\frac{1}{L}+O(QL^{-3})du=\frac{7\ln(1/\delta)}{8|\alpha|}+O(\delta^{-1}|\alpha|^{-3}).\end{split}

By choosing
\delta=\alpha^{-2}
and combining the last estimate with (
72
) we obtain from (
59
), as
\alpha\to-\infty
,

\frac{7}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\alpha}\right]=\frac{7\ln(1/\delta)}{8|\alpha|}+O\big(\sqrt{\delta}+|\alpha|\delta+\delta^{-1}/|\alpha|^{3}\big)\sim\frac{7}{4}\frac{\ln|\alpha|}{|\alpha|},

and the claim follows.
∎

7
Proof of Theorem
1.4
– Total Number of Jumps

Recall that
M=M(n)=n/2+\alpha n^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2})
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Our goal is to establish that there exists a continuous random variable
A_{\alpha}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

n^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}-\frac{2}{7}n\ln n\bigg)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}A_{\alpha}.

(73)

For this purpose we fix an
\epsilon>0
and
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
n
sufficiently large so that Lemma
4.1
applies. In particular, there exists
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

U_{T_{n,M,\delta}}\sim\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\delta}\quad\text{ and }\sum_{0\leq t\leq T_{n,M,\delta}}U_{t}=\frac{2}{7}n\ln n+\frac{4}{7}n\ln\delta+\chi n\pm\epsilon n,

(74)

where
T_{n,M,\delta}=\inf\{t>0:U_{t}\leq n^{1/2}/\delta\}
. In the following lemma we consider all
t\geq T_{n,M,\delta}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\delta>0
. As
n\to\infty
,

n^{-1}\sum_{t\geq T_{n,M,\delta}}U_{t}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds,

where
X
satisfies (
1
) and thus depends on
\delta
through the initial condition
X_{0}=\delta^{-1}
.

Proof.

We use the notation from Section
5
and consider the process of unhappy particles after
T_{n,M,\delta}
, i.e.
U^{\prime}_{s}=U_{\lfloor s\rfloor+T_{n,M,\delta}}
for all
s\geq 0
. Hence,

n^{-1}\sum_{t\geq T_{n,M,\delta}}U_{t}=n^{-1}\sum_{t\geq 0}U^{\prime}_{t}=n^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{\prime}_{s}ds=n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds.

(75)

Let
S>0
. For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\pi_{m}:0=s_{0}<s_{1}<...<s_{m}=S
be a partition of the interval
[0,S]
such that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup_{i\in\{1,...,m\}}|s_{i}-s_{i-1}|=0
.
Recall Lemma
5.1
, from which we obtain that the process
(n^{-1/2}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}})_{s\geq 0}
converges weakly to
X
as
n\to\infty
in the Skorokhod space
D([0,S],\mathbb{R})
. Since, for each
i\in\{1,...,m\}
, the mapping
F(f)=\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}f(s)
,
f\in D([0,S],\mathbb{R})
, is continuous with respect to the Skorokhod metric, this implies that as
n\to\infty

n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}X_{s}.

Noting that

\int_{0}^{S}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}

we obtain, for all
a\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{S}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\leq a\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}\leq a\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}X_{s}\leq a\right).

Analogously, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{S}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\leq a\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\inf_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}X_{s}\leq a\right).

As
X
has almost surely continuous paths,
\int_{0}^{S}X_{s}ds
is a Riemann integral for almost every realisation and hence

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\inf_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}X_{s}=\int_{0}^{S}X_{s}ds=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\sup_{s\in[s_{i-1},s_{i}]}X_{s}.

This yields the equality

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{S}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\leq a\right)=\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{S}X_{s}ds\leq a\right),\quad a\geq 0.

As
U^{\prime}\geq 0
, we readily obtain, for all
a\geq 0
,

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\leq a\right)&\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{S}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\leq a\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{S}X_{s}ds\leq a\right).\end{split}

(76)

Additionally, since
U^{\prime}_{s}=0
for all
s\geq T^{\prime}_{n,M,\delta}
we estimate that

\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\geq a\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{S}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\geq a\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}T^{\prime}_{n,M,\delta}>S\right),\quad a\geq 0,

from which we readily deduce using Lemma
5.2
that, for all
a\geq 0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{\prime}_{sn^{1/2}}ds\geq a\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{S}X_{s}ds\geq a\right)+\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}\left(T>S\right).

The claim is established by letting
S\to\infty
,(
76
) and (
75
).
∎

Recall Lemma
2.7
, where we established that the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds
from the previous statement equals the hitting time of zero of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with suitable parameters depending on
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
. As the distribution of such a stopping time is well-known, see Lemma
2.8
, this connection is the key element in the proof of the convergence in distribution of
\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds+\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta
as
\delta\to 0
, which is stated below.

Lemma 7.2
.

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
there exists a continuous random variable
\tilde{A}_{\alpha}
such that as
\delta\to 0

\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds+\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\tilde{A}_{\alpha},

where
X
satisfies (
1
) and thus depends on
\delta
through the initial condition
X_{0}=\delta^{-1}
. In particular, for
\alpha=0
,

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{A}_{0}\geq a)=\text{\emph{erf}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}e^{-7a/4}\right),\quad a\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Let us begin with the case
\alpha=0
. Abbreviate
\mathbb{P}_{0,7/4,1,1/\delta}
with
\mathbb{P}_{\delta}
. From Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
we obtain for all
a\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\delta}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds\geq a\right)=\left(\frac{7}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{\delta^{-1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a}^{\infty}\left(e^{7s/2}-1\right)^{-3/2}e^{7s/2}\exp\left(-\frac{7\delta^{-2}}{4(e^{7s/2}-1)}\right)ds.

The substitution
u=\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}(e^{7s/2}-1)^{-1/2}/2
leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds\geq a\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}(e^{7a/2}-1)^{-1/2}/2}\exp(-u^{2})du=\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}}{2\sqrt{e^{7a/2}-1}}\right).

(77)

As for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
we have
a-\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta\geq 0
when
\delta>0
is sufficiently small, we conclude that for all
a\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{P}_{\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds+\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta\geq a\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}}{2\sqrt{\exp(7a/2-2\ln\delta)-1}}\right)=\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}e^{-7a/4}\right).

We now turn to the general case with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, where we abbreviate
\mathbb{P}_{2\alpha,7/4,1,1/\delta}
with
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}
.
Furthermore, let
R
be an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process that under
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}
satisfies the SDE

dR_{s}=\left(2\alpha-\frac{7}{4}R_{s}\right)ds+dB_{s},\;s>0,\quad\text{with}\quad R_{0}=\delta^{-1}.

Then, the shifted process
\tilde{R}=R-8\alpha/7
is also an O-U process under
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}
, since

d\tilde{R}_{s}=-\frac{7}{4}\tilde{R}_{s}ds+dB_{s},\;s>0,\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{R}_{0}=\delta^{-1}-8\alpha/7.

If we define the stopping time

\tau_{x}=\inf\{s\geq 0:\tilde{R}_{s}=-8x/7\},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

then
\tau_{\alpha}=T(R)=\inf\{s\geq 0:R_{s}=0\}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}
. So, Lemma
2.7

implies that

\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds
and
\tau_{\alpha}
have the same distribution under
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}
. Further, we obtain analogously to (
77
) that

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}\left(\tau_{0}+\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta\geq a\right)=\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}-8\alpha/\sqrt{7}}{2\sqrt{\exp(7a/2-2\ln\delta)-1}}\right),\quad a\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, for all
a\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}X_{s}ds+\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta\geq a\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\delta}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\delta}\left(\tau_{0}+\frac{4}{7}\ln\delta\geq a-(\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0})\mid\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\delta}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}-8\alpha/\sqrt{7}}{2\sqrt{\exp(7(a-(\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0}))/2-2\ln\delta)-1}}\right)\right].

Note that
\tau_{0}-\tau_{\alpha}
is independent of
\delta
for
\delta
small enough, as for
\alpha\leq 0
, the difference
\tau_{0}-\tau_{\alpha}
represents the time the process
\tilde{R}
started in
-8\alpha/7
needs to hit
0
. For
\alpha\geq 0
, this observation follows by a very similar argument for
\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0}
. Together with the dominated convergence theorem, we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\delta}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}-8\alpha/\sqrt{7}}{2\sqrt{\exp(7(a-(\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0}))/2-2\ln\delta)-1}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}\delta^{-1}-8\alpha/\sqrt{7}}{2\sqrt{\exp(7(a-(\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0}))/2-2\ln\delta)-1}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2e^{7(a-(\tau_{\alpha}-\tau_{0}))/4}}\right)\right],

from which the claim readily follows.
∎

1.4
. Since (

) occurs with probability at least
