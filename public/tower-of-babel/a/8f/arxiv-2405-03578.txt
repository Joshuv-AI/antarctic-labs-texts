Equivariant algebraic K \mathrm{K} -theory and Artin L L -functions
Elden Elmanto, Ningchuan Zhang

Abstract
. In this paper, we generalize the Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjecture relating special values of Dedekind zeta functions to algebraic K \mathrm{K} -groups. The former has been settled by Rost–Voevodsky up to the Iwasawa Main Conjecture. Our generalization extends the scope of this conjecture to Artin L L -functions of Galois representations of finite, function, and totally real number fields. The statement of this conjecture relates norms of the special values of these L L -functions to sizes of equivariant algebraic K \mathrm{K} -groups with coefficients in an equivariant Moore spectrum attached to a Galois representation. We prove this conjecture in many cases, integrally, except up to a possible factor of powers of 2 2 in the non-abelian and totally real number field case. In the finite field case, we further determine the group structures of their equivariant algebraic K \mathrm{K} -groups with coefficients in Galois representations. At heart, our method lifts the Möbius inversion formula for factorizations of zeta functions as a product of L L -functions, to the E 1 E_{1} -page of an equivariant spectral sequence converging to equivariant algebraic K \mathrm{K} -groups. Additionally, the spectral Mackey functor structure on equivariant K \mathrm{K} -theory allows us to incorporate certain ramified extensions that appear in these L L -functions.

The study of the Riemann

1.1.
What is done in this paper

In this paper, we propose and prove in some cases, a

Inverting the order of the group erases the difference between equivariant homotopy groups and homotopy groups of the homotopy fixed points. A simpler version of

Remark 1.7

(Equivariant Moore spectra)

One of the key characters of the second author’s thesis

Let us recall that in classical topology, the Moore spectrum associated to an abelian group

.
2

2

This is different from

However, we note that the existence and uniqueness of equivariant Moore spectra are subtle questions. If

\pi^{G}_{*}(\mathrm{Ind}_{H}^{G}M)\cong\pi_{*}^{H}(M)

More precisely, if we have a representation of the form
\rho=\bigoplus_{i}\mathrm{Ind}_{H_{i}}^{G}\chi_{i}
, which is conjugate to a sum of inductions of abelian characters

on subgroups

, then we can construct a lift of it to the sphere spectrum by setting
\mathrm{M}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{i}\mathrm{Ind}_{H_{i}}^{G}\mathrm{M}(\chi_{i})^{\oplus[E:\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi_{i}]]}
; that this is a Moore spectrum for

Proposition

6.10

Theorem

1.3
for

reduces to the primitive abelian character

1.3.

To our knowledge, the first instance of a

1.4.
Future work

In future work, we hope to remove the assumptions in
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
. Two major ones are:

(1)

The Galois representations are assumed to be a sum of inductions of abelian characters on subgroups in
Theorem

1.2
.

(2)

In the number field case, we can only prove
Theorem

1.3
when
n
is even and the number fields are totally real.

The first assumption is related to the
Brauer Induction Theorem
(see
[
48
, Theorems 10.20]
), which says that the complex representation ring

of a finite group

is generated by abelian characters up to inductions. This only implies any complex representation is a
virtual sum
of inductions of abelian characters, not necessarily a

sum. See more discussions in
Remark

6.11
. In future works, we hope to construct integral equivariant Moore spectra for general Galois representations of finite groups as
G
-CW spectra. This will help us prove
Theorem

1.3
for general higher-dimensional Galois representations.

The second assumption is related to
regulators
of
\zeta
-functions and free parts of algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-groups of number fields. On the
\mathrm{K}
-theory side, our proof relies crucially on the finiteness of

when

is totally real. In this case, the equivariant cellular chain complex to compute the Bredon cohomology
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{G}(\mathbb{D}\mathrm{M}(\chi);\underline{\mathrm{K}}_{4n-1}(\mathcal{O}_{F}))
is a bounded complex of finite abelian groups. Hence the size of

is equal to the Euler number of the complex if it is the only non-zero cohomology group. On the

unless

holds up to powers of

is the leading coefficient in the Taylor expansion of

at

is the

for general number fields, using corresponding non-equivariant Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjecture for those number fields (see

2.
Zeta and

We begin with an exposition of some concepts in number theory and arithmetic algebraic geometry. We hope that the exposition will be friendly to homotopy theorists who are not necessarily well-acquainted with the apparatus of zeta and
L
-functions. We will follow Serre’s exposition in
[
47
]
.

2.1.
Zeta functions

To begin with, the most fundamental function in the subject is arguably the following:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a finite type
\mathbb{Z}
-scheme of which is relatively equidimensional over
\mathbb{Z}
of relative dimension
d
. We define the
Hasse–Weil zeta function
attached to
X
to be

\zeta(X,s)=\prod_{x\in|X|}\frac{1}{1-\#\kappa(x)^{-s}};

where
|X|
is the topological space (set) of closed points on the scheme
X
and
\#k
denotes the cardinality of a finite field
k
. Notice that the residue field
\kappa(x)
of a closed point
x\in|X|
is also finite, since
X
is finite type over
\mathbb{Z}
. This product is absolutely convergent over the domain
\mathrm{Re}(s)>d
in
\mathbb{C}
.

Example 2.2
.

If
X=\spec(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a finite field, then we have

\zeta(\mathbb{F}_{q},s)=\frac{1}{1-q^{-s}}.

Example 2.3
.

If the structure map
X\rightarrow\spec(\mathbb{Z})
factors through
\spec(\mathbb{F}_{q})\rightarrow\spec(\mathbb{Z})
for some finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then we can describe the Hasse-Weil zeta function in terms of point count. Namely, we have an equality (see proof in
Example

2.15
):

\zeta(X,s)=\exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})}{m}\cdot q^{-ms}\right).

This is often called the
Weil zeta function
of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-scheme
X
. In particular, when
X=\spec\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we recover the formula in
Example

2.2
.

Example 2.4
.

Suppose that
X=\spec(\mathcal{O}_{F})
where
F
is a number field and
\mathcal{O}_{F}
is its ring of integers. Then we recover the
Dedekind zeta function
, in the sense we get equalities:

(2.1)

\zeta_{F}(s):=\zeta(\mathcal{O}_{F},s)=\prod_{(0)\neq\mathfrak{p}\trianglelefteq\mathcal{O}_{F}}\left(1-(\#\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p})^{-s}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{(0)\neq\mathcal{I}\trianglelefteq\mathcal{O}_{F}}\frac{1}{(\#\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathcal{I})^{s}},

where the product and the summation range over all non-zero
prime
ideals and all non-zero ideals and of
\mathcal{O}_{F}
, respectively. The last equality boils down to the unique factorization of ideals in the Dedekind domain
\mathcal{O}_{F}
. When
F=\mathbb{Q}
, we recover the
Riemann zeta function
:

\zeta(s):=\zeta{Q}(s)=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}.

The next theorem records analytic properties of the Hasse-Weil zeta function in certain situations.

Theorem 2.5
.

The
\zeta
-functions in the three examples above admit meromorphic continuations to the complex plane.

Proof.

We give a sketch of proof for each case in turn.

(1)

When
X=\spec\mathbb{F}_{q}
, this is obvious since
\zeta(\mathbb{F}_{q},s)=\frac{1}{1-q^{-s}}
is a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
with a simple pole at
s=0
.

(2)

When
X
is a smooth variety of dimension
d
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the meromorphic continuation follows from the rationality part of the Weil Conjectures by Dwork in
[
18
]
. This can be proved using the Lefschetz fixed point theorem for étale cohomology. Namely, we have an identity
[
38
, Theorem 27.6]
:

\zeta(X,s)=\prod_{r=0}^{2d}\det\left[1-\mathrm{Fr}_{q}\cdot q^{-s}\mid\mathrm{H}^{r}_{\acute{e}t}(\overline{X};\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})\right]^{(-1)^{r+1}},

where
\overline{X}=X\times_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\spec\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
\mathrm{Fr}_{q}
is the
q
-th power Frobenius endomorphism on
\overline{X}
, and
\ell
is a prime number that does not divide
q
.

(3)

When
X=\spec\mathcal{O}_{F}
for a number field
F
, the summation (
2.1
) for the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{F}(s)
converges absolutely and uniformly when
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geq 1+\delta
for every
\delta>0
. One first extends
\zeta_{F}
to the strip
0<\mathrm{Re}(s)\leq 1
. The analytic continuation follows from the functional equation of
\zeta_{F}
in this region. This is a standard result in analytic number theory. See
[
41
, Corollary VII.5.11.(i)]
. ∎

Remark 2.6
.

The existence of analytic/meromorphic continuation of the Hasse-Weil
\zeta
-functions for finite type varieties over
\mathbb{Z}
is open in general. Some other known cases include
cellular
schemes like
\mathbb{A}^{d},\mathbb{P}^{d}
over
\mathcal{O}_{F}
(since their zeta functions are products of translations of
\zeta_{F}(s)
) and elliptic curves (as a consequence of the celebrated modularity theorem).

Proposition 2.7
.

In all three examples above,
\zeta(X,s)
is a rational number when
s
is a non-positive integer.

Proof.

Again, the case when
X
is a smooth variety over a finite field follows from the rationality part of the Weil Conjectures. For
X=\mathcal{O}_{F}
, one can first show
\zeta_{F}(1-n)=0
unless
F
is totally real and
n
is even. The rationality of
\zeta_{F}(1-2n)
when
F
is totally real was proved in
[
28
]
.
∎

2.2.
Artin
L
-functions of Galois representations

In this subsection, we introduce
L
-functions associated to Galois representations. We work with pseudo-Galois coverings of schemes to incorporate ramifications.

Definition 2.8
.

A finite surjective morphism of noetherian, normal, integral schemes
\pi\colon Y\to X
is called a
pseudo-Galois
covering if the induced map on function fields
k(X)\to k(Y)
is a finite Galois extension and the natural homomorphism
\mathrm{Aut}_{X}(Y)\to\mathrm{Gal}(k(Y)/k(X))
is an isomorphism. A ring map
R\to R^{\prime}
is called pseudo-Galois if the induced map
\spec R^{\prime}\to\spec R
is a pseudo-Galois covering. If the group
G=\mathrm{Gal}(k(Y)/k(X))
is fixed, we say that
\pi
is a
pseudo
G
-Galois cover
.

Remark 2.9
.

The concept in
Definition

2.8
was given the name pseudo-Galois extensions by Suslin and Voevodsky in
[
50
, Definition 5.5]
.

Example 2.10
.

(1)

Any Galois covering of normal integral schemes is pseudo-Galois.

(2)

Suppose that
X
is a normal integral scheme with function field
F
and let
F^{\prime}
be a finite, Galois extension of
F
. Let
Y
be the normalization of
X
into
F^{\prime}

[
49
,
Tag 035H
]
, then the map
Y\rightarrow X
is an example of a pseudo-Galois covering.

(3)

As a more classical version of the above example, we can consider
F^{\prime}/F
is a Galois extension of number fields. Then one can check this extension map restricts to their rings of integers
\mathcal{O}_{F}\to\mathcal{O}_{F^{\prime}}
, which is a pseudo-Galois extension of Dedekind domains.

(4)

Geometrically, a pseudo-Galois covering of Riemann surfaces is a ramified covering space. Given a compact Riemann surface
X
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between ramified coverings of
X
and Galois extensions of the field of meromorphic functions on
X
. See
[
20
, Theorem 8.12]
.

Let
\pi\colon Y\to X
be a pseudo
G
-Galois covering,
y\in|Y|
be a closed point and
x=\pi(y)
. We recall the following definitions and results needed to define Artin
L
-functions (see
[
49
,
Tag 0BSD
]
):

•

\mathcal{D}(y)=\{g\in G\mid g(y)=y\}
is the decomposition group of
y
.

•

One can show that there are natural surjections:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}(y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Gal}(\kappa(y)/\kappa(x)).}

•

\mathcal{I}(y)
, the inertia subgroup of
y
, is the kernel of the surjection above. The map
\pi
is unramified at
x
if
\mathcal{I}(y)
is trivial (for any
y
above
x
).

•

\mathrm{Fr}_{y/x}\in\mathcal{D}(y)/\mathcal{I}(y)
corresponds to the Frobenius element in
\mathrm{Gal}(\kappa(y)/\kappa(x))
under the isomorphism
\mathrm{Gal}(\kappa(y)/\kappa(x))\cong\mathcal{D}(y)/\mathcal{I}(y)
.

•

If
y^{\prime}
is another point above
x
such that
g(y)=y^{\prime}
for some
g\in G
. Then
g^{-1}\mathcal{D}(y^{\prime})g=\mathcal{D}(y)
,
g^{-1}\mathcal{I}(y^{\prime})g=\mathcal{I}(y)
, and
g^{-1}\mathrm{Fr}_{y^{\prime}/x}g=\mathrm{Fr}_{y/x}
.

•

We will write
\mathrm{Fr}_{x}
and
\mathcal{I}(x)
for the conjugacy classes of the element
\{\mathrm{Fr}_{y/x}\}
and the subgroups
\{\mathcal{I}(y)\}
for all
y
above
x
.

Definition 2.11
.

Suppose that
Y\rightarrow X
is a pseudo-Galois covering of normal integral schemes such that
G=\mathrm{Gal}(k(Y)/k(X))
. Let
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be a representation of
G
valued in a finite dimensional complex vector space
V=\mathbb{C}^{N}
. The
Artin
L
-function
of
\rho
is defined to be the Euler product:

L(X,\rho,s)=\prod_{x\in|X|}\frac{1}{\det\left[\operatorname{id}-\#\kappa(x)^{-s}\cdot\rho(\mathrm{Fr}_{x})\left|V^{\mathcal{I}(x)}\right.\right]}.

This product converges absolutely to a complex analytic function over the domain
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\dim X=\dim Y
. Notice in each Euler factor:

•

\mathrm{Fr}_{x}
is represented by an element
\mathrm{Fr}_{y/x}\in\mathcal{D}(y)/\mathcal{I}(y)\subseteq G/\mathcal{I}(y)
for some
y
above
x
. We are allowed to evaluate
\rho
on
\mathrm{Fr}_{y/x}
only after restricting the representation to the fixed point subspace
V^{\mathcal{I}(y)}
by the inertia subgroup;

•

the determinant does not depend on the choice of
y
over
x
.

Proposition 2.12

(
[
41
, Proposition VII.10.4]
)
.

The Artin
L
-functions satisfy the following identities with respect to operations on representations:

(1)

L(X,1_{G},s)=\zeta(X,s).
where
1_{G}
is the
1
-dimensional trivial representation of
G
.

(2)

(Additivity)
L(X,\rho_{1},s)\cdot L(X,\rho_{2},s)=L(X,\rho_{1}\oplus\rho_{2},s)
.

(3)

(Descent) Notice the Galois representation
\rho
factors uniquely as
\rho\colon G\twoheadrightarrow G/\ker\rho\xrightarrow{\rho^{\prime}}\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. Then

L(X,\rho,s)=L(X,\rho^{\prime},s),

where the pseudo-Galois covering on the right hand side is
Y/_{\ker\rho}\to X
. Thus it makes sense to drop the total space
Y
from the notation of an Artin
L
-function.

(4)

(Induction) Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
. Then for any finite-dimensional complex representation
\rho
of
H
, we have an induction formula:

L(X,\mathrm{Ind}_{H}^{G}\rho,s)=L(Y/_{H},\rho,s).

Corollary 2.13

(Factorization of zeta functions,
[
41
, Corollary VII.10.5]
)
.

For any normal subgroup
H\trianglelefteq G
, we have

\zeta(Y/_{H},s)=\prod_{H\subseteq\ker\rho}L(X,\rho,s)^{\dim\rho},

where
\rho
ranges through all irreducible complex representations of
G
whose kernels contain
H
. If
G
is abelian, write
G^{\vee}=\hom(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
for the group of its complex abelian characters. Then for any subgroup
H\leq G
, we have

\zeta(Y/_{H},s)=\prod_{\chi\in(G/H)^{\vee}}L(X,\chi,s),

where complex abelian characters of
G/H
are identified with those of
G
that are trivial on
H
.

Proof.

This follows from
Proposition

2.12
and the decomposition of the regular representation
\rho_{G/H}
of the quotient group
G/H
.

\zeta(Y/_{H},s)=L(X,\mathrm{Ind}_{H}^{G}1_{H},s)=L(X,\rho_{G/H},s)=L\left(X,\bigoplus_{H\subseteq\ker\rho}\rho^{\oplus\dim\rho},s\right)=\prod_{H\subseteq\ker\rho}L(X,\rho,s)^{\dim\rho}.

∎

Example 2.14
.

Let
Y/X=\spec\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\spec\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\chi\colon\mathrm{Aut}(Y/X)\cong C_{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a complex character. Denote the Frobenius element by
\mathrm{Fr}

L(\mathbb{F}_{q},\chi,s)=\frac{1}{1-\chi(\mathrm{Fr})q^{-s}}.

Example 2.15
.

When

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-scheme, the Artin

where
\rho
is an
E

of
\mathbb{C}
. It follows that the Artin

only depends on the character of the representation
\rho

is the special case of the one above when

is the

We see that the two expressions coincide as follows. For each closed point

with residue field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}

to be the closed point

are identified in

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{x\in X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})}\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho(\mathrm{Fr}_{x})|V^{\mathcal{I}(x)}\right]\cdot\frac{q^{-ms}}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{x\in X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})}\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho(\mathrm{Fr}_{x})|V^{\mathcal{I}(x)}\right]\cdot\frac{\#\kappa(x)^{-s}}{[\kappa(x):\mathbb{F}_{q}]}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{x\in|X|}\left([\kappa(x):\mathbb{F}_{q}]\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho(\mathrm{Fr}_{x_{m}})\mid V^{\mathcal{I}(x)}\right]\cdot\frac{\#\kappa(x_{m})^{-s}}{[\kappa(x_{m}):\mathbb{F}_{q}]}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{x\in|X|}\exp\left(\left.\mathrm{tr}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\rho(\mathrm{Fr}_{x})^{m}\frac{\#\kappa(x)^{-ms}}{m}\right)\right|V^{\mathcal{I}(x)}\right]

2.3.
Möbius inversion formulae

In
Corollary

2.13
, we express zeta functions as products of
L
-functions associated to irreducible representations of Galois groups. Ideally, we want a procedure to extract each
L(X,\chi,s)
for each character
\chi
and explain a Quillen–Lichtenbaum phenomenon for it. However, we are unable to separate out characters which are conjugate. In fact, by
[
16
, Theorem 1.2]
and
Proposition

2.18
, if the characters are conjugate then their special values are conjugate and therefore the same from the point-of-view of
\mathrm{K}
-theory. Noting that two abelian characters
\chi,\tau:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
are conjugate if and only if they have the same kernel, we prove the following key computational tool which should be thought of as a Möbius Inversion Formula.

Proposition 2.24

(Möbius Inversion Formula)
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Denote the group of complex characters of
G
by
G^{\vee}
. For a normal subgroup
H\trianglelefteq G
, we can view
(G/H)^{\vee}
as a subgroup of
G^{\vee}
by

(G/H)^{\vee}:=\hom(G/H,\mathbb{C}^{\times})\cong\{\chi\in G^{\vee}\mid H\subseteq\ker\chi\}.

Given a family of complex (meromorphic) functions
g(s,\chi)
indexed by
\chi\in G^{\vee}
, we define
f(s,G/H)
for each normal subgroup
H\trianglelefteq G
by setting

(2.3)

f(s,G/H):=\prod_{\chi\in(G/H)^{\vee}}g(s,\chi).

Then for any cyclic quotient
G/H\cong C_{m}
of
G
where
m=p_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda}^{d_{\lambda}}
is the prime factorization of
m
, we have the following Möbius Inversion Formula:

\prod_{\tau\in G^{\vee},\ker\tau=H}g(s,\tau)=\prod_{j=0}^{\lambda}\left(\prod_{1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{j}\leq\lambda}f\left(s,(G/H)/_{C_{p_{k_{1}}\cdots p_{k_{j}}}}\right)\right)^{(-1)^{j}},.

Here the
j=0
factor is set to be
f(s,G/H)
.

Proof.

On the right hand side of (
2.3
), separating primitive (injective) characters of
G/H
from the non-primitive ones, we obtain:

(2.4)

\prod_{\tau\in G^{\vee},\ker\tau=H}g(s,\tau)=\prod_{\tau\colon G/H\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}}g(s,\tau)=f(s,G/H)\cdot\left(\prod_{\tau\in(G/H)^{\vee}\text{, non-primitive}}g(s,\tau)\right)^{-1}

Notice any non-primitive character
\tau
of
G/H\cong C_{m}
must factor through a further quotient
(G/H)/_{C_{p_{i}}}\cong C_{m}/C_{p_{i}}
for some prime factor
p_{i}
of
m
. By (
2.3
), we have

(2.5)

\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}f(s,(G/H)/_{C_{p_{i}}})=\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}\prod_{\tau\in\left((G/H)/_{C_{p_{i}}}\right)^{\vee}}g(s,\tau).

Comparing this with (
2.4
), we observe the right hand side of (
2.5
) over-counts those characters
\tau\colon G/H\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
whose kernels contain
C_{p_{i}p_{j}}
for some distinct pair
p_{i},p_{j}
of prime factors of
m
. The Möbius inversion formula then follows from the Inclusion and Exclusion Principle.
∎

Next, we apply
Proposition

2.24
to the factorization formulas for zeta functions in

2.13
. For

.

Let

Proof.

By

(for Galois covers of varieties) and

3.1.
Reminders on
\mathrm{K}
-theory

The zeroth algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-group of a ring, denoted by
\mathrm{K}_{0}(R)
, is the Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective modules over
R
. While this is a relatively elementary definition, it stores rather subtle information about a ring. For example, say
D
is a Dedekind domain with fraction field
F
, then the ideal class group of
F
aka
\mathrm{Pic}(D)
is a summand of
\mathrm{K}_{0}(D)
; more precisely we have a canonical short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathrm{Pic}(D)\longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{0}(D)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow 0;

where the first map is defined by observing that any ideal of
D
is projective. At this point, as already mentioned in the introduction, the reader might know that
\mathrm{K}_{1}
has something to do with units. Indeed, if
R
is a field or, more generally, a semilocal ring then
\mathrm{K}_{1}(R)\cong R^{\times}
, the group of units in
R
. This could seem unmotivated
3
3

3

Unless one already knows that one is looking for a higher analog of the class number formula.
but there is a check for the “correctness” of the definition of the functor of higher
K
-groups. This comes under the name “Bass’ fundamental theorem”. Indeed, for any ring
R
(possibly even just an associative, unital ring) one should have a 4-term exact sequence (where the last map is, in fact, a split surjection)

0\rightarrow\mathrm{K}_{n}(R)\longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{n}(R[t])\oplus\mathrm{K}_{n}(R[t^{-1}])\longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{n}(R[t,t^{-1}])\overset{\partial}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{K}_{n-1}(R)\rightarrow 0.

We refer the reader to
[
57
, V.8]
for further explanation. Hence, once one has defined the functor of
\mathrm{K}_{n}
, one should recover the functor of
\mathrm{K}_{n-1}
. This principle even applies for any theory of negative
K
-groups.

Quillen solved the problem of defining higher
\mathrm{K}
-groups in
[
44
]
. He built on an earlier definition of his which is somewhat more elementary. Higher
\mathrm{K}
-theory was defined via Quillen’s
+
-construction
[
42
]
which is, informally, the universal way to abelianize the fundamental group of a space without changing its homology; we refer to
[
57
, IV. 1]
for a textbook discussion and
[
25
,
45
,
1
]
for modern exposition. The positive homotopy groups of the algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-theory of a ring is then calculated as

\mathrm{K}_{i}(R):=\pi_{i}(\mathrm{BGL}(R)^{+})\qquad i\geq 1,

where
\mathrm{BGL}(R):=\colim\mathrm{BGL}_{n}(R)
. This is sufficient to give meaning to the groups that appear in the Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjectures. From this point of view, the Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjectures is ultimately a relationship between the homotopy groups of the “derived abelianization” of the classifying space of
\mathrm{GL}_{\infty}(R)
and zeta functions.

For the purposes of this paper, algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-theory in this guise is not enough. For one, it is not true that
\mathrm{K}(R)
is functorially equivalent to
\mathrm{BGL}(R)^{+}\times\mathrm{K}_{0}(R)
.
4
4

4

If
R
is not regular, then we even have negative homotopy groups in
\mathrm{K}
-theory!
For another, we need certain extra structure of algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-theory that is not immediately visible using the
+
-construction. The viewpoint that we must take is the one taken by Blumberg-Gepner-Tabuada in
[
9
]
. To formulate this perspective, we set
\mathcal{C}\mathrm{at}{}^{\mathrm{perf}}_{\infty}
to be the
\infty
-category of small, stable idempotent
\infty
-categories. This
\infty
-category houses the
\infty
-category of perfect complexes on rings in the sense that there is a functor

\mathrm{Perf}\colon\mathrm{CAlg}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}\mathrm{at}{}^{\mathrm{perf}}_{\infty}.

Algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-theory is then a universal functor

\mathrm{K}\colon\mathcal{C}\mathrm{at}{}^{\mathrm{perf}}_{\infty}\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}\mathrm{pt}{},

which preserves final objects and bifiber sequences in
\mathcal{C}\mathrm{at}{}^{\mathrm{perf}}_{\infty}
(Verdier sequences); we refer to
[
13
, Appendix A]
for a modern exposition.

This viewpoint unveils clearly certain structures and properties that algebraic
K
-theory enjoys. One that is relevant for this paper are “wrong-way” maps for
\mathrm{K}
-groups. For example, if
f\colon R\rightarrow S
is a morphism of rings which is perfect

49

Tag 0685
]
and finite so that the forgetful functor on derived categories preserves perfect complexes

49

Tag 0B6G
]

then we have an induced map on
\mathrm{K}

\mathrm{K}_{*}(S)\rightarrow\mathrm{K}_{*}(R)
. Of course such a structure could be defined using more classical methods, but we will need to keep track of the coherences on this construction as we do via the language of spectral Mackey functors in
Section

4.2
.

The last thing that the reader should keep in mind, though perhaps not strictly needed for the purposes of this paper, is the relationship between
\mathrm{K}
-theory and étale/motivic cohomology. The relevance of motivic cohomology, then still a conjecture object, to values of zeta functions was posited by Beilinson
[
6

and Lichtenbaum

33

:

\mathrm{H}^{i-j}_{\mathrm{mot}}(X;\mathbb{Z}(-j))\Longrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{-i-j}(X).

Here

is a smooth scheme over field, in which case this spectral sequence is the work of many mathematicians, beginning with Bloch and Bloch–Lichtenbaum
[
8
]
, and then later Friedlander-Suslin
[
21
]
, Levine
[
32
,
31
]
and Voevodsky
[
54

The existence of the above spectral sequence indicates that one can access information stored in these higher
\mathrm{K}
-groups, once we can satisfactorily describe motivic cohomology. For the purposes of

-functions, the flagship theorem in this direction is the comparison of motivic cohomology with étale/syntomic cohomology as conjectured by Beilinson-Lichtenbaum and proved by to Rost–Voevodsky
[

3.2.
Statements of the classical Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjectures

The most basic case of the Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjecture comes from Quillen’s own computation of the
\mathrm{K}
-groups of finite fields. In
[
42
]
, he computed the groups as follows:

(3.2)

\mathrm{K}_{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&t=0;\\
\mathbb{Z}/(q^{n}-1),&t=2n-1>0;\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

From this, the Quillen–Lichtenbaum Conjecture reads as:

(3.3)

\zeta(\mathbb{F}_{q},-n)=\frac{1}{1-q^{n}}=-\frac{\#\mathrm{K}_{2n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}{\#\mathrm{K}_{2n-1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})},\qquad n\geq 1.

Notice this identity even “holds” at
n=0
in the sense that
\zeta(\mathbb{F}_{q},s)
has a simple pole at

\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\acute{e}t}(A^{\prime};\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(n))_{G}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\acute{e}t}(A;\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(n)),

where
\ell\neq 2
in the number field case and
\ell\nmid q
in the function field case. As both étale cohomology groups and the Galois group are finite, taking Pontryagin duals turns codescent into descent:

\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\acute{e}t}(A;\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(n))\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\acute{e}t}(A^{\prime};\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(n))^{G}.

By Rost–Voevodsky and the motivic spectral sequence, we have natural isomorphisms when
n\geq 2

\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\acute{e}t}(A;\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}(n))\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{K}_{2n-2}(A)^{\wedge}_{\ell}.

This yields the Galois descent on even-degree
\mathrm{K}
-groups when
n\geq 2

\mathrm{K}_{2n-2}(A)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{K}_{2n-2}(A^{\prime})^{G},\qquad\text{ (invert 2 in the number field case).}

Note that this argument fails when
A^{\prime}/A
is only a
pseudo
-Galois extension or when
\ell=2
in the number field case, because the first step in proving
[
30
, Proposition 2.12]
uses the Tate spectral sequence for Galois extensions.

5.
Equivariant Moore spectra and its cellular filtration

This section is the technical heart of the paper and uses crucially the construction of a cellular presentation of equivariant Moore spectra due to Rezk and the second author as in
[
59
, §3.3]
. The first key result here is

5.10
where the norms of

-values are incarnated as the ratio between Euler numbers of the

page of a spectral sequence built from a cellular filtration on equivariant Moore spectra. Turning the page once, we compute the Bredon cohomology of these Moore spectra with coefficients in equivariant algebraic
\mathrm{K}

5.1.
Equivariant Moore spectra of abelian characters

Let’s recall the construction of the equivariant Moore spectra following the second author’s thesis
[
59
]
. If
A
is an abelian group, then the Moore spectrum associated to
A
, denoted usually by
\mathrm{M}A
, is characterized uniquely as a
connective
spectrum whose homology group is given as follows:

\pi_{*}(\mathrm{M}A\otimes\mathrm{H}\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{H}_{*}(\mathrm{M}A;\mathbb{Z})=\begin{cases}A,&*=0;\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

In other words we have an equivalence

\mathrm{M}A\otimes\mathrm{H}\mathbb{Z}\simeq\mathrm{H}A.

Note, however, unlike the formation of Eilenberg-MacLane spectra, the formation of Moore spectra
\mathrm{M}A
is
not
functorial in
A
; this complication presents itself in the second author’s thesis.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
A
be an abelian group with an action by a group
G
via the homomorphism
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{Aut}(A)
. An
equivariant Moore spectrum
of
\rho
, if it exists, is a
G
-action on the Moore spectrum
\mathrm{M}A
such that the induced
G
-action on
\mathrm{H}_{0}(\mathrm{M}A;\mathbb{Z})=A
is isomorphic to
\rho
. In other words,
\mathrm{M}(\rho)
is a lifting

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}\mathrm{pt}{}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{B}G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Ab}=\mathsf{Ab}^{\{0\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Ab}{Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}_{*}(-;\mathbb{Z})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists\mathrm{M}(\rho)?}

Any complex representation
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
of a finite group
G
necessarily factors through
\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathcal{O}_{E})
for some number field
E
; we abusively call this representation
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathcal{O}_{E})
. An integral equivariant Moore spectrum
\mathrm{M}(\rho)
of
\rho
, if it exists, is a
G
-action on the Moore spectrum of
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\oplus N}
such that the induced action on its zeroth homology group is given by

.

Remark 5.2
.

Steenrod asked the question whether
\mathrm{M}(\rho)
always exists and Carlsson constructed a counterexample for the group
C_{2}\times C_{2}
in
[
14
]
.

Remark 5.3
.

Even if
\mathrm{M}(\rho)
exists, the lifting is not unique unless multiplication by the group order of
G
is an automorphism of

. One example is the trivial and sign representations of
C_{2}
on
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\sigma
be the sign representation of
C_{2}
. Then depending on the parity of

, any representation sphere of the form

is an equivariant Moore spectrum for

with the trivial or the sign

-action. Moreover, Beaudry–Goerss–Hopkins–Stojanoska showed in
[

that the set of homotopy classes of

-actions on

can be identified as:

[\mathrm{B}C_{2},\mathrm{B}\mathrm{GL}_{1}(\mathrm{S}^{0})]\overset{\sim}{\longleftarrow}[\mathrm{B}C_{2},\mathrm{BO}]=\widetilde{\mathrm{KO}}^{0}(\mathbb{RP}^{\infty})=\mathbb{Z}_{2}.

The Atiyah-Segal completion theorem implies that
\mathrm{S}^{n(1-\sigma)}
corresponds to the element
\pm n\in\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on the right hand side. Hence, the sets of homotopy classes of
C_{2}
-actions on
\mathrm{S}^{0}
that induce trivial and sign representations on

can be identified with

and

, respectively.

First, we give a construction of integral equivariant Moore spectra for abelian characters

for a finite group

-functions with sizes of equivariant algebraic

Construction 5.4

(Equivariant Moore spectra for abelian characters, see also

Let

(5.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi\colon G}

(1)

Remark 5.5

Technically, we can also set

Construction 5.6

(Equivariant cellular filtration on equivariant Moore spectra)

whose homology group concentrated in degree

5.2.
Bredon cohomology of equivariant Moore spectra of abelian characters

From
Corollary

5.10
, the mere existence of the cellular
E_{1}
-spectral sequence gives an interpretation of the norm of special values of these
L
-functions in terms of Euler numbers of the said spectral sequence. In this subsection, we compute these Bredon cohomology groups. The key point is the already established structure results of these Mackey functors — namely that they are weak fixed point Mackey functors as proved in
Section

4.3
.

The equivariant Moore spectra admits a cell structure whose cells are concentrated in non-positive (homological) degrees. Nonetheless, we show that the cohomology with coefficients in an arbitrary Mackey functor is concentrated only degrees
0
and
-1
. We will employ the following rather non-standard definition: a
weak fixed point Mackey functor
is an
\mathsf{Ab}
-valued Mackey functor
\underline{A}
such that the comparison map
\underline{A}(G/H)\rightarrow\underline{A}(G/e)^{H}
is an isomorphism.

Remark 5.11
.

Let
A
be an abelian with an action by a finite group
G
. In
[
52
, §6]
, the fixed point Mackey functor of this action is defined by setting
\underline{A}(G/H)=A^{H}
, the restriction maps being inclusions of fixed points, and transfer maps being relative trace maps.
Proposition

4.7
says any
G
-Mackey functor is a
weak
fixed point Mackey functor when the group order is levelwise invertible. However, they are not necessarily fixed point Mackey functors in the sense above, since their transfers maps are not specified. See
[
51
, Corollary 6.5]
for a classification of
G
-Mackey functors valued in
k
-vector spaces where
|G|
is invertible in the field
k
.

We will need the following computation of Bredon cohomology of the equivariant Moore spectra.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
\underline{A}
be a weak fixed point
C_{m}
-Mackey functor and write
A:=\underline{A}(G/e)
. Then

\mathrm{H}_{C_{m}}^{*}(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m});\underline{A})=\begin{cases}A/\left[\cup_{i=1}^{\lambda}A^{C_{p_{i}}}\right],&*=0;\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
[\cup_{i=1}^{\lambda}A^{C_{p_{i}}}]
is the additive closure of the subset
\cup_{i=1}^{\lambda}A^{C_{p_{i}}}
in
A
.

Proof.

From the description of the
C_{m}
-CW structure of
\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m})
in
5.6
, we know that the equivariant cellular chain complex to compute this Bredon cohomology is

\displaystyle\underline{A}(C_{m}/C_{p_{1}\cdots p_{\lambda}})\to\cdots\to\bigoplus_{1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{s}\leq\lambda}\underline{A}(C_{m}/C_{p_{k_{1}}\cdots p_{k_{s}}})\to\cdots\to\underline{A}(C_{m}/e)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{C_{p_{1}\cdots p_{\lambda}}}\to\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\lambda}A^{C_{p_{1}\cdots\widehat{p_{k}}\cdots p_{\lambda}}}\to\cdots\to\bigoplus_{1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{s}\leq\lambda}A^{C_{p_{k_{1}}\cdots p_{k_{s}}}}\to\cdots\to\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\lambda}A^{C_{p_{k}}}\to A,

where the degrees of the first and the last terms are
-l
and
0
, respectively. The differentials in this complex are alternating sums of inclusions of fixed points. We want to prove cohomology of this complex is concentrated in the top degree
0
. As
C_{p_{1}\cdots p_{\lambda}}
is canonically isomorphic to the product
C_{p_{1}}\times\cdots\times C_{p_{\lambda}}
, we have an identification of subsets (groups) of
A
:

A^{C_{p_{k_{1}}\cdots p_{k_{s}}}}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{s}A^{C_{p_{k_{j}}}}.

In this sense, the complex is a Čech complex for the partial covering
\cup A^{C_{p_{j}}}
of
A
. The claim is then a result of the following lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
A
be an abelian group and
\{A_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
be a finite collection of subgroups of
A
. Consider the following Čech cochain complex
\check{C}^{\bullet}\{A_{i}\subseteq A\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
:

\displaystyle 0\to\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{k=1}^{n}\bigcap_{i\neq k}A_{i}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{s}\leq n}\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}A_{k_{i}}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\longrightarrow A\to 0,

where the differentials are alternating sums of inclusions of subgroups (intersections). Suppose the complex is graded so that the degree of
A
is zero. Then

\mathrm{H}^{*}(\check{C}^{\bullet}\{A_{i}\subseteq A\mid 1\leq i\leq n\})=\begin{cases}A/[\cup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}],&*=0;\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We prove this by induction. When
n=0
, the statement is trivial since the cochain complex is
[0\to A\to 0]
concentrated in degree
0
.

Suppose the claim has been verified for collections of
n-1
subgroups of
A
. Note that we have a short exact sequence of cochain complexes:

\displaystyle 0\to\check{C}^{\bullet}\{A_{i}\subseteq A\mid 1\leq i\leq n-1\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\check{C}^{\bullet}\{A_{i}\subseteq A\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\check{C}^{\bullet}\{(A_{i}\cap A_{n})\subseteq A_{n}\mid 1\leq i\leq n-1\}[-1]\to 0.

By the inductive hypothesis, the only non-zero part of the long exact sequence of cohomology groups of this short exact sequence is

\displaystyle 0\to\mathrm{H}^{-1}\left(\check{C}^{\bullet}\{A_{i}\subseteq A\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow A_{n}/[\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}(A_{n}\cap A_{i})]

\displaystyle\overset{\iota_{n}}{\longrightarrow}A/[\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}]\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{0}\left(\check{C}^{\bullet}\{A_{i}\subseteq A\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}\right)\to 0.

The second term above is equal to
A_{n}/(A_{n}\cap[\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}A_{i}])
. Then it is straight forward to check that the map
\iota_{n}
is injective with cokernel
A/[\cup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}]
. This proves the inductive step.
∎

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
\underline{A}
be a
C_{m}
-Mackey functor.
Then

\mathrm{H}_{C_{m}}^{*}(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m});\underline{A})=0,\qquad*\not=0,-1.

Proof.

By

By the induction formulas for Bredon cohomology, we have isomorphisms:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{*}_{C_{m}}\left(\left(C_{p^{v}}\right)_{+}\boxtimes\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m^{\prime}});\underline{A}\right)

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{H}^{*}_{C_{m^{\prime}}}\left(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m^{\prime}});\underline{A}(C_{p^{v}}\times-)\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{*}_{C_{m}}\left(\left(C_{p^{v}}/C_{p}\right)_{+}\boxtimes\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m^{\prime}});\underline{A}\right)

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{H}^{*}_{C_{m^{\prime}}}\left(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m^{\prime}});\underline{A}(C_{p^{v}}/C_{p}\times-)\right).

Notice both

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{0}_{C_{m^{\prime}}}(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m^{\prime}});\underline{A}(C_{p^{v}}\times-))\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{0}_{C_{m}}(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m});\underline{A})\to 0\to\cdots.

∎

6.1.

We are now ready to assemble the proof of

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\chi\colon\mathrm{Gal}\left(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a primitive/injective character. Then

\mathrm{Norm}_{\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi]/\mathbb{Q}}L(\mathbb{F}_{q},\chi,-n)=\pm\frac{\#\pi_{2n}^{C_{m}}\left(\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\otimes\mathrm{M}(\chi)\right)}{\#\pi_{2n-1}^{C_{m}}\left(\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\otimes\mathrm{M}(\chi)\right)}\qquad n\geq 1,

where the minus sign is taken only

Proof.

Consider the
E_{1}

E^{1}_{s,t}=\bigoplus_{1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{s}\leq\lambda}\mathrm{K}_{t}\left(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m/{(p_{k_{1}}\cdots p_{k_{s}})}}}\right)\Longrightarrow\pi_{s+t}^{C_{m}}\mathrm{Map}\left(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m}),\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\right),d^{r}_{s,t}\colon E^{r}_{s,t}\to E^{r}_{s-r,t+r-1}.

By

5.10
, we have an equality:

\mathrm{Norm}_{\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi]/\mathbb{Q}}L(\mathbb{F}_{q},\chi,-n)=(-1)^{\dim{Q}\chi^{C_{m}}}\cdot\frac{\text{Euler number }(E^{*,2n}_{1},d_{1})}{\text{Euler number }(E^{*,2n-1}_{1},d_{1})}.

The
E_{2}
-page of the spectral sequence is

E_{2}^{s,t}=\mathrm{H}_{C_{m}}^{s}(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m});\underline{\mathrm{K}}_{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}))\Longrightarrow\pi_{s+t}^{C_{m}}\mathrm{Map}\left(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m}),\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\right).

Proposition

4.9
says the algebraic
\mathrm{K}
-groups
\underline{\mathrm{K}}_{t}(-)
is a weak fixed point Mackey functor of finite abelian groups. By

5.12
, we have

\mathrm{H}_{C_{m}}^{s}(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m});\underline{\mathrm{K}}_{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}))\neq 0

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Euler number }(E^{*,2n}_{1},d_{1})}{\text{Euler number }(E^{*,2n-1}_{1},d_{1})}=\frac{\#E_{2}^{0,2n}}{\#E_{2}^{0,2n-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\#\pi_{2n}^{C_{m}}\mathrm{Map}\left(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m}),\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\right)}{\#\pi_{2n-1}^{C_{m}}\mathrm{Map}\left(\mathrm{M}(\psi_{m}),\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\right)}=\frac{\#\pi_{2n}^{C_{m}}\left(\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\otimes\mathrm{M}(\chi)\right)}{\#\pi_{2n-1}^{C_{m}}\left(\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\otimes\mathrm{M}(\chi)\right)}.

∎

Corollary 6.2
.

We have

\pi_{*}^{C_{m}}(\mathrm{K}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})\otimes\mathrm{M}(\chi))=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}/\mathrm{Norm}_{\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi]/\mathbb{Q}}L(\mathbb{F}_{q},\chi,-n)^{-1}\cong\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Im}\chi]/L(\mathbb{F}_{q},\chi,-n)^{-1},&*=2n-1>0;\\
0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

6.2.

In the last step to prove the Main

Proposition 6.10
.

Let

be a number field and
\rho\colon G\to\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathcal{O}_{E})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be an

is isomorphic to a sum of inductions of abelian characters on subgroups of

G
-CW complex structure on the Moore spectrum
\mathrm{M}(\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\oplus N})
such that induced action on the homology groups is isomorphic to
\rho

Proof.

The case of abelian characters has been explained in

. Suppose
\rho=\bigoplus_{i}\mathrm{Ind}_{H_{i}}^{G}\chi_{i}
(

can be repeated) and each equivariant Moore spectrum
\mathrm{M}(\chi_{i})
of

has been constructed. Then set

\mathrm{M}(\rho):=\bigoplus_{i}\mathrm{Ind}_{H_{i}}^{G}\left(\mathrm{M}(\chi_{i})^{\oplus[E:\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi_{i}]]}\right).

Notice when
\zeta_{m}\in E
, we have
\mathcal{O}_{E}
is
C_{m}
-equivariantly isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{m}]^{\oplus[E:\mathbb{Q}[\zeta_{m}]]}
, where the group acts by multiplication by roots of unity. It follows that
\mathrm{M}(\chi_{i})^{\oplus[E:\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi_{i}]]}
is an equivariant Moore spectrum for the character
\widetilde{\chi}_{i}\colon H\xrightarrow{\chi_{i}}\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Im}\chi_{i}]^{\times}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
after scalar extensions. Then we have

-equivariant isomorphisms:

\mathrm{H}_{0}(\mathrm{M}(\rho);\mathbb{Z})\cong\bigoplus_{i}\mathrm{Ind}_{H_{i}}^{G}\left(\mathrm{H}_{0}(\mathrm{M}(\chi_{i});\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus[E:\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{Im}\chi_{i}]]}\right)\cong\bigoplus_{i}\mathrm{Ind}_{H_{i}}^{G}\widetilde{\chi}_{i}=\rho.\qed

Remark 6.11
.

The Brauer Induction Theorem (see
