1.
Introduction

An entire function
E(z)
is called a
Bank–Laine function
if
E(z)=0
implies that
E^{\prime}(z)\in\{-1,1\}
. In particular,
E(z)
is called
special
if
E(z)=0
implies that
E^{\prime}(z)=1
. It is known that every Bank–Laine function
E
is written as
E=f_{1}f_{2}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are two normalized solutions of the second order differential equation

(1.1)

f^{\prime\prime}+A(z)f=0,

where
A(z)
is an entire function; see
[
9
, Proposition 6.4]
. All solutions of equation (
1.1
) are entire functions. A pair
(f_{1},f_{2})
of solutions of (
1.1
) is called
normalized
if
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are linearly independent and the
Wronskian determinant

W(f_{1},f_{2})=f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime}-f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}
, which is a nonzero constant, is equal to
1
. Denote
F=f_{2}/f_{1}
. Then
F
is a locally univalent meromorphic function. The quotient
F/F^{\prime}=f_{1}f_{2}
is a Bank–Laine function, and all Bank–Laine functions arise in this way. Note that a normalized pair
(f_{1},f_{2})
can be recovered from
F
by the formulas
f_{1}^{2}=1/F^{\prime}
and
f_{2}^{2}=F^{2}/F^{\prime}
. Zeros of a Bank–Laine function
E
are zeros and poles of
F
, and
E
is special if and only if
F
is entire. See
[
9
]
for the basics about the second order differential equation (
1.1
).

Bank and Laine
[
1
,
2
]
initiated the study on the complex oscillation of second order linear differential equation (
1.1
) in the framework of Nevanlinna theory. See
[
8
,
9
]
for the basic notation and fundamental results of Nevanlinna theory. Recall that the
order
of an entire function
f
is defined as

\rho(f)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M(r,f)}{\log r},

where
M(r,f)
is the maximum modulus of
f
on the circle
|z|=r
. When
A
is transcendental, an application of the lemma on the logarithmic derivative easily yields that all nontrivial solutions of (
1.1
) satisfy
\rho(f)=\infty
. Also recall that the
exponent of convergence
of zeros of an entire function
f
is defined as

\lambda(f)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log n(r,0,f)}{\log r},

where
n(r,0,f)
denotes the number of zeros of
f
in the disc
\{z:|z|\leq r\}
. Bank and Laine
[
1
,
2
]
proved: For two normalized solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of equation (
1.1
) and
E=f_{1}f_{2}
, if
\rho(A)\not\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\lambda(E)\geq\rho(A)
; if
\rho(A)<1/2
, then
\lambda(E)=\infty
. Later, Shen
[
13
]
and Rossi
[
12
]
relaxed the condition
\rho(A)<1/2
to the case
\rho(A)=1/2
independently, where they actually showed that if
1/2\leq\rho(A)<1
, then the conclusion
\lambda(E)\geq\rho(A)
can be improved to satisfy the inequality

(1.2)

\frac{1}{\rho(A)}+\frac{1}{\lambda(E)}\leq 2.

Based on these results, Bank and Laine conjectured that
\max\{\lambda(f_{1}),\lambda(f_{2})\}=\infty
whenever
\rho(A)\not\in\mathbb{N}
; see also
[
9
, Chapter 4]
.
The Bank–Laine conjecture
has attracted much interest; see the surveys
[
7
,
10
]
and references therein. However, Bergweiler and Eremenko
[
4
,
5
]
disproved this conjecture by constructing counterexamples for the coefficient
A
such that
\rho(A)
is not an integer and equation (
1.1
) has two linearly independent solutions such that
\lambda(f_{1})<\infty
and
f_{2}
has no zeros.

All Bank–Laine functions have order of growth at least
1
. Bergweiler and Eremenko’s construction shows for the first time that there exists a special Bank–Laine function
E
such that
\lambda(E)=\lambda
for any
\lambda\in[1,\infty)
. Though the construction is very sophisticated, Bergweiler and Eremenko have started from a family of locally univalent meromorphic functions
F=f_{2}/f_{1}
with
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
being two normalized solutions such that
\lambda(f_{i})<\infty
,
i=1,2
, of the second order differential equation

(1.3)

f^{\prime\prime}-\left(\frac{1}{4}e^{2z}+\frac{1}{2}b_{1}e^{z}+b_{2}\right)f=0,

where
b_{1}\not=0
and
b_{2}
are two constants. Indeed, all nontrivial solutions of the equation
f^{\prime\prime}+(e^{z}-K)f=0
such that
\lambda(f)<\infty
have been clearly characterized in
[
3
]
; see also
[
9

. In

of equation (
1.3
) and, in particular, proved the following

Theorem 1.1

(
[
14

Let

and
b_{2}
be two constants. Suppose that (
1.3

. Then

,

and

, where

a_{0}
,

are constants such that
a_{0}a_{k}\not=0
and

\begin{split}2c_{0}(k+1-i)a_{i-1}=(2ic+i^{2})a_{i},\qquad i=1,\cdots,k.\end{split}

Note that equation (
1.3
) takes a slightly different form from that in
[
14
]
. By Theorem
1.1
we give a different formulation from the results in

and

such that

if and only if there are two distinct nonnegative integers

such that

and

. In particular, it is possible that
\min\{\lambda(f_{1}),\lambda(f_{2})\}=0
. Letting

, then equation (

) takes the form

(1.4)

f^{\prime\prime}-\left[\frac{1}{4}e^{2z}+\frac{1}{2}(k_{2}-k_{1})e^{z}+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}+1)^{2}}{4}\right]f=0.

Choosing
c_{0}=-1/2
, we may write the two linearly independent solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
as

\begin{split}f_{1}=\kappa_{1}e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{z}}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}a_{i}e^{iz}\right)e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{z}},\end{split}

where

\begin{split}-(k_{1}+1-i)a_{i-1}=(2ic+i^{2})a_{i},\qquad i=1,\cdots,k_{1}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}f_{2}=\kappa_{2}e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{z}}=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k_{2}}b_{j}e^{jz}\right)e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{z}},\end{split}

where

\begin{split}(k_{2}+1-j)b_{j-1}=(2jc+j^{2})b_{j},\qquad j=1,\cdots,k_{2}.\end{split}

Suppose that
(f_{1},f_{2})
is a normalized pair of solutions of (
1.4
). It is easy to see that
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
should satisfy
a_{k_{1}}b_{k_{2}}=\frac{k_{1}!k_{2}!}{[(k_{1}+k_{2})!]^{2}}a_{0}b_{0}=1
and a simple calculation shows that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{i}=\frac{k_{2}!}{(i+k_{2})!}a_{0},&\qquad i=1,\cdots,k_{1},\\
b_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}k_{1}!}{(j+k_{1})!}b_{0},&\qquad j=1,\cdots,k_{2}.\end{array}\right.

Let

\begin{split}F=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k_{2}}b_{j}e^{jz}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}a_{i}e^{iz}}\right)e^{e^{z}}.\end{split}

We choose
a_{0}=b_{0}
. Let
A_{0}=B_{0}=1
and
A_{i}=a_{i}/a_{0}
and
B_{j}=b_{j}/b_{0}
. For every pair
(m,n)
of two non-negative integers
m
and

, we consider the rational function

(1.5)

\begin{split}R_{m,n}(z)=\frac{P_{n}(z)}{Q_{m}(z)}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n}B_{j}z^{j}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}z^{i}}.\end{split}

Thus we may write

\begin{split}h_{m,n}(z)=R_{m,n}(z)e^{z}=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n}B_{j}z^{j}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}z^{i}}\right)e^{z}.\end{split}

It follows that
A_{m}B_{2n}=\frac{m!(2n)!}{[(m+2n)!]^{2}}=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}
.
Then the meromorphic (entire when
m=0
) function

(1.6)

\begin{split}g_{m,n}(z)=h_{m,n}(e^{z})=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n}B_{j}e^{jz}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{iz}}\right)e^{e^{z}}\end{split}

satisfies

(1.7)

\begin{split}g^{\prime}_{m,n}(z)=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}\frac{e^{e^{z}+(m+2n+1)z}}{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{iz}\right)^{2}}.\end{split}

Since
A_{j}
are all positive constants,
g^{\prime}_{m,n}(z)\not=0
for all

and

is increasing on
\mathbb{R}
, and satisfies

as

as well as
g_{m,n}(x)\to+\infty
as

. Bergweiler and Eremenko’s construction starts from the functions

in (

) with

and two or infinitely many different even numbers

. We shall consider the generic choice of

and prove the following

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\gamma>1
. Then there exists a sequence
(n_{k})
of nonnegative integers, with
n_{1}=0
, such that the function
h:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
which satisfies
h(0)=0
and which is linear on the intervals
[2\pi(k-1),2\pi k]
and has slope
2n_{k}+1
there satisfies

(2.1)

\begin{split}h(x)=x^{\gamma}+O(x^{\gamma-2})+O(1),\quad x\to\infty\end{split}

and

(2.2)

\begin{split}2n_{k}+1=\gamma(2\pi k)^{\gamma-1}+O\left(k^{\gamma-2}\right)+O(1),\quad k\to\infty.\end{split}

Moreover, letting
\alpha:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a function such that
\alpha(x)=(\log(x+2\pi))^{-2}
and
\mathfrak{h}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a function linear and non-decreasing on each interval
[2\pi(k-1),2\pi k]
such that
\mathfrak{h}(0)=0
and
\mathfrak{h}(x)=O(\alpha(x)x^{\gamma})
as
x\to\infty
, then the function
g(x)
defined on
[0,\infty)
by
h(g(x))+\mathfrak{h}(g(x))=x^{\gamma}
satisfies

(2.3)

\begin{split}g(x)=x+O(\alpha(x)x)+O(x^{-1})+O(x^{1-\gamma}),\quad x\to\infty\end{split}

and

(2.4)

\begin{split}g^{\prime}(x)=1+O(\alpha(x))+O(x^{-1})+O(x^{1-\gamma}),\quad x\to\infty,\end{split}

where
g^{\prime}
denotes either the left or right derivative of

.

Proof.

In the case
\gamma>1
, the proof for the two estimates (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are the same as that of
[
5
, Lemma 3.1]
. To prove (
2.3
), we note that (
2.1
) implies that

where
\omega(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. Using (
2.1
) again we see that

\begin{split}h(g(x))+\mathfrak{h}(g(x))=x^{\gamma}=x^{\gamma}[1+\gamma\omega(x)+O(\omega(x)^{2})]+O(x^{\gamma-2})+O(1)+O(\alpha(x)x^{\gamma})\end{split}

as
x\to\infty
. Note that the two terms

and

above vanish in the case
\gamma=1
. This yields

\begin{split}\omega(x)=O(\alpha(x))+O(x^{-2})+O(x^{-\gamma}),\end{split}

from which (

) follows. Similarly as in
[

, Lemma 3.1]
, we have

\begin{split}(h+\mathfrak{h})^{\prime}(x)=\gamma x^{\gamma-1}+O(x^{\gamma-2})+O(1)+O(\alpha(x)x^{\gamma-1})\end{split}

and thus

\begin{split}(h+\mathfrak{h})^{\prime}(g(x))=\gamma x^{\gamma-1}+O(x^{\gamma-2})+O(1)+O(\alpha(x)x^{\gamma-1}).\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\gamma x^{\gamma-1}}{(h+\mathfrak{h})^{\prime}(g(x))}=1+O(\alpha(x))+O(x^{-1})+O(x^{1-\gamma}),\end{split}

which is (
2.4
). This completes the proof.

M=\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}+1
. From now on we shall assume that there exists a constant
C>1
such that
\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}\leq C(m+2n)
. Then clearly

(2.42)

\begin{split}M\leq CN.\end{split}

The estimate in (
2.17
) implies that
C\to 1
as
N\to\infty
by (
2.2
). Below we begin to analyse the asymptotic behavior of
\phi(z)
and prove the following

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
M,N
be two integers as before. Then

(1)

if
\mathfrak{m}>m
, then
\phi(x)
has at least one fixed point
p
and each fixed point
p
of
\phi
satisfies
p=\log N+O(1)
as
k\to\infty
;

(2)

if
\mathfrak{m}=m
, then
\phi(x)
has uniquely one fixed point
p
such that
p=\log N+O(1)
as
k\to\infty
;

(3)

if
\mathfrak{m}<m
, then
\phi(x)
has uniquely one fixed point
p
such that
p=\frac{l-1}{l}\log N+O(1)
for an integer
l=M-N
as
k\to\infty
;

(4)

in all of the above three cases,
\phi(x)<x
for
x<p+O(1)
and
\phi(x)>x
for
x>p+O(1)
and, moreover, we have
\phi(x)\leq\log N+O(1)
for all
x\leq\log N
.

Proof.

Let
d:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
d(x)=g_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}}(x)-g_{m,n}(x)
. We consider the zeros of
d
. Now

(2.43)

\begin{split}d^{\prime}(x)=\left[e^{(M-N)x}-\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n})!}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}\frac{\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{i}=0}^{\mathfrak{m}}A_{\mathfrak{i}}e^{\mathfrak{i}x}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{ix}\right)^{2}}\right]D(e^{x}),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}D(e^{x})=\frac{1}{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n})!}\frac{e^{(m+2n+1)x}e^{e^{x}}}{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\mathfrak{m}}A_{i}e^{ix}\right)^{2}}.\end{split}

Note that
D(e^{x})\not=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
2.2
it is possible that
\mathfrak{m}<m
or
\mathfrak{m}=m
or
\mathfrak{m}>m
, and the case
\mathfrak{m}<m
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{m}=0
and
m=1
, occurs only when
\delta\gamma<1
.

When
M>N
, from (
2.21
) we see that
d(x)<0
if
x
is negative and of sufficiently large modulus and increases to
+\infty
as
x\to+\infty
. This implies that
\phi
has at least one fixed point.
In particular, if
\mathfrak{m}\leq m
, since
e^{(M-N)x}
is strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
, (
2.43
) implies that the derivative
d^{\prime}(x)
has exactly one sign change, namely at the point
q
, such that
d^{\prime}(x)<0
when
x<q
and
d^{\prime}(x)>0
when
x>q
. This implies that
\phi
has exactly one fixed point
p>q
. In the case
\mathfrak{m}>m
, it is possible that
(M-N)\geq 2(\mathfrak{m}-m)
or
(M-N)<2(\mathfrak{m}-m)
, the derivative
d^{\prime}(x)
may have more than one sign change, namely at the point
q
, which implies that
\phi
may have more than one fixed point.

We denote
p
to be a fixed point of
\phi
. To determine the asymptotic behavior of
p
as
N\to\infty
, we
note (
2.21
) implies

(2.44)

\begin{split}&\ -\log\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}M!+Mp-2\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{p})-\log\left(1+\frac{e^{p}}{M}\right)\\
=&\ -\log\binom{m+2n}{m}N!+Np-2\log Q_{m}(e^{p})-\log\left(1+\frac{e^{p}}{N}\right)+\beta(k).\end{split}

Firstly, in the case
\mathfrak{m}>m
, we write
p=\log N+r
. Then it follows from (
2.44
) that

\begin{split}(M-N)r=\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}M!}{\binom{m+2n}{m}N!}-(M-N)\log N+\log\frac{1+\frac{N}{M}e^{r}}{1+e^{r}}+2\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})}{Q_{m}(Ne^{r})}+\beta(k).\end{split}

Now,

\begin{split}&\ \left|\log\frac{1+\frac{N}{M}e^{r}}{1+e^{r}}\right|=\left|\log\left(1-\frac{M-N}{M}\frac{e^{r}}{1+e^{r}}\right)\right|\\
\leq&\ \log\left(1-\frac{M-N}{M}\right)\leq\left|\log\frac{N}{M}\right|=\log\left(1+\frac{M-N}{N}\right)\leq\frac{M-N}{N}.\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}r=\frac{\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}}+\log\frac{M!}{N!}-(M-N)\log N+2\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})}{Q_{m}(Ne^{r})}}{M-N}+\beta(k).\end{split}

An application of Stirling’s formula now yields

\begin{split}r=\frac{\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}}}{M-N}+\frac{M\log\frac{M}{N}}{M-N}-1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log\frac{M}{N}}{M-N}+2\frac{\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})}{Q_{m}(Ne^{r})}}{M-N}+\beta(k).\end{split}

Note that

(2.45)

\begin{split}\frac{\log\frac{M}{N}}{M-N}\sim\frac{\log\frac{M}{N}}{N(M/N-1)}\leq\frac{1}{N}.\end{split}

Thus we may write the asymptotic formula for
r
in the form

(2.46)

\begin{split}r=\frac{\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}}}{M-N}+2\frac{\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})}{Q_{m}(Ne^{r})}}{M-N}+\beta(k).\end{split}

We need to show that
r=O(1)
as
N\to\infty
. When
0\leq\delta\gamma\leq 1
, we know from Lemma
2.2
that
m_{k}
is equal to either
0
or
1
. Since
\mathfrak{m}>m
, we have

\begin{split}1<\frac{(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n})!}{(m+2n)!}\frac{m!}{\mathfrak{m}!}\frac{(2n)!}{(2\mathfrak{n})!}=\frac{(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n})\cdots(m+2n+1)}{\mathfrak{m}\cdots(m+1)\cdot(2\mathfrak{n})\cdots(2n+1)}\leq C^{M-N}.\end{split}

Therefore,

(2.47)

\begin{split}0<\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}}=\log\frac{[(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n})!][m!][(2n)!]}{[(m+2n)!][\mathfrak{m}!][(2\mathfrak{n})!]}\leq(M-N)\log C.\end{split}

Moreover, by (
2.14
) and the function
x\to(x\log x)/(x-1)
is increasing on the interval
(1,C]
and since
\lim_{x\to 1}(x\log x)/(x-1)=1
, we deduce from (
2.46
) that

(2.48)

\begin{split}r=2\frac{\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})}{Q_{m}(Ne^{r})}}{M-N}+O(1).\end{split}

Recall that
A_{i}=\frac{(2n)!}{(i+2n)!}
,
i=1,\cdots,m
. When
r\leq 0
, if
r
has large modulus, then by (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) we see that
\frac{N}{i+2n}
is bounded and thus
\frac{N}{i+2n}e^{r}<1/2
for all
i=1,\cdots,m
so that
\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})/Q_{m}(Ne^{r})=O(1)
. Thus by (
2.46
) we see that
r=O(1)
when
r\leq 0
. On the other hand, if
r>0
, we suppose that
r
is large. If
\delta\gamma\leq 1
, then
\mathfrak{m}=1
and
m=0
and thus
\log Q_{1}(Ne^{r})=\log(1+e^{r})=r+o(1)
. Since
M-N=2(\mathfrak{n}-n)-1
we see that
M-N=1,3,\cdots
, which together with (
2.48
) implies that
r=O(1)
. If
\delta\gamma>1
, in a similar way we have

(2.49)

\begin{split}\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})=\mathfrak{m}r+\log(N^{\mathfrak{m}}A_{\mathfrak{m}})+O(1)=\mathfrak{m}r+O(1).\end{split}

Moreover, by (
2.7
) we see that
\mathfrak{m}\leq 2m
. Together with (
2.45
) we have

(2.50)

\begin{split}\frac{\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(Ne^{r})}{Q_{m}(Ne^{r})}}{M-N}=\frac{\mathfrak{m}-m}{M-N}r+\frac{(\mathfrak{m}-m)\log\frac{N}{M}}{M-N}+O(1)=\frac{\mathfrak{m}-m}{M-N}r+O(1).\end{split}

By Lemma
2.2
,
\mathfrak{m}-m
is an odd integer. Since
M-N=2(\mathfrak{n}-n)-(\mathfrak{m}-m)
, we see that
2(\mathfrak{m}-m)/(M-N)\not=1
. Recall that
m_{k}
is obtained in a similar way as
2n_{k}+1
obtained from (
2.13
). Then we have

(2.51)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{m}-m=m_{k+1}-m_{k}=\delta\gamma(\delta\gamma-1)\alpha(2\pi k)(2\pi)^{\delta\gamma-1}k^{\delta\gamma-2}+O(k^{\delta\gamma-3})+O(1),\end{split}

as
k\to\infty
. If
1<\delta\gamma\leq 2
, then
\mathfrak{m}-m
is bounded and thus we may obtain that
r=O(1)
just as in the case
\mathfrak{m}=1
and
m=0
. If
\delta\gamma>2
, then we also have
\gamma\geq\delta\gamma>2
. It follows from the estimate in (
2.13
) that

(2.52)

\begin{split}2(\mathfrak{n}-n)=2(n_{k+1}-n_{k})=\gamma(\gamma-1)(2\pi)^{\gamma-1}k^{\gamma-2}(1+\beta(k)).\end{split}

When
k
is large, then from (
2.51
) and (
2.52
) we see that
\frac{\mathfrak{m}-m}{M-N}\leq\varepsilon
for a small
\varepsilon>0
. Then we see from (
2.48
) and (
2.50
) that
r=O(1)
. From the above discussions, we also have
r=O(1)
when
r>0
. We conclude that
p=\log N+O(1)
when
\mathfrak{m}>m
. Thus we have the first assertion.

Secondly, in the case
\mathfrak{m}=m
, by Lemma
2.2
we must have
\delta\gamma\leq 1
and
\mathfrak{m}=m=0
or
\mathfrak{m}=m=1
. When
\mathfrak{m}=m=0
, we know from
[
5
]
that
r=o(1)
as
N\to\infty
. When
\mathfrak{m}=m=1
, we also write
p=\log N+r
. Now, the two terms
2\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{p})
and
2\log Q_{m}(e^{p})
in (
2.44
) cancel out. Then by following the same arguments as in previous case we easily obtain that
r=\beta(k)
. Thus we have second assertion.

Thirdly, in the case
\mathfrak{m}<m
, by Lemma
2.2
we must have
\delta\gamma<1
and
\mathfrak{m}=0
and
m=1
. Suppose that
M-N=l
for an integer
l\geq 1
and write
p=\frac{l-1}{l}\log N+r
. Since
\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}/\binom{m+2n}{m}=1/N
and
M-N=l
, then by similar arguments as before, we obtain, instead of equation (
2.48
), that

(2.53)

\begin{split}r=2\frac{-\log Q_{1}(N^{(l-1)/l}e^{r})}{M-N}+\beta(k).\end{split}

Note that
(l-1)/l\leq 1
. When
r\leq 0
, we only need to follow the same arguments as in previous case to obtain that
r=O(1)
; when
r\geq 0
, since
Q_{1}(N^{(l-1)/l}e^{r})>1
, we see from (
2.53
) that
r=O(1)
. Thus we have the third assertion.

Finally, together with the zeros of
d(x)
we may say that
\phi(x)\leq x
when
x<p+O(1)
and also that
\phi(x)\geq x
when
x>p+O(1)
in all the three cases
\mathfrak{m}>m
,
\mathfrak{m}=m
and
\mathfrak{m}<m
. If
\mathfrak{m}\geq m
, then from the first and second assertions we have
\phi<x
when
x<p=\log N+O(1)
and thus
\phi<x+O(1)\leq\log N+O(1)
when
x\leq\log N
. If
\mathfrak{m}<m
, then
\mathfrak{m}=0
and
m=1
and we have from (
2.21
) that

(2.54)

\begin{split}&\ -\log\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}M!+e^{\phi}+M\phi-2\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{\phi})-\log\left(1+\frac{e^{\phi}}{M}\right)\\
=&\ -\log\binom{m+2n}{m}N!+e^{x}+Nx-2\log Q_{m}(e^{x})-\log\left(1+\frac{e^{x}}{N}\right)+\beta(k).\end{split}

Suppose that
\phi-\log N\geq 0
is unbounded. Note that
\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{x})=0
and
\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{x})=O(\log N)
. Also,
\frac{e^{x}}{N}\leq 1
and

\begin{split}\log\left(1+\frac{N}{M}e^{\phi-\log N}\right)=\log\left(\frac{N}{M}e^{\phi-\log N}\right)+O(1).\end{split}

Note that
\log N=O(\log k)
. By the estimate in (
2.17
) we have
(\log N)/N)=\beta(k)
. Then, together with Stirling’s formula, it follows from (
2.54
) that

(2.55)

\begin{split}e^{\phi-\log N}+\frac{M-1}{N}(\phi-\log N)=\frac{e^{x}}{N}+x-\log N+O(1).\end{split}

However, since
x\leq\log N
, if we let
\phi-\log N\to\infty
, then we get a contradiction from (
2.55
). This implies that we always have
\phi\leq\log N+O(1)
when
x\leq\log N
. Thus we have the fourth assertion and also complete the proof.

∎

We note that
\mathfrak{m}\geq m
for all
k\geq 1
when we assume that
\delta\gamma\geq 1
in Lemma
2.2
. We also note that, if
\gamma>2
, writing
\epsilon=\min\{1,\gamma-2-\varepsilon\}
for a small
\varepsilon>0
, then by (
2.51
) and (
2.52
) we have

\begin{split}\frac{\log N_{k}}{N_{k+1}-N_{k}}=O\left(\frac{1}{k^{\epsilon}}\right)=\beta(k).\end{split}

This implies that the first term in (
2.46
) is always of type
\beta(k)
in all of the three cases
\mathfrak{m}>m
,
\mathfrak{m}=m
and
\mathfrak{m}<m
. Then from the above discussions in the proof of Lemma
2.5
we always have
p=\log N+O(1)
.

In the proof of Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.5
, though the error terms
\beta(k)=O((\log(k+2))^{-2})
and
O(1)
may depend on
r
, we may suitably choose a new

so that they are only dependent on the constant
C
in (
2.42
). Below we shall always assume that the terms
O(1)
appearing are only dependent on the constant

, not on other variables.

Lemma 2.6
.

For
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n},m,n,M,N
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
there exist positive constants
c_{1},\cdots,c_{8}
depending only on the constant
C
in (
2.42
) such that

(2.56)

\begin{split}|\phi(x)-x|\leq c_{1}e^{-x/2}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{N}\quad\text{for}\quad x>8\log N\end{split}

and

(2.57)

\begin{split}\left|\phi(x)-\frac{M}{N}x+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{M!}{N!}\right|\leq c_{2}e^{x}\quad\text{for}\quad x<\log N.\end{split}

Moreover, with
s_{m,n}
defined in (
2.37
) we have

(2.58)

\begin{split}\left|\phi(x)-x\right|\leq c_{3}\quad\text{for}\quad x>s_{m,n}\end{split}

and

(2.59)

\begin{split}\left|\phi(x)-\frac{M}{N}x+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{M!}{N!}\right|\leq c_{4}\quad\text{for}\quad x<\log N.\end{split}

Finally,

(2.60)

\begin{split}|\phi^{\prime}(x)-1|\leq c_{5}\left(e^{-x/2}+\frac{1}{(\log(k+2))^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{c_{5}}{(\log(k+2))^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad x>8\log N\end{split}

and

(2.61)

\begin{split}\left|\phi^{\prime}(x)-\frac{M}{N}\right|\leq\frac{c_{6}e^{x}}{N}\quad\text{for}\quad x<\log N,\end{split}

as well as

(2.62)

\begin{split}c_{7}\leq|\phi^{\prime}(x)|\leq c_{8}\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
y=\phi(x)
so that
g_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}}(x)=g_{m,n}(y)
. Recall that in (
2.20
) the term
R(y,N)=\beta(k)
. Therefore, by (
2.21
) we have

(2.63)

\begin{split}&\ -\log\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}M!+e^{x}+Mx-2\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{x})-\log\left(1+\frac{e^{x}}{M}\right)\\
=&\ -\log\binom{m+2n}{m}N!+e^{y}+Ny-2\log Q_{m}(e^{y})-\log\left(1+\frac{e^{y}}{N}\right)+\beta(k).\end{split}

Suppose first
x<\log N
. Then by Lemma
2.5
we have
y<\log N+O(1)
. It follows that

\begin{split}y-\frac{M}{N}x+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{N!}{M!}=&\ \frac{1}{N}\left(\log\frac{\binom{m+2n}{m}}{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}+e^{x}-e^{y}-2\log\frac{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{x})}{Q_{m}(e^{y})}\right)\\
&\ -\frac{1}{N}\left(\log\left(1+\frac{e^{x}}{M}\right)+\log\left(1+\frac{e^{y}}{N}\right)+\beta(k)\right).\end{split}

Thus

\begin{split}\left|y-\frac{M}{N}x+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{N!}{M!}\right|\leq&\ \frac{1}{N}\left(\left|\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}}\right|+e^{x}+e^{y}+2\left|\log\frac{Q_{m}(e^{y})}{Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{x})}\right|\right)\\
&\ +\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{e^{x}}{M}+\frac{e^{y}}{N}+\beta(k)\right).\end{split}

Recall that
\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}/\binom{m+2n}{m}=-\log N
when
\mathfrak{m}<m
and also the estimate in (
2.49
) when
\mathfrak{m}\geq m
.
Then, together with the estimate in (
2.47
), we have

\begin{split}\left|y-\frac{M}{N}x+\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{N!}{M!}\right|\leq c+\frac{d(e^{x}+e^{y})}{N}.\end{split}

This yields (
2.57
) and also (
2.59
) by Lemma
2.5
.

Suppose now that
x>\log N
. Using

\begin{split}\log\left(1+\frac{e^{x}}{M}\right)=x-\log M+\log\left(1+\frac{M}{e^{x}}\right)\end{split}

and the corresponding formula for
\log(1+e^{y}/N)
, we may write (
2.63
) as

(2.64)

\begin{split}&\ -\log\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}M!+e^{x}+(M-1)x-2\log Q_{\mathfrak{m}}(e^{x})+\log\left(1+\frac{M}{e^{x}}\right)\\
=&\ -\log\binom{m+2n}{m}N!+e^{y}+(N-1)y-2\log Q_{m}(e^{y})+\log\left(1+\frac{N}{e^{y}}\right)+\beta(k).\end{split}

We write
x=\log N+s
and
y=\log N+t
and note that, since
x>\log N
, we have
t>s-O(1)
by Lemma
2.5
. We obtain

\begin{split}&\ -\log\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}M!+Ne^{s}+(M-1)(\log N+s)-2\mathfrak{m}s+\log\left(1+\frac{M}{Ne^{s}}\right)\\
=&\ -\log\binom{m+2n}{m}N!+Ne^{t}+(N-1)(\log N+t)-2mt+\log\left(1+\frac{1}{e^{t}}\right)+O(1)\end{split}

and hence, using Stirling’s formula together with the estimates in (
2.47
), we have

\begin{split}e^{t}-e^{s}=&\ \frac{1}{N}\left(-\log\frac{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}}\frac{M!}{N!}+(M-N)\log N+\log\frac{M}{N}\right)\\
&\ +\left(\frac{M-2\mathfrak{m}}{N}-\frac{1}{N}\right)s-\left(1-\frac{2m+1}{N}\right)t+O(1)\\
\leq&\ \frac{M}{N}s+O(1)\leq Cs+O(1).\end{split}

It follows that

(2.65)

\begin{split}0<\phi(x)-x=t-s\leq e^{t-s}-1\leq Cse^{-s}+O(e^{-s})=O(1)\quad\text{for}\quad x>\log N.\end{split}

For
x>8\log N
we have
s=x-\log N>3x/4
and thus
2s/3>x/2
. It follows that
se^{-s}=O(e^{-2s/3})=O(e^{-x/2})
and thus (
2.65
) yields (
2.56
). Noting that
s_{m,n}=\log N+O(1)
by Lemma
2.4
we can also deduce (
2.58
) from (
2.65
).

Since
g^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}}(x)=g^{\prime}_{m,n}(\phi(x))\phi^{\prime}(x)
by the chain rule, (
1.7
) yields that

\begin{split}\phi^{\prime}(x)=&\ \frac{\binom{m+2n}{m}}{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}\frac{(m+2n)!}{(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n})!}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{iy}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{i}=0}^{\mathfrak{m}}A_{\mathfrak{i}}e^{\mathfrak{i}x}\right)^{2}}e^{e^{x}+(\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}+1)x-e^{y}-(m+2n+1)y}\\
=&\ \frac{\binom{m+2n}{m}}{\binom{\mathfrak{m}+2\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{m}}}\frac{N!}{M!}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{iy}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{i}=0}^{\mathfrak{m}}A_{\mathfrak{i}}e^{\mathfrak{i}x}\right)^{2}}e^{e^{x}+Mx+\log M-e^{y}-Ny-\log N}.\end{split}

Using (
2.64
) and (
2.65
) we obtain

\begin{split}\log\phi^{\prime}(x)=y-x+\log\left(1+\frac{M}{e^{x}}\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{N}{e^{y}}\right)+\beta(k)\end{split}

for
x>\log N
. Since
y>x-O(1)\geq\log N-O(1)
by Lemma
2.5
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{N}{e^{y}}\leq e^{O(1)}\frac{M}{e^{x}}\leq e^{O(1)}\frac{M}{N}\leq O(1)+\beta(k).\end{split}

Together with (
2.56
) and (
2.58
) and since for
A>0
there exists
B>0
such that
|t-1|\leq B|\log t|
whenever

, the same arguments as the ones used before now yields (

), as well as

(2.66)

\begin{split}|\log\phi^{\prime}(x)|=O(1)\quad\text{for}\quad x>\log N.\end{split}

Similarly, (

and hence, by the estimates for

2.2.
Definition of a quasimeromorphic map

Now we begin to define a quasimeromorphic map by gluing functions
g_{m,n}
with different pair of values
(m,2n)
as in
[
5
, Section 3.2]
. By modifying the functions
g_{m,n}
slightly, we obtain closely related functions
u_{m,n}
and
v_{m,n}
and then glue restrictions of these maps to half-strips along horizontal lines to obtain quasimeromorphic maps
U
and
V
which are defined in the right and left half-plane respectively. Then we will glue these functions along the imaginary axis to obtain a quasimeromorphic map
G
in the plane. The following definitions of
U
and
V
are direct quotation from
[
5
, Section 3.2]
.

The maps
U
,
V
and
G
will commute with complex conjugation, so it will be enough to define them in the upper half-plane. We begin by constructing the map
U
. Instead of
g_{m,n}
we consider the map

\begin{split}u_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad u_{m,n}=g_{m,n}(z+s_{m,n}).\end{split}

Note that
g_{m,n}
is increasing on the real line, and maps
[0,\infty)
onto
[2,\infty)
.

Let
(n_{k})
and
(m_{k})
be the two sequences from Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
, respectively and write
N_{k}=m_{k}+2n_{k}+1
. Put
U(z)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}
in the half-strip

\begin{split}\Pi_{k}^{+}=\{x+iy:x>0,\quad 2\pi(k-1)<y<2\pi k\}.\end{split}

The function
U
will be discontinuous. In order to obtain a continuous function we consider the function
\psi_{k}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
defined by
u_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}(x)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\psi_{k}(x))
. The function
\psi_{k}
is closely related to the function
\phi
considered in Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
. In fact, denoting by
\phi_{k}
the function
\phi
corresponding to
n_{k}
and
n_{k+1}
, then

\begin{split}\psi_{k}(x)=\phi_{k}(x+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}})-s_{m_{k},n_{k}}.\end{split}

We then define
U:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C}
by interpolating between
u_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and
u_{m_{k},n_{k}}
as follows: If
2\pi(k-1)\leq y<2\pi k
, say
y=2\pi(k-1)+2\pi t
where
0\leq t<1
, then we put

\begin{split}U(x+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\psi_{k}(x)+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(x+iy+t(\psi_{k}(x)-x)).\end{split}

Actually, by
2\pi i
-periodicity we have

\begin{split}U(x+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\psi_{k}(x)+2\pi it).\end{split}

The function
U
defined this way is continuous in the right-half plane.

We now define a function
V
in the left half-plane. In order to do so, we define

\begin{split}v_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z<0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad v_{m,n}(z)=g_{m,n}\left(\frac{z}{m+2n+1}+s_{m,n}\right).\end{split}

Note that
v_{m,n}
maps
(-\infty,0]
monotonically onto
(1,2]
.

Let
(n_{k})
and
(m_{k})
be as before and put
\mathcal{N}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}N_{j}
. This time we would like to define
V(z)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)
in the half-strip

\begin{split}\{x+iy:x<0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\},\end{split}

but again this function would be discontinuous. In order to obtain a continuous function we again interpolate between
v_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and
v_{m_{k},n_{k}}
. Similarly as before we consider the map
\phi_{k}:(-\infty,0]\to(-\infty,0]
defined by
v_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}(x)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\psi_{k}(x))
. Then we define
V:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z\leq 0\}\to\mathbb{C}
by interpolating between
v_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and
v_{m_{k},n_{k}}
as follows: If
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
, say
y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}+2\pi N_{k}t
where
0\leq t<1
, then we put

\begin{split}V(x+iy)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\psi_{k}(x)+iy)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(x+iy+t(\psi_{k}(x)-x)).\end{split}

This map
V
is continuous in the left half-plane.

Now we define our map
G
by gluing
U
and
V
along the imaginary axis. In order to do this we note that by construction we have
U(iy)=V(i(h(y)+\mathfrak{h}(y)))
and thus
U(ig(y))=V(iy^{\gamma})
for
y\geq 0
, with the maps
h
,
\mathfrak{h}
and
g
from Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
. Therefore, we will consider a homeomorphism
Q
of the right half-plane

\begin{split}H^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z\geq 0\}\end{split}

onto itself, satisfying
Q(\bar{z})=\overline{Q(z)}
, such that
Q(\pm iy)=\pm ig(y)
for
y\geq 0
while
Q(z)=z
for
\Re z\geq 1
. We thus have to define
Q(z)
in the strip
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\Re z<1\}
. For
|\Im z|\geq 1
we define
Q
in this strip by interpolation; that is, we put

\begin{split}Q(x\pm iy)=x\pm i((1-x)g(y)+xy)\quad\text{if}\quad 0<x<1\quad\text{and}\quad y\geq 1.\end{split}

In the remaining part of the strip we define
Q
by

\begin{split}Q(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z|z|^{\gamma-1},&\text{if}\quad 0<|z|<1;\\
z,&\text{if}\quad|z|>1,\quad\text{but}\quad 0<\Re z<1\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq|\Im z|\leq 1.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Note that
h(y)+\mathfrak{h}(y)=y
for
0\leq y\leq 2\pi
since
m_{1}=n_{1}=0
implies that
N_{1}=1
, and thus
g(y)=y^{\gamma}
for
0\leq y<1
. Thus
Q(\pm iy)=\pm ig(y)
also for
|y|\leq 1
. Moreover, we have
g(1)=1
, meaning that the above expressions for
Q(z)
do indeed coincide for
|\Im z|=1
. We conclude that the map
W=U\circ Q
satisfies

\begin{split}W(\pm iy)=U(\pm ig(y))=V(\pm iy^{\gamma})\quad\text{for}\quad y\geq 0.\end{split}

Let now
\rho\in(1/2,1]
. We choose the constant
\gamma=1/(2\rho-1)
and put
\sigma=\rho/(2\rho-1)
. We see that
\gamma\geq 1
. Then the map

\begin{split}G(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W(z^{\rho}),&\text{if}\quad|\arg z|\leq\frac{\pi}{2\rho};\\
V(-(-z)^{\sigma}),&\text{if}\quad|\arg(-z)|\leq\frac{\pi}{2\sigma},\end{array}\right.\end{split}

is continuous in
\mathbb{C}
. Here, for
\mu>0
, we denote by
z^{\mu}
the principal branch of the power which is defined in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
.

2.3.
Estimation of the dilatation

With the definition in subsection
2.2
, we now estimate the dilatation. After making some changes to the notations, the process will be the same as that in
[
5
, Section 3.3]
, except that we have a slightly different error term when applying Lemma
2.6
.

In the case
\gamma>1
, together with the estimates in (
2.15
)–(
2.18
), we may replace each error term
O(1/k^{\delta})
, where

is a constant, appearing in

In the case
\gamma=1
, we have
n_{1}=0
and

for all

. By the comments below Theorem
1.2
, we only need to consider the case

. In this case the notation

used in the proof of Lemma
2.3
–Lemma

in subsection

shall be replaced by a bounded term

. Now, since

or
m_{k}=1
, it is possible that

,

or

. In all these three cases, it is easy to check that the estimates in Lemma

and Lemma

hold with the new definition of

. We note that the increasing diffeomorphism
\phi
in (
2.19
) is just the identity when

. Further, when
\mathfrak{m}>m
or
\mathfrak{m}<m
, by the equation (

) we easily show that the fixed point of

, say
p
, satisfies

. Recall from Lemma
2.2
that the subsequence
(m_{k_{i}})
such that
m_{k_{i}}=1
satisfies
k_{i}\geq ci^{1/\delta\gamma}
for a fixed positive constant

. Then, after making some slightly modifications to Lemma
2.6
, we may replace any sum of the type
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/k^{1+\delta}
in
[

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1/k_{i}
, which also converges.

By the above discussions, below we only recall some notations that will be used in the following proof and the calculations will be omitted. For a quasimeromorphic map
f
, let

\begin{split}\mu_{f}(z)=\frac{f_{\bar{z}}(z)}{f_{z}(z)}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{f}(z)=\frac{1+|\mu_{f}(z)|}{1-|\mu_{f}(z)|}.\end{split}

We have to estimate
K_{G}(z)-1
. Write

(2.67)

\begin{split}q(x+iy)=x+iy+t(\psi_{k}(x)-x)=x+iy+\left(\frac{y}{2\pi}-(k-1)\right)(\psi_{k}(x)-x).\end{split}

Thus

(2.68)

\begin{split}q_{z}(z)=1+a(z)-ib(z)\quad\text{and}\quad q_{\bar{z}}=a(z)+ib(z),\end{split}

with

(2.69)

\begin{split}a(x+iy)=\frac{t}{2}(\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)-1)\quad\text{and}\quad b(x+iy)=\frac{1}{4\pi}(\psi_{k}(x)-x).\end{split}

Then from the proof in
[
5
, Section 3.3]
we deduce that

(2.70)

\begin{split}K_{U}(z)-1\leq\frac{4(1+r(x))r(x)}{\min\{1,\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)\}},\end{split}

where

(2.71)

\begin{split}r(x)=|\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)-1|+|\psi_{k}(x)-x|.\end{split}

Then we can show that
K_{U}(z)-1<\infty
, which together with (
2.56
) and (
2.62
) shows that
U
is quasimeromorphic in the right half-plane

. Moreover, by using (

) and (

) and

is quasimeromorphic in

.

On the other hand, to obtain the estimate of

for

, we have
V(z)=V_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z))
where, instead of (
2.67
), we have

\begin{split}q(x+iy)=x+iy+\frac{1}{N_{k}}\left(\frac{y}{2\pi}-\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\right)\left(\psi_{k}(x)-x\right)\end{split}

for

, with

\begin{split}\psi_{k}(x)=N_{k}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}+s_{m_{k+1}.n_{k+1}}\right)-N_{k}s_{m_{k},n_{k}}.\end{split}

Now (
2.68
) holds with

as in (
2.69
), but

\begin{split}b(x+iy)=\frac{1}{4\pi N_{k}}\left(N_{k}\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}\right)-x\right).\end{split}

Instead of (
2.71
) and (
2.70
) we obtain

\begin{split}K_{V}(z)-1&\leq\frac{4(1+r(x))r(x)}{\min\{1,\phi_{k}^{\prime}(x)\}}\end{split}

with

Then, as in

,

Thus, in both of the two cases

,

. This theorem, together with the existence theorem for quasiconformal mappings
[

, § V.1]
, yields that there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\tau:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
and a meromorphic function

\begin{split}G(z)=F(\tau(z))\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(z)\sim z\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.\end{split}

2.4.
Completion of the proof

We now begin to estimate the counting functions of the zeros and poles of
F
, respectively. For two positive quantities
S_{1}(r)
and
S_{2}(r)
, below we shall use the notation
S_{1}(r)\asymp S_{2}(r)
, provided that there are two positive constants
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
such that
d_{2}S_{2}(r)\leq S_{1}(r)\leq d_{1}S_{2}(r)
.

Let
r>0
and choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2\pi(k-1)<r\leq 2\pi k
. It follows from the construction of
(n_{k})
and
(m_{k})
and (
2.18
) that

(2.73)

\begin{split}n(r,0,U)\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\sim 2\mathcal{N}_{k}\sim 2(2\pi)^{\gamma-1}k^{\gamma}\leq d_{1}r^{\gamma}\end{split}

for some positive constant
d_{1}
. On the other hand, we also have

\begin{split}n(r,0,U)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\sim\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}_{k}\sim\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{\gamma-1}k^{\gamma}\geq d_{2}r^{\gamma}\end{split}

for some positive constant
d_{2}
. Therefore,
n(r,0,U)\asymp r^{\gamma}
. Similarly, if
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}<r\leq 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
, then

\begin{split}n(r,0,V)\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\sim 2\mathcal{N}_{k}\sim 2(2\pi)^{\gamma-1}k^{\gamma}\leq d_{3}r\end{split}

for some positive constant
d_{3}
and also that

\begin{split}n(r,0,V)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\sim\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}_{k}\sim\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{\gamma-1}k^{\gamma}\geq d_{4}r\end{split}

for some constant
d_{4}
. Therefore,
n(r,0,V)\asymp r
. Since
\sigma=\rho\gamma
, we conclude that
n(r,0,G)\asymp r^{\rho\gamma}
. Now (
2.72
) yields
n(r,0,F)\asymp r^{\rho\gamma}
and hence
N(r,0,F)\asymp r^{\rho\gamma}
as
r\to\infty
.

Recall that the function
g(x)
in Lemma
2.1
satisfies
g(x)=x(1+o(1))
as
x\to\infty
and also that
x(1+o(1))/3\leq g(x)\leq x(1+o(1))
as
x\to\infty
when
\gamma=1
. When
1<\delta\gamma\leq\gamma
, by the same arguments as above we have
N(r,\infty,F)\asymp(\log r)^{-2}r^{\delta\rho\gamma}
; when
0\leq\delta\gamma\leq 1
, similarly as for equation (
2.73
) we have
N(r,\infty,F)\leq d_{5}r^{\delta\rho\gamma}
for a positive constant
d_{5}
and, moreover, there is an infinite sequence
(r_{n})
such that
N(r_{n},\infty,F)\geq d_{6}r_{n}^{\delta\rho\gamma}
for a constant
d_{6}
.

Next we note that there is a set
\Omega_{1}
with finite linear measure such that, for all
|z|\not\in\Omega_{1}
, the functions
P_{n}(e^{z})
and
Q_{n}(e^{z})
satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp(-(2n+\varepsilon)r)\leq\left|P_{n}(e^{z})\right|\leq\exp((2n+\varepsilon)r),&\\
\exp(-(m+\varepsilon)r)\leq\left|Q_{m}(e^{z})\right|\leq\exp((m+\varepsilon)r),&\end{array}\right.

respectively, where
\varepsilon>0
is a small constant. This implies that

\begin{split}|g_{m,n}(z)|\leq\exp((m+\varepsilon)r+(2n+\varepsilon)r)\left|\exp(e^{z})\right|\end{split}

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|\not\in\Omega_{1}
. For simplicity, we denote
L=\max\{m,2n\}
and
L_{k}=\max\{m_{k},2n_{k}\}
. Clearly this implies that there is a set
\Omega_{2}
of finite linear measure such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|v_{m,n}(z)|\leq\exp((L+\varepsilon)r)\left|\exp(e^{z})\right|\quad\text{for}\quad z\in H^{-}\quad\text{and}\quad|z|\not\in\Omega_{2},&\\
|u_{m,n}(z)|\leq\exp((L+\varepsilon)r)\left|\exp(e^{z})\right|\quad\text{for}\quad z\in H^{+}\quad\text{and}\quad|z|\not\in\Omega_{2}.&\end{array}\right.

Let
x+iy\in H^{-}
with
\Im z\geq 0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq\Im z<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
. With
t=(y-2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1})/{2\pi N_{k}}
we have
0\leq t<1
and

\begin{split}|V(x+iy)|=|v_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\psi_{k}(x)+iy)|\leq\exp(L_{k}+\varepsilon)r).\end{split}

Thus

(2.74)

\begin{split}|V(z)|\leq\exp((L_{k}+\varepsilon)r),\end{split}

for
z\in H^{-}
and
|z|\not\in\Omega_{2}
. For
z\in H^{+}
and
|z|\not\in\Omega_{2}
we have

(2.75)

\begin{split}|g_{m,n}(z)|\leq g_{m,n}(\Re z)\leq\exp((L+\varepsilon)r)\exp\left(e^{\Re z}\right).\end{split}

Again, assuming that
\Im z\geq 0
, we choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2\pi(k-1)\leq\Im z<2\pi k
. Then

\begin{split}|U(z)|=|u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z))|=|g_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z)+s_{m_{k},n_{k}})|\leq\exp((L_{k}+\varepsilon)r)\exp\left(e^{\Re z}\right),\end{split}

where
q(z)
is defined by (
2.67
). Noting that
m_{k}+2n_{k}=O(k^{\gamma-1})=O(|z|^{\gamma-1})
by (
2.2
) and thus, by Lemma
2.4
,

(2.76)

\begin{split}s_{m_{k},n_{k}}=\log N_{k}+O(1)=\log(m_{k}+2n_{k})+O(1)=O(\log|z|),\end{split}

we deduce from (
2.56
) and (
2.75
) that

\begin{split}\log|U(z)|\leq&\ (L_{k}+\varepsilon)r+\exp(\Re q(z)+s_{m_{k},n_{k}})\\
\leq&\ \exp((1+o(1))z)+O(|z|^{\gamma})\leq\exp((1+o(1))z).\end{split}

Together with (
2.74
) we conclude that

\begin{split}\log|G(z)|\leq\exp((1+o(1))z^{\rho})\end{split}

as
|z|\to\infty
outside the set
\Omega_{2}
. Hence (
2.72
) yields that

\begin{split}\log|F(z)|\leq\exp((1+o(1))z^{\rho})\end{split}

as
|z|\to\infty
outside an exceptional set
\Omega_{3}
of finite linear measure. Then, together with (
2.77
), the lemma on the logarithmic derivative
[
8
,
9
]
now implies that
E=F/F^{\prime}
satisfies

\begin{split}m\left(r,\frac{1}{E}\right)=O(r^{\rho}),\end{split}

outside a set of finite linear measure. By the relation
N(r,\infty,F)\leq d_{5}(\log r)^{-2}r^{\delta\rho\gamma}
and
N(r,0,F)\asymp r^{\rho\gamma}
, and since the zeros and poles of
F
are simple, we have

(2.77)

\begin{split}N\left(r,0,E\right)\asymp r^{\rho\gamma}.\end{split}

We conclude that

\begin{split}T\left(r,E\right)\asymp r^{\rho\gamma}.\end{split}

In particular,
E
has finite order.
E
is clearly a Bank–Laine functions and thus
E
is the product of two solutions of (
1.1
). The lemma on the logarithmic derivative, together with (
1.9
) and (
2.77
) also implies that

\begin{split}m(r,A)=2m\left(r,\frac{1}{E}\right)+O(\log r)=O(r^{\rho}).\end{split}

We thus have
\lambda(E)=\rho(E)=\rho\gamma
and
\rho(A)\leq\rho
. Since
1/\rho+1/\rho\gamma=2
by the definition of
\gamma
, we deduce from (
1.2
) that actually
\rho(A)=\rho
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Recall from the introduction that
A_{i}=\frac{2n!}{(i+2n)!}
,
i=1,\cdots,m
and
B_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}m!}{(m+j)!}
,
j=1,\cdots,2n
. We note that for
|z|>B_{2n-1}/B_{2n}=m+2n
,

(3.1)

\begin{split}\left|P_{n}(z)-B_{2n}z^{2n}\right|=&\ \left|\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}B_{j}z^{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}\left|B_{j}\right||z|^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}\frac{\left|B_{j}\right||z|^{2n-1}}{|z|^{2n-1-j}}\\
&\ \leq\left|B_{2n-1}\right||z|^{2n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}\frac{1}{2^{2n-1-j}}\leq\frac{B_{2n-1}}{B_{2n}|z|}(B_{2n}|z|^{2n})\end{split}

and similarly

(3.2)

\begin{split}\left|Q_{m}(z)-A_{m}z^{m}\right|\leq\frac{A_{m-1}}{A_{m}|z|}(A_{m}|z|^{m})\end{split}

and thus in particular
R_{m,n}(z)\not=0,\infty
for
|z|>m+2n
. Then we can follow the way as in
[
5
, Section 3.4]
to estimate the asymptotics of
F
,
E
and
A
.

Let
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon_{3}<\varepsilon<1
and, for
l\in\{1,2\}
, put

\begin{split}H_{\varepsilon_{l}}^{+}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\quad|\arg z|\leq(1-\varepsilon_{l})\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}.\end{split}

Given
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{+}
with
\Im z>0
, we choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
2\pi(k-1)\leq\Im z<2\pi k
. We then have
k=O(\Re z)
and hence, by (
2.2
),
\log(m_{k}+2n_{k})=o(\Re z)
and
m_{k}+2n_{k}=o(e^{z})
as
z\to\infty
in
H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{+}
. We deduce from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that

\begin{split}R_{m_{k},n_{k}}(e^{z})\sim\frac{B_{2n_{k}}}{A_{m_{k}}}e^{(2n_{k}-m_{k})z}.\end{split}

Recall that
A_{m_{k}}B_{2n_{k}}=\frac{m_{k}!(2n_{k})!}{[(m_{k}+2n_{k})!]^{2}}
. By looking at the proof of Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
, and together with Stirling’s formula, we have

\begin{split}\log R_{m_{k},n_{k}}(e^{z})\sim 2\log B_{2n_{k}}-\log(A_{m_{k}}B_{2n_{k}})+(2n_{k}-m_{k})z\sim(2n_{k}-m_{k})z.\end{split}

It follows that

\begin{split}\log g_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)=(2n_{k}-m_{k})(1+o(1))z+e^{z}\sim e^{z}\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty\quad\text{in}\quad H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{+}.\end{split}

With
q(z)
as in (
2.67
) we have

\begin{split}\log U(z)=\log u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z))=\log g_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z)+s_{m_{k},n_{k}})\end{split}

and
q(z)+s_{m_{k},n_{k}}\sim z
as
z\to\infty
in
H_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{+}
by (
2.76
). Thus
q(z)+s_{m_{k},n_{k}}\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{+}
if
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{+}
and
|z|
is sufficiently large and we deduce from the last two equations that

\begin{split}\log U(z)\sim\exp(q(z)+s_{m_{k},n_{k}})=\exp((1+o(1))z)\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty\quad\text{in}\quad H_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{+}\end{split}

and thus

\begin{split}\log G(z)=\log U(z^{\rho})=\exp((1+o(1))z^{\rho})\quad\text{for}\quad|\arg z|<(1-\varepsilon_{2})\frac{\pi}{2\rho}.\end{split}

This implies that

\begin{split}\log F(z)=\log G(\tau^{-1}(z))=\exp((1+o(1))z^{\rho})\quad\text{for}\quad|\arg z|<(1-\varepsilon_{3})\frac{\pi}{2\rho}\end{split}

and hence

(3.3)

\begin{split}\log\log F(z)\sim z^{\rho}\quad\text{for}\quad|\arg z|<(1-\varepsilon_{3})\frac{\pi}{2\rho}\end{split}

as
|z|\to\infty
. An asymptotic equality like (
3.3
) can be differentiated by passing to a smaller sector. If
|\arg z|<(1-\varepsilon_{4})\pi/(2\rho)
, then
\{\zeta:|\zeta-z|=c|z|\}
is contained in
\{\zeta:|\arg z|<(1-\varepsilon_{3})\pi/(2\rho)\}
for sufficiently small
c
. Thus we can obtain (
1.10
) by following exactly the same process as in
[
5
, Section 3.4]
. We omit those details.

The proof of (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) is similar. Note that
N=m+2n+1
. From the proof of Lemma
2.3
we have

\begin{split}h_{m,n}(z)-1=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}N!}\frac{e^{z}z^{N}}{Q_{m}(z)^{2}}\left(1-U(z)\right),\end{split}

where
U(z)
satisfies

\begin{split}|U(z)|\leq\left(1+\frac{m}{1+2n}\right)\left(\left|\frac{z}{z+N-2m}\right|+\left|\frac{12z}{(z+N-2m)^{2}}\right|\right).\end{split}

Then, by the estimates in (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), we have

\begin{split}h_{m,n}(z)=1+\frac{z^{N}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}N!}+O(z^{N+1})=1+(1+O(z))\frac{z^{N}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}N!}\end{split}

as
z\to 0
. Thus, by Lemma
2.4
together with Stirling’s formula we have

\begin{split}\log(v_{m,n}-1)&=\log\left(g_{m,n}\left(\frac{z}{N}+s_{m,n}\right)-1\right)=\log\left(h_{m,n}\left(e^{\frac{z}{N}+s_{m,n}}\right)-1\right)\\
&=-\log\binom{m+2n}{m}N!+z+Ns_{m,n}+\log(1+O(e^{\frac{z}{N}}))\\
&=z+\log(1+O(e^{\frac{z}{N}}))+O(N\log N).\end{split}

Similarly as before, for
l\in\{1,2\}
, we consider the sectors

\begin{split}H_{\varepsilon_{l}}^{-}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\quad|\arg(-z)|\leq(1-\varepsilon_{l})\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}.\end{split}

For
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{-}
with
\Im z>0
we choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq\Im z<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
. We can deduce from (
2.2
) and (
2.18
) that

\Re z/N_{k-1}+s_{m_{k},n_{k}}\to-\infty
as
z\to\infty
in
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{-}
. This implies that
e^{z/N_{k-1}+s_{m_{k},n_{k}}}\to 0
and hence
O(e^{z/N_{k-1}+s_{m_{k},n_{k}}})\to 0
as
z\to\infty
in
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{-}
. Moreover,
N_{k}\log N_{k}=o(|z|)
as
z\to\infty
in
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{-}
, again by (
2.16
) and (
2.18
). It follows that
\log(v_{m_{k},n_{k}}-1)\to z
as
z\to\infty
in
z\in H_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{-}
. Then we can obtain (
1.12
) by following exactly the same process as in
[
5
, Section 3.4]
. We omit those details.
