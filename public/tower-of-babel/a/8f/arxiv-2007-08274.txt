Asymptotic Enumeration and Limit Laws for Multisets: the Subexponential Case
Konstantinos Panagiotou, Leon Ramzews

Abstract
For a given combinatorial class 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} we study the class 𝒢 = Mset ​ ( 𝒞 ) {\mathcal{G}}=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}}) satisfying the multiset construction, that is, any object in 𝒢 {\mathcal{G}} is uniquely determined by a set of 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} -objects paired with their multiplicities. For example, Mset ​ ( ℕ ) \text{{Mset}}(\mathbb{N}) is (isomorphic to) the class of number partitions of positive integers, a prominent and well-studied case. The multiset construction appears naturally in the study of unlabelled objects, for example graphs or various structures related to number partitions. Our main result establishes the asymptotic size of the set 𝒢 n , N {\mathcal{G}}_{n,N} that contains all multisets in 𝒢 {\mathcal{G}} having size n n and being comprised of N N objects from 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} , as n n and N N tend to infinity and when the counting sequence of 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} is governed by subexponential growth; this is a particularly important setting in combinatorial applications. Moreover, we study the component distribution of random objects from 𝒢 n , N {\mathcal{G}}_{n,N} and we discover a phenomenon that we baptise extreme condensation : taking away the largest component as well as all the components of the smallest possible size, we are left with an object which converges in distribution as n , N → ∞ n,N\to\infty . The distribution of the limiting object is also retrieved. Moreover and rather surprisingly, in stark contrast to analogous results for labelled objects, the results here hold uniformly in N N .

Introduction & Main Results

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a combinatorial class, that is, a countable set endowed with a size function
\lvert\cdot\rvert:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}:=\{C\in{\mathcal{C}}:\lvert C\rvert=n\}

{\mathcal{C}}
-multisets

consists of all objects of the form

\big\{(C_{1},d_{1}),\dots,(C_{k},d_{k})\big\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ \penalty\ C_{i}\in{\mathcal{C}},d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k,

where
(C_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
are pairwise distinct and
d_{i}
is the multiplicity of the object
C_{i}
in the multiset. In simple words, a
{\mathcal{C}}
-multiset is a finite unordered collection of elements from
{\mathcal{C}}
such that multiple occurrences of each element are admissible. For example, if
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{N}
, then
\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}})
contains all partitions of natural numbers, a prominent object. The multiset construction is omnipresent in combinatorial settings, for example when
{\mathcal{C}}
is some class of connected unlabelled graphs; this makes
{\mathcal{G}}
the class of unlabelled graphs having connected components in
{\mathcal{C}}
. For many historical references and examples we refer the reader to the excellent books
[
18
,
30
]

{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence, multisets are also called

[

Given

In this paper we consider the prominent and broad case in which the counting sequence
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
subexponential
, and our aim is to study the class

– what is

Example.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be the class of unlabelled trees, that is, isomorphism classes of connected and acyclic graphs. Then

|{\mathcal{T}}_{n}|\sim c\cdot n^{-5/2}\cdot\rho^{-n}

Similar counting sequences, in particular with a polynomial term

\frac{c_{n-1}}{c_{n}}\sim\rho\quad\text{and}\quad c_{n}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}c_{k}c_{n-k}\sim 2C(\rho)<\infty,\quad n\to\infty.

Important examples for subexponential sequences are of the form
c_{n}\sim\lambda(n)\cdot n^{-\alpha}\cdot\rho^{-n}
for
\alpha>1
and
\lambda(n)
any slowly varying function, see
[
16
]
.

Let us now return to the question investigated in this paper. Given a subexponential

and all

g_{n,N}=[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)

is the generating series of some combinatorial class (that is,

\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
.
A directly related result in this context is
[
5

\cal C
-multiset
\mathsf{G}_{n}
is given by a weighted sum of independent Poisson random variables. Equivalently, this means that
g_{n,N}
can be determined asymptotically for
fixed

N
as
n\to\infty
; thus the enumeration problem is well understood for a bounded number of components. On the other end of the spectrum, let
m\equiv m(C)\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
c_{m}>0
and
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{m-1}=0
. When
C(x)
is the generating function of a combinatorial class, this means that the size of the smallest possible object in
{\mathcal{C}}
is
m
, and so, any
\cal C
-multiset in
{\cal G}_{n}
has at most
n/m
components. In particular,
n-mN\geq 0
, and if
n-mN=O(1)
then the structure of
any

\cal C
-multiset of size
n
with
N
components is rather simple: except for a bounded number of components of bounded size, all other components are of the smallest possible size
m
.
Our first main result adresses the enumeration problem in all other remaining cases, namely when
N,n-mN\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is subexponential and
0<\rho<1
. Let
m=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}:c_{m}>0\}
. Then, as
n,N,n-mN\to\infty
,

[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)\sim A\cdot N^{c_{m}-1}\cdot c_{n-m(N-1)},

(1.2)

where

A=\frac{1}{\Gamma(c_{m})}\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C(\rho^{j})-c_{m}\rho^{jm}}{j\rho^{jm}}\right).

The proof is in Section
3.2
. Some discussion and remarks are in place. First, by considering real-valued sequences
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
the formula in Theorem
1.1
gives us the asymptotic behaviour of the coefficients of
G(x,y)
with a priori no combinatorial interpretation. However,
if
the sequence is integer-valued and corresponds to the counting sequence of a combinatorial class
\cal C
, then Theorem
1.1
is an enumeration result: it provides us with the number of
\cal C
-multisets of size
n
and
N
components, where
N,n-mN\to\infty
.
Second, in combinatorial applications, note that we
always
have that
\rho<1
, as otherwise the subexponentiality of
C(x)
would imply that
c_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. That is, the assumption
0<\rho<1
imposes no restriction in the combinatorial setting.

Let us make a third remark that will pave the way to the following results.
From here on
we solely consider the combinatorial setting.
Note the right hand side of (
1.2
): this formula establishes an explicit connection between
g_{n,N}
and
c_{n-m(N-1)}
, that is, we do not need the actual counting sequence of
\cal C
to make statements about
g_{n,N}
. Moreover, a closer look at this formula reveals an unexpected fact. The number of possible ways to choose a multiset of
N
objects from
{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
is given by
\binom{c_{m}+N-1}{N}\sim N^{c_{m}-1}/\Gamma(c_{m})
(this is just a number partition of
N
in
c_{m}
parts). Hence the right hand side of (
1.2
) is proportional to the number of possibilities to choose
N
objects from
{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
and one object from
{\mathcal{C}}_{n-m(N-1)}
; that is, a “typical” object from
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}
should essentially consist of a big component with more or less
n-m(N-1)
vertices and
N-1
components of the smallest possible size
m
. This is rather extreme, as the size of a component of an object in
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}
is bounded from above by
n-m(N-1)
.

Our next result formalizes this intuition. For
G=\{(C_{1},d_{1}),\dots,(C_{k},d_{k})\}\in{\mathcal{G}}
denote by
{\mathcal{L}}(G):=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\lvert C_{i}\rvert
the size of one of its largest components. We show that except for a term
\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
, that is, a quantity that is bounded in probability, the largest component in a uniformly drawn object
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
from
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}
has indeed size very close to
n-mN
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is subexponential. Then, as
n,N,n-mN\to\infty
,

{\mathcal{L}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=n-mN+{\cal O}_{p}(1).

The proof can be found in Section
3.3
. We call the phenomenon established in Theorem
1.2

extreme condensation
: we observe that typically our objects have a giant component that is
essentially as large as possible
; its size is close to the largest possible size
n-m(N-1)
. In particular, virtually all other components are of smallest possible size
m
. We are not aware of any other object with a comparable behaviour, at least not in the analytical
(\rho>0
) setting considered here.

Our final main result addresses the last remaining bit and describes the shape of a typical object from
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
when we remove a component of largest size and all components of the smallest possible size
m
. This
remainder
is a multiset of stochastically bounded size and number of components, and we determine the limiting distribution.
To formulate our statement we need some additional notation. Define the class
{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}=\bigcup_{k>m}{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
equipped with the modified size function
\lvert C\rvert_{>m}:=\lvert C\rvert-m
for
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}
. The associated generating function
C_{>m}(x)
thus equals
(C(x)-c_{m}x^{m})/x^{m}
; here subtracting
c_{m}x^{m}
accounts for the fact that we remove objects of (the smallest) size
m
and dividing through
x^{m}
all objects in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
,
k>m
, are treated as objects with size
k-m
. Similar to the formula in (
1.1
) (setting
y=1
) the class of all multisets
{\mathcal{G}}_{>m}:=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}}_{>m})
therefore has generating series

G_{>m}(x):=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C(x^{j})-c_{m}x^{j}}{jx^{jm}}\right).

Further, the size of an object
G
in
{\mathcal{G}}_{>m}
is given by
\lvert G\rvert_{>m}:=\lvert G\rvert-m\kappa(G)
. As the coefficients of
C_{>m}(x)
are given by
(c_{k+m})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
we deduce that
C_{>m}(x)
is also subexponential with radius of convergence
\rho
and
G_{>m}(\rho)<\infty
. Define a random variable
\Gamma G_{>m}(\rho)
on
{\mathcal{G}}_{>m}
specified by

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\Gamma G_{>m}(\rho)=G\right]=\frac{\rho^{\lvert G\rvert_{>m}}}{G_{>m}(\rho)}=\exp\left(-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C(\rho^{j})-c_{m}\rho^{j}}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)\rho^{\lvert G\rvert-m\kappa(G)},\quad G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{>m}.

(1.3)

We remark in passing that this is the – well-known – Boltzmann distribution (on
{\cal G}_{>m}
) about which we will talk later extensively. For
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
let the remainder
{\mathcal{R}}(G)
be the multiset obtained after removing all tuples
(C,d)\in G
with
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
and one object of largest size from
G
(this choice can be done in a canonical way by numbering all elements in
{\mathcal{C}}
). That means, if the object of largest size has multiplicity
d>1
replace
d
by
d-1
, otherwise remove the object and its multiplicity
1
completely from the set. Then the distribution in (
1.3
) is the limit of the remainder
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})
, see Section
3.4
for the proof.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is subexponential. Then, as
n,N,n-mN\to\infty
, in distribution

{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\to\Gamma G_{>m}(\rho).

We proceed with an application of our results to Benjamini-Schramm convergence of unlabelled graphs with many components. The Benjamini-Schramm limit of a sequence of graphs describes what a uniformly at random chosen vertex typically sees in its neighbourhood and is a special instance of local weak convergence, see also
[
2
,
6
]
. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
we form the
rooted
graph
(G,o)
by distinguishing a vertex
o\in V
. Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the collection of all these rooted graphs.
Then two graphs
(G,o)
and
(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
in
{\mathcal{B}}
are called isomorphic,
(G,o)\simeq(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
, if there exists an edge-preserving bijection
\Phi
on the vertex sets of
G
and
G^{\prime}
such that
\Phi(o)=o^{\prime}
. Hence, the collection
{\mathcal{B}}_{*}={\mathcal{B}}/{\simeq}
of equivalence classes in
{\mathcal{B}}
under the relation
\simeq
contains all unlabelled rooted graphs.

Set
B_{k}(G,o)
to be the induced subgraph of
(G,o)\in{\mathcal{B}}_{*}
containing all vertices within graph distance
k
from the root
o
.
Then we say that a sequence of (labelled or unlabelled) simple connected locally finite graphs
(\mathsf{G}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(possibly random) converges in the
Benjamini-Schramm
(BS) sense to a limiting object
(\mathbbmss{G},\mathbbmss{o})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{*}
if for a vertex
o_{n}
being selected uniformly at random from
\mathsf{G}_{n}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Pr}\left[B_{k}(\mathsf{G}_{n},o_{n})\simeq(G,o)\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[B_{k}(\mathbbmss{G},\mathbbmss{o})\simeq(G,o)\right],\quad k\in\mathbb{N},(G,o)\in{\mathcal{B}}_{*}.

(1.4)

Back to our setting, we consider
{\mathcal{C}}
to be a class of unlabelled finite connected graphs (with subexponential counting sequence and
m\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the size of the smallest possible graph in
{\mathcal{C}}
) such that
{\mathcal{G}}=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}})
is the class of unlabelled graphs with connected components in

. Let as before

. In order to adapt to the setting above we let

denote the connected component around a uniformly at random chosen root

Proposition 1.4
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is subexponential. Assume that
mN/n\to\lambda\in[0,1)
as

. If the sequence
(\mathsf{C}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to a limit object
(\mathbbmss{C},\mathbbmss{o})
in the BS sense, then
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
converges as

to a limit object
(\mathbbmss{G},\mathbbmss{o})
in the BS sense given by the law

(1-\lambda)\delta_{(\mathbbmss{C},\mathbbmss{o})}+\lambda\delta_{(C_{m},o_{m})},

where
o_{m}
is a vertex chosen uniformly at random among the
m
vertices in
\mathsf{C}_{m}
. In particular, if

we have that
(\mathbbmss{G},\mathbbmss{o})=(\mathbbmss{C},\mathbbmss{o})
.

The proof is found in Section
3.5
.

Proof Strategy

The main idea in the proof is to consider a randomized algorithm/stochastic process that generates
\cal C
-multisets. As it turns out, such an algorithm that outputs elements from
{\mathcal{G}}
(with a priori
no control
on the size or the number of components!) can be designed by defining the so-called Boltzmann distribution on
{\mathcal{G}}
, see Section
3.1
for all details. The crucial property of this algorithm is that all choices it makes are
independent
. Our first contribution is to establish explicitly the connection between the choices of the algorithm and its output; hence the probability that the output is in
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}
can be linked to an event regarding the actual choices of the algorithm. Our second and main contribution is to actually compute the probability of this event; as we will see, this is not at all an easy task, since the involved random variables are not identically distributed and interfere in a complex way with the parameters of the generated object.

In contrast to this work, most proofs in the literature about enumeration of multisets are either conducted from a purely analytical generating function perspective or involve somehow the
conditioning relation
representing the (heavily dependent) number of component frequencies in
\mathsf{G}_{n}
of a particular size by independent random variables with negative binomial distributions. This is related to the alternative (and equivalent) representation of the generating function for
\cal C
-multisets (
1.1
) given by

G(x,y)=\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-yx^{k})^{-c_{k}}.

(1.5)

As opposed to analysing the component spectrum in
[
34
,
22
,
4
,
3
]
, our arguments are in the style of
[
48
,
41
,
45
,
46
,
39
]
: we use the Pólya-Boltzmann model representing
\mathsf{G}_{n}
as random
{\mathcal{C}}
-objects attached to cycles of a random permutation, which is helpful to get rid of cumbersome appearances of symmetries and which gives rise to Poisson distributions instead of negative binomials; this difference is reflected by the two different representations (
1.1
) and (
1.5
). Then we show that the size associated to fixpoints is dominant and the subexponentiality-feature often referred to as “single big jump” guarantees that in fact only one object receives the entire possible size.

1.1
(More) Related Work

In this section we put our results in the broader context of (asymptotic) enumeration of multisets/weighted integer partitions. The most prominent assumption is that the counting sequence of
{\mathcal{C}}
fulfils
c_{n}\sim\lambda(n)\cdot n^{-\alpha}\cdot\rho^{-n}
as
n\to\infty

for some slowly varying function
\lambda(\cdot)
and parameters
\alpha\in\mathbb{R},0<\rho\leq 1
. Then there emerge three cases depending on the parameter
\alpha
determining the behaviour of

at or near its radius of convergence
\rho
, each giving rise to a fundamentally different picture.

In the
expansive
case
\alpha<1
, which in particular includes the classical and prominent setting
c_{k}\equiv 1
of integer partitions, the number of
\cal C
-multisets

is well-understood
[
22
]
. However, general results about the uniformly drawn element
\mathsf{G}_{n}
– in particular the distribution of the number of components that is of interest here – are not known without any extra conditions. For example, under the assumption of
Meinardus
scheme of conditions, a set of analytic assumptions that in particular imply that
\rho=1
, the number of components of
\mathsf{G}_{n}
fulfils various local limit theorems, see
[
34
]
.
The authors of
[
24
]
state that expansive multisets with counting sequence

for some
C>0
fulfil Meinardus conditions. For example, integer partitions
(c_{k}=1)
and plane partitions

are encapsulated by this approach. Hence, they call the Meinardus case

.
For quasi-expansive sequences it is established in
[
35
]
that the size of the largest component of
\mathsf{G}_{n}
is with high probability of size
\Theta(1)\cdot n^{1/(2-\alpha)}\log n
.
The broader picture here is that the number of components in
\mathsf{G}_{n}
is typically unbounded and the size of the largest component is sublinear in
n
. On the other hand, much less is known for the number of
\cal C
-multisets with
N
components

and the typical shape of

for arbitrary

. Nevertheless, there is one important exception, namely the case of integer partitions. There, depending on whether
N
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{1/2})
or
\omega(n^{1/2})
the asymptotic behaviour of the number of partitions of
n
into
N
parts is given by different formulas, see
[
29
]
. For
N\geq(1+\varepsilon)/(\sqrt{2/3}\pi)\sqrt{n}\log n
for any

[
26
]

\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
depends on the asymptotic regime of
N
; this, however, is a topic for a completely different paper.

The
logarithmic
case, where
\alpha=1
and
\lambda\equiv\lambda(\cdot)
constant, is concomitant with similar effects. The number of components in
\mathsf{G}_{n}
is typically of order

[
3

and, denoting by

the

implying that

To our knowledge, there are no results (regarding asymptotic enumeration or structural properties) for all other

In contrast to all previous cases, the phenomenon of condensation is observed in the

To get a handle on this expression we exploit a powerful description of the distribution of

.
In the next steps, the notation

That is, multiple occurrences of identical elements are allowed and

(1)

Let
(P_{j})_{j\geq 1}

(2)

Let

(3)

For

Lemma 3.3

[

This statement paves the way to study

3.1.1
Remarks to Theorem
1.1

In Theorem
1.1
we consider
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to be a real-valued non-negative sequence and assume
0<\rho<1
. In complete analogy to the discussion prior to this subsection let
P_{j}\sim\mathrm{Po}\left(C(\rho^{j})/j\right)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
(C_{j,1},\dots,C_{j,P_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in (

), and assume that all these variables are independent.
As a matter of fact, also in this (more general) case we obtain exactly the same representation of
[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)
in terms of
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
defined in (
3.3
) without using the combinatorial Boltzmann model.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
C(x)
be a power series with non-negative real-valued coefficients and radius of convergence
0<\rho<1
at which
C(\rho)<\infty
. Then

[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)=G(\rho)\rho^{-n}\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right],\quad n,N\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

We begin with the simple observation

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\right]&=[x^{n}y^{N}]\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}\right]x^{\ell}y^{k}\\
&=[x^{n}y^{N}]\sum_{k\geq 0}y^{k}\sum_{\sum_{j\geq 1}jp_{j}=k}\prod_{j\geq 1}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{j}=p_{j}\right]\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 1}j\sum_{1\leq i\leq p_{j}}C_{j,i}=\ell\right]x^{\ell}.\end{split}

(3.6)

We will study this expresion by first simplifying the sum over
\ell
, then the sum over all
p_{j}
’s, and eventually the sum over
k
. We begin with the sum over
\ell
. For a
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-valued random variable
A
let
A(x):=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[A_{j}=\ell\right]x^{\ell}
denote its probability generating series. Then, if
(A_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-valued random variables,

\displaystyle(A_{1}+\dots+A_{m})(x)=\prod_{1\leq j\leq m}A_{j}(x),\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.7)

Let us write
C_{j}(x)
for the probability generating series of
jC_{j,i}
; note that the actual value of
i
is not important, since the
(C_{j,i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are iid. Then, whenever
\sum_{j\geq 1}p_{j}
is finite, (
3.7
) implies

\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 1}j\sum_{1\leq i\leq p_{j}}C_{j,i}=\ell\right]x^{\ell}=\prod_{j\geq 1}C_{j}(x)^{p_{j}}.

Noting that
jC_{j,1}
takes only values in the lattice
j\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we obtain

C_{j}(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[jC_{j,1}=\ell\right]x^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[C_{j,1}=\ell\right]x^{j\ell}=\frac{1}{C(\rho^{j})}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}c_{\ell}\rho^{\ell}x^{j\ell}=\frac{C((\rho x)^{j})}{C(\rho^{j})}.

We deduce

\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 1}j\sum_{1\leq i\leq p_{j}}C_{j,i}=\ell\right]x^{\ell}=\prod_{j\geq 1}\left(\frac{C((\rho x)^{j})}{C(\rho^{j})}\right)^{p_{j}}.

This puts the sum over
\ell
in (
3.6
) in compact form. To simplify the sum over the
p_{j}
’s in (
3.6
) define independent random variables
(H_{j})_{j\geq 1}
with
H_{j}\sim\mathrm{Po}\left(C((\rho x)^{j})/j\right)
. Then

\sum_{\sum_{j\geq 1}jp_{j}=k}\prod_{j\geq 1}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{j}=p_{j}\right]\left(\frac{C((\rho x)^{j})}{C(\rho^{j})}\right)^{p_{j}}=\frac{G(\rho x,1)}{G(\rho,1)}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 1}jH_{j}=k\right].

By similar reasoning as before the probability generating function of
jH_{j}
is given by

\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[H_{j}=\ell\right]y^{j\ell}=\exp\left(-C((\rho x)^{j})/j\right)\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{(C((\rho x)^{j})y^{j}/j)^{\ell}}{\ell!}=\frac{\exp\left(C((\rho x)^{j})y^{j}/j\right)}{\exp\left(C((\rho x)^{j})/j\right)}.

Applying (
3.7
), where we set
A_{j}:=jH_{j}
, in combination with this identity and plugging everything into (
3.6
) yields

\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 1}jH_{j}=k\right]y^{k}=\frac{G(\rho x,y)}{G(\rho)}.

All in all, we have shown that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\right]=G(\rho)^{-1}[x^{n}y^{N}]G(\rho x,y)
. With
[x^{n}]F(ax)=a^{n}[x^{n}]F(x)
for any power series
F
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
we finish the proof.
∎

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
,

be as in the previous section, see (

),
where

With this notation

The driving idea behind these definitions is that the random variables

’s might be large. This brings us to our general proof strategy. First of all, we will study our probability space conditioned on

Our first aim is to study the distribution – in particular the tails – of

(and

Proposition 3.5
.

There exists

The first inequality follows directly from the definition of

\frac{C(z^{j})}{c_{m}z^{jm}}\leq 1+z^{j}\frac{1}{c_{m}}\sum_{k>m}c_{k}\rho^{jk-j(m+1)}=1+z^{j}\frac{\rho^{-2m}}{c_{m}}\sum_{k>m}c_{k}\rho^{jk-j(m+1)+2m}.

Since

Thus, as

Lemma 3.6
.

There exist constants

We split up

). Using Lemma

\mathrm{Pr}\left[P=N\right]=[x^{N}]F(x)\sim\frac{1}{G(\rho)}B(\rho_{A})[x^{N}]A(x)\sim\frac{B(\rho^{-m})}{G(\rho)\Gamma(c_{m})}\cdot{N^{c_{m}-1}}{}\rho^{mN},\quad N\to\infty,

(3.11)

Similarly, for

∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let

Lemma 3.8

There exists an

such that for all

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq N}\{P_{j}=p_{j}\}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq A\cdot N\cdot\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathrm{Po}\left(\frac{C(\rho^{j})}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)=p_{j}\right].

Using the definition of conditional probability and recalling that the

P_{j}\sim\mathrm{Po}\left(C(\rho^{j})/j\right)

\displaystyle\textrm{Pr}\Biggl[\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq N}

\displaystyle\{P_{j}=p_{j}\}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\Biggr]\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]}\cdot\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}\left(\frac{C(\rho^{j})}{j}\right)^{p_{j}}\frac{1}{p_{j}!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\frac{C(\rho^{j})}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)\rho^{mN}\cdot\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathrm{Po}\left(\frac{C(\rho^{j})}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)=p_{j}\right].

, cf. (

). As it will be necessary later, we show uniform tails bounds that hold for the joint distribution of
P_{1}
and
R
conditioned on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

There exist
A>0
and
0<a<1
such that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq A\cdot a^{p+r},\quad p,r,N\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

We will prove the claimed bound by showing appropriate bounds for the moment generating function
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda R}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]
. Let us fix any
0<\lambda<-\log(\rho)/2
such that
\rho e^{\lambda}<1
. Then
\rho^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j}<\rho
for all
j\geq 2
. Recall that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[C_{j,i}=k\right]={c_{k}\rho^{jk}}/{C(\rho^{j})},k\in\mathbb{N},j\geq 2,i\geq 1
, see (
3.5
). We obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(j(C_{j,i}-m))}\right]=\sum_{s\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[C_{j,i}=s+m\right]\mathrm{e}^{\lambda js}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda jm}\frac{C(\rho^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{C(\rho^{j})},\quad i\geq 1,j\geq 2.

Let
\Omega_{N,p}
be the set of all
\mathrm{p}=(p_{2},\dots,p_{N})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N-1}
such that
(p,p_{2},\dots,p_{N})\in\Omega_{N}
, i.e.
p=N-\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}jp_{j}
, and let
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}
be the event

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}:=\{P_{1}=p\}\cap\bigcap_{2\leq j\leq N}\{P_{j}=p_{j}\}.

Then by Markov’s inequality and the independence of the
C_{j,i}
’s and the
P_{j}
’s, for any
\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{N,p}

\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda{R}}\geq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda r}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda{R}}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}\right]=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\prod_{j=2}^{N}\left(\frac{C(\rho^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{C(\rho^{j})\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm}}\right)^{p_{j}}.

Abbreviate
\tau_{j}:=C((\rho\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})^{j})/(\rho\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})^{jm}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
3.8
there exists
A_{1}>0
such that

\begin{split}\text{Pr}[P_{1}=p,\penalty\ &R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}]=\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{N,p}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{p}}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\\
&\leq A_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}N\exp\left(-\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\frac{C(\rho^{j})}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)\frac{(C(\rho)/\rho^{m})^{p}}{p!}\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{N,p}}\prod_{2\leq j\leq N}\frac{(\tau_{j}/j)^{p_{j}}}{p_{j}!}.\end{split}

(3.15)

With Proposition
3.5
we find
A_{2}>0
with

\exp\left(-\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\frac{C(\rho^{j})}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{m}\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\frac{1}{j}\right)\leq A_{2}N^{-c_{m}}.

Let
H_{j}\sim\mathrm{Po}\left(\tau_{j}/j\right)
be independent for
j=2,\dots,N
and set
\tau:=\text{exp}(\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\tau_{j}/j)
. Moreover, abbreviate
B:=C(\rho)/\rho^{m}
. From (
3.15
) we obtain that there is an
A_{3}>0
such that

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]&\leq A_{3}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}N^{1-c_{m}}\cdot\tau\cdot\frac{B^{p}}{p!}\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{N,p}}\prod_{j=2}^{N}\mathrm{Pr}\left[H_{j}=p_{j}\right].\end{split}

(3.16)

Note that

\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{N,p}}\prod_{j=2}^{N}\mathrm{Pr}\left[H_{j}=p_{j}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}jH_{j}=N-p\right]=\tau^{-1}\cdot[x^{N-p}]\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{\tau_{j}}{j}x^{j}\right).

Observe that in the last expression we actually have to restrict the summation to the interval
2\leq j\leq N
; however,
[x^{M}]\text{exp}(\sum_{j\geq 2}{\tau_{j}}x^{j}/j)=[x^{M}]\text{exp}(\sum_{2\leq j\leq M}{\tau_{j}}x^{j}/j)
for all
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{\tau_{j}}{j}x^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(c_{m}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{x^{j}}{j}\right)\cdot\exp\left(-c_{m}x+\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{x^{j}}{j}(\tau_{j}-c_{m})\right)=:G(x)\cdot H(x).

By Proposition
3.5
there exists a constant
A_{4}>0
such that

\tau_{j}\leq c_{m}(1+A_{4}(\rho\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})^{j}).

With this at hand we deduce
that
H(x)
has radius of convergence (at least)
(\rho\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})^{-1}
, which by our choice of
\lambda
is
>1
. Note that
G(x)=(1-x)^{-c_{m}}
, which shows together with Lemma
3.2
that
G
has property (

S
2

) with radius of convergence
1
.
As
G(x)
only has positive coefficients, by Lemma
2.3
and the remark in (
2.2
) there is an
A_{5}>0
such that

[x^{N-p}]G(x)H(x)\leq A_{5}(N-p)^{c_{m}-1},\quad p=0,\dots,N-1.

All in all,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}jH_{j}=N-p\right]\leq A_{5}\tau^{-1}(N-p)^{c_{m}-1},\quad p=0,\dots,N-1.

(3.17)

For the case
p=N
note that the probability that
\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}jH_{j}=0
equals
\tau^{-1}
.
Putting the pieces together, we get from (
3.16
) that there is an
A_{6}>0
such that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq A_{6}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}N^{1-c_{m}}\left(\frac{B^{N}}{N!}+\frac{B^{p}}{p!}(N-p)^{c_{m}-1}\cdot\mathbf{1}[p\neq N]\right).

(3.18)

Observe that
N^{1-c_{m}}B^{N}/N!\leq e^{-\lambda N}
for
N
large enough. Additionally, if
N/2\leq p<N
, then for
N
large enough

N^{1-c_{m}}\frac{(e^{\lambda}B)^{p}}{p!}(N-p)^{c_{m}-1}=(1-p/N)^{c_{m}-1}\frac{(e^{\lambda}B)^{p}}{p!}\leq N^{1-c_{m}}\cdot\frac{(e^{\lambda}B)^{p}}{p!}\leq 1

and for
0\leq p\leq N/2

N^{1-c_{m}}\frac{(e^{\lambda}B)^{p}}{p!}(N-p)^{c_{m}-1}\leq\max\{2^{1-c_{m}},1\}\cdot e^{e^{\lambda}B}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathrm{Po}\left(e^{\lambda}B\right)=p\right]\leq\max\{2^{1-c_{m}},1\}\cdot e^{e^{\lambda}B}

is also bounded. Plugging these bounds into (
3.18
) completes the proof.
∎

We have just proven that
P_{1},R
have (joint) exponential tails when conditioned on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
. The next lemma is the last essential step towards the proof of Theorem
1.1
, where we estimate
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]
. Recall from (
3.9
) that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[L+R=n-mN\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right],\quad\text{where}\quad L=\sum_{1\leq i\leq P_{1}}(C_{1,i}-m).

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
C(x)
be subexponential. Then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\sim c_{n-m(N-1)}\rho^{n-mN},\quad n,N,n-mN\to\infty.

Proof.

For the entire proof we abbreviate
\widetilde{N}:=n-mN
. Then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]=\sum_{p\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}\mathrm{Pr}\left[L=\widetilde{N}-r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N},P_{1}=p,R=r\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right].

(3.19)

For brevity, let us write in the remainder

{\cal D}_{N,p,r}={\cal P}_{N}\cap\{P_{1}=p\}\cap\{R=r\}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{\widetilde{N}}:=\text{Pr}\big[C_{1,1}=\widetilde{N}+m\big]=\frac{c_{n-m(N-1)}\rho^{n-m(N-1)}}{C(\rho)}.

We will show that

\text{Pr}\big[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\big]\sim p\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}\quad\text{for}\quad p,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\text{ as }\widetilde{N}\to\infty.

(3.20)

Let
a\in(0,1)
be the constant guaranteed to exist from Lemma
3.9
, and choose
\delta>0
such that

(1+\delta)a<1
. We will also show that there are
C>0,N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\text{Pr}\big[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\big]\leq C(1+\delta)^{p+r}\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ p,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\widetilde{N}\geq N_{0}.

(3.21)

From the two facts (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) the statement in the lemma can be obtained as follows. We will assume throughout that
\delta
is fixed as described above, say for concreteness
\delta=(a^{-1}-1)/2
, and choose an
0<\varepsilon<1
arbitrarily. Moreover, we will fix
K\in\mathbb{N}
in dependence of
\varepsilon
only, and we will split the double sum in (
3.19
) in three parts with
(p,r)
in the sets

B_{\leq}=\{(p,r):0\leq p,r\leq K\},\quad B_{>,\cdot}=\{(p,r):p>K,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},\quad B_{\cdot,>}=\{(p,r):p\in\mathbb{N}_{0},r>K\}.

We will show that the main contribution to
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal P}_{N}\right]
stems from
B_{\leq}
, while the other two parts contribute rather insignificantly. Let us begin with treating the latter parts.
Observe that using Lemma
3.9

and (
3.21
) we obtain that there is a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that for all

r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
K\geq K_{0}(\varepsilon)

\begin{split}\sum_{p\geq K}\mathrm{Pr}\left[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]&\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{p\geq K}(1+\delta)^{p+r}\cdot a^{p+r}\cdot Q\\
&\leq\varepsilon\cdot((1+\delta)a)^{r}\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}.\end{split}

Since
(1+\delta)a<1
, summing this over all
r
readily yields for
c=(1-(1+\delta)a)^{-1}
that

\sum_{(p,r)\in B_{>,\cdot}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq c\varepsilon\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}.

(3.22)

Completely analogously with the roles of
p,r
interchanged we obtain that also

\sum_{(p,r)\in B_{\cdot,>}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq c\varepsilon\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}.

(3.23)

It remains to handle the part of the sum in (
3.19
) with
p,r\in B_{\leq}
. Using (
3.20
) we infer that

\sum_{(p,r)\in B_{\leq}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\sim\sum_{(p,r)\in B_{\leq}}p\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}.

Using Lemma
3.9
once again note that we can choose
K
large enough such that

\sum_{0\leq p\leq K}\sum_{r\geq K}p\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq A\sum_{0\leq p\leq K}\sum_{r\geq K}pa^{p+r}\leq\varepsilon

and that

\left|\sum_{p\geq 0}p\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]-\sum_{0\leq p\leq K}p\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\right|=\left|\sum_{p>K}p\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Altogether this establishes that

\left|\sum_{(p,r)\in B_{\leq}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[L=\widetilde{N}-r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\cal D}_{N,p,r}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[P_{1}\mid{\cal P}_{N}\right]Q_{\widetilde{N}}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon Q_{\widetilde{N}}.

Corollary
3.7
asserts that
\mathbb{E}\left[P_{1}\mid{\cal P}_{N}\right]\to C(\rho)\rho^{-m}
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, combining this with (

) and (

) we obtain from (

) that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mid{\cal P}_{N}\right]\sim C(\rho)\rho^{-m}\cdot Q_{\widetilde{N}}
, which is the claim of the lemma.

In order to complete the proof it remains to show the two claims (
3.20
) and (
3.21
). We begin with (

). Note that for
p,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\text{Pr}\big[L=\widetilde{N}-r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N},P_{1}=p,R=r\big]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}C_{1,i}=\widetilde{N}-r+pm\right].

(3.24)

Recall that
\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=k]=c_{k}\rho^{k}/C(\rho)
, where
\rho
is the radius of convergence of
C
. Since
C
is subexponential,
c_{k-1}\sim\rho c_{k}
and thus the distribution of the

’s is also subexponential with
\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=k-1]\sim\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=k]
.
We obtain with Lemma

(i)
that the latter probability is
\sim p\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=\widetilde{N}-r+pm]
, as
\widetilde{N}\to\infty
.
Moreover, as
\widetilde{N}\to\infty
,
\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=\widetilde{N}-r+pm]\sim Q_{\widetilde{N}}
, and (
3.20
) is established.

We finally show (
3.21
). Our starting point is again (
3.24
). Note that with Lemma
2.2

(ii)
there are
C>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the sought probability is at most
C(1+\delta)^{p}\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=\widetilde{N}-r+pm]
for all
\widetilde{N}-r+pm\geq N_{0}
. Moreover, as we have argued in the previous paragraph, the distribution of
C_{1,1}
is subexponential with
\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=k-1]\sim\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=k]
; we thus may choose
C
and
N_{0}
large enough such that in addition
\text{Pr}[C_{1,1}=\widetilde{N}-r+pm]\leq C(1+\delta)^{r}Q_{\widetilde{N}}
. This establishes (
3.21
) if
\widetilde{N}-r+pm\geq N_{0}
. To treat the remaining cases, note that in this situation we have

. Since the probability generating series of

is subexponential with radius of convergence 1, we obtain that
C(1+\delta)^{r}Q_{\widetilde{N}}>1
for sufficiently large
\widetilde{N}
; thus (
3.21
) is trivially true in this case.
∎

3.3
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let us begin with (re-)collecting all basic definitions that will be needed in the proof. Suppose that
C(x)
is subexponential with radius of convergence
0<\rho<1
and set
m:=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}:c_{k}>0\}
, see also (
3.1
). Moreover, let
P_{j}\sim\mathrm{Po}\left(C(\rho^{j})/j\right),j\in\mathbb{N}
and
C_{j,1},\dots,C_{j,P_{j}},{j\in\mathbb{N}}
have the distribution specified in (
3.5
), that is,
\mathrm{Pr}\left[C_{j,i}=k\right]={c_{k}\rho^{jk}}/{C(\rho^{j})},k,i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. We assume that all these random variables are independent.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
,
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
be as in (
3.3
), that is, with

P=\sum_{j\geq 1}jP_{j},\quad L=\sum_{1\leq i\leq P_{1}}(C_{j,i}-m),\quad R=\sum_{j\geq 2}j\sum_{1\leq i\leq P_{j}}(C_{j,i}-m)

we have that
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}=\{P=N\}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\{L+R=n-mP\}
.

With this notation at hand, let
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
be a uniformly drawn random object from
{\cal G}_{n,N}
, meaning that the number of atoms is
n
and the number of components
N
. According to Lemma
3.3
and using that the Boltzmann model induces the uniform distribution on objects of the same size, we infer that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathsf{G}_{n,N}=G\right]=\frac{1}{|{\cal G}_{n,N}|}=\frac{\rho^{n}/C(\rho)}{|{\cal G}_{n,N}|\rho^{n}/C(\rho)}=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\Lambda G=G\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\right]}=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\Lambda G=G\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\right],\quad G\in{\cal G}_{n,N},

that is, studying the distribution of
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
boils down to considering the distribution of
\Lambda G
conditional on both
{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
.
This is the starting point of our investigations. In particular,
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
has
N
components with sizes given by the vector
(C_{j,i}:1\leq j\leq N,1\leq i\leq P_{j})
. Our aim is here to study the properties of that vector in the conditional space given by
{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
. To this end, set

M^{*}:=\max_{j\geq 1,1\leq i\leq P_{j}}C_{j,i}\quad\text{ and }\quad C^{*}_{p}:=\max\{C_{1,1},\dots,C_{1,p}\}\quad\text{for $p\in\mathbb{N}$}.

(3.25)

Then the statement of the theorem is that, conditional on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N},{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
, we have that
M^{*}=n-mN+\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
; since the total number of atoms is
n
, the number of components is
N
, and the smallest component contains
m
atoms, this immediately implies that there are
N+\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
components with exactly
m
atoms, and all remaining components have a total size of
\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
as well.

The general proof strategy in the remaining section is as follows. We first show in Lemma
3.11
that both
P_{1},R
are “small” in the conditioned space; this makes sure that only a bounded number of entries in the vector
(C_{j,i})_{j\geq 2,1\leq i\leq P_{j}}
are larger than
m
, and that this total excess is bounded. Hence, the remaining number of
n-(N-P_{1})m+\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
atoms is to be found in the components with sizes in
(C_{1,i})_{1\leq i\leq P_{1}}
. In Lemma
3.11
we exclude that
P_{1}
grows too large conditioned on
{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
; indeed, we show that it is stochastically bounded. Then the property of subexponentiality guarantees that only the maximum of the
C_{1,i}
’s dominates the entire sum, cf. Lemma
2.2

(iii)
, and Theorem
1.2
follows.

Let us now fill this overview with details. Recall Lemma
3.9
, which says that
P_{1},R
have (joint) exponential tails given
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
. We show that conditioning in addition to
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
does not change the behaviour qualitatively. The proof can be found at the end of the section.

Lemma 3.11
.

There exist constants
A>0
and
0<a<1
such that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p,R=r\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq A\cdot a^{p+r},\quad p,r,n,N\in\mathbb{N}.

With this lemma the proof of the theorem can be completed as follows. Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Abbreviate
\widetilde{N}=n-mN
. With
M^{*}
as in (
3.25
) we will show that there is
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[|M^{*}-\widetilde{N}|\geq K\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]<\varepsilon

for

sufficiently large, which is the statement of the theorem. According to Lemma
3.11

there exist constants

such that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq C_{R},P_{1}\geq C_{P}\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]<\varepsilon/2,\quad n,N,\widetilde{N}\in\mathbb{N}.

We deduce

\mathrm{Pr}\left[|M^{*}-\widetilde{N}|\geq K\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\sum_{0\leq r\leq C_{R}}\sum_{1\leq p\leq C_{P}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[{|M^{*}-\widetilde{N}|}\geq K\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N},R=r,P_{1}=p\right].

(3.26)

Note that we only need to consider values of
p
which are larger than
1
as

excludes
R=r\leq C_{R}<\widetilde{N}
.
The event “
{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N},R=r,P_{1}=p
” implies that

for all

and

. Recall the definition of

from (

for

large enough.
It follows that
C^{*}_{p}>m+r
and hence we are allowed to interchange

and

in this conditioned space. That yields

\mathrm{Pr}\left[|M^{*}-\widetilde{N}|\geq K\mid{\mathcal{E}}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{N},R=r,P_{1}=p\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[|C^{*}_{p}-\widetilde{N}|\geq K\mid S_{p}=\widetilde{N}-r+pm\right],

.

(iii)

, and the proof is completed.

3.4
Proof of Theorem
1.3

For the proof of this theorem we use the equivalent definition of multisets in which all objects not occurring in
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
are counted with multiplicity
d=0
.
Let
G=\{(C,d_{C}):C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}\}\cup\{(C,0):C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\}\in{\mathcal{G}}
and assume that
N(n)\equiv N
is such that
N(n),n-mN(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Let us write
R_{n,N}
for the object obtained after removing (i.e. setting the multiplicity to
0
) all objects of size
m
and a largest component (i.e. subtracting the multiplicity by one) from
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
. The statement of the theorem is equivalent to showing that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R_{n,N}=G\right]\to\exp\left(-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C(\rho^{j})-c_{m}\rho^{jm}}{j\rho^{jm}}\right)\rho^{\lvert G\rvert-m\kappa(G)},\quad n\to\infty,

see also (
1.3
). Defining the family of multiplicity counting functions
(d_{C}(\cdot))_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}
by
(d_{C}(G))_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}=(d_{C})_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}
for
G=\{(C,d_{C}):C\in{\mathcal{C}}\}\in{\mathcal{G}}
we immediately obtain that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R_{n,N}=G\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\right].

Let
S>\max\{m,\lvert G\rvert\}
be some arbitrary integer to be specified later. We infer that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R_{n,N}=G\right]\leq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\right].

To obtain a lower bound, since
S>\lvert G\rvert
, we observe that
\{\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\}
is the same as
\{\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\}\cap\{\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=0\}
. Moreover, note that
\lvert R_{n,N}\rvert\leq S
implies
d_{C}(R_{n,N})=0
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>S}
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[R_{n,N}=G\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C},\lvert R_{n,N}\rvert\leq S\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert R_{n,N}\rvert>S\right].

Let
\varepsilon>0
. According to Theorem
1.2
there is
S_{1}>\max\{m,\lvert G\rvert\}
so that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert R_{n,N}\rvert>S_{1}\right]<\varepsilon
. Hence
\mathrm{Pr}\left[R_{n,N}=G\right]
differs by at most
\varepsilon
from
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]
for all
S>S_{1}
.
Let us write
L_{n,N}
for the size of a largest component in
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
. Theorem
1.2
guarantees that
L_{n,N}
is unbounded whp, and so we obtain for any
S\in\mathbb{N}

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C},\lvert L_{n,N}\rvert>S\right]+o(1).

However, the event
\{\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C},\lvert L_{n,N}\rvert>S\}
is equivalent to the event
\{\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C},\lvert L_{n,N}\rvert>S\}
, since we obtain
R_{n,N}
by removing all components with size
m
and a largest component (of size
>S
) from
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
. Now we add and subtract
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,s}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C},\lvert L_{n,N}\rvert\leq S\right]=o(1)
in order to get rid of the event
\lvert L_{n,N}\rvert>S
and arrive at the fact

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(R_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]+o(1).

Combining all previous facts yields that for
n
sufficiently large

\big|\mathrm{Pr}\left[R_{n,N}=G\right]-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]\big|\leq 2\varepsilon

(3.28)

and thus we are left with estimating
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]
.
For
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S}:=(v_{C})_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}
denote by
G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})
the generating series of
{\mathcal{G}}
such that
x
marks the size,
y
the number of components and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S}=(v_{C})_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}
the multiplicities of
(C)_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}
, or in other words: for
\ell,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{S}:=(t_{C})_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\lvert{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}\rvert}
the coefficients are given by

g_{\ell,k,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{t}}_{S}}=\lvert\{G\in{\mathcal{G}}:\lvert G\rvert=\ell,\kappa(G)=k,\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(G)=t_{C}\}\rvert.

Setting
v_{C}=1
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}
we obtain the generating series
G(x,y)
counting only size and number of components by
x
and
y
respectively. As
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
is drawn uniformly at random from
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}
the proof reduces to determining

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]=\frac{[x^{n}y^{N}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{s}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}_{S}}]G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})}{[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)}.

The following lemma, whose proof is shifted to the end of this section, accomplishes this task.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}=(d_{C})_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}
with
D:=\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}\lvert C\rvert d_{C}
and
D^{\prime}:=\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}d_{C}
. Then

\frac{[x^{n}y^{N}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{s}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}_{S}}]G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})}{[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)}\to\rho^{D-mD^{\prime}}\prod_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}(1-\rho^{\lvert C\rvert-m}),\quad n\to\infty.

Lemma
3.12
yields directly for sufficiently large
n

\biggl\lvert\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]-\rho^{\lvert G\rvert-m\kappa(G)}\prod_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}(1-\rho^{\lvert C\rvert-m})\biggr\rvert<\varepsilon.

Now observe that with defining
C_{m+1,S}(x):=\sum_{m<\ell\leq S}\lvert{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell}\rvert x^{\ell}
we obtain

\lim_{S\to\infty}\prod_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}(1-\rho^{\lvert C\rvert-m})=\lim_{S\to\infty}\prod_{m<\ell\leq S}\exp\left(|{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell}|\log(1-\rho^{\ell-m})\right)=\lim_{S\to\infty}\exp\left(-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C_{m+1,S}(\rho^{j})}{j\rho^{jm}}\right).

By the continuity of
\exp\left(\cdot\right)
and monotone convergence this equals
G_{>m}(\rho)^{-1}
. Choose
S_{2}>\max\{m,\lvert G\rvert\}
large enough such that
\prod_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}(1-\rho^{\lvert C\rvert-m})
differs at most by
\varepsilon
from
G_{>m}(\rho)^{-1}
for all
S>S_{2}
. Summarizing, fixing
S\geq\max\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
we obtain for sufficiently large
n

\big|\mathrm{Pr}\left[\forall C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}:d_{C}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})=d_{C}\right]-\rho^{\lvert G\rvert-m\kappa(G)}G_{>m}(\rho)^{-1}\big|\leq 2\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary the proof of the theorem is finished with (
3.28
).

Proof of Lemma
3.12
.

First we determine
G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})
explicitly. Define the multivariate generating series

C(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})=y\left(C(x)+\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}(v_{C}-1)x^{\lvert C\rvert}\right),

where as usual
x
marks the size,
y
the number of components (which by convention is always
1
for
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
) and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S}
objects in
{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}
. Note that these parameters are clearly additive when forming multisets.
Hence, according to
[
18

the formula (
1.1
) extends to the multivariate version

\displaystyle G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C(x^{j},x^{j},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S}^{j})}{j}\right),

(3.29)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S}^{j}=(v_{C}^{j})_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}
. Setting
v_{C}=1
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}
we see that
G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}})\equiv G(x,y)
such that
[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)=\lvert{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}\rvert
. By elementary algebraic manipulations we reformulate (

\displaystyle\begin{split}G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})&=G(x,y)\exp\left(\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{m+1,S}}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(x^{\lvert C\rvert}yv_{C})^{j}}{j}-\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(x^{\lvert C\rvert}y)^{j}}{j}\right)\right)\\

.

\displaystyle[x^{n}y^{N}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}_{S}}]G(x,y,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{S})

Proof of Proposition
1.4

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

It is a well-known fact that the weak convergence of
(\mathsf{G}_{n},o_{n})
to
(\mathbbmss{G},\mathbbmss{o})
in (
1.4
) is equivalent to showing that for any bounded and continuous function
f:{\mathcal{B}}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathsf{G}_{n},o_{n})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbbmss{G},\mathbbmss{o})\right].

For any finite graph
G
denote by
o_{G}
a vertex chosen uniformly at random from its vertex set. Let
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})
denote a (canonically chosen) largest component of
\mathsf{G}_{n,N}
and
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})
the remainder after removing all objects of size
m
and
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})
. Let
f:{\mathcal{B}}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary bounded and continuous function. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathsf{G}_{n,N},o_{n})\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f({\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N}),o_{{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})})\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f({\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N}),o_{{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})})\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathsf{C}_{m},o_{m})\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\notin{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\cup{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right].

According to Theorem
1.2
we have that
\lvert{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\rvert=n-mN+\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
implying
\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]\sim(n-mN)/n\to 1-\lambda
. As the size of
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\in{\mathcal{C}}
tends to infinity and
(\mathsf{C}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges in the BS sense to
(\mathbbmss{C},\mathbbmss{o})
we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[f({\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N}),o_{{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})})\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]\to(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbbmss{C},\mathbbmss{o})\right],\quad n,N\to\infty.

Theorem
1.3
entails that
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})
has a limiting distribution and hence
\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]\to 0
. As
f
is bounded

\mathbb{E}\left[f({\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N}),o_{{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})})\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Finally, we obtain by combining Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
that
n-\lvert{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\cup{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\rvert=mN+\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
and hence
\mathrm{Pr}\left[o_{n}\notin{\mathcal{R}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\cup{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{G}_{n,N})\right]\sim mN/n\to\lambda
. Thus,

\lim_{n,N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathsf{G}_{n,N},o_{n})\right]=(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbbmss{C},\mathbbmss{o})\right]+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathsf{C}_{m},o_{m})\right].

∎
