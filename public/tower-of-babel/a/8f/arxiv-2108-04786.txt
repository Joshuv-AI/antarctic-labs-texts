Tangled Paths: A Random Graph Model from Mallows Permutations Thanks: An extended abstract of this paper appeared at EUROCOMB 2023 [ 19 ] .
Jessica Enright, Kitty Meeks, William Pettersson, John Sylvester

Abstract
We introduce the random graph 𝒫 ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathcal{P}(n,q) which results from taking the union of two paths of length n ⩾ 1 n\geqslant 1 , where the vertices of one of the paths have been relabelled according to a Mallows permutation with parameter 0 < q ⁡ ( n ) ⩽ 1 0<q(n)\leqslant 1 . This random graph model, the tangled path, goes through an evolution: if q q is close to 0 0 the graph bears resemblance to a path, and as q q tends to 1 1 it becomes an expander. In an effort to understand the evolution of 𝒫 ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathcal{P}(n,q) we determine the treewidth and cutwidth of 𝒫 ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathcal{P}(n,q) up to log factors for all q q . We also show that the property of having a separator of size one has a sharp threshold. In addition, we prove bounds on the diameter, and vertex isoperimetric number for specific values of q q .



keywords
Mallows permutations, random graphs, treewidth, cutwidth.

Introduction

Given two graphs
G,H
on a common vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
, and a permutation
\sigma
on
[n]
, it is natural to consider the following graph

\layer(G,\sigma(H))=\left([n],\;E(G)\cup\left\{\sigma(x)\sigma(y):xy\in E(H)\right\}\right),

which is the union of two graphs where the second graph has been relabelled by a permutation
\sigma
. Constructions of graphs via unions are very natural, and have appeared in several contexts, see Section
1.2
. Let
P_{n}
be the path on
[n]
that connects
i
to
i+1
, for
i\in[n-1]
, and let
S_{n}
be the set of all permutations on
[n]
. Consider the following scenario: one must choose a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
with the goal of making
\layer(P_{n},\sigma(P_{n}))
as different from a path as possible. There are many parameters one may use to measure the difference between a connected graph
G
and a path; for example one may look at the diameter
\mathsf{diam}(G)
or the vertex isoperimetric number
\varphi(G)
, as the path is extremal for these parameters. The treewidth
\mathsf{tw}(G)
which, broadly speaking, measures how far (globally) the graph is from being a tree
[
33
]
, is another natural candidate. Given two or more paths one can build a grid-like graph (see
[
18
, Lemma 8]
for more details) which would have treewidth and diameter
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. If we choose a permutation uniformly at random, then as a consequence of a result of Kim & Wormald
[
31
, Theorem 1]
, with high probability the resulting graph is a bounded degree expander. Thus, in this case, the graph
\layer(P_{n},\sigma(P_{n}))
has treewidth
\Theta(n)
and diameter
\Theta(\log n)
, so by these parameters it is essentially as far from a path as a sparse graph can be.

The example above shows that even restricting the input graphs to paths can produce rich classes of graphs. Having seen what happens for a uniformly random permutation, one may ask about the structure of
\layer(P_{n},\sigma(P_{n}))
when
\sigma
is drawn from a distribution on
S_{n}
that is not uniform. One of the most well known non-uniform distributions on
S_{n}
is the Mallows distribution, introduced by Mallows
[
37
]
in the late 1950s in the context of statistical ranking theory. Recently it has been the subject of renewed interest for other applications, and as an interesting and natural model to study in its own right, see Section
1.2
. The distribution has a parameter
q
which, roughly speaking, controls the amount of disorder in the permutation.

For real
q>0
and integer
n\geqslant 1
, the
(n,q)
-
Mallows measure

\mu_{n,q}
on
S_{n}
is given by

\mu_{n,q}(\sigma)=\frac{q^{\operatorname{Inv}(\sigma)}}{Z_{n,q}}\qquad\text{for any }\sigma\in S_{n},

(1)

where
\operatorname{Inv}(\sigma)=|\{(i,j)\,:\,\text{$i<j$ and $\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)$}\}|
is the number of inversions in the permutation
\sigma
and
Z_{n,q}
is given explicitly by the following
formula
[
5
, Equation (2)]
:

Z_{n,q}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1+q+\cdots+q^{i-1}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q}.

When
q\rightarrow 0
, the distribution
\mu_{n,q}
converges weakly to the degenerate distribution on the identity permutation. We extend
\mu_{n,q}
to
q=0
by setting
\mu_{n,0}
to be the probability measure assigning
1
to the identity permutation. On the other hand if
q=1
then
\mu_{n,1}
is the uniform measure on
S_{n}
. One can see that
\sigma\sim\mu_{n,q}
has distribution
\mu_{n,1/q}
when reversed.

We study the random graph given by
\layer(P_{n},\sigma(P_{n}))
, where
\sigma\sim\mu_{n,q}
and
0\leqslant q:=q(n)\leqslant 1
. From now on we call this random graph the
tangled path
model and denote it by
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
. Thus a random graph
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
has vertex set
[n]
and (random) edge set
E(P_{n})\cup\{\sigma(i)\sigma(i+1):i\in[n-1]\}
, where
\sigma\sim\mu_{n,q}
. We restrict to
q\in[0,1]
as reversing the permutation does not affect our construction (up to a relabelling, see (
8
)). We also identify any multi-edges created as one edge, however this detail is not important for any of our results. This paper will focus on
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
; as we have seen already combining paths can give rise to interesting and varied graphs, and the Mallows permutation gives our model a parameter
q
which, roughly speaking, increases the ‘tangled-ness’ of the graph. Other reasons for using Mallows permutations are that they are well studied (see Section
1.2
), and they are mathematically tractable since they can be generated by a sequence of independent random variables (see Section
3.1
).

By the above,
\mathcal{P}(n,0)
is a path and
\mathcal{P}(n,1)
is an expander with high probability; the latter follows from
[
31
, Theorem 1]
but we also give a self-contained proof in this paper. Our ultimate aim is to understand the structure of
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
for intermediate values of
q
, and this paper takes the first steps in this direction. Informally, if
q
is not tending to
1
too fast, then
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
is ‘path-like’; we show that if
q<1
is fixed the diameter is linear (Theorem
1.3
), and there is a sharp threshold for having a single cut vertex at
q_{c}=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\log n}
(Theorem
1.2
). For
q\rightarrow 1
sufficiently fast, it makes more sense to measure the complexity of the internal structure of
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
by how much it differs from a tree. Here we show that, up to logarithmic factors, the treewidth
[
33
]
of
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
grows at rate
(1-q)^{-1}
(Theorem
1.4
) until the graph becomes an expander at around
q=1-\frac{1}{n}
(Theorem
1.1
), indicating that, in the sense of treewidth, the complexity of the structure grows smoothly with
q
. This behaviour contrasts with the binomial/Erdős-Rényi random graph
[
21
]
where treewidth increases rapidly from being bounded by a constant, to
\Theta(n)
as the average degree rises from below one to above one
[
17
,
34
]
.

Further motivation for this line of study comes from practical algorithmic applications. Many real-world systems – including social, biological and transport networks – involve qualitatively different types of edges, where each type of edge generates a “layer” with specific structural properties
[
32
,
43
]
. For example, when modelling the spread of disease in livestock, one layer of interest arises from physical adjacency of farms, and so is determined entirely by geography. A second epidemiologically-relevant layer could describe the pairs of farms which share equipment: this is no longer fully determined by geography, but will nevertheless be influenced by the location of farms, as those that are geographically close are more likely to cooperate in this way. It is known that algorithmically useful structure in individual layers of a graph is typically lost when the layers are combined adversarially
[
18
]
. The present work can be seen as an attempt to understand the structure of graphs generated from two simple layers which are both influenced to some extent by a shared underlying “geography”. In this setting the treewidth
\mathsf{tw}
is a natural parameter as many
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problems become tractable when parametrised by
\mathsf{tw}

[
15
, Ch. 7]
.

For an integer

For any fixed

is

The diameter
\mathsf{diam}(G)
of a graph

is the length of the longest shortest path between any pair of vertices. Theorem

implies that
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\log n\right)

. On the other hand

Theorem 1.3
.

For any

. Then, there exists a constant

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{diam}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\!\left(n,q\right)\right)\geqslant cn\,\right)\leqslant n^{-1/10}.

This result follows from bounds on the number of cut vertices used to prove Theorem

The treewidth
\mathsf{tw}(G)
of a graph
G
is one less than the minimum size of the largest vertex subset (i.e. bag) in a tree decomposition of
G
, minimised over all such decompositions. The cutwidth
\mathsf{cw}(G)
is the greatest number of edges crossing any real point under an injective function
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, minimised over all
f
. See Section
2.1
for full definitions of these quantities. It is known that for any graph
G
we have
\mathsf{tw}(G)\leqslant\mathsf{cw}(G)
, however there may be a multiplicative discrepancy of order up to
n
. We show there is at most only a constant factor discrepancy for
G=\mathcal{P}(n,q)
in certain ranges of
q
, and give bounds for all

which are tight up to a
\log
factor.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any constant
\kappa>0
, let
0<q\leqslant 1-\kappa\cdot\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}
. Then, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that w.h.p.

c_{1}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(1/q)}}+1\right)\leqslant\mathsf{tw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))\leqslant\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))\leqslant c_{2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(1/q)}}+1\right).

Furthermore, if
1-\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\leqslant q\leqslant 1
, then w.h.p.

10^{-5}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{1}{1-q},\;n\right\}\leqslant\mathsf{tw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))\leqslant\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))\leqslant 5\cdot\max\left\{\frac{1}{1-q}\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\right),\;n\right\}.

Observe that if
q\rightarrow 1
then
\log(1/q)\approx 1-q
and so when
q=1-\Theta\left((\log\log n)^{2}/\log n\right)
we have
\sqrt{\log(n)/\log(1/q)}\approx-\log(1-q)/(1-q)
. Thus, the two upper bounds on the cutwidth are equal up to constants for this range of
q
. Hence, for this range of
q
, the upper bound for the cutwidth given in the second equation is tight and the lower bound for treewidth is off by a multiplicative factor of order
\log\log n
.

The lower bounds on treewidth in Theorem
1.4
are proved by relating the treewidth to the occurrence of certain permutations as consecutive patterns in the underlying Mallows permutation. The upper bounds on cutwidth are proved by controlling the density of long edges.

Many works have studied properties of a typical permutation sampled from the Mallows measure, in particular the longest increasing subsequence

Random graphs have been heavily studied since their introduction in the late 1950s

[

4

[
12

[
22

shows the union of two uniformly random trees on the same vertex set is an expander with high probability.

There are also several papers which consider the graphs formed from (permuted) unions of graphs. In particular independent sets in the union of two Hamiltonian cycles
[
2
]
, and the treewidth of a union of two graphs glued using a permutation
[
3
]
and
[
46

[
1

42

[

. Unions of dense graphs with random graphs, namely ‘randomly perturbed graphs’, have been studied intensely, see
[

1.3
Outline of the Paper

In Section
2
we cover some basic notation, definitions and concentration inequalities. In Section
3
we state some known facts about the Mallows distribution, in particular defining the
q
-Mallows process, before introducing our notions of ‘flushing’ and ‘local’ events that are useful later in the paper. Section
4
establishes properties of the tangled path in the case where
q
is close to one. The first result in this section shows that when
q=1
(i.e. a uniformly random permutation) the tangled path is an expander with high probability. Then, a bound on the probability of events under the
q
-Mallows measure by that of the
1
-Mallows measure is shown; this is useful later when bounding the treewidth. The beginning of Section
5
focuses on bounding the probability of flushing events. In the remainder of Section
5
, these bounds are used to prove a sharp threshold for
(1,\alpha)
-separators and a linear bound on the diameter. Arguably the most interesting techniques and proofs are in Section
6
. To prove a lower bound on the treewidth we use consecutive patterns in the Mallows permutations to find smaller tangled paths with a higher

The

In this section we describe a random process which generates a Mallows permutation. It is easier to prove results via this process rather than with

The

-Mallows Process

In this section we introduce the

[

(6)

(7)

To visualise this process: start with an empty bookshelf and at each time

3.2
Local Events and Concentration

As the
q
-Mallows process
(r_{i})
is generated by a sequence
(v_{i})
of independent random variables, it is convenient to describe events in terms of
(v_{i})
. An example of such an event is the flush (
13
) given by
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\left\{\text{for each }i>k\text{ we have }v_{i}\leqslant i-k\right\}
. This event will be introduced formally in Section
3.3
and is closely related to the existence of cut vertices, see Section
5
. In this section we are interested in events defined in terms of the sequence
(v_{i})
which, unlike the flush, become pairwise (asymptotically) independent when the indices of the events are suitably well spaced. To begin we describe a class of events satisfying this criterion; we call these local events. We then show in Lemma
3.3
that sums of indicators of local events concentrate.

For integers
n\geqslant 1
,
i\in[n]
, and
\ell\geqslant 1
, we say that an event is
\ell
-local to
i
if the event is completely determined by the values of
v_{j}
in the range
j\in[i-\ell,i+\ell]
. As an example the event
(\{v_{i}\leqslant 5\}\cup\{v_{i+1}=2\})\cap\{v_{i-2}=3\}
is
2
-local to
i
. We say a random variable
X_{i}
is
\ell
-local to
i
if it is a weighted indicator random variable of an event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
which is
\ell
-local to
i
, that is
X_{i}=c_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
where
c_{i}\geqslant 0
are real numbers. We prove several results by showing that certain small subgraphs are present. Often the number of these subgraphs can be expressed as sums of local random variables, the following lemma, based on Chebyshev’s inequality, is then useful.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
,
\ell\geqslant 1
be integers and
S\subseteq[n]
non-empty. Let
X=\sum_{i\in S}X_{i}
where each
X_{i}
is a non-negative random variable
\ell
-local to
i
and
M=\max_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}^{2}\,\right]
. Then, for any
x\geqslant 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\left|X-\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\right|>x\cdot\sqrt{M\cdot|S|\cdot\ell}\,\right)\leqslant 5/x^{2}.

Proof.

We seek to apply the second moment method to
X
, and thus we must calculate or bound terms of the form
\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}\cdot X_{j}\,\right]
. For any
i,j\in S
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}\cdot X_{j}\,\right]\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}^{2}\,\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{j}^{2}\,\right]}\leqslant M.

(11)

Observe, each
X_{i}
only depends on (at most) the values
v_{a}
for
a\in[i-\ell,i+\ell]
, and so

\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}\cdot X_{j}\,\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}\,\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{j}\,\right]\qquad\text{ for any $i,j$ with }|i-j|>2\ell,

(12)

by independence of the sequence
(v_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. Thus, since
X_{i}\geqslant 0
, by (
11
) and (
12
) we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in S}\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}\cdot X_{j}\,\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{i}\,\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{j}\,\right]\leqslant\sum_{i,j\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\{|i-j|\leqslant 2\ell\}}\cdot M\leqslant|S|(4\ell+1)M\leqslant 5|S|\ell M.

Thus applying Chebyshev’s inequality
[
38
, Theorem 3.6]
, for any
x\geqslant 0
, gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\left|X-\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\right|\geqslant(x/\sqrt{5})\cdot\sqrt{5|S|\ell M}\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\left|X-\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\right|\geqslant(x/\sqrt{5})\cdot\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\,\right)\leqslant(x/\sqrt{5})^{-2},

concluding the proof. ∎

Flushing Events

In this section we define two events with respect to the sequence of values

(13)

and say there is a

).

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{L}_{k}

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n,\ell\geqslant 1
be integers,
0<q<1
, and
(c_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be any non-negative real sequence. Let
\mathcal{E}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{F}_{i}

\mathcal{B}_{i}

\mathcal{D}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}

is given by (
14

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,X\neq Y\,\right)\leqslant n^{-7},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\,Y\,\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]+n^{-7}\cdot\sum_{i}c_{i}.

The event
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is
\max\{b_{n},\ell\}
-local to

as
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is
\ell
-local to

and
\mathcal{L}_{i}

), is
b_{n}
-local to

\mathcal{E}=\cap_{i\in[n]}\{v_{i}\leqslant b_{n}(q)\}

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{E}^{c}\,\right)\leqslant n^{-7}

\mathcal{E}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i}

\mathcal{E}_{i}

\mathcal{D}_{i}

(v_{j})_{i<j\leqslant i+b_{n}(q)}

\mathcal{F}_{i}

\mathcal{L}_{i}

4.1

Recall the definition of

Lemma 4.1

For any integer

Proof.

Observe that there are

∎

Recall the definitions of the edge and vertex isoperimetric numbers
\phi

Lemma 4.2

For any integer

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\varphi(\mathcal{P}(n,1))\leqslant\frac{1}{40}\,\right)\leqslant 1000\cdot n^{7/2}\cdot\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}.

Proof.

Observe that
\varphi(\mathcal{P}(n,1))\geqslant\phi(\mathcal{P}(n,1))/4

\mathcal{P}(n,1)

from above.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\layer(P_{n},\sigma(P_{n}))
where
\sigma\sim\mu_{n,1}
. Now, if the edge isoperimetric number of
\mathcal{P}
is at most
\alpha
then for any set
S\subset[n]
there can be at most
\alpha|S|
edges of either the permuted or un-permuted path in
\partial(S)
. That is, for any
\alpha>0
we have

\{\phi(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\alpha\}\subseteq\bigcup_{s=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\bigcup_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\;\;\bigcup_{S\subseteq V:|S|=s,\left|\partial(S)\cap E(P_{n})\right|=k}\{\left|\partial(S)\cap E(\sigma(P_{n}))\right|\leqslant\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor-k\}.

(17)

By Lemma
4.1
there are at most
2\cdot\binom{n-1}{k}
sets
S
(of any size) such that
\left|\partial(S)\cap E(P_{n})\right|=k
. So by applying this bound, the union bound, and rewriting (
17
), where we ignore the

, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\phi(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\alpha\,\right)\leqslant 2\sum_{s=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}\max_{S\subseteq V:|S|=s}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\left|\partial(S)\cap E(\sigma(P_{n}))\right|\leqslant\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor\,\right).

(18)

We will now bound the probability on the right-hand side, note that since
\sigma
is a uniform permutation this is the same for all
S
with
|S|=s
.

To begin we will view the action of the uniform random permutation
\sigma
on
P_{n}
as a relabelling of
V(P_{n})=[n]
. Under this relabelling a given set
S\subseteq V(P_{n})
, with
|S|=s
, is equally likely to be mapped to any other
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(P_{n})
with
|S^{\prime}|=s
. The number of
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(P_{n})
with an edge boundary of size
k\geqslant 0
is at most
2\cdot\binom{n-1}{k}
by Lemma
4.1
. Since
|S^{\prime}|=s
there are
s!
ways to order the elements of
S
within
S^{\prime}
and
(n-s)!
ways to organise the remaining elements within the path. Thus, if we restrict to a fixed
0<\alpha\leqslant 1/10
, then for any
S\subseteq V
with
|S|=s
we have

\!\!\!\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\left|\partial(S)\cap E(\sigma(P_{n}))\right|\leqslant\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor\,\right)\leqslant 2\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}\frac{s!(n-s)!}{n!}\leqslant 2n\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\frac{s!(n-s)!}{n!},

(19)

where the last bound holds since
\alpha s\leqslant n/20
and
\binom{x}{y}
is increasing in
y
provided
y<\lfloor x/2\rfloor
.

Recall that for any integers
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
and any real number

such that
|x|\leqslant n
we have

\binom{n}{k}\leqslant\left(\frac{n\mathrm{e}}{k}\right)^{k},\qquad\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{n}\leqslant n!\leqslant\mathrm{e}\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^{n}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{x}.

(20)

Thus, as
s\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and
0<\alpha\leqslant 1/10
are fixed, by (
20
) and monotonicity of
\binom{x}{y}
we have

\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\leqslant\left(\frac{n\mathrm{e}}{\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor\rfloor}\right)^{\lfloor\alpha\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor\rfloor}\leqslant\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}}{\alpha\left(1-\frac{4}{\alpha n}\right)}\right)^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}}\leqslant\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{4}{\alpha}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}}=\mathrm{e}^{2}\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}},

(21)

provided that
n>4/\alpha
. Observe that
s=n/2
maximises
s^{s}\left(n-s\right)^{n-s}
, thus (
20
) gives

s!\cdot(n-s)!\leqslant\mathrm{e}\sqrt{s}\left(\frac{s}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{s}\cdot\mathrm{e}\sqrt{n-s}\left(\frac{n-s}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{n-s}\leqslant\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}n}{\mathrm{e}^{n}}\cdot s^{s}\left(n-s\right)^{n-s}\leqslant\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}n}{\mathrm{e}^{n}}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{n}.

(22)

For
n>4\alpha
, inserting the bounds from (
20
), (
21
) and (
22
) into (
18
) and (
19
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\phi(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\alpha\,\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{s=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}\cdot 2n\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\alpha s\rfloor}\frac{s!(n-s)!}{n!}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2n^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2}\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}}\cdot 2n\mathrm{e}^{2}\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\alpha n}{2}}\frac{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}n}{\mathrm{e}^{n}}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\mathrm{e}^{6}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot n^{7/2}\cdot\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha n}2^{-n},

One can check that if we fix
\alpha=1/10
then

. Thus taking

gives

for

as

.
∎

4.2
Relating Different
q
-Mallows Measures

The aim of this section is to prove the following result which allows us to relate properties satisfied by the non-uniform tangled path (
q\neq 1
) to those satisfied in the uniform case. Let
2^{\binom{n}{2}}
denote the set of all (labelled)
n
-vertex graphs.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
0<q<1
. Then, the following holds:

(i)

For any
A\subseteq S_{n}
, we have

\mu_{n,q}(A)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{9n^{2}(1-q)}\cdot\mu_{n,1}(A).

(ii)

For any
B\subseteq 2^{\binom{n}{2}}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,q)\in B\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{9n^{2}(1-q)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,1)\in B\,\right).

(iii)

If
q=1-o\!\left(n^{-2}\right)
, then for any
B\subseteq 2^{\binom{n}{2}}
we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,q)\in B\,\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,1)\in B\,\right)+o\!\left(1\right).

In order to prove this result we must first prove a lemma which bounds the ratio between densities of the truncated geometric and uniform distributions.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any integers
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
, and
0<q<1
, we have

\frac{\nu_{k,q}(i)}{\nu_{k,1}(i)}\leqslant 1+9k(1-q)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{9k(1-q)}.

Proof.

For ease of notation we will sometimes use the parametrisation
q=1-x
. By the definition (
6
) of
\nu_{k,q}
, for any
i\leqslant k
we have

\nu_{k,q}(i)=\frac{(1-q)q^{i}}{1-q^{k}}=\frac{x(1-x)^{i}}{1-(1-x)^{k}}.

(23)

We need a ‘reverse Bernoulli inequality’: for any integer
r\geqslant 1
and real
y
satisfying
|y|\leqslant 1/(2r)
,

(1+y)^{r}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{r}{i}y^{i}\leqslant 1+ry+\sum_{i=2}^{r}\left(ry\right)^{i}\leqslant 1+ry+(ry)^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{r-2}2^{-i}\leqslant 1+ry+2(ry)^{2}.

(24)

We will now begin with the case
1-1/(4k)\leqslant q<1
. Applying (
24
) to (
23
), for
|x|\leqslant 1/(4k)
and
i\leqslant k
, we have

\nu_{k,q}(i)\leqslant\frac{x(1-ix+2(ix)^{2})}{1-(1-kx+2(kx)^{2})}=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac{1-ix+2(ix)^{2}}{1-2kx}=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\left(1+\frac{2kx-ix+2(ix)^{2}}{1-2kx}\right).

Recall that
\nu_{k,1}(i)=1/k
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. Thus, by the above and
|x|\leqslant 1/(4k)
, we have

\frac{\nu_{k,q}(i)}{\nu_{k,1}(i)}\leqslant 1+\frac{2kx+2(kx)\cdot(1/4)}{1-2/4}=1+5kx=1+5k(1-q).

(25)

We now deal with the remaining case
0<q<p
where
p:=1-1/(4k)
. By (
23
) we have

\frac{\nu_{k,q}(i)}{\nu_{k,p}(i)}=\frac{(1-q)q^{i}}{1-q^{k}}\cdot\frac{1-p^{k}}{(1-p)p^{i}}=\frac{1-q}{1-p}\cdot\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{i}\cdot\frac{1-p^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\leqslant\frac{1-q}{1-p}=4k(1-q).

(26)

Thus by (
25
) and (
26
), for any
0<q\leqslant 1-1/(4k)
we have

\frac{\nu_{k,q}(i)}{\nu_{k,1}(i)}=\frac{\nu_{k,q}(i)}{\nu_{k,p}(i)}\cdot\frac{\nu_{k,p}(i)}{\nu_{k,1}(i)}=4k(1-q)\cdot\left(1+5k\cdot 1/(4k)\right)=9k(1-q).

(27)

The first inequality in the statement follows as
1+9k(1-q)
is an upper bound for (
25
) and (
27
). Then the second follows since
1+y\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{y}
for all
y
. ∎

Using this lemma we can now prove the main result in this section.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

By the description of the
q
-Mallows process we see that for every integer
n\geqslant 1
,
0<q\leqslant 1
, and
A\subset S_{n}
, there exists a set
\{(x_{k}^{i})_{k=1}^{n}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}
of inputs to the
q
-Mallows process such that

\mu_{n,q}(A)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\prod_{k\in[n]}\nu_{k,q}(x_{k}^{i}).

Furthermore, since the
q
-Mallows is a deterministic function of random inputs, the set
\{(x_{k}^{i})_{k=1}^{n}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}
does not depend on
q
. Thus, by Lemma
4.4
, for any event
A\subseteq S_{n}
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{n,q}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\prod_{k\in[n]}\nu_{k,q}(x_{i}^{j})\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\prod_{k\in[n]}\mathrm{e}^{9k(1-q)}\cdot\nu_{k,1}(x_{i}^{j}).

Then, using the bound
k\leqslant n
, we can deduce Item
(i)
since

\displaystyle\mu_{n,q}(A)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{9n^{2}(1-q)}\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\prod_{k\in[n]}\nu_{k,1}(x_{i}^{j})=\mathrm{e}^{9n^{2}(1-q)}\cdot\mu_{n,1}(A).

Item
(ii)
: Recall that the tangled path

is generated deterministically from a Mallows permutation

by the construction

. Thus for any

such that

if and only if

. The result now follows from Item

.

Item

, for any

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,q)\in B\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{9n^{2}(1-q)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,1)\in B\,\right)=\mathrm{e}^{o(1)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,1)\in B\,\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{P}(n,1)\in B\,\right)+o(1),

by Item

and as
\mathrm{e}^{o(1)}=1+o(1)
by the Taylor expansion.∎

5
Cut Vertices and Diameter

The main aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
which shows that

is a sharp threshold for the existence of a cut vertex. This is achieved by equating the existence of a cut vertex to a combination of flush events, then bounding the number of cut vertices. We then use bounds on the number of cut vertices obtained while proving Theorem
1.2
to establish Theorem
1.3
, which shows that the diameter is linear when
0<q<1
is fixed.

5.1
Relating Unit Separators to Flush Events

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
we say that a vertex
v
is a cut vertex in
G
if its removal separates the graph into two or more disjoint components. The aim of this section is to prove Lemma
5.1
which shows that cut vertices are determined by flush events.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
be integers and
v_{i}\sim\nu_{i,q}
for all
i\in[n]
. Recall the flush event given by (
13
):

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\left\{\text{For each }i>k\text{ we have }v_{i}\leqslant i-k\right\}.

For an integer
k\in[n]
we define also define the
reverse flush
event by

\mathcal{R}_{k}=\left\{\text{for each }i>k\text{ we have }v_{i}>k\right\}.

The name we give to this event is quite fitting: Suppose the flush event
\mathcal{F}_{k}
holds in a permutation
\sigma
and let
\sigma^{R}
be the reverse of
\sigma
. Then, the reverse flush event
\mathcal{R}_{k}
holds for
\sigma^{R}
.

We define two events
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{R}}
which stipulate that

separates all elements
i>k
from all elements
i<k
in
\sigma
. In
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}
the elements
i>k
are to the left of
k
in
\sigma
, and in
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{R}}
they are to the right of
k
in
\sigma
(see Figure

). These events are disjoint for
n>1
, and are given by

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}_{k}\cap\{v_{k}=1\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}_{k}\cap\{v_{k}=k\}.

(28)

Our next result shows that together these two events characterise cut vertices in
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
.

.

Let

5.2
Bounds on the Probability of Flush Events

Our first result in this section gives algebraic expressions for
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
and
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{R}_{k}\,\right)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For integers
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, and
0<q<1
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q^{n-k+i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{R}_{k}\,\right)=q^{k(n-k)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right).

Proof.

Observe that for each
i>k
we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{i}\leqslant i-k\,\right)=\frac{1-q^{i-k}}{1-q^{i}}
by (
10
), thus

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)=\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{i}\leqslant i-k\,\right)=\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{i-k}}{1-q^{i}}=\left(\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}(1-q^{i})\right)^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\left(1-q^{i-k}\right),

(29)

where the first equality holds since
(v_{i})
are independent. By shifting some indices we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-q^{n-k+i})\cdot\prod_{i=k+1}^{n-k}(1-q^{i})\right)^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-k}\left(1-q^{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q^{n-k+i}},

as claimed. Now, again by (
9
), we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{i}>k\,\right)=\frac{q^{k}-q^{i}}{1-q^{i}}
for any
i>k
and thus

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{R}_{k}\,\right)=\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{i}>k\,\right)=\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{q^{k}-q^{i}}{1-q^{i}}=q^{k(n-k)}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{i-k}}{1-q^{i}}=q^{k(n-k)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right),

where the last equality follows from the second inequality of (
29
). ∎

The following technical lemma is used for obtaining tight bounds on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
0<q<1
, we have

\frac{q\log q}{6(1-q)}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log(1-q^{i})-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\log q}-\frac{3\log(1-q)}{2}\leqslant-\frac{1-q}{q\log q}.

Proof.

Recall the Euler-Maclaurin summation formula
[
25
, Section 9.5]
: for any real function
f(x)
with derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
and any integers
-\infty<a<b<\infty
we have

\sum_{a\leqslant i<b}f(i)-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}f(x)\Big|_{a}^{b}=\frac{1}{12}f^{\prime}(x)\Big|_{a}^{b}+R_{2},

(30)

where
f(x)\big|_{a}^{b}=f(b)-f(a)
and
R_{2}
is real and satisfies
|R_{2}|\leqslant\frac{1}{12}\left|f^{\prime}(x)\big|_{a}^{b}\right|
.

In our case we set
f(x)=\log(1-q^{x})
, thus
\frac{1}{2}f(x)\Big|_{a}^{b}=-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-q^{a}}{1-q^{b}}\right)
. The derivative of
f
is

f^{\prime}(x)=-q^{x}\log(q)/(1-q^{x})=\log(1/q)\left(1/(1-q^{x})-1\right)>0.

Notice that
f^{\prime}(x)
is decreasing in
x
, thus as
f^{\prime}(a)>f^{\prime}(b)
since
b>a
, we have

0\geqslant\frac{1}{12}f^{\prime}(x)\Big|_{a}^{b}+R_{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}f^{\prime}(x)\Big|_{a}^{b}=\frac{q^{a}\log q}{6(1-q^{a})}-\frac{q^{b}\log q}{6(1-q^{b})}.

So by (
30
) and setting
a=1
, for any
b\geqslant 1
we have

\displaystyle\frac{q\log q}{6(1-q)}-\frac{q^{b}\log q}{6(1-q^{b})}\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i<b}\log(1-q^{i})-\int_{1}^{b}\log(1-q^{x})\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-q}{1-q^{b}}\right)\leqslant 0.

Now if we take
b\rightarrow\infty
then the integral and sum converge since
q<1
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{q\log q}{6(1-q)}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log(1-q^{i})-\int_{1}^{\infty}\log(1-q^{x})\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\log(1-q)}{2}\leqslant 0.

(31)

Observe that the substitution
y=q^{x}
yields

\int_{1}^{\infty}\log(1-q^{x})\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\log q}\int_{0}^{q}\frac{\log(1-y)}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(32)

The right-hand side of (
32
) contains the Dilogarithm function
\operatorname{Li}_{2}(q)
given by

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(q):=\int_{0}^{q}\frac{\log(1-y)}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{j}}{j^{2}},\qquad\text{for $0\leqslant q\leqslant 1$},

(33)

where the second equality is by
[
35
, (1.3)]
, thus
\operatorname{Li}_{2}(1)=\pi^{2}/6
. Integration by parts gives

\int_{0}^{q}\frac{\log(1-y)}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y=\ln(y)\ln(1-y)\big|_{0}^{q}+\int_{0}^{q}\frac{\log(y)}{1-y}\,\mathrm{d}y=\ln(q)\ln(1-q)+\int_{1-q}^{1}\frac{\log(1-x)}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where we used the substitution
x=1-y
. By (
33
) and the above we have

\int_{0}^{q}\frac{\log(1-y)}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y=\ln(q)\ln(1-q)+\operatorname{Li}_{2}(1)-\operatorname{Li}_{2}(1-q)=\ln(q)\ln(1-q)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q)^{j}}{j^{2}}.

Note that
0\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q)^{j}}{j^{2}}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-q)^{j}=\frac{1-q}{q}
. The result then follows from (
31
).
∎

We can now apply this approximation to prove bounds on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any integers
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, and
0<q<1
, we have

\frac{q\log q}{6(1-q)}\leqslant\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\log q}-\frac{3\log(1-q)}{2}\leqslant\frac{2q^{\min\{n-k,k\}}}{(1-q)(1-q^{\min\{n-k,k\}})}-\frac{1-q}{q\log q}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q^{n-k+i}}
for any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. Thus

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\left(1-q^{i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\left(1-q^{n-k+i}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-q^{i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-q^{k+i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\left(1-q^{n-k+i}\right).

Now, since
\log(1-x)\geqslant-x/(1-x)
for all
x<1
, we have

0\leqslant-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\left(1-q^{n-k+i}\right)\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{q^{n-k+i}}{1-q^{n-k+i}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-q^{n-k+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}q^{n-k+i}\leqslant\frac{q^{n-k+1}}{(1-q)(1-q^{n-k+1})},

and similarly
0\leqslant-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-q^{k+i}\right)\leqslant\frac{q^{k+1}}{(1-q)(1-q^{k+1})}
. Thus

0\leqslant-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\left(1-q^{n-k+i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-q^{k+i}\right)\leqslant\frac{2q^{\min\{n-k,k\}}}{(1-q)(1-q^{\min\{n-k,k\}})}.

The result now follows by inserting these bounds into (
34
) and then using Lemma
5.3
to bound the remaining sum in (
34
).
∎

The following lemma gives a better bound on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
than Lemma
5.4
when
q
is close to
1
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any integers
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, and
0<q<1
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{q\left(1-q^{\min\{k,n-k\}}\right)}{2(1-q)}\right).

Proof.

By (
10
),
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{i}\leqslant i-k\,\right)=1-\frac{q^{i-k}-q^{i}}{1-q^{i}}
. Independence of the random variables
v_{i}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{i}\leqslant i-k\,\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-k}\left(1-\frac{q^{i}-q^{k+i}}{1-q^{k+i}}\right)\leqslant\prod_{i=1}^{n-k}\exp\left(-\frac{q^{i}-q^{k+i}}{1-q^{k+i}}\right),

where the last inequality follows since
1+x\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{x}
for all real
x
. This gives the following, where in the last step we apply the expression for the sum of a geometric series:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\frac{q^{i}-q^{k+i}}{1-q^{k+i}}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}q^{i}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{q(1-q^{k})(1-q^{n-k})}{(1-q)(1-q^{n})}\right).

Observe that
1-q^{n}=(1+q^{n/2})(1-q^{n/2})
and
\max\{k,n-k\}\geqslant n/2
, thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{q}{1-q}\cdot\frac{(1-q^{k})(1-q^{n-k})}{(1+q^{n/2})(1-q^{n/2})}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{q}{1-q}\cdot\frac{1-q^{\min\{n-k,k\}}}{1+q^{n/2}}\right),

and finally
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{q(1-q^{\min\{n-k,k\}})}{2(1-q)}\right)
since
1+q^{n/2}\leqslant 2
.
∎

5.3
Bounds on the Number of Cuts

For
1/2<\alpha<1
we let the random variable
X_{n}(\alpha)
denote the number of vertices
k\in[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]
which are cut vertices in
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
. We have the following by Lemma
5.1
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer,
1/2<\alpha<1
, and
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1
. Then

X_{n}(\alpha)=\sum_{k=\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil}^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}}+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{R}}}\right).

Restricting the ranges of
q
and
k
in Lemma
5.4
gives the following result.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
1/2<\alpha<1
,
n\geqslant(\frac{100}{1-\alpha})^{5}
be an integer,
and
0<q\leqslant 1-n^{4/5}
. Then, for any integer
k\in[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]
, we have

\mathrm{e}^{-1/6}\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{6}\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}\right).

Proof.

Recall
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}=1\,\right)=\frac{1-q}{1-q^{k}}\geqslant 1-q
from (
6
). Since
k\in\big[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\big]
, we have

q^{k}\leqslant q^{\min\{k,n-k\}}\leqslant(1-1/n^{4/5})^{(1-\alpha)n}\leqslant(\mathrm{e}^{-1/n^{4/5}})^{(1-\alpha)n}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\alpha)n^{1/5}}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-100},

(35)

where the last step uses
n\geqslant(\frac{100}{1-\alpha})^{5}
. By independence,
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}=1\,\right)
and so

(1-q)\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)=\frac{1-q}{1-q^{k}}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}\cdot(1-q)\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right).

(36)

By (
36
) it suffices to bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
. Taking
x=q-1>-1
, the bounds on
\log
from (
2
) give

\frac{1}{\log q}\geqslant-\frac{1}{1-q},\quad\log q\geqslant-\frac{1-q}{q},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\log q}\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{1-q}.

(37)

Thus, applying (
37
) to the bound on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
in Lemma
5.4
gives

\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\geqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\log q}+\frac{3\log(1-q)}{2}+\frac{q\log q}{6(1-q)}\geqslant-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}+\frac{3\log(1-q)}{2}-\frac{1}{6}.

(38)

Inserting (
38
) into (
36
) gives the lower bound in the statement.

For the upper bound applying (
37
) and (
35
) to Lemma
5.4
gives

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}-\frac{3\log(1-q)}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+\frac{2q^{\min\{k,n-k\}}}{(1-q)(1-q^{\min\{k,n-k\}})}-\frac{1-q}{q\log q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+3n^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-n^{1/5}/4}+\frac{1}{q}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2+\frac{1}{q}.

(39)

Let
f(q)=\mathrm{e}^{3+1/q}\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}
and observe that applying (
39
) to (
36
) gives the bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant f(q)
. Now, as
f(q)
is monotone decreasing in
q
, for any
q\leqslant 1/3
we have
f(q)\geqslant f(1/3)=\mathrm{e}^{6}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}}(2/3)^{5/2}>1
. Thus since
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\max\{f(q),1\}
holds and
\mathrm{e}^{3+1/q}
is monotone decreasing in
q
we can simplify the bound to
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{6}\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}
, as claimed. ∎

The next lemma shows
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{R}}
is ‘rarer’ than
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}
, except if
q=1
where they are equiprobable.

Lemma 5.8
.

For integers
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, and
0<q<1
, we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{R}}\,\right)=q^{k(n-k+1)-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right).

Proof.

Recall that
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}=j\,\right)=\frac{(1-q)q^{j-1}}{1-q^{k}}
by (
6
), and so
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}=k\,\right)=q^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}=1\,\right)
. The result follows from independence and since
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{R}_{k}\,\right)=q^{k(n-k)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
by Lemma
5.2
.
∎

Lemma
5.9
below shows that for a certain range of
q
there are many cut vertices. This proves one side of Theorem
1.2
and will also be key to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
1/2<\alpha<1
,
n\geqslant\big(\frac{100}{\min\{1-\alpha,\;2\alpha-1\}}\big)^{5}
be an integer, and
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1-n^{-4/5}
. Then, for any
x>0
,

\quad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,X_{n}(\alpha)<\frac{2\alpha-1}{10}\cdot n(1-q)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}\left(1-\frac{1000x}{\sqrt{2\alpha-1}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}\log n}{n(1-q)^{7/2}}}\right)\,\right)\leqslant\frac{5}{x^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{7}}.

Proof.

To begin, by Corollary
5.6
and Lemma
5.7
,

\sum_{k=\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil}^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\geqslant((2\alpha-1)n-2)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-1/6}(1-q)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}.

Since
n
satisfies
n\geqslant\big(\frac{100}{\min\{1-\alpha,\;2\alpha-1\}}\big)^{5}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]\geqslant\frac{2\alpha-1}{10}\cdot n\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}.

(40)

We want to show that
X_{n}(\alpha)
concentrates around
\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]
, but we cannot apply Lemma
3.3
directly as
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}
is not
\ell
-local for any reasonable
\ell
. Recalling
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\left\{v_{i}\leqslant i-k\text{ for all }k<i\leqslant k+b_{n}\right\}
from (
16
), where
b_{n}=\left\lceil\frac{8\log n}{\log(1/q)}\right\rceil
by (
14
), define the event
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}_{k}\cap\{v_{k}=1\}
, and let

Y_{n}(\alpha)=\sum_{k=\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil}^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}}.

We note two facts that follow from the first part of the statement in Lemma
3.4
, the bound
\log(1/q)\geqslant 1-q
, and the assumption
n\geqslant\big(\frac{100}{\min\{1-\alpha,\;2\alpha-1\}}\big)^{5}
. Firstly,
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}
is
\ell
-local to
k+\lceil\frac{b}{2}\rceil
, where
\ell=\lceil\frac{1+b_{n}}{2}\rceil\leqslant\frac{5\log n}{1-q}
. Secondly,
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}\,\right)\leqslant(1+n^{-7})\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)
. It follows that
Y(\alpha)
is the ‘localisation’ of
X(\alpha)
according to Lemma
3.4
, where
c_{i}=1
for all
i\in[n]
, also

\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]=\sum_{k=\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil}^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\,Y_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]\leqslant(1+n^{-7})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right].

(41)

Recall
S
and
M
from Lemma
3.3
. In this setting
S=\big[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\big]
and, by Lemma
5.7
,

M=\max_{k\in S}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{L}}\,\right)\leqslant(1+n^{-7})\cdot\max_{k\in S}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant 2\cdot\mathrm{e}^{6}(1-q)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}.

Since
10^{2}\mathrm{e}^{6}\left(1-\frac{2}{(2\alpha-1)n}\right)^{-1}\leqslant 250^{2}
, we have the following by comparison with (
40
):

M\cdot|S|\cdot\ell\leqslant 2\mathrm{e}^{6}(1-q)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}\cdot\left(2\alpha-1\right)n\cdot\frac{5\log n}{1-q}\leqslant\frac{250^{2}\log n}{1-q}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right].

(42)

Finally, for any
x>0
, by Lemma
3.3
, (
41
), and (
42
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,Y_{n}(\alpha)<\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{(250x)^{2}\log n}{1-q}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]}\,\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,Y_{n}(\alpha)<\mathbb{E}\left[\,Y_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]-x\cdot\sqrt{M\cdot|S|\cdot\ell}\,\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 5/x^{2}.

(43)

Now, by (
40
), we have

\frac{(250x)^{2}\cdot\log n}{\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]\cdot(1-q)}\leqslant\frac{(250x)^{2}\cdot\log n}{\frac{2\alpha-1}{10}\cdot n\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}\cdot(1-q)}\leqslant\frac{(1000x)^{2}}{2\alpha-1}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}}\log n}{n\cdot(1-q)^{7/2}}

The result follows by (
5.3
) as
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,Y_{n}(\alpha)\neq X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right)\leqslant n^{-7}
by Lemma
3.4
. ∎

5.4
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We now use our bounds on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)
and
X_{n}(\alpha)
to establish Theorem
1.2
, which shows that
q=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\log n}
is a sharp threshold for having a vertex cut separating the graph into two macroscopic pieces.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

As we are proving a statement concerning a limit in
n
we can assume
n\geqslant\big(\frac{100}{\min\{1-\alpha,\;2\alpha-1\}}\big)^{5}
. We break the proof into three cases depending on the value of
q
; in the first two cover the
0
-statement, and the last case deals with the
1
-statement.

Recall that
X_{n}(\alpha)
is the number of cut vertices in
[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]
and thus
\{X_{n}(\alpha)\geqslant 1\}=\{\exists\text{ an }(1,\alpha)\text{-separator}\}
. By Markov’s inequality, Corollary
5.6
, and Lemma
5.8
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,X_{n}(\alpha)\geqslant 1\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\,X_{n}(\alpha)\,\right]\leqslant 2n\cdot\max_{k\in[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant 2n\cdot\max_{k\in[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right).

To prove the
0
-statement it suffices to show
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)
or
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)
is
o\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
when
k\in[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]
.

Case (i)

\left[(1-q)^{-1}\geqslant n^{4/5}\right]
:
If
q=1
then the result follows from Lemma
4.5
, so we can assume
q\neq 1
. In this case we have
q\geqslant 1-n^{-4/5}
and
\min\{k,n-k\}-1\geqslant 5\sqrt{n}
, so

\frac{1-q^{\min\{k,n-k\}}}{1-q}=\frac{(1-q)(1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{\min\{k,n-k\}-1})}{1-q}\geqslant\sum_{i=0}^{5\sqrt{n}}(1-n^{-4/5})^{i}.

(44)

Now, by Bernoulli’s inequality, we have

\frac{1-q^{\min\{k,n-k\}}}{1-q}\geqslant\sum_{i=0}^{5\sqrt{n}}(1-i\cdot n^{-4/5})\geqslant 4\sqrt{n}.

(45)

Using this bound in combination with Lemma
5.5
and the fact
q\geqslant 1-n^{-4/5}\geqslant 1/2
yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\,\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{q}{2}\cdot\frac{\left(1-q^{\min\{k,n-k\}}\right)}{1-q}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\cdot 4\sqrt{n}\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{n}}.

Case (ii)

[(1-q)^{-1}<n^{4/5},\text{ and }\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}-\log n+\frac{5\log\log n}{2}\rightarrow\infty]
:
By Lemma
5.7
we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{6}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}\right)\cdot(1-q)^{5/2}=\exp\left(6-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}+\frac{5\log(1-q)}{2}\right),

(46)

for
k\in[\lceil(1-\alpha)n\rceil,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]
as
n\geqslant\big(\frac{100}{\min\{1-\alpha,\;2\alpha-1\}}\big)^{5}
. Differentiating the exponent of (
46
) gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\,q}\left(6-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}+\frac{5\log(1-q)}{2}\right)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)^{2}}-\frac{5}{2(1-q)}=\frac{15q-\pi^{2}-15}{6(1-q)^{2}}.

(47)

From (
47
) we see that the exponent of the bound on
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)
from (
46
) is monotone decreasing in
q
provided
q\in[0,1)
. Hence to bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)
it suffices to evaluate (
46
) for the smallest
q
in the scope of this case. Thus, if we let
w(n)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}-\log n+\frac{5\log\log n}{2}
then it suffices to bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{E}_{k}\,\right)
in the case
w(n)\rightarrow\infty
arbitrary slowly and we will assume, for convenience, that
w(n)\leqslant\sqrt{\log\log n}
. As
q\geqslant 1-2/\log n
we have
\log(1-q)\leqslant 2-\log\log n
. Thus by (
46
)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}\,\right)\leqslant\exp\left(6-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}+\frac{5\log(1-q)}{2}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(7-\log n-w(n)\right)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{7}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-w(n)}}{n}=o\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Case (iii)

\left[q\geqslant 0,\text{ and }\frac{\pi^{2}}{6(1-q)}-\log n+\frac{9\log\log n}{2}\rightarrow-\infty\right]
:
In this final case we will show that
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,X_{n}(\alpha)\geqslant 1\,\right)=1-o\!\left(1\right)
by applying Lemma
5.9
which bounds
X_{n}(\alpha)
from below.
If we let

Remark 5.10
.

The events

Proof of Theorem

We now prove Theorem

, which shows the diameter is linear when

is bounded away from
1
. Theorem
1.3
follows from the next lemma and an earlier bound on the number of cut vertices.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph containing a Hamiltonian path. Then if there exists a set
C\subseteq V
such that each vertex in
C
is a cut vertex of

, then
\mathsf{diam}(G)\geqslant|C|+1
.

Proof.

Label the vertices from
1
to
n
along the Hamiltonian path
P
. Denote the cut vertices by

, where

, ordered with respect to the vertex labelling of

.

Claim
.

Any path from vertex

to vertex

must include every vertex of
C
.

Proof of Claim.

Since the graph contains an
n
-vertex path as a subgraph (i.e. a Hamiltonian path), we must have that for

the removal of the cut vertex
c_{i}
separates the graph into exactly two connected graphs, one containing the subpath
[1,\dots,c_{i}-1]
and the other containing the subpath

to

in

. Since the path does not go through

and

however this contradicts our earlier observation about the cut vertex
c_{i}
.
∎

By the claim, the shortest path connecting

to

has length at least

. This follows as

and
n
cannot be in

, since removing them does not disconnect the graph due to the existence of the path

on

as a subgraph. ∎

We note that one cannot completely remove the assumption of a subpath of length

Proof of Theorem

Let

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem

6.1
Lower Bound on Treewidth

The main results in this section are Lemmas

and

, which give two separate bounds on treewidth. The first bound is tight, but it only holds for small values of
q
. Whereas the second holds for any

, however it is only known to be tight up to a

factor. Both bounds follow from finding a minor with good expansion properties, however the probability of finding such a minor is calculated slightly differently in the two different cases.

Before we begin in earnest, we must introduce consecutive patterns in permutations. Given a list of
k
distinct integers
w=w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
, the

of
w
, written
\mathsf{st}(w)
, is the unique permutation of
[k]
that is order-isomorphic to
w
. That is, we obtain
\mathsf{st}(w)
by replacing the smallest element among
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
with
1
, the second smallest with
2
, and so on. We say that
\pi\in S_{n}

contains
\sigma\in S_{k}
consecutively
if there exists an index
i\in[n-k+1]
such that
\mathsf{st}\!\left(\pi(i),\dots,\pi(i+k-1)\right)=\sigma
; otherwise, we say that
\pi

avoids
\sigma
consecutively
.

We now give a lemma relating consecutive patterns in permutations to minors in the related tangled paths. Figure
5
illustrates this Lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
be integers and let
\pi\in S_{n}
contain
\sigma\in S_{k}
consecutively. Then,
\layer(\pi(P_{n}),P_{n})
contains
\layer(\sigma(P_{k}),P_{k})
as a minor.

Proof.

Let
\pi(i),\dots,\pi(i+k-1)
be such that
\mathsf{st}\!\left(\pi(i),\dots,\pi(i+k-1)\right)=\sigma
and
s_{1}<\cdots<s_{k}
be the elements of
\{\pi(i),\dots,\pi(i+k-1)\}
ordered increasingly. Define
P^{\prime}
to be the path of length
s_{k}-s_{1}+1
on
\{s_{1},s_{1}+1,\dots,s_{k}\}
. Finally, let the graph
H
be the union of
P^{\prime}
and the set of edges
\{\pi(i+j)\pi(i+j+1)\}_{j=0}^{k-2}
. Observe that
H
is isomorphic to a copy of
\layer(\sigma(P_{k}),P_{k})
where the edges of
P_{k}
have been subdivided. Now, as

the result follows.
∎

The second follows from translation invariance of Mallows permutations

Lemma 6.3

(
[

)
.

Let
(i,i+1,\ldots,i+k-1)\subseteq[n]
be a sequence of consecutive elements. If

then

\mathsf{st}\left(\pi(i),\ldots,\pi(i+k-1)\right)\sim\mu_{k,q}

Given

\mu_{n,q}(S)=\sum_{\sigma\in S}\mu_{n,q}(\sigma)
for any

.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
be integers, and
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1
. Then, for any

we have

\mu_{n,q}\left(\mathsf{Forb}_{n}(S)\right)\leqslant\mu_{k,q}\left(\mathsf{Forb}_{k}(S)\right)^{\lfloor n/k\rfloor}.

Proof.

To begin, observe that if a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}

\mathsf{st}\!\left(\pi(ik+1),\dots,\pi(ik+k)\right)\notin S

and by Lemma
6.3

\mathsf{st}\!\left(\pi(ik+1),\dots,\pi(ik+k)\right)
has distribution

. Thus, for any

\mu_{n,q}\left(\mathsf{Forb}_{n}(S)\right)\leqslant\prod_{i=0}^{\lfloor n/k\rfloor-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{st}\!\left(\pi(ik+1),\dots,\pi(ik+k)\right)\notin S\,\right)=\mu_{k,q}\left(\mathsf{Forb}_{k}(S)\right)^{\lfloor n/k\rfloor},

as claimed.

Lemma 6.5

6.4

6.2
Upper Bound on Cutwidth

Recall the definition of the cutwidth of a graph

given by (

\mathsf{cw}(G)=\min_{f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z},\text{ injective}}\;\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\;\left|\left\{ij\in E(G):f(i)\leqslant x<f(j)\right\}\right|.

(60)

In this section we prove Lemma
6.7
, which gives the upper bounds on cutwidth in Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer,
c>0
be any constant, and
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1-c(\log\log n)^{2}/\log n
. Then there exists a constant
C
such that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))>C\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(1/q)}}+1\right)\,\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

Additionally, if
q\geqslant 1-(\log\log n)^{2}/\log n
, then

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))>\frac{5}{1-q}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\right)\,\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

We will begin by outlining a sketch of the proof of Lemma
6.7
.

Proof Sketch:
For an upper bound on
\mathsf{cw}\left(\mathcal{P}(n,q)\right)
we fix
f:[n]\rightarrow[n]
in (
60
) to be the identity map denoted
\ID
. That is, we order the vertices of
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
using the ordering of the un-permuted path
P_{n}
with edges
(i)(i+1)
for
i\in[n-1]
. We bound the number of edges crossing any vertex
i
by bounding the number of elements with values
j>i
are inserted next to elements
k<i
by the
q
-Mallows process. To do this we show that, for
b_{n}=\lceil\frac{8\log n}{\log(1/q)}\rceil
given by (
14
) and some suitable
\ell,L
where
L\geqslant\ell
, the following events hold with high probability:

(i)

no insert position
v_{i}
has value greater than
b_{n}
,

(ii)

after
L
steps the leftmost
b_{n}
places contain only elements added at most
L
steps ago,

(iii)

within any window of
L
steps there are at most
\ell
values of
v_{i}
greater than
\ell
.

The events
(i)
and
(iii)
control the number of long edges created from new entries being added far away from the left-hand end of the process. The event
(ii)
is more subtle, it ensures that the left-hand end of the permutation grown by the
q
-Mallows process cannot retain entries that were inserted long ago, again preventing long edges caused by new elements lying next to old ones. We show that if
(i)
-
(iii)
hold, then the number of edges crossing any vertex is
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
.

Having concluded the proof sketch we now formalise event described in
(ii)
. To do this we introduce the sparse flush event, which is a relaxation of the local flush event
\mathcal{L}_{k}
given by (
16
). For positive integers
n,k,b
where
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
and
b\leqslant n-k
and real
L\leqslant n-k
we say there is a
sparse flush

\mathcal{S}(k,b,L)
of
b
items at step
k
with length
L
if the following event holds:

\mathcal{S}(k,b,L)=\left\{\text{there exist $k\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{b}\leqslant k+L$ such that $v_{t_{i}}\leqslant i$ for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant b$}\right\}.

(61)

To give an intuition on the sparse flush we must first recall the local flush event (
16
) given by
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\left\{\text{for each }k<i\leqslant k+b_{n}(q)\text{ we have }v_{i}\leqslant i-k\right\}
, where
b_{n}=\left\lceil 8\log(n)/\log(1/q)\right\rceil
. Recall also that no insert position is greater than
b_{n}
w.h.p. by (
15
). Thus, if the local flush event
\mathcal{L}_{k}
holds for some vertex
k
then w.h.p., no element
j\geqslant k+b_{n}
is inserted next to any element
i\leqslant k
. If we choose
b_{n}=\left\lceil 8\log(n)/\log(1/q)\right\rceil
in (
61
) then, conditional on
\mathcal{S}(k,b_{n},L)
, the values
(v_{t_{i}})_{i=1}^{b_{n}}
form a (non-consecutive) local-flush, that is
v_{t_{1}}\leqslant 1,v_{t_{2}}\leqslant 2,\dots,v_{t_{b_{n}}}\leqslant b_{n}
. This sequence of insert positions ensures that w.h.p. no element
j\geqslant k+L
is inserted next to any element
i\leqslant k
, and thus no such edge
ij
is present in the permuted path. If
q
is very close to
1
, the local flush event only occurs with small probability; the next two lemmas show that if we take
L
significantly larger than
b_{n}
, then the sparse flush holds w.h.p., even for
q
tending to
1
quite fast.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
n,b\geqslant 1
be integers,
0<q<1
, and
\lambda\geqslant 1
. Let
L=\lambda\left(b+\frac{q}{1-q}+\frac{1}{\log q}\log\frac{1-q}{1-q^{b}}\right)
and
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-L
be an integer. Then,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{S}(k,b,L)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{(1-q)q^{b}}{1-q^{b}}\right)^{\lambda-1-\log\lambda}.

Proof.

Recall that each element
x
in the
q
-Mallows process
(r_{n})
is inserted at step
x
and relative position
v_{x}\sim\nu_{x,q}
. The sparse flush
\mathcal{S}(k,b,L)
consists of a sequence of
b
steps
(t_{i})_{i=1}^{b}
where element
t_{i}
is inserted at relative position at most
i
. We call these steps
good
. Conditional on
t_{i-1}
, the
(i-1)
th
good step, we define the random variable
\tau_{i}=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
to be the additional number of steps we must wait for the
i
th
good step (where
1\leqslant i\leqslant b
and
t_{0}=k
).

Let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\tau_{i}
be the total number of steps we have to wait to have
b
good steps and observe that
\mathcal{S}(k,b,L)=\{T\leqslant L\}
. We now aim to bound
T
. Since
(v_{x})_{x\geqslant 0}
are independent and
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}\leqslant i\,\right)\geqslant 1-q^{i}
by (
10
), it follows that
\tau_{i}\preceq X_{i}
, for independent
X_{i}\sim\operatorname{Geo}\!\left(p_{i}\right)
, where
p_{i}=1-q^{i}
. That is, the time between the
i-1
th
and
i
th
good step is stochastically dominated by a geometric random variable with success probability
1-q^{i}
. We now set

X=\sum_{i=1}^{b}X_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{p_{i}}.

Observe that
T\preceq X
and so
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{S}(k,b,L)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,X>L\,\right)
. We bound the latter probability using Lemma
2.2
. To do this we need bounds on
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]
. For the upper bound:

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{1-q^{i}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-q}+\int_{1}^{b}\frac{1}{1-q^{x}}\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{1-q}+\int_{1}^{b}\left(1+\frac{q^{x}}{1-q^{x}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}x,

since
1/(1-q^{x})=1+q^{x}/(1-q^{x})
is decreasing in
x
. Thus, by integrating we have

\mu\leqslant\frac{1}{1-q}+b-1-\left[\frac{\log\left(1-q^{x}\right)}{\log q}\right]_{x=1}^{b}=b+\frac{q}{1-q}+\log\left(\frac{1-q}{1-q^{b}}\right)/\log(q).

(62)

Similarly, we can obtain the following lower bound:

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{1-q^{i}}\geqslant\int_{1}^{b}\frac{1}{1-q^{x}}\;\mathrm{d}x\geqslant b+\log\left(\frac{1-q}{1-q^{b}}\right)/\log(q).

(63)

We apply Lemma
2.2
to
X
where
p_{*}=\min_{i}p_{i}\geqslant 1-q
,
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]
and
\lambda>1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{S}(k,b,\lambda\mu)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,X\geqslant\lambda\mu\,\right)\leqslant\lambda^{-1}(1-p_{*})^{\mu\left(\lambda-1-\log\lambda\right)}\leqslant q^{\mu\left(\lambda-1-\log\lambda\right).}

We now insert the lower bound on
\mu
from (
63
) into the bound in the line above to give

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{S}(k,b,\lambda\mu)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant q^{\left(b+\log\left(\frac{1-q}{1-q^{b}}\right)/\log(q)\right)\cdot\left(\lambda-1-\log\lambda\right)}=\left(\frac{(1-q)q^{b}}{1-q^{b}}\right)^{\lambda-1-\log\lambda}.

To conclude we observe that
L\geqslant\lambda\mu
by (
62
). ∎

We now plug some specific values into Lemma
6.8
for use in the proof of Lemma
6.7
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer,
0<q<1
, and
b_{n}=\left\lceil\frac{8\log n}{\log(1/q)}\right\rceil\geqslant 1
. Then for any
L\geqslant\frac{100}{1-q}\left(\frac{1}{1-q}+\log n\right)
and integer
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-L
we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{S}(k,b_{n},L)^{c}\,\right)=o\!\left(n^{-10}\right).

Proof.

Observe that
q^{b_{n}}\leqslant n^{-8}
and
\mathcal{S}(k,b_{n},L)\supseteq\mathcal{S}(k,b_{n},D)
for any
L\geqslant D
. Now let

D=10\left(b_{n}+\frac{q}{1-q}+\log\left(\frac{1-q}{1-q^{b_{n}}}\right)/\log(q)\right).

Applying the inequalities
-1/\log q\leqslant 1/(1-q)
and
-\log(1-q)\leqslant 1/(1-q)
yields

D\leqslant 10\left(-\frac{8\log n}{\log q}+1+\frac{q}{1-q}-\frac{\log\left(1-q\right)}{-\log(q)}\right)\leqslant\frac{100}{1-q}\left(\frac{1}{1-q}+\log n\right).

Finally,
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{S}(k,b_{n},D)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{(1-q)q^{b}_{n}}{1-q^{b_{n}+1}}\right)^{10-1-\log 10}\leqslant\left(\frac{n^{-8}}{1-n^{-8}}\right)^{6}=o\!\left(n^{-10}\right)
by Lemma
6.8
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma
6.7
, which provides upper bounds on the cutwidth.

Proof of Lemma
6.7
.

As mentioned at the start of this section we bound the cutwidth from above by fixing the injection
f:[n]\rightarrow[n]
in (
60
) to be the identity
\ID:[n]\rightarrow[n]
.

Before beginning in earnest, we treat the case
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1/n^{2}
as this is a straightforward deduction from an earlier result. Indeed, if
q<1/n^{2}
then by Theorem
3.2
we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P})\geqslant 2\,\right)\leqslant\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,|\sigma(i)-i|\geqslant 2\,\right)\leqslant n\cdot 2q^{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(n^{-3}\right).

(64)

Thus, from now on we can assume
q\geqslant 1/n^{2}
. The remainder of the proof is as follows. First, we introduce the event
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
, which essentially controls how many long edges originate from any small set of consecutive vertices. We then prove a claim bounding the cutwidth conditional on this event. To apply the claim we then bound the probability that the event fails, breaking into two cases for different values of
q
. Finally, we conclude by relating the different cases to the lemma’s statement. We now begin with the formal definition of the event
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
.

Let
b_{n}=\left\lceil 8\log(n)/\log(1/q)\right\rceil
and define the event
\mathcal{E}=\bigcap_{k\in[n]}\{v_{k}\leqslant b_{n}\}
. For integers
i\in[n]
and
L\geqslant\ell\geqslant 1
, we recall the sparse flush event
\mathcal{S}(i,b_{n},L)
from (
61
), and also define the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(i,\ell,L)

\displaystyle=\left\{\text{there are at most $\ell$ values $k\in[i,\max\{i+L,n\}]$ such that $\nu_{k}>\ell$}\right\}.

The aforementioned event
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
is an intersection of these previous three events

\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)=\mathcal{E}\cap\left(\bigcap_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n-L}\mathcal{S}(i,b_{n},L)\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{B}(i,\ell,L)\right).

The first two intersected events in
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
imply that for every element
i
inserted by the
q
-Mallows process, at most the
L
elements following it can potentially create an edge that crosses the vertex
i\in[n]
. The final event in the intersection states that of these
L
elements, all but at most
\ell
will be inserted in the
\ell
closest positions to the left-hand end of the permutation at the time of insertion. The next claim shows that if this event holds then the cutwidth is bounded.

Claim
.

For any integers
L\geqslant\ell\geqslant 1
we have
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)\subseteq\{\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant 4(\ell+1)\}.

Proof of Claim.

Recall that we fixed the injection
f
in the definition of the cutwidth (
60
) to be
\ID
. Under
f
the edges of the un-permuted path
P_{n}\subseteq\layer(\sigma(P_{n}),P_{n})
contribute at most
1
to the cutwidth. We now bound the contribution by the edges of the permuted path
\sigma(P_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(n,q)
.

Given vertices
i,j,k\in[n]
we say that the edge
jk\in E(\sigma(P_{n}))
is
bad
(with respect to
i
) if
j<i
and
k>i
. We claim that if
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
holds then, for any
i\in[n]
, there are at most
4\ell
edges which are bad for
i
. We show this by keeping track of where elements are inserted during the
q
-Mallows process
(r_{a})_{a=1}^{n}
. By definition if an edge is bad for
i
, then exactly one of its endpoints must have value greater than
i
. If
i=n
then there are no bad edges for
i
, so we assume that
i<n
. Observe for any element
k\in\{i+1,\dots,n\}
there are at most
2
bad edges with endpoint
k
, since
k
can be adjacent to at most two elements
j<i
in the final permutation
r_{n}
. We now partition the elements
k\in\{i+1,\dots,n\}
into three disjoint sets
A_{i}
,
B_{i}
and
C_{i}
based on their value
k
and
v_{k}
, the insert position of the element
k
relative to the left-hand end of
r_{k-1}
:

A_{i}=\{k\in\{i+1,\dots,i+L\}:v_{k}>\ell\},\quad B_{i}=\{i+1,\dots,i+L\}\backslash A_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad C_{i}=\{i+L+1,\dots,n\}.

See Figure
6
for an example. We will count the contribution of each set to the bad edges.

Contribution from
(A_{i})
: Conditional on
\mathcal{B}(i,\ell,L)
, we have
|A_{i}|\leqslant\ell
. Thus, in total the elements in
A_{i}
contribute at most
2\ell
bad edges.

Contribution from
(B_{i})
: The left-most
\ell
positions of
r_{i}
each have value at most
i
. By the definition of the
q
-Mallows process (
7
), no additional element with value at most
i
can ever occupy any of the
\ell
left-most positions. Since each element
k\in B_{i}
is inserted within the
\ell
left-most places, there can only be
2\ell
total bad edges for
i
with an endpoint
k\in B_{i}
.

Contribution from
(C_{i})
: Observe that if
i\geqslant n-L
then
C_{i}
is empty. Otherwise, as
\mathcal{S}(i,b_{n},L)
holds, we have
r_{k}(j)>i
for all
k>i+L
and
j\leqslant b_{n}
. That is, after step
i+L
of the
q
-Mallows process the permutation will only have elements with value at least
i
in its
b_{n}
left-most positions. However, conditional on
\mathcal{E}
, no element is inserted at position greater than
b_{n}
relative to the left-hand end of the permutation. Thus, the elements
k>i+L
create no bad edges.

Collecting these contributions, if we condition on
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
then there are in total at most
4\ell
bad edges for any
i\in[n]
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
there exists an
i
, such that all edges crossing
x
are either bad for
i
or have
i
as an endpoint. The result follows as there are at most
4
edges with endpoint
i
, since
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
has maximum degree
4
.
∎

6.7
. Observe the ‘random’ sequence
\mathbf{x}=(1,1,2,4,2,1,3,1,5,1,2,3,1,2,1)

,

. The permutation generated by the

. We let

be the location of the cut (as this maximises the cut) and observe that there are

. We also have the sets
A_{3}=\{4,9\}
,

whose vertices are represented as squares, diamonds and hexagons respectively.

We now bound the failure probability of each of the constituent events in
\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)
. To begin, we set

L=\frac{100}{1-q}\left(\frac{1}{1-q}+\log n\right).

Recall that
q\geqslant 1/n^{2}
and so
b_{n}=\left\lceil 8\log(n)/\log(1/q)\right\rceil\geqslant\left\lceil 8\log(n)/(2\log n)\right\rceil\geqslant 1
. Thus, by (
15
), and Lemma
6.9
(since
b_{n}\geqslant 1
) plus the union bound, respectively, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{E}^{c}\,\right)\leqslant n^{-7}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\cup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n-L}\left(\mathcal{S}_{i,b_{n},L}\right)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant n\cdot o\!\left(n^{-10}\right)=o\!\left(n^{-9}\right).

(65)

We bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{B}(i,\ell,L)^{c}\,\right)
by showing the number of large inputs to the
q
-Mallows process is stochastically dominated by a sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables. Observe that
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}>\ell\,\right)\leqslant q^{\ell}
for any integers
\ell\leqslant k
by (
9
), and
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,v_{k}>\ell\,\right)=0
if
k<\ell
. Let
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in[L]}
be a set of independent Bernoulli random variables with success probability
q^{\ell}
. Then, since each
v_{k}
is independent, for any integers
\ell,L\geqslant 1
and
i\in[n]
:

\left|\{k\in[i,i+L]:v_{k}>\ell\}\right|\preceq\sum_{j=1}^{L}X_{j}.

If
\ell>Lq^{\ell}
and
\ell>\mathrm{e}
, then taking
\delta=\ell/(Lq^{\ell})-1>0
in Lemma
2.3
we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\sum_{j=1}^{L}X_{j}>\ell\,\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{Lq^{\ell}}\leqslant\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ell}{Lq^{\ell}}-1}}{(\frac{\ell}{Lq^{\ell}})^{\frac{\ell}{Lq^{\ell}}}}\right)^{Lq^{\ell}}\leqslant\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}Lq^{\ell}}{\ell}\right)^{\ell}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{\log(Lq^{\ell})\cdot\ell}.

(66)

We now break into two cases making different choices for
\ell
depending on the value of
q
. These cases roughly correspond to the two bounds in the lemma. First, note that by (
2
) we have

1-q\leqslant\log(1/q)\leqslant(1-q)/q.

(67)

Case (i)

\left[1/n^{2}\leqslant q\leqslant 1-\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\right]
:
Set
\ell=10\cdot\sqrt{\log(n)/\log(1/q)}
, and so by (
67
) we have

\ell=10\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(1/q)}}\leqslant 10\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{1-q}}\leqslant 10\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log^{2}n}{(\log\log n)^{2}}}=\frac{10\log n}{\log\log n}.

(68)

The lower bound on
q
in this case ensures that
\ell\geqslant 10\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(n^{2})}}>\mathrm{e}
, and (
67
) gives

L\cdot q^{\ell}=L\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-10\sqrt{\log(1/q)\log n}}\leqslant L\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-10\sqrt{(1-q)\log n}}\leqslant L\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-10\sqrt{\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\cdot\log n}}\leqslant(\log n)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{(\log n)^{10}},

for large
n
. Thus, for large
n
,
Lq^{\ell}<\ell
and so (
66
) and the bound on
\ell
from (
68
) give

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\sum_{j=1}^{L}X_{j}>\ell\,\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}Lq^{\ell}}{\ell}\right)^{\ell}\leqslant L^{\ell}\cdot q^{\ell^{2}}\leqslant\left(\log^{2}n\right)^{\frac{10\log n}{\log\log n}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-100\log n}\leqslant n^{-80}.

\lozenge

Case (ii)

\left[q\geqslant 1-\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\right]
:
Set
\ell=5\log(1-q)/\log q=5\log\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\right)/\log(1/q)
. Now,

L\cdot q^{\ell}=\frac{100}{1-q}\left(\frac{1}{1-q}+\log n\right)\cdot\left(1-q\right)^{5}\leqslant 100\left(\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\right)^{3}+100(\log n)\left(\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\right)^{4}<\frac{1}{\log n},

and thus
\log(Lq^{\ell})<-\log\log n
. Also by (
67
) we have

\ell=\frac{5\log\frac{1}{1-q}}{\log(1/q)}\geqslant\frac{q\cdot 5\log\frac{1}{1-q}}{1-q}\geqslant\frac{5q}{1-q}\cdot\log\frac{\log n}{(\log\log n)^{2}}.

Hence, for suitably large
n
, we have

\ell\geqslant 5\left(1-\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log n}\right)\frac{\log n}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\cdot\left(\log\log n-2\log\log\log n\right)\geqslant\frac{4\log n}{\log\log n}>\mathrm{e}.

To conclude this case, by (
66
) and the above, for suitably large
n
we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\sum_{j=1}^{L}X_{j}>\ell\,\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{\log(Lq^{\ell})\cdot\ell}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\log(\log n)\cdot\frac{4\log n}{\log\log n}}=n^{-4}.

\lozenge

It follows from Cases (i) and (ii) that for large
n
(and the appropriate
\ell:=\ell(q)
) we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\cup_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{B}(i,\ell,L)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant n\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\sum_{i=1}^{L}X_{i}>\ell\,\right)\leqslant n\cdot n^{-4}=n^{-3},

by the union bound. Thus, again for suitably large
n
, combining this with (
65
) gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathcal{W}(\ell,L)^{c}\,\right)\leqslant n^{-7}+o\!\left(n^{-9}\right)+n^{-3}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(n^{-3}\right).

Thus by the claim and Cases (i) and (ii) we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))>4(\ell+1)\,\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(n^{-3}\right)
if
q\geqslant n^{-2}
. All that remains is to relate the cases to the statement of the lemma.

For the first statement of this lemma, the range
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1-(\log\log n)^{2}/\log n
is covered by Case (i) and the fact that
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\,\mathsf{cw}(\mathcal{P}(n,q))\geqslant 2\,\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(n^{-3}\right)
if
q\leqslant 1/n^{2}
from (
64
). We now let
q=1-c(\log\log n)^{2}/\log n
, where
0<c\leqslant 1
is fixed. Then, for suitably large
n
, we have
\log\frac{1}{1-q}=\log\left(\frac{\log n}{c(\log\log n)^{2}}\right)\leqslant\log\log n
. Thus, by (
67
), we have

\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(1/q)}}=\frac{\sqrt{\log(1/q)\log n}}{\log(1/q)}\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{(1-q)\log n}}{\log(1/q)}=\frac{\sqrt{c(\log\log n)^{2}}}{\log(1/q)}\geqslant\sqrt{c}\cdot\frac{\log\frac{1}{1-q}}{\log(1/q)},

and so if we set
C:=C(c)=5/\sqrt{c}
then the first statement follows from Case (ii). The second statement follows directly from Case (ii) as
5\log(1-q)/\log q\leqslant 5\log\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\right)/(1-q)
by (
67
).∎

7
Conclusion & Open Problems

In this paper we introduced the
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
model, found a sharp threshold for the existence of cut vertices, determined the treewidth up to a
\log
factor, and proved bounds on the vertex expansion and diameter for restricted values of
q
. Roughly speaking, the tangled path has three regimes:
path-like
when
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1-\Theta(1/\log n)
,
intermediate
where
1-\Theta(1/\log n)\leqslant q\leqslant 1-\Theta(1/n)
, and
expander
if
1-\Theta(1/n)\leqslant q\leqslant 1
. In the path-like regime there are no long edges and lots of independence, and in the expander regime one should be able to couple to the uniform case. The intermediate regime seems to be the trickiest and most interesting to analyze.

There are a wealth of open problems for
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
as one could study the effect of
q
on almost any graph property/index of interest for sparse graphs. One fundamental problem is to determine the number of edges in
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
, recalling that we disregard multi-edges. This deceptively non-trivial problem is related to clustering of consecutive numbers in Mallows permutations
[
45
]
. It would also be nice to close the gap for treewidth by obtaining tight bounds for all
q
.

Theorem
1.2
proves that
q=1-\pi^{2}/(6\log n)
is a sharp threshold for containing a single vertex whose removal separates the graph into two macroscopic components. A key open problem is to determine if there is a notion of monotone property in the setting of tangled paths which guarantees the existence of a threshold (or even a sharp threshold). One candidate feature (for a property to be monotone with respect to) is the number of inversions in the permutation generating
\mathcal{P}(n,q)
. However, one issue with parameterizing by the number of inversions is the fact that the tangled paths generated by
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
and its reverse
\sigma^{R}=(\sigma_{n},\dots,\sigma_{1})
are isomorphic, but the number of inversions may differ greatly as
\operatorname{Inv}(\sigma^{R})=\binom{n}{2}-\operatorname{Inv}(\sigma)
.
