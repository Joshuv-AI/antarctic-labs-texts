Approximate Bayesian Optimisation for Neural Networks
Nadhir Hassen, Irina Rish

Abstract
A body of work has been done to automate machine learning algorithms and to highlight the importance of model choice. Automating the process of choosing the best forecasting model and its corresponding parameters can result to improve a wide range of real-world applications. Bayesian optimisation (BO) uses a black-box optimisation methods to propose solutions according to an exploration-exploitation trade-off criterion through acquisition functions. BO framework imposes two key ingredients: a probabilistic surrogate model that consists of prior belief of the unknown dynamic of the model and an objective function that describes how optimal the model-fit. Choosing the best model and its associated hyperparameters can be very expensive, and is typically fit using Gaussian processes (GPs). However, since GPs scale cubically with the number of observations, it has been challenging to handle objectives whose optimisation requires many evaluations. In addition, most real-datasets are non-stationary which makes idealistic assumptions on surrogate models. The necessity to solve the analytical tractability and the computational feasibility in a stochastic fashion enables to ensure the efficiency and the applicability of Bayesian optimisation. In this paper we explore the use of approximate inference with Bayesian Neural Networks as an alternative to GPs to model distributions over functions. Our contribution is to provide a link between density-ratio estimation and class probability estimation based on approximate inference, this reformulation provides algorithm efficiency and tractability.



1
Introduction

Bayesian Optimisation is a powerful methodology for optimising black-box functions that are generally noisy, expensive to evaluate or have not accessible derivatives. The BO framework consists to propose candidate solutions according to an
acquisition function
that encodes an exploration-exploitation trade-off to optimise a cost function for various hyperparameter configurations. These parameter can correspond to learning rates, layer depths, width of the neural network or dropout rates during the training phase. We can formulate the problem of finding a global optimiser of an unknown objective function
\mathbf{f}
as follow

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\star}=\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the space of interest and
\mathbf{x}
denotes the variable that corresponds to the hyperparameter setting which can be continuous, discrete or mixed. BO can be applied to larger space that involves categorical input or combinatorial search spaces with multiple categorical inputs. We assume that the black-box function
\mathbf{f}
has no explicit form and can be evaluated at any arbitrary point
\mathbf{x}
in the domain. Typically, the BO is based on two key components, the surrogate model that approximates
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
and the acquisition function that measures the utility of new input points by trading off exploration and exploitation. The surrogate model is a probabilistic model that identifies which acquisition function should be computed. The most common probabilistic model is the Gaussian process (GP) due to its flexibility and its ability to handle uncertainty estimates. The GP is characterised by its mean which captures the estimated value of the function and whose variance determines the level of uncertainty of the prediction for a specific location. In particular, the Bayes rule is applied to prescribe a prior belief over the possible objective functions and sequentially updates the posterior belief as the data are observed to query for the most promising objective based on the acquisition function
\mathbf{f}
. Surrogate BO models present some issues due to GPs idealistic assumptions. In fact, GPs present notably limitations due to the computational complexity which scale to
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. They are poorly calibrated to real-datasets, that is, the stationary assumption assumes the GP kernel translation-invariant where noise processes are complex and heteroscedastic. In addition, they are not equipped to deal with conditional dependency structures
Jenatton et al., 2017
. Alternatively, we can express an acquisition function in a special form that does not impose analytical tractability constraint on the surrogate model. Expected Improvement (EI) presented by
Bergstra et al., 2011a
plays the role of criterion that corresponds to utility-function. The improved target value which is unknown converges reasonably in practice.
Mockus et al., 1978
demonstrate that EI function can be expressed as
relative ratio
between two densities termed
the tree-structured Parzen estimator
(TPE)
Bergstra et al., 2011b
. This method which is based on density estimation can naturally deal with tree-structured inputs, discrete inputs, and scales linearly with the number of observations.

However these methods are not adaptive to non-stationary surrogate functions which may lead to numerical instability and scale poorly to higher dimensions.
Menon and Ong, 2016
and
Tiao et al., 2021
exploit the link between density ratio estimation (DRE) and the EI function by class-probability estimation (CPE). This method has the advantage to scale to higher dimensions and enable to build a generic classifier that can capture non-linear tendency to solve the non-stationary issue. However, this method results in numerical instabilities for unbalanced data and are not suited to non-Gaussians likelihoods. That is, the expected improvement function may include fewer candidates for the under-presented functions, yields to miss-representation. To address these issue, we expose new algorithms that can solve these limitations. First, we propose a
linearized link-function
to accounts the under-presented class by using a GP surrogate model. This method is based on Laplace’s method and Gauss-Newton approximations to the Hessian. Our method can improve generalization and be useful when validation data is unavailable (e.g., in non-stationary settings) to solve heteroscedastic behaviours. Our experiments demonstrate that our BO by Gauss-Newton approach competes favorably with state-of-the-art black-box optimization algorithms.

Background

Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\{(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
denotes a set of data point with a smooth function
f:\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}
over a bounded input domain
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The observation
y\sim\mathcal{N}(f(\mathbf{x}),\sigma^{2})
denotes the noisy observation of the blackbox function
f
with noise variance
\sigma^{2}
. The goal is to find a global optimizer of the function
f
to trade-off between exploration and exploitation during the search process. A surrogate model is introduced to encode the beliefs over the data points by maximizing an acquisition function which queries the batch-points that maximize the information gain and minimize the uncertainty of the model. We generally use the
Expected Improvement
(
Mockus et al., 1978
) acquisition function for its tight link to
density estimation
. We define
\tau=\Phi(\gamma)^{-1}
a threshold with some constant
\gamma
that denotes an arbitrary quantile of the observed
y
values. The utility function
I_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})
quantifies the non-negative improvement over
\tau
such as

\displaystyle I_{\gamma}=\max(\tau-y,0),

and the EI function determines new query points by maximising expected gain relative to the the posterior predictive distribution
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathcal{D}_{n})
observed so far

\displaystyle\alpha_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{D}_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathcal{D}_{n})}\left[I_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})\right].

Clearly, when
\gamma=0
yields to
\tau=\min_{n}y_{n}
, this dependency occurs due to the CDF mapping from
\tau
to
\gamma
. Following
Yamada et al., 2011
we define the
\gamma
-
relative density ratio
of two aribitrary function
\ell(\mathbf{x})
and
g(\mathbf{x})
as follow

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\ell(\mathbf{x})}{\gamma\ell(\mathbf{x})+\left(1-\gamma\right)g(\mathbf{x})},

where
\gamma\ell(\mathbf{x})+\left(1-\gamma\right)g(\mathbf{x})
denotes the
\gamma
-
mixture density
with mixing proportion
0\leq\gamma<1
. We recover the original density ratio
r_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\ell(\mathbf{x})/g(\mathbf{x})
when
\gamma=0
, in addition we can express the relative density ratio with the the original one such as

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\gamma+\frac{g(\mathbf{x})}{\ell(\mathbf{x})}\left(1-\gamma\right)\right)^{-1}.

At this stage we need to define the corresponding distributions of
\ell(\mathbf{x})
and
g(\mathbf{x})
with respect the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D}_{n})
such that

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D}_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ell(\mathbf{x})&\mbox{if}&y<\tau\\
g(\mathbf{x})&\mbox{if}&y\geq\tau\end{array}\right.

Interestingly,
Bergstra et al., 2011a
showed that the EI acquisition function can be expressed as proportional to
\gamma
-
relative density ratio
such as
\alpha_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})
. Therefore the global optimization problem is reduced to maximizing the
\gamma
-
relative density ratio

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\star}=\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}_{n})=\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}),

this optimization problem can be optimized using a variety of approaches from
density estimation
literature. In this paper we treat the case of Bayesian neural network parametrized by the hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
that consist of the prior and the likelihood precisions specified by a model
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, for a Bayesian neural network, we explicitly include
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the acquisition function as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{n})}\left[I_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right],

where

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right].

A Bayesian model can then be defined using a likelihood and a prior to be able to compute the posterior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D},\mathcal{M})\propto p(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{M})
. In most case this posterior is intractable we then resort to approximation techniques. The main contribution of this work is to link Bayesian optimization to approximate inference using density ratio-estimation.

3
Methodology

3.1
Approximate Acquisition Function for Bayesian Neural Network

We consider the context of Bayesian neural network (BNN) and our goal is to find an approximate acquisition function that will enable to perform the optimisation loop borrowed from deep learning theory. We first introduce a feature extractor
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
with parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
and use a likelihood function
p(\mathcal{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
that map the inputs to the output class targets
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
, such as

\displaystyle p(\mathcal{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta})).

Generally, in approximate inference theory, we use an inverse link function
\mathbf{g}^{-1}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]:=\mathbf{g}^{-1}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))
. By imposing a prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
on the likelihood parameters we aim to compute the posterior given the data
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
. Typically, we choose a Gaussian prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
, we then make a probabilistic prediction for new inputs
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
using the posterior predictive

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}_{\star}|\mathbf{x}_{\star},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}_{\star}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{\star},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right].

The exact posterior inference is in general intractable or complicated to compute due to high-dimensional integral, the
model evidence
or
marginal likelihood

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\int p(\mathcal{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})d\boldsymbol{\theta}
is infeasible in general. We need to resort to approximate inference techniques such as
mean-field-variational-inference
or
Laplace approximation
to approximate
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})=\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{p(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}
. Following
Cheng and Chu, 2004
we can discriminate between two pairs of distributions inputs
\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x})
and infer the global mixture distribution, therefore we need to express the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D}_{n})
in terms of our function value
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We introduce a linearized form of the neural network using a first-order Taylor approximation around
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})&\mbox{if}&y<\tau\\
\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})&\mbox{if}&y\geq\tau\end{array}\right\}

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{map}}
which corresponds to the
maximum a posteriori
. Note that
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
is not restricted to Gaussian family and can depend non-linearly on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. We reformulate the EI function to include the posterior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\alpha_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\left[U(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\tau)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right](\tau-\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\left(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\frac{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})}{\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x})}\right)^{-1}\right]:=r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}),

(3.1)

where we follow
Yamada et al., 2011
to link the acquisition function to the
\gamma
-relative density ratio, the acquisition function is equivalent to
\gamma
-relative density ratio up to a certain constant factor (
Sugiyama et al., 2012a
). Doing so, we recover the formulation given by
Bergstra et al., 2011b
under the distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
. Refer to
Appendix.A
for the full derivation. The last expression in equation (
3.1
) is reduced to an expectation of mixture density with respect to the prior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
with mixing proportion
0\leq\gamma<1
. Since this formulation is based on
Tree-structured Parzen estimator
(TPE) it provides two advantages: First, we identify a monotonically non-decreasing function which allows to maximize this optimization problem easily. Second, we can estimate separately both
\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x})
using a tree-based variant of kernel density estimation (KDE) (
Silverman, 1986
). The main advantage of this method is to reduce the computational cost to
\mathcal{O}(N)
compared to more generic surrogate model as Gaussian processes (GP) which scale to
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. One major issue is that the
\gamma
-relative density ratio based methods are exacerbated by
error sensitivity
(
Terrell and Scott, 1992
), particularly in high dimension and make the optimization problem difficult to solve. To overcome these pitfalls, one alternate solution is to link the approximate acquisition function to
Class-probability-estimation
(CPE) (
Cheng and Chu, 2004
,
Sugiyama et al.,
2012b
,
Bickel et al., 2007
). This method belongs to classification family algorithm and enables to have a closed form of the objective function. First, we follow
Menon and Ong, 2016
formalism to introduce a binary target variable
z
to discriminate against the quantile value
\tau
, more formally we have

\displaystyle z=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}1&\mbox{if}&y<\tau\\
0&\mbox{if}&y\geq\tau\end{array}\right\}.

Having defined
\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}|z=1,\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x})=1-\psi(\mathbf{x}|z=1,\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we can apply the Bayes’ rule and rewrite the
\gamma
-relative-density function as

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{z},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\frac{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\gamma\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})+(1-\gamma)\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\pi(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right],

where we established the link between the class-probability and the
\gamma
-relative density ratio. In comparison to TPE, this method is always bounded above
1/\gamma
for all
\gamma>0
and does not suffer from singularity. In the case where we approximate the posterior by a variational distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
we obtain an approximate expression denoted by
\hat{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})
. Refer to the
Appendix.B
for the full derivation. Ultimately, in Bayesian inference, we wish to maximize the
marginal
acquisition function which marginalizes out the uncertainty about the hyperparameters.

3.2
Model Evidence

In this Bayesian learning framework, the ultimate goal is to maximize the
marginal likelihood
function which depends on the acquisition function under the variational distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Now we turn our attention to express the likelihood function
p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\text{Bernoulli}(\mathbf{z}|\pi(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}))
in order to formulate the optimization problem. We give an explicit formulation about how the acquisition function depends on the hyperparameters wrt the variational distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and how it should be optimized. That is, we specify a prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for a particular model
\mathcal{M}
, the model might consists of the choice of network architecture, hyperparameters of the likelihood and prior variance. We are interested in maximizing the marginal class-posterior probabilities considered as the objective function, the optimization problem takes the following form

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Pi(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M}),

optimizing the posterior class-probabilities is equivalent to optimizing the marginal acquisition function, we have

\displaystyle\Pi(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\int p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{q(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})}\biggl(\gamma\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\pi(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})\right]+(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[1-\pi(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})\right])\biggr),

(3.2)

where
q(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})
denotes the approximate marginal likelihood for target values
\mathbf{y}
. The expression above in equation (
3.2
) uses the marginal likelihood for neural network in order to approximate the posterior distribution (
MacKay, 1992
). This method is based on a local quadratic approximation of
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
around the maximum
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}
resulting in a Gaussian approximation denoted by
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and an approximation to the marginal likelihood denoted by
q(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})
given the model
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\log p(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})\approx\log q(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M}):

\displaystyle=\log p(\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}|\mathcal{M})-\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}\right)^{T}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}\right)^{T}\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\log p(\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}|\mathcal{M})-\frac{1}{2}\log\text{det}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}}\right)

(3.3)

where
\mathcal{H}:=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta\theta}}^{2}\log p(\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})
denotes the Hessian matrix and
\mathcal{J}:=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star})
denotes the gradient evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\star}
. Exponentiating the approximation to the joint distribution in equation (
3.3
) and dividing by the marginal likelihood, we obtain the Laplace approximation as a Gaussian distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{map}}
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=-\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\theta})\rvert_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{\emph{map}}}}\right]^{-1}
.
The full derivation is given in
Appendix.D
. This expression shows that the optimization of the surrogate model is reduced to maximizing the approximate
\gamma
-relative-density ratio
\hat{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{z},\mathcal{D})
. We can express the objective function as the
proper scoring rule
, we are therefore interested to minimize

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D},\gamma,\mathcal{M}):=-\frac{1}{N}\biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\pi(\mathbf{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta})\gamma+(1-\pi(\mathbf{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}))(1-\gamma)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log q(\mathcal{D}_{n}|\mathcal{M})\biggr).

(3.4)

The last expression in equation (
3.4
) shows that we would like points
\mathbf{x}
with high probability under the posterior-class probability
\pi(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
and low probability under
1-\pi(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Since we use the tree-structured formalism, we first sample from the approximate distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and evaluate the expected ratio according to class-binary variable
\mathbf{z}
. On each iteration, the algorithm returns the candidate
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
with the greatest expected improvement. See (Algorithm
1
) for the pseudo-code of the proposed method.

3.3
The choice of the approximate distribution

There is mainly two concurrent approaches in approximate inference: i)
Mean-field Variational Inference (VI)
: this method approximates the posterior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
by a decoupled variational distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
optimized by maximizing the
evidence-lower-bound
or ELBO to the marginal likelihood (
Blundell et al., 2015
). ii)
Laplace approximation
: proposed by
MacKay, 1992
is based on approximating the posterior by a Gaussian distribution around the mode
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{\emph{map}}}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
with
q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{\emph{map}}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=-\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\theta})\rvert_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{\emph{map}}}}\right]^{-1}.

We follow the approach of Laplace approximation, to compute the covariance, we need to compute the Hessian of
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
wrt
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
But computing the Hessian involves computing the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}^{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times P}
and the Hessian
\mathcal{H}^{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times P\times P}
of the function value
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
, more formally we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{f}(\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta\theta}}^{2}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})-\mathcal{J}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}\mathbf{f}}}^{2}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial^{2}f_{c}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}\quad\text{ and }\mathcal{J}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial f_{c}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}},

when dealing with network Hessian, this becomes infeasible and we resort to approximate the covariance, we can therefore approximate
\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
using
generalized Gauss-Newton
(GGN) approximation as in
Martens, 2020
and
Schraudolph, 2002
, then we can rewrite the approximate covariance as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta\theta}}^{2}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\approx-\mathcal{J}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}\mathbf{f}}}^{2}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})\mathcal{J}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}).

This approximation guarantees to be positive semi-definite and can be further approximated to diagonal and Kronecker-factored approximation for efficient storage and inverse computation. In particular, the last expression assumes that
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})=0
(
Bottou et al., 2018
). Since we have defined the function value
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
as a linear function of its predictor, yields the Hessian to vanish. Further this linearization will allow to convert the original function value expression to a
generalized linear model
(GLM) which enable to perform space function inference.

Related Work

Bayesian optimization literature has been extensively based on modeling a probabilistic surrogate model which plays an important role to compute the acquisition function. The common choice is a Gaussian process (GP) due to its flexibility and ability to handle uncertainity, however the GP-based BO suffers from scalability issue and specially when dealing with neural networks as in
Snoek et al., 2015
. Specific treatment has been done in this direction as in
White et al., 2020
and in
Perrone et al., 2018
for transfer learning. Recently, a body of work has been extended to handle non-stationary data which is the biggest limitation of GPs,
Springenberg et al., 2016
used a flexible parametric model as neural network to overcome the scalability issue through stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. The most common pitfalls of surrogate model is the difficulty to handle discrete and conditional variables,
Bergstra et al., 2011b
showed a simplistic technique by applying the density ratio using TPE technique which can overcome this issue by approximating the acquisition function. Expected Improvement (EI)
Jones et al., 1998

acquisition function remains a good choice, because it is easy to evaluate and to optimize and consistently performs well in practice. A different body of works has been extended to Kernel density estimation (KDE), many alternatives have been proposed in this direction including KL importance estimation procedure (KLIEP) (
Sugiyama et al.,
2012b
), unconstrained least-squares importance fitting (ULSIF)in
Gretton et al., 2009
which based on covariate shift, and relative ULSIF in (
Yamada et al., 2011
).
A relatively closed to our work,
Kleinegesse and Gutmann, 2019
proposed implicit models where the likelihood is intractable but the sampling from the model is possible, they use utility function to link the mutual information between parameters and the data, they achieve this by utilising Likelihood-Free Inference by Ratio Estimation (LFIRE) to approximate posterior distributions. In our work, we make use of density ratio estimation approach and we restrict ourselves to link the CPE (
Menon and Ong, 2016
,
Tiao et al., 2021

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we formulated a novel framework of Bayesian optimization problem that concerns uncertainty in density ratio estimation. Instead of trying to patch the deficiencies of the surrogate model, we reduce the Expected Improvement function to a probabilistic classification problem. We cast this observation in the Bayesian Neural Network setting, by seeking directly an approximate acquisition function, we therefore identified the link between the class-posterior probability and approximate density ratio estimation under
Generalized Gauss Newton
method where we considered a linearized neural network. Further we demonstrate that our CPE-approach termed BOGGN outperforms against the state-of-the-art derivative-free global optimization methods under different variants of classifiers. The efficiency of our method leads to a fertile area of research in BO and approximate inference for future work.

Appendix.A : Approximate acquisition function

We give the full derivation of approximate acquisition function, following
Bergstra et al., 2011b
. In the case of the classification case, we use the expectation of the improvement utility function
U(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\tau)
over the posterior predictive distribution
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z},\mathcal{D})
where
\mathbf{y}_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{C}
. We have

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\left[U(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\tau)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})(\tau-\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})\right](\tau-\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

(7.1)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})(\tau-\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})(\tau-\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}d\boldsymbol{\theta}

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})|\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{D})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})(\tau-\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[\tau\gamma\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathbf{y}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{D})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}\right]d\boldsymbol{\theta}

(7.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\biggl(\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\tau\gamma\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathbf{y}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}\right]\biggl)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\biggl\{\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]\left(\tau\gamma-\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathbf{y}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}\right)\biggl\}

where in equation (
7.2
) we applied the Bayes’rule, in equation (
7.3
) we have defined
\gamma=\Phi(\tau):=p(\mathbf{y}\leq\tau|\mathcal{D})
and we used in equation (
7.1
) the posterior identity

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right].

The denominator is evaluated to

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D})\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})d\mathbf{y}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\gamma\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})(1-\gamma)\right].

Therefore, we can show that the approximate acquisition function is equivalent to the approximate
\gamma
-relative density ratio (
Yamada et al., 2011

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D},\tau)

\displaystyle=k\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[\gamma\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})(1-\gamma)\right]}

where we recover an approximate formulation of the Expected Improvement (

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})

p(\boldsymbol{\theta})

q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})

Appendix.B : Class-posterior probability

We provide the class probability estimation according to the approximate
\gamma
-relative-density-ratio, this will establish the link between the class-posterior probability and the relative density-ration with respect to the approximate posterior distribution
{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
. First, we rewrite the approximate
\gamma

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D},\tau)

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\frac{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\gamma\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})(1-\gamma)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\frac{p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z}=1,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}{\gamma p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z}=1,\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y},\mathcal{D})+(1-\gamma)p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z}=0,\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\frac{p(\mathbf{z}=1|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})/p(\mathbf{z}=1)}{\gamma p(\mathbf{z}=1|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D}))/p(\mathbf{z}=1)+(1-\gamma)p(\mathbf{z}=0|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})/p(\mathbf{z}=0)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})/\gamma}{\gamma\pi(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})/\gamma+(1-\gamma)(1-\pi(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))/(1-\gamma)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\gamma}\right],

where
\pi(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=p(\mathbf{z}=1|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\mathcal{D})
denotes the likelihood of the model with respect to the hyperparameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Appendix.D: Optimizing the objective function

Recall the objective function to be minimized takes the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D},\gamma|\mathcal{M}):=-\frac{1}{N}\biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\pi(\mathbf{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta})\gamma+(1-\pi(\mathbf{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}))(1-\gamma)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log q(\mathcal{D}_{n}|\mathcal{M})\biggr),

To compute the approximate marginal distribution
q(\mathcal{D}_{n}|\mathcal{M})
, we make probabilistic prediction for new input
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
, in the case of the true posterior predictive
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
, we have

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}_{\star}|\mathbf{x}_{\star},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{D})=\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}_{\star}|\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{\star},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right],

where we resort to posterior approximation techniques, in this paper we consider the Laplace approximation with
Generalized-Gauss-Newton
, see section (
3.3
). Regardless of the posterior approximation chosen, we approximate this expectation by Monte Carlo sampling. In fact, the predictive can handle non-Gaussian likelihood and can depend on non-linearly
\mathbf{f}
on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}
. Therefore we estimate the approximate posterior
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})
against the model likelihood
p(\mathcal{D|\boldsymbol{\theta}})
such as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right]\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}p(\mathbf{y}|\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})),

(7.6)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\sim q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{map}},\Sigma_{\text{ggn}})
. Interestingly, since the Laplace-GGN posterior corresponds to the posterior of the linearized model, the Bayesian regression model turns to a

7.5
Equivalence of GLM formulation to Gaussian process

The Bayesian GLM forumlation of the likelihood in equation (
7.5
) in weight space is equivalent to a Gaussian process (GP) in function space with a specific kernel (
Rasmussen and Williams, 2006
). We can write the GP-equivalent log-joint distribution as follow

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{f}_{n})+\log p(\mathbf{f}),

where the GP prior is specified by its mean
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})
and its covariance
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
under the prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
that takes the following form

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\psi_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\psi_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{m}_{0})

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=Cov_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\psi_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}),\psi_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{S}_{0}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})^{T},

where
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{\emph{map}}}
. In the case of a single output, the Laplace-GGN approximation to GP posterior
q(\mathbf{f}^{\star})
at a new location
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
is given by (
Rasmussen and Williams, 2006
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\star}|\mathbf{x}_{\star},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{N}(\psi(\mathbf{x}_{\star},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{\emph{map}}}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}^{2})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\star}^{2}=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}_{\star},\mathbf{x}_{\star})-\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}_{\star},\mathbf{x}_{N})\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{N})+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}_{N},\mathbf{f}_{N})\right)^{-1}\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{\star}),

where
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{x}_{\star},\mathbf{x}_{N})
denotes the kernel
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(.,.)
evaluated between
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
and the
N
training points, and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbf{y}_{N},\mathbf{f}_{N})
is a diagonal matrix. Analogoous to the GLM formulation in equation (
7.6
), we obtain the GP predictive

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{GP}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{f})}\left[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}_{s}),

where
\mathbf{f}_{s}\sim q(\mathbf{f})
. This conversion from weight space to functional space allows further approximations, that is, since the parametric weight space posterior approximation sparsify the covariances of the parameters, the functional posterior approximation consider sparsity in data space. Therefore functional approximations of a GP model are orthogonal to parametric approximations in weight space.
