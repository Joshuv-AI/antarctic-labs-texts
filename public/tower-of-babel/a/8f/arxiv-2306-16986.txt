Acoustic radiation force on a heated spherical particle in a fluid including scattering and microstreaming from a standing ultrasound wave
Bjørn G. Winckelmann, Henrik Bruus

Abstract
Analytical expressions are derived for the time-averaged, quasi-steady, acoustic radiation force on a heated, spherical, elastic, solid microparticle suspended in a fluid and located in an axisymmetric incident acoustic wave. The heating is assumed to be spherically symmetric, and the effects of particle vibrations, sound scattering, and acoustic microstreaming are included in the calculations of the acoustic radiation force. It is found that changes in the speed of sound of the fluid due to temperature gradients can significantly change the force on the particle, particularly through perturbations to the microstreaming pattern surrounding the particle. For some fluid-solid combinations, the effects of particle heating even reverse the direction of the force on the particle for a temperature increase at the particle surface as small as 1 K.

Introduction

A particle suspended in a fluid perturbed by an acoustic wave experiences a time-averaged force, termed the acoustic radiation force

Theoretical studies of

Gorkov 1962
in 1962.
Doinikov published two series of papers taking into account the effects of fluid viscosity
Doinikov 1994a
;
Doinikov 1994b
in 1994 and the effects of heat conduction
Doinikov 1997a
;
Doinikov 1997b
;
Doinikov 1997c
in 1997, where he included both the linear scattering of the acoustic wave and the nonlinear steady acoustic microstreaming developing around the particle.
More recent developments were made by Settnes and Bruus in 2012
Settnes and Bruus 2012
, Karlsen and Bruus in 2015
Karlsen and Bruus 2015
, and Doinikov, Fankhauser, and Dual in 2021
Doinikov et al. 2021
.
A detailed study of
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
on small particles in a thermoviscous fluid was conducted in our recent work
Winckelmann and Bruus 2023
. Here, the effects of particle vibrations, acoustic scattering, temperature and density dependent material parameters, and thermoviscous microstreaming were included in the analytical derivation of
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
, and it was found that microstreaming effects may dominate
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
when the viscous- and thermal boundary layer widths

and

a
. The importance of the microstreaming patterns for

In this paper we derive analytical expressions for

;
Doinikov 1994b
;

;

II
Governing equations

Our model includes an isotropic, elastic, solid particle suspended in a Newtonian fluid. The particle is assumed to be heated by either an external source (e.g. a laser) or an internal source (e.g. an exothermic chemical reaction), and consequently heat conduction leads to the formation of a temperature gradient in the surrounding fluid. The fluid is perturbed by a monochromatic adiabatic acoustic wave with frequency
f
and angular frequency
\omega=2\pi f
. All physical fields
g(\bm{r},t)
describing the system in space
\bm{r}
and time
t
are expanded in perturbation series, and the material parameters
q(\bm{r},t)
vary through their dependency of temperature
T
and density
\rho
,

\displaystyle g(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=g_{0}(\bm{r},t)+\re\Big[g_{1}(\bm{r},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\Big]+g_{2}(\bm{r},t),

(1a)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=q_{0}[T_{0}(\bm{r},t)]+\re\Big[q_{1}(\bm{r},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\Big],

(1b)

\displaystyle q_{1}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\partial q}{\partial T}\bigg)_{T=T_{0}}T_{1}(\bm{r},t)+\bigg(\frac{\partial q}{\partial\rho}\bigg)_{\rho=\rho_{0}}\rho_{1}(\bm{r},t).

(1c)

The zeroth-order fields
g_{0}(\bm{r},t)
describe a quiescent fluid, with a background temperature field
T_{0}(\bm{r},t)
, and the zeroth-order parameters
q_{0}(\bm{r},t)
are assumed to be functions of
T_{0}
only, so their density dependency is neglected in the following. The complex-valued first-order fields
g_{1}
describe the linear acoustic response, which follows the actuation frequency
f
. The second-order fields
g_{2}(\bm{r},t)
describe a non-linear response containing small second-order harmonics and a steady time-averaged response. Only the time-averaged second-order effects are considered here, and they are denoted by angled brackets, e.g.
\big\langle g_{2}(\bm{r},t)\big\rangle
. The acoustic oscillations and streaming generally depend on the background temperature field
T_{0}(\bm{r},t)
due to the temperature dependencies of all physical parameters
q_{0}
. We assume that the temperature field develops on a timescale much slower than an acoustic oscillation period
f^{-1}
, and the complex-valued acoustic fields
g_{1}(\bm{r},t)
are calculated as steady fields at any given time
t
using the instantaneous temperature field
T_{0}(\bm{r},t)
.

The objective is to compute
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
by the time average of the stress
\bm{\sigma}
integrated over the vibrating particle surface
\partial\Omega(t)
with normal vector
\bm{n}
,

\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}=\bigg\langle\oint_{\partial\Omega(t)}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}\>\mathrm{d}S\bigg\rangle.

(2)

Assuming that the particle drift is negligible during an acoustic period,
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
can be written as
Doinikov 1994a
;
Karlsen and Bruus 2015
,

\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}=\oint_{\partial\Omega_{0}}\langle\bm{\sigma}_{2}-\rho_{0}\bm{v}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\rangle\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}S,

(3)

where
\partial\Omega_{0}
is the equilibrium surface of the particle, and
\bm{v}
is the velocity field of the fluid surrounding the particle.

II.1
Thermal diffusion

The temperature field is treated as a transient background field. Heat diffusion is assumed to dominate over the heat convection caused by the acoustic streaming
\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle
, thus we assume a low Péclet number,

\displaystyle\textit{P\'{e}}=\frac{L|\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle|}{D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}\ll 1,\qquad D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}=\frac{k^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}}{\rho_{0}c_{p0}}.

(4)

Here,
L
is the characteristic length scale for heat diffusion,
\big|\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle\big|
is the magnitude of the steady streaming field in the fluid,
D^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}
is the thermal diffusivity,
\rho_{0}
is the mass density of the quiescent medium at a given temperature
T_{0}
,
k^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}
is the thermal conductivity, and
c_{p0}
is the specific heat capacity at constant pressure. The time average of the particle motion is assumed to be zero, and the temperature in both the solid particle and the fluid thus follow the heat diffusion equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T_{0}=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}c_{p0}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big(k^{\mathrm{th}}_{0}\bm{\nabla}T_{0}\big)+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}c_{p0}}P,

(5)

where
P
is the power density absorbed by the medium due to an external or internal source of energy.

We assume that gradients in the temperature field
T_{0}
are small enough that material parameters
q_{0}
only deviate slightly from their ambient value
q_{0}^{\infty}
, and that the change is linear in the deviation
\Delta T_{0}
from the ambient temperature,

\displaystyle\Delta T_{0}(\bm{r},t)=T_{0}(\bm{r},t)-T^{\infty}_{0}

(6a)

\displaystyle q_{0}\approx q_{0}^{\infty}(1+a_{q}\Delta T_{0}),\qquad a_{q}=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{\infty}}\Big(\frac{\partial q_{0}}{\partial T}\Big)_{T_{0}^{\infty}},

(6b)

\displaystyle|a_{q}\Delta T_{0}|\ll 1,

(6c)

\displaystyle\text{and terms containing $\partial_{r}T_{0}(\bm{r},t)$ are neglected}.

(6d)

The assumption (
6d
) is justified by a numerical study presented in the Supplemental Material
Note1
. The assumption is based on the fact that the temperature field
\Delta T_{0}
falls off as
\Delta T_{0}\sim r^{-1}
, where
r
is the distance to the particle center, whereas
\partial_{r}T_{0}\sim r^{-2}
. We argue that the primary perturbation to
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
is due to the enhancement of a directional microstreaming caused by temperature-induced, long-ranged perturbations of the acoustic fields in the bulk. Consequently, the dominating terms are likely to be the ones decaying slowly with
r^{-1}
.

Viscous fluid dynamics

The surrounding fluid is described by the fluid velocity field
\bm{v}
and the fluid stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s

(8c)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=p(\rho).

(8d)

We also introduce the isentropic compressibility

\displaystyle\kappa_{s}=\frac{1}{\rho}\Big(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p}\Big)_{s}=\frac{1}{\rho c^{2}},\qquad\textrm{(fluids)},

(10b)

The adiabatic assumption in Eq. (

II.3
Isotropic elastic solid mechanics

The linear elastic solid is described by the the mechanical displacement field
\bm{u}
, and the solid stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
, which is expressed in terms of the transverse and longitudinal speeds of sound
c_{\mathrm{tr}}
and
c_{\mathrm{lo}}

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}=\rho c_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}[\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u})^{\textsf{T}}]+\rho(c_{\mathrm{lo}}^{2}-2c_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2})(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u})\,\textbf{{I}}.

(14)

The mechanical displacement field
\bm{u}
can then be determined in time and space from the Cauchy equation,

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}.

(15)

For solids, the isentropic compressibility

can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\kappa_{s}=\frac{1}{\rho(c_{\mathrm{lo}}^{2}-\tfrac{4}{3}c_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2})},\qquad\textrm{(solids)}.

(16)

Expanding the fields into a perturbation series with
\bm{u}_{0}=\bm{0}
, one can describe the acoustic vibrations by,

-\rho_{0}\omega^{2}\bm{u}_{1}=\rho_{0}c_{\mathrm{tr0}}^{2}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}_{1}+\rho_{0}\Big(c_{\mathrm{lo0}}^{2}-c_{\mathrm{tr0}}^{2}\Big)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}_{1}),

(17)

where we have used assumption (
6d
). Following Ref.
Karlsen and Bruus 2015
, we also define the velocity field
\bm{v}_{1}
and the complex-valued ”viscosity”
\eta_{0}
of the solid by,

\bm{v}_{1}=-\mathrm{i}\omega\bm{u}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{0}=\mathrm{i}\frac{\rho_{0}c_{\mathrm{tr0}}^{2}}{\omega},\quad\text{for solids}.

(18)

The second-order response is not calculated for solids, as the time-averaged second-order velocity field is zero, and the steady thermal expansion is negligible.

The zeroth-order heat diffusion

\displaystyle P(\bm{r},t)=P_{0}\,\Theta(1-{\hat{r}})\Theta(t),

\displaystyle=D^{\mathrm{th\infty\prime}}_{0}\nabla^{2}T_{0}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}^{\infty\prime}c^{\infty\prime}_{p0}}P_{0},\quad t>0,

(21a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T_{0}

\displaystyle=D^{\mathrm{th\infty}}_{0}\nabla^{2}T_{0}.

(21b)

around the particle is illustrated in Fig.
3
(b). In general at zero heating,
\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle
contains several multipole components, as shown by the unit-vector plot on the left half of the figure (
\Delta T_{0}^{\mathrm{surf}}=0~\textrm{K}
). Remarkably, the heating of the particle results in a strong enhancement of a unidirectional (dipole) component as shown in the right half of the figure (
\Delta T_{0}^{\mathrm{surf}}=1~\textrm{K}
). It is this change in morphology of the microstreaming field
\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle
that causes the heating-induced change of the force coefficient
D^{\Delta T_{0}}_{n}
in
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
, Eq. (
51
), and the corresponding change
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\Delta T_{0}}
of the contrast factor, Eq. (
54e
). The amplitude and direction of the unidirectional component in the microstreaming correlates with the amplitude and sign of
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\Delta T_{0}}
, exemplified by the nine examples plotted in Fig.
2
.

A key assumption of our analysis is that the heated particle does not execute a significant time-averaged motion, such that the temperature field
\Delta T_{0}(\bm{r},t)
can develop in the surrounding fluid from a stationary heat source. Since the heat-induced perturbation in
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
is established on a timescale around
1~\textrm{s}
, see Fig.
2
, our analysis is primarily relevant for determining the final equilibrium position of the heated particle. The dynamics of slowly-moving particles may be described by the theory, however the description becomes increasingly inaccurate in the transient phase the faster the particle is moving. Regardless of such inaccuracies in the detailed dynamics, our model has a robust prediction regarding whether the particle migrates to a node (
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}>0
) or an antinode (
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}<0
) in the pressure field as heating of the particle may change the sign of
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}
as seen for the 1-
µm
-radius polystyrene particle in ethanol and the 5-
µm
-radius polystyrene particle in oil in Fig.
2
(b) and (c), respectively. The detailed dynamics only affects the time it takes the particle to reach its temperature-dependent equilibrium position. By combining particle heating with tuning of the solute by introducing various solvents, other cases of sign reversal in acoustophoresis may be obtained. One example is to add iodixanol to an aqueous solution of particles with an initial positive contrast factor to obtain a negative contrast factor, the so-called medium-tuning technique
Augustsson et al. 2016
. Subsequently, a second sign reversal may be obtained by heating the particle.

V
Conclusion

We have derived an analytical theory for the acoustic radiation force
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
on a heated spherical solid particle in an incident standing plane wave. The theory assumes that the external or internal heating of the particle is low enough that only small perturbations in the physical parameters of the solid particle and the external fluid occur. Further, effects of thermal convection are assumed negligible. In Section
III.1
we analyze how the temperature increase
\Delta T_{0}
diffuses from the heated particle into the surrounding fluid, and we derive the analytical expression (
24
); in Section
III.2
we compute how an incident pressure wave scatters on the slightly heated sphere; and in Section
III.3
we derive the main result of our work, the general expression (
49
) for
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
in terms of the force coefficients
D_{n}^{i}
Eq. (
49
), as well as the analytical expression (
51
) for
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
in the long-wavelength limit. Furthermore, in relation to Eq. (
III.3
), we point out that the heat-induced change
D_{n}^{\Delta T_{0}}
in the force coefficient
D_{n}
does not depend on the boundary-layer thickness
\delta_{s}
, but instead on the scattering induced by the long-range,
1/r
-decaying, thermal changes in the bulk acoustic properties of the fluid. Consequently,
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
can be greatly perturbed by particle heating even for particles of large radius
a\gtrsim\delta_{s}
in the long-wavelength limit,
a\ll\lambda
.

In Section
IV
, we analyze the changes to
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
in an incident standing plane wave, by studying numerically and analytically the changes
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\Delta T_{0}}
to the acoustic contrast factor due to the temperature increase
\Delta T_{0}
caused by the heated particle. We find in Fig.
2
that by heating a polystyrene particle of radius
a=1
, 2, and
5~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
suspended in water, ethanol, or oil, significant quantitative (up to an order of magnitude) and even qualitative changes (sign reversal) to the acoustic contrast factor
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}
occur. We point out that the opposite sign in the observed heat-induced change
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\Delta T_{0}}(t)
of
\Phi_{\mathrm{ac}}
for water and for the two organic liquids ethanol and oil is due to the opposite signs of the thermal sound-speed coefficient
a_{c}
, see Eq. (
29h
) and Table
2
.

The crucial role of microstreaming in causing the observed heat-induced changes in
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
is illustrated in Fig.
3
(b). The analysis shows how a unidirectional component in the microstreaming is strongly enhanced by the heating of the fluid surrounding the heated particle. The drag force resulting from this microstreaming component is a main cause of the resulting changes of the acoustic radiation force
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
.

We have extended the analytical theory of the acoustic radiation force on single spherical solid particle suspended in a homogeneous Newtonian fluid, to take into account heating of the particle. Examples where particle heating may greatly affect the force on the particle are explored. The heat-induced change in the acoustic contrast factor found in this work provides an additional control parameter for acoustofluidic handling of suspended microparticles. We speculate that this control may be obtained by optical methods such as absorbtion of laser-light by dyed particles. We hope that the presented analysis will inspire experimental efforts in the field of microscale acoustofluidics trying to improve particle-sorting, -separation, and -trapping techniques based on our predictions.
