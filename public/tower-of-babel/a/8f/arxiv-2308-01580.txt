Finite Element Approximation of the Hardy constant
Francesco Della Pietra, Giovanni Fantuzzi, Liviu I. Ignat, Alba Lia Masiello, Gloria Paoli, Enrique Zuazua

Abstract
. We consider finite element approximations to the optimal constant for the Hardy inequality with exponent p = 2 p=2 in bounded domains of dimension n = 1 n=1 or n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . For finite element spaces of piecewise linear and continuous functions on a mesh of size h h , we prove that the approximate Hardy constant converges to the optimal Hardy constant at a rate proportional to 1 / | log ⁡ h | 2 1/{\left|\log h\right|}^{2} . This result holds in dimension n = 1 n=1 , in any dimension n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 if the domain is the unit ball and the finite element discretization exploits the rotational symmetry of the problem, and in dimension n = 3 n=3 for general finite element discretizations of the unit ball. In the first two cases, our estimates show excellent quantitative agreement with values of the discrete Hardy constant obtained computationally. MSC 2020: 46E35, 65N30 Keywords: Hardy inequality, Hardy constant, Finite Element Method



1.
Introduction

In his celebrated work
[
19
]
, G. H. Hardy proved that

(1.1)

\left(\dfrac{p-1}{p}\right)^{p}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{{\left|u\right|}^{p}}{x^{p}}dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{p}\;dx

for all
1<p<+\infty
and all
u\in W^{1,p}(0,1)
with
u(0)=0
. This inequality, nowadays called the
Hardy inequality
, was extended in
[
20
]
to open sets
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
in
n\geq 2
dimensions and
p\in(1,n)
, giving

(1.2)

\left(\frac{n-p}{p}\right)^{p}\int_{\Omega}\frac{{\left|u\right|}^{p}}{{\left|x\right|}^{p}}\;dx\leq\int_{\Omega}{\left|\nabla u\right|}^{p}\;dx

for all
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. The inequality holds also when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and it is trivial when
n=p
.

The Hardy inequality has received considerable attention because it finds applications in several fields. For example, it is related to Heisenberg’s
uncertainty principle

[
13
]
and, for
p=2
, it is useful in describing properties of Schrödinger operators with inverse square potentials
[
14
]
.
Further extensions of the inequality exist and the literature is broad. We refer readers to
[
1
,
8
,
11
,
16
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
for a general overview.

It is well-known that the constants in
Equations

1.1
and
1.2
are optimal, meaning that

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{p-1}{p}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(0,1)\\
u(0)=0\end{subarray}}\;\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{p}\;dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{-p}{\left|u\right|}^{p}\;dx}

\displaystyle\text{if }n=1

and

(1.3b)

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{n-p}{p}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\inf_{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\left|\nabla u\right|}^{p}\;dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\left|x\right|}^{-p}{\left|u\right|}^{p}\;dx}

\displaystyle\text{if }n\geq 2.

These infima are not attained, but one can easily construct minimizing sequences. For example, one can approximate the function
u(x)={\left|x\right|}^{(p-n)/p}
with functions in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
satisfying the correct boundary conditions.

In this work, we fix
p=2
and consider the problem of approximating the optimal Hardy constant using the finite element method.
Specifically, in dimension
n=1
, define the discrete Hardy constant as

(1.4)

S_{h}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V_{h}\\
v(0)=0\end{subarray}}\;\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}|v^{\prime}|^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}{\left|v\right|}^{2}\,dx}},

where
V_{h}
is the space of function in
H^{1}(0,1)
that are piecewise linear on a ‘triangulation’ of size
h
(see
Section

2
for a precise definition).
The approximation properties of
V_{h}
in
H^{1}(0,1)
guarantee that, as
h
decreases,
S_{h}
converges to the optimal value
1/4
of the minimization problem in
Equation

1.3a
for
p=2
. We prove that this convergence is logarithmic by establishing the following asymptotic expansion for
S_{h}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all sufficiently small triangulation size
h
,

S_{h}=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}+o\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

We also prove the same square logarithmic convergence, this time without the optimal prefactor, in
n\geq 3
dimensions when the domain
\Omega=B
is the unit ball. This restriction is justified because the Hardy constant is independent of the domain
\Omega
, and is convenient because then the minimization problem
Equation

1.3b
enjoys a rotational symmetry. In particular, the minimization can be restricted to functions
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(B)
depending only on the radial coordinate
r
. Thus, the optimal Hardy constant for
p=2
and dimension
n\geq 3
is

(1.5)

S^{n}:=\frac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{1}(0,1)\\
u(1)=0\end{subarray}}\;\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dr}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-3}{\left|u\right|}^{2}\,dr}}.

We define its discrete version as

(1.6)

S^{n}_{h}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V_{h}\\
v(1)=0\end{subarray}}\;\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-1}{\left|v^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dr}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-3}{\left|v\right|}^{2}\;dr}

and prove the following statement.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
n\geq 3
and every sufficiently small triangulation size
h
,

S^{n}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right)\leq S^{n}_{h}\leq S^{n}+\frac{(n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}{4{\left|\log(h)\right|}^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

Finally, in the special case of
n=3
dimensions, we prove a square logarithmic convergence rate for the discrete Hardy constant even when the rotational symmetry of the unit ball is not exploited. Precisely, let
V_{h}^{3}
be the space of functions in
H^{1}_{0}(B)
that are piecewise linear on a general triangulation of the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see
Section

2
for a precise definition) and recall from
Equation

1.5
that
S^{3}=1/4
. We establish the following estimates.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n=3
and let
V_{h}^{3}
be a triangulation of
B
of size
h
. There exists a positive constant
C
such that, for every sufficiently small triangulation size
h
,

\frac{1}{4}+\frac{C}{{\left|\log(h)\right|}^{2}}\leq\min_{v\in V_{h}^{3}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla v\right|}^{2}\;dx}{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|v\right|}^{2}dx}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}+o\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

The lower bound in this result holds in fact for any dimension
n\geq 3
, with the constant
\frac{1}{4}
replaced by the Hardy constant
S^{n}
and with a constant
C
that depends on
n
(see
Section

4.1
).

Estimating the convergence rates for numerical approximations of optimal constants for functional inequalities is not a new problem. For example, approximations of the optimal Poincaré constant were studied in
[
5
]
, while convergence rates for finite element approximations to the Sobolev constant were established in
[
2
]
. There is also a related literature on estimating eigenvalues of operators, see for instance
[
7
,
33
,
18
,
30
]
.
While each of these problems presents its own challenges for numerical analysis, one can categorize functional inequalities into four broad classes with increasing complexity:

(1)

Inequalities where the equality is attained by a smooth function. This is the case, for example, for the Poincaré inequality in smooth domains.

(2)

Inequalities where the equality is attained, but not by a smooth function. Examples in this class include Poincaré-type inequalities for elliptic operators in nonsmooth domains or with singular potentials.

(3)

Inequalities where the equality is attained only when the underlying domain is the full space. The Sobolev inequality analyzed in
[
2
]
belongs to this class.

(4)

Inequalities where the equality is not attained, even on the full space.

The Hardy inequality falls in the last class of problems and, as such, poses unique challenges. Indeed, to prove the upper bounds in
Theorems

1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
one can follow the strategy in
[
2
]
and apply finite element interpolation estimates to minimizing sequences for the problems in
Equations

1.3a
and
1.3b
. However, there are many possible minimizing sequences, so care must be taken to choose one with fast convergence properties. Finding lower bounds on the discrete Hardy constant is also not straightforward. In
[
2
]
, the gap between the Sobolev constant and its finite element approximation was estimated from below using a quantitative version of the Sobolev inequality from
[
15
]
, which estimates how far a function is from attaining equality.
Quantitative Hardy inequalities also exist (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
4
,
31
,
17
]
and
[
16
, Section 2.5]
) and a version due to Wang & Willem
[
32
]
suffices in dimension
n=3
to derive the lower bound in
Theorem

1.3
.
For the lower bounds in
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
, instead, we follow a strategy inspired by ‘calibration methods’ from the calculus of variations (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Section 1.2]
), which is slightly more involved but is particularly well-suited to the one-dimensional nature of the variational problems in
Equation

1.4
and
Equation

1.6
.
The idea, loosely speaking, is to add to the Hardy inequality terms that integrate to zero and make the inequality evident.
This strategy is known to produce sharp estimates for principal eigenvalues of elliptic operators and of the
p
-Laplacian in dimension
n=1
if
p
is an even integer
[
9
]
, and it has recently received attention in the optimization community because it lends itself to efficient numerical implementation
[
12
,
22
,
9
,
21
]
. Here, we use it to derive lower bounds for the discrete Hardy constant that not only show optimal dependence on the mesh size, but also exhibit an excellent quantitative agreement with computational results. The ability to produce explicit and accurate estimates is the main advantage of our ‘calibration’ approach compared to using a quantitative Hardy inequality.

2.
Finite Element Spaces

We start with a review of key notions about the finite element method. Readers are referred to
[
28
, Chapter 3]
and
[
29
]
for details. We work in dimension
n=3
, but all results carry over to dimension
n=1
upon replacing polyhedra with intervals.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a polyhedral domain (i.e., a finite union of polyhedra) and let
h>0
. A family
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of polyhedra is called a
triangulation
of
\overline{\Omega}
if

•

Every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a subset of
\Omega
with non-empty interior
\overset{\circ}{T}
;

•

\overset{\circ}{T}_{1}\cap\overset{\circ}{T}_{2}=\emptyset
for all
T_{1}\neq T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
;

•

If
T_{1}\neq T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
have
T_{1}\cap T_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then they share a common face, side or vertex;

•

{\rm diam}(T)\leq h
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

The vertices of the polyhedra in the triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
are called
interpolation nodes
.

We restrict our attention to
affine
triangulations, meaning that every element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is the image of a reference polyhedron
\hat{T}
under a
C^{1}
, invertible and affine map.
In particular, we will fix
\hat{T}
to be the unit simplex.
We also assume that the triangulations are
shape regular
, meaning that there exists a constant
\sigma>0
such that

\dfrac{h_{T}}{\rho_{T}}\leq\sigma\qquad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

where
\rho_{T}
is the radius of the largest ball inscribed in
T
and
h_{T}
is the diameter of
T
.
Finally, we impose that our meshes are
uniform
, meaning that we require
h/h_{T}
to be uniformly bounded in
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. As usual, for a given triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we set without loss of generality

\displaystyle{h:=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}}\,h_{T}.

In dimension
n=3
, let
B
be the open unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
B_{h}\subset B
be an open polyhedral approximation of
B
such that the boundary vertices of
\overline{B_{h}}
lie on
\partial B
and
{\left|B\setminus B_{h}\right|}\leq h^{2}
. Such a polyhedral domain
B_{h}
exists because
B
is smooth and convex.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a triangulation of
B_{h}
and denote by
V^{3}_{h}
the space of functions in
H^{1}_{0}(B)
that vanish on
B\setminus B_{h}
and whose restriction to each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is linear. In dimension
n=1
, we define the space
V_{h}
of continuous and piecewise linear functions on a triangulation (or
mesh
) of
B_{h}=B=(0,1)
in a similar way.

Next, we introduce the finite element interpolation operator.

Definition 2.2
.

The
interpolation operator

\Pi_{h}:C^{0}(\overline{B})\to V^{3}_{h}
maps any continuous function
f
to the continuous and piecewise linear function
\Pi_{h}f
satisfying
\Pi_{h}f(x_{i})=f(x_{i}),
where
x_{i}
are the interpolation nodes.

In dimension
n\leq 3
the interpolation operator is well-defined for every function in
H^{2}(B)
because this space embeds continuously into
C^{0}(\overline{B})
. The following result is a restatement of
[
29
, Theorem 5.1-4]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be an affine, uniform and shape regular triangulation of a polyhedral domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that, for every
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,

(2.1)

{\left\|\nabla(\Pi_{h}f-f)\right\|}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}h{\left\|D^{2}f\right\|}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

A similar estimate holds if the polyhedral domain
\Omega
is replaced by a
C^{\infty}
domain (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Lemma 5.2-3]
), except the
L^{2}
norm of
D^{2}f
must be replaced with the full
H^{2}
norm of
f
. For convenience, we recall this result only in the case of the ball
B
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n=3
. There exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that, for every
f\in H^{2}(B)
,

(2.2)

{\left\|\nabla f\right\|}_{L^{2}(B\setminus B_{h})}\leq C_{2}h{\left\|f\right\|}_{H^{2}(B)}.

Combining
Theorem

2.1
and
Lemma

2.2
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
n=3
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every
f\in H^{2}(B)
,

(2.3)

{\left\|\nabla(\Pi_{h}f-f)\right\|}_{L^{2}(B)}\leq Ch{\left\|f\right\|}_{H^{2}(B)}.

3.
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

This section is dedicated to proving
Theorem

1.1
. In
Section

3.1
, we use a calibration-type argument to establish the lower bound

(3.1)

S_{h}\geq\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{6+{\left|\log h\right|}}\right)^{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

The argument, although technical, is interesting because it reveals a nontrivial good minimizing sequence for the minimization in
Equation

1.3a
, which includes a sinusoidal term. We the interpolate a convenient approximation of this function in
Section

3.1
to establish the upper bound

(3.2)

S_{h}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{|\log h|-3\log|\log h|}\right)^{2}+o\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

This and
Equation

3.1
immediately imply the asymptotic expansion for
S_{h}
stated in
Theorem

1.1
.

Throughout this section, we shall assume for simplicity that the finite element space
V_{h}\subset H^{1}(0,1)
is based on a uniform mesh whose elements have equal length
h
. All of our arguments, however, extend immediately to spaces
V_{h}
defined using meshes with elements
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
that satisfy
ch\leq x_{i+1}-x_{i}\leq h
for some constant
c
independent of
h
. Indeed, it suffices to replace
h
with
ch
in all of our proofs and results.

3.1.
Proof of the lower bound

Let
U_{h}
be the space of functions in
H^{1}(0,1)
that vanish at
x=0
and are linear on
(0,h)
, but not necessarily on the rest of the interval
(0,1)
. Since the finite element space
V_{h}
is strictly contained in
U_{h}
, we have that

(3.3)

S_{h}>\mu_{h}:=\inf_{u\in U_{h}}\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}{\left|u\right|}^{2}\,dx}}.

This estimate, of course, is not expected to be sharp due to the strict gap between
V_{h}
and
U_{h}
. However, as stated in the next theorem, we can compute
\mu_{h}
exactly. This is enough to prove the lower bound in
Equation

3.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

There holds
\mu_{h}=1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
, where
\delta_{h}
solves

(3.4)

\frac{1}{4}+\delta_{h}\tan\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{2\delta_{h}}+\delta_{h}\log h\right)-\delta_{h}^{2}=0.

In particular, for
h\ll 1
we have

(3.5)

\mu_{h}=\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{6+{\left|\log h\right|}}\right)^{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

This result could be established by solving the optimality conditions for the minimization problem defining
\mu_{h}
in
Equation

3.3
. Here, however, we present an alternative strategy that applies in general and can produce estimates for
\mu_{h}
from below even when the associated optimality conditions cannot be solved analytically. To ease the presentation we break the argument into three steps, which correspond to
Lemmas

3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
below.
The first step is to prove the lower bound
\mu_{h}\geq 1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
when
\delta_{h}
solves
Equation

3.4
.

Lemma 3.2

(Lower bound on
\mu_{h}
)
.

Let
\delta_{h}
satisfy
Equation

3.4
. Then,
\mu_{h}\geq 1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
.

Proof.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, set

(3.6)

F_{\lambda}(u):=\int_{0}^{1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{2}-\lambda\frac{u^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx

and observe that

(3.7)

\mu_{h}=\max\left\{\lambda:\;F_{\lambda}(u)\geq 0\quad\forall u\in U_{h}\right\}.

Since every
u\in U_{h}
has the linear representation
u(x)=(x/h)u(h)
for
x\in(0,h)
, we can rewrite

F_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{h}\left(1-\lambda\right)u(h)^{2}+\int_{h}^{1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{2}-\lambda\,\frac{u^{2}}{x^{2}}\;dx.

We now use a calibration approach to find
\lambda
for which
F_{\lambda}(u)
is nonnegative irrespective of the choice of
u\in U_{h}
. Such a value
\lambda
is then a lower bound on
\mu_{h}
.

The idea is to add to
F_{\lambda}(u)
terms that sum to zero and that, at least for some carefully chosen value of
\lambda
, make the inequality
F_{\lambda}(u)\geq 0
evident. To this end, observe that if
\varphi
is any continuously differentiable function on
[h,1]
such that
\varphi(1)=0
, then the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

\int_{h}^{1}\left(\frac{\varphi(x)}{x}u^{2}\right)^{\prime}\,dx+\frac{1}{h}\varphi(h)u(h)^{2}=0.

After expanding the derivative inside the integral using the product and chain rules, we can add this expression to
F_{\lambda}(u)
without changing its value to obtain

(3.8)

F_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{h}\left[1-\lambda+\varphi(h)\right]u(h)^{2}+\int_{h}^{1}{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}^{2}+2\frac{\varphi}{x}uu^{\prime}+\left(x\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi-\lambda\right)\frac{u^{2}}{x^{2}}\;dx.

The inequality
F_{\lambda}(u)\geq 0
is satisfied if we can find
\varphi
and
\lambda
such that

(3.9a)

\displaystyle 1-\lambda+\varphi(h)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.9b)

\displaystyle x\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi-\lambda

\displaystyle\geq\varphi^{2}\quad\forall x\in[h,1],

(3.9c)

\displaystyle\varphi(1)=0.

Indeed, in this case we have

(3.10)

F_{\lambda}(u)\geq\frac{1}{h}\underbrace{\left[1-\lambda+\varphi(h)\right]}_{\geq 0}u(h)^{2}+\int_{h}^{1}\underbrace{\left(u^{\prime}+\frac{\varphi}{x}u\right)^{2}}_{\geq 0}\,dx,

which is manifestly nonnegative for every function
u\in U_{h}
.

There remains to find
\varphi
and
\lambda
that satisfy the three conditions in
Equation

3.9
. Fix
\delta>0
to be determined below and set

\lambda=\frac{1}{4}+\delta^{2}
.
If we require that
Equation

3.9b
be satisfied with equality, we obtain a differential equation with the boundary condition
\varphi(1)=0
, whose solution is given by

(3.11)

\varphi(x)=\delta\tan\left[\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)+\delta\log x\right]-\frac{1}{2}.

Note that this function is smooth on
[0,h]
for
\delta
small enough. Then, we substitute this function into
Equation

3.9a
and rearrange the inequality to obtain

(3.12)

\frac{1}{4}+\delta\tan\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{2\delta}+\delta\log h\right)-\delta^{2}\geq 0.

This inequality holds with equality when
\delta=\delta_{h}
is the solution of
Equation

3.4
. All conditions in
Equation

3.9
are then satisfied with equality. We conclude that
\lambda=1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
is feasible for the maximization problem in
Equation

3.7
, whence
\mu_{h}\geq\lambda=1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
.
∎

The second step is to derive an asymptotic expansion for
\delta_{h}
when
h\ll 1
.

Lemma 3.3

(Asymptotic expansion for
\delta_{h}
)
.

Let
\delta_{h}
solve
Equation

3.4
. For
h\ll 1
, we have the asymptotic expansion

(3.13)

\delta_{h}=\frac{\pi}{6+{\left|\log h\right|}}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}}\right).

Proof.

Rewrite
Equation

3.4
as

\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\delta_{h}}\right)-\delta_{h}{\left|\log h\right|}=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4\delta_{h}^{2}-1}{4\delta_{h}}\right),

where we have used the identity
\log h=-{\left|\log h\right|}
valid for
h\leq 1
. Anticipating that
\delta_{h}\ll 1
when
h\ll 1
, we can apply a Taylor expansion to find that

\frac{\pi}{2}+{2\delta_{h}}+o(\delta_{h})-\delta_{h}{\left|\log h\right|}=\frac{\pi}{2}+4\delta_{h}+o(\delta_{h}).

Solving for
\delta_{h}
gives
Equation

3.13
. The correctness of this expansion is confirmed by

.

The third and final step to prove
Theorem

3.1
is to complement the lower bound on
\mu_{h}
from
Lemma

3.2
with a matching upper bound.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\delta_{h}
satisfy
Equation

3.4
. Then,
\mu_{h}\leq 1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
.

Proof.

It suffices to find a function
u_{h}\in U_{h}
such that

(3.14)

\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|u_{h}^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}{\left|u_{h}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}=\frac{1}{4}+\delta_{h}^{2}.

Setting
\lambda=1/4+\delta_{h}^{2}
to ease the notation, this is equivalent to solving the equation
F_{\lambda}(u_{h})=0
where the functional
F_{\lambda}
is as in
Equation

3.6
. Since the value of
\lambda
was chosen to satisfy the conditions in
Equation

3.9
with equality, we find from
Equation

3.8
that

(3.15)

F_{\lambda}(u_{h})=\int_{h}^{1}\left(u_{h}^{\prime}+\frac{\varphi}{x}u_{h}\right)^{2}\,dx

for any
u_{h}\in U_{h}
.
We should therefore take
u_{h}
to solve the differential equation

(3.16)

u_{h}^{\prime}+\frac{\varphi}{x}u_{h}=0

on
(h,1)
, and extend it by a linear function to
(0,h)
while ensuring that
u_{h}(0)=0
. The differential equation
Equation

3.16
can be solved analytically if
\varphi
is as in
Equation

3.11
with
\delta=\delta_{h}
, giving

(3.17)

u_{h}(x)=\begin{cases}A\,\frac{x}{\sqrt{h}}\cos\!\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\delta_{h}}\right)+\delta_{h}\log h\right)&\text{for }x\in[0,h]\\[4.30554pt]
A\,\sqrt{x}\;\cos\!\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\delta_{h}}\right)+\delta_{h}\log x\right)&\text{for }x\in(h,1]\end{cases}

for an arbitrary normalization constant
A
. This function satisfies
Equation

3.14
by construction for any
A\neq 0
, which is the desired result.
∎

We conclude by remarking that
Lemma

3.4
is not required to obtain the lower bound on
S_{h}
stated in
Equation

3.1
: that result already follows from
Lemma

3.2
,
Lemma

3.3
, and inequality
Equation

3.3
. Nevertheless, the extra analysis is valuable because it provides functions
u_{h}\in U_{h}
that, as
h\to 0
, form a good minimizing sequence for the minimization problem defining the Hardy constant in
Equation

1.3a
. In the next section, we interpolate an approximation of
u_{h}
to estimate the discrete Hardy constant
S_{h}
from above with optimal errors.

3.2.
Proof of the upper bound

We now prove the upper bound in
Equation

3.2
. Recall that, in dimension
n=1
, the discrete Hardy constant
S_{h}
is the optimal value of the optimization problem in
Equation

1.4
. It therefore suffices to construct a function
v_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

(3.18)

\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|v_{h}^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}\,{v_{h}^{2}}\;dx}}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{|\log h|-3\log|\log h|}\right)^{2}+o\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

We will take
v_{h}
to be the piecewise linear interpolation of an element
v_{\varepsilon}
of a minimizing sequence
\{v_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
for the minimization problem in
Equation

1.3a
. The construction requires a suitable choice of
\varepsilon
as a function of the mesh size
h
and, most importantly, a good choice of
v_{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, there are many possible minimizing sequences
\{v_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
for
Equation

1.3a
, and not all converge at the same rate as
\varepsilon
tends to zero. The lower bound analysis of
Section

3.1
suggests one should define
v_{\varepsilon}
by replacing
h
with
\varepsilon
in
Equation

3.17
. To simplify the algebra in what follows, however, it will be more convenient to work with the function

(3.19)

v_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\in(0,\varepsilon),\\
\sqrt{x}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi\log x}{\log\varepsilon}\right),&x\in(\varepsilon,1),\end{cases}

which approximates the function in
Equation

3.17
for small
\varepsilon
. Crucially, this function is linear on the interval
(0,\varepsilon)
. If we choose
\varepsilon=mh
to be an interpolation node, therefore,
v_{\varepsilon}
coincides with its piecewise linear interpolation
\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}
on
(0,\varepsilon)
. On the interval
(\varepsilon,1)
, instead, we can estimate the error between
v_{\varepsilon}
and
\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}
using
Theorem

2.1
because
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(\varepsilon,1)
. This allows us to establish
Equation

3.18
for
v_{h}=\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}
and a suitable choice of
\varepsilon
.

We start by calculating the values of some norms of
v_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
\varepsilon<1
, the function
v_{\varepsilon}
in
Equation

3.19
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{v_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|v_{\varepsilon}(x)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|v_{\varepsilon}(x)^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}x^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{32}{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}+\frac{\pi^{4}}{2{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{3}}.

Proof.

By direct calculation.
∎

We will also use the following estimates, which relate a function
f\in H^{1}(0,1)\cap H^{2}(\varepsilon,1)
that vanishes on
[0,\varepsilon]
to its piecewise linear interpolation
\Pi_{h}f
on a mesh of size
h
when
\varepsilon=mh
is an interpolation node. The proof is analogous to that of
Lemma

A.6
in the appendix, so we do not report it for brevity.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varepsilon=mh\in(0,1)
be an interpolation node. Assume
f\in H^{1}(0,1)\cap H^{2}(\varepsilon,1)
vanishes on
[0,\varepsilon]
. Set

\mathcal{E}_{h}(f):=\frac{h}{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}x^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{h^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}x^{2}\,dx.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
f
,
h
and
\varepsilon
, such that

(3.20a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx+C\mathcal{E}_{h}(f)

(3.20b)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}{\left|\Pi_{h}f\right|}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}{\left|f\right|}^{2}\,dx-C\mathcal{E}_{h}(f).

We are now ready to prove that the piecewise linear function
v_{h}=\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}
satisfies
Equation

3.18
when
v_{\varepsilon}
is as in
Equation

3.19
and
\varepsilon
is a carefully chosen interpolation node. Precisely, set
\varepsilon=mh
for some integer
m
to be specified below and observe that
v_{\varepsilon}(x)=0
for all
x\in[0,\varepsilon]
. Then, we can apply
Equations

3.20a
and
3.20b
to estimate

(3.21)

\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|(\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon})^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}\,{|\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\;dx}}\leq\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|v_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx+C\mathcal{E}_{h}(v_{\varepsilon})}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}\,{|v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\;dx}-C\mathcal{E}_{h}(v_{\varepsilon})}

Using the calculations reported in
Lemma

3.5
we find that

\mathcal{E}_{h}(v_{\varepsilon})\lesssim\left(h\varepsilon^{-1}+h^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\right){\left|\log\varepsilon\right|},
so there exist a constant
C
, different from the one in
Equation

3.21
but still independent of
\varepsilon
and
h
, such that

(3.22)

\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|(\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon})^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}\,{|\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\;dx}}\leq\frac{\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}}+C\left(\dfrac{h^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\dfrac{h}{\varepsilon}\right)}{1-C\left(\dfrac{h^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\dfrac{h}{\varepsilon}\right)}.

Next, set
m=\lfloor|\log h|^{3}\rfloor
, so
\varepsilon=mh\sim h{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}
satisfies in particular
\varepsilon\leq h{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}
. With this choice we can estimate

{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}\geq{\left|\log\left(h{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}\right)\right|}^{2}=(|\log h|-3\log|\log h|)^{2}

and

\dfrac{h^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\dfrac{h}{\varepsilon}\sim\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{6}}+\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}}=o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

If we substitute these estimates into
Equation

3.22
and take
h\ll 1
, so we can apply the inequality
1/(1-z)\leq 1+2z
valid for
z\leq 1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}{\left|(\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon})^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2}\,{|\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\;dx}}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\dfrac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{|\log h|-3\log|\log h|}\right)^{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{|\log h|^{2}}\right)\right]\left[1+o\left(\frac{1}{|\log h|^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{|\log h|-3\log|\log h|}\right)^{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{|\log h|^{2}}\right).

This is precisely
Equation

3.18
for
v_{h}=\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}
, which implies the upper bound on
S_{h}
claimed in
Equation

3.2
.

We conclude by remarking that while the choice of
m
could in principle be optimized, this can only improve the terms that are asymptotically smaller than
1/{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}
when
h\ll 1
. The leading-order term
\pi^{2}/{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}
, instead, is optimal in light of the lower bound in
Equation

3.1
.

4.
Proof of
Theorem

1.3

We now turn to proving the upper and lower bounds from
Theorem

1.3
, which apply to the discrete Hardy constant in
n=3
dimensions for general triangulations of the unit ball. The main difference with the arguments for
n=1
dimensions is in the proof of the lower bound, which we present in
Section

4.1
: rather than following a calibration argument, we exploit a Hardy inequality with a logarithmic remainder term
[
32
]
. We note, however, that the proof of this inequality given in
[
27
, Section 2.5]
relies on a completion-of-the-square argument, very similar in spirit to what our calibration strategy achieves in
Equations

3.10
and
3.15
. We remark also that while in
Section

4.1
we fix
n=3
, our arguments immediately generalize to any dimension
n\geq 3
and yield

\min_{v\in V_{h}^{n}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla v\right|}^{2}\;dx}{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|v\right|}^{2}dx}\geq S^{n}+\frac{C_{n}}{{\left|\log(h)\right|}^{2}},

where
V_{h}^{n}
is the space of piecewise linear functions on a triangulation of the
n
-dimensional unit ball,
S^{n}=(n-2)^{2}/4
is the Hardy constant, and the constant
C_{n}>0
depends on
n
.

The upper bound part of
Theorem

1.3
, instead, is proved in
Section

4.2
with the same interpolation strategy used for
n=1
. For this we must fix
n=3
because the finite element interpolation estimates from
Theorem

2.3
are not valid in higher dimensions.

4.1.
Proof of the lower bound

We exploit the following Hardy inequality with a logarithmic remainder term. The statement, adapted from
[
27
, Theorem 2.5.2, p. 25]
, is a particular case of general quantitative Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg inequalities from
[
32
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(See
[
27
, Theorem 2.5.2]
)
.

Let
R>0
be such that
0\in\Omega\subset\overline{\Omega}\subset B_{R}(0)
. There exists a positive constant
K(n,R)
such that every
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
satisfies

(4.1)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\,dx-\left(\frac{n-2}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{\Omega}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\phi^{2}\,dx\geq K\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\left(\log\frac{|x|}{R}\right)^{-2}\,dx.

By density, this inequality holds for all
\phi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Because of the definition of space
V_{h}^{3}
in
Section

2
, we can take
R=2
and apply the above inequality to any function in
V_{h}^{3}
.

In particular, let
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{3}
be the function attaining the minimum in the definition of
S^{3}_{h}
, that is,

S^{3}_{h}=\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{B}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\,v_{h}^{2}\;dx}}.

Using inequality
Equation

4.1
for
n=3
we obtain

\displaystyle S^{3}_{h}-\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{B}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx}-\frac{1}{4}\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\,v_{h}^{2}\;dx}{\displaystyle{\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\,v_{h}^{2}\;dx}}

\displaystyle\geq K\;\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{B}{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{\left(\log\tfrac{|x|}{2}\right)^{-2}\,dx}}}{\displaystyle{\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\,v_{h}^{2}\;dx}}=K\,\dfrac{\displaystyle{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{\left(\log\tfrac{|x|}{2}\right)^{-2}}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\,v_{h}^{2}\;dx}}\;.

We claim that there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\left(\log\tfrac{|x|}{2}\right)^{-2}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{1}}{|\log h|^{2}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{1}}{|\log h|^{2}}\int_{B}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx.

Then, upon setting
C=KC_{1}
and using
Equation

1.3b
for
n=3
and
p=2
, we obtain

S^{3}_{h}-\frac{1}{4}\geq\frac{C}{|\log h|^{2}}\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{B}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx}}{\displaystyle{\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}\,v_{h}^{2}\;dx}}\geq\frac{C}{4|\log h|^{2}},

which immediately implies the lower bound in
Theorem

1.3
.

There remains to prove
Equation

4.2
.
Let
B_{h}
be the ball of radius
h
centered at the origin and observe that we can write
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\mathcal{T}_{h}^{1}\cup\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{h}^{1}

\displaystyle:=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\text{ such that }T\cap B_{h}=\emptyset\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\text{ such that }T\cap B_{h}\neq\emptyset\}.

For any
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{1}
,
x\in T
implies
|x|\geq h
, so

(4.3)

\int_{T}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx\geq\frac{1}{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}\int_{T}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx\qquad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{1}.

On the other hand, if
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}}\int_{T}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}}\left[\int_{T\cap B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx+\int_{T\setminus B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}}\int_{T\setminus B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx.

The integrals over
T\setminus B_{h}
can be estimated as before because
x\in T\setminus B_{h}
implies
{\left|x\right|}\geq h
, so

(4.4)

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}}\int_{T\setminus B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx\geq\frac{1}{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{2}}\int_{T\setminus B_{h}}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx.

As far as the integral over
B_{h}
, we apply the coarea formula and integrate by parts to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}\frac{1}{|\log(s/2)|^{2}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{s}}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}\right)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}\int_{B_{h}}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx-2\int_{0}^{h}\left(\int_{B_{s}}{\left|\nabla v_{h}\right|}^{2}\,dx\right)\frac{1}{{\left|\log(s/2)\right|}^{3}}\,ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}\int_{B_{h}}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx-2\int_{B_{h}}{\left|\nabla v_{h}\right|}^{2}\,dx\,\int_{0}^{h}\frac{1}{{\left|\log(s/2)\right|}^{3}}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}\left[1-2\int_{0}^{h}\frac{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}{{\left|\log(s/2)\right|}^{3}}\,ds\right]\int_{B_{h}}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx.

Now, since

\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{0}^{h}\frac{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}{{\left|\log(s/2)\right|}^{3}}\,ds=0

there exists a positive constant
C_{2}<1
such that

(4.5)

\int_{B_{h}}\frac{|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}}{|\log(|x|/2)|^{2}}\,dx\geq\frac{C_{2}}{{\left|\log(h/2)\right|}^{2}}\int_{B_{h}}|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}\,dx

for all sufficiently small
h
. We can now sum up the estimates
Equation

4.3
,
Equation

4.4
and
Equation

4.5
to arrive at the claimed inequality
Equation

4.2
. The lower bound in
Theorem

1.3
is therefore proved.

4.2.
Proof of the upper bound

To prove the upper bound from
Theorem

1.3
, it suffices to find a function
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{3}
such that

(4.6)

\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla v_{h}\right|}^{2}dx}{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|v_{h}\right|}^{2}dx}\leq\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{4\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}+o\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

As in
Section

3.2
, we will take
v_{h}=\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}
to be the piecewise linear interpolation of a function
v_{\varepsilon}
that is close to attaining the minimum in
Equation

1.3b
, where
\varepsilon
is a small parameter to be determined as a function of the mesh size
h
. We make here the particular choice

(4.7)

v_{\varepsilon}(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{{\left|x\right|}+\varepsilon}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi\log({\left|x\right|}+\varepsilon)}{\log\varepsilon}\right)-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi\log(1+\varepsilon)}{\log\varepsilon}\right).

The following result follows from direct calculations.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, the function
v_{\varepsilon}
defined in
Equation

4.7
belongs to
H^{1}_{0}(B)\cap H^{2}(B)
. In particular, for
\varepsilon\ll 1
we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\left|B\right|}}\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|v_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}{2}+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\left|B\right|}}\int_{B}{\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}}\right)+O\left(\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\left|B\right|}}\int_{B}\left({\left|x\right|}+\varepsilon\right)^{2}{\left|D^{2}v_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{9}{32}{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\log\varepsilon}\right).

We will also use the following estimates, which are similar to those in
Lemma

3.5
and
Lemma

A.5
. The proof is similar to that of
Lemma

A.5
, which is reported in the appendix, except that one must use the finite element interpolation estimates from
Theorem

2.3
instead of those in
Theorem

2.1
. The details are omitted for brevity.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every mesh size
h
and every function
f\in H^{1}_{0}(B)\cap H^{2}(B)
, set

\mathcal{E}_{h}(f):=h^{2}{\left\|f\right\|}^{2}_{H^{2}(B)}+h{\left\|f\right\|}_{H^{2}(B)}{\left\|\nabla f\right\|}_{L^{2}(B)}.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of both
f
and
h
, such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla\Pi_{h}f\right|}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}{\left|\nabla f\right|}^{2}dx+C\mathcal{E}_{h}(f),

\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|\Pi_{h}f\right|}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|f\right|}^{2}dx-C\mathcal{E}_{h}(f),

With these results in hand, it is relatively straightforward to show that the function
v_{h}=\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}\in V_{h}^{3}
satisfies
Equation

4.6
for a suitable choice of
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(h)
. Indeed, by
Lemma

4.2
, for every
\varepsilon>0
we can estimate

\displaystyle{\left\|v_{\varepsilon}\right\|}^{2}_{H^{2}(B)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx+\int_{B}{\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx+\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{B}\left({\left|x\right|}+\varepsilon\right)^{2}{\left|D^{2}v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}\right).

This, in turn, implies that
\mathcal{E}_{h}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq\left(h^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}+h\varepsilon^{-1}\right){\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}
.
Using
Lemma

4.2
,
Lemma

4.3
, and this last estimate we then find

\displaystyle\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx}{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx+C\mathcal{E}_{h}(v_{\varepsilon})}{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx-C\mathcal{E}_{h}(v_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}}+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}}\right)+C\left(\frac{h^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{h}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\displaystyle 1+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}}\right)-C\left(\frac{h^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{h}{\varepsilon}\right)}.

We now fix
\varepsilon=h{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}
and obtain

\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|\nabla\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx}{\displaystyle\int_{B}{\left|x\right|}^{-2}{\left|\Pi_{h}v_{\varepsilon}\right|}^{2}dx}\leq\dfrac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{\pi}{{\left|\log h\right|}-3\log{\left|\log h\right|}}\right)^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}}+h{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}\right)}{\displaystyle 1+O\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}}+h{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}\right)}.

Since

, this inequality implies
Equation

for all sufficiently small

1.3
.

6.
Open problems

We conclude with a list of open problems.

(1)

Determine the exact prefactor for the
O(1/{\left|\log(h)\right|}^{2})
correction to the asymptotic value of the discrete Hardy constant in
n\geq 3
dimensions.
Our analysis does not provide this because the leading-order corrections in the upper and lower bounds in
Theorem

1.2
do not match as the mesh size
h
tends to zero. The good quantitative agreement between the values of
S_{h}^{n}
computed numerically in
Section

5
and the lower bounds in
Equation

A.1
suggests that

S_{h}^{n}=S^{n}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log(h)\right|}^{2}}+o\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log(h)\right|}^{2}}\right).

Confirming this precisely, even with computer assistance, is challenging due to the slow decay of higher-order logarithmic corrections. If this prediction is correct, however, then one should be able to improve upper bound in
Equation

A.16
.

(2)

Generalize our estimates to the Hardy inequality with exponent
p\in(1,n)
. The calibration technique we employed has already been used to prove sharp lower bounds on the optimal constant for the one-dimensional Poincaré inequality in
W^{1,p}_{0}
when
p
is an even integer
[
9
]
. We wonder if those arguments carry over first to the Hardy inequality with exponent
p\neq 2
, and then to its finite element approximations.

(3)

Generalize our estimates to refinements of the Hardy inequality in dimension
n=2
, which was not considered here. In particular, we wonder if one can estimate the convergence rate of finite element approximations for logarithmic versions of the Hardy inequality (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
).

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

In this appendix, we prove the upper and lower bounds reported in
Theorem

1.2
, which apply to the discrete Hardy constant
S_{h}^{n}
defined in
Equation

1.3b
for any dimension
n\geq 3
. We follow essentially the same strategy used in
Section

3
for the case of
n=1
dimensions, but the computations are more involved. There is only one minor technical difference in the proof of the lower bound, which we point out explicitly.

A.1.
Proof of the lower bound

Let us first prove the lower bound part of
Theorem

1.2
. We shall in fact establish the lower bound

(A.1)

S_{h}^{n}\geq S^{n}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\left(\frac{8(n-1)}{n(n-2)}+h+{\left|\log h\right|}\right)^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right),

which is asymptotically equivalent to that in the theorem for
h\ll 1
. The proof follows the same strategy as in
Section

3.1
with only one difference: the space
U_{h}
in that section is replaced by the space
W_{h}
of functions in
H^{1}(0,1)
that vanish at
r=1
and that are linear both on
(0,h)
and on
(1-h,1)
. Since the space
W_{h}
contains the finite element space
V_{h}
used to define
S_{h}^{n}
, the inequality in
Equation

A.1
follows from a lower bound on

(A.2)

\mu_{h}^{n}:=\inf_{v\in W_{h}}\dfrac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-1}{\left|v^{\prime}\right|}^{2}\,dr}}{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-3}v^{2}\,dr}}.

We will compute this quantity using a calibration-type argument. To simplify the notation, let us introduce for every positive integer
m
the function

(A.3a)

f_{m}(h):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(1-h)^{2-n+k}.

Let us also set

(A.3b)

g_{n}(h):=f_{n}(h)-2f_{n-1}(h)+f_{n-2}(h).

Theorem A.1
.

Suppose
\delta_{h}
and
\gamma_{h}
solve

(A.4a)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n^{2}-2n}{2}-2\delta_{h}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\delta_{h}^{2}+n\delta_{h}\tan\left(\gamma_{h}+\delta_{h}\log h\right)\right)=\frac{n-2}{n-1}\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4}+\delta_{h}^{2}\right)^{2}

(A.4b)

\displaystyle\frac{n-2}{2}+\delta_{h}\tan\left(\gamma_{h}+\delta_{h}\log(1-h)\right)=\frac{1}{h}\left[f_{n}(h)-g_{n}(h)\left(\frac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}+\delta_{h}^{2}\right)\right].

Then,
\mu_{h}^{n}=S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
. In particular, for
h\ll 1
we have

(A.5)

\mu_{h}^{n}=S^{n}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\left(\frac{8(n-1)}{n(n-2)}+h+{\left|\log h\right|}\right)^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

This result is an immediate consequence of the following three lemmas. The first establishes the lower bound
\mu_{h}^{n}\geq S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
. The second provides an asymptotic expression for
\delta_{h}
when
h\ll 1
. The third shows that
\mu_{h}^{n}\leq S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
, from which we conclude that
\mu_{h}^{n}=S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
.

Lemma A.2
.

If
\delta_{h}
and
\gamma_{h}
solve
Equation

A.4
, then
\mu_{h}\geq S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
.

Proof.

Set

F_{\lambda}(v):=\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-1}{\left|v^{\prime}\right|}^{2}-\lambda r^{n-3}v^{2}\,dr

and observe that

(A.6)

\mu_{h}^{n}=\max\left\{\lambda:\;F_{\lambda}(v)\geq 0\quad\forall v\in W_{h}\right\}.

By definition of
W_{h}
, every
v\in W_{h}
satisfies

v(x)=\begin{cases}h^{-1}(h-r)\,v(0)+h^{-1}r\,v(h)&\text{if }r\in(0,h),\\
h^{-1}(1-r)\,v(1-h)&\text{if }r\in(1-h,1).\end{cases}

Moreover, every continuously differentiable function
\varphi(r)
on
[h,1-h]
satisfies

\int_{h}^{1-h}\left(r^{n-2}\varphi(r)\,v^{2}\right)^{\prime}\,dr=(1-h)^{n-2}\varphi(1-h)v(1-h)^{2}-h^{n-2}\varphi(h)v(h)^{2}

by the fundamental theorem of calculus.
Expanding the derivative under the integral using the chain rule, and using the piecewise linear representation of
v
, we can rewrite

(A.7)

F_{\lambda}(v)=\frac{h^{n-2}}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\begin{pmatrix}v(0)\\
v(h)\end{pmatrix}^{\top}M(n,h,\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}v(0)\\
v(h)\end{pmatrix}\\[4.30554pt]
+\int_{h}^{1}r^{n-1}|v^{\prime}|^{2}+2r^{n-2}\varphi(r)vv^{\prime}+\left[(r^{n-2}\varphi)^{\prime}-\lambda\right]v^{2}\;dx\\[4.30554pt]
+h^{n-3}\left[f_{n}(h)-g_{n}(h)\lambda-h\varphi(1-h)\right]v(1-h)^{2},

where the functions
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
are as in
Equation

A.3
and

M(n,h,\lambda):=\begin{pmatrix}(n-1)(n-2)-2\lambda&(n-2)(1-n-\lambda)\\
(n-2)(1-n-\lambda)&(n-1)(n-2)\left(1-\lambda+n\varphi(h)\right)\end{pmatrix}.

The right-hand side of
Equation

A.7
is nonnegative if the matrix
M(n,h,\lambda)
is positive semidefinite, the integrand in the second line is a square, and the coefficient of the last term is nonnegative. This is true if
\varphi
and
\lambda
satisfy

(A.8a)

\displaystyle(n-1)(n-2)-2\lambda\geq 0,

(A.8b)

\displaystyle(n-1)\left[(n-1)(n-2)-2\lambda\right]\left[1-\lambda+n\varphi(h)\right]\geq(n-2)(1-n-\lambda)^{2},

(A.8c)

\displaystyle{f_{n}(h)}-g_{n}(h)\lambda-h\varphi(1-h)\geq 0,

(A.8d)

\displaystyle\left(r^{n-2}\varphi\right)^{\prime}-\lambda=r^{n-3}\varphi^{2}.

To satisfy these four conditions, we set

\lambda=S^{n}+\delta^{2}
for some
\delta
to be determined below, and solve the differential equation in
Equation

A.8d
to find

\varphi(r)=\frac{n-2}{2}+\delta\tan\left(\gamma+\delta\log r\right),

where
\gamma
is an integration constant. Then, we substitute this function into
Equations

A.8a
,
A.8b
and
A.8c
. After some rearrangement, we conclude that
\delta
and
\gamma
should satisfy

(A.9a)

\displaystyle n(n-2)-4\delta^{2}\geq 0,

(A.9b)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n^{2}-2n}{2}-2\delta^{2}\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\delta^{2}+n\delta\tan\left(\gamma+\delta\log h\right)\right)\geq\frac{n-2}{n-1}\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4}+\delta^{2}\right)^{2},

(A.9c)

\displaystyle\frac{n-2}{2}+\delta\tan\left(\gamma+\delta\log(1-h)\right)\leq\frac{1}{h}\left[f_{n}(h)-g_{n}(h)\frac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}-g_{n}(h)\delta^{2}\right].

These inequalities hold with equality if
\delta=\delta_{h}
and
\gamma=\gamma_{h}
. The choice
\lambda=S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
is thus feasible for the maximization problem in
Equation

A.6
, so
\mu_{h}\geq S^{n}+\delta_{h}^{2}
.

(A.13a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr+C\mathcal{E}_{h}(f),

(A.13b)

r\in[0,\varepsilon]
. We can therefore estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr=

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}+\left(\Pi_{h}f-f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr+\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f-f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr

(A.14)

\displaystyle+2\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f-f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We now use the bounds
\varepsilon\leq r\leq 1
and the interpolation inequality
Equation

2.1
on the interval
(\varepsilon,1)
, which is valid for every
f\in H^{2}(\varepsilon,1)
, to further estimate
Equation

A.14
as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr+c^{2}h^{2}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}dr

\displaystyle+2ch\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr+c^{2}h^{2}\varepsilon^{-(n+1)}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n+1}dr

\displaystyle+2ch\varepsilon^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n+1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This implies
Equation

A.13a
for any constant
C\geq C_{1}:=\max\{2c,c^{2}\}
. This constant can be taken to be independent of
\varepsilon
because the constant
c
in the interpolation inequality
Equation

2.1
is an increasing function of the diameter of the integration domain
[
29
]
, and can therefore be replaced by a larger constant (also denoted by
c
) independently of
\varepsilon
.

Next, we derive inequality
Equation

A.13b
using similar arguments. We start from the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\Pi_{h}f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr\geq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr-\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\Pi_{h}f-f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr

(A.15)

\displaystyle-2\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\Pi_{h}f-f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
f(1)=0
by assumption, then both
f
and
\Pi_{h}f-f
satisfy the Hardy inequality with optimal constant
S^{n}
. Using this inequality, and the fact that
\Pi_{h}f=f
in
(0,\varepsilon)
by assumption, we can further estimate
Equation

A.15
as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|\Pi_{h}f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr\geq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f\right|}^{2}r^{n-3}dr-S^{n}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f-f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr

\displaystyle-2S^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\left|f^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}{\left|\left(\Pi_{h}f-f\right)^{\prime}\right|}^{2}r^{n-1}dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Applying to this inequality the same estimates used on
Equation

A.14
yields
Equation

A.13b
for any constant
C\geq C_{2}:=\max\{2S^{n}c,S^{n}c^{2}\}
, which may be again chosen to be independent of
\varepsilon
. Estimates
Equations

A.13a
and
A.13b
clearly hold simultaneously for
C=\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.
∎

Finally, we establish the following refinement of the upper bound on
S_{h}^{n}
from
Theorem

1.2
, which is asymptotically equivalent to the latter when
h\ll 1
.

Theorem A.7
.

For every
n\geq 3
and all sufficiently small mesh sizes
h
,

(A.16)

S_{h}^{n}\leq\dfrac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}+\left(\dfrac{(n+1)\pi}{2{\left|\log h\right|}-6\log{\left|\log h\right|}}\right)^{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon=mh
be an interpolation node.
We can then follow exactly the same steps as in
Section

3.2
, except that we let
v_{\varepsilon}
be defined as in
Equation

A.12
and replace the results in
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.6
with those in
Lemmas

A.5
and
A.6
. We obtain

S^{n}_{h}\leq\dfrac{\dfrac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}+\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{{\left|\log\varepsilon\right|}^{2}}+C\left(h\varepsilon^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}+h^{2}\varepsilon^{-(n+1)}\right)}{1-C\left(h\varepsilon^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}+h^{2}\varepsilon^{-(n+1)}\right)}.

for some constant
C
independent of both
h
and
\varepsilon
.
We now recall that
\varepsilon=mh
and set

m=\left\lfloor h^{\frac{1-n}{n+1}}\,{\left|\log h\right|}^{\frac{6}{n+1}}\right\rfloor.

This gives
\varepsilon\sim h^{2/(n+1)}{\left|\log h\right|}^{6/(n+1)}
and, in particular,
\varepsilon\leq h^{2/(n+1)}{\left|\log h\right|}^{6/(n+1)}
. We can thus find another constant, also denoted by
C
and independent of
h
, such that

S^{n}_{h}\leq\dfrac{\dfrac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}+\left(\dfrac{(n+1)\pi}{2{\left|\log h\right|}-6\log{\left|\log h\right|}}\right)^{2}+C\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}}+\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{6}}\right)}{1-C\left(\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{3}}+\dfrac{1}{{\left|\log h\right|}^{6}}\right)}.

This inequality implies
Equation

A.16
when the mesh size
h
is sufficiently small.
∎
