Preconditioners for computing multiple solutions in three-dimensional fluid topology optimization Thanks: Submitted DATE.
Ioannis P. A. Papadopoulos, Patrick E. Farrell

Abstract
Topology optimization problems generally support multiple local minima, and real-world applications are typically three-dimensional. In previous work [I. P. A. Papadopoulos, P. E. Farrell, and T. M. Surowiec, Computing multiple solutions of topology optimization problems , SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing, (2021)], the authors developed the deflated barrier method, an algorithm that can systematically compute multiple solutions of topology optimization problems. In this work we develop preconditioners for the linear systems arising in the application of this method to Stokes flow, making it practical for use in three dimensions. In particular, we develop a nested block preconditioning approach which reduces the linear systems to solving two symmetric positive-definite matrices and an augmented momentum block. An augmented Lagrangian term is used to control the innermost Schur complement and we apply a geometric multigrid method with a kernel-capturing relaxation method for the augmented momentum block. We present multiple solutions in three-dimensional examples computed using the proposed iterative solver.



keywords

topology optimization, multiple solutions, deflated barrier method, preconditioning, multigrid

Introduction

Topology optimization has become a prominent tool in engineering design.
The objective is to find the optimal distribution of a continuum within a given domain that minimizes a problem-specific cost functional, without prior knowledge of the shape or topology of the solution
[
11
]
.

Topology optimization problems are generally nonconvex and can support multiple local minima. A major challenge in topology optimization is to identify multiple local minimizers, so that the best one (in performance, manufacturability, or aesthetics) may be chosen. Continuation in model parameters is often used to promote convergence to “better” local minima. However, Stolpe and Svanberg
[
65
]
show that such methods can be ineffective even on the simplest of topology optimization models.

In recent work, Papadopoulos et al.
[
51
]
developed an algorithm, called the deflated barrier method, that can systematically discover
multiple
stationary points of topology optimization problems formulated using a density approach.
The deflated barrier method combines the techniques of barrier methods
[
33
,
34
,
36
,
60
,
61
,
67
,
70
]
, primal-dual active set solvers
[
12
]
, and deflation
[
26
,
27
]
. In one example
[
51
, Fig. 1]
, the authors found 42 stationary points of a fluid topology optimization problem constrained by the Navier–Stokes equations in a rectangular domain with five small holes removed. However, an application of the deflated barrier method requires the solution of many linear systems similar to those solved in an all-at-once (simultaneous analysis and design, SAND) approach. In
[
51
]
, a sparse LU factorization was used to solve these linear systems, which is not generally affordable for three-dimensional problems.

In this work, we develop preconditioners for the linear systems arising in the deflated barrier method when applied to the model proposed by Borrvall and Petersson
[
15
]
. The goal of this model is to minimize the power dissipation of a fluid in Stokes flow, subject to a volume constraint restricting the proportion of the domain that the fluid can occupy. The preconditioners we develop make it feasible to identify multiple solutions to the Borrvall–Petersson problem in three dimensions with the deflated barrier method.
A number of strategies have been proposed for the solution of three-dimensional topology optimization of fluid flow in other contexts
[
2
,
3
,
4
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

We choose a discontinuous Galerkin

[

2
Topology optimization of Stokes flow

Given a volume constraint on a fluid in a fixed Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, the Borrvall–Petersson model attempts to minimize the energy lost by the flow due to viscous dissipation, whilst maximizing the flow velocities at the applied body force. More precisely, the objective is to find
(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)\in H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g},\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}\times C_{\gamma}
that minimizes

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\alpha(\rho)|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\nu|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}-2\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\right)\text{d}x,

(BP)

where
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the velocity of the fluid,
\rho:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the material distribution of the fluid and

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g}}(\Omega)^{d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}:\boldsymbol{v}|_{\partial\Omega}=\boldsymbol{g}\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g},\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g}}(\Omega)^{d}:\mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{v})=0\;\text{a.e.\ in}\;\Omega\},

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\eta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega):0\leq\eta\leq 1\;\text{a.e.},\;\;\int_{\Omega}\eta\;\text{d}x\leq\gamma|\Omega|\right\}.

Here,
H^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle(u,v)_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}uv+\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x,

(1)

which induces the norm
\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\coloneqq(v,v)_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{1/2}
.
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
denotes the vector space of essentially bounded measurable functions equipped with the essential supremum norm, i.e.
\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\coloneqq\inf\{c\geq 0:|\eta(x)|\leq c\;\text{for a.e.}\;x\in\Omega\}
. Furthermore,
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
is a body force,
\nu>0
is the (constant) viscosity, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the volume fraction. The restriction,
|_{\partial\Omega}
, is to be understood in the boundary trace sense
[
24
, Ch. 5.5]
. Moreover, the boundary data
\boldsymbol{g}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, with
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Gamma)>0
, i.e.
\Gamma
has nonzero Hausdorff measure on the boundary. Borrvall and Petersson introduced the inverse permeability term,
\alpha
, which models the influence of the material distribution on the flow. For values of
\rho
close to one,
\alpha(\rho)
is small, permitting fluid flow; for values of
\rho
close to zero,
\alpha(\rho)
is very large, restricting fluid flow. The function
\alpha
satisfies the following properties:

(A1)

\alpha:[0,1]\to[\underline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha}]
with
0\leq\underline{\alpha}<\overline{\alpha}<\infty
;

(A2)

\alpha
is monotonically decreasing;

(A3)

\alpha(0)=\overline{\alpha}
and
\alpha(1)=\underline{\alpha}
;

(A4)

\alpha
is twice continuously differentiable,

(A5)

\alpha
is strongly convex,

generating an operator also denoted
\alpha:C_{\gamma}\to L^{\infty}(\Omega;[\underline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha}])
. Typically, in the literature,
\alpha
takes the form
[
15
,
25
]

\displaystyle\alpha(\rho)=\bar{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\rho(q+1)}{\rho+q}\right),

(2)

where
q>0
is a penalty parameter, so that
\lim_{q\to\infty}\alpha(\rho)=\bar{\alpha}(1-\rho)
. Borrvall and Petersson
[
15
, Sec. 3.2]
remark that as
q\to\infty
the material distribution tends to a 0-1 solution.

The following existence theorem is due to Borrvall and Petersson
[
15
, Th. 3.1]
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a Lipschitz domain, with
d=2
or
d=3
and
\alpha
satisfies properties
(A1)
–
(A5)
. Then, there exists a pair
(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)\in H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g},\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)\times C_{\gamma}
that minimizes
J
(as defined in
eq.

BP
).

3
The deflated barrier method

In this section, we summarize how the deflated barrier method finds multiple solutions of fluid topology optimization problems.

3.1
Forming the barrier functional

The first step in the algorithm is to form a barrier-like objective from the original objective functional. This is both to aid convergence and to provide an opportunity for deflation to discover multiple local minima (described in Section
3.2
).
Consider the minimization problem: find the minimizers,
(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)\in H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g},\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}\times C_{\gamma}
, of

\displaystyle J_{\mu}^{\epsilon_{\text{log}}}(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)\coloneqq J(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)-\mu\int_{\Omega}\log(\rho+\epsilon_{\text{log}})+\log(1+\epsilon_{\text{log}}-\rho)\mathrm{d}x,

(BF)

where
\mu\geq 0
is the barrier parameter and
0<\epsilon_{\text{log}}\ll 1
. We note that the box constraints imposed by the barrier-like terms are never active as
0\leq\rho\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
. A similar result to one shown by Evgrafov
[
25
, Sec. 4]
guarantees the existence of a minimizer to
eq.

BF

[
50
, Prop. 4.2]
. As with the original optimization problem,
eq.

BP
, the minimizer is not necessarily unique.

The deflated barrier method targets the first-order optimality conditions of this problem, which we derive next.
Define the following forms to be used in stating the first-order optimality conditions:

\displaystyle c(\rho,\eta;\boldsymbol{u},\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime}(\rho)|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}-\frac{\mu}{\rho+\epsilon_{\text{log}}}+\frac{\mu}{1+\epsilon_{\text{log}}-\rho}+\lambda\right]\eta\;\mathrm{d}x,

(3)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v};\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\alpha(\rho)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\;\mathrm{d}x,\quad l(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\;\mathrm{d}x,

(4)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u},p)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\int_{\Omega}p\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\;\mathrm{d}x,\quad d(\lambda,\zeta;\rho)\coloneqq-\zeta\int_{\Omega}(\gamma-\rho)\mathrm{d}x.

(5)

The proof of the following proposition, concerning the first-order optimality conditions of
eq.

BF
, follows from the result in
[
50
, Prop. 2.5]
.

Proposition 2
.

Fix
\mu\geq 0
,
0<\epsilon_{\mathrm{log}}\ll 1
and suppose that
(\boldsymbol{u},\rho)\in H^{1}_{\boldsymbol{g},\mathrm{div}}(\Omega)^{d}\times C_{\gamma}
is a strict local or global minimizer of
eq.

BF
. Then, there exist unique Lagrange multipliers
p\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, such that, for all
(\eta,\boldsymbol{v},q,\zeta)\in C_{[0,1]}\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{R}
, the following necessary first-order optimality conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle c(\rho,\eta-\rho;\boldsymbol{u},\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(6)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v};\rho)+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)

\displaystyle=l(\boldsymbol{v}),

(7)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u},q)

\displaystyle=0,

(8)

\displaystyle d(\lambda,\zeta;\rho)

\displaystyle=0.

(9)

Here
C_{[0,1]}\coloneqq\cup_{\gamma\in[0,1]}C_{\gamma}
, i.e. we relax the volume constraint on the variation in the material distribution.

Under suitable conditions,
eq.

6
–
eq.

9
can be reformulated as a complementarity problem. After a suitable discretization, throughout the deflated barrier method, the nonlinear complementarity problems are solved using the Benson–Munson (BM) algorithm
[
12
]
, a Newton-like algorithm that can enforce the true box constraints on
\rho
. A thorough description of this strategy is given in
[
51
, Sec. 3.1]
and
section

4.2
. We emphasize that the barrier-like terms are
not
used to enforce box constraints, which are handled by BM, and the enlarged box constraints
[-\epsilon_{\text{log}},1+\epsilon_{\text{log}}]
are never active for any
\mu\geq 0
. The barrier-like terms are included solely to aid nonlinear convergence via continuation, and to find multiple solutions via deflation.

3.2
Deflation

Deflation is a mechanism to systemically discover multiple solutions of a nonlinear system with a Newton-like algorithm
[
26
,
27
]
. Let
Z
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Consider the nonlinear system
F(z)=0,

F:Z\to Y
that has the solutions
z_{1},\dots,z_{l}
. Given a suitable initial guess, a Newton-like algorithm might converge to the solution
z_{1}
. Now, deflation modifies the nonlinear system in order to remove the known solution whilst retaining undiscovered solutions. This is done via a deflation operator
\mathcal{M}(z;z_{1}):Y\to Y
that ensures that
\mathcal{M}(z;z_{1})F(z)
has a root if the original problem
F(z)=0
has an unknown solution, and ensures that a Newton-like method applied to the newly deflated system does not converge to
z=z_{1}
.
In this work, the following shifted deflation operator
[
26
]
is used

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{z}_{1})=\left(\frac{1}{\|\rho-\rho_{1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}+1\right)\mathcal{I},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{z}=(\rho,\boldsymbol{u},p,\lambda)
and
\mathcal{I}:Y\to Y
is the identity operator. Deflation can be very efficiently implemented; the BM update of the deflated system can be expressed as a (nonlinear) scaling of the BM update of the
undeflated
system evaluated at the same iterate
[
26
, Sec. 3]
. This implementation detail is crucial for this work as it implies that preconditioning strategies for the undeflated BM systems can be immediately applied to the deflated systems. A discussion of how the nonlinear scaling is cheaply computed is given in
[
51
, Sec. 3.2]
.

3.3
Prediction, continuation, and deflation

The deflated barrier method
[
51
, Alg. 3.1]
is an iterative procedure consisting of three steps. The algorithm is initialized with barrier parameter
\mu=\mu_{0}
and the idea is to follow branches (a generalization of the central path) of solutions as
\mu\to 0
. At iteration
k
of the deflated barrier method, the barrier functional subproblem at
\mu=\mu_{k}
is constructed. In the
prediction step
, a cheap initial guess is computed for the barrier functional subproblem from the solution on the same branch at
\mu=\mu_{k-1}
. In the
continuation step
, the BM solver is used to find a solution to the first-order optimality conditions
eq.

6
–
eq.

9
at
\mu=\mu_{k}
, using the initial guess computed in the prediction step, whilst deflating away all other known solutions at
\mu=\mu_{k}
. In the
deflation step
, we search for new branches of solutions at
\mu=\mu_{k}
, using solutions discovered at
\mu=\mu_{k-1}
as initial guesses. The deflation step is terminated if a pre-determined number of branches have already been found or the BM solver fails to converge (by reaching a specified number of iterations without converging). The algorithm terminates when the first-order optimality conditions have been solved (for multiple solutions) at a barrier parameter value of zero.

Discretization and linearization

4.1
Finite element discretization

The linear systems that arise in the deflated barrier method will be tied to our choice of finite element discretization. In this work, we choose a discontinuous Galerkin (DG)
k
th-order
\mathrm{BDM}_{k}\times\mathrm{DG}_{k-1}

[
16

17
]

\mathcal{T}_{h}
denote a shape-regular triangulation of the domain
\Omega
. We define the spaces
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{g}}}
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega),\boldsymbol{v}|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(T)^{d}\,\forall\,T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

(11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{g}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}:(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{g})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega\},

(12)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}
denotes the set of polynomials of order
k
. The degrees of freedom for two dimensions are given in
[
17
, Sec. 2]
and for three dimensions in
[
16
, Sec. 2]
. Similarly,
X_{\mathrm{DG}_{k-1}}
denotes the set of discontinuous piecewise
(k-1)
-th order polynomials:

X_{\mathrm{DG}_{k-1}}\coloneqq\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):v|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k-1}(T)\,\forall\,T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

(13)

Some finite element methods for fluid flow, such as the Taylor–Hood finite element pair, do not satisfy the incompressibility constraint
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0
pointwise. Failure to satisfy the incompressibility constraint pointwise has been observed to support instabilities that result in nonphysical solutions
[
43
,
46
]
. In the BDM finite element pair,
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}\subset X_{\mathrm{DG}_{k-1}}
. Therefore, for any solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
satisfying the incompressibility constraint
eq.

8
, we have that
\|\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
. Hence, the solution is pointwise divergence-free.

The finite element space for the material distribution is denoted by
C_{[0,1],h}
and is defined by

\displaystyle C_{[0,1],h}\coloneqq\{\eta_{h}\in X_{\mathrm{DG}_{0}}:0\leq\eta_{h}\leq 1\},

(14)

where
X_{\mathrm{DG}_{0}}
is the set of piecewise constant finite element functions. As the test functions of

take the value of one in their respective element and are zero elsewhere, in practice we discretize the material distribution with

Since the discontinuous velocity space is not

–

\displaystyle[\![\boldsymbol{\phi}]\!]_{F}=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{+}\otimes\boldsymbol{n}_{F}^{+}+\boldsymbol{\phi}^{-}\otimes\boldsymbol{n}_{F}^{-}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\!\!\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Phi}}\}\!\!\}_{F}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Phi}}^{+}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Phi}}^{-}\right).

\displaystyle c(\rho_{h},\eta_{h}-\rho_{h};\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\lambda_{h})

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h};\rho_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})

\displaystyle=l_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),

\displaystyle=0.

4.2
The Benson–Munson linear system

The BM solver
[
12
]
attempts to find a solution of a complementarity problem via linearizations of the residual constrained to the inactive set. First, the discrete Newton system is formed and the active set is defined. The active set contains the degrees of freedom that satisfy a strict complementarity condition in the primal and residual vectors. Next, the rows and columns of the Jacobian in the Newton system associated with the active set degrees of freedom are set to those of the identity. Finally, the rows on the right-hand side vector associated with the active set degrees of freedom are fixed to zero. Once the update,
\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
, of this modified system is computed, the new iterate
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{k}+\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
is component-wise projected onto the box constraints.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consider the box constraints
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Consider the mixed complementarity problem given by

either

\displaystyle\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\;\;

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i}=0,

(23)

or

\displaystyle\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\;\;

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i}\geq 0,

(24)

or

\displaystyle\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\;\;

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i}\leq 0.

(25)

We define
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}:\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\;i=1,\dots,n\}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows, for
i=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})]_{i}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i}&\text{if}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\\
\min\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i},0\}&\text{if}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i},\\
\max\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i},0\}&\text{if}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}.\end{cases}

(26)

We denote the component-wise projection onto the true box constraints by
\pi

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})_{i}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}&\text{if}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}<\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i},\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}&\text{if}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}&\text{if}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{i}>\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{i}.\end{cases}

(27)

Consider a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and the subset of indices

. Then, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{S,S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is defined by zeroing the rows and columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
in

and placing a 1 on the diagonal of the zeroed rows and columns. Similarly for any column vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is constructed by zeroing the rows in the set
\{1,\dots,n\}\backslash S

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
.

The proof of the invertibility of

5
Preconditioning

In this section, we develop a preconditioner for solving

A direct sparse LU factorization of the matrix in

eq.

28
and the barrier-like terms in
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
. In the following subsections we introduce a nested block preconditioning method for solving
eq.

28
, where the Schur complements are controlled with an augmented Lagrangian term.
As outermost Krylov solver, we use a preconditioned FGMRES method
[
59
]
.

We precondition (

\mathbb{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
,
\mathbb{B}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}})
,
\mathbb{C}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}})^{\top}
and,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}\end{pmatrix}.

(39)

We know that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
is invertible by
proposition

7

\mathbb{A}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}
.
The matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}
resembles the linear system that arises in the discretization of the Stokes–Brinkman equations.
The Schur complement is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}-\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

The reason we use a

\mathrm{DG}_{0}

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
is a diagonal matrix,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}
is also sparse.

Thus far, the application of block preconditioning has reduced the solution of the full matrix
eq.

28
to the following:

1.

Apply an outer FGMRES solver;

2.

Apply the reciprocal of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
;

3.

Invert the diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
;

4.

Apply the inverse of the
2\times 2

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}

For now we assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}
is invertible and defer discussions of invertibility to later.

We must now develop solvers for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}
as given in
eq.

40
. One option is to use a direct solver. However, we can further reduce the computational work with another application of block preconditioning. Consider taking the inner Schur complement in
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}
with respect to the pressure block. Using the notation of
eq.

35
,
\mathbb{B}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}
,
\mathbb{C}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
, and
\mathbb{A}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
. The innermost Schur complement takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}\coloneqq-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}.

(41)

This time,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}
is dense, and we employ an augmented Lagrangian approach.

We first recall some known results for the Stokes and Stokes–Brinkman equations. Let
-\Delta_{h}
denote the negative discretized Laplacian matrix. In the context of the incompressible Stokes equations,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}(-\Delta_{h})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}
is spectrally equivalent to the viscosity-scaled pressure mass matrix
\nu^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}

[
63
,
68
]
, see also
[
22
, Th. 5.22]
.
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
is a sparse mass matrix and can be cheaply factorized or solved with a multigrid method. Therefore, a good approximation to the Schur complement of the pure Newtonian Stokes problem is given by
\nu^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
. The idea is that the momentum block can then be solved with a direct solver, multigrid methods, or other alternative solvers. Unfortunately, in the context of the Stokes–Brinkman equations, Popov
[
54
]
noted that the presence of the Brinkman term
\alpha(\rho_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
in the momentum block
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
renders the approximation given by
\nu^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
ineffective. Popov proposed a Schur complement preconditioning technique based on incomplete LU factorization, but such factorizations do not generally yield mesh-independent preconditioners. An alternative is a preconditioning scheme utilized by Borrvall and Petersson in their original paper
[
15
, Sec. 2.6]
based on the work of Cahouet and Chabard
[
19
]
. However, during numerical experiments, we found that an augmented Lagrangian approach performed better for the problems in this work, see
[
50
, Ch. 5.3.1]
.

We now propose an augmented Lagrangian strategy to control the second Schur complement
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}
. The essential idea is to add a term to the momentum equation that does not change the exact solution, but does change the Schur complement
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}
; in particular, by scaling the augmentation appropriately,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}
can be almost perfectly approximated with a scaled pressure mass matrix. The cost of this approach is that it makes the augmented momentum block more difficult to solve. The augmented Lagrangian approach has been shown to be robust for a variety of difficult saddle-point systems such as the stationary Navier–Stokes equations at high Reynolds number
[
31
]
, implicitly-constituted anisothermal non-Newtonian flow
[
32
]
, and magnetohydrodynamics
[
45
]
. Hence, this approach has potential for extension to different fluid topology optimization problems.

There are two possible augmented Lagrangian approaches: continuous and discrete. These approaches are mathematically equivalent for exactly divergence-free elements such as the one employed here. We choose to introduce the method in the discrete setting. Post-discretization, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
in
eq.

28
is
modified by adding an augmented Lagrangian term

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}},

(42)

where
\gamma_{d}\gg 0
, and the right-hand side of
eq.

28
is modified so that the solution of linear system remains unchanged (since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
is known). In particular, if the current velocity iterate is divergence-free, then
\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
and no modification to the right-hand side is required. While it does not change the solution, the addition of the augmented Lagrangian term influences the nature of the inner Schur complement. In particular,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1}
becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1,\gamma_{d}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}\end{pmatrix},

(43)

and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}.\end{split}

(44)

Remark 12
.

As noted in
remark

11
, it is possible that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
is indefinite and, therefore, it is unclear if
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1,\gamma_{d}}
is invertible. If we were to encounter such a case, then the BM linear system solve would fail. Here, the deflated barrier method would repeatedly halve the step size in the barrier parameter
\mu
(keeping the initial guess the same). We did not observe this behaviour in our numerical experiments. Henceforth, we assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1,\gamma_{d}}
is invertible. We note that if
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{1,\gamma_{d}}
is invertible, then
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
is invertible. This is seen by applying Sylvester’s law of inertia to the Schur complement decomposition
eq.

36

[
22
, Ch. 4]
.

The action of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
is required during the solve. As already mentioned,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
is dense and, hence, it is expensive to assemble and apply its inverse. In the next proposition we show that the action of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
can be approximated with
-\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}^{-1}_{p}
with increasing accuracy as
\gamma_{d}\to\infty
. In practice, values of
\gamma_{d}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{5})
were sufficient to approximate the action of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
with
-\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}^{-1}_{p}
to tolerances of
\mathcal{O}(10^{-10})
.

Proposition 13
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
are invertible and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
has been modified to ensure it has full rank (see
remark

8
). Consider the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\;\;i=1,\dots,n_{p}.

(45)

Then,
\lambda_{i}\to-1
,
i=1,\dots,n_{p}
as
\gamma_{d}\to\infty
.

Proof.

We note that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
is symmetric positive-definite and, therefore, invertible. Our assumptions satisfy the requirements of
[
14
, Lem. 4.1]
and thus

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}_{2,\gamma_{d}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}_{2}+\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}.

(46)

By left multiplying
eq.

45
by
\gamma_{d}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
then
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
and applying
eq.

46
we see that

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{d}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}\right]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{i}.

(47)

As
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
is invertible, then
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for all
i=1,\dots,n_{p}
. Hence, by rearranging
eq.

47
we observe that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=\gamma_{d}(\lambda_{i}^{-1}+1)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{i}.

(48)

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
is invertible, its eigenvalues (denoted
\delta_{i}
) are nonzero, and satisfy

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=\gamma_{d}(\lambda_{i}^{-1}+1),\;\;i=1,\dots,n_{p}.

(49)

Therefore, by taking the limit
\gamma_{d}\to\infty
we conclude that
\lambda_{i}\to-1
for all
i=1,\dots,n_{p}
.

If assembled naïvely, the triple matrix product
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
, as it occurs in the augmented Lagrangian term, is expensive to compute. However, it can be verified that the augmented Lagrangian term
\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
corresponds to augmenting the weak form
a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h};\rho_{h})
in
eq.

19
by

\displaystyle\gamma_{d}\int_{\Omega}\Pi(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\Pi(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\mathrm{d}x,

(50)

where
\Pi
is the projection onto the discretized pressure space. The projection is the identity for the BDM-DG pair. Therefore, assembling
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\top}
is equivalent to assembling the matrix associated with the bilinear form
\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\mathrm{d}x
, where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n_{\boldsymbol{u}}
, are the basis functions of the velocity finite element space.

With the proposed nested block preconditioning, we have reduced solving linear systems involving
eq.

28

1.

Apply an outer FGMRES solver;

2.

Apply the reciprocal of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
;

3.

Invert the diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
;

4.

Factorize and solve the block-diagonal pressure mass matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
;

5.

Apply the action of the inverse of the augmented momentum block
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
.

Factorizing
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
with a direct solver such as MUMPS
[
6

is faster than factorizing the full matrix in
eq.

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}
only needs to be factorized once at the start of the algorithm. With ideal inner solvers, most of the computational time during the run of the deflated barrier method is spent on factorizing
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
at each BM iteration.

5.2
A specialized multigrid scheme for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}

As already mentioned, the tradeoff for using an augmented Lagrangian term to control the Schur complement
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}
is that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
becomes difficult to solve, due to the semi-definite term with a large coefficient
\gamma_{d}\gg 0
. In the past two decades, there has been progress on specialized multigrid schemes, based on the pioneering work of Schöberl
[
62
]
, to handle the effects of the augmented Lagrangian term in
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}
. Strategies based on Schöberl’s work have been shown to be extremely effective in parameter-robust preconditioning of the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations
[
30
,
31
]
, Oseen–Frank models of cholesteric liquid crystals
[
71
]
, implicitly-constituted non-Newtonian incompressible flow
[
28
,
32
]
, and magnetohydrodynamics
[
45
]
. Related multigrid schemes have also been analyzed in the context of the
\boldsymbol{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{H}(\mathrm{curl};\Omega)
Riesz maps
[
7
]
and, more relevant to our problem, an
\boldsymbol{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
-conforming discretization of the Stokes equations
[
40
]
.

In this work, we construct a mesh hierarchy and construct our multilevel hierarchy geometrically. The coarse-level operators are induced from
eq.

18
–
eq.

21
via rediscretization. Moreover, in all our examples, we choose a direct solver for the coarse-level solver. Schöberl’s analysis gives sufficient conditions on the relaxation method and transfer operators to achieve robustness in the context of multigrid cycles applied to symmetric positive-definite problems augmented with a parameter-dependent positive semi-definite term. The first is that the relaxation method must stably capture the kernel of the semi-definite term. The second requirement is that the prolongation operator must have a continuity constant that is independent of
\gamma_{d}
. As noted by Hong et al.
[
40
, Sec. 1]
, in a nested mesh hierarchy, an exactly divergence-free function on the coarse-grid will be divergence-free on the fine-grid. Therefore, in our context, the natural prolongation operator suffices thanks to our choice of discretization, and we only discuss the relaxation method in this work. The kernel of the semi-definite term involving
\gamma_{d}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}:(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\;\text{for all}\;\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}\},

(51)

i.e. all functions with divergence zero. For large
\gamma_{d}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}
becomes increasingly singular. Common relaxation methods like Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel do not offer
\gamma_{d}
-robust smoothing and yield ineffective multigrid cycles. To understand the degradation of Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel as
\gamma_{d}\to\infty
, it is fruitful to view the relaxation method as a subspace correction method
[
72
,
73
]
. Consider the space decomposition

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathrm{BDM}_{k}}=\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i},

(52)

where the sum is not necessarily direct. A subspace correction method solves for an approximation of the error in each subspace, and combines them (additively or multiplicatively) to form an updated guess for the solution. In the classical Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel iterations, the decomposition,
eq.

52
, is given by
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}\}
where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n_{\boldsymbol{u}}
, are the velocity basis functions. The difference between Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel is whether the updates are applied additively (Jacobi) or multiplicatively (Gauss–Seidel).

A sufficient condition for the subspace correction method induced by the decomposition
eq.

52
to be robust in
\gamma_{d}
for a symmetric positive-definite matrix, is that the decomposition captures the kernel
\mathcal{N}_{h}
in the following sense
[
14
,
62
,
31
,
40
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{h}=\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\cap\mathcal{N}_{h}.

(53)

In other words, the decomposition must be sufficiently rich so that any divergence-free velocity can be written as a combination of divergence-free functions from the subspaces
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
1
1

1

In addition, the decomposition must be stable, but we do not elaborate here.
. Jacobi fails this criterion, as each
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}
is not divergence-free. A decomposition satisfying
eq.

53
for the BDM-DG discretization was developed by Hong et al.
[
40
, Sec. 4.5]
, where the decomposition is the so-called
star
patch around every vertex of the mesh. This decomposition is visualized in
fig.

3
for a
\mathrm{BDM}_{1}
discretization in two dimensions. The same topological decomposition extends to higher orders and three dimensions. Since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
is not guaranteed to be positive-definite, the theory does not guarantee robust convergence. Nevertheless, we find that a small number of FGMRES iterations preconditioned with the vertex-star patch iteration is very effective as a smoother, as reported in
[
31
]
and subsequent works.

Injecting the active set

A complication arises in the representation of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
on the coarser levels. By first ignoring the BM active set, we note that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}_{\gamma_{d}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
can be assembled by injecting the current finite element iterates,
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
and
\rho_{h}
, to the relevant level on the mesh hierarchy, assembling the submatrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
, applying the Dirichlet boundary conditions of the injected velocity to the relevant rows and columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
, and subtracting the triple matrix product
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{\top}
from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
. The triple matrix product is sparse and cheap to compute as
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{\mu}
is diagonal on all levels. However, we found that an accurate representation of the active set on the coarser levels is essential for the convergence of the multigrid scheme. This is similar to other experiences reported in the literature
[
23
,
41
]
. Hence, the difficulty lies in defining the active set on the coarser levels. An obvious choice is to use the definitions of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{I}
in
algorithm

1
defined via the injected material distribution iterate. However, in numerical experiments we found that this choice resulted in poor iteration counts.

Consider a two-grid method with the fine-level triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
h=H/2
, obtained by a uniform refinement of the simplices in coarse-level triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{H}
. As the material distribution is discretized with
\mathrm{DG}_{0}
elements, each degree of freedom
i
associated with the fine-level material distribution iterate can be associated with an element
K_{h}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
in the fine level and analogously with the degrees of freedom of the coarse-level material distribution iterate with elements in the coarse level. We say that a fine-level element
K_{h}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is in the active set
\mathcal{A}_{h}
(written as
K_{h}\in\mathcal{A}_{h}
) if the degree of freedom associated with
K_{h}
is in the active set
\mathcal{A}_{h}
. This definition naturally extends to the coarse-level elements and active sets.

We now utilize an idea inspired by the work of Hoppe
[
41
]
and Engel and Griebel
[
23
]
to define the coarse-level active sets. A coarse-level element,
K_{H}\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
containing the parent fine-level elements
K_{h,1},\dots,K_{h,s}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is defined to be in the coarse-level active set
\mathcal{A}_{H}
if

\displaystyle|\{K_{h,j}\in\mathcal{A}_{h}:j=1,\dots,s\}|\geq m,

(54)

where
m\in[1,s]
and
s=4
in two dimensions and
s=8
in three dimensions. In other words, the coarse-level element is in the coarse-level active set if it contains
m
or more fine-level parent elements that are in the fine-level active set. By starting at the finest-level active set that is defined by
algorithm

1
, we recursively define all the active sets in mesh hierarchy via
eq.

54
. Experiments revealed that a good choice for
m
is
m=s/2
, i.e. a coarse-level element is active if at least half of its parent fine-level elements are active. A summary of the multigrid strategy is given in
fig.

4
.

5.2
to solve

.

Remark 14
.

The choice of
eq.

54
is more generous than utilizing the definition of the fine-level active set directly with the injected material distribution iterate. In particular, some coarse cells that are “borderline” between the active set and inactive set are placed in the active set by
eq.

54
but in the inactive set when defining the active set using the injected material distribution. Numerically, we see that the iteration counts suffer if the criteria for a coarse cell to be in the coarse-level active set are too strict.

We see that the Krylov iterations per BM iteration are robust to the mesh size for both preconditioning strategies. The preconditioning strategy with an LU factorization for the augmented momentum block
(aL1)

[
51

\gamma_{d}=0

eq.

28

(aL2)

section

A natural question is the sensitivity of the solver with respect to the augmented Lagrangian parameter
\gamma_{d}
. We wish to explore the following:

(H1)

The effect of roundoff error as
\gamma_{d}
increases;

(H2)

The improvement of the approximation of
-\gamma_{d}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}_{p}^{-1}
for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}_{2,\gamma_{d}}^{-1}
;

(H3)

The robustness of the vertex-star patch multigrid cycle for increasing
\gamma_{d}
.

An unfortunate side-effect of increasing
\gamma_{d}
is the expectation of high roundoff error. This would manifest in the nonlinear solver stagnating at higher residual norm values than desired. However, as
\gamma_{d}
increases, we expect that the Schur complement approximation by the pressure mass matrix improves as shown in
proposition

13
. Hence, the number of outer Krylov FGMRES iterations per BM iteration should decrease as
\gamma_{d}\to\infty
. Finally, the multigrid cycle should be robust to the value of
\gamma_{d}
. Hence, we expect that the number of inner Krylov FGMRES iterations preconditioned by the multigrid cycle to approximate the inverse of the augmented momentum block should stay roughly constant with increasing
\gamma_{d}
. In
table

3
we test these hypotheses.

We investigate
(H1)
and
(H2)
via the
(aL1)
solver (with varying
\gamma_{d}
). We fix a mesh with mesh size
h=0.0361
and choose the discretization used previously. We consider the deflated barrier method subproblem at
\mu=105
and
\mu=1
for the first branch. The nonlinear solves are terminated when the decrease in the residual norm stagnates. The outer FGMRES solver’s absolute and relative tolerances are set to
10^{-15}
and
10^{-5}
, respectively. The middle four columns confirm our first two hypotheses. At
\gamma_{d}=10^{7}
we achieve the optimal number of outer FGMRES iterations per BM iteration. The approximation of the Schur complement with the pressure mass matrix in near-perfect. However, we note that for each increase in the order of magnitude after
\gamma_{d}=10^{2}
, the roundoff error causes the nonlinear solver to stagnate at an order of magnitude higher. We believe a good compromising choice is
\gamma_{d}=10^{4}
.

(H3)
we utilize the

Conclusions

In this work we extended the application of the deflated barrier method to discovering multiple three-dimensional solutions of the fluid topology optimization model of Borrvall and Petersson
[
15
]
. This was achieved by developing preconditioners for the linear systems that arise in the deflated barrier method. The preconditioning strategy reduces the discretized
4\times 4
-block matrix solve to an outer FGMRES method and the solve of one diagonal matrix, one block-diagonal matrix (that can be factorized once or quickly solved with multigrid), and an augmented momentum block. Moreover, we develop a geometric multigrid cycle for the augmented momentum block that consists of a specialized relaxation method and a characterization of the active set on coarser levels. We found that the preconditioner is robust to the mesh size and successfully computed three solutions of a 3D cross-channel problem and seven solutions of a 3D five-holes quadruple-pipe problem.
