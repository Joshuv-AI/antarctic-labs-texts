Multivariate rank via entropic optimal transport: sample efficiency and generative modeling
Shoaib Bin Masud, Matthew Werenski, James M. Murphy, Shuchin Aeron

Abstract
The framework of optimal transport has been leveraged to extend the notion of rank to the multivariate setting while preserving desirable properties of the resulting goodness-of-fit (GoF) statistics. In particular, the rank energy (RE) and rank maximum mean discrepancy (RMMD) are distribution-free under the null, exhibit high power in statistical testing, and are robust to outliers. In this paper, we point to and alleviate some of the practical shortcomings of these proposed GoF statistics, namely their high computational cost, high statistical sample complexity, and lack of differentiability with respect to the data. We show that all these practically important issues are addressed by considering entropy-regularized optimal transport maps in place of the rank map, which we refer to as the soft rank . We consequently propose two new statistics, the soft rank energy (sRE) and soft rank maximum mean discrepancy (sRMMD) , which exhibit several desirable properties. Given n n sample data points, we provide non-asymptotic convergence rates for the sample estimate of the entropic transport map to its population version that are essentially of the order n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} when the starting measure is subgaussian and the target measure has compact support. This result is novel compared to existing results which achieve a rate of n − 1 n^{-1} but crucially rely on both measures having compact support. We leverage this result to demonstrate fast convergence of sample sRE and sRMMD to their population version making them useful for high-dimensional GoF testing. Our statistics are differentiable and amenable to popular machine learning frameworks that rely on gradient methods. We leverage these properties towards showcasing the utility of the proposed statistics for generative modeling on two important problems: image generation and generating valid knockoffs for controlled feature selection.

Introduction

It is well-known that in one dimension, the notions of rank and quantile with respect to the distribution of the data are naturally defined via the cumulative distribution function (cdf) and its generalized inverse, respectively. This is because
\mathbb{R}
has a canonical ordering, which is naturally captured by the cdf. Based on these notions, a number of goodness-of-fit (GoF) statistics and tests have been proposed in the literature, such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
[
1
]
, Wilcoxon rank test
[
2
]
, and more recently rank-quantile tests
[
3
]
. These rank- and quantile-based statistics are computationally feasible, non-parametric, and are distribution-free under the null, making them desirable in a number of applications, e.g. unsupervised change point detection
[
4
]
, tests of independence
[
5
,
6
]
, and hierarchical clustering
[
7
]
.

Recently, meaningful multivariate extensions of the notion of rank and quantile maps were proposed in the pioneering works
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, and more recently in
[
11
]
based on the theory of optimal transportation
[
12
,
13
]
. For a detailed discussion, we refer the reader to a recent survey on the topic
[
14
]
and references therein. Essentially, these ideas leverage the convex geometry of the optimal transport (OT) problem with the squared Euclidean metric as the ground cost, where under some mild conditions the optimal maps are gradients of strictly convex functions
[
15
,
16
]
. These extensions and the corresponding high-dimensional analogues of the rank-based GoF statistics based on these extensions retain some of the useful properties of their one dimensional counterparts, namely they are computationally feasible for small sample sizes and are distribution-free under the null.

In this paper we focus on the multivariate rank-based GoF statistic proposed in
[
11
]
, namely the rank energy and rank maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), based on a particular choice of the reference measure when defining the multivariate ranks via optimal transport maps. These statistics are shown to be distribution-free in finite samples (under the null), consistent against alternatives, exhibit high power in statistical testing for heavy-tailed distributions, and are robust to outliers.

However, as we discuss in detail in Section
2.2.2
, practical use of the rank energy and rank MMD suffer from the well-known curse of dimensionality associated with the estimation of OT maps as well as high computational costs for large sample sizes. Furthermore, rank energy and rank MMD suffer from a lack of differentiabilty in the data, limiting a direct use of iterative gradient-based optimization methods. This inhibits their use for learning a generative model by using these GoF statistics as a loss function, as has been successfully done with MMD and Wasserstein distances
[
17
,
18
]
.

In this context, our paper makes the following main contributions.

(C1)

We introduce the multivariate notion of the
soft rank
that exploits the recent developments in computational optimal transport, namely entropic regularization
[
19
]
. We then propose the
soft rank energy (sRE)
, a new multivariate GoF statistic, and the related
soft rank maximum mean discrepancy (sRMMD)
. In Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
, we establish the properties of soft rank energy and its convergence to rank energy, which together suggest its use for measuring GoF in two-sample testing.

(C2)

We provide a novel result (Theorem
2
) on the convergence rate of a sample-driven estimate for general entropic maps, from a subgaussian measure to a measure with a compact support, that enjoys a convergence rate of
n^{-1/2}
to the population entropic map, even in high dimensions. We note that the subgaussian assumption on the starting measure is a significant weakening of the assumptions compared to a recent result of
[
20
]
that assumes compactness of both measures albeit providing a faster rate of
n^{-1}
. In this context we also note that our method of proof is different from
[
20
]
, which relies on strong concavity furnished by the compactness assumption on both measures. Specifically, we adapt the methodology developed in
[
21
]
and use empirical process theory arguments to establish our result under the weakened assumptions.

(C3)

Since the target measure in defining multivariate ranks has a compact support, we utilize the previous result in Theorems
4
and
5
to establish the statistical convergence of sample sRE and sample sRMMD, respectively, to their population versions with rate
n^{-1/2}

(C4)

We show the practical advantages of our proposed methods on several real generative modeling problems. First, we use sRE and sRMMD as the loss functions in a simple generative model architecture to produce MNIST-digits. Under an appropriate choice of the entropic regularization parameter, we show that using sRE and sRMMD as the loss functions can generate all of the digits successfully, and does not suffer from mode collapse. We then utilize the sRMMD in a deep generative model in order to produce valid knockoffs
[
22
]

Background on Optimal Transport and Rank Energy

Throughout this work we consider measures contained in
\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\Omega)
, the space of absolutely continuous measure on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
i.e. those characterized by a density function with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We will assume throughout that
\Omega
is bounded and contained in the ball
B_{2}^{d}(0,r)
.

2.1
Optimal Transport

Given two distributions
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, where
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
denotes the space of Borel probability measures on
\Omega
, the
Monge problem

[
23
]
seeks a measurable map
T:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
that pushes
P
to
Q
with a minimal cost. Precisely, it solves

\inf_{T}\int\frac{1}{2}\|x-T(x)\|^{2}dP(x),\;\;\text{subject to}\;\;T_{\#}P=Q,

(1)

where
T_{\#}P
denotes the
push-forward measure
, which satisfies
(T_{\#}P)[A]=P[T^{-1}(A)]
for all measurable sets
A
.

Throughout we will make heavy use of the optimal map
T
which minimizes (
1
). It is therefore important to establish the existence, uniqueness, and important properties of
T
, which are established by the following celebrated theorem.

Theorem 1

(Brenier-McCann
[
15
,
16
]
)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\Omega)
and
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. Then there exists a convex function
\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose gradient
\nabla\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
pushes
P
forward to
Q
. Moreover, if
P
and
Q
have finite second moments, then
\nabla\phi
is the unique (up to sets of measure 0) solution to the Monge problem (
1
).

2.1.1
Entropic Optimal Transport

Towards developing the notion of soft rank energy we first state a relaxation of the
Monge problem
, where instead of a map, one seeks an optimal “coupling”
\pi
between a source distribution
P
and a target distribution
Q
. The
Kantorovich relaxation

[
24
]
solves

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\int\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y),

(2)

where
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of joint probability measure on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega\otimes\Omega)
with marginals
P
and
Q
. When a solution to the Monge problem
1
exists, then the solution to Kantorovich relaxation coincides with it in the sense that the optimal plan is concentrated on
\{(x,T(x)):x\in\text{supp}(P)\}

[
24
]
.

The statistic we propose relies on an
entropy-regularized
version of (
2
). For
\varepsilon>0
, the primal formulation of the entropy-regularized optimal transport is given by

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\int\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}\text{d}\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\text{KL}(\pi\ ||\ P\otimes Q),

(3)

where

\text{KL}(\pi|P\otimes Q)\triangleq\int\ln\left(\frac{d\pi(x,y)}{dP(x)dQ(y)}\right)d\pi(x,y).

This problem has been extensively studied both for its theoretical properties, as well as for the efficient algorithms that are used to solve it (See
[
25
,
19
,
26
]
and references therein).
Importantly, (
3
) admits the following dual formulation that follows from the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality theorem
[
27
]
, a derivation of which may be found in
[
28
,
19
]

\displaystyle\max_{f,g}\int f(x)dP(x)+\int g(y)dQ(y)-\varepsilon\int\int\exp\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f(x)+g(y)-(1/2)\|x-y\|^{2}\right)\right]dP(x)dQ(y)+\varepsilon,

(4)

where the maximization is over the pairs
f\in L^{1}(P),g\in L^{1}(Q)
of functions over
\Omega
. The optimal entropic potentials for
\varepsilon
are the pair of functions
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
which achieve the maximum in (
4
). Furthermore, there is an optimality relation between (
3
) and (
4
) given by

d\pi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\exp\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}\right)\right]dP(x)dQ(y),

(5)

where
\pi_{\varepsilon}
denotes the solution to (
3
). We emphasize the fact that
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is not a map, but a diffused coupling, and that the degree of diffusion depends on the entropic regularization parameter
\varepsilon

[
19
]
. In the finite sample setting, entropic regularized optimal transport significantly reduces computational complexity
[
25
]
and also yields a differentiable loss function
[
29
]
.

2.2
Rank and Quantile Maps

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider a random variable
X\sim P
. When
d=1
, the rank map, or cumulative distribution function, is
F_{X}(t)=\mathbb{P}\{X\leq t\}
, and the quantile map is its generalized inverse,
F_{X}^{-1}(p)=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:p\leq F_{X}(t)\}
. The rank and quantile maps are always monotonic increasing and are continuous when
P
has a density. When
F_{X}
is continuous one can show that the random variable
F_{X}(X)
is distributed according to
\text{Unif}([0,1])
. Similarly, when the quantile map is continuous
F_{X}^{-1}(U)
is distributed according to
X
where
U\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
.

The key insight in using the theory of optimal transport to define multivariate rank and quantile maps comes from noticing that in one dimension, the optimal map betwwen two measures,
P
and
Q
, is given by
T=F_{Q}^{-1}\circ F_{P}
, where
F_{P}
is the rank map of
P
and
F_{Q}^{-1}
is the quantile map of
Q

[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. When
Q=\text{Unif}([0,1])
, one has
F_{Q}^{-1}=\text{Id}
and therefore
F_{Q}^{-1}\circ F_{P}=\text{Id}\circ F_{P}=F_{P}
. By the push-forward constraint we know that
F_{P}\#P=\text{Unif}([0,1])
which is just one way of observing that
F_{P}(X)\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
. The main intuition is that the rank map is exactly the optimal map from
P
to
\text{Unif}([0,1])
. Analogously, with Theorem
1
ensuring the existence of a unique optimal
T
that is monotone (being a gradient of a convex function),
[
11
]
generalizes the notion of rank and quantile maps to dimensions

as optimal transport maps to and from
\text{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
.

(Definition 2.1
[

]
)

Let

\mathtt{R}=\nabla\phi
and
\mathtt{Q}=\nabla\phi^{*}

is the

convex function as in Theorem

denotes the standard convex conjugate

2

for any proper function

f^{*}(y)\triangleq\sup_{y}\langle x,y\rangle-f(y)
for all

of the convex function
\phi

With the high-dimensional analogue of the rank defined, we can now state the candidate GoF statistics proposed in
[

Definition 2

(Definition 3.2
[
11
]
)
.

Let
P_{X},P_{Y}\sim\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\Omega)

be independent. Let
P_{\lambda}=\lambda P_{X}+(1-\lambda)P_{Y}

\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}
be the multivariate rank map of

as in Definition

. The
(population) rank energy
is defined

\displaystyle\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq C_{d}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathbb{P}\big(a^{\top}\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X)\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(a^{\top}\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y)\leq t\big)\right)^{2}dtd\kappa(a)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y)||-\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X^{\prime})||-\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y^{\prime})||,

(6)

where
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\triangleq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\kappa(\cdot)
is the uniform measure on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, and
C_{d}=\left(2\Gamma(d/2)\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\pi}(d-1)\Gamma\big((d-1)/2\big)
is an appropriate normalizing constant.

Note that
\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}^{2}
closely resembles the definition of energy distance
[
30
]
,

\text{En}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\triangleq C_{d}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathbb{P}\big(a^{\top}X\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(a^{\top}Y\leq t\big)\right)^{2}dtd\kappa(a)

a widely used GoF statistic in two-sample testing. This definition is motivated by the continuity and uniqueness of characteristic functions. Namely,
P_{X}=P_{Y}
if and only if
a^{\top}X\overset{d}{=}a^{\top}Y
(that is, equality in distribution) for
\kappa
-almost everywhere
a
; indeed, the integration over the sphere aggregates the discrepancy in characteristic functions in every direction. For a discussion showing the equivalence of the formulation in (
2
) using integrals over
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and the formulation in terms of expectations of
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}
, see
[
31
]
. One advantage of
\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}^{2}
over the energy distance is that
\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}^{2}
is distribution-free under the null for all sample sizes
[
11
]
.

One can generalize the rank energy by replacing the pairwise distance with a kernel function
[
32
]
. Given
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a characteristic kernel
3
3

3

a kernel
k
is said to be characteristic if the map
P\rightarrow\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\cdot,x)dP(x)
is injective, where
P
is a measure defined on the topological space
\mathcal{X}
.
, we can interpret this distance as
rank maximum mean discrepancy
,

\displaystyle\mathtt{RMMD}_{\lambda}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}[k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}[k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y)].

(7)

Note that
\mathtt{RMMD}_{\lambda}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}
closely follows the definition of maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
[
33
]
, which is a widely used statistic in the framework of two-sample testing:
\mathtt{MMD}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[k(X,X^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}[k(X,Y)].

One can simply view
\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}^{2}
and
\mathtt{RMMD}_{\lambda}^{2}
as the “rank-transformed” energy distance and maximum mean discrepancy, respectively.

In practice one can rarely evaluate the
\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}^{2}
and
\mathtt{RMMD}_{\lambda}^{2}
directly since the exact multivariate rank

often unavailable. Instead the statistic must be estimated from samples, a procedure outlined in the next subsection.

2.2.1
Sample Rank Map and Rank Energy

Given i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\sim P
, the empirical measure is defined as
P^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}}
where
\delta_{X_{i}}
is a Dirac distribution placed at
X_{i}
. Given two empirical measures
P^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}},Q^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{Y_{j}}
, where
Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{m}\sim Q
, the plug-in estimate of the transport map
T^{m}
between
P
and
Qf
is obtained by solving (
1
) between
P^{m},Q^{m}

T^{m}\triangleq\argmin_{T:T_{\#}P^{m}=Q^{m}}\int\frac{1}{2}||x-T(x)||^{2}dP^{m}(x)=\argmin_{T:T_{\#}P^{m}=Q^{m}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}||X_{i}-T(X_{i})||^{2}.

(8)

This problem can be converted to a standard linear program and solved either by tailored methods or general linear program solvers
[
19
]
. To obtain the sample rank map for a measure
P
, one performs the procedure above with
Q=\text{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
and takes
\mathtt{R}^{m}
to be the estimate
T^{m}
. In practice, one may generate the samples from
\text{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
using a pseudo-random sequence of points. In
[
11
]
, Halton sequences
[
34
]
are used and we do the same in our experiments. Nevertheless, any sequence which weakly converges to
\text{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
can be used.

With the sample rank map defined, one can immediately extend the definition of the rank energy. Given two sets of samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\sim P_{X}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim P_{Y}
, define
P^{m+n}
as

P^{m+n}=\frac{1}{m+n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{j}}\right),

the empirical mixture of the two sets of samples. Next, let
Q^{n+m}=\frac{1}{m+n}\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}\delta_{U_{i}}
where
U_{i}\sim\text{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
. Using these measures, one obtains
\mathtt{R}^{m+n}
and then computes the sample rank energy as

\displaystyle\mathtt{RE}_{m,n}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\triangleq\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\|\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{j})\|-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\|\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{j})\|

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\|\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{i})-\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{j})\|.

(9)

The authors in
[
11
]
also define the sample version of rank maximum mean discrepancy as

\displaystyle\mathtt{RMMD}_{m,n}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k(\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{j}))+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{i}),\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{j})),

(10)

for some characteristic kernel
k
.
Both
\mathtt{RE}_{m,n}^{2}
and
\mathtt{RMMD}_{m,n}^{2}
are distribution-free under the null for any fixed sample size
[
11
]
.

2.2.2
Practical Issues with Rank Energy

While the rank energy enjoys certain desirable properties, it also suffers from important drawbacks. We focus on two below.

Complexity, sample and computational:

In practice, to compute the test statistic proposed in
[
11
]
, one needs to solve the discrete version of the Monge problem. Given
n
samples, solving this problem exactly using typical methods requires
O(n^{3}\log n)
computations
[
19
]
, and the standard procedure is not amenable to parallelization. To make matters worse, when the samples are in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, (
8
)
has a large sample complexity in the sense that when
\lambda=m/(m+n)
and in the absence of further assumptions, one only has

\frac{1}{m+n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(X_{i})\|+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\mathtt{R}^{m+n}(Y_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}(Y_{j})\|\right]\lesssim(m+n)^{-1/d}.

Unfortunately, this rate which depends exponentially on
d
can be tight
[
35
]
, implying the need for extremely large sample sizes when working in high dimension to get a faithful estimate of the map
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda}
which is required to estimate
\mathtt{RE}_{\lambda}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}
. Together, these observations mean the approach of
[
11
]

Another reason that one may be interested in obtaining the rank statistic is to use it as a loss function for generative modelling. For example, if

are real samples and

are from a standard model (e.g.,

4

This gradient descent approach will not work for the rank energy in typical settings for two reasons. [

[
39
]

[
40

and

4.3
Utility as a Loss Function

There are many examples in practice where one will try to train a generative model to create high-dimensional data (for example images, video or, audio signals), which requires a measure of how closely an artificially generated dataset matches a real dataset. In high dimensions there is a need for test statistics that converge rapidly since otherwise one won’t be able to distinguish model performance from statistical fluctuations. This is one of the main reasons (along with computational ease) that MMD and energy distances have become prominent in generative modelling. These statistics both have
n^{-1/2}
convergence rates to their population variants, which is also achieved (up to logarithmic factors) by both of our proposed sRE and sRMMD. We believe this makes sRE and SRMMD strong contenders for high-dimensional generative modelling.

Another important note is that the computation of a high fidelity estimate of the soft rank energy is easily done through the use of Sinkhorn’s algorithm
[
25
]
which is based on fixed point iterations. One can perform a fixed number of iterations and then employ automatic differentiation
[
45
]
to obtain a gradient through this method. This approach is not novel to this work and so-called “Sinkhorn layers” have become popular in the neural network literature
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
. With these in hand, the computation of the soft rank energy and soft rank MMD becomes straightforward: simply attach a series of Sinkhorn layers to the end of a generative model to obtain the soft rank map, then use that soft rank map to transform the data, and then compute the soft rank energy or MMD on the transformed data.

5.1
sRE and sRMMD as the Loss Function in a Generative Model

Generative modeling is used to implicitly approximate a complex, high-dimensional distribution from a finite number of samples. When trained successfully, it allows one to draw new samples from the underlying distribution. A seminal framework to learn the generative model is the
generative adversarial network (GAN)

[
49
]
that optimizes a difficult minimax program. A simpler approach known as a

[

[
50

. A brief description of the model architecture and the training procedure is given below.

Architecture:

We use the same architecture used in
[
39
]
which consists of (a) a generative network and (b) an auto-encoder
[
51
]
. The generative network consists of 3 intermediate ReLU nonlinear layers and
one logistic sigmoid output layer. The auto-encoder has 4 layers, 2 for the encoder and 2 for the decoder with sigmoid nonlinearities. The auto-encoder is used to learn an almost lossless low-dimensional latent space for the high-dimensional, complex data. The generative network is used to generate samples in this low-dimensional space. The trained decoder then turns these samples into meaningful high-dimensional data. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
39
]
.

Training:

We train the auto-encoder and generator network separately. First an auto-encoder is learned to produce a low-dimensional representation for the MNIST digits with latent dimension of
8
. Then we train the generative network to learn to generate samples in this low-dimensional representation space via minimizing either
\mathtt{sRE}
or
\mathtt{sRMMD}
. Both
\mathtt{sRE}
and

[

with a maximum of 5000 iterations. To compute

with the bandwidth parameter
\sigma=(1,2,4,8,16,32)
.

Both the auto-encoder and the generator are trained on a minibatch size of

using the Adam optimizer
[
52
]
with a learning rate of

5.2
Generating Valid Knockoffs using sRMMD

In applications where the goal is to discover relevant features that can explain certain outcomes (e.g., metabolites or genes related to Crohn’s disease

5.2.1
An sRMMD-Based Knockoff Generator

We use a generative model similar to the one used in
[
60
]
. The generative model has a deep neural network

6
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this paper, we identified some major limitations in use of the recently proposed multivariate rank-based GoF statistics based on the theory of optimal transportation, namely high sample and computational complexity in high dimensions and lack of differentiability, which limits their use in gradient-based machine learning methods. We show that using entropic maps derived from entropic regularization of the optimal transportation problem alleviates these issues and leads to efficient statistics for GoF testing. Furthermore, we show that this regularization allows the use of these GoF statistics for generative modeling in high dimensions.

One future research direction is to evaluate the effect of different distributions as the target distribution in place of
\text{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
such as spherical uniform distribution
[
9
]
. It may be important to characterize the effect of this choice for the soft rank map and soft rank-based statistics proposed in this paper.

A related problem concerns the dependence on
\varepsilon
in the entropic regularization. In particular, the
convergence rate
of the sRE and sRMMD to the RE and RMMD as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
is the subject of ongoing work. While Proposition
2
provides a coarse convergence result, we conjecture that a more precise bound may hold and that the extra smoothness assumptions on the measures may not be required for this result to be true, and that compactness and together with weaker smoothness assumptions may suffice. Beyond analyzing the convergence rate in
\varepsilon
, it is important to understand the advantages of taking
\varepsilon\gg 0
, beyond the improvements in statistical and computational complexity. For example, one may wish to understand the impact
\varepsilon
has on the robustness, or stability, of sRE in response to small perturbations in the distributions. Finally, extending the result of Theorem
2
to relax the compactness assumption on the target measure as well is an important direction for future research.

Proposition 8
.

Let
P
be a
\sigma^{2}
-subgaussian. Let
Q\in\mathcal{P}(B_{2}^{d}(0,r))
. Then, there exist smooth optimal potentials
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
for
S_{\varepsilon}(P,Q)
such that,

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}\leq d\sigma^{2}+(\|x\|+\sqrt{2}d\sigma)\|y\|-\|x\|^{2},

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}(r+\|x\|)^{2}.

Proof.

We note that one can chose
f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\varepsilon\log\left(\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(g_{\varepsilon}(Y)-1/2\|x-Y\|^{2})\right)\right)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(y)=-\varepsilon\log\left(\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(X)-1/2\|X-y\|^{2})\right)\right)

and
\mathbb{E}[f_{\varepsilon}(X)]\geq 0,\mathbb{E}[g_{\varepsilon}(X)]\geq 0
. Given these choices, we note that by the convexity of
-\varepsilon\log
and Jensen’s inequality that

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log\left(\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(X)-1/2\|X-y\|^{2})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}-\varepsilon\log\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(X)-1/2\|X-y\|^{2})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|X-y\|^{2}-f_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|X-y\|^{2}.

This implies that

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|X-y\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})-\mathbb{E}[X]^{\top}y-\frac{1}{2}(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2})+x^{\top}y

\displaystyle\leq d\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{2d}\sigma\|y\|-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+\|x\|\|y\|.

Similarly, applying Jensen’s inequality as above but to
f_{\varepsilon}
we obtain,

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|Y-x\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(r+\|x\|)^{2}.

∎

For our analysis this proposition implies the following bound on

for

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[d\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{2d}\sigma r+r^{2}+2r\|x\|\right]\right).

Using Proposition

\displaystyle\varepsilon\log\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle=f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{2d}\sigma\|y\|-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+\|x\|\|y\|+\frac{1}{2}(r+\|x\|)^{2}

\displaystyle=d\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{2d}\sigma r+\|x\|r+\frac{1}{2}(r^{2}+2\|x\|r)

\displaystyle=d\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{2d}\sigma r+r^{2}+2r\|x\|.

Dividing by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{2\left\lVert X\right\rVert r}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{2\left\lVert X\right\rVert r}{\varepsilon}\right)\bm{1}[\left\lVert X\right\rVert<(4rd\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon)]+\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{2\left\lVert X\right\rVert r}{\varepsilon}\right)\bm{1}[\left\lVert X\right\rVert\geq(4rd\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{8rd\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert X\right\rVert^{2}}{2d\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{8rd\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+2.

Plugging this in above and using that
a\geq C_{1}r^{2}
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

The main body of the proof is spent in establishing a result similar to Proposition
5
. It starts with the following result:

Proposition 9
.

Let
P,Q
be
\sigma^{2}
-subgaussian and let
P^{n},Q^{n}
be the random empirical measures. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\chi\in L^{(}\pi^{n}_{\varepsilon})}\int\chi d\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}-\int\int\left[e^{\chi(x,y)}-1\right]\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP(x)dQ^{n}(y)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP^{n}-dP\right]

where
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}
is the random optimal coupling of
(P,Q^{n}),
the function
\gamma^{n,n}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\exp(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(y)-1/2\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}])
, and
(f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n},g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n})
are the optimal potentials for
P^{n},Q^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
(f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n},g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n})
be the optimal potentials for
(P^{n},Q^{n})
and define
\eta(x,y)=\varepsilon\chi(x,y)+f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(y)
for any
\chi\in L^{1}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n})
. Applying Proposition
3
and calculating one has

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}(P,Q_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\int\eta d\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}-\varepsilon\int\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\eta(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right)dP(x)dQ^{n}(y)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int\chi(x,y)d\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}+\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP(x)+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(y)dQ^{n}(y)-\varepsilon\int\int\left[e^{\chi(x,y)}-1\right]\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(x,y)dP(x)dQ_{n}(y).

Rearranging, one obtains

\int\chi(x,y)d\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}-\int\int\left[e^{\chi(x,y)}-1\right]\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(x,y)dP(x)dQ_{n}(y)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[S_{\varepsilon}(P,Q^{n})-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}\right].

Since this holds uniformly over
\chi\in L^{1}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n})
we can take the supremum on the left as well

\displaystyle\sup_{\chi\in L^{1}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n})}

\displaystyle\int\chi(x,y)d\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}-\int\int\left[e^{\chi(x,y)}-1\right]\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(x,y)dP(x)dQ_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[S_{\varepsilon}(P,Q^{n})-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}\right]

We now turn our attention to the right side. Let
(f_{\varepsilon}^{n},g_{\varepsilon}^{n})
be optimal for
(P,Q^{n})
. By optimality we have

\displaystyle\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}=S_{\varepsilon}(P^{n},Q^{n})

\displaystyle\geq\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}-\varepsilon\int\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[f_{\varepsilon}^{n}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}^{n}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right)dP^{n}(x)dQ^{n}(y)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}-\varepsilon\int\left[\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[f_{\varepsilon}^{n}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}^{n}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right)dQ^{n}(y)\right]dP^{n}(x)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}-\varepsilon\int 1dP^{n}(x)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}.

Comparing the first and last we have shown

\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}\geq\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}.

From here we can develop a bound as follows

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}(P,Q_{n})-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}

\displaystyle=\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}

\displaystyle=\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}-f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n}dQ^{n}+\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}-f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP+\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}+\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP^{n}-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}dP^{n}-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}

\displaystyle=\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}[dP^{n}-dP]+\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}[dP-dP^{n}].

Note that
f_{\varepsilon}^{n}
is independent of
P^{n}
, and so conditioned on
Q^{n}
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}[dP-dP^{n}]\right]=0.

Backtracking and taking expectations we have shown

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\chi\in L^{(}\pi^{n}_{\varepsilon})}\int\chi d\pi_{\varepsilon}^{n}-\int\int\left[e^{\chi}-1\right]\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}(x,y)dP(x)d^{n}Q(y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\varepsilon}(P,Q^{n})-\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dP-\int g_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}dQ^{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}[dP^{n}-dP]+\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n}[dP-dP^{n}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\int f_{\varepsilon}^{n,n}[dP^{n}-dP]

which proves the result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

Using the notation that
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}
is the random independently estimated map and
X^{m}=(X_{1},...,X_{m}),Y^{n}=(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})
are the samples used to evaluate the statistic (which are independent of
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}
) we immediately have the following

||\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m,n}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}-\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}||_{L^{2}}^{2}=\underset{\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\underset{X^{n},Y^{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m,n}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}-\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\right)^{2}\bigg|\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}\right]\right].

We first consider the inner expectation. For brevity we suppress the conditioning on
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}
and also introduce the following six collections of random variables

\displaystyle R^{X}\triangleq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||,\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{R}^{X}\triangleq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{j})||,

\displaystyle R^{Y}\triangleq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||,\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{R}^{Y}\triangleq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})||,

\displaystyle R^{XY}\triangleq\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||,\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{R}^{XY}\triangleq\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})||.

These are further compressed into

\displaystyle\hat{R}

\displaystyle\triangleq\hat{R}^{XY}-\hat{R}^{X}-\hat{R}^{Y},

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\triangleq R^{XY}-R^{X}-R^{Y}.

We also introduce the notation

\displaystyle E^{X}

\displaystyle\triangleq\underset{X,X^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X^{\prime})||,

\displaystyle E^{Y}

\displaystyle\triangleq\underset{Y,Y^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y^{\prime})||,

\displaystyle E^{XY}

\displaystyle\triangleq 2\underset{X,Y}{\mathbb{E}}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)||,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\triangleq E^{XY}-E^{X}-E^{Y}.

With this notation we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m,n}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}-\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}-E)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}([\hat{R}-R]-[R-E])^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}-R)^{2}+2\mathbb{E}(R-E)^{2}

We now control the two expectations separately:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}-R)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left([\hat{R}^{XY}-R^{XY}]+[R^{X}-\hat{R}^{X}]+[R^{Y}-\hat{R}^{Y}]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{XY}-R^{XY})^{2}+3\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{X}-R^{X})^{2}+3\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{Y}-R^{Y})^{2}.

Next we control these three expectations separately:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{XY}-R^{XY})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})]-[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})]||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})]||\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 8\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||^{2}+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})]||^{2}

\displaystyle=8\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||^{2}+8\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{i})||^{2}

\displaystyle=8\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)||^{2}+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}\mathbb{E}\lambda||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)||^{2}+(1-\lambda)||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)||^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(P_{\lambda})}^{2}.

In the third and fourth lines we have used the reverse triangle inequality followed by the triangle inequality to re-group the terms.
In the second to last line we have made use of the inequality valid for all
a,b\geq 0,c\in(0,1)

a+b\leq\frac{c}{\min(c,1-c)}a+\frac{1-c}{\min(c,1-c)}b=\frac{1}{\min(c,1-c)}\left[ca+(1-c)b\right].

The last line follows from the fact that
P_{\lambda}=\lambda P_{X}+(1-\lambda)P_{Y}
.
Next we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{X}-R^{X})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{j})]-[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})]||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||^{2}+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||^{2}

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||^{2}

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)||^{2}.

Again in the third and fourth lines we have used the reverse-triangle inequality followed by the triangle inequality.
An exactly analogous calculation for
Y
shows

\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{Y}-R^{Y})^{2}\leq 4\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)||^{2}.

Combining these two we have similarly to above that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{X}-R^{X})^{2}+\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}^{Y}-R^{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\left[||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)||^{2}+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)||^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(P_{\lambda})}^{2}.

Combining bounds we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}-R)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{24}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(P_{\lambda})}^{2}+\frac{12}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(P_{\lambda})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{36}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(P_{\lambda})}^{2}.

Now we turn our attention to
\mathbb{E}(R-E)^{2}
. First we show that
R-E
is mean-zero:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}R

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)||-\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X^{\prime})||-\mathbb{E}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y)-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y^{\prime})||

\displaystyle=E^{XY}-E^{X}-E^{Y}=E.

Subtracting
E
from the first and last shows
\mathbb{E}[R-E]=0
. Using this we have

\mathbb{E}[(R-E)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(R-E)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[R-E]^{2}=\text{Var}(R-E).

To control the variance we apply the Efron-Stein inequality (
[
44
]
Theorem 3.1) to the function

f(X_{1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{n})=\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||.

First note that we have the bounds

\displaystyle|f(X_{1},...,X_{i-1},X_{i},X_{i+1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{n})-f(X_{1},...,X_{i-1},X_{i}^{\prime},X_{i+1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{n})|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||\right)-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i}^{m}\left(||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||\right|+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i}^{m}\left|||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{j})||\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})||+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i}^{m}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})||

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{m}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}^{\prime})||

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{m}

where we have in the last line used the
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
maps into
[0,1]^{d}
and the diameter is
\sqrt{d}
. A completely analogous computation shows

|f(X_{1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{i-1},Y_{i},Y_{i+1},...,Y_{n})-f(X_{1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{i-1},Y_{i}^{\prime},Y_{i+1},...,Y_{n})|\leq\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{m}.

Using this bound in the Efron-Stein inequality we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(R-E)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(R-E)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{1},...,X_{i-1},X_{i},X_{i+1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{n})-f(X_{1},...,X_{i-1},X_{i}^{\prime},X_{i+1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{n})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{i-1},Y_{i},Y_{i+1},...,Y_{n})-f(X_{1},...,X_{m},Y_{1},...,Y_{i-1},Y_{i}^{\prime},Y_{i+1},...,Y_{n})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{m}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{9d}{2m}+\frac{9d}{2n}

\displaystyle=\frac{9d(m+n)}{2mn}.

Collecting terms we have, conditionally on the estimate of
\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m,n}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}-\mathtt{sRE}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(P_{X},P_{Y})^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}(\hat{R}-R)^{2}+2\mathbb{E}(R-E)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{72}{\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)}||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(P_{\lambda})}^{2}+\frac{9d(m+n)}{mn}.

Unconditioning and applying either Theorem
2
or Theorem
3
completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
5

The proof is essentially the same as the proof of Theorem
4
. The key difference is that we require a kernel analog of the reverse-triangle, followed by triangle inequality trick:

\displaystyle|||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})||-||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})|||

\displaystyle\leq||[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})]-[\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})]||

\displaystyle\leq||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||+||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||

This is achieved through

\displaystyle|k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))-k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j}))|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|[k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))-k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))]+[k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))-k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j}))]|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))-k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))|+|k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j}))-k(\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i}),\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j}))|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle l||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(X_{i})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(X_{i})||+l||\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{m+n}(Y_{j})-\mathtt{R}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(Y_{j})||.

With this key inequality established, one can show the result by following the proof of Theorem
4
, substituting this inequality in the two places where the reverse-triangle followed triangle inequality is used. In both places these inequalities are done inside of a squaring so ultimately we gain a factor of

.

Related Works on Knockoff Generation

The seminal work
[
57
]
assumes that the joint feature distribution follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution and satisfies the pairwise exchangeability condition (
18
) via approximating only the first two moments (mean and covariance). In cases where the distributions are not multivariate Gaussian, the second order method in
[
57
]
cannot guarantee any control of the FDR. In contrast, methods like knockoffGAN
[
58
]
, deep knockoff
[
60
]
, auto-encoding knockoff
[
59
]
focus on learning generative models to sample knockoffs. KnockoffGAN is a complex architecture which consists of four different neural networks and optimizes a difficult minimax problem. A comparatively simpler approach adopted by deep knockoff employs MMD
[
33
]
as the discriminating statistic for testing pairwise exchangeability in (
18
). As we see in Section
14.2
the generator learned using the MMD performs poorly in high dimensions, and fails to approximate the input distribution properly. Auto-encoding knockoff uses a variational autoencoder
[
52
]
that learns a low-dimensional latent space for the high-dimensional data. The performance of the auto-encoding knockoff depends on the latent space dimension. A higher dimensional latent space
can be used to make the model better but may lead to diminished power if the covariates violate the low-dimensional approximation. Another method, called deep direct likelihood knockoff (DDLK)
[
61
]
minimizes the KL (Kullback-Liebler) divergence to test for pairwise exchangeability.
