Data-induced multiscale losses and efficient multirate gradient descent schemes
Juncai He, Liangchen Liu, Richard Tsai

Abstract
This paper investigates the impact of multiscale data on machine learning algorithms, particularly in the context of deep learning. A dataset is multiscale if its distribution shows large variations in scale across different directions. This paper reveals multiscale structures in the loss landscape, including its gradients and Hessians inherited from the data. Correspondingly, it introduces a novel gradient descent approach, drawing inspiration from multiscale algorithms used in scientific computing. This approach seeks to transcend empirical learning rate selection, offering a more systematic, data-informed strategy to enhance training efficiency, especially in the later stages.



1
Introduction

In many supervised learning setups, the input data are commonly embedded in high-dimensional Euclidean spaces for convenience. To clarify, we are referring to the non-label part of the datasets (i.e. the features). A natural question arises concerning the distributions of the training data in the embedding space and their potential consequences on the efficacy of learning.
A substantial body of literature
(
Niyogi et al. 2008
;
Peyré 2009
;
Carlsson 2009
)
has delved into the “manifold hypothesis”, which suggests the data distributions behind the complex representations might concentrate near a simpler, lower-dimensional manifold. Empirical validations
(
Schölkopf et al. 1998
;
Brand 2002
;
Saul & Roweis 2003
)
and the development of the theoretical testing framework
(
Narayanan & Mitter 2010
;
Fefferman et al. 2016
)
have lent support to such hypothesis, and recent research
(
Medina et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2022
)
has explored its variants.

The manifold hypothesis has sparked exciting works and numerous insights discovered by the manifold learning community
(
Roweis & Saul 2000
;
Tenenbaum et al. 2000
;
Belkin & Niyogi 2003
;
Donoho & Grimes 2003
;
Weinberger & Saul 2006
)
. More recently, it has found applications in the realm of deep learning in various areas, including approximation theory
(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Cloninger & Klock 2021
;
Schonsheck et al. 2022
)
, intrinsic dimensions
(
Brahma et al. 2015
;
Ansuini et al. 2019
;
Pope et al. 2021
)
, initialization
(
Tiwari & Konidaris 2022
)
, and the effects of training
(
He et al. 2023
)
.

Nevertheless, the predominant techniques and analysis in deep learning implicitly assume the distribution of features is full-dimensional in the embedding space and that it has the same scale across different directions. This assumption is evident in the prevalent initialization methods
(
Glorot & Bengio 2010
;
He et al. 2015
)
for parameters, including those acting on the input data.

However, even under the simplistic model of principal component analysis (PCA)
(
Pearson 1901
;
Hotelling 1933
)
, many real-world datasets exhibit a fast-decay spectrum in their principal directions, suggesting data distributions across different directions can manifest drastically different scales. We refer to datasets with variations in the orders of magnitude of scales across different principal directions as
multiscale datasets
, prompting a pertinent question: How do multiscale datasets impact algorithms, particularly those heavily reliant on data, such as gradient descent (GD) in deep learning?
This article aims to unveil the potential impacts of multiscale data on machine learning algorithms, with a specific focus on deep learning problems. Notably, the multiscale nature of data passes down to the learning problem, giving rise to a multiscale loss landscape from various perspectives.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithms with varying learning rates
(
Robbins & Monro 1951
;
You et al. 2019
;
Smith & Topin 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
have been instrumental in the success of deep learning tasks. While it is widely believed that adopting specific learning rate schedules can significantly enhance the generalization ability of deep neural networks (DNNs), such learning rate schemes have primarily relied on empirical evidence and arguments. This suggests that there is still room for further understanding and improvement of SGD-based algorithms when applied to real-world multiscale datasets.

In the scientific computing community,
multiscale algorithms such as
(
Engquist & Tsai 2005
;
Tao et al. 2010
)
can efficiently compute dynamical systems where they leverage the fast averaging and near independence in components due to the gap in two widely separated scales in the systems. Drawing inspiration from these concepts, we propose a novel yet simple explicit gradient descent scheme that adopts learning rates based on multiscale information from the data. The objective is to bridge the gap between the empirical nature of learning rate choices and a more systematic, multiscale approach, ultimately aiming for the efficiency and adaptability of training algorithms for models defined on real-world multiscale datasets.
While our focus doesn’t extend to addressing SGD’s selection of minima, we argue that the proposed algorithm is suitable for accelerating the later/final stages of gradient-based training of DNNs.

Our main contributions include:

1.

We prove that multiscale data and
\ell^{2}
loss lead to multiscale landscapes. Specifically, we derive a multiscale expansion of the loss gradients and reveal component-wise multiscale characteristics within gradients and Hessians. We verify these theoretical findings by numerically investigating DNN models on the CIFAR dataset.

2.

We propose an explicit multirate gradient descent (MrGD) scheme that leverages multiscale information to construct an appropriate learning rate schedule for much-enhanced convergence speed.

3.

We establish a comprehensive and rigorous theory demonstrating that the MrGD scheme achieves a quasi-optimal convergence rate for linear problems and can be extended to convex functions.

1.1
Related Work

Multiscale landscape of loss in learning.

There has been considerable discussion about the origins of the multiscale expansion form in the loss gradient.
(
Mei et al. 2018
)
suggests a possible origin of the loss with a multiscale structure arising from the effect of noise in various statistical models.
(
Kong & Tao 2020
)
demonstrates that a simple 2-layer neural network (NN) using a periodic activation function trained with multiscale data leads to a loss exhibiting a multiscale expansion. They also show that deterministic gradient descent can become stochastic with a sufficiently large learning rate.
(
Ma et al. 2022
)
numerically observe a multiscale behavior in the loss landscape of neural network loss functions, which is manifested in two ways: (1) in the vicinity of minima, the loss combines a continuum of scales and grows sub-quadratically, and (2) over a larger region, the loss displays several distinct scales. Recently,
(
He et al. 2023
)
analyzed linear regression on two-scale data sampled from a distribution that concentrates around a lower dimensional linear subspace. The regression solutions reflect scales corresponding to the underlying data. Their results also reveal a non-trivial relationship between the number of data points and the magnitude of the smaller scale, which is crucial for the stability of the linear model, a concept that extends to deep neural networks.
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
extended the data distributions to embedded curved manifolds, providing closed-form solutions for local linear regression on hypersurfaces and curves, indicating a potential influence of the multiscale geometric properties of the underlying data manifold on the regression solutions.

Larger learning rates in GD and SGD

Several studies, both numerical
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2017
;
You et al. 2019
;
Smith & Topin 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
and theoretical
(
Oymak 2021
;
Wang & Yuan 2023
;
Das Gupta et al. 2023
;
Grimmer 2023
)
, have shown that an appropriately larger learning rate can benefit the training of GD and SGD in general convex optimization and deep learning. However, most of these studies focus on improvements for better generalization, such as Warm Restart
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2017
)
for training general NNs towards flat local minima
(
He et al. 2019
)
, or for specific problems like matrix factorization
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
and deterministic and stochastic Hamiltonian dynamical systems
(
Li et al. 2023
)
. In this work, we propose a multiscale landscape perspective of the empirical loss and demonstrate that appropriately larger learning rates may also contribute to improved convergence during training.

Multiscale Hessian

A multitude of studies have utilized local quadratic approximations and second-order information of the loss to scrutinize the training dynamics of deep neural networks
(
Wu et al. 2018
;
Du et al. 2018
;
Jacot et al. 2018

On the other hand, of particular concern to us is the learning rate schedule for the gradient-based training of neural networks. Inevitably, the learning rate is limited by the stability of the SGD, a constraint determined by the eigenvalues of the loss’s Hessian. Thus, the characteristics of the Hessian play a crucial role in unraveling many underlying issues.

3
Multirate gradient descent

In this section, we introduce and analyze a novel gradient descent algorithm, tailored for the cases discussed in the previous section. It will become evident that by a blend of large and small learning rates, the algorithm capitalizes on the multiscale characteristics, resulting in accelerated convergence.

4
Conclusion

This paper shows that multiscale data leads to empirical loss functions whose gradients and Hessians inherit the multiscale properties. Utilizing these properties, the introduced explicit MrGD scheme converges with a near-optimal rate for convex problems. The theories developed in this work partially explain the so-called learning rate warm-up strategy employed in neural network training.

Appendix B

In this section, we showcase that there exists the same structure in the gradient flow of DNNs compared to linear and logistic regressions when data have the same multiscale structure. For simplicity, let us first prove the result for data with two scales. Then, we generalize our results to multiscale data by induction.

B.1
Two-scale structure of gradient flow in DNNs

For data with two scales, we assume the data points concentrate around a low-dimensional subspace with a small variance in the orthogonal complement. More specifically,

(44)

In the rest of this subsection, we prove the two-scale structure of the gradient flow for DNNs. First, have

We use proof by induction.
For
\ell=1
, we have

f^{1}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma\left(W^{1}_{0}x^{0}+b^{1}+\varepsilon W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right),

(55)

where
W^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}W^{1}_{0}\\
W^{1}_{1}\end{pmatrix}
and
W^{1}_{0},W^{1}_{1}
correspond to the
x^{0},x^{1}
components of
\mathbf{x}
respectively.
We first define

f^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})=\sigma\left(W^{1}_{0}x^{0}+b^{1}\right)=f^{1}(\mathbf{x}^{0})=f^{1}\left(\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right).

(56)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left[f^{1}(\mathbf{x})-f^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=\left[\sigma\left(W^{1}_{0}x^{0}+b^{1}+\varepsilon W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right)-\sigma\left(W^{1}_{0}x^{0}+b^{1}\right)\right]_{i}

(57)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sigma^{\prime}(\xi_{\left[W^{1}\mathbf{x}+b^{1}\right]_{i},\left[W^{1}_{1}\varepsilon x^{1}\right]_{i}})\left[W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right]_{i},

where
\xi_{\left[W^{1}\mathbf{x}+b^{1}\right]_{i},\left[W^{1}_{1}\varepsilon x^{1}\right]_{i}}
depends on
\left[W^{1}\mathbf{x}+b^{1}\right]_{i}
, and
\left[W^{1}_{1}\varepsilon x^{1}\right]_{i}
. Since
\varepsilon x^{1}
is a part of
\mathbf{x}^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}x^{0}\\
\varepsilon x^{1}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{x}
, it means that
\xi_{\left[W^{1}\mathbf{x}+b^{1}\right]_{i},\left[W^{1}_{1}\varepsilon x^{1}\right]_{i}}
depends on
\mathbf{x}^{1}
with parameters
W^{1}
and
b^{1}
which are parts of
\theta
. Thus, we can write

f^{1}(\mathbf{x})-f^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})=\varepsilon f^{1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1};\theta)=\varepsilon f^{1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}).

(58)

Moreover, we have

\|\varepsilon f^{1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\|=\left\|\sigma\left(W^{1}_{0}x^{0}+b^{1}+\varepsilon W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right)-\sigma\left(W^{1}_{0}x^{0}+b^{1}\right)\right\|\leq\varepsilon{\rm Lip}(\sigma)\|W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\|.

(59)

This finishes the proof for
\ell=1
.
Note that
f^{1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})
depends also on the previous scale such that
f^{1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})=f^{1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})
, but for brevity we only include the component corresponding to the smallest scale. The same convention will be adopted throughout the rest of the proof.

We prove them by induction for
\ell>1
. Now, we assume that results hold for
1,2,\cdots,\ell-1
, let us prove it for
\ell
. According to the definition and assumption for
\ell-1
, we have

f^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x})+b^{\ell}\right)=\sigma\left(W^{\ell}(f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+\varepsilon f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}))+b^{\ell}\right).

(60)

Similarly, we have

f^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0}):=\sigma\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right)=\sigma\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}\left(\mathbf{x}^{0}\right)+b^{\ell}\right)=f^{\ell}\left(\mathbf{x}^{0}\right).

(61)

Again,

\displaystyle\left[f^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})-f^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=\left[\sigma\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{1}+\varepsilon f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)-\sigma\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{1}\right)\right]_{i}

(62)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sigma^{\prime}(\xi_{\left[W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right]_{i},\left[\varepsilon f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right]_{i}})\left[W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon f^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1};\theta)=\varepsilon f^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}).

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\varepsilon f^{\ell}_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sigma\left(W^{\ell}(f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+\varepsilon f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}))+b^{\ell}\right)-\sigma\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right)\right\|

(63)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon{\rm Lip}(\sigma)\left\|W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}))\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon{\rm Lip}(\sigma)\left\|W^{\ell}\right\|\left\|f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}))\right\|.

Finally, we have the bound for
\|f^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\|
by induction.
∎

B.1.2
Two-scale structure in
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\ell}

Recall that
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{\ell}}=\left<(f(\mathbf{x};\theta)-g)\widetilde{\lambda}^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\left(f^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}\right>_{N}
, we will study the two-scale structure in
\lambda^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
and
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\ell}
in this subsection.
Before that, we first have the following two-scale structure in

.

Lemma B.2
.

For any DNN defined in
Equation

6
and dataset with two scales as in
Equation

43
under Assumption
2.1
, we have

In particular, we have

D^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})=\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right)=\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}\left(\mathbf{x}^{0}\right)+b^{\ell}\right)=D^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{0}),

(65)

and

\|D^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\|\leq{\rm Lip}(\sigma^{\prime})\left({\rm Lip}(\sigma)\right)^{\ell-1}\left\|W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right\|\prod_{k=2}^{\ell}\left\|W^{k}\right\|.

(66)

Proof.

Given
\sigma\in C^{2}
and the decomposition of

in Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle D^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x})+b^{\ell}\right)

(67)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}+\varepsilon W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right)+\varepsilon{\rm diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\right)W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})

\displaystyle=D^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+\varepsilon D^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1};\theta)

\displaystyle=D^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+\varepsilon D^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1}),

where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
and each
\left[\xi\right]_{i}
is determined by
\left[W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right]_{i}
, and
\varepsilon\left[W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right]_{i}
via the intermediate value theorem.
In addition, we have the following bound

\displaystyle\left\|\varepsilon D^{\ell}_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}+\varepsilon W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)-\sigma^{\prime}\left(W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+b^{\ell}\right)\right\|

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon{\rm Lip}(\sigma^{\prime})\left\|W^{\ell}f^{\ell-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon{\rm Lip}(\sigma^{\prime})\left({\rm Lip}(\sigma)\right)^{\ell-1}\left\|W^{1}_{1}x^{1}\right\|\prod_{k=2}^{\ell}\left\|W^{k}\right\|.

∎

Corollary B.3
.

We have

\left\|{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell}_{1}\right)\right\|\leq\|D^{\ell}_{1}\|.

(69)

Proof.

Given the properties of matrix norm and vector norm, we have

\left\|{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell}_{1}\right)\right\|=\|D^{\ell}_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|D^{\ell}_{1}\|.

(70)

∎

Based on the two-scale structure in
D^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
, we have the following two-scale structure for
\lambda^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})
.

Lemma B.4
.

For any DNN defined in
Equation

6
and dataset with two scales as in
Equation

43
under Assumption
2.1
, we have

(72)

In particular, we have the following recursive definitions of
\lambda^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})
and
\lambda^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})
:

\left(\lambda^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right)^{T}=\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right)^{T}{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right)W^{\ell+1}

(73)

and

\displaystyle\left(\lambda^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda^{\ell}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1};\theta,\varepsilon)\right)^{T}

(74)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)^{T}{\rm diag}(D_{0}^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x}^{0}))W^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle+\left(\lambda_{0}^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right)^{T}\left({\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell+1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)W^{\ell+1}\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)^{T}{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell+1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)W^{\ell+1},

with

Furthermore, we have the explicit upper bound for

(76)

Proof.

We prove it by induction.
For
\lambda^{L}
, by definition, we have

\left(\lambda^{L}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial f^{L}}\right)^{T}=W^{L+1}.

(77)

To make

holds for

and
\lambda^{L}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})=0
.
Thus, for any

\left(\lambda^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial f^{\ell+1}}\cdot\frac{\partial f^{\ell+1}}{\partial f^{\ell}}\right)^{T}=\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})\right)W^{\ell+1}.

(78)

in Lemma
B.2
and
\lambda^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})
in
Equation

71

\displaystyle\left(\lambda^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+\varepsilon\lambda^{\ell+1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)^{T}{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})+\varepsilon D^{\ell+1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{1})\right)W^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\lambda^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right)^{T}{\rm diag}\left(D^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{0})\right)W^{\ell+1}

and

(80)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\prod_{\ell+1\leq k\neq i\leq L}}\left({\rm diag}\left(D^{i}_{1}\right)W^{i}\left({\rm diag}\left(D^{k}_{0}\right)W^{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle diag}\left(D^{L}_{0}\right)W^{L}\cdots

Thus, we can bound

\displaystyle\|\lambda^{\ell}_{1}\|\leq\left\|W^{L+1}\right\|\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}\left\|W^{k}\right\|\left(\sum_{\ell+1\leq i\leq L}\|D^{i}_{1}\|\prod_{\ell+1\leq k\neq i\leq L}\left\|D_{0}^{k}\right\|\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon\right).

(82)

Proof.

B.4
of

B.2

Appendix C
Proof of multiscale gradient components and multiscale Hessian:

C.1
Multiscale gradient components in logistic regression

Proof.

The label
g(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a probability. Let
\mathbf{p}_{i}:=g(\mathbf{x}_{i})
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{ij}=1
, and
p_{ij}>0
. Then, the cross-entropy loss can be explicitly written as:

\mathcal{L}_{c}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathbf{p}_{i}\cdot\log\big(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w},b)\big)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{ij}\log\big([f(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w},b)]_{j}\big).

Recall that:

\left[f({\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w},b})\right]_{j}=\frac{e^{\mathbf{w}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{j}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}e^{\mathbf{w}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{i}}},\quad j=1:k,

and note
\log(f_{j})=(\mathbf{w}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{j})-\log(\sum_{i}e_{i})
, with
e_{j}:=e^{\mathbf{w}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{j}},\,f_{j}:=[f(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w},b)]_{j}
, for clarity. Therefore, for any weight
\mathbf{w}_{l}
:

\frac{\partial\log(f_{j})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{l}}=(\mathbf{\delta}_{jl}-\frac{e_{l}}{\sum_{i}e_{i}})\mathbf{x}^{T}

Then:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{c}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{l}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{ij}(\delta_{jl}-\frac{e_{l}}{\sum_{i}e_{i}})\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}(p_{il}-\frac{e_{l}}{\sum_{i}e_{i}})=\left<\big(g(\mathbf{x})_{l}-f(\mathbf{x})_{l}\big)\mathbf{x}^{T}\right>_{N}.

(123)

where the multiplier
\left(g(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{l}-f({\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{w},b})_{l}\right)\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
in general. Therefore, it is straightforward to see that components in the loss gradient
exhibits a similar multiscale behavior as the dataset, where the magnitude of the gradient aligns with the scale of the data distribution:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{c}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{l}}\sim\big.\left(\mathcal{O}(1),\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{1}),\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{2})\dots\big.\right).

(124)

Organizing the gradient for each
\mathbf{w}_{l}
into the matrix form leads us to the desired result.
∎

C.2
Multiscale gradient components in neural network

Proof.

The gradient of the loss concerning the first layer parameter
W^{1}
is given by:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{1}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},W)-g_{i}\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial W^{1}},

where from the multiplicative representation:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial W^{1}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial W^{1}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(W^{1}\mathbf{x},W_{2})}{\partial W^{1}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}^{T}.

Without loss of generality, we focus on the gradient of the first neuron, and let
\mathbf{w}_{11}
denote the first row of the weight matrix
W^{1}
, such that
\mathbf{w}_{11}\cdot\mathbf{x}
produces the first entry in the first hidden layer. Similarly, we have

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(W^{1}\mathbf{x},W_{2})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}^{T}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}^{T}.

Since
f
is a scalar function and
\mathbf{w}_{11}^{T}\mathbf{x}
is also a scalar, we set
c_{1}:=\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(W^{1}\mathbf{x},W_{2})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}^{T}\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}
for simplicity, which yields
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}}=c_{1}\mathbf{x}^{T}
, and the same applies for any row of the weight matrix
W^{1}
. Let
c_{i}
denote the corresponding coefficient for the
i
-th row, and we have
\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial W^{1}}=\mathbf{c}\mathbf{x}^{T}

where
\mathbf{c}=[c_{1},c_{2},\dots]^{T}
. Plug in the expression back to the loss gradient produces:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},W)-g_{i}\right)c_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}.

(125)

The gradient expression in
Equation

125
holds for any neuron in the first layer and closely matches with
Equation

123
. Thus, we can deduce that the gradient for
W^{1}
also explicitly relies on the data distribution, where, if the data distribution satisfies
2.1
, the gradient will have dimension-wise multiscale characteristics, akin to
Equation

124
.

∎

C.3
Multiscale Hessian in neural network

Proof.

Continued from
Equation

125
, to derive the Hessian we have:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{w}_{11}}\mathcal{L}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x}_{i})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{11}}c_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}+\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},W)-g_{i}\right)\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{w}_{11}}f(\mathbf{x}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}+\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},W)-g_{i}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{f}(W^{1}\mathbf{x}_{i},W_{2})}{\partial(\mathbf{w}_{11}^{T}\mathbf{x})^{2}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}.

Note that
\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},W)-g_{i}\right),\,\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{f}(W^{1}\mathbf{x},W_{2})}{\partial(\mathbf{w}_{11}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}
, setting:

s_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=c_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}+\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},W)-g_{i}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{f}(W^{1}\mathbf{x},W_{2})}{\partial(\mathbf{w}_{1j}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}}\in\mathbb{R},

leads to

\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{w}_{1j}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\approx\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right)X^{T}X,

which finishes the proof.
∎

0
. Therefore, we first give a lower bound on the denominator:

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1-R^{i}_{i+1}\kappa_{i+1}^{-1}}{1-R^{i}_{i}\kappa_{i}^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1-\eta_{i}\sigma_{i+1,d_{i+1}}}{1-\eta_{i}\sigma_{i,d_{i}}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{1-\frac{r}{\eta\kappa_{c}}}{1-\frac{1}{\eta\kappa_{c}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-\frac{r}{\eta\kappa_{c}}\right)-\log\left(1-\frac{1}{\eta\kappa_{c}}\right).

Recall the log-inequality:

1-\frac{1}{x}\leq\log(x)\leq x-1\;\text{ for }x>0,\quad\implies\quad\frac{-x}{1-x}\leq\log(1-x)\leq-x\;\text{ for }x<1.

Since
\eta\kappa_{c}>1
and
r<1
, the log-inequality yields the following bound on the denominator:

\log\left(\frac{1-R^{i}_{i+1}\kappa_{i+1}^{-1}}{1-R^{i}_{i}\kappa_{i}^{-1}}\right)\geq\frac{-\frac{r}{\eta\kappa_{c}}}{1-\frac{r}{\eta\kappa_{c}}}+\frac{1}{\eta\kappa_{c}}=\frac{\eta\kappa_{c}(1-r)-r}{\eta\kappa_{c}(\eta\kappa_{c}-r)}.

As for the numerator for
F_{i,i+1}
, since
r_{i}=r\ll 1
, it satisfies:

\left(-\log(r_{i})+\log\left(\frac{\left|r_{i}-R^{i+1}_{i+1}\right|}{1-R^{i+1}_{i+1}\kappa_{i+1}^{-1}}\right)\right)\geq\log\left(\frac{\left|1-\eta_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\right|}{1-\eta_{i+1}\sigma_{i+1,d_{i+1}}}\right)>0.

Hence, we derive a corresponding upper bound:

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\left|1-\eta_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\right|}{1-\eta_{i+1}\sigma_{i+1,d_{i+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{c}-\eta\kappa_{c}r}{\eta\kappa_{c}r-r}\right)=\log(\kappa_{c})+\log(1-\eta r)-\log(r)-\log(\eta\kappa_{c}-1)

\displaystyle\leq-\log(r)-\log(\eta\kappa_{c}-1)+\kappa_{c}-\eta r-1.

Next, for
F_{i,j}
with
j=i+2:m
, we denote
k=j-(i+1)\geq 1
and its numerator becomes:

\left(-\log(r_{i})+\log\left(\frac{\left|r_{i}-R_{i+1}^{j}\right|}{\left|1-R_{i+1}^{j}\right|}\right)\right)=-\log(r)+\log\left(\frac{1-\eta r^{k+1}}{1-\eta r^{k}}\right)\geq 0,

since
r\ll 1
. Again, we derive a corresponding upper bound by applying the log-inequality:

\log\left(\frac{1-\eta r^{k+1}}{1-\eta r^{k}}\right)=\log(1-\eta r^{k+1})-\log(1-\eta r^{k})\leq-\eta r^{k+1}+\frac{\eta r^{k}}{1-\eta r^{k}}\sim o(r^{k}).

Therefore, there is a constant
C>0
(depends on
k
,
\varepsilon
, and
\eta
)
s.t.
the numerator is bounded above:

\log\left(\frac{1-\eta r^{k+1}}{1-\eta r^{k}}\right)\leq Cr^{k},

(141)

for any
r\in(0,\varepsilon)
. This finishes the proof.

∎

D.3
Proof of Theorem
3.12
(convergence for convex problems):

Proof.

First, by the fundamental Theorem of calculus, we have the following identity

\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})=\mathcal{H}_{\theta}(\theta-\theta^{*}),

where
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is obtained by integrating
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}
along the path from
\theta^{*}
to
\theta
. More precisely, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\theta}=\int_{t=0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})dt,

where
\theta_{t}=t\theta+(1-t)\theta^{*}
. Given Assumption
3.10
for
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
, using the triangle inequality for integral, we can deduce that
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
also satisfies Assumption
3.10
for any
\theta
.
As a result,

\theta^{k}_{j,s+1}-\theta^{*}=\theta^{k}_{j,s}-\theta^{*}-\eta_{j}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{k}_{j,s})=(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\eta_{j}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{j,s})(\theta^{k}_{j,s}-\theta^{*}).

(142)

The idea of the remaining proof is to decompose the error term into different orthogonal spaces, and show the error will be bounded on each of the space. To this end, let us denote the component of the error in the
i
-th space by

v_{i}^{j,s}:=\Pi_{i}\left(\theta^{k}_{j,s}-\theta^{*}\right),

where
\Pi_{i}
is the row-orthogonal matrix defined in
Equation

29
. Then, we apply
\Pi_{i}
to
Equation

142
to get the error in the
i
-th space for the next step, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|v_{i}^{j,s+1}\right\|=

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\eta_{j}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{j,s})\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\Pi^{T}_{\ell}v_{\ell}^{j,s}\right)\right\|

(143)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}_{d_{i}}-\eta_{j}\Pi_{i}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{j,s}\Pi_{i}^{T}\right)v_{i}^{j,s}\right\|+\sum_{\ell\neq i}\eta_{j}\left\|\Pi_{i}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{j,s}\Pi_{\ell}^{T}v_{\ell}^{j,s}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}_{d_{i}}-\eta_{j}\Pi_{i}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{j,s}\Pi_{i}^{T}\right)\right\|\left\|v_{i}^{j,s}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}\sum_{\ell\neq i}\left\|\Pi_{\ell}(\theta_{j,s}^{k}-\theta^{*})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}_{d_{i}}-\eta_{j}\Pi_{i}\mathcal{H}^{k}_{j,s}\Pi_{i}^{T}\right)\right\|\left\|v_{i}^{j,s}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}\left\|\theta_{j,s}^{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(1-R_{i}^{j}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)\left\|v_{i}^{j,s}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}C\quad&\text{if }j\leq i,\\
\left|1-R_{i}^{j}\right|\left\|v_{i}^{j,s}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}C\quad&\text{if }j>i.\end{cases}.

By repeating the above process from
v_{i}^{j,n_{j}}
to
v_{i}^{j,0}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|v_{i}^{j,n_{j}}\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(1-R_{i}^{j}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)^{n_{j}}\left\|v_{i}^{j,0}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}CE_{i}^{j}\quad&\text{if }j\leq i,\\
\left|1-R_{i}^{j}\right|^{n_{j}}\left\|v_{i}^{j,0}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}CE_{i}^{j}\quad&\text{if }j>i,\end{cases}

(144)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(1-R_{i}^{j}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)^{n_{j}}\left\|v_{i}^{j+1,n_{j+1}}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}CE_{i}^{j}\quad&\text{if }j\leq i,\\
\left|1-R_{i}^{j}\right|^{n_{j}}\left\|v_{i}^{j+1,n_{j+1}}\right\|+\delta\eta_{j}CE_{i}^{j}\quad&\text{if }j>i.\end{cases}.

since
\theta^{k}_{j,0}=\theta^{k}_{j+1,n_{j+1}}
, where

E_{i}^{j}=\begin{cases}\sum_{s=0}^{n_{j}-1}\left(1-R_{i}^{j}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)^{s}\quad&\text{if }j\leq i,\\
\sum_{s=0}^{n_{j}-1}\left|1-R_{i}^{j}\right|^{s}\quad&\text{if }j>i.\end{cases}.

(145)

By re-applying the inequality in
Equation

144
repeatedly on
\left\|v_{i}^{1,n_{1}}\right\|
we have

\left\|v_{i}^{1,n_{1}}\right\|\leq c_{i}\|v_{i}^{m,0}\|+\delta C\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\eta_{j+1}C_{i}^{j}E_{i}^{j},

(146)

where

c_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{i}\left(1-R_{i}^{j}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)^{n_{j}}\prod_{j=i+1}^{m}\left|1-R^{j}_{i}\right|^{n_{j}},

(147)

matched with the definition given in
Equation

131
for all
i=1:m
and

C_{i}^{j}=\begin{cases}\prod_{s=1}^{j}\left(1-R_{i}^{s}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)^{n_{s}}\quad&\text{if }j\leq i,\\
\prod_{s=1}^{i}\left(1-R_{i}^{s}\kappa_{i}^{-1}\right)^{n_{s}}\prod_{s=i+1}^{j}\left|1-R^{s}_{i}\right|^{n_{s}}\quad&\text{if }j>i.\end{cases}.

(148)

Adopting the notation
\theta^{k}=\theta^{k}_{1,n_{1}}
, for a fixed
k
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\theta^{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\Pi_{i}(\theta^{k}-\theta^{*})\right\|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|v_{i}^{1,n_{1}}\right\|

(149)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\|v_{i}^{m,0}\|+\delta C\sum_{i=1}^{m}1\times\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}C_{i}^{j}E_{i}^{j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{i=1:m}\{c_{i}\}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|v_{i}^{m,0}\|+\delta mC\max_{i=1:m}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}C_{i}^{j}E_{i}^{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i=1:m}\{c_{i}\}\left\|\theta^{k-1}-\theta^{*}\right\|+\delta mC\max_{i=1:m}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}C_{i}^{j}E_{i}^{j}\right\}

since

.
Recalling
Equation

130
in the proof of Theorem
3.4
in Appendix
D.1
, we have

\max_{i=1:m}\{c_{i}\}=c_{m}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-R_{m}^{j}\kappa_{m}^{-1}\right)^{n_{j}},

(150)

if

satisfy the condition in
Equation
