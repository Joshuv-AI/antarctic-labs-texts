Asymptotic Müntz-Szász Theorems
Jim Agler, John E. M c Carthy

Abstract
We define a monomial space to be a subspace of L 2 ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) {\rm L}^{2}([0,1]) that can be approximated by spaces that are spanned by monomial functions. We describe the structure of monomial spaces.



1
Introduction

What sorts of subspaces in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
can be limits of spans of monomials?
Specifically, let

\mathbb{S}=\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\re s>-\frac{1}{2}\},

so that
s\in\mathbb{S}
if and only if
x^{s}\in{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
. For
S
a finite subset of
\mathbb{S}
we let
\mathcal{M}(S)
denote the span in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
of the monomials whose exponents lie in
S
, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}(S)=\{\sum_{s\in S}a(s)x^{s}\,|\,a:S\to\mathbb{C}\}.

We refer to sets in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
that have the form
\mathcal{M}(S)
for some finite subset
S
of
\mathbb{S}
as
finite monomial spaces
.
We are interested in what the limits of such spaces are.

Definition 1.1
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a subspace of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
\{\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
is a sequence of closed subspaces, we say that
\{\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
tends to
\mathcal{M}
and write

\mathcal{M}_{n}\to\mathcal{M}\ \text{ as }\ n\to\infty

if

\mathcal{M}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}\,|\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\dist(f,\mathcal{M}_{n})=0\}.

There are alternative ways to frame this definition; see Proposition
4.1
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that a subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is a
monomial space
if there exists a sequence
\{\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
of finite monomial spaces such that
\mathcal{M}_{n}\to\mathcal{M}
.

The goal of this paper is to study monomial spaces, which have a rich structure and are intimately related to the Müntz-Szasz theorem and generalizations thereof.

1.1
Monotone Monomial Spaces

In this subsection we recall several classical results that can be interpreted as facts about monomial spaces. In each example we consider there is a limit
\mathcal{M}_{n}\to\mathcal{M}
of finite monomial spaces that is monotone, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{j}\ \ \text{ whenever }\ \ i\leq j.

For a detailed account of the results in this section, see
[
4
]
and
[
7
]
.

Example 1.3
.

The Weierstrass Approximation Theorem

Let
S_{n}=\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
. The Weierstrass Theorem, which implies that the polynomials are dense in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
implies that

\mathcal{M}(S_{n})\to{\rm L}^{2}([0,1]).

In particular,
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is a monomial space.

Example 1.4
.

Classical Müntz-Szász Theorem

This was proved in
[
23
,
27
]
.
Fix a strictly increasing sequence of nonnegative integers
s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,
and let

S_{n}=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}.

Then there exists a space
\mathcal{M}
such that

S(\mathcal{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{M}.

Furthermore,

\mathcal{M}={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])\ \ \ \text{ if and only if }\ \ \ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s_{k}}=\infty.

Example 1.5
.

Szász’s Theorem (real case), (a.k.a Full Müntz-Szász Theorem in
{\bf{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}
)
.
This was proved in
[
28
]
.
Fix a sequence of distinct real numbers
s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,
in
\mathbb{S}
and let

S_{n}=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}.

Then there exists a space
\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{M}(S_{n})\to\mathcal{M}.

Furthermore,

\mathcal{M}={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])\ \ \ \text{ if and only if }\ \ \ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2s_{k}+1}{(2s_{k}+1)^{2}+1}=\infty.

Example 1.6
.

Szász’s Theorem (complex case)

Any of the proofs known to the authors of the previous example, including Szász’s original proof, can be adapted to show that if
s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,
is a sequence of distinct points in
\mathbb{S}
and

S_{n}=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\},

then there exists a space
\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{M}(S_{n})\to\mathcal{M},

and where,

\mathcal{M}={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])\ \ \ \text{ if and only if }\ \ \ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\re s_{k}+1}{|s_{k}+1|^{2}}=\infty.

What happens in the above examples when
\mathcal{M}\not={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
?

Example 1.7
.

The Clarkson-Erdős Theorem.

With the setup of Example
1.4
, assume that
\mathcal{M}\not={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
. Then Clarkson and Erdős proved
in
[
12
]
that the elements of
\mathcal{M}
extend to be analytic on
\mathbb{D}
! Furthermore, if
f\in\mathcal{M}
, then
f
has a power series representation of the form

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{s_{k}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

This result was generalized to arbitrary real powers in
(-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
by Erdélyi and Johnson
[
16
]
,
who showed that if
\mathcal{M}\not={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
, then every
f
in
\mathcal{M}
is analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}\setminus(-1,0]

In honor of this remarkable theorem we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.8
.

We say that
\mathcal{M}
is a Clarkson-Erdős space
if there exist a sequence
\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots\}
in
\mathbb{S}

of distinct points
such that

\mathcal{M}(\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\})\to\mathcal{M}

where
\mathcal{M}\not={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
.

We want to allow for multiplicities.
If an entry
s
is repeated in a sequence, this corresponds to multiplicity in the following way.
The first occurrence of
s
in
S_{n}
gives the function
x^{s}
in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. The second occurrence
gives
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}x^{s}=x^{s}\log x
. If
s
occurs
k
times, then
\mathcal{M}_{n}
contains
the functions
x^{s},x^{s}\log x,\dots,x^{s}(\log x)^{k-1}
.
This leads to the following generalization of a Clarkson-Erdős space.

Definition 1.9
.

We say that
\mathcal{M}
is an Erdélyi-Johnson space
if there exist a sequence
\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots\}
in
\mathbb{S}
, with multiplicities allowed,
such that

\mathcal{M}(\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\})\to\mathcal{M}

where
\mathcal{M}\not={\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
.

1.2
A Non-monotone Monomial Space

In the study of monomial spaces it is natural to consider the class of
monomial operators
, i.e., the class of bounded operators
T
acting on
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
that take monomials to monomials, i.e.,

\forall_{s\in\mathbb{S}}\ \ \exists_{\tau\in\mathbb{S}}\ \ \exists_{c\in\mathbb{C}}\ \ Tx^{s}=cx^{\tau}.

(1.10)

In
[
2
]
the authors studied the special case wherein it is assumed that there exists a fixed number
m
such that in (
1.10
)
\tau
can be chosen to equal
s+m
for all
s
. We call these
flat monomial operators.

In the course of proving that flat monomial operators leave
{\rm L}^{2}([a,1])
invariant for each
a\in[0,1]
, the authors discovered the following example.

Example 1.11
.

Fix
\rho\in[0,1]
and choose an increasing sequence of integers
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+N_{n}}=\sqrt{\rho}.

If

S_{n}=\{n+1,n+2,\ldots,n+N_{n}\},

then

\mathcal{M}(S_{n})\to{\rm L}^{2}([\rho,1]).

In particular,
{\rm L}^{2}([\rho,1])
is a monomial space for each
\rho\in[0,1]
, where here and afterwards
we identify
{\rm L}^{2}([\rho,1])
with the subspace of
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
consisting of functions that vanish a.e. on
[0,\rho]
.

1.3
Characterization of monomial spaces

The Hardy operator
H:{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])\to{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is defined by

Hf(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt.

(1.12)

This was introduced by Hardy in
[
20
]
, where he proved it was bounded.
As
Hx^{s}=\frac{1}{s+1}x^{s}
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
, the Hardy operator leaves invariant every monomial space.
The converse is true.

Theorem 1.13
.

A closed subspace of
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is a monomial space if and only if it is invariant for
H
.

A proof of
1.13
that uses real analysis techniques is given in
[
1
]
.

Corollary 1.14
.

A bounded operator
T
on
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is a monomial operator if and only if for every
\mathcal{M}\in{\rm Lat}(H)
,
the space
T\mathcal{M}
is in
{\rm Lat}(H)
.

1.4
A Decomposition Theorem

Definition 1.15
.

We say a space
\mathcal{M}
in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is a
singular space
if
\mathcal{M}
is a monomial space that does not contain any Clarkson-Erdős space.

Theorem 1.16
.

Every monomial space
\mathcal{M}
has a unique decomposition,

\mathcal{M}=\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}+\mathcal{M}_{1}},

where
\mathcal{M}_{0}
is an Erdélyi-Johnson space and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is a singular space.

1.5
Atomic Spaces

Unitary monomial operators can be characterized using a theorem of Bourdon and Narayan
[
8
]
. Their description is equivalent to the following reformulation, from
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.17
.

The operator

T:x^{s}\mapsto c(s)x^{\tau(s)}

is a unitary map from
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
to
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
if and only if
\tau
is a holomorphic automorphism of
\mathbb{S}
and

c
is given by

c(s)\ =\ c_{0}\frac{1+\overline{\tau(0)}+\tau(s)}{1+s},

where
c_{0}
is a constant
satisfying

|c_{0}|\ =\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2{\mathrm{R}e\,}\tau(0)}}.

Definition 1.18
.

We say a space
\mathcal{M}
in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is
atomic
if there exist
\rho\in(0,1)
and a unitary monomial operator
T
such that
\mathcal{M}=T{\rm L}^{2}([\rho,1])
.

We can describe all atomic spaces in the following way.

Theorem 1.19
.

The functions

e_{n}(x)\ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{(\ln x)^{k}}{k!},\qquad n\geq 0.

(1.20)

form an orthonormal basis for
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
.
Furthermore, the operator
J
defined on
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
by requiring

J(e_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{n}&\mbox{if }n\text{ is even}\\
-e_{n}&\mbox{if }n\text{ is odd}\end{array}\right.

is a unitary monomial operator, corresponding to the choice

\tau(s)\ =\ \frac{-s}{1+2s},\qquad c(s)\ =\ \frac{1}{1+2s}

(1.21)

in Theorem
1.17
.

The polynomials

p_{n}(t)\ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{(t)^{k}}{k!},\qquad n\geq 0,

are the Laguerre polynomials. They are the orthogonal polynomials on
[0,\infty)
for the measure
e^{-t}dt
.
Under the change of variables
x=e^{-t}
they become the functions
e_{n}
in (
1.20
).
Their connection to the Hardy operator was shwon in
[
22
]
.

If
c
is real, the multiplication operator
M_{x^{ic}}
is also a unitary monomial operator.
These two operators can be used to build the general atomic space.

1.

For any
w>0
, define

\mathcal{A}_{1,w}\ :=\ L^{2}([e^{-2w},1]).

2.

Define

\mathcal{A}_{-1,w}\ :=\ J\,\mathcal{A}_{1,w}

3.

For any
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\{1\}
, define

\mathcal{A}_{\tau,w}\ :=\ M_{x^{ic}}\,\mathcal{A}_{-1,w^{\prime}},\qquad{\rm where\ }2ic=\frac{\tau+1}{\tau-1},\ w^{\prime}=(1+4c^{2})w.

(The reason for the strange scaling is to simplify the formulas in Section
5
).
Atomic spaces are all of the form
\mathcal{A}_{\tau,w}
.

Theorem 1.22
.

Every atomic space is equal to
\mathcal{A}_{\tau,w}
for exactly one pair
(\tau,w)\in{\mathbb{T}}\times(0,\infty)
.

1.6
The Structure of Singular Spaces

Say
\mathcal{M}
is
finitely atomic
if
\mathcal{M}
is a finite sum of atomic spaces.
If

\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ w_{k}\ \delta_{\tau_{k}}

is a finitely atomic measure on
\mathbb{T}
, with distinct atoms
\tau_{k}
, we define a finitely atomic space in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
by the formula

\mathcal{M}(\mu)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{\tau_{k},w_{k}}

Theorem 1.23
.

The assignment
\mu\mapsto\mathcal{M}(\mu)
extends by weak-* sequential continuity to
a map from the positive singular Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}
into closed subspaces of
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
. When extended,

\mu_{n}\to\mu\text{ weak-* }\implies\mathcal{M}(\mu_{n})\to\mathcal{M}(\mu).

1.7
The main idea

The Hardy operator is unitarily equivalent to
1-S^{*}
, where
S
is the unilateral shift, via a unitary

U:{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])\to H^{2}
that we call the Sarason transform, described in Section
2
.
It follows that the invariant subspaces of
H
can be described by Beurling’s theorem
[
6
]
in terms of model spaces, the invariant subspaces for the backward shift described for example in
[
18
]
.
However, all the theorems above have been stated in terms that are
intrinsic
to
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
.
We believe that finding proofs that are also intrinsic to
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
will illuminate this space with a new light.
So far, the authors have only succeeded in doing this for some of these results.

Open Problem 1.24
.

Find real analysis proofs to Theorems
1.16
and
1.23
.

2
The Sarason Transform

2.1
The Definition

We let
k_{\alpha}
denote the Szegö kernel function for
{\rm H}^{2}
, the classical Hardy space of square integrable functions on
\mathbb{D}
, i.e.,

k_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}z},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

As the monomials are linearly independant in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
, there is a well defined map
L
defined on polynomials in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
into
{\rm H}^{2}
defined by the formula

L(\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}x^{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\frac{1}{n+1}k_{\frac{n}{n+1}}

Noting that

\langle\,L(x^{i})\,,L(x^{j})\,\rangle_{{\rm H}^{2}}=\langle\,x^{i}\,,x^{j}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

for all nonnegative integers
i
and
j
, it follows that

\langle\,L(p)\,,L(q)\,\rangle_{{\rm H}^{2}}=\langle\,p\,,q\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

for all polynomials
p
and
q
, i.e.,
L
is isometric. Hence, as the polynomials are dense in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
,
L
has a unique extension to an isometry
U
defined on all of
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
. Finally, noting that

\{\frac{n}{n+1}\,|\,n\text{ is a nonegative integer}\}

is a set of uniqueness for
{\rm H}^{2}
, it follows that the range of
L
is dense in
{\rm H}^{2}
, which implies that
U
is a unitary transformation from
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
onto
{\rm H}^{2}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We let
U
denote the unique unitary transformation from
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
onto
{\rm H}^{2}
that satisfies

U(x^{n})=\frac{1}{n+1}k_{\frac{n}{n+1}}

for all nonnegative integers
n
.

We call
U
the Sarason transform, as it is similar to the transform from
L^{2}([0,\infty))
onto
{\rm H}^{2}
used in

[
26
]
.

2.2
Moments in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
and Interpolation in
{\rm H}^{2}

As the monomials are dense in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
, a function
f\in{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
is uniquely determined by its moment sequence

\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}\ f(x)dx,\qquad n=0,1,\ldots.

Similarly, as the sequence
\{1-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\,|\,n=0,1,\ldots\}
is a set of uniqueness for
{\rm H}^{2}
, a function
h\in{\rm H}^{2}
is the unique solution
g
in
{\rm H}^{2}
to the interpolation problem

g(\frac{n}{n+1})=h(\frac{n}{n+1}),\qquad n=0,1,\ldots

The following proposition follows immediately from Definition
2.1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Fix a sequence of complex numbers
w_{0},w_{1},w_{2},\ldots
. If
f
in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
solves the moment problem

\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}\ f(x)\ dx=w_{n},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,

then
Uf\in{\rm H}^{2}
and solves the interpolation problem

Uf(\frac{n}{n+1})=(n+1)w_{n},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots\ .

If
h\in{\rm H}^{2}
solves the interpolation problem

h(\frac{n}{n+1})=w_{n},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,

then
U^{*}h\in{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
and solves the moment problem

\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}\ U^{*}h(x)\ dx=\frac{1}{n+1}w_{n},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots\ .

The correspondence between moments and interpolation described in the preceding proposition allows us to easily calculate the Sarason transform of many common functions. We illustrate this with the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}
, then

U^{*}(k_{\alpha})(x)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}}x^{\tfrac{\bar{\alpha}}{1-\bar{\alpha}}},\qquad x\in[0,1].

(2.4)

If
Re\ \beta>-\frac{1}{2}
, then

U(x^{\beta})\ =\ \frac{1}{\beta+1}k_{\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\bar{\beta}+1}}.

Proof.

We note that the two assertions of the lemma are equivalent. Therefore it suffices to prove (
2.4
). Since the left and right hand sides of (
2.4
) are in
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
, to show (
2.4
) it suffices to show that for each
n\geq 0

\langle\,x^{n}\,,U^{*}k_{\alpha}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}=\langle\,x^{n}\,,\frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}}x^{\tfrac{\bar{\alpha}}{1-\bar{\alpha}}}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}.

But

\displaystyle\langle\,x^{n}\,,\frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}}x^{\tfrac{\bar{\alpha}}{1-\bar{\alpha}}}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\langle\,x^{n}\,,x^{\tfrac{\bar{\alpha}}{1-\bar{\alpha}}}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\ x^{n}\ \overline{x^{\tfrac{\bar{\alpha}}{1-\bar{\alpha}}}}\ dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\ \frac{1}{n+\tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\ \frac{1}{1-\frac{n}{n+1}\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}k_{\frac{n}{n+1}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=(Ux^{n})(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\langle\,Ux^{n}\,,k_{\alpha}\,\rangle_{{\rm H}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\,x^{n}\,,U^{*}k_{\alpha}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

∎

For
S
a measurable set in
[0,1]
let
\chi_{S}
denote the characteristic function of
S
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
s\in[0,1]
, then

U\chi_{[0,s]}(z)=\sqrt{s}\ e^{\tfrac{1}{2}\ln s\ \frac{1+z}{1-z}}

(2.6)

Proof.

We first observe that

\displaystyle\frac{s^{n+1}}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}x^{n}\ dx

\displaystyle=\langle\,\chi_{[0,s]}\,,x^{n}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=\langle\,U\chi_{[0,s]}\,,Ux^{n}\,\rangle_{{\rm H}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\,U\chi_{[0,s]}\,,\frac{1}{n+1}k_{\frac{n}{n+1}}\,\rangle_{{\rm H}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}U\chi_{[0,s]}(\frac{n}{n+1}),

so that

U\chi_{[0,s]}(\frac{n}{n+1})=s^{n+1}

for all
n\geq 0
. On the other hand if for
w>0
we let
E_{w}
denote the singular inner function defined by

E_{w}(z)=e^{-w\frac{1+z}{1-z}}=e^{w}e^{-\frac{2w}{1-z}},

we have that

\displaystyle E_{w}(\frac{n}{n+1})

\displaystyle=e^{w}e^{-2w(n+1)}=e^{w}(e^{-2w})^{n+1}

for all
n\geq 0
. Hence if we choose
w=-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln s
,

U\chi_{[0,s]}(\frac{n}{n+1})=e^{-w}E_{w}(\frac{n}{n+1})

for all
n\geq 0
. Since
\{1-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\,|\,n\geq 0\}
is a set of uniqueness for
{\rm H}^{2}
, it follows that

U\chi_{[0,s]}(z)=e^{-w}E_{w}(z)

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
, which implies (
2.6
).
∎

2.3
A Formula for the
Sarason Transform

Proposition 2.7
.

If
f\in{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
, then

Uf(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)x^{\frac{z}{1-z}}dx

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle Uf(z)

\displaystyle=\langle\,Uf\,,k_{z}\,\rangle_{{\rm H}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\,f\,,U^{*}k_{z}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle(Lemma\eqref{sa.lem.10})\qquad

\displaystyle=\langle\,f\,,\frac{1}{1-\bar{z}}x^{\tfrac{\bar{z}}{1-\bar{z}}}\,\rangle_{{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-z}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)x^{\frac{z}{1-z}}dx

∎

To obtain a nonrigorous, but highly interesting proof of the proposition,
let us
define the Sarason Transform
of a measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
,
\mathcal{S}\{\mu\}
, to be the holomorphic function

\mathcal{S}\{\mu\}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\int x^{\frac{z}{1-z}}d\mu(x),\qquad|z|<1.

Note that

\mathcal{S}(\chi_{[0,s]})=\sqrt{s}\ e^{\ln\sqrt{s}\ \frac{1+z}{1-z}}=U(\chi_{[0,s]}).

We have

\mathcal{S}\{\delta_{s}\}\ =\ \frac{1}{1-z}s^{\frac{1}{1-z}-1}=\frac{1}{1-z}s^{\frac{z}{1-z}}.

So formally we get

Uf=\mathcal{S}(\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\delta_{x}\ dx)=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\mathcal{S}(\delta_{x})\ dx=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\frac{1}{1-z}x^{\frac{z}{1-z}}dx,

the formula in Proposition 2.17.

2.4
Transforms of

The Hardy operator

{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])

2.6
The Sarason Transform and the Laplace Transform

Recall that the Laplace Transform is defined by the formula

\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)dt.

Further, if for
f\in{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
we define
f^{\sim}
by the formula

f^{\sim}(t)=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}f(e^{-t}),\qquad t\in(o,\infty),

then the assignment
f\mapsto f^{\sim}
is a Hilbert space isomorphism from
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
onto
{\rm L}^{2}(0,\infty)
. By making the substitution
x=e^{-t}
we find that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f(x)x^{\frac{z}{1-z}}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\infty}^{0}f(e^{-t})\ e^{-t\frac{z}{1-z}}\ (-e^{-t})\ dt

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\frac{t}{2}}f(e^{-t})\ e^{-t(\frac{z}{1-z}+\frac{1}{2})}\ dt

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}f^{\sim}(t)\ e^{-t(\frac{1}{2}\frac{1+z}{1-z})}\ dt

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\{f^{\sim}\}(\frac{1}{2}\frac{1+z}{1-z}).

Hence,

Uf(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\mathcal{L}\{f^{\sim}\}(\frac{1}{2}\frac{1+z}{1-z}).

So after changes of variable from
{\rm L}^{2}([0,1])
of the disc to
{\rm L}^{2}(0,\infty)
and from
{\rm H}^{2}
to
{\rm H}^{2}
of the right half plane, the Sarason Transform is simply the Laplace Transform.

Lemma 3.1

(H^{*})^{j}\ 1=(-1)^{j}\frac{(\ln x)^{j}}{j!}

(3.2)

z^{n}\sim\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\frac{(\ln x)^{j}}{j!}
