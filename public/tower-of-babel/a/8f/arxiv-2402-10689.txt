Multi-Cultural Commonsense Knowledge Distillation
Tuan-Phong Nguyen, Simon Razniewski, Gerhard Weikum ♠ \spadesuit Max Planck Institute for Informatics, Saarbrücken, Germany ♢ \diamondsuit Bosch Center for Artificial Intelligence, Renningen, Germany {tuanphong,weikum}@mpi-inf.mpg.desimon.razniewski@de.bosch.com

Abstract
Despite recent progress, large language models (LLMs) still face the challenge of appropriately reacting to the intricacies of social and cultural conventions. This paper presents Mango , a methodology for distilling high-accuracy, high-recall assertions of cultural knowledge. We judiciously and iteratively prompt LLMs for this purpose from two entry points, concepts and cultures. Outputs are consolidated via clustering and generative summarization. Running the Mango method with GPT-3.5 as underlying LLM yields 167K high-accuracy assertions for 30K concepts and 11K cultures, surpassing prior resources by a large margin. For extrinsic evaluation, we explore augmenting dialogue systems with cultural knowledge assertions. We find that adding knowledge from Mango improves the overall quality, specificity, and cultural sensitivity of dialogue responses, as judged by human annotators. Data and code are available for download 1 1 1 https://bit.ly/cultural-csk .

Introduction

Motivation:

The theme of enhancing computers with commonsense knowledge (CSK) has been revived in recent years, to improve the robustness and versatility of AI-powered NLP methods (e.g.,
Bosselut et al. 2019
;
Hwang et al. 2021
;
West et al. 2022
;
Choi 2022
). CSK collections, such as ConceptNet
Speer et al. 2017
and ATOMIC
Sap et al. 2019
, capture assertions about mundane objects and human activities in a broad sense, so as to provide universal background knowledge.
However, norms for human behavior are often diverse, with substantial diversity across different parts of the world and different socio-cultural groups.
For example, when two people meet, they shake hands in the Western world, but this would be uncommon in
Southeast Asia (where people
wai
each other), and teenagers have yet another way of greeting.
Therefore, to adapt AI applications to specific user contexts, the goal of this research is to capture
culturally aware
commonsense knowledge,
CCSK
for short.

An NLP task where cultural knowledge can play a game-changing role is human-computer dialogue, where the system should consider the human’s cultural background. An even more challenging case is conversations between two partners from different cultures; we call this case
intercultural dialogue
.
Computers can take one, both or none of the roles in this setting; in the latter case, it could still be important to have cultural knowledge to analyze and post-process the dialogue (e.g., generate a de-briefing summary).

State of the Art and its Limitations:

On this nascent direction, resources for cultural knowledge have limited scope and scale (e.g.,
Acharya et al. 2020
, StereoKG
Deshpande et al. 2022
, Candle
Nguyen et al. 2023b
). The by far largest of these is the Candle knowledge base,
which comprises ca. 60K assertions automatically distilled from nearly 1M sentences mined from a large web crawl.
This is still insufficient in coverage of concepts and cultural groups.
Note that cultures are not just geo-regions, but should ideally consider also demographic and social traits of the respective groups (e.g., female teenagers in Korea, French people of North-African descent, etc.).

An obvious idea is to abandon explicit assertions, and instead rely on the latent knowledge provided by large language models (LLMs).
Indeed, LLMs perform well on tasks that require understanding the personal background of users in conversational settings. However, there are severe disadvantages:
(i) strong dependence on the quality of the training corpus (which may exhibit certain biases and risks of hallucination);
(ii) lack of transparency of what piece of knowledge is utilized in which context;
(iii) as a result, lack of controllability for programmed applications;
and
(iv) lack of interpretability by end-users.
For these reasons, our approach is to construct transparent and tangible collections of explicit assertions, which can later be judiciously injected into LLMs for specific tasks.

Challenges and Research Questions:

The twofold challenge is to expand the
coverage
of cultural groups and culture-specific assertions, while maintaining or even improving the
quality
of the assertions.
This poses three research questions:

•

RQ1:
How can we systematically capture more and diverse cultural groups?

•

RQ2:
How can we substantially enlarge the amount of captured assertions, going beyond single-sentence retrieval from the web?

•

RQ3:
How can we ensure the specificity of assertions, while avoiding undue stereotypes and limiting redundancy due to frequency bias?

Approach and Contribution:

The methodology devised in this work, called
Mango

(
m
ulti-cultur
a
l commo
n
sense knowled
g
e distillati
o
n),
comprises two stages: a coverage-oriented stage for
assertion generation
, and a quality-oriented stage for
assertion consolidation
. The first stage addresses RQ1 and RQ2, and the second addresses RQ3.

To enlarge the pool of candidates, we leverage
a large language model (LLM), specifically GPT-3.5, to generate assertions. Unlike prior works that tapped into web crawls, this is an implicit way of tapping the LLM’s pre-training collection. Compared to web retrieval, the scales are similar but the advantage is that LLM training data involves efforts to remove spam and offensive content.
Asking an LLM to generate assertions requires judicious prompting. For high coverage of diverse cultures, an important novelty in our method is to construct prompts for both concepts (incl. human activities) from an existing (culturally agnostic) commonsense knowledge base and groups from a large pool of cultures.

To ensure the informativeness of assertions and to tame the redundancy of generated candidates (RQ3), the second stage consists of steps for assertion consolidation.
This is carried out by clustering the pool of assertions
into topically and culturally coherent groups, considering both the key concept in an assertion and the culture to which it refers. The LLM is leveraged again to generate a concise summary statement for each cluster. This exploits the LLM’s language skills, but does not rely on actual knowledge by the LLM.

The main contributions of this work are:

1.

The
Mango
methodology for efficiently distilling CCSK from LLMs, at high precision and recall.

2.

Running the
Mango
method with GPT-3.5, to obtain 167K assertions for 30K concepts and 11K cultures, substantially surpassing prior CCSK resources in size and quality.

3.

An extrinsic evaluation for intercultural dialogues, where the injection of
Mango
assertions significantly improves the specificity and cultural sensitivity of responses.

Cultural Commonsense Knowledge Distillation Workflow

We propose
Mango
, a workflow for distilling and consolidating cultural commonsense knowledge (CCSK) from large language models (LLMs).

Definition 1

(Cultural commonsense knowledge assertion)
.

A cultural commonsense knowledge (CCSK) assertion
a=(c;g;s)
is a triple of a concept
c
; a culture/cultural group
g
; and a natural-language statement
s
that represents a cultural belief, norm, or common practice about the concept
c
that applies to group
g
.
An example CCSK assertion is: (
tipping
;
Japan
;
Not a common practice
).

The goal of
Mango
is to generate CCSK for diverse cultures, covering a wide variety of concepts. Moreover, by clustering similar assertions, we obtain ranking signals for the assertions, which can be useful for downstream applications.

Our workflow consists of 2 phases, each consisting of 2 steps: (1a) generating CCSK for a given concept, (1b) generating CCSK for a given culture (Section
3.1
), (2a) clustering similar CCSK assertions, and (2b) generating cluster representatives (Section
3.2
).
An overview of our workflow is depicted in Figure
1
.
We also provide input and output examples for each step in Table
1
.

3.1
Phase 1: Assertion Generation

People of different cultures may have different perspectives on certain concepts.
These differences are what we are interested in, as they are crucial in situations where cross-cultural knowledge is required to understand one another.
One might ask LLMs to generate CCSK for a given pair of concept
and
culture (e.g., prompting for perceptions of
tipping
in
Japan
).
However, the main drawback of this approach is that it requires concept and culture pairings, which is problematic because of the large number of possible combinations, and the fact that some combinations may not make sense.

Instead, we propose to use LLMs to generate CCSK for a given concept or a given cultural group
separately
.
This way we let the model decide which concepts are relevant for a given cultural group and vice versa, which will reduce the chance of nonsensical concept-culture combinations, hence reducing costs.
Example prompts and outputs can be seen in Table
1
.

For each prompt, we provide the LLM with 5 pairs of example assertions randomly drawn from a set of human-written CCSK assertions.
Each example consists of a concept, and 2 different perspectives on that concept associated with 2 or more different cultures (more details on prompt templates can be found in Appendix
A.1
).
We implement our workflow using GPT-3.5.
However, in practice, other LLMs could be used.
We also run each prompt several times at a high temperature (i.e., high creativity), that can hopefully lead to different output assertions given the same input.

Step 1a: Generating CCSK for a given concept

We seed this step with concepts from ConceptNet
Speer et al. 2017
, which is a popular commonsense knowledge base constructed by human annotations and consists of a wide range of everyday concepts.
In addition, we also use cultural concepts from Candle
Nguyen et al. 2023b
as inputs for this step.
Candle is a cultural knowledge base constructed by extracting relevant sentences from a cleaned web crawl.

Step 1b: Generating CCSK for a given culture

The seed cultures used for this step are taken from the Candle dataset, which include geo-locations (countries, continents, geo-regions), and religions.

Iterative Generation

Step 1a and Step 1b are processed independently, and each step can generate new cultural groups and concepts, respectively.
These new concepts and cultural groups can be fed back to the corresponding approach in order to generate new CCSK assertions.

3.2
Phase 2: Assertion Consolidation

It is natural that our CCSK generation approaches produce similar assertions that have very little or zero semantic differences.
The repetitions can come from the fact that there exist concepts with similar meanings (e.g.,
tea
,
drinking tea
, and
tea drinking
), and terms referring to the same groups (e.g.,
East Asia
,
East Asian cultures
, and
East Asian countries
) in the input.
Furthermore, as we run each prompt several times, even at high output randomness (temperature), repetitions are inevitable.

While untreated redundancy is undesirable, we propose to take advantage of these redundancies in order to rank our assertions.
Specifically, by grouping together semantically similar assertions, we create frequency signals, which can be useful for downstream applications which only look for a subset of highest-significance assertions.

Step 2a: Assertion Clustering

As we generate hundreds of thousands of assertions in Phase 1, it is prohibitively expensive to run a clustering algorithm on all assertions at once.
It would even be infeasible for algorithms such as the Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering (HAC) algorithm to process such a large amount of data points.
Instead, we propose a divide-and-conquer approach to clustering this large set of assertions.
Our approach consists of 3 substeps:

1.

Clustering similar concepts:
The concepts in the assertions generated in Phase 1 are clustered into groups of similar concepts.

2.

Clustering similar cultures:
Similarly to concept clustering, we group together similar cultures.

3.

Clustering subsets of assertions:
For each pair of concept cluster and culture cluster, we only cluster the subset of assertions associated with any of the concepts and any of the cultures in those clusters.

As the sizes of the entire concept and culture sets and the corresponding assertion subsets are substantially smaller than that of the entire set of generated assertions, standard clustering algorithms can process them efficiently.
In our experiment, we use SentenceBert embeddings
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
and the HAC algorithm for all 3 substeps.
Nevertheless, in practice, other text embedding models and clustering algorithms could be used.
An example assertion cluster can be found in Table
1
, in which the respective concept cluster includes
tipping
,
leaving tip
,
tipping at restaurants
,
tipping service staff
, and the culture cluster includes
Japan
,
Japanese
,
Japanese culture
.

Step 2b: Representative Generation

For each cluster of CCSK assertions, we generate a
full sentence
as the representative of the cluster.
We also obtain the frequency of each cluster by adding up all the frequencies of its member assertions.

For the final output, we store a set of CCSK cluster instances, each containing the following information:

concept
(the representative concept of the CCSK cluster),

culture
(the representative culture of the CCSK cluster),

statement
(the representative sentence of the CCSK cluster),

similar statements
(the set of the statements of the member assertions),
and

frequency
(the frequency sum of the cluster).

After Phase 1, we obtain 581,563 distinct CCSK assertions.
We perform various simple filters to clean these assertions:

•

Removing too long or too short assertions: We restrict the length of assertion to be between 2 and 25 words.

•

Removing assertions that have more than one sentence in the generated statements.

•

Removing assertions whose cultures are too general or noisy: We construct a dictionary of words that a valid culture should not contain. The assertion generation steps costed $71.

Quality of
Mango
Assertions

We evaluate the assertion quality of
Mango
by comparison to several resources of similar kind.

Compared Resources:

Our baseline resources are Quasimodo
Romero et al. 2019
, StereoKG
Deshpande et al. 2022
and Candle
Nguyen et al. 2023b
.
StereoKG and Candle are specialized in culture-specific knowledge.
Quasimodo is a general commonsense knowledge base, but for this comparison, we only consider Quasimodo assertions that contain geo-locations or religions.

StereoKG and Candle store assertions in the form of natural-language sentences, while Quasimodo assertions originally come in triple format (subject-predicate-object).
Therefore, for comparability, we verbalized the Quasimodo assertions using GPT-3.5.

For our resource, in addition to the full assertion set (
Mango
{}^{\textit{full}}
), we also evaluate
Mango
{}^{\textit{top}}
, which contains the top-ranked assertions up to the same size as Candle (i.e., 60K assertions).

Metrics:

CCSK assertions should satisfy a set of conditions, that are sometimes in conflict. We employ three dimensions
Bhatia and Shwartz 2023
to evaluate their intrinsic quality:

1.

Cultural Relevance (CR)
measures the plausibility of the assertion, i.e., whether the statement actually represents cultural knowledge.

2.

Stereotype Avoidance (SA)
assesses if the assertion avoids reinforcing cultural stereotypes and presenting offensive materials.

3.

Linguistic Accuracy (LA)
checks if the assertion is grammatical, meaningful and understandable as a standalone sentence.

Setup:

Our evaluation is conducted on the Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) platform.
Specifically, we ask human annotators on MTurk to evaluate each assertion along the 3 dimensions, each on a 3-point Likert scale:
negation
(0),
ambiguity
(1) and
affirmation
(2) (the evaluation questions can be found in Appendix
B
and Figure
3
).
For each resource, we randomly sample 300 assertions for evaluation,
each assessed by 3 different workers.

6.1
Next Utterance Generation

The input of this task includes: (1) a narrative that involves interactions between 2 people of different cultures, and (2) their ongoing dialogue.
The task is to generate the utterance of the next speaker.
To excel in this task, LLMs need to leverage cultural knowledge such that the generated responses are natural, consistent, culturally sensitive and specific.
We provide an example of input and output for this task in Table
5
.
More examples and details of prompting techniques can be found in Appendix
C
.

Input

Narrative

John, an American, is visiting his friend Kenji, who lives in Tokyo. They are paying their bill for dinner at a restaurant.

Ongoing dialogue

John:
That’s a great meal, Kenji. I really liked the sushi.

Kenji:
My pleasure, John. I’m glad you enjoyed it.

John:
Let me see the bill. It is 8,000 yen. I’m gonna leave 10,000 yen.

Output

Method 1: Vanilla GPT-3.5

Kenji:
Thank you, John. You’re too kind. Next time, dinner is on me. It’s a very generous tip too.

Injected CCSK:

Input Construction:

First, using GPT-3.5 and a small set of few-shot examples, we generate short narratives involving 2 people of different cultures, e.g.,
“Liz from England is visiting her friend Qiang in China. They are preparing tea together.”
.
Then, for each narrative, we ask the LLM to generate a possible dialogue between the 2 participants in that situation.
Finally, the first 3 utterances of each generated dialogue are used to construct test samples.
Prompt templates and narrative examples can be found in Appendix
C.1
and Table
8
.

Methods:

We compare 2 methods, without and with explicit CCSK assertions in the prompts.

Method 1 (Vanilla LLM)
,
prompts an LLM with the task description, the input narrative, and the dialogue history.
On the other hand,
Method 2 (LLM with explicit CCSK)
,
alters the prompts of
Method 1
by augmenting relevant CCSK.
For that, we retrieve the top-2 assertions from
Mango
, based on cosine similarity between embeddings of the input narrative and our CCSK assertions.
We provide more details on the retrieval technique in Appendix
C.2
.
The templates for prompting LLMs with this task can be found in Appendix
C.3
.

We experiment with 3 different LLMs from some of the most popular model families: (i) GPT-3.5

Specifically,
we show side-by-side the outputs of the 2 methods given the same input narrative, and ask the evaluators to choose the better one based on the following dimensions:

1.

Naturalness:
The response does not sound awkward or unnatural.

2.

Consistency:
The response does not contradict the narrative or previous utterances.

3.

Specificity:
The response contains specific details rather than vague/generic information.

4.

Cultural Sensitivity:
The response shows respect and understanding of the cultures.

5.

Overall Quality:
The overall satisfaction with the response.

Dimensions 1-3 and 5 are established criteria for evaluating dialogues
Mehri et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023

7
Conclusion

We presented
Mango
, a methodology to distill and consolidate CCSK from LLMs.
We executed the
Mango
workflow using GPT-3.5, obtaining a collection of 167K assertions covering 11K cultures and 30K concepts, which surpasses existing resources in quality and coverage by a large margin.
In the extrinsic evaluation, we showed that by augmenting explicit assertions from
Mango
into prompts, LLMs can perform better in intercultural dialogue generation tasks as their responses were judged more specific, more culturally sensitive, and better overall quality by human evaluators.

Limitations

Although our method for CCSK distillation outperforms text extraction by a large margin in terms of quality and size, there are three inherent limitations when using GPT-3.5: (1) Since GPT-3.5 is a commercial product provided via an API only, it cannot be guaranteed that our pipeline is long-term reproducible. (2) Since the training process of GPT-3.5 is undocumented, we do not know what corpus it has been trained on, and in particular, whether there might be systematic gaps in its training corpus (know what you don’t know).
(3) Not specific to GPT-3.5, the usage of LLMs for CCSK distillation means losing the ability to trace assertions to their source, compared with text extraction.

Ethics Statement

Adapting conversational AI to cultural contexts is important for opening systems to diverse communities. At the same time, CCSK represents dual-use technology, as it can also be used to reinforce stereotypes, and discriminate.

Our data represents the outputs of a research prototype. We recommend against using our data in production systems without a careful evaluation of benefits (of having a system that is more culturally adept) and associated challenges and risks (of stereotype perpetuation etc.).

C.2
CCSK Retrieval

We use a simple embedding-based retrieval approach.
First, we compute the embeddings of all assertions in our
Mango
dataset using the SentenceBert model
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
.
Then, given a narrative, we replace the names of the people with “X” and “Y” in order to reduce the distractions for the embedding model.
For each given narrative, we retrieve the top-2 most similar CCSK assertions from
Mango
based on cosine similarity of the embeddings.
We also restrict the similarity to be better than 0.5.
We only consider narratives that have relevant CCSK assertions retrieved from
Mango
in the human evaluation.
Examples of CCSK retrieval can be seen in Table
9
.
