Branching with selection and mutation I: Mutant fitness of Fréchet type
Su-Chan Park, Joachim Krug, Léo Touzo, Peter Mörters

Abstract
We investigate two stochastic models of a growing population subject to selection and mutation. In our models each individual carries a fitness which determines its mean offspring number. Many of these offspring inherit their parent’s fitness, but some are mutants and obtain a fitness randomly sampled from a distribution in the domain of attraction of the Fréchet distribution. We give a rigorous proof for the precise rate of superexponential growth of these stochastic processes and support the argument by a heuristic and numerical study of the mechanism underlying this growth.

Introduction

While the theory of branching processes is undoubtedly one of the best developed areas of probability theory, stochastic branching models
that incorporate effects of selection and mutation have only recently caught the attention of mathematicians and physicists. This is despite the
unquestionable relevance of these effects to the evolution of populations in nature and in the laboratory
Park2010
;
Wiser2013
.

By contrast, deterministic high density models of a population undergoing selection and mutation have been studied for quite some time. The model most closely associated with our stochastic process is Kingman’s model
Kingman1978
. This is a dynamical system on the space of probability measures describing the fitness distribution of a population. After
t
generations the fitness distribution
p_{t}
of the population is replaced by

p_{t+1}(dx)=(1-\beta)\frac{x\,p_{t}(dx)}{\int x\,p_{t}(dx)}+\beta\mu(dx).

Here a proportion
1-\beta
of the new generation has been selected from the current generation proportionally to their fitness
and a proportion
\beta
are mutants that get a new fitness, sampled independently from their past using the mutant fitness
distribution
\mu
. Note that this model is only well-defined if the mean fitness remains finite and therefore requires moment bounds for
the mutant fitness distribution. Kingman’s model undergoes a condensation phase transition,
which is further studied in
Dereich2013
. Variants of the model
have been considered in
Yuan
;
Yuan2020
;
Yuan2022
.

The study of individual based, stochastic models is much more recent. Park and Krug
Park2008
studied Kingman’s model for an unbounded
fitness distribution alongside a random model of fixed, finite population size.
Despite its highly simplified nature, the finite population model with an exponential distribution
of fitness effects is qualitatively consistent with the fitness increase observed in Lenski’s long-term
evolution experiment with bacteria
Sibani1998
;
Wiser2013
.
A generalization of this model that includes the response of the immune system to a population
of pathogens was considered in
Bianconi2011
.

The first papers studying branching process models
that express the selective advantage of a fit individual in terms of its offspring distribution are
Dereich2017
, which deals
with Weibull type fitness distributions and puts the focus on the condensation phenomenon in that model, and
Mailler2021

which looks at the growth of the fittest family in the case of Gumbel type distributions. Both papers are limited to bounded
fitness distributions and implicitly rely on the analogy to Kingman’s original model, though of course the methods of study
are entirely different in a stochastic setting. The present paper initiates the study of branching processes with selection and mutation for
unbounded fitness distributions. We focus on the case of Fréchet type fitness distributions where the mathematical challenge is linked to
the fact that the analogous Kingman model is ill-defined
Park2008
.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In Sec.
2
, we introduce the models and state the main theorem. Sec.
3
explains the heuristics behind
the formal results and in Sec.
4
we present a rigorous proof of the theorem. Sec.
5
contains refined
results for the empirical frequency distribution for one of our models. These results are not yet accessible by a complete rigorous mathematical
analysis, so that we resort to a numerical and heuristic study and a rigorous analysis of an approximating deterministic system. In Sec.
6
we provide a short discussion that places our results into the context
of previous work and points to directions for future research.

2
Models and main result

We study two models of a population evolving in discrete generations. In both models all individuals are assigned a fitness value, which is
a positive real number. As model parameters we fix a probability distribution
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
from which the random fitness values
F
are sampled,
and a mutation probability
\beta\in(0,1)
.

In both models we start from generation
t=0
with a
single individual
1
1

1

The generalization to multiple individuals is straightforward.

with fitness
f
.
Each individual in the population in generation
t\geq 0
produces a Poisson random number of
offspring with mean given by its fitness. With probability
1-\beta
an offspring individual
inherits its parent’s fitness and is added to the population at generation
t+1
.
Otherwise, with probability
\beta
, it is a mutant. The two models differ in the fate of the mutants.

•

Fittest mutant model (FMM):
Every mutant is assigned a fitness sampled independently from
\mu
.
Only the fittest mutant (if there is one) is added to the population at generation
t+1
. All other mutants die instantly.

•

Multiple mutant model (MMM):
Every mutant is assigned a fitness sampled independently from
\mu
and is added to the population at generation
t+1
.

We write
X(t)
for the number of individuals in generation
t
and study the growth of the populations conditioned
on the event of survival, i.e. when
X(t)\neq 0
for all times
t
.
It is easy to see that the population size of the
MMM dominates the population size of the FMM at all times. Because the growth is determined by the fittest
mutants we expect both models to grow at the same rate and to show this, it suffices to find an upper bound for the MMM and a matching lower bound for the FMM.

Naturally, the rate of growth depends on the mutant fitness distribution
\mu
. If
\mu
is an unbounded distribution
in both models individuals of ever increasing fitness occur and hence the population will grow
superexponentially fast. By contrast, if
\mu
is bounded we can only have exponential growth. Indeed, if

\mu
is continuous with essential supremum one,
then for a closely related continuous time model of immortal individuals,
it is shown in
(
Dereich2017
, Remark 1)
that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log X(t)}{t}=\lambda^{*},

where
\lambda^{*}\in[1-\beta,1)
is the unique solution of the equation

\beta\int\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}-(1-\beta)x}\,\mu(dx)=1

if
\beta\int\frac{1}{1-x}\,\mu(dx)\geq 1
, and otherwise
\lambda^{*}:=1-\beta
.
Further details on the long term growth of the process depend on the classification
of
\mu
according to its membership in the max domain of attraction of an
extremal distribution. By the celebrated Fisher-Tippett theorem
there are three such universality classes, see for example
(
Resnick
, Proposition 0.3)
. These are

•

the
Weibull class
, which roughly occurs if
\mu
is bounded with mass decaying slowly
near the essential supremum,

•

the
Gumbel class
, which roughly occurs if the mass of
\mu
is decaying quickly near the essential supremum,
which may be finite or infinite,

•

the
Fréchet class
, which roughly occurs if
\mu
is unbounded with mass decaying slowly
near infinity.

The assignment of mutant fitness distributions to extreme value universality classes plays an important role in the interpretation of evolution experiments
Joyce2008
, and
representatives of all three classes have been identified empirically
Das2022
.

In the present paper, we are mainly interested in the asymptotic behaviour of the population size
X(t)

in the so far unexplored case that
\mu
belongs to the Fréchet class (or, in short, is of
Fréchet type
).
Precisely, this means that the tail function

G(x):=\mu((x,\infty))=\mathbb{P}(F>x)

is regularly varying with index
-\alpha
for some
\alpha>0
. In other words, there exists a function

\ell\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
which is slowly varying at infinity such that
G(x)=x^{-\alpha}\ell(x)
.
As in this case
\mu
is an unbounded distribution, the process
(X(t)\colon t\geq 0)
will grow
superexponentially fast on survival and therefore our discussion will focus on the
limiting quantity

\displaystyle\nu=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log X(t)}{t}.

(1)

Our main result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Given
\alpha>0
, let
T\in\mathbb{N}
be the unique number such that

\frac{(T-1)^{T}}{T^{T-1}}<\alpha\leq\frac{T^{T+1}}{(T+1)^{T}}

and define

\displaystyle\nu(\alpha):=\frac{1}{T}\log\frac{T}{\alpha}.

(2)

Let
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the size of the population in either the FMM or the MMM.
Then, almost surely on survival,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\log X(t)}{t}=\nu(\alpha).

Before presenting the proof of the theorem in Sec.
4
, in the next section we motivate the expression (
2
).

3
Motivation of the main result

Here we explain the statement of Theorem
1

by a heuristic analysis of the FMM.
For convenience we take the fitness distribution to be of Pareto form,
G(x)=x^{-\alpha}
for
x\geq 1
and
G(x)=1
for
x<1
. Moreover, throughout this section we assume
that the initial fitness
f
is so large that the fluctuations induced by Poisson sampling are negligible at all times, which implies that both the total population size and the
sizes of subpopulations of mutants are well approximated by their expectations.
Denoting the fitness of the mutant that is added to the population at generation
t

by
W_{t}
, we can then write

\displaystyle X(t)\approx(1-\beta)^{t}f^{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta)^{t-i}W_{i}^{t-i},

(3)

where the factors
1-\beta
account for the fact that (apart from the added mutant) only the unmutated fraction of the population survives to the next generation.
For the same reason the total number
N_{t}
of mutants produced in generation
t
(including the ones that die immediately) is approximately

N_{t}\approx\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}X(t).

Since the probability that the largest fitness
W_{t}
among
N_{t}
independent and identically distributed
random variables with common distribution
G
is smaller than
x
is
(1-x^{-\alpha})^{N_{t}}
,
the random variable
W_{t}
can be sampled as

W_{t}=\left(1-Z_{t}^{1/N_{t}}\right)^{-1/\alpha}\approx X(t)^{1/\alpha}Y_{t},\quad Y_{t}:=\left(\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\log\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\right)^{-1/\alpha},

where
Z_{t}
is uniformly distributed in the interval
(0,1)

and we have approximated
Z_{t}^{1/N_{t}}\approx 1+(1/N_{t})\log Z_{t}
.
Note that
Y_{t}
does not depend on
X(t)
.

To proceed,
we define
\omega_{t}
as

\displaystyle\omega_{t}:=\frac{\log X(t)}{\log f},

which implies that
X(t)=f^{\omega_{t}}
and
W_{t}\approx Y_{t}f^{\omega_{t}/\alpha}
.
Inserting these relations into (
3
) we obtain

\displaystyle f^{\omega_{t}}\approx(1-\beta)^{t}f^{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta)^{t-i}Y_{i}^{t-i}f^{(t-i)\omega_{i}/\alpha}.

(4)

In the limit
f\to\infty
the sum on the right hand side is dominated by the term with the largest exponent. Correspondingly, the
\omega_{t}
can be well approximated by the solution
\chi_{t}
of the recursion relation

\displaystyle\chi_{t}=\max\left\{t,\frac{t-1}{\alpha}\chi_{1},\frac{t-2}{\alpha}\chi_{2},\ldots,\frac{t-k}{\alpha}\chi_{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{\alpha}\chi_{t-1}\right\}

(5)

with
\chi_{1}=1
.
We now argue that the
\chi_{t}
grow at least exponentially.
Since for any
t_{0}\geq 1
and any positive integer
m

\chi_{t_{0}+m}\geq\frac{m}{\alpha}\chi_{t_{0}},

we have, for any
n\geq 1

\chi_{t_{0}+nm}\geq\left(\frac{m}{\alpha}\right)^{n}\chi_{t_{0}}.

Correspondingly

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\chi_{t}}{t}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\chi_{t_{0}+nm}}{nm}\geq\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{m}{\alpha},

(6)

where we have assumed that the limit is well-defined.
Since (
6
) is valid for any integer
m\geq 1
, an optimal
lower-bound can be found by maximizing the right hand side. As shown by Lemma
4
in Sec.
4
, the maximizer over the positive integers is precisely the function
\nu(\alpha)
in Theorem
1
. As the population
size depends exponentially on
\omega_{t}
or
\chi_{t}
, the heuristic argument makes it plausible that
\nu(\alpha)
is a lower bound
on the double-exponential growth rate of
X(t)
. Remarkably, Theorem
1

states that the bound is tight, and moreover applies also to the MMM. Informally this implies that the population at time
t
is dominated by the fittest mutant that was generated at time
t-T
. As a consequence the mutant frequency distribution changes periodically with period
T
(see Sec.
5
for further discussion).

Proof of Theorem
1

4.1
Preparation for the proof

In this subsection we collect some tools that will be used in the proofs of the lower and upper bounds
in the estimate leading to Theorem
1
. The lower bound will be verified in Section
4.2

and the upper bound in Section
4.3
.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
let
W_{t}
be the fitness of the fittest of the mutants in generation
t

and
W_{t}=0
if there are no mutants in generation
t
. Our first observation is that
under the weak assumption
G(x)>0
for all large
x
(which always holds if
\mu

is of Fréchet type) either the sequence
(W_{t})
is unbounded or the branching
process dies out in finite time. Heuristically speaking, on survival the accumulated number of mutants is unbounded almost surely, which naturally entails unbounded largest fitness.

Lemma 2
.

Almost surely on survival the sequence
(W_{t})
is unbounded.

Proof.

We first show that the branching process can be coupled to a sequence
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{t})

of independent Bernoulli variables with success parameter
\beta
and an independent
sequence
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{t})
of independent fitnesses with distribution
\mu

such that
on survival up
to generation
t
we have, for all
1\leq i\leq t
,

•

\xi_{i}=1
if there is at least one mutant in generation
i
, and

•

W_{i}\geq F_{i}\xi_{i}
.

Indeed, once the random variables
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{t})
and
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{t})
are generated with the given law we generate the branching process as follows: Produce the offspring in the
n
th generation
as a Poisson distribution with the right parameter given by the previous generation
(possibly zero). If there is at least one offspring use
\xi_{n}
to decide whether it is a mutant and if so give it fitness
F_{n}
. Then use other newly sampled Bernoulli variables with parameter
\beta
and fitnesses to decide whether other variables are mutants and if they are decide their fitness. Then surival implies

W_{n}\geq\xi_{n}F_{n}
as required.

Now
N:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\xi_{i}
is binomially distributed with parameters
\beta>0

and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. We infer that, for any fixed
x>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\leq x~\text{ for all }i\leq t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(F_{i}\xi_{i}\leq x~\text{ for all }i\leq t)+\mathbb{P}(\text{extinction})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{t}{t\choose i}\beta^{i}(1-\beta)^{t-i}\mathbb{P}(F\leq x)^{i}+\mathbb{P}(\text{extinction})

\displaystyle=(\beta\mathbb{P}(F\leq x)+(1-\beta))^{t}+\mathbb{P}(\text{extinction}).

Since
\mathbb{P}(F\leq x)<1
and
\beta>0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\leq

\displaystyle\,x~\text{ for all }i)=\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\leq x~\text{ for all }i\leq t)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}(\beta\mathbb{P}(F\leq x)+(1-\beta))^{t}+\mathbb{P}(\text{extinction})=\mathbb{P}(\text{extinction}),

hence
\mathbb{P}((W_{t})
is unbounded
)=\mathbb{P}(\text{survival})
as claimed.
∎

We next describe the distribution of
W_{t}
given the process at time
t-1
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that at generation
t-1
there are
n
individuals with
fitness
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
, …,
F_{n}
and set
\mathcal{X}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}
. Then,
for all
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{t}>x)=1-e^{-\beta\mathcal{X}G(x)}.

Proof.

First fix a positive integer
n
and suppose
W_{t}^{{}_{(n)}}
is the largest of
n
independently
sampled fitnesses and
W^{{}_{(0)}}_{t}=0
. Let
\bar{G}(x)=1-G(x)

and note that

\mathbb{P}(W^{{}_{(n)}}_{t}>x)=1-\mathbb{P}(W^{{}_{(n)}}_{t}\leq x)=1-\bar{G}(x)^{n}.

Now let
N
be the number of
mutants in generation
t
, which is Poisson distributed with
mean
\beta\mathcal{X}
. Hence, for
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{t}>x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{{}_{(n)}}>x)\mathbb{P}(N=n)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-\bar{G}(x)^{n})\mathbb{P}(N=n)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bar{G}(x)^{n}\frac{(\beta\mathcal{X})^{n}}{n!}e^{-\beta\mathcal{X}}=1-e^{-\beta\mathcal{X}(1-\bar{G}(x))}.

As
1-\bar{G}(x)=G(x)
the proof is complete
∎

The next two results concern the potential limit
\nu(\alpha)
. We first characterise
\nu(\alpha)

as a maximum and then as the growth rate in a recursion relation. Note that the first result easily implies that
\nu(\alpha)

is decreasing, as well as continuous and positive.

Lemma 4
.

We have

\nu(\alpha)=\max\{\tfrac{1}{m}\log(m/\alpha)\colon m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

In particular, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle m\leq\alpha e^{\nu(\alpha)m}\quad\text{ and }\quad T=\alpha e^{\nu(\alpha)T}.

(8)

Proof.

First observe that
\alpha>m^{m+1}/(m+1)^{m}
for
m<T
and

\alpha\leq m^{m+1}/(m+1)^{m}
for
m\geq T
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since

m(m+1)\left(\frac{1}{m}\log\frac{m}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{m+1}\log\frac{m+1}{\alpha}\right)=\log\frac{m^{m+1}}{(m+1)^{m}\alpha},

we have the desired result.
∎

Remark 5
.

Let
\alpha_{T}:=T^{T+1}/(T+1)^{T}
.
The equality
m=\alpha e^{\nu(\alpha)m}
holds iff (
m=T
) or (
m=T+1
and
\alpha=\alpha_{T}
).

For the remainder of this subsection, we abbreviate
\nu:=\nu(\alpha)
.

Lemma 6
.

For some positive sequence
(a_{n})
we define inductively

\chi_{t}:=\chi_{t}(\alpha,(a_{n})):=\max\big\{a_{t},\tfrac{t-1}{\alpha}\chi_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\chi_{t-1}\big\}.

(9)

Then, if
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}e^{-\nu n}=0
, there are positive constants
c
and
c^{\prime}
such that

c^{\prime}e^{\nu t}\leq\chi_{t}\leq ce^{\nu t}\quad\text{ for all $t\geq 1$,}

and therefore we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\chi_{t}}{t}=\nu.

Proof.

Abbreviate
\hat{\chi}_{t}:=\chi_{t}/c^{\prime}

with
c^{\prime}=e^{-\nu T}\min\{\chi_{1},\chi_{2},\ldots,\chi_{T}\}
.
Obviously,
\hat{\chi}_{t}\geq e^{\nu t}
for
t\leq T
.
Now assume
n\geq T
and
\hat{\chi}_{t}\geq e^{\nu t}
for all
t\leq n
.
By the assumption and (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{n+1}\geq\frac{T}{\alpha}\hat{\chi}_{n+1-T}\geq\frac{T}{\alpha}e^{\nu(n+1-T)}=e^{\nu(n+1)}.

Induction gives

\hat{\chi}_{t}\geq e^{\nu t}
and hence
\chi_{t}\geq c^{\prime}e^{\nu t}
for all
t\geq 1
.

Now, choose a positive integer
n_{0}
such that
a_{n}\leq e^{\nu n}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Let
\bar{\chi}_{t}=\chi_{t}/c
with

.
Obviously,

\bar{\chi}_{t}\leq 1\leq e^{\nu t}
for all

.
Now let

and assume that
\bar{\chi}_{t}\leq e^{\nu t}
for all

.
Then,

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\tfrac{a_{n+1}}{c},\tfrac{n}{\alpha}\bar{\chi}_{1},\tfrac{n-1}{\alpha}\bar{\chi}_{2},\ldots,\tfrac{n-k+1}{\alpha}\bar{\chi}_{k},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\bar{\chi}_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{e^{\nu(n+1)},\tfrac{n}{\alpha}e^{\nu},\tfrac{n-1}{\alpha}e^{2\nu},\ldots\tfrac{n-k+1}{\alpha}e^{\nu k},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\nu n}\right\}\leq e^{\nu(n+1)},

where we have used (

By induction, we have
\chi_{t}\leq ce^{\nu t}
for all

.

Lemma 7
.

Define

\displaystyle I_{t}:=\max\{i<t\colon\chi_{t}=\tilde{\chi}_{i}(t)\}.

(11)

Then

is bounded.

Proof.

By Lemma
6

for all

.
Since there is

such that
c^{\prime}e^{\nu t}>a_{t}
for all

,

,

\displaystyle\chi_{t}=\max\left\{\tfrac{t-1}{\alpha}\chi_{1},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\chi_{t-1}\right\}.

Now it is enough to show that

Note that, for

,

\displaystyle ce^{\nu t}A(m)\geq\tfrac{m}{\alpha}\chi_{t-m}\geq c^{\prime}e^{\nu t}A(m),

where

Since

\tfrac{m}{\alpha}\chi_{t-m}<c^{\prime}e^{\nu t},\text{ for all $m\geq m_{0}$.}

As the right hand side is a lower bound of

cannot be larger than

, as desired.

Remark 8
.

If we choose
T^{\prime}>\sup\{t-I_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{N}\}
, then, for all

\displaystyle\chi_{t}=\max\{a_{t},(t-1)\chi_{1}/\alpha,\ldots,\chi_{t-1}/\alpha\}=\max\{\tilde{\chi}_{t-T^{\prime}}(t),\ldots,\tilde{\chi}_{t-1}(t)\}.

In words,
\chi_{t}
is completely determined
by

for

.
This fact will play an important role in the proof of Theorem

We conclude the subsection with two estimates for classical Galton-Watson processes.

Lemma 9
.

Consider a supercritical Galton-Watson process
(\mathcal{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with Poisson offspring distribution with mean
\theta>1
,
starting in generation 0 with a single
individual. Fix
0<x<1
and an integer
n\geq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{X}_{t}\geq x^{t}\theta^{t}\text{ for all }1\leq t\leq n\right)\geq 1-n\frac{(1-x)^{-2}}{\theta-1}.

(12)

Proof.

First note that
(see, e.g.,
H63
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\theta^{t},\quad\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{X}_{t})=\frac{\theta^{2t}-\theta^{t}}{\theta-1},

and that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{X}_{t}\geq x^{t}\theta^{t}\text{ for all }1\leq t\leq n\right)\geq 1-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{X}_{t}\leq x^{t}\theta^{t}\right),

(13)

where we have used the sub-additivity of probability measure.
Using Chebyshev’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{X}_{t}\leq x^{t}\theta^{t}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{t}-\mathcal{X}_{t}\geq\theta^{t}-x^{t}\theta^{t}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{X}_{t}-\theta^{t}|\geq\theta^{t}-x^{t}\theta^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-x^{t}\right)^{-2}\frac{\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{X}_{t})}{\theta^{2t}}=\left(1-x^{t}\right)^{-2}\frac{(1-\theta^{-t})}{(\theta-1)}\leq\frac{(1-x)^{-2}}{\theta-1},

which, along with (
13
), gives the claimed inequality.
∎

Lemma 10
.

For a Galton-Watson process
(\mathcal{X}_{t})
with
\mathcal{X}_{0}=K_{0}
and generation dependent
offspring distribution
N_{t}
with
\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]\leq N
for all
t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{X}_{t}\leq KN^{t}B^{t}\text{ for all }t\geq 1\big)\geq 1-\frac{K_{0}}{K(B-1)},

for all
B>1
and
K>0
.

Proof.

By Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}_{t}\geq KN^{t}B^{t})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{t}]}{KN^{t}}B^{-t}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{t+1}|\mathcal{X}_{t}]=\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]
,
we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}_{t}]=K_{0}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathbb{E}[N_{i}]\leq K_{0}N^{t}
,
which gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}_{t}\geq KN^{t}B^{t})\leq\frac{K_{0}}{KB^{t}}.

Since

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}_{t}\leq KN^{t}B^{t}\text{ for all }t\geq 1)\geq 1-\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}_{t}\geq KN^{t}B^{t}),

a geometric sum gives the claimed inequality.
∎

at generation

4.3
Proof of the upper bound

In this subsection we show that, for given
\alpha>0
and all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log M_{t}}{t}\leq\nu(\alpha^{\prime})\Big)=1.

We now construct an MMM with special initial conditions.

,
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha/(1+3\varepsilon)

\nu^{\prime}=\nu(\alpha^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle\hat{n}:=\frac{T}{\Delta}

We define, for a given

\displaystyle\chi_{t}^{\prime}:=e^{\nu^{\prime}t},\quad\kappa_{n,t}:=e^{\nu^{\prime}(t-T+n\Delta)},\quad g_{n,t}:=f^{\kappa_{n,t}/\alpha},\quad h_{n,t}:=f^{(1+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n,t}/\alpha}.

We consider the MMM

with an initial condition
such that
there are
T
different mutant classes with fitness

for

.
We only consider

Now assume that we have proved, for all

\displaystyle\lim_{f\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log\tilde{M}_{t}(f)}{t}\leq\nu(\alpha^{\prime})\Big)=1.

12
with
B=f^{\kappa_{0,m}/\alpha^{\prime}}/h_{0,m}=f^{2\varepsilon\kappa_{0,m}/\alpha}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{A}_{0,m}|A_{0,m,m}\cap D_{m-1})\geq 1-\left(f^{2\varepsilon\kappa_{0,m}/\alpha}-1\right)^{-1}.

Therefore,
defining

b_{0,m}:=\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^{2}\theta_{m}}f^{-\chi^{\prime}_{m}}+\left(f^{2\varepsilon\kappa_{0,m}/\alpha}-1\right)^{-1},

we have

\mathbb{P}(A_{0,m}|D_{m-1})\geq 1-b_{0,m}
.

Finally, we move on to

\mathbb{P}(A_{n,m}|D_{m-1})
for
1\leq n\leq\hat{n}
.
Using (
19
) with
K=f^{\chi_{m}^{\prime}-\kappa_{n-1,m}}
,

a=h_{n-1,m}
,
and
Y_{m}G(a)\leq\theta_{m}f^{\chi_{m}^{\prime}-(1+\varepsilon/2)\kappa_{n-1,m}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{n,m,m}|D_{m-1})\geq 1-\theta_{m}f^{-\varepsilon\kappa_{n-1,m}/2}.

Using Lemma
12
with
B=f^{\delta\kappa_{n,m}/\alpha^{\prime}}/h_{n,m}=f^{\varepsilon\kappa_{n,m}/\alpha}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{A}_{n,m}|A_{n,m,m}\cap D_{m-1})\geq 1-\left(f^{\varepsilon\kappa_{n,m}/\alpha}-1\right)^{-1}.

Therefore,
defining

b_{n,m}:=\theta_{m}f^{-\varepsilon\kappa_{n-1,m}/2}+\left(f^{\varepsilon\kappa_{n,m}/\alpha}-1\right)^{-1},

we have

\mathbb{P}(A_{n,m}|D_{m-1})\geq 1-b_{n,m}.

We define

b_{m}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\hat{n}+1}b_{n,m}\text{ for }m\geq T,\quad\text{ and }\phi(f):=\sum_{m=T-1}^{\infty}b_{m}.

Recall that we have assumed
(1-\beta)f>2
and
(1-\beta)f^{\varepsilon/\alpha}>2
.
Since
b_{m}
for given
m
is a bounded function
of
f
which is decreasing to zero and

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}b_{m}2^{m}=0,

there is
m_{0}
such that
|b_{m}|<2^{-m}

for all
m>m_{0}
. Hence the series defining
\phi(f)
converges
uniformly for sufficiently large
f

and, accordingly,
\lim_{f\rightarrow\infty}\phi(f)=0
.
Therefore, for sufficiently large
f
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A(f))\geq\prod_{m=T-1}^{\infty}(1-b_{m})\geq 1-\phi(f),

and
\displaystyle\lim_{f\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(A(f))=1
,
which completes the proof.
∎

5
Empirical frequency distributions

Apart from the fact that the population is dominated by a single mutant class at all times, the proof of the double-exponential growth rate
\nu
presented in Sec.
4
does not give any insight into the structure of the population. However, since the solution
\chi_{t}
of the recursion relation (
9
)
correctly describes the asymptotic growth of
X(t)
, it provides a natural starting point for addressing this question at least on a heuristic level.
In this section, we analyze the recursion relation in more depth to understand the
demographic structure in the long time limit, which turns out to display a rather
rich behaviour.

5.1
Numerical solution of the recursion relation

To characterize the empirical frequency distribution we introduce the following quantities:

\displaystyle J_{i}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\log W_{i}}{\log W_{t}}\approx\frac{\chi_{i}}{\chi_{t}},\;\;P(t):=\frac{\log X(t+1)-\log X(t)}{\log W_{t}}\approx\alpha\frac{\chi_{t+1}-\chi_{t}}{\chi_{t}},

\displaystyle R_{i}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\log W_{i}^{t-i}-\log X(t)}{\log X(t)}=\frac{t-i}{\alpha}J_{i}(t)-1,

where the second approximate relations in the definitions of
J_{i}(t)
and
P(t)
become equalities in the formal deterministic limit
f\to\infty
(see Sec.
3
).
The ratio
J_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
compares the log-fitness of the mutant class born at time
i
to the log-fitness of the current fittest mutant.
Since
X(t+1)\approx(1-\beta)X(t)\bar{F}_{t}
with
\bar{F}_{t}
denoting the mean fitness
of the population at generation
t
,

P(t)
quantifies the mean fitness at generation
t
on the same scale. The decomposition in Eq. (
3
) shows that the fraction of the population in mutant class
i
at time
t
is proportional to
W_{i}^{t-i}
, and therefore
R_{i}(t)\in[-1,0]
serves as proxy of the (logarithmic) frequency distribution at generation
t
over mutant classes
i
.

(a) Plots of
R_{i}(t)
vs.
J_{i}(t)

for
\alpha=1
at generations
t=16
,
17
,
\ldots
,
24
.
The vertical line at each panel indicates the location of the mean fitness
P(t)
.
(b) Data collapse plots of
R_{i}(t)
vs.
J_{i}(t)
for each column of (a).

In Fig.
2
(a), we plot
R_{i}(t)
against
J_{i}(t)
for nine consecutive
generations, obtained by numerically solving the recursion relation (
5
) for
\alpha=1
. The salient feature is the periodic behavior
of the frequency distribution
with period
3
; note that
T=3
for
\alpha=1
.
To illustrate the accuracy of the periodic behaviour, we present
data-collapse plots in Fig.
2
(b). In most regions of
J_{i}
,
the collapse looks perfect (since the number of mutant classes increases with the number of generations, the frequency distributions at different times cannot be identical).
A rigorous proof of the periodicity will be given in Sec.
5.2
.

The periodicity was taken into account in the numerical estimates of
\nu
reported in Fig.
1
. Rather than monitoring
\log\chi_{t}/t
, which converges very slowly,
we computed the quantity

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}(t):=\frac{1}{T}\log\frac{\chi_{t+T}}{\chi_{t}},

which approaches a constant in a relatively short time.

5.2
Periodicity of
\chi_{t}e^{-\nu t}

By Lemma
6
, we know that

\displaystyle c_{t}:=\chi_{t}e^{-\nu t}

is bounded away from zero and infinity. Now we show that
c_{t}
is not only bounded, but eventually becomes periodic.

Proposition 17
.

For any sequence
(a_{n})
in the recursion relation (
9
), there is a
t_{1}
such that

c_{t}=c_{t+T}
for all
t\geq t_{1}
.

Proof.

In this proof,
k
and
k^{\prime}
are exclusively used as integers in the range
1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq T
.
Since
\chi_{t+T}\geq e^{\nu T}\chi_{t}
(see Sec.
3
), the sequence
(c_{k+nT})_{n}
is nondecreasing and bounded. Consequently,

\displaystyle C_{k}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}c_{k+nT}

(28)

is well defined. Note that

\max\{C_{k}:1\leq k\leq T\}
becomes the optimal upper bound in Lemma
6
.
If
n
satisfies
nT>T^{\prime}
with
T^{\prime}>\max\{t-I_{t}\}
(see Remark
8
), then we have

\displaystyle c_{k+nT}

\displaystyle=e^{-\nu(k+nT)}\max\left\{\frac{k+nT-t^{\prime}}{\alpha}\chi_{t^{\prime}}:k+nT-T^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}<k+nT\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{s}{\alpha}e^{-\nu s}c_{k+nT-s}:1\leq s\leq T^{\prime}\right\}.

(29)

Taking
n
to infinity, we get

\displaystyle C_{k}=\max\left\{\frac{s}{\alpha}e^{-\nu s}C_{k-s}:1\leq s\leq T^{\prime}\right\},

(30)

and, by definition,
C_{k+mT}=C_{k}
for any integer
m
.
Since
Te^{-\nu T}/\alpha=1
and
C_{k-T}=C_{k}
, we can rewrite (
30
) as

\displaystyle C_{k}=\max\left\{\frac{s}{\alpha}e^{-\nu s}C_{k-s}:1\leq s\leq T+1\right\}.

Comparing terms with
s=T-1,T,T+1
for any
k
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T-1}{T}e^{\nu}C_{k+1}=\frac{T-1}{\alpha}e^{-\nu(T-1)}C_{k+1}\leq C_{k},

\displaystyle\frac{T+1}{T}e^{-\nu}C_{k-1}=\frac{T+1}{\alpha}e^{-\nu(T+1)}C_{k-1}\leq C_{k},

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{T+1}{T}\leq\frac{C_{k+1}e^{\nu}}{C_{k}}\leq\frac{T}{T-1},

for all
k
. Let
\varphi_{k}=C_{k}e^{\nu}/C_{k-1}
with
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{T+1}=C_{1}e^{\nu}/C_{T}
. Then,

C_{k}=C_{1}e^{-\nu(k-1)}\prod_{j=2}^{k}\varphi_{j},

for
k>1
. Setting
k=T+1
and considering
C_{T+1}=C_{1}
, we have

\prod_{j=1}^{T}\varphi_{j}=e^{\nu T}=\frac{T}{\alpha}.

To sum up,
C_{k}
takes the form

\displaystyle C_{k}=C_{0}e^{-\nu k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\varphi_{j},

where
C_{0}
is a positive constant (note that
\chi_{t}(\alpha,(C_{0}a_{n}))=C_{0}\chi_{t}(\alpha,(a_{n}))
) and
\varphi_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{T+1}{T}\leq\varphi_{k}\leq\frac{T}{T-1},\quad\prod_{j=1}^{T}\varphi_{j}=\frac{T}{\alpha}=e^{\nu T}.

(31)

If
\alpha=\alpha_{T}
(see Remark
5
), then
e^{\nu}=(T+1)/T
and the only possible value of

\varphi_{k}
is
\varphi_{k}=e^{\nu}

for all
k
because of (
31
).

To simplify (
29
) for large
n
, we use the following observation.
For
p\in\mathbb{N}
with
X:=1/T
and

C_{k-(T\pm p)}=C_{k\mp p}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T-p}{\alpha}e^{-\nu(T-p)}C_{k+p}

\displaystyle=\frac{T-p}{T}e^{\nu p}\frac{C_{k+p}}{C_{k}}C_{k}=C_{k}\frac{T-p}{T}\prod_{j=1}^{p}e^{\nu}\frac{C_{k+j}}{C_{k+j-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\frac{T-p}{T}\left(\frac{T}{T-1}\right)^{p}=C_{k}\frac{1-pX}{(1-X)^{p}},

and

\displaystyle\frac{T+p}{\alpha}e^{-\nu(T+p)}C_{k-p}

\displaystyle=\frac{T+p}{T}e^{-\nu p}\frac{C_{k-p}}{C_{k}}C_{k}=C_{k}\frac{T+p}{T}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{C_{k-j}}{e^{\nu}C_{k-j+1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\frac{T+p}{T}\left(\frac{T}{T+1}\right)^{p}=C_{k}\frac{1+pX}{(1+X)^{p}}.

Since
\sup_{p\geq 2}(1+pX)/(1+X)^{p}<1
for all nonzero
X
not smaller than
-1
,
relating
s
and
p
by
p=|s-T|

we can choose
\varepsilon>0
such that

C_{k}-\varepsilon>se^{-\nu s}C_{k-s}/\alpha
for all
s
with
|s-T|>1
and for all
k
.
By (
28
), for this
\varepsilon
, there is an integer
m_{0}
such that

C_{k}-\varepsilon<c_{k+nT}\leq C_{k}
for all
n\geq m_{0}
and for all
k
.
If
n>m_{0}
, then

c_{k+nT}>C_{k}-\varepsilon>se^{-\nu s}C_{k-s}/\alpha\geq se^{-\nu s}c_{k-s+nT}/\alpha,

for all
s
with
|s-T|>1
, which reduces (
29
) to

\displaystyle c_{k+(n+1)T}=\max\left\{c_{k+nT},\tfrac{T+1}{T}e^{-\nu}c_{k-1+nT},\tfrac{T-1}{T}e^{\nu}c_{k+1+nT}\right\}.

(32)

In the following,
n
is assumed so large that (
32
) is valid for all
k
.
Defining
\delta_{k,n}:=
1-c_{k+nT}/C_{k}
with the convention
\delta_{k+mT,n}:=\delta_{k,n+m}
for integer
m

and using the definition of
\varphi_{k}
, we can write

\displaystyle\delta_{k,n+1}=\min\left\{\delta_{k,n},1-\tfrac{T+1}{T\varphi_{k}}(1-\delta_{k-1,n}),1-\tfrac{T-1}{T}\varphi_{k+1}(1-\delta_{k+1,n})\right\}.

As
c_{k+nT}\rightarrow C_{k}
, we have
\delta_{k,n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Accordingly,
if
(T+1)/(T\varphi_{k})<1
, then the term with
(T+1)/(T\varphi_{k})
cannot be a minimum for large
n
.
The same argument is applicable to the term with
(T-1)\varphi_{k+1}/T
.

If
\alpha=\alpha_{T}
, then
\varphi_{k}=(T+1)/T
for all
k
and, accordingly, we have

\delta_{k,n+1}=\min\{\delta_{k,n},\delta_{k-1,n}\}
for all
k
and for all sufficiently large
n
.
If there is
m
and
k^{\prime}
such that
\delta_{k^{\prime},m}=0
, then
\delta_{k^{\prime},n}=0
for all
n\geq m
and

\delta_{k^{\prime}+1,m+1}=0
, which again gives
\delta_{k^{\prime}+2,m+2}=0
and so on. Therefore, we have
\delta_{k,n}=0
for all
n>m+T
and
all
k
. Hence, to complete the proof for this case, we need to elicit a contradiction if

\delta_{k,n}
is strictly positive for all
n
and for all
k
.
Since
\delta_{k,n}
is a nonincreasing sequence of
n
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{k,n+s}

\displaystyle=\min\{\delta_{k,n+s-1},\delta_{k-1,n+s-1}\}=\min\{\delta_{k,n+s-2},\delta_{k-1,n+s-2},\delta_{k-1,n+s-1}\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\delta_{k,n+s-2},\delta_{k-1,n+s-1}\}=\min\{\delta_{k,n},\delta_{k-1,n+s-1}\},

for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\delta_{k-1,n+s-1}
should approach zero monotonically as
s\rightarrow\infty
,
there should be
s_{0}
such that
\delta_{k-1,n+s-1}<\delta_{k,n}
for all
k
and for all
s>s_{0}
.
Therefore, we get
\delta_{k,n+s+T}=\delta_{k-1,n+s+T-1}=\delta_{k-2,n+s+T-2}=\delta_{k,n+s}
for all
s>s_{0}
.
Since
\delta
cannot increase, we conclude that
\delta_{k,n}
is a constant
for all sufficiently large
n
. If
\delta_{k,n}
is strictly positive for all
n
as assumed,
C_{k}
cannot be a limit and
we arrive at a contradiction.
Therefore, there is
t_{1}
such that
c_{t+T}=c_{t}
for all
t\geq t_{1}
in this case.

If
\alpha\neq\alpha_{T}
, then there is at least one
\varphi_{k}
such that

(T+1)/T<\varphi_{k}
. If
\varepsilon
also satisfies
\varepsilon/C_{k}<1-(T+1)/(T\varphi_{k})
, then
we can write

\displaystyle\delta_{k,n+1}=\min\left\{\delta_{k,n},1-\tfrac{T-1}{T}\varphi_{k+1}(1-\delta_{k+1,n})\right\},

for all
n>m_{0}
.
If
\varphi_{k+1}<T/(T-1)
, then
\delta_{k,n}
will eventually be smaller than
1-(T-1)\varphi_{k+1}/T
and
we have
\delta_{k,n+1}=\delta_{k,n}=0
for all large
n
.
On the other hand, if
\varphi_{k+1}=T/(T-1)>(T+1)/T
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{k,n+1}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\delta_{k,n},\delta_{k+1,n}\right\},

\displaystyle\delta_{k+1,n+1}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\delta_{k+1,n},1-\tfrac{T-1}{T}\varphi_{k+2}(1-\delta_{k+2,n})\right\}.

(33)

Since it is impossible for all
\varphi_{k}
to be
T/(T-1)
, there exists
k^{\prime}
such that

\varphi_{k+i}=T/(T-1)
for
1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}
and

\varphi_{k+k^{\prime}+1}<T/(T-1)
. Therefore,

\delta_{k+k^{\prime},n+1}=\delta_{k+k^{\prime},n},

for all sufficiently large
n
. Once
\delta_{k+k^{\prime},m}=0
, then
\delta_{k+i,n}=0
for all
0\leq i\leq k^{\prime}
and for all
n>m+T
by (
33
). If
\varphi_{k+k^{\prime}+1}>(T+1)/T
, we can repeat the above procedure.
If
\varphi_{k+k^{\prime}+1}=(T+1)/T
, we have

\delta_{k+k^{\prime}+1,n+1}=\min\left\{\delta_{k+k^{\prime}+1,n},\delta_{k+k^{\prime},n},1-\tfrac{T-1}{T}\varphi_{k+k^{\prime}+2}(1-\delta_{k+k^{\prime}+2,n})\right\}=0

for all sufficiently large
n
.
Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

5.3
Non-uniqueness of periodic solutions

Proposition
17
and its proof have shown the general periodic solutions for
t>t_{1}
to be of the form

\displaystyle\chi_{t}=\chi_{t_{1}}\prod_{k=t_{1}+1}^{t}\varphi_{k},\quad c_{t}=c_{t_{1}}e^{-\nu(t-t_{1})}\prod_{k=t_{1}+1}^{t}\varphi_{k},

where the
\varphi_{k}
satisfy
\varphi_{T+k}=\varphi_{k}
and (
31
).
Since
(T+1)/T\leq e^{\nu}<T/(T-1)
, setting
\varphi_{i}=e^{\nu}
for all
i
satisfies (
31
),
which gives the constant sequence
c_{t}=c_{t_{1}}
. We refer to this solution as the homogeneous state.
Recall that the homogeneous state is the unique possibility for
\alpha=\alpha_{T}
, as shown right after (
31
).
By constructing an appropriate sequence
(a_{n})
, we now show that any set
\{\varphi_{k}\}
that satisfies the conditions (
31
) can give rise to a periodic solution
c_{t}
.
Therefore, the periodic solution
c_{t}
is not unique and can vary substantially with
(a_{n})
unless
\alpha=\alpha_{T}
or
T=1
.

Proposition 18
.

Let

\displaystyle a_{t}=\max\left\{\frac{T-i+t-1}{\alpha}\psi_{i}\colon 0\leq i<T\right\},\quad\psi_{i}:=\prod_{j=1}^{i}\varphi_{j},

(34)

with the convention
\prod_{j=1}^{0}=1
.
Then

\displaystyle\chi_{t}=\psi_{t+T-1}:=\prod_{j=1}^{t+T-1}\varphi_{j},

(35)

where the
\varphi_{j}
are as in (
31
) with periodicity
\varphi_{T+j}=\varphi_{j}
.

Proof.

To find
a_{1}
, we observe that
for
0\leq i<T-1

\displaystyle\frac{T-i-1}{\alpha}\psi_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{T-i}{\alpha}\psi_{i}\frac{T-i-1}{T-i}\varphi_{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T-i}{\alpha}\psi_{i}\frac{(T-i-1)T}{(T-i)(T-1)}=\frac{T-i}{\alpha}\psi_{i}\frac{T^{2}-(i+1)T}{T^{2}-(i+1)+i}\leq\frac{T-i}{\alpha}\psi_{i},

where we have used
\varphi_{i}\leq T/(T-1)
.
Therefore, we get

\chi_{1}=a_{1}=\frac{T}{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{T}\varphi_{j}=\psi_{T},

which is (
35
) for
t=1
. Note that this
\chi_{1}
is trivially valid for
T=1
.

Now assume (
35
) is valid up to
t=n
.
Then,

\chi_{n+1}=\max\left\{\frac{T-i+n}{\alpha}\psi_{i}:0\leq i\leq n+T-1\right\}.

For
i\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T-i+n}{\alpha}\psi_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{T-i+n+1}{\alpha}\psi_{i-1}\frac{T-i+n}{T-i+n+1}\varphi_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T-i+n+1}{\alpha}\psi_{i-1}\frac{(T-i+n)(T+1)}{(T-i+n+1)T}

\displaystyle=\frac{T-i+n+1}{\alpha}\psi_{i-1}\frac{T^{2}+(n+1-i)T+n-i}{T^{2}+(n+1-i)T}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T-i+n+1}{\alpha}\psi_{i-1},

and
for
n+T-1>i\geq n
and
T>1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T-i+n}{\alpha}\psi_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{T-i+n-1}{\alpha}\psi_{i+1}\frac{T-i+n}{T-i+n-1}\frac{1}{\varphi_{i+1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T-i+n-1}{\alpha}\psi_{i+1}\frac{(T-i+n)(T-1)}{(T-i+n-1)T}

\displaystyle=\frac{T-i+n-1}{\alpha}\psi_{i+1}\frac{T^{2}+(n-1-i)T+i-n}{T^{2}+(n-1-i)T}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T-i+n-1}{\alpha}\psi_{i+1}.

Therefore, we have

\chi_{n+1}=\frac{T}{\alpha}\psi_{n}=\psi_{n}\prod_{j=n+1}^{T+n}\varphi_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{T+n}\varphi_{j}.

Induction completes the proof.
∎

For a realization of (
34
) in the branching process, consider an initial condition such that there are
T
different mutant classes with fitness
f_{i}:=f^{\psi_{i}/\alpha}
(
0\leq i<T
)
and the number
N_{i}
of individuals with fitness
f_{i}
is

N_{i}=\left\lfloor f^{(T-i-1)\psi_{i}/\alpha}\right\rfloor.

Notice that this initial condition with
\varphi_{j}=e^{\nu^{\prime}}
together with a shift in time
was used in the proof of Lemma
16
.
In the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
as in Sec.
3
,
X(t)
is well approximated by
f^{\chi_{t}}
with
a_{t}
in (
34
).

In the original branching process, the sequence
(a_{n})
depends both on the initial condition and the stochastic evolution in the early time regime before the deterministic approximation through the recursion relation (
9
) becomes valid.
To see this, we recall from Sec.
3
how the recursion relation arises from the stochastic process.
Since on survival the total population size as well as the largest fitness increases indefinitely,
there should be a generation
t_{0}
such that
X(t_{0}+1)>K
for any preassigned
K
.
Let
W_{0}
be the largest fitness at generation
t_{0}
,
define
Y=X(t_{0}+1)
and introduce a shifted time variable
t^{\prime}=t-t_{0}
.
If
K
is extremely large,
X(t^{\prime})
can be well approximated as

\displaystyle X(1)=Y\approx Y^{\chi_{1}},\quad W_{1}\approx Y^{\chi_{1}/\alpha},

\displaystyle X(2)=Y^{a_{2}}+Y^{\chi_{1}/\alpha}+1\approx Y^{\chi_{2}},\quad W_{2}\approx Y^{\chi_{2}/\alpha},

\displaystyle X(3)=Y^{a_{3}}+Y^{2\chi_{1}/\alpha}+Y^{\chi_{2}/\alpha}+1\approx Y^{\chi_{3}},\quad W_{3}\approx Y^{\chi_{3}/\alpha}

where
Y^{a_{n}}
is the population size of all mutant classes that appeared prior to generation
t_{0}
. Since
Y^{a_{n}}\leq YW_{0}^{n}
, we naturally have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}e^{-\nu n}=0
, and
(a_{n})
is a permissible sequence that can be entered
into the recursion relation (
9
).

5.4
Empirical frequency distribution for large
\alpha

Whereas the preceding subsection has shown that the empirical frequency distribution
at long times is generally non-universal, we will now argue that it nevertheless has a well-defined limit for
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.
Let us begin with the homogeneous state. In this case,

\displaystyle J_{i}(t)=e^{-\nu(t-i)},\quad P(t)\equiv\alpha(e^{\nu}-1),

\displaystyle R_{i}(t)=\frac{(t-i)}{\alpha}J_{i}(t)-1=-\frac{1}{\nu\alpha}J_{i}(t)\log J_{i}(t)-1.

Since
\nu\alpha\rightarrow 1/e
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
,
the homogeneous state for all sufficiently large
\alpha
is well described by

6
Summary and Discussion

In this article we have provided a detailed characterization of the superexponential population growth in two closely related stochastic models of evolution. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first rigorous analysis of a branching process with selection and random mutations drawn from an unbounded fitness distribution. A remarkable feature of the models considered here is the emergence of an integer-valued time scale
T
which depends (discontinuously) on the index
\alpha
of the underlying Fréchet distribution. A partial understanding of the resulting periodic behaviour of the population structure was achieved in a deterministic approximation. Further work on this problem is needed, addressing in particular how the stochastic initial phase of the process determines the non-universal aspects of the asymptotic population distribution.

It is instructive to compare our findings for the branching process to the earlier analysis of a stochastic fixed finite population version of Kingman’s model in
Park2008
. In both cases the long-time behavior is dominated by, and can quantitatively understood in terms of extremal mutation events in the past. However, in the fixed finite population model the likelihood of generating mutants that exceed the current population fitness declines with time, and the dynamics reduces to a modified record process, where the takeover of the population by a fit mutant is instantaneous compared to the waiting time for the next fitter mutant. As a consequence, the population at time
t
is dominated by a mutant that arose at a time of order
t
in the past. By contrast, in the branching process with Fréchet-type distributions, the declining probability of exceeding the current fitness
is compensated by the rapid growth of the population in such a way that the time lag since the birth of the currently dominant mutant takes on a fixed value
T
.

It is reasonable to expect that the growth of the population fitness in the branching process is intermediate between that of the fixed finite population model and the deterministic infinite population model. For Fréchet type fitness distributions the deterministic model is ill-defined, but the analysis of the fixed finite population model predicts a polynomial increase of
the fitness with exponent
1/\alpha

Park2008
, which is indeed much slower than the superexponential growth in the branching process. For unbounded Gumbel type distributions the growth law of the fitness is known for infinite as well as for finite populations
Kingman1978
;
Park2008
. The corresponding behaviour of the branching process will be addressed in future work.
