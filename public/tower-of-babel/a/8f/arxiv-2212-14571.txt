Hypergraph Counting and Mixed p p -Spin Glass Models under Replica Symmetry
Partha S. Dey ⋆, Qiang Wu †

Abstract
. Fluctuation problems in mean-field spin glass theory enjoy a rich history. In this paper, we address this question in the high-temperature regime by studying the fluctuation of the partition function in the general mixed p p -spin glass models under the weak external field assumption: h = ρ ​ N − α , ρ > 0 , α ∈ [ 1 / 4 , ∞ ] h=\rho N^{-\alpha},\rho>0,\alpha\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\infty] . By extending the cluster expansion approach to this generic setting, we convert the fluctuation problem as a hypergraph counting problem and thus obtain a new multiple-transition phenomenon in terms of the fluctuation of the partition function. A byproduct of our results is a new critical inverse temperature obtained from optimal second-moment control of the partition function. All our fluctuation results hold up to the critical inverse temperature. Combining with multivariate Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs, we also obtain an explicit convergence rate under appropriate moment assumptions on the general symmetric disorder coupling. Our results have several further implications. – First, our approach works for both even and odd pure p p -spin models. The leading cluster structures in the odd p p case are different and more involved than in the even p p case. This gives a combinatorial explanation for the folklore that odd p p -spin is usually more complicated than even p p . – Second, in the mixed p p -spin setting, the cluster structures differ depending on the relation between the minimum effective even and odd p p -spins: p e p_{e} and p o p_{o} , respectively. As an example, at h = 0 h=0 , there are three sub-regimes: p e < p o , p o < p e < 2 ​ p o , p e ⩾ 2 ​ p o p_{e}<p_{o},p_{o}<p_{e}<2p_{o},p_{e}\geqslant 2p_{o} , wherein the first and third ones, the mixed p p -spin model behaves essentially like a pure p p -spin model, and only in the second regime, it is more like a mixture. This presents another criterion for classifying mean-field spin glass models compared to the work of Auffinger and Ben Arous (Ann. Probab. 41 (2013), no. 6, 4214–4247), where the idea is based on complexity computations for spherical models. – Third, our framework naturally implies a multi-scale fluctuation phenomenon conjectured in the work of Bovier and Schertzer (Probab. Theory Relat. Fields (2024)), at h = 0 h=0 case for pure p p -spin model. Our results suggest that this phenomenon holds for general mixed p p -spin models under weak external fields, including h = 0 h=0 . We also extend the approach in this paper to general multi-species mixed p p -spin models.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

Cluster expansion, a powerful tool rooted in mathematical physics, has been used to rigorously study many problems in a wide range of statistical mechanics models. For example, cluster expansion was used in developing the Pigorov-Sinai theory to prove phase transitions for the Ising model. The rough idea is to decompose the configuration space into clusters of simple structures and then deal with the simple clusters instead to understand the log-partition function. We invite interested readers to
[
32
]
for more details on this classical treatment.

However, there are scarce results on cluster expansion to derive rigorous solutions in the disordered spin system, such as spin glass models. In the seminal paper
[
1
]
, Aizenman, Lebowitz, and Ruelle first utilized the idea of cluster expansion in the celebrated Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) spin glass model and obtained a rigorous solution of the model by proving a Gaussian central limit theorem of free energy. The results hold at high temperatures in the zero external field. The clusters they obtained are a collection of finite-sized simple loops in a complete graph. Similar results exist for the diluted SK model
[
39
]
and Hopfield model
[
24
,
53
]
. However, all these results are essentially restricted to the two-spin and zero external field cases. It was believed that the cluster expansion would not work in the presence of an external field
[
59
,
60
]
. Recently, in
[
27
]
, the authors made some progress by showing that the cluster expansion still works under weak external fields. A new cluster structure, a collection of simple paths with finite length, emerges instead. Based on this, they established a one-step transition behavior with a critical exponent of
1/4
for the fluctuation of free energy under weak external fields.

In this paper, we extend this technique to a more general setting, the mixed
p
-spin glass models, which have highly complicated physical behavior compared to the simple SK model. In the mixed
p
-spin setting, the spin interactions are present not only among two sites but on any finite
p\geqslant 3
sites and their mixtures. To carry out the cluster expansion technique to the
p
-spin setting has the following main challenges. First, in order for the later counting procedure to be feasible, one needs to use second-moment control for the partition function to exclude the infinite-size cluster structures. In the SK case, a Gaussian trick was used to approximate the second moment due to the quadratic-type interactions. However, this is not possible for the general
p
-spin case. Besides that, In the SK case, the second-moment analysis immediately reduces the cluster structure to a finite size. Nonetheless, in the
p
-spin setting, the usual second-moment control can only reduce the hypergraph cluster size to
\log(N)
. To bypass this, we carefully analyze the structures with size at most
\log(N)
and show that under the correct scaling, contributions of large-size structures tend to zero in probability. Second, it is not even clear what should be the geometry of cluster structures. In the SK case, finite size “loop” and “path” are competitively contributing to the partition function. However, there is no proper notion of “loop” and “path” on general hypergraphs. Another fundamental question would be how many different possible cluster structures are contributing. Third, analyzing the mixed
p
-spin model can fundamentally differ from the pure
p
-spin case, where
p
is fixed. SK model can be treated as the pure
2
-spin model. In the mixed
p
-spin setting, where
p
is not fixed, this makes the challenges addressed more significant. Later one can see that the results for mixed
p
-spin and pure
p
-spin models are indeed quite different.

Despite the above challenges, we are able to give a complete characterization of the dominant cluster structures for all pure and mixed
p
-spin models under different external fields. As a result, we obtain new fluctuation results for the partition function in general mixed
p
-spin glass models and new
multiple-transition
behavior under weak external fields. This naturally induces the fluctuation results for the free energy. Besides that, our results also have the following interesting implications. First, for fixed
p\geqslant 3
, the fluctuation behaviors, accordingly the cluster structure geometry, are quite different for even and odd
p
. In many classical results of
p
-spin glass models, it has been a mystery why the odd and even
p
-spin model behave quite differently. For example, many important results
[
58
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
19
,
35
]
can only be proven in even
p
case rigorously. Our results give a combinatorial explanation of this folklore. Second, a byproduct of our analysis is a simple characterization of a new critical inverse temperature
\beta_{f}
for general spin glass models.
The threshold is derived from an optimal second-moment estimate of the partition function. It is worth noting that this new threshold has a very simple form. To our knowledge, it has never been found in the literature. Some numerical simulation has been given in Figure
1
, suggesting that this new threshold is neither the usual static phase transition point nor the dynamical phase transition point. It is of great interest to investigate the physical nature of

; we left this for future work. Third, a multi-scale fluctuation phenomenon was originally conjectured in
[

]
for the pure

-spin model with no external field. From our analysis, it can be immediately seen that this phenomenon holds for the general mixed

-spin model with external fields.
Finally, for mixed

-spin models, in terms of fluctuation and cluster geometry, there are three new regimes w.r.t

-spin model, while the other behaves more like a mixture model. This can be treated as a criterion for the classification of spin glass models.

We also emphasize that our idea is robust enough to extend to other variants, such as the multi-species mixed

1.2.
Main results

As mentioned before, a byproduct of our results is a new critical threshold. Before presenting its precise form, let us recall the existing critical thresholds in spin glass theory. For SK model, it is known that the static phase transition threshold is
\beta=1
. The model exhibits the so-called replica symmetric phase for
\beta<1
, and the replica symmetry breaking phase for
\beta>1
. Replica symmetry (breaking) is defined via the asymptotic distribution of the inner product of two configurations sampled from the SK Gibbs measure; if it is concentrated at some fixed point, then the model is called replica symmetry, symmetry breaking otherwise. Note that, in the seminal paper of Aizenman et.al
[
1
]
, the threshold for the second moment of the partition function is also
\beta=1
. On the other hand, there is another threshold known as the dynamical phase transition point, which separates the fast and slow mixing regions for the associated Glauber dynamics. For the SK model, the dynamic threshold is also believed to be
\beta=1

[
6
]
.

SK model is special in the sense that all thresholds coincide with each other. However, it is more involved in the general
p
-spin case. For pure
p
-spin models with
p\geqslant 3
, to the best of our knowledge, there is no explicit characterization of the static phase transition threshold yet. Some existing literature
[
23
]
has implicit form obtained via Parisi formula
[
58
,
45
]
, but in general it is hard to compute directly. Besides that, the dynamic phase transition threshold is widely believed to be strictly smaller than the static phase transition point, see
[
6
]
. In the current paper, we derived a new threshold
\beta_{f}
from a sharp second-moment estimate of
\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)
. Namely, for
\beta<\beta_{f}
, the second moment stays bounded and diverges for
\beta>\beta_{f}
. Numerical simulation given in Figure
1
suggests that this is neither the static nor the dynamic phase transition threshold.

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\hat{\omega}(\Gamma),

\displaystyle\text{ where }\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)=\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\Gamma}\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\tanh(\beta J_{\boldsymbol{i}}/N^{(p-1)/2}),\text{ for }\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p},

and

p

the number of hyperedges used and the number of odd-degree vertices in

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{(3,p),0}&:=\{\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{E}_{N,p}:(\left|\Gamma\right|,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|)=(3,0)\}\text{ and }\\

1.3.
Prior results

This section briefly summarizes the previous fluctuation results in the
p
-spin models and the study of transitional behavior while changing the external field. The first fluctuation result for pure
p
-spin models can be traced back to 2002, in which Bovier, Kurkova, and Löwe
[
15
]
used martingale approach to prove a central limit theorem for the free energy up to a
\beta_{p}<\beta_{c}
.
The Gaussian disorder played a key role in their proof. Later Knöpfel and Löwe
[
38
]
adapted the idea of Aizenman et. al.
[
1
]
to obtain a decomposition in (
3
), where the term
\log\bar{Z}_{N}
is essentially a sum of i.i.d. random variables, and they proved that the variance order of
\log\hat{Z}_{N}
is smaller than that of
\log\bar{Z}_{N}
using concentration results. Therefore, the fluctuation of limiting free energy is dominated by
\log\bar{Z}_{N}
.

The proof worked for general symmetric disorders with finite exponential moments. Again, this result also holds up to some
\tilde{\beta}_{p}<\beta_{c}
. In short, those two results were both established in the zero external field setting for pure
p
-spin models and a strictly sub-region of the whole high-temperature regime. More importantly, it was unclear what happens in the term
\hat{Z}_{N}
in both works. On the other hand, in 2017, Chen, Dey, and Panchenko
[
19
]
extended the fluctuation results to the mixed even
p
-spin models in the presence of an external field, and they proved that in this case, the Gaussian fluctuation of free energy persists for all
\beta<\infty
. The key idea is based on Stein’s method and superconcentration results using the Parisi formula. However, one weakness is the even
p
restriction, which seems a common hurdle in many other problems, see
[
21
,
20
,
58
,
22
]
. Recently, Banerjee and Belius
[
10
]
used the weighted cycle counting technique to obtain the fluctuation results for mixed
p
-spin models at zero external fields with a non-vanishing 2-spin term. Note that this technique is different from the loop counting arising in cluster expansion. One notable weakness is that it works up to some implicitly high temperature and is also restricted to zero field cases.

Although there has been much progress in understanding the fluctuation of free energy at zero and positive external field, the transition behavior between them has long been elusive, especially in the Ising spin case. In the SK model (
p=2
) at a high-temperature regime, the authors
[
27
]
utilized various approaches to prove that in the high-temperature regime, there are three sub-regimes concerning the strength of the weak external field
h=\rho N^{-\alpha}
for some
\rho>0,\alpha\in[0,\infty]
. In the sub-critical case,
\alpha>\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
, the fluctuation is nearly the same as
h=0
. In the critical case
\alpha=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
, the fluctuation order does not change, but the asymptotic mean and variance now have some extra corrections. In the supercritical case, the fluctuation order becomes linear. One of their approaches is based on cluster expansion. They also established similar results in some other generalized models, such as the multi-species and diluted SK models. In the spherical SK model, Baik et.al.
[
9
]
studied the transition behavior using the steepest descent method originating in random matrix theory, and some results are computer-aided. The results in the high-temperature regime are consistent with
[
27
]
for the Ising spin case. A concurrent work by Landon and Sosoe
[
40
]
obtained similar results by conducting a fully rigorous analysis, and the idea is essentially the same. All those results are restricted to the
p=2
spin case. In particular, extending the random matrix technique used in the spherical SK model to the spherical
p
-spin setting is challenging, and nothing is known. In summary, the transition behavior from zero to positive field for the general
p
-spin model is a complete mystery. Besides that, establishing results up to the critical inverse temperature is another challenge.

1.4.
Multivariate Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs

In the previous part, we briefly sketch how to decompose
\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)
into a collection of different clusters in different regimes. To establish the limit theorems described in Theorem
1.9
,
1.12
and Corollary
1.14
, we apply the following version of multivariate Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs. Stein’s method is a modern way to prove normal approximation; we refer interested readers for the background to
[
18
]
.

Theorem 1.16

(
[
51
]
*Theorem 2.1)
.

Assume that
(\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{W}^{\prime})
is an exchangeable pair of
\mathds{R}^{d}
valued random vectors such that

\E\boldsymbol{W}=0,\E\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{W}^{T}=\Sigma

where
\Sigma\in\mathds{R}^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix and
\boldsymbol{W}
has finite third moment in each coordinate. Suppose further that

(26)

\displaystyle\E(\boldsymbol{W}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{W})=-\Lambda\boldsymbol{W}\quad\text{a.s.},

for an invertible matrix
\Lambda
. If
\boldsymbol{Z}
has
d
-dimensional standard normal distribution, we have for every three times differentiable function
f
,

\displaystyle\left|\E f(\boldsymbol{W})-\E f(\Sigma^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z})\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\left|f\right|_{2}A+\frac{1}{12}\left|f\right|_{3}B

where
\left|f\right|_{2}:=\sup_{i,j}\left\|\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}f\right\|_{\infty},\left|f\right|_{3}:=\sup_{i,j,k}\left\|\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}f\right\|_{\infty}
,
\lambda^{(i)}:=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left|(\Lambda^{-1})_{m,i}\right|
,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\lambda^{(i)}\sqrt{\var\E((W_{i}^{\prime}-W_{i})(W_{j}^{\prime}-W_{j})\mid\boldsymbol{W})}

\displaystyle\text{ and }B

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\lambda^{(i)}\E\left|(W_{i}^{\prime}-W_{i})(W_{j}^{\prime}-W_{j})(W_{k}^{\prime}-W_{k})\right|.

In our setting, the random vector we will work on is a collection of random variables indexed by different cluster structures. For example, in the regime
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
for pure even
p
-spin model,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}=\left(\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq S_{(3,p),0}}\omega(\Gamma),\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq S_{(2,p),2}}\omega(\Gamma)\right).

Moreover, the linearity condition (
26
) is satisfied with
\Lambda
of the form
\lambda D
, where
\lambda=o(1)
is a scalar, and
D
is a deterministic matrix.
The details for checking the associated conditions are left to Section
6
. We remark that Stein’s method proof presents a more straightforward and systematic picture than the moment-based method and comes with a convergence rate under minimal moment assumptions.

2.1.
A word on the proof

We briefly discuss the extra challenges for the proof of the main results in mixed
p
-spin setting and our strategy to deal with it. The first and core challenge is caused by the flexibility of
p
-hyperedges. We must show that the dominant clusters can not include large
p
-hyperedges. This is similar to the large hypergraph decay results in Proposition
4.3
. The high-level idea for establishing this result is to decompose the large
p
-hyperedge into several smaller hyperedges, this will increase the cluster size, and then we use the large hypergraph decaying results to conclude. Once we know that the dominant clusters will have finitely many hyperedges and all the hyperedges are finite tuples, the next challenge will be identifying the leading clusters. Although the cluster enjoys the above nice property, there still can be many possibilities due to various combinations of different
p
-hyperedges. This part needs very careful analysis. In particular, the leading clusters change w.r.t. two parameters, external field strength, and the mixture of hyperedges. Finally, after obtaining all the dominant clusters, we use multivariate Stein’s method in Theorem
1.16
to establish the joint convergence of different clusters to the normal limit. The computations there become more involved in the pure
p
-spin case. We highlight the ideas in Section
7
.

\left|\mathscr{S}\right|=k<\infty
, for the number of spins
N>0
, let

\displaystyle\{1,2,\ldots,N\}=\bigcup_{s\in\mathscr{S}}I_{s},\quad\text{and}\ I_{s}\cap I_{t}=\emptyset\ \text{for}\ s\neq t.

Assume the density ratio of species
s\in\mathscr{S}
is
\lambda_{s}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\left|I_{s}\right|}{N}
. We denote

\Lambda:=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{k}).

For each
p>3
, let

\Delta_{p}^{2}:=(\Delta_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}}^{2})_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\in\mathscr{S}}\in\mathds{R}_{+}^{\left|\mathscr{S}\right|^{p}}

be a symmetric
p
-dimensional tensor. For
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
, the Hamiltonian of multi-species pure
p
-spin model can be defined as follows:

(34)

\displaystyle H_{N,\Delta_{p}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{1}{N^{(p-1)/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\Delta_{s(\boldsymbol{i})}J_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}},

where
s(\boldsymbol{i})
maps each coordinate of the
p
-tuple
\boldsymbol{i}
to a species in
\mathscr{S}
. The Hamiltonian of multi-species mixed
p
-spin model is

(35)

\displaystyle H_{N,\Delta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\sum_{p\geqslant 3}H_{N,\Delta_{p}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

To make sure the model is well-defined, it needs the following fast decaying assumption,

(36)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\geqslant 3}\left\|\Lambda^{\otimes p}\Delta_{p}^{2}\right\|_{\infty}/(p-2)!<\infty.

Here the limiting structure function is

\displaystyle\xi(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{p\geqslant 3}\frac{1}{p!}\langle\Lambda^{\otimes p}\Delta_{p}^{2},\boldsymbol{x}^{\otimes p}\rangle\text{ for }\boldsymbol{x}\in[-1,1]^{k}.

Parisi formula for the multi-species SK model was first established in
[
46
]
under the positive semi-definite assumption on
\Delta
. For general indefinite
\Delta
, computing the limiting free energy still remains a challenging open question. The fluctuation results for the general multi-species SK model at a high-temperature regime were obtained in
[
28
]
. In the spherical setting, some progress has been made in evaluating the limiting free energy. Recently, for multi-species mixed
p
-spin spherical models, Bates and Sohn established the Crisanti-Sommers formula in
[

12
]
under the positive semi-definite assumption on
\Delta
. Shortly after these works, Subag
[
56

55
]
utilized the Thouless-Anderson-Palmer (TAP) approach instead to compute the limiting free energy and ground state energy for multi-species pure
p
-spin case. Notably, this approach works for general indefinite
\Delta
. The fluctuation results for both Ising and spherical multi-species mixed
p
-spin models are still missing. Using the intuition from the last section, we can easily obtain the fluctuation results for general indefinite multi-species Ising mixed
p
-spin models in the entire high-temperature regime.

First, let us introduce the characterization of the critical inverse temperature for multi-species models.

\displaystyle\beta_{f}(\xi):=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in(0,1]^{k}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}I(x_{i})}{\xi(\boldsymbol{x})}}.

Analogue of Theorem

4.
Large Hypergraph Decay Regime

4.1.
Large hypergraph decay

We begin with the proof of the property that large hypergraph contributions decay fast in the whole high-temperature regime. Compared to the SK (
p=2
) model, the Gaussian trick (see
[
27
]
*Lemma 4.1) for controlling the second moments of
\hat{Z}_{N}
is not available any more due to the loss of quadratic features. Instead, we seek an integral approximation of the targeted discrete sum, then directly control the integral upper bound.

We first look at the second moment of
\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)
. Define
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\right|=p
for
\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}
. Recall that

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\tanh(\beta\theta_{p}\cdot J_{\boldsymbol{i}}/N^{(p-1)/2})\text{ for }\boldsymbol{i}\in\cup_{p\geqslant 2}\mathscr{E}_{N,p}

and

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)=\E_{h}\prod_{p\geqslant 2}\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}(1+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\cdot\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}).

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
. For
\beta<\beta_{f}(\xi)
, we have for all
N
large,

\displaystyle\E[(\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h))^{2}]\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{\beta_{f}^{2}-\beta^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{4}\beta^{2}}{\beta_{f}^{2}-\beta^{2}}\right)

where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Remark 4.2
.

In Lemma
4.1
we do not need
\theta_{2}
to be
0
. However, when
\theta_{2}=0
one can get a better
N
-dependent upper bound.
Moreover, using
\E\tanh^{2}(\beta J/N^{(p-1)/2})\leqslant\beta^{2}/N^{p-1}
, one can replace
\E(\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h))^{2}
by
\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}h^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\prod_{p\geqslant 3}\left(\theta_{p}^{2}\beta^{2}/N^{p-1}\right)^{\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}}
in Lemma
4.1
, where
|\Gamma|_{p}
is the number of
p
-hyperedges in
\Gamma
.

Proof.

We notice that

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)^{2}=\E_{h}\prod_{p\geqslant 2}\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}(1+(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}+\tau_{\boldsymbol{i}})\cdot\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\tau_{\boldsymbol{i}}\cdot\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}).

After taking the expectation with respect to the disorder, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{N}(\beta,h):=\E\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)^{2}

\displaystyle=\E_{h}\prod_{p\geqslant 2}\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}(1+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\tau_{\boldsymbol{i}}\E\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\E_{\tilde{h}^{2}}\prod_{p\geqslant 2}\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}(1+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\cdot\E\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2})\leqslant\E_{\tilde{h}^{2}}\exp\left(\sum_{p\geqslant 2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\E\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}\cdot\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\right)

where
\tilde{h}^{2}:=\tanh^{-1}(\tanh^{2}(h))\leqslant h^{2}
. Note that,

\displaystyle\E\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}\leqslant\beta^{2}\theta_{p}^{2}/N^{p-1}\text{ for }\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}

and

\displaystyle\left|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\right)^{p}-p!\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\right|\leqslant N^{p}-(N)_{p}=N^{p}\left(1-\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(1-k/N)\right)\leqslant\binom{p}{2}N^{p-1}.

Thus

(38)

\displaystyle\phi_{N}(\beta,h)

\displaystyle\leqslant\E_{\tilde{h}^{2}}\prod_{p\geqslant 2}\exp\biggl(N\beta^{2}\theta_{p}^{2}/p!\cdot|S_{N}/N|^{p}+\beta^{2}\theta_{p}^{2}/2(p-2)!\biggr)

(39)

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\sum_{p\geqslant 2}\theta_{p}^{2}/(p-2)!\biggr)\cdot\E_{\tilde{h}^{2}}\exp\biggl(N\beta^{2}\cdot\xi(|S_{N}/N|)\biggr)

where
S_{N}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}
and
\xi(x)=\sum_{p\geqslant 2}\theta_{p}^{2}x^{p}/p!
. Continuing from the r.h.s, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\tilde{h}^{2}}\exp\biggl(N\beta^{2}\cdot\xi(|S_{N}/N|)\biggr)

\displaystyle=e^{-N\log\cosh\tilde{h}^{2}}\cdot\E_{0}\exp\biggl(N\beta^{2}\cdot\xi(|S_{N}/N|)+\tilde{h}^{2}\cdot S_{N}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\E_{0}\exp\biggl(N\beta^{2}\cdot\xi(|S_{N}/N|)+\tilde{h}^{2}\cdot S_{N}\biggr).

Here the expectation
\E_{0}
is with respect to the i.i.d.
\{-1,+1\}
valued
\sigma_{i}
with mean
0
. Our goal is to derive a uniform bound on the r.h.s. of the above expression.

Now with
t=\beta/\beta_{f}\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\beta^{2}\cdot\xi(x)\leqslant t^{2}\cdot I(x)\text{ for all }x\in[0,1]

where

\displaystyle I(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+x)\ln(1+x)+\frac{1}{2}(1-x)\ln(1-x).

Moreover, we will use the fact that

\displaystyle\pr(|S_{N}|\geqslant Nx)\leqslant 2\exp(-NI(x)),

and

\displaystyle x^{2}/2\leqslant I(x)\leqslant x^{2}/2\cdot(1+x^{2}/2)

for all
x\in[0,1]
. With

\displaystyle f_{N}(x)=N\beta^{2}\xi(x/N)+\log\cosh(\hat{h}\cdot x)\leqslant Nt^{2}\cdot I(x/N)+\log\cosh(\hat{h}\cdot x),

which is convex and increasing in
[0,N]

we can write

\displaystyle\E_{0}\exp\biggl(N\beta^{2}\cdot\xi(|S_{N}/N|)+\hat{h}\cdot S_{N}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\E_{0}\exp(f_{N}(|S_{N}|))

\displaystyle=\E_{0}\exp(f_{N}(|S_{N}|\wedge k_{N}))+\E_{0}(\exp(f_{N}(|S_{N}|))-\E_{0}\exp(f_{N}(k_{N})))\mathds{1}_{|S_{N}|>k_{N}}

for some
k_{N}
, to be chosen later so that

\displaystyle t_{N}:=t\cdot\sqrt{1+k_{N}^{2}/2N^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{(1+t^{2})/2}

or

\displaystyle 1-t_{N}^{2}\geqslant(1-t^{2})/2>0.

Thus, we have with
\eta\sim\N(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\E_{0}\exp(f_{N}(|S_{N}|\wedge k_{N}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\E_{0}\exp(t_{N}^{2}S_{N}^{2}/2N+\hat{h}\cdot S_{N})

\displaystyle=\E\exp(N\log\cosh(\eta\cdot t_{N}+\sqrt{N\hat{h}^{2}})/\sqrt{N})\leqslant\E\exp(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(\eta\cdot t_{N}+\rho^{2})^{2}).

Here, we used the Gaussian trick in the equality. Simplifying we get

\displaystyle\E_{0}\exp(f_{N}(|S_{N}|\wedge k_{N}))

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-t_{N}^{2})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\exp(\rho^{4}/2(1-t_{N}^{2})).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\E_{0}(\exp(f_{N}(|S_{N}|))

\displaystyle-\E_{0}\exp(f_{N}(k_{N})))\mathds{1}_{|S_{N}|>k_{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=k_{N}+1}^{N}(\exp(f_{N}(k))-\exp(f_{N}(k-1)))\cdot\pr(|S_{N}|\geqslant k)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=k_{N}+1}^{N}f_{N}^{\prime}(k)\exp(f_{N}(k))\cdot 2\exp(-NI(k/N)).

Simplifying, we get

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(\beta^{2}\xi^{\prime}(1)+\hat{h})\cdot\sum_{k=k_{N}+1}^{N}\exp(-(1-t^{2})NI(k/N)+\hat{h}k)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(\beta^{2}\xi^{\prime}(1)+\hat{h})\cdot\sum_{k=k_{N}+1}^{N}\exp(-(1-t^{2})k^{2}/2N+\rho^{2}k/\sqrt{N})

\displaystyle\leqslant c\sqrt{N}\int_{k_{N}/\sqrt{N}}^{\infty}\exp(-(1-t^{2})x^{2}/2+\rho^{2}x)\ dx

\displaystyle\leqslant c\sqrt{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\exp(\rho^{4}/2(1-t^{2}))\cdot\pr(\eta\geqslant\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\cdot(k_{N}/\sqrt{N}-\rho^{2}/(1-t^{2}))).

By choosing

\displaystyle 4\rho^{4}/(1-t^{2})^{2}+8\log N/(1-t^{2})\leqslant k_{N}^{2}/N\leqslant(1-t^{2})N

we get the stated result.

\blacksquare

From the analysis, it is relatively easy to derive the decay results for large graph contributions in
p
-spin models. We formulate this into the following Proposition. First denote that

(40)

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{N,m}:=\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p},\left|\Gamma\right|\leqslant m}\hat{h}^{\mid\partial\Gamma|}\cdot\hat{\omega}(\Gamma).

Proposition 4.3

(Large hypergraph error decay)
.

If
\beta<\beta_{f}
, for
m\geqslant O_{\beta,\rho}(\log N)
, we have

(41)

\displaystyle N^{p_{m}-2}\cdot\left\|\hat{Z}_{N}-\hat{Z}_{N,m}\right\|_{2}\to 0\text{ as }N\to\infty.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Recall that
\beta_{N,p}^{2}=N^{(p-1)}\E\tanh^{2}(\beta J/N^{(p-1)/2})
and
h=\rho N^{-\alpha}
,

(42)

\displaystyle\phi_{N}(\beta,h)=\E\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)^{2}=\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\hat{h}^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\prod_{p\geqslant 3}\left(\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}\beta_{N,p}^{2}}{N^{p-1}}\right)^{\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}},

where
\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}
denote the number of
p
-hyperedges in the hypergraph
\Gamma
, and
\left|\Gamma\right|=\sum_{p\geqslant 3}\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}
.
On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p},\left|\Gamma\right|\geqslant m}\hat{h}^{2\left|\Gamma\right|}\prod_{p\geqslant 3}\left(\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}\beta_{N,p}^{2}}{N^{p-1}}\right)^{\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}}\leqslant e^{-2my}\phi_{N}(e^{y}\beta,h).

Following the bound for
\phi_{N}(\beta,h)
derived before in Lemma
4.1
and the Remark
4.2
, we have

(43)

\displaystyle\phi_{N}(\beta,h)\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{\beta_{f}^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{4}\beta_{N,p}^{2}}{\beta_{f}^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{2}}\right).

Then

\displaystyle N^{2(p_{m}-2)}\cdot\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p},\left|\Gamma\right|\geqslant m.}\hat{h}^{2\left|\Gamma\right|}\cdot\prod_{p\geqslant 3}\left(\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}\beta^{2}}{N^{p-1}}\right)^{\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant cN^{2(p_{m}-2)}\cdot\exp(-2my),

as long as
e^{y}\beta<\beta_{f}
or
y<\log(\beta_{f}/\beta)
.
From the last step, as long as
my-(p_{m}-2)\log N\to\infty
we have the required result.

\blacksquare

In the last proposition, we proved that the very large size clusters’ contribution to
\hat{Z}_{N}
decays exponentially fast. This allows us to deal with much smaller hypergraphs and simplify the problem significantly. From the proof, it can be seen that this large graph decaying regime intrinsically characterizes the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{f}
. In the SK model (
p=2
case)
[
27
]
, this type of result is strong enough to obtain a reduction of the clusters to finite size since the structures in the ordinary graph are relatively simple. However, in the
p
-spin case, this reduction can at most reduce the cluster size to
\log N
, which is due to the complicated structures in the hypergraph. In the following part, we are devoted to establishing a further reduction, namely reducing the
\log N
size clusters to finite size. The key technique we utilized is a form of chaos expansion and second-moment computations. The strategy is as follows.

Proposition 4.4
.

For given mixed
p
-spin models
\xi
, in the regime
\beta<\beta_{f}(\xi)
and external field strength
\alpha\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
, let

(44)

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{N,4}:=\sum_{\left|\Gamma\right|\leqslant 4,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|\geqslant 0}\hat{h}^{\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\cdot\hat{\omega}(\Gamma),

we have

(45)

\displaystyle N^{p_{m}-2}\cdot\left\|\hat{Z}_{N,m}-\hat{Z}_{N,4}\right\|_{2}\to 0

as
N\to\infty
.

Remark 4.5
.

The rescaling
N^{p_{m}-2}
corresponds to the smallest variance cluster (see Table
2
). The goal is to show that even under this strongest scaling, any clusters of size greater than 4 are in subleading order. Thus, in all regimes, it suffices to consider clusters of size less than or equal to 4.

Proof.

The proof is divided into two steps. First, we establish the results in the setting of pure
p
-spin models, then we prove a helpful lemma that suffices to establish the results for mixed
p
-spin models.
For the pure
p
-spin model, the second moment of the LHS is the sum over
\ell=5,6,\ldots,m
of

\displaystyle N^{2(p-2)}\cdot\sum_{\left|\Gamma\right|=\ell,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|\geqslant 0}\hat{h}^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\E\prod_{i\in\Gamma}\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta J_{i}}{N^{(p-1)/2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{2(p-2)}\cdot\sum_{\left|\Gamma\right|=\ell,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|\geqslant 0}\hat{h}^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2\ell}}{N^{(p-1)\ell}}.

Now the RHS can be bounded by

\displaystyle N^{2(p-2)}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2\ell}}{N^{(p-1)\ell}}\frac{1}{\ell!}\E_{\hat{h}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}}\right)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{2(p-2)}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2\ell}}{N^{(p-1)\ell}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell!(p!)^{\ell}}\E_{\hat{h}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\right)^{p\ell}

Recall that,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N})
are i.i.d. with mean
\hat{h}=\tanh(h)
. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\bar{\sigma}_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\sigma}_{N})
be the centered version of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. Thus, we can further bound the RHS by

\displaystyle=N^{2(p-2)}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2\ell}}{N^{(p-1)\ell}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell!(p!)^{\ell}}\cdot N^{p\ell/2}\cdot\E_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\sigma}_{i}+\sqrt{N\hat{h}^{4}}\right)^{p\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{2(p-2)}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2\ell}}{N^{(p-1)\ell}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell!(p!)^{\ell}}\cdot N^{p\ell/2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{cp\ell}+\sqrt{\rho}\right)^{p\ell}\approx N^{(p-2)(2-\ell/2)}\cdot(cp\ell)^{p\ell/2},

In the above derivation,
\E_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
is the expectation with respect to
\bar{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, which is basically same as
\E_{0}
used in the proof of Lemma
4.1
. Since
h
is in the regime
\alpha\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
, that is,
\sqrt{N\hat{h}^{4}}=O(1)
, the constant
c
in the last two steps depends on
\rho
. The inequality follows from the classical results about moment estimates for i.i.d sum of bounded random variables
[
50
,
41
]
. Notice that
p\geqslant 3
, if
4<\ell<m
, where
m
is some finite constant independent of
N
, then the polynomial term
N^{(p-2)(1-\ell/2)}
decays much faster. Note that even if
m
can be as large as
O(\log N)
, the polynomial decay still dominates.

Next, we argue why the above results also suffice for mixed
p
-spin models. For mixed
p
-spin models, we need to bound

(46)

\displaystyle N^{2(p_{m}-2)}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p};\\
\left|\Gamma\right|=k,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\hat{h}^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\prod_{p\geqslant 3}\E\left(\tanh^{2}(\beta\theta_{p}J/N^{(p-1)/2})\right)^{\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}},

where
\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}
is the number of
p
-hyperedges in
\Gamma
, and
\left|\Gamma\right|=\sum_{p\geqslant 3}\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}
. Continuing the above expression

\displaystyle\beta^{2k}N^{2(p_{m}-2)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p};\\
\left|\Gamma\right|=k,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\hat{h}^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\prod_{p\geqslant 3}\frac{\theta_{p}^{2\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}}}{N^{(p-1)\left|\Gamma\right|_{p}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\beta^{2k}N^{2(p_{m}-2)+k}\cdot\E_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\left(\sum_{p\geqslant 3}\theta_{p}^{2}\cdot\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i})^{p}}{N^{p}p!}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=N^{2(p_{m}-2)+k}\cdot\E_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\left(\beta^{2}\xi\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\sigma}_{i}+\hat{h}^{2}\right)\right)^{k}.

Notice that for mixed
p
-spin models,
\xi\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\sigma}_{i}+\hat{h}^{2}\right)\leqslant C\cdot\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\sigma}_{i}+\hat{h}^{2}\right)^{p_{m}}

, that is, mixed
p
-spin is dominated by the leading pure

-spin model up to a constant factor. Thus the mixed

-spin results can be deduced from the pure

\blacksquare

In the mixed
p
-spin case, there is a further roadblock that even for a fixed small-size cluster, one could have infinitely many different clusters. This is due to the flexibility over

and any

Proposition 4.6

For a mixed

-spin model with structure function

(47)

\displaystyle N^{p_{m}-2}\cdot\sum_{\Gamma:e_{\max}\geqslant k,|\Gamma|\leqslant 4}\hat{h}^{\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\omega(\Gamma)\to 0

in probability as

, where

The above results basically say that for the small-sized clusters obtained in the mixed

-spin setting, the leading contributions can not contain any large

-hyperedges. This enables us to focus on analyzing the clusters of small size and small hyperedges. This proof of this proposition basically follows from the idea that any large

Proof of Proposition

.

Without loss of generality, we focus on the case

\displaystyle N^{2(p_{m}-2)}\cdot\left(\sum_{\Gamma:p_{2}\geqslant k}\hat{h}^{2\left|\partial\Gamma\right|}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\theta_{p_{i}}^{2}\beta^{2}}{N^{p_{i}-1}}\right).

Proof of Lemma

First, we show that the function

\displaystyle\phi(e^{t})=\left(\log(I(e^{t})/I(1))\right)^{\prime}\text{ and }I(e^{t})/I(1)=\E(e^{tX}),t\leqslant 0

\pr(X=2k)=1/(\log 2\cdot 2k(2k-1)),k\geqslant 1.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi(e^{t})=\left(\log(I(e^{t})/I(1))\right)^{\prime\prime}>0\text{ for }t<0.

In particular, for

Now, for

. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}u^{p}/p!=I(u)\text{ and }\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}u^{p-1}/(p-1)!=I^{\prime}(u),

which implies that

\displaystyle\phi(u)=\frac{uI^{\prime}(u)}{I(u)}=p\text{ and }u=\phi^{-1}(p).

Thus, we have

5.
Dominant Clusters for
p
-Spin Models

In the previous section, we reduced the size of clusters contributing to
\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta,h)
to 4. In this section, we will identify the leading clusters in various regimes among those finite size cluster structures. The competing influences of those leading clusters naturally give rise to the multiple transition picture described in Section
1
and
2
.

In the pure
p
-spin models, the underlying hypergraph is uniform and the leading clusters can change depending on strength of the weak external field. However, in the mixed
p
-spin case, the underlying graph is a general non-uniform hypergraph, where all possible hyperedges can appear. The leading clusters now can also change with respect to
p
. This is the fundamental reason of the two parameter multiple transition summarized in Table
2
. This extra freedom on selecting any
p
-hyperedges creates further roadblocks for identifying the leading clusters. Even for a fixed-size cluster, there still can be many different cluster structures since one can always choose any
p
-hyperedges in the cluster. Although the mixed
p
-spin model looks complicated, it turns out the ideas obtained while analyzing the pure
p
-spin case are still helpful in the mixed case. We start with pure
p
-spin model results. The general strategy of those analyses can be reformulated as a discrete optimization problem over the set of possible leading clusters.

We will use
c=((a_{1},p_{1}),\ldots,(a_{t},p_{t}),\ell)
to denote a cluster structure with
a_{i}
many
p_{i}
hyperedges for
i=1,2,\ldots,t
and
\ell
many odd-degree vertices. Let

\displaystyle V_{c}:=\sum_{\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{c}}\hat{h}^{\ell}\cdot\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)=\hat{h}^{\ell}\cdot N^{-\sum a_{i}(p_{i}-1)/2}\cdot\sum_{\Gamma}\omega(\Gamma)=\hat{h}^{\ell}\cdot N^{-\sum a_{i}(p_{i}-1)/2}\cdot W_{c}.

We have,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{N}-1=\sum_{c\in\mathscr{C}}V_{c}

where the sum is over all cluster structures
\mathscr{C}
.
Recall that
{W}_{c}
is the sum of
\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)
over all hypergraphs
\Gamma\in\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p}
with a given cluster structure
c
. We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}(c):=-2\alpha\ell-\sum_{i}a_{i}(p_{i}-1)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}p_{i}+\ell\right)

as the real number such that
\var(V_{c})=\Theta(N^{\mathcal{X}(c)})
. Here is the general abstract framework, we need to find

\mathscr{C}_{\star}:=\argmax_{c\in\mathscr{C}}{\mathcal{X}(c)}\text{ and }\gamma_{\star}=\max_{c\in\mathscr{C}}{\mathcal{X}(c)}

where
\mathscr{C}
is the collection of all possible cluster structures contributing to the variance of the partition function. In particular, we have

\displaystyle N^{\gamma_{\star}/2}\cdot(\hat{Z}_{N}-1)\approx N^{\gamma_{\star}/2}\cdot\sum_{c\in\mathscr{C}_{\star}}V_{c}.

Thus the goal is to find the cluster structure(s) having the highest order of variance in each regime. We will instantiate this framework while analyzing different cases in the following subsections.

5.1.
Clusters for pure
p
-spin model at
h=0

We first deal with the case of
h=0
for pure
p
-spin models. The key tool is the second-moment method. In particular, we calculate the variance order of all possible clusters and identify the dominant ones. If
h=0
, by the decomposition of
\hat{Z}_{N}
, we know that the effective clusters should have zero odd-degree vertices. Since the cluster size has been reduced to 4 in Section
4
, there are only a few cases left. Specifically, we find that the leading clusters for odd and even
p
are
((4,p),0)
and
((3,p),0)
, respectively. This discrepancy explains why even and odd
p
-spin models behave differently, as suggested in the past literature. Besides that, the extra hyperedge in odd
p
-spin case creates quite delicate structures. This can be seen from the edge intersection mechanism in Figure
2
. Later, this subtlety will become even more apparent when we analyze the case with a weak external field and apply Stein’s method to establish the CLT. Accordingly, the analysis in the odd
p
case is more involved.

Recall that the leading structures are already given in (
12
). Examples of the cluster structures are presented in Figures
2
and
3
. Also, recall that
\ell_{p}
is defined as

(52)

\displaystyle\ell_{p}=\begin{cases}4&\text{ if $p$ is odd, }\\
3&\text{ if $p$ is even.}\end{cases}

We state this result formally in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

For pure
p
-spin models with
p\geqslant 3
fixed and
h=0
, we have

(53)

\displaystyle N^{\frac{\ell_{p}}{4}(p-2)}\left(\hat{Z}_{N,4}-1-\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{(\ell_{p},p),0}}\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)\right)\to 0

in probability as
N\to\infty
.

Note that
N^{-\ell_{p}(p-2)/2}
is the variance order of clusters
(\ell_{p},0)
. This proposition essentially says that the cluster
(\ell_{p},0)
is the leading contribution to
\hat{Z}_{N}(\beta)
. Recall that the notation
\mathcal{S}_{(\ell_{p},0)}
for the set of cluster structures were defined in Section
1.5
.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Notice that
\ell_{p}(p-2)/4\leqslant(p-2)
, by Proposition
4.4
, we know that the contribution of all clusters with
\left|\Gamma\right|>4
will converge to 0 in probability under the given rescaling. All we need to consider is the contributions from cluster sizes 3 and 4. Since given the even degree restriction, single hyperedge (
\left|\Gamma\right|=1
) can not form a cluster with zero odd-degree vertices (
\left|\partial\Gamma\right|=0)
. Neither do two hyperedges without forming multi-edges. In order to prove (
53
), one needs to identify the dominant clusters in
\hat{Z}_{N,4}
. Therefore it naturally leads to the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\argmax_{a=3,4}\mathcal{X}(((a,p),0))\text{ such that }2\text{ divides }ap.

In general, the zero external field condition requires each cluster to have no odd-degree vertices. Besides that, for a fixed-size cluster, it is clear that the leading contribution would be the case where each vertex’s degree is exactly 2, otherwise, the number of possible structures is much smaller thus the contribution is in subleading terms.

Now let us compute the contribution of leading clusters. For even
p
, by the structural restriction, we know that
a=3,4
. In general, for
\left|\Gamma\right|=a
, since the total weights on the hyperedges are fixed, one needs to count how many structures of size
a
are. In this case, there are
N^{ap/2}
different ways to form the
(a,0)
cluster; equivalently, we need to choose
ap/2
vertices among
N
. Each of such structures has a variance of order
N^{-a(p-1)}
. Therefore, the total contribution is of order
N^{-a(p-2)/2}
. It is clear that the dominant cluster is
a=3
. Therefore, under the scaling
N^{3(p-2)/4}
, the second moment of all other structures converges to 0 as
N\to\infty
. The odd
p
case is much more simpler, since the restriction
\frac{ap}{2}\in\mathbb{N}
needs
a
even, thus
a=4
. The variance order, in this case, is
\Theta(N^{-2(p-2)})
. The desired results follow similarly as in the even case.

\blacksquare

5.2.
Clusters for pure
p
-spin model with
h\neq 0

Once an external field is present, the even degree restriction on clusters is no longer needed. Although the large hypergraph decay results in Proposition
4.3
and
4.4
reduce the cluster size to 4, there are still a lot of possible structures by allowing odd-degree vertices. It is observed that the new clusters with odd-degree vertices must have smaller cluster sizes. This observation seems natural but actually has a bit of subtlety. Take
(3,0)
and
(3,1)
cluster as an example for any particular structure of those two kinds, allowing odd-degree vertices clearly decrease the variance order. However, the number of cluster structures for
(3,1)
also increases, making it less obvious what is the concluding effect and needs more precise quantitative control. By explicit computation, we observed that the new dominant cluster has to be of a smaller size to boost the variance in order to compete with the cluster contribution at
h=0
. A similar but more quantitative issue is whether the system prefers more or fewer odd-degree vertices if there are any. More odd-degree vertices will diminish the contribution of each particular structure more significantly but also increases the number of possible structures. A similar trade-off makes the analysis quite delicate. Regarding this, it is natural to see that the strength of the external field essentially controls how many odd-degree vertices should appear in the new leading cluster. This fundamentally characterizes the multiple transition picture in Table
1
.

Guided by the above observations, when
\alpha
decreases from
\infty
(corresponds to
h=0
case), some new clusters beyond
(3,0),(4,0)
in (
12
) will appear, an immediate question is when those new clusters can have same variance order as the
h=0
clusters. Based on the above observations, we found that the new dominant clusters are
(3,1),(2,2),(1,p)
in (
20
). To find the threshold
\alpha_{c}
, we need to compute the variance order of those new clusters. One can see in Table
1
, the clusters at
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
for even and odd
p
are still different. However, if the external field gets stronger for
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
, specifically at
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
,
i.e.,
the second threshold, the dominant clusters become unified for both even and odd
p
. Finally, in the second unified regime
\mathcal{R}_{3}
, the leading cluster becomes even simpler and just a single hyperedge. As it indicates, the external field gets stronger and stronger, the cluster size becomes smaller and smaller.

To explain the details, we will use the notation
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
to represent the set of leading cluster structures for a given
\alpha
, as listed in Table
1
:

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\alpha):=\{\text{cluster structures in the last column of Table\penalty\ \ref{tab:trans} for given $\alpha$}\}.

To compute the first threshold
\alpha_{c}
, we follow the insights obtained in the SK (
p=2
) case
[
27
]
, where the transition is very simple and only two clusters appear and compete with each other. The threshold
\alpha_{c}
is the point where the contributions from two clusters achieve a balance. In the
p
-spin case, since the cluster structures are more involved, we need to first identify all possible dominant clusters and then compute their contributions. Depending on the strength of external fields, the final step is to find when those leading clusters’ contributions are in the same order.

We summarize the main results in the following Proposition. The proof details will give a formal explanation for the above heuristic discussions.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
p\geqslant 3
and
\alpha\geqslant\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
be fixed. We have

(55)

\displaystyle N^{\gamma}\left(\hat{Z}_{N,4}-1-\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha)}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)\hat{h}^{b}\right)\to 0

in probability as
N\to\infty
, where
\gamma
depending on
\alpha
is as given in the third column of Table
1
.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Let us start with instantiating the optimization framework first. Recall
\gamma_{i}\leqslant(p-2)
uniformly for
i=1,2,3
, for same reason, it suffices to consider the clusters of size
a\leqslant 4
.
As we did in the
h=0
case, now consider the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant 4,\\
a+b>2,\\
0\leqslant b\leqslant ap\end{subarray}}\mathcal{X}\left(((a,p),b)\right)\text{ s.t. }2\mid(ap+b),a>0,b\geqslant 0.

We briefly explain all those constraints. By allowing an external field, the degree of vertices can be both even and odd. Thus the cluster size
a
can be any integer from 1 to 4 inclusive without creating multi-edges. The number of odd-degree vertices
b
can be ranging from 0 to
ap
. As in the
h=0
case, notice that the possible leading clusters must have the following property. Whenever the degree is even, it must be exactly 2. Whenever the degree is odd, it must be exactly 1. Because for each fixed
(a,b)
, in this way the number of possible structures is maximal and gives dominant contributions. Thus there are
N^{(ap-b)/2}
different ways to choose degree 2 vertices, and
N^{b}
different ways to choose the degree one vertices. This explains the restriction
2\mid ap+b
.

For
p
even, it is clear that
b
needs be even by the restriction
\frac{ap+b}{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Similarly, for
p
odd, it needs
a,b
to be even or odd in the meantime. In general, for fixed
(a,b)
in a given regime
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
, the variance order of the corresponding structure is
N^{(ap+b)/2}\cdot N^{-a(p-1)-2b\alpha}=N^{-\frac{a}{2}(p-2)-\frac{b}{2}(4\alpha-1)}
.

First, let us consider the case
\alpha=\alpha_{c}(p)
. Recall the definition of
\alpha_{c}
in (
13
), for even
p
, the variance is of order
N^{-(p-2)(\frac{a}{2}+\frac{b}{4})}
. In this case, the restriction is
a=1,2,3,4
and
b
is a non-negative integer. It is not hard to see that clusters
(2,2),(3,0)
have the largest variance. For odd
p
case, the variance is of order
N^{-(p-2)(\frac{a}{2}+\frac{b}{2})}
. Now the restriction becomes
a=1,2,3,4
and
b\in\mathbb{N}
are even or odd at the same time. The leading clusters, in this case, are
(4,0),(2,2),(3,1)
.
Furthermore, if
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
, the new leading clusters’ contribution will be in subleading order. The dominant clusters are the same as the
\alpha=\infty
case. The variance order in this regime is
\Theta(N^{-\ell_{p}(p-2)/2})
.

Now we analyze the case
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
similarly. For even
p
, the variance is of order
N^{-a(p-2)/2-b/2}
. The even
b
restriction gives the leading clusters
(2,2),(1,p)
. For odd
p
, the restriction becomes
a,b
are both even or odd. The leading clusters are also
(2,2),(1,p)
. If
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},\alpha_{c})
, since the variance order in general is
N^{-\frac{a}{2}(p-2)-\frac{b}{2}(4\alpha-1)}
, it is clear that

\displaystyle-(p-2)-(4\alpha-1)>-\frac{1}{2}(p-2)-\frac{p}{2}(4\alpha-1)

for
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},\alpha_{c})
. The l.h.s and r.h.s. are the exponent of the variance of
(2,2),(1,p)
clusters respectively. One can also perform similar computations for the other cases. In summary, the leading cluster for
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},\alpha_{c})
is
(2,2)
for both even and odd
p
. The variance order, in this case, is
N^{3-4\alpha-p}
.

The analysis in the regime
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
is very similar to the previous case. The leading cluster is
(1,p)
, and the variance order is
N^{1-2\alpha p}
. The computation details are very similar to the previous cases and are left to interested readers.

\blacksquare

5.3.
Clusters for mixed
p
-spin model with
h=0

In the pure
p
-spin case, one needs to identify the leading cluster structures on complete
p
-uniform hypergraphs, where all hyperedges are of
p
-tuples for fixed
p\geqslant 3
. In the mixed
p
-spin case, we need to deal with the general hypergraph where all possible
p
-hyperedges can appear. This additional flexibility over
p
creates a new transition with respect to
p
and further challenges in the analysis. As we discussed, although the results in Proposition
4.3
and
4.4
reduce the size of possible leading clusters to 4 or less, there still can be infinitely many different structures even for a fixed-size cluster. For example, in
h=0
case, consider the clusters of size
\left|\Gamma\right|=4
, the 4 hyperedges technically can be any
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}\geqslant 3
tuples with total degree
\sum_{i=1}^{4}p_{i}
even. To get over this issue, it basically needs a similar type of decaying result with respect to
p
as dealing with the external field in Proposition
4.3
and
4.4
.

We introduce the following strategy to deal with it. Notice that for a fixed finite size cluster, if there is any large
p
-hyperedge, one can always decompose it into several smaller hyperedges. For example, a
9
-hyperedge can be decomposed into a
4
-hyperedge and a
5
-hyperedge. In this way, the cluster size would increase. By the results in Proposition
4.4
, any clusters with a size greater than 4 are in subleading order. Thus we need to prove that any finite-size cluster with a large
p
-hyperedge must be in subleading order. The formal statement of this result is presented in Proposition
4.6
.

With the above result, it suffices to consider clusters that have finitely many hyperedges, and those hyperedges are finite
p
-tuples. Notice that the variance order of cluster structures has negative dependence on
p
in the exponent, thus it suffices to consider to minimum effective even and odd
p
-spins
p_{e},p_{o}
for identifying the leading clusters in the mixed setting. The rest of the analysis will be similar to the pure
p
-spin case, except that the cluster structures are now more complicated due to the extra variation on
p
. The analysis has to be very careful. We start analyzing the zero external field case in this part. Before stating the main results, we recall some necessary notations,

\displaystyle p_{e}=\min\{p\geqslant 3,p\ \text{even and}\ \theta_{p}\neq 0\},\ p_{o}=\min\{p\geqslant 3,p\ \text{odd and}\ \theta_{p}\neq 0\},\ \ p_{m}=\min\{p_{e},p_{o}\}.

The associated cluster structure for
\alpha=\infty
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\textup{mix},0}:=\{\text{the cluster structures in the last column of Table\penalty\ \ref{tab:transm} for $\alpha=\infty$}\}.

In the mixed setting, we use the 3-tuples
(a_{e},a_{o},b)
to characterize the cluster structure (check the notation in Section
1.5
). Note that
a_{e},a_{o},b
represent the number of
p_{e}
-hyperedges,
p_{o}
-hyperedges, and the number of odd-degree vertices in the associated cluster. The scaling exponent
\gamma_{1}
is

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}(p_{e}-2),&\text{ if }p_{e}<p_{o},\\
\frac{1}{2}p_{o}+\frac{1}{4}p_{e}-\frac{3}{2},&\text{ if }p_{o}<p_{e}<2p_{o},\\
(p_{o}-2),&\text{ if }p_{e}\geqslant 2p_{o}.\end{cases}

Here is the formal statement of the results. The proof strategy is similar to the pure
p
-spin case by instantiating the optimization framework.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
p_{m}\geqslant 3
be fixed and
h=0
. We have

(56)

\displaystyle N^{\gamma_{1}}\cdot\left(\hat{Z}_{N,4}-1-\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{mix},0}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)\right)\to 0

in probability as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

Following a similar strategy as before, first, by the definition of
\gamma_{1}
, it is easy to see that
\gamma_{1}\leqslant p_{m}-2
for
p_{m}\geqslant 3
. Thus by Proposition
4.4
, it suffices to consider cluster sizes of 3 and 4. By Proposition
4.6
, one needs to consider the clusters with
p
-hyperedges with finite
p
. Notice that the variance component has negative dependence on
p
, thus it suffices to focus on the clusters with
p_{e},p_{o}
-hyperedges. In particular, we consider the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{e}+a_{o}=3,4;\\
a_{e},a_{o}\geqslant 0.\end{subarray}}\mathcal{X}\left(((a_{e},p_{e}),(a_{o},p_{o}),0)\right)\text{ s.t. }\frac{a_{e}p_{e}+a_{o}p_{o}}{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.

Let us first explain the constrains above. The size
3,4
restriction is due to simple sub-hypergraphs condition with even degree vertices. The constraints
\frac{a_{e}p_{e}+a_{o}p_{o}}{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
is by a similar reason as before,
i.e.,
the possible dominant clusters enjoy the property that every vertex has degree 2, otherwise, it’s in subleading order. From this condition, it is easy to notice that
a_{o}
can not be odd.

We first consider
a_{o}=0
, then
a_{o}+a_{e}=3,4
implies
a_{e}=3,4
, a simple calculation of the variance order indicates that
a_{e}=3,a_{o}=0
is one possible dominant cluster, its variance is of order
N^{3p_{e}/2}\cdot N^{-3(p_{e}-1)}=N^{-3(p_{e}-2)/2}
. On the other hand, for
a_{o}=2
, similarly we get
a_{e}=1,2
, and clearly
a_{e}=1
dominants, thus
a_{e}=1,a_{o}=2
is another possible dominant cluster, the variance is
N^{(p_{e}+2p_{o})/2}\cdot N^{-(p_{e}-1)-2(p_{o}-1)}=N^{-p_{o}-p_{e}/2+3}
. The last possible dominant cluster is
a_{e}=0,a_{o}=4
, whose variance is of order
N^{2p_{o}}\cdot N^{-4(p_{o}-1)}=N^{-2(p_{o}-2)}
. For all those 3 possibilities, we identify the dominant one among various regimes depending on the relation of
p_{e},p_{o}
.

If
p_{e}<p_{o}
, it is clear that

\displaystyle\frac{-3(p_{e}-2)}{2}+p_{o}+\frac{p_{e}}{2}-3=-\frac{p_{e}}{2}+p_{o}>0,

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{-3(p_{e}-2)}{2}+2(p_{o}-2)=2p_{o}-\frac{3p_{e}}{2}-1\geqslant 2p_{e}+2-\frac{3p_{e}}{2}-1>0.

Therefore, the dominant cluster in this regime is the
(3,0,0)
cluster. Similarly one can identify the leading clusters in the other three cases:
p_{o}<p_{e}<2(p_{o}-1),p_{e}=2(p_{o}-1),p_{e}\geqslant 2p_{o}
. In the case
p_{o}<p_{e}<2(p_{o}-1)
, the dominant cluster is
(1,2,0)
. While for
p_{e}\geqslant 2p_{o}
, the dominant cluster is
(0,4,0)
. One can regard
p_{e}=2(p_{o}-1)
as the transition point with respect to
p
, where clusters
(1,2,0),(0,4,0)
coexist.

\blacksquare

5.4.
Clusters for mixed

The analysis in the

-spin model is already delicate, we now turn to a more challenging task, which is analyzing the transitional behavior by adding some weak external field. As indicated in Table

However, with the important observations in the pure

:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{mix}}(\alpha):=\{\text{the cluster structures in the last column of Table\penalty\ \ref{tab:transm} for a given $\alpha$.}\}

One can always check the cluster structures listed in Table

. Now we state the main results.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
p_{m}\geqslant 3
be fixed and
\alpha\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\infty)
. We have

(58)

\displaystyle N^{\gamma}\left(\hat{Z}_{N,4}-1-\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{mix}}(\alpha)}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}\hat{h}^{b}\hat{\omega}(\Gamma)\right)\to 0

in probability as
N\to\infty
, where
\alpha
depending on
\alpha,p_{e},p_{o}
is as given in the third column of Table

Proof.

One can easily verify the fact that
\gamma_{i}\leqslant p_{m}-2
for
i=1,2,3
, thus it suffices to focus on the clusters of size
a_{e}+a_{o}
no greater than 4. Due to the effect of external field, now the even degree restriction no longer holds. In particular, we have the following instance of the optimization problem to identify dominant clusters in different regimes.

\displaystyle\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{e}+a_{o}=1,2,3,4;\\
a_{e},a_{o}\geqslant 0,b\geqslant 0.\end{subarray}}\mathcal{X}\left(((a_{e},p_{e}),(a_{o},p_{o}),b)\right)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\frac{a_{e}p_{e}+a_{o}p_{o}+b}{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.

Let us first briefly explain the constraints. Notice the size of clusters is
\left|\Gamma\right|=a_{e}+a_{o}
,
i.e.,
the sum of the number of
p_{e}
-hyperedges and
p_{o}
-hyperedges. For the same reason as in the proof of Proposition
4.6
, we focus on the clusters with only
p_{e},p_{o}
hyperedges. Due to the possible presence of a weak external field, the size of the cluster can be
\{1,2,3,4\}
. On the other hand, the possible dominant clusters must have the property that every even-degree vertex has degree exactly 2, and odd-degree vertices must have degree 1. This was argued in the previous proofs. Therefore, it needs
\frac{a_{e}p_{e}+a_{o}p_{o}+b}{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. From this restriction, one can easily see that
b
, the number of odd-degree vertices, must be even or odd as
a_{o}
in the meantime.

Note that in general the variance order of
(a_{e},a_{o},b)
cluster is

(59)

\displaystyle N^{\frac{a_{e}p_{e}+a_{o}p_{o}+b}{2}}\cdot N^{-a_{e}(p_{e}-1)-a_{o}(p_{o}-1)-2b\alpha}=N^{-\frac{a_{e}(p_{e}-2)}{2}-\frac{a_{o}(p_{o}-2)}{2}-\frac{b(4\alpha-1)}{2}}.

Let us first analyze the regime
\mathcal{R}_{1}
. In particular, for
\alpha=\alpha_{c}(p_{e},p_{o})
, recall the definition of
\alpha_{c}
in (
27
),

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}=\begin{cases}p_{e}/8,&\text{ if }2<p_{e}<p_{o}-1,\\
(p_{e}-2)/4,&\text{ if }2<p_{e}=p_{o}-1,\\
p_{e}/8,&\text{ if }p_{o}<p_{e}<2(p_{o}-1),\\
(p_{o}-1)/4,&\text{ if }p_{e}\geqslant 2(p_{o}-1).\end{cases}

We drop the dependence on
p_{e},p_{o}
for convenience if not causing any confusion. If
p_{e}<p_{o}-1
, then
\alpha_{c}=p_{e}/8
. Plugging into (
59
), the variance order for general
(a_{e},a_{o},b)
cluster is
N^{-(\frac{a_{e}}{2}+\frac{b}{4})(p_{e}-2)-\frac{a_{o}}{2}(p_{o}-2)}
. Recall the observation
a_{o}
and
b
must be even or odd in the meantime. It’s easy to see that for fixed
b
, by the condition
p_{e}<p_{o}-1
, we know that the dominant cluster prefers
p_{e}
-hyperedges in order to maximize variance. This implies that the dominant cluster should have
a_{o}=0
. This reduces the problem to the pure
p_{e}
-spin model case, as we did, the dominant clusters at
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
should be
a_{e}=2,b=2
and
a_{e}=3,b=0
.

Now if
p_{e}=p_{o}-1
, in this case
\alpha_{c}=(p_{e}-2)/4
. The variance in (
59
) becomes
N^{-\frac{a_{e}}{2}(p_{e}-2)-\frac{a_{o}}{2}(p_{o}-2)-\frac{b}{2}(p_{e}-3)}
. Since
p_{e}<p_{o}
, clearly
a_{e}=3,a_{o}=0,b=0
is one dominant cluster. The variance order is
N^{-\frac{3(p_{e}-2)}{2}}
. One can also check that
a_{e}=1,a_{o}=1,b=1
is another cluster with variance order
N^{-\frac{3(p_{e}-2)}{2}}
. This implies that all clusters with
a_{e}+a_{o}>2,b>1
are in subleading order. To find other possible dominant clusters, it needs to decrease either
a_{e}+a_{o}
or
b
. However, by the structural property, we know
a_{e}=1,a_{o}=1,b=0
, and
a_{e}+a_{o}=1,b=1
can not happen.

If
p_{e}=p_{o}+1
, then
\alpha_{c}=p_{e}/8
. The variance order is
N^{-(\frac{a_{e}}{2}+\frac{b}{4})(p_{e}-2)-\frac{a_{o}}{2}(p_{o}-2)}
. Similarly we know one dominant cluster without odd-degree vertices (
b=0
) is
a_{o}=2,a_{e}=1,b=0
with variance order
N^{-p_{o}+3-p_{e}/2}
. It is easy to find that the variance order of the cluster
a_{e}=1,a_{o}=1,b=1
is also
N^{-p_{o}+3-p_{e}/2}
. Similarly as in previous case, we know that other possible dominant clusters are
a_{e}=1,a_{o}=1,b=0
and

, which can not appear due to the hypergraph structural restrictions.

If
p_{o}+1<p_{e}<2(p_{o}-1)

. This case is very similar to the last case. The zero odd vertices dominant cluster is still
a_{o}=2,a_{e}=1,b=0
. While the cluster

can not appear, since now the difference

cluster. The variance is indeed dominant one

.

If
p_{e}=2(p_{o}-1)
, then
\alpha_{c}=(p_{o}-1)/4
. In general the variance is of order
N^{-\frac{a_{e}}{2}(p_{e}-2)-\frac{a_{o}+b}{2}(p_{o}-2)}
. Clearly since

. The variance order is

. Note that any clusters of size 4 with odd-degree vertices are in subleading order. To find other possible dominant clusters, it needs to decrease the size of cluster. For size 3 cluster with no odd-degree vertices, the only choice is
a_{e}=1,a_{o}=2,b=0
. It can be seen that the variance is
N^{-p_{o}+3-p_{e}/2}=N^{-2(p_{o}-2)}

is another dominant cluster with the same variance order. Similarly one can find the last dominant cluster in this case

Finally for

Eventually, when

In a similar fashion, one can analyze the other regimes, such as

6.
CLT using Stein’s Method

After reducing
\hat{Z}_{N}
to contribution from finite sized clusters, we apply the multivariate Stein’s method to establish the joint central limit theorem. The computation details for using Stein’s method are presented below.

In this section, we analyze the case of pure
p
-spin glass models. Fix
p\geqslant 3
. The case
p=2
has already been analyzed in
[
27
]
. For simplicity, we suppress the dependence on
p,N
in the computations below, unless needed to avoid confusion.

Note that, we only have to consider contributions from clusters
\Gamma
, with
(\left|\Gamma\right|,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|)
in the set

(60)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{p}:=\begin{cases}\{(3,0),(2,2),(1,p)\}&\text{ if $p$ is even}\\
\{(4,0),(3,1),(2,2),(1,p)\}&\text{ if $p$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Before stating the main theorem, let us first fix some notations. Recall that

(61)

\displaystyle\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=N^{(p-1)/2}\tanh(\beta J_{\boldsymbol{i}}\cdot N^{-(p-1)/2})\approx\beta J_{\boldsymbol{i}},\qquad\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}

are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance

(62)

\displaystyle\beta_{N,p}^{2}=N^{(p-1)}\E\tanh^{2}(\beta J/N^{(p-1)/2})\approx\beta^{2}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\omega(\Gamma)=\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\Gamma}\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}

for a sub-hypergraph
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{E}_{N,p}
. Given two integers
k\geqslant 1,\ell\geqslant 0
, with
kp\equiv\ell\mod 2
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}:=\{\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{E}_{N,p}\mid\left|\Gamma\right|=k,\left|\partial\Gamma\right|=\ell\}

as the set of all sub-hypergraphs with
k
many hyperedges and
\ell
many odd-degree vertices. We also define

\displaystyle t_{k,\ell}:=(kp+\ell)/2\in\mathds{N},

so that
\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|=\Theta(N^{t_{k,\ell}})
. Note that, the maximum number of vertices present in a subgraph
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}
is
\ell+(kp-\ell)/2=(kp+\ell)/2
. We also define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}:=\{\Gamma\setminus\{\boldsymbol{i}\}\mid\boldsymbol{i}\in\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\}

as the set of sub-hypergraphs
\Pi
with
(k-1)
hyperedges such that adding the hyperedge
\boldsymbol{i}
to
\Pi
makes it an element in
\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}\right|=k\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|/N_{p}\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{i}.

Now, we consider the random variables

\displaystyle W_{k,\ell}:=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}}\omega(\Gamma).

Note that, we have
\E W_{k,\ell}=0
and

\displaystyle\nu_{k,\ell}^{2}:=\var(W_{k,\ell})=\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|\cdot\beta_{N,p}^{2k}.

Lemma 6.1

(Limiting variance)
.

For
k\geqslant 1,\ell\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle u_{k,\ell}(p)^{2}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|\cdot N^{-(kp+\ell)/2}=\frac{1}{k!\cdot\ell!\cdot p!^{k/2}}\E\left(H_{\ell}(\eta)\cdot H_{k}\left(H_{p}(\eta)/\sqrt{p!}\right)\right),

where
\eta\sim\N(0,1)
. In particular,

\displaystyle u_{3,0}(p)^{2}=\frac{1}{3!\cdot(p/2)!^{3}},\ u_{3,1}(p)^{2}=\frac{1}{(p+1)\cdot((p-1)/2)!^{3}},\ u_{2,2}(p)^{2}=\frac{p}{2\cdot p!},\ v_{1,p}(p)^{2}=\frac{1}{p!}.

Proof.

The proof relies on two key ingredients. First we rewrite
\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|
as an expectation of the product of spin variables. Recall
\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}
represents the number of cluster structures with
k
hyperedges and
\ell
odd-degree vertices. We have

(63)

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|=\frac{1}{k!\ell!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{i}_{1},\boldsymbol{i}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{i}_{k}\text{ distinct}\\
j_{1},\cdots,j_{\ell}\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\E_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{1}}\cdots\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{k}}\tau_{j_{1}}\cdots\tau_{j_{\ell}}\right),

where for each
r=1,2,\cdots,k
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{r}}:=\prod_{s=1}^{p}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{i}_{r}(s)}
for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{r}}\in\{-1,+1\}^{p}
, and the expectation
\E_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is with respect to all
(\sigma_{i},\tau_{i},1\leqslant i\leqslant N)
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed over
\{+1,-1\}
. It is clear that whenever there is a parity between the sites of
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{1}},\cdots\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{k}}
and all the rest of sites in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
appear even number of times, the contribution in the expectation will be 1, otherwise, the contribution will be zero. The next key step is to connect the r.h.s. with the Hermite polynomial. We note the following fact:

\displaystyle\frac{p!}{N^{p/2}}\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots j_{p}\leqslant N}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{p}}

(64)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{N^{p/2}}B_{p}(-\sqrt{N}\tilde{x},-N,-2!\sqrt{N}\tilde{x},\cdots,-(p-1)!N\cdot(N\tilde{x})^{\frac{1}{2}1_{p\ \text{odd}}})

where
B_{p}(\cdot)
is the
p
-th complete exponential Bell polynomial
[
5
]
, and
\tilde{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}
. As
N\to\infty
, the r.h.s. tends to
B_{p}(x,-1,0,0,\cdots,0)=H_{p}(x)
, where
H_{p}(x)
is Hermite polynomial with
x:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\tilde{x}
. Using this fact, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|\cdot N^{-(kp+\ell)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{(kp+\ell)/2}}\E_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{i}_{1},\boldsymbol{i}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{i}_{k},\\
1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots<j_{\ell}\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{1}}\cdots\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{k}}\tau_{j_{1}}\cdots\tau_{j_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!p!^{k/2}}\left(\frac{-1}{N^{p/2}}\right)^{k}B_{k}(-N^{p/2}\sqrt{p!}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{1}},\cdots)\cdot\frac{1}{\ell!}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{N^{\ell/2}}B_{\ell}(-\sqrt{N}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\cdots)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=\frac{1}{N^{p/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=N^{-\ell/2}\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1}<\cdots j_{\ell}\leqslant N}\tau_{j_{1}}\cdots\tau_{j_{\ell}}
. Following the last step, one can also use the fact in (
6
) to rewrite
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
in terms of
p
-th Bell polynomial, then let
N\to\infty
, by Central limit theorem, we have the r.h.s. converges to

\displaystyle\E_{\eta}\frac{1}{k!\cdot\ell!\cdot p!^{k/2}}\left(H_{k}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p!}}H_{p}(\eta)\right)\cdot H_{\ell}(\eta)\right)\quad\text{where}\ \eta\sim\N(0,1).

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

We also define

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{k,\ell}:=W_{k,\ell}/\nu_{k,\ell}

as the cantered and scaled version of
W_{k,\ell}
with mean zero and variance one. We define the random vectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}:=(W_{k,\ell})_{(k,\ell)\in\mathscr{S}_{p}}\text{ and }\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}:=(\widehat{W}_{k,\ell})_{(k,\ell)\in\mathscr{S}_{p}}.

Note that, for
p
even there are three co-ordinates and for
p
odd there are four co-ordinates in
\boldsymbol{W}
. It is easy to check that the variance-covariance matrix of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}
is the identity matrix. We will work with
\widehat{W_{k\ell}}
for simplicity and scale them appropriately at the end. Here is the formal statement of using multivariate Stein’s method to prove the normal limit.

Theorem 6.2
.

For the
\left|\mathscr{S}_{p}\right|
-dimensional random vector
\boldsymbol{W}=(W_{k,\ell})_{(k,\ell)\in\mathscr{S}_{p}}
with mean and variance structure
\Sigma
specified above in the Lemma
6.1
. For any thrice differentiable function
f
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\E f(\boldsymbol{W})-\E f(\Sigma^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z})\right|\leqslant C\left(\left|f\right|_{2}N^{-p}+\left|f\right|_{3}N^{-p/2}\right),

where
C
is some constant depending only on
\beta
and the distribution of disorder
J_{12}
.

Now we apply Theorem
1.16
to prove Theorem
6.2
. To do that, we will use the following Glauber dynamics. First we select a
p
-hyperedge
{\boldsymbol{I}}
from
\mathscr{E}_{N,p}
uniformly at random, then replace
\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}
by an i.i.d. copy
\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{\prime}
. We denote the corresponding new random variables
W_{k,\ell},\boldsymbol{W}
as
W^{\prime}_{k,\ell},\boldsymbol{W}^{\prime}
, respectively. Define,
\Delta W_{k,\ell}:=W_{k,\ell}^{\prime}-W_{k,\ell}
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{W}:=\boldsymbol{W}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{W}
.

First we check the linearity condition. Define

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,\ell}:=k\cdot N_{p}^{-1},\quad\text{ where }N_{p}:=\binom{N}{p}.

Lemma 6.3

(Linearity of conditional expectation)
.

We have

\displaystyle\E\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{W}\mid\boldsymbol{W}\right)=-\Lambda\boldsymbol{W}

where
\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_{k,\ell},(k,\ell)\in\mathscr{S}_{p})
.

Proof.

It is enough to check the linearity condition for any
(k,\ell)\in\mathscr{S}_{p}
. Using the fact that
\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{\prime}
has mean zero, we have

\displaystyle\E(\Delta W_{k,\ell}\mid\boldsymbol{W})

\displaystyle=\E\left((\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{\prime}-\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}})\cdot\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{I}}}\omega(\Pi)\ \Biggr|\ \boldsymbol{W}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}({\boldsymbol{i}})}\omega(\Pi)=-\frac{k}{N_{p}}W_{k,\ell}=-\lambda_{k,\ell}W_{k,\ell}.

Here, we use the fact that any subgraph
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{kl}
will appear exactly
k
times in the sum.

\blacksquare

Thus, to use Theorem
1.16
we need to upper bound the variance of the conditional moment and the absolute third moment of
\Delta\boldsymbol{W}
.
First, we proceed towards analyzing the variance of the conditional second moment of
\Delta W_{k,\ell}
. We observe that

\displaystyle\E\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{2}=2\lambda_{k,\ell}\cdot\nu_{k,\ell}^{2}=2k\cdot N_{p}^{-1}\cdot\beta_{N,p}^{2k}\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|.

Define, the i.i.d. random variables

(65)

\displaystyle\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}.

Note that,

\displaystyle\E(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}})=\E(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}})=0.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\left|\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{\prime}\right|^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{2}-2\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{\prime}+\xi_{\boldsymbol{I}}+2\beta_{N,p}^{2})\cdot\left(\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{I}}}\omega(\Pi)\right)^{2}.

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle N_{p}\cdot\left(\E\left(\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{2}\ \biggr|\ (\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}})_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\right)-\E\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}+2\beta_{N,p}^{2})\cdot\left(\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\omega(\Pi)\right)^{2}-2k\beta_{N,p}^{2k}\left|\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}\right|

\displaystyle=A_{k,\ell}+2\beta_{N,p}^{2}\cdot B_{k,\ell}+2C_{k,\ell}+4\beta_{N,p}^{2}\cdot D_{k,\ell}

where

(66)

\displaystyle A_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\omega(\Pi)^{2},

\displaystyle B_{k,\ell}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}(\omega(\Pi)^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{2(k-1)}),

\displaystyle C_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\neq\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\omega(\Pi_{1})\omega(\Pi_{2}),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle D_{k,\ell}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\neq\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\omega(\Pi_{1})\omega(\Pi_{2}).

In particular, we need to upper-bound each of the terms

\left\|A_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2},\left\|B_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2},\left\|C_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2},\left\|D_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2}.

We will analyze the different clusters for even and odd
p
separately as the arguments are quite subtle.

Lemma 6.4

(Variance of conditional second moment control)
.

Let
p\geqslant 3
be fixed. For
(k,\ell)\in\mathscr{S}_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|A_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|B_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|C_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|D_{k,\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant cN^{-p}\cdot\nu_{k,\ell}^{4}

for some constant
c_{k,\ell,p}
depending only on
k,\ell,p,\left\|J\right\|_{4}
.

We also need to control variance of conditional mixed moments. We proceed in a similar way. For
(k,\ell),(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\in\mathscr{S}_{p}
we have

\displaystyle\E\left(\Delta W_{k,\ell}\cdot\Delta W_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\right)=0.

Thus,

\displaystyle N_{p}\cdot\left(\E\left(\Delta W_{k,\ell}\cdot\Delta W_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\ \biggr|\ (\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}})_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\right)-\E\left(\Delta W_{k,\ell}\cdot\Delta W_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}+2\beta_{N,p}^{2})\cdot\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}}\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})=C_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}+2\beta_{N,p}^{2}\cdot D_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}

where

(67)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}&:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}}\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})\text{ and }\\
D_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}&:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}}\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Pi^{\prime}).\end{split}

We have the following result.

Lemma 6.5

(Variance of conditional mixed moment control)
.

Let
p\geqslant 3
be fixed. For
(k,\ell),(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\in\mathscr{S}_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|C_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|D_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant cN^{-p}\cdot\nu_{k,\ell}^{2}\nu_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{2}

for some constant
c
depending only on
k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},p,\left\|J\right\|_{4}
.

Now, we bound the absolute third moment. Using the bound
|xyz|\leqslant(|x|^{3}+|y|^{3}+|z|^{3})/3
, it is enough to control the absolute third moment for each cluster separately.

Lemma 6.6

(Upper bound for absolute third moment)
.

We have

\displaystyle N_{p}\cdot\E\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{3}=N^{-p/2}\cdot\beta^{3k}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{3k}\cdot\nu_{k,\ell}^{3}\cdot O(1).

Proof.

Recall that,

\displaystyle\Delta W_{k,\ell}=(\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{\prime}-\omega_{\boldsymbol{I}})\cdot\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{I}}}\omega(\Pi).

Moreover, the hyperedge
\boldsymbol{i}
does not appear in any subgraph in
\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\E\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{3}\leqslant 8N_{p}^{-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathscr{E}_{N,p}}\left(\E|\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}|^{4}\cdot\E\left|\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\omega(\Pi)\right|^{4}\right)^{3/4}.

We have

\displaystyle\E|\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}|^{4}\leqslant\beta^{4}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}

and

\displaystyle\E\left|\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\omega(\Pi)\right|^{4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{4}\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}}\quad\E\prod_{i=1}^{4}\omega(\Pi_{i}).

Note that, the expectation
\E\prod_{i=1}^{4}\omega(\Pi_{i})
is zero if any of the edges is present only once in
\cup_{i=1}^{4}\Pi_{i}
; otherwise it is bounded by
\E\left|\omega(\Pi)\right|^{4}\leqslant\beta^{4(k-1)}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4(k-1)}
as each
\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}}
has
(k-1)
hyperedges and each hyperedge has
p
many vertices. Moreover,

\displaystyle\left|\{(\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\Pi_{3},\Pi_{4})\in(\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}})^{4}\mid\text{each edge appears at least twice in }\cup_{i=1}^{4}\Pi_{i}\}\right|

\displaystyle=O(N^{2\cdot((kp+\ell)/2-p)})=N^{-2p}\cdot O(\nu_{k,\ell}^{4})

as each element in
\mathscr{S}_{k,\ell}
can have at most
(kp+\ell)/2-p
many vertices and each vertex must repeat at least twice in the union.
Thus, we finally have

\displaystyle\E\left|\Delta W_{k,\ell}\right|^{3}=N^{-3p/2}\cdot\beta^{3k}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{3k}\cdot\nu_{k,\ell}^{3}\cdot O(1).

Using
N_{p}\leqslant N^{p}/p!
, we now complete the proof.

\blacksquare

6.1.
Control for
(3,0)
clusters with even
p

Note that
\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=\omega_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{2}
has mean zero and variance upper bounded by
\beta^{4}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}
. For the term
A_{3,0}
, we have

(68)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\cdot\omega(\Pi)^{2}\omega(\Pi^{\prime})^{2}\right).

Due to the property
\E\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}=0
, there must be at least two copies of the hyperedges
\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
. We compute the order of contributions in each different cases.

•

If
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=p
, then
\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
are the same hyperedge. The remaining
p/2
vertices in each of
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}
are arbitrary. Thus, the contribution is upper bounded by
\beta_{N,p}^{12}\cdot O(N^{p}\cdot N^{2\cdot p/2})=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4})
as
\nu_{3,0}^{2}=\Theta(N^{3p/2})
.

•

The second possibility is
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=p/2
, where
\boldsymbol{i}
must also appear in
\Pi^{\prime}
, and
\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
must appear in
\Pi
. Thus, all the vertices are determined by
\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
. The contribution is upper bounded by
\beta_{N,p}^{12}\cdot O(N^{3p/2})=O(N^{-3p/2}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4})
.

•

The last case is
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=0
. But this is impossible, since we still need
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}
, at the same time, we also need
\Pi\cup\boldsymbol{i}
to be 3-loop cluster. This contradicts the assumption that
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=0
.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\left\|A_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4}).

Next we analyze the second term
B_{3,0}
,

\displaystyle\left\|B_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\ \Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left((\omega(\Pi)^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{4})\cdot(\omega(\Pi^{\prime})^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{4})\right).

The expectation inside is zero if
\Pi\cap\Pi^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Moreover,
each summand can be bounded by
\beta_{N,p}^{8}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{8}
. Using the fact that the number of pairs of 3-loops with non-empty intersection can at most be
N^{2p}
, we get the order of

\displaystyle\left\|B_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4}).

For
C_{3,0}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|C_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\neq\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\ \Pi^{\prime}\neq\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\cdot\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Gamma)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})\omega(\Gamma^{\prime})\right).

Compared to the analysis in the case of
A_{3,0}
, now there is only one case left
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=p
which makes nonzero contributions. Let us argue why the case
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=p/2
is impossible. It needs
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
, as each hyperedge must appear at least twice. Combining with the fact that
\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\Pi^{\prime}\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\Pi^{\prime}\cup\{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\},\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\}
are all 3-loops, this implies that
\Pi^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}
, a contradiction with the
\Pi^{\prime}\neq\Gamma^{\prime}
assumption. Therefore, the only possible case is
\boldsymbol{i}=\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
, whose contribution gives the bound

\left\|C_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\beta_{N,p}^{12}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}\cdot O(N^{2p})=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4}).

Finally, we analyze the term
D_{3,0}
,

\displaystyle\left\|D_{3,0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\neq\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\ \Pi^{\prime}\neq\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{3,0}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left(\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Gamma)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})\omega(\Gamma^{\prime})\right).

This case is even more subtle. Compared to the analysis for
C_{3,0}
, now for
\boldsymbol{i}\neq\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
we do not need the condition that
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\Pi,\Gamma
. We claim that the only possibilities are the following two cases:

•

Case
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=0
: This case is delicate. Note that
\Pi,\Gamma
share
p
vertices and in total
\Pi\cup\Gamma
can have at most
2p
vertices. Same holds for
\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
. Now, each edge must appear at least twice in the union of
\Pi,\Gamma,\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
. Thus, at most
2p
vertices, those present in
\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
, will be involved in the union. The contribution in this case is of order
\beta_{N,p}^{8}\cdot O(N^{2p})=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4})
.

•

Case
\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=p
: This case is similar to
C_{3,0}
and the contribution is of order
\beta_{N,p}^{8}\cdot O(N^{2p})=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{3,0}^{4})
.

6.2.
Control for
(2,2)
clusters with general
p

We have

(69)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\cdot\omega(\Pi)^{2}\omega(\Pi^{\prime})^{2}\right).

Since
\E\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}=0
, if
\boldsymbol{i}\neq\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
, we must have
\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\Pi,\boldsymbol{i}\in\Pi^{\prime}
. But now
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}
are single hyperedges. Thus we must have
\Pi=\{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\},\Pi^{\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{i}\}
. Due to the condition that
\{\boldsymbol{i}\}\cup\Pi,\{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\}\cup\Pi^{\prime}
all form
(2,2)
cluster, we know there are total
N^{p+1}
different structures. The contribution of each structure is of order
\beta_{N,p}^{8}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}
, thus in total the contribution is
\beta_{N,p}^{8}\cdot O(N^{p+1})=O(N^{-p-1}\cdot\nu_{2,2}^{4})
as
\nu_{2,2}^{2}=\Theta(N^{p+1})
.

On the other hand, if
\boldsymbol{i}=\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
, then we do not have to put further restrictions on
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}
. Since
\{\boldsymbol{i}\}\cup\Pi,\{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\}\cup\Pi^{\prime}
form
(2,2)
cluster, it is clear that there are
N^{p+2}
different ways, and the total contribution is of order
\beta_{N,p}^{8}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}\cdot O(N^{p+2})=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{2,2}^{4})
. Combining, we get

\displaystyle\left\|A_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{2,2}^{4}).

Next we analyze the second term
B_{2,2}
,

\displaystyle\left\|B_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\,\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left((\omega(\Pi)^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{4})\cdot(\omega(\Pi^{\prime})^{2}-\beta_{N,p}^{4})\right).

As we did in the case of
B_{3,0}
, the order of the summand can be bounded by a constant. Note that, we must have
\Pi=\Pi^{\prime}
; otherwise by the independence property of different edges, the contribution is zero. There are at most
N^{p+2}
different ways and the order is

\displaystyle\left\|B_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{2,2}^{4}).

For
C_{2,2}
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|C_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\neq\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\ \Pi^{\prime}\neq\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\cdot\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Gamma)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})\omega(\Gamma^{\prime})\right).

Compared to the analysis in the case of
A_{3,0}
, now there is only one case left
\boldsymbol{i}=\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
that can make nonzero contributions. Let us argue why the case
\boldsymbol{i}\neq\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
is now impossible. It needs
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
, but now
\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
are both different single hyperedges. The only possible case is
\boldsymbol{i}=\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
. In this case, one needs to pair the hyperedges
\Pi,\Gamma
with
\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
such that each hyperedge appear twice for avoiding zero contribution. Thus the contribution is of order

\displaystyle\left\|C_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\beta_{N,p}^{8}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}\cdot O(N^{p+2})=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{2,2}^{4}).

Finally, we analyze the term
D_{2,2}
,

\displaystyle\left\|D_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\neq\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\ \Pi^{\prime}\neq\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{2,2}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\E\left(\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Gamma)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})\omega(\Gamma^{\prime})\right).

Compared to the case of
C_{2,2}
, this time we do not need
\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}
to appear twice. The set of vertices in
\boldsymbol{i}
is a subset of the set of vertices in
\Pi\cup\Gamma
. Thus there are at most
N^{p+1}
different ways, where two different hyper-edges appear exactly twice and they build a
(2,2)
cluster. The total contribution is of order

\displaystyle\left\|D_{2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(N^{p+1}\beta_{N,p}^{4})=O(N^{-p-1}\cdot\nu_{2,2}^{4}).

Control for
(1,p)
clusters with general
p

This is the case of single hyperedge clusters. Moreover, the terms
B_{1,p},C_{1,p},D_{1,p}
are zero. For term
A_{1,p}
, we have

(70)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{1,p}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\E\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\leqslant N_{p}\beta_{N,p}^{4}\left\|J\right\|_{4}^{4}=O(N^{-p}\cdot\nu_{1,p}^{4}).

6.4.
Control of mixed clusters product moments for even
p

Note that we have

\displaystyle\left\|C_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{k,\ell}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}},\Pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{-\boldsymbol{i}},\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{-\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}}\E\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}\cdot\omega(\Pi)\omega(\Gamma)\omega(\Pi^{\prime})\omega(\Gamma^{\prime})\right)\text{ and }

Thus, we bound the contribution for each mixed product moment.

6.4.1.
Clusters

\left\|C_{3,0,2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. When
\boldsymbol{i}\neq\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}

\left|\boldsymbol{i}\cap\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right|=p

\left\|D_{3,0,2,2}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, due to different cluster structures of

and
\Pi^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime}
, it is easy to see that the nonzero contribution is still from the case

Clusters

appear twice. This can only happen when

. Therefore, the contribution is of the order

.

\left\|D_{3,0,1,p}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, for the same reason as in

.

Clusters
(2,2),(1,p)
analysis

, one must have the hyperedges in

appear twice. This can only happen when

. Therefore, the contribution is of order

.

6.5.
Control for
(4,0)
clusters with odd
p

The analysis of the odd
p
,
(4,0)
cluster case is much more delicate than the even
p
,
(3,0)
case. The major difficulty arises in the cluster
(4,0)
, as there can be many different ways to share vertices for the given 4 different hyperedges. This is in sharp contrast to the even
p
case, where different hyperedges can only share
p/2
vertices between two hyperedges. This can be seen in some very simple cases, for example for
p=5
, shown in Figure
2
. In general, we introduce the following equations, whose solutions characterize this sharing mechanism uniquely.

(71)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{p}=\{(x,y,z)\mid x+y+z=p,x\geqslant y\geqslant z\geqslant 0,y>0\}.

Note that in the above equation (
71
), only
z
can be 0. Essentially the numbers
x,y,z
correspond to how many vertices a hyperedge can share with the remaining three hyperedges. It is easy to check that there exists a bijective map between the solutions in

Recall the definition of hypergraph clusters with structure

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{a_{e},a_{o},b}=\{\Gamma\subseteq\cup_{p\geqslant 3}\mathscr{E}_{N,p}|\left|\Gamma\right|_{p_{e}}=a_{e},\left|\Gamma\right|_{p_{o}}=a_{o},\left|\partial\Gamma\right|=b\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{a_{e},a_{o},b}:=W_{a_{e},a_{o},b}/\nu_{a_{e},a_{o},b}.

In general, we have the random vector and its rescaled version,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}:=(W_{a_{e},a_{o},b})_{(a_{e},a_{o},b)\in\mathscr{S}_{\text{mix}}}\ \text{and}\ \widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}:=(\widehat{W}_{a_{e},a_{o},b})_{(a_{e},a_{o},b)\in\mathscr{S}_{\text{mix}}}.

9.
Open Questions

9.1.
Cluster expansion beyond the large hypergraph decaying regime

Currently, this cluster expansion framework is restricted to the high temperature and weak external field regimes. The main reason is that the large hypergraph decaying property is only true in this regime. Otherwise, one has to deal with very large cluster structures. Counting those clusters and applying Stein’s method to prove CLT does not work anymore. Therefore, extending this framework beyond the large graph decaying regime is of great interest. This relates to many other important open questions in mean field spin glasses, such as the Almeda-Thouless conjecture and the Gumble fluctuation of free energy at low temperatures. We further remark that analytical methods developed in
[
27
]
should give the fluctuation results in high temperature and strong external field (
\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
), however, the disorder has to be Gaussian and the results can possibly be proved up to some higher temperature than
\beta_{f}
.

9.2.
Classification of spin glass models

The classification of spin glass models was addressed in
[
7
]
, where the authors asked whether 1-step replica symmetry breaking (RSB) mixed
p
-spin spherical models are pure-like. They studied the problem using a complexity-based approach. In
[
8
]
, Auffinger and Zeng constructed some examples which are both 2-step RSB but one is pure-like, and the other one is a full mixture. The definitions of “pure-like” and “full-mixture” are based on the complexity of the energy function. In this article, our results on the mixed Ising
p
-spin case can also be treated as a classification of the mixed
p
-spin models in terms of cluster structures. For simplicity, consider the
h=0
case; we proved that if
p_{e}<p_{o}
, the mixed model behaves essentially like the pure
p_{e}
-spin model, while in the case
p_{e}\geqslant 2p_{o}
, the mixed model is more like pure
p_{o}
-spin model. Only in the case
p_{o}<p_{e}<2p_{o}
the mixed
p
-spin model behaves really like a mixture. The question is whether this is a solid criterion to classify general mixed
p
-spin models. Indeed, this question is asked in the low-temperature regime, and our results answer the high-temperature case. Besides that, it is also observed that if the external field is very strong, then it seems that the system does not have a mixture regime. Specifically, if
p_{e}<p_{o}
, the model is like a pure
p_{e}
-spin case. If
p_{e}>p_{o}
, it is more like a pure
p_{o}
-spin model. It is also interesting to investigate classification under the effects of an external field.

9.3.
Cluster expansion in vector spin case

Although extending the technique beyond the large graph decaying regime is fundamentally challenging, it is still hopeful to use this technique to obtain new results in other more complicated but not well-understood spin glass models. For example, in the vector spin glass model, the spins at each site take vector values and interact via inner products. The phase transition and fluctuation results are both not rigorously well-understood. There are a few results
[
48
,
47
]
concerning the limiting free energy using the Parisi formula. For example, Potts spin glass model, as a particular case of the vector spin model, has been studied a lot in physics community
[
31
,
25
,
29
,
30
,
17
,
36
,
37
,
42
]
, where some more complicated phase transition is expected even in the high-temperature regime. However, mathematically rigorous proof for this has long been elusive. As indicated in this article, cluster expansion can be used to establish fluctuation results and also detect the sharp phase transition threshold. It would also be interesting to extend this technique to those models; this is currently under investigation by the authors.

9.4.
Beyond the distributional limit of free energy

For simplicity, consider the zero external field case; the results in this article give a way to decompose the partition function as several simpler components. Recall the definition of the Gibbs measure

\displaystyle dG_{N,\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\exp(\beta H_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))\cdot Z_{N}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

One can use the identity
\exp(\sigma x)=\cosh(x)+\sigma\sinh(x)
to rewrite the Gibbs average as a mean with respect to the measure defined on the space of “loops”. The question is whether this can be used to study other aspects of the spin glass system. For example, the overlap as an order parameter carries much information about the model. It is essential to study the limiting behavior of the overlap. More generally, the question is whether cluster expansion can be used to study questions beyond the distributional behavior of the free energy. We further remark that the above questions in the low-temperature regime would be more interesting due to the replica symmetry-breaking phase.

9.5.
Understanding the threshold
\beta_{f}

The form of
\beta_{f}
comes from an optimal second-moment control of the partition function in Lemma
4.1
. As the numerical simulation in Figure
1
suggested,
\beta_{f}
is not the usual static phase transition point. It is not clear whether this is due to technical difficulties or there is some unknown physical phenomenon that breaks down at
\beta_{f}
. Since it possesses such a simple and explicit form, it will be very interesting to investigate the physical nature of
\beta_{f}
. On the other hand, as shown in Figure
1
,
\beta_{f}\to\sqrt{2\ln 2}
as
p\to\infty
, which is located between the two thresholds
\sqrt{\ln 2}
,
2\sqrt{\ln 2}
discovered in
[
15
]
for REM. A first step towards understanding
\beta_{f}
would be investigating the differences for REM in regimes
\beta\in(\sqrt{\ln 2},\sqrt{2\ln 2})
and
\beta\in(\sqrt{2\ln 2},2\sqrt{\ln 2})
.
