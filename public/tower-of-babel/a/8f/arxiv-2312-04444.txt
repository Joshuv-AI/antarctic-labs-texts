Parameter Inference for Hypo-Elliptic Diffusions under a Weak Design Condition
Yuga Iguchi, Alexandros Beskos

Abstract
We address the problem of parameter estimation for degenerate diffusion processes defined via the solution of Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) with diffusion matrix that is not full-rank. For this class of hypo-elliptic SDEs recent works have proposed contrast estimators that are asymptotically normal, provided that the step-size in-between observations Δ = Δ n \displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{n} and their total number n \displaystyle n satisfy n → ∞ \displaystyle n\to\infty , n ​ Δ n → ∞ \displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty , Δ n → 0 \displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0 , and additionally Δ n = o ( n − 1 / 2 ) \displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2}) . This latter restriction places a requirement for a so-called ‘rapidly increasing experimental design’. In this paper, we relax this limitation and develop a general contrast estimator satisfying asymptotic normality under the weaker condition Δ n = o ( n − 1 / p ) \displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p}) for general p ≥ 2 \displaystyle p\geq 2 . Such a result has been obtained for elliptic SDEs, but its derivation in a hypo-elliptic setting is highly non-trivial and requires a separate approach. We provide numerical results to illustrate the advantages of the developed theory. Keywords: Stochastic Differential Equation; hypo-elliptic diffusion; high-frequency observations; experimental design of observations.

Introduction

We consider the problem of parameter inference for multivariate Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) determined via a degenerate diffusion matrix. Let
\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space and let
\displaystyle W=(W_{t}^{1},\ldots,W_{t}^{d})
,
\displaystyle t\geq 0
, be the
\displaystyle d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion,
\displaystyle d\geq 1
, defined on such a space. We introduce
\displaystyle N
-dimensional SDE models,
\displaystyle N\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle dX_{t}=\mu(X_{t},\theta)dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}(X_{t},\theta)dW_{t}^{j},\qquad X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(1.1)

with
\displaystyle\mu,A_{j}(\cdot,\theta):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq d
, for parameter
\displaystyle\theta
. This work focuses on the case where the diffusion matrix
\displaystyle A=[A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}]
is not of full-rank, i.e. the matrix
\displaystyle a=AA^{\top}
is not positive definite. The law of the solution to (
1.1
) is assumed to be absolutely continuous with respect to (w.r.t.) the Lebesgue measure. Such models are referred to as
hypo-elliptic diffusions
and constitute an important class of Markovian processes used in a wide range of applications. E.g., this class includes the (underdamped) Langevin equation, the Synaptic-Conductance model
(
Ditlevsen and Samson 2019
)
,
the
\displaystyle\mathrm{NLCAR}(p)
model
(
Tsai and Chan 2000
;
Stramer and Roberts 2007
)
, the Jansen-Rit neural mass model
(
Buckwar et al. 2020
)
, the SIR epidemiological model
(
Spannaus et al. 2022
)
and the quasi-Markovian generalised Langevin equations (
\displaystyle q
-GLE)
(
Vroylandt et al. 2022
)
. We investigate two (sub-)classes of hypo-elliptic SDEs which cover a wide range of models, including the ones mentioned in the above examples. The first model class is specified via the following SDE:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dX_{t}&=\begin{bmatrix}dX_{S,t}\\
dX_{R,t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{S}(X_{t},\beta_{S})\\
\mu_{R}(X_{t},\beta_{R})\end{bmatrix}dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{N_{S}}\\
A_{R,j}(X_{t},\sigma)\end{bmatrix}dW_{t}^{j},\\
X_{0}&=x\equiv\begin{bmatrix}x_{S}\\
x_{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{aligned}

(Hypo-I)

The involved drift functions and diffusion coefficients are specified as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta_{\beta_{S}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{S}},\quad\mu_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta_{\beta_{R}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{R}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{R,j}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta_{\sigma}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{R}},\quad 1\leq j\leq d,

for positive integers
\displaystyle N_{S},N_{R}
such that
\displaystyle N=N_{S}+N_{R}
.
Here, the unknown parameter vector is

\theta=(\beta_{S},\beta_{R},\sigma)\in\Theta=\Theta_{\beta_{S}}\times\Theta_{\beta_{R}}\times\Theta_{\sigma}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\beta_{S}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{\beta_{R}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{\sigma}},

for positive integers
\displaystyle N_{\beta_{S}},\,N_{\beta_{R}},\,N_{\sigma}
satisfying
\displaystyle N_{\beta_{S}}+N_{\beta_{R}}+N_{\sigma}=N_{\theta}
. The parameter space
\displaystyle\Theta
is assumed to be a compact subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
.
Later on we place a condition on (
Hypo-I
),
related to the so-called
Hörmander’s condition
,
so that such an SDE gives rise to a hypo-elliptic process, i.e. its finite-time transition distributions admit a Lebesgue density.
In brief, the condition will guarantee that randomness from the rough component
\displaystyle X_{R,t}
propagates into the smooth component
\displaystyle X_{S,t}
via the drift
\displaystyle\mu_{S}
. The second hypo-elliptic model class we treat in this work is specified via the following SDE:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hskip-5.69046ptdX_{t}&=\begin{bmatrix}dX_{S_{1},t}\\
dX_{S_{2},t}\\
dX_{R,t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{S_{1}}(X_{S,t},\beta_{S_{1}})\\
\mu_{S_{2}}(X_{t},\beta_{S_{2}})\\
\mu_{R}(X_{t},\beta_{R})\end{bmatrix}dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{N_{S_{1}}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{N_{S_{2}}}\\
A_{R,j}(X_{t},\sigma)\end{bmatrix}dW_{t}^{j},\\
X_{0}&=x\equiv\begin{bmatrix}x_{S_{1}}\\
x_{S_{2}}\\
x_{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{aligned}

(Hypo-II)

where we have set
\displaystyle X_{S,t}=\bigl[X_{S_{1},t}^{\top},X_{S_{2},t}^{\top}\bigr]^{\top}
.
Also, we now have the parameter vector:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\theta=(\beta_{S_{1}},\beta_{S_{2}},\beta_{R},\sigma)\in\Theta

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Theta_{\beta_{S_{1}}}\times\Theta_{\beta_{S_{2}}}\times\Theta_{\beta_{R}}\times\Theta_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\beta_{S_{1}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{\beta_{S_{2}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{\beta_{R}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{\sigma}},

with integers
\displaystyle N_{\beta_{S_{1}}},N_{\beta_{S_{2}}}
such that
\displaystyle N_{\beta_{S}}=N_{\beta_{S_{1}}}+N_{\beta_{S_{2}}}
, and the drift functions are now specified as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{S_{1}}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{S}}\times\Theta_{N_{\beta_{S_{1}}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{S_{1}}},\quad\mu_{S_{2}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta_{N_{\beta_{S_{2}}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{S_{2}}},

with integers
\displaystyle N_{S_{1}},N_{S_{2}}\geq 1
so that
\displaystyle N_{S_{1}}+N_{S_{2}}=N_{S}
. Again,
\displaystyle\Theta
is assumed to be compact. Note that the drift
\displaystyle\mu_{S_{1}}
depends on the smooth component

\displaystyle X_{S,t}
and not on
\displaystyle X_{R,t}
, thus randomness from
\displaystyle X_{R,t}
does not directly propagate onto
\displaystyle X_{S_{1},t}
.
We refer to an SDE of the form (
Hypo-II
) as a
highly degenerate diffusion
.
Throughout the paper the class (
Hypo-II
) is treated separately from (
Hypo-I
). Later on we introduce a Hörmander-type condition guaranteeing that (
Hypo-II
) gives rise to hypo-elliptic SDEs.

We consider parameter estimation for the classes (
Hypo-I
), (
Hypo-II
), given discrete-time observations of the full vector
\displaystyle X_{t}

at instances
\displaystyle t_{i}=i\Delta_{n}
,
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
,
for step-size
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}>0
. We develop contrast estimators for models in (
Hypo-I
), (
Hypo-II
),
and study their asymptotic properties under a

high-frequency complete observation
scenario. In particular, we assume the setting
\displaystyle n\to\infty
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0
,
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty
.
Over the last two-three decades, numerous works have studied parametric inference for diffusion processes in a high-frequency setting.
These works were initially focused on
elliptic
diffusions, i.e. SDEs in (
1.1
) with a positive definite matrix
\displaystyle a=AA^{\top}
.
The work in
Kessler 1997
proposed a contrast estimator for scalar elliptic diffusions and proved asymptotic normality (via a CLT) under the design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
.
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
extended such a result to multivariate elliptic SDEs and proposed an adaptive-type contrast estimator achieving a CLT under the same design condition as in
Kessler 1997
.
In contrast, hypo-elliptic SDEs have been relatively under-explored until recently, though an interesting empirical study, without analytical results, was provided by
Pokern et al. 2009
.

To obtain asymptotic results in the hypo-elliptic setting similar to the ones available for elliptic SDEs, one must deal with a number of challenges. A standard Euler-Maruyama discretisation leads to a Dirac measure due to the involved degenerate diffusion matrix.
This can be resolved by considering a higher-order Itô-Taylor expansion for
\displaystyle X_{S,t}
that propagates additional Gaussian variates onto the smooth components.
For class (
Hypo-I
)
such a direction leads to a discretisation scheme with Gaussian increments of size
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{n}^{3/2})
for the smooth components.
However, recent works
(
Ditlevsen and Samson 2019
;
Gloter and Yoshida 2021
;
Iguchi et al. 2024
)
have highlighted that introduction of high-order Gaussian variates in the smooth components must be accompanied by an appropriate high-order mean approximation of the rough components. If such a balance is not achieved, estimation of
\displaystyle\beta_{R}
becomes biased.
Within class (
Hypo-I
),
Ditlevsen and Samson 2019
developed a non-degenerate discretisation scheme in the setting of
\displaystyle N_{S}=1
and of the diffusion matrix
\displaystyle A
being diagonal.

Ditlevsen and Samson 2019
proposed contrast estimators separately for
\displaystyle\beta_{S}
and
\displaystyle(\beta_{R},\sigma)
and proved a CLT under
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
,
i.e. for a ‘rapidly increasing experimental design’
(
Prakasa-Rao 1988
)
.

Gloter and Yoshida 2020
;
Gloter and Yoshida 2021
addressed the issue of disjoint estimation, within (
Hypo-I
), by working with an approximate Gaussian density for the complete vector
\displaystyle X_{t}
and providing a joint contrast estimator for the full parameter vector
\displaystyle(\beta_{R},\beta_{S},\sigma)
.
They showed that the estimator is asymptotically normal under the same design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
.
For bivariate models in class (
Hypo-I
),
Melnykova 2020
used a local linearisation
(
Biscay et al. 1996
)
of the drift function to obtain a non-degenerate Gaussian discretisation scheme and construct a contrast estimator attaining a CLT under
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
.
For class (
Hypo-II
),
Iguchi et al. 2024
worked in a general multivariate setting and established a joint contrast estimator achieving a CLT under
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
.
The design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
assumed in the above works can be restrictive in practice. Indicatively, if a user tries to design a dataset with a large interval
\displaystyle T_{n}=n\Delta_{n}
and a large number of samples
\displaystyle n
to obtain accurate estimation results, then
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
should be set to a quite small value so that
condition
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}^{2}=T_{n}\Delta_{n}\to 0
is satisfied, e.g. for
\displaystyle T_{n}=1000
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
could be much less than
\displaystyle 10^{-3}
.
Datasets with such small step-sizes are not always available in applications.

An apparent open question in the hypo-elliptic setting is the weakening of the design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
.
Indicatively,
Melnykova 2020
;
Gloter and Yoshida 2020
required
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})

to control terms of size
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{n}^{-q})
,
\displaystyle q\geq 1/2
, when proving consistency for their contrast estimators. Thus, to arrive to a CLT under a weaker design condition, one needs to develop a different approach compared to previous works to prove consistency without requiring that
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
. Furthermore, the construction of a general contrast function for degenerate SDEs is not straightforward due to the degenerate structure of the diffusion matrix.
For class (
Hypo-I
),
Iguchi et al. 2022
developed a contrast estimator achieving the CLT under the weaker condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/3})
via a novel closed-form transition density expansion for SDEs within (
Hypo-I
). However, a general asymptotic theory under the condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
,
has yet to be established for degenerate SDEs.

Our work closes the above gap in the research of hypo-elliptic SDEs. We propose a general contrast estimator for a wide class of hypo-elliptic diffusion models and show its asymptotic normality under the weaker design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
. Specifically, our contributions include the following:

(a)

We develop two contrast estimators for the two model classes (
Hypo-I
) and (
Hypo-II
), for the purposes of joint estimation of the unknown parameter vector
\displaystyle(\beta_{S},\beta_{R},\sigma)
.
The contrast functions are based on approximate log-likelihood terms which we construct by making use of Gaussian approximations with high-order mean and variance expansions, while at the same time dealing with the degenerate structure of the involved SDEs.

(b)

We show that within the high-frequency, complete observation regime, a CLT is obtained provided that the design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
, for
\displaystyle p\geq 2
, is satisfied.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to define a contrast estimator achieving a CLT under the weak design condition that
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}^{p}\rightarrow 0
, for arbitrarily large
\displaystyle p\geq 2
, in the hypo-elliptic setting. For reference, Table
1
summarises existing works with corresponding design conditions, including our contribution in this setting.

(c)

We provide numerical experiments to demonstrate that the proposed estimator is asymptotically unbiased in the high-frequency complete observation regime under the weaker design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
, for
\displaystyle p\geq 3
. The numerical results highlight that the estimators requiring
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
proposed in the literature can indeed suffer from bias when
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}^{2}
is not sufficiently small.

(d)

Our methodology is relevant beyond the setting of
high-frequency and/or
complete observations. The developed Gaussian approximation of the SDE transition density can be used as part of a broader data augmentation algorithm (e.g., MCMC or EM)
given
low-frequency and/or
partial observations. Analytical results for such a setting are beyond the scope of this paper.
We provide a numerical result showcasing the advantage of the use of the developed approximate log-likelihood under the weaker condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 3
, in a setting of high-frequency partial observations.

To add to point (d) above, often in applications only smooth components are observed.

Ditlevsen and Samson 2019
;
Iguchi et al. 2024
have shown empirically that filtering procedures incorporating
the developed approximate likelihood of the full state vector (within a data augmentation approach) can lead to asymptotically unbiased parameter estimation in a partial observation regime.

Model Assumptions and Contrast Estimators

\displaystyle{X}_{t}^{(\mathrm{II})}

2.1
Conditions for Hypo-Ellipticity

We first introduce some notation.
We write the drift function of the Stratonovich-type SDE corresponding to the Itô-type one, given in (
Hypo-I
) or (
Hypo-II
), as

\displaystyle\textstyle A_{0}(x,\theta):=\mu(x,\theta)-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathcal{L}_{k}A_{k}(x,\theta)
,

\displaystyle(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta.

We often treat vector fields
\displaystyle V:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
as differential operators via the relation
\displaystyle\textstyle{V\leftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{N}V^{i}\partial_{x,i}}
. For two vector fields
\displaystyle V,W:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

\displaystyle[V,W]=VW-WV
. That is, for the SDE vector fields
\displaystyle A_{k},A_{l}
,
\displaystyle 0\leq k,l\leq d

\displaystyle\displaystyle[A_{k},A_{l}](x,\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{k}^{i}(x,\theta)\partial_{x,i}A_{l}(x,\theta)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{l}^{i}(x,\theta)\partial_{x,i}A_{k}(x,\theta),\qquad(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta.

For
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq j\leq N
, we define the projection operator
\displaystyle\mathrm{proj}_{i,j}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{j-i+1}
as

\displaystyle x=\bigl[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\bigr]^{\top}\mapsto\mathrm{proj}_{i,j}(x)=\bigl[x_{i},\ldots,x_{j}\bigr]^{\top}
.

Hypo-I
) and (
Hypo-II

Hypo-I
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{S},A_{j}\in C_{p}^{K+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\,;\mathbb{R}^{N_{S}}),\ \ \mu_{R}\in C_{p}^{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\,;\mathbb{R}^{N_{R}}),\quad 1\leq j\leq d.

\displaystyle(ii)
. For class (
Hypo-II
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{S_{1}}\in C_{p}^{K+4}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\,;\mathbb{R}^{N_{S_{1}}}),\ \ \mu_{S_{2}},A_{j}\in C_{p}^{K+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\,;\mathbb{R}^{N_{S_{2}}});

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{R}\in C_{p}^{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\,;\mathbb{R}^{N_{R}}),\quad 1\leq j\leq d.

Remark 2.1
.

We use
H1
to introduce some structure upon the degenerate SDE models (
Hypo-I
) and (
Hypo-II
) and so that contrast functions developed later on are well-defined.

H1
is stronger than Hörmander’s condition. The latter states that there is
\displaystyle M\in\mathbb{N}
so that for any
\displaystyle(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{span}\Bigl\{V(x,\theta):V(x,\theta)\in\bigcup_{1\leq m\leq M}\mathbf{V}_{m}\Bigr\}=\mathbb{R}^{N},

where we have set

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{0}=\bigl\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}\bigr\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{k}=\big\{[A_{l},A]:A\in\mathbf{V}_{k-1},\,0\leq l\leq d\bigr\}
,
\displaystyle k\geq 1
.

If
\displaystyle\mu(\cdot,\theta),A_{j}(\cdot,\theta)\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{N})
then Hörmander’s condition implies that there exists a smooth Lebesgue density for the law of
\displaystyle X_{t}
,
\displaystyle t>0
, for any initial condition
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(see e.g.
Nualart 2006
). Thus, (
Hypo-I
), (
Hypo-II
)
belong to the class of hypo-elliptic SDEs. We note that
H1
is satisfied for most hypo-elliptic SDEs used in applications, as e.g. is the case for the models cited in Section
1
. Condition
H2
allows the SDE coefficients to lie in the larger class

\displaystyle C_{p}^{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\displaystyle K\in\mathbb{N}

rather than
\displaystyle C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
\displaystyle K
is determined so that the contrast functions proposed later are well-defined and error terms obtained from Itô-Taylor expansion in constructing the contrast functions are appropriately controlled. We also note that the drift functions in the smooth components, i.e.
\displaystyle\mu_{S}
in (
Hypo-I
) and
\displaystyle\mu_{S_{1}},\mu_{S_{2}}
in (
Hypo-II
), require more regularity compared to
\displaystyle\mu_{R}
. This is because our construction of the contrast functions uses higher order stochastic Taylor expansions for those functions. More details are given in Sections
2.2.2
,
2.2.3
.

Example 1
.

We provide some examples of SDEs satisfying the above conditions in applications.

i.

Underdamped (standard) Langevin equation for
one-dimensional particle with a unit mass:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dq_{t}&=p_{t}dt;\\
dp_{t}&=(-U^{\prime}(q_{t})+\gamma p_{t})dt+\sigma dW_{t},\end{aligned}

(2.3)

where
\displaystyle\theta=(\gamma,\sigma)
is the parameter vector and
\displaystyle U:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is some smooth potential with polynomial growth. The values of
\displaystyle q_{t}
and
\displaystyle p_{t}
represent the position and momentum of the particle, respectively. This model belongs in class (
Hypo-I
) and indeed satisfies condition
H1
(i).

ii.

Quasi-Markovian Generalised Langevin Equation (
\displaystyle q
-GLE) for the case of an one-dimensional particle with an one-dimensional auxiliary variable:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dq_{t}&=p_{t}dt;\\
dp_{t}&=(-U^{\prime}(q_{t})+\lambda s_{t})dt;\\
ds_{t}&=(-\lambda p_{t}-\alpha s_{t})dt+\sigma\,dW_{t},\end{aligned}

(2.4)

where
\displaystyle\theta=(\lambda,\alpha,\sigma)
is the parameter vector and
\displaystyle U
is as in (
2.3
). Note that component
\displaystyle s_{t}
is now introduced as an auxiliary variable to capture the non-Markovianity of the memory kernel. The
\displaystyle q
-GLE class has been recently actively studied as an effective model in physics (see e.g.
Leimkuhler and Matthews 2015
) and parameter estimation of the model also has been investigated in
Kalliadasis et al. 2015
. The drift function of the smooth component
\displaystyle q_{t}
is now independent of the rough component
\displaystyle s_{t}
. Model (
2.4
) belongs in the SDE class (
Hypo-II
) and satisfies condition
H1
(ii). Details can be found, e.g., in
Iguchi et al. 2024
.

2.2
Contrast Estimators for Degenerate SDEs

Under
H1
-
H2
, we define contrast functions for the classes of hypo-elliptic SDEs (
Hypo-I
) and (
Hypo-II
), so that the corresponding parameter estimators attain a CLT under the design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
. The development of our contrast functions is related to the approaches of
Kessler 1997
;
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
where, in an elliptic setting, contrast functions delivering CLTs with
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
are obtained. However, carrying forward such earlier approaches to the hypo-elliptic diffusion is far from straightforward, mainly due to the degeneracy of the diffusion matrix. After a brief review of the construction of contrast functions that deliver a CLT with
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
in the elliptic case, we proceed to the treatment of the hypo-elliptic class of models. Hereafter, we make use of superscripts
\displaystyle(\mathrm{I})
and
\displaystyle(\mathrm{II})
as necessary to specify the class of hypo-elliptic SDEs we work with.

2.2.1
Review of Contrast Estimators for Elliptic SDEs

We review the construction of contrast estimators for elliptic SDEs in
Kessler 1997
;
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
, where the diffusion matrix
\displaystyle a(x,\sigma)=A(x,\sigma)A(x,\sigma)^{\top}
for the SDE in (
1.1
) is now assumed to be positive definite for any
\displaystyle(x,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta_{\sigma}
.

Remark 2.2
.

We introduce the following notation. For a vector
\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a symmetric matrix
\displaystyle\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(y;\Sigma)=y^{\top}\Sigma y
.

Step 1.
Via an Itô-Taylor expansion, one obtains high-order approximations for the mean and variance of
\displaystyle{X}_{t_{i}}
given
\displaystyle{X}_{t_{i-1}}
, that is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[{X}_{t_{i}}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\bigr]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=r_{K_{p}}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{K_{p}+1});

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}\bigl[{X}_{t_{i}}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\bigr]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Delta\cdot\Xi_{K_{p}}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{K_{p}+1}),

where
\displaystyle K_{p}=[p/2]
, with
\displaystyle r_{K_{p}}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\displaystyle\Xi_{K_{p}}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
determined as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle r_{K}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)={X}_{t_{i-1}}+\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{p}}\Delta^{k}\cdot\mathcal{L}^{k-1}\mu({X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Xi_{K_{p}}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)=\sum_{0\leq k\leq K_{p}}\Delta^{k}\cdot\Sigma_{k}({X}_{t_{i-1}},\sigma).

In the above expression for
\displaystyle\Xi_{K_{p}}
, we have
\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}=a=AA^{\top}
, and
\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}
,
\displaystyle k\geq 1
, are matrices available in closed-form and which include high-order derivatives of the SDE coefficients.

Step 2.

Kessler 1997
;
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
make use of a Gaussian density with mean
\displaystyle r_{K_{p}}(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)
and variance
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\cdot\Xi_{K_{p}}(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)
as a proxy for the intractable transition density of
\displaystyle X_{t_{i}}
given
\displaystyle X_{t_{i-1}}
. Furthermore, to ensure that the approximation is well-defined (notice that
\displaystyle\Xi_{K_{p}}
is not guaranteed to be positive-definite) and avoid cumbersome technicalities, they apply a formal Taylor expansion on
\displaystyle\Xi_{K_{p}}^{-1}(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)
and
\displaystyle\log\det\Xi_{K_{p}}(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)

around
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=0
so that the positive definiteness of the matrix
\displaystyle a(x,\sigma)
is exploited.
Thus, they define the estimator as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{p,n}^{\,\mathrm{Elliptic}}=\mathrm{arg\,min}_{\theta\in\Theta}\ell_{p,n}^{\,\mathrm{Elliptic}}(\theta),\,p\geq 2,

for the contrast function:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ell_{p,n}^{\,\mathrm{Elliptic}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{K_{p}}\Delta_{n}^{k}\,\cdot\biggl\{\tfrac{1}{\Delta_{n}}\mathbf{q}\bigl({X}_{t_{i}}-r_{K_{p}}(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta);\mathbf{G}_{k}({X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)\bigr)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbf{H}_{k}({X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)\biggr\},

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
are analytically available and correspond to the coefficients of the
\displaystyle\Delta^{k}
-term in the formal Taylor expansion of

\displaystyle\Xi_{K_{p}}^{-1}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)

and of
\displaystyle\log\det\Xi_{K_{p}}(\Delta,{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta)
at
\displaystyle\Delta=0
, respectively.

Remark 2.3
.

Due to the fact that
\displaystyle K_{p}=[p/2]
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
,
the contrast function/estimator has the same form for
\displaystyle p=2k,2k+1,\,k\in\mathbb{N}
. However, as
Kessler 1997
;
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
noted in their works, when
\displaystyle p=2
, a simpler estimator based upon the Euler-Maruyama discretisation is asymptotically normal under the condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
, with a contrast function given as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ell^{\mathrm{EM}}_{n}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggl\{\tfrac{1}{\Delta_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{q}\bigl({X}_{t_{i}}-X_{t_{i-1}}-\Delta_{n}\,\mu(X_{t_{i-1}},\beta);a^{-1}({X}_{t_{i-1}},\sigma)\bigr)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\log\det a({X}_{t_{i-1}},\sigma)\biggr\},

2.2.2
Contrast Estimators for SDE Class (
Hypo-I
)

We will adapt the strategy of
Kessler 1997
;
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
to construct contrast functions for degenerate SDEs, starting from the hypo-elliptic class (
Hypo-I
).
However, the development is not straightforward because now the diffusion matrix
\displaystyle a(x,\sigma)
is not positive definite.
Another important difference between the hypo-elliptic and the elliptic setting is that an Itô-Taylor expansion for moments of the SDE can involve
\displaystyle\Delta
with varying orders across smooth and rough components.

Example 2
.

We consider the under-damped Langevin equation defined in (
2.3
).
For the model, the Itô-Taylor expansion gives:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}[{X}_{t_{i+1}}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}]=\sigma^{2}\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{\Delta^{3}}{3}&\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2}\\[2.84544pt]
\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2}&\Delta\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{4})&\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{3})\\
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{3})&\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\end{bmatrix},\quad\Delta=t_{i+1}-t_{i}.

Thus, the order of
\displaystyle\Delta
is larger for the smooth component. Note that the leading term
\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\Delta^{3}/3
in the variance of
\displaystyle X_{\Delta}^{1}
derives from the Gaussian variate
\displaystyle\textstyle\sigma\int_{0}^{\Delta}W_{s}ds
arising in the Itô-Taylor expansion of
\displaystyle X_{\Delta}^{1}
.

One must now find a positive definite matrix instead of
\displaystyle a(x,\theta)
to obtain a general contrast function in the form of (
2.2.1
) in a hypo-elliptic setting while dealing with such structure of varying scales amongst the components of degenerate SDEs. We thus begin by considering a standardisation of
\displaystyle X_{\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}
(conditionally on
\displaystyle\textstyle X_{0}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}=x
) via subtracting high-order mean approximations from smooth/rough components and dividing with appropriate
\displaystyle\Delta
-terms. In particular, we introduce the
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued random variables as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{Y}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}:=m^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{p}(\Delta,x,X_{\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})},\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta^{3}}}\Bigl(X_{S,\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}-{r}_{S,K_{p}+1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta,x;\theta)\Bigr)\\
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\Bigl(X_{R,\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}-{r}_{R,K_{p}}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta,x;\theta)\Bigr)\end{bmatrix},

where we have set
\displaystyle K_{p}=[p/2]
and for
\displaystyle q\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{r}_{S,q}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta,x,\theta)\\[2.84544pt]
{r}_{R,q}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta,x,\theta)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{S}\\[2.84544pt]
x_{R}\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\frac{\Delta^{k}}{k!}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{L}^{k-1}\mu_{S}(x,\theta)\\
\mathcal{L}^{k-1}\mu_{R}(x,\theta)\end{bmatrix},\quad x=\begin{bmatrix}x_{S}\\
x_{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

(2.6)

with this latter quantity obtained from an Itô-Taylor expansion of
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X_{\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}]
.
We note that
\displaystyle Y_{p,\Delta}
is well-defined under
H2
\displaystyle(2K_{p})
.
Also, note that different orders (by one) of mean approximation are used for smooth and rough components in the above standardisation. We will provide some more details on this in Remark
2.4
later in the paper. We denote the Lebesgue density of the distribution of
\displaystyle X_{\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}
given
\displaystyle X_{0}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}=x
and
\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta
as

\displaystyle\textstyle y\mapsto{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(X^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\Delta}\in dy)}/{dy}=p^{X^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\Delta}}(x,y;\theta).

Transformation of random variables gives that, for
\displaystyle(x,y,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle p^{X^{(\mathrm{I})}_{\Delta}}(x,y;\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta^{3N_{S}+N_{R}}}}p^{{Y}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}}\left(\xi;\theta\right)\big|_{\xi=m_{p}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta,x,y,\theta)},\ \

where

\displaystyle p^{{Y}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}}(\xi;\theta)\equiv\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(Y_{p,\Delta}^{(\mathrm{I})}\in d\xi)/d\xi
denotes the Lebesgue density of the law of
\displaystyle{Y}^{(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}
given the parameter
\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta
.
Following the standarisation, an Itô-Taylor expansion now gives, under
H2
\displaystyle\,(2J)
,
\displaystyle J\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[{Y}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}\bigl({Y}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}\bigr)^{\top}\right]=\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Delta^{j}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{j}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)+R^{(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta^{J+1},x,\theta),

(2.7)

where
\displaystyle R^{(\mathrm{I})}\in\mathcal{S}
,
for some analytically available
\displaystyle N\times N
matrices
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{j}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)
. In particular, matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)
has the following block expression:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{SS}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Sigma}_{SR}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)\\[2.84544pt]
\bm{\Sigma}_{RS}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Sigma}_{RR}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)\end{bmatrix},

where we have set:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{RR}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{R,k}(x,\sigma)A_{R,k}(x,\sigma)^{\top}\equiv a_{R}(x,\sigma);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{SR}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathcal{L}_{k}\mu_{S}(x,\theta)A_{R,k}(x,\sigma)^{\top},\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{RS}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)=\bm{\Sigma}_{SR}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)^{\top};

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{SS}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathcal{L}_{k}\mu_{S}(x,\theta)\mathcal{L}_{k}\mu_{S}(x,\theta)^{\top}\equiv\tfrac{1}{3}a_{S}(x,\theta).

Matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)
plays a role similar to
\displaystyle a(x,\theta)
in the elliptic setting due to the following result whose proof is in Section
A.2
of the Supplementary Material.

Lemma 2.1
.

Under
H1
\displaystyle(i)
, the matrices
\displaystyle a_{R}(x,\sigma)
,
\displaystyle a_{S}(x,\theta)
and
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)
are positive definite for all
\displaystyle(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta
.

We form a Gaussian approximation for
\displaystyle p^{{Y}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{\,p,\Delta}}\left(\xi;\theta\right)
to obtain a contrast function that will be well-defined due to the positive definiteness of matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{(\mathrm{I})}(x,\theta)
. We introduce some notation. For
\displaystyle h>0
,
\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta
,
\displaystyle 0\leq i\leq n
,
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq K
and
\displaystyle K\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{p,i}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(h,\theta):={m}_{p}^{(\mathrm{I})}(h,{X}_{t_{i-1}}^{(\mathrm{I})},{X}_{t_{i}}^{(\mathrm{I})},\theta);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta):=\bm{\Sigma}^{(\mathrm{I})}({X}_{t_{i}},\theta),\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{i,j}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta):=\bm{\Sigma}_{j}^{(\mathrm{I})}({X}_{t_{i}},\theta);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}^{(\mathrm{I})}_{K,i}(h,\theta):=\bm{\Sigma}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)+\sum_{1\leq j\leq K}h^{j}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{i,j}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta),\quad\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta):=\bigl(\bm{\Sigma}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\bigr)^{-1}.

Note that the above functions are well-defined under
H2
\displaystyle(2K_{p})
; see (
2.6
)-(
2.7
).
We write the formal Taylor expansion of

\displaystyle\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K,i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(h,\theta)\bigr)^{-1}
and
\displaystyle\log\det\bm{\Xi}_{K,i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(h,\theta)
up to the level
\displaystyle K\in\mathbb{N}
as

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq K}h^{k}\cdot\mathbf{G}_{i,k}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta),\;\sum_{0\leq k\leq K}h^{k}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{i,k}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta),

respectively, where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{i,k}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\partial_{h}^{k}\,\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K,i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(h,\theta)\bigr)^{-1}\,\big|_{h=0};

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i,k}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\partial_{h}^{k}\bigl(\log\det\bm{\Xi}_{K,i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(h,\theta)\bigr)\big|_{h=0},\qquad 0\leq k\leq K.

For instance, one has:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{i,0}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{\mathrm{(I)}}(\theta),\quad\mathbf{G}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=-\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\,\bm{\Sigma}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\,\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{i,2}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=-\bigl(\mathbf{G}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)+\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}_{i,2}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\bigr)\bm{\Lambda}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i,0}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=\log\det\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{i}(\theta),\quad\mathbf{H}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bm{\Lambda}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{i}(\theta)\,\bm{\Sigma}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\Bigr];

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i,2}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{G}_{i,1}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}^{(\mathrm{I})}_{i,1}(\theta)+\bm{\Lambda}^{(\mathrm{I})}_{i}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}_{i,2}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\Bigr].

Note that the terms
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{i,k}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i,k}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)
involve the inverse of the matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{i}^{(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)
, and are well-defined following Condition
H1
\displaystyle(i)

and Lemma
2.1
. We now construct the contrast function
\displaystyle\ell_{p,n,\Delta}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)
,
\displaystyle\theta=(\beta_{S},\beta_{R},\sigma)\in\Theta
, for the hypo-elliptic class (
Hypo-I
) as follows. Under Condition
H2
\displaystyle(2K_{p})
:

(i)

For
\displaystyle p=2
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ell_{2,n}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl\{\mathbf{m}_{2,i}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta_{n},\theta)^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{i-1}(\theta)\,\mathbf{m}_{2,i}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta_{n},\theta)+\log\det\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}_{i-1}(\theta)\Bigr\}.

(2.8)

(ii)

For
\displaystyle p\geq 3
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\ell_{p,n}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{K_{p}}\Delta_{n}^{j}\cdot\bigl\{\mathbf{m}_{p,i}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta_{n},\theta)^{\top}\,\mathbf{G}_{i-1,j}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\,\mathbf{m}_{p,i}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\Delta_{n},\theta)+\mathbf{H}_{i-1,j}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)\bigr\}.

Thus, the contrast estimator
\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{p,n}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}=(\hat{\beta}_{S,p,n},\,\hat{\beta}_{R,p,n},\,\hat{\sigma}_{p,n})
,

2.2.3
Contrast Estimators for SDE Class (
Hypo-II
)

To construct the contrast function and corresponding estimator for the highly degenerate diffusion class defined via (
Hypo-II
),
we proceed as above, that is we consider an appropriate standardisation that takes under consideration the scales of the three components
\displaystyle(X_{S_{1},\Delta},X_{S_{2},\Delta},X_{R,\Delta}),\,\Delta>0
. Note that the first smooth component
\displaystyle X_{S_{1},\Delta}
has a variance of size
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{5})
due to the largest (as
\displaystyle\Delta\rightarrow 0
) variate in the Itô-Taylor expansion being
\displaystyle\textstyle\int_{0}^{\Delta}\int_{0}^{s}W_{u}duds
, where we have made use of the fact that the drift
\displaystyle\mu_{S_{1}}
is a function only of the smooth components.
Thus, under Condition
H2
\displaystyle\,(2K_{p})
, we introduce:

FitzHugh-Nagumo Model

We consider the FitzHugh-Nagumo (FHN) model belonging in class (
Hypo-I
), and specified via the following bivariate SDE:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} dX_{t}=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}(X_{t}-(X_{t})^{3}-Y_{t}-s)dt;\\
dY_{t}=(\gamma X_{t}-Y_{t}+\alpha)dt+\sigma dW_{t}^{1},\end{aligned}

(4.1)

with
\displaystyle\theta=(\varepsilon,\gamma,\alpha,\sigma)
. We consider parameter estimation of the FHN model in both complete and partial observation regimes, where only the component
\displaystyle X_{t}
is observed in the latter case.

4.2.1
Complete Observation Regime

We consider the estimator
\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{p,n}(=\hat{\theta}_{p,n}^{\,(\mathrm{I})})=(\hat{\varepsilon}_{p,n},\hat{\gamma}_{p,n},\hat{\alpha}_{p,n},\hat{\sigma}_{p,n})
for
\displaystyle p=2,4
. As
Melnykova 2020
, we fix
\displaystyle s=0.01
and set the true values to
\displaystyle\theta^{\dagger}=(0.10,1.50,0.30,0.60)
. We generate
\displaystyle M=50
independent datasets by using the LG discretisation for the (
Hypo-I
) class defined in
Gloter and Yoshida 2020
;
Iguchi et al. 2022
with a step-size
\displaystyle\Delta=10^{-4}
. Then, we obtain
\displaystyle M=50
synthetic complete observations for the FHN model by sub-sampling from the above datasets so that
\displaystyle n=250,000
,
\displaystyle T_{n}=5,000
and
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=0.02
.
To obtain the minima of the
contrast functions we used the Adam optimiser with the same settings as the experiments with the
\displaystyle q
-GLE earlier, except for (step-size) = 0.01.

4.2.2
Partial Observation Regime

We use the proposed contrast function, and the corresponding approximate log-likelihood, under the partial observation regime where only the smooth component
\displaystyle\{X_{t_{i}}\}
is observed.
In particular, we compute maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) based upon a closed-form marginal likelihood constructed from the Gaussian approximation corresponding to the contrast function
\displaystyle\ell_{p,n}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)
for
\displaystyle p=2,3
. To be precise, we use the high-order expansions for the mean and the variance, but do not make use of the Taylor expansions for the inverse of the covariance and of its log-determinant, so that we obtain quantities that correspond to proper density functions.
Here, we mention that due to the structure of the FHN model, the obtained one-step Gaussian approximation corresponding to the developed contrast function for
\displaystyle p=2,3
is a linear Gaussian model w.r.t. the hidden component
\displaystyle Y_{t_{i}}
given the observation
\displaystyle X_{t_{i}}
. Thus, one can make use of the Kalman Filter (KF) and calculate the marginal likelihood of the partial observations.
See e.g.
Iguchi et al. 2024
where a marginal likelihood is built upon KF for the
\displaystyle q
-GLE model belonging in class (
Hypo-II
). We provide the details of the Gaussian approximation and the marginal likelihood for the FHN model in Section
D
of the Supplementary Material.
Then, the MLE for partial observation is defined as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{p,n}\bigl(=(\hat{\varepsilon}_{p,n},\hat{\gamma}_{p,n},\hat{\alpha}_{p,n},\hat{\sigma}_{p,n})\bigr)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta\in\Theta}\log f_{p,n}(\{X_{t_{i}}\}_{i=0,\ldots,n};\theta),

(4.2)

for
\displaystyle p=2,3
, where
\displaystyle f_{p,n}(\{X_{t_{i}}\}_{i=0,\ldots,n};\theta)
is the marginal likelihood. As with the experiment under complete observations, we fix
\displaystyle s=0.01
and set the true values to

\displaystyle\theta^{\dagger}=(0.10,1.50,0.30,0.60)
. We generate
\displaystyle M=50
independent datasets from the LG discretisation with a step-size
\displaystyle\Delta=10^{-4}
.
Then, we obtain
\displaystyle 50
synthetic partial observations of
\displaystyle\{X_{t_{i}}\}_{i=0,\ldots,n}
with
\displaystyle n=200,000
,
\displaystyle T_{n}=1,000
and
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=0.005
by sub-sampling from the datasets and removing the rough component
\displaystyle\{Y_{t_{i}}\}_{i=0,\ldots,n}
. The Nelder-Mead method is applied to optimise the log-marginal likelihood for
\displaystyle p=2,3
with the initial guess
\displaystyle\theta_{0}=(0.5,0.5,0.5,0.5)

5
Proof of Main Results

We provide the proof for our main results, Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, which demonstrate the asymptotic normality of the proposed contrast estimators under the design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
. We show all details of the proof for class (
Hypo-II
) as the proof for (
Hypo-I
) follows from similar arguments. Throughout this section, for simplicity of notation, we frequently omit the subscript
\displaystyle(\mathrm{II})
appearing in the estimator and the functions introduced in Section
2.2.3
. A number of technical results are collected in the Supplementary Material. We make use of the notation
\displaystyle o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}(\cdot)
: for a sequence of random variables
\displaystyle\{F_{n}\}
and a numerical sequence
\displaystyle\{\alpha_{n,\Delta_{n}}\}
depending on
\displaystyle n
or
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
, we write
\displaystyle F_{n}=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}(\alpha_{n,\Delta_{n}})

if

\displaystyle{\textstyle{F_{n}}/{\alpha_{n,\Delta_{n}}}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}0}
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0
and
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty
.
Throughout this section,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
is an arbitrary integer, and we set
\displaystyle K_{p}=[p/2]
as before.

5.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1
(Consistency)

We show the consistency of the contrast estimator via the following procedure:

Step 1.

We show that if
\displaystyle n\to\infty
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0
and
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty
, then
\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{S_{1},p,n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}\beta_{S_{1}}^{\dagger}
. In particular:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{S_{1},p,n}-\beta_{S_{1}}^{\dagger}=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}(\Delta_{n}^{3/2}).

(5.1)

Step 2.

Making use of the rate of convergence found in (
5.1
), we show that if
\displaystyle n\to\infty
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0
and
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty
, then
\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{S_{2},p,n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}\beta_{S_{2}}^{\dagger}
. In particular:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{S_{2},p,n}-\beta_{S_{2}}^{\dagger}=o_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}(\Delta_{n}^{1/2}).

(5.2)

Step 3.

Using the rates obtained in
(
5.1
)-(
5.2
), we show that if
\displaystyle n\to\infty
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0
and
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty
, then
\displaystyle(\hat{\beta}_{R,p,n},\hat{\sigma}_{p,n})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}(\beta^{\dagger}_{R},\sigma^{\dagger})
.

In the sequel, we express the matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}(x,\theta)=(\bm{\Sigma}^{(\mathrm{II})}(x,\theta))^{-1},\,(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta
, as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{1}S_{1}}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{1}S_{2}}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{1}R}(x,\theta)\\
\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{2}S_{1}}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{2}S_{2}}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{2}R}(x,\theta)\\
\bm{\Lambda}_{RS_{1}}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Lambda}_{RS_{2}}(x,\theta)&\bm{\Lambda}_{RR}(x,\theta)\end{bmatrix},

for block matrices
\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\iota_{1}}\times N_{\iota_{2}}}
,
\displaystyle\iota_{1},\iota_{2}\in\{S_{1},S_{2},R\}
. Note that from Lemma 13 in
Iguchi et al. 2024
we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{S_{1}S_{1}}(x,\theta)=720\,a_{S_{1}}^{-1}(x,\theta),\qquad(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\Theta,

where
\displaystyle a_{S_{1}}(x,\theta)
is specified in the definition of the matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\,(\mathrm{II})}(x,\theta)
in (
2.11
).

Remark 5.1
.

The strategy outlined above is technically different from proofs in
Melnykova 2020
;
Gloter and Yoshida 2020
, where they require that
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
to prove consistency.
Our proof of consistency proceeds without relying on
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
, and such an approach then leads to a CLT under the weaker condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
. More details on this point are given in Remark
5.2
.

6
Conclusions

We have proposed general contrast estimators for a wide class of hypo-elliptic diffusions specified in (
Hypo-I
) and (
Hypo-II
), and showed that the estimators achieve consistency and asymptotic normality in a high-frequency complete observation regime under the weakened design condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 2
.
For elliptic diffusions, contrast estimators under such a general weak design condition have been investigated in earlier works, e.g.
Kessler 1997
;
Uchida and Yoshida 2012
, however
in the context of hypo-elliptic SDEs, established results (e.g.
Ditlevsen and Samson 2019
;
Gloter and Yoshida 2021
;
Melnykova 2020
;
Iguchi et al. 2024
) before our work typically relied on a condition of ‘rapidly increasing experimental design’, i.e. the requirement that
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
.
The numerical experiments in
Section
4
illustrated cases where for a given step-size
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
in-between observations and given
\displaystyle n
,
estimators requiring the condition
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
can induce unreliable biased parametric inference procedures. In contrast, the bias was removed upon consideration of estimators which required the weaker design condition

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
,
\displaystyle p\geq 3
.
We stress that computations w.r.t. the contrast function that delivers estimators under the weakened design condition can be automated via use of symbolic programming and automatic differentiation, so that that users need only specify the drift and diffusion coefficients of the given SDE model.

Our research opens up several potential future directions.

1.

Development and analytical study of estimators under alternative observation designs, such as partially observed coordinates and/or low-frequency observation settings.
Such designs are important from a practical perspective but have yet to be investigated analytically even for elliptic diffusions. The results in this paper are already relevant for such different observation designs as they can be regarded as providing a minimum requirement on the step-size
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
(for given
\displaystyle n
) that needs to be used when considering smaller amounts of data compared to the complete observation regime treated in this work. E.g., in a low-frequency setting where
data augmentation
procedures (within an Expectation-Maximisation or MCMC setting) will introduce latent SDE values,
the user-specified step-size
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
will need to satisfy the weakened design conditions obtained in our work (i.e. if the likelihood-based method is not supported in the case where imputed variables were indeed observations, there is absolutely no basis for the method that uses imputed values to provide reliable estimates).
In general, it is of interest to explore the type of convergence rates and step-size conditions obtained and required, respectively, in CLTs under such different observation designs.

2.

Parametric inference for Mckean-Vlasov type hypo-elliptic SDEs and related interacting particle system models.
Recently, parametric inference for SDEs with coefficients depending on the (empirical) law of the process has become an active research area in Statistics, e.g.
Amorino et al. 2023
;
Sharrock et al. 2023
, though these works focus on elliptic models. Law-dependent hypo-elliptic SDEs appear in several applications
(
Baladron et al. 2012
)
, and it is of high interest to establish estimators and analytic results that cover such hypo-elliptic models.

3.

Non-parametric estimation for hypo-elliptic SDEs.
Established results in the literature have focused on elliptic diffusions. In the scalar case,
Hoffmann 1999
studied non-parametric estimation of drift and diffusion functions from high-frequency observations and obtained optimal convergence rates, with extensions to low-frequency observations regimes given in
Gobet et al. 2004
.
Abraham 2019
studied a Bayesian non-parametric approach using high-frequency data. For multivariate elliptic models, see
Nickl and Ray 2020
for a work in a Bayesian setting and
Oga and Koike 2024
which proposed a non-parametric drift estimator based on deep-learning and obtained a convergence rate under the condition of
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
. Providing results for hypo-elliptic models along the above lines makes up a very interesting research direction.

\displaystyle 1/n^{2}
. We omit the detailed proof.

C.3
Proof of Lemma
5.8

Let
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
and
\displaystyle p\geq 2
. Making use of

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\dagger}}\bigl[\mathbf{m}_{p,i}^{l_{1}}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\mathbf{m}_{p,i}^{l_{2}}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\bigr]=[\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})]_{l_{1}l_{2}}+[\mathbf{C}(\Delta_{n}^{K_{p}+1},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger})]_{l_{1}l_{2}},\quad 1\leq l_{1},l_{2}\leq N,

for some
\displaystyle[\mathbf{C}]_{l_{1}l_{2}}\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
\displaystyle F^{(2),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})=F^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})+F^{(2,\mathrm{II}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})
with

\displaystyle\displaystyle F^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=[\mathbf{M}_{n}^{-1}]_{kk}\sum_{j=0}^{K_{p}}\Delta_{n}^{j}\Bigl\{\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bigl(\partial_{\theta,k}\mathbf{G}_{i-1,j}(\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)\,\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})\Bigr]+\partial_{\theta,k}\mathbf{H}_{i-1,j}(\theta^{\dagger})\Bigr\};

\displaystyle\displaystyle F^{(2,\mathrm{II}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n}R^{{(2,\mathrm{II}),k}}\bigl(\sqrt{n\Delta_{n}^{2K_{p}+2}},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger}\bigr),

where
\displaystyle R^{{(2,\mathrm{II}),k}}\in\mathcal{S}
. We will show that under the event
\displaystyle D_{\Delta_{n},i-1}=\{\omega\in\Omega\,|\,\det\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})>0\}
,
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n
, it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{K_{p}}\Delta_{n}^{j}\Bigl\{\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bigl(\partial_{\theta,k}\mathbf{G}_{i-1,j}(\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})\Bigr]+\partial_{\theta,k}\mathbf{H}_{i-1,j}(\theta^{\dagger})\Bigr\}=R(\Delta_{n}^{K_{p}+1},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger}),

(C.6)

where
\displaystyle R\in\mathcal{S}
. Due to the invertibility of the matrix
\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})
under the event
\displaystyle D_{\Delta_{n},i-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{\theta,k}\log\det\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)^{-1}\partial_{\theta,k}\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\partial_{\theta,k}\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)^{-1}\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr],\quad 1\leq k\leq N_{\theta},

(C.7)

where in the last equality we have used
\displaystyle N=\mathrm{Tr}[\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)^{-1}\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})]
. Taylor expansion of
\displaystyle\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)^{-1}
and
\displaystyle\log\det\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})
at
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=0
yield:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Bigl[\bigl(\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})\bigr)^{-1}\Bigr]_{l_{1}l_{2}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{K_{p}}\Delta_{n}^{j}\,\bigl[\mathbf{G}_{i-1,j}(\theta^{\dagger})\bigr]_{l_{1}l_{2}}+R^{1}_{l_{1}l_{2}}(\Delta_{n}^{K_{p}+1},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger}),\quad 1\leq l_{1},l_{2}\leq N;

(C.8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\det\bm{\Xi}_{K_{p},i-1}(\Delta_{n},\theta^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{K_{p}}\Delta_{n}^{j}\,\mathbf{H}_{i-1,j}(\theta^{\dagger})+R^{2}(\Delta_{n}^{K_{p}+1},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger}),

(C.9)

where
\displaystyle R^{1}_{l_{1}l_{2}},\,R^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
so that they are continuously differentiable w.r.t.
\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta
and
\displaystyle\partial_{\theta,k}R^{1}_{l_{1}l_{2}},\,\partial_{\theta,k}R^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq N_{\theta}
. Hence, (
C.6
) immediately follows from (
C.7
), (
C.8
) and (
C.9
).

Thus, (
C.6
) gives

\displaystyle\displaystyle F^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{n}R^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}\bigl(\sqrt{n\Delta_{n}^{2K_{p}+2}},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger}\bigr),&1\leq k\leq N_{\beta_{S}},\,N_{\beta}+1\leq k\leq N_{\theta};\\
\tfrac{1}{n}R^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}(\sqrt{n\Delta_{n}^{2K_{p}+1}},{X}_{t_{i-1}},\theta^{\dagger}),&N_{\beta_{S}}+1\leq k\leq N_{\beta},\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle R^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Thus, under the event
\displaystyle\textstyle{D_{\Delta_{n}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}D_{\Delta_{n},i-1}}
, if
\displaystyle n\to\infty
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\to 0
and
\displaystyle n\Delta_{n}\to\infty
with
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/p})
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}F^{(2),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\{F^{(2,\mathrm{I}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})+F^{(2,\mathrm{II}),k}_{i-1}(\theta^{\dagger})\bigr\}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\dagger}}}0,

and now the proof of Lemma
5.8
is complete.

Appendix D
Supporting Material for Numerical Experiments

We provide the details to construct the marginal likelihood used in the numerical experiment of parameter estimation of FHN model (
4.1
) under a partial observation regime in Section
4.2.2
in the main text.

To construct the marginal likelihood used in the computation of the MLE (
4.2
), we introduce (locally) Gaussian approximations associated with the contrast function
\displaystyle\ell_{p,n}^{\,(\mathrm{I})}(\theta)
,
\displaystyle p=2,3
, defined in (
2.8
) and (
2.9
). We note that when
\displaystyle p=2
, the scheme corresponds to the local Gaussian (LG) scheme developed in
Gloter and Yoshida 2020
;
Gloter and Yoshida 2021
. The one-step Gaussian approximations for
\displaystyle p=2,3
are written in the following form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{Z}_{t_{i+1}}=\begin{bmatrix}{X}_{t_{i+1}}\\[2.84544pt]
{Y}_{t_{i+1}}\end{bmatrix}=a(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i}},\theta)+b(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i}},\theta)Y_{t_{i}}+c_{p}(\Delta_{n},{X}_{t_{i}},\theta)w_{i+1},\qquad p=2,3,

(D.1)

where
\displaystyle a,b,c_{p}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
are explicitly determined and
\displaystyle\{w_{i+1}\}_{i=0,\ldots,n-1}
is i.i.d. sequence of standard normal random variables. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle a(\Delta,x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}&x+\tfrac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}(x-x^{3}-s)+\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}(1-3x^{2})(x-x^{3}-s)-\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2\varepsilon}(\gamma x+\beta)\\[5.69046pt]
&(\gamma x+\beta)\Delta\end{bmatrix};

\displaystyle\displaystyle b(\Delta,x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}+\bigl\{-(1-3x^{2})+\varepsilon\bigr\}\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\\[5.69046pt]
1-\Delta\end{bmatrix},

for
\displaystyle\Delta>0
,
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\theta=(\varepsilon,\gamma,\alpha,\sigma)
. Also, the covariance matrix
\displaystyle\Sigma_{p}\equiv c_{p}c_{p}^{\top},\,p=2,3
, is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}(\Delta,x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{\Delta^{3}}{3}\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}&-\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2}\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}\\[5.69046pt]
-\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{2}\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon}&\Delta\,\sigma^{2}\end{bmatrix};

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}(\Delta,x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Sigma_{2}(\Delta,x,\theta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{\Delta^{4}}{4}L_{1}(x,\theta)L_{2}(x,\theta)&\Delta^{3}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{6}\sigma L_{2}(x,\theta)+\tfrac{1}{3}L_{1}(x,\theta)L_{3}(x,\theta)\bigr)\\[5.69046pt]
\Delta^{3}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{6}\sigma L_{2}(x,\theta)+\tfrac{1}{3}L_{1}(x,\theta)L_{3}(x,\theta)\bigr)&\Delta^{2}\sigma L_{3}(x,\theta)\end{bmatrix},

where we have set:

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{1}(x,\theta)=-\tfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon},\quad L_{2}(x,\theta)=\tfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{2}}\times\bigl\{-(1-3x^{2})+\varepsilon\bigr\},\quad L_{3}(x,\theta)=-\sigma.

Thus, given the observation
\displaystyle X_{t_{i}}
, the scheme (
D.1
) is interpreted as a linear Gaussian model w.r.t. the hidden component
\displaystyle Y_{t_{i}}
. For the linear Gaussian state space model (
D.1
), the Kalman Filter (KF) recursion formula can be obtained.
We recall
\displaystyle\Delta_{n}
is the step-size of the observation and
\displaystyle n
is the number of data.
For the simplicity of notation, we write
\displaystyle Z_{k}=(X_{k},Y_{k}),\,0\leq k\leq n
instead of
\displaystyle Z_{t_{k}}=(X_{t_{k}},Y_{t_{k}})
and
\displaystyle Z_{0:n}\equiv\{(X_{k},Y_{k})\}_{k=0,\ldots,n}
.
Then, it holds that

Y_{k}|X_{0:k}\sim\mathscr{N}(m_{p,k},Q_{p,k}),\quad 0\leq k\leq n,\ \ p=2,3,

where the filtering mean
\displaystyle m_{p,k}
and variance
\displaystyle Q_{p,k}
is defined as follows (the derivation can be found in Appendix F in
Iguchi et al. 2024
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle m_{p,k}=\mu^{Y}_{p,k-1}+\frac{\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{YX}}{\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XX}}(X_{k}-\mu^{X}_{p,k-1}),\qquad Q_{p,k}=\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{YY}-\frac{\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{YX}\,\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XY}}{\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XX}},

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{X}_{p,k-1}\\[5.69046pt]
\mu^{Y}_{p,k-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=a(\Delta_{n},X_{k-1},\theta)+b(\Delta_{n},X_{k-1},\theta)m_{p,k-1};

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XX}&\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XY}\\[5.69046pt]
\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XY}&\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{YY}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Sigma_{p}(\Delta_{n},X_{k-1},\theta)+b(\Delta_{n},X_{k-1},\theta)\,Q_{p,k-1}\,b(\Delta_{n},X_{k-1},\theta)^{\top}.

Since it holds that
\displaystyle X_{k}|X_{0:k-1}\sim\mathscr{N}(\mu^{X}_{p,k-1},\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XX})
, the marginal likelihood
\displaystyle f_{p,n}(X_{0:n};\theta),\,p=2,3
, is obtained as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{p,n}(X_{0:n};\theta)=\varphi_{0}(X_{0})\times\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varphi\bigl(X_{k}\,;\,\mu^{X}_{p,k-1},\,\Lambda_{p,k-1}^{XX}\bigr),

where
\displaystyle\varphi_{0}
is the density of
\displaystyle X_{0}
and
\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot\,;\,\mu,v)
is the density of (scalar) Gaussian random variable with the mean
\displaystyle\mu
and the variance
\displaystyle v
.
