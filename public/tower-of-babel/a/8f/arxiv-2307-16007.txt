Inertia of Kwong matrices
Rajendra Bhatia, Tanvi Jain

Abstract
. Let r r be any real number and for any n n let p 1 , … , p n p_{1},\ldots,p_{n} be distinct positive numbers. A Kwong matrix is the n × n n\times n matrix whose ( i , j ) (i,j) entry is ( p i r + p j r ) / ( p i + p j ) . (p_{i}^{r}+p_{j}^{r})/(p_{i}+p_{j}). We determine the signatures of eigenvalues of all such matrices. The corresponding problem for the family of Loewner matrices [ ( p i r − p j r ) / ( p i − p j ) ] \begin{bmatrix}(p_{i}^{r}-p_{j}^{r})/(p_{i}-p_{j})\end{bmatrix} has been solved earlier.

15A18, 15A48, 15A57, 42A82

1.
Introduction

Let
f
be a nonnegative
C^{1}
function on
(0,\infty).

Let
n
be a positive integer and
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{n}
distinct positive real numbers.
The
n\times n
matrix

L_{f}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{f(p_{i})-f(p_{j})}{p_{i}-p_{j}}\end{bmatrix}

(1)

is called a
Loewner matrix
associated with
f.

These matrices play an important role in several areas of analysis,
one of them being Loewner’s theory of operator monotone functions.
A central theorem in this theory asserts that
f
is operator monotone if and only if all Loewner matrices associated with
f
are positive semidefinite.
See
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
14
]
.

Closely related to Loewner matrices are the matrices

K_{f}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{f(p_{i})+f(p_{j})}{p_{i}+p_{j}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

These too have been studied in several papers.
In
[
10
]
Kwong showed that all matrices
K_{f}
are positive semidefinite
if (but not only if)
f
is operator monotone.
Because of this the matrices
K_{f}
are sometimes called
Kwong matrices
.
Audenaert
[
1
]
has characterised all functions
f
for which all
K_{f}
are positive semidefinite.

Of particular interest are the functions
f(t)=t^{r},
where
r
is any real number.
For these functions we denote
L_{f}
and
K_{f}
by
L_{r}
and
K_{r},
respectively.
Thus

L_{r}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{p_{i}^{r}-p_{j}^{r}}{p_{i}-p_{j}}\end{bmatrix},

(3)

and

K_{r}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{p_{i}^{r}+p_{j}^{r}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

Another fundamental theorem of Loewner’s says that the function
f(t)=t^{r}
is operator monotone
if and only if
0\leq r\leq 1.

Thus all matrices
L_{r}
are positive semidefinite if and only if
0\leq r\leq 1.

From the work of Kwong and Audenaert cited above it follows that
all matrices
K_{r}
are positive semidefinite if and only if
-1\leq r\leq 1

and positive definite if and only if
-1<r<1.

In their work
[
5
]
Bhatia and Holbrook studied the matrices
L_{r}
for values of
r
outside the interval
[0,1].

Among other things, they showed that
when
1<r<2
every matrix
L_{r}
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.
This is in striking contrast to the case
0<r<1,

in which all eigenvalues of
L_{r}
are positive.
This led the authors
[
5
]
to make a conjecture about the signature of eigenvalues of
L_{r}
as
r
varies over real numbers.

Let
A
be any
n\times n
Hermitian matrix.
The
inertia
of
A
is the triple

{\rm In}(A)=(\pi(A),\zeta(A),\nu(A)),

where
\pi(A),

\zeta(A)
and
\nu(A)
are respectively the numbers of positive, zero and negative eigenvalues of
A.

By the results of Loewner cited above

\text{\rm In}\,L_{r}=(n,0,0)
when
0<r<1,

and the result of Bhatia-Holbrook says that
\text{\rm In}\,L_{r}=(1,0,n-1)

when
1<r<2.

The conjecture in
[
5
]
described the inertia of
L_{r}
for other values of
r.

In
[
7
]
Bhatia and Sano made two essential contributions to this problem.
They provided a better understanding of the problem for the range
1<r<2,

and they also obtained a solution for the range
2<r<3.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
be the
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n}

defined as

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=0\right\}.

(5)

An
n\times n
Hermitian matrix is said to be
conditionally positive definite
(cpd) if

\langle x,Ax\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

It is said to be
conditionally negative definite
(cnd) if
-A
is cpd.
If
A
is nonsingular and cnd with all entries nonnegative, then
\text{\rm In}\,A=(1,0,n-1).

Bhatia and Sano
[
7
]
showed that the matrix
L_{r}
is cnd when
1<r<2,

thus explaining the result in
[
5
]
.
They also showed that
L_{r}
is cpd when
2<r<3.

In the same paper
[
7
]
the authors found an interesting difference between the
inertial properties of
L_{r}
and
K_{r}
in the range
2<r<3.

They showed that
K_{r}
is nonsingular and cnd in the interval
1<r<3,

and hence
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=(1,0,n-1)
for such
r.

Thus, there arises the problem of studying
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}
parallel to that of
\text{\rm In}\,L_{r}.

The inertia of
L_{r}
was completely determined by Bhatia, Friedland and Jain in
[
4
]
.
The corresponding theorem on
K_{r}
was proved by us shortly afterwards.
This was announced in
[
6
]
.
The aim of the present paper is to publish our proof.
Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1
.

Let
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{n}
and
r
be any positive real numbers and let
K_{r}
be the matrix defined in (
4
).
Then

(i)

K_{r}
is singular if and only if
r
is an odd integer smaller than
n.

(ii)

When
r
is an odd integer smaller than or equal to
n,

the inertia of
K_{r}
is given as follows

\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=\begin{cases}\left(\lceil\frac{r}{2}\rceil,n-r,\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor\right)&r=1(\,\textrm{mod}\,4)\\
\left(\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor,n-r,\lceil\frac{r}{2}\rceil\right)&r=3\,(\textrm{mod}\,4).\end{cases}

(iii)

Suppose
k<r<k+2<n,

where
k
is an odd integer.
Then

\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=\begin{cases}\left(\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil,0,n-\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil\right)&k=1\,(\textrm{mod}\,4)\\
\left(n-\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil,0,\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil\right)&k=3\,(\textrm{mod}\,4).\end{cases}

(iv)

If
n
is odd,
then
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=\text{\rm In}\,K_{n}
for
r>n-2
;
and if
n
is even,
then
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=\text{\rm In}\,K_{n}=\left(\frac{n}{2},0,\frac{n}{2}\right)
for
r>n-1.

There is a striking similarity and a striking difference between the behaviour of the signs of eigenvalues of
L_{r}
and
K_{r}.

As
r
moves over
(0,\infty)
the eigenvalues of both flip signs at certain integral values of
r.

For
L_{r}
these flips take place at all integers
r\leq n-1,

and each time all but one eigenvalue change signs.
For
K_{r}
the flips take place at all odd integers
r\leq n-1.

At
r=1
all but one eigenvalue change signs,
and after that all but two eigenvalues change signs.

Figure 1.

Figure 1 is a schematic representation of the eigenvalues of
K_{r}
for
n=6
and
r\geq 0.

In an earlier paper
[
6
]
we studied the inertia of another family
P_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}(p_{i}+p_{j})^{r}\end{bmatrix}.

The structure of the proof there has been the template of subsequent works on this kind of problem.
See, e.g.,
[
4
]
and the recent work
[
13
]
on the Kraus matrix.
(Warning
:
The authors
[
13
]
use the symbol
K_{r}
for something different from our Kwong matrix).
Our proof here follows the same steps as in these papers
;

the details are different at some crucial points.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1

Two Hermitian matrices
A
and
B
are said to be
congruent
if there exists an invertible matrix
X
such that
B=X^{*}AX.

The Sylvester law of inertia says that
A
and
B
are congruent if and only if
\text{\rm In}\,A=\text{\rm In}\,B.

Let
D
be the diagonal matrix
D=\text{\rm diag}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}).
Then for every
r>0

K_{-r}=D^{-r}K_{r}D^{-r},

(6)

and hence,

\text{\rm In}\,K_{-r}=\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}.

(7)

The substitution
p_{i}=\text{\rm e}^{2x_{i}},

x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},
gives

K_{r}=\Delta\tilde{K}_{r}\Delta,

(8)

where
\Delta=\text{\rm diag}\left(\text{\rm e}^{(r-1)x_{1}},\ldots,\text{\rm e}^{(r-1)x_{n}}\right),
and

\tilde{K}_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\cosh\,r(x_{i}-x_{j})}{\cosh\,(x_{i}-x_{j})}\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

By Sylvester’s law
\text{\rm In}\,\tilde{K}_{r}=\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}.

When
n=2
we have

\tilde{K}_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\frac{\cosh\,r(x_{1}-x_{2})}{\cosh\,(x_{1}-x_{2})}\\
\frac{\cosh\,r(x_{1}-x_{2})}{\cosh\,(x_{1}-x_{2})}&1\end{bmatrix}.

So,
\det\,\tilde{K}_{r}=1-\frac{\cosh^{2}r(x_{1}-x_{2})}{\cosh^{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})}.

This is positive if
0<r<1,

zero if
r=1,

and negative if
r>1.

The inertia of
K_{r}
is
(2,0,0)
in the first case,

(1,1,0)
in the second case, and
(1,0,1)
in the third.
All assertions of Theorem
1
are thus valid in the case
n=2.

We will use the following extension of Sylvester’s law.
A proof is given in
[
6
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
n\geq r,

and let
A
be an
r\times r
Hermitian matrix and
X
an
r\times n
matrix of rank

r.
Then

{\rm In}\,\,X^{\ast}AX={\rm In}\,\,A+(0,n-r,0).

(10)

We now prove part (ii) of the theorem.
Let
r
be an odd integer,

r\leq n.
Then

\frac{p_{i}^{r}+p_{j}^{r}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}=p_{i}^{r-1}-p_{i}^{r-2}p_{j}+p_{i}^{r-3}p_{j}^{2}-\cdots+p_{j}^{r-1}.

So the matrix
K_{r}
can be factored as

K_{r}=W^{*}VW,

where
W
is the
r\times n
Vandermonde matrix given by

W=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots&p_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\vdots\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{1}^{r-1}&p_{2}^{r-1}&\cdots&p_{n}^{r-1}\end{bmatrix}.

and
V
is the
r\times r
antidiagonal matrix with entries
(1,-1,1,-1,\cdots,-1,1)

down its sinister diagonal.
So by the generalised Sylvester’s law (
10
) we have for every odd integer
r\leq n,

\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=\text{\rm In}\,V+(0,n-r,0).

The matrix
V
is nonsingular and its eigenvalues are
\pm 1.

In the case
r=1\,(\textrm{mod}\,4),

\text{\rm tr}\,V=1
and the multiplicity of
1
as an eigenvalue of
V
exceeds by one the multiplicity of
-1.

In the case
r=3\,(\textrm{mod}\,4),

\text{\rm tr}\,V=-1
and the multiplicity of
-1
as an eigenvalue of
V
exceeds by one the multiplicity of
1.

This establishes part (ii) of Theorem
1
.

Next, let
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}
be real numbers,
not all of which are zero,
and let
f
be the function on
(0,\infty)
defined as

f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\frac{x^{r}+p_{j}^{r}}{x+p_{j}}.

(11)

Theorem 3
.

Let
n
be an odd number.
Then for every positive real number
r>n-1,

the function
f
in equation (
11
)
has at most
n-1
zeros in
(0,\infty).

Proof.

Consider the function

g
defined as

g(x)=f(x)\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x+p_{j}).

(12)

Expanding the product,
we can write

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}x+\cdots+\alpha_{n-1}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle\ +\beta_{0}x^{r}+\beta_{1}x^{r+1}+\cdots+\beta_{n-1}x^{r+n-1}.

(13)

The function
g
can be written as

g(x)=x^{r}h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x),

where

h_{1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(x+p_{j})\textrm{ and }h_{2}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}p_{i}^{r}\prod_{j\neq i}(x+p_{j}).

Since both
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are Lagrange interpolation polynomials of degree at most
n-1
and not all
c_{i}
are zero,
neither of the polynomials
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
is identically zero.
Hence
g
is not identically zero.
Now, consider the function
g_{0}
defined as

g_{0}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\frac{x^{r}-p_{i}^{r}}{x-p_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x-p_{j}).

Then a calculation shows that

\displaystyle g_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}x+\cdots+\alpha_{n-2}x^{n-1}-\alpha_{n-1}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle\ +\beta_{0}x^{r}-\beta_{1}x^{r+1}+\cdots+\beta_{n-1}x^{r+n-1}.

(14)

By the Descartes rule of signs (
[
12
]
, p.46), the number of positive zeros of
g
is no more than the number of sign changes in the sequence of coefficients

\left(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n-1}\right).

Let this number of sign changes be
s,

and let
s_{0}
be the number of sign changes in the coefficients in (
14
).
Since
n
is odd, we have

s+s_{0}\leq 2n-1.

We know that
g_{0}
has at least
n
positive zeros
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}.

So,
s_{0}\geq n,

and hence
s\leq n-1.

Hence
g
has at most
n-1
positive zeros,
and therefore so does
f.

We can deduce the following.

Corollary 4
.

Let
n
be an odd number, and let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
and
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
be two
n
-tuples of distinct positive numbers.
Then for every
r>n-1,

the
n\times n
matrix

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{p_{i}^{r}+q_{j}^{r}}{p_{i}+q_{j}}\end{bmatrix}

(15)

is nonsingular.
So, in particular if
n
is odd,
then for every
r>n-1,
the matrix
K_{r}
is nonsingular.

Proof.

If the matrix (
15
) is singular, then there exists a nonzero tuple
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
such that

f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\frac{x^{r}+q_{j}^{r}}{x+q_{j}}

has at least
n
zeros
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}.

But this is not possible by Theorem
3
.
So, the matrix (
15
) and hence, the matrix
K_{r}
is nonsingular for all odd
n
and
r>n-1.

We complete the proof of Theorem
1

using a ”snaking” process
:

the validity of the theorem is extended by alternatively increasing
n
and
r.

For any positive numbers
p
and
q
and any real
r,
we have

\frac{p^{r}+q^{r}}{p+q}=p^{r-1}-p\frac{p^{r-2}+q^{r-2}}{p+q}q+q^{r-1}.

This gives us the identity

K_{r}=D^{r-1}E-DK_{r-2}D+ED^{r-1},

(16)

where
D
is the diagonal matrix
\text{\rm diag}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})

and
E
the matrix all whose entries are one.
For
1\leq j\leq n,

let
\mathcal{H}_{j}
be the subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left\{x:\sum x_{i}=0,\sum p_{i}x_{i}=0,\ldots,\sum p_{i}^{j-1}x_{i}=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{x:Ex=0,EDx=0,\ldots,ED^{j-1}x=0\right\}.

Evidently,
\textrm{dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{j}=n-j
and
\mathcal{H}_{j+1}\subset\mathcal{H}_{j}.

It will be convenient to use the notation
K_{r}^{(n)}
to indicate an
n\times n
matrix of the type
K_{r}.

When the superscript
n
is not used,
it will be understood that a statement about
K_{r}
is true for all
n.

Recall that
K_{r}
is known to be positive definite for
0<r<1.

The relation (
6
) then shows that it has the same property for
-1<r<0.

K_{0}
is the Cauchy matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{p_{i}+p_{j}}\end{bmatrix}
and is positive definite.
Thus
K_{r}
is positive definite for
-1<r<1.

Now let
1<r<3.

Then
-1<r-2<1.

Using the identity (
16
) we see that
if
x
is a nonzero vector in
\mathcal{H}_{1},

then

\langle x,K_{r}x\rangle=-\langle Dx,K_{r-2}Dx\rangle<0.

So, the matrix
K_{r}
is conditionally negative definite and has at least
n-1
negative eigenvalues.
Since all entries of
K_{r}
are positive, it has at least one positive eigenvalue.
Thus

\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=(1,0,n-1),

(17)

for
1<r<3.

By Corollary
4
,

K_{r}^{(3)}
is nonsingular for
r>2.

So,
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}^{(3)}
does not change for
r>2.

This shows that
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}^{(3)}=(1,0,2)
for all
r>1.

So, the theorem is established when
n=3.

Now let
n>3
and
3<r<5.

Using the identity (
16
) and the case
1<r<3
of the theorem that has been established
we see that if
x
is a nonzero vector in
\mathcal{H}_{2},

then
\langle x,K_{r}x\rangle>0.

So, the matrix
K_{r}
has at least
n-2
positive eigenvalues.
By the
n=3
case already proved,
we know that
K_{r}
has a
3\times 3
principal submatrix
with two negative eigenvalues.
So, by Cauchy’s interlacing principle,

K_{r}
must have at least two negative eigenvalues.
We conclude that

\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}=(n-2,0,2),\textrm{ if }3<r<5.

(18)

In particular, this shows that
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}^{(5)}=(3,0,2)
if
3<r<5,

and since
K_{r}^{(5)}
is nonsingular for
r>4,

it has the same inertia for all
r>3.

So, Theorem
1
is established when
n=5.

Next consider the case
n=4.

Let
r>3.

The matrix
K_{r}^{(4)}
has a principal submatrix
K_{r}^{(3)}
whose inertia is
(1,0,2).

So, by the interlacing principle
K_{r}^{(4)}
has at least two negative eigenvalues.
On the other hand
K_{r}^{(4)}
is a principal submatrix of
K_{r}^{(5)}

whose inertia is
(3,0,2).

So, again by the interlacing principle
K_{r}^{(4)}
has at least two positive eigenvalues.
Thus
\text{\rm In}\,K_{r}^{(4)}=(2,0,2)
for all
r>3,

and Theorem
1
is established for
n=4.

This line of reasoning can be continued.
Use the space
\mathcal{H}_{3}
at the next stage to go to the interval
5<r<7.

Then use the established case
n\leq 5
to extend the validity of the theorem to first the case
n=7,
and then
n=6.

3.
Remarks

1.

In Theorem 1.1(v) of
[
4
]
it was shown that all nonzero eigenvalues of
L_{r}
are simple.
We have not been able to prove a corresponding statement for
K_{r}.

2.

An
n\times n
real matrix
A
is said to be
strictly sign-regular
(SSR) if for every
1\leq k\leq n,
all
k\times k
sub-determinants of

A
are nonzero and have the same sign. If this is true for all
1\leq k\leq m
for some
m<n,
then we say
A
is in the class
\textrm{SSR}_{\mbox{m}}.
See
[
9
]
for a detailed study of such matrices.
In
[
4
]
it was shown that the matrix
L_{r}
is in the class
\textrm{SSR}_{\mbox{r}}
if
r=1,2,\ldots,n-1,

and in the class SSR for all other
r>0.

This fact was then used to prove the simplicity of nonzero eigenvalues of
L_{r}.

Let
n=4
and consider the matrix
K_{3}(1,2,5,10).

It can be seen that the leading
2\times 2
principal subdeterminant of this matrix is
-5,
while the determinant of the top right
2\times 2
submatrix is
35.

So this matrix is not in the class
\textrm{SSR}_{\mbox{2}}.

3.

Let
p_{1}<p_{2}
and
q_{1}<q_{2}
be two ordered pairs of distinct positive numbers such that
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}\cap\{q_{1},q_{2}\}
is nonempty.
With a little work it can be shown that the determinant of the
2\times 2
matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{p_{i}^{r}+q_{j}^{r}}{p_{i}+q_{j}}\end{bmatrix}
is positive if
0<r<1
and negative if
r>1.

Using this one sees that for
n=3
and
r\neq 1,
the matrix
K_{r}
is SSR.

4.

There is a curious and intriguing connection between the inertia of
K_{r}
and that of another family.
For
r\geq 0,

let
B_{r}
be the
n\times n
matrix

B_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}|p_{i}-p_{j}|^{r}\end{bmatrix}.

(19)

This family has been studied widely in connection with interpolation of scattered data and splines.
The inertias of these matrices were studied by Dyn, Goodman and Micchelli in
[
8
]
.
In view of our theorem their result can be stated as

\text{\rm In}\,B_{r}=\text{\rm In}\,K_{r+1}

(20)

for all
r\geq 0.

It will be good to have an understanding of what leads to this remarkable equality.
By Sylvester’s law (
20
) is equivalent to saying that
B_{r}
and
K_{r+1}
are congruent.
In a recent work
[
11
]
the authors construct an explicit congruence between these two matrices, thus providing an alternative proof of (
20
).
