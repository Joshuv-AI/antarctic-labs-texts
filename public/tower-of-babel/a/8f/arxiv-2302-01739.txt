Abstract
The reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) is an emerging technology that changes how wireless networks are perceived, therefore its potential benefits and applications are currently under intense research and investigation. In this letter, we focus on electromagnetically consistent models for RISs inheriting from a recently proposed model based on mutually coupled loaded wire dipoles. While existing related research focuses on free-space wireless channels thereby ignoring interactions between RIS and scattering objects present in the propagation environment, we introduce an RIS -aided channel model that is applicable to more realistic scenarios, where the scattering objects are modeled as loaded wire dipoles. By adjusting the parameters of the wire dipoles, the properties of general natural and engineered material objects can be modeled. Based on this model, we introduce a provably convergent and efficient iterative algorithm that jointly optimizes the RIS and transmitter configurations to maximize the system sum-rate. Extensive numerical results show the net performance improvement provided by the proposed method compared with existing optimization algorithms.



I
Introduction

The
reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)
technology is gaining growing attention in academia and industry owing to its ability to turn the stochastic nature of the wireless propagation channel—always conceived as a black-box—into an optimizable variable
[
1
]
.
RISs
are dynamic engineered metasurfaces, which are made of inexpensive scattering elements (unit cells) and low-complex/limited-power electronic circuits, which are spaced at sub-wavelength distances: the essentials are their dynamic reconfigurability in terms of scattering parameters performed in a nearly passive manner.
In this context, adequate communication and channel models for RISs are of paramount importance to obtain a deep understanding of their achievable performance and to fully exploit their potential in future wireless networks
[
2
]
.

Related work.
In sub-wavelength implementations, there is an impelling need of considering the mutual coupling interactions among the unit cells: the discrete dipole approximation (DDA)—which is an analytical method for modeling the electromagnetic scattering from arbitrarily shaped objects
[
3
,
4
]
—comes to help. In particular, the authors of
[
5
]
have recently introduced a mutual coupling-aware and electromagnetically consistent model for
RIS
with arbitrarily spaced unit cells, which are modeled as loaded wire dipoles. This model is based on the concept of mutually coupled antennas and accounts for near- and far-field channel conditions, thus offering great analytical tractability while ensuring electromagnetic consistency. Indeed, by capitalizing on the DDA, the polarizabilities of general natural or engineered materials (as is the case for
RISs
) are modeled by adjusting the parameters of the wire dipoles and the loads
[
4
,
6
,
3
]
.

In
[
7
]
, the authors have introduced a closed-form analytical formulation of the model in
[
5
]
, while in
[
8
]
the model is exploited to derive the optimal
RIS
configuration that maximizes the received power in a single-user and single-antenna setup. A closed-form expression is given in the absence of mutual coupling, whereas a convergent iterative algorithm is proposed in the presence of mutual coupling. Such optimization framework is generalized in
[
9
]
for the multi-
RIS
multi-user
multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO)
interference channel.

With the exception of
[
9
]
, such works are applicable to free-space propagation in the presence of an RIS. On the other hand, the authors of
[
9
]
model the multipath from
environmental scattering objects (ESOs)
by relying on a statistical fading model, which is superimposed (additive component) to the RIS-induced scattering. Inherently, this approach does not account for the interactions (e.g., multiple reflections) between the RIS and the scattering objects. Nevertheless, recent results show that multiple reflections between closely-located scatterers (engineered or not) cannot be ignored
[
10
]
.

Contributions.
In this letter, we further extend the model in
[
5
]
to account for the multipath propagation originated from the presence of
ESOs
, which are modeled as a collection of loaded wire dipoles according to the DDA. In particular, the parameters of the dipoles modeling the
ESOs
(e.g., the length and the loads) are chosen to match the material properties of the objects
[
4
]
, while the RIS loads are tunable on-demand. The proposed approach inherently captures the interactions between the RIS and the
ESOs
unlike the additive model in
[
9
]
, which we prove is obtained as a particular case.

Based on the proposed model, we formulate a provably convergent optimization algorithm for computing the optimal load impedances of the RIS and the transmit precoding that maximize the
sum mean squared error (SMSE)
in a multiple-input single-output multi-user network, dubbed as
SARIS
. Compared with the state-of-the-art optimization algorithm introduced in
[
9
]
, SARIS is shown to provide a higher sum-rate while requiring fewer iterations and less execution time.

II-A

Model Novelty: Modeling the
ESOs

The analytical expression of

in (

) clearly unveils the difference between the proposed model for the

RIS
designs that may fail to fully exploit its properties. Moreover, since the load impedances
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{US}}
can be arbitrarily chosen to mimic any natural material, while all the other self and mutual impedances depend mainly on the geometry of the scenario (e.g., see
[
7
,
11
]
), the proposed model subsumes deterministic and statistical multipath channel models, i.e., in the case where the locations of the
ESOs
are assumed to be unknown (e.g., random).

III
Problem Formulation and Solution

To optimize
\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}}
subject to the constraint
\mathrm{R}_{0}\geq 0
in (
5
) and
\mathbf{W}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{M\times L}
subject to the constraint
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq P
, we consider the
SMSE
as objective function. The motivation for using the
SMSE
and its relation with the optimization of the sum-rate in (
II
) is detailed in
[
13
]
. The
SMSE
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SMSE}\!

\displaystyle=\!\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{L}|\big(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell}+\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\ell}(\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}})\big)\mathbf{w}_{k}|^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle-2\!\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\!\mathrm{Re}\{\!\big(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell}\!+\!\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\ell}(\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}})\big)\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\}\!+\!L(1\!+\!\sigma_{n}^{2}).

Hence, the optimization problem is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{W},\,\,\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\ell,\,\,k=1}^{L}|\big(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell}+\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\ell}(\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}})\big)\mathbf{w}_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-2\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Re}\{\big(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell}+\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\ell}(\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}})\big)\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\}\vskip 7.11317pt

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\!

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq P,\,\,\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\}\!=\!\mathrm{R}_{0},\,\,\mathrm{Im}\{\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\}\!\in\!\mathcal{Q}^{N}\!.

(18)

Due to the presence of the
ESOs
, the optimization problem in (
III
) is more challenging to solve as compared with those in
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
. In fact, the matrix
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}
is, in general, not a diagonal matrix even if the mutual coupling between the
ESOs
is negligible, i.e.,
\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}}
is diagonal. This is because the matrices
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}
are not diagonal matrices, in general. To tackle the problem in (
III
), we decouple it into two convex sub-problems that are solved iteratively, as detailed next.

III-A

Precoding Optimization

First, we solve the problem in (
III
) with respect to the optimization variable
\mathbf{W}
while keeping
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}
fixed. The resulting problem is convex and its solution is found by evaluating the KKT conditions. In this regard, let the Lagrangian and its gradient be

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W},\mu)

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}^{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\!)\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}^{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\!)\mathbf{W}\}

\displaystyle+\mu(\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-P),

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W},\mu)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}^{\mathrm{H}}+\mu\mathbf{I}_{M}\big)\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}},

(19)

respectively. Hence, the optimal closed-form solution is given by letting the expression in (
19
) to zero as
[
13
, Eqs. (28), (29)]

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}})

\displaystyle\!=\!\bigg(\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}^{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\!)\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\!)\!+\!\frac{L\sigma_{n}^{2}}{P}\mathbf{I}_{M}\!\bigg)^{\!-1}\!\!\mathbf{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E}}^{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}\!)

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}})

\displaystyle\!=\!\sqrt{P}\,\overline{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}})/(\|\overline{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}})\|_{\mathrm{F}})\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{M\times L}.

(20)

III-B

RIS
Optimization

Then, we solve the problem in (
III
) with respect to the optimization variable
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}
while keeping
\mathbf{W}
fixed. To this end, we devise an iterative algorithm that, similar to
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
, leverages the repeated application of the Neumann series for efficiently tackling the inversion of
\big(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}}\big)
. With respect to
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
, however, we introduce an improved algorithm that accounts for the necessary conditions to make the Neumann series accurate by design. This is detailed next.

Specifically, at the
(i+1)
th iteration, the optimization variable
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}
is set to
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}^{i+1}=\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}^{i}+\mathbf{\Delta}
, where
\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathrm{diag}[\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{H}}]\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{N\times N}
is a diagonal matrix containing small improvements to the
RIS
configuration. With this approximation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\ell}^{i+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}})=-\mathbf{z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L},\ell}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\big(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}}^{i+1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}

\displaystyle\approx-\mathbf{z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L},\ell}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\Big[\mathbf{G}^{i}-\mathbf{G}^{i}\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{G}^{i}\Big]\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}

\displaystyle=\!\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{T}}\big[\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L},\ell}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\mathbf{G}^{i})\mathbf{G}^{i}\!-\!\mathbf{z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L},\ell}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\mathbf{G}^{i}\big]\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}

(21)

where
\mathbf{G}^{i}\triangleq(\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}}^{i})^{-1}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{T}}&1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell}+\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\ell}^{i+1}(\mathbf{Z}_{\textnormal{\tiny{RIS}}})\big)\mathbf{w}_{j}\approx\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{H}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{j}

(22)

with

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L},\ell}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\mathbf{G}^{i})\mathbf{G}^{i}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{E2E},\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell}-\mathbf{z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L},\ell}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}}\mathbf{G}^{i}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{S}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{O}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{T}\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}\end{bmatrix}

(23)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R},\ell})^{\mathrm{T}}&(\bar{\mathbf{h}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell})^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{N+1\times M}.

(24)

As a result, the
SMSE
can be approximated as follows:

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{SMSE}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle\approx\displaystyle\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{L}|\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{H}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{k}|^{2}-2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Re}\{\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{H}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\}

(25)

The approximation in (
21
) is accurate provided that the condition
\|\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{G}^{i}\|\ll 1
is fulfilled at each iteration. In
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
, this condition is taken into account by choosing very small values for the elements of
\boldsymbol{\delta}
. This approach, however, ignores the actual values of
\mathbf{G}^{i}
. To ensure that the Neumann series approximation is accurate by design at each iteration of the algorithm, we include the condition
\|\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{G}^{i}\|\ll 1
as a constraint of the optimization problem to be solved. This leads to the following simplified and robust problem formulation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\ell,k=1}^{L}|\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{H}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{k}|^{2}-2\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Re}\{\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{H}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{N+1}=1,\quad|{\delta}_{n}|\leq{1}/{\|\mathbf{G}^{i}\|},\,n=1,\ldots,N

(26)

where the constraint on
|{\delta}_{n}|
ensures that the Neumann series approximation is sufficiently accurate at each iteration. As in (
19
)–(
20
), the convex optimization problem in (
III-B
) is solved by setting the gradient of the Lagrangian to zero as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\big(\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{\ell}^{i+1}\mathbf{W}\|^{2}-2\mathrm{Re}\{\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{\ell}^{i+1}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\}\big)

\displaystyle+\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}(|\delta_{n}|^{2}-1/\|\mathbf{G}^{i}\|)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\big(\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{\ell}^{i+1}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}(\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{\ell}^{i+1})^{\mathrm{H}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}-\bar{\mathbf{H}}_{\ell}^{i+1}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\big)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}},

(27)

which has the following closed-form solution
[
13
, Eq. (22)]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\big(\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R},\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}-\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R},\ell}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}(\bar{\mathbf{h}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d},\ell})^{\mathrm{H}}\big)

(28)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R},\ell}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}(\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{i+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R},\ell})^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N}\Big)^{-1}\mathbf{b}

(29)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}

\displaystyle={\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}/\big({\max\,\{|\tilde{\delta}_{n}|\}_{n=1}^{N}}\,{\|\mathbf{G}^{i}\|}\big)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{N\times 1}.

(30)

The normalization in (
30
) aims to strike a trade-off between the accuracy of the Neumann series approximation and the speed of convergence of the algorithm, i.e., the optimum is obtained when the inequality constraint in (
III-B
) is an equality. Hence, the small improvements
\boldsymbol{\delta}
are chosen as large as possible depending on the matrix
\mathbf{G}^{i}
at each iteration.

The complete algorithm that iterates between the solutions in (
20
) and (
30
), dubbed as
SARIS
, is provided in Algorithm
1
. Specifically, once (
30
) is computed, the value of
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}
is updated as
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}^{i+1}=\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}^{i}+j\mathrm{Im}\{\mathbf{\Delta}\}
, with
\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{H}})
, in order to preserve the constraint on the real part of
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}
. Also, the imaginary part of
\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RIS}}^{i+1}
is projected onto the feasible set
\mathcal{Q}^{N}
. Compared with existing solutions, SARIS benefits from the inherent low-complexity of Algorithm
1
, since the latter iterates between two simple closed-form expressions.

III-C

Computational Complexity and Convergence Analysis

The computational complexity of (
20
) and (
30
) is
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}(M+3L))
and
\mathcal{O}(N(3N^{2}+N(L+1)+3LM+M+L))
, respectively. The complexity of (
30
) is dominated by the inversion of an
N\times N
matrix and is greater than the complexity of (
20
), since usually
N\gg M
. Therefore, the total complexity of Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{O}(N(3N^{2}+N(L+1)+3LM+M+L))
multiplied by the number of required iterations to converge.

The proposed alternating optimization algorithm decouples the original non-convex problem in (
III
) into a series of convex sub-problems in the two optimization variables taken separately. Hence, the global objective function, i.e., the
SMSE
, is a non-increasing function across two consecutive iterations. Moreover, since it is, by definition, lower-bounded by zero, Algorithm
1
converges to a critical point of the problem in (
III
), which is a sufficient but not necessary condition for optimality
[
13
,
9
]
.

V
Conclusions

In this letter, we have generalized a recently proposed model for RISs, based on mutually coupled loaded wire dipoles, to account for the presence of
ESOs
, i.e., the multipath from scattering objects. The proposed approach relies on the DDA for scattering objects, and the interactions between the RIS elements and the
ESOs
are explictly taken into account. Based on the obtained end-to-end channel model, an efficient algorithm for optimizing the precoding at the transmitter and the configuration of the RIS, dubbed as SARIS, has been proposed. Numerical results demonstrate that it outperforms benchmark schemes available in the literature.
