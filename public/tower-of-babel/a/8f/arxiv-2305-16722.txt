MEASURE CONTRACTION PROPERTY, CURVATURE EXPONENT AND GEODESIC DIMENSION OF SUB-FINSLER ℓ p \ell^{{p}} -HEISENBERG GROUPS
Samuël Borza, Kenshiro Tashiro

Abstract
We initiate the study of synthetic curvature-dimension bounds in sub-Finsler geometry. More specifically, we investigate the measure contraction property 𝖬𝖢𝖯 ⁡ ( K , N ) \mathsf{MCP}(K,N) , and the geodesic dimension on the Heisenberg group equipped with an ℓ p \ell^{p} -sub-Finsler norm. We show that for p ∈ ( 2 , ∞ ] p\in(2,\infty] , the ℓ p \ell^{p} -Heisenberg group fails to satisfy any of the measure contraction properties. On the other hand, if p ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) p\in(1,2) , then it satisfies the measure contraction property 𝖬𝖢𝖯 ⁡ ( K , N ) \mathsf{MCP}(K,N) if and only if K ≤ 0 K\leq 0 and N ≥ N p N\geq N_{p} , where the curvature exponent N p N_{p} is strictly greater than 2 ​ q + 1 2q+1 ( q q being the Hölder conjugate of p p ). We also prove that the geodesic dimension of the ℓ p \ell^{p} -Heisenberg group is min ⁡ ( 2 ​ q + 2 , 5 ) \min(2q+2,5) for p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) . As a consequence, we provide the first example of a metric measure space where there is a gap between the curvature exponent and the geodesic dimension.

Introduction

Sub-Finsler geometry is a broad generalization of Riemannian geometry that encompasses Finsler geometry and sub-Riemannian geometry. The Heisenberg group, the prototype example of sub-Riemannian geometry, can be endowed with a sub-Finsler metric modelled on the
\ell^{p}
-norm of the Euclidian space. This
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group is the focus of this work. We will study it from the point of view of the analysis of metric measures spaces, investigating the validity of a synthetic curvature-dimension condition and computing a notion of dimension relevant in metric geometry.

Synthetic definitions of lower curvature bounds based on the theory of optimal transport have been introduced to take into account non-smooth metric spaces. Roughly speaking, the optimal way to transport a mass, i.e. a probability measure, to another one in a metric measure space is characterised by a Wasserstein geodesic on the set of probability measures. The seminal contributions by Lott–Villani and Sturm showed in
[
LV09
]
and
[
Stu06
,
Stu06a
]
independently that in Riemannian geometry, a lower bound
K
on the Ricci curvature and an upper bound
N
on the topological dimension are equivalent to a type of
K
-convexity for the

N
-entropy along the Wasserstein geodesics. This alternative characterisation of curvature-dimension bounds can be expressed solely in terms of the Riemannian distance and volume, without explicitly referencing a differential structure. This observation leads to the introduction of the
curvature dimension condition

\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
on general metric measure spaces.

The equivalence between a curvature-dimension condition and an entropic convexity along Wasserstein geodesics can be extended to Finsler manifolds with sufficient regularity. In this setting, Ohta showed in
[
Oht09
]
that
\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
is equivalent to having a lower bound
K
of the
N
-weighted Ricci curvature. Here, the
N
-weighted Ricci curvature is a tensor that depends on a given smooth volume form since, in Finsler geometry, there is a priori no canonical smooth measure.

In sub-Riemannian geometry, the curvature-dimension condition is known to fail. The first result in this direction was given by Juillet in
[
Jui09
]
which proved that the Heisenberg group equipped with its sub-Riemannian structure and the Lebesgue measure does not satisfy
\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
for any
K\in\mathds{R}
and any
N\geq 1
. The intuition behind this is that if we view the Heisenberg group as a Gromov–Hausdorff limit of a sequence of Riemannian manifolds, its Ricci curvature will diverge, indicating that they are not so called
Ricci limit spaces
. This result has then been generalised to all sub-Riemannian manifolds in
[
Jui20
]
(for strict sub-Riemannian structures) and in
[
RS23
]
(for strictly positive smooth measures).

Many examples of sub-Riemannian manifolds, however, are known to satisfy a relaxation of the curvature-dimension condition, called the
measure contraction property
introduced by Ohta in
[
Oht07
]
(see
Definition

3
). Given a metric measure space
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
and a Borel subset
\Omega\subseteq X
with
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
, the
t
-geodesic homothety
\Omega_{t}
from a point
x\in X
is the set of all
t
-intermediate points
\gamma(t)
of all minimising constant speed geodesics
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to X
joining
x
to
\Omega
. The measure contraction property
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
, where
K
is still meant to represent a synthetic “lower bound on the Ricci curvature” and
N
an “upper bound on the dimension”, consists of a type of convexity of the map
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}):\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{R}
. When
K=0
, and if
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
has negligible cut locus (see
Definition

1
), then the
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
is equivalent to
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})\geq t^{N}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
. Juillet demonstrated in
[
Jui09
]
that the three-dimensional Heisenberg group satisfies the measure contraction property
\mathsf{MCP}(0,5)
. Moreover, the pair
(K,N)=(0,5)
is optimal in the sense that the Heisenberg group does not satisfy
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
whenever
K>0
or
N<5
. The measure contraction property has also been found to hold in ideal Carnot groups
[
Rif13
]
, corank 1 Carnot groups
[
Riz16
]
, in generalised H-type Carnot groups
[
BR18
]
, or in the Grushin plane
[
BR19
]
. The optimal constant
N
, i.e. the infimum one, such that
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
holds is called the
curvature exponent
. In the present work, we investigate the measure contraction property and the curvature exponent in sub-Finsler geometry for the first time. It is worth noting that in Finsler geometry, the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
condition is implied by having a lower bound
K
on the
N
-Ricci curvature, as shown in
[
Oht09
, Theorem 1.2]
.

The
geodesic dimension
, on the other hand, is determined by the asymptotic rate of growth of the volume of measurable set under geodesic homotheties. When a given metric measure space has sufficient regularity, it is the optimal
N
such that
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})\sim t^{N}
as
t\to 0^{+}
.
Since the geodesic dimension considers the asymptotic behavior of
\Omega_{t}
as
t\to 0^{+}
,
it explains more local features than the measure contraction property.
It is related with the curvature exponent:
it was shown in
[
Riz16
]
that the geodesic dimension is always a lower bound for the curvature exponent (see
Theorem

8
).
Moreover, in many cases, see
[
Rif13
,
Riz16
,
BR18
,
BR19
]
, the curvature exponent is equal to the geodesic dimension.

In this work, we initiate the study of synthetic curvature-dimension bounds and geodesic dimension in sub-Finsler geometry. We examine the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group for any
p\in\mathopen{[}1,\infty\mathclose{]}
, which consists of the Heisenberg group equipped with an
\ell^{p}
-sub-Finsler norm and the Lebesgue measure. It is a generalisation of the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group, which is the
\ell^{2}
-Heisenberg group in our notation. The curvature exponent and the geodesic dimension of the usual sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group are known to be
5
, see
[
Jui09
]
. The choice to focus on this family of sub-Finsler spaces is motivated by the fact that they exhibit a variety of behaviors that will be seen to be determining factors in relation with the measure contraction property, the curvature exponent, and the geodesic dimension. The
\ell^{1}
- and
\ell^{\infty}
-Heisenberg groups have
branching
geodesics, and have large (non-negligible) cut locus. The
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group have
C^{2}
geodesics if
p<2
, while they are only
C^{1}
if
p>2
.

In the strictly convex case, i.e., when
p\in\mathopen{(}1,\infty\mathclose{)}
, the main techniques are the following. By Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle, we are able to write an exponential map
\mathrm{exp}^{t}_{x}
, that is, the projection of the Hamiltonian flow at time
t
from
x
. It is then used to estimate the volume of the
t
-geodesic homothety

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})=\int_{\mathrm{exp}^{-1}(\Omega)}\mathrm{Jac}\circ\mathrm{exp}^{t}_{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{3},

where the integrand is the Jacobian determinant of the exponential map
\mathrm{exp}^{t}_{x}
at time
t
. The definition of the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
is then shown to be equivalent to a differential inequality on
\mathrm{Jac}\circ\mathrm{exp}^{t}
(see
Proposition

40
). In Finsler or sub-Finsler geometry, when it can be well-defined, the exponential map is typically not smooth. In our case, it will be smooth except for a negligible set, and studying what happens near non smooth points will be crucial.

The geometry underlying the structure of the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group is described in
Section

2
using special trigonometric functions known as Shelupsky’s
p
-trigonometric functions. They can be defined geometrically, as in
[
Lok19
]
, or seen to be solutions to a kind of
1
-dimensional
q
-Laplace equation, where
q:=(1-1/p)^{-1}
is the Hölder conjugate of
p
, which is studied in
[
PU03
,
EGL12
,
GK14
]
and the references therein. We analyse the geometric and geodesic structure of the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group in
Section

3
. As a byproduct of our work, we establish the cotangent injectivity radius of the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg in
Proposition

25
. The Jacobian determinant is written out explicitly in
Lemma

27
, and we study it comprehensively in
Section

4
.

Regarding the curvature exponent of the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group,
we prove following results.

Theorem A

(Curvature exponent for
p\in(1,\infty)\setminus\{2\}
,
Theorem

42
and
Theorem

)
.

1)

When
p\in\mathopen{(}1,2\mathclose{)}
, the curvature exponent

of the sub-Finsler Heisenberg group equipped with the Lebesgue measure is finite and greater than

.
In other words,

if and only if

.

2)

When

, the sub-Finsler

-Heisenberg group equipped with the Lebesgue measure does not satisfy the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
for any

and any

.

Note that the case

was studied in
[

]
: the
\ell^{2}
-Heisenberg group satisfies
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
if and only if
K\leq 0
and
N\geq 5
. Notice that we do not know the exact value of the curvature exponent
N_{p}
when
p\in\mathopen{(}1,2\mathclose{)}
and the value

is a non-optimal lower bound on

. The failure of the measure contraction property for

is linked to the loss of regularity of the exponential map. Geometrically, when

, geodesics from a given point have

-corners, and the

-homothety
\Omega_{t}
infinitesimally shrinks to a small subset when passing through these corners.
This is also observed from the fact that the derivative of the Jacobian determinant diverges to the infinity at the

.
When

, the exponential map is

However,

at non-smooth corners,
seen as a curve in the Euclidean space.
The lower bound

for the curvature exponent is observed along the horizontal line geodesic tangent to such non-smooth corners.
Indeed, we can observe that the

along such a straight horizontal geodesic is small as

.
This observation reflects the fact that the Jacobian determinant and its differental vanishes on such straight horizontal lines,

However, this lower bound

is not the curvature exponent.
Indeed, we will see that an exponent greater than

Furthermore,

If confirmed, this would represent a significant contrast to the sub-Riemannian context, where the curvature exponent has been consistently observed along horizontal lines. We leave open the question of which geodesics and at what value the curvature exponent is truly realized in the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group.

As for the geodesics dimension, we have the following.

Theorem B

(Geodesic dimension for

.

1)

When

.

When

While the measure contraction property relies on the differentiability properties of the Jacobian determinant of the exponential map, the geodesic dimension is related to its discontinuous singularity. If

, then the Jacobian determinant is discontinuous and diverges to infinity on the horizontal line tangent to the non-smooth corner (see

Finally, in

-Heisenberg group are isometric metric measure spaces, so we only need to consider one of them.

Theorem C

(

and geodesic dimension for

)
.

The
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group (resp. the

-Heisenberg group) equipped with the Lebesgue measure does not satisfy

for all

Because of the fact that the norms

are convex but not strictly convex, the corresponding sub-Finsler structures on the Heisenberg group is highly branching, and has non-negligible cut locus.
Therefore, an exponential map can not be well-defined as a diffeomorphism onto a full-measure subset of the Heisenberg group.
However, since the norm is polygonal,
its geodesics can be explicitly written by using a quadratic polynomial (see
[

,
DM14

for a treatment of sub-Finsler Heisenberg groups with polygonal norms).
By using an explicit formula for the geodesics,

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})

As will be seen in the proof of

, this will force the geodesic dimension of the space to be as small as it possibly can,

which is the Hausdorff dimension of the Heisenberg group. Indeed, Theorem 6 of

shows that the Hausdorff dimension is a lower bound on the curvature exponent.

The results of this work, summarised in
Figure

, are both unexpected and new. They deviate from what would typically be expected in sub-Riemannian geometry, where the curvature exponent and the geodesic dimension are consistently observed to be equal, as mentioned above. In the case of strictly sub-Finsler

-Heisenberg groups (i.e., when

), we show that there is always a gap between the geodesic dimension and the curvature exponent. Moreover, it neatly differs from Finsler geometry as well. For instance, in Finsler spaces
(\mathds{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
equipped with any norm
\|\cdot\|
, the curvature exponent and geodesic dimension both equal

.

We anticipate that numerous specificities observed in the case of the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group, such the impact of non-smoothness or branchingness, will constitute shared characteristics of sub-Finsler geometry. We would also like to emphasize the fact that, although the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group appears to be a natural extension of sub-Riemannian geodesics, we have discovered that it is far from being a straightforward generalization. In addition to being considerably more technical, there are profound underlying differences (albeit the fact that the
p
-trigonometry functions look notationally similar the usual sine and cosine functions).

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Measure contraction property and geodesic dimension

On a Riemannian manifold
(M,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
, the condition

\mathrm{Ric}\geq K,\text{ and}\dim(M)\leq N

(1)

is often a natural assumption taken to prove significant theorems such as Bonnet-Myers theorem, Bishop-Gromov inequality, and Lévy-Gromov’s isoperimetric inequality. The measure contraction property
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
is one of the popular synthetic notions of curvature-dimension bounds generalising
Equation

1
to abstract metric measure spaces. For reasons that we will soon see, this is the condition that we investigate in the present work for the Heisenberg group equipped with a sub-Finsler structure.

Defining the measure contraction property in full generality would require some understanding of optimal transport. In order to make this work more concise, we will therefore only provide an equivalent definition for spaces that have a negligible cut locus. For a more detailed explanation of the measure contraction property, we recommend that the reader refer to
[
Oht07
]
and
[
Stu06a
]
.

For the rest of this section,
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
will denote a geodesic metric measure space.

Definition 1
.

We say that
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
has negligible cut locus if for any
x\in X
, there exists a negligible set
\mathcal{C}(x)
and a measurable map
\Phi_{x}:X\setminus\mathcal{C}(x)\times\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to M
, such that the curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to M:t\mapsto\Phi_{x}(y,t)
is the unique minimising geodesic between
x
and
y
.

For a bounded Borel set
\Omega\subseteq X
such that
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
and
t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, the
t
-intermediate set
\Omega_{t}
from a point
x\in X
is the set

\Omega_{t}:=\left\{\gamma(t)\mid\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to X\text{ constant speed minimising geodesic},\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)\in\Omega\right\}.

It is not difficult to see that if
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
has negligible cut locus, then the
t
-intermediate set can be expressed, up to a set of measure zero, as

\Omega_{t}=\left\{\Phi_{x}(y,t)\mid y\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{C}(x)\right\}.

Definition 2
.

We say that
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
is
non-branching
if two minimising constant speed geodesics
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to X
are identically equal whenever
\gamma_{1}|_{\mathopen{[}a,b\mathclose{]}}=\gamma_{2}|_{\mathopen{[}a,b\mathclose{]}}
for some
\mathopen{[}a,b\mathclose{]}\subseteq\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
.

For any
K\in\mathds{R}
, define the function

\mathsf{s}_{K}(t):=\begin{cases}\sin(\sqrt{K}t)/\sqrt{K},&\text{if}\ K>0\\
t,&\text{if}\ K=0\\
\sinh(\sqrt{-K}t)/\sqrt{-K},&\text{if}\ K<0\end{cases}.

Definition 3

(
[
Oht07
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

Let
K\in\mathds{R}
and
N>1
, or
K\neq 0
and
N=1
. A geodesic metric measure space
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
with negligible cut locus satisfies the
(K,N)
-measure contraction property
, or
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
, if for all
x\in X
, for all measurable set
\Omega\subseteq X
with
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<0
(and
\Omega\subseteq B(x,\pi\sqrt{(N-1)/K})
, and for all
t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, it holds

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})\geq\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{\mathsf{s}_{K}(t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)/\sqrt{N-1})}{\mathsf{s}_{K}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)/\sqrt{N-1})}\right]^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}(y),

(2)

where by convention
0/0=1
, and the term in square bracket is 1 if
K\leq 0
and
N=1
.

Remark 4
.

The condition
\Omega\subseteq B(x,\pi\sqrt{(N-1)/K})
if
K>0
is reminiscent of the computation of the distortion coefficients for the
N
-dimensional model space with constant curvature
K
(see for example
[
Vil09
, Definition 14.19 and Theorem 14.20]
).

The general definition of the measure contraction property can be found in
[
Oht07
, Definition 2.1]
. We mention a few of its features, that are valid in general, not only for spaces with negligible cut locus. As proven in
[
Oht07
, Corollary 3.3]
, a Riemannian manifold
(M,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
with
\mathrm{Ric}\geq K
and
\dim(M)\leq N
satisfies the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
. Conversely, if
(M,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
satisfies the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
then
\dim(M)\leq N
and if the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
holds with
N=\dim(M)
then
\mathrm{Ric}\geq K
. The
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
for Riemannian manifolds will generally not imply that
\mathrm{Ric}\geq K
, as remarked in
[
Stu06a
, Remark 5.6]
. If
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
satisfies the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
, then it is shown in
[
Oht07
, Lemma 2.4]
that it also satisfies the
\mathsf{MCP}(K^{\prime},N^{\prime})
for
K^{\prime}\leq K
and
N^{\prime}\geq N
. If the metric measure space satisfies
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
with
K>0
, then it must be compact. This is Bonnet-Myers’ theorem, see
[
Oht07
, Theorem 4.3]
.

The class of
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
-spaces is notable since it is designed to correspond to spaces with nonnegative curvature. Since sub-Finsler Carnot groups appear as metric tangents to sub-Finsler manifolds (see
[
Le 17
, Theorem 3.5]
), it is expected that they exhibit properties of spaces with nonnegative curvature.
A
seems to indicate that the
\mathsf{MCP}
partially fails to capture that in the sub-Finsler setting. However it is worth noting when the
\mathsf{MCP}
actually holds because several important properties are satisfied for an
\mathsf{MCP}
-space. For instance, sharp Poincaré and
p
-Poincaré inequalities hold under measure contraction property (see
[
Han20
]
and
[
HM21
]
), and the
L^{1}
-localisation technique is also available (see
[
Mil21
, Section 3.8]
).

From
Definition

3
, it can be seen that when the space has negligible cut locus, the
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
holds if and only if
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})\geq t^{N}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
for all
t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, for all measurable
\Omega\subseteq X
with
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
, and all
x\in X
. This equivalence does not hold in general, that is when the space does not have a negligible cut locus. Yet, one implication remains true.

Proposition 5

(Same proof as in
[
Stu06a
, Proposition 2.1]
)
.

If
(X,\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
is a metric measure space (not necessarily with a negligible cut locus) satisfying the
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
, then
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})\geq t^{N}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
for all measurable set
\Omega\subseteq X
with
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
and all
x\in X
.

We can now introduce the curvature exponent, which was first coined in
[
Rif13
]
.

Definition 6
.

The
curvature exponent

N_{\mathrm{curv}}
of a metric measure space
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
is the number defined by

N_{\mathrm{curv}}:=\inf\left\{N>1\mid\textsf{MCP}(0,N)\penalty\ \text{is satisfied}\right\}.

Besides the measure contraction property and the curvature exponent, we will also investigate the so-called geodesic dimension. This quantity was introduced in
[
ABR18
]
for sub-Riemannian manifolds, and extended to metric measure spaces in
[
Riz16
]
(see also
[
BMR22
, Section 4.5]
).

Definition 7
.

For
x\in X
and
s>0
,
define the number

C_{s}(x):=\sup\left\{\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t^{s}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}\Big|\ \Omega\text{ Borel, bounded, }\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\in\mathopen{(}0,\infty\mathclose{)}\right\},

(3)

where
\Omega_{t}
denotes the
t
-intermediate set of
\Omega
from
x
.
The geodesic dimension at
x
is the number

N_{\mathrm{geo}}(x):=\inf\{s>0\mid C_{s}(x)=+\infty\}=\sup\{s>0\mid C_{s}(x)=0\}\in\mathopen{[}0,\infty\mathclose{]}.

The geodesic dimension of
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
, denoted by
N_{\mathrm{geo}}
, is given by

N_{\mathrm{geo}}:=\sup\left\{N_{\mathrm{geo}}(x)\mid x\in X\right\}.

The rationale behind the definition of the geodesic dimension is that, as explained in
[
ABR18
]
, for structures regular enough, say for sub-Riemannian manifolds
(M,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{CC}},\mu)
equipped with the Carnot-Carathéodory distance
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{CC}}
and a smooth measure
\mu
, the geodesic dimension is the number such that

\mu(\Omega_{t})\sim t^{N_{\mathrm{geo}}(x)},\text{
as }t\to 0^{+},

for every measurable set
\Omega\subseteq M
and all
x\in M
. Here, we write
f(t)\sim g(t)
(as
t\to 0^{+}
) if there exists
C\neq 0
such that
f(t)=g(t)(C+o(1))
(as
t\to 0^{+}
). Roughly speaking, the geodesic dimension is thus more local in essence than the curvature exponent.

The relationship between curvature exponent, geodesic dimension, and Hausdorff dimension is made clearer by the following statement.

Theorem 8

(
[
Riz16
, Theorem 6]
and
[
BMR22
, Theorem 4.19]
)
.

For a measure metric space
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\mathfrak{m})
, it holds

N_{\mathrm{curv}}\geq N_{\mathrm{geo}}\geq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}),

where
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d})
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of
(X,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d})
.

The curvature exponent is often found to be equal to the geodesic dimension. This is the case for every
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with
\mathrm{Ric}\geq 0
, for which one finds
N_{\mathrm{curv}}=N_{\mathrm{geo}}=n
.
It is also known that this equality also holds for a large class of Carnot groups equipped with a left-invariant sub-Riemannian metric, see
[
Riz16
]
and
[
BR18
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the present work in fact provides the very first example of sub-Finsler Carnot groups which has a curvature exponent strictly greater than its geodesic dimension.

Remark 12

Lemma 13
.

Given
\theta_{0}\in\mathopen{(}-\frac{\pi_{p}}{4},\frac{\pi_{p}}{4}\mathclose{)}\setminus\{0\}
, the radius of convergence of
\sin_{p}
at
\theta=\theta_{0}
is no less than
\left\lvert\theta_{0}\right\rvert/(p+1)
.

Proof.

From
Theorem

11
, the
n
-th derivative (
n\geq 2
) of
\sin_{p}
can be written as follows:

\sin_{p}^{(n)}\theta=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{n,k}\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{(n-k)p-n}\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{kp-n+1}\sigma_{n},

for some
a_{n,k}\in\mathds{R}
, and where

\sigma_{n}=\begin{cases}\sgn(\sin_{p}\theta)&if $n$ is even,\\
\sgn(\cos_{p}\theta)&if $n$ is odd.\end{cases}

Since
\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert<\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert
by assumption, it is easy to check that

\max_{k=1,\dots,n-1}\{\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{(n-k)p-n}\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{kp-n+1}|\}=\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{(n-1)p-n}\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{p-n+1}.

(7)

Next, we compute an upper bound for the coefficients
\left\lvert a_{n,k}\right\rvert
. By the Leibniz rule, the
n
-th derivative of
\sin_{p}\theta
is a priori a sum of
2^{n-2}
terms. Among those,
the number of the terms that have a common factor
\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{(n-k)p-n}\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{kp-n+1}
is
\binom{n-2}{k-1}
. Moreover, the coefficients in front of all these factors take the form

(p-1)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(c_{i}p-i)\prod_{j=1}^{n-k-1}(d_{j}p-j+1),

where
c_{i}\in\{1,\dots,i\}
and
d_{j}=\{1,\dots,j\}
. Clearly, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert(p-1)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(c_{i}p-i)\prod_{j=1}^{n-k-1}(d_{j}p-j+1)\right\rvert\leq{}

\displaystyle(p-1)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(ip+i)\prod_{j=1}^{n-k-1}(jp+j)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(p-1)(p+1)^{n-2}(k-1)!(n-k-1)!.

Therefore, we obtain an upper bound of
|a_{k}^{n}|
as

|a_{n,k}|\leq\binom{n-2}{k-1}(k-1)!(n-k-1)!(p-1)(p+1)^{n-2}\leq(n-2)!(p+1)^{n}.

Combined with
Equation

7
, we can estimate the radius of convergence using the root test

\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{\left\lvert\frac{1}{n!}\sin_{p}^{(n)}\theta\right\rvert}={}

\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{\left\lvert\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{n,k}\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{(n-k)p-n}\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{kp-n+1}\sigma_{n}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{\frac{(p+1)^{n}}{n}\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{(n-1)p-n}\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{p-n}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(p+1)\frac{\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{p-1}}{\left\lvert\sin_{p}\theta\right\rvert}\leq\frac{p+1}{\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert}.

The last inequality follows from analysing

\tan_{p}\theta:=\frac{\sin_{p}\theta}{\sin^{\prime}_{p}\theta}=\frac{\sin_{p}\theta}{\left\lvert\cos_{p}\theta\right\rvert^{p-1}\sgn(\cos_{p}\theta)}.

If
\theta>0
(the other case is similar), then performing a second derivative on
\tan_{p}\theta
reveals that it is convex for
0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi_{p}}{4}
. The tangent to
\tan_{p}
at
\theta=0
which must lie below the
p
-tangent by convexity, is observed to be
\theta
, which concludes the proof.
∎

3
Geometry of the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group

3.1
Sub-Finsler structures on the Heisenberg group

The Heisenberg group
\mathds{H}
is the connected and simply connected Lie group whose Lie algebra is graded, nilpotent, satisfying
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{2}
and such that
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is two-dimensional and generates
\mathfrak{h}
. In particular,
\mathds{H}
is three-dimensional and fixing a basis
X,Y,
and
Z=[X,Y]
of
\mathfrak{h}
induces global coordinates on
\mathds{H}
, called
exponential coordinates
, through the map
(x,y,z)\mapsto\mathrm{exp}(xX+yY+zZ)
. By the Campbell-Hausdorff formula, the law group in these coordinates writes as

(x,y,z)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(xy^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y)).

With a slight abuse of notation, the left-invariant vector fields on
\mathds{H}
whose values at the identity are
X,Y
and
Z
will also be denoted by
X,Y
and
Z
respectively. Their expression in exponential coordinates is

X=\partial_{x}-\frac{y}{2}\partial_{z},\ Y=\partial_{y}+\frac{x}{2}\partial_{z},\ Z=\partial_{z}.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}:=\left\{X,Y\right\}
is left-invariant and bracket generating, that is to say
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathcal{D}_{g})=\T_{g}(\mathds{H})
for all
g\in\mathds{H}
.

The Heisenberg group
\mathds{H}
has been studied extensively when equipped with its natural sub-Riemannian structure, i.e. with a scalar product on
\mathcal{D}
that turns
X
and
Y
into an orthonormal basis. In this work, we are going to study the Heisenberg endowed with a
sub-Finsler
structure.
Carnot groups endowed with sub-Finsler metrics have been extensively studied in connection with geometric group theory (see
[
Sto98
,
BL13
,
DM14
,
Tas22
]
), with optimal control theory (see
[
BBLS17
,
Lok19
,
Lok21
]
), and more recently with isoperimetric problems (see
[
FMRS23
]
).

In the case of the Heisenberg group, fixing a sub-Finsler structure generally means that we associate a norm
\|\cdot\|
to the distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Central to the present work is the standard
p
-norm: for
v=u_{1}X+u_{2}Y\in\mathcal{D}
and
p\in\mathopen{[}1,\infty\mathclose{]}
, the
\ell^{p}
-sub-Finsler metric is defined by

\|v\|_{p}=\begin{cases}|u_{1}|+|u_{2}|&\text{ if }p=1;\\
\left(|u_{1}|^{p}+|u_{2}|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{ if }p\in\mathopen{(}1,\infty\mathclose{)};\\
\max(u_{1},u_{2})&\text{ if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

(9)

We will refer to the sub-Finsler manifold formed by the Heisenberg group and its
\ell^{p}
-sub-Finsler metric as the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group.

Once a sub-Finsler structure with distribution
\mathcal{D}
and norm
\|\cdot\|
is fixed, an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
is said to be
horizontal
is
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\mathcal{D}_{\gamma(t)}
for almost every
t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, and its length is given by

\mathrm{L}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

while the induced distance of
\mathds{H}
is

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(g_{0},g_{1}):=\inf\left\{\mathrm{L}(\gamma)\mid\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}\text{ horizontal},\gamma(0)=g_{0},\gamma(1)=g_{1}\right\}.

By the Chow–Rashevskii theorem, this distance is well-defined and finite since
\mathcal{D}
is bracket-generating.
Notice that the length of a horizontal curve is invariant up to reparametrization.
Therefore, we sometimes require a horizontal curve to have a constant speed i.e.
\|\dot{\gamma}\|\equiv\mathrm{L}(\gamma)
.

Remark 16
.

Note that the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg groups are bi-Lipschitz equivalent to each other, so they all have the same Hausdorff dimension of 4.

3.2
Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle and Hamilton’s Equations

The metric geometry of a sub-Finsler Heisenberg group is governed by the study of sub-Finsler minimising geodesics, i.e., curves that minimise the length
between two points
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
, which are found by solving the following minimisation problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\gamma}(t)=u_{1}(t)X(\gamma(t))+u_{2}(t)Y(\gamma(t))\\
&\mathrm{L}(\gamma)\to\min\\
&\gamma(0)=g_{0},\ \gamma(1)=g_{1}\end{aligned}.\right.

(10)

Instead of minimising the length between two points as in Problem
Equation

10
, it is possible to alternatively look to minimise the energy.
The energy of a horizontal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
is defined by

\mathrm{E}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The corresponding minimising problem is then the following:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\gamma}(t)=u_{1}(t)X(\gamma(t))+u_{2}(t)Y(\gamma(t))\\
&\mathrm{E}(\gamma)\to\min\\
&\gamma(0)=g_{0},\ \gamma(1)=g_{1}\end{aligned}.\right.

(11)

The equivalence between the minimisation problems
Equation

10
and
Equation

11
is proven as in the sub-Riemannian case, see
[
ABB20
, Lemma 3.64.]
. By using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it can be shown that a horizontal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
is a minimiser of the energy functional
\mathrm{E}
if and only if it is a constant speed minimiser of the length functional
\mathrm{L}
.

Remark 17
.

Equivalently, the minimisation problem
Equation

10
can also be rewritten as the following time-optimal control problem for a horizontal curve
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathds{H}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\gamma}(t)=u_{1}(t)X(\gamma(t))+u_{2}(t)Y(\gamma(t))\in(L_{\gamma(t)})_{\ast}B\\
&T\to\min\\
&\gamma(0)=g_{0},\ \gamma(T)=g_{1}\end{aligned}.\right.

(12)

where
L_{P}
is the left-translation, and
B\subset\mathcal{D}_{e}
is a compact convex neighbourhood of
0
(with other technical assumptions, see
[
Lok21
, Main Assumption]
).
This minimising problem gives length minimisers for the non-reversible sub-Finsler Heisenberg group whose unit ball is
B
.
In this setting, the exponential map cannot be defined in general. However, length minimisers on non-strictly convex sub-Finsler structure, such as
\ell^{1}
- or
\ell^{\infty}

We apply Pontryagin’s maximum principle to obtain necessary conditions that a minimiser of
Equation

11
must satisfy. We refer the reader to
[
AS04
]
, for example, for a reference on Pontryagin’s maximum principle.

For
\nu\in\mathds{R}
, the Hamitonian
\mathcal{H}^{\nu}:\T^{*}(\mathds{H})\times\mathds{R}^{2}\to\mathds{R}
is defined as

\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda,u)=u_{1}h_{X}(\lambda)+u_{2}h_{Y}(\lambda)+\frac{\nu}{2}\|u_{1}X(\pi(\lambda))+u_{2}Y(\pi(\lambda))\|^{2},

where
\pi:\T^{\ast}(\mathds{H})\to\mathds{H}
is the canonical projection and for a vector field
V
on
\mathds{H}
, we have denoted by
h_{V}
the function on
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
defined by
h_{V}(\lambda):=\langle\lambda,V(\pi(\lambda))\rangle
. Note that
h_{X}
,
h_{Y}
, and
h_{Z}
form a system of coordinates on each fibers of
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
.

For a fixed control
u\in\mathds{R}^{2}
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda,u)
is smooth and the Hamiltonian vector field
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}^{\nu}(\cdot,u):\T^{*}(\mathds{H})\to\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
is then the unique vector field on
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
satisfying

\sigma(\cdot,\overrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}^{\nu}(\lambda,u))=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{\lambda}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\cdot,u),

where
\sigma
denotes the canonical symplectic form of
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
.

Theorem 18

(Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle)
.

Let
(u(t),\gamma(t))
be a solution to the minimisation problem
Equation

11
. Then there exists an absolutely continuous curve
\lambda:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
satisfying
\pi(\lambda(t))=\gamma(t)
, and a number
\nu\in\{0,-1\}
, such that for almost all
t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}

(i)

\lambda(t)\neq 0
if
\nu=0
;

(ii)

\dot{\lambda}(t)=\overrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}^{\nu}(\lambda(t),u(t))
;

(iii)

\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda(t),u(t))=\max_{u\in\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda(t),u)
.

We will say that
\lambda:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
is a normal (resp. abnormal) extremal trajectory if it satisfies the conclusions of
Theorem

18
with
\nu=-1
(resp.
\nu=0
). The sub-Finsler Heisenberg group does not admit any non-trivial abnormal extremal, exactly as in the sub-Riemannian case. This fact does not depend on the cost functional, but only on the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

We now turn to the study of normal extremals. From now on, we will focus on the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group, that is
\mathds{H}
equipped with the sub-Finsler norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
with

. Here, Shelupsky’s
p
-trigonometric described in

We can obtain an explicit expression for the maximised Hamiltonian.

Proposition 19
.

If we denote by
H:\T^{*}(\mathds{H})\to\mathds{R}
the maximised Hamiltonian from part (iii) of
Theorem

18
with
\nu=-1
, we have that

H(\lambda):=\max_{u\in\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda,u)=\frac{1}{2}(|h_{X}(\lambda)|^{q}+|h_{Y}(\lambda)|^{q})^{2/q}.

Furthermore, for a given
\lambda\in\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
, the maximum above is attained by taking

u_{1}=r\cos_{p}(\theta^{\circ}),\text{ and }u_{2}=r\sin_{p}(\theta^{\circ}),

where
(r,\theta)
are the
\ell^{q}
-polar coordinates of
(h_{X}(\lambda),h_{Y}(\lambda))
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
,
u\in\mathds{R}^{2}
, and introduce
A,B>0
,
\varphi\in\mathopen{[}0,2\pi_{p}\mathclose{)}
, and
\theta\in\mathopen{[}0,2\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
by setting

u_{1}=A\cos_{p}(\varphi),\ u_{2}=A\sin_{p}(\varphi),\ h_{X}(\lambda)=B\cos_{q}(\theta),\ h_{Y}(\lambda)=B\sin_{q}(\theta).

Then, by
Equation

4
we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda,u)={}

\displaystyle u_{1}h_{X}(\lambda)+u_{2}h_{Y}(\lambda)-\frac{1}{2}(|u_{1}|^{p}+|u_{2}|^{p})^{2/p}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle AB(\cos_{p}(\varphi)\cos_{q}(\theta)+\sin_{p}(\varphi)\sin_{q}(\theta))-\frac{1}{2}A^{2}\leq AB-\frac{1}{2}A^{2},

with equality if and only if
\varphi=\theta^{\circ}
. The function
A\mapsto AB-\frac{1}{2}A^{2}
attains its unique maximum at
A=B
, which means that

\displaystyle H(\lambda):={}

\displaystyle\max_{u\in\mathds{R}^{2}}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}(\lambda,u)=\frac{1}{2}B^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(|B\cos_{q}(\theta^{\circ})|^{q}+|B\sin_{q}(\theta^{\circ})|^{q})^{2/q}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(|\langle\lambda,X(\pi(\lambda))\rangle|^{q}+|\langle\lambda,Y(\pi(\lambda))\rangle|^{q})^{2/q}.

∎

We denote by

\mathcal{J}^{t}
is well defined, but its derivative has singularities when

. On the singular point
\mathcal{S}_{0}
,
the exponential map is only continuous,
hence the Jacobian may not be well-defined.
In this section, we shall consider the following three cases, which are sorted by regularity; the Jacobian on
\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
in

, on
\mathcal{S}_{0}
in

, and its differential on
\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
in
Section

4.3
.

4.1
Jacobian at regular points

If
(r,\theta,w)\in\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
,
the exponential map
\mathrm{exp}_{e}^{t}
is at least
C^{1}
,
thus its Jacobian
\mathcal{J}^{t}
is continuous.
The homogeneity and symmetry properties
Equation

17
,
Equation

18
, and
Equation

19
implies that

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\mathcal{J}^{ct}(r/c,\theta,w/c),\ \mathcal{J}^{t}(r,-\theta,-w)=\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w),\\
\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta+n\tfrac{\pi_{q}}{2},w)=\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w),\text{ for }c\neq 0\text{ and }n\in\mathds{Z}.\end{gathered}

(20)

We shall also write
\mathcal{J}
instead of
\mathcal{J}^{1}
for simplicity (resp.
D
for
D^{1}
). We can obtain an expression for the Jacobian by performing a simple computation involving Shelupsky’s trigonometric functions.

Lemma 27
.

For all
(r,\theta,w)\in\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
with
w\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)=\frac{r^{3}t}{w^{4}}\Big[2

\displaystyle-\big(\sin_{q}(wt+\theta)\sin_{p}(\theta^{\circ})+\cos_{q}(wt+\theta)\cos_{p}(\theta^{\circ})\big)

(21)

\displaystyle-\big(\sin_{p}(wt+\theta)^{\circ}\sin_{q}(\theta)+\cos_{p}(wt+\theta)^{\circ}\cos_{q}(\theta)\big)

\displaystyle-wt\big(\sin_{p}(wt+\theta)^{\circ}\cos_{p}(\theta^{\circ})-\cos_{p}(wt+\theta)^{\circ}\sin_{p}(\theta^{\circ})\big)\Big],

and
\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,0)
for
\theta\notin\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathds{Z}
is obtained by smoothly extending
Equation

21
as
w\to 0:

\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,0)=\frac{(q-1)^{2}r^{3}t^{5}}{12}\left|\cos_{q}\theta\sin_{q}\theta\right|^{2q-4}.

(22)

In the identity
Equation

21
, it is worth recalling that the functions
\sin_{p}(x^{\circ})
and
\cos_{p}(x^{\circ})
have explicit forms given by:

\sin_{p}(x^{\circ}):=\left\lvert\sin_{q}(x)\right\rvert^{q-1}\sgn(\sin_{q}(x)),\ \cos_{p}(x^{\circ}):=\left\lvert\cos_{q}(x)\right\rvert^{q-1}\sgn(\cos_{q}(x)).

For its proof,
we introduce the reduced Jacobian function
\mathcal{J}_{R}:\mathds{R}^{2}\to\mathds{R}
by setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w):={}2

\displaystyle-\big(\sin_{q}(w+\theta)\sin_{p}(\theta^{\circ})+\cos_{q}(w+\theta)\cos_{p}(\theta^{\circ})\big)

(23)

\displaystyle-\big(\sin_{p}(w+\theta)^{\circ}\sin_{q}(\theta)+\cos_{p}(w+\theta)^{\circ}\cos_{q}(\theta)\big)

\displaystyle-w\big(\sin_{p}(w+\theta)^{\circ}\cos_{p}(\theta^{\circ})-\cos_{p}(w+\theta)^{\circ}\sin_{p}(\theta^{\circ})\big).

In other words, we define
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
so that

\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)=\frac{r^{3}t}{w^{4}}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,wt)

holds for all
(r,\theta,w)\in D^{t}
. This definition of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
allows us to simplify the expression for
\mathcal{J}^{t}
in terms of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
, which will be useful in the remainder of the work.

Remark 28
.

When
p=2
(and thus
q=2
), the expression
Equation

21
simplifies to

\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)=\frac{r^{3}t}{w^{4}}\left(\sin\left(\frac{wt}{2}\right)-\frac{wt}{2}\cos\left(\frac{wt}{2}\right)\right)\sin\left(\frac{wt}{2}\right),

by the standard trigonometric addition formulas. This coincides with the well-known computations in the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group, see
[
Jui09
, Proposition 1.12.]
.

Proof.

The case
w\neq 0
is a straightforward and long computation, so we omit the detail.
We will compute the limit to
w\to 0
by using L’Hospital’s rule.
By induction, we obtain that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{w}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=(k-1)(\cos_{q}^{(k)}\theta\sin_{q}^{(1)}\theta-&\sin_{q}^{(k)}\theta\cos_{q}^{(1)}\theta)\\
&+\cos_{q}^{(k+1)}\theta\sin_{q}\theta-\sin_{q}^{(k+1)}\theta\cos_{q}\theta.\\
\end{split}

(24)

Here,
\sin_{q}^{(k)}\theta
and
\cos_{q}^{(k)}\theta
denote the
k
-th derivatives of
\sin_{q}\theta
and
\cos_{q}\theta
, respectively. Using
Theorem

11
, we can evaluate the derivatives of
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
with respect to
w
at
w=0
. We find that
\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=\partial_{w}^{2}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=\partial_{w}^{3}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=0
, while

\partial_{w}^{4}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=2(q-1)^{2}\left\lvert\cos_{q}(\theta)\sin_{q}(\theta)\right\rvert^{2q-4}.

The conclusion follows by L’Hospital’s rule.
∎

Remark 29
.

The function
\mathcal{J}_{R}
is continuous everywhere, and it is smooth on any open domain where (the projection of) the singular sets
\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{1}
have been removed.

It will be useful for the rest of this work to write down the derivative of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
with respect to
w
:

\displaystyle\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w):=

\displaystyle(q-1)\left\lvert\cos_{q}(w+\theta)\sin_{q}(w+\theta)\right\rvert^{q-2}

(25)

\displaystyle\times\Big[\sin_{q}(w+\theta)\cos_{q}(\theta)-\cos_{q}(w+\theta)\sin_{q}(\theta)

\displaystyle-w\big(\sin_{q}(w+\theta)\sin_{p}(\theta^{\circ})+\cos_{q}(w+\theta)\cos_{p}(\theta^{\circ})\big)\Big].

On the region
\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
,
we are going to check that the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}^{t}
is positive by studying the sign of the reduced Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{R}
.

Lemma 30
.

For every
(\theta,w)\in\mathopen{[}0,2\pi_{q}\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0,2\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
, we have
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)>0
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)=0
if and only if
w\in 2\pi_{q}\mathds{Z}
.

Proof.

By the symmetries
Equation

20
,
we can assume
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}]
.
We express
Equation

23
in a more compact notation as
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)=\mathcal{J}_{R,1}(\theta,w)+w\mathcal{J}_{R,2}(\theta,w)
.

By the duality inequality
Equation

6
, it is clear that
\mathcal{J}_{R,1}(\theta,w)
is non-negative for every
(\theta,w)
, and this term vanishes if and only if
w+\theta=\theta\operatorname{mod}2\pi_{q}
, that is,
w\in 2\pi_{q}\mathds{Z}
.

Geometrically, the term
\mathcal{J}_{R,2}(\theta,w)
can be interpreted (see
Figure

6
) as the scalar product of the two vectors given by

(\cos_{p}\theta^{\circ},\sin_{p}\theta^{\circ})\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ (-\sin_{p}(w+\theta)^{\circ},\cos_{p}(w+\theta)^{\circ}).

Figure 6
:
The positivity of
\mathcal{J}_{R,2}
for
w\in(\pi_{q},2\pi_{q})

As such, we know that
\mathcal{J}_{R,2}(\theta,w)<0
if
w\in\mathopen{(}0,\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
,
\mathcal{J}_{R,2}(\theta,w)>0
if
w\in\mathopen{(}\pi_{q},2\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
and
\mathcal{J}_{R,2}(\theta,w)=0
if
w\in\pi_{q}\mathds{Z}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)>0
for
(\theta,w)\in\mathopen{[}0,\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}\pi_{q},2\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
.

To prove the positivity of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
for
(\theta,w)\in\mathopen{[}0,\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0,\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
, we now need to consider the partial derivative of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
with respect to
w
, already seen in
Equation

25
, which we express as
\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)=(q-1)\left\lvert\cos_{q}(w+\theta)\sin_{q}(w+\theta)\right\rvert^{q-2}\mathcal{K}(\theta,w)
.

The directional derivative of
\mathcal{K}
towards
w-\theta
is

(\partial_{w}-\partial_{\theta})\mathcal{K}(\theta,w)=(q-1)w\left\lvert\cos_{q}\theta\sin_{q}\theta\right\rvert^{q-2}\left(\sin_{q}(w+\theta)\cos_{q}\theta-\cos_{q}(w+\theta)\sin_{q}\theta\right).

Using the same reasoning as before and considering the scalar product of the two vectors:

(\cos_{q}\theta,\sin_{q}\theta)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ (\sin_{q}(w+\theta),-\cos_{q}(w+\theta)),

we can conclude that
(\partial_{w}-\partial_{\theta})\mathcal{K}(\theta,w)
is positive for
w\in\mathopen{(}0,\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
and
\theta\in\mathopen{[}0,\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathclose{]}
.

Therefore, to complete the proof, we need to verify that
\mathcal{K}(\theta,w)
is non-negative for
w=0
and
\theta\in\mathopen{[}0,\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathclose{]}
, as well as for
\theta=\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}
and
w\in\mathopen{(}0,\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
. We find that

\mathcal{K}(\tfrac{\pi_{q}}{2},w)=\sin_{q}w-w\cos_{q}w,\text{ and }\mathcal{K}(\theta,0)=0.

By considering the scalar product of the two vectors

(\cos_{q}w,\sin_{q}w)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ (-w,1),

we can show that
\mathcal{K}(\frac{\pi_{q}}{2},w)\geq 0
, see
Figure

7
. If
\cos_{q}w\leq 0
, there is nothing to prove so we only consider
\cos_{q}w\geq 0
.

Figure 7
:
The positivity of
\sin_{q}w-w\cos_{q}w
.

Indeed, recall that the area of the
\ell^{q}
-fan spanned by
(0,0),(1,0),(\cos_{q}w,\sin_{q}w)
is
\frac{w}{2}
, as shown in
Definition

9
. On the other hand,
(-w,1)
is the point such that the area of the
\ell^{\infty}
-fan spanned by
(0,0),(0,1),(-w,1)
is also
\frac{w}{2}
. It is clear that the
\ell^{\infty}
ball includes the
\ell^{q}
ball, so the

-angle between
(\cos_{q}w,\sin_{q}w)

\frac{\pi_{2}}{2}
. This shows that
\mathcal{K}(\frac{\pi_{q}}{2},w)\geq 0
for all

.

In conclusion, since
(\partial_{w}-\partial_{\theta})\mathcal{K}(\theta,w)
is positive, it implies that
\mathcal{K}(\theta,w)
is positive for
(\theta,w)\in\mathopen{[}0,\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathclose{]}\times\mathopen{(}0,\pi_{q}\mathclose{)}
. Therefore,
\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
is positive on the same domain, and checking that
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=0
shows that
\mathcal{J}_{R}>0
when

.

To complete the proof, it suffices to observe
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)=0
when

by simply substituting these values into

.
∎

Remark 31
.

The derivative
\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
may be discontinuous and infinite on the singular set

, however,

\mathcal{J}_{R}
.

Proposition 32

(Positivity of Jacobian on
\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
)
.

For
t>0
and
(r,\theta,w)\in\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
,

\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)>0
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)=\frac{r^{3}t}{w^{4}}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,wt)
,
where
\theta\in[0,2\pi_{q}]
and
wt\in(0,2\pi_{q})
.
Therefore by Lemma
30
,

\mathcal{J}^{t}
is positive when
w\neq 0
.
When
w=0
, we must have
\theta\notin\frac{\pi_{q}}{2}\mathds{Z}
by definition of
\mathcal{R}^{t}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}^{t}
and the positivity then follows from
Equation

22
.
∎

4.2
Jacobian at singular points

Next we consider the singular points
\mathcal{S}_{0}
.
Since the exponential map is not differentiable on
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq D^{t}
for
p>2
,
the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}^{t}
itself has singularities.
The singular set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is independent of
t
,
we restrict ourselves to
t=1
.
The computation of limits of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
as
(\theta,w)
approaches the singular set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is achieved by considering asymptotic estimates of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
.
By the symmetry of the Jacobian,
we can assume
\theta=0
.
We will compute the asymptotic of
\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
at
(0,0)
.

Lemma 33
.

Suppose that both
|w+\theta|
and
|\theta|
are strictly smaller than the radii of convergence at
\theta=0
of the
q
-trigonometric functions
\sin_{q}(\theta)
and
\cos_{q}(\theta)
.
Then, the function
\mathcal{J}_{R}
has the uniformly convergent series representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)={}

\displaystyle\frac{q-1}{q^{2}(q+1)}\big(\left\lvert w+\theta\right\rvert^{2q}+\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert^{2q}\big)+\frac{2(q-1)^{2}}{q^{2}}\left\lvert w+\theta\right\rvert^{q}\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{q-1}{q+1}\left(\left\lvert w+\theta\right\rvert^{q}\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert^{q-2}+|w+\theta|^{q-2}|\theta|^{q}\right)(w+\theta)\theta+E(\theta,w),

where the remainder term
E
is the infinite series

E(\theta,w)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}|w+\theta|^{\alpha}|\theta|^{\beta}(w+\theta)^{\gamma}\theta^{\delta}

(26)

with
\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta\geq 3q
and
c_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}\in\mathds{R}
.

Proof.

The proof of this result involves substituting the expansions found in
Theorem

14
into the expression
Equation

23
for
\mathcal{J}_{R}
.
∎

To estimate the limit of
\mathcal{J}
,

Lemma

33
is not enough.
Indeed,
since
\mathcal{J}=\frac{r^{3}t}{w^{4}}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
,
we need to consider the limit of
\frac{\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)}{w^{4}}
as
(\theta,w)\to(0,0)
.
If a sequence of points
(\theta,w)
converges to
(0,0)
with
|w|=o(|\theta|)
,
then we cannot control the error term
\frac{E_{3}}{w^{4}}=O\left(\frac{|\theta|^{3q}}{w^{4}}\right)
.

It is quite hard to compute the higher order terms of the fractional Taylor series of
q
-trigonometric functions.
Hence, we will use the Taylor series expansion of
\mathcal{J}_{R}
at
(\theta,0)
.
In the following lemma,
we can see that the radius of convergence of the Taylor expansion around
(\theta,0)
can cover the above cases.

Lemma 34
.

For
\theta\in\left(-\frac{\pi_{q}}{4},\frac{\pi_{q}}{4}\right)\setminus\{0\}
,
the radius of convergence of the Taylor series at
(\theta,0)

\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)=\frac{w^{4}}{12}(q-1)^{2}\left\lvert\cos_{q}(\theta)\sin_{q}(\theta)\right\rvert^{2q-4}+\sum_{k=5}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\partial_{w}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)w^{k},

is at least
|\theta|/(q+1)
.

Proof.

From the formula
Equation

24
,
the radius of convergence of
w\mapsto\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)
is equal to that of
q
-trigonometric functions,
for which a bound is given in
Lemma

13
.
∎

With these expansions, we are able to compute some limits of
\mathcal{J}(r,\theta,w)
as points approach
\mathcal{S}_{0}
. The techniques used in the proof will also be used later on in
Section

6
, when dealing with the geodesic dimension.

Proposition 35
.

For
(r_{0},\theta_{0},0)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
,
we have

\lim_{(r,\theta,w)\to(r_{0},\theta_{0},0)}\mathcal{J}(r,\theta,w)=\begin{cases}\frac{r_{0}^{3}}{12}&(p=2),\\
0&(p<2).\end{cases}

If
p>2
,
then for almost all directions
(u,v)\in\mathds{S}^{1}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{2}
,
we have

\liminf_{s\to 0}\mathcal{J}(r_{0},\theta_{0}+su,sv)=+\infty.

Computing the full limits when
p>2
would require a more detailed analysis, which we do not pursue here.

Proof.

If
p\leq 2
,
then the Jacobian is continuous on these points and the limit is given by the limit
\theta\to\theta_{0}
of
Equation

22
.

From now on, we will thus assume that
p>2
.
Since
\mathcal{J}(r,\theta,w)=r^{3}\frac{\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)}{w^{4}}
,
we consider the limit of
\frac{\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)}{w^{4}}
as
(\theta,w)\to(0,0)
.
Fixing a constant
\varepsilon_{q}\in\mathopen{(}0,q+1\mathclose{)}
, we can classify points around
(0,0)
into the following three cases.

1)

|\varepsilon_{q}w|\leq|\theta|
;

2)

|w|\leq|\theta|\leq|\varepsilon_{q}w|
;

3)

|\theta|\leq|w|
.

Up to a subsequence, we only need to consider the limit
(\theta,w)\to(0,0)
in these three cases respectively.

Case 1
(
|\varepsilon_{q}w|\leq|\theta|
)
.

In this case,
we can apply
Lemma

34
.
In addition,
suppose that
|\theta|
is smaller than the radius of convergence of the
q
-trigonometric functions at
0
.
Combining
Theorem

14
and
Equation

24
,
we can write

\partial_{w}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,0)=\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}c_{k,l}^{1}|\theta|^{lq-k}\tau_{k},

(27)

where

\tau_{k}=\begin{cases}1&(k:\penalty\ \text{even}),\\
\sgn(\theta)&(k:\penalty\ \text{odd}),\end{cases}

and where
c_{k,l}^{1}
are constants explicitly given by

\displaystyle c_{k,l}^{1}=\sum_{n=0}^{l}a_{n}b_{l-n}\{

\displaystyle(k-1)((l-n)q)_{k}(nq+1)-(k-1)(nq+1)_{k}(l-n)q

\displaystyle+((l-n)q)_{k+1}-(nq+1)_{k+1}\}.

In the formula above, we have used the descending product notation

(\alpha)_{k}:=\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-k+1).

Notice that a priori the series start from
l=0
.
However, it can easily be checked that
c_{k,0}^{1}=c_{k,1}^{1}=0
for all
k\geq 4
,
and
c_{4,2}^{1}=2(q-1)^{2}>0
.

For fixed
\theta
and
w
satisfying
|\varepsilon_{q}w|\leq|\theta|
, we can substitute
Equation

27
in
Lemma

34
to obtain

\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)=\sum_{k=4}^{\infty}\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{k,l}^{1}}{k!}w^{k}|\theta|^{lq-k}\tau_{k}.

We note that the double series is absolutely convergent in
k
and in
l
by Tonelli’s theorem. Consequently,
we can exchange the sums and we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)={}

\displaystyle w^{4}|\theta|^{2q-4}\sum_{k=4}^{\infty}\frac{c_{k,2}^{1}}{k!}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)^{k-4}+\sum_{k=4}^{\infty}\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\frac{c_{k,l}^{1}}{k!}w^{k}|\theta|^{lq-k}\tau_{k}

(28)

\displaystyle=:{}

\displaystyle w^{4}|\theta|^{2q-4}P_{1}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)+E_{1}(\theta,w),

where
P_{1}:\mathopen{[}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{q}},\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{q}}\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{R}
is a real analytic function,
and
E_{1}
is an error term of order at most
w^{4}|\theta|^{3q-4}
.

By the positivity of Jacobian on regular points seen in
Proposition

32
,
the principal term is non-negative on this domain.
Moreover,
since
P_{1}
is an analytic function on a closed interval,
there are (if they exist at all) finitely many zero points.
In particular,
the analytic function
P_{1}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)
is positive almost everywhere.

By the compactness of the domain
\mathopen{[}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{q}},\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{q}}\mathclose{]}
,
we can assume that
P_{1}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)
converges to some non-negative number.
If the limit is positive,
we have

\frac{\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)}{w^{4}}\sim|\theta|^{2q-4}P_{1}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)\to+\infty.

The Jacobian diverges to
+\infty
almost surely in the sense of the statement,
since there are only finitely many zero points of
P_{1}
.

Case 2
(
|w|\leq|\theta|\leq|\varepsilon_{q}w|
)
.

In this case, we apply
Lemma

33
.
Combined with the binomial theorem

|w+\theta|^{\alpha}=|\theta|^{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\alpha}{k}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)^{k},

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)={}

\displaystyle w^{4}|\theta|^{2q-4}\sum_{k=4}^{\infty}c_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)^{k-4}+E(\theta,w)

(29)

\displaystyle=:{}

\displaystyle w^{4}|\theta|^{2q-4}P_{2}\left(\frac{w}{\theta}\right)+E(\theta,w),

where the constants
c_{k}^{2}
are explicitly written by

c_{k}^{2}=\frac{q-1}{q^{2}(q+1)}\left[\binom{2q}{k}+\binom{0}{k}\right]+\frac{2(q-1)^{2}}{q^{2}}\binom{q}{k}-\frac{q-1}{q+1}\left[\binom{q+1}{k}+\binom{q-1}{k}\right],

where the function
P_{2}:[-1,1]\to\mathds{R}
is real analytic, and
E
is given in (
26
).
A priori the series starts from
k=0
,
however we can check that
c_{k}^{2}=0
for
k=0,1,2,3
and
c_{4}^{2}=2(q-1)^{2}>0
.
Since
|\theta|\leq\varepsilon_{q}|w|
,
the error term
E(\theta,w)
is of order at most
|w|^{3q}
.
The rest of the arguments follow in the same way as in Case 1.

Case 3
(
|\theta|\leq|w|
)
.

We apply again
Lemma

33
, together with the binomial series theorem

|w+\theta|^{\alpha}=|w|^{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\alpha}{k}\left(\frac{\theta}{w}\right)^{k}.

Therefore, we have the following series representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)={}

\displaystyle|w|^{2q}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[c_{k,0}^{3}+c_{k,2q}^{3}\left|\frac{\theta}{w}\right|^{2q}+c_{k,q}^{3}\left|\frac{\theta}{w}\right|

(30)

\displaystyle+c_{k,q-1}^{3}\left|\frac{\theta}{w}\right|^{q-2}\frac{\theta}{w}+c_{k,q+1}^{3}\left|\frac{\theta}{w}\right|^{q}\frac{\theta}{w}\Bigg]\left(\frac{\theta}{w}\right)^{k}+E(\theta,w)

\displaystyle=:{}

\displaystyle|w|^{2q}P_{3}\left(\frac{\theta}{w}\right)+E(\theta,w),

where
c_{k,\alpha}^{3}
are constants given by

\displaystyle c_{k,0}^{3}=\frac{q-1}{q^{2}(q+1)}\binom{2q}{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ c_{k,2q}^{3}=\frac{q-1}{q^{2}(q+1)}\binom{0}{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ c_{k,q}^{3}=\frac{2(q-1)^{2}}{q^{2}}\binom{q}{k},

\displaystyle c_{k,q-1}^{3}=-\frac{q+1}{q-1}\binom{q+1}{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ c_{k,q+1}^{3}=-\frac{q+1}{q-1}\binom{q-1}{k},

where the function
P_{3}:[-1,1]\to\mathds{R}
is a finite sum of fractional analytic functions (see the discussion after
Theorem

14
),
and
E
is given in (
26
). Since
\mathcal{J}_{R}
is nonnegative by
Lemma

30
, the function
P_{3}
is also nonnegative on
\mathopen{[}-1,1\mathclose{]}
(considering
|w|
small enough).
The only difference from cases 1 and 2 is that the role of analytic function of the leading term is replaced with
P_{3}
, which is a sum of fractional analytic functions.
In cases 1 and 2,
we used the fact that the number of zero points of an analytic function on a closed interval is finite.
We now explain that the same property holds also for
P_{3}
.
Indeed,
P_{3}
is analytic (in the classical sense) on
\mathopen{[}-1,1\mathclose{]}\setminus\{0\}
,
so we only need to check that

is not the accumulation point of the zero points of

.
Since the set of exponents

\{k,k+q-1,k+q,k+q+1,k+2q\}_{k=0,1,\dots}

is discrete in
\mathds{R}
, we can rearrange it to be an increasing set of exponents and the desired property follows by adapting the proof of
the classical identity theorem for analytic functions.
This observation shows that the number of zero points of
P_{3}
is finite.
The rest of the proof follows in the same way with cases 1 and 2.
∎

With the change of coordinates

Proposition 39

Proof.

Inequality
Equation

2

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})\geq t^{N}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega),

(33)

for all

, and all

, where

We first show that

32

32

\left\lvert\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)\right\rvert\geq t^{N}\left\lvert\mathcal{J}(r,\theta,w)\right\rvert,

By

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})={}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(\tilde{\Omega}_{t})=\mathfrak{m}(\mathrm{exp}_{e}^{t}(\tilde{A}))=\int_{\mathrm{exp}_{e}^{t}(\tilde{A})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\tilde{A}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{exp}_{e}^{t})^{*}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\tilde{A}}\left\lvert\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w

The

Remark 41
.

Based on the definitions introduced in this section, one can readily observe that

N_{p}(\theta,w)=1+\frac{w\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)}{\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)}=\frac{\partial_{t}\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)}{\mathcal{J}^{t}(r,\theta,w)}\Big|_{t=1}.

s\mapsto 1+\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}
.

We claim that
P(s)
vanishes if and only if
s=0
. Indeed, if
1+s<0
, then it is easy to see that all the terms of
P(s)
are positive. The differential of
P(s)
is

\displaystyle\partial_{s}P(s):={}

\displaystyle 2q\left\lvert 1+s\right\rvert^{q-2}\Big[\left\lvert 1+s\right\rvert^{q}(1+s)-1-(q+1)s-\frac{q(q+1)}{2}s^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 2q(1+s)^{q-2}\Big[(1+s)^{q+1}-1-(q+1)s-\frac{q(q+1)}{2}s^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle\text{if }1+s>0.

Notice that
1+(q+1)s+\frac{q(q+1)}{2}s^{2}
is the first three term of the binomial expansion of
(1+s)^{q+1}
. Therefore we can see that the content in the bracket of
\partial_{s}P(s)
is concave on
\mathopen{(}-1,\infty\mathclose{)}
, and
P(s)
has a unique zero point at
s=0
. The following limits are easily obtained:

\lim_{s\to 0}1+\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}=5,\ \lim_{s\to+\infty}1+\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}=\lim_{s\to-\infty}1+\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}=2q+1.

The map
s\mapsto 1+\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}
is thus continuous for all
s\in\mathds{R}
and bounded at infinity.

To establish that
N_{p}>2q+1
when
p<2
, it is sufficient to observe, following a lengthy computation, that

\partial_{s}\left(\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}\right)=\frac{2q\left\lvert 1+s\right\rvert^{4q+2}+\cdots\text{ finitely many lower order terms}\cdots}{(\cdots)^{2}}.

This expression is therefore strictly positive for
s
small enough, which means that
1+\frac{s\partial_{s}P(s)}{P(s)}
converges to
2q+1
as
s
approaches
-\infty
by values strictly greater than
2q+1
.
∎

Lemma 47

Lemma 48

(Integral asymptotic over the domain
A_{t}^{1}
)

\int_{-Wt}^{Wt}\int_{\varepsilon_{q}\left\lvert w\right\rvert}^{\delta}\frac{1}{w^{4}}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\underset{\ \ t\to 0^{+}}{\sim}\begin{cases}t&if $p<3$\\

We assume that

Inside the domain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})=\frac{t^{4}R^{4}}{4}

\displaystyle\Big[\underbrace{\int_{-Wt}^{Wt}\int_{\delta}^{\Theta}\frac{1}{w^{4}}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w}_{\text{domain }A_{t}^{0}}+\underbrace{\int_{-Wt}^{Wt}\int_{\varepsilon_{q}\left\lvert w\right\rvert}^{\delta}\frac{1}{w^{4}}\mathcal{J}_{R}(\theta,w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w}_{\text{domain }A_{t}^{1}}

7
The
\ell^{1}
- and
\ell^{\infty}
-Heisenberg groups

We will finally consider the
\ell^{p}
-Heisenberg group, where
p=1
or
p=\infty
. As the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group and the
\ell^{\infty}
-Heisenberg group are isomorphic as metric measure spaces, it is sufficient to focus on the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group.
In this case,
the Hamiltonian formalism pursued in
Section

3
is more difficult to implement due to the fact that the
\ell^{1}
-sub-Finsler norm is not strictly convex. As we have seen, the dual angle
\theta^{\circ}
is not always uniquely determined by
\theta
if
p=1
. We will adopt the approach proposed in
[
DM14
]
and
[
BL13
]
, based on Busemann’s isoperimetric inequality in the plane with an
\ell^{1}
-norm. In those works, from which we borrow the following result, the authors also studied the Heisenberg group equipped with polygonal sub-Finsler metrics.

We say that a continuous path in
\mathds{R}^{2}
is an
\ell^{\infty}
-arc if it parametrises a subset of an
\ell^{\infty}
-sphere. There are three kinds of geodesics in the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group.

Proposition 53

(
[
BL13
, Section 6.]
)
.

A horizontal curve
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))
joining the identity to
(x,y,z)
is a minimising constant speed geodesic of the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group if and only if,

(i)

when
0\leq|z|\leq\frac{1}{2}|xy|
, the projection
(x(t),y(t))
is a minimising constant speed geodesic for the
\ell^{1}
-norm on the plane, joining
(0,0)
to
(x,y)
and covering a signed area
z
. These geodesics are never unique unless
\left\lvert z\right\rvert=\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert xy\right\rvert
;

(ii)

when
\frac{1}{2}|xy|\leq|z|\leq\max(|x|,|y|)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|xy|
, the projection
(x(t),y(t))
is an
\ell^{\infty}
-arc with
3
edges, parametrised by constant speed (with respect to the
\ell^{1}
-length of
\mathds{R}^{2}
), and covering a signed area
z
. These geodesics are unique;

(iii)

when
\max(|x|,|y|)^{2}-\frac{|xy|}{2}<|z|
, the projection
(x(t),y(t))
is an
\ell^{\infty}
-arc with
4
edges, parametrised by constant speed (with respect to the
\ell^{1}
-length of
\mathds{R}^{2}
), and covering a signed area
z
. These geodesics are unique unless
x
or
y
vanish.

Plane curves of “staircase type” are examples of projection of geodesics of case (i) in
Proposition

53
. On the other hand, the geodesics of case (ii) and (iii) are projected onto arcs of squares, with the sides parallel to the
x
-axis and
y
-axis. We shall denote by
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
and
H_{3}
the regions of
\mathds{H}
associated with cases (i), (ii), and (iii), respectively.

The
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group does not have negligible cut locus in the sense of
Definition

1
. Indeed, we can write down explicitly the set
\mathcal{C}(e)
of points joined from the identity by multiple minimising geodesics, and observe that it clearly has a non-zero measure:

\mathcal{C}(e)=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathds{H}\mid|z|\leq\frac{1}{2}|xy|,\text{ or else }\left\lvert xy\right\rvert=0\text{ and }\left\lvert z\right\rvert>0\right\}.

Moreover, the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group is branching. It suffices, for example, to consider a small open neighbourhood
\Omega
of
(x,y,z)\in\mathrm{int}(H_{2})
, where
|x|<y
and
z>0
. Then, a geodesic from the identity to a point in
\Omega
is of type (ii). More specifically, it is an
\ell^{\infty}
-arc with three edges: the first one going in the direction
\partial_{x}
, the second in the direction
\partial_{y}
and the third in the direction
-\partial_{x}
. They all coincide at least on a portion of their first edge, before branching (see
Definition

2
).

Building upon this last observation, it is possible to prove that the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group does not satisfies the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
, for any
K\in\mathds{R}
and any
N\geq 1
.

Theorem 54
.

The
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group equipped with the Lebesgue measure does not satisfies the
\mathsf{MCP}(K,N)
for all
K\in\mathds{R}
and
N\geq 1
.

Proof.

As in the beginning of the proof of
Theorem

43
, it is enough to show that
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
fails. We are going to find a Borel subset
\Omega
of
\mathds{H}
with
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
such that
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})=0
for some
t\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
. By
Proposition

5
, this will disprove the measure contraction property
\mathsf{MCP}(0,N)
for all
N\geq 1
.

As we observed previously, a point
h=(x,y,z)\in H_{2}
such that
|x|<y
and
z>0
is connected to the identity by a unique geodesic
\gamma_{h}
. This geodesic is an
\ell^{\infty}
-arc consisting of three segments with directions
\partial_{x}
,
\partial_{y}
, and
-\partial_{x}
. We denote the lengths of these segments as
l_{1}(h)
,
l_{2}(h)
, and
l_{3}(h)
, respectively.

Let us fix a point
h_{0}\in H_{2}
with

\frac{l_{1}(h_{0})}{l_{2}(h_{0})}=\frac{l_{3}(h_{0})}{l_{2}(h_{0})}=\frac{1}{2}.

Then there is a neighborhood
\Omega
of
h_{0}
in
H_{2}
such that for all
h\in\Omega
,

\frac{l_{1}(h)}{l_{2}(h)},\frac{l_{3}(h)}{l_{2}(h)}\in\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}\right).

Since geodesics are constant speed,
we can see that for all
h\in\Omega
and
t\in[0,\frac{1}{8}]
,

\gamma_{h}(t)
is in the
x
-axis of
\mathds{H}
.
In particular,

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})=0
for
t\in[0,\frac{1}{8}]
.

∎

Lastly, we calculate the geodesic dimension of the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group, which will be derived from the following observations.

Let
h=(x,y,z)\in H_{1}\cap\{x,y>0\}
, and
\alpha:=x+y
. For
t\in(0,1)
, we define

F_{t,\alpha}:=\left\{(ut,(\alpha-u)t,v)\in\mathds{H}\mid u\in\mathopen{[}0,\alpha\mathclose{]}{},\penalty\ |v|\leq\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}\right\}\subseteq H_{1}.

Note that
\alpha=\|(x,y)\|_{1}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{1}(0,h)
,
and therefore all minimising constant speed geodesic defined on
\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
joining
0
to
h
will pass through
F_{t,\alpha}
at time
t
.
In other words,

\left\{\gamma(t)\mid\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}\text{ minimising constant speed geodesic from $0$ to $h$}\right\}\subseteq F_{t,\alpha}.

In the following lemma, we show that for
t
sufficiently small, the inclusion above is, in fact, an equality.

Lemma 55
.

For
h=(x,y,z)\in\mathrm{int}(H_{1})\cap\{x,y>0\}
,
define the number
T=T(h)\in(0,1)
by

T(h):=\min\left(\frac{\min(x,y)}{\alpha},\frac{\frac{1}{2}xy-z}{x\alpha},\frac{\frac{1}{2}xy+z}{y\alpha}\right),

where
\alpha:=x+y
. Then, for all
t\leq T
,

\left\{\gamma(t)\mid\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}\text{ minimising constant speed geodesic from $0$ to $h$}\right\}=F_{t,\alpha}.

(45)

Proof.

We are going to establish the following claim, which implies the lemma:

\text{For all $t\leq T$ and all $g=(ut,(\alpha-u)t,v)\in F_{t,\alpha}$,
$g^{-1}h$ is in $H_{1}$}.

(46)

Indeed, by
Proposition

53
and left-invariance,
Equation

46

yields that there is a minimising geodesic
\gamma_{2}
from
g
to
h
such that the projection to
xy
-plane is an
\ell^{1}
-geodesic.
Moreover,
by the definition of
F_{t,\alpha}

there is a minimising geodesic
\gamma_{1}
from
0
to
g
with the same property.
The concatenation of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
is, after reparametrisation, a minimising constant speed geodesic defined on
\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
from
0
to
h
.
Since the choice of
g
is arbitrary,
this implies (
45
).

We now proceed to prove
Equation

46
. A direct computation shows that

g^{-1}h=\left(x-ut,y-(\alpha-u)t,z-v+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}\right).

Therefore, we have that
g^{-1}h\in H_{1}
if and only if

\left\lvert z-v+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}\right\rvert\leq\frac{|x-ut||y-(\alpha-u)t|}{2}.

Since
t\leq T\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\min(x,y)
and
u\in\mathopen{[}0,\alpha\mathclose{]}
,
both
x-ut
and
y-(\alpha-u)t
are positive.
Hence the desired inequality is equivalent to

-\frac{(x-ut)(y-(\alpha-u)t)}{2}\leq z-v+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}\leq\frac{(x-ut)(y-(\alpha-u)t)}{2},

(47)

for all
t\leq T
,

|v|\leq\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}
and
u\in[0,\alpha]
.
Since
|v|\leq\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}
, we have

z-v+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}\leq z+\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2},

as well as

z-v+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}\geq z-\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}.

We set the functions
G_{1}(u)
and
G_{2}(u)
by

G_{1}(u):=z-\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}+\frac{(x-ut)(y-(\alpha-u)t)}{2}

and

G_{2}(u):=z+\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}+t\frac{x(\alpha-u)-yu}{2}-\frac{(x-ut)(y-(\alpha-u)t)}{2}.

Thus, the inequality
Equation

47
holds if
G_{1}(u)\geq 0
and
G_{2}(u)\leq 0
, for all
u\in\mathopen{[}0,\alpha\mathclose{]}
. The function
G_{1}(u)
(resp.
G_{2}(u)
) is linear in
u
, and attains its minimum at
u=\alpha
(resp. its maximum
u=0
). Therefore, we obtain

G_{1}(u)\geq G_{1}(\alpha)=z+\frac{xy}{2}-ty\alpha\geq 0,\text{ and }G_{2}(u)\leq G_{2}(0)=z-\frac{xy}{2}+tx\alpha\leq 0,

since, in the first case,
t\leq T\leq\frac{\frac{xy}{2}+z}{y\alpha}
, and since, in the second case,
t\leq T\leq\frac{\frac{xy}{2}-z}{x\alpha}
.

Theorem 56
.

The geodesic dimension of the
\ell^{1}
-Heisenberg group equipped with the Lebesgue measure is
4

Proof.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathrm{int}(H_{1})
be a compact subset with
0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
. We introduce

T(\Omega):=\min\{T(h)\mid h\in\Omega\},

and

m=:\min\{x+y\mid(x,y,z)\in\Omega\},\ M:=\max\{x+y\mid(x,y,z)\in\Omega\}.

By the definition of
T(h)
in
Lemma

\Omega
is chosen to be compact, the constants

are both positive and finite. By

for all

\Omega_{t}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in[m,M]}F_{t,\alpha}=\left\{(ut,(\alpha-u)t,v)\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ \alpha\in[m,M],u\in[0,\alpha],\penalty\ |v|\leq\frac{u(\alpha-u)t^{2}}{2}\right\}.

Hence, the volume of
\Omega_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})={}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Proj}(\Omega_{t})}xy\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

by Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{m}^{M}\int_{0}^{\alpha}u(\alpha-u)t^{2}\left\lvert\det\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Psi(u,\alpha)\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle\Psi(u,\alpha):=(ut,(\alpha-u)t)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle t^{4}\int_{m}^{M}\int_{0}^{\alpha}u(\alpha-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha=t^{4}\frac{M^{4}-m^{4}}{24}.

In particular, we have that

\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t^{4}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{t})}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}=\frac{M^{4}-m^{4}}{24\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}>0,

and thus from

C_{4}(e)=+\infty

N_{geod}\leq 4

On the other hand, the geodesic dimension is no less than the Hausdorff dimension (see

N_{\mathrm{geo}}\geq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\mathds{H}=4
. Therefore we have
N_{\mathrm{geo}}=4

∎
