A survey of Heffter arrays
Anita Pasotti, Jeffrey H. Dinitz

Abstract
Heffter arrays were introduced by Archdeacon in 2015 as an interesting link between combinatorial designs and topological graph theory. Since the initial paper on this topic, there has been a good deal of interest in Heffter arrays as well as in related topics such as the sequencing of subsets of a group, biembeddings of cycle systems on a surface, and orthogonal cycle systems. This survey presents an overview of the current state of the art of this topic. We begin with an introduction to Heffter arrays for the reader who is unfamiliar with the subject, then we give a unified and comprehensive presentation of the major results, showing some proof methods also. This survey also includes sections on the connections of Heffter arrays to several other combinatorial objects, such as problems on partial sums and sequenceability, biembedding graphs on surfaces, difference families and orthogonal graph decompositions. These sections are followed by a section discussing the variants and generalizations of Heffter arrays which have been proposed. The survey itself is complemented by a list of unsolved problems as well as an updated and complete bibliography.



Dedicated to Doug Stinson on the occasion of his 66th birthday.

Introduction and Background

The notion of a Heffter array was introduced by Archdeacon
[
7
]
in 2015. The original motivation was to aid in an embedding problem in topological graph theory, but they were also introduced as a very interesting combinatorial design object. The name is derived from
their connection to Heffter’s first difference problem, a problem related
to the construction of Steiner triple systems
[
24
]
.
Specifically, in 1896 Heffter
[
49
]
introduced his famous
First Difference Problem

which asks if, given
v\equiv 1\pmod{6}
, the set
\left\{1,2,\ldots,\frac{v-1}{2}\right\}
can be
partitioned into
\frac{v-1}{6}
triples
\{x,y,z\}
such that either
x+y=z
or
x+y+z=v
.
The problem was eventually solved in 1939 by Peltesohn
[
68
]
. We will show later in this survey that this problem is essentially solved (with an even stronger property) by a Heffter array with 3 rows and
\frac{v-1}{6}
columns.

We begin with some definitions.
Let
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
be the cyclic group of odd order
v
whose elements are denoted by
0
and
\pm i
where

i=1,\ldots,\frac{v-1}{2}
. A
half-set

L
of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus\{0\}
of size
\frac{v-1}{2}

that contains
exactly one of each pair
\{x,-x\}
. A
Heffter system

D(v,k)
, on a half-set
L
of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
, is a partition of
L
into
parts of size
k
such that the elements in each part sum to
0
modulo
v
, see
[
7
,
60
]
.
Clearly a necessary condition for the existence of such a system is
v\equiv 1\pmod{2k}
.

Example 1.1

A Heffter system
D(31,5)
:

\{\{6,7,-10,-4,1\},\{-9,5,2,-11,13\},\{3,-12,8,15,-14\}\}.

It is easy to see that starting from a Heffter system
D(v,3)
one immediately gets a solution to the Heffter’s First
Difference Problem, as shown in the following example.

Example 1.2

A Heffter system
D(31,3)
:

\{\{6,3,-9\},\{7,5,-12\},\{2,8,-10\},\{-4,-11,15\},\{1,13,-14\}\}.

Then
\{\{6,3,9\},\{7,5,12\},\{2,8,10\},\{4,11,15\},\{1,13,14\}\}
is a solution to the Heffter’s First
Difference Problem with
v=31
. Using this Heffter system we get the following base blocks
\{\{0,6,9\},\{0,7,12\},\{0,2,10\},\{0,4,15\},\{0,1,14\}\}
which can then be developed in
\mathbb{Z}_{31}
to construct a Steiner triple system of order 31. A detailed discussion of the construction of cycle systems from Heffter systems can be found in Section
5
.

Two Heffter systems
D_{m}=D(2mn+1,m)
and
D_{n}=D(2mn+1,n)
on the same
half-set
L
of
\mathbb{Z}_{2mn+1}
are
orthogonal
if each part of
D_{m}
intersects each
part of
D_{n}
in exactly one
element, see
[
7
]
. By a direct check one can see that the Heffter systems of Examples
1.1
and
1.2
are orthogonal.

In
[
7
]
Archdeacon defined a
Heffter array

H(m,n)
as an
m\times n
array whose rows form a
D(2mn+1,n)

and whose columns form a
D(2mn+1,m)
; these are called the row and column Heffter
systems, respectively. Note that a Heffter array
H(m,n)
is equivalent to a pair of orthogonal Heffter
systems. An
H(m,n)
gives two orthogonal Heffter systems by definition; its associated row system and its associated column system.
Conversely, two orthogonal Heffter systems
D(2mn+1,m)
and
D(2mn+1,n)
give rise to an
H(m,n)

whose cell of indices
i,j
contains the only element which the
i
-th part of the first Heffter system shares with the
j
-th part of the second one.

Example 1.3

Starting from the orthogonal Heffter systems of Examples
1.1
and
1.2

we obtain the following Heffter array
H(3,5)
whose elements belong to
\mathbb{Z}_{31}
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr 6&7&-10&-4&1\\
\hline\cr-9&5&2&-11&13\\
\hline\cr 3&-12&8&15&-14\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

In the same paper Archdeacon proposed a variation of Heffter arrays which allows for some empty cells. Two Heffter systems

D_{h}=D(2mh+1,h)
and
D_{k}=D(2nk+1,k)
on the same half-set of size
mh=nk
are
sub-orthogonal
if
each part of
D_{h}
intersects each part of
D_{k}
in
at most
one element (see
[
5
]
for information on sub-orthogonal factorizations). We note here that the term
sub-orthogonal
has not been widely adopted, hence for the remainder of this survey we will
use
orthogonal
to denote either orthogonal or sub-orthogonal. As
before, we can form an
m\times n
array, denoted by
H(m,n;h,k)
, whose cell of indices
i,j
either
contains the common element in the
i
-th part
of
D_{h}
and the
j
-th part of
D_{k}
, if any, or is empty otherwise.
The
H(m,n)
defined above is nothing but a
H(m,n;n,m)
.

In other words, when empty cells are allowed, a Heffter array can be defined as follows (this is now the standard definition):

Definition 1.4

A
Heffter array

H(m,n;h,k)
is an
m\times n
matrix with entries from
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
such that:

(a)

each row contains
h
filled cells and each column contains
k
filled cells;

(b)

for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}\setminus\{0\}
, either
x
or
-x
appears in the array;

(c)

the elements in every row and column sum to
0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
.

Example 1.5

An
H(6,12;8,4)
over
\mathbb{Z}_{97}
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr&&-1&2&5&-6&&&-25&26&29&-30\\
\hline\cr&&3&-4&-7&8&&&27&-28&-31&32\\
\hline\cr 9&-10&&&-13&14&33&-34&&&-37&38\\
\hline\cr-11&12&&&15&-16&-35&36&&&39&-40\\
\hline\cr-17&18&21&-22&&&-41&42&45&-46&&\\
\hline\cr 19&-20&-23&24&&&43&-44&-47&48&&\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Note that by the pigeon-hole principle zero cannot appear in the array, hence
Condition (b) of Definition
1.4
is equivalent to requiring that
the elements appearing in the array form a half-set of
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
.
It is easy to see that trivial necessary conditions for the existence of an
H(m,n;h,k)
are
mh=nk
,
3\leq k\leq m
, and

3\leq h\leq n
.

If a Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
is square, that is
m=n
, then necessarily
h=k
. In this case
it is a
H(n,n;k,k)
and is denoted by
H(n;k)
. And just to clarify, a
H(n,k)
(note the comma in place of the semicolon) is a Heffter array with no empty cells and with
n
rows and
k
columns,
or a
H(n,k;k,n)
.

Example 1.6

An
H(5;4)
over
\mathbb{Z}_{41}
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr&17&-8&-14&5\\
\hline\cr 1&&18&-9&-10\\
\hline\cr-6&2&&19&-15\\
\hline\cr-11&-12&3&&20\\
\hline\cr 16&-7&-13&4&\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

A Heffter array is an
integer Heffter array
if condition
(c)
of Definition
1.4

is strengthened so that the elements in every row and every column, seen as integers in
\pm\{1,2,\ldots,nk\}
, sum to zero in
\mathbb{Z}
.

,

and

A class of Heffter arrays of fundamental importance and which are particularly useful in recursive constructions of are so called
shiftable
arrays, see
[
9
]
.
An
n\times n
array
A
(possibly with empty cells) whose elements are integers is shiftable if each row and each column of
A

contains the same number of positive and negative numbers.
Given a shiftable array
A
and a nonnegative integer
x
, let
A\pm x
denote the array where
x
is added to all the positive entries in
A

and
-x
is added to all the negative entries.
Define the
support
of
A
as the set containing the absolute value of the elements appearing in
A
.
Note that if
A
is shiftable with support
S
and
x
is a nonnegative integer, then
A\pm x
has the same row and column sums as
A
and has support
S+x
.
In the case of a shiftable integer Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
, the array

H(m,n;h,k)\pm x
has row and column sums equal to zero and its support is
\{1+x,2+x,\ldots,nk+x\}
.
The arrays of Examples
1.5
and
1.6
are shiftable. The existence of shiftable square Heffter arrays was established
by constructive proofs in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 1.9

[
9
]

There exists a shiftable integer
H(n;k)
if and only if
k
is even and
nk\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

Many of the known square Heffter arrays have a diagonal structure which has been shown to be useful for recursive constructions
and for the applications to biembeddings. In addition, this diagonal structure has been extremely useful in computer searches for Heffter arrays. Given a square array of order
n
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the
i
-th diagonal is defined by

D_{i}=\{(i,1),(i+1,2),\ldots,(i-1,n)\}
. All the arithmetic on row and column indices is performed modulo
n
, where the set of reduced
residues is
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. The diagonals
D_{i},D_{i+1},\ldots,D_{i+k-1}
are
k

consecutive diagonals
.
Given
n\geq k\geq 1
, a partially filled array
A
of order
n
is
k
-
diagonal
if its nonempty cells are exactly those of
k
diagonals.
Moreover, if these diagonals are consecutive,
A
is said to be
cyclically

k
-
diagonal
. The array of Example
1.6
is cyclically
4
-diagonal.

Shiftable cyclically
4
-diagonal
H(n;4)
are constructed in
[
9
]
for all
n\geq 4
. In view of their particular structure,
these arrays can be used to add four filled cells per row and column to an existing Heffter array
H
if
H

contains four consecutive diagonals of empty cells. This is described in the following lemma and example.

Lemma 1.10

[
9
]

If there exists an integer Heffter array
H(n;k)
which has
s
disjoint sets of four consecutive empty diagonals, then there exists an
integer

H(n;k+4s)
. Furthermore, if the
H(n;k)
is shiftable, then the
H(n;k+4s)
is shiftable too.

Concerning the search for Heffter arrays constructed from diagonals, a useful notation was introduced in
[
40

and has been used in several subsequent papers including
[

All the constructions in
[
40

were based on filling in the cells of a fixed collection of diagonals. To aid in the constructions the authors defined the following procedure for filling a sequence of cells on a diagonal. It is termed
diag
and it has six parameters, as follows.

In an
n\times n
array
A
the procedure

diag(r,c,s,\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\ell)
installs the entries of an array
A
as follows:

A[r+i\Delta_{1},c+i\Delta_{1}]=s+i\Delta_{2}\mbox{ \ for \ }i=0,1,\dots,\ell-1.

Here all arithmetic on the row and column indices is performed modulo
n
,
where the set of reduced residues is
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
The following summarizes the parameters used in the
diag
procedure:

\bullet

r
denotes the starting row,

\bullet

c
denotes the starting column,

\bullet

s
denotes the starting symbol,

\bullet

\Delta_{1}
denotes how much the row and column indices change at each step,

\bullet

\Delta_{2}
denotes how much the symbol changes at each step, and

\bullet

\ell
is the length of the chain.

We now turn to the case of
rectangular Heffter arrays without empty cells
H(m,n)
, or so-called tight Heffter arrays.
A complete constructive solution is presented in
[
8
]
. Historically speaking, it was this paper that introduced the notion of shiftable Heffter arrays. The main theorem of that paper is given next.

Theorem 1.16

[
8
]

There exists an
H(m,n)
if and only if
m,n\geq 3
.
Moreover, these arrays are integer if and only if
mn\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
.

It has not been proven that integer rectangular arrays with empty cells exist for all possible orders. We note here that the necesary conditions for the existence of an integer
H(m,n;h,k)
are that

3\leq h\leq n
,
3\leq k\leq m
,
mh=nk
and
nk\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
.
Recent partial results for integer rectangular arrays with empty cells can be found in
[
65
]
. We summarize these results in the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.17

[
65
]

Let
m,n,h,k
be such that
3\leq h\leq n
,
3\leq k\leq m
and
mh=nk
.
Set
d=\gcd(h,k)
. There exists an integer

H(m,n;h,k)
in each of the following cases:

(\rm{1})

d\equiv 0\pmod{4}
;

(\rm{2})

d\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with
d\geq 5
and
nk\equiv 3\pmod{4}
;

(\rm{3})

d\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and
nk\equiv 0\pmod{4}
;

(\rm{4})

d\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
nk\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
.

Theorem 1.18

[
65
]

Let
m,n,h,k
be such that
3\leq h\leq n
,
3\leq k\leq m
and
mh=nk
.
If
h\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
k\neq 5
is odd, then
there exists an integer
H(m,n;h,k)
.

This survey is organized as follows. In Section
2
we discuss results on simple and globally simple Heffter arrays. Then in Section
3
we give the application of Heffter arrays to biembedding complete graphs on topological surfaces. In Section
4
we consider the problem of sequencing subsets of a group. Section
5
gives results concerning the connection between Heffter arrays, difference families and graph decompositions and in Section
6
we survey results concerning variants and generalizations of Heffter arrays. Finally in Section
7
we give some open problems related to Heffter arrays and the other objects covered in this survey.

2
Simple Heffter Arrays

In
[
7
]
, Archdeacon showed that Heffter arrays can be used to construct (simple) cycle decompositions of the complete graph
if they satisfy an additional condition, called
simplicity
.
This concept is also related to several open problems on partial sums and sequenceability, these questions will be discussed in the Section
4
.

Let
T
be a finite subset of an additive group
G
. Given an ordering
\omega=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k})
of the elements in
T
, let
s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}t_{j}

be the
i
-th partial sum of
\omega
.
The ordering
\omega
is said to be
simple
if its partial sums
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}
are pairwise distinct.
We point out that, if
\sum_{t\in T}t=0
, then an ordering
\omega
of the elements in
T
is simple if and only if it does not contain any
proper subsequence that sums to
0
. We also note that if
\omega=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k})
is a simple ordering so is
\omega^{-1}=(t_{k},t_{k-1},\ldots,t_{1})
.

In the following, with a little abuse of notation, we will identify each row and column of an
H(m,n;h,k)
with the subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
of size
h
and
k
, respectively,
whose elements are the entries of the nonempty cells of such a row or column. For example we can view the first
row of the
H(8;7)
of Example
1.11
as the subset
R_{1}=\{8,16,25,-27,-29,31,-24\}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{113}
.

Definition 2.1

An
H(m,n;h,k)
is
simple
if each row and each column admits a simple ordering.

It is easy to see that since each row and each column of an
H(m,n;h,k)
does not contain
0
or
2
-subsets of the form

\{x,-x\}
, and is such that
s_{h}=0
and
s_{k}=0
, respectively, then every
H(m,n;h,k)
with
h,k\leq 5
is simple (indeed
any
subset of size
k\leq 10
with these properties is simple
[
3
]
).

Example 2.2

The
H(8;7)
of
Example
1.11
is simple. To verify this property we need to provide a simple ordering for each row and each column.
One can check that the
\omega_{i}
’s are simple orderings of the rows and the

3
Applications to biembeddings

One of the main reasons that Archdeacon introduced the notion of Heffter arrays is their connection with biembedding cycle systems on a topological surface.
Several papers focus on this application, see, for instance
[
23
,
33
,
36
,
39
]
.
We emphasize that there has been previous work done in the area of biembedding cycle systems, especially triple systems, see
[
41
,
42
,
45
,
46
]
. It is interesting to
note that some of the earliest examples of Heffter arrays are derived from direct
constructions of biembeddings. In particular, in
[
9
]
the authors used current graphs (developed by Youngs
[
72
]
) based on Möbius ladders with
n=4m+1
rungs and on cylindrical ladders with
n=4m
rungs to construct diagonal Heffter arrays
H(n;3)
. So not only do Heffter arrays yield biembeddings (as will be detailed in this section), but biembeddings have been used to make Heffter arrays.

We recall some basic definitions, see
[
61
]
and also note that
[
48
]
and
[
62
]
are excellent references for graphs on surfaces.
An
embedding
of a graph (or multigraph)
\Gamma
in a surface
\Sigma
is a continuous injective
mapping
\psi:\Gamma\to\Sigma
, where
\Gamma
is viewed with the usual topology as
1
-dimensional simplicial complex.
The connected components of
\Sigma\setminus\psi(\Gamma)
are called
\psi
-
faces
.
If each
\psi
-face is homeomorphic to an open disc, then the embedding
\psi
is said to be
cellular
.
In this context, we say that two embeddings
\psi:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma
and
\psi^{\prime}:\Gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\Sigma^{\prime}
are isomorphic if and
only if there is a graph isomorphism
\sigma:\Gamma\rightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma(F)
is a
\psi^{\prime}
-face if and only
if
F
is a
\psi
-face.
A circuit decomposition of a graph
\Gamma
is a collection of edge-disjoint subgraphs of
\Gamma
such that every edge of
\Gamma
belongs to exactly one circuit.

Definition 3.1

A
biembedding
of two circuit decompositions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of a simple graph
\Gamma
is a face
2
-colorable embedding
of
\Gamma
in which one color class is comprised of the circuits in
\mathcal{D}
and
the other class contains exactly the circuits in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

If the faces are cycles the biembedding is said to be
simple
.
When one is not interested in the circuits, we simply speak of a biembedding of the graph
\Gamma
.
Equivalently, a biembedding of a simple graph
\Gamma
is a face
2
-colorable embedding of
\Gamma
.

Example 3.2

A biembedding of the complete graph
K_{7}
on the torus. Note that the black cells are a Steiner triple system (a circuit decomposition of
K_{7}
into triples) and the white cells are also a Steiner triple system.

In order to state our main theorem connecting Heffter arrays to biembeddings (Theorem
3.11
), we need the notion of compatible orderings of the rows and columns of the Heffter array.

Definition 3.3

Let
A
be a partially filled array with
F
filled cells whose elements are pairwise distinct.
Let
\omega_{r}
(
\omega_{c}
, respectively) be any ordering of the rows (columns, respectively) of
A
.
Then
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
are
compatible
orderings if
\omega_{r}\circ\omega_{c}
is a cycle of order
F
.

Example 3.4

Given the
H(3,5)
from Example
1.3
, use the natural ordering of the rows and columns to get row cycles

\omega_{r}=(6,7,{-10},{-4},1)(-9,5,2,{-11},13)(3,{-12},8,15,{-14})

and the column cycles

\omega_{c}=(6,{-9},3)(7,5,{-12})({-10},2,8)({-4},{-11},15)(1,13,{-14})
.
Then we see that

\omega_{r}\circ\omega_{c}=(6,5,8,{-4},13,3,7,2,15,1,{-9},{-12},{-10},{-11},{-14})

is a single cycle (of order 15) and hence
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
are compatible orderings.

We will discuss the connection between compatible orderings and biembeddings below. It is because of this connection that the existence of compatible orderings for a given array has been considered in several papers, see
[
23
,
26
,
33
,
39
]
. The following theorem details the necessary conditions for there to exist
orderings
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
of the row and column Heffter systems of a Heffter array that are compatible.

Theorem 3.5

[
23
,
39
]

If there exist compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
for a Heffter array

H(m,n;h,k)
, then
either:

(1)

m,n,h
and
k
are all odd;

(2)

m
is odd,
n
is even and
h
is even; or

(3)

m
is even,
n
is odd and
k
is even.

Hence, if a square Heffter array

admits compatible orderings, then
nk
is odd.
This condition is also sufficient when

is small and the array is cyclically
k
-diagonal.
In fact the following holds:

Proposition 3.6

[
26
]

Let
3\leq k<200
be an integer and
let

be a cyclically

\omega_{r}
and

of

and

are both odd.

In view of Theorem

and

[

[

Theorem 3.10

Let

Compatible orderings are the link between Heffter arrays and biembeddings, while the simplicity of a Heffter array will guarantee the biembedding is simple also. The following fundamental theorem from
[
7
]
gives the connection between Heffter arrays with compatible orderings and biembeddings. The interested reader is referred to that paper for the full details of this connection, but briefly, using the compatible orderings of the Heffter array one obtains a current graph which in turn gives the rotation of the edges around each vertex. The rows and columns give the cycles in the two cycle decompositions.

Theorem 3.11

[
7
]

Given a Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
with compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
on the rows and
\omega_{c}
on the columns,
there exists a biembedding of the complete graph
K_{2nk+1}

on an orientable surface such that every edge is
on a face of size
h
and a face of size
k
. Moreover, if
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
are both simple, then all
faces are simple cycles.

A
k
-cycle system of order

is a set of cycles of length
k
, denoted by
C_{k}
, whose edges partition the edges of
K_{n}
,
one speaks also of a

-decomposition of

.
Restating the previous theorem in terms of biembeddings of cycle systems we have the following.

Theorem 3.12

[
7
]

Given a Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
with simple compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
on the rows and
\omega_{c}
on the columns, there exists an orientable biembedding
of a

-decomposition and a

-decomposition both of
K_{2nk+1}
, or equivalently, there is an orientable biembedding
of an

-cycle system and a
k
-cycle system both in

.

We note again that a discussion of the construction of cycle systems from Heffter arrays will be given in Section

[

The following problem was introduced in
[
26
]
in view of its importance in looking for compatible orderings for Heffter arrays.
However, it can also be considered as a tour problem on a toroidal board which is interesting
per se
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
toroidal partially filled array. By
r_{i}
we denote the orientation of the
i
-th row of
A
,
precisely
r_{i}=1
if it is from left to right and
r_{i}=-1
if it is from right to left. Analogously, for the
j
-th
column, if its orientation
c_{j}
is from top to bottom then
c_{j}=1
otherwise
c_{j}=-1
. Assume that an orientation

\mathcal{R}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})

and
\mathcal{C}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
is fixed. Given an initial filled cell
(i_{1},j_{1})
consider the sequence

L_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{C}}(i_{1},j_{1})=((i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{\ell},j_{\ell}),

(i_{\ell+1},j_{\ell+1}),\ldots)

where
j_{\ell+1}
is the column index of the filled cell
(i_{\ell},j_{\ell+1})
of the row
R_{i_{\ell}}
next
to

(i_{\ell},j_{\ell})
in the orientation
r_{i_{\ell}}
,
and where
i_{\ell+1}
is the row index of the filled cell of the column
C_{j_{\ell+1}}
next to

(i_{\ell},j_{\ell+1})
in the orientation
c_{j_{\ell+1}}
.
The problem is the following:

Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem.
[
26
]

Given a toroidal partially filled array
A
,
do there exist
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{C}
such that the list
L_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{C}}
covers all the filled
cells of
A
?

The Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem for a given array
A
is denoted by
P(A)
.
Also, given a filled cell
(i,j)
, if
L_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{C}}(i,j)
covers all the filled positions of
A
, then

(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{C})
is said to be a solution of
P(A)
.

The relationship between globally simple Heffter arrays, Crazy Knight’s

Theorem 3.16

Let
A
be a globally simple Heffter array

If there exists a solution of

, then there exists a biembedding of

K_{2nk+1}
on an orientable surface whose face boundaries are

-cycles.

[

Let

\gcd(n,n-1-\alpha-2t)=1

with row and column orderings that are
both simple and compatible.

Theorem 3.18

[
23
]

Let
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
,
n>k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and either:
(a)

.

Definition 3.19

Two Heffter arrays

are said to be equivalent if one can
be obtained from the other by (i) rearranging rows or columns; (ii) replacing every entry

The following theorem gives a lower bound on the number of inequivalent Heffter arrays

[

The other case, that is

[

]

4
Sequencing Subsets of a Group

We know from Theorem
3.11
that if there is a simple Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
(with compatible orderings), then there is a biembedding of
K_{2nk+1}

on an orientable surface such that every edge is
on a face of size
h
and a face of size
k
and all
faces are simple cycles. It is thus a natural question to ask just when the rows and columns of a Heffter array have a simple ordering, or more generally, when subsets of a group have a simple ordering. Indeed, if this is always the case, then Heffter arrays would always be simple and the condition for simplicity of the array would be unnecessary. It has indeed been an active area of research to show this, but at this time it is still not known for all subsets (and all groups).
In this section we give some conjectures relating to the sequencing of subsets of groups as well as some of the results in this area.

We begin with the more classical notion of a sequenceable group.
Let
G
be a group of order
n
(written additively). Given an ordering
\omega=(0,g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n-1})
of the elements in
G
, let
s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}g_{j}

be the
i
-th partial sum of
\omega
. The group
G
is
sequenceable
if there exists some ordering of the elements of
G
where all of these partial sums

s_{0}=0,s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}
are distinct
(equivalently, if
G
admits a simple ordering), while it is
R-sequenceable
if
s_{0}=0,s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-2}
are distinct
and in addition
s_{n-1}=0
.
The concept of sequenceable group was introduced in 1961 by Gordon
[
47
]
,
even if similar ideas for cyclic groups were already presented in 1892 by Lucas
[
58
]
.
Alspach et al.
[
4
]
proved that any finite abelian group is either sequenceable or R-sequenceable,
confirming the Friedlander-Gordon-Miller conjecture.

Here is a short summary of the results concerning the sequencing and R-sequencing of groups. For an extensive survey on this topic the reader can refer to
[
66
]
.

Proposition 4.1

Results on sequencing of groups.

(\rm{1})

An abelian group is sequenceable if and only if it has a unique element of order
2
.

(\rm{2})

No non-abelian group of order less than
10
is sequenceable. But Keedwell conjectures that all non-abelian groups of order greater than
10
are sequenceable.

(\rm{3})

Keedwell’s conjecture has been proved true for the following classes of groups:

(\rm{a})

All non-abelian groups of order
n
,
10\leq n\leq 32
;

(\rm{b})

The dihedral groups
D_{n}
;

(\rm{c})

A_{5}
and
S_{5}
;

(\rm{d})

Solvable groups with a unique element of order
2
;

(\rm{e})

Some non-solvable groups with a unique element of order
2
;

(\rm{f})

Some groups of order
pq
,
p
and
q
odd primes.

Proposition 4.2

Results on R-sequencing of groups.

(\rm{1})

If
G
is an R-sequenceable group, then its Sylow
2
-subgroup is either trivial or non-cyclic.

(\rm{2})

The following groups are R-sequenceable:

(\rm{a})

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
n
odd;

(\rm{b})

Abelian groups of order
n
,
gcd(n,6)=1
;

(\rm{c})

D_{n}
,
n
even;

(\rm{d})

Q_{2n}
if

is a prime of the form

, for which
-2
is a primitive root;

(\rm{e})

Non-abelian groups of order
pq
,
p<q
odd primes, with
2
a primitive root of
p
.

The problem of ordering the elements of a

of a given group in such
a way that all the partial sums are pairwise distinct is more recent than the sequenceable group question. Nonetheless it has also been extensively studied, most recently because of its connection to Heffter arrays, but not only for this reason. Indeed there are several open conjectures on partial sums of a finite group.

For example, several years ago Alspach made
the following conjecture, whose validity would shorten some cases of known proofs
about the existence of cycle decompositions.

Conjecture 4.3

Let
T\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\sum_{t\in T}t\neq 0
. Then there exists an ordering of the elements of
T
such that the partial sums are all
distinct and non-zero.

The first published paper on this problem is by Bode and Harborth
[

in which the authors proved that Conjecture
4.3

is valid if
|T|=v-1
or
|T|=v-2
or if

Recently, several other papers on this topic have been published, see
[

Another conjecture, very close to the Alspach’s conjecture, was originally proposed in 1971 by Graham

for cyclic groups of prime order and then extended in 2015 by

to every cyclic group as follows.

Conjecture 4.4

[

Let

Clearly Conjecture

In

[

Also, if we consider Conjectures

For Conjecture

Finally, we recall that a subset of an (additive) abelian group is said to be

5
Connections with difference families and graph decompositions

In this section we discuss the connection between Heffter arrays and cycle decompositions of the complete graph. We
have already discussed the connection between Heffter arrays and biembeddings of two cycle systems (cycle decompositions). In this section we will explicitly show how one can produce the two cycle decompositions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
and that these cycle decompositions have the property that if two edges are in the same cycle of
\mathcal{D}
, then they will be in different cycles in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

First of all we recall some basic definitions about graphs and graph decompositions.
Good references about these concepts are
[
70
]
and
[
12
]
, respectively.
Given a graph
\Gamma
, by
V(\Gamma)
and
E(\Gamma)
we mean the vertex set and the edge set of
\Gamma
, respectively,
and by
{}^{\lambda}\Gamma
the multigraph obtained from
\Gamma
by repeating each edge
\lambda
times.
We denote by
K_{q\times r}
the complete multipartite graph with
q
parts each of size
r
,
obviously
K_{q\times 1}
is nothing but the complete graph
K_{q}
. Finally, by
P_{k}
we mean the path
of length
k
.

Let
\Gamma
be a subgraph of a graph
K
. A
\Gamma
-
decomposition
of
K
is a set
\mathcal{D}
of subgraphs
of
K
isomorphic to
\Gamma
, called
blocks
, whose edges partition
E(K)
.
As already remarked in the previous section, a
C_{k}
-decomposition of
K_{v}
is also called a
k
-
cycle system of order

v
.

Definition 5.1

Two graph decompositions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of the same graph
K
are
orthogonal

if for every
B\in\mathcal{D}
and every
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
,
B
intersects
B^{\prime}
in at most one edge.

We note the following obvious fact connecting the notions of biembedded cycle systems to orthogonal cycle systems. From this fact, many of our earlier results on biembeddings of cycle systems give results on orthogonal cycle systems.

Proposition 5.2

If there is a simple biembedding
of a cycle system
\mathcal{D}
and a cycle system
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
in
K_{v}
, then
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
are orthogonal cycle systems of order
v
.

If the vertices of
\Gamma
belong to a group
G
,
given
g\in G
, by
\Gamma+g
one means the graph whose vertex set is
V(\Gamma)+g
and whose edge set is
\{\{x+g,y+g\}\mid\{x,y\}\in E(\Gamma)\}
.
An
automorphism group
of a
\Gamma
-decomposition
\mathcal{D}
of
K
is a group of bijections on
V(K)
leaving
\mathcal{D}
invariant.
A
\Gamma
-decomposition of
K
is said to be
regular under a group

G
or
G
-
regular
if it admits
G

as an automorphism group acting sharply transitively on
V(K)
.
Heffter arrays are related to
cyclic
decompositions, namely decompositions regular under a cyclic group.
We recall the following result.

Proposition 5.3

Let
K
be a graph with
v
vertices. A
\Gamma
-decomposition
\mathcal{D}
of
K
is cyclic if and only if,
up to isomorphisms, the following conditions hold:

(\rm{1})

the set of vertices of
K
is
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
;

(\rm{2})

for all
B\in\mathcal{D}
,
B+1\in\mathcal{D}
.

Clearly, to describe a cyclic decomposition it is sufficient to exhibit a complete system
\mathcal{B}

of representatives for the orbits of
\mathcal{D}
under the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
. The elements of
\mathcal{B}

are called
base blocks
of
\mathcal{D}
.

One of most efficient tools applied for constructing regular decompositions is the
difference method
, see
[
1
]
.
In particular, difference families over graphs have been introduced by Buratti
[
14
]
, see also
[
17
]
.
Given a graph
\Gamma
with
vertices in an additive group
G
, the
list of differences
of
\Gamma

is the multiset
\Delta\Gamma
of all possible differences
x-y
between two adjacent vertices of
\Gamma
.
So, denoting by
D(\Gamma)
the set of
di-edges
of
\Gamma
, i.e., the set of all
ordered

pairs
(x,y)
with
\{x,y\}\in E(\Gamma)
, one can write:

\Delta\Gamma=\{x-y\mid(x,y)\in D(\Gamma)\}.

More generally, if
\mathcal{F}=\{\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots,\Gamma_{\ell}\}
is a collection of graphs with vertices
in
G
, one sets
\Delta\mathcal{F}=\Delta\Gamma_{1}\cup\Delta\Gamma_{2}\cup\ldots\cup\Delta\Gamma_{\ell}
, where in the union
each element has to be counted with its multiplicity.

Definition 5.4

Let
J
be a subgroup of a group
G
and let
\Gamma
be a graph.
A collection
\mathcal{F}
of graphs isomorphic to
\Gamma
and with vertices in
G

is said to be a
(G,J,\Gamma,\lambda)
-
difference family
(briefly DF)
over
G
relative to
J
if each element of

G\setminus J
appears exactly
\lambda
times in
\Delta\mathcal{F}
while no element of
J
appears there.

In the case of
J=\{0\}
one simply says that
\mathcal{F}
is a
(G,\Gamma,\lambda)
-DF.
Also, if
t
is a divisor of
v
, by writing
(v,t,\Gamma,\lambda)
-DF one means a
(\mathbb{Z}_{v},\frac{v}{t}\mathbb{Z}_{v},\Gamma,\lambda)
-DF
where
\frac{v}{t}\mathbb{Z}_{v}
denotes the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
of order
t
.
Analogously,
(v,\Gamma,\lambda)
-DF means
(\mathbb{Z}_{v},\Gamma,\lambda)
-DF.
The connection between relative difference families and decompositions of a complete multipartite multigraph is given by the following result.

Proposition 5.5

[
17
]

If
\mathcal{F}=\{\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots,\Gamma_{\ell}\}
is a
(G,J,\Gamma,\lambda)
-DF,
then
\mathcal{D}=\{\Gamma_{i}+g\mid i=1,\ldots,\ell;g\in G\}

is a
G
-regular
\Gamma
-decomposition of
{}^{\lambda}K_{q\times r}
, where
q=|G:J|
and
r=|J|
.
In particular, if
\mathcal{F}
is
(v,\Gamma,1)
-DF then
\mathcal{D}
is a cyclic
\Gamma
-decomposition of
K_{v}
.

Now we show how starting from a simple Heffter array one can construct a pair of orthogonal cyclic cycle decompositions of the complete graph
K_{v}

or of

K_{2\ell}-I

For every

6
Variants and generalizations

In this section we will discuss
several of the variants and generalizations of Heffter arrays that have been researched in earlier publications.

6.1
Weak Heffter arrays

In the original paper on Heffter arrays
[
7
]
, Archdeacon also proposed the following weaker type of Heffter array. These weaker Heffter arrays again have applications to biembedding cycle systems as well as to orthogonal cycle systems.
Two Heffter systems
D_{h}=D(2mh+1,h)
and
D_{k}=D(2nk+1,k)
with
mh=nk
are
weakly sub-orthogonal

if the
i
-th part of
D_{h}
has at most one element
a_{i,j}
such that either
a_{i,j}
or
-a_{i,j}

is in the
j
-th part of
D_{k}
. Form a
weak Heffter array

H(m,n;h,k)
by placing
a_{i,j}
in
the cell on the
i
-th row and the
j
-th column.
The upper sign on
\pm
or
\mp
is the
row sign
corresponding to its sign on
a_{i,j}
in
D_{h}
,
the lower sign is the
column sign
used in
D_{k}
.
Using the row signs we get row sums 0 and the column signs give column sums 0.

The following is a formal definition of a weak Heffter array.

Definition 6.1

A
weak Heffter array

H(m,n;h,k)
is an
m\times n
matrix
A
such that:

(\rm{a_{1}})

each row contains
h
filled cells and each column contains
k
filled cells;

(\rm{b_{1}})

for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}\setminus\{0\}
, there is exactly one cell of
A
whose element is one of the following:

x,-x,\pm x,\mp x
, where the upper sign on
\pm
or
\mp
is the row sign and
the lower sign is the column sign;

(\rm{c_{1}})

the elements in every row and column (with the corresponding sign) sum to
0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
.

Example 6.2

Consider the following Heffter systems on a half-set of
\mathbb{Z}_{25}
:

D_{4}=\{\{1,-7-6,12\},\{2,-4,10,-8\},\{-3,-11,9,5\}\}

and

D_{3}=\{\{1,2,-3\},\{-7,-4,11\},\{-6,-10,-9\},\{12,8,5\}\}.

Note that they are weakly sub-orthogonal and starting from these two systems one gets
the following
3\times 4
weak Heffter array over
\mathbb{Z}_{25}
without empty cells :

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr 1&-7&-6&12\\
\hline\cr 2&-4&\pm 10&\mp 8\\
\hline\cr-3&\mp 11&\pm 9&5\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

To date there are no published papers specifically on weak Heffter arrays, but one is in preparation, see
[
31
]
.
In
[
7
]
Archdeacon showed how these arrays are related to current graphs and hence to biembeddings
(in this case on a
non-orientable
surface).
Also, by reasoning analogous to that in Section
5
, it is easy to see that the following holds.

Proposition 6.3

If there exists a simple weak
H(m,n;h,k)
, then there exist
a
(2mh+1,C_{h},1)
-DF and a
(2nk+1,C_{k},1)
-DF.

Proposition 6.4

If there exists a simple weak
H(m,n;h,k)
, then there exist
a cyclic
C_{h}
-decomposition and
a cyclic
C_{k}
-decomposition both of
K_{2nk+1}
.
These decompositions are orthogonal.

6.2
\lambda
-fold relative Heffter arrays

In
[
34
,
35
]
the authors proposed a natural generalization of Heffter arrays,
which is related to signed magic arrays, difference families,
graph decompositions and biembeddings. In this generalization, the array is missing the elements
of a subgroup
J\subset\mathbb{Z}_{v}
and each element of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus J
occurs exactly
\lambda
times in the array. We give the following definition.

Definition 6.5

Let
v=\frac{2nk}{\lambda}+t
be a positive integer,
where
t
divides
\frac{2nk}{\lambda}
, and
let
J
be the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
of order
t
.
A
\lambda
-
fold Heffter array
A
over
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
relative to
J
, denoted by
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
, is an
m\times n
array
with elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus J
such that:

(\rm{a_{2}})

each row contains
h
filled cells and each column contains
k
filled cells;

(\rm{b_{2}})

the multiset
\{\pm x\mid x\in A\}
contains each element of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus J
exactly
\lambda
times;

(\rm{c_{2}})

the elements in every row and column sum to
0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
.

The terminology is due to their connection with
relative
difference families.
Trivial necessary conditions for the existence of a
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
are
mh=nk
,
2\leq h\leq n
and
2\leq k\leq m
.
It is easy to see that condition
(\rm{b_{2}})
of Definition
6.5
asks that
for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus J
with
x
different from the involution the number of occurrences of
x
and
-x
in the array is
\lambda
,
while if the involution exists and it does not belong to
J
, then it has to appear exactly
\frac{\lambda}{2}
times,
also no element of
J
appears in the array.

If
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
is a square array, then it is denoted by
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(n;k)
.
If
\lambda=t=1
one finds again the classical concept of Heffter array, i.e. an

{}^{1}H_{1}(m,n;h,k)
is just an
H(m,n;h,k)
.
In general if
\lambda=1
, then it is omitted; the same holds for
t
.
Note also that if
\lambda=1
, then
3\leq h\leq n
and
3\leq k\leq m
.

Example 6.6

A
{}^{3}H_{2}(4;3)
whose elements belong to
\mathbb{Z}_{10}
and a
{}^{4}H_{4}(4;2)
whose elements belong to
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr&1&2&-3\\
\hline\cr 4&&4&2\\
\hline\cr-3&2&&1\\
\hline\cr-1&-3&4&\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr 1&-1&&\\
\hline\cr-1&1&&\\
\hline\cr&&3&-3\\
\hline\cr&&-3&3\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

As was the case for Heffter arrays, a
\lambda
-fold relative Heffter array is called
integer
if condition (
\rm{c_{2}}
) in Definition
6.5

is strengthened so that the elements in every row and every column, seen as integer in

\pm\left\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{v}{2}\rfloor\right\}
, sum to
0
in
\mathbb{Z}
.
The
{}^{3}H_{2}(4;3)
of Example
6.6
is not an integer Heffter array, while the
{}^{4}H_{4}(4;2)
in the same example is integer.

Example 6.7

An integer
{}^{2}H(5;3)
over
\mathbb{Z}_{16}

(note that the involution
appears exactly once in the array) and an integer
{}^{2}H(6;4)
over
\mathbb{Z}_{25}
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr 3&5&-8&&\\
\hline\cr&2&5&-7&\\
\hline\cr&&3&1&-4\\
\hline\cr-4&&&6&-2\\
\hline\cr 1&-7&&&6\\

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr&&1&-2&-5&6\\
\hline\cr&&-3&4&7&-8\\
\hline\cr-9&10&&&1&-2\\
\hline\cr 11&-12&&&-3&4\\
\hline\cr 5&-6&-9&10&&\\
\hline\cr-7&8&11&-12&&\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

6.2.1
Necessary conditions

In
[
35
]
the authors established some necessary conditions for the existence of a
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
.
Firstly, it is trivial to note that Conditions (
\rm{a_{2}})
and (
\rm{b_{2}})
of Definition
6.5
imply that
if there exists a
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
with either
h=2
or
k=2
, then
\lambda
has to be even.

Proposition 6.8

[
35
]

Suppose that there exists a
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
and set
v=\frac{2nk}{\lambda}+t
.
If either
v
is odd or
v
and
t
are even, then
\lambda
has to be a divisor of
nk
.

Proposition 6.9

[
35
]

If
\lambda\equiv 2\pmod{4}
,
v=\frac{2nk}{\lambda}+t\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and
t
is odd, then a
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)

cannot exist.

About the integer case there are the following more restrictive conditions.

Proposition 6.10

[
34
,
35
]

Suppose that there exists an integer
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
with
\lambda
odd.

(\rm{1})

If
t
divides
\frac{nk}{\lambda}
, then

\frac{nk}{\lambda}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
or
\frac{nk}{\lambda}\equiv-t\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}.

(\rm{2})

If
t=\frac{2nk}{\lambda}
, then
h
and
k
must be even.

(\rm{3})

If
t\neq\frac{2nk}{\lambda}
does not divide
\frac{nk}{\lambda}
, then

\frac{2nk}{\lambda}+t\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

It is known that these conditions are not sufficient. For instance in
[
34
]
it is proved that
there is no integer
H_{3n}(n;3)
for
n\geq 3
and no integer
H_{8}(4;3)
, moreover in
[
31
]

it is shown the nonexistence of an integer
H_{t}(4;3)
for
t=4,6
.

j
-th column, respectively, of
A
.
Now we have to define a simple ordering of a
multiset
.
We consider the elements of
\mathcal{E}(A)
indexed by the set
skel(A)
(analogously for the rows and the columns).
For example let

be the
{}^{4}H_{4}(4;2)

.
Here
\mathcal{E}(A)=\{-1,-1,1,1,-3,-3,3,3\}
, we can view this multiset as the set

\{t_{b}\mid b\in skel(A)\}=\{t_{(1,1)},t_{(1,2)},t_{(2,1)},t_{(2,2)},t_{(3,3)},t_{(3,4)},t_{(4,3)},t_{(4,4)}\}
,
where
t_{(1,1)}=1
,
t_{(1,2)}=-1
,
t_{(2,1)}=-1
,
t_{(2,2)}=1
,
t_{(3,3)}=3
,
t_{(3,4)}=-3
,

t_{(4,3)}=-3
and
t_{(4,4)}=3
.
Given a finite multiset
T=[t_{b_{1}},\dots,t_{b_{k}}]
whose (not necessarily distinct) elements are indexed by a set
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\}
and a cyclic permutation
\alpha
of

we say that the list
\omega_{\alpha}=(t_{\alpha(b_{1})},t_{\alpha(b_{2})},\ldots,t_{\alpha(b_{k})})
is the ordering of the elements of

associated to
\alpha
. In case the elements of
T
belong to an abelian group
G
, we define
s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}t_{\alpha(b_{j})}
, for any

, to be the
i
-th partial sum of
\omega_{\alpha}
and we set
\mathcal{S}(\omega_{\alpha})=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
.
The ordering
\omega_{\alpha}
is said to be

if
s_{b}\neq s_{c}
for all
1\leq b<c\leq k
.

Proposition 6.27

If there exists a simple
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
, then there exist
a
(2mh+t,t,C_{h},\lambda)
-DF and a
(2nk+t,t,C_{k},\lambda)
-DF.

Proposition 6.28

If there exists a simple
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(m,n;h,k)
, then there exist a cyclic
C_{h}
-decomposition
\mathcal{D}
and
a cyclic

-decomposition

both of

.
If
\lambda=1
the decompositions
\mathcal{D}
and

are orthogonal.

Also for these generalized arrays to get biembeddings one has to find compatible orderings.
Note that in Section
3
we have defined compatible orderings only when
\mathcal{E}(A)
is a set.
Given an
m\times n
partially filled array
A
, by
\alpha_{R_{i}}
we will denote a cyclic permutation of the nonempty cells of the
i
-th row

R_{i}
of

\omega_{R_{i}}
(omitting the dependence on
\alpha_{R_{i}}
) the associated ordering of
\mathcal{E}(R_{i})

j

of

\alpha_{C_{j}}
and
\omega_{C_{j}}

If for any

Now, given a

.

]
the authors showed that the necessary conditions of Theorem

Theorem 6.29

[

]

Let

be a

6.3
Signed Magic Arrays

The notion of a signed magic array was introduced by Khodkar, Schulz and Wagner
[
57
]
in 2017. These are intimately related to (relative) Heffter arrays. We begin with the definition.

Definition 6.30

A
signed magic array

SMA(m,n;h,k)
is an
m\times n
array with entries from
X
,
where
X=\left\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots,\pm\frac{nk-1}{2}\right\}
if

is odd and

if

(\rm{a_{3}})

each row contains
h
filled cells and each column contains
k

(\rm{b_{3}})

every integer from the set
X
appears exactly once in the array;

(\rm{c_{3}})

the sum of each row and of each column is
0
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 6.31

As was shown in
[
35
]
, a signed magic array
SMA(m,n;h,k)
with
nk
even is an integer
{}^{2}H(m,n;h,k)
, while
in general the converse is not true. For instance, the
{}^{2}H(6;4)
of Example
6.7
is not a signed magic array.
On the other hand, in the particular case in which either
h=2
or
k=2
an
integer
{}^{2}H(m,n;h,k)
is precisely an
SMA(m,n;h,k)
, see
[
35
]
for details.

Existence results on signed magic arrays can be found in
[
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
64
,
65
]
. We note that nearly all the results and techniques used on signed magic arrays are analogous to those for Heffter arrays.
In particular, the existence of an
SMA(m,n;h,k)
has been determined in the square case and when the array has no empty cells, as shown in the following two theorems.

Theorem 6.32

[
57
]

There exists an
SMA(n,n;k,k)
if and only if either
n=k=1
or
3\leq k\leq n
.

Theorem 6.33

[
57
]

There exists an
SMA(m,n;n,m)
if and only if one of the following cases occurs:

(1)

m=n=1
;

(2)

m=2
and
n\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
;

(3)

n=2
and
m\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
;

(4)

m,n>2
.

Also the cases where each column contains 2 or 3 filled cells have been solved.

Theorem 6.34

[
53
]

There exists an
SMA(m,n;h,2)
if and only if one of the following cases occurs:

(1)

m=2
and
n=h\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
;

(2)

m,h>2
and
mh=2n
.

Theorem 6.35

[
56
]

There exists an
SMA(m,n;h,3)
if and only if
3\leq m
,
h\leq n
and
mh=3n
.

Necessary and sufficient conditions have been established also in the case when
h
and
k
are both even.

Theorem 6.36

[
64
]

Let
h,k
be two even integers with
h,k\geq 4
. There exists an
SMA(m,n;h,k)
if and only if

4\leq h\leq n
,
4\leq k\leq m
and

.

Recent partial results for the general case can be found in
[

]
. We summarize them in the following two theorems.

Theorem 6.37

[

]

Let

be four integers such that

,

and

.

[

]

Let
m,n,h,k
be four integers such that

6.4
Non-zero sum Heffter arrays

While studying the conjecture of Alspach (Conjecture
4.3
), the authors of
[
30
]
proposed the following variant of a Heffter array which was termed a
non-zero sum Heffter array
. The formal definition follows.

Definition 6.39

Let
v=\frac{2nk}{\lambda}+t
be a positive integer,
where
t
divides
\frac{2nk}{\lambda}
, and
let
J
be the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
of order
t
.
A
\lambda
-
fold non-zero sum Heffter array
A
over
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
relative to
J
, denoted by
{}^{\lambda}NH_{t}(m,n;h,k)
, is an
m\times n
array
with elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
such that:

(\rm{a_{4}})

each row contains
h
filled cells and each column contains
k
filled cells;

(\rm{b_{4}})

the multiset
\{\pm x\mid x\in A\}
contains each element of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus J
exactly
\lambda
times;

(\rm{c_{4}})

the sum of the elements in every row and column
is different from
0

in
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
.

If the array is square, then
h=k\geq 1
, while if
m\neq n
then at least one of
h
and
k
has to be greater than
1
.
A square non-zero sum Heffter array is denoted by
{}^{\lambda}NH_{t}(n;k)
. If
\lambda=1
or
t=1
, then it is omitted.

Example 6.40

An
NH(7;5)
over
\mathbb{Z}_{71}
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr 10&&16&-1&-2&-3&\\
\hline\cr&4&&-6&-7&-5&22\\
\hline\cr-30&29&9&-8&&&18\\
\hline\cr-11&&-12&-28&-31&26&\\
\hline\cr&-14&-15&-13&&-17&25\\
\hline\cr 27&-34&20&&-19&&-32\\
\hline\cr 24&23&&&21&-35&33\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

In
[
30
]
the authors focused on the case in which
\lambda=1
and they proved that the trivial necessary conditions are also sufficient in the very general case
in which the array is rectangular, empty cells are allowed and for every arbitrary subgroup
J
. We would like to note, however, that in the
square case
the proof of existence is quite easy when there exists an
H_{t}(n;k)
. Begin with the
H_{t}(n;k)
and find a transversal of filled cells
T
(this must exist). Then change the sign of the element in each of the cells of
T
and note that now no row or column can add to
0
.
Hence we have constructed an
NH_{t}(n;k)
. The general case is more involved and we emphasize that the proof
of the general result is not constructive.

Theorem 6.41

[
30
]
There exists an
NH_{t}(m,n;h,k)
if and only if
mh=nk
,
m\geq k\geq 1
,
n\geq h\geq 1
and
t
divides
2nk
.

These arrays have been introduced since, as with classical ones, they are related to open problems on partial sums, difference families, graph decompositions and biembeddings. The interested reader is referred to
[
30
]
where all the following results can be found.

By reasoning similar to what was done in Section
5
, it is easy to see that if a row (column, respectively) of a
NH_{t}(m,n;h,k)

admits an ordering whose partial sums are pairwise distinct and non-zero then one can construct a path of length
h
(
k
, respectively).
For example, starting from the first row of the
NH(7;5)
of Example
6.40
and considering its
natural ordering one obtains the path
\Gamma=[0,10,26,25,23,20]

of length
5
, clearly
\Delta\Gamma=\pm R_{1}
.
A non-zero sum Heffter array is said to be
simple
if every row and every column admits an ordering such that the partial sums are pairwise distinct and non-zero.
Hence, since every row and column of a non-zero sum Heffter array can be viewed as a set
T
considered in Conjecture
4.3
, we have that if
Conjecture
4.3
were true, then every non-zero sum Heffter array would be simple. Investigating arrays having this additional property is important in view of the following results.

Proposition 6.42

[
30
]

If there exists a simple
{}^{\lambda}NH_{t}(m,n;h,k)

a
(2mh+t,t,P_{h},\lambda)
-DF and a
(2nk+t,t,P_{k},\lambda)
-DF.

Proposition 6.43

[
30
]

If there exists a simple
{}^{\lambda}NH_{t}(m,n;h,k)
, then there exist

P_{h}
-decomposition
\mathcal{D}
and
a cyclic
P_{k}
-decomposition
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
both of

.
If
\lambda=1
the decompositions
\mathcal{D}
and

are orthogonal.

the authors focused on globally simple non-zero sum Heffter arrays,

6.5
Heffter configurations

Heffter arrays are generalized to
Heffter configurations
in
[
18
]
.
We recall that a
(v,b,k,r)
-configuration is a pair
(V,{\cal B})
where
V
is a set of
v

points
and

\cal B
is a
b
-set of
k
-subsets (
blocks
) of
V
such that any two distinct points are contained
together in at most one block and any point occurs in exactly
r
blocks.
Such a configuration is
resolvable
if there exists a partition of
\cal B
into
r

parallel classes
each
of which is a partition of
V
, see
[
19
,
43
]
.
Also, it is
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
-
additive

if its point set is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
and each block sums to zero.

Definition 6.50

A
(nk,nr,k,r)

Heffter configuration
(briefly HC) is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
-additive resolvable

(nk,nr,k,r)
-configuration where the point set is a half-set of
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
.
It is proper if
r\geq 3
and it is simple if each block admits a simple ordering.

The above terminology is justified by the fact
that a
(nk,nr,k,r)
-HC is equivalent to
r
mutually sub-orthogonal Heffter systems on the same half-set
of
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
.
Indeed each parallel class is nothing but a Heffter system D
(nk,k)
.
Hence,
a Heffter system D
(nk,k)
is a
(nk,n,k,1)
-HC and an
Heffter array
H(n;k)
, say
A
, is a
(nk,2n,k,2)
-HC whose two parallel classes are the rows of
A
and the columns of
A
.

The construction of proper HCs appears to be very hard. On the other
hand some infinite series have been obtained recursively by means
of small examples such as the following.

Example 6.51

The integer shiftable
H(5;4)
below
gives a
(20,15,4,3)
-HC whose three parallel classes are:
the rows;
the columns;
and the diagonals
D_{1},\ldots,D_{5}
.

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr-1&2&17&-18&\\
\hline\cr 6&10&-13&&-3\\
\hline\cr 9&-5&&-8&4\\
\hline\cr-14&&-16&11&19\\
\hline\cr&-7&12&15&-20\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Generalizing Proposition
5.9
, we can state the following.

Theorem 6.52

A simple
(nk,nr,k,r)
-HC gives rise to
r
mutually orthogonal cyclic
C_{k}
-decompositions of
K_{2nk+1}
.

6.6
Further generalizations

Another natural generalization is to work in an arbitrary additive group
G
and not necessarily in the cyclic group.
Some work in this direction has been done in
[
37
]
where the authors proposed the very
general concept of an
Archdeacon array
.

Definition 6.53

[
37
]

An
Archdeacon array
over an abelian group
(G,+)
is an
m\times n
array
A

with elements in
G
, such that:

(\rm{b_{5}})

the multiset
\{\pm x\mid x\in A\}
contains each element of
G\setminus\{0\}
at most once;

(\rm{c_{5}})

the elements in every row and column sum to
0
in
G
.

Example 6.54

An Archdeacon array on
\mathbb{Z}_{41}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, for
d\geq 3
:

\begin{array}[]{|r|r|r|r|r|}\hline\cr(-1,1)&(0,-1)&(-14,0)&(9,0)&(6,0)\\
\hline\cr(2,-1)&(-7,1)&&(-5,0)&(10,0)\\
\hline\cr(17,0)&(12,0)&(-16,0)&&(-13,0)\\
\hline\cr(-18,0)&(15,0)&(11,0)&(-8,0)&\\
\hline\cr&(-20,0)&(19,0)&(4,0)&(-3,0)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Clearly, Heffter arrays are a particular kind of Archdeacon arrays.
Examples, existence results and applications of these arrays can be found in Section
5
of
[
37
]
.

Another general concept termed a
quasi-Heffter array
has recently been proposed in
[
25
]
. In that paper the reader can find all the details
on the applications of these arrays to biembeddings and their full automorphism group.

Definition 6.55

[
25
]

Let
v=2nk+t
be a positive integer, where
t
divides
2nk
, and let
J

be the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
of order
t
. A
quasi-Heffter array

A

over
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
relative to
J
, denoted
by
QH_{t}(m,n;h,k)
, is an
m\times n
array with elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{v}
such that:

(\rm{a_{6}})

each row contains
h
filled cells and each column contains
k
filled cells;

(\rm{b_{6}})

the multiset
\{\pm x\mid x\in A\}
contains each element of
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus J
exactly once.

So in this case the definition does not require any property on the row and column sums.
In general, given
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{v}
one may require that the sums of the rows and of the columns belong to
S
.
Clearly, if
S=\{0\}
we find the classical concept of a Heffter array and if
S=\mathbb{Z}_{v}\setminus\{0\}
we get
the notion of a non-zero sum Heffter array. For other choices of
S
we have further variants of the classical concept having again applications to difference families, graph decompositions and biembeddings.

7
Conclusions and Open Problems

Heffter arrays are a relatively new type of combinatorial design with original paper on this topic published in 2015. Since that time there have been a number of papers published on this subject as well as on topics that are related to Heffter arrays. Here we have surveyed all the major results of these papers.
We believe that there are many more interesting questions relating to Heffter arrays and related subjects and we fully expect researchers to make good progress on these in the years to come.
We conclude this survey with a short list of open problems.

•

Complete the construction of classical Heffter arrays in the rectangular case with empty cells, for known results see Theorems
1.17
and
1.18
.

•

Construct new infinite classes of globally simple Heffter arrays, for known results see Theorems
2.5
,
2.6
and
2.7
.

•

Determine new solutions to the Crazy Knight’s Tour Problem, see Section
3
.

•

Determine new results concerning Conjectures
4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
.

•

Establish more restrictive necessary conditions for integer
{}^{\lambda}H_{t}(n;k)
, in order to improve Proposition
6.10
.
