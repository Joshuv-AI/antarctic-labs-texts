The continuum limit of k-space cavity angular momentum is controlled by an infinite range difference operator.
Per Kristen Jakobsen, Masud Mansuripur

Abstract
A wavepacket (electromagnetic or otherwise) within an isotropic and homogeneous space can be quantized on a regular lattice of discrete 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -vectors. Each 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -vector is associated with a temporal frequency ω \omega ; together, 𝐤 \mathbf{k} and ω \omega represent a propagating plane-wave. While the total energy and total linear momentum of the packet can be readily apportioned among its individual plane-wave constituents, the same cannot be said about the packet’s total angular momentum. One can show, in the case of a reasonably smooth (i.e., continuous and differentiable) wave packet, that the overall angular momentum is expressible as an integral over the k-space continuum involving only the Fourier transform of the field and its 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -space gradients. In this sense, the angular momentum is a property not of individual plane-waves, but of plane-wave pairs that are adjacent neighbors in the space inhabited by the 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -vectors, and can be said to be localized in the 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -space. Strange as it might seem, this hallmark property of angular momentum does not automatically emerge from an analysis of a discretized 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -space. In fact, the discrete analysis shows the angular momentum to be distributed among 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -vectors that pair not only with nearby 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -vectors but also with those that are far away. The goal of the present paper is to resolve the discrepancy between the discrete calculations and those performed on the continuum, by establishing the conditions under which the highly non-local sum over plane-wave pairs in the discrete 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -space would approach the localized distribution of the angular momentum across the continuum of the 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -space.

The total energy content of a wavepacket consisting of a superposition of discrete plane-waves can be shown to be the sum of the energies of the individual modes. Similarly, the linear momentum of the packet can be expressed as the sum of the linear momenta of its individual plane-wave constituents. However, these individual (discrete) plane-waves do not carry angular momentum (neither spin nor orbital); the overall angular momentum of the packet arises from interference between distinct pairs of plane-waves. A problem with the aforementioned discretization scheme is that the packet’s total angular momentum does
not
appear to converge toward the well-known continuum limit when
L\rightarrow\infty
15

In accordance with the standard method of computing the angular momentum for a smooth (i.e., continuous and differentiable) EM wavepacket
2
,
9
,
11
, those pairs of plane-waves that participate in generating the packet’s angular momentum are localized in the k-space, in the sense that a plane-wave and its immediate neighbors (in k-space) are the only pairs whose mutual interference contributes to the packet’s overall angular momentum
15
. Stated differently, the interference between two plane-waves whose k-vectors are not near-neighbors should give rise to an angular momentum density
\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{r},t)
that averages out to zero when integrated over the entire xyz-space. In the case of spin angular momentum, one can show that the E-fields of adjacent plane-waves contribute in an “additive” way, so that each plane-wave member of the continuum appears to possess its own spin angular momentum—even though, in reality, this angular momentum arises from interference between each plane-wave and its adjacent k-space neighbors
15
. In contrast, in the case of orbital angular momentum, the E-fields of adjacent plane-waves are found to contribute in a “subtractive” way, so that the packet’s orbital angular momentum cannot be apportioned among the individual plane-wave members of the continuum; rather, it is the k-space gradients of the E-field components (i.e., local derivatives of
E_{x},E_{y},E_{z}
with respect to
k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
that participate in the creation of the packet’s orbital angular momentum
15
. This localization of the angular momentum within the k-space—be it the association of spin with individual k-vectors, or the allocation of orbital angular momentum in proportion to the k-space gradient of the EM field—is something that does not emerge naturally from a discretized k-space in the limit when
L\rightarrow\infty

In the present paper, we address the discrepancy between the discrete and continuum formulations of the EM angular momentum by examining a simplified model in which a scalar wave in two-dimensional (2D) Cartesian
xy
-space exhibits a similar discrepancy.

In Sec.2, we study the solution of the scalar wave equation in a homogeneous and isotropic 2D space, derive expressions for the energy

In Secs.3 and 4, we expand the 2D field into plane-wave modes, and proceed to take a closer look at the conserved entities energy, linear momentum, and angular momentum, first in the infinite continuum of the

As compared to the electromagnetic field, the scalar field has, as expected, only orbital angular momentum. 2
Conserved quantities for a 2D scalar field

The defining equation for a 2D scalar field is the 2D linear wave equation

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}u-c^{2}\nabla^{2}u=0,

(1)

where
u=u(t,x,y)
is the scalar field and
\nabla^{2}=\partial_{xx}+\partial_{yy}
.

It is well known that the 2D wave equation is variational with Lagrangian density given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u)^{2}.

(2)

Observe that the Lagrangian density is of the general type

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(t,x,y,u,u_{t},u_{x},u_{y}).

In the context of Noether’s theorem
5
,
13
, the Lagrangian

\displaystyle L[u]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}dtdxdy\;\mathcal{L}(t,x,y,u,u_{t},u_{x},u_{y}),

(3)

corresponding to a Lagrangian density of this type, is said to be
{\it invariant}
under an infinitesimal variation of the form

\displaystyle u(t,x,y)\rightarrow u(t,x,y)+\epsilon\eta(t,x,y),

if there exists functions
F_{1},F_{2}
and
F_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u}\eta+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{t}}}\eta_{t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{x}}}\eta_{x}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{y}}}\eta_{y}=\partial_{t}F_{1}+\partial_{x}F_{2}+\partial_{y}F_{3}.

(4)

Given this, there exists a local conservation law of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}j_{1}+\partial_{x}j_{2}+\partial_{y}j_{3}=0,

where the components of the Noether
{\it current}
are given by

\displaystyle j_{1}

\displaystyle=F_{1}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{t}}},

\displaystyle j_{2}

\displaystyle=F_{2}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{x}}},

\displaystyle j_{3}

\displaystyle=F_{3}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{y}}}.

(5)

This is Noether’s theorem for Lagrangians of the type (
3
).

Consider now an infinitesimal time translation of the form

\displaystyle t\rightarrow t+\epsilon.

(6)

This infinitesimal time translation induces a variation on scalar fields of the form

\displaystyle u(t,x,y)\rightarrow u(t,x,y)+\epsilon u_{t}(t,x,y).

(7)

Thus, for this case

\displaystyle\eta(t,x,y)=u_{t}(t,x,y).

Using (
2
), we now have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u}\eta+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{t}}}\eta_{t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{x}}}\eta_{x}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{u_{y}}}\eta_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{t}u_{tt}-c^{2}u_{x}u_{tx}-c^{2}u_{y}u_{ty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{y}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{L}.

Thus, according to (
4
), the Lagrangian (
3
), corresponding to the Lagrangian density (
2
) for the 2D wave equation (
1
), is invariant under the variation (
7
). For this case we clearly have

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L},

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=F_{3}=0.

The components of the Noether current corresponding to the infinitesimal time translation (
6
) are thus

\displaystyle j_{1}=

\displaystyle F_{1}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{y}^{2})-u_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{y}^{2}),

\displaystyle j_{2}=

\displaystyle F_{2}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-u_{t})(-c^{2}u_{x})=c^{2}u_{t}u_{x},

\displaystyle j_{3}=

\displaystyle F_{3}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-u_{t})(-c^{2}u_{y})=c^{2}u_{t}u_{y}.

Defining for convenience the quantities

\displaystyle e(t,\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{y}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}(t,\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle-c^{2}u_{t}\nabla u,

the local conservation law, corresponding to time translation, can be written in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}=0.

(8)

The positive quantity
e(t,\mathbf{x})
is by definition the energy density, and
j(t,\mathbf{x})
the energy flux density for the scalar field.

For any domain
A
in the plane with boundary curve
S
and unit normal on
S
, pointing out of
A
, we get from (
8
) that

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=-\int_{S}dl\;\mathbf{j}\cdot\mathbf{n}=c^{2}\int_{S}dl\;u_{t}\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}

Here
\mathcal{E}
is the, time dependent, total energy of the scalar field inside the domain
A

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)=\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;e(t,\mathbf{x}).

(9)

Whether or not the energy of the scalar field inside
A
is conserved, depends on the domain, and the boundary conditions the scalar field satisfies at the boundary of that domain.

The energy will clearly be conserved if we assume that the domain D is so large, or the scalar field so localized, that it is negligible in a sufficiently small region around the boundary.

If we cannot assume that the scalar field is negligible in a sufficiently small region around the boundary, the energy can still be conserved if, on any section of the boundary, either
u
is constant in time, or
\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
.

In Appendix A, using the same approach, we find the conserved quantities corresponding to infinitesimal space translations and rotations. These are, by definition, total linear momentum

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\;\gradient{u},

(10)

and total angular momentum

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\;\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\nabla u.

(11)

In Appendix A we also show how the linear momentum flux and angular momentum flux for the scalar field follow directly from the expression for the Noether current. These formulas are the analogues to the linear and angular momentum fluxes
7
for the electromagnetic field.

3
The continuum limit for energy

In this section we will find the formulas for the total energy for a scalar field in the whole plane, which we will here denote by the
continuum
, and for a square cavity of side length
L
, in terms of continuous and discrete modes, respectively. We will then investigate how the formula for the total energy in the cavity approaches the one for the continuum in the continuum limit, defined by letting
L
approach infinity. Detailed calculations and derivations from this section are left out of the main text and can be found in Appendix B.

3.1
The continuum

For the continuum we will look for solutions to the equation for the scalar field

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}u-c^{2}\nabla^{2}u=0,

(12)

of the form

\displaystyle u(t,\mathbf{x})=e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t)}.

Here
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
, and the frequency
\omega
is positive. For modes to exist we must have

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=c^{2}k_{x}^{2}+c^{2}k_{y}^{2}.

Given that
\omega>0
, this equation has exactly two solutions

\displaystyle\omega=\pm\omega(k),

where

\displaystyle\omega(k)

\displaystyle=ck,

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\equiv\norm{\mathbf{k}}=(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

These are the two branches of the dispersion relation for the scalar field equation (
12
).

The two different branches of the dispersion relation give rise to two different families of modes

\displaystyle e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega(k)t)},\;\;e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\omega(k)t)}.

These modes, also known as
plane waves
, are known to be complete, and using them, we can write the general complex solution to equation (
12
) in the form

\displaystyle u(t,\mathbf{x})=\int d\mathbf{k}\;e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left\{A(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}+B(\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t}\right\},

(13)

where
A(\mathbf{k})
and
B(\mathbf{k})
are the
spectral amplitudes
defining the solution. In this formula the integral goes over the whole 2D space of
\mathbf{k}
-vectors.

We are here focused on real solutions, and the reality of
u(t,\mathbf{x})
is ensured by imposing the condition

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{k})=A(-\mathbf{k})^{*},

(14)

on the spectral amplitudes.

Let us now specialize the general space-time formula (
9
) for the total energy of the scalar field in a domain
A
, to the case of the continuum,
A=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d\mathbf{x}\;e(t,\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d\mathbf{x}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{y}^{2}\right).

(15)

From the mode expansion (
13
) of the scalar field we immediately have that

\displaystyle u_{t}=

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{k}\;(-i\omega(k))e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left\{A(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}-B(\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t}\right\},

\displaystyle\nabla u=

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{k}\;(i\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left\{A(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}+B(\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t}\right\},

(16)

and, inserting these expansions into (
15
), it is straightforward to find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}.

Note that the formula for total energy of the scalar field, expressed in terms of spectral amplitudes, is time invariant, as it should be. Also note that the total energy in the scalar field is simply a sum over the individual energies in all the plane waves that are used to construct the field. This is what our intuition leads us to expect as well.

3.2
A square cavity

For a scalar field in a square cavity

\displaystyle A=[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}]\crossproduct[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}],

the modes and dispersion relation are the same as for the continuum

\displaystyle u(t,\mathbf{x})=e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega(k)t)},\;\;e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\omega(k)t)},

where
\omega(k)=ck
.

However, not all these plane waves satisfy the required periodic boundary conditions

\displaystyle u(t,x-\frac{L}{2},y)

\displaystyle=u(t,x+\frac{L}{2},y),

\displaystyle u(t,x,y-\frac{L}{2})

\displaystyle=u(t,x,y+\frac{L}{2}).

These conditions lead to a discrete set of possible
\mathbf{k}
-vectors, and corresponding frequencies

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)(n_{x},n_{y}),

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\pi c}{L}\right)(n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(17)

Here the quantities
n_{x}
and
n_{y}
run over all integers, and we have defined
n=\norm{\mathbf{n}}=(n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

The plane wave modes for the scalar field in the square cavity are then of the form

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega_{n}t)},\;\;\;b_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\omega_{n}t)}.

Here,
a_{\mathbf{n}}
and
b_{\mathbf{n}}
are the discrete spectral amplitudes.

The general real solution to the scalar field equation for a square cavity is then of the form

\displaystyle u(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left\{a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{\mathbf{n}}t}+b_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{\mathbf{n}}t}\right\},

(18)

where we have imposed the reality conditions

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{n}}=a_{-\mathbf{n}}^{*}.

We now want to find the formula for the conserved energy in terms of the discrete modes for the scalar field.
In this context it is useful to know that the total energy of the scalar field in the cavity is conserved. We do not need to assume that the scalar field vanishes in a region close to the boundary. This occurs because the energy flux density satisfy periodic boundary conditions, if the scalar field does. The same is true for linear momentum, but it is not true for angular momentum. The culprit for angular momentum is the factor
\mathbf{x}^{\perp}
occurring in the angular momentum flux density. It destroys periodicity.
Thus in order to ensure that all three of total energy, linear momentum, and angular momentum, are conserved, we must assume that the scalar field decays so fast away from the origin that it is vanishingly small close to the boundary of the cavity. This is what we will assume from now on. The expressions for linear momentum- and angular momentum flux densities can be found in Appendix A.

The total energy of the scalar field inside the square cavity is given by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}(t)=\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}u_{y}^{2}\right).

(19)

From the expansion of the scalar field in terms of discrete cavity modes (
18
) we have

\displaystyle u_{t}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;(-i\omega_{n})e^{i\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left\{a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}-b_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{n}t}\right\},

\displaystyle\nabla u=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;(i\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})e^{i\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left\{a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}+b_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{n}t}\right\}.

(20)

Inserting these formulas into the expression for the total energy in the square cavity (
19
), we find, using derivations similar those in the continuum case, that the total energy in the cavity is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}=L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}.

(21)

As in the continuum case, we note that the total energy of the scalar field in the cavity is a sum over the energies residing in individual plane wave modes that the scalar field.

3.3
Continuum limit

For a fixed size of the cavity, the allowed
\mathbf{k}
-vectors form a uniform square grid in the 2D space of all possible vectors. This square grid is unbounded and the density of the grid is fixed by the distance between nearest neighbors. This distance will be denoted by
\triangle

\displaystyle\triangle=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\triangle k_{x}=\triangle k_{y}=\triangle.

(22)

Note that in the continuum limit, which is defined by letting
L
approach infinity, the distance
\triangle
approaches zero.

Since the spectral amplitudes,
A(\mathbf{k})
and
B(\mathbf{k})
, determining the scalar field for the continuum case (
13
), are
{\it densities}
in
\mathbf{k}
-space, we must make the identification

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;A(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}),

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;B(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}),

(23)

where

\displaystyle\triangle\mathbf{k}=\triangle k_{x}\triangle k_{y}=\triangle^{2}.

(24)

Inserting (
23
) into (
21
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}=

\displaystyle L^{2}\triangle^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\omega_{n}^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\omega_{n}^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E},

in the continuum limit,
L\rightarrow\infty
.

Thus, in the continuum limit, the discrete mode formula for total energy of the scalar field in the square cavity approaches the continuum mode formula. This is exactly what one would expect.

4
The continuum limit for linear momentum

In this section we derive the formula for the linear momentum in terms of continuum modes, and for a square cavity in terms of discrete modes, and show that the first is the continuum limit of the second. Detailed calculations and derivations in this section are left out of the main text and can be found in Appendix C.

From expressions (
16
), and the expression for the total linear momentum of the scalar field (
10
), using calculations similar to those for the energy, we find that the formula for total linear momentum of the scalar field in the continuum, expressed in terms of spectral amplitudes, is time invariant, as it should be, and takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}-\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\},

and that the total linear momentum of the scalar field in a square cavity, expressed in terms of discrete cavity modes takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{L}=L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}\left\{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}.

As was the case for the total energy, we note that the total linear momentum content of the scalar field is simply a sum over the individual momenta of all the plane waves that are used to construct the field. This is again what intuition leads us to expect.

Proceeding as in for the case of the total energy we now have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{L}

\displaystyle=L^{2}\triangle^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\omega_{n}\left\{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\omega_{n}\left\{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}-\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}.

Thus, in the continuum limit, the formula for the total momentum of the scalar field inside the cavity approaches the continuum mode formula. This is also just what we should expect.

5
The continuum limit for angular momentum

In this section we will finally investigate the continuum limit for the angular momentum in a square cavity.
We have previously made a note of the fact that energy and momentum in the scalar field reside in each individual plane wave comprising the field. This is the reason why deriving the continuum limit for the energy and linear momentum was so unproblematic. For the angular momentum, which, as we shall see, does
not
reside in the individual plane wave modes, the continuum limit is much harder to determine.
Detailed calculations and derivations for this section are left out of the main text and can be found in Appendix D.

5.1
The continuum

Using the formulas (
16
), and the expression for the total angular momentum of the scalar field (
11
), we find using calculations similar to the ones for energy and linear momentum, that
he formula for total angular momentum of the scalar field in the continuum, expressed in terms of spectral amplitudes, takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{A(\mathbf{k})^{*}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})-B(\mathbf{k})^{*}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})\right\}.

It is perhaps not immediately clear, as it was for the formulas for the total energy and linear momentum, that the formula for the total angular momentum actually produces a real quantity.

Using the properties (
14
) for the spectral amplitudes
A
and
B
, we have however,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{*}

\displaystyle=i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{A(\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})^{*}-B(\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})^{*}\right\}

\displaystyle=i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(-\mathbf{k})^{*}-B(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(-\mathbf{k})^{*}\right\}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{A(\mathbf{k})^{*}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})-B(\mathbf{k})^{*}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J},

so
\mathcal{J}
is indeed real.
Note that, contrary to the cases of energy and linear momentum, the total angular momentum of a scalar field is not simply the sum of the angular momenta of the individual modes, but rather contains a gradient in
\mathbf{k}
-space. Thus, individual modes do not have angular momentum. Angular momentum rather arise from local relations
between
individual plane wave modes.

5.2
A square cavity

In this section we will find a formula for the total angular momentum of the scalar field inside the square cavity.
Using expressions (
20
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{L}

\displaystyle=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\;\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\nabla u

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\;\omega^{\prime}S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle\left\{a^{\prime}ae^{-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}+a^{\prime}be^{-i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-b^{\prime}ae^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-b^{\prime}be^{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}\right\},

(25)

where we are using the compact notation
a=a_{\mathbf{n}},a^{\prime}=a_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
, etc, and
where the symbol
S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}
is defined by

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}=\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;(\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\mathbf{k})e^{i(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}+\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}})}.

Evaluating this integral is an elementary but somewhat tedious exercise, and we will not detail the solution in this report. The final expression is rather simple and given by

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}=iL^{2}\left(n_{x}\delta_{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}}\frac{(-1)^{\absolutevalue{n_y+n_y'}}}{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}}-n_{y}\delta_{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}}\frac{(-1)^{\absolutevalue{n_x+n_x'}}}{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}}\right).

(26)

Note that in order for this formula to be correct, we must let
\frac{(-1)^{0}}{0}
be assigned the value
0
, whenever it occurs.

Formula (
25
) for the total angular momentum consists of four terms. Let us denote the contribution to the angular momentum from these four terms by
\mathcal{J}_{i},i=1,\dots 4
.

We now insert the expression (
26
) into the first of these terms,
\mathcal{J}_{1}
, and rewrite it into the following particular form that we will need for doing the continuum limit in the next section

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\}.

(27)

Here
f_{1}
is a sequence given by

\displaystyle(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}=\omega_{n}a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}.

In the expression for
\mathcal{J}_{1}
,
\Lambda_{x}
, and
\Lambda_{y}
are operators acting on doubly infinite sequences

\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}=\alpha_{n_{x},n_{y}}
according to

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}(\alpha)_{nx,ny}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\;P_{s}\alpha_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}(\alpha)_{n_{x},n_{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\;P_{s}\alpha_{n_{x},n_{y}+s},

where the symbol
P_{q}
is defined by

\displaystyle P_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\absolutevalue{q}}}{q},\;\;\;q\neq 0,

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=0.

In a similar way we get the following formulas for the other components of the total angular momentum,
\mathcal{J}_{j},j=2,3,4

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}b_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{2})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{2})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{3}

\displaystyle=-iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{3})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{3})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{4}

\displaystyle=-iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}b_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{4})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{4})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\},

where

\displaystyle(f_{2})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathbf{n}}a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{n}t},

\displaystyle(f_{3})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathbf{n}}b_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t},

\displaystyle(f_{4})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{n}b_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\omega_{n}t}.

The total angular momentum of the scalar field in the cavity is now given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\;\mathcal{J}_{j}.

From this formula, it is clear that in parallel with the continuum case, the angular momentum in the cavity resides not in the individual plane waves, but rather in the relations between the waves. In contrast to what we observed for the continuum case, here, the angular momentum does not arise from the relations between nearby modes. The formula seems to be telling us that the total angular momentum is the sum over quantities, these are the
\Lambda
operators, that quantify relations between each individual plane wave and
all
other plane waves. For a finite sized cavity, no matter the size,
angular momentum seems to be totally delocalized in
\mathbf{k}
-space.

5.3
The continuum limit

Recall that the formula for the total angular momentum for the scalar field in a square cavity is the sum of four terms,
\mathcal{J}_{i},i=1,\dots,4
.

The formula for
\mathcal{J}_{1}
has been found to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\}.

(28)

In this section it is convenient to rewrite the operators
\Lambda_{x}
and
\Lambda_{y}
. Using the fact the
P_{-s}=-P_{s}
, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}(a)_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\;P_{s}a_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=-\infty}^{-1}\;P_{s}a_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}}+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\;P_{s}a_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\;P_{s}\left(a_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}}-a_{n_{x}-s,n_{y}}\right).

The operator
\Lambda_{y}
is rewritten in a similar way.

Now, using formulas (
22
), (
23
) and (
24
), we have

\displaystyle(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{n}a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}}

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\omega(k_{n})A(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})e^{-i\omega(k_{n})t}.

(29)

We now define a function
F_{1}
in
\mathbf{k}
-space by the formula

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mathbf{k})=\omega(k)A(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}.

(30)

Given this, (
29
) can be written in the form

\displaystyle(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;F_{1}(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle\Updownarrow

\displaystyle(f_{1})_{n_{x},n_{y}}

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;F_{1}(\triangle n_{x},\triangle n_{y}).

We now prepare the operators
\Lambda_{x}
and
\Lambda_{y}
for passing to of the continuum limit.

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{x})(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{s}}{s}\left((f_{1})_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}}-(f_{1})_{n_{x}-s,n_{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{s}}{s}\left(F_{1}(\triangle(n_{x}+s),\triangle n_{y})-F_{1}(\triangle(n_{x}-s),\triangle n_{y})\right)

\displaystyle=\triangle\mathbf{k}\;\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{s}}{s}\left(F_{1}(\triangle n_{x}+\triangle s,\triangle n_{y})-F_{1}(\triangle n_{x}-\triangle s,\triangle n_{y})\right)

\displaystyle=-\triangle^{3}\;\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}),

(31)

where we have defined a new operator
\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}
, acting on functions
G(\mathbf{k})
defined on
\mathbf{k}
-space according to

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(G)(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{G(\mathbf{k}+(\triangle s,0))-G(\mathbf{k}-(\triangle s,0))}{\triangle s}.

Treating the operator
\Lambda_{y}
in the same way we find that

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{y})(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=-\triangle^{3}\;\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}),

(32)

where the operator
\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}(G)(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{G(\mathbf{k}+(0,\triangle s))-G(\mathbf{k}-(0,\triangle s))}{\triangle s}.

Inserting (
31
) and (
32
) into (
28
), and again using (
23
) and (
24
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}=iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\triangle\mathbf{k}\;A(-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})e^{-i\omega(k_{n})t}\left\{\triangle n_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})-\triangle n_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\right\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}A(-\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}\left\{k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k})\right\}.

(33)

The two operators
\mathbb{D}_{x}
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}
are the continuum limits of
\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}
in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{x}(G)(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(G)(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{G(\mathbf{k}+(\triangle s,0))-G(\mathbf{k}-(\triangle s,0))}{\triangle s},

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{y}(G)(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}(G)(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{G(\mathbf{k}+(0,\triangle s))-G(\mathbf{k}-(0,\triangle s))}{\triangle s}.

It is clear from these formulas that the operators
\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(G)
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}(G)
are highly non-local. For example, computing the value of
\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(G)(\mathbf{k})
, at some chosen point
\mathbf{k}
, will involve wave numbers arbitrarily distant from
\mathbf{k}
, no matter the value of
L
. It is not evident at all what the continuum limit of such highly non-local entities should be.

Nevertheless, in order to find the continuum limit for the total angular momentum in a square cavity, the precise nature of the two operators
\mathbb{D}_{x}(G)
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}(G)
must be understood.

5.3.1
A simplifying assumption; the factor-of-two problem

We start by observing that if we define the following operator,
\mathbb{D}
, acting on functions,
f(x)
, on the real line

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(x+\triangle s)-f(x-\triangle s)}{\triangle s},

then both
\mathbb{D}_{x}
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}
will be fully understood, if
\mathbb{D}
is. Recall here that
\triangle=\frac{2\pi}{L}
.

We evidently can rewrite the previous formula into the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(x+\triangle)-f(x-\triangle)}{\triangle}+\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}R(f)(x,L),

(34)

where we have defined

\displaystyle R(f)(x,L)=\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(x+\triangle s)-f(x-\triangle s)}{\triangle s}.

Observe that
{\it if}
we postulate that the last term in (
34
) vanishes

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}R(f)(x,L)=0,

we get simply

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=2f^{\prime}(x).

Returning to the discussion in the previous section we conclude that, assuming the postulate, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{x}(G)(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\partial_{k_{x}}G(\mathbf{k}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{y}(G)(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\partial_{k_{y}}G(\mathbf{k}).

Given this we have

\displaystyle\left\{k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{1})(\mathbf{k})\right\}

\displaystyle=2\left\{k_{x}\partial_{k_{y}}F_{1}(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\partial_{k_{x}}F_{1}(\mathbf{k})\right\}

\displaystyle=2(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})F_{1}(\mathbf{k}).

Note that here, as elsewhere in this paper, we identify vectors pointing along the z-axis with their z-component.

Using the definition of the function
F_{1}
from (
30
) we have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})F_{1}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})(\omega(k)A(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t})

\displaystyle=\omega(k)\;(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})\;e^{-i\omega(k)t}.

This formula is true because

\displaystyle(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})\omega(k)=\frac{\omega^{\prime}(k)}{\norm{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\mathbf{k})=0.

Thus, from our postulate, the continuum limit of
\mathcal{J}_{1}
derived in the previous section (
32
), turns into

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}\rightarrow-2i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})^{-2i\omega t}.

In Appendix D, on page
The continuum
, we prove that an expression like this vanishes.
In a similar way we find that
\mathcal{J}_{4}
vanishes in the continuum limit.

For
\mathcal{J}_{2}
, using the same approach as for
\mathcal{J}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}=iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}b_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{2})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{2})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\triangle\mathbf{k}\;B(-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})e^{-i\omega(k_{n})t}\left\{\triangle n_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})-\triangle n_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}})\right\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}B(-\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}\left\{k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k})\right\},

where now

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathbf{k})=\omega(k)A(\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t}.

Thus we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow-2i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega B(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k}).

Following the exact same approach we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{3}

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k}),

from which we conclude that the continuum limit for the total angular momentum
\mathcal{J}_{L}
in the cavity is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{L}

\displaystyle\rightarrow-2i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{B(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})-A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})\right\}

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{J}.

We almost get the correct continuum limit using our postulate; it is however too large by a factor of 2.

5.3.2
A conjecture and a resolution of factor-of-two problem

Defining
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}
to be the exact continuum limit of the sum of
\mathcal{J}_{2}
and
\mathcal{J}_{3}
, found using the exact same approach as on page
5.3
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\big\{B(-\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}\left(k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle-A(-\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t}\left(k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{3})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{3})(\mathbf{k})\right)\big\},

where

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\omega(k)A(\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t},

\displaystyle F_{3}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\omega(k)B(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t}.

Recall that the total angular momentum for a scalar field in the continuum is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\big\{B(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle-A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})\big\}.

(35)

Since the operators
\mathbb{D}_{x}
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}
at this point are unresolved formal limits of the extremely non-local operators
\mathbb{D}_{x}^{L}
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}^{L}
, it is not at all evident that expression
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}
contains all the time independent terms, or that its time dependent terms vanishes. At this point we know so little about the nature of the operators
\mathbb{D}_{x}
and
\mathbb{D}_{y}
that we just can’t decide one way or the other.

There is however a strong formal similarity between the formulas for
\mathcal{J}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}
.
In fact, if we simply assume the correspondence

\displaystyle\left(k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{2})(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\omega(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})e^{i\omega(k)t},

\displaystyle\left(k_{x}\mathbb{D}_{y}(F_{3})(\mathbf{k})-k_{y}\mathbb{D}_{x}(F_{3})(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\omega(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})e^{-i\omega(k)t},

then
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{J}
, and the continuum limit of the total angular momentum of a scalar field in a square cavity will be exactly equal to the correct expression, with no factor of two appearing.

From what we did in the previous section, it is also clear that if the correspondence holds, then the terms
\mathcal{J}_{1}
and
\mathcal{J}_{4}
vanish in the continuum limit.

Furthermore, it follows from our work in the previous subsection that the correspondence will be correct if the following conjecture holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=f^{\prime}(x),

(36)

where we recall from that section that

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(x+\triangle s)-f(x-\triangle s)}{\triangle s}.

6
Proving the conjecture

Recall that the conjecture, as stated, is

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=f^{\prime}(x).

Note that a sufficient condition for the correctness of the spectral continuum formula (
35
), is that the spectral functions
A(\mathbf{k})
and
B(\mathbf{k})
are defined for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and are continuously differentiable.

The spectral functions are complex valued, but it is evident that we only need to prove the conjecture for real valued continuously differentiable function
f(x)
, defined on the whole real line.

Let us start by rewriting the
\mathbb{D}
operator in the following way

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\;\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(x_{0}+\triangle s)-f(x_{0}-\triangle s)}{\triangle s}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\sum_{s\neq 0}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(x_{0}+\triangle s)}{\triangle s}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\left\{\left(\sum_{s\neq 0}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{\delta(x-x_{0}-\triangle s)}{\triangle s}\right)(f)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\left\{\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})(f)\right\},

(37)

where we have used the fact that
\triangle=\frac{2\pi}{L}
, and where we have defined a generalized function

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})=\sum_{s\neq 0}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{\delta(x-x_{0}-\triangle s)}{\triangle s}.

(38)

Next, observe that if
{\comb}
is the generalized function known as the
Dirac comb
, we have

\displaystyle\left(-\left(\frac{1}{\triangle}\sinc{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}\right)^{\prime}\comb{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\triangle^{2}}\sinc^{\prime}{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}\comb{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\triangle^{2}}\left(\frac{\cos\pi\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}{\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}}-\frac{\sin\pi\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}{\pi\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)^{2}}\right)\comb{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\triangle^{2}}\sum_{s\neq 0}\left(\frac{\cos s\pi}{s}-\frac{\sin s\pi}{\pi s^{2}}\right)\delta\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}-s\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\neq 0}(-1)^{s-1}\frac{\delta\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}-s\right)}{s\triangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\neq 0}(-1)^{s-1}\frac{\delta\left(x-x_{0}-\triangle s\right)}{s\triangle},

(39)

where we have made use of the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{\cos x\pi}{x}-\frac{\sin x\pi}{\pi x^{2}}\rightarrow 0,\;\text{when}\;x\rightarrow 0.

Using (
38
) and (
39
) we see that the generalized function
\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})=-\left(\frac{1}{\triangle}\sinc{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}\right)^{\prime}\comb{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)}.

(40)

We are now going to compute the Fourier transform of the generalized function (
40
). For that purpose we note that in this paper we are using the following convention for the Fourier transform

\displaystyle F(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\;f(x)\;e^{ikx},

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)\;e^{-ikx}.

(41)

Using this convention, we have for any function, or generalized function, f,

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)\;

\displaystyle\rightarrow\;\triangle e^{ikx_{0}}\;F(\triangle k),

\displaystyle f^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\triangle}\right)\;

\displaystyle\rightarrow\;-ik\triangle^{2}e^{ikx_{0}}\;F(\triangle k).

Note that with our convention (
41
), the Fourier transform of the Dirac
\comb
is not equal to itself. We rather have

\displaystyle\comb(x)\rightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\comb(k).

From these simple facts we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})

\displaystyle\rightarrow-\left(\frac{1}{\triangle^{2}}\right)\left(-ik\triangle^{2}e^{ikx_{0}}\;\rect(\triangle k)\right)*\left(\frac{\triangle}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\;\comb(\triangle k)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(ike^{ikx_{0}}\rect(\triangle k)\right)*\left(\frac{\triangle}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\comb(\triangle k)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}(k-\frac{s}{\triangle})\rect(\triangle k-s),

(42)

where we have used the fact that the Fourier transform of the
\sinc
function is a rectangle

\displaystyle\rect(k)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1,\;\;\;\absolutevalue{k}\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
0.\;\;\;\absolutevalue{k}>\frac{1}{2}\\
\end{aligned}\right.,

and where
*
is the convolution operator.

The Fourier transform of
\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})
takes for
x_{0}=0
the form of a periodic piecewise linear function of slope equal one, and period
\frac{1}{\triangle}
. A classical
saw tooth
function.

Figure 1:
The real part of the Fourier transform of
\mathbf{\mathcal{D}}(x-x_{0})
for
x_{0}=0
and
\triangle=1
.

Using our convention for the Fourier transform, it is a simple matter to verify that the Fourier transform of the generalized function
-\delta^{\prime}(x-x_{0})
is given by

\displaystyle-\delta^{\prime}(x-x_{0})\rightarrow\frac{ik}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}.

(43)

Now, from (
37
) we have for the Fourier transform of
\mathbb{D}(f)(x_{0})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x_{0})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\left\{\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}(k-\frac{s}{\triangle})\rect(\triangle k-s)\right)(F)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}(k-\frac{s}{\triangle})\rect(\triangle k-s)\right\}

(44)

Figure 2:
The real part of the integrand from formula (

) for the case when
\triangle=1
,
x_{0}=0
and the Fourier transform of f(x) is a Gaussian centered at
k=0

Observe that when we take the limit when
\triangle\rightarrow 0
, the first period of the periodic piecewise linear function (
42
), which is displayed in figure
1
, will grow without bound.

From this observation, together with identity (
43
) and figure
2
, we can now immediately conclude that

Proposition 1
.

The conjecture is true for any function f(x) whose Fourier transform has compact support.

Thus, compact support for the Fourier transform of a function
f(x)
is a sufficient condition for the truth of the statement

\displaystyle D(f)(x)=f^{\prime}(x).

This sufficient condition is however too strong for our purpse. It is well known that functions whose Fourier transform has compact support are in fact analytic, and we need to prove the conjecture for the much larger class of continuously differentiable functions. The Fourier transform of such functions does not in general have compact support.

We will next show that the conjecture is also true for functions whose Fourier transform does not have compact support, but rather decay sufficiently fast for large
k
.
In order to show this we rewrite (
44
) in the following way

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x_{0})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}(k-\frac{s}{\triangle})\rect(\triangle k-s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)\frac{ik}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\rect(\triangle k-s)

\displaystyle-\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(\triangle k-s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)\frac{ik}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ikx_{0}}

\displaystyle-\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)e^{ikx_{0}}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(\triangle k-s).

(45)

The first term in (
45
) is, according to (
43
), the Fourier transform of the generalized function
-\delta^{\prime}(x-x_{0})
, applied to the Fourier transform,
F(k)
, of our function
f(x)
. Thus, if the second term in (
45
) is zero, we can conclude that

\displaystyle D(f)(x_{0})=-\delta^{\prime}(x-x_{0})(f).

(46)

Thus, the conjecture is true for functions for which the second term in (
45
) is exactly zero.

Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)e^{ikx_{0}}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(\triangle k-s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)e^{ikx_{0}}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(\triangle k+s))

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(-k)e^{-ikx_{0}}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(-\triangle k+s))

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)^{*}e^{-ikx_{0}}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(\triangle k-s))

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\;F(k)e^{ikx_{0}}\frac{s}{\triangle}\rect(\triangle k-s))\right\}^{*}.

(47)

From (
47
), (
45
), the definition of convergence for a doubly infinite series, and the discussion above leading up to (
46
), we can now conclude that

Lemma 1
.

Let
f(x)
be a function whose Fourier transform,
F(k)
, is a function, and let

\displaystyle S(x_{0},\triangle)=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{s}{\triangle}\int_{\frac{s-\frac{1}{2}}{\triangle}}^{\frac{s+\frac{1}{2}}{\triangle}}dk\;F(k)e^{ikx_{0}}.

Then

1.

The conjecture is true for
f(x)
if

\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;S(x_{0},\triangle)=0
.

2.

The conjecture is false for
f(x)
if
\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;S(x_{0},\triangle)
does not exist, or if the limit exists but is nonzero.

This lemma tells us exactly what we need to do to verify that the conjecture is true for any particular
f(x)
.

Let
f(x)
be continuously differentiable. Then it’s Fourier transform,
F(k)
, satisfy the bound

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{F(k)}\leq\alpha\;k^{-\gamma},\;\;\;\text{for}\;k>c,

for some numbers
\alpha>0,c>0
, and
\gamma>2
.
Given this, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{S(x_0,\triangle)}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{s}{\triangle}\int_{\frac{s-\frac{1}{2}}{\triangle}}^{\frac{s+\frac{1}{2}}{\triangle}}dk\;k^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\gamma-1}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{s}{\triangle}\left(\left(\frac{s-\frac{1}{2}}{\triangle}\right)^{1-\gamma}-\left(\frac{s+\frac{1}{2}}{\triangle}\right)^{1-\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\triangle^{\gamma-2}}{\gamma-1}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}s\left(\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1-\gamma}-\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1-\gamma}\right),

(48)

and since

\displaystyle s\left(\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1-\gamma}-\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1-\gamma}\right)\sim(\gamma-1)\;s^{-(\gamma-1)},\;\;\;\text{when}\;\;s\rightarrow\infty,

the infinite series in (
48
) converges. Given this, and the assumption that
\gamma>2
, it is now evident that

\displaystyle\lim_{\triangle\rightarrow 0}\;S(x_{0},\triangle)=0.

Thus we have

Theorem 1
.

Let
f(x)
be continuously differentiable. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=f^{\prime}(x),\;\;\;\text{for all}\;x\in\mathbb{R}

7
Discussion

For the physical problem described in the introduction, which was the motivation for this paper, Theorem 1 represents all we need to know about the
\mathbb{D}
-operator.

However, beyond this context, exploring further properties of the
\mathbb{D}
-operator, and how it relates to differentiation, is certainly of mathematical interest. We have not explored this topic deeply in the current paper, but based on extensive numerical calculations, some of which are displayed in Appendix E, and from comments from scientists not directly involved in this work, we will suggest the following two conjectures on the extension of Theorem 1

Conjecture 1
.

If
f(x)
is continuous for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=f^{\prime}(x),

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
where
f(x)
is differentiable.

Conjecture 2
.

If
f(x)
is discontinuous at one or more points in
\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(f)(x)\neq f^{\prime}(x),

for all but a discrete set of points.

The discrete set of points referred to in this second conjecture are typically points that have a symmetric position with respect to the points of discontinuity for the function
f(x)
. An example of this is the middle point of the hat function discussed on Appendix E.

Appendix A

In this appendix we detail the derivation of the conserved quantities of linear and angular momentum for a 2D scalar field, which was left out of the main text.

Linear Momentum

Consider an infinitesimal space translation along a direction
\mathbf{a}=(a,b)
, with
a^{2}+b^{2}=1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\rightarrow\mathbf{x}+\epsilon\;\mathbf{a}.

This infinitesimal space translation induces a corresponding variation on scalar fields of the form

\displaystyle u(t,x,y)\rightarrow u(t,x,y)+\epsilon(a\;u_{x}+b\;u_{y}).

Thus, for this case

\displaystyle\eta(t,x,y)=a\;u_{x}+b\;u_{y},

and for this variation we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u}\;\eta+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}\ \eta_{t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}\;\eta_{x}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{y}}\;\eta_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}\;(a\;u_{xt}+b\;u_{yt})+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}\;(a\;u_{xx}+b\;u_{xy})+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{y}}\;(a\;u_{xy}+b\;u_{yy})

\displaystyle=a\;(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}\;u_{xt}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}\;u_{xx}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{y}}\;u_{xy})

\displaystyle+b\;(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}\;u_{yt}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}\;u_{xy}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{y}}\;u_{yy})

\displaystyle=a\;\partial_{x}\mathcal{L}+b\;\partial_{y}\mathcal{L},

so the functional is invariant with
F_{1}=0,\;F_{2}=a\;\mathcal{L},\;F_{3}=b\;\mathcal{L}
.

The components of the Noether current are from (
5
)

\displaystyle j_{1}

\displaystyle=-(a\;u_{x}+b\;u_{y})\;u_{t}=-u_{t}\;(a\;u_{x}+b\;u_{y}),

\displaystyle j_{2}

\displaystyle=\;a(\frac{1}{2}\;u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;c^{2}\;u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;c^{2}\;u_{y}^{2})-(a\;u_{x}+b\;u_{y})\;(-c^{2}\;u_{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\;a\;u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;a\;c^{2}\;u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;a\;c^{2}\;u_{y}^{2}+a\;c^{2}\;u_{x}^{2}+b\;c^{2}\;u_{x}\;u_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\;a\;u_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\;a\;c^{2}\;u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;a\;c^{2}\;u_{y}^{2}+b\;c^{2}\;u_{x}\;u_{y},

\displaystyle j_{3}

\displaystyle=b\;(\frac{1}{2}\;u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;c^{2}\;u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;c^{2}\;u_{y}^{2})-(a\;u_{x}+b\;u_{y})\;(-c^{2}\;u_{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\;b\;u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\;b\;c^{2}\;u_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\;b\;c^{2}\;u_{y}^{2}+a\;c^{2}\;u_{x}\;u_{y},

and the conservation law is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}j_{1}+\partial_{x}j_{2}+\partial_{y}j_{3}=0.

In order to simplify the conservation law we introduce a vector
\mathbf{p}
and a Cartesian tensor
f_{\mathbf{p}}
of rank 2, defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-u_{t}\;\gradient{u},

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{p}}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=c^{2}\;\gradient{u}\;\gradient{u}-\frac{1}{2}\;c^{2}\;\Tr(\grad{u} \; \grad{u})\;I+\frac{1}{2}\;u_{t}^{2}\;I,

where
I
is the identity matrix.

Using these quantities, we can write the local conservation law in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\mathbf{a}\dotproduct\mathbf{p})+\divergence{(\vb{a} \vdot f_{\mathbf{p}})}=0.

(49)

The argument leading up to (
49
) is true for all vectors
\mathbf{a}
. We therefore have the conservation law

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{p}+\divergence{f_{\mathbf{p}}}=0.

(50)

By definition,
\mathbf{p}
is the linear momentum density and
f_{\mathbf{p}}
is the linear momentum flux density.

The total linear momentum inside some domain
A
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;\mathbf{p}=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\;\gradient{u},

and from (
50
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{P}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;\partial_{t}\mathbf{p}=-\int_{S}dl\;f_{\mathbf{P}}\dotproduct\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{S}dl\;\left\{(\frac{1}{2}u_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}(\nabla u)^{2})\mathbf{n}+c^{2}\nabla u\;\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\right\}.

(51)

As was the case for the energy, the linear momentum will clearly be conserved if we assume that the domain D is so large, or the scalar field so localized, that it is negligible in a sufficiently small region around the boundary. If the field is not negligible near the boundary, neither vanishing u, nor the flux of u, at the boundary, will ensure that the total momentum of the scalar field inside A is conserved.

Angular Momentum

Consider an infinitesimal rotation of the form

\displaystyle(x,y)\rightarrow(x,y)+\epsilon(-y,x).

This infinitesimal rotation induces a variation on scalar fields of the form

\displaystyle u(t,x,y)\rightarrow u(t,x,y)+\epsilon(xu_{y}-yu_{x}).

(52)

Thus, for this case

\displaystyle\eta(t,x,y)=xu_{y}-yu_{x},

and for this variation we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u}\;\eta+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}\ \eta_{t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}\;\eta_{x}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{y}}\;\eta_{y}

\displaystyle=u_{t}(xu_{y}-yu_{x})_{t}-c^{2}u_{x}(xu_{y}-yu_{x})_{x}-c^{2}(xu_{y}-yu_{x})_{y}

\displaystyle=u_{t}(xu_{yt}-yu_{xt})-c^{2}u_{x}(u_{y}+xu_{xy}-yu_{xx})-c^{2}u_{y}(xu_{yy}-u_{x}-yu_{xy})

\displaystyle=(xu_{t}u_{yt}-yu_{t}u_{xt})-c^{2}(u_{x}u_{y}+xu_{x}u_{xy}-yu_{x}u_{xx})

\displaystyle-c^{2}(xu_{y}u_{yy}-u_{y}u_{x}-yu_{y}u_{xy})

\displaystyle=xu_{t}u_{yt}-yu_{t}u_{xt}-c^{2}u_{x}u_{y}-c^{2}xu_{x}u_{xy}+c^{2}yu_{x}u_{xx}

\displaystyle-c^{2}xu_{y}u_{yy}+c^{2}u_{y}u_{x}+c^{2}yu_{y}u_{xy}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(xu_{t}^{2})_{y}-\frac{1}{2}(yu_{t}^{2})_{x}-c^{2}(\frac{1}{2}x(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2}))_{y}

\displaystyle+c^{2}(\frac{1}{2}y(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2}))_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{-yu_{t}^{2}+c^{2}y(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2})\right\}_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{xu_{t}^{2}-c^{2}x(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2})\right\}_{y}.

So the functional is invariant with

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(-yu_{t}^{2}+c^{2}y(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2})\right),

\displaystyle F_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(xu_{t}^{2}-c^{2}x(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2})\right).

(53)

Using (
52
) and (
53
) in the formula for the components of the Noether current (
5
), we find that

\displaystyle j_{1}

\displaystyle=F_{1}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial_{u_{t}}}

\displaystyle=-u_{t}(xu_{y}-yu_{x}),

\displaystyle j_{2}

\displaystyle=F_{2}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial_{u_{x}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}yu_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}yu_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}yu_{y}^{2}+c^{2}xu_{x}u_{y},

\displaystyle j_{3}

\displaystyle=F_{3}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial_{u_{y}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}xu_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}xu_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}xu_{y}^{2}-c^{2}yu_{x}u_{y},

and the local conservation law is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}j_{1}+\partial_{x}j_{2}+\partial_{y}j_{3}=0.

As for energy and linear momentum, in order to simplify the local conservation law, we introduce a scalar quantity
j
and a vector quantity
f_{j}

\displaystyle j(t,\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle-u_{t}\;\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\nabla u,

\displaystyle f_{j}(t,\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\perp}\cdot f_{\mathbf{p}}.

Note that the cross product of all plane vectors points in the
z
direction; the scalar
m
is here understood to be the
z
component of the cross product
\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\nabla u
. We have also here introduced the notation
\mathbf{x}^{\perp}=(-y,x)
.

Observe that the relation between linear momentum density and angular momentum density

\displaystyle j=\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\mathbf{p},

is the same as for the electromagnetic field, and is the one our intuition would lead us to expect.
Using these quantities, we can write the local conservation law for angular momentum in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}j+\nabla\cdot f_{j}=0.

By definition,
j
is the angular momentum density and
f_{j}
is the angular momentum flux density.

The total angular momentum inside some domain
A
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;j=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\;\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\nabla u.

Like for the energy and the linear momentum, the angular momentum inside the domain
A
is conserved if we assume that the domain D is so large, or the scalar field so localized, that it is negligible in a sufficiently small region around the boundary. If the scalar field is not negligible in a small region around the boundary, the total angular momentum of the scalar field inside
A
is in general not conserved. An exception to this general rule occur when
A
takes the form of a circular disk. For this case the angular momentum is conserved if, on any section of the boundary, either
u=
const or
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=0
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;c^{2}k^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;c^{2}k^{2}A(\mathbf{k})B(\mathbf{k})^{*}e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;c^{2}k^{2}B(\mathbf{k})A(\mathbf{k})^{*}e^{2i\omega t}.

Finally inserting (
57
) and (
58
) into the expression for the energy (
54
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;(\omega^{2}+c^{2}k^{2})\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;(-\omega^{2}+c^{2}k^{2})A(\mathbf{k})B(\mathbf{k})^{*}e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;(-\omega^{2}+c^{2}k^{2})B(\mathbf{k})A(\mathbf{k})^{*}e^{2i\omega t}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}+\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\},

where in the last step we used the dispersion relation.

A square cavity

We proceed like for the continuum case by splitting the space-time formula for the energy into the sum of two pieces

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}=\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}c^{2}\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;(\nabla u)^{2}.

(59)

These integrals now go over the cavity only, not the whole physical space, like for the continuum case.

Inserting the formulas (
20
) into the formula for
\mathcal{E}_{1}
from (
59
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\;\omega\omega^{\prime}e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\left\{ae^{-i\omega t}-be^{i\omega t}\right\}\left\{a^{\prime}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}-b^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\;\omega\omega^{\prime}S_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\left\{aa^{\prime}e^{-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}-ab^{\prime}e^{-i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-ba^{\prime}e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}+bb^{\prime}e^{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}\right\},

(60)

where we are using the compact notation
a=a_{\mathbf{n}},a^{\prime}=a_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
, etc.

The quantity
S_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}
is by definition

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;e^{i(\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}+\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=L^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}^{\prime}},

(61)

where
\delta_{\mathbf{p}}
is the Kronecker delta.

Inserting (
61
) into the expression for the kinetic energy (
60
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}^{2}a_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-2i\omega_{n}}

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}^{2}b_{\mathbf{n}}b_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{2i\omega_{n}}.

(62)

An entirely similar calculation leads to the following formula for
\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;c^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;c^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}a_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-2i\omega_{n}t}

\displaystyle+\frac{L^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;c^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}b_{\mathbf{n}}b_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{2i\omega_{n}t}.

(63)

Adding together the expressions for
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, (
62
) and (
63
), and using the dispersion relation (
17
), we get the following simple expression of the total energy, of the scalar field in a square cavity, expressed in terms of discrete cavity modes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}=L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}^{2}\left\{\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}.

Appendix C

In this appendix we detail calculations relating to the continuum limit of linear momentum, which are left out of the main text.

The continuum

Using expressions (
16
) and the expression for the total linear momentum of the scalar field (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=-\int d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\nabla U

\displaystyle=-\int d\mathbf{x}\int d\mathbf{k}d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\omega\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\;e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\left\{Ae^{-i\omega t}-Be^{i\omega t}\right\}\left\{A^{\prime}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}+B^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}\right\}

\displaystyle=-(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\omega\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\;\delta(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\left\{AA^{\prime}e^{-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}+AB^{\prime}e^{-i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-BA^{\prime}e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-BB^{\prime}e^{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}\right\}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}-\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\mathbf{k}A(\mathbf{k})A(-\mathbf{k})e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\mathbf{k}B(\mathbf{k})B(-\mathbf{k})e^{2i\omega t}.

(64)

Observe that the integrand in each of last two time dependent terms of (
64
) is an odd function of
\mathbf{k}
, when restricted to circles
\omega(k)=
const. From this it follows that both those terms vanish.

Thus, the formula for total linear momentum of the scalar field, expressed in terms of spectral amplitudes, is time invariant, as it should be, and takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{A(\mathbf{k})}^{2}-\mathbf{k}\absolutevalue{B(\mathbf{k})}^{2}\right\}.

As for the total energy, we note that the total linear momentum in the scalar field is simply a sum over the individual momenta in all the plane waves that are used to construct the field. This is again what intuition leads us to expect.

A square cavity

Using the expressions (
20
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{L}

\displaystyle=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\nabla u

\displaystyle=-\int_{A}d\mathbf{x}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\;\omega\mathbf{k}^{\prime}e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\left\{ae^{-i\omega t}-be^{i\omega t}\right\}\left\{a^{\prime}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}+b^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\;\omega\mathbf{k}^{\prime}L^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\left\{aa^{\prime}e^{-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}+ab^{\prime}e^{-i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-ba^{\prime}e^{i(\omega-\omega^{\prime})t}-bb^{\prime}e^{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}\right\}

\displaystyle=L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}\left\{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}a_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-2i\omega_{n}t}

\displaystyle-L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}b_{\mathbf{n}}b_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{2i\omega_{n}t}.

(65)

Note that when
\mathbf{n}\rightarrow-\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\omega_{n}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}a_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-2i\omega_{n}t}\rightarrow\omega_{n}\mathbf{k}_{-\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-2i\omega_{n}t}=-\omega_{n}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}a_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-2i\omega_{n}t}.

(66)

Because of this, the second, time dependent term in (
65
), vanishes. For a similar reason, the third, time dependent term in (
65
), also vanishes.

Thus, we get the following simple expression of the total linear momentum, of the scalar field in a square cavity, expressed in terms of discrete cavity modes

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{L}=L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\;\omega_{n}\left\{\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{a_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{n}}\absolutevalue{b_{\mathbf{n}}}^{2}\right\}.

Appendix D

In this appendix we detail calculations relating to the continuum limit of angular momentum, which are left out of the main text.

The continuum

Using expressions (
16
) and the expression for the total angular momentum of the scalar field (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-\int d\mathbf{x}\;u_{t}\;\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\nabla u

(67)

\displaystyle=-\int d\mathbf{x}\int d\mathbf{k}d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}\;(\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\mathbf{k})\;e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\left\{A^{\prime}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}-B^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}\right\}\left\{Ae^{-i\omega t}+Be^{i\omega t}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\int d\mathbf{k}d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}\;S(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\left\{A^{\prime}Ae^{-i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)t}+A^{\prime}Be^{-i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}-B^{\prime}Ae^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}-B^{\prime}Be^{i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)t}\right\},

(68)

where

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{x}\;(\mathbf{x}\crossproduct\mathbf{k})e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{x}\;(xk_{y}-yk_{x})e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=-i\int d\mathbf{x}\;(k_{y}\partial_{k_{x}}-k_{x}\partial_{k_{y}})e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=i\;(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})\int d\mathbf{x}\;e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=i(2\pi)^{2}\;(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})\delta(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime}).

(69)

Inserting (
69
) into (
68
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})\delta(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\;

\displaystyle\left\{A^{\prime}Ae^{-i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)t}+A^{\prime}Be^{-i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}-B^{\prime}Ae^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}-B^{\prime}Be^{i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)t}\right\}

\displaystyle=i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}\delta(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})\;

\displaystyle\left\{A^{\prime}Ae^{-i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)t}+A^{\prime}Be^{-i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}-B^{\prime}Ae^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}-B^{\prime}Be^{i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)t}\right\},

where we have used the rules for distributional derivatives, and the fact that the differential operator commutes with functions of
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\omega(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})=(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})\omega(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}).

The formula for
\mathcal{J}
now simplifies into

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{B(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})-A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})\right\}

\displaystyle+i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\;A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\;B(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})e^{2i\omega t}.

(70)

The integrand of the last two terms are time dependent, and since the total angular momentum of the scalar field is conserved, they must both cancel. That they do so is not immediately obvious, but observe that for the first of the two time dependent terms in (
70
), we have

\displaystyle i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\;A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\;\left\{A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})+A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})\right\}e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\;\left\{A(-\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})+A(\mathbf{k})(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(-\mathbf{k})\right\}e^{-2i\omega t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\;(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})(A(\mathbf{k})A(-\mathbf{k}))e^{-2i\omega t}.

Note that on each circle
C
, defined by
\omega(k)=
const, we end up having to solve an integral of the form

\displaystyle\int_{C}dl\;\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\phi,

where
\phi
is a scalar function.

We conclude, from a version of Stokes theorem
1
,
13
, that this integral is zero. One can also show this fact explicitly by doing the integral using polar coordinates. Thus, the last two terms in (
70
) both cancel.

The formula for total angular momentum of the scalar field, expressed in terms of spectral amplitudes, becomes time invariant, as it should be, and takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=-i(2\pi)^{2}\int d\mathbf{k}\;\omega\left\{A(\mathbf{k})^{*}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})A(\mathbf{k})-B(\mathbf{k})^{*}(\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\nabla_{\mathbf{k}})B(\mathbf{k})\right\}.

A square cavity

Observe that

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}=iL^{2}\left(n_{x}\delta_{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}}\frac{(-1)^{\absolutevalue{n_y+n_y'}}}{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}}-n_{y}\delta_{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}}\frac{(-1)^{\absolutevalue{n_x+n_x'}}}{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}}\right),

can be nonzero only when

\displaystyle n_{x}^{\prime}=-n_{x},\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;n_{y}^{\prime}\neq-n_{y},

or if

\displaystyle n_{y}^{\prime}=-n_{y},\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;n_{x}^{\prime}\neq-n_{x}.

For these two cases,
S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}
takes the form

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}n_{x}P_{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}},\;\;\;\mathbf{n}=(n_{x},n_{y}),\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=(-n_{x},n_{y}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=-iL^{2}n_{y}P_{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}},\;\;\;\mathbf{n}=(n_{x},n_{y}),\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=(n_{x}^{\prime},-n_{y}),

where

\displaystyle P_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\absolutevalue{q}}}{q},\;\;\;q\neq 0,

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=0.

For the first contribution to the total angular momentum in the cavity,
\mathcal{J}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=-iL^{2}\sum_{n_{x},n_{y},n_{y}^{\prime}}\;n_{x}P_{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}}\omega_{-n_{x},n_{y}^{\prime}}a_{-n_{x},n_{y}^{\prime}}a_{n_{x},n_{y}}e^{-i(\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}}+\omega_{-nx,ny^{\prime}})t}

\displaystyle+iL^{2}\sum_{n_{x},n_{x}^{\prime},n_{y}}\;n_{y}P_{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}}\omega_{n_{x}^{\prime},-n_{y}}a_{n_{x}^{\prime},-n_{y}}a_{n_{x},n_{y}}e^{-i(\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}}+\omega_{n_{x}^{\prime},-n_{y}})t}.

Note that in this formula, and in the ones to follow, we have introduced the notation

\displaystyle\omega_{p,q}=\frac{2\pi c}{L}(p^{2}+q^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We evidently have that

\displaystyle\omega_{-p,q}=\omega_{p,-q}=\omega_{p,q}.

We will rewrite formula
\mathcal{J}_{1}
into a form that will be more convenient when we consider the continuum limit,
L\rightarrow\infty
, in section
5.3
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}=-iL^{2}\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}a_{n_{x},n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}}}\{n_{x}\sum_{n_{y}^{\prime}}P_{n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}}\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}^{\prime}}a_{-n_{x},n_{y}^{\prime}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}^{\prime}}t}

\displaystyle-n_{y}\sum_{n_{x}^{\prime}}P_{n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}}\omega_{n_{x}^{\prime},n_{y}}a_{n_{x}^{\prime},-n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x}^{\prime},n_{y}}t}\}

\displaystyle n_{y}+n_{y}^{\prime}=u,\;n_{x}+n_{x}^{\prime}=v

\displaystyle=-iL^{2}\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}a_{n_{x},n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}}}\{n_{x}\sum_{u}P_{u}\omega_{n_{x},-n_{y}+u}a_{-n_{x},-n_{y}+u}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},-n_{y}+u}t}

\displaystyle-n_{y}\sum_{v}P_{v}\omega_{-n_{x}+v,n_{y}}a_{-n_{x}+v,-n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{-n_{x}+v,n_{y}}t}\}

\displaystyle n_{x}\rightarrow-n_{x},\;n_{y}\rightarrow-n_{y}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}a_{-n_{x},-n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}}}\{n_{x}\sum_{u}P_{u}\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}+u}a_{n_{x},n_{y}+u}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}+u}t}

\displaystyle-n_{y}\sum_{v}P_{v}\omega_{n_{x}+v,n_{y}}a_{n_{x}+v,n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x}+v,n_{y}}t}\}.

For any sequence
\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}=\alpha_{n_{x},n_{y}}
define two operators

\displaystyle\Lambda_{x}(\alpha)_{nx,ny}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\;P_{s}\alpha_{n_{x}+s,n_{y}},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}(\alpha)_{n_{x},n_{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\;P_{s}\alpha_{n_{x},n_{y}+s}.

Given these two operators, we can write
\mathcal{J}_{1}
in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}a_{-n_{x},-n_{y}}e^{-i\omega_{n_{x},n_{y}}}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{1})_{n_{x},n_{y}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{1})_{n_{x},n_{y}}\right\}

\displaystyle=iL^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}a_{-\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}\left\{n_{x}\Lambda_{y}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}-n_{y}\Lambda_{x}(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}\right\},

where

\displaystyle(f_{1})_{\mathbf{n}}=\omega_{n}a_{\mathbf{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}.

Appendix E

In this appendix we present numerical calculations of the
\mathbb{D}(f)
for some select functions,
f
. Some of these functions are covered by Theorem 1 from section
6
, and can thus our calculations can be though of as a sanity check on the proof of that theorem. Other functions are not covered by the Theorem, and our numerical calculations can be considered to be a first stab at figuring out if Theorem 1 holds true for a class of functions larger than the continuously differentiable ones.

In this numerical section, for any function
f(x)
we consider, we evidently cannot take the limit as
L
approaches infinity. This is what is required in order to calculate
\mathbb{D}(f)
. In order to not confuse the presentation, we introduce here the notation

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(x)=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(x+\triangle s)-f(x-\triangle s)}{\triangle s}.

Thus
\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(x)
.

In this section we are restricted to fixed but large values of
L
, and for a given function, we consider the calculation to support the conjecture,
\mathbb{D}(f)(x)=f^{\prime}(x)
, if for a chosen accuracy goal,
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(x)\approx f^{\prime}(x)
to that accuracy.

Shifted Gaussian

Let us start by considering a shifted Gaussian

\displaystyle f(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\gamma}}e^{-\gamma(x-x_{0})^{2}},

which is covered by Theorem 1.
For the calculations presented in this section we have used the parameter values
\gamma=1
and
x_{0}=20
for the Gaussian function. We evaluate
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)
at the point
\bar{x}=18
, and for the four first figures we use
L=10^{5}
. We terminate the alternating infinite series defining
\mathbb{D}^{L}
for a
s=s_{m}
such that the truncation error for the series is less than a prescribed accuracy goal for
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(\bar{x})
. Given this,
s_{m}
is a function of
L
. Since the series is now finite, the calculation of
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(\bar{x})
only uses points on the x-axis inside the interval
[\bar{x}-s_{m}\triangle,\bar{x}+s_{m}\triangle]
. This is the
sample interval
for the calculation. The accuracy goal for the calculations in this section is set to
10^{-8}
.

In figure
3
, we show the Gaussian function, the point where
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)
is evaluated, and the sample interval for the choice
L=10^{5}
. The figure clearly illustrates the non-locality of the
\mathbb{D}
operator.

Figure 3:
Gaussian function together with evaluation point
\bar{x}
, and sample interval used for calculating
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(\bar{x})
.
L=10^{5}

In figure
4
, we show the terms in the series defining the quantity
\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(\bar{x})
. It is worth noting that for the given value of
L
, and an accuracy goal of
10^{-8}
we need to include
10^{5}
terms in our series. This is easy to handle numerically for the Gaussian, but for functions that decay slower than a Gaussian, and for larger values of
L
, and higher accuracy, the calculations quickly become very challenging. We will soon see examples of this.

, where we sum the series from

In the figure we also include the exact value for the derivative of

\mathbb{D}^{L}(f)(\bar{x})

when

Complex functions

In this section we generalize the
\mathbb{D}
operator to an operator on functions,
f(z)
, of a complex variable. The formula for the operator is

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(z)=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{(s-1)}\frac{f(z+\xi\triangle s)-f(z-\xi\triangle s)}{\xi\triangle s},

where
\xi
is a complex number of unit length,
\absolutevalue{\xi}=1
. Thus for the operator
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}
our sampling occur along a straight line in the complex plane. The direction of the line is determined by the complex number
\xi
.

Let us consider the complex function

\displaystyle f(z)=\arcsin(z).

Let us pick the point where we want to evaluate
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)
to be
\bar{z}=0.5i
, let
L=10^{4}
, the accuracy be
10^{-6}
, and let
\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1+i)
.
In figure
21
we show a plot of the complex argument of
f(z)
, together with the evaluation point and a small section of the sample line. The branch cuts for the function are marked with gray.

Figure 21:
Plot of argument of the
\arcsin
, together with the evaluation point
\bar{z}
and part of the sample line. The branch cuts are displayed in gray.

We find that in order to achieve the desired accuracy we need to include
10^{10}
terms in the series defining the quantity
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(\bar{z})
. This is the highest accuracy we can reach in a reasonable time while directly summing up the terms in the series. Summing up the series using estimation methods, we find that using
10^{20}
terms in the series, and calculating with
25
digits accuracy,
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(\bar{z})=f^{\prime}(z)
within machine precision.

We have also observed using numerical computations that the quantity
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(\bar{z})
does not appear to depend on
\xi
, meaning that the quantity is independent on which sample line, through the point
\bar{z}
, we use. Since
f(z)=\arcsin(z)
is analytic at the point
\bar{z}=0.5i
, this is perhaps not so unexpected. If our
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}
operator is equal to the complex derivative, this is of course exactly what would have to happen. On the same theme, we find that if we apply our operator to a complex function that is
not
analytic, for example
f(z)=\absolutevalue{z}
, then the quantity
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(z)

does
depend on the direction of the sample line.

We have done investigations like the above for several different functions of a complex variable, and all these cases support the complex version of the original conjecture (
36
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(\bar{z})=\frac{df}{dz}(\bar{z}),

independently of
\xi
, if
f
is analytic in
z=\bar{z}
.

Our original conjecture, which was inspired by deriving the continuum limit of the total angular momentum of a scalar field in a square cavity, was restricted to functions defined on the real axis. The sample line was also restricted to being located on the real axis. However, if a function on the real axis can be extended to complex variables, one can apply the extended
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}
operator discussed in this section to compute
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(x)
for x on the real line, where the sample line now is in the complex plane. We have investigated several such situations where the extended function is analytic in a domain containing the real axis. In all such cases we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\xi}(f)(x)=\mathbb{D}(f)(x),

independently of
\xi
. Thus, we conjecture that
\mathbb{D}_{\xi}
is truly an extension of
\mathbb{D}
.
