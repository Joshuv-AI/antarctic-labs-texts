Abstract
. We construct an importance sampling method for computing statistics related to rare events for weakly interacting diffusions. Standard Monte Carlo methods behave exponentially poorly with the number of particles in the system for such problems. Our scheme is based on subsolutions of a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation on Wasserstein space which arises in the theory of mean-field (McKean-Vlasov) control. We identify conditions under which such a scheme is asymptotically optimal. In the process, we make connections between the large deviations principle for the empirical measure of weakly interacting diffusions, mean-field control, and the HJB equation on Wasserstein space. We also provide evidence, both analytical and numerical, that with sufficient regularity of the HJB equation, our scheme can have vanishingly small relative error in the many particle limit.

Consider the weakly interacting particle system:

\displaystyle dX^{i,N,s,y}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(X^{i,N,s,y}_{t},\mu^{N,s,y}_{t})dt+\sigma(X^{i,N,s,y}_{t},\mu^{N,s,y}_{t})dW^{i}_{t},\quad X^{i,N,s,y}_{s}=y_{i}

on some stochastic basis

satisfying the usual conditions, where

,

,

are independent

-dimensional standard
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-Brownian motions initialized at

,

. Here

is the space of probability measures with finite second moment (see Definition

denotes the total number of particles,

indexes a particular particle, and

(2)

\displaystyle\mu^{N,s,y}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i,N,s,y}_{t}},\quad t\in[s,T].

The

’th entry of the vector

contains the deterministic initial condition for the
i
’th particle at time

. The entries

for

are ignored for any particular value of

; we include them in defining (

) for convenience, since our purpose will be to consider the sequence of solutions to (

) as

. We will at times identify

with its projection onto

In this paper, we design a control-based importance sampling scheme for estimating functionals of the form

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\left(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})\right)\biggr],\quad G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

The basic method goes as follows: letting
v^{N}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[s,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{dN}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a chosen bounded function, or control, for each

and

, we consider the

version of the interacting particle system (

(4)

\begin{split}d\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}&=[b(\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t},\hat{\mu}^{i,N,s,y}_{t})+\sigma(\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t},\hat{\mu}^{i,N,s,y}_{t})v^{N}_{i}(t,\hat{X}^{1,N,s,y}_{t},...,\hat{X}^{N,N,s,y}_{t})]dt\\
&+\sigma(\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t},\hat{\mu}^{i,N,s,y}_{t})d\hat{W}^{i}_{t},\quad\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{s}=y_{i}.\end{split}

Girsanov’s theorem allows us to convert statistics for the controlled system (
4
) into statistics for (
1

that reduce the variance of our target statistic compared with the uncontrolled version, or in the best case cause the relative variance to vanish as

]

Our main results, stated rigorously in Section

3
) as

(Theorem

number of simulations suffices (Theorem

Our importance sampling scheme is related to and inspired by previous work concerning the development of asymptotically optimal importance sampling methods for estimating expectations of the form

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\epsilon}G\left(X^{\epsilon,s,y}_{T}\right)\right)\biggr],\quad G\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where

(6)

\begin{split}dX^{\epsilon,s,y}_{t}&=b(X^{\epsilon,s,y}_{t})dt+\sqrt{\epsilon}\sigma(X^{\epsilon,s,y}_{t})dW_{t},\\

\displaystyle=b(X^{s,\nu}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{s,\nu}_{t}))dt+\sigma(X^{s,\nu},\mathcal{L}(X^{s,\nu}_{t}))dW_{t},\quad X^{s,\nu}_{s}\sim\nu.

1.3.
Log-efficiency and asymptotic optimality in control-based importance sampling

Recall our definition of the controlled particle system (
4
), and let

(13)

\displaystyle Z^{i,N,s,y}\coloneqq\exp\biggl(-\int_{s}^{T}v^{N}_{i}(t,\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t},...,\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t})\cdot d\hat{W}^{i}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{T}|v^{N}_{i}(t,\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t},...,\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t})|^{2}dt\biggr).

By Girsanov’s theorem, an unbiased estimator for (
3
) is given by

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(-NG(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y,j})\right)\prod_{i=1}^{N}Z^{i,N,s,y,j}

where
(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y,j},Z^{1,N,s,y,j},...,Z^{N,N,s,y,j})
are
M
independent samples of
(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y},Z^{1,N,s,y}...,Z^{N,N,s,y})
, and

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}}.

We note that the standard Monte Carlo method estimator, which we denote by
\delta_{N}
, corresponds to (
14
) with
v_{i}^{N}=0
, so that
Z^{i,N,s,y,j}\equiv 1
.

We define the
relative error
of the estimator (
14
) by

(16)

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\hat{\delta}_{N}^{2}-\left[\mathbb{E}\hat{\delta}_{N}\right]^{2}}{\left[\mathbb{E}\hat{\delta}_{N}\right]^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-2NG(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y}))\prod_{i=1}^{N}(Z^{i,N,s,y})^{2}\biggr]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}.

To control the size of (
16
) we either need to increase the number of samples
M
or reduce the variance by choosing
v_{i}^{N}
so that

(17)

\displaystyle R(\hat{\delta}_{N})\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-2NG(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y}))\prod_{i=1}^{N}(Z^{i,N,s,y})^{2}\biggr]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}))\biggr]^{2}},

is close to 1. With this in mind, we define our first measure of the efficiency for an importance sampling scheme (
14
) in terms of the asymptotic behavior of
R(\hat{\delta}_{N})
as
N\rightarrow\infty
:

Definition 1.4
.

An importance sampling scheme of the form (
14
) is called
log-efficient
if

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\log R(\hat{\delta}_{N})=0.

Note that, in our definition, we do not require that
R(\hat{\delta}_{N})\to 1
or even
R(\hat{\delta}_{N})=\mathcal{O}(1)
; instead we only ask that as
N\rightarrow\infty
,
R(\hat{\delta}_{N})
does not grow exponentially in
N
.

The notion of log-efficiency can also be described in terms of large deviations theory. For the uncontrolled system, from (
9
) in Theorem
1.2
we know that if
G\in C_{b}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and Assumption
1.1
holds, then

(18)

\begin{split}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T}))]&=-\mathcal{G}(s,\nu;\mu\mapsto G(\mu_{T}))\coloneqq-\gamma_{1},\\
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T}))]&=-\mathcal{G}(s,\nu;\mu\mapsto 2G(\mu_{T}))\coloneqq-\gamma_{2}.\end{split}

Thus, for the standard Monte Carlo scheme the relative error can be written in terms of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
as follows:

(19)

\displaystyle\rho(\delta_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\exp(N[2\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}+o(1)])-1}

Starting from the inequality
\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{T})]^{2}
, taking logarithms, multiplying by
-\frac{1}{N}
, and taking the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
shows that
\gamma_{2}\leq 2\gamma_{1}.
Evidently, the relative error
\rho(\delta_{N})
grows exponentially in
N
if the inequality is strict, i.e.,
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=2\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}>0
. Exponentially many samples are therefore required to reduce the relative error below a given tolerance in that case.

Now, we assume that the the relative error for the importance sampling scheme (
16
) admits the same representation as the standard Monte Carlo importance sampling scheme in (
19
), with analogous quantities
\hat{\gamma}_{1}
,
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
, and
\hat{\gamma}=2\hat{\gamma}_{1}-\hat{\gamma}_{2}
. That is:

(20)

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\exp(N[\hat{\gamma}+o(1)])-1}.

Note that in the same way as with standard Monte Carlo, we can conclude via Jensen’s inequality that
\hat{\gamma}\geq 0
. Then we can compare the relative error
\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})
to the Monte Carlo relative error
\rho(\delta_{N})
by comparing the corresponding quantities
\hat{\gamma}
and
\gamma
:

1.

If
\hat{\gamma}<\gamma
, then the importance sampling scheme is more efficient than standard Monte Carlo in the sense that asymptotically (as
N\rightarrow\infty
), it yields a smaller relative error.

2.

If
\hat{\gamma}=0
, then the importance sampling scheme is log-efficient and, if
\gamma>0
,

\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\exp(o(N))-1}=o(\rho\left(\delta_{N}\right)).

The object of our first result Theorem
2.4
presented in the next section is to show conditions under which, for our choice of controls,
\hat{\gamma}<\gamma
or
\hat{\gamma}=0
.

Though the assumptions underlying Theorem
2.4
preclude the relative error of our importance sampling scheme from growing exponentially, extremely fast subexponential growth rates may still be possible. To obtain a more refined result, one needs to study the behavior of the
o(1)
terms in
R(\hat{\delta}_{N})
. To that end, our second main result, Theorem
2.5
, assumes an expansion of the prelimit quantities on the left-hand side of (
18
) leading to an expression for
R(\hat{\delta}_{N})
of the form

(21)

\displaystyle R(\hat{\delta}_{N})=\exp\left(a_{0}N+a_{1}+a_{2}N^{-1}+...\right).

Note that here
a_{0}
plays the same role as
\hat{\gamma}
in (
20
). Analysis of the coefficients in the power series appearing in the exponential shows that, under stronger regularity assumptions than required for our Theorem
2.4
,
a_{0}=a_{1}=0
, which implies that the importance sampling scheme has
vanishing
relative error:

Definition 1.5
.

An estimator of the form (
14
) is said to have
vanishing relative error
if, for
\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})
as in (
16
),

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})=0.

We conclude this chapter by comparing to the small-noise diffusion regime. Our discussion in this section parallels that of the first section in
[
76
]
, in which similar importance sampling scheme for small-noise diffusions is presented along with numerical schemes for estimating the optimal controls. However, we note that in our paper stating the LDP and its underlying assumptions is somewhat more involved due to the measure-valued state space.

In
[
76
]
, the authors also provide sufficient conditions for a log-efficient importance sampling scheme for small-noise diffusions to have vanishing relative error. For our Theorem
2.5
, we instead opt to perform an analysis in the spirit of the later
[
72
]
. The paper
[
72

furthered the analysis of the small-noise case by expanding the
o(1)
terms in the relative error into powers of the small-noise parameter

to recover log-efficiency from the first-order expansion, and the vanishing relative error result from of
[
76

from the second-order expansion (see also the related Section 3 of
[

2.
The HJB Equation and Statement of Main Results

We now turn to the full statement of our importance sampling scheme in terms of the solution to an HJB equation on Wasserstein space, explain its relationship to the rate function (
10
), and give a formal derivation of the importance sampling scheme and the HJB equation as the zero viscosity limit of a sequence of zero variance controls.

Starting with the Hamiltonian function which acts on
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
p\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by:

(22)

\displaystyle H_{0}(\mu,p,\Gamma)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b(x,\mu)\cdot p(x)-\frac{1}{2}p(x)\cdot[\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x,\mu)p(x)]+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x,\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Gamma(x)\mu(dx),

the HJB equation on Wasserstein space is given by:

(23)

\begin{split}-\partial_{t}\Psi(t,\nu)+H_{0}(\nu,\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[\cdot],\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[\cdot])&=0,\qquad t\in[0,T),\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\Psi(T,\nu)&=G(\nu),\qquad\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).\end{split}

Recall here that
\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the derivative of
\Psi
in the measure-valued variable
\mu
, denotes the Lions derivative of
\Psi
at
\nu
(Definition
A.1
in Appendix
A
).

We will make use of the following notion of subsolutions to (
23
):

Definition 2.1
.

We call
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a
classical subsolution
to (
23
) if:

(i)

\Psi\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

(ii)

\partial_{t}\Psi(t,\mu)-H_{0}(\mu,\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[\cdot],\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[\cdot])\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,T),\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(iii)

\Psi(T,\mu)\leq G(\mu),\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(iv)

There exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T],\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[z]|

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T],\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}|\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[z]|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|z|^{2}),

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T],\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[\cdot,\cdot]\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq C.

Note that under the above definition, a classical subsolution
\Psi
to (
23
) is also a viscosity subsolution in the sense of Definition 3.5 in
[
62
]
. Moreover, a classical
solution

\Psi
to (
23
) which also satisfies the bounds of item (iv) in Definition
2.1
is also a classical subsolution.

Remark 2.2
.

Under certain sufficient conditions stated in Proposition 3.1 of
[
65
]
, the HJB equation (
23
) coincides with the large deviations rate function
S^{\nu}_{s,T}
from (
10
) in the sense that
\Psi
solving (
23
) is given by:

(24)

\displaystyle\Psi(s,\nu)=\inf_{\mu\in C([s,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\{S^{\nu}_{s,T}(\mu)+G(\mu_{T})\}.

Stated more concretely,
[
65
]
shows that if
\sigma
is constant,
b
is Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
G(\mu)\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\mu(dz)
for some
C>0
, then any
\Psi\in C^{1,2}_{b}([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
satisfying (
23
) in the classical sense must be given by (
24
). Note that while
[
65
]
restricts to the case of Markovian feedback controls for the infimization problem (
9
), we know thanks to the convexity of the running cost in the definition of the rate function that the restricted infimization problem is equivalent to the full one (see Proposition 5.8 in
[
11
]
).

Moreover, Definition 3.5 of
[
62
]
states a natural notion of viscosity solutions to (
23
), and Theorem 3.8 therein establishes sufficient conditions for the value function
(s,\nu)\mapsto\inf_{\mu\in C([s,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\{S^{\nu}_{s,T}(\mu)+G(\mu_{T})\}
to be the unique viscosity solution to (
23
). Although that theorem does not allow for the linear growth in the control present in our drift term nor the quadratic running cost in the definition of
S^{\nu}_{s,T}
, it appears the results should hold under this same set of assumptions or similar; see Remarks 2.11 and 3.2 in
[
63
]
.

Whether the value function is indeed the unique viscosity solution to (

parallels the small-noise regime, and helped us to identify the correct PDE for designing our importance sampling scheme (compare (

2.1.
Formal derivation of the HJB equation (
23
)

In this section, we will see formally how to arrive at the expression for the control used in our importance sampling scheme in terms of the HJB equation on Wasserstein space (
23
).

Following pp. 1778-1779 of
[
76
]
, we let
\hat{\Phi}^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\times\oplus_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the solution to the backward Kolmogorov equation with terminal condition
\exp(-NG^{N}(x_{1},...,x_{N}))=\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{x}))
associated to the system of SDEs (
1
), so that
\hat{\Phi}^{N}
satisfies:

(25)

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,t,x}_{T}))],\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd},t\in[0,T],

and

(26)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x)&+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x)\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x)=0,\qquad t\in[0,T),x=(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},\\
\hat{\Phi}^{N}(T,x)&=\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{x})),x\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}.\end{split}

For fixed
N
, choosing controls

(27)

\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=\sigma^{\top}(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\frac{\partial_{x_{i}}\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})}{\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})}

in the construction of
\hat{\delta}_{N}
from (
14
) leads to a
deterministic
estimator for (
3
) with

variance:

(28)

\displaystyle e^{-NG(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y}_{T})}\prod_{i=1}^{N}Z^{i,N,s,y}=\hat{\Phi}^{N}(s,y_{1},...,y_{N}).

The change of measure described above is commonly known as the Doob
h
-transform, and the equality (
28
) can be seen via an application of Itô’s formula.

Next defining
\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=-\frac{1}{N}\log\hat{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})
, we have that
\tilde{\Phi}^{N}
is a solution to the second-order HJB equation:

(29)

\begin{split}-\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})&-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{b(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})\\
&-\frac{N}{2}|\sigma^{\top}(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})\biggr\}=0,\qquad x_{1},...,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,T),\\
\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(T,x_{1},...,x_{N})&=G(\mu^{N}_{x}),\qquad x_{1},...,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{split}

and the choice of controls from (
27
) is given in terms of
\tilde{\Phi}^{N}
by:

(30)

\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=-N\sigma^{\top}(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\partial_{x_{i}}\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N}).

As per (
28
), in (
30
) we have constructed a zero-variance estimator for (
3
) from solutions of (
29
):

(31)

\displaystyle\exp(-N\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(s,y_{1},...,y_{N}))=\mathbb{E}[e^{-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})}]=e^{-NG(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y}_{T})}\prod_{i=1}^{N}Z^{i,N,s,y},

so if one had access to
\tilde{\Phi}^{N}
in order to construct
v^{N}
, one would already be able to compute the desired expectation explicitly.

Until this point, we have merely derived PDEs for the exact expectation that we wish to compute. To derive an importance sampling scheme that is effective for all large values of
N
, we assume that, given the symmetries in the coefficients and terminal condition in (
29
), there exists
\Phi^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is fully
C^{2}
in the sense of Definition
A.2
such that
\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})=\tilde{\Phi}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x_{1},...,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then by Proposition
A.3
, we get:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b(z,\mu^{N}_{x})\cdot\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[z]-\frac{1}{2}|\sigma^{\top}(z,\mu^{N}_{x})\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[z]|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(z,\mu^{N}_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[z]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(z,\mu^{N}_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[z,z]\mu^{N}_{x}(dz)=0,\qquad x_{1},...,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,T),

\displaystyle\Phi^{N}(T,\mu^{N}_{x})

\displaystyle=G(\mu^{N}_{x}),\qquad x_{1},...,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The zero-variance optimal control from (
30
) can then be expressed as

\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=-\sigma^{\top}(x_{i},\mu^{N}_{x})\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[x_{i}].

Finally, supposing that the equation for
\Phi^{N}
holds not only for measures of the form
\mu^{N}_{x}
, but for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the above becomes:

(32)

such that
P_{2},\bar{P}_{2}
are symmetric and positive semidefinite. By Section 4 of
[
65
]
, the unique classical solution to (
23
) is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(t,\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z^{\top}\Lambda(t)z\mu(dz)+\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)\biggr]^{\top}[\Gamma(t)-\Lambda(t)]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)+\gamma(t)\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)+\chi(t),

where:

(45)

\begin{split}\dot{\Lambda}(t)+\Lambda(t)B+B^{\top}\Lambda(t)-2\Lambda(t)\sigma\sigma^{\top}\Lambda^{\top}(t)&=0,\Lambda(T)=P_{2},\\
\dot{\Gamma}(t)+\Gamma(t)[B+\bar{B}]+[B+\bar{B}]^{\top}\Gamma(t)-2\Gamma(t)\sigma\sigma^{\top}\Gamma^{\top}(t)&=0,\Gamma(T)=P_{2}+\bar{P}_{2},\\
\dot{\gamma}(t)+[B+\bar{B}]^{\top}\gamma(t)-2\Gamma(t)\sigma\sigma^{\top}\gamma(t)+2\Gamma(t)b_{0}&=0,\gamma(T)=p_{1},\\
\dot{\chi}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{\top}(t)\sigma\sigma^{\top}\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)\cdot b_{0}+\sigma^{\top}\Lambda(t)\sigma&=0,\chi(T)=p_{2}.\end{split}

The above Riccati equations admit unique solutions
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\chi\in\mathbb{R},\Lambda,\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
\Lambda,\Gamma
are symmetric and positive semidefinite for all
t
.

Then, using:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[z]=2\Lambda(t)z+2[\Gamma(t)-\Lambda(t)]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)+\gamma(t)

the controls from (
33
) in Theorem
2.4
are given by

(46)

\begin{split}v_{i}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})&=-\sigma^{\top}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[x_{i}]\\
&=-\sigma^{\top}\biggl[2\Lambda(t)x_{i}+2[\Gamma(t)-\Lambda(t)]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu^{N}_{x}(dz)+\gamma(t)\biggr].\end{split}

Note that using Remark 5.2 in
[
7
]
, we can extend to the case where
m>1
, though we refrain from doing so for simplicity. We could also allow for time dependence in the coefficients, but for the sake of simplicity we do not make this extension here.

Despite the fact that
G
from (
44
) does not satisfy the Assumption
2.3

(B1)
, as we will see in Remark
3.6
, the controls (
46
) yield not only the log-efficiency proved in Theorem
1.4
, but in fact zero relative error for all
N
.

Our first example from the linear-quadratic regime is chosen to have symmetries such that the relative error of the standard Monte Carlo estimator
\delta_{N}
and of the importance sampling estimator
\hat{\delta}_{N}
(
14
) with the choice of control (
46
) are both easily computable.

Example 3.1
.

Let
G(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\mu(dx),d=m=1
, and

\displaystyle X^{i,N,s,y}_{t}=y_{i}+\int_{s}^{t}X^{i,N,s,y}_{\tau}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{j,N,s,y}_{\tau}d\tau+\sigma W^{i}_{t}.

Symmetries in this problem allow us to calculate the target expectation (
3
), as well as the Monte Carlo relative error
\rho(\delta_{N})
and importance sampling relative error
\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})
(
16
), explicitly.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{T}))\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{y_{i}+\int_{s}^{T}X^{i,N,s,y}_{t}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{j,N,s,y}_{t}dt+\sigma W^{i}_{T}\biggr\}\biggr)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\biggr)\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-\sigma W^{1}_{T}\biggr)\biggr]^{N}

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\biggr)\exp(N\sigma^{2}(T-s)/2),

and similarly:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-2NG(\mu^{N}_{T}))\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{y_{i}+\int_{s}^{T}X^{i,N,s,y}_{t}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{j,N,s,y}_{t}dt+\sigma W^{i}_{T}\biggr\}\biggr)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\biggr)\exp(2N\sigma^{2}(T-s)),

where in the last step we have used the formula for the moment generating function of a normal random variable. Thus:

\displaystyle\rho(\delta_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\exp(2N\sigma^{2}(T-s))}{\exp(N\sigma^{2}(T-s))}-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\exp(N\sigma^{2}(T-s))-1}.

Now, to construct the control (
46
) for our importance sampling scheme, we have that in the setup of (
44
),
b_{0}=P_{2}=\bar{P}_{2}=\bar{p}_{1}=p_{2}=0
,
B=p_{1}=1
, and
\bar{B}=-1
. Thus the solution to the system of ODEs (
45
) is
\Lambda(t)=\Gamma(t)=0
,
\gamma(t)=1
, and
\chi(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t-T)
, and so the solution to the HJB equation (
23
) is

\displaystyle\Psi(t,\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\mu(dx)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t-T).

Our control from Theorem
2.4
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{v}(s,x,\mu)\equiv-\sigma.

Then, to compute the relative error of the importance sampling scheme, we have

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{T})\prod_{i=1}^{N}\exp\biggl(2\sigma\hat{W}^{i}_{T}-\int_{s}^{T}\sigma^{2}dt\biggr)\biggr]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{T}-\sigma\hat{W}^{i}_{T}))\biggr]\exp\biggl(-N\sigma^{2}(T-s)\biggr)}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{T}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}

where

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}=y_{i}+\int_{s}^{t}\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{\tau}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{\tau}-\sigma^{2}d\tau+\sigma\hat{W}^{i}_{t}.

Continuing, we have

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{y_{i}+\int_{s}^{T}\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{t}-\sigma^{2}dt\biggr\}\biggr)\biggr]\exp\biggl(-N\sigma^{2}(T-s)\biggr)}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{T}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-(T-s)\sigma^{2})\biggr)\biggr]\exp\biggl(-N\sigma^{2}(T-s)\biggr)}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}_{T}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\biggr)\exp\biggl(2N\sigma^{2}(T-s)\biggr)\exp\biggl(-N\sigma^{2}(T-s)\biggr)}{\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\biggr)\exp(N\sigma^{2}(T-s))}-1}

\displaystyle=0.

This shows that where the Monte Carlo estimator has relative error which increases exponentially as
N\rightarrow\infty
or
T\rightarrow\infty
, our importance sampling estimator actually has zero variance for all
N
and
T
.

The reader familiar with small-noise importance sampling schemes for SDEs may realize at this point that the controls computed in Example
3.1
can also be derived using the classical small-noise theory. In that vein, we make the following remark relating a special case of our importance sampling method to the classical small-noise importance sampling scheme:

Remark 3.2
.

In the linear-quadratic regime,
Y^{N,s,y}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X^{i,N,s,y}
satisfies the small-noise SDE:

\displaystyle dY^{N,s,y}_{t}

\displaystyle=[b_{0}+(B+\bar{B})Y^{N,s,y}_{t}]dt+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}d\mathcal{W}^{N}_{t},

\displaystyle Y^{N,s,y}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i},

where for each
N
,
\mathcal{W}^{N}
is the standard one-dimensional Brownian motion
\mathcal{W}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W^{i}_{t}
. Thus, when
G(\mu)=g(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\mu(dx))
for some
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, estimating functionals of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T}))]=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-Ng(Y^{N,s,y}_{T}))]

can also be done using standard small-noise importance sampling for SDEs, i.e., the appropriate control is
\hat{v}(t,y)=-\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi(t,y)
where
\psi
satisfies the standard first-order HJB equation:

(47)

\begin{split}-\partial_{t}\psi(t,y)&-[b_{0}+(B+\bar{B})y]\cdot\partial_{y}\psi(t,y)+\frac{1}{2}|\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi(t,y)|^{2}=0,\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,T),\\
\psi(T,y)&=g(y),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{split}

— see, e.g., equations (1.12) and (2.11) in
[
76
]
. The resulting importance sampling estimator is given by

(48)

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{N}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp(-Ng(\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{T}))\exp\left(-\sqrt{N}\int_{s}^{T}\hat{v}(t,\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{t})\cdot d\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{N}_{t}-\frac{N}{2}\int_{s}^{T}|\hat{v}(t,\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{t})|^{2}dt\right)

where
\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{t}
denotes the
j
’th realization of
\hat{Y}^{N,s,y}_{t}
solving

\displaystyle d\hat{Y}^{N,s,y}_{t}

\displaystyle=[b_{0}+(B+\bar{B})\hat{Y}^{N,s,y}_{t}+\sigma\hat{v}(t,\hat{Y}^{N,s,y}_{t})]dt+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}d\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{N}_{t},\quad\hat{Y}^{N,s,y}_{s}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}.

In our linear-quadratic regime (
44
), the choice of parameters
P_{2}=0,p_{1}=[p,...,p]^{\top}
(so that
g(y)=py+y^{\top}\bar{P}_{2}y+p_{2}
) is therefore covered by the small-noise theory.

To show that the two importance sampling estimators (
48
) and (
14
) are always identical in this case, we make the ansatz
\Psi(t,\nu)=\psi\left(t,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\right)
. Then
\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[z]=\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\nu(dx)\right),\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[z]\equiv 0,
and (
23
) is given by:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\psi\left(t,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\right)

\displaystyle-\biggl(b_{0}+[B+\bar{B}]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\biggr)\cdot\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left|\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\right)\right|^{2}=0,

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204ptt\in[0,T),\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\psi\left(T,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\right)

\displaystyle=g\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)\right),\qquad\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

which we see holds for any
\nu
by substituting
y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\nu(dz)
into (
47
). Then the controls from (
33
) in Theorem
2.4
are given by

\displaystyle v_{i}^{N}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=-\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right),\forall i\in\{1,...,N\}

and the importance sampling estimator (
14
) is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(-Ng\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{t}\right)\right)\exp\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{\int_{s}^{T}\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{t}\right)\cdot d\hat{W}^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{T}\left|\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{t}\right)\right|^{2}dt\biggr\}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(-Ng(\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{T}))\exp\left(-\sqrt{N}\int_{s}^{T}\hat{v}(t,\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{t})\cdot d\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{N}_{t}-\frac{N}{2}\int_{s}^{T}|\hat{v}(t,\hat{Y}^{N,s,y,j}_{t})|^{2}dt\right),

where in the first line above

\displaystyle d\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[b_{0}+B\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{t}+\frac{1}{N}\bar{B}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{t}-\sigma\sigma^{\top}\partial_{y}\psi\left(t,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{j,N,s,y}_{t}\right)\right]dt+\sigma d\hat{W}^{i}_{t},

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{i,N,s,y}_{s}

\displaystyle=y_{i}.

Note, however, that not all examples in the linear-quadratic case (
44
) can be framed as a small-noise SDE problem. For instance, even when
d=1
,
P_{2}\neq 0
, one needs to consider both
Y^{N,s,y}
and
\tilde{Y}^{N,s,y}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(X^{i,N,s,y}_{t})^{2}
. For the latter, the martingale term will be
\frac{2\sigma}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{T}X^{i,N,s,y}_{t}dW^{i}_{t}
, which cannot be written in terms of
\mathcal{W}^{N}
.

Our next example
3.3
shows that our importance sampling scheme can yield zero relative error even in the situation where the particles from (
1
) are non-interacting, and hence IID. Again, the fact that the relative error of the importance sampling scheme is zero can be viewed as the consequence of a more general principle discussed in Remark
3.4
.

Example 3.3
.

Consider now the system:

\displaystyle X^{i,N,y}_{t}=y-\int_{0}^{t}X^{i,N,y}_{s}+W^{i}_{t}

where
d=m=1
, and we fix
s=0
and suppress it in the notation for simplicity. We are now dealing with IID diffusions, that is, there is no interaction between the particles.

Suppose we take
G(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\mu(dx).
Then in the notation of (
44
),
\bar{P}_{2}=\bar{p}_{1}=p_{1}=\bar{B}=b_{0}=p_{2}=0
,
\sigma=P_{2}=1
,
B=-1
and
y_{i}=y
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the known density of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
X^{1,N,y}_{T}
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,y}_{T}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}(X^{i,N,y}_{T})^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-(X^{1,N,y}_{T})^{2}\right)\right]^{N}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(Ny^{2}/(1-2e^{2T})\right)(2-e^{-2T})^{-N/2}

and similarly

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2NG(\mu^{N,y}_{T})\right)\right]=\exp\left(2Ny^{2}/(2-3e^{2T})\right)(3-2e^{-2T})^{-N/2}.

Then:

\displaystyle\rho(\delta_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\exp\left(2Ny^{2}\left[(2-3e^{2T})^{-1}-(1-2e^{2T})^{-1}\right]\right)\left(\frac{(2-e^{-2T})^{2}}{3-2e^{-2T}}\right)^{N/2}-1}.

Meanwhile, the optimal control can be found from solving the ODEs (
45
) to get
\Lambda(t)=\Gamma(t)=\frac{e^{2t}}{2e^{2T}-e^{2t}},\gamma=0,\chi(t)=\log(\sqrt{2-e^{2t-2T}})
, so the control (
46
) is given by:

\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=\frac{2e^{2t}}{e^{2t}-2e^{2T}}x_{i}.

Then the relative error
\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\left(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{T})^{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\exp\biggl(-4\int_{0}^{T}\frac{e^{2t}}{e^{2t}-2e^{2T}}\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{t}d\hat{W}^{i}_{t}-4\int_{0}^{T}\frac{e^{4t}}{(e^{2t}-2e^{2T})^{2}}(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{t})^{2}dt\biggr)\biggr]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N,y}_{T}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{T})^{2}+2\int_{0}^{T}\frac{e^{2t}}{e^{2t}-2e^{2T}}\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{t}d\hat{W}^{i}_{t}+2\int_{0}^{T}\frac{e^{4t}}{(e^{2t}-2e^{2T})^{2}}(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{t})^{2}dt\biggr)\biggr]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-NG(\mu^{N}))\biggr]^{2}}-1}

where

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{t}=y+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\frac{2e^{2s}}{e^{2s}-2e^{2T}}-1\right]\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{s}ds+\hat{W}^{i}_{t}.

By Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{t})^{2}\frac{e^{2t}}{e^{2t}-2e^{2T}}

\displaystyle=y^{2}\frac{1}{1-2e^{2T}}+2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{4s}}{(e^{2s}-2e^{2T})^{2}}(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{s})^{2}ds+2\int_{0}^{t}\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{s}\frac{e^{2s}}{e^{2s}-2e^{2T}}d\hat{W}^{i}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\log\biggl(\frac{2e^{2T}-e^{2t}}{2e^{2T}-1}\biggr).

Setting
t=T
, we get

\displaystyle(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{T})^{2}+2\int_{0}^{T}\frac{e^{4s}}{(e^{2s}-2e^{2T})^{2}}(\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{s})^{2}ds+2\int_{0}^{T}\hat{X}^{i,N,y}_{s}\frac{e^{2s}}{e^{2s}-2e^{2T}}d\hat{W}^{i}_{s}=-y^{2}\frac{1}{1-2e^{2T}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\biggl(\frac{e^{2T}}{2e^{2T}-1}\biggr),

so, continuing:

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{\delta}_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{\exp\biggl(\frac{2N}{1-2e^{2T}}y^{2}\biggr)\frac{e^{2NT}}{(2e^{2T}-1)^{N}}}{\exp\left(2Ny^{2}/(1-2e^{2T})\right)}(2-e^{-2T})^{N}-1}=0.

This shows that in this situation as well, while the standard Monte Carlo method yields a relative error which grows exponentially in
N
for fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}
,
T>0
, and grows exponentially in
|y|
for fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
T>0
, our importance sampling scheme has zero error.

In this trivial case, the controlled particles are IID, so for both the importance sampling scheme and standard Monte Carlo we only need to simulate one particle (making the analysis in
N
a bit useless in practice, but the analysis in
|y|
still holds true when
N=1
).

Remark 3.4
.

This last example can be viewed through the following lens. Suppose that
b(x,\mu)=b(x),\sigma(x,\mu)=\sigma(x)
(so that the particles are IID when given the same initial condition) and that

\displaystyle=0,\qquad t\in[0,T),\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\langle\psi(T,\cdot),\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle g(\cdot),\mu\rangle,\qquad\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

which of course is satisfied if we have a unique solution

\displaystyle-\dot{\psi}(t,x)-b(x)\cdot\partial_{x}\psi(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}|\sigma^{\top}(x)\partial_{x}\psi(t,x)|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial^{2}_{x}\psi(t,x)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad t\in[0,T),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\psi(T,x)

\displaystyle=g(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For a related discussion, see Remark 3.3 in
[

]
. Equation (

[

]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2g(\hat{X}^{s,y}_{T})\right)\exp\left(-2\int_{s}^{T}v(t,\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})\cdot d\hat{W}_{t}-\int_{0}^{T}|v(t,\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})|^{2}dt\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-g(X^{s,y}_{T}))]^{2}

where

\displaystyle dX^{s,y}_{t}=b(X^{s,y}_{t})dt+\sigma(X^{s,y}_{t})dW_{t},X^{s,y}_{s}=y

and

\displaystyle d\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t}=[b(\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})+\sigma v(t,\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})]dt+\sigma(\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})d\hat{W}_{t},\hat{X}^{s,y}_{s}=y.

Here

and

are standard

.
Then we have, for

the empirical measure on IID copies of solutions

,
\hat{X}^{s,y}_{T}
respectively (which we denote by

,
\hat{X}^{i,s,y}_{T},i\in\mathbb{N}

are given by
v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})=-\sigma^{\top}(x_{i})\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu^{N}_{x})[x_{i}]=-\sigma^{\top}(x_{i})\partial_{x}\psi(t,x_{i})=v(t,x_{i})
, the importance sampling relative error from (

) satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\left(-2NG(\hat{\mu}^{N,s,y}_{T})\right)\prod_{i=1}^{N}(Z^{i,N,s,y})^{2}\biggr]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\left(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})\right)\biggr]^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}-2g(\hat{X}^{i,s,y}_{T})-2\int_{s}^{T}v(t,\hat{X}^{i,s,y}_{t})d\hat{W}^{i}_{t}-\int_{s}^{T}|v(t,\hat{X}^{i,s,y}_{t})|^{2}dt\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(X^{i,s,y}_{T}))\biggr]^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2g(\hat{X}^{s,y}_{T}))\exp(-2\int_{s}^{T}v(t,\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})\cdot d\hat{W}_{t}-\int_{s}^{T}|v(t,\hat{X}^{s,y}_{t})|^{2}dt\right)\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(-g(X^{s,y}_{T})\right)]^{-2}\right)^{N}-1

\displaystyle=1-1

\displaystyle=0.

Thus our importance sampling scheme yields an estimator with zero relative error in the setting of non-interacting diffusions and linear
G
.

This also shows that in the non-interacting regime, even when
G
is linear, solving (
23
) is as difficult as solving for the exact (non-zero viscosity) solution to the HJB equation associated with the importance sampling scheme for one particle (
49
). We take this remark as motivation to construct importance sampling schemes for the empirical measure of weakly interacting diffusions based on the joint small-noise and large
N
limit large deviation principles derived in
[
26
]
and
[
33
]
. We expect that using such a scheme, in the non-interacting and linear
G
regime, the small noise importance sampling scheme of, e.g.,
[
76
]
will be recovered, and solving the resulting first-order HJB equation on Wasserstein space will be equivalent to solving the standard zero-viscosity HJB equation found as Equation (2.10) therein.

Remark 3.5
.

Note, despite the framework of our linear-quadratic example and the discussion in Remarks
3.2
and
3.4
, that in Theorem
2.4
, we have the freedom to choose
G(\mu)
to be arbitrarily non-linear so long as it is sufficiently smooth to have a classical subsolution in the sense of Definition (
2.1
). That is, it need not take the form
G(\mu)=\langle g,\mu\rangle
or even
G(\mu)=g_{1}(\langle g_{2},\mu\rangle)
. Also, as evidenced in the examples of Subsection
4.2
, we expect that even if
G
is not smooth, so that only a (weak) subsolution to (
23
) can be recovered, our importance sampling scheme can be expected to still yield sub-exponentially growing (log-efficient –see Definition
1.4
), or even vanishing relative error (Definition
1.5
) — see Tables
3
and
2
, respectively.

Moreover, our importance sampling scheme treats the empirical measure of IID diffusions and of weakly interacting particles uniformly. The former case can in some sense be seen as a “Sanov’s Theorem” type generalization of the “Cramér’s Theorem” type results found in
[
37
]
, since using the ideas outlined in that paper would essentially correspond to taking independent particles and
G(\mu)=g(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz))
.

We end this section by observing that the above examples are not anomalous within the linear-quadratic setting. Namely, the exact desired expectation (
3
), and hence the relative error of the standard Monte Carlo estimator, can always easily be found by solving a system of ODEs. Moreover, from this we can make the observation that the relative error of the importance sampling estimator with control (
46
) will in fact always have zero relative error.

Note that in the linear-quadratic regime, the right hand side of prelimit representation from (
12
) in Proposition
1.3
with
\tilde{b}=b
and
F(\mu)=G(\mu_{T})
takes the form of a standard linear-quadratic stochastic control problem. We see then that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})\right)]=\bar{y}^{\top}\Lambda_{N}(s)\bar{y}+\gamma_{N}(s)\cdot\bar{y}+\chi_{N}(s),

so the desired expectation from (
3
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})\right)\right]=\exp\left(-N[\bar{y}^{\top}\Lambda_{N}(s)\bar{y}+\gamma_{N}(s)\cdot\bar{y}+\chi_{N}(s)]\right).

In the above we denote by
(y_{1},...,y_{N})=\bar{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, and
\Lambda_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN},\gamma_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},\chi_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the Riccati equations:

\displaystyle\dot{\Lambda}_{N}(t)+\Lambda_{N}(t)B_{N}+B_{N}^{\top}\Lambda_{N}(t)-2N\Lambda_{N}(t)\sigma_{N}\sigma_{N}^{\top}\Lambda_{N}^{\top}(t)

\displaystyle=0,\Lambda_{N}(T)=P_{N2},

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{N}(t)+B_{N}^{\top}\gamma(t)-2N\Lambda_{N}(t)\sigma_{N}\sigma^{\top}_{N}\gamma_{N}(t)+2\Lambda_{N}(t)b_{0N}

\displaystyle=0,\gamma_{N}(T)=p_{N1},

\displaystyle\dot{\chi}_{N}(t)-\frac{N}{2}\gamma_{N}^{\top}(t)\sigma_{N}\sigma_{N}^{\top}\gamma_{N}(t)+\gamma_{N}(t)\cdot b_{0N}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\sigma}_{i,N}^{\top}\Lambda_{N}(t)\bar{\sigma}_{i,N}

\displaystyle=0,\chi_{N}(T)=p_{2}.

Here

\displaystyle b_{0N}

\displaystyle=[b^{\top}_{0},...,b^{\top}_{0}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

\displaystyle\sigma_{N}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times N},

\displaystyle p_{1N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}[p_{1}^{\top},...,p_{1}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

\displaystyle B_{N}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(B)+\frac{1}{N}\bar{B}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN},

\displaystyle P_{2N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\text{diag}(P_{2})+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\bar{P}_{2N}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}

and
\bar{B}_{N}
is the block matrix with every
d\times d
entry given by
\bar{B}
,
\bar{P}_{2N}
is the block matrix with every
d\times d
entry given by
\bar{P}_{2}
,
\bar{\sigma}_{i,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
has i’th entry
\sigma
and the rest
0
.

It may seem initially that we need to solve
dN\times dN
Riccati equations in order to get this value, but in fact we can find that, letting
\Lambda_{N}^{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denote the
d\times d
block matrix in the
i,j
position, that
\Lambda_{N}^{i,j}=\bar{\Lambda}_{N}
for all
i\neq j
and
\Lambda_{N}^{i,i}=\hat{\Lambda}_{N}
for all
i\in\{1,...,N\}
, and hence
\gamma_{N}^{i}=\bar{\gamma}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
i
. Thus we need only solve:

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{\Lambda}}_{N}

\displaystyle+\bar{\Lambda}_{N}[\bar{B}+B]+\frac{1}{N}[\hat{\Lambda}_{N}-\bar{\Lambda}_{N}]\bar{B}+[B+\bar{B}]^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}+\frac{1}{N}\bar{B}^{\top}[\hat{\Lambda}_{N}-\bar{\Lambda}_{N}]-2N[(N-2)\bar{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}^{\top},

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt+\hat{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}^{\top}+\bar{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\hat{\Lambda}_{N}^{\top}]=0,

\displaystyle\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\bar{P}_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\Lambda}}_{N}

\displaystyle+(1-\frac{1}{N})\bar{\Lambda}_{N}\bar{B}+(1-\frac{1}{N})\bar{B}^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}+\hat{\Lambda}_{N}[\bar{B}/N+B]+[\bar{B}/N+B]^{\top}\hat{\Lambda}_{N}-2N[(N-1)\bar{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}^{\top},

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt+\hat{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\hat{\Lambda}_{N}^{\top}]=0,

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{N}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\bar{P}_{2}+\frac{1}{N}P_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{\gamma}}_{N}

\displaystyle+[\bar{B}+B]^{\top}\bar{\gamma}_{N}-2N[(N-1)\bar{\Lambda}_{N}+\hat{\Lambda}_{N}]\sigma\sigma^{\top}\bar{\gamma}_{N}+2[(N-1)\bar{\Lambda}_{N}+\hat{\Lambda}_{N}]b_{0}=0,

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{N}(T)

\displaystyle=p_{1}/N,

\displaystyle\dot{\chi}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle-\frac{N^{2}}{2}\bar{\gamma}_{N}^{\top}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\bar{\gamma}_{N}+N\bar{\gamma}_{N}\cdot b_{0}+N\sigma^{\top}\hat{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma=0,

\displaystyle\chi_{N}(T)

\displaystyle=p_{2}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-NG(\mu^{N,s,y}_{T})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-N\left[\bar{y}^{\top}\Lambda_{N}(s)\bar{y}+\gamma_{N}(s)\cdot\bar{y}+\chi_{N}(s)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-N\left[\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}y_{i}^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(s)y_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}y^{\top}_{i}\hat{\Lambda}_{N}(s)y_{i}+\bar{\gamma}^{\top}(s)\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}+\chi_{N}(s)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\Biggl(-N\Biggl[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\right)^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(s)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}y^{\top}_{i}\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{N}(s)-\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(s)\right]y_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\bar{\gamma}^{\top}(s)\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}+\chi_{N}(s)\Biggr]\Biggr)

Note that this is useful not only for establishing the true desired expectation (
3
), but also allows for us to calculate the relative error for the standard Monte Carlo estimator
\rho(\delta_{N})
by substituting 2
G
for

in (

) with

and
\bar{P}_{2}
modified to
2P_{2},2p_{1},
and
2\bar{P}_{2}
, respectively. We will use this fact in the numerical example in Subsection

.

Also note that we can see directly how to write the right-hand side of the previous display as a function of
\mu^{N}_{y}
. Letting

(50)

\begin{split}\Phi^{N}(t,\nu)&=N^{2}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)\right]^{\top}\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(t)\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)\right]+N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z^{\top}[\hat{\Lambda}_{N}(t)-\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(t)]z\mu(dz)\\
&\hskip 227.62204pt+N\bar{\gamma}_{N}^{\top}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)+\tilde{\chi}_{N}(t)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z^{\top}\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(t)z\mu(dz)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)\right)^{\top}\left[\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(t)-\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(t)\right]\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)\right)+\tilde{\gamma}_{N}^{\top}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu(dz)+\tilde{\chi}_{N}(t),\end{split}

where
\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(t)=N\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{N}(t)-\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(t)\right],\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}=N(N-1)\bar{\Lambda}_{N}(t)+N\hat{\Lambda}(t),\tilde{\gamma}_{N}(t)=N\gamma(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(-N\Phi^{N}(s,\mu^{N}_{x})\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-NG(\mu^{N,s,x}_{T})\right)\right],

and get the Riccati equations:

(51)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\Lambda}}_{N}

\displaystyle+\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}B+B^{\top}\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}-2\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}^{\top}=0,\quad\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(T)=P_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\Gamma}}_{N}

\displaystyle+\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}[B+\bar{B}]+[B+\bar{B}]^{\top}\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}-2\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}^{\top}=0,\quad\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(T)=\bar{P}_{2}+P_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle+[B+\bar{B}]^{\top}\tilde{\gamma}_{N}(t)-2\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(t)\sigma\sigma^{\top}\tilde{\gamma}_{N}(t)+2\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(t)b_{0}=0,\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{N}(T)=p_{1},

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\chi}}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\gamma}_{N}^{\top}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\tilde{\gamma}_{N}+\tilde{\gamma}_{N}\cdot b_{0}+\sigma^{\top}\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}\sigma+\frac{1}{N}\sigma^{\top}[\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}-\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}]\sigma=0,\quad\tilde{\chi}_{N}(T)=p_{2}.

Note firstly that it is easily verified that
\Phi^{N}(t,\nu)
from (

) satisfies the prelimit HJB equation (
32
) for all
\nu
. Also observe that, other than the perturbation of the ODE for

by the addition of the term

, these are the same Riccati equations as (

\displaystyle v^{N}_{i}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{\top}\biggl[2\tilde{\Lambda}(t)x_{i}+2[\tilde{\Gamma}(t)-\tilde{\Lambda}(t)]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu^{N}_{x}(dz)+\tilde{\gamma}(t)\biggr]

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{\top}\biggl[2\Lambda(t)x_{i}+2[\Gamma(t)-\Lambda(t)]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\mu^{N}_{x}(dz)+\gamma(t)\biggr],

that is, it is the same as the control from Theorem

Remark 3.6
.

The above discussion implies that in the linear-quadratic regime, the relative error of our importance sampling scheme is always zero.

This can also be derived via the following observation: In (
32
), when we use the ansatz that the solution takes the form of (
50
), since
\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(\mu)[z_{1},z_{2}]=2[\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(t)-\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(t)]
, the only term appearing from the second-order term
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Phi^{N}(t,\mu)[z,z]\mu(dz)
is
\frac{1}{N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(t)-\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(t)]=\frac{1}{N}\sigma^{\top}[\tilde{\Gamma}_{N}(t)-\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}(t)]\sigma
. Thus, when deriving the form of the Riccati equations (
51
), it will only show up for
\tilde{\chi}_{N}
, which collects the constant in
\mu
terms.

More generally, if we have a solution to (
23
) such that
\partial_{\mu}\Psi=\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{N}
for all
N
, where
\Phi^{N}
solves (
32
), then the importance sampling scheme has zero relative error for all
N
, as per the discussion in Section
2.1
. Supposing, as in the linear-quadratic case, that we have a solution
\Psi
to (
23
) such that
\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Psi(t,\mu)[z_{1},z_{2}]=C(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and that
\sigma
does not depend on
x,\mu
, then
\Psi(T,\nu)=G(\nu)
and:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\Psi(t,\nu)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b(z,\nu)\cdot\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[z]-\frac{1}{2}|\sigma^{\top}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[z]|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}(z,\nu)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[z]+\frac{1}{2N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\partial^{2}_{\mu}\Psi(t,\nu)[z,z]\nu(dz)=-\frac{1}{2N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}C(t),

so letting
\lambda\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R})
solve
\dot{\lambda}(t)=-\frac{1}{2N}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}C(t),\lambda(T)=0
,
\Phi^{N}(t,\mu)=\Psi(t,\mu)+\lambda(t)
is a solution to (
32
), and
\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{N}=\partial_{\mu}\Psi
for all
N
.

It thus clear that letting
m>1
or having the coefficients
b_{0},B,\bar{B},\sigma
in (
44
) depend on time does not change the fact that our importance sampling scheme yields a zero-variance estimator in the linear-quadratic regime. Also note that this discussion also applies in the linear-quadratic setting for small noise importance sampling schemes — compare Equations (2.7) and (2.10) in
[
76
]
and recall that if
b
is linear in
x
,
\sigma
is constant, and
G
is quadratic in
x
therein that solutions to equation (2.10) will have second derivatives which are constant in
x
.

This reflects a difficulty present in both our setting and the small noise setting for importance sampling schemes based on large deviation principles and zero-viscosity HJB equations — the hurdle of actually computing the derivative of a solution to the HJB equation and hence the control to be used, numerically or otherwise, persists, and in the main class of problems for which a solution can be computed analytically (the linear-quadratic regime), the solution to the zero-viscosity HJB equation is no easier to solve for than the desired expectation. This means that efficient methods for numerically computing
\partial_{\mu}\Psi
appearing in the definition of the control (
33
) in Theorem
2.4
, or otherwise numerically obtaining the control, are highly desirable. Designing and implementing such schemes is an interesting avenue for future research.

Here we present numerical results based on examples in the linear-quadratic regime of (
3

0
for all

Remark 4.3
.

We can think about the controls from Examples
4.1
and
4.2
in terms of the role they play in sampling the “rare event” of
G
being close to
0
for the particle system in each case. When the absolute value appears outside the empirical mean, as in Example
4.1
, we only need to be concerned with controlling the magnitude of the empirical mean, a characteristic of the particle ensemble. This is reflected in the controls, which are identical for every particle and force them in the opposite direction of the sign of the empirical mean. Meanwhile, in the situation of Example (
4.2
), to make
G
close to zero we need the position of each individual particle to be near
0
. Thus, the control uses information about the position of each particle relative to the origin. However, information about the ensemble of particles also still must appear because of the global interaction in the particles’ dynamics, which we see in the form of the difference in the proportions of the particles which are positive and negative at a given time.

We note that a similar combination of global information on the ensemble of particles and local information about the
i
’th particle is also used for controls in the standard linear-quadratic regime of Section
3
. See in particular the control used in our simple numerical example in Subsection
4.1
. Numerical experiments confirm that the role of both the global and local information captured in the control is necessary for variance reduction in such cases. For example, using the simplified control
\tilde{v}(t,x_{1},...,x_{N})=-\sigma e^{B(T-t)}\text{sign}(x_{i})
in Example
4.2
, we observed performance that was comparable or even worse than standard Monte Carlo.

It is also worth commenting on the choice of initial conditions in the examples of Subsection
4.2
. We choose
y_{i}=.4
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\nu=\delta_{.4}
is away from the region of discontinuity of
\partial_{\mu}\Psi(t,\cdot)[z]
. We observed that if we took initial conditions such that
\nu
is closer to the region of discontinuity many more rogue trajectories appeared in the controlled dynamics and an extremely high sample size was needed before the importance sampling scheme could be seen to perform better than standard Monte Carlo. This is once again similar to the case of small-noise diffusions—see Table 3.1 compared to Table 3.2 in
[
76
]
and the discussion on pp. 1786-1787 therein.

We end this section by remarking on the interesting problem of verifying that the formal viscosity solutions to the HJB equation (
23
) constructed in Examples
4.1
and
4.2
are indeed viscosity solutions in the sense of
[
62
]
. To our knowledge, there are no existing examples in the current literature of explicit viscosity solutions which are not also classical solutions to an HJB equation on Wasserstein space.

6.
Conclusions and Future Work

We have derived an importance sampling scheme for exponential functionals of the empirical measure of weakly interacting diffusions. Using the connection between the large deviations rate function of
[
5
]
and mean-field optimal control, the asymptotic performance of the proposed scheme is characterized in terms of subsolutions of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation on Wasserstein space. We provide both numerical and analytical evidence that sufficient smoothness of such a solution can yield relative error which vanishes as the number of particles becomes large. We also numerically explore the impact of lack of smoothness of the solution of the HJB equation on the performance of the proposed importance sampling scheme.

In future work, a major hurdle to overcome will be adapting the scheme to situations where subsolutions of the HJB equation cannot be constructed analytically. In the standard small noise setting, this is also a major issue, and when the dimension of the system becomes large advanced techniques such as the use of machine learning, optimization software, and neural networks to identify the solution of the HJB equation and/or the optimal control are used
[
67
,
69
,
73
,
76
]
. In the mean-field setting, methods of stochastic control are already being used to address the issue of numerically constructing solutions to the HJB equation (
23
), see e.g.
[
58
,
55
,
28
,
78
,
48
,
16
,
25
]
. Moreover, using the calculus of variations form of the rate function of Dawson-Gärtner
[
19
]
, there are some examples in the literature where perturbation expansions have been made to approximate the optimal path of the controlled McKean-Vlasov equation corresponding to certain types of rare events
[
42
,
6
]
. The marriage of such techniques with our proposed scheme would allow for applications beyond the linear quadratic regime and perturbations thereof, and perhaps even allow for extensions to finite-time probabilities and problems of metastability as discussed in Remark
2.6
.

It may also prove useful for some target statistics to design an importance sampling scheme using the moderate deviations principle for the empirical measure
[
13
,
12
]
. This is known to aid with the problems discussed above in the small noise setting due to the linearization of the HJB equation under the moderate deviations scaling
[
23
,
29
]
. As discussed in Remark
3.4
, this can likely also be supplemented via use of an importance sampling scheme arising from large deviations of the empirical measure in the joint small noise and large
N
limit as derived in
[
33
,
26
]
.

Another interesting avenue for future research is to see how our methodology can be extended to the setting where the interacting particles also share a common driving noise
[
3
,
34
]
.

Lastly, it is of great interest to establish rigorously the asymptotic expansions for the prelimit HJB equation presumed in Theorem
2.5
—see Remark
2.7
.

Definition A.1
