Quantum measurements and equilibration: the emergence of objective reality via entropy maximisation
Emanuel Schwarzhans, Felix C. Binder, Marcus Huber, Maximilian P. E. Lock

Abstract
Textbook quantum physics features two types of dynamics, reversible unitary dynamics and irreversible measurements. The latter stands in conflict with the laws of thermodynamics and has evoked debate on what actually constitutes a measurement. With the help of modern quantum statistical mechanics, we take the first step in formalising the hypothesis that quantum measurements are instead driven by the natural tendency of closed systems to maximize entropy, a notion that we call the Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis. In this paradigm, we investigate how objective measurement outcomes can emerge within an purely unitary framework, and find that: (i) the interactions used in standard measurement models fail to spontaneously feature emergent objectivity and (ii) while ideal projective measurements are impossible, we can (for a given form of Hamiltonian) approximate them exponentially well as we collect more physical systems together into an “observer” system. We thus lay the groundwork for self-contained models of quantum measurement, proposing improvements to our simple scheme.



I
Introduction

The mathematical formulation of quantum theory includes two types of dynamics for a closed quantum system: unitary evolution, and measurement. The latter is often referred to as a collapse of the wavefunction, instantaneously updating the state of the system, and is at the heart of many interpretational discussions of quantum mechanics (e.g. the “measurement problem”). For projective measurements, this is an apparent contradiction of the laws of thermodynamics, as it does not conserve energy, allows for an arbitrary decrease in entropy
Guryanova et al. 2020
, and allows for cooling to zero-temperature with finite resources, in violation of Nernst’s formulation of the
3^{\text{rd}}
law
Nernst 1906
;
Taranto et al. 2022
. In this work, we investigate a potential resolution to this apparent contradiction by modelling the process of measurement as the system of interest and its environment undergoing an equilibration process, thus being driven by an
increase
in entropy.

In the widely-used von Neumann measurement scheme
Von Neumann 1930
, subsequently developed into what is sometimes referred to as the standard model of measurement
Busch and Lahti 1996
, a carefully-timed interaction correlates the system of interest with some probe or pointer system. This allows, for example, an investigation of the restrictions placed by quantum theory on incompatible observables. Quantum Darwinism
Zurek 2003
;
Zurek 2006
;
Zurek 2009
broadens this model to include the environment, and thus the observer itself, highlighting the role of the redundant encoding of information about the system state in the measurement basis (the “pointer basis”) into the environment degrees of freedom, arguably removing the need for an explicit “Heisenberg cut”. Demanding that this redundancy be such that multiple observers (i.e. parts of the environment) independently
agree on the system state, and that the only correlations between them be their shared information about the system, implies that the total state of the system and observers necessarily exhibits Spectrum Broadcast Structure
Horodecki et al. 2015
;
Le and Olaya-Castro 2019
;
Korbicz 2021
. The appearance of this structure has been demonstrated theoretically for a dielectric sphere in a photonic environment
Korbicz et al. 2014
, in a spin-spin model
Mironowicz et al. 2017
;
Mironowicz et al. 2018
, and for Quantum Brownian Motion
Tuziemski and Korbicz 2016
.

The process of measurement, specifically the transformation from quantum indeterminacy to an objective fact, is irreversible, and occurs without precise control of all microscopic degrees of freedom of the system’s environment. A dynamical model of measurement must therefore explain how the system and its environment evolve spontaneously and irreversibly towards a state structure exhibiting objectivity. Since spontaneous and irreversibile processes in nature are those associated with the process of equilibration, we hypothesise that any measurement is an entropy-increasing transition towards an equilibrium state, and refer to this as the Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis. The notion that measurements correspond to an increase in entropy has been discussed at various points since the early years of quantum theory,
e.g.

Szilard 1929
;
Von Neumann 1930
;
Misra et al. 1979
;
Daneri et al. 1962
;
Allahverdyan et al. 2013
, but is absent from dynamical models of measurement. Recent advances in quantum statistical mechanics, specifically in the equilibration of closed quantum systems
Gogolin and Eisert 2016
allow us to address this absence, and as a first step in this direction, we seek to determine the circumstances under which a general system and environment equilibrate to states exhibiting objective properties, including the Spectrum Broadcast Structure mentioned above.

In this work, we constrain the form of the interactions capable of producing such equilibrium states, and show that while it is impossible to reach this state structure exactly without violating the laws of thermodynamics, one can approach it exponentially as we increase the marcroscopicity of “observers” for whom the measurement outcome should be objective. We find that the usual form of interactions employed in the standard model of measurement in fact does not result in an equilibrium state exhibiting objectivity, or even any correlations whatsoever between the system and its environment.

We begin by revisiting the concepts of objectivity and Spectrum Broadcast Structure and how they connect, as well as the role of the time-averaged state in equilibration phenomena. We then derive our main results and discuss the consequences of our findings as well as future directions and open questions.

II
Equilibration of quantum systems

To investigate the conditions under which objectivity emerges as a result of equilibration, we will employ the notion of equilibration on average
Gogolin and Eisert 2016
, whereby the equilibrium state is given by the infinite-time average. That is, if a system evolving as
\rho(t)
equilibrates on average, then the equilibrium state is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}:=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\rho(t)dt.

(1)

This is the unique state which maximises the von Neumann entropy given all conserved quantities

Gogolin et al. 2011
;
Gogolin and Eisert 2016
. To illustrate the sense in which states equilibrate to their time average, we now review two examples of equilibration on average.

First, consider a system evolving unitarily via some time-independent Hamiltonian
H
, so that
\rho(t)=U(t)\rho(0)U^{\dagger}(t)
with
U(t)=e^{iHt}
, and denote by
\{p_{j}\}_{j}
the diagonal elements of
\rho(0)
in the eigenbasis of
H
. The effective dimension – sometimes called the inverse participation ratio – is defined as
d_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho(0)]:=(\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2})^{-1}
. Assuming that no two energy gaps of
H
are equal in magnitude (which also implies that
H
is non-degenerate), then an arbitrary bounded operator
O
satisfies
Reimann 2008
;
Short 2011

\langle|\textnormal{Tr}[O\rho(t)-O\rho_{\infty}]|^{2}\rangle\leq\frac{||O||^{2}}{d_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho(0)]},

(2)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the average over time and
||\cdot||
is the standard operator norm. As a second example, consider
\rho(t)
to be some subsystem of a larger, unitarily evolving system,
i.e.

\rho(t)=\textnormal{Tr}_{X}[U(t)\tilde{\rho}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)]
for some
\tilde{\rho}(0)
and tensor factor
X
, and again assume that no two energy gaps of
H
are equal. In this case, the average trace distance between
\rho(t)
and
\textnormal{Tr}_{X}[\tilde{\rho}_{\infty}]
satisfies
Linden et al. 2009

\langle D\left[\rho(t),\textnormal{Tr}_{X}[\tilde{\rho}_{\infty}]\right]\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{d^{2}}{d_{\mathrm{eff}}[\tilde{\rho}(0)]}},

(3)

where
d
is the dimensionality of the Hilbert space to which the subsystem
\rho(t)
belongs.

We note three issues for both of these examples to be considered equilibration. First, both rely on an effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}[\rho(0)]
being in some sense large. This quantity measures the number of significantly occupied energy levels participating in the evolution of
\rho(t)
, and its typically large size for large systems can be argued for on both physical and mathematical grounds. Second, there is the necessity for some finite equilibration time, and third, the possibility of Poincaré recurrences. All three of these issues, as well as generalisations beyond the condition of no equal energy gaps, are addressed in detail in
Gogolin and Eisert 2016
.

III
Objectivity and quantum states

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
denote the Hilbert space corresponding to some
d_{\mathrm{S}}
-dimensional system of interest, and let
\{\mathcal{H}_{k}\}
with
k=1,\ldots,N
denote the Hilbert spaces corresponding to
N
systems which form the environment of the system of interest, and which we will associate with observers, for whom we seek objectivity. Note that we do not
a priory
impose any criteria for what is to be considered as an observer. In order for a fact about the system to be considered objective (and thus classical), multiple observers must be able to discover it in such a way that they agree with each other and that none of them changes the fact through their observation. As noted above, formalising this notion in the context of quantum theory
Ollivier et al. 2004
and combining it with the requirement that observers be uncorrelated except via the system (“strong independence”), is equivalent to demanding that the state of the system and the observers exhibits Spectrum Broadcast Structure
Horodecki et al. 2015
. A state has Spectrum Broadcast Structure with respect to a pointer basis
\{|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\}
, where
i=1,\ldots,d_{\mathrm{S}}
, if it is of the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{SBS}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{S}}}p_{i}\,|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(i)},

(4)

where
p_{i}
is the probability associated with outcome
\left|\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right\rangle
, and
\rho_{k}^{(i)}
is the state on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
conditioned on that outcome, and where
\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)}=0
for
i\neq j
. As in
Tuziemski and Korbicz 2016
, we will find it more convenient to express this orthogonality in terms of the fidelity, namely
F(\rho_{k}^{(i)},\rho_{k}^{(j)})=0
for
i\neq j
, where
F(\sigma,\rho):=\textnormal{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}\right)^{2}
.

The structure in Eq. (
4
) thus represents a state where quantum information has become redundantly encoded, and through this can be considered classical
Blume-Kohout and Zurek 2006
. Note, however, that this is only true in the pointer basis, in accordance with no-cloning theorem
Wootters and Zurek 1982
. This state structure holds if and only if the conditions of Quantum Darwinism and Strong Independence are met
Le and Olaya-Castro 2019
. A projective measurement can then be understood as a post-selection performed on the process by which an initially uncorrelated state between system and observers tends to a state with Spectrum Broadcast Structure in the measurement basis. Consequently, to say that a projective measurement has been performed, one must first arrive at a state structure exhibiting objectivity.

IV
Measurement as an equilibration process

With this framework, we can now state precisely what it means to demand that objectivity emerge as a consequence of equilibration, and thus the maximization of entropy. To wit, we assume that system and observers are initially uncorrelated and seek the conditions under which they equilibrate to a state of objectivity, specifically when
\rho_{\infty}=\rho_{SBS}
. Note that this demand does not restrict the observer states
\rho_{k}^{(i)}
in Eq. (
4
), beyond the orthogonality requirement described above. As above, we denote the measurement basis on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
by
\{\left|\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right\rangle\}_{i}
, and the outcome probabilities by
\{p_{i}\}
,
i.e.

p_{i}=\left\langle\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\left|\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right\rangle
, where
\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}
is the initial state of the system.

Note that the infinite-time average is a pinching map with respect to the Hamiltonian generating the evolution
Gogolin et al. 2011
,
i.e.

\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{E}\Pi_{E}\rho(0)\Pi_{E},

(5)

where
H=\sum_{E}E\,\Pi_{E}
is the spectral decomposition of
H
, and we therefore have
[H,\rho_{\infty}]=0
. Consequently, to equilibrate to a state with a given structure, the Hamiltonian must commute with all states of that structure. In particular, for Spectrum Broadcast Structure, we must have
[H,\rho_{\mathrm{SBS}}]=0
, which can be interpreted as an extension of the commutation relation defining the pointer basis
Zurek 1981
to the observers’ systems. As we discuss below, no such Hamiltonian exists, which precludes exact equilibration to states with this structure.

V
Exact projective measurements are impossible

Let us first examine a weaker requirement than that of Spectrum Broadcast Structure.
We write the initial state
{\rho(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\otimes\rho_{E,0}}
, where
\rho_{E,0}
is the state on
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and we demand a “classical-quantum”
Devetak and Winter 2004
equilibrium state between the system and the observers,
i.e.

\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{S}}}p_{i}\,|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\rho_{E}^{(i)}
, where the
\{\rho_{E}^{(i)}\}_{i}
are some density matrices on
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Such a state may violate the Strong Independence condition.
As shown in Appendix
B
, this requirement implies that
H
takes the form

H=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)},

(6)

for some Hermitian matrices
\{H_{E}^{(i)}\}_{i}
on
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Note that Eq. (
6
) can only be considered as a sum of a free system Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{S}}
and some interaction
H_{int}
in the case where the pointer basis consists of eigenstates of
H_{\mathrm{S}}
. For measurement in any other basis, it corresponds to an interaction term alone, necessitating the so-called measurement limit, often used in dynamical models of measurement, where free terms in the Hamiltonian are neglected
i.e.

H\approx H_{int}
(see
e.g.

Busch and Lahti 1996
).

When we now demand, in addition to the above, that the observers’ states unambiguously encode the system state,
i.e.
that
F(\rho_{E}^{(i)},\rho_{E}^{(j)})=0
for
i\neq j
, we find that this only holds when
\rho_{E}^{(i)}=0

\forall i
(see Appendix
B
for proof). In particular,
F(\rho_{k}^{(i)},\rho_{k}^{(j)})
is bounded below by
1/d_{E}^{2}
, where
d_{E}
is the dimensionality of
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Consequently, it is impossible for an initially uncorrelated state to equilibriate exactly to a state with the properties listed above, as one might expect. This precludes equilibration to a state exhibiting objectivity, as defined in
Horodecki et al. 2015
. The stronger condition of Spectrum Broadcast Structure (which combines objectivity with Strong Independence) is then likewise impossible. In
Guryanova et al. 2020
, the authors found that it was possible to perform an ideal projective measurement given access to a zero-temperature pointer state, but here we see that, adopting the Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis, it is impossible even given access to such a resource. That the lower bound of
F(\rho_{k}^{(i)},\rho_{k}^{(j)})
rapidly decreases with dimensionality however, does allow the approximation an objective state as the size of the environment increases, as we will discuss later.

VI
Equilibration and the standard model of measurement

As discussed above, dynamical models of measurement usually assume a degree of temporal control such that a particular unitary transformation can be performed on the system and some apparatus/environment, generated the product of observables acting on the system and the apparatus
Busch and Lahti 1996
. For example, one such model was used to investigate the transition to Spectrum Broadcast Structure in
Tuziemski and Korbicz 2016
. In our context, let us denote such a Hamiltonian by
H=X_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes Y
, where
X_{\mathrm{S}}
is any operator whose eigenbasis is the pointer basis, and where
Y
is an observable on
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. In this case, as shown in Appendix
C
, the equilibrium state is given by

\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{S}}}p_{i}\,|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\rho_{E},

(7)

for some state
\rho_{E}
on
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. This highlights the distinction between the standard model of measurement and the equilibration paradigm; given temporal control, one can of course generate the desired correlations between the system and the observers, but considering a spontaneous and uncontrolled process, one finds that on average there are no correlations whatsoever between the system and the observers.

VII
Approximating projective measurements

While exact equilibration to a state with Spectrum Broadcast Structure is impossible, there remains the question of how well an equilibrium state can approximate this structure. We saw earlier that, with increasing dimension of
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
, one can approach the desired orthogonality condition required for objectivity (though recall that this was a weaker condition than Spectrum Broadcast Structure). In the same vein, we will now see that by collecting the observers’ systems together into composite systems, which we call macro-observers, one can approach Spectrum Broadcast Structure exponentially as the size of these macro-observers increases.

As discussed above, equilibration to a state exhibiting the appropriate statistics in the pointer basis, with no coherences between different pointer states, and allowing correlations between the observers and the system in this basis requires a conditional structure of the Hamiltonian given in Eq. (
6
). With the Strong Independence condition in mind, which demands that the observers should be uncorrelated except via their correlations with the system, we expand this to consider an interaction of the form

H=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}H_{k}^{(i)}.

(8)

We can see this structure as coupling
k
to
\mathrm{S}
with coupling strength
c_{k}
, such that for the system state
i
, the action
H_{k}^{(i)}
is performed. Assuming
H
in Eq. (
8
) to be non-degenerate and starting with the state
{\rho(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k,0}}
, we arrive at the equilibrium state

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{S}}}p_{i}\,|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(i)},

(9)

where
\rho_{k}^{(i)}
are obtained from
\rho_{k,0}
by applying the pinching map with respect to
H_{k}^{(i)}
. This state has the form required for Spectrum Broadcast Structure, but it remains to check the orthogonality condition. To that end, let
\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m,k}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle
denote the eigenvector of
H_{k}^{(i)}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\varepsilon_{m,k}^{(i)}
. For simplicity of presentation, we assume that
H_{k}^{(i)}
and
H_{k}^{(j)}
have no common eigenvectors, and in Appendix
D
we show how to obtain a similar result without this assumption. We will find it useful to define the quantity

\eta_{k}^{(ij)}:=\sum_{m}\sqrt{\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m,k}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m,k}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle}.

(10)

Now, let
M<N
denote the number of macro-observers, and let
N_{q}
denote the set of
k
labels to be collected together to form a given macro-observer
q
(see Fig.
1
). We can then write Eq. (
9
) in terms of macro-observers as
\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{S}}}p_{i}\,|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{M}\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)}
, where
\{\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)}:=\bigotimes_{k\in N_{q}}\rho_{k}^{(i)}\}
are states on
\bigotimes_{k\in N_{q}}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. As we show in Appendix
D
, the fidelity between the conditional states of the macro-observers satisfies

F(\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)})\leq e^{-\gamma_{q}^{(ij)}|N_{q}|},

(11)

where

\gamma_{q}^{(ij)}:=-2\ln\left(\max_{k\in N_{q}}\eta_{k}^{(ij)}\right)>0,

(12)

Thus as we increase each
|N_{q}|
,
i.e.
the the number of degrees of freedom collected together to form each macro-observer
q
, the equilibrium state tends exponentially towards one with Spectrum Broadcast Structure for the macro-observers. In other words, as we coarse-grain our view of observers towards ever-larger collections of microscopic systems, we tend exponentially towards a state of objectivity commensurate with the occurrence of a measurement; macroscopic observers will see objective facts about the system.

8
. It depicts the interaction of system

VIII
Discussion and conclusions

Quantum measurement is often described as the ‘collapse of the wavefunction’ when a quantum system makes contact with an observer. One is then led to the question, what exactly can constitute an observer?
Brukner 2015
. The Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis allows us to constrain this question, giving the answer that an observer is whatever system interacts with the system of interest such that the result equilibrates to a state that approximates objective state structure asymptotically. In this paradigm, we have shown the impossibility of exact projective measurements (in accordance with known results
Guryanova et al. 2020
). However, they can be approximated exponentially as more and more microscopic systems are incorporated into a given macroscopic observer. This accords with the intuition that small systems should not be considered observers
Brukner 2021
, but that larger ones can be
Brukner 2022
;
Wiseman et al. 2022
.

Our results provide a physical interpretation of the long-time objectivity of the spin-spin model studied in
Mironowicz et al. 2017
. Furthermore, understanding the initially uncorrelated state as an out-of-equilibrium resource, the Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis provides a novel perspective on engines based on the extraction of work via quantum measurements
Elouard et al. 2017
;
Elouard and Jordan 2018
, and thus a potential path to resolving the foundational questions regarding the source of this energy
Jordan et al. 2020
.

In the simple version of an equilibriating measurement presented above, the Strong Independence condition on the equilibrium state led to the demand that observers interact via a Hamiltonian with a conditional form, and surprisingly, that the particular conditional form given by the standard model of measurement does not satisfy the most minimal requirement for measurement, namely correlation of observer and system. Thus, while an average over such Hamiltonians appears to result in objectivity at a given time
Korbicz et al. 2017
, any given Hamiltonian of the ensemble does not equilibrate to an objective one. More broadly, the conditional form imposed by Strong Independence does not resemble the usual forms of interaction found in nature, suggesting that a the model needs to be expanded if the Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis is to be employed in the modelling of real-world experiments.

In this regard, two immediate generalisations of our work may suffice. First, one might allow for vanishingly small correlations violating the Strong Independence condition. It seems likely that the requirement that such violations be vanishingly small will nonetheless strongly constrain the form of the interaction. A second, and in our view more promising generalisation, would be the inclusion of an initial “pre-measurement” step, whereby one allows some time-controlled interaction to correlate an apparatus with the system of interest, whose subsequent equilibration with some observers leads to a state of objectivity between the system of interest and the observers; in this case the requirement that the system of interest be undisturbed in the measurement basis would be less restrictive in the equilibrium step. Indeed many measurements modelled via projection operators do not leave the system in an eigenstate corresponding to the measurement outcome,
e.g.
photon detection.

Our work provides a new thermodynamically consistent framework for tackling questions regarding the role of the preparation/driving of measurement apparatuses and the characterisation of the necessary out-of-equilibrium resources in the measurement process. Since the equilibrium states considered are not thermal, it is of interest to examine the relationship between our model and many-body localisation
Abanin and Papić 2017
and in particular whether a pre-thermalisation phenomenon
Gring et al. 2012
may be at play. This is reinforced by the work of
Le et al. 2021
, showing that thermality and objectivity are often at odds. In addition, one may go beyond the simple statement of the Measurement-Equilibration Hypothesis above to investigate whether the quality of the measurement depends on the amount of entropy dissipated in the process, and how this may relate to the timescale of the measurement
Strasberg et al. 2020
.

While ours is only a first step towards a thermodynamically self-contained model for quantum measurements, we hope that this will resolve many of the foundational issues that manifest through the apparent inconsistency of text-book postulates of quantum measurements and thermodynamic laws.

Appendix A
Setting and notation

In the following appendices, we prove the results stated in the main text. First, for ease of reference, we recall the mathematical setting as well as some notation.

We assume a Hilbert space structure given by
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{E}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where we denote the system that is being measured by the subscript S, the total environment by the subscript E and the observers’ systems which constitute the environment by
k
. The Hamiltonian of the system will be denoted by
H
, and the measurement (
i.e.
“pointer”) basis by
\{|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\}_{i}
. The probability of measurement outcome
i
is then given by

p_{i}:=\textnormal{Tr}\left[\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\right],

(13)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}
is the initial state on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
. The time average of a quantity
A(t)
is denoted by

\displaystyle\langle A(t)\rangle_{T}:=\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}A(t)dt.

For equilibrating systems the equilibrium state is given by the infinite-time average state

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}:=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-iHt}\rho(0)e^{iHt}dt.

Equivalently, this state is given by the pinching map with respect to the Hamiltonian
Gogolin et al. 2011
:

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{n}\Pi_{n}\rho(0)\Pi_{n},

(14)

where the
\Pi_{n}
are projectors onto degenerate subspaces of
H
such that
H=\sum_{n}E_{n}\Pi_{n}
.

For simplicity, when an operator acts non-trivially only on a given tensor factor, we will suppress tensor products with the identity operator,
e.g.
writing
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{j-1}\mathds{1}_{k}\otimes H_{j}\otimes\bigotimes_{k^{\prime}=j+1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{k^{\prime}}
as simply
H_{j}
.

We recall the definition of Spectrum Broadcast Structure: a state has this structure with respect to the measurement basis
\{|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\}_{i}
if it is of the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{SBS}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{S}}}p_{i}\,|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(i)},

(15)

where
\rho_{k}^{(i)}
is the state on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
conditioned on the state
\left|\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right\rangle
on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
, and where
\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)}=0
for
i\neq j
. Since, for states
\sigma
and
\rho
,
\sigma\rho=0\iff F(\sigma,\rho)=0
, where the Uhlmann fidelity is defined as
F(\sigma,\rho)=\textnormal{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}\right)^{2}
, one can equivalently use the condition
F(\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)})=0
in the definition of Spectrum Broadcast Structure.

Appendix B
The impossibility of ideal projective measurements

In this appendix, we demonstrate the impossibility of performing an exact projective measurement according to the Measurement Equilibration Hypothesis. In particular, we prove that an initially uncorrelated state cannot equilibrate to one with Spectrum Broadcast Structure; this follows from a stronger result, described in the following. Firstly, we allow the possibility of initial correlations between the observers’ systems, but not with the system to be measured i.e.
\rho(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}
, where
\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}
is the initial state of the system and
\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}
the initial state of the environment. Secondly, we demand equilibration to a classical-quantum state
Devetak and Winter 2004
with respect to the measurement basis, with the appropriate probabilities,
i.e.
a state of the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{CQ}}=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}

(16)

for some
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}
on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{E}}
. Thirdly, we demand orthogonality of the conditional states
\{\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\}_{i}
,
i.e.

\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}=0
, or equivalently
F(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)},\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)})=0
, for
i\neq j
. These are weaker requirements than for Spectrum Broadcast Structure, as can be seen by comparing Eqs. (
16
) and (
15
). Thus when we prove that they cannot be simultaneously satisfied, the impossibility of equilibration to a state with Spectrum Broadcast Structure follows as a consequence.

The proof proceeds by first constraining the form of the Hamiltonian that would be necessary to equilibrate to a classical-quantum state, and then showing that the resulting fidelity between the conditional states
\{\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\}_{i}
is necessarily non-zero.

B.1
Constraining the Hamiltonian

Recalling that if a system equilibrates on average, it equilibrates to its infinite-time average
Gogolin and Eisert 2016
, our first step is to find which Hamiltonians can give rise to an infinite-time average of the required clasical-quantum form,
i.e.
Eq. (
16
). Since the infinite-time average is also given by the pinching map with respect to the Hamiltonian, Eq. (
14
), it will commute with the Hamiltonian:

[\rho_{\infty},H]=0
. Consequently, if
\rho_{\infty}=\rho_{\mathrm{CQ}}
, then
[\rho_{\mathrm{CQ}},H]=0
. We now seek the constraints that the latter equation imposes on the form of
H
. Letting
\{\left|\right.\!{\lambda}\!\left.\right\rangle\}
denote an arbitrary basis of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{E}}
, we can take the tensor product with the pointer basis on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{S}}
to write any Hamiltonian in the form

H=\sum_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|,

(17)

where
C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}
are the matrix elements of
H
in this basis. The relevant commutator is then

\displaystyle[H,\rho_{\mathrm{CQ}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}p_{l}\sum_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\left[|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\,,\,|{l}\rangle\!\langle{l}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(l)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{j}\sum_{i\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\left(C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}-C_{ji\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{j}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}0.

Now, since we seek a Hamiltonian which equilibrates to the SBS state for any initial system state,
i.e.
any
\{p_{i}\}
, this implies that

\sum_{i\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\left(C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}-C_{ji\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{j}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\right)=0\quad\forall j.

(19)

Noting that
\forall i,j
with
i\neq j
, the tensor factors
|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|
,
|{j}\rangle\!\langle{i}|
and
|{j}\rangle\!\langle{j}|
are all linearly independent, we then see that Eq. (
19
) implies the following

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\left(C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}-C_{ji\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{j}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall i,\,j,\,i\neq j,

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}C_{ii\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\left[|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|,\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\right]

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall i.

(21)

Again, due to the linear independence of
|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|
and
|{j}\rangle\!\langle{i}|
, we must have that
C_{ij\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}=0

\forall i\neq j,\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}
, in order for Eq. (
20
) to be satisfied.
For the infinite-time average state to have the structure of Eq. (
16
) (for an arbitrary initial system state), the Hamiltonian must then be of the form

H=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\text{, where}\;\;\;\;H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}:=\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}C_{ii\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|.

(22)

This generates unitary evolution of the form

\displaystyle e^{-iHt}=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t),

(23)

where
U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t):=\exp\left[-it\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}C_{ii\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|{\lambda}\rangle\!\langle{\lambda^{\prime}}|\right]
.
We can then write

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}=\Big\langle\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t)\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\sum_{j}|{j}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)\dagger}(t)\Big\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left\langle\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\left|\right.\!{j}\!\left.\right\rangle|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes\Big\langle U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t)\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)\dagger}(t)\Big\rangle_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}

(24)

which implies that

\displaystyle\Big\langle U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t)\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)\dagger}(t)\Big\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\,\begin{cases}0&i\neq j\\
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}&i=j.\end{cases}

(25)

Now, let us denote the eigenvalues of
H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}
by
\{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}\}_{n}
, with associated eigenvectors
\{\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle\}_{n}
. Writing
U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t)
and
U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}(t)
in the left hand side of Eq. (
25
) in the eigenbasis of
H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}
and
H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}
respectively, we have

\Big\langle U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}(t)\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)\dagger}(t)\Big\rangle_{\infty}=\sum_{n,m}\Big\langle e^{-i(\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)})t}\Big\rangle_{\infty}\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)}}\!\left.\right\rangle|{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)}}|,

(26)

and so in order to satisfy Eq. (
25
), we must have that

\Big\langle e^{-i(\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)})t}\Big\rangle_{\infty}\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)}}\!\left.\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0\quad\forall n,m\;\mathrm{where}\;n\neq m.

(27)

If the
\{H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\}_{i}
are each nondegenerate, and have no eigenvalues in common, i.e.
\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}\neq\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)}

\forall n,m
with
n\neq m
, then the above equation holds independently of the initial state
\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}
. Note that both of these conditions are true if and only if the total Hamiltonian is non-degenerate. In the case where there are values of
\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}
and
\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)}
that coincide,
i.e.
there are some degeneracies, then we must have
\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m}^{(j)}}\!\left.\right\rangle=0
for each pair of coinciding eigenvalues, requiring a initially rank-deficient state. Assuming either non-degeneracy of the total Hamiltonian, or the appropriate rank-deficiency of the initial environmental states in the case of degeneracies, we arrive at a state of the form

\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\quad\text{with}\quad\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}=\sum_{n}\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle|{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}|.

(28)

As an aside, we note that this state satisfies the faithfulness requirement in
Guryanova et al. 2020
(i.e.:
\sum_{i}\textnormal{Tr}[|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|\otimes\Pi_{i}\rho_{\infty}]=1
for orthogonal projectors
\Pi_{i}
with
[\Pi_{i},\rho_{E}^{(i)}]=0
).

We have so far made no demands on the microscopic structure on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{E}}
, aside from the potential rank-deficient initial states required to deal with degeneracies in
H
. A Hamiltonian of the form of Eq.
22
is therefore necessary for the formation of classical-quantum states in general.

B.2
Bounding the fidelity between conditional states

We now show that the orthogonality requirement,
i.e.

F(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)},\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)})=0
for
i\neq j
, cannot be satisfied exactly. In particular, we show that

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)},\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)})\geq\dfrac{1}{d_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}},

(29)

where
d_{\mathrm{E}}:=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{E}})
.

First, note that the conditional time-averaged state
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}
in Eq. (
28
) is a result of the pinching map with respect to the projectors
\{|{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}|\}_{n}
. Denoting the number of these projectors (
i.e.
the number of distinct eigenvalues of
H_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}
) by
d^{(i)}
, we can rewrite the pinching map as a mixed-unitary channel
Tomamichel 2016
;
Watrous 2018
as follows

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}=\sum_{n=1}^{d^{(i)}}|{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}|\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}|{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}|=\dfrac{1}{d^{(i)}}\sum_{y=1}^{d^{(i)}}U_{y}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{y}^{(i)\dagger}

(30)

where
U_{y}^{(i)}:=\sum_{n=1}^{d^{(i)}}e^{-i\tfrac{2\pi ny}{d^{(i)}}}|{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}}|
.
Noting that
U^{(i)}_{d^{(i)}}=\mathds{1}
, we write

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}=\dfrac{\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}}{d^{(i)}}+\dfrac{1}{d^{(i)}}\sum_{y=1}^{d^{(i)}-1}U_{y}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{y}^{(i)\dagger}

(31)

and thus

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}[(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)}]=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{d^{(i)}d^{(j)}}\Bigg\{\textnormal{Tr}\Big[\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}^{2}\Big]+\textnormal{Tr}\Big[\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\sum_{y=1}^{d^{(i)}-1}U_{y}^{(j)}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{y}^{(j)\dagger}\Big]+\textnormal{Tr}\Big[\sum_{y=1}^{d^{(i)}-1}U_{y}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{y}^{(i)\dagger}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\Big]

\displaystyle+\textnormal{Tr}\Big[\sum_{y=1}^{d^{(i)}-1}U_{y}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{y}^{(i)\dagger}\sum_{y^{\prime}=1}^{d^{(i)}-1}U_{y^{\prime}}^{(j)}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}U_{y^{\prime}}^{(j)\dagger}\Big]\Bigg\}.

(32)

Since that the trace of the product of two positive operators is itself positive, we can remove the second, third and fourth terms to obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)}\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)})\geq\dfrac{\textnormal{Tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}^{2})}{d^{(i)}d^{(j)}}.

(33)

Now, note that the fidelity between two states satisfies
F(\rho,\sigma)\geq\textnormal{Tr}(\rho\sigma)

Jozsa 1994
, allowing us to bound the Fidelity

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(i)},\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(j)})\geq\dfrac{\textnormal{Tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}^{2})}{d^{(i)}d^{(j)}}.

(34)

Thus, the fidelity between any two time-averaged states with the same initial state cannot be zero. It is therefore impossible to equilibrate to a classical-quantum state, Eq. (
16
), satisfying the orthogonality condition. As a consequence, it is impossible for an initially uncorrelated state to equilibrate to a state with Spectrum Broadcast Structure, as this is a stronger condition, as discussed above.

Finally, we note that since
d^{(i)}
is bounded by the Hilbert space dimension of the environment,
i.e.

d_{\mathrm{E}}\geq d^{(i)}
, Eq. (
29
) follows immediately from Eq. (
34
).

Appendix C
The standard measurement model does not equilibrate to a correlated state

In this section we consider the Hamiltonian used in the von-Neumann measurement scheme (or the “standard model of measurement”)
Busch and Lahti 1996
:

\displaystyle H=X_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes Y_{\mathrm{E}},

(35)

where
X_{\mathrm{S}}
is diagonal in the measurement basis,
X_{\mathrm{S}}=\sum_{i}x_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}
. By carefully controlling the time for which this Hamiltonian is applied, one can correlate the system S with the environment E in this basis. However, without temporal control – specifically in an equilibration paradigm – this Hamiltonian leads to an equilibrium state that does not feature any correlations between system and environment, as we now show.

The unitary operator generated by Eq. (
35
) is given by

\displaystyle e^{-\mathrm{i}Ht}=e^{-iX_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes Y_{\mathrm{E}}t}=e^{-i(\sum_{i}x_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes Y_{\mathrm{E}})t}=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes e^{-ix_{i}Y_{\mathrm{E}}t},

(36)

As in the previous section, we assume that system and environment are initially uncorrelated, such that the initials state is given by

\displaystyle\rho(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}\otimes\rho_{{}_{\mathrm{E}},0},

(37)

and we calculate the time evolved state of the total system

\displaystyle\rho(t)=e^{-iHt}\rho(0)e^{iHt}=e^{-iX_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes Y_{\mathrm{E}}t}\Big(\rho_{\mathrm{S,0}}\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\Big)e^{iX_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes Y_{\mathrm{E}}t}=\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(ij)}(t)

(38)

where
p_{ij}
are the elements of the initial system state in the pointer basis,
i.e.

\rho_{\mathrm{S},0}=\sum_{ij}^{d_{s}}p_{ij}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|
, and we have defined
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(ij)}(t):=e^{-ix_{i}Y_{\mathrm{E}}t}\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}e^{ix_{i}Y_{\mathrm{E}}t}
. Now, writing the spectral decomposition
Y_{\mathrm{E}}=\sum_{n}\varepsilon_{n}|{\varepsilon_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}}|
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{(ij)}(t)=\sum_{m,n}e^{-i(x_{i}\varepsilon_{m}-x_{j}\varepsilon_{n})t}\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{mn}|{\varepsilon_{m}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n}}|

(39)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{mn}:=\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\mathrm{E},0}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m}}\!\left.\right\rangle
. Examining this together with the total state in Eq. (
38
), we see that the time average of the total state is determined by

\displaystyle g_{mn}^{(ij)}(T):=\left\langle e^{-i(x_{i}\varepsilon_{m}-x_{j}\varepsilon_{n})t}\right\rangle_{T}=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{if}\;\;x_{i}\varepsilon_{m}=x_{j}\varepsilon_{n}\\
\dfrac{ie^{[i(x_{i}\varepsilon_{m}-x_{j}\varepsilon_{n})T]}-1}{(x_{i}\varepsilon_{m}-x_{j}\varepsilon_{n})T}&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

(40)

Assuming
{x_{i}\varepsilon_{m}\neq x_{j}\varepsilon_{n}\;\;\forall i,j,m,n}
we find an equilibrium state of the desired diagonal form in the pointer basis:

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{\mathrm{S}}\otimes\sum_{m}\rho_{\mathrm{E}}^{mm}|{\varepsilon_{m}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m}}|.

(41)

However, upon examination we see that the environment is independent of
i
, and this state features no correlations between system and environment. The environment therefore can not reveal any information about the system, and such von-Neumann-type Hamiltonians are incapable of (even approximately) equilibrating to states exhibiting objectivity.

Appendix D
Asymptotically approaching Spectrum Broadcast Structure

In this section we show an equilibrium state with Spectrum Broadcast Structure can be obtained approximately by coarse-graining the environment. The purpose of this is to show one example of how Spectrum Broadcast Structure can emerge. This should be considered as a first step; further research is needed to expand this framework into a realistic model of measurements.

As was shown in Appendix
B.1
, to obtain a classical-quantum equilibrium state (of which Spectrum Broadcast Structure is an example), one requires a conditional form of the Hamiltonian. In conjunction with the condition of Strong Independence this prompts us to consider a “star-shaped” Hamiltonian (see Fig.
1
in the main text) given by

H=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\otimes\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}H_{k}^{(i)}

(42)

where
H_{k}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)
and
c_{k}
are the different couplings between the system and the observer systems (e.g. representing distance-dependent couplings to spatially distributed observers). This generates the unitary evolution

e^{-iHt}=\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{(i)}(t),

(43)

where
U_{k}^{(i)}(t):=e^{-ic_{k}H_{k}^{(i)}t}
.

Assuming a separable initial state,
i.e.

\rho(0)=\rho_{S,0}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k,0}
, the state at time
t
can be written

\rho(t)=\sum_{i,j}\left\langle\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right|\rho_{S,0}\left|\right.\!{j}\!\left.\right\rangle|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|_{S}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(ij)}(t)

(44)

where

\rho_{k}^{(ij)}(t):=U_{k}^{(i)}(t)\rho_{k,0}U_{k}^{(j)}(t).

(45)

In order to calculate the equilibrium state
\rho_{\infty}
, we must therefore calculate the infinite-time average of
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(ij)}(t)
. First, letting
\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(i)}
denote the
n_{k}^{\text{th}}
energy eigenvalue of
H_{k}^{(i)}
, we define

\rho_{k}^{mn(ij)}:=\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{k,0}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}\!\left.\right\rangle.

(46)

Then the finite-time average of
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(ij)}(t)
over the interval
T
is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(ij)}(t)\right\rangle_{T}=\left\langle\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{m_{k}n_{k}}\exp\left[-ic_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}\right)t\right]\rho_{k}^{mn(ij)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}|\right\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\sum_{m_{1}n_{1}}\exp\left[-ic_{1}\left(\varepsilon_{m_{1}}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{n_{1}}^{(j)}\right)t\right]\rho_{1}^{mn(ij)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{1}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{1}}^{(j)}}|\right)\otimes\left(\sum_{m_{2}n_{2}}\exp\left[-ic_{2}\left(\varepsilon_{m_{2}}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{n_{2}}^{(j)}\right)t\right]\rho_{2}^{mn(ij)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{2}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{2}}^{(j)}}|\right)\otimes\ldots\right\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}n_{1}}\sum_{m_{2}n_{2}}\ldots\sum_{m_{N}n_{N}}\left\langle\exp\left[-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}\right)t\right]\right\rangle_{T}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{mn(ij)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}|

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}f_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}^{(ij)}(T)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{mn(ij)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}|

(47)

where we have used the notation

\displaystyle\sum_{\{a_{k}\}}=\sum_{a_{1}}\sum_{a_{2}}...\sum_{a_{N}},

(48)

and defined

f_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}^{(ij)}(T):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\,\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}\\
i\frac{\exp\left[-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}\right)T\right]-1}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}-\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}\right)T}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(49)

Noting that
\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}\}_{i,m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}}
are the eigenvalues of
H
, which can be seen from Eq. (
42
), we see that the cases where
f^{(ij)}_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}=1
for
i\neq j
and
\{m_{k}\}\neq\{n_{k}\}
correspond to degeneracies in
H
. Further note that such cases are particularly “fine-tuned”, as the coupling constants and energy eigenvalues have to conspire in order to yield this result. Thus, assuming that the total Hamiltonian is non-degenerate, the first case in Eq.
49
never occurs, and we have

\left\langle\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(ij)}(t)\right\rangle_{\infty}=0\qquad\text{ for }i\neq j.

(50)

Combining this with Equations
44
and
47
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\rho(t)\right\rangle_{\infty}=\sum_{i}\left\langle\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right|\rho_{S,0}\left|\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right\rangle|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\left\langle\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(ii)}(t)\right\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\sum_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}f_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}^{(ii)}(\infty)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k,0}^{mn(ii)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(i)}}|,

(51)

where
f^{(ii)}_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}(\infty):=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}f^{(ii)}_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}(T)
, and again
p_{i}:=\left\langle\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right|\rho_{S,0}\left|\right.\!{i}\!\left.\right\rangle
is the probability of outcome
i
for the projective measurement
\{|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\}_{i}
on the system. Due to the assumption of non-degeneracy of the total Hamiltonian, the conditional Hamiltonians
H_{k}^{(i)}
have to be non-degenerate as well, and consequently
\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}\neq\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(i)}

\forall\,m_{k}\neq n_{k}
, and thus only the
m_{k}=n_{k}
terms in
f^{(ii)}_{\{m_{k}\}\{n_{k}\}}(\infty)
are nonzero. Equation (
49
) then implies that

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\sum_{m_{1}\ldots m_{N}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{mm(ii)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{m_{k}}\rho_{k}^{mm(ii)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\rho_{k}^{(i)},

(52)

where

\rho_{k}^{(i)}:=\sum_{m_{k}}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|\,\rho_{k,0}\,|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|

(53)

is the initial state of observer system
k
(
i.e.

\rho_{k,0}
) subject to the pinching map with respect to
H_{k}^{(i)}
. Now let us gather together the
N
factors of the environment into
M
“macro-observers”, and let
N_{q}
denote the set of environment labels belonging to the
q^{\text{th}}
macro-observer, so that
{\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{k}=\bigotimes_{q=1}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{q}}
with
\mathcal{E}_{q}:=\bigotimes_{k\in N_{q}}\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Then

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}=\sum_{i}p_{i}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{i}|_{S}\bigotimes_{q=1}^{M}\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\qquad\text{ where }\qquad\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)}:=\bigotimes_{k\in N_{q}}\rho_{k}^{(i)},

(54)

We now show that the fidelity
F\left(\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)}\right)
between conditional states
\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)}
of each macro-observer
q
tends rapidly to 0 as the macro-observer size increases. To do so, we use the inequality

\displaystyle\sqrt{F(\sigma,\rho)}\leq\sum_{m}\sqrt{\textnormal{Tr}[E_{m}\rho]}\sqrt{\textnormal{Tr}[E_{m}\sigma]}

(55)

where the
\{E_{m}\}
are the elements of an arbitrary POVM, to bound
F\left(\rho_{k}^{(i)},\rho_{k}^{(j)}\right)
. Choosing the POVM elements to be projectors onto the eigenbasis of
H_{k}^{(i)}
,
i.e.

\{|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|\}_{m_{k}}
. Since
\rho_{k}^{(i)}=\sum_{m_{k}}\rho_{k}^{mm(ii)}|{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|
we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{F(\rho_{k}^{(i)},\rho_{k}^{(j)})}\leq\sum_{m_{k}}\sqrt{\rho_{k}^{mm(ii)}}\sqrt{\textnormal{Tr}[\sum_{n_{k}}\rho_{k}^{nn(jj)}|{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}\rangle\!\langle{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}|\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}|]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{k}}\sqrt{\rho_{k}^{mm(ii)}}\sqrt{\sum_{n_{k}}\rho_{k}^{nn(jj)}|\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}\rangle|^{2}}=\sum_{m_{k}}\sqrt{\sum_{n_{k}}\rho_{k}^{mm(ii)}\rho_{k}^{nn(jj)}|\langle{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}|{\varepsilon_{n_{k}}^{(j)}}\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{k}}\sqrt{\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle}.

(56)

In the cases where
H^{(i)}=H^{(j)}
or
\rho_{k,0}=\dfrac{1}{d_{k}}\mathds{1}
we see that the fidelity is bounded above by 1, as one would expect.

To see that
F\left(\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)}\right)
approaches zero exponentially as the macro-observers grow, we recall that
F(\bigotimes_{k}\sigma_{k},\bigotimes_{k}\rho_{k})=\prod_{k}F(\sigma,\rho)
. Combining this with the inequality in (
D
), we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{F(\bigotimes_{k\in N_{q}}\rho_{k}^{(i)},\bigotimes_{k\in N_{q}}\rho_{k}^{(j)})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in N_{q}}\sqrt{F(\rho_{k}^{(i)},\rho_{k}^{(j)})}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k\in N_{q}}\left(\sum_{m_{k}}\sqrt{\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{k\in N_{q}}\eta_{k}^{(ij)},

(57)

where we have defined
\eta_{k}^{(ij)}:=\sum_{m_{k}}\sqrt{\left\langle\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right|\rho_{k}^{(i)}\rho_{k}^{(j)}\left|\right.\!{\varepsilon_{m_{k}}^{(i)}}\!\left.\right\rangle}
. Further defining
N_{q}^{(ij)}:=\{k\in N_{q}^{(ij)}:\eta_{k}^{(ij)}<1\}
and
\eta_{max}^{(ij)}:=\max_{k\in N_{q}^{(ij)}}\eta_{k}^{(ij)}
. We then have

\displaystyle F(\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)})\leq\left(\eta_{max}^{(ij)}\right)^{2|N_{q}^{(ij)}|},

(58)

which can be simplified by defining
\gamma^{(ij)}_{q}=-2\ln(\eta_{\mathrm{max}}^{(ij)})
to finally obtain

\displaystyle F(\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)})\leq e^{-\gamma^{(ij)}_{q}|N_{q}^{(ij)}|}.

(59)

Assuming that the conditional Hamiltonians
H_{k}^{(i)}
and
H_{k}^{(j)}
have at least one distinct eigenvector, the upper bound in (
59
) exponentially tends to 0 for increasing macro-observer size. The equilibrium state therefore tends exponentially to one with Spectrum Broadcast Structure state as the size of the macro-observers (and thus the environment as a whole) increases. Further assuming that
H_{k}^{(i)}
and
H_{k}^{(j)}
have no eigenvectors in common, we find the simpler result stated in the main text,
i.e.

\displaystyle F(\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(i)},\tilde{\rho}_{q}^{(j)})\leq e^{-\gamma^{(ij)}_{q}|N_{q}|},

(60)

where
|N_{q}|
is the number of observer systems
k
collected into the macro-observer
q
.
