Abstract
In this paper, I formalize intelligence measurement in games by introducing mechanisms that assign a real number—interpreted as an intelligence score—to each player in a game. This score quantifies the ex-post strategic ability of the players based on empirically observable information, such as the actions of the players, the game’s outcome, strength of the players, and a reference oracle machine such as a chess-playing artificial intelligence system. Specifically, I introduce two main concepts: first, the Game Intelligence (GI) mechanism, which quantifies a player’s intelligence in a game by considering not only the game’s outcome but also the “mistakes” made during the game according to the reference machine’s intelligence. Second, I define gamingproofness , a practical and computational concept of strategyproofness. To illustrate the GI mechanism, I apply it to an extensive dataset comprising over a billion chess moves, including over a million moves made by top 20 grandmasters in history. Notably, Magnus Carlsen emerges with the highest GI score among all world championship games included in the dataset. In machine-vs-machine games, the well-known chess engine Stockfish comes out on top.

Introduction

In this paper, I formalize intelligence measurement in games by introducing mechanisms that assign a real number—interpreted as an intelligence score—to each player in a game. This score quantifies the ex-post strategic ability of the players based on empirically observable information, such as the actions of the players, the game’s outcome, strength of the players (if available), and a reference oracle machine such as a chess-playing artificial intelligence (AI) system.

I introduce a novel framework called
n
-person (
n\geq 1
) games with
partial knowledge
, where players possess limited knowledge about aspects of the game, including actions, outcomes, and other players. As an example, playing an
actual
game of chess represents a two-person zero-sum game with partial knowledge, even though the idealized version of chess is a game of perfect information.

In this framework, I define a novel and practicable mechanism, called the
Game Intelligence
(GI), that assigns an intelligence score to a player in a game by assessing not only the game’s outcome but also the “mistakes” made relative to a reference machine’s intelligence. Specifically, a machine outputs a probability distribution over the outcomes to each possible action a player might choose at any node. The GI mechanism is a function of the best moves predicted by the machine, the actual moves chosen by the player, moves of the other players, the game’s outcome, and the Elo ratings of the players (if applicable).

In the context of chess, the GI mechanism can be informally defined as:

GI=f(\text{Outcome}\,(\text{Moves}),\textit{Mistakes}\,(\text{Moves},\text{Machine},\text{Outcomes}),\text{Elo rating}),

where
f
is a real-valued function that

•

increases with an increase in the player’s own payoff,

•

decreases with an increase in the magnitude of mistakes,

•

increases with an increase in the opponent’s Elo rating.

Put differently, the higher the points a player gains, the smaller the magnitude of mistakes made in the game, and the higher the opponent’s Elo rating (if applicable), the greater the GI score.
Mistakes
is a real-valued function that measures the magnitude of a players’ mistakes in the game, which depends on the actions, a chess engine’s pawn-based evaluations, the results of engine self-play, the potential counterfactual outcomes, and their magnitude.

A chess AI system is usually referred to as an engine or a machine.

I illustrate the applicability of the GI mechanism by analyzing more than a billion moves in human-vs-human and machine-vs-machine chess games. Over a million of these moves are made by the world’s top chess grandmasters. The dataset includes all world chess championship games from 1898 onwards and numerous elite tournaments. Notably, Magnus Carlsen emerges with the highest GI score in a dataset of all world championship games (a subset of the dataset). However, when considering the larger dataset (both world championship and elite tournament games), Bobby Fischer, Magnus Carlsen, and Garry Kasparov stand out as having the highest GI scores, and the GI scores of these three grandmasters are statistically and practically indifferent.

From a theoretical perspective, I explore four main properties of machines and mechanisms. First, I begin by checking the dynamic consistency of machines, which means that a machine’s evaluation remains unchanged both before and after a move. While dynamic consistency is arguably a theoretically desirable property, it is practically impossible for a finite machine to possess this property in many real-world games. Nonetheless, dynamically consistent machines simplify the calculation process for various mechanisms, including the GI mechanism. Second, I check whether there always exist “maximally intelligent” plays in
n
-person games with partial knowledge, given a specific machine and mechanism. Though the answer is in the affirmative, this is largely a theoretical exercise since players typically do not have access to machines while they play games. Third, to assess different mechanisms, I introduce a simple concept of “consistency”: A mechanism is consistent when the intelligence score it attributes does not conflict with either the game’s outcome or the machine’s evaluation. I show that both the GI mechanism and its variants satisfy this consistency notion.

Fourth, I present the concept of “gamingproofness,” which is a property of a mechanism that is based on whether players can manipulate their intelligence scores from a subjective and computational point of view. A mechanism is called gamingproof if a human player cannot strategically play a suboptimal move to increase her expected intelligence score. I show that the expected GI score is gamingproof when the human player’s
estimation
of the machine’s deviation from her move is less accurate than her
own evaluation
of the deviation.
To put it more simply, this condition becomes clearer if one looks at when it fails to hold: if the human player either has access to a superior machine or possesses greater intelligence than the machine used to calculate her intelligence score, then the mechanism would not be gaming-proof.

In this section, I present a summary of the computational results applied to more than one billion chess moves, including over a million moves made by the top 20 chess grandmasters in history. The full dataset, summarized by Table
1

To provide some background information, chess is a two-person game in which the players (White and Black) alternate turns, making moves until one player wins or the game ends in a draw. Chess moves can be analyzed using powerful AI systems, usually called engines, some of which are much stronger than the best human players. The analysis in this paper mostly relies on evaluations by the leading chess AI, Stockfish.

The GI score is a numerical value. An average human GI score is set at 100, with a standard deviation of 15. A higher GI score indicates a higher intelligence score, while a lower GI indicates the opposite. The GI score captures the trade-off between following the engine’s top recommended move and deviating from it. A high GI score implies that the benefits of deviating from the engine’s suggestions outweigh the potential costs. Conversely, a low GI score suggests that the player takes too many risks.

GPL is simply the sum of a player’s expected losses in a game. GPL measures the difference between the perfect moves as determined by an engine and the actual moves made by the player. A lower GPL suggests that a player’s decisions are closer to the engine’s recommendations, which implies better move accuracy. Conversely, a higher GPL indicates greater deviation from the engine’s recommended moves. For example, a GPL of 1.0 suggests that the deviations from perfect moves are equivalent to the loss of a game, considering that a win is usually worth 1 point. In contrast, a GPL of 0 means that the player played a perfect game according to the engine. However, note that playing the top engine-recommended moves do not usually win games at the top level of chess. Since all elite players use engines to prepare, these top moves are commonly known, and they often lead to draws.

Related works

The present study focuses on actual play in games, instead of complete plans of action (i.e., strategies). This approach is inspired by what can be called as “Bonanno’s program” (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
and more recently
[
6
]
), which advocates a shift of emphasis towards describing actual play in games. While this paper draws inspiration from Bonanno’s program, its framework is distinct, designed specifically to evaluate behavior in partial knowledge settings.

Under partial knowledge games, players do not have complete knowledge/information about aspects of the game, including other players, strategies, game tree, and outcomes. Although this study does not explicitly aim to model unawareness in the way it has been modelled, it does allow for the possibility that players may be—and usually are—unaware of certain aforementioned aspects of the game. For example, a player may be unaware of certain moves in a chess game. In that respect, I was inspired by the burgeoning body of research on unawareness that began with
[
11
]
. Early studies on unawareness, such as those by
[
11
]
and
[
16
]
, were not specifically based on game theory (though the latter study considers a multi-agent setting). However, game theory has been increasingly used in this field. For a review of the literature on unawareness, see
[
32
]
.

In addition, there are game theoretical frameworks, some of which were in part inspired by chess, which focus on equilibrium concepts and/or strategies
[
22
,
15
,
21
]
. In contrast to these solution concepts and frameworks, the present study does not assume equilibrium, strategy, or rational play.

Backgammon, chess, and Go

I was inspired by the prior work of computer scientists and programmers who developed AI systems for playing backgammon, such as TD-Gammon
[
37
]
and GNU Backgammon, and chess such as Deep Blue
[
9
]
and Stockfish. Such engines allow players to evaluate the quality of their moves and their performance via metrics such as “error rate” in backgammon, and “average centipawn loss” and “total pawn loss”
[
1
]
in chess.

As is well-known, the main weakness of the pawn-based quality measures, such as the average centipawn loss metric, is that losing
c>0
centipawns in a winning position is in general very different from losing
c
centipawns in an equal position. However, traditional backgammon engines, some recent chess engines—such as Leela Chess Zero, which is inspired by AlphaZero
[
33
]
, and some Go engines such as AlphaGo Zero and its variants—adopt a different approach. These engines determine the best move in a position by maximizing the probability of winning a game, implicitly assuming that winning a game is worth 1 point and drawing is worth a half-win, thereby equating the expected score with the win probability. Unlike the earlier methods, GPL is a metric that takes into account the relative weights of winning, drawing, and losing, and it applies to both zero-sum games and non-zero-sum games, where ‘winning’ has no clear meaning.

A common limitation of the existing methods for analyzing games is that they are purely engine-based, where the evaluations of these engines assume that they play against themselves. Clearly, these metrics do not capture the ‘human’ aspect of games. The main contribution of the GI score as compared to the existing literature is that it takes into account almost all empirically observable information in a game. This includes the game’s outcomes, players’ Elo ratings (where applicable), the relative weights of potential rewards, as well as the machine evaluations.

Human and machine intelligence

For over a century, psychometric tests have been widely used in psychology to evaluate an individual’s cognitive abilities
[
35
]
. These assessments typically measure skills such as verbal, mathematical, and spatial reasoning. For an in-depth exploration of intelligence measurement in both human and artificial agents, readers are referred to
[
4
,
13
,
7
,
31
,
17
]
.

Measuring machine intelligence has a long history in AI research. The Turing Test
[
38
]
is a classic method for evaluating intelligence through natural language conversations, while the Universal Intelligence Measure
[
25
]
offers a formal definition of intelligence and a quantitative measure based on Kolmogorov complexity and AIXI
[
20
]
. Additionally, some alternatives have been proposed, such as the C-test and the anytime universal intelligence test, which aim to assess various aspects of intelligence
[
19
,
18
]
.

While aforementioned theoretical works define machine intelligence in terms of average performance (i.e., rewards) across various environments, my definition of game intelligence for a player encompasses not only rewards and opponents, but also the evaluation of the player’s actions by the reference machine’s intelligence. The novelty of the GI score is that it combines these elements in a unique way that has a clear interpretation, where players in a game can be ranked based on their GI score. Like many intelligence tests, GI can be applied to both humans and machines. Furthermore, I introduce the concept of gamingproofness for intelligence test mechanisms as a means of comparing and contrasting different types of mechanisms.

Strategyproofness and gamingproofness

In strategic games and mechanism design, a key question is how resistant the mechanism is to manipulation. The concept of gamingproofness proposed in this paper is related to the concept of strategyproofness studied in social choice, mechanism design, and sports. Selected works that define strategyproofness include
[
10
,
12
,
26
,
2
,
8
]
and
[
28
]
.

I have introduced gaming-proofness as a simple computational version of strategy-proofness for
n
-person games with partial knowledge. Gamingproofness differs from the well-known notions of strategyproofness in that it uses the notion of play rather than a strategy profile as an object of analysis. In essence, a mechanism is considered gamingproof if no player can increase their intelligence score by deliberately making a suboptimal move, as viewed from their own perspective. If the player has a greater computing power or intelligence than the machine used to measure the player’s intelligence, then the mechanism is typically not gamingproof. If a mechanism satisfies gaming-proofness, then the intelligence scores become more reliable. Without gaming-proofness, one might question whether the data truly represents a player’s skill, or if the player is simply trying to falsely increase their perceived intelligence.

Bounded rationality

As mentioned above, the primary aim of this paper is to assess player intelligence in
n
-person games using game outcomes and evaluations from machines. While players may have varying degrees of rationality, my focus is not on classifying them as rational, boundedly rational, or irrational. Instead, I concentrate on evaluating their intelligence given their information and cognitive constraints. Bounded rationality has been a topic of study for a long time, but the concept has no single definition
[
34
]
.
[
30
]
provides a comprehensive overview of the subject in his textbook. In this paper, the players could be considered “boundedly rational.” They have incomplete knowledge of important game aspects, such as the game tree and possible outcomes, and their limited foresight prevents them from performing full backward induction. Despite these limitations, some players in my setup can also be considered “rational” in some sense. They make the best decisions they can, given their information, environment, time, and computational constraints.

a^{*}_{i}(x)\in\arg\max_{a_{i}(x)\in A_{i}(x)}\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a_{i}(x))(r_{j}).

In simple words, a machine-optimal action at a node is the action with the highest evaluation according to machine
M_{i}
.

6

6

As noted earlier, notation
a_{i}(x)
refers to both the action taken at node
x
and the (possibly terminal) node that the action leads to.
A machine-optimal action is also known as the “best move” in a given position. A
human-optimal
action is simply an action that is machine-optimal with respect to
M^{h}_{i}
. In other words, a human-optimal action gives the highest subjective evaluation at a position with respect to human’s prior.

Partial knowledge

In this paper, I do not assume that players have common knowledge of each other’s priors, nor do I assume perfect recall. Specifically, given an information set
h\in H
, player
i=I(h)
knows that it is their turn to choose an action, but may not know all the elements of the information set
h
, nor the set of all available actions,
A_{i}(h)
. Players also do not necessarily know their own or other players’ future available actions, or the outcome of every play. As noted above, a player might not know the complete set of possible outcomes in a position.

Players’ knowledge about the game, including actions, outcomes, and other players, is captured by their subjective “evaluation.” Formally, player
i
’s
evaluation
of action
a_{i}(x)
at node
x
is defined as:

\displaystyle EV_{i}(a_{i}(x),M^{h}_{i})=\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M^{h}_{i}(a_{i}(x))(r_{j}).

In this framework, the question of whether players have perfect recall is not directly relevant. It is important to note that this does not negate the potential benefits a player with perfect recall may have within a game. However, the mechanisms in focus here operate from an ex-post perspective, similar to the post-game analysis typically done in chess.

4.2
Mechanisms

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all stochastic machines. Define
\mathcal{E}_{G}
be the set of all numerical (e.g., Elo) ratings of the players in a game
G
. In a game
G
, a (stochastic)
mechanism
is a profile
\mu
of functions whose
i
th component is defined as
\mu_{i}:\bar{A}\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{E}_{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For a given machine
M
, player
i
, play
\bar{a}
, and ratings of the players the function assigns a real number. The interpretation of
\mu_{i}
is that it is a measure of human intelligence in reference to a machine’s intelligence. Put differently,
\mu_{i}(\bar{a},M)
assigns an
intelligence score
(i.e., a real number) to player
i
for play
\bar{a}
given reference machine
M
.

Practicable mechanisms

The main purpose of this section is to define a practicable mechanism that assigns an intelligence score to plays. To facilitate this, I first introduce the concepts of “expected point loss” and “game point loss” (GPL).

Definition 2

(Expected point loss)
.

Let
M_{i}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))
be a machine’s evaluation of the best action for player
i
at position
x_{k}
and
M_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))
be machine’s evaluation of
i
’s actual action. Then, the
expected point loss
of action
a^{k}_{i}(x_{k})
is defined as

EPL_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))=\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j}).

The EPL of action
a^{k}_{i}(x_{k})
is the difference between the evaluation of the best move
a^{*}_{i}(x_{k})
for player
i
at node
x_{k}
and the evaluation of player
i
’s actual move
a^{k}_{i}(x_{k})
.

Definition 3

(Game point loss)
.

Let
\bar{a}\in\bar{A}
be a play and
a^{j}_{i}
be player
i
’s action at position
x_{k}
in play
\bar{a}
, where
\bar{a}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l_{i}}_{i})
for some
l_{i}
. Then, player
i
’s
game point loss
(GPL) is defined as,

GPL_{i}(\bar{a})=\sum_{k=1}^{l_{i}}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl).

The GPL of a player is the sum of the expected point losses of all their moves in a play
\bar{a}
. From these two metrics, I next define the “game intelligence” mechanism.

Definition 4

(Game Intelligence (raw))
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\in\boldsymbol{\bar{A}}
be a full play. Then, the raw
game intelligence
(GI) score of
\bar{a}
is defined as

GI^{\text{raw}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}).

The raw GI of a full play
v
is defined as the difference between a player’s actual game point, denoted as
R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})
, and their GPL. If (Elo) rating of the opponent is available, then the raw GI score is weighted according to this rating. In any case, the raw GI score is converted to the GI score via standardization, as described in section
6.2
.

Next, I introduce the concept of the expected Game Intelligence (EGI) score for plays.

Definition 5

(Expected Game Intelligence)
.

Let
\bar{a}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l}_{i})
be player
i
’s play where
a^{l}_{i}
is human-optimal at node
x_{l}
. Then, the
expected game intelligence
(EGI) of
\bar{a}_{i}
is defined as

EGI_{i}(\bar{a}_{i})=EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-GPL_{i}(\bar{a}_{i})

=\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M^{h}_{i}(a^{l}_{i}(x_{l}))(r_{j})-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl).

The intuition behind the EGI score is that it replaces the actual outcome
R_{i}(\bar{a})
with the expected outcome of the game, according to player
i
’s prior
M^{h}_{i}(a^{l}_{i}(x_{l}))
.

5
Theoretical results

5.1
Maximal intelligence

An important theoretical question is whether there exists a play with the “highest” intelligence score.

Definition 6

(Maximal intelligence score)
.

A full play
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
is called
maximally intelligent
or said to have a
maximal intelligence score
for machine
M
with respect to mechanism
\mu
if for every player
i
and for every alternative play
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\setminus\hat{a}_{i}(x_{k})
that deviates from action
a^{k}_{i}
in
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
,

\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}},M)\geq\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\setminus\hat{a}_{i}(x_{k}),M).

As the name suggests, a maximally intelligent play maximizes the intelligence score of each player.

Proposition 1

(Existence of maximally intelligent play)
.

In every
n
-person game with partial knowledge, for every mechanism
\mu
and every machine
M
, there exists a full play
\bar{a}\in\bar{A}
that has the maximal intelligence score for machine
M
with respect to mechanism
\mu
.

Proof.

The proof strategy is to apply a standard backward induction argument. Let
G
be a finite
n
-person game with partial knowledge. I prove that for every mechanism
\mu
, for every machine
M
, there exists a maximally intelligent full play
\bar{a}
in this game.

Step 1: First, because the game is finite, every full play in the game has a last (non-terminal) node. Since there are no subsequent non-terminal nodes, there exists a “maximally intelligent” action that has the highest intelligence score according to mechanism
\mu
at every such node.

Step 2: Consider a second-to-last (non-terminal) node of the game. By step 1, note that for every action taken in the current node, the game has a maximally intelligent action in the next node. I can now use backward induction logic to show that the second-to-last node has a maximally intelligent action. Step 2a: Consider each possible action that a player can take in the second-to-last node, and assume that every subsequent player will play their maximally intelligent action in the next node. Step 2b: Use the maximally intelligent actions from the subsequent nodes to determine the payoff that each player would receive if they took a particular action in the second-to-last node.

Repeat step 2 for each predecessor node of the game, using the maximally intelligent actions from the subsequent nodes to determine the maximally intelligent actions for each node. By the end of this finite process, the full play that consists of maximally intelligent actions at every node starting from the root must be maximally intelligent. Therefore, every
n
-person game with partial knowledge has a maximally intelligent full play. ∎

While it is theoretically possible to find a maximally intelligent full play in games, this approach is not in general practical for human vs. human real-world games. This is because the existence result assumes the knowledge of the machine using which the intelligence score is calculated. An access to such a machine is not generally possible in real-world games.

5.2
Dynamic consistency of machines

A common theoretical question I receive is whether a machine’s evaluations should be “dynamically consistent.”

Definition 7
.

A machine
M
is called
dynamically consistent
in game
G
if for every player
i
, for every node
x_{k}\in X
, and every action
a_{i}(x_{k})
that leads to node
x_{k+1}
,
M_{i}(a_{i}(x_{k}))=M_{i}(x_{k+1})
. A machine
M
is called
dynamically consistent
if it is dynamically consistent in every game.

Dynamic consistency requires that a machine’s evaluation of any action should not change after the action has been played. While dynamic consistency sounds like a desirable theoretical property, in my opinion, it is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for a “good” machine. For instance, a machine could mistakenly evaluate each position in a chess game as a win for White and still be dynamically consistent. On the other hand, Stockfish is an example of a machine that achieves superhuman performance despite not being dynamically consistent. That being said, if a machine were dynamically consistent, then the machine’s evaluations of the initial position and the final position would suffice to calculate the GI score as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 2

(GI and dynamic consistency)
.

Let
G
be a sequential two-person constants-sum game with partial knowledge.

1.

For every full play
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\in\boldsymbol{\bar{A}}
, where
l_{1}=l_{2}
and
x_{2l_{1}-1}
is the terminal node,

GI_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=GI_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})+R_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-R_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(x_{0})(r_{j})+\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(x_{2l_{1}-1})(r_{j})

(5.1)

2.

For every play
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\in\bar{A}
, where
l_{1}\neq l_{2}
and
x_{l_{1}+l_{2}-1}
is the terminal node,

GI_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=GI_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})+2R_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(x_{0})(r_{j})+\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{2}(x_{l_{1}+l_{2}})(r_{j})

(5.2)

Proof.

Recall that
GI_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})
. Assume that
l_{1}=l_{2}
, and let
x_{2l_{1}-1}
be the terminal node. Then,

GI_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\sum_{k=1}^{l_{1}}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl).

and

GI_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\sum_{k=1}^{l_{2}}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{2}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{2}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl).

Thus,

GI_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=GI_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})+R_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-R_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})

+\sum_{k=1}^{l_{2}}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{2}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{2}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl)

-\sum_{k=1}^{l_{1}}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{1}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl).

Dynamic consistency of
M
and constant-sum property of
G

imply that for every
i\neq i^{\prime}
,

\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})+\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i^{\prime}}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k+1}))(r_{j})=R_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})+R_{2}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}).

Thus,
GI_{1}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})
simplifies to Equation
5.1
. Equation
5.2
can be obtained analogously.
∎

Dynamic consistency, which requires that a machine’s evaluation of any action should not change after the action has been played, does not seem to be a desirable property in the real world. In fact, any machine with finitely limited computing power must be dynamically inconsistent, since it is always possible to design a game whose solution requires a computing power greater than that of the machine. If the machine were dynamically consistent in that game, then its evaluation at the root of the game would be “arbitrary.”

5.3
Consistency of mechanisms

As mentioned above, it may be an unrealistic expectation for machines to be dynamically consistent in real-world settings. However, it is more feasible to require mechanisms to maintain a level of “consistency” that aligns with the outcomes of plays and machine evaluations.

In what follows, I provide a formal definition of consistency in this framework.

Definition 8

(Consistency)
.

Let
G
be a game. A mechanism
\mu
is called
consistent
if for every player
i
and every pair of plays
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}
in
\boldsymbol{\bar{A}}
the following three conditions are satisfied.

1.

R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})>R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
and
GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})<GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
implies that
\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})>\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.

2.

Assume
GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.
R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
if and only if
\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.

3.

Assume
R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.
GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\leq GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
if and only if
\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.

To put it simply, a mechanism is consistent if the value it assigns to a full play contradicts neither the outcome of the full play nor the GPL evaluation of the machine that is used to calculate the value of the mechanism.

The three conditions in the definition of consistency specify how the mechanism should behave in different situations. For instance, if a play results in worse evaluation for both the human and the machine compared to another play, then the intelligence score of the latter must be greater than the intelligence score of the former according to the mechanism. Conditions (2) and (3) apply a comparative statics logic: if one of the evaluations (either the human evaluation or the machine evaluation) is held fixed, then the mechanism should be consistent with the other evaluation.

There is only one case where the concept of consistency does not apply. This is when there are two plays,
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
leads to a strictly worse evaluation for the machine than
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}
leads to a strictly worse evaluation for the human than
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
. In this case, consistency does not place any restrictions on the intelligence score of these plays. The reason is that sometimes a move that is optimal for a human may not be optimal for a machine, and vice versa. I next show that GI mechanism is consistent.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mu
be the GI mechanism. Then, any mechanism of the form
\alpha R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\beta GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})+\delta
, where
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, is consistent.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}
in
\boldsymbol{\bar{A}}
be a pair of plays. First, assume that
R(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq R(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
and
GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\leq GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
. It implies that

R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})-GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}).

Thus,
GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.

Second, assume that
GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
. Then,
GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
if and only if

R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})+GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}).

=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}}).

Thus,
GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
if and only if
R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
.

Third, assume that
R(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
. Then,

GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
if and only if

GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})-GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}).

Therefore,
GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})\geq GI(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})
if and only if
GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{b}})\geq GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})
.

For analogous reasons as above, the mechanism given by
\alpha R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\beta GPL_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})+\delta
, where
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
is a consistent mechanism.
∎

While the metrics such as GPL is useful and informative, it is easy to see that purely engine-based metrics are not consistent in part because they do not depend on the outcome of a game. Analogously, the intelligence measures discussed in section
3
would not be consistent.

5.4
Gamingproofness

A common theoretical question in mechanism design is to consider whether players can manipulate the mechanisms. To address this potential concern and capture a simple and computational notion of strategyproofness, I next introduce the concept of “gamingproofness.”

Let
\hat{M}^{h}_{i}:X\rightarrow\Delta R
denote a
hypothetical machine
where
\hat{M}^{h}_{i}(x)
is the probability distribution that human player
i
believes machine
M_{i}
assigns at node
x
.

Definition 9

(Gamingproofness)
.

Let
\bar{a}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l-1}_{i},a^{l}_{i})
be player
i
’s play in which
a^{l}_{i}
is a human-optimal action at node
x_{l}
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l-1}_{i},a^{l^{\prime}}_{i})
be another play such that
a^{l^{\prime}}_{i}\neq a^{l}_{i}
. A mechanism
\mu
in a game
G
is called
gamingproof
for player
i
at node
x_{l}
if for every
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i}
,
\mu_{i}(\bar{a}_{i},\hat{M}^{h}_{i})\geq\mu_{i}(\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i},\hat{M}^{h}_{i})
. A mechanism
\mu
is called
gamingproof
if it is gamingproof for every player
i
at every node
x
such that
I(x)=i
.

In simple words, a gamingproof mechanism ensures that no player can increase their intelligence score by intentionally making a suboptimal move, as viewed from their own perspective.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\bar{a}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l-1}_{i},a^{l}_{i})
be player
i
’s play in which
a^{l}_{i}
is a human-optimal action at node
x_{l}
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l-1}_{i},a^{l^{\prime}}_{i})
be another play where
a^{l^{\prime}}_{i}\neq a^{l}_{i}
. EGI mechanism is gamingproof for player
i
at node
x_{l}
, if, for every
a^{l^{\prime}}_{i}
, the following inequality holds:

EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},M^{h}_{i})\geq EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})-EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},\hat{M}_{i}).

Proof.

By definition, GI mechanism is gamingproof for player
i
at node
x_{l}
if
EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i}(x_{l}),M^{h}_{i})\geq EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i}(x_{l}),M^{h}_{i})
implies that
EGI(\bar{a}_{i})\geq EGI(\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i})
. The latter inequality implies that

EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-GPL_{i}(\bar{a}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})\geq EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-GPL_{i}(\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})

or, more explicitly,

\displaystyle EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\left(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}\hat{M}_{i}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}\hat{M}_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-\sum_{k=1}^{l^{\prime}}\left(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}\hat{M}_{i}(a^{*}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}\hat{M}_{i}(a^{k}_{i}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\right).

Observe that GPL of player
i
before node
x_{l}
is the same in the calculation of
EGI(\bar{a}_{i})
and
EGI(\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i})
. Therefore, the inequality can be written as

EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-EV_{i}(a^{*}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})+EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})\geq EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-EV_{i}(a^{*}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})+EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},\hat{M}_{i}).

This simplifies to

EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},M^{h}_{i})-EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},M^{h}_{i})\geq EV_{i}(a^{l^{\prime}}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})-EV_{i}(a^{l}_{i},\hat{M}_{i}),

as desired.
∎

The intuition behind Proposition
4
is based on the relationship between the human player’s evaluation of a particular game position and their belief of the machine’s evaluation. Specifically, the human player’s evaluation of the machine’s deviation from a human-optimal move must be worse than or equal to their own evaluation of the deviation.

In other words, a human player cannot strategically play a suboptimal move to increase their intelligence score, provided that their own evaluation of the position is superior to what they perceive as the machine’s evaluation. This premise holds true only if the human’s judgment is more accurate than their own approximation of the machine’s judgment.

6
The dataset, computational methods, and results

As shown by Table
1
, the full dataset consists of about 1.5 billion chess moves played by top grandmasters, regular players, and machines in engine-vs-engine competitions. Overall, I estimate that the total execution time for completing all computational tasks took about 700 hours on a computer equipped with an Intel
®
Core
TM
i7-6700 CPU, 4 cores/8 threads. This estimate includes all tasks such as data collection and cleaning, annotating games with raw (i.e., centipawn) Stockfish evaluations, and computing the relevant metrics for every move and game.

6.1
World championships and elite tournaments

This dataset consists of approximately 1.2 million individual moves played in approximately 15,000 games by world-class chess grandmasters. The games were downloaded in Portable Game Notation (PGN) format from pgnmentor.com. The dataset includes games from world championships and many elite tournaments played by top grandmasters.

I used these PGNs to obtain Stockfish raw (centipawn) evaluations from the Lichess Masters Database. Although this database is not publicly available to download, Lichess.org allows users to search opening positions to identify games between top players. The database includes many games that have already been annotated with Stockfish evaluations. I wrote a script to isolate the first 20 moves of each game, then used the Lichess Masters Database API to match these sequences with existing records. I downloaded and separated those PGN files that were (at least partially) annotated. I completed the missing evaluations by running them through Stockfish 16 with a depth setting of 20.

I have evaluated a subset of roughly 18% of these games by Stockfish 16—the state-of-the-art chess analysis engine as of July 2023—on a personal computer. This process involved annotating every position in these games using the Stockfish centipawn evaluations. To calculate the GPL of the players in these games, I used the empirical win-draw-loss percentages as a function of centipawn loss. These percentages were derived from a dataset where Stockfish played against itself.

I compared my Stockfish evaluations with Lichess Stockfish evaluations of around 87,000 positions. The results closely mirrored those reported in section
2
. To conserve computational resources, I continued using Lichess’s Stockfish evaluations in the dataset, with the exception of those games which had missing evaluations or the games of Bobby Fischer, most of which were not part of the Lichess database.

Cleaning the data involved separating non-classical games, such as rapid, blitz, online, blindfold, simul, and exhibition matches, from the classical ones. Additionally, games played by Fischer, Carlsen, and Kasparov in which the Elo rating was below 2500 were omitted, as this rating is generally accepted as the threshold for grandmaster-level play. I expanded the dataset by downloading games from almost all major tournaments and included the games which had at least one top-20 player (or Karpov) according to all-time Elo rankings.

Interestingly, there were very few Bobby Fischer games in the Masters Database. Thus, I analyzed as many of his classical games as possible using Stockfish 16 with a depth of 20. The games of Fischer and the corrected games are the only entries in my dataset that do not use Lichess’s Stockfish evaluations.

Once the dataset grew to more than a million moves, I decided I had reached a logical stopping point. This was to avoid overusing the computational resources of my university as well as the resources provided by Lichess, which is funded by user donations.

6.2
Regular players

To standardize the raw GI scores, I first computed the sample average and standard deviation in a large dataset of regular players, downloaded from Lichess Open Database, which consists of all games except bullet games played by their users (slightly more than one million players).
8
8

8

While official world championships exist for classical, rapid, and blitz chess, bullet chess (including hyper-bullet) does not have such recognition. Therefore, bullet games were excluded from this dataset.
This dataset consists of two parts, the games played in August 2023 (part 1) and in September 2023 (part 2). Table
6
presents the average raw GI scores, GPLs, and their standard deviations. To check the robustness of these numbers, I have computed the same statistics for each part separately. As the table shows, both the averages and the standard deviations are remarkably close in each part.

Before standardizing the raw GI scores, I weighted them using the Elo ratings of the opponents since the dataset includes players from all levels. The weight decreases as the Elo rating of the opponent decreases, and vice versa. To provide some background, the Elo rating system is used to rank players based on their past performance. Given two players with Elo ratings
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
, the expected score for player
i
, represented by
ES
is computed using the following formula:

ES(E_{i},E_{j})=\frac{1}{1+10^{(E_{j}-E_{i})/400}}.

Let
GI^{\prime\text{raw}}
be the player’s raw GI score before weighting, and
E_{j}
be the player’s opponent. Then, the raw GI score
GI^{\text{raw}}
is calculated as follows.

\displaystyle GI^{\text{raw}}=GI^{\prime\text{raw}}-(1-2\times ES(E_{j},2800))\penalty\ |GI^{\prime\text{raw}}|,

where
|\cdot|
is the absolute value operator. The intuition behind this formula is as follows: If the player’s opponent has an Elo of
E_{j}=2800
, then the initial raw GI score remains the same, as this rating corresponds to the top 99 percentile in Lichess. As the rating of the opponent,
E_{j}
, decreases so will
ES(E_{j},2800)
, hence a lower raw GI score will be assigned for winning against weaker players.

Next, to adjust the mean to 100 and the standard deviation to 15, I use the modified Z-score formula as follows.

z_{i}=15\times\frac{(x_{i}-\mu)}{\sigma}+100.

In this formula,
x_{i}
represents individual data points. The multiplication by 15 and the addition of 100 are adjustments to scale and shift the data to the desired mean and standard deviation.

The overall transformation can be expressed in the form of a linear function
z_{i}=b+a\times x_{i}
. The parameters
a
and
b
in this context are determined as follows:
a=\frac{15}{\sigma}
and
b=-\frac{15\mu}{\sigma}+100
. These values of
a
and
b
are then used to transform each data point
x_{i}
to the corresponding
z_{i}
, ensuring the normalized dataset has a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15:

GI=-\frac{15\mu}{\sigma}+100+\frac{15}{\sigma}GI^{\text{raw}}
.

The combined dataset is processed to calculate player-specific statistics. I considered only the players with at least 50 games and an average GPL of at least 0, which rules out the ‘superhuman’ players who managed to play at least as good as Stockfish in 50 games, which is practically impossible for a human, which reduced dataset to 797,410,344 moves in total. Among these players, the average raw GI score is -3.1027 and the average GPL is 0.8085, where both values are rounded. As a result, I obtain the following linear equation that transforms raw GI score to the GI score:

\displaystyle GI=157.57+18.55\times GI^{\text{raw}}.

As a result, the mean GI score will be 100, and its standard deviation will be 15, which is in line with how the Intelligence Quotient (IQ) is presented. Hence, I refer to the GI score in this context as Chess Intelligence or, informally, the Chess IQ.

6.3
Engine-vs-engine games

As summarized in Table
3
, this dataset includes over 200 million moves played by engines under CCRL 40/15 testing conditions. These conditions, formulated by the Computer Chess Rating Lists (CCRL) organization, ensure a level playing field for computer chess analysis. Games are played with a time limit of 40 moves in 15 minutes on an Intel i7-4770k processor. To further promote fairness, the use of specialized engine opening books is prohibited, and a common opening book limited to a depth of 12 moves is used.

Additionally, all the games are played with the ponder feature deactivated, which means that each engine ‘thinks’ only on its turn. This implies that the standard way to compute the GPL (as well as traditional metrics such as average centipawn loss) would produce meaningless results because the evaluations of two different machines will obviously be very different. To define the GI score and the GPL in this setting, I use the fact that an engine always chooses the top move according to its own evaluation. Therefore, the expected loss of an engine’s move (say, Stockfish) can be measured by the evaluations of the opponent engine’s (say, Komodo) preceding move and the move that follow’s Stockfish’s move. Essentially, each engine measures the GPL of its opponent. Definition
4
is updated for this framework as follows.

Definition 10

(Game Intelligence in machine-vs-machine games)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\in\boldsymbol{\bar{A}}
be a full play,
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
i^{\prime}\in\{1,2\}
with
i\neq i^{\prime}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}\in\boldsymbol{\bar{A}}
be a full play and
a^{j}_{i}
be player
i
’s action at position
x_{k}
in full play
\boldsymbol{\bar{a}}
, where
\bar{a}_{i}=(a^{1}_{i},a^{2}_{i},...,a^{l_{i}}_{i})
for some
l_{i}
.
Then, the
game intelligence
(GI) of
\bar{a}
is defined as

GI_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})=R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\bar{a}})-\sum_{k=1}^{l_{i}}\Bigl(\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i^{\prime}}(a^{k+1}_{i^{\prime}}(x_{k+1}))(r_{j})-\sum^{m}_{j=1}r_{j}M_{i^{\prime}}(a^{k}_{i^{\prime}}(x_{k}))(r_{j})\Bigl).

The main difference between the standard GI score and this definition is that in the former, the expected point loss of a move is measured by a counterfactual move that an engine would make in the same position. By contrast, in the above definition, the expected loss of an engine move is measured by the difference in evaluations by the opponent engine’s move just before and right after the original engine’s move. Essentially, the intelligence of each machine is measured with the intelligence of the other one.

Table
8
and Table
9
show the GI rankings of the engines that played at least 1000 games under CCRL conditions. The top five engines are Stockfish, Houdini, Komodo, Rybka, and Torch, respectively. These engines each have a negative GPL, which indicates that they perform better than the engines evaluating them. Note that in human-vs-human games, GPLs are all positive, as Stockfish is significantly stronger than humans.

7
Concluding remarks

Intelligence is a complex construct that cannot be perfectly captured by the outcome of a single game, even in highly skill-based games like chess. If a player receives a large reward in a game, this does not necessarily indicate high intelligence, as chance may significantly affect the outcome. For instance, in backgammon, a player may make a poor move but still have a positive probability of winning the game. Furthermore, “machine-optimal” moves may not always be optimal from a human perspective, and conversely, “human-optimal” moves may not be machine-optimal. For example, top chess players occasionally play “suboptimal” moves to increase their probability of winning a game. Thus, to assess intelligence in games, one must take into account both human- and machine-based (counterfactual) decisions.

The Game Intelligence (GI) is a practical mechanism that has broad applicability and can be used to assign intelligence score to players and teams across a wide range of
n
-person games, including sports, board games, and video games, as well as individual decision problems. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when implementing the GI mechanism in sports or games where AI systems have not yet achieved a level of intelligence sufficient for measuring human intelligence.

The GI mechanism can also be used to assign intelligence scores to AI systems themselves. A potential future research direction is to use GI scores to train AI agents, as they provide more refined feedback than the simple binary outcome of a game.
