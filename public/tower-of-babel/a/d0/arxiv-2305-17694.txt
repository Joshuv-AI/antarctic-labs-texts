Geometric considerations on planetary surface temperatures Preprint: APS/123-QED
Sabin Roman

Abstract
We propose a formula for computing the average planetary surface temperatures based solely on the solar irradiance and the bond albedo. The formula is empirically derived from data on Earth, Venus and Titan, and a model is proposed to justify it. We introduce the concept of planetary inner albedo, as a complement to the usual bond albedo. A geometric proof is given for the main finding of the paper, which can be summarized as follows: the ratio of the inner to outer albedo is a constant, related to the universal parabolic constant. Furthermore, we extend the surface temperature formula to gas giants, giving the temperature at which condensates (e.g., of ammonia) start forming within their atmosphere, particularly for Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus. Based on model complexity, applicability and accuracy, the heating mechanism via atmospheric reflectivity (a mirror effect) performs much better than the alternatives. Responses to reviewers are included at the end.



I
Introduction

Determining the average surface temperature of Earth-like planets (rocky, with a significant atmosphere of pressure greater than
10
kPa) is a fundamental question in planetary science, and has important implications in particular for Earth’s biosphere
(
Easterling et al. 2000
;
Duffy et al. 2021
)
. Black body estimates markedly underestimate the temperature, not accounting for the full effect of the atmosphere. Calculation of surface temperatures based on the ideal gas law or the lapse rate can give accurate estimates, but do not account for the energy balance of incoming and outgoing radiation
(
Reinhart 2018
)
. Given the complexity of the atmospheric system, finding universal regularities can seem unlikely. However, finding such regularities is essential to our understanding, one example being the linear decrease of temperature with altitude in the troposphere
(
Wallace and Hobbs 2006
)
.

In this work we highlight a linear relationship with universal applicability. Specifically, we plot the ratio of net solar irradiance to emitted radiation for Titan, Earth and Venus versus their bond albedo. Surprisingly, a linear fit matches the data very well, see Fig.
1
. This implies that the slope is a constant independent of the features of the celestial bodies, leading us to propose a geometric rationale for it, which we explore in Section 2. This provides a way to account for incoming solar radiation and atmospheric features in determining the surface temperature. It is striking that the albedo provides sufficient information in this regard. In Section 3, we discuss how applying the formula to gas giants seems to determine the temperature at which condensates form in their atmosphere. Condensation forms key problem related to atmospheric dynamics of the gas giants and the formula indicates where solar radiation plays the dominant role in determining the temperature versus other process such as convection. We have included Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus in Fig.
1
and the linear fit uses the data for rocky bodies in Table
1
.

We can interpret Fig.
1
as follows. Consider the a planet with albedo
\alpha
and incoming radiation
I
. The amount of radiation passing (net irradiance) is
I(1-\alpha)
. If the planet radiates at
I_{p}
with an inner albedo
\beta
(fraction of radiation reflected back to the surface), then amount of radiation getting through is
I_{p}(1-\beta)
. Conservation of energy requires
I(1-\alpha)=I_{p}(1-\beta)
, and thus:

\frac{I(1-\alpha)}{I_{p}}=1-r\alpha

(1)

where we define
r=\beta/\alpha
as the slope of the linear fit in Fig.
1
. We find that
r\simeq 1.298
.

The surface radiant exitance is calculated using the Stefan-Boltzmann law, namely
I_{p}=\sigma T_{p}^{4}
where
T_{p}
is the surface temperature. Thus, equation (
1
) can used to estimate the surface temperature for a given celestial body, provided it is rocky, with an appreciable atmosphere. If
\beta=0
, this ignores any atmospheric effect and we obtain the black body temperature, which underestimates measured values. If
\beta=\alpha
this accounts for inner reflection but does not consider radiation coming from a nearby source. The proximity of the radiation source leads to a geometric enhancement of the inner albedo, which we detail in the next section. The temperature estimates for different celestial bodies are listed in Table
1
and are in a remarkably good agreement with measurements.

The universality of (
1

r
is geometric in nature, a possibility which we explore in the next section.

II
Model of inner albedo

Given the large differences in atmospheric composition, surface features and other characteristics between Venus, Earth and Titan, the fact that (
1
) holds empirically so well suggests that
r
is geometric in nature. In this section we propose a model to account for equation (
1
), which can justify the universal nature of
r
.

Consider a reflective sphere
S
with a rough surface such that lights scatters in the outward tangent half-space at every point. We make two main simplifying assumptions, which will be discussed at the end of the section. The first assumption is that we restrict our analysis to a two dimensional cross-section made by a plane through the centre of
S
, see Fig.
2
. The cross-sectional circle is surrounded by a set
M
of circular segments that are transparent to incoming light but reflective on their inner surface. Furthermore, the mirrors
M
are at a close distance from the surface relative to the radius of the sphere
S
. The incoming light has irradiance
I_{0}
.

We are interested in how the light reflects from the inner surface of
M
, and we look at a segment
AB
of
M
in Fig.
3
. The light reflects off
M
at an intensity
I_{1}
and then reflect off the surface
S
at an intensity
I_{2}
. The second key assumption is that when the light reflects off
S
it does so with a wavefront well-approximated by the parabola
P
with focus
F
from Fig.
3
.

Let
l_{1}
be the length of the circle segment
AB
and
L_{1}
be the length of the parabolic segment
EG
. The following equality holds between the incoming light
I_{0}+I_{1}
(radiation passing through
M
plus the reflected) and reflected off the surface with intensity
I_{2}
:

l_{1}(I_{1}+I_{0})=L_{1}I_{2}

(2)

This express conservation of energy. In addition, we expect
I_{0}=I_{2}
because
S
cannot emit more than it receives. Hence:

I_{1}=\left(\frac{L_{1}}{l_{1}}-1\right)I_{0}

(3)

From the geometry in Fig.
2
we can deduce the equality
FG\simeq AB\simeq l_{1}
, see Appendix A. The ratio of the parabolic segment
EG
of length
L_{1}
to the linear segment
FG\simeq l_{1}
is given by the universal parabolic constant
p=\log{(1+\sqrt{2})}+\sqrt{2}\simeq 2.2956
. Then, the ratio between the reflected light from
M
and the outgoing light from
S
is:

\frac{I_{1}\sum_{i}l_{i}}{I_{0}C}=(p-1)\alpha

(4)

where
\sum_{i}l_{i}
is the summed lengths of all the segments of
M
, which make up a fraction
\alpha
of the circumference
C
of the circle. Thus, we can consider the inner albedo of
M
to be
\beta=r\alpha
where
r=p-1\simeq 1.2956
. This theoretical value gives a close match of temperature predictions with data, see Table
1
.

The above analysis made two key simplifying assumptions: (1) a restriction to a two-dimensional setting, and (2) the wavefront of the light emitted from the surface
S
is parabolic. Regarding assumption (2), we see that the parabola
P
approximates a circular wavefront, which is represented by a grey curve in Fig.
3

Regarding assumption (1), we can generalize the argument to three dimensions. A first intuition would be to consider radially symmetric generalizations, such as the light having a paraboloidal wavefront. However, this is not justified because the reflective segments
M
are irregular (e.g., the shape of clouds) and not necessarily circular disks. The two dimensional restriction in Fig.
2
does qualitatively capture a cross section of a planet with an atmosphere. This can be generalized by considering cross-sectional thin slabs wherein the wavefront consists of short parabolic cylinders. This implies a translational symmetry over short distances and keeps the proportions and above calculations unchanged. Huygens’ principle is again obeyed in virtue of that fact that a short parabolic cylinder approximates a thin cross-section of a sphere.

The argument we have presented is geometric in nature and applies to rocky celestial bodies such as Venus, Earth and Titan. In the next section we discuss extensions to gas giants.

III
Discussion and applications

The physical interpretation of equation (
1
) is relatively straightforward. The solar radiation that reaches the ground heats it up. The ground then emits thermal radiation that gets reflected by the clouds. The reflected radiation adds to the heating of the ground that will then radiate more as a consequence. The ground keeps heating up until the net radiation getting through to outer space matched the incoming radiation, as (
1
) states. As we have show, this physical mechanism is enough to account for the surface heating of celestial bodies in the solar system with a notable atmosphere.

Because the inner albedo is proportional to the planetary bond albedo, and the constant of proportionally is geometric in nature, this indicates that the physical processes determining the inner albedo are the same as the bond albedo. For this to happen it is sufficient that the bottom-side of clouds has the same reflective properties as their top-side, which is a reasonable assumption. The spectrum of incident light is different for the top and bottom-side of the clouds but if the reflection is uniform across all wavelengths then the planetary albedo
\alpha
and inner albedo
\beta
are determined by the same underlying physical processes. Furthermore, the model encapsulated in equation (
1
) is arguably the simplest possible model that could achieve such a result, as it accounts for the two main influences on the temperature (the Sun and the atmosphere) via one parameter each, for a total of two measurable parameters.

We can interpret formula (

).

IV
Conclusion

We have presented the empirical equation (
1
) that allows us to determine surface temperatures of rocky celestial bodies with substantial atmospheres. Remarkably, only the solar irradiance and bond albedo are sufficient to determine the surface temperature. We have provide a geometric justification for this fact, where the universal parabolic constant pays a key role in determining the ratio between inner and outer bond albedo. In addition, we have extended the applicability of equation (
1
) to gas giants, finding the temperature where condensates start to form. We hope the work inspires future studies into these matters.

In terms of comparison with other theories, we only note that early work by Hansen
Hansen et al. 1981
reduces a climate model to an empirical formula with 11 fined tuned parameters to account for a temperature change of at most a few degrees on Earth’s surface. More recent models tend to be even more complex
Rind 1999
. The proposed mechanism in this paper is based on cloud reflectivity and conservation of energy, using only 2 measurable parameters to account for the entire temperature at the surface of all celestial bodies in the solar system with a notable atmosphere. Hence, based on model complexity (number of parameters and equations), applicability and accuracy, the heating mechanism we propose performs much better than the alternatives in accounting for and explaining measured temperatures.

Reviewer 1

My first reason for suggesting rejection is the physical basis for the formula proposed. The model set up in Equation 1 balances the incoming solar radiation, reduced by reflection from the Bond albedo, against the radiation emitted from the surface, reduced by an “inner albedo” representing scattering. This omits the centrally important process of absorption and re-emission by the atmosphere. This does not act like a mirror reflecting light back to the surface and allowing some to pass through, as is proposed in this model. A typical energy balance model would balance (at the top of the atmosphere) incoming solar radiation, outgoing radiation from the surface that has not been absorbed by the atmosphere, and outgoing radiation emitted directly from the atmosphere. This key process is omitted from the model.

How successful has the theory based on absorption and re-emission processes in the atmosphere in accounting for surface temperatures? A specific model comparison would be needed in terms of model complexity (number of parameters, equations etc.), accuracy and overall connection to other established, proven mechanisms. I do not necessarily see any reason why the processes of absorption and re-emission would be precluded from the mechanism proposed in this paper provided that: (1) they fill in an explanatory gap in determining surface temperature and (2) that they would occur at a higher level of granularity in modelling the physical systems at play. However, regarding (1), there is little to no explanatory gap left between the predictions of the model proposed in this paper and the observed temperatures. Regarding (2), a more detailed model should not impact the physical predictions at a coarser scale, but would have to be consistent with them. Given the potentially large literature on the topic of atmospheric absorption and re-emission the reviewer did not pinpoint any specific literature or model. This is unfortunate as concrete examples would serve as a more constructive feedback and give the chance for a comparison of specific theories. We have included a comparison with work by Hansen
Hansen et al. 1981
in the current (updated) version of the paper, see the Conclusion section.

However, I have tried to keep an open mind that the model proposed could be an idealised representation than somehow includes atmospheric emission, so assessed the empirical results based on Figure 1 on their own merits. The match of Equation 1 to real data (for constant r) appears to be an extraordinary result, as it matches two physically separate processes. The Bond albedo alpha relates to reflection of mostly visible light with short wavelengths from the Sun, by features like clouds or reflective surfaces, while the “inner albedo” beta must correspond to the interaction (absorption or scattering, by gases like CO2) of the atmosphere with longer infrared wavelengths emitted by the surface. These two processes have no intrinsic relation - is it perfectly plausible that the Earth could have a more reflective surface (e.g. more clouds or desert coverage) but the same amount of CO2 in its atmosphere. If Equation 1 holds for constant r, it would therefore show a very surprising result about two apparently independent processes.

Reviewer 2

This manuscript presents an interesting theoretical work estimating the temperature of celestial bodies with significant global-scale atmospheres. The most important result is equation (1) presented in the manuscript, which establishes a relationship between the input radiation (i.e., the absorbed solar radiation) to a celestial body and the radiation radiated by the celestial body. The input radiation is determined by the incoming solar radiation and the Bond albedo (alpha). The incoming solar radiation at a given celestial body is further determined by the distance between the Sun and the celestial body. Therefore, the Bond albedo is the only parameter controlling the input radiation. On the other hand, the output radiation (i.e., the radiation a celestial body radiates) is expressed as the blackbody radiation at the surface (or a corresponding pressure level) multiplied by a factor less than 1. The factor is further expressed as a function of a new parameter of inner albedo (beta), 1-beta. The author further suggests that the ratio between the Bond albedo (alpha) and the inner albedo (beta) is a constant for these celestial bodies with significant atmospheres. Therefore, equation (1) can be used to determine the temperature of these celestial bodies with significant atmospheres with known Bond albedos. The author also presented a geometric proof and brief discussion on its applications. The topic and idea are interesting, but I have major concerns as follows.

The reviewer has accurately summarized the paper.

The author claimed a constant parameter for the ratio between the Bond albedo and the inner albedo (i.e., r) for these astronomical bodies with significant atmospheres. However, such a constant should be carefully examined. For Earth and Titan, there are updated values for the Bond albedo (e.g., Loeb et al., 2020; Creecy et al., 2021). I expect that the updates of Bond albedo and solar constant at Titan and Earth do not significantly affect the value of the ratio r. But it is still preferred to use more precise values. For Venus, the global-average Bond albedo surface temperature has large uncertainties due to limitations in the current available observations (e.g., Tomasko et al., 1980; Titov et al., 2013). In addition, it does not make sense that Venus’ surface radiation plays any significant role in its radiation budget (equation (1)) considering its thick atmosphere. The radiation from the surface will be completely blocked by its thick atmosphere before it escapes into space.

The most serious trouble for equation (1) comes from giant planets. First, Uranus and Neptune have a similar temperature profile of the troposphere and Bond albedo, but the solar constant (i.e., the incoming radiation) is significantly different between the two ice giants because of the different distances from the Sun for the two planets. It is hard to believe that they follow equation (1) with a constant (i.e., r = 1.3).

In the first version of the paper we initially dismissed Uranus and Neptune due to a not significant deviation of our prediction and the usual black-body estimate. It is peculiar that the reviewer mentions them. In the current version, we have found for Uranus similar results to Saturn and Jupiter, with condensation and evaporation of ethane occurring at the temperature predicted by our model.

Second, the study by Hanel et al. (1983) seems to underestimate the Bond albedo of Saturn (personal communication with a group of scientists who are re-examining Saturn’s radiant energy budget with the Cassini long-term multi-instrument observations).

Finally, the energy budget is more complicated for giant planets than for terrestrial bodies. Unlike terrestrial bodies, internal heat plays a critical role in the energy budget of the atmospheric system for giant planets. Jupiter, Saturn, and Neptune all have confirmed internal heat, and it plays a critical role in the energy budgets of the three giant planets. But the internal heat is not considered in equation (1).

Indeed, the internal heat is not considered as the formula determines the temperature due to radiation at the boundary where condensation starts and convection becomes important.

The physics of the new parameter (i.e., inner albedo) suggested by the author is unclear. The radiation budget is determined by the absorbed solar radiation (i.e., the left-hand expression of equation (1)) and the emitted thermal radiation. The emitted thermal radiation is equivalent to the right-hand expression of equation (1), but the emitted thermal radiation has much clearer physics than the right-hand expression including the inner albedo. For Titan and Earth, both radiations from the solid surface and atmosphere (mainly the troposphere) contribute to the total emitted radiation. The temperature decreases with height in the troposphere for both Titan and Earth, so the surface’s radiation is stronger than the atmospheric radiation. That is the reason why there is a factor smaller than 1 (i.e., 1-beta) before the term of the surface’s radiation on the right-hand side of equation (1) if we use the surface’s blackbody radiation (i.e.,
