Fixed width treelike neural networks capacity analysis – generic activations
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

We consider the capacity of
treelike committee machines
(TCM) neural networks. Relying on Random Duality Theory (RDT),
[
42
]
recently introduced a generic framework for their capacity analysis. An upgrade based on the so-called
partially lifted
RDT (pl RDT) was then presented in
[
45
]
. Both lines of work focused on the networks with the most typical,
sign
, activations. Here, on the other hand, we focus on networks with other, more general, types of activations and show that the frameworks of
[
42
,
45
]
are sufficiently powerful to enable handling of such scenarios as well. In addition to the standard
linear
activations, we uncover that particularly convenient results can be obtained for two very commonly used activations, namely, the
quadratic
and
rectified linear unit (ReLU)
ones. In more concrete terms, for each of these activations, we obtain both the RDT and pl RDT based memory capacities upper bound characterization for
any
given (even) number of the hidden layer neurons,
d
. In the process, we also uncover the following two, rather remarkable, facts: 1) contrary to the common wisdom, both sets of results show that the bounding capacity decreases for large
d
(the width of the hidden layer) while converging to a constant value; and 2) the maximum bounding capacity is achieved for the networks with precisely
two
hidden layer neurons! Moreover, the large
d
converging values are observed to be in excellent agrement with the statistical physics replica theory based predictions.

Index Terms: TCM neural networks; Capacity; Lifted random duality theory; Different activations
.

1
Introduction

Demand for efficient handling and interpretation of large data sets, has grown rather rapidly over the last 15-20 years. Machine learning (ML) clearly distinguished itself as a particularly helpful set of concepts capable of providing the needed technical and scientific resources to meet such a high demand. Consequently, a fast development of various ML branches ensued. Neural networks (NN), as one of such branches, quickly became one of the focuses of strong research interests and among the fastest growing research fields. Many great results and quite a few remarkable breakthroughs that relate to both theoretical and practical NN aspects have been obtained. Moreover, many of the well known results achieved in prior decades – that for a long time served as academic prototypes – have been revisited and brought to practical usability. Among the most prominent of them are certainly those that relate to one of the key NN features, the so-called, network’s
memory capacity
(see, e.g.,
[
26
,
9
,
52
,
51
,
50
,
8
,
22
,
16
,
48
,
3
]
). Here we continue the same trend and focus on several important capacity related questions and provide a strong theoretical progress. Before discussing in more detail some of the main problems and our technical contributions, we find it convenient to first introduce the basics of the NN models of our interest.

1.1
Feed forward neural networks – mathematical basics

To be able to properly introduce the network’s memory capacity as the main object of our interest, we first discuss the underlying network architecture.

Architecture:
We are interested in multilayered multi-input single-output feed-forward neural networks with
L-2
hidden layers and
d_{i}
(
i\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}
) nodes (neurons) in the
i
-th layer. To ensure a notational facilitation, two additional layers, indexed by
i=1
and
i=L
are artificially added and they correspond to the network input and output, respectively (although the input and output of the network are basically artificial NN layers, to be in agreement with the introduced indexation, we will refer to them as networks layers). The way the network operates is basically determined by specifying
the vectors of threshold functions,
{\bf f}^{(i)}(\cdot)=[{\bf f}_{1}^{(i)}(\cdot),{\bf f}_{2}^{(i)}(\cdot),\dots,{\bf f}_{d_{i+1}}^{(i)}(\cdot)]^{T}
. Each threshold function
{\bf f}_{j}^{(i)}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
describes how neuron
j
in layer
i
operates. The network effectively functions by taking the outputs of the nodes from layer
i
as the inputs of the nodes in layer
i+1
and transforming them into the new outputs (in layer
i+1
) via a linear combination governed by the matrix of weights
W^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}\times d_{i+1}}
. After setting
{\bf d}=[d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{L}]
(with
d_{1}=n
and
d_{L}=1
) and denoting by
{\bf b}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i+1}},i=1,2,\dots L
the so-called thresholds vectors and by
{\bf x}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}
and
{\bf x}^{(i+1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i+1}}
the inputs and outputs of the neurons in layer
i
, one
has the following:

Mathematical formalism of NN with architecture
A({\bf d}\text{, }{\bf f}^{(i)})
:

\mbox{{input:}}\triangleq{\bf x}^{(1)}\quad\longrightarrow

{\bf x}^{(i+1)}={\bf f}^{(i)}(W^{(i)}{\bf x}^{(i)}-{\bf b}^{(i)})

\longrightarrow\quad\mbox{{output:}}\triangleq{\bf x}^{(L+1)}
.

(1)

The architecture of the network,
A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(i)})
, is fully specified by the vectors
{\bf d}
and
{\bf f}^{(i)}
. Also, when the vectors of functions,
{\bf f}^{(i)}
, are identical, we write
A({\bf d};{\bf f})
instead of
A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(i)})
.

Memory capacity:
As mentioned earlier, one of the most fundamental features of any neural net (including single neurons as special cases) is their ability to properly memorize/store a large amount of data. A simple way to see how the above formalism achieves this is the following: assume the existence of
m
data pairs
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
,
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, with
{\bf x}^{(0,k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
being the
n
-dimensional data vectors and
{\bf y}^{(0,k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
being their associated labels. Determining matrices
W^{(i)}
such that

{\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(L+1)}={\bf y}^{(0,k)}\qquad\forall k,

(2)

is then sufficient to properly relate given data vectors to their corresponding labels. If the network architecture
A({\bf d},{\bf f}^{(i)})
is given then its
memory capacity,
C(A({\bf d},{\bf f}^{(i)}))
,
is defined as the largest sample size,
m
, such that (
2
) holds for any collection of data pairs
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
,
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
with certain prescribed properties. Since the memory capacity plays one of the most important roles in understanding the overall neural nets’ functioning mosaic, finding both, its precise theoretical characterization and the corresponding fast algorithmic procedure that achieves it, is of utmost importance. Of our particular interest in this paper are the theoretical aspects and we below provide a host of results that for many well known architectures almost fully characterize their capacities.

To facilitate the presentation, a few structural and technical assumptions are in place as well. Many of them, however, are aligned with the ones discussed in
[
42
,
45
]
. To avoid an unnecessary repetition, we only briefly recall on these and refer for a more detailed exposition to
[
42
,
45
]
. On the other hand, we place the most emphasis on those that are substantially different and particularly relevant to the results that we present in this paper.

1.2
Technical assumptions

To facilitate the exposition and to make the final results cleaner and easier to use, we will rely on several architectural and data related assumptions. We state them below before starting the analytical considerations. As they are fairly common and rather prevalent in the literature, we avoid discussing them in deep details.

Network architecture assumptions:
1-hidden layer
treelike committee machine
type of neural networks with
generic
zero-threshold activation functions in the hidden layer are considered. This means the following:
(i)
We assume
L=3
,
{\bf b}^{(i)}=0
,
W^{(1)}=I_{n\times n}
, and
W^{(3)}={\bf w}^{T}
, where
{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}\times 1}
(i.e.
W^{(3)}
is a
d_{2}
-dimensional row vector that will be specified depending on the type of the considered activation functions).
(ii)
We also define
d\triangleq d_{2}
and
\delta\triangleq\delta_{1}=\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}=\frac{n}{d}
. While the presented mathematical concepts will hold for any
d
, to be able to get concrete capacity values, we eventually assume that
d
is any (even) natural number.

(iii)
In the first layer, we consider
identity
neuronal functions, i.e. we take
{\bf f}^{(1)}({\bf x}^{(1)})={\bf x}^{(1)}
. In the hidden layer, we take the
generic
zero-threshold activations
{\bf f}_{j}^{(2)}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}}\rightarrow R
with
{\bf f}_{j}^{(2)}(\cdot)={\bf f}_{k}^{(2)}(\cdot)
for any
j\neq k
. Finally, at the output, we take zero-threshold sign activation
{\bf f}^{(3)}(W^{(3)}{\bf x}^{(3)}-{\bf b}^{(3)})=\mbox{sign}\left(W^{(3)}{\bf x}^{(3)}\right)
. For notational simplicity, we denote this architecture by
A({\bf d};[{\bf f}^{(1)},{\bf f}^{(2)},{\bf f}^{(3)}])=A({\bf d};[I,{\bf f}^{(2)},\mbox{sign}])\triangleq A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(2)})
.
(iv)

The matrix
W^{(i)}
can be generically full or with a particular structure. Both types of structuring have been of interest throughout the literature. A particular type of sparse structuring, where the support of
W^{(i)}
’s
j
-th row,
\mbox{supp}\left(W_{j,:}^{(i)}\right)
, satisfies

\mbox{supp}\left(W_{j,:}^{(i)}\right)={\mathcal{S}}^{(j)}
, with
{\mathcal{S}}^{(j)}\triangleq\{(j-1)\delta+1,(j-1)\delta+2,\dots,j\delta\}
, makes the above architecture correspond to what is in the literature referred to as the
treelike committee machines
(TCM). Precisely such architectures will be of our interest in this paper. For the completeness, we also add that if the matrix is full then the above architecture corresponds to what is in the literature typically referred to as the
fully connected committee machines
(FCM).

Data related assumptions:

(i)

Binary
labeling
{\bf y}_{i}^{(0,k)}\in\{-1,1\}
, as the most standard type of labeling, is assumed as well (choosing
sign
perceptron as neuronal function at the output naturally complements the binary labeling choice as well).
(ii)
Inseparable data sets are not allowed (for example, indistinguishable/contradictory pairs (or subgroups) like
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
and
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},-{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
can not appear).
(iii)
Data sets of statistical nature will be of our main interest. We particularly focus on
{\bf x}^{(0,k)}
as iid standard normals. This follows into the footsteps of the trend established in the classical single perceptron references (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
16
,
34
,
9
,
52
,
51
,
50
]
) and allows for, expectedly, a fairly universal statistical treatment. It is also useful to note that for providing universal capacity upper bounds, any type of acceptable data set (including even nonstatistical ones) actually suffices.

1.3
Prior work

The problems of our interest are well known and have been studied in various forms for almost 70 years. Naturally, the early studies related to the single neurons while the more recent ones emphasize the importance of understanding the multi-neuron or multi-layered architectures. Given the pace at which the ML and NN fields are developing, the underlying relevant literature is rather vast and growing. We below highlight the results that we view as most closely related to our own.

Since the memory capacity of spherical perceptrons is directly connected to several fundamental questions in integral geometry, the early capacity considerations were related to some of the geometrical/probabilistic classic works (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
9
,
50
,
22
]
). Possibly the most famous of them states that the capacity of the spherical
sign
perceptrons
doubles the dimension
of the data ambient space,
n
, i.e.,
C(A(1;\mbox{sign}))\rightarrow 2n
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. After being initially obtained as a remarkable closed form combinatorial geometry fact in
[
26
,
9
,
52
,
51
,
50
,
8
,
22
]
, it was decades later rediscovered and reproved in various different forms in a plethora of different fields ranging from machine learning and pattern recognition to information theory, probability, and statistical physics (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
48
,
10
,
33
,
11
,
12
,
16
,
34
,
40
]
).

Sign perceptrons networks:
Despite the elegance of the single perceptron results, the corresponding multi-perceptron ones are not easy to obtain. Particularly scarce are the TCM related ones. While a bit more is known about the FCM ones, a direct connection between the two is not apparent. Besides the trivial fact that the FCM capacities upper-bound the corresponding TCM ones, one may also (somewhat ad-hoc) view the TCM capacities as roughly the FCM ones divided by
d
. Although non necessarily rigorous (or even fully correct) such a viewing suggests a potential usefulness of FCM results. Still, an overwhelming majority of known results indicates that the memory capacity is unavoidably related to the total number of the network weights,
w=\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}d_{i}d_{i+1}
. For example, the famous VC-dimension
qualitative
memory capacity upper bound gives the scaling
O(w\log(w))
. It is interesting to note that for NNs with 1-hidden layer,
w=d_{1}d_{2}+d_{2}=(n+1)d
for FCM and
w=d_{1}+d_{2}=n+d
for TCM. This, on the other hand, for large
d_{i}
’s and huge
n
, gives, the above mentioned, “
division by
d
” FCM – TCM capacity relation. A couple of lower bounding results are known as well. For example,
[
7
]
argued that the capacity of a shallow 3-layer network (similar to the one studied here) scales as
O(nd)
. On the other hand,
[
49
]
obtained recently a stronger version, where, for the networks with more than three layers, the capacity is shown to be roughly at least
O(w)
.

Obtaining precise results of
non-scaling
type in network architectures turned out to be a much harder challenge. This seems particularly surprising given the simplicity and elegance of the corresponding single perceptron ones. Until the very recent appearance of
[
45
,
42
]
there was hardly any mathematically rigorous result that could provide even remotely close sign perceptron networks capacity estimates.
[
42
]
utilized the Random duality theory (RDT) and developed a generic framework for the analysis of TCM network capacities. As a results of the framework, strong upper bounds were obtained for any given (odd) number of the nodes in the hidden layer.
[
45
]
went a step further, utilized a partially lifted RDT variant (pl RDT) and substantially lowered the upper bounds of
[
42
]
.

Different activations networks:
Given the importance of a single sign perceptron, studying their merging into a large architectural structure is a natural transition. Two things should be kept in mind when making such a transition though. First, it is not clear a priori that the results that hold for the single perceptron will hold in a similar fashion for the network of perceptrons. Second, the sign perceptrons are not continuous functions and handling them algorithmically when using or training the network might impose computationally/numerically unsurpassable obstacles. If on top of that, one adds the above mentioned analytical hardness, the need for potentially less simple but easier to use activation factions is rather obvious.

As the discreteness is typically perceived as the main source of both analytical and algorithmic sign perceptrons hardness, the natural choice for different activations leads towards allowing various continuous ones as well. Many of them have already found a steady place in NN architectures. Examples include but are not limited to ReLU, quadratic, tanh, erf and so on. Such activations make things a bit easier and, consequently, a little bit more is known about their capacities. For example,
[
54
]
suggested for deep nets and
[
20
]
proved for 4-layer nets that the capacity is at least
O(w)
for sigmoids.
[
57
,
19
]
showed similar results for ReLU while additionally restricting on the number of nodes. Such a restriction though was later on removed in
[
55
]
for both tanh and ReLU.

Statistical physics – Replica theory:
Given that the
precise
capacity characterizations (as functions of the number of the hidden layer neurons
d
) do not allow for any
qualitative/scaling
descriptions (say, of the
O(\cdot)
type), the mathematically rigorous results are often very hard to achieve. In such situations, statistical physics replica methods are an excellent (and often irreplaceable) tool to produce, non-rigorous, but expectedly
precise
analyses. As the hardness of the precise analytical studying of various NN features has been recognized in the mid-eighties of the last century, the application of the replica methods in capacity characterization has been around for close to four decades. The foundational concepts of such an approach were laid out in the pioneering works
[
17
,
16
]
, whre various forms of single perceptrons were discussed. Here, we focus more on the ensuing ones that relate to the network architectures. In particular,
[
14
,
5
]
studied the very same, TCM architecture, as we do (as well as the above mentioned, related, FCM one). For the sign perceptrons, they obtained the closed form replica symmetry based capacity predictions for any number of the neurons in the hidden layer,
d
. They established the corresponding large
d
scaling behavior. Both of these results were proven as mathematically rigorous capacity upper bounds in
[
45
,
42
]
. Moreover,
[
14
,
5
]
showed that their replica symmetry based large
d
predictions violate the mathematically rigorous ones obtained through the uniform-bounding extension of
[
9
,
52
,
51
,
50
]
given in
[
15
]
. This contradiction was remedied in
[
14
,
5
]
by studying the first level of replica symmetry breaking (rsb) and showing that it lowers the capacity. Related large
d
scaling rsb considerations were also presented in
[
24
]
for both the committee and the so-called parity machines (PM) (more on the earlier PM replica considerations can be found in, e.g.,
[
6
,
4
]
). Also, for the FCM architecture, a bit later,
[
47
,
53
]
obtained the large
d
scaling that matches the upper-bounding one of
[
15
]
. Particularly relevant to our work are two very recent lines of work.
[
2
]
first obtained the first level of rsb capacity predictions for the TCM architectures with the ReLU activations and
[
56
]
moved things even further and obtained similar rsb predictions for several different activations, including the well known linear, ReLU, erf, quadratic, and tanh. A key difference with respect to our results should also be noted. Namely, both sets of results,
[
2
]
and
[
56
]
, relate to the networks with large
d
(basically, to the networks with
d\rightarrow\infty
), whereas our results are obtained for any given (even)
d
.

Practical achievability:
Another line of work attracted a lot of interest over the last several years and should be mentioned as well. It is related to the design and analysis of efficient algorithms that can be used to train the networks to potentially approach the capacity. The key focus has been on showing that the simple gradient based methods might actually perform quite well in this context. The so-called mild over-parametrization (moderately larger number of free parameters,
w
, compared to the size of the data set,
m
) is deemed as sufficing to ensure excellent performance of gradient based methods. More on the recent progress in these directions can be found in, e.g.,
[
13
,
18
,
1
,
21
,
23
,
25
,
46
,
27
,
58
]
. These results mostly relate to FCMs but are also extendable to TCMs as well.

1.4
Contributions

The main object of our study is the so-called
n
-scaled memory capacity of the TCM NNs with various (different from standard
sign
one) activation functions in the hidden layer. In other words, we study

c(d;{\bf f}^{(2)})\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C(A([n,d,1];{\bf f}^{(2)}))}{n}.

(3)

A very strong progress in characterizing
C(A([n,d,1];\mbox{sign}))
for any given (odd)
d
has been made in
[
42
]
. In particular, utilizing the powerful Random Duality Theory (RDT) mathematical engine,
[
42
]
provides an explicit upper bound
\hat{c}(d;\mbox{sign})
on
c(d;\mbox{sign})
. Numerical results obtained for smaller values of
d
suggested a strong benefit in adding more neurons in a network architecture context. On the other hand, we, in this paper, make a substantial progress in several different aspects including both methodological and practical ones.

A summary of the main technical results of the paper:

(i)
We first show that the main framework from
[
42
,
45
]
(established relying on the RDT and pl RDt principles) for the capacity analysis of sign perceptron networks can be utilized for different activations as well.
(ii)
To produce concrete capacity estimates, we then focus on several particular activations for which neat and convenient results can be obtained. We fist start with the
linear
activations and show that the capacity of the TCM network with one hidden layer network is identical to the single spherical perceptron. We then switch to the
quadratic
activations and obtain the close form analytical RDT based capacity upper bounds and their refined pl RDT counterparts. Finally, we consider the
ReLU
activations and obtain the corresponding analytical closed form RDT and pl RDT results.
(iii)
For all activations, we conduct the needed numerical evaluations to obtain the concrete capacity values as well.
(iv)
All our results are obtained for an extremely challenging scenario where the number of the hidden layer neurons,
d
, can be any even positive integer. This allows us to uncover two rather fascinating phenomena: 1) Both thr RDT and the pl RDT estimates are decreasing for large
d
while converging to a
constant
(not dependent on
d
) value; and 2) The maxima of both estimates for both quadratic and ReLU activations are achieved for
d=2
neurons in the hidden layer. This is a bit contrary to the common wisdom and in a strike contrast with the corresponding behavior of the sign activations where the capacity estimates grow with
d
. In particular, one effectively has that when it comes to the memory capabilities, uncontrollably increasing the width of the hidden layer may not always be among the most recommended architecture building strategies.

Algebraic description of network data processing

To put everything on the right mathematical track and facilitate writing and overall presentation, we first set
W\triangleq W^{(2)}
. After recalling that
W^{(1)}=I
and
W^{(3)}={\bf w}^{T}
, we then have for any
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}

{\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(2)}={\bf f}^{(1)}(W^{(1)}{\bf x}^{(1)})={\bf f}^{(1)}({\bf x}^{(1)})={\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)},

(4)

and

{\bf x}^{(2)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(3)}={\bf f}^{(2)}(W^{(2)}{\bf x}^{(2)})={\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)}),

(5)

and

{\bf x}^{(3)}={\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(4)}={\bf f}^{(3)}(W^{(3)}{\bf x}^{(3)})={\bf f}^{(3)}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf x}^{(3)})=\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})).

(6)

A very neat closed-form explicit relation between the input and the output of the network can be obtained by connecting beginning in (
4
) and end in (
6
)

{\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(4)}=\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})).

(7)

For network to operate properly, it is then both necessary and sufficient that the following condition holds

{\bf y}^{(0,k)}=\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})).

(8)

After setting

{\bf y}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\bf y}^{(0,1)}&{\bf y}^{(0,2)}&\dots&{\bf y}^{(0,m)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad X\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}^{(0,1)}&{\bf x}^{(0,2)}&\dots&{\bf x}^{(0,m)}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(9)

one can then rewrite (
8
) in generic matrix form as

\left(\exists W\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times n}|\|{\bf y}^{T}-\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(WX^{T}))\|_{2}=0\right)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\left(\left(X,{\bf y}\right)\mbox{is memorized}\right),

(10)

and the
k
-th data pair
\left({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)}\right)
are the
k
-th rows of
m\times n
matrix
X
and
m\times 1
column vector
{\bf y}
. This further leads to the following (effectively an alternative to (
10
)):

Algebraic memorization characterization of the
{\bf f}^{(2)}
-activated hidden layer TCMs:

\displaystyle\hskip-21.68121pt0=\xi\triangleq\min_{W,Q}

\displaystyle\hskip-7.22743pt\|{\bf y}-\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle\hskip-7.22743ptXW^{T}=Q

(11)

\Longleftrightarrow
Data set
\left(X,{\bf y}\right)
is properly memorized.

Clearly, the above algebraic formulation is the key mathematical problem on the path towards ensuring proper network data memorization. We therefore analyze it below in more detail.

3
Random Duality Theory (RDT) based capacity analysis

{\bf y}

\displaystyle\xi=\min_{Z,Q}

\displaystyle\|{\bf 1}-\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle XZ=Q,

(12)

where a cosmetic change,
Z=W^{T}
, is introduced to facilitate writing. As in

42
]

Z

\displaystyle\xi=\min_{Z,Q}

\displaystyle\|{\bf 1}-\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle XZ=Q

(13)

\displaystyle\|Z_{:,j}\|_{2}=1

\displaystyle\mbox{supp}(Z_{:,j})={\mathcal{S}}^{(j)},1\leq j\leq d,

with
\|Z_{:,j}\|_{2}
being the norm of the
j

\displaystyle\xi=\min_{{\bf z}^{(j)},Q}

\displaystyle\|{\bf 1}-\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle X^{(j)}{\bf z}^{(j)}=Q_{:,j},1\leq j\leq d,

\displaystyle\|{\bf z}^{(j)}\|_{2}=1

\displaystyle{\bf z}^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\delta},Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times d},

where

Summary of the RDT’s main principles

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{1) \emph{Finding underlying optimization algebraic representation}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{2) \emph{Determining the random dual}}\\
\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{3) \emph{Handling the random dual}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{4) \emph{Double-checking strong random duality.}}\end{array}

As in

]
, all the key results (including both simple to more complicated ones) are formulated as lemmas and theorems.

1)
Algebraic memorization characterization:
The following lemma summarizes the above algebraic discussion by providing a precise resulting optimization representation of the network memorization property
and is a mirrored analogue to Lemma 1 in
[
42
]
.

Lemma 1
.

(Algebraic optimization representation)
Assume a 1-hidden layer TCM with architecture
A([n,d,1];{\bf f}^{(2)})
. Any given data set
\left({\bf x}^{(0,k)},1\right)_{k=1:m}
can not be properly memorized by the network if

f_{rp}(X)>0,

(16)

where

f_{rp}(X)\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\|{\bf z}^{(j)}\|_{2}=1,Q}\max_{\Lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times d}}\|{\bf 1}-\mbox{\emph{sign}}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\Lambda_{:,j})^{T}X^{(j)}{\bf z}^{(j)}-{\rm tr}\,(\Lambda^{T}Q),

(17)

and
X\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}^{(0,1)}&{\bf x}^{(0,2)}&\dots&{\bf x}^{(0,m)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
.

Proof.

Immediate consequence of Lemma 1 in
[
42
]
.
∎

Of our interest below is mathematically the most challenging, so-called
linear
, regime with

\alpha\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}.

(18)

The above lemma is of purely algebraic nature and as such it holds for any given data set
\left({\bf x}^{(0,k)},1\right)_{k=1:m}
. To analyze (
16
) and (
17
), the RDT further proceeds by accounting for a statistical
X
.

2)
Determining the random dual:
We follow the standard RDT practice and utilize the so-called concentration of measure property, which basically means that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, we have (see, e.g.
[
30
,
39
,
32
,
42
,
45
]
)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{X}\left(\frac{|f_{rp}(X)-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}(f_{rp}(X))|}{{\mathbb{E}}_{X}(f_{rp}(X))}>\epsilon\right)\longrightarrow 0.

(19)

Moreover, the following so-called random dual theorem is another key ingredient of the RDT machinery and is a mirrored alternative to Theorem 1 from
[
42
]
.

Theorem 1
.

(Memorization characterization via random dual) Let
d
be any even positive integer. Consider TCM with
d
neurons in the hidden layer, and architecture
A([n,d,1];{\bf f}^{(2)})
, and let the elements of
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
G\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times d}
, and
H\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\delta\times d}
be iid standard normals. Set

\displaystyle\phi(Q)

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\|{\bf 1}-\mbox{\emph{sign}}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

\displaystyle f_{rd}(G,H)

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\phi(Q)=0,\|{\bf z}^{(j)}\|_{2}=1}\max_{\Lambda\in R^{m\times d},\|\Lambda\|_{F}=1}\left({\rm tr}\,(\Lambda^{T}G)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\Lambda_{:,j}\|_{2}(H_{:,j})^{T}{\bf z}^{(j)}-{\rm tr}\,(\Lambda^{T}Q)\right)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G,H}f_{rd}(G,H).\vskip 0.0pt

(20)

One then has

\displaystyle\hskip-21.68121pt(\phi_{0}>0)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(f_{rd}>0)\longrightarrow 1\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(f_{rp}>0)\longrightarrow 1\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(A([n,d,1];{\bf f}^{(2)})\quad\mbox{fails to memorize data set}\quad(X,{\bf 1}))\longrightarrow 1\right).

Proof.

Immediate consequence of Theorem 1 in
[
42
]
.
∎

3)
Handling the random dual:
Proceeding as in
[
42
]
, one first solves the inner maximization over
\Lambda
and then the minimization over
{\bf z}^{(j)}
to obtain for
f_{rd}(G,H)
from (
20
)

\displaystyle f_{rd}(G,H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\phi(Q)=0}\sqrt{\|G-Q\|_{F}^{2}-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|G_{:,j}-Q_{:,j}\|_{2}\|H_{:,j}\|_{2}+\|H\|_{F}^{2}}.

(22)

This then further gives

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G,H}f_{rd}(G,H)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\phi(Q)=0}\|G-Q\|_{F}-1,

(23)

where, as discussed in
[
42
]
, the above equality obtained relying on the concentrations can be replaced by an inequality if one alternatively relies on the Cauchy-Schwartz inequalities (both options are perfectly sufficient for the subsequent analysis).

The remaining focus is on the residual optimization over
Q
. To that end we set

\phi_{i}(Q_{i,1:d})\triangleq\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q_{i,1:d}){\bf w}),

(24)

and further write

\displaystyle\min_{\phi(Q)=0}\|G-Q\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\min_{\phi(Q)=0}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min_{\phi_{i}(Q_{i,1:d})=1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})},

with

z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})\triangleq\min_{\phi_{i}(Q_{i,1:d})=1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}.

(26)

To further facilitate writing and remove unnecessary notations, we will set

\displaystyle{\bf g}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle G_{i,1:d}^{T}

\displaystyle{\bf q}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle Q_{i,1:d}^{T},

(27)

and

z_{i}({\bf g};{\bf f}^{(2)})\triangleq z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})=\min_{\phi_{i}(Q_{i,1:d})=1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}(Q_{i,1:d}){\bf w}\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf q}^{T}){\bf w}\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}.

(28)

The above mechanism is generic and in principle applies to any type of activation
{\bf f}^{(2)}
. To obtain concrete capacity estimates, we below proceed by considering several particular activation examples that have attracted a significant attention in NN literature.

3.1
Different
{\bf f}^{(2)}
activations

We focus on three well known
{\bf f}^{(2)}
activations: i)
linear
, ii)
quadratic
, and iii)
ReLU
. For each of them we obtain relatively elegant final capacity bounding estimates.

3.1.1
Linear hidden layer activations –
{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x}

Since the linear function is odd (i.e., since
f^{(2)}(-{\bf x})=-{\bf x}=-{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})
), we can, without a loss of generality, assume that, say,
{\bf w}={\bf 1}
, where
{\bf 1}
is the column vector of appropriate dimension with all of its components equal to one. However, as the analysis below shows, linearity is a very particular form of activation where such an assumption is actually not needed. In fact, any
{\bf w}
suffices. This should be kept in mind for later on when we study other two types of activations where
{\bf w}
will have to take particular forms to ensure that network functioning actually makes sense at all. From (
24
)
and (
26
), we then recognize the key optimization problem of interest

z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})=\min_{\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q_{i,1:d}){\bf w})=1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}(Q_{i,1:d}){\bf w}\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}.

(29)

For the linear activation, one can then rewrite (
29
) as

z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{lin})=z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}(Q_{i,1:d}){\bf w}\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf q}^{T}){\bf w}\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{{\bf q}^{T}{\bf w}\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}.

(30)

Proceeding by writing Lagrangian, we then further have

z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{lin})=\min_{{\bf q}^{T}{\bf w}\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{{\bf q}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda({\bf q}^{T}{\bf w}).

(31)

Relying on the strong duality, one then also finds

z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{lin})=\min_{{\bf q}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda({\bf q}^{T}{\bf w})=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\min_{{\bf q}}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda({\bf q}^{T}{\bf w}).

(32)

To solve the inner optimization over
{\bf q}
, we then consider the following derivative

\frac{d\left(\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda({\bf q}^{T}{\bf w})\right)}{d{\bf q}}=2({\bf q}-{\bf g})+\lambda{\bf w}.

(33)

After equalling the above derivative to zero one then finds

{\bf q}={\bf g}-\frac{\lambda{\bf w}}{2}.

(34)

Plugging back this value in the objective in (
32
), one obtains

z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{lin})=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\min_{{\bf q}}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda({\bf q}^{T}{\bf w})=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\|{\bf w}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}-\lambda{\bf g}^{T}{\bf w}.

(35)

Optimizing further over
\lambda
, one finds

\lambda_{opt}=\max\left(-\frac{2{\bf g}^{T}{\bf w}}{\|{\bf w}\|_{2}^{2}},0\right).

(36)

After plugging
\lambda_{opt}
back in (
35
), one obtains

z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{lin})=\frac{\left(\max\left(-{\bf g}^{T}{\bf w},0\right)\right)^{2}}{\|{\bf w}\|_{2}^{2}}=\left(\max\left(-{\bf g}^{T}\frac{{\bf w}}{\|{\bf w}\|_{2}},0\right)\right)^{2}=\left(\max\left(g_{i},0\right)\right)^{2},

(37)

where
g_{i}
is a standard normal random variable. Connecting (
23
), (
25
), (
30
), and (
37
), we finally have

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\phi(Q)=0}\|G-Q\|_{F}-1

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha{\mathbb{E}}z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{lin})}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha{\mathbb{E}}\left(\max\left(g_{i},0\right)\right)^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{2}}-1.

We summarize the above results in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

(Memory capacity;
linear
activation) Assume the setup of Theorem
1
. For linear
{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x}
, let
c(d;\mbox{lin})\triangleq c(d;{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x})
be the
n
-scaled memory capacity from (
3
). One then has the following for such

(
n
-scaled) memory capacity:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptc(d;\mbox{lin})=\mathbf{2}.

If and only if the sample complexity
m
is such that
\alpha\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}\geq c(d;\mbox{lin})
then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(A([n,d,1];\mbox{sign})\quad\mbox{fails to memorize data set}\quad(X,{\bf 1}))\longrightarrow 1.

(39)

Proof.

Follows immediately from the above discussion.
∎

The above lemma effectively states that only when the sample complexity
m
is such that
m>2n
(with
n
being the data vectors’ ambient dimension) the network fails to memorize the data. Consequently, one has for the memory capacity of the
d
hidden layer
linearly
activated neurons TCMs,
C(A([n,d,1];\mbox{lin}))=2n
. This shows that the capacity does not change as the width of the hidden layer increases. Moreover, it shows that the capacity remains equal to the capacity of the single spherical perceptron neuron (see, e.g.,

[

,
9
,
52
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
).

4)
Double checking the strong random duality:
Strictly speaking the above analysis establishes the upper bound on the capacity. However, given the presence of the underlying convexity and the strong deterministic duality, the machinery of
[
39
,
29
,
37
]
ensures that the strong random duality is in place as well which then implies that the established upper bounds are in fact tight.

3.1.2
Quadratic hidden layer activations –
{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x}^{2}

Since the quadratic function
{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x}^{2}\geq 0
one needs to carefully make a choice for vector
{\bf w}
which ensures that the network functioning is of any use. Clearly, some of the components of
{\bf w}
must be negative. Given the symmetry of the quadratic function, a natural choice that is typically considered in the literature in such situations is
{\bf w}
with
\frac{d}{2}
1s and
\frac{d}{2}
-1s. For the concreteness, we set

{\bf w}=\begin{bmatrix}-{\bf 1}_{\frac{d}{2}\times 1}\\
{\bf 1}_{\frac{d}{2}\times 1}\end{bmatrix},

(40)

and to facilitate exposition avoid dimensional subscripts and simply write

{\bf w}=\begin{bmatrix}-{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 1}\end{bmatrix},

(41)

assuming that the size of the column vectors
{\bf 1}
is
\frac{d}{2}\times 1
. As earlier, relying on (
24
), (
26
), (
3
), and (
29
), we then recognize the following key optimization problem of interest

z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}(Q_{i,1:d}){\bf w}\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{ij}-Q_{ij})^{2}=\min_{{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf q}^{T}){\bf w}\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{({\bf q}^{2})^{T}{\bf w}\geq 0}\|{\bf g}-{\bf q}\|_{2}^{2}.

(42)

After effectively splitting the problem into two parts, an interesting formulation can be obtained

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\min_{{\bf q}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}({\bf g}_{i}-{\bf q}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}({\bf g}_{i}-{\bf q}_{i})^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}.

(43)

For a moment, we find it convenient to set
\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}=b
, define
{\bf g}^{(r)}\triangleq{\bf g}_{\frac{d}{2}+1:d}
, and look at the following optimization problem

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(r)}({\bf g}^{(r)},b)=\min_{{\bf q}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}({\bf g}_{i}-{\bf q}_{i})^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}=b.

(44)

One can then trivially rewrite (
3.1.2
) as

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(r)}({\bf g}^{(r)},b)=\min_{{\bf q}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}-2\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}{\bf q}_{i}+b

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}=b.

(45)

Solving (
3.1.2
) then gives

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(r)}({\bf g}^{(r)},b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}-2\sqrt{b}\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}+b\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}-2\sqrt{b}\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}+b\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}-\sqrt{b}\right)^{2}.

One can then rewrite (
3.1.2
) as

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\min_{{\bf q},b}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}({\bf g}_{i}-{\bf q}_{i})^{2}+z_{i}^{(r)}({\bf g}^{(r)},b)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}\leq b.

(47)

A few additional algebraic transformations give

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\min_{{\bf q},b}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}{\bf q}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}+z_{i}^{(r)}({\bf g}^{(r)},b)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}\leq b.

(48)

We again for a moment set
\sum_{i=}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf q}_{i}^{2}=b_{1}\leq b
, define
{\bf g}^{(l)}\triangleq{\bf g}_{1:\frac{d}{2}}
, and find

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\min_{b_{1}\leq b}

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(l)}({\bf g}^{(l)},b_{1})+z_{i}^{(r)}({\bf g}^{(r)},b),

(49)

where, analogously to (
46
), we also have

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(l)}({\bf g}^{(l)},b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}-\sqrt{b_{1}}\right)^{2}.

(50)

Clearly,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}\qquad\implies\qquad z_{i}({\bf g})=0.

(51)

On the other hand, if
\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}>\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}
then
b_{1}=b
and the optimization from (
49
) becomes

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\min_{b}\left(\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}-\sqrt{b}\right)^{2}+\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}-\sqrt{b}\right)^{2}\right).

(52)

Optimizing over
b
one then finds

\displaystyle\sqrt{b}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}}{2},

(53)

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}>\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}\qquad\implies\qquad z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\frac{\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}\right)^{2}}{2}.

(54)

Writing (
51
) and (
54
) in a more compact form gives

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\frac{\left(\max\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf g}_{i}^{2}},0\right)\right)^{2}}{2}.

(55)

Moreover, one also has

\displaystyle z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})=\frac{\left(\max\left(a_{i}^{(1)}-a_{i}^{(2)},0\right)\right)^{2}}{2},

(56)

where
a_{i}^{(1)}
and
a_{i}^{(2)}
are independent chi random variables with
\frac{d}{2}
degrees of freedom. Connecting (
23
), (
25
), (
42
), and (
56
), we finally have

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\phi(Q)=0}\|G-Q\|_{F}-1

(57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{G}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{i}(G_{i,1:d})}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha{\mathbb{E}}z_{i}({\bf g};\mbox{quad})}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\left(\max\left(a_{i}^{(1)}-a_{i}^{(2)},0\right)\right)^{2}}{2}}-1.

Given the pdf of the chi random variable with
\frac{d}{2}
degrees of freedom

\displaystyle f_{\chi}(a)=\frac{2^{1-\frac{d}{4}}}{\gamma(\frac{d}{4})}a^{\frac{d}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{a^{2}}{2}},

(58)

one can also find

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\max\left(a_{i}^{(1)}-a_{i}^{(2)},0\right)\right)^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{a_{i}^{(1)}}\left(a_{i}^{(1)}-a_{i}^{(2)}\right)^{2}f_{\chi}(a_{i}^{(2)})f_{\chi}(a_{i}^{(1)})da_{i}^{(2)}da_{i}^{(1)}.

(59)

We summarize the above results in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

(Memory capacity upper bound;
quadratic
activation) Assume the setup of Theorem
1
. For quadratic
{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x}^{2}
, let
c(d;\mbox{quad})\triangleq c(d;{\bf f}^{(2)}({\bf x})={\bf x}^{2})
be the
n
-scaled memory capacity from (
3
). Let
a_{i}^{(1)}
and
a_{i}^{(2)}
be independent, chi distributed, random variables with
\frac{d}{2}
degrees of freedom and let
f_{\chi}(a)
be as in (
58
). One then has the following

(

5
Conclusion

In this paper we studied the treelike committee machines (TCM) neural networks and their memory capabilities. Differently form the common practice, we here instead of typical
sign
perceptron hidden layer neuronal activations consider a generic set of activations. Utilizing a powerful mathematical concept called Random Duality Theory (RDT),
[
42
]
established a generic statistical framework for the 1-hidden layer TCMs analysis that can study on a very precise level the scaled capacities for any given number of the neurons in the hidden layer,
d
. Among other things, such a machinery effectively enabled avoiding the qualitative/descriptive scaling types of estimates typically prevalent in the capacity analysis literature. Moreover, studying the
sign
perceptron activations, it also made a very strong progress towards obtaining, in a mathematically rigorous way, their
exact

n
-scaled capacities. For small values of
d
, it also made a very first rigorous progress in over 30 years over the previously best known bounds of
[
15
]
. Since the results of
[
42
]
are, in general, of the upper-bounding type,
[
45
]
proceeded further by considering the so-called partially lifted (pl) RDT variant and significantly lowered the estimates from
[
42
]
. Such a lowering further resulted in ensuring a universal (over the entire range of
d
) improvement over the previously best known results of
[
15
]
.

We here adopt the same strategy and utilize both the plain RDT and the partial RDT to characterize the 1-hidden layer TCM capacities with neuronal activations substantially different from the classical
sign
one. We first establish a universal framework for studying generic activations and then consider three particular activations types that have attracted a strong interest in recent NN literature: (i)
linear
; (ii)
quadratic
; and (iii)
ReLU
. For the linear activation we show that the plain RDT
exactly
characterizes the capacity. Moreover, we show that, no matter how wide the hidden layer is, the capacity remains equal to the capacity of the single spherical
sign
perceptron. For the quadratic and ReLU activations we obtain that the plain RDT predictions are decreasing functions of
d
that converge to a constant value. The maximum bounding value is in both cases obtained for the smallest possible
d=2
. Moreover, for the pl RDT and quadratic activation, we obtain a strong improvement over the plain RDT through the entire range of the considered
d
’s. At the same time, the bounding capacity maintains the decreasing in
d
property with the maximal value again being achieved for (the minimal possible)
d=2
. For the ReLU, we obtained that the pl RDT offers no improvement over the plain RDT for
d=2
which means that the same, decreasing in
d
, trend applies to these activations as well. Moreover, we uncover that another of the trends observed in
[
42
]
manifests itself here as well. Namely, the bounds obtained in
[
42
]
precisely matched the corresponding statistical physics replica symmetry based predictions obtain in
[
14
,
5
]
. Here, we also observe that the
linear
activation predictions precisely match the ones obtained through the replica considerations in
[
56
,
2
]
. Moreover, the
d\rightarrow\infty
converging values of our both plain RDT and pl RDT closely approach the corresponding ones obtained in
[
56
]
.

Various extensions are possible as well. It is rather clear that the first next one is to conduct the analysis with the
fully lifted
(fl) RDT (see, e.g.,
[
44
]
). Also, we here consider only three well known activation functions. Many others are of interest as well, e.g., sigmoid, erf, tanh and so on. More complex multi-layered network architectures including both TCM and FCM or PM based ones are of interest as well. All of these extensions, we will discuss in separate papers.
