Abstract
. We consider a model of learning and evolution in games whose action sets are endowed with a partition-based similarity structure intended to capture exogenous similarities between strategies. In this model, revising agents have a higher probability of comparing their current strategy with other strategies that they deem similar, and they switch to the observed strategy with probability proportional to its payoff excess. Because of this implicit bias toward similar strategies, the resulting dynamics – which we call the NRD – do not satisfy any of the standard monotonicity postulates for imitative game dynamics; nonetheless, we show that they retain the main long-run rationality properties of the replicator dynamics, albeit at quantitatively different rates. We also show that the induced dynamics can be viewed as a stimulus-response model in the spirit of Erev & Roth (1998) , with choice probabilities given by the NLC rule of Ben-Akiva (1973) and McFadden (1978) . This result generalizes an existing relation between the replicator dynamics and the EW algorithm in online learning, and provides an additional layer of interpretation to our analysis and results.

1.
Introduction

In the mass-action interpretation of evolutionary game theory, a nonatomic population of strategically-minded agents interact repeatedly via a set of simple myopic rules, and the governing dynamics arise from the aggregation of these individual interactions.
Typically, these rules are described by a
revision protocol
– that is, a rule which determines how frequently agents playing a given strategy consider switching to a different one, and with what criteria.
Thus, taken together, a population game and a revision protocol collectively define an aggregate dynamical system which uniquely prescribes the course of play starting from any initial population state.

A common feature of many revision protocols is the notion of
imitation:

the revising agent observes the behavior of a randomly selected individual, and then switches to the strategy of the observed agent with a probability that may depend on the revising agent’s incumbent payoff, the payoff of the observed strategy, or both.
The archetypal example of this process is the
PPI
(
PPI
) protocol of
Helbing (1992)
, where the switching probability is proportional to the payoff excess of the observed strategy over the incumbent one, and the distribution of actions in the population evolves according to the
RD
of
Taylor & Jonker (1978)
.
Beyond this simple revision rule, different models of imitation have been considered by
Björnerstedt & Weibull (1996)
,
Binmore & Samuelson (1997)
,
Schlag (1998)
,
Fudenberg & Imhof (2006)
;
Fudenberg & Imhof (2008)
,
Mertikopoulos & Viossat (2022)
, and many others;
for a summary, see
Weibull (1995)
,
Sandholm (2010a)
,
Hadikhanloo et al. (2022)
, and references therein.

Invariably, in all these revision rules, the revising agent inspects the strategy of “the first person they meet in the street”
(
Weibull, 1995
)
.
This premise is certainly reasonable when there are no prior beliefs or selection biases in the population;
however, when the players’ strategies share a set of exogenous similarities,
an agent may be biased toward sampling similar strategies rather than dissimilar ones.
[For example, when commuting from home to work, an agent may prefer to compare routes or departure times for the same mode of transport instead of a different, less familiar one.]
We are thus led to the following question:

How are the dynamics affected if players are more likely to observe

and compare strategies that are more similar to their current ones?

Our contributions.

To address this question, we examine a simple model of evolution and learning in games whose action sets are endowed with a partition-based
similarity structure
, i.e., a hierarchy of nested partitions – or
similarity classes
– that capture the degree of exogenous similarity between distinct strategies.
In our model, the revising agent has a higher probability of comparing payoffs with agents playing similar strategies, and the greater the degree of similarity between strategies, the greater the probability of such a comparison being made.
Then, after observing their opponent’s payoff,
the agent considers imitating the observed strategy only if its payoff is higher than their current one,
and they consummate the switch with probability proportional to the difference in payoffs.

When there are no exogenous similarities between strategies, this model reduces to the
PPI
protocol of
Helbing (1992)
mentioned above, and the population follows the
RD
of
Taylor & Jonker (1978)
.
In our approach, the additional affinity toward similar, more familiar strategies introduces an implicit selection bias, which in turn leads to quite different aggregate dynamics.
We call the resulting law of evolution the
NRD
(
NRD
), in reference to the nested similarity structure that drives the players’ payoff comparisons.

Related work on similarity-based reasoning.

The idea that similarities play an important role for decision-making goes back at least to
Luce (1956)
, with
Debreu (1960)
pointing out that realistic models of probabilistic choice should allow for violations of the principle of “irrelevance of added alternatives”
(
Luce, 1959
)
.
This issue is illustrated clearly by a canonical example from transportation due to
McFadden (1974b)
, where two similar options (a blue bus and a red bus) are each chosen less frequently than a dissimilar one (a car), even when all options are otherwise equally desirable in terms of utilities.
In this regard, the need to account for similarities when taking decisions has had a lasting impact in lasting impact in theoretical and econometric models of discrete choice
(
McFadden, 1981
;
McFadden, 2001
;
Ben-Akiva & Lerman, 1985
;
Anderson et al., 1992
)
.

In a learning context,
Gilboa & Schmeidler (1995)
considered a model where agents reason by drawing analogies to similar situations encountered in the past, and they provided a concrete axiomatization of decision rules that output the “best” action based on its past performance in similar cases.
More recently,
Steiner & Stewart (2008)
examined an analogous setting where players continually encounter new strategic, normal-form games and extrapolate beliefs from similar past situations, ultimately generating contagion of actions across different games through a “transfer learning” process.
This idea was taken further by
Mengel (2012)
who introduced a partition of all games encountered into categories, and considered a process of simultaneous learning of actions and partitions.
A crucial point where our conclusions differ with these works is that the dynamics of
Mengel (2012)

destabilize
strict equilibria, whereas strict equilibria
are always stable
under the
NRD
.

One reason for this discrepancy is that the similarities mentioned above concern events and situations encountered in the past, not the players’ actions per se – in this sense, similarities are
endogenous
, not
exogenous
.
In the context of extensive-form games,
Jehiel (2005)
– and, more recently,
Jehiel & Samet (2005)
;
Jehiel & Samet (2007)
– examined a dynamic behavioral model where agents bundle together decision nodes into distinct, static similarity classes, and agents only try to learn based on the average behavior in each class.
While the model of
Jehiel & Samet (2005)
;
Jehiel & Samet (2007)
is related in spirit to our approach (in both cases, the notion of similarity is determined exogenously and it does not fluctuate over time), their focus is on partitions of sequences of moves in extensive-form games, which have no analogue in the partition of one-shot actions in a population game.

Finally, in a recent unpublished manuscript,
Börgers & Mailath (2020)
introduced a model of learning in finite games with exogenous similarities in the signals received by the players.

These similarities need not be transitive or symmetric (so they do not necessarily comprise a partition-based structure), leading to the novel solution concept of a
similarity equilibrium
.

subsequently showed that similarity-based learning only converges to similarity equilibria – i.e., if the players’ learning process converges, the limit is a similarity equilibrium.
This property is reminiscent of the internal consistency of the

– i.e., if the

is othwerwise quite different to our own, so no further analogies can be drawn.

2.3.
Revision protocols and evolutionary dynamics

Evolutionary game dynamics are commonly derived from explicit microfoundations describing the agents’ efforts to increase their individual payoffs
(
Weibull, 1995
;
Benaïm & Weibull, 2003
;
Sandholm, 2010a
;
Sandholm, 2010b
)
.
In this context, each agent occasionally receives opportunities to switch actions – say, based on the rings of a Poisson alarm clock – and, at such moments, they choose a new action by applying a
revision protocol
.

Typically, a revision protocol is defined by specifying the
conditional switch rates

\varrho_{\alpha\beta}(\pi,x)
at which an
\alpha
-strategist might consider switching to strategy
\beta
when the population is at state
x\in\mathcal{X}
and the payoff vector is
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
In this way, for any given population game
\mathcal{G}\equiv\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A},v)
, a revision protocol
\varrho
induces the
mean dynamics

(MD)

\dot{x}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}x_{\beta}\varrho_{\beta\alpha}(v(x),x)-x_{\alpha}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}\varrho_{\alpha\beta}(v(x),x).

The dynamics (
MD
) specify the absolute rate of change in the use of each action
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
as the difference between inflows
to

\alpha
from other actions and outflows
from

\alpha
to other actions.
To motivate the analysis to come, we show how this approach provides microfoundations for the
RD
, the most widely studied dynamics in evolutionary game theory.

(

)
.

Following

(PPI)

.

\alpha

, a

.
If the payoff of the revising agent is lower than the comparator’s, the agent switches to the comparator strategy with probability proportional to the payoff excess

Accordingly, the

3.2.
NPPI
and the
NRD

Building on the
PPI
protocol described in the previous section, we introduce below a more general family of revision protocols under which agents give additional consideration to switches to “similar”, more familiar actions.
For example,
a commuter may be more likely to contemplate switching to a different instance of their current transport mode than a different mode altogether
(
Ben-Akiva, 1973
;
Ben-Akiva & Lerman, 1985
;
McFadden, 1981
;
McFadden, 2001
, e.g., taking a different combination of metro lines rather than switching to a bus, cf.)
.
Likewise,
when deciding to replace a computer or other technology with network externalities, a consumer may be more likely to consider models manufactured by the maker of their current machine than those of other manufacturers, particularly in contexts with switching costs
(
Katz & Shapiro, 1994
;
Farrell & Klemperer, 2007
)
;
etc.

Formally, given a similarity structure
\mathcal{P}_{0}\mathrel{}\succcurlyeq\mathrel{}\mathcal{P}_{1}\mathrel{}\succcurlyeq\mathrel{}\dotsb\mathrel{}\succcurlyeq\mathrel{}\mathcal{P}_{L}
of
\mathcal{A}
as above, we will consider a multi-stage revision protocol that unfolds as follows:

(1)

When given an opportunity to switch strategies, a revising agent playing
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
first decides which strategies to consider as candidates for imitation.
To account for the increased familiarity of more similar strategies, the pool of candidates from
\alpha
is

(13)

\cand(\alpha)=\begin{cases}[\alpha]_{0}&with probability $\lambda_{0}$,\\
[\alpha]_{1}&with probability $\lambda_{1}$,\\
\dotso\\
[\alpha]_{L-1}&with probability $\lambda_{L-1}$,\end{cases}

where
the “intra-level” sampling probabilities
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
satisfy

(14)

\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{L-1}=1

and, as before,

(15)

[\alpha]_{k}=\{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}:\mathcal{S}_{k}\ni\alpha\}

is the set of strategies of
\mathcal{A}
that are similar to
\alpha
at level
k
.

In words, conditioning on the degree of similarity between strategies, the revising agent considers
all possible switches – i.e., to
any
strategy, irrespective of similarity with the agent’s incumbent strategy – with conditional probability
\lambda_{0}
,
all switches to a strategy with at least one degree of similarity with conditional probability
\lambda_{1}
,
and more generally,
all strategies with at least
k
degrees of similarity with conditional probability
\lambda_{k}
.
Specifically, in our running example, an agent taking the bus to work would first contemplate whether they want to only consider switching to another bus line (
2
degrees of similarity),
or to some other means of public transportation (at least
1
degree of similarity),
or to consider all possible means of commuting from home to work (i.e., with no regard for similarities).

(2)

Conditioned on the above choice of
k\in\{0,\dotsc,L-1\}
, the revising
\alpha
-strategist meets another individual – the comparator – drawn uniformly at random from the sub-population of agents playing a strategy from the chosen similarity class
[\alpha]_{k}
.
Hence, if the population is at state
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the probability that a revising
\alpha
-strategist encounters a
\beta
-strategist is

\displaystyle P^{\textup{meet}}_{\alpha\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}\,x_{\beta}+\lambda_{1}x_{\beta}\frac{\one\{\beta\overset{\cramped{1}}{\sim}\alpha\}}{\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\overset{\cramped{1}}{\sim}\alpha}x_{\alpha^{\prime}}}+\dotsb+\lambda_{L-1}x_{\beta}\frac{\one\{\beta\overset{\cramped{L-1}}{\sim}\alpha\}}{\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\overset{\cramped{L-1}}{\sim}\alpha}x_{\alpha^{\prime}}}

(16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}x_{\beta\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}[\alpha]_{k}}

where, as per (
17
), the notation

(17)

x_{\beta\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}[\alpha]_{k}}=\one\{\beta\overset{\cramped{k}}{\sim}\alpha\}\frac{x_{\beta}}{x_{[\alpha]_{k}}}

stands for the relative frequency of
\beta
-strategists within the sub-population of
[\alpha]_{k}
-type strategists (i.e., the part of the population playing a strategy with at least
k
degrees of similarity to
\alpha
).

(3)

Finally, as in the case of (
PPI
), the revising agent observes the comparator’s payoff:
if it is higher than their own, the revising agent switches to the comparator’s strategy with probability proportional to the observed payoff excess;
otherwise, the revising agent skips the opportunity to switch.

Hence, putting together all of the above yields the
NPPI
protocol

(NPPI)

\varrho_{\alpha\beta}(\pi,x)=P^{\textup{meet}}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\cdot[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}\,x_{\beta\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}[\alpha]_{k}}[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+},

where, to avoid trivialities, we assume that
\lambda_{0}>0
(so all strategies are sampled with positive probability).
Then, to derive the induced dynamics, a direct substitution into (
MD
) gives

(18)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}x_{\alpha}\left[\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}x_{\beta\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}[\alpha]_{k}}[v_{\alpha}(x)-v_{\beta}(x)]_{+}-\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}x_{\beta\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}[\alpha]_{k}}[v_{\beta}(x)-v_{\alpha}(x)]_{+}\right]

so,
after rearranging,
we obtain the
NRD

(NRD)

\dot{x}_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}\,[v_{\alpha}(x)-u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)]

where

(19)

u_{\mathcal{S}}(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}}x_{\alpha\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}\mathcal{S}}v_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{x_{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}}x_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}(x)

denotes the mean payoff of the class
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.
The protocol (
NPPI
) and the dynamics (
NRD
) will be our main object of study, so
we close this section with some remarks intended to highlight their most salient features.

Remark 1
.

We begin by noting that (
PPI
) and (
RD
) are immediately recovered from (
NPPI
) and (
NRD
) respectively by taking
\lambda_{0}=1
and
\lambda_{1}=\dotsb=\lambda_{L-1}=0
.
In this case, revising agents do not take into account similarities between strategies when selecting a comparator, so it stands to reason that the induced dynamics boil down to (
RD
).

§

Remark 2
.

Moving forward, it is important to note that comparison probabilities under (
NPPI
) are always higher between similar strategies compared to dissimilar ones, and the closer the similarity, the higher the probability of carrying out a comparison.
To make this precise, define the
degree of similarity
between
\alpha
and
\beta
as

(20)

\deg(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\max\{k=0,\dotsc,L-1:\alpha\overset{\cramped{k}}{\sim}\beta\}

i.e.,
\alpha
and
\beta
are similar at level
\deg(\alpha,\beta)
but not at level
\deg(\alpha,\beta)+1
.
Then the probability that
\beta
is considered by an
\alpha
-strategist as a candidate for imitation is

(21)

P^{\textup{cand}}_{\alpha\beta}=\prob(\beta\in\cand(\alpha))=\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{\deg(\alpha,\beta)}

so
P^{\textup{cand}}_{\alpha\beta}
is increasing in
\deg(\alpha,\beta)
, just like the encounter probability
P^{\textup{meet}}_{\alpha\beta}
of (
16
).

On the other hand, the agents’ imitation rates under (
NPPI
) also depend on the payoff excess
[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}
of
\beta
over
\alpha
;
more precisely, by (
17
) we have

(22)

r_{\alpha\beta}(\pi,x)=\frac{\varrho_{\alpha\beta}(\pi,x)}{x_{\beta}}=\frac{P^{\textup{meet}}_{\alpha\beta}(x)}{x_{\beta}}\cdot[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}=[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}\cdot\!\!\sum_{k=0}^{\deg(\alpha,\beta)}\!\!\frac{\lambda_{k}}{x_{[\alpha]_{k}}}

so the total imitation rate is not always higher for more similar strategies (after all, a less familiar strategy could still be a favorable candidate for imitation, even if it is encountered with lower probability).
However, for any
given
value of
[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}
, the imitation rate of
\beta
-strategists by
\alpha
-strategists is highest when
\alpha
and
\beta
are
most
similar (e.g., different metro lines), and lowest when
\alpha
and
\beta
are
least
similar (e.g., different transport modes altogether).
This increased affinity toward similar strategies is an
intrinsic
feature of the agents’ revision mechanism and it is due to the fact that, ceteris paribus, an agent is more likely to
compare strategies with another agent if the other agent’s strategy is more similar to their own.

§

Remark 3
.

Building on the previous remark, it is also natural to ask what happens if a revising agent meets another agent from the population uniformly at random (that is,
P^{\textup{meet}}_{\alpha\beta}(x)=x_{\beta}
) but is
extrinsically
more likely to imitate strategies that are more similar to their own.
In this case, the agents’ imitation rates can be written as

(23)

r_{\alpha\beta}(\pi,x)=\Theta_{\alpha\beta}\cdot\frac{P^{\textup{meet}}_{\alpha\beta}(x)}{x_{\beta}}\cdot[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}=\Theta_{\alpha\beta}\,[\pi_{\beta}-\pi_{\alpha}]_{+}

where the imitation coefficients
\Theta_{\alpha\beta}
are of the form

(24)

\Theta_{\alpha\beta}=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\dotsb+\theta_{\deg(\alpha,\beta)}

for some
\theta_{0},\dotsc,\theta_{L-1}\geq 0
(so
\Theta_{\alpha\beta}
increases with the degree of similarity between
\alpha
and
\beta
).

The expression (
23
) for
r_{\alpha\beta}
inflates the probability of imitating a similar strategy by a factor of
\Theta_{\alpha\beta}
, so the revising agent is more likely to imitate the strategy of another agent if the other agent’s strategy is more similar to their own.
Mutatis mutandis, this observation also applies to the agents’ imitation rates (
22
) under (
NPPI
), but where the two models differ is that, in (
22
), the reinforcement of similar strategies arises
intrinsically
, as a consequence of the fact that an agent is more likely to compare strategies with another agent if the other agent’s strategy is more similar to their own.
By contrast, the reinforcement of similar strategies in (
23
) is
extrinsic
, and it occurs
despite
the fact that players meet other players uniformly at random.

The difference between these two models – intrinsic vs. extrinsic – can also be seen in the induced dynamics.
Indeed, substituting
\varrho_{\alpha\beta}=r_{\alpha\beta}x_{\beta}
in (
MD
) and rearranging yields the dynamics

(
NRD
{}_{\textup{extr}}
)

\dot{x}_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\theta_{k}\,x_{[\alpha]_{k}}[v_{\alpha}(x)-u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)]

where the subscript “
extr
” alludes to the extrinsic nature of the model (
23
).
Compared to (
NRD
), the main factor setting (

extr

) apart is that the state-
independent
coefficients
\lambda_{k}
in the former are replaced by the state-
dependent
factors
\theta_{k}x_{[\alpha]_{k}}
in the latter.
Since
x_{[\alpha]_{k}}\leq 1
, this adjustment means that the impact of the intra-class revision terms
v_{\alpha}(x)-u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)
is toned down in (

extr

) relative to (
NRD
).
In turn, this reflects the fact that comparisons to similar strategies occur less frequently in the extrinsic model (
23
) rather than the intrinsic model (
22
), so the relative ranking of strategies within a similarity class is likewise less impactful in (

extr

) than it is in (
NRD
).

The extrinsic dynamics (

extr

) are clearly interesting in their own right, but a full analysis would take us too far afield, so we do not undertake it here.

Remark 4
.

It is also worth noting that summing (
NRD
) over all alternatives in a given similarity class
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
yields the similarity class dynamics

(25)

\dot{x}_{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}}\dot{x}_{\alpha}=x_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\lambda_{k}\,[u_{\mathcal{S}}(x)-u_{[\mathcal{S}]_{k}}(x)]

where, as before,
[\mathcal{S}]_{k}
denotes the
k
-th level ancestor of
\mathcal{S}
.
These dynamics are formally analogous to (
NRD
) with
\ell
levels of nesting instead of
L
, and with a coarser similarity hierarchy that does not differentiate actions beyond
\ell
degrees of similarity.
However, the average payoffs
u_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
and
u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)
appearing in (
25
) depend on the
complete
population state
x\in\mathcal{X}
, not only on the aggregate population shares
x_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Because of this, the evolution of the population cannot be described by (
25
) alone if
\ell<L
, i.e., (
25
) does not stand on its own as an autonomous dynamical system.

§

Remark 5
.

As a final point, we note that the per capita growth rate
g_{\alpha}(x)=\dot{x}_{\alpha}/x_{\alpha}
of
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
under (
NRD
) is

(26)

g_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}\,[v_{\alpha}(x)-u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)]=v_{\alpha}(x)-\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}\,u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)

i.e.,
g_{\alpha}(x)
is a convex combination of payoff differences between
v_{\alpha}(x)
and the sub-population means
u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)
evaluated at all levels of resemblance to
\alpha
, from the maximum possible degree (when
k=L-1
) to no similarities whatsoever (when
k=0
).
An important consequence of this is that, for any pair of strategies
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

(27)

g_{\alpha}(x)-g_{\beta}(x)=v_{\alpha}(x)-v_{\beta}(x)-\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\lambda_{k}\,[u_{[\alpha]_{k}}(x)-u_{[\beta]_{k}}(x)]\,.

Clearly, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are
siblings
– that is, children of the same parent, so
[\alpha]_{k}=[\beta]_{k}
for all
k=0,\dotsc,L-1
–
Eq.

27
gives
g_{\alpha}(x)-g_{\beta}(x)=v_{\alpha}(x)-v_{\beta}(x)
, so the dynamics (
NRD
) exhibit payoff-monotone growth rates within the
finest
non-trivial similarity class.
12
12

12

Following
Sandholm (2010a)
;
Sandholm (2015)
and
Weibull (1995)
, payoff monotonicity means here that
g_{\alpha}(x)\geq g_{\beta}(x)
if and only if
v_{\alpha}(x)\geq v_{\beta}(x)
.
In view of (
22
),
Weibull (1995)
would call (
NPPI
) an “imitative” protocol, but this clashes with
Sandholm (2010a, Chap. 5.4)
, where a payoff monotonicity condition is appended to the definition of “imitative”;
for a detailed discussion of
this issue,
see
Mertikopoulos & Viossat (2022)
.

On the other hand, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are not siblings, the convex combination of class payoff differences in (
27

v_{\alpha}(x)-v_{\beta}(x)
, and with a different sign.
Thus, in stark contrast to (

), the nested dynamics (

)
are not payoff-monotonic
, a fact which poses significant obstacles to their analysis.
We will revisit this point in the next section.

4.1.

We are now in a position to state and prove our main results concerning the long-run rationality properties of (

These are as follows:

.

4.

Before discussing the proof of

On that account, it is fairly surprising to see that they satisfy a range of properties that have long been associated with payoff monotonicity (such as the elimination of strictly dominated strategies and the stability properties of

To illustrate the intricacies that this entails, consider the simple case where

4.2.
Proof outline

We close this section with an outline of the proof of
Theorems

1
and
1
(full proofs are provided in
Appendix

A
).
In contrast to standard arguments relying on payoff monotonicity, the enabling machinery for the rationality properties of (
NRD
) stated in
Theorem

1
requires the construction of a specific “energy function” generalizing the well-known
KL
(
KL
) divergence

(35)

D(p,x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\alpha}\log\frac{p_{\alpha}}{x_{\alpha}}\,.

With a fair degree of hindsight, we will instead consider the
nested
KL
divergence

(NKL)

D_{L}(p,x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}p_{\mathcal{S}}\log\frac{p_{\mathcal{S}}}{x_{\mathcal{S}}}.

where the weights
\delta_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\dotsc,L
, are given by the somewhat mysterious expression

(36)

\delta_{\ell}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{\ell}/(\Lambda_{\ell-1}\Lambda_{\ell})&\quad\text{if $\ell=1,\dotsc,L-1$},\\[3.0pt]
1&\quad\text{if $\ell=L$},\end{cases}

with
\Lambda_{\ell}=\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{\ell}
denoting the total intra-class revision rate at level
\ell
and below (recall here our normalization assumption
\Lambda_{L-1}=\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{L-1}=1
).

Intuitively,
D_{L}
can be viewed as an affine combination of the ordinary
KL
divergence between
p
and
x
at different degrees of similarity:
in particular, the
\ell
-th level terms in (
NKL
) add up to the standard
KL
divergence between the class state variables
(p_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}})_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}
and
(x_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}})_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}
in
\simplex(\mathcal{P}_{\ell})
.
As for the – admittedly opaque – choice of weights
\delta_{k}
in the definition of
D_{L}
, the specific assignment (
36
) has been judiciously chosen to provide the following fundamental link between the nested divergence (
NKL
) and the nested dynamics (
NRD
):

Proposition 2
.

Fix a state
p\in\mathcal{X}
and let
x(t)
be an interior solution of (
NRD
).
Then:

(37)

\frac{d}{dt}D_{L}(p,x(t))=\langle v(x(t)),x(t)-p\rangle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}v_{\alpha}(x(t))\,(x_{\alpha}(t)-p_{\alpha})

In words,
Proposition

2
states that, under (
NRD
), the rate of change of the nested
KL
divergence relative to a base point
p\in\mathcal{X}
is the difference between the population average payoff
u(x)=\langle v(x),x\rangle
and the average payoff
\langle v(x),p\rangle
of a group of mutants with actions distributed according to
p

(for a range of similar results in the context of game dynamics, see
Mertikopoulos & Sandholm, 2016
,
Mertikopoulos & Zhou, 2019
, and references therein).
The key property highlighted by this interpretation is that two similarity-
dependent
objects – the nested dynamics (
NRD
) and the nested
KL
divergence (
NKL
) – give rise to a similarity-
agnostic
quantity that only depends on the game and the population states involved, and is otherwise oblivious of the similarity hierarchy
\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
.

The main ingredient of the proof of
Proposition

2
is that, under the specific weight assignment (
36
), the time derivative of the nested divergence
D_{L}
becomes a telescoping sum, with only the finest (
\ell=L
) and coarsest (
\ell=0
) levels surviving in the
RHS
of (
37
).
This requires some delicate algebraic manipulations, which we detail below.

Proof of
Proposition

2
.

By the definition of the nested
KL
divergence, we have

(38)

\frac{d}{dt}D_{L}(p,x(t))=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\,\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}p_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{\dot{x}_{\mathcal{S}}}{x_{\mathcal{S}}}\,.

Thus, by substituting the class dynamics (
25
) into (
38
) and rearranging, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{L}(p,x(t))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}p_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\lambda_{k}\,[u_{\mathcal{S}}(x)-u_{[\mathcal{S}]_{k}}(x)]

(39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\lambda_{k}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}p_{\mathcal{S}}[u_{\mathcal{S}}(x)-u_{[\mathcal{S}]_{k}}(x)]\,.

To proceed, note that, for all
k\leq\ell
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}p_{\mathcal{S}}u_{[\mathcal{S}]_{k}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\mathrel{}\preccurlyeq_{\ell}\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{k}}p_{\mathcal{S}}u_{[\mathcal{S}]_{k}}(x)

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}u_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(x)\sum_{\mathcal{S}\mathrel{}\preccurlyeq_{\ell}\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{k}}p_{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}u_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(x)\,p_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}=\langle v(x),p\rangle_{k}

where the notation
\langle v(x),p\rangle_{k}
stands for

(41)

\langle v(x),p\rangle_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}p_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}u_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(x)\quad\text{for all $p,x\in\mathcal{X}$ and all $k=0,\dotsc,L$},

and
we used the facts that

\union\{\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{S}\mathrel{}\preccurlyeq_{\ell}\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{k}\}=\mathcal{S}_{k}

and

\intersect\{\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{S}\mathrel{}\preccurlyeq_{\ell}\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{k}\}=\varnothing
whenever
k\leq\ell
.
In this way, (
39
) can be unpacked one attribute at a time to yield

(
39
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\lambda_{k}\,[\langle v(x),p\rangle_{\ell}-\langle v(x),p\rangle_{k}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}\lambda_{0}\langle v(x),p\rangle_{0}-\delta_{1}\lambda_{0}\langle v(x),p\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle+\delta_{2}[\lambda_{0}\langle v(x),p\rangle_{0}+\lambda_{1}\langle v(x),p\rangle_{1}]-\delta_{2}(\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1})\,\langle v(x),p\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle\;\>\vdots

(42)

\displaystyle+\delta_{L}[\lambda_{0}\langle v(x),p\rangle_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{L-1}\langle v(x),p\rangle_{L-1}]-\delta_{L}(\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{L-1})\,\langle v(x),p\rangle_{L}

and hence, regrouping all same-level payoff terms, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{L}(p,x(t))

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}(\delta_{1}+\dotsb+\delta_{L})\cdot\langle v(x),p\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\;\>\vdots

\displaystyle+[\lambda_{k}(\delta_{k+1}+\dotsb+\delta_{L})-\delta_{k}(\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{k-1})]\cdot\langle v(x),p\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle\;\>\vdots

\displaystyle-\delta_{L}(\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{L-1})\cdot\langle v(x),p\rangle_{L}

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{L}\left[\lambda_{k}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}-\delta_{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{\ell}\right]\,\langle v(x),p\rangle_{k}

where, in the last line, we used the empty sum convention
\sum_{j\in\varnothing}\alpha_{j}=0
.

To proceed, we claim that

(44)

\delta_{k+1}+\dotsb+\delta_{L}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{k}}=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{k}}\quad\text{for all $k=0,\dotsc,L-1$}\,.

Indeed, for
k=L-1
, we recover the definition (
36
) of
\delta_{L}
.
Otherwise, assume inductively that (
44
) holds for some
k<L-1
;
then, by (
36
), we get

(45)

\delta_{k}+\delta_{k+1}+\dotsb+\delta_{L}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\Lambda_{k}\Lambda_{k-1}}+\frac{1}{\Lambda_{k}}=\frac{\lambda_{k}+\Lambda_{k-1}}{\Lambda_{k}\Lambda_{k-1}}=\frac{\Lambda_{k}}{\Lambda_{k}\Lambda_{k-1}}=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{k-1}},

which completes the inductive step and proves our claim.
Hence, combining (
44
) with (
36
), we get

(46)

\lambda_{k}(\delta_{k+1}+\dotsb+\delta_{L})=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\Lambda_{k}}=\delta_{k}\Lambda_{k-1}=\delta_{k}(\lambda_{0}+\dotsb+\lambda_{k-1})

for all
k=1,\dotsc,L-1
.
Thus, substituting (
44
) and (
46
) back into (
43
), we finally obtain

(47)

\frac{d}{dt}D_{L}(p,x(t))=\langle v(x),p\rangle_{0}-\langle v(x),p\rangle_{L}.

However, since the only

-th level similarity class is
\mathcal{A}
itself (and
p_{\mathcal{A}}=1
), we readily get

(48)

\langle v(x),p\rangle_{0}=p_{\mathcal{A}}\;u_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}x_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}(x)=\langle v(x),x\rangle

and our assertion follows.

In this section, we discuss a continuous-time model for learning in games, which reveals a deep relation between the
NRD
and the
NLC
rule of
Ben-Akiva (1973)
and
McFadden (1978)
.
To establish a baseline, we begin by describing the non-nested case in
Section

5.1
below;
subsequently, we present the details of this relation in full in
Sections

5.2
and
5.3
right after.

5.1.
Logit choice,
EW
, and the replicator dynamics

Our starting point is the following stimulus-response model of evolution in the spirit of
Erev & Roth (1998)
:

•

At each instance
t\geq 0
, the agents observe the cumulative payoff
y_{\alpha}(t)
of each pure strategy
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
up to time
t
, viz.

(51)

y_{\alpha}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}v_{\alpha}(x(\tau))\>d\tau

•

Subsequently, players select actions with probabilities that are exponentially proportional to the aggregate propensity scores
y_{\alpha}
, reinforcing in this way those actions that seem to be performing better.
As a result, in our nonatomic setting, the relative share of
\alpha
-strategists at any given time are determined by the exponential choice rule

(52)

x_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{\exp(y_{\alpha}(t)/\mu)}{\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(y_{\beta}(t)/\mu)}

where
\mu>0
is a “temperature parameter” that indicates the players’ sensitivity to changes in the observed payoffs.
When
\mu\to\infty
, players are relatively indifferent and tend to employ all strategies in a near-uniform mix;
by contrast,
when
\mu\to 0
, players are extremely sensitive to payoff changes and essentially “best-respond” to the vector of aggregate scores
y(t)
.

The main difference between this model and the revisionist viewpoint of the previous sections is that, instead of specifying the inflows and outflows to and from each strategy,
Eq.

52
directly prescribes the distribution of the population as a function of the cumulative propensity scores (
51
).
Nevertheless, despite these very different starting points, these two processes yield the same evolutionary dynamics:
indeed, by expressing
Eqs.

51
and
52
in differential form, we obtain the
EW
(
EW
) dynamics

(EW)

\dot{y}=v(x)\qquad x=\logit(y)

where

(LC)

\logit_{\alpha}(y)=\frac{\exp(y_{\alpha}/\mu)}{\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(y_{\beta}/\mu)}

denotes the logit choice map underlying (
52
).
Then, by a straightforward differentiation, we readily get the system

(53)

\dot{x}_{\alpha}=\mu^{-1}\,x_{\alpha}[v_{\alpha}(x)-u(x)]

which, up to the multiplicative factor
\mu^{-1}
, is simply the replicator equation (
RD
) induced by the revision protocol (
PPI
).

Remark
.

The
EW
scheme (
EW
) is one of the staples of the online learning literature and its origins can be traced to the work of
Vovk (1990)
,
Littlestone & Warmuth (1994)
and
Auer et al. (1995)
;
Auer et al. (2002)
on multi-armed bandits.
The specific derivation of the replicator dynamics that we presented here goes back at least as far as
Rustichini (1999)
but versions of this model have also been studied in different contexts by
Börgers & Sarin (1997)
,
Posch (1997)
,
Hopkins (2002)
,
Beggs (2005)
,
Leslie & Collins (2005)
,
Hofbauer et al. (2009)
,
Mertikopoulos & Moustakas (2010)
,
Coucheney et al. (2015)
, and many others;
for a partial survey, see
Mertikopoulos et al. (2024)
and references therein.

A key byproduct of this equivalence between (
RD
) and (
EW
) is that the latter immediately inherits all the rationality properties of the former, including – but not limited to –
Theorem

1
.
This suite of properties has contributed significantly to the popularity of (
EW
) in the literature on learning in games, so a natural question that arises is whether a similar link can be established between the nested dynamics (
NRD
) and a suitable model for learning in the presence of similarities.
We address this question in the following sections.

5.2.
NLC

Going back to the derivation of the dynamics (
EW
), a key observation is that the action selection rule (
LC
) puts all alternatives on an equal footing and is oblivious to any exogenous similarities they might possess.
Albeit reasonable in certain contexts, this feature of (
LC
) can become problematic when the inclusion of irrelevant alternatives ends up conflating the action choices of the population and lead to undesirable behaviors.

For an illustration, consider the following adaptation of the classical “red bus / blue bus” paradox of
McFadden (1974b)
.
To wit, suppose that a population of commuters can take a car or bus to work and, on average,
commuting by car takes half as long as commuting by bus.
Accordingly, if the induced propensities (
51
) for employing each alternative are, say,
y_{\textsf{car}}=-1/2
and
y_{\textsf{bus}}=-1
, the ratio of commuters taking a car over a bus under (
LC
) would be
x_{\textsf{car}}/x_{\textsf{bus}}=\exp(-1/2)/\exp(-1)\approx 1.6
in favor of taking a car, in tune with the fact that commuting by bus takes much longer.
The paradox now comes up if the company operating the bus network paints half of the buses blue and the other half red.
This change has absolutely no effect on the travel time of a bus, but since the commuters’ new set of alternatives is
\mathcal{A}=\{\textsf{car},\textsf{red bus},\textsf{blue bus}\}
, the ratio of commuters taking a car under (
LC
) would drop to

To avoid this artificial inflation of similar alternatives,
Ben-Akiva (1973)
and
McFadden (1974a)
;
McFadden (1978)
introduced the so-called
NLC
(
NLC

instead, it singles out an alternative by progressively filtering out similarities within the set of all possible alternatives, one attribute at a time.
In more detail, given a similarity structure
\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
and a profile of propensity scores

y=(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
on
\mathcal{A}
,
the
NLC
model unfolds as follows:

(1)

First, the propensity score of a similarity class
\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
,
k=0,\dotsc,L-1
, is defined inductively as

(54)

y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}=\mu_{\ell}\log\!\!\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\mathrel{}\vartriangleleft\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}\!\!\exp\left(\frac{y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}}{\mu_{\ell}}\right)

where

\mu_{\ell}>0

\ell=1,\dotsc,L

uncertainty level
relative to the
\ell

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
of
\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
.
Thus, starting with the individual alternatives of
\mathcal{A}
– that is, the
leaves

\{\alpha\}\in\mathcal{P}_{L}

\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
– propensity scores are backpropagated along
\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}

\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{\mathcal{A}\}
of
\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
.
The reason for defining the class scores
y_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
in this precise way is discussed in detail below;
for now, we only note that (
54

(2)

An alternative
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}\equiv\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathrel{}\vartriangleright\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathrel{}\vartriangleright\mathrel{}\dotsb\mathrel{}\vartriangleright\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{L}\equiv\{\alpha\}

(55)

P_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}(y)=\frac{\exp(y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}/\mu_{\ell})}{\exp(y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}/\mu_{\ell})}=\frac{\exp(y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}/\mu_{\ell})}{\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\ell}\overset{\cramped{}}{\sim}\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}\exp(y_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\ell}}/\mu_{\ell})}

for all
\ell=1,\dotsc,L

In particular, (
55

x_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}
for all classes
\mathcal{S}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
(i.e., the coarsest ones);
subsequently, once a class
\mathcal{S}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
has been selected, (
55

x_{\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}\mathcal{S}_{1}}
for all children
\mathcal{S}_{2}
of
\mathcal{S}_{1}

\mathcal{P}_{2}

x_{\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathbin{}{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}|{\nonscript\mkern-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}\mathbin{}\mathcal{S}_{1}}

\mathcal{P}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
until reaching the finest partition
\mathcal{P}_{L}
and selecting an alternative
\{\alpha\}\equiv\mathcal{S}_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{L}

In this way, unrolling the conditional probabilities (

(NLC)

P_{\alpha}(y)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\;\frac{\exp\big(y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}/\mu_{\ell}\big)}{\exp\big(y_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}/\mu_{\ell}\big)}\quad\text{for all $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}$}

The first thing of note is the precise expression (
54

The reason for this definition is the conditional probability rule (

in particular, treating the parent class

As for the uncertainty parameters
\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{L}>0

for large values of

by contrast, when

\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}

Since coarser attributes inherently come with higher levels of uncertainty, we will assume throughout that

Now, other than selecting a primitive alternative

for the relative share of individuals playing an element of

Fix a propensity score profile

1.

For all

Proof.

Our proof is by reverse induction on the level

of
\mathcal{S}

Then, assuming inductively that our assertion holds for some

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\mathrel{}\vartriangleleft\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}\;\;P_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}}(y)=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\mathrel{}\vartriangleleft\mathrel{}\mathcal{S}_{\ell-1}}\;\;\;\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\exp(y_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}/\mu_{k})}{\exp(y_{\mathcal{S}_{k-1}}/\mu_{k})}

6.

We conclude our paper with a discussion of an important link between (

To provide the necessarily context,
McFadden (1978)
presents (

15

For its part, the logit rule (

Expanding on previous work by

To set the stage for all this, recall that the logit choice rule (

\displaystyle y_{\mathcal{A}}=\log\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\exp\left(y_{\alpha}\right)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\langle y,x\rangle-h(x)\right\}.

From an evolutionary perspective, this implies that the dynamics (
