Architecture of Smart Certificates for Web3 Applications Against Cyberthreats in Financial Industry
1 st Stefan Kambiz Behfar, 2 nd Jon Crowcroft

Abstract
This study addresses the security challenges associated with the current internet transformations, specifically focusing on emerging technologies such as blockchain and decentralized storage. It also investigates the role of Web3 applications in shaping the future of the internet. The primary objective is to propose a novel design for ’smart certificates,’ which are digital certificates that can be programmatically enforced. Utilizing such certificates, an enterprise can better protect itself from cyberattacks and ensure the security of its data and systems. Web3 recent security solutions by companies and projects like Certik, Forta, Slither, and Securify are the equivalent of code scanning tool that were originally developed for Web1 and Web2 applications, and definitely not like certificates to help enterprises feel safe against cyberthreats. We aim to improve the resilience of enterprises’ digital infrastructure by building on top of Web3 application and put methodologies in place for vulnerability analysis and attack correlation, focusing on architecture of different layers, Wallet/Client, Application and Smart Contract, where specific components are provided to identify and predict threats and risks. Furthermore, Certificate Transparency is used for enhancing the security, trustworthiness and decentralized management of the certificates, and detecting misuses, compromises, and malfeasances.



I
INTRODUCTION

Blockchain Technology (BT) has come into existence with a number of promising potential applications. After the emergence of Bitcoin, BT triggered fundamental debates in multiple areas, mostly related to the possibility to design smart contracts (self-enforcing contracts) and to apply them to a range of activities, including capital formation (Initial Coin Offerings and their transformations), digital securities, and Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs). All this led to the establishment of new paradigms, including the crypto-economy and so-called Decentralized Finance (DeFi). Not surprisingly, many companies invested and established consortia in BT, channeling increasingly ingest amounts, reaching an all-time record of 26.8 billion dollar in 2022 (CBInsights [1]), despite one of the most difficult years in terms of financial losses. The cryptocurrency market created by BT has reached a total market capitalization of more than 143 billion dollar (CoinMarketCap, 2019 [2]). Moreover, Chowdhury et al. (2018) [3] have shown that BT has been applied to many sectors including the public and academic institutes. Also, governments have applied the use of blockchain in the public sector (Berryhill, 2018 [4]).

BT implements a structural decentralization, promising the establishment of a radically new way of conducting business transactions, especially among untrusted entities. Cryptography, transparency, and decentralized smart contracts and smart ledgers are the key elements supporting verification, identification, authentication, integrity, and immutability. BT provides the exchange of transactions in a vast network of untrusted entities as well as chronologically linked and duplicated digital ledgers in a decentralized database. Furthermore, it eliminates the need to rely on a centralized authority by offering independent verification guarantees. Additionally, because there are no centralized authorities, blockchain services can apply immutability to prevent abuse even in the case of a hostile insider, which increases security properties for distributed systems (Maleh et al., 2020 [5]).

Given that BT is a cutting-edge technology with a lot of potential, there are doubts about how reliable it is (Natarajan et al, 2019 [6]). If a system had such authority, then its most potent actors may collude to alter the blockchain or stop its contents from being broadcast. There have been numerous documented cyberattacks, and blockchain implementations have several cybersecurity flaws (Luu et al., 2016 [7], Taylor et al., 2019 [8]). By transferring tiny quantities of ”dust coins” to the personal wallets of the target victims, hackers were able to compromise the privacy and anonymity of Bitcoin (BTC) users, as evidenced by the attack on the Litecoin blockchain network in 2019. By undertaking a combined analysis of the addresses, hackers could discover the owner of each wallet and follow its international activity (Omelchenko, 2019 [9]).

Financial institutions are impacted by the growth of Blockchain-based financial services in a big way. Blockchain technology and smart contracts, for instance, can remove intermediaries, centralized administration, fraud, and theft, making it simpler for the transparency mechanism to reduce financial services liability (Chang et al., 2020 [10]). In the financial industry, decentralized Blockchain networks eliminate financial intermediaries by allowing each financial partner to contribute to the network via a P2P network so that direct money transfers can be made. In addition, each financial organization maintains a distributed ledger with a finite volume of financial transactions per block. In conventional banking systems, the intermediary bank was entirely responsible for coordinating all transactions between multiple organizations. Blockchain technology removes the intermediary, enabling the majority of network nodes to confirm the legitimacy of a transaction. Due to the possibility of stored transactions leaking ”sensitive and trading information,” integrating public Blockchain and distributed ledgers would be extremely difficult (see Hameed et al. 2022 [11]). In order to address the integration challenges of distributed ledgers with Blockchain technology, Wang and Kogan (2018) [12] suggested a Blockchain-based privacy preservation technique to safeguard users’ financial information. A private Blockchain network’s financial transactions are protected by homomorphic encryption and zero-knowledge proof. The majority of Blockchain systems designed for financial transactions have ”high transaction costs,” ”propagation delays,” and ”latency”. Zhong et al. (2019) [13] presented a safe and simple payment method to effectively use Blockchain-based financial system services and to ensure system security and robustness.

Web3 refers to the next generation of the World Wide Web, which is characterized by decentralized technologies and applications built on blockchain and other distributed ledger technologies. The scope of this paper covers Web3 which differs from Web 2.0, emphasizing the decentralized, trustless, and blockchain-based nature of Web3 which is conceptualized as a third-generation technology, mainly characterized by the semantics of decentralized intelligence (Behfar, 2023 [14-15]).
In a paper, Su et al (2021) [16] claimed to present the first study that analyzes and measures real-world attacks on Ethereum Dapps based upon the forensic evidence recorded on the blockchain. Chen et al (2023) [17] also presented an empirical study that uncovers unfair trades on popular DEX services on Ethereum and Binance Smart Chain. By joining and analyzing 60 million transactions, they found 671, 400 unfair trades on all six measured DEXes, including Uniswap, Balancer, and Curve.

The success of Web3 links to innovation in new security challenges created by different application architectures. A blockchain, network nodes, and smart contracts are used to handle logic and state in Web3 applications which are built in a decentralized fashion without relying on the traditional application logic and database layers that exist in Web2. Users can still update data by publishing new content or making purchases through a frontend that links to those nodes. Users are required to sign transactions using their private keys, which are commonly managed in wallets. This architecture is meant to protect user privacy and control and allow for complete transparency, open access, and immutability of transactions.

The design has security trade-offs, just like any other system. As opposed to Web2, the blockchain does not rely on actors to be trustworthy, and is more difficult to update the system to fix security issues. Although users retain identity control, there are no middlemen to offer remedies in the event of attacks or major compromises. Because wallets are still software, which will never be completely reliable, they can still leak private data like an Ethereum address (see Dang, 2022 [18]).
Due to all the above-mentioned Web3 security flaws, we propose an architecture to improve the capacity building in its digital infrastructure security. Ultimately we provide design of smart certificates (digital certificates that can be programmatically enforced) composed of various components with different layers suitable for deployment in any target domain against cyberattacks. Using such smart certificates, Web3 applications can implement best practices for secure data exchange, such as encryption and authentication, and ensure that all participants in the network are adhering to these practices. In section 2 we will discuss the research design including the research questions, and in section 3 we will provide the solution including the architecture of vulnerability detection and monitoring, usecases, and smart certificates, we will discuss the implementation in detail, and finally investigate the certificate transparency and its integration into the smart certificates.

By integrating various monitoring tools, utilizing AI for enhanced threat prediction, and leveraging the benefits of blockchain, the system provides a robust and user-friendly approach to cybersecurity, which contains:

1. Vulnerability Scanning Tool:
This tool is integrated into the solution to secure the chain component. Its primary function is to scan for potential vulnerabilities within the Web3 applications. By identifying these vulnerabilities, the system can take proactive measures to address them before they are exploited.

2. Attack Correlation Component:
This component is central to the architecture and integrates several monitoring mechanisms:

•

Social Media Monitoring: This monitors (social) media connected components for potential threats related to vulnerabilities that might affect the Web3 applications.

•

Wallet Monitoring: Given the decentralized nature of blockchain, wallet transactions and activities are monitored to detect any unusual patterns or security breaches.

•

Attacks’ Prediction: This is based on a repository of known vulnerabilities from the application and smart contract layer. By analyzing past vulnerabilities and current system states, the system can predict potential future attacks.

3. Data Flow:
The vulnerability scanning tool feeds its findings into the attack correlation component. The data from social media monitoring, wallet monitoring, and the vulnerabilities’ repository are all processed to predict potential attacks.

4. AI Integration:
The system employs artificial intelligence to enhance its prediction capabilities. By analyzing the data from various sources, the AI can determine patterns and relationships that might not be immediately apparent. This allows for a more accurate prediction of potential threats.

5. Real-time Detection:
One of the primary objectives of the architecture is to detect threats in real-time. By continuously monitoring various data sources and using AI to analyze this data, the system can quickly identify and respond to threats as they emerge.

6. Blockchain Integration:
The decentralized nature of blockchain is leveraged to enhance the security of the system. By distributing the decision rules (smart contracts) across all nodes in the network, the system becomes more dynamic and resilient. This decentralization ensures that even if one node is compromised, the overall system remains secure.

7. User-Centric Approach:
The enduser, whether human or IoT device, is at the core of the proposed solution. The system is designed to be unobtrusive, only intervening when necessary. This ensures that the user experience is not disrupted while still maintaining a high level of security.

III-B

Usecases

Here are some hypothetical use cases that demonstrate the practicality and applicability of the architecture:

Usecase 1: Social Media Threat Detection

Scenario:
An employee on the browser with a running Web3 application is connected to a social media platform with a discovered vulnerability. This is picked up by the Social Media Monitoring component.

How the Architecture Responds:
The system’s AI analyzes and determines the severity of the threat. The vulnerability scanning tool checks if the Web3 application in question is vulnerable to the discovered vulnerability. If a potential threat is detected, the system alerts the application’s security via the smart certificate and recommends mitigation steps.

Usecase 2: Suspicious Wallet Activity

Scenario:
A user’s wallet starts making multiple transactions to unknown addresses within a short period, which is unusual for this user.

How the Architecture Responds:
The Wallet Monitoring component detects this unusual pattern. The AI analyzes past transactions from this wallet and confirms that this behavior is out of ordinary. The smart certificate then temporarily freezes the wallet’s transactions and sends an alert to the user, asking them to verify the transactions.

Usecase 3: Predictive Attack Prevention

Scenario:
The Attacks’ Prediction component, using data from the vulnerabilities’ repository and current system states, predicts a potential DDoS attack on a Web3 application.

How the Architecture Responds:
The system proactively scales up its resources to handle the increased traffic. It could also deploy additional security measures, such as rate limiting and IP filtering, to mitigate the effects of the attack. When the attack occurs, the system is prepared and can handle the increased load without significant disruption.

Usecase 4: Real-time Smart Contract Breach

Scenario:
A smart contract on a decentralized application is executing transactions that are draining funds from user accounts.

How the Architecture Responds:
The real-time detection component identifies the unusual smart contract behavior. The AI analyzes the smart contract’s code and identifies a potential vulnerability being exploited. The smart certificate then halts the smart contract’s operations and alerts the developers with details about the breach and the suspected vulnerability.

Usecase 5: Regulatory Compliance Check

Scenario:
A financial institution using a Web3 application needs to ensure that all its transactions are compliant with GDPR.

How the Architecture Responds:
The blockchain integration component, with its decentralized decision rules, ensures that all transactions are GDPR compliant. If a non-compliant transaction is detected, it’s flagged, and the institution is alerted. The system also provides detailed logs and reports that the institution can use during regulatory audits.

III-C

Why smart certificates ?

Smart certificates can embed programmable logic, allowing them to automatically execute or validate certain conditions without manual intervention. This can be particularly useful for automating security and compliance checks. Web3 Application (User Interface) provides a user interface that allows users to interact with the smart certificate system. Users can issue, verify, and manage certificates using this interface.

Fig. 4:
Architecture of smart certificates for Web3 application.

In this architecture, Figure 4, when an issuer issues a smart certificate through Smart Contract 1, the certificate metadata (e.g., recipient’s name, issuer’s name, date of issuance) and the cryptographic hash of the certificate document are recorded on the blockchain. Recipients or third parties can then verify the certificate’s authenticity by checking the hash on Smart Contract 2. If a certificate needs to be revoked, Smart Contract 3 handles the revocation process. Overall, this architecture enables a secure, transparent, and decentralized approach to certificate management, leveraging the benefits of blockchain technology and smart contract for Web3 applications.

III-D

Implementation of smart certificates for Web3

Implementing smart certificates for Web3 applications involves a combination of blockchain development, smart contract programming, and cryptographic operations. Here’s a high-level overview of the steps in the implementation:

•

Blockchain Platform: Select a suitable blockchain platform that supports smart contract functionality. Ethereum is a popular choice for Web3 applications due to its robustness and support for smart contracts.

•

Smart Certificate Data Structure: Determine the data structure of the smart certificate, including fields such as certificate ID, recipient’s name, issuer’s name, certificate metadata, and other relevant information.

•

Smart Contract Development: Write smart contracts to handle the issuance, verification, and revocation of smart certificates. Create separate contracts for each functionality, such as a certificate issuance contract, a verification contract, and a revocation contract.

•

Test the smart contracts and user interface to ensure their correctness and security. Deploy the smart contracts to the chosen blockchain network, make them accessible to users. Monitor the system for any potential issues or vulnerabilities. Regularly update and improve the smart contract code and user interface as needed. It is essential to follow best practices and conduct thorough testing to ensure the security and reliability of the smart certificate system.

In a DEX context, this CD smart contract can be integrated to offer users the ability to issue, verify, and potentially trade CD tokens representing ownership of certificates. Here’s how it can be used:

Listing 1: Smart contract to define the Certificate of Deposit (CD).

// SPDX-License-Identifier: MIT

pragma solidity ˆ0.8.0;

contract CertificateOfDeposit {

struct Certificate {

uint256 id;

address owner;

uint256 depositAmount;

uint256 maturityDate;

bool isRevoked;

}

Certificate[] public certificates;

uint256 public nextCertificateId;

event CertificateIssued(uint256 indexed id, address indexed owner, uint256 depositAmount, uint256 maturityDate);

event CertificateRevoked(uint256 indexed id); }

function issueCertificate(uint256 depositAmount, uint256 maturityDate) external {

require(maturityDate > block.timestamp, ”Maturity date must be in the future.”);

require(depositAmount > 0, ”Deposit amount must be greater than 0.”);

certificates.push(Certificate(nextCertificateId, msg.sender, depositAmount, maturityDate, false));

nextCertificateId++;

emit CertificateIssued(nextCertificateId - 1, msg.sender, depositAmount, maturityDate);

}

function verifyCertificate(uint256 certificateId) external view returns (bool) {

require(certificateId < certificates.length, ”Certificate does not exist.”);

Certificate storage certificate = certificates[certificateId];

return !certificate.isRevoked && certificate.maturityDate ¡= block.timestamp;

function revokeCertificate(uint256 certificateId) external {

require(certificateId ¡ certificates.length, ”Certificate does not exist.”);

Certificate storage certificate = certificates[certificateId];

require(certificate.owner == msg.sender, ”Only the owner can revoke the certificate.”);

require(!certificate.isRevoked, ”Certificate is already revoked.”);

certificate.isRevoked = true;

emit CertificateRevoked(certificateId);

}

}

III-E

Certificate transparency

Certificate Transparency (CT), see reference [19], is crucial for enhancing the security and trustworthiness of the Internet’s Public Key Infrastructure (PKI). It aids in detecting misissued certificates, whether due to errors, compromises, or malfeasance by Certificate Authorities (CAs). CT helps prevent Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attacks by making it difficult for fraudulent certificates to go unnoticed, thereby increasing the accountability of CAs and enhancing user trust in web communications. The public logging of certificates allows for rapid response to security incidents and democratizes the oversight of certificate issuance. Researchers, security professionals, and the general public can monitor and audit certificate issuance, maintaining the integrity of the web’s security infrastructure. This broad oversight is essential in safeguarding against cyber threats and ensuring the reliability of secure internet communications, making CT a critical component in the modern web ecosystem.

Integration of Certificate Transparency (CT) into the architecture of smart certificates can be approached by focusing on the secure, transparent, and decentralized management of certificates. The architecture overview includes: Smart Contract 1, dedicated to Certificate Issuance, handles the process of issuing smart certificates, where issuers are required to provide necessary information and credentials. Smart Contract 2 focuses on Certificate Verification, ensuring the authenticity and validity of smart certificates by enabling recipients or third parties to check the cryptographic hash of the certificate stored on the blockchain. Smart Contract 3 deals with Certificate Revocation, managing the invalidation of certificates due to reasons such as fraud or expiration, with all revocation information being recorded on the blockchain. The Blockchain Network acts as the backbone of this system, functioning as a distributed ledger that records all certificate-related transactions and data, thereby guaranteeing transparency, immutability, and decentralization in the certificate management system. Complementing these components is the Web3 Application, which serves as a user interface, allowing users to issue, verify, and manage their certificates seamlessly.

There are also some specific concerns that we are working on to address in our future study:

Question:
How are false positives of the models addressed?

Response:
False positives are a common concern in threat detection models. To address this, one can employ a multi-tier validation system. Initially, potential threats identified by the model undergo a secondary validation process, which involves heuristic checks and pattern analysis. Additionally, one needs to continuously train and fine-tune the models using feedback loops, ensuring that the rate of false positives decreases over time. Regular audits and manual reviews also play a crucial role in refining the model’s accuracy.

Question:
How is response time optimized with increased work of evidence capturing and threat prediction time?

Response:
One needs to employ parallel processing and distributed computing techniques to optimize response time. By processing evidence capturing and threat prediction simultaneously across multiple nodes, one could ensure swift response times. Additionally, by utilizing edge computing principles, processing data closer to the data source further reduces latency.

Questions:
How is the centralized repo of vulnerabilities secured? How can one address concerns about it being a potential giveaway to hackers?

Response:
The centralized repo could be secured using state-of-the-art encryption techniques, multi-factor authentication, and strict access controls. Additionally, the repo does not store explicit vulnerabilities but rather signatures of vulnerabilities. This ensures that the data remains unintelligible and unusable to potential hackers.

Question:
What could be a realistic collaboration method for forming the centralized repo with many stakeholders in a decentralized network?

Response:
Collaboration could be facilitated through a consensus-driven approach. Stakeholders can propose additions or modifications to the repo. These proposals undergo a validation process where multiple nodes in the network verify the authenticity and relevance of the proposed data. Once a consensus is reached, the data is added to the repo. This method ensures transparency, accuracy, and collective decision-making.

Question:
How could we address the implementation complexity in real-time, especially considering layer-wise security?

Response:
While layer-wise security does introduce complexity, the proposed architecture is designed with modularity in mind. Each layer operates both independently and in conjunction with other layers. By employing microservices architecture and containerization, one could ensure that each component can be updated, scaled, or modified without affecting the entire system. This modular approach simplifies real-time implementation and allows for seamless integration of security measures across layers.

CONCLUSION

A significant aspect of Web3 security relies on the ability of blockchains, that are software-controlled distributed networks, to be resilient to human intervention. Here, the space of humans could involve network defenders in the form of builders, security teams, and Web3 application endusers. In order to boost Web3 security, one must first categorize and profile different cyberattack types particular to Web3 application environments. This profiling task will drive the design of enterprise tools and frameworks to secure digital infrastructures and applications built on top of Web3 technology.

The desired outcome of this study is a usability-friendly cybersecurity smart certificate for Web3 applications. Such a certificate needs to incorporate security by design features identifying major cyberthreats affecting Web3 security alongside their causes. The sources of these causes often reflect security by design features that should necessarily be integral to technical and managerial solution suites boosting Web3 application security. This article focused on specific layers, Media, Wallet/Client, Application and Smart Contract, where for each layer specific components are provided to ensure robust evidence capturing, which thereafter is processed for identifying and predicting cybersecurity threats and risks. According to Dang (2022) [18], there are at least four bases that would advance preventative Web3 security:

•

Source for vulnerabilities: There needs to be a source for known Web3 vulnerabilities such as National Vulnerability Database (NVD). In fact, Web3 needs a decentralized equivalent. For now, scattered information lives across places such as SWC Registry, Rekt, Smart Contract Attack Vectors, and DeFi Threat Matrix. Bug bounty programs such as those run by Immunefi.

•

Security decision-making standards: The decision-making model for critical security design and individual incidents in Web3 is currently undefined. How Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs), security professionals, and service providers like Alchemy and Infura work together to tackle emerging security challenges needs to be made clearer. There are useful lessons from how huge open-source communities built processes and formed the OpenSSF and CNCF advisory committees to address security issues.

•

Authentication: The majority of DApps, including the most well-known ones, do not yet authenticate or sign their API responses. This means that there is a gap in confirming that the response is coming from the intended app and that the data hasn’t been altered in any way when a user’s wallet gets data from these apps. Users must assess an app’s security trustworthiness in a world where apps do not follow fundamental security best practices.

•

Easier key management: The Web3 paradigm’s user ability to transact is supported by cryptographic keys. These keys are also hard to manage properly; entire businesses continue to be built around managing keys. The complexity involved with managing private keys is the primary consideration that drives users to choose hosted wallets rather than non-custodial ones. Ideally, further security innovation will provide users with better usability and protections for such scenarios.
