Abstract
. We establish that a generalized Hölder continuous function on an ( m − 2 ) (m-2) -Ahlfors regular compact set in ℝ m {\mathbb{R}}^{m} can be approximated by solutions of an elliptic equation, with the rate of approximation determined by the continuity modulus of the function.



1.
Introduction

Approximation of functions by some better ones is a classical topic in Analysis. We just mention the Weierstrass theorem on real polynomial approximations, the Lavrentiev-Keldysh-Mergelyan theorem on approximation of continuous function of a complex variable by polynomials, and Walsh’s results on the rational approximation, to name just a few classical results. Up to now, there are numerous studies of approximation by polynomials, analytic, and harmonic functions, with common feature of these functions being that they possess a lot of algebraic and analytic structures, which enrich possible methods of analysis (Math.Sci.Net contains several thousand references on this topic.) A detailed discussion, with a number of references, can be found in the books
[
2
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
23
]
; some most recent developments are presented, in particular, in papers by P. Paramonov, P. Gauthier and their co-operators (see, e.g., their latest publications
[
20
]
,
[
12
]
and references therein).

Along with this direction, there is an interest in approximations by means of more special objects, in particular, by solutions of rather general differential equations, which possess considerably fewer structures, therefore the approaches developed for the former type of problems fail. A fundamental result was obtained here by F.Browder in
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
, where for continuous functions on a compact set of zero Lebesgue measure, there was established the property of approximation by solutions of a very general class of differential equations, including the elliptic ones or order
{\mathbf{r}}
, having the form
{\mathcal{L}}u\equiv{\mathcal{L}}(X,D_{X})u=0.
The conditions imposed on the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
were rather permissive, namely, the coefficients of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
as well the ones of its formal dual
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},
should belong to
C^{1}
in some domain
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, with
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
possessing the property of the local uniqueness in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
for the Cauchy problem. In this setting, using advanced machinery of Functional Analysis, F.Browder proved that for any compact set
{\mathbf{K}}\subset\boldsymbol{\Omega},
such that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathbf{K}}
is connected, any continuous function on
{\mathbf{K}}
can be approximated in
C({\mathbf{K}})
by
C^{{\mathbf{r}}}
solutions of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}u=0.

This extraordinary result was later extended in the direction of further relaxing the conditions imposed on the structure of the operator and its coefficients. However, certain questions, considered in the study of polynomial and analytic approximations, remained untouched upon in
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
and further studies. Namely, these questions concern finding a relation between the quality of the approximated function and the quality (say, the convergence rate) of the approximation. For the traditional approximation methods, there are classical results of this kind. One should mention here, of course, the results by D. Jackson and S. Bernstein connecting the smoothness class of a function on an interval and the rate of its polynomial approximation. Such results have been later called ’the constructive description of functional classes.’ Further developments by S. Nikolski, V. Dzyadyk, I. Shevchuk, V. Belyi, V. Andrievski, N. Lebedev, and many others dealt with such constructive description of various classes of functions in terms of approximation by algebraic and trigonometrical polynomials, analytical and entire functions, but not by more general ones.

Relatively recently, a progress was made in finding such constructive description of Hölder classes in terms of approximation by harmonic functions, which required, as it was explained above for the qualitative approximation problem, a number of new ideas.

In the present paper, we combine two problems described above. For a compact set
{\mathbf{K}}
of zero Lebesgue measure (more exactly, an Ahlfors-David
(m-2)
-regular set), we find a necessary and sufficient condition for a function to belong to a prescribed generalized Hölder class in terms of uniform approximation by solutions of a given elliptic second order divergence form equation with Dini continuous coefficients. We use some ideas developed recently in
[
1
]
and
[
21
]
where the approximation by harmonic functions was considered. Here, it turns out that the sufficient condition in constructive terms for a function to belong to a Hölder class is proved rather elementarily. On the opposite, the proof of the necessary condition, namely that a function in the class under consideration can, in fact, be approximated by solutions of the equation, requires some tricky calculations. The advantage of our results here, compared, say, with the ones in
[
3
]
, is that we not only prove the existence of approximating solutions but present these approximations explicitly and give sharp error estimates.

To explain, in the introductory manner, our results, we recall the general philosophy of approximation of functions. Having a ’bad’ function
f
, one approximates it by ’nice’ functions
v_{\delta}
in some class. The closer
v_{\delta}
is to
f
in some metric, the worse is the approximating function
v_{\delta},
in particular, its norm in a proper space may grow while
\delta\to 0.
Quantitative approximation results specify the correspondence between
\delta
and this worsening of the approximating function. In our case, the function
f
is continuous on a compact set
{\mathbf{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

m\geq 3,
moreover, it belongs to some generalized Hölder class
C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}})
corresponding to a continuity modulus
\omega(t).
The worsening of the approximating function
v_{\delta}
is measured by the smallness of the
\delta
-neighborhood
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
of
{\mathbf{K}},
where
v_{\delta}
is ’nice’ in the proper sense, and by the size of the gradient of
v_{\delta}
on
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}.
In this setting, the main results of the paper are as follows (the detailed formulations can be found further on in the text.)

The sufficient condition:
If for a function
f\in C({\mathbf{K}})
for any sufficiently small
\delta>0,
there exists a function
v_{\delta}
on
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
such that

|f(X)-v_{\delta}(X)|\leq C\omega(\delta)\,\mbox{on}\,{\mathbf{K}}\,\mbox{and}\,|\nabla(v_{\delta})(X)|\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\omega(\delta)}{\delta}\,\mbox{on}\,{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}.

(1.1)

then
f\in C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}}).

The necessary condition
. If
{\mathbf{K}}
is sufficiently regular, for example, is an a Lipschitz surface of codimension
2
and
f\in C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}})
(or, more generally, is Aflfors
(m-2)
-regular), then for any small
\delta,
there exists a function
v_{\delta}
on
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
satisfying (
1.1
) and being, additionally, the weak solution in
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
of a fixed second order elliptic equation
{\mathcal{L}}u=0
in divergence form, with Dini continuous coefficients.

Being put together, these two results can be understood as an example of a ’self-improving’ property. Namely, if a function
f
can be approximated in the sense of (
1.1
) by
some
functions
v_{\delta},
it can be, in fact, approximated, in the same sense and with the same quality, by essentially more special functions, solutions of the prescribed elliptic equation.

Shortly on the structure of the paper. In the second section we present some known basic facts we need, concerning the Haußdorff measure and Ahlfors-David regular sets. Although the results of the paper are valid for a more general class of compact sets, the Ahlfors-David regular ones admit a more explicit and visual description. We describe here some key facts about the Green function for elliptic equations with moderately regular coefficients as well. Then, in Sect.3, we establish the sufficient approximation condition. The essential part of our further considerations is based on the construction of an extension of a continuous function from a compact set
{\mathbf{K}}
to its neighborhood, with control over the derivatives of the extension; this construction is described in Sect.4. After deriving in Sect.5 some important estimates of integrals involving the continuity modulus, in the next sections we justify the integral representation for the extended function using the Green function, and finally present the construction of the approximating solutions of the elliptic equation and establish the estimates for the solution, which justifies the necessity part of the approximation theorem.

Our reasoning concerns approximation of an individual Hölder continuous function. It is interesting to extend our considerations in order to study the metric properties of the approximating operator. While we consider the functions on a compact set which is Ahlfors
(m-2)
-regular (since it directly extends the previously studied case of a curve in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
), certain modifications of our approach enable one to handle
\theta
-regular sets of any order
\theta<{m-1}.
We plan to treat these and other related topics in further publications.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Haußdorf measure

We recall the definition of the Haußsdorff measure (see, e.g.
[
9
]
). We denote by
\wr U\wr
the diameter of the set
U\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.
Let
{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a bounded set. A finite or countable collection of subsets
U_{k}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is called
\delta
-cover of
{\mathcal{E}},

\delta>0,
if
\wr U_{k}\wr\leq\delta
and
{\mathcal{E}}\subset\cup U_{k}.
For a fixed
\vartheta\geq 0
and
\delta>0
, we denote by
{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}^{\vartheta}({\mathcal{E}})
the quantity

{\mathcal{H}}^{\vartheta}_{\delta}({\mathcal{E}})=\inf\left\{\sum_{k}\wr U_{k}\wr^{\vartheta}:\{U_{k}\}\,\mbox{is}\,\mbox{a}\,\delta\mbox{-cover}\,\mbox{of}\,{\mathcal{E}}\right\}.

It is not excluded that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}^{\vartheta}({\mathcal{E}})
equals zero. As
\delta\searrow 0,
this infimum increases and tends to a limit, which is allowed to be zero or infinity. This limit,

{\mathcal{H}}^{\vartheta}({\mathcal{E}})=\lim_{\delta\to 0}{\mathcal{H}}^{\vartheta}_{\delta}({\mathcal{E}}),

is called the
\vartheta
-dimensional Haußdorff measure of the set
{\mathcal{E}},

It is known, see, e.g.,
[
10
]
, that
{\mathcal{H}}^{\vartheta}
is a regular Borel measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

It follows from the definition that for any set
{\mathcal{E}},
there exists a unique
\theta\geq 0
such that
{\mathcal{H}}^{\vartheta}({\mathcal{E}})=\infty
for
\vartheta<\theta
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\vartheta}({\mathcal{E}})=0
for
\vartheta>\theta
. This number
\theta
is called the Haußdorff dimension of
{\mathcal{E}},

\theta:=\dim_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathcal{E}})
. Only sets of Haußdorff dimension
\theta
(but not all of them by far) may have finite positive Haußdorff measure
{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}
. Discrete sets (but not only they) have zero Haußdorff dimension.

The Haußdorff measure is a convenient generalization of the Lebesgue measure. For
\theta=m
, it coincides with the
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, up to a constant factor, determined by the dimension. For subsets in a compact Lipschitz surface
\Sigma
of dimension
\theta,

1\leq\theta<m,
the Haußdorff measure, again, up to a constant factor, coincides with the surface measure on
\Sigma
, generated in the standard way by the Lebesgue measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.
The Haußdorff measure is invariant with respect to shifts and rotations; it is also homogeneous under a dilation
{\mathcal{D}}_{r}
with ratio
r
:

{\mathcal{D}}_{r}:x\mapsto rx,r>0:

{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}_{r}({\mathcal{E}}))=r^{\theta}{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}({\mathcal{E}}).
More generally, under a bi-Lipschitz mapping the Haußdorff measure changes in a controllable way: if
c|x-y|\leq|F(x)-F(y)|\leq C|x-y|
then

c^{\theta}{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}({\mathcal{E}})\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}(F({\mathcal{E}}))\leq C^{\theta}{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}({\mathcal{E}}).

2.2.
Ahlfors regular sets

The union of a finite collection of sets having Haußdorff dimension
\theta
is, again, a set of this Haußdorff dimension. On the other hand, the union of finitely many sets of different Haußdorff dimension has Haußdorff dimension coinciding with the largest one of these sets. So, generally, a set may have pieces of different Haußdorff dimension. The following definition describes
sets which have one and the same Haußdorff dimension everywhere.

Definition 2.1
.

Let, for a point
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

B_{r}(X)
denote the open ball with center
X
and radius
r.
The set
{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is called ’Ahlfors regular of dimension
\theta
’ (called sometimes ’Ahlfors-David regular’, or, shorter, ’
\theta
-regular’) if there exist positive constants
C_{\pm}=C_{\pm}({\mathcal{E}},\theta)
such that for any
X\in{\mathcal{E}},

C_{-}r^{\theta}\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{\theta}({\mathcal{E}}\cap B_{r}(X))\leq C_{+}r^{\theta},0<r\leq\wr{\mathcal{E}}\wr.

In particular, this property is preserved under bi-Lipshitz mappings.
It can be considered as a possible convenient generalization of the notion, for
\theta=1
, of chord-ark curves, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
. Compact Lipschitz surfaces, as well as relatively open sets on them, are, obviously, Ahlfors regular. Ahlfors regular sets arise in many topics of Analysis. One can find a discussion of application of this notion, e.g., in
[
6
]
.,
[
14
]
,
[
16
]
, etc.

Lemma 2.2

Let

,

and any point

there exists a finite collection of points
{\mathcal{Z}}=\{Z_{l},\,l=1,\dots,{\mathbf{m}}\},
such that

(5^{\theta}C({\mathcal{E}}))^{-1}(Rr^{-1})^{\theta}\leq{\mathbf{m}}\leq 2^{\theta}C({\mathcal{E}})(Rr^{-1})^{\theta},

(2.1)

all points
Z_{l}
are contained in
{\mathcal{E}}\cap B_{R}(X_{0})
, the balls

are disjoint and the balls
B_{5r}(Z_{l})

The, quite elementary, proof of this statement in

]
shows also that if the set

can be constructed in such way that

simultaneously–it is only the upper estimate that is needed in our considerations. It follows also that the balls

form a covering of the

-neighbourhood

of the set
{\mathcal{E}}.

2.4.
The Green function and weak solutions

2.4.1.
Elliptic equations

In the paper we consider general second order elliptic equations. Unlike the case of analytic or harmonic approximation, the question about the behavior of fundamental solutions at infinity is rather complicated. However for our needs, it suffices to consider the operator in a nice bounded domain
\boldsymbol{\Omega};
we accept as
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
the sufficiently large ball and use the Green function instead.

We consider the second order formally self-adjoint divergence form elliptic operator in
\boldsymbol{\Omega},

{\mathcal{L}}=-\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}a_{j,k}(X)\partial_{k}.

About the matrix of coefficients

\mathfrak{a}(X)=(a_{j,k}(X))

we suppose that it is real, symmetric, continuous, moreover, it is Dini continuous, namely, for
X,Y\in\boldsymbol{\Omega},
for a certain continuity modulus
\omega_{0}(t)
such that

\omega_{0}(2t)\leq A\omega_{0}(t);\,\int_{0}\frac{\omega_{0}(t)}{t}dt<\infty,

it satisfies

|a_{j,k}(X)-a_{j,k}(Y)|\leq\omega_{0}(|X-Y|);

(2.2)

finally, it is also uniformly elliptic,
\mathfrak{a}(X)\geq\kappa>0
in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

With this operator, we associate the
sesquilinear form

{\mathbf{a}}[u,v]={\mathbf{a}}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}[u,v]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(X)\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle dX,

(2.3)

for
u,v
in the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}),
where (and further on) the angle brackets denote the scalar product in
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
and the symbol
dX
is used for the integration with respect to the
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

For a measure
\mu
in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
with locally finite variation, a function
u
with locally integrable derivative is called the
weak solution
of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}u=\mu
if

{\mathbf{a}}[u,\psi]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\bar{\psi}(X)d\mu(X)

(2.4)

for all
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Of course, if the coefficients of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
are more regular, say, belong to
C^{1},
and the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure,
d\mu=gdX,
the weak solution is, in fact the classical strong solution of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}u=g
(see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.)

2.4.2.
The Green function

An important role in our construction will be played by the Green function. The existence and main properties of the Green function in our setting are established in the classical papers
[
15
]
,
[
13
]
, see also
[
19
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
).

Let
{\mathcal{L}}=-\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}a_{j,k}\partial_{k}
be an elliptic operator in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,m\geq 3,
with the matrix of coefficients,
\mathfrak{a}(X)=(a_{j,k}(X)),
being bounded and uniformly positive,
\mathfrak{a},\,\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\in L_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}).
Then there exists the unique Green function
G(X,Y)\geq 0
such that

{\mathbf{a}}[\phi(.),G(X,.)]=\phi(X),\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}),

(2.5)

where
{\mathbf{a}}
is the sesquilinear form of
{\mathcal{L}}
see (
2.3
); moreover,

G(X,Y)\leq K_{1}|X-Y|^{2-m},\,X,Y\in\boldsymbol{\Omega},

(2.6)

and for
|X-Y|<\frac{1}{2}{\rm dist}\,(X,\partial\boldsymbol{\Omega}),

G(X,Y)\geq K_{2}|X-Y|^{2-m},

(2.7)

where the constants
K_{1},K_{2}
depend only on
\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{L_{\infty}},\|\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\|_{L_{\infty}}
(and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.)

Additionally, if the coefficients of
{\mathcal{L}}
are Dini continuous, see (
2.2
), the following estimate holds (see Theorem 3.3 in
[
13
]
):

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that the boundary of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is sufficiently regular. Then

|\nabla_{X}G(X,Y)\leq C|X-Y|^{1-m},\,|\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\leq C|X-Y|^{1-m},

(2.8)

|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq C|X-Y|^{-m},

(2.9)

for all
X,Y\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

We will use these results for the case when
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is a (sufficiently large) ball containing the compact set
{\mathbf{K}}
under consideration, therefore, the regularity conditions are surely satisfied.

The Green function
G(X,Y)
produces the solution of the Dirichlet problem:
for a function
f\in L_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}),
the scalar product

u(X)=\int_{\Omega}G(X,Y)f(Y)dY\equiv\langle f(.),G(X,.)\rangle

defines the (unique) weak solution in
\overset{\circ}{H}{}^{1}
of the equation

{\mathcal{L}}u=f.
Moreover,
by Theorem 6.1 in
[
15
]
, for any measure
\mu
on
\boldsymbol{\Omega},
having bounded variation, the integral

u(X)=(G(X,.),\mu(.))\equiv\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}G(X,Y)d\mu(Y)

represents a weak solution of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}u=\mu,
vanishing on
\partial\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

The Green function will be used in the analysis of the integral representations related to the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
and in constructing the required approximation.
The standard integral representation is valid for an elliptic equation for a domain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\boldsymbol{\Omega},
see (see, e.g., (9.3) in
[
19
]
):

u(X)={\mathbf{a}}[u(.),G(X,.)]-\int_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}G(X,Y)\partial_{\nu_{\mathfrak{a}}(Y)}u(Y)d\sigma(Y).

(2.10)

This formula involves the boundary term, usually called the single layer potential, containing the conormal derivative of
u
on a surface
\Gamma,
the boundary of
\Omega^{\prime}

\partial_{\nu_{\mathfrak{a}}(X)}u(X):=\sum_{j,k}a_{j,k}(X)\nu_{j}(X)\partial_{k}u(X)=\langle\mathfrak{a}(X)\nabla u(X),\nu(X)\rangle,\,X\in\Gamma,

(2.11)

where, recall, the angle parentheses denote the standard scalar product in
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
and
\nu_{j}(X)
are components of the unit normal vector
\nu(X)
of
\Gamma
.
It is possible that a part of the boundary of
\Omega^{\prime}
lies on the boundary of
\boldsymbol{\Omega};
in this case the integral over the corresponding part of
\Gamma
in (
2.10
) vanishes.

3.
The constructive description. Sufficient conditions

Further on, we consider the space
C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}})
of continuous functions on the compact set
{\mathbf{K}}
with continuity modulus, a concave function
\omega(t),
with decaying
\frac{\omega(t)}{t},
satisfying

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\omega(\tau)}{\tau}d\tau\leq C^{\prime}\omega(\tau),\,\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(\tau)}{\tau^{2}}d\tau\leq C^{\prime\prime}\frac{\omega(t)}{t},

(3.1)

for some constants
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a compact set with zero Lebesgue measure and let
\omega(t)
be a continuity modulus satisfying (
3.1
). For
\delta>0,
denote by
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
the
\delta
-neighborhood of
{\mathbf{K}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.
Suppose that for a function
f(X),

X\in{\mathbf{K}},
for any
\delta>0
(equivalently, for a sequence of such
\delta
tending to zero), there exists a function
v_{\delta}
on
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
such that

|f(X)-v_{\delta}(X)|\leq c_{1}\omega(\delta),\,X\in{\mathbf{K}},

(3.2)

and

|\nabla v_{\delta}(X)|\leq c_{2}\omega(\delta)\delta^{-1},\,X\in{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta},

(3.3)

with some constants
c_{1},c_{2}

(
depending, generally, on the function
f
)
. Then
f\in C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}}).

Proof.

Recall that the open ball with radius
\delta
and center
X
is denoted by
B_{\delta}(X).
For a given
\delta>0,
we take two arbitrary points
X_{1},X_{2}\in{\mathbf{K}}
such that
|X_{1}-X_{2}|<\frac{\delta}{2}.
Then both balls
B_{\delta}(X_{j})
lie inside
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
and, therefore, the straight segment connecting

and
X_{2}
lies inside
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
as well. Let
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\frac{X_{1}-X_{2}}{|X_{1}-X_{2}|}.
Then

\displaystyle|f(X_{2})-f(X_{1})|\leq|f(X_{2})-v_{\delta}(X_{2})|+|v_{\delta}(X_{2})-v_{\delta}(X_{1})|+|v_{\delta}(X_{1})-f(X_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{1}\omega(\delta)+\frac{\delta}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla v_{\delta}(X_{1}+\frac{\delta}{2}t\boldsymbol{\nu})|dt\leq

\displaystyle 2c_{1}\omega(\delta)+c_{2}\frac{\delta}{2}\frac{\omega(\delta)}{\delta}\leq C\omega(\frac{\delta}{2}).

∎

We can see that there are no requirements that the approximating functions

are solutions to some equation or whatever else. The remaining part of the paper is devoted to constructing, for a given function

and an arbitrary

such approximating functions, satisfying (

), (
3.3
), which are weak solutions of a given second order elliptic equation.

4.
The extension of a continuous function

It is known since long ago, see
[
18
]
,
[
24
]
, that a continuous function on a compact set in the Euclidean space, with a given continuity modulus
\omega(t)
, admits an extension to the whole space with the same continuity modulus. The following statement provides us with an extension which, additionally, is infinitely differentiable outside
{\mathbf{K}}
, with the first order derivative controlled by this continuity modulus and by the distance to the compact. The continuity modulus
\omega(t)
is fixed, and it is supposed to be a concave function with decaying
\frac{\omega(t)}{t},
satisfying (
3.1
)

In the following reasoning (and further on in the paper), the symbol
c
without subscript denotes constants whose value is of no importance, somehow depending on the function
f
and the set
{\mathbf{K}}
, which may vary from one formula to another one and even within one and the same formula.
For a point
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\setminus{\mathbf{K}}
we denote by
d(X)
the distance of
X
to
{\mathbf{K}}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

m\geq 3,
be a compact set with connected complement in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Let
f
be a continuous function,
f\in C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}})
with continuity modulus
\omega.
Then there exists an extension
f_{0}
of
f
,
f_{0}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}\setminus{\mathbf{K}})
, such that

|f_{0}(X_{1})-f_{0}(X_{2})\leq c\omega(|X_{1}-X_{2}|);\,X_{1},X_{2}\notin{\mathbf{K}}

(4.1)

|\nabla f_{0}(X)|\leq c\frac{\omega(d(X))}{d(X)},\,X\notin{\mathbf{K}};

(4.2)

Remark
.

In fact, for any
k>0,
the estimate
\nabla^{k}f_{0}(X)=O(\frac{\omega(d(X))}{d(X)^{k}})
holds.

Proof.

The construction of
f_{0}
goes in the following way. Let
f_{1}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
be the McShane-Whitney continuation of
f
(see
[
18
]
,
[
24
]
) such that

|f_{1}(X_{1})-f_{1}(X_{2})|\leq c\omega(|X_{1}-X_{2}|),\,X_{1},X_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

There exists (see, e.g.
[
22
]
) a smooth approximation
d_{0}(X)>0,

d_{0}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}\setminus{\mathbf{K}}),
of the distance function
d(X)
, such that

c_{1}d(X)\leq d_{0}(X)\leq c_{2}d(X),\,X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\setminus{\mathbf{K}},

(4.3)

and also

|{\rm grad}\,d_{0}(X)|\leq c,\,|{\rm grad}\,^{2}d_{0}(X)|\leq\frac{c}{d(X)},

with constant
c
depending only on the compact
{\mathbf{K}}.

We set
\rho(X)=\frac{d_{0}(X)}{2c_{2}},
where
c_{2}
is the constant in (
4.3
), so that
\rho(X)\leq\frac{d(X)}{2},

and denote
\phi(X)=\rho(X)^{-2},\,X\notin{\mathbf{K}};
obviously,

|\nabla\phi(X)|\leq cd(X)^{-3}.

(4.4)

We also set
\psi(X)=\phi(X)^{m/2}=\rho(X)^{-m};
for the derivatives of
\psi,
we have

|\nabla\psi(X)|\leq cd(X)^{-m-1}.

(4.5)

The function whose existence is proclaimed in Lemma
4.1
is constructed as the averaging of the function
f_{1}
with variable radius depending on
\rho(X).

We denote by
{\mathbf{B}}(X)
the ball
B_{\rho(X)}(X),
centered at the point
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}
and with radius
\rho(X);
this ball satisfies
{\rm dist}\,({\mathbf{B}}(X),{\mathbf{K}})\geq d(X)/2
. Let the even function
{\mathbf{h}}\geq 0,

{\mathbf{h}}(r)\in C^{\infty}_{0}((-1,1))
, satisfy

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}{\mathbf{h}}(|X|^{2})dX=1.

Now we introduce the mollification kernel
{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y),
smooth in both variables, by

{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)=\psi(X){\mathbf{h}}(\phi(X)|X-Y|^{2}),

thus,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)dY\equiv\int_{{\mathbf{B}}(X)}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)dY=1.

Using this kernel, we introduce the mollified function
f_{0}
by setting

f_{0}(X)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)f_{1}(Y)dY\equiv\int_{{\mathbf{B}}(X)}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)f_{1}(Y)dY,

so,
f_{0}
is a variable radius mollification of the function
f_{1}
with radius vanishing as the point approaches
{\mathbf{K}}
(in a somewhat different setting, the idea of using a variable radius mollification was earlier implemented by E. Dyn’kin,
[
7
]
, and V. Burenkov,
[
5
]
). The smoothness of
f_{1}
follows from the smoothness of the kernel
{\mathcal{K}}.

For a given point
X\notin{\mathbf{K}},
let
X_{0}
be (one of) the point(s) in
{\mathbf{K}},
closest to
X,

d(X)=|X-X_{0}|.

Then

f(X)-f(X_{0})=\int_{{\mathbf{B}}(X)}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)(f_{1}(Y)-f(X_{0}))dY.

(4.6)

Since for
Y\in{\mathbf{B}}(X)
we have
|Y-X_{0}|\leq|Y-X|+|X-X_{0}|\leq cd(X),
it follows

|f_{1}(Y)-f(X_{0})|\leq c\omega(d(X)),

(4.7)

in particular, for
Y=X,
we have

|f_{1}(X)-f(X_{0})|\leq c\omega(d(X)).

(4.8)

This implies that
f_{0}
is, in fact, a continuous extension of the function
f
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

Our aim now is to estimate the derivatives of
f_{0}.

For any constant
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}},
the derivatives of
f_{0}
and of
f_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}:=f_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}
, of course, coincide. Therefore, when estimating derivatives of
f_{0}
at the point
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\setminus{\mathbf{K}},
we may handle the function
f_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}
instead, using the value
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}=f(X_{0})
(where, recall,
X_{0}
is the closest to
X
point in
{\mathbf{K}}
). We keep in mind here that, by (
4.7
) and (
4.8
),

|f_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}(Y)|\leq c\omega(d(X)),\,\mbox{for}\,Y\in{\mathbf{B}}(X).

(4.9)

So, we have

\nabla f_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}(X)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)f_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}(Y)dY=\int_{{\mathbf{B}}(X)}\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)f_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}(Y)dY.

Since
|f_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}(Y)|=|f_{1}(Y)-f(X_{0})|\leq c\omega(d(X))
for
Y\in{\mathbf{B}}(X)
,

|\nabla f_{0}(X)|\leq c\omega(d(X))\int_{{\mathbf{B}}(X)}\left|\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)\right|dY.

For the derivatives of the kernel
{\mathcal{K}},
we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)=

(4.10)

\displaystyle(\nabla\psi(X)){\mathbf{h}}(\phi(X)|X-Y|^{2})+\psi(X)\nabla_{X}{\mathbf{h}}(\phi(X)|X-Y|^{2});

where

\nabla_{X}{\mathbf{h}}(\phi(X)|X-Y|^{2})={\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(\phi(X)|X-Y|^{2})\left(\nabla\phi(X)|X-Y|^{2}+2\phi(X)(X-Y)\right).

(4.11)

Using (
4.4
), (
4.5
), and the fact that
|X-Y|\leq\rho(X)
, we obtain from (
4.10
), (
4.11
) that

|\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)|\leq cd(X)^{-m-1},

and, therefore,

\int_{{\mathbf{B}}(X)}|\nabla_{X}{\mathcal{K}}(X,Y)|\leq cd(X)^{-1}.

We recall (
4.9
), which gives us

|\nabla f_{0}(X)|\equiv|\nabla f_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}}(X)|\leq c\frac{\omega(d(X))}{d(X)}.

∎

Finally, we can take a smooth function
\chi(X)\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}),

\chi(X)=1
in a neighborhood of
{\mathbf{K}}
and consider
\chi f_{0}
instead of
f_{0},
to have a continuation of
f
with compact support in
\boldsymbol{\Omega},
with all the required properties.

\omega(t)
and the extended function
f_{0}
constructed in Sect.
4
. Let, as before,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be an open ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m},
such that

Suppose that
{\mathcal{L}}
is an elliptic operator in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
with coefficients satisfying the conditions in Sect.
2.4
and let

be the Green function for
{\mathcal{L}}
in

We suppose that our compact set
{\mathbf{K}}
satisfies the following condition.

Condition A.
For any
\delta>0
there exists a set
{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta)
of points
Z_{l}\in{\mathbf{K}},

l=1,\dots,M(\delta),
such that the distance between any two points in

is not less than
\delta,
additionally, for some constant
c_{0},
the set
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta},
the
\delta

{\mathbf{K}}
is covered by the collection of closed balls
B_{c_{0}\delta}(Z_{l}),

{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}\subset\bigcup_{Z_{l}\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta)}B_{c_{0}\delta}(Z_{l})

(5.1)

and, finally, for any
R\geq\delta,
some constant
c_{1}
and for any point
{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the quantity
M({\mathbf{X}},R,\delta)
of the points of
{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta)
in the ball
\overline{B_{R}({\mathbf{X}})}
is not greater than
c_{1}\left(\frac{R}{\delta}\right)^{m-2},

in particular, the number
M(\delta)
of balls in
{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta)
satisfies

M(\delta)\leq c_{1}\left(\frac{\wr{\mathbf{K}}\wr}{\delta}\right)^{m-2}.

(5.2)

As follows from Lemma
2.2
, if the set
{\mathbf{K}}
is
(m-2)
-regular, condition
A
is satisfied. As the visual example of such sets
{\mathbf{K}}
, one can consider a compact Lipschitz surface with codimension
2
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m},
as well as a finite union of such surfaces.

Having the set
{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta),
for a fixed
c>0,
we denote by
{\mathcal{B}}_{c}(\delta)
the union of balls with radius
c\delta
centered at the points
Z_{l}\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta),

{\mathcal{B}}_{c}(\delta)=\bigcup_{Z_{l}\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta)}B_{c\delta}(Z_{l}).

(5.3)

The value of
c
in (
5.3
) will be fixed so that
c>\max(2c_{0},c_{1}),
where
c_{0},c_{1}
are the constants in Condition
A
, and we will omit it in the notation, writing simply
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta):={\mathcal{B}}_{c}(\delta).
With this choice, the set
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)
covers
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}.

Let
f(X)
be a function on
{\mathbf{K}}
with the continuity modulus
\omega
, i.e.,
f\in C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}}),
and let
f_{0}
be its extension to
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
as constructed in Section
4
, in particular, supported in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

For a fixed point
X\in(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathbf{K}}),
we denote the distance from
X
to
{\mathbf{K}}
by
\delta=d(X)
and let
\delta_{1}
satisfy

2c_{0}\delta_{1}<\delta,
where
c_{0}
is the constant in (
5.1
).

We introduce the set
\Theta_{\delta_{1}}\subset\boldsymbol{\Omega},

\Theta_{\delta_{1}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus\overline{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})}.

(5.4)

It follows that the initial point
X
belongs to
\Theta_{\delta_{1}},
this means, it lies outside
\overline{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})}.
The distance of the points in
\Theta_{\delta_{1}}
to
{\mathbf{K}}
can be estimated from below,

{\rm dist}\,(\Theta_{\delta_{1}},{\mathbf{K}})\geq c_{0}\delta_{1}.

(5.5)

In fact, to justify (
5.5
), we suppose that
{\rm dist}\,({\mathbf{K}},\Theta_{\delta_{1}})<c_{0}\delta_{1}
and consider a point
{\mathbf{X}}
in the joint boundary of
\Theta_{\delta_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})
where (
5.5
) breaks down. Let
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
be its closest point in
{\mathbf{K}},
so that
{\rm dist}\,({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{K}})\equiv d({\mathbf{X}})=|{\mathbf{X}}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}|.

The point
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
must belong to one of the balls
B_{c_{0}\delta_{1}}(Z_{l}),

Z_{l}\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta_{1}).

Therefore, for this particular
l,

|Z_{l}-{\mathbf{X}}|\leq|Z_{l}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}|+|{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{X}}|<c_{0}\delta_{1}+c_{0}\delta_{1}=2c_{0}\delta_{1}.

This would means that
{\mathbf{X}}
is an interior point in
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})
and not a boundary point, contrary to what was assumed.

The boundary
\Sigma(\delta_{1}):=\partial({\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1}))
of
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})
is a piecewise smooth surface; it consists of a finite set of domains on spheres in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m},
having piecewise smooth
m-2
-dimensional boundaries.
We denote by
\sigma
the
m-1
- dimensional surface measure on
\Sigma({\delta_{1}})
, generated by the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Since the surface measure of a sphere equals
\sigma(\partial(B_{c\delta_{1}}(Z)))=c_{m}\delta_{1}^{m-1}
and the quantity
M(\delta_{1})
of balls satisfies (

), we have

\sigma(\partial{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1}))\leq cM(\delta_{1})\delta_{1}^{m-1}\leq c\wr{\mathbf{K}}\wr^{m-2}\delta_{1}^{2-m}\times\delta_{1}^{m-1}=c\delta_{1}.

(5.6)

Further on, when estimating the expressions in the integral representations to follow, we will use the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

For the continuity modulus
\omega
as above, the following estimate holds

\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY<\infty,\,\delta_{1}>0.

(5.7)

Proof.

First of all, since the set
{\mathbf{K}}
has Lebesgue measure zero, we may replace the integration domain in (
5.7
) by
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{K}}
. We introduce the notation
\delta_{n}=2^{1-n}\delta_{1},

n=1,2,\dots;
for each
n
, consider the covering of
{\mathbf{K}}
by the balls with radius
c_{1}\delta_{n},
in accordance with Condition
A
(in particular, for an
(m-2)
-regular set
{\mathbf{K}}
, using Lemma
2.2
), and construct the corresponding neighborhoods
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{n})
of the compact set
{\mathbf{K}}
. Accordingly, the domain
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{K}}
admits the representation

{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{K}}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{G}}_{n}

where
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
denotes
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{n})\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{n+1}).
Therefore,

\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{K}}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY.

(5.8)

On the set
{\mathcal{G}}_{n},
we have
d(Y)\geq c\delta_{n}
and
\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}\leq C\frac{\omega(\delta_{n+1})}{\delta_{n+1}}.
The quantity
M(\delta_{n})
of balls composing
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{n})
, by Condition
A
, is majorated by
\left(\frac{\wr{\mathbf{K}}\wr}{\delta_{n}}\right)^{m-2},
therefore,

\operatorname{meas\,}_{m}({\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{n}))\leq c\delta_{n}^{m}\left(\frac{\wr{\mathbf{K}}\wr}{\delta_{n}}\right)^{m-2}\leq c\delta_{n}^{2},

which implies

\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq c\frac{\omega(\delta_{n+1})}{\delta_{n+1}}\operatorname{meas\,}_{m}({\mathcal{G}}_{n})\leq c\omega(\delta_{n+1})\delta_{n}^{2}.

We substitute the last estimate in (
5.8
) and obtain

\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq\sum_{n}\frac{\delta_{1}}{2^{n}}\omega\left(\frac{\delta_{1}}{2^{n}}\right)<\infty.

∎

Lemma
5.1
implies the following important property.

Corollary 5.2
.

As
\delta_{1}\to+0,
the integral in (
5.7
) tends to zero.

We will also need the following localized estimate.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X_{0}
be an arbitrary point in
{\mathbf{K}}
and let
r\geq\delta.
Then

{\mathbf{I}}(X_{0},r):=\int\limits_{B_{r}(X_{0})\cap{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1})}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq cr^{m-2}\delta_{1}\omega(\delta_{1}).

(5.9)

Proof.

We use the notations for
\delta_{n},{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
from the previous proof. Then

{\mathbf{I}}(X_{0},r)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\limits_{B_{r}(X_{0})\cap{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY.

(5.10)

For
Y\in B_{r}(X_{0})\cap{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
, we have

d(Y)\geq c_{1}\delta_{n+1},\,\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}\leq c\frac{\omega(\delta_{n+1})}{\delta_{n+1}}.

The ball
B_{r+2c_{1}\delta_{n}}(X_{0})
contains no more than

\left(\frac{r+2c_{1}\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}}\right)^{m-2}\leq c\left(\frac{r}{\delta_{n}}\right)^{m-2}

points of the set
{\mathcal{Z}}(\delta_{1})
, therefore,

\operatorname{meas\,}_{m}(B_{r}(X_{0})\cap{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{n}))\leq c\delta_{n}^{m}\left(\frac{r}{\delta_{n}}\right)^{m-2}=cr^{m-2}\delta_{n}^{2}.

The latter inequality enables us to estimate the integral in (
5.10
):

\sum_{n}\int\limits_{B_{r}(X_{0})\cap{\mathcal{G}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}\leq c\sum_{n}\frac{\omega(\delta_{n+1})}{\delta_{n+1}}r^{m-2}\delta_{n}^{2}\leq c\delta_{1}\omega(\delta_{1})r^{m-2}.

(5.11)

∎

The reasoning in the proof of Lemma
5.3
produces one more important estimate. For
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}}
and an arbitrary
\delta>0
, we have
B_{c_{1}\delta}(X_{0})\subset{\mathcal{B}}(\delta).
Therefore,

\int_{B_{\delta}(X_{0})}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}\leq c\delta^{m-1}\omega(\delta).

(5.12)

6.
The integral representation

For a fixed function
f
on
{\mathbf{K}}
with continuity modulus
\omega,
as above, let
f_{0}
be the extension of
f
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
constructed in accordance with Lemma
4.1
. The aim of this section is to justify the integral representation for the function
f_{0}
in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.
At the moment, we may not apply immediately the general formula (
2.10
) since the derivatives of
f_{0}
may have rather strong singularities when approaching the set
{\mathbf{K}}.
Therefore, a limit procedure is needed in order to justify the resulting formula.

6.1.
Integral representation outside
{\mathbf{K}}

Recall that
f_{0}
vanishes at the boundary of the ball
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

We fix some point
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}.
For a sufficiently small
\delta_{1}>0,
such that
X\in\Theta_{\delta_{1}}
(see the definition in (
5.4
)), and for the function
f_{0}
which by construction is smooth outside
{\mathbf{K}}
, we apply the formula for the integral representation in the domain
\Theta_{\delta_{1}},
which is possible since
f_{0}
is smooth in this domain; we use the Green function
G(X,Y)
for the ball
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.
The boundary of
\Theta_{\delta_{1}}
consists of two parts. The first one is the boundary of the ball
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.
Since
G
vanishes on this sphere, this part of the boundary does not contribute to the integral representation. The other part of the boundary of
\Theta_{\delta_{1}}
is the interior part, namely, the joint boundary
\Sigma(\delta_{1})
of
\Theta_{\delta_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta_{1}),
the latter being the neighborhood of the compact
{\mathbf{K}}
. Thus, for
X\in\Theta_{\delta_{1}},
we have

\displaystyle f_{0}(X)=\int_{\Theta_{\delta_{1}}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y),\nabla_{Y}f_{0}(Y)\rangle dY+

(6.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma(\delta_{1})}G(X,Y)\partial_{\nu_{\mathfrak{a}}(Y)}f_{0}(Y)d\sigma(Y).

When
\delta_{1}
is sufficiently small, namely when the distance from
X
to
\Sigma({\delta_{1}})
is greater than
\frac{1}{2}d(X)
, the Green function
G(X,Y)
and its derivative
G(X,Y)
satisfy for
Y\in\Sigma(\delta_{1})
the estimates

G(X,Y)\leq cd(X)^{2-m},\,|\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq cd(X)^{1-m},

according to (
2.6
), (
2.8
).

We are able now to estimate the integrals in (
6.1
). For the surface integral, by (
4.2
) and (
5.6
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Sigma(\delta_{1})}G(X,Y)\partial_{\nu_{\mathfrak{a}}(Y)}f_{0}(Y)d\sigma(Y)\right|\leq

(6.2)

\displaystyle cd(X)^{1-m}\int_{\Sigma(\delta_{1})}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq cd(X)^{1-m}\delta_{1}\frac{\omega(\delta_{1})}{\delta_{1}}=cd(X)^{1-m}\omega(\delta_{1}).

Estimate (
6.2
) shows that the surface integral in (
6.1
) tends to zero as
\delta_{1}\to 0.

Now we consider the volume integral in (
6.1
). With
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}
still fixed, we use estimate (
2.8
) for the gradient of the Green function and, again, estimate (
4.2
) for the gradient of the function
f_{0}.
Together with Lemma
5.1
and Corollary
5.2
, this implies that as
\delta_{1}\to 0,
the volume integral tends to

\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y),\nabla f_{0}(Y)\rangle dY.

So, we may pass to the limit as
\delta_{1}\to 0
in (
6.1
), use Corollary
5.2
, and obtain the integral representation of the function
f_{0}

outside

{\mathbf{K}}
:

f_{0}(X)=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y),\nabla f_{0}(Y)\rangle dY,\,X\notin{\mathbf{K}}.

(6.3)

Our next task is to show that this representation for
f_{0}=f
is valid for all points
in

{\mathbf{K}}
as well. For this, we need the following estimate.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}
satisfy Condition
A

and let
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}}
. Then for
r>0,

{\mathbf{I}}(X_{0},r)\equiv\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}|Y-X_{0}|^{1-m}dY\leq c\omega(r).

(6.4)

Proof.

We set
r_{n}=2^{-n}r,

n=0,\dots,

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}=B_{r_{n}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n+1}}(X_{0}).
Then the integral in (
6.4
) splits into the sum

{\mathbf{I}}(X_{0},r)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathbf{J}}_{n};\,{\mathbf{J}}_{n}\equiv\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}|Y-X_{0}|^{1-m}dY.

When
Y
belongs to the spherical annulus
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
, we have
|Y-X_{0}|\geq r_{n+1},
and, therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}\leq r^{1-m}_{n+1}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq r_{n+1}^{1-m}\int_{B_{r_{n}}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY

\displaystyle\leq cr_{n+1}^{1-m}r_{n}^{m-1}\omega(r_{n})\leq c\omega(2^{-n}r).

We take into account the properties of the continuity modulus
\omega
to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}(X_{0},r)\leq c\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega(2^{-n}r)\leq c\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega(2^{-t}r)dt

\displaystyle=c\int_{1}^{\infty}\omega(\tau^{-1}r)\frac{d\tau}{\tau}=c\int_{0}^{\rho}s^{-1}\omega(s)ds\leq c\omega(r.)

∎

Our next aim is to determine the behavior of the quantity

{\mathbf{H}}(X,\delta_{0})=\int_{B_{2\delta_{0}}(X)}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}|X-Y|^{1-m}dY

(6.5)

as the point
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}
approaches
{\mathbf{K}},
while
\delta_{0}=d(X).
Note that in (
6.5
), the ball where the integration is performed, although having center
X
outside
{\mathbf{K}}
, still may contain a certain portion of
{\mathbf{K}}.
Therefore, for
Y\in{\mathbf{K}},
in other words, when
d(Y)=0,
we set formally
\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}=0,
so that the integral in (
6.5
) makes sense.

Lemma 6.2
.

For
X\notin{\mathbf{K}},

{\mathbf{H}}(X,\delta_{0})\leq c\omega(\delta_{0}).

(6.6)

Proof.

We consider two domains in the ball
B_{2\delta_{0}}(X)
, where separate estimates will be performed.

If
Y
belongs to the ball
B_{\delta_{0}/2}(X),
we have
d(Y)\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}.
Therefore, for the integral
{\mathbf{I}}^{(1)}
over this ball, the estimate holds

{\mathbf{I}}^{(1)}\leq\frac{\omega(\delta_{0}/2)}{\delta_{0}}\int_{B_{\delta_{0}/2}(X)}|X-Y|^{1-m}dY\leq c\omega(\delta_{0}/2)\leq c\omega(\delta_{0}).

For the integral
{\mathbf{I}}^{(2)}
over the remaining part of the ball
B_{2\delta_{0}}(X)
, the points
X,Y
are controllably separated,
|X-Y|\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}.
Let
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}}
be a point in
{\mathbf{K}}
such that
|X-X_{0}|=\delta_{0}
. Then the ball
B_{2\delta_{0}}(X)
is contained in the ball
B_{4\delta_{0}}(X_{0})
, and therefore, by Lemma
6.1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}^{(2)}=\int\limits_{B_{2\delta_{0}}(X)\setminus B_{\delta_{0}/2}(X)}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}|X-Y|^{1-m}dY

\displaystyle\leq c\delta_{0}^{1-m}\int_{B_{4\delta_{0}}(X_{0})}\frac{\omega(\delta(Y))}{d(Y)}dY

\displaystyle\leq c\delta_{0}^{1-m}\delta_{0}^{m-1}\omega(2\delta_{0})\leq c\omega(\delta_{0}).

We sum these estimates for
{\mathbf{I}}^{(1)}
and
{\mathbf{I}}^{(2)}
to obtain (
6.6
).
∎

6.2.
Integral representation on
{\mathbf{K}}

We define the function
f_{0}^{*}
on the compact
{\mathbf{K}}
in the following way: for a point
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}},
we set

f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})={\mathbf{a}}[f_{0}(.),G(X_{0},.)]\equiv\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\langle\nabla f_{0}(Y),\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY.

(6.7)

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
X\notin{\mathbf{K}},

X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}},

|X-X_{0}|=d(X)=\delta.

Then

|f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f_{0}(X)|\leq c\omega(\delta).

(6.8)

Similarly, for
X_{0},X_{1}\in{\mathbf{K}},

|X_{0}-X_{1}|=\delta,

|f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f_{0}^{*}(X_{1})|\leq c\omega(\delta).

(6.9)

Proof.

We present the proof of (
6.8
). The reasoning establishing (
6.9
) is quite similar.

We set
r_{n}=2^{n+1}\delta,

n\geq 1.
Since the function
f_{0}
is smooth outside
{\mathbf{K}}
, at the points
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}
the integral representation (
6.1
) is valid. The aim of this lemma consists in establishing that this integral representation is valid for all points in
{\mathbf{K}}
as well. So, we have

\displaystyle f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f_{0}(X)=

(6.10)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY-\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle dY=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}(G(X_{0},Y))\rangle dY-\int\limits_{B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle dY+

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\limits_{B_{r_{n}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n-1}}(X_{0})}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}(G(X_{0},Y)-G(X,Y))\rangle dY

\displaystyle\equiv(I_{0}(X_{0})-I_{0}(X))+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(I_{n}(X_{0})-I_{n}(X)).

We recall the estimates (
2.8
) for the Green function
G(X,Y):

|\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq c|X-Y|^{1-m},\,|\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)|\leq c|X_{0}-Y|^{1-m}.

Therefore, for the first term on the last line in (
6.10
), we have, by Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.2
,

\displaystyle I_{0}(X_{0})\leq c\int_{B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}|Y-X_{0}|^{1-m}|\nabla f_{0}(Y)|dY\leq

(6.11)

\displaystyle c\int\limits_{B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}|Y-X_{0}|^{1-m}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq c\omega(r_{0})\leq c\omega(\delta),\,\mbox{and,}\,\mbox{similarly,}

\displaystyle I_{0}(X)\leq c\int{B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}|Y-X_{0}|^{1-m}|\nabla f_{0}(Y)|dY\leq

\displaystyle c\int\limits_{B_{2r_{0}}(X)}|Y-X|^{1-m}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq c\omega(\delta).

To handle the sum on the last line in (
6.10
), we use the estimate (
2.9
) for the Green function, which implies

|\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)-\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq c|X_{0}-X|\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}|Y-(\tau X+(1-\tau)X_{0})|^{-m}.

For
Y\notin B_{r_{n}}(X_{0})
,
n>1
, we have
|Y-X_{0}|\geq 2\delta=|X-X_{0}|,
therefore,

|Y-(\tau X+(1-\tau)X_{0})|\geq|Y-X_{0}|-(1-\tau)|X-X_{0}|\geq|Y-X_{0}|-\delta\geq\frac{1}{2}|X-X_{0}|,

which implies

|\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)-\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq c\delta|Y-X_{0}|^{-m}.

As a result,

\displaystyle|I_{n}(X_{0})-I_{n}(X))|\leq c\delta\int\limits_{B_{r_{n}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n-1}}(X_{0})}|Y-X_{0}|^{-m}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq

\displaystyle c\delta(2^{n}\delta)^{-m}\frac{\omega(2^{n}\delta)}{2^{n}\delta}\int_{B_{r_{n}}}dY=c\delta(2^{n}\delta)^{-m}\frac{\omega(2^{n}\delta)}{2^{n}\delta}\times(2^{n}\delta)^{m}=c2^{-n}\omega(2^{n}\delta)

Therefore, for the sum in (
6.10
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1}^{\infty}(I_{n}(X_{0})-I_{n}(X))\leq c\sum_{1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\omega(2^{n}\delta)\leq c\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(2^{t}\delta)}{2^{t}}dt=

(6.12)

\displaystyle c\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(\delta\varsigma)}{\varsigma^{2}}d\varsigma=c\delta\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}}d\upsilon\leq c\omega(\delta),

by (
3.1
).

The substitution of (
6.11
) and (
6.12
) into (
6.10
) gives (
6.8
) and thus proves the Lemma.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
X_{0}
be a point
{\mathbf{K}}.
Let
f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})
be constructed as in (
6.7
). Then

f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})=f(X_{0}).

Proof.

We take an arbitrary small
\delta>0
and find a point
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}
such that
\frac{\delta}{2}\leq|X-X_{0}|\leq\delta
(such point, of course, exists since
{\mathbf{K}}
possesses no interior points.) Let, further,
X_{1}\in{\mathbf{K}}
be (one of) the closest point(s) in
{\mathbf{K}}
to
X
and we set
d(X)=|X_{1}-X|
. Then
d(X)\leq\delta
and
|X_{0}-X_{1}|\leq|X_{0}-X|+|X_{1}-X|\leq 2\delta.
By Theorem
6.3
,

|f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f_{0}(X)|\leq|f_{0}^{*}(X_{0}))-f^{*}_{0}(X_{1})|+|f_{0}^{*}(X_{1})-f_{0}(X)|\leq c\omega(\delta).

We recall now that the function
f_{0}
satisfies
|f(X_{0})-f_{0}(X)|\leq c\omega(\delta),
according to the way how
f_{0}
was constructed. Therefore,

|f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f(X_{0})|\leq|f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f_{0}(X)|+|f_{0}(X)-f(X_{0})|\leq c\omega(\delta).

Since
\delta>0
was chosen arbitrarily, it follows that
f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-f(X_{0})=0.

∎

Thus, we have established that the integral representation (
6.3
) is valid not only for
X\notin{\mathbf{K}}
but for
X\in{\mathbf{K}}
as well, this means on the whole domain
\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

7.
Construction of the approximating function

7.1.
Formulation of main theorem

The aim of this section is to prove the approximation theorem.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

m\geq 3,
be a compact set satisfying Condition
A
. Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be an elliptic operator in the neighborhood of
{\mathbf{K}},
satisfying conditions of Sect.
2.4.2
. Suppose that the function
f(X),

X\in{\mathbf{K}},
belongs to
C^{\omega}({\mathbf{K}}),
where
\omega
is a continuity modulus as above. Then for some constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0,
for any
\delta>0
there exists a function
v_{\delta}\in H^{1}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
, which is a weak solution of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}v_{\delta}=0
in the
\delta
-neighborhood
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
of
{\mathbf{K}},
satisfies the estimate
|\nabla v_{\delta}|\leq c_{2}\frac{\omega(\delta)}{\delta}
in
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
, and it approximates
f
on
{\mathbf{K}}
:

|f(X)-v_{\delta}(X)|\leq c_{3}\omega(\delta),\,X\in{\mathbf{K}}.

It stands to reason that if the set
{\mathbf{K}}
is
\theta
-regular for
\theta=m-2
, the Condition
A
is satisfied, therefore, the inference of Theorem
7.1
is valid.

7.2.
The approximation

Having
\delta
fixed,
we introduce the function

v_{\delta}(X)=\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle dY.

(7.1)

The property of main importance for our studies is the following.

Lemma 7.2
.

The function
v_{\delta}
is a weak solution of the equation
{\mathcal{L}}v_{\delta}=0
in
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta).

Proof.

By the definition of a weak solution, see (
2.4
) with
\mu=0
in
{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)
, it suffices to show that for any function
\varpi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}}(\delta)),

{\mathbf{a}}[v_{\delta},\varpi]=0,

where
{\mathbf{a}}
is the sesquilinear form (
2.3
).
So, we take an arbitrary function
\varpi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}}(\delta))
and obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}[v_{\delta},\varpi]=\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(X)\nabla_{X}v_{\delta}(X),\nabla\varpi(X)\rangle dX=

(7.2)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(X)\nabla_{X}\left(\langle\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle dY\right),\nabla\varpi(X)\rangle dX=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(X)\left(\langle\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}G(X,Y)\rangle dY\right),\nabla\varpi(X)\rangle dX.

We change the order of integration in (
7.2
):

{\mathbf{a}}[v_{\delta},\varpi]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}{\mathbf{a}}[G(.,Y),\varpi(.)]\rangle dY

Due to the main property of the Green function (Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
, see (
2.5
)), we have

{\mathbf{a}}[G(.,Y),\varpi(.)]=\varpi(Y).

Therefore,

{\mathbf{a}}[v_{\delta},\varpi]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla\varpi(Y)\rangle dY,

and the last expression equals zero since
\varpi(Y)=0
on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta).

∎

7.3.
The approximation estimate

Next, for
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}}
, it follows from (
7.1
) that

f(X_{0})-v_{\delta}(X_{0})=f_{0}^{*}(X_{0})-v_{\delta}(X_{0})=\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY.

(7.3)

To estimate the quantity in (
7.3
), we set again
r_{n}=2^{n}\delta,

n=0,1,\dots,
denote
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}={\mathcal{B}}(\delta)\bigcap(B_{r_{n}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n-1}}(X_{0})),
and consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY=

(7.4)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)\cap B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY.

We estimate separately the terms in (
7.4
). First, according to estimate (
2.8
) for the derivative of the Green function,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)\cap B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X_{0},Y)\rangle dY\right|\leq

(7.5)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}|Y-X_{0}|^{1-m}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq c\omega(\delta),

by Lemma
6.1
. Further, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle dY\right|\leq cr_{n-1}^{1-m}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{n}}\frac{\omega(\delta(Y))}{\delta(Y)}dY\leq

(7.6)

\displaystyle cr_{n-1}^{1-m}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)\bigcap B_{r_{n}(X_{0})}}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\leq cr_{n}^{1-m}r_{n}^{m-2}\delta\omega(\delta)=cr_{n}^{-1}\delta\omega(\delta)=c2^{-n}\omega(\delta),

the last inequality following from (
5.9
). As a result, the sum on the last line in (
7.4
) is bounded by

c\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n}\omega(\delta)=c\omega(\delta).

(7.7)

We, finally, collect (
7.4
), (
7.5
), (
7.6
), (
7.7
), which gives us

|f(X_{0})-v_{\delta}(X_{0})|\leq c\omega(\delta).

(7.8)

7.4.
Gradient estimate

For any points
X
in the
\delta
-neighborhood
{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta}
of
{\mathbf{K}}
and
Y\in\partial{\mathcal{B}}(\delta),
we have
|Y-X|\geq(c_{1}-1)\delta,
by the triangle inequality.

We set here
r_{n}=3c_{1}2^{n}\delta,

n=0,1,\dots.
For
X\in{\mathbf{K}}_{\delta},
we estimate derivatives of the function
v_{\delta}(X)
(defined in (
7.1
)), starting with

\nabla_{X}v_{\delta}(X)=\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta)}\mathfrak{A}[X,Y]dY,

(7.9)

where the integrand
\mathfrak{A}[X,Y]
denotes

\mathfrak{A}[X,Y]\equiv\nabla_{X}\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{a}(Y)\nabla_{Y}f_{0}(Y),\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)\rangle.

Therefore,

|\nabla_{X}v_{\delta}(X)|\leq\left|\int\limits_{(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta))\cap B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})}\mathfrak{A}[X,Y]dY\right|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\int\limits_{{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}\mathfrak{A}[X,Y]dY\right|,

(7.10)

where
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{K}}
is (one of) the point(s) in
{\mathbf{K}}
lying on the distance
\delta
from
X
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
is a shorthand for

{\mathcal{S}}_{n}\equiv(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta))\cap(B_{r_{n}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n-1}}(X_{0}))

We recall the estimate (
2.9
) for the double gradient of the Green function. It implies

|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq cr_{0}^{-m},\,\mbox{for}\,Y\in(\Omega\setminus{\mathcal{B}}(\delta))\cap B_{r_{0}}(X_{0})

and

|\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}G(X,Y)|\leq cr_{n}^{m},\,\mbox{for}\,Y\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}.

As a result, the inequalities (
7.9
), (
7.10
) and the estimate (
4.2
) for the gradient of the function
f_{0}
give us

\displaystyle|\nabla v_{\delta}(X)|\leq c\left(r_{0}^{-m}\int_{B_{r_{0}}(X)}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{-m}\int\limits_{B_{r_{n}}(X)}\frac{\omega(d(Y))}{d(Y)}dY\right)\leq

\displaystyle c\left(r_{0}^{-m}\rho_{0}^{m-1}\omega(r_{0})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{0}^{-m}r_{n}^{m-1}\omega(r_{n})\right)=

\displaystyle c\left(\frac{\omega(r_{0})}{r_{0}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(r_{n})}{r_{n}}\right)=\frac{c}{\delta}\left(\omega(3c_{1}\delta)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(3\cdot 2^{n}\delta)}{3\cdot 2^{n}c_{1}}\right)\leq c\frac{\omega(\delta)}{\delta},

due to the calculation as in the last line in (
6.12
).

The results of the last two subsections show that the function
v_{\delta}
does in fact satisfy all requirements claimed; this concludes the proof of our main theorem.
