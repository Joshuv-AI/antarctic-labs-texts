RSK in last passage percolation: a unified approach
Duncan Dauvergne, Mihai Nica, Bálint Virág

Abstract
We present a version of the RSK correspondence based on the Pitman transform and geometric considerations. This version unifies ordinary RSK, dual RSK and continuous RSK. We show that this version is both a bijection and an isometry, two crucial properties for taking limits of last passage percolation models. We use the bijective property to give a non-computational proof that dual RSK maps Bernoulli walks to nonintersecting Bernoulli walks.

Introduction

The Robinson-Schensted-Knuth (RSK) correspondence originates in the study of representations of the symmetric group,
Robinson 1938
. In probability, it has been mainly used for understanding different models of two-dimensional directed last passage percolation.

Across these models, several versions of RSK have been used in the literature. RSK has also been used to construct the directed landscape, an object that is expected to be the universal limit of such models, see
Dauvergne et al. 2018
. The goal of this paper is to introduce a version of RSK that unifies three commonly used versions: ordinary RSK, dual RSK, and continuous RSK. We use this unified setting to prove some basic important properties of RSK: bijectivity and isometry.
The results in this paper are central to establishing the scaling limit of the longest increasing subsequence and other KPZ models in
Dauvergne and Virág 2021
. Our approach keeps probability applications in mind and avoids representation theory concepts entirely. We work with a global perspective, using last passage values as opposed to local bumping algorithms.

One important new contribution of this work is in understanding an infinite-time version of the RSK bijection. Surprisingly, this version turns out to be much simpler than ordinary RSK! We also obtain parallel descriptions for RSK and its inverse, allowing us to give a purely global geometric description for the RSK inverse map.

Background

A version of RSK first appeared in
Robinson 1938
. The bijection was later extended in
Schensted 1961
, and in
Knuth 1970
. Classical treatments of RSK can be found in
Stanley 1999
,
Fulton 1997
,
Romik 2015
and
Sagan 2013
.
Schensted 1961
and
Greene 1974
tied RSK to longest increasing subsequences, and therefore last passage percolation, see
Vershik and Kerov 1977
and
Logan and Shepp 1977
. We use Greene’s description as the
definition
of RSK. An independent line of research started with the discovery of Pitman’s
2M-X
theorem,
Pitman 1975
. The two ideas were first unified in depth in
Biane et al. 2005
. That work has versions of many of the results presented here, and is rooted in representation theory – part of our goal is to give a treatment where concepts of representation theory are not prerequisite.

Versions of the isometry property and its strengthening (Proposition
3.12
.(i)) were shown in
Noumi and Yamada 2002
, and also in
Biane et al. 2005
, and
Dauvergne et al. 2018
. Theorem
1.1
(ii) is more classical, and can be shown with a path-crossing argument.

There are other generalizations for RSK. Geometric RSK is a finite temperature version of ordinary RSK initiated by
Kirillov 2001
, see also
Noumi and Yamada 2002
,
Corwin et al. 2014
.
Noumi and Yamada 2002
have finite and zero temperature versions of many of the results presented here, obtained using matrix methods.

In particular, isometry in the geometric setting was shown in
Noumi and Yamada 2002
, see also
Corwin 2020
and
Dauvergne 2020
. Further extensions, including randomized versions are studied in
O’Connell and Pei 2013
,
Bufetov and Matveev 2018
,
Garver et al. 2018
,

Aigner and Frieden 2020
, and
Dauvergne 2020
.

2.1
Basic definitions

Recall that a function

\lim_{y\to x^{+}}f(y)=f(x),\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\lim_{y\to x^{-}}f(y)\text{ exists }.

(4)

Basic geometric properties

Next, we collect some basic geometric facts about last passage paths. We start by showing that disjoint optimizers always exist. As in the previous section,
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}

Lemma 2.2

Lemma

Lemma 2.3

For any

Next, we give a metric composition law. The proof is again immediate from the definitions. For this lemma and in the sequel, we use the shorthand notation

(t,\mathbf{m})=\Big((t,m_{1}),\dots,(t,m_{k})\Big)\quad\text{and similarly}\quad(\mathbf{x},\ell)=\Big((x_{1},\ell),\dots,(x_{k},\ell)\Big).

Lemma 2.4

(Metric composition law)
.

Let

, let
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=(\mathbf{x},\ell;\mathbf{y},m)\in([0,\infty)\times\{1,\dots,n\})^{k}

Lemma 2.5

Let

Let

(8)

Since
|\tau^{\ell}|_{f},|\tau^{r}|_{f}\leq f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]
by definition of
f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]
, (
8
) can only hold if both
\tau^{r},\tau^{\ell}
are optimizers from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
. Therefore
\tau^{\ell}\in S
so by the minimality of
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
we have
\pi=\tau^{\ell}=\pi^{\prime}
. Therefore
S
contains a unique minimal element, the leftmost optimizer. The existence of the rightmost optimizer follows by a symmetric argument.
∎

3
The melon

Recall that the melon
Wf\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
of a function
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
is given by

Wf_{k}(y)=f[(0,n)^{k}\to(y,1)^{k}]-f[(0,n)^{k-1}\to(y,1)^{k-1}]

(9)

with the convention that
f[p^{0}\to q^{0}]=0
.
We say that
f,g\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
are
isometric
if

f[(\mathbf{x},n)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]=g[(\mathbf{x},n)\to(\mathbf{y},1)]

for all
k
-tuples
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
. In other words, last passage values between the top and bottom boundaries in the environments defined by
f
and
g
are equal. Isometry is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{D}^{n}
, which we denote by
f\sim g
.

The main goal of the section is to show that
f
is isometric to
Wf
. To do this, we will show that
Wf
agrees with iterated applications of
2
-line melon maps to
f
, alternately known as
Pitman transforms
.

3.1
The Pitman transform

From the definition of the melon map for
n=2
we immediately get the following result.

Lemma 3.1

(The Pitman transform)
.

For
f\in\mathcal{D}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle Wf_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f[(0,2)\to(x,1)]

(10)

\displaystyle Wf_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)-Wf_{1}(x).

(11)

Our main goal in Section
3.1
is to prove that in the
2
-line case,
Wf\sim f
. The first step is
Lemma
3.2
, which shows that
W
preserves single-point last passage values. This has the most technical proof in the paper, and consists of careful manipulations of the definitions. The proof is complicated by the fact that the function
f
is only cadlag, rather than continuous, see Lemma 4.2 in
Dauvergne et al. 2018
for the easier continuous case. We defer to the proof to the Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The map
W
maps
\mathcal{D}^{2}
to itself. Moreover, for
f\in\mathcal{D}^{2}
and
0\leq x\leq y
, we have that

f\big[(x,2)\to(y,1)\big]=Wf\big[(x,2)\to(y,1)\big]

(12)

Lemma
3.2
and the definition of
W
allow us to identify both the image of the two-line map
W
, and the fact that it is idempotent.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that two functions
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
are
Pitman ordered
, if

f_{2}(t)\leq f_{1}(t^{-})\quad\text{for all }t.

When this is the case, we will write
f_{2}\preceq f_{1}
.

Remark 3.4
.

With this definition the set
\mathcal{D}_{\uparrow}^{n}
introduced in Theorem
1.1
(iii) can be written simply as
\mathcal{D}_{\uparrow}^{n}=\{f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}:f_{n}\preceq\ldots\preceq f_{1}\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{n}
. Since the lines are ordered in this way, it is clear that for
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
there is a disjoint optimizer from
(0,n)^{k}\to(y,1)^{k}
following the leftmost possible paths, i.e.
f_{1}(x)+\ldots+f_{k}(x)=f[(0,n)^{k}\to(x,1)^{k}]
. In short,

Wf=f

(13)

for
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f=(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathcal{D}^{2}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

(Wf)_{1}=f_{1}
,

(ii)

(Wf)_{2}=f_{2}
,

(iii)

Wf=f
,

(iv)

f_{2}\preceq f_{1}
.

Moreover, the Pitman transform is idempotent,
W^{2}=W
and the the image of Pitman transform is precisely the set of all Pitman ordered functions:

W(\mathcal{D}^{2})=\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\uparrow}=\{f\in\mathcal{D}^{2}:f_{2}\preceq f_{1}\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
Wf_{1}+Wf_{2}=f_{1}+f_{2}
. This gives the equivalence of (i), (ii) and (iii). For (iv), observe that
f_{2}\preceq f_{1}
if and only if

f[(0,2)\to(x,1)]=f_{1}(x)+\sup_{0\leq y\leq x}f_{2}(y)-f_{1}(y^{-})=f_{1}(x)

for all
x
, or in other words
Wf_{1}=f_{1}
, giving the equivalnce of (i) and (iv).
Lemma
3.2
then implies the claims about idempotency and image.
∎

Next, we extend Lemma
3.2
to deal with disjoint
k
-tuples. For the proof, we need the notion of a path from
p^{+}
to
q
. This is defined the same way as the path
\pi
from
p=(x,n)
to
q=(y,m)
as in (
5
), with the vertical line

\{x\}\times\{m,\ldots,n\}
removed. The last passage value

f[p^{+}\to q]

is the supremum of
|\pi|_{f}
over all paths
\pi
from
p^{+}
to
q
.
Analogously, we define paths from
p
to
q^{-}
and
p^{+}
to
q^{-}
. paths, and last passage values
f[p\to q^{-}]
and
f[p^{+}\to q^{-}]
. We can think of the last passage value
f[p\to q]
as
f[p^{-}\to q^{+}]
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
p=(x,2)
,
q=(y,1)
with
0\leq x<y
. Then

Wf[p^{+}\to q]=f[p^{+}\to q],\quad Wf[p\to q^{-}]=f[p\to q^{-}],\quad Wf[p^{+}\to q^{-}]=f[p^{+}\to q^{-}].

Proof.

Since the functions
f_{i}
and
Wf_{i}
are cadlag, we have

f[p^{+}\to q]=\lim_{z\downarrow x}f[(z,2)\to q],\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad Wf[p^{+}\to q]=\lim_{z\downarrow x}Wf[(z,2)\to q].

Lemma
3.2
then implies the first claim. The others are proven analogously.
∎

Lemma 3.7

(Isometry of the Pitman transform)
.

For
f\in\mathcal{D}^{2},

Wf\sim f
. That is, for every pair of
k
-tuples
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=(\mathbf{x},2;\mathbf{y},1)
, we have

f\big[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\big]=Wf[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}].

Proof.

Define
N:\mathbb{R}\to\{0,1,\dots\}
by
N(z)=\#\{i:z\in[x_{i},y_{i}]\}
. Disjointness of a
k
-tuple
\pi
from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
implies the following.

•

Let
Z=N^{-1}(\{2,\dots\})
, that is the set of points
z
that are contained in at least two intervals
[x_{i},y_{i}]
. Then
Z\times\{1,2\}\subset\cup\pi
.

•

Let
I_{1},\dots,I_{\ell}
be the connected components of
N^{-1}(1)
, let
\hat{a}_{j}\leq\hat{b}_{j}
be the endpoints of
I_{j}
and set
a_{j}=(\hat{a}_{j},2),b_{j}=(\hat{b}_{j},1)
. Then
I_{j}\times\{1,2\}\cap(\cup\pi)
is a path from
a_{j}^{*}
to
b_{j}^{*}
for all
j
. Here
*\in\{+,-\}
, depending on whether the corresponding end of
I_{j}
is open or closed.

•

(\cup\pi)\cap(N^{-1}(\{0\})\times\{1,2\})=\emptyset.

Therefore letting
\rho_{i}
be the path
I_{j}\times\{1,2\}\cap(\cup\pi)
from
a_{j}^{*}
to
b_{j}^{*}
, we can write

\cup\pi=Z\times\{1,2\}\,\cup\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}\rho_{i}.

(14)

Moreover, given arbitary
a_{i}^{*}\to b_{i}^{*}
paths
\rho_{i}
, there is a
\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}
path
\pi
so that (
14
) holds. Therefore

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=df(Z\times\{1,2\})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f[a_{i}^{*}\to b_{i}^{*}].

By the preservation of the sum
f_{1}+f_{2}
, the first term doesn’t change when we apply
W
. By Corollary
3.6
, nor does the remaining sum.
∎

3.2
Repeated Pitman transforms, cars, and the melon

Next, we build the
n
-line melon map. We first extend the Pitman transform to functions
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
by applying it to two lines at a time. For
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, define
\sigma_{i}:\mathcal{D}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}^{n}
by

\sigma_{i}f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{i-1},W(f_{i},f_{i,i+1})_{1},W(f_{i},f_{i,i+1})_{2},f_{i+2}\dots,f_{n}).

Proposition 3.8

(Isometry property of
\sigma_{i}
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
,
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=(\mathbf{x},n;\mathbf{y},1)
. Then for
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, we have

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\sigma_{m}f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}].

(15)

Proof.

We first assume
m\in\{2,\dots,n-2\}
.
By the metric composition law, Lemma
2.4
, applied twice, we can write

f[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\sup_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\Big(f[\mathbf{p}\to(\mathbf{z},m+2)]+f[(\mathbf{z},m+1)\to(\mathbf{w},m)]+f[(\mathbf{w},m-1)\to\mathbf{q}]\Big).

(16)

For a fixed pair
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w})
, when we apply
\sigma_{m}
to
f
, the first and third terms under the supremum in (
16
) do not change since the relevant components
f_{i}
do not change. The middle term is preserved under the transformation
\sigma_{m}
by Lemma
3.7
. Hence the right hand side of (
16
) is also preserved under the map
f\mapsto\sigma_{m}f
. The cases
m=1,n-1
are similar with one of the terms in (
16
) removed.
∎

For
m\leq n
, define
\tau_{m}:\mathcal{D}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}^{n}
by

\tau_{m}=\sigma_{m}\sigma_{m+1}\cdots\sigma_{n-1}.

We will build the
n
-line Pitman transform from composing the maps
\tau_{i}
.

Remark 3.9

(Cars)
.

The maps
\tau_{m}
can be used to give a connection between particle systems and last passage percolation as follows. The functions
t\mapsto\tau_{k}f_{k}(t)
, with
k=1,\ldots,n
can be thought of as deterministic versions of the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process, tasep.

Informally, think of
n
cars moving on a single-lane highway in the same, typically negative direction. The cars cannot pass each other. The derivative
f^{\prime}_{k}(t)
is the desired velocity of car
k
at time
t
.

However, cars cannot always move at their desired velocity. Cars ignore cars behind them, but are often forced to avoid cars ahead of them, in which case they slow down just enough to avoid collision. The key feature of these models is that the interaction, rather than the direction of movement is totally asymmetric. Slowing down could mean being forced to back up if the car ahead does. The function
\tau_{k}f_{k}(t)
is the position of the
k
th car at time
t
.

A different model for the movement of the cars is that ignore the cars ahead of them and are forced to speed up to avoid collisions from cars behind them. For this model, the location of car
k
is then given by
\sigma_{k-1}\cdots\sigma_{1}f_{k}(t)
.
This version is push-asep and first passage percolation.

Stochastic models that fit in this setting include tasep, discrete-time tasep, push-asep (which also has totally asymmetric interactions), Brownian tasep and the Hammersley process.

In light of the remark, the following lemma states a deterministic equivalence between exclusion processes and last passage percolation.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
1\leq m\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for
y\geq 0
, we have

f[(0,n)\to(y,m)]=\tau_{m}f_{m}(y).

(17)

Proof.

We show this by induction on
n-m
. The
n=m
case is true by definition of
\tau_{n}=Id
. For
m<n
, we have by the metric composition law, Lemma
2.4
,

\displaystyle f[(0,n)\to(y,m)]

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\leq y}f[(0,n)\to(z,m+1)]-f_{m}(z^{-})+f_{m}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\leq y}\tau_{m+1}f_{m+1}(z)-f_{m}(z^{-})+f_{m}(y)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{m}\tau_{m+1}f_{m}(y),

where the second equality follows from the inductive hypothesis, and the third equality is simply the definition of
\sigma_{m}
.
By the definition of
\tau_{m}
this equals the right hand side of (
17
).
∎

Definition 3.11
.

We will build the
n
-line map
\omega:\mathcal{D}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}^{n}
from the functions
\tau_{m}
as follows. Define

\omega=\tau_{n-1}\tau_{n-2}\cdots\tau_{1}=\sigma_{n-1}\sigma_{n-2}\sigma_{n-1}\cdots\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n-1}.

(18)

Informally speaking, if we think of
\sigma_{i}
as “sorting” the
i
-th and
i+1
-st functions
f_{i}
and
f_{i+1}
, then
\omega
is precisely the “bubble sort” algorithm to sort the entire ensemble
f
.

In the following proposition, we show that
\omega=W
, the melon map from (
9
).

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\omega f
be the melon of a function
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
. Then:

(i)

(Isometric equivalence)
\omega f\sim f
.

(ii)

(Idempotence)
\omega^{2}=\omega
.

(iii)

(Image) We have
\omega(\mathcal{D}^{n})=\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}=\{f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}:f_{n}\preceq\dots\preceq f_{1}\}.

(iv)

\omega=W
from (
9
).

To prove Proposition
3.12
we need two short lemmas.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
m<n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\tau_{m}^{2}=\tau_{m+1}\tau_{m}
.

Proof.

Let
g=\tau_{m+1}\tau_{m}f
. We want to show that
g=\sigma_{m}g
. The functions
g
and
\sigma_{m}g
can only differ in the coordinates
m,m+1
. By Lemma
3.5
it suffices to show that
g_{m}=\sigma_{m}g_{m}
.

By Lemma
3.10
, we have

\sigma_{m}g_{m}(y)=\tau_{m}\tau_{m}f_{m}(y)=\tau_{m}f[(0,n)\to(y,m)]=f[(0,n)\to(y,m)],

where the last equality is by Proposition
3.8
repeatedly applied to
(f_{m},\ldots,f_{n})
.
Since
\tau_{m+1}
does not change coordinate
m
, Lemma
3.10
gives

g_{m}(y)=\tau_{m+1}\tau_{m}f_{m}(y)=\tau_{m}f_{m}(y)=f[(0,n)\to(y,m)].\qed

Lemma 3.14
.

For
m=1,\ldots,n-1
we have

\sigma_{m}\omega=\omega
.

Proof.

The statement for
m=n-1
follows since
\sigma_{n-1}^{2}=\sigma_{n-1}
by Lemma
3.5
. Now assume
m<n-1
.
When
i-j\geq 2
, the functions
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{j}
commute because they act on disjoint coordinates. Thus, by Lemma
3.13
we have

\sigma_{m}\omega=\tau_{n-1}\cdots\tau_{m+2}\sigma_{m}\tau_{m+1}\tau_{m}\cdots\tau_{1}=\tau_{n-1}\cdots\tau_{m+2}\tau^{2}_{m}\cdots\tau_{1}=\tau_{n-1}\cdots\tau_{m+2}\tau_{m+1}\tau_{m}\cdots\tau_{1}=\omega.\qed

Proof of Proposition
3.12
.

Part (i) follows immediately from Proposition
3.8
. Part (ii) follows from Lemma
3.14
.

For (iii), Lemma
3.5
(iii) implies that
\omega
is the identity on
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
, so
\omega(\mathcal{D}^{n})\supset\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
. For the other direction, note that
\sigma_{i}
does not change
\omega f
by Lemma
3.14
. By
Lemma
3.5
(iv), this implies that
f_{i+1}\preceq f_{i}
for all
i
, so
\omega(\mathcal{D}^{n})\subset\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
.

Finally, for (iv),

Wf=W\omega f=\omega f,

where the first equality is a consequence of part (i), and the second is a consequence of (
13
), since
\omega f\in\mathcal{D}_{\uparrow}
by (iii).
∎

Remark 3.15
.

We mention without proof that by applying a similar framework in the
n=3
case,
\omega
can alternately be defined as
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}
, instead of
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
. This yields a braid relation for the operators
\sigma_{i}
, which implies that for larger
n
,
W=\omega=\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{\binom{n}{2}}}
whenever the product of transpositions
(i_{1}\;i_{1}+1)\cdots(i_{\binom{n}{2}}\;i_{\binom{n}{2}}+1)
is a reduced word for the reverse permutation. This was shown in
Biane et al. 2005
in the case of continuous functions
f
.

4
Minimal and maximal elements

In this section, we show that the melon map picks out the minimal element in the equivalence class of isometric environments under a certain natural preorder. Recall that a preorder is partial order without the antisymmetry requirement, i.e.
f\leq g
and
g\leq f
are both allowed for
f\not=g
.
While this perspective is not necessary for the proofs in later sections, it is helpful for developing intuition about the melon map and its inverse.

For
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
, let
\lambda_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\rho_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
be the leftmost and rightmost optimizers in
f
from
(\mathbf{x},n)
to
(\mathbf{y},1)
, see Lemma
2.6
.
We say that
f\trianglelefteq g
if:

\lambda_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\lambda_{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\rho_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\rho_{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\;\;\text{ for all }\;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}.

Our goal is to prove the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
with
f(0)=0
, the melon
Wf
is the unique minimal element with respect to
\trianglelefteq
in the isometry class of
f
.

Remark 4.2
.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
shows that
Wf\trianglelefteq g
for any
f,g\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
with
f\sim g
. The condition that
f(0)=0
is only necessary for the uniqueness statement. Indeed, consider the example where
f_{i}(x)=1
for all
x\geq 0
so that
df
consists of a line of atoms at
\{0\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then
Wf_{1}=n
and
Wf_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 2
so
Wf\neq f
. However, it is not difficult to check that
\lambda_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lambda_{Wf}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\rho_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\rho_{Wf}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
so we have both
f\trianglelefteq Wf
and
Wf\trianglelefteq f
.

The main lemma needed for Proposition
4.1
concerns the Pitman transform
W:\mathcal{D}^{2}\to\mathcal{D}^{2}
. Its proof is in a similar vein to the proof of Lemma
3.2
. As a result, we defer it to the appendix where it is proven with Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}^{2}
and let
x\leq y
. Then

\rho_{Wf}(x,y)\leq\rho_{f}(x,y)\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda_{Wf}(x,y)\leq\lambda_{f}(x,y).

We can extend this to general optimizers using the same ideas as in Lemma
3.7
. As the proof is essentially identical, we omit it.

Lemma 4.4
.

For all
f\in\mathcal{D}^{2}
, we have
Wf\trianglelefteq f
.

We can extend this to
\sigma_{i}
by a simple application of the metric composition law. The proof is essentially identical to the proof of Proposition
3.8
so we omit it.

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, we have
\sigma_{i}f\trianglelefteq f
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

By Lemma
4.5
and the definition, we have
Wf\trianglelefteq f
for all
f
. Now, for any
g
in the same isometry class as
f
, we have
Wf=Wg\trianglelefteq g
. Since
\trianglelefteq
is only a preorder, to prove that
Wf
is the
unique
minimal element we still need to check that if
f\trianglelefteq Wf
then
f=Wf
.

Indeed, since
Wf
is Pitman ordered the leftmost optimizer
\lambda_{Wf}(0^{k},x^{k})
simply follows the top
k
paths
Wf_{1},\dots,Wf_{k}
on the interval
(0,x)
. Since no disjoint
k
-tuple is to the left of this optimizer, the same must be true for
\lambda_{f}(0^{k},x^{k})
if
f\trianglelefteq Wf
. Therefore for all
x,k
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}Wf_{i}(x)=f[(0^{k},n)\to(x^{k},1)]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(0^{-})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}f_{i}(0)-f_{i}(0^{-}).

Since
f(0^{-})=0
by definition and
f(0)=0
by assumptions, this implies that
Wf=f
.
∎

Just as
Wf
identifies a minimal element of the isometry class of
f
, we would also like to identify a maximal element. We can do this with a straightforward symmetric definition if we first restrict to functions defined on
[0,t]
rather than
[0,\infty)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
be the set of
n
-tuples of cadlag functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}
with only positive jumps. For
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, we can define the truncated melon map
W_{t}:\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}\to\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
by (
9
). Again, we can think of
W_{t}f
as picking out a distinguished minimal element in the isometry class of
f
in
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
. In the finite setting, a conjugation of the map
W_{t}
produces a maximal element.
For
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, recall
R_{t}f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
is the rotation of
f
by 180 degrees. Define
M_{t}=R_{t}W_{t}R_{t}
. Then we have the following.

Proposition 4.6
.

For every
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
with
f(t^{-})=f(t)
,
M_{t}f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
is the unique maximal element in the isometry class of
f
with respect to
\trianglelefteq
.

Proof.

The main idea is that the reflection operator
R
sends left most-geodesics to right-most geodesics and vice versa. For any
f
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
, we have

\lambda_{Rf}(R\mathbf{x},R\mathbf{y})=R[\rho_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho_{Rf}(R\mathbf{x},R\mathbf{y})=R[\lambda_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})].

Therefore

\rho_{RW_{t}Rf}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=R[\lambda_{W_{t}Rf}(R\mathbf{x},R\mathbf{y})]\geq R[\lambda_{Rf}(R\mathbf{x},R\mathbf{y})]=\rho_{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

where the inequality uses that

. A similar calculation for
\lambda_{RW_{t}Rf}
shows that
f\trianglelefteq M_{t}f
. Using that

for all

yields the that
g\trianglelefteq M_{t}f
for all

. Finally, uniqueness follows from the same reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, but working with rightmost instead of leftmost optimizers.
∎

The infinite bijection

In this section we find the inverse of the melon map

Lemma 5.1

Proposition 5.8

Let

\limsup_{t\to\infty}f_{j+1}(t)-f_{j}(t)=\infty

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that

, there is an optimizer
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k})
from
(0,n)^{k}
to

such that
\pi_{i}\neq[0,\infty)\times\{n+k-i\}
for some

. Let

be the maximal index for which this holds, let

be the index of the line on which

contains a semi-infinite interval, and let

be the time when

jumps to line

, so that

and

. Since

, we have that

(t,\infty)\times\{j+1\}\cap(\cup\pi)=\emptyset.

Since

is an optimizer, for any

to a compact set

6
The finite bijection

In Section
5
, we studied the bijectivity of the melon map in the infinite-time setting. The goal of this section is to construct a version in the finite-time setting. This version is more similar to classical RSK: an additional Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern will play the role of the second Young tableaux.

Like the infinite case, it is straightforward to check that the maps
W_{t}
and
M_{t}
are inverses of each other on appropriate domains. However, unlike the infinite case, in the finite case, the image of
M_{t}
is a rare subset of
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
and so the bijection loses usefulness.
Instead, our strategy is to take an element of
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
and map it to an element of
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{*t}
, see Corollary
5.5
. This allows us to define the RSK correspondence on the finite domain
[0,t]
in terms of the maps
W,M
defined to the infinite domain
[0,\infty)
.

For
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, and a
k
-tuple of functions
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
where each
g_{i}:[t,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and
g(t)=f(t)
, define the
concatenation

f\oplus g\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
to be equal to
f
on
[0,t]
and
g
on
[t,\infty)
. Note that our notation implicitly depends on
t
. For
\alpha>0
, we write
f\oplus\alpha
to mean
f\oplus g_{\alpha}
, where
dg_{\alpha}
is purely atomic on
(t,\infty)\times\{1,\dots,n\}
with atoms of size
\alpha i
at locations
(t+i,i)
. The concatenations
f\oplus\alpha
are set up so that for large enough
\alpha
,
f\oplus\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{*t}
, see Figure
3
.

Given this, one direction of the finite RSK correspondence will essentially be the map
f\mapsto W(f\oplus\alpha)
for large
\alpha
. We can then invert this correspondence using
M
:

MW(f\oplus\alpha)|_{[0,t]}=M(f\oplus\alpha)|_{[0,t]}=(f\oplus\alpha)|_{[0,t]}=f.

(30)

Here the first equality follows from Proposition
5.7
, the second follows from Corollary
5.5
, and the third is by definition.

While (
30
) clearly hints at the existence of a bijection, there are a few more things to check. We need to figure out what information about
f
is actually used in the map
f\mapsto W(f\oplus\alpha)
, identify the image of this map, and then show that if we start with data in that space, then
WM
is also the identity on that space. The rest of this section is devoted to doing this.
We will also present our version of the finite RSK correspondence in a way that more closely resembles the classical RSK bijection between matrices and Young tableaux. Our correspondence will be related to classical RSK and dual RSK in Section
8
.

First, let
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t\uparrow}=W_{t}(\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t})
be the space of Pitman ordered sequences
w_{n}\preceq\dots\preceq w_{1}
in
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\operatorname{GT}_{n}
be the space of triangular arrays
g=\{g_{i}(j):i\leq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}
satisfying the inequalities

g_{i}(j)\geq g_{i}(j-1),\qquad g_{i}(j-1)\geq g_{i+1}(j),

(31)

for all
i,j
where these quantities are defined. Such an array is called a
Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern
of depth
n
. These inequalities amount to saying that the
j
th row
g(j)
is an ordered
i
-tuple, and that consecutive rows interlace.
Next, define the space

\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}=\{h=(w,g)\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t\uparrow}\times\operatorname{GT}_{n}:w(t)=g(n)\}.

The space
\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
is the analogue of the set of pairs of Young tableaux with the same shape, see Section
8
.
The finite-
t
RSK correspondence,
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
, maps
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
into
\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
, where
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(f)=(W_{t}f,G_{t}f)
, and for
k\leq s\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}G_{t}f_{i}(s)=f[(0,n)^{k}\to(t,n-s+1)^{k}].

(32)

Lemma 6.1
.

The map
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}:\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}\to\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
is well-defined. That is,
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}f\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
for any
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.12
(iii),
W_{t}f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t\uparrow}
, and
W_{t}f(t)=G_{t}f(n)
by definition. It remains to verify the Gelfand-Tsetlin inequalities (
31
) for
G_{t}f
. Both of these types of inequalities have similar proofs and follow from quadrangle inequalities that are similar in spirit to Lemma
2.5
. We prove only the first inequality; the second one is similar. By (
32
), the inequality
G_{t}f_{i}(s)\geq G_{t}f_{i}(s-1)
is equivalent to the quadrangle inequality

\begin{split}&f[(0,n)^{i}\to(t,n-s+1)^{i}]+f[(0,n)^{i-1}\to(t,n-s+2)^{i-1}]\\
\geq\;\;&f[(0,n)^{i}\to(t,n-s+2)^{i}]+f[(0,n)^{i-1}\to(t,n-s+1)^{i-1}].\end{split}

(33)

To prove (

Remark 6.5
.

Just as we can build the melon map
W
as a composition of
2
-line Pitman transforms, see Definition
3.11
, we can also build the
n
-line RSK correspondence by composing
\binom{n}{2}

2
-line correspondences. More precisely, we build up the melon
Wf
using the maps
\sigma_{i}f
as in (
18
), but every time we apply one of the maps
\sigma_{i}
to an intermediate function

, we also record the additional value
g_{i+1}(t)
.

The map
(g_{i},g_{i+1})\mapsto(\sigma_{i}g_{i},\sigma_{i}g_{i+1};g_{i+1}(t))
is a
2
-line
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
correspondence and hence is invertible, therefore so is the whole correspondence. Moreover, the
\binom{n}{2}
additional values that we record with this procedure correspond to the
\binom{n}{2}
entries in the Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern
G_{t}g_{i}(j),1\leq i\leq j\leq n-1
that cannot be read off of
W_{t}f
.

Though this basic idea is fairly simple, we found that the method we chose present is more straightforward and geometrically intuitive.

Remark 6.6
.

Our
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
map is based on one method of embedding
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
into
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{*t}
by adding heavy weights after time
t
. There are clearly many ways to do this, and different methods will result in different bijections. One common feature of these bijections is that the key data that they see about
f
beyond its melon
Wf
will be a collection of left-to-right last passage values from time
0
to time
t
. Though we will not prove it here, all left-to-right last passage values are contained in
G_{t}f
, just as all bottom-to-top last passage values are contained in
W_{t}f
by Proposition
3.12
(i).

Another option for constructing an RSK-like bijection would be to add heavy weights
before
time
0
, essentially embedding
f
as an element of
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
. We could also add weights to both sides of
[0,t]
to embed
f
as a different distinguished element of an isometry class.

Bijections related to RSK exploring the use of different left-to-right or bottom-to-top last passage values have been constructed in
Dauvergne 2020
and
Garver et al. 2018
.

6.1
Bijectivity for lattice specializations and other restrictions

Bijectivity of the cadlag RSK correspondence
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}:\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}\to\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
naturally implies that for any subset
A\subset\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, that
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
is also a bijection from
A
to
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
. For certain subsets
A
, we can explicitly identify
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
, allowing us to recover previously known bijections and identify some new ones. In the next set of examples, we gather together the restricted bijections that correspond to classical integrable models of last passage percolation. In the
t=\infty
setting where the Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern is dropped, these examples correspond exactly to those introduced immediately after Theorem
1.5
.

For these examples, we say that a cadlag function
f
with positive jumps is
pure-jump
if
df
is an atomic measure.

Example 6.7
.

Let
t>0
.

1.

Continuous functions.
If
A
is the set of continuous functions
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
then
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
is the set of pairs
(w,g)\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
such that
w
is also in
A
(e.g.
w
is continuous as well). This setting of continuous functions is studied in detail in
Biane et al. 2005
.

2.

Unit jumps.
Let
A
be the set of pure-jump functions
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, such that every jump of each
f_{i}
has size
1
, and such that all jumps of
f_{i},f_{j}
are at distinct locations for
i\neq j
.
The
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
is the set of pairs
(w,g)\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
, where
w
is also in
A
, and all entries of
g
are nonnegative integers. In this setting, the space
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
is equivalent to the decorated Young Tableau defined in
Nica 2017
.

3.

Real jumps at integer times.
Let
A
be the set of pure-jump functions
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, such that every
f_{i}
only jumps at integer times. Then
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
is the set of pairs
(w,g)\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
, where
w
is also in
A
.

4.

Integer jumps at integer times.
Let
A
be the set of pure-jump functions
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
, such that every
f_{i}
only jumps at integer times and all jumps have integer values. Then
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
is the set of pairs
(w,g)\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
, where
w
is also in
A
, and all coordinates of
g
are nonnegative integers.

5.

Bernoulli paths.
Suppose that additionally,
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
A
be the set of all functions
f\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}
that are linear with slope in
\{0,1\}
on every integer interval
[i,i+1]
. Then
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
is the set of pairs
(w,g)\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{t}
, where
w
is also in
A
, and all coordinates of
g
are nonnegative integers.

It is easy to verify each of the five examples above from the explicit formulas for
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
and
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}^{-1}
in Section
6
. Example
4
above corresponds to the usual RSK correspondence via Corollary
8.4
and Example
5
corresponds to the dual RSK correspondence via Corollary
8.7
.

7
Preservation of uniform measure

In each of the five examples in Example
6.7
, there are natural measures on
A
that push forward tractable measures on
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
. By taking a limit as
t\to\infty
, we can also get tractable pushforward measures under the original melon map
W:\mathcal{D}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}^{n}
. Each of these measures corresponds to a classical integrable model of last passage percolation. This is summarized in the following table, essentially repeated from the introduction.

\begin{array}[]{c|l|l}\text{Example}&\text{Measure on $\mathcal{D}^{n}$}&\text{LPP model}\\
\hline\cr\ref{Ex:restrictions}.1&\text{Independent Brownian motions}&\text{Brownian LPP}\\
\ref{Ex:restrictions}.2&\text{Independent Poisson counting processes}&\text{Poisson lines LPP}\\
\ref{Ex:restrictions}.3&\text{Independent discrete-time geometric random walks}&\text{Geometric LPP}\\
\ref{Ex:restrictions}.4&\text{Independent discrete-time exponential random walks}&\text{Exponential LPP}\\
\ref{Ex:restrictions}.5&\text{Piecewise linear walks with independent Bernoulli slopes}&\text{S-J model}\end{array}

In all five examples above, the pushforward of these measures under
W
is the nonintersecting version of these objects. This is known in all cases, e.g. see
O’Connell 2003
and references therein, or Section 6 of
Dauvergne et al. 2019
. The standard proofs of these facts require explicit computations involving determinants and Doob transforms.
Here we give an alternate approach that is computation-free. We demonstrate this in the case of Bernoulli walks, Example

We start with a more precise setup.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, a function
f:[0,k]\to\mathbb{R}
is a
Bernoulli path
if on every integer interval
[n,n+1]
,

is linear with slope in
\{0,1\}
. A
Bernoulli walk
of drift
d\in[0,1]
is a random Bernoulli path whose slopes are independent Bernoulli random variables of mean
d
, and an
n
-dimensional Bernoulli walk
with drift vector
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
is an element of
\mathcal{D}^{n}
whose components are independent Bernoulli walks of drift

. See Figure

for an illustration.

Now, for

and an ordered vector

, let
\nu_{t}(x)
denote the uniform measure on
n
-tuples of ordered Bernoulli paths
f:[0,t]\to\mathbb{R},f=(f_{1}\geq\dots\geq f_{n})
that satisfy
f(0)=0,f(t)=x
. There are only finitely many such
k
-tuples, so uniform measure is well-defined.
A measure
\mu
on the space of
n
-tuples of ordered Bernoulli paths

X=(X_{1}\geq X_{2}\geq\dots\geq X_{n}),\qquad X_{i}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}

is a
Bernoulli Gibbs measure
if for any integer
t>0
, the conditional distribution under
\mu
of
X|_{[0,t]}
given
X|_{[t,\infty)}
is
\nu_{t}(X(t))
.
We start by showing that
W
maps Bernoulli walks to Bernoulli Gibbs measures.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
Y\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
be a Bernoulli walk of drift
d
. Then the law of
WY\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\uparrow}
is a Bernoulli Gibbs measure satisfying

\lim_{t\to\infty}WY(t)/t=d^{\circ}

(40)

almost surely, where
d^{\circ}=(d^{\circ}_{1}\geq\dots\geq d^{\circ}_{n})
are the order statistics of
d
.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
and let
A,\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
be as in Example
6.7
.5. The map RSK
t
applied to
Y
up to time
t\in\mathbb{N}
gives an
n
-tuple of ordered paths
W_{t}Y
and a Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern
G_{t}Y
. We first show that the law of
W_{t}Y
given
G_{t}Y
is
\nu_{t}(WY(t))
, and then use this to deduce the Bernoulli Gibbs property.
We first consider the case
d_{i}=1/2
for all
i
, so that the law of
Y|_{[0,t]}
is the uniform measure on
A
.

By the bijectivity of
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
in Example
6.7
.5, the law of
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}Y
is uniform on
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}(A)
. Therefore, conditionally on
G_{t}Y
, which determines
WY(t)
, the law of
W_{t}Y
is
\nu_{t}(WY(t))
. As an aside, the conditional law of
G_{t}Y
given
W_{t}Y
is also independent and uniform on Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns with
n
th row
WY(t)
.

Now for general
d\in[0,1]^{n}
, the law of
Y
up to time
t\in\mathbb{N}
is the uniform measure on
A
biased by the Radon-Nikodym derivative

2^{nt}\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{Y_{i}(t)}(1-d_{i})^{t-Y_{i}(t)}.

Since this derivative only depends on
Y(t)
, the conditionally law of
Y
given
Y(t)
does not depend on the original drift vector
d
. Moreover, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

Y_{1}(t)+\ldots+Y_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{i}G_{t}Y_{j}(i),

and so
Y(t)
can be expressed from the the Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern
G_{t}Y
. Therefore conditionally on
G_{t}Y
, the law of
W_{t}Y=WY|_{[0,t]}
does not depend on the drift
d
. Therefore as in the

WY|_{[0,t]}
given

is still
\nu_{t}(WY(t))
.

We now use this conditional law to prove the Bernoulli Gibbs property. First, this conditional law implies the stronger claim that for any integers
s\leq t
, the conditional law of
WY|_{[0,s]}
given
WY|_{[s,t]}
and
G_{t}Y
is still
\nu_{t}(WY(s))
. Therefore it suffices to show that as

, that
WY|_{[0,s]}
and
G_{t}Y
are asymptotically independent. For this, it is enough to show that for any
k,\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with
\ell\geq n-k+1
, for all large enough

we have

Y[(0,n)^{k}\to(\ell,t)^{k}]=Y[(s,n)^{k}\to(\ell,t)^{k}]+\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}Y_{i}(s).

(41)

Indeed, the right side of (
41
) only depends on
Y|_{[s,\infty)}-Y(s)
which is independent of
WY|_{[0,s]}
, and
G_{t}Y
can be expressed from the left hand side by varying
\ell,k
. Equation (
41
) is equivalent to the claim that for large enough

, the rightmost disjoint optimizer from
(0,n)^{k}
to

follows the bottom

paths up to time

We now show that

satisfies (
40
). Define operators
\Lambda_{k}:\mathcal{D}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}^{n}
by
\Lambda_{k}f(t)=f(kt)/k
. By the law of large numbers, as

uniformly on compact sets. Since

is continuous with respect to the uniform-on-compact topology and commutes with

by definition of last passage, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Lambda_{k}WY(1)=\lim_{k\to\infty}W\Lambda_{k}Y(1)=Wy(1).

Finally,

applied to linear functions just sorts them, so

.
∎

Next, we show that there is a unique Bernoulli Gibbs measure satisfying (

Theorem 7.2
.

For any
d\in[0,1]^{n}
with

\mu_{d}
on ordered

\lim_{t\to\infty}WY(t)/t=d\qquad\text{a.s.}

(42)

Proof.

Let
\mu_{d}
denote the law of
WY^{d}
, where
Y^{d}
is a Bernoulli walk of drift
d=(d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n})
.

Now let
X
be a sample from an arbitrary Bernoulli Gibbs measure satisfying (
42
). To show that
X\sim\mu_{d}
, it suffices check that
X|_{[0,s]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}WY^{d}|_{[0,s]}
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\epsilon>0
and
v=(n,n-1,\ldots,1)
and set

\bar{d}_{i}=(d_{i}+\epsilon v_{i})\wedge 1,\qquad\underline{d}_{i}=(d_{i}-\epsilon v_{i})\vee 0,\qquad\bar{X}\sim\mathcal{\mu}_{\bar{d}},\qquad{\underline{X}}\sim\mathcal{\mu}_{\underline{d}}.

When

, by (
42
), for all

we have

\mathbb{P}(\underline{X}_{i}(t)\leq X_{i}(t))\to 1
as

. Otherwise

, but in this case
\underline{X}_{i}=0
as well, so

a.s. Thus, after a symmetric upper bound, we get that

\mathbb{P}A_{t}\to 1\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty,\qquad\text{ where }A_{t}=\{\underline{X}(t)\leq X(t)\leq\overline{X}(t)\}.

(43)

Now, the proof of Lemmas 2.6/2.7 in
Corwin and Hammond 2014
shows that if

coordinatewise,

, and
Y\sim\nu_{t}(x),Y^{\prime}\sim\nu_{t}(x^{\prime})
then we have the stochastic dominance

, so there exists a coupling with
Y_{i}(t)\leq Y^{\prime}_{i}(t)
for all

. Thus, for

for

\underline{X}|_{[0,t]}\preceq X|_{[0,t]}\preceq\overline{X}|_{[0,t]}.

(44)

As

, since
\mathbb{P}A_{s}\to 1
this implies (

) unconditionally.
Now let
\epsilon\to 0
. The laws
\mu_{d}
restricted to

are continuous in

in the total variation norm, since the laws of

are themselves continuous in

in total variation. Therefore (

. In this case

and

both have distribution

∎

Corollary 7.3

(Metric Burke property)
.

Last passage percolation across an
n

are Bernoulli walks with drifts

, then

we have

Y[(x,n)\to(y,1)]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;Z[(x,n)\to(y,1)].

Burke’s theorem normally refers to a certain invariance between arrivals and departures in a queuing processes; Corollary

is a kind of Burke property because it shows an invariance in the last passage value under exchanging the rows of the underlying environment. See
O’Connell and Yor 2002

The proof framework in this section goes through essentially verbatim if we start with a vector of independent geometric random walks, as in Example
6.7

are embedded in

as pure-jump paths with jumps at integer times. The Pitman ordering condition on

means that the output is a vector of geometric walks conditioned so that

for all

8
Embedding classical versions of RSK

In this section, we relate our RSK map
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
to the usual RSK and dual RSK correspondences for nonnegative matrices. These correspondences are connected to last passage percolation in the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We start with the connection to the standard RSK correspondence.

8.1
Young tableaux

We recall some basic combinatorial objects, see e.g.
Stanley 1999
for a detailed reference. A
partition
is a weakly decreasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots\lambda_{k})
of positive integers. The
size
of the partition is
|\lambda|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
. To any partition
\lambda
, the
Young diagram
associated to
\lambda
is the set of squares
Y(\lambda)=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:1\leq i\leq\lambda_{j}\}
.
A
semistandard Young tableau
of shape
\lambda
is a filling of the corresponding Young diagram with positive integers such that the entries are strictly increasing along columns and weakly increasing along rows.

There is a natural correspondence between Young tableaux and Pitman ordered cadlag paths with only integer-valued positive jumps at positive integer times as in Example
6.7
.4. Consider a Young tableau
T
of shape
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots\lambda_{k})
. Define
w\in\mathcal{D}_{\uparrow}^{k}
by setting

w_{i}(t)=\text{$\#$ of entries in row $i$ of $T$ that are $\leq t$}.

In other words, the path
w_{i}
has jumps precisely at the times
t
which are equal to the entries of the
i
-th row of
T
. It is straightforward to check that with this definition, each
w_{i}
is a cadlag path with positive integer jumps at integer times. The fact that the entries of
T
are strictly increasing along columns implies that
w_{i}(t^{-})\geq w_{i+1}(t)
for all
i,t
, and so the
w\in\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\uparrow}
. This map from Young tableaux to Pitman ordered paths on this space is invertible. Moreover, for
n>k
we can extend the collection
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})
to a collection
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
of Pitman ordered paths by setting
w_{i}=0
for
i>k
.

There is also a well-known correspondence between Young tableaux and Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns with nonnegative integer entries. Namely, for a Young tableau
T
of shape
\lambda
whose largest entry is less than or equal to
m
, define a Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern
g=\{g_{i}(j):i\leq j\leq m\}
by setting
g_{i}(j)
to be equal to the number of entries in row
i
of
T
that are less than or equal to
j
.

8.2
Classical RSK via Greene’s theorem

The RSK correspondence is a map between the space of nonnegative matrices with integer entries and pairs of semistandard Young tableaux
(P,Q)
of equal shape. Typically it is described using a local bumping algorithm. However, the RSK bijection can alternately be described using last passage percolation. For the restriction of RSK to permutation matrices (the Robinson-Schensted correspondence) this is due to
Greene 1974
. A version of Greene’s theorem for RSK is also well-known, but appears to be folklore and we do not know of an original reference. See, for example, Theorem 24 in
Hopkins 2014
or
Krattenthaler 2006
, Theorem 8.

In the following, we describe RSK based on this connection with Greene’s theorem in the language of last passage values. For two points
p=(x,n),q=(y,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
x\leq y
and
n\geq m
, we say that a sequence of vertices
\pi=(\pi_{1}=p,\dots,\pi_{k}=q)
is a
directed path from
p
to
q
if
\pi_{i}-\pi_{i-1}\in\{(1,0),(0,-1)\}
for all
i
. For an array
A=\{A_{u}:u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
of nonnegative numbers, we can define the weight of any path
\pi
from
p
to
q
by

|\pi|_{A}=\sum_{v\in\pi}A_{v}.

(45)

We also define the last passage value

A[p\to q]=\max_{\pi}|\pi|_{A},

(46)

where the maximum is taken over all possible paths
\pi
from
p
to
q
. More generally, for vectors
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k}),\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})
, define the
multi-point last passage value

A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\max_{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}}|\pi_{1}|_{A}+\cdots+|\pi_{k}|_{A}

(47)

where the maximum now is taken over all possible
k
-tuples of
disjoint
paths, where each
\pi_{i}
is a path from
p_{i}
to
q_{i}
. This is defined so long as a disjoint
k
-tuple exists. We also introduce the shorthand
A[p^{*k}\to q^{*k}]
for the
k
-point last passage value from

(p-(0,k-1),\dots,p-(0,1),p)\to(q,q+(1,0),\dots,q+(k-1,0)).

The value
A[p^{*k}\to q^{*k}]
is best thought of as a last passage value with
k
disjoint paths from
p
to
q
, hence the similar notation to the corresponding object in the cadlag setting. We are forced to stagger the start and end points of the paths to allow for disjointness.

Now for an
n\times m
matrix of nonnegative integers
A
(equivalently, a restriction of a nonnegative array to the set
\{1,\dots,m\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}
), we can define a semistandard Young tableau, called the
recording tableau

Q
with at most
n\wedge m
rows and entries in
\{1,\dots,m\}
by letting

A[(1,n)^{*k\wedge i}\to(i,1)^{*k\wedge i}]=\text{$\#$ of entries in rows $1,\dots,k$ of $Q$ that are $\leq i$}.

Similarly, define a semistandard Young tableau, called the
insertion tableau

P
with at most
n\wedge m
rows and entries in
\{1,\dots,n\}
by letting

A[(1,n)^{*k\wedge i}\to(m,n-i+1)^{*k\wedge i}]=\text{$\#$ of entries in rows $1,\dots,k$ of $P$ that are $\leq i$}.

The RSK correspondence is the map
A\mapsto(Q,P)
. Observe that with these definitions
P
and
Q
have the same shape determined by the last passage values
A[(1,n)^{*k}\to(m,1)^{*k}],k=1,\dots,m\wedge n
. By the correspondences between semistandard Young tableaux and Pitman ordered collections of cadlag paths and Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns we can associate to
(Q,P)
a pair
(WA,GA)\in\mathcal{G}^{n}_{m}
. Unravelling the bijections in Section
8.1
, we get that for all
t\in[1,m]
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}WA_{j}(t)=A[(1,n)^{*k\wedge{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}}\to({\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor},1)^{*k\wedge{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}}],

(48)

and for
t<1
, we have
WA_{j}(t)=0
. Also, for
1\leq k\leq i\leq n
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}GA_{j}(i)=A[(1,n)^{*k}\to(m,n-i+1)^{*k}].

(49)

8.3
Classical RSK and the melon map

For a nonnegative
n\times m
matrix
A
, define
f^{A}\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{m}
by

f^{A}_{k}(0^{-})=0,\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad f^{A}_{k}(t)-f^{A}_{k}(s)=\sum_{r\in(s,t]}A_{r,k}.

(50)

We will show that discrete last passage values across
A
equal last passage values across

Proposition 8.1
.

For all tuples of points
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
such that
A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]
is defined, we have

A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=f^{A}[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}],

To prove Proposition
8.1
, we will show that lattice last passage values can be equivalently defined using unions of possibly overlapping paths.

We first prove this for endpoints that lie in a packed staircase configuration.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
be such that
p_{i}=p_{i-1}+(1,1)
and
q_{i}=q_{i-1}+(1,1)

A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]=\max_{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}}\sum_{v\in\bigcup_{i}\pi_{i}}A_{v},

where the maximum is over all
k
-tuples of paths
\pi_{i}
from
p_{i}
to
q_{i}
, without any disjointness condition enforced. In the union in (

The fact that

Without loss of generality, by passing to order statistics, we may assume that the maximum is achieved on a

\hat{\tau}_{1}(x)\leq\hat{\tau}_{2}(x)\dots\leq\hat{\tau}_{k}(x),

Now consider the set

Suppose not. Then there exists an

\hat{\pi}_{i}(x)-2\geq\hat{\pi}_{\ell}(x)\qquad\text{ for all }i>\ell.

For any

) completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
8.1
.

Any disjoint lattice paths from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
can be mapped to disjoint cadlag paths, so we have
A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]\leq f^{A}[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]
. Now let

f^{A}\{\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\}=\max_{\pi}|\pi|_{f},

where the maximum is now over
k
-tuples from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{q}
with the disjointness condition removed. In
|\pi|_{f}
we only count weights once even if they are covered by multiple paths. Let
\pi
be a
k
-tuple that achieves this maximum, and define a new
k
-tuple
{\left\lfloor\pi\right\rfloor}
by setting
{\left\lfloor\pi\right\rfloor}_{i}(t)=\pi_{i}({\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor})
for all
i,t
. Since
f^{A}
has only positive jumps and is constant between integer times,
{\left\lfloor\pi\right\rfloor}
also achieves this maximum. Each
{\left\lfloor\pi\right\rfloor}_{i}
corresponds to a discrete lattice path
\pi^{\prime}_{i}
from
p_{i}
to
q_{i}
, and we have the equality

|{\left\lfloor\pi\right\rfloor}|_{f^{A}}=\sum_{v\in\bigcup_{i}\pi^{\prime}_{i}}A_{v}.

Therefore by Lemma
8.3
,
f^{A}\{\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\}\leq A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]
. Since
f^{A}[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]\leq f^{A}\{\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\}
, we have that
f^{A}[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]\leq A[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}]
as well.
∎

Finally, we can show that the usual RSK bijection is a special case of the cadlag RSK bijection.

Corollary 8.4
.

Let
A
be an
n\times m
matrix. Define
f^{A}\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{m}
via the formula in (
50
). Then with
(WA,GA)
as in (
48
) and (
49
) we have that

(WA,GA)=(W_{m}f^{A},G_{m}f^{A})=\operatorname{RSK}_{m}(f^{A}).

Proof.

By Proposition
8.1
and tracing through the definitions, it suffices to show that

f^{A}[(0,n)^{k}\to(t,j)^{k}]=f^{A}[(1,n)^{*k\wedge{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}}\to({\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor},j)^{*k\wedge{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}}]

(58)

for all
t,j,k
with
k\leq n+1-j
. For
k\geq{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}
, both sides pick up all weights of
A
in the box
\{1,\dots,{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}
. For
k<{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}
, notice that since
f^{A}(t)=0
for all
t<1
and
f^{A}
is unchanging between integer times, that
f^{A}[(0,n)^{k}\to(t,j)^{k}]=f^{A}[(1,n)^{k}\to({\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor},j)^{k}]
. Moreover, essential disjointness at times
1
and
{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}
implies that any disjoint
k
-tuple from
(1,n)^{k}
to
({\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor},j)^{k}
has the same length as some disjoint
k
-tuple from
(1,n)^{*k}\to({\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor},j)^{*k}
.
∎

Remark 8.5
.

While the RSK correspondence is defined only for matrices with nonnegative integer entries, the maps (
49
) and (
48
) are still defined for matrices
A
with nonnegative real entries; there is just no longer a connection with Young tableaux. Proposition
8.1
, Lemma
8.2
, and Corollary
8.4
still hold in this generality and the proofs go through verbatim.

8.4
Dual RSK

The dual RSK correspondence can also be connected with
\operatorname{RSK}_{t}
via lattice last passage. The necessary version of Greene’s theorem for dual RSK is Theorem 10 in
Krattenthaler 2006
. As the details connecting cadlag RSK and dual RSK are similar to the case of the usual RSK correspondence, we only include theorem statements here.

Let
A
be an
n\times m
matrix of
0
s and
1
s. For two points
p=(x,k),q=(y,\ell)
with
x\leq y
and
k\geq\ell
, we say that
\pi=(\pi_{1}=p,\dots,\pi_{k}=q)
is a
dual path from
p
to
q
if
\pi_{i}-\pi_{i-1}\in\{(1,s):s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\}
for all
i
. That is
\pi
is a path that moves strictly to the right and weakly up at every step.
Definitions (
45
), (
46
), and (
47
) still make sense for dual paths and we write
A\{\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\}
for a last passage value with dual paths.

Now, for a filling
Q
of a Young diagram
Y
, we write
Q^{T}
for the transposed filling of the transposed Young diagram
Y^{T}
, i.e. a cell
(a,b)\in Y
if and only if
(b,a)\in Y^{T}
and
Q^{T}(b,a)=Q(a,b)
. For an
n\times m
matrix
A
of
0
s and
1
s, we define a semistandard Young tableau
P
with at most
n\wedge m
rows and entries in
\{1,\dots,m\}
by letting

A\{(1,n)^{*k\wedge i}\to(i,1)^{*k\wedge i}\}=\text{$\#$ of entries in rows $1,\dots,k$ of $Q^{T}$ that are $\leq i$}.

Also define a semistandard Young tableau
P
with at most
n\wedge m
rows and entries in
\{1,\dots,n\}
by letting

A\{(1,n)^{*k\wedge i}\to(m,n-i+1)^{*k\wedge i}\}=\text{$\#$ of entries in rows $1,\dots,k$ of $P$ that are $\leq i$}.

The dual RSK correspondence is the map
A\mapsto(Q,P)
which maps
0-1
matrices to pairs of semistandard Young tableaux such that the shapes of
Q
and
P
are
conjugate
, i.e
Q^{T}
has the same shape as
P
. Observe that with the above definitions
Q^{T}
and
P
have the same shape.

The fact that
Q
, rather than
Q^{T}
, is a semistandard Young tableau is a consequence of the differences in the definition of paths and dual paths. Nonetheless, to connect this definition to cadlag RSK it is still
Q^{T}
that we want to write as a collection of Pitman ordered paths
(WA_{1},\dots WA_{n})
. To do this, we embed
Q^{T}
not as a collection of cadlag paths with jumps, but rather as a collection of paths with piecewise linear increments. For all
t\in\{1,\dots m\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we write

\sum_{j=1}^{k}WA_{j}(t)=A\{(1,n)^{*k\wedge t}\to(t,1)^{*k\wedge t}\}.

(59)

We also set
WA(0)=0
, and let each line
WA_{i}
be linear on every interval
[t,t+1]
with
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
Q
is a semistandard Young tableau, with this definition each line
WA_{i}
either has slope
0
or slope
1
on every interval. We also turn
P
into a Gelfand-Tsetlin pattern
G
in the usual way. For
1\leq k\leq i\leq n
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}GA_{j}(i)=A\{(1,n)^{*k}\to(m,n-i+1)^{*k}\}.

(60)

We now connect this description to cadlag RSK. For an
n\times m

\{0,1\}
-matrix
A
, define
\ell^{A}\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{m}
by letting

\ell^{A}_{k}(0)=0,\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad\ell^{A}_{k}(t)-\ell^{A}_{k}(s)=\sum_{r\in[s+1,t]}A_{r,k}\qquad\text{ for }s,t\in\mathbb{Z},

(61)

and by letting each
\ell^{A}_{k}
be linear between integers. We then have the following analogue of Proposition
8.1
.

Proposition 8.6
.

Let
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}
be such that
A\{\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\}
is defined. Then

A\{\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}\}=\ell^{A}[\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{q}],

(62)

The proof of Proposition
8.6
is similar to the proof of Proposition
8.1
.
Proposition
8.6
leads to an analogue of Corollary
8.4
.

Corollary 8.7
.

Let
A
be an
n\times m
matrix of
0
s and
1
s. Then with
(WA,GA)
as in (
59
) and (
60
) we have that

(WA,GA)=(W_{m}\ell^{A},G_{m}\ell^{A})=\operatorname{RSK}_{m}(\ell^{A}).

Remark 8.8
.

While the dual RSK correspondence is defined only for matrices with
\{0,1\}
entries, the maps (
60
) and (
59
) are still defined for matrices
A
with arbitrary real entries; there is just no longer a connection with Young tableaux. Proposition
8.6
and Corollary
8.7
still hold in this generality.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Set

s(x,y)=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}f_{2}(z)-f_{1}(z^{-})=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}s(z,z),

so that we have

s(x,y^{-})=\sup_{z\in[x,y)}f_{2}(z)-f_{1}(z^{-}).

For each
x
, the function
s(x,\cdot)
is increasing. Also, since the functions
f_{i}
are cadlag with positive jumps, we have that
s(x,\cdot)
is cadlag. (Note that this would not hold if we allowed negative jumps in
f_{1}
.) We also have

s(x,y)-s(x,y^{-})=[s(y,y)-s(x,y^{-})]^{+}\leq f_{2}(y)-f_{2}(y^{-}).

(63)

We can explicitly write last passage values across
f
as

f\big[(x,2)\to(y,1)\big]=f_{1}(y)-f_{2}(x^{-})+s(x,y).

(64)

Specializing to the case
x=0
, and using that
Wf_{1}+Wf_{2}=f_{1}+f_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle Wf_{1}(t)=f_{1}(t)+s(0,t)\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad Wf_{2}(t)=f_{2}(t)-s(0,t).

(65)

From the fact that
s(0,\cdot)
is cadlag and increasing, the function
Wf_{1}
is cadlag with only positive jumps. Also, by (
63
), the function
Wf_{2}
is cadlag with only positive jumps, so
W
maps
\mathcal{D}^{2}
to itself.
The last passage value across
Wf
is

\displaystyle Wf\big[(x,2)\to(y,1)\big]

\displaystyle=Wf_{1}(y)-Wf_{2}(x^{-})+\sup_{z\in[x,y]}Wf_{2}(z)-Wf_{1}(z^{-}).

Substituting the formulas (
65
) we get that this equals

f_{1}(y)+s(0,y)-f_{2}(x^{-})+s(0,x^{-})+\sup_{z\in[x,y]}[s(z,z)-s(0,z)-s(0,z^{-})].

(66)

By comparing with (
64
), we can see that the lemma will follow from the equality

s(x,y)-s(0,y)-s(0,x^{-})=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}[s(z,z)-s(0,z)-s(0,z^{-})].

(67)

To prove (
67
), we divide into cases. First suppose that
s(0,x^{-})=s(0,y)
. In this case, since
s(0,\cdot)
is nondecreasing, we have that
s(0,z)=s(0,z^{-})=s(0,x^{-})=s(0,y)
for all
z\in[x,y]
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[x,y]}[s(z,z)-s(0,z)-s(0,z^{-})]

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}s(z,z)-s(0,y)-s(0,x^{-})

\displaystyle=s(x,y)-s(0,y)-s(0,x^{-}).

We turn to the case when
s(0,x^{-})<s(0,y)
. By definition,

s(0,y)=s(0,x^{-})\vee s(x,y),\quad\text{so}\quad s(0,y)=s(x,y).

(68)

Set

z_{0}=\sup\{z\in[x,y]\,:\,s(0,z^{-})=s(0,x^{-})\}.

The function
s(0,\cdot^{-})
is left continuous, so this is in fact a maximum. In particular, since
s
is nondecreasing, for each
z_{1}>z_{0}

s(0,z_{1})\geq s(0,z_{1}^{-})>s(0,z_{0}^{-}).

So we have, by definition of
s

s(0,z_{1})=s(0,z_{0}^{-})\vee s(z_{0},z_{1})=s(z_{0},z_{1}).

By the right continuity of
s(0,\cdot)
and
s(z_{0},\cdot)
, as
z_{1}\downarrow z_{0}
we get

s(0,z_{0})=s(z_{0},z_{0}).
By choosing
z=z_{0}
in the supremum on the right hand side of (
67
) we get

-s(0,x^{-})=-s(0,z_{0}^{-})\leq\sup_{z\in[x,y]}[s(z,z)-s(0,z)-s(0,z^{-})]

(69)

Since
s(z,z)\leq s(0,z)
, and the fact that
s
is nondecreasing, the right hand side can be upper bounded by

\sup_{z\in[x,y]}[-s(0,z^{-})]=-s(0,x^{-}).

so (
69
) is in fact an equality. Since
s(0,y)=s(x,y)
by (
68
), this proves the preservation of last passage values in (
67
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We use the notation from the proof of Lemma
3.2
in this appendix. Recall that

s(x,y)=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}f_{2}(z)-f_{1}(z^{-})=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}s(z,z).

Let

I_{f}(x,y)=\{z\in[x,y]:s(z,z)=s(x,y)\}.

This is the set of all possible jump times from line
2
to
1
for geodesics from
(x,2)
to
(y,1)
in
f
.
Also set
r(x,y)=\sup_{z\in[x,y]}[s(z,z)-s(0,z)-s(0,z^{-})]
and let

I_{Wf}(x,y)=\{z\in[x,y]:r(z,z)=r(x,y)\}.

By (
66
) this is the set of all possible jump times from line
2
to
1
for geodesics from
(x,2)
to
(y,1)
in
Wf
.
Then the desired ordering on geodesics holds if and only if

\inf I_{Wf}(x,y)\leq\inf I_{f}(x,y),\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sup I_{Wf}(x,y)\leq\sup I_{f}(x,y).

Again, we first deal with the case when
s(0,x^{-})=s(0,y)
. In this case, for all
w\in[x,y]
we have

r(w,w)=s(w,w)-2s(0,x^{-}),

so
I_{f}(x,y)=I_{Wf}(x,y)
. Now suppose
s(0,x^{-})<s(0,y)
. Define

A=\sup\{z\in[x,y]:s(0,z^{-})=s(0,x^{-})\},\qquad B=\inf\{z\in[x,y]:s(0,z)=s(0,y)\}.

We clearly have
A\leq B
. Moreover,

s(0,y)=s(x,y)
in this case, and so for
z<I
, we must have
s(z,z)<s(x,y)
. Hence

I_{f}(x,y)\subset[B,y]
. To complete the proof, we show
I_{Wf}(x,y)\subset[x,A].
Since (
69
) is an equality in this case, see the discussion following that inequality, at every point
w\in I_{Wf}(x,y)
, we have
r(w,w)=-s(0,x^{-})
. Moreover, for
w>A
, we have

\displaystyle r(w,w)

\displaystyle=s(w,w)-s(0,w)-s(0,w^{-})\leq-s(0,w^{-})<-s(0,x^{-}).

Therefore
I_{Wf}(x,y)\subset[x,A].

∎
