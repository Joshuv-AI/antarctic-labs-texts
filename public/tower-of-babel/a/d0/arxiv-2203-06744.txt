Abstract
We consider dynamic versions of epistemic logic as formulated in [ 4 ] . That paper proposed a family of logical languages ℒ ⁡ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) parameterized by action signatures . In addition to ℒ ⁡ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) , we consider two fragments ℒ 0 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) and ℒ 1 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) of it. We review the syntax and semantics of these languages ℒ 0 ​ ( 𝚺 ) , ℒ 1 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}),{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) , and ℒ ⁡ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) , as well as the sound proof systems for the validities in them. It was shown in [ 16 ] that validity in ℒ ⁡ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) is Π 1 1 \Pi^{1}_{1} -complete, so there are no recursively axiomatized complete logical systems for it. On the positive side, this paper proves the strong completeness of the axiomatization of ℒ 0 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) and the weak completeness of ℒ 1 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) . The work involve a detour into term rewriting theory. And since at the heart of the argument is modal filtration, it gives the finite model property and hence decidability. We also give a translation of ℒ 1 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) into PDL, hence we obtain a second proof of the decidability of ℒ 1 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) . The paper closes with some results on expressive power. These are mostly concerned with comparing ℒ 1 ​ ( 𝚺 ) {\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}) with modal logic together with transitive closure operators. We also answer a natural question about the languages we get by varying the action signature. In particular, we prove that a logical language with operators for private announcements is more expressive than one for public announcements.



This paper was intended to be the “journal” version of our 1998 paper
“The logic of common knowledge, public announcements,
and private suspicions.” It was mainly written in 2004, with a few bibliographic additions
coming a few years later.

1
Introduction

One of the goals of
dynamic epistemic logic
is to
construct logical languages which allow one to represent
a variety of possible types of changes affecting the
information states
of agents in a multi-agent setting.
One wants (formal) logical systems with primitive operations corresponding
to (informal) notions such as
public announcement
,

completely private announcement
,

private announcement to one agent with suspicion by another
,
etc. And then after the logics are formulated, one would ideally
want technical tools to use in their study and application.

The full formulation of such logics is somewhat of a complicated
story. In the first place, one needs a reasonable syntax.
On the semantic side, there is an unusual feature in that the
truth of a sentence at a point in one model often depends on
the truth of a related sentence in a
different
model.
In effect, the kinds of actions we are interested in give
rise to functions, or relations more generally, on the class
of all possible models.

There have been some proposals on logical systems for dynamic epistemic
logic beginning with the work of
Plaza
[
17
]
,
Gerbrandy
[
12
,
13
]
, and
Gerbrandy and Groeneveld
[
14
]
.
These papers formulated logical systems for the informal notions
of public announcement and completely private announcement. They
left open the matter of axiomatizing the logics in the presence
of common knowledge operators. (Without the
common knowledge operators, the systems are seen to be
variants on standard multi-model logic.) And they also left open the
question of the decidability of these systems.
Our work began with complete axiomatizations for these systems
and for more general systems. Our results were presented
in
[
5
]
. As it happens, the logics which we constructed
in
[
5
]
did not have the friendliest syntax.
The first two authors pursued this matter for some time and eventually
came to the proposals in
[
4
]
.
The logical systems for the validities of
the ultimate languages are certainly related to those in
[
5
]
.
But due to the different formulation of the overall syntax
and semantics, all of the work on soundness and completeness had
to be completely reworked. The main purpose of the present paper
is to present these results. Secondarily, we present some technical
results on the systems such as results on expressive power.

Contents of this paper: a high level view

This paper is a long technical development, and for this reason
we did not include
a full-scale motivation of the logical systems themselves.
For a great deal of motivational material,
one should see
[
5
]
.
Section
2
formulates all of the definitions
needed in the paper. The presentation is based closely on
the work in
[
4
]
, so readers familiar with that paper
may use Section
2
as a review.
Other readers of this paper could take the logical systems here to
simply be extensions of
propositional
dynamic logic which allow one to make
“transitions from model to model”
in addition to transitions “inside a given model.”
In addition, Section
2.8
is new in this paper.

The logical languages studied in this paper are presented in
Section
3
.
Section
3.2
presents some
examples of the semantics, chosen to foreshadow
work in Section
7
on expressive power.
The logical system is presented in Section
4
,
along with a soundness result for most of the system. One especially significant
inference rule, called the
Action Rule
is studied in Section
5
.
The completeness theorem for the logic comes in Section
6
.
The final section deals with questions of expressive power, and it may be read
after Section
4
.

Two aspects of our overall machinery are worth pointing out.
The first is the use of the
canonical action model
. This is a semantic
object built from syntactic objects (sequences of
simple actions
, terms
in our language
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
). We would like to
think that the use of
the canonical model makes for a more elegant presentation than one would
otherwise have.

The second feature is a
term rewriting system

for dealing with assertions in the language. The
semantic equivalences in dynamic epistemic logic are sufficiently complicated that
the ‘subsentence’ relation is not the most natural
or useful one for many purposes. Instead,
one needs to handcraft various ordering relations for use in inductive proofs,
or in defining translations. For just one example, in the logic of public announcements
one has an
announcement-knowledge axiom
in a form such as

[!\varphi]\Box\psi\leftrightarrow(\varphi\rightarrow\Box[!\varphi]\psi).

(1.1)

This is for a logic with just one agent, and in this discussion we are forgetting
about common knowledge. To prove that the logic has a translation
t

back to ordinary modal logic, one wants to use the equivalence
in (
1.1
) above
as the key step in the translation, defining
([\varphi]\Box]\psi)^{t}
to
be
\varphi^{t}\rightarrow(\Box[!\varphi]\psi)^{t}
. But then one needs to have some reason
to say that both
\varphi
and (more critically)
\Box[!\varphi]\psi

are of
lower complexity
than the original
formula
[\varphi]\Box\psi
. There are several ways to make this precise.
One is to directly assign an element of some well-founded set to each
formula and then use this as a measure of complexity. This is done in
[
9
]
,
and the well-founded set is the natural numbers. Our treatment is
different, mainly because it goes via term rewriting.
In effect, one takes the well-founded set to be the sentences themselves,
with the order given by substitution using laws such as (
1.1
), but oriented
in a specified direction (left-to-right, for example).
Then the fact that we have a well-founded relation is a result one proves
about oriented sets of laws (term rewriting systems).
In our case, we use an interpretation constructed by hand.

Our term rewriting system is presented and studied in Section
6.1
.
It actually deals not with
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
but with a different language called
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
.
An important by-product of our completeness proof via rewriting
is a Normal Form Theorem for
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
.
This is an interesting result in itself, and it does not follow from other completeness
proofs such as those in
[
9
]
, or in the paper
[
9
]
, which we discuss next.
In order to use the rewriting system.
one needs to know that substitution of “equivalent” objects preserves
“equivalence” and that the proof system itself is strong enough to
reduce sentences to normal form.
Unfortunately, this “obvious” point takes a great deal of work.
The details are all in this paper, and to our knowledge no other source presents
complete proofs on these matters.

Comparison with some other work

The first version of this paper is our 1998 conference publication
[
5
]
.
We ourselves had several versions of this paper issued as technical
reports or posted on web sites, and so in some sense the results
here are
public
but not
published
.
In the past ten years, the subject of dynamic epistemic logic
has taken off in a serious way. It sees several papers a year.
But it seems fair to say that the overall topics of investigation are not
the logical systems presented in the any of the original papers but rather
are adaptations of the logics to settings involving probability, belief revision,
quantum information, and the like.
Still, the work reported here has been the subject of several
existing publications.
And so it makes sense
for us to make the case that the results in this paper are still relevant
and do not follow from previously published results.

The book
Dynamic Epistemic Logic
by
Hans van Ditmarsch, Wiebe van der Hoek, and Barteld Kooi
[
9
]

is a textbook presentation containing
some of the content of this paper and
[
4
]
,
with additional material on model checking, belief revision, and other topics.
Section 6.4 presents the syntax of what it calls
action model logic

and writes as
{\cal L}^{\rm act}_{KC\otimes}(A,P)
.
At first glance, the syntax of
{\cal L}^{\rm act}_{KC\otimes}(A,P)

seems fairly close to the language
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
presented in
[
4
]

and reviewed
in Section
3
of this paper.
A relatively small difference concerns the
action models
in

{\cal L}^{\rm act}_{KC\otimes}(A,P)
.
(In general, action models are like Kripke models together with
a “precondition” function mapping worlds to sentences.)
These are required to have the property that
each agent’s accessibility relation is an equivalence relation; our treatment
is more general and hence can present logics for epistemic actions such as
“cheating” in games.
But the main difference is that our language
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})

uses what everyone would take to be a bona fide
syntax: sentences and program expressions are
linearly ordered strings of symbols.
In contrast, the syntax of
{\cal L}^{\rm act}_{KC\otimes}(A,P)

employs action models
directly
. Structured but unordered
objects occur inside of sentences. In a different context, it would be
like studying formal language and their connection to automata by studying
something that was like a regular expression but allowed
finite automata to directly occur inside some syntactic object.

We have no objection ourselves to
this move. In fact, this was the presentation we chose in
our first version
[
5
]
of this paper.
But over the years we have found quite a lot of resistance to
this presentation
on the grounds that one is “mixing syntax and semantics.” Again, we do not
assert this objection, but we
address
it by employing the large technical
machinery that we see in
[
4
]
and also Sections
2

and
3
. (We are keenly aware of the irony of our being
criticized for “mixing syntax and semantics” in the ten-year-old publication
[
5
]
,
and after we “un-mixed” them in
[
4
]
, we find others making the same natural
move.)

We would also like to mention the paper “Logics of Communication and Change”,
van Benthem, van Eijck and Kooi
[
7
]
.
This paper presents completeness theorem
for a logical system in the same family as ours.
But as it happens, the paper studies a different system.
To see the differences, it is worthwhile to note that the source of much
of the work in this area concerns assertions of common knowledge following
an action of some sort. In our setting, these would be written
[\alpha]\Box^{*}\varphi
.
It turns out that there is no equivalence of the form (
1.1
) for assertions like
this. This leads us to our Action Rule, an inference rule allowing the derivation
of sentences of the kind under discussion.
The idea in
[
7
]

is to start with propositional dynamic logic, called E-PDL
in the paper (‘E’ for ‘epistemic’). Then one adds
modalities corresponding to update models

[{\sf U},e]
; this modality would be like our
[\alpha]
, but in
[
7
]

as in
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
, this
U
is an action model
rather than a bona fide syntactic object. Then one proves that
the addition can be translated away. This very interesting and suggestive
result is not available in our setting. The paper
[
7
]
notes
that “Indeed, reduction axioms are not available, as the logic with
epistemic updates is more expressive than the logic without them.
We think this is an infelicity of design.” This is somewhat debatable,
since the additional expressive power in E-PDL is not something in wide use.
“We have found no practical use for these [complex combinations of agent
acessibility relations] at present, but they are the price that we cheerfully pay
for having a language living in expressive harmony with its dynamic superstructure

\ldots
.” It also holds that “it’s completeness theorem [from
[
5
]
] is correspondingly
mess.” This extra “mess” is
due partly to the strictly “syntactic syntax”, partly to the explicit rewriting work
(taken for granted in
[
7
]
), and partly to the noted “disharmony”.
It is not known whether E-PDL is actually stronger than the logical
systems in this paper. If it is, then this would show that the completeness result here
is definitely not a special case of that in
[
7
]
. But again, the matter is open at this time:
one cannot derive our result from
[
7
]
since the systems are
prima facie different.

2.1
State models and propositions

We fix a set
{\sf AtSen}
of
atomic sentences
and also
a set
{\cal A}
of
agents
. All of our definitions are
relative to these sets.

A
state model
is a triple

{{\mathbf{S}}}=(S,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal A}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}_{\!\!{\mathbf{S}}},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathbf{S}}})

consisting of a set
S
of “states”; a family

\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}_{\!\!{\mathbf{S}}}
of
binary accessibility
relations
\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}_{\!\!{\mathbf{S}}}\subseteq S\times S
, one for each agent
A\in{\cal A}
;
and a “valuation” (or a “truth” map)

{\|.\|}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\,:{\sf AtSen}\rightarrow{\cal P}(S)
, assigning to
each atomic sentence
p
a set
\|p\|_{{\mathbf{S}}}
of states.
These are exactly Kripke models generalized by having one
accessibility relation for each agent. We use the terminology of
state models because there are other kinds of models,

action models

and
program models
, in our study.
When dealing with a single fixed state model
{\mathbf{S}}
, we often drop
the subscript
{\mathbf{S}}
from
all the notation.

Definition

Let
SMod
be the collection of all state models.
An
epistemic proposition
is an operation

\varphi
defined on
SMod
such that for all

{\mathbf{S}}\in\mbox{\sf SMod}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\subseteq S
.

The collection of epistemic propositions is closed in
various ways.

1.

For each atomic sentence
p
we have an atomic proposition
p

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}=\|p\|_{{\mathbf{S}}}
.

2.

If

\varphi
is an epistemic proposition,
then so is

\neg{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}
, where

(\neg{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}})_{{\mathbf{S}}}=S\setminus{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
.

3.

If
C
is a set or class of epistemic propositions, then
\bigwedge C
is an epistemic proposition,
where

(\bigwedge C)_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}}\quad=\quad\bigcap\{{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}:{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}\in C\}.

4.

Taking
C
above to be empty, we
have an
“always true”
epistemic proposition
tr
, with

\mbox{\sf\bf tr}_{{\mathbf{S}}}=S
.

5.

We also may take
C
in part
3
to be a two-element set

\{{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}},{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}}\}
; here we write

{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}\wedge{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}}
instead of
\bigwedge\{{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}},{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}}\}
.
We see that if
\varphi
and
\psi
are
epistemic propositions, then so is

{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}\wedge{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}}
, with

({\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}\wedge{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}})_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\cap{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
.

6.

If
\varphi
is an epistemic proposition and
A\in{\cal A}
, then

\Box_{A}{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}
is an epistemic proposition, with

(\Box_{A}{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}})_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}}\quad=\quad\{s\in S:\mbox{if $s\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}t$, then
$t\in{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}$}\}.

(2.2)

7.

If
\varphi
is an epistemic proposition and
{\cal B}\subseteq{\cal A}
, then

\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}
is an epistemic proposition, with

(\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}})_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}}\quad=\quad\{s\in S:\mbox{if $s\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!{\cal B}^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!$}t$,
then
$t\in{\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}$}\}.

Here
s\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!{\cal B}^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!$}t
iff there is a sequence

s=u_{0}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A_{1}}}{{\rightarrow}}\quad u_{1}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A_{2}}}{{\rightarrow}}\quad\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A_{n}}}{{\rightarrow}}\quad u_{n+1}=t

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in{\cal B}
.
In other words, there is a
sequence of arrows
labelled with agents from the set
{\cal B}
taking
s
to
t
.
We allow
n=0
here, so
\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!{\cal B}^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!
includes
the identity relation on
S
.

Syntactic and semantic notions

It will be important for us to make a sharp distinction between
syntactic and semantic notions. We have already begun to do
this, speaking of
atomic
sentences
and atomic
propositions
.
The difference for us is that atomic sentences are entirely
syntactic objects: we won’t treat an atomic sentence
p
as
anything except an unanalyzed mathematical object. On the
other hand, this atomic sentence
p
also has associated
with it the atomic proposition
p
.
As defined in point 1,

p
will be a function whose domain is the
(proper class of) state models, and it is defined by

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$\unboldmath}}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\quad=\quad\{s\in S:s\in\|p\|_{{\mathbf{S}}}\}.

(2.3)

This difference may seem pedantic at first, and surely
there are times when it is sensible to blur it. But
for various reasons that will hopefully become clear,
we need to insist on it.

Up until now, the only syntactic objects
have been the atomic sentences
p\in{\sf AtSen}
. But we can build
the collections of
finitary
and
infinitary
sentences
by the same definitions that we have seen,
and then the work of the past section is the
semantics

of our logical languages. For example, we have sentences

p\wedge q
,
\Box_{A}\neg p
, and
\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}q
. These
then have corresponding epistemic propositions as their semantics:

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$\unboldmath}}\wedge{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}}
,
\Box_{A}\neg{\mbox{\boldmath$p$\unboldmath}}
,
and
\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}}
,
respectively. Note that the latter is a properly infinitary proposition
(and so
\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}q
is a properly infinitary sentence);
it abbreviates an infinite conjunction.

2.2
Updates

A
transition relation
between state models
{\mathbf{S}}
and
{\mathbf{T}}
is
a relation
between the sets
S
and
T
;
i.e., a subset of
S\times T
.
An
update

{\bf r}
is a pair
of operations

\mbox{\bf r}\quad=\quad({\mathbf{S}}\mapsto{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r}),{\mathbf{S}}\mapsto\mbox{\bf r}_{{\mathbf{S}}}),

where for each
{\mathbf{S}}\in\mbox{\sf SMod}
,

\mbox{\bf r}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\,:{\mathbf{S}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})

is a transition relation.
We call
{\mathbf{S}}\mapsto{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})
the
update map
,
and
{\mathbf{S}}\mapsto\mbox{\bf r}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
the
update relation
.

We continue our general discussion by noting that
the collection of updates is closed in various ways.

1.

Skip:
there is an update called “Skip” and denoted

1
, with
{\mathbf{S}}({\mbox{\boldmath$1$\unboldmath}})={\mathbf{S}}
, and

{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$\unboldmath}}}_{\mathbf{S}}
is the identity relation on
{\mathbf{S}}
.

2.

Sequential Composition
: if
r
and
s
are epistemic updates, then
their composition
\mbox{\bf r};\mbox{\bf s}
is again an epistemic update,
where
{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r};\mbox{\bf s})={\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})(\mbox{\bf s})
, and

(\mbox{\bf r};\mbox{\bf s})_{\mathbf{S}}=\mbox{\bf r}_{{\mathbf{S}}};{\mbox{\bf s}}_{{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})}
. Here, we use on the
right side the usual composition
;
of relations.
1
1

1

We
are writing relational composition in left-to-right order in this
paper.

3.

Union
(or
Non-deterministic choice
):
If
X
is any set of epistemic updates,
then the union
\bigsqcup X
is an epistemic update,
defined as follows.
For each
{\mathbf{S}}
, the set of states of the model

{\mathbf{S}}(\bigsqcup X)

is the
disjoint union
of all the sets of states in each model
{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})

for
\mbox{\bf r}\in X
:

\{(s,\mbox{\bf r}):\mbox{\bf r}\in X\mbox{ and }s\in{\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})\}.

Similarly, each accessibility relation
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
is defined as
the disjoint union of the corresponding accessibility relations in

(t,\mbox{\bf r})\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}(u,\mbox{\bf s})\quad\mbox{iff}\quad\mbox{ if $\mbox{\bf r}=\mbox{\bf s}$ and $t\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}u$ in
${\mathbf{S}}(\mbox{\bf r})$}.

The valuation
\|p\|_{{\mathbf{S}}(\bigsqcup X)}
in
{\mathbf{S}}(\bigsqcup X)

between
{\mathbf{S}}

{\mathbf{S}}(\bigsqcup X)

\mbox{\bf r}\sqcup\mbox{\bf s}

\mbox{\bf r}\sqcup\mbox{\bf s}=\bigsqcup\{\mbox{\bf r},\mbox{\bf s}\}

(

\mbox{\bf r}^{*}\quad=\quad\bigsqcup\{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$\unboldmath}},\mbox{\bf r},\mbox{\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\bf r},\ldots,\mbox{\bf r}^{n},\ldots\}

,

in

2.3
Action models and program models

Let
\Phi
be the collection of
all epistemic propositions.
An
(epistemic) action model
is a triple

{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=(\Sigma,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal A}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$},\mbox{pre})
,
where

,

relations on
\Sigma

To model non-deterministic actions and non-simple actions (whose appearances

are not uniform
on states), we define

that we decompose complex actions (‘programs’) into “simple” ones:
they correspond to sets of simple, deterministic actions from a given
action
model.

A

is defined as
a pair

consisting
of an action model

{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
and a set
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
of

designated simple actions
. Each of the simple actions
\gamma\in\Gamma

may be thought of as a possible “deterministic
resolution” of the non-deterministic action
\pi
.
As announced above, the intuition about the map called

is that
an action is executable in a given state only if all its preconditions
hold at that state.
We often spell out an epistemic program model
as
(\Sigma,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal A}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$},\mbox{pre},\Gamma)

rather than
((\Sigma,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal A}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$},\mbox{pre}),\Gamma)
.
Also, we usually drop the word “epistemic” and just refer
to these as
program models
.

Action signatures

Definition

An

.
When we deal with an action signature, our notation for the

occasionally redundant); thus the action types come with a
number that indicates their poisition in the fixed enumeration
of the action signature.

An action signature
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
together
with an assignment of epistemic propositions to the action types
in
\Sigma
gives us a full-fledged action model.
And this is the exact sense in which an

is
an abstraction of the notion of

.
We shall use action signatures in constructing logical languages.

Let
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
be an action signature,
let

be the number of action types in
\Sigma
,
let
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
, and let

in the following way:

1.

, and the

.

3.

To summarize:

.

5.3
Soundness of the Action Rule

As we have mentioned, the Action Rule is the main rule in the
system for
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
that goes beyond the rules of

{\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
. It is an induction rule which in the
setting of epistemic logic allows us
to prove that common knowledge obtains after various actions.
Here again is our statement of the Action Rule:

Let
\alpha
be a simple action.
Let
\psi
be a sentence, and let
{\cal C}
be a set
of agents. Let there be sentences
\chi_{\beta}
for
all
\beta
such that

\alpha\rightarrow_{{\cal C}}^{*}\beta

(including
\alpha
itself),
and such that

1.

\vdash\chi_{\beta}\rightarrow[\beta]\psi
.

2.

If
A\in{\cal C}
and
\beta\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}{\gamma}
,
then
\vdash(\chi_{\beta}\wedge\mbox{\sc Pre}(\beta))\rightarrow\Box_{A}\chi_{\gamma}
.

From these assumptions, infer

\vdash\chi_{\alpha}\rightarrow[\alpha]\Box_{{\cal C}}^{*}\psi
.

Remark

We use
\rightarrow_{{\cal C}}^{*}
as an abbreviation for the
reflexive and transitive closure
of the relation
\bigcup_{A\in C}\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}
.
Recall
from Proposition
5.1

that there are
only finitely many

\beta
such that

\alpha\rightarrow_{{\cal C}}^{*}\beta
.
So even though the Action Rule might look like it
takes infinitely many premises, it really only takes finitely many.

Remark
It is possible to drop the Composition Axiom
in favor of a more involved version of the Action Rule.
The point is we shall later introduce normal forms for
sentences, and using
the Composition Axiom will greatly simplify these normal
forms.
Moreover, adding the
Composition Axiom leads to shorter proofs.
The Action Rule here is geared towards those normal forms.
So if we were to drop the Composition Axiom, we would
need a stronger, more complicated,
formulation of the Action Rule, one which involved

sequences
of actions. It is not terribly difficult
to formulate such a rule, and completeness can be obtained
by an elaboration of the work which we shall do.

s\in[\![\langle\alpha\rangle\Diamond_{{\cal C}}^{*}\varphi]\!]_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}}

,

Proof

6
Completeness theorems

In this section, we prove the completeness of our logical systems
for
{\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
and
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
. (See Figure
1
for these.)
Recall that
the difference between the two languages is that the second
has the common-knowledge propositional operators
\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}

while the first does not. This difference makes the bigger
system much more expressive, and more difficult to study.
As it happens, the extension of

{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
to the full logic
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
,
the extension via the action iteration operation
\pi^{*}
,
leads to a logical language whose validity problem is
complete
\Pi^{1}_{1}
. So there cannot be a recursively
axiomatized logical system for the validities of
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
.
Returning to the smaller
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
, even here the
common knowledge operators
\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}

give a logical system which is not compact. So we cannot have a

strongly
complete logic for it; that is,
we cannot axiomatize the notion
of validity under hypotheses
T\models\varphi
. The best one can hope for is
weak completeness:
\vdash\varphi
if and only if
\models\varphi
.
We prove this in Theorem
6.24
.
As a result of some preliminary results aimed toward that
result, we establish the
strong completeness of our axiomatization
of the weaker logic
{\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
. The work there is
easier because it relies on a translation into modal logic.

In a later section,
we show that in contrast to our translation results
for
{\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
, the larger language

{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
cannot
be translated into
{\cal L}
or even to
{\cal L}(\Box^{*})

(modal logic with extra modalities
\Box_{{\cal B}}^{*}
).
So completeness results for
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})

cannot simply be based on translation.

The ideas

Our completeness proofs are somewhat involved,
and it might help the reader to have a preview of some of the
ideas before we get started.
For,
{\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
, the leading idea is that we can
translate the logic back to ordinary modal logic. Then we
get completeness by taking any logical system for modal
logic and adding whatever principles are needed in order
to make the translation. This idea is simple enough, and
after looking at a few examples one can see how
the translation of
{\cal L}_{0}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
to modal logic should
go. But the formal definition of the translation is
complicated. Our definition goes via a
term rewriting
system
related to the axioms of the logic, and in particular
we’ll need to do prove the
termination
of our system.
It would have been nice to use some off-the-shelf results
of term rewriting theory to get the termination of our system,
but this does not seem to be possible. In any case, we prove
termination by establishing a
decreasing interpretation

of
the system; that is, rewriting a sentence
leads to a decrease in an order
<
that we study at length.
Our interpretation is exponential rather than
polynomial, and it was found by hand.

For the record, here are some of the reasons why we
feel that the study of our system is complicated:

1.

The original statement of axioms such as the Action-Knowledge
Axiom is in terms of actions of the form
[\sigma_{i}\vec{\psi}]

2.

On the other hand, the Action Rule is best stated in terms

[\sigma_{i}\vec{\psi}]

In a term-rewriting setting, this point and the previous one
work against each other. Our work will be to work with the

simple actions.

3.

Again mentioning term rewriting, we’ll need to pick an orientation

[\alpha][\beta]\varphi\leadsto[\alpha;\beta]\varphi

[\alpha;\beta]\varphi\leadsto[\alpha][\beta]\varphi

2

2

We chose the first alternative, and for this reason, we’ll
need a formulation of the Action-Knowledge Axiom as an infinite scheme.

4.

Our language has the program union operator

\sqcup

sound for sums, we need to reformulate things to avoid

is not difficult, but it would have been nice to avoid it.

Recall that in the languages

At this point, we can put together several results from our

The soundness half being easy, we only need to

,

,

, and

6.4
Extensions to the completeness theorem

We briefly mention extensions of the Completeness
Theorem
6.24
.

First, consider the case of S5 (or K45) actions. We
change our logical system by restricting to these
S5 actions, and we add the S5 axioms to our
logical system. We interpret this new system on
S5 models.
It is easy to check that applying an S5 action to an S5 model
gives another S5 model.
Further, the S5 actions are closed under composition.
Finally, if
\alpha
is an S5 action
and
\alpha\rightarrow_{A}\beta
, then
\beta
also is an S5 action.
These easily imply the soundness
of the new axioms.
For completeness, we need only check that
if we assume the S5 axioms, then the filtration
{\cal F}

from the previous section has the property that
each
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
is an equivalence relation. This is
a standard exercise in modal logic (see, e.g., Fagin et al
[
10
]
,
Theorem 3.3.1).

Second, we also have completeness not only for the
languages
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
, but also for
the languages in
[
4
]
that are constructed from

families

{\cal S}
of action signatures.
This construction is most significant in the case when

{\cal S}
is an infinite set of signatures; for example,
{\cal S}

might contain a copy of every finite signature. In that setting,
the language
{\cal L}({\cal S})
would not be the language of any
finite signature. But for the (weak) completeness result
of this section, the advantage of the extended definition is
lost.
The point is that for single sentences, we may restrict attention
to a finite subset of
{\cal S}
. And for finite sets
{\cal S}
,

{\cal L}({\cal S})
is literally the language of the coproduct signature

\oplus\{{\mathbf{\Sigma}}:{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\in{\cal S}\}.

We already have completeness for such languages.

Our final extension concerns the move from actions
as we have been working them to actions which change
the truth values of atomic sentences.
If we make this move, then the axiom of Atomic Permanence
is no longer sound. However, it is easy to formulate
the relevant axioms. For example, if we have an
action
\alpha
which effects the change
p:=p\wedge\neg q
,
then we would take an axiom

[\alpha]p\leftrightarrow(\mbox{\sc Pre}(\alpha)\rightarrow p\wedge\neg q)
.
Having made these changes, all of the rest of the
work we have done goes through. In this way,
we get a completeness
theorem for this logic.

At this point, we know that

is a sublogic of

7.2
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
is more expressive than

{\cal L}_{1}

Recall that
{\cal L}_{1}
in this paper is multi-agent modal logic
together with the common-knowledge operators
\Box^{*}_{{\cal B}}
for
sets of agents.
Our main result here is that

{\cal L}_{1}
is strictly weaker than
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
, the logic
obtained by adding public announcements to
{\cal L}_{1}
.

We define a
rank

|\varphi|
on sentences from

{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pri}})
.
Let
|p|=0
for
p
atomic,
|\neg\varphi|=|\varphi|
,

|\varphi\wedge\psi|=\max(|\varphi|,|\psi|)
,

|\Diamond_{A}\varphi|=1+|\varphi|
, for all
A\in{\cal A}
,
and

|\Diamond_{{\cal B}}^{*}\varphi|=1+|\varphi|
for all
{\cal B}\subseteq{\cal A}
.

Games for
{\cal L}_{1}

The main technique in the proof is an
adaptation of Fraisse-Ehrenfeucht games to the setting
of modal logic.
Let
({\mathbf{S}},s)
and
({\mathbf{T}},t)
be states; i.e.,
model-world pairs. By recursion on the natural
number

n
we define a game

G_{n}(({\mathbf{S}},s),({\mathbf{T}},t))
.
For
n=0
,

I\!I
immediately wins if the following holds:
for all
p\in{\sf AtSen}
,

({\mathbf{S}},s)\models p
iff
({\mathbf{T}},t)\models p
.
And if
s
and
t
differ on some atomic sentence,

I
immediately wins. Continuing, here is how we define

G_{n+1}(({\mathbf{S}},s),({\mathbf{T}},t))
.
As in the case of the
G_{0}
games, we first check if

s
and
t
differ on some atomic sentence.
If they do, then
I
immediately wins. Otherwise,
the play continues. Now
I
can make two types of moves.

1.

A
\Diamond_{A}
-move:

I
has a choice of playing from
{\mathbf{S}}
or from
{\mathbf{T}}
, and also
some agent
A
.
If
I
chooses
{\mathbf{S}}
, then
I
continues by choosing
some
s^{\prime}
such that
s\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}s^{\prime}
in
{\mathbf{S}}
.
Then
I\!I
replies with some
t^{\prime}\in T
such that

t\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}t^{\prime}
.
Of course, if
I
had chosen in
{\mathbf{T}}
, then
I\!I
would have
chosen in
S
.
Either way, points
s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}

are determined, and the two players then play

G_{n}(({\mathbf{S}},s^{\prime}),({\mathbf{T}},t^{\prime}))
.

2.

A
\Diamond^{*}_{{\cal B}}
-move:

I
plays by selecting
{\mathbf{S}}
(or
{\mathbf{T}}
, but we ignore this
symmetric case below), and some set
{\cal B}
of agents,
and then
I
continues by playing some
s^{\prime}
(say)
reachable from
s
in the reflexive-transitive closure

\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!{\cal B}^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!
of
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal B}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
;
I\!I
responds with a
point
t^{\prime}

in the other model,
{\mathbf{T}}
, which is similarly
related to
t
.

We write
({\mathbf{S}},s)\sim_{n}({\mathbf{T}},t)
if
I\!I

has a winning strategy in the game

G_{n}(({\mathbf{S}},s),({\mathbf{T}},t))
. It is easy to check that
by induction on
m

that if
({\mathbf{S}},s)\sim_{n}({\mathbf{T}},t)

and
m<n
, then
({\mathbf{S}},s)\sim_{m}({\mathbf{T}},t)
.

Proposition 7.2

If
({\mathbf{S}},s)\sim_{n}({\mathbf{T}},t)
,
then for all
\varphi
with
|\varphi|\leq n
,

({\mathbf{S}},s)\models\varphi
iff
({\mathbf{T}},t)\models\varphi
.

The proof is standard, except perhaps for
the easy extra step for
\Diamond^{*}
moves.

We have two results that show that public announcements add
expressive power to
{\cal L}_{1}
. The first is for
one agent on arbitrary models, and the second is
for two agents on equivalence
relations.

In the first result, let
{\cal A}
be a singleton
\{A\}
.
We drop the
A
from the notation.

Theorem 7.3

The
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
sentence

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible in
{\cal L}_{1}
, even
by a set of sentences.

Proof

Fix a number
n
. We first show that

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible by any single
sentence of
{\cal L}_{1}
of rank
n
.
Let
A_{n}
be the cycle

a_{0}\ \rightarrow\ a_{1}\ \rightarrow\ \cdots\ \rightarrow\ a_{n+2}\ \rightarrow\ a_{n+3}\ \rightarrow\ \cdots a_{2n+4}\ =\ a_{0}.

We set
p
true everywhere except
a_{n+2}
and
q
true only
at
a_{0}
.

Announcing
p
means that we delete
a_{n+2}
.
So
A_{n}(\mbox{Pub}\ p)
splits into two disjoint pieces.
this means that in
A_{n}(\mbox{Pub}\ p)
,
a_{1}
does not satisfy

\Diamond^{*}q
. But
a_{n+3}
does satisfy it.

We show that
a_{1}
and
a_{n+3}
agree
on
all sentences of
{\cal L}_{1}
of rank
\leq n
.
For this, we show that
I\!I
has a
winning strategy in the
n
-round game on
between
(A_{n},a_{n+1})
and
(A_{n},a_{n+3})
.
Then we appeal to
Proposition
7.2
.

I\!I
’s strategy is as follows: if
I
ever makes a

\Diamond^{*}
move,
I\!I
should make a move on the other side
to the exact same point. (Recall that
A_{n}
is a cycle.)
Thereafter,
I\!I
should mimic
I
’s moves exactly.
Since the play will end with the same point in the two
structures,
I\!I
wins.
But if
I
never makes a
\Diamond^{*}
move, the play will
consist of
n

\Diamond
-moves.
I\!I
should simply
make the same moves in the appropriate structures.
Since
a_{n+2}
is
n+1

steps from
a_{1}
, and
a_{0}=a_{2n+4}
is
n+1
steps from
a_{n+3}
,

I\!I
will win the play
in this case.

So at this point we conclude that for all
n
,

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible by any single
sentence of
{\cal L}_{1}
of rank
n
.
We conclude by extending this to show that

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible by
any
set
of sentences of
{\cal L}_{1}
. Suppose towards
a contradiction
that
\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
were equivalent to the set

T\subseteq{\cal L}_{1}
. Consider the following models
{\cal A}

and
{\cal B}
:
{\cal A}=\oplus_{n\geq 0}(A_{n},a^{n}_{1})
; i.e., take
the disjoint union of the models

\oplus A_{n}
, and then identify all points
a^{n}_{1}
.
We also rename the common
a^{n}_{1}
point to be
a
,
and we take this as the
distinguished point. So we consider
({\cal A},a)
.
Similarly, let
{\cal B}=\oplus_{n\geq 0}(A_{n},a^{n}_{n+3})
.
For clarity we’ll rename each point
a^{m}_{j}
to be

b^{m}_{j}
. And we write
b
for the distinguished point of
{\cal B}
.
The construction
insures that
({\cal A},a)\models\neg\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
and
({\cal B},b)\models\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
.

By definition of
T
,
({\cal B},b)\models\varphi
for all
\varphi\in T
.
Let
\varphi\in T
be such that
({\cal A},a)\models\neg\varphi
.
Let
m=|\varphi|
.
Let

{\cal C}\quad=\quad\oplus(\{(A_{n},a^{n}_{1}):n\neq m\}\cup\{(A_{m},a^{m}_{m+3})\}).

Notice that we switched exactly one of the identified points.
Just as before, we’ll rename the points of
{\cal C}
and call the overall
distinguished point
c
. It is easy to check
that

({\cal C},c)\models\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
.
And since this sentence is equivalent to
T
, we also have

({\cal C},c)\models\varphi
. But we now show that

({\cal A},a)
and
({\cal C},c)
agree on all sentences of rank
\leq m
.
This implies that
({\cal A},a)\models\varphi
, giving the needed contradiction.

Here is a winning strategy for
I\!I
in the
m
-round game between

({\cal A},a)
and
({\cal C},c)
.
If
I
opens with anything besides
c^{m}_{m+3}
or
a^{m}_{1}
,

I\!I
should play the corresponding point on the other side
and thereafter play in the obvious way. If
I
opens with

c^{m}_{m+3}
,
I\!I
should play with
a^{m}_{1}
and thereafter
play with basically the same strategy as in the first part
of this theorem; similarly, if
I
opens with
a^{m}_{1}
,
I\!I

should reply
c^{m}_{m+3}
and thereafter play via the strategy
in the first part of this proof, where we dealt with single sentences.

Partition models

Often in epistemic logic one is concerned with models
in which every accessibility relation
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
is an equivalence
relation. We can obtain a version of
Theorem
7.3
which shows that
even on this smaller class of models, public announcements add
expressive power to
{\cal L}_{1}
. However, we must use two agents:
with one agent and an equivalence relation
\Box^{*}\varphi
is equivalent to
\Box\varphi
. Thus every sentence in

{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
on one-agent equivalence relations is
equivalent to a purely modal sentence.

Theorem 7.4

The
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
sentence

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond_{A,B}^{*}q
is not expressible
by any set of sentences of

{\cal L}_{1}
, even on the class of models in which
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
and
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{B}$}\hskip 7.22743pt

are equivalence relations.

Proof

We fix a number
N
and first show that

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible by any single
sentence of
{\cal L}_{1}
of rank
N
.
Let
n
be the smallest even number strictly
larger than
N
.
Let
C_{n}
be as defined in Section
3.2
above.

Note that
C_{n}
does have the property that
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt

and
\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{B}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
are equivalence relations. In addition,

(\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$})^{*}
is the same relation as

\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt
.
So
\Box^{*}_{A}\varphi
is equivalent to

\Box_{A}\varphi
(and similarly for
B
).
Finally,
(\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}\cup\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{B}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$})^{*}
is the universal relation.
So if any point whatsoever satisfies a sentence

, then all points satisfy it.

The analysis of Section
3.2
shows that
the points
a_{n+1}
and
a_{3n+1}
differ on
our sentence
\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond_{A,B}^{*}q
.

We continue by showing
that
I\!I
has a
winning strategy in the
n
-round game on
C_{n}

from
a_{n+1}
and
a_{3n+1}
. If
I
ever makes a

\Diamond^{*}
move, then
I\!I
should move to the same

I
perfectly.
Thus we may assume that
I
never makes any
\Diamond^{*}
moves.
In this case,
I
will never move either point to

a_{1}
,
a_{2n+1}
, or
a_{4n+1}
. In other words,
all points in the play will satisfy

.
So as long as

plays
\Diamond
-moves,
I\!I
can
follow arbitrarily. Once again, since the game goes for
n
rounds,
this will be a winning strategy.

At this point we know that

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible by any single
sentence of
{\cal L}_{1}
of rank
n>N
. We use the same idea
as in Theorem
7.3

to show that

\langle\mbox{Pub}\ p\rangle\Diamond^{*}q
is not expressible by any set
of sentences of
{\cal L}_{1}
. In fact, virtually the same proof
goes through.

7.3
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pri}})

It is natural to assume that

.

Theorem 7.5

The
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pri}})
-sentence
\chi=\langle\mbox{Pri}^{A}\ p,{\sf true}\rangle\Diamond_{A}^{*}\Diamond_{B}\neg p

cannot be expressed by any set
T

of sentences of

We shall use the models

and

from Section

.
It would be good to keep the picture in mind while reading

Lemma 7.6

For all

.
That is, there is a bisimulation between

Lemma 7.7

Let

\langle\mbox{Pri}^{A}\ p,{\sf true}\rangle\Diamond_{A}^{*}\Diamond_{B}\neg p

This point was mentioned in Section

\dashv

We define a function
|\varphi|
on
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
:

|p|=0
for

|\neg\varphi|=|\varphi|
,

|\varphi\wedge\psi|=\max(|\varphi|,|\psi|)
,

|\Diamond_{A}\varphi|=1+|\varphi|
,
|\Diamond_{B}\varphi|=1
,

|\Diamond_{{\cal B}}^{*}\varphi|=|\varphi|
for all

,
and
|[\mbox{Pub}\ \varphi]\psi|=\max(|\varphi|,|\psi|)
.

Lemma 7.8

Let
J\subseteq N^{+}
,

f\,:J\rightarrow N^{+}
,
and
j\in J
.
Let
\varphi\in{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
, and let
1\leq k\leq f(j)
.
If
f(j)-k\geq|\varphi|
, then

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\varphi\quad\mbox{iff}\quad({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\varphi\quad\mbox{iff}\quad({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\varphi.

Proof

By induction on
\varphi
. The base step for atomic
p

and the induction steps for the boolean connectives
are all trivial.

The induction step for
\Diamond_{A}\varphi

Assume that
f(j)-k\geq|\Diamond_{A}\varphi|
.
We prove the needed equivalences in a cycle.

Assume first that
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\Diamond_{A}\varphi
.
Since
a\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}a
and
a\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}b
in
{\mathbf{S}}_{f}
,
there are two cases.
If
a
itself satisfies
\varphi
in
A
, then
the reflexive arrow on
c^{j}_{k}

shows that
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\Diamond_{A}\varphi
.
And if
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},b)\models\varphi
, then
clearly
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\Diamond\varphi
via
c^{j}_{k}\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}b
.

Second, assume that

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\Diamond_{A}\varphi
.
We shall not go into details on the subcases parallel to
what we saw in the previous paragraph.
The only subcase worth mentioning is where

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k+1})\models\varphi
. Note here that

f(j)-(k+1)\geq|\Diamond_{A}\varphi|-1=|\varphi|
.
In particular, we have
k+1\leq f(j)
.
And so by our induction hypothesis,
({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\varphi
.
Thus
({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\Diamond_{A}\varphi
.

Finally,
assume that
({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\Diamond_{A}\varphi
.
Remembering that
a\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}c^{j}_{1}
in
B
,
the important subcase is where

({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},c^{j}_{1})\models\varphi
.
By Lemma
7.6
,

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{1})\models\varphi
.
Also,
f(j)-1\geq f(j)-(k+1)\geq|\varphi|
.
So by the induction hypothesis,
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\varphi
.
And then
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\Diamond_{A}\varphi
.

The induction step for
\Diamond_{B}\varphi

Recall that
|\Diamond_{B}\varphi|=1
. Since we assume

f(j)-k\geq 1
, we have
k<f(j)
.
In particular,

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\not\models\Diamond_{B}\varphi
.
All
three needed statements are automatically false,
so all three are equivalent.

The induction step for
\Diamond_{{\cal B}}^{*}\varphi

Assume
f(j)-k>|\Diamond^{*}_{A}\varphi|=|\varphi|
.
We again have a cycle of equivalences.
Before we turn to them, we remind the reader that
with two agents,
{\cal B}
can
be
\{A\}
,
\{B\}

Assume first that
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
.

If

So
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
.
The other case is where

and

.
Note in this case that
c^{j}_{k}\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}x

all non-null paths from

c^{j}\,\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{A}$}\hskip 7.22743pt$}b

Thus in this case

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
.

Next, assume that

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
.
Let
x
be such that

x\neq a
.
By the bisimulation noted in Lemma

({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},x)\models\varphi
.
Also,
b\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!A^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!$}c^{j}_{k}\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!A^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!$}x
.
So

({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
.

Suppose finally that
({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
.
If
({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\models\varphi
, then
by induction hypothesis
we have
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\varphi
. So
in this case
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\Diamond_{A}^{*}\varphi
as well.
The other case is where

({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},x)\models\varphi
for some
x\neq a
.
By bisimulation again,

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},x)\models\varphi
. And in
{\mathbf{S}}_{f}

a\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\longrightarrow\hskip-12.28577pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\!\!A^{*}}$}\hskip 7.22743pt\!\!$}x

Thus

The induction step for
[\mbox{Pub}\ \varphi]\psi

Assume that
f(j)-k>|[\mbox{Pub}\ \varphi]\psi|=\max(|\varphi|,|\psi|)

We remind the reader
of the definitions and of some of the notation used in

In this notation,

[\![[\mbox{Pub}\ \varphi]\psi]\!]_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}_{f}}\quad=\quad\{x\in S_{f}:\mbox{if $x\in[\![\varphi]\!]_{\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}}_{f}}$, then

.

Case I:
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\not\models\varphi
.
Since

f(j)-k\geq|[\mbox{Pub}\ \varphi]\psi|\geq|\varphi|

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\not\models\varphi
, and

({\mathbf{T}}_{f,j},a)\not\models\varphi

all of the model-world pairs satisfy our

Case II:
({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\varphi

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},b)\not\models\varphi

Then by induction hypothesis,

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},c^{j}_{k})\models\varphi

\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}_{f}

are

Each point satisfies

Case III:

({\mathbf{S}}_{f},a)\models\varphi

,

(\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}_{f},(a,\mbox{Pri}^{A}))\quad\equiv\quad(\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}_{f},(c^{j}_{k},\mbox{Pri}^{A}))\quad\equiv\quad(\hat{{\mathbf{T}}}_{f,j},(a,\mbox{Pri}^{A}))

7.4
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
lacks the finite model property

We conclude with a result on
iterating
epistemic
actions. We gave a semantics of sentences of the form

[\alpha^{*}]\varphi
earlier in the paper. The ability to
iterate actions is useful in algorithms and even in
informal presentations of scenarios.
As it happens,
the iteration operation on actions makes our systems
quite expressive, so much so that the finite model
property fails for
{\cal L}_{1}({\mathbf{\Sigma}})
as soon as
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

contains public announcements.

Proposition 7.9

[\mbox{Pub}\ \Diamond{\sf true}]^{*}\Diamond\Box{\sf false}

is satisfiable, but not in any finite model.

Proof

A state
s
in a model
{\mathbf{S}}
is called an

end state
if
s
has no successors.
For each model
{\mathbf{S}}
, let
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}
be the same
model, except with the end states removed.

{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}
is isomorphic to what we have written
earlier as
{\mathbf{S}}\otimes(\mbox{Pub}\ \Diamond{\sf true})
, that
is, the result of publically announcing that
some world is possible. So we see that
our sentence

[\mbox{Pub}\ \Diamond{\sf true}]^{*}\Diamond\Box{\sf false}

holds of
s
just in case the following holds
for all
n
:

1.

s\in{\mathbf{S}}^{(n)}
, the
n
-fold
application of the derivative operation to
{\mathbf{S}}
.

2.

s
has some child which is an end node
(and hence
t
would not belong to
{\mathbf{S}}^{(n+1)}
).

It is clear that any model of

[\mbox{Pub}\ \Diamond{\sf true}]^{*}\Diamond\Box{\sf false}

must be infinite, since the sets

S^{(n+1)}\setminus S^{(n)}
are pairwise disjoint
and nonempty.

There are well-known models of

[\mbox{Pub}\ \Diamond{\sf true}]^{*}\Diamond\Box{\sf false}
.
One would be the set of decreasing sequences
of natural numbers, with
s\rightarrow t
iff

t
is a one-point extension of
s
.
The end nodes are the sequences that end in
0
.
For each
n
,
{\mathbf{S}}^{(n)}
is the submodel
consisting of the sequences which end in
n
.

\dashv

This has several dramatic
consequences for this work. First,
it means that the logics cannot be translated into
the modal mu-calculus, since that logic is known to
have the finite model property. More importantly, we have the following
extension of this result:

Theorem 7.10
(Miller and Moss
[
16
]
)

Concerning
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
:

1.

\{\varphi:\varphi\mbox{ is satisfiable}\}
is
\Sigma^{1}_{1}
-complete.

2.

\{\varphi:\varphi\mbox{ is satisfiable on a finite (tree)
model}\}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-complete.

Indeed, these even hold when
{\cal L}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mbox{\scriptsize pub}})
is
replaced by small fragments, such as the fragment built from

[\mbox{Pub}\ \Diamond{\sf true}]
,
\Diamond^{*}
,
\Diamond
, and boolean connectives
(yet without atomic sentences); or,
the fragment built from arbitrary iterated relativizations
and modal logic (without
\Diamond^{*}
).
These negative results go via
reduction from domino problems.

The upshot is that logics which allow for arbitrary finite
iterations of epistemic actions are not going to be axiomatizable.

We feel that our results on expressive power
are just a sample of what could be done in this area.
We did not investigate the next natural questions:
Do announcements with suspicious outsiders extend the
expressive power of modal logic with all secure private
announcements and common knowledge operators? And then
do announcements with common knowledge of suspicion
add further expressive power?
