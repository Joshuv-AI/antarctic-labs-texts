A new class of distances on complex projective spaces
Rafał Bistroń, Michał Eckstein, Shmuel Friedland, Tomasz Miller, Karol Życzkowski

Abstract
. The complex projective space ℙ ⁡ ( ℂ n ) \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n}) can be interpreted as the space of all quantum pure states of size n n . A distance on this space, interesting from the perspective of quantum physics, can be induced from a classical distance defined on the n n -point probability simplex by the ‘earth mover problem’. We show that this construction leads to a quantity satisfying the triangle inequality, which yields a true distance on complex projective space belonging to the family of quantum 2 2 -Wasserstein distances.

: 15A42; 15A69; 54E35.

Keywords
: Metric on complex projective space, Wasserstein distance, triangle inequality, quantum pure states.

1.
Introduction

Assume that
\mathcal{H}
is an
n
-dimensional space over the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
.
A pure state
|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
is a vector of length one, which we identify with
e^{\mathbf{i}\theta}|\phi\rangle
, with
\mathbf{i}^{2}\vcentcolon=-1
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. After introducing an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{e}_{i}=|i\rangle
, for
i\in[n]\vcentcolon=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we identify
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathbb{C}^{n}
equipped with the standard inner product,
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}
. Then, the space of pure states in
\mathcal{H}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{n}
is identified with the complex projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
of complex dimension
n-1
.

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
can be equipped with a variety of different distance functions, for instance the one induced by the Fubini–Study metric. Another standard choice, known in physics literature as the Hilbert–Schmidt distance, arises as follows:
Let
\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
be the space of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
For
T\in\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote by
\|T\|_{F}=\sqrt{\tr TT^{*}}
the Frobenius (aka Hilbert–Schmidt) norm. On
\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
define a distance

d_{\text{HS}}(S,T)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|S-T\|_{F}
. For states in
\mathcal{H}
, which can be identified with one-dimensional projectors in
\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
as follows:
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\ni\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, the above distance reduces to

(1.1)

d_{\text{HS}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sqrt{1-|\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}|^{2}}=\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}^{*}\mathbf{y})-|\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}|^{2}}.

The goal of this paper is to consider more general types of distances, such that
d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{2}
is a quadratic form on the wedge product
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}
in the exterior product space
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
, which can be identified with the space of skew-symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Namely, assume that
\mathbf{E}
is a self-adjoint positive semidefinite operator on
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. Define

(1.2)

d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\vcentcolon=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|,\quad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{H}.

We study the following problem: For which
\mathbf{E}
does formula (
1.2
) yield a distance on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
?

The study of distances on complex projective spaces is of paramount interest for quantum information processing
[
1
,
14
,
18
]
. They serve as a measure of proximity of quantum states and have multifarious applications in quantum metrology and sensing
[
6
,
10
]
, and quantum machine learning
[
2
,
15
]
.

The distances generated by the
Monge transport problem
enjoy an important classical property:
the distance between any two spin-coherent states,
each concentrated around a given point on the sphere,
is equal to the geodesic distance between
these points
[
26
]
.

A generalised approach to the transport problem by
Kantorovich
[
13
]

allowed one to introduce several classes of
distances based on the
quantum transport problem

[
4
,
11
,
25
]
.
A more general approach by Wasserstein
was also recently applied

[
7
,
19
,
20
]

to construct distances in the space of quantum states.

In this work analyze a particular class of distances (
1.2
) directly connected to the quantum optimal transport problem — see
[
5
,
3
,
9
]
.
Interestingly, the problem of proving the triangle
inequality in its general form stated in Conjecture
2.1

is related to the problem of describing the
set in the complex plane
covered by diagonal product of special unitary matrices

[
17
]
.

2.
Survey of the results

The function
d_{\mathbf{E}}
given by formula (
1.2
) is manifestly symmetric and non-negative. Furthermore, it is non-degenerate, and hence a
semi-distance
if and only if
(\ker\mathbf{E})\cap(\mathcal{H}\wedge\mathcal{H})=\{0\}
.
Therefore, our central question boils down to the problem: For which
\mathbf{E}
does formula (
1.2
) satisfy the
triangle inequality
,

(2.1)

\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|\leq\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|+\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{z}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|,\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})?

In Section
4
we show (Corollary
4.7
) that it is sufficient to prove (
2.1
) for every triple of orthonormal vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
in
\mathcal{H}
.

We shall call the operators satisfying (
2.1
) the
triangular operators
. In Section
4
we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for an operator
\mathbf{E}
to be triangular (Theorem
4.1
) in terms of the eigenvalues of 3-dimensional projectors. We also present a simple sufficient condition (Proposition
4.8
) based on the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{E}
. Then, in Section
5
we show (Proposition
5.5
) that the set of all triangular operators on a given Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
has the structure of a 2-cone. We study its symmetries and extreme rays (Theorem
5.7
).

A special class of semi-distances arises when the operator
\mathbf{E}
is of the form

(2.2)

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})=d_{ij}\,\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j},

for some
d_{ij}\geq 0
and
\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[n]
. The induced semi-distance
d_{\mathbf{E}}
is called the
quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distance

[
5
,
9
]
. These are discussed in Section
6
. Let
D(\mathbf{E})=[d_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{+}
be the symmetric non-negative matrix with zero diagonal, induced by the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{E}
. It is easy to see that
\mathbf{E}
of the form (
2.2
) is a triangular operator only if
D(\mathbf{E})
is a
distance matrix
(see Definition
6.1
), i.e.

(2.3)

\displaystyle d_{ij}\leq d_{ik}+d_{kj},\quad\text{ for all }i,j,k\in[n].

Indeed, if (
2.3
) is violated for a triple of indices
i,j,k
then (
2.1
) fails for
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}_{i}
,
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{u}_{j}
,
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}_{k}
.

Basing on extensive analytical and numerical studies we conjecture that (
2.3
) is actually a necessary
and sufficient
condition for an operator
\mathbf{E}
of the form (
2.2
) to be triangular.

Conjecture 2.1
.

An operator
\mathbf{E}
of the form (
2.2
) is triangular if and only if
D(\mathbf{E})
is a distance matrix.

In other words, Conjecture
2.1
claims that every operator triangular
\mathbf{E}
of the form (
2.2
) is induced by some distance matrix (cf. Definition
6.2
).

For
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
there is only one operator
\mathbf{E}
, up to a multiplicative prefactor, it is of the form (
2.2
) and it is trivially a triangular operator. For
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
every operator
\mathbf{E}
is of the form (
2.2
) (see Corollary
3.2
). In Section
4
we prove (Corollary
4.2
) that for
\dim\mathcal{H}=3
the conjecture holds.

In Section
6
we show the validity of Conjecture
2.1
for a wide class of operators. Specifically, Theorem
6.4
proves Conjecture
2.1
for all operators
\mathbf{E}
induced by the Euclidean distance, i.e. operators, the eigenvalues of which are given by
d_{ij}=\|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}
for some
n
-tuple of distinct points
\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for any
n=\dim\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore, Theorem
6.8
shows that Conjecture
2.1
holds if
d_{ij}=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}
, that is if
\mathbf{E}=O\wedge O
for some operator
O\in\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Finally, in Section
6.4
we present an example, based on Proposition
4.8
, of a triangular operator induced by the
\ell_{1}
distance.

Lastly, in Appendix
B
we provide numerical evidence for the general validity of Conjecture
2.1
. Appendix
A
contains a technical lemma needed in the proof of Theorem
4.6
.

Preliminary results

3.1.
Certain properties of eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators

Denote
\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})\supset\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{H})\supset\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
the space of linear operators of
\mathcal{H}
to itself, the self-adjoint operators
T^{*}=T
, and the cone of positive semidefinite operators, respectively. For
T\in\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
let
\|T\|_{F}=\sqrt{\tr TT^{*}}
be the Frobenius norm. Assume that
T\in\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{H})
.
Assume that
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}
is an orthonormal set of eigenvectors of
T
with the corresponding eigenvalues:

\lambda_{\max}(T)=\lambda_{1}(T)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}(T)=\lambda_{\min}(T).

When there is no ambiguity concerning
T
we shall simply write
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(T)
.

3.2.
2-tensor product and 2-exterior algebra

Let
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}=\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. We endow
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}
with the induced inner product

\langle\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{v}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle.

We identify
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
with
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
via the correspondence
\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
. Then the inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is given by
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr A^{*}B
. The space
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
decomposes as a direct sum of symmetric
\mathrm{S}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n}
and skew-symmetric matrices
\mathrm{A}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n}
respectively.
Note that these two subspaces are orthogonal, and invariant under the conjugation
A\mapsto A^{*}
. The space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}
is a direct sum of symmetric and skew-symmetric tensors
\mathrm{S}^{2}(\mathcal{H})\oplus\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. In mathematics,
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is called the
2
-exterior product of
\mathcal{H}
, while in physics it is the
2
-fermion space. An “irreducible” element in
\mathrm{S}^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is a rank-one tensor
\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
, which corresponds to a symmetric rank-one matrix.
An “irreducible” element in
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
corresponds to

(3.1)

\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{x})\neq\mathbf{0},

which corresponds to a rank-two skew-symmetric matrix.
Using the Lagrange identity

(3.2)

\|\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}-|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|^{2}

one can write the Hilbert–Schmidt distance (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
as
d_{\text{HS}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\|
.
Note that
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
generates a two-dimensional vector space in
\mathcal{H}
. If
\dim\mathcal{H}=3
, then we can identify
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}
with the vector orthogonal to span
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, whose length is
\|\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\|
. In this case, the vector
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}
can be identified with the cross product
\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{y}
. (One can define the cross product
\times
on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
as in the real case.)

Assume that
\mathbf{W}\subset\mathcal{H}
is a
k
-dimensional subspace. Denote by
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W})\subset\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
the subspace generated by
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}
for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{W}
. Clearly,
\dim\Lambda^{2}(W)={\dim\mathbf{W}\choose 2}
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{S}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
. Assume that
\mathbf{W}
is a nontrivial subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. Then by restriction of
\mathbf{A}
to
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W})
we denote an operator
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W}))
such that

\langle\mathbf{B}X,Y\rangle=\langle\mathbf{A}X,Y\rangle\textrm{ for all }X,Y\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W}).

Note that if
\mathbf{A}
is positive (semidefinite) then
\mathbf{B}
is positive (semidefinite).

Assume that
\dim\mathcal{H}=n\geq 2
. Let
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}
be an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j},1\leq i<j\leq n
is an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}\wedge\mathcal{H}
. Note that if
\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j}
is viewed as a skew-symmetric matrix, then its rank equals
2
. Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n(n-1)/2}
be an orthonormal basis in
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, for
n>3
, the vectors
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n(n-1)/2}
need not to be irreducible. Indeed, we can choose
A_{1}
corresponding to a skew-symmetric matrix of maximum rank
2\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. The case
n=3
is special:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a three dimensional subspace of a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{C}
. Then:

(a)

Any vector in
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is of the form
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}
.

(b)

Any orthonormal basis in
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is of the form
\mathbf{u}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{3},\mathbf{u}_{3}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{1}

\mathcal{H}

Proof.

It is enough to assume that
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Then
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
is viewed as the space of skew-symmetric matrices

\mathrm{A}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}=\left\{X\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3},X=\begin{bmatrix}0&x_{1}&x_{2}\\
-x_{1}&0&x_{3}\\
-x_{2}&-x_{3}&0\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

The inner product on
\mathrm{A}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}
is
\langle X,Y\rangle=\tr X^{*}Y
.
Assume that
X\neq 0
. Recall that since
X
is skew-symmetric it follows that
\det X=0
. That is, at least one eigenvalue of
X
is zero. Let
\mathbf{u}_{3},\|\mathbf{u}_{3}\|=1
be the corresponding eigenvector of
X
:
X\mathbf{u}_{3}=0
. Complete
\mathbf{u}_{3}
to an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{3}
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Let
U=[\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{u}_{2}]\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
. Note that
U
is unitary. Set
Y=U^{\top}XU=[U^{\top}[(X\mathbf{u}_{1})(X\mathbf{u}_{2})\mathbf{0}]
. Note that
Y
is skew-symmetric and the last column of
Y
is zero. Hence,

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&y_{1}&0\\
-y_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}=y_{1}(\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}-\mathbf{e}_{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\top})=y_{1}\sqrt{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{2},

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=y_{1}\sqrt{2}(\bar{U}\mathbf{e}_{1})\wedge(\bar{U}\mathbf{e}_{2}).

This shows part (a).

Observe that
\langle X,X\rangle=1
iff
|y_{1}|=1/\sqrt{2}
. By considering
U^{\top}XU
we can assume as above that

X=\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\,\,0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
\,\,0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Suppose we make a change of coordinates in the subspace spanned by
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}
:

\mathbf{u}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}u_{ij}\mathbf{e}_{j},\quad i\in[2],\quad U=[u_{ij}]\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}.

Then
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}
are two orthonormal vectors if and only if
U
is unitary.
It is straightforward to show that
\mathbf{u}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{2}=\det U\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{2}
.

Assume now that
Z\in\mathrm{A}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}
has norm one and is orthogonal to
X
.
So
Z=\mathbf{z}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{z}_{2}
, where
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}
are orthogonal and
\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|=\|\mathbf{z}_{2}\|=1
.
Recall that the intersection of the subspaces
\mathbf{W}_{1}=\text{span}(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2})
and

\mathbf{W}_{2}=\text{span}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2})
are either equal or their intersection contains exactly one line spanned by the vector
\mathbf{y},\|\mathbf{y}\|=1
. Let us now change the bases in
\mathbf{W}_{1}
and
\mathbf{W}_{2}
into
\{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}\}
and
\{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w}\}
, respectively. If
\mathbf{W}_{1}=\mathbf{W}_{2}
then we choose
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}
and
X
and
Z
are not orthogonal. Hence
\mathbf{W}_{1}\neq\mathbf{W}_{2}
. Again, without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{e}_{1}
. Hence
\mathbf{w}=a\mathbf{e}_{1}+b\mathbf{e}_{3}
. The condition
\tr X^{*}Z=0
yields that
a=0
. Hence we can assume that
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{e}_{3}
. Let us now pick a vector
V\in\mathrm{A}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}
of length one orthogonal to both
X
and
Z
. Representing
X
and
Z
as skew-symmetric matrices we deduce that
that
V=\zeta\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{3}
, where
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
and

.

3.3.
The action of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
on
\mathcal{H}
and a variational formula

Let
C\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. We associate with
C
an anti-linear operator
C^{\vee}
on
\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
:

C^{\vee}(a\mathbf{x}+b\mathbf{y})=\bar{a}C^{\vee}(\mathbf{x})+\bar{b}C^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}),

such that the following equality holds

(3.3)

\langle C^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\rangle=\langle C,\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\rangle\textrm{ for }\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{H}.

We shall only be interested in the operators of the form
C=\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}
, for some
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{H}
. Recall that

\langle\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{y}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}\rangle\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\rangle

and hence

(3.4)

(\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b})^{\vee}(\mathbf{y})=\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{b}\rangle\mathbf{a}-\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{b}.

Note also that

\langle C^{\vee}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}\rangle=-\langle C,\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\rangle.

In what follows we need to consider the following variation of a quadratic form in
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}
:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{S}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
. Then

(3.5)

d\left\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\right\rangle=\\
=2\Re\left(\left\langle\big(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}),d\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\left\langle\big(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}),d\mathbf{y}\right\rangle\right).

Suppose furthermore that
\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
,
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
\|\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|>0
. Then

(3.6)

d\|\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|=\\
\frac{1}{\|\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|}\;d\left\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\right\rangle.

The proof of the lemma is straightforward and we leave it to the reader.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathrm{S}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
and consider the critical points of
\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\rangle
on the product state space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\times\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Then
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star})\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\times\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is a critical point if and only if the following
equalities hold

(3.7)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}^{\star})=\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}),\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}\rangle\mathbf{x}^{\star},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|=1,

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=-\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}),\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}\rangle\mathbf{y}^{\star},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}^{\star}\|=1.

In particular, for
\lambda=\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}),\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}\rangle
and any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star})-\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{u}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star}\rangle=

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star})-\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{x}^{\star}\wedge\mathbf{v}\rangle=0.

Proof.

Since we impose the conditions
\|\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{y}\|=1
we introduce the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,\mu\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle
, with
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that

\lambda d\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle=2\lambda\Re\langle\mathbf{x},d\mathbf{x}\rangle,\quad\mu d\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle=2\mu\Re\langle\mathbf{y},d\mathbf{y}\rangle.

Combine the above identity with (
3.5
) to deduce (
3.7
).
The equalities (
3.6
) follows straightforwardly from (
3.5
) and
(
3.3
).
∎

Note the the variety

4.
The triangle inequality

In this section we study some general necessary and sufficient conditions for an operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{B}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
to be triangular. Observe that for any
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{B}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
one has the equality

\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|=\big\|\sqrt{\mathbf{E}^{*}\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\big\|.

Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
.

The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional vector space of dimension at least
3
. Assume that
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
. Then, the triangle inequality holds (
2.1
)
if and only if the following condition holds:

For any 3-dimensional subspace
\mathbf{W}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, with
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W})
denoting the restriction of
\mathbf{E}^{2}
to
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W})
, we have:

(4.1)

\sqrt{d_{12}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W}))}\leq\sqrt{d_{23}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W}))}+\sqrt{d_{13}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W}))}.

In next subsections we provide the intermediate steps of the proof and the final proof is given on page
4.3
. For the most part we discuss the case when
\mathbf{E}
is positive definite.
Then, the case of a positive semidefinite
\mathbf{E}
can be viewed as a limiting case of positive definite
\mathbf{E}
.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
4.1
is the proof of validity of Conjecture
2.1
for
\dim\mathcal{H}=3
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\dim\mathcal{H}=3
. An operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
is triangular if and only if
d_{12}(\mathbf{E})\leq d_{23}(\mathbf{E})+d_{13}(\mathbf{E})
.

4.1.
The case where
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
are linearly dependent

In this subsection we show that if
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
are in a two-dimensional
\mathbf{W}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

then the triangle inequality holds.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
are linearly dependent. Then the triangle inequality (
2.1
) holds.
Equality holds if either
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}=0
or
\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}=0
, or span
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
is a 2-dimensional subspace
\mathbf{W}
and the restriction of
\mathbf{E}^{2}
to
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W})
is zero.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
then
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
are colinear and equality in (
2.1
) holds. Similarly, if
\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
equality in (
2.1
) holds.
Assume that
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that

\mathbf{W}=\textrm{span}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\textrm{span}(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}),\quad\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0)^{\top},\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1)^{\top}.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be the restriction of
\mathbf{E}^{2}
to
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W})
. If
\mathbf{A}=0
then the triangle inequality holds trivially.
It is left to consider the case where
\mathbf{A}
is identity on the one dimensional space
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbf{W})
. That is
\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{u}\wedge\mathbf{v})\|=\|\mathbf{u}\wedge\mathbf{v}\|
for
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{W}
.
Without loss of generality we can assume that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(1,0)^{\top},\;\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})^{\top},|y_{1}|^{2}+|y_{2}|^{2}=1,\;\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})^{\top},|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}=1,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}\|=|y_{2}|,\quad\|\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}\|=z_{2}>0,\quad\|\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}\|=|y_{1}z_{2}-y_{2}z_{1}|>0.

If
z_{2}\geq y_{2}
one has strict inequalities in (
2.1
).
To show the inequality (
2.1
) it is enough to consider the case
1\geq y_{2}>z_{2}>0
. It is straightforward to show that

z_{2}+|y_{1}z_{2}-y_{2}z_{1}|-y_{2}\geq z_{2}-y_{2}+|z_{1}|y_{2}-|y_{1}|z_{2}=\\
=z_{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1-y_{2}^{2}}\right)-y_{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1-z_{2}^{2}}\right)=\\
=y_{2}z_{2}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{1+\sqrt{1-y_{2}^{2}}}-\frac{z_{2}}{1+\sqrt{1-z_{2}^{2}}}\right).

It is left to show that

f(y_{2},z_{2})\vcentcolon=\frac{y_{2}}{1+\sqrt{1-y_{2}^{2}}}-\frac{z_{2}}{1+\sqrt{1-z_{2}^{2}}}>0,\quad\textrm{ for }y_{2}>z_{2}>0.

Fix
z_{2}
and observe that
f(y_{2},z_{2})
increases on
[0,1]
in
y_{2}
. Hence

f(y_{2},z_{2})>f(z_{2},z_{2})=0
for
y_{2}\in(z_{2},1)
.
∎

4.2.
A minimum problem

Assume that
n=\dim\mathcal{H}\geq 3
and let
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
be positive definite.
Consider the minimum problem

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{E})=\min_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{y}\|=\|\mathbf{z}\|=1}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}),

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|+\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|.

Clearly, the triangle inequality holds if and only if
\mu(\mathbf{E})=0
. The following lemma follows straightforwardly from Lemma
3.3
:

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
n\geq 3
and
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
is positive definite.
Let
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
be defined as in (
4.2
). Assume that
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
are linearly independent. Then

(4.3)

\displaystyle df(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|}\Re\Big(\big\langle(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}))^{\vee}(\mathbf{z}),d\mathbf{x}\big\rangle-\big\langle(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}))^{\vee}(\mathbf{x}),d\mathbf{z}\big\rangle\Big)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|}\Re\Big(\big\langle(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}))^{\vee}(\mathbf{z}),d\mathbf{y}\big\rangle-\big\langle(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}))^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}),d\mathbf{z}\big\rangle\Big)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|}\Re\Big(\big\langle(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}))^{\vee}(\mathbf{x}),d\mathbf{y}\big\rangle-\big\langle(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}))^{\vee}(\mathbf{y}),d\mathbf{x}\big\rangle\Big).

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that
n\geq 3
and
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
is positive definite.
Let
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
be defined as in (
4.2
). Then
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{3}
is a nontrivial critical point of
f
if
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
are linearly independent and the following conditions hold:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|}\big(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|}\big(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{y})=\lambda\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|}\big(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{z})+\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|}\big(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{x})=\mu\mathbf{y},

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|}\big(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}\|}\big(\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\big)^{\vee}(\mathbf{y})=\nu\mathbf{z},

where

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|,

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|+\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|.

Suppose furthermore that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})<0
. Then
\mathbf{z}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, and either
\mathbf{x}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{y}
or

(4.5)

\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|=0.

Proof.

The proof of (
4.4
) is similar to the proof of Proposition
3.4
. Introduce Lagrange multipliers
\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,\mu\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle,\nu\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}\rangle
to deduce the first three equalities in (
4.4
). Now take inner product of the first equality with
\mathbf{x}
, the second equality with
\mathbf{y}
, the third equality with
\mathbf{z}
to deduce the last three equalities.

Assume now that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})<0
. Observe that
\lambda,\mu,-\nu<0
.
Take an inner product of the first identity with
\mathbf{z}
and the last identity with
\mathbf{x}
. Use the equality (
3.3
), and the fact that
\mathbf{z}\wedge\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{x}=0
to deduce the equalities

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\rangle=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\rangle=-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|\nu\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}\rangle.

Assume that
\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle\neq 0
. Then we have the equality

(4.6)

\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|\lambda=-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|\nu,

Let

a=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|,>0\quad b=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|>0,\quad c=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|>0.

Then

\lambda=a-c<0,\quad\mu=b-c<0,\quad\nu=a+b>0.

The equality (
4.6
) boils down to

c(a-c)=-b(a+b)\Rightarrow\big(c-(a+b)\big)(c+b)=0\Rightarrow c-(a+b)=0\Rightarrow f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=0,

which contradicts our assumption
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})<0
. Hence
\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle=0
.
Similarly,
\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\rangle=0
.

Now, take the inner product of the first identity with
\mathbf{y}
and the inner product of the second identity with
\mathbf{x}
to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\rangle=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\rangle=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|\mu\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\rangle.

Assume that
\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\neq 0
. Then

a(a-c)=b(b-c)c\Rightarrow(a-b)(a+b-c)=0\Rightarrow a=b,

which proves (
4.5
).
∎

4.3.
The three dimenisonal case

Recall (Corollary
3.2
) that if
\dim\mathcal{H}=~3
then every operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{B}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
is of the form (
2.2
). In order to simplify the notation let us assume that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{u}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{3})=d_{23}\mathbf{u}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{3},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{u}_{3}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{1})=d_{13}\mathbf{u}_{3}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{u}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{2})=d_{12}\mathbf{u}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{2},

\displaystyle\text{with }\lambda_{23}\geq\lambda_{13}\geq\lambda_{12}>0.

The following theorem is a refined version of Theorem
4.1
for
n=3
:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a three dimensional Hilbert space.
Assume that
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
and
let
\mu(\mathbf{E})
be given by (
4.2
).
Then,

(4.8)

\mu(\mathbf{E})=\min(0,d_{12}+d_{13}-d_{23}).

Furthermore:

(a)

We have
\mu(\mathbf{E})=0
if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

Either
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}
or
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
are colinear. If
d_{23}<d_{13}+d_{12}
there are no other cases.

(ii)

We have
d_{23}=d_{12}+d_{13}
and the vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{H})
are linearly independent,
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}_{1}
, and both
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are orthogonal to
\mathbf{z}
. Furthermore,
if
d_{12}=d_{13}
then
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{u}_{3}
and if

d_{13}>d_{12}
then either
\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}=\{\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{3}\}

or

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\sin\theta\mathbf{u}_{2}+e^{\mathbf{i}\psi}\cos\theta\mathbf{u}_{3},\;\mathbf{y}=-e^{-\mathbf{i}\psi}\sin\phi\mathbf{u}_{2}+\cos\phi\mathbf{u}_{3},

\displaystyle\text{with }\theta,\phi\in(0,\pi/2),\psi\in[0,2\pi),\quad\tan\theta\tan\phi=\frac{d_{13}}{d_{12}}.

(b)

If
d_{23}>d_{12}+d_{13}
then
\mu(\mathbf{E})=-d_{23}+d_{12}+d_{13}
. The minimum is achieved if and only if
\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}=\{\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{3}\}
and
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}_{1}
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.1
yields that there exists an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{3}
such that (
4.7
) holds.
We first show the inequality

(4.10)

\mu(E)\leq\min(0,d_{12}+d_{13}-d_{23}).

Clearly,
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=0
, hence
\mu(\mathbf{E})\leq 0
.
Assume that
d_{23}>d_{12}+d_{13}
. Then
f(\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{3},\mathbf{u}_{1})=d_{12}+d_{13}-d_{23}<0
. This proves (
4.10
).

Without loss of generality we assume that
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
. It is sufficient to study the cases
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{0},\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Since
\mathbf{E}
is positive definite, Proposition
4.3
yields that strict triangle inequality holds.
We thus restrict ourselves to the case where
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
are linearly independent, and
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\mu(\mathbf{E})\leq 0
for some
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
Lemma
4.5
yields that
\mathbf{z}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
.
Without loss of generality we can assume
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0,0)^{\top}
.
Recall that the standard basis
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
induces a standard orthonormal basis

\mathbf{e}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{3},\mathbf{e}_{3}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
Then, in this orthonormal basis, the operator
\mathbf{E}^{2}
is represented by a positive definite Hermitian matrix
H=[h_{ij}]\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
.
Let

U=[u_{ij}]\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
with
\det U=1
. Change the basis
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3}

to a new orthonormal basis

\mathbf{g}_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{1},\quad\mathbf{g}_{i+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}u_{ij}\mathbf{e}_{j+1}\textrm{ for }i\in[2].

Observe that
\mathbf{g}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{g}_{3}=\mathbf{e}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{3}
. In this basis
\mathbf{E}^{2}
is represented by a Hermitian matrix
G=[g_{ij}]\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
. Choose
U
such that the
2\times 2
principal submatrix
[g_{ij}]_{i,j\in\{2,3\}}
is diagonal. That is,
g_{23}=0
:

G=\begin{bmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}&g_{13}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&0\\
g_{31}&0&g_{33}\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, we can assume that
g_{22}\geq g_{33}>0
.

As
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
we can assume that
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
are of the form:

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(\sin\theta)\mathbf{g}_{2}+s(\cos\theta)\mathbf{g}_{3},\quad\mathbf{y}=-t(\sin\phi)\mathbf{g}_{2}+(\cos\phi)\mathbf{g}_{3},

\displaystyle\text{with }\quad\theta,\phi\in[0,\pi/2],\quad s,t\in\mathbb{C},|s|=|t|=1.

Then
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y}=\big(\sin\theta\cos\phi+st\cos\theta\sin\phi\big)\mathbf{g}_{2}\wedge\mathbf{g}_{3}
and

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|=\big|\sin\theta\cos\phi+st\cos\theta\sin\phi\big|\sqrt{g_{11}},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|=\sqrt{g_{22}\cos^{2}\theta+g_{33}\sin^{2}\theta},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|=\sqrt{g_{22}\cos^{2}\phi+g_{33}\sin^{2}\phi}.

Hence,

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|+\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z})\|-\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|=\\
\;\;=\sqrt{g_{22}\cos^{2}\theta+g_{33}\sin^{2}\theta}\,+\sqrt{g_{22}\cos^{2}\phi+g_{33}\sin^{2}\phi}\,-\\
-|\sin\theta\cos\phi+st\cos\theta\sin\phi|\sqrt{g_{11}}.

Since we assumed that
\mu(\mathbf{E})=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
we must have the equality

|\sin\theta\cos\phi+st\cos\theta\sin\phi|=\sin\theta\cos\phi+\cos\theta\sin\phi\Rightarrow s=e^{\mathbf{i}\psi},t=e^{-\mathbf{i}\psi}.

As we assumed that
g_{23}=0
it follows that the eigenvalues of the submatrix
[g_{ij}]_{i=j=2}^{3}
are
g_{22}\geq g_{33}
. The Cauchy interlacing theorem and the maximum characterization of
d_{23}^{2}

g_{22}\geq d_{13}^{2}\geq g_{33}\geq d_{12}^{2},\quad g_{11}\leq d_{23}^{2}.

Hence, employing the function
F
defined in (
A.1
), we have

(4.12)

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\geq F(d_{13},d_{12},t,\theta,\phi),\textrm{ for }t=\frac{d_{23}}{d_{13}+d_{12}}.

We first consider the case
d_{23}<d_{12}+d_{13}
. Then
t<1
and
Lemma
A.1
implies that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})>0
contrary to our assumption that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\mu(\mathbf{E})\leq 0
. Hence, in this case
\mu(\mathbf{E})=0
. That is, the triangle inequality holds, and equality in the triangle inequality holds if either
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{y}\wedge\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
.

Assume now that
d_{23}=d_{12}+d_{13}
. Note that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\leq d_{12}+d_{13}-d_{23}=0
. Next observe that
t
in the inequality (
4.12
) satisfies
t\leq 1
. Lemma
A.1
then implies that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\geq 0
.
Therefore
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\mu(\mathbf{E})=0
in this case too. That is, the triangle inequality holds.

We now consider the equality case in the triangle inequality, that is
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=0
. Consequently,
g_{11}=d_{23}^{2}=(d_{12}+d_{13})^{2}
. In this case we claim that
G
is a diagonal matrix
\diag(d_{23}^{2},d_{13}^{2},d_{12}^{2})

5.
The 2-cone of triangular operators

Let

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{1}(\mathcal{H})=\{S\in\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{H}),\tr S=1\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{+,1}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathrm{S}_{1}(\mathcal{H}).

Observe that
\mathrm{S}_{+,1}(\mathcal{H})
is a compact convex set of density operators on

of real dimension

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space. A set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
is called
a 2-cone if the following conditions hold:

(a)

0\in\mathcal{C}
.

(b)

If
T\in\mathcal{C}
then
tT\in\mathcal{C}
for
t\in[0,\infty)
.

(c)

If
S,T\in\mathcal{C}
then
\sqrt{S^{2}+T^{2}}\in\mathcal{C}
.

A 2-cone
\mathcal{C}
is trivial if
\mathcal{C}=\{0\}
.

For a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
and
p>0
denote

(5.2)

\mathcal{S}^{p}=\{T^{p},\,T\in\mathcal{S}\}\subseteq\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H}).

The following lemma is straightforward:

Lemma 5.2
.

A set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
is a 2-cone if and only if

\mathcal{C}^{2}
is a cone in
\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
.

An element
T\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}
is called and an extreme ray if and only if
T^{2}
is an extreme ray in
\mathcal{C}^{2}
. That is, if
T^{2}=T_{1}^{2}+T_{2}^{2}
for some
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
then
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are colinear. Denote by Ext
(\mathcal{C})
the set of extreme rays of
\mathcal{C}
.
Observe that the set of extreme rays of the cone
\mathcal{C}^{2}
is determined by
\text{Ext}^{2}(\mathcal{C})=\text{Ext}(\mathcal{C}^{2})
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume that
\dim\mathcal{H}=n
and let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
be a closed nontrivial 2-cone.
Then
\mathrm{Ext}(\mathcal{C})\neq\emptyset
and every
T^{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
is the sum of at most

n^{2}
of squares of distinct extreme rays of
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathrm{S}_{+,1}(\mathcal{H}))^{1/2}.

Then
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{2}
is a compact convex set in
\mathrm{S}_{+,1}(\mathcal{H})
. Carathéodory’s theorem yields that Ext
(\mathcal{C}_{1})
, the set of the extreme points of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, is nonempty, and every point of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a convex combination of at most
\dim\mathcal{C}_{1}+1
distinct
extreme points of
\mathcal{C}_{1}

[
21
]
. As
\dim\mathcal{C}_{1}\leq\dim\mathrm{S}_{+,1}(\mathcal{H})=n^{2}-1
we deduce the proposition.
∎

Definition 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space of

\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))

Assume that

Proof.

Clearly,

=\zeta^{2}\left(\frac{\nu_{1,2}}{2\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\nu_{1,1}^{2}}{8\lambda_{1}^{3}}-\frac{\nu_{2,2}}{2\lambda_{2}}+\frac{\nu_{2,1}^{2}}{8\lambda_{2}^{3}}-\frac{\nu_{3,2}}{2\lambda_{2}}+\frac{\nu_{3,1}^{2}}{8\lambda_{3}^{3}}\right)+O(\zeta^{3}).

The equality
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}
yields
that first coefficient of the above Taylor expansion is zero. The equality (
5.8
) yields that the second coeffcient of the above Taylor expansion is zero. We claim that the coefficient of
\zeta^{2}
is positive.

Consider first

\frac{\nu_{1,2}}{\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\nu_{2,2}}{\lambda_{2}}-\frac{\nu_{3,2}}{\lambda_{3}}\\
=\frac{|b_{12}|^{2}}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}}+\frac{|b_{13}|^{2}}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}+\frac{|b_{12}|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}-\frac{|b_{23}|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3})}+\frac{|b_{13}|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}+\frac{|b_{23}|^{2}}{\lambda_{3}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3})}.

As we assume that
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}
the above expression is positive,
unless
b_{12}=b_{13}=b_{23}=0
.

Next we consider the expression:

\displaystyle-\frac{\nu_{1,1}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}^{3}}+\frac{\nu_{1,1}^{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{3}}+\frac{\nu_{3,1}^{2}}{\lambda_{3}^{3}}=-\frac{(b_{22}+b_{33})^{2}}{\lambda_{1}^{3}}+\frac{b_{22}^{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{3}}+\frac{b_{33}^{2}}{\lambda_{3}^{3}}.

In view of the last equality in (
5.9
) we deduce that if
(b_{22},b_{33})^{\top}\neq\mathbf{0}
then
b_{22}b_{33}<0
. As
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}
we deduce that the above expression is positive, unless
b_{11}=b_{22}=b_{33}=0
. As
\mathbf{B}\neq 0
we must have that the coefficient of
\zeta^{2}
in the expression (
5.11
) is positive. Hence for small nonzero
\zeta
the operator
\mathbf{F}(\zeta)
is not in
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{H})
, contrary to our assumptions.

We now consider the case when
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}>0
and
\lambda_{1}=2\lambda_{2}
. Let
U\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
be a unitary matrix of the form

\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&u_{22}&u_{23}\\
0&u_{32}&u_{33}\end{bmatrix}
.
Note that
U^{*}\mathbf{E}^{2}U=\mathbf{E}^{2}
. Now choose
U
such that the matrix
U^{*}\mathbf{B}U=\mathbf{C}=[c_{ij}]
, such that
c_{23}=0
. Thus, without loss of generality we can assume that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}
, and
b_{23}=0
.
Then
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{F}(\zeta))=\nu_{1}(\zeta)
and we have the same Taylor expansion as before, with
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
, and equalities (
5.7
) hold.
We claim that equalities (
5.10
) hold, where first we let the term

\frac{|b_{23}|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}=0
and then set
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
.
This equalities can be deduced as follows. Assume that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}
, and
b_{23}=0
. Then we have equalities (
5.10
) with
b_{23}=0
. Let
\lambda_{2}\searrow\lambda_{3}
.
Use the same arguments as before to deduce the contradiction.

We are left with the case
\lambda_{3}=0
,
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}>0
.
As we pointed out before we must have
b_{33}=0
, hence
b_{13}=b_{23}=0
. Use a unitary matrix
\begin{bmatrix}u_{11}&u_{12}&0\\
u_{21}&u_{22}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}
to deduce that we can assume that
\mathbf{B}
is a diagonal matrix
\diag(b_{11},b_{22},0)
where
b_{11}=-b_{22}\neq 0
. Then

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{F}(\zeta))=\lambda_{1}^{2}+|b_{11}||\zeta|>\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{F}(\zeta))=\lambda_{2}^{2}-|b_{11}||\zeta|\textrm{ for }\zeta\in(0,r).

Again,
\mathbf{F}(\zeta))\not\in\mathcal{T}_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{H})
contradictory to our assumptions.
∎

6.
Special subclasses of triangular operators

6.1.
Distance-induced triangular operators

In this section we consider a special subset of operators
\mathbf{E}
, which are of the form (
2.2
). Recall that the eigenvalues of such operators can be organised into a symmetric matrix with zero diagonal,
D(\mathbf{E})=[d_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{+}
. Then, using the basis
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{i}
of
\mathcal{H}
, we can write

(6.1)

\displaystyle d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})\|=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}d_{ij}^{2}|x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}|^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{where }\quad\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i},\quad y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}.

Semi-distances (
6.1
) are known under the name of quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distances. They arise from the quantum optimal transport problem as follows (see
[
5
,
9
]
for the details):

Let
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}\in\S_{+,1}(\mathcal{H})
be two density operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
\Gamma^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=\big\{\rho^{AB}\in\S_{+,1}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}),\,\tr_{A}\rho^{AB}=\rho^{B},\tr_{B}\rho^{AB}=\rho^{A}\big\}
be the set of their “quantum couplings”. For a matrix
D(\mathbf{E})
as above define the corresponding
quantum cost matrix
,
C_{\mathbf{E}}\in\S_{+}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
, as

\displaystyle C_{\mathbf{E}}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j>i=1}^{n}d_{ij}\,(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})^{*}.

The quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distance on
\S_{+,1}(\mathcal{H})
associated with such a
C_{\mathbf{E}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\min_{\rho^{AB}\in\Gamma^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})}\tr\big(\rho^{AB}\,C_{\mathbf{E}}^{2}\big)}.

If either of the states
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}
is pure, then
\Gamma^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=\{\rho^{A}\otimes\rho^{B}\}
. Consequently, if
\rho^{A}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
\rho^{A}=\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{*}
, for two vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=\sqrt{\sum_{j>i=1}^{n}d_{ij}^{2}\,\Tr\big[(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{*})(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})^{*}\big]}=d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

A particular example of such an operator
\mathbf{E}
is as follows:
Assume that
O\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
and eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{i}
,

(6.2)

\displaystyle O\mathbf{u}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i},\quad\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0,\quad\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij},\text{ for }i,j\in[n].

Then,
O
induces an operator
\mathbf{E}=O\wedge O
such that
(O\wedge O)(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{y})=(O\mathbf{x})\wedge(O\mathbf{y})
(see
[
8
]
). Hence,

(O\wedge O)(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j},\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n.

That is,
d_{ij}(O\wedge O)=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}
for
i<j
. We shall call such operators
\mathbf{E}

reducible
.

Let
X=\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a set of
n
points equipped with a distance function
d
. (Observe that it is sufficient to consider
N\leq n-1
.) Then, the values of
d
can be organised into a symmetric matrix with zero diagonal,
D(\mathbf{E})=[d_{ij}]\vcentcolon=[d(\mathbf{p}_{i},\mathbf{p}_{j})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{+}
. It leads to the following concept:

Definition 6.1
.

A real
n\times n
matrix
D=[d_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called a
distance matrix
if it satisfies the following properties:

(a)

D
is symmetric:
D^{\top}=D
.

(b)

D
is positive semi-definite:
d_{ij}\geq 0
, for
i,j\in[n]
.

(c)

D
has zero diagonal:
d_{ii}=0
for
i\in[n]
.

(d)

The triangle inequality holds:

(6.3)

d_{ij}+d_{jk}\geq d_{ik},\quad\textrm{ for all }i,j,k\in[n].

We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the set of all
n\times n
distance matrices. A distance matrix is called positive if all its off-diagonal entries are positive.
A distance matrix
D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
is
\ell_{p}^{N}
-induced
if
d_{ij}=\|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{p}
for some
\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
.

For a symmetric nonnegative matrix
S=\vcentcolon S^{\circ 1}
denote

(6.4)

S^{\circ p}\vcentcolon=[s_{ij}^{p}],\quad s_{ij}^{p}=s_{ji}^{p}\geq 0,\quad\text{ for }i,j\in[n]\text{ and }p>0.

It is straightforward to show

(6.5)

D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}\Rightarrow D^{\circ p}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}\textrm{ for }p\in(0,1).

Definition 6.2
.

We say that an operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
is
induced by a distance function

d
on the
n
-point set
X
if
\mathbf{E}
is of the form (
2.2
) and
D(\mathbf{E})
is a distance matrix.

Conjecture
2.1
asserts that every triangular operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
of the form (
2.2
) on a Hilbert space of dimension
n
is induced by some distance function on an
n
-point set
X
.

Note that
D(\mathbf{E}^{p})=D^{\circ p}(\mathbf{E})
, which means that if
\mathbf{E}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is of the form (
2.2
) then
\mathbf{E}^{p}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
for all
p\in(0,1]
. Recall also that Proposition
5.6
implies that if an operator
\mathbf{E}
is triangular then so is
\mathbf{E}^{U}\vcentcolon=(U\wedge U)^{*}\,\mathbf{E}\,(U\wedge U)
, for any ‘local’ unitary
U\in\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Clearly, if
\mathbf{E}
is of the form (
2.2
) then so is
\mathbf{E}^{U}
.

Observe that any operator
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
can be written as a ‘global’ rotation of an operator
\mathbf{E}
of the form (
2.2
). That is, for any
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
there exist a unitary
V\in\mathrm{B}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
and an operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
of the form (
2.2
) such that
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}=V^{*}\mathbf{E}V
. However, such a ‘global’ rotation of a triangular operator
\mathbf{E}
will not, in general, yield a triangular operator, regardless of whether
\mathbf{E}
was of the form (
2.2
) or not.

It is easy to provide a counterexample: Let
\dim\mathcal{H}=4
and let
\mathbf{E}
be induced by the following distance matrix:

(6.6)

\displaystyle d_{12}=2,

\displaystyle d_{13}=3,

\displaystyle d_{23}=1,

\displaystyle d_{14}=1,

\displaystyle d_{24}=3,

\displaystyle d_{34}=2.

Then,
\mathbf{E}
is triangular. Let now
V\in\mathrm{B}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathcal{H}))
be defined as

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{4},\;\text{ if }i=1,j=3,\\
\mathbf{u}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{3},\;\text{ if }i=1,j=4,\\
\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j},\;\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then,
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}=V^{*}\mathbf{E}V
is
not
triangular, because

\displaystyle d_{\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})+d_{\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}}(\mathbf{u}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{3})=3>d_{\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{3})=1.

In the next subsections we provide analytical evidence for the general validity of Conjecture
2.1
. For further, numerical, evidence see Appendix
B
.

6.2.
Triangular operators induced by the Euclidean distance

Let us start with triangular operators induced by the ‘line geometry’, that is
\ell_{2}^{1}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda(\mathcal{H}))
be induced by the
\ell_{2}^{1}
distance, that is

(6.7)

d_{ij}=|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}|>0,\quad\text{ for }\;i,j\in[n],

with
\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
\mathbf{E}
is a triangular operator for any
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

For
n=2
the assertion follows from Proposition
4.3
, while for
n=3
it follows from Theorem
4.6
. Let us then assume that
n>3
and, on the strength of Corollary
4.7
, let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be three orthonormal vectors.

Let us set
\mathbf{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and compute

\displaystyle d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j})^{2}|x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}|^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{i}\mathbf{p}_{j})|x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{i}\mathbf{p}_{j})\left(|x_{i}|^{2}|y_{j}|^{2}-x_{i}y_{j}\bar{x}_{j}\bar{y}_{i}-x_{j}y_{i}\bar{x}_{i}\bar{y}_{j}+|y_{i}|^{2}|x_{j}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{H}^{2}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{H}^{2}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-2\big(\langle\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{H}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-|\langle\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|^{2}\big).

As
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{H}
are three orthonomal vectors we can complete them to an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
. In this basis
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{H}^{2}
are respectively represented by positive definite Hermitian matrices
H=[h_{ij}],H^{2}=[h_{ij}^{(2)}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sqrt{h_{11}^{(2)}+h_{22}^{(2)}-2(h_{11}h_{22}-|h_{12}|^{2})},

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\sqrt{h_{11}^{(2)}+h_{33}^{(2)}-2(h_{11}h_{33}-|h_{13}|^{2})},

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\sqrt{h_{22}^{(2)}+h_{33}^{(2)}-2(h_{22}h_{33}-|h_{23}|^{2})}.

We thus have

\displaystyle d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})-d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-2\big(h_{11}h_{33}-|h_{13}|^{2}+h_{22}h_{33}-|h_{13}|^{2}\big)+

\displaystyle\quad+2h_{33}^{(2)}+2\big(h_{11}h_{22}-|h_{12}|^{2}\big)+

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{h_{11}^{(2)}+h_{33}^{(2)}-2\big(h_{11}h_{33}-|h_{13}|^{2}\big)}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sqrt{h_{22}^{(2)}+h_{33}^{(2)}-2\big(h_{22}h_{33}-|h_{23}|^{2}\big)}.

Now, let us define the matrix
G\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
, as
G=[g_{ij}]=[h_{ij}]
, for
i,j\in[3]
, then
G^{2}=[g_{ij}^{(2)}]
. Clearly,
g^{(2)}_{ii}\leq h_{ii}^{(2)}
for
i\in[3]
and
g_{ij}=h_{ij}
, thus

\displaystyle d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})-d_{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq-2\big(g_{11}g_{33}-|g_{13}|^{2}+g_{22}g_{33}-|g_{13}|^{2}\big)+

\displaystyle\quad+2g_{33}^{(2)}+2\big(g_{11}g_{22}-|g_{12}|^{2}\big)+

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{g_{11}^{(2)}+g_{33}^{(2)}-2\big(g_{11}g_{33}-|g_{13}|^{2}\big)}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sqrt{g_{22}^{(2)}+g_{33}^{(2)}-2\big(g_{22}g_{33}-|g_{23}|^{2}\big)}

\displaystyle=d_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+d_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})-d_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq 0.

where
\mathbf{G}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}G_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}
for some orthonormal basis
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{w}_{3}
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. But the operator
\mathbf{G}
is triangular, hence the last inequality follows.
∎

Thanks to the 2-cone structure of the set of triangular operators, the above theorem can be easily generalised to operators induced by the general Euclidean distance.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda(\mathcal{H}))
be induced by the
\ell_{2}^{N}
distance, that is

(6.8)

d_{ij}=\|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}>0,\quad\text{ for }\;i,j\in[n],

with
\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then,
\mathbf{E}
is a triangular operator for any
n\geq 2
and any
N\leq n-1
.

Proof.

We have

d_{ij}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}-\mathbf{p}_{j}^{k}\big)^{2}},\quad\text{ with }\;\mathbf{p}_{i}=(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{i}^{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\text{ for }i\in[n].

Set

\mathbf{E}_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\big|\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}-\mathbf{p}_{j}^{k}\big|\,\mathbf{u}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{u}_{j},\quad\text{ for }\;k\in[N].

Theorem
6.3
yields that
\mathbf{E}_{k}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, for all
k\in[N]
. Then, Proposition
5.5
implies that
\mathbf{E}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}_{k}^{2}}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
.

Observe that by translation
\mathbf{p}\mapsto\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{a}
we can assume that
\mathbf{p}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
.
As span
(\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n})
is at most of dimension
n-1
, we can assume without loss of generality that
N\leq n-1
.
∎

We now recall the known results due to Schoenberg
[
22
, Theorem 1]
, see also
[
16
]
, that gives simple necessary and sufficient conditions on a distance on
[n]
points, which is realised
as the Euclidean distance on some
n
distinct points in
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
D=[d_{ij}]\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Then there exists
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
such that

d_{ij}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|
for
i,j\in[n]
if and only if the following symmetric matrix
A=[a_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
is positive semidefinite:

(6.9)

a_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\big(d^{2}_{1(i+1)}+d^{2}_{1(j+1)}-d^{2}_{(i+1)(j+1)}\big),\quad i,j\in[n-1].

Assume that
A
is positive semidefinite with
\rank A=r\in[n-1]
.
Then
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
but not in
\mathbb{R}^{r-1}
.

Assume that
d(i,j),i,j\in[n]
is a distance on
[n]
. It is straightforward to show that for each
\gamma\in(0,1)
the function
d(i,j)^{\gamma}
is a distance on
[n]
. (Concavity of the function
x^{\gamma}+y^{\gamma}
.)
In the paper
[
23
, Theorem 3]
it is shown that if
d(i,j),i,j\in[n]
is a distance on
[n]
induced by the Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1},
then the distance
d^{\gamma}
is induced also by the Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

The following theorem is proven by Schoenberg
[
24
, Theorem 5]
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Assume that
p\in[1,2]
, and
D=[\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|_{p}]
. Then, for
\gamma\in[0,p/2]
the distance matrix
D^{\circ\gamma}
induced by
\ell_{2}^{n-1}
.

Together with Theorem
6.4
it yields the following result:

Corollary 6.7
.

Let the operator
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda_{2}(\mathcal{H}))
be induced by
\ell_{p}^{N}
for
p\in[1,2]
. Then
\mathbf{E}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, for all
\gamma\in(0,p/2]
.

6.3.
Reducible triangular operators

Let us now turn to the case of reducible triangular operators, that is
\mathbf{E}=O\wedge O
for some
O\in\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
Denote

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}-\diag(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}),\quad\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Theorem 6.8
.

Assume that
\dim\mathcal{H}\geq 3
. Let
\mathbf{E}\in\mathrm{S}_{+}(\Lambda_{2}(\mathcal{H}))
and
D(\mathbf{E})
is a distance matrix. If
\mathbf{E}
is reducible,
then
\mathbf{E}
is a triangular operator.

To prove this theorem we will need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.9
.

Assume that
n\geq 4
and

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-2})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

\displaystyle a_{i}\geq b_{i}\geq a_{i+1}\geq 0\textrm{ for }i\in[n-1].

Assume that
\Delta(\mathbf{a})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Then
\Delta(\mathbf{b})\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}
.

Proof.

Assume that
D=[d_{ij}]\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Suppose that
d_{ij}=0
for
i\neq j
.
It is straightforward to show that
d_{ik}=d_{jk}
for all
k\in[n]
.
Assume that
\Delta(\mathbf{a})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and the inequalities (
6.10
) hold.

Suppose that

a_{n}=0
. Then
a_{i}a_{n}=0
for
i\in[n-1]
. Hence,
\Delta(\mathbf{a})=0
. Therefore,
a_{2}=\ldots=a_{n}=0
, and
b_{2}=\ldots=b_{n-1}=0
. Thus
\Delta(\mathbf{b})=0\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}
.

We now assume that
a_{n}>0
and prove the lemma by induction.
Observe that for
1\leq i<j<k\leq n
we have the equality

a_{i}a_{j}\geq\max(a_{i}a_{j},a_{i}a_{k},a_{j}a_{k})
.
Assume that
\mathbf{a}
in (
6.10
) is fixed.
Let
n=4

\displaystyle b_{1}b_{3}+b_{2}b_{3}-b_{1}b_{2}\geq b_{1}a_{4}+b_{2}a_{4}-b_{1}b_{2}=b_{1}(a_{4}-b_{2})+b_{2}a_{4}\geq

\displaystyle a_{1}(a_{4}-b_{2})+b_{2}a_{4}=b_{2}(a_{4}-a_{1})+a_{1}a_{4}\geq a_{2}(a_{4}-a_{1})+a_{1}a_{4}\geq 0.

The last inequality follows from the assumption that
\Delta(\mathbf{a})\in\mathcal{D}_{4}
.

Assume now that lemma holds for
n=m\geq 3
and assume that
n=m+1

b_{i}b_{k}+b_{k}b_{j}\geq b_{i}b_{j}\textrm{ for }1\leq i<j\leq k\leq n.

Suppose first that

. Then this inequality follows from induction hypothesis
for
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})^{\top},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-2})^{\top}
.
Similarly, we deduce the above inequality if

6.8

Appendix A
A trigonometrical lemma

In this Appendix we prove a technical lemma, which is used in the proof of Theorem
4.6
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
a,b,t>0,
an consider the following function
F
and its minimum:

(A.1)

\displaystyle F(a,b,t,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,+\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\phi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\phi}\;-

\displaystyle-(a+b)t\sin(\theta+\phi),\quad\text{ for }\;\theta,\phi\in[0,\pi/2],

\displaystyle\omega(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\min_{\theta,\phi\in[0,\pi/2]}F(a,b,t,\theta,\phi).

If
a=b
then

(A.2)

\omega(a,a,t)=2a(1-t)

and the minimum is attained if and only if
\theta+\phi=\pi/2
.

If
a\neq b
then the following conditions hold:

(a)

For
t\in(0,1)
the inequality
\omega(a,b)>0
holds.

(b)

For
t=1
the equality
\omega(a,b)=0
holds. It is achieved if and only if

(A.3)

\displaystyle\text{either }\theta+\phi=\pi/2\text{ or }\tan\theta\tan\phi=\frac{a}{b},\quad\text{with }\theta,\phi\in(0,\pi/2).

(c)

For
t>1
the equality
\omega(a,b)=(a+b)(1-t)
holds. It is achieved if and only if
\theta+\phi=\pi/2
, with
\theta,\phi\in(0,\pi/2)
.

Proof.

Clearly,

F(a,a,t,\theta,\phi)=2a-2at\sin(\theta+\phi)\geq 2a-2at.

As
(\phi,\theta)\in[0,\pi/2]
, equality holds if and only if
\theta+\phi=\pi/2
.

Assume that
a\neq b
. Clearly,

(A.4)

\omega(a,b,t)\leq(a+b)(1-t)=F(a,b,t,0,\pi/2)=F(a,b,t,\pi/2,0).

We first study the case
0<t\leq 1
.
Let us consider the critical points of
F(a,b,t,\theta,\phi)
in
(0,\pi/2)^{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{(b-a)\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}=t\cos(\theta+\phi),

\displaystyle\frac{(b-a)\sin\phi\cos\phi}{\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\phi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\phi}}=t\cos(\theta+\phi),

Hence, the two left hand sides of the above equality are equal. Subtract the square of the first left hand side from the square of the second left hand side, divide by
(b-a)^{2}
, and take the common denominator to deduce:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\varphi\big(a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\big)-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\theta\big(a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\big)

\displaystyle=a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\theta(\sin^{2}\varphi-\sin^{2}\theta)+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\sin^{2}\theta(\cos^{2}\varphi-\cos^{2}\theta)

\displaystyle=a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\theta(\sin^{2}\varphi-\sin^{2}\theta)+b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\sin^{2}\theta(-\sin^{2}\varphi+\sin^{2}\theta)

\displaystyle=a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\theta(\sin^{2}\varphi-\sin^{2}\theta)-b^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi\sin^{2}\theta(\sin^{2}\varphi-\sin^{2}\theta)

\displaystyle=(\sin^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\varphi)(b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\varphi-a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi).

to deduce that one of the equalities hold:

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\phi=(\sin\theta-\sin\phi)(\sin\theta+\sin\phi)=0,

\displaystyle b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\varphi-a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi=0.

Since we assumed that
\theta,\phi\in(0,\pi/2)
we have the following two possiblities

(A.5)

\displaystyle\text{either }\theta=\phi\in(0,\pi/2)\quad\text{or}\quad\tan\theta\tan\phi=\frac{a}{b},\text{ with }\theta,\phi\in(0,\pi/2).

Let us first consider the second possibility in (
A.5
):

\displaystyle\tan\phi=\frac{a}{b}\cot\theta\;\Rightarrow\;\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}=b\sin\theta\sqrt{\tan^{2}\phi+1}=\frac{b\sin\theta}{\cos\phi},

\displaystyle\tan\theta=\frac{a}{b}\cot\phi\;\Rightarrow\;\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\phi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\phi}=\frac{b\sin\phi}{\cos\theta}

Use the equality
a=b\tan\phi\tan\theta
to deduce

\displaystyle\cos\theta\cos\phi F(a,b,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=b\,(\sin\theta\cos\theta+\sin\phi\cos\phi)-

\displaystyle-b\,(\cos\theta\cos\phi+\sin\theta\sin\phi)\,t\,(\sin\theta\cos\phi+\sin\phi\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=b\,(\sin\theta\cos\theta+\sin\phi\cos\theta)(1-t).

Hence,

(A.6)

\displaystyle F(a,b,t,\theta,\phi)\begin{cases}>0,\quad\textrm{ if }t\in(0,1),\\
=0,\quad\textrm{ if }t=1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\textrm{for}\quad\tan\theta\tan\phi=\frac{a}{b}\textrm{ and }\theta,\phi\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}).

We now discuss the first possibility in (
A.5
):

F(a,b,t,\theta,\theta)=2\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+b^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,-(a+b)t\sin(2\theta),\text{ with }\theta\in(0,\pi/2).

Without loss of generality let us assume that
a+b=1
. Fix
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
,
and consider the function
F(a,b,t,\theta,\theta)
for
a,b\geq 0
and
a+b=1
. Recall that this function is strictly convex on the above interval in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let us use Lagrange
multipliers to find the critical point of
F(a,b,\theta,\theta)
:

F_{a}(a,b,\theta,\theta)=\frac{a\cos^{2}\theta}{F(a,b,\theta,\theta)}=F_{b}(a,b,\theta,\theta)=\frac{b\sin^{2}\theta}{F(a,b,\theta,\theta)}\;\Rightarrow\;\tan^{2}\theta=\frac{a}{b}.

This case is a special case of the second possibility in (
A.5
), hence (
A.6
) applies.

We now consider the boundary cases when at least
\theta
or
\phi
are in the set
\{0,\frac{\pi}{2}\}
. Let us assume that
\theta=0
. Then,

\displaystyle F(a,b,t,0,\phi)

\displaystyle=a+\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}\phi+b^{2}\sin^{2}\phi}\,-(a+b)t\sin\phi

\displaystyle\geq a+b\sin\phi-(a+b)t\sin\phi

\displaystyle\geq a(1-t\sin\phi)+b(1-t)\sin\phi\geq a(1-t)

Hence, for
0<t<1
, we have
F(a,b,t,0,\phi)>0
for all
\phi\in[0,\pi]
.
For
t=1
we have the inequality
F(a,b,t,0,\phi)\geq 0
for all
\phi\in[0,\pi]
. Equality is achieved for
\phi=\frac{\pi}{2}
. Similar results apply to other boundary cases.
These results establish the cases (a) and (b).

Assume that
t>1
. Then,

F(a,b,t,\theta,\phi)=F(a,b,1,\theta,\phi)-(a+b)(t-1)\sin(\theta+\phi)\geq(a+b)(1-t).

Hence, equality holds in (
A.4
). Furthermore,
\omega(a,b,t)=F(a,b,t,\theta,\phi)
if and only if
\theta+\phi=\pi/2
and one of the equalities in (
A.5
) holds.
We now show that the second equality in (
A.5
) does not hold.
Indeed, as
\phi=\pi/2-\theta
we get that
\tan\phi=\cot\theta
and
\tan\theta\tan\phi=1\neq b/a
. This proves the case (c).
∎
