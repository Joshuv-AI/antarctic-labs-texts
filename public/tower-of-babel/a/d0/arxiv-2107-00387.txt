Modified sampling method with near field measurements
Xiaodong Liu, Shixu Meng, and Bo Zhang

Abstract
This paper investigates the inverse scattering problems using sampling methods with near field measurements. The near field measurements appear in two classical inverse scattering problems: the inverse scattering for obstacles and the interior inverse scattering for cavities. We propose modified sampling methods to treat these two classical problems using near field measurements without making any asymptotic assumptions on the distance between the measurement surface and the scatterers. We provide theoretical justifications based on the factorization of the near field operator in both symmetric factorization case and non-symmetric factorization case. Furthermore, we introduce a data completion algorithm which allows us to apply the modified sampling methods to treat the limited-aperture inverse scattering problems. Finally numerical examples are provided to illustrate the modified sampling methods with both full- and limited- aperture near field measurements.



keywords

inverse scattering, sampling method, near field measurements, limited-aperture

Introduction

Inverse scattering plays an important role in non-destructive testing, medical imaging, geophysical exploration and numerous problems associated with target identification. In the last thirty years, sampling methods for shape reconstruction in inverse scattering problems have attracted a lot of interest.
Classical examples include the linear sampling method
[
6
]
, the singular sources method
[
25
]

and the factorization method
[
15
]
.
The basic idea is to design an indicator which is large inside the underlying scatterer and relatively small outside.
We refer to the monographs
[
3
,
7
,
16
]
for a comprehensive introduction.
Recently, other types of sampling methods are proposed for the inverse scattering problems,
e.g., orthogonality sampling
[
25
,
10
,
11
]
, direct sampling method
[
12
,
19
]
, single-shot method
[
17
]
,
reverse time migration
[
5
]
.
These sampling methods inherit many advantages of the classical ones, e.g., they are independent of any a priori information on the geometry and physical properties of the unknown objects. The main feature of these sampling methods is that only inner product of the measurements with some suitably chosen functions is involved in the imaging function and thus these sampling methods are robust to noises.
In all of the sampling methods, the measurements may be divided into two types: far-field measurements and near field measurements. Generally speaking, the analysis is more rich in the far-field case compared to the near field case. This paper is devoted to sampling methods in the near field case with both full- and partial- aperture data.

Compared to orthogonal/direct sampling methods using far-field measurements
[
25
,
10
,
19
,
11
]
, the study on near field orthogonal/direct sampling method is relatively limited. The near field measurements appear in two classical inverse scattering problems: the inverse scattering for obstacles (see for instance the monograph
[
7
]
) and the interior inverse scattering for cavity
[
27
,
26
,
18
,
4
,
24
]
. Is it possible to treat these two classical inverse scattering problems using one unified framework? The answer is yes and this is one of our main results in this paper. We survey literatures on both problems as follows. Concerning the inverse scattering for obstacles, there have been considerable works on the reverse time migration
[
5
]
and direct sampling methods
[
12
]
using near field measurements. In these works, numerical algorithms illustrated the performance of the imaging method and the analysis was done when the measurement surface is not close to the scatterer. Our work differs from these existing literatures because we propose modified sampling methods to treat these two classical problems using near field measurements without making any asymptotic assumptions on the distance between the measurement surface and the scatterers.
One difficulty in this treatment is the non-symmetric factorization
[
13
,
24
]
of the near field operator and the other difficulty is the necessity to consider the “distance” related fundamental solution (as contrary to considering the plane wave in the far-field case). We show in this paper how to design a sampling method to overcome these difficulties. Furthermore, the sampling method is completely theoretically justified in the sense that the proposed imaging function has both an upper and lower bound which peak when the sampling point is at the obstacle boundary. On the other aspect, there seems to be no attempts to design orthogonal/direct imaging methods in the interior inverse scattering for cavity where the measurements are in the near field naturally. In the cavity setting, it is not possible to asymptotically consider the near field measurements whereby the existing methods
[
5
,
12
]
do not apply.
Fortunately, our proposed sampling method is in a unified framework, which allows us to treat the interior inverse scattering for cavity by a similar sampling method using near field measurements. Once again, this modified sampling method provides both theoretical justifications and efficient numerical algorithms. This seems a first attempt on orthogonal/direct sampling methods in the interior inverse scattering for cavity.

[
2
]
for the waveguide case. We aim to apply a similar data completion algorithm in the near field case to recover the full-aperture data and then apply our modified sampling methods.

The paper is further organized as follows. In Section
2

We also provide a preliminary result on the coercivity estimate of the single-layer operator. The modified sampling methods using near field measurements are investigated in Section
3

Such modified sampling methods give both theoretical justifications and numerical algorithms, no matter the associated near field operators have symmetric factorizations or not. The key idea is to design properly chosen functions to overcome the above two difficulties associated with non-symmetric factorization and “distance” related fundamental solution. Section

Mathematical model and setup

2.1
Mathematical models

We consider two classical inverse scattering problems with near field measurements: the inverse scattering for obstacles and the interior inverse scattering for cavities. Throughout the paper we focus on the two dimensional case. The three dimensional case is similar yet to be done. In both problems, let
k>0
be the wave number. A point source
\phi(\cdot;y)
at
y
is the fundamental solution with the following explicit expression

\phi(x;y)=\frac{i}{4}H^{1}_{0}(k|x-y|),\quad x\not=y,

(1)

where
H^{1}_{0}
is the Hankel function of the first kind of order zero
[
7
]
.

Inverse scattering for obstacles
: Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open and bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
such that
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
is connected. The domain
\Omega
is referred as the obstacles. The scattering for the obstacles due to a point source
\phi(\cdot;y)
is to find scattered wave field
u^{s}(\cdot;y)
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}u^{s}(\cdot;y)+k^{2}u^{s}(\cdot;y)=0\quad

in

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega},\quad

(2)

\displaystyle u^{s}(\cdot;y)=-\phi(\cdot;y)\quad

on

\displaystyle\quad\partial\Omega,

(3)

\displaystyle\lim_{r:=|x|\to\infty}\sqrt{r}\left(\frac{\partial u^{s}(\cdot;y)}{\partial r}-iku^{s}(\cdot;y)\right)=0.

(4)

This scattering problem is well-posed, see for instance
[
7
]
.
Here we have assumed a sound-soft obstacle.

Let
\partial B:=\{x:|x|=r_{o},\,r_{o}>0\}
be the measurement surface and
B:=\{x:|x|<r_{o},\,r_{o}>0\}
includes
\Omega
as its interior. The inverse problem is to determine
\partial\Omega
from the following
near field
measurements:

\{u^{s}(x;y):x,y\in\partial B\}.

(5)

The inverse problem has a unique solution
[
7
]
.

Interior inverse scattering for cavities
: Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open, connected and bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial D
. The domain

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}u^{s}(\cdot;y)+k^{2}u^{s}(\cdot;y)=0\quad

\displaystyle\quad D,\quad

(6)

\displaystyle u^{s}(\cdot;y)=-\phi(\cdot;y)\quad

\displaystyle\quad\partial D.

(7)

If
k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the domain
D
, this scattering problem is well-posed, see for instance
[
18
,
27
]
. Again here we have assumed a sound-soft cavity.

Let
\partial C:=\{x:|x|=r_{i},\,r_{i}>0\}
be the measurement surface and
C:=\{x:|x|<r_{i},\,r_{i}>0\}
is in the interior of
D
. The inverse problem is to determine
\partial D
from the following
near field
measurements:

\{u^{s}(x;y):x,y\in\partial C\}.

(8)

In the interior inverse scattering for cavity problem, we always make the assumptions that
k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the domain

and

. Note that the assumption that

is not an eigenvalue of

[

,

.

2.2
Estimate of Single-layer operator

Let
\Gamma
be either
\partial D
or
\partial\Omega
and assume that
k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the domain
\Omega
and
D
, respectively. We define
\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}:H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\to H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
by

\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g=h,

(9)

where, for any
g\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
,
h\in H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
is the unique solution to

g(x)=\int_{\Gamma}\overline{\phi(x;y)}h(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad x\in\Gamma.

(10)

We note that the above integral equation is uniquely solvable due to that
k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the domain
D
or
\Omega
, see for instance
[
23
,
7
,
16
]
.

The following coercivity estimate plays an important role in the analysis of our imaging function.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be either
\partial D
or
\partial\Omega
.
For any
g\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
,

\big|\langle\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g,g\rangle_{\Gamma}\big|\geq c_{0}\|g\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}.

(11)

Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma}
denotes the duality paring between
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
and
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

We prove the theorem for
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
. The proof of the case that
\Gamma=\partial D
is exactly the same.

(a) We first show

\displaystyle-\Im\langle\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g,g\rangle_{\Gamma}<0,\quad\forall g\not=0.

(12)

Let
\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g=h
. Let
w^{\pm}(x):=\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{\phi(x;y)}h(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}
for
x\in\Omega^{\pm}
where
\Omega^{-}=\Omega
and
\Omega^{+}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
. From equation (
10
), we have from the jump relations of the single layer potential that
\overline{h}=\frac{\partial\overline{w}^{-}}{\partial\nu}|_{\partial\Omega}-\frac{\partial\overline{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu}|_{\partial\Omega}
(here
\nu
is the outward normal to
\partial\Omega
). Then we have that

\displaystyle-\Im\langle\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g,g\rangle_{\Gamma}=\Im\overline{\langle h,w^{-}\rangle_{\Gamma}}=\Im\langle\frac{\partial\overline{w}^{-}}{\partial\nu}-\frac{\partial\overline{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu},\overline{w}^{-}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Im\Big(\|\nabla\overline{w}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}+\|\overline{w}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\frac{\partial\overline{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu}{w}\,\mbox{d}s\Big).\mbox{ (Integration by parts)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Im\int_{\partial B_{R}}\frac{\partial\overline{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu}{w}\,\mbox{d}s=\Im\int_{\partial B_{R}}\frac{\partial{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu}\overline{w}\,\mbox{d}s.

The proof is completed if

\displaystyle-\Im\langle\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g,g\rangle_{\Gamma}=\Im\int_{\partial B_{R}}\frac{\partial{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu}\overline{w}\,\mbox{d}s<0,\quad\forall g\not=0.

(13)

This is valid due to the following. Assume on the contrary that

\displaystyle-\Im\langle\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g,g\rangle_{\Gamma}=\Im\int_{\partial B_{R}}\frac{\partial{w}^{+}}{\partial\nu}\overline{w}\,\mbox{d}s\geq 0,

Since
\overline{w}
is a radiating solution to the Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{B_{R}}
, by
[
7
, Theorem 2.13]

\overline{w}
vanishes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{B_{R}}
, and by unique continuation
\overline{w}
vanishes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
and hence
w|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. Since
{w}
satisfies the Helmholtz equation in
\Omega
, and the assumption that
-k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in
\Omega
, we conclude that
w
vanishes in
\Omega
. Recall that
\overline{w}
vanishes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
, then by the jump relation of the single layer potential, we conclude that
h
vanishes and hence
g
vanishes. This proves (
12
).

(b) Note that
\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}
is the sum of a strictly coercive self-adjoint operator and a compact operator
[
7
, Lemma 5.38]
. This together with
[
3
, Lemma 7.28]
or
[
7
, Lemma 5.37]
, we conclude that

\big|\langle\mathrm{T}_{\Gamma}g,g\rangle_{\Gamma}\big|\geq c_{0}\|g\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}.

and this completes the proof.
∎

3
Inverse scattering for extended obstacles

3.1
Near field operator and its factorization

The near field operator
\mathrm{N}:L^{2}(\partial B)\to L^{2}(\partial B)
is denoted by

\big(\mathrm{N}g\big)(x):=\int_{\partial B}u^{s}(x;y)g(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad x\in\partial B.

(14)

To facilitate the analysis, we define
\mathrm{H}:L^{2}(\partial B)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
by

\big(\mathrm{H}g\big)(x):=\int_{\partial B}\phi(x;y)g(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

(15)

and thereby
\mathrm{H}^{*}:H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial B)
is given by (which can be directly verified again by the definition that
\langle\mathrm{H}^{*}h,g\rangle_{\partial B}=\langle h,\mathrm{H}g\rangle_{\partial\Omega}
)

\big(\mathrm{H}^{*}h\big)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{\phi(x;y)}h(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad\forall h\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega).

(16)

For any function
g
in a complex Banach space, we define the “conjugate operator”
\mathrm{R}
by
\mathrm{R}g=\overline{g}
.

The near field operator has the following factorization.

Theorem 2
.

It holds that

\mathrm{N}=-\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H},

(17)

where
\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is defined via (
9
)–(
10
).

Proof.

Let
w(x):=\int_{\partial B}u^{s}(x;y)g(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
. From the definition of
\mathrm{H}
(
15
) and superposition principle, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta w+k^{2}w=0\quad

in

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega},\quad

\displaystyle w=-\mathrm{H}g\quad

on

\displaystyle\quad\partial\Omega,

and
w
satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition. Thereby we look for

w(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\phi(x;y)}\psi(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}

with unknown density
\psi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. From the boundary condition of
w
on
\partial\Omega
, we have that

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\phi(x;y)}\psi(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}=-\mathrm{H}g\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

Taking the conjugate of the above equation yields

\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\overline{\psi(y)}\,\mbox{d}s_{y}=-\overline{\mathrm{H}g}\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

This together with the definition of
\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}
(
9
)–(
10
) yields that the density is solved by
\overline{\psi}=\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}(-\overline{\mathrm{H}g})=\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}(-\mathrm{H}g)
. Therefore we have that, for any
x\in\partial B
,

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{N}g\big)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w(x)=\mathrm{R}\overline{w}=\mathrm{R}\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\overline{\psi(y)}\,\mbox{d}s_{y}=\mathrm{R}\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\big(\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}(-\mathrm{H}g)\big)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}g.

This completes the proof.
∎

There are other ways to factorize the near field operator
[
13
]
using near field measurements. The key point is that these factorizations are non-symmetric factorizations which is in contrast to the symmetric factorization using far-field measurements. We show in the following section how to design a sampling method even though the factorization is non-symmetric. Furthermore, the sampling method is completely theoretically justified in the sense that the proposed imaging function has both an upper and lower bound which peak when the sampling point is at the obstacle boundary.

3.2
Imaging function

We introduce the imaging function

I_{obstacle}(z):=\big|\langle\mathrm{N}\varphi_{z},\overline{\varphi_{z}}\rangle_{\partial B}\big|

(18)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\partial B}
denotes the
L^{2}(\partial B)
-inner product, and
\varphi_{z}
is given by

\varphi_{z}(y):=\sum_{n=-M}^{M}\frac{4}{i|y|\pi(1+\delta_{0n})}\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{H_{n}^{(1)}(k|y|)}\cos(n\theta_{yz}),\quad y\in\partial B

(19)

with
M
chosen to be a positive integer and
\theta_{xy}
denoting the angle between
x
and
y
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover we also consider

\varphi_{z,\infty}(y):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{4}{i|y|\pi(1+\delta_{0n})}\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{H_{n}^{(1)}(k|y|)}\cos(n\theta_{yz}),\quad y\in\partial B,

(20)

which is the infinite series version of (
19
).

Remark 1
.

The function

\varphi_{z,\infty}
(
20
) is well-defined. Indeed, note that
H_{n}^{(1)}(k|y|)=H_{n}^{(1)}(kr_{o})
does not vanish, so first
\varphi_{z}(y)
(
19
) is well-defined. Letting
M\to+\infty
, we have from the asymptotic of Hankel and Bessel functions
[
7
, Section 3.5]
that

\displaystyle H_{n}^{(1)}(kr_{0})\sim\frac{2^{n}(n-1)!}{\pi i(kr_{o})^{n}},\quad J_{n}(k|z|)\sim\frac{(k|z|)^{n}}{2^{n}n!},\quad n\gg 1,

which yields that

\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{H_{n}^{(1)}(k|y|)}\sim\frac{(k|z|)^{n}\pi i(kr_{o})^{n}}{2^{n}n!2^{n}(n-1)!}

and hence by ratio test
\varphi_{z,\infty}
(
20
) is well-defined for sampling points
z
in a bounded sampling region.

The next theorem gives the explicit expressions of
\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}(x)
and
\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}(x)
.

Theorem 3
.

It holds that

\Big(\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\Big)(x)=\sum_{n=-M}^{M}J_{n}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz}),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

(21)

and

\Big(\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}\Big)(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz})=J_{0}(k|x-z|),\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

(22)

Here the convergence is uniformly for
x\in\partial\Omega
and
z
in a bounded sampling region.

Proof.

We first prove (
21
).
From
[
3
,
7
]
,

\phi(x;y)=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|y|)J_{n}(k|x|)\cos(n\theta_{xy}),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,y\in\partial B.

This together with (
19
) yields that

\displaystyle\Big(\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\Big)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B}\phi(x;y)\varphi_{z}(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{o}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Big(\frac{i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(kr_{o})J_{n}(k|x|)\cos(n\theta_{xy})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big(\sum_{n=-M}^{M}\frac{4}{ir_{o}\pi(1+\delta_{0m})}\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{H_{n}^{(1)}(kr_{o})}\cos(m\theta_{yz})\Big)\,\mbox{d}\theta_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-M}^{M}J_{n}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz}),

where in the last step we have applied the orthogonality property that

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(n\theta_{xy})\cos(m\theta_{yz})\,\mbox{d}\theta_{y}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big(\cos(n\theta_{x})\cos(n\theta_{y})+\sin(n\theta_{x})\sin(n\theta_{y})\big)\big(\cos(m\theta_{z})\cos(m\theta_{y})+\sin(m\theta_{z})\sin(m\theta_{y})\big)\,\mbox{d}\theta_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(n\theta_{x})\cos(n\theta_{z})\pi(1+\delta_{0n})+\sin(n\theta_{x})\sin(n\theta_{z})\pi=\pi(1+\delta_{0n})\cos(n\theta_{xz}),

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta.
This completes the proof of (
21
). Letting
M\to\infty
and using the asymptotic of the Bessel function
[
7
, Section 3.5]
, we prove

\Big(\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}\Big)(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz}),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

where the convergence is uniformly for
x\in\partial\Omega
and
z
in a bounded sampling region.
Note that
[
3
,
7
]
when
|x|>|z|>0
,

H_{0}^{(1)}(k|x-z|)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz}),\quad|x|>|z|>0

and when
|z|>|x|>0

H_{0}^{(1)}(k|x-z|)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|z|)J_{n}(k|x|)\cos(n\theta_{zx}),\quad|z|>|x|>0,

we then take the real parts of the above equations to get

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz})=J_{0}(k|x-z|),\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

This proves (
22
). This completes the proof.
∎

The following theorem plays an important role in the analysis of the imaging function. Recall the assumption that
k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in the domain
\Omega
.

Theorem 4
.

There exists positive constants
c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime\prime}_{1}
and
c^{\prime}_{2},c^{\prime\prime}_{2}
independent of
z
such that

c^{\prime}_{1}\|\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\big|I_{obstacle}(z)\big|\leq c^{\prime}_{2}\|\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(24)

and

c^{\prime\prime}_{1}\|\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\big|I_{obstacle}(z)\big|\leq c^{\prime\prime}_{2}\|\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(25)

Proof.

From the factorization of
\mathrm{N}
, we have that

-\langle\mathrm{N}\varphi_{z},\overline{\varphi_{z}}\rangle_{\partial B}=\langle\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z},\overline{\varphi_{z}}\rangle_{\partial B}=\overline{\langle\mathrm{H}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z},\varphi_{z}\rangle}_{\partial B}=\overline{\langle\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z},\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\rangle}_{\partial\Omega}.

(26)

By Theorem
3
, we have that
\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}
is indeed a real-valued function in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, therefore

\displaystyle\big|I_{obstacle}(z)\big|=\big|\langle\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z},\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\rangle\big|.

Therefore we can apply the coercivity estimate in Lemma
1
to derive that

\displaystyle\big|I_{obstacle}(z)\big|=\big|\langle\mathrm{T}_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z},\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\rangle\big|\geq c^{\prime}_{1}\|\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}.

The upper bound follows from the fact that

∎

Remark 2
.

Though the factorization (
17

\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}
and
\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}
are indeed real-valued functions in

by Theorem

It is seen from Theorem

\Big(\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z,\infty}\Big)(x)=J_{0}(k|x-z|),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

(27)

and when

\Big(\mathrm{H}\varphi_{z}\Big)(x)\approx J_{0}(k|x-z|),\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

In this sense, we find that when the sampling point

peaks when

coincides with

Unfortunately, it is not possible to plot

Therefore from Theorem

\partial\Omega

Remark 3

The main feature of sampling methods in the literature
[

In these works, numerical algorithms illustrated the performance of the imaging method and the analysis was done when the measurement surface is not close to the scatterer, which might be due to that those suitably chosen functions are “distance” related function (as contrary to considering the plane wave in the far-field case), e.g. depending on the distance between the measurement surface and the scatterers. The chosen functions

In the sampling method using far-field measurements

4
Interior inverse scattering for cavities

4.1
Near field operator and its factorization

The near field operator
\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}:L^{2}(\partial C)\to L^{2}(\partial C)
is denoted by

\big(\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}g\big)(x):=\int_{\partial C}u^{s}(x;y)g(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad x\in\partial C.

(29)

To facilitate the analysis, we define
\mathrm{S}:L^{2}(\partial C)\to H^{1/2}(\partial D)
by

\big(\mathrm{S}g\big)(x):=\int_{\partial C}\phi(x;y)g(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad x\in\partial D,

(30)

and thereby
\mathrm{S}^{*}:H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to L^{2}(\partial C)
is given by (which can be directly verified by the definition that
\langle\mathrm{S}^{*}h,g\rangle_{\partial C}=\langle h,\mathrm{S}g\rangle_{\partial D}
)

\big(\mathrm{S}^{*}h\big)(x)=\int_{\partial D}\overline{\phi(x;y)}h(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y},\quad\forall h\in H^{-1/2}(\partial D).

(31)

The near field operator has the following factorization.

Theorem 5
.

It holds that

\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}=-\mathrm{S}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}\mathrm{S},

(32)

where
\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}:H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
is defined via (
9
)–(
10
).

Proof.

The proof is almost the same as the proof of Theorem
2
. For completeness we outline the proof.
Let
w(x):=\int_{\partial C}u^{s}(x;y)g(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}
for
x\in D
. From the definition of
\mathrm{S}
(
30
) and superposition principle, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta w+k^{2}w=0\quad

in

\displaystyle\quad D,\quad

\displaystyle w=-\mathrm{S}g\quad

on

\displaystyle\quad\partial D.

This is a classical boundary value problem which can be solved via integral equation method. In particular, we look for

w(x)=\int_{\partial D}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\psi(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}

with unknown density
\psi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
. From the boundary condition of
w
on
\partial D
, we have that

\int_{\partial D}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\psi(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}=-\mathrm{S}g\mbox{ on }\partial D.

This together with the definition of
\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}
(
9
)–(
10
) yields that the density is solved by
\psi=\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}(-\mathrm{S}g)
. Therefore we have that, for any
x\in\partial C
,

\big(\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}g\big)(x)=w(x)=\int_{\partial D}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\psi(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}=\int_{\partial D}\overline{\phi(x;y)}\big(\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}(-\mathrm{S}g)\big)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}=-\mathrm{S}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}\mathrm{S}g.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Imaging function

The imaging function is given by

I_{cavity}(z):=\big|\langle\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}\psi_{z},\psi_{z}\rangle_{\partial C}\big|

(33)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\partial C}
denotes the
L^{2}(\partial C)
-inner product, and
\psi_{z}
is given by

\psi_{z}(y):=\sum_{n=-\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathfrak{M}}\frac{4}{i|y|\pi(1+\delta_{0n})}\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{J_{n}(k|y|)}\cos(n\theta_{yz}),\quad y\in\partial C,

(34)

where
\mathfrak{M}
is chosen to be a positive integer. Note that
k^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in
C
, therefore
J_{n}(k|y|)=J_{n}(kr_{i})
never vanishes for any
n
, i.e.
\psi_{z}
(
34
) is well-defined. We now prove the resolution analysis result for the cavity case.

Theorem 6
.

There exists positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
independent of
z
such that

c_{1}\|\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}\leq I_{cavity}(z)\leq c_{2}\|\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}.

(35)

Proof.

From the factorization of
\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}
(
32
), we have that

-\langle\mathrm{N}_{\partial C}\psi_{z},\psi_{z}\rangle_{\partial C}=\langle\mathrm{S}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}\mathrm{S}\psi_{z},\psi_{z}\rangle_{\partial C}=\langle\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}\mathrm{S}\psi_{z},\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\rangle_{\partial D}.

(36)

Therefore we can apply the coercivity estimate in Lemma
1
to derive that

\displaystyle I_{cavity}(z)=\big|\langle\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}\mathrm{S}\psi_{z},\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\rangle_{\partial D}\big|\geq c_{1}\|\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}.

The upper bound follows from the fact that
\mathrm{T}_{\partial D}
is bounded. Note that both the positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are independent of
z
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
6
states that the imaging function
I(z)
is qualitatively the same as
\|\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}
. The next theorem gives the explicit expression of
\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}(x)
.

Theorem 7
.

\Big(\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\Big)(x)=\sum_{n=-\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathfrak{M}}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz}),\quad x\in\partial D.

(37)

Proof.

From
[
3
,
7
]
,

\phi(x;y)=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|y|)\cos(n\theta_{xy}),\quad x\in\partial D,y\in\partial C.

This together with (
34
) yields that

\displaystyle\Big(\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\Big)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial C}\phi(x;y)\psi_{z}(y)\,\mbox{d}s_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{i}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Big(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|x|)J_{n}(kr_{i})\cos(n\theta_{xy})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big(\sum_{n=-\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathfrak{M}}\frac{1}{r_{i}\pi(1+\delta_{0m})}\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{J_{n}(kr_{i})}\cos(m\theta_{yz})\Big)\,\mbox{d}\theta_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathfrak{M}}H_{n}^{(1)}(k|x|)J_{n}(k|z|)\cos(n\theta_{xz}),

where we have applied the orthogonality property (
23
).
This completes the proof.
∎

When
\mathfrak{M}
is large, it can be seen from the series expansion of Hankel and Bessel function that

\Big(\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\Big)(x)\approx H_{0}^{(1)}(k|x-z|),\quad|x|>|z|,\quad x\in\partial D.

(38)

We find that, as the sampling point
z
approaches
x\in\partial D
from the interior of the cavity
D
,
\big(\mathrm{S}\psi_{z}\big)(x)
peaks when
z
coincides with
x
.
Therefore from Theorem
6
and Theorem
7
, we may conclude that the imaging function
I_{cavity}(z)
peaks when the sampling point
z
approaches the boundary
\partial D
from the interior of the cavity
D
.

Remark 5
.

We have seen a difference between the obstacle case and the cavity case. In the obstacle case, we have considered the limit case (
20
) by letting
M\to\infty
in (
19
). This is not feasible in the cavity case since we have the following asymptotic for
|y|=r_{i}

\frac{J_{n}(k|z|)}{J_{n}(k|y|)}\sim\left(\frac{|z|}{|r_{i}|}\right)^{n},\quad n\gg 1

(39)

which does not allow us to take the limit
\mathfrak{M}

\to\infty
in the definition of
\psi_{z}
(
34
) for all sampling points
z
. It is the same reason that would limit us to consider a
\mathfrak{M}
that is not too large in the numerical examples, since otherwise
\psi_{z}
(
34
) would become too large which may lead to numerical instability. Since the radius
r_{i}
of
C
appears in the denominator of of the asymptotic (
39
), smaller
r_{i}
may lead to numerical instability for noisy measurements. We further illustrate these in the numerical examples section.

5
Imaging with limited-aperture data

In this section we consider imaging with the following limited-aperture “backscattering” data

\{u^{s}(x;y):\,x,y\in\Gamma_{0}\}

(40)

where
\Gamma_{0}:=\{r(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha):\theta\in[-\alpha,\alpha]\}
is a subset of the measurement circle
\Gamma
(which is either
\partial C
or
\partial B
), here
0<\alpha\leq\pi
(
\alpha=\pi
gives full-aperture data) and we denote the radius of
\Gamma
by
r
in this section without the danger of confusion. In the following, we first apply a data completion algorithm to recover the full-aperture data in appropriate sense and then apply the modified sampling methods in the previous sections. The idea of the data completion algorithm is to represent the full-aperture data in the form of Fourier series, and to relate the corresponding Fourier coefficients to the limited-aperture data via a prolate matrix. This is in a similar fashion to the far-field case
[
9
]
which considers two data completion algorithms: one based on Fourier series and the other one based on boundary integral equation. For completeness and to shed light on the near field case, we give the details on the data completion algorithm based on Fourier series in a modified way. We refer to
[
9
]
for more related data completion algorithms.

Notation 8
.

In this section we are going to work with matrices and vectors with negative indexes for notational convenience. For
-N_{1}\leq n\leq N_{2}
, we denote by

b:=\Big(b_{n}\Big),\quad-N_{1}\leq n\leq N_{2}

as a
N_{1}+N_{2}+1
dimensional vector.

For
-M_{1}\leq m\leq M_{2}
and
-N_{1}\leq n\leq N_{2}
, we denote by

A:=\Big(A_{mn}\Big),\quad-M_{1}\leq m\leq M_{2},-N_{1}\leq n\leq N_{2}

as a
(M_{1}+M_{2}+1)\times(N_{1}+N_{2}+1)
dimensional matrix.

Limited-aperture data using Fourier basis
: To begin with, we introduce polar coordinates
x=r\cos\theta_{x}
with
\theta_{x}\in(-\pi,\pi)
. At a fixed
y\in\Gamma_{0}
, for the limited-aperture backscattering far field measurements
u^{s}(x;y)
with

\theta_{x}\in[-\alpha,\alpha]
, we introduce the the infinite dimensional vector
C^{\alpha}_{\infty}
with
p
-th entry given by
c^{\alpha}_{p}

c_{p}^{\alpha}:=\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}u^{s}(x;y)\overline{\phi_{p}(\theta_{x})}\,\mbox{d}s_{\theta_{x}},\qquad p=0,\pm 1,\cdots,

(41)

here the Fourier basis is given by
\phi_{p}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ip\theta},\quad p=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots
.

Full-aperture data using Fourier basis
: At a fixed
y\in\Gamma_{0}
, for the full-aperture backscattering far field measurements
u^{s}(x,y)
where
x\in\Gamma
, we introduce the infinite dimensional vector
C_{\infty}
with
p
-th entry given by
c_{p}

c_{p}:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u^{s}(x;y)\overline{\phi_{p}(\theta_{x})}\,\mbox{d}s_{\theta_{x}},\qquad p=0,\pm 1,\cdots.

(42)

The full-aperture backscattering far field measurements in the Fourier basis correspond to the infinite dimensional matrix
C_{\infty}
.

Furthermore, given the knowledge of
C_{\infty}
, we can write down
u^{s}
at a fixed
y\in\Gamma_{0}
in Fourier series as

u^{s}(x;y)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{m}\phi_{m}(\theta_{x}),\quad\theta_{x}\in(-\pi,\pi),

(43)

and approximate
u^{s}
using a truncated Fourier series as

u^{s}(x;y)\approx\sum_{m=-J}^{J}c_{m}\phi_{m}(\theta_{x}),\quad\theta_{x}\in(-\pi,\pi),

(44)

for
J
large enough so that the approximation error is sufficiently small in the
L^{2}
sense.

Relation between limited-aperture data and full-aperture data
:
We first derive a relation between the limited-aperture data and full-aperture data as follows.

Lemma 9
.

Let
C^{\alpha}_{\infty}
and
C_{\infty}
be given by (
41
) and (
42
) respectively. It holds that

C^{\alpha}_{\infty}=\mathbb{P}C_{\infty},

(45)

where the infinite dimensional matrix
\mathbb{P}
is the prolate matrix (with dimension infinity) given by (
46
) whose
mn
-th entry is given by

\mathbbm{p}_{mn}:=\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\phi_{m}(\theta)\overline{\phi_{n}(\theta)}\,\mbox{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}e^{i(m-n)\theta}\,\mbox{d}\theta=\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\alpha}{\pi},&m=n\\
\frac{\sin((m-n)\alpha)}{\pi(m-n)},&m\not=n\end{array}.

(46)

Proof.

Assume that the full-aperture measurements are given, then there is the Fourier series expansion (
43
). Plugging this expression into the definition
(
41
)
yields

c^{\alpha}_{p}=\mathbbm{p}_{mp}c_{m}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}
is symmetric, this proves (
45
) and completes the proof.
∎

Finite dimensional case
:
In practice, the measurements are discrete data. This motivates us to consider a finite dimensional space consisting of
\phi_{m}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{im\theta},\,m=0,\pm 1,\cdots,\pm J
for a sufficiently large
J
.

The following theorem in the finite dimensional case follows immediately from Lemma
9
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
C^{\alpha}:=\Big(c^{\alpha}_{p}\Big)_{-J\leq p\leq J}
and
C:=\Big(c_{p}\Big)_{-J\leq p\leq J}
with
c^{\alpha}_{p}
and
c_{p}
given by (
41
) and (
42
) respectively. It holds that

C^{\alpha}=\mathbb{P}C,

(47)

where
\mathbb{P}
is the
(2J+1)\times(2J+1)
prolate matrix given by (
46
).

From limited-aperture data to full-aperture data
:
Now it is clear that the limited-aperture data is related to the full-aperture data via (
47
). Our goal is then to find
C
or its approximation from
C^{\alpha}
via (
47
) at each fixed
y\in\Gamma_{0}
. From the properties of the prolate matrix in
[
9
, Lemma 3.2]
, we have that the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{P}
are all positive, but they are clustered near
1
and
0
, and hence the matrix
\mathbb{P}
is severely ill-conditioned. In fact the eigenvalues decay exponentially to
0
when
J
becomes large. We refer to
[
28
,
29
]
for more details on the prolate matrix. Therefore we can only hope to invert
\mathbb{P}
using regularization techniques in order to find
C
from
C^{\alpha}
. We shall apply
the following Regularization (
48
)
to find approximate inverse of
\mathbb{P}
.

Regularization
: Similar to
[
9
]
, our choice is to consider the regularization such that

\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{U}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}+\epsilon}\right)\mathbb{U}^{*},

(48)

where
\epsilon>0
is a regularization parameter, and

\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{U}\Sigma\mathbb{U}^{*},\qquad\Sigma=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{-J},\sigma_{-J+1},\cdots,\sigma_{J}).

In this case, we take
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
to approximate
\mathbb{P}^{-1}
. From the point of view of Slepian’s spheroidal wave functions
[
28
,
29
]
, this method attempts to use some information of the spheroidal wave functions with “small energy” on the interval
[-\alpha,\alpha]
.
Now we are ready to summarize the imaging algorithm with limited-aperture data.

Imaging Algorithm.

•

Data completion for
\{u^{s}(x;y):\,x\in\Gamma,\,y\in\Gamma_{0}\}
: At each fixed

, recover the full-aperture data

approximately using the limited-aperture data
\{u^{s}(x;y):\,x\in\Gamma_{0},\,y\in\Gamma_{0}\}
.

–

Step I: Compute
C^{\alpha}=\Big(c_{p}^{\alpha}\Big)
from the measurements
\{u^{s}(x;y):\,x\in\Gamma_{0}\}
by (
41
).

–

Step II: Approximate
C
by
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}C^{\alpha}
, where
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
is the approximate inverse of
\mathbb{P}
using
Regularization (
48
).

–

Step III: Recover the full-aperture data by (
44
).

•

At each fixed
x\in\Gamma
, repeat the above Data Completion to recover the full-aperture data
\{u^{s}(x;y):\,y\in\Gamma,\,x\in\Gamma\}
approximately using the recoved data
\{u^{s}(x;y):\,y\in\Gamma_{0},\,x\in\Gamma\}
in the above step (note the reciprocity relation

[

]
).

and

are two

matrices containing pseudo-random values

6.3
Limited-aperture

In this subsection, we illustrate the performance of our modified sampling methods using limited-aperture data. We consider
64
sensors (of the total
128
sensors) equidistantly located on the upper half circle
\partial B_{+}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\partial B_{5}:\,x_{2}>0\}
.
These data are then stored in the matrices
\mathbb{N}_{partial}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{64\times 64}
.
We consider the following two strategies for the limited-aperture problems.

•

Reconstruct the obstacles using the limited-aperture data directly with the indicator function

\displaystyle W_{limit}(z)\ :=|\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}^{T}\mathbb{N}_{partial}\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}|,

where
\widetilde{\Phi}_{z}=(\varphi_{z}(y_{1}),\varphi_{z}(y_{2}),\cdots,\varphi_{z}(y_{64}))^{\top}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{64}
.

•

Recover the full aperture data using the data completion algorithm in Section
5
, where the Regularization (
48
) is used with parameter
\epsilon=10^{-3}
. With the recovered full aperture data, we then apply the modified sampling methods to reconstruct the obstacles with the indicator function (
54
).

The first two columns of Figure
6
show the reconstructions using these two strategies, respectively. For comparison, we show in the third column of Figure
6
the results using full aperture data.
10\%
noise is added in all of these reconstructions.
It is observed that the upper half parts of the obstacles can be well reconstructed and the resolution indeed can be improved with the help of the data completion algorithm.
