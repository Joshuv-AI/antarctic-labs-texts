Fine-grained analysis of non-parametric estimation for pairwise learning
Junyu Zhou, Shuo Huang, Han Feng, Puyu Wang, Ding-Xuan Zhou

Abstract
In this paper, we are concerned with the generalization performance of non-parametric estimation for pairwise learning. Most of the existing work requires the hypothesis space to be convex or a VC-class, and the loss to be convex. However, these restrictive assumptions limit the applicability of the results in studying many popular methods, especially kernel methods and neural networks. We significantly relax these restrictive assumptions and establish a sharp oracle inequality of the empirical minimizer with a general hypothesis space for the Lipschitz continuous pairwise losses. Our results can be used to handle a wide range of pairwise learning problems including ranking, AUC maximization, pairwise regression, and metric and similarity learning. As an application, we apply our general results to study pairwise least squares regression and derive an excess generalization bound that matches the minimax lower bound for pointwise least squares regression up to a logrithmic term. The key novelty here is to construct a structured deep ReLU neural network as an approximation of the true predictor and design the targeted hypothesis space consisting of the structured networks with controllable complexity. This successful application demonstrates that the obtained general results indeed help us to explore the generalization performance on a variety of problems that cannot be handled by existing approaches. † † * The corresponding author is Puyu Wang.

However, there is no work providing a generalization analysis of pairwise learning with general losses under relaxed assumptions.

In this paper, we investigate a comprehensive generalization analysis of pairwise learning under general settings by significantly removing the restrictive assumptions on both the loss functions and the hypothesis space.
Specifically, we establish an oracle inequality of the empirical minimizer for a general hypothesis space when the loss is Lipschitz continuous and satisfies the symmetric property. Our results extend the existing literature in the following two aspects. First, we consider the general losses that are Lipschitz continuous and symmetric. Various common-used surrogate losses including the hinge loss, least squares loss and logistic loss satisfy these assumptions, which makes our results applicable to a wide range of pairwise learning problems including ranking, pairwise regression and metric and similarity learning.
Second, we consider the general hypothesis spaces satisfying general capacity assumptions of the covering number. The hypothesis space is not required to be convex or a VC-class, which makes our results useful for studying the generalization performance of neural networks and kernel methods.

As an application, we apply our general results to study the generalization bounds of pairwise least squares regression. The derived oracle inequality implies that if we can find a suitable hypothesis space with controllable capacity such that the approximation error is small enough, then the desired excess generalization error bounds can be established.
Our key idea for designing the targeted hypothesis space is to construct a novel structured deep ReLU network as an approximation of the true predictor
f_{\rho}
according to the specific anti-symmetric structure of
f_{\rho}

Then, considering the hypothesis space consisting of the networks with this structure, we develop the excess generalization error bound in the order of

Main Results

Let
\rho
be a population distribution defined on the sample space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the bounded closed input space and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
is the output space.
Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a pairwise loss function. Given a predictor
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the generalization error (the true risk)
\mathcal{E}(f)
with the loss
\ell
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})d\rho(z)d\rho(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\ell(f(X,X^{\prime}),Y,Y^{\prime})].

Given a sample
S=\{Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
independently drawn from
\rho
, the empirical error of
f
based on
S
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{z}(f):=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\ell(f(X_{i},X_{j}),Y_{i},Y_{j}).

Let
f_{\rho}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{E}(f)
be the true predictor (the target function) that minimizes the generalization error over the space
\mathcal{F}
consisting of all measurable functions from
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\hat{f}_{z}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{E}_{z}(f)
be the empirical minimizer with the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
.
In this paper, we are interested in studying the statistical generalization performance of
\hat{f}_{z}
, which is measured by the
excess generalization error

\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})
, i.e., the distance between the expected error of
\hat{f}_{z}
and the least possible error
\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})
. We consider using the following error decomposition to study the excess generalization error

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})\}+\{\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})\},

\displaystyle=:S(\mathcal{H})+D(\mathcal{H}),

(2.1)

where
f_{\mathcal{H}}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{E}(f)
. The terms
S(\mathcal{H})
and
D(\mathcal{H})
are called the estimation error and the approximation error, respectively, and will be estimated part by part.
Throughout this paper, we denote by
C_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
the constant depends on parameters
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
, and denote
C
an absolute constant. These constants may differ from line to line and are always assumed to be greater than or equal to
1
. We denote
\mathbf{1}_{[A]}
the indicator function that takes value
1
if the event
A
happens and
0
otherwise. Let
\lceil C\rceil
denote the least integer number greater than or equal to
C
. For a measurable set
A\subset\mathcal{X}
, let
\rho_{\bm{x}}(A):=Prob\{A\}=\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mathbf{1}_{[A]}]
and
\rho(A|x):=Prob\{A|X=x\}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{[A]}|X=x\}
.

Our analysis requires the following assumptions on the true predictor
f_{\rho}
and the loss
\ell
.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a constant
\eta>0
such that

\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}:=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}|f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})|\leq\eta.

Both the true predictors of pairwise least squares regression and ranking problems satisfy Assumption
1
. For the pairwise least squares regression with loss
\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})=(f(x,x^{\prime})-y+y^{\prime})^{2}
, it is known that the true predictor has the form
f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=\tilde{f}_{\rho}(x)-\tilde{f}_{\rho}(x^{\prime})
, where
\tilde{f}_{\rho}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
is the Bayes rule for pointwise least squares problem
[
46
]
. One often assume that the distribution of
Y
is bounded by a constant
B>0
, i.e.,
Prob\{|Y|\leq B\}=1
, then we can show that Assumption
1
holds with
\eta=2B
by noting
\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}\leq 2\|\tilde{f}_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}\leq 2B
. For ranking problems with the hinge loss
\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})=(1-sgn(y-y^{\prime})f(x,x^{\prime}))_{+}
, the work
[
23
]
proved that the true predictor has the form
f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=sgn(T(x,x^{\prime})-T(x^{\prime},x))
, where
T(x,x^{\prime})=Prob\{Y>Y^{\prime}|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}
. Then it is easy to show that
\eta=1
in this case since
|sgn(t)|\leq 1
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a constant
K>0
, such that

\left|\ell(t_{1},y,y^{\prime})-\ell(t_{2},y,y^{\prime})\right|\leq K\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|,\text{ for any }t_{1},t_{2}\in[-\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})},\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}]\text{ and }y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}.

Here, we only consider the Lipschitz property of
\ell
over
\big[-\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})},\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}\big]
since the values predicted by
f_{\rho}
always lie inside this interval. Assumption
2
holds for the hinge loss with
K=1
and the pairwise least squares loss with
K=8B
if the distribution of
Y
is bounded by
B>0
.

With a little abuse of notation, for any probability measures
\rho
, we denote
L^{2}_{\rho}
as the metric induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}}
. Here,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}}
is
L^{2}
norm defined by
\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\rho}}=(\int|f|^{2}d\rho)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Recall that
\rho_{{\bm{x}}}
is the marginal distribution of
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
, we define two empirical probability measures based on the observed sample
S
as
\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\delta_{(X_{i},X_{j})}
, where
\delta_{(\cdot)}
is the counting measure. For any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, we define the norms

\displaystyle\big\|f\big\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{{\bm{x}}}\times\mu_{n}}}

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[f(X,X_{i})\big|X_{i}\big]^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\big\|f\big\|_{L^{2}_{\nu_{n}}}

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}f(X_{i},X_{j})^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.3)

Our analysis also needs the assumption on the capacity of the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
(T,d)
be a metric space. Consider a subset
K\subset T
and let
\epsilon>0
. A subset
\mathcal{N}\subset K
is called an
\epsilon
-net of
K
if every point in
K
is within a distance
\epsilon
of some point of
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.

\forall x\in K,\ \exists x_{0}\in\mathcal{N}:d(x,x_{0})\leq\epsilon.

The smallest possible cardinality of an
\epsilon
-net of
K
is called the covering number of
K
and is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(K,d,\epsilon)
.

Assumption 3
.

There exist constants
s_{1},s_{2}\geq e
and
,V_{1},V_{2}>0
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{H},L^{2}_{\rho_{{\bm{x}}}\times\mu_{n}},\epsilon\right)\leq s_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{V_{1}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{H},L^{2}_{\nu_{n}},\epsilon\right)\leq s_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{V_{2}}.

Any bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space
[
38
]
and the space consisting of bounded deep neural networks
[
35
,
5
]
satisfy Assumption
3
. However, many important spaces do not satisfy this assumption, e.g., RKHS. We introduce the following assumption as an alternative to Assumption

to handle this setting.

Assumption 4
.

There exist constants
s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}\geq 1
and
V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{H},L^{2}_{\rho_{{\bm{x}}}\times\mu_{n}},\epsilon\right)\right)\leq s_{1}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{V_{1}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{H},L^{2}_{\nu_{n}},\epsilon\right)\right)\leq s_{2}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{V_{2}^{\prime}}.

It was shown in
[
4
,
14
]
that the convergence rate of the excess risk for a learning problem is of the order
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
. However, the convergence rate of this order is often quite slow since we only utilize the first order property (the uniform boundness) of the function class. To obtain a fast convergence rate, we introduce the variance condition (the second order property) of a function class, which has been extensively studied on establishing tight concentration inequality and deriving fast convergence rates of the excess risk
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
10
]
.

Definition 2

(Variance-expectation bound)
.

Let
M>0
and
\beta\in[0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a function class consisting of functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
with nonnegative first moment, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[f]\geq 0
for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
. We say that
\mathcal{F}
has a variance-expectation bound with parameter pair
(\beta,M)
, if for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]\leq M(\mathbb{E}[f])^{\beta}.

(2.4)

Remark 1
.

In statistical learning theory, we often set
\mathcal{F}
to be the shifted hypothesis space
\{\ell(f)-\ell(f^{*}):f\in\mathcal{H}\}
, where
f^{*}
is either the true predictor
f_{\rho}
or the oracle
f_{\mathcal{H}}
(best predictor in
\mathcal{H}
). The inequality (
2.4
) holds with
\beta=0
for any uniformly bounded function class. There are numerous cases in which (
2.4
) also holds with
\beta\in(0,1]
. For instance, (
2.4
) is satisfied for ranking
[
14
]
and for binary classification
[
9
]
if the distribution
\rho
over
\mathcal{Z}
satisfies a low-noise condition.
[
6
]
showed that (
2.4
) holds with
\beta=\min\{1,2/r\}
if the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
is convex and the modulus of convexity
\delta
of the loss satisfies
\delta(\epsilon)\geq c\epsilon^{r}
. Further, we will prove later (
2.4
) is satisfied with
\beta=1
for pairwise least squares regression when the distribution
\rho
is bounded (see Lemma
11
for more details).

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\mathcal{H}
be an arbitrary hypothesis space with uniform bound

\displaystyle\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})=\ell(f(x^{\prime},x),y^{\prime},y).

(2.5)

Let
M\!>\!0
and
\beta\!\in\![0,1]
, suppose that the shifted hypothesis space
\mathcal{F}\!:=\!\{\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})-\ell(f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime}):f\in\mathcal{H}\}
has a variance-expectation bound with parameter pair

. The following statements hold true.

(a)

If the capacity of

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})\!-\!\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\big(\beta+2\big)\big(\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})\big).

If the capacity of

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})

Remark 2
.

Besides ranking and pairwise least squares regression, our results can also be applied to learning problems whose predictor is independent of the order of input sample pairs, e.g., the metric learning and the similarity learning problems. Let us show how to apply Theorem
1
to metric learning problems. In distance metric learning, we aim to learn a Mahalanobis distance
f(x,x^{\prime})=(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}M(x-x^{\prime})
, where
M\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}
is a positive semi-definite matrix. Let
\tau(y,y^{\prime})
be a function of labels such that
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=1
if

and
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=-1
else. The performance of

on a sample pair
(z,z^{\prime})
is measured by a loss function
\ell\big(\tau(y,y)(f(x,x^{\prime})-b)\big)
, where

is a bias term and
\ell
is a convex and Lipschitz loss. The hypothesis space here is typically set to be
\mathcal{H}=\{(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}M(x-x^{\prime}):M\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+},\|M\|\leq\eta\}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes a regularization norm. Since the Mahalanobis distance and the function

are both symmetric, then (

) holds. In addition, it’s easy to verify that Assumptions

and

hold by noting that

is assumed to be bounded, and
\beta=0
due to the uniform boundedness of the hypothesis space. Note
\mathcal{H}
is a bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space, Theorem

provides an oracle inequality for metric learning problems under Assumption

.

3
Application

We apply our main results to study the generalization performance of pairwise learning problems with deep ReLU networks. Specifically, we show that the optimal excess generalization error rate can be achieved for pairwise least squares regression, where constructing a hypothesis space consisting of the structured deep ReLU neural networks according to the specific form of the true predictor plays an important role.

3.1
A novel approximation of the true predictor

In this subsection, we first construct a novel structured deep ReLU network as an approximation of the true predictor
f_{\rho}
. Then, considering the hypothesis space consisting of the networks with this structure, we establish a sharp oracle inequality in the order of
O((\frac{\log(n)}{n})^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}})
for general anti-symmetric losses. We will show in the next subsection that, the excess error rate that matches the minimax lower bound for least squares regression
[
18
,
35
]
can be obtained when the least squares loss is considered.

In pairwise learning, we aim to learn a predictor
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that determines whether the sample
x
has higher priority than the sample
x^{\prime}
or not. Note the value predicted by
f
depends on the order of the input samples
x,x^{\prime}
. We often expect
f
to be anti-symmetric, i.e.,
f(x,x^{\prime})=-f(x^{\prime},x)
.
The following assumption is introduced to guarantee that the true predictor
f_{\rho}
satisfies the anti-symmetric property.

Assumption 5
.

The loss
\ell
is anti-symmetric with respect to any predicted values and labels, i.e., for any possible predicted value
t\in\mathbb{R}
and labels
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
, there holds

\displaystyle\ell(t,y,y^{\prime})=\ell(-t,y^{\prime},y).

It’s obvious that the hinge loss
\ell(t,y,y^{\prime})=(1-sgn(y-y^{\prime})t)_{+}
for ranking and the least squares loss
\ell(t,y,y^{\prime})=(t-y+y^{\prime})^{2}
for regression satisfy this assumption.

A good approximation of a model should be one that has the same structure as itself. Hence,
the basic idea of designing an alternative of
f_{\rho}
is to construct a model that has the same structure as
f_{\rho}
.
The following proposition shows that the true predictor for a pairwise problem has an anti-symmetric structure, which suggests the structure of the approximation of
f_{\rho}
. The proof of Proposition
1
can be found in Section
4.2
.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumption
5
, the true predictor
f_{\rho}
is anti-symmetric, i.e., for almost
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, there holds

f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=-f_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x).

Furthermore, there holds

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}f_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x).

(3.1)

The nice decomposition of
f_{\rho}
given in the above proposition indeed tells us how to find a targeted approximation, i.e.,
we consider making use of a series of ReLU networks to approximate
f_{\rho}
with the structure in (
3.1
). Before introducing our approximation, we give the definition of deep ReLU neural networks, which are the basis of our structured networks. We denote
\sigma(t)=(t)_{+}
the ReLU activation function acting componentwise on the vectors. Let
w_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be the dimension of the input space,
w_{L}=1
and
w_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be the width of the
l
-th layer for
l=1,\ldots,L-1
, where
L\in\mathbb{N}
is the depth of the network. A deep ReLU neural network
h
from
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
to
\mathbb{R}
with depth
L
has the form

\displaystyle h(\widetilde{x})=a^{\top}\sigma(T_{L-1}\sigma(T_{L-2}\cdots\sigma(T_{1}(\widetilde{x})^{\top}+b_{1})\cdots+b_{L-2})+b_{L-1})+b_{L}\text{ for any }\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}},

(3.2)

where
T_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{l}\times w_{l-1}}
indicates the connection matrix between the
l
-th layer and the
{(l-1)}
-th layer,
b_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{l}}
is the bias, and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{L-1}}
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{0}
denote the number of nonzero elements of the corresponding matrices and vectors. We define the number of nonzero weights and computation units of
h
by
(\|a\|_{0}+1)+\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}\|T_{l}\|_{0}+\|b_{l}\|_{0}
and
\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}w_{l}
, respectively. We say a network
h
has the complexity
(L,W,U)
if its depth, the number of nonzero weights and computation units are
L,W
and
U
.

Now, we can give our structured approximation network
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

\displaystyle f(x,x^{\prime})=g\big(\pi_{\eta}(h(x,x^{\prime})),\pi_{\eta}(h(x^{\prime},x))\big)\text{ for }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},

(3.3)

where
\pi_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
are two-layer ReLU networks with fixed complexity, and
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a deep ReLU network defined in (
3.2
) with complexity
(L,W,U)
. The value of
(L,W,U)
will be properly chosen later for specific problems (see Theorem
4
for least squares regression as an example).
Let us give some explanations of (
3.3
). First, we use a deep ReLU network
h
to approximate
\frac{1}{2}f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
and
\frac{1}{2}f_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x)
by
h(x,x^{\prime})
and
h(x^{\prime},x)
, respectively. Note that
\|f_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}
is bounded by
\eta
, we hope that the values of
h
also lie in
[-\eta/2,\eta/2]
. Hence, we consider improving our estimate
h
by projecting the values of
h
onto
[-\eta/2,\eta/2]
, i.e., projecting
h
to
\gamma_{\eta}(h)
with the projection operator
\gamma_{\eta}
defined as

\displaystyle\gamma_{\eta}(t):=\left\{\begin{aligned} \eta/2,\ \ &t>\eta/2\\
t,\ \ &t\in[-\eta/2,\eta/2]\\
-\eta/2,\ \ &t<-\eta/2.\end{aligned}\right.

Note
\gamma_{\eta}(h)
can be written as a linear combination of ReLU activation, i.e.,
\gamma_{\eta}(t)=\sigma(t+\eta/2)-\sigma(t-\eta/2)-\eta/2
.
It implies that
\gamma_{\eta}(h)
is indeed a two-layer ReLU network with
a=[1,-1]
,
b_{1}=[-\eta/2,-\eta/2]
and
b_{2}=-\eta/2
. For ease of reading, we use a two-layer ReLU network
\pi_{\eta}
to denote
\gamma_{\eta}
here.
With this, two main items
\frac{1}{2}f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
and
\frac{1}{2}f_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x)
are addressed. It remains to find a ReLU network to handle the difference of the values of
\pi_{\eta}(h)
between the sample pair
(x,x^{\prime})
and its reverse order pair
(x^{\prime},x)
.
By noting that the difference operator can also be represented by a two-layer ReLU network
g
, i.e.,
x-y=\sigma(x-y)-\sigma(y-x)
, we can give our final approximation
f(x,x^{\prime})=g\big(\pi_{\eta}(h(x,x^{\prime})),\pi_{\eta}(h(x^{\prime},x))\big)
. Figure
1
gives the specific structure of
f(x,x^{\prime})
.

Figure 1
:
Structure of the designed anti-symmetric deep ReLU network (
3.3
) with input
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Note that the complexity of a network
f
of the form (
3.3
) can be computed by summing up the corresponding complexities of each sub-network
h,\pi_{\eta}
and
g
directly. Specifically, the depth of
f
is the summation of the depth of
h,\pi_{\eta}
and
g
. The number of nonzero weights and computation units of
f
are
2(W_{h}+W_{\pi_{\eta}})+W_{g}+c
and
2(U_{h}+U_{\pi_{\eta}})+U_{g}+c^{\prime}
respectively, where
W_{\gamma}
and
U_{\gamma}
are the corresponding parameters of each sub-network
\gamma\in\{h,\pi_{\eta},g\}
, and
c
and
c^{\prime}
are absolute constants.
The hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
consists of all possible predictors of the form (
3.3
) is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(L,W,U):=\{\text{$f$ of form \eqref{eq:structured_NNs}: the complexity of $f$ does not exceed $(L,W,U)$.}\}

(3.4)

Here, we use the parameters
(L,W,U)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
to measure the capacity (size) of
\mathcal{H}
. As these parameters increase, the capacity of the hypothesis is getting larger.

Unlike the covering number used in Theorem
1
, we employ the pseudo-dimension to characterize the hypothesis space here. An advantage of using pseudo-dimension is that any boundedness assumptions on the parameters of neural networks are not required.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{F}^{+}:=\{(x,x^{\prime},t):f(x,x^{\prime})>t,f\in\mathcal{F}\}
be its subgraph set, then the pseudo-dimension
Pdim(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

Pdim(\mathcal{F}):=VC(\mathcal{F}^{+})
,

where
VC(\mathcal{F}^{+})
is the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
. Further, if
Pdim(\mathcal{F})<\infty
, then we call
\mathcal{F}
a VC-class.

The following lemma reveals a relation between the covering number and pseudo-dimension
[
42
, Theorem 2.6.7]
and will be used frequently later.

Lemma 1
.

For a VC-class
\mathcal{F}
of functions with uniform bound
F
, one has for any probability measure
\rho
,

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},L^{2}_{\rho},\epsilon F)\leq C\,Pdim(\mathcal{F})(16e)^{Pdim(\mathcal{F})}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{2(Pdim(\mathcal{F})-1)}

for an absolute constant
C>0
and
0<\epsilon<1
, where
L^{2}_{\rho}
denotes the
L^{2}
norm with respect to the measure
\rho
.

Lemma
1
implies that any bounded VC-class satisfies Assumption
3
. Specifically, for any hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
which is a VC-class and uniformly bounded by
\eta>0
,
\mathcal{H}
satisfies Assumption
3
with
s_{1}=s_{2}=C_{\eta,Pdim(\mathcal{H})}
and
V_{1}=V_{2}=2(Pdim(\mathcal{H})-1)
, respectively. Conversely, if we further assume that the inequalities therein hold with any probability measures, then Assumption
3
can be employed as an alternative definition of a VC-class.

Now, we can establish the following theorem on an oracle inequality of the empirical minimizer
\hat{f}_{z}
trained by the structured deep ReLU networks of the form (
3.3
). The proof of Theorem
2
can be directly derived by Theorem
1
, detailed proof can be found in Section
4.2
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
and
5
hold. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the space of structured deep ReLU networks (
3.4
) and
V=Pdim(\mathcal{H})
be its pseudo-dimension. Let
M>0
and
\beta\in[0,1]
, suppose that the shifted hypothesis space
\mathcal{F}:=\{\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})-\ell(f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime}):f\in\mathcal{H}\}
has a variance-expectation bound with parameter pair
(\beta,M)
. Then for any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K,M,\beta}\Big(\frac{V\log(n)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}\log(4/\delta)+C_{\eta,K}\frac{V}{n}\log^{2}(\delta/2)+\big(\beta+2\big)\big(\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})\big).

3.2
Pairwise least squares regression with deep ReLU networks

In this subsection, we focus on the pairwise least squares regression with loss
\ell(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})=\big(f(x,x^{\prime})-y+y^{\prime}\big)^{2}
. We first estimate the approximation error with
\mathcal{H}
of the form (
3.4
). Then, by combining the approximation error bound with Theorem
2
and choosing the proper capacity of
\mathcal{H}
, we derive the nearly optimal excess generalization error bound for pairwise least squares regression.

The estimates of the approximation error
\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})
are closely related to the true predictor
f_{\rho}
, which is known to be
f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=\tilde{f}_{\rho}(x)-\tilde{f}_{\rho}(x^{\prime})
for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}

[
46
]
. Here
\tilde{f}_{\rho}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
is the regression function.
Before stating our approximation results, we first introduce some notations and standard assumptions.

To define the Sobolev spaces, we assume that
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
in the remainder of this subsection. Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
be the smoothness index, the Sobolev space
W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
is defined as a class consisting of functions lying in
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
along with their partial derivatives up to order
r
. The norm in
W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
is defined as

\displaystyle\big\|f\big\|_{W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{d})}:=\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\|\alpha\|_{1}\leq r}\big\|D^{\alpha}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})},

where
\|\alpha\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\alpha_{i}|
denotes the
l^{1}
norm of
\alpha
,
D^{\alpha}f=\frac{\partial^{\|\alpha\|_{1}}f}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}
denotes the weak partial derivative of
f
with order
\alpha
.

The following assumption assumes that all
r
-th derivatives of the
\tilde{f}_{\rho}
exist and their
L^{\infty}
norms are bounded.

Assumption 6
.

Assume
\|\tilde{f}_{\rho}\|_{W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 1
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since Sobolev norm dominates
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
norm, then we know that Assumption
6
implies Assumption
1
with
\eta=2
.

We also require the distribution of
Y
to be uniformly bounded, which ensures that Assumption
2
holds.

Assumption 7
.

There exists a constant
B>0
such that

Prob\{|Y|\leq B\}=1.

We are in a position to give our estimate of the approximation error, whose proof can be found in Section
4.2
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose Assumption
6
holds, and the structured hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}(L,W,U)
is defined by (
3.4
). Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a deep ReLU network
f
of form (
3.3
) with depth at most
C_{d,r}\log(1/\epsilon)
and the number of nonzero weights and computation units at most
C_{d,r}\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{r}}\log(1/\epsilon)
such that

\displaystyle\big\|f-f_{\rho}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{2d})}\leq\epsilon.

Further, if we set
W=U=\lceil\exp(L)\rceil
, then the approximation error can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\leq C_{d,r}\Big(\frac{L}{\exp(L)}\Big)^{\frac{2r}{d}}.

Now, we can derive the excess generalization error bound by combining Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
together.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Assumptions
6
and
7
hold, and the structured hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}(L,W,U)
is defined by (
3.4
). If we set
W=U=\lceil\exp(L)\rceil
, then for any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})\leq C_{d,r,B}\frac{L^{2}\exp(L)\log(n)}{n}\log^{2}(4/\delta)+C_{d,r}\Big(\frac{L}{\exp(L)}\Big)^{\frac{2r}{d}}.

Setting
L=\big\lceil\frac{d}{2r+d}\log(n)\big\rceil
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})\leq C_{d,r,B}\log^{\tau}(n)n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+d}}\log^{2}(4/\delta),

where
\tau=\max\{3,\frac{2r}{d}\}
.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
4
establishes the excess risk rate
O(n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+d}})
for pairwise least squares regression, which matches the minimax lower rate for pointwise least squares regression
[
18
,
35
]
. We note that the minimax lower rate
O\big(n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+s}}\big)
of the excess risk for pairwise learning is developed in
[
17
]
by using the kernel method, where
r\in(1/2,3/2]
is the smoothness parameter of
f_{\rho}
, and
s\in(0,1)
is the effective dimension measuring the capacity of the corresponding RKHS.
[
23
]
studied ranking with deep ReLU networks when the hinge loss is considered, they derived the excess risk rate
O\big(n^{-\frac{r(\theta+1)}{2d+r(\theta+2)}}\big)
, where
r>0
denotes the smoothness of the target function, and
\theta>0
is the parameter of noise condition. As we mentioned before, the results of previous works
[
14
,
34
]
cannot be used for studying the kernel methods or neural networks with the least squares loss, which demonstrates that our general results indeed help us to explore the generalization performance on a variety of problems that cannot be handled by existing approaches.

4
Proofs

4.1
Proofs for an oracle inequality

In this section, we present the proof of Theorem
1
. The oracle inequality established in Theorem
1
is obtained by deriving upper bounds of the estimation error
S(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})
defined in (
2
). In classical statistical learning theory with pointwise loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}

[
15
,
42
]
, the estimation error is often bounded by
2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathbb{E}[\ell(f(X),Y)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(X_{i}),Y_{i})|
, where the analysis of this empirical term heavily depends on the independence of
\{\mathbb{E}[\ell(f(X),Y)]-\ell(f(X_{i}),Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. However, for pairwise learning with loss
\ell
and predictor
f\in\mathcal{H}
, the terms
\{l(f(X_{i},X_{j}),Y_{i},Y_{j})\}_{i\neq j}^{n}
in the double-index summation
\mathcal{E}(f)-\mathcal{E}_{z}(f)
are dependent, which cannot be handled by the standard techniques in the empirical process theory directly. We consider employing the Hoeffding decomposition
[
20
]
to overcome this dependency difficulty.

Given i.i.d. sample
S=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and a symmetric kernel
q:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, an U-statistic
U_{n}
associated with kernel
q
is defined as
U_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}q(Z_{i},Z_{j})
. A degenerate U-statistic
W_{n}
is an U-statistic such that
\mathbb{E}[W_{n}|Z_{i}]=0
for any
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Hoeffding decomposition breaks
\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]-U_{n}
into the summation of an i.i.d. term and a degenerate U-statistic term. i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]-U_{n}=2T_{n}+W_{n},

where

\displaystyle h(Z_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]-\mathbb{E}[q(Z_{i},Z)|Z_{i}],

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i}),

\displaystyle\hat{h}(Z_{i},Z_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[U_{n}]-h(Z_{i})-h(Z_{j})-q(Z_{i},Z_{j}),

\displaystyle W_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\hat{h}(Z_{i},Z_{j}).

Note for any
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
, there holds
\mathbb{E}[W_{n}|Z_{i}]=0.
Then
W_{n}
is a degenerate U-statistic.

We now apply Hoeffding decomposition to the estimation error
\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})
. For any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, from (
2.5
) we know the kernel defined as
q_{f}(z,z^{\prime}):=l(f(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})-l(f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime}),y,y^{\prime})
is symmetric. Further, denote by
U_{n}^{f},h_{f},T^{f}_{n},\hat{h}_{f},W^{f}_{n}
the corresponding Hoeffding decomposition terms associated with
q_{f}
. That is,
U_{n}^{f}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}q_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j}),\ h_{f}(Z_{i})=\mathbb{E}[q_{f}(Z_{i},Z)|Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}U_{n}^{f}
,
T^{f}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{f}(Z_{i})
,
\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})=\mathbb{E}[U^{f}_{n}]-h_{f}(Z_{i})-h_{f}(Z_{j})-q_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})
, and
W^{f}_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})
. Then the estimation error can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}_{z}(\hat{f}_{z})+\mathcal{E}_{z}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})+\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}_{z}(\hat{f}_{z})+\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{E}(\hat{f}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})+\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\rho})-\mathcal{E}_{z}(\hat{f}_{z})\}+\{\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\rho})+\mathcal{E}(f_{\rho})-\mathcal{E}(f_{\mathcal{H}})\}

\displaystyle=\{EU^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}-U^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}\}+\{U^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}-EU^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}\}

\displaystyle=2T^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}+W^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}-2T^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}-W^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\{2T^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}-2T^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}\}+\{W^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}-W^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}\}

\displaystyle=:S_{1}(\mathcal{H})+S_{2}(\mathcal{H}),

(4.1)

where in the first inequality we have used the fact that
\mathcal{E}_{z}(\hat{f}_{z})\leq\mathcal{E}_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})
. Here,
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})
consists of i.i.d. terms which can be bounded by using standard techniques in empirical process theory. For bounding the degenerate U-statistics term
S_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, we exploit the decoupling methods in the theory of U-processes
[
16
,
14
]
. We estimates
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})
and
S_{2}(\mathcal{H})
in the following two subsections, respectively.

4.1.1
Upper bounds for
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{f}(z):=\mathbb{E}\big[\ell(f(x,X),y,Y)-\ell(f_{\rho}(x,X),y,Y)|X=x,Y=y\big]:f\in\mathcal{H}\}
be the function class consisting of the conditional expectation of the symmetric kernels
q_{f}
. From the definition of
g_{f}
, we know
T_{n}^{f}=\mathbb{E}[g_{f}(Z)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f}(Z_{i})
and

\displaystyle S_{1}(\mathcal{H})=2\bigg(\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z_{i})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f_{\mathcal{H}}}(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}\big[g_{f_{\mathcal{H}}}(Z)\big]\bigg).

(4.2)

We estimate
\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z_{i})
and
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f_{\mathcal{H}}}(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}\big[g_{f_{\mathcal{H}}}(Z)\big]
separately. We will use the Bernstein concentration inequality to control the second term directly. The first term cannot be estimated by the Bernstein concentration inequality due to the appearance of the empirical risk minimizer
\hat{f}_{z}
depending on the sample. We will bound it by using the tools (e.g., local complexities and sharp concentration inequalities) in empirical process theory
[
10
,
4
]
.

To guarantee that the local complexity has good properties, we enlarge the function class by introducing the star-shaped class and the star-hull of a function class as follows.

Definition 4
.

A function class
\mathcal{F}
is called a star-shaped class around
0
if for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
\alpha f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 5
.

Given a function class
\mathcal{F}
. Denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{\alpha f:\alpha\in[0,1],f\in\mathcal{F}\}

(4.3)

the star hull of
\mathcal{F}
around
0
.

Intuitively speaking, a star-shaped class around
0
contains all the line segments between
0
and any point in
\mathcal{F}
. The star-hull of
\mathcal{F}
is the smallest star-shaped class that contains
\mathcal{F}
.

Since the star hull
\mathcal{G}^{*}
contains
\mathcal{G}
, we know the term
\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z_{i})
in
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})
can be bounded by
\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}}\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{f}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f}(Z_{i})\right|
. However, this bound is quite loose. Indeed, this upper bound controls the deviation from the generalization errors and empirical errors simultaneously over the whole class
\mathcal{G}^{*}
, which might be much more larger than that of the empirical risk minimizer
\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z_{i})
. To derive sharp error bounds, we introduce
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}:=\{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}:\mathbb{E}[g_{f}^{2}]\leq r\}
with
r>0
, a small subset of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
consisting of predictors satisfying a mild variance condition. The corresponding local complexity is defined as

\phi(r)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}}\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{f}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f}(Z_{i})\right|\right].

(4.4)

We will use the above notion of local complexity and the corresponding fixed point to introduce a sharp concentration inequality. To this end, we need to show that (
4.4
) is a sub-root function
[
4
]
.

Definition 6
.

A function
\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is sub-root if it is non-negative, nondecreasing and if
r\mapsto\psi(r)/\sqrt{r}
is nonincreasing for
r>0
.

Intuitively, sub-root functions increase slowly. From
[
4
]
we know they are continuous and have a unique positive fixed point
r^{*}
. i.e., there exists a unique
r^{*}\in[0,\infty)
such that
\psi(r^{*})=r^{*}.
It is clear that (
4.4
) is non-negative and nondecreasing on
[0,\infty)
. Since
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is star-shaped, we can show
\phi(r)/\sqrt{r}
is nonincreasing on
[0,\infty)
by following the arguments in the proof of Lemma 3.4 in
[
4
]
. Then, from the above definition we know (
4.4
) is sub-root and has a unique fixed point
r^{*}
.

The following lemma from
[
10
, Theorem 5.4]
establishes a sharp concentration inequality in terms of the fixed point of a sub-root function.

Lemma 2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
M>0
and
\beta\in[0,1]
, and
\mathcal{F}
be a star-shaped class around
0
. Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly bounded by a constant
b>0
and has a variance-expectation bound with parameter pair
(\beta,M)
. Let
r^{*}
be the unique fixed point of the following sub-root function

\psi(r)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]\leq r}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f]\right|\right].

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\kappa>1
, with probability
1-\delta
, we have

\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\ \mathbb{E}[f]\leq\dfrac{\kappa}{\kappa-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Z_{i})+C_{\kappa,M,\beta}\left(\left(r^{*}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}+\left(\dfrac{b\log(1/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}\right).

Lemma
2
with
\psi(r)=\phi(r)
implies that
\mathbb{E}[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z_{i})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]+C_{K,M,\beta}\big((r^{*})^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}+\big(\frac{b\log(1/\delta)}{n}\big)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}\big)
with high probability. However, this upper bound that depends on the fixed point
r^{*}
is too rough to explore the behavior of
g_{\hat{f}_{z}}
. The following proposition introduces the estimates of
r^{*}
in terms of the sample size
n
and the capacity of the hypothesis space, which helps us get a more explicit convergence rate of
\mathbb{E}[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z_{i})
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
r^{*}
be the fixed point of the local complexity
(\ref{eq:local_complexity})
. Suppose we are under the same conditions as that in Theorem
1
.

•

If the capacity of
\mathcal{H}
satisfies Assumption
3
, then

r^{*}\leq C_{\eta,L}\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}\frac{\log(n)}{n}.

•

If the capacity of
\mathcal{H}
satisfies Assumption
4
, then

r^{*}\leq C_{\eta,L}\max\bigg\{\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{2+V_{1}^{\prime}}},\frac{\log(n)}{n}\bigg\}.

Proof.

Define
\rho_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
the empirical measure on
\mathcal{Z}
. For any
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}
, we have
\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|g_{1}(Z_{i})-g_{2}(Z_{i})|^{2}
. Let
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the i.i.d. Rademacher variables, i.e.,
Prob\{\epsilon_{i}=1\}=Prob\{\epsilon_{i}=-1\}=1/2
. Then, for
r\geq r^{*}
, according to the standard symmetrization method and the chaining lemma
[
38
]
, there holds

\displaystyle\phi(r)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\Big|\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}g(Z_{i})\Big|\bigg|Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n}\bigg]\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t)}dt\Big],

(4.5)

where
S:=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(Z_{i})^{2}
.

Before proving the two cases, we need to control
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t)
and
\mathbb{E}[S]
. We first upper bound
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t)
by the covering number of
\mathcal{H}
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
t
-net of
\mathcal{G}
and define
N:=\lceil\frac{A}{t}\rceil
, where
A:=\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}}\|g_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}
. We claim that
\{\frac{i}{N}g_{t}:i=1,...,N,g_{t}\in\mathcal{M}\}
is a
2t
-net of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
. Indeed, for any
g_{f}^{*}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}
. we know there exists a
g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}
and an
\alpha\in[i/N,(i+1)/N]
for some
i\in\left\{0,...,N-1\right\}
, such that
g_{f}^{*}=\alpha g_{f}
. Notice that there exists a
g_{t}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\|g_{t}-g_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}\leq t
. Then we conclude that
\|(i/N)g_{t}-g_{f}^{*}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}=\|(i/N)g_{t}-\alpha g_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}\leq i/N\|g_{t}-g_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}+|i/N-\alpha|\|g_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}\leq(i/N)t+t\leq 2t
. Further, according to the Exercise
4.2.10
in
[
37
]
with the fact
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}\subset\mathcal{G}^{*}
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t\Big)\leq\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{G}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t/2\Big)\leq\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{G},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t/4\Big)\left\lceil\frac{A}{t}\right\rceil.

(4.6)

Now, we estimate the covering number of
\mathcal{G}
with repect to metric
L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}
. For any
g_{f_{1}},g_{f_{2}}\in\mathcal{G}
, there holds

\displaystyle\big\|g_{f_{1}}-g_{f_{2}}\big\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\ell(f_{1}(X_{i},X),Y_{i},Y)-\ell(f_{2}(X_{i},X),Y_{i},Y)\big|X_{i},Y_{i}\big]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\ell(f_{1}(X_{i},X),Y_{i},Y)-\ell(f_{2}(X_{i},X),Y_{i},Y)\big)^{2}\big|X_{i},Y_{i}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(f_{1}(X_{i},X)-f_{2}(X_{i},X)\big)^{2}\big|X_{i}\big]

\displaystyle=K^{2}\big\|f_{1}-f_{2}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\mu_{n}\times\rho_{\bm{x}}}},

where in the first inequality we have used the Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectation, and in the second inequality we have used Lipschitz property of the loss (Assumption
2
). Hence, the above inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{G},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t\Big)\leq\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{H},L^{2}_{\mu_{n}\times\rho_{\bm{x}}},t/K\Big).

(4.7)

According to Assumptions
1
and
2
, we know the constant
A=\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}}\|g_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{n}}}\leq\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}}\|g_{f}\|_{\infty}\leq K\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\|f-f_{\rho}\|_{\infty}\leq 2K\eta
. Combining (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) together, we finally have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t\Big)\leq\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{H},L^{2}_{\mu_{n}\times\rho_{\bm{x}}},t/4K\Big)\left\lceil\frac{2K\eta}{t}\right\rceil.

(4.8)

Now, we are in a position to derive upper and lower bounds for
\mathbb{E}[S]
, which will be used in the estimates of the entropy integral later. Recall that
S=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(Z_{i})^{2}
. Let
\{Z_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an independent copy of
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the upper bound can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f}(Z_{i})^{2}-\mathbb{E}[g_{f}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[g_{f}^{2}]\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}g_{f}(Z_{i})^{2}\bigg]+\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\mathbb{E}[g_{f}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 8K\eta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}g_{f}(Z_{i})\bigg]+r

\displaystyle=8K\eta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}g_{f}(Z_{i})-\epsilon_{i}\mathbb{E}[g_{f}]+\epsilon_{i}\mathbb{E}[g_{f}]\bigg]+r

\displaystyle\leq 8K\eta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\big(g_{f}(Z_{i})-g_{f}(Z_{i}^{\prime})\big)\bigg]+8K\eta\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}}\mathbb{E}[g_{f}]\ \mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\bigg]+r

\displaystyle=8K\eta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{f}(Z_{i})-g_{f}(Z_{i}^{\prime})\bigg]+r\leq 16K\eta\phi(r)+r,

where in the first and third inequalities we have used symmetrization method, and in the second inequality we have used the well-known Ledoux-Talagrand contraction principle
[
29
]
with
x\mapsto x^{2}
and the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}
, and in the last equality we have used the fact that
\{\epsilon_{i}\big(g(Z_{i})-g(Z_{i}^{\prime})\big)\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{g(Z_{i})-g(Z_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are identically distributed.

For the lower bound, we can show that there exists a
g_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*}
such that
\mathbb{E}[g_{0}^{2}]=r
if
r<\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}[g_{f}^{2}]
. Indeed, if
r<\sup_{g_{f}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}[g_{f}^{2}]
, by definition we know there exists a
g\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]>r
. Setting
\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{r}{\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]}}\in(0,1)
and
g_{0}:=\alpha g
, we know
\mathbb{E}[g^{2}_{0}]=r
and
g_{0}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}
since
\mathcal{G}^{*}_{r}
is star shaped. Therefore, we have the following lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{0}(Z_{i})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}[g_{0}^{2}]=r.

Now, we consider the following two cases.

First case
: suppose Assumption
3
holds.

According to (
4.8
), there holds

Then, (
4.1.1
) can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\phi(r)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K}\sqrt{\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}}{n}}\ \mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2K\eta}{t}\right)}dt\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K}\sqrt{\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}}{n}}\ \mathbb{E}\bigg[\sqrt{2S\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{S}\Big)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K}\sqrt{\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}}{n}}\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[S]\log\bigg(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{\mathbb{E}[S]}\bigg)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K}\sqrt{\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}}{n}}\ \sqrt{\big(C_{\eta,K}\phi(r)+r\big)\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r}\Big)}.

(4.9)

where in the second inequality we have used Lemma 3.8 in
[
32
]
, and in the third inequality we have used Jensen’s inequality since the function
x\mapsto\sqrt{x\log(C_{\eta,K}/x)}
is concave, and in the last inequality we have used the upper and lower bounds of
\mathbb{E}[S]
obtained as above.

Recall that
r^{*}
is a fixed point of
\phi(r)
, i.e.,
\phi(r^{*})=r^{*}
. Taking the limit
r\to r^{*}
and by the continuity of
\phi(r)

[
4
]
, there holds

\displaystyle r^{*}\leq C_{\eta,K}\sqrt{\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}}{n}r^{*}\log\left(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r^{*}}\right)}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle r^{*}\leq C_{\eta,K}\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}}{n}\log(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r^{*}})

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle r^{*}\leq C_{\eta,K}\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}\frac{\log(n)}{n}.

The proof of the first case is complete.

Second case
: suppose Assumption
4
holds.

According to (
4.8
), there holds

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathcal{G}_{r}^{*},L^{2}_{\rho_{n}},t\Big)\leq s_{1}^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{t}\Big)^{V_{1}^{\prime}}+\log\bigg(\bigg\lceil\frac{2\eta K}{t}\bigg\rceil\bigg)

Then, (
4.1.1
) can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\phi(r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{t}\Big)^{V_{1}^{\prime}}}+\sqrt{\log\bigg(\bigg\lceil\frac{2\eta K}{r}\bigg\rceil\bigg)}dt\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{1}{t}\Big)^{\frac{V_{1}^{\prime}}{2}}dt\Big]+\sqrt{\big(C_{\eta,K}\phi(r)+r\big)\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r}\Big)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\frac{2\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}}{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\Big[S^{\frac{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}{4}}\Big]+\sqrt{\big(C_{\eta,K}\phi(r)+r\big)\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r}\Big)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\frac{2\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}}{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}\bigg(C_{\eta,K}\phi(r)+r\bigg)^{\frac{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}{4}}+\sqrt{\big(C_{\eta,K}\phi(r)+r\big)\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r}\Big)}\Bigg),

where in the first inequality we have used the inequality
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for
a,b\geq 0
, and the second inequality follows from (
4.1.1
) directly, and in the last inequality we have used Jensen’s inequality with the concave function
x\mapsto(x)^{\frac{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}{4}}
.

Taking the limit

, there holds

\displaystyle r^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{n}}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}(r^{*})^{\frac{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}{4}}}{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}+\sqrt{r^{*}\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r^{*}}\Big)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\implies r^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{n}}\max\bigg\{\frac{\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}(r^{*})^{\frac{2-V_{1}^{\prime}}{4}}}{2-V_{1}^{\prime}},\sqrt{r^{*}\log\Big(\frac{C_{\eta,K}}{r^{*}}\Big)}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\implies r^{*}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K}\max\bigg\{\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}(2-V_{1}^{\prime})^{-\frac{4}{2+V_{1}^{\prime}}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{2+V_{1}^{\prime}}},\frac{\log(n)}{n}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\implies r^{*}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K}\max\bigg\{\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{2+V_{1}^{\prime}}},\frac{\log(n)}{n}\bigg\},

where in the last inequality we have used the fact
(2-V_{1}^{\prime})^{-\frac{4}{2+V_{1}^{\prime}}}\leq 1
since
V_{1}^{\prime}\in(0,1)
.
The proof of the proposition is complete.

\displaystyle S_{1}(\mathcal{H})\leq C_{\eta,K,M,\beta}\bigg(\big(r^{*}\big)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}+\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}\log(4/\delta)\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}).

: Suppose Assumption

\displaystyle S_{1}(\mathcal{H})\leq C_{\eta,K,M,\beta}\Big(\frac{\max\{V_{1},\log(s_{1})\}\log(n)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}\log(4/\delta)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}).

(4.11)

Second case
: Suppose Assumption
4
holds.

According to Proposition
2
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, there holds

\displaystyle S_{1}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,K,M,\beta}\max\bigg\{\sqrt{s_{1}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{(2+V_{1}^{\prime})(2-\beta)}},\Big(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}}\log(4/\delta)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[g_{\hat{f}_{z}}(Z)\big]+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}).

The proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

4.1.2
Upper bounds for
S_{2}(\mathcal{H})

We begin by introducing some notations that will be used frequently later. Let
\mathcal{Q}:=\{h(z,z^{\prime}):h(z,z^{\prime})=h(z^{\prime},z)
for almost
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\}
be some class of symmetric functions from
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Denote a empirical probability measure on
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
by
\xi:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum^{n}_{i\neq j}\delta_{(Z_{i},Z_{j})}
. For any
h\in\mathcal{Q}
, we define the
L^{2}_{\xi}
norm of
h
by

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}=\bigg(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}|h(Z_{i},Z_{j})|\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Further, we define
\mathcal{W}:=\{\hat{h}_{f}:f\in\mathcal{H}\}
as the class consisting of the kernel of the degenerate U-statistics defined in Section
4
.

We introduce a probability bound of the degenerate U-statistic, which controls the degenerate U-statistic in terms of the expectations of the corresponding Rademacher chaos and Rademacher complexities.

Lemma 4

(
[
14
]
)
.

Define the supremum of a degenerate U-statistic over the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
as

Z=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right|,

where
\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})=\mathbb{E}[U_{n}^{f}]-h_{f}(Z_{i})-h_{f}(Z_{j})-q_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})
, which is defined in Section
4
.
Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
n
and
t>0
,

Prob\big\{Z>C\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]+t\big\}\leq\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{C}\min\left(\left(\dfrac{t}{\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]}\right)^{2},\dfrac{t}{\mathbb{E}[M]+Fn},\left(\dfrac{t}{F\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2/3},\sqrt{\dfrac{t}{F}}\right)\right),

where
\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{n}
are i.i.d. Rademacher variables and
M=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H},k=1,...,n}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{k})\bigg|,F=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\|\hat{h}_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})},

Z_{\epsilon}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\bigg|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\bigg|,U_{\epsilon}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sup_{\alpha:\|\alpha\|_{2}\leq 1}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\alpha_{j}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j}).

Notice that

S_{2}(\mathcal{H})=-W^{\hat{f}_{z}}_{n}+W^{f_{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}\leq\frac{2}{n(n-1)}Z.

For any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, we set the equation

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{C}\min\left(\left(\frac{t}{\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]}\right)^{2},\frac{t}{\mathbb{E}[M]+Fn},\left(\frac{t}{F\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2/3},\sqrt{\frac{t}{F}}\right)\right)=\delta/2,

and solve it for variable
t
, then by Lemma
4
we know with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, there holds

S_{2}(\mathcal{H})\leq\frac{C\log^{2}(2/\delta)}{n^{2}}\Big(\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]+\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]+\mathbb{E}[M]+Fn\Big).

Therefore, to derive upper bounds for
S_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, we suffice to estimate the Rademchaer chaos
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]
and the Rademacher complexities
\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]
and
\mathbb{E}[M]
.

We first estimate
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]
. Since the Rademacher chaos
Z_{\epsilon}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big|\sum^{n}_{i\neq j}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\big|
conditioned on sample
S
is not sub-Gaussian with respect to the metric on
\mathcal{H}
induced by any empirical norm of
\hat{h}_{f}
, then we cannot apply the classical chaining methods directly
[
37
,
38
]
. Instead, we introduce two lemmas that estimate
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]
directly. The first lemma from
[
44
]
established a maximal inequality of
Z_{\epsilon}
. The second lemma is very similar to Theorem 2 in
[
44
]
, which controls
\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]
in terms of an entropy integral.

Lemma 5

(
[
44
]
)
.

Let
\{h_{1},...,h_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{Q}
be a finite class of functions contained in
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\{\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{N}\}
be i.i.d. Rademacher variables, then there holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\max_{k\in\{1,...,N\}}\Big|\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h_{k}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\bigg]\leq 2\sqrt{2}en\log(N+1)\max_{k\in\{1,...,N\}}\|h_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}.

With the above maximal inequality, we can bound the Rademacher chaos in terms of an entropy integral by using the same arguments in classical chaining methods
[
37
,
38
]
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\{\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{N}\}
be i.i.d. Rademacher variables, then there holds

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\bigg|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\bigg]\leq n\Big(72\sqrt{2}e\int_{0}^{D/2}\log\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\Big)\,dt+D\Big),

where
D=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}
(Note that
D=D(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
depends on the sample).

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{N}_{k}
be a
\frac{D}{2^{k}}
-net of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to the metric
L^{2}_{\xi}
. From the definition of the net (see Definition
1
), we can define a map
\pi_{k}
from
\mathcal{Q}
to
\mathcal{N}_{k}
such that
\big\|h-\pi_{k}(h)\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\leq\frac{D}{2^{k}}
for all
h\in\mathcal{Q}
. Then
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\|h-\pi_{k}(h)\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\leq\frac{D}{2^{k}}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Further, notice that
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(h-\pi_{k}(h)\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\big|
is uniformly bounded for any
k
. Then, by Dominated Convergence Theorem, we know
\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\big|\sum_{i\neq j}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(h-\pi_{k}(h)\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\big|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\big]\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Take an arbitrary
h_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
, we can set
\mathcal{N}_{0}=\{h_{0}\}
since
\pi_{0}(h)=h_{0}
for any
h\in\mathcal{Q}
. Then, conditioned on the sample
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\}
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(h-h_{0}\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h_{0}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(h-h_{0}\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]+\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h_{0}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|^{2}\bigg]\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(h-h_{0}\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]+\sqrt{n(n-1)}\big\|h_{0}\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(h-\pi_{k}(h)\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\sum_{m=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(\pi_{m}(h)-\pi_{m-1}(h)\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\bigg]+nD

\displaystyle\to\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in\mathcal{Q},\\
(\pi_{k}(h),\pi_{k-1}(h))\in\\
\mathcal{N}_{k}\times\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\end{subarray}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\big(\pi_{k}(h)-\pi_{k-1}(h)\big)(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|\Bigg]+nD

(4.12)

as
k\to\infty
, where in the second inequality we have used Jensen’s inequality, and in the first equality we just expand the term
\big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h_{0}\big|^{2}
and compute its expectation, and in the last step we have used Dominated Convergence Theorem.

Denote by
|A|
the cardinality of a set
A
, we know
\big|\mathcal{N}_{k}\times\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\big|=\big|\mathcal{N}_{k}\big|\big|\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\big|\leq\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},\frac{D}{2^{k}}\big)^{2}
. Notice that for any
h\in\mathcal{Q}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\big\|\pi_{k}(h)-\pi_{k-1}(h)\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\leq\big\|\pi_{k}(h)-h\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}+\big\|h-\pi_{k-1}(h)\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\leq\frac{3}{2^{k-1}}D
. Then. by Lemma
5
, (
4.12
) can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{Q}}\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}h\Big|\bigg|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(2\sqrt{2}e\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\log\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},D/2^{k}\big)^{2}+1\Big)\dfrac{3D}{2^{k-1}}+D\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(24\sqrt{2}e\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\frac{D}{2^{k+1}}}^{\frac{D}{2^{k}}}\log\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)^{2}+1\Big)\,dt+D\bigg)

\displaystyle=n\bigg(24\sqrt{2}e\int_{0}^{\frac{D}{2}}\log\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)^{2}+1\Big)\,dt+D\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(72\sqrt{2}e\int_{0}^{\frac{D}{2}}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt+D\bigg),

where in the last inequality we have used the inequality
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t)^{2}+1\leq\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t)^{3}
since
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t)\geq 2
for
t\leq\frac{D}{2}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Applying Lemma
6
with
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{W}=\{\hat{h}_{f}:f\in\mathcal{H}\}
, we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{\epsilon}\big|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\big]

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(72\sqrt{2}e\int_{0}^{F}\log\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\Big)\,dt+F\bigg),

(4.13)

where
F:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\|\hat{h}_{f}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})}\geq D
.

Now we estimate
\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]
. Denote by
H_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
a square matrix with zero diagonal such that its entries
(H_{f})_{i,j}=\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})
for
i\neq j
, and let
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be i.i.d. Rademacher variables. Then, we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sup_{\|\alpha\|_{2}\leq 1}\alpha^{\top}H_{f}\epsilon\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\|H_{f}\epsilon\|_{2}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\|H_{f}\epsilon\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\epsilon^{\top}H_{f}^{2}\epsilon\Big].

(4.14)

From above, we know the estimates of
\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]
reduce to the estimates of the Rademacher chaos. Indeed, we first suppose that
\mathcal{Q}=\{J_{f}:f\in\mathcal{H}\}
is any class such that

\displaystyle J_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})=\big(H^{2}_{f}\big)_{i,j}=\sum_{k\neq i,k\neq j}^{n}\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{k})\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{j},Z_{j})

(4.15)

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Then, there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\epsilon^{\top}H_{f}^{2}\epsilon\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}J_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}J_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\hat{h}_{f}^{2}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big].

(4.16)

The first term of the above equality is a Rademacher chaos, which will be estimated using Lemma
6
. The second term equals
\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\|\hat{h}_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\big]
, which will be controlled by the uniform boundedness of
\hat{h}_{f}
directly. Combining (
4.1.2
) and (
4.1.2
) together, and bounding the covering number of
\mathcal{Q}
by that of
\mathcal{W}
, we can control
\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]
as follows

Lemma 7
.

Let
D=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\|\hat{h}_{f}\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}
, there holds

\mathbb{E}\big[U_{\epsilon}\big|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\big]\leq CnD\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt+1}.

Proof.

According to (
4.1.2
) and (
4.1.2
), there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}^{2}|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}J_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\hat{h}_{f}^{2}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n\bigg(72\sqrt{2}e\int_{0}^{D/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt+D\bigg)+n^{2}D

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2}D\Big(\int_{0}^{D/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt+1\Big),

where in the first inequality we have used Lemma
6
and the fact
D=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\|\hat{h}_{f}\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}
.

Now, we are in a position to estiamte the covering number
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)
. Denote by
\|H\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}(H_{i,j})^{2}}
the Frobenius norm of a matrix
H
. For any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, the
L^{2}_{\xi}
norm of
J_{f}
(defined in (
4.15
)) can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\big\|J_{f}\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}J_{f}^{2}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}J_{f}^{2}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n(n-1)}}\big\|H_{f}^{2}\big\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n(n-1)}}\big\|H_{f}\big\|_{F}^{2}=\big\|\hat{h}_{f}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\xi}}

\displaystyle\leq D\big\|\hat{h}_{f}\big\|_{L^{2}_{\xi}}.

Therefore, we know
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{Q},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\leq\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t/D\big)
for any
t\in(0,D/2)
. Then, there holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}^{2}|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2}D\Big(\int_{0}^{D/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t/D\big)\big)\,dt+1\Big)

\displaystyle=Cn^{2}D^{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{1/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt+1\Big).

By Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectation, we know
\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]\leq\big(\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}^{2}|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]\big)^{1/2}
. Then, we finally conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[U_{\epsilon}|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\big]\leq CnD\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt+1}.

The proof of this lemma is complete.
∎

For the last term
\mathbb{E}[M]
, we can control it just by the uniform boundedness of the class
\mathcal{W}
. Indeed, recall that
F=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\|\hat{h}_{f}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})}
, then we immediately get the upper bounds of
\mathbb{E}[M]
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H},k=1,\ldots,n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\hat{h}_{f}(Z_{i},Z_{k})\big|\leq nF.

(4.17)

From (
4.13
) and Lemma
7
, we know the only thing left now is the estimates of the covering number
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big).
Combining all the above results, and carefully controlling
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)
in terms of the capacity of the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
, we can establish an upper bound for
S_{2}(\mathcal{H})
as the following proposition.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
, and (
2.5
) hold, and the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
is uniformly bounded by
\eta>0
.

•

If the capacity of
\mathcal{H}
satisfies Assumption

, then for any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, there holds

\displaystyle S_{2}(\mathcal{H})\leq C_{\eta,L}\max\{V_{1},V_{2},\log(s_{1}),\log(s_{2})\}\frac{\log^{2}(\delta/2)}{n}.

•

If the capacity of
\mathcal{H}
satisfies Assumption
4
, then for any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, there holds

S_{2}(\mathcal{H})\leq C_{\eta,L}\max\{s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}\}\frac{\log^{2}(\delta/2)}{n}\frac{1}{1-\max\{V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime}\}}.

The following lemma studies the covering numbers of the sum of the function classes, which will be used in the proof of Lemma
8
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\big(\Omega,\Gamma,p\big)
be a measure space. Suppose
\mathcal{W}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{W}_{m}\subset L^{2}\big(\Omega,\Gamma,p\big)
and define
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{i}:=\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}:w_{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{i}\big\}
as the direct sum of the classes
\mathcal{W}_{i}
for
i=1\ldots,m
. Then, for any
t>0
, the covering numbers of above function classes satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{i},L^{2}_{p},t\Big)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W}_{i},L^{2}_{p},t/m\big).

Proof.

Let
N_{i}\subset\mathcal{W}_{i}
be a
\frac{t}{m}
-net of
\mathcal{W}_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,m
. Then, we claim that
N=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}N_{i}:=\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w^{*}_{i}:w^{*}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{i}\}
is a
t
-net of
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{i}
. Indeed, for any
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{i}
, we know there exists a
w^{*}_{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{i}
such that
\|w_{i}-w^{*}_{i}\|_{L^{2}_{p}}\leq t/m
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then, we have
\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}w^{*}_{i}\|_{L^{2}_{p}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|w_{i}-w^{*}_{i}\|_{L^{2}_{p}}\leq t
, which means
N
is
t
-net of
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{i}
. Further, notice that
|N|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}|N_{i}|
and
N_{i}
is an arbitrary
\frac{t}{m}
-net of
\mathcal{W}_{i}
. Then, the proof of this lemma is complete.
∎

The proof of Lemma
8
is given as follows.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

Recall that by Lemma
4
, we know with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, there holds

S_{2}(\mathcal{H})\leq\frac{C\log^{2}(2/\delta)}{n^{2}}\Big(\mathbb{E}[Z_{\epsilon}]+\mathbb{E}[U_{\epsilon}]+\mathbb{E}[M]+Fn\Big).

Notice that the constant
F=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\|\hat{h}_{f}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})}\leq 4\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\|q_{f}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z})}\leq 4K\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\big\|f-f_{\rho}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}\leq 8\eta K
. According to (
4.13
), Lemma
7
, and (
4.17
), with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, there holds

\displaystyle S_{2}(\mathcal{H})\leq C_{\eta,K}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{0}^{8\eta K}\log\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\Big)\,dt+\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1/2}\log\big(\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)\big)\,dt}+1\bigg)\bigg].

Now, we are in a position to estimate the covering number
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{W},L^{2}_{\xi},t\big)
. We first define three classes of functions from
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}

Proof of Proposition

By tower property of the conditional expectation, the generalization error with a predictor

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\big[\ell\big(f(X,X^{\prime}),Y,Y^{\prime}\big)\big|X,X^{\prime}\big]\big],

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\big[\ell(t,Y,Y^{\prime})\big|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\big]

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}}\ell(t,y,y^{\prime})\,d\rho(y|x)\,d\rho(y^{\prime}|x^{\prime}).

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we provide the generalization analysis of pairwise learning under general settings. Specifically, we establish an oracle inequality of the empirical minimizer in the order of
O((\frac{\log(n)}{n})^{\frac{1}{2-\beta}})
for a general hypothesis space with the Lipschitz continuous and symmetric loss. Our general results greatly relax the conditions in previous works and can be widely applied to various learning problems. As an application, we apply our general results to conduct a comprehensive generalization analysis for pairwise learning with structured deep ReLU networks. In particular, the excess generalization error bound of order
O(n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+d}})
that matches the minimax lower bound is achieved for pairwise least squares regression.
