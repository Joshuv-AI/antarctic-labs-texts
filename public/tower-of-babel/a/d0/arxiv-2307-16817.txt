Baxter operators in Ruijsenaars hyperbolic system III. Orthogonality and completeness of wave functions


Abstract
In the previous paper we showed that the wave functions of the quantum Ruijsenaars hyperbolic system diagonalize Baxter Q-operators. Using this property and duality relation we prove orthogonality and completeness relations for the wave functions or, equivalently, unitarity of the corresponding integral transform.

Introduction

1.1
Ruijsenaars system: known results

The Hamiltonians of the hyperbolic Ruijsenaars system
[
R1
]
are given by commuting symmetric difference operators

H_{r}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[n]\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
j\notin I\end{subarray}}\frac{\sh^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi}{\omega_{2}}\left(x_{i}-x_{j}-\imath g\right)}{\sh^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi}{\omega_{2}}\left(x_{i}-x_{j}\right)}\cdot T^{-\imath\omega_{1}}_{I,x}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
j\notin I\end{subarray}}\frac{\sh^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi}{\omega_{2}}\left(x_{i}-x_{j}+\imath g\right)}{\sh^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi}{\omega_{2}}\left(x_{i}-x_{j}\right)}.

(1.1)

They act on functions of
n
coordinates

\bm{x}_{n}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

(1.2)

and contain products of shift operators

T^{a}_{I,x}=\prod_{i\in I}T_{x_{i}}^{a},\qquad\left(T^{a}_{x_{i}}\,f\right)(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}+a,\ldots,x_{n})

(1.3)

defined for any subset

I\subset[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}.

(1.4)

One can also consider gauge equivalent Macdonald operators

M_{r}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[n]\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
j\notin I\end{subarray}}\frac{\sh\frac{\pi}{\omega_{2}}\left(x_{i}-x_{j}-\imath g\right)}{\sh\frac{\pi}{\omega_{2}}\left(x_{i}-x_{j}\right)}\cdot T^{-\imath\omega_{1}}_{I,x}

(1.5)

collected into a generating function

\displaystyle M_{n}(\lambda;g)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\lambda^{n-r}\,(-1)^{r}\,M_{r}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega}).

(1.6)

Here we assume
M_{0}=1
.

Both families of operators are parametrized by three constants: periods
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
and coupling constant
g
,
which originally are supposed to be real positive, but we keep them to be complex unless otherwise stated and assume the following restrictions:

\mathrm{Re}\,\omega_{1}>0,\qquad\mathrm{Re}\,\omega_{2}>0,\qquad 0<\mathrm{Re}\,g<\mathrm{Re}\,\omega_{1}+\mathrm{Re}\,\omega_{2}

(1.7)

and

\nu_{g}=\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{g}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}>0.

(1.8)

The gauge equivalence between Rujisenaars and Macdonald operators is established by means of the measure function

\mu(\bm{x}_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\not=j\end{subarray}}^{n}\mu(x_{i}-x_{j}),\qquad\mu(x)=S_{2}(\imath x|\bm{\omega})S_{2}^{-1}(\imath x+g|\bm{\omega}).

(1.9)

Here
S_{2}(z|\bm{\omega})
is the double sine function, see Appendix
A
.
Namely,

\sqrt{\mu(\bm{x}_{n})}\,M_{r}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega})\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu(\bm{x}_{n})}}=H_{r}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega}).

(1.10)

Macdonald operators are symmetric with respect
to the bilinear pairing

\displaystyle\bigl(\Phi|\Psi\bigr)_{\mu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\Phi(-\bm{x}_{n})\Psi(\bm{x}_{n})

(1.11)

related to the measure
\mu(\bm{x}_{n})d\bm{x}_{n}
, and with respect to the hermitian pairing

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Phi(\bm{x}_{n})}\Psi(\bm{x}_{n})

(1.12)

as well once the periods
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
and the coupling constant
g
are supposed to be real.

There exists another family of commuting operators
Q_{n}(\lambda)
parameterized
by
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and called Baxter
Q
-operators. These are integral operators

\left[Q_{n}(\lambda)f\right](\bm{x}_{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{y}_{n}\,Q(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n};\lambda)\,f(\bm{y}_{n})

(1.13)

with the kernel

Q(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n};\lambda)=d_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})\,e^{{2\pi\imath}\lambda(\underline{\bm{x}}_{n}-\underline{\bm{y}}_{n})}{K}(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n})\,\mu(\bm{y}_{n}).

(1.14)

Here and in what follows we denote

{K}(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{m})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}{K}(x_{i}-y_{j})

(1.15)

where
{K}(x)
is the following function

{K}(x)=S_{2}^{-1}\Bigl(\imath x+\frac{g^{\ast}}{2}\Big|\bm{\omega}\Bigr)S_{2}^{-1}\Bigl(-\imath x+\frac{g^{\ast}}{2}\Big|\bm{\omega}\Bigr)

(1.16)

and

g^{\ast}=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-g.

(1.17)

We also use notation

\underline{\bm{x}}_{n}=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}.

(1.18)

The normalization constant

d_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})=\left[\sqrt{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}S_{2}(g|\bm{\omega})\right]^{-n}

(1.19)

is inserted for the convenience: with this normalization eigenvalue of the
Q
-operator has the simplest form. Note that this constant differs from the one used in our previous work
[
BDKK2
]
by the factor
1/n!
, which we instead include in the measure function (
1.9
).

Under assumptions (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) in
[

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\lambda)\,Q_{n}(\rho)

\displaystyle=Q_{n}(\rho)\,Q_{n}(\lambda)\,,

(1.20)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\lambda)\,M_{n}(\rho;g)

\displaystyle=M_{n}(\rho;g)\,Q_{n}(\lambda)\,.

(1.21)

Commutativity relations (
1.20
) and (
1.21

Q
-operators and Macdonald operators should have common eigenfunctions. They
can be constructed recursively

\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n-1}}(\bm{x}_{n-1})\,,

(1.22)

step by step, starting from the plane wave

\Psi_{\lambda_{1}}(x_{1})=e^{{2\pi\imath}{}\lambda_{1}x_{1}}
,
by application of the raising operators
\Lambda_{n}(\lambda)

\Psi_{{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})\,\Lambda_{n-1}(\lambda_{n-1})\cdots\Lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2})\,e^{{2\pi\imath}\lambda_{1}x_{1}}.

(1.23)

In some cases, to avoid misunderstanding, we use more detailed notation
for eigenfunctions
\Psi_{{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\Psi_{{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega})
.
Raising operator
\Lambda_{n}(\lambda)
is an integral operator

\begin{split}\left[\Lambda_{n}(\lambda)f\right](\bm{x}_{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}d\bm{y}_{n-1}\,\Lambda(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n-1};\lambda)f(\bm{y}_{n-1})\end{split}

(1.24)

with the kernel

\Lambda(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n-1};\lambda)=d_{n-1}(g|\bm{\omega})\,e^{{2\pi\imath}\lambda(\underline{\bm{x}}_{n}-\underline{\bm{y}}_{n-1})}{K}(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n-1})\,\mu(\bm{y}_{n-1}).

(1.25)

The normalization constant is the same as in (
1.19
).
The kernel of the raising operator is obtained from the corresponding integral kernel
of the
Q
-operator (
1.14
) by the replacement
\bm{y}_{n}\to\bm{y}_{n-1}

and
d_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})\to d_{n-1}(g|\bm{\omega})
.

Note that the operator
\Lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1})
can be defined in a natural way
as the operator of multiplication by the function
e^{{2\pi\imath}\lambda_{1}x_{1}}
so that
the formula (
1.23
) can be rewritten as

\Psi_{{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})\,\Lambda_{n-1}(\lambda_{n-1})\cdots\Lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2})\,\Lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1})\,1

(1.26)

or in explicit terms

\displaystyle\Psi_{{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n};g|\bm{\omega})=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{d_{k}(g|\bm{\omega})}{k!}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Biggl\{\prod_{1\leq l\neq m\leq i}\frac{S_{2}\big(\imath(x_{i,l}-x_{i,m})\big|\bm{\omega}\big)}{S_{2}\big(\imath(x_{i,l}-x_{i,m})+g\big|\bm{\omega}\big)}

(1.27)

\displaystyle\times\prod\limits_{j=1}^{i+1}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{i}\frac{S_{2}(\imath(x_{i+1,j}-x_{i,k})+\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}+g}{2}|\bm{\omega})}{S_{2}(\imath(x_{i+1,j}-x_{i,k})+\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-g}{2}|\bm{\omega})}\Biggr\}\,e^{2\pi\imath\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\Bigl(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{i}x_{i,j}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{i-1}x_{i-1,j}\Bigr)}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{i}dx_{i,j}

Here the inversion formula (
A.3
) in used and we assume
x_{n,i}\equiv x_{i}
.

(1.28)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\lambda)\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\hat{{K}}(\lambda-\lambda_{j})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,.

(1.29)

The

coincides with the generating

e^{2\pi\lambda_{j}\omega_{1}}

(1.30)

Here we use the notation

for any

\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\left(\hat{\omega}_{1},\hat{\omega}_{2}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{2}},\frac{1}{\omega_{1}}\right),\qquad\hat{g}=\frac{g}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}},\qquad{\hat{g}^{\ast}}=\hat{\omega}_{1}+\hat{\omega}_{2}-\hat{g}=\frac{g^{\ast}}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}.

(1.31)

The proof of both relations (
1.28

1.29

\displaystyle M_{n}(\lambda;g)\,\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})=(\lambda-e^{2\pi\lambda_{n}\omega_{1}})\,\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})\,M_{n-1}(\lambda;g)\,,

(1.32)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\lambda)\,\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})=\hat{{K}}(\lambda-\lambda_{n})\,\Lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n})\,Q_{n-1}(\lambda)\,.

(1.33)

Moreover, it enjoys the remarkable duality property with respect to interchange of space and spectral variables. Similar to (

\hat{\mu}(\lambda)=S_{2}(\imath\lambda|\hat{\bm{\omega}})S_{2}^{-1}(\imath\lambda+{\hat{g}^{\ast}}|\hat{\bm{\omega}})\,

\hat{\mu}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\

Next we introduce counterparts of the Macdonald operators and their generating function

\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle M_{r}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n};\hat{g}^{*}|\hat{\bm{\omega}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[n]\\

(1.36)

Altogether we have two pairs of inequalities on coupling constant

Notice that they are equivalent in the case of real periods
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}

\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{x}_{n}}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n}):=\Psi_{\bm{x}_{n}}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n};{\hat{g}^{\ast}}|\hat{\bm{\omega}})=\hat{\Lambda}_{n}(x_{n})\,\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{x}_{n-1}}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n-1}),\qquad\hat{\Psi}_{x_{1}}(\lambda_{1})=e^{{2\pi\imath}{}\lambda_{1}x_{1}}

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{x}_{n}}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{d_{k}(\hat{g}^{*}|\hat{\bm{\omega}})}{k!}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Biggl\{\prod_{1\leq l\neq m\leq i}\frac{S_{2}\big(\imath(\lambda_{i,l}-\lambda_{i,m})\big|\hat{\bm{\omega}}\big)}{S_{2}\big(\imath(\lambda_{i,l}-\lambda_{i,m})+\hat{g}^{*}\big|\hat{\bm{\omega}}\big)}

.

1.2
Orthogonality and completeness: expectations

In the present paper we prove orthogonality and
completeness relations for the eigenfunctions

\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})
. To start with let us itemize what we should expect on the formal level. Assume that periods
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
and the coupling constant
g
are real.

Macdonald operators are formally self-adjoint with respect to the scalar product (
1.12
)
so that we expect the orthogonality relation for the eigenfunctions in the following general form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=A(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,\delta(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n})

(1.46)

where
A(\bm{\lambda}_{n})
is normalization constant and

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\delta(\lambda^{\prime}_{k}-\lambda_{w(k)})

(1.47)

is the kernel of the identity operator on the space
of the symmetric functions of spectral variables
\bm{\lambda}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Due to the duality (
1.41
) we expect analogous orthogonality relation over spectral variables

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime})\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=B(\bm{x}_{n})\,\delta(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime},\bm{x}_{n})

(1.48)

where

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n},\bm{x}_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\delta(x^{\prime}_{k}-x_{w(k)})

(1.49)

is the kernel of the identity operator on the space
of the symmetric functions of coordinate variables
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The relation (
1.48
) can be regarded as a completeness relation. By formal linear algebra manipulations we then see that

\displaystyle A(\bm{\lambda}_{n})=\frac{c_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})}{\hat{\mu}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})}\ \ ;\ \ B(\bm{x}_{n})=\frac{c_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})}{\mu(\bm{x}_{n})}

(1.50)

where
c_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})
is some constant which does not
depends on
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
and
\bm{x}_{n}
.
One of the main results of this paper is the proof of the formulae (
1.46
), (
1.48
), which shows that actually
c_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})=1
.

The two different recursive integral representations of the wave function

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n+1}}(\bm{x}_{n+1})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{y}_{n}\,\Lambda(\bm{x}_{n+1},\bm{y}_{n};\lambda_{n+1})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{y}_{n}),

(1.51)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n+1}}(\bm{x}_{n+1})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{\gamma}_{n}\,\hat{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}_{n+1},\bm{\gamma}_{n};x_{n+1})\,\Psi_{\bm{\gamma}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})

(1.52)

together with orthogonality relations (
1.46
), (
1.48
) allow us to express the kernel of raising operators
\Lambda_{n+1}(\lambda_{n+1})
and
\hat{\Lambda}_{n+1}(x_{n+1})
, as well as of the corresponding
Q
-operators via scalar products of wave functions. Indeed, multiplying (
1.52
) by

\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\gamma}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})
and integrating
over
\bm{x}_{n}
with the needed measure
\mu(\bm{x}_{n})
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\gamma}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n+1}}(\bm{x}_{n+1})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{\nu}_{n}\,\hat{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}_{n+1},\bm{\nu}_{n};x_{n+1})\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\gamma}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\nu}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=

\displaystyle c_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{\nu}_{n}\,\hat{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}_{n+1},\bm{\nu}_{n};x_{n+1})\,\left(\hat{\mu}(\bm{\gamma}_{n})\right)^{-1}\,\delta(\bm{\gamma}_{n},\bm{\nu}_{n})=

\displaystyle c_{n}(g|\bm{\omega})\left(\hat{\mu}(\bm{\gamma}_{n})\right)^{-1}\,\hat{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}_{n+1},\bm{\gamma}_{n};x_{n+1})\,.

Finally, the expression for the integral kernel in dual space is fixed
in the following form by the scalar product

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}_{n+1},\bm{\gamma}_{n};x_{n+1})=c_{n}^{-1}(g|\bm{\omega})\,\hat{\mu}(\bm{\gamma}_{n})\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\gamma}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n+1}}(\bm{x}_{n+1}).

1.3
Statements

Our main result may be formulated as follows. Let the periods and the coupling constant be real satisfying the conditions (
1.7
). Denote by
L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)
the Hilbert space of symmetric functions square integrable with respect to the measure
\mu(\bm{x}_{n})d\bm{x}_{n}
and by
L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\hat{\mu}\right)
the Hilbert space of symmetric functions square integrable with respect to the measure
\hat{\mu}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})d{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}
. The corresponding norms are

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\mu}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,|\phi(\bm{x}_{n})|^{2},

(1.59)

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})\,|\varphi({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})|^{2}.

(1.60)

For any finitely supported smooth functions
\varphi(\bm{\lambda}_{n})
and
\phi(\bm{x}_{n})

define linear operators

\displaystyle(T\varphi)(\bm{x}_{n})=\int d\bm{\lambda}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\varphi(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,,

(1.61)

\displaystyle(S\phi)(\bm{\lambda}_{n})=\int d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\phi(\bm{x}_{n})\,.

(1.62)

Denote also by
R
the total reflection operator in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

Rf({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})=f(-{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}),\qquad-{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}=(-\lambda_{1},\ldots,-\lambda_{n}).

(1.63)

Theorem 1
.

For any smooth, compactly supported function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

T\varphi\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)
and the following relation holds

\displaystyle\|T\varphi\|_{\mu}^{2}=\|{\varphi}\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}\,.

(1.64)

As such,
T
extends to a linear isometry
T\colon L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\hat{\mu}\right)\mapsto L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)
.

Theorem 2
.

(i)

For any smooth, compactly supported function
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

S\phi\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\hat{\mu}\right)
and the following relation holds

\displaystyle\|S\phi\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}=\|{\phi}\|_{\mu}^{2}\,.

(1.65)

As such,
S
extends to a linear isometry
S\colon L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)\mapsto L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\hat{\mu}\right)
.

(ii)

Operators
S
and
T
establish unitary isomotphisms between the spaces
L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)
and
L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\hat{\mu}\right)
and are connected by the relation

S=RT^{\ast}=RT^{-1},

(1.66)

so that in
L^{2}
-sense

STf({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})=f(-{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}),\qquad TSg(\bm{x}_{n})=g(-\bm{x}_{n}).

(1.67)

In particular, we have the relations of orthogonality and completeness

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\hat{\mu}^{-1}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,\delta(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n}),

(1.68)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d{\bm{\lambda}}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}({\bm{\lambda}}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime})\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\mu^{-1}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\delta(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime},\bm{x}_{n}).

1

3
Orthogonality and completeness of wave functions

We fix
\omega_{1}\,,\omega_{2}
and
g
to be real satisfying conditions (
1.7
). Our aim is to show that the functions
\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})
form a complete orthogonal set in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\text{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)
of symmetric functions of variables
\bm{x}_{n}
with the scalar product

\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle_{\mu}=\int d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Phi(\bm{x}_{n})}\,\Psi(\bm{x}_{n}).

(3.1)

It is natural to introduce the dual Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\text{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\hat{\mu}\right)
of symmetric functions of variables
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
with the scalar product

\langle\varphi|\psi\rangle_{\hat{\mu}}=\int d\bm{\lambda}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,\overline{\varphi(\bm{\lambda}_{n})}\,\psi(\bm{\lambda}_{n}).

(3.2)

The essential difference between the two Hilbert spaces is the difference in the measure
\mu\rightleftarrows\hat{\mu}
. To avoid misunderstanding we label each scalar product and the related norm

\displaystyle\|\Phi\|_{\mu}^{2}=\int d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,|\Phi(\bm{x}_{n})|^{2}\,,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|{\varphi}\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}=\int d\bm{\lambda}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,|\varphi(\bm{\lambda}_{n})|^{2}\,

(3.4)

by the corresponding index
\mu
or
\hat{\mu}
.

For real
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
the functions
\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})
do not belong to the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\text{sym}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu\right)
. However, they allow us to
define a linear transform from the space of smooth function to the space of smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle(T\varphi)(\bm{x}_{n})=\int d\bm{\lambda}_{n}\,\hat{\mu}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\,\varphi(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\,.

(3.5)

See
1

Note that the relation
\|T\varphi\|_{\mu}^{2}=\|{\varphi}\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}
can be extended by a standard polarization procedure to the relation

\displaystyle\langle T\varphi|T\psi\rangle_{\mu}=\langle\varphi|\psi\rangle_{\hat{\mu}}.

(3.6)

The latter identity is an exact formulation of the orthogonality relation

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\bm{x}_{n}\,\mu(\bm{x}_{n})\,\overline{\Psi}_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}_{n}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\left(\hat{\mu}(\bm{\lambda}_{n})\right)^{-1}\,\delta(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}_{n},\bm{\lambda}_{n})

(3.7)

in a sense of distributions.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section
3.2
. In Section
3.1
we explain main steps of this proof on
n=1
example. The proof of Theorem
2
, which is equivalent to completeness relation, is also given in Section
3.2
.

3.1
Fourier transform as
n=1
example

In the simplest case
n=1
the measure is trivial
\mu(x)=\hat{\mu}(\lambda)=1
and
the eigenfunction coincides with the plane wave.
Hence, the transform
T
(
3.5
) is the Fourier transform and it
is possible to illustrate the whole scheme step by step
using this example.
Namely, we prove that the system of functions

\{e^{2\pi\imath\lambda x},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}

forms an orthogonal system in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
For brevity we use standard notation
\hat{\varphi}(x)
for the Fourier transform
instead of general notation
(T\varphi)(x)
. We have

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,e^{2\pi\imath\lambda x}\,\varphi(\lambda)\,.

(3.8)

For smooth, compactly supported functions
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}

the function
\hat{\varphi}(x)
rapidly decreases as
|x|\to\infty
(which can be seen by integration by parts). This guarantees the convergence of the integral

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\overline{\hat{\varphi}(x)}\,\hat{\varphi}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}.

(3.9)

Due to this convergence it is possible to introduce regularization inside the integral using appropriate function
f_{\varepsilon}(x)
which has pointwise limit:
\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0+}f_{\varepsilon}(x)=1
, so that

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}.

(3.10)

The first step is trivial, and on the second step we use dominated convergence theorem which
states that the limit can be taken out of the integral, provided that there exists function
g(x)
, such that
|f_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq g(x)
for all
\varepsilon,x
and the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,g(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}

(3.11)

is convergent.

The simplest example of regularization is the following

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,e^{-\varepsilon\,|x|}\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{-\varepsilon\,|x|}\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}.

(3.12)

The equality
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,e^{-\varepsilon\,|x|}=1
is evident and the condition of dominated convergence theorem is fulfilled for
g(x)=1
since
e^{-\varepsilon\,|x|}\leq 1
.

Let us consider a more complicated regularization which
will admit generalization to any
n

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\,e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\,K(z-x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\,K(z-x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}\,.

Here we recall that

\hat{g}=\frac{g}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}>0.

(3.13)

Using asymptotics of
K
-functions one may readily check that

\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}K(z-x)=1

(3.14)

pointwise.
Indeed, it is evident that
e^{2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\to 1
as

\varepsilon\to 0
and for
z\to+\infty
we have (
A.17
)

\displaystyle K(z-x)\to e^{\pi\hat{g}\left(x-z\right)}.

(3.15)

Next to use dominated convergence theorem recall inequality
(
A.19
)

|K(y)|\leq C\,e^{-\pi\nu_{g}|y|},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}

where
\nu_{g}=\mathrm{Re}\,\hat{g}=\hat{g}>0
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left|e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\,K(z-x)\right|\leq C\,e^{\pi(\hat{g}-2\varepsilon)(z-x)-\pi\hat{g}|z-x|}\leq Ce^{-2\pi\varepsilon|z-x|}\leq C,

(3.16)

where on the second step we used inequality
a\leq|a|
and the fact that
2\varepsilon<\hat{g}
.

The main goal of regularization is the following.
In the initial integral

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\overline{\hat{\varphi}(x)}\,\hat{\varphi}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,e^{-2\pi\imath\lambda^{\prime}x}\,\overline{\varphi(\lambda^{\prime})}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,e^{2\pi\imath\lambda x}\,\varphi(\lambda)

the order of integrations is fixed from the very beginning and
can not be changed. One can not invoke Fubini’s theorem because the

x
-integral is not absolute convergent separately.
Regularization improves absolute convergence of the

x
-integral so that it is possible to invoke Fubini’s theorem,
change order of integrations and then calculate the
x
-integral first to obtain delta sequence.
In the case of the first regularization we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\overline{\hat{\varphi}(x)}\,\hat{\varphi}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{-\varepsilon\,|x|}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,e^{-2\pi\imath\lambda^{\prime}x}\,\overline{\varphi(\lambda^{\prime})}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,e^{2\pi\imath\lambda x}\,\varphi(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,d\lambda\,\overline{\varphi(\lambda^{\prime})}\,\varphi(\lambda)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{-\varepsilon\,|x|}\,e^{2\pi\imath(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})x}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,d\lambda\,\overline{\varphi(\lambda^{\prime})}\,\varphi(\lambda)\,\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\,\left[\frac{1}{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-\imath\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}+\imath\varepsilon}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\,\varphi(\lambda)\,.

In the last line we rescaled
\varepsilon\rightarrow 2\pi\varepsilon
. In fact, the proof of the last equality is the same as the proof of the well-known formula in the theory of the generalized functions

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-\imath\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}+\imath\varepsilon}\right]=2\pi\imath\,\delta(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}).

(3.17)

Thus, we proved the equality
\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\|\varphi\|^{2}
and as a consequence
the orthogonality relation in the form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{2\pi\imath(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})x}=\delta(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})

(3.18)

where integral is understood in the sense of generalized functions as described above.

Let us consider the second regularization as an illustrative example to the general case of arbitrary
n

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\,K(z-x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d\lambda^{\prime}\,d\lambda\,\overline{\varphi(\lambda^{\prime})}\,\varphi(\lambda)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\,K(z-x)\,e^{2\pi\imath(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})x}.

The last integral coincides with the simplest basic integral
J
from the previous section (
2.3
) and can be calculated in a closed form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,e^{\pi\hat{g}(z-x)+2\pi\varepsilon(x-z)}\,K(z-x)\,e^{2\pi\imath(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})x}=\frac{1}{d_{1}(g|\bm{\omega})}\,\hat{K}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}+\textstyle\frac{\imath\hat{g}}{2}-\imath\varepsilon)\,e^{2\pi\imath(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})z}.

In Section
3.2
we show that the last expression is a delta sequence

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\,\hat{K}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}+\textstyle\frac{\imath\hat{g}}{2}-\imath\varepsilon)\,e^{2\pi\imath(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})z}=d_{1}(g|\bm{\omega})\,\delta(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\,.

(3.19)

In the case of Fourier transform we are able to use the
well-known fact that for smooth, compactly supported functions

\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
the function
\hat{\varphi}(x)
rapidly
decreases as
|x|\to\infty
that guarantees the convergence
of the integral

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}

(3.20)

and then prove the equality

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}

(3.21)

using dominated convergence theorem and corresponding restriction on
f_{\varepsilon}(x)
. In general case we do not know the same property of rapid decay for the transformed function
T\varphi
, although it is probably true even in the case of complex periods and coupling constant.
So, in general case we slightly change the logic and prove convergence of the needed
integral using Fatou’s lemma. The corresponding steps in the case of Fourier transformation are as follows.

•

At the first step we consider regularized integral with
f_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq 0
and prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,|\varphi(\lambda)|^{2}.

(3.22)

•

At the second step we use Fatou’s lemma which states that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\varliminf_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}\leq\varliminf_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,|\varphi(\lambda)|^{2}

(3.23)

and therefore the needed integral is convergent

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,|\varphi(\lambda)|^{2}.

(3.24)

•

Convergence of the needed integral allows us to use dominated convergence theorem in the same way as before, so that

\displaystyle\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,f_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\hat{\varphi}(x)|^{2}

(3.25)

and therefore
\|\hat{\varphi}\|^{2}=\|\varphi\|^{2}
.

The double sine function

The double sine function
S_{2}(z):=S_{2}(z|\bm{\omega})
, see
[
Ku
]
and references therein, is a meromorphic function that satisfies two functional relations

\frac{S_{2}(z)}{S_{2}(z+\omega_{1})}=2\sin\frac{\pi z}{\omega_{2}},\qquad\frac{S_{2}(z)}{S_{2}(z+\omega_{2})}=2\sin\frac{\pi z}{\omega_{1}}

(A.1)

and inversion relation

S_{2}(z)S_{2}(-z)=-4\sin\frac{\pi z}{\omega_{1}}\sin\frac{\pi z}{\omega_{2}},

(A.2)

or equivalently

S_{2}(z)S_{2}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-z)=1.

(A.3)

The function
S_{2}(z)
has poles at the points

z_{m,k}=m\omega_{1}+k\omega_{2},\qquad m,k\geq 1

(A.4)

and zeros at

z_{-m,-k}=-m\omega_{1}-k\omega_{2},\qquad m,k\geq 0.

(A.5)

For
\omega_{1}/\omega_{2}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
all poles and zeros are simple. The residues of
S_{2}(z)
and
S^{-1}_{2}(z)
at these points are

\displaystyle\underset{z=z_{m,k}}{\operatorname{Res}}\,S_{2}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}}{2\pi}\frac{(-1)^{mk}}{\prod\limits_{s=1}^{m-1}2\sin\dfrac{\pi s\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}\prod\limits_{l=1}^{k-1}2\sin\dfrac{\pi l\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}},

(A.6)

\displaystyle\underset{z=z_{-m,-k}}{\operatorname{Res}}\,S^{-1}_{2}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}}{2\pi}\frac{(-1)^{mk+m+k}}{\prod\limits_{s=1}^{m}2\sin\dfrac{\pi s\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}\prod\limits_{l=1}^{k}2\sin\dfrac{\pi l\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}}.

(A.7)

In the analytic region
\mathrm{Re}\,z\in(0,\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})
we have the following integral representation for the logarithm of
S_{2}(z)

\ln S_{2}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2t}\left(\frac{\sh\left[(2z-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})t\right]}{\sh(\omega_{1}t)\sh(\omega_{2}t)}-\frac{2z-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}t}\right).

(A.8)

It is clear from this representation that the double sine function is homogeneous

S_{2}(\gamma z|\gamma\omega_{1},\gamma\omega_{2})=S_{2}(z|\omega_{1},\omega_{2}),\qquad\gamma\in(0,\infty)

(A.9)

and invariant under permutation of periods

S_{2}(z|\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=S_{2}(z|\omega_{2},\omega_{1}).

(A.10)

The double sine function can be expressed through the Barnes double Gamma function
\Gamma_{2}(z|\bm{\omega})

[
B
]
,

S_{2}(z|\bm{\omega})=\Gamma_{2}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-z|\bm{\omega})\Gamma_{2}^{-1}(z|\bm{\omega}),

(A.11)

and its properties follow from the corresponding properties of the double Gamma function.

It is also connected to the Ruijsenaars hyperbolic Gamma function
G(z|\bm{\omega})

[
R2
]

G(z|\bm{\omega})=S_{2}\Bigl(\imath z+\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{2}\,\Big|\,\bm{\omega}\Bigr)

(A.12)

and to the Faddeev quantum dilogarithm
\gamma(z|\bm{\omega})

[
F
]

\gamma(z|\bm{\omega})=S_{2}\Bigl(-\imath z+\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{2}\,\Big|\,\bm{\omega}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(\frac{\imath\pi}{2\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}\Bigl[z^{2}+\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2}^{2}}{12}\Bigr]\Bigr).

Both

and

were investigated independently.

(C.1)

where

\displaystyle\delta\big(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n}\big)=\frac{1}{n!}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\delta(x_{k}-y_{w(k)}).

This identity is written in a compact formal way and should be understood
in the following sense:
for any test (e.g. smooth with a compact support) function
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
we have

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n}\prod_{a<b}^{n}(x_{a}-x_{b})^{2}\,f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\frac{e^{\imath\lambda\sum_{a=1}^{n}(x_{a}-y_{a})}}{\prod_{a,b=1}^{n}\left(x_{a}-y_{b}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=\\
(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(2\pi\imath)^{n}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}f\big(y_{w(1)},\ldots,y_{w(n)}\big).

(C.2)

First of all we prove the equivalent identity

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}e^{\imath\lambda\sum_{a=1}^{n}(x_{a}-y_{a})}\,\frac{\prod_{a<b}^{n}\left(x_{a}-x_{b}\right)\left(y_{b}-y_{a}\right)}{\prod_{a,b=1}^{n}\left(x_{a}-y_{b}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=(2\pi\imath)^{n}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}(-1)^{s(w)}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\,\delta(x_{k}-y_{w(k)})

(C.3)

where
s(w)
is the sign of the permutation
w
.
Let us start from the simplest example
n=1
. We have to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}}{\left(x-y-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=2\pi\imath\,\delta(x-y)\,,

or equivalently

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}}{x-y-\imath\varepsilon}\,dx=2\pi\imath\,f(y)\,.

First of all we transform integral with the test function.
We divide integral on two parts: the first
integral can be calculated by residues and due
to cancelation of singularity at
x=y
it is possible to put

\varepsilon\to 0
in the second part

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}}{x-y-\imath\varepsilon}\,dx=f(y)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}}{x-y-\imath\varepsilon}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y-\imath\varepsilon}\,e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}\,dx=\\[6.0pt]
2\pi\imath\,f(y)\,e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y-\imath\varepsilon}\,e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}\,dx\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}2\pi\imath\,f(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y}\,e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}\,dx.

Due to the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma the second contribution
tends to zero in the limit
\lambda\to\infty
so that we obtain
after removing
\varepsilon
-regularization and taking limit
\lambda\to\infty

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x-y)}}{x-y-\imath\varepsilon}\,dx=2\pi\imath\,f(y)\,.

The whole consideration in the case
n=2
is almost identical
to the case of general
n
.
We have to prove the following relation

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}\,x_{12}\,y_{21}}{\prod_{a,b=1}^{2}\left(x_{a}-y_{b}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=(2\pi\imath)^{2}\,\bigl[\delta(x_{1}-y_{1})\,\delta(x_{2}-y_{2})-\delta(x_{1}-y_{2})\,\delta(x_{2}-y_{1})\bigl].

Here we denoted
x_{12}=x_{1}-x_{2}
. First of all we use Cauchy determinant identity

\frac{x_{12}\,y_{21}}{\prod_{a,b=1}^{2}\left(x_{a}-y_{b}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=\frac{1}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}-\frac{1}{\left(x_{1}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}.

(C.4)

Let us consider the convolution of the first term with
the test function

\displaystyle\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,f(x_{1},x_{2})\,\frac{e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}

and introduce two commuting operators
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
acting on the test function

\displaystyle X_{1}f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})=f(y_{1}\,,x_{2}),\qquad X_{2}f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})=f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})\,.

As a consequence of evident identity

1=(1-X_{1}+X_{1})(1-X_{2}+X_{2})=(1-X_{1})(1-X_{2})+X_{1}(1-X_{2})+X_{2}(1-X_{1})+X_{1}X_{2}

and explicit formulas

\displaystyle X_{1}(1-X_{2})f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})=f(y_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\,;

\displaystyle X_{2}(1-X_{1})f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})=f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\,;

\displaystyle(1-X_{1})(1-X_{2})f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})=(1-X_{1})\left[f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]=

\displaystyle f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,x_{2})+f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})

we obtain the following useful representation for the function
f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})

\displaystyle f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})=f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})+\left[f(y_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]+\left[f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]+

\displaystyle\left[f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,x_{2})+f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]

Note that the first term does not depend on
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
,
second term does not depend on
x_{1}
and is equal to zero at the point
x_{2}=y_{2}
,
third term does not depend on
x_{2}
and is equal to zero at the point
x_{1}=y_{1}
.
The Taylor expansion of the last term in vicinity of the point
x_{1}=y_{1}\,,x_{2}=y_{2}
starts from the contribution
\sim(x_{1}-y_{1})(x_{2}-y_{2})
because it turns to zero at points
x_{1}=y_{1}
and
x_{2}=y_{2}
independently. In the first three terms the corresponding integrals can be calculated by residues
and we obtain

\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\frac{f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\,(2\pi\imath)^{2}\,e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}+\\[6.0pt]
2\pi\imath\,e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}\,\int dx_{2}\,\frac{\left[f(y_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{2}-y_{2})}}{x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon}+\\[6.0pt]
2\pi\imath\,e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}\,\int dx_{1}\,\frac{\left[f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon}+\\[6.0pt]
\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\frac{\left[f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})-f(x_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{1}\,,x_{2})+f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})\right]\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}.

Inside of remaining integrals all singularities of integrand
are cancelled so that it is possible to perform the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Due to the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma all contributions with integrals
tend to zero in the limit
\lambda\to\infty
and we have
after removing
\varepsilon
-regularization and
\lambda\to\infty

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\frac{f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=(2\pi\imath)^{2}\,f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})

The second term in (
C.4
) is obtained by
y_{1}\rightleftarrows y_{2}

so that finally one obtains the stated result

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\frac{f(x_{1}\,,x_{2})\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}\,x_{12}y_{21}}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{1}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=\\[6.0pt]
(2\pi\imath)^{2}\,\left[f(y_{1}\,,y_{2})-f(y_{2}\,,y_{1})\right].

It is evident that the symmetric part of the function
f(x_{1},x_{2})
does not contribute so that
the nontrivial contribution is due to antisymmetric part of the function
f(x_{1},x_{2})
.
Antisymmetric part of the test function
f(x_{1},x_{2})
should be zero at
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
without loss of generality it is possible to use representation

f(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}-x_{2})\phi(x_{1},x_{2})
, where
\phi(x_{1},x_{2})
can be generic because
antisymmetric part of the function
\phi(x_{1},x_{2})
does not contribute.
Finally one obtains the formula (
C.2
) in the case
n=2

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\frac{x^{2}_{12}\,\phi(x_{1}\,,x_{2})\,e^{\imath\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})}}{\left(x_{1}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{1}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{1}-\imath\varepsilon\right)\left(x_{2}-y_{2}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=\\[6.0pt]
(2\pi\imath)^{2}\,\left[\phi(y_{1}\,,y_{2})+\phi(y_{2}\,,y_{1})\right].

In general case we again use Cauchy determinant identity in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{k<j}x_{kj}\,y_{jk}}{\prod_{k,j=1}^{n}(x_{k}-y_{j}-\imath\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\det\left(\frac{1}{x_{k}-y_{j}-\imath\varepsilon}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(-1)^{s(\sigma)}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{k}-y_{\sigma(k)}-\imath\varepsilon}

In analogy with
n=2
we shall prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int dx_{1}\,\cdots dx_{n}\,\frac{f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,e^{\imath\lambda\sum_{k}(x_{k}-y_{k})}}{\prod_{k}\left(x_{k}-y_{k}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=(2\pi\imath)^{n}\,f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})

and then use the same identity with evident permutations.
We introduce the natural generalization of the operators
X_{k}

\displaystyle X_{k}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{1},\ldots,y_{k},\ldots,x_{n})

and the main expansion

1=\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-X_{k}+X_{k})=\\
\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-X_{k})+\sum_{k=1}X_{k}\prod_{i\neq k}^{N}(1-X_{i})+\sum_{k,p=1}X_{k}X_{p}\prod_{i\neq k,p}^{N}(1-X_{i})+\ldots+\prod_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}

Due to the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma all contributions containing

\prod_{i}(1-X_{i})\,f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
in integrand are regular
at corresponding points so that corresponding integrals
tend to zero in the limit
\lambda\to\infty
. In the needed limit only one term

\prod_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}
survives and produces
(2\pi\imath)^{n}\,f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
in the full anagoly with the case
n=2
.
Then for the whole sum we obtain

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n}\,f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\frac{\prod_{k<j}x_{kj}\,y_{jk}\,e^{\imath\lambda\sum_{a=1}^{n}(x_{a}-y_{a})}}{\prod_{a,b=1}^{n}\left(x_{a}-y_{b}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=\\[6.0pt]
(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(2\pi\imath)^{n}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}(-1)^{s(w)}\,f\big(y_{w(1)},\ldots,y_{w(n)}\big)

(C.5)

and this identity is equivalent to (
C.3
).
The next step is very similar to the case
n=2
.
Indeed, only the antisymmetric part of the test function

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
gives nontrivial contribution so that without
loss of generality it is possible to use the following
representation for the test function

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
where
\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\prod_{k<j}x_{kj}
.
We have an evident relation

\displaystyle\Delta\big(x_{w(1)},\ldots,x_{w(n)}\big)=(-1)^{s(w)}\,\Delta\big(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\big)

and as consequence one obtains (
C.2
)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n}\,\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\frac{\prod_{k<j}x^{2}_{kj}\,e^{\imath\lambda\sum_{a=1}^{n}(x_{a}-y_{a})}}{\prod_{a,b=1}^{n}\left(x_{a}-y_{b}-\imath\varepsilon\right)}=\\[6.0pt]
(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(2\pi\imath)^{n}\,\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\,\phi\big(y_{w(1)},\ldots,y_{w(n)}\big).
