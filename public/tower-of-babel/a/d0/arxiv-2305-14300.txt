Distributed CONGEST Algorithms against Mobile Adversaries
Orr Fischer, Merav Parter

Abstract
In their seminal PODC 1991 paper, Ostrovsky and Yung introduced the study of distributed computation in the presence of mobile adversaries which can dynamically appear throughout the network, analogous to a spread of a virus. Over the years, this setting has been studied mostly under the assumption that the communication graph is fully-connected. Resilient CONGEST algorithms for general graphs, on the other hand, are currently known only for the classical static setting, i.e., where the set of corrupted edges (or nodes) is fixed throughout the entire computation. We fill this missing gap by providing round-efficient simulations that translate given CONGEST algorithms into equivalent algorithms that are resilient against f f -mobile edge adversaries, i.e., where the adversary controls a (possibly distinct) subset of f f edges F i F_{i} in each round i i . Our main results are: • Perfect-Security with Mobile Eavesdroppers. A translation of any r r -round f f - static -secure algorithm into an equivalent Θ ⁡ ( f ) \Theta(f) - mobile -secure algorithm with Θ ⁡ ( r ) \Theta(r) rounds. We also show that the f f -static-secure algorithms of [Hitron, Parter and Yogev, DISC 2022 & ITCS 2023] can be modified into f f - mobile -secure algorithms with the same number of rounds. • Resilience with Mobile Byzantine Adversaries. An f f -mobile-byzantine simulation which is based on a decomposition of the graph into low-diameter edge-disjoint spanning trees. This provides us with near-optimal CONGEST compilers for expander graphs. It also leads to near-optimal compilers in the congested-clique model against Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) -mobile adversaries. For general ( 2 ​ f + 1 ) (2f+1) edge-connected graphs with f f -mobile adversary, we almost match the bounds known for the f f -static setting, when provided a trusted pre-processing phase. Our results are based on a collection of tools borrowed from the area of interactive coding [Gelles, Found. Trends Theor. Comput. Sci. 2017], linear sketches and low-congestion graph decomposition. The introduced toolkit might have further applications for resilient computation.

1.1
New Results

We present a new algorithmic framework for distributed computation in the presence of mobile edge adversaries, in which the set of corrupted edges changes dynamically and adaptively throughout the execution. We investigate two main adversarial settings: (i) mobile eavesdroppers where the key objective is
security
of information and (ii) mobile byzantine adversaries where we strive for maintaining the correctness of the computation.

Technical Overview

1.2.1
Perfect-Security with Mobile Adversaries

Simulating a given
f
-static-secure
r
-round algorithm
\mathcal{A}
securely in the

-mobile setting is based on the following observation: Assume that all but a subset of
f

, hold

. We then claim that the resulting compiled algorithm,

, is

-mobile secure. Observe that all the messages of

exchanged over the edges of

are distributed uniformly at random, in the eyes of the adversary. We then use the

-static security guarantees of

to show that the information exchanged over

We therefore conclude that our key task is in providing all, but at most

[

To improve upon the extra

-mobility, see Thm.
1.2
), we show that a white-box combination of the Bit-Extraction procedure of Chor et al.
[
16
]
with the framework of
[
41
,
42
]
yields
f
-mobile algorithms with the same number of rounds. As an appetizer, we provide the following very simple, yet at first glance, surprising observation which serves as the basis for adapting
[
41

42
]
to the

-mobile setting.

Key Observation: Mobile-Secure Unicast is Easy.
At the heart of the algorithms of
[
41
,
42
]
lies a (static) secure unicast procedure of Jain
[
45
]

to exchange a secret message in

controlled by the

It is then easy to prove perfect security provided that the following minimal condition holds. Let

be the edges controlled by the adversary in round

and

This exercise illustrates that mobile security is

allows one to handle the general case of

congestion.

We now turn to consider the considerably more challenging task of providing resilience in the presence of

Informally, a

Omitting the Dependency in
f

The improved bound is obtained by replacing the sparse recovery sketches by

The basic intuition for this procedure is the following: Given that we have

-sampling sketches), then the root observes all real mismatches w.h.p. Note, however, that some of these observed mismatches might be

-sketches. To overcome this, we set a minimal threshold
\Delta

\Delta

\ell=O(\log f)

At the beginning of each phase
j\in[\ell]

of its received messages, for each

v_{r}

\Delta_{j}
accordingly.

The remaining caveat is the assumption on correctly receiving the root’s information. This might not hold, in general, as the adversary may introduce
f
incorrect mismatches in each phase when downcasting the sketch information from the root. To overcome this last hurdle, we combine error correction codes with the RS-compilers. The root

Handling Adversaries with Bounded Round-Error-Rates.

In

4.1
, we consider a stronger setting in which the adversary can corrupt “on average”

We provide a network extension to this paradigm, that is somewhat different than the approach taken in prior works, e.g., in
[
43
]

for distinct nodes

\sigma

In the context of this paper, an

4
Resilience to Bounded Round-Error Corruption Rate

In this section, we consider a stronger adversary, which is allowed to corrupt
f
edges per round “on average”, allowing e.g. for specific rounds with unbounded faults. We assume that all nodes terminate exactly at some time
r^{\prime}
for some given
r^{\prime}
, and the adversary may corrupt in total
r^{\prime}\cdot f
communication transmissions in the duration of the protocol. Our goal is given a protocol
\mathcal{A}
, to construct a protocol
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with the same output as
\mathcal{A}
which is resilient to this adversary.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given a distributed knowledge of a weak
(k,\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{TP}},\eta)
tree packing
\mathcal{T}
for graph
G
, then for any
r
-round algorithm
\mathcal{A}
over
G
, there is an equivalent
r^{\prime}
-round algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
for
G
that is resilient to round-error rate
f
, where
r^{\prime}=\widetilde{O}(r\cdot\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{TP}})
and
f=\Theta(\frac{k}{\eta\log{n}})
.

Similarly to
Section

3.2
, we would like to simulate
\mathcal{A}
in a round-by-round manner, but in this setting we cannot correct all mismatches in every round, since the adversary may invest a “large budget” of faults in it.

We use an approach referred to as the “rewind-if-error” paradigm for interactive coding. For a comprehensive exposition of the rewind-if-error paradigm, and interactive coding in general, we refer to the excellent survey of
[
33
]
.

Intuitively, our approach is as follows: If we have a round error rate of
f
, the number of rounds in which there can be more than
\alpha f
faulty messages is at most
O(1/\alpha)
-fraction of the rounds. This means that if we apply the algorithm of Section
3
on each simulated round such that it is resilient to
\alpha f
total faults, in all but
O(1/\alpha)
-fraction of the rounds there are no mismatches after a correction phase in the
entire network
. Rounds with more faults may cause errors; to remedy this, we combine the approach of Section
3
with a global variant of the rewind-if-error scheme, in which if
any
error in the past transcript is detected in
any
part of the network, the entire network “rewinds” a step (by having nodes delete the last symbol of the transcript). This in turn may cause several issues, such that causing some nodes to have more rounds in their transcript than other nodes, which is why rewinding needs to be done with some care.

4.1
Algorithm Description

We assume that the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for the fault-free setting is for the
1
-
CONGEST
model. This can be assumed w.l.o.g. by paying a
O(\log{n})
factor in its round complexity. Throughout the section, a symbol is a value in
\{0,1,\bot\}
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}=\left\{h_{i}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\Theta(\log{n})}\right\}
be the pairwise-independent hash function family of

.

Our algorithm has

iterations (called

), containing three phases: a round-initialization phase, a message-correcting phase, and a rewind-if-error phase. We design each phase to take

).

Each node

(resp.,

(resp., sent from

Theorem C.2
.

Let
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
be a collection of trees obtained by a packing process described above, where

for any

\max_{e}l_{e}=O(\eta\alpha(\log{n}+\log{\alpha}))

Proof.

For any step
i
, the following holds due to our choice of
T_{i}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in T_{i}}(a^{h^{i+1}_{e}/\eta}-a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in T_{i}}(a^{(h^{i}_{e}+1)/\eta}-a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta})\leq\alpha\sum_{e\in T^{*}_{i}}(a^{(h^{i}_{e}+1)/\eta}-a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta})

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{e\in T^{*}_{i}}a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta}(a^{1/\eta}-1)\leq\alpha\sum_{e\in T^{*}_{i}}a^{l_{e}/\eta}(a-1)/\eta~.

We sum on
i=1,\dots,k
:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{e\in T_{i}}(a^{(h^{i}_{e}+1)/\eta}-a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta})\leq\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{e\in T^{*}_{i}}a^{l_{e}}(a-1)/\eta~,

where the last inequality holds since
a^{x}-1\leq x(a-1)
for any
0\leq x\leq 1
. By switching the order of summation, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{i:e\in T_{i}}(a^{(h^{i+1}_{e})/\eta}-a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{i:e\in T_{i}}(a^{(h^{i}_{e}+1)/\eta}-a^{h^{i}_{e}/\eta})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(a-1)\sum_{e\in E}(a^{l_{e}/\eta}\cdot\sum_{i:e\in T^{*}_{i}}(1/\eta))

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(a-1)\sum_{e\in E}a^{l_{e}/\eta}~.

As the left sum is a telescopic, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}(a^{l_{e}/\eta}-1)\leq\alpha(a-1)\sum_{e\in E}a^{l_{e}/\eta}

\displaystyle\implies\left(\sum_{e\in E}a^{l_{e}/\eta}\right)-|E|\leq\alpha(a-1)\sum_{e\in E}a^{l_{e}/\eta}

\displaystyle\implies(1+\alpha-\alpha a)\sum_{e\in E}a^{l_{e}/\eta}\leq|E|\implies(1+\alpha-\alpha a)\max_{e\in E}a^{l_{e}/\eta}\leq|E|~.

Finally, by taking
\log_{a}
from both sides:

\displaystyle\max_{e\in E}(l_{e}/\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{a}|E|-\log_{a}(1+\alpha-\alpha a)=(\log_{2}{|E|}-\log_{2}(1+\alpha-\alpha a))/\log_{2}{\frac{\alpha+2}{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle=(\log_{2}{|E|}+\log_{2}(1+\alpha))/\log_{2}{\frac{\alpha+2}{\alpha+1}}\leq(\log_{2}{|E|}+\log_{2}(1+\alpha))/(1/(2+2\alpha))

\displaystyle=O(\alpha(\log{n}+\log{\alpha})),

where the last inequality holds since

.

∎
