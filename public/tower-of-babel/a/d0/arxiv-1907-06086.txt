Skew Generalized Polycyclic Codes with Derivations
Shikha Patel 1, Om Prakash ∗1

Abstract
In this paper, we first consider the iterated skew polynomial ring ℛ ⁡ [ z 1 ; τ 1 , δ τ 1 ] \mathscr{R}[z_{1};\tau_{1},\delta_{\tau_{1}}] [ z 2 ; τ 2 , δ τ 2 ] [z_{2};\tau_{2},\delta_{\tau_{2}}] , where ℛ \mathscr{R} is a finite ring with unity. Then we use this structure for the construction of skew generalized polycyclic codes over the ring ℛ \mathscr{R} and finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} , where q = p m q=p^{m} for some positive integer m m . Further, we derive the structure of the generator and parity check matrices for skew generalized polycyclic codes. Furthermore, we improve the Bose-Chaudhuri-Hocquenghem (BCH) lower bound for a minimum distance of skew generalized polycyclic codes with non-zero derivations over a finite field. Moreover, we find a sufficient condition for a code to be a maximum-distance-separable (MDS) code. In addition, we provide examples of MDS codes to show the importance of our results. A comparative summary of our work with other linear codes is also discussed.

Introduction

Coding theory mainly studies the methods that are efficient and preserve the accuracy of information during transmission from one place to another. In coding theory, linear codes have been studied for the last seven decades. Initially, linear codes were studied over the binary field. In
1970
, Blake
Blake
initiated the study of codes over finite rings. Cyclic codes are the most important class of linear codes from an implementation point of view and also play a prominent role in the development of algebraic coding theory. These codes have been studied over several finite rings and produce many new and optimal codes, refer
Blake1
;

In

\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field, and

\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Besides the commutative case, skew polynomial rings also have many applications in constructing algebraic codes with better parameters. It is well-known that the factorization of a polynomial plays a key role in studying these codes. More factorization leads to a rich algebraic structure that is capable of producing codes with good parameters. Therefore, it is logical to extend these codes over a structure in which polynomials have more factorization than the commutative structure. One smart choice is the skew polynomial ring. We always have
more choices to construct new codes from the codes over the skew polynomial ring than a commutative ring. That is one of the major motivations to study codes over a skew polynomial ring.

In

Preliminaries

Recall that a linear code
\mathcal{L}
of length
n
over a ring
R
is considered as an
R
-submodule of
R^{n}
and each member of
\mathcal{L}
is a codeword. The rank of
\mathcal{L}
is the minimum number of generators for
\mathcal{L}
as a module, and the free rank of
\mathcal{L}
is the rank of
\mathcal{L}
if
\mathcal{L}
is free. A linear code of length
n
and rank

is denoted by
[n,k]
. The Hamming weight for a codeword
c=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}),
is denoted by
w_{H}(c)
and defined by
w_{H}(c)=\mid\{i~:c_{i}\neq 0\}\mid.
Now, the Hamming distance between two codewords
c^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime\prime}
is defined as
d_{H}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})=w_{H}(c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\prime})
and for
\mathcal{L}
, it is
d_{H}(\mathcal{L})=\min\{d_{H}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})~:~c^{\prime}\neq c^{\prime\prime},c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\}.
Therefore, an
[n,k]
linear code is compactly represented by
[n,k,d_{H}]
where
d_{H}
is the Hamming distance. Also, the Euclidean inner product
``\cdot"
between any two vectors
c^{\prime}=(c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime}_{2},\dots,c^{\prime}_{n})
and
c^{\prime\prime}=(c^{\prime\prime}_{1},c^{\prime\prime}_{2},\dots,c^{\prime\prime}_{n})
of
R^{n}
is defined by
c^{\prime}\cdot c^{\prime\prime}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c^{\prime}_{i}c^{\prime\prime}_{i}
. It is evident that the (Euclidean) dual of
\mathcal{L}
defined by
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}=\{c^{\prime}\in R^{n}~:~c^{\prime}\cdot c^{\prime\prime}=0,
for all
c^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\}
is a linear code when
\mathcal{L}
is linear. In addition,
\mathcal{L}
is said to be a
self-orthogonal
code if
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
, and a
self-dual
code if
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.

If
\mathcal{L}
is an
[n,k,d]
code, then from
the Singleton bound, its minimum distance is bounded above by
d\leq n-k+1.

A code that satisfies the above bound with equality is called
maximum-distance-separable (MDS)
. A code is
almost MDS
if its minimum distance is one unit less than the MDS bound and
optimal
if it has the highest possible minimum distance for a given length and dimension.
