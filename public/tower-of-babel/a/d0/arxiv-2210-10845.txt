Obstacle problems with double boundary condition for least gradient functions in metric measure spaces
Josh Kline

Abstract
. In the setting of a metric space equipped with a doubling measure supporting a ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) -Poincaré inequality, we study the problem of minimizing the BV-energy in a bounded domain Ω \Omega of functions bounded between two obstacle functions inside Ω \Omega , and whose trace lies between two prescribed functions on the boundary of Ω . \Omega. If the class of candidate functions is nonempty, we show that solutions exist for continuous obstacles and continuous boundary data when Ω \Omega is a uniform domain whose boundary is of positive mean curvature in the sense of Lahti, Malý, Shanmugalingam, and Speight (2019). While such solutions are not unique in general, we show the existence of unique minimal solutions. Our existence results generalize those of Ziemer and Zumbrun (1999), who studied this problem in the Euclidean setting with a single obstacle and single boundary condition.

1.
Introduction

Given some function
f
on the boundary of a domain
\Omega
and a function
\psi
on
\overline{\Omega}
, the obstacle problem for least gradient functions is the problem of minimizing the BV-energy in
\Omega
over all functions
u\in BV(\Omega)
whose trace agrees with
f
almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega

and
such that
u\geq\psi
almost everwhere in
\Omega.
In the Euclidean setting, this problem was first studied by Ziemer and Zumbrun in
[
52
]
, where they showed that a continuous solution exists for
\psi\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and
f\in C(\partial\Omega)
such that
f\geq\psi
on
\partial\Omega,
provided the boundary of
\Omega
has nonnegative mean curvature and is not locally area-minimizing. Their work generalized some of the earlier results of Sternberg, Williams, and Ziemer
[
51
]
, who introduced and studied the Dirichlet problem for least gradient functions (without obstacle) in the Euclidean setting. Recently existence, uniqueness, and regularity of an anisotropic formulation of the obstacle problem for least gradients in the Euclidean setting was studied in
[
12
]
. For the relationship between the obstacle problem for least gradients and dual maximization problems in the Euclidean setting, see
[
48
]
.

In
[
35
]
, existence and regularity of solutions to the obstacle problem for least gradients were studied in the setting of a metric space equipped with a doubling measure and supporting a Poincaré inequality. Here solutions were not required to attain the boundary condition in the sense of traces; competing functions need only satisfy the obstacle condition inside the domain and satisfy the boundary condition outside the domain.

In this paper, we continue the study of obstacle problems for least gradient functions in the metric setting. In contrast to
[
35
]
however, we insist that solutions address the boundary condition in the sense of traces, following
[
52
]
in the Euclidean setting. Furthermore, we include a second obstacle function and consider a
double
boundary condition. That is, for
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\overline{\Omega}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
f,g\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega),
we consider the problem

\min\{\|Du\|(\Omega):u\in BV(\Omega),\,\psi_{1}\leq u\leq\psi_{2},\,f\leq Tu\leq g\},

where the inequalities are in the almost everywhere sense. By defining
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)
to be the class of BV-functions in
\Omega
satisfying the obstacle and boundary conditions, we refer to solutions to this problem as
strong solutions
to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem (see Section 2.3 below for the precise definitions). We adopt the following standing assumptions:

•

(X,d,\mu)
is a complete metric measure space supporting a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality, with
\mu
a doubling Borel regular measure,

•

\Omega\subset X
is a bounded domain with
\mu(X\setminus\Omega)>0,

•

\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega)<\infty
,
\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is doubling, and
\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is lower codimension 1 Ahlfors regular, see (
2.3
),

•

\mathcal{H}(\{z\})=0
for all
z\in\partial\Omega.

where
\mathcal{H}
is the codimension 1 Hausdorff measure on
\partial\Omega
, see (
2.2
). The assumption that singletons on the boundary of the domain are
\mathcal{H}
-negligible is necessary to obtain Lemma
2.17
, see Example
2.20
. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a uniform domain with boundary of positive mean curvature as in Definition
2.11
. Let
f,g\in C(\partial\Omega)
and
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
be such that
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing.
Then there exists a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem.

By
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega}),
we mean that these functions are extended real valued functions, and continuous with respect to the standard topology on the extended real line. As such we can consider continuous obstacle functions
\psi_{1}\equiv-\infty
and
\psi_{2}\equiv\infty.
However throughout this paper, we do insist that the boundary functions
f
and
g
are real-valued, so as to utilize certain extension results from
[
37
]
.

Strong solutions to this problem may fail to be unique (see
[
37
]
and the discussion below), and so a comparison theorem for strong solutions will not hold in general. However, in Proposition
4.4
and Remark
4.5
, we show that unique
minimal
and
maximal
strong solutions exist for continuous obstacle and boundary functions, and we obtain the following comparison-type result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in C(\partial\Omega)
such that
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
and
g_{1}\leq g_{2}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.
Let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
be such that
\psi_{1}\leq\psi_{2}
and
\varphi_{1}\leq\varphi_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Suppose that

\mathcal{K}_{1}(\Omega):=\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\varphi_{1},f_{1},g_{1}}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing\neq\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{2},\varphi_{2},f_{2},g_{2}}(\Omega)=:\mathcal{K}_{2}(\Omega).

If
u_{1}
is the minimal strong solutions to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}
-obstacle problem and
u_{2}
is a strong solution (not necessarily minimal) to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem, then
u_{1}\leq u_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Likewise, if
v_{1}
is a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}
-obstacle problem and
v_{2}
is the maximal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem, then
v_{1}\leq v_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.

Problems involving related double boundary conditions have been studied in a variety of different contexts. In stochastic analysis in particular, double-boundary (non-)crossing problems, where one tries to determine the probability that a stochastic process remains between two prescribed boundaries, have been studied extensively and have many statistical applications. For a sampling, see
[
8
,
10
,
13
,
39
]
and references therein. Related notions of double boundary layers also appear in fluid dynamics and perturbation theory, see
[
9
,
30
]
for example. As such, it seems natural to consider such a double boundary condition in the context of BV-energy minimizers.

By setting
\psi_{2}\equiv\infty,
and
f=g,
we recover the classical obstacle problem for least gradients. If in addition we set
\psi_{1}\equiv-\infty,
then we recover the Dirichlet problem for least gradient functions (also referred to as the least gradient problem). As mentioned above, the least gradient problem was first studied in the Euclidean setting by Sternberg, Williams, and Ziemer
[
51
]
, who showed that unique solutions exist for continuous boundary data, provided that the boundary of the domain has nonnegative mean curvature and is not locally area-minimizing.

Since its introduction in
[
51
]
, existence, uniqueness, and regularity of the least gradient problem above have been studied extensively in the Euclidean setting; for a sampling, see
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
26
,
29
,
41
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
50
,
53
]
and the references therein. In particular, weighted versions of this problem have applications to current density impedance imaging, see for example
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
.

In recent decades, analysis in metric spaces has become a field of active study, in particular when the space is equipped with a doubling measure and supports a Poincaré inequality, see for example
[
3
,
6
,
23
,
25
]
. A definition of BV functions and sets of finite perimeter was extended to this setting by Miranda Jr. in
[
42
]
, and consequently, a theory of least gradient functions in metric spaces has been developed in recent years, see for example
[
18
,
19
,
22
,
27
,
29
,
32
,
37
,
38
,
40
]
. In
[
37
]
, Lahti, Malý, Shanmugalingam, and Speight studied the Dirichlet problem for least gradients, originally introduced in
[
51
]
, in the metric setting. To do so, they introduced a definition of positive mean curvature in the metric setting (Definition
2.11
below) and showed that solutions exist for continuous boundary data if the boundary of the domain satisfies this condition. It is this curvature condition that we assume in Theorem
1.1
.

In
[
37
]
it was also shown that in the weighted unit disc, uniqueness and continuity of solutions may fail even for Lipschitz boundary data. As the problem we study in this paper is a generalization of this Dirichlet problem, we cannot guarantee uniqueness or continuity of strong solutions to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem. For this reason, our comparison-type result, Theorem
1.2
, is stated in terms of minimal and maximal strong solutions. For more on existence, uniqueness, and regularity of solutions to the weighted least gradient problem, see
[
17
,
26
,
53
]
.

To construct our solution, we adapt the program first implemented by Sternberg, Williams, and Ziemer to obtain solutions to the Dirichlet problem for least gradients in the Euclidean setting in
[
51
]
. There they constructed a least gradient solution by first solving the Dirichlet problem for boundary data consisting of superlevel sets of the original boundary data. This method relies upon the fact, first discovered by Bombieri, De Giorgi, and Giusti
[
7
]
, that characteristic functions of superlevel sets of least gradient functions are themselves of least gradient. The construction of solutions to the obstacle problem for least gradients in
[
52
]
is an adaptation of the program from
[
51
]
.

In the metric setting, the construction of solutions to the Dirichlet problem for least gradients given in
[
37
]
is also inspired by the method established in
[
51
]
. However both
[
51
]
and
[
52
]
utilize smoothness properties and tangent cones for the boundaries of certain solution sets, tools which are not available in the metric setting. Thus the construction in
[
37
]
is a further modification of the method from
[
51
]
. As we are studying the double obstacle problem with double boundary condition in the metric setting, our construction is inspired by that of
[
37
]
.

In
[
37
]
, the authors defined weak solutions to the Dirichlet problem for least gradients (Definition
2.7
below) which are easily obtained via the direct method of calculus of variations for a large class of boundary data. They then used these weak solutions to construct their strong solution. Since we have introduced a double boundary condition, and thus competitor functions are not fixed outside the domain, it is difficult to obtain weak solutions of this form by the direct method. For this reason, we define a family of
\varepsilon
-weak solutions (Definition
2.12
below), which consider the BV-energy in slight enlargements of the domain
\Omega.
By controlling these
\varepsilon
-weak solutions as
\varepsilon\to 0,
we obtain the proper building blocks from which to construct our strong solution to the original problem in the manner of
[
37
]
. Since our argument involves enlargements of
\Omega
, we assume that
\Omega
is a uniform domain in order to use the strong BV extension results obtained in
[
33
]
.

The structure of our paper is as follows: in Section 2, we introduce the basic definitions, notations, and assumptions used throughout the paper. In Section 3, we prove preliminary results regarding solutions to the double obstacle, double boundary problem when the obstacle and boundary functions are characteristic functions of certain open sets. In Section 4, we use these preliminary results to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. In Section 5, we provide examples illustrating how, in the absence of obstacles, strong solutions to the double boundary problem may not be solutions to the Dirichlet problems for either boundary condition. In this section, we also study how solutions of the double obstacle, double boundary problem converge to solutions of the double obstacle, single boundary problem as the double boundary data converge to a single datum (see Theorem
5.5
below).

2.
Background

2.1.
General metric measure spaces

Throughout this paper, we assume that
(X,d,\mu)
is a complete metric measure space, with
\mu
a doubling Borel regular measure supporting a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality (defined below). By
doubling
, we mean that there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that

0<\mu(B(x,2r))\leq C\mu(B(x,r))<\infty

for all
x\in X
and
r>0.
By iterating this condition, there exist constants
C_{D}\geq 1
and
Q>1
such that

(2.1)

\frac{\mu(B(y,r))}{\mu(B(x,R))}\geq C_{D}^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{Q}

for every
0<r\leq R
,
x\in X
, and
y\in B(x,R).
In this paper we let
C
denote constants, depending, unless otherwise stated, only on
\Omega
and the doubling and Poincaré inequality constants (see below), whose precise value is not needed. The value of
C
may differ even within the same line.

Complete metric spaces equipped with doubling measures are necessarily proper, i.e. closed and bounded sets are compact. For any open set
\Omega\subset X
, we define
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
as the space of functions that are in
L^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})
for every
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega,
that is, for every open set
\Omega^{\prime}
such that
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
is a compact subset of
\Omega.
Other local function spaces are defined analogously. For a ball
B=B(x,r)
and
\lambda>0
, we often denote
\lambda B:=B(x,\lambda r).
If
A,B\subset X,
then by
A\sqsubset B
, we mean that
\mu(A\setminus B)=0.
By a
domain
, we mean a nonempty connected open set in
X.

We say that
X
is
A
-
quasiconvex
if
A\geq 1
and for all points
x,y\in X,
there exists a curve
\gamma
connecting
x
and
y
such that

\ell(\gamma)\leq Ad(x,y).

We say that
\Omega
is an
A
-
uniform domain
if
A\geq 1
and for all
x,y\in\Omega,
there exists a curve
\gamma
in
\Omega
connecting
x
and
y
such that
\ell(\gamma)\leq Ad(x,y)
and

\min\{\ell(\gamma_{x,z}),\ell(\gamma_{z,y})\}\leq A\dist(z,X\setminus\Omega)

for all
z\in\gamma.
By
\gamma_{x,z}
, we mean any subcurve of
\gamma
joining
x
to
z.

2.2.
Newtonian spaces and BV functions

For a function
u:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},
we say that a Borel function
g:X\to[0,\infty]
is an
upper gradient
of
u
if the following inequality holds for all non-constant compact rectifiable curves
\gamma:[a,b]\to X,

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq\int_{\gamma}gds,

whenever
u(x)
and
u(y)
are both finite, and
\int_{\gamma}gds=\infty
otherwise. Here
x
and
y
denote the endpoints of the curve
\gamma.
Upper gradients were originally introduced in
[
24
]
.

Let
\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X)
be the class of all functions in
L^{1}(X)
for which there exists an upper gradient in
L^{1}(X).
For
u\in\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X),
we define

\|u\|_{\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X)}=\|u\|_{L^{1}(X)}+\inf_{g}\|g\|_{L^{1}(X)},

where the infimum is taken over all upper gradients
g
of
u
. Now, we define an equivalence relation in
\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X)
by
u\sim v
if and only if
\|u-v\|_{\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X)}=0.

The
Newtonian space

N^{1,1}(X)
is defined as the quotient
\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X)/\sim,
and it is equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{N^{1,1}(X)}=\|u\|_{\widetilde{N}^{1,1}(X)}.
One can analogously define
N^{1,1}(\Omega)
for an open set
\Omega\subset X.
When
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
or when
X
is a Carnot group, we have that
N^{1,1}(X)=W^{1,1}(X).
For more on Newtonian spaces, see
[
49
]
or
[
6
,
21
,
25
]
.

To define functions of bounded variation on metric spaces, we follow the definition introduced by Miranda Jr. in
[
42
]
. For an open set
\Omega\subset X
and a function
u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),
we define the
total variation
of
u
in
\Omega
by

\|Du\|(\Omega)=\inf\left\{\liminf_{i\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}g_{u_{i}}d\mu:N^{1,1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\ni u_{i}\to u\text{ in }L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\right\},

where
g_{u_{i}}
are upper gradients of
u_{i}.
For an arbitrary set
A\subset X,
we define

\|Du\|(A)=\inf\left\{\|Du\|(\Omega):A\subset\Omega,\Omega\subset X\text{ open}\right\}.

We say that a function
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is in
BV(\Omega)
(i.e. of
bounded variation
in
\Omega
) if
\|Du\|(\Omega)<\infty.
We equip
BV(\Omega)
with the norm

\|u\|_{BV(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|Du\|(\Omega).

This definition of of the BV class coincides with the standard definition in the Euclidean setting, see
[
2
,
11
]
. For more on the BV class in metric spaces, see
[
1
,
4
]
.

It was shown in
[
42
, Theorem 3.4]
that if
u\in BV(X)
, then
\|Du\|(\cdot)
is a finite Radon measure on
X.
Furthermore, the BV energy is lower semi-continuous with respect to
L^{1}
convergence. That is, for an open set
\Omega\subset X
, if
u_{k}\to u
in
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),
then

\|Du\|(\Omega)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|Du_{k}\|(\Omega).

This property is crucial to prove existence of the minimizers considered in this paper.

A measurable set
E\subset X
is of
finite perimeter
if
\|D\chi_{E}\|(X)<\infty,
and we denote the
perimeter
of
E
in
\Omega
by

P(E,\Omega)=\|D\chi_{E}\|(\Omega).

It was also shown in
[
42
, Proposition 4.2]
that the following coarea formula holds: if
\Omega\subset X
is open and
u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),
then

\|Du\|(\Omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(\{u>t\},\Omega)dt.

If
u\in BV(X),
then this holds with
\Omega
replaced by any Borel set
A\subset\Omega.

2.3.
Poincaré inequality and consequences

Throughout this paper, we assume that
X
supports a
(1,1)
-
Poincaré inequality
. That is, there exist constants
\lambda,C_{I}\geq 1
such that for every ball
B
, every locally integrable function
u
, and every upper gradient
g
of
u
, we have that

\fint_{B}|u-u_{B}|d\mu\leq C_{I}\rad(B)\fint_{\lambda B}g\,d\mu.

Here and throughout the paper,

u_{B}=\fint_{B}u\,d\mu=\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}u\,d\mu.

It was shown in
[
31
]
that the
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality is equivalent to the
relative isoperimetric inequality
: given
E\subset X,

\min\{\mu(B\cap E),\mu(B\setminus E)\}\leq C\rad(B)P(E,\lambda B)

for each ball
B\subset X.

Given
E\subset X
, we define its
codimension
1
Hausdorff measure
by

(2.2)

\mathcal{H}(E)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\inf\left\{\sum_{i}\frac{\mu(B_{i})}{\rad(B_{i})}:B_{i}\text{ balls in }X,\,E\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{i},\,\rad(B_{i})<\delta\right\}.

We say that
\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is
lower codimension
1
Ahlfors regular
if there exists
C>0
such that

(2.3)

\mathcal{H}(B(x,r)\cap\partial\Omega)\geq C\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{r}

for every
x\in\partial\Omega
and
0<r<2\diam(\partial\Omega).

In
[
1
,
4
]
, it was shown that if
\mu
is doubling and
X
supports a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality, then there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that whenever
E\subset X
is of finite perimeter and
A\subset X
is a Borel set, we have

C^{-1}\mathcal{H}(A\cap\partial_{M}E)\leq P(E,A)\leq C\mathcal{H}(A\cap\partial_{M}E).

Here
\partial_{M}E
is the
measure-theoretic boundary
of
E,
which is the set of all points
x\in X
such that

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(B(x,r)\cap E)}{\mu(B(x,r))}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(B(x,r)\setminus E)}{\mu(B(x,r))}>0.

For an extended real-valued function
u
on
X
, we define the approximate upper and lower limits of
u
by

\displaystyle u^{\vee}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(\{u>t\}\cap B(x,r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}=0\right\},

\displaystyle u^{\wedge}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(\{u<t\}\cap B(x,r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}=0\right\}.

From the Lebesgue differentiation theorem,
u^{\vee}=u^{\wedge}

\mu
-a.e. if
u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(X).
The points for which
u^{\wedge}(x)=u^{\vee}(x)
are said to be
points of approximate continuity
of
u.
We denote by

S_{u}=\{u^{\wedge}<u^{\vee}\},

the so called
jump set
of
u
. If
u\in BV(X)
, then by
[
4
, Proposition 5.2]
,
S_{u}
is of
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}
-measure. Furthermore, by
[
4
, Theorem 5.3]
, the Radon measure
\|Du\|(\cdot)
has the following decomposition:

(2.4)

d\|Du\|=g\,d\mu+d\|D^{j}u\|+d\|D^{c}u\|.

Here,
g\in L^{1}(X)
and
g\,d\mu
is the part of
\|Du\|
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
. We call
\|D^{j}u\|
the
jump-part
of
u
, which is supported in
S_{u}
and is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}|_{S_{u}}.
We call the measure
\|D^{c}u\|
the
Cantor-part
of
\|Du\|
. This measure does not detect sets of
\sigma
-finite
\mathcal{H}
-measure.

The following Leibniz rule for BV functions was shown in
[
34
, Theorem 4.7]
. If
\Omega\subset X
is open and
u,v\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
, then

(2.5)

\|D(uv)\|(\Omega)\leq\int_{\Omega}|u|^{\vee}d\|Dv\|+\int_{\Omega}|v|^{\vee}d\|Du\|.

2.4.
Least gradient functions and obstacle problems

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset X
be open, and let
u\in BV_{\text{loc}}(\Omega).
We say that
u
is of
least gradient
in
\Omega
if

\|Du\|(V)\leq\|Dv\|(V),

whenever
v\in BV(\Omega)
with
\overline{\{x\in\Omega:u(x)\neq v(x)\}}\subset V\Subset\Omega.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
X
with
\mu(X\setminus\Omega)>0,
and let
f\in BV_{\text{loc}}(X).
We say that
u\in BV_{\text{loc}}(X)
is a
weak solution to the Dirichlet problem for least gradients in
\Omega
with boundary data
f
, or simply,
weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
, if
u=f
on
X\setminus\Omega
and

\|Du\|(\overline{\Omega})\leq\|Dv\|(\overline{\Omega})

whenever
v\in BV(X)
with
v=f
on
X\setminus\Omega.

Given a bounded domain
\Omega\subset X
and a function
u
on
\Omega,
we say that
u
has a
trace
at a point
x\in\partial\Omega
if there is a number
Tu(x)\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\fint_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}|u-Tu(x)|d\mu=0.

We record the following lemma regarding traces of superlevel sets. For proof, see
[
29
]
for example.

Lemma 2.8
.

(
[
29
, Lemma 4.6]
)

Let
f\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega),
and suppose that

-a.e. on

Then for
\mathcal{L}
-a.e.

, we have that
T\chi_{\{u>t\}}=\chi_{\{f>t\}}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
X
and

We say that a function

is a
strong solution to the Dirichlet problem for least gradients in

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on

with

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega,
we must have

\|Du\|(\Omega)\leq\|Dv\|(\Omega).

Weak and strong solutions to Dirichlet problems on a domain

are necessarily of least gradient in
\Omega.

Definition 2.10
.

A weak (strong) solution
u
to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
is called the

weak (strong) solution if every weak (strong) solution
u^{\prime}
to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data

satisfies
u\leq u^{\prime}

\mu

(or in
\Omega
in the case of strong solutions). Likewise, a weak (strong) solution

weak (strong) solution if every weak (strong) solution

to the Dirichlet problem satisfies
u\geq u^{\prime}

\mu
-a.e. in
X
(or in
\Omega
in the case of strong solutions). We note that minimal and maximal weak and strong solutions are necessarily unique

-a.e.

In
[
37
]
, it is shown that for a set

of finite perimeter, there always exists

such that
\chi_{E}
is a weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data

We call

. Moreover, for such

However without additional assumptions on the domain, it is not possible to guarantee that a weak solution set will be a

; that is,

may not be a strong solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data

. There exist boundary data for which weak solutions exist, but no strong solution exists. For this reason, the following definition was introduced in
[
37
]
.

Definition 2.11
.

Given a domain
\Omega\subset X
, we say that the boundary
\partial\Omega
has
positive mean curvature
if for each

there exists a non-decreasing function
\phi_{x}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
and a constant

such that for all

with

, where

is the minimal weak solution set to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(x,r)}
.

In
[
37
]
, it was shown that if
\Omega
has boundary of positive mean curvature and

then for every open set

of finite perimeter such that

are strong solutions. Moreover, if

is a strong solution, then extending

outside

by

yields a weak solution. This phenomenon also holds for some other classes of boundary data. In
[

, Proposition 4.13]
, it was shown that if
\Omega
has boundary of positive mean curvature with

and if

then weak solutions to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data

are strong solutions, and strong solutions extended outside

by

are weak solutions.

In this paper, we study a generalization of the Dirichlet problem for least gradients, namely by adding obstacle functions in the domain and by loosening the boundary condition to allow a range of admissible boundary values. We define weak and strong solutions for this modified problem as follows.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\Omega\subset X
be a domain with
\mu(X\setminus\Omega)>0,
and let
f,g\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(X).
Additionally let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\overline{\Omega}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},
and let

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega):=\{u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(X):\psi_{1}\leq u\leq\psi_{2}\text{ in }\Omega,\;f\leq u\leq g\text{ in }X\setminus\Omega\},

where the inequalities are in the pointwise
everywhere
sense.
For
\varepsilon>0,
let

\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in X:\dist(x,\Omega)<\varepsilon\}.

We say that
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)
is an
\varepsilon
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem
if, for all
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega),
we have that

\|Du\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\leq\|Dv\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon}).

Remark 2.13
.

From the definitions, we see that if
0<\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{2}
and
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
-weak solutions respectively, then it follows that

\|Du_{1}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon_{1}})\leq\|Du_{2}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon_{1}}).

Furthermore if

, then we have that
\|Dv_{1}\|(X\setminus\overline{\Omega})=\|Dv_{2}\|(X\setminus\overline{\Omega})
for all

. As such, if
u
is an
\varepsilon_{0}
-weak solution for some

then
u
is an an
\varepsilon
-weak solution for all
\varepsilon>0.
Hence in the case where
f=g
, we say that
u
is a
weak solution
to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem if

\|Du\|(\overline{\Omega})\leq\|Dv\|(\overline{\Omega})

for all
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega).

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain, and let
f,g\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega).
Additionally, let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\overline{\Omega}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, and let

\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega):=\{u\in BV(\Omega):\psi_{1}\leq u\leq\psi_{2}\text{ in }\Omega,\;f\leq Tu\leq g\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}

where the inequalites are in the
\mu
and
\mathcal{H}
-a.e. sense respectively.
We say that
u\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)
is a
strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem
if

\|Du\|(\Omega)\leq\|Dv\|(\Omega)

for all
v\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega).

In the case where
\psi_{1}=\chi_{A},

\psi_{2}=\chi_{B},

f=\chi_{F}
and
g=\chi_{G},
we set

\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}:=\mathcal{K}_{\chi_{A},\chi_{B},\chi_{F},\chi_{G}}

in either of the above definitions for ease of notation.

While the inequalities in the definition of
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)
above are in the almost everywhere sense, we note that the inequalities in the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)
are in the
everywhere
sense. Since these functions pertain to
\varepsilon
-weak solutions, this will not be too restrictive for our purposes.

Note that by taking
\psi_{1}\equiv-\infty
and
\psi_{2}\equiv\infty
and by letting
f=g
, we recover the standard Dirichlet problem for least gradients as a special case of this formulation. Likewise, we obtain any combination of obstacle problem with upper or lower obstacles or boundary data. Moreover, we can define minimal and maximal strong solutions in the same manner as Definition
2.10
:

Definition 2.15
.

A strong solution
u
to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem is called the
minimal
(
maximal
) strong solution if any strong solution
u^{\prime}
satisfies
u\leq u^{\prime}
(
u\geq u^{\prime}
)
\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
We note that minimal and maximal strong solutions are necessarily unique
\mu
-a.e.

If
E\subset X
is a weak solution set to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{F}
for some
F\subset X,
then
E
is a
1
-quasiminimal set as in
[
27
, Definition 3.1]
. Thus by
[
27
, Theorem 4.2]
,
E
satisfies a uniform measure density condition inside
\Omega
. That is, by modifying
E
on a set of measure zero if necessary, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
depending only on the doubling and Poincaré inequality constants such that for all

\frac{\mu(B(x,r)\cap E)}{\mu(B(x,r))}\geq\gamma_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mu(B(x,r)\setminus E)}{\mu(B(x,r))}\geq\gamma_{0}

whenever
0<r<\diam(X)/3
such that
B(x,2r)\subset\Omega.
We refer to this modified

as a
regularized
weak solution set. Furthermore, it was shown in
[

that such regularized weak solution sets are uniformly locally porous.

Theorem 2.16
.

(
[
27
, Theorem 5.2]
)

There exists a constant
C_{P}\geq 1
such that for every set
E
which is
1
-quasiminimal set in
\Omega
and for every

and

such that
B(x,10r)\subset\Omega
, there exist points

such that

B(y,r/C_{P})\subset E\cap\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad B(z,r/C_{P})\subset\Omega\setminus E.

The constant
C_{P}
depends only on the doubling and Poincaré constants.

We conclude this section by using the above porosity property to prove the following useful lemma, which gives us a bound on the size of certain regularized minimal weak solutions to Dirichlet problems.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with
\mu(X\setminus\Omega)>0
, and suppose that the following hold:

(i)

\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is lower codimension 1 Ahlfors regular (
2.3
), and
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega)<\infty
.

(ii)

\mathcal{H}(\{z\})=0
for all
z\in\partial\Omega,

(iii)

\partial\Omega
satisfies the positive mean curvature condition (Definition
2.11
).

Then for all
z\in\partial\Omega
and
R>0
such that
X\setminus B(z,4R)\neq\varnothing
, there exists
0<r_{0}<R
such that
E_{B(z,r_{0})}\subset B(z,R),
where
E_{B(z,r_{0})}
is the regularized minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}.

Proof.

Let
z\in\partial\Omega
and
R>0
such that
X\setminus B(z,4R)
is nonempty. Towards a contradiction, suppose that
E_{B(z,r)}\setminus B(z,R)\neq\varnothing
for all
0<r<R
, where
E_{B(z,r)}
is the regularized minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r)}.
By
[
28
, Lemma 6.2]
, for each
0<r<R,
there exists
\rho\in[r/2,r]
such that
B(z,\rho)
is of finite perimeter in
X
and

P(B(z,\rho),X)\simeq\frac{\mu(B(z,\rho)}{\rho}.

Therefore, we can choose a sequence
0<r_{k}<R/4
such that
r_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty,
where
P(B(z,r_{k}),X)<\infty
,
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap\partial B(z,r_{k}))=0,
and

P(B(z,r_{k}),X)\simeq\frac{\mu(B(z,r_{k}))}{r_{k}}.

By lower codimension 1 Ahlfors regularity, we have that

P(B(z,r_{k}),X)\leq C\mathcal{H}(B(z,r_{k})\cap\partial\Omega),

and since
\mathcal{H}(\{z\})=0,
it follows that

\lim_{k\to\infty}P(B(z,r_{k}),X)=0.

Letting
f_{k}:=\chi_{B(z,r_{k})},
we have that
f_{k}\to 0
in
BV(X).
Letting
E_{k}:=E_{B(z,r_{k})}
be the regularized minimal weak solution set to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f_{k}
, it follows from
[
22
, Proposition 3.3]
that there exists a set
E\subset X
of finite perimeter such that
\chi_{E_{k}}\to\chi_{E}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
by passing to a subsequence if necessary. In fact, the sequence converges pointwise monotonically a.e. as well (see
[
37
, Lemma 3.8]
). Moreover,
\chi_{E}
is a weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with zero boundary values. Hence we have that
P(E,X)=0,
and from the relative isoperimetric inequality it follows that
\mu(E)=0.
Thus, we have that

(2.18)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu(E_{k})=0.

For each
y\in\partial\Omega\setminus B(z,R/2),
there exists
r>0
such that
P(B(y,r),X)<\infty
and
B(y,r)\cap B(z,r_{k})=\varnothing
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}.
This is possible since
r_{k}<R/4
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Noting that
1-\chi_{E_{k}}
is a weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{X\setminus B(z,r_{k})},
it follows from
[
37
, Lemma 3.3]
that
\mu(E_{B(y,r)}\cap E_{k})=0,
where
E_{B(y,r)}
is the minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(y,r)}.
By positive mean curvature, there exists
r_{y}>0
such that
B(y,r_{y})\sqsubset E_{B(y,r)},
hence
\mu(B(y,r_{y})\cap E_{k})=0.
Thus,
B(y,r_{y})
contains no interior points of
E_{k}.
If there exists
\zeta\in\partial E_{k}\cap B(y,r_{y}),
then since
E_{k}
is a regularized weak solution and thus satisfies the uniform measure density condition, there exists
\tau>0
such that
B(\zeta,2\tau)\subset\Omega
and

0<\mu(B(\zeta,\tau)\cap E_{k})\leq\mu(B(y,r_{y})\cap E_{k}),

a contradiction. Therefore, we have that
E_{k}\cap B(y,r_{y})=\varnothing.

By compactness, we can cover
\partial\Omega\setminus B(z,R/2)
with a finite collection of balls
\{B(y_{i},r_{y_{i}})\}_{i}
such that
B(y_{i},r_{y_{i}})\cap E_{k}=\varnothing
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Since this covering is independent of
k
, it follows from a Lebesgue number argument that

\inf_{k}\dist(E_{k},\partial\Omega\setminus B(z,R/2))>0.

By our original supposition, there exists
x_{k}\in E_{k}\setminus B(z,R)
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
\dist(x_{k},\partial\Omega\cap B(z,R/2))\geq R/2,
and so it follows that

\inf_{k}\dist(x_{k},\partial\Omega)>0.

Thus the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k}
is compactly contained in
\Omega,
and so there exists
x_{0}\in\Omega
and a subsequence, also denoted
\{x_{k}\}_{k}
, such that
x_{k}\to x_{0}
as
k\to\infty.

Since
X
is complete and
\mu
is doubling and supports a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality, it follows that
X
is
A
-quasiconvex with
A\geq 1
depending only on the doubling and Poincaré constants, see
[
25
]
for example. Choose
r_{0}>0
sufficiently small so that
B(x_{0},40Ar_{0})\subset\Omega.
Choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that
x_{k}\in B(x_{0},Ar_{0})
and

(2.19)

\mu(E_{k})<\frac{\mu(B(x_{0},r_{0}))}{C_{D}(3C_{P}A)^{Q}},

where
C_{D}
and
Q
are from (
2.1
), and
C_{P}
is the constant from Theorem
2.16
. This is possible by (
2.18
). By the doubling property, we have that for sufficiently large
k,

\mu(B(x_{k},r_{0}))\geq\frac{\mu(B(x_{0},Ar_{0}))}{C_{D}A^{Q}}\geq\frac{\mu(B(x_{0},r_{0}))}{C_{D}(3C_{P}A)^{Q}},

and so by (
2.19
), there exists
y\in B(x_{k},r_{0})\setminus E_{k}.
Recall that
C_{P}\geq 1.

By quasiconvexity of
X,
there exists a curve
\gamma
joining
x_{k}
to
y
such that

\ell(\gamma)\leq Ad(x_{k},y)\leq Ar_{0},

and so there exists
y_{k}\in\partial E_{k}\cap B(x_{k},Ar_{0}).
By our choices we have that
B(y_{k},20Ar_{0})\subset\Omega,
and so by
[
27
, Theorem 5.2]
, there exist points
x,x^{\prime}\in B(y_{k},Ar_{0})
such that

B(x,Ar_{0}/C_{P})\subset E_{k}\cap\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad B(x^{\prime},Ar_{0}/C_{P})\subset\Omega\setminus E_{k}.

Note that
x\in B(x_{0},3Ar_{0}),
and so from the doubling property and the fact that
A\geq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu(E_{k})\geq\mu(B(x,Ar_{0}/C_{P}))\geq\frac{\mu(B(x_{0},r_{0}))}{C_{D}(3C_{P})^{Q}}\geq\frac{\mu(B(x_{0},r_{0}))}{C_{D}(3C_{P}A)^{Q}}.

This contradicts (
2.19
), completing the proof.
∎

We note that the conclusion of Lemma
2.17
may fail if one removes the assumption that all singletons on the boundary are
\mathcal{H}
-negligible, as shown by the following example:

Example 2.20
.

Equip
\mathbb{R}
with the measure
\mu(x)=\omega(x)d\mathcal{L}(x)
where

\omega(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}|x|+\frac{1}{2},&-1\leq x\leq 1\\
1,&1<|x|,\end{cases}

and consider
\Omega=(-1,1)\subset\mathbb{R}.
Then
\mathcal{H}(\{-1\})=\mathcal{H}(\{1\})=1,
and
\mathcal{H}
is lower codimension 1 Ahlfors regular on
\partial\Omega.
Moreover, if we consider the ball centered at one of the boundary points, say
B(1,r)=(1-r,1+r),
for any
0<r\leq 2,
we see that the minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(1,r)}
is the interval
(0,1+r).
This is due to the fact that the weight
\omega
attains a minimum at the origin, and so sets agreeing with
B(1,r)
outside of
\Omega
have smallest perimeter when they are intervals with a left endpoint at the origin. Likewise, for
0<r\leq 2,
the minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(-1,r)}
is the interval
(-1-r,0).
Hence,
\partial\Omega
is of positive mean curvature in the sense of Definition
2.11
, but the conclusion of Lemma
2.17
fails.

3.
Preliminary results for open set obstacles and data

In this section, we assume that
\Omega\subset X
is a bounded domain such that
\mu(X\setminus\Omega)>0
,
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega)<\infty
,
\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is doubling, and that
\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is lower codimension 1 Ahlfors regular as in (
2.3
). Furthermore, we assume that
\mathcal{H}(\{z\})=0
for all
z\in\partial\Omega.

In order to construct strong solutions to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem for continuous
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f
and
g
, we first construct strong solutions for the problem when obstacles and data consist of superlevel sets of the original obstacle and boundary functions. To do this, we first show existence of certain weak solutions. However, since we consider a problem with a double boundary condition as opposed to a fixed single boundary condition, it becomes difficult to prove existence using a definition of weak solutions similar to Definition
2.7
. For this reason, we begin by proving existence of weak
\varepsilon
-solutions, as in Definition
2.12
.

In this section we prove results for obstacle sets
A,B
and boundary sets
F,G
which have very specific properties (see the hypotheses of Proposition
3.3
, for example). As we shall see in Section 4, by assuming the hypothesis that
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi,f,g}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing
for
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and
f,g\in C(\partial\Omega)
in Theorem
1.1
, it follows that almost all of the superlevel sets of the obstacle and boundary functions will have the properties used in this section.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A,B\subset\overline{\Omega}
and
F,G\subset X
be Borel measurable, and suppose that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)\cap BV_{\text{loc}}(X)\neq\varnothing
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. Furthermore, an
\varepsilon
-weak solution exists of the form
u=\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}
for some measurable set
E_{\varepsilon}\subset X.

We note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing
implies that
A\subset B
and
F\setminus\Omega\subset G\setminus\Omega.

Proof.

For each
\varepsilon>0,
let

\lambda_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{\|Du\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon}):u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)\}<\infty.

Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)\cap BV_{\text{loc}}(X)\neq\varnothing
, we have that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}<\infty.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N},
let
u_{k}^{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
be such that

\|Du_{k}^{\varepsilon}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\to\lambda_{\varepsilon}

as
k\to\infty.
Since truncation will not increase the BV energy, and since the obstacles and boundary conditions are characteristic functions of sets, without loss of generality, we may assume that
0\leq u_{k}^{\varepsilon}\leq 1.
Thus, we have that

\sup_{k}\|u_{k}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq\mu(\Omega_{\varepsilon})<\infty,

and so
\{u_{k}^{\varepsilon}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
BV(\Omega_{\varepsilon}).
Therefore by
[
42
, Theorem 3.7]
, there exists a subsequence (not relabeled) and a function
u_{\varepsilon}\in BV(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
such that
u_{k}^{\varepsilon}\to u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
and pointwise
\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}.
By lower semicontinuity of the BV-energy, we have that

\|Du_{\varepsilon}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|Du_{k}^{\varepsilon}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=\lambda_{\varepsilon}.

By modifying
u_{\varepsilon}
on a set of
\mu
-measure zero if necessary, hence not changing the BV-energy, we may assume that
\chi_{A}\leq u_{\varepsilon}\leq\chi_{B}
everywhere in
\Omega
and
\chi_{F}\leq u_{\varepsilon}\leq\chi_{G}
everywhere in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\Omega.
Extending
u_{\varepsilon}
outside
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
by
\chi_{F},
we have that
u_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
, and so it follows that
\|Du_{\varepsilon}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=\lambda_{\varepsilon}.
Thus,
u_{\varepsilon}
is an
\varepsilon
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. Moreover, by the coarea formula we have that

\|Du_{\varepsilon}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=\int_{0}^{1}P(\{u_{\varepsilon}>t\},\Omega_{\varepsilon})dt,

and so there exists
0<t<1
such that
P(\{u_{\varepsilon}>t\},\Omega_{\varepsilon})\leq\|Du_{\varepsilon}\|(\Omega_{\varepsilon}).
Therefore letting
E_{\varepsilon}:=\{u_{\varepsilon}>t\},
we have that
\chi_{A}\leq\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\leq\chi_{B}
in
\Omega
and
\chi_{F}\leq\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\leq\chi_{G}
in
X\setminus\Omega
since
u_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega).
Thus
\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega),
and so it follows that
u_{\varepsilon}=\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}
is an
\varepsilon
-weak solution as well.
∎

Since we wish to prove that strong solutions exist when obstacles and boundary data consist of open sets, we need to ensure that the traces behave as we wish. We will use the following lemma, which requires positive mean curvature of the boundary of the domain as in Definition
2.11
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with boundary of positive mean curvature. Let
A,B\subset\overline{\Omega}
be relatively open, let
F\subset G\subset X
be open, and suppose that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)\cap BV_{\text{loc}}(X)\neq\varnothing
. If
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F\cap B,
then there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
and for all
\varepsilon
-weak solution sets
E_{\varepsilon}
to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem, we have that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\setminus E_{\varepsilon})=0.

Suppose in addition that
\chi_{A}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega
and that
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}A)=0,
where
\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}A
is the boundary of
A
relative to
\overline{\Omega}.
Then for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G},
there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
and for all
\varepsilon
-weak solution sets
E_{\varepsilon}
to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem, we have that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap E_{\varepsilon})=0.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first assertion. Let
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F\cap B.
Then there exists
r>0
such that
B(z,r)\subset F\subset G
and
B(z,r)\cap\overline{\Omega}\subset B.
By Lemma
2.17
, there exists
0<r_{0}<r
such that
E_{0}=E_{B(z,r_{0})}\subset B(z,r)\subset F,
where
E_{0}
is the regularized minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}.
By the positive mean curvature condition, there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0}.

Now let
\varepsilon>0
and let
E_{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
-weak solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. By
[
42
, Proposition 4.7]
, we have that

P(E_{0}\cap E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})\leq P(E_{0},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega}).

If
P(E_{0}\cap E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})>P(E_{0},\overline{\Omega}),
then we have that
P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})<P(E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega}).
In this case,

\displaystyle P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle<P(E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega}).

Since
E_{0}\subset F
and
\chi_{E_{\varepsilon}}\in\widetilde{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega),
we have that
E_{0}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\subset F\setminus\overline{\Omega}\subset E_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.
Therefore
(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon})\setminus\overline{\Omega}=E_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega},
and so it follows that

\displaystyle P(E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle<P(E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega})=P(E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon}).

This is a contradiction, since
E_{\varepsilon}
is an
\varepsilon
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem and
\chi_{E_{0}\cup E_{\varepsilon}}\in\widetilde{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
by the choice of
r_{0}.
Thus, we have that
P(E_{0}\cap E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})\leq P(E_{0},\overline{\Omega}),
and so
E_{0}\cap E_{\varepsilon}
is a weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
B(z,r_{0}).
Since
E_{0}
is the minimal weak solution to the said Dirichlet problem, we have that
E_{0}\sqsubset(E_{0}\cap E_{\varepsilon}),
and so it follows that
E_{0}\sqsubset E_{\varepsilon}.
Since
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0},
we have that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\setminus E_{\varepsilon})=0.

To prove the second assertion, let
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G}
such that
\dist(z,A)>0.
Since
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}A)=0
and since
\chi_{A}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega,
this holds for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G}.
Let
r>0
be such that
B(z,r)\cap(\overline{G}\cup A)=\varnothing.
As above, via Lemma
2.17
, choose
r_{0}>0
sufficiently small so that
E_{0}\subset B(z,r),
where
E_{0}
is the regularized minimal weak solution set to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}.
By positive mean curvature, there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0}.

Now let
\varepsilon>0
and let
E_{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. Again using
[
42
, Proposition 4.7]
, we have that

P(E_{0}\setminus E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{\varepsilon}\setminus E_{0},\overline{\Omega})\leq P(E_{0},\overline{\Omega})+P(E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega}).

We use the same argument as above. If
P(E_{0}\setminus E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})>P(E_{0},\overline{\Omega}),
then we would have that
P(E_{\varepsilon}\setminus E_{0},\Omega_{\varepsilon})<P(E_{\varepsilon},\Omega_{\varepsilon}),
a contradiction. Therefore, it follows that
P(E_{0}\setminus E_{\varepsilon},\overline{\Omega})\leq P(E_{0},\overline{\Omega}),
and minimality of the weak solution
E_{0}
implies that
\mu(E_{0}\cap E_{\varepsilon})=0.
Therefore, since
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0},
it follows that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap E_{\varepsilon})=0.\qed

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section. Note that we now assume that
\Omega
is a uniform domain in order to accommodate extensions of functions in
BV(\Omega)
to
BV(X)
in the manner of
[
33
]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded uniform domain with boundary of positive mean curvature. Let
A,B\subset\overline{\Omega}
be relatively open, let
F\subset G\subset X
be open, and suppose that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)\cap BV_{\text{loc}}(X)\neq\varnothing
. In addition, suppose that

(i)

P(F,X),P(G,X)<\infty,

(ii)

\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap(\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}A\cup\partial F\cup\partial G))=0,

(iii)

\chi_{F}\leq\chi_{B}
and
\chi_{A}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.

Then there exists a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem.

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\chi_{E_{k}}
be a
1/k
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. The existence of such
1/k
-weak solutions is guaranteed by Lemma
3.1
. Then we have that

P(E_{k},\Omega_{1/k})=\lambda_{1/k}<\infty,

and by the definition of
1/k
-weak solutions, it follows that
\lambda_{1/(k+1)}\leq\lambda_{1/k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Therefore, it follows that

\sup_{k}P(E_{k},\Omega)\leq\sup_{k}P(E_{k},\Omega_{1/k})<\infty,

and since

\sup_{k}\|\chi_{E_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\mu(\Omega)<\infty,

we have that
\{\chi_{E_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
BV(\Omega).
Hence, by
[
42
, Theorem 3.7]
, there exists a subsequence
\{\chi_{E_{k_{j}}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and a measurable set
E\subset\Omega
such that
\chi_{E_{k_{j}}}\to\chi_{E}
in
L^{1}(\Omega).
Passing to a further subsequence if necessary (keeping the same notation), we also have pointwise convergence
\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
By lower semicontinuity of the BV energy, it follows that

(3.4)

P(E,\Omega)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}P(E_{k_{j}},\Omega)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{1/k_{j}}<\infty,

and so
E
is a set of finite perimeter in
\Omega.

Since each
\chi_{E_{k}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega),
it follows from the pointwise
\mu
-a.e. convergence that
\chi_{A}\leq\chi_{E}\leq\chi_{B}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
For
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F,
there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that
\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\setminus E_{k})=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
by Lemma
3.2
. Therefore, again by the pointwise
\mu
-a.e. convergence, we have that
\mu(B(x,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\setminus E)=0.
Hence
T\chi_{E}(z)=1=\chi_{F}(z).
Likewise, by Lemma
3.2
, we also have that
T\chi_{E}(z)=0=\chi_{G}(z)
for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G}.
For
z\in\partial\Omega\cap G\setminus F,
we necessarily have that
\chi_{F}(z)=0\leq T\chi_{E}(z)\leq 1=\chi_{G}(z).
Since
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap\partial G)=0
by hypothesis, we therefore have that
\chi_{F}\leq T\chi_{E}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega,
and so
\chi_{E}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega).

It remains to show that
\chi_{E}
is a minimizer in the sense of Definition
2.14
. To do this, let
v\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
and suppose that
P(E,\Omega)>\|Dv\|(\Omega).
Since truncation does not increase BV-energy, we may assume that
0\leq v\leq 1.
By modifying
v
on a set of
\mu
-measure zero if necessary, hence not changing the BV-energy, we may also assume that
\chi_{A}\leq v\leq\chi_{B}
everywhere in
\Omega.
Since
\Omega
is a uniform domain, it follows from
[
33
, Theorem 3.1]
that there exists an extension
Ev\in BV(X)
such that
Ev|_{\Omega}=v
and
\|DEv\|(\partial\Omega)=0,
and again by truncation we may assume that
0\leq Ev\leq 1.
Now, let
\tilde{v}:X\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

\tilde{v}=Ev\chi_{\Omega\cup(G\setminus F)}+\chi_{F\setminus\Omega}.

Thus we have that
\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega).
We note that
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega)<\infty
implies that
P(\Omega,X)<\infty.
Therefore by the Leibniz rule for BV functions (
2.5
), we have

\displaystyle\|D\tilde{v}\|(X)

\displaystyle\leq\|DEv\chi_{\Omega\cup(G\setminus F)}\|(X)+P(F\setminus\Omega,X)

\displaystyle\leq\|DEv\|(X)+P(\Omega,X)+P(F,X)+P(G,X)<\infty,

and so it follows that
\tilde{v}\in BV(X).

We claim that
\|D\tilde{v}\|(\partial\Omega)=0.
Indeed, we have that

\|DEv\|(\partial\Omega\cap(G\setminus\overline{F}))=0,

and so for all
\eta>0
, there exists an open set
U_{\eta}\supset\partial\Omega\cap G\setminus\overline{F}
such that

\|D\tilde{v}\|(U_{\eta}\cap(G\setminus\overline{F}))=\|DEv\|(U_{\eta}\cap(G\setminus\overline{F}))\leq\|DEv\|(U_{\eta})<\eta.

This follows since
\tilde{v}=Ev
on the open set
U_{\eta}\cap(G\setminus\overline{F})
. Thus we have that

\|D\tilde{v}\|(\partial\Omega\cap(G\setminus\overline{F}))=0.

Furthermore, since
T\tilde{v}=1

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega\cap F,
it follows that
\tilde{v}^{\wedge}=\tilde{v}^{\vee}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega\cap F.
Thus the jump set
S_{\tilde{v}}
of
\tilde{v}
satisfies
\mathcal{H}(S_{\tilde{v}}\cap\partial\Omega\cap F)=0,
and so
\|D\tilde{v}\|(\partial\Omega\cap F)=0.
This follows from the decomposition (
2.4
) of the Radon measure
\|D\tilde{v}\|(\cdot)
and the fact that
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega)<\infty.
Similarly, since
T\tilde{v}=0
on
\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G},
we have that
\mathcal{H}(S_{\tilde{v}}\cap\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G})=0,
and so
\|D\tilde{v}\|(\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G})=0.
Since
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap(\partial G\cup\partial F))=0,
it follows that
\|D\tilde{v}\|(\partial\Omega)=0.

Therefore we have that

\displaystyle P(E,\Omega)>\|Dv\|(\Omega)=\|D\tilde{v}\|(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\|D\tilde{v}\|(\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|D\tilde{v}\|(U):U\text{ open, }\overline{\Omega}\subset U\}.

Hence, there exists an open set
U\supset\overline{\Omega}
and
J\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
with
j>J,
we have that

\displaystyle P(E,\Omega)>\|D\tilde{v}\|(U)\geq\|D\tilde{v}\|(\Omega_{1/k_{j}})\geq P(E_{k_{j}},\Omega_{1/k_{j}})=\lambda_{1/k_{j}}.

The last inequality is due to the fact that
\tilde{v}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
and
\chi_{E_{k_{j}}}
is a
1/k_{j}
-weak solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. Since this holds for all sufficiently large
j,
we have that

P(E,\Omega)>\liminf_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{1/k_{j}},

which contradicts (
3.4
). Thus we have that
P(E,\Omega)\leq\|Dv\|(\Omega),
and so
u:=\chi_{E}
is a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In the previous proposition, it is possible to obtain the same result by replacing the uniform domain assumption with the assumption that
\Omega
is a BV extension domain where extensions do not accumulate BV-energy on the boundary. That is, if
Eu\in BV(X)
is the extension of
u\in BV(\Omega),
then
\|DEu\|(\partial\Omega)=0.
It was shown in
[
33
]
that uniform domains have this property, and so for simplicity we assume that
\Omega
is a uniform domain.

The following lemma is an analogue of Lemma
3.2
for strong solution sets.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
3.3
. Then for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F,
there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that for any strong solution set
E
to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem, we have that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\setminus E)=0.

Similarly, for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G},
there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that for any strong solution set
E
to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem, we have that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\cap E)=0.

Proof.

For
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F\cap B,
there exists
r>0
such that
B(z,r)\subset F
and
B(z,r)\cap\overline{\Omega}\subset B.
By Lemma
2.17
, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
E_{0}\subset B(z,r),
where
E_{0}
is the regularized minimal weak solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}.
By positive mean curvature, there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0}.

By
[
37
, Proposition 4.8]
,
E_{0}
is also the minimal strong solution set, and so we have that
T\chi_{E_{0}}=\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.
As
\chi_{E}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
, we have that
T\chi_{E}(y)=1=T\chi_{E_{0}}(y)
for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
y\in\partial\Omega\cap B(z,r_{0})
. Since
T\chi_{E_{0}}(y)=0
for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
y\in\partial\Omega\setminus B(z,r_{0}),
we see that
T\chi_{E\cap E_{0}}=\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.
Likewise by our choice of
r_{0},
we have that
\chi_{F}\leq T\chi_{E\cup E_{0}}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega,
and
\chi_{E\cup E_{0}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega).

By
[
42
, Proposition 4.7]
, we have that

P(E\cap E_{0},\Omega)+P(E\cup E_{0},\Omega)\leq P(E_{0},\Omega)+P(E,\Omega).

If
P(E\cap E_{0},\Omega)>P(E_{0},\Omega),
then
P(E\cup E_{0},\Omega)<P(E,\Omega),
which is a contradiction since
\chi_{E\cup E_{0}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega)
and
E
is a strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. Hence
P(E\cap E_{0},\Omega)\leq P(E_{0},\Omega),
and so
E\cap E_{0}
is a strong solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}.
By minimality of the strong solution set
E_{0},
we have that
E_{0}\cap\Omega\sqsubset E,
and since
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0},
it follows that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\setminus E)=0.

For
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G},
we proceed as we did in Lemma
3.2
, using positive mean curvature and Lemma
2.17
to choose
r_{0}>0
and
\rho_{z}>0
such that

B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0}\subset B(z,r)\subset X\setminus(\overline{G}\cup A).

Again,
E_{0}
is the regularized minimal weak (and thus strong) solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}.

By our choice of
r_{0},
we see that
T\chi_{E_{0}\setminus E}=\chi_{B(z,r_{0})}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e., and likewise that
\chi_{F}\leq T\chi_{E\setminus E_{0}}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.
We also have that
\chi_{E\setminus E_{0}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega).
As in Lemma
3.2
, we have that

P(E_{0}\setminus E,\Omega)+P(E\setminus E_{0},\Omega)\leq P(E_{0},\Omega)+P(E,\Omega),

and by the same argument as above, minimality of the strong solution
E_{0}
implies that
E_{0}\sqsubset E_{0}\setminus E.
Since
B(z,\rho_{z})\sqsubset E_{0},
it follows that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\cap E)=0.\qed

By a similar argument as in the previous lemma, we can show that both the intersection and union of strong solution sets are strong solution sets.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with boundary of positive mean curvature.
For
i\in\{1,2\},
let
A_{i},B_{i}\subset\overline{\Omega}
be relatively open, let
F_{i}\subset G_{i}\subset X
be open, and suppose that

(i)

P(F_{i},X),P(G_{i},X)<\infty,

(ii)

\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap(\partial_{\overline{\Omega}}A_{i}\cup\partial F_{i}\cup\partial G_{i}))=0,

(iii)

\chi_{F_{i}}\leq\chi_{B_{i}}
and
\chi_{A_{i}}\leq\chi_{G_{i}}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.

In addition, suppose that
A_{1}\sqsubset A_{2},

B_{1}\sqsubset B_{2},

F_{1}\sqsubset F_{2}
, and
G_{1}\sqsubset G_{2}.
If
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are strong solution sets to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}:=\mathcal{K}_{A_{1},B_{1},F_{1},G_{1}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}:=\mathcal{K}_{A_{2},B_{2},F_{2},G_{2}}
-obstacle problems respectively, then
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
and
E_{1}\cup E_{2}
are strong solution sets to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problems respectively.

Proof.

From
[
42
, Proposition 4.7]
, we have that

(3.8)

P(E_{1}\cap E_{2},\Omega)+P(E_{1}\cup E_{2},\Omega)\leq P(E_{1},\Omega)+P(E_{2},\Omega)<\infty,

and so
\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}},\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}\in BV(\Omega).
Since
\chi_{A_{1}}\leq\chi_{E_{1}}
and
\chi_{A_{1}}\leq\chi_{A_{2}}\leq\chi_{E_{2}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega,
we have that
\chi_{A_{1}}\leq\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Likewise we have that
\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}\leq\chi_{E_{1}}\leq\chi_{B_{1}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega,
and so

\chi_{A_{1}}\leq\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}\leq\chi_{B_{1}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Similarly,
\chi_{B_{2}}\leq\chi_{E_{2}}\leq\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega,
and since
\chi_{E_{1}}\leq\chi_{B_{1}}\leq\chi_{B_{2}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega
, it follows that

\chi_{A_{2}}\leq\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}\leq\chi_{B_{2}}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
For
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F_{1},
it follows from Lemma
3.6
that there exists
\rho_{z,1}>0
such that
B(z,\rho_{z,1})\cap\Omega\sqsubset E_{1}.
Likewise, for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F_{2},
there exists
\rho_{z,2}>0
such that
B(z,\rho_{z,2})\cap\Omega\sqsubset E_{2}.
Since
F_{1}\sqsubset F_{2},
for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F_{1}
there exists
\rho_{z}=\min\{\rho_{z,1},\rho_{z,2}\}>0
such that

B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\sqsubset E_{1}\cap E_{2}.

Thus
T_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}(z)=1=\chi_{F_{1}}(z)
for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap F_{1}.
Similarly, it follows from Lemma
3.6
that for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{G}_{1},
there exists
\rho_{z}>0
such that

\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\cap E_{1}\cap E_{2})=\mu(B(z,\rho_{z})\cap\Omega\cap E_{1})=0.

Thus
T\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}(z)=0=\chi_{G_{1}}(z)
for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega\cap\overline{G}_{1}.
Since
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega\cap\partial G_{1})=0
, it follows that

\chi_{F_{1}}\leq T\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}\leq\chi_{G_{1}}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega,
and so
\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}.
Using Lemma
3.6
in a similar fashion, we have that

\chi_{F_{2}}\leq T\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}\leq\chi_{G_{2}}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.
Thus it follows that
\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}.

Now if
P(E_{1}\cap E_{2},\Omega)>P(E_{1},\Omega),
then
P(E_{1}\cup E_{2},\Omega)<P(E_{2},\Omega)
by (
3.8
) . However this is a contradiction since
\chi_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}
and
E_{2}
is a strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem. Therefore
P(E_{1}\cap E_{2},\Omega)\leq P(E_{1},\Omega),
and since
\chi_{E_{1}\cap E_{2}}\in\mathcal{K}_{1},
we have that
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
is a strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}
-obstacle problem. By a similar argument,
E_{1}\cup E_{2}
is a strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem.
∎

We conclude this section by showing that minimal strong solution sets exist. This is an adaptation of
[
37
, Proposition 3.7]
which gave a similar result for weak solutions to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
\chi_{F}
for
F\subset X
open.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
3.3
. Then a unique minimal strong solution set
E
exists to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{E}:=\{E\subset\Omega:\chi_{E}\text{ is a strong solution to the $\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}$-obstacle problem}\},

and let
\alpha:=\inf_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\mu(E).
Since
\mathcal{E}\neq\varnothing
by Proposition
3.3
, we have that
\alpha<\infty.

Let
\{E_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{E}
be such that
\mu(E_{k})\to\alpha
as
k\to\infty.
Let
\widetilde{E}_{1}=E_{1}
and for
k\in\mathbb{N},
let

\widetilde{E}_{k+1}=\widetilde{E}_{k}\cap E_{k+1}.

By Lemma
3.7
, we have that
\widetilde{E}_{k}\in\mathcal{E}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}.

Letting
E=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\widetilde{E}_{k},
it then follows that

\mu(\widetilde{E}_{k})\to\alpha=\mu(E)

as
k\to\infty.
We note that
\chi_{A}\leq\chi_{E}\leq\chi_{B}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega
since the
\widetilde{E}_{k}
are strong solution sets, and likewise by Lemma
3.6
it follows that
\chi_{F}\leq T\chi_{E}\leq\chi_{G}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.

Since
\chi_{\widetilde{E}_{k}}\to\chi_{E}
in
L^{1}(\Omega),
it follows from lower semicontinuity of the BV energy that

P(E,\Omega)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}P(\widetilde{E}_{k},\Omega)<\infty.

Hence
\chi_{E}\in BV(\Omega)
and so
\chi_{E}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}(\Omega).
Since the
\widetilde{E}_{k}
are strong solution sets, it follows that
E
is a strong solution set.

Let
E^{\prime}
be another strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. By Lemma
3.7
,
E\cap E^{\prime}
is a strong solution set, and so it follows that

\alpha\leq\mu(E\cap E^{\prime})\leq\mu(E)=\alpha.

Hence
\mu(E\setminus E^{\prime})=0,
and so
E
is the minimal strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{A,B,F,G}
-obstacle problem. Uniqueness
\mu
-a.e. follows from minimality.
∎

In this section, we assume that

Let

Then for

-a.e.

∎

We now prove Theorem

.

From these facts and continuity of the obstacle and boundary functions, it follows that for each

\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{L}
-a.e.

Moreover from continuity of

. Modifying

-obstacle problem yield strong solutions to the problem where the obstacles and boundary data are superlevel sets of the original functions:

.

Let

and let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
. Furthermore, suppose that
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing.

-obstacle problem for

-a.e.

\mu(V)=0.

Let
x\in\Omega\setminus{(U\cup V)}
, and suppose that
u^{\prime}(x)<u(x).
By the definition of
u
, there exists
t\in I
such that
u^{\prime}(x)<t\leq u(x)
and
x\in E_{t}.
By the density of
I^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}
, there exists
t^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
such that

u^{\prime}(x)<t^{\prime}<t\leq u(x).

Since
x\not\in V,
we have that
x\in E_{t^{\prime}}\setminus\{u^{\prime}>t^{\prime}\}.
This is a contradiction, as
x\not\in U,
and so we have that
u(x)\leq u^{\prime}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega\setminus(U\cup V).
Thus
u
is the minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem. Uniqueness
\mu
-a.e. follows from the minimality.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

For
f,g,\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
as above, we note that if
v\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}(\Omega),
then
-v\in\mathcal{K}_{-\psi_{2},-\psi_{1},-g,-f}(\Omega).
Thus there exists a unique minimal strong solution
w
to the
\mathcal{K}_{-\psi_{2},-\psi_{1},-g,-f}
-obstacle problem. It then follows that
-w
is the unique maximal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,g}
-obstacle problem.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Existence of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
follows from Proposition
4.4
. From the hypotheses, we have that
\psi_{1}\leq\psi_{2}\leq u_{2}
,
\psi_{1}\leq u_{1},
and
\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\leq u_{1}\leq\varphi_{1}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Thus we see that
\psi_{1}\leq\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\leq\varphi_{1}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Similarly
\psi_{2}\leq u_{2}\leq\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
,
u_{1}\leq\varphi_{1}\leq\varphi_{2}
, and
u_{2}\leq\varphi_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega,
and so it follows that
\psi_{2}\leq\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\leq\varphi_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.

Let
z\in\partial\Omega
such that
f_{1}(z)\leq f_{2}(z),

g_{1}(z)\leq g_{2}(z)
,
f_{1}(z)\leq Tu_{1}(z)\leq g_{1}(z)
, and
f_{2}(z)\leq Tu_{2}(z)\leq g_{2}(z).
These conditions hold for
\mathcal{H}
-a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega.
For such
z
, it follows that

\displaystyle T\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}(z)

\displaystyle=\min\{Tu_{1}(z),Tu_{2}(z)\}

\displaystyle T\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}(z)

\displaystyle=\max\{Tu_{1}(z),Tu_{2}(z)\},

(see the proof of

, Lemma 4.4]
, for example). Thus,

f_{1}\leq T\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\leq g_{1}\text{ and }f_{2}\leq T\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\leq g_{2}

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega.
By
[
36
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have that

\displaystyle\|D\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\|(\Omega)+\|D\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\|(\Omega)\leq\|Du_{1}\|(\Omega)+\|Du_{2}\|(\Omega)<\infty,

and so it follows that
\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\Omega)
and
\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(\Omega).

If
\|D\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\|(\Omega)>\|Du_{1}\|(\Omega),
then

This is a contradiction, since
u_{2}
is a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem and
\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}(\Omega),
and so it follows that

\|D\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\|(\Omega)\leq\|Du_{1}\|(\Omega).

In particular,
\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
is a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}
-obstacle problem. A similar argument shows that
\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
is a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem. Since
u_{1}
is a minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{1}
-obstacle problem, it follows that

u_{1}\leq\min\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\leq u_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.

An analogous argument shows that
\max\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is a strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{2}
-obstacle problem, and so maximality of

implies that

v_{1}\leq\max\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\leq v_{2}

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.

∎

5.
Stability with respect to single boundary condition

In this section we compare the obstacle problem with double boundary condition to the standard problem when only one boundary condition is imposed. We begin by providing straightforward examples, in the special case without obstacles, that for continuous boundary data
f\leq g
, minimal and maximal strong solutions to the
\mathcal{K}_{f,g}
-problem are not in general strong solutions to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
or
g
. Here we set

\mathcal{K}_{f,g}(\Omega):=\{u\in BV(\Omega):f\leq Tu\leq g\;\mathcal{H}\text{-a.e.\ on }\partial\Omega\}.

Example 5.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset X
be a bounded domain. If
f,g\in C(\partial\Omega)
are both not constant such that

\sup_{\partial\Omega}f<\inf_{\partial\Omega}g,

then
u\equiv\sup_{\partial\Omega}f
is the minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{f,g}
-problem. Likewise,
v\equiv\inf_{\partial\Omega}g
is the maximal strong solution. However, neither
u
nor
v
are strong solutions to the Dirichlet problems with boundary data
f
or
g
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},
and consider the continuous functions
f,g:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
given by

f(x,y)=y\qquad\text{and}\qquad g(x,y)=y+1.

From Proposition
4.4
, the minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{f,g}
-problem is given by
u(x)=\sup\{t\in I:x\in E_{t}\}
where
E_{t}
is the minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{F_{t},G_{t}}
-problem. As in the previous section, we let
F_{t}:=\{f>t\}
and
G_{t}:=\{g>t\}.

Since
\Omega
is the unit disk, it follows that for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the minimal strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{F_{t},G_{t}}
-obstacle problem is a subset of
\Omega
which has boundary in
\Omega
consisting of straight line segments. Denoting
E_{F_{t}}
and
E_{G_{t}}
the minimal strong solutions to the Dirichlet problems with boundary data
\chi_{F_{t}}
and
\chi_{G_{t}}
respectively, we see that
E_{F_{t}}
is the region of the disk bounded by the arc
F_{t}
and the chord joining the endpoints of
F_{t},
and
E_{G_{t}}
is the analogous region (see Figure
2
). The minimal strong solution
E_{t}
to the
\mathcal{K}_{F_{t},G_{t}}
-obstacle problem can be chosen from the sets
E\subset\Omega
for which
E_{F_{t}}\subset E\subset E_{G_{t}}
and such that
\partial E\cap\Omega
is a straight line segment lying in
E_{G_{t}}\setminus E_{F_{t}}.

For
t<1/2,
the set described above with the smallest perimeter, that is, whose boundary in
\Omega
is comprised by the shortest line segment is the set
E_{G_{t}}.
Likewise for
1/2\leq t
,
E_{F_{t}}
is the minimal strong solution set. Hence, we have that

E_{t}=\begin{cases}E_{G_{t}},&t<1/2\\
E_{F_{t}},&1/2\leq t.\end{cases}

Note that for
t<0,

G_{t}=\partial\Omega
and so
E_{G_{t}}=\Omega.
Likewise for
t>1,

F_{t}=\varnothing,
and so
E_{F_{t}}=\varnothing.
From the definition of
u
, it follows that
0\leq u\leq 1,
and

u(x,y)=\begin{cases}y+1,&-1<y<-1/2\\
1/2,&-1/2\leq y\leq 1/2\\
y,&1/2<y.\end{cases}

Hence,

Tu(x,y)=\begin{cases}g(x,y),&-1<y<-1/2\\
1/2,&-1/2\leq y\leq 1/2\\
f(x,y),&1/2<y,\end{cases}

and so
u
is not a strong solution to the Dirichlet problem with either boundary data
f
or
g,
see Figure
2
.

Remark 5.3
.

For
u,

f
, and
g
as in Example
5.2
, it follows that
u
is the strong minimal solution to both the
\mathcal{K}_{Tu,g}
and
\mathcal{K}_{f,Tu}
-problems, and
u
is the minimal strong solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
Tu.
Thus, this example shows that all three scenarios are possible: for continuous boundary data
f
and
g
with
f\leq g
, the minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{f,g}
-problem may be the minimal strong solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
or
g.

In Example
5.2
, the solution
u
fails to be a solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
or
g
in part because the ranges of
f
and
g
overlap and the range of
f
is not contained in the range of
g
. In the metric setting, this proves to be a sufficient condition for the distinction of solutions to the
\mathcal{K}_{f,g}
-problem and Dirichlet problems, as shown in the following proposition.

For the remainder of this section, assume that
\Omega
is a bounded uniform domain with boundary of positive mean curvature such that
\mathcal{H}(\partial\Omega)<\infty
,
\mathcal{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
is doubling, and that (
2.3
) holds. Furthermore, assume that
\mathcal{H}(\{z\})=0
for all
z\in\partial\Omega.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
f,g\in C(\partial\Omega)
be such that
f\leq g

\mathcal{H}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega,
and let
u\in\mathcal{K}_{f,g}(\Omega)
be the minimal strong solution to the
\mathcal{K}_{f,g}
-obstacle problem. If

\inf_{\partial\Omega}f<\inf_{\partial\Omega}g\leq\sup_{\partial\Omega}f<\sup_{\partial\Omega}g,

then
\inf_{\partial\Omega}g\leq u\leq\sup_{\partial\Omega}f

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
In particular,
u
is not a strong solution to the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
or
g
.

Proof.

By uniqueness
\mu
-a.e. of the minimal strong solution
u
, we may assume that
u
has the form given in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. That is,

u(x)=\sup\{t\in I:x\in E_{t}\},

where

I\subset J
is a countable dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
, and
E_{t}
is the minimal strong solution set to the
\mathcal{K}_{F_{t},G_{t}}
-obstacle problem. As in the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
F_{t}=\{\Ext f>t\}
and
G_{t}=\{\Ext g>t\}
, where
\Ext f,\Ext g\in C(X)\cap BV(X)
are the extensions given in
[
37
, Section 5]
.

For each
t\in I
with
\inf_{\partial\Omega}f<t<\inf_{\partial\Omega}g,
we have that
G_{t}=\partial\Omega
and
\varnothing\neq F_{t}\subsetneq\partial\Omega.
Thus
\mu(E_{t})>0
and since
\chi_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{K}_{F_{t},G_{t}},
it follows that
P(E_{t},\Omega)=0.
We then have that
E_{t}=\Omega
. Indeed,
E_{t}
must have full measure in
\Omega,
or otherwise by connectedness of
\Omega,
there would exist some ball
B\subset\Omega
such that
\min\{\mu(B\cap E_{t}),\mu(B\setminus E_{t})\}>0.
However, by the relative isoperimetric inequality and the fact that
P(E_{t},\Omega)=0,
this is a contradiction, and so
E_{t}=\Omega.
Since this holds for all
t\in I,
it follows from the definition of
u
that
\inf_{\partial\Omega}g\leq u

\mu
-a.e. in
\Omega.
Likewise for all
t\in I
such that
\sup_{\partial\Omega}f<t,
we have that
F_{t}=\varnothing,
and so
E_{t}=\varnothing.
Thus
u\leq t,
and since this holds for all such
t
, it follows that
u\leq\sup_{\partial\Omega}f.

Since
f
and
g
are continuous, and the outer inequalities in the statement of the proposition are strict, there are subsets of
\partial\Omega
with
\mathcal{H}
-positive measure where
Tu\neq f
and where
Tu\neq g.
Thus
u
cannot be a strong solution to either the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
f
or
g
.
∎

By replacing the function
g

5.2

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and for all

\partial\Omega

\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},g_{j},f}(\Omega),

\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},f,h_{k}}(\Omega),

\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},g_{j},h_{k}}(\Omega),
for all

(a)

(c)

Proof.
