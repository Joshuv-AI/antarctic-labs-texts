Symbolic Defect of Monomial Ideals
Benjamin Oltsik

Abstract
. Given a monomial ideal I I , we study two functions that quantify ways to measure the difference between symbolic powers and usual powers of I I . In many cases we determine the asymptotic growth rate of these two functions. We also perform explicit computations by using the symbolic polyhedron.

Primary: 13A02, 13E05, 13H15

1.
Introduction

Symbolic powers of ideals
I
(Definition
2.1
), denoted
I^{(n)}
, in a Noetherian commutative ring
R
have long been a topic of interest in commutative algebra
[
3
]
. They are directly related to the study of primary decomposition of ideals. In algebraic geometry, a special case of symbolic powers appear as ideals of functions which vanish on a given variety to a certain order.
The definition that we work with is (see Remark
2.2
):

I^{(n)}\coloneqq\bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(R/I)}I^{n}R_{\mathfrak{p}}\cap R.

It is natural to study the minimal set of generators for symbolic powers, but calculating such minimal generating sets is often difficult. Even counting the minimal number of generators
\mu(I^{(n)})
has proven challenging. Dao-Montaño
[
4
]
, study this question asymptotically via the
symbolic analytic spread
, which is essentially the growth rate of the function
n\mapsto\mu(I^{(n)})
. In this paper, our first object of focus is the
symbolic defect
function, originally defined in
[
7
]
:

\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)\coloneqq\mu\left(\left.\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$I^{(n)}$}\right/\hskip-2.0pt\raise-3.0pt\hbox{$I^{n}$}\right).

We aim to study the asymptotic growth rate of symbolic defect for monomial ideals.
We will use the following asymptotic notation: for functions
f
and
g
, we say

•

f(n)=O(g(n))
if
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}<\infty
,

•

f(n)\sim g(n)
if
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=1
,

•

f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
if
0<\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}<\infty
.

A result of Drabkin and Guerrieri
[
6
]
implies that the symbolic defect function is eventually quasi-polynomial for monomial ideals. In Section 3, we show,

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
without embedded primes. Then
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=O(n^{r-2})
.

One of the classical avenues for studying asymptotic questions is via convex polyhedra such as the the Newton and symbolic polyhedra
[
12
]
,
[
2
]
. This approach inspires the study of a new invariant, the
integral symbolic defect
(Definition
4.1
):

{\rm isdef}_{I}(n)\coloneqq\mu\left(\left.\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\overline{I^{(n)}}$}\right/\hskip-2.0pt\raise-3.0pt\hbox{$\overline{I^{n}}$}\right).

While there is no immediate comparison between
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
and
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
, we prove that their
O
-asymptotic growth is the same in a special case:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal in
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
such that
I^{(n)}=(I^{n})^{\operatorname{sat}}
for all
n\geq 1
(e.g., when
\dim(R/I)=1
). Then
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)=O(n^{r-2})
.

In Sections 5 and 6 we turn our attention to the problem of computing
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
, or at least computing its leading term and a quasi-period. We say
a
quasi-period because it may not be minimal, but it is a multiple of the minimal quasi-period. In Section 6 we do this for a special class of monomial ideals.

Example 1.3
.

Let
I=(x^{a},y)\cap(y^{b},z)\cap(z^{c},x)
. Then

(i)

\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)\sim(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)n
, where
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\left(\frac{a(bc-b+1)}{abc+1},\frac{b(ac-c+1)}{abc+1},\frac{c(ab-a+1)}{abc+1}\right)
.

(ii)

A quasi-period of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
is
abc+1
.

In Section 5, we use the symbolic polyhedron to calculate the exact symbolic defect function of
I=(xy,yz,xz)
. This strengthens the case
a=b=c=1
of Example
1.3
, as well as recovers the results of
[
14
]
:

Example 1.4
.

For
I=(xy,xz,yz)
,

\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{2}n-2&n\equiv 0\pmod{2}\\
\frac{3}{2}n-\frac{3}{2}&n\equiv 1\pmod{2}\end{cases}.

We end with a list of open questions in Section 7.

2.
Background

Definition 2.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative, unital, Noetherian ring, and let
I
be an ideal. We define the
n
-th
symbolic power
of
I
to be

I^{(n)}:=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(R/I)}I^{n}R_{\mathfrak{p}}\cap R.

Remark 2.2
.

The literature has a competing definition where the intersection is taken over just the minimal primes of
I
. In the case where
I
has no embedded primes, these definitions coincide, but they may differ in general. For example,
I=(x^{2},xy)
has
I^{(n)}=(x^{2},xy)^{n}
by our definition, but with the alternate definition, the symbolic powers would be
(x)^{n}
.

Another way to represent symbolic powers is via a saturation. Indeed, a result from McAdam
[
13
]
states the associated primes of
R/I^{n}
eventually stabilize for large enough
n
. Let
A^{*}(R/I)
be this stabilized set, called the
asymptotic primes
of
I
.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
9
, Lemma 2.2]
Let

J=\bigcap\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{p}\in A^{*}(R/I)\setminus\operatorname{Ass}(R/I)\\
\operatorname{depth}(\mathfrak{p},R/I)\geq 1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{p}.

Then,

I^{(n)}=(I^{n}:J^{\infty})=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}(I^{n}:J^{i}).

Remark 2.4
.

Note that if
J=\mathfrak{m}
, then
I^{(n)}=(I^{n})^{\text{sat}}
for all
n
.

To ordinary powers and symbolic powers, we define the two graded algebras:
\mathcal{R}(I)=\oplus_{n\geq 0}I^{n}t^{n}
, the Rees algebra, and
\mathcal{R}_{s}(I)=\oplus_{n\geq 0}I^{(n)}t^{n}
, the symbolic Rees algebra. While the former is always Noetherian, in general, the latter may not be (see Example 2.4 in
[
8
]
). Denote
\ell(I)=\dim(\mathcal{R}(I)\otimes R/\mathfrak{m})
, and
s\ell(I)=\dim(\mathcal{R}_{s}(I)\otimes R/\mathfrak{m})
, called the
analytic spread of
I
and
symbolic analytic spread of
I
, respectively.

For any
R
-module
M
, denote by
\mu(M)
the minimal number of generators of
M
. For ideals
I
, the growth rate of
\mu(I^{n})
and
\mu(I^{(n)})
have been studied. Indeed, from Nakayama’s lemma, we have that
\ell(I)=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\mu(I^{n})=O(n^{t-1})\}
, and, for when
\mathcal{R}_{s}(I)
is Noetherian,
s\ell(I)=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\mu(I^{n})=O(n^{t-1})\}

[
4
]
are well-known invariants, particularly
\ell(I)
. We wish to expand the study of these invariants in regards to the following function:

Definition 2.5

(Galetto-Geramita-Shin-Van Tuyl
[
7
]
)
.

Let
R
be either local or graded with unique homogeneous maximal ideal,
\mathfrak{m}
, and residue field
k
. The
symbolic defect function
of a homogeneous ideal
I
is the numerical function

\operatorname{sdef}_{I}:{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\quad\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n):=\mu\left(\left.\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${I^{(n)}}$}\right/\hskip-2.0pt\raise-3.0pt\hbox{${I^{n}}$}\right)=\dim_{k}\left(\frac{I^{(n)}}{I^{n}+\mathfrak{m}I^{(n)}}\right)<\infty.

This function is designed to be a measurement of “closeness” between
I^{(n)}
and
I^{n}
.

A result of Drabkin and Guerrieri
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
states that, for a homogeneous ideal
I
such that
\mathcal{R}_{s}(I)
is Noetherian,
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
is eventually quasi-polynomial. Note that this result is not dependent on field characteristic or cardinality. A useful fact about monomial ideals is, when we view them as homogeneous ideals in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
, that
\mathcal{R}_{s}(I)
is always Noetherian
[
11
, Theorem 3.2]
. This allows us to invoke Drabkin and Guerrieri’s theorem to conclude that
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
is eventually quasi-polynomial for any monomial ideal. In this paper, we wish to determine an upper-bound of the degree(s) of the quasi-polynomial in this paper, specifically for monomial ideals in polynomial rings.

For monomial ideals, there are many other results at our disposal regarding symbolic powers. For example, the following allows for much easier calculations:

Lemma 2.6
.

[
11
, Lemma 3.1]
Let
I
be a monomial ideal with monomial primary decomposition,
I=Q_{1}\cap\cdots\cap Q_{s}
. Set
\max(I)
to be the set of maximal associated primes, and, for each
\mathfrak{p}\in\max(I)
, let
Q_{\subseteq\mathfrak{p}}=\bigcap\limits_{\sqrt{Q_{i}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}}Q_{i}
. Then,

I^{(n)}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\max(I)}(Q_{\subseteq\mathfrak{p}})^{n}.

In particular, if
I
does not have embedded primes, then
I^{(n)}=Q_{1}^{n}\cap\cdots\cap Q_{s}^{n}
.

Example 2.7
.

If
I
is a monomial ideal in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
and if
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
is associated to
I
, then
I^{(n)}=I^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. In particular, if
r=2
then
I^{(n)}=I^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.

We now introduce a few tools to help work with monomial ideals.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]=k[\mathbf{x}]
, and let
I
be a monomial ideal. We define the
Newton polyhedron of
I
, denoted
\operatorname{NP}(I)
, as the convex hull of exponent vectors for monomials in
I
:

\operatorname{NP}(I)=\operatorname{cvxhull}\{\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}:{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{b}}\in I\},

where
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r})
and
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}=x_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots x_{r}^{b_{r}}
. In this paper, we will make no distinction between a monomial and its corresponding exponent vector in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

The Newton polyhedron allows the study of monomial ideals from the perspective of Euclidean geometry. An important property to note is that
\operatorname{NP}(I^{n})=n\operatorname{NP}(I)
for all
n\geq 1
. This allows
\operatorname{NP}(I)
to be quite useful when studying powers of monomial ideals. We can now define another polyhedron to study symbolic powers:

Definition 2.9
.

[
1
, Definition 3.2]
Let
I
and
Q_{i}
be as in Lemma
2.6
. Then the
symbolic polyhedron
of
I
, denoted
\operatorname{SP}(I)
, is

\operatorname{SP}(I)=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\max(I)}\operatorname{NP}(Q_{\subseteq\mathfrak{p}}).

In particular, if
I
has no embedded primes, then
\operatorname{SP}(I)=\operatorname{NP}(Q_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\operatorname{NP}(Q_{s})
.

Remark 2.10
.

For Newton and symbolic polyhedra of monomial ideals in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
, we use variables
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{r}
to describe coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. If
r=3
, we use coordinates
u,v,w
.

Example 2.11
.

Let
I=(xy,xz,yz)\subseteq k[x,y,z]
. This is one of the simplest examples of ideals with non-trivial symbolic powers; for example,
I^{2}=(x^{2}y^{2},x^{2}yz,xy^{2}z,xyz^{2},x^{2}z^{2},y^{2}z^{2})
, but
I^{(2)}=(x^{2}y^{2},x^{2}z^{2},y^{2}z^{2},xyz)
.
Thus,
\operatorname{NP}(I)\neq\operatorname{SP}(I)
. In particular,

\operatorname{NP}(I)=\begin{cases}u+v\geq 1\\
u+w\geq 1\\
v+w\geq 1\\
u+v+w\geq 2\\
u,v,w\geq 0,\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{SP}(I)=\begin{cases}u+v\geq 1\\
u+w\geq 1\\
v+w\geq 1\\
u,v,w\geq 0.\end{cases}

Figures
1
and
2
are graphs of these two bodies. In particular, note that
(1/2,1/2,1/2)
is in
\operatorname{SP}(I)
but not in
\operatorname{NP}(I)
, which corresponds to
xyz\in I^{(2)}\setminus I^{2}
. In Section 5, we study this ideal further.

Example 2.12
.

[
1
, Lemma 3.7]
. Let
I
be an ideal without embedded primes such that
I
is an intersection of pure powers. That is,
I=\bigcap\limits_{1\leq i\leq s}Q_{i}
, where
Q_{i}=(x_{1}^{a_{i1}},x_{2}^{a_{i2}},\ldots,x_{r}^{a_{ir}})
, where
a_{ij}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}\cup\{-\infty\}
, with the convention that
x_{i}^{-\infty}=0
, and
\sqrt{Q_{i}}
distinct for each
i
. Then,
\operatorname{SP}(I)
consists of the non-negative solutions of:

\begin{cases}\frac{1}{a_{11}}u_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{12}}u_{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{a_{1r}}u_{r}\geq 1,\\
\vdots\\
\frac{1}{a_{s1}}u_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{s2}}u_{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{a_{sr}}u_{r}\geq 1,\par\end{cases}

where
\frac{1}{-\infty}=0
.

We note that containment in either polyhedron
does not
imply containment in the ideal. That is, if
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{b}}\in I^{n}
, then
\mathbf{b}\in n\operatorname{NP}(I)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}
, but the converse may not be true. Similarly, if
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{b}}\in I^{(n)}
, then
\mathbf{b}\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}
, without the converse necessarily holding. However, we can extend
I
so that the converse always holds:

Definition 2.13
.

Let
I
be an ideal in a ring
R
. We say an element
r\in R
is
integral
over
I
if
r
satisfies a monic, polynomial equation of the form,

r^{n}+a_{1}r^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n}=0

where
a_{i}\in I^{i}
. The
integral closure
of
I
, denoted
\overline{I}
, is the set of all elements in
R
that are integral over
I
. If
I=\overline{I}
, we say
I
is
integrally closed
.

The integral closure of an ideal is, itself, an ideal. Furthermore, if
I
is a monomial ideal, then
\overline{I}
is also a monomial ideal
[
12
]
. In fact, we can do even better:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
I\subseteq k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]=k[\mathbf{x}]
be a monomial ideal. Then,

\overline{I}=(\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{b}}:\mathbf{x}^{a\mathbf{b}}\in I^{a}\text{ for some }a\geq 1).

A big advantage of working with integral closure is that
\operatorname{NP}(\overline{I})=\operatorname{NP}(I)
, and
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{b}}\in\overline{I^{n}}
if and only if
\mathbf{b}\in n\operatorname{NP}(I)
. We will see soon in Theorem
4.2
that a similar result holds for
\operatorname{SP}(I)
.

There is one more way to define
\operatorname{SP}(I)
, and that is via a limiting body:

Theorem 2.15
.

[
1
, Corollary 3.16]
For a monomial ideal
I
,

\operatorname{SP}(I)=\bigcup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)}).

For further reading on limiting bodies and their relation with analytic spread, see
[
10
]
.

3.
Growth of Symbolic Defect of Monomial Ideals

This brief section will determine an upper-bound for all monomial ideals without embedded primes. In a paper by Dao and Montaño
[
4
]
, they prove the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
Let
I
be a monomial ideal without embedded primes in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
. Then,

\operatorname{s\ell}(I)\leq r-\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{\operatorname{bigHeight}I}\right\rfloor,

where
\operatorname{bigHeight}I
denotes the maximal height of an associated prime of
I
.

Note that in
[
4
]
, they employ the alternate definition of symbolic powers, where the intersection described in Definition
2.1
is taken over the minimal primes of
I
. In the case without embedded primes, our definition and their definition coincide.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

If
\mathfrak{m}
is an associated prime, then by Example
2.7
,
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=0
. Otherwise, the bound in Theorem
3.1
is no more than
r-1
. Since
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)\leq\mu(I^{(n)})
, the conclusion follows.
∎

In the next section we prove a similar result for classes of ideals that have embedded primes.

4.
Integral Closure and Symbolic Defect

By the nature of Newton polyhedra, a monomial’s exponent vector is within the Newton polyhedron of a monomial ideal if and only if the monomial is in the integral closure of the ideal. With this in mind, it is natural to expand the study of symbolic polyhedra and consider the interactions with integral closure.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
I
be an ideal of a commutative ring
R
. We define the
integral symbolic defect of
I
as

\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n):=\mu\left(\left.\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\overline{I^{(n)}}$}\right/\hskip-2.0pt\raise-3.0pt\hbox{$\overline{I^{n}}$}\right)=\dim_{k}\left(\frac{\overline{I^{(n)}}}{\overline{I^{n}}+\mathfrak{m}\overline{I^{(n)}}}\right)

The following result will be essential in calculating
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]=k[\mathbf{x}]
. Then
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{u}}\in\overline{I^{(m)}}
if and only if
\mathbf{u}\in(m\operatorname{SP}(I))\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Assume
\mathbf{u}\in(m\operatorname{SP}(I))\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}
. Then by Theorem
2.15
,

\mathbf{u}\in\left(\bigcup_{n\to\infty}\frac{m}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)})\right)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}=\bigcup_{n\to\infty}\frac{m}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0},

so
\mathbf{u}\in\frac{m}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}
for some
n
. Note that, for any
t\geq 1
,

\frac{m}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)})=\frac{m}{nt}\operatorname{NP}((I^{(n)})^{t})\subseteq\frac{m}{nt}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(nt)}).

Thus, we may assume that
m\leq n
. It follows that

\frac{m}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)})\subseteq\frac{m}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(m)})\subseteq\operatorname{NP}(I^{(m)}).

So
\mathbf{u}\in\operatorname{NP}(I^{(m)})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}
, and we are done.

(\Rightarrow)
If
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{u}}\in\overline{I^{(m)}}
, then

\mathbf{u}\in\operatorname{NP}(I^{(m)})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}=m\left(\frac{1}{m}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(m)})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}\right)\subseteq m\left(\bigcup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{NP}(I^{(n)})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}_{\geq 0}\right).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

Geometrically, integral symbolic defect corresponds to the number of points in
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
that are minimal. That is, the number of points
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
\mathbf{u}\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
but
\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{e}_{i}\not\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
, for every standard unit vector,
\mathbf{e}_{i}
. This includes any points that are not in
I^{(n)}
but are in
n\operatorname{SP}(I)
.

Finding
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
amounts entirely to a geometric problem. Precisely, we desire to count the generators in the affine semigroup corresponding to the symbolic polyhedron that are not contained in the Newton polyhedron. As such, it is easier to calculate
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
over
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
. Directly comparing
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
and
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
, however, still proves to be challenging. This is a bit surprising, considering the result from Hà-Nguy
\tilde{\text{\^{e}}}
n
[
10
, Corollary 4.4]
, implicating
\mu(I^{n})=\Theta(\mu(\overline{I^{n}}))
and
\mu(I^{(n)})=\Theta(\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}}))
.

Example 4.4
.

If
I
is a monomial ideal, often one will find that
\mu(I)\leq\mu(\overline{I})
, but this is not always the case. An counterexample due to Michael DiPasquale is the ideal
I=(x^{3},x^{2}yz^{2},xy^{2}z^{2},x^{2}y^{2}z,y^{3})
, whose integral closure is
\overline{I}=(x^{3},x^{2}y,xy^{2},y^{3})
. This is also an example of a monomial ideal whose integral closure is primary, but the ideal itself is not, although the converse is known to be true.

Despite Hà-Nguy
\tilde{\text{\^{e}}}
n’s aforementioned result, it is unknown whether
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}
and
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}
grow similarly. Certainly, if
I
is normal and the symbolic powers are integrally closed, then
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
for all
n
. Furthermore, the following conditions are sufficient, albeit artificial:

Proposition 4.5
.

(1)

If
\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}})\not\sim\dim_{k}K_{n}-\mu(\overline{I^{n}})
, where
K_{n}=\mathfrak{m}\overline{I^{(n)}}\cap\overline{I^{n}}/\mathfrak{m}\overline{I^{n}}
, then
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=O(\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n))
.

(2)

If
\mu(I^{(n)}\cap\overline{I^{n}}/I^{n})=O(\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n))
, then
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=\Theta(\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n))
.

Proof.

We will assume that for
n\gg 0
,
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
and
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
are non-zero, for otherwise the results are immediate.

For (1), consider the short exact sequence,

0\to\overline{I^{n}}\to\overline{I^{(n)}}\to\overline{I^{(n)}}/\overline{I^{n}}\to 0.

By tensoring with
k
and applying Nakayama’s lemma, we get

\mu(\overline{I^{n}})+\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)=\dim_{k}{K_{n}}+\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}}),

and thus,

\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)=\dim_{k}{K_{n}}+\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}})-\mu(\overline{I^{n}})

Let
P(n)=\dim_{k}{K_{n}}-\mu(\overline{I^{n}})
. If we consider
F_{n}=\frac{\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)}{\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)}
, then

F_{n}\leq\frac{\mu(I^{(n)})}{\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}})+P(n)}.

Since
\mu(I^{(n)})=\Theta(\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}}))

[
10
, Corollary 4.4]
, we can consider the numerator of the latter fraction to be a polynomial of degree
n^{s\ell(I)-1}
, and the denominator to be
P(n)
plus a polynomial of degree
n^{s\ell(I)-1}
. If
\deg(P(n))>s\ell(I)-1
, then
F_{n}\to 0
. If
\deg(P(n))<s\ell(I)-1
,
F_{n}
is bounded above by a finite value. If
\deg(P(n))=s\ell(I)-1
but the leading coefficients are not the same, then
F_{n}
is still bounded above by a finite value. Thus, the only case where
F_{n}
is not bounded above with this inequality is if
P(n)\sim\mu(\overline{I^{(n)}})
.

For (2), consider the diagram:

Both of the above conditions are not easy to verify, and it is unknown what particular ideals satisfy the conditions. As such, a precise, verifiable relation between

Due to the geometric nature of
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
, we can more easily study the growth of
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
. First, we establish the following lemma:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
I,J\subset k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
be monomial ideals such that
I\subsetneq J
. We will say the number of monomials in
J/I
is finite if there are finitely many elements of
J/I
with a residue class represented by a monomial. Then the number of monomials in
M=J/I
is finite if and only if
\operatorname{Ass}(J/I)=\{\mathfrak{m}\}
. Furthermore, if
J/I
has finitely many monomials, so does
\overline{J}/\overline{I}
.

Proof.

Assume
\operatorname{Ass}(M)\neq\{\mathfrak{m}\}
. Since
J\neq I
,
\operatorname{Ass}(M)\neq\emptyset
, so there exists
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(M)
,
\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{m}
. Let
m
be a monomial such that
\operatorname{Ann}(m+I)=\mathfrak{p}
, and let
x_{i}\not\in\mathfrak{p}
. Then, for all
f\geq 1
,
x_{i}^{f}m\not\in I
. Thus, there are infinitely many monomials in
M
.

Conversely, assume
\operatorname{Ass}(M)=\{\mathfrak{m}\}
. Then, for every
x_{i}
, there exists
f_{i}
such that, for any
m\in J
,
x_{i}^{f_{i}}m\in I
. That is, there is an upper-bound for each exponent of a non-zero monomial in
M
, hence only finitely many monomials exist.

Now assume
J/I
has finitely many monomials so that
\operatorname{Ass}(J/I)=\{\mathfrak{m}\}
. If
\overline{J}=\overline{I}
, the module is trivial. Otherwise, letting
J=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s})
, this means that, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, there exists
b_{ij}
such that
x_{j}^{b_{ij}}n_{i}\in I
. Now, let
m\in\overline{J}
. Then, by Proposition
2.14
, for some
a\geq 1
,
m^{a}\in J^{a}
. Let
m^{a}=pn_{g_{1}}\cdots n_{g_{a}}
, where
p
is some monomial and each
g_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, perhaps not all distinct. Let
B_{j}=\sum{b_{g_{a}j}}
. Multiplying by
x_{j}^{B_{j}}
, we get

x_{j}^{B_{j}}m^{a}=p(x_{j}^{b_{g_{1}}j}n_{g_{1}}\cdots x_{j}^{b_{g_{a}}j}n_{g_{a}})\in I^{a}.

Further multiplying the left-hand side by
x_{j}^{(a-1)B_{j}}
yields
x_{j}^{aB_{j}}m^{a}=(x_{j}^{B_{j}}m)^{a}\in I^{a}
. Thus,
x_{j}^{B_{j}}m\in\overline{I}
. This holds for all
j\in\{1,\ldots r\}
, so every element of
\overline{J}/\overline{I}
is annihilated by a power of
\mathfrak{m}
, so the only associated prime is
\mathfrak{m}
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

The modules
(I^{n})^{\text{sat}}/I^{n}
and
\overline{(I^{n})^{\text{sat}}}/\overline{I^{n}}

Proof.

If

For all other cases, by the previous lemma, it suffices to show that the only associated prime of

is

.

We would like to thank the anonymous referee for the following improved proof of the result: suppose

∎

The above lemma is necessary so that we may use the theory of Ehrhart (quasi)-polynomials. Now, we may prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

First, suppose

denote the projection of

5.
Calculating Symbolic Defect with the Symbolic Polyhedron

We will exploit the symmetries of the ideal
I=(xy,xz,yz)=(x,y)\cap(x,z)\cap(y,z)
to provide an explicit formula for
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
. While this result can also be calculated with graph-theoretic techniques
[
6
]
, this displays an alternative method. Furthermore, this method seems to be special to this ideal, but could potentially have some expansion into higher dimensions.

It is first important to note that, since
\dim(R/I)=1
,
(I^{n})^{{\rm sat}}=I^{(n)}
for all
n
. Furthermore,
I
satisfies
I^{n}=\overline{I^{n}}
and
I^{(n)}=\overline{I^{(n)}}
for all
n\geq 1
(see Lemma
6.1
), so
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
. Thus, from Theorem
1.2
, we a priori know that
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=O(n)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
M=I^{(n)}/I^{n}
. Then
M=(x^{a}y^{b}z^{c}:\frac{3}{2}n\leq a+b+c<2n,\text{ and one of }a=b,b=c,\text{ or }a=c)
. In other words, it is generated by the points on the edges of
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
.

Proof.

By Example
2.11
,

n\operatorname{SP}(I)=\begin{cases}u+v\geq n\\
u+w\geq n\\
v+w\geq n\\
u,v,w\geq 0.\end{cases}

Note that the intersection of the first three planes is
(n/2,n/2,n/2)
. Thus, no element of
n\operatorname{SP}(I)
has all three entries less than
n/2
. In particular, an element
(a,b,c)\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)
satisfies
a+b+c\geq\frac{3}{2}n
. Since
n\operatorname{NP}(I)=n\operatorname{SP}(I)\cap\{u+v+w\geq 2n\}
,
x^{a}y^{b}z^{c}\in M
satisfies
\frac{3}{2}n\leq a+b+c<2n
.

Now, fix some
n\geq 1
, and let
x^{a}y^{b}z^{c}\in M
. We will show that there exists a “path” of unit vectors from an edge lattice point of
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
to
(a,b,c)
.

We use induction on
N=a+b+c
. If
n
is even, the base case is that
N=\frac{3}{2}n
, and
(a,b,c)=(n/2,n/2,n/2)
, which is an lattice point on the edge itself. If
n
is odd,
N=\frac{3}{2}n+\frac{1}{2}
, and so
(a,b,c)
is some permutation of
(\frac{1}{2}(n+1),\frac{1}{2}(n+1),\frac{1}{2}(n-1))
, all of which lie on the edges.

Now, assume the claim holds for
N
and suppose
(a,b,c)\in M
satisfy
a+b+c=N+1
. If
(a,b,c)
is on an edge, we are done. Otherwise, one of
(a-1,b,c)
,
(a,b-1,c)
, or
(a,b,c-1)
is in
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
, and thus has a path of coordinate unit vectors up to it. Simply add the appropriate coordinate vector in the appropriate direction.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The generation of
M
in the above proposition is a minimal generation. Indeed, any monomial corresponding to a point on an edge, of
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
,
m
, satisfies, by nature,
x^{-1}m,y^{-1}m,z^{-1}m\not\in I^{(n)}
. The purpose of Proposition
5.1
is to demonstrate that they, in fact, generate the entirety of
M
.

Proof of Example
1.4
.

By Proposition
5.1
, this is equivalent to counting lattice edge points on
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
. It is clear that for every increase of
N
with
\frac{3}{2}n\leq N<2n
, there are three more edge points. If
n
is even, we get only one point at
N=\frac{3}{2}n
. So, we get
3\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-2)\right)+1=\frac{3}{2}n-2
for
n

3\left(\frac{1}{2}(n-1)\right)=\frac{3}{2}n-\frac{3}{2}
for

∎

In this section, we use the symbolic polyhedron to determine symbolic defect for the family of ideals,

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. A monomial
x_{i}^{b_{i}}x_{j}^{b_{j}}\in(x_{i}^{a_{i}},x_{j}^{a_{j}})^{n}
if and only if
\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}\right\rfloor\geq n
.

Proof.

One can see that
(x_{i}^{a_{i}},x_{j}^{a_{j}})^{n}=(x_{i}^{na_{i}},x_{i}^{a_{i}(n-1)}x_{j}^{a_{j}},\ldots,x_{i}^{a_{i}}x_{j}^{a_{j}(n-1)},x_{j}^{na_{j}})
. Thus,
x_{i}^{b_{i}}x_{j}^{b_{j}}\in(x_{i}^{a_{i}},x_{j}^{a_{j}})^{n}
if and only if there exists
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
such that
b_{i}\geq a_{i}(n-t)
and
b_{j}\geq{a_{j}}t
. We will show the existence of such a
t
is equivalent to satisfying the inequality.

First, assume such a
t
exists. Then
n-\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}\leq t\leq\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}
. In particular,
t
is an integer between
n-\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}
and
\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}
. Thus,
\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}\rfloor-\lceil n-\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}\rceil\geq 0
. Since
-\lceil-u\rceil=\lfloor u\rfloor
for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
, this rearranges to
\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}\right\rfloor\geq n
.

Assume now that
b_{i}
and
b_{j}
satisfy the inequality. Equivalently, we have
\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}\rfloor-\lceil n-\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}\rceil\geq 0
. This means there is an integer
\ell
, in between
\frac{b_{j}}{a_{j}}
and
n-\frac{b_{i}}{a_{i}}
. If
\ell
can only be chosen to be negative, then
b_{i}\geq a_{i}(n-\ell)\geq a_{i}n
, but since
b_{j}\geq 0
by assumption,
\ell^{\prime}=0
also satisfies
b_{i}\geq a_{i}(n-\ell^{\prime})
,
b_{j}\geq a_{j}\ell^{\prime}
. Thus,
\ell
can be chosen to be non-negative. Similarly, if
\ell
can only be chosen to be greater than
n
, we have
a_{j}n\leq a_{j}\ell\leq b_{j}
. But then if
\ell^{\prime\prime}=n
, we get
b_{j}>a_{j}\ell^{\prime\prime}
and
b_{i}\geq a_{j}(n-\ell^{\prime\prime})
, so
\ell
maybe chosen to be, at most,
n
. So we have the existence of a
t
, i.e.
t=\ell
.
∎

6.4
, we have that
\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}}{a_{ij}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{ji}}\right\rfloor\geq n
for all
j\neq i
, but, there is a
j^{\prime}
such that
\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}-1}{a_{ij^{\prime}}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b_{j^{\prime}}}{a_{j^{\prime}i}}\right\rfloor<n
. It follows that
\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}-1}{a_{ij^{\prime}}}\right\rfloor<\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}}{a_{ij^{\prime}}}\right\rfloor
, which can only occur if
\frac{b_{i}}{a_{ij^{\prime}}}
is an integer.
∎

We now have the following:

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
I
be as in Corollary
6.4
. If
m={\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{b}}
is a minimal generator of
I^{(n)}
, then, for each
1\leq i\leq r
, there is some
j\neq i
such that in the first orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\mathbf{b}
lies on one of finitely many hyperplanes parallel to
\frac{u_{i}}{a_{ij}}+\frac{u_{j}}{a_{ij}}=n
. Moreover, the number of possible planes on which
\mathbf{b}
may lie is constant.

Proof.

Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. Since
m
is minimal, choose a
j
such that
\left\lfloor\frac{b_{i}-1}{a_{ij}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{ji}}\right\rfloor<n
. By Corollary
6.4
and Proposition
6.5
, it follows that
n-\ell\leq\left\lfloor\frac{b_{j}}{a_{ji}}\right\rfloor<n-\ell+1
, where
b_{i}=a_{ij}\ell
. Thus, the value of
b_{j}
has only finitely many possibilities, namely
a_{ji}
possibilities, and thus only finitely many planes parallel to
\frac{u_{i}}{a_{ij}}+\frac{u_{j}}{a_{ij}}=n
can contain a minimal generator.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

Note that, using the above notation, if
b_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{a_{ij}}
and
b_{j}\equiv 0\pmod{a_{ji}}
, then we get
\frac{1}{a_{ij}}(a_{ij}\ell_{1})+\frac{1}{a_{ji}}(a_{ji}\ell_{2})=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\geq n
. By minimal generation, we get that
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}-\frac{1}{a_{ij}}<n
or
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}-\frac{1}{a_{ji}}<n
. But since
\frac{1}{a_{ij}},\frac{1}{a_{ji}}\leq 1
, it follows that
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=n
. Thus, this minimal generator lies on the face
\frac{u_{i}}{a_{ij}}+\frac{u_{j}}{a_{ij}}=n
.

We are now ready to calculate
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
for our family. We have the following formulation:

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
I=(x^{a},y)\cap(y^{b},z)\cap(z^{c},x)=(x^{a}z,y^{b}x,z^{c}y,xyz)
. If
m=x^{u}y^{v}z^{w}
is a minimal generator of
I^{(n)}/I^{n}
, then:

(1)

If
\frac{1}{a}u+v=n
,
m=x^{an-av}y^{v}z^{\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor,cn+cav-can\}}
for some
1\leq v\leq n-1
,

(2)

If
\frac{1}{b}v+w=n
,
m=x^{\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{w}{c}\right\rfloor,an-abn+abw\}}y^{bn-bw}z^{w}
for some
1\leq w\leq n-1
,

(3)

If
\frac{1}{c}w+u=n
,
m=x^{u}y^{\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{u}{a}\right\rfloor,bn-bcn+bcu\}}z^{cn-cu}
for some
1\leq u\leq n-1
.

Proof.

We will just prove (1), as (2) and (3) are analogous. So assume that
u=an-av
.

The defining inequalities for
n\operatorname{NP}(I)
is as follows:

n\operatorname{NP}(I)=\begin{cases}u+(a-1)v+(a-1)(b-1)w\geq(ab-b+1)n\\
(c-1)u+(a-1)(c-1)v+w\geq(ac-a+1)n\\
(b-1)(c-1)u+v+(b-1)w\geq(bc-c+1)n\\
\frac{1}{a}u+v\geq n\\
\frac{1}{b}v+w\geq n\\
\frac{1}{c}w+u\geq n\\
u,v,w\geq 0\end{cases}.

The defining inequalities for
n\operatorname{SP}(I)
are the latter four equations defining
n\operatorname{NP}(I)
.

From the fourth and seventh inequalities, we see
0\leq v\leq n
. If
v=0
, then
u=an
and
w\geq n
. But this means that all of the first three inequalities are satisfied, so
m\in n\operatorname{NP}(I)
. So, if
v=0
,
m
cannot be a minimal generator in
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
. For similar reasons, no minimal generator can have
v=n
.

To see that
m\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)
, observe:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}(an-av)+v

\displaystyle=n

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}v+\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor,cn+cav-can\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{b}v+n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor\geq n,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor,cn+cav-can\}+an-av

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{c}(cn+cav-can)+an-av=n.

Now, to see
m\not\in n\operatorname{NP}(I)
, we consider it by cases.
If the
w
-coordinate of
m
is
n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor
,
w\leq n
. Thus, with the first inequality, we see

an-av+(a-1)v+(a-1)(b-1)w\leq an-av+(a-1)v+(a-1)(b-1)n=(ab-b+1)n-v<(ab-b+1)n.

If
w=cn+cav-can
, since
v<n
, considering the third inequality yields:

a(b-1)(v-n)+bcn-cn+v\leq bcn-cn+v<(bc-c+1)n.

Now, note
\frac{1}{a}(an-av-1)+v=n-v-\frac{1}{a}+v=n-\frac{1}{a}<n
, so
x^{-1}m\not\in I^{(n)}/I^{n}
. Also,
\frac{1}{a}(an-av)+v-1=n-v+v-1=n-1<n
, so
y^{-1}m\not\in I^{(n)}/I^{n}
. Finally, to see
z^{-1}m\not\in I^{(n)}/I^{n}
, we take it in cases. If
n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor\leq cn+cav-can
,

\frac{1}{c}(cn+cav-can-1)+an-av=n-\frac{1}{c}<n.

If
n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor\geq cn+cav-can
,

\frac{v}{b}+n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor-1<n+1-1=n.

Since all monomials of this form are minimal generators, all other monomials with first two exponents
an-av
and
v
will have a final exponent, at least,
\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor,cn+cav-can\}
, so there are no other possible minimal generators residing on the face,
\frac{1}{a}u+v=n
.
∎

We have now established a (potentially strict) subset of the generators of
I^{(n)}/I^{n}
. Let
M(n)
be this set of generators. However, even if this is a strict subset, we have the following:

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
I
be as in Proposition
6.8
. Suppose
M(n)
is a strict subset of the minimal generators of
I^{(n)}/I^{n}
. That is, suppose there is a minimal generator not residing on a face. Then this minimal generator must have the exponent vector,
R_{n}=(\lceil n\alpha\rceil,\lceil n\beta\rceil,\lceil n\gamma\rceil)
. That is, the minimal generator must be
x^{\lceil n\alpha\rceil}y^{\lceil n\beta\rceil}z^{\lceil n\gamma\rceil}
.

Proof.

Suppose
(u,v,w)
are the coordinates of a minimal generator of
I^{(n)}/I^{n}
that does not reside on any face. By Remark
6.7
,
a
does not divide
u
,
b
does not divide
v
, and
c
does not divide
w
. It follows that
n<\frac{1}{a}u+v<n+1
,
n<\frac{1}{b}v+w<n+1
,
n<\frac{1}{c}w+u<n+1
. Thus, we have
v=n-\left\lfloor\frac{u}{a}\right\rfloor
,
w=n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor
,
u=n-\left\lfloor\frac{w}{c}\right\rfloor
.

Let’s focus on the possible values of
v
. It is evident that
1\leq v\leq n
. If
1\leq v\leq n-1
, then
x^{an-av}y^{v}z^{\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor,cn+cav-can\}}
has already been shown to be a generator. If
n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor\geq cn+cav-can
, then we have two generators with two identical coordinates, so they must be the same generator. Otherwise,
n-\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\rfloor<cn+cav-can
. With some rearranging, we get
\left\lfloor\frac{(abc+1)v}{b}\right\rfloor>(ac-c+1)n
. It follows that
\lceil n\beta\rceil\leq v\leq n
. By analogous arguments, we have
\lceil n\alpha\rceil\leq u\leq n
, and
\lceil n\gamma\rceil\leq w\leq n
. It follows that
(u,v,w)
is generated by
R_{n}\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)
, and thus, by minimality, must be equal to
R_{n}
.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

The point
R_{n}
above may not be a minimal point of
n\operatorname{SP}(I)
. For example, if
a=2
,
b=3
, and
c=4
,
R_{4}=(4,3,4)
, which is generated by
(3,3,4)\in n\operatorname{SP}(I)
. Furthermore,
R_{n}
itself may not be in
n\operatorname{SP}(I)\setminus n\operatorname{NP}(I)
. It also may reside on a face. However, the assumptions of Proposition
6.9
specify that a minimal point not on the faces exists, and when this is the case, this generator is
R_{n}
.

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
I
be as in Proposition
6.8
, and
M(n)
be the set of generators described in Proposition
6.8
. Then
\#M(n)\sim(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)n
.

Proof.

We use inclusion-exclusion principle. Clearly, the individual types of the monomials given in Proposition
6.8
add up to
3(n-1)
, so we need to consider the monomials that are multiple types. Note that from hereon, we are only interested in the leading coefficient, so we drop the -1 from
3(n-1)
.

We focus on the number of monomials that are type (1) and type (3). This is the same as the pairs
(u,v)
such that
1\leq u,v\leq n-1
,
an-av=u
,
v=\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{u}{a}\right\rfloor,bn-bcn+bcu\}
, and
\max\{n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor,cn+cav-can\}=cn-cu
. We can determine this by determining the
v
such that
1\leq v\leq n-1
,
1\leq an-av\leq n-1
,
v\geq bn-bcn+bc(an-av)
and
n-\left\lfloor\frac{v}{b}\right\rfloor\leq cn+cav-can
. With some simplification, the latter two are satisfied when
v\geq n\beta
.

Furthermore, every
n\beta\leq v\leq n-1
satisfies
an-av\geq 1
. Finally, if
v\geq n\beta
, then
a(n-v)\leq a(n-n\beta)=an(1-\beta)=an\left(\frac{\alpha}{a}\right)=\alpha n<n
. Since
an-av
is an integer strictly less than
n
, we have
an-av\leq n-1
.

Thus, there are
n-1-\lceil n\beta\rceil
possible generators of types (1) and (3). This grows like
(1-\beta)n
. Applying analogous arguments for the rest, we conclude that leading term of
\#M(n)
is
3n-n(1-\alpha+1-\beta+1-\gamma)=n(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)
.
∎

We obtain the immediate conclusion:

Proof of
1.3
.(i).

Apply Propositions
6.9
and
6.11
.
∎

From the data, we can verify Example
1.3
(ii).

Proof of
1.3
.(ii).

Observe
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=\#M(n)+0
or
\#M(n)+1
depending on whether the point
R_{n}=(\lceil n\alpha\rceil,\lceil n\beta\rceil,\lceil n\gamma\rceil)
is a minimal generator off of a face or not. We will show that this occurs for
n
if and only if it occurs for
n+\ell(abc+1)
for any
\ell\geq 1
.
Indeed, consider the first coordinate of
R_{n+\ell(abc+1)}
:

\lceil(n+\ell(abc+1))\alpha\rceil=\lceil n\alpha+\ell(a(bc-b+1))\rceil=\lceil n\alpha\rceil+a\ell(bc-b+1).

Thus, the first coordinate of
R_{n}
and
R_{n+\ell(abc+1)}
are congruent modulo
a
. If we assume
R_{n}
is a minimal generator not on a face, then
\lceil n\alpha\rceil\not\equiv 0\pmod{a}
. This occurs if and only if the first coordinate
R_{n+\ell(abc+1)}
is not a multiple of
a
. Applying the same argument for the other coordinates,
R_{n}
is a minimal generator not on a face implies
R_{n+\ell(abc+1)}
is not on a face. Finally, to see
R_{n+\ell(abc+1)}
is a minimal generator, consider the inequality
n\leq\frac{1}{a}\lceil n\alpha\rceil+\lceil n\beta\rceil<n+1
, which holds because
R_{n}
is a minimal generator not on a face. Then
n+\ell(abc+1)\leq\frac{1}{a}(\lceil(n+\ell(abc+1))\alpha\rceil)+\lceil(n+\ell(abc+1))\beta\rceil<n+\ell(abc+1)+1
via simple addition, and the other inequalities hold too.

∎

Example 6.12
.

Let
I=(x^{2},y)\cap(y^{3},z)\cap(z^{4},x)=(xyz,x^{2}z,y^{3}x,z^{4}y)
. Then,
P=\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{5},\frac{4}{5}\right)
, and
R_{n}
is a minimal generator not on a face if and only if
n\equiv 3\pmod{5}
. In particular,

\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)=\begin{cases}0&n=1\\
\frac{11}{5}n-2&n\equiv 0\pmod{5}\\
\frac{11}{5}n-\frac{6}{5}&n\equiv 1\pmod{5}\text{ and }n\neq 1\\
\frac{11}{5}n-\frac{7}{5}&n\equiv 2\pmod{5}\\
\frac{11}{5}n-\frac{3}{5}&n\equiv 3\pmod{5}\\
\frac{11}{5}n-\frac{4}{5}&n\equiv 4\pmod{5}\\
\end{cases}.

Note that, in this case, although
abc+1=25
is a quasi-period, it is not the minimal quasi-period of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}
.

Remark 6.13
.

In Drabkin and Guerrieri’s proof that (under the appropriate conditions)

is eventually quasi-polynomial, he determines that a quasi-period is the least common multiple of the degrees of the generators of
\mathcal{R}_{s}(I)
as an
R
-algebra
[
6
]
. However, the quasi-period calculated from Example

(ii),
abc+1
, looks nothing like a least common multiple. This suggests perhaps a deeper, number-theoretical relationship between
a
,

,

Open Questions

We have seen some results on the growth of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
, but we still have many unanswered questions about the function. These include, but are not limited to:

Question 1
.

Given an arbitrary monomial ideal
I
,

1.

What is the degree of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
?

2.

What are the coefficients of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
?

3.

Are the leading coefficients of the quasi-polynomial on each branch equal?

4.

What is the minimum period for the symbolic defect function?

5.

How large does
n
have to be before adhering to the quasi-polynomial structure?

One can ask the very same questions about
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
. Furthermore, as stated in Section 4, there is no immediately known relationship between growth rates of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
and
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
. So, we also ask,

Question 2
.

How do the growth rates of
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
and
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
compare? For what ideals does
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)=O(\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n))
?

Lastly, the methods of Section 5 and 6 seem to be specific, but the natural question is,

Question 3
.

Can the methods of Section 5 and/or 6 be expanded upon to calculate
\operatorname{sdef}_{I}(n)
or
\operatorname{isdef}_{I}(n)
for more general ideals?
