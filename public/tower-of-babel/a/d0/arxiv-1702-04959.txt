Provable Accelerated Gradient Method for Nonconvex Low Rank Optimization
Huan Li, Zhouchen Lin

Abstract
Optimization over low rank matrices has broad applications in machine learning. For large scale problems, an attractive heuristic is to factorize the low rank matrix to a product of two much smaller matrices. In this paper, we study the nonconvex problem min 𝐔 ∈ ℛ n × r ⁡ g ⁡ ( 𝐔 ) = f ⁡ ( 𝐔𝐔 T ) \min_{\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}}g(\mathbf{U})=f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T}) under the assumptions that f ⁡ ( 𝐗 ) f(\mathbf{X}) is restricted μ \mu -strongly convex and L L -smooth on the set { 𝐗 : 𝐗 ⪰ 0 , rank ( 𝐗 ) ≤ r } \{\mathbf{X}:\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r\} . We propose an accelerated gradient method with alternating constraint that operates directly on the 𝐔 \mathbf{U} factors and show that the method has local linear convergence rate with the optimal dependence on the condition number of L / μ \sqrt{L/\mu} . Globally, our method converges to the critical point with zero gradient from any initializer. Our method also applies to the problem with the asymmetric factorization of 𝐗 = 𝐔 ~ ​ 𝐕 ~ T \mathbf{X}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T} and the same convergence result can be obtained. Extensive experimental results verify the advantage of our method.

Introduction

Low rank matrix estimation has broad applications in machine learning, computer vision and signal processing. In this paper, we consider the problem of the form:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times n}}f(\mathbf{X}),\hskip 10.00002pts.t.\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,

(1.1)

where there exists minimizer
\mathbf{X}^{*}
of rank-
r
. We consider the case of
r\ll n
. Optimizing problem (
1.1
) in the
\mathbf{X}
space often requires computing at least the top-
r
singular value/vectors in each iteration and
O(n^{2})
memory to store a large
n

n
matrix, which restricts the applications with huge size matrices. To reduce the computational cost as well as the storage space, many literatures exploit the observation that a positive semidefinite low rank matrix can be factorized as a product of two much smaller matrices, i.e.,
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T}
, and study the following nonconvex problem instead:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}}g(\mathbf{U})=f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T}).

(1.2)

A wide family of problems can be cast as problem (
1.2
), including matrix sensing
(
Bhojanapalli et al., 2016b
)
, matrix completion
(
Jain et al., 2013
)
, one bit matrix completion
(
Davenport et al., 2014
)
, sparse principle component analysis
(

)
and factorization machine
(

)
. In this paper, we study problem (
1.2
) and aim to propose an accelerated gradient method that operates on the
\mathbf{U}
factors directly. The factorization in problem (
1.2
) makes

nonconvex, even if

is convex. Thus, proving the acceleration becomes a harder task than the analysis for convex programming.

1.2
Our Contributions

In this paper, we use Nesterov’s acceleration scheme for problem (
1.2
) and an efficient accelerated gradient method with alternating constraint is proposed, which operates on the
\mathbf{U}
factors directly. We back up our method with provable theoretical results. Specifically, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

We establish the curvature of local restricted strong convexity along a certain trajectory by restricting the problem onto a constraint set, which allows us to use the classical accelerated gradient method for convex programs to solve the constrained problem. We build our result with the tool of polar decomposition.

2.

In order to reduce the negative influence of the constraint and ensure the convergence to the critical point of the original unconstrained problem, rather than the reformulated constrained problem, we propose a novel alternating constraint strategy and combine it with the classical accelerated gradient method.

3.

When
f
is restricted
\mu
-strongly convex and restricted
L
-smooth, our method has the local linear convergence to the optimum solution, which has the same dependence on
\sqrt{L/\mu}
as convex programming. As far as we know, we are the first to establish the convergence matching the optimal dependence on
\sqrt{L/\mu}
for this kind of nonconvex problems. Globally, our method converges to a critical point of problem (
1.2
) from any initializer.

Notations and Assumptions

For matrices
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}

as the spectral norm and

as their inner products. We denote

as the smallest singular value of
\mathbf{U}
and
\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{U})=\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}
as the largest one. We use
\mathbf{U}_{S}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
as the submatrix of
\mathbf{U}
with the rows indicated by the index set
S\subseteq\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
,
\mathbf{U}_{-S}\in\mathcal{R}^{(n-r)\times r}
as the submatrix with the rows indicated by the indexes out of
S
and
\mathbf{X}_{S,S}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
as the submatrix of
\mathbf{X}
with the rows and columns indicated by
S
.
\mathbf{X}\succeq 0
means that
\mathbf{X}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. For the objective function
g(\mathbf{U})
, its gradient w.r.t.
\mathbf{U}
is
\nabla g(\mathbf{U})=2\nabla f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T})\mathbf{U}
. We assume that
\nabla f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T})
is symmetric for simplicity. Our conclusions for the asymmetric case naturally generalize since
\nabla g(\mathbf{U})=\nabla f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T})\mathbf{U}+\nabla f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T})^{T}\mathbf{U}
in this case. Denote the optimum solution set of problem (
1.2
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{*}=\{\mathbf{U}^{*}:\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r},\mathbf{U}^{*}{\mathbf{U}^{*}}^{T}=\mathbf{X}^{*}\}.

(1.3)

where
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is a minimizer of problem (
1.1
). An important issue in minimizing

is that its optimum solution is not unique, i.e., if
\mathbf{U}^{*}
is the optimum solution of problem (
1.2
), then
\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{R}
is also an optimum solution for any orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
. Given
\mathbf{U}
, we define the optimum solution that is closest to
\mathbf{U}
as

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}(\mathbf{U})=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{R},\mbox{ where }\mathbf{R}=\mbox{argmin}_{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}=\mathbf{I}}\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{U}\|_{F}^{2}.

1.3.1
Assumptions

In this paper, we assume that
f
is restricted
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth on the set
\{\mathbf{X}:\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r\}
. We state the standard definitions below.

Definition 1
.

Let
f:\mathcal{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
be a convex differentiable function. Then,
f
is restricted
\mu
-strongly convex on the set
\{\mathbf{X}:\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r\}
if, for any
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\{\mathbf{X}:\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r\}
, we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{Y})\geq f(\mathbf{X})+\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}.

Definition 2
.

Let
f:\mathcal{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
be a convex differentiable function. Then,
f
is restricted
L
-smooth on the set
\{\mathbf{X}:\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r\}
if, for any
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\{\mathbf{X}:\mathbf{X}\succeq 0,\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r\}
, we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{Y})\leq f(\mathbf{X})+\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}

and

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\mathbf{Y})-\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\|_{F}\leq L\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\|_{F}.

1.3.2
Polar decomposition

Polar decomposition is a powerful tool for matrix analysis. We briefly review it in this section. We only describe the left polar decomposition of a square matrix.

Definition 3
.

The polar decomposition of a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
has the form
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}
where
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
is positive semidefinite and
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
is an orthogonal matrix.

If
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
is of full rank, then
\mathbf{A}
has the unique polar decomposition with positive definite
\mathbf{H}
. In fact, since a positive semidefinite Hermitian matrix has a unique positive semidefinite square root,
\mathbf{H}
is uniquely given by
\mathbf{H}=\sqrt{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{T}}
.
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
is also unique.

In this paper, we use the tool of polar decomposition’s perturbation theorem to build the restricted strong convexity of
g(\mathbf{U})
. It is described below.

Lemma 1
.

(
Li, 1995
)

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
be of full rank and
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}
be its unique polar decomposition,
\mathbf{A}+\triangle\mathbf{A}
be of full rank and
(\mathbf{H}+\triangle\mathbf{H})(\mathbf{Q}+\triangle\mathbf{Q})
be its unique polar decomposition. Then, we have

\displaystyle\|\triangle\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\leq\frac{2}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{A})}\|\triangle\mathbf{A}\|_{F}.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\Omega_{S}\cap\mathcal{X}^{*}

From the restricted convexity of

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &f(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{T})-f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T})\\

For problem (

\Omega_{S}
is a convex set and from Theorem

behaves locally like a strongly convex and smooth function when restricted on the set
\Omega_{S}
. Thus, we can use the classical method for convex programming to solve problem (

), e.g., the accelerated gradient method
3

However, it is still more challenging than convex programming since we should guarantee that all the variables in Theorem

belong to
\Omega_{S}
, while it is not required in convex programming. So the conclusion in
Necoara et al., 2018
cannot be applied to problem (

\mathbf{y}^{k+1}
is not a convex combination of
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}

\mathbf{x}^{k}

However, there remains a practical issue that when solving problem (
2.4
), we may get stuck at a local minimum of problem (
2.4
) at the boundary of the constraint
\mathbf{U}\in\Omega_{S}
, which is not the optimum solution of problem (
1.2
). In other words, we may halt before reaching the acceleration region, i.e., the local neighborhood of the optimum solution of problem (
1.2
). To overcome this trouble, we propose a novel alternating trajectory strategy. Specifically, we define two sets
\Omega_{S^{1}}
and
\Omega_{S^{2}}
as follows

\displaystyle\Omega_{S^{1}}=\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}:\mathbf{U}_{S^{1}}\succeq\epsilon\mathbf{I}\},\hskip 10.00002pt\Omega_{S^{2}}=\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}:\mathbf{U}_{S^{2}}\succeq\epsilon\mathbf{I}\}

and

S^{1}
and
S^{2}
are of size

such that

and
\mathbf{U}^{*}_{S^{2}}

1.2

, rather than a critical point of problem (
2.4
).

4.1

Bhojanapalli et al.
Bhojanapalli et al., 2016a
used the gradient descent to solve problem (

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{k+1}=\mathbf{U}^{k}-\eta\nabla g(\mathbf{U}^{k}).

With the restricted strong convexity and smoothness of

, Bhojanapalli et al.
Bhojanapalli et al., 2016a
proved the linear convergence of gradient descent in the form of

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|\mathbf{U}^{N+1}-P_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}(\mathbf{U}^{N+1})\|_{F}^{2}\\
\leq&\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{U}^{*})}{\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}\frac{\mu}{L+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{N}\|\mathbf{U}^{0}-P_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}(\mathbf{U}^{0})\|_{F}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(4.6)

As a comparison, from Theorem
4
, our method converges linearly within the error of

\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}^{*})\min\{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S^{1}}^{*}),\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S^{2}}^{*})\}}{\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{N}
, where
N
is the total number of inner iterations. From Lemma
3
, we know
\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}^{*}_{S})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{rn}}\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}^{*})
in the worst case and it is tight
Avron & Boutsidis, 2013
. Thus, our method has the convergence rate of
\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{U}^{*})}{\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{nr(L++\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2})}}\right)^{N}
in the worst case. When the function
f
is ill-conditioned, i.e.,
\frac{L}{\mu}\geq nr
, our method outperforms the gradient descent. This phenomenon is similar to the case observed in the stochastic optimization community: the non-accelerated methods such as SDCA
Shalev-Shwartz & Zhang, 2013

while the accelerated methods such as Accelerated SDCA
Shalev-Shwartz & Zhang, 2016

Lin et al., 2015
and Katyusha

have the complexity of
O\left(\sqrt{\frac{mL}{\mu}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
, where

is the sample size. The latter is tight when
\frac{L}{\mu}\geq m
for stochastic programming
Woodworth & Srebro, 2016
. In matrix completion, the optimal sample complexity is
O(rn\log n)

Candès & Recht, 2009
. It is unclear whether our convergence rate for problem (

) is tight or there exists a faster method. We leave it as an open problem.

4.1.1
Dropping the Dependence on

Our convergence rate has an additional dependence on
n
compared with the gradient descent method. It comes from
\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}^{*}_{S})
, i.e., Lemma
2
. In fact, we use a loose relaxation in the last inequality of (

\frac{2\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S})}\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{S}-\mathbf{U}_{S}\|_{F}\leq\frac{2\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S})}\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{F}
. Since
\mathbf{U}_{S}\in\mathcal{R}^{r\times r}
and
\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}
, a more suitable estimation should be

\displaystyle\frac{2\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S})}\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{S}-\mathbf{U}_{S}\|_{F}\approx\frac{2\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S})}\sqrt{\frac{r}{n}}\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{F}\approx\frac{2r\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U})}\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{F}.

(4.7)

In practice, (
4.7
) holds when the entries of
\mathbf{U}^{t,k}
and
\mathbf{V}^{t,k}
converge nearly equally fast to those of
\mathbf{U}^{t,*}
, which may be expected in practice. Thus, under the condition of (
4.7
), our convergence rate can be improved to

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{U}^{*})}{r\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{N}.

We numerically verify (

.

4.1.2
Examples with Ill-conditioned Objective
f

Although the condition number
\frac{L}{\mu}
approximate to 1 for some famous problems in machine learning, e.g., matrix regression and matrix completion
Chen & Wainwright, 2015
, we can still find many problems with ill-conditioned objective, especially in the computer vision applications. We give the example of low rank representation (LRR)
Liu et al., 2013
. The LRR model is a famous model in computer vision. It can be formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{X}}\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\hskip 10.00002pts.t.\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{A},

where
\mathbf{A}
is the observed data and
\mathbf{D}
is a dictionary that linearly spans the data space. We can reformulate the problem as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{X}}\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}\hskip 10.00002pts.t.\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r.

We know
L/\mu=\kappa(\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{D})
, which is the condition number of
\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{D}
. If we generate
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times n}
as a random matrix with normal distribution, then
E\left[\mbox{log}\kappa(\mathbf{D})\right]\sim\mbox{log}n
as
n\rightarrow\infty

Edelman, 1988
and thus
E\left[\frac{L}{\mu}\right]\sim n^{2}
. We numerically verify on MATLAB that if
n=1000
, then
\frac{L}{\mu}
is of the order
10^{7}
, which is much larger than
O(n)
.

5
Global Convergence

In this section, we study the global convergence of Algorithm
1
without the assumption that

is restricted strongly convex. We allow the algorithm to start from any initializer. Since we have no information about
\mathbf{U}^{*}
when

is far from
\mathbf{U}^{*}

. That is to say, after each inner loop, we check whether

using the volume sampling subset selection algorithm.

We first consider the inner loop and establish Lemma
6
. We drop the outer iteration number
t
for simplicity and leave the proof in Appendix C.

Lemma 6
.

Assume that
\{\mathbf{U}^{k},\mathbf{V}^{k}\}
is bounded and
\mathbf{U}^{0}\in\Omega_{S}
. Let
\eta\leq\frac{1-\beta_{\max}^{2}}{\hat{L}(2\beta_{\max}+1)+2\gamma}

,

M=\max\{\|\mathbf{U}^{k}\|_{2},\|\mathbf{V}^{k}\|_{2},\forall k\}
,
\beta_{\max}=\max\left\{\beta_{k},k=0,\cdots,K\right\}
,
\beta_{k}=\frac{\theta_{k}(1-\theta_{k-1})}{\theta_{k-1}}
and
\gamma
is a small constant. Then, we have

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{U}^{K+1})-g(\mathbf{U}^{0})\leq-\sum_{k=0}^{K}\gamma\|\mathbf{U}^{k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{k}\|_{F}^{2}.

Now we consider the outer loop. As discussed in Section
3
, when solving problem (
2.4
) directly, we may get stuck at the boundary of the constraint. Thanks to the alternating constraint strategy, we can cancel the negative influence of the constraint and establish the global convergence to a critical point of problem (
1.2
), which is described in Theorem

O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
operations,
\mathbf{U}^{T,K+1}
is an approximate zero gradient point in the precision of
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
\{\mathbf{U}^{t,k},\mathbf{V}^{t,k}\}
is bounded and
\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{S^{\prime}}^{t,K+1})\geq\epsilon,\forall t
. Let
\eta
be the one defined in Lemma

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla g(\mathbf{U}^{T,K+1})\right\|_{F}\leq 21\left(\frac{1}{\eta\theta_{K}}+\frac{\hat{L}}{\theta_{K}}\right)\varepsilon

with probability of

. The volume sampling subset selection algorithm needs

Proof.

We follow three steps to prove the theorem.

Step 1. Firstly, we bound the difference of two consecutive variables, i.e.,
\mathbf{U}^{t,k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{t,k}
.

From Lemma
6
we have

\displaystyle\gamma\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|\mathbf{U}^{t,k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{t,k}\|_{F}^{2}\leq g(\mathbf{U}^{t,0})-g(\mathbf{U}^{t,K+1}).

Summing over
t=0,\cdots,T
yields

\displaystyle\gamma\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|\mathbf{U}^{t,k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{t,k}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(g(\mathbf{U}^{t,0})-g(\mathbf{U}^{t,K+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(g(\mathbf{U}^{t,0})-g(\mathbf{U}^{t+1,0})\right)\leq g(\mathbf{U}^{t,0})-f(\mathbf{U}^{*}{\mathbf{U}^{*}}^{T}).

So after
T=2\frac{g(\mathbf{U}^{t,0})-f(\mathbf{X}^{*})}{\varepsilon^{2}}
outer iterations, we must have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|\mathbf{U}^{t,k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{t,k}\|_{F}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|\mathbf{U}^{t+1,k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{t+1,k}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}

(5.1)

for some
t<T
. Thus, we can bound
\|\mathbf{U}^{t^{\prime},k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{t^{\prime},k}\|_{F}
by
\varepsilon
, where
t^{\prime}=t
or
t^{\prime}=t+1
. Moreover, from Lemma
13
in Appendix C, we can bound
\|\mathbf{U}^{t^{\prime},k+1}-\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1}\|_{F}

\|\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1}-\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k}\|_{F}
and
\|\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1}-\mathbf{V}^{t^{\prime},k}\|_{F}
by
\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta_{k}}
.

Step 2. Secondly, we bound parts of elements of the gradient, i.e.,
\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t,K+1})\right)_{-S^{1}}
and
\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t,K+1})\right)_{-S^{2}}
.

From the optimality condition of (
5
), we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\theta_{k}}{\eta}\hskip-1.42271pt\left(\hskip-1.42271pt\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1}\hskip-1.42271pt-\hskip-1.42271pt\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k}\hskip-1.42271pt\right)\hskip-1.42271pt+\hskip-1.42271pt\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1})\hskip-1.42271pt-\hskip-1.42271pt\nabla g(\mathbf{V}^{t^{\prime},k})\hskip-1.42271pt\in\hskip-1.42271pt\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1})\hskip-1.42271pt+\hskip-1.42271pt\partial I_{\Omega_{S^{j}}}(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1})

for

when
t^{\prime}=t
and
j=2
when
t^{\prime}=t+1
. From Lemmas
10
and
13
, we can easily check that

\displaystyle\left\|-\frac{\theta_{k}}{\eta}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1}-\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k}\right)+\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1})-\nabla g(\mathbf{V}^{t^{\prime},k})\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{14\varepsilon}{\eta\theta_{k}}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\mbox{dist}\left(0,\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1})+\partial I_{\Omega_{S^{i}}}(\mathbf{Z}^{t^{\prime},k+1})\right)\leq\frac{14\varepsilon}{\eta\theta_{k}},\forall k=0,\cdots,K,

which leads to

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t,K+1})\right)_{-S^{1}}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{14\varepsilon}{\eta\theta_{K}},

(5.2)

and

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,1})\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{14\varepsilon}{\eta\theta_{K}},

(5.3)

where
\mathbf{A}_{-S}
means the submatrix with the rows indicated by the indexes out of
S

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,0})\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,1})\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}\\
\leq&\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,0})-\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,1})\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}\\
\leq&\left\|\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,0})-\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,1})\right\|_{F}\leq\hat{L}\|\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,0}-\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,1}\|_{F}\leq\frac{5\hat{L}\varepsilon}{\theta_{K}},\end{aligned}

(5.4)

where we use Lemma

in the last inequality. Combing (

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,0})\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{19\varepsilon}{\eta\theta_{K}}.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{19\varepsilon}{\eta\theta_{K}}\geq&\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t+1,0})\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}^{t,K+1})\mathbf{Q}^{T}\right)_{-S^{2}}\right\|_{F}\\

(5.5)

Step 3. We bound all the elements of the gradient. Recall that we require

;

;
Tseng, 2008
becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{k}=(1-\theta_{k})\mathbf{U}^{k}+\theta_{k}\mathbf{Z}^{k},

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{k+1}=\mathbf{Z}^{k}-\eta\nabla g(\mathbf{V}^{k}),

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{k+1}=(1-\theta_{k})\mathbf{U}^{k}+\theta_{k}\mathbf{Z}^{k+1},

(6.3)

and it is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{k}=\mathbf{U}^{k}+\beta_{k}(\mathbf{U}^{k}-\mathbf{U}^{k-1}),

(6.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{k+1}=\mathbf{V}^{k}-\eta\nabla g(\mathbf{V}^{k}).

(6.5)

where
\beta_{k}
is defined in Lemma
6
. Another choice is a constant of
\beta<1
. Theorem
6
establishes the convergence rate for the above two recursions. We leave the proof in Appendix D.

Theorem 6
.

Assume that
\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
and
\mathbf{V}^{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r}
satisfy
\|\mathbf{V}^{k}-P_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}(\mathbf{V}^{k})\|_{F}\leq\min\left\{0.01\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{U}^{*}),\frac{\mu\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{U}^{*})}{6L\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}}\right\}
. Let
\eta
be the one in Lemma
6
. Then, we can have

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{U}^{k+1})+\nu\|\mathbf{U}^{k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{k}\|_{F}^{2}-g(\mathbf{U}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\frac{\gamma}{\frac{5}{\eta^{2}\mu\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{U}^{*})}+\nu}}\left[g(\mathbf{U}^{k})+\nu\|\mathbf{U}^{k}-\mathbf{U}^{k-1}\|_{F}^{2}-g(\mathbf{U}^{*})\right].

where
\gamma=\frac{1-\beta_{\max}^{2}}{4\eta}-\frac{\beta_{\max}\hat{L}}{2}-\frac{\hat{L}}{4}>0
and
\nu=\frac{1+\beta_{\max}^{2}}{4\eta}-\frac{\hat{L}}{4}>0
.

Consider the case that
\beta_{k}
is a constant. Then, we know that all of the constants
\gamma,\nu,\hat{L}
and
\frac{1}{\eta}
are of the order
O\left(L\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}\right)
. Thus, the convergence rate of recursion (
6.4
)-(
6.5
) is in the form of

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\mu\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{U}^{*})}{L\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}}\right)^{N},

which is the same as that of the gradient descent method in (
4.6
). Thus, although the convergence of the classical accelerated gradient method for problem (
1.2
) can be proved, it is not easy to build the acceleration upon the gradient descent. As a comparison, Algorithm
1
has a theoretical better dependence on the condition number of
\frac{L}{\mu}
. Thus, the reformulation of problem (
1.2
) to a constrained one is necessary to prove acceleration.

The Asymmetric Case

In this section, we consider the asymmetric case of problem (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\min_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times m}}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}),

(7.1)

where there exists a minimizer
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
of rank-
r
. We follow
Park et al., 2018
to assume
\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})=0
. In the asymmetric case, we can factorize
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}
and reformulate problem (
7.1
) as a similar problem to (
1.2
). Moreover, we follow
(
Park et al., 2018
;
Wang et al., 2017
)
to regularize the objective and force the solution pair
(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}})
to be balanced. Otherwise, the problem may be ill-conditioned since
\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\right)\hskip-1.42271pt(\delta\widetilde{\mathbf{V}})
is also a factorization of
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}
for any large
\delta

Park et al., 2018
. Specifically, we consider the following problem

\displaystyle\min_{\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times r},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times r}}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T})+\frac{\mu}{8}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(7.2)

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*}=\mathbf{A}\Sigma\mathbf{B}^{T}
be its SVD. Then,
(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}=\mathbf{A}\sqrt{\Sigma},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{*}=\mathbf{B}\sqrt{\Sigma})
is a minimizer of problem (
7.2
). Define a stacked matrix
\mathbf{U}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\end{array}\right)
and let
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{T}&\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{T}&\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\end{array}\right)
. Then we can write the objective in (
7.2
) in the form of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})
, defined as
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})=f(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T})+\frac{\mu}{8}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{8}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{4}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\|_{F}^{2}
. Since
f
is restricted
\mu
-strongly convex, we can easily check that
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})
is restricted
\frac{\mu}{4}
-strongly convex. On the other hand, we know that
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})
is restricted
\left(L+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)
-smooth. Applying the conclusions on the symmetric case to
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X})
, we can apply Algorithm
1
to the asymmetric case. From Theorem
4
, we can get the convergence rate. Moreover, since
\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{X}^{*})=2\sigma_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})=&\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})\\
\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})^{T}&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{\mu}{4}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}\\
-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{*}\end{array}\right)\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}^{*}}^{T},-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{*}}^{T}\right)\\
=&\frac{\mu}{4}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}\\
-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{*}\end{array}\right)\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}^{*}}^{T},-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{*}}^{T}\right)\end{aligned}

and
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}^{*})\|_{2}=\frac{\mu}{4}\|\mathbf{X}^{*}\|_{2}
, where
\mathbf{X}^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}^{*}}^{T}&\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{*}}^{T}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}^{*}}^{T}&\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{*}}^{T}\end{array}\right)
and we use
\nabla f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})=0
, we can simplify the worst case convergence rate to

\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{r}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})}{\|\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*}\|_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{(m+n)rL}}\right)^{N}
. As a comparison, the rate of the gradient descent is
\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{r}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})}{\|\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*}\|_{2}}\frac{\mu}{L}\right)^{N}

Park et al., 2018
.

In the asymmetric case, both
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{*}
are of full rank. Otherwise,
\mbox{rank}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{*})<r
. Thus, we can select the index set
S^{1}
from
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{0}
and select
S^{2}
from
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{0}
with the guarantee of
\sigma_{r}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{0}_{S^{1}})\geq\frac{\sigma_{r}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{0})}{\sqrt{2r(n-r+1)}}
and
\sigma_{r}(\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{0}_{S^{2}})\geq\frac{\sigma_{r}(\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{0})}{\sqrt{2r(m-r+1)}}

9
Conclusions

In this paper, we study the factorization based low rank optimization. A linearly convergent accelerated gradient method with alternating constraint is proposed with the optimal dependence on the condition number of
\sqrt{L/\mu}
as convex programming. As far as we know, this is the first work with the provable optimal dependence on
\sqrt{L/\mu}
for this kind of nonconvex problems. Our method can also be applied to the asymmetric factorization.

Many open problems remain in this area. 1. What is the lower complexity bound of problem (
1.2
)? Is our theoretical upper complexity bound tight on the dependence of
n
when
L/\mu\geq O(n)
? 2. Can the original AGD have the guaranteed acceleration theoretically? In other words, can we improve Necoara, Nesterov and Glineur
Necoara et al., 2018
’s results without the uniqueness assumption and have a better result than Theorem
6
?
