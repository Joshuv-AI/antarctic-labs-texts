Probability of entering an orthant by correlated fractional Brownian motion with drift: exact asymptotics
Krzysztof Dȩbicki, Lanpeng Ji, Svyatoslav Novikov

Abstract




Abstract:

For
\{\boldsymbol{B}_{H}(t)=(B_{H,1}(t),\ldots,B_{H,d}(t)),t\geq 0\}
,
where

\{B_{H,i}(t),t\geq 0\},1\leq i\leq d
are mutually independent fractional Brownian motions
we obtain the exact asymptotics of

\mathbb{P}(\exists t\geq 0:A\boldsymbol{B}_{H}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}t>\boldsymbol{\nu}u),\ \ \ \ u\to\infty,

where
A

is a non-singular
d\times d
matrix and

\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are such that there exists some
1\leq i\leq d
such that

\mu_{i}>0,\nu_{i}>0.

Key Words:
multi-dimensional fractional Brownian motion; extremes; exact asymptotics; large deviations; quadratic programming problem; dimension reduction.

AMS Classification:
Primary 60G15; secondary 60G70

1.
Introduction

Consider a vector-valued Gaussian process

\{{\boldsymbol{X}}(t),t\geq 0\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)=A\boldsymbol{B}_{H}(t)
with

A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
a non-singular matrix and
\{\boldsymbol{B}_{H}(t)=(B_{H,1}(t),\ldots,B_{H,d}(t)),t\geq 0\}

with
\{B_{H,i}(t),t\geq 0\},1\leq i\leq d
(
d\in\mathbb{N}
) being mutually independent fractional Brownian motions (fBms),
i.e., centered Gaussian processes with stationary increments, continuous sample paths and variance functions

\text{Var}(B_{H,i}(t))=t^{2H}
,
H\in(0,1)
.

We focus on the exact asymptotic behavior of the
probability
that a drifted correlated fractional Brownian motion
\boldsymbol{X}
enters orthant

\mathcal{O}_{u}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):x_{i}>\nu_{i}u,i=1,\ldots,d\}
over an infinite-time horizon, i.e.,

(1)

\displaystyle P(u):=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t\geq 0:\boldsymbol{X}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}t\ \in\mathcal{O}_{u}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}\forall_{i=1,\ldots,d}X_{i}(t)-\mu_{i}t>\nu_{i}u\right),

as
u\to\infty

for
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We are interested in the case that the above probability is a rare event,
that is,
P(u)\to 0
as
u\to\infty
, for which we shall assume that there exists some
1\leq i\leq d
such that

\mu_{i}>0,\nu_{i}>0.

The probability
P(u)
defined in (
1
)
is of interest both for theory-oriented studies and for applied-mathematics problems. One of important motivations to analyze (
1
) stems from
ruin theory
, where

P(u)
describes simultaneous ruin probability in infinite-time horizon of
d
dependent business lines
whose risk processes
R_{i}(t),t\geq 0
are modeled by

R_{i}(t)=\nu_{i}u+\mu_{i}t-X_{i}(t),

where
\nu_{i}u
represents the initial capital,
\mu_{i}
is the net profit rate and

X_{i}(t)
is the net loss up to time
t
; we refer to
[
14
]

for the formal justification of the use of fractional Brownian motion to model the risk process.

In the 1-dimensional case,
d=1
, the exact asymptotics for
P(u)

was derived in the seminal paper by Hüsler & Piterbarg
[
10
]
; see also
[
3
,
11
,
7
]

for extensions to other classes of stochastic
processes with stationary increments.

In the multidimensional case, the exact asymptotics of
P(u)
as
u\to\infty
is known only for the special Brownian model, i.e., when
H=1/2
; see
[
4
]
. The strategy of the proof there, although in its roots based on
the double sum
technique developed in the 1-dimensional setting for extremes of Gaussian processes (see, e.g.
[
16
,
15
,
17
]
),
needed new ideas that in several key steps of the argumentation significantly differ from methods used in the 1-dimensional case.
In particular, one of difficulties is the lack of Slepian-type inequalities that could be applied in the multidimensional setting, which was overcomed in
[
4
]
by the heavy use of independence of increments property of Brownian motion.

In this contribution, we aim to complement the findings of
[
4
]
by tackling the fBm problem (
1
)
for
H\neq 1/2
. Interestingly, in contrary to the Brownian case, the full analysis of all the cases
needs to consider two separate scenarios
described by the local behaviour of function

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(t)=(\zeta_{1}(t),\ldots,\zeta_{d}(t)):=(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)/t^{H},t>0

in the neighbourhood of the unique point
t_{0}
that minimizes function

(2)

\displaystyle g(t):=\frac{1}{t^{2H}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}(AA^{\top})^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}

over
t\geq 0
, where the point
t_{0}u
has a natural interpretation as the
most probable time

for the process
\{\boldsymbol{X}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}t,t\geq 0\}
to enter orthant
\mathcal{O}_{u}
;
see also Section
2
. Let
I\subset\{1,...,d\}
be the set of coordinates that contribute to the
asymptotics of
P(u)
, as
u\to\infty
; see Section
2
for the details about how to specify
I
.

In the first scenario, when
H<{1}/{2}
, or
H>1/2
and
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(t_{0})={0}
for all
i\in I
,
the local steepness of the correlation function of
X_{i}
is higher than
the local steepness of
\zeta_{i}(t)
in the neighbourhood of the point

t_{0}
. This case can be solved by an adaptation of the technique developed in

[
5
]
for extremes of centered vector-valued Gaussian processes over a finite time horizon.

The complementary case,
H>1/2
but
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(t_{0})\neq{0}
for some
i\in I
, is different from the previous one
since for those coordinates
i\in I
for which
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(t_{0})\neq{0}

the local steepness of the correlation function of
X_{i}
is lower than
the local steepness of
\zeta_{i}(t)
in the neighbourhood of the point

t_{0}
.
This scenario needs a novel approach which leads also to a different asymptotics of
P(u)
as
u\to\infty
.

The results derived in this contribution go in line with recent findings on the tail asymptotics of extremes of
vector-valued Gaussian processes, where most of the available literature deals with centered marginal processes
or over a compact parameter space
[
5
,
1
,
12
,
13
,
2
]
.

, and write
\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{y}=(x_{1}y_{1},\ldots,x_{d}y_{d})

\boldsymbol{0}:=(0,\ldots,0)

Denote by

.

For two positive functions

.

2.
Preliminary results

It is known that approximation of the probability
P(u)

depends on the solution of a related quadratic optimization problem.
In particular,
in the light of
[
6
, Theorem 1]
, the logarithmic
asymptotics can be derived and takes the following form

(3)

\displaystyle-\ln P(u)\sim\frac{\widehat{g}}{2}u^{2(1-H)},\quad\text{with}\ \ \widehat{g}=\inf_{t\geq 0}g(t),

where

(4)

\displaystyle g(t)=\frac{1}{t^{2H}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{v},\quad\Sigma=AA^{\top}.

The properties of function
g
, in particular, the existence of its minimizer and expansions in the neighbourhood of this point (when exists) are crucial to the exact asymptotic analysis.
In order to introduce some further notation and for further reference, we present a lemma on a quadratic optimization problem stated in
[
8
]
(see also
[
9
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},d\geq 2
be a positive definite matrix. If
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(-\infty,0]^{d}
, then the quadratic programming problem

P_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{b}):\text{minimise $\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}$ under the linear constraint }\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b}

has a unique solution
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}
and there exists a unique non-empty
index set
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that

(5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{I}=\boldsymbol{b}_{I}\not=\boldsymbol{0}_{I},\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{I^{c}}=\Sigma_{I^{c}I}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}\geq\boldsymbol{b}_{I^{c}},\quad\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}>\boldsymbol{0}_{I},

(6)

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}=\boldsymbol{b}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}>0,

where
I^{c}=\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus I.

Moreover, denoting
\boldsymbol{w}=\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}
we have

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{I}=\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}>\boldsymbol{0}_{I},\quad\boldsymbol{w}_{I^{c}}=\boldsymbol{0}_{I^{c}}.

The next lemma includes some properties of the function
g
and its relative
g_{I}(t)=\frac{1}{t^{2H}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)^{\top}_{I}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)_{I}
with
I

the index set as in Lemma
2.1
.
We defer its proof to Appendix.

Lemma 2.2
.

Function
g\in C^{1}(0,\infty)
and achieves its unique minimum at

(8)

\displaystyle t_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{4(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I})^{2}(1-2H)^{2}+16H(1-H)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}}-2(1-2H)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}}{4\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}(1-H)}>0

with

(9)

\displaystyle g(t_{0})=\inf_{t>0}\frac{1}{t^{2H}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}_{I}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}=g_{I}(t_{0}),

where

\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{b}(t_{0}),\quad\text{with \ }\boldsymbol{b}(t)=\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t,

and
I=I(t_{0})
being the index set corresponding to the solution of
P_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{b})
. Moreover,

(10)

\displaystyle g_{I}(t_{0}+t)=g_{I}(t_{0})+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}_{I}(t_{0})}{2}t^{2}(1+o(1)),\ \ \ \ t\to 0,

where

g^{\prime\prime}_{I}(t_{0})=\frac{1}{t_{0}^{2H+1}}\left(4\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}(1-H)t_{0}+2(1-2H)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{II}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}\right)>0.

Remarks 2.3
.

(a). Note that
t_{0}
given in (
8
) is actually an equation of
t_{0}
because
I=I(t_{0})
is a set function of
t_{0}
. Here, for any fixed
t>0,

I(t)\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
is the index set of the solution to the quadratic programming problem
P_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{b}(t))
, see Lemma
2.1
. We remark that, for specific problems,
both the index set
I
and
t_{0}
can be identified explicitly; see Example
3.3
in Section

or the examples presented in
[
4
]
.

Hereafter we shall use the notation
\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{b}(t_{0})=\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t_{0}
, and
I=I(t_{0})
for the
essential
index set of the quadratic programming problem
P_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{b})
. Furthermore,
let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}
be the unique solution
of
P_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{b})
. If
I^{c}=\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus I\neq\emptyset
, we define
the

and the

(11)

\displaystyle K=\{j\in I^{c}:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}=\Sigma_{jI}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}=\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\},\quad\text{ and }J=\{j\in I^{c}:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}=\Sigma_{jI}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}>\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\},

3.
Main Result

In this section we present the main result of this contribution.
Recall that through the whole paper we assume that there exists some
1\leq i\leq d
such that

\mu_{i}>0,\nu_{i}>0.

Denote
\boldsymbol{W}_{I}(t)=D\boldsymbol{B}_{H,I}(t)
, with
D
the matrix such that
DD^{\top}=\Sigma_{II}
.
We define
generalized Pickands constant
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{I}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{H}_{I}(T)\in(0,\infty),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{I}(T)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}}e^{\frac{1}{t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{w}_{I}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{I}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\in[0,T]}\boldsymbol{W}_{I}(t)-\frac{1}{2t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}t^{2H}>\boldsymbol{x}_{I}\right)d\boldsymbol{x}_{I},

with
\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{I}=\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I}.

We remark that
\mathcal{H}_{I}
is well-defined, finite and positive, since it is a multiple of the multidimensional Pickands constant
\mathcal{H}_{2H,V}
defined in (2.5) of
[
5
]
with
V=\left(2t_{0}^{4H}\right)^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}_{I})\Sigma_{II}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}_{I})
.

For
K,J
defined in (
11
) (note that
I^{c}=K\cup J
), we denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{K,J}:=

\displaystyle\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}g_{I}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})y^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I^{c}\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{1}{2t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}}\mathbb{I}_{\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{K}<y[[(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}]^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu})_{I^{c}}]_{K}\right)}d\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}dy,&K\neq\emptyset,\\
\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{\left\lvert J\right\rvert}\pi g_{I}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})}/\sqrt{\left\lvert(\Sigma^{-1})_{JJ}\right\rvert},&K=\emptyset,I^{c}=J\neq\emptyset,\\
\sqrt{\pi g_{I}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})},&I^{c}=\emptyset.\end{array}\right.

Following Section
2
, the logarithmic asymptotics of
P(u)

as
u\to\infty
depends on
t_{0}
, the minimizer of function
g

defined in (
4
).
As stated in the following theorem, the exact asymptotics of
P(u)

splits on two scenarios.
In order to catch an intuition behind this division, for a while let us consider
the 1-dimensional problem

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}B_{H,i}(t)-\mu_{i}t>\nu_{i}u\right),

assuming
\mu_{i},\nu_{i}>0.

By self-similarity of
B_{H,i}
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}B_{H,i}(t)-\mu_{i}t>\nu_{i}u\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}\frac{B_{H,i}(t)}{\nu_{i}u+\mu_{i}t}>1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}\frac{B_{H,i}(t)}{\nu_{i}+\mu_{i}t}>u^{1-H}\right)

and thus, following the same lines of reasoning as in Section
2
but for 1-dimensional setting,
the logarithmic asymptotics of the above is determined by
t_{0,i}
, the unique minimizer of

\zeta_{i}(t)=\frac{\nu_{i}+\mu_{i}t}{t^{H}}
,
t>0
, that is the point that satisfies

{\zeta_{i}}^{\prime}({t_{0,i}})={0}
or equivalently

H{\nu_{i}}=(1-H){t_{0,i}}{\mu_{i}}.

This leads to two cases, where the play between the value of the optimizing point
t_{0}

and the optimizers
t_{0,i}
of

\zeta_{i}(t)
for
i\in I

is crucial:

\diamond

H<{1}/{2}
, or
H>1/2
and
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(t_{0})={0}
for all
i\in I
.
Then the local steepness of the correlation function of
X_{i}
is higher than
the local steepness of
\zeta_{i}(t)
in the neighbourhood of the point

t_{0}
.

\diamond

H>1/2
but
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(t_{0})\neq{0}
for some
i\in I
.
For the coordinates
i\in I
for which
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}(t_{0})\neq{0}

the local steepness of the correlation function of
X_{i}
is lower than
the local steepness of
\zeta_{i}(t)
in the neighbourhood of the point

t_{0}
.

The following theorem constitutes the main finding of this contribution.
Let us recall notation
x_{-}=\max(0,-x),\,x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have, as
u\to\infty,

(i).

If
H<{1}/{2}
, or
H>1/2
and
H\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}=(1-H)t_{0}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}
, then

\displaystyle P(u)\sim\mathcal{H}_{I}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{K,J}}{\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}u^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert(1-H)+1/H+H-2}e^{-\frac{g_{I}(t_{0})}{2}u^{2(1-H)}}.

(ii).

If
H>1/2
but
H\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}\neq(1-H)t_{0}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}
, then

\displaystyle P(u)\sim\frac{t_{0}^{H(2\left\lvert I\right\rvert-d-2)}\mathcal{C}_{K,J}}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}\left(\sum_{i\in I}\left(w_{i}\mu_{i}-\frac{H}{t_{0}}w_{i}b_{i}\right)_{-}\right)u^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert(1-H)+1-H}e^{-\frac{g_{I}(t_{0})}{2}u^{2(1-H)}},

where

\sum_{i\in I}\left(w_{i}\mu_{i}-\frac{H}{t_{0}}w_{i}b_{i}\right)_{-}>0.

Remarks 3.2
.

We remark on the role of the index set
J
played in the asymptotics when it is non-empty. It is concluded from
[
4
]
that the index set
J
and the corresponding components do not play any role in the exact asymptotics of
P(u)
for
H=1/2
. From Theorem
3.1
, we have the same observation if additionally
K=\emptyset
. Indeed, this can be seen by inserting the second scenario value of (
3
) and noting that

\left\lvert\Sigma_{II}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert\left\lvert(\Sigma^{-1})_{JJ}\right\rvert.
However, if
K\neq\emptyset
, then the inner integration in the first scenario of (
3
) seems difficult to simplify (unless
(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}
is of a simple form, e.g., a diagonal block matrix) and thus it is hard to conclude in general whether
J
is not playing any role. Note in passing that if
K\neq\emptyset
and
J=\emptyset
, then the inner integration in the first scenario of (
3
) becomes

\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{\left\lvert K\right\rvert}}/\sqrt{\left\lvert(\Sigma^{-1})_{KK}\right\rvert}\ \cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{Y}_{K}<t_{0}^{-H}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K}-\Sigma_{KI}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}\right)y\right),

with
\boldsymbol{Y}_{K}\overset{d}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0}_{K},\Sigma_{KK}-\Sigma_{KI}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\Sigma_{IK}\right)
. This leads to a formulation of
\mathcal{C}_{K,\emptyset}
that is consistent with the constant involving
K
in
[
4
]
.

We conclude this section with an illustrative example, where we will see how the index sets
I,K,J
and the optimal point
t_{0}
are derived and how the different cases may appear. Our purpose of this example is not to be as genenral as possible, but to be restrictive so that it includes an interesting scenario.

Example 3.3
.

We consider a
4
-dimensional Gaussian process with independent (positive or negative) drifted fBm components. Precisely, let

d=4,\ \ \Sigma=\mathrm{Id},\ \ \nu_{i}>0,i=1,2,3,4,\ \ \mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0,\ \ \mu_{3},\mu_{4}<0.

We also assume that

(16)

\displaystyle\infty=:t^{\prime}_{3}>t^{\prime}_{2}:=\frac{\nu_{3}}{\left\lvert\mu_{3}\right\rvert}>\frac{\nu_{4}}{\left\lvert\mu_{4}\right\rvert}=:t^{\prime}_{1}>t^{\prime}_{0}:=0.

Denote

I_{1}=\{1,2,3,4\},\ \ I_{2}=\{1,2,3\},\ \ I_{3}=\{1,2\},\ \ I^{c}_{j}=\{1,2,3,4\}\setminus I_{j},\ j=1,2,3.

It can be seen that

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I_{j}}+t\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I_{j}}>\boldsymbol{0}_{I_{j}},\ \ \boldsymbol{\nu}_{I_{j}^{c}}+t\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I_{j}^{c}}\leq\boldsymbol{0}_{I_{j}^{c}},\ \ \ \ t\in[t^{\prime}_{j-1},t^{\prime}_{j})

and thus, by Lemma
2.1
,

I(t)=I_{j},\ t\in[t^{\prime}_{j-1},t^{\prime}_{j}),\ \ \ \ j=1,2,3.

Further, it follows from Lemma
2.2
that the optimal point
t_{0}
is eqaul to the

t_{0}^{(k)}
defined as (
8
) with
I_{k}
, such that (see (
9
))

g_{I_{k}}(t_{0}^{(k)})=\min_{j=1,2,3}g_{I_{j}}(t_{0}^{(j)}).

For illustration purpose, we shall assume that we have chosen the model parameters such that
k=3
. Thus, the essential index set is given by
I=I_{3}=\{1,2\}
and

t_{0}=t_{0}^{(3)}=\frac{\sqrt{4(\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\mu_{i})^{2}(1-2H)^{2}+16H(1-H)\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}^{2}\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}^{2}}-2(1-2H)\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\mu_{i}}{4\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}^{2}(1-H)}\in[t^{\prime}_{2},\infty).

We further assume that the model parameters were chosen such that
t_{0}=t^{\prime}_{2}
. In such a case, we have

I=\{1,2\},\ \ K=\{3\},\ \ J=\{4\}.

Next, let us discuss different cases distinguished according to
H\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}=(1-H)t_{0}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}
is valid or not.
Following the notation introduced at the beginning of this section,
H\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}=(1-H)t_{0}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}
means that
t_{0}=t_{0,1}=t_{0,2}
and
\zeta_{1}^{\prime}(t_{0})=\zeta_{2}^{\prime}(t_{0})=0.
In contrast,
H\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}\neq(1-H)t_{0}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}
means that
t_{0}
falls between
t_{0,1}
and
t_{0,2}
, and one of
\zeta^{\prime}_{1}(t_{0})
and
\zeta^{\prime}_{2}(t_{0})
is positive while the other is negative.
Consequently, we can obtain the exact asymptotics for

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}\forall_{i=1,\ldots,4}B_{H,i}(t)-\mu_{i}t>\nu_{i}u\right),\ \ u\to\infty,

by applying Theorem
3.1
, where, with
\Phi(\cdot)
denoting the standard normal distribution function,

\mathcal{C}_{K,J}=2\pi t_{0}^{2H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}g^{\prime\prime}_{I}(t_{0})y^{2}}\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_{3}}{t_{0}^{H}}y\right)dy.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

First note that by self-similarity of the fBms,

\displaystyle P(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}\boldsymbol{X}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}t>\boldsymbol{\nu}u\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\geq 0}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)u^{1-H}\right).

Hereafter, for simplicity we denote
v=u^{1-H}.
Furthermore, denote

\Delta_{v}=[t_{0}-\ln(v)/v,t_{0}+\ln(v)/v],\quad\widetilde{\Delta}_{v}=[0,\infty)\setminus[t_{0}-\ln(v)/v,t_{0}+\ln(v)/v].

It follows that

(17)

\displaystyle p(v)\leq P(u)\leq\Pi(v)+p(v),

where

p(v)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\in\Delta_{v}}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)v\right),\quad\Pi(v)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\in\widetilde{\Delta}_{v}}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)v\right).

The proof consists of two steps. In Step 1, we obtain the asymptotics of
p(v),v\to\infty
. In Step 2, we derive a suitable upper bound for
\Pi(v)
for all large enough
v
, which confirms asymptotic negligibility of

\Pi(v)
with respect to
p(v)
as
v\to\infty
.
The proof is then completed by combining these results. Without loss of generality, we shall only consider the most involved case where
K\neq\emptyset
and
J\neq\emptyset.

Before delving into all the details, for any
M\in(0,\infty]
we introduce

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{K,J,M}=\int_{[-M,M]}e^{-\frac{1}{4}g_{I}^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})y^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I^{c}\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{1}{2t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}}I_{\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{K}<y[[(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}]^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu})_{I^{c}}]_{K}\right)}d\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}dy.

We note that
\mathcal{C}_{K,J}
, given in (
3
) is actually equal to
\mathcal{C}_{K,J,\infty}
.

Step 1: Analysis of
p(v)

The idea is to split the interval
\Delta_{v}
into smaller intervals.
It turns out that we need to distinguish two different cases (i) and (ii) as stated in Theorem
3.1
, for case (i) we shall use intervals of the classical Pickands length, but for case (ii) we need to use intervals of a length that is shorter than the Pickands length. These two cases will be discussed seperately below.

Case (i):
H<{1}/{2}
, or
H>1/2
and
H\boldsymbol{\nu}_{I}=(1-H)t_{0}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{I}
.
Denote, for any fixed integer
T>0
and
v>0

\displaystyle\triangle_{j;v}=\triangle_{j;v}(T)=[t_{0}+jTv^{-1/H},t_{0}+(j+1)Tv^{-1/H}],\ \ \ -N_{v}-1\leq j\leq N_{v},

where
N_{v}=\lfloor T^{-1}\ln(v)v^{1/H-1}\rfloor

(we denote by
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
the floor function). Also denote
N_{v,M}=\lfloor T^{-1}Mv^{1/H-1}\rfloor
for any
M>0
.
By applying the Bonferroni’s inequality, we have

(19)

\displaystyle p_{1}(v)\geq p(v)\geq p_{2,M}(v)-\pi_{M}(v),

where

p_{1}(v)=\sum_{j=-N_{v}-1}^{N_{v}}p_{j;v},\ \ p_{2,M}(v)=\sum_{j=-N_{v,M}}^{N_{v,M}}p_{j;v},\ \ \pi_{M}(v)=\sum_{-N_{v,M}\leq j<l\leq N_{v,M}}p_{j,l;v},

with

p_{j;v}=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\in\Delta_{j;v}}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)v\right)

and

(20)

\displaystyle p_{j,l;v}=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\in\triangle_{j;v}}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)v,\ \exists_{t\in\triangle_{l;v}}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)v\right).

Next, we shall deal with the single-sum
p_{1}(v)
,
p_{2,M}(v)
and the double-sum
\pi_{M}(v)
, respectively. For the asymptotics of the single-sum terms, we shall use the following uniform version of a generalized Pickands lemma. The proof of Lemma
4.1
is displayed in Appendix.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
T>0
. We have, as
v\to\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists_{t\in[t_{0}+\tau v^{-1/H},t_{0}+(\tau+T)v^{-1/H}]}\boldsymbol{X}(t)>(\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}t)v\right)

\displaystyle\sim v^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{I}(T)}{\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0}+\tau v^{-1/H})}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I^{c}\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{1}{2t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}}I_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{K}<-(\tau v^{1-1/H})[[(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}]^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu})_{I^{c}}]_{K})}d\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}},

holds uniformly in
\tau
such that
\left\lvert\tau\right\rvert\leq T(N_{v}+1)
,
where
K
is the weakly essential index set defined in (
11
).

With Lemma
4.1
, it is straightforward to check that, as
v\to\infty,

(21)

\displaystyle p_{2,M}(v)\sim v^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert+1/H-1}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{I}(T)}{T}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{K,J,M}}{\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0})},

where
\mathcal{C}_{K,J,M}
is given in (
18
). Indeed, by Lemma
4.1

\displaystyle p_{2,M}(v)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle v^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert+1/H-1}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{I}(T)}{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I^{c}\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{1}{2t_{0}^{2H}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}}I_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{K}<-(jTv^{1-1/H})[[(\Sigma^{-1})_{I^{c}I^{c}}]^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu})_{I^{c}}]_{K})}d\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}.

Thus, the claim in (

\displaystyle p_{1}(v)\sim v^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert+1/H-1}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{I}(T)}{T}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{K,J}}{\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0})},

Next, we consider the term

, where it is sufficient to assume

\displaystyle p(\tau_{1},\tau_{2};v)=\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\exists\begin{array}[]{l}t\in[t_{0}+\tau_{1}v^{-1/H},t_{0}+(\tau_{1}+1)v^{-1/H}]\\

For any fixed

\displaystyle p(\tau_{1},\tau_{2};v)\leq C_{M}\exp\left(-C_{M}^{-1}(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})^{2H}\right)v^{-|I|}e^{-\frac{v^{2}g_{I}(t_{0})}{2}}.

holds uniformly in

Recall the defination of

in (

\displaystyle p(v)\sim v^{-\left\lvert I\right\rvert+1/H-1}\mathcal{H}_{I}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{K,J}}{\sqrt{(2\pi t_{0}^{2H})^{d}\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0})},\ \ v\to\infty.

\displaystyle v^{-|I|}\varphi_{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\left(v\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}}e^{(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1],t_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]:\boldsymbol{\chi}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})>\boldsymbol{x}\right)d\boldsymbol{x}.

Note that, similarly to (
43
),

\boldsymbol{\widetilde{w}}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\leq\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}

holds for all large
v
.

In order to find a tight upper bound for the integral in (
), similarly to the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we shall verify conditions of
[
12
, Lemma 8]
as stated in
(
44
)-(
46
) with
H
replaced by
1/2
.

Inequality (
44
)
.
Recall that
F\subseteq I=\{1,\ldots,d\}
is defined such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{F}>\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{I\setminus F}<\boldsymbol{0}
in the integral.
There exist
C_{M}>0
,
v_{M}>0
such that for all
v\geq v_{M}
and all
t_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1]
and
t_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]
,

v^{2}\biggl\lvert\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})-\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\biggr\rvert\leq\sup_{s\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1]\cup[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]}\biggl\lvert\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}_{I}(t_{0}+sv^{-2})\biggr\rvert\leq C_{M},

and therefore, by
(
50
), for any small
\epsilon>0

there exist
C_{M,\epsilon}>0
and
v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that for all
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}
and all
t_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1]
,
t_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]
,

\displaystyle\biggl\lvert\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})\right)\biggr\rvert=\biggl\lvert\left(-v^{2}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})+\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\widetilde{R}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}^{-1}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\left(-v^{2}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\boldsymbol{x}\right)\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq v^{2}\biggl\lvert\left(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\widetilde{R}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right)\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}^{-1}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\biggr\rvert+C_{M,\epsilon}+\epsilon\left|\boldsymbol{x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{M,\epsilon}+\epsilon((\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})+\left|\boldsymbol{x}\right|),

where in the last inequality we use (here
||A||=\max_{i,j}\left\lvert a_{ij}\right\rvert
for a matrix
A
, and (
50
) is applied)

\displaystyle v^{2}\left\Arrowvert\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\widetilde{R}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right\Arrowvert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\Arrowvert\begin{array}[]{cc}v^{2}(r_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{1})-r_{v}(t_{1},\tau_{1}))\Sigma_{V,V}&v^{2}(r_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-r_{v}(t_{1},\tau_{2}))\Sigma_{V,I\backslash V}\\
v^{2}(r_{v}(\tau_{2},\tau_{1})-r_{v}(t_{2},\tau_{1}))\Sigma_{I\backslash V,V}&v^{2}(r_{v}(\tau_{2},\tau_{2})-r_{v}(t_{2},\tau_{2}))\Sigma_{I\backslash V,I\backslash V}\end{array}\right\Arrowvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{M,\epsilon}+\epsilon(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}).

Thus, we conclude that, for any small
\epsilon>0

there exist
C_{M,\epsilon}>0
and
v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that for
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}
,
inequality (
44
) holds.

Inequality (
45
)
.
For any
t_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1]
and
t_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]
, we have, for the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})
, that

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})))=v^{2}\left(\widetilde{R}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2},t_{1},t_{2})-\widetilde{R}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}^{-1}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\widetilde{R}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},t_{1},t_{2})\right).

Using (
50
) and some tedious calculations, we can show that,
for any small
\epsilon>0
there exist
C_{M,\epsilon}>0
and
v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that for all
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}
and all
t_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1]
,
t_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]
,

\displaystyle||\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})))||\leq C_{M,\epsilon}+\epsilon(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}).

Thus, for any small
\epsilon>0

there exist
C_{M,\epsilon}>0
and
v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that for
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}

inequality (
45
) holds.

Inequality (
46
)
.
We have, using (
35
), that, for any
F\subseteq I,

(53)

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F}^{\top}\chi_{v,F}(t_{1},t_{2})-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F}^{\top}\chi_{v,F}(s_{1},s_{2})\right)\leq v^{2}\text{Var}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F}(t_{1},t_{2})-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F}(s_{1},s_{2})\right).

Further, it follows that there exist
C_{M}>0
,
v_{M}>0
such that for all
v\geq v_{M}
and all
t_{1},s_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1]
,
t_{2},s_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]
,

\displaystyle v^{2}\text{Var}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F}(t_{1},t_{2})-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F}(s_{1},s_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2v^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F\cap V}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F\cap V}(t_{1})-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F\cap V}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F\cap V}(s_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2v^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F\cap(I\backslash V)}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F\cap(I\backslash V)}(t_{2})-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{F\cap(I\backslash V)}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{v,F\cap(I\backslash V)}(s_{2})\right)

(54)

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}(|t_{1}-s_{1}|+|t_{2}-s_{2}|),

where the last inequality follows by an application of (
49
).
The above confirms that inequality (
46
) is satisfied.

To sum up,
we have checked that the conditions of
[
12
, Lemma 8]
are satisfied, and therefore,
for any small
\epsilon>0
there exist
C_{M,\epsilon},v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that for all
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}
,

(55)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}}e^{(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t_{1}\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{1}+1],t_{2}\in[\tau_{2},\tau_{2}+1]:\boldsymbol{\chi}_{v}(t_{1},t_{2})>\boldsymbol{x}\right)d\boldsymbol{x}\leq e^{C_{M,\epsilon}+\epsilon(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})}.

It remains to estimate
\varphi_{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\left(v\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right)
. By (
50
), we have, for any small
\epsilon>0
there exist
C_{M,\epsilon},v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that, for all
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}
,

(56)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varphi_{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\left(v\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right)&\leq C_{M,\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}^{\top}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}^{-1}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\right)\\
&\leq C_{M,\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}^{\top}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\epsilon(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\right).\end{aligned}

On the other hand, we have by Lagrange’s mean value theorem that, for some
x\in[\tau_{1},\tau_{2}]
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{v}^{\top}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\boldsymbol{b}_{v}^{\top}(\tau_{1},\tau_{1})\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{1})+2v^{-2}(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\boldsymbol{b}_{v}^{\top}(\tau_{1},x)\Sigma^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{V}\\
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}_{I\backslash V}(t_{0}+xv^{-2})\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, for any small
\epsilon>0
, there exists
v_{M,\epsilon}
such that, for all
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}

(57)

\displaystyle v^{2}\left\lvert\boldsymbol{b}_{v}^{\top}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-g_{I}(t_{0}+\tau_{1}v^{-2})-2v^{-2}(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{I}(t_{0})^{\top}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{V}\\
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}_{I\backslash V}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}\right\rvert\leq\epsilon(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}).

Now take
V=\{i\in I:\zeta^{\prime}_{i}(t_{0})\leq 0\}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}_{I}(t_{0})\neq\boldsymbol{0}_{I}
,
\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{I}(t_{0})>\boldsymbol{0}_{I}
and
2\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{I}(t_{0})^{\top}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}_{I}(t_{0})=g^{\prime}_{I}(t_{0})=0
, we know that both
V
and
I\backslash V
are non-empty. Hence, using once again that
\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{I}(t_{0})>\boldsymbol{0}_{I}
, we obtain

(58)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{I}(t_{0})^{\top}\Sigma_{II}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{V}\\
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}_{I\backslash V}(t_{0})\end{pmatrix}>0.

Therefore, combining (
56
)-(
58
) we have that, for any small
\epsilon>0
there exist
C,C_{M,\epsilon},v_{M,\epsilon}>0
such that for all
v\geq v_{M,\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}(v\boldsymbol{b}_{v}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{M,\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0}+\tau_{1}v^{-2})-C(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{M,\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{v^{2}}{2}g_{I}(t_{0})-C(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\right).

This, combined with (
) and (
55
), establishes the claim of the lemma.
