A Central Pattern Generator Network for Simple Control of Gait Transitions in Hexapod Robots based on Phase Reduction
Norihisa Namura, Hiroya Nakao

Abstract
We present a model of the central pattern generator (CPG) network that can control gait transitions in hexapod robots in a simple manner based on phase reduction. The CPG network consists of six weakly coupled limit-cycle oscillators, whose synchronization dynamics can be described by six phase equations through phase reduction. Focusing on the transitions between the hexapod gaits with specific symmetries, the six phase equations of the CPG network can further be reduced to two independent equations for the phase differences. By choosing appropriate coupling functions for the network, we can achieve desired synchronization dynamics regardless of the detailed properties of the limit-cycle oscillators used for the CPG. The effectiveness of our CPG network is demonstrated by numerical simulations of gait transitions between the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits, using the FitzHugh-Nagumo oscillator as the CPG unit.

Introduction

Legged locomotion is a key ability for many terrestrial organisms.
For instance, humans use bipedal locomotion, cats and horses use quadruped locomotion, and insects use hexapodal locomotion.
To adapt to various terrains or to change the speed of locomotion,
gait transitions often occur in vertebrates (e.g., horses
[

[

Some neurophysiological experiments, such as the one for decerebrated cats
[

are generated in the neuronal network called the

[

In invertebrates, CPGs also exist as distributed neuronal circuits that control gait patterns

7

The CPG can produce self-sustained rhythms for generating basic gait patterns without external stimulation or sensory feedback,
and it can further be modulated by the signals from the brain or sensory organs
to switch between various rhythmic patterns, i.e., gait patterns
[
8
]
.

Compared to wheeled robots, legged robots have the advantage of adapting to uneven terrains and physical environments more efficiently,
and many studies have been conducted on biped robots
[

, quadruped robots
[

]
, hexapod robots
[

,
12

, and salamander robots
[
13

.
In particular, hexapod robots are more stable than biped or quadruped robots and have been applied, e.g., to humanitarian demining
[
14
]
and exploration of the surface of Mars
[
15
]
.
Since gait transitions are also important for hexapod robots,
methods for realizing typical insect gaits and transitions between them using hexapod robots have been studied

For controlling the locomotion of legged robots, CPG-based control strategy is an efficient bio-inspired method
because it provides self-oscillations and requires less computational costs

CPG networks are widely modeled as coupled limit-cycle oscillators.
In rhythmic systems of mutually coupled limit-cycle oscillators like the CPG networks, synchronization can occur, leading to the alignment of their rhythms with each other.
Synchronization of weakly coupled limit-cycle oscillators can be analyzed by the phase reduction theory
[

,

,
27

which can approximately reduce the nonlinear multidimensional dynamics of the oscillator to a single-variable phase equation characterized by the natural frequency and phase response property of the oscillator.
Phase reduction can also be useful for engineering limit-cycle oscillators,

39

In this study, we present a CPG network for controlling gait transitions in hexapod robots, which allows various types of smooth limit-cycle oscillators to be used as the CPG unit.
We use phase reduction to derive six phase equations from the mathematical model of the CPG network consisting of six limit-cycle oscillators with weak coupling.
Using the symmetries of the typical hexapod gaits, i.e., the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits, we further reduce the six phase equations to two independent phase-difference equations.
By choosing appropriate coupling functions for the network, we can achieve all typical gaits irrespective of the detailed properties of the limit-cycle oscillators.
Compared to existing studies, our CPG network provides a simpler and more general framework in which the CPG model and coupling functions can be optimized.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of our CPG network by numerical simulations of gait transitions between the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits, adopting the FitzHugh-Nagumo oscillator as the CPG unit.

Phase dynamics of the CPG network

We assume that the dynamics of the six limit-cycle oscillators in the CPG network (Fig.

\dot{\bm{X}}_{6}={}&s\bm{F}(\bm{X}_{6})+\varepsilon\left(c_{1}\tilde{\bm{H}}_{1}(\bm{X}_{6},\bm{X}_{3})+c_{2}\tilde{\bm{H}}_{2}(\bm{X}_{6},\bm{X}_{5})\right),\end{aligned}

(1)

where
\bm{X}_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\;(k=1,2,3,4,5,6)

t
,

\bm{X}

,

\tilde{\bm{H}}_{1,2}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
represent smooth coupling functions between the oscillators.
We assume that the original system
\dot{\bm{X}}=\bm{F}(\bm{X})
without scaling (
s=1
) has an exponentially stable limit cycle
\chi
of natural period
T
and frequency
\omega=2\pi/T
,
where the state on
\chi
at time
t
is represented as
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{0}(t)
and satisfies
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{0}(t)=\tilde{\bm{X}}_{0}(t+T)
,
given the origin state
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{0}(0)
on the limit cycle.
Then, the period and frequency of the limit cycle
\chi
of the time-rescaled vector field
s\bm{F}
are
T/s
and
s\omega
, respectively.

The mathematical model of weakly coupled identical limit-cycle oscillators can be analyzed by phase reduction theory
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
We first introduce an asymptotic phase function
\Theta:\mathcal{B}\to[0,2\pi)
into the basin
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
\chi

satisfying
\nabla\Theta(\bm{X})\cdot\bm{F}(\bm{X})=\omega
for all
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{B}
,
where
\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}b_{n}
represents the scalar product of two vectors
\bm{a}
,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Using the phase function, we can define the phase value
\theta
of the oscillator state
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{B}
by
\theta=\Theta(\bm{X})
,
which increases constantly at the natural frequency
\omega
over time,

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}(t)=\nabla\Theta(\bm{X}(t))\cdot\bm{F}(\bm{X}(t))=\omega,

(2)

where we regard that the phase values
0
and
2\pi
are identical.
The state on
\chi
can be represented by
\bm{X}_{0}(\theta)=\tilde{\bm{X}}_{0}(t=\theta/\omega)
as a
2\pi
-periodic function of
\theta
,
which satisfies
\bm{X}_{0}(\theta)=\bm{X}_{0}(\theta+2\pi)
.

Since all coupling terms are of
O(\varepsilon)
and sufficiently weak, we can assume that the state of each limit-cycle oscillator is close to
\chi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{k}(t)=\bm{X}_{0}(\theta_{k}(t))+O(\varepsilon)\quad(k=1,2,3,4,5,6),

(3)

where
\theta_{k}=\Theta(\bm{X}_{k})
for
k=1,2,3,4,5,6
.
Substituting Eq. (
3
) into Eq. (
1
) and neglecting errors of
O(\varepsilon^{2})
, we can derive the following phase equations:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\theta}_{1}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{1})\cdot\left(b_{1}c_{1}\bm{H}_{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{4})+b_{2}c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\right),\\
\dot{\theta}_{2}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{2})\cdot b_{1}c_{1}\bm{H}_{1}(\theta_{2},\theta_{5}),\\
&+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{2})\cdot\left(c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})+b_{2}c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{2},\theta_{3})\right),\\
\dot{\theta}_{3}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{3})\cdot\left(b_{1}c_{1}\bm{H}_{1}(\theta_{3},\theta_{6})+c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{3},\theta_{2})\right),\\
\dot{\theta}_{4}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{4})\cdot\left(c_{1}\bm{H}_{1}(\theta_{4},\theta_{1})+b_{2}c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{4},\theta_{5})\right),\\
\dot{\theta}_{5}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{5})\cdot c_{1}\bm{H}_{1}(\theta_{5},\theta_{2}),\\
&+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{5})\cdot\left(c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{5},\theta_{4})+b_{2}c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{5},\theta_{6})\right),\\
\dot{\theta}_{6}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon\bm{Z}(\theta_{6})\cdot\left(c_{1}\bm{H}_{1}(\theta_{6},\theta_{3})+c_{2}\bm{H}_{2}(\theta_{6},\theta_{5})\right).\end{aligned}

(4)

Note that the frequency of each oscillator is scaled to
s\omega
.
Here,
\bm{Z}:[0,2\pi)\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the phase sensitivity function (PSF) of the oscillator defined by
\bm{Z}(\theta)=\left.\nabla\Theta(\bm{X})\right|_{\bm{X}=\bm{X}_{0}(\theta)}
, which characterizes the linear response of the oscillator phase
\theta
to small perturbations.
The PSF can be calculated as the
2\pi
-periodic solution to the following adjoint equation
[
26
,
40
]
:

\displaystyle\omega\frac{d}{d\theta}\bm{Z}(\theta)=-\bm{J}(\bm{X}_{0}(\theta))^{\top}\bm{Z}(\theta),

(5)

where
\bm{J}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the Jacobian matrix of the vector field
\bm{F}
, i.e.,
\bm{J}(\bm{X})=\nabla\bm{F}(\bm{X})
.
The PSF satisfies the following normalization condition:

\displaystyle\bm{Z}(\theta)\cdot\frac{d\bm{X}_{0}(\theta)}{d\theta}=1.

(6)

The functions
\bm{H}_{1,2}:[0,2\pi)\times[0,2\pi)\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
are mutual coupling functions (MCFs) defined by

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{1,2}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{H}}_{1,2}(\bm{X}_{0}(\theta_{i}),\bm{X}_{0}(\theta_{j})),

(7)

which depend on two phases
\theta_{i}
and
\theta_{j}
for
i,j\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
.
From Eq. (
3
),

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{H}}_{1,2}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})

\displaystyle=\bm{H}_{1,2}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})+O(\varepsilon)

(8)

holds for
i,j\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
.

Since the frequency of the oscillator is
s\omega
and therefore the relative phase
\theta_{k}-s\omega t
is a slow variable,
we can conduct the averaging approximation
[
23
,
24
,
27
]

over one period
T/s
and obtain the following averaged phase equations by neglecting errors of
O(\varepsilon^{2})
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\theta}_{1}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon(b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4})+b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})),\\
\dot{\theta}_{2}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{5})\\
&+\varepsilon(c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})+b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{3})),\\
\dot{\theta}_{3}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon(b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{3}-\theta_{6})+c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{3}-\theta_{2})),\\
\dot{\theta}_{4}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon(c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{4}-\theta_{1})+b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{4}-\theta_{5})),\\
\dot{\theta}_{5}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{5}-\theta_{2})\\
&+\varepsilon(c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{5}-\theta_{4})+b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{5}-\theta_{6})),\\
\dot{\theta}_{6}={}&s\omega+\varepsilon(c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{6}-\theta_{3})+c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\theta_{6}-\theta_{5})),\\
\end{aligned}

(9)

where the functions
\Gamma_{1,2}
are called phase coupling functions (PCFs), which are
2\pi
-periodic functions represented by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1,2}(\varphi)=\left\langle\bm{Z}(\psi)\cdot\bm{H}_{1,2}(\psi,\psi-\varphi)\right\rangle_{\psi}.

(10)

Here, we denote the averaging of a smooth
2\pi
-periodic function
g
over one period of oscillation as

\displaystyle\left\langle g(\psi)\right\rangle_{\psi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(\psi)d\psi.

(11)

Now, we assume that the MCFs are given in the following specific form:

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{1,2}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=\frac{\bm{Z}(\theta_{i})P_{1,2}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})}{\left\langle\left\|\bm{Z}(\psi)\right\|^{2}\right\rangle_{\psi}},

(12)

where
P_{1,2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are arbitrary
2\pi
-periodic smooth functions.
As discussed in
[
36
]
, these MCFs give the most energy-efficient forms of the mutual coupling that maximize the absolute value of the corresponding PCFs at each value of the phase difference under a given power, leading to fast mutual synchronization of the oscillators.
Introducing
\varphi=\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
, the PCFs are obtained from the above MCFs as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1,2}(\varphi)=P_{1,2}(\varphi).

(13)

By choosing the functional forms of
P_{1,2}(\varphi)
, we can realize any forms of
\Gamma_{1,2}
.
Thus, we need to consider only the PCFs
\Gamma_{1,2}
in designing the CPG network, and then MCFs
\bm{H}_{1,2}
follow from Eq. (
12
).
We note that, in general, the functional form of the PCF depends on both the PSF and MCF.
For the above particular MCF, the PCF become independent of the PSF and we can arbitrarily design it to realize desired synchronization dynamics.

II.2
Symmetric gait patterns

Next, we describe the hexapod gaits considered in this study.
The six legs of the hexapod robots are called the left front (LF), left middle (LM), left hind (LH), right front (RF), right middle (RM), and right hind (RH), as shown in Fig.
1
.
We consider
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4},\theta_{5},\theta_{6})
to be the phases of (LF, LM, LH, RF, RM, RH), respectively.

We assume that the hexapod gaits have the following symmetries:

\displaystyle\theta_{6}-\theta_{3}=\theta_{5}-\theta_{2}=\theta_{4}-\theta_{1}

\displaystyle\mod 2\pi,

(14)

\displaystyle\theta_{3}-\theta_{2}=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}=\theta_{6}-\theta_{5}=\theta_{5}-\theta_{4}

\displaystyle\mod 2\pi,\begin{aligned} \end{aligned}

(15)

where Eq. (
14
) indicates that the phase differences between the contralateral legs in the front, middle, and hind positions are equal,
and Eq. (
15
) indicates that the phase differences between the adjacent ipsilateral legs are equal, i.e.,
the phase shift of the middle legs from the front legs is the same as that of the hind legs from the middle legs on both sides.

As an example, we consider three typical hexapod gaits, i.e., the slowest gait called the
wave gait
, the middle-speed gait called the
tetrapod gait
, and the fastest gait called the
tripod gait

[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.
Figure
2
shows the diagrams of the three gaits, where the states (stance or swing) of the six legs are plotted in gray and black colors, respectively.
In the wave gait (also called a metachronal gait), the hexapod robot moves the six legs, i.e., (RH), (RM), (RF), (LH), (LM), and (LF), in order at equal phase intervals of
\pi/3
.
In the tetrapod gait, the legs form three synchronized pairs, i.e., (LH, RM), (LM, RF), and (LF, RH), which are moved in order at equal phase intervals of

.
Finally, in the tripod gait, the legs form two synchronized triplets, (RF, LM, RH) and (LF, RM, LH), which are alternatively moved (i.e., with the phase interval of

The phase shifts of the legs in the three types of the gaits correspond to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4},\theta_{5},\theta_{6})\\
&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(0,\frac{1}{3}\pi,\frac{2}{3}\pi,\pi,\frac{4}{3}\pi,\frac{5}{3}\pi\right)\quad(\textrm{wave}),\\
\\
\left(0,\frac{2}{3}\pi,\frac{4}{3}\pi,\frac{2}{3}\pi,\frac{4}{3}\pi,0\right)\quad(\textrm{tetrapod}),\\
\\
\left(0,\pi,0,\pi,0,\pi\right)\quad(\textrm{tripod}),\end{array}\right.\end{aligned}

(16)

when the LF is chosen as the reference,

We can find that all the three gaits satisfy the symmetries given in Eqs. (

) and (
15
).
We note that other hexapod gaits are also possible as described in Appendix

II.3
Equations for the phase differences

We now show that the six phase equations can be reduced to two independent phase-difference equations by using the symmetries (

) and (
15
) of the gaits.
First, we consider the symmetry of the phase differences between the contralateral legs given in Eq. (

), which yields

\displaystyle\theta_{k+3}-\theta_{m+3}=\theta_{k}-\theta_{m}\mod 2\pi\quad(k,m=1,2,3).

(17)

Plugging Eqs. (
14
) and (
17
) into Eq. (

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{k+3}-\dot{\theta}_{k}=\varepsilon c_{1}\left(\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{k+3}-\theta_{k})-b_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{k+3})\right).

determining excitatory or inhibitory coupling should be chosen as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}b_{2}=1&\quad\mathrm{if\;}\Gamma_{2}\mathrm{\;is\;an\;odd\;function},\\
b_{2}=-1&\quad\mathrm{if\;}\Gamma_{2}\mathrm{\;is\;an\;even\;function}.\end{array}\right.

Thus, the dynamics of the six phases can be described by two independent equations for the phase differences
\alpha
between the contralateral legs and
\beta
between the adjacent ipsilateral legs,

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon c_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)-b_{1}\Gamma_{1}(-\alpha))\coloneqq\varepsilon\Gamma_{\mathrm{alpha}}(\alpha),

(28)

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\beta)\coloneqq\varepsilon\Gamma_{\mathrm{beta}}(\beta).

(29)

By controlling
\alpha
and
\beta
, we can realize different gait patterns.
The fixed point
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
of Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
) for each gait is

\displaystyle(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\pi,\frac{1}{3}\pi\right)\quad(\textrm{wave}),\\
\\
\left(\frac{2}{3}\pi,\frac{2}{3}\pi\right)\quad(\textrm{tetrapod}),\\
\\
\left(\pi,\pi\right)\quad(\textrm{tripod}).\end{array}\right.

For these fixed points corresponding to the individual gaits to be realizable, they should be linearly stable.
Moreover, we need the stability of the periodic solution satisfying the symmetries (
14
) and (
15
).
These will be analyzed in the next section.

II.4
Stability analysis

We first discuss the stability conditions for the periodic solution of the CPG network dynamics satisfying the symmetries (
14
) and (
15
).
We then describe the conditions for the fixed point
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
to ensure that the gaits are stable.

For the periodic solution of the CPG to maintain the symmetry (
14
), the variable
\bm{\delta}=\left[\delta_{1}\;\delta_{2}\right]
defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{41}-\varphi_{52},

(35)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{63}-\varphi_{52}

(36)

should be stable at the fixed point
\bm{\delta}=[0\;0]\mod 2\pi
, where
\varphi_{41}=\theta_{4}-\theta_{1}
,
\varphi_{52}=\theta_{5}-\theta_{2}
, and
\varphi_{63}=\theta_{6}-\theta_{3}
.
Hereafter in this section, for simplicity, we omit writing ‘mod
2\pi
’ for the fixed points.
From Eq. (
18
), the dynamics of
\delta_{1,2}
are represented as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\delta}_{1}={}&\dot{\varphi}_{41}-\dot{\varphi}_{52}\\
={}&c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\varphi_{41})-b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(-\varphi_{41})\\
&-c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\varphi_{52})+b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(-\varphi_{52}),\end{aligned}

(37)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\delta}_{2}={}&\dot{\varphi}_{63}-\dot{\varphi}_{52}\\
={}&c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\varphi_{63})-b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(-\varphi_{63})\\
&-c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(\varphi_{52})+b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma_{1}(-\varphi_{52}),\end{aligned}

(38)

respectively.
The fixed point
\bm{\delta}=0
is linearly stable if all eigenvalues of
the Jacobian
\partial\dot{\bm{\delta}}/\partial\bm{\delta}
have negative real parts
at
\bm{\delta}=0
.
By using the chain rule of derivatives, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\dot{\bm{\delta}}}{\partial\bm{\delta}}&=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{1}}{\partial\delta_{1}}&\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{1}}{\partial\delta_{2}}\\
\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{2}}{\partial\delta_{1}}&\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{2}}{\partial\delta_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{1}}{\partial\varphi_{41}}-\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{1}}{\partial\varphi_{52}}&\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{1}}{\partial\varphi_{63}}-\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{1}}{\partial\varphi_{52}}\\
\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{2}}{\partial\varphi_{41}}-\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{2}}{\partial\varphi_{52}}&\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{2}}{\partial\varphi_{63}}-\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{2}}{\partial\varphi_{52}}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\varepsilon(c_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha)+b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(-\alpha))\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
1&2\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}

(39)

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
\partial\dot{\bm{\delta}}/\partial\bm{\delta}
are negative if

\displaystyle\varepsilon(c_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha)+b_{1}c_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(-\alpha))<0.

(40)

If we use an odd or even function as
\Gamma_{1}
, this condition can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\varepsilon c_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha)<0

(41)

by taking
b_{1}
as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}b_{1}=1&\quad\mathrm{if\;}\Gamma_{1}\mathrm{\;is\;an\;odd\;function},\\
b_{1}=-1&\quad\mathrm{if\;}\Gamma_{1}\mathrm{\;is\;an\;even\;function}.\end{array}\right.

Since
\varepsilon>0
and
c_{1}>0
,
\bm{\delta}=\bm{0}
is stable if

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha)<0.

(44)

This condition cannot be satisfied for the whole range of
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
, but
it is sufficient if this condition holds in an interval
\alpha\in[\alpha_{\mathrm{init}},\alpha^{*}]
(or
[\alpha^{*},\alpha_{\mathrm{init}}]
),
where
\alpha_{\mathrm{init}}
and
\alpha^{*}
are the initial value and the fixed point of
\alpha
, respectively.

From now on, we assume that the above condition is satisfied.
For the symmetry (
15
), or equivalently (
22
) of the periodic solution to be kept, the variable
\delta_{3}
defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{3}=\varphi_{12}+\varphi_{32}

(45)

should be stable at the fixed point
\delta_{3}=0
.
By adding Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
) and using Eq. (
25
),
the rate of change
\dot{\delta}_{3}
at
\delta_{3}=0
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\delta}_{3}={}&\varepsilon\left(b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\varphi_{12})-2c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(-\varphi_{12})\right)\\
&+\varepsilon\left(c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(\varphi_{32})-2b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma_{2}(-\varphi_{32})\right)\\
={}&\varepsilon c_{2}(-\Gamma_{2}(\beta)-b_{2}\Gamma_{2}(-\beta))\\
={}&0.\end{aligned}

(46)

The variable
\delta_{3}=0
is linearly stable if

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{3}}{\partial\delta_{3}}={}&\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{3}}{\partial\varphi_{12}}+\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{3}}{\partial\varphi_{32}}\\
={}&\varepsilon\left(b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(\varphi_{12})+2c_{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(-\varphi_{12})\right)\\
&+\varepsilon\left(c_{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(\varphi_{32})+2b_{2}c_{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(-\varphi_{32})\right)\\
={}&3\varepsilon c_{2}(\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(\beta)+b_{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(-\beta))<0,\end{aligned}

(47)

where we used the chain rule of derivatives.
This quantity can be calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\dot{\delta}_{3}}{\partial\delta_{3}}=6\varepsilon c_{2}\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(\beta)

(48)

for both odd and even functions of
\Gamma_{2}
using the condition (
II.3
).
Since
\varepsilon>0
and
c_{2}>0
,
\delta_{3}=0
is stable if

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(\beta)<0.

(49)

Although this condition does not hold for all
\beta\in[0,2\pi)
,
it is sufficient if this condition holds in the interval
\beta\in[\beta_{\mathrm{init}},\beta^{*}]
(or
[\beta^{*},\beta_{\mathrm{init}}]
),
where
\beta_{\mathrm{init}}
and
\beta^{*}
are the initial value and the fixed point, respectively.

We have shown the conditions for the linear stability of the symmetric periodic orbit.
We now focus on the stability of the fixed point
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
.
The fixed point
\alpha=\alpha^{*}
is linearly stable if the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{alpha}}(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=c_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(\alpha^{*})-b_{1}\Gamma_{1}(-\alpha^{*}))=0,

(50)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{alpha}}(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=c_{1}(\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{*})+b_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(-\alpha^{*}))<0.

(51)

If we use an odd or even function for
\Gamma_{1}
and choose
b_{1}
as given by the condition (
II.4
),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=0,

(52)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle<0

(53)

are the conditions for the linear stability.
The stability condition (
53
) for the fixed point
\alpha^{*}
is consistent with the condition (
44
) for the symmetry (
14
).

The fixed point
\beta=\beta^{*}
is linearly stable if the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(\beta^{*})

\displaystyle=0,

(54)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}_{2}(\beta^{*})

\displaystyle<0,

(55)

which follow from
c_{2}>0
and Eq. (
29
).
The stability condition (
55
) for the fixed point
\beta^{*}
is consistent with the condition (
49
) for the symmetry (
15
).

III
Gait control methods

In this section, we explain the methods for gait control.
Each gait is generated by moving the legs when the corresponding phase values exceed a threshold, where the timing is adjusted by a mutually synchronized solution of the CPGs with appropriate PCFs
\Gamma_{1,2}
.
Gait transitions can be conducted by changing the PCFs
\Gamma_{1,2}
between odd and even functions to switch the fixed points and by alternating
b_{1,2}
according to the conditions (
II.4
) and (
II.3
).
The timescale parameter
s
is adjusted so that the duration of the swing phase is the same for all of the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits.

III.1
Determining gaits from CPG outputs

We first describe the method for determining the swing or stance phase from the CPG output.
We choose one component
x_{\mathrm{out}}
of the CPG unit (oscillator) as the output, although the oscillator itself is multidimensional.
The corresponding leg is in the swing phase if the output
x_{\mathrm{out}}
is above a threshold
\sigma
and in the stance phase otherwise
[
20
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{swing}&\mathrm{if}\quad x_{\mathrm{out}}>\sigma,\\
\mathrm{stance}&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

We assume the output
x_{\mathrm{out}}
to have only two extremum points within one period.
The threshold
\sigma
for each gait is determined to realize the following duty factor
r_{\mathrm{DF}}
:

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{DF}}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{st}}}{T_{\mathrm{sw}}+T_{\mathrm{st}}},

(58)

where
T_{\mathrm{sw}}
and
T_{\mathrm{st}}
represent the durations of the swing phase and stance phase in one gait period
T/s
, respectively,
where
T_{\mathrm{sw}}+T_{\mathrm{st}}=T/s
holds.
The duty factors for the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits are chosen as
5/6
,
2/3
, and
1/2
, respectively.

The timescale parameter
s
is chosen as

\displaystyle s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{6}\quad(\textrm{wave}),\\
\\
\frac{1}{3}\quad(\textrm{tetrapod}),\\
\\
\frac{1}{2}\quad(\textrm{tripod}),\end{array}\right.

so that the duration of the swing phase of each leg is the same for all of the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits.

III.2
Functional forms of the coupling functions

We here describe the odd function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}
and even function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}
that we use for gait transitions.
Both of these functions are necessary, because for the tripod gait with the fixed point
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})=(\pi,\pi)
,
any
2\pi
-periodic odd function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}(\varphi)
has a fixed point at
\varphi=\pi
, preventing the tripod gait from switching to other gaits,
and any
2\pi
-periodic even function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}(\varphi)
satisfies
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}(\pi)=0
, where the linear stability of
\varphi=\pi
is not guaranteed, i.e., tripod gait is not linearly stable.
We thus switch the odd and even functions to realize gait transitions.

Since the fixed points of the three gaits are
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})=

(\pi,\pi/3)
,
(2\pi/3,2\pi/3)
, and
(\pi,\pi)
,
there can be six cases of transitions:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{3}\pi\to\frac{2}{3}\pi,&\frac{2}{3}\pi\to\frac{1}{3}\pi,\\
\\
\frac{1}{3}\pi\to\pi,&\pi\to\frac{1}{3}\pi,\\
\\
\frac{2}{3}\pi\to\pi,&\pi\to\frac{2}{3}\pi.\end{matrix}

(64)

Here, we choose the odd function as the PCFs
\Gamma_{1,2}
for the transitions
\pi/3\to\pi
and
2\pi/3\to\pi
because the
2\pi
-periodic odd function always has a fixed point at
\pi
.
We choose the even function for other transitions.

We next present the odd function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}
and even function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}
used in this study.
We use an odd function defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}(\varphi)=10\sum_{k=1}^{10}k\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{2}\right)\sin(k\varphi),

(65)

which is shown in Fig.
3
(a).
This odd function has only one stable fixed point at
\varphi^{*}=\pi
in
(-\pi,\pi]
, which satisfies the stability condition (
53
).
Also, this odd function satisfies
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}(\varphi)<0
in
\varphi\in[\pi/3,\pi]

for the stability conditions (
44
) and (
49
).
We define the even function by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}(\varphi)=2\cos(\varphi)+1,

(66)

which is shown in Fig.
3
(b).
This even function has only one stable fixed point at
\varphi^{*}=2\pi/3
in
(-\pi,\pi]
, which satisfies the stability condition (
55
).
Also, this even function satisfies
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}(\varphi)<0
in
\varphi\in[\pi/3,\pi]

for the stability conditions (
44
) and (
49
).
We note that, if we consider the transitions between other gaits as given in Appendix A, we need to design other even functions depending on the target fixed point.

III.3
Gait generation and gait transitions

We here explain the method for determining the PCF
\Gamma_{1,2}
and
b_{1,2}
for the gait transitions of the wave, tetrapod, and tripod gaits.

III.3.1
Wave gait

Since the fixed point is
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})=(\pi,\pi/3)
,
we let
\Gamma_{1}
be the odd function and
\Gamma_{2}
be the even function:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{1}(\alpha)&=\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}(\alpha),\\
\Gamma_{2}(\beta)&=-\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}(\beta+\pi),\end{aligned}

(67)

where
\Gamma_{2}(\beta)
has a stable fixed point at
\beta^{*}=\pi/3
.
The associated parameters
b_{1,2}
are determined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} b_{1}&=1,\\
b_{2}&=-1\end{aligned}

(68)

from the conditions (
II.4
) and (
II.3
).

III.3.2
Tetrapod gait

Since the fixed point is
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})=(2\pi/3,2\pi/3)
in this case,
we choose both
\Gamma_{1,2}
as the even functions:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{1}(\alpha)&=\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}(\alpha),\\
\Gamma_{2}(\beta)&=\Gamma_{\mathrm{even}}(\beta).\end{aligned}

(69)

The associated parameters
b_{1,2}
are determined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} b_{1}&=-1,\\
b_{2}&=-1\end{aligned}

(70)

from the conditions (
II.4
) and (
II.3
).

III.3.3
Tripod gait

Since the fixed point is
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})=(\pi,\pi)
,
we choose both
\Gamma_{1,2}
as the odd functions:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{1}(\alpha)&=\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}(\alpha),\\
\Gamma_{2}(\beta)&=\Gamma_{\mathrm{odd}}(\beta).\end{aligned}

(71)

The associated parameters
b_{1,2}
are determined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} b_{1}&=1,\\
b_{2}&=1\end{aligned}

(72)

from the conditions (
II.4
) and (
II.3
).

V
Conclusions

In this study, we presented a simple CPG network that can control the gait transitions in hexapod between the different types of gaits whose symmetries are described in Sec.
II
B.
In our CPG network, the dynamics of the six CPG oscillators can be reduced to the dynamics of two independent phase differences
between the contralateral legs on the left and right sides and between the adjacent ipsilateral legs on both sides.
By appropriately assuming the functional form of the mutual coupling functions,
the desired synchronization dynamics can be achieved irrespective of the detailed properties of the oscillators.
Also, since the coupling between the oscillators is assumed weak, the output signals of the CPGs maintain approximately constant amplitudes and do not hinder the determination of the swing-stance phase.
We demonstrated that our CPG network can achieve the wave-tetrapod-tripod gait transitions (speed increasing) and tripod-tetrapod-wave gait transitions (speed decreasing) smoothly by numerical simulations.
The present CPG network is also applicable to other gait patterns in Appendix
A
, which possess the symmetries described in Sec.
II
B.

The advantage of the present CPG framework is the ability to provide a general CPG network solely using two independent phase-difference dynamics.
Therefore, the performance of the gait transitions can easily be optimized, compared to most existing studies that rely on specific models of the CPG oscillators.
At the same time, since the present framework allows us to use various types of limit-cycle oscillators as the CPG unit,
we can employ not only existing models of oscillators but also artificially designed oscillators
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
as the CPG unit.
In our future work, we will focus on optimizing the coupling functions within the present CPG network to achieve faster gait transitions.
