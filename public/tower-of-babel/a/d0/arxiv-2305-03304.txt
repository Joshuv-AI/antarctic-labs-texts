Functional integrals in geometric approach to quantum theory
I. Frolov

Abstract
In quantum mechanics, one can express the evolution operator and other quantities in terms of functional integrals. The main goal of this paper is to prove corresponding results in geometric approach to quantum theory. We apply these results to the formalism of L-functionals.



Keywords
functional integral; Banach space; geometric approach

2
Functional integrals

Let us consider symbols of linear operators acting in the space
\cal L.

The evolution operator can be represented in the form

\sigma(t)=e^{tH}=\lim_{N\to\infty}(1+\frac{tH}{N})^{N}.

For
N\to\infty
the symbol of the operator
1+{\frac{tH}{N}}
can be approximated by
\exp{\frac{t}{N}}\underline{H}
:

\underline{1+\frac{tH}{N}}=e^{\frac{t}{N}\underline{H}}+O(N^{-2}).

Using this relation we obtain an expression for the symbol of the evolution operator;

\underline{\sigma(t)}=\lim_{N\to\infty}I_{N}(t),

(2)

where

I_{N}(t)=e^{\frac{t}{N}\underline{H}}*...*e^{\frac{t}{N}\underline{H}}

(
N
factors).

In many cases
I_{N}(t)
can be interpreted as an approximation to a functional integral. Notice, however, that even without this interpretation we can apply the Laplace or stationary phase method to the calculation of
I_{N}(t)
. This allows us to obtain some results that often are obtained in the language of functional integrals without using this language.

Let us consider a class of symbols generalizing
q-p
symbols and Wick symbols of quantum mechanics.

We start with complete topological vector space
\cal L.

We assume that the symbol of an operator
A
acting in
\cal L
is a (generalized) function
\underline{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})
of two variables (a function on
{\cal M}\times\cal M^{\prime}
) and that the
symbol of the product
C=AB
of operators
A
and
B
can be expressed in terms of the symbols of operators
A
and
B
by the formula

\underline{C}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\int d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}\underline{B}(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\underline{A}(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})e^{c(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})+c(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})-c(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})},

(3)

where
c(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}),R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
are functions on
\cal M\times\cal M^{\prime}
and
d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}
is the measure on this space. (We assume that
\cal M\times\cal M^{\prime}
is a measure space. This assumption can be weakened to allow infinite-dimensional spaces with integration defined for some class of functions on these spaces.)
Taking
A=B=C=1
in (
3

1=\int d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}e^{c(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})+c(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})-c(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})}R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}).

(4)

If
R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
is represented in the form

R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=e^{-r(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})}

the formulas (
3

4

\underline{C}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\int d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}\underline{B}(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})\underline{A}(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})e^{c(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})+c(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})-c(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})-r(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})},

(5)

1=\int d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}e^{c(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})+c(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})-c(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})-r(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})}.

(6)

It follows that the symbol
\underline{C}(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})
of the product
C

N

\underline{C}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=\int d\gamma_{1}d\gamma^{\prime}_{1}...d\gamma_{N-1}d\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1}\underline{A}_{N}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1})...\underline{A}_{2}(\gamma_{2},\gamma^{\prime}_{1})\underline{A}_{1}(\gamma_{1},\gamma^{\prime})e^{\rho_{N}},

(7)

\rho_{N}=c(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1})+c(\gamma_{N-1},\gamma^{\prime}_{N-2})+...+c(\gamma_{1},\gamma^{\prime})-c(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})-r(\gamma_{1},\gamma^{\prime}_{1})-...-r(\gamma_{N-1},\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1}).

(8)

We see that in our case

I_{N}(t)=\int d\gamma_{1}d\gamma^{\prime}_{1}...d\gamma_{N-1}d\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1}e^{\frac{t}{N}(\underline{H}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1})+\underline{H}(\gamma_{N-1},\gamma^{\prime}_{N-2})+...+\underline{H}(\gamma_{1},\gamma^{\prime}))}e^{\rho_{N}}.

(9)

Notice that assuming that operators at hand have trace we can express the trace in terms of symbols:

TrA=\int d\alpha d\beta^{\prime}\underline{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})e^{\tau(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})},

(10)

where
\tau(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=c(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})-r(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})

To justify this formula we verify that

(12)

(13)

Let us show that one can use (

3
Covariant and contravariant symbols

Let us consider Banach spaces
\cal L
and
\cal L^{\prime}
and systems of vectors

e_{\alpha}\in\cal L
,
e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\in\cal L^{\prime}.
Here
\alpha\in\cal M,\beta^{\prime}\in\cal M^{\prime}
,
\cal M\times\cal M^{\prime}
is a measure space. (Again we can consider a more general case when
\cal M\times\cal M^{\prime}
is a space with integration defined for some class of functions on this space.) We assume that linear combinations of vectors
e_{\alpha}
are dense in
\cal L
and linear combinations of vectors
e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}
are dense in
\cal L^{\prime}.
(In other words these systems of vectors are overcomplete.)

Let us fix a non-degenerate pairing
\langle l,l^{\prime}\rangle
between
\cal L
and
\cal L^{\prime}.
(We can consider either bilinear pairing or a pairing that is linear with respect to one argument and antilinear with respect to the second argument.)

We assume that

\langle l,l^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{\cal M\times\cal M^{\prime}}dmdm^{\prime}\langle l,e^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle R(m,m^{\prime})\langle e_{m},l^{\prime}\rangle,

(25)

where
dmdm^{\prime}
is the measure on
\cal M\times\cal M^{\prime}
. In different notations, this formula can be written as

\int dmdm^{\prime}\ |e^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle R(m,m^{\prime})\langle e_{m}|=1.

(26)

We define covariant symbol
\underline{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})
of operator
A
acting in
\cal L
by the formula

\underline{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\frac{\langle Ae_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}.

(27)

In bra-ket notations

\underline{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\frac{\langle e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}|A|e_{\alpha}\rangle}{\langle e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}|e_{\alpha}\rangle}.

(28)

In particular, we can assume that
\cal L

=\cal L^{\prime}
is a Fock space (Hilbert space of Fock representation of canonical commutation relations) and the overcomplete system of vectors in this space coinsists of eigenvectors of annihilation operators (of Poisson vectors). Then the covariant symbol coincides with Wick symbol.

Recall that the Wick symbol can be defined in the following way. Represent the operator in normal form (creation operators
\hat{a}^{*}(f)
from the left, annihilation operators
\hat{a}(g)
from the right). Remove hats.
Resulting polynomial of
a^{*},a
is a Wick symbol of the operator.

Notice that the spaces
\cal L
and
\cal L^{\prime}
are on equal footing in our construction; hence we can define the covariant symbol of an operator
B
acting in
\cal L^{\prime}
in a similar way. We say that operators
A
and
B
are dual if
\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,By\rangle,
it is easy to check that symbols of dual operators coincide

\underline{B}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\frac{\langle e_{\alpha},Be^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}={\underline{A}}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime}).

(29)

The covariant symbol
\underline{C}=\underline{A}*\underline{B}
of operator
C=AB
is given by the formula

\underline{C}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\int dmdm^{\prime}\ \underline{B}(\alpha,m^{\prime})\underline{A}(m,\beta^{\prime})R(m,m^{\prime})\frac{\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle\langle e_{m},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}.

(30)

This formula agrees with (
3
) if we take

\langle e_{m},e^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle=e^{c(m,m^{\prime})}.

We define contravariant symbol

\mathring{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})

of operator
A
acting in
\cal L
by the formula

Ae_{\alpha}=\int d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}\mathring{A}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})e_{\gamma}.

(31)

It is easy to express covariant symbols in terms of contravariant symbols

\underline{A}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})=\int d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}\mathring{A}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\frac{\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\langle e_{\gamma},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle}.

To calculate the contravariant symbol
\mathring{C}

of the product
C=AB
of operators
A,B
we notice that

Ce_{\alpha}=\int d\gamma_{1}d\gamma_{1}^{\prime}d\gamma_{2}d\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\mathring{A}(\gamma_{2},\gamma_{2}^{\prime})\langle e_{\alpha},e^{\prime}_{\gamma_{2}^{\prime}}\rangle R(\gamma_{2},\gamma_{2}^{\prime})\mathring{B}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\langle e_{\gamma_{2}},e^{\prime}_{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}\rangle R(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})e_{\gamma_{1}}.

(32)

Hence
\mathring{C}
can be expressed in terms of contravariant symbols of factors by the formula

\mathring{C}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})}\int d\beta d\beta^{\prime}\langle e_{\beta},e^{\prime}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle\mathring{A}(\beta,\gamma^{\prime})R(\beta,\gamma^{\prime})\mathring{B}(\gamma,\beta^{\prime})R(\gamma,\beta^{\prime}).

(33)

This expression has the form (
5
) with

e^{c(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})}=R(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}),\;\;\;e^{-r(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})}=\langle e_{\gamma},e^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle.

Notice that a bounded operator always has a covariant symbol. Moreover, an operator
A
has a covariant symbol if it is unbounded but the vectors
e_{\alpha}
belong to the domain where
A
is defined. However, it is non-trivial to say whether there exists an operator with a given covariant symbol. For contravariant symbols, the situation is the opposite. It is easy to give conditions for the existence of an operator with a given contravariant symbol, but if we know an operator it is non-trivial to say whether is has a contravariant symbol.

As in Section 3 we consider Banach spaces
\cal L
and
\cal L^{\prime}

We fix a representation

We require that linear combinations of vectors

To define covariant and contravariant symbols starting with vectors

It follows from the irreducibility of the representation

If
\cal M

One says that the vectors

L-functionals

5.1
First definition

Let us consider a unital associative algebra with generators
\gamma(f)
obeying canonical commutation relations (CCR):

\gamma(f)\gamma(g)-\gamma(g)\gamma(f)=i(f,g)

(35)

(Weyl algebra).

Here
f
and
g
are elements of real vector space
E
equipped with non-degenerate antisymmetric inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
, generators
\gamma(f)
depend linearly of
f
. We assume that Weyl algebra is a complex algebra equipped with an antilinear involution and
generators
\gamma(f)
are self-adjoint with respect to this involution.

Let us fix a representation of the Weyl algebra (representation of canonical commutation relations) in Hilbert space
\cal F
. We assume that generators are represented by self-adjoint operators
\hat{\gamma}(f)
; hence we can consider unitary operators
V_{f}=\exp(i\hat{\gamma}(f))
. It is easy to check that

V_{f}V_{g}=V_{f+g}\exp(\frac{i}{2}(f,g)).

(36)

These relations are formally equivalent to (
35
). We consider the smallest linear subspace of the space of bounded linear operators in
\cal F
containing all operators
V_{f}
; the closure of this space in norm-topology is a
C^{*}
-algebra that can be regarded as an exponential form of Weyl algebra (see, for example,
[
14
]
and references therein for the mathematical theory of Weyl algebra).
We will work with this algebra denoted by
\cal W.
The space of continuous linear functionals on
\cal W
will be denoted by
\cal L.
Notice that a functional
L\in\cal L

Second definition

Let us consider another form of canonical commutation relations:

5.3
Clifford algebra

Clifford algebra is defined by canonical anticommutation relations

[a(f),a(f^{\prime})]_{+}=[a^{*}(g),a^{*}(g^{\prime})]_{+}=0,\;\;\;\;[a(f),a^{*}(g)]_{+}=(f,g)

(51)

In other words to define Clifford algebra we take the definition of Weyl algebra and replace commutators with anticommutators.

The results above can be generalized to Clifford algebra. The main difference is that the symbols should be considered as functions of anticommuting variables.

The simplest way to understand this is to notice that Clifford algebra can be regarded as super Weyl algebra.

Recall that for
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-graded space
E
and Grassmann algebra
\Lambda
one can define a
\Lambda
-point of
E
as a formal linear combination
\sum\lambda_{A}e_{A}
where
e_{A}
is a basis of
E
and the coefficients
\lambda_{A}
are even for even
e_{A}
, odd for odd
e_{A}
. The set
E(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
- points can be regarded as vector space.
If
E
is an algebra this set also can be considered as an algebra. If for all Grassmann algebras
\Lambda
the set
E(\Lambda)
is a Lie algebra one says that
E
is a super Lie algebra. Similarly if for all Grassmann algebras the set
E(\Lambda)
is a Weyl algebra one can say that
E
is a super Weyl algebra. In particular, if f
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-graded space
E
is purely odd and equipped with a structure of Clifford algebra then the set of
\Lambda
-points is a Weyl algebra. This means that Clifford algebra can be considered as super Weyl algebra.
