Squeezing equivalence of quantum harmonic oscillators under different frequency modulations
Stanley S. Coelho, Lucas Queiroz, Danilo T. Alves

Abstract
The papers by Janszky and Adam [Phys. Rev. A 46 , 6091 (1992)] and Chen et al. [Phys. Rev. Lett. 104 , 063002 (2010)] are examples of works where one can find the following equivalences: quantum harmonic oscillators subjected to different time-dependent frequency modulations, during a certain time interval τ \tau , exhibit exactly the same final null squeezing parameter ( r f = 0 r_{f}=0 ). In the present paper, we discuss a more general case of squeezing equivalence, where the final squeezing parameter can be non-null ( r f ≥ 0 r_{f}\geq 0 ). We show that when the interest is in controlling the forms of the frequency modulations, but keeping free the choice of the values of r f r_{f} and τ \tau , this in general demands numerical calculations to find these values leading to squeezing equivalences (a particular case of this procedure recovers the equivalence found by Jansky and Adams). On the other hand, when the interest is not in previously controlling the form of these frequencies, but rather r f r_{f} and τ \tau (and also some constraints, such as minimization of energy), one can have analytical solutions for these frequencies leading to squeezing equivalences (particular cases of this procedure are usually applied in problems of shortcuts to adiabaticity, as done by Chen et al. ). In this way, this more general squeezing equivalence discussed here is connected to recent and important topics in the literature as, for instance, generation of squeezed states and the obtaining of shortcuts to adiabaticity.

More specifically, in Ref.

In Ref.
Chen et al. 2010
, Chen
et al.
have studied ways of accelerating the frictionless cooling process of atoms in harmonic traps based on a single parameter: the angular frequency
\omega(t)
of the TDHO.
This procedure is related to problems of shortcuts to adiabaticity, as the system is prepared to maintain the same initial and final states (less than a global phase factor) and energies, as an adiabatic process, but in a shorter time than that needed to reproduce a conventional adiabatic process

Thus, since the initial and final populations are the same of an usual adiabatic process

;

;

In the present paper, we discuss a more general case of squeezing equivalence, where the final squeezing parameter can be non-null (
r_{f}\geq 0
), and valid for discontinuous or continuous frequency modulations (as far as we know, this more general case of squeezing equivalence has never been discussed in the literature, and, in addition, the nomenclature “squeezing equivalence” itself is being proposed in the present article).
Given that the quantum fluctuations of the position and momentum operators, quantum fluctuations of the energy value, mean number of excitations and its fluctuations, and the transition probabilities between different states, depend on the squeezing parameter, the more general case of squeezing equivalence presented here implies that all these quantities will also be the same for the TDHOs, for
t>\tau

Taking as basis the Lewis-Riesenfeld (LR) dynamical invariant method

and
\tau

r_{f}
and
\tau

).
On the other hand, when the interest is not in previously controlling the form of the intermediary frequencies, but rather
r_{f}
and
\tau
(and also some constraints, such as frequency limitations and minimization of energy), one can have analytical solutions to find these frequencies leading to squeezing equivalence (particular cases of this procedure are usually applied in problems of shortcuts to adiabaticity, as done by Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2010
).

II
Analytical Method

In this section, by means of the LR method
Lewis and Riesenfeld 1969
, we make a brief review of the solution of the Schrödinger equation associated with the TDHO with time-dependent frequency, introducing the squeezing parameter and quantities related to it. These topics are well known and
found in the literature (see, for instance, Refs.
Janszky and Yushin 1986
;
Agarwal and Kumar 1991
;
Hong-Yi and Zaidi 1988
;
Pedrosa 1997
;
Pedrosa et al. 1997
;
Tibaduiza et al. 2020b
), being the novelty of the present paper in the introduction and
discussion of a more general case of squeezing equivalence, which allows different TDHOs to be configured in such a way that they have the same non-null final squeezing parameter.

II.1
The wave function of the time-dependent quantum harmonic oscillator

Let us consider the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(x,t)}{\partial t}=\hat{H}(t)\Psi(x,t),

(1)

for a one-dimensional TDHO with mass
m_{0}
and time-dependent angular frequency
\omega(t)
, whose Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}(t)
is

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{0}\omega(t)^{2}\hat{x}^{2},

(2)

where
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
are the position and momentum operators, respectively.
According to the LR method
Lewis and Riesenfeld 1969
;
Pedrosa 1997
;
Pedrosa et al. 1997
, the general solution of Eq. (
1
) is a superposition of orthonormal “expanding modes”

\displaystyle\Psi(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\Psi_{n}(x,t),

(3)

in which the time-independent coefficients
C_{n}
depend only on the initial conditions and
\Psi_{n}(x,t)
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(x,t)=\exp[i\alpha_{n}(t)]\Phi_{n}(x,t),

(4)

where
\alpha_{n}(t)
are phase functions, defined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(t)=-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{m_{0}\rho(t^{\prime})^{2}},

(5)

and the functions
\Phi_{n}(x,t)
are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}\biggl[\frac{1}{\pi\hbar\rho(t)^{2}}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{4}}\exp\biggl\{\frac{im_{0}}{2\hbar}\biggl[\frac{\dot{\rho}(t)}{\rho(t)}+\frac{i}{m_{0}\rho(t)^{2}}\biggr]x^{2}\biggr\}{\cal H}_{n}\biggl[\frac{x}{\hbar^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho(t)}\biggr],

(6)

with
{\cal H}_{n}
being the Hermite polynomials of order
n
.
The function
\rho(t)
in Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) is a real parameter which is solution of the Ermakov-Pinney equation
Pinney 1950
;
Cariñena and de Lucas 2009
;
de Lima et al. 2009
;
Mancas and Rosu 2015
:

\displaystyle\ddot{\rho}(t)+\omega(t)^{2}\rho(t)=\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}\rho(t)^{3}}.

(7)

For the case in which the frequency is always constant [
\omega(t)=\omega_{0}
], the solution of Eq. (
7
) is
\rho(t)=\rho_{0}
, in which

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{0}\omega_{0}}},

(8)

so that
\Psi_{n}(x,t)
falls back to the wave function
\Psi_{n}^{(0)}(x,t)
of a quantum harmonic oscillator with time-independent mass and frequency
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
.
An enlightening discussion of the connection between the Ermakov-Pinney equation (
7
) and classical mechanics is found in Refs.
Huang et al. 2020
;
Anderson et al. 1994
.

II.2
Squeezing parameter and related quantities

It is well known that the quantum states
\Psi_{n}(x,t)
of the TDHO are squeezed (see, for instance, Refs.
Pedrosa 1997
;
Pedrosa et al. 1997
).
Thus, we can define the squeezing parameter
r(t)
and the squeezing phase
\phi(t)
[with
r(t)\geq 0
and
0\leq\phi(t)\leq 2\pi
] in terms of the parameter
\rho(t)
, so that they can be written in the instantaneous basis
Martínez-Tibaduiza et al. 2021
as

\displaystyle r(t)=\cosh^{-1}\{[\lambda(t)]^{\frac{1}{2}}\},

(9)

where

\displaystyle\lambda(t)=\frac{m_{0}^{2}\dot{\rho}(t)^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho(t)^{2}}+m_{0}^{2}\omega(t)^{2}\rho(t)^{2}+2m_{0}\omega(t)}{4m_{0}\omega(t)},

(10)

and

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\cos^{-1}\left\{\frac{1+m_{0}\omega(t)\rho(t)^{2}-2\cosh^{2}[r(t)]}{2\sinh[r(t)]\cosh[r(t)]}\right\}.

(11)

Defining the variance of a given operator
\hat{{\cal O}}
in the state
\Psi_{n}(x,t)
as
\sigma_{\hat{{\cal O}}}^{2}(n,t)=[\langle\hat{{\cal O}}^{2}\rangle(n,t)]-[\langle\hat{{\cal O}}\rangle(n,t)]^{2}
, in which

\displaystyle\langle\hat{{\cal O}}\rangle(n,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\Psi_{n}^{*}(x,t)\hat{{\cal O}}\Psi_{n}(x,t),

(12)

we can write the variances of the position [
\sigma_{\hat{x}}^{2}(n,t)
] and momentum [
\sigma_{\hat{p}}^{2}(n,t)
] operators in terms of
r(t)
and
\phi(t)
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{x}}^{2}(n,t)\;=

\displaystyle\;\,\{\sinh^{2}[r(t)]+\cosh^{2}[r(t)]+2\sinh[r(t)]\cosh[r(t)]\cos[r(t)]\}\overline{\sigma}_{\hat{x}}^{2}(n,t),

(13)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{p}}^{2}(n,t)\;=

\displaystyle\;\,\{\sinh^{2}[r(t)]+\cosh^{2}[r(t)]-2\sinh[r(t)]\cosh[r(t)]\cos[r(t)]\}\overline{\sigma}_{\hat{p}}^{2}(n,t),

(14)

where
\overline{\sigma}_{\hat{x}}^{2}(n,t)
and
\overline{\sigma}_{\hat{p}}^{2}(n,t)
are defined respectively by

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}_{\hat{x}}^{2}(n,t)=\biggl(n+\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\frac{\hbar}{m_{0}\omega(t)},

(15)

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}_{\hat{p}}^{2}(n,t)=\biggl(n+\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\hbar m_{0}\omega(t).

(16)

Note that
\langle\hat{x}\rangle(n,t)=\langle\hat{p}\rangle(n,t)=0
, just as in the case where
\omega(t)=\omega_{0}
for any time interval.
In addition, we can also define the mean energy
E(n,t)=\langle\hat{H}(t)\rangle(n,t)
:

\displaystyle E(n,t)=\cosh[2r(t)]\overline{E}(n,t),

(17)

where
\overline{E}(n,t)=(n+1/2)\hbar\omega(t)
, and the energy variance
\sigma_{\hat{H}}^{2}(n,t)
as
Beau and del
Campo 2020
;
Galve and Lutz 2009

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{H}}^{2}(n,t)=\frac{\hbar^{2}\omega(t)^{2}}{2}(n^{2}+n+1)\sinh^{2}[2r(t)].

(18)

One can also determine the mean number of excitations
N(n,t)
, and its variance
\sigma_{\hat{N}}^{2}(n,t)
, for a system subjected to this time-varying potential. These quantities are given respectively by
Kim et al. 1989
;
Marian 1992
;
Moeckel and Kehrein 2009

\displaystyle N(n,t)=n+(2n+1)\sinh^{2}[r(t)],

(19)

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{\hat{N}}^{2}(n,t)=\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}+n+1)\sinh^{2}[2r(t)].

(20)

This means that a system can be excited due to the temporal variations in its frequency.
Because of this, the transition probability
{\cal P}(t)_{\mu\to\nu}
between different states
\mu
and
\nu
is given by
Coelho et al. 2022

\displaystyle{\cal P}(t)_{\mu\to\nu}=\frac{2^{\mu+\nu}[\min(\mu,\nu)!]^{2}[N(0,t)]^{\frac{|\nu-\mu|}{2}}}{\mu!\nu![N(0,t)+1]^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left|\sum_{q=\frac{|\nu-\mu|}{2}}^{\frac{\mu+\nu}{2}}\frac{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\mu+\nu}{2}\\
q\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\mu+\nu+2q-2}{4}\\
\frac{\mu+\nu}{2}\end{array}\right)q!}{\bigl(q-\frac{|\nu-\mu|}{2}\bigr)![N(0,t)+1]^{\frac{q}{2}}}\right|^{2},

for even values of
|\nu-\mu|
and
{\cal P}(t)_{\mu\to\nu}=0
for odd values of
|\nu-\mu|
, where
\min(\mu,\nu)
is the smallest value between
\mu
and
\nu
.
For a TDHO initially in the fundamental state, the probability of this system to be excited to different energy levels is
{\cal P}_{e}(t)=1-{\cal P}(t)_{0\,\to\,0}

Tibaduiza et al. 2020a
.

III
The model

Let us consider the frequency model defined by

\displaystyle\omega(t)=\begin{cases}\omega_{0},&t<0,\\
\omega_{\text{int}}(t),&0\leq t\leq\tau,\\
\omega_{f},&t>\tau,\end{cases}

(26)

where
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{f}
are the initial and final frequencies,
\tau
is the duration of the time interval separating these frequencies, and
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
is a time-dependent intermediary frequency.
Note that
\omega(t)
does not necessarily have to be continuous at
t=0
and
t=\tau
, i.e., we can have
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}\neq\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}\neq\omega_{f}
.
In this section, we show two approaches for obtaining the parameter
\rho(t)
associated with model (
26
), and discuss the effects caused by frequency changes on the quantities that depend on the squeezing parameter in the interval
t>\tau
.

III.1
Solution of the parameter
\rho(t)

Due to the form of Eq. (
26
), the parameter
\rho(t)
can be written as [hereafter, the same convention of indices and time-intervals is considered for
r(t)
and all related quantities]:

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\begin{cases}\rho_{0},&t<0,\\
\rho_{\text{int}}(t),&0\leq t\leq\tau,\\
\rho_{f}(t),&t>\tau,\end{cases}

(27)

in which
\rho_{0}
is given by Eq. (
8
), and
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
and
\rho_{f}(t)
are the parameters corresponding to the intermediary and final frequencies, respectively.
A first approach to determine
\rho(t)
is discussed in Sec.
III.1.1
, and is based on the formal solution of Eq. (
7
)
Pinney 1950
;
Cariñena and de Lucas 2009
;
de Lima et al. 2009
;
Mancas and Rosu 2015
, in which it is assumed that
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
is prescribed.
A second approach, discussed in Sec.
III.1.2
, is based on methods to find shortcuts to adiabaticity
Chen et al. 2010
;
Beau and del
Campo 2020
and consists of defining an ansatz for
\rho(t)
and,
using Eq. (
7
), determining the correspondent frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
.
The advantage of using one or the other approach will become clear in Sec.
V
.
Next, we calculate the solutions for
\rho(t)
using these two approaches.

III.1.1
Interval
0\leq t\leq\tau
: formal approach

For the interval
0\leq t\leq\tau
, the solution based in formal approach for
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
is given by
Pinney 1950
;
Cariñena and de Lucas 2009
;
de Lima et al. 2009
;
Mancas and Rosu 2015

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)=\sqrt{A_{\text{int}}u_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}+B_{\text{int}}v_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}+2C_{\text{int}}u_{\text{int}}(t)v_{\text{int}}(t)},

(28)

where
u_{\text{int}}(t)
and
v_{\text{int}}(t)
are two linearly independent solutions of the classical harmonic oscillator equation:

\displaystyle\ddot{z}(t)+\omega_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}z(t)=0,

(29)

and the constants
A_{\text{int}}
,
B_{\text{int}}
and
C_{\text{int}}
can be determined from the initial conditions

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}=\rho_{0},\;\;\;\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}=0,

(30)

and by the relation

\displaystyle A_{\text{int}}B_{\text{int}}-C_{\text{int}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\{m_{0}W[u_{\text{int}}(t),v_{\text{int}}(t)]|_{t=0}\}^{2}},

(31)

where
W[u_{\text{int}}(t),v_{\text{int}}(t)]=u_{\text{int}}(t)\dot{v}_{\text{int}}(t)-\dot{u}_{\text{int}}(t)v_{\text{int}}(t)
is the Wronskian determinant of
u_{\text{int}}(t)
and
v_{\text{int}}(t)
, which results in

\displaystyle A_{\text{int}}\;=\;

\displaystyle\;\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}v_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}|_{t=0}+\dot{v}_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}|_{t=0}}{m_{0}\omega_{0}\{W[u_{\text{int}}(t),v_{\text{int}}(t)]|_{t=0}\}^{2}},

(32)

\displaystyle B_{\text{int}}\;=\;

\displaystyle\;\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}u_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}|_{t=0}+\dot{u}_{\text{int}}(t)^{2}|_{t=0}}{m_{0}\omega_{0}\{W[u_{\text{int}}(t),v_{\text{int}}(t)]|_{t=0}\}^{2}},

(33)

\displaystyle C_{\text{int}}\;=\;

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}u_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}v_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}+\dot{u}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}\dot{v}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}}{m_{0}\omega_{0}\{W[u_{\text{int}}(t),v_{\text{int}}(t)]|_{t=0}\}^{2}}.

(34)

III.1.2
Interval
0\leq t\leq\tau
: ansatz approach

For the interval
0\leq t\leq\tau
, the solution based in ansatz approach for
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
is defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{6}\Gamma_{j}a_{j}(t),

(35)

where
a_{j}(t)
are linearly independent functions, and
\Gamma_{j}
are constants determined by the following boundary conditions at
t=0

Chen et al. 2010
;
Beau and del
Campo 2020
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}=\rho_{0},\;\;\;\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}=0,\;\;\;\ddot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=0}=0,

(36)

and, at
t=\tau
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\delta,\;\;\;\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\epsilon,\;\;\;\ddot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\gamma.

(37)

Using these conditions, one can find the solutions for
\Gamma_{j}
in terms of
a_{j}(t)|_{t=0}
and
a_{j}(t)|_{t=\tau}
, but the expressions are too long to be written here.
After determining
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
, the strategy is to obtain the frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
using the Ermakov-Pinney equation (
7
):

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{int}}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}\rho_{\text{int}}(t)^{4}}-\frac{\ddot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)}{\rho_{\text{int}}(t)}}.

(38)

As will be seen later, the presence of the term
\epsilon
in Eq. (
37
) is essential for the generation of squeezing after
t>\tau
(unlike what happens in the context of shortcuts to adiabaticity, where
\epsilon=0
, which naturally leads to a null final squeezing).
Thus, the techniques usually used in the context of shortcuts to adiabaticity are extended to set up the parameter
\rho(t)
[solution of the Ermakov-Pinney equation (
7
)] and, consequently, the intermediate frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
, to produce protocols with non-zero final squeezing.
This allows us to control, for different intermediate frequencies, the value that
r_{f}
can take on.
In summary, the squeezing equivalence found in protocols for shortcuts to adiabaticity
(leading to
r_{f}=0
) is a particular case of the squeezing equivalence found in more general protocols leading to
r_{f}>0
.
The term
\gamma
, on the other hand, is associated with continuous or discontinuous frequency transitions at
t=\tau
[if
\gamma=0
, we have
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\omega_{f}
, otherwise,
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}\neq\omega_{f}
].

III.1.3
Interval
t>\tau

For the interval
t>\tau
, the general solution for
\rho_{f}(t)
has the form

\displaystyle\rho_{f}(t)=\sqrt{A_{f}u_{f}(t)^{2}+B_{f}v_{f}(t)^{2}+2C_{f}u_{f}(t)v_{f}(t)},

(39)

where
u_{f}(t)=\sin(\omega_{f}t)
and
v_{f}(t)=\cos(\omega_{f}t)
.
By means of the continuity conditions

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\rho_{f}(t)|_{t=\tau},\;\;\;\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\dot{\rho}_{f}(t)|_{t=\tau},

(40)

and the relation

\displaystyle A_{f}B_{f}-C_{f}^{2}=\frac{1}{(m_{0}\omega_{f})^{2}},

(41)

one can express the coefficients
A_{f}
,
B_{f}
and
C_{f}
in terms of the parameter
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
and its time derivative
\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)
, both calculated at
t=\tau
[see Eq. (
37
)]:

\displaystyle A_{f}\;=

\displaystyle\,\;\biggl(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\omega_{f}^{2}}+\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}\omega_{f}^{2}\delta^{2}}-\delta^{2}\biggr)\cos^{2}(\omega_{f}\tau)+\delta^{2}+\frac{\epsilon\delta\sin(2\omega_{f}\tau)}{\omega_{f}},

(42)

\displaystyle B_{f}\;=

\displaystyle\;\,\biggl(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\omega_{f}^{2}}+\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}\omega_{f}^{2}\delta^{2}}-\delta^{2}\biggr)\sin^{2}(\omega_{f}\tau)+\delta^{2}-\frac{\epsilon\delta\sin(2\omega_{f}\tau)}{\omega_{f}},

(43)

\displaystyle C_{f}\;=

\displaystyle\;\,\frac{[m_{0}^{2}(\omega_{f}^{2}\delta^{4}-\epsilon^{2}\delta^{2})-1]\sin(2\omega_{f}\tau)}{2m_{0}^{2}\omega_{f}^{2}\delta^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon\delta\cos(2\omega_{f}\tau)}{\omega_{f}}.

(44)

Effectively, obtaining
\rho_{f}(t)
comes down to determining the constants
\delta
and
\epsilon
.

III.2
Squeezing parameter and related quantities for
t>\tau

It is interesting to analyze the consequences generated by the intermediary frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
on the dynamics of the system for
t>\tau
.
From Eqs. (
9
), (
10
) and (
39
)-(
44
), we have that

\displaystyle r_{f}=\cosh^{-1}[(\lambda_{f})^{\frac{1}{2}}],

(45)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}=\frac{m_{0}^{2}\epsilon^{2}+\delta^{-2}+m_{0}^{2}\omega_{f}^{2}\delta^{2}+2m_{0}\omega_{f}}{4m_{0}\omega_{f}}.

(46)

Note that the squeezing parameter is time-independent for
t>\tau
, because it only depends on
\delta
and
\epsilon
(ignoring the terms
m_{0}
and
\omega_{f}
, which we assume are prescribed).
In other words, it is enough to know the values of
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
and
\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)
in
t=\tau
to determine the dynamics of the system after the instant
\tau
.
Therefore, all quantities that depend only on
r_{f}
will be time-independent for
t>\tau
, where the frequency of the TDHO is
\omega_{f}
.
Besides this, the squeezing phase
\phi_{f}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{f}(t)=\cos^{-1}\biggl\{\frac{\sech(r_{f})}{4m_{0}\omega_{f}\sinh(r_{f})}\bigl\{2m_{0}^{2}\omega_{f}\epsilon\delta\sin[2\omega_{f}(t-\tau)]+[m_{0}^{2}(\omega_{f}^{2}\delta^{2}\;-\epsilon^{2})-\delta^{-2}]\cos[2\omega_{f}(t-\tau)]\bigr\}\biggr\}.

(47)

As can be seen, in contrast with
r_{f}
,
\phi_{f}(t)
is time-dependent.
Consequently,
\sigma_{\hat{x}}^{2}(n,t)
and
\sigma_{\hat{p}}^{2}(n,t)
will also depend explicitly on time for
t>\tau
, because these quantities depend on
\phi_{f}(t)
.

Furthermore, we obtain the following expression for the mean energy
E_{f}(n)
:

\displaystyle E_{f}(n)=\cosh(2r_{f})\overline{E}_{f}(n),

(48)

where
\overline{E}_{f}(n)=(n+1/2)\hbar\omega_{f}
.
Notably, the mean energy of the system for
t>\tau
is time-independent, as expected.
However, it can be seen that
E_{f}(n)\geq\overline{E}_{f}(n)
, even when the initial and final frequencies are the same (
\omega_{f}=\omega_{0}
)
Coelho et al. 2022
.
The argument is similar for
\sigma_{\hat{H}}^{2}(n,t)
in the interval
t>\tau
, where
\omega(t)=\omega_{f}
and
r(t)=r_{f}
[see Eq. (
18
)].
Using Eqs. (
19
), (
45
), (
46
) and (
48
), one can determine the mean number of excitations
N_{f}(n)
as

\displaystyle N_{f}(n)=\frac{E_{f}(n)-\overline{E}_{f}(0)}{2\overline{E}_{f}(0)}.

(49)

We highlight that there is excitation even for
n=0
since
E_{f}(0)>\overline{E}_{f}(0)
, which means that, under modulations in its frequency, a TDHO initially in its ground state can become excited.
Given this, the transition probability between different states, after the instant
\tau
, is obtained by substituting Eq. (
49
) (with
n=0
) into Eq. (
II.2
).
So, even when the frequency returns to its initial value (
\omega_{f}=\omega_{0}
) after an instant
\tau
, one can find a non-zero
\mu\to\nu
transition probability. This is a direct consequence of Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
).
The Eqs. (
45
)-(
49
) generalize, for a generic intermediary frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
and a final frequency
\omega_{f}
, the results found in Ref.
Coelho et al. 2022
for the particular case where
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)=\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{f}=\omega_{0}
.

IV
General condition for squeezing equivalence of quantum harmonic oscillators under different frequency modulations

In this section, we discuss how to configure a set of TDHOs, subject to different intermediary frequency modulations (discontinuous or continuous), so that they have the same squeezing parameter
r_{f}
.
We also discuss the physical implications of this equivalence.

Let us consider another TDHO with frequency
\omega^{\prime}(t)
, defined by

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}\omega_{0},&t<0,\\
\omega_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t),&0\leq t\leq\tau,\\
\omega_{f}^{\prime},&t>\tau.\end{cases}

(50)

The associated parameter
\rho^{\prime}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}\rho_{0},&t<0,\\
\rho_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t),&0\leq t\leq\tau,\\
\rho_{f}^{\prime}(t),&t>\tau.\end{cases}

(51)

The solutions for
\rho_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)
and
\rho_{f}^{\prime}(t)
are obtained following the same steps mentioned in Sec.
III.1
.
We represent these solutions by inserting a prime superscript in all objects with index “int” or
f
in Eqs. (
28
) to (
44
).
Thus, following this convention, the squeezing parameter
r_{f}^{\prime}
can be written by substituting
\omega_{f}\to\omega_{f}^{\prime}
,
\delta\to\delta^{\prime}
and
\epsilon\to\epsilon^{\prime}
into Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
), leading to

\displaystyle r_{f}^{\prime}=\cosh^{-1}[(\lambda_{f}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}],

(52)

in which

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{f}=\frac{m_{0}^{2}\epsilon^{\prime 2}+\delta^{\prime-2}+m_{0}^{2}\omega_{f}^{\prime 2}\delta^{\prime 2}+2m_{0}\omega_{f}^{\prime}}{4m_{0}\omega_{f}^{\prime}}.

(53)

Given that, in general,
\omega_{f}\neq\omega_{f}^{\prime}
,
\delta\neq\delta^{\prime}
and
\epsilon\neq\epsilon^{\prime}
, then
r_{f}\neq r_{f}^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, even when
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)\neq\omega_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)
, it is possible to determine a set of conditions which, if met, result in
r_{f}=r_{f}^{\prime}
.
Comparing Eqs. (
45
), (
46
), (
52
) and (
53
), it can be seen that the squeezing equivalence is achieved when

\displaystyle\omega_{f}=\omega_{f}^{\prime},\;\;\;\delta=\delta^{\prime},\;\;\;\epsilon=\epsilon^{\prime}.

(54)

Thus, Eq. (
54
) shows how to configure two TDHOs, with different intermediary frequency modulations, so that they result in a same value for the squeezing parameter or, in other words, in a squeezing equivalence.

As can be seen, the squeezing equivalence depends on the boundary conditions that
\rho(t)
,
\rho^{\prime}(t)
,
\dot{\rho}(t)
and
\dot{\rho}^{\prime}(t)
satisfy at
t=\tau
.
From Eqs. (
3
)-(
6
), one can see that these parameters determine
the behavior of the wave functions
\Psi(x,t)
and
\Psi^{\prime}(x,t)

Huang et al. 2020
;
Anderson et al. 1994
, so that the squeezing equivalence is a consequence
from that
\rho_{f}(t)=\rho_{f}^{\prime}(t)
and
\dot{\rho}_{f}(t)=\dot{\rho}_{f}^{\prime}(t)
(see Sec.
III.1.3
).
It is worth noting that
\Psi(x,t)
and
\Psi^{\prime}(x,t)
differ by a phase factor
[
\alpha_{n}(t)\neq\alpha_{n}^{\prime}(t)
] in the interval
t>\tau
[see Eq. (
5
)].
However, these phase factors do not contribute to the physical quantities investigated here.
This, in turn, means that all physical quantities investigated here, depending on the squeezing parameter
r_{f}
(see Sec.
III.2
) and, consequently, on the parameters
\rho(t)
and
\dot{\rho}(t)
, will be the same for
t>\tau
.

In summary, two TDHOs with different intermediary frequencies [
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)\neq\omega_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)
]
can have be configured [Eq. (
54
)] in such a way that
they exhibit the same final squeezing parameter (
r_{f}=r_{f}^{\prime}\geq 0
).
This is defined here a squeezing equivalence between these
two TDHOs.
We highlight that the criteria of squeezing equivalence proposed here
implies in the invariance of physical properties of the TDHOs,
in such a way that all physical quantities depending on the squeezing parameter will be the same for
t>\tau
(as, for instance, quantum fluctuations of the
position and momentum operators, quantum fluctuations of the energy, mean number of excitations and its fluctuations, and the transition probabilities between different states).
As mentioned before, a particular case of this squeezing equivalence
(
r_{f}=r_{f}^{\prime}=0
) is found, for example, in Refs.
Janszky and Adam 1992
;
Chen et al. 2010
,
whereas here we are discussing a more general case (
r_{f}=r_{f}^{\prime}\geq 0
).

The extension of this squeezing equivalence to two different TDHOs with
k
intermediary frequency modulations is natural.
To do this, simply consider that the time-dependent intermediary frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
in Eq. (
26
) is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{int}}(t)=\begin{cases}\omega_{1}(t),&0\leq t<t_{1},\\
\omega_{2}(t),&t_{1}\leq t<t_{2},\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\omega_{k}(t),&t_{k-1}\leq t\leq\tau,\end{cases}

(55)

where
k\geq 1
.
For
t<0
and
t>\tau
, the angular frequency remains
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{f}
, respectively.
The parameter
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
associated with Eq. (
55
) is written as

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)=\begin{cases}\rho_{1}(t),&0\leq t<t_{1},\\
\rho_{2}(t),&t_{1}\leq t<t_{2},\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\rho_{k}(t),&t_{k-1}\leq t\leq\tau,\end{cases}

(56)

and is constructed in the same way as already discussed.
Thus, for squeezing equivalence to occur, defining
\delta_{k}=\rho_{k}(t)|_{t=\tau}
and
\epsilon_{k}=\dot{\rho}_{k}(t)|_{t=\tau}
, it is sufficient that

\displaystyle\omega_{f}=\omega_{f}^{\prime},\;\;\;\delta_{k}=\delta_{k}^{\prime},\;\;\;\epsilon_{k}=\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}.

(57)

Moreover, the generalization of the squeezing equivalence to several different TDHOs with
k
intermediary frequency modulations is also direct, it is enough that

\displaystyle\omega_{f}=\omega_{f}^{\prime}=\omega_{f}^{\prime\prime}=\omega_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}=\ldots,

(58)

as well as

\displaystyle\delta_{k}=\delta_{k}^{\prime}=\delta_{k}^{\prime\prime}=\delta_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}=\ldots,

(59)

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=\epsilon_{k}^{\prime}=\epsilon_{k}^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}=\ldots,

(60)

in which “
\ldots
” indicates the several different TDHOs.

V.2
Relationship between squeezing equivalence and shortcuts to adiabaticity

In the context of shortcuts to adiabaticity, TDHOs are utilized to describe the fast frictionless cooling of atoms and quantum gases in harmonic traps
Salamon et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2010
;
Chen and Muga 2010
;
Stefanatos et al. 2010
;
Choi et al. 2012
;
Choi et al. 2013
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
;
Beau and del
Campo 2020
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Dupays et al. 2021
.
The measure of the degree of nonadiabaticity, given by
Q^{*}(t)
, can be written in terms of the squeezing parameter
r(t)
by
Q^{*}(t)=\cosh[2r(t)]

Galve and Lutz 2009
.
In our case, we can define the initial and final values of
Q^{*}(t)
as given by
Q_{0}^{*}=Q^{*}(t<0)
and
Q_{f}^{*}=Q^{*}(t>\tau)
, respectively. Thus, for nonadiabatic transformations one has
Q_{0}^{*}=1
and
Q_{f}^{*}>1
, whereas for adiabatic transformations,
Q_{0}^{*}=Q_{f}^{*}=1

Galve and Lutz 2009
.
This latter condition implies that
r_{0}=r_{f}=0
, which leads to null fluctuations in the energy [Eq. (
18
)], as well as a mean number of excitations that does not change [Eq. (
19
)] [consequently,
\sigma_{\hat{N}}^{2}(n,t)=0
)], so that the initial and final populations will be the same, as required by protocols that seek shortcuts to adiabaticity
Chen et al. 2010
;
Beau and del
Campo 2020
.
In this way, a null squeezing parameter in the intervals
t<0
and
t>\tau
are the key step to reach protocols that lead to shortcuts to adiabaticity
Choi et al. 2012
;
Choi et al. 2013
.

In this scenario, considering, for example, two different TDHOs, one can see that by making
\delta=\delta^{\prime}=1/(m_{0}\omega_{f})^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\epsilon=\epsilon^{\prime}=0
in Eq. (
54
), in addition to obtaining the same squeezing parameter
r_{f}=r_{f}^{\prime}=0
[see Eqs. (
45
), (
46
), (
52
) and (
53
)], we also obtain
Q_{f}^{*}=Q_{f}^{*\prime}=1
, i.e., two adiabatic protocols.
Thus, this means that different protocols that seek shortcuts to adiabaticity share the same final squeezing parameters, or in other words, there is an squeezing equivalence between two different adiabatic protocols.
These ideas can also be extended to
k
different intermediate frequency modulations and several different TDHOs by using Eqs. (
57
)-(
60
).
Consequently, the squeezing equivalence (in this case, a null equivalence) can also be used as a way of obtaining different trajectories that result in adiabatic protocols.

To illustrate the ideas mentioned above, let us start assuming that
\omega(t)
and
\omega^{\prime}(t)
are given by Eqs. (
26
) and (
50
).
Note that the intermediary frequencies are not prescribed in this case.
In this way, let us consider for
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
and
\rho_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)
the following ansatz:

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{6}\Gamma_{j}a_{j}(t),\;\;\;a_{j}(t)=(1+\beta t)^{j-1},

(66)

and

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{6}\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}a_{j}^{\prime}(t),\;\;\;a_{j}^{\prime}(t)=\exp(j\kappa t),

(67)

in which
\beta
and
\kappa
are parameters with frequency dimension, and
\Gamma_{j}
and
\Gamma_{j}^{\prime}
are written in terms of
a_{j}(t)
and
a_{j}^{\prime}(t)
, respectively, calculated at
t=0
and
t=\tau
(see Sec.
III.1.2
).
For simplicity, let us also consider a continuous transition in the frequencies at
t=\tau
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{0}\omega_{f}}},\;\;\;\delta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{0}\omega_{f}^{\prime}}},\;\;\;\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}=0.

V.3
Squeezing equivalence taking into account minimization of the intermediary mean energy

In the more general context of processes in which the initial and final populations are not the same, it can be useful to analyze the minimization of the mean energy in the intermediary interval, because large transient energies can lead to anharmonic effects
Chen and Muga 2010
;
Huang et al. 2020
.
Since the quasi-optimal
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
that minimizes the mean energy is know (note that the conditions
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\delta
and
\dot{\rho}_{\text{int}}(t)|_{t=\tau}=\epsilon
do not modify the parameter
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
, found in Ref.
Chen and Muga 2010
, that minimizes the mean energy), then the Ermakov-Pinney equation (
7
) can be used to determine the frequency
\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
that minimizes the intermediary mean energy.
The quasi-optimal
\rho_{\text{int}}(t)
can be written as
Chen and Muga 2010

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{int}}(t)=\begin{cases}\rho_{0}\sum_{l=0}^{5}c_{l}(t/\tau)^{l},&0\leq t/\tau<\sigma,\\
\rho_{0}f(t),&\sigma\leq t/\tau<1-\sigma,\\
\rho_{0}\sum_{l=0}^{5}d_{l}(t/\tau)^{l},&1-\sigma\leq t/\tau\leq 1,\end{cases}

(69)

where

\displaystyle f(t)=\sqrt{[B^{2}-(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}](t/\tau)^{2}+2Bt/\tau+1},

(70)

in which
B=\sqrt{(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}+\omega_{0}/\omega_{f}}-1
,
\sigma
is related to a prescribed instant, and the coefficients
c_{l}
and
d_{l}
are obtained analytically from the boundary conditions (
36
) and (
37
) (however, the expressions are too long to be written here).
This implies in minimization of the intermediary mean squeezing parameter [see Eq. (
17
)].
Thus, imposing the squeezing equivalence conditions given in Eq. (
54
) [or its variants (
57
)-(
60
)], one can obtain, for instance, a function
\omega_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)
so that
\omega_{\text{int}}^{\prime}(t)\neq\omega_{\text{int}}(t)
, but such that
r_{f}^{\prime}=r_{f}
.

VI
Final Remarks

We pointed out that relevant problems, as the generation of squeezed states
Janszky and Adam 1992
and shortcuts to adiabaticity

Chen et al. 2010
, involve a particular case of squeezing equivalence: time-dependent quantum harmonic oscillators which have the same initial frequency
\omega_{0}
, the same final frequency
\omega_{f}
, and the same initial squeezing parameter
r_{0}=0
, subjected to different intermediary frequency modulations during a certain time interval, exhibit exactly the same final squeezing parameter
r_{f}=0
.
Here, we discussed a more general case of squeezing equivalence, in which a set of time-dependent quantum harmonic oscillators, subjected to different intermediary frequency modulations during a certain time interval
\tau
, can display the same non-null squeezing parameter
r_{f}
after
\tau
.
Taking as basis the Lewis-Riesenfeld dynamical invariant method, we showed that the conditions for achieving this equivalence are such that, for the different oscillators, their final frequencies
\omega_{f}
, the solutions
\rho(t)
of the Ermakov-Pinney equation (
7
), and
\dot{\rho}(t)
, must be equal for
t>\tau
[Eqs. (
54
) and (
58
)-(
60
)].
Given that the quantum fluctuations of the position and momentum operators, quantum fluctuations of the energy value, mean number of excitations and its fluctuations, and the transition probabilities between different states, depend on the squeezing parameter [Eqs. (
9
)-(
II.2
)], the more general case of squeezing equivalence presented here implies that all these quantities will be the same for
t>\tau
, for all time-dependent quantum harmonic oscillators.

As applications, in Sec.
V
, we demonstrated that when the interest is in controlling the forms of intermediary frequency modulations, but keeping free
r_{f}
and
\tau
, this in general leads to numerical solutions to find values of
r_{f}
and
\tau
, which lead to a squeezing equivalence. When applied to a particular case, this procedure recovers the squeezing equivalence found by Jansky and Adams
Janszky and Adam 1992
.
On the other hand, when the interest is not in previously controlling the form of the intermediary frequencies, but rather
r_{f}
and
\tau
, one can have analytical solutions to find these frequencies leading to squeezing equivalence. Particular cases of this procedure are usually applied in problems of shortcuts to adiabaticity, as done by Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2010
, so that particular cases of the squeezing equivalence can be used as a way of obtaining different frequency modulations that result in adiabatic protocols (in this case, a null squeezing equivalence).
In addition, we discuss the squeezing equivalence between oscillators with generic frequency modulations and one with a modulation that minimizes the intermediary mean energy.

The more general squeezing equivalence between time-dependent quantum harmonic oscillators with different intermediary frequency modulations, discussed here, is connected to recent and important topics in the literature, which may be relevant, for example, in the area of quantum sensing, since the squeezing can be used to reduce noise in quantum systems, improving the precision of quantum measurement devices
Degen et al. 2017
;
Pezzè et al. 2018
;
Rashid et al. 2016
;
Xin et al. 2021
.
