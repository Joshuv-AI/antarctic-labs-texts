Heads, Tails, and AI Fails: LLMs, Randomness, and Human Judgments
Katherine Van Koevering, Jon Kleinberg

Abstract
Randomness is central to human cognition and to many applications in which large language models are deployed, yet probabilistic token generation does not imply that LLMs can produce unbiased random sequences. We study how contemporary LLMs generate binary random sequences using the classic behavioral-science paradigm of simulated coin flips. Across single flips, 20-flip sequences, n-gram statistics, run lengths, alternation rates, and next-flip predictability, we compare model outputs to both true Bernoulli baselines and human data from prior work. We find that LLMs reproduce several canonical human randomness biases, including over-alternation, aversion to long runs, and first-flip biases, but often amplify them or introduce model-specific distortions. Increasing temperature reduces some rigid patterns but does not eliminate systematic structure. We further investigate over-alternation through prompt-framing experiments, continuation tests, corpus searches, and residual-stream probes, finding evidence that this bias is not explained by direct memorization or tokenization alone. These results show that LLMs are unreliable generators of randomness and imperfect simulators of human randomness judgments, with implications for applications requiring stochasticity, fair sampling, or behavioral simulation.

Introduction

Understanding LLM performance on predictions of randomness offers a valuable test setting for LLM mimicry of humans as well as informs a variety of LLM applications. Few things are as consistently human as our ability to perceive patterns. Inversely, humans are generally terrible at recognizing (and producing) randomness. Our failures with randomness are predictable, consistent, and measurable in a way that few human biases are. These systematic failures of randomness are one of the most well studied and consistent phenomena of human cognition, with well over a hundred years of research stretching from philosophy to physics

The combination of consistency and extensive documentation makes randomness production an ideal setting in which to analyze LLM mimicry of human biases. Given that both a perfect non-human response (uniform randomness) and a perfect human response (as studied for over a century by behavioral scientists) are extraordinarily well-defined, this problem allows detailed analysis of the extent to which various production models reproduce these fallacies. The most common set-up for human experiments is the production of “random” binary sequences, which we mimic here for easy comparison to the extensive historical analysis of human behavior.

In this work, we explore how LLMs approach randomness and where and how they fail. There are three possibilities: (1) LLMs could behave like humans, replicating human biases in randomness generation; (2) they could be further than humans from uniform randomness, producing more deterministic or structured outputs; or (3) they could be closer than humans to uniform randomness, introducing noise beyond human tendencies. Each scenario has implications. If LLMs replicate human biases, they may serve as useful simulators of human cognition, but also inherit human failures. If they are more random, they may fail to capture human-like heuristics, limiting their use in simulation studies, but be more useful in tasks requiring pure randomness or games. If they are less random, they may be unsuitable for tasks requiring stochasticity. Additionally, this dichotomy of humanness has previously been explored as one aspect of measuring advanced AI
Persaud (2005)

This paper makes four contributions. First, we provide a systematic evaluation of LLM-generated binary random sequences across model families, prompts, and decoding temperatures. Second, we compare model outputs to both IID Bernoulli baselines and human behavioral data, showing that LLMs reproduce but often amplify canonical human randomness biases, including over-alternation, aversion to long runs, over-balancing, and first-flip effects. Third, we show that these biases remain even when temperature is increased, implying that stochastic decoding alone is not a reliable route to unbiased randomization. Fourth, we investigate possible sources of the dominant over-alternation bias through prompt-framing experiments, continuation tests, corpus searches, and internal probing, finding evidence against simple explanations based solely on prompt wording, direct memorization, or tokenization.

LLMs, Randomness, and Human Judgments

Unlike humans, when LLMs are used for almost any task, the temperature variable is a key parameter. Higher temperature seems likely to give more random (and thus less consistent) results. However, in tests, setting temperature above 1.5 proved impractical, as the model began to return results that held random text and we were unable to parse a coin flip from the text. As such, we test for temperatures from 0 to 1.5.
1
1

1

We treat temperature 0 as a practical low-stochasticity setting rather than a formal guarantee of determinism, since proprietary APIs may introduce backend nondeterminism, load-balancing effects, or undocumented decoding behavior.
.

Although LLMs define probability distributions over next tokens, their outputs are not random in the sense required by a Bernoulli coin-flip process. Given a prompt, the model produces logits over the vocabulary. Decoding then converts these logits into a sampled token, with temperature rescaling the distribution before sampling. At low temperature, decoding concentrates probability mass on the highest-probability tokens and may become effectively deterministic; at high temperature, lower-probability tokens become more likely, but the distribution remains shaped by the model’s learned logits and by additional decoding parameters such as top-p or top-k sampling. Thus, unbiased random generation requires more than sampling noise: the model must assign appropriate probability mass to the relevant outcomes, and the decoding procedure must preserve that balance.

3.2.2
Fraction of Heads

LLM vs True Randomness.
In an 8-flip sequence, a fair coin yields a binomial distribution centered at 4 heads. LLMs deviate from this expectation by overproducing sequences with moderate head counts and underproducing extremes. As shown in Figure
3
, models like Gemma, GPT-4, Llama 3, and Llama 3.1 tend to cluster around 4 heads per sequence, especially at higher temperatures. At lower temperatures, Llama 3 and Llama 3.1 show a slight but significant bias toward heads, while GPT-3.5 exhibits a strong tails bias that diminishes as temperature increases.

LLM vs Human Judgments.
Humans are known to overcorrect for randomness, producing sequences that are “too balanced” compared to true stochastic processes
Nickerson and Butler (2009)
. LLMs replicate and even amplify this tendency. At high temperatures, most models average just over 4 heads per 8 flips — closely mirroring human behavior, but with even tighter clustering. GPT-3.5 again diverges: at low temperatures, it shows a tails bias not typically observed in human data, though it converges toward human-like distributions at higher temperatures.

3.2.3
Runs and N-Grams

LLM vs True Randomness.
In a fair coin sequence, we expect an average of 3.5 alternations in 8 flips, a predictable distribution of run lengths, and n-gram frequencies consistent with a Bernoulli process. LLMs deviate from all three.

Alternations.
As shown in Figure
2
, all models over-alternate, especially at low temperatures. GPT-4 often outputs maximally alternating sequences like “H, T, H, T, …” at temperature 0. Llama 3 prefers a slightly less regular pattern (e.g., “H, T, H, H, T, H, T”) but still exceeds the expected alternation count. Even at higher temperatures, most models average around 5 alternations — well above the random baseline.

Runs.
LLMs underproduce long runs (Figure
3
). At low temperatures, nearly all models generate fewer runs than expected across all lengths. As temperature increases, some models (e.g., GPT-4, Llama 3) begin to overproduce runs of length 2 while still suppressing longer runs. GPT-3.5 is an exception: it produces a wider range of run lengths, including long runs that are rare in other models. Gemma underproduces 2-runs but comes close to the expected number of 3-runs.

N-Grams.
LLMs underproduce repeated n-grams like (T,T,T) and (H,H,H), especially at low temperatures. While some n-grams (e.g., (T,H), (H,T)) approach expected frequencies at higher temperatures, others remain suppressed. All models show a near-complete aversion to (H,H,H), and GPT-3.5 overuses (T,T) and (T,T,T), consistent with its earlier tails bias.

LLM vs Human Judgments.
Humans also over-alternate and avoid long runs
Nickerson and Butler (2009)
;
Bar-Hillel et al. (2014)
. LLMs replicate these tendencies but often exaggerate them. For example, while humans alternate about 60% of the time, LLMs often exceed this, especially at low temperatures. The suppression of long runs is more extreme in LLMs than in human data. In n-gram usage, LLMs mirror human preferences for certain patterns (e.g., (H,T,H), (T,H,T)) but diverge in magnitude and consistency. GPT-3.5’s variability and occasional long runs resemble human noisiness more than the rigid patterns of other models.

3.2.4
Predictability and Mean Squared Error

LLM vs True Randomness.
In a truly random sequence, each coin flip is independent and identically distributed, yielding a Mean Squared Error (MSE) of 0.25 when predicting the next flip from prior context. To assess the predictability of LLM-generated sequences, we prompted each model to generate 20 coin flips and trained a LASSO regression model to predict the 8th flip based on the preceding 7. Features included run lengths, alternations, and other sequence statistics known to influence human randomness generation
Kleinberg et al. (2017)
;
Rapoport and Budescu (1992)
.

As shown in Figure
4
, most LLMs produce sequences that are significantly more predictable than a fair coin. At low temperatures, MSE values are well below 0.25, indicating strong internal structure, and even at high temperatures MSE rarely approaches randomness.

LLM vs Human Judgments.
Human-generated sequences are imperfect but relatively difficult to predict. Prior work shows that the best achievable MSE on human-generated coin flips is slightly above 0.24
Kleinberg et al. (2017)
. Compared to this benchmark, most LLMs perform substantially worse, with lower MSEs indicating greater predictability.

GPT-3.5 again stands out: its high MSE values at elevated temperatures rival those of human data, indicating that its outputs may be less biased or more chaotic than those of other models.

3.2.5
Correlates of Predictability

LLM vs True Randomness.
In a truly random sequence, each flip is independent, so the correlation between the 8th flip and any of the previous seven should be near zero. To test this, we measured the correlation between the 8th flip and each of the preceding flips in 20-flip sequences generated by various LLMs (Figure
5
). Most models exhibit strong correlation or anti-correlation patterns, particularly with the immediately preceding flip (flip 7). GPT-4 and Gemma show strong negative correlation with flip 7, consistent with their over-alternation tendencies. These dependencies persist, though they weaken, across earlier positions in the sequence. As temperature increases, the magnitude of these correlations decreases but remains non-negligible.

LLM vs Human Judgments.
Human-generated sequences also exhibit dependencies between adjacent flips, often due to cognitive biases like the gambler’s fallacy and a preference for alternation
Nickerson and Butler (2009)
. LLMs replicate this tendency, but with greater rigidity. The strong anti-correlation between the 8th and 7th flips in GPT-4 and Gemma reflects an exaggerated version of human alternation bias, while GPT-3.5’s lack of consistent correlation mirrors the noisier, less structured nature of human-generated sequences.

4
Why Do LLMs Generate Human-Like Sequences?

The preceding analyses establish that LLM-generated coin-flip sequences are systematically biased toward alternation. We now investigate
why
. We do not attempt a complete causal decomposition; instead, we test and rule out several simpler explanations, finding that the bias is robust to prompt rephrasing, is not explained by direct memorization or tokenization, and is encoded early in the network but expressed only at final layers. Unless otherwise noted, experiments used
T{=}1.0
and 8B-scale models for open-weight families; procedural details appear in the Appendix.

4.1
Tokenization modulates but does not explain the bias

In space-separated format, all tested tokenizers encode individual
H
and
T
as single tokens, meaning each flip is a separate forward-pass decision. Gemma 2’s richer vocabulary (22/33 multi-flip patterns as single tokens versus 15/33 for other families) does not eliminate its bias.

Output format causally modulates alternation rate. Across GPT-4, Llama 3, and Llama 3.1, the concatenated format (no separators) substantially reduces alternation: Llama 3 drops from 0.649 to 0.382—
below
the Bernoulli baseline of 0.500. This sign reversal shows that the space-separated format is a necessary enabling condition, but format alone cannot be the cause since the bias persists across space, comma, and word formats.

Llama 3.1 emits multi-flip tokens (e.g.,
HT
) at 24.6% of generation steps versus 0.5% for Llama 3. However, masking all multi-flip tokens at generation time leaves alternation rate unchanged (
\Delta{=}+0.015
,
p{=}0.28
), ruling out bundling as the mechanism behind Llama 3.1’s near-Bernoulli statistics.

4.2
Models do not retrieve memorized sequences

If models retrieved fixed templates, they would continue their own prefixes at above-chance rates. Across five models, position-wise agreement between original and continued sequences is
{\approx}0.50
at both
K{=}4
and
K{=}10

One exception: Llama 3’s HTHH fingerprint.

Llama 3 begins 97.4% of sequences with
HTHH

T{\leq}0.6

T{=}1.5

Statistical continuation

All models except GPT-3.5 show a distinct pattern: while position agreement is at chance, bigram distributions between original tails and continuations are highly correlated (
p{<}0.001

4.3
Coin flip sequences are rare

We searched 500K documents each from C4 and Wikipedia, 115K from stats.stackexchange.com, and 1M from Dolma. C4 and Wikipedia yielded zero qualifying H/T sequences of length
{\geq}10

Large-scale n-gram counts via the infini-gram API reveal that H/T letter sequences in pretraining corpora are overwhelmingly code artifacts (ML logs, variable names), not coin-flip demonstrations. 4.4
The bias is domain-general

We tested eight semantically distinct framings of binary random processes: coin flips, Bernoulli trials, bitstrings, random walks, dice (odd/even), treatment/control assignment, and others. Above-chance alternation persists across
all
framings and all six tested models, including framings with no coin-flip vocabulary. The
treatment
framing consistently elicits the highest alternation—reaching 0.80 for Llama 3.1. This is not because clinical text contains alternating sequences; corpus analysis shows clinical binary sequences are
anti-alternating
(mean rate 0.326).

4.5
The bias is encoded early but expressed late

Logistic probes trained on residual-stream activations recover next-flip identity at near-ceiling accuracy from layer 0 across four tested architectures (Llama 3, Llama 3.1, Llama-3.2-3B, Gemma 2). However, the logit lens—projecting hidden states through the unembedding matrix—remains at or below chance until the final
{\sim}25\%
of layers, then rises sharply to 0.66–0.89 at the output.

This dissociation indicates that alternation-predictive information is present in the residual stream from the first layer but encoded orthogonally to the output vocabulary. The network spends most of its depth rotating this representation into alignment with the H/T subspace. Final-layer logit-lens accuracy tracks sequence-level bias (LASSO MSE) monotonically: strongly biased models commit more decisively than near-Bernoulli models.

5
Conclusion

5.1
Summary of findings

We evaluated whether LLMs can generate random binary sequences in a setting with well-established human and stochastic baselines. Across models and temperatures, outputs were systematically structured: models overproduced heads, began sequences with heads, generated overly balanced sequences, over-alternated, avoided long runs, and produced sequences that were more predictable than both Bernoulli randomness and human-generated sequences. These patterns show that probabilistic token generation does not imply unbiased random generation. Rather, the comparison to human data reveals a more specific pattern: LLMs often reproduce canonical human randomness biases, but in amplified and model-specific forms. Additional analyses of prompt framing, continuation, probing, and corpus frequency suggest that over-alternation is not merely an artifact of one prompt, direct memorization of explicit examples, or tokenization alone. Instead, it appears to reflect learned sequence priors that are expressed differently across models and are only partially mitigated by temperature.

5.2
Consequences and Future Work

These findings have practical consequences. Applications requiring unbiased stochasticity—fair sampling, randomized trials, lottery mechanisms, or exploration in agentic systems—should not rely on LLM-generated randomness, even at elevated temperatures. When true randomness is required, it should be injected explicitly via external PRNGs or post-processing rather than through temperature tuning or prompt engineering alone.

The biases we document also bear on the growing use of LLMs as simulated human participants in behavioral research. Our results suggest such simulations may systematically distort findings: LLMs replicate human-like heuristics but amplify them in ways that could mislead researchers about human cognition. This does not preclude their use—for some purposes, human-like stochasticity may be preferable to machine-like pseudo-randomness. More broadly, there are contexts where sharing human biases is desirable (cooperative games, ecologically valid stimuli) and contexts where it is harmful (fair allocation, cryptographic applications). Because LLMs can both mimic and exaggerate human biases, system designers must determine which regime applies and choose architecture and prompts accordingly.

Future work might extend these analyses to other randomness domains (dice, shuffling, mixed-strategy games), investigate whether fine-tuning or RLHF specifically amplifies these biases, or develop debiasing interventions that preserve useful stochasticity while removing predictable structure.

5.3
Limitations

First, our primary task is binary coin-flip generation. This task is useful because it has well-established human and stochastic baselines, but it does not exhaust all forms of randomness. Other domains may exhibit different model-specific biases. Second, some models are accessed through commercial APIs or hosted endpoints. These systems may use undocumented decoding implementations, backend nondeterminism, safety filters, or model updates. We therefore treat temperature and other decoding parameters as practical interface settings rather than fully controlled algorithmic variables. Third, we do not fully isolate training data, instruction tuning, RLHF, tokenization, and decoding as causal factors. Our analyses provide evidence against several simple explanations, but they do not constitute a complete causal mechanism.
Fourth, our comparisons to human data rely on previously published studies and as such are reference points rather than experimental controls. Finally, invalid or unparseable outputs occur more often at high temperature, and our analysis may understate the practical difficulty of using high-temperature LLM outputs as random generators.

A.4
Output Parsing

We parsed model responses with a deterministic script. The response text was uppercased and stripped of punctuation. Word forms (
head
,
heads
,
tail
,
tails
) were replaced with H and T respectively via regular expression. All remaining H and T characters were then extracted in order as the flip sequence.

Responses containing fewer than 10 parseable flips were marked invalid. For the main analyses, invalid trials were discarded and the model was re-queried until the target number of valid sequences was reached (up to three re-attempts per trial). Responses were never truncated or padded; sequences with the wrong length were re-sampled entirely. We report invalid-response rates by model and temperature in Appendix
A.5
.

B.2
Notes on Model Responses

It is worth noting that Gemma, ChatGPT and Llama reacted very differently to this task. ChatGPT frequently reminded the user that they were not a random number generator, did not have a coin, and that users may wish to complete this task a different way. Llama, on the other hand, did not respond this way. In fact, at least once, it claimed to be using a random number generator to generate the flips. It also, on at least one occasion, reported a flip as “bad flip (retrying)”. Gemma refused the prompt entirely unless asked to “simulate” the flips instead, so we make that adjustment for the prompts for Gemma. All models would, at the higher temperatures, occasionally return unintelligible text, stop part way through, or otherwise fail to complete the task.
